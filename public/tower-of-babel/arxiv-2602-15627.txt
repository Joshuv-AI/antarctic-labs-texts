Fastest first-passage time for multiple searchers with finite speed
Denis S. Grebenkov, Ralf Metzler, Gleb Oshanin

Abstract
We study analytically and numerically the mean fastest first-passage time (fFPT) to an immobile target for an ensemble of N N independent finite-speed random searchers driven by dichotomous noise and described by the telegrapher’s equation. In stark contrast to the well-studied case of Brownian particles—for which the mean fFPT vanishes logarithmically with N N —we uncover that the mean fFPT is bounded from below by the minimal ballistic travel time, with an exponentially fast convergence to this bound as N → ∞ N\to\infty . This behavior reveals a dramatic efficiency advantage of physically realistic, finite-speed searchers over Brownian ones and illustrates how diffusive macroscopic models may be conceptually misleading in predicting the short-time behavior of a physical system. We extend our analysis to anomalous diffusion generated by Riemann-Liouville-type dichotomous noises and find that target detection is more efficient in the superdiffusive regime, followed by normal and then subdiffusive regimes, in agreement with physical intuition and contrary to earlier predictions.

fastest for superdiffusion and longest for subdiffusion, which agrees
with physical intuition. The trend is reversed for
x_{0}/(vT_{0})<1
,
when the target can be reached in a single step (see SM.C
sm
).

Conclusions.
We revisited the biologically relevant problem of parallel
search by
N
independent random searchers for an immobile target, a
paradigmatic setting for extreme first-passage statistics in many
applications. In the standard Brownian-motion framework, the mean
fFPT decreases only logarithmically with
N
and formally vanishes as

N\to\infty
, implying both instantaneous arrival in the infinite-
N

limit and a weak benefit from deploying multiple searchers. This
framework further predicts subdiffusive dynamics to outperform normal
diffusion—an artifact of the unphysical possibility of arbitrarily
fast propagation.

Fixing this shortcoming, we formulated the search dynamics using
dichotomous noise, leading to the telegrapher’s equation with finite
propagation speed and compact, time-dependent support of the position
PDF. In this physically consistent setting, the mean fFPT converges,
as
N\to\infty
, to a finite lower bound equal to the shortest
possible, ballistic travel time. Remarkably, the convergence to this
bound is exponentially fast in
N
, demonstrating a dramatic
efficiency gain over Brownian search. From a biological perspective,
it is thus meaningful to invest the energy to produce many searchers
to substantially speed up underlying random search processes. For
intermediate
N
, the convergence remains slow and mimics the familiar
logarithmic behavior, thereby reconciling earlier results with
finite-speed dynamics.

We extended our analysis to anomalous transport via the
Riemann-Liouville dichotomous process. This yielded a clear hierarchy:
superdiffusive search is most efficient, followed by diffusive scaling
and then subdiffusion, in contrast to predictions from
continuous-space fractional diffusion models.

Overall, our results show that imposing a finite propagation speed
fundamentally alters the extreme first-passage behavior, resolves
long-standing paradoxes of Brownian statistics, and identifies
finite-velocity searchers as a superior strategy for rapid parallel
target detection. Future research directions include determining the
full fFPT distribution and extending the discrete-space approach
developed in Ref.
Lawley20b
.

SM.A
Exact solution for a single particle

For completeness, we compute the survival probability of a
single

particle on the positive semi-axis. This computation was performed in

Malakar18
(see also
urna
;
Mori20
). Here we start from the same
backward Fokker-Planck equations and provide an alternative computation,
which yields the same results. Let
S_{\pm}(t|x_{0})=\mathbb{P}_{x_{0},\pm}\{\tau>t\}
denote the survival probability for a particle launched at
x_{0}
with
the velocity
\pm v
. The switching between positive and negative velocities
can be easily implemented into the backward Fokker-Planck eqution:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}S_{+}(t|x_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v\partial_{x_{0}}S_{+}(t|x_{0})-\lambda S_{+}(t|x_{0})+\lambda S_{-}(t|x_{0}),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}S_{-}(t|x_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-v\partial_{x_{0}}S_{-}(t|x_{0})-\lambda S_{-}(t|x_{0})+\lambda S_{+}(t|x_{0}).

The action of the time derivative onto the second equation reads

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}S_{-}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(\lambda+v\partial_{x_{0}})\partial_{t}S_{-}+\lambda\partial_{t}S_{+}

(S18)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(\lambda+v\partial_{x_{0}})\bigl[-v\partial_{x_{0}}S_{-}+\lambda(S_{+}-S_{-})\bigr]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\lambda\bigl[v\partial_{x_{0}}S_{+}-\lambda(S_{+}-S_{-})\bigr]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v^{2}\partial_{x_{0}}^{2}S_{-}-2\lambda\partial_{t}S_{-},

i.e., one recovers the standard form of the telegrapher’s equation (see, e.g.,

Bena06
),

(\partial_{t}^{2}+2\lambda\partial_{t}-v^{2}\partial_{x_{0}}^{2})S(t|x_{0})=0\qquad(x_{0}>0),

(S19)

where we dropped the subscript minus. The initial condition for the
survival probability is
S(0|x_{0})=1
for any
x_{0}>0
. In addition, one
imposes the constraint
\lim\limits_{x_{0}\to\infty}S(t|x_{0})=1
, since a
particle started from infinity cannot reach the origin in a finite
time. As a particle started from
x_{0}=0
with a negative velocity
immediately hits the origin, one has
S(t|0)=0
. Finally, as we aim at
dealing with the second-order (in time) equation (
S19
), we
need to specify the initial value of the first time derivative, for
which we take
\partial_{t}S(0|x_{0})=0
. This is a consequence of the
initial condition
S_{\pm}(0|x_{0})=1
and of the above backward
Fokker-Planck equation for
S_{-}(t|x_{0})
.

The Laplace transform of Eq. (
S19
) yields

\displaystyle(p^{2}+2\lambda p-v^{2}\partial_{x_{0}}^{2})\tilde{S}(p|x_{0})

\displaystyle\quad=(2\lambda+p)S(0|x_{0})+S^{\prime}(0|x_{0})=2\lambda+p\qquad(x_{0}>0),

where

\tilde{S}(p|x_{0})=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{-pt}\,S(t|x_{0})\qquad(p>0).

(S21)

Its solution on the positive semi-axis reads

\tilde{S}(p|x_{0})=\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{p}e^{-\sqrt{p^{2}+2\lambda p}\,x_{0}/v},

(S22)

whereas its inverse Laplace transform is given by

\displaystyle S(t|x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\Theta(t-x_{0}/v)\biggl[e^{-\lambda x_{0}/v}

(S23)

\displaystyle+\lambda x_{0}/v\int\limits_{\lambda x_{0}/v}^{\lambda t}dz\frac{e^{-z}I_{1}(\sqrt{z^{2}-(\lambda x_{0}/v)^{2}})}{\sqrt{z^{2}-(\lambda x_{0}/v)^{2}}}\biggr],

where
I_{\nu}(z)
is the modified Bessel function of the first kind and
\Theta(z)
is the Heaviside step function:
\Theta(z)=1
for
z>0
and
0
otherwise.
One sees that the survival probability is strictly unity for
t<x_{0}/v
, and
then it exhibits a jump
e^{-\lambda x_{0}/v}

possibility to reach the target by a single displacement. The negative time
derivative of

gives the FPT-PDF

\displaystyle H(t|x_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta(t-x_{0}/v)e^{-\lambda x_{0}/v}+\Theta(t-x_{0}/v)(x_{0}/v)\lambda

(S24)

\displaystyle\times\frac{e^{-\lambda t}I_{1}(\lambda\sqrt{t^{2}-(x_{0}/v)^{2}})}{\sqrt{t^{2}-(x_{0}/v)^{2}}}.

The jump in the survival probability leads to a Dirac-
\delta
distribution
in the FPT-PDF at

(S25)

where

It is convenient to express the above results in terms of the

(S26)

Since the survival probability vanishes at infinity,
S(\infty|x_{0})=0

\int\limits_{1}^{\infty}dz\frac{e^{-\gamma z}I_{1}(\gamma\sqrt{z^{2}-1})}{\sqrt{z^{2}-1}}=\frac{1-e^{-\gamma}}{\gamma}\qquad(\gamma>0).

(S27)

Mean fFPT

Let us now consider the mean fFPT

\displaystyle 1-e^{-\gamma}-\gamma\int\limits_{1}^{y}dz\frac{e^{-\gamma z}I_{1}(\gamma\sqrt{z^{2}-1})}{\sqrt{z^{2}-1}}

\displaystyle\gamma\int\limits_{y}^{\infty}dz\frac{e^{-\gamma z}I_{1}(\gamma\sqrt{z^{2}-1})}{\sqrt{z^{2}-1}}\quad(y\geq 1),

2
Asymptotic behavior at large

We seek an accurate uniform approximation of

4
Diffusion limit

The diffusion limit corresponds to

and
\lambda\to\infty
with
D=v^{2}/(2\lambda)
being fixed. In this case, one has
\gamma=x_{0}v/(2D)\to\infty

First, we check that the survival probability

approaches the expected
limit of ordinary diffusion as
\gamma\to\infty

in Eq. (
S26
) can be replaced by unity, whereas

vanishes.
Using the asymptotic behavior of the modified Bessel function,
I_{1}(z)\simeq e^{z}/\sqrt{2\pi z}

Eq. (
S26

\displaystyle S(t|x_{0})

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle 1-\gamma\int\limits_{1}^{\gamma t/T}dz\frac{e^{-\gamma z+\gamma\sqrt{z^{2}-1}}}{(z^{2}-1)^{3/4}\sqrt{2\pi\gamma}}\approx 1-\frac{\sqrt{\gamma}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\limits_{1}^{\gamma t/T}dz\frac{e^{-\gamma/(2z)}}{(z^{2}-1)^{3/4}}

(S53)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\frac{\sqrt{\gamma/2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int\limits_{2/\gamma}^{2t/T}dz\frac{e^{-1/z}(\gamma/2)}{(\gamma^{2}z^{2}/4-1)^{3/4}}\approx 1-\frac{\sqrt{\gamma/2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int\limits_{0}^{2t/T}dz\frac{e^{-1/z}(\gamma/2)}{(\gamma/2)^{3/2}z^{3/2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int\limits_{0}^{2t/T}dz\frac{e^{-1/z}}{z^{3/2}}=1-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int\limits_{\sqrt{T/(2t)}}^{\infty}dz\,e^{-z^{2}}=\mathrm{erf}(\sqrt{T/(2t)}),

\overline{\mathcal{T}_{N}}=T\frac{1+B_{N,\gamma}}{\gamma}.

In order to evaluate the limit

\frac{B_{N,\gamma}}{\gamma}\approx\int\limits_{0}^{\sqrt{\gamma}}\frac{dz}{z^{3}}\bigl[\mathrm{erf}(z)\bigr]^{N}-\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}\int\limits_{0}^{\sqrt{\gamma}}dzz\bigl[\mathrm{erf}(z)\bigr]^{N}.

(S56)

The second integral vanishes as
\gamma\to\infty

\lim\limits_{\gamma\to\infty}\frac{B_{N,\gamma}}{\gamma}\approx\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dz}{z^{3}}\bigl[\mathrm{erf}(z)\bigr]^{N}.

(S57)

Its large-

asymptotic behavior follows from the analysis of the mean fFPT
for ordinary diffusion,

\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\bigl[\mathrm{erf}(x_{0}/\sqrt{4Dt})\bigr]^{N}=\frac{x_{0}^{2}}{2D}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dz}{z^{3}}\bigl[\mathrm{erf}(z)\bigr]^{N},

(S58)

so that

\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dz}{z^{3}}\bigl[\mathrm{erf}(z)\bigr]^{N}\simeq\frac{1}{2\ln N}+\mathcal{O}\bigl([\ln(N)]^{-2}\bigr).

(S59)

We therefore recover the leading-order term of the mean fFPT in the diffusion
limit.

5
Variance and higher-order moments of the fFPT

According to Eq. (
S26
), the
k
th-order moment of the fFPT is

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{T}_{N}^{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\,t^{k-1}[S(t|x_{0})]^{N}

(S60)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t_{\min}^{k}\biggl\{1+k\int\limits_{1}^{\infty}dy\,y^{k-1}[f_{\gamma}(y)]^{N}\biggr\},

where the function
f_{\gamma}(y)
is defined in Eq. (
S34
), and

t_{\min}=x_{0}/v
. In particular, the variance reads

\frac{\mathrm{var}\{\mathcal{T}_{N}\}}{t_{\min}^{2}}=2\int\limits_{1}^{\infty}dy(y-1)[f_{\gamma}(y)]^{N}-\biggl(\int\limits_{1}^{\infty}dy[f_{\gamma}(y)]^{N}\biggr)^{2}.

(S61)

In the limit
N\to\infty
with fixed
\gamma
, one employs again the Taylor
expansion (
S36
) to get

\frac{\mathrm{var}\{\mathcal{T}_{N}\}}{t_{\min}^{2}}\simeq(1-e^{-\gamma})^{N}\frac{4(e^{\gamma}-1)^{2}}{\gamma^{4}N^{2}}\bigl(2-(1-e^{-\gamma})^{N}\bigr),

(S62)

which is valid for
N\gg N_{\gamma}
. While the mean fFPT approaches its
minimal value
t_{\min}
, the variance vanishes exponentially fast, i.e., the
PDF of the fFPT becomes concentrated near
t_{\min}
.

In the intermediate regime
3\leq N\ll N_{\gamma}
, one can adapt the asymptotic
technique from Sec.
3
to analyze the variance. However, we
skip this analysis and employ numerical computation of the integrals in
Eq. (
S61
) to illustrate its behavior (see Fig.
3
).

SM.C
Simulations for anomalous diffusion

In this Section, we describe Monte Carlo simulations for anomalous diffusion
governed by dichotomous noise. As the particles move independently from each
other, we restrict the construction to a single particle trajectory, omitting
the index
k
enumerating different particles.

The dichotomous noise process can be formally defined in terms of independent
identically distributed random variables
\delta_{i}
, obeying the exponential
law with the mean
1/\lambda
,

\displaystyle\eta(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v(-1)^{n(t)+n_{0}},

\displaystyle n(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min\left\{n>0:\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}\geq t\right\},

(S63)

where
n_{0}
characterizes the choice of the sign in the first step (in our
simulations, we set
n_{0}=1
to start with the negative sign). Substituting
Eq. (
S63
) into Eq. (
5
), one gets

\displaystyle x(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{0}+\frac{vT_{0}^{1-\beta}(-1)^{n_{0}}}{\Gamma(\beta+1)}

(S64)

\displaystyle\hskip-17.07182pt\times\biggl\{t^{\beta}+2\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n(t)}(-1)^{i}\bigl(t-(\delta_{1}+\ldots+\delta_{i})\bigr)^{\beta}\biggr\},

where we introduced a shortcut notation
\beta=(\alpha+1)/2
. For
practical purposes, we aim at evaluating
x(t)
at times

t_{i}=\delta_{1}+\ldots+\delta_{i}
. Note that
n(t_{n})=n
by construction
such that

\displaystyle x(t_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{0}+\frac{vT_{0}^{1-\beta}(-1)^{n_{0}}}{\Gamma(\beta+1)}\biggl\{t_{n}^{\beta}+2\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n-1}(-1)^{i}(t_{n}-t_{i})^{\beta}\biggr\},

\displaystyle x(t_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{0}+\frac{vT_{0}^{1-\beta}(-1)^{n_{0}}}{\Gamma(\beta+1)}t_{1}^{\beta}.

(S65)

In the case of ordinary diffusion (
\alpha=1
), we have
\beta=1
and obtain

x(t_{n})=x(t_{n-1})+v\delta_{n}(-1)^{n-1+n_{0}}\quad(n=1,2,\ldots),

(S66)

with
x(0)=x_{0}
, as expected. This recurrence relation is used for
Monte Carlo simulations in the diffusive regime (
\alpha=1
). In
fact, for
N
particles, we advance their positions in parallel by
generating waiting times. When one particle has crossed the target
(i.e.,
x(t_{n})<0
), the simulation is stopped, and the crossing time
is evaluated. We mention a potential drawback of this simulation: if
one particle is advanced by large
\delta_{n}
, another particle might
cross the origin earlier. However, as the probability of getting
abnormally large
\delta_{n}
is exponentially small, the induced error
is small. We checked that it was actually negligible.

The situation is different for the anomalous case (
\alpha\neq 1
). In
fact, for each step
t_{n}
, one needs to re-evaluate the sum in
Eq. (
S65
) that would result in much slower simulations. Once
the simulation is stopped when
x(t_{n})<0
, the crossing time
t_{c}
,
lying between
t_{n-1}
and
t_{n}
, is evaluated by solving numerically
the equation
x(t_{c})=0
, where
x(t)
is given by Eq. (
S64
)
with
n(t)=n-1
.

It is worth noting that the minimal time to reach the target for a
single particle corresponds to a single move, so that

x(t)=x_{0}-vT_{0}^{1-\beta}t^{\beta}/\Gamma(\beta+1)
, from which our
Eq. (
16
) follows. When
x_{0}/(vT_{0})
is large enough,

t_{\min}
is a monotonously decreasing function of
\beta
(or

\alpha
), as intuitively expected; in particular, superdiffusion
allows to reach the target
faster
than subdiffusion. However,
if
x_{0}/(vT_{0})\lesssim 1
, the opposite trend occurs, i.e.,
t_{\min}
monotonously
increases with
\alpha
. Here we thus return to the counter-intuitive
situation when superdiffusion may look less efficient than
subdiffusion. We emphasize however, that this
"
paradox
"
is
rather artificial. In fact, when
\overline{x^{2}(t)}\sim t^{\alpha}
,
the MSD with a larger
\alpha
grows faster only at long times; in
turn, it grows slower at short times. As the first-passage time is
determined by short times, we are in the seemingly

"
paradoxical
"
setting when superdiffusion is less efficient
than subdiffusion.
