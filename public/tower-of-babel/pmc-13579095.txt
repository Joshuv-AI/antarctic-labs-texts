Apolipoprotein E DNA Methylation Linked to Cognitive Decline in Postmenopausal Women With Breast Cancer
Authors: Emily K. Mewborn, Lacey W. Heinsberg, Myeong‐ga Cho, Kirk I. Erickson, Catherine M. Bender, Yvette P. Conley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundCognitive decline is common among individuals with breast cancer (BC), and carriage of the apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 allele increases risk. APOE DNA methylation (DNAm) may influence cognition through regulation of neuroinflammatory and amyloid‐related pathways. Exercise is a potential factor affecting DNAm and cognition. This study examined associations between APOE DNAm and cognition in postmenopausal women with BC and evaluated moderation by APOE4 carriage status and exercise.MethodsData were drawn from a 6‐month randomized trial of moderate‐intensity exercise versus usual care. Cognition was assessed at baseline and 6 months, and change scores were calculated. APOE4 status (carrier, non‐carrier) and baseline DNAm (M‐value) were determined from peripheral blood. Regression models tested cross‐sectional and longitudinal associations, adjusting for age and education. Moderation by APOE4 status and intervention group was also tested.ResultsAmong 102 participants, APOE DNAm was consistently suggestively associated with cognition. Higher DNAm at promoter‐region CpGs (cg04406254, cg14123992, cg20051876) was linked to poorer cognition, with direction and magnitude differing by APOE4 status and group. APOE4 carriers with higher DNAm showed better cognitive performance, and exercisers with higher DNAm showed improved outcomes, suggesting exercise may buffer epigenetic effects.ConclusionsFindings may implicate APOE DNAm as a biomarker of cognition and highlight exercise supporting cognitive health in women with BC, although replication is needed.

Introduction

Up to 75% of women with breast cancer (BC) experience cognitive symptoms, both before and after receiving BC treatment (Ahles et al. 2012). As survival rates improve, more women with BC are living longer with these effects, underscoring the importance of identifying biological and modifiable contributors to cognitive decline in this population.

Carriage of the apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 allele has been associated with poorer cognitive outcomes in women with BC (Ahles et al. 2024), suggesting that genetic risk may interact with cancer‐related exposures to shape cognitive trajectories. The APOE gene consists of four exons and two single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (rs429358 and rs7412) located on the fourth exon, which determine the three APOE alleles (2, 3, and 4). These alleles encode for APOE protein isoforms (ApoE2, ApoE3, and ApoE4) that vary in function, including cholesterol transport, neuroinflammation, and amyloid beta clearance (Di Gerlando et al. 2024). Due to functional differences, cognitive decline differs based on the APOE allele, with ε2 decreasing risk, ε3 carrying a neutral risk, and ε4 increasing risk (Farrer 1997).

Beyond inherited APOE genotype differences, regulation of APOE expression may also occur through epigenetic mechanisms, modifications of the genome that can regulate gene activity without altering the DNA sequence. Among these, DNA methylation, which involves the addition of methyl groups to dinucleotides at cytosine‐phosphate‐guanine (CpG) islands, is of particular interest because it reflects interactions between genetic makeup, environmental exposures, and disease processes. Once thought to be a permanent change, DNA methylation is instead a dynamic process, influenced by internal and external factors (Bochtler et al. 2017). For the APOE gene, changes in DNA methylation may alter gene expression in an isoform‐specific manner, potentially influencing APOE protein levels and potentially contributing to cognitive decline pathology.

Despite the well‐established role of the APOE genotype as a risk factor for cognitive decline, including in women with BC (Ahles et al. 2024), the role of APOE DNA methylation in cognition remains poorly understood. Exercise, which is known to influence DNA methylation patterns (Etayo‐Urtasun et al. 2024), may represent a modifiable factor in this process, though its role in cognition among women with BC is unclear. To address these gaps, the purpose of this study was to evaluate associations between APOE DNA methylation and cognitive function in postmenopausal women with breast cancer, using data from a six‐month randomized controlled exercise trial. Associations were examined cross‐sectionally at baseline and longitudinally over the six‐month intervention period. The study also explored whether APOE genotype and exercise assignment moderated these associations.

Materials and Methods

Description of Parent Study and Participants

This study was a secondary analysis of data collected as part of the Exercise Program in Cancer and Cognition (EPICC) study (R01CA196762; NCT02793921), a 6‐month randomized controlled trial (RCT) investigating the effect of a moderate‐intensity aerobic exercise intervention on neurocognitive function in 153 postmenopausal women with BC (Bender et al. 2024). Eligibility criteria have been described in detail by Bender et al. (2024), and included postmenopausal (surgical or natural menopause) women less than 80 years of age with hormone receptor‐positive BC (ductal carcinoma in situ, Stage I–IIIa). All participants received endocrine therapy. Demographic and neurocognitive data were collected prior to randomization (baseline, T1) and within 2 weeks after completion of the 6‐month intervention (post‐intervention, T2). Participants were randomized to either a usual care control group or an intervention group balanced on age and baseline executive function (Bender et al. 2024). The intervention group completed 150 min per week of supervised moderate‐intensity aerobic exercise over 6 months (Gentry et al. 2018). The exercise regimen included a 5–10 min warm‐up, followed by 45–60 min of moderate‐intensity exercise (measured objectively using heart rate sensors every 15 min), followed by a 5–10 min cool down (Bender et al. 2024). Participants could choose their form of exercise, which could include treadmill, elliptical machine, stationary bicycles, or walking track. The control group was asked to maintain their usual activities for the duration of the 6‐month study but was not instructed to avoid exercise. The University of Pittsburgh Institutional Review Board approved this study. All participants provided informed consent prior to data collection.

APOE Genotype and DNA Methylation Data Collection

The DNA extracted from peripheral blood samples which were collected at T1 and T2 was stored. For the current study, APOE genotype data was collected using the stored DNA. APOE genotype was determined using real‐time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with TaqMan assays targeting the rs429358 and rs7412 single nucleotide polymorphisms. Because only a small number of participants were homozygous for the ε4 allele (ε4/ε4), genotypes were grouped as either APOE4 carriers (individuals with one or two ε4 alleles) or noncarriers.

The companion study of the EPICC trial (R01CA221882) obtained genome‐wide DNA methylation data from the banked DNA using the Illumina Infinium Methylation EPIC BeadChip array by the Center for Inherited Disease Research (CIDR) at Johns Hopkins University (Genetic Resources Core Facility, RRID:SCR_018669). Genome‐wide DNA methylation data underwent rigorous quality control and cleaning as detailed elsewhere (S. Liu et al. 2025). For the current study, only the baseline DNA methylation data were used. From these data, 13 CpG sites located within the APOE gene region (UCSC Genome Browser hg19/GRCh37 positions Chromosome 19:45409048–45,412,650) ± a 2 kb upstream (5′) and downstream (3′) were extracted for analysis. Baseline DNA methylation values were analyzed as M‐values, with positive values reflecting higher DNA methylation levels and negative values reflecting lower levels. The locations and Illumina annotation data for the APOE CpG sites are depicted in Table S1.

Cognitive Domain Testing

Comprehensive neurocognitive performance covering five domains (i.e., attention, executive function, memory, processing speed, and visuospatial ability), was assessed using the validated Cambridge Neuropsychological Test Automated Battery (CANTAB) Connect as part of neurocognitive battery testing (Cambridge Cognition 2023). Neurocognitive testing was completed at T1 and T2. An exploratory factor analysis with principal component analysis identified seven cognitive factors: Psychomotor Speed, Learning and Memory, Verbal Memory, Working Memory, Attention, Executive Function, and Mental Flexibility. Full details of the cognitive factor derivation are described in Bender et al. (2024).

Subjective cognition was evaluated with total score from the Patient Assessment of Own Functioning Inventory (Chelune et al. 1986), a validated instrument for assessing perceived cognitive difficulties. Unlike objective cognitive testing, this measure captures self‐reported cognitive function. This tool has been validated in postmenopausal women with BC (Bell et al. 2013). Total score ranges from 0 to 160, where a higher score means worse perceived cognitive function.

Analytical Cohorts

Of the 153 participants enrolled in the EPICC trial, 102 had available baseline DNA methylation data and cognitive testing scores, comprising the cross‐sectional analytic cohort (see Model 1a–1b). Of these, 85 also had complete 6‐month cognitive assessments and APOE4 genotype data and comprised the longitudinal analytic cohort (see Model 2a–2c). Reasons for exclusion from the longitudinal cohort included missing 6‐month cognitive data (n = 16) and missing APOE genotype (n = 1).

Statistical Analysis

Analyses were performed using R version 4.4.2. Descriptive statistics, including t‐tests or Wilcoxon Signed‐Rank tests as appropriate, were used to assess the relationships among variables, including comparisons between APOE4 carriers and noncarriers and between intervention groups. DNA methylation data were analyzed as M values throughout.

We conducted two sets of primary regression analyses and three sets of exploratory moderation analyses, organized around cross‐sectional (Aim 1) and longitudinal (Aim 2) research questions (see Data S1). All regression models were adjusted for age (years) and education level (years). Smoking status was not included as a covariate due to the small number of smokers (n = 2). Because APOE4 carrier status may modify, rather than simply confound, DNA methylation‐cognition associations, it was analyzed as a moderator. This decision also mitigated potential bias from the small and uneven distribution of carriers across groups. For the same reason, a three‐way DNA methylation × APOE4 × intervention group interaction was not tested.

Regression β coefficients, 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and p‐values were reported. To account for multiple comparisons, p‐values were adjusted using the Benjamini‐Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction, applied separately within each of the five analysis families (see models below). Nominal p‐values < 0.05 were considered statistically suggestive, with FDR‐adjusted p‐values reported alongside for transparency. Given the exploratory nature of the moderation analyses (Models 1b, 2b, and 2c), these results should be interpreted cautiously.

Cross‐Sectional

Examine the association between baseline APOE DNA methylation and baseline cognitive performance.

As detailed above, participants with both baseline DNA methylation and cognitive data were included (n = 102). Linear regression examined associations between site‐specific baseline DNA methylation (primary predictor) and baseline cognitive scores (outcome) for domains that were normally distributed (processing speed, learning and memory, verbal memory, working memory, attention, and executive function) while controlling for age and education level (Model 1a). For mental flexibility, which was not normally distributed, robust regression was used. In separate exploratory models (Model 1b), an interaction term was added to test whether APOE4 carrier status moderated the DNA methylation‐cognition association.

Baseline Cognitive Score=β0+β1CpGmethylation+β2age+β3years of education+ε

Exploratory Model 1b

Baseline Cognitive Score=β0+β1CpGmethylation+β2APOE4carriage+β3age+β4years of education+β5CpGmethylation×APOE4carriage+ε

Longitudinal

Examine the association between baseline APOE DNA methylation and cognitive performance over 6 months.

As detailed above, participants with baseline DNA methylation data and cognitive data at both baseline and 6 months were included (n = 85). First, change scores were calculated as 6‐month scores minus baseline values, with positive scores indicating improved cognition over 6 months and negative scores indicating cognitive decline over 6 months. Change scores (Δ) were modeled as a function of baseline DNA methylation using linear regression, adjusting for age, education, and intervention group (Model 2a). With only two timepoints, this approach is mathematically equivalent to a two‐level mixed‐effects model while remaining more interpretable. Intervention group was included as a covariate because both DNA methylation and cognitive change may be influenced by exercise, and because the goal was to identify biological correlates of cognitive change rather than to re‐test intervention efficacy.

In two sets of exploratory models interaction terms were added to test whether APOE4 carrier status (Model 2b) or intervention group (Model 2c) moderated the longitudinal DNA methylation‐cognition association.

As shown in Table 3, study variables did not differ between exercise and control groups with the exception of APOE4 carrier distribution, with 16 carriers in the exercise group and 5 in the control group. Subjective cognition was not statistically suggestive or significant in any models. Regression results are presented below by model type. Exploratory Model 1b: DNA Methylation*APOE ε4

In exploratory moderation analyses (Table S4), suggestive interactions were observed between baseline APOE DNA methylation and APOE4 carrier status on cognitive performance at seven CpG sites. Among ε4 carriers, higher DNA methylation was associated with poorer cognitive performance at four CpG sites: cg04406254 with executive function (β = −2.521, 95% CI = −4.626, −0.416, p = 0.019) and mental flexibility (β = −3.509, 95% CI = −5.495, −1.523, p = 0.0005, p‐adjusted = 0.007); cg05501958 with mental flexibility (β = −2.750, 95% CI = −5.172, −0.319, p = 0.027); cg18768621 with mental flexibility (β = −1.771, 95% CI = −3.195, −0.348, p = 0.015); and cg20051876 with attention (β = −2.115, 95% CI = −3.530, −0.700, p = 0.004). Only mental flexibility at cg04406254 was significant after multiple testing correction (p‐adjusted = 0.007).

In contrast, higher DNA methylation was associated with better cognitive performance among APOE4 carriers at three CpG sites: cg12049787 with mental flexibility (β = 0.992, 95% CI = (0.059, 0.476), p = 0.037); cg21879725 with learning and memory (β = 1.876, 95% CI = (0.398, 3.353), p = 0.013) and working memory (β = 1.677, 95% CI = (0.133, 3.221), p = 0.034); and cg26190885 with attention (β = 0.939, 95% CI = (0.013, 1.866), p = 0.047) and working memory (β = 1.341, 95% CI = (0.280, 2.402), p = 0.014).

Longitudinal

Model 2a

Longitudinal analyses included 85 participants who had baseline DNA methylation data and cognitive scores at both baseline and 6‐month follow‐up, regardless of intervention group assignment. Statistically suggestive results (p < 0.05) are presented in Table 5, with full results presented in Tables S5–S7. When modeling 6‐month change in cognitive performance as a function of baseline DNA methylation, controlling for age, education, and intervention group (Table S5), one CpG site, cg04406254, was associated with a decrease in attention (β = −0.420, 95% CI = −0.762, −0.078, p = 0.017). Notably, when the intervention group was not included as a covariate, six CpG sites were associated with changes in cognitive changes (data not shown). This suggests that some associations between DNA methylation and cognition may have been confounded by intervention group assignment, a possibility formally tested in the moderation analyses described below. No associations remained significant after FDR correction.

Exploratory Model 2b: DNA Methylation*APOE4

In exploratory moderation analyses (Table S6), suggestive interactions were observed between baseline DNA methylation and APOE4 carrier status in relation to changes in cognitive performance. Specifically, among APOE4 carriers, higher DNA methylation was associated with greater improvements in performance in cognitive factors compared to non‐carriers at four CpG sites: cg04406254 with executive function (β = 3.097, 95% CI = 0.574, 5.619, p = 0.017); cg08955609 with learning and memory (β = 0.634, 95% CI = 0.006, 1.262, p = 0.048); cg18799241 with attention (β = 1.259, 95% CI = 0.544, 1.974, p = 0.001); and cg26190885 with psychomotor speed (β = 0.779, 95% CI = 0.146, 1.412, p = 0.016) (Figure 2). In contrast, among APOE4 carriers, higher DNA methylation was associated with a greater decrease in cognitive performance at one CpG site compared to noncarriers: cg18768621 with psychomotor speed (β = −0.810, 95% CI = −1.613, −0.006, p = 0.048). No associations remained significant after FDR correction.

Discussion

This exploratory study demonstrates that APOE DNA methylation may be associated with cognitive function in postmenopausal women with early‐stage BC, with effects observed both cross‐sectionally and over 6 months (Figure 1). In general, these results signaled that higher DNA methylation was associated with poorer cognition, particularly in attention, and several of these associations differed by APOE4 carrier status. Our results suggest that exercise may buffer some of the adverse effects of elevated DNA methylation at specific sites, highlighting a potentially modifiable pathway for preserving cognitive function in this population.

Exercise has been shown to alter DNA methylation patterns through alterations in DNA methyltransferase activity (Etayo‐Urtasun et al. 2024). Previous studies demonstrate that both genome‐wide and gene‐specific DNA methylation (Etayo‐Urtasun et al. 2024) can be modified by exercise, with evidence from rodent models suggesting that exercise‐induced gene demethylation of the brain‐derived neurotropic factor (BDNF) gene is associated with better learning and memory (Cho et al. 2025; Gomez‐Pinilla et al. 2011). In the EPICC study, which investigated the role of 6 months of moderate‐intensity aerobic exercise (150/min per week), a similar pattern was observed, with exercise appearing to blunt cognitive decline, particularly in psychomotor efficiency (Bender et al. 2024). While these results are promising, the optimal type, intensity, duration, and frequency of exercise needed to maximize the benefits of exercise on genome‐wide and gene‐specific DNA methylation remain unclear.

Interestingly, APOE DNA methylation is also influenced by estrogen, which provides important context for interpreting these results in postmenopausal women with BC. Postmenopausal women typically have reduced estrogen levels compared to premenopausal women. Additionally, all women in this study received endocrine therapy, further lowering their estrogen. Estrogen is thought to be neuroprotective and antiatherogenic partly through its role in increasing APOE expression in the brain (Srivastava et al. 1997). Although the influence of estrogen replacement on APOE DNA methylation is poorly understood, it may alter DNA methylation patterns associated with cognition, with effects varying by APOE genotype (Panitch et al. 2024).

Hormone replacement therapy with estrogen has been found to reduce the risk of cognitive decline when initiated early in the menopause transition (Gleason et al. 2015). However, in our sample of postmenopausal women with hormone‐positive BC, estrogen replacement therapy is contraindicated due to the risk of BC recurrence. Instead, women with BC typically receive adjuvant endocrine therapy with anti‐estrogen properties for 5–10 years after initial BC treatment (Burstein et al. 2014). Lower estrogen levels contribute to the neurocognitive decline experienced by women with BC. However, DNA methylation‐guided precision health interventions, like exercise, represent an alternate approach to protect against cognitive decline in this BC population.

Additionally, DNA methylation in promoter regions can directly impact and reduce gene transcription. Therefore, increased DNA methylation in the APOE promoter region has the potential to reduce APOE production, which in turn affects lipid transport, neuronal repair, and amyloid‐beta accumulation throughout the brain. However, the impact may depend on which APOE isoform is suppressed. For example, the protective APOE ε2 isoform is protective against dementia (Liu et al. 2013), so suppression of this protein may not be preferable. In contrast, suppression of the APOE ε4 isoform, which impairs neuronal repair and promotes neuroinflammation and amyloid‐beta plaque deposition (Liu et al. 2013), may be beneficial.

Notably, all three CpG sites located in the APOE promoter region (cg14123992, cg04406254, cg20051876) were associated with cognitive performance. In general, APOE DNA methylation was associated with lower cognitive scores at baseline over 6 months, with some exceptions. Importantly, when APOE4 carrier status was included as an interaction term, higher baseline DNA methylation in APOE4 carriers was associated with improvements in specific cognitive domains. These findings support the notion that increased DNA methylation in APOE4 carriers may suppress production of the harmful APOE4 isoform, leading to cognitive improvement, or at least preventing decline.

Others have found that DNA methylation in the APOE promoter region has been associated with a 32% higher odds of dementia, independent of the APOE genotype (Karlsson et al. 2018). Similarly, Di Gerlando et al. (2024) found that individuals carrying the APOE4 allele have increased DNA methylation in region 1 of the APOE gene, which encompasses the promoter region in which these CpG sites are found.

Taken together, our findings support replicating this work with larger sample sizes and more sample diversity to explore the potential utility of using APOE DNA methylation sites as biomarkers for predicting cognitive decline, especially among APOE4 carriers. Unlike APOE genotype, which is fixed across the lifespan, DNA methylation is potentially reversible, offering a modifiable early indicator of cognitive decline.

Limitations and Strengths

Several limitations should be considered. First, the generalizability of these findings is potentially limited by the composition of the study sample, which consisted predominantly of White, postmenopausal women with BC. Therefore, these results may not apply to men, premenopausal women, individuals without BC, or more diverse populations. Second, because of the modest sample size, moderation analyses were exploratory. Although consistent patterns emerged across many sites, most associations did not remain statistically significant after multiple testing correction; therefore, replication in larger cohorts is needed for confirmation. Additionally, this study explored methylation only from a peripheral blood source, which may differ from tissue‐derived methylation and was not powered to examine longitudinal methylation change.

Despite these limitations, the study has several notable strengths. Specifically, this study leveraged data from a rigorously conducted RCT with 99.8% adherence, enabling examination of both cross‐sectional and longitudinal associations using a comprehensive, validated neurocognitive battery. Importantly, this is among the first studies to evaluate APOE DNA methylation in relation to cognition in women with BC. Furthermore, the exploration of exercise as a potential modifier provides novel insight into a modifiable pathway for supporting cognitive health. Finally, this study integrated DNA methylation and genotype data, highlighting the complementary role of both genetic and epigenetic factors on cognition.

Conclusion

APOE4 carriage is a well‐established non‐modifiable risk factor for cognitive decline, both with and without BC. This study provides new possible biological insights into the association between cognition and APOE DNA methylation, a potentially modifiable epigenetic mechanism. While these results need replication, these findings suggest that APOE DNA methylation may serve as a biomarker or intervention target to help slow cognitive decline, particularly in the context of an individual's APOE genotype. Importantly, the results also suggest that regular moderate‐intensity exercise may serve as a practical intervention to alter adverse APOE DNA methylation patterns and thereby support cognition health over time.
