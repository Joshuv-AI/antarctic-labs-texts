Intraspinal abnormalities in MRI-evaluated congenital scoliosis: a systematic review and meta-analysis of prevalence, geographic variation, vertebral anomaly patterns, and Cobb angle
Authors: Nizar Algarni, Khalid Alrasheed, Faisal Alkhunein, Mohammed Alorayyidh, Rahaf Alrasheed, Yousef Marwan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Intraspinal abnormalities affected 35% of congenital scoliosis patients.•Diastematomyelia was the most frequent intraspinal abnormality.•Failure of segmentation had the highest pooled abnormality prevalence.•Mixed vertebral anomalies were associated with higher abnormality odds.•Cobb angle was higher with abnormalities, but not significantly.

Introduction

Congenital scoliosis (CS) is a structural spinal deformity resulting from abnormal vertebral development during embryogenesis, accounting for a clinically significant subset of pediatric spinal deformities. It is estimated to occur in 0.5–1.0 per 1,000 live births and is commonly classified according to the underlying vertebral anomaly as failure of formation, failure of segmentation, or mixed defects [[1], [2], [3]]. Patients with CS frequently have associated abnormalities involving the intraspinal, genitourinary, cardiac, musculoskeletal, rib, gastrointestinal, and other organ systems [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. Given the close embryologic relationship between vertebral and neural development, intraspinal abnormalities represent a particularly important group of associated abnormalities in this population. Commonly detected abnormalities include diastematomyelia, syringomyelia, tethered cord, low-lying conus medullaris, Chiari malformation, fatty filum, and intraspinal masses [5,6]. Although some intraspinal abnormalities may be clinically silent, their recognition is important because certain lesions, such as split cord malformation and tethered cord, may require neurosurgical evaluation or influence the timing and planning of deformity correction [1,5]. However, not all MRI-detected abnormalities require intervention before scoliosis surgery, and management depends on the specific abnormality, neurological status, and clinical context [5]. Therefore, whole-spine magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is widely used in the diagnostic and preoperative evaluation of patients with CS, particularly before corrective surgery [5,7]. The present review is intended primarily to inform MRI evaluation and risk stratification in referral and preoperative settings, particularly before deformity surgery.

The reported prevalence of intraspinal abnormalities in CS varies substantially across studies, with prior studies reporting estimates from 15% to more than 50% [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. This variability likely reflects differences in cohort composition, including surgical versus nonsurgical populations, patient demographics, imaging practices, definitions of intraspinal abnormalities, geographic setting, and study design. A previous systematic review and meta-analysis by Wang et al. included 10 studies published up to 2019 and reported that 671 of 1,863 patients with CS who underwent MRI had intraspinal abnormalities, corresponding to a pooled estimate of 37%. In that review, diastematomyelia, syringomyelia, and tethered cord were the most frequently reported intraspinal abnormalities [6].

Since that prior review, additional studies have provided larger samples, broader geographic representation, more detailed reporting of vertebral anomaly patterns, and radiographic parameters such as Cobb angle [3,5,[11], [12], [13]]. Recent data suggest that intraspinal abnormalities may be more common in patients with failure of segmentation or mixed vertebral deformities than in those with failure of formation, and some cohorts have reported larger Cobb angles among patients with intraspinal abnormalities [3,10]. However, the overall relationship between intraspinal abnormalities, vertebral anomaly type, and curve severity remains uncertain across studies. Given that the overall prevalence of intraspinal abnormalities has already been estimated in a prior meta-analysis, an important remaining question is whether risk differs according to vertebral anomaly pattern and Cobb angle. Clarifying these differences may help identify higher-risk subgroups and refine MRI-based clinical risk stratification.

Therefore, an updated systematic review and meta-analysis is warranted. The present study aimed to estimate the pooled prevalence of intraspinal abnormalities in patients with CS and to evaluate associations with sex, geographic region, vertebral anomaly pattern, and Cobb angle.

Methods and materials

A systematic review and meta-analysis was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (registration number: CRD420251276010). This study adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) guidelines [14].

Data source and search strategy

A systematic search of MEDLINE via PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Cochrane was performed from inception until January 1, 2026. The search combined the following terms: (``MRI'' OR ``magnetic'' OR ``resonance'' OR ``imaging'') AND ``congenital'' AND ``scoliosis.'' Full search strategies for each database are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they enrolled patients with a confirmed diagnosis of CS. Eligible studies were required to assess specific intraspinal abnormalities using MRI and to provide sufficient data to calculate the prevalence of these abnormalities. Some included studies used routine or consecutive MRI evaluation, whereas others reported MRI-evaluated subsets. MRI indications were not consistently reported. Intraspinal abnormalities were defined as MRI-detected abnormalities of the spinal cord and associated neural-axis structures reported by the included studies, including diastematomyelia, syringomyelia, tethered cord, low-lying conus, Chiari malformation, fatty filum, intraspinal masses or tumors, spinal cysts, dural ectasia, spina bifida (excluding occulta), and other spinal cord abnormalities. Definitions and reporting criteria were based on those used in the original studies. Reported terminology was harmonized to these predefined diagnostic categories when terminology could be mapped unambiguously. Source-specific or ambiguous categories that could not be reassigned without reinterpretation were retained separately. Observational studies, including cross-sectional studies, prospective or retrospective cohort studies, and case-control studies, were considered eligible if they were published as English-language, peer-reviewed full-text articles. Studies were excluded if they focused on non-CS, including idiopathic, neuromuscular, or syndromic scoliosis, unless data for CS patients were reported separately. Case reports, small case series, reviews, editorials, conference abstracts, and animal or cadaveric studies were excluded.

Study selection and data extraction

After duplicate removal, 2 authors independently screened all titles and abstracts of retrieved records, followed by full-text review of potentially eligible reports. Disagreements were resolved by discussion or consultation with a third author. For overlapping reports, data were extracted from the most complete dataset to avoid double-counting. The 2 Lin et al. publications [11,12] were based on the same 1,289-patient surgical cohort and were therefore treated as a single study. Quantitative data used in the meta-analyses were extracted from Lin et al. [12], while Lin et al. [11] provided complementary information on vertebral anomaly location. The cohort was included only once in each pooled analysis. Data extraction was then performed independently by 2 authors using a standardized spreadsheet. Extracted variables included the first author's name, publication year, country, total CS patients sample size, number of CS patients with intraspinal abnormalities, age, sex, CS subtypes, specific intraspinal abnormality subtypes, and Cobb angle. When available, group-specific data were extracted for patients with and without intraspinal abnormalities, including CS subtype counts and Cobb angle means, standard deviations, or ranges. Geographic region and publication era were derived from country and publication year for subgroup analyses.

Methodological quality assessment

The methodological quality of included studies was assessed independently by 2 authors using the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS) [15]. Noncomparative studies were scored across 8 items, with a maximum score of 16, while comparative studies were scored across 12 items, with a maximum score of 24. Each item was scored on a scale of 0 to 2. For noncomparative studies, scores of ≤8, 9–14, and 15–16 were considered poor, moderate, and good quality, respectively. For comparative studies, scores of ≤14, 15–22, and 23–24 were considered poor, moderate, and good quality, respectively. Disagreements were resolved by consensus or consultation with a third author.

Statistical analysis

All meta-analyses were performed using R version 4.6.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with the meta package. Pooled prevalence estimates were calculated using random-effects models with logit-transformed proportions, inverse-variance weighting, and restricted maximum-likelihood estimation of between-study variance. Study-specific confidence intervals for proportions were calculated using the Clopper–Pearson exact binomial method, and pooled estimates were back-transformed to proportions for reporting. Results were reported as proportions with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and prediction intervals. Analyses were performed for the overall prevalence of intraspinal abnormalities, sex-specific prevalence, CS subtype-specific prevalence, and specific intraspinal abnormality subtypes. Subgroup analyses were conducted by geographic region, publication era, and sex for the overall prevalence of intraspinal abnormalities. Comparisons of CS subtypes between patients with and without intraspinal abnormalities were analyzed using random-effects Mantel–Haenszel odds ratios (ORs). Two-by-two tables were checked for zero-event cells. When zero-event cells were present, a continuity correction of 0.5 was applied. Cobb angle differences were analyzed using random-effects mean differences (MDs) when group-specific sample size, mean, and standard deviation were available. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test, I², and τ². Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the influence of individual studies, and funnel plots were used to assess small-study effects when sufficient studies were available. Statistical significance was defined as p < .05.

Methodological quality assessment

For the 16 noncomparative studies, the average MINORS score was 8.7 (range, 7–10), suggesting overall moderate quality, although several studies fell within the poor-quality range. The 2 comparative studies both had scores of 15, suggesting moderate quality (Table 2). The most common methodological limitations were retrospective data collection, absence of prospective sample-size calculation, incomplete reporting of loss to follow-up, and limited reporting of unbiased outcome assessment (Supplementary Table 2).

Overall prevalence of intraspinal abnormalities

The pooled prevalence of intraspinal abnormalities was 35% across 18 studies involving 4,463 patients (95% CI, 29%–40%). Heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 91.1%, p < .0001), with a prediction interval of 16%–60%. Leave-one-out analysis showed stable pooled estimates. Visual inspection of the funnel plot revealed no obvious asymmetry, although the assessment was limited (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Pooled prevalence of intraspinal abnormalities in congenital scoliosis patients across 18 studies comprising 4,463 patients. Sex-specific prevalence of intraspinal abnormalities

Among male patients, the pooled prevalence of intraspinal abnormalities was 31% (95% CI, 24%–39%). Heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 85.6%, p < .0001), with a wide prediction interval (12%–60%). Leave-one-out analysis showed stable pooled estimates, while funnel plot partial asymmetry suggested possible small-study effects (Fig. 4). Among female patients, the pooled prevalence of intraspinal abnormalities was 39% (95% CI, 32%–48%). Heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 87.7%, p < .0001), with a prediction interval of 16%–68%. Leave-one-out analysis showed stable estimates, while the funnel plot partial asymmetry suggested possible small-study effects (Fig. 5). In the sex subgroup analysis, the pooled prevalence was higher among female patients than male patients. Association between CS types and intraspinal abnormalities

All 3 vertebral-type comparisons were based on the same 10 studies comprising 3,713 patients. Discussion

This updated systematic review and meta-analysis included 18 studies comprising 4,463 patients with CS. The pooled prevalence of intraspinal abnormalities was 35%, consistent with the 37% estimate reported in the previous meta-analysis of 10 studies and 1,863 patients [6]. Beyond confirming the overall prevalence, the expanded evidence base allowed evaluation of geographic variation, vertebral anomaly patterns, and Cobb angle. Sex-specific prevalence was 31% among male patients and 39% among female patients, comparable to the rates reported by Wang et al. of 30% and 41%, respectively [6]. These findings further support the absence of a significant association between patient sex and the presence of intraspinal abnormalities in CS.

Furthermore, subgroup analysis by geographical region showed that the Middle East had the highest reported prevalence of intraspinal abnormalities among patients with CS, at 37%, followed by South Asia and East Asia at 34%, and North America at 33%. No statistically significant subgroup difference was identified, and the similar pooled estimates suggest that marked geographic variation was not evident among the populations represented. To our knowledge, no previous study has directly compared regional differences in the prevalence of intraspinal abnormalities. However, geographic coverage was incomplete, with only 3 North American studies and no included cohorts from Europe, Africa, Latin America, or Australasia, and regional estimates may also have been influenced by differences in referral patterns, MRI indications, and cohort composition. Thus, the findings suggest broadly similar prevalence among the populations represented, but broader geographic generalization remains uncertain.

This meta-analysis showed that, among the 1,474 patients with intraspinal abnormalities, diastematomyelia was reported in 53.5%, followed by syringomyelia in 40.4% and tethered cord in 29.9%. This finding contrasts with previous reports in the literature, which identified tethered cord as the most common intraspinal abnormality among patients with CS [7,17,18]. Differences in diagnostic terminology and reporting across studies should be considered when comparing the frequencies of specific abnormalities.

Regarding vertebral anomalies, the current study showed that failure of segmentation and mixed vertebral anomalies were associated with a higher prevalence of intraspinal abnormalities, at 47% and 40%, respectively, compared with failure of formation, which showed a lower prevalence of 26%. This finding is consistent with previous studies reporting a stronger relationship between segmentation defects, mixed vertebral anomalies, and underlying intraspinal abnormalities [3,10,18]. Therefore, patients with CS who present with failure of segmentation or mixed vertebral anomalies may represent a higher-risk subgroup for intraspinal abnormalities, although these associations do not establish that vertebral anomaly pattern is an independent predictor. From a clinical perspective, this finding supports the importance of MRI evaluation, particularly before surgical intervention [5,7]. However, the prevalence of intraspinal abnormalities among patients with failure of formation also remained clinically relevant. Thus, vertebral anomaly pattern may inform risk assessment but should not be used in isolation to determine the need for MRI.

Cobb angle was higher among patients with intraspinal abnormalities than among those without. However, this difference was not statistically significant, and heterogeneity was substantial. Some individual cohorts reported larger Cobb angles in patients with intraspinal abnormalities [3,13]. In contrast, the findings of the present review suggest that curve magnitude alone may not reliably distinguish patients with and without intraspinal abnormalities. Therefore, Cobb angle should be considered alongside vertebral anomaly pattern, neurologic findings, age, and surgical context rather than used as an isolated criterion for MRI evaluation. One possible explanation for this discrepancy is the heterogeneity among the included studies, including differences in patient age, sample size, and the proportion of surgically treated patients.

This study has several strengths. It updates the prior evidence base by including more studies and a larger pooled sample, while also evaluating vertebral anomaly types, geographic region, publication era, and Cobb angle, providing a broader and more clinically relevant analysis. In addition, random-effects models, prediction intervals, and sensitivity analyses were used to account for between-study variability and assess the robustness of the pooled estimates. However, this study also has limitations. Substantial heterogeneity remained despite subgroup analyses and may reflect differences in MRI protocols, age distributions, referral patterns, surgical versus nonsurgical cohort composition, single- versus multicenter study design, and definitions of CS and individual intraspinal abnormalities across studies. MRI indications were also not consistently reported, and selective imaging based on neurologic findings, planned surgery, deformity severity, or other clinical concerns cannot be excluded. These factors may have influenced the reported prevalence of intraspinal abnormalities and may limit generalizability to unselected patients with newly diagnosed CS. The vertebral anomaly comparisons were based on unadjusted study-level data and could not account for potential confounding by age, deformity characteristics, referral patterns, or MRI practices. Despite this heterogeneity, leave-one-out sensitivity analyses showed stable pooled estimates, suggesting that the overall result was not driven by a single study. Cobb angle analyses were limited by the small number of studies with complete group-specific data.

The included studies did not consistently distinguish symptomatic from incidentally detected intraspinal abnormalities, precluding separate analysis by symptom status. Future studies should report this distinction more systematically to better define the clinical relevance of MRI-detected abnormalities for surgical planning. Future studies with standardized methods and consistent reporting are needed to improve the reliability and comparability of findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this meta-analysis demonstrated that intraspinal abnormalities are relatively common in MRI-evaluated cohorts of patients with CS, with an overall pooled prevalence of 35%. Failure of segmentation and mixed vertebral anomalies were associated with a higher prevalence of intraspinal abnormalities in unadjusted study-level analyses, suggesting that these patients may represent a higher-risk subgroup warranting greater clinical suspicion, although vertebral anomaly pattern alone should not determine MRI use.
