No light, no problem: provision of light during simulated transport does not alter broiler chick performance or welfare outcomes
Authors: Deliah McGregor, Tory Shynkaruk, Carolin AB Adler, Trever G Crowe, Karen Schwean-Lardner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCommercial transport of newly hatched poultry species typically occurs under darkness. The impact of providing light during chick transportation has not been investigated and previous research suggests blue light may have a calming effect on broilers. This study investigated how chicks responded to simulated transportation under dark compared to white or blue light at two durations (4 or 7 h). Commercial chick transport baskets containing Ross 308 broiler chicks (n = 11,340) were placed onto separate vibration platforms located in 9 rooms (3 per light treatment), each with two vertical stacks of baskets (1 per duration treatment). Immediately after simulated transport, 5 chicks from each room were euthanized to assess blood parameters, gastrointestinal tract length and weights, and residual yolk sac weight. Remaining chicks were housed in floor pens under white light until 35 d. Performance data collected included body weight, feed intake, feed efficiency, and mortality. Welfare data included heterophil/lymphocyte (H/L) ratio, novel object test, and tonic immobility. The experimental design was a 3 × 2 factorial arrangement in a completely randomized block design consisting of 2 trials, with trial as block. Data were analyzed using Proc Mixed (SAS 9.4) and Tukey’s range test, with differences declared significant when P ≤ 0.05. Light treatment did not affect any performance or welfare parameters. Immediately post transport, chicks in the 7-h treatment had higher partial pressure of carbon dioxide (P = 0.03), sodium (P = 0.01), reduced plasma glucose (P = 0.01), shorter ileum (P = 0.05), and heavier cecum (P = 0.01). Throughout rearing, birds that underwent simulated transport for 7 h had reduced growth from 0 to 14 d (P < 0.01) and consumed less feed from 0 to 35 d (P = 0.01). At 32 d, birds exposed to simulated transport for 7 h had lower H/L ratios (P = 0.04). Mortality was unaffected by light and duration. The results suggest that changing the current industry practice of transporting chicks in darkness, compared to white or blue light, does not improve broiler performance or welfare. Introduction

In Canada, 1,058 million broiler hatching eggs were set in 2025 (Canadian Hatching Egg Producers, 2025), with most chicks then transported by road from hatcheries to farm. These journeys can range from a few to several hours (Mitchell and Kettlewell, 2009; NFACC, 2019) and are typically managed entirely by hatcheries following recommendations outlined by the National Farm Animal Care Council (NFACC, 2016). Transport is widely recognized as a stressful event for chicks due to factors such as thermal conditions (Mitchell and Kettlewell, 1998), feed and water restriction (Careghi et al., 2005; Willemsen et al., 2010), vibration, noise, and social disruption (Nicol and Scott, 1990; Mitchell and Kettlewell, 2009). Newly hatched chicks are often transported soon after hatching, which is a critical developmental period that can influence long-term welfare and performance (Decuypere et al., 2001; Valros et al., 2008; Mitchell, 2009).

Lighting, a key environmental factor in poultry management, may offer a potential strategy to alleviate transport-related stress. Birds possess a more complex visual system than humans, including four types of cones with color-differentiating oil droplets (Jones et al., 2007). These complexities enable birds to perceive shorter wavelengths, such as blue light, more vividly (Lewis and Morris, 2000; 2006), thereby necessitating that lighting programs be tailored to avian spectral sensitivity. In rearing barns, blue light has been shown to reduce stress and fearfulness (Mohamed et al., 2014; Remonato Franco et al., 2022b), possibly due to the limited ability of short wavelengths to penetrate the skull and activate the pineal gland and hypothalamic photoreceptors (Lewis and Morris, 2000; Baxter et al., 2014). Broilers raised under blue or green light demonstrate increased sitting and dozing (Praynito et al., 1997a,b), reduced fear and aggression (Mohamed et al., 2017; Khaliq et al., 2018), and enhanced comfort and feeding behaviors (Lucena et al., 2020). Additionally, some studies have suggested that shorter wavelengths may enhance growth and feed efficiency (Rozenboim et al., 1999; Cao et al., 2008) although these findings are inconsistent and often confounded by differences in light intensity measurements (lux, gallilux, W/m2). When intensity is corrected for avian spectral sensitivity using gallilux, no significant effects of wavelength on performance have been observed (Remonato Franco et al., 2022a). However, it is important to note that these studies focused on the effects of light during rearing, not during transportation from the hatchery, where chicks are yolk-sac reliant for nutrition.

The beneficial (calming) effects of blue light may be useful during transport to potentially offset detrimental effects of transport-related stressors. This may be crucial for longer journeys, as greater durations have been associated with reduced early growth, altered blood parameters, and increased mortality in broilers (Chou et al., 2004; Careghi et al., 2005; Bergoug et al., 2013). Elevated physiological stress can exacerbate depletion of energy reserves due to increased metabolic demand (EFSA, 2012; Bergoug et al., 2013). Although chicks rely on yolk sac reserves for energy during the first 72 h post hatch (Romanoff, 1960; Speake et al., 1998), the increase in metabolic demand combined with a 24 to 48-h spread of hatch (Decuypere et al., 2001) can affect chick growth (Tona et al., 2003). Blood parameters may also be affected by duration of transportation. Partial pressure of carbon dioxide may decrease due to panting as a result of thermal stress, leading to respiratory alkalosis (Sherwood et al., 2013; Borges et al., 2004). However, temperature-controlled trucks are utilized in Canada for chick transport, so this may not be an issue. Sodium levels can both rise or decline due to dehydration and defecation (Thomas et al., 2008; Vanderhasselt et al., 2013), while glucose may rise due to corticosterone-induced gluconeogenesis, then fall as reserves deplete (Zhang et al., 2009; Voslářová et al., 2011).

The heterophil/lymphocyte (H/L) ratio is a widely accepted hematological indicator of physiological stress in poultry. Heterophils are part of the innate immune system (Minias et al., 2018) and respond to stress by proliferating and altering morphology, while lymphocytes, responsible for adaptive immunity, typically decrease in number under stress conditions (Scanes, 2016; Sherwood et al., 2013). This shift in leukocytes results in an elevated H/L ratio, which reflects the bird’s physiological response to a chronic environmental stressor such as transportation (Gross and Siegel, 1983; Davis et al., 2018). Additionally, assessing the affective state of birds is important when considering welfare outcomes. Fearfulness in poultry can be evaluated using different methodologies, including the tonic immobility test and the novel object test. Tonic immobility is characterized by a temporary state of motor inhibition and is triggered by physical restraint and simulates a predator attack (Jones and Faure, 1981; Forkman et al., 2007). Once a tonic state is induced, the time until the bird rights itself is recorded, with longer durations indicating higher fearfulness (Sinkalu et al., 2016). The novel object test assesses neophobia by determining latency to peck unfamiliar objects (Forkman et al., 2007). Unlike the tonic immobility test, the novel object test is unaffected by handling and can be performed in the bird's home environment, making it minimally invasive and a practical tool for assessing fear (Cockrem, 2007). Longer latencies indicate higher fearfulness, although responses may diminish with age due to habituation (Hocking et al., 2001).

To the authors’ knowledge, no published data are available on the effects of provision of light during chick transportation. Therefore, the objective of this study was to investigate how performance and welfare outcomes were impacted by simulated transportation under white or blue light as compared to the industry standard of transportation under dark conditions.

Materials and methods

The procedures for this experiment were approved by the University of Saskatchewan Animal Care Committee (20230082), and all animals were cared for as specified in the Guide to the Care and Use of Experimental Animals by the Canadian Council of Animal (2017).

Experimental design

This experiment studied the effect of 3 light treatments (blue, white, or dark) during simulated transport for 2 durations (4 or 7 h) over two blocked trials by assessing impacts on performance, health, physiology, stress, and fear of broilers. To achieve this, platforms were designed to mimic conditions on actual road transport trucks. Nine separate platforms were located at the rear end of 9 individual rooms behind a blackout curtain. Within this area, light was provided via a blue or white LED lamp (AgriLamp, 11 W ES16/27, Greengage Lighting Ltd., Edinburgh, UK) or no lamp (dark) as a control. Light intensity was equalized to 4.6 gallilux between the white and blue treatments and was 0 gallilux for the dark treatment. For the blue light treatment, the peak wavelength was at 456 nanometers. The vibration platforms were built from a plywood base that was supported on six tennis balls that allowed the base to move and vibrate (Fig. 1). In the center of each platform, a 5″ random orbital palm sander was attached and connected to an autotransformer to adjust the voltage provided, allowing the frequency of vibration to be controlled. Fans (Air Circulating Box Fan, Lasko, West Chester, PA, USA) were provided to maintain adequate ventilation in the simulated transport testing area.

Simulated transportation platform apparatus.

Within each trial, 5,670 (N = 11,340) Ross 308 as-hatched broiler chicks were obtained from a commercial hatchery located 25 km from the University of Saskatchewan. Chicks were put on a commercial transportation truck after processing and delivered immediately to the research facility. Upon arrival, chick quality was assessed and birds were placed into commercial transport baskets in vertical stacks of 6 baskets (Fig. 2). In each stack, 4 baskets contained chicks with a space allowance of 26 cm2/bird. These baskets were placed in 2 stacks on each platform (1 stack/duration treatment). The other 2 baskets in the stacks remained empty, with 1 attached to the platform and 1 located on top of the stack. Data from an accelerometer, fastened to the middle basket of the left stack, were used to calculate vibration frequency and the transformers and sanders were adjusted as needed to equalize the vibration frequency across stacks. A frequency of 20 Hz and an average amplitude of displacement of 0.2 mm were achieved. After 4 h of simulated transportation, the corresponding stack of baskets was removed, and a counterweight of equal mass was placed on the platform to ensure weight distribution was consistent for the 7-h treatment.

Vertical stacks of commercial chick transport baskets under blue light treatment on simulated transportation platform. The top and bottom baskets were empty, with the middle 4 stacks containing chicks with a space allowance of 26 cm2/bird.

Birds and housing

Following the removal of stacks from the platforms, chicks were group weighed and randomly assigned to 10 floor pens (2 × 2.3 m) within each of the 9 rooms and were reared to 35 d. Half of the pens in each room housed chicks from the 4-h treatment (n = 5/room) and the other half housed chicks from the 7-h treatment. From each stack, 5 chicks were randomly selected for post-transport sampling, while the remaining 63 chicks were housed (estimated final stocking density of 31 kg/m2). Pens were bedded with wood shavings to a depth of 7.5 to 10 cm. Water and commercial feeds were provided ad libitum; starter ration from 0 to 14 d (fine crumble), grower ration from 15 to 25 d (coarse crumble), and finisher ration from 25 to 35 d (coarse crumble).

Lighting treatments were only provided during the simulated transport period. During rearing all broilers were raised under white light (AgriLamp, 11 W ES16/27, Greengage Lighting Ltd., Edinburgh, UK). Light intensity was set at 40 lux from 0 to 6 d and 10 lux from 7 to 35 d. Photoperiod was 22L:2D at placement and decreased to 18L:6D by d 4, with a 15 min dawn-to-dusk program.

Data collection

Physiological Measures. After simulated transport, 5 birds per stack (n = 90) were sampled for blood parameters, residual yolk sac weight, and gastrointestinal tract measurements. Approximately 1.0 mL of blood was collected from the jugular vein into a lithium heparin blood tube using a 25-gauge 1″ needle. The blood sample was transferred into a CG8+ i-STAT cartridge (Abbott, Chicago, IL, USA) and inserted into a blood analyzer (i-STAT Alinity, Abbot, Chicago, IL, USA). Each blood sample was analyzed for pH, partial pressure of carbon dioxide (pCO2), partial pressure of oxygen (pO2), sodium, glucose, hematocrit, and hemoglobin. Following blood collection, chicks were euthanized via cervical dislocation and yolk sac weights and gastrointestinal tract (duodenum, jejunum, ileum, cecum) lengths and weights were recorded.

Performance measures. Two pens/duration/room were group-weighed daily from 2 to 7 d (chicks were not weighed on d 1 to allow them to habituate to their new environment). Feed intake was measured by weighing feeders daily from 1 to 7 d in the same pens. At 14 and 35 d all birds on trial were weighed on a pen basis. Feeder weights were also recorded, and these data were used to determine feed intake, feed efficiency, and growth performance. Twice daily health checks were performed to identify any culls or mortalities, which were sent for necropsy (Prairie Diagnostic Services, Western College of Veterinary Medicine, University of Saskatchewan) to diagnose cause of death/culling by a pathologist.

Novel Object Test. A novel object test was performed daily from 1 to 7 d, then at 16, 23, and 30 d. Seven distinct novel objects (Fig. 3) were used during the first week, with 1 object randomly selected each day (3 of the 7 objects used during the first week were also used at 16, 23, and 30 d). The latency for 3 individuals to peck the object was recorded, with a maximum latency of 600 s.

The 7 distinct novel objects used during the novel object tests.

Tonic Immobility. Tonic immobility was tested on 2 randomly selected birds at 1, 7, and 31 d from each duration per room. Each bird was placed on its back and manually restrained for 15 s in a U-shaped cradle. If the bird was successfully induced, latency to rise was recorded with a maximum duration of 600 s. If induction was unsuccessful (the bird righted itself within 10 s of restraint being removed), the procedure was attempted again. Only three induction attempts were permitted per bird after which the bird was returned to its home pen and another bird was selected to replace it.

Heterophil/Lymphocyte Ratio. Blood samples were collected from 2 birds/pen at 8 and 32 d. A 1 mL sample was collected from the branchial vein into an EDTA tube using a 25-gauge 1″ needle and was used to make duplicate blood smears. Once dry, the prepared microscope slides were stained using PROTOCOLTM Hema 3TM (Fisher Scientific, Ottawa, Canada). A light microscope (Microscope B-290 TB, Optika©, Bergamo, Italy) was used to read slides under 100x field of view with oil magnification to determine the H/L ratio. The number of heterophils and lymphocytes in the field of view were recorded until a count of 100 was reached.

Statistical analysis

The statistical design of this study was a nested randomized complete block design, with light nested within room. The study consisted of two trials which served as the blocking variable. Room served as a replicate unit for light treatment (n = 6/treatment) and stack was the replicate unit for duration treatment (n = 18/treatment) during simulated transport, whereas pen was the replicate unit for duration treatment (n = 30) during rearing. Data were tested for normality assumptions using Proc Univariate (SAS® 9.4, Cary, NC, USA), and if those were not met, data were (log +1) transformed prior to analyses. If block was significant, it was included in the random statement. Proc Mixed was used to analyze differences among group means and Tukey’s test was used to separate the means. Differences were considered significant when P ≤ 0.05.

Results and discussion

As expected, growth was unaffected by providing either blue or white light during simulated transport of broiler chicks. Birds were only exposed to the lighting treatments for 4 or 7 h, which was unlikely to be long enough to impact growth. However, duration of simulated transport did influence BW and BW gain during early life. Broilers were heavier up to 14 d and grew faster from 0 to 14 d after 4 h of simulated transport compared to 7 h (Table 1). While significant, the differences in weight were small (1-6 g) and no difference was identified at 35 d. Previous research also found longer transport duration can impair growth in early life (Baião et al., 1998; Bergoug et al., 2013). Bergoug et al. (2013) reported transportation durations of 4 or 10 h negatively affected broiler BW until 21 d compared to birds that were not transported. Although chicks can utilize nutrients from the yolk sac for up to 72 h (Romanoff, 1960; Speake et al., 1998), delayed access to feed can result in weight loss during holding before transport and depressed growth rate after access to feed following transport (Carehgi et al., 2005). In the current study, the longer duration of simulated transport combined with an unknown spread of hatch could have led to depressed early growth. There were no interactions noted between light treatment and duration, suggesting that providing light during simulated transport did not mitigate the negative effects of the longer duration.

Provision of light during stimulated transport did not affect feed intake or feed efficiency throughout the 35-d rearing period (Table 2). Chicks that underwent simulated transported for 7 h consumed less feed from 0 to 35d (P = 0.01) but had better feed efficiency compared to the group exposed for 4 h. In comparison, Bergoug et al. (2013) tested transport durations of 0, 4, or 10 h on broiler chicks and reported no effect on feed intake or efficiency in 2 experiments, however in a third, birds subjected to 10 h of transport consumed more feed at 21, 28, and 35 d and had poorer FCR at 21 d. Feed restriction, occurring from time of hatch to placement, results in shorter and lighter intestinal segments, reduced villus height, and decreased crypt depth, all of which impair nutrient absorption and feed efficiency (Jong et al., 2017; Jha et al., 2019). Therefore, it would be expected that chicks exposed to the 7-h duration of simulated transport would have poorer feed efficiency compared to the shorter duration of 4 h, in line with Bergoug et al. (2013). However, it was observed that chicks exposed to simulated transport for 4 h consumed more feed throughout the trial and had a faster growth rate until 14 d but were less efficient over the whole rearing period. The birds exposed to simulated transport for 7 h may have had delayed growth initially, but compensatory mechanisms allowed them to attain BW comparable to birds exposed for 4 h without excessive feed intake.

Mortality/culls and their diagnoses were unaffected by either light or duration (data not shown), which suggested that short-term exposure to light (4-7 h) during simulated transport had no benefit on bird survival. Chou et al. (2004) reported that increasing transport distance (from 50 to 300 km) was correlated with higher mortality in broiler chicks, although the increase was negligible (1.2 to 1.4 %).

Transport stress in poultry can elicit physiological changes detectable through blood parameters. Activation of the sympathetic-adrenal-medullary and hypothalamic-pituitary adrenal axes during stress leads to increased respiration, cardiovascular activity, and metabolic shifts, including gluconeogenesis and altered glucose uptake (Siegel, 1980; Zhang et al., 2009; Sherwood et al., 2013). These responses can result in changes to blood gas concentrations and the acid-base balance (Borges et al., 2004; Sherwood et al., 2013), electrolytes (Thomas et al., 2008), metabolites (Zhang et al., 2009; Sherwood et al., 2013), and hematological variables (Thomas et al., 2008; Bergoug et al., 2013). However, physiological stress indicators for newly hatched chicks are not well established, as compared to more mature birds (Khosravinia, 2015). None of the blood parameters assessed in this study were influenced by providing light during simulated transport (Table 3), which suggests that the brief exposure to white or blue light for 4 or 7 h did not elicit physiological changes detectable in blood chemistry. Chicks under simulated transported for 4 h had lower pCO2 (P = 0.03) and sodium concentration (P = 0.01), and a higher plasma glucose level (P = 0.01) compared to chicks in the 7-h treatment (Table 3). The reduced pCO2, indicative of hypocapnia, could have been caused by hyperventilation due to stress in the shorter duration group, whereas chicks in the longer duration treatment would have had more time to adapt to the transport environment (Broom and Johnson, 2019). The elevated glucose values in the 4-h group may also support this explanation as they may have resulted from acute hypothalamic-pituitary adrenal axis activation due to stress, causing corticosterone-induced glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis, where glucose is mobilized from fat stores and protein stores (Zhang et al., 2009; Sherwood et al., 2013). Zhao et al. (2022) reported increased glucose serum concentrations in layer chicks transported for 4 and 8 h compared to 2 h and the abundance of metabolic enzymes in transported chicks suggested that transport resulted in higher gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis and a higher energy demand. Glucose is then circulated in the blood and utilized by chicks to cope with stressors. The elevated sodium concentration observed in the 7-h group may suggest partial mitigation of electrolyte imbalance through yolk sac utilization, thereby aiding in restoration of homeostasis (Khosravinia, 2015). This aligns with previous findings by Khosravinia (2015), who reported higher sodium in broiler chicks transported for 1,000 km compared to 200 km. Increased sodium concentrations can also be caused by dehydration (Vanderhasselt et al., 2013). However, the hematocrit test, another indicator of dehydration (González et al., 2007; Vanderhasselt et al., 2013), was unaffected as were hemoglobin levels. Bergoug et al. (2013), also reported no differences in hematocrit values in broiler chicks after 4 or 10 h of transportation.

Gastrointestinal tract development and yolk sac utilization can be used as indicators of early-life health, growth potential, and welfare in broiler chicks (Jha et al., 2019; Wagt et al., 2020). The yolk sac provides essential nutrients and energy immediately post-hatch, supporting metabolic demands and organ development within the first 72 h after hatch prior to feed access (Romanoff, 1960; Speake et al., 1998). Efficient yolk sac absorption is associated with improved health and early growth (Wagt et al., 2020), while poor utilization may indicate physiological stress or delayed development (Vasconcelos et al., 2024). Similarly, gastrointestinal tract development, assessed via the length and weight of intestinal segments, is related to nutrient absorption and growth performance (Jha et al., 2019). Development of the chick gastrointestinal tract is a complex process that begins during late embryogenesis and continues post-hatch (Uni et al., 1995; Noy and Sklan, 1999; Jha et al., 2019) and typically relies on fat from the yolk sac (Noy and Sklan, 1999). Stressors within transport, such as feed deprivation, can impair gastrointestinal tract development, compromising long-term productivity (Barri, 2008; Jong et al., 2017).

Light treatment had no effect on residual yolk sac weight or the length and weight of the duodenum, jejunum, ileum, or cecum (Table 4). This suggests that the brief exposure of 4 or 7 h to the light treatments was not sufficient to enhance yolk sac utilization or gut development. Duration also had no effect on residual yolk sac weight, suggesting the 3 h difference between duration treatments did not influence utilization. It was reported that extended transport over 800-1000 km reduced yolk sac weight (Khosravinia, 2015). The reduction in residual yolk sac weight could have been due to increased energy demand due to rapid metabolism related to transport-related stressors (Khosravinia, 2015). Differences in duration likely explain the conflicting results, as the previous study transported chicks up to 20 h. Chicks from the 4-h group had longer ileum (P = 0.05) and lighter cecum (P = 0.01) immediately post-transport compared to chicks exposed to simulated transport for 7 h. This could reflect a larger absorptive surface area (Jha et al., 2019), leading to the increased feed intake and growth observed in the current study. The heavier cecum observed in the 7-h group may be due to the extended period allowing for additional development.

Exposure to light during simulated transport on day of hatch did not affect H/L ratios at 8 or 32 d of age (data not shown). Previous research by Mohamed et al. (2014) and Remonato Franco et al. (2022b) found that broilers reared under blue light exhibited lower H/L ratios than those under white light, suggesting reduced physiological stress. However, in those studies, birds were exposed to monochromatic lighting throughout rearing, whereas in the present study, exposure was limited to 4 or 7 h. The lack of effect may indicate that short-term exposure to blue light is insufficient to elicit measurable changes in white blood cell counts. Duration of simulated transport had no effect on H/L ratios at 8 d; however, at 32 d, birds in the 4-h group had higher H/L ratios than those in the 7-h treatment (0.53 vs. 0.49; P = 0.04). This was unexpected, as longer exposure to transport stressors was hypothesized to increase physiological stress. One possible explanation is that birds in the 7-h group had more time to adapt to the transport environment, reducing the impact of subsequent stressors such as handling and placement (Broom and Johnson, 2019). Wicaksono et al. (2020) similarly found that a longer transport duration of 180 min compared to 30 min resulted in lower H/L ratios, suggesting adaptation may play a role.

Neither tonic immobility nor the response to a novel object were affected by either light or duration in the present study (Table 5). This suggests that providing light during simulated transport did not influence the birds fear response. In previous research, blue light reduced fear responses, suggesting a positive effect on affective state, compared to white or green light (Mohamed et al., 2014; Remonato Franco et al., 2022b). The disparity between the previous and current work may indicate the short exposure period in the current study was insufficient to elicit long-term changes in fearfulness. The lack of effect observed between the duration treatments suggests the difference between 4 and 7 h of simulated transport was not enough to influence fearfulness. It is possible that birds may have habituated to the vibration and environmental conditions within the simulated transport, which were consistent and lacked the variability of actual transport vehicles.

The results of this study demonstrate that implementing blue or white light, compared to the industry standard of dark, during simulated transport for 4 or 7 h does not improve the welfare or performance of broiler chicks. Light treatments had no measurable effect on growth, stress physiology, gut development, yolk sac utilization, or fear responses. This lack of effect supports the continued practice of transporting newly hatched chicks under darkness from hatchery to farm. Findings from this study also highlighted the importance of transport duration in shaping early-life physiology and performance. Future research into practical solutions to optimize chick physiology, welfare, and growth between hatching and arrival on farm would be beneficial.
