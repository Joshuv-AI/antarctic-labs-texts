Yi-Shen-Hua-Shi granule added to supportive care reduces proteinuria in IgA nephropathy: a multicenter randomized controlled trial
Authors: Yufei Chen, Jingyi Tang, Xueling Li, Xianwen Zhang, Fan Zhang, Rong Zheng, Lianli Shen, Shu Rong, Jingyuan Xie, Qin Wang, Yifei Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIgA nephropathy is the most common primary glomerulonephritis globally. Current management relies on supportive care, but many patients remain at risk for progressive disease, especially those with persistent proteinuria. This study evaluated the efficacy of Yi-Shen-Hua-Shi granule in adult patients with IgA nephropathy and persistent proteinuria. We conducted a multicenter, randomized, controlled trial involving adults with biopsy-confirmed IgA nephropathy, estimated glomerular filtration rate ≥30 mL/min/1.73 m2, and persistent proteinuria despite optimized supportive therapy. Participants were randomized 1:1 to receive Yi-Shen-Hua-Shi granule (10 g, orally, twice daily) plus best supportive care, or best supportive care alone, for 8 weeks. The primary endpoint was change in 24-hour urinary protein from baseline to week 8. Secondary endpoints included changes in renal function and safety assessments. Data were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models according to the intention-to-treat principle. A total of 97 patients were included. At 8 weeks, the Yi-Shen-Hua-Shi group showed a significantly greater reduction in 24-hour proteinuria compared with controls, with an estimated mean difference of ≈340 mg (95% CI 89.59 to 588.62; p = 0.01). The benefit was most pronounced among participants with baseline proteinuria >1000 mg, whose reduction was approximately 505 mg greater than controls (95% CI 152.45 to 859.42; p = 0.01). No significant differences in estimated glomerular filtration rate or adverse event rates were observed between groups. Yi-Shen-Hua-Shi granule, added to best supportive care, safely produced a greater short-term reduction in proteinuria, particularly in patients with high baseline proteinuria.Keywords: IgA nephropathy, proteinuria, randomized controlled trial, traditional Chinese medicine, chronic kidney disease, immunomodulation

Introduction

IgA nephropathy is a leading cause of chronic kidney disease (CKD) and end-stage kidney disease in Asia and many Western countries [1–3]. Despite advances in the understanding of IgA nephropathy pathogenesis, optimal management remains challenging. The Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes guidelines recommend supportive therapy centered on blood pressure control and renin-angiotensin system (RAS) inhibition as the mainstay of treatment to delay CKD progression and reduce proteinuria [4]. However, even with maximal supportive therapy, a substantial proportion of patients continue to experience persistent proteinuria and progressive renal function decline, highlighting the need for additional treatment strategies [5].

The use of corticosteroids and other immunosuppressive agents has been explored in numerous trials, with conflicting results regarding efficacy and substantial concerns about adverse events. While some studies demonstrated reductions in proteinuria and attenuation of renal deterioration with steroid therapy, these benefits were often offset by an increased risk of infections and other complications, especially in populations with moderate or advanced CKD [6–8]. Recently, novel therapies targeting the multi-hit pathogenesis of IgA nephropathy-such as endothelin receptor antagonists, budesonide formulations, and complement inhibitors-have shown promise, but longer-term outcome data are still lacking and accessibility may be limited in some settings [9–12].

In this context, traditional Chinese medicine has received growing attention as a potential adjunct or alternative for the management of CKD, including IgA nephropathy. TCM approaches are widely used for kidney diseases in East Asia, and several herbal formulations have demonstrated anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, and renoprotective effects in both preclinical and clinical studies [13,14]. Among these, the Yi-Shen-Hua-Shi granule is a compound herbal preparation derived from well-established Chinese medicinal principles, focused on strengthening the kidney and eliminating dampness (“yi shen hua shi”), a pathophysiological concept in traditional Chinese medicine closely associated with proteinuric kidney diseases [15,16]. Experimental studies have suggested that the components of Yi-Shen-Hua-Shi granule, including Astragalus membranaceus and Poria cocos, may reduce proteinuria and glomerular injury through modulation of immune and inflammatory pathways, as well as preservation of podocyte structure and function [17–20].

Preliminary clinical evidence from small-scale trials indicates that Yi-Shen-Hua-Shi granule may reduce proteinuria and slow renal function decline in patients with IgA nephropathy and other glomerular diseases, with a favorable safety profile [21–23]. However, existing studies are limited by methodological weaknesses, small sample sizes, and a lack of appropriate controls, precluding definitive conclusions regarding the efficacy of Yi-Shen-Hua-Shi granule for IgA nephropathy in routine clinical practice.

Accordingly, we conducted a multicenter, prospective, randomized controlled trial to evaluate the efficacy of Yi-Shen-Hua-Shi granule in adult patients with IgA nephropathy and persistent proteinuria, compared with routine best supportive care alone.

Methods

The present study adheres to the 2025 Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines (Table S1) [24].

Study design and participants

This study was designed as a multicenter, randomized, controlled clinical trial conducted at four tertiary nephrology centers in Shanghai, China, between December 2022 and March 2024. Ethical approval was granted by the Medical Ethics Committee of Longhua Hospital Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Approval No. 2022LCSY089). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. The trial was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2200065760). This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Eligible participants were adults aged 18–65 years with biopsy-proven IgA nephropathy within the preceding 2 years, an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) ≥30 mL/min/1.73 m2, and 24-h urinary protein excretion continued existence despite at least three months of optimized supportive care, including stable doses of RAS inhibitors. Key exclusion criteria included secondary IgA nephropathy (e.g., associated with liver disease, systemic autoimmune disorders), rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis, crescent formation in >50% of glomeruli, eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m2, poorly controlled hypertension (>150/100 mmHg), diabetes mellitus, pregnancy or breastfeeding, history of glucocorticoid or immunosuppressive therapy within six months, or any severe infection or malignancy within the last year. Patients with known allergy to ingredients of Yi-Shen-Hua-Shi granule or poor adherence potential were also excluded.

Randomization

After baseline assessment, eligible participants were randomly assigned (1:1) in a parallel-group fashion to receive either the Yi-Shen-Hua-Shi granule plus ongoing best supportive care (intervention group) or best supportive care alone (control group) for 8 weeks. Randomization was performed centrally using a computer-generated sequence.

Due to the visible characteristics of the herbal granules and distinct taste, participant blinding was not feasible. However, all primary and secondary outcomes were objective laboratory-based measures, which are less prone to expectation bias. Moreover, outcome assessors and statisticians remained blinded to group allocation to minimize subjective influence and analytic bias.

Interventions

The intervention group received Yi-Shen-Hua-Shi granule (manufactured by Guangzhou Consun Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., batch-certified by the State Food and Drug Administration) at a dose of 10 g, orally, twice daily after meals for 8 weeks (Table S2 lists the components of Yi-Shen-Hua-Shi granule and its corresponding effects). The granule is formulated from standardized herbal ingredients including Astragalus membranaceus, Poria cocos, Atractylodes macrocephala, and others, in accordance with published consensus and regulatory standards [16]. Compliance was assessed by granule packet count at each visit. Both groups received background best supportive care, including strict blood pressure control (<130/80 mmHg), dietary salt restriction (<6 g/d), lipid management, and stable use of RAS inhibitors or other antihypertensive agents as appropriate. Initiation of sodium-glucose linked transporter-2 inhibitors, targeted-release budesonide, or sparsentan was not permitted during the intervention period.

Assessments and endpoints

Participants were evaluated at baseline, 4 weeks, and 8 weeks. At each visit, clinical assessment, laboratory testing (including serum creatinine, eGFR calculated by the CKD-EPI equation, 24-h urinary protein, uric acid, and blood urea nitrogen), and adverse event monitoring were conducted.

The primary efficacy endpoint was change in 24-h urinary protein excretion from baseline to week 8. Secondary endpoints included changes in eGFR, serum creatinine, uric acid, blood urea nitrogen, and the occurrence of any adverse events. Safety assessment included monitoring of laboratory parameters, clinical symptoms, and adverse events, which were classified and graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0 [25].

Sample size

Assuming a difference in mean reduction of 24-h urinary protein excretion of 0.4 g/d (standard deviation 0.7 g) between groups, with two-sided alpha of 0.05 and 80% power, a minimum of 57 participants per group was required. Allowing for up to 15% attrition, the target enrollment was set at 114 patients.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 17 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA) and R version 4.2.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-tailed P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Continuous variables are reported as mean (standard deviation) or median (interquartile range) as appropriate, and categorical variables are summarized as counts and percentages. Differences between groups were assessed using the Welch Two Sample t-test or Wilcoxon rank sum test for continuous variables and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

The primary and secondary efficacy endpoints were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models, which included fixed effects for group, time, and their interaction, with a random intercept for each participant to account for within-subject correlation across repeated measures. In addition to absolute changes, relative percentage changes from baseline in 24-h urinary protein were calculated, and the proportion of patients achieving a ≥ 50 % reduction was analyzed using chi-square tests. Age and sex were included as basic covariates in the primary model. The linear mixed-effects models inherently accommodate missing data under the assumption of missing at random [26]. Thus, cases with occasional missing outcome measures contributed their available data to the model. Prespecified subgroup analyses were performed according to baseline proteinuria category (<500 mg/d vs. [500–1000] mg/d vs. >1000 mg/d).

To examine the robustness of our results, we conducted several sensitivity analyses. First, we performed multiple imputations for missing data, generating 10 multiply imputed datasets using the chained equations method; each dataset was analyzed with the same linear mixed-effects model, and results were combined according to Rubin’s rules. Second, given the positive skewness commonly observed in proteinuria, an additional analysis was performed using log-transformed 24-h urinary protein values. Third, a mixed model for repeated measures with an exchangeable within-subject covariance structure was applied to account for correlations among repeated visits. This model constrained baseline means to be equal between groups, consistent with randomization. Finally, we included interactions of age and sex with visit to verify that the treatment effect was not confounded by these demographic factors.

Results

Among the 97 participants, the mean age was 42.0 ± 9.7 years overall, with Yi-Shen-Hua-Shi granule group (43.0 ± 10.2) and the control group (41.0 ± 9.2) showing comparable distributions (p = 0.33). Within-group longitudinal change

In the Yi-Shen-Hua-Shi granule group, 24-h urinary protein excretion decreased from baseline over time (Table 2). At week 4, the mean reduction was −198.99 mg/d (95% CI −406.18 to 8.20; p = 0.06). By week 8, a significant further reduction was observed (−356.79 mg/d; 95% CI, −528.60 to −184.96; p < 0.01). In contrast, the control group showed no significant change in proteinuria at either week 4 (98.79 mg/d; 95% CI −47.66 to 245.23; p = 0.18) or week 8 (−54.47 mg/d; 95% CI −171.36 to 62.42; p = 0.35). The mean relative reduction in 24-h urinary protein from baseline to week 8 was 19.46 % (95 % CI 4.13 to 34.79) in the Yi-Shen-Hua-Shi granule group and -13.30 % (95 % CI -35.83 to 9.24) in the control group (p = 0.02). A ≥ 50 % reduction in proteinuria was achieved in 13 (26.53 %) and 7 (14.58 %) participants, respectively (χ2 = 2.11, p = 0.15). Although the difference in responder rate did not reach statistical significance, the numerical trend favored the Yi-Shen-Hua-Shi granule group.

No significant within-group changes in eGFR were observed in the Yi-Shen-Hua-Shi group at week 4 (−0.48 mL/min/1.73 m2; 95% CI −2.60 to 1.65; p = 0.66) or week 8 (0.22 mL/min/1.73 m2; 95% CI −2.82 to 3.27; p = 0.88). For serum creatinine, neither group exhibited significant changes from baseline at either time point. Similarly, blood urea nitrogen and uric acid remained stable throughout the study in both groups, with no significant within-group differences detected.

Between-group differences in longitudinal change

Mixed-effects linear regression revealed a significant interaction between treatment group and time on 24-h urinary protein excretion (Table 3). At baseline, there was no significant difference between the Yi-Shen-Hua-Shi granule group and the control group (p = 0.06). However, compared to the control group, the Yi-Shen-Hua-Shi granule group showed a significantly greater reduction in 24-h urinary protein at both week 4 (β = −244.12 mg/d; 95% CI −479.60 to −8.63; p = 0.04) and week 8 (β = −339.05 mg/d; 95% CI −588.52 to −89.59; p = 0.01) (Figure 2).

In sensitivity analyses, multiple imputations for missing data, log-transformed outcomes, and the application of a mixed model for repeated measures produced results consistent with the primary analysis. The treatment × time interaction at week 8 remained statistically significant in all models (p < 0.05) (Tables S3–S5). Additional terms testing interactions of age and sex with visit were not significant (p > 0.05) (Table S6), indicating that the observed treatment effect was robust to assumptions about data distribution, covariance structure, and demographic covariates.

In stratified analyses by baseline 24-h urinary protein, significant treatment effects were observed in patients with baseline proteinuria >1000 mg (Table 4 and Figure 3c). In this subgroup, the Yi-Shen-Hua-Shi granule group showed greater reductions in urinary protein at both 4 weeks (β = −400.35 mg/d; 95% CI −740.33 to −60.36; p = 0.02) and 8 weeks (β = −505.94 mg/d; 95% CI −859.42 to −152.45; p = 0.01). No significant group differences were detected in subgroups with lower baseline proteinuria (<500 mg or 500–1000 mg) (Table 4, Figures 3a and 3b).

Safety

No significant difference in the incidence of adverse events was observed between the two groups (Table S7). Most events were mild (CTCAE Grade 1-2) and transient, primarily involving mild gastrointestinal discomfort. No Grade 3 or higher events and no serious treatment-related adverse events were reported. Liver and renal function parameters remained stable throughout the study (Table S8).

Discussion

In this multicenter, randomized controlled trial, we found that adding Yi-Shen-Hua-Shi granule to optimized supportive care in adults with IgA nephropathy and persistent proteinuria led to significantly greater reductions in 24-h urine protein at 8 weeks compared to supportive care alone, particularly in patients with high baseline proteinuria. These results suggest that Yi-Shen-Hua-Shi granule, a compound Chinese herbal medicine, may provide additional renoprotective benefits in selected patients with IgA nephropathy.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies investigating traditional Chinese medicine in glomerular diseases, with several randomized trials demonstrating antiproteinuric and anti-inflammatory effects of traditional Chinese medicine either as adjunctive or stand-alone therapies [27–29]. A systematic review by Ma et al. showed that traditional herbal formulas reduced proteinuria and stabilized renal function in IgA nephropathy compared to conventional interventions, with a favorable safety profile [14]. Our trial builds upon this evidence, providing robust data from a multicenter, randomized, controlled setting and focusing on a standardized herbal granule with broad clinical acceptance in China.

The observed benefit was particularly pronounced among patients with baseline 24-h proteinuria >1000 mg, in whom proteinuria reduction exceeded 450 mg compared to control in 8 weeks. This is notable given that persistent proteinuria is a strong predictor of renal progression, and that some patients have suboptimal response to RAS blockers and supportive measures alone [30]. In contrast, no significant group differences were observed in patients with lower baseline proteinuria, which may be attributed to a lower degree of glomerular injury or a ceiling effect, making further proteinuria reduction less evident. Additionally, spontaneous remission rates are higher in patients with mild proteinuria. This finding is consistent with risk stratification strategies advocated by international guidelines and supports individualized patient selection in future research and clinical application [4]. Notably, the subgroup findings should be interpreted as exploratory given the limited sample size per stratum and potential for inflated type I error.

The beneficial effects observed in this study may be related to the multi-component and multi‑target actions of Yi-Shen-Hua-Shi granules. Preclinical studies have shown that major constituents such as Astragalus membranaceus and Poria cocos possess immunomodulatory, anti-inflammatory, and podocyte-protective properties. These effects are mediated through attenuation of mesangial proliferation, inhibition of pro-inflammatory signaling pathways (e.g., TGF-β1/Smad, NF-κB), and preservation of glomerular barrier integrity [18,31,32]. Such mechanisms are consistent with the clinical reduction in proteinuria observed in our participants. However, these mechanistic interpretations are based on experimental data, and the current clinical findings should be viewed as preliminary evidence supporting their potential translational relevance. Further mechanistic and translational studies are warranted to confirm these pathways in patients with IgA nephropathy.

Importantly, Yi-Shen-Hua-Shi granule was generally well-tolerated, with no significant increase in adverse events or laboratory abnormalities compared to control. This aligns with prior evidence and suggests that the granule may be safely added to standard therapy for selected patients. However, as with all traditional Chinese medicine interventions, monitoring for potential herb-drug interactions and allergic reactions remains prudent [33].

Our study has several limitations. First, Although the intervention lasted only 8 weeks, short-term reduction in proteinuria has been associated with improved long-term renal prognosis. Nevertheless, the durability of Yi-Shen-Hua-Shi granule’s effect requires longer follow-up. Second, while randomization and allocation concealment minimized bias, blinding was not feasible for patients due to the distinct taste and appearance of the herbal granules; however, endpoint assessment was blinded. Third, as most participants were of East Asian descent, generalizability to other populations may be limited. Fourth, due to data availability, we only adjusted for age and sex, lacking adjustment for important covariates such as lifestyle factors and comorbid conditions, which may confound the outcomes. Fifth, the trial recruited fewer patients than planned, which might have reduced statistical power and increased risk of both type I and II errors.

Despite these limitations, our study provides important evidence supporting the adjunctive use of Yi-Shen-Hua-Shi granule in patients with IgA nephropathy and significant proteinuria. Future studies should include adequately powered, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials with extended follow-up to evaluate the durability of proteinuria reduction and effects on hard renal endpoints (e.g., eGFR slope, kidney failure, and mortality). Given the exploratory signal in participants with baseline proteinuria >1000 mg/day, future protocols may consider stratified or enrichment designs with prespecified subgroup analyses. Mechanistic studies incorporating biomarker profiling and translational approaches (e.g., immune pathways, gut-kidney axis, and multi-omics) are warranted to clarify underlying mechanisms and identify responders. Comparative effectiveness and combination studies with contemporary therapies (e.g., SGLT2 inhibitors, targeted-release budesonide, endothelin receptor antagonists) and multinational cohorts will further define its role within KDIGO-guided care and strengthen external validity. Long-term pharmacovigilance should assess herb-drug interactions, dose optimization, and quality control standardization.

Conclusion

In conclusion, Yi-Shen-Hua-Shi granule, when added to optimized supportive care, appeared safe and demonstrated a promising short-term antiproteinuric effect in patients with IgA nephropathy, particularly among those with high baseline proteinuria. This compound herbal therapy may serve as a potential adjunct in the management of IgA nephropathy, pending confirmation in larger and longer-term trials.
