When the Highway Is Closed
Authors: Krissada Meemook, Mann Chandavimol
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Left atrial appendage occlusion (LAAO) has matured into a mainstream stroke-prevention strategy for patients with atrial fibrillation who cannot tolerate long-term oral anticoagulation; but actually, this procedure is based on one big thing that everyone thinks is normal: that the catheter can go from the femoral vein, up to the normal inferior vena cava (IVC), and cross the septum to the appendage easily. In this issue of JACC: Case Reports, Yap et al1 remind us of what happens when that assumption fails. Confronting a congenital anomalous IVC with azygos continuation, an anatomy that erases the hepatic segment of the cava and reroutes venous return through the azygos system into the superior vena cava, they abandoned the femoral highway. They reached the appendage from above, implanting a 31-mm Watchman device through the right internal jugular vein.1

Anomalous IVC with azygos continuation is rare, with an estimated prevalence of roughly 0.2% to 0.3% in the general population2 and higher figures among patients with congenital heart disease.3 For most of a cardiologist’s career, this anomaly remains an incidental finding on cross-sectional imaging. However, as LAAO volumes increase and the treated population ages, operators will increasingly encounter patients for whom the standard femoral route is unavailable, including those with interrupted IVCs, indwelling filters, caval occlusions, and complex venous drainage. Therefore, the true value of this report lies less in a single successful implantation than in the reproducible, step-by-step thought process it demonstrates. That thought process begins, and largely succeeds or fails, with imaging. The authors did not improvise at the table. Preprocedural computed tomography (CT) angiography of the neck confirmed a patent, catheter-suitable right internal jugular vein; dedicated cardiac CT characterized the landing zone and predicted the optimal fluoroscopic projection before the first puncture; and 3-dimensional reconstructions turned an unfamiliar trajectory into a rehearsed one. This is the real lesson for the community: in nonfemoral LAAO, multimodality imaging is not an adjunct but the enabling technology. Pairing preprocedural CT for access and device selection with continuous intraprocedural transesophageal echocardiography for transseptal puncture and deployment is what converts an anatomic dead end into a planned, appropriately sized implant.

The report is also honest about the price of coming from above, and that honesty is instructive. A superior approach inverts the geometry every LAAO operator has internalized. Transseptal puncture from the jugular demands a different needle orientation and carries its own risk of aortic root or posterior wall injury; the angulated trajectory to the right atrium complicates sheath advancement and coaxial alignment with the appendage. The authors’ solution, staged balloon septal dilatation, a cascade of wire and catheter exchanges, and a delivery sheath hand-modified with a heat gun to negotiate the caval angle, is resourceful and clearly effective. This is exactly the real-world problem-solving that rarely reaches print. It also constructively points toward an unmet need: purpose-built, superior-approach delivery systems that would enable future operators to achieve coaxiality without improvisation.

Read alongside the small but growing literature on alternative access, the case fills in a coherent map. Transjugular LAAO has now been described in isolated reports,4,5 including tailored approaches rehearsed with 3-dimensional printing,4 and transhepatic access has been used when both femoral and jugular routes are compromised.6 What emerges is a spectrum rather than a single fallback: transfemoral remains the default; transjugular is the natural next choice when the appendage anatomy and neck veins cooperate; transhepatic is reserved for when they do not. Choosing among them is exactly the kind of individualized, imaging-driven planning the authors model, and it aligns with the emphasis on procedural planning and operator experience in contemporary LAAO guidance.7,8

Some caution is warranted before this becomes a movement. This is a single patient, treated at a high-volume structural heart program with immediate access to advanced CT, expert echocardiography, and operators comfortable improvising. The older, comorbid profile that makes such patients candidates for LAAO, including a high CHA2DS2-VASc score and chronic kidney disease, is also the profile least forgiving of a prolonged procedure and contrast burden. A complication-free result is genuinely encouraging, but a single case cannot speak to reproducibility, learning curve, or the rate of the very complications the authors rightly flag. We should celebrate feasibility without mistaking it for established safety.

None of this diminishes the achievement. Yap et al1 took an approach that would have ended most LAAO attempts and, through disciplined planning and adaptive technique, delivered the therapy the patient needed. The broader message is one every interventionalist should absorb. As indications broaden, anatomy will continue to present problems that our standard playbook did not anticipate, and the operators who thrive will be those who treat preprocedural imaging as a strategy rather than paperwork. The femoral vein is a convention, not a requirement. Reports like this one broaden our understanding of possibilities and, equally important, document the challenges so the next operator facing a closed highway has a guide to follow.
