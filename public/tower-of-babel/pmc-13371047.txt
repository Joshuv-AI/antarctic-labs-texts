Combined transcriptomic and proteomic analysis reveals the influence of sulfur availability on nitrogen and carbon metabolism and lipid accumulation in Tribonema minus (Xanthophyceae)
Authors: Jian Hong, Yijia Liu, Bohan Yu, Chengwu Zhang, Baoyan Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCompared with single-cell microalgae, the filamentous microalgae Tribonema have stronger anti-predation ability and can be harvested more easily during large-scale production. To evaluate the potential of Tribonema as a raw material for biodiesel and bioproducts, this study conducted the comprehensive analysis of the biomass concentration and total lipid accumulation patterns of 12 Tribonema strains under nitrogen (N) and sulfur (S) nutritional regulation. Furthermore, by integrating multi-omics technologies, this study elucidated the potential mechanisms underlying lipid metabolism in T. minus under S-stress conditions. The results showed that under N concentration treatments (0, 3, and 18 mM), the biomass concentration of all 12 Tribonema strains initially increased and then decreased with increasing N concentration, while the total lipid content showed no significant correlation with N concentration. Under S concentration treatments (0, 0.3, and 1.2 mM), the biomass of all Tribonema species significantly increased with increasing S concentration. The total lipid and fatty acid content peaked at the lowest S concentration. A combined transcriptomic and proteomic analysis in T. minus revealed that under S-deficient conditions, the expression of genes and proteins related to N metabolism (e.g., nitrate reductase and nitrite reductase) was downregulated, and those related to S metabolism (e.g., ATP sulfurylase and APS kinase) were upregulated, thereby balancing intracellular levels of N and S to maintain normal growth and metabolism. The genes and proteins encoding the rate-limiting enzymes in the glycolysis pathway were upregulated, promoting pyruvate synthesis. The expression of genes and proteins associated with lipid synthesis (e.g., DGAT and PDAT) was significantly upregulated, thereby promoting the accumulation of lipids. This study demonstrated that the regulation of S availability significantly impacts the metabolic network of Tribonema, providing theoretical support for its application in biodiesel and other high-value bioproducts.Keywords: Tribonema, Sulfur availability, Carbon metabolism, Nitrogen metabolism, Lipids

Introduction

The filamentous microalgae of the Tribonema genus belong to the phylum Heterokontophyta, class Xanthophyceae, order Trobonematales, and family Tribonemataceae [17]. According to Algaebase records, the genus currently comprises 38 species, 4 varieties, and 1 forma (https://www.algaebase.org). Many Tribonema species are ideal candidates for large-scale cultivation and wastewater treatment due to their rapid growth rates, strong environmental adaptability, and ease of harvesting [13, 43]. Among them, Tribonema minus, the most extensively studied species in this genus, demonstrates significant potential for the development of bioresources. T. minus possesses highly efficient lipid accumulation capabilities, with the lipid content reaching 40–60% of the cell weight [44, 45]. Within its fatty acid composition, palmitoleic acid (POA) and eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) account for over 20% and 3% of the cell weight, respectively [44, 45, 47, 51]. POA and EPA are high-value omega-7 and omega-3 conjugated fatty acids, respectively, exhibiting excellent biological activity, with extensive applications in nutritional health and pharmaceutical fields [19, 27]. Furthermore, T. minus demonstrates significant potential in aquaculture, where its inclusion in fish feeds effectively enhances immune activity and promotes growth [52]. Other species within the genus Tribonema, such as Tribonema aequale and Tribonema utriculosum, also demonstrate application potential in lipid and EPA accumulation, aquaculture feed supplementation, and wastewater treatment [6, 26, 39, 46].

Essential inorganic nutrients such as carbon (C), nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and sulfur (S) significantly influence microalgal cell growth, reproduction, and metabolism [3]. Nutrient limitation is an effective biochemical pathway for controlling and altering the microalgal cell cycle while enhancing lipid accumulation [7]. N, the second most abundant element in microalgae after carbon, plays a crucial role in the synthesis of proteins, enzymes, chlorophyll, energy transfer molecules (ATP and ADP), and genetic material (RNA and DNA) [3]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that N limitation/starvation promotes substantial lipid synthesis and accumulation in microalgae [40]. Guo et al. [18] and our recent study [13] revealed that the lipid content of T. minus decreased under N starvation conditions. N limitation/starvation failed to induce substantial lipid accumulation in T. minus, unlike the pattern observed in most microalgae, where N deficiency leads to high lipid accumulation. Notably, under high N conditions, the lipid content of T. minus was higher in the later stages of cultivation than under low N or N-deficient conditions. N starvation markedly affected T. minus growth, with biomass concentration peaking at an initial N concentration of 3 mM (NaNO3). Neither low nor high N concentrations promoted biomass accumulation in T. minus [13].

S plays a crucial role in maintaining cellular structure and function [25]. It participates in the synthesis of cysteine and methionine, constitutes the S-containing lipids of cell membranes, and stabilizes protein structures through disulfide bonds [14]. Furthermore, S is a component of various coenzymes and cofactors, including thiamine and coenzyme A, which regulate cellular osmotic pressure and N metabolism processes [48]. Our previous study revealed that increasing the S concentration (inorganic sulfate, SO42−) markedly enhanced the growth of T. minus, promoting biomass accumulation. Meanwhile, S limitation/deficiency rapidly accelerated lipid accumulation in T. minus, particularly under S-deficient conditions where lipids could be accumulated at higher levels in a short period of time [13].

T. minus possessed a unique lipid synthesis mechanism, in which lipid production was regulated by S deficiency but not by N deficiency, as demonstrated in our previous work. To investigate whether this characteristic is shared across the genus Tribonema, we selected 12 Tribonema strains and conducted cultivation experiments under varying N and S concentrations in this study. By analyzing their growth and lipid content, we aimed to reveal the biochemical response patterns of Tribonema strains under different cultivation conditions. We analyzed the transcriptome and proteome of T. minus under S-sufficient and S-deficient conditions to elucidate the molecular regulatory mechanisms governing its growth and lipid metabolism in response to fluctuating S availability. By combining a comparative study of 12 strains of Tribonema with a multi-omics approach, this study provided new insights into sulfur-regulated lipid accumulation in Tribonema and other related research.

Materials and methods

Microalgal strains and culture medium

The laboratory maintained 12 strains of Tribonema, 8 of which were obtained from the Culture Collection of Algae at the University of Göttingen (SAG) (Tribonema aequale SAG200.80, Tribonema minus SAG880-3, Tribonema ulotrichoides SAG21.94, Tribonema utriculosum SAG22.94, Tribonema viride SAG23.94, Tribonema vulgare SAG 24.94, Tribonema sp. SAG2178, and Tribonema sp. SAG2172); the other four strains were obtained from Freshwater Algae Culture Collection at the Institute of Hydrobiology (FACHB) (Tribonema sp. LJYY-2, Tribonema sp. FACHB1786, Tribonema sp. LXQQ36, and Tribonema sp. LY14-4).

The culture medium used for the seed of algal strains was BBM medium modified by our laboratory (mBBM). The specific composition and concentrations were 18 mM NaNO3, 0.30 mM MgSO4·7H2O, 0.43 mM NaCl, 0.43 mM K2HPO4, 1.29 mM KH2PO4, 0.17 mM CaCl2·2H2O, 0.18 mM H3BO3, 0.03 mM ZnSO4·7H2O, 7.28 μM MnCl2·4H2O, 4.92 μM Na2MoO4·2H2O, 6.29 μM CuSO4·5H2O, 1.68 μM Co(NO3)2·6H2O, 0.13 mM EDTA-Na2, 0.55 mM KOH, and 1.79 μM FeSO4·7H2O.

Experimental design

Before the experiment, the seed of algal strains was prepared by propagating it in mBBM medium within sterile conical flasks. Subsequently, the culture was transferred to a Ø6.5 × 65 cm columnar photobioreactor for further expansion under the following conditions: light intensity, 50 μmol·m−2·s−1 and cultivation temperature, 25 ± 1 °C. When the 12 strains of Tribonema entered the logarithmic growth phase (approximately 6–7 days), the algal culture was filtered and concentrated using sterile gauze. The culture was then washed at least twice with fresh mBBM medium to remove old medium and dead algal cells. The algal cells were then inoculated into Ø6 × 65 cm columnar photobioreactors at an initial concentration of approximately 0.2 g·L−1. The experiments included 12 algal strains, 3 N concentrations (0, 3, and 18 mM), and 3 S concentrations (0, 0.3, and 1.2 mM) as variables. These concentration levels were chosen based on our previous study [13], in which 18 mM N and 0.3 mM S corresponded to the standard mBBM medium and were therefore used as the normal (control) conditions. All treatments were performed under a constant light intensity of 300 μmol·m−2·s−1 and air enriched with 1% CO2 (v/v) for 12 days, with 3 biological replicates per condition.

The experiments for transcriptomic and proteomic analyses were designed with two initial S concentrations: an S-deficient group (mBBM medium lacking SO42−, denoted as 0S) and an S-sufficient group (1.2 mM SO42− concentration in mBBM medium, denoted as SS). Algal sludge samples were collected on days 1, 3, and 5 (filtered and rinsed under sterile conditions), rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C.

Experimental methods

Morphological observation and 18S rDNA sequence analysis

Cellular morphology was observed using light microscopy (CX41RF; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a digital imaging system of CapStudio.

Microalgal DNA was extracted using a TaKaRa Mini BEST Plant Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (TaKaRa Bio, Beijing). The 18S gene was then amplified in 25 μL PCR reactions using the primers NS1-F (5’GTAGTCATATGCTTGTCTC3’) and LR3-R (GGTCCGTGTTTCAAGAC) and KOD-Plus-Neo DNA polymerase (product code: KOD-401). The 18S gene sequence was compared against other microalgal 18S gene sequences in the NCBI database using the BLAST algorithm. Multiple sequence alignment of the 12 Tribonema strains’ sequences and other reference sequences was constructed using the ClustalX 2.1 aligner. In addition, a molecular phylogenetic tree was constructed from the multiple sequence alignment using maximum likelihood methods (ML) in the MEGA 6.06 software package.

Measurement of biomass concentration, lipid content and fatty acids content

Algal samples were collected daily for biomass measurement. 10 mL of algal culture was filtered through a pre-weighed 0.45 μm glass fiber filter, dried at 105 ℃ to constant weight, and the dry biomass concentration was calculated from the weight difference divided by 10 mL.

Every 3 days, algal cells were harvested for freeze-drying, followed by the determination of total lipid content. The lipid content was measured following the method described by Gao et al. [11]. Total lipid was extracted by the method of Khozin-Goldberg et al. [21] with some modifications. 50–80 mg of freeze-dried algal powder was firstly extracted with 2 mL of dimethyl sulfoxide–methanol mixture (V:V = 1:9) in a water bath of 50 °C for 1.5 h, and then it was re-extracted with 4 mL of diethyl ether–hexane mixed solution (V:V = 1:1) in an ice bath for 1.5 h. The supernatant of the extracting solution was collected into the same glass vial and the extraction process was repeated.

Fatty acid methyl esterification was performed by referring to the method of Bigogno et al. [1]. 25 mg of lyophilized algal powder was accurately weighed and added to 2 mL of 2% H2SO4–methanol solution, along with 100 μL of 0.25% heptadecanoic acid standard (C19:0), and the mixture was filled with argon and stirred in a water bath at 80 °C for 1.5 h. After cooling, 1 mL each of deionized water and hexane was added, and the upper organic phase was centrifuged after thorough mixing and transferred to the sample bottle. The fatty acid composition and its content were determined using gas chromatography (GC). The specific procedure of GC was described in Gao et al. [10].

Transcriptomic analysis

RNA extraction, purification, reverse transcription, library preparation, and Illumina sequencing were performed by Novogene Co., Ltd., Japan (Beijing, China). The raw files were available from the NCBI SRA database under the accession number: PRJNA1439532. Total RNA samples were extracted using an RNA extraction kit and analyzed. mRNA was enriched, and a reverse transcription kit was used to synthesize double-stranded cDNA. End repair, A-tailing, and adapter ligation were performed, before conducting fragment selection and PCR amplification. Subsequently, library preparation and quality control were conducted, followed by Illumina sequencing. Libraries were constructed using a standard NEB protocol. Briefly, fragmented mRNA was used as template and random oligonucleotides as primers to synthesize first-strand cDNA with M-MuLV reverse transcriptase. The RNA strand was then degraded by RNase H, and second-strand cDNA was synthesized using DNA polymerase I and dNTPs. After purification, the double-stranded cDNA underwent end repair, A-tailing, and adapter ligation. cDNA fragments of approximately 250–300 bp were selected using AMPure XP beads, followed by PCR amplification and a second AMPure XP purification to obtain the final library. For quality control, libraries were first quantified using a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer and diluted to 1.5 ng/µL. Insert size was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, and libraries with the expected insert size were further quantified by qRT-PCR. Only libraries with an effective concentration > 2 nM were used for sequencing. Sequencing reads were first assembled into transcripts for transcriptomics analysis [15]. The Corset program [9] was applied to cluster Trinity-assembled transcripts based on shared read information. Corset first aggregates transcripts that share reads into preliminary clusters, and then uses expression profiles across samples together with hierarchical clustering (H-cluster) to split clusters when transcripts show distinct expression patterns across conditions. Each final cluster is treated as a “gene”. All clustered sequences were annotated in parallel against the seven databases (NR, NT, KO, Swiss-Prot, PFAM, GO, and KOG). In most cases, annotations were consistent across databases. When discrepancies occurred, functional assignments were determined based on the best-hit alignment (highest similarity/lowest E-value) and priority was given to curated databases (e.g., Swiss-Prot and KO) for functional interpretation.

Proteomic analysis

Total protein extraction was performed using a Bradford Protein Quantification Kit, followed by protein detection via sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. Enzymatic digestion and desalting were conducted using proteases (trypsin). TMT labeling was performed using Thermo Scientific Tandem Mass Tags according to the manufacturer’s protocol [33], followed by fraction separation using an L-3000 high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system. Liquid chromatography/mass spectrometry (LC–MS) analysis was conducted using a UHPLC system and mass spectrometer [50]. Protein identification and detection were performed using the Proteome Discoverer version 2.4 (PD2.4, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) software against protein databases. For proteome analysis, raw files obtained from mass spectrometry detection were searched against corresponding databases. The database used for peptide identification was the protein database translated from our transcriptome assembly, ensuring consistency between transcriptomic and proteomic analyses. When necessary, public databases such as UniProt/NCBInr were used for functional annotation. Proteins were identified based on search results while undergoing data quality control. To improve data reliability and reduce false positives, Proteome Discoverer (PD) applied stringent filtering during quality control. Only peptide-spectrum matches (PSMs) with ≥ 99% confidence were retained, and proteins were accepted only if they contained at least one unique peptide. A false discovery rate (FDR) cutoff of 1% was applied at both peptide and protein levels, and any identifications exceeding this threshold were removed. Additional QC metrics included peptide length distribution, precursor mass-error distribution, number of unique peptides per protein, protein sequence coverage, and protein molecular-weight distribution. Protein quantification was performed in Proteome Discoverer using PSM-level reporter ion intensities. Specifically, relative abundances were first obtained for each PSM based on peak area, then summarized to unique peptides by combining all corresponding PSMs, and finally aggregated to the protein level using all unique peptides assigned to each protein. Differential analysis was conducted by comparing biological replicates between defined sample pairs. For each protein, the fold change (FC) was calculated as the ratio of mean relative abundances between two groups. Statistical significance was assessed using a two-tailed t-test, and proteins with P ≤ 0.05 were considered significant. Proteins with FC ≥ 1.5 and P ≤ 0.05 were defined as upregulated, while those with FC ≤ 0.67 and P ≤ 0.05 were defined as downregulated.

Proteins were annotated using common functional databases, including COG, GO, and KEGG. Protein quantification and differential analysis were performed. Finally, functional analysis of differentially expressed proteins was conducted, encompassing GO and KEGG functional differential analyses. Functional enrichment was performed only for differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) defined by the thresholds in the differential analysis (FC ≥ 1.5 or ≤ 0.67 with P ≤ 0.05). GO enrichment was conducted by mapping DEPs to GO terms and applying a hypergeometric test to identify GO categories significantly over-represented relative to the background of all identified proteins. GO terms with P ≤ 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. KEGG pathway enrichment was performed using the same hypergeometric test framework, comparing DEPs to the background set of all identified proteins, and pathways with P ≤ 0.05 were defined as significantly enriched. These analyses highlight the major biological functions and pathways associated with DEPs.

The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium (https://proteomecentral.proteomexchange.org) via the iProX partner repository [5, 6, 30] with the dataset identifier PXD075898.

Data statistics

Statistical analysis was performed using Excel 2023 and Origin 2025, with P-values < 0.05 indicating significant differences.

Results and discussion

Morphological characteristics and molecular phylogenetic analyses of 12 Tribonema strains

There were no significant differences in the microscopic morphology of the 12 Tribonema strains, except that the cells of the T. vulgare exhibited a more pronounced swelling (Fig. 1). Tribonema consists of an unbranched filament formed by a row of slender, cylindrical cells. Individual cells are 2–5 times longer than they are wide, and the filaments are relatively large, reaching lengths of 0.5–3 mm. The cell wall consists of two H-shaped lamellae that interlock to protect the cell. There are one or more chloroplasts per cell. The chloroplasts are periplasmic, discoid or lamellar in shape [28].

Microscopic morphology of the 12 Tribonema strains. Scale bar represents 10 μm

After sequencing and assembly, 18S rDNA sequences from 12 Tribonema strains were obtained. As shown in Fig. 2, with the Heterococcus serving as the outgroup, the 18S rDNA phylogenetic tree indicated that the 12 Tribonema strains primarily cluster into two major clades. Among them, Tribonema sp. LY14-4, T. utriculosum, and T. vulgare formed a small clade. Tribonema sp. SAG2178 and T. aequale form a small clade. These two small clades, together with T. utriculosum and T. intermixtum, formed a major clade. Tribonema sp. FACHB1786 and T. ulotrichoides form a single clade. Tribonema sp. LXQQ-36, Tribonema sp. LJYY-2, Tribonema sp. SAG2172, T. viride, along with T. minus and T. affine, formed a small clade; these two small clades merged to form a second evolutionary clade. The 18S rDNA sequences show little variation among the different species.

Effects of different initial N and S concentrations on the growth of the 12 Tribonema strains

Analysis of the growth of the 12 Tribonema strains under 3 N concentrations (0, 3, and 18 mM) during the early growth phase showed largely consistent growth trends across all 3 N concentrations (Fig. 3). From day 4, biomass differences emerged among the experimental groups. The 0 mM-N group exhibited slow growth rates, essentially entering a plateau phase, whereas the 3 and 18 mM-N groups exhibited significantly higher growth rates than the 0 mM-N group. By day 12, all 12 strains achieved their lowest biomass under 0 mM-N conditions, which was significantly lower than that of the other 2 N concentrations (P < 0.05). Except for the algal strain Tribonema sp. SAG2172, which showed no significant difference in biomass between 3 and 18 mM N concentrations, the final biomass of the remaining 11 strains exhibited an initial increase and then a decrease with increasing initial N concentration. Specifically, the highest biomass was achieved in the 3 mM-N group, which was significantly higher than the other two N concentration treatment groups (P < 0.05). Under 3 mM-N conditions, the biomass of the 12 Tribonema strains ranged from 3.51 to 6.45 g/L; two strains had a biomass exceeding 6 g/L, with Tribonema sp. LJYY-2 achieving the highest biomass (6.41 g/L), followed by T. ulotrichoides SAG22.94. No significant difference existed between these two strains (P > 0.05), but both strains had significantly higher biomass levels than the other strains (P < 0.05). Previous studies indicated that nitrogen effects on microalgal growth were both concentration-dependent and source-dependent, with moderate N supply often supporting biomass productivity but excessive N sometimes causing growth suppression due to metabolic imbalance or increased assimilation costs [10, 12]. In our study, nitrate was the only N source, and all 12 Tribonema strains produced significantly higher biomass at 3 mM than at 18 mM. This suggested that an intermediate N level was more favorable for growth under our cultivation conditions, whereas higher nitrate may impose an energetic burden that limited biomass accumulation.

Growth of the 12 Tribonema strains cultured for 12 days under 3 N concentrations (0, 3, and 18 mM)

Growth analysis of the 12 Tribonema strains under 3 S concentrations (0, 0.3, and 1.2 mM) revealed similar growth patterns across all 3 S concentrations (Fig. 4). The 0 mM-S group exhibited slow growth rates and entered a plateau phase, whereas the 0.3 mM-S (normal S concentration in BBM medium) and the 1.2 mM-S (4 times the S concentration in BBM medium) groups exhibited significantly higher growth rates than the 0 mM-S group (P < 0.05). By day 12 of cultivation, the final biomass of all 12 strains exhibited a significant increase with increasing initial S concentration, with the 1.2 mM-S group achieving the highest biomass (P < 0.05). Microscopic observation of the cells revealed that the growth of all 12 Tribonema strains was significantly inhibited under 0 mM-S conditions, with paler cell coloration and shorter filaments (not displayed). S deficiency has been shown to impair the synthesis of growth-related proteins and their associated cofactors, thereby disrupting the Calvin cycle and inhibiting cell proliferation [25, 34]. At a concentration of 1.2 mM-S, the biomass of the 12 Tribonema strains ranged from 5.18 to 7.09 g/L, with the biomass of 2 strains exceeding 7 g/L. T. utriculosum SAG22.94 exhibited the highest biomass, followed by T. minus SAG880-3 (7.03 g/L). High S concentrations promoted microalgal growth, which was also observed in Scenedesmus acuminatus, S. obliquus, and Coccomyxa sp. IITRSTKM4 [25, 41, 53].

Effects of different initial N and S concentrations on the lipid contents of 12 Tribonema strains

In terms of the total lipid content of the 12 Tribonema strains cultured under 3 initial N concentrations, the results indicated that the lipid content of all 12 algal strains increased to varying degrees with extended cultivation time at the same N concentration; however, the effect of different N concentrations on lipid content varied among algal species (Fig. 5). On day 12 of cultivation, the total lipid content of T. vulgare SAG 24.94, Tribonema sp. LXQQ36, and Tribonema sp. SAG2172 decreased with increasing N concentration, with the 0 mM-N group exhibiting the highest lipid content (P < 0.05). The total lipid content in T. minus SAG880-3, T. ulotrichoides SAG21.94, and Tribonema sp. FACHB1786 increased with increasing N concentration, with the 18 mM-N group exhibiting the highest lipid content (P < 0.05). No clear pattern was observed between lipid content and N concentration in the remaining six Tribonema strains. At 0 mM-N, the total lipid content of the 12 Tribonema strains ranged from 16.59% to 53.06% of dry weight (DW); furthermore, 2 strains exceeded 40% DW, with Tribonema sp. SAG2172 exhibiting the highest total lipid content. At 3 mM-N, the total lipid content ranged from 20.22% to 48.32% DW across the 12 strains, with 2 strains exceeding 40% DW, with Tribonema sp. SAG2172 exhibiting the highest total lipid content, followed by T. minus SAG880-3 (44.61% DW). The total lipid content of the 12 Tribonema strains under 18 mM-N conditions ranged from 23.79% to 45.67% DW. Three strains exhibited a total lipid content exceeding 40% DW, with T. minus SAG880-3 showing the highest total lipid content, followed by Tribonema sp. SAG2172 (42.59% DW) and Tribonema sp. FACHB1786 (41.55% DW). N deficiency is the most widely used strategy to induce lipid accumulation in algae [36], in the present study, this method was found to be applicable to T. vulgare SAG 24.94, Tribonema sp. LXQQ36, and Tribonema sp. SAG2172, but was unsuitable for the other nine Tribonema strains.

The total lipid content of the 12 Tribonema strains cultured under 3 initial S concentrations on days 6 and 12 is shown in Fig. 6. The results revealed that the initial S concentration consistently influenced lipid accumulation in the 12 Tribonema strains. Under S-deficient conditions (0 mM-S), total lipid content rose rapidly and reached high levels by day 6, and in most strains the lipid content at day 6 was only slightly lower than that at day 12. This indicated that S deficiency accelerated lipid accumulation within a short time frame. On day 6 of cultivation, the total lipid content of all 12 Tribonema strains under 0 mM-S conditions was significantly higher than that under 0.3 mM-S and 1.2 mM-S conditions (P < 0.05), and the total lipid content of the 12 Tribonema strains ranged from 18.17% to 44.20% DW. Two strains exceeded 40% DW, with T. minus exhibiting the highest total lipid content. At day 12 of cultivation, the total lipid content of all 12 Tribonema strains was significantly lower under 1.2 mM-S conditions than under 0S and 1S conditions (P < 0.05). The lipid content of the 12 Tribonema strains under the 1.2 mM-S conditions showed limited changes over time.

The 12 Tribonema strains exhibited significant differences in lipid accumulation capacity and diverse responses to N deficiency, whereas their responses to S deficiency were highly consistent. Our previous studies revealed no significant correlation between intracellular N content and total lipid content in T. minus (neither positive nor negative), whereas the intracellular S content has been shown to exhibit a regulatory effect on lipid accumulation [13]. Specifically, in a previous study, when the intracellular S content fell below 0.6% of the dry weight, the total lipid content exhibited a significant negative correlation with the intracellular S content [13]. Studies on many other algae, such as Scenedesmus sp. and Chlamydomonas reinhardtii, have also shown that S deficiency significantly promotes the accumulation of intracellular lipids [16, 29]. However, lipid accumulation in these algae was typically also induced by N stress. This finding suggests that the metabolic regulatory mechanism for N, S, and lipids in the genus Tribonema is somewhat unique.

Fatty acids are the core components of all cell membrane systems and storage lipids [32]. The fatty acids of Tribonema are myristic acid (C14:0), palmitic acid (C16:0), palmitoleic acid (C16:1), stearic acid (C18:0), oleic acid (C18:1), linoleic acid (C18:2), linolenic acid (C18:3), arachidonic acid (C20:4) and eicosapentaenoic acid (C20:5, EPA), among which palmitic acid and palmitoleic acid are the main fatty acids (Fig. 7). The effects of changes in initial S concentration on the fatty acid content of 12 Tribonema strains were consistent with the corresponding trends in lipid content, with the lowest fatty acid content observed under 1.2 mM-S conditions (P < 0.05). Among the 12 algal strains, 10 exhibited significantly lower fatty acid content under 0.3 mM-S conditions compared to 0 mM-S conditions (P < 0.05), while 2 strains (T. ulotrichoides and T. utriculosum) showed significantly higher fatty acid content under 0.3 mM-S conditions compared to 0 mM-S conditions (P < 0.05). Under 0 mM-S conditions, T. minus accumulated the highest fatty acid content, reaching 46.11% of dry weight, followed by T. sp. LJYY-2, with a fatty acid content of 45.28% of dry weight.

Comparative study on S and N metabolism in T. minus under S-deficient and S-sufficient conditions

Currently, research on sulfate metabolism in algae is limited, and related studies are confined to a few model species, such as Chlamydomonas reinhardtii and Phaeodactylum tricornutum [8, 22]. In terms of the S metabolism of T. minus, the genes encoding ATP sulfurylase, adenosine-5′-phosphosulfate kinase (APS kinase), APS reductase, and sulfite reductase show overall upregulation under S-deficient conditions (Fig. 8). Among these, the sulfite reductase gene exhibited the highest upregulation. Proteomic analysis indicated that the fold change in protein content for these enzymes ranges from − 0.69 to 0.45, suggesting that the overall protein content differences were not substantial. Notably, the transcript–protein discrepancy suggests that regulation in T. minus may occur predominantly at the transcriptional level under short-term S stress, while protein abundance remains relatively stable. APS reductase catalyzes the reduction of APS, which is a key step in S assimilation in plants [8, 42]. Studies have shown that APS reductase activity is enhanced to promote sulfate uptake and utilization under S-deficient conditions or when increased cysteine was required for glutamine synthesis [42, 49]. In T. minus, the cysteine synthase gene and protein levels are both upregulated under S-deficient conditions, suggesting that S demand may be a negative feedback mechanism for cysteine levels. Moreover, in Chlorella sorokiniana, cysteine synthesis was inhibited when sulfate was available and activated by S starvation [4]. Genes encoding glutathione synthetase and glutathione peroxidase were upregulated, whereas their protein levels showed both upregulation and downregulation. Glutathione reductase was upregulated at both transcript and protein levels. These enzymes are components of the glutathione/antioxidant system, which links nitrogen metabolism with carbon/sulfur metabolism by regulating cellular redox balance and assimilatory pathways. Thus, a certain amount of glutathione accumulates under S-deficient conditions, leading to a significant reduction in the levels of S-containing amino acids methionine and cysteine. Additionally, the gene encoding S-adenosyl methionine (SAM) synthase, which is essential for converting methionine to SAM, was upregulated at the gene level but downregulated at the protein level. This opposite trend in T. minus suggests a possible post-transcriptional control that limits SAM synthase accumulation under S starvation, consistent with the observed decline in methionine abundance. Similar results were observed in Anabaena sp. PCC 7120 and Galdieria sulphuraria, where cellular methionine and cysteine levels markedly decreased under low S concentrations and S starvation [20, 35]. Therefore, free S-containing amino acids may decrease, while S-containing metabolites such as glutathione and SAM related compounds may accumulate or be preferentially retained under S-deficient conditions. Our data collectively indicate that T. minus responds to S deficiency by transcriptional activation of S-assimilation and glutathione-related pathways, while maintaining relatively stable protein abundance of core S enzymes, implying rapid but tightly controlled metabolic adjustment. This accumulation likely served to rapidly restore free S-containing amino acid production upon S replenishment, thereby reestablishing normal cellular physiological functions.

Different S supply strategies inevitably lead to alterations in S metabolism, and maintaining equilibrium between S and N metabolism has been shown to be essential for achieving optimal cellular metabolism [25]. Consequently, the levels of N assimilation proteins are considered an indicator of balanced S:N ratios. Transcriptomic and proteomic analyses of T. minus revealed downregulated expression (both at the gene and protein levels) of enzymes involved in N metabolism, including nitrate reductase, nitrite reductase, glutamate synthase, and glutamate:2-oxoglutarate amidotransferase (Fig. 8). Consequently, N metabolism-related genes and proteins exhibited reduced expression under S deprivation to maintain the S:N ratio. Reports have indicated reduced nitrate uptake and assimilation capacity under S-limited conditions [31]. Collectively, these findings suggest that S is involved in N assimilation, possibly due to decreased cysteine content during S deficiency, leading to the accumulation of O-acetylserine, which acts as a feedback inhibitor. In this study, under S starvation, we also observed downregulation of the gene encoding serine acetyltransferase, which synthesized O-acetylserine. This progressive downregulation of gene expression over cultivation time further confirmed the accumulation of O-acetylserine and the resulting negative feedback regulation. Taken together, our multi-omics data demonstrate that S deficiency triggered a coordinated shift in T. minus, which upregulated S-assimilation pathways while suppressing N-assimilation machinery to rebalance intracellular S:N homeostasis.

Comparison of lipid synthesis in T. minus under S-deficient and S-sufficient conditions

The lipid content was significantly higher under S-deficient (0S) conditions than under S-sufficient (SS) conditions. To further investigate the molecular responses of T. minus to lipid accumulation under different S supplementation conditions, the expression levels of genes and proteins associated with lipid synthesis in T. minus were compared (Fig. 9). Fatty acid synthesis and triglyceride synthesis represent two critical steps in the synthesis and accumulation of storage lipids in microalgae [38]. Glycolysis provides energy and NADPH for lipid synthesis while also supplying pyruvate, the primary precursor of acetyl-CoA for fatty acid synthesis. Glycolysis involves three irreversible rate-limiting enzymes, including glucokinase/hexokinase, 6-phosphofructokinase, and pyruvate kinase. Transcriptomic and proteomic analyses revealed overall upregulation of the genes and proteins encoding these three key enzymes, suggesting that S-deficient conditions may enhance the glycolytic pathway in T. minus.

Pyruvate produced during glycolysis was catalyzed by pyruvate dehydrogenase to generate acetyl-CoA, a precursor required for fatty acid synthesis. Both the gene and protein expression levels of this enzyme were significantly elevated under S-deficient conditions, providing ample precursors for de novo fatty acid synthesis. Simultaneously, the gene encoding acetyl-CoA carboxylase/biotin carboxylase (ACACA), the rate-limiting enzyme in the de novo fatty acid synthesis pathway, was upregulated under S-deficient conditions, with differential expression reaching over fourfold. This upregulation progressively increased with extended cultivation time. The protein expression of this enzyme also increased under S-deficient conditions. Similarly, in the marine diatom Emiliania huxleyi, genes related to fatty acid biosynthesis such as acetyl-CoA carboxylase were also affected by low S conditions [2]. Additionally, under S-deficient conditions, key enzymes involved in regulating fatty acid synthesis pathways, including 3-oxoacyl-ACP synthase, 3-oxoacyl-ACP reductase, 3-hydroxyacyl-ACP dehydratase, and enoyl-ACP reductase, exhibited upregulation at both the gene and protein levels. With each cycle of condensation, reduction, dehydration-oxidation, and re-reduction, the carbon skeleton of malonyl-ACP increased by two carbons. After multiple rounds of chain elongation, the resulting acyl-ACP provided two pathways for triglyceride synthesis. One pathway involved lysophosphatidate formation via glycerol-3-phosphate O-acyltransferase in chloroplasts. The other pathway was the release of free fatty acids into the cytoplasm through the action of thioesterase. These fatty acids were then broken down into acetyl-CoA by long-chain fatty CoA synthetases (LACS) in the cytoplasm/endoplasmic reticulum. Acetyl-CoA was similarly converted into lysophosphatidate by glycerol-3-phosphate O-acyltransferase. Subsequently, lysophospholipid acyltransferase converted this into phosphatidate, which was further processed by phosphatidate phosphatase to yield diacylglycerol (DAG). In the acyl-CoA-dependent pathway, DAG and acetyl-CoA serve as substrates to form triacylglycerols (TAGs) under the catalysis of diacylglycerol O-acyltransferase (DGAT) [23]. Another non-acyl-CoA-dependent pathway converts phospholipids and glycolipids into TAGs via phospholipid:diacylglycerol acyltransferase (PDAT) and galactolipid:galactolipid galactosyltransferase, respectively [24]. Transcriptomic and proteomic analyses revealed that the genes and proteins encoding these enzymes were upregulated under S-deficient conditions. Additionally, DAG can be synthesized from monoacylglycerol via 2-acylglycerol O-acyltransferase, whose gene expression was upregulated under S-deficient conditions, while its protein expression showed both up- and downregulation. This may result from excessive DAG accumulation via phospholipid synthesis, leading to decreased protein expression of related enzymes—a form of post-transcriptional negative feedback regulation. Other enzymes, such as acylglycerol lipase, catalyze the hydrolysis of monoglyceride into glycerol and fatty acids. UDP-sulfoquinovose synthase (SQD1) and sulfoquinovosyltransferase (SQD2) catalyze the synthesis of sulfoquinovosylglycerol diester (SQDG). Both genes and proteins were downregulated under S-deficient conditions. SQDG can stabilize pigment complexes, maintaining the structural and functional integrity of thylakoid membranes [37]. Therefore, reduced SQDG biosynthesis under S-deficient conditions further impaired algal photosynthesis. Collectively, these findings indicate that pathways associated with TAG synthesis were upregulated in T. minus under S-deficient conditions, whereas those related to membrane lipid (SQDG) synthesis were downregulated.

Conclusion

In this study, the final biomass of the 12 Tribonema strains exhibited consistent trends in response to varying N and S concentrations. Regarding the total lipid content, no significant pattern emerged in lipid accumulation across the 12 strains under varying initial N concentrations. However, all 12 Tribonema strains achieved the highest total lipid content under conditions of S deficiency. This result indicated that within a certain S concentration range, high S concentrations promoted biomass accumulation in the 12 Tribonema strains, whereas low S concentrations were more conducive to total lipid accumulation. For transcriptomic and proteomic analyses of T. minus under S-deficient conditions, N metabolism-related genes and proteins in T. minus were downregulated, while those associated with S metabolism were upregulated. N assimilation processes were inhibited by S deficiency to balance intracellular N-to-S ratios. The rate-limiting enzymes in the glycolytic pathway were upregulated, directing intracellular carbon flux toward pyruvate synthesis via glycolysis. Concurrently, key enzymes involved in lipid synthesis, such as pyruvate dehydrogenase, DGAT, and PDAT, were upregulated, thereby promoting the substantial accumulation of fatty acids and triglycerides under S-deficient conditions. These findings suggest that sulfur-regulated lipid accumulation in Tribonema could be exploited for industrial lipid production, and future work may focus on process scale-up or targeted metabolic engineering to further improve yields.
