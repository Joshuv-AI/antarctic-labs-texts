Applications of Machine Learning in Noninvasive Anemia Diagnosis: A Systematic Review Based on Cross‐Industry Standard Process for Data Mining
Authors: Mohammad Hadi Ghahroudi, Niloofar Mohammadzadeh, Hadiseh Ghadiri Bidhendi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsAnemia is a disorder caused by insufficient red blood cell count or hemoglobin concentration, affecting nearly a quarter of the global population each year. An undiagnosed condition can escalate into life‐threatening complications, particularly among pregnant women and children. Although anemia causes identifiable symptoms, accurate diagnosis typically requires invasive venipuncture with quality‐controlled analyzers and reagents operated by trained personnel. This diagnostic procedure is laborious and costly in deprived regions and a less resource‐demanding alternative would be beneficial. This systematic review aims at outlining the recent efforts for creating such noninvasive alternative procedures via machine learning (ML).MethodsA search was conducted through PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus via tailored queries, yielding 1923 entries. Following screening, 58 eligible records were included in the qualitative synthesis. The Cross‐Industry Standard Process for Data Mining (CRISP‐DM) phases guided the selection of the main characteristics of each paper.ResultsThe contributions span a variety of algorithms and input data. The most frequent acquisition sites and data types were eye conjunctiva images, fingertip PPG signals, and palmar area images, respectively. Estimation of hemoglobin was of more interest than anemia classification. The light source in most experiments was at low energy or near‐infrared spectrum. The smartphone served as a convenient device for signal acquisition or model deployment via a mobile app.ConclusionSubstantial progress has been achieved in ML applications for noninvasive anemia detection, leading to breakthroughs in prevention and diagnosis. Nonetheless, there were challenges to building robust procedures, such as data scarcity and imbalance, limited diversity, and confounding factors, some of which have been addressed recently.Keywords: anemia, Cross‐Industry Standard Process for Data Mining, deep learning, machine learning, noninvasive

Introduction

Anemia is a prevalent condition which stems from a low count of healthy red blood cells (RBC) or inadequate hemoglobin (Hb) concentration to deliver oxygen to the body's tissues and organs. In 2021, more than 1.9 billion people, constituting 24.3% of the population, were affected by anemia, resulting in 52.0 million Years Lived with Disability (YLDs) that represented 5.7% of all YLDs [1]. Depending on the patient's age and severity of blood shortage, this condition may cause various symptoms and ailments, including weakness, tiredness, shortness of breath, and tachycardia. Anemia diagnosis includes drawing venous blood (phlebotomy) and acquiring Hb, hematocrit (HCT), and RBC indices such as mean cell volume, mean cell Hb concentration, and reticulocyte count via complete blood count (CBC) test. Manifestations like forceful heartbeat, strong peripheral pulses, paleness of skin, and mucous membranes (in the case of Hb < 8–10 g/dL (80–100 g/L)) can be observed through a physical examination on regions with vessels close to the surface, such as the mucous membranes, nail beds, and palmar creases [2]. Undiagnosed or untreated anemia can evolve into more severe conditions such as symptomatic chronic heart failure, prematurity and stillbirth in anemic pregnant women, and even higher odds of depression in adult men and women [3, 4, 5]. These results imply the importance and value of early detection of anemia and its variations.

The accurate and precise measurement of blood analytes necessary for anemia diagnosis, mostly Hb, requires laboratories equipped with quality‐controlled hematology analyzers, related reagents and calibrators, along with trained laboratory personnel. The analyzers usually measure Hb via the gold standard cyanmethemoglobin method, which is rather inexpensive and reliable but requires potassium cyanide as the reagent which brings toxicity and difficult safe disposal process at high concentrations [6]. Consequently, reliable assessment of anemia in resource‐limited settings and deprived areas, where this condition is also more prevalent, is costly and challenging. Therefore, there is a necessity for easy‐to‐use and low‐maintenance tools or devices that offer acceptable performance for anemia detection or screening in such areas. Furthermore, phlebotomy is usually too painful and difficult to perform on patients with low pain tolerance like infants, or those with fragile veins like seniors, posing adverse effects for these groups. Invasive procedures are also a potential way to spread communicable diseases and infections, therefore, strict guidelines have been set to enhance the safety of phlebotomy and the quality of the specimens [7]. In conclusion, a considerable opportunity for developing noninvasive procedures exists, especially for the case of prevalent anemia.

Early detection and treatment of low RBC count or Hb concentration can prevent the complications from deteriorating and this has been shown possible thanks to the applications of ML for noninvasive, low‐cost, and rapid diagnosis [8]. The benefits of ML in medicine have become evident just like other areas of utilization in recent years. Reviews have presented evidence on the practicality of noninvasive approaches for detecting anemia. For example, Barulina et al. [9] presented a concise description of contributions to noninvasive anemia diagnosis using deep learning (DL) specifically. The researchers categorized methods based on input data sources such as the eye's conjunctiva and retinal fundus, electrocardiogram (ECG) and photoplethysmogram (PPG) signals, and the finger's nail bed and tip. They investigated some of the adopted DL techniques to enhance performance. Asare et al. [10] conducted systematic research on employing ML algorithms for anemia detection regarding medical images. The researchers included studies with sufficient data set size and imagery data acquired from either the tongue, eye's conjunctiva, palpable palm, or the fingernail. The research by Mohamed et al. [11] also focused on images particularly obtained from the conjunctiva, fingernails, and palm, and compared various ML and DL algorithms across different body sites. Furthermore, Dimauro et al. [12] conducted a comprehensive survey akin to ours, utilizing the Population, Intervention, Outcome (PIO) paradigm to illustrate trends in acquisition sites and devices over the years, which were toward the eye conjunctiva and camera or smartphone, respectively. However, the analysis included studies up to mid‐2020. Another analogous research by Hasan et al. [13] investigated the recent contributions in Hb estimation via cellphone. This state‐of‐the‐art review comprised invasive and minimally invasive procedures besides noninvasive ones, although merely smartphone‐based. The researchers categorized techniques in terms of the signal spectrum, body site, sensor type, processing method, and ML algorithm.

Although there have been valuable studies reviewing the employment of DL algorithms, imagery data, and smartphones, they solely focused on ANNs, visual features acquired from specific parts of body, and one category of end‐user devices. Therefore, we seek to further incorporate the most recent studies which involved other ML algorithms or types of input data and capturing devices. This study aims to provide a well‐developed and comprehensive review of diverse contributions in noninvasive anemia diagnosis and encompasses different aspects of ML usage in this matter. Materials and Methods

The present study was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) checklist to record and identify different stages of the literature search process and ensure the eligibility of included manuscripts. The search strategy, selection criteria, tools utilized for data extraction and management, and the variables for which data were sought are listed and defined throughout the following sections.

Search Strategy

Three scientific databases including PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus were searched systematically on March 27, 2025. The tailored search strategies were composed for each database using Boolean operators and keywords associated with four semantic categories: AI or ML, Anemia or Hemoglobin, Diagnosis, and Noninvasive. The studies between January 1, 2019 and March 27, 2025 were retrieved. The resulting queries for each database are listed in Table 1.

Following manuscript retrieval, two reviewers (H. G. and H. G. B.) independently assessed the materials for eligibility. The inter‐reviewer agreement was almost perfect (Cohen's κ = 0.94). A study was excluded if any of the mentioned criteria were not satisfied. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus and consultation with the third reviewer (N. M.). We used EndNote X8 for reference management and screening, and Microsoft Excel 2016 to summarize and organize the key facts of each paper as a spreadsheet. The variables that were considered as columns are further described. Furthermore, we used the Python programming language with Pandas (version 2.0.0) and Matplotlib (version 3.7.1) libraries to retrieve, visualize, and interpret the gathered information.

Selected Materials

A total of 1923 records were retrieved from the databases. Following duplicate removal, 1003 records were discarded, resulting in 883 unique ones. The remaining literature underwent the screening process based on title and abstract which excluded the majority of studies, with 102 of them being preserved. Next, the eligibility of each record was evaluated based on the criteria. Finally, 58 studies were included in the qualitative synthesis. Quality Appraisal

Following the screening and selection of eligible publications, they were further assessed for Risk of Bias (RoB) and applicability via the Prediction model Risk Of Bias ASsessment Tool (PROBAST) [14]. This tool is designed for the appraisal of prediction model studies in terms of RoB and applicability to the intended population and setting. The PROBAST can be utilized for research in developing, validating, or updating either diagnostic or prognostic models; hence, it suits the type of studies targeted by this review. The checklist includes 20 signaling questions across 4 domains (participants, predictors, outcome, and analysis), allowing for assessment from each specific aspect. The RoB assessment was conducted by two independent evaluators (H. G. and H. G. B.). In the event of a disagreement on responses to signaling questions, it was handled through consensus. In general, we regarded a domain as having a high RoB if at least two undesirable answers to signaling questions had been recorded, unless there was a major cause of RoB.

Data Items

Throughout the literature review, multiple variables or outcomes of each work were selected for data extraction. These items were each given a column in the spreadsheet in an order based on the CRISP‐DM methodology [15]. Each phase of this reference model, along with the associated characteristics, was considered for tabulating literature information. The selected data items and their corresponding CRISP‐DM phase are further described and depicted in Figure 2.

The data items regarded for summarizing the included literature, grouped based on the phases of the Cross‐Industry Standard Process for Data Mining (CRISP‐DM) framework. Each orange node represents a CRISP‐DM phase, while the surrounding blue nodes specify the corresponding data items.

The Business/Problem understanding stage emphasizes comprehending the needs of potential end users and transforming them into questions to be answered by Data Mining (DM) solutions. The main objective of an original study can be solving the discovered problems by this phase. Therefore, we devised the Aim and Study population variables to grasp the purpose in terms of the target problem and subjects. To capture the Data understanding related information, we placed the Data source which is the type and origin of input data (e.g., image, PPG, text), the Body part they acquired from (e.g., conjunctiva, fingertip, palm), and Sample size. Furthermore, the Smartphone‐based data collection variable was considered to determine if a paper used a cellphone camera to record the input signal. Besides cellphones, some studies designed and manufactured a Portable stand‐alone Device for data collection, which is also documented. The steps from preprocessing raw data to the final feature extraction are discussed in the Data preparation phase. The methods proposed by the literature for this stage are summarized as multiple variables. Preprocessing techniques comprise the utilized augmentation, enhancement, and feature extraction and selection methods. Input features that resulted from the preprocessing and were fed into the algorithm. We also regarded a few items related to a subset of studies, including whether image RoI extraction was performed automatically or manually and the type or wavelength of the light source. The process of model selection, hyperparameter tuning, and training happens in the Modeling phase. Rolling back from this phase to Data preparation is possible since various algorithms accept different data types. For storing this kind of information, we first devised an Applied algorithm(s) column containing the name of any learning algorithm implemented by a study. Then, we stored the specifications of Hardware and Software tools employed to train and evaluate ML algorithms. Additionally, explanation methods or ablation studies performed to understand the model mechanism are recorded under the Model explanation techniques column. Next, the Evaluation phase comprises a thorough assessment of the developed model, and it is important to ensure the obtained metrics meet the study's objectives. Finally, researchers decide whether to keep the DM method based on depicted performance. To capture the information related to this phase, we first listed all the evaluation criteria used by literature under the Performance measures column. Then, the Best performance measures acquired on the test or validation set along with the Best model and associated input data were documented. Lastly, the purpose of the Deployment phase is to implement a usable product based on the resulting model within the decision‐making process of the consumer. The studies that deployed the model as a Mobile app were discerned by this variable.

Based on the task column in Table 2, the majority of researchers (33 studies) focused on regressing the continuous total Hb value (SpHb). Nonetheless, classification as anemic or non‐anemic was under attention in 22 studies. Further, three studies conducted both classification and regression experiments.

Regarding the sample size column, the highest number of samples belongs to the Sarsam et al. [56] work on English tweets. From the imagery data aspect, Babenko et al. [36] included the greatest number of records which are whole‐eye images. Moreover, the study by Mitani et al. [73] ranks high regarding the quantity of participants. And the most extensive nonimagery data collection belongs to Kwon et al.'s [64] study on ECGs.

The number of papers that specified the distinct wavelengths of their light source is depicted for each band in Figure 5. Most studies experimented with lights in the near‐infrared spectrum, and some focused on higher wavelengths of the visible spectrum. There are three noticeable peaks at 660, 850, and 940 nm, while other wavelengths were analyzed by one to three studies each.

The distribution of studies based on light source wavelengths used for data acquisition.

There are studies in which the source of illumination covers a continuous spectrum rather than discrete values; hence, they were not included in Figure 5. For instance, Banerjee et al. [37] captured absorbance values of 450–650 nm with a resolution of 0.47 nm from the neonates' nail bed via a CCD‐based spectrometer. Wang et al. [50] study covered the broadest range by utilizing a halogen lamp with an emission spectrum of 300–1770 nm to derive dynamic spectral data. Additionally, some studies relied on ambient light to illuminate the acquisition site, which was either the conjunctiva, fingernail, or palpable palm.

Various body parts were investigated by the literature for noninvasive data acquisition and anemia diagnosis. The number of studies per body part is featured in Figure 6. Some studies assessed multiple body regions for noninvasive anemia detection. The research by Sarsam et al. [56] is not included in Figure 6 since it analyzed textual data.

Number of studies based on the body site from which they acquired data.

The eye is the most frequent data origin examined by 26 studies, which all captured images. Within this group, the conjunctiva is the most preferred region, subsequent to the retina, sclera, and external eye. In the second place, finger was investigated in 25 studies. These studies usually recorded the PPG signal, followed by the image and video. The third place belongs to palpable palm with 7 studies, in which 4 captured images [22, 32, 33, 35], 1 recorded video [45], 1 measured the transmitted Near Infrared (NIR) light [21], and another recorded an electromagnetic signal from the palm ulnar artery [68]. Other body sites and signals have been considered in one or two papers each, including images of lip mucosa [46, 47], ECG from the heart [64], Magneto Plethysmogram (MPG) from the radial artery [59], and textual information posted on the Twitter platform [56]. Moreover, some researchers obtained and investigated multiple signals simultaneously, such as images of conjunctiva and fingernail [53], reflectance signals from fingertip and thigh [54], and images of conjunctiva, fingernail, and palpable palm [22, 35].

Figure 7 depicts the preferences for evaluation criteria in classification versus regression problems.

The frequency of performance measures reported in the literature for classification and regression tasks.

According to the above figure, the top 3 metrics for assessing the model's ability to categorize anemia status are sensitivity, accuracy, and precision. On the contrary, Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), Mean Square Error (MSE), R 2, and R scores are the most common criteria for evaluating a Hb regressor model. However, there are metrics such as AUROC that have applications in both tasks.

A total of 29 studies collected the data samples via smartphone. The most frequently targeted body areas by these experiments include: 1—eye's conjunctiva, 2—fingertip, 3—eye's sclera, and 4—palm. In terms of data type, the most frequent choices are: 1—image, and 2—video which was captured in lengths of 10, 15, 30, or 100 s from the palm [45] or fingernail, which transformed into PPG signals [24, 29, 40, 52, 58, 61, 62]. Furthermore, 11 studies deployed the resulting ML model as an app to offer convenient noninvasive anemia detection or Hb value estimation. Nearly all of the proposed applications acquired data via the cellphone camera [23, 30, 34, 39, 45, 48, 49, 53, 60, 63], except for Munadi et al. [55], which built a portable Internet of Things (IoT) prototype to read the oxygen saturation (SpO2) signal by a MAX30100 sensor from the tip of the index finger. The signal was transmitted by an ESP8266 Wi‐Fi module over the HTTP protocol to a ThingSpark database, which was then processed by employing Feedforward Neural Network (FNN) and K‐Nearest Neighbors (KNN) algorithms. A user can further monitor the Hb value through the Android app after fetching the computed value from the remote online database.

Moreover, 13 papers considered assembling their own gear for data collection, which are outlined in Table 3. Almost all of these studies targeted Hb estimation besides Aiwale et al. [19], which classified the presence of anemia.

Some researchers specified the hardware infrastructure for modeling, which varied depending on the type of learning algorithm. Aiwale et al. [19] and Mitani et al. [73] trained deep Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) with the help of Google Tensor Processing Unit (TPU) accelerators. Other researchers mainly utilized NVIDIA Graphics Processing Unit (GPU). Chen et al. [23] and Xu et al. [30] opted for the RTX 3090 to train CNNs for segmentation and regression and run a reinforcement learning algorithm for tuning CNN, respectively. Likewise, Hu et al. Distribution of the software tools utilized in the reviewed studies for modeling.

The preprocessing operations range from basic signal processing (e.g., denoising, thresholding, normalization, cropping, resizing, and contrast adjustment) to more sophisticated techniques such as augmentation, over‐/down‐sampling, feature extraction/selection, color space transformation, and Region of Interest (RoI) detection. In total, 27 studies extracted RoI automatically, which was conducted mostly via image processing, and others utilized DL models for this purpose. These models include the UNet, You Only Look at Coefficients (YOLACT), You Only Look Once (YOLO), and Mask Region‐based Convolutional Neural Network (Mask R‐CNN) families.

Furthermore, there is a wide variability in input features, including image data, signal‐based features, and metadata. The features utilized by the literature can be categorized into color space channel values (RGB, CIELAB, and HSL), values of one‐dimensional signals (e.g., PPG, MPG, ECG), their first‐ or second‐order derivatives, information resulted from wavelet or Fourier transformations and their ratios, and biometric or demographic data (age, gender, BMI, blood pressure, and ethnicity). Furthermore, some researchers recorded video to predict Hb concentration. They mainly extracted tens of time‐domain features from the resulting PPG and its derivative. The intensity variations between palm video frames were utilized in one study [45] to compute time‐domain features like deoxygenation and reoxygenation times and slopes and were used along with age and gender, which resulted in 87 features in total. Another model [62] estimated glucose and creatine values besides Hb, given age, gender, and 27 features from the PPG signal, its 1st and 2nd derivatives, and the Fourier series.

Due to the incomprehensibility of the knowledge rules derived by the CNNs, some authors opted to undertake experiments manifesting the region of focus for the CNN model. Zhao et al. [57] presented importance heatmaps featuring regions that mostly contributed to the model's decision, which were generated by visual model explanation tools GradCAM [74] and Guided Backpropagation [75]. The added value of ultra‐widefield (UWF) ophthalmoscopy was further validated by training an extra model on center‐cropped fundus images. Experiment results depicted model attention toward the surroundings of the optic disc, retinal vessels, and peripheral retina made available by UWF images, implying the benefit of this modality. Using GradCAM, Kato et al. [25] concluded the model's higher attention to the lower marginal areas of conjunctiva for accurate Hb value prediction. Likewise, Mitani et al. [73] captured saliency maps via gradient‐based visualization techniques, including GradCAM, Smooth Integrated Gradients [76, 77], and Guided Backpropagation. They revealed the model's tendency toward the optic disc and the branching blood vessels in fundus images, which was a similar conclusion to that of Khan et al. [16], who utilized GradCAM. The explanatory value of these methods was further extended to nonimagery data in the study by Kwon et al. [64]. The researchers applied GradCAM [74] to ECG signals and suggested that CNN focuses mostly on the QRS complex to detect anemia. The model also focused on T‐wave and P‐wave regions, albeit their impact was less compared to that of the QRS complex. Another way to evaluate the importance of different image parts in label prediction is through ablation studies. In the work of Babenko et al. [36], the external eye photograph underwent ablation experiments by masking each specified area. They showed that other regions also contribute to the detection performance along with the pupil and iris. Images were further converted to grayscale and resulted in rather performance deterioration, implying the value of color information.

The ultimate performance varied considerably across studies. Among the common metrics for regression task, the most dispersion belonged to MSE (median: 0.655 (g/dL)2, IQR: 1.343 (g/dL)2), followed by MAE (median: 0.737 g/dL, IQR: 0.830 g/dL), and RMSE (median: 0.685 g/dL, IQR: 0.589 g/dL). Regarding the prevalent metrics for classification task, the most scattered one was precision (median: 97%, IQR: 11.26%), succeeded by sensitivity (median: 95.35%, IQR: 9.30%), and accuracy (median: 96.49%, IQR: 5.35%). In terms of the overall research quality, Table 4 depicts the PROBAST RoB and applicability assessment results per publication. The number of table rows exceeds the number of studies due to considering a separate assessment for the external validation of a study, as per PROBAST's instruction. Further, the percentage of each assessment category per domain across all papers is demonstrated by Figure 10, based on which 28 studies obtained a low RoB rating, 30 received a high RoB rating, and 3 demonstrated unclear RoB. Specifically, the RoB of participants domain was rated as low, unclear, and high in 59, 1, and 1 papers, respectively. The predictors domain achieved low RoB in all papers. The RoB of outcome domain was rated as low, unclear, and high in 52, 7, and 2 papers. And finally, the RoB of analysis domain was rated as low, unclear, and high in 31, 2, and 28 papers. Therefore, most of the high RoB studies were classified as such due to issues in the analysis domain. The applicability concerns were low across the majority of papers, except for two with unclear concerns in the participants and outcome domains, and another with high concerns in the outcome domain.

Discussion

The number of publications per year illustrated an overall growing trend except for the reduction in 2020, which may be caused by the COVID outbreak during this period and the shift of the research community toward this disease. Further, the lower number of publications in 2024 may be due to saturation in this field and the already published near‐ideal solutions.

In terms of task, total Hb regression appealed more to the researchers. This finding indicates the higher benefit of the estimated Hb rather than anemia classification for end users and the fact that by simply thresholding the regressor's output, a classification can be performed. Covering a large range of Hb concentrations in the data set is key to building a regressor with enough generalization and predictive abilities [71].

Regarding data set size, the study on English tweets [56] comprised the largest sample size, which is reasonable regarding the low size of each sample and convenient access to a vast amount of them through the platform. Furthermore, well‐known NLP models are typically trained on data sets containing hundreds of thousands of samples [78]. Overall, previous research has identified the data set size as the main factor contributing to the model's performance and suggested increasing it through augmentation [10].

From the light source aspect, all of the research conducted on PPG signals captured light at low energy or in the NIR band. This decision is rational since certain wavelengths at these spectra are less absorbed by blood water and therefore can be detected [79]. Further, the prominence of the NIR spectrum and PPG for correlation with Hb in live tissues has been manifested [13].

The contributions of ML research in this field span across different types of algorithms and body parts. From the sclera and conjunctiva of the eye, face, tongue, and nail bed, all the way to palpable palm and foot, or ECG and PPG signals, and even Twitter comments have been evaluated for the potential in anemia detection or Hb estimation. This wide range of input data types demands various learning strategies, each tailored to a specific kind of data. Consequently, a wide range of ML models have been implemented to handle these signals appropriately. Including supervised regression and classification algorithms, and diverse ANN architectures. In some studies, the prediction of such models was further refined by combining them through ensemble learning schemes such as bagging, boosting, stacking, and voting. Similar to Barulina et al. [9] and Hasan et al. [13], the eye's conjunctiva was shown to be the preferred body region for anemia detection. The high prevalence of the eye as an acquisition site can be due to the high density of capillaries in this region, especially the palpebral conjunctiva, which results in high sensitivity and correlation with Hb changes. However, there are factors with a confounding effect on conjunctival features, including trauma, infections, changes in meibomian glands due to aging, dry eye, and heavy makeup [39]. Therefore, the anterior ciliary artery region of the eye was suggested as being less prone to these factors [43]. The whole eye [36] or retinal fundus [57, 73] images provided promising performance but required extra imaging equipment. Moreover, the fingertip is a convenient source widely used to capture PPG signals, albeit ethnicity, skin color, body temperature, and nail thickness may interfere with the signals acquired from the finger or palm [42]. Nonetheless, the nail bed was stated as being less susceptible to skin tone variations as opposed to weight [28, 37]. Yet the pressure on the finger must be controlled, and any trivial movement or excessive pressure may interfere with the signal and damage the performance. So, the participants should stay still as much as possible for proper readings. This issue has been addressed in some studies by developing a mechanism for standard pressure application and release on the fingernail [40] and palm [45], albeit with limited use cases, which suggests an opportunity for further contributions. A distinguished data source, lip mucosa, was first adopted by Mahmud et al. [46, 47], who took images and characterized lip mucosa as a more convenient and reliable feature extraction region to classify subjects, regarding the high vascularization in the oral mucous membrane and its detectable appearance related to anemia severity. The two contributions differ mainly in terms of ML algorithms. Researchers stated that the usage of cosmetics and cigarettes may hinder the optimal classification because of the induced changes in lip color and texture. Therefore, further encompassing smokers and lipstick consumers in the study population and devising counteractive processing techniques is advised. The comparison of different data acquisition regions [35, 54] and prediction based on multiple regions concurrently [53] appeared not to be explored enough.

The estimation of multiple blood analytes was investigated in some studies that utilized images or one‐dimensional signals from the eye or fingertip. These components include Hb, total serum bilirubin, and SpO2 [37]; Hb and glucose [61]; Hb, RBC count, and differential counts of neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils, and basophils [50]; Hb, iron, folic acid, and vitamin B12 [49]; albumin, aspartate aminotransferase, estimated glomerular filtration rate, urine albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio, calcium, thyroid‐stimulating hormone, Hb, white blood count, and platelets [36]; and Hb, HCT, and RBC count [73]. These multioutput regression models can help identify the cause from which anemia originated, such as iron or B12 deficiency. Furthermore, blood pressure and creatinine were found to potentially interfere with the predicted Hb level in the presence of renal disease [31]; therefore, the noninvasive estimation of these factors is proposed for future investigation.

Across the input modalities, such as conjunctival, palmar, fingernail, or fundus imagery and PPG, MPG, or ECG signals, the prediction targets (Hb or anemia status) are proxied through a set of latent physiological properties that extends beyond anemia. The tissue's chromophore composition, derived from concentrations of oxyhemoglobin, deoxyhemoglobin, melanin, and bilirubin, affects the reflected or transmitted spectra in skin, mucosa, and ocular surfaces. Any condition that disturbs blood perfusion or tissue oxygenation will alter this optical spectrum. In terms of cardiovascular hemodynamics, PPG/ECG features reflect changes in stroke volume, peripheral resistance, pulse transit time, and rate variability. These factors serve as general signs of cardiopulmonary health and are not unique to anemia. Defining noninvasive Hb estimation as a function approximation over these properties suggests transferability to related issues (e.g., dehydration, shock, hypoxemia, dysautonomia), contingent upon addressing domain shifts (skin tone, lighting, device optics) during the Data Understanding and Preparation stages of CRISP‐DM.

In terms of evaluation criteria, most researchers took sensitivity into account while evaluating classifiers. This further implies the prioritization of avoiding missed diagnoses, since the proposed systems aim for initial screening, and the decisive detection of suspected anemic patients will be done via more thorough subsequent examinations [42]. The studies predominantly opted for discrimination (classification) and regression metrics, with calibration not being efficiently utilized. This indicates that researchers mostly evaluate the model's ability in detecting anemia or estimating Hb while overlooking the match between the predicted probability and actual observed frequencies of anemia. The older statistical calibration methods may have been overshadowed by modern DL. Furthermore, neural networks are often highly confident with poorly calibrated output probabilities, which necessitates extra processing. Nonetheless, anemia detection is primarily a rigid task, and the model needs to output a binary value; hence, researchers may consider metrics such as AUROC or accuracy are sufficient to prove the model's capability. On the other hand, a well‐calibrated model provides significant advantages in resource‐limited settings, where a specialist must allocate scarce resources, such as laboratory blood tests or iron supplements.

Considering the role of mobile health (mHealth), the smartphone camera served as an easy‐to‐access and frequent device for capturing both the image and PPG signal resulted from the video. The proposed mobile applications mainly employed the built‐in camera, while external IoT devices that paired with the cellphone have not been studied enough. Therefore, a thorough investigation and development of this type of IoT device may be more reliable by providing dedicated hardware. The use of cellphones or customized gadgets minimizes hardware barriers, improves cost‐efficiency, and facilitates clinical adoption. Although effective clinical deployment requires consistent signal acquisition, robustness across skin tones and ages, and integration into existing workflows. Moreover, the regulatory framework should be reinforced by predefining the intended use (screening vs. diagnostic aid) and validating real‐world generalizability.

In terms of target population, studies targeted various age groups, including anemic and healthy individuals, and some acquired data from patients with specific clinicodemographic profiles. Researchers have dedicated a considerable portion of their contributions to pediatric subjects [22, 24, 26]. Five of them targeted children aged 6–59 months [20, 32, 33, 34, 35]. These studies captured images of the conjunctiva, palpable palm, and fingernail for anemia classification purposes. Albeit gathering data from minors below 59 months brought some difficulties, such as the risk of injury or infection and focusing the camera on the conjunctiva. Overall, these contributions proposed the palmar region as a more accurate and convenient area than the conjunctiva and fingernail for anemia classification. Since the signal obtained from the fingertip is sensitive to pressure changes, and taking an image from a child's conjunctiva is rather challenging. Therefore, the palm was demonstrated to be more reliable for this age group of children. Moreover, some experiments involved children aged 3–13 years [72], neonates aged 28–40 weeks [37], and infants [54, 61] to predict Hb value given the optical signal from the fingertip. Surgical patients were the population of interest in a few papers for Hb prediction based on palpebral conjunctiva images [23, 44, 51]. Preoperative or injured patients may benefit from systems that can be useful in urgent situations, and further research should be conducted on this topic. The target subjects in some studies encompassed pregnant women for predicting Hb value based on fingertip [40, 61, 69] and palm [45] regions. Nonetheless, the mere focus on this category was not explored enough. Moreover, diabetic patients were targeted for Hb estimation based on images of the eye fundus [16] or external eye [36]. The majority of the experiments were conducted on participants with various profiles and incorporated subjects from different age groups, genders, ethnicities, and health conditions.

It is worth mentioning that the underlying etiology was not specified in the majority of articles, which aimed to detect anemia broadly, regardless of the causative condition. Iron deficiency anemia and anemia of chronic disease are the two most prevalent subtypes, and their distinction is critical since their treatments differ [80]. Therefore, further modeling or review studies on the differential diagnosis of anemia subtypes would be valuable contributions.

The public data sets were the source of data samples for some analyses. The Eyes‐defy‐anemia [81] was frequently used for anemia classification based on the eye's conjunctiva [27, 42] or sclera [41]. This data set provides Hb concentration, age, and gender, along with segmentation masks for both palpebral and forniceal regions of the conjunctiva from Italian and Indian patients aged 19–88 years old. The images of this data set were captured by a smartphone. The UK Biobank is another public database that was utilized for Hb estimation based on retinal fundus images and patient metadata [73]. This large database resulted from a prospective study involving over half a million participants, which is accessible upon valid request [82]. The data samples were mostly collected privately by the research team, and there are a limited number of public repositories such as Eyes‐defy‐anemia. As a result, a significant contribution is to compose and release public multimodal data sets for noninvasive anemia detection.

Multimodal data were employed in several studies, typically by combining physiological signals with patient metadata as input features. From most frequent to least, these features included age, gender, weight, height, BMI, blood pressure, smoking status, ethnicity, and heart rate. Studies that conducted direct ablation or baseline comparisons confirmed the performance gains of the multimodal approaches in terms of AUROC and MAE [73], RMSE and PCC [69], AUROC [36] compared to their signal‐only counterparts. The common use of age and gender relates to the high correlation of these two features with Hb thresholds for anemia [83]. While spatial, chromatic, and signal‐based features successfully isolate the physiological manifestations of anemia, demographic variables calibrate the algorithm to the individual patient.

A considerable amount of research was conducted to develop DL solutions for anemia diagnosis. These studies employed various neural networks for RoI extraction, classification, or regression. The proposed strategies mainly comprised two stages: RoI localization and anemia detection. The former involved networks such as UNet [23, 25, 48], Mask R‐CNN [44, 51], YOLACT [30], and YOLO [24, 34] to extract the conjunctiva or fingernail area. The networks based on MobileNet [84], ResNet [85], and Inception [86] families stood out among other architectures for anemia detection. Some studies compared multiple CNN architectures for anemia detection [16, 18, 23, 47, 51] or RoI extraction [48]. The CNNs were mainly pretrained on the ImageNet [87] data set. The superiority of CNN compared to other ML algorithms was verified by some researchers [20, 35], although data scarcity can damage the performance of CNN more than non‐DL models [42]. A variety of signal types were processed by CNN, which ranged from images to ECG signals. Even though the Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) is ostensibly better suited for time series, the CNN with 12‐lead ECG and age and gender as inputs appeared superior [64].

Given that DL models' performance was on par with traditional ML models, the computational costs and black‐box nature of DL‐based models may not be justified. While both sets of algorithms attained near‐perfect scores, the DL models performed better in classification than in regression tasks when compared to traditional feature engineering and ML approaches.

The gold standard procedure for Hb measurement is the direct cyanmethemoglobin method conducted with a spectrophotometer [88]. It offers a reliable low error margin, which is essential regarding the sharp clinical thresholds for anemia. Across the studies, the median MAE was low from a mathematical perspective, but not necessarily in a clinical context. Because a model can predict a Hb level of 12.3 g/dL (normal) for a patient with a true Hb level of 11.5 g/dL (mild anemia), leading to missed care. Hence, the output of regression models at the decision threshold should be regarded with caution and interpreted as an interval rather than a precise value. The high dispersion of MSE is mathematically expected since it squares the error and is sensitive to outliers. The studies reported near‐optimal performance for the classification tasks, which is attributable to the relative simplicity of this task compared to regression. The higher average sensitivity may align with the fact that most studies prioritized this metric. Overall, we can conclude that considering the anemia detection as an end‐to‐end task can yield promising performance without the necessity of predicting and thresholding the Hb levels. These findings imply that many models performed well on average, with some suffering from prediction errors on harder samples. Nonetheless, considering the 46% high RoB in the analysis domain, primarily stemmed from the absence of optimism adjustment, the near‐optimal metrics might be inflated due to overfitting on validation data and should be verified via an independent generalizability assessment.

The noninvasive anemia diagnosis is a mature field with models offering highly promising performances. Yet the majority of studies relied on traditional supervised learning, and alternative learning paradigms such as unsupervised, semi‐supervised, reinforcement, and federated learning are less investigated. Although certain studies incorporated unconventional training methods, including reinforcement learning [30], unsupervised learning [56, 70], and generative models [18].

According to the quality appraisal results, nearly half of the articles exhibited high RoB, which largely stemmed from the analysis domain. The main methodological weaknesses of these studies were as follows:

The Lack of Optimism Adjustment

The absence of k‐fold cross‐validation, bootstrapping, or other similar methods contributed to high RoB in some studies. The issue may be a result of the time‐consuming nature of these techniques, particularly for DL models, as each fold may last too long. However, this computational burden should not justify ignoring optimism adjustment unless the data set is large enough to include as many data variations as possible in each set after a single train/validation/test split. Usage of diverse performance metrics and statistical significance tests further reduces bias in model evaluation.

Outcome Determination and Timing

Here, the outcome is defined as Hb value or anemia status. An unreliable outcome determination process and a long interval between outcome and predictor measurements raised RoB in some studies. This time interval should be as low as possible, especially for the task of noninvasive Hb estimation, since the Hb value can change from the time of blood sample acquisition to the moment of noninvasive signal collection. Nonetheless, the vagueness of the outcome measurement process hindered the reproducibility and questioned the validity of the ground truth data in some experiments. The consistency of the measurement process across the data sources must be further ensured and mentioned explicitly.

Limited Number of Outcome Events (Anemic Cases) Per Candidate Predictor Variable (EPV)

According to PROBAST, at least 10 outcome events are required per candidate predictor for a relatively low RoB. Nonetheless, this value was lower in some cases due to the scarcity of anemic participants or high dimensionality of the feature vector. Class imbalance can further decrease EPV, which is a well‐known issue in the medical domain. Its solutions include oversampling or undersampling of the minority or majority classes, respectively, and ML algorithms specifically designed to handle class imbalance. High feature dimensionality was especially noticeable in studies that extracted a large number of features and then conducted extensive feature selection, which is appropriate when the data set is comprehensive.

The unclear RoB was mostly associated with the outcome domain. The time interval between outcome and predictor (noninvasive signal) measurements and their order was occasionally unspecified, which made RoB unclear because of the potential impact of the predictor knowledge on the outcome determination and vice versa. This temporal gap raises uncertainty about the correlation between the ML models and true, real‐time hemoglobin levels. Another reason was the incalculable EPV due to the lack of information on the proportion of anemic participants. We should note that in studies that employed DL models, the exact number of predictors was rather implicit and could not be enumerated for EPV calculation. Consequently, EPV may not be suitable for judging the bias in such cases, and more appropriate indicators such as the number of trainable parameters relative to the number of all or positive samples should be considered.

In terms of applicability, the questionable reliability of the determined outcomes raised the concerns in one case. Further, the lack of information on the reference standard method for determining anemia status and the absence of details for participants' demographics, study setting, or dates resulted in two cases of unclear applicability.

During the quality assessment, the publications were also inspected for external validation, revealing that only a limited subset incorporated such validation. Models trained on curated data sets with restrictive inclusion criteria may underperform in various real‐world clinical environments. This observation underscores the necessity for increased efforts in gathering and employing extensive external data sets for the independent validation of model performance and generalizability assessment. This is especially significant due to the dependence on smartphones or inexpensive hardware, which adds further variability in data collection.

This illustrates the phases of CRISP‐DM in relation to the results observed for the corresponding data items. Moreover, the primary challenges faced by high RoB studies, as identified by PROBAST, were associated with particular stages of CRISP‐DM.

Conclusion

Anemia is a prevalent malady affecting nearly a quarter of the world's population each year. An undiagnosed condition can result in heart failure, premature birth, and stillbirth. The standard practice for diagnosis includes laboratory equipment, materials, and personnel who examine the invasively drawn blood sample. The manifestations of anemia on the body surfaces offer an opportunity for developing noninvasive procedures to detect this disease, which mostly benefits fragile patients and underprivileged areas. Recent years have seen extensive research on this matter, resulting in significant breakthroughs thanks to ML. The present study aimed to summarize these contributions in a structured way. The findings of this study could be enhanced through quantitative synthesis, such as meta‐analysis. Nonetheless, this was deemed beyond the scope of the current research regarding its type and is recommended as a future contribution.

Researchers encountered various limitations throughout experiments. Issues related to insufficient data set size appeared most frequently, which often stemmed from a lack of accessibility to extra resources or facilities required for handling large amounts of data. This issue can be addressed via data augmentation, transfer learning, cross‐dataset collaboration, and open data sharing initiatives. Additionally, we regularly encountered a lower number of anemic patients, resulting in class imbalance and low EPV. In these cases, some authors devised oversampling, undersampling, and data generation methods to augment the minority class. The limited population diversity was another common challenge, resulting from the exclusion of specific patient groups in some cases. Expanding eligibility criteria and collaborating with other institutions to enroll underrepresented populations can resolve this issue. Furthermore, some authors reported difficulties in data collection, particularly images, due to the challenges of accessing certain body sites or the need for specialized hardware. Hence, future studies can explore easier alternative imaging modalities. Confounding variables were frequent in some approaches, particularly those handling images, such as device variation, light conditions, camera quality or position, and imaging angle or distance, which can be alleviated by standardizing capture protocols such as employing fixed rigs, controlled illumination, color/gray cards, exposure/white‐balance lock, and RAW format. Developing accurately calibrated models that can assist in the triage of anemic patients is another valuable future contribution. Lastly, there is space for extra efforts in improving models' generalizability via external validation, employing new learning paradigms such as federated learning, and utilizing prospective data captured at different time points.
