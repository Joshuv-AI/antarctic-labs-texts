Transvenous embolization with detachable coils for a hypoglossal canal dural arteriovenous fistula: illustrative case

Dural arteriovenous fistulas (dAVFs) are described as abnormal connections between dural arteries and dural venous sinuses and/or cortical veins. dAVFs account for approximately 10%–15% of all intracranial arteriovenous malformations.1, 2 Hypoglossal canal dAVFs (HCDAVFs) are a subtype of dAVF that has also been referred to in the literature as dAVF of the inferior petrosal sinus, jugular bulb, marginal sinus, foramen magnum, or skull base.1 HCDAVFs are relatively rare, comprising about 4% of all dAVFs, and are mainly characterized by the involvement of the anterior condylar confluence (ACC) and/or the anterior condylar vein (ACV).1–5

As with most dAVFs, these lesions are most effectively described by their arterial supply, pattern of venous drainage, and location of the fistulous point. In HCDAVFs, the arterial supply most often comes from the neuromeningeal division of the ascending pharyngeal artery (APhA) and the transmastoid branches of the occipital artery (OA).6–9 Supply can be further supplemented by meningeal branches of the V3 and V4 segments of the vertebral artery and/or bilateral clival branches of the meningohypophyseal trunk.2–7 The fistulous point at the level of the ACV is a hallmark of HCDAVFs.2 Venous drainage patterns most commonly include anterograde drainage to the internal jugular vein (IJV) and/or vertebral venous plexus (type 1), orbital venous drainage via reflux to the cavernous sinus (type 2), or direct cortical drainage to perimedullary spinal or cerebellar pial veins (type 3).1, 2

The hypoglossal canal offers a uniquely complicated area for the formation of dAVFs as the ACC is known to have the potential for many complex venous anastomoses involving the orbit, the sinuses of the posterior fossa, and the vertebral venous system.1–3, 5–10 For diagnostic purposes, it is important to recognize that these anastomoses can create clinical presentations that are characteristic of fistulas in other locations, and in terms of treatment, they are a crucial factor when considering various approaches.2–10 The complex vasculature in this area contributes to variable clinical presentation, including pulsatile tinnitus (PT), orbital symptoms, hypoglossal nerve palsy, and intracranial hemorrhage.1, 2, 5–7

Treatment of these lesions is indicated by a high-grade fistula with orbital, cortical, or perimedullary venous reflux or intolerable clinical symptoms, such as PT.1, 8, 11–15 Based on supply/drainage patterns and access to the fistulous point, treatment and clinical cure can be achieved by surgery, endovascular treatment, radiosurgery, or a combination of these strategies.

Endovascular treatment, including transarterial and transvenous approaches, has demonstrated the highest rate of clinical cure in these lesions with low rates of morbidity and mortality.1–3, 10–15

Illustrative Case

A 74-year-old male with a past medical history of hyperlipidemia presented with complaints of significant fatigue, headaches, dizziness, and PT for 3 months. Brain magnetic resonance imaging revealed asymmetry at the left skull base. A diagnostic cerebral angiogram demonstrated a left-sided anterior HCDAVF, which was further classified as type I by both Cognard and Borden classification schemes.16, 17 Diagnostic cerebral arteriography revealed contralateral supply from the right APhA, which crossed the midline, providing supply via collaterals that ran across the basilar part of the occipital bone (Fig. 1A). Additionally, supply was noted from enlarged components of the posterior division of the left APhA (Figs. 1B and 2A and B). No supply was observed from the OA, the internal carotid arteries, or the posterior circulation (Fig. 1). Dyna computed tomography (CT) and venography demonstrated the fistulous connection at the level of the ACV (Figs. 3 and 4).18 Superselective catheterization of the left ACV via the left IJV was followed by coil embolization (Fig. 4).

A: Diagnostic angiography of the left common carotid artery, lateral view, reveals an HCDAVF supplied by the APhA neuromeningeal trunk (white arrows). B: Diagnostic angiography of the left external carotid artery, lateral view, reveals in more detail the supply to the HCDAVF via the hypoglossal division of the APhA (white arrows).

Superselective DSA of the left APhA, lateral (A) and frontal (B) views, reveals the fistulous connection at the level of the ACV (white arrow) with dominant drainage posterior to the occipital plexus as well as anterior to the IJV (black asterisk). Also note the supply of the posterior meningeal artery by the left APhA (dashed white arrow), the retrograde filling of the clival supply from the left ICA (dashed black arrows), and the cross-filling of the contralateral APhA (white asterisk).

Cone-beam CT angiography performed through injection of the left APhA and maximum intensity projection reconstructed with a thickness of 10 mm in the three planes (per Raz et al.19), demonstrating the fistulous connection (black arrows) at the level of the ACV in the hypoglossal canal.

Lateral unsubtracted (A) and subtracted (B) and frontal unsubtracted (C) and subtracted (D) venography studies demonstrate the fistulous point at the level of the ACV and anterograde drainage to the IJV as well as drainage toward the occipital plexus.

Transvenous coil embolization was performed under general anesthesia with femoral access. Following diagnostic angiography with a 5-Fr catheter, an 8-Fr catheter was positioned in the left IJV and navigated to the level of the left ACV. A Headway Duo (MicroVention) microcatheter was advanced into the fistulous component, and detachable coils were placed (Figs. 4 and 5).

Postembolization cerebral angiography of the left APhA (A and B) and the left common carotid artery (C) demonstrates coil placement in the fistulous pouch and successful occlusion of the previously visualized HCDAVF.

Intraoperative angiography confirmed successful occlusion of the HCDAVF following TVE with detachable coils (Fig. 5). The patient’s PT was resolved immediately postprocedure.

Our case has been reported in line with the CARE criteria.20

Discussion

Observations

dAVFs of the hypoglossal canal are a rare subtype of vascular malformation. Consideration of the complex vasculature in and around the hypoglossal canal and proximity to cranial nerve XII is integral to successful therapeutic planning.2, 3, 5–10 HCDAVFs can be treated with surgical ligation, endovascular techniques, and stereotactic radiosurgery, in combination or alone. Surgical ligation is associated with comparable rates of angiographic occlusion to endovascular techniques but results in lower rates of clinical cure and carries considerable morbidity and mortality risks, including hypoglossal nerve injury.1, 2, 14 Surgical ligation might be most pertinent in cases in which fistulas with a type 3 drainage pattern have eloquent or poorly accessible arterial feeders and/or cortical drainage veins.1, 2, 14

Endovascular treatment encompasses both transarterial and TVE with liquid embolic agents, detachable coils, or both. Transarterial embolization (TAE) for dAVFs located in the posterior fossa or skull base is associated with higher rates of recurrence, risk of cranial neuropathy given adjacent vasa nervorum and complex external carotid–internal carotid anastomoses, rerouting of venous outflow, and potentially inadequate distal access due to small vessel caliber or tortuosity.2–4, 8, 9 When there is sufficient and nontortuous transvenous access to the fistulous point, TVE is likely preferable to TAE for HCDAVFs.1–3, 10–15 TVE via the ipsilateral IJV for HCDAVF has demonstrated high rates of angiographic occlusion and clinical cure.1–4, 14 The current case is a variant of the previously described superselective transvenous approach.21 Complications associated with TVE with detachable coils include transient or permanent hypoglossal nerve palsy, potentially due to mass effect on the nerve from the coil mass in the fistulous pouch.2–4 For both TAE and TVE, reflux into feeding arteries and direct neurotoxicity of dimethyl sulfoxide solvent in Onyx to the lower cranial nerves are possible.3, 15

Radiosurgery alone has shown high rates of angiographic occlusion but low rates of clinical cure.1, 2, 14 In cases such as the one presented here, where uncontrollable clinical symptoms and not high-grade lesion classification have indicated treatment, treatment with a low clinical cure rate is not favorable.

Lessons

In our case, treatment of this Borden type I and Cognard type I lesion was indicated by the intolerability of the patient’s PT and nonspecific symptoms, including headache. Sufficient access to the fistulous point by transvenous route facilitated the choice to use detachable coils over liquid embolic agents and ruled out the need for surgery. As confirmed by intraoperative angiography, TVE with detachable coils provided both successful angiographic occlusion of the lesion and clinical cure with no complications.

Selecting the appropriate treatment strategy for the successful treatment of HCDAVFs is predicated on a comprehensive understanding of the anatomical features of the lesion, namely arterial feeders, venous drainage patterns, and location of the fistulous connection. We present a case in which TVE with detachable coils via the ipsilateral IJV successfully occluded the previously visualized shunt to the ACV and provided a clinical cure for the patient’s PT and headaches without complications.

Limitations include those inherent to case reports and retrospective study design.
