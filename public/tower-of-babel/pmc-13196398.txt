Tailoring virus-inspired nanoparticles for advanced drug and gene delivery
Authors: Ruilin Zhou, Miao Xu, Xuting Zheng, Jiabin Xu, Jiatong Li, Huinan Tong, Chunyang Zhao, Jiali Han, Longlong Luo, Heran Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractViruses, as naturally evolved gene delivery systems, exhibit exceptional efficiency in overcoming biological barriers, targeting specific cells, and inducing robust immune responses. Inspired by these characteristics, virus-mimicking nanocarriers have become a promising strategy for enhancing drug and gene delivery. This review provides a detailed exploration of virus-inspired nanodelivery systems, with particular emphasis on their structural and functional biomimetic properties. We first focus on natural viral delivery platforms, such as viral nanoparticles and virus-like particles. Then, we discuss the application of biomimetic strategies that mimic viral morphology and topological structure in overcoming multiple physiological barriers in the body. Particularly, we examine bioinspired strategies that mimic key viral infection functions, including overcoming physiological barriers, cell adhesion and surface recognition, membrane fusion and cellular internalization, environmental perception and endosomal escape, intracellular trafficking and organelle targeting, and immune modulation. Additionally, we highlight the integration of intelligent strategies to optimize delivery efficiency. Finally, we discuss the current challenges and future opportunities in the field, aiming to advance virus-inspired nanomedicine toward clinical applications.Keywords: Virus-inspired nanoparticles, Drug delivery, Gene therapy, Viral nanoparticles, Virus-like particles, Immune modulation, Targeted delivery, Biomimetic nanoparticles

Introduction

The concept of viruses was first formally proposed in the late 19th century, although recorded cases of viral infections including smallpox, rabies, and poliomyelitis date back to ancient times [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Viruses have long been viewed as highly pathogenic, capable of rapid spread among populations. The COVID-19 pandemic has once again underscored the formidable power of viruses, as nanoscale biological entities (20–300 nm) composed of a genetic core enclosed by a functional protein shell [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. These viruses are adept at delivering genetic material into host cells, to support their replication and the production of new viral progeny [9]. Moreover, through long-term evolution, viruses have developed into potent inducers of immune responses, triggering antigen-specific immunity that further highlighting their complex interactions with the host immune system [10,11].

Recent advancements in nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems (Nano-DDSs) have highlighted their effectiveness in delivering a wide array of therapeutic agents [[12], [13], [14], [15]]. However, despite these advancements, current Nano-DDSs face persistent challenges, including limited ability to overcome biological barriers, inefficient biological recognition, rapid clearance from the bloodstream, and unsatisfactory clinical outcomes, highlighting the urgent need for novel approaches to enhance Nano-DDS functionality [[16], [17], [18], [19]]. Drawing on the structural characteristics, biological functions, and infection mechanisms, viruses have increasingly become an inspiring influence as a powerful source of inspiration for developing drugs and genes [[20], [21], [22], [23]]. Their natural ability to deliver cargo effectively, while navigating physiological barriers and targeting specific tissues and cells, has positioned them as potential candidates for drug and gene delivery platform design [24,25]. Therefore, virus-inspired strategies offer a promising solution for the design of advanced drug and gene delivery platforms [26,27]. As researchers continue to probe the questions: Why do viruses excel in transmission, invasion, and gene delivery? and Can we develop non-pathogenic drug/gene delivery systems that mimic these viral capabilities through biomimetic strategies? Answering these questions will pave the way for the advancement of drug and gene delivery platforms with great potential for further improvement.

Compared with traditional reviews on Nano-DDS, which mainly focus on the material optimization, simple functionalization, and direct utilization of viral morphology or components, this review explores various virus-inspired strategies for drug and gene delivery, delving into their underlying bionic mechanisms (TOC). We begin by examining the structural and functional properties of viruses, which serve as the foundation for designing virus-inspired Nano-DDS. First, we focus on natural virus-based Nano-DDS, including viral nanoparticles (VNPs), virus-like particles (VLPs), and nanocarriers constructed from virus-derived components. Next, we summarize how the structural parameters, including size, shape, and surface topological structure, influence Nano-DDS design. Emphasizing functional biomimicry, we explore systems inspired by key biological processes during viral infection, including overcoming physiological barriers, cell adhesion and surface recognition, membrane fusion and cellular internalization, environmental perception and endosomal escape, intracellular trafficking and organelle targeting, and immune modulation. Additionally, we highlight virus-inspired Nano-DDS that integrate both structural and functional bionics to enhance delivery efficiency. Lastly, we conclude by discussing the future challenges and opportunities in the development of virus-inspired drug and gene delivery systems. We believe that, by adopting the unique perspective of the complete viral infection pathway, this review provides an in-depth analysis of structure-activity relationships and underlying mechanisms, thus offering significant novelty and academic value.

Characteristics of viruses

The advent of advanced scientific tools, such as electron microscopy and electron tomography, has greatly enhanced our understanding of viruses, allowing for detailed insights into their composition and function (see Fig. 1) [28,29]. Viruses are composed of genomes (either DNA or RNA) encased in protein shells formed from capsid proteins, which are folded polypeptides [[30], [31], [32], [33]]. Some complex viruses also feature lipid membranes and glycoproteins, further complicating their architecture. Virions, the complete viral particles, may be viewed as nucleoprotein assemblies but exhibit significant diversity in their size, shape, molecular composition, and structural organization [34]. Common shapes include spherical forms, as seen in human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), filamentous structures like those of Ebola viruses (EV), rod-shaped configurations such as the tobacco mosaic viruses (TMV), and the distinctive bullet-shaped morphology of rabies viruses (RABV). Additionally, an ongoing outbreak, triggered by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), features the virus's distinctive corona resembling a crown [[35], [36], [37], [38], [39], [40]]. In addition to serving as a basis for viral classification, the morphological diversity of viruses is closely linked to their functional properties [[41], [42], [43]].

Structurally, viruses are exceptionally well-suited for efficient cargo delivery into host cells through a multi-step process involving binding, invasion, and release [[44], [45], [46], [47]]. The viral capsid is a dynamic structure that undergoes conformational changes during the viral life cycle, playing essential roles in infection [[48], [49], [50]]. The core, located at the center of the virus, harbors the genetic material necessary for replication, while the capsid protects the nucleic acid and directly facilitates infection by mediating binding to host cell receptors and initiating membrane fusion [51,52]. Upon infection, viruses exhibit high sensitivity to cellular recognition signals, allowing for the controlled release of genetic contents in response to defined environmental stimuli [53]. The viral infection process involves several key steps: binding to host cell surface receptors, entry via endocytosis or direct fusion, and release of the viral genome for replication [54]. Once inside the host cells, viruses undergo molecular transformations in response to changes within the intracellular milieu, triggering the release of their nucleic acid to initiate the replication cycle [[55], [56], [57]]. Furthermore, the highly immunogenic nature of viral capsids can stimulate the immune system, triggering both innate defense mechanisms and adaptive immune responses that contribute to controlling the invasion and aiding in the elimination of the virus [58].

Given the structural and biological features of viruses, they hold significant potential for applications in the biomedical field. For example, in cell engineering, viruses such as Sendai virus are used to aid in cell fusion, while in genetic manipulation, viruses serve as vectors for gene delivery, facilitating chromosomal integration in target cells [59]. Additionally, oncolytic viruses such as Newcastle disease virus, herpes simplex virus-1, reoviruses, and adenoviruses have been engineered to specifically recognize and eliminate tumor cells. These viruses take advantage of their capacity to produce therapeutic transgenes at elevated levels and to stimulate robust antitumor immune responses, making them powerful tools in cancer therapy [[60], [61], [62], [63], [64]]. Moreover, drawing inspiration from the natural functions of viruses in delivering RNA and other cargo, as well as their capacity to efficiently penetrate human barriers, researchers are increasingly turning to virus-inspired strategies. These viral properties offer valuable insights that can inform the construction of advanced drug and gene delivery platforms, leading to more efficient, targeted, and versatile therapeutic solutions.

Virus-based Nano-DDS

The development of virology and virus-inspired drug delivery technologies spans centuries, transitioning from early observations of viral diseases to modern therapeutic innovations. Virus-based Nano-DDS refer to sophisticated systems that harness natural viral components to mimic virus-like activities. These include VNPs and VLPs, as well as NPs that incorporate viral elements, such as surface ligands and capsid proteins. Recent advancements in this domain are discussed, highlighting their applications in targeted drug and gene delivery, immunotherapy, and the improvement of therapeutic outcomes across various medical fields (Fig. 2).

Characteristics of VNPs and VLPs

VNPs comprise a wide range of genetically encoded and versatile shells, including natural proteinaceous nanostructures derived from mammalian viruses, plant viruses, and bacteriophages [[65], [66], [67]]. These nanoparticles (NPs) may possess infectious or noninfectious properties [9,68]. In contrast, VLPs are non-genomic variants of VNPs, consisting of highly ordered and repetitive nanostructures that spontaneously assemble from various types of viral structural proteins [27,[69], [70], [71]]. VLPs retain the empty shell and structural characteristics of viruses, while lacking the ability to infect or replicate. Additionally, they can induce robust immune responses without possessing infection risk [[72], [73], [74]].

VNPs and VLPs are among the highly evolved and versatile nanoscale materials generated by natural processes. Their fundamental architecture can be “programmed” in various ways using genetic engineering and chemical modification techniques [75]. These particles can be manufactured using mature and scalable production platforms. Their internal cavities are capable of carrying drugs, imaging reagents, quantum dots, or various NPs [76]. Additionally, the outer surface can be functionalized with targeting ligands to enable cell-specific delivery. The amino acid sequences of coat proteins can also be reprogrammed, enabling the incorporation of additional functionalities via chemical conjugation [[77], [78], [79]]. This flexibility opens a wide range of possibilities, including enhanced biocompatibility, reversible binding of active molecules, and increased targeting efficiency, making them ideal candidates for medical applications, such as diagnostics, cancer therapies, gene therapies, immunotherapy, vaccines, imaging, and theranostics (Table 1) [94].

VNPs in gene therapy

Viruses have long been nature's "gene therapists" due to their ability to efficiently enter cells and deliver genetic material [95,96]. VNPs have gained considerable attention in gene therapy, which aims to alter gene expression or modify the biological properties of cells for therapeutic purposes. Repurposing VNPs for human gene therapy takes advantage of their uniform morphology, efficient cellular entry, biocompatibility, water solubility, and ease of functionalization [37]. Notably, VNPs such as adenoviruses, retroviruses, lentiviruses, and adeno-associated viruses (AAV) have been designed and utilized as vectors for the delivery of genes [[97], [98], [99]]. This has led to a variety of viruses being used as delivery vectors in current gene therapy applications, accounting for approximately 59% of the 22 approved in vivo and ex vivo gene therapies, with over 70% of gene therapy programs in clinical trials utilizing this platform [100]. Retroviruses, which replicate through a DNA intermediate, provide an excellent option for ex vivo gene transfer applications, as they integrate into the host DNA, ensuring long-term gene expression in dividing cells [101]. Adenoviruses, non-enveloped viruses with double-stranded DNA, mediate transient gene expression in a wide range of cells and feature simple purification and large transgene capacity [102]. AAV, non-enveloped single-stranded DNA viruses, exhibit high stability, high efficiency, scalable production and reduced immunogenicity compared with retroviruses, making them preferred vectors for gene delivery [103]. Lentiviral vectors, capable of delivering large gene fragments into both proliferating and non-proliferating cells, enable sustained gene expression and stable integration into the host genome [104].

Nevertheless, each platform has inherent limitations. Retroviral vectors suffer from low titers, instability, poor stability, and low in vitro transfection efficiency, as well as restricted tropism for non-dividing post-mitotic cells, thereby limiting their application to proliferating cells [105]. Adenoviral vectors may trigger strong immune responses due to their high immunogenicity [106]. AAV vectors are costly, have complex manufacturing processes, and are limited in their packaging capacity (∼4.5 kb). When integrated into both dividing and non-dividing cells, LVV may induce off-target mutations and pose oncogenic risks after integration into the genomes of both dividing and non-dividing cells [104]. Therefore, the selection of viral vectors for gene therapy must carefully consider these advantages and limitations to achieve optimal outcomes.

VLPs in immunotherapy

Immunotherapy, particularly for cancer, utilizes therapeutic vaccines to modulate the immune system [107]. VLPs, with their intrinsic immunostimulatory properties, are promising tools in cancer immunotherapy. Unlike chemically synthesized nanocarriers, VLPs offer advantages including their size, repeated surface structures, and capacity to stimulate both innate and adaptive immunity. They are also safer, as they cannot replicate, and present favorable biocompatibility and clinical translation potential [108]. For example, Ling et al. delivered CRISPR/Cas9 ribonucleoprotein (RNP) through VLPs, achieving neuronal cell-specific gene editing and treating refractory diseases such as Huntington's disease [109]. P22 phage-derived VLPs have been used to carry B and T epitopes from ovalbumin (OVA), significantly enhancing immune responses in cancer models [88]. Wu et al. demonstrated that conjugating the bacteriophage Qβ with a GD2 surrogate antigen 9-NHAc-GD2 boosted antibody responses, showing the promise of VLPs as carriers for anti-cancer vaccines [83]. Similarly, Alm et al. utilized VLPs based on Qβ to showcase diverse mannoside ligands, which could be efficiently captured by dendritic cells (DCs) and directed to endosomes, leading to a T helper 1-type immune reaction, typified by proinflammatory cytokine expression [84]. In addition, Zheng et al. developed a universal and modular VLP-based nanocarrier platform, Nov-S-Catcher003, by engineering the norovirus S domain fused with SpyCatcher003, facilitating antigen delivery using a plug-and-play mechanism via SpyTag003-linked antigens [110]. Functionalized with tumor antigens, these VLPs effectively promote dendritic cell maturation, migrate to lymph nodes, and elicit tumor-specific T-cell responses in mice, reshaping the tumor microenvironment and suppressing tumor growth, highlighting their potential in cancer vaccine development.

Advancements in functionalized VNPs and VLPs

Recent advancements in the functionalization of VNPs and VLPs have led to improved delivery capabilities. Diagnostic and therapeutic agents can be encapsulated into the core, or modified on the protein coat's interior and exterior surfaces through chemical or physical methods. Additionally, VNPs and VLPs can be conjugated with targeting ligands to achieve tissue-specific delivery. Shan et al. developed self-assembling hepatitis B core protein (HBc)-based VLPs that were able to encapsulate doxorubicin (DOX) without chemical modification. DOX-loaded HBc-based VLPs selectively targeted cancer cells via interaction with overexpressed integrin αvβ3 and exhibited potent tumor growth inhibition (90.7% TGI) and reduced cardiotoxicity in tumor-bearing mice compared to free DOX [111]. Furthermore, cargo encapsulation within VLPs typically involves disassembly of VLPs into subunits under extreme pH, temperature, or denaturation conditions, followed by reassembly with cargo packaging under renaturation conditions. These particles offer the unique advantages of a well-defined structure, targeted invasion, efficient intracellular transport, and activation of the innate immune system [112]. Consequently, thermal-sensitive VLPs represent a novel loading strategy in such cases. Lu et al. utilized HBc-based VLPs to load the lipophilic near-infrared dye, IR780, due to their ability to withstand temperatures up to 90 °C [113]. The encapsulation process relied on the potential channel opening between protein subunits at the denaturation temperature, thereby facilitating drug transport across the shell into the inner cavity while maintaining the structural integrity of the VLPs.

Rabies virus glycoprotein

Overcoming the blood-brain barrier (BBB) in drug delivery to the brain remains a significant challenge [123]. One promising approach is the use of NPs modified with rabies virus glycoprotein (RVG), which facilitates BBB penetration through receptor-mediated endocytosis [124]. Researchers have leveraged this property for brain tumor therapies. For example, Qiao et al. developed the metal-organic framework-based nanocarrier MILB@LR, which was coated with the outer membrane component of wild-type RABV for brain targeting [125]. RVG peptides can specifically target neural receptors and efficiently cross the blood-brain barrier, significantly enhancing the efficiency of drug delivery to the brain, offering distinct advantages in the diagnosis and treatment of neurological diseases. Cheng et al. developed an RVG-camouflaged gold yarnball (RVG@GY) to enhance brain tumor targeting through dual hierarchical pathways and RVG-mediated transport along the spinal cord [126]. Beyond cancer, the RVG peptide has shown promise in neurological disorders including epilepsy, Alzheimer's disease, and Parkinson's disease. Schlich et al. designed novel nanocarriers for systemic small interfering RNA (siRNA) delivery to downregulate neuronal α-synuclein, a core pathological hallmark of Parkinson's disease. These nanocarriers were conjugated with an RVG-derived targeting peptide, enabling efficient siRNA internalization in primary mouse hippocampal and cortical neurons. This approach achieved effective α-synuclein gene silencing without impairing cell viability [127]. This demonstrates the potential of RVG-decorated liposomes in Parkinson's disease mouse models. Currently, this technology is evolving toward intelligent responsiveness, multimodal theranostics, and long-circulating biomimetic carriers. However, challenges remain, including peptide degradation, short retention time in vivo, off-target effects, and poor preparation reproducibility, all of which require further optimization to advance clinical translation.

Other specific peptides

Viruses are adept at overcoming tissue tropism barriers during infection due to their preferential localization and exploitation of certain tissues or cell types within the host. As an example, hepatitis C virus and hepatitis B virus (HBV) primarily infect the liver; SARS-CoV-2, EV, and influenza virus target the respiratory tract; Nipah virus, Japanese encephalitis virus, and RABV can invade the brain; norovirus and rotavirus colonize. These virus-host interactions, driven by virus-specific peptides, hold significant potential in biotechnological and biomedical applications. Liu et al. engineered filamentous phages displaying the RGD peptide on their sidewalls to regenerate brain tissue in stroke-induced lesions [117]. These viral nanofibers, coated on injectable silk protein microparticles, promoted neural stem cell adhesion, proliferation, and neuronal differentiation in vitro [128]. When injected into stroke cavities in rat models, the nanofibers stimulated angiogenesis and neurogenesis, leading to limb motor function recovery. Additionally, TGN, a 12-amino acid peptide obtained through in vivo phage display screening, exhibits a strong affinity for the brain, particularly for the cerebral vasculature [129]. Zhao et al. constructed an HBc nanocage with the insertion of TGN to enhance the delivery of phenytoin, an antiepileptic agent, to the brain [130]. This nanocage exhibited specific brain targeting, enhancing the antiepileptic efficiency of phenytoin by approximately 100-fold in pilocarpine-induced epilepsy models. Both in vivo mouse and in vitro human neural three-dimensional cortical organoids studies showed high penetration ability, offering a promising strategy for treating refractory epilepsy and other intractable neurological diseases.

Structure-biomimetic Nano-DDS

Viruses represent a quintessential class of biomolecular assemblies, characterized by nanoscale dimensions and a precise three-dimensional structure that performs well-defined biological functions. As natural nanoscale building blocks, viruses are formed via molecular self-assembly and adopt compact, highly efficient architectures essential for viral infection and replication within host organisms [131]. The unique structural features of viruses regulate their interactions with the surrounding microenvironment, their preferential tropism toward specific organs and cell populations, and their crosstalk with the host immune system (see Table 4) [[132], [133], [134]]. Primarily composed of protein capsids and nucleic acid cores, viruses efficiently deliver genetic cargo into host cells with high specificity and accuracy during intracellular delivery [135,136]. Such inherent structural properties of viruses offer critical guidance for the rational design of Nano-DDS. By mimicking viral particle size, morphology, core–shell architecture, and spiky surface topography, researchers can greatly optimize the design and biological performance of Nano-DDS (Fig. 3).

Size

A major characteristic of viruses is their nanoscale size, typically ranging from 20 to 300 nm. This size precisely matches the permeability thresholds of biological barriers within the body, allowing viruses to avoid rapid clearance by the kidneys (<5.5 nm) while minimizing nonspecific phagocytosis by the mononuclear phagocyte system in the liver, spleen, and other organs. At the same time, it satisfies the particle size requirements for the enhanced permeability and retention effect of tumor tissues. By emulating viral size and morphology, researchers have developed nanocarriers with improved biological distribution and selective targeting abilities. Ultra-small virusoid NPs with particle sizes <10 nm mimic the structure of picornaviruses and other ultra-small viruses [139]. Although they exhibit excellent tissue penetration capabilities, they have an extremely short half-life, which hinders effective tumor accumulation [140]. Pseudovirus nanocarriers with particle sizes ranging from 10 to 50 nm align with the natural dimensions of small viruses such as adeno-associated viruses [139]. This not only avoids rapid renal clearance but also reduces nonspecific phagocytosis by the mononuclear phagocyte system, thus greatly improving blood circulation stability [141]. For instance, Lu et al. developed a biotin-avidin-system-based nanovaccine (42 nm). Such a particle size mimics the optimal delivery dimensions of viruses such as parvoviruses and adeno-associated viruses, effectively evading phagocytosis by the reticuloendothelial system and prolonging circulation time in vivo. By combining tumor-associated antigens with CpG ODN (an immunostimulant), the nanoparticle leverages size-mediated lymphatic accumulation to promote recognition and uptake by antigen-presenting cells, thereby enhancing tumor immunotherapy [142]. Pseudovirus nanocarriers with particle sizes ranging from 50 to 200 nm represent the optimal delivery size range for mimicking common viruses such as adenoviruses and herpesviruses [139]. This range is currently the most commonly used particle size range for virus-mimicking nanodrug delivery systems and is suitable for systemic tumor-targeted delivery [139]. Large-sized pseudovirus nanocarriers with particle sizes >200 nm mimic the structures of large viruses such as poxviruses [139]. They are easily retained and phagocytosed by the mononuclear phagocyte system, including the liver and spleen, resulting in shortened circulation time and poor systemic delivery efficiency [143].

Morphology

Moreover, virus morphology plays a distinct role in biological distribution and selective targeting capabilities. Lee et al. engineered gold nanorods (AuNRs) to mimic the RABV morphology and surface glycoprotein properties. By leveraging the morphological advantages of the rod-like structure with a high aspect ratio, they reduced nonspecific binding between the carrier and the cell membrane while enhancing specific interactions with receptors on the surface of neuronal cells. This facilitated cellular uptake into neuronal cells and induced a hyperthermal effect in response to near-infrared laser irradiation [144]. A series of studies have focused on imitating the icosahedral capsid protein structure of viruses to enhance the capacity of the immune system to recognize and internalize them [145,146]. For example, icosahedral hollow NPs were designed to simulate the viral structure. The virus-mimicking structure and repetitive surface epitopes endowed them with inherent immunogenicity, effectively activating the maturation and antigen presentation of dendritic cells, thereby making them suitable for tumor immunotherapy and recurrence prevention [147].

Core-shell structure

The core-shell structure of viruses serves a key function in improving gene delivery efficiency. The protein shell of viruses possesses a unique spatial conformation, surface charge distribution, and biomimetic morphology, enabling it to effectively withstand harsh external physiological challenges. Meanwhile, the hollow core possesses ample cavity volume and spatial stability, enabling it to efficiently encapsulate genetic material through electrostatic binding, hydrophobic interactions, and physical entrapment [148]. Zhong et al. designed a multifunctional polydopamine-doped virus-like structured NP (GNR@HPMO@PVMSN) to achieve PAI-guided chemo-/photothermal therapy (PTT) [149]. This innovative "ship-in-a-bottle" approach facilitated the synthesis of GNR core within the cavity, retention of rough surface features by virus-like mesoporous silica shell, and efficient drug loading in the interior space. Chen et al. fabricated Au@HSN-PGEA nanocapsules with a rattle-like rough structure and gold nanorod cores enveloped in polycationic mesoporous silica, which retains surface roughness and interior space for drug loading [150]. This design preserves surface roughness while providing internal space for drug loading. These findings underscore the indispensable value of virus-mimetic core–shell architectures for constructing multifunctional therapeutic platforms, broadening their practical utility in integrated diagnosis and treatment.

Spiky surface topography

A distinguishing feature of many viruses is their spiky surface topography. This rough, inhomogeneous surface topology enhances interactions with biological membranes, facilitating efficient entry into host cells. In many studies, researchers have simulated the roughness and irregularity of the virus surface to endow NPs with the ability to attach to epithelial cells (Fig. 4) [155]. Xu et al. developed rod-like mesoporous silica NPs (MSNs) with virus-like rough surfaces via the conjugation of negatively charged silica shell particles (13 nm) [156]. This design aimed to enhance cellular uptake and delivery efficiency, outperforming conventional MSNs with smooth surfaces. Niu et al. synthesized NPs with virus-like rough surface topography by grafting –NH2 groups and PEI monomers on the surface of MSNs [157]. Recently, Zhao's group pioneered the synthesis of virus-like MSNs with nanotubes formed radially on the surface, simulating spike proteins via a single-micelle epitaxial growth strategy [158]. These spike-structured NPs facilitated enhanced cell membrane binding, significantly improving cellular uptake and intratumoral penetration. The introduction of spike structures led to improved drug delivery performance and increased biological interactions, confirming the importance of topographical features in viral mimics for enhanced adhesion and cellular internalization. Adopting a similar synthetic strategy, Zhang et al. constructed spiky MSNs and partially coated the NP surface with gold nanoshells. This structure provided higher photothermal efficacy and a reduced contact area, and was applied to treat ocular infections induced by multidrug-resistant bacteria [159].

Leveraging the biomimetic topological structure of the spikes, the vaccine achieves highly efficient nasal-to-brain targeted delivery and bypasses the blood-brain barrier following intranasal administration. The virus-like spike morphology significantly enhances adhesion and retention in the nasal mucosa, elevates transepithelial transport efficiency, and reaches the brain lesion via the olfactory nerve pathway [163,164].

Functional-biomimetic Nano-DDS

During the initial stage of viral infection, it efficiently navigates multiple biological barriers to enter the host cell, where it connects to specific cell surface receptors, followed by internalization via endocytosis or membrane fusion. After escaping from endosomes, it undergoes a multistep biological transformation and releases its genetic material into the cytoplasm or nucleus of the host cell responding to the intracellular microenvironment. Meanwhile, the immune system is activated to defend against viral infiltration. Viruses have evolved mechanisms allowing them to exploit cellular pathways and mechanisms for replication and immune evasion, allowing them to persist and spread within the host. Similarly, NPs can be engineered to mimic these viral strategies, achieving precise therapeutic outcomes through enhanced targeting, cell entry, and overcoming barriers (see Table 5).

Overcome physiological barriers

In vivo drug delivery faces several complex biological and biochemical barriers, such as the BBB, mucus layer, and epithelial barriers. Certain viruses have acquired unique approaches for penetrating the physiological barriers and avoiding immune system recognition, providing valuable insights for designing advanced drug delivery systems (Fig. 5). As mentioned before, the BBB restricts approximately 98% of small-molecule drugs from entering the brain, but certain viral-derived proteins, such as RVG, can facilitate receptor-mediated transcytosis, enabling NPs to cross the BBB effectively. Additionally, by mimicking the bullet-like or rod-shaped structure of the rabies virus, NPs have the potential to cross the BBB and penetrate deeply into tumor tissues.

In the case of oral drug delivery, the mucus layer and intestinal epithelium represent major challenges. Viruses like the influenza virus exhibit hydrophilic surfaces with a combination of positive and negative charges, which help them navigate the mucus layer by minimizing electrostatic interactions. This viral property has been integrated into NPs to enhance their ability to penetrate mucus and achieve efficient drug delivery. For example, Zhang et al. fabricated MSN functionalized with inclusion of the cationic cell-penetrating peptide (KLPVM) and anionic glutaric anhydride, which reduced mucin binding and enhanced mucus penetration [168]. Inspired by the viral morphology, Sang et al. developed core-shell MSN (∼80 nm) with virus-like nano spikes (VSN), further modified with L-alanine (CVSN) to enable chiral recognition [169]. Their nano spikes enhanced bio-adhesion, mucus penetration, and transmembrane transport by anchoring to bio-membranes and facilitating chiral recognition. Yang et al. developed multifunctional ligand-switchable NPs (Pep/Gal-PNPs), which mimicked the unique surface properties of viruses and replicated the interactions between viruses and the host organism [170]. After oral administration, pH-activated extensible cell-penetrating peptide (Pep), resembling spike glycoproteins present on the viral envelope, assumed an extended conformation and protruded from the surface under acidic conditions in a virus-like manner, promoting successful traversal of intestinal barriers and subsequently reaching systemic circulation.

In the case of respiratory drug delivery, viruses like influenza viruses have evolved to interact with mucins in the mucus layer, enabling them to bypass mucosal barriers. Martins et al. developed a low molecular weight polyethylene glycol (PEG)-coated nanobromhexine lipid particle (NBL) platform for efficient pulmonary drug delivery, demonstrating mucoadhesive properties with a 60% mucin-binding efficiency and effective mucus layer penetration [171]. The NBL system mimicked virus-like receptor-binding and -cleaving activities through the dynamic binding of PEG with mucin, mimicking the interaction of viruses with mucin surface glycans, thus facilitating transport across the mucus and cellular uptake.

In addition to their ability to enter cells, viruses can also traverse tissues by moving from one cell to another. While artificial cell-penetrating peptides (e.g., polyarginine peptides, Pep-1, transportan) have been developed to mimic viral transduction domains (e.g., HIV Tat, HSV-1 VP22 proteins) for NP delivery across cellular barriers, they lack the capacity to achieve tissue-level penetration comparable to viruses [172]. This limitation underscores the potential of designing a more effective penetration strategy inspired by virions to enhance both cell entry and deep tissue infiltration. For example, Zhang et al. developed virion-mimicking membrane-disrupting NPs (MBNs) that achieve tumor-triggered dual penetration of cells and tissue [172]. They created dendritic prodrugs composed of arginine-rich peptides, mimicking viral protein transduction motifs and globular protein structures which self-assemble into virion-like NPs with highly ordered secondary structures. In another case, Su's group synthesized a viral glycoprotein-mimicking peptide, which contained a type II collagen adhesion motif and a cell-penetrating peptide sequence activated by matrix metalloproteinase. It mimicked the strategy of viral cell infection, enabling the Nano-DDS to actively target the cartilage matrix and be specifically activated in the microenvironment of diseased cells, making it highly efficient for the treatment of arthritis [173].

We then discuss the viral invasion processes, specifically attachment, recognition, and membrane fusion, which have significantly inspired the design of nanocarriers (Fig. 6). Initially, viral attachment to cell membranes is mediated by interactions with glycolipids or glycoproteins, which increases the density of viral particles on the cell surface and facilitates receptor recruitment [174,175]. These initial nonspecific, low-affinity interactions cause the virus to aggregate on the cell surface, enhancing the likelihood of binding and highlighting the potential for mimicking these strategies in the design of Nano-DDSs(Table 3). For example, Li et al. developed a virus-mimicking and GSH-responsive hollow mesoporous tetrasulfide-bridged organosilica (ssss-VHMS) to deliver NaCl nanocrystals to tumor sites [176]. The ssss-VHMS invaded tumor cells via spike surface-assisted endocytosis, bypassing Na/K-ATPase transmembrane ion transporters. Lv et al. investigated nanosized oncolytic viral light particles (L-particles), which lack nucleocapsids but retain viral envelopes and tegument proteins. These NPs were designed to optimize interactions with multiple receptors or specific adhesion molecules on cell surfaces, thereby reducing off-target effects and enhancing internalization efficiency [177]. The viral L-particles naturally recognize and enter tumor cells through interactions between their intact viral envelopes and receptors on the plasma membrane of tumor cells, leveraging the virus's inherent infection mechanisms without the need for additional modifications. Wu et al. developed gene-core protein-shell NPs [178]. By simulating the surface binding of viruses and cell membranes, it achieved complete biological shielding and targeted localization of safe, selective and efficient expression of TRAIL plasmids (pTRAIL) transfection, thereby achieving efficient tumor suppression. In another example, the authors assembled EphA2 and CD13-targeted peptides to self-assemble and construct VLPs for efficient encapsulation of siRNA [179]. Peptides rich in arginine simulated the structure and entry mechanism of viruses, induced the formation of filamentous feet, increased the number, length and membrane coverage of filamentous pseudopodia, strengthen NP–cell membrane interactions, and promote efficient siRNA transfection, improving the cellular uptake by 10.9 times.

Meanwhile, the receptor-mediated endocytosis model is a multistep process essential for viral recognition and hijacking of the host endocytic machinery [180]. Figueroa et al. addressed the challenge of poor drug availability in targeted tissues by developing virus-mimetic NPs capable of highly specific cell recognition [181]. They engineered NPEXPAng-I NPs to mimic the viral recognition process, involving three consecutive checks to enhance specificity and uptake in mesangial cells. These NPs were designed with an angiotensin-II type 1 receptor ligand for initial receptor attachment, an angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) for proligand activation, and an agonist to facilitate cellular internalization, demonstrating exceptional receptor avidity and specificity in vitro and in vivo and achieving 5- to 15-fold higher accumulation in the kidney mesangium. Similarly, drawing inspiration from the adenovirus type 2 infiltration strategy, Fleischmann et al. engineered hetero-multivalent block-copolymer NPs capable of sequential ligand-receptor interactions to enhance the targeting efficiency for glomerular mesangial cells [182]. These NPs were functionalized with a flexible angiotensin-II type 1 receptor antagonist to facilitate initial binding and a concealed αVβ3 integrin agonist to mediate subsequent endocytosis. These findings suggest that mimicking viral multistep recognition processes can significantly enhance NP targeting and accumulation in specific cells, paving the way for efficient drug delivery in renal diseases. In addition, targeted modification of viral surface proteins can significantly enhance specific cell-surface recognition ability for hard-to-transfect target cells. The Biasco team optimized the envelope protein of the baboon endogenous virus to enable infection of quiescent hematopoietic stem cells and modified the protease cleavage site to promote efficient membrane fusion [183]. Through genetic engineering on VLP surfaces, molecules capable of specifically binding to the integrin receptors (VLA-4 and VLA-5) on the surface of HSPCs were displayed. This strategy can be applied to in vivo gene therapy for hemoglobinopathies such as sickle cell disease.

Membrane fusion and cellular internalization

Upon binding to cell surface receptors, viruses enter eukaryotic cells primarily through two mechanisms: membrane fusion or endocytosis. Enveloped viruses often utilize membrane fusion to directly integrate their lipid bilayer with the membrane of the cell, enabling the deposition of genetic material into the cytosol. This method bypasses the endosomal pathway, thereby avoiding endosomal entrapment and optimizing transport efficiency [184]. Dou et al. encapsulated siRNA in a lipid bilayer, whose surface was covalently anchored to DbLYTAC through copper-free click chemistry to form VNP. The NPs directly fused with the cell membrane (rather than endocytosis), bypassing the endosome-lysosome retention, thereby enhancing the intracellular entry efficiency of siRNA and maintaining its activity in gene silencing and targeted protein degradation to treat tumors [185]. Gao et al. designed a virus-inspired fusogenic vesicle for high-throughput, fast detection of exosomal microRNAs within 2 h.135 Vir-FVs, incorporating fusogenic proteins like the HA-neuraminidase protein and the human parainfluenza virus fusion protein, targeted sialic acid-containing receptors on exosomes, promoting fusion [186].

The high cholesterol content in viral envelopes is critical for this fusion process, and viral-mimicking carriers with similar cholesterol-rich membranes have shown enhanced cytosolic delivery efficiency through membrane fusion [187]. For instance, Hindi et al. engineered muscle-specific fusogens, Myomaker and Myomerger, onto viral membranes to develop pseudotyped lentiviral vectors (Mymk + Mymg-LVs) for skeletal muscle targeting [188]. These fusogens facilitated efficient fusion with activated myogenic progenitors, enabling the delivery of μDystrophin in a Duchenne muscular dystrophy mouse model. Yu's team mimicked the composition of viral envelopes as well as their interfacial hydrophilic and hydrophobic properties. By increasing the cholesterol content in exosome membranes, they significantly enhanced interactions with target cell membranes, prompting exosomes to enter cells via membrane fusion rather than endocytosis, thereby greatly reducing lysosomal degradation. This strategy provides a new approach for siRNA delivery [184]. Alternatively, viruses can enter cells via endocytosis, where they are internalized into endosomes. Feng et al. developed a chemically engineered Ad as a gene delivery vector [114]. By precisely assembling conjugating functional materials onto single, live Ad particles particles, they created a modified virus that enters host cells through phagocytosis, rather than the receptor-facilitated internalization commonly observed in native Ad.

Environmental perception and endosomal escape

To avoid degradation by lysosomes, viruses must escape the endosomal compartment after cellular internalization, which is a rate-limiting step for efficient intracellular delivery of viral genetic materials [54,189]. Some viruses, such as Japanese encephalitis virus, demonstrate the ability to escape the endosomal compartment by triggering specific proteins or membrane disruptions that allow them to breach the endosomal membrane [190]. This process is often accompanied by the regulation of cellular autophagy, facilitating viral replication. Leveraging these properties, Li et al. developed a Japanese encephalitis virus-mimicking virosome decorated with anti-PDL1 antibodies for cancer therapy [191]. By promoting autophagic activity and inhibiting the progression of autophagy, this nano drug induced the apoptosis of tumor cells and further activated the immune responses. However, the direct use of viral proteins for nucleotide delivery is often limited by safety concerns. To overcome this, synthetic macromolecules with pH-responsive membrane destabilization capabilities have been developed to mimic viral functions [192]. In the case of the influenza virus, the surface hemagglutinin (HA) proteins facilitate their escape from endosomal compartments to evade degradation [193]. Park et al. demonstrated the use of a viral fusion protein (HA protein from influenza virus) to coat mRNA-loaded NPs, forming an HA-mRNA-NP complex that mimicked the viral endosomal escape mechanism [194]. Li's team modularly synthesized a series of lipopeptides VNPs: one end was a type II collagen-targeted peptide WYRGRL, the middle was a cationic fragment with different arginine/histidine ratios, and the end was a hydrophobic C22 chain [195]. By simulating the viral source arginine, which was rich in cell-penetrating peptides, it promoted sufficient endocytosis and effective lysosomal escape, treating osteoarthritis.

Throughout infection, viruses have developed mechanisms to sense and react to external signals, including receptor binding, exposure to low pH, redox properties, and enzyme-induced covalent modifications [196]. A critical step in viral infection is the uncoating of the viral envelope and release of the viral genome, typically triggered by pH-activated conformational changes in viral proteins, leading to virus-endosome fusion [197,198]. By designing the vectors to respond to the pH changes, researchers can enable them to lose their coating and navigate the mildly acidic interiors of early and late endosomes, thus preventing lysosomal degradation. Cheng et al. designed a synthetic polymer named VIPER (virus-inspired polymer for endosomal release), which integrated a polycation block for nucleic acid condensation and a pH-sensitive block that triggers the display of a lytic peptide in acidic environments, facilitating cytosolic trafficking [199]. Under neutral pH conditions, the polycationic block effectively encapsulates plasmid DNA, while the pH-sensitive block mediates the spatial concealment of the lytic peptide, maintaining the structural stability and low membrane toxicity of the complex. Upon entry into acidic endosomes, the pH-sensitive block undergoes a conformational change, exposing and activating the cleavage peptide. Furthermore, various synthetic bionic nanocarriers have been developed to achieve efficient endosomal escape. For example, Gao et al., engineered polysaccharide nanocomplex (CEEPG NPs) by conjugating endosomolytic poly(alkyl methacrylate-co-methacrylic acid) fragments onto barley β-glucans, featuring pH-responsive endosomal escape and immune-targeting properties for siRNA delivery [200]. This system mimics viral capsid proteins, undergoing conformational changes under acidic conditions to destabilize membranes and facilitate endosomal escape.

Viruses can also respond to intracellular signals such as enzymatic and redox stimuli to achieve endosomal escape and biotransformation. Rouge's group reported a multi-layered core-shell structure Nano-DDS inspired by the dynamic multi-dimensional structure of viruses [201]. Its core was stabilized by a metal-organic framework and encapsulated in nucleic acid nanocapsules, ultimately endowing it with an enzyme-triggered mechanism to control the release and expression of mRNA in cells. During endosome maturation, as the highly expressed proteases within the lumen, the outer layer of nucleic acid nanocapsules undergoes specific enzymatic degradation, causing the protective structure of the core-shell outer layer to rapidly disintegrate; consequently, the internal metal-organic framework core loses its protection and undergoes structural collapse; The stably encapsulated mRNA is gradually released from the carrier framework.

Intracellular trafficking and organelle targeting

To further enhance gene and drug delivery, some virus-inspired systems utilize intracellular transport mechanisms that mimic viral pathways. A notable feature of viruses is their ability to precisely target specific organelles, such as mitochondria and the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), enabling sub-organelle-level delivery. By emulating these viral strategies, drug delivery systems can enhance therapeutic efficacy through sub-organelle targeting (Fig. 7). For example, Yuan et al. developed a nonviral cationic liposome (Lipo-Par) covalently linked with the cationic polypeptide pardaxin to enhance gene transfection efficiency [201]. This system used caveolin-mediated endocytosis to actively target the ER, where it leveraged the cytoskeleton to facilitate nuclear transport. Its potential release mechanism closely mimicked viral delivery pathways: the endoplasmic reticulum microenvironment induced conformational changes in the liposome membrane and the pardaxin peptide, reducing the electrostatic binding force between the carrier and the gene, thereby promoting gene dissociation and release. The gene was then actively transported via the cytoskeleton, directed to the nuclear pore complex, and entered the cell nucleus, where subsequent transcription and expression take place. This strategy mimicked the viral strategy of utilizing the ER and cytoskeleton for efficient gene delivery, improving transfection efficiency. Ni et al. developed a mitochondrial targeting transdermal Nano-DDS (PTX/Mtlip Gel) for melanoma treatment. This system integrated dendritic lipopeptide (DLP, with lysine residues used to mimic viral capsids) -modified liposomes (Mtlip) into a hydrogel matrix [202], leveraging the positive charge of Mtlip to promote cellular uptake and mitochondrial targeting, thus increasing cytotoxicity. Similarly, Chen et al. developed a coronavirus-mimicking nanocomplex (SDN) for cancer theranostics, which combined chlorin e6-loaded lipid carriers (CeNLC, virus core) and functionalized MnO2 NPs (virus spike) [203]. SDN enhanced mitochondrial targeting and facilitated mitochondria-specific photodynamic therapy, tumor hypoxia relief, and macrophage-mediated anticancer immunity, offering a promising approach to cancer treatment. Tuerong's research team developed an engineered-optimized VLP [74]. By mimicking the structure of the viral shell with a GCN4 protein, it realized viral trans-nuclear membrane transport through nuclear-cytoplasmic shuttle technology. This VLP was used to treat hereditary retinal diseases such as retinitis pigmentosa and can also assist CAR-T cell therapy for cancer.

Immune modulation

The immune system has evolved sophisticated mechanisms to detect and counter viral infections, responding to specific viral nucleic acid sequences, such as CpG ODN, single-strand RNA, and double-strand RNA [[204], [205], [206], [207]]. Upon viral entry, antigen-presenting cells (APCs) process these signals, triggering T and B lymphocyte activation and initiating adaptive immune responses [208]. Recent pioneering studies have demonstrated that many virus-mimicking Nano-DDS can induce strong antigen-specific anti-tumor responses (Fig. 9) [209]. For example, Wang et al. developed erythrocyte-mediated antiviral immunization via erythrocytes functionalized with virus-mimetic NPs loaded with an antigen from a virus and a TLR-stimulating agent [210]. These NPs, attached to erythrocytes, promoted APC maturation and activation, eliciting both humoral immunity and cell-mediated immunity and systemic antiviral T cell responses. Inspired by the robust innate immunity induced by herpesvirus, Zhao et al. engineered a herpesvirus-mimicking NP (Vir-ZM@TD) that encapsulated DNAzyme and utilized viral-like features to activate anti-tumor innate immunity. This NP achieved efficient DNAzyme delivery and induced mitochondrial DNA stress, which enhanced the immune response against tumors [211]. Chu et al. developed virus-mimic NPs using indocyanine green, double-stranded RNA analogs (Poly I:C), and chemically self-assembling cyclic gadolinium-based GAMP (c·Gd·GAMP, a STING agonist) [212]. Here, Poly I:C mimicked viral RNA and further enhanced the secretion of IFN-β by activating the corresponding RNA recognition pathway. Peng et al. conducted surface engineering modifications on the influenza virus strain and developed a bioorthogonal optimized viral immune NPs that mimicked the characteristics of virus-induced cellular responses and the protective effect of viral membranes, triggering immunogenic death of tumor cells for the treatment of solid tumors [213]. In another study, the authors formed NPs through the self-assembly of viral capsid proteins or engineered proteins, which had repetitive antigen-presenting structures, which could efficiently activate B-cell responses and enhance the intensity of vaccine-induced immune responses [214]. Recent studies have emphasized the role of temporal regulation in immune activation for optimizing adaptive responses. For example, Lou et al. proposed a strategy of chronological management of adjuvant effects in mRNA vaccines, inspired by the natural dynamics of IFN-I expression during viral infection [215]. Their study demonstrated that the approach of delivering an adjuvant after mRNA administration enhanced CD8+T cell responses and antigen presentation via increased p-MHC I expression while reducing cardiac and liver toxicity.

Viruses have developed complex mechanisms to escape detection by the host immune system, providing valuable inspiration for designing immune-evasive Nano-DDS (see Fig. 8). For example, influenza viruses often mutate their surface proteins to escape immune recognition [216], while SARS-CoV-2 modifies its spike protein to enhance host cell binding and evade immune attacks [217]. Mimicking these viral immune-evasion strategies, NPs can be engineered to evade immune recognition, prolong circulation time, and enhance delivery efficiency. Immune-evasive NPs may incorporate surface modifications such as PEGylation or cloaking with host-derived proteins to avoid phagocytosis and complement activation [211,218]. Additionally, mimicking viral latency mechanisms, NPs can target immune-privileged tissues to deliver therapeutic agents with minimal immune response. For example, Hussein et al. introduced a novel immune-evasive nanocarrier, VP28-heparin, inspired by the immunological stealth properties of viral capsid proteins [219]. Importantly, the system was non-immunogenic, mitigated heparin-induced thrombocytopenia risk, and allowed reversible anticoagulation with protamine sulfate. This immune-evasive strategy demonstrated the potential of leveraging viral mechanisms to enhance therapeutic delivery and safety, providing a promising alternative for anticoagulant therapies. In addition, engineered AAV has been a promising vector to evade immune responses [220]. In conclusion, these strategies offer promising solutions for overcoming the challenges of immune clearance, enabling more efficient drug delivery, and improving therapeutic outcomes in various diseases.

Multifunctional Nano-DDS

Multifunctional virus-mimicking Nano-DDS are designed to emulate multiple viral traits simultaneously, incorporating various viral peptides or proteins to achieve advanced functionalities, including surface recognition, cell entry, endosomal escape, environmental response, organelle targeting, and immune evasion [221]. For instance, Mao's group designed a virus-like bionic Nano-DDS [222]. Cationic liposomes modified with heparan sulfate were used to simulate the protein shell of viruses, and natural platycosaponins were functionalized as “spikes” by taking advantage of their amphiphilic structure. By administering the vaccine through the lungs and simulating the viral infection route, the targeting effect of platycosaponin was utilized to enhance the retention of the vaccine in the lungs and its interaction with DCs, thereby strengthening the mucosal, cellular and humoral immune responses. Kong et al. developed peptidyl virus-like particles (pVLPs) by co-assembling two viral peptides (Fc-FF-V3 and Fc-FF-NLS) into NPs that mimic the human immunodeficiency virus and SV40 [120]. These peptides endowed the pVLPs with selective nuclear import and cell-penetrating abilities, enabling the encapsulation and nuclear delivery of CRISPR/Cas9 DNA (Cas9, a protein linked to Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats). The resulting pVLPs with biodegradable properties exhibited exceptional biocompatibility and cell-targeting efficiency, highlighting their potential for precise genetic cargo delivery. Jiang's group coated HMnO2 (HD) loaded DNAzyme with lipid-doped red blood cell membranes (HM) to increase the blood circulation time and stability of nucleic acid drugs, simulating the protection of nucleic acids by viral envelopes [223]. The Cskc peptide was used to mediate the transmembrane transport across the BBB and target metastatic tumors, while HA2 peptide promoted the fusion of HM and lysosomal membranes through conformational changes, inducing the release of HD into the cytosol, simulating the virus entry process and the lysosomal escape process of nucleic acids. Subsequently, HD achieved responsive release of Mn2+ and DNAzyme in a high intracellular reducing environment for the specific degradation of miR-19a and the activation of cGAS-STING.

Intelligent design on Nano-DDS

Nano-DDS that mimic viruses have emerged as cutting-edge tools in nanomedicine. By harnessing the advantages of viral morphology and functionality, these systems enable precise cell targeting, efficient intracellular delivery, and improved therapeutic outcomes (Fig. 10). This section provides an overview of innovative virus-inspired nanocarriers, focusing on morphology-functional integrated systems, theranostic platforms, and bio-integrated designs.

Morphology-function integrated biomimetic strategy

Given the sophisticated entry mechanisms and signal-responsive capabilities of viruses, a morphology–function integrated biomimetic strategy has emerged as a highly promising approach for constructing efficient and intelligent Nano-DDS. Wu et al. developed a new approach to create artificial tobacco mosaic virus agents, which mimicked the high-aspect-ratio morphology of natural TMV and used ingeniously designed functionally structured dendritic molecules with ingenious design to mimic viral subunits and improve antitumor functions [224]. Remarkably, ATMVs not only induce lysis of the first infected cells, but also possess the ability to disseminate the effect to neighboring cells, facilitating deep penetration into solid tumors and achieving extensive cell lysis. This innovative approach demonstrated significant efficacy in overcoming large, drug-resistant colon tumors (LoVo/Adr, ≈500 mm3), presenting a promising avenue for the development of versatile virus-like therapeutic agents with optimized morphological features and multifunctional antitumor capabilities.

In a similar design philosophy, viral capsid proteins protect the genome from degradation and, when activated by acidic endosomal environments, undergo conformational changes that enable endosomal escape within minutes, allowing the virus to enter the cytoplasm and subsequently be transported to the nucleus [[225], [226], [227], [228]]. Wannasarit et al. engineered a new polymeric nanoparticle (ADMAP), which mimics the architecture and functional characteristics of virus capsid proteins [229]. The ADMAP NPs are fabricated by conjugating endosomolytic poly(lauryl methacrylate-co-methacrylic acid) with acetalated dextran, forming a system where the endosomolytic polymer imitates viral capsid proteins to promote endosomal release, and acetalated dextran provides a viral-core-like structure for carrying payloads. Within the acidic milieu of late endosomes, the water-soluble polymer within the vesicle undergoes protonation, destabilizing the vesicle membrane, while the acetalated glucan backbone undergoes acid hydrolysis, causing the carrier structure to depolymerize. This results in synergistic drug release, effectively preventing the drug from being degraded by lysosomes. The addition of a tumor-penetrating peptide increased antitumor efficacy in three-dimensional spheroids, leading to spheroid disruption.

Drug loading into Nano-DDS

The efficient loading and stable delivery of drugs are central to the development of virus-mimicking Nano-DDS. Based on the structural characteristics of the these nanocarriers and the physicochemical properties of the drugs, four distinct drug-loading strategies are currently employed [230]. One approach utilizes the hollow core-shell structure of VLPs and VNPs to encapsulate small molecules, nucleic acids, and protein-based drugs during the particle self-assembly process, thereby effectively preventing enzymatic degradation and clearance by the reticuloendothelial system [231]. For example, inspired by the protective mechanism of helical viral capsid proteins toward RNA, Lv et al. developed an RNA encapsulation strategy using a DNA six-helix bundle (6 H B) as an RNase-proof protective sheath, where RNA is encapsulated inside the central cavity of the DNA 6 H B, thus protecting the internal RNA from degradation by ribonucleases in the environment [232]. Drug loading can also be achieved through electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic interactions [233]. This method is simple and gentle to implement, but the drug tends to dissociate prematurely in vivo [234]. Furthermore, drugs can be anchored to the surface of Nano-DDS via biodegradable chemical bonds such as ester and amide bonds, which significantly enhances drug stability in vivo and enables precise, targeted drug release at the site of the lesion. This approach is commonly used for the delivery of highly active drugs, such as peptides and chemotherapeutic agents [235]. In addition, the current mainstream approach for preparing virus-mimetic nanocarriers for theranostic applications involves simultaneously assembling the drug and the bionic material, thereby achieving particle formation and uniform drug loading in a single step [236].

Integrated with advanced materials

The integration of virus-mimetic bionic design with advanced materials is essential for developing efficient Nano-DDS. These systems leverage the biocompatibility and functional properties of materials such as hydrogels and microneedles to enable targeted delivery and sustained release of therapeutic agents. To effectively deliver antigens to skin-resident APCs, Wang et al. designed self-adjuvanted protein NPs containing conserved influenza antigens and employed dissolvable microneedle patches (MNPs) for skin vaccination in mice to enhance the magnitude and breadth of immune responses [237]. They synthesized influenza matrix protein 2 ectodomain (M2e) NPs via an ethanol desolvation method and constructed a bilayer nanoparticle structure (NA2-FliC/M2e) by chemically cross-linking neuraminidase (NA) and flagellin (FliC) onto the M2e cores. Incorporating these NPs into MNPs significantly enhanced M2e- and NA-specific antibody levels, germinal center B cell numbers, and IL-4 positive splenocytes, and conferred protection in mice against homologous and heterosubtypic influenza viruses. To address challenges such as needle-induced injury and the uneven spatial distribution of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) in therapeutic interventions for ischemic stroke and other nervous system disorders, Liu et al. designed a novel microneedle (MN) platform made from gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) for the sustained and controlled local delivery of AAV carrying human VEGF (AAV-VEGF) [115]. In an adult rat ischemic stroke model, GelMA MNs loaded with AAV-VEGF were implanted within the ischemic core and penumbra, leading to a homogeneous distribution of the therapeutic agent and achieving high transfection efficiency. The study demonstrated that AAV-VEGF promoted angiogenesis and neurogenesis, while the porous structure of GelMA MNs facilitated gradual degradation and sustained release of AAV-VEGF, thereby promoting functional recovery and reducing cytotoxicity risks.

During the wound healing process, Wang et al. developed an innovative hydrogel spray containing glucose oxidase-modified, virus-like hollow mesoporous copper oxide (CuO) nanospheres, aimed to enhancing the healing of diabetic foot ulcers [238]. The CuO nanospheres, under hyperglycemic conditions, decomposed glucose to generate hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), which, through a Fenton-like reaction, produced toxic hydroxyl radicals (•OH) with potent antibacterial properties [239]. Simultaneously, the release of Cu2+ ions stimulated keratinocyte proliferation and endothelial cell tubulogenesis, promoting angiogenesis. The hydrogel also alleviated hypoxia by catalyzing the conversion of intracellular H2O2 into oxygen [240].

Theranostic design for Nano-DDS

In the realm of theranostics, virus-inspired Nano-DDS combine diagnostic and therapeutic capabilities, providing dual-modality solutions for disease management. These systems often incorporate imaging agents, such as magnetic resonance imaging contrast agents or photothermal agents, alongside therapeutic molecules to enable real-time monitoring of treatment efficacy. Shahrivarkevishahi et al. engineered a novel photothermal agent, PhotothermalPhage (PTPhage), by functionalizing VLPs of bacteriophage Qβ with croconium dyes, combining the superior photophysical properties of croconium dyes with the biocompatibility and low immunogenicity of VLPs [116]. PTPhage demonstrated enhanced photothermal conversion efficiency, solubility, and cellular uptake compared to free dye, leading to significant tumor ablation and prolonged survival in a triple-negative breast cancer mouse model. Additionally, PTPhage promoted strong immune responses, enhancing the activation of dendritic cells and T lymphocytes, suggesting its potential as an effective photoimmunotherapy system [241].

Challenges and perspective

Virus-inspired Nano-DDS utilize viral components of both natural and synthetic origin to mimic the capabilities of viruses, such as cell invasion, immune evasion, and tissue targeting, thereby providing multifaceted solutions for precise intracellular drug and gene delivery. These systems exploit viral characteristics such as efficient biological barrier penetration, targeted intracellular delivery, and immunomodulation, demonstrating tremendous potential. The integration of biomaterials science with virus-mimicking technologies opens exciting new possibilities for multimodal therapy, immunomodulation, and diagnostic imaging.

However, the path from laboratory innovation to clinical application is fraught with multifaceted challenges (Fig. 11). One of the primary hurdles in the clinical translation of virus-inspired Nano-DDS is ensuring their safety. The prolonged systemic circulation of these nanocarriers, while beneficial for targeted drug accumulation, may also lead to unintended off-target effects or toxicity [244]. Viral-derived proteins and components might interact with endogenous cellular pathways in unpredictable ways, potentially exacerbating adverse effects [245]. Another ongoing challenge lies in optimizing the absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) profiles of virus-inspired Nano-DDS. To mitigate these risks, comprehensive biosafety evaluations, including long-term toxicity and immunogenicity studies, are essential to ensure clinical viability and minimize adverse effects before these strategies can be reliably used in medical settings. The main obstacles to the industrialization and clinical translation of virus-inspired drug delivery systems include immature manufacturing processes, inconsistent quality control standards, difficulties in scalable production, unclear regulatory guidelines, and clinical translation challenges stemming from safety and efficacy validation requirements.

The future of virus-inspired Nano-DDS lies in leveraging interdisciplinary synergies and emerging technologies. Advances in synthetic biology, artificial intelligence, and high-throughput materials discovery are expected to accelerate the rational design of virus-mimicking NPs, optimizing their therapeutic efficacy and safety. The convergence of these systems with personalized medicine approaches holds promise of tailored therapeutics, ensuring maximal efficacy and minimal toxicity. With ongoing technological and scientific advancements, these systems hold the potential to revolutionize modern medicine, driving transformative advancements in disease prevention, diagnosis, and treatment.
