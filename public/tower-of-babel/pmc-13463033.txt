Women’s preferences for breast cancer risk-reducing medications: a discrete choice experiment study
Authors: Qi Gao, Si Ning Serene Goh, Alexis Jiaying Khng, Mikael Hartman, Yi Wang, Jingmei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite strong evidence that chemoprevention reduces breast cancer risk in high-risk women, uptake remains low. We hypothesized that healthy women evaluate preventive medications differently from patients and that understanding delivery preferences may inform more acceptable strategies. We conducted a discrete choice experiment with eight attributes: reduction in lifetime breast cancer risk by age 70, treatment duration, modality, frequency, medical recommendation, fertility impact, side effects, and total cost. Women aged 21–59 without prior breast cancer were recruited in Singapore. Each participant completed choice tasks under two perceived lifetime risk scenarios (30% and 80%). Preferences were analyzed using mixed logit models, with conditional relative importance and marginal willingness-to-pay estimated. Among 618 women, respondents generally preferred risk-reducing treatment over no treatment. Across risk scenarios, shorter duration, minimal fertility impact, and fewer side effects were consistently valued. Cost strongly influenced choices, although less so at higher baseline risk. Cost and side effects were the most important attributes in both scenarios. Willingness-to-pay estimates indicated strong aversion to severe side effects and long durations, and a preference for specialist over general practitioner recommendations. Preferences varied by sociodemographic and clinical characteristics. No significant preference differences were observed across delivery modalities.Subject terms: Cancer, Health care, Medical research, Oncology, Risk factors

Introduction

Effective medical screening relies on having an available intervention that yields better outcomes than waiting for symptoms to appear. Without a preventive option, screening becomes the default strategy for many conditions, including breast cancer1.

Primary prevention is generally preferred over secondary detection. When available, it offers more benefits at the population level2. For breast cancer, however, prevention has lagged. Current approaches focus on early detection through mammography, not on stopping the disease before it starts. Lifestyle changes are often recommended, but their impact is limited and difficult to sustain. These strategies are especially impractical for lower-income populations3.

Medications such as tamoxifen and raloxifene can reduce the risk of breast cancer in women at high risk4. However, the uptake is low. In the United States, about 15% of women aged 35-79 are eligible for chemoprevention, but fewer than 5% accept it when offered5. Chemoprevention is often declined for several reasons: many women are unaware of their high-risk status; healthcare providers may lack the time or expertise to discuss preventive options; and concerns about side effects or associations with chemotherapy5.

Traditionally, tamoxifen has been given as a daily oral tablet at a standard dose of 20 mg. This regimen is linked to rare but serious side effects, including endometrial cancer and thromboembolic events6. To reduce toxicity and improve acceptability, lower-dose alternatives such as 5 mg/day oral tamoxifen have been studied in recent trials7,8. In addition, transdermal delivery shows promise for reducing systemic exposure while achieving therapeutic drug concentrations in breast tissue9,10. Such innovations could improve the benefit-to-risk perception and encourage greater uptake of preventive therapy.

Advances to reduce side effects may improve the acceptability of breast cancer chemoprevention, but an often-overlooked factor influencing uptake is how the medication is delivered. Would women prefer a daily pill, a topical gel, or an occasional injection? Little is known about delivery preferences, especially among high-risk women who stand to benefit most. As conventional prevention pills may feel overly medicalized or unnecessary to a healthy woman who is healthy, an insight into these preferences could help guide the development of more acceptable prevention options and improve uptake.

In this study, we used a discrete choice experiment (DCE) to explore preferences for breast cancer risk-reducing medication (primary prevention) among women. We focused on delivery methods, side effects, and other key factors that influence decision-making. Our goal is to inform strategies that make chemoprevention more acceptable and aligned with women’s preferences.

All women included in the analytical dataset reside in Singapore and have no personal history of breast cancer. “Do not wish to disclose” for these questions ranged from 0.4% to 1.3%, the highest for the question “known family history of breast cancer”. DCE results

Table 2 presents the regression results based on participants’ responses to all 10 choice tasks (Q1–Q10) in the DCE questionnaire. For ease of interpretation, the levels of the “reduction in risk” attribute were defined as “reduction to half” and “reduction to one-tenth” in Table 2. The estimated coefficients represent the marginal utility associated with each attribute level relative to its reference category. A positive coefficient indicates a preference for that level compared to the reference, while a negative coefficient reflects the opposite. Overall, the results suggest that women were more inclined to choose breast cancer risk-reducing treatments in real-life settings (coefficientNone option = −2.670, 95% CI: −3.188 to −2.153) (Table 2). Participants preferred treatments with shorter durations, minimal impact on fertility, and fewer side effects. Relative to a treatment duration of 1 year, the coefficients for three-year and five-year treatments were −0.311 (95% CI: −0.412 to −0.211) and −0.496 (95% CI: −0.605 to −0.388), respectively. These findings indicate that participants were significantly less likely to choose treatments of longer duration when shorter options were available. Similarly, relative to treatments with no impact on fertility, treatments associated with temporary or permanent fertility effects were both significantly less preferred (cannot be pregnant during the treatment: coefficient = −0.166, 95% CI: −0.262 to −0.070; can never be pregnant after the treatment: coefficient = −0.341, 95% CI: −0.463 to −0.219). In terms of side effects, participants showed a strong aversion to severe side effects, with a coefficient of −0.606 (95% CI: −0.715 to −0.498) relative to moderate side effects.

Women’s preferences for risk reduction and treatment frequency varied depending on the risk level (Table 3). Although both the 30% and 80% scenarios represent elevated risk levels relative to the general population, participants demonstrated a strong preference for treatment that reduced breast cancer risk from 30% to 3% compared to a reduction to 15% (coefficient = 0.636, 95% CI: 0.474 to 0.797). In contrast, no statistically significant preference was observed for reducing risk from 80% to 8% compared with a reduction to 40% (coefficient = −0.086, 95% CI: −0.251 to 0.079). While participants did not exhibit significant preferences regarding treatment frequency under the 80% risk scenario, the “quarterly” frequency level was significantly associated with a utility loss compared to the “daily” frequency under the 30% risk scenario (coefficient = −0.640, 95% CI: −1.245 to −0.036).

Cost was another significant determinant of women’s preferences for breast cancer risk-reducing treatments. As treatment cost increased, participants experienced a significant decline in utility. Notably, cost sensitivity appeared to attenuate under a higher given risk level. Specifically, compared to the S$500 cost level, the coefficient for S$1000 was −0.336 (95% CI: −0.497 to −0.176) in the 30% risk scenario and −0.184 (95% CI: −0.354 to −0.014) in the 80% risk scenario; for the S$3000 level, the coefficients were −0.922 (95% CI: −1.136 to −0.707) and −0.721 (95% CI: −0.919 to −0.523), respectively (Table 3).

Marginal willingness-to-pay (WTP)

The WTP estimates for various treatment attributes at 30% and 80% baseline risk levels are presented in Table 4. Participants at 30% baseline risk were willing to pay S$9052 (95% CI: –11,080 to –7025) to avoid the “None option”, while those at 80% risk were willing to pay S$12,322 (95% CI: –15,474 to –9169). For risk reduction, participants at 30% risk were willing to pay an additional S$1823 (95% CI: 1302 to 2343) to reduce the risk to 3% compared to 15%, whereas those at 80% risk showed no statistically significant difference (S$-88, 95% CI: −572 to 396). As the duration of treatment increased, women’s WTP decreased. Participants at 30% risk were willing to pay S$899 (95% CI: −1375 to −423) to avoid a 3-year treatment and S$1767 (95% CI: −2342 to −1193) to avoid a 5-year treatment compared to a 1-year treatment. Under the 80% risk level, participants were willing to pay S$1462 (95% CI: −2132 to −792) and S$1863 (95% CI: −2573 to −1153) to avoid treatments lasts for 3 and 5 years, respectively. For treatment recommendation, participants at 30% risk were willing to pay S$569 more (95% CI: 112 to 1026) for specialist advice over family physician advice. At 30% risk, inability to get pregnant during treatment reduced WTP by S$596 (95% CI: −1046 to −146), and permanent infertility reduced WTP by S$1411 (95% CI: −1970 to −852). Similar preferences were observed under the 80% risk scenario. Participants were willing to pay S$613 (95% CI: −1160 to −65) and S$1024 (95% CI: −1654 to −393) to avoid temporary and permanent infertility, respectively. Compared with treatments with moderate side effects, treatments with severe side effects were associated with a negative WTP of S$–1964 (95% CI: −2523 to −1405) at 30% risk and a negative WTP of S$–2441 (95% CI: −3213 to −1669) at 80% risk, respectively.

The preference heterogeneity across different demographic groups

The results of preference heterogeneity across demographic groups are presented in the Supplementary Table 3. Compared with younger women (21–39), women aged 40–49 were less willing to undergo real risk-reduction treatment, and women aged 40–59 were less concerned about treatment-related infertility. Women with a history of benign breast disease were significantly more likely to opt for risk-reduction treatment. Higher education was associated with greater concern about side effects but also greater willingness to accept higher treatment costs. Marital and parental status also shaped preferences: married women and those with children were more willing to pursue risk-reduction treatment in real life, and both groups expressed less concern about the potential impact on fertility compared with their unmarried or childless counterparts. Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4 present the relative attribute importance of participants grouped by age and family history of breast cancer. Younger women (21–39) placed greater weight on treatment duration and fertility concerns, while older women emphasized cost, modality, frequency, and side effects; additionally, women with a family history valued efficacy more, but their concerns about cost and side effects shifted depending on the risk scenario.

Discussion

Given the strides in breast cancer risk stratification without corresponding advances in risk-reduction strategies, we aimed to understand how women without cancer prefer risk-reduction to be delivered. This information can inform the development of more effective and acceptable risk-reduction interventions. Our study of 618 women in Singapore revealed that they were generally open to some form of intervention rather than opting out. Contrary to our hypothesis that healthy women might favor non-pill or non-injection delivery methods, which are perceived as more “medical”, our study population remained largely indifferent to delivery modality (i.e., pill, gel/cream, patch, injection). Instead, affordable cost, mild side-effect profiles, meaningful risk reductions (especially from lower baseline levels), shorter durations, and minimal impact on fertility were found to have greater influence on preferences.

Our findings align with the established BRCA findings regarding the dominant importance of efficacy and reproductive safety, as well as the indifference to modality. Liede et al. performed a DCE study on 622 BRCA carriers12. Attributes studied were the extent of breast cancer risk reduction, treatment duration, impact on fertility, hormone levels, risk of uterine cancer, and ease and mode of administration. The two most influential factors in treatment preferences were the extent of breast cancer risk reduction and the impact on fertility (i.e., not risk-reduction delivery method). We initially expected to find preferences for the more non-clinical and “alternative” modes of administration (i.e., gel vs. injection or pills). Indeed, in a focus group study by Karavites et al., participants were given the choice and selected a gel over a pill, mainly due to the expectation of fewer side effects13. However, these preferences remain secondary to considerations such as cost and side effects.

Our work added treatment cost as a consideration, which was not modeled in the Liede et al. study12. In our previous DCE study looking at women’s preferences for testing to predict breast cancer risk in Singapore (a step before risk reduction), financial incentives also featured strongly as a consideration, especially when it is known that participants value the immediacy and perceived urgency of treatment interventions more than prevention14,15. Even though medications such as Tamoxifen are more cost-effective in the preventive setting than the treatment setting, especially for high-risk individuals, the adage “An apple a day keeps the doctor away” comes to mind16. True preventive healthcare should ideally be as accessible and low-cost as basic nutrition.

The shift in attribute priorities from cost and side effects dominating in the 80% risk scenario to risk reduction, duration, and effect on pregnancy in the 30% scenario highlights that baseline risk level significantly shapes decision-making. An interesting detail we observed is that although women facing an 80% given risk are more likely to opt for risk-reducing treatment than those at 30% risk, the relative importance they assign to the magnitude of risk reduction differs markedly. Even though the high-risk group stands to gain more clinically, the low-risk group values the degree of risk reduction nearly ten times more than the high-risk group. Cognitive biases (e.g., certainty effect, zero-risk bias) may help to explain this finding. For example, cutting risk down to near-zero (to 3%, from 30%) feels emotionally stronger than a larger absolute reduction (to 8%, from 80%)17. Perhaps in the 80% to 40% scenario, the remaining risk may feel too sizable to ignore, motivating the women to accept the treatment. Patients may also prioritize avoiding adverse treatment outcomes (such as cost and side effects) over marginal gains in efficacy. The results could also signal a numeracy issue in the population. Individuals with lower numeracy often struggle to interpret and weigh statistical risk appropriately, relying more on emotional framing and presentation than on the numerical magnitude of benefit18.

Our results also highlighted the importance of tailoring interventions for women from different demographic groups. Factors such as age, prior benign breast disease, education, marital status, and whether having children affected women’s preference for different attributes. As expected, younger women, women who were not married, and women without children were more concerned with the adverse impact on pregnancy. In practice, communication strategies need to be designed carefully to clearly explain the risk involved, and the program should be structured to address the distinct concerns of women across different demographic groups.

This study has several strengths. Few DCEs have directly focused on chemoprevention uptake for breast cancer outside of high-risk groups. Although some high-risk women may have received clinical recommendations for chemoprevention, evidence suggests that many do not fully understand their personal breast cancer risk or the implications of preventive treatments19. Factors such as complex risk information, limited consultation time, medical jargon, cognitive overload, psychological coping mechanisms, and variable follow-up can all contribute to incomplete awareness. By presenting hypothetical risk scenarios to a broader population, our survey captures preferences and concerns that are relevant not only to women formally classified as high-risk but also to those who may encounter chemoprevention in real-world settings without fully understanding their risk. Most preference research in breast cancer or related cancers, such as ovarian cancer prevention, focuses on surgical strategies20,21. Our relatively large dataset (n = 618) provides statistical power and supports scenario and subgroup analyses. The eight attributes we studied captured clinical, practical, and psychosocial considerations, providing a broad view of decision factors. The inclusion of two distinct risk perceptions (30% vs. 80% lifetime risk) allows us to understand how context shapes preferences. Furthermore, we explored the impact of demographic factors on preference.

In terms of limitations, the study was conducted in Singapore, where healthcare financing and cultural attitudes toward prevention may differ from other settings. Results may not apply globally. Nevertheless, this context is highly relevant, as Singapore represents an Asian healthcare system where breast cancer incidence is rising, and preventive strategies remain underused22. In addition, the DCE was administered online by a trusted third-party professional survey research vendor. Participants were invited to complete the choice cards electronically. This may introduce selection bias, as women without internet access, with limited English proficiency, or with a lower willingness to participate in online research were less likely to be represented. While our DCE scenarios included some attribute levels beyond current clinical practice (e.g., very high-risk thresholds, shorter treatment durations, alternative delivery formulations), this approach is consistent with stated-preference methodology and provides insights into potential future clinical developments. Future studies should validate these findings in specific clinical populations where such scenarios are most applicable. Finally, the exclusion of women with prior breast cancer means insights from survivors (who may have different perspectives) are missing. However, this allowed us to focus specifically on primary prevention preferences among women without a cancer history, which was the primary aim of the study.

In conclusion, this study found that women are receptive to breast cancer chemoprevention, but preferences vary with baseline risk. Consistently valued attributes include minimal side effects, shorter duration, and minimal impact on fertility. However, no significant preference was found among the delivery methods.

Methods

DCE design

A DCE was designed to elicit preferences by presenting respondents with structured hypothetical scenarios that vary systematically across key attributes and levels. These scenarios were intentionally constructed to span a wide range of breast cancer risk levels and intervention characteristics, enabling the identification of preference structures, trade-offs, and key drivers of decision-making, rather than to mirror existing clinical practice.

Eight attributes were incorporated into the final DCE: the reduction in lifetime risk of developing breast cancer by age 70, treatment duration, treatment modality, treatment frequency, medical recommendation, impact on fertility, side effects, and total treatment cost. The full DCE design, including attribute levels, is detailed in Supplementary Table 1. We imposed the following prohibitions on treatment scheduling: oral pills (i.e., pills to be taken by mouth), topical gel or cream applied directly to the breast area, and skin patches must not be paired with the “Every 3 months” schedule; and injections must not co-occur with the “Daily” timing alternative. These constraints ensure that only realistic combinations of treatment mode and frequency appear in the choice sets.

Twenty blocks of questionnaires were used, with 10 tasks per questionnaire. Each task consisted of two stages. In the first stage, a choice needs to be made between two hypothetical treatment profiles. In the second stage, participants indicated whether they would accept their chosen option if it were offered in a real-life setting. An example choice card is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. Two baseline cancer risks were considered under the attribute of breast cancer risk by age 70, i.e., 30% by age 70 and 80% by age 70, to understand how preferences vary according to baseline risk. The inclusion of very high lifetime breast cancer risk levels (e.g., 80%) was intended to test the sensitivity of stated preferences to extreme risk scenarios and to assess how risk magnitude influences trade-offs across treatment attributes. Each block of DCE questions contained five tasks on a 30% baseline risk and five tasks on an 80% baseline risk. To minimize potential ordering or framing effects, half of the participants completed the tasks under 30% baseline risk first, followed by the tasks under 80% baseline risk, while the other half completed the tasks in reverse order. The DCE questionnaire was designed using Sawtooth software (Lighthouse Studio Advanced Teaching Suite). The balanced overlap option was used to enhance response efficiency (Supplementary material 2).

Participants were eligible for inclusion if they were female, aged between 21 and 59 years, currently residing in Singapore, and reported no personal history of breast cancer. Although chemoprevention eligibility typically begins at age 35, we included younger women to examine how preferences evolve across life stages, as early attitudes toward preventive interventions may shape future uptake and decision-making once eligibility is reached. Such insights are also critical for informing the design of programs aimed at enhancing uptake and maximizing health benefits in the future. Individuals were excluded if they had diagnosed cognitive impairments or mental health conditions that could compromise their ability to comprehend or reliably complete the survey, such as severe depression, schizophrenia, or intellectual disabilities. In addition, individuals who did not speak or understand English were excluded, as the survey was administered exclusively in English.

To ensure representation across different life stages and screening eligibility groups, the sample was stratified into three age groups: 21–39 years, 40–49 years, and 50–59 years. Additional background information was collected to characterize participants and to support potential interaction analyses. Variables included age, ethnicity, history of benign breast disease (excluding breast cancer), family history of breast cancer, personal connections to individuals diagnosed with breast cancer, self-perceived risk, level of concern about breast cancer relative to other diseases, nationality, education level, employment status, marital and parenthood status, and housing type. The questionnaire for demographic information collection is provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Sample size calculations

A total sample size of 300 participants was required for the three age strata, with 100 participants per stratum. Under two baseline risk scenarios, the required sample size increased to 600. To account for an anticipated 20% rate of invalid or incomplete responses, we aimed to recruit a total of 750 participants. In addition, we conducted a simulation in Sawtooth software to check the adequacy of the sample size.

Data collection

Data collection was conducted by an external research vendor via an online survey administered in June 2025. A pilot test was conducted with the first 30 participants to assess the clarity and functionality of the survey instrument, including the DCE component. No modifications were required based on pilot feedback.

A test DCE task with the same choice task format as the main questionnaire was included before the formal DCE tasks to familiarize the participants with the questions and to serve as a quality control measure (Supplementary Fig. 2). In this test task, participants were asked to choose between two alternatives that differed across attribute levels, with one option intentionally constructed to be clearly dominant (e.g., offering greater risk reduction, fewer side effects, and lower cost). Participants who failed to select the dominant option were excluded from the final analytic sample, as this indicated potential difficulties in understanding the choice tasks or a lack of attention to the survey. In addition, respondents with incomplete demographic information were excluded to ensure the validity and completeness of covariate-adjusted analyses.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were first used to examine the demographic distribution of participants. To analyze women’s preferences, we estimated mixed logit (MXL) models that allow for random taste variation across individuals, which is particularly suited for capturing preference heterogeneity in health-related decision-making. All categorical variables were dummy coded, with the reference levels constrained to zero, allowing direct interpretation of utility differences relative to the base category. Model estimation was conducted in R using the Apollo package11. To examine whether breast cancer risk level influenced preferences, we further stratified the sample by risk scenario and estimated subgroup MXL models.

We then assessed the conditional relative importance of each attribute under the 30% and 80% risk scenarios by measuring the range between its highest and lowest estimated preference weights within each class. Attributes with greater conditional relative importance values were interpreted as having a more pronounced impact on women’s decisions regarding test uptake. To enable meaningful comparisons, conditional relative importance scores were scaled within each class so that the total importance across all attributes summed to 100%.

To aid interpretation, we also calculated marginalWTP estimates for each attribute level by separately estimating results using continuous cost. These were computed as the negative of the ratio of the attribute coefficient to the cost coefficient, with standard errors and 95% confidence intervals obtained using the Delta method. Finally, we explored potential preference heterogeneity by interacting key attributes with demographic variables (e.g., age, educational level, marital status).
