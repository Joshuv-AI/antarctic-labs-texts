Descriptive analysis of routine childhood vaccination coverage of selected vaccines in Nigeria (1980–2023) using the Global Burden of Disease Study (2023)
Authors: Adewunmi Akingbola, Abiodun Adegbesan, Olajumoke Adewole, Nwaeze Emmanuel, Opeyemi Joshua Alabi, Petra Mariaria
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRoutine childhood immunization remains an effective public health intervention for reducing childhood morbidity and mortality from vaccine-preventable diseases. This study examined trends in routine childhood vaccination coverage in Nigeria from 1980 to 2023. We conducted a retrospective secondary descriptive analysis using routine childhood vaccination coverage estimates from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2023. Annual national coverage estimates for BCG, DTP3, Polio3, MCV1, and HepB3 vaccines were analyzed from 1980 to 2023. Temporal trends were assessed using line plots and forest plots with associated 95% uncertainty intervals, and the highest and lowest performing years for each vaccine were identified. Coverage for all assessed vaccines was lowest in 1980, followed by gradual expansion during the late 1980s and early 1990s, stagnation between the mid-1990s and early 2000s, and improvement after 2005. Peak coverage occurred between 2019 and 2021 for most vaccines, with BCG reaching the highest in 2021 at 73.6% (95% UI: 64.7–78.3). However, no vaccine achieved the WHO target of 90% national coverage throughout the study period. Although Nigeria has made measurable progress in routine childhood immunization over four decades, coverage remains below optimal levels, highlighting the need for targeted and equitable immunization strategies.

Introduction

Across the globe, routine childhood vaccinations have been found to be the most cost-effective approach for reducing childhood disease burden, especially when compared with other interventions that may demand infrastructural investments.1 About 2–3 million lives are saved worldwide every year contributing substantially to the reduction in global infant mortality rate from 65 per 1,000 live births in 1990 to 29 in 2018.2 In the United States, these vaccinations have dramatically reduced morbidity, mortality, and disability caused by vaccine-preventable diseases, with estimated 21 million hospitalizations, 732,000 deaths, and 322 million cases of disease averted in the United States between 1994 and 2013.3 Among infectious diseases targeted by vaccines recommended before 1980, polio, measles, and rubella, have achieved elimination status as defined by the World Health Organization4 – and smallpox has been eradicated.5 Diphtheria and tetanus have declined markedly in incidence with routine immunization and are well controlled whereas the incidence of pertussis and mumps has declined when compared with pre-vaccine levels but still fluctuates given periodic outbreaks since vaccination was introduced.4,5 Universally, the public health burden of diseases targeted in the childhood immunization program between 1980 and 2005 has decreased by more than 80%.5 While it has been established that routine vaccination plays a key role in protecting children from preventable diseases,6 vaccines can have several economic benefits.7 By preventing an episode of the disease through a vaccine, the economic costs are averted. This is particularly important for low and middle-income countries (LMICs) where a large part of medical expenditure is out-of-pocket. Routine vaccination has a positive effect on social and health equity among populations. Infectious disease incidence and mortality are often associated with poverty, and exacerbated by lack of access to clean water, sanitation, and basic hygiene among the poor. Routine childhood vaccinations are, thus, estimated to avert the largest burden of diseases, associated medical expenses, and loss in economic productivity in the poorest segments of the society.8,9 A recent study in 41 Gavi-eligible LMICs found that universal coverage of the measles, rotavirus, and pneumococcal conjugate vaccines would avert a total of 12.6 million cases of catastrophic health expenditure which might have otherwise propelled patients into poverty.8 In the absence of protection against infectious diseases, children may also incur early recurrent infections that may negatively impact cognitive ability and development.10 The theoretical basis of the long-term benefits of vaccines is anchored in the widely accepted “fetal origins” hypothesis.11 Malnutrition, infection, pregnancy and birth complications, and under-stimulation during the first 1000 d of life can have lasting impact on health, cognitive, and economic outcomes well into old age. A longitudinal study conducted by Nandi A et al among selected children each in India, Ethiopia and Vietnam showed that when measles vaccine is given at the age of 6–18 months of life, children develop higher anthropometric Z score, percentage points higher on standardized cognition tests.12 The association between National Immunization Programme of India and height-for-age and weight-for-age z-scores among children selected years has been found to be higher compared to other children.13 In this study, we aim to examine the trends in routine childhood vaccination coverage in Nigeria from 1980 to 2023. This objective will focus on how coverage rates changed for selected routine childhood vaccines during the period 1980–2023. We also aim to determine which vaccines have achieved the highest and lowest coverage overall, as well as annually and which periods were trends of increase, stagnation and decrease observed?

Methods

Data source

The vaccination coverage estimates used in this study were derived from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2023,14 which synthesizes multiple data sources including WHO – UNICEF Joint Reporting Form data, Demographic and Health Surveys, Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys, and other administrative and survey datasets using advanced statistical modeling approaches. Specifically, the GBD study employed spatiotemporal Gaussian process regression and meta-regression Bayesian regularized trimmed models to account for data heterogeneity, reporting bias, uncertainty, and temporal trends in vaccine coverage estimates across countries and years. These have been well documented in literature.15

For the present study, we extracted annual national coverage estimates for BCG, DTP3, Polio3, MCV1, and HepB3 in Nigeria from 1980 to 2023, and the specific vaccines were selected because they represent core indicators of routine immunization system performance and are widely used in monitoring national and global vaccination progress. We conducted a retrospective secondary descriptive trend analysis using descriptive visualization techniques, including line plots and forest plots, to assess temporal patterns in vaccination coverage and associated uncertainty intervals. Since this study uses an open-access, publicly available dataset from the Global Burden of Disease Study, which does not involve the collection of personal or identifiable information, it does not require ethical approval. The dataset provides aggregated, de-identified data on vaccination coverage, making it suitable for analysis without any ethical concerns related to privacy or consent. As such, this research adheres to the guidelines for the use of secondary data from publicly accessible sources.

Statistical analysis

To analyze the trends in vaccination coverage, we used line plots to visualize how the coverage for key vaccines evolved from 1980 to 2023. These plots were created for each selected vaccine, including BCG, DTP3, Polio3, MCV1, and HepB3, with the mean vaccination coverage plotted against each year. This allowed us to clearly observe the overall trajectory of vaccine uptake, highlighting significant increases, plateaus, and declines. In addition to the trend visualization, we identified the highest and lowest performing years for each vaccine based on the mean coverage. The highest performing year for each vaccine represents the year with the greatest level of coverage, while the lowest performing year indicates the period with the least coverage. These years were highlighted in the analysis to pinpoint critical periods of potential success and challenge in vaccination efforts. Forest plots were also used to show the uncertainty intervals around the mean coverage, reflecting the uncertainty in the estimates for each year and this helped us to understand the data and it provided a clearer picture of trends over time. The latest version R-4.6.0 software was used in the analysis.

Results

This section describes the trends of each vaccine coverage between 1980 and 2023 whilst highlighting the years with the highest and lowest vaccination coverage.

Results for the BCG vaccine

The highest performing year for the BCG vaccine was 2021, with a mean coverage of 0.736 (95% UI: 0.647–0.783) and marks the peak in vaccination coverage. Conversely, the lowest performing year was 1980, with a mean coverage of 0.319 (95% UI: 0.214–0.444). Figure 1 clearly shows that coverage steadily increased from 1980 through to 1990, with wider uncertainty intervals in the early years, indicating greater uncertainty in the estimates and the intervals narrowed as the years progressed, particularly after the early 2000s, suggesting more precise estimates of coverage. The uncertainty intervals in recent years are consistently narrow, reflecting either higher certainty about the vaccination coverage or influence of data density, modeling and estimation processes but got wider from 2021. The trend plot in Figure 2 corroborates Figure 1 and demonstrates the yearly coverage rates over time, with notable peaks and valleys. The plot shows an initial rise in coverage from 1980 to the early 1990s, followed by a decline between 1990–2000 and subsequent gradual increase in coverage, with another steep rise from around 2017 to 2021. The last few years show the highest coverage rates, reaching 0.736 in 2021, highlighting the potential success of recent vaccination campaigns and improved immunization efforts. Discussion

This descriptive analysis demonstrates that routine childhood vaccination coverage in Nigeria from 1980 to 2023 followed a trajectory characterized by early low uptake, expansion in the late 1980s and early 1990s, stagnation during the mid-1990s to early 2000s, and renewed improvement after 2005. These trends are broadly consistent with patterns observed across many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), although Nigeria’s experience reflects important contextual disruptions.

The low baseline coverage observed in 1980 aligns with global evidence showing that immunization systems in many sub-Saharan African countries were still underdeveloped during the early years of the Expanded Programme on Immunization. WHO global coverage reports indicate that DTP3 coverage across Africa remained below 40% throughout much of the 1980s due to weak primary health care infrastructure and limited cold-chain capacity.16 Nigeria’s similarly low early coverage therefore reflects structural health system limitations rather than antigen-specific challenges. The potential increase in coverage observed through the late 1980s and early 1990s parallels the global scale-up associated with the Universal Childhood Immunization initiative. International coordination and outreach expansion during this period resulted in marked increases in coverage across LMICs.1 Nigeria’s upward trajectory during this phase appears consistent with that broader global momentum.

However, the stagnation and fluctuations observed between the mid-1990s and early 2000s contrast with the steady progress reported in some other LMICs during the same period.17 A key contextual explanation is Nigeria’s documented vaccine resistance episode during the 2003–2004 polio vaccine boycott in Northern Nigeria. Yahya18 described how mistrust in government and misinformation significantly reduced polio vaccine uptake. Subsequent analyses in literature confirmed that this resistance extended beyond polio vaccination and disrupted routine immunization systems more broadly.19

BCG achieved higher coverage levels in this study, likely because it is typically administered as a single dose shortly after birth, often during initial contact with health facilities, whereas DTP3, Polio3, and HepB3 require multiple visits over time. This suggests that while initial vaccine acceptance may be relatively high, sustaining completion of multidose vaccination schedules remains a challenge, likely due to barriers such as missed follow-up visits, transportation difficulties, healthcare access limitations, and health system disruptions. The plateau in DTP3 and Polio3 coverage observed in this study aligns with those findings, suggesting that sociopolitical factors undermined broader immunization performance during that period. However, it is important to note that the study did not test for the influence of any contextual factors. The renewed upward trend after 2005 corresponds with intensified immunization strengthening efforts and expanded global financing mechanisms. Nigeria’s engagement with Gavi, the Vaccine Alliance, has been associated with improvements in cold-chain infrastructure, vaccine introduction, and routine immunization strengthening.20 The introduction and steady increase of HepB3 coverage in this study mirrors patterns observed in other Gavi-supported countries, where new antigen introduction is followed by gradual system consolidation.20 These improvements likely reflect both financial investment and enhanced programmatic oversight.

Despite long-term progress, vaccination coverage in Nigeria remained below the WHO Immunization Agenda 2030 target of at least 90% national coverage of all routine childhood vaccines16 and 95% for measles-containing vaccines due to the measles disease’s high transmissibility. This finding is consistent with Nigeria Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS) data, which show persistent regional disparities in full immunization coverage, particularly between northern and southern states.21 Broader LMIC analyses demonstrate that socioeconomic status, maternal education, and rural residence remain strong predictors of incomplete immunization.22 Wiysonge et al. further identified contextual barriers including poverty, geographic inaccessibility, and weak health facility distribution as major determinants of low coverage in sub-Saharan Africa.23 These structural inequalities likely explain why national-level improvements did not translate into universal coverage. Several vaccines reached peak coverage around 2019–2021. This period coincides with Nigeria’s certification as wild polio-free in 2020, which may have strengthened immunization systems and surveillance. However, global modeling studies suggest that the COVID-19 pandemic caused significant disruptions to routine immunization services worldwide, with declines in DTP3 coverage observed in many countries during 2020.24 Nigeria’s relative stability or rebound during this period may therefore reflect catch-up campaigns or intensified outreach rather than uninterrupted system resilience. Slight widening of uncertainty intervals in recent years suggests continuing vulnerability and the need for sustained monitoring. Future immunization strategies in Nigeria should include contingency plans to reduce disruptions in vaccine coverage during public health or sociopolitical crises. Strengthening the National Immunization Programme alongside investments in water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) may also help reduce outbreaks of vaccine-preventable and other infectious diseases. Future studies should further assess vaccine effectiveness among zero-dose, partially vaccinated, and fully vaccinated children, as well as explore the broader effects of immunization on child health and wellbeing.

Overall, these findings reinforce the need for more work to be done to achieve universal vaccine coverage in Nigeria. Nigeria’s trajectory reflects both the gains achievable through sustained investment and the fragility of progress when governance or public confidence is disrupted. Continued strengthening of primary health care systems, targeted interventions in underserved regions, and strategies to rebuild and sustain community trust will be critical to achieving equitable and durable vaccination coverage.

Limitations

This study has some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the analysis relied on modeled national estimates from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2023 in conjunction with primary survey data, and the results are therefore dependent on the assumptions, data sources, and statistical modeling approaches underlying the GBD framework. In addition, national-level estimates may mask important subnational disparities in vaccination coverage across different regions and populations in Nigeria, particularly in underserved and conflict-affected areas. The underlying datasets used by GBD may also be affected by reporting inconsistencies and recall bias from household surveys. Finally, as a descriptive analysis, this study cannot establish causal relationships between observed vaccination trends and specific health policies or interventions over time.

Recommendations

The findings of this analysis suggest that although Nigeria has achieved measurable improvements in routine childhood vaccination coverage over the past four decades, progress has been uneven and remains below WHO thresholds of at least 90% national coverage. To accelerate gains and ensure sustainability, several targeted interventions are warranted. First, immunization strengthening efforts should prioritize subnational equity rather than focusing solely on national averages. Persistent disparities between northern and southern states as evidenced in literature, require geographically targeted strategies. Therefore, the National Primary Health Care Development Agency (NPHCDA) should implement ward-level microplanning using routine immunization data to identify and track zero-dose and under-immunized children. States consistently performing below national averages based on Nigeria’s own immunization data, should receive conditional funding tied to measurable improvements in DTP3 and MCV1 coverage. Performance-based grants could incentivize local government areas (LGAs) to improve outreach delivery and reporting accuracy.

The persistently lower coverage observed for DTP3, Polio3, and HepB3 vaccines compared with BCG suggests the need to strengthen follow-up and completion of multi-dose vaccination schedules in Nigeria. Many children may receive initial contact vaccines such as BCG at birth but fail to complete subsequent doses likely due to several factors. To address this, research must be done to identify the specific barriers, reminder and recall systems using mobile phone messaging, community health workers, and digital immunization tracking should be expanded to reduce dropout rates between vaccine doses. Outreach vaccination services should also be intensified in rural and hard-to-reach communities, particularly for vaccines requiring multiple visits such as DTP3 and HepB3.

The decline and stagnation in MCV1 and Polio3 coverage observed during the 1990s and early 2000s highlight the importance of sustaining mass immunization campaigns and strengthening community trust in vaccination programs. Public health authorities should continue targeted supplementary immunization activities for measles and polio in regions with historically low uptake, while integrating vaccination education into maternal and child health services to improve acceptance. In areas affected by insecurity or weak health infrastructure, partnerships with local community leaders, traditional institutions, and non-governmental organizations may help improve vaccine access and reduce missed children. Strengthening disease surveillance systems for measles and poliovirus outbreaks is also critical to rapidly identify gaps in population immunity.

Although BCG coverage showed the highest and most consistent improvement over time, national coverage remained below WHO threshold throughout the study period. This needs to be investigated further and measures taken to address the factors influencing its low coverage. Expanding maternal and newborn healthcare services, especially in underserved communities, may indirectly improve early childhood vaccination uptake. In addition, efforts to sustain recent gains after 2015 should include increased domestic funding for routine immunization, stronger cold-chain infrastructure, and improved subnational monitoring systems to identify states and local government areas with persistently low vaccine coverage.

Finally, future research should prioritize subnational analysis and implementation efforts. Understanding why certain states may have consistently outperformed others as established in literature, could generate replicable best practices. Linking vaccination data with socioeconomic indicators would also help policymakers design more targeted interventions. Sustained political commitment, targeted resource allocation, and community-centered strategies will be essential if Nigeria is to transition from incremental progress to universal and equitable vaccination coverage.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this descriptive analysis demonstrates that Nigeria has made measurable progress in routine childhood vaccination coverage from 1980 to 2023, transitioning from very low baseline levels to more stable and improved uptake in recent years. However, the trajectory has not been linear, with periods of stagnation and vulnerability reflecting the influence of political instability, health system weaknesses, and community trust dynamics. Although peak coverage was achieved between 2019 and 2021 for several vaccines, national levels remain below WHO targets. The narrowing uncertainty intervals over time could be related to estimates stability, data density or could be a result of the estimation processes used, yet sustaining and accelerating coverage gains will require deliberate investment in primary health care strengthening, equitable resource allocation, and proactive community engagement. Achieving universal and durable immunization coverage in Nigeria will depend on vaccine availability and building resilient systems capable of withstanding sociopolitical and public health shocks.
