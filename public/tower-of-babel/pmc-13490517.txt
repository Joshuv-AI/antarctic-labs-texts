Size- and lifetime-aware disjoint dominating set formation and multi-armed-bandit-based scheduling for energy-efficient IoT-WSNs in sustainable smart city applications
Authors: Ahmed Salim, Ahmed M Khedr, Ahmed A El-Sawy, Bader Alwasel, Walid Osamy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe versatility and broad applicability of IoT-based heterogeneous wireless sensor networks (HWSNs) make them key enablers of sustainability objectives in sustainable smart cities (SSCs). However, their heterogeneous architecture must be carefully managed to ensure reliable and energy-efficient operation. To prolong network lifetime and avoid premature node depletion, effective energy management mechanisms are essential. One promising approach is to exploit the concept of dominating sets (DSs), whereby sensor nodes are partitioned into disjoint DSs, and only one set is activated at a time. In this work, we propose EAAS-S4C-MAB, an enhanced framework for IoT-based HWSNs in SSCs that combines skyline-based DS formation, four-case, size- and lifetime-aware scheduling, and energy-aware data collection within the active DS. In the DS formation phase, the proposed Energy-Attentive Algorithm with Skyline (EAAS) generates multiple valid disjoint DS candidates per iteration, evaluates them jointly in terms of lifetime and size, and applies a BNL_Skyline procedure to retain only non-dominated candidates. In the scheduling phase, the proposed S4C-MAB algorithm classifies DSs into four cases according to size and lifetime and uses a multi-armed bandit to learn the relative priority of these cases online, replacing fixed case weights with adaptive case-level preferences. These learned priorities are combined with a dynamically recomputed adjusted lifetime, along with case-dependent activation caps and cooldown intervals, to guide DS activation. During communication, the active DS nodes form a chain, and the node nearest to the sink serves as the chain leader for final data delivery. Simulation results show that EAAS-S4C-MAB achieves more balanced DS utilization, reduces the premature depletion of fragile sets, and significantly extends network lifetime compared to other baseline methods, while preserving network coverage and connectivity.Keywords: Dominating set, Heterogeneous wireless sensor networks, Network lifetime, Sustainable urbanization, Multi-armed-bandit, Energy consumption, Sustainable smart city, Modern citiesSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

With rapid global urbanization, the concept of the Sustainable Smart City (SSC) has emerged as an important approach for addressing the challenges associated with modern urban development. As the urban population continues to grow, SSCs are expected to provide efficient, reliable, and sustainable services across multiple domains, including smart governance, smart water and waste management, smart transportation, and smart connectivity. One of the key technological enablers of these services is the Internet of Things (IoT), particularly through Wireless Sensor Networks (WSNs), which support data acquisition, monitoring, and communication for a wide range of smart applications.

In IoT-based WSNs, sensor nodes are typically deployed to monitor physical environments and forward collected data to a sink node through multi-hop communication. Unlike regular sensor nodes, sink nodes are generally equipped with abundant energy, stronger computing capabilities, wider communication ranges, and larger memory capacity1–4. Despite their broad adoption in agricultural monitoring, healthcare, smart homes, and industrial automation5, WSNs still face several practical limitations, including restricted battery capacity, limited communication range, constrained storage, and limited processing capability6,7. These limitations become even more critical in Heterogeneous WSNs (HWSNs), where nodes differ in energy level, sensing capability, and communication resources. As a result, efficient energy management remains one of the most important design challenges for ensuring network stability, survivability, and long operational lifetime in SSC environments5,8.

Many strategies have been proposed to conserve energy and prolong the lifetime of WSNs. Since sensing and communication are the major sources of energy consumption, effective energy-aware scheduling is essential for extending network service time. Existing approaches include clustering-based routing, machine-learning-assisted cluster-head selection, and sleep–awake scheduling9,10. For example, Juwaied and Jackowska-Strumillo9 proposed ML-enhanced variants of the DEEC protocol, namely DEEC-KNN and DEEC-KM, by integrating K-Nearest Neighbours and K-Means into the cluster-formation process to improve energy efficiency, packet delivery, and network stability. Although such clustering-based methods improve energy utilization, they do not explicitly coordinate the activation of disjoint node sets for sleep–awake scheduling. One common approach is to alternate sensor nodes between sleep and active states11. Because WSNs are often densely deployed, temporarily switching a subset of nodes to sleep mode can significantly reduce energy consumption without compromising monitoring functionality. Under this strategy, only the nodes in the active set perform sensing and communication, while the remaining nodes stay in a low-power sleep state. This work builds on the concept of Dominating Sets (DSs) in graph theory to regulate the sleep–awake schedule of heterogeneous sensor nodes. A DS is a subset of nodes such that every node in the network either belongs to the subset or has at least one neighbor in it. To further reduce energy consumption, the network can be partitioned into disjoint DSs, only one set is active at a time12. This sleep–awake scheduling strategy prolongs network lifetime by distributing sensing and data-gathering tasks across multiple DSs.

Related work

The rapid increase of smart sensing devices within Sustainable Smart Cities (SSCs) has generated a pressing need for algorithmic approaches that are both resource-efficient and adaptive to the limited resources and heterogeneous characteristics of Wireless Sensor Networks (WSNs)13–16. The successful realization of technological advancements in SSCs relies on robust infrastructure as well as the integration of e-governance policies for efficient resource management17–19. Integrating WSNs with analogous technologies offers a wide array of applications in SSCs and has the potential to yield comprehensive strategies for sustainable communities20–22. Data insights from WSNs can be used to make SSCs more sustainable by enabling city planners and decision-makers to make informed choices that promote environmentally friendly urban development. Extending the lifetime of WSNs not only reduces environmental impact but also improves data reliability and frees up resources for other sustainable initiatives23–25. Consequently, there is a growing need to design paradigms that simultaneously conserve resources and address multiple facets of SSCs. While much of the literature focuses on energy-saving methods for homogeneous WSNs–where devices have identical energy reserves, connectivity, and processing capabilities–heterogeneous networks present added challenges due to diverse resource capacities26–28. Efficient design and resource management in heterogeneous WSNs (HWSNs) can reduce environmental impact, lower costs, and improve quality of life in smart cities29–31.

To address energy constraints in WSNs, various solutions have been proposed. Since sensing and transmission are the most energy-intensive operations, many studies adopt a sleep–awake scheme as an effective means of conserving node energy19–21. For example, Threshold LEACH (T-LEACH)32 extends network lifetime by reducing energy consumption in data transmission and aggregation. Unlike LEACH, which selects cluster heads in every round, T-LEACH retains the current cluster heads until their residual energy drops below a dynamic threshold. This reduces cluster-head changes and the associated advertisement overhead. It also considers node heterogeneity and residual energy when selecting the optimal CH. EEM-TDMA33 is an energy-efficient TDMA scheduling method for LEACH-based wireless sensor networks. In this approach, each cluster head sends its cluster size to the base station, the base station determines the maximum cluster capacity, and then broadcasts that value back so all cluster heads can build a common TDMA schedule without inter-cluster-head communication. This reduces control overhead, saves energy, and improves packet delivery and network lifetime. T-LEACHSAS34is an improved LEACH-based protocol for wireless sensor networks that saves energy in two ways: it keeps a cluster head in place until its energy falls below a threshold, and it uses sleep–awake scheduling so sensor nodes stay asleep except when they need to sense or transmit in their TDMA slot. This reduces control overhead, idle listening, and unnecessary transmissions, so the network lasts longer than with basic LEACH and T-LEACH32. CDS-LEACH35 is a modified LEACH protocol for wireless sensor networks that selects cluster heads not only by energy level, but also by whether they belong to a Connected Dominating Set (CDS). This improves connectivity inside clusters, helps data reach cluster heads more reliably, and reduces overall energy use. The paper reports that CDS-LEACH performs better than standard LEACH, especially in denser networks, with later node death, lower cluster-head energy expenditure, and more data delivered. Enhanced LEACH36is a modified LEACH protocol for wireless sensor networks that improves cluster-head selection by considering nodes’ remaining energy and allowing all nodes to rejoin cluster-head election instead of excluding recent cluster heads. This leads to better energy use, later first-node death, more surviving nodes, and more packets delivered than standard LEACH, so the network lasts longer. Recent studies have also explored machine learning (ML)-assisted clustering and routing protocols to improve energy efficiency and network stability in WSNs. Juwaied and Jackowska-Strumillo9 proposed two ML-enhanced variants of the Distributed Energy-Efficient Clustering (DEEC) protocol, namely DEEC-KNN and DEEC-KM. These approaches integrate K-Nearest Neighbours (K-NN) and K-Means clustering into the cluster-head selection process to improve adaptive cluster formation, packet delivery, and network lifetime. Their results showed improved network stability and up to 110% improvement in packet delivery compared with the original DEEC protocol. Similarly, Juwaied10 introduced EMO-PEGASIS, a dual-phase machine-learning-based PEGASIS enhancement that combines K-means clustering with K-NN-based routing decisions to reduce energy consumption and end-to-end delay. Although these ML-assisted protocols demonstrate the potential of adaptive learning methods for WSN optimisation, they remain clustering- or routing-oriented frameworks and do not explicitly address the coordinated formation of multiple disjoint dominating sets, DS size–lifetime balancing, or activation scheduling.

Although LEACH-based, PEGASIS-based, and ML-assisted routing protocols improve energy efficiency, delay, and load balancing, they do not explicitly address the coordinated formation and activation of multiple disjoint dominating sets. This limitation has motivated more structured DS-based approaches. One promising strategy leverages the concept of Dominating Sets (DSs), where nodes are partitioned into disjoint DSs and only one set is active at a time27,28. Determining the minimum DS is, however, an NP-Hard problem23. Early works such as27,28 proposed centralized approximation techniques with approximation factors O(łog n) and , respectively. The DS technique employing a dual-greedy approach for sleep–awake scheduling was first presented in27 and later extended in28 through the concept of maximum disjoint DSs (Domatic Partition, DP) in Unit Disk Graphs (UDGs) for homogeneous networks. To overcome scalability issues in centralized schemes,30,37 explored distributed algorithms for DS construction. In29, DSs were generated using a distributed Maximal Independent Set (MIS) principle. The work in38 proposed a three-stage approach with Pseudo DSs to form smaller Connected DSs (CDSs) while improving connectivity and coverage. In20, CDS connectivity was enhanced using a non-trivial potential function, and a greedy algorithm was applied to generate three-connected components from Tutte’s decomposition of a two-connected graph.

Additional DS-based enhancements have been proposed. For example,24 presented a clustering approach (EBDSC) relying on DSs, and25 introduced a decentralized selection method with a Wait Before Start (WBS) policy to allow dominant nodes to self-announce based on neighborhood size. In31, a cell layout was used to address the DP problem by constructing cliques per cluster and implementing a distributed nucleus algorithm (DNA) that executes in finite iterations under the congested message-passing model. Although these algorithms improved DS construction efficiency, most focused on homogeneous settings or static energy assumptions. Some works, such as22, generated CDSs in heterogeneous environments using local search strategies; however, their complexity remains high, and scalability is limited.

A significant step toward addressing energy heterogeneity in SSC-based WSNs was taken in the original EADDSA framework39, which introduced the Energy Attentive Algorithm (EAA) to form energy-aware disjoint DSs and a scheduling strategy to extend network lifetime. While EADDSA improved lifetime compared to prior methods, it optimized DS formation using energy only, without explicitly considering DS size or workload balance, and employed a scheduling policy that could overuse certain sets. Motivated by these gaps, we propose EAAS-S4C-MAB, an enhanced DS-based framework for IoT-enabled HWSNs in SSC applications. EAAS-S4C-MAB integrates EAAS, a multi-objective DS formation algorithm that balances lifetime and size using the BNL_Skyline procedure, with S4C-MAB, a size- and lifetime-aware scheduling strategy employing a four-case policy with activation caps and cooldowns. By jointly optimizing DS formation and scheduling, EAAS-S4C-MAB achieves more balanced energy consumption, mitigates premature depletion of fragile sets, and extends network lifetime beyond that of the original EADDSA.

Proposed EAAS-S4C-MAB technique

The proposed EAAS-S4C-MAB (Energy-Aware Disjoint Dominating Set Algorithm with Skyline and Four-Case Multi-Armed Bandit Scheduling) consists of three tightly coupled components that operate sequentially to maximize the lifetime of heterogeneous wireless sensor networks (HWSNs) while maintaining coverage and connectivity.

EAAS: energy- and size-balanced disjoint ds construction. In this phase, the network is modeled as a graph, and the algorithm iteratively constructs disjoint dominating sets (DSs) that satisfy connectivity, disjointness, and positive-energy constraints. EAAS generates multiple valid DS candidates per iteration and evaluates them based on two objectives: (i) lifetime L(D), defined by the minimum residual energy in the set divided by the per-round consumption rate, and (ii) size |D|, i.e., the number of active nodes. The BNL_Skyline procedure is then applied to identify the set of non-dominated DSs in the lifetime–size space. The final DS for the iteration is selected from the Skyline set using either a composite-score rule or a knee-point heuristic, thereby balancing robustness and energy efficiency.

The final DS for the iteration is selected from the Skyline set using either a composite-score rule or a knee-point heuristic, thereby balancing robustness and energy efficiency.

S4C-MAB: size- and lifetime-aware DS scheduling with four-case policy. In this scheduling phase, the disjoint DSs produced by EAAS are classified into four cases according to their size and residual lifetime: small–high, large–high, small–low, and large–low. Each case is associated with a specific activation cap and cooldown interval in order to prevent premature depletion of fragile sets and to maintain steady use of robust sets. In each scheduling round, the scheduler recomputes an adjusted lifetime for every DS, incorporating a size penalty and, when available, a load penalty. A lightweight multi-armed bandit mechanism then learns a time-varying priority weight for each case based on the observed scheduling reward, and the DS with the maximum case-weighted adjusted lifetime is selected for activation. In this way, scheduling decisions adapt online as node energies and case utilities evolve.

System model and problem formulation

In our proposed framework, we represent the WSN as a graph, denoted as , where V and represent the set of nodes and the communication links between them. If we take nodes i and j ∈ V, an edge between them exists when i is within the communication range of j. A fundamental assumption in our model is the symmetry of communication links between nodes within the WSN, meaning that all nodes in the network share an identical communication range. Despite this symmetry, the battery-powered nodes exhibit heterogeneity in their initial energy levels. Additionally, we consider a constant energy usage rate across all nodes in the network.

A subset D ⊆ V is called a dominating set (DS) if every node outside D is adjacent to at least one node in D. Formally, D is a DS of G if, for every node , there exists a node u ∈ D such that . In the context of WSNs, the nodes selected in the DS can act as active sensing, aggregation, or forwarding nodes on behalf of their neighboring nodes, while non-selected nodes can remain in a low-power sleep state. Therefore, rotating the activation of different DSs can reduce redundant communication and extend the network lifetime. A connected dominating set (CDS) is a stricter form of a DS. A subset C ⊆ V is a CDS if it is a dominating set of G and the subgraph induced by C, denoted by G[C], is connected. Thus, while a DS guarantees that every non-selected node is covered by at least one selected neighbor, a CDS additionally guarantees connectivity among the selected nodes 40. In this work, the selected sets are required to be DSs rather than CDSs because communication among active nodes is handled separately through the chain-based data-gathering phase. A DS with a smaller size tends to exhibit superior energy efficiency, as fewer active nodes per round allow more nodes to enter a low-power sleep state. However, smaller DSs can increase the workload per node, potentially reducing the DS lifetime if low-energy nodes are selected. Conversely, larger DSs distribute the load over more nodes but keep more devices active, consuming more total energy per round. Therefore, a trade-off exists between DS size and DS lifetime.

To address this balance, we allow DS selection to consider both the minimum residual energy of the set and its size, promoting solutions that are energy-aware while avoiding excessively large or small DSs. A strategy involves substituting an existing DS with another disjoint DS and transitioning the nodes in the previous set to sleep mode. This entails employing as many disjoint DSs as possible, utilizing each for a specific duration, and subsequently replacing it with another set. The primary goal is to improve the HWSN lifespan by carefully selecting disjoint DSs based on both energy and size considerations. The lifespan of a DS is dependent on the node with the lowest energy, factoring in its energy consumption rate, while its size affects per-node workload. The cumulative network lifetime is calculated as the sum of the lifetimes of all disjoint DSs.

Overview of multi-armed bandits

A multi-armed bandit (MAB) is a sequential decision-making framework in which a learner repeatedly selects one action, called an arm, and observes a reward only for the chosen action. The goal is to maximize cumulative reward over time while balancing exploration of less-sampled arms and exploitation of arms that currently appear most rewarding. The stochastic MAB formulation was introduced by Robbins, with later regret-optimal allocation results established by Lai and Robbins, and practical finite-time upper-confidence-bound (UCB) policies developed by Auer et al.42,43.

Overview of skyline operator

The Skyline operator44 identifies points (tuples) in a dataset that are optimal with respect to multiple, often conflicting, criteria. A classic example is selecting a hotel based on price and distance to the beach. Typically, hotels closer to the beach are more expensive, and cheaper hotels are farther away. The Skyline operator returns only those hotels that are not worse in both criteria than any other hotel.

DS scheduling algorithm for EAAS

After EAAS identifies DSs, the scheduling phase aims to maximize the HWSN lifetime by allocating DS activation rounds according to both residual lifetime and set size. Although a fixed scheduling strategy has low computational complexity, it ignores structural differences among DSs. As a result, some sets, particularly small sets with high per-node load, may be depleted prematurely.

In EAAS, each DS is characterized by its estimated lifetime L(D) and size |D|. These two quantities allow the scheduler to classify each DS into one of four cases:

Case A: small size and high lifetime, referred to as small–strong.

Case A: small size and high lifetime, referred to as small–strong.

Case B: large size and high lifetime, referred to as large–strong.

Case C: small size and low lifetime, referred to as small–weak.

Case C: small size and low lifetime, referred to as small–weak.

Case D: large size and low lifetime, referred to as large–weak.

Case D: large size and low lifetime, referred to as large–weak.

These cases guide how frequently and for how long each DS should be activated. Small–strong sets can save energy by allowing many nodes to sleep, but they should be activated in short bursts with cooldown periods to avoid rapid depletion. Large–strong sets can operate for longer periods because the communication load is distributed over more nodes. Small–weak and large–weak sets are used more conservatively and mainly serve as backup coverage options.

Bandit-based scheduling justification and reward meaning

The DS scheduling problem is naturally a sequential decision-making problem. At each round, the scheduler selects one DS for activation, but the long-term effect of this activation is not fully known before the action is taken. Activating a DS reduces the residual energy of its active nodes, may deplete bottleneck nodes, can reduce the lifetime of other candidate DSs, and may invalidate some residual DSs after pruning. Therefore, the quality of a scheduling decision can only be evaluated after observing its effect on the remaining candidate pool.

A fixed policy does not use this feedback and may repeatedly activate fragile or energy-expensive DSs. Conversely, a purely greedy policy may repeatedly select the DS with the highest immediate EAAS score, which can accelerate the depletion of critical nodes and reduce future scheduling flexibility. The MAB formulation provides a lightweight online mechanism that balances these two behaviors. It exploits DS cases that have historically preserved future network utility while still exploring less frequently selected cases whose usefulness may change as the network evolves.

In this work, the bandit is applied at the DS-case level rather than at the individual-DS level. This reduces the learning dimension and avoids unstable estimates when the number or validity of candidate DSs changes over time due to energy depletion. The bandit component does not replace the energy-aware EAAS score; instead, it adaptively adjusts the scheduler’s preference according to the observed ability of each DS case to preserve future network operating capability.

Unlike a strict scheduler, S4C-MAB does not terminate merely because the strict eligible set is empty. Instead, it applies a two-stage rescue rule. First, it relaxes temporary cooldown and consecutive-use restrictions and reconsiders all valid residual DSs with positive adjusted lifetime. If such candidates exist, it still selects according to the learned weighted score. If no relaxed candidate exists but valid residual DSs remain, the scheduler activates the valid residual DS with the largest residual lifetime. Therefore, the algorithm terminates only when no valid residual DS remains. This rescue mechanism prevents premature stopping caused by temporary activation constraints. It also aligns the execution logic with the main objective of lifetime-aware DS scheduling: maintaining feasible coverage for as long as possible while favoring DSs that offer strong lifetime, suitable size, and good long-term observed reward.

Ablation study: effect of skyline-based DS selection

To evaluate the contribution of skyline-based filtering in the DS formation stage, we compare the proposed EAAS with a non-skyline variant, denoted as EAA-NS. Both methods use the same DS candidate generation procedure, but differ in the selection strategy. In EAA-NS, the DS is selected directly from the full candidate pool using a scalar objective, whereas EAAS first applies skyline filtering to retain only non-dominated DS candidates before final selection.

Table 6 shows that skyline filtering consistently improves the average network lifetime across all tested network sizes. For 50 nodes, the average lifetime increases from 54.81 to 56.33, corresponding to an improvement of approximately 2.78%. For 100 nodes, the lifetime increases from 87.52 to 95.21, corresponding to an improvement of about 8.79%. The gain becomes more pronounced for larger networks: for 150 nodes, the average lifetime increases from 110.16 to 120.79, corresponding to an improvement of approximately 9.64%, while for 200 nodes it increases from 133.95 to 146.06, corresponding to an improvement of about 9.03%.

These results indicate that skyline filtering becomes increasingly beneficial as the network grows. In small networks, the number of feasible DS candidates is relatively limited, so the opportunity for skyline filtering to eliminate dominated solutions is smaller. As the number of nodes increases, however, the candidate pool becomes richer and more diverse, which increases the likelihood of dominated DSs appearing. In such cases, skyline filtering becomes more effective because it removes inferior candidates and preserves only high-quality trade-offs between DS lifetime and DS size.

The observed improvements can therefore be explained by the role of skyline filtering in enhancing DS selection quality. Without skyline filtering, EAA-NS selects DSs directly using a scalar objective, which may favor candidates that perform well in one objective but remain suboptimal in the overall lifetime–size trade-off. This can lead to the early selection of inefficient DSs and to less balanced energy depletion across nodes. In contrast, EAAS considers only Pareto-efficient DS candidates after skyline pruning. By eliminating candidates that are inferior in both lifetime and size, skyline filtering prevents clearly suboptimal DSs from being selected and leads to more balanced DS formation.

Another important observation is that the gain from skyline filtering is stable across different network sizes and becomes stronger in medium and large networks. This suggests that skyline filtering does not merely exploit a particular parameter setting, but systematically improves the quality of the selected DSs. As network connectivity and the number of feasible DS options increase, skyline filtering ensures that these additional choices are exploited more effectively.

Overall, the ablation results demonstrate that skyline-based filtering is a critical component of the proposed framework. The performance gains arise not from changes in candidate generation or parameter tuning, but from improved decision-making during DS selection. By enabling multi-objective selection without requiring manual adjustment of objective weights, skyline filtering improves DS robustness and provides a stronger foundation for subsequent scheduling. These results confirm that the effectiveness of EAAS-S4C-MAB depends not only on adaptive scheduling, but also on high-quality DS formation enabled by skyline-based selection.

An ablation study was conducted to evaluate the contribution of the main components in the proposed EAAS–S4C-MAB framework. The tuned configuration selected from the sensitivity study, and , was used as the full method. In this study, S4C control refers specifically to the cooldown/cap control mechanism, which regulates repeated DS activation through cooldown restrictions and activation caps. Six alternative variants were considered: S4C-MAB without EAAS scoring, EAAS–S4C without MAB/UCB, EAAS–MAB without cooldown/cap control, EAAS–S4C-MAB without the size penalty, a round-robin (RR) baseline, and a random-selection baseline. The contribution of MAB/UCB was more limited under the tuned setting. This indicates that cooldown/cap control does not maximize FND alone; instead, it regularizes repeated DS activation and preserves accumulated residual DS lifetime/capacity. Thus, the full EAAS–S4C-MAB configuration should be interpreted as a balanced scheduler rather than an absolute FND-maximizing variant.

The DS-size penalty had only a marginal effect under the tested settings. Removing the size penalty changed FND by less than seven rounds in the high-connectivity regime and less than one round in the low-connectivity regime. This confirms the sensitivity-study observation that has a weaker effect than EAAS scoring and cooldown/cap control. The size penalty is therefore retained in the full method as a regularization term, but it is not the dominant driver of performance.

This indicates that the ablation variants mainly affect the distribution of energy depletion across nodes and DSs rather than the average per-round communication cost. Therefore, the ablation discussion focuses on FND, AccLifetime, and DSCount rather than Avg. E/R.

Overall, the ablation study supports three main conclusions. First, EAAS scoring is essential for delaying first-node death, especially in high-connectivity networks with richer DS diversity. Second, the proposed full EAAS–S4C-MAB method substantially outperforms RR and random selection in FND under both connectivity regimes. Third, cooldown/cap control introduces a deliberate trade-off: disabling it improves FND but substantially reduces accumulated residual DS lifetime. Therefore, the full EAAS–S4C-MAB framework provides a balanced scheduling strategy that improves early-node survival relative to conventional baselines while preserving residual DS lifetime more effectively than the no-cooldown/cap variant.

The results also indicate that EAAS–S4C-MAB can be tuned according to the target lifetime objective. The FND-prioritized configuration uses and , which achieved the highest FND among the tested full-method configurations. In contrast, larger values favored AccLifetime, indicating that the exploration coefficient controls a trade-off between delaying first-node death and preserving accumulated residual DS lifetime. Comparison with state-of-the-art methods

The proposed framework, EAAS-S4C-MAB, was evaluated against five representative baselines: T-LEACH32, EEM-TDMA33, T-LEACHSAS34, CDS-LEACH35, and Enhanced LEACH36. These protocols represent different lifetime-extension strategies in WSNs, including threshold-based cluster-head retention, sink-assisted TDMA scheduling, sleep–awake duty cycling, connectivity-aware cluster-head selection, and residual-energy-aware cluster-head election. However, unlike the proposed method, they remain fundamentally cluster-based rather than DS-centric. Accordingly, only the network-level metrics FND and AvgEconsPerRound were used for fair cross-protocol comparison, since these metrics are directly comparable across DS-based and cluster-based methods.

To ensure a fair and consistent evaluation, all baseline protocols and the proposed EAAS-S4C-MAB framework were implemented under the same simulation environment. Specifically, all methods used the same first-order radio energy model, the same node deployment area, the same randomly generated network instances, the same sink locations, the same initial energy distribution, and the same stopping conditions. Two connectivity regimes were considered over a fixed deployment region with n=100 nodes. These regimes correspond to communication radii of 18 m and 25 m. For each regime, 30 independent network instances were generated using different random seeds, and the same set of instances was reused for every compared protocol. The sink was placed at two representative locations, namely the corner position (0, 0) and the center position (50, 50). For protocols originally designed for homogeneous WSNs, the same heterogeneous initial energy values were assigned to their nodes, and their original cluster-head selection or scheduling mechanisms were applied under this common energy setting. The ranking observed in the figures is also technically meaningful. The density effect is equally revealing. By comparing the lower-density and higher-density results in Fig. 10, it becomes clear that EAAS-S4C-MAB benefits from increased node density far more than the baseline protocols. Overall, the figures and tables consistently support the same conclusion. Figure 10 shows that EAAS-S4C-MAB achieves by far the best stability period; Fig. 11 shows that it also yields the lowest average energy dissipation; Fig. 12 confirms that it dominates the joint performance space across all scenarios; and Fig. 13 together with Fig. 14 demonstrate that it is much less sensitive to sink relocation than the baselines. Therefore, the main contribution of EAAS-S4C-MAB is not simply that it reduces energy consumption relative to improved LEACH variants, but that it converts network redundancy into balanced, long-lived, and deployment-robust operation through DS-aware formation and scheduling.

Results summary

The observed performance differences across protocols can be directly explained by their underlying design principles. Among the baselines, T-LEACHSAS34 consistently achieves the best performance because it combines threshold-based cluster-head retention with sleep–awake scheduling. This dual mechanism reduces both cluster-head reselection overhead and idle listening, leading to lower energy dissipation and delayed node death. Enhanced-LEACH36 performs next best due to its residual-energy-aware cluster-head election, which improves energy distribution but still relies on periodic clustering and does not exploit structural redundancy. T-LEACH32 provides moderate gains by avoiding frequent cluster-head changes, but its static threshold mechanism cannot adapt to dynamic energy variations across the network.

CDS-LEACH35 improves connectivity by incorporating connected dominating set (CDS) membership into cluster-head selection; however, this CDS usage remains auxiliary and does not support explicit DS scheduling or lifetime-aware activation. As a result, its performance is limited compared to DS-based approaches. EEM-TDMA33 exhibits the weakest performance because its primary focus is on reducing control overhead through centralized TDMA scheduling, without addressing energy balancing or structural redundancy. Consequently, energy consumption remains high, and node depletion occurs early.

The sensitivity to sink relocation from (0, 0) to (50, 50) can be explained by the communication structure of each protocol. In T-LEACH, T-LEACHSAS, Enhanced LEACH, and CDS-LEACH, data delivery depends strongly on the cluster-head layer, so moving the sink toward the center reduces the average cluster-head-to-sink transmission distance and significantly improves FND. T-LEACHSAS benefits the most among these methods because the shorter sink distance complements its threshold-based cluster-head retention and sleep–awake energy saving. Enhanced LEACH also shows good improvement because, although it improves cluster-head election fairness, it still remains highly dependent on sink-facing cluster-head transmissions. CDS-LEACH benefits from sink relocation because its connectivity-aware cluster-head placement becomes more effective when the final transmission distance to the sink is reduced. By contrast, EEM-TDMA shows only limited sensitivity because its primary improvement lies in reducing scheduling overhead through sink-centralized TDMA construction rather than in changing the structural routing backbone. The proposed EAAS-S4C-MAB framework exhibits the lowest sensitivity because its lifetime performance is driven mainly by skyline-based DS formation, adaptive DS scheduling, and balanced use of alternative DS backbones, while the final sink transmission is handled by the active DS node nearest to the sink. Consequently, its performance depends far less on sink geometry alone than that of the other baselines.

The superior performance of EAAS-S4C-MAB stems from its DS-centric operation, where multiple disjoint DS candidates are generated, filtered via skyline dominance, and scheduled adaptively using a multi-armed bandit policy. This enables balanced utilization of network resources, prevents overuse of weak structures, and continuously adapts to changing energy conditions. In contrast, all baseline protocols operate at the cluster-head level and therefore lack explicit mechanisms for managing multiple alternative backbones.

Statistical significance analysis

To validate the robustness of the proposed EAAS-S4C-MAB protocol, Welch’s two-sample t-tests were conducted against five baseline protocols: EEMTDMA, CDS-LEACH, T-LEACH, ENHANCED-LEACH, and T-LEACHSAS. The tests were performed across all network configurations, including two node-density settings, two sink-location scenarios, and two evaluation metrics: first node death (FND) and average energy consumption per round (AvgEconsPerRound). Each comparison was based on 30 independent simulation runs for EAAS-S4C-MAB and 30 independent simulation runs for the corresponding baseline protocol. In total, 40 pairwise statistical comparisons were conducted: A Bonferroni correction was applied to control the family-wise error rate across the 40 comparisons. For each comparison, the mean, standard deviation, and 95% confidence interval were computed. Welch’s two-sample t-test was used because it does not assume equal variances between EAAS-S4C-MAB and the baseline protocols. The resulting Welch t-statistic, degrees of freedom, and two-sided p-value were then reported for each comparison.

The statistical results show that EAAS-S4C-MAB outperforms all baseline protocols in every tested scenario. All 40 Welch t-tests remained statistically significant after Bonferroni correction, providing strong evidence that the observed improvements are not due to random variation.

Conclusion

This paper presented EAAS-S4C-MAB, an enhanced DS-based framework for extending the lifetime of heterogeneous wireless sensor networks (HWSNs). In the formation stage, the proposed Energy-Attentive Algorithm with Skyline, denoted as EAAS, jointly considers energy and DS size and retains non-dominated DS candidates through skyline selection. In the scheduling stage, S4C-MAB classifies DSs into four cases and applies size- and lifetime-aware activation policies to guide DS usage more effectively. By jointly enhancing both DS formation and DS scheduling, the framework reduces premature DS depletion and achieves more balanced energy utilization across the network. In this work, we first evaluated the proposed EAAS DS formation method and then evaluated the complete EAAS-S4C-MAB framework, which integrates EAAS with S4C-MAB scheduling.

For EAAS, comprehensive simulations and comparisons with well-known DS-based algorithms, including MDP, MFMDP, LSearch, VDepth, and FDepth, showed that the proposed method consistently achieved the highest network lifetime across all tested network sizes and densities. Specifically, the proposed method achieved lifetime ranges of 96.47–195.68 for 200 nodes, 68.43–165.98 for 150 nodes, 43.84–145.04 for 100 nodes, and 31.21–79.85 for 50 nodes. These results represent clear improvements over the original EADDSA, whose corresponding lifetime ranges are 81.75–185.54, 57.59–159.71, 35.47–141.59, and 30.11–77.89, respectively.

In addition, the comparative results showed that EAAS-S4C-MAB consistently outperformed T-LEACH32, EEM-TDMA33, T-LEACHSAS34, CDS-LEACH35, and Enhanced LEACH36 in the two directly comparable network-level metrics, namely FND and AvgEconsPerRound. Across all evaluated deployment scenarios, the proposed framework achieved the highest stability period and the lowest average per-round energy consumption, with especially large gains in higher-density settings. The results further showed that its performance was markedly less sensitive to sink relocation than that of the compared baselines, indicating stronger robustness to deployment geometry.

Overall, by integrating energy- and size-aware DS construction with adaptive four-case scheduling and energy-aware communication, EAAS-S4C-MAB delivers substantial lifetime extension without introducing prohibitive computational overhead. The results confirm that the proposed framework effectively transforms network redundancy into longer lifetime, lower energy dissipation, and more balanced network operation.

Limitation and future work

Although the proposed EAAS-S4C-MAB framework improves energy balancing and network lifetime, several limitations remain. First, the current framework focuses mainly on energy-aware DS formation and scheduling, while fault tolerance, link reliability, and critical-node protection are not explicitly optimized. Second, the candidate DS generation, skyline filtering, and online scheduling procedures may become computationally expensive in large-scale and dense WSN deployments.

From a practical deployment perspective, the framework can be integrated into IoT monitoring systems as an energy-aware backbone scheduling layer. The selected DS nodes serve as active sensing and aggregation nodes, while non-selected nodes remain in low-power mode. This reduces redundant communication and extends the operational lifetime of battery-powered IoT deployments. Since the framework is modular, the sink, edge controller, or SDN controller can execute the candidate-generation, skyline-selection, and scheduling modules, while sensor nodes only follow the resulting activation decisions.

In future work, the framework can be extended by developing a hybrid optimization algorithm that jointly considers energy efficiency, load balancing, DS size, and fault tolerance during DS formation and scheduling. Critical-node awareness can also be incorporated by assigning each node a criticality score based on coverage dependency, DS invalidation impact, residual energy, communication burden, and topological importance. This score can then be used as an additional skyline objective, a penalty in the EAAS utility function, a cooldown adjustment, or a reward penalty in the scheduler. Such an extension would discourage repeated activation of structurally important nodes and help preserve network robustness. Another promising direction is to extend the current UCB-based scheduler toward reinforcement learning. Unlike the present MAB design, which offers a low-complexity solution suitable for resource-constrained WSNs, an RL-based scheduler could incorporate richer state information, including residual-energy distribution, node centrality, link quality, predicted traffic load, and historical activation patterns. This may improve long-term scheduling decisions, although it would introduce additional computational and training overhead. Scalability and dynamic-network adaptation remain important future directions. In large dense networks, the cost of candidate DS generation, skyline filtering, and online scheduling may increase substantially. This limitation can be addressed through edge/cloud-assisted computation, where the sink or an edge server performs computationally intensive tasks such as skyline generation, candidate pruning, and scheduling decisions, while sensor nodes only follow lightweight activation commands. Cluster-based decomposition can also partition the network into smaller regions, allowing EAAS–S4C-MAB to operate locally within each cluster. In addition, parallel skyline filtering, hierarchical PEGASIS chains, and multi-leader aggregation can further reduce preprocessing and communication overhead. The current framework assumes a static topology; node mobility, sink mobility, and stochastic link failures are not explicitly modeled. In highly dynamic networks, the candidate DS pool may need to be regenerated or updated online. The framework also assumes that residual energy and energy-consumption estimates are available with sufficient accuracy. In practice, measurement noise, delayed reporting, or hardware variation may affect scheduling decisions. Therefore, while the proposed framework is suitable for moderate-sized WSNs, large-scale and highly dynamic deployments require distributed, hierarchical, adaptive, or edge-assisted extensions.
