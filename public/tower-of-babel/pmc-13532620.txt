A National Study of Tetanus Vaccine Knowledge, Receipt and Acceptance Among Emergency Department Patients
Authors: Francesco Sergi, Elisa Vaezazizi, Shaokui Ge, Christopher Alvarez, Jesus Torres, Brian Chinnock, Michael Gottlieb, Vijaya Arun Kumar, Kristin L Rising, Efrat Rosenzweig Kean, Stephanie Eucker, Robert M Rodriguez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThe Tetanus, Diphtheria, Pertussis (Tdap, “tetanus”) vaccine is recommended for adults every 10 years. In a national sample of adult emergency department (ED) patients, we sought to determine (1) knowledge and up‐to‐date status of the tetanus vaccine; (2) reasons for nonreceipt of the vaccine; (3) vaccine acceptability if offered in the ED; and (4) reasons for nonacceptance in the ED if offered.MethodsThis was a cross‐sectional survey study among non‐critically ill adults at 10 US EDs, asking questions about knowledge, receipt, and acceptability of the tetanus vaccine. Vaccine receipt was determined by self‐report. Aggregate responses are reported as descriptive statistics. Logistic regression modeling was performed, and data are presented as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsOf 4326 patients approached, 3285 (75.9%) agreed to participate, and 5 were excluded because of incomplete responses. Among 3280 participants, 13.4% had not heard of the vaccine; and 58.0% were not up‐to‐date. Factors associated with not being up‐to‐date included age 34–48 (aOR 0.65; 95% CI 0.53–0.81), African American race (aOR 1.46; 95% CI 1.19–1.79), no primary care doctor (aOR 1.29; 95% CI 1.04–1.61) and no insurance (aOR 1.86; 95% CI 1.36–2.56). Among participants not up‐to‐date, 21.6% would accept it during their ED visit. Primary reasons for nonacceptance were needing more information, concerns about side effects, and preference to get it from their doctor.ConclusionsOver half of adult ED patients are not up‐to‐date on the tetanus vaccine but only one‐fifth would accept it if offered during an ED visit. ED‐centered efforts to increase uptake should provide messaging to address vaccine hesitancy.Keywords: diphtheria, pertussis, tetanus, vaccine acceptance, vaccine hesitancy

Introduction

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recommend the Tetanus, Diphtheria, and Pertussis (Tdap) vaccine for adults every 10 years, with an earlier dose administration threshold of 5 years for people who have contaminated wounds or burns [1]. Tdap (as part of wound care) is the most commonly administered vaccine in emergency departments (EDs).

While the Tdap vaccine is often called the “tetanus vaccine,” increasing rates of pertussis and the resurgence of diphtheria highlight the importance of its other components. The United States (US) saw a sixfold increase in pertussis cases in 2024 as compared to 2023 [2], and early 2025 reports suggest that pertussis cases may continue to climb [3]. Additionally, the global resurgence of diphtheria along with increasing rates of travel and migration, signal the potential for spread of diphtheria to the US [4]. Waning immunity, gaps in vaccine coverage, and declining acceptance of public health vaccination efforts put populations in the US at risk for these disease entities [5].

Underserved populations lacking primary care have particularly low vaccination rates for a number of vaccines [6, 7]. Given the fact that EDs have developed successful interventions to provide COVID‐19 and influenza vaccinations, they could similarly develop programs for Tdap administration (beyond current delivery during wound care visits) [8, 9, 10, 11]. This national study aimed to determine adult ED patients' (1) knowledge and up‐to‐date status of the Tdap vaccine; (2) reasons for not being up‐to‐date; (3) acceptability of the tetanus vaccine if offered in the ED; and (4) reasons for not accepting the vaccine if offered. This information could inform the development of ED‐based strategies to increase Tdap vaccine coverage among medically underserved populations.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This was a preplanned analysis of data from a national vaccination surveillance study [6]. We used best practices in survey instrument development methods, including review of survey templates with a patient community advisory board (PCAB) and pilot testing for comprehension and consistency. We then conducted a cross‐sectional survey study from April 18–December 31, 2024, at 10 EDs in 8 US cities (Chicago, Illinois; Detroit, Michigan; Durham, North Carolina; Philadelphia, Pennsylvania; and Fresno, Los Angeles, San Francisco, and Sylmar, California). These EDs reported an estimated total of 637,392 visits in 2024. This study was approved by a central institutional review board (IRB) with reliance at 5 sites and by individual IRBs at the others.

Selection of Participants

A priori sample size calculation determined that 2401 participants (across all sites) were needed to ensure 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for primary outcomes within a 2% margin of error. To balance enrollment across the participating EDs, sites stopped enrollment at 400–450 enrollees. We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [12].

During weekday daytime blocks, research staff at each site reviewed ED dashboards to identify eligible adult (age ≥ 18 years) patients. We excluded prisoners and patients who were on psychiatric holds, in respiratory isolation, or were unable to participate in a survey study because of critical illness, altered mental status, or intoxication. We offered participation to eligible patients via a scripted consent form and administered surveys orally in English, Spanish, and (at two sites) Mandarin. Survey question responses were recorded in REDCap. Participants were not compensated for participation.

Measurements

Based on feedback from our PCAB, we used the term “tetanus vaccine” instead of Tdap throughout our surveys. Participants were asked: (1) Have you heard about the tetanus vaccine? (2) Have you ever received it? (3) (if yes), when did you last receive it? (if no) why (what are the reasons) you have not received it? (4) (if no or unsure about receipt), would you accept the tetanus vaccine today in the ED? and (5) (if would not accept), why would you not accept the tetanus vaccine? Questions about why participants had not received the tetanus vaccine and why they would not accept it were open‐ended. Research staff then categorized these responses using a predefined list of options, with an “other reason” category for answers that did not fit existing classifications. Tetanus vaccine receipt was determined by self‐report.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were up‐to‐date status and willingness to accept the tetanus vaccine in the ED. Secondary outcomes included knowledge about the tetanus vaccine, reasons for not being up‐to‐date, and reasons for declining the tetanus vaccine if offered in the ED. Participants unsure of vaccination within the past 10 years were considered not up‐to‐date, consistent with standard clinical practice in EDs.

Analysis

We summarized participant characteristics as counts and frequency percentages, and key survey responses were summarized as percentages with 95% CIs, excluding nonresponses in proportion denominators. Multiple logistic regression models were fitted with incomplete vaccination knowledge, incomplete up‐to‐date status, and willingness to accept the vaccine as dependent variables; adjustment factors included age interquartile ranges (IQR), sex, city, and characteristics associated with low vaccination coverage (African American race, Hispanic ethnicity, non‐English primary language, lack of primary care, and lack of insurance). Model results are presented in forest plots as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% CIs and two‐tailed p‐values (p‐values < 0.05 considered significant). Analyses were conducted in RStudio (version 2023.12.1).

Their median age was 49 years (IQR 33–65); 1792 (54.6%) were female; 967 (29.6%) were African American, 1030 (31.5%) were Hispanic or Latino/a, and 882 (27.0%) were White non‐Latino/a; 588 (17.9%) spoke Spanish primarily; 689 (21.0%) lacked primary care; and 249 (7.9%) lacked insurance. Knowledge About the Tetanus Vaccine

Only 440 participants (13.4%, 95% CI 12.0%–14.3%) had not heard of the tetanus vaccine (Table 2). Factors associated with not having heard of the tetanus vaccine in adjusted models included age 34–48 (aOR 0.46; 95% CI 0.33–0.64), age 49–64 (aOR 0.72; 95% CI 0.52–0.98), male sex (aOR 1.49; 95% CI 1.19–1.88), African American race (aOR 2.77; 95% CI 1.99–3.89), Hispanic race (aOR 1.67; 95% CI 1.14–2.46), other race (aOR 2.31; 95% CI 1.47–3.62), did not speak English (aOR 2.08; 95% CI 1.44–2.99), no primary care doctor (aOR 1.35; 95% CI 1.01–1.79), and participation in the cities of Sylmar (aOR 0.22; 95% CI 0.12–0.38) and Durham (aOR 0.48; 95% CI 0.27–0.82).

Participant Up‐to‐date Status With the Tetanus Vaccine

Most participants 1902 (58.0%, 95% CI, 56.3%–59.6%) were not up‐to‐date with tetanus vaccination. Factors associated with not being up to date included age 34–48 (aOR 0.65; 95% CI 0.53–0.81), African American race (aOR 1.46; 95% CI 1.19–1.79), other race (aOR 1.68; 95% CI 1.22‐2.32), no primary care doctor (aOR 1.29; 95% CI 1.04–1.61), no insurance (aOR 1.86; 95% CI 1.36–2.56), and participation in the cities of Sylmar (aOR 0.73; 95% CI = 0.53–0.99), Chicago (aOR 2.37; 95% CI = 1.79–3.17), and Durham (aOR 0.64; 95% CI = 0.47–0.87) (Figure 1).

Adjusted odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals for characteristics associated with not being up to date with tetanus vaccination among 3280 adult patients in ten emergency departments in eight U.S. cities, April 18–December 31, 2024.

The most common reasons for not being up‐to‐date were “I have not had a wound recently” (29.0%), “I haven't gotten around to it” (19.6%), “I didn't know about it/didn't know I should get it” (18.2%), and “I am not worried about contracting tetanus” (17.1%).

Participant Willingness to Accept the Tetanus Vaccine If Offered in the ED

Among participants who were not up‐to‐date, 410 (21.6%; 95% CI 19.6%–23.5%) would accept the tetanus vaccine if offered in the ED. Factors associated with willingness to accept the vaccination were age 34–48 (aOR 0.38; 95% CI 0.26–0.55), age 49–64 (aOR 0.57; 95% CI 0.39–0.82), African American race (aOR 1.68; 95% CI 1.14–2.49), other race (aOR 1.74; 95% CI 1.03–2.91), and participation in the cities of Sylmar (aOR 2.98; 95% CI 1.83–4.89), Philadelphia (aOR 0.57; 95% CI 0.34–0.93), Chicago (aOR 0.35; 95% CI 0.21–0.57), and Detroit (aOR 0.51; 95% CI 0.31–0.86) (Figure 2).

Adjusted odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals for characteristics associated with willingness to receive tetanus vaccination if offered during the emergency department visit among 1902 adult patients who were not up‐to‐date in ten emergency departments in eight U.S. cities, April 18–December 31, 2024.

The most common reasons for unwillingness to accept the tetanus vaccine in the ED were needing more information (32.4%), concerns about side effects (11.8%), preference to get the vaccine from their doctor or pharmacy (11.8%), feeling too sick in the ED (8.8%), and concerns about the expense (5.9%).

Discussion

In this national ED‐based study, most adults were familiar with the tetanus vaccine, but more than half were not up‐to‐date. We identified notable disparities in up‐to‐date vaccination status. While adults aged 34–48 were more likely to be current on the tetanus vaccine, African‐Americans, and those without insurance and primary care were less likely to be current on the tetanus vaccine. Among participants who were not up‐to‐date, only one fifth said they would accept the tetanus vaccine if offered in the ED.

We found lower up‐to‐date rates (42%) than the rates reported in the National Health Interview Survey (NHIS), which showed that 59.2% of U.S. adults were up‐to‐date [13]. Considering that the NHIS survey is a household survey, our lower rates may reflect the ED setting's more medically underserved population, which includes people who are experiencing homelessness and who lack primary care. Nevertheless, we found similar disparities as the NHIS did among Black and Hispanic/Latino/adults, confirming long‐standing racial discrepancies in vaccine receipt [14].

Given that increasing Tdap vaccination rates among adults can reduce the spread of tetanus, diphtheria, and pertussis, efforts to prevent and mitigate outbreaks should leverage the ED safety‐net setting [15]. With over 155 million visits annually, EDs could have a particularly broad impact on uptake rates, especially in medically vulnerable populations [16]. Given that Tdap vaccines are stocked in all EDs, vaccine availability would not be a barrier, as it has been for other vaccine delivery programs [17].

Our finding of high tetanus vaccine hesitancy suggests the need for the development of vaccine messaging platforms (information about benefits and side effects to address vaccine hesitancy) before broad implementation of non‐wound care related administration of the tetanus vaccine in EDs. As with other platforms, these should be tested prospectively in randomized trials [9, 11]. Widespread implementation must also consider impacts on ED resources and workflow. EDs should use vaccine information in electronic medical records, when available, to focus on patients who are not up‐to‐date.

Limitations

This was a public health surveillance study of a general ED population. Most participants were not in the EDs for wound care, and our findings may not reflect attitudes regarding the acceptance of the tetanus vaccine during wound care presentations. Although we could not objectively verify the primary outcome of tetanus vaccine receipt over the last 10 years, self‐report of tetanus vaccine receipt is standard ED practice (i.e., when ED patients are unsure about the tetanus vaccine status, they are generally assumed to have not received it). Free form responses regarding reasons for missing and not accepting the tetanus vaccine were subject to misclassification. All sites in this study were urban, academically affiliated EDs; our findings may not generalize to patient populations seen in dissimilar EDs, especially rural sites.

Conclusions

We identified substantial gaps and disparities in up‐to‐date tetanus vaccine status and willingness to accept the tetanus vaccine in the ED. Although over half of adult ED patients are not up‐to‐date, only a fifth would accept it if offered during an ED visit. In order to improve tetanus vaccine uptake, ED delivery programs must address vaccine hesitancy.
