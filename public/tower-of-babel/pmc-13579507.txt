Impact of Water Composition on Coffee Sensory Properties: A Comprehensive Analysis
Authors: Christina J Birke Rune, Ida Steen, Eva R Kjærgaard, Zeina Maroun, Wender L P Bredie, Henrik G Kjaergaard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWater quality and the composition of minerals in the water impact coffee extraction and thus influence the sensory experience of coffee. This study investigated the effect of water hardness and alkalinity on the sensory properties of brewed coffee (Colombian Kachalu) using duo–trio screening and descriptive sensory analyses. Coffees brewed with ultrapure water (UPW), used as the reference, were compared with brews prepared from UPW containing additions of calcium (Ca2 +), magnesium (Mg2 +), and bicarbonate (HCO3 −), spanning from the lower levels found within the “ideal brewing zone,” and up to higher experimental extremes designed to test the limits of their sensory impact. Across all sensory evaluations, water hardness alone produced no marked flavor changes, even when calcium was added at concentrations above those typically found in drinking water. In contrast, bicarbonate had a clear and pronounced effect on the sensory profile across the full tested range. Increasing bicarbonate levels, from brew‐zone concentrations and up to the experimental extreme of 300 mg/L, substantially reduced perceived acidity, fruitiness, and complexity, while increasing roasted and bitter notes. Small differences observed in samples containing both calcium and bicarbonate were attributable to reductions in effective bicarbonate concentration due to calcium carbonate (CaCO3) precipitation rather than to any direct flavor contribution from calcium. Overall, the findings indicate that, under the experimental conditions investigated, alkalinity, not hardness, was the primary determinant of sensory changes in brewed coffee. Maintaining appropriate bicarbonate levels is therefore essential for preserving desirable flavor attributes such as acidity and complexity, whereas variations in calcium within realistic ranges showed little sensory relevance.Practical ApplicationsThis study shows that bicarbonate levels in water, rather than water hardness, were the main factor shaping the sensory profile of the Colombian Kachalu coffee used in this study. Even small increases in bicarbonate made the coffee less acidic and fruity and more flat or bitter. These findings suggest that controlling alkalinity may be more important than adjusting hardness when designing water for coffee brewing. Consumers, cafés, and equipment manufacturers can use these findings to optimize water composition and improve flavor consistency across different brewing environments.Keywords: bicarbonate buffering capacity, calcium, coffee flavor, sensory analysis, water hardness

Introduction

To brew a cup of coffee, only two ingredients are needed: roasted coffee beans and water. Yet the sensory profile of brewed coffee is shaped by a complex interplay of variables, including coffee variety, terroir, post‐harvest processing, degree of roasting, brew temperature, brewing method, and serving temperature (Münchow et al. 2020; SCA 2018; Steen et al. 2017; Sunarharum et al. 2014). Despite the insignificant taste of water itself, water composition is increasingly recognized as an important factor for coffee flavor as it influences both extraction chemistry and final sensory perception (Carr 2019; Córdoba et al. 2021; Fibrianto et al. 2018; Giacomini et al. 2020; Kumstova 2015; SCA 2018; Seninde et al. 2020; Wellinger et al. 2017; Won Kang et al. 2022). However, water composition has not been studied as systematically as other factors influencing coffee quality.

The coffee industry and scientific literature commonly emphasize water hardness, defined by calcium (Ca2 +) and magnesium (Mg2 +) concentrations, as the dominant factor influencing coffee flavor, while alkalinity, the buffering capacity provided by carbonate (CO3 2 −) and bicarbonate (HCO3 −), is often considered to play only a minor or technical role, that is, important mainly for equipment maintenance, such as preventing corrosion or limescale buildup (SCA 2018; Wellinger et al. 2017). It is suggested that hardness promotes flavor extraction, whereas alkalinity merely buffers coffee acids, affecting perceived acidity without fundamentally altering coffee flavor (Navarini and Rivetti 2010; Sunarharum et al. 2014). However, recent findings suggest that perceived acidity in coffee is more closely linked to pH than to the absolute concentration of organic acids, indicating a more substantial sensory role for alkalinity than previously assumed (Liu et al. 2025; Rune et al. 2023). A well‐circulated “ideal brewing zone” has been proposed in the literature (Colonna‐Dashwood and Hendon 2015; SCA 2018; Wellinger et al. 2017), presenting recommended hardness and alkalinity ranges assumed to support optimal technical and sensory performance (Figure 1), though recent consumer studies suggest preferences may vary more widely than those recommended (Koji 2024; Won Kang et al. 2022). The SCAA (Specialty Coffee Association of America) standard defines this ideal brewing zone as approximately 17–85 mg/L calcium hardness (expressed as CaCO3) and 40–75 mg/L bicarbonate alkalinity (SCA 2018). Water within these ranges is expected to optimize extraction, maintain desirable acidity, and avoid the sensory flattening caused by excessive buffering. These values have become widely adopted in both industry practice and water‐formulation products, despite limited sensory validation. At the same time, mineral composition of drinking water varies widely across regions, resulting in substantial differences in both hardness and alkalinity, as can be seen in Figure 1.

Water hardness and alkalinity are also critical for technical reasons. Low levels of alkalinity (< 40 ppm CaCO3) may lead to corrosion in coffee machines, while high levels of both hardness and alkalinity promote scaling and limescale buildup in boilers (SCA 2018). These maintenance‐driven concerns may explain why such narrow ranges are emphasized in brewing recommendations. However, it remains unclear whether these parameters hold the same importance from a sensory perspective. In fact, to our knowledge, no study has sensorially tested the ideal brewing zone using controlled, replicated discrimination or descriptive sensory methods. This raises the question: Is the sensory role of water composition, particularly hardness, overstated in popular guidelines?

While the broader importance of water in coffee brewing is acknowledged, relatively few experimental studies have examined its direct sensory impact in coffee‐specific matrices (Junge et al. 2022). Past studies by Lockhart et al. (1955) and Pangborn et al. (1971) showed that distilled water yielded overly sour brews, while water with high carbonate levels produced flat and bitter profiles. These early findings provided foundational support for water guidelines but lacked modern sensory methodological robustness and did not account for compound‐specific aroma changes reported in more recent chemical analyses (Blumberg et al. 2010; Matei et al. 2012; Navarini et al. 1999).

More recently, Hendon et al. (2014) used density functional theory (DFT) calculations to model interactions between water minerals and coffee compounds such as lactic, malic, citric, quinic, and chlorogenic acids, as well as caffeine and eugenol. Their results suggested magnesium binds more strongly to desirable flavor compounds than calcium, implying improved extraction potential. However, these insights remain theoretical, as no sensory data were collected to validate them. Recent experimental work (Bratthäll et al. 2024) tested this assumption directly and found that calcium and magnesium did not significantly alter the extraction of organic acids in coffee, suggesting these ions, which contribute to hardness, have limited chemical influence under normal brewing conditions.

Other recent work has explored sensory outcomes more directly. Navarini and Rivetti (2010) demonstrated that bicarbonate concentration significantly affects espresso crema quality. Fibrianto et al. (2018), using 11 different commercial waters, found minor differences in sweetness, chocolate aroma, and oily mouthfeel but not in pH‐driven attributes. Dadali (2021) analyzed volatile profiles of Turkish coffee brewed using hard or soft water and found significant differences in only four of 30 aroma compounds. Córdoba et al. (2021), in a comprehensive review, highlighted the persistent gap in high‐quality sensory data on brewing water. Similarly, Matei et al. (2012) emphasized the need for coupling volatile compound data with controlled sensory methods to validate flavor outcomes.

Despite this limited evidence base, water composition has become a focal point in specialty coffee, driving a market of commercial filters, mineral kits, and bottled “ideal water” products. The commercial development has likely advanced more rapidly than the available science.

We acknowledge that experienced cuppers and baristas may detect subtle flavor differences caused by water composition. However, we hypothesize that the current brewing recommendations, particularly the tight hardness and alkalinity windows defined by SCA and others, are too narrow to produce sensory differences detectable by a trained sensory panel. Furthermore, we hypothesize that alkalinity, rather than hardness, exerts a stronger influence on the sensory attributes of brewed coffee, specifically acidity, bitterness, and complexity.

To investigate these hypotheses, the sensory evaluation was designed as a sequential investigation. An initial a descriminative analysis (duo‐trio) evaluated literature‐based mineral concentrations to determine whether these produced detectable sensory differences and to guide the selection of mineral concentrations for the subsequent descriptive analysis (DA1). Based on these findings, a second descriptive analysis (DA2) evaluated a broader range of water compositions.

Therefore, this study aims to test the sensory relevance of water hardness and alkalinity in brewed coffee, using both discriminative and descriptive sensory analyses. We specifically evaluate whether water compositions within and beyond the “ideal brewing zone” produce detectable sensory differences and whether calcium and magnesium (hardness) or bicarbonate (alkalinity) are the primary drivers of those effects.

Materials and Methods

Mineral‐Enriched Water Solutions

Water samples with defined mineral compositions were prepared by first making concentrated stock solutions of each mineral salt. All chemicals were of food grade. Calcium sulphate dihydrate (CaSO4·2H2O), calcium chloride dihydrate (CaCl2·2H2O), sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3), sulfuric acid, and mixed pH indicators were obtained from Sigma–Aldrich. Magnesium sulphate heptahydrate (MgSO4·7H2O) and sodium hydroxide solutions were obtained from Fisher Scientific.

The mineral salts were precisely weighed using an analytical balance and dissolved in ultrapure water (UPW, Type 1), which had a resistivity of 18.2 MΩ·cm and was filtered at 0.2 µm using a Millipore Direct‐Q 5 UV system equipped with a 254 nm UV lamp. A specific volume of each stock solution was then mixed with UPW in a volumetric flask to achieve the desired final mineral concentrations in the brewing water.

Masses of both stock solution and UPW were measured on a laboratory scale. Stock solutions were prepared from UPW and assumed to have densities comparable to UPW. Final calcium and bicarbonate concentrations were calculated from the recorded additions and nominal stock solution concentrations. All final solutions were within ± 3 mg/L of the intended calcium‐to‐bicarbonate (buffer) target ratio.

Coffee Samples and Serving

All coffee samples used in the sensory evaluations were brewed using Colombian Kachalu coffee beans (Peter Larsen Kaffe) from the same batch. The coffee was selected as a commercially available specialty coffee with a balanced sensory profile and was classified by the producer as a slow‐roasted medium roast. The same coffee was used throughout all sensory evaluations to ensure that water composition, rather than differences among coffees, constituted the experimental variable.

All brewing followed a standardized French press protocol adapted from the Specialty Coffee Association's Golden Cup standard (SCAA 2015) to ensure consistency across tests. Coffee was ground immediately prior to brewing using a Wilfa WSCG‐2+ coffee grinder set to 12 (French press setting). Brewing was conducted using Bodum Chambord French press brewers.

For each brew, 50 g of ground coffee was combined with 900 ± 5 g of water. The water was first brought to a boil in a kettle and then allowed to cool for 1 min to reach 92°C before pouring. After adding the water to the grounds, the brew was steeped for 4 min total. At 3.5 min, the crust was broken using 10 strokes and surface foam removed. At exactly 4 min, the plunger was pressed down, and the coffee was immediately transferred to a thermos preheated to 70°C to prevent further extraction and preserve temperature prior to serving. Brewing was performed by the same two‐person brewing team throughout each sensory study. All brewing steps followed the above standardized, timer‐controlled protocol, and the brewers performed the procedure synchronously using identical timing and handling procedures for crust breaking, plunging, and transfer to the thermos.

Prior to the main sensory sessions, pilot tests were conducted to determine the maximum allowable holding time in thermoses and the optimal cooling period after pouring to achieve a consistent evaluation temperature. Based on these tests, holding time never exceeded 30 min.

Coffee brewing is inherently associated with some experimental variation, even when brewing parameters are carefully standardized. Although this variation cannot be completely eliminated, the use of a consistent brewing protocol for all samples, together with repeated preparation across the triplicate sensory evaluations, minimizes the influence of individual brewing variation on comparisons between water compositions.

Sensory Evaluations

All sensory evaluations were conducted in the same sensory laboratory designed according to (ISO 8589 2010), with controlled lighting, odor, temperature conditions (approx. 21°C), and relative humidity. The evaluations included one discriminative test (duo–trio) and two descriptive analyses (DA1 and DA 2).

The water used to brew the coffee samples across all sensory evaluations was prepared by systematically varying calcium (Ca2 +), magnesium (Mg2 +), and bicarbonate (HCO3 −). The sensory evaluations were conducted as a sequential investigation. The duo–trio test served as an initial screening across a broad range of mineral concentrations. The subsequent descriptive analyses evaluated representative brewing‐water compositions. The second descriptive analysis (DA2) expanded the range of mineral compositions evaluated based on the findings from the first descriptive analysis (DA1). Figure 1 shows the experimental water compositions relative to commonly cited “ideal brewing zones” for coffee, as proposed by (Colonna‐Dashwood and Hendon 2015), the SCAA (SCAA 2009), and Wellinger et al. (2017). Figure 1 includes water compositions used in the duo–trio tests (25 comparisons across calcium, magnesium, and bicarbonate and their mixtures) as well as those used in DA1 and DA2 (three and five samples, respectively). Together, this overview illustrates how the experimental samples span values both within and beyond recommended hardness and alkalinity ranges, enabling a systematic sensory evaluation of the relevance of these commonly applied water guidelines.

Duo–Trio Test

A balanced‐reference duo–trio test was selected because it provides a simple reference‐based discrimination task suitable for untrained assessors. This test was used as a screening method to assess whether panelists could detect sensory differences between coffees brewed with different water compositions. The procedure followed standard discrimination‐testing guidelines, and test sets were randomized across assessors to control for order effects.

The study was conducted across two sessions, held one week apart, with 31 and 25 untrained assessors participating. All participants were informed about the nature of the test and provided written consent prior to evaluation. Before testing, assessors received a short introduction to the duo–trio protocol, including identification of the reference sample and focusing on key perceptual differences, to ensure consistent test execution.

The reference sample consisted of coffee brewed with UPW, while the experimental samples were brewed with UPW containing varying concentrations of magnesium, calcium, or bicarbonate (see Table 3). A total of 15 duo–trio sets were conducted in the first session and 10 in the second. Samples were served in standard white cupping bowls (200 mL), with 20 mL per serving, coded with randomized three‐digit numbers. All coffees were brewed using the same protocol and served at an evaluation temperature of 55°C ± 5°C (Steen et al. 2017). To minimize sensory fatigue and cross‐sample carry‐over, assessors used UPW and plain crackers as palate cleansers between sets.

Descriptive Analysis

Panel Training and Sensory Vocabulary

Two descriptive sensory analyses (DA1 and DA2) were conducted to evaluate the sensory effects of water composition on brewed coffee. In each DA, a panel consisting of 10 trained assessors (ISO 3972 2011; ISO 3972 2011) were selected from the Future Consumer Lab at the University of Copenhagen. All assessors had several years of experience with descriptive sensory evaluation.

For the first DA, panel training comprised 2 days: Day 1 included 2.5 h of vocabulary development and consensus training, followed by Day 2 with 2 h of calibration and continued consensus training, followed by formal evaluation.

For the second DA, panel training was extended due to the broader sensory space of the samples. Training was conducted over 4 consecutive days: Day 1 (3 h) for vocabulary development and consensus training, Day 2 (2 h) for calibration and continued consensus training, and Days 3 and 4 (2 h per day) for formal evaluation.

The formal evaluations were conducted in triplicate using independently randomized serving orders for each panelist and each replicate to minimize potential context, order, and memory effects, consistent with established recommendations for descriptive analysis (Lawless and Heymann 2010; Lestringant et al. 2018). For DA1, the attributes were acidity, bitter, sweet, roasted, chocolate, fruity, body, and astringency. For DA2, the attributes were acidic, bitter, roasted, chocolate, fruity, astringency, aftertaste, and complexity. Attribute definitions and physical reference standards used during training are provided in Table 1. Perceived complexity was included as a collative attribute reflecting the multidimensionality of the coffee flavor profile.

Sample Design and Evaluation Procedure

The first descriptive analysis was performed on three coffee samples brewed with different water compositions to identify sensory attributes affected by the addition of calcium, magnesium, and bicarbonate to UPW (Table 2). The selected compositions represented UPW, a hardness‐only condition, and a hardness‐plus‐bicarbonate condition relevant to commonly recommended brewing‐water formulations. A calcium concentration of 132 mg/L was selected to match the molar concentration of 80 mg/L magnesium, allowing a direct comparison of the sensory effects of the two cations. The second descriptive analysis investigated the sensory impact of higher and combined concentrations of calcium and bicarbonate (magnesium was not used in this analysis). Five samples were evaluated (Table 2), selected to represent a wider range of mineral compositions spanning hardness and alkalinity extremes. The expanded concentration range was included to further investigate trends observed during the screening phase and to evaluate sensory responses across a broader hardness–alkalinity space beyond commonly recommended brewing‐water formulations.

In both analyses, samples were rated on 15‐cm unstructured line scales anchored from “a little” to “a lot.” A randomized presentation design was applied. In DA1, samples were evaluated in triplicate, whereas DA2 followed a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with four replicates.

For both descriptive analyses, coffee samples were served in porcelain cups preheated to 60°C. Each cup contained 30 mL of brewed coffee served at approximately 55°C ± 2°C (Steen et al. 2017). Samples were evaluated immediately after pouring to ensure consistent tasting conditions. Ultrapure water and plain crackers were provided as palate cleansers throughout all sessions.

Chemical Analysis

Bicarbonate (HCO3 −) concentrations were measured by titration of the water sample using sulfuric acid (H2SO4; Sigma–Aldrich, ACS reagent 95.0%–98.0%, PubChem ID: 329752826). The titration was performed using 40 g of water sample and three drops of mixed indicator (0.02% methyl red + 0.1% bromocresol green; Sigma–Aldrich, PubChem ID: 329753931) in a 100 mL beaker under constant mixing. The titration was performed using a 25 mL burette filled with 0.02 M H2SO4 (prepared from 95 wt% H2SO4 solution). The method was evaluated using demineralized water samples with known bicarbonate concentrations, which showed that the measured concentrations were underestimated on average by 13%.

pH Measurements

The pH of all water solutions and brewed coffee samples was measured in two separate sessions using calibrated laboratory equipment.

In the first session, measurements were conducted using a VWR pHenomenal pH Meter (pH 1100L). Calibration was verified using standard buffer solutions (pH 4.00, 7.00, and 10.00) before each sample set, ensuring accuracy across brews with differing compositions and expected pH ranges. Each sample, whether water or coffee, was measured in triplicate. The electrode was rinsed with demineralized water between readings to minimize contamination and carryover.

In the second session, additional pH measurements were performed on the three coffee samples shown in Table 6 using a Mettler Toledo Seven Compact pH meter equipped with a Mettler Toledo InLab Ultra‐Micro‐ISM electrode (Mettler‐Toledo, Glostrup, Denmark). This setup was selected for its high precision and suitability for small‐volume, low‐buffer capacity samples such as brewed coffee. Each sample was also measured in triplicate during this session.

Data Collection and Analysis

Discriminative Duo–Trio Analysis

Data were collected and analyzed in R (R Core Team 2024). To determine at which level (Test Levels 1–5) the panelists significantly (p < 0.05) could discriminate between samples in the duo–trio test, the analysis was performed using the SensR package (Christensen et al. 2018).

The Two Descriptive Analyses

Data were collected using the FIZZ software (Biosystemes) in both DA evaluations and afterward analyzed in R (R Core Team, 2024). To investigate the difference between the different coffee samples, ANOVA was used to test for significant differences (p < 0.05). When the coffee samples were found significantly different, a pairwise comparison (Tukey test) was performed.

Results and Discussion

Duo–Trio Test

The duo–trio screening test followed a balanced‐reference design, comparing coffee brewed with UPW to coffees brewed with UPW containing varying levels of magnesium, calcium, bicarbonate ions, or combinations thereof (Table 3). Across all magnesium‐containing samples, no significant differences were detected relative to the UPW reference. For calcium, a significant difference was only observed at the lowest tested concentration (15 mg/L; p = 0.022), while the highest concentration (240 mg/L) approached significance (p = 0.054). No significant differences were detected at the intermediate concentrations (30–120 mg/L) or in samples containing both calcium and bicarbonate. Importantly, the calcium series did not exhibit a consistent concentration‐dependent response. Therefore, the isolated significant result at 15 mg/L should be interpreted cautiously, as the overall calcium series did not provide convincing evidence for a systematic sensory effect of calcium under the conditions investigated.

In contrast, bicarbonate‐containing samples showed clear and consistent effects. Coffees brewed with 120 mg/L bicarbonate (p = 0.049) and 240 mg/L bicarbonate (p < 0.001) were perceived as significantly different from the reference. A significant difference also appeared when 80 mg/L magnesium was combined with 60 mg/L bicarbonate (p = 0.015). Because none of the corresponding hardness‐only samples (calcium or magnesium) were significant, these effects are most plausibly attributed to bicarbonate rather than magnesium.

These results indicated that no significant sensory differences were found between the reference and coffees brewed with mineral concentrations within, or even well beyond, the recommended brewing ranges (SCA 2018; Wellinger et al. 2017), except for samples containing elevated bicarbonate. This finding aligns with earlier work by Navarini and Rivetti (2010), who reported that bicarbonate influences sensory properties in espresso, and challenges existing industry guidelines that emphasize hardness levels without direct sensory validation.

Panelists were also asked to describe the perceived difference from the reference. Across all tests, acidity and bitterness were the attributes most frequently cited. However, only bicarbonate‐containing samples showed a consistent directional response, with panelists describing them as less acidic. This aligns with bicarbonate's known buffering effects, which neutralize organic acids and reduce perceived acidity (Keast and Breslin 2003; Matei et al. 2012).

In summary, the duo–trio screening results demonstrate that bicarbonate and not calcium nor magnesium is the key mineral parameter driving detectable sensory differences in brewed coffee prepared from the Colombian Kachalu beans. These findings support previous reports linking elevated bicarbonate levels to muted acidity and reduced flavor brightness (He et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2025) and are consistent with a recent consumer study showing minimal sensory changes across a wide hardness range unless alkalinity is elevated (Koji 2024). While theoretical models have suggested that magnesium may enhance extraction of certain compounds (Hendon et al. 2014), no such perceptual effects were observed here.

Because duo–trio testing only establishes whether a difference is detectable, and not which sensory attributes are responsible, these findings motivated a more detailed evaluation using descriptive sensory analysis. Attribute‐specific effects of hardness and alkalinity are explored in Sections 3.3.1 and 3.3.2.

Sensory Analysis of Low Levels of Minerals and Bicarbonate

A descriptive analysis was performed on a limited set of coffee brews from three representative water compositions (Table 4): (1) UPW; (2) UPW containing 132 mg/L calcium and 80 mg/L magnesium; and (3) UPW containing 132 mg/L calcium, 80 mg/L magnesium, and 60 mg/L bicarbonate (Table 2). No significant differences were found among the three coffee samples, indicating that the tested water compositions did not influence the perceived coffee flavor. These findings are consistent with the duo–trio test, which also showed no significant differences in coffees brewed with water containing the mineral levels found to be ideal for coffee flavor as reported in the literature (SCA 2018; Colonna‐Dashwood and Hendon 2015; Wellinger et al. 2017).

Coffees brewed with hardness minerals alone exhibited sensory profiles broadly similar to those brewed with UPW. Although the bicarbonate‐containing sample showed numerical differences for some sensory attributes, none of these differences were statistically significant at p ≤ 0.05 (Table 2). Panel descriptions suggested slightly higher body, more pronounced roasted and chocolate‐like notes, and somewhat reduced perceived acidity and fruitiness. Taken together, both the duo–trio test and the descriptive analysis indicate that hardness minerals (calcium and magnesium) had limited sensory impact at the concentration levels typically cited in the literature as ideal (Matei et al. 2012).

Although the observed differences were not statistically significant, the tendency toward reduced perceived acidity in the bicarbonate‐containing sample is consistent with the known chemistry of acid–base interactions during coffee brewing. The perceived acidity of coffee is correlated with the titratable acidity of coffee extracts since the reaction of the acids with the receptors of the mouth is basically a titration up to the pH of 6.6, which is the pH of the mouth. However, perceived acidity in coffee is a complex sensory attribute that is influenced not only by pH and titratable acidity but also by the concentration and composition of individual acids and interactions within the coffee matrix (Rune et al. 2023; Yeager et al. 2021). Alkaline water (pH > 7) will neutralize some of the acidic extracts in coffee and thus reduce the perceived acidity of coffee (He et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2025; Wellinger et al. 2017).

Finding a good explanation for the sensory properties of adding cations to coffee water is more difficult. According to the theoretical study by Hendon et al. (2014), the cations (calcium and magnesium) coordinate to nucleophilic organic molecules in coffee. However, this theoretical mechanism does not appear to translate into perceptible sensory effects in our study, as was also found in recent findings (Koji 2024).

Therefore, we hypothesize that hardness has little to no effect and that bicarbonate plays a dominant role, contrary to common perception within the coffee community. The new extreme test levels were determined based on the results from the discriminative test and on the average bicarbonate and calcium levels in tap water in the Copenhagen area (Figure 1). These levels were then evaluated in DA2 using a trained sensory panel.

Sensory Analysis of Water Compositions Spanning Hardness and Alkalinity Extremes

The second descriptive analysis evaluated the influence of higher calcium and bicarbonate concentrations, both individually and in combination (Table 3). Ultrapure water was used as the reference, and four experimental waters were tested: sample A (264 mg/L calcium), sample B (300 mg/L bicarbonate), sample C (264 mg/L calcium + 300 mg/L bicarbonate), and sample D (132 mg/L calcium + 150 mg/L bicarbonate). Ten trained panelists assessed the resulting coffees. Significant differences were found for all sensory attributes except astringency, with multiple attributes showing highly significant differences across samples. Coffee brewed with calcium (sample A) was not significantly different from the reference in any of the attributes, thereby confirming the results from the two previous tests that calcium alone has no significant effect on the perceived coffee taste. This reinforces the limited sensory impact of calcium observed in earlier consumer and expert evaluations (Koji 2024). Coffee brewed with bicarbonate and no calcium (sample B), however, was significantly different from the reference in acidic, bitter, roasted, and fruity and not in chocolate and aftertaste intensity. Bicarbonate at this concentration (300 mg/L) significantly increases the perception of bitter and roasted and decreases the perception of acidic, fruit, and complexity to almost no perception, making the coffee appear more bitter, dull, and flat. Therefore, this research agrees with the findings of Wellinger et al. (2017) that bicarbonate makes the coffee appear flat, weak, and dull. However, our findings diverge from theirs regarding water hardness, as no significant sensory differences in coffee brewed with calcium‐only water were observed. Our results are also consistent with those of Liu et al. (2025), who found that high bicarbonate levels suppressed fruit and floral attributes in light roasts and contributed to sensory dullness.

The PCA score plot (Figure 2) provides a multivariate visualization of DA2 presented in Table 5. The first two principal components explained 95.8% of the total variance (PC1 = 61.9%; PC2 = 33.9%), demonstrating that the PCA captured nearly all of the variation in the sensory profiles. Together, the statistical comparisons and PCA summarize the relationships among the coffees brewed with the different water compositions. A consistent pattern emerges across attributes: Bicarbonate concentration was associated with the largest sensory change, while calcium alone produces comparatively minor effects. This is especially evident when comparing the reference water with sample A (containing only calcium) and sample B (containing only bicarbonate). As shown in Table 5, no significant differences were observed between the reference and sample A, whereas sample B differed significantly from the reference in acidic, bitter, roasted, fruity, and complexity (all p < 0.001). In the PCA score plot, sample B is clearly separated from the reference, while sample A shows less pronounced separation. This observation aligns with the chemical mechanism in which bicarbonate neutralizes organic acids, thereby reducing perceived acidity and altering the balance of bright, fruit‐forward notes in the cup (He et al. 2024; Matei et al. 2012).

Calcium becomes relevant primarily when it is mixed with bicarbonate, as in samples C and D (Figure 2). The plots suggest a subtle increase in chocolate and aftertaste intensity under combinations of calcium and moderate bicarbonate concentrations. Similar effects have been reported previously: Calcium ions can promote the extraction of certain hydrophobic or high‐molecular‐weight compounds, contributing to fuller body, cocoa‐like notes, and lingering aftertaste (Navarini et al. 1999). Although these changes are smaller than the bicarbonate‐driven effects, they agree with findings from sensory evaluations of mineralized waters showing that certain hardness levels can subtly enhance body or chocolate‐like flavors (Fibrianto et al. 2018).

Overall, the PCA visually summarize the observed sensory trends as presented in Table 5, demonstrating that bicarbonate concentration exerted the strongest influence on acidity‐related attributes, while calcium contributed secondary, more nuanced effects that emerged primarily in combination with alkalinity. These observations indicate that under the experimental conditions investigated, bicarbonate had a greater influence than calcium hardness on the sensory profile of brewed Colombian Kachalu coffee.

The Effect of Hardness and Alkalinity Parameter Individually

To illustrate the effect of calcium and bicarbonate relative to the reference water, Figure 3A presents the sensory profiles of three samples from DA2 (reference, sample A, and sample B) in the form of a spider plot. From Figure 3A, it is seen that sample A (264 mg/L calcium) shows a similar profile to the reference, however slightly less intense in all attributes. In the coffee industry, the popular belief is that water hardness also changes the coffee flavor. In DA2, no statistically significant differences between coffee brewed on the reference water and the coffee brewed with the calcium water (sample A) were observed, suggesting that calcium alone may have little sensory impact under the tested conditions. This observation is also reflected in the PCA score plot (Figure 2), where the reference and sample A cluster closely together, and is supported by the descriptive analysis (Table 5), which showed no significant differences between these samples for any sensory attribute.

Further, this should be viewed in the context that the amount of calcium in sample A is unrealistically high, compared to Copenhagen tap water (see Figure 1). Copenhagen tap water, which is very hard, typically contains 110 mg/L calcium (and 20 mg/L magnesium), which lies between that of UPW and sample A. This confirms that hardness alone does not change the taste of coffee, compared to UPW in the range relevant for coffee water, a conclusion that is further supported by recent findings from Koji (2024), who also observed minimal sensory changes in calcium‐modified waters without elevated alkalinity. The absence of a detectable sensory difference between the coffee brewed with waters varying in calcium (and magnesium) concentrations aligns with recent compositional evidence showing that divalent cations do not alter the extraction of key organic acids in coffee at realistic levels. Bratthäll et al. (2024) demonstrated, using GC–MS and NMR, that both calcium and magnesium up to 100 mg/L had negligible influence on the extraction of citric, malic, lactic, and quinic acids. Together with the low sensory relevance of most of these acids in brewed coffee (Rune et al. 2023), these findings suggest that water hardness does not directly affect either the chemical or perceptual dimensions of coffee acidity.

As opposed to the calcium level, the bicarbonate level in this test (sample B, 300 mg/L bicarbonate) is comparable to tap water in Denmark, which typically varies between 200 and 400 mg/L. We observe a clear sensory separation between sample B and both the reference and sample A in the spider plot, indicating a pronounced influence of bicarbonate on the flavor profile of the Colombian Kachalu coffee. This strong effect of bicarbonate is also evident in the PCA score plot (Figure 2), where sample B is clearly separated from the reference and sample A. Together with the significant differences observed in Table 5 and the attribute profiles shown in Figure 3A, these findings confirm the major role of bicarbonate in shaping the sensory profile.

The Effect of the Mixture of Hardness and Alkalinity

To illustrate the combined effect of calcium and bicarbonate, Figure 3B presents the sensory profiles of the reference sample (UPW) and the two mixed‐mineral samples from DA2: Sample C (264 mg/L calcium + 300 mg/L bicarbonate) and sample D (132 mg/L calcium + 150 mg/L bicarbonate). Sample D was brewed with half the concentrations of both calcium and bicarbonate, compared to sample C. Based on the results in Section 3.3.1, sample C was expected to resemble Sample B sensorially, as both had the same target bicarbonate concentration. However, while samples B and C showed similar intensities for roasted and bitter attributes, sample C exhibited intermediate intensities for acidity‐related attributes (Table 5).

This observation is supported by DA2 (Table 5), which shows that sample C exhibited intermediate intensities for the acidity‐related attributes, and is reflected in the PCA score plot (Figure 2) and the sensory profiles (Figure 3B). The higher acidity and fruitiness of sample C can therefore be explained by a lower effective bicarbonate concentration relative to sample B. This interpretation is consistent with earlier results (Section 3.3.1), which demonstrated that decreasing bicarbonate concentration systematically increased perceived acidity and fruitiness.

Measured bicarbonate concentrations confirmed this interpretation. Although samples B and C were formulated with the same target bicarbonate concentration, sample C contained substantially less bicarbonate than intended, consistent with partial calcium carbonate (CaCO3) precipitation in the presence of elevated calcium. This is further supported by pH measurements (Table 6): Sample B had an average water pH of 8.4, while sample C had a lower pH of 7.5. Sample A, containing only calcium, had a pH of 6.7. The coffees brewed with sample C also had a lower pH than those brewed with sample B (4.9 vs. 5.2, respectively). Together, these observations are consistent with reduced effective bicarbonate concentration in sample C, most plausibly resulting from CaCO3 precipitation prior to brewing.

The similarity in roasted and bitter intensity between samples B and C, together with the intermediate acidity and fruitiness of sample C, suggests that acidity‐related attributes were more sensitive to differences in effective bicarbonate concentration than roasted and bitter attributes. This pattern is also reflected in the PCA score plot (Figure 2), where sample C occupies an intermediate position relative to the other water compositions. From a sensory perspective, sample D would be expected to fall closer to either the reference or sample C rather than exactly midway between them, despite containing half the salt concentration. This expectation arises because taste perception generally follows a logarithmic rather than linear relationship with stimulus intensity (Varshney and Sun 2013). As shown in Figure 3B, this pattern is observed in the present data: For all significant attributes, sample D is positioned between the reference and sample C, but for most attributes, it lies closer to the reference (with the exception of chocolate flavor and aftertaste intensity). The higher similarity to the reference, however, is not statistically significant, as sample D is not significantly different from either the reference or sample C for any attribute other than acidic (Table 5). The reference and sample C are, however, significantly different from each other in bitter, fruity, and acidic.

Measured bicarbonate levels in sample D were also lower than the target concentration, indicating partial calcium carbonate (CaCO3) precipitation, although to a lesser extent than in sample C. The reduced bicarbonate content helps explain why sample D appears closer to the reference than anticipated. In practical terms, sample D ends up chemically and sensorially closer to UPW, reinforcing the dominant role of bicarbonate concentration in shaping the sensory profile of brewed coffee.

This drop in pH from water to brewed coffee is expected, as coffee introduces organic acids into the solution. However, waters with higher bicarbonate levels (such as samples B and C) buffered this acidity more effectively, resulting in a higher pH of the final brew. These findings are consistent with previous reports showing that the buffering action of bicarbonate significantly alters the final pH of brewed coffee (Matei et al. 2012).

Linking pH With Sensory Perception

The sensory profiles of the brewed coffees showed clear differences between water types, particularly in acidity, bitterness, roasted flavor, fruity character, and complexity. The most pronounced effects were observed in sample B, which had the highest bicarbonate concentration and pH. This sample was perceived as significantly less acidic and less fruity, aligning with its more neutral pH and supporting the hypothesis that bicarbonate buffers acids, reducing their sensory impact.

Bitterness and roasted notes were also more prominent in samples B and C. These shifts may reflect changes in the perception or extraction of bitter compounds under more alkaline conditions, though the exact mechanism remains to be clarified. Importantly, complexity was rated lowest in sample B, suggesting that elevated bicarbonate levels not only reduce acidity but also contribute to a flatter, less vibrant flavor profile.

Together, these results indicate that bicarbonate impacts coffee flavor primarily through its pH‐buffering capacity. While this study did not analyze specific changes in chemical composition, the findings highlight the importance of water alkalinity in shaping sensory outcomes and challenge the current assumptions that emphasize mineral hardness as the primary driver. This interpretation is consistent with recent findings indicating that perceived acidity in coffee is not directly determined by the absolute concentration of individual organic acids but is likely influenced by broader physicochemical factors related to proton activity and matrix interactions (Rune et al. 2023). The combination of recent chemical and sensory evidence supports this interpretation. Bratthäll et al. (2024) showed that calcium and magnesium have no measurable influence on organic‐acid extraction, while Rune et al. (2023) demonstrated that most organic acids in brewed coffee occur below their sensory detection thresholds. Taken together, these results explain why differences in water hardness did not produce measurable sensory changes in the present study and reinforce the central role of alkalinity and pH buffering in shaping the sensory expression of acidity.

Limitations and Future Research

This study is the first to systematically investigate the effects of water hardness (calcium and magnesium) and alkalinity (bicarbonate) on the sensory properties of brewed Colombian Kachalu coffee.

The study used only one type of coffee for the sensory analyses. The coffee was a commercially available Colombian specialty coffee classified by the producer as a slow‐roasted medium roast. However, quantitative roast characterization (e.g., roast color or Agtron value), roast profile, and roast duration were not available. As roast degree is known to influence coffee flavor and sensory characteristics (Münchow et al. 2020), the sensory findings cannot be generalized across different origins or roast levels. The results demonstrate that water composition, particularly bicarbonate concentration, strongly affects pH and thereby perceived acidity. However, based on Rune et al. (2023), it is acknowledged that roast degree can influence acidity perception, as it alters both the concentration and types of organic acids present in brewed coffee. We hypothesized that lighter‐roasted coffees, which contain higher levels of organic acids, may respond differently to variations in water chemistry due to altered acid–buffer interactions during brewing. Future research should include a broader range of roast levels and coffee origins to evaluate whether these interactions remain consistent across more diverse sensory profiles.

While this study used pH as a proxy for perceived acidity, targeted organic acid profiling, such as for chlorogenic acids (CGAs), quinic, citric, and malic acids, was not included. This decision was based on previous findings (Rune et al. 2023), which demonstrated that perceived acidity in brewed coffee is more strongly associated with pH than with the absolute concentration of specific organic acids. Given this, pH was considered a sufficient and relevant chemical parameter for exploring sensory differences related to water composition, particularly bicarbonate. Nonetheless, individual acids do contribute to flavor nuances, especially in light‐to‐medium roasts. Future work could include targeted acid profiling to complement pH data and clarify how specific acids interact with mineral content to influence both acidity and bitterness perception. Furthermore, total dissolved solids (TDS) and/or extraction yield were not measured. Although all coffees were prepared using a standardized and time‐controlled brewing protocol with identical brewing conditions across all samples, and were freshly prepared across the replicated sensory evaluations, the absence of such measurements means that potential differences in extraction between the different water compositions cannot be completely excluded. Inclusion of these parameters would have provided additional physicochemical characterization of the brews and should be considered in future studies.

While this study focused on sensory evaluation by trained assessors, future work could explore how differences in water composition are perceived by consumers under everyday brewing conditions.

Conclusion

This study systematically evaluated the sensory relevance of water hardness (calcium and magnesium) and alkalinity (bicarbonate) in brewed Colombian Kachalu coffee across a range of experimentally selected water compositions spanning and exceeding commonly recommended “ideal brewing zones.” Under the conditions tested, variations in calcium and magnesium hardness, even at concentrations well beyond those typically recommended for coffee brewing, did not result in reliably discriminable sensory differences when assessed by trained sensory panels using discriminative and descriptive methods.

In contrast, bicarbonate concentration exerted a clear and consistent influence on coffee sensory properties. Increasing bicarbonate levels substantially reduced perceived acidity, fruitiness, and overall complexity, while enhancing bitterness and roasted notes. These effects were robust across multiple sensory methods and were closely associated with bicarbonate‐driven pH buffering in the brewed coffee.

Together, our findings indicate that, under the experimental conditions investigated, the hardness‐related limits defined within current brewing guidelines may be more restrictive than warranted from a sensory perspective, whereas alkalinity played a dominant role in shaping coffee flavor. The strong sensory impact of bicarbonate suggests that its recommended range remains sensorially meaningful, while the emphasis placed on narrow hardness windows is not supported by the present sensory evidence.

Overall, this work demonstrates that, for the Colombian Kachalu coffee and water compositions investigated, optimizing bicarbonate concentration is more critical than fine‐tuning calcium or magnesium hardness for achieving desirable flavor attributes in brewed Colombian Kachalu coffee, particularly with respect to acidity and complexity. The present results suggest that the narrow hardness and alkalinity ranges promoted in current brewing guidelines may reflect machine‐maintenance considerations more than demonstrable sensory advantages.
