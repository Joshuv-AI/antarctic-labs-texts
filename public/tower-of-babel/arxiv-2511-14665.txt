THE SOLVER’S PARADOX IN FORMAL PROBLEM SPACES
Milan Rosko

Abstract
This paper investigates how global decision problems over arithmetically represented domains acquire reflective structure through class-quantification. Arithmetization forces diagonal fixed points whose verification requires reflection beyond finitary means, producing Feferman-style obstructions independent of computational technique. We use this mechanism to analyze uniform complexity statements—including 𝖯 \mathsf{P} vs. 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} —showing that their difficulty stems from structural impredicativity rather than methodological limitations. The focus is not on deriving separations but on clarifying the logical status of such arithmetized assertions.

1 Introduction

Exposition
.

Classical and constructive frameworks alike treat mathematical problems as objects formulated within a given formal system, analyzed by finitary methods internal to that system. This separation between
problem
and
analysis
is typically regarded as benign: a problem is specified syntactically, its semantics is understood relative to a background theory, and its resolution is sought by familiar deductive or computational means. Yet this division breaks down for a broad class of global decision problems—most notably, problems that quantify over a space of all problems or all computational procedures: the
Complexity Zoo
.

Figure 1:
Illustration: An evolving map of complexity classes, illustrating the expanding structure of the
Complexity Zoo
, a project of
Aaronson 2002
.

The central observation of this paper is that once a problem space is represented arithmetically, e.g., via
Gödel 1931
, quantification over that space necessarily ranges over the representational environment in which the quantification itself is encoded. Consequently, any global assertion of the form

\forall P\in\mathcal{C}\;\Phi(P)\quad\text{or equivalently}\quad\forall e\;\Phi(e),

(1.1)

where
e
is the code of a problem in
\mathcal{C}
, automatically acquires a self-referential instance. By the
Diagonal Lemma

[
Boolos et al. 2007
]
, there exists a problem
P^{\ast}
whose code
\ulcorner P^{\ast}\urcorner
satisfies

P^{\ast}\;\leftrightarrow\;\Phi(\ulcorner P^{\ast}\urcorner),

(1.2)

yielding a
bona fide
fixed point internal to the encoded problem space.

The consequences are immediate: global statements over a representable problem class cannot remain external to the syntactics in which it is formalized: it must contain, among its instances, a case that reflects the behavior of the system upon itself. Thus, the act of formalizing such problems transports
Impredicativity

[
Whitehead and Russell 1927
;
Girard et al. 1989
]
from the object-level formulation into the meta-level verification conditions. This transport mechanism produces reflective obstructions of the kind encountered in
Gödel’s Incompleteness Theorems
, analysis of local reflection
[
Feferman 1968
;
Feferman 1991
;
Kleene 1952
]
, and the modal semantics of provability logic
[
Löb 1955
]
.

The
Solver’s Paradox
(Lemma
2.9
) provides a minimal
“model”
of this phenomenon. A
“total classifier”
attempting to assign determinate statuses to all problems in its domain generates a self-referential instance whose correct classification requires the system to affirm its own soundness on that very problem. Any such attempt imports a
\Pi^{0}_{2}
reflection condition and produces an obstruction to verification: solving the problem requires settling a meta-problem that cannot be resolved within the same finitary framework.

This paper develops a unified account of these mechanisms and applies them to several global decision problems, with particular emphasis on the formalization of
\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{NP}
. The analysis shows that the arithmetized statement of the conjecture inherits reflective structure through the uniformity clauses implicit in its definition. The resulting fixed-point instances exhibit the same verification barriers found in local
Reflection Principles
, suggesting that the long-standing intractability of such problems may reflect structural
“ill-posedness”
rather than limitations of existing proof techniques. We articulate the following transport principle:

Whenever a
problem
quantifies over an arithmetically represented
problem space
, it necessarily inherits the capacity to refer about its representation, thereby inducing fixed points and reflective obstructions.

The Roadmap: This principle, once made explicit, allows for a systematic analysis of global problems whose epistemic status depends on their interaction with the expressive resources of the systems in which they are stated. The remainder of the paper develops this framework in detail. Section
2
introduces a minimal reflective model via the
Solver’s Paradox
. Section
3
formalizes the
“transport”
of
Impredicativity
through class-quantification and
Gödel Numbers
, establishing the central
fixed point theorem
. Section
4
applies this analysis to the arithmetized formulation of
\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{NP}
, exhibiting the resulting verification collapse. Section
5
examines the epistemic implications for global decision problems, culminating in a classification of fixed-point sinks as boundary objects for
“problems”
in Section
6
and
7
.

2 The Solver’s Paradox

2.1 Impredicativity Transport in SAT

Exposition
.

Increasing the expressive resources available to a representable problem class does not alleviate the impredicativity transported by universality; it amplifies it. The reason is structural: as soon as the evaluator for a class is arithmetically definable, the class already contains the capacity to internalize meta-level constraints. Extending the language—adding parameters, quantifiers, or higher-order encodings—enlarges the representational envelope within which diagonalization operates. The fixed-point mechanism therefore acquires additional modes of re-entry rather than being neutralized. We illustrate the point with a minimal satisfiability instance. SAT serves here not only as the canonical complexity-theoretic benchmark, see
Cook 1971
;
Karp 1972
;
Schaefer 1978
;
Allender et al. 2009
, but also as a recursively enumerable language whose definability already suffices for the internal deployment of diagonal methods. In this setting, even the simplest encodings exhibit how universality reintroduces the very impredicativity one might hope to avoid.

Theorem 2.1

(Universality Forced Impredicativity)
.

Let
\mathsf{SAT}
denote the class of propositional satisfiability instances, encoded by a primitive–recursive bijection with
\mathbb{N}
. Let
S
be a total computable function intended to decide
\mathsf{SAT}
, and
let

C_{S}(\varphi)\;\equiv\;\text{``$S$ correctly classifies the instance $\varphi$''}.

(2.3)

Assume:

(i)

Universality:
for every Turing machine
M
and every input
x
there exists a formula
\mathsf{SAT}(M,x)
such that

M(x)\text{ accepts}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\mathsf{SAT}(M,x)\in\mathsf{SAT}.

(2.4)

(ii)

Representability:
the predicate

\mathsf{Out}_{S}(w,b)
expressing

“
S
outputs
b
on input
w
”
is arithmetically definable.

(iii)

Correctness Encoding:
for every instance
\varphi
, the
statement
C_{S}(\varphi)
is uniformly reducible to a SAT formula

\varphi_{S,\varphi}
.

Then there exists a SAT instance
\psi_{S}
such that
S
misclassifies
\psi_{S}
.

Lemma 2.2

(Internal Diagonalization)
.

Under conditions (i)–(iii), there exists a SAT instance

\psi_{S}
satisfying

\psi_{S}\;\leftrightarrow\;\neg C_{S}(\psi_{S}).

(2.5)

Define the arithmetic predicate

\theta(x)\;\equiv\;\neg C_{S}(x),

where
x
ranges over
Gödel-Representable
SAT instances. By representability,
\theta(x)
is arithmetically definable. By Lemma
3.4
(representation closure),
x\mapsto\theta(x)
lies in the domain of diagonalization. Applying the
Diagonal Lemma
to
\theta(x)
yields a sentence
\psi

with

\psi\;\leftrightarrow\;\theta(\ulcorner\psi\urcorner)\;\equiv\;\neg C_{S}(\ulcorner\psi\urcorner).

(2.6)

By universality,
\psi
has a corresponding SAT instance

\psi_{S}\in\mathsf{SAT}
encoding the same computation.
Thus
\psi_{S}
satisfies the required equivalence: Let
\psi_{S}
be as in Lemma
2.2
.

1.

Suppose first that
S(\psi_{S})=\mathsf{SAT}
. Then
C_{S}(\psi_{S})
holds, so
\psi_{S}
is true iff
\neg C_{S}(\psi_{S})
holds, yielding a contradiction.

2.

Suppose instead that
S(\psi_{S})=\mathsf{UNSAT}
. Then
C_{S}(\psi_{S})
is false, so
\neg C_{S}(\psi_{S})
holds, and hence
\psi_{S}
is true. Thus
S
again misclassifies
\psi_{S}
.

In both cases
S
fails. No total computable solver for
\mathsf{SAT}
can correctly classify its own internalized correctness instance.
∎

Exposition
.

The crucial point is that universality converts meta-classification into an object-level SAT instance. The
“meta-question”
becomes an ordinary object level formula in
\mathsf{SAT}
, and diagonal closure forces the existence of a self-referential instance that no total classifier can handle correctly. Misclassification is therefore a structural consequence of representability, not a limitation of particular algorithms.
A concrete illustration shows that diagonal obstruction arises even in the smallest representational setting. Consider a toy SAT domain with only two formulas and a classifier whose entire behavior fits in a single table.

Example 2.3
.

Let

\Phi_{0}:=p,\quad\Phi_{1}:=\neg p,

(2.7)

interpreted as
\mathsf{SAT}
and
\mathsf{UNSAT}
respectively. Define a total classifier
S
by

S(p)=\mathsf{SAT},\quad S(\neg p)=\mathsf{UNSAT}.

(2.8)

We encode the statements

S(\varphi)=\mathsf{SAT}\quad\text{and}\quad S(\varphi)=\mathsf{UNSAT}

(2.9)

by the formulas
p
and
\neg p
themselves. Thus the classifier’s output is representable inside the same two-element instance space it acts upon. There exists
\Psi_{S}\in\{p,\neg p\}
such that

\Psi_{S}\;\leftrightarrow\;\neg\bigl(S(\Psi_{S})=\mathsf{SAT}\bigr).

(2.10)

Let
\theta(x)
express
“
S(x)=\mathsf{UNSAT}
”
. Since
\theta
ranges over
\{p,\neg p\}
, diagonalization reduces to checking the two possibilities. Setting
\Psi_{S}
to the formula encoding
\theta(\Psi_{S})
yields the desired fixed point (
2.10
). The classifier
S
necessarily misclassifies
\Psi_{S}
: If
S(\Psi_{S})=\mathsf{SAT}
, makes
\Psi_{S}
satisfiable, contradicting the output. If
S(\Psi_{S})=\mathsf{UNSAT}
, then
\Psi_{S}
is satisfiable, again contradicting the output. In both cases,
S
fails.

Remark
.

Even in a two-formula universe, once a classifier’s own output is representable in the domain it classifies, an internal fixed point arises and
forces
misclassification. No additional expressive resources are required. This example shows that no sophistication or expressive strength is needed to generate diagonal obstruction. Once a classifier’s output can be encoded within the same domain it attempts to classify, even the smallest structure admits a fixed point forcing failure. The collapse of meta-level correctness into object-level representation is therefore intrinsic to any unbounded, representable problem class.

2.2 Total Classifiers and Encoded Problem Spaces

Definition 2.4

(Gödel-Representable Class)
.

A class of problems
\mathcal{C}
is
Gödel-Representable
if there exists a primitive–recursive encoding

\mathsf{code}:\mathcal{C}\to\mathbb{N},\quad P\mapsto\ulcorner P\urcorner,

(2.11)

together with a primitive–recursive decoding map

\mathsf{decode}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathcal{C},\quad e\mapsto P_{e},

(2.12)

such that
\mathsf{decode}(\mathsf{code}(P))=P
for all
P\in\mathcal{C}
, and the evaluation relation for each
P_{e}
is arithmetically definable.

Definition 2.5

(Total Classifier)
.

A
total classifier
is a total computable function on a
Gödel-Representable
class
\mathcal{C}

\mathsf{Solved}:\mathbb{N}\to{\mathsf{Solvable},\mathsf{Unsolvable}},

(2.13)

intended to assign to each code (
e
) the correct solvability status of the corresponding problem,
P_{e}\in\mathcal{C}
.

2.3 The Self-Misclassification Instance

Problem 2.6

(Self-Misclassification Problem)
.

Define
P_{1}
to be the problem whose code
\ulcorner P_{1}\urcorner
satisfies

P_{1}\;\leftrightarrow\;\bigl(\mathsf{Solved}(\ulcorner P_{1}\urcorner)=\mathsf{Unsolvable}\bigr).

(2.14)

Lemma 2.7

(Existence of
P_{1}
)
.

There exists a problem
P_{1}
(unique up to provable equivalence) satisfying this specification in any arithmetically sound extension of
\mathrm{I}\Sigma_{1}
. By applying the
Diagonal Lemma
to
\varphi(x)
expressing that the problem encoded by
x
asserts its own misclassification, one obtains a sentence
\psi
with

\psi\;\leftrightarrow\;\varphi(\ulcorner\psi\urcorner),

(2.15)

which defines
P_{1}
.

Theorem 2.8

(Reflective Obstruction for Total Classifiers)
.

No arithmetically sound total classifier
\mathsf{Solved}
on a
Gödel-Representable
class
\mathcal{C}
can classify
P_{1}
correctly. In particular,

\mathsf{Solved}(\ulcorner P_{1}\urcorner)=\mathsf{Solvable}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;P_{1}\text{ is false},

(2.16)

\mathsf{Solved}(\ulcorner P_{1}\urcorner)=\mathsf{Unsolvable}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;P_{1}\text{ is true}.

(2.17)

These equivalences jointly preclude correctness.

Remark
.

The contradiction arises solely from:

(i)

totality over all
Gödel Numbers
,

(ii)

representational closure under decoding.

No explicit self-mapping is embedded in
\mathsf{Solved}
; the fixed point is forced by the representational structure.

Example 2.9

(Solver’s Paradox)
.

The
Solver’s Paradox
provides a minimal setting in which the structural mechanism of unbounded quantification becomes explicit. Quantifying over a
Gödel-Representable
problem space automatically produces a fixed point, and any attempt to classify all problems uniformly must confront the corresponding self-referential instance:

…Let
P
be the problem:

“Is the classification output for
\ulcorner P\urcorner
correct?”

…Let
Q
be the problem:

“Is the classification correct
in virtue of
its own diagonal form?”

…Let
C
be the global problem:

“Must any total classifier deciding between
\{P,Q\}
on the coded problem space
misclassify at least one of them?”

Or simpler:

If the Diagonal Lemma holds,

must at least one problem remain open?

The fixed point embodied by
P
forces the classifier to evaluate its own behaviour, and
Q
makes the dependence on diagonal closure explicit. The combined global question
C
exhibits the essential phenomenon: once the domain of quantification contains its own representations, any total classification procedure inherits a self-referential instance that cannot be uniformly witnessed. The
Solver’s Paradox
thereby serves as the minimal model of the impredicative transport mechanism.

3 Fixed Point Theorem

3.1 PSSP Isomorphism

Thesis 3.1

(Problem–Solutions–Statement–Proofs Isomorphism)
.

For any recursively representable class of problems
\mathcal{C}
equipped with a primitive–recursive evaluation predicate, global assertions over
\mathcal{C}
exhibit the same structural features as arithmetical provability over sentences. In particular:

1.

Arithmetization.
Each problem
P_{e}\in\mathcal{C}
behaves as a uniformly coded predicate, and global quantifiers over
\mathcal{C}
act as internal quantifiers over these coded predicates.

2.

Diagonal Closure.

Because the evaluation predicate for
\mathcal{C}
is arithmetically definable, any global assertion

G\equiv\forall e\,\Phi(e)

(3.18)

induces a fixed point
P^{\ast}
satisfying

P^{\ast}\;\leftrightarrow\;\Phi(\ulcorner P^{\ast}\urcorner),

(3.19)

3.

Reflection Analogue.
Verification of
G
requires validating the fixed–point instance

\Phi(\ulcorner P^{\ast}\urcorner),

(3.20)

which is equivalent to asserting a local
Reflection Principle
for the evaluator of
\mathcal{C}
. Thus total global classification over
\mathcal{C}
behaves like affirming the soundness of a provability predicate.

Consequently, problem–quantification and provability share a common architecture: representability forces diagonalization, and diagonalization forces reflection. Global problems over
Gödel-Representable
domains therefore inherit the same limitations as arithmetical provability.

Theorem 3.2

(No Universal Problem Solver)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a
Gödel-Representable
class of problems with primitive–recursive coding and decoding maps and an arithmetically definable evaluation predicate, as in Thesis
3.1
. For every total computable function

\mathsf{Solved}:\mathbb{N}\to\{\mathsf{Solvable},\mathsf{Unsolvable}\},

(3.21)

intended to assign to each code
e
the correct solvability status of the problem
P_{e}\in\mathcal{C}
, there exists a problem
P_{\mathsf{Solved}}\in\mathcal{C}
such that
\mathsf{Solved}
does not classify
P_{\mathsf{Solved}}
correctly. In particular, no total computable problem solver on
\mathcal{C}
can be arithmetically sound.

Work in a sound, recursively axiomatizable extension of
\mathrm{I}\Sigma_{1}
that represents
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathsf{Solved}
. By arithmetization, there is a formula
\sigma(x)
expressing

\sigma(x)\;\equiv\;\text{\ltxml@oqmark@open\textquotedblleft\penalty\hskip-0.0002pt\hskip 0.0002pt$x$ is classified as $\mathsf{Unsolvable}$ by $\mathsf{Solved}$\textquotedblright\ltxml@oqmark@close{}}.

(3.22)

Applying the
Diagonal Lemma
to
\sigma(x)
yields a sentence
\psi
such that

\psi\;\leftrightarrow\;\sigma(\ulcorner\psi\urcorner),

(3.23)

so that the corresponding problem
P_{\mathsf{Solved}}
satisfies

P_{\mathsf{Solved}}\;\leftrightarrow\;\bigl(\mathsf{Solved}(\ulcorner P_{\mathsf{Solved}}\urcorner)=\mathsf{Unsolvable}\bigr).

(3.24)

By exhaustive analysis:

(i)

if
\mathsf{Solved}(\ulcorner P_{\mathsf{Solved}}\urcorner)=\mathsf{Solvable}
, then
P_{\mathsf{Solved}}
is false by the defining biconditional, so the classification is incorrect.

(ii)

if
\mathsf{Solved}(\ulcorner P_{\mathsf{Solved}}\urcorner)=\mathsf{Unsolvable}
, then
P_{\mathsf{Solved}}
is true, and again the classification is incorrect. Thus every total computable
\mathsf{Solved}
misclassifies at least one problem in
\mathcal{C}
.

∎

Remark
.

Thesis
3.1
identifies the structural correspondence between global assertions over
\mathcal{C}
and arithmetical provability. Theorem
3.2
is the direct diagonal consequence: once a problem class is representable and its evaluation predicate is arithmetically definable, any total problem solver on that class admits a self-referential counterexample. For every solver there is a problem that cannot be correctly solved by it.

3.2 Global Assertions over Coded Domains

Exposition
.

Unbounded assertions over a class of problems forces a fixed point, and that the verification of such assertions requires a form of reflection incompatible with finitary justification. Throughout,
\mathrm{T}
denotes a sound, recursively axiomatizable extension of
\mathrm{\mathrm{I}\Sigma_{1}}
.

Definition 3.3

(Class-Quantification)
.

A sentence exhibits class-quantification over a recursively representable class
\mathcal{C}
if it has the form

\forall P\in\mathcal{C}\;\Phi(P)\quad\text{or equivalently}\quad\forall e\;\Phi(P_{e}).

(3.25)

Lemma 3.4

(Representation Closure)
.

If
\mathcal{C}
is recursively representable, then for every formula
\varphi(x)
the numeral
\ulcorner\forall e\,\varphi(e)\urcorner
lies in the domain of class-quantification.

Any global quantifier over a
Gödel
-coded domain necessarily ranges over its own encoded representation. This is the structural source of the diagonal phenomena to follow.
∎

Lemma 3.5

(Fixed Points)
.

Let
\Phi(P)
define a property of problems in
\mathcal{C}
, and set
\varphi(x)\equiv\Phi(P_{x})
. The global assertion

G\;\equiv\;\forall P\in\mathcal{C}\,\Phi(P)

(3.26)

induces a problem
P^{\ast}\in\mathcal{C}
satisfying the fixed-point condition

P^{\ast}\;\leftrightarrow\;\Phi(\ulcorner P^{\ast}\urcorner).

(3.27)

The
Diagonal Lemma
applied to
x\mapsto\varphi(x)
produces a sentence
\psi
satisfying
\psi\leftrightarrow\varphi(\ulcorner\psi\urcorner)
. Interpreting
\psi
as a code of a problem yields the required
P^{\ast}
. Self-reference emerges
“automatically”
from the quantifier structure.
∎

3.3 Verification and Reflection

Lemma 3.6

(Verification)
.

Let
\mathrm{T}
be as above. A global assertion
G
is
verified in
\mathrm{T}
if:

(i)

\mathrm{T}\vdash G
, and

(ii)

the epistemic justification for
G
consists of this derivation together with soundness assumptions for
\mathrm{T}
formalizable as reflection for the syntactic class of
G
.

Verification is a finitary proof accompanied by a minimal soundness assumption:
\mathrm{T}
is correct about the relevant
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
computational facts. This is the smallest admissible metatheoretic commitment. Any opposing viewpoint is strictly longer and more complicated. See
Inductive Inference
of
Solomonoff 1964a
;
Solomonoff 1964b
.
∎

Theorem 3.7

(Meta-Reflection Requirement)
.

Let
G\equiv\forall P\in\mathcal{C}\,\Phi(P)
and let
P^{\ast}
be the fixed point from Lemma
3.5
. Let
U
be any sound meta-theory formalizing
\mathrm{T}
and proving
\mathrm{T}
sound for
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
evaluation over
\mathcal{C}
. If
\mathrm{T}
verifies
G
, then

U\vdash\mathrm{Prov}_{\mathrm{T}}(\ulcorner\Phi(\ulcorner P^{\ast}\urcorner)\urcorner)\rightarrow\Phi(\ulcorner P^{\ast}\urcorner).

(3.28)

Hence also

U\vdash\mathrm{Prov}_{\mathrm{T}}(\ulcorner\Phi(\ulcorner P^{\ast}\urcorner)\urcorner)\rightarrow P^{\ast}.

(3.29)

If
\mathrm{T}\vdash G
, then
\mathrm{T}
proves
\Phi(\ulcorner P^{\ast}\urcorner)
. Meta-theory
U
therefore proves
\mathrm{Prov}_{\mathrm{T}}(\ulcorner\Phi(\ulcorner P^{\ast}\urcorner)\urcorner)
. By soundness for
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
evaluation predicates,
U
infers the corresponding instance of reflection. The fixed-point identity
P^{\ast}\leftrightarrow\Phi(\ulcorner P^{\ast}\urcorner)
transfers this to
P^{\ast}
itself.
∎

Observation
.

Global class-quantification produces three structural consequences:

(i)

a diagonal fixed point internal to the coded domain,

(ii)

a verification load that collapses to local reflection at that fixed point,

(iii)

the impossibility of validating all such reflection instances within any consistent, recursively axiomatizable finitary theory.

Remark
.

These mechanisms are invariant across domains: whenever a problem class is representable and quantification is global, the resulting assertion inherits
Impredicativity
.

Theorem 3.8

(Reduction to Arithmetized Syntax)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a
Gödel-Representable
problem class whose evaluation predicate is arithmetically definable. Then for every problem
P\in\mathcal{C}
there exists a sentence
\theta_{P}
such that:

(i)

P
is solvable (in the intended semantics of
\mathcal{C}
) iff
\theta_{P}
is true in the standard model
\mathbb{N}
,

(ii)

P
is provably solvable in a theory
\mathrm{T}
iff
\mathrm{T}\vdash\theta_{P}
.

Thus problem evaluation in
\mathcal{C}
reduces to sentence evaluation in the arithmetical theory representing
\mathcal{C}
.

Logic 3.9

(Equivalence of FOL)
.

If the correspondence
P\mapsto\theta_{P}
failed to preserve syntactic equivalence, then no standard arithmetization of syntax could be carried out, as the induced coding would not support the canonical internal definitions of substitution and provability, see
[
Hájek and Pudlák 1998
;
Visser 1984
]
.

Theorem 3.10

(Structural Undissolvability of
\mathsf{P}\text{ vs. }\mathsf{NP}
)
.

Let
\mathrm{T}
be any consistent, recursively axiomatizable, arithmetically sound
extension of
\mathrm{I}\Sigma_{1}
. If
\mathrm{T}
arithmetizes
\mathsf{NP}
-verification,
then the formal sentence expressing
\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{NP}

[
Cook 1971
]
requires, for its
verification in
\mathrm{T}
, a
Reflection Principle
that is not provable in
\mathrm{T}
.
Consequently, no such theory can internally justify
\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{NP}
.

Remark
.

The obstruction established herein does not conflict with the classical
Relativization Barrier
of
Baker et al. 1975
, the
Natural-proofs Barrier
of
Razborov and Rudich 1997
. Those results constrain specific families of proof techniques. By contrast, the present obstruction arises from the arithmetized verification conditions of the uniform statement itself. The diagonal fixed point is forced by representability and class-quantification, not by the expressive limitations of any particular method. Thus the argument is orthogonal to the standard barriers: it neither circumvents nor violates them, but operates at a level where technique restrictions are not yet in play.

4 Application: Uniformity in Class–NClass Separations

4.1 Deterministic and Nondeterministic Classes

Exposition
.

We apply the transport mechanism to uniform complexity assertions. Let
\mathsf{Class}
be a recursively representable family of deterministic machines (e.g. polynomial-time Turing machines) and
\mathsf{NClass}
a recursively representable nondeterministic class (e.g. nondeterministic polynomial time). Both admit arithmetized evaluation predicates. The uniform separation problem asks whether every nondeterministic language has a deterministic realization.

Definition 4.1

(Uniform Determinization Statement)
.

The assertion
\mathsf{Class}=\mathsf{NClass}
is formalized by

\varphi_{\mathsf{Class=NClass}}:\;\exists M\in\mathsf{Class}\;\forall x\;\mathsf{Correct}(M,x),

(4.30)

where
\mathsf{Correct}(M,x)
expresses that
M
decides a fixed complete problem for
\mathsf{NClass}
on input
x
.

Exposition
.

Every machine
N\in\mathsf{NClass}
possesses an arithmetically definable verification predicate
\mathsf{Eval}_{\mathsf{NClass}}(e,x,y)
. Hence the class is
predicate-closed
: quantifying over
\mathsf{NClass}
amounts to quantifying over a
Gödel
-coded space of first-order definable predicates. Any uniform deterministic solver must therefore operate over the entire encoded predicate domain, so uniformity intrinsically
“imports”
the
Impredicativity
analyzed in Section
3
.

Example 4.2

(Combinatorial Escalation via First-Order Encodings)
.

Consider the optimal-move problem for the
Rubik’s Cube
. Let
\mathsf{At}(s,p,c)
record
“sticker”
positions,
\mathsf{Row}_{xy}(r,p)
specify face-slices,
\mathsf{RotXYZ}(m)
encode moves, and
\mathsf{Next}(m,c,c^{\prime})
denote transitions. Transition constraints are first-order expressible:

\displaystyle\mathsf{RotXYZ}(m)\;\wedge\;\mathsf{Row}_{xy}(r,p_{1},p_{2},p_{3})

(4.31)

\displaystyle\quad\to\;\forall s_{1}\,s_{2}\,s_{3}\;\bigl(

(4.32)

\displaystyle\qquad\mathsf{At}(s_{1},p_{1},c)\;\wedge\;\mathsf{At}(s_{2},p_{2},c)\;\wedge\;\mathsf{At}(s_{3},p_{3},c)

(4.33)

\displaystyle\qquad\to\;\mathsf{Next}(m,c,c^{\prime})\bigr).

(4.34)

Despite combinatorial blowup, the transition system remains first-order definable. Thus, the
“global optimality question”
—whether a given solver outputs minimal solutions for
all
configurations—already quantifies over a predicate-closed,
Gödel-Representable
domain and thereby inherits the
Impredicativity
mechanism.

Lemma 4.3

(First-Order Evaluation Schema for
\mathsf{NP}
)
.

There exists a first-order formula
\mathsf{Eval}_{\mathsf{NP}}(e,x,y)
of arithmetic such that:

(i)

For every code
e
of a nondeterministic polynomial-time Turing machine
M_{e}
and every input
x
, there is a polynomial
p_{e}
with

M_{e}\text{ accepts }x\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\exists y\;\bigl(|y|\leq p_{e}(|x|)\;\wedge\;\mathsf{Eval}_{\mathsf{NP}}(e,x,y)\bigr).

(4.35)

(ii)

For every language
L\in\mathsf{NP}
there exists a code
e
such that for all
x
,

x\in L\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\exists y\;\mathsf{Eval}_{\mathsf{NP}}(e,x,y).

(4.36)

In particular, the class
\mathsf{NP}
is predicate-closed: every
\mathsf{NP}
language is obtained by instantiating the single first-order schema
\mathsf{Eval}_{\mathsf{NP}}
at an appropriate machine code
e
.

Fix a
Gödel Numbering
of nondeterministic
Turing Machines
and encode runs of
M_{e}
on input
(x,y)
as finite time–space diagrams over
\mathbb{N}
, with predicates for tape symbols, head position, state, and transition. The local transition constraints and acceptance condition are first-order expressible, yielding a uniform formula
\mathsf{Eval}_{\mathsf{NP}}(e,x,y)
that holds exactly when
(x,y)
describes an accepting run of
M_{e}
in time bounded by a polynomial in
|x|
. Every
\mathsf{NP}
language admits such a verifier, so each
L\in\mathsf{NP}
arises by fixing a single parameter
e
in this schema. Thus
\mathsf{NP}
fits the predicate-closed pattern of Lemma
4.4
.
∎

Remark
.

The predicate-closure of
\mathsf{NP}
is not merely a formal artifact. Empirically, every standard computational model capable of expressing nondeterministic polynomial-time verification admits a uniform encoding of NP problems by a single evaluation schema. This robustness underlies
Kleene Realizability

[
Kleene 1952
]
, and the practical fact that all contemporary computational systems can represent NP verification tasks in a common structural format. The uniform first-order evaluator thus reflects a stable empirical invariance of implementable computation.

Lemma 4.4

(Predicate-Closed Representation Induces Impredicativity)
.

Let
\mathsf{C}
be a class of procedures such that:

(i)

each code
e
has a first-order definable evaluation predicate
\mathsf{Eval}_{\mathsf{C}}(e,x,y)
,

(ii)

\mathsf{Eval}_{\mathsf{C}}
is arithmetically representable in a finitary theory
\mathrm{T}
.

Then any global solver claim

\exists M\in\mathsf{Class}\;\forall e\in\mathsf{C}\;\forall x\;\mathsf{Trans}(e,M,e,x)

(4.37)

quantifies over a
Gödel
-coded predicate space and induces a fixed point
e^{\ast}
satisfying

P^{\ast}\;\leftrightarrow\;\mathsf{Trans}(e^{\ast},M,e^{\ast},x^{\ast}).

(4.38)

Hence verifying such a claim requires a
Reflection Principle
for
P^{\ast}
, and is therefore impredicative relative to
\mathrm{T}
.

Observation
.

The
Diagonal Lemma
applied to
x\mapsto\mathsf{Trans}(x,M,x,\cdot)
yields the fixed point. The correctness of a uniform solver at
e^{\ast}
is equivalent to the relevant reflection instance. Finitary theories cannot discharge this obligation.

4.2 Uniformity Implies Class-Quantification

Observation
.

Although
\varphi_{\mathsf{Class=NClass}}
has the form
\exists M\forall x
, its semantics require
M
to solve
every

\mathsf{NClass}
problem. This embeds a suppressed quantifier over all encoded nondeterministic machines.

Proposition 4.5

(Implicit Class-Quantification)
.

Assume
\varphi_{\mathsf{Class=NClass}}
. Then there exists a computable map
G
such that

\forall e\bigl(\mathsf{NClass\_code}(e)\rightarrow\mathsf{Trans}(e,G(e))\bigr).

(4.39)

Exposition
.

If
M
solves a complete
\mathsf{NClass}
problem, then
G(e)
is defined by precomposing
M
with the standard reduction from
L(e)
to the complete problem. The uniform solver thus induces a global deterministic simulation claim.

4.3 Diagonal Fixed Points

Theorem 4.6

(Diagonal Fixed Point for Class–NClass Equivalence)
.

If
\mathsf{Class}=\mathsf{NClass}
, then there exists
e^{\ast}
such that the encoded problem
P^{\ast}
satisfies

P^{\ast}\;\leftrightarrow\;\mathsf{Trans}(e^{\ast},G(e^{\ast})).

(4.40)

Exposition
.

P^{\ast}
asserts that the uniform deterministic solver correctly resolves its own representational instance. This is the complexity-theoretic version of the classical self-verifying fixed point.

Theorem 4.7

(Verification Collapse for Class–NClass)

Let
\mathrm{T}
be a sound, recursively axiomatizable extension of
\mathrm{I}\Sigma_{1}
. If
\mathrm{T}

, then for the fixed point
P^{\ast}
of Theorem
4.6
every sound meta-theory
U
formalizing
\mathrm{T}
proves

\mathrm{Prov}_{T}(\ulcorner\mathsf{Correct}(M,\ulcorner P^{\ast}\urcorner)\urcorner)\rightarrow P^{\ast}.

(4.41)

If
P^{\ast}
is a
\Pi^{0}_{2}
-fact unprovable in
\mathrm{T}
, then
\varphi_{\mathsf{Class=NClass}}
is not verifiable in
\mathrm{T}
.

Observation
.

Verification forces reflection at
P^{\ast}
. Since no finitary theory can uniformly validate such reflection,
\mathsf{Class}=\mathsf{NClass}
inherits a built-in obstruction. Uniformity collapses the verification conditions to a fixed point the theory cannot justify.

Corollary 4.8

(Reflective Obstruction for
\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{NP}
)
.

With
\mathsf{Class}=\mathsf{P}
and
\mathsf{NClass}=\mathsf{NP}
, the arithmetized sentence expressing

without affirming a nontrivial

.

Exposition
.

The hidden universal quantifier ranges over all polynomial-time verifiable predicates. The induced fixed point asserts the correctness of a putative polynomial-time SAT-solver on its own

An informal analogue may be seen by considering the following meta-problems:

Let
Q

“Into which class (

) does the problem

\mathsf{P}\text{ vs.\ }\mathsf{NP}

Let

Once problems range over a Gödel-coded domain, questions of this form

\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{NP}
is the arithmetized manifestation of this pattern.

5 Structural Limits of Unbounded Problem Specifications

5.1 From Uniform Witnessing to Structural Constraint

Observation
.

In a constructive setting, the validity of a problem is determined by the existence of a witness or method that realizes its solution. Under the
Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov
(BHK) interpretation, see
Troelstra and van Dalen 1988
;
Troelstra and van Dalen 2014
. A global assertion

\forall P\in\mathcal{C}\,\Phi(P)

(5.42)

is justified only when a uniform construction transforms an arbitrary coded problem into a witness for its instance of
\Phi
. When the quantifier ranges over an unbounded representable domain, this uniformity requirement encounters a principled obstruction. A problem class is
unbounded
when it is
Gödel-Representable
and its evaluator is arithmetically definable. Quantification over such a class necessarily ranges over its own representational codes. Consequently any global specification over
\mathcal{C}
imports the diagonal phenomenon exhibited earlier: it contains a fixed point
P^{\ast}
satisfying

P^{\ast}\leftrightarrow\Phi(\ulcorner P^{\ast}\urcorner).

(5.43)

The constructive justification of the global statement therefore requires a witness for the instance
\Phi(\ulcorner P^{\ast}\urcorner)
, which in turn depends on evaluating the system’s procedure on its own code. The obstruction arises not from the content of
\Phi
but from the architecture of unbounded quantification. A global assertion is
constructively unstable
when the structure of its quantifiers prevents a uniform witnessing procedure. For unbounded representable domains, instability follows directly: the diagonal instance forces any purported witness to certify the behaviour of the evaluator on its own representation. No uniform construction can discharge this requirement. Hence, global claims of the form

\exists M\,\forall x\,\Phi(M,x)

(5.44)

become unstable whenever their semantics entail an implicit universal quantifier over an unbounded coded problem space. The difficulty is structural. The syntax may appear modest, yet the constructive reading exposes an internal dependency on a self-referential case.

5.2 Independence and the Persistence of Obstruction

Exposition
.

Independence from a given finitary theory does not remedy constructive instability. If a global assertion is unstable relative to
\mathrm{T}
, an extension
\mathrm{T}^{\prime}
inherits the same difficulty. The fixed point generated by the unbounded quantifier reappears within any theory capable of representing the underlying problem class. Thus the obstacle persists across axiomatic strengthening: extending the theory may diagnose the source of instability but does not remove the requirement for a witness at the diagonal instance.

A global problem is
“epistemically ill-posed”
when, relative to constructive standards, the very form of the problem prevents the existence of a uniform witness. For unbounded classes the decisive feature is the transport of self-reference. Attempting to realize a global specification invokes a diagonal instance whose constructive witnessing would demand an evaluation method that certifies its own behaviour.

“Ill-posedness”
does not concern the truth of the assertion but the feasibility of its constructive interpretation. Certain uniform complexity claims—most notably equivalences between deterministic and nondeterministic classes—fall within this pattern (we shall predict an impredicable instance). Their resistance reflects not only computational intractability but a deeper structural mismatch between their quantifier architecture and the requirements of constructive justification.

5.3 Structural Expectation of Unsolvable Instances

The framework developed above yields a general criterion: whenever a problem quantifies over an unbounded,
Gödel-Representable
domain, one should expect the emergence of instances that lack constructive witnesses. This expectation is not an empirical conjecture but a direct consequence of representational completeness. Unbounded quantification forces self-reference; self-reference necessitates fixed points; and fixed points obstruct uniform witnessing.

Global problems defined over such domains therefore exhibit an inherent tendency toward constructive failure. The difficulty is embedded in the representation itself: the problem is not merely
hard
, but structurally unable to support the uniform witnessing required for constructive validation.

6 The Matryoshka as Obstruction

6.1 Persistence of Reflective Barriers

Thesis 6.1
.

We formalize the persistence of reflective obstructions across theories. Let
\mathrm{T}\subseteq\mathrm{T}^{\prime}
be consistent, recursively axiomatizable extensions of
\mathrm{I}\Sigma_{1}
. A diagonal fixed point generated by a global assertion may be undecidable in
\mathrm{T}
, while
\mathrm{T}^{\prime}
can diagnose
\mathrm{T}
’s incompleteness without resolving the instance itself. This nested pattern motivates the
Matryoshka Principle
.

Thesis 6.2

(Matryoshka Principle)
.

A sentence
\varphi
satisfies the
Matryoshka Principle
between
\mathrm{T}
and
\mathrm{T}^{\prime}
if:

(i)

\mathrm{T}^{\prime}
proves that
\mathrm{T}
neither proves nor refutes
\varphi
, and

(ii)

\mathrm{T}^{\prime}
itself neither proves nor refutes
\varphi
.

Thus
\varphi
exhibits nested undecidability: the stronger theory verifies the incompleteness of the weaker one but inherits the same unresolved fixed-point obstruction.

Remark
.

Condition (i) asserts that
\mathrm{T}^{\prime}
can certify the incompleteness of
\mathrm{T}
at
\varphi
. Condition (ii) asserts that
\mathrm{T}^{\prime}
also fails to settle
\varphi
. Hence
\varphi
is nested between
\mathrm{T}
and
\mathrm{T}^{\prime}
: it is recognized as undecidable by the stronger theory, yet remains undecided within it.

Lemma 6.3

(Löbian Constraint)
.

If
\mathrm{T}^{\prime}
is sound and
\varphi
satisfies the
Matryoshka Principle
between
\mathrm{T}
and
\mathrm{T}^{\prime}
, then

\mathrm{T}^{\prime}\nvdash\;\varphi\;\leftrightarrow\;\neg\mathrm{Prov}_{\mathrm{T}}(\ulcorner\varphi\urcorner).

(6.45)

Exposition
.

Were
\mathrm{T}^{\prime}
to prove

\varphi\leftrightarrow\neg\mathrm{Prov}_{T}(\ulcorner\varphi\urcorner),

(6.46)

Löb’s Theorem
would yield
\mathrm{T}^{\prime}\vdash\varphi
, contradicting Definition
6.2
. Therefore
\varphi
cannot be reduced to a canonical Gödel sentence for
\mathrm{T}
. The diagonal obstruction persists and cannot be simplified into a standard unprovability pattern.

6.2 Multiplicity of Nested Fixed Points

Theorem 6.4

(Proliferation)
.

Classically, for any consistent
\mathrm{T}\subseteq\mathrm{T}^{\prime}
extending
\mathrm{I}\Sigma_{1}
, there exist infinitely many pairwise non-equivalent sentences
\{\varphi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfying the Matryoshka Principle between
\mathrm{T}
and
\mathrm{T}^{\prime}
.

Remark
.

Diagonalizing against offsets of the
\mathrm{T}
-provability predicate produces

\varphi_{n}\;\leftrightarrow\;\neg\mathrm{Prov}_{T}(\ulcorner\varphi_{n}\urcorner\oplus n),

(6.47)

with the coding offset ensuring nonequivalence. Each
\varphi_{n}
remains undecidable in
\mathrm{T}
, is recognized as such by
\mathrm{T}^{\prime}
, yet is not decidable in
\mathrm{T}^{\prime}
itself. Reflective obstructions therefore proliferate systematically.

Proposition 6.5

(Nested Obstruction for Global Problems)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a recursively representable class of problems. If a global assertion over
\mathcal{C}
induces a diagonal fixed point requiring reflection for its verification, then its formalization satisfies the
Matryoshka Principle
between any
\mathrm{T}\subseteq\mathrm{T}^{\prime}
capable of arithmetizing
\mathcal{C}
.

Remark
.

As shown in Section
3
, the diagonal instance requires a
Reflection Principle
that no finitary theory can justify. A stronger theory
\mathrm{T}^{\prime}
can diagnose the failure of
\mathrm{T}
to prove the instance, yet
\mathrm{T}^{\prime}
cannot resolve the instance itself. The reflective obstruction is therefore nested and persists across levels of formal strength by classic logic.

Corollary 6.6

(The
\mathsf{P}
vs.
\mathsf{NP}
Matryoshka Pattern)
.

For every pair
\mathrm{T}\subseteq\mathrm{T}^{\prime}
of consistent, recursively axiomatizable extensions of
\mathrm{I}\Sigma_{1}
, the arithmetized sentence expressing
\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{NP}
satisfies the Matryoshka Principle between
\mathrm{T}
and
\mathrm{T}^{\prime}
.

Exposition
.

The uniformity of
\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{NP}
forces a diagonal fixed point asserting the correctness of a purported polynomial-time solver on its own
Gödel Number
. Verification requires reflection beyond the reach of any finitary theory. Hence the sentence is undecidable in
\mathrm{T}
, its undecidability is recognized by
\mathrm{T}^{\prime}
, and yet
\mathrm{T}^{\prime}
leaves the statement unresolved.

The
Matryoshka Principle
captures a stable form of the
Reflection Principle
semantically: a stronger theory can explain the failure of a weaker theory without overcoming the same obstacle. The fixed point reappears unchanged at each level of the hierarchy. Once verification demands reflection over a coded domain, no finitary theory can internalize the required inference.

6.3 Categorical Perspective on Diagonal Closure

Observation
.

A complementary perspective on the
Matryoshka Principle
arises from abstract fixed-point theorems, notably
Lawvere’s Fixed-point Theorem

[
Lawvere 1969
;
Lambek and Scott 1986
]
. In a diagonalizable category, the existence of a weakly point-surjective evaluation map

e:D\to[D,\Omega]

(6.48)

ensures that every endomap of the predicate object admits a fixed point.

A
Gödel-Representable
(problem) class with an arithmetically definable evaluator therefore carries the structural resources of diagonalization. Global quantification over such a domain inevitably ranges over its own representational environment, and a fixed point re-enters the domain whether or not it was intended. If one attempted to impose a global classification while preventing this self-inclusion, one would need to abandon weak point-surjectivity, thereby weakening the representational apparatus to the point that uniform global assertions could no longer be formulated. The nesting of reflective problems is thus not an artifact of encoding but a structural necessity.

7 Conclusion

7.1 Structural Limits of Global Reasoning

Exposition
.

The analysis developed above identifies a single architectural mechanism governing a broad class of global problems. Whenever an assertion quantifies over an unbounded
Gödel
-representable domain, the quantifier necessarily ranges over the representational environment that encodes the assertion itself. By arithmetical necessity this induces a diagonal fixed point
P^{\ast}
internal to the problem space. Under a constructive reading, justification of the global claim requires a uniform witness for the instance
\Phi(\ulcorner P^{\ast}\urcorner)
, and it is precisely here that the structural obstruction arises.

The fixed point embodies a self-referential demand: the witnessing procedure must certify the behaviour of the evaluator on its own representation. No uniform BHK-witness can discharge this requirement. Thus the inability to verify the global claim is not an artifact of technique but a consequence of the problem’s quantifier architecture.

7.2 Expressive Reach vs. Constructive Horizon

Exposition
.

Finitary systems possess expansive expressive resources. They can represent diverse problem classes, encode arbitrary predicates, and formulate global conditions over them. Yet their constructive horizon is more restricted. Unbounded quantification transports self-reference into the problem space, and diagonal closure ensures that the resulting fixed point cannot be uniformly witnessed. In this precise sense, finitary systems can
formulate
more global problems than they can constructively
justify
.

This asymmetry is structural. It follows directly from representability, unbounded quantification, and the constructive requirement that global claims be uniformly witnessed. Nothing in the analysis depends on special-purpose methods or on features peculiar to particular domains.

Uniform complexity assertions such as
\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{NP}
typify this interaction. Their surface syntax is elementary, yet the uniformity they demand quantifies over an unbounded Gödel-coded class of nondeterministic verifiers. The induced fixed point asserts that any proposed uniform deterministic solver must witness its own correctness on its representation. This requirement fails constructively, placing the global uniformity assertion beyond uniform justification.

Corollary 7.1

(Uniform Complexity Obstruction)
.

Any global deterministic–nondeterministic equivalence over an unbounded
Gödel-Representable
class generates a diagonal instance whose verification requires a uniform constructive witness. By the analysis of Section
6
, such witnesses do not exist, and the resulting obstruction persists across extensions of the background theory.

Remark
.

Section
6
established that constructive obstructions generated by unbounded quantification persist through hierarchies of theories. A
“stronger theory”
may diagnose why a
“weaker theory”
cannot justify a global claim, but the
“stronger theory”
inherits the same diagonal fixed point and the same absence of a uniform witness. The obstruction migrates upward unchanged. The
Matryoshka Principle
articulates this recursion of difficulty: the fixed point re-enters at every level of formal strength.

7.3 Epistemic Implications

Observation
.

The broader implication is that syntactic well-formedness does not guarantee epistemic legitimacy. A global problem might be perfectly expressible yet impose witnessing conditions incompatible with constructive justification. The longstanding resistance of certain classical conjectures may thus reflect not merely their complexity but a deeper structural ill-posedness rooted in the interaction between representability and global quantification. These observations delineate principled boundaries on global reasoning within finitary frameworks.

Criterion 7.2

(Constructive Reformulation Principle)
.

A global problem should be reformulated whenever its quantifier structure ranges over an unbounded
Gödel
-representable domain in a manner that forces a diagonal instance lacking a uniform BHK-witness. In such cases the original formulation might be ill-posed: the verification requirements exceed what any finitary constructive justification can provide.

Criterion 7.3

(Diagonal Instance)
.

The diagonal instance arises at the level of the arithmetized
problem universe
. The argument does not require the complexity-bounded subclass to be closed under diagonalization; it requires only representability of its evaluation predicate in arithmetic.

In the end the choice is stark. One may permit unbounded quantification over an inference domain that is itself representable, thereby accepting the attendant
Impredicativity
and the fixed points it generates, or one may impose disciplined typing and constructive semantics in the style of BHK so that global claims demand only those witnesses that the framework is designed to supply. The former grants maximal expressive reach at the cost of importing self-reference into every universal assertion; the latter constrains expression but preserves the coherence of constructive justification. The boundary between these options marks the structural limit of global reasoning in finitary systems.
