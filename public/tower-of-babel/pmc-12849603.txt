Cough-induced rectus sheath hematoma during pregnancy—a rare presentation of abdominal pain

Published: 2025

Background

The diagnosis and management of acute abdominal pain during pregnancy can pose significant challenges as multiple conditions can be accompanied with similar clinical manifestations, including obstetric causes and non-obstetric-related conditions. Rectus sheath hematoma (RSH) is a rare clinical entity typically resulting from a tear of the rectus muscle or damage to the superior or inferior epigastric arteries or one of their branches [1].

Here we presented a rare case of rectus sheath hematoma in a 36-year-old woman at 32 weeks of gestation who developed an acute right-sided RSH following an episode of coughing.

Case presentation

A 36-year-old, gravida 3, para 1, woman at the gestational age of 32–3/7 weeks was admitted to our hospital with a complaint of gradually increasing right-sided abdominal pain that commenced after a coughing episode one week prior to presentation. The course of pregnancy had so far been otherwise unremarkable except for gestational diabetes mellitus. Her obstetrical history included one term delivery and one abortion with dilation & curettage treatment. The patient had no history of trauma or previous surgical procedures. Besides, the patient denied the use of any anticoagulant medications and reported no personal or family history of bleeding disorders.

Her general condition was stable. Physical examination showed tenderness over the entire right abdomen. Fetal ultrasound assessment revealed a normal progression of pregnancy with a healthy fetal growth. Cardiotocography (CTG) showed reassuring tracing. However, a large (17.94 cm × 4.84 cm), oval, well-circumscribed, heterogeneous extra-uterine mass was identified adjacent to the rectus abdominus muscles (Fig. 1). Laboratory tests were performed at the time of admission and did not show any significant findings, and hemoglobin level was 121 g/L with a normal coagulation profile. Following multidisciplinary consultation with the surgeon, radiologist, and also the pharmacist, a contrast-enhanced abdominal computed tomography (CT) was performed after obtaining informed consent from the patient. CT scan indicated that the hematoma was localized within the posterior compartment of the rectus sheath, deep to the rectus abdominis muscle, and manifested as a right-sided paramedian mass extending longitudinally from the costal margin to the inguinal ligament with no evidence of active bleeding (Fig. 2). Given the patient’s hemodynamic stability and unremarkable CTG findings, a conservative management strategy was adopted, including closely monitoring of hemoglobin levels and serial ultrasound evaluations. Hemoglobin decreased from 121 to 78 g/L and transfusion of 5 units of packed red blood cells was applied. Additional conservative treatments included analgesia for pain control, local compression of the hematoma, and bed rest. Subsequent clinical assessment revealed rapid resolution of pain symptoms with a stable hemoglobin level (99 g/L) (Fig. 3). Follow-up ultrasound evaluation comfirmed no expansion of the hematoma. The patient was discharged 3 weeks after admission.

Changes of hemoglobin level

Prior to admission for delivery, the size of the hematoma decreased to 9.68 cm × 2.04 cm. Due to concern that vaginal delivery could potentially aggravated the hematoma, the patient required an elective cesarean delivery at 37 weeks of gestation. Abdominal entry through a midline vertical incision was undertaken to avoid disruption of the RSH. Given the absence of active bleeding and the documented regression of the hematoma pre-delivery, no surgical drain was placed. The patient recovered well without any complications and was discharged on the fifth postoperative day. On follow-up examination two months postoperatively, the hematoma had further resolved, measuring 5.52 cm × 1.27 cm (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Previous studies showed that RSH occurred most commonly in women and only a limited number of cases were reported during pregnancy. RSH often presents with severe, aggravating abdominal pain, which mimics other common conditions. Differential diagnosis of RSH includes gynecological and gastrointestinal concerns, as well as obstetrical conditions such as preterm labor, uterine rupture, and placental abruption [1, 2]. RSH is becoming more prevalent with an aging population and growing use of anticoagulant medications. Reported predisposing factors of RSH include anticoagulant therapy, hypertension, paroxysmal coughing, trauma, severe vomiting, strenuous physical activities, advanced age, previous abdominal interventions, pregnancy, obesity, and coagulation disorders [3]. In the pregnant case, the development of RSH was most likely attributed to paroxysmal coughing.

Due to its anatomical structure, the hematoma can accumulate a substantial volume of blood which may affect the hemodynamics of both the mother and fetus, leading to hypovolemia, hemorrhagic shock, fetal hypoperfusion, even maternal and fetal mortality [4]. Thus, it is of paramount importance to appropriately diagnose and timely treat RSH complicating pregnancy. Physical examination of RSH may include Fothergill’s sign, Carnett’s sign, Cullen’s sign, and Turner’s sign [5]. Laboratory test can reveal anemia, even prolonged partial thromboplastin time or decreased fibrinogen level in cases of significant bleeding.

Ultrasound is recommended as the initial imaging tool during pregnancy, with a sensitivity of 85% to 96% and the advantage of avoiding fetal radiation exposure [6, 7]. However, sonography can falsely indicate intra-abdominal hemorrhage that results in unnecessary surgical interventions. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can complement sonography by enhancing the interpretation of RSH and distinguishing RSH from other abdominal masses. However, MRI may have a lower diagnostic value in acute hematomas within the first 48 h [8]. Computed tomography scanning, more superior to ultrasound, is considered the gold standard for RSH diagnosis and has a sensitivity of 98%−100%. While involving radiation, diagnostic CT usually results in fetal exposure of less than 50 mGy [7, 9]. Contrast enhanced CT scanning has a high density resolution and indicate clear visualization of vascular structures, organs and pathological tissues. It is particularly advantageous for assessing small blood vessels and soft tissue lesions compared to conventional CT examination. In addition, post-processing techniques, including 3D reconstruction and multi-plane reconstruction, based on enhanced CT examinations, assist doctors in conducting in-depth analysis and diagnosis of lesions. The iodinated contrast agents are classified as pregnancy category B drugs by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), which are considered safe for pregnant and lactating women [10, 11]. In this case, after consulting with the pharmacist, we applied a contrast-enhanced CT protocol as the diagnostic benefit was judged to outweigh the potential risk.

The principle of management for RSH depends on the severity of the hemorrhage and the hemodynamic status of both the mother and fetus [3, 12]. For hemodynamically stable patients, conservative management has been considered as the most effective treatment approach due to the self-limiting nature of RSH. Patients can be treated with pain medication, fluid resuscitation, blood transfusion, compression of the hematoma, serial laboratory evaluations and ultrasounds. Whereas surgical intervention or angiographic embolization is indicated for patients with hemodynamic instability or signs of hematoma enlargement [2, 13]. Previous literature report the safety of vaginal deliveries under intensive monitoring during both intrapartum and postpartum periods. If a cesarean section is required, a midline vertical abdominal incision should be considered to avoid disruption of the RSH [2, 14].

Conclusion

RSH remains a rare but potentially severe entity in the differential diagnosis for abdominal pain during pregnancy. Early diagnosis and customized treatment are paramount in mitigating surgical risks and guaranteeing the safe of both the mother and the fetus. Conservative management of RSH is recommended in hemodynamically stable patients with reassuring fetal status.
