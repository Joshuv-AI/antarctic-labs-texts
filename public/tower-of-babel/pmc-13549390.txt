Best Evidence Summary for the Prevention of Ventilator-Associated Pneumonia
Authors: Wenjuan Wu, Yuanxin Pan, Xiang An, Jiamin Lyu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo retrieve, appraise, and synthesize the best available evidence on the prevention of ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) from Chinese and international sources, and to provide evidence-based guidance for clinical nursing practice.MethodsGuided by the 6S evidence pyramid model, a comprehensive electronic search was conducted in BMJ Best Practice, UpToDate, the Guidelines International Network, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, the Scottish Intercollegiate Guidelines Network, the Registered Nurses’ Association of Ontario, the American Association for Respiratory Care, the American Thoracic Society, CHEST, the Cochrane Library, the IBI Evidence-Based Health Care Center database, Embase, PubMed, Medlive, the Chinese Thoracic Society, the Chinese Biomedical Literature Database, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, and Wanfang Database. The search period ranged from January 1, 2016, to January 1, 2026. Evidence summaries, clinical practice guidelines, systematic reviews, and other evidence-based resources related to VAP prevention were retrieved. After quality appraisal and evidence synthesis, the best evidence was summarized.ResultsA total of 24 publications were included, comprising 2 clinical decision support resources, 10 systematic reviews, 5 guidelines, and 7 evidence summaries. Thirty-three recommendations were synthesized into eight practical domains covering organizational strategies, prevention bundles, airway management, positioning, nutrition, device management, pharmacologic approaches, and non-recommended interventions.ConclusionThis evidence summary systematically synthesizes the best available evidence regarding VAP prevention and provides evidence-based guidance for standardized clinical management. These findings may support clinical decision-making and the development of nursing practice strategies for VAP prevention; however, further studies are needed to evaluate the effectiveness of implementing these recommendations in specific clinical settings.

Introduction

Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) is defined as pneumonia that develops more than 48 hours after endotracheal intubation or tracheostomy in patients receiving mechanical ventilation. Pneumonia occurring within 48 hours after ventilator weaning or extubation is also considered within the spectrum of VAP.1 VAP is among the most common infections in patients undergoing mechanical ventilation. According to the National Healthcare Safety Network (NHSN) of the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the median incidence of VAP in medical-surgical intensive care units (ICUs) in the United States is 1.1 cases per 1000 ventilator-days.2 In contrast, the International Nosocomial Infection Control Consortium (INICC) has reported persistently higher VAP rates in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) over the past 2 decades. The reported VAP rate decreased from 24.1 cases per 1000 ventilator-days during 2002–2005 to 11.96 cases per 1000 ventilator-days during 2015–2020,3 but it remained substantially higher than the CDC/NHSN estimate. These data underscore the need to evaluate and strengthen preventive strategies, particularly in LMICs. VAP is the second most common health care–associated infection (HAI) in pediatric ICUs. Critically ill children are at increased risk because of immature immune function and prolonged exposure to mechanical ventilation. The economic burden of VAP varies across settings, with reported attributable costs of 9,966 US dollars4 in the United States and 20,965.28 euros in Spain5 A study including data from 630 ICUs in 45 LMICs across Africa, Asia, Eastern Europe, Latin America, and the Middle East from 2015 to 2020 included 204,770 patients 1,480,620 patient-days, 637,850 ventilator-days, and 7635 VAP cases. Among patients without HAIs, the length of stay was 6.57 days and mortality was 14.06%; among patients with VAP, the length of stay increased to 22.54 days and mortality reached 36.89%.3 VAP prolongs mechanical ventilation and hospitalization, increases antimicrobial use and hospital costs, and is associated with increased morbidity and mortality, thereby adversely affecting outcomes in critically ill patients.1 Although several international and national guidelines and reviews have addressed VAP prevention, existing recommendations vary in scope, evidence sources, and clinical implementation strategies. Many guidelines primarily provide broad recommendations, whereas evidence from systematic reviews, evidence summaries, and clinical decision support resources remains dispersed and requires further integration for bedside application. In addition, updates in preventive strategies, particularly those involving nursing procedures, multidisciplinary collaboration, and implementation approaches, require continuous evidence synthesis. Therefore, this evidence summary aimed not to replace existing guidelines but to integrate and appraise the most recent high-level evidence from multiple sources, including clinical decision support resources, guidelines, systematic reviews, and evidence summaries. By translating heterogeneous evidence into structured and actionable recommendations, this study seeks to provide a practical evidence framework to support standardized nursing practice and clinical decision-making for VAP prevention.

Methods

Eligibility Criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: the study population consisted of adult patients receiving mechanical ventilation; the content addressed preventive measures for ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP); the publication type was an evidence summary, guideline, systematic review, or expert consensus; and the publication language was Chinese or English. Studies were excluded if they were direct translations or duplicate publications, if the full text was unavailable, or if they failed to meet the quality appraisal criteria.

Evidence Search Strategy

Evidence-based questions were formulated according to the PIPOST model proposed by the Fudan University JBI Evidence-Based Nursing Center.6 In this model, the first P, population, referred to the target population for clinical translation of evidence: adult patients receiving mechanical ventilation. I, intervention, referred to preventive measures for VAP. The second P, professional, referred to the professionals responsible for clinical translation of evidence: intensive care physicians and nurses. O, outcome, referred to a reduction in the incidence of VAP. S, setting, referred to the clinical setting for evidence translation: intensive care units. T, type of evidence, referred to the type of evidence resources: clinical decision support resources, evidence summaries, guidelines, systematic reviews, and expert consensus statements. According to the 6S evidence pyramid model,7 computerized decision support systems, topic-based evidence summaries, synopses of systematic reviews or evidence-based summaries, and systematic reviews or evidence syntheses were searched sequentially from the highest to the lower levels of evidence. The search strategy was based on the following terms: “Pneumonia, Ventilator-Associated” or “ventilator-associated pneumonia,” combined with “prevention,” “guideline,” “meta-analysis,” and “systematic review.” The following databases and websites were searched: BMJ Best Practice, UpToDate, Guidelines International Network, National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, Scottish Intercollegiate Guidelines Network, Registered Nurses’ Association of Ontario, American Association for Respiratory Care, American Thoracic Society, CHEST, Cochrane Library, JBI Evidence-Based Health Care Center database, Embase, PubMed, Medlive, Chinese Thoracic Society, Chinese Biomedical Literature Database, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, and Wanfang Database. The search period was from January 1, 2016, to January 1, 2026. Evidence related to VAP prevention was systematically retrieved.

Using PubMed as an Example, the Search Strategy Was as Follows:

#1 “pneumonia, ventilator-associated”[MeSH Terms]

#2 “ventilator-associated pneumonia”[Title/Abstract] OR “pneumonia, ventilator-associated”[MeSH Terms]

#3 “ventilator-associated pneumonia”[Title/Abstract] OR “pneumonia, ventilator-associated”[MeSH Terms] AND guideline[Filter] OR meta-analysis[Filter] OR practice guideline[Filter] OR systematic review[Filter] AND 2016:2026[pdat]

Quality Appraisal Criteria

For evidence summaries, the original studies corresponding to the cited evidence were traced, and the appropriate appraisal tools from the JBI Evidence-Based Health Care Center in Australia were selected according to the study design. Guidelines were appraised using the Appraisal of Guidelines for Research and Evaluation II (AGREE II) instrument.8 Systematic reviews, expert consensus statements, and randomized clinical trials were appraised using the 2016 JBI critical appraisal tools for systematic reviews, expert consensus statements, and randomized clinical trials, respectively.9

Quality Appraisal Process

Three reviewers independently conducted literature screening, methodological quality appraisal, evidence extraction, and evidence grading. Each reviewer completed the evaluation independently using predefined criteria and the appropriate appraisal tools according to evidence type. Disagreements were discussed among the reviewers until consensus was achieved. When consensus could not be reached, a fourth reviewer participated in the discussion and made the final decision. Because this study synthesized multiple types of evidence resources, including guidelines, systematic reviews, clinical decision support resources, and evidence summaries, different appraisal instruments were applied according to study type. Therefore, Cohen’s kappa coefficient was not calculated; instead, agreement was ensured through independent assessment and consensus procedures.

Evidence Extraction and Synthesis

The JBI evidence pregrading system developed by the JBI Evidence-Based Health Care Center in Australia in 2014 was used to preliminarily grade the included evidence.10 The research team jointly discussed, confirmed, and graded the extracted evidence. When conclusions differed across evidence sources, the following principles were applied: priority was given to evidence-based evidence, higher-quality evidence, more recently published evidence, and international guidelines. Given the heterogeneity of included evidence resources, including different study designs, interventions, outcomes, and methodological frameworks, quantitative statistical pooling was not performed. Instead, evidence was synthesized using a descriptive narrative approach. Publication bias was not assessed using statistical methods because this study did not conduct meta-analysis or generate pooled effect estimates. No subgroup analyses were conducted because this study aimed to synthesize and translate the best available evidence rather than compare intervention effects among different patient populations or intervention subgroups.

Results

Study Selection

A total of 1511 records were initially identified. After duplicate records were removed and titles, abstracts, and full texts were screened according to the predefined eligibility criteria, 24 publications were ultimately included. Among these, 2 were clinical decision support resources, 10 were systematic reviews, 5 were guidelines, and 7 were evidence summaries. Discussion

Compared with existing VAP prevention guidelines and bundle strategies, the contribution of this evidence summary lies not in proposing new preventive interventions, but in integrating and translating existing evidence into a structured nursing-oriented framework. Previous guidelines have provided important recommendations for VAP prevention; however, evidence from different sources is often dispersed across clinical guidelines, systematic reviews, and evidence summaries. By synthesizing evidence from multiple levels and organizing recommendations into eight practical domains, this study provides a more accessible framework to support bedside implementation and evidence-based nursing practice.

Organizational Strategies and Evidence Implementation

Evidence statements 1 to 5 first clarify that VAP prevention and control require a comprehensive and scientifically grounded management system. This responsibility should not rest solely with infection control departments but should involve coordinated efforts across hospital infection management, medical affairs, nursing, ICUs, respiratory medicine, laboratory medicine, and other disciplines. China’s first health industry standard specifically addressing the prevention and control of VAP, Standard for Prevention and Control of Ventilator-Associated Pneumonia (WS/T 863–2025), provides relevant specifications. The standard requires hospital administrative departments to formulate rules, regulations, and standard operating procedures and to allocate sufficient full-time infection prevention and control personnel to ensure implementation. In clinical practice, responsibilities should be clearly defined across disciplines, forming a closed loop of surveillance, early warning, and intervention. Nursing managers are responsible for compliance audits and staffing allocation; clinicians lead case diagnosis and treatment; infection control personnel and information engineers collaborate on targeted surveillance and data analysis with feedback; and isolation measures for multidrug-resistant organisms depend on timely reporting by the laboratory department and rapid responses from clinical staff.35

Evidence statements 6 to 9 indicate that the core of translating individual evidence statements into clinical practice is the establishment of a dynamic and iterative training mechanism. Training programs should integrate local microbiological data provided by epidemiologists and updated evidence from the latest literature evaluated by evidence-based methodology experts, thereby ensuring that training content is continuously optimized rather than static. The VAP bundle represents an integrated prevention framework requiring multidisciplinary coordination and standardized implementation. Within this framework, individual interventions should be translated into specific clinical responsibilities according to professional roles. Continuous education, competency assessment, and adherence monitoring are essential to maintain implementation quality. Meanwhile, bedside nursing practices, including hand hygiene, cuff pressure monitoring, and positioning management, remain critical components of daily VAP prevention. Toothbrushing-based oral care requires nursing implementation and professional guidance from dental specialists. Chen et al36 reported that, after implementation of bundled management in patients receiving mechanical ventilation, the incidence of VAP decreased from 25.79‰ to 12.95‰, in-hospital mortality decreased from 10.37% to 4.06%, and both the detection rate of multidrug-resistant organisms (12.77% vs 43.94%) and infection rate (0.52% vs 4.78%) were significantly reduced. In addition, Chen et al37 reported that, among older patients, the use of the plan-do-check-act cycle combined with bundled interventions not only reduced the incidence of VAP and shortened the duration of mechanical ventilation but also significantly reduced the number and timing of pressure injuries and alleviated their severity. These findings suggest that this combined management model has comprehensive value in improving multiple adverse outcomes among critically ill older patients. Therefore, in clinical practice, bundled implementation of multiple interventions may be more effective than single interventions in improving clinical outcomes.

Nursing-Led Airway Management Interventions

Evidence statements 10 to 20 mainly concern bedside interventions for VAP prevention that are performed frequently and involve multiple technical details. These measures focus on oral care, cuff management, and airway maintenance, forming an implementation model led primarily by nurses and respiratory therapists, with collaboration from physicians and pharmacists. Strict adherence to hand hygiene and aseptic technique is the fundamental requirement for all procedures. For oral care, the evidence specifies both frequency, every 6 to 8 hours, and method, toothbrushing combined with rinsing. In particular, the recommendation that chlorhexidine should not be used routinely represents an important shift and requires teams to reassess long-standing nursing routines. Clinical pharmacists should participate in evaluating the risk of mucosal irritation and antimicrobial resistance associated with chlorhexidine. Luo et al38 reported that oral care solution, care method, timing of the first oral care session, and frequency of care are key factors influencing oral care effectiveness, and that oral status is closely associated with the incidence of VAP. In addition, evidence recommends the combined use of toothbrushing, rinsing, and deep pharyngeal suctioning to prevent VAP. However, clinical implementation should also consider safety indicators, procedural complexity, and nursing workload. For cuff management, close collaboration between physicians and nurses is required. Continuous cuff pressure control combined with subglottic secretion drainage can effectively prevent microaspiration. Zhang et al39 improved the cuff pressure management process in patients with artificial airways and found that this intervention standardized cuff pressure monitoring and reduced clinical nursing risks. Respiratory therapists also play a key role in airway humidification, management of condensate in ventilator circuits, and installation of bacterial filters for patients with infectious diseases. Their professional expertise directly affects the safety of ventilator circuits.

Positioning, Mobilization, and Nutrition Strategies

Evidence statements 21 and 22 focus on reducing VAP risk through physical interventions. Elevating the head of the bed to 30°to 45°is a low-cost, high-yield foundational measure that should be implemented and accurately documented by nurses. Encouraging early mobilization and rehabilitation training requires a collaborative network in which intensivists assess the appropriate timing for initiation, rehabilitation therapists develop activity plans, and nurses provide monitoring. Although the level of evidence for this domain is relatively low, the overall value of early mobilization in shortening the duration of mechanical ventilation and improving prognosis has been widely recognized in rehabilitation medicine. This reflects a shift in VAP prevention from passive defense toward active health promotion.

Evidence statements 23 to 25 involve the development of nutritional support strategies and medication management, requiring the joint participation of clinical nutritionists, pharmacists, and nurses. Nutritionists and physicians should select appropriate enteral access routes according to individual aspiration risk, gastrointestinal function, and expected duration of feeding. For mechanically ventilated patients, the choice between orogastric, nasogastric, and postpyloric feeding should be individualized based on clinical assessment and available evidence. During feeding implementation, nurses should no longer regard routine monitoring of gastric residual volume as the sole indicator of feeding intolerance; this update requires a shared change in team cognition. The recommendation against routine stress ulcer prophylaxis is a practice-changing evidence statement. It requires pharmacists and physicians to strictly evaluate indications for medication use and avoid excessive acid suppression, which may increase the risk of gastric bacterial colonization. This decision should be individualized according to the patient’s underlying diseases and current clinical condition, with careful consideration of benefits and risks.

Device Management and Interventions Not Routinely Recommended

Evidence statements 26 to 31 mainly address standardized management of ventilator-associated equipment. In VAP prevention, physicians and respiratory therapists should first jointly follow the principles of prioritizing noninvasive ventilation and conducting daily assessments of extubation readiness, thereby reducing the duration of invasive ventilation at the source. On this basis, the evidence specifies management parameters for ventilators and related accessories. Ventilator circuits should not be changed routinely or frequently, and heat and moisture exchangers should be replaced every 5 to 7 days to reduce unnecessary use of medical resources and contamination risk. For humidification device management, condensate generated within ventilator circuits during the use of heated humidifiers may become contaminated by the patient’s own airway microbiota. If not properly managed, contaminated condensate may flow directly into the lower respiratory tract and cause infection, or it may reflux into the humidification chamber and generate contaminated aerosols that can be inhaled. Therefore, condensate collection bottles should always be positioned at the lowest point of the circuit, kept upright, and emptied promptly. Humidification devices, closed suction systems, nebulizers, and other devices connected to the endotracheal tube also require appropriate disinfection and clearly defined replacement intervals. Regarding ventilator circuit replacement, Lorente et al40 reported that circuits need to be replaced only when visibly damaged or contaminated. For patients receiving long-term mechanical ventilation, weekly replacement of tubing is generally recommended; however, tubing should be replaced immediately if visible contamination or malfunction occurs. For closed suction systems, a systematic review by Huang et al22 found that closed suction was more effective than open suction in preventing VAP, shortening ICU length of stay, and reducing respiratory tract microbial colonization. However, a meta-analysis including 7 randomized clinical trials and 1098 patients showed that bacterial colonization at the suction catheter tip increased significantly after more than 72 hours of use, suggesting that the suction system should be replaced every 72 hours.41 Implementation of these device management standards depends heavily on standardized cleaning and disinfection of reusable devices by central sterile supply departments, as well as regular maintenance and disinfection of ventilators and related equipment by clinical engineers. These supporting processes collectively create an environmental safety barrier.

Evidence statements 32 and 33 involve medication management and related nonpharmacologic measures. For antimicrobial stewardship, systemic or topical antimicrobial agents should not be used to prevent VAP, and routine oral antimicrobial administration for digestive tract decontamination should be avoided. Clinicians should strictly review indications to prevent inappropriate prophylactic antimicrobial use. Selective digestive tract decontamination (SDD) refers to the use of nonabsorbable antimicrobial agents in the oropharynx and gastrointestinal tract, with or without parenteral antibiotics. Selective oropharyngeal decontamination (SOD) refers to the application of topical antimicrobial agents only to the oropharynx. Given the potential increased risk of infection with antimicrobial-resistant organisms associated with SDD, SOD is preferred if a decontamination strategy is considered; however, it should still be implemented cautiously under antimicrobial stewardship principles. In addition, Meng et al42 reported that routine use of proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) for stress ulcer prophylaxis is not necessary in patients receiving mechanical ventilation. PPIs did not reduce the incidence of stress ulcers or VAP-related mortality and may even increase the risk of VAP. However, because the number of included studies was limited, large randomized clinical trials are still needed to further clarify the effect of stress ulcer prophylaxis on VAP.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the quality of the synthesized recommendations depended on the methodological rigor and reporting quality of the available evidence resources. Second, because this study synthesized heterogeneous secondary evidence, including guidelines, systematic reviews, and evidence summaries, quantitative meta-analysis and statistical assessment of publication bias were not performed. Therefore, potential heterogeneity and publication bias should be considered when interpreting the findings. Third, the included evidence mainly focused on adult patients receiving mechanical ventilation in intensive care settings; therefore, the applicability of these recommendations to pediatric populations, specialized ICU populations, and healthcare systems with different resources requires further validation.

Future Perspectives

Future research should focus on several unresolved issues in VAP prevention. Although prevention bundles are widely recommended, the relative contribution of individual bundle components remains unclear. High-quality multicenter studies are needed to determine optimal intervention combinations and evaluate implementation strategies that improve adherence in different ICU settings. Further research should also explore nursing-led interventions and cost-effective approaches, particularly in resource-limited environments.

Conclusions

This evidence summary synthesized current evidence for VAP prevention and developed 33 recommendations across eight clinical domains. The findings highlight that effective VAP prevention requires integrated multidisciplinary strategies, including evidence-based bedside interventions, education, adherence monitoring, and quality improvement. By translating diverse evidence sources into a structured nursing-oriented framework, this study provides practical support for standardized VAP prevention practices and future implementation efforts. Further research is needed to determine the effectiveness and feasibility of these recommendations across different clinical settings.
