HPCAL1 as a novel prognostic biomarker in acute myeloid leukemia and its correlation with immune infiltrates
Authors: Linna Cheng, Huiyang Zhang, Mingyue Shi, Zunmin Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute myeloid leukemia (AML), a heterogeneous malignancy with complex molecular mechanisms, urgently requires improved prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets. This study investigated Hippocalcin-like 1（HPCAL1）, a calcium-binding protein with dual functionality dependent on cancer type, as a potential prognostic marker and investigated its interaction with the tumor immune microenvironment in AML. Through analyses of multiple independent cohorts (GSE13159, GSE34184, GSE24395 and institutional data), we revealed that significant HPCAL1 overexpression in AML samples was correlated with poor survival outcomes. ESTIMATE analysis revealed increased immune activity in the HPCAL1-high group, which was supported by differential expression profiling, which identified 617 genes enriched in inflammatory/immune pathways. Coexpression network and protein interaction analyses further implicated HPCAL1 in immune regulation, particularly through NOD-like receptor signaling, as confirmed by GSEA/GSVA. Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis of the GSE130756 dataset revealed monocyte-specific HPCAL1 expression with altered cellular communication patterns in AML, whereas deconvolution analysis revealed that increased monocyte proportions in HPCAL1-high samples were associated with adverse prognoses. Our findings establish HPCAL1 as a novel prognostic indicator in AML, potentially by mediating its effects through immune microenvironment modulation and monocyte population dynamics. These results provide a foundation for future mechanistic studies exploring the role of HPCAL1 in AML pathogenesis and its therapeutic potential.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23386-0.Keywords: Acute myeloid leukemia, HPCAL1, Biomarker, Immune infiltratesSubject terms: Cancer, Cell biology, Immunology

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML), accounting for approximately 50% of leukemia death cases1, is a heterogeneous hematological malignancy characterized by the aberrant clonal expansion of myeloid progenitor cells2. Despite intensive chemotherapy regimens achieving 60% remission rates in younger patients, the 5-year survival rate is lower than 10% in those over 65 years3, compounded by frequent relapse and therapy-related mortality4. Additionally, the heterogeneity of AML leads to significant variations in clinical presentation, treatment response, and prognosis, posing substantial challenges for the diagnosis and treatment of AML5,6. Current therapeutic strategies, including chemotherapy and stem cell transplantation, are limited by issues such as treatment resistance and adverse side effects7, highlighting the need for the identification of novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Despite advancements in understanding the molecular basis of AML, there remains a critical gap in identifying reliable prognostic indicators for guiding treatment decisions and improving patient outcomes. This clinical urgency underscores the imperative for identifying novel biomarkers addressing three critical gaps: (1) prognostic stratification beyond current cytogenetic/molecular markers, (2) therapeutic targets circumventing chemoresistance mechanisms, and (3) mechanisms underlying immune microenvironment interactions.

Hippocalcin-like 1（HPCAL1）is a calcium-binding protein involved in various cellular processes, including signal transduction and cell differentiation8. Recent studies have revealed the involvement of HPCAL1 in cancer biology, particularly in solid tumors, where a variety of functions, most often but not exclusively in an oncogenic content, have been demonstrated for different cancer types. While demonstrating oncogenic potential in non-small cell lung cancer9, cholangiocarcinoma10 and glioblastoma11, it paradoxically suppresses hepatocellular carcinoma progression12. This tissue-specific duality extends to fundamental cellular processes; recent work has established HPCAL1 as (a) an activator of Wnt/β-catenin11, (b) an autophagy receptor that modulates cadherin 2 turnover13, (c) a ferroptosis amplifier through lipid peroxidation13,14, and (d) a kinase regulator of the cancer stem cell marker DCLK115. Notably, these mechanisms intersect with established AML pathogenesis pathways, including Wnt/β-catenin16,17, autophagy18 and iron metabolism dysregulation19. However, the hematological context remains completely unexplored; no studies have investigated the expression patterns, prognostic relevance, or functional role of HPCAL1 in AML.

Here, to understand the role of HPCAL1 in the development and progression of AML, we conducted a multiomic analysis using available online resources, including transcriptome data and single-cell sequencing data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) resources. We supplemented these findings by conducting analyses of in-house samples. Our collective findings revealed that high HPCAL1 expression is correlated with poor prognosis in AML patients. Furthermore, its expression was associated with an increased monocyte proportion and immune activity. These results indicate that HPCAL1 might serve as a prognostic marker and play a critical role in immune pathways in AML, warranting further investigation into its mechanistic involvement in disease progression and therapeutic response.

Materials and methods

Patient sample Preparation

AML bone marrow samples were collected from newly diagnosed AML patients at Henan Provincial People’s Hospital (Zhengzhou, China). AML diagnoses were based on the French–American–British (FAB) classification system. Normal bone marrow samples were obtained from individuals with no history of hematological disorders or other significant diseases. Mononuclear cells (MNCs) were isolated via density gradient centrifugation with Ficoll Hypaque Solution (Haoyang Institute of Biotechnology, Tianjin, China). All experiments involving human samples were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards established by the institutional and national research committees and were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Henan Provincial People’s Hospital (approval number 2018-51).

RNA extraction and qRT‒PCR

Total RNA was extracted via TRIzol reagent (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and RNA concentrations were measured via a NanoDrop instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Thereafter, cDNA templates were synthesized via cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (Kermey, ZhengZhou, China). The qRT‒PCRs were performed with Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Kermey, ZhengZhou, China) in a StepOne™ real-time PCR system (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Gene expression quantification was performed via the comparative threshold cycle (2−ΔΔCt) method with normalization to β-actin as the endogenous control, with target-specific primer pairs (Table S1) designed following MIQE guidelines.

Acquisition and processing of Raw transcriptomic data

The standardized TCGA-LAML dataset was downloaded from the UCSC Xena website (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/). We used the gene annotation files to reannotate the expression profiles and extracted mRNA expression data on the basis of the human genome information provided by the HGNC database. The reannotated mRNA expression data were used for subsequent analyses.

The series matrix files for the GSE13159, GSE34184, and GSE24395 cohorts were downloaded from the GEO repository (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). HPCAL1 expression values were extracted directly from the series matrix files. On the basis of sample metadata, the GSE13159 samples were categorized into healthy donors and leukemia subtypes20, the GSE34184 samples were categorized into healthy donors and AML patients21, and the GSE24395 samples were categorized into hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) and leukemia stem cells (LSCs)22. Differential expression analysis of HPCAL1 was subsequently performed between these defined groups within each cohort.

Survival analyses

Survival analyses were conducted via R language (version 4.2.2) with the survival package (version 3.5–5)23 and the survminer package (version 0.4.9)24. To determine the optimal cutoff point for dichotomizing patients into high- and low-expression groups, we used the surv_cutpoint function from the survminer R package. This method computes all potential cutoffs and selects the value that yields the maximum standardized log-rank statistic, thus providing the most significant separation between survival curves. Kaplan‒Meier (K‒M) survival analysis was then performed on the basis of the optimal survival cutoff values. The same stratification approach was used in other downstream analyses of the TCGA-LAML dataset where indicated.

ESTIMATE analysis

ESTIMATE analysis was performed via the ESTIMATE package (version 1.0.13)25 in R to analyze the immune score and tumor purity of the TCGA-LAML gene expression data on the basis of optimal survival cutoffs. Statistical comparisons of the score values were performed via Student’s t test.

Differential expression and coexpression analyses

Differential expression analysis of genes was performed between the HPCAL1 high and low groups in the TCGA dataset via the limma package (version 3.56.2)26 in R. Differential expression criteria for genes involved |logFC| > 1 (|FC| > 2) changes and adjusted P values (adj. P) < 0.05. The results of the differential expression analysis were visualized via volcano plots and heatmaps generated via hierarchical clustering. Coexpression analyses were performed to identify HPCAL1-interacting genes via Pearson correlation. Genes were considered significantly coexpressed if they met the criteria of |cor| > 0.5 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.01.

Gene function and pathway enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses27–29 were performed to identify gene/protein functions and biological pathways associated with HPCAL1, respectively, via sets of differentially expressed genes or coexpressed genes as inputs. Analyses were performed on the indicated datasets via the clusterProfiler package (version 4.8.2)30 in R, with a significance threshold of p < 0.01 for the enrichment results.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) and gene set variation analysis (GSVA) were performed to explore pathway-level alterations. For GSEA, the differential expression and coexpression analysis results were analyzed via the clusterProfiler package (v4.8.2)30 in R with the KEGG pathway dataset. Gene sets were ranked by expression fold change, and pathways showing significant enrichment were defined by a normalized enrichment score (NES) absolute value ≥ 1, nominal P < 0.05, and FDR < 0.25. For GSVA, the GSVA package (v1.48.3)31 was employed to calculate sample-specific pathway activity scores on the basis of KEGG gene sets. Differential pathway activity between the HPCAL1 high- and low-expression groups was assessed via the limma package, with adj. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Download and processing of Single-cell data for AML

Single-cell data for AML patients were downloaded from the GEO database, specifically from the GSE130756 dataset. The GSE130756 dataset contains single-cell data from 40 AML patient samples and only 3 normal samples, leading to a significant imbalance in sample numbers. To mitigate the impact of this imbalance on the statistical results, we used the R package Seurat (version 4.3.0.1)32 to read all single-cell data from the samples. We then selected 5 AML samples whose data quality was comparable to that of the 3 normal samples for further analysis. Following the pipeline reported in previous literature and standard quality control criteria for single-cell data, we filtered out abnormal cells on the basis of the following criteria: (i) number of detected genes > 500 (nFeature_RNA > 500); (ii) mitochondrial gene proportion < 10% (percent_mito < 10); (iii) ribosomal gene proportion > 5% (percent_ribo > 5); and (iv) number of UMIs per cell < 20,000 (nCount_RNA < 20000). Additionally, we removed irrelevant genes, such as MALAT1, mitochondrial-related genes, ribosomal-related genes, and hemoglobin-related genes. We also employed the DoubletFinder package (version 2.0.3)33 to detect and remove doublets, assuming a 4% doublet formation rate. The final dataset included the following samples and their respective cell counts: GSM4135770 (778), GSM4135771 (1116), GSM4135772 (569), GSM4135774 (2444), GSM4135779 (2948), GSM4135785 (1749), GSM4135794 (2948), and GSM4135808 (2698). The resulting single-cell matrix comprised 14,524 genes and 15,250 cells.

To remove batch effects and perform dimensionality reduction and clustering, we first used the Harmony package (version 0.1.1)34 to correct for batch effects in the integrated datasets. We subsequently normalized and scaled the filtered data via the normalizeData and scaleData functions in the Seurat package32. The top 2000 highly variable genes were identified via the FindVariableFeatures function for further analysis. Principal component analysis was conducted on the scaled merged dataset, and the first 30 principal components were used to construct a K-nearest neighbor graph via the FindNeighbor function. Cell clusters were assigned via the FindClusters function with a resolution value of 0.4, and visualization was performed via t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE).

Cell Annotation, cell proportion and cell communication analysis

After clustering, we used the FindAllMarkers function in Seurat to identify DEGs specific to each cluster. These DEGs were then annotated on the basis of previously reported marker genes and annotations from the CellMarker 2.0 database35 to define cell subpopulations. Finally, we compared the proportions of cell types between the AML group and the normal group to reveal differences in cellular composition.

To identify the primary cell types in the AML that engage in cell communication, both as signal senders and receivers, with other cells. Using the CellChat package (version 1.6.1)36 in R, we conducted cell communication analysis on AML samples and normal samples. By comparing the cell communication results between the two groups, we identified the main cell types responsible for signaling in AML.

Deconvolution analysis

To accurately infer the cellular composition and gene expression profiles within the tumor microenvironment, we utilized the R package BayesPrism (version 2.0)37,38 for deconvolution analysis. This method leverages single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data, which have been rigorously annotated to provide reliable reference expression profiles. By applying BayesPrism to bulk RNA-seq data from the TCGA-LAML cohort, we were able to estimate the cell type proportions and gene expression levels for each sample. We subsequently conducted comprehensive statistical analyses on the basis of the deconvolution results to further elucidate the biological and clinical implications.

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses

Clinical data for TCGA AML patients were downloaded from the cBioPortal database (available at https://www.cbioportal.org/study/summary?id=aml_tcga_gdc). Clinical variables, including age, sex, FAB subtype, TMB_NONSYNONYMOUS (nonsynonymous tumor mutation burden), BM_BLAST_PERCENTAGE (bone marrow blast percentage), WBC (white blood cell count), PB_BLAST_PERCENTAGE (peripheral blood blast percentage), hsa04621 (NOD-like receptor signaling pathway), monocyte populations, and HPCAL1 expression, were included in univariate Cox regression analyses. Variables significantly associated with AML prognosis in the univariate analysis were subsequently incorporated as covariates in multivariate Cox regression models, along with hsa04621, monocyte populations, and HPCAL1. Both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed via the ezcox package in R.

Statistical analysis

All measurement data are expressed as the means ± SDs and were analyzed via GraphPad Prism software. For comparisons between two groups, Student’s t test was used. For comparisons among more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance was used. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001 were considered significant.

Results

High HPCAL1 expression is associated with poor prognosis in AML

Given the absence of normal bone marrow controls in the TCGA-LAML dataset, we analyzed HPCAL1 expression and its clinical correlation in two additional independent AML cohorts from GEO (GSE13159 and GSE34184). By integrating data from the leukemia-focused GSE13159 dataset, our analysis revealed significant transcriptional upregulation of HPCAL1 in AML, chronic myeloid leukemia (CML), and chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) samples relative to healthy donor samples, with the most pronounced increase in AML blasts. This finding underscores the functional significance of HPCAL1 dysregulation in AML (Fig. 1A). Stratified analysis revealed pronounced HPCAL1 overexpression in distinct AML subtypes (Figure S1A), including inv(16)/t(16;16), t(11q23)/MLL-rearranged, and normal karyotype/other abnormalities, relative to nonleukemic controls. This finding was corroborated in the GSE34184 cohort (Figure S1B), where MLL-rearranged AML samples (n = 15) presented higher HPCAL1 expression than normal marrow samples did (n = 9). Notably, progenitor-level analysis of the GSE24395 dataset revealed a striking difference between FACS-sorted leukemic stem cells (LSCs) and normal hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), with LSCs showing increased HPCAL1 transcript levels (Fig. 1B).

To validate these observations clinically, we performed RT‒qPCR quantification in a prospectively collected cohort of 24 treatment-naïve AML patients and 24 age-matched healthy donors. Compared with the control cohort, the AML cohort demonstrated significant HPCAL1 overexpression (Fig. 1C). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis revealed robust diagnostic potential, with an AUC of 0.884 for distinguishing neoplastic from normal samples (Fig. 1D).

We categorized the TCGA-LAML samples into high-HPCAL1 and low-HPCAL1 groups on the basis of the optimal cutoff values for survival. Survival analysis revealed that HPCAL1 has statistically significant prognostic implications in the TCGA-LAML dataset (Fig. 1E). Specifically, higher expression of HPCAL1 was associated with poorer outcomes in AML patients. Collectively, these data demonstrated that increased HPCAL1 expression is a potential marker for AML diagnosis and prognosis.

HPCAL1 promotes immune responses in AML

As with solid cancers, immune responses significantly impact patient outcomes in AML39,40. To identify how HPCAL1 expression impacts the composition of the TME in AML, we applied the ESTIMATE algorithm to assess the presence of different immune and stromal cells in clinical AML samples from the TCGA-LAML cohort. This analysis revealed no differences in the stromal score between the groups, although highly significant differences were found, where the HPCAL1-high cohort presented higher immune and ESTIMATE scores but lower tumor purity scores (Fig. 2A). These results imply that differences primarily originate from immune cell infiltration levels rather than stromal cell abundance; moreover, the changes in ESTIMATE (combining both immune and stromal components) and purity scores also suggest that HPCAL1 expression predominantly influences immune cell composition rather than stromal components in the AML microenvironment.

To further investigate how HPCAL1 could impact the immune microenvironment in AML, we performed differential expression analysis on the basis of the DEGs identified between the HPCAL1-high and HPCAL1-low groups in the TCGA-LAML cohort. Using DESeq2/limma-voom analysis, we identified a total of 466 significantly upregulated genes and 151 downregulated genes between the HPCAL1-high and HPCAL1-low samples (Fig. 2B–C). Subsequent functional and pathway enrichment analyses of these DEGs revealed 773 biological processes (BP), 73 cellular components (CC) and 80 molecular functions (MF), along with 35 KEGG pathways (Table S2). To further rationalize these hits, we considered that the most relevant pathways involved genes coexpressed with HPCAL1. Accordingly, coexpression analysis within the TCGA-LAML dataset revealed 496 genes that were significantly coexpressed with HPCAL1. Further functional and pathway enrichment analyses of the genes coexpressed with HPCAL1 revealed 253 BPs, 39 CCs and 41 MFs along with 53 KEGG pathways (Table S3, Figure S2A–D). Intersection of the enrichment analysis results from both analyses revealed a proportion of shared BP, CC and MF terms (Figure S2E) along with 11 KEGG pathways (Fig. 2D and Table S4). The latter KEGG included signatures of many pathways linked to inflammatory and immune processes, including neutrophil trap formation, chemokine signaling, B-cell receptor signaling and the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway. Together, these results suggest that increased HPCAL1 expression affects the immune landscape and activity in the AML tumor microenvironment.

To further investigate how HPCAL1 affects the immune microenvironment in AML, we used the GeneMANIA database to construct a protein‒protein interaction network based on proteins that interact with HPCAL1. We further applied these data to Metascape analysis to identify major cellular processes associated with HPCAL1 protein interactions (Fig. 2E). Among the top 5 GO terms identified, the strongest score was retrieved for ‘response to stimulus’, a term with a broad association with response to various external or internal signals, including the activation of numerous signaling cascades, metabolic changes and stress and immune responses.

HPCAL1 actives NOD-like receptor signaling in AML

To supplement the above findings, we conducted GSEA against ranked gene expression from the differential expression and coexpression analyses presented in the previous section. By intersecting the results from the two analyses, we identified 100 GSEA pathways (Fig. 3A), further constructing a bubble plot for the top 25 pathways ranked by normalized enrichment scores (NESs) (Fig. 3B). Among these pathways, 20 of the 25 pathways presented positive NES values for the high-HPCAL1 group, indicating significant activation. Notably, most pathways were associated with immune- and inflammation-related processes, including NOD-like receptor signaling, which contained the greatest number of enriched genes (Fig. 3C). Consistently, GSVA revealed predominantly upregulated pathways associated with high-HPCAL1 samples (Fig. 3D–E), which again aligned with numerous immune- and inflammation-related pathways (Table S5). This finding again revealed prominent upregulation of the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway (hsa04621) in the high-HPCAL1 samples (Fig. 3F).

On the basis of these findings, we sought to further explore the relationship between HPCAL1 and the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway in AML. A comparison of the GSVA scores for the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway and HPCAL1 expression revealed a significant positive correlation between these variables in the total AML cohort (Fig. 3G). These findings suggest that increased HPCAL1 expression is associated with increased activity of the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway. Consistent with these findings, RT‒qPCR analysis of our in-house cohort of 24 AML samples revealed a positive correlation between HPCAL1 expression and the expression of 15 key genes involved in NOD-like receptor signaling (Fig. 3H and S3). Furthermore, applying these findings to the TCGA-LAML cohort to assess the relationship between NOD-like receptor signaling activity and patient outcomes revealed that higher pathway activity corresponded to poorer AML prognosis (Fig. 3I). Together, these results suggest that the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway regulated by HPCAL1 plays an important role in AML development and/or progression.

Single-cell analysis reveals hematopoietic hierarchy disruption in AML

To characterize the cellular heterogeneity of AML and its association with HPCAL1 dysregulation, we performed scRNA-seq analysis on bone marrow samples from 3 healthy donors and 5 AML patients (GSE130756). Following rigorous quality control (Figure S4A-B) and batch-effect correction, t-SNE visualization revealed distinct clustering patterns between physiological and malignant hematopoiesis (Fig. 4A–B, S4C–D). Systematic cell type annotation via literature-curated markers and GeneMarker2.0 identified nine hematopoietic lineages35: eosinophils, erythrocytes, granulocyte‒monocyte progenitors (GMPs), HSCs, lymphocytes, monocytes, natural killer cells, plasma cells, and pre-B cells (Fig. 4C‒D).

The quantification of cell type proportions revealed significant increases in HSCs and pre-B cells in AML, indicating enhanced self-renewal and disrupted early lymphopoiesis. Conversely, marked decreases were observed in eosinophils, erythrocytes, and GMPs, reflecting impaired granulocytic/erythroid differentiation (Fig. 4E‒G). Notably, the monocyte and lymphocyte frequencies remained unchanged. The stability of the monocyte proportion amid functional perturbation prompted focused investigations of immunomodulatory genes, leading us to prioritize HPCAL1 analysis across compartments. These cellular alterations highlight dysregulated hematopoiesis in AML and provide insights for the development of targeted therapies.

Coordinated HSC signaling rewiring and monocytic dysfunction sustain AML progression

To characterize the role of HPCAL1 in AML cell crosstalk, we integrated and batch-corrected single-cell data from AML patients. Following batch correction, dimensionality reduction, and clustering (Fig. 5A–B), systematic quantification revealed striking compartment-specific HPCAL1 expression. The highest level of expression was detected in monocytes, followed by HSCs (Fig. 5C). This pattern suggested a potential role of HPCAL1 in the pathogenesis of AML, particularly in the regulation of monocyte function and the self-renewal capacity of HSCs. The high expression of HPCAL1 in these cell types may contribute to the altered communication patterns observed in AML, further supporting its importance in disease development and progression. To explore the role of high-HPCAL1-expressing monocytes and HSCs in the pathogenesis of AML, we further conducted single-cell data analysis to assess cellular communication. Systematic evaluation of CellChat-derived ligand‒receptor interactions revealed increased global communication entropy in AML patients compared with normal controls (Fig. 5D). Specifically, HSCs exhibited the most profound signaling rewiring in AML (Fig. 5E–F), indicating a critical disruption in the signaling pathways that regulate HSC function. This altered communication could impair the normal self-renewal and differentiation processes of HSCs, contributing to the aberrant hematopoiesis observed in AML. Monocytes in AML manifested a unique immune-evasive signature characterized by near-complete collapse of outgoing signaling (Fig. 5E–F). This dysfunctional signaling axis particularly affects antigen presentation pathways to facilitate leukemic cell immune escape. These alterations in HSC and monocyte communication networks may mechanistically connect HPCAL1 overexpression to AML pathogenesis through HSC self-renewal reprogramming and monocytic immunoreceptor paralysis.

Concurrently, eosinophil-associated signaling circuits were markedly attenuated, whereas hyperactivated crosstalk was observed in erythroid precursors, lymphoid lineages, natural killer cells, plasma cells, and pre-B cells (Figure S5). This coordinated multicellular signaling reprogramming results in a leukemic niche that simultaneously sustains primitive cell self-renewal and subverts antitumor immunity through dual mechanisms.

Dysregulation of the HPCAL1-Monocyte axis disturbs myeloid homeostasis to accelerate AML progression

Having established that HPCAL1-high monocytes and HSCs exhibit profound cell‒cell communication deficits at single-cell resolution, we next sought to determine whether these alterations translate to population-level changes in AML bone marrow composition and clinical outcomes.

We applied deconvolution analysis to the TCGA-LAML transcriptomic data, leveraging our annotated scRNA-seq dataset as a reference. Monocytes constitute the most abundant immune population in AML microenvironments (Fig. 6A). Additionally, the proportion of monocytes was significantly greater in the HPCAL1-high group than in the HPCAL1-low group (Fig. 6B). Prognostic analysis revealed that a higher monocyte count was a predictor of significantly shorter overall survival (Fig. 6C). Moreover, HPCAL1 expression was strongly correlated with monocyte proportion across the cohort (r = 0.32, P = 6.62e-5; Fig. 6D), suggesting coordinated dysregulation. These results suggest that the HPCAL1-monocyte axis contributes substantially to AML progression, where elevated HPCAL1 expression appears to promote functional impairment of monocytes, potentially facilitating an immune-suppressive microenvironment that may accelerate disease. The observed interrelationships among HPCAL1 expression levels, monocyte abundance, and patient mortality highlight the possible therapeutic relevance of this pathway.

HPCAL1 serves as a novel independent prognostic indicator in AML

Comprehensive survival modeling through Cox regression analyses established a critical hierarchy of prognostic determinants in AML. Univariate analysis revealed three factors significantly associated with poor overall survival: HPCAL1 expression (HR = 1.63, 95% CI 1.12–2.39, p = 0.010), monocyte populations (HR = 1.91, 95% CI 1.07–3.41, p = 0.030), and NOD-like receptor pathway activity (hsa04621; HR = 2.71, 95% CI 1.05–7.00, p = 0.040). Age (HR = 1.03, 95% CI 1.02–1.05, p < 0.001) and white blood cell count (WBC; HR = 1.01, 95% CI 1.00–1.01, p = 0.020) also demonstrated significant prognostic value (Fig. 7).

Univariate Cox regression analysis of survival-related factors. The forest plot displays hazard ratios (HRs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and p values for multiple variables, including BM blast percentage, FAB classification, HPCAL1 expression, monocyte count, PB blast percentage, TMB (non-synonymous mutations), WBC count, age, gender, the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway (KEGG ID: hsa04621,www.kegg.jp/kegg/kegg1.html).

In multivariate models adjusted for these clinically relevant covariates, HPCAL1 retained independent prognostic significance (adjusted HR = 1.60, 95% CI 1.07–2.39, p = 0.022), whereas the initial associations observed for monocyte populations (adjusted HR = 1.14, 95% CI 0.62–2.10, p = 0.674) and NOD pathway activity (adjusted HR = 1.39, 95% CI 0.52–3.76, p = 0.512) were statistically attenuated (Fig. 8). Age and WBC count maintained robust independent prognostic value across all multivariate frameworks (age: adjusted HR = 1.04, 95% CI 1.02–1.05, p < 0.001; WBC count: adjusted HR = 1.01, 95% CI 1.00–1.01, p = 0.002–0.007).

Multivariate Cox regression analyses. Phenotypes significantly associated with AML prognosis in univariate Cox regression analyses were included as covariates in separate multivariate models evaluating the independent prognostic value of (a) HPCAL1 expression, (b) monocyte populations, and (c) the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway (KEGG ID: hsa04621,www.kegg.jp/kegg/kegg1.html).

These results establish HPCAL1 as a novel independent prognostic biomarker beyond conventional clinical parameters, notably highlighting that age and the WBC count remain fundamental clinical determinants of AML prognosis. However, the prognostic effects of immune features are context dependent, and their initial univariate significance may be confounded by clinical covariates.

Discussion

Prominent pathological indicators currently used to predict prognosis in AML include recurrent mutations in genes such as FLT3, NPM1, CEBPA, WT1, DNMT3A, IDH1/IDH2, and TP5341–45. However, these markers have limitations in fully capturing the heterogeneity of this malignancy. Therefore, identifying novel biomarkers remains crucial for improving prognostic predictions and enabling personalized therapies. In this study, we analyzed HPCAL1 expression in AML samples via the GEO and TCGA databases and in clinical samples, revealing that HPCAL1 was highly expressed in AML tissues compared with normal tissues. Furthermore, HPCAL1 exhibited promising diagnostic value in distinguishing AML patients from healthy individuals on the basis of the evidence obtained from the ROC curves. Moreover, Kaplan‒Meier plotter revealed that high expression of HPCAL1 might be a risk factor that affects the survival of AML patients. Taken together, these results suggest that HPCAL1 may be a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker for AML. This result is consistent with previous studies showing that HPCAL1 functions as an oncogene in central cancers9,10,12.

To elucidate the potential role of HPCAL1 in AML, we performed a comprehensive analysis of AML transcriptomic data from the TCGA, GEO and scRNA-seq datasets. ESTIMATE analysis revealed that the HPCAL1-high group exhibited increased immune activity within the tumor microenvironment, which may reflect an adaptive immune response to tumors. This observation aligns with the notion that certain immune pathways can be upregulated in response to tumorigenesis, potentially influencing disease progression46,47. The upregulation of immune pathways in the high HPCAL1 expression group suggested a complex interplay between HPCAL1 expression and the immune microenvironment, which could have significant implications for understanding the mechanisms of AML and developing targeted therapies. Differential expression analysis and functional enrichment analysis revealed that the genes related to HPCAL1 are predominantly involved in immune and inflammatory pathways, further underscoring the role of HPCAL1 in modulating immune responses in AML. Our pathway-related analyses revealed that the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway is a critical pathway associated with HPCAL1 expression in AML. This pathway is known to play a pivotal role in the immune response, particularly in the recognition of pathogens and the activation of inflammatory responses48,49. Our findings indicate that increased HPCAL1 expression is correlated with increased activity of the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, suggesting that HPCAL1 may increase immune activity within the tumor microenvironment. This relationship is particularly relevant given the established link between inflammation and cancer progression, highlighting the potential of HPCAL1 as a therapeutic target in AML.

Our scRNA-seq analysis revealed monocytes as the predominant expressers of HPCAL1 in the AML microenvironment, followed by HSCs. Monocytes are known for their critical functions in immune surveillance and inflammation, acting as precursors to macrophages and dendritic cells. Their elevated presence in the tumor microenvironment has been associated with both protumorigenic and antitumorigenic activities, depending on their polarization state (M1 vs. M2)50. Our data, which establish a strong correlation between high HPCAL1 expression and poor patient prognosis, led us to hypothesize that HPCAL1 may be preferentially expressed in or associated with protumorigenic ‘M2-like’ TAMs. This population is known to promote immune suppression, angiogenesis, and leukemic cell survival51. While the present study did not directly profile macrophage subsets, this compelling correlation suggests a potential novel role for HPCAL1 as a marker or functional mediator within this detrimental macrophage subset. Future studies are warranted to spatially resolve HPCAL1 expression within the AML TME via multiplex immunohistochemistry or single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to test this hypothesis formally and investigate whether HPCAL1 contributes to the mechanisms of M2-like polarization or function. HSCs are multipotent cells capable of self-renewal and differentiation into all types of blood cells, including blood and immune cells52. In our study, the significant correlation between HPCAL1 expression and monocyte or HSC proportions suggested that HPCAL1 may influence the inflammatory milieu and hematopoiesis to promote AML development. Further analysis of cell communication revealed that HSCs and monocytes presented significant changes in communication signals in AML, indicating a critical role of HSC and monocyte functions in AML development. The altered HSCs communication could impair their normal self-renewal and differentiation processes, contributing to the aberrant hematopoiesis observed in AML53. The impairment of monocyte communication likely contributes to weakened immune responses and promotes disease progression54. Collectively, these results establish a spatially coordinated HPCAL1-monocyte/HSC axis in which cellular proportion anomalies align with molecular expression patterns to jointly accelerate AML progression through dual microenvironmental remodeling—monocytic paralysis and HSC reprogramming. Consequently, HPCAL1 functions not only as a prognostic indicator but also as a central mediator of tumor-immune crosstalk. These findings establish HPCAL1 as a novel druggable target for AML therapeutics.

Our integrative survival analysis revealed a critical hierarchy of prognostic factors in AML. The persistent significance of HPCAL1 in multivariate models—even after rigorous adjustment for established clinical covariates—solidified its role as an independent biomarker of adverse outcomes. Notably, monocyte populations and NOD-like receptor pathway activity were attenuated upon covariate adjustment. These findings suggest that the prognostic power of HPCAL1 operates through mechanisms distinct from those of conventional inflammatory cascades.

While these results establish HPCAL1 as a novel and independent prognostic biomarker in AML through multiomics integration, several limitations warrant acknowledgment. The inferred roles of HPCAL1 in NOD-like receptor signaling and monocyte/HSC communication remain mechanistically unverified. Future studies employing CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockdown or overexpression should be conducted to confirm its causal role in AML pathogenesis. Furthermore, while scRNA-seq reveals HPCAL1-associated transcriptional programs, it cannot capture dynamic expression changes during disease progression or therapy. Additionally, although HPCAL1 has diagnostic and prognostic potential, its druggability and interactions with standard AML therapies (e.g., venetoclax-based regimens) remain unexplored.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that HPCAL1 is significantly upregulated in AML compared with normal hematopoietic tissues and may serve as a promising diagnostic and prognostic biomarker. Its high expression is independently associated with inferior overall survival and is positively correlated with enhanced immune activity within the tumor microenvironment, suggesting its potential value in predicting the response to immunotherapy. These findings support the clinical relevance of HPCAL1 and provide a rationale for future studies investigating tailored anti-HPCAL1 strategies, particularly in combination with immunotherapeutic approaches. However, further validation through expanded clinical cohorts and functional experiments in vivo are necessary to confirm these observations and translate them into clinical applications.
