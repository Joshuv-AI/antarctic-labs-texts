Abstract
We present gas-phase abundances of carbon (C), α \alpha -elements (O, Ne, Si, and Ar) and iron (Fe) obtained from stacked spectra of high- z z star-forming galaxies with the deep Near Infrared Spectrograph medium-resolution data from the James Webb Space Telescope Advanced Deep Extragalactic Survey. Our 564 sources at z = 4 z=4 –7 have a median stellar mass of log ⁡ ( M ∗ / M ⊙ ) = 8.46 \log(M_{*}/M_{\odot})=8.46 and a median star-formation rate of log ⁡ ( SFR / M ⊙ ​ yr − 1 ) = 0.30 \log(\mathrm{SFR}/M_{\odot}\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}})=0.30 , placing them close to the star-formation main sequence. We find that the stacked spectrum of all our 564 sources has relatively low [ C / O ] = − 0.70 [\mathrm{C/O}]=-0.70 , moderate [ Ne / O ] = − 0.09 [\mathrm{Ne/O}]=-0.09 , and low [ Ar / O ] = − 0.28 [\mathrm{Ar/O}]=-0.28 values at a low gas-phase metallicity of 12 + log ⁡ ( O / H ) = 7.71 12+\log(\mathrm{O/H})=7.71 ( Z ∼ 0.1 ​ Z ⊙ Z\sim 0.1~Z_{\odot} ), suggesting dominant yields of core-collapse supernovae evolved from massive stars. The detection of a weak Si iii ] emission line in our stacked spectrum provides a silicon-to-oxygen abundance ratio of [ Si / O ] = − 0.63 [\mathrm{Si/O}]=-0.63 , which is lower than that of stars in the Milky Way disc and lower than expected by chemical evolution models, suggesting silicon depletion onto dust grains. Likewise, this Si/O value is lower than that we newly derive for two individual z > 6 z>6 galaxies (GN-z11 and RXCJ2248) with negligible dust attenuation. By performing spectral stacking in bins of M ∗ M_{*} , SFR, specific SFR (sSFR), and ultra-violet (UV) continuum slope β UV \beta_{\mathrm{UV}} , we identify [Fe iii ] line detections in the high-sSFR bin and the blue- β UV \beta_{\mathrm{UV}} bin, both of which exhibit supersolar Fe/O ratios, while their C/O, Ar/O, and Si/O ratios are comparable to those of the all-sources stack. Our findings support a chemically young gas composition with rapid dust depletion in the general population of high- z z star-forming galaxies, while raising the possibility of anomalous, selective Fe/O enhancement at the very early epoch of star formation.



1
Introduction

Chemical abundances of galaxies provide key insights into star formation and galaxy evolution.
The first generation of stars formed from hydrogen (H) and helium (He) synthesised during the Big Bang
(
Cyburt et al. 2016
, e.g.,)
, and subsequently stars of various masses assembled to form galaxies.
These stars contribute to enrich elements more massive than He (so-called metals), with the stellar mass governing which metals are synthesised and ultimately released into the interstellar medium (ISM).
Massive stars with
\sim 8
–100 solar masses (
M_{\odot}
) evolve into core-collapse supernovae (CCSNe; e.g.,
Nomoto et al. 2013
) at the end of stellar lifetimes (typically
\sim 3
–40 Myr, which slightly depends on metallicity;
Portinari et al. 1998
), ejecting a large amount of carbon (C) and
\alpha
-elements such as oxygen (O) and neon (Ne).
Conversely, after
\sim 40
Myr, dying low-mass stars with
\sim 1
–8
M_{\odot}
eject their outer layers via stellar winds during the asymptotic giant branch (AGB) phase
(
Herwig 2005
, e.g.,)
.
AGB stars with
\sim 4
–7
M_{\odot}
contribute to nitrogen (N) enrichment via the CNO cycle, while low-mass AGB stars with
\sim 1
–4
M_{\odot}
release C
(
Kobayashi et al. 2011
;
Karakas & Lattanzio 2014
, e.g.,)
.
White dwarf (WD) remnants emerge after the outer layers are ejected, and sufficient mass accretion from companion stars onto the WDs lead to Type-Ia SNe
(
Maoz et al. 2014
, e.g.,)
, which produce iron (Fe) and heavy
\alpha
-elements such as silicon (Si) and argon (Ar)
(
Kobayashi et al. 2020a
, e.g.,)
.
Due to the longer delay time of AGB stars and Type-Ia SNe, C/O, N/O, Si/O, Ar/O, and Fe/O ratios are expected to increase with increasing galaxy age and metallicity
(
Maiolino & Mannucci 2019
, see also)
, as predicted by many chemical evolution models
(
Vincenzo et al. 2016
;
Suzuki & Maeda 2018
;
Kobayashi et al. 2020b
, e.g.,)
.
In fact, many observations for absorption lines of individual stars in the Milky Way (MW) have shown that C/O, N/O, and Fe/O ratios generally increase with metallicity
(
Nicholls et al. 2017
, e.g.,)
, while the observations of individual stars are only limited to the Local Group
(
McWilliam et al. 2013
, e.g.,)
.

Abundance ratios based on emission lines from ionised gas within
galaxies at low redshifts (
z\sim 0
) have been actively investigated
(
Lequeux et al. 1979
;
Izotov & Thuan 1999
;
Izotov et al. 2006
, e.g.,)
.
Such gas-phase abundances can be affected by dust depletion, in particular, a significantly large fraction of Fe is depleted onto dust grains compared to C, N, and O
(
Whittet 2003
;
Jenkins 2009
;
Roman-Duval et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
.
This explains the general observational trends that show that although the gas-phase C/O and N/O ratios increase with metallicity in the high metallicity regime
(
Pilyugin et al. 2012
;
Berg et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
, the gas-phase Fe/O ratio actually decreases with metallicity
(
Izotov et al. 2006
;
Méndez-Delgado et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
.
Whereas there are a number of interesting exceptions that suggest other contributions
(
Telles et al. 2014
;
Kumari et al. 2018
, e.g.,)
, a general understanding of gas-phase abundances at
z\sim 0
would be as follows:
\alpha
-elements from CCSNe dominate at the early epoch, followed by C and N from AGB stars together with Fe and heavy
\alpha
-elements from Type-Ia SNe
(
Kobayashi et al. 2020b
, e.g.,)
, while Fe is depleted onto dust grains.
Indeed,
z\sim 0
dwarf galaxies show
\alpha
-element ratios of Ne/O and Ar/O relatively constant within a wide range of gas-phase metallicity
(
Izotov et al. 2006
, i.e.,
12+\log(\mathrm{O/H})\sim 7.2
–8.5;)
, while delayed enrichment of Ar, a heavy
\alpha
-element compared to O, has been observed in planetary nebulae
(
Pottasch & Bernard-Salas 2006
;
Pagomenos et al. 2018
;
Arnaboldi et al. 2022
)
.

It is worth mentioning that C/O and N/O studies based on emission line observations were extended to
z\sim 2
–3
(
Steidel et al. 2016
;
Kojima et al. 2017
;
Hayden-Pawson et al. 2022
;
Llerena et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
prior to the James Webb Space Telescope (
JWST
;
Gardner et al. 2023
;
Rigby et al. 2023
).
However, the launch of this telescope has enabled studies of chemical abundances even at
z>4
(see Appendix
A
).
Using spectroscopic data of the
JWST
/Near Infrared Spectrograph (NIRSpec;
Ferruit et al. 2022
;
Jakobsen et al. 2022
), various studies have shown that several
z>4
galaxies have C/O and Ne/O ratios comparable to those at
z\sim 0

(
Arellano-Córdova et al. 2022
;
Isobe et al. 2023b
;
Tang et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
.
Some studies have reported that some
z\gtrsim 4
galaxies have low C/O ratios
(
Jones et al. 2023
;
Stiavelli et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
and low Ar/O ratios
(
Bhattacharya et al. 2025
;
Stanton et al. 2025
)
close to those of CCSNe.
These results are interpreted to imply a gas composition dominated by CCSN yields with little contribution from low-mass stars.
Additionally, it is noteworthy that stellar absorption lines of massive quiescent galaxies at
z=1
–3 show deficits in C and Fe relative to
\alpha
-elements such as Mg, indicating that their gas was likely enriched predominantly by CCSNe during earlier epochs
(
Beverage et al. 2025
)
.
These findings are in line with the general understanding at
z\sim 0
.

Interestingly, this understanding has been challenged by an increasing number of
JWST
results.
Some metal-poor galaxies at
z\gtrsim 4
are reported to have high C/O ratios
(
D’Eugenio et al. 2024
;
Ji et al. 2025a
;
Nakajima et al. 2025
;
Scholtz et al. 2025a
)
, low Ne/O ratios
(
Isobe et al. 2023b
)
, or high Ar/O ratios
(
Bhattacharya et al. 2025
)
, attributed to the possible contributions of Population III (PopIII) SNe
(
Vanni et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
, massive (
\gtrsim 30

M_{\odot}
) CCSNe
(
Watanabe et al. 2024
)
, and even theoretical pair-instability SNe (PISNe) whose progenitors have
\sim 200
–300
M_{\odot}

(
Takahashi et al. 2018
)
, respectively.
Although
Curti et al. 2025a
have recently reported that a Wolf-Rayet (WR) galaxy at
z\sim 2
has a detection of the [Fe
iii
]
\lambda
4658 line, which has been commonly used at
z\sim 0

(
Izotov et al. 2006
, e.g.,)
, the [Fe
iii
] line is usually very faint and hence extremely difficult to observe at higher
z
.
In a complementary manner, higher ionisation Fe lines
(
Ji et al. 2024
;
Tacchella et al. 2025
)
, the Fe
ii
complex
(
Ji et al. 2025b
;
Nakane et al. 2025
)
, and the ultra-violet (UV) stellar continuum shape
(
Nakane et al. 2024
;
Nakane et al. 2025
)
have been utilised to estimate Fe/O ratios, some of which exceed the solar abundance ratio.
Such high Fe/O ratios in young galaxies may imply inclusions of (very) massive stars resulting in such as PISNe
(
Isobe et al. 2022
;
Goswami et al. 2022
;
Fukushima et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
or even
>300\ M_{\odot}
stars
(
Kojima et al. 2021
)
, while the possibility of Type-Ia SNe with short delay times has also been discussed
(
Nakane et al. 2025
)
.
This line of argument is similar to that for the
JWST
-
discovered population of N/O
-
enhanced galaxies (NOEGs;
Ji et al. 2025a
) at
z>4

(
Bunker et al. 2023
;
Cameron et al. 2023
;
Isobe et al. 2023b
;
Topping et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
, in that many papers have actively discussed the possibility of N-rich winds from WR stars
(
Watanabe et al. 2024
;
Kobayashi & Ferrara 2024
;
Fukushima & Yajima 2024
, e.g.,)
, very massive stars
(
Vink 2023
)
, and even supermassive stars with
10^{3}\ M_{\odot}

(
Nagele & Umeda 2023
;
Charbonnel et al. 2023
;
Nandal et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
, which are not necessarily required in some scenarios with AGB stars
(
D’Antona et al. 2023
;
Rizzuti et al. 2024
;
McClymont et al. 2025b
, e.g.,)
.

One of the key issues to be addressed here is whether these abundance ratios are common in
z>4
galaxies, as abundance ratios based on fainter emission lines tend to be biased more strongly towards the readily observable population.
For example, the possibility of active galactic nuclei (AGNs) has been discussed for about half of the NOEGs reported so far
(
Übler et al. 2023
;
Ji et al. 2024
;
Maiolino et al. 2024a
;
Napolitano et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
.
Complementary to individual galaxy studies, the stacking technique is commonly employed to characterise the typical properties of galaxy populations in which the target signals are not individually detectable
(
Roberts-Borsani et al. 2024
;
Kumari et al. 2024
;
Glazer et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
.
In a previous work, we focused on N/O ratios
(
Isobe et al. 2025
)
, illustrating that the stacked spectrum of galaxies without reported AGNs
(
Juodžbalis et al. 2025
;
Scholtz et al. 2025b
)
has a lower N/O ratio than most of the NOEGs, implying their rarity.
Regarding other abundance ratios, several studies report low C/O ratios in stacked spectra of
z>4
galaxies
(
Hu et al. 2024
;
Hayes et al. 2025
)
, suggesting a gas composition dominated by CCSNe.
No other abundance ratios have been measured with stacked spectra at
z>4
.
In particular, compared to C/O and Ne/O ratios, the current small sample sizes of
z>4
galaxies with Ar/O and Fe/O measurements prevent a reliable assessment of their general values.

Another important topic is the nature of surprisingly large dust reservoirs that have formed at high
z

(
Watson et al. 2015
;
Dayal et al. 2022
;
Witstok et al. 2022
;
Witstok et al. 2023a
;
Algera et al. 2023
;
Algera et al. 2024
;
Sun et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
.
Dust grains are thought to consist mainly of carbonaceous and silicate grains containing C and Si, respectively
(
Li & Draine 2001
, e.g.,)
, and most interstellar Fe is thought to be incorporated into silicate grains
(
Zhukovska et al. 2018
, e.g.)
.
Unlike carbonaceous grains, which arise mainly from low-mass AGB stars with 2–3
M_{\odot}
, silicates are produced by massive AGB stars (
>4
–5
M_{\odot}
) and CCSNe, indicating their much shorter enrichment delay time of
\lesssim 30
–40 Myr
(
Schneider & Maiolino 2024
, e.g.,)
, and thus, their dominance within
\lesssim 100
Myr
(
Hirashita & Murga 2020
, e.g.,)
.
Simulations have predicted that silicate grains tend to be larger at earlier epochs of star formation
(
Hou et al. 2017
, e.g.,)
, resulting in the flattening of the observed average dust attenuation curve at
z>4.5

(
Markov et al. 2025
;
McKinney et al. 2025
;
Shivaei et al. 2025
)
.
Although direct observational evidence such as the UV bump for carbonaceous grains has been observed out to high redshifts
(
Witstok et al. 2023b
;
Markov et al. 2025
;
Ormerod et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
, strong silicate features at restframe
\sim 10
–20
\mu
m
(
Mathis 1990
;
van Breemen et al. 2011
, e.g.,)
are difficult to observe at high-
z
(
z\sim 2
–10), because they fall beyond the wavelength coverage of the
JWST
Mid-Infrared Instrument (MIRI).
Alternatively, as for the Fe/O ratios at
z\sim 0

(
Izotov et al. 2006
;
Méndez-Delgado et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
, low Si/O ratios have been discussed to suggest dust depletion
(
Garnett et al. 1995
, e.g.,)
.
In addition, Si is a heavy
\alpha
-element produced by both CCSNe and Type-Ia SNe, suggesting a similar evolution to Ar enrichment.
Given that Ar is a noble gas with negligible dust depletion
(
Sofia & Jenkins 1998
)
, a significant deficit of Si compared to Ar might indicate Si depletion onto dust grains.

This paper aims at obtaining typical values of C/O, Ne/O, Ar/O, Si/O, and Fe/O ratios by stacking spectra of galaxies at
z>4
.
We carry out the stacking with different bins of galaxy properties to explore potential dependencies of the abundance ratios.
We present our data and sample in Section
2
, analysis in Section
3
, results and discussions in Section
4
, and conclusions in Section
5
.

We analyse the same spectroscopic dataset as
Isobe et al. 2025
, which use the
JWST
Advanced Deep Extragalactic Survey (JADES;
Eisenstein et al. 2023a
) data obtained with the NIRSpec micro-shutter array (MSA;
Jakobsen et al. 2022
;
Ferruit et al. 2022
) in the GOODS-S and GOODS-N fields.
The dataset contains the complete observations of the four programmes: PIDs 1180, 1181, 1210, and 3215
(
Bunker et al. 2024
;
D’Eugenio et al. 2025
;
Eisenstein et al. 2023b
)
and part of the two programmes: PIDs 1286 and 1287.
JADES provides medium-resolution grating and low-resolution prism observations with resolution
R\sim 1000
and
R\sim 100
(R1000 and R100, hereafter).
This paper uses the R1000 data to resolve important emission line pairs separated by a small wavelength (Section
3.2
for more details).
The R1000 data were obtained with three bands of F070LP-G140M, F170LP-G235M, and F290LP-G395M, which cover a wide wavelength range of 1–5.3
\mu
m.

A description of the JADES data reduction is available in the NIRSpec Data Release 3 (DR3) paper
(
D’Eugenio et al. 2025
)
and will also be documented in the forthcoming DR4 paper (Scholtz et al. in preparation), so only the main points are summarised here.
The NIRSpec Guaranteed Time Observations (GTO) Team reduced the observed data using the pipeline constructed by the European Space Agency (ESA) NIRSpec Science Operations Team
(
Ferruit et al. 2022
)
and the NIRSpec GTO Team
(
Alves de Oliveira et al. 2018
)
.
Instead of the standard 5-pixel (0.5”) full-microshutter extraction aperture
(
D’Eugenio et al. 2025
)
, we choose a 3-pixel box-car aperture to increase the signal-to-noise ratio (S/N) of the spectrum, particularly for compact sources, at shorter wavelengths with the narrower
JWST
point spread functions.
The 3-pixel box-car aperture extractions will be made available in the DR4 (Scholtz et al. in preparation).
The standard pipeline incorporates error propagation and employs variance-conserving resampling to conservatively account for correlated noise
(
Dorner et al. 2016
)
.

We construct our parent sample with the following criteria:

•

Spectra with flags of ‘6’, ‘7’, or ‘8’
(
D’Eugenio et al. 2025
)
, whose
z
values are reliably determined with multiple emission lines

•

Spectra with [O
iii
]
\lambda
5007 whose S/N ratio is higher than 3 based on the R1000 observations (Section
3.1
)

•

Spectra with
z=4
–7 to widely cover from O
iii
] to [Ar
iii
], where the extensive coverage provided by all three NIRSpec bands means that most of the rest-frame wavelength range is covered for each galaxy (see Section
3.2
)

•

Excluding broad-line active galactic nuclei (AGNs) based on H
\alpha
line broadening
(
Juodžbalis et al. 2025
)
and AGN candidates selected with emission line diagnostics
(
Scholtz et al. 2025b
)
.

These selection criteria provide 564 spectra.
Note that the last criterion is set to mitigate the possibility of anomalous chemical evolution originating from AGN activity.
In fact, the stack of similarly selected spectra (i.e., ‘Non-AGN’ stack in
Isobe et al. 2025
) does not exhibit N/O and N/C ratios as high as those of high-
z
NOEGs, but the broad-line AGN stack does.
However, it is worth noting that our sample does not fully exclude a hidden population of low-luminosity AGNs whose signatures are not identified individually
(
Geris et al. 2025
)
.

The majority of our sample has measurements of the following galaxy properties: stellar mass (
M_{*}
), star-formation rate (SFR), specific SFR (
\mathrm{sSFR}\equiv\mathrm{SFR}/M_{*}
), and UV continuum slope
\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}
, which allow us to investigate possible dependencies of chemical abundances on these properties (see Section
3.1
).
Most of our spectra have the corresponding NIRCam imaging from the JADES
(
Rieke et al. 2023
;
Eisenstein et al. 2023b
)
, the
JWST
Extragalactic Medium-band Survey (JEMS;
Williams et al. 2023
), and the First Reionisation Epoch Spectroscopic Complete Survey (FRESCO;
Oesch et al. 2023
), whose source detection and photometric measurements have been conducted by Robertson et al. (in preparation).
We follow the spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting routine of
Simmonds et al. 2025
, except that the redshift is fixed to the value from the NIRSpec data.
Using Kron photometry convolved to a common resolution with an error floor of 5% on each band, we derive
M_{*}
and SFR values using the SED fitting code
Prospector

(
Johnson et al. 2019
;
Johnson et al. 2021
)
, following the setup of
Tacchella et al. 2022
with a continuity star-formation history (SFH) from
Leja et al. 2019
, which comprises eight SFR bins.
The shortest time bin is 5 Myr, while the rest of the bins are equally spaced in log space depending on the redshift.
The ratios between adjacent SFR bins are allowed to vary following a Student’s t-distribution
(
Student 1908
)
with a width of 0.3.
In this work, we use the SFR values averaged over the recent 10 Myr (so-called SFR
10
).
We have 546 spectra with measurements of
M_{*}
and SFR
10
, which comprise the bulk of our 564 spectra.
The remaining 18 sources without
M_{*}
or SFR
10
do not have enough NIRCam photometric points to constrain the best-fit SED model.
We derive sSFR values from these
M_{*}
and SFR
10
values.
We use
\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}
values derived by
Saxena et al. 2024
from the restframe 1340–2700 Å continuum of the JADES R100 spectra, which requires
S/N>3
in the restframe UV continuum.
We have 350 of our 564 spectra that satisfy the criterion and have the
\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}
values.
Note that
Saxena et al. 2024
have reported sources at
z>5.5
, while their
\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}
measurements exist at
z>4
, which we use in this paper.
It should also be mentioned that the
S/N

M_{*}
galaxies.

3.1
Spectral stacking in different galaxy property bins

We produce stacked spectra of the R1000 data in the same manner as
Isobe et al. 2025
and
Geris et al. 2025
, as summarised below.
We combine spectra of different NIRSpec bands for a given source.
When the wavelengths of two NIRSpec bands overlap, we adopt the band with the longer wavelength, as it is generally more sensitive
(
Ferruit et al. 2022
)
.
Conducting emission line fitting for individual spectra, we obtain redshifts and [O
iii
]
\lambda
5007 fluxes.
This line fitting provides redshifts, [O
iii
]
\lambda
5007 fluxes, and S/N ratios, which are used for the sample selection (Section
2
).
We shift the individual spectra to the restframe based on the
z
values, with resampling onto a common wavelength grid.
We set a spectral pixel size to half the full-width half maximum of the NIRSpec line-spread function (LSF) based on point-like sources derived by
de Graaff et al. 2024
.
This LSF has a spectral resolution typically twice as high as the nominal resolution assumed for uniformly illuminated shutters.
We resample the spectra using
spectres

(
Carnall 2017
)
, which propagates the errors associated with the individual spectra.

Finally, we renormalise the resampled spectra by their [O
iii
]
\lambda
5007 fluxes and construct the stacked spectrum by taking the median of the normalised spectra at each wavelength of the common wavelength grid, without applying any weighting.
This forms the basis of all results presented in this paper.
This stacking approach reduces the impact of a few bright outliers, allowing the resulting spectrum to more faithfully reflect the typical properties of the individual galaxies.
As the uncertainty of the median cannot generally be derived analytically, we perform Monte Carlo simulations to estimate errors of the median stacked spectra.
We generate 1000 stacked spectra by randomly perturbing the individual spectra according to their errors, assuming a normal distribution, and then compute the standard deviation at each wavelength.
In the case of “mean” stacking, we have verified that this method reproduces analytic error propagation of the individual spectra
(
Isobe et al. 2025
, also)
.
We do not use bootstrap errors for emission lines, as they tend to overestimate the flux variance by incorporating significant variations in the underlying continuum emission across different sources.

Following the method presented above, we conduct spectral stacking for all of our 564 spectra, which is referred to as the “all-sources stack” hereafter, shown in Fig.
1
.
In addition, we divide our parent sample into thirds based on
M_{*}
, SFR, sSFR, and
\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}
values (Section
2
).
We define the low-value stack as comprising sources with the given property below the 33rd percentile, the mid-value stack as those between the 33rd and 67th percentiles, and the high-value stack as those above the 67th percentile.
This is made for statistical purposes only to ensure enough sources in each bin.
When referring to individual properties
M_{*}
, SFR, and sSFR, we replace ‘value’ with the corresponding property (e.g., low-
M_{*}
stack).
However, we specially call the low-, mid-, and high-value stacks of
\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}
as blue-, mid-, red-
\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}
stacks, respectively, because this terminology is widely used and intuitively understood.
The different stacks are shown in Fig.
9
, while the number of stacked spectra and median properties of each stack are listed in Table
1
.
We stress that more than 100 spectra are used for every stack.

3.2
Emission line measurements

We model our stacked spectra in the wide wavelength range of
\simeq 1500
–7300 Å, which is set to be covered by more than 70% of the spectra in each stack.
We assume that each emission line profile has a velocity width
\sigma=\sqrt{\sigma_{\mathrm{int}}^{2}+\sigma_{\mathrm{LSF}}^{2}}
, where
\sigma_{\mathrm{int}}
is an intrinsic velocity width common to all emission lines, and
\sigma_{\mathrm{LSF}}
is the velocity width of
de Graaff et al. 2024
’s LSF.
Given a potential Balmer break or jump, continua with
\lambda<3640
and
>3820
Å are modelled with independent power-law functions, which are connected with a linear function within
3640\leq\lambda\leq 3820
Å.
We perform
\chi^{2}
fitting to our stacked spectra.
We define a line with
S/N>3
as a detection, and adopt
3\sigma
upper limits for undetected lines.
The best-fit model of the all-sources stack is shown in Figure
2
as an example.

Table
2
summarises the emission line fluxes used to measure nebular properties in Section
3.3
.
Every stack has a detection of [O
iii
]
\lambda
4363, which provides a constraint on the electron temperature
T_{\mathrm{e}}
, ensuring that chemical abundances can be measured with the direct-
T_{\mathrm{e}}
method (e.g.,
Peimbert 1967
; Section
3.3
).
We find [Ne
iii
] and C
iii
] detections in all our stacks, many of which have [Ar
iii
] detections.

Interestingly, five of our stacks (the all-sources and all mid-value stacks) have Si
iii
]
\lambda
1892 detections, which are difficult to observe even at
z\sim 0

(
Berg et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
.
This allows us to obtain Si/O ratios based on the direct-
T_{\mathrm{e}}
method at
z>4
.
We note that we do not use Si
iii
]
\lambda
1883 fluxes to measure Si/O ratios due to the nearby absorption feature at
\sim 1881
Å seen in most of our stacks.
Additionally, we detect the [Fe
iii
] line in two of our stacks (the high-sSFR and blue-
\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}
stacks) as shown in Figure
3
, which is widely used to determine Fe/O ratios at
z\sim 0

(
Izotov et al. 2006
;
Méndez-Delgado et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
.

3.3
Nebular properties

We obtain the nebular colour excess
E(B-V)
, electron temperature of [O
iii
] (
T_{\mathrm{e}}
[O
iii
]), and electron density of [S
ii
] (
n_{\mathrm{e}}
[S
ii
]) values of our stacks.
We use PyNeb
(
Luridiana et al. 2015
)
to obtain the
E(B-V)
value from the combination of observed H
\beta
/H
\alpha
, H
\gamma
/H
\alpha
, and H
\gamma
/H
\beta
ratios by assuming Case B recombination, the
T_{\mathrm{e}}
[O
iii
] value from the [O
iii
]
\lambda
4363/[O
iii
]
\lambda
5007 ratio, and the
n_{\mathrm{e}}
[S
ii
] value from the [S
ii
]
\lambda
6716/[S
ii
]
\lambda
6731 ratio.
Appendix
C
reports the atomic data that we use in this paper.
We calculate
E(B-V)
,
T_{\mathrm{e}}
[O
iii
], and
n_{\mathrm{e}}
[S
ii
] values iteratively so that their values are consistent with each other
(
Isobe et al. 2022
)
.
Note that, when we adopt the attenuation curve of
Calzetti et al. 2000
, our stacks have
E(B-V)=0.15
–0.25, which corresponds to the
V
-band attenuation
A_{V}=0.35
–0.58.

Shivaei et al. 2025
have recently reported that the attenuation curve of galaxies with attenuation in the range of
0.1<A_{V}<0.6
at
z=3
–7 lies between the attenuation curve of
Calzetti et al. 2000
and the extinction curve of the Small Magellanic Cloud (SMC;
Gordon et al. 2003
), which suggests that either of the curves is applicable for our stacks.
We adopt the
Calzetti et al. 2000
’s curve in this paper, as it provides abundance ratios that are more conservative for our conclusions than those of the SMC, as mentioned later in this section.
We derive
n_{\mathrm{e}}
[S
ii
] in the range
\log(n_{\mathrm{e}}[\text{{S\,ii}}]/\mathrm{cm^{-3}})=0.6
–4.7.
We estimate the errors of these properties using the 1
\sigma
errors of the emission line ratios.
With the obtained
E(B-V)
value and

We estimate element abundance ratios by calculating ionisation correction factors (ICFs).

We use Cloudy

, e.g.,)

(

Here, we check the robustness of our results under different assumptions and methodologies.
First, we create stacked spectra of galaxies whose continuum is modelled with the same function as for our stacks (see Section

We confirm that this analysis changes

Second, we test the extinction curve of the Small Magellanic Cloud

We find that the assumption of the SMC extinction curve changes

Third, we test the empirical

Fourth, we test AGN photoionisation models

The AGN models can impact

Finally, we compute Cloudy photoionisation models with silicate dust depletion.

In the second bottom rows of Figs.

Although it is true that dominant CCSN yields can decrease the Si/O ratio to make the plateau in the low metallicity regime of the K20 model, our Si/O ratios are even lower than the plateau, suggesting that a chemically young gas composition alone cannot explain our low Si/O ratios.
Instead, Fig.
7
illustrates that dust depletion can significantly decrease the gas-phase Si/O ratio.
For example, the dashdot curve shows dust depletion of the LMC with the H column density of
N_{\mathrm{H}}=10^{21}

) under the assumption of the empirical

(

.
We find that the K20 model with the LMC dust depletion can explain the low Si/O ratio of the all-sources stack.
Interestingly, all our stacks have
E(B-V)=0.1
–0.2 (Table

Our findings closely resemble those reported at intermediate

The low Si/O ratios of our stacks suggest Si depletion onto dust grains in general star-forming galaxies at
z=4
–7.
Given a CCSN-dominated gas composition suggested in Section

,

–7 star-forming galaxies may generally undergo a rapid creation of silicate dust by CCSNe and subsequent rapid growth in the ISM
(

This scenario is consistent with the flattening of the average dust attenuation curve observed at
z>4.5

(
Markov et al. 2025
)
.
Our stacks have at most
\log(\mathrm{sSFR/Gyr^{-1}})=1.35

Myr, suggesting enough time for CCSNe to produce dust
(
Schneider & Maiolino 2024

.
On the other hand, RXCJ2248 has

Myr
(
Topping et al. 2024
)
, which might cause negligible attenuation and depletion of dust.

It is noteworthy that the dust-to-metal mass ratios (D/M) of

\sim 0.1\,Z_{\odot}

(

Z\sim 0.5\,Z_{\odot}

(
Russell & Dopita 1992
, e.g.,)
.
This suggests that typical high-

Supporting this, observations of
z\sim 2
–4 damped Lyman-

De Cia et al. 2016

show nearly constant D/M ratios across

–0.5

At high redshift, certain mechanisms may help maintain high D/M ratios even at low metallicities, such as high dust condensation efficiencies or accretion timescales that are insensitive to metallicity

;
McKinnon et al. 2016

What is the origin of the Fe/O enhancement?

The Fe/O enhancement is observed in our high sSFR stack with
\log(\mathrm{sSFR/Gyr^{-1}})=1.35
and blue
\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}
stack with
\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}=-2.49
.
The high sSFR value is equivalent to
t_{\mathrm{dbl}}=45
Myr, which implies the onset of the starburst
(
Mihos & Hernquist 1994
;
Feulner et al. 2005
;
Knapen & James 2009
, e.g.,)
.
In particular, the blue
\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}
value is accompanied by
E(B-V)=0.16
, suggesting a very blue intrinsic
\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}
of
\sim-2.7
.
This blue
\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}
can be explained by models with
\lesssim 10
–20 Myr regardless of whether the nebular continuum is taken into account
(
Cullen et al. 2024
;
Saxena et al. 2024
)
.
Such a young starburst is likely related to the Fe/O enhancement.

One possible scenario is to include exotic massive SNe such as PISNe introduced to explain Fe/O enhancements (see Section
1
).

Vanni et al. 2024
have reported that supersolar Fe/O gas can be ejected not only from PISNe but also from other PopIII SNe.
The PopIII scenario obviously requires a very young, metal-poor starburst because normal Population II (PopII) stars form immediately after the metallicity exceeds
\sim 10^{-4.5}
–10
-3

Z_{\odot}

(
Bromm & Loeb 2003
;
de Bennassuti et al. 2017
, e.g.,)
, which is simulated to take
\sim 10
Myr after the first PopIII stars form
(
Rusta et al. 2025
)
.
Note that the high-sSFR stack and blue-
\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}
stack have higher log([O
iii
]
\lambda
5007/H
\beta)=0.74
and lower log(He
ii
\lambda
1640/H
\beta)=(-0.61)
–(
-0.67
) (attenuation corrected) than
those simulated for galaxies dominated by PopIII stars (e.g.,
>50
% in
M_{*}
;
Rusta et al. 2025
).

Fig.
8
shows the models of Fe/O and Si/O abundances presented by
Vanni et al. 2024
and Ar/O by Salvadori et al. (in preparation), which are predicted for galaxies solely imprinted by PopIII SNe with different progenitor masses (10–100
M_{\odot}
) and exploding with a variety of energies: PopIII hypernova (HN), CCSN, and faint SN are assumed to have explosion energies of (5–10)
\times 10^{51}
, (0.9–1.5)
\times 10^{51}
, and (0.3–0.6)
\times 10^{51}

Vanni et al. 2023
for details).
These models are computed by assuming PopIII yields of
Heger & Woosley 2010

We find that, although the Fe/O-Ar/O relations of the PopIII models overlap with those of the high-sSFR stack within its 1
\sigma
error, the models predict significantly higher Si/O ratios than those of our stacks at a given Fe/O.

Another possible scenario would be Type-Ia SNe.
Although the K20 model predicts an increase in Fe/O at a higher metallicity of
12+\log(\mathrm{O/H})\gtrsim 8.4
, we can increase Fe/O at a lower metallicity (or a shorter timescale) in young starbursts by assuming intermittent bursts associated with “rejuvenation” that recycles gas of past star formation
(
McClymont et al. 2025b
, e.g.,)
or efficient coolants to suppress massive star formation, which results in a top-light IMF
(
Nakane et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
.
However, Fig.
8
illustrates that the K20 model including the ejecta of Type-Ia SNe cannot reproduce the high Fe/O, low Si/O, and low Ar/O ratios observed in the high-sSFR stack and blue-
\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}
stack simultaneously, which is because Type-Ia SNe enhance not only Fe/O but also Ar/O and Si/O ratios
(
Iwamoto et al. 1999
, e.g.,)
.

It is worth mentioning that, to explain similar abundance patterns reported in
z\sim 0
extremely metal-poor galaxies
(
Kojima et al. 2021
, e.g.,)
,
Watanabe et al. 2024
have constructed chemical evolution models of CCSNe whose inner materials are mixed, some of which accrete to the remnant (so-called mixing-and-fallback process; e.g.,
Umeda & Nomoto 2002
;
Umeda & Nomoto 2003
).
CCSNe that undergo the mixing-and-fallback process (CCSNe-MF, hereafter) can decrease heavy
\alpha
-elements, which are simply ejected by the normal CCSN model.

Watanabe et al. 2024
assume stars with 9–40
M_{\odot}
to follow
Kroupa 2001
’s IMF and to evolve into CCSNe.
The CCSN-MF yields are calculated based on the explosive nucleosynthesis code of
Tominaga et al. 2007
.

Watanabe et al. 2024
adopt
Ishigaki et al. 2018
’s parametrisation to express an outer boundary of mixing mass (
M_{\mathrm{mix}}
) as
M_{\mathrm{mix}}=M_{\mathrm{cut}}+x(M_{\mathrm{CO}}-M_{\mathrm{cut}})
, where
M_{\mathrm{cut}}
is the inner boundary of mixing mass,
M_{\mathrm{CO}}
is the mass of the CO core, and
x
is the mixing region factor.

Watanabe et al. 2024
mix ejecta of the CCSNe-MF and Type-Ia SNe
(
Iwamoto et al. 1999
)
, which we refer to as the CCSN-MF+Type-Ia SN model hereafter.

Fig.
8
shows the CCSN-MF+Type-Ia SN model with
x=0.2
and metallicity
Z=0.004
, which predicts the highest Fe/O ratio of the models shown in
Watanabe et al. 2024
at a given Ar/O ratio.
In addition, we plot Si/O ratios calculated with the same model.
We find that the CCSN-MF+Type-Ia SN model is closer to the observed ratios than the K20 model, which reaches the edge of the lower error bar of the observed Fe/O ratio of the high-sSFR stack.
However, the CCSN-MF+Type-Ia SN model predicts a significantly higher Si/O ratio than that of our stacks at a given Fe/O.

Here, we discuss the possibility of dust depletion and destruction.
As shown by the grey arrows in Fig.
8
, the assumption of the LMC dust depletion would decrease the Fe/O ratios of the models more than the Si/O ratios, rendering these models even less consistent with the abundance ratios of these stacks.
It is worth noting that
Curti et al. 2025a
report that a WR galaxy at
z\sim 2
has a higher gas-phase Fe/O ratio than
z\sim 0
galaxies at a given metallicity, which may point to dust displacement and sublimation in the presence of intense, localised star formation.
Although neither the high-sSFR stack nor the blue-
\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}
stack shows prominent WR features (Fig.
9
), the timescale for metal accretion onto dust grains in metal-poor environments (of order several 100 Myr; e.g.,
Asano et al. 2013
) may exceed the timescale over which WR features fade.
In addition, a recent computational study
(
Hansson et al. 2025
)
report that
the median binding energy of Si (14.8 eV) on the surface of silicates exceeds that of Fe (6.0 eV), which may imply that Si is more readily adsorbed and relatively more resistant to sublimation.
However, it remains unclear whether conditions within H
ii
regions could achieve a temperature range in which Fe sublimates while Si remains largely unaffected.
The temperature may reach a few thousand K, as inferred from the sublimation temperature of silicates, with the exact value depending on the assumed grain size and lifetime
(
Hansson et al. 2025
)
.
This question would benefit from dedicated quantitative simulations for the H
ii
region with various solid phases of iron.

It is difficult to conclude that the currently accessible models satisfactorily explain the high Fe/O and low Si/O ratios observed in the high-sSFR stack and blue-
\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}
stack.
However, the discussions above highlight the importance of further investigating supernova yields under varying progenitor characteristics and explosion scenarios, as well as considering alternative mechanisms of dust depletion and destruction.

5
Conclusions

We present C/O, Ne/O, Ar/O, Si/O, and Fe/O ratios of stacked spectra using 564 sources at
z=4
–7 with the
JWST
/NIRSpec R1000 data from the JADES.
We perform spectral stacking with galaxy property bins of
M_{*}
, SFR, sSFR, and
\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}
.
The large number of high-quality spectra enables us to detect the weak emission lines of Si
iii
]
\lambda
1892 and [Fe
iii
] in some of our stacks.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to measure Si/O and Fe/O ratios in the general galaxy population at
z>4
.
In summary, the all-sources stack:

•

exhibits low C/O, moderate Ne/O, and low Ar/O ratios compared to the individual galaxies at low
z
(
\sim 0
) and high
z
(
>4
) for a similarly low
12+\log(\mathrm{O/H})
.
These ratios are in very good agreement with those of the K20 chemical evolution model, which is dominated by CCSN yields in the low-metallicity regime.
These findings suggest that general star-forming galaxies at
z=4
–7 have a chemically young gas composition with dominant CCSN yields.

•

has a non-zero
E(B-V)=0.18
and a Si/O ratio of
\log(\mathrm{Si/O})=-1.81
, which is lower than that of the
z\sim 0
galaxies, the MW stars, and the K20 model without dust depletion, while Si/O ratios of GN-z11 and RXCJ2248 with
E(B-V)=0
at high
z
are comparable to the K20 model.
These findings suggest Si depletion on dust grains, which may be rapidly created by CCSNe.

•

\sim 0.4

However, the high-sSFR stack and blue-

The high Fe/O ratios are comparable to those of
z>4

\beta_{\mathrm{UV}}

These results suggest selective Fe/O enhancement at the very early epoch of star formation.
It is currently difficult to explain the observed abundance patterns satisfactorily, which may hint at physical processes not fully captured.
