Young measure relaxation gaps for controllable systems with smooth state constraints
Nicolas Augier, Milan Korda, Rodolfo Rios-Zertuche

Abstract
In this article, we tackle the problem of the existence of a gap corresponding to Young measure relaxations for state-constrained optimal control problems. We provide a counterexample proving that a gap may occur in a very regular setting, namely for a smooth controllable system state-constrained to the closed unit ball, provided that the Lagrangian density (i.e., the running cost) is non-convex in the control variables. The example is constructed in the setting of sub-Riemannian geometry with the core ingredient being an unusual admissible curve that exhibits a certain form of resistance to state-constrained approximation. Specifically, this curve cannot be approximated by neighboring admissible curves while obeying the state constraint due to the intricate nature of the dynamics near the boundary of the constraint set. This example therefore demonstrates the impossibility of Filippov–Wazewski type approximation in the presence of state constraints. Our example also presents an occupation measure relaxation gap.

Introduction

1.1
Main question

A problem of theoretical and practical interest is:

When is
M_{\mathrm{c}}=M_{\mathrm{y}}
?

(1)

Here,
M_{\mathrm{c}}
denotes the minimal cost of an optimal control problem, with the infimum taken over measurable controls, and
M_{\mathrm{y}}
denotes its relaxation using Young measures; we will define these shortly.

As we shall see, the Young measure relaxation is equivalent to the convexification of the associated differential inclusion (see Lemma
31
).
Understanding when state constraints impede the approximation, by admissible curves in the interior, of a trajectory of the convexified differential inclusion that lies on the boundary, is a question of great theoretical importance.
It has been tackled in
[
6
,
5
,
17
,
4
]
providing many precise estimates and sufficiency conditions for equality (
1
) to hold.
This question is important for motion planning of a multitude of state-constrained control systems, including even one of the most natural controllable systems, namely, the sub-Riemannian ones with linear control dependence.

On the more applied side, there exist algorithms based on moment-sums-of-squares hierarchies aiming to compute
M_{\mathrm{c}}
through the so-called occupation measure relaxation, whose optimal value coincides with
M_{\mathrm{y}}
, and answering question (
1
) would amount to understanding their applicability, as we explain at the end of this section.
In our paper
[
4
]
, we give sufficient conditions for the absence of occupation measure relaxation gaps, which are shown to be equivalent to equality (
1
).
In the present work, we give examples of systems that exhibit such a relaxation gap in circumstances that otherwise seem very natural.
We will describe these examples in Section
1.2
.

To clarify the meaning of question (
1
) and to present our findings effectively, we will begin by outlining the problems of interest, along with the necessary notations and terminology.
Throughout, “smooth” will mean
C^{\infty}
.

Let
\Omega
be a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and choose an initial point

, a target set

a map that will define the controlled vector field, and

a function giving the final cost.

The original (classical)
Bolza problem
that we want to consider and eventually relax is that of finding the minimal cost
M_{\mathrm{c}}=M_{\mathrm{c}}^{L,g,U}({\Omega},X)
given by

\displaystyle M_{\mathrm{c}}=

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[c]{\underset{\displaystyle u}{\mathrm{inf}}}\quad\int_{0}^{T}L(t,\gamma(t),u(t))\,dt+g(\gamma(T))\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle

(2)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[c]{{\mathrm{subject~to}}}\quad

\displaystyle u\colon[0,T]\to U\;\text{measurable},\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle

\displaystyle\gamma(t)={\mathbf{x}_{0}}+\int_{0}^{t}f(s,\gamma(s),u(s))\,ds\in\Omega,\;t\in[0,T],\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle

\displaystyle\gamma(T)\in X.

Here we are minimizing the sum of the integral of

When

.
It is well known that minimizers may fail to exist for the problem (

We will sometimes abbreviate the notation

by omitting the parameters that are clear from the context; for example, if it were clear that we were working with

A
Young measure
is a family
(\nu_{t})_{t\in I}
of Borel probability measures on the set of controls
U
indexed by a parameter

in an interval

and such that, for every Borel subset

,

is measurable, and for every

and every

, the function
u\mapsto f(t,x,u)
is

integrable, that is,

\int_{U}\|f(t,x,u)\|d\nu_{t}(u)<+\infty.

The relaxation we consider is the
Young measure relaxation

M_{\mathrm{y}}=M_{\mathrm{y}}^{L,g,U}({\Omega},X)
, namely,

\displaystyle M_{\mathrm{y}}=

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[c]{\underset{\displaystyle(\nu_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}}{\mathrm{inf}}}\quad\int_{0}^{T}\int_{U}L(t,\gamma(t),u)\,d\nu_{t}(u)\,dt+g(\gamma(T))\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle

(3)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[c]{{\mathrm{subject~to}}}\quad

\displaystyle(\nu_{t})_{t}\text{ a Young measure},\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle

\displaystyle\gamma(t)={\mathbf{x}_{0}}+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U}f(s,\gamma(s),u)\,d\nu_{s}(u)\,ds\in\Omega,\quad t\in[0,T],\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle

\displaystyle\gamma(T)\in X.

Here the contenders are the Young measures
(\nu_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
with the additional constraint that they induce admissible integral curves
\gamma
contained in
\Omega
, starting at
{\mathbf{x}_{0}}
and ending in
X
. Unlike the controls
u
contending in problem (
2
), the Young measures
(\nu_{t})_{t}
in (
3
) naturally encode highly oscillatory behavior. An important motivation to use relaxed controls is that minimizers for this problem are known to exist under relatively mild conditions, such as bounds of the form
c(|u|^{p}-1)\leq L(t,x,u)\leq C(|u|^{p}+1)
with
c,C>0
and

[
16
, Th. 4.6]
, which are trivially verified if

is compact and

is continuous; see also
[

,

,

]
for other existence results.
Since a feasible control

, introducing the Young measure relaxation makes the infimum
M_{\mathrm{y}}\leq M_{\mathrm{c}}
. However, it is possible to have a positive difference in some situations; this difference
M_{\mathrm{c}}-M_{\mathrm{y}}
is known as a

.

We will be interested in identifying some situations where
M_{\mathrm{c}}>M_{\mathrm{y}}
, that is, where the gap
M_{\mathrm{c}}-M_{\mathrm{y}}
is strictly positive. It was predicted in
[
14
]
that in this case the minimizers would be abnormal trajectories, in the terminology usually associated with the Pontryagin Maximum Principle. There are two known mechanisms for the appearance of such gaps:

•

Reachability. Sometimes the target set is only reachable using Young measures and there are no curves
\gamma
induced by ordinary controls
u
joining the initial point to the target set, so we have
M_{\mathrm{y}}<+\infty=M_{\mathrm{c}}
, and the minimizing Young measure is an abnormal trajectory. Examples of this type were found in
[
19
,
14
]
.

•

Failure of the constrained Filippov–Wažewski approximation. In this case, the state constraints may cause the appearance of minimizing Young measures that cannot be approximated by curves as in the Filippov–Wažewski theorem
[
6
]
.

To the best of our knowledge, no examples of this type have been found to date, except in rather contrived settings (see e.g.
[
11
, §1]
). We will give examples of this type in a very regular setting, where the occurrence of the relaxation gap may come as a surprise.

Occupation measure relaxation.

A motivation to study the Young measure relaxation (
3
) is that its optimal cost is equal to the optimal cost of another relaxation, called
occupation measure relaxation

M_{\mathrm{o}}
; in other words, we have
M_{\mathrm{o}}=M_{\mathrm{y}}
(see
[
4
, Lem. 1]
for details). This relaxation reads as follows:

\displaystyle\hskip-85.35826ptM_{\mathrm{o}}=

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[c]{\underset{\displaystyle\mu,\mu_{\partial}}{\mathrm{inf}}}\quad\int_{[0,T]\times\Omega\times U}L(t,x,u)\,d\mu(t,x,u)+\int_{\Omega}g(x)d\mu_{\partial}(x)\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle

(4)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[c]{{\mathrm{subject~to}}}\quad

\displaystyle\mu\text{ a positive Borel measure on }[0,T]\times\Omega\times U,\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle

\displaystyle\mu_{\partial}\text{ a positive Borel measure on }X,\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle

\displaystyle\int_{[0,T]\times\Omega\times U}\|f(t,x,u)\|d\mu+\int_{X}\|x\|\,d\mu_{\partial}(x)<+\infty,\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle

\displaystyle\text{and, for all $\phi\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)$},\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle

\displaystyle\int_{[0,T]\times\Omega\times U}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}\cdot f(t,x,u)\,d\mu+\phi(0,{\mathbf{x}_{0}})-\int_{U}\phi(T,x)d\mu_{\partial}(x)=0.

The convexity properties of the set of relaxed occupation measures (i.e., measures feasible in (
4

2
) into a linear program for which there exist computationally tractable solution schemes
[
3
,
10
,
12
]
. These schemes rely on the the so-called moment-sums of squares hierarchies
[
12
]
, which are applicable when all the data of the problem are semi-algebraic. The main focus of this paper is thus of great significance to the effectiveness of the algorithms described in
[
12
,
10
]
, which compute
M_{\mathrm{o}}
while aiming to find
M_{\mathrm{c}}
.
Our counterexample shows that the applicability of algorithms relying on this relaxation fails in some cases.

1.2
Main contributions

In this paper we find several instances of gaps
M_{\mathrm{c}}-M_{\mathrm{y}}>0
arising from failures of the Filippov-Wažewski approximation that arise in highly regular settings and are caused solely by the interaction of the dynamics and the state constraint set.

As we explained above, to the best of our knowledge, examples of this type were only known in situations that seemed quite forced and artificial.

While our main result is the example described below in Section
1.2.1
, we also include, for completeness, several variations whose relevance we will explain together with their statements below. Finally, we present also a statement formalizing what we mean by a failure of the Filippov-Wažewski approximation.

1.2.1
A gap in a Lagrange problem

We expose here a new gap phenomenon which is entirely caused by the interplay of the dynamics with the state constraint
\Omega
, despite the considered control systems enjoy nice controllability properties.

We are interested in building a family of examples exhibiting a relaxation gap on circumstances that seem natural:

P1.

The controlled vector fields are of the form
f(t,x,u)=u_{1}f_{1}(x)+u_{2}f_{2}(x)
, where the couple of vector fields
(f_{1},f_{2})
is smooth and Lie bracket generating, i.e.,
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
, together with the nested Lie brackets

[f_{1},f_{2}],\quad[f_{1},[f_{1},f_{2}]],\quad[f_{1},[f_{1},[f_{1},f_{2}]]],\quad\dots,\quad\underbrace{[f_{1},[f_{1},[f_{1},\dots,[f_{1}}_{d-2},f_{2}]]\dots],\vskip-8.5359pt

span
\mathbb{R}^{d}
at every point.
The associated sub-Riemannian structure induced by
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
is thus
C^{\infty}
, free, equi-regular, of rank 2; its step is equal to
d-2
and its growth vector is
(2,1,1,\dots,1)
(see
[
1
]
for the definitions).

P2.

The state constraint
\overline{\Omega}
is the closure of an open ball
\Omega=B(0,1)
and the initial point is in its interior,
{\mathbf{x}_{0}}\in\Omega
.

P3.

There is an open set
V
such that the target set can be taken to be any nonempty subset
X\subset V\cap\overline{\Omega}
.

(Gaps may occur in a Lagrange problem)
.

Let
\Omega
be the ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

,

, and

be any subset of

containing

. There is a Lagrangian density

, a controlled vector field

induced by a rank two sub-Riemannian structure, a point

–

),

\displaystyle M_{\mathrm{c}}(\overline{\Omega},X)>M_{\mathrm{y}}(\overline{\Omega},X),

\displaystyle M_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathbb{R}^{d},X)=M_{\mathrm{y}}(\mathbb{R}^{d},X).

This result will be proven in Section
4
, where it is restated as Theorem

Note that the Lagrangian density

is necessarily non-convex in the control variable, as it would otherwise contradict

1.2.4
Filippov–Wažewski breakdown phenomenon at the boundary

Denote by

1.3
Ideas and organization of the paper

Idea of the mechanism.

In order to obtain a gap, we look for a situation in which the minimizer cannot be approximated well enough by nearby admissible curves. To achieve this in the sub-Riemannian setting, our strategy is to start with a minimizer
\eta
that simply follows one of the vector fields generating the sub-Riemannian structure, namely,
\eta^{\prime}(t)=f_{1}(\eta(t))=(1,0,\dots,0)
;
\eta
is thus just a straight line. Then construct
\Omega
to be diffeomorphic to a ball while spiraling around
\eta
in a way that the sub-Riemannian velocity distribution at
\eta
remains tangent to the boundary of the domain
\Omega
(see Figure
2
).
This way, we cut away part of the space that would be necessary for admissible curves to approximate
\eta
both in position and velocity (i.e., with similar controls and similar cost), and instead the spiral interacts with the sub-Riemannian structure to force admissible curves to become more oscillatory the closer they get to
\eta
and their cost increases.
Also, while
\eta
is an admissible curve, since the running cost has four wells in the control variable located at the points
(\pm 1,\pm 1)
, the cost of
\eta
as a curve with control
u=(1,0)
is larger than the cost of the Young measure
\nu_{t}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\delta_{(1,1)}+\delta_{(1,-1)})
supported at two controls
(1,\pm 1)
inducing speeds
f_{1}\pm f_{2}
whose average is
\eta^{\prime}=f_{1}
.

Overview of the proofs and organization of the paper.

To prove Theorem
1
, we first describe the geometric objects involved in Section
2
. Then, in Section
3
we prove an important auxiliary result, Theorem
11
, that is a version of Theorem
1
but with a non-smooth running cost. This is eventually replaced by a smooth running cost in Section
4
, where the proof of Theorem
1
(restated as Theorem
24
) is finished.

Theorem
3
is deduced from Theorem
24
(restated as Theorem
25
) in Section
5
using a well-known extension technique that we review in Section
A.1
that turns any Bolza problem into a Mayer problem. We also use the equivalence (reviewed in Section
A.2
) between the Young measure relaxation and the relaxation consisting in the convexification of the differential inclusion.

In Section
6
we deduce Theorems
2
and
4
(restated as Theorem
26
); here the trick consists of leveraging a terminal cost that, upon optimizing, enforces the condition that the minimizers end in
X
.

The failure of the Filippov-Wažewski approximation is discussed in Section
7
, where Theorem
5
is rephrased as Corollary
29
.

3.2
Results needed for the proof of Theorem
11

Section
3.2.1
collects the technical lemmas needed to deduce equations (
19
) and (
20
), which are significantly easier than (
21
), to which the rest of the section is devoted.

The setting we describe in Section
2
features a domain
\Omega
that spirals around the straight curve
\eta
that turns out to be the integral curve of the relaxed minimizer.
The sub-Riemannian structure makes it very hard to traverse the spiral through any curve other than
\eta
. A curve
\gamma
that almost never coincides with
\eta
must go around the spiral, and in Sections
3.2.2
and
3.2.3
we show that this must make its cost significantly larger than that of
\eta
, even as
\eta
and
\gamma
may be arbitrarily close to each other.

In Section
3.2.3
, most of the work is devoted to ensuring that we can apply a result in topology known as the Dog-on-a-Leash Theorem
[
7
, Th. 3.11]
, which we use to formalize the intuitive notion that, if a curve
\gamma
traverses a domain that spirals
k
times around
\eta
, then the curve necessarily rotates at least
k
times around
\eta
. Once we have established that fact, we also obtain some estimates for the cost by applying the Uniform Ball-Box Theorem
[
8
]
. Qualitatively, the state constraint
\Omega
forces the approximating trajectories
\gamma
to achieve rotations in the
(x_{d-1},x_{d})
-plane along the spiral, and provides a fixed lower bound for the corresponding winding number given in Lemma
21
. Since the latter plane is spanned by the directions given by Lie brackets of highest orders (also known as the nonholonomic directions), it turns out that the cost of turning becomes too expensive in order to have a suitable approximation of the curve
\eta
.

In Section
3.2.4
we prove some a priori estimates that show that any curve that is almost a minimizer must be very close to
\eta
. We conclude the proof of (
21
) in Section
3.2.5
.

3.2.2
The ball-box theorem and some consequences

The uniform ball-box theorem.

We state the version of the theorem that we will use, which is a simplified version of what the reader will find in
[
8
]
.

Let
\mathbb{R}^{d}
be endowed with a sub-Riemannian structure induced by analytic vector fields
X_{1},\dots,X_{m}
with commutators
[X_{i},X_{j}]\coloneqq X_{i}X_{j}-X_{j}X_{i}
.

For a multiindex
I=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
with entries
1\leq i_{j}\leq m
, we set
|I|=i_{1}+i_{2}+\dots+i_{k}
. We also let

[X_{I}]=[X_{i_{1}},[X_{i_{2}},\dots[X_{i_{k-1}},X_{i_{k}}]]\dots],

be the corresponding commutator of order
k
; when
k=1
, this means that
[X_{(i)}]=X_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,m
. We assume that the vector fields
\{[X_{I}]:|I|\leq n\}
generate the entire space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
at every point
(i.e., they satisfy the Chow condition).

For each
1\leq k\leq n
, let
\mathcal{L}^{k}(p)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the subspace spanned by
\{[X_{I}]:|I|\leq k\}
. Assume that all points
p\in M
are regular in the sense that the vector spaces
\mathcal{L}^{k}(p)
have constant dimension
\dim\mathcal{L}^{k}(p)
globally for all
p
; we will thus use the shorthand
\dim\mathcal{L}^{k}
instead of
\dim\mathcal{L}^{k}(p)
.

The family
\{[X_{I_{1}}],\dots,[X_{I_{n}}]\}
of commutators is said to be a
minimal basis
if, at each
p
, the vectors
[X_{I_{1}}](p),\dots,[X_{I_{n}}](p)
generate
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and if
|I_{j}|=s
whenever
\dim\mathcal{L}^{s-1}<j\leq\dim\mathcal{L}^{s}
(setting
\dim\mathcal{L}^{0}=0
).

With a fixed minimal basis
\{[X_{I_{1}}],\dots,[X_{I_{n}}]\}
, let
\operatorname{Box}(p,\rho)
the set of all points
q
that can be reached from
p
by

q=\phi^{[X_{I_{n}}]}_{r_{n}}\cdots\phi^{[X_{I_{2}}]}_{r_{2}}\phi^{[X_{I_{1}}]}_{r_{1}}(p),\qquad|r_{k}|\leq\rho^{|I_{k}|},

where
\phi^{X}_{t}(P)
denotes the flow of the vector field
X
for time
t
starting at the point
P
, i.e.,
\phi^{X}_{0}(P)=P
and
d\phi^{X}_{t}(P)/dt=X(\phi^{X}_{t}(P))
.

Denote by
\overline{B}^{SR}(p,\rho)
the closed sub-Riemannian ball centered at
p
of radius
\rho>0
, consisting of all points
q
that can be joined to
p
with a horizontal curve of arc length
\leq\rho
.

Theorem 17

(Uniform ball-box
[
8
, Cor. 3]
, simplified)
.

Fix a minimal basis
\{[X_{I_{1}}],\dots,[X_{I_{n}}]\}
.
Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a compact set. There exist constants
c,C,\delta_{0}>0
such that, for every
p\in K
,
0<\varepsilon\leq\delta_{0}
, we have

\operatorname{Box}(p,c\varepsilon)\subseteq\overline{B}^{SR}(p,\varepsilon)\subseteq\operatorname{Box}(p,C\varepsilon).

Let us now specialize this theorem to our situation. Let
M=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
K
be a compact set,
n=d
,
m=2
, and

I_{1}=(1),\qquad I_{2}=(2),\qquad I_{k}=(1,2,\underbrace{1,1,\dots,1}_{k-3}),\qquad\text{for $3\leq k\leq d$},

so that
[X_{I_{k}}]=f_{k}
for
1\leq k\leq d
.
This gives:

Corollary 18
.

There exist constants

and

,

\varphi(y+[-\bar{c}\rho,\bar{c}\rho]\times[-\bar{c}\rho,\bar{c}\rho]\times[-\bar{c}\rho^{2},\bar{c}\rho^{2}]\times[-\bar{c}\rho^{3},\bar{c}\rho^{3}]\times\cdots\times[\bar{c}\rho^{d-1},\bar{c}\rho^{d-1}])\\
\subseteq\overline{B}^{SR}(p,\rho)\subseteq\\
\varphi(y+[-\bar{C}\rho,\bar{C}\rho]\times[-\bar{C}\rho,\bar{C}\rho]\times[-\bar{C}\rho^{2},\bar{C}\rho^{2}]\times[-\bar{C}\rho^{3},\bar{C}\rho^{3}]\times\cdots\times[\bar{C}\rho^{d-1},\bar{C}\rho^{d-1}]),

with
\varphi
as defined in (

.

Proof.

Immediately from the definition we see that
[X_{I_{k}}]=f_{k}
is a minimal basis. Apply Theorem

. Denote the standard basis of

\mathring{\gamma}(t)=\tau(\varphi^{-1}(\gamma(t))+(0,\xi_{b}(\gamma_{1}(t)))),\qquad t\in[-a_{\delta},a_{\delta}].

Recall that
\varphi^{-1}

\gamma

\Omega

\mathring{\gamma}

Regularization of the Lagrangian and proof of Theorem

The Lagrangian density
{L^{\vee\!\vee}}
defined in (

However this lack of regularity is not crucial to the obtainment of results analogous to those of Theorem

Theorem 24
.

In the situation described in Section
2
, and under Assumptions

9

1

24

24

appropriately transformed by the diffeomorphism defined in Proposition

Proof.

Take a
C^{\infty}

and supported in the unit ball

for
0<\epsilon<(1-\lambda)a
. Let also

is supported in the ball

is a

\displaystyle|\bar{L}_{\epsilon}(x,u)-{L^{\vee\!\vee}}(x,u)|\leq C(K)\epsilon

\displaystyle\text{for $(x,u)\in K\subseteq\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}$ with $|x_{1}\pm a|>\epsilon$},

\displaystyle 0\leq\bar{L}_{\epsilon}(x,u)\leq\sup_{y\in\overline{\Omega}}{L^{\vee\!\vee}}(y,u)

(36)
