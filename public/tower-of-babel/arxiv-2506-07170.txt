Rectangular Duals on the Cylinder and the Torus


Abstract
A rectangular dual of a plane graph G G is a contact representation of G G by interior-disjoint rectangles such that (i) no four rectangles share a point, and (ii) the union of all rectangles is a rectangle. In this paper, we study rectangular duals of graphs that are embedded in surfaces other than the plane. In particular, we fully characterize when a graph embedded on a cylinder admits a cylindrical rectangular dual. For graphs embedded on the flat torus, we can test whether the graph has a toroidal rectangular dual if we are additionally given a regular edge labeling , i.e. a combinatorial description of rectangle adjacencies. Furthermore we can test whether there exists a toroidal rectangular dual that respects the embedding and that resides on a flat torus for which the sides are axis-aligned. Testing and constructing the rectangular dual, if applicable, can be done efficiently.

Introduction

In a
contact representation

\mathcal{R}

G

\mathcal{R}(w)

\mathcal{R}(u)

A famous example are Koebe’s coin graphs
[

Further examples include contact graphs of triangles

A
rectangular dual
of a plane graph

is a commonly studied type of contact representation
where each vertex

is mapped to a non-empty, axis-aligned rectangle satisfying two conditions (see
Fig.

2
):

(i)

No four rectangles share a point;

(ii)

the union of the rectangles is a rectangle.

It has been characterized repeatedly when a plane graph

admits a rectangular dual
[

,

,

,

,

,
and how to test these conditions and construct the rectangular dual in linear time
[

,

,

]
.
Rectangular duals have originally been studied due to their relation to floor plans in architecture
[
40
]

and VLSI design
[

,

]
, and to rectangular cartograms from cartography
[

,

]
.
Here we are interested in generalizing rectangular duals from the plane to other surfaces,
specifically, to the cylinder and the torus as shown in
Fig.

.

A common way to represent the surface of the cylinder [torus] is with a parallelogram
Q
,
called the
fundamental
or
canonical polygon
,
where one pair [both pairs] of opposite sides of
Q
are identified;
imagine cutting the cylinder [torus] along a path [two cycles] and then mapping the surface onto
Q
.
We also speak of
Q
as the
flat cylinder
or
flat torus
, accordingly.
If
Q
has axis-parallel sides, we call it the
rectangular flat cylinder/torus
;
otherwise, we call it
slanted
.
We assume that we have a standing cylinder and
thus call the identified sides of a flat cylinder
Q
the left and right side.

A
cylindrical
[
toroidal
] graph
G
is a graph embedded on the cylinder [torus] such that no two edges cross.
Note that while a planar triangulated graph has a unique embedding in the plane (up to the choice of the outer face),
this is not the case for a toroidal triangulated graph:
Given one embedding, we can obtain another one by, roughly speaking,
cutting the torus along a cycle through the hole (or around the body),
twisting it and glueing it back together; this operation is known as a
Dehn twist
.
Hence, when working with a toroidal graph we may also need a combinatorial description
of how the graph is embedded on the flat torus (defined in
Section

2
).
For rectangular duals of cylindrical and toroidal graphs,
we consider representations on a fundamental polygon
Q

that maintain the characteristic properties of planar rectangular duals.
In particular, we assume that no four rectangles share a point.
Similar to how a planar rectangular dual is bounded by four axis-aligned line segments,
we want both the top and bottom of a cylindrical rectangular dual to be bounded by a horizontal line.
On the torus on the other hand, we have no border and thus require the rectangular dual to fully cover
Q
.
See
Figs.

1
,
2
and
3
for examples and
Section

2
for precise definitions.
Since rectangles may extend across an (identified) side of the fundamental polygon,
we can, unlike in the case of planar rectangular duals, realize

separating triangles
(i.e. a triangle that is not the boundary of a face), or also loops or parallel edges.

An important notion when working with a rectangular dual is a
regular edge labeling (REL)

Introduced by He

In addition, RELs are fundamental tool in a variety of related applications, such as

simultaneous and partial representation extensions, and morphing
[

,
23
,
21
,
13
,
14

Bernard, Fusy, and Liang
[

Bonichon and Lévêque
[

studied toroidal RELs,
yet did not explore RELs as a tool to construct toroidal rectangular duals.
This and also the recent work on visibility representations on the torus and Klein bottle
[
5
]
, motivated this work.

Contribution.

In this paper, we characterize exactly when a cylindrical graph (with fixed embedding)
has a rectangular dual on the flat cylinder.
For toroidal graphs, a similar characterization remains open,
but we provide a significant and non-trivial step by arguing when a given REL can be realized as a rectangular dual.
More precisely, given a toroidal graph
G
, a REL
\mathcal{L}
, and an embedding on a flat torus,
we can decide in linear time whether
G
has a rectangular dual
\mathcal{R}
on a flat torus that
realizes

\mathcal{L}
,
i.e. the REL that arises from
\mathcal{R}
is
\mathcal{L}
.

While the flat torus in
Fig.

3
is
slanted
(some of its sides are not axis-parallel),
it would be preferred to have a drawing on the
rectangular flat torus
.
Yet there are toroidal graphs (such as the one in
Fig.

3
) that have a toroidal rectangular dual,
but none on the rectangular flat torus.
So we also study the question how to test whether a toroidal graph
G

has a rectangular dual on a rectangular flat torus.
We show that if given the REL
\mathcal{L}
and an embedding on a rectangular flat torus,
then we can test whether we can find a rectangular dual that respects these input constraints.

In terms of presentation, we first study toroidal graphs in
Section

3
,
since some results for cylindrical graphs (in
Section

4

Our proofs are constructive, and the corresponding representations can be found in linear or quadratic time.

Preliminaries

We start with a little primer on working on the flat torus, in particular,
the classification of curves in homotopy classes and important basic results.
This is based on Stillwell
[
41
]
and Chambers, Erickson, Lin, and Parsa
[
11
]
.
We then give necessary definitions and notation on graphs, rectangular duals,
and regular edge labelings, and make some basic observations on cycles in toroidal graphs on the flat torus.

2.1
Working with the Flat Torus

The (unit-square) flat torus
\mathbb{T}
is the metric space obtained
by identifying opposites sides of the unit square
[0,1]^{2}
in the Eucledian plane via
(x,0)\sim(x,1)
and
(0,y)\sim(1,y)
.
The
universal covering plane (UCP)
is obtained by tiling the plane with infinitely many copies of
\mathbb{T}
.
So equivalently,
\mathbb{T}
is the quotient space
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.
The
covering map
(or
projection map
) is the function
\pi\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{T}
via
\pi(x,y)=(x\bmod 1,y\bmod 1)
.
We assume the unit-square flat torus
\mathbb{T}
whenever we consider toroidal graph embeddings,
yet for some toroidal rectangular duals, we need a non-square flat torus
Q
.
For those, UCP and covering map can be defined correspondingly by tiling the plane with copies of
Q
, and defining the
covering map as the inverse of this operation.
If a toroidal graph is given on the surface of a torus in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
then one can compute an embedding on the flat torus in linear time
[
36
]
.

Curves.

Throughout, we call a closed, simple, directed curve on a surface
\Sigma
(plane/cylinder/torus) simply a
curve
.
Two curves
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
on
\Sigma
are
homotopic
if one can be continuously deformed into the other.
More formally, interpreting a curve as a map from
[0,1]
to
\Sigma
, the curves
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
are
homotopic

if there exists a continuous map, a
homotopy
,
f\colon[0,1]\times[0,1]\to\Sigma
where
f(0,\cdot)=\mathcal{C}
and
f(1,\cdot)=\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
.
A curve is
contractible
if it is homotopic to a single point and
non-contractible
otherwise.

Any curve
\mathcal{C}
on
\mathbb{T}
can be
lifted
to a curve in the UCP
by continuing on the adjacent copy of
\mathbb{T}
rather than continuing on the other side.
We call this a
covering curve
of
\mathcal{C}
and use
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
to denote it.
There are infinitely many covering curves of
\mathcal{C}
, depending on which copy of
G
and which point of
\mathcal{C}
we use to start building it.
Sometimes we use the notation
\overline{\mathcal{C}}
for one copy of
\mathcal{C}
in the UCP, obtained by picking an arbitrary point
p
on some
covering curve
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
, and then walking from point
p
along
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
until we have walked the entire length of
\mathcal{C}
.
Note that a covering curve
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
of a non-contractible curve
\mathcal{C}
goes towards infinity in both direction,
while a copy
\overline{\mathcal{C}}
only walks the length of
\mathcal{C}
once.

Crossings.

For a curve
\mathcal{C}
, let
\mathcal{C}[a,b)
be the part of
\mathcal{C}
between points
a
and
b
,
including the endpoint if we use a bracket and excluding it if we use a parenthesis.
For two directed curves
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
with a maximal common sub-curve
\mathcal{C}[p,q]
(possibly
p=q
),
we say that
\mathcal{C}

crosses

\mathcal{C}^{\prime}

at
\mathcal{C}[p,q]

if
\mathcal{C}
enters
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
from one side at
p
and leaves it at
q
towards the opposite side;
otherwise the two curves are said to
touch
.
We call such a crossing
left-to-right
if
\mathcal{C}
enters
p
left of
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
and
leaves
q
right of
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
; otherwise we call it
right-to-left
.
Summing over all maximal shared sub-curves, the
algebraic crossing number
of
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
is

\hat{\imath}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime})=\lvert\{\mathcal{C}\text{ crosses }\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\text{ left-to-right}\}\rvert-\lvert\{\mathcal{C}\text{ crosses }\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\text{ right-to-left}\}\rvert\text{.}

Correspondingly, we say that
\mathcal{C}

crosses

\mathcal{C}^{\prime}

algebraically left-to-right [right-to-left]

if
\hat{\imath}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime})>0
[
\hat{\imath}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime})<0
].
Two homotopic curves
C,C^{\prime}
may cross each other, but not algebraically,
if
we have
\hat{\imath}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime})=0
.

Orbits and Homotopy Classes.

The sides of
\mathbb{T}
are two non-contractible curves
\color[rgb]{0.121,0.47,0.705}M
and
\color[rgb]{0.89,0.102,0.109}H
that intersect exactly once.
The vertical curve
\color[rgb]{0.121,0.47,0.705}M
is called
meridian
or
first orbit
and we assume
\color[rgb]{0.121,0.47,0.705}M
is directed bottom-to-top.
The horizontal curve
\color[rgb]{0.89,0.102,0.109}H
is called
horizon
or
second orbit
(or sometimes
latitude
)
and we assume
\color[rgb]{0.89,0.102,0.109}H
is directed left-to-right.
(For a slanted flat torus, the naming is such that
\color[rgb]{0.89,0.102,0.109}H
is not vertical and
\color[rgb]{0.121,0.47,0.705}M
is not horizontal.)

It is known that the pair
{\color[rgb]{0.121,0.47,0.705}M}
and
{\color[rgb]{0.89,0.102,0.109}H}
are the generators of the fundamental group of
\mathbb{T}

which is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
and coincide with its homology group.
We do not define these terms precisely, but only express its implications to the extent that we need them.
For a directed curve
\mathcal{C}
, define
m(\mathcal{C}):=\hat{\imath}(\mathcal{C},{\color[rgb]{0.121,0.47,0.705}M})
and
h(\mathcal{C}):=\hat{\imath}({\color[rgb]{0.89,0.102,0.109}H},\mathcal{C})
,
that is, the number of times
\mathcal{C}
(algebraically) crosses the meridian left-to-right and the horizon right-to-left (bottom-to-top).
(Note that the order of curve and orbit are
not
the same for both.)
The pair
(m(\mathcal{C}),h(\mathcal{C}))
is called the
homotopy class
of
\mathcal{C}
and always coprime.

The following observations are well known
[
41
]
.
Orbits
\color[rgb]{0.121,0.47,0.705}M
and
\color[rgb]{0.89,0.102,0.109}H
have homotopy class
(0,1)
and
(1,0)
, respectively.
Contractible curves have homotopy class
(0,0)
.
We call two curves
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}

parallel
if they have the same homotopy class
and
reverse-parallel
if
(m(\mathcal{C}),h(\mathcal{C}))={-}(m(\mathcal{C}^{\prime}),h(\mathcal{C}^{\prime}))
.
Two curves
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
with
\hat{\imath}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime})=0
are either parallel, reverse-parallel,
or at least one is contractible.
For two [reverse-]parallel curves
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
and third curve
\mathcal{D}
,
it holds that
\hat{\imath}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})=\hat{\imath}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime},\mathcal{D})
[resp.
\hat{\imath}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})=-\hat{\imath}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime},\mathcal{D})
].
Furthermore, the algebraic crossing number of two curves
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}

can be computed from the homotopy classes alone with

\hat{\imath}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime})=m(\mathcal{C})h(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})-h(\mathcal{C})m(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})\text{.}

Consider a copy
\overline{\mathcal{C}}
of a curve
\mathcal{C}
of homotopy class
(m(\mathcal{C}),h(\mathcal{C}))
in the UCP.
Note that it crosses the meridian algebraically left-to-right exactly
m(\mathcal{C})
times
and the horizon algebraically right-to-left exactly
h(\mathcal{C})
times.
Therefore,
\overline{\mathcal{C}}
connects a point
p
with point
p+(m,h)
; see
Fig.

4
.
If we stretch
\overline{\mathcal{C}}
to a straight line segment, it has slope
\tfrac{h(\mathcal{C})}{m(\mathcal{C})}
(where a line of slope
\pm\infty
is vertical).
Hence, all homotopy classes are given by
\mathbb{Q}\cup\pm\infty
(
m
can be 0).
Furthermore, the
standard form
of the covering curve of
\mathcal{C}
is a line of slope
\tfrac{h(\mathcal{C})}{m(\mathcal{C})}

that goes through a grid point; see
Fig.

.

2.2
Cylindrical and Toroidal Graphs

Throughout,
G
denotes a connected (not necessarily simple) graph with vertices
V(G)
and with edges
E(G)
.
Graph
G
comes with a fixed, crossing-free embedding on a surface
\Sigma

described by giving the
rotation system
(a cyclic order of edges around each vertex) that determines the faces.
An
outer face
is an unbounded face on
\Sigma
(a cylindrical graph has two while a toroidal graphs has none);
all other faces are called
inner faces
.
We assume that the one outer face of a plane graph and the two outer faces of a cylindrical graph are part of the input.
For a toroidal graph
G
embedded on the unit-square flat torus
\mathbb{T}
,
we additionally get the intersections of
E(G)
with meridian
{\color[rgb]{0.121,0.47,0.705}M}
and horizon
{\color[rgb]{0.89,0.102,0.109}H})
.
(After local deformations, we may assume that the unique crossing of
\color[rgb]{0.121,0.47,0.705}M
and
\color[rgb]{0.89,0.102,0.109}H
lies in a face of
G
,
and that
{\color[rgb]{0.121,0.47,0.705}M},\color[rgb]{0.89,0.102,0.109}H
do not go through vertices or along edges.)
We can store these crossings via the
planarization

G^{\times}\!

obtained by subdividing an edge
e
with a dummy-vertex whenever
e
is crossed by an orbit,
adding a dummy-vertex for where
{\color[rgb]{0.121,0.47,0.705}M}
crosses
{\color[rgb]{0.89,0.102,0.109}H}
,
and then adding edges between dummy-vertices that are consecutive on orbits.
When we speak of finding something ‘in linear [quadratic] time’,
then we mean ‘in time linear [quadratic] in the size of
G^{\times}
’,
i.e. the number of crossings of orbits with edges counts for the input-size.

Graph
G
is called
(internally) triangulated
if all (inner) faces are triangles.
An
outer vertex
is one on an outer face; all others are
inner vertices
.
A
chord
of a face
f
is an edge not on
f
for which both endpoints are on
f
.
(In particular, if there is a vertex on
f
that is incident to a loop, but
f
is not the loop,
then the loop is considered a chord of
f
.)
For a toroidal graph
G
, the
cover graph

\tilde{G}
of
G

is obtained by pasting a copy of
G
into each copy of
\mathbb{T}
in the UCP.

Since we allow non-simple graphs, we have to define separating triangles in cylindrical and toroidal graphs.

Different Rectangular Duals.

A planar rectangular dual
\mathcal{R}
has a
frame

if there are exactly four rectangles incident to the outer face of
\mathcal{R}
.
A simple planar graph
G
then has a planar rectangular dual with a frame if and only if it is a

properly triangulated planar (PTP) graph

[
26
]
:
it is planar, internally triangulated, contains no separating triangle, and the outer face is a 4-cycle.
We define rectangular duals as well as analogous necessary conditions for cylindrical and toroidal graphs
in the respective sections.

A
cylindrical rectangular dual

\mathcal{R}
of cylindrical graph
G
is a contact representation of
G

on a flat cylinder
Q
with rectangles such that

(i)

no four rectangles share a point, and

(ii)

the union of the rectangles form a strip from the left to the right side of
Q
,
that is, an area bounded by two parallel lines.

We say that
\mathcal{R}
has a
frame
if there is exactly one rectangle incident to each outer face of
\mathcal{R}
.
Note that we can assume without loss of generality that
Q
is a rectangular flat cylinder and axis-aligned; see
Fig.

5
.

Due to some particularities of cylindrical rectangular duals and their realized RELs,
the generalization of PTP graphs for cylindrical graphs has a rather specific extra property
if the graph is not planar.
A graph
G
is a
properly triangulated cylindrical (PTC) graph

if
G
is cylindrical, internally triangulated, all loops, parallel edges, and separating triangles are non-contractible,
the outer faces have no non-consecutive occurrences of the a vertex and no chord,
and any vertex
u
with parallel edges to neighbors that have loops has the following degree:

\bullet

If
u
is on an outer face,
\deg(u)\geq 4
;

\bullet

if
u
has parallel edges to two neighbors that have loops,
\deg(u)\geq 6
;

\bullet

otherwise
\deg(u)\geq 5
.

We see in
Section

4
why this is needed.
Having no non-consecutive occurrences of a vertex on an outer face implies
that it is bounded by a cycle, a pair of parallel edges, or a loop.
Furthermore, we want to clarify that since
G
may not be simple,
a separating triangle can also be spanned by a pair of parallel edges between two vertices
x
and
y
and a loop at
x
.

Figure 5
:

Q
is a rectangular flat cylinder.
If otherwise, we can assume that the top and bottom sides are horizontal,
consider an infinite chain of copies of

glued together in the plane,
and then pick a rectangular flat cylinder

that covers exactly one copy of each point of

A
toroidal rectangular dual
of a toroidal graph
G
is a contact representation of
G

on a flat torus
Q
with rectangles such that

(i)

no four rectangles share a point, and

(ii)

the union of the rectangles fills
Q
.

We generally assume that
Q
is oriented such that the rectangles are axis aligned.
A graph
G
is a
properly triangulated toroidal (PTT) graph

if
G
is toroidal, triangulated, and all loops, parallel edges, and separating triangles are non-contractible.
(The last property is also called
essentially 4-connected

since then the universal cover graph of
G
is 4-connected
[

]
.
If the universal cover graph is simple,
then
G
also contains no contractible loops or parallel edges.)

Regular Edge Labelings.

Any rectangular dual
\mathcal{R}
of a graph
G
naturally gives rise to a 2-coloring and an orientation of the edges of
G
:
An edge

3
Rectangular Dual on the Torus

In this section, we give an algorithm to decide whether a given toroidal graph
G
on the flat torus
\mathbb{T}

admits a toroidal rectangular dual,
assuming that a toroidal REL
\mathcal{L}
has been specified.
To this end, we first classify toroidal RELs and then describe construction algorithms.

3.1
Toroidal RELs: the Good, the Bad, and the Ugly

We classify
\mathcal{L}
based on whether it can be realized by a rectangular dual
on the rectangular or slanted flat torus or not at all.

A “Bad” REL.

For
i\in[2]
, an edge
e\in L_{i}(G)
is called
lonely
if it does not belong to any cycle of
L_{i}(G)
.
We call
\mathcal{L}

unrealizable
if it has a lonely edge, and
realizable
otherwise.
As we show in
Section

3.2
, only a realizable REL can come from a rectangular dual.

A “Good” REL.

For
i\in[2]
, an edge
e\in L_{i}(G)
is called
orbital

if it lies on an
i
-orbital cycle of
L_{i}(G)
.
We call
\mathcal{L}

i
-orbital
if all edges in
L_{i}(G)
are orbital,
and
orbital
if it is
1-orbital
and
2-orbital
.
(The REL in
Fig.

3
is not
2-orbital
: the
red
edge
(d,i)

does not belong to a
2-orbital

red
cycle.)
Note that orbital implies realizable.
Moreover, we show below that ‘orbital’ is equivalent to ‘realizable by a rectangular dual on the rectangular flat torus’.

An “Ugly” REL.

Presuming
\mathcal{L}
is realizable, we call the REL
slanted
if,
for some
i\in[2]
, graph
L_{i}(G)
contains an edge that is not
i
-orbital.
Such a REL is realizable by a toroidal rectangular dual, but not on the rectangular flat torus.

We can test whether

3.2
From Realizable REL to Toroidal Rectangular Dual

We now show that what we defined as
realizable RELs
is indeed a characterization,
that is, they are exactly those RELs
that can arise from a toroidal rectangular duals.
That realizability is necessary is easy to show:
If graph

,

Lemma 3.11
.

If a PTT graph

Proof 3.12
.

Consider the universal covering plane of

\mathcal{R}
into each copy of

.
We only show that every

Run-Time Analysis.

While our proof of existence occasionally went over to the (infinite) UCP,
all vital steps to find the rectangular dual can be done efficiently.
Finding
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
takes linear time, as does splitting the graphs, computing the dual graphs, and computing longest paths.
Since any edge is visited at most four times in total by either
C_{1}
or
C_{2}
, the total length of these circuits is
\mathcal{O}(\lvert V(G)\rvert)
,
so splitting graph
G
at both
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
gives
\mathcal{O}(\lvert V(G)\rvert)
vertices in total, which hence also
bounds the total size of all patches together.
So we can compute
\mathcal{R}_{P}
for all patches
P
in linear time.
Computing curves
\mathcal{C}_{M}
and
\mathcal{C}_{H}
can then be done in
\mathcal{O}(n)
time, where
n
is the input-size
(recall that this counts not only
\lvert V(G)\rvert
but also how many crossings there are on
\color[rgb]{0.121,0.47,0.705}M
and
\color[rgb]{0.89,0.102,0.109}H
).
This also means that we had to translate
\mathcal{O}(n)
patches, a result that becomes useful below.

With this, we know
\mathcal{R}
implicitly:
We know
Q
from the endpoints of the curves, and
we know what the rectangular dual of each patch
looks like, but we do not know how often each patch needs to be copied to where.
To compute the full rectangular dual
\mathcal{R}
, we first must analyze how many patches could be relevant.
Recall that
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}
was obtained by first filling the entire plane with
(d_{2}(s_{2}){-}1)\times(d_{1}(s_{1}){-}1)
-rectangles;
we call these the
tiles
.
Let
\mathcal{T}
be the set of tiles that intersect
Q
;
to obtain our rectangular dual we first compute
\mathcal{T}

and then fill each tile with the rectangular dual of the corresponding patch.

To bound
\lvert\mathcal{T}\rvert
, we split it into three sets.
Let
\mathcal{T}_{1}
be all those tiles that are intersected by the boundary of
Q
.
Let
\mathcal{T}_{2}
be all those tiles that have
L_{\infty}
-distance at most 2 from a tile in
\mathcal{T}_{1}
,
i.e., the tile lies within a
5\times 5
-grid of tiles that is centered at a tile in
\mathcal{T}_{1}
.
Let
\mathcal{T}_{3}
be all remaining tiles that intersect
Q
.
We first bound
\lvert\mathcal{T}_{1}\rvert
.
Recall that we translated
\mathcal{O}(n)
patches while computing
\mathcal{C}_{M}
, therefore
\mathcal{C}_{M}
intersects
\mathcal{O}(n)
tiles.
It follows that the tiles containing
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
have
L_{\infty}
-distance in
\mathcal{O}(n)
;
hence
\overline{p_{1}p_{2}}
can also only intersect
\mathcal{O}(n)
tiles.
So each side of
Q
intersects
\mathcal{O}(n)
tiles, or
\lvert\mathcal{T}_{1}\rvert\in\mathcal{O}(n)
.
This immediately implies
\lvert\mathcal{T}_{2}\rvert\in\mathcal{O}(n)
since each tile in
\mathcal{T}_{1}
adds
\mathcal{O}(1)
tiles to
\mathcal{T}_{2}
.
To bound
\lvert\mathcal{T}_{3}\rvert
, consider a vertex
v
for which
\mathcal{R}(v)
lies fully within
Q
.
Observe that
\mathcal{R}(v)
can intersect at most a
3\times 3
-grid of tiles,
because an intersection with the horizontal [vertical] boundary of a tile means that
\color[rgb]{0.89,0.102,0.109}C_{2}
[
\color[rgb]{0.121,0.47,0.705}C_{1}
] went through
v
;
this can happen at most twice in each direction.
Therefore, each vertex whose rectangle lies fully inside
Q
gives rise to at most 9 tiles in
\mathcal{T}_{3}
.
Vice versa for any tile
T
in
\mathcal{T}_{3}
, there must be some vertex
v
for which
\mathcal{R}(v)
intersects
T
.
Therefore, all of
\mathcal{R}(v)
lies within tiles that have
L_{\infty}
-distance 2 from
T
.
None of these tiles is in
\mathcal{T}_{1}
by definition of
\mathcal{T}_{2}
, so all of
\mathcal{R}(v)
lies within
Q
.
So the tiles intersected by vertices that lie fully in
Q
cover all tiles of
\mathcal{T}_{3}
,
and since each such vertex has only one rectangle in
\mathcal{R}
we have
\lvert\mathcal{T}_{3}\rvert\in\mathcal{O}(\lvert V(G)\rvert)
.

Putting it all together, we have
\mathcal{O}(n)
tiles that intersect
Q
,
and computing the rectangular dual means translating
\mathcal{O}(n)
patches and intersecting them with
Q
,
which takes
\mathcal{O}(\lvert V(G)\rvert)
time per patch.
So the total time to compute
\mathcal{R}
is
\mathcal{O}(n\lvert V(G)\rvert)
.

We note here that the time-bound in
Theorem

3.17
is usually a vast overestimation;
normally one would expect far fewer than
\Theta(n)
tiles to intersect
Q
,
and there would be either few tiles or most of the corresponding patches would not have
\Theta(\lvert V(G)\rvert)
rectangles.
But we cannot rule out the possibility that a single patch (with
\Theta(\lvert V(G)\rvert)
vertices)
is intersected
\Theta(n)
times by a side of
Q
;
this could happen for example if both sides of
Q
are nearly vertical and
Q
hence becomes a very long and skinny sliver.

4
Rectangular Dual on the Cylinder

Recall that a graph
G
is a
properly triangulated cylindrical (PTC) graph

if
G
is cylindrical, internally triangulated, all loops, parallel edges, and separating triangles are non-contractible,
the outer faces have no non-consecutive occurrences of a vertex and no chord,
and the following
degree-condition
holds: If
\{u,v\}
is a parallel edge and
v
is incident
to a loop, then
\deg(u)\geq 4
, and either
u
is on an outer face, or
\deg(u)\geq 6
, or
\deg(u)=5

and at least one neighbour of
u
is not incident to a loop.
In this section, we prove that every PTC graph admits a cylindrical rectangular dual.
We first show how to find a cylindrical REL and
then how to construct the rectangular dual via reduction to the toroidal case.

4.1
Cylindrical RELs: Construction via Canonical Orderings

The construction of a cylindrical REL is heavily based on the idea of a
(3,1)
-canonical order
,
defined by Biedl and Derka
[
6
]
.
This is a partition of the vertices of a planar 4-connected triangulation
into an ordered sequence of vertex sets that satisfies some conditions.
This partition can be used to obtain a REL of a PTP graph.
Since we have additional conditions, we cannot use the approach from Biedl and Derka
[
6
]

as a black box and instead re-do and expand it.
We need a helper-lemma, whose proof closely follows Biedl and Derka
[
6
, Lemma 1]

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
G
be a PTC graph with outer faces
f_{s}
and
f_{t}
and with
V(f_{s})\neq V(G)
.
Then there exists a set
V^{\prime}\subset V(f_{t})
such that

\bullet

V^{\prime}
contains no vertex of
f_{s}
,

\bullet

G\setminus V^{\prime}
is a PTC graph, and

\bullet

V^{\prime}
is either

\circ

a single vertex
z
with
\deg(z)\geq 4
(a
singleton
),

\circ

it induces a path
\{z_{1},\dots,z_{k}\}
with
\deg(z_{i})=3
for
i\in[k]
(a
fan
),

\circ

V^{\prime}=V(f_{t})=\{z_{1},\dots,z_{k}\}
with each
z_{i}
,
i\in[k]
, incident to a vertex
x
that spans a loop (a
loop neighborhood
), or

\circ

5
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we studied rectangular duals on the torus and on the cylinder.
For toroidal graphs with a given REL,
we characterized when this REL can correspond to a rectangular dual;
if also given an embedding on the flat torus then we can test
whether the corresponding flat torus can be made a rectangle.
For cylindrical graphs, we need not be given a REL:
we can characterize for any cylindrical graph whether it has a rectangular dual on the rectangular flat cylinder.
The conditions can be tested, and if they hold, the corresponding rectangular duals can be found in linear time.

Our main open problem concerns toroidal graphs without REL and/or embedding on the flat torus.
Does every properly-triangulated toroidal graph have a realizable REL?
Bonichon and Lévêque
[
7
]
show that it has a so-called ‘balanced 4-orientation’
from which a REL can be derived, but is this always realizable?
Secondly, given a REL
\mathcal{L}
, can we choose an embedding on
\mathbb{T}
that makes
\mathcal{L}
orbital?
By exhaustive case analysis we can argue that this is not always possible for the graph in

Fig.

3
, not even if we are allowed to change the REL.
What are necessary and sufficient conditions, and can they be tested in linear time?
