LLM-BabyBench: Can Language Models Plan in Worlds They Can Simulate?
\name Idriss Malek *† \email im2868@nyu.edu, \name Omar Choukrani * \email omar.choukrani@mbzuai.ac.ae, \name Daniil Orel * \email daniil.orel@mbzuai.ac.ae, \name Anh Duy Le Dinh \email anhduy.ledinh@mbzuai.ac.ae, \name Zhuohan Xie \email zhuohan.xie@mbzuai.ac.ae, \name Zangir Iklassov \email zangir.iklassov@mbzuai.ac.ae, \name Martin Takáč \email martin.takac@mbzuai.ac.ae, \name Salem Lahlou \email salem.lahlou@mbzuai.ac.ae

Abstract
Interactive benchmarks for LLMs usually report a single metric, even though succeeding in them requires models to show several different skills. While those benchmarks are useful for measuring the overall capability of models to perform the downstream task, it is harder to identify which skill was lacking and caused failure solely from the aggregate score. For example, in benchmarks where models are supposed to navigate a simulated world to perform a task, failures can come from perception, an ambiguous instruction, retrieval, missing commonsense about what actions do, unknown dynamics, or planning. LLM-BabyBench recasts the procedurally generated BabyAI gridworld as a fully observable, purely textual environment designed to evaluate planning separately from those factors. The nature of the environment and the structure of the prompt control for the other factors cited above. To evaluate the models, we define the PPD suite: Predict asks for the state that follows an action sequence, Plan asks for an action sequence that reaches a goal, and Decompose asks for a subgoal sequence that achieves a mission. Across seven frontier and open models, queried zero-shot, we find simulation far ahead of planning for every model, close to saturated for the strongest. The length of the required solution governs planning failure, and each of the four strongest models has a horizon beyond which single-attempt success collapses. The responses of claude-opus-4-6 and claude-sonnet-4-6, which show their working step by step, reveal that they plan by proposing L-shaped corridors, which is consistent with their successful plans staying near-optimal while success falls. At the subgoal level, decomposition precision likewise falls to zero on long missions, while comprehension of those missions persists.

Introduction

Language models are increasingly used as agents that act over many steps, for example to call tools, browse the web, control robots or run multi-stage workflows.
To evaluate the performance of LLMs in simulated low-stakes environments, many interactive benchmarks have been developed. Usually, those benchmarks assess the models’ performance through a single metric that represents the overall success on the task
(
Shridhar et al., 2021
;
Savva et al., 2019
;
Fan et al., 2022
;
Liu et al., 2024
;
Paglieri et al., 2025
)
. While this aggregated measure is a fair summary of overall performance in a particular setting, it is a poor basis for a claim about any single capability, especially planning
(
Li et al., 2024
)
. For example, an agent failing to fetch a key in a household simulator can be due to perception, an ambiguous instruction, retrieval from a long context, lack of commonsense knowledge, or a misunderstanding of the environment’s transition dynamics. Studies of planning conclude that LLMs cannot plan autonomously
(
Valmeekam et al., 2023b
;
Kambhampati et al., 2024
)
, and the collapse of reasoning models on long puzzles has been read both as a limit of reasoning
(
Shojaee et al., 2025
)
and as errors in executing a plan the model already knows
(
Sinha et al., 2026
)
. Such conflicting readings may be due to the tendency to treat overall performance as an indicator of planning capability.

The goal of
LLM-BabyBench
is to evaluate planning while holding the other factors fixed. We transform the BabyAI gridworld
(
Chevalier-Boisvert et al., 2019
;
Chevalier-Boisvert et al., 2023
)
into a purely textual and fully observable environment. The instructions are written in Baby Language, a small formal grammar, which guarantees that the missions themselves are unambiguous; one ambiguity in how our prompt defines a subgoal argument is documented in Appendix
A.5
. The prompt contains all the information needed to solve the problems, which avoids failures due to retrieval, perception, or misunderstanding of the world’s rules. The environment also allows programmatic validation, which avoids relying on LLM-as-a-judge and the bias that comes with it
(
Ye et al., 2025
)
.

We also introduce a novel evaluation protocol, named PPD.
Predict
asks the model to predict the final state given an initial state and an action sequence.
Plan
expects an action sequence that leads to a specific final state, and measures both success rate and the efficiency of the trajectory provided.
Decompose
supplies a high-level mission and asks for a sequence of subgoals that achieves it when executed.

Our contributions are:

•

LLM-BabyBench
, a fully observable textual gridworld, and the
PPD suite
, a standardised evaluation harness to assess planning capabilities of LLMs.

•

The OmniBot
, a deterministic, full-visibility expert that solves every instance and is used as a verifier to evaluate LLMs.

•

New levels
with variable densities and sizes, specifically tailored for the
Plan
task. They hold task type, action set and goal structure fixed, which lets us show that completion tracks the length of the solution rather than the size of the grid.

•

Three assistance-aware metrics
to evaluate the
Decompose
task, which measure both whether a model’s decomposition succeeds and how much it helps a verified solver.

•

An evaluation of seven frontier and open models
showing that simulation accuracy is close to saturated for the strongest models while planning completion collapses at long horizons.

The benchmark instances are available at
https://huggingface.co/datasets/salem-mbzuai/LLM-BabyBench
under the MIT license (24,000 instances: 3 tasks
\times
16 levels
\times
500 seeds; the experiments reported here use a 20-seed subset of each level; the supplementary datasheet documents composition, hosting and maintenance), and the environment, the OmniBot and the evaluation harness at
https://github.com/Idriss-Malek/LLM-BabyBench
.

Related Work

Interactive benchmarks for LLM agents.

Many benchmarks have been developed to assess different capabilities of LLMs. In particular, there is a large corpus of interactive benchmarks that evaluate navigation and task completion in simulated worlds. ALFRED
(
Shridhar et al., 2020
)
and ALFWorld
(
Shridhar et al., 2021
)
pair household instructions with embodied or text-based execution. Habitat
(
Savva et al., 2019
)
evaluates photorealistic navigation. TextWorld
(
Côté et al., 2018
)
and ScienceWorld
(
Wang et al., 2022
)
evaluate LLMs on text games. MineDojo
(
Fan et al., 2022
)
and Voyager
(
Wang et al., 2024
)
use Minecraft to test LLMs’ behaviour in open worlds. There are also several suites that aggregate many tests under one harness: AgentBench
(
Liu et al., 2024
)
, SmartPlay
(
Wu et al., 2024
)
, BALROG
(
Paglieri et al., 2025
)
, Crafter
(
Hafner, 2022
)
, MiniHack
(
Samvelyan et al., 2021
)
, and NetHack
(
Küttler et al., 2020
)
. Closest to our setting, BabyAI-Text
(
Carta et al., 2023
)
renders BabyAI observations as text, and BALROG
(
Paglieri et al., 2025
)
uses it to evaluate LLM agents: the model reads a partial, egocentric description, acts one step at a time, and each episode is scored on completion.
LLM-BabyBench
instead gives the full grid with absolute coordinates, asks for a complete plan in a single response, and splits the evaluation into the three PPD tasks.

Planning benchmarks.

A second family of benchmarks was designed to evaluate planning directly. PlanBench
(
Valmeekam et al., 2023a
)
and the accompanying analysis
(
Valmeekam et al., 2023b
)
generate problems in domains from the International Planning Competition (IPC) and validate them using formal rules. Even for reasoning models, the autonomous generation rate collapses on long-horizon tasks
(
Valmeekam et al., 2025
)
.
Natural Plan

(
Zheng et al., 2024
)
and TravelPlanner
(
Xie et al., 2024
)
evaluate LLMs in scheduling and itinerary domains, where extracting constraints from long tool outputs is necessary for planning. PPNL
(
Aghzal et al., 2023
)
evaluates grid path planning in language and studies grid size, obstacle count and distance to the goal, observing that difficulty is temporal rather than spatial for its strongest configuration. Its follow-up
(
Aghzal et al., 2024
)
samples instances by optimal path length on maze-like
25\times 25
grids, finds that success falls with path length and depends on the geometry, and reports that
15\times 15
mazes are easier than
25\times 25
ones at the same path lengths, the smaller mazes also having fewer obstacles. We hold obstacle count fixed instead: stratifying by solution length within a fixed grid size reproduces the collapse, and at matched obstacle count the larger, less dense grid is easier for the three strongest models (Appendix
A.2
). Finally, CogEval
(
Momennejad et al., 2023
)
evaluates planning over graph-structured cognitive maps and documents hallucinations and loops.

Factoring evaluation into abilities.

Embodied Agent Interface
(
Li et al., 2024
)
decomposes embodied decision making into goal interpretation, subgoal decomposition, action sequencing and transition modelling, using VirtualHome and BEHAVIOR as testbeds. Several agent architectures
(
Song et al., 2023
;
Ahn et al., 2022
)
rely implicitly on the same separation between high-level planners and low-level actors. The PPD suite differs in that all three abilities are measured in one procedurally generated world against one deterministic oracle, so a model’s prediction accuracy and its planning performance refer to the same dynamics and the same ground truth. Embodied Agent Interface also refines LLM subgoals into actions with a search-based planner, but scores whether the resulting trajectory is feasible and reaches the goal; we instead let a deterministic solver insert the subgoals the model omitted and measure success by how many it has to add.

Probing world models.

Vafa et al. (2024)
show that generative models can hold incorrect world models even if they perform well on next-token prediction.
Li et al. (2023)
and
Gurnee and Tegmark (2024)
found that those models can still hold coherent internal representations of board state and of space and time.
Hao et al. (2023)
even manage to use an LLM’s own world model as a simulator inside search. We make no claim about internal representations. The
Predict
task only checks empirically that predicting and simulating the dynamics of the world are not the limiting factors for planning.

LLMs as components of planning systems.

There is a substantial literature about LLMs being used for a fast approximate solution before an exact oracle verifies and completes it: affordance-grounded skill selection
(
Ahn et al., 2022
)
, translation of free-form plans into admissible actions
(
Huang et al., 2022a
)
, closed-loop replanning from environment feedback
(
Huang et al., 2022b
)
, program- and code-structured plans
(
Liang et al., 2023
;
Singh et al., 2023
)
, translation into a formal problem for a classical planner
(
Liu et al., 2023
)
, and few-shot grounded planning for embodied agents
(
Song et al., 2023
)
.
Kambhampati et al. (2024)
argues that LLMs cannot plan autonomously and should instead generate candidates for sound external verifiers. A parallel line of work trains sequence models on search traces so that the model itself learns to search
(
Lehnert et al., 2024
;
Gandhi et al., 2024
)
. In the
Decompose
task, the Assistance Curve Integral tests whether the solutions provided by LLMs are worth refining. The subgoals themselves follow the standard notion of a temporally extended action
(
Sutton et al., 1999
)
.

LLM-BabyBench
and the PPD suite

3.1
LLM-BabyBench
and OmniBot

We build on BabyAI
(
Chevalier-Boisvert et al., 2019
)
, a procedurally generated grid world over the MiniGrid platform
(
Chevalier-Boisvert et al., 2023
)
. In this environment, an agent has to perform predefined tasks that are written in a small formal language named Baby Language. Baby Language combines verbs (go to, pick up, open, put next to), objects (door, key, ball, box), colours and relative locations (on your left, behind you) to provide simple instructions. The agent can open doors and pick up, carry and drop keys, balls and boxes. All items are colour-coded. MiniGrid defines seven actions (left, right, forward, pickup, drop, toggle, done); our textual interface exposes six, dropping
done
. BabyAI was built to study how Reinforcement Learning and Imitation Learning algorithms learn to follow grounded language instructions. To assess LLMs and their planning abilities, we adapt BabyAI to a purely textual, fully observable interface. Unlike the original BabyAI agent, the model receives all object positions regardless of its location or orientation. Except for the
Plan
task, for which we designed specific levels, the two other tasks use the original BabyAI levels.

Giving full visibility to the agent alters what the optimal path is, as there is no longer any need for blind exploration in this new setting. In particular, the Explore Subgoal is no longer needed. Full visibility also changes how locked doors are handled: the original bot can only fetch a matching key it has already seen and must otherwise explore for it, whereas the OmniBot sees every key and goes straight to it. Together with the exclusion of three levels that can generate unsolvable instances (Appendix
A.1.1
), this is what allows it to solve every instance we include. To produce ground truth, we extend BabyAI’s hand-coded expert into an “OmniBot” that exploits full visibility to choose near-optimal paths. The OmniBot solves
100\%
of benchmark instances and plays two roles: it supplies near-optimal trajectories (for the efficiency metrics) and it can execute an LLM’s subgoals, optionally inserting its own (for the
Decompose
metrics, see Section
3.2
). While BabyAI’s original bot was designed to be initialised with the original instruction, OmniBot can be initialised with any sequence of subgoals. The bot then appends whatever else the episode turns out to require. The number of subgoals added by the OmniBot is in particular used to assess the LLMs’ own decomposition. The size of the oracle’s own from-scratch decomposition of the mission, that is its initial plan plus every subgoal it inserts at runtime (the oracle subgoal count of Table
6
), is the quantity
M_{\text{help}}
that normalises the ACI metric below.

LLM-BabyBench
is a minimal environment with clear and simple dynamics. That simplicity allows us to separate spatial planning from:

•

Perception
: partial observability and visual parsing (ALFRED
(
Shridhar et al., 2020
)
, Habitat
(
Savva et al., 2019
)
) can make a perception failure look like a planning failure. In
LLM-BabyBench
, the complete grid is given as text.

•

Instruction ambiguity
: free-form goals (
Natural Plan

(
Zheng et al., 2024
)
, ALFWorld
(
Shridhar et al., 2021
)
) conflate planning with interpretation. Baby Language is a small formal grammar with templated instructions.

•

Retrieval
: where planning depends on facts buried in long context or tool output (
Natural Plan

(
Zheng et al., 2024
)
), a failure may be a retrieval failure. In
LLM-BabyBench
, every object’s coordinate is stated explicitly.

•

World knowledge
: open-world tasks (MineDojo
(
Fan et al., 2022
)
) require commonsense about what actions afford to perform planning. In
LLM-BabyBench
, all six actions and their deterministic effects are given in the prompt.

•

Dynamics learning
: when transitions are unknown, failure may lie in learning the rules rather than in using them. In
LLM-BabyBench
, all dynamics are stated explicitly.

•

Verification
: LLM-judged or self-reported success is can be gamed and is noisy. In
LLM-BabyBench
, verification is done by the OmniBot, which is deterministic and independent.

3.2
The PPD suite

In order to use
LLM-BabyBench
to assess LLMs, we introduce the PPD (
Predict
,
Plan
,
Decompose
) suite:

•

Predict
: given the initial state and an action sequence, output the final state (position and orientation). This task evaluates whether the model can track the
LLM-BabyBench
transition rules over an action sequence.

•

Plan
: given the initial state and a navigation goal (GoNextTo a target object), output an action sequence that reaches it. Correctness is evaluated by executing the sequence, and the LLM output is compared to the OmniBot’s near-optimal solution to assess efficiency
1
1

1

The OmniBot is a strong but not always strictly optimal reference: on rare instances a model finds a shorter valid path, so measured efficiency can marginally exceed 1.
.

•

Decompose
:
given the initial state and a high-level mission, output a subgoal sequence (from GoNextTo, Open, Close, Pickup, Drop) that, when executed, achieves the mission.

Figure
1
provides a conceptual overview of our PPD framework.

On
Predict
we use success rate. On
Plan
we use success rate together with efficiency, which compares the length of the OmniBot’s own sequence with the number of actions the model used.

Decompose
requires more care, since subgoals, unlike actions, are not directly executable. Simply comparing the sequence output by LLMs with the subgoal sequence computed by the OmniBot would be misleading, as a valid subgoal sequence is not unique. We therefore rely on the OmniBot to translate the LLM’s subgoals into actions, adding any necessary intermediate subgoals along the way. All the metrics we rely on to assess LLMs on
Decompose
are based on how many additional subgoals the OmniBot needs to add to the output of the LLMs to complete the mission. The OmniBot, however, is never given the final goal. If the LLM fails to break the original task down into at least its essential subgoals, not even the OmniBot can supply the missing steps. Given the task
Open a green door then pick the red ball
, for instance, the LLM must produce at least
Open green door
and
Pick red ball
.
The Comprehension Rate (CR) measures the success rate when the OmniBot is allowed to add as many subgoals as needed; it captures whether the LLM understood the subgoal logic and the final goal. This metric is essential to isolate planning. For models that have low CR, it is difficult to judge whether the failure is in planning or instruction understanding. The Precision Rate (PR) measures the success rate with no help from the OmniBot. A large gap between CR and PR shows that the model understands what to do but is unable to write a fully correct decomposition. Finally, the Assistance Curve Integral (ACI) measures how much help the model needs from the OmniBot. We integrate success over the number of subgoals the bot is permitted to insert, from
0
to the oracle’s own from-scratch decomposition size
M_{\text{help}}
, and normalise by
M_{\text{help}}
. With
N_{\text{help}}
the number of subgoals the OmniBot actually inserts, this gives the closed form
\max\!\big(0,(M_{\text{help}}-N_{\text{help}})/M_{\text{help}}\big)
for an episode that reaches the goal, and 0 for one that does not, as used in Appendix
A.10
: the fraction of the oracle’s work that the model’s plan removes. It is bounded by CR and PR by construction, and is near 1 when the model is capable of designing near-perfect subgoal sequences. More details are presented in Appendix
A.10
.

Summary:

LLM-BabyBench
is a procedurally generated gridworld, given entirely as text and used to evaluate LLMs on three tasks:

•

Predict
: can the model simulate the dynamics?

•

Plan
: can it produce actions that reach a goal?

•

Decompose
: can it break a mission into subgoals?

4
Results and Discussion

We evaluate seven models: four reasoning-capable models, claude-opus-4-6, claude-sonnet-4-6, gpt-5.4 and Kimi-K2.5 (open-weight), and three non-reasoning open-weight models, Llama-4-Maverick, Llama-4-Scout and Qwen2.5-72B. For all four models exposing an extended-reasoning control, extended reasoning was left at its default setting in every run; the scores below therefore characterise single-pass planning, not test-time-scaled planning. The prompt structure and querying protocol are detailed in Appendix
A.1.4
.

For an action sequence that never meets a blocked cell or a closed door, the final state can be computed from the agent’s position and orientation alone, without consulting the layout.

4.2.1
Success falls with solution length

Completion rates on Plan are low overall (Table
3
): only three
models clear 70% at pass@1. We found that the main factor responsible for the decreasing performance of the models is the length of the required solution (Table
4
). For every model but Llama-4-Maverick, single-attempt success is a monotone decreasing function of oracle plan length. Stratifying by path length within a fixed grid size, reproduces the collapse: claude-sonnet-4-6 falls from 97% to 38% across path-length terciles inside the Medium grid alone, and Llama-4-Maverick from 54% to 0% inside the
8\times 8
grid, whose prompts list at most eight objects; in this grid the object list varies only between five and eight entries, so longer prompts alone do not explain the collapse. Conversely, at matched obstacle count a larger grid is easier for the strong models (claude-opus-4-6: 48.3% on Large-120 against 80.0% on Ultra-120), since it lowers density without a proportional increase in path length. Grid size degrades completion largely because larger grids demand longer plans: the mean oracle length rises from 6.6 actions on Small to 24.4 on Ultra. Larger grids also contain fewer instances that a direct route solves, an effect analysed in Appendix
A.7
. We define the
planning horizon
of each model as the solution length
L_{50}
at which single-attempt success falls to 50%, the plan-length analogue of the horizon length
H_{0.5}
of
Sinha et al. (2026)
. The planning horizon is roughly 30 actions for Kimi-K2.5, 23–25 for the Claude models, 9 for gpt-5.4, and under 7 for the Llama and Qwen models, which record almost no success beyond 15 actions. The full analysis, with per-level results, correlations and density effects, is given in Appendix
A.2
.

Comparing the
Plan
results with the
Predict
results of Section
4.1
shows that the strongest models cannot reliably plan in settings they can simulate. On instances whose optimal plan runs 21–30 actions, claude-opus-4-6 returns a valid plan 48.1% of the time and Kimi-K2.5 59.6%, and beyond 30 actions 22.2% and 36.4%. On action sequences of 26 or more, the same two models recover the final state 77% and 93% of the time. While the two tasks are evaluated on different levels, the gap is too large to attribute to that difference alone: these models simulate accurately over horizons at which they cannot reliably plan. Moreover, the mean Manhattan distance between the predicted and the true final position on Predict, averaged over all samples with correct ones counting as zero, is 0.07 to 0.51 cells for the three strongest models, against a mean
ball_distance
of 7 to 19 cells across the seven models on the densest Ultra configuration (Ultra-180), and 7 to 9 cells for the same three models.

4.2.2
Efficiency across grid sizes

The
LLM-BabyBench
world is simple, deterministic and fully specified. As the grid grows larger, the rules, the action set and the state representation are unchanged, and breadth-first search is able to solve every instance in polynomial time in the grid area. Thus, we expect a system that planned by searching over the known dynamics under a fixed budget to output results that degrade gradually as the grid grows. For a reasonable grid size, the generated paths should still succeed, although the number of actions can be expected to increasingly deviate from the optimal. We observe that this is not the case in our experiments. Instead, completion collapses as the size and difficulty of the level grow, while efficiency stays stable: efficiency conditional on success stays within four points across grid sizes for the Claude models and Kimi-K2.5, and between 0.83 and 0.91 for gpt-5.4 (Table
11
). The other three models (Llama-4-Maverick, Llama-4-Scout and Qwen2.5-72B) succeed on 14, 2 and 1 of 240 Ultra samples, too few to support the ratio.

The responses of claude-opus-4-6 and claude-sonnet-4-6, the two models whose responses show the procedure step by step (Appendix
A.7
), show one specific behaviour that is consistent with this trend: they compute the displacement to the target, propose an L-shaped corridor of two straight runs joined by a turn, scan the object list for anything lying on that corridor, shift the corridor by a row or a column when the scan reports a blocker, and commit on the first clean scan. Depending on the level, a path between the initial state and final state that can be found by this procedure is not guaranteed to exist. Some levels, for example, require moving in the opposite direction to avoid obstacles. However, when such a corridor exists, it is near-optimal by construction, since nothing shorter joins the same endpoints. Efficiency therefore stays flat across sizes, since the models only produce corridor routes, and a corridor route is either near-optimal or invalid. The chance that no corridor is clear grows with the distance to be covered, which is consistent with the rising failure rate on the larger and denser grids.

Takeaway:

•

Success falls with plan length. The planning horizon
L_{50}
is
\approx
30 actions for Kimi-K2.5, 23–25 for the Claude models, 9 for gpt-5.4 and under 7 for the Llama and Qwen models.

•

The strongest models simulate far beyond where they can plan: past 30 actions, claude-opus-4-6 and Kimi-K2.5 produce valid plans only 22% and 36% of the time.

•

Successful plans stay near-optimal: the Claude models’ responses show them composing L-shaped corridors.

Across all models, standalone decomposition performance is high for very short missions (1–2 subgoals), where it suffices to translate the task from Baby Language into a succession of subgoals, but collapses immediately for slightly longer missions and vanishes for tasks that require ten or more subgoals (PR = 0.0% across the board at 10+). Drop
it appends whenever a mission involves picking something up (Appendix

). Therefore, it may be too restrictive to judge models solely by the PR metric. A subgoal decomposition that is not self-sufficient can still be used as a fast first guess before a slow verifier validates and completes it.

4.3.2
Assistance to a solver

The Assistance Curve Integral (ACI) measures the practical worth of such a first guess: how much of a downstream solver’s work it removes. Several works propose using LLMs as approximate solvers whose output classical deterministic algorithms verify or complete. The performance of such models when incorporated into complex mechanisms is hard to assess in general. ACI provides a simple numerical measure of this performance in
LLM-BabyBench
.

Overall (Table
5
), ACI ranges from 0.49 to 0.79 at pass@1: the best model (Kimi-K2.5, 0.79) hands a solver a plan that is most of the way there, while the weakest (Qwen2.5-72B, 0.49) leaves the solver with roughly half the work. However, a cheap approximator is most needed when the horizon is long enough that the search space is too large. Hence, ACI is most informative for long tasks, since this is where a deterministic solver grows expensive and we may want an automated model to supply a first guess that reduces the solver’s workload. Unfortunately, ACI suffers from the same decline in performance with the number of subgoals needed.

At pass@1, ACI decays monotonically with horizon for every model (Table
7
; claude-opus-4-6 falls from 0.81 at 1–2 subgoals to 0.32 at 10+, Kimi-K2.5 from 0.96 to 0.38), so the LLM’s draft is worth least to the solver on the long tasks where automation is most wanted. For short-horizon decomposition, an LLM removes most of a solver’s work and an LLM-plus-solver pipeline is attractive, but for long-horizon decomposition, today’s models do not yet provide a draft worth refining, and the solver must still do most of the search.

4.3.3
Omissions in long decompositions

The results show that models generally understand what the mission asks them to do, but fail to plan the world interactions required to execute it. Comprehension rate stays high even for missions of 10+ subgoals whereas ACI and PR collapse at that length. This is not primarily a problem of ordering: the subgoals models emit are generally in a workable order, and CR stays between 53 and 72% on the same instances (Table
6
). This behaviour is due to the models systematically omitting the execution machinery needed to accomplish tasks given, especially actions such as opening doors and clearing blocked paths (Table
40
). For example, in a BossLevel mission asking it to “pick up a green key after opening a red door and going to the blue door”, gpt-5.4 includes an
OpenSubgoal
for the red door but none for the other doors the route goes through (Appendix
A.6
).

4.3.4
Which missions the oracle can plan up front

Some levels in
LLM-BabyBench
necessitate more careful planning than others. Suppose a level requires picking up a ball, but the path towards that object is blocked by a closed red door. The model must anticipate this obstacle and plan the necessary subgoals in advance: first picking up a red key and then using it to open the door before proceeding to the target object. OmniBot uses another strategy: since it operates directly in the environment, it starts with only the main subgoal (Pick up ball) and, when it reaches the closed red door, improvises the necessary subgoals to solve the level.

Whether the level is static (OmniBot does not improvise) or not, and how many subgoals OmniBot inserts at runtime after crafting its initial plan, can be seen as another proxy for level difficulty. On the 177 cells where the oracle never improvises, PR is between 64 and 88% at pass@1 for every model. On the 69 cells where it has to add three or more subgoals of its own, PR is between 0 and 12% for six models and 44% for Kimi-K2.5, while CR stays between 70 and 87% throughout (Appendix
A.4
). Whether a mission can be planned in full by the oracle before execution begins is a strong predictor of PR, although for every model at least one of subgoal count, path length and room count correlates with PR more strongly (Table
34
), and models fail predominantly in non-static levels. Another display of this behaviour is how models perform on one-room levels. In levels of this type, since there are no doors, OmniBot can always solve the task only relying on its initial plan. In those levels, CR and PR coincide for every model but Qwen2.5-72B, so PR and ACI separate the models mainly on multi-room levels, where PR ranges from 12% (Qwen2.5-72B) to 65% (Kimi-K2.5) (Table
30
).

Takeaway:

•

Models understand the missions (CR 76–89%) but often fail to write self-sufficient plans (PR 36–72%). PR is 0% for every model at 10+ subgoals.

•

Long plans fail by omission: they leave out door openings and path clearing, mostly in missions where the oracle must improvise at runtime.

•

LLM drafts help a solver mostly on short missions.

5
Limitations

LLM-BabyBench
removes the factors that
usually accompany planning in interactive benchmarks (perception, partial observability, instruction ambiguity,
retrieval, commonsense understanding of the effects of actions, unknown dynamics), so the scores we report are not confounded by them. Some non-planning factors remain: the Precision Rate requires models to reproduce an executor convention (Appendix
A.1.7
), our definition of the
GoNextToSubgoal
argument admits two readings (Appendix
A.5
), and on the largest grids a plan must be checked against a list of up to 180 objects (Appendix
A.7
). With respect to these removed factors, the performance shown in
LLM-BabyBench
should be seen as an upper bound on the planning performance of grounded LLMs. In practice an agent must plan while it is also seeing, remembering, disambiguating and forming beliefs in a partially observable world, and there its performance would be worse than what we report here. We do not measure how much each removed factor costs a planner in practice.

The four models that expose an extended-reasoning control were run with its default setting, so our results characterise single-pass planning; test-time scaling may extend the horizons we report.
Predict
and
Plan
are also evaluated on different levels, so the comparison between simulation and planning contrasts trends rather than matched instances.

Another limitation is that BabyAI and MiniGrid have been open-source for years and the levels we use carry their original names, so we cannot rule out that models have seen descriptions of these dynamics, or expert trajectories, during training. Memorisation is a limited threat here, since instances are procedurally generated from seeds and scored by execution. The low
Plan
and
Decompose
scores at long horizons also suggest that any such exposure does not translate into strong planning performance.

6
Conclusion

We propose
LLM-BabyBench
, a fully observable textual gridworld in which perception, instruction ambiguity, world knowledge, unknown dynamics and retrieval are controlled by construction (Section
5
notes the residual non-planning factors), together with the PPD suite and an evaluation harness in which every answer is checked by execution. Relying on this environment, we show that, queried zero-shot with extended reasoning at its default setting, the strongest models cannot reliably plan in environments they simulate accurately.

In
Plan
, single-attempt success collapses as the required plan grows longer. In
Decompose
, models still understand what a mission requires well beyond the length at which they can write a complete subgoal sequence for it, and when a deterministic bot completes their drafts, the drafts save it little work on long missions. In both tasks, the reliability of plan synthesis decreases with the number of sequential decisions a plan requires, counted in primitive actions for
Plan
and in subgoals for
Decompose
. For the strongest models, the main cause of failure is therefore plan synthesis: they simulate the environment well enough to check a plan they are given. Because our setting controls the other factors listed in Section
3.1
, these scores are an upper bound on what the same models, queried under the same protocol, can be expected to achieve in grounded applications. We hope
LLM-BabyBench
can serve as a controlled testbed for studying what would let LLMs plan better.

Broader Impact Statement

LLM-BabyBench
is a synthetic, procedurally generated gridworld. It contains no personal data and involves no human subjects. Its intended use is to measure whether language models can plan when perception, instruction ambiguity, world knowledge, unknown dynamics and retrieval are controlled by construction. The main risk of misuse is to read a high score on it as evidence that a model can plan in real-world settings. The benchmark controls the factors that make real-world planning harder, so, for a given model and protocol, its scores should be read as an upper bound on real-world planning performance, not on what the model can reach with more test-time computation. Better planning in language-model agents would make autonomous agents more capable in settings where their actions have consequences; the benchmark is designed to measure this capability, not to train it, although its released expert action sequences could be used as training data, which would also contaminate the public seeds. On the positive side, a controlled measure of where and why planning fails can help practitioners decide when an agent’s plans need an external verifier before deployment.

A.1.4
Prompt Structure and Querying Protocol

All main results were obtained with a zero-shot structured prompt. Every query is a single-turn prompt built from three parts.

1.

Environment description
, produced by a structured formatter: a fixed natural-language header stating the world rules (room layout; the six available actions, namely left, right, forward, pickup, drop and toggle; the fact that only forward changes the agent’s position; that occupied cells are impassable; and the
(x,y)
coordinate convention with origin at the top-left corner), followed by a bulleted specification of the concrete instance: number and size of rooms, total grid size, the agent’s initial position and facing direction, an exhaustive list of objects with type, color, position (and lock status for doors), and the mission string.

2.

Task instruction
, specific to the benchmark task: for
Predict
, the action sequence to simulate and a request for the resulting agent state; for
Plan
, the goal of reaching the red ball and a request for an action sequence; for
Decompose
, the definitions of the five admissible subgoals (
OpenSubgoal
,
CloseSubgoal
,
PickupSubgoal
,
DropSubgoal
, and
GoNextToSubgoal (x, y)
) and a request for a chronologically ordered subgoal sequence.

3.

Format anchor
, enforcing machine-parsable output: the model must end its response with a fixed phrase followed by the answer in a rigid syntax, namely
The agent’s final state is: ((x, y), dir)
for
Predict
,
The LLM’s action sequence is: action1, action2, ...
for
Plan
, and a
<START>
…
<END>
-delimited subgoal list for
Decompose
. A deterministic parser extracts the answer from the last occurrence of the anchor.

No in-context examples, chain-of-thought scaffolding, or system prompt were used, though models were free to reason before the anchor phrase.

All models were queried at temperature
t=0.7
with
3
independent samples per (model, level, seed) cell. Reasoning models were used at their default setting. For Kimi-K2.5, the default settings seems to reason for 4-6 minutes. All runs use the first 20 primes (2–71) as seeds for every level, with moonshotai/Kimi-K2.5, Qwen/Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct, meta-llama/Llama-4-Maverick-17B-128E-Instruct-FP8 and meta-llama/Llama-4-Scout-17B-16E-Instruct queried via DeepInfra and claude-opus-4-6, claude-sonnet-4-6 and gpt-5.4 via the Anthropic and OpenAI APIs directly, with a per-level output cap of 2048, 4096 or 8192 tokens (8192 throughout for Plan; 16384 for Kimi-K2.5).Each (model, task) block comprises 16 levels or level configurations
\times

A.1.6
Why OmniBot and LLM Decompositions Are Not Compared Directly

One could evaluate the LLM’s decomposition by comparing its proposed subgoal stack with that of the OmniBot. This comparison is not fair, because the two systems produce and revise their subgoals in different ways.

The OmniBot interacts with the environment in real time and can dynamically update its subgoal stack as it gathers new information or encounters obstacles. In contrast, the LLM generates a fixed list of subgoals in advance, without the ability to revise them during execution. Since the LLM has full visibility of the grid at generation time, it is theoretically possible to anticipate all necessary steps and contingencies upfront.

Even assuming a perfect LLM with ideal reasoning capabilities, its generated subgoals will generally not match those of the OmniBot. The mismatch is caused by the LLM having to commit to a plan in advance while the OmniBot adapts as it goes, so it does not reflect the quality of the LLM’s reasoning.

Figure 4:
BabyAI GoTo level with seed 54. The task is to “go to the blue key”. This example illustrates why the OmniBot and a perfect LLM may produce different subgoal sequences.

Consider the example shown in Figure
4
, where the agent must go to the blue key. The OmniBot begins the episode with the subgoal stack:

[GoNextToSubgoal(blue key)]

As the episode progresses, the OmniBot moves toward the blue key. Upon reaching the blue door blocking the path, it appends a new subgoal:

[GoNextToSubgoal(blue key), OpenSubgoal]

After opening the door, the OmniBot removes the now-completed
OpenSubgoal
, returning the stack to its original form before completing the task.

In contrast, the LLM must anticipate the need to open the door and generate the complete subgoal sequence upfront:

[GoNextToSubgoal(blue key), OpenSubgoal, GoNextToSubgoal(blue door)]

The difference arises because the OmniBot follows a dynamic subgoal policy, while the LLM must commit to a static plan.

Therefore, a direct comparison between the LLM’s subgoals and those of the OmniBot is misleading for two reasons:

1.

The OmniBot’s subgoal stack is dynamic and evolves over time.

2.

A perfect LLM will include subgoals that are never explicitly added by the OmniBot, since they are only needed transiently and are handled reactively by the bot.

Directly matching subgoal stacks would therefore penalise even optimal LLM predictions, so such a metric is unsuitable for evaluating decomposition quality.

A.1.7
Why the OmniBot Always Appends a Drop

The OmniBot revises its own subgoal stack while an episode runs. CR allows a model to rely on these revisions, and PR does not. A typical UnblockPickup episode starts from the default expansion of the mission,

\texttt{[DropSubgoal, PickupSubgoal, GoNextToSubgoal(green ball)]},

and then grows and shrinks as the bot meets obstacles it has to clear. Partway through it is holding

[DropSubgoal, PickupSubgoal, GoNextToSubgoal(green ball),

\displaystyle\texttt{DropSubgoal, GoNextToSubgoal(20,12), PickupSubgoal]},

having decided to move a blocking object out of the doorway, and later it inserts an
OpenSubgoal
when it reaches a closed door. Each inserted subgoal is popped once satisfied, so the stack returns to its original shape before the mission completes. A model writing a static plan has to anticipate all of this in advance.

One element of the default expansion needs explanation, because it looks redundant and a model would have no reason to guess it. Whenever a mission requires picking an object up, the bot places a
DropSubgoal
at the bottom of the stack, so the agent puts down whatever it is carrying once the pickup has served its purpose. In the episode above the mission is only to pick up the green ball, and the trailing drop appears to accomplish nothing.

It is a safety mechanism, and Figure
5
shows why it is needed. The mission there is to pick up a grey ball and then go to the ball in front of the agent, before two further clauses. If the agent keeps holding the grey ball it picked up, the ball is no longer on the grid in front of it, and the second clause becomes unsatisfiable through no fault of the plan that produced it. Dropping the object as soon as it is no longer required keeps the world in a state where later clauses can still be satisfied. The consequence for our metrics is that PR requires a model to reproduce a convention of the executor rather than a property of the task, which is part of why we read a PR of zero as the absence of a self-sufficient decomposition only; the decomposition may still be usable.

Figure 5:
SynthSeq, seed 166. The mission is “pick up a grey ball and go to the ball in front of you, then go to a box and put a purple box next to the red door”. Nothing in the mission asks the agent to put the grey ball down, but an agent that keeps holding it makes the second clause impossible to satisfy.

A.1.8
The Evaluation Process

As direct comparison between subgoals generated by the LLM and those used by the OmniBot is not meaningful (as discussed above), we propose a new evaluation strategy tailored specifically to the
LLM-BabyBench
Decompose task. This procedure involves the following steps:

1.

We prompt the LLM to decompose the level’s instruction into a sequence of subgoals.

2.

The bot is initialised with this sequence of subgoals.

3.

The bot then executes an episode. During execution, we record the number of additional subgoals the bot needs to append in order to complete the task (whether by success, reaching the maximum number of steps, or depleting the subgoal stack).

4.

For each episode, we record the success status and the number of subgoals added. These values are used to compute three metrics: Comprehension Rate (CR), Precision Rate (PR), and Assistance Curve Integral (ACI).

A.1.9
What Comprehension Rate Measures

The Comprehension Rate (CR) measures the proportion of episodes successfully completed, regardless of how many subgoals the OmniBot needs to add during execution. To achieve a high CR, it suffices for the LLM to generate a plausible decomposition of the instruction into subgoals. As noted in Section
3.1
, such a decomposition usually enables the OmniBot to solve the level successfully, even if the initial stack does not account for the specific obstacles present in the environment. For example, in Figure
4
, the LLM only needs to understand that the level’s mission
go to the blue key
can be reformulated as the subgoal
GoNextTo(blue key)
to positively contribute to the CR metric.

This makes CR a relatively undemanding metric: it allows the OmniBot to freely intervene and compensate for incomplete or imprecise LLM outputs, and it does not require the LLM to reason about the environment’s layout. Rather, the LLM only needs to reformulate the instruction mechanically into a sequence of subgoals from a predefined set. For this reason, we interpret CR as a measure of the LLM’s task comprehension: its ability to understand and translate a high-level instruction into a structured goal representation.

A.1.10
What Precision Rate Measures

The
Precision Rate
(PR) quantifies the proportion of episodes successfully completed without the OmniBot needing to insert any additional subgoals during execution. A high PR indicates that the language model has fully anticipated all necessary steps and obstacles, explicitly encoding them as subgoals in its initial output.

For instance, in Figure
4
, the shortest correct decomposition is
[GoNextToSubgoal(blue key), OpenSubgoal, GoNextToSubgoal(blue door)]
, which the model must generate unaided to count toward PR.

PR is especially demanding in complex scenarios that involve multiple intermediate steps. Consider the example in Figure
6
: with OmniBot assistance, the following short sequence is enough to complete the mission:

[DropSubgoal, GoNextToSubgoal(red box), PickupSubgoal, GoNextToSubgoal(yellow ball), OpenSubgoal, GoNextToSubgoal(green door)]
.

However, completing the task without any subgoal insertion by the bot requires the model to generate a significantly longer and more detailed sequence:

[DropSubgoal, GoNextToSubgoal(red box), PickupSubgoal, GoNextToSubgoal(yellow ball), OpenSubgoal, GoNextToSubgoal(blue door), PickupSubgoal, GoNextToSubgoal(blue key), DropSubgoal, GoNextToSubgoal(drop position), OpenSubgoal, GoNextToSubgoal(green door), OpenSubgoal, GoNextToSubgoal(purple door), OpenSubgoal, GoNextToSubgoal(blue door), PickupSubgoal, GoNextToSubgoal(yellow ball), OpenSubgoal, GoNextToSubgoal(green door), OpenSubgoal, GoNextToSubgoal(grey door)]
.

Figure 6:
BabyAI BossLevel (seed 81). The task is to “open a green door, then put a yellow ball next to the red box”. This mission illustrates a clear contrast in planning complexity: with unlimited assistance from the OmniBot, a concise list of just 6 subgoals is sufficient for success, whereas achieving the same outcome without any assistance requires an extended sequence of 22 subgoals.

A.1.11
What the Assistance Curve Integral Measures

While the
Comprehension Rate
measures the success rate with unlimited help from the OmniBot, and the
Precision Rate
measures success without any assistance, the
Assistance Curve Integral
(ACI) provides a smooth interpolation between these two extremes. Rather than selecting an arbitrary threshold
k
, ACI integrates the success rate as a function of the number of subgoals added, from
0
to a predefined
limit
. This limit is
M_{\text{help}}
, the size of the OmniBot’s own from-scratch decomposition of the mission, which depends only on the level and the seed (Section
3.1
).
The integral is then normalised by dividing by
limit
, such that a perfect model achieves an ACI of 1.

A high ACI indicates that the model typically requires little assistance to solve tasks, whereas a low ACI suggests heavy reliance on OmniBot interventions.

Example bar chart of the success rate as a function of the number of subgoals added by the OmniBot. The blue area represents the unnormalised ACI.

A.5
The Coordinate Convention in
GoNextToSubgoal

Our prompt describes
GoNextToSubgoal, (x,y)
as an instruction to go next to the cell
(x,y)
. The intended argument is the target object’s own coordinate, and the executor then walks the agent to a free adjacent cell facing it. A model can also read the instruction as asking for the cell the agent should finish on, and pass an adjacent coordinate instead. The executor then places the agent next to that cell, one square too far from the object, and the
OpenSubgoal
or
PickupSubgoal
that follows has nothing in front of it. The following response is typical of the second reading:

A sample that applies “next to” twice

The green door is at (19, 7). [...] The agent can approach from (19, 8) which is south of the door [...] Let me go next to the door and open it.

(GoNextToSubgoal, (19, 8))

(OpenSubgoal)

Tables
36
and
37
give the frequency of each reading and the completion rate conditional on it. The effect is large for every model that uses the adjacent form, though it varies widely, from 11 points for Llama-4-Maverick to 52 for Llama-4-Scout.
What distinguishes claude-opus-4-6 is only how often it does so. Its aggregate Open score follows arithmetically from the mix, since
0.45\times 93\%+0.55\times 6\%\approx 45\%
, within rounding of the 44% that Table
28
reports. The same tendency appears elsewhere in its output: before a
PickupSubgoal

The pattern is the same across instances. The model computes the displacement to the target, proposes an L-shaped corridor of two straight runs joined by a turn, scans the object list for anything lying on that corridor, and shifts the corridor by a row or a column when the scan reports a blocker. It commits on the first clean scan. In the traces we examined the branching factor of this search is three or four. It is systematic, although it falls well short of a shortest-path algorithm. Successful plans of claude-opus-4-6, claude-sonnet-4-6, Kimi-K2.5, gpt-5.4 and Llama-4-Maverick run 1.28 to 1.37 times the free-space lower bound, which is an L-route plus its two or three turns. Obstacle checking in failed responses.
On the Ultra track claude-opus-4-6’s failed responses contain more obstacle checking than its successful ones, 7.9 mentions of
block
against 2.5, in 3,598 characters against 2,714. A representative failed response scans row
y{=}14

x{=}13

y{=}13

x{=}24

y{=}15

y{=}14

Nothing in these responses maintains a frontier, a set of positions already visited, or a record of which corridors have been eliminated, so when no simple corridor is clear, the procedure has no stored state to backtrack to and ends on a route it has not verified. This is consistent with the execution profile of Table
43
: the plan runs as intended for the first 20 to 55% of its actions, then meets the distractor the scan never reached, and every action after that fires from a pose the plan was not written against.

Two limits apply to this evidence. First, occurrence counts for
check
and
block
are a lexical proxy for verification rather than a measurement of it. Second, they are available only for the models whose responses carry some working text, which excludes Kimi-K2.5 and Qwen2.5-72B (Appendix
A.9
). Third, the procedural account is drawn from one response read in full, and checked against further responses with model assistance.

On this account, the models verify candidate routes but keep no record of the routes they have already ruled out, and the failure is due to this missing record. The account predicts that supplying such a record should reduce the collapse, and that a prompt asking only for more careful checking should not, since the failing responses already check more than the successful ones. We have not tested this.
