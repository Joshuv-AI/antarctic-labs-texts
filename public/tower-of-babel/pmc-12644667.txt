Identifying urban green space health priorities using asymmetric impact performance analysis in Kangbashi district Ordos China
Authors: Yiqun Yang, Jiaqi Zhang, Dong Gao, Qin Guo, Siwen Hao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUrban green spaces (UGS) are essential to improving public health, promoting well-being, and fostering sustainable urban living. While numerous studies have explored the health benefits of green spaces, few have addressed how to scientifically determine construction priorities for urban green healthy spaces (UGHS). This study investigates residents’ satisfaction with UGHS in Kangbashi District, Ordos City——a representative urban area in northern China characterized by high-standard green infrastructure and rapid development as a planned “new town” model——to identify key environmental attributes and establish improvement priorities. Using a structured questionnaire survey, we collected data on attributes related to residents’ health, well-being, and social interaction. A combination of the Delphi method and Asymmetric Impact-Performance Analysis (AIPA) was applied to evaluate both the performance and impact asymmetry of each UGHS attribute. The results revealed that residents’ satisfaction with UGHS is asymmetric, and different attributes have varying effects on satisfaction and dissatisfaction. Among the evaluated attributes, biodiversity, accessibility, number and area of activity places and facilities, and privacy were identified as low-performing but high-impact factors. These attributes should be prioritized in future UGHS development. This study demonstrates the utility of the AIPA method in green space evaluation and provides practical insights for guiding health-oriented and sustainable urban green space planning, particularly in emerging cities across northern China.Keywords: Urban green healthy space (UGHS), Residents’satisfaction, Asymmetric impact-performance analysis (AIPA), Three-factor theory, Construction prioritization, Kangbashi district, OrdosSubject terms: Sustainability, Psychology and behaviour

Introduction

Rapid urbanization processes and sustained population growth have exerted tremendous pressure on urban green spaces, while the impacts of climate change have further exacerbated the vulnerability of urban ecosystems1. Although extensive research has comprehensively demonstrated the positive effects of urban green spaces on residents’ health2–5, significant gaps remain in existing research regarding how to optimize green space attribute configurations to maximize health benefits. Particularly in rapidly developing urban areas, there is a lack of systematic theoretical guidance and empirical support for scientifically determining priorities in green space development under resource constraints6.

In this context, the core challenge facing urban planners has shifted from “how to construct high-quality urban green spaces” to “how to strategically design and prioritize the allocation of green space attributes to maximize residents’ physical and mental health benefits under limited resource conditions.” Traditional planning approaches often overlook the complex nonlinear relationships between green space characteristics and health outcomes, making it difficult to provide scientific basis for optimal resource allocation7,8.

To address this issue, this study employs Kangbashi District in Ordos, Inner Mongolia, as a case study. Based on structured questionnaire surveys and resident satisfaction data, we adopt the Asymmetric Impact-Performance Analysis (AIPA) method to explore the construction of priority strategies for health-oriented Urban Green Health Spaces (UGHS). This research focuses on addressing the following key questions:

(1) Which environmental attributes significantly influence residents’ satisfaction with UGHS?

(2) Do these attributes exhibit asymmetric characteristics in their impact on satisfaction?

(3) What is the priority ranking for developing health-oriented UGHS in Kangbashi District?

This study makes five significant contributions in terms of theoretical innovation and practical application. At the theoretical level: it introduces the AIPA method to the field of UGHS satisfaction assessment for the first time, providing a novel analytical framework for understanding the asymmetric impact mechanisms of green space attributes; it systematically reveals the asymmetric performance-satisfaction relationships of green space attributes, identifying key elements requiring priority improvement based on empirical evidence. At the methodological level: it demonstrates the systematic application pathway of environmental psychology theory in planning practice. At the practical level: it proposes targeted strategies focusing on “high-impact, low-performance” attributes, providing efficiency-maximizing solutions for green space development under resource constraints; it offers evidence-based guidance for similar cities to design and construct UGHS rationally, efficiently, and sustainably, contributing to the development of low-carbon, livable, and healthy cities.

The structure of this paper is organized as follows: Sect. 2 provides a comprehensive literature review, examining the conceptual connotations of UGHS, the evolution of assessment methods, and theoretical foundations; Sect. 3 details the characteristics of the study area, elaborates on survey design, data collection procedures, and the AIPA analytical framework; Sect. 4 presents empirical research results, including descriptive statistics, asymmetric impact analysis, and priority identification; Sect. 5 discusses in depth the theoretical and practical implications of research findings, conducts comparative analysis with existing literature, and discusses research limitations; Sect. 6 summarizes policy recommendations and outlines future research directions.

Literature review

From urban green spaces(UGS) to urban green health spaces(UGHS)

Urban Green Space (UGS) refers to artificial or semi-artificial green infrastructure that provides ecosystem services including provisioning, supporting, regulating, and socio-cultural functions for urban residents. UGS encompasses diverse types, including traditional green spaces, parks and squares, community gardens, green corridors and greenways, green roofs, façade greening, roadside green belts, protective green spaces, affiliated green spaces, as well as scenic gardens and historic sites with historical and cultural value, providing extensive ecological, recreational, aesthetic, and health benefits to cities9,10. Urban Green Health Spaces (UGHS), as a specialized subset of UGS, refers to green spaces specifically designed and managed based on evidence-based planning principles with the core objective of promoting urban residents’ physical and mental health and well-being. Compared to traditional UGS, UGHS deeply integrates interdisciplinary theories from environmental psychology, environmental behavior studies, and public health, with greater focus on spatial development guided by “One Health” concepts, providing urban residents with comprehensive health and well-being benefits across physiological, psychological, environmental, and social dimensions11,12.

Environmental psychology frameworks

The theoretical foundations of UGHS primarily derive from environmental psychology frameworks, particularly Attention Restoration Theory (ART) and Stress Reduction Theory (SRT)13. ART, proposed by Rachel Kaplan and Stephen Kaplan, posits that natural environments possess key qualities that enable cognitive abilities to recover from mental fatigue14. SRT, proposed by Roger S. Ulrich, emphasizes that natural environments can rapidly and effectively reduce psychological and physiological stress through mechanisms that trigger immediate, unconscious positive emotional responses, subsequently leading to physiological relaxation (reduced heart rate, blood pressure, and cortisol levels)15.

These theories provide a foundation for the scientific identification of specific UGHS attributes. For example, meditation spaces in urban green areas help residents escape from daily stress and cognitive burden by providing “being away” (distance from daily noise) and “extent” (sufficient immersive spatial experience). The tranquil and harmonious atmosphere they create can rapidly trigger positive emotional responses, reduce sympathetic nervous system activity, increase parasympathetic nervous system activity, thereby lowering physiological stress indicators such as heart rate, blood pressure, and muscle tension16. Biodiversity enhances environmental “extent” by providing richer “soft fascination” elements (plant forms, colors, animal species, sounds, etc.), thereby promoting cognitive restoration17.

Empirical evidence of health outcomes

Extensive literature confirms the multidimensional significant health benefits that UGHS brings to urban residents. Regarding physical health, UGHS effectively promotes physical exercise by providing accessible activity spaces, reduces obesity rates, improves cardiovascular health (lowering heart rate and blood pressure), enhances respiratory system function, and is significantly associated with reductions in all-cause and disease-specific mortality18–21. In terms of mental and psychological health, UGHS can significantly alleviate stress, anxiety, and depression symptoms, improve cognitive function, attention, and emotional states, with even brief exposure to nature providing quantifiable restorative effects22–24. At the social level, UGHS serves as an important platform for promoting social interaction, enhancing community cohesion, and providing social support, with particularly significant health improvement effects for vulnerable groups (populations with low socioeconomic status and elderly people), contributing to social equity promotion25,26.

Identified research gaps and contemporary challenges

Despite extensive research confirming the health benefits of urban green spaces, several critical gaps remain in the literature. First, existing research primarily focuses on the mechanisms of green space exposure effects (air quality improvement, physical exercise promotion, stress reduction, etc.) rather than providing actionable guidance for spatial planning and design priorities. While mechanistic research is valuable for understanding how green spaces influence health, it provides limited guidance on which specific design attributes should be prioritized under resource constraints.

Second, existing assessment frameworks are rarely specifically adapted to the unique characteristics and health promotion functions of UGHS. In resource-limited urban contexts, maximizing the efficiency of UGHS construction quality improvement becomes particularly important. Especially for non-first-tier cities, rapid development creates opportunities for innovative green space planning while facing constraints due to limited resources and land use competition. Under these circumstances, traditional approaches that treat all green space attributes equally may lead to suboptimal resource allocation, missing opportunities to maximize health benefits through strategic improvements27,28.

From traditional to asymmetric impact-performance analysis (AIPA)

User satisfaction-based assessment has received attention for its ability to capture subjective experiences that determine green space utilization and health outcomes. Satisfaction surveys can reveal gaps between design intentions and user experiences, identify underperforming attributes, and guide resource allocation decisions. However, traditional satisfaction assessment tools, particularly conventional Importance-Performance Analysis (IPA), assume symmetric relationships between attribute performance and satisfaction—an assumption increasingly challenged by consumer behavior and service quality research29,30.

Traditional IPA, developed by Martilla and James (1977), plots attributes on a two-dimensional grid based on perceived importance and performance, assuming that improvement in any attribute produces proportional increases in overall satisfaction31. Although this method has been widely applied in tourism, healthcare, urban planning, and other fields, empirical research consistently demonstrates that this symmetric assumption fails to capture the complex nonlinear relationships between attribute performance and user satisfaction32.

Asymmetric Impact-Performance Analysis (AIPA) addresses these limitations by identifying three categories of attributes based on asymmetric impact patterns: (1) Excitement factors: produce disproportionately high satisfaction when performing well but generate minimal dissatisfaction when absent; (2) Basic factors: cause significant dissatisfaction when performing poorly but produce limited satisfaction gains when exceeding expectations; (3) Performance factors: maintain symmetric satisfaction relationships33,34.

This approach, based on Kano’s three-factor theory, has been successfully applied across various fields. In healthcare settings, advanced medical technology has been proven to be an excitement factor—its presence significantly increases patient satisfaction, while its absence does not proportionally decrease satisfaction35. In restaurant evaluation, atmosphere and special menu items are excitement factors, while food safety and basic service are basic factors. In urban park assessment, aesthetic features and unique design elements are excitement factors, while basic maintenance and safety are basic factors.

The application of AIPA in green space evaluation is particularly important because certain attributes may inherently be excitement factors due to their novelty, uniqueness, or ability to exceed user expectations. For example, specialized therapeutic gardens or innovative playground facilities may produce disproportionate satisfaction when present, while their absence would not significantly detract from basic green space enjoyment. Similarly, biodiversity corridors or educational signage may serve as excitement factors, elevating experience quality beyond basic recreational needs. Understanding these asymmetric patterns enables planners to strategically prioritize investments, focusing on high-impact attributes rather than merely addressing basic deficiencies.

Integrated theoretical framework and policy implications

This study effectively addresses the aforementioned research gaps by integrating the three-factor theory (derived from the Kano model) with environmental psychology frameworks to guide survey design and result interpretation. The three-factor theory provides an analytical framework for categorizing UGHS attributes based on their asymmetric satisfaction impacts, while ART and SRT provide theoretical support for the selection and conceptualization of specific attributes. For example, survey questions about meditation spaces and quiet areas are designed based on ART’s “being away” principle, while biodiversity-related items draw upon SRT’s emphasis on natural complexity and fascination. This theoretical integration enables the research to move beyond descriptive analysis toward normative planning recommendations that are both scientifically grounded and practically applicable.

This theoretical framework has important policy implications: planners can prioritize excitement factors that produce disproportionate health benefits while ensuring basic factors meet minimum standards, rather than pursuing uniform improvement across all green space attributes. This targeted approach is particularly valuable in resource-constrained environments, where strategic allocation of limited funds can maximize population health outcomes. By identifying attributes that serve as excitement factors in local contexts, planners can concentrate investments on high-impact improvements that significantly enhance user satisfaction and health benefits, while maintaining adequate performance in basic factors to prevent dissatisfaction.

Materials and methods

Study area

Kangbashi District is located in the geographic center of Ordos City, Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region, in northern inland China. Ordos City represents a unique transformation model among China’s resource-dependent cities, transitioning from coal mining-based economy to a diversified, environmentally sustainable urban development approach36,37. This transformation context makes Kangbashi particularly representative for UGHS research, as it demonstrates how emerging cities can integrate ecological principles from their initial planning stages rather than retrofitting existing urban areas.

Kangbashi was specifically selected as the study site for several distinctive reasons that extend beyond general urbanization trends. First, as a planned city developed from scratch since 2004, it provides a controlled environment to study purpose-built green infrastructure without the constraints of pre-existing urban fabric. Second, Kangbashi represents China’s “new town” development model, which has been replicated in over 200 cities nationwide, making findings broadly applicable to similar urban contexts38. Third, the district’s rapid population growth reates a unique demographic mix of residents with diverse expectations for urban green spaces, providing rich variation in satisfaction patterns essential for AIPA methodology.

As the political and administrative hub of Ordos—one of China’s cities with the highest GDP per capita in recent years—Kangbashi exemplifies the transformation of resource-based cities into ecologically livable and health-oriented urban environments37. Since its development began in 2004, Kangbashi has rapidly grown into China’s first national demonstration district of environmental art and the first national AAAA-level urban tourism area39.

According to the latest official statistics (2023), Kangbashi has achieved a built-up area green coverage rate of 43.1%, a green space ratio of 40.38%, and a per capita park green space area of 64.1 square meters, far exceeding China’s national standard (≥ 9 m²) and well above the average of many developing countries. With 15 open-access urban parks distributed across the district, every resident can reach a green space within a 10-minute walk, highlighting a well-planned and accessible green infrastructure network40 (Fig. 1).

Kangbashi’s unique combination of emerging urban development and high standard green infrastructure makes it a representative case for studying Urban Green Healthy Spaces (UGHS). As urbanization continues and public demand for natural exposure increases, residents in Kangbashi are placing greater emphasis on the quality, accessibility, and health functions of green spaces. Therefore, selecting Kangbashi as a case study provides both practical relevance and broader applicability, offering valuable insights into the challenges and strategies of constructing UGHS in rapidly developing cities.

Survey design, data processing, and regression-based analysis

An online questionnaire was developed using the professional survey platform Wenjuanxing (https://www.wjx.cn/), and data were collected in the Kangbashi District from June to September 2023 through both on-site distribution and QR code scanning in parks, streets, squares, and greenways. The sampling strategy employed systematic spatial sampling across all 15 urban parks and major green corridors to ensure representative coverage of different UGHS types. Survey locations were selected based on visitor density patterns identified through preliminary observations, with data collection conducted during peak usage times (weekday evenings 18:00–20:00 and weekends 09:00–17:00) to capture diverse user demographics. The target sample size of 600 + was determined using confidence level of 95% and margin of error of 4%, considering Kangbashi’s adult population of approximately 100,000 residents.

To minimize the influence of seasonal variation on respondents’ perception of green spaces, representative photographs of Kangbashi’s urban green areas in all four seasons were shown to participants before completing the questionnaire.

The questionnaire consisted of two main sections. The first section collected respondents’ basic demographic and behavioral information, including gender, age, education level, frequency of park visits, and whether they had underlying health conditions. The second section assessed satisfaction with various attributes of Kangbashi’s urban green healthy spaces (UGHS) using a five-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”), to capture the degree of agreement with each attribute.

A total of 688 questionnaires were collected. After removing 231 responses that either had uniform answers across all items or were completed in under 60 s, 457 valid responses were retained for analysis. All data were processed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Reliability and validity tests were first conducted to ensure the quality of the satisfaction-related indicators. Subsequently, dummy-variable-based linear regression models were applied to compare the influence of each attribute at higher and lower levels of satisfaction. This comparative analysis of “reward” and “punishment” effects served as a foundation for the following Asymmetric Impact-Performance Analysis (AIPA).

Evaluation attributes and indicators

The selection of evaluation attributes in this study was based on a combination of literature review and expert consultation using the Delphi method41. The literature review focused on three main research streams: environmental psychology studies examining human-nature interactions in urban settings42,43, urban green space construction and planning methodologies44,45, and green infrastructure development specifically in Chinese contexts46. This review was guided by the Three-Factor Theory (satisfaction theory) to identify attributes that could function as basic, performance, or excitement factors, and nature-based healing theory to ensure inclusion of attributes supporting psychological restoration, stress reduction, and physical health promotion.

The expert consultation involved ten professionals who met specific qualification criteria: each held either a doctoral degree or a senior professional title and possessed at least five years of specialized experience in urban planning, landscape architecture, or healthy city development. Experts were selected through purposive sampling, beginning with the identification of key professionals from leading Chinese universities and planning institutes. This was followed by snowball sampling to include additional practitioners with extensive experience in green space planning under relevant environmental and cultural contexts. Three rounds of expert discussions were conducted with five professionals in urban planning, landscape design, and healthy city development. Consensus was reached on incorporating attributes related to psychological perception, physiological health, and social interaction into the evaluation framework.

Initially, a comprehensive list of attributes was proposed, including: microclimate regulation, plant species richness, green-looking ratio, greening rate, dust retention and noise reduction, biodiversity, accessibility, the number and area of activity sites and their facilities, greenways, privacy, spatial ambiance, design quality, recreational facilities, meditation spaces, and topographic features. A pilot survey was then conducted across three representative green spaces in Kangbashi (Yijin Horo Street Park, Kangbashi Central Park, and Ulan Mulun River Greenway), with 50 questionnaires systematically distributed and 46 valid responses collected. The pilot survey aimed to test attribute relevance, question clarity, and response patterns across different UGHS types.

Based on feedback from the pilot survey and expert evaluations, the attribute system was refined.、Specifically, the indicators for plant species, green-looking ratio, and greening rate were consolidated into “green-looking ratio”; the attributes “activity site quantity and area with supporting facilities” and “greenway” were merged into “number and area of activity spaces with facilities”; and “design quality” was removed due to its relatively low relevance to green and healthy spaces.

The final set of evaluation attributes comprised eleven indicators, namely:

microclimate regulation, green-looking ratio, dust retention and noise reduction, biodiversity, accessibility, number and area of activity spaces with facilities, privacy, spatial ambiance, recreational facilities, meditation spaces, and topography (Table 1). These indicators collectively reflect the ecological quality, perceptual experience, and functional service dimensions of urban green healthy spaces, providing a comprehensive framework for evaluating residents’ satisfaction with UGHS.

Asymmetric impact-performance analysis (AIPA)

To effectively identify how individual attributes of Urban Green Healthy Spaces (UGHS) influence residents’ satisfaction and to determine their construction priorities, this study adopts the Asymmetric Impact-Performance Analysis (AIPA) as the core analytical method. AIPA assumes a nonlinear and asymmetric relationship between attribute performance and overall satisfaction, and considers each attribute as an independent contributor to satisfaction47. Based on this model, attributes can be classified into three categories: basic factors, performance factors, and excitement factors.

Basic factors reflect the minimum expectations of residents. When these attributes perform poorly, satisfaction drops significantly; however, even when they perform well, the improvement in satisfaction is limited. In contrast, excitement factors substantially increase satisfaction when they perform well, but their poor performance has relatively little negative impact. Performance factors fall in between, exhibiting a relatively stable and linear influence on satisfaction47.

A higher IA value indicates a stronger positive (reward) effect, while a lower value indicates a more sensitive negative (punishment) effect. According to the value of IA, attributes are categorized as follows33,34: attributes with IA < − 0.1 are classified as basic factors, those with IA between − 0.1 and + 0.1 are considered performance factors, and attributes with IA > + 0.1 are identified as excitement factors.

This analytical framework enables evidence-based policy recommendations aligned with both theoretical frameworks: basic factors (identified through AIPA) correspond to essential green infrastructure that must meet minimum standards to prevent resident dissatisfaction, while excitement factors represent opportunities for innovative green space features that can significantly enhance wellbeing and create distinctive UGHS characteristics. The performance factors guide resource allocation for incremental improvements with predictable satisfaction returns.

This analytical framework enables a more accurate understanding of how different green space attributes influence satisfaction under varying performance levels. It also provides practical, evidence-based recommendations for priority setting and targeted improvements in UGHS development, particularly in the context of limited planning resources.

To assess the robustness of subsequent regression analyses and IA classification results, we conducted sensitivity analyses by introducing age group as a covariate. The results demonstrated that the classification of UGHS attributes remained stable, suggesting that the core structural relationships were not significantly biased by the demographic distribution. Additionally, we performed an interaction analysis between age group and accessibility. The results revealed a significant interaction effect (p < 0.001), indicating that the influence of accessibility on satisfaction was more pronounced among older respondents. This finding further reinforces the necessity of prioritizing accessibility in green space design for aging populations.

Although this group was slightly overrepresented compared to national census data, the sample still ensured category diversity across all key variables. This allowed for the robust capture of factors influencing overall satisfaction, and the slight oversampling did not pose a significant issue.

While the sample may not be perfectly representative in terms of univariate population distribution, the structural relationships among the key research variables can still be considered generalizable. Additionally, the diversity in both respondents’ frequency of visiting green spaces and self-reported health conditions further supports the validity and stability of the sample for the purposes of this study.

Reliability and validity

To assess the internal consistency of the measurement instrument, reliability analysis was conducted. The results showed a Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.921, indicating excellent internal reliability and strong stability of the questionnaire items.

In addition, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to evaluate the construct validity of the satisfaction scale. The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) value was 0.926, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (p < 0.001), indicating that the data were suitable for factor analysis. The analysis extracted a single principal component from the 11 variables, explaining 55.364% of the total variance, which meets the commonly accepted threshold for unidimensional constructs.

These results together suggest that the scale used in this study possesses both high reliability and good construct validity, supporting its applicability for further regression and AIPA analyses.

Overall satisfaction with urban green healthy spaces (UGHS)

To assess residents’ general perceptions of Urban Green Healthy Spaces (UGHS) in Kangbashi District, the overall satisfaction score was calculated based on the survey responses. The results show a mean satisfaction score of 3.97, with a median of 4.00 and a standard deviation of 0.70, indicating that the majority of respondents expressed a positive attitude toward the existing green spaces. Satisfaction scores ranged from 1.82 to 5.00, suggesting the presence of noticeable individual differences.

As shown in Fig. 2, the distribution of satisfaction scores is slightly right-skewed and concentrated between 3.5 and 4.5, implying that while the green space system is generally well received, there is still room for improvement in achieving higher levels of satisfaction and reducing dissatisfaction among certain groups.

Comparative analysis of reward and punishment effects

To examine the asymmetric influence of UGHS attributes on residents’ overall satisfaction, a comparative reward–punishment regression analysis was conducted. Two separate regression models were estimated for the high- and low-satisfaction groups, and the respective regression coefficients—reward index (βri) and punishment index (βpi) —were obtained for each attribute.

An Impact Ratio (IR) was then used to compare the relative strength of an attribute’s reward and punishment effects. Based on established thresholds, attributes were categorized into three types: excitement factors (IR ≥ 1.2), basic factors (IR ≤ 0.8), and performance factors (0.8 < IR < 1.2).

As shown in Table 3, five attributes were classified as basic factors, including microclimate regulation, biodiversity, accessibility, activity spaces and facilities, and dust retention and noise reduction. These reflect fundamental expectations among residents, whose absence tends to strongly decrease overall satisfaction.

The classification of attributes based on the Impact Asymmetry Index (IA) yielded results consistent with the previous reward–punishment analysis using the Impact Ratio (IR), indicating the robustness and reliability of the data.

A two-dimensional matrix was constructed by plotting the IA values on the vertical axis and the mean performance scores on the horizontal axis. The results presented in Table 4; Fig. 5 show that most attributes fall into either basic or performance categories, with no attributes identified as excitement factors. This indicates that current UGHS features in Kangbashi primarily provide essential or expected functions, but may lack elements that exceed expectations or create “surprise” effects for users.

In terms of distribution, biodiversity, accessibility, and the number and area of activity spaces and their facilities were classified as basic factors with relatively low performance, suggesting that they should be prioritized for improvement. In contrast, microclimate regulation, although also a basic factor, received a relatively high mean score, indicating that it currently meets resident expectations and can be considered a “satisfactory basic” feature.

Among performance factors, privacy stood out as an attribute with relatively low performance and should be targeted for improvement through better spatial structure and design. Other performance attributes, including topography, green-looking ratio, spatial ambiance, dust retention and noise reduction, facilities, and meditation space, all showed relatively high performance and can be considered existing strengths that should be maintained in future planning.

As illustrated in Fig. 5, the AIPA matrix clearly visualizes the positioning of each attribute along the two dimensions of impact type and performance level, offering practical insights for targeted and differentiated improvements in UGHS planning. In summary, the integrated use of sensitivity analysis, interaction modeling, VIF diagnostics, and heteroskedasticity correction techniques confirms that the study’s results are statistically robust and applicable to planning for diverse population groups, including the elderly and frequent park users.

Discussion

Pathways through which UGHS attributes influence residents’ satisfaction

This study revealed a notable asymmetric effect in the relationship between urban green healthy space (UGHS) attributes and residents’ satisfaction. That is, different attributes do not influence satisfaction and dissatisfaction equally. Based on the three-factor theory of satisfaction and the findings from the AIPA analysis, a conceptual pathway model was developed to illustrate how environmental attributes impact UGHS satisfaction (Fig. 6).

Conceptual pathway model of how UGHS attributes influence residents’ satisfaction.

According to this model, residents interact with UGHS primarily through two pathways: (1) behavioral engagement, including passing through and staying activities, and (2) visual perception of natural elements. Various UGHS attributes—categorized as basic, performance, or excitement factors—affect residents’ satisfaction through multiple sensory dimensions, such as sight, sound, smell, touch, and affective perception.

Among these, basic factors trigger dissatisfaction only when underperforming; performance factors exert a more balanced, linear influence on both satisfaction and dissatisfaction; and excitement factors, if present, can significantly enhance satisfaction when exceeding expectations. In this study, no excitement factors were identified in Kangbashi, which may explain the relatively high but non-exceptional satisfaction levels among residents. Further studies are needed to determine whether this absence of excitement factors also occurs in cities with lower overall satisfaction.

The observed asymmetry stems from Kangbashi’s unique context as a resource-based city in an arid climate. Unlike humid southern cities like Chengdu where biodiversity often functions as a performance factor, Kangbashi’s historical resource dependency and water scarcity intensify residents’ sensitivity to ecological services. Therefore, residents regard biodiversity as an essential compensation for past environmental deficits—which explains why it is classified as a basic factor rather than a performance factor. This contextual distinction is crucial for understanding how geographic and climatic factors influence residents’ expectations and satisfaction patterns across different urban environments.

Specifically, the analysis showed that microclimate, biodiversity, accessibility, and the number and area of activity spaces and facilities are basic factors that reflect residents’ essential expectations for UGHS. These should be prioritized in future green space planning. Meanwhile, spatial experience attributes such as privacy, topography, green-looking ratio, meditation space, spatial ambiance, and environmental service attributes including facilities, and dust retention and noise reduction were identified as performance factors. These attributes have a dual nature: they can either enhance or diminish satisfaction depending on their performance. As performance factors influence satisfaction in a relatively stable and linear manner, improving them is also important for enhancing the user experience.

This study’s identified urban green space health priorities have a significant corroborative and supplementary relationship with conclusions from a broader range of academic literature. For example, the findings that accessibility, and activity spaces and facilities are “basic factors” align with a general consensus that these attributes are core fundamentals of health-oriented urban green spaces49–51. Literature suggests that close-proximity accessibility to urban green spaces is directly linked to residents’ physical activity, mental health, and quality of life51,52, and is particularly important for the elderly and vulnerable groups51,52. Furthermore, the quality and maintenance of facilities are proven to be key factors in encouraging physical activity and social interaction among adults50. However, the unique finding that biodiversity is a “basic factor” in Kangbashi reveals its specific function in the context of an arid, resource-based city, which differs from some literature that views biodiversity as an independent conservation objective to be weighed against urban services53,54. This finding underscores that urban green space planning must consider the city’s specific socio-ecological history. Concurrently, the conclusion that privacy is a “performance factor” is consistent with Attention Restoration Theory and Stress Reduction Theory, indicating that providing perceived dimensions like “serenity” and “refuge” is crucial for psychological restoration, and that these attributes can significantly enhance the user experience rather than merely meeting basic needs.These comparisons collectively show that while there are universal principles in healthy green space planning, the specific type and importance of priorities require a deep satisfaction analysis tailored to the city’s particular social and environmental context.

Comparative analysis across urban contexts

To contextualize our findings within the broader landscape of urban green space research, we compared our results with studies from different urban contexts. For economically developed, densely populated megacities such as Beijing and Shanghai, where social interaction needs are strong and residents have high demands for activity spaces, diverse facilities, and privacy, the focus should be on optimizing spatial layouts and building multi-level and multi-functional UGHS networks. While ensuring accessibility, spatial quality should be improved through microclimate regulation and landscape design to alleviate pressure from high-density environments.

For cities with profound historical and cultural heritage, such as Xi’an and Nanjing, cultural inheritance and green space construction need to be organically integrated. Cultural elements should be incorporated into biodiversity protection and spatial ambiance creation to build green spaces that combine health functions with cultural identity, meeting residents’ pursuit of spiritual and cultural life.

For relatively remote, ecologically fragile resource-based cities like Ordos, priority should be given to addressing basic needs such as biodiversity and accessibility, building drought-resistant ecological corridors, balancing resource input and residents’ health needs, and exploring sustainable green development paths.

Identification and prioritization of low-performing attributes

The analysis further identified that essential functional attributes (biodiversity, accessibility, activity facilities)spatial experience attribute (privacy) are the lowest-performing attributes influencing satisfaction in Kangbashi UGHS. These attributes should be considered priority targets for improvement in future development plans.

According to the three-factor model, both low-performing basic and performance factors contribute to resident dissatisfaction. These attributes typically fall within the realm of residents’ expectations and represent essential functional needs. As such, they hold a higher weight in satisfaction judgment and should be prioritized in UGHS upgrades.

It is important to note that basic factors tend to have a stronger negative impact on satisfaction when underperforming, compared to performance factors. If left unaddressed, they may trigger stronger dissatisfaction and erode overall satisfaction levels. Therefore, in the future development of UGHS in Kangbashi, priority should first be given to low-performing basic factors—namely biodiversity, accessibility, and activity facilities—followed by low-performing performance factors, such as privacy. And we can use professional planning tools to improve these findings. For the enhancement of biodiversity, we could introduce native drought-tolerant species (e.g., Artemisia ordosica) in habitat corridors, increasing ecological resilience while reducing irrigation needs by 30%. For accessibility optimization: implement hierarchical path networks with shaded rest areas at 200 m intervals, prioritizing connections to transit nodes. For privacy solutions: use landform modulation (1.2–1.8 m berms) and layered vegetation instead of fences to avoid social segregation. For activity facilities: develop modular spaces accommodating intergenerational activities.

Based on the national urban classification from social, cultural, and economic perspectives, different types of cities should adopt differentiated strategies in the planning and development of Urban Green Healthy Spaces (UGHS). For economically developed, densely populated megacities such as Beijing and Shanghai, where social interaction needs are strong and residents have high demands for activity spaces, diverse facilities, and privacy, efforts should focus on optimizing spatial layouts, building multi-level and multi-functional UGHS networks. While ensuring accessibility, spatial quality should be improved through microclimate regulation and landscape design to alleviate the pressure from high-density environments. For cities with profound historical and cultural heritage, such as Xi’an and Nanjing, cultural inheritance and green space construction need to be organically integrated. Cultural elements should be incorporated into biodiversity protection and spatial ambiance creation to build green spaces that combine health functions with cultural identity, meeting residents’ pursuit of spiritual and cultural life. For relatively remote, ecologically fragile resource-based cities like Ordos, priority should be given to addressing basic needs such as biodiversity and accessibility, building drought-resistant ecological corridors, balancing resource input and residents’ health needs, and exploring sustainable green development paths.

This also provides enlightenment for urban policymakers. In Kangbashi, basic factors can be influenced by mandating the proportion of local species, implementing 5-, 10-, and 15-minute walking accessibility standards, and guiding the construction of theme-based functional activity spaces. Performance factors of the city can be influenced by formulating the Guidelines for Urban Green Space Activity Spaces in Ordos, which put forward implementable suggestions for the elevation design, plant proportion, and spatial design of urban green spaces, thereby helping to improve urban governance and stimulate urban cultural connotations. Excitement factors can be influenced by encouraging urban builders to incorporate more innovative, smart, and green methods into the construction of urban green spaces through means such as tax reductions and exemptions.

Limitations and future prospects

The analysis further identified that biodiversity, accessibility, and the number and area of activity spaces and their facilities—all basic factors—along with privacy—a performance factor—are the lowest-performing attributes influencing satisfaction in Kangbashi UGHS. These attributes should be considered priority targets for improvement in future development plans.

This study focused primarily on UGHS attributes from the perspectives of environmental psychology and urban landscape planning. However, socioeconomic dimensions—particularly income-mediated access disparities—were not incorporated. Future research should integrate equity metrics using Gini coefficients of green space distribution. Additionally, while AIPA identifies attribute categories, it cannot capture synergistic effects (e.g., how biodiversity enhancement amplifies microclimate regulation). Hybrid methods like structural equation modeling (SEM) could unravel these interactions.

Moreover, while AIPA provides a practical framework for classifying attributes based on asymmetric effects, it does not reveal the precise functional relationships between each attribute and overall satisfaction. Future studies could adopt non-linear modeling or structural equation modeling to better understand these mechanisms.

Finally, the current study was conducted in Kangbashi District, a newly developed urban area with relatively high levels of infrastructure and green space investment. The findings may not fully reflect conditions in cities with lower levels of urban development. Future research should explore comparative studies across different types of cities to examine the generalizability of asymmetric influence patterns.

Conclusions

Urban Green Healthy Spaces (UGHS) are vital for promoting public health and well-being in rapidly urbanizing regions. This study demonstrates that residents’ satisfaction with UGHS is shaped by asymmetric effects, where unmet basic needs such as biodiversity, accessibility, activity facilities, and privacy exert greater influence on overall evaluations than well-performing attributes.

By applying Asymmetric Impact-Performance Analysis (AIPA) to the case of Kangbashi, the research highlights a practical and theory-informed framework for identifying priorities in green space planning. The absence of “excitement factors” indicates that while current provisions meet essential expectations, opportunities remain for innovation and the creation of exceptional experiences.

The study’s contribution lies in bridging theoretical satisfaction models with actionable strategies for resource-constrained urban contexts. Its insights are particularly relevant to emerging cities with arid climates and rapid urban growth, offering transferable guidance for building low-carbon, equitable, and health-oriented urban futures.
