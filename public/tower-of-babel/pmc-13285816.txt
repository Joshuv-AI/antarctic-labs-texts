Impact of BMI on Two-year Outcomes Following Meniscal Repairs and Meniscectomies in Pediatric and Adolescent Patients: A Nationwide Cohort Analysis☆
Authors: Priya L. Jayakumar, Rayyan Abid, Robert J. Burkhart, Jeremy M. Adelstein, Logan M. Good, R. Justin Mistovich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe prevalence of meniscus injuries and subsequent reconstructions is increasing. Youth obesity has also increased and may put patients at a higher risk of post-operative complications.Hypothesis/purposeOur aim was to assess post-operative medical and orthopaedic outcomes as well as healthcare utilization following meniscus repairs and meniscectomies in pediatric and adolescent patients. We hypothesized that patients with a higher BMI would exhibit higher rates of post-operative complications.MethodsThe TriNetX database was queried for patients 21 years of age or younger with a history of meniscus repairs or meniscectomies, yielding 8,355 patients. Three analyses were performed comparing patients with a BMI = 18.5-24.9 (healthy weight) to BMI ≤ 18.5 (underweight), BMI = 25-30 (overweight), and BMI = 30-35 (obese). Frequency of medical and orthopaedic complications as well as healthcare utilization was analyzed at 30, 90, and 180 days. Orthopaedic complications were also assessed at 2 years post-operatively. After Bonferroni correction, P < .001 was considered statistically significant.ResultsRates of most medical and orthopaedic complications were low in the overall cohort, never exceeding 0.43% and 1.22%, respectively. Prevalence of ED visits peaked at 4.21% at 180-day follow-up. The overall rate of revision meniscal surgery within 2-years was 9.08%. All medical outcomes and healthcare utilization rates were comparable between BMI cohorts.ConclusionsRegardless of a patient's BMI, meniscus repairs and meniscectomies are generally safe in young patients.Key Concepts(1)Meniscus repairs and meniscectomies are safe procedures for children.(2)Body mass index does not impact post-operative medical outcomes.(3)Unlike adults, obesity does not impact post-operative orthopaedic outcomes and rates of revision surgery.Level of EvidenceIII

Introduction

Meniscal tears are one of the most common knee injuries in children [1]. The prevalence of meniscal injuries has risen, potentially due to earlier and increased involvement in organized sports, particularly those with high-energy contact and frequent pivoting actions [2,3]. Most pediatric meniscal tears require arthroscopic repair, resection, or, in rare cases, replacement [2]. Between 2007 and 2011, meniscal repairs and partial meniscectomies increased by 55% and 38%, respectively, among patients aged 5-19 years old [4].

In recent years, obesity rates in children and adolescents have also increased, becoming a major public health concern [5,6]. An elevated BMI leads to more momentum traveling through the knee and accordingly increases the risk of meniscal injury [2]. Additionally, in pediatric and adolescent patients, a higher BMI has been shown to lead to more complex meniscal tears [7]. Obesity has also been associated with worse clinical outcomes following a variety of meniscus procedures in adult patients, including partial meniscectomy, meniscal resection, and posterior medial meniscus root repair, with an increased incidence of clinical osteoarthritis, decreased post-operative knee function, and an increased risk of a subsequent knee surgery in obese patients [[8], [9], [10]].

However, current literature investigating the impact of elevated BMI on post-operative outcomes following meniscal repair and resection primarily involves adult patient cohorts. To our knowledge, there exists a paucity of data regarding outcomes following meniscal procedures in the pediatric and adolescent population. Therefore, we designed a study to investigate the relationship between obesity and medical and orthopaedic complications following meniscal repair or meniscectomy in children utilizing a large, nationwide database. We hypothesized that, similar to adults, as BMI increases in pediatric and adolescent patients, the risk of post-operative complications will also increase after meniscal surgery.

Methods

Data source

Data for this cohort study were collected from the TriNetX Collaborative Research Platform. This database comprises information from over 100 healthcare organizations and contains more than 120 million patients, as of November 2025. All data are deidentified and adheres to guidelines regarding the minimum thresholds for reporting patient data. As a result, per Section §164.514(b) (1) of the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) Privacy Rule, studies involving the TriNetX database are exempt from Western Institutional Review Board approval. Data is organized by insurance billing codes, including Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) and International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes, which were used to identify the study population.

Patient selection

As of November 2025, there were 160,270,119 patients in the TriNetX database. Of the total population in network, 29,963,227 individuals were 21 years of age or younger. We queried for CPT codes 29880, 29,881, 29,882, and 29,883 to identify patients who have undergone either a meniscus repair or meniscectomy, resulting in a total of 433,637 candidate patients. Patients who underwent ACL reconstructions, tibial spine fracture internal fixations, extra-articular ligament reconstructions, osteochondral autograft or allograft procedures (OATS), or autologous chondrocyte implantation (ACI) were excluded. The overall study population ultimately included 8,355 patients. Four cohorts were created for a sub-analysis to compare patients with a low BMI ≤18.5 (underweight), normal BMI of 18.5-24.9 (healthy weight), high BMI of 25-30 (overweight), and very high BMI of 30-35 (obese). All cohorts were 1:1 propensity matched for age at index surgery, sex, race, and medical comorbidities such as primary hypertension, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease.

Patient demographics

Demographic information for the overall study population was collected from the TriNetX platform and is described in Table 1. Mean age at index surgery was 15.1 ± 2.92. The patient population consisted of 3,055 females (36.56%), 5,294 males (63.56%), and 10 of unknown sex (0.11%) (Table 1). Approximately 60% of the population was Caucasian, 16% Black or African American, 3% Asian, 1% Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, and 1% American Indian or Alaskan Native. The remaining 19% of patients were of other or unknown race. Outcome measures

Medical and orthopaedic complications as well as healthcare utilization were assessed. Medical complications included surgical site infection (SSI), deep vein thrombosis (DVT), and sepsis. Healthcare utilization included post-operative emergency department (ED) visits and ICU admissions. Orthopaedic outcomes that were evaluated included return to the OR (RTOR), manipulation under anesthesia (MUA), lysis of adhesions, irrigation and debridement (I&D), arthrofibrosis (by querying for knee ankylosis and contractures), and implant/graft complications. Revision meniscal repairs and meniscectomies were analyzed by querying for instances of meniscal repairs or resections following the index surgery.

In addition to overall RTOR rates for each BMI cohort, a sub-analysis was performed that divided each BMI cohort by patients who underwent a primary meniscus repair versus primary meniscectomy. Return to OR rates for repairs and meniscectomies were then analyzed based on the primary procedure.

Occurrence rates of each outcome were evaluated at 30, 90, and 180-day follow-up as well as 2 years post-operatively. Each outcome was identified by querying for the appropriate CPT and ICD-10 codes, as outlined in Supplemental Table 1.

Statistical analysis

The TriNetX platform was utilized for all data analysis. The patient's age at index surgery and population BMI was reported as a mean ± standard deviations (SD). All other variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. To protect patient privacy, frequencies of n ≤ 10 were rounded up to 10 by the TriNetX database. In all sub-analyses, complication rates were compared using odds ratios (ORs) and risk difference P-values. A Bonferroni correction was applied to P < .05, resulting in P < .001 being considered statistically significant.

BMI = 18.5-24.9 (healthy weight) vs. BMI = 30-35 (obese) outcomes

There were originally 833 patients in the obese cohort and 2,941 healthy weight patients. After propensity matching, 815 individuals were in each cohort. There were no statistically significant differences among orthopaedic complications between both cohorts. Within 2 years, overall revision meniscus repair rates were comparable between groups: 2.82% (22/815) among the healthy weight cohort and 2.31% (18/815) among the obese cohort. Revision meniscectomies were slightly higher in the healthy weight group at 6.02% (47/815) compared to the obese cohort at 3.59% (28/815), but this difference was not significant (OR 1.722 [1.067, 2.78], P = .02). When analyzing RTOR rates further by primary procedure, no statistically significant difference was observed between cohorts.

At 90-day follow-up, ED visit rates for the healthy weight and obese cohorts were 2.84% (15/815) and 2.58% (21/815), respectively. All other medical complications were not observed throughout the follow-up period or had too few instances to be reported.

Discussion

Our multi-center retrospective analysis found that meniscal repairs and meniscectomies are safe procedures for children and adolescents with low frequencies of post-operative complications regardless of the patient's BMI. Risk of repeat meniscectomy of the knee was slightly elevated in the high BMI cohort, but this difference was not statistically significant. Despite higher BMI being associated with more complex meniscus tears, we demonstrated no significant differences in rates of revisions procedures, medical complications, orthopaedic complications, or ED visits due to BMI [7].

Medical complications

Serious medical complications, including SSI and DVT, were rare in the overall cohort throughout short-term follow-up with prevalence never exceeding 0.5%. While little data exists regarding these trends among children, this is congruent with past studies of meniscus procedures in the adult population. For example, in a sample of 6,354 meniscus repairs and 99,372 meniscectomies, Lai et al. found that the prevalence of venous thromboembolism, superficial SSI, and deep SSI also did not exceed 0.5% in either cohort at 30-day follow-up [11]. Sochacki et al. demonstrated similarly low rates of sepsis, DVT, and infection in a sample of 22,064 meniscectomies and 5,516 meniscal repairs at 30 days [12].

Our analysis demonstrated no significant difference in medical complications when comparing patients with normal BMI vs low, high, and very high BMI. Our sub-analysis comparing patients with BMI less than 25 and greater than 30 also showed no significant differences. This is mostly consistent with past studies of adult samples. For example, in a controlled regression analysis of five cohorts with BMIs ranging between 25 and 40, Ford et al. found that there were no significant differences in rates of complications 30 days after meniscectomy or meniscus repair. These complications included superficial and deep SSI, pneumonia, UTI, acute renal failure, cardiac arrest, DVT, and sepsis [13]. Basques et al. used multivariate analysis to demonstrate that there were no significant differences in severe adverse events between patients with BMI less than 25 and patients with BMI 25-30, 30-35, and 35-40 [14].

Orthopaedic complications

Few studies have analyzed rates of postoperative complications following meniscus repair and meniscectomy in the pediatric population. However, we found that the prevalence of complications was low in the overall cohort throughout the follow-up period, and this aligns with past studies of outcomes following meniscus repair and meniscectomy in the adult population. For example, at 2-year follow-up, 0.14% of patients underwent MUA, and arthrofibrosis was rare at 1.22%. Werner et al. found that, at 6-month follow-up, 0.30% of 7,418 patients with meniscus repair and 0.11% of 219,964 patients with partial meniscectomy experienced MUA [15]. Lysis of adhesions was also rare, occurring in 0.20% of meniscal repair patients and 0.06% of partial meniscectomy patients compared to 0.22% in our own sample [15]. Graft complications were also rare, occurring in 0.67% of patients.

Compared to past studies, our sample demonstrated low rates of revision surgeries at medium-term follow-up with 5.58% of patients undergoing meniscectomy and 3.5% undergoing meniscus repair at 2-year follow-up. In a sample of 129 meniscus repairs, 149 partial meniscectomies, and 46 discoid saucerizations, Shieh et al. found that 13% required a revision procedure at a mean 40-month follow-up [16]. Similarly, in a systematic review with 301 meniscus repairs in patients less than 18 years old, Liechti et al. found a revision rate of 17.3% at a mean 16.6-month follow-up [17].

We found no significant difference in orthopaedic complications or revision surgeries based on BMI at any point of follow-up. These trends are reflected in past studies of the adult population. In a retrospective study of 100 patients with BMI less than 25 and 116 patients with BMI greater than 25 who underwent meniscus repair, Sommerfeldt et al. found no difference in rates of failure at mean 19-month follow-up [18]. However, they had an insufficient number of patients to comment on BMI greater than 35 [18]. In a sample of 59,450 meniscectomies and 4,773 meniscal repairs, Reiter et al. also found no significant difference in rates of any adverse outcomes at 30-day follow-up [19]. Finally, we found that, among patients who underwent primary meniscectomy, twice as many later received a revision meniscectomy than a meniscus repair in both the healthy and overweight cohorts, but this difference was also not significant.

Healthcare utilization

There were less than 10 instances of ICU admission by 180-day follow-up. The prevalence of ED visits, however, was 4.21% at 180-days years. Past studies have primarily described short-term rates of hospital readmission following meniscus procedures in the adult population. For example, Lai et al. demonstrated 0.9% and 0.8% readmission rate at 30-day follow-up for meniscal repair and meniscectomy, respectively [11].

There were no significant differences in rates of ED visits or ICU admissions when comparing normal BMI with low, high, and very high BMI at any point of follow-up. This is consistent with past studies of hospital readmission following meniscus procedures. Using a multivariate analysis of risk factors following meniscectomy or meniscus repair, Reiter et al. found that BMI was not a significant risk factor for unplanned hospital readmission following either procedure at 30-day follow-up. Basques et al. compared patients with BMI less than 25 vs BMI 25-30, BMI less than 25 vs BMI 30-35, and BMI less than 25 vs BMI of at least 35 using univariate analysis and determined that there were no significant differences in odds of hospital readmission [14]. Interestingly, Ford et al. demonstrated that Class I obesity was lower rates of readmission following arthroscopic meniscus repair or meniscectomy [13].

Limitations

This study has a number of limitations characteristic of analyses relying on large, multi-center databases. For example, patient selection for this sample is biased towards hospital systems participating in the TriNetX collaborative database, and variation in documenting health issues among these institutions may lead to inconsistencies in reporting. This may impact the study's external validity. Further, propensity matching was used when comparing cohorts, but this reduces sample size and may have limited the ability to detect small differences in medical outcomes. Reliance on ICD-10 and CPT codes also obscures more detailed patient information and characteristics, such as injury morphology, found in studies that analyze individual patients. The TriNetX database also does not capture patient-reported outcomes (PROMs) or other clinical measures of knee function. Thus, our study was unable to include any analysis of pain levels, functional status, or other quality of life metrics. Future studies should aim to prospectively enroll patients and incorporate subjective measures of pain, function, and quality of life when evaluating the impact of BMI on post-surgical outcomes. Additionally, evaluating complication rates by sex would be a worthwhile future study. Regardless, this study offers a robust, multi-center report of outcomes following meniscus repair and meniscectomy in the pediatric population, comparing these outcomes among multiple BMI subgroups.

Conclusions

Pediatric and adolescent patients who undergo meniscus repair or meniscectomy experience favorable outcomes with low rates of post-operative medical complications, orthopaedic complications, and ED visits. Although, at 2-year follow-up, overall revision repair rates were high at 9.08%. Despite previous associations of higher BMI with more complex forms of meniscus injury, we demonstrated no significant differences in any post-operative outcomes due to BMI.
