Adsorption behavior and mechanism of methyl green on synthesized MoS2 nanosheets
Authors: Juan A. Ramos-Guivar, Melissa-Alisson Mejía-Barraza, Yamerson Canchanya-Huaman, Mercedes del Pilar Marcos-Carrillo, Renzo Rueda-Vellasmin, José Moreno-Tarazona, Noemi-Raquel Checca-Huaman, Bruno L. ABSTRACT
Two-dimensional molybdenum disulfide (MoS2) is a promising adsorbent for wastewater treatment because of its layered structure, tunable surface chemistry, and accessible edge sites. Here, commercial MoS₂ (MoS₂-C) and hydrothermally synthesized MoS₂ nanosheet assemblies (MoS₂-S) were comparatively evaluated for methyl green (MG) adsorption. MoS₂-S exhibited improved colloidal stability, near-complete MG removal (> 98%) across pH 2–12, and an operational equilibrium time of approximately 15 min. The kinetic data were best described by the pseudo-first-order model for MoS₂-C and by the pseudo-second-order model for MoS₂-S; these fits were treated as empirical descriptions rather than direct mechanistic evidence. The MoS₂-S equilibrium data were most parsimoniously described by the Langmuir model, with a maximum adsorption capacity of 7.1 × 102 mg g−1. Charge-corrected XPS revealed preservation of the dominant Mo4+ component after adsorption, together with the appearance of Mo5+, an increased Mo6+ fraction, modest changes in oxygen- and sulfur-related components, and MG-derived C–N signals. These results support a mixed adsorption mechanism, with predominantly non-covalent uptake on MoS₂-C and a stronger defect-associated, site-specific contribution on MoS₂-S. Box–Behnken optimization produced a significant model (R2 = 0.925; adjusted R2 = 0.884) with non-significant lack of fit and identified adsorbent dose as the principal operational factor.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11671-026-04859-1.

Introduction

The textile industry releases substantial quantities of synthetic dyes into aquatic environments through processing effluents [1, 2]. Cationic dyes are particularly problematic because of their persistence and potential ecotoxicity [3]. Methyl green (MG; C₂₆H₃₃Cl₂N₃) is a basic, dicationic triphenylmethane dye whose positive charge favors interaction with negatively charged surfaces. Its extended aromatic structure limits biodegradation and contributes to its persistence in water [2–4].

Several approaches have been investigated for MG removal. Photocatalytic systems based on CuSe/graphene oxide, CdFe₂O₄, and Fe(II)-o-phenanthroline supported on zeolite Y achieved removal efficiencies ranging from 45.4% to nearly 100%, but required irradiation times of 45–180 min [5–7]. Ultrafiltration membranes have also been evaluated, with performance dependent on membrane composition and operating conditions [8, 9]. Peroxielectrocoagulation can achieve very high removal, although its energy demand and need for electrochemical equipment may limit large-scale application [10]. By contrast, adsorption generally requires less energy and simpler equipment, making it an attractive option for treating dye-contaminated effluents [11].

Adsorbents investigated for MG include activated carbons derived from pig hooves and corn cobs [12, 13], almond shells [14], activated Dodonaea viscosa [15], chitosan derivatives [16–20], Fe₃O₄/sawdust/multiwalled carbon nanotube composites [4], and alginate-based hydrogels [21]. Although several of these materials exhibit high removal efficiencies or adsorption capacities, their practical use may be constrained by narrow optimal-pH ranges, long equilibrium times, swelling, or limited structural stability. These limitations motivate the development of robust adsorbents that combine rapid uptake, high capacity, and tolerance to variable water chemistry.

Two-dimensional materials have attracted considerable attention because of their layered structures and abundant edge and surface sites [22]. Molybdenum disulfide (MoS₂), in particular, offers sulfur-rich surfaces, tunable interfacial chemistry, and affinity for diverse pollutants [23, 24]. Bulk 2H-MoS₂ consists of S–Mo–S layers held together by Van der Waals forces and has an indirect band gap of approximately 1.2 eV, whereas few-layer MoS₂ exhibits a direct band gap near 1.8 eV and a higher proportion of accessible edge sites [25–27]. These characteristics have supported applications in photocatalysis [28, 29] and adsorption. For example, MoS₂-based materials have achieved adsorption capacities near 200 mg g−1 for methylene blue and retained more than 80% effectiveness over five reuse cycles [30]. MoS₂ has also shown greater affinity for cationic dyes than for anionic dyes [22].

Although MoS₂-based materials have previously been investigated for the adsorption of cationic dyes such as methylene blue and rhodamine B, the relationship between the structural and colloidal characteristics of MoS₂ and its interaction with methyl green remains insufficiently understood. MG constitutes a distinct adsorption probe because it is a bulky dicationic triphenylmethane dye with an extended aromatic structure and multiple nitrogen-containing positively charged groups. These molecular characteristics make its adsorption particularly sensitive to the surface charge, accessibility, dispersion state, and defect chemistry of the adsorbent. Accordingly, the novelty of the present study does not rely solely on applying MoS₂ to a different dye, but on establishing how the transformation of commercial multilayer MoS₂ into hydrothermally synthesized nanosheet assemblies modifies its MG adsorption behavior. Commercial MoS₂ (MoS₂-C) and synthesized MoS₂ nanosheets (MoS₂-S) were therefore comparatively investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), STEM-EDS, dynamic light scattering (DLS), ζ-potential measurements, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and Attenuated Total Reflectance Fourier Transform Infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectroscopy. This integrated approach was used to correlate morphology, colloidal stability, surface charge, defect density, and oxygen-containing surface species with adsorption kinetics, equilibrium behavior, and pH tolerance. MoS₂-S exhibited improved dispersion, a persistently negative surface charge, and a defect-rich, oxygen-functionalized surface, resulting in near-complete MG removal within minutes, high adsorption efficiency over pH 2–12, and a Langmuir maximum capacity of approximately 710 mg g−1.

Thus, the present work provides incremental mechanistic understanding by demonstrating that the enhanced MG uptake is governed by the combined effects of nanosheet accessibility, colloidal stabilization, electrostatic attraction, and defect-associated interfacial interactions. Response surface methodology (RSM) based on a Box-Behnken design (BBD) was additionally employed to quantify the effects and interactions of the principal operational variables.

Experimental

Materials and chemicals

All chemicals were of analytical grade and used as received. The following reagents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Mexico: ammonium heptamolybdate tetrahydrate ((NH4)6Mo7O24·4H2O, ACS reagent 81–83%), thiourea ((NH2)2CS, reagent plus ≥ 99%), and ethanol (Sigma Aldrich ≥ 99.5%). Hydrochloric acid (HCl, 36–38% RA/ACS) was obtained from CTR Scientific Mexico.

Commercial reference and hydrothermal synthesis of MoS₂-S

Commercial MoS₂ powder (Sigma-Aldrich Mexico; Reference Number 69860; CAS 1317-33-5) was selected as the bulk reference material and used without additional purification. This sample is denoted hereafter as MoS₂-C. The nanostructured MoS₂ sample, referred to as MoS₂-S, was prepared through a two-step hydrothermal protocol that combined microwave-assisted heating with subsequent conventional hydrothermal treatment. Briefly, 1 mmol of (NH₄)₆Mo₇O₂₄·4H₂O and 21 mmol of (NH₂)₂CS were dissolved in 60 mL of deionized water (18 MΩ·cm) and stirred continuously for 1 h to obtain a homogeneous precursor solution. The pH was then adjusted to 2 with diluted HCl. The mixture was placed in a Teflon vessel and treated in a CEM MARS-6 microwave-assisted hydrothermal system at 220 °C for 1 h under magnetic stirring, using 600 W and 800 psi. The resulting suspension was transferred to a digestion bomb and further heated at 220 °C for 8 h with a temperature ramp of 1 °C min−1. After the reaction, the system was cooled naturally to room temperature. The black solid product was collected by centrifugation at 4500 rpm for 1 h, washed five times with a 1:1 water/ethanol mixture, and finally dried under vacuum at 80 °C for 24 h. The reaction yield was calculated to be 91%.

Material characterization

The crystalline structure of the powder samples was examined using a Rigaku Ultima IV diffractometer operating in Bragg–Brentano geometry with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å), an accelerating voltage of 40 kV, and a current of 30 mA. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were recorded over a 2θ range of 10–80°, using a step size of 0.02° and a counting time of 8 s per point. The diffraction peaks were indexed according to hexagonal 2H-MoS₂ using the standard diffraction data for MoS₂ (JCPDS No. 37–1492) and the reported lattice parameters of bulk 2H-MoS₂ [31]. The isolated (002) and (110) reflections were fitted by nonlinear least-squares analysis using a local linear background and a pseudo-Voigt peak profile, which combines Gaussian and Lorentzian contributions and is commonly employed to describe powder-diffraction peaks [32, 33]. The fitted peak position and full width at half maximum (FWHM) were obtained from angular intervals surrounding each reflection. For MoS₂-C, the selected intervals were 13.5–15.5° for (002) and 57.5–59.5° for (110). For MoS₂-S, wider intervals of 11–17° and 54–64° were used because of the pronounced peak broadening. Adsorption studies

Batch adsorption assays were performed using aqueous MG solutions as the target contaminant and either MoS₂-C or MoS₂-S as the adsorbent. Unless otherwise specified, all experiments were carried out at 300 K under agitation at 500 rpm, and each test was performed in duplicate. For the time-dependent kinetic experiments, 25 mg of adsorbent was added to 15 mL of MG solution with an initial concentration of 50 mg L−1 at pH 6. Fourteen sampling times within the 5–150 min interval were selected to establish the adsorption profile and determine the equilibrium time. The effect of pH was examined by adjusting the MG solution to pH 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 while maintaining the same adsorbent mass, solution volume, initial dye concentration, contact time of 45 min, and temperature.

Equilibrium isotherm experiments were conducted at pH 6 and 300 K using 25 mg of adsorbent dispersed in 15 mL of MG solution for 45 min. For MoS₂-C, the initial MG concentrations were 4, 10, 15, 20, 50, 75, 100, 200, 300, 400, 500, and 600 mg L−1. For MoS₂-S, the investigated concentration range was extended to 20, 50, 100, 200, 250, 300, 400, 500, 600, 700, 800, 900, and 1000 mg L−1.

The influence of adsorbent dose was evaluated by contacting 10, 15, 20, 25, or 30 mg of MoS₂-C or MoS₂-S with 15 mL of MG solution (50 mg L−1, pH 6) for 45 min at 300 K.

MG concentrations were quantified using a UV–Vis calibration curve prepared from standard solutions containing 2–10 mg L−1 MG. Absorbance spectra were recorded between 600 and 700 nm with an AVANTES UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Apeldoorn, The Netherlands). After each adsorption run, aliquots were filtered twice to remove suspended particles, transferred to plastic cuvettes, and analyzed by UV–Vis spectroscopy. Experimental design

The effects of pH, adsorbent dose, contact time, and initial dye concentration on MG adsorption by MoS₂-S were investigated using response surface methodology (RSM) with a Box–Behnken design (BBD) [36]. MG removal efficiency (%R) was selected as the response variable. BBD was chosen because it efficiently estimates linear, interaction, and quadratic effects while avoiding experimental combinations in which all factors are simultaneously set at their extreme levels. For k factors and C₀ center points, the required number of experiments is N = 2 k(k − 1) + C₀ [37]. A preliminary three-factor design comprising 15 runs, including three center points, evaluated pH, adsorbent dose (g L−1), and initial MG concentration (mg L−1) at three coded levels: − 1, 0, and + 1 (Table 1).

Results and discussion

X-ray diffraction and peak-broadening analysis

Figure 1 compares the XRD patterns of MoS₂-C and MoS₂-S. The commercial sample exhibits sharp reflections at approximately 14.53°, 29.18°, 32.87°, 33.69°, 36.04°, 39.73°, 49.98°, and 58.53°, assigned to the (002), (004), (100), (101), (102), (103), (105), and (110) planes of hexagonal 2H-MoS₂, respectively [31, 40]. The narrow peaks indicate comparatively high crystallinity and extended stacking order. In contrast, MoS₂-S presents a broad (002) reflection at 13.88°, an unresolved group of reflections near 33.4° associated with the (100), (101), and (102) planes, a weak contribution near 39.7° assigned to the (103) plane, and a broad (110) reflection at 58.83° [40]. Such peak broadening and overlap are characteristic of nanoscale MoS₂ with reduced long-range order and structurally disordered or curved layers [31, 32, 40]. The fitted peak positions, interplanar spacings, observed and instrument-corrected FWHM values, and apparent coherent-domain dimensions for both samples are summarized in Table S2.

For MoS₂-C, the (002) and (110) reflections yielded interplanar distances of 6.096 and 1.577 Å, corresponding to apparent lattice parameters of c = 12.192 Å and a = 3.154 Å, respectively. These values are close to those reported for bulk 2H-MoS₂, approximately a = 3.16 Å and c = 12.3 Å [31]. For MoS₂-S, the interlayer distance increased to 6.381 Å, while the apparent lattice parameters were c = 12.761 Å and a = 3.139 Å. The approximately 4.7% increase in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${d}_{002}$$\end{document} indicates that the principal structural modification occurs along the stacking direction and is consistent with interlayer distances reported for hydrothermally prepared MoS₂ nanosheets [40].

After correction for the instrumental broadening of 0.06°, the apparent coherent dimensions obtained from the (002) and (110) reflections were 21.7 and 25.3 nm for MoS₂-C, and 4.68 and 2.81 nm for MoS₂-S, respectively. The (002) value for MoS₂-S corresponds to approximately eight coherently stacked layers, in reasonable agreement with the approximately ten layers observed by HRTEM. The substantially smaller coherent dimensions of MoS₂-S confirm reduced stacking order and limited in-plane crystallographic coherence. These values represent coherently diffracting domains and should not be interpreted as the overall 200–300 nm dimensions of the nanoflower-like assemblies observed by TEM [32, 34].

Morphological analysis

Figure 2a–c shows representative TEM images of MoS₂-C. The commercial material consists of stacked multilayer sheets with lateral dimensions of approximately 0.15–1.5 µm. An interplanar spacing of 0.62 nm was measured in a region containing approximately 16 layers, consistent with reported values for 2H-MoS₂ [41]. The corresponding SAED pattern shown in Fig. 2d contains discrete reflections, confirming the comparatively high crystallinity observed by XRD.

On the other hand, Fig. 2e–g shows representative TEM images of MoS₂-S. The bottom-up synthesis route significantly modified its morphology compared with the commercial MoS₂-C. The synthesized material consists of thinner and more curved nanosheets assembled into complex hierarchical nanoflower-like structures with lateral dimensions of approximately 200–300 nm. Nanoscale MoS₂ can exhibit different morphologies, including monolayers, few-layer nanosheets, nanoflowers, and quantum dots, whose properties are strongly influenced by their dimensionality and structural organization [42, 43]. In the present case, the nanosheet assembly may expose a greater proportion of edge and defect sites than the more compact multilayer sheets of MoS₂-C. However, because N₂ adsorption–desorption measurements were not performed, differences in BET specific surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution between the two samples cannot be quantitatively established from TEM observations alone. The interplanar distance was estimated as 0.61 nm from approximately ten stacked layers. Moreover, the SAED pattern shown in Fig. 2h displays diffuse rings, indicating a lower degree of crystallinity than that observed for MoS₂-C.

The STEM images and EDS maps in displayed Fig. 2i–s confirm the spatial co-localization of Mo and S in the analyzed regions of both samples. Quantitative stoichiometry was not derived from STEM-EDS because the Mo-L and S-K emission lines overlap strongly and the spectra represent localized nanoscale regions. Elemental stoichiometry and surface chemical states were instead evaluated by XPS, as discussed in Sect. 3.8.

Taken together, the TEM, SAED, and STEM results demonstrate that MoS₂-C contains comparatively ordered multilayer domains, whereas MoS₂-S forms curved nanosheet assemblies with reduced crystallographic coherence and a greater proportion of exposed edges and defect-associated regions. These structural differences provide a qualitative basis for the distinct colloidal and adsorption behaviors of the two samples; however, they should not be interpreted as quantitative evidence of differences in BET surface area.

Colloidal studies

The colloidal behavior of both MoS₂ dispersions was evaluated by dynamic light scattering (DLS) and ζ-potential measurements. Figure 3a and b shows that the effective hydrodynamic diameter generally increased with concentration for both materials, indicating progressively stronger interparticle interactions. However, the magnitude and onset of aggregation differed markedly between MoS₂-C and MoS₂-S.

Fig. 3Effective hydrodynamic diameter as a function of concentration for a MoS₂-C and b MoS₂-S, and ζ-potential as a function of pH for c MoS₂-C and d MoS₂-S

Effective hydrodynamic diameter as a function of concentration for a MoS₂-C and b MoS₂-S, and ζ-potential as a function of pH for c MoS₂-C and d MoS₂-S

In the case of commercial MoS₂ (Fig. 3a), the effective diameter increases moderately from approximately 720 nm at low concentrations to about 850 nm at 1200 mg L−1. This gradual growth suggests the presence of loosely bound agglomerates that become more prominent as particle–particle collisions increase at higher concentrations. The relatively large baseline size even at low concentration indicates that the commercial material is already partially aggregated, likely due to broader size distribution, irregular morphology, and weaker surface charge stabilization.

By contrast, MoS₂-S (Fig. 3b) exhibits smaller hydrodynamic diameters at low and intermediate concentrations, remaining near 510–530 nm up to approximately 800 mg L−1. The diameter increases to approximately 650 nm at 1200 mg L−1, indicating the onset of stronger aggregation at the highest concentration. These results are consistent with improved dispersion and more effective electrostatic stabilization relative to MoS₂-C.

The ζ-potential measurements in shown Fig. 3c and d further clarify the stability differences. For MoS₂-C, the ζ-potential changes from slightly positive values at low pH to negative values as pH increases, with an estimated point of zero charge near pH 3.7. At neutral and alkaline pH, values of approximately − 10 to − 15 mV remain well below the commonly used |ζ| ≈ 30 mV criterion for strong electrostatic stabilization, consistent with the aggregation observed by DLS.

MoS₂-S exhibits negative ζ-potential values throughout the investigated pH range. Values approached − 25 to − 30 mV in the acidic-to-neutral region and generally remained near or below − 20 mV over most of the range, becoming less negative at the highest pH. This stronger negative surface charge explains the smaller hydrodynamic diameters and delayed aggregation of MoS₂-S.

Overall, the DLS and ζ-potential results demonstrate that MoS₂-S has greater aqueous dispersion stability than MoS₂-C. Reduced aggregation increases the accessibility of the nanosheet–solution interface and can therefore facilitate contact between MG molecules and available adsorption sites.

Equilibrium time determination

Figure 4a shows that the MG adsorption onto MoS₂-C increased rapidly during the initial stage and subsequently approached a plateau, while the corresponding nonlinear kinetic fits are presented in Fig. 4b. Figure 5a and c shows the effect of adsorbent dose on the MG removal efficiency for MoS₂-C and MoS₂-S, respectively. For both materials, the MG removal efficiency increases with increasing adsorbent dose, reflecting the progressive availability of adsorption sites. In the case of MoS₂-C, the MG removal increases from approximately 78% at 0.7 g L-1 to above 90% at 1.7 g L-1, followed by a slight plateau at higher doses. This saturation behavior indicates that most MG molecules in solution are effectively captured once a sufficient number of adsorption sites becomes available.

A similar trend is observed for MoS₂-S; however, removal efficiencies are consistently higher across the entire dosage range, reaching nearly complete removal (> 98%) even at moderate doses. The rapid attainment of maximum removal efficiency suggests more favorable interactions between MG molecules and the synthesized MoS₂ surface. The improved dispersion, negative surface charge, and oxygen-containing surface species observed for MoS₂-S may contribute to this behavior, although the possible contribution of specific surface area cannot be evaluated without BET measurements. The UV–Vis spectra in the insets corroborate these observations, showing a pronounced decrease in the characteristic MG absorption band around 623.8 nm with increasing adsorbent dose, eventually approaching baseline levels at higher doses.

Figure 5b and d presents the effect of solution pH on MG adsorption using MoS₂-C and MoS₂-S, respectively. For MoS₂-C, the MG removal efficiency exhibits a clear dependence on pH, with lower efficiencies under strongly acidic conditions and significantly enhanced removal in neutral to alkaline environments. This trend indicates that adsorption is favored at higher pH values, where the interaction between MG molecules and the adsorbent surface is maximized.

In contrast, MoS₂-S demonstrates consistently high MG removal efficiency across the entire pH range studied, maintaining removal percentages close to 100% from pH 2 to 12. This pH-insensitive behavior highlights the robustness of the synthesized material and suggests that effective adsorption can occur under a wide range of solution conditions. The UV–Vis spectra further support this conclusion, as the MG absorption peak diminishes substantially across all pH values for MoS₂-S, indicating efficient dye removal regardless of solution acidity or alkalinity.

Overall, the comparative analysis reveals that MoS₂-S outperforms MoS₂-C in terms of adsorption efficiency, dose effectiveness, and pH tolerance. The enhanced performance of MoS₂-S is particularly advantageous for practical water treatment applications, where operational conditions such as adsorbent loading and pH can vary significantly. These results underscore the importance of material synthesis in optimizing adsorption performance for dye removal.

Adsorption-isotherm parameters obtained by nonlinear fitting of the MG equilibrium data for MoS₂-S

The Langmuir model provided an excellent fitting result (R2 = 0.991) and a comparatively low BIC value. The fitted maximum capacity was qₘₐₓ = 7.1 × 102 mg g−1, indicating a high apparent capacity for MG. Although the fit is consistent with saturation of a finite population of accessible sites, it should be interpreted as an empirical equilibrium description rather than definitive proof of an ideal homogeneous monolayer (Fig. 6).

Equilibrium adsorption isotherm for MG on MoS₂-S at pH 6, 300 K, and an adsorbent dose of 0.8 g L−1. The curves represent nonlinear fits of the Langmuir, Freundlich, Sips, and Redlich–Peterson models; n = 13 experimental points

The Freundlich model (see, e.g., Fig. 6) also describes the equilibrium data reasonably well, with an R2 value of 0.972. The Freundlich constant 1/n=0.43 (i.e., n>1) indicates favorable adsorption over the investigated concentration range. However, the significantly higher BIC value compared to the one found by the Langmuir model suggests that the Freundlich model provides an inferior statistical description of the data. This implies that surface heterogeneities play a secondary role relative to monolayer adsorption behavior.

The Sips model, which combines features of the Langmuir and Freundlich isotherms and accounts for surface heterogeneity at low concentrations while converging to Langmuir behavior at high concentrations, yields the highest correlation coefficient (R2 = 0.993). The estimated saturation capacity (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${q}_{s}$$\end{document}) is slightly higher than the Langmuir \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${q}_{\mathrm{m}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{x}}$$\end{document}, indicating consistency between these models. However, the BIC value for the Sips model is comparable to the one obtained from the Langmuir model, suggesting that the additional fitting parameter does not provide a statistically significant improvement. This outcome indicates that the adsorption system does not require the added complexity of a three-parameter model.

Similarly, the Redlich–Peterson model exhibits a high R2 value (0.991), reflecting good agreement with the experimental data. Nevertheless, its higher BIC value relative to the Langmuir model indicates overparameterization without meaningful gains in explanatory power. The convergence of the Redlich–Peterson parameters toward Langmuir-like behavior further supports the predominance of monolayer adsorption.

Considering R2, BIC, and model complexity together, the Langmuir approach provided the most appropriate and parsimonious description of MG adsorption on MoS₂-S. The high fitted capacity confirms the strong uptake potential of the synthesized material and provides a consistent basis for comparison with other adsorbents, without implying that the real surface is perfectly homogeneous.

Since equilibrium data were best described by the Langmuir model, the fitted parameters were used to estimate the amount of adsorbent required for a single-stage batch adsorber. The derivation of the mass balance, the schematic representation of the batch adsorption system, and the corresponding design projections are presented in Sect. S1 of the Supplementary Material (Figs. S2 and S3).

Comparison of reported adsorption parameters found for different adsorbents used in the MG removal

A wide variety of adsorbent materials have been explored (see Table 6), ranging from inorganic nanocomposites and metal oxides to biomass-derived carbons, biopolymers, and hybrid materials. Inorganic systems such as Ag–Zn–Ni–Fe₂O₄@SiO₂ and ZnO nanoparticles synthesized via N. latifolia exhibit moderate to high adsorption capacities (32.3 and 593.4 mg g−1, respectively) with removal efficiencies close to or exceeding 99%, typically under mildly acidic to neutral pH conditions and equilibrium times between 60 and 120 min. Similarly, metal-oxide-based composites and modified chitosan materials demonstrate efficient MG removal, often exceeding 95% efficiency, although adsorption capacities vary significantly depending on surface functionalization and synthesis route.

Carbon-based adsorbents, including activated carbons derived from agricultural wastes (e.g., PHAC, corn cobs, almond shells, and sawdust), generally show good removal efficiencies (≈95–99%), but comparatively lower adsorption capacities, typically below 100 mg g−1, with equilibrium times ranging from 60 to 180 min. These materials are often highlighted for their low cost and sustainability, despite their moderate adsorption performance.

Biopolymer-based systems, particularly chitosan and its chemically modified derivatives (e.g., epichlorohydrin-crosslinked chitosan and chitosan Schiff-base composites), have been demonstrated enhanced adsorption capacities (up to ~ 194 mg g−1) and high removal efficiencies (> 96%). The incorporation of metal oxides or Schiff-base functionalities significantly improved adsorption performance by increasing active binding sites and electrostatic interactions with MG molecules.

Superadsorbent hydrogels and acid-activated bentonite also exhibit notable adsorption capacities (353–1915 mg g−1), although in some cases the removal efficiency or detailed operational conditions are not fully specified, limiting direct comparison across systems.

Notably, the MoS₂-based adsorbent reported in this work stands out by combining a high adsorption capacity (≈710 mg g−1) with near-complete removal efficiency (≈99%), achieved at pH 6, a short equilibrium time (≈15 min), and a relatively low adsorbent dosage (≈1.7 g L−1). Compared to most previously reported systems (see Table 6, e.g.), this material offers a favorable balance between adsorption capacity, kinetics, and operational simplicity, underscoring its potential as an efficient and competitive adsorbent for MG removal from aqueous media.

Statistical analysis

MoS₂-S was selected for multivariate optimization because it consistently outperformed MoS₂-C in the preceding experiments, exhibiting higher removal, faster uptake, greater adsorption capacity, and stable performance over a broad pH range. Restricting the Response Surface Methodology (RSM) analysis to the better-performing material allowed the effects and interactions of the operational variables to be evaluated within a practically relevant adsorption domain.

Before interpreting the response surface model, the distribution of the response data obtained from the BBD was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk normality test. As shown in Fig. S1 of the supplementary material, the data did not deviate significantly from normality (p > 0.05), supporting the use of parametric statistical analyses, including ANOVA and quadratic model fitting.

As shown in Table 7, the observed MG removal ranged from 91.44% (Run 6) to 99.57% (Run 4), and the predicted values were generally close to the experimental means. The triplicated center-point condition (12.5 min, 175 mg L−1, and 0.4 g L−1; Runs 9, 10, and 15) provided an estimate of pure experimental error. Higher removal was generally associated with the highest adsorbent dose (0.7 g L−1), whereas the lowest responses occurred at 0.1 g L−1. The overall agreement between observed and predicted values supports use of the quadratic model for interpolation and process optimization within the studied domain.

Contact time did not significantly affect MG removal within the investigated interval (p = 0.5243), confirming that extending the treatment from 5 to 20 min provides no significant improvement. Therefore, the time factor is interpreted as an operational robustness parameter rather than a variable governing the adsorption response. Within the evaluated experimental domain, MG removal was mainly controlled by adsorbent dose, the interaction between initial concentration and dose, and the quadratic effect of dose. These results demonstrate that the adsorption process remains highly efficient from the shortest experimentally evaluated contact time of 5 min onward.

As shown in Table 8, the quadratic model was significant (p < 0.0001), with R2 = 0.925, adjusted R2 = 0.884, and predicted R2 = 0.728. The difference between adjusted and predicted R2 was below 0.20, and the adequate precision value of 15.602 was well above the commonly used minimum value of 4. The lack of fit was not significant (p = 0.6263), indicating that the model residual was not distinguishable from pure experimental error. Adsorbent dose (C), the concentration–dose interaction (BC), and the quadratic dose term (C2) were significant at p < 0.05, whereas contact time and initial concentration were not significant as isolated linear terms. ATR-FTIR and XPS evidence of the MG adsorption mechanism

The ATR-FTIR spectra of the pristine adsorbents are shown in Fig. 10. Both materials exhibit a broad band between 3100 and 3500 cm−1, assigned to surface hydroxyl groups and adsorbed water [50]. The bands near 1100 and 940 cm−1, which are more evident for MoS₂-S, are attributed to Mo–O- and S-containing vibrations, respectively [50, 51]. Fig. 11displays, for pure MG, C–H stretching bands between approximately 2980 and 2920 cm−1, a characteristic aromatic skeletal vibration near 1570 cm−1, and bands at approximately 1470 and 1365 cm−1 associated with C=N⁺ and aromatic C–N groups, respectively [15, 52, 53]. After adsorption, these MG-related bands remain detectable but decrease markedly their intensities for both adsorbents, confirming dye retention without substantial disruption of the chromophoric structure. These changes are compatible with electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, Van der Waals forces, and aromatic–surface interactions [53, 54]. Nevertheless, ATR-FTIR alone cannot establish whether adsorption is predominantly physical or chemical.

After the MG adsorption, MoS₂-C exhibits measurable changes in its surface composition and chemical environment, as shown in Fig. S5. The Mo4+ component shifts from 230.1 to 229.6 eV, whereas Mo⁶⁺ shifts from 233.9 to 233.0 eV. A new Mo5+ contribution appears at approximately 230.7 eV, and the S2− component shifts from 163.1 to 162.6 eV. The Mo–O, C–O/O–N, and S–O components occur at 530.4, 532.1, and 533.3 eV after the MG adsorption, while the appearance of a C–N contribution near 285.7 eV confirms the retention of MG-derived nitrogen-containing groups. The increased carbon contribution is also consistent with MG uptake, whereas the Si signal observed in Fig. S5a is likewise present in the survey spectrum of the commercial MG reagent shown in Fig. S5f. These changes indicate modification of the local chemical environment at the MoS₂-C–MG interface. Therefore, adsorption on MoS₂-C is not considered exclusively physical, although non-covalent interactions remain the predominant contribution.

For MoS₂-S after adsorption, the survey spectrum displayed in Fig. 13a shows an increased oxygen contribution and a lower Mo:S ratio than in the pristine sample, consistent with the MG coverage and modification of the surface composition. In the Mo 3d region, the principal Mo4+ component remains at approximately 229.9 eV, while a Mo5+ contribution appears near 231.1 eV and the relative Mo⁶⁺ contribution increases from 13.4% to 28.1%. The oxidized-sulfur fraction changes slightly from approximately 16.4% to 17.3%. In the O 1 s region, the Mo–O, C–O/O–N, and S–O components change from 530.8, 531.9, and 533.4 eV to 530.9, 532.1, and 533.0 eV, respectively, corresponding to modest shifts of + 0.1, + 0.2, and − 0.4 eV. The appearance of a C–N contribution near 286.2 eV further confirms the presence of the adsorbed MG. The complete binding-energy comparison and corresponding observed shiftings for both materials are summarized in Table 9. Collectively, these results demonstrate modification of the surface chemical environment after adsorption and support localized interactions involving Mo centers, sulfur- and oxygen-associated species, and defect-related sites.

Taken together, the adsorption-model and spectroscopic results support mixed adsorption mechanisms for both materials, with different relative contributions, as illustrated in Fig. 14. For MoS₂-C, the PFO model provided the best empirical kinetic fit (R2 = 0.994, RSS = 4.16, and BIC = − 11.7), while XPS revealed moderate changes in Mo and S species and the emergence of Mo5+ and MG-derived C–N components. MG uptake by MoS₂-C is therefore considered predominantly non-covalent, involving electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, Van der Waals forces, and aromatic–surface interactions, with a secondary localized surface-chemical contribution [53, 54]. For MoS₂-S, the PSO model provided the best empirical kinetic description (R2 = 0.999, RSS = 0.501, and BIC = − 41.4), while the Langmuir model described the equilibrium data with a maximum capacity of approximately 710 mg g−1. These model fittings are treated as empirical descriptions rather than direct mechanistic proof. The XPS results—particularly the preservation of the dominant Mo4+ state, appearance of Mo5+, increased Mo6+ fraction, modest changes in oxygen- and sulfur-related components, and emergence of C–N species—support a stronger defect-associated and site-specific contribution for MoS₂-S. Accordingly, the MG adsorption on MoS₂-S is described as a mixed process involving electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, aromatic–surface interactions, and localized interactions at defect-associated and oxygen-containing surface sites [53–58].

Conclusions

Hydrothermally synthesized MoS₂-S outperformed and commercial MoS₂-C both applied for the MG adsorption resulted in a near-complete removal efficiency (> 98%) across pH 2–12 with an operational equilibrium time of approximately 15 min and a Langmuir maximum capacity of about 710 mg g−1. The MoS₂-C kinetic data were best represented by the pseudo-first-order model, whereas the MoS₂-S data were best represented by the pseudo-second-order model; both were interpreted as empirical fittings rather than direct evidence of a unique rate-controlling mechanism. The Box–Behnken model was significant (R2 = 0.925; adjusted R2 = 0.884) with (1) non-significant lack of fit and identified adsorbent dose, (2) the concentration–dose interaction, and (3) the quadratic dose term as the principal contributors within the studied domain. ATR-FTIR and consistently charge-corrected XPS data support mixed adsorption mechanisms for both materials. The MG uptake by MoS₂-C is predominantly non-covalent, whereas MoS₂-S exhibits a stronger defect-associated and site-specific contribution together with electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, and aromatic–surface interactions. No individual kinetic-model fit or binding-energy shift was treated as definitive proof of chemisorption. These results establish MoS₂-S as a high-capacity, rapidly acting adsorbent for cationic dyes, while further validation with real effluents and regeneration cycles is required to assess long-term practical performance.
