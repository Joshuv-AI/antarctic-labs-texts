AnnoCaseLaw: Legal Annotation Benchmark of Civil Negligence
Rilton Franzone, Magnus Sesodia, Alina Petrova, John Armour, Thomas Lukasiewicz, Oana-Maria Camburu, Puneet K. Dokania, Philip Torr, Christian Schroeder de Witt, Fabio J. Fehr

Abstract
Language models offer a way to scale costly, labour-intensive expert annotation, but their ability to reproduce expert judgments remains uncertain. This is particularly challenging in law, where annotators must distinguish the reasoning supporting judicial decisions from other legally relevant discussion. We present AnnoCaseLaw 1 1 1 Benchmark Code as a densely annotated benchmark comprising 471 471 U.S. civil-negligence court opinions with more than 12,000 12,000 expert highlights across five annotation categories. We evaluate proprietary frontier, open-weight, legal-specialised, and decision models on their ability to reproduce these annotations. Models perform better on Facts and Outcome than on Application of Law to Facts, Procedural History, and Relevant Precedents. On Application of Law to Facts, proprietary frontier models achieve the strongest balance of reasoning coverage and precision, while the open-weight model Gemma 4 achieves comparable Macro-F1 to proprietary frontier models at a fraction of the estimated inference cost. Our findings highlight the importance of evaluating annotation coverage and cost alongside aggregate scores when assessing the feasibility of automated legal annotation.

High-quality data annotation is essential for developing and evaluating AI systems, yet remains costly and labour-intensive, particularly in specialised domains. Language models offer a way to scale annotation, with recent studies demonstrating potential cost and efficiency advantages over human annotation
(
Tan et al., 2024
;
Parfenova et al., 2025
;
Gu et al., 2026
)
. However, models still struggle with legal annotations requiring nuanced understanding, such as identifying complex legal references
(
Schepers et al., 2025
)
. Expert legal annotations remain scarce, and some existing datasets rely on keyword-based annotation methods
(
Huang et al., 2024
;
Huang, 2025
)
. This raises the question:
can language models reliably reproduce expert legal annotations?

Identifying the reasoning supporting a judicial decision is particularly demanding, as models may confuse legally worded passages with those that actually support an outcome
(
Kalamkar et al., 2022
;
Cheng et al., 2026
)
. Civil negligence provides a useful setting for studying this problem, as cases with different factual circumstances share a common legal framework, including duty of care and breach. Appellate courts are particularly suitable for studying legal reasoning because they review lower-court decisions against an established factual record, drawing on legal arguments and precedent. Their opinions provide a rich source of evidence on how legal principles are applied to facts.

We present AnnoCaseLaw (Figure
1
), a benchmark comprising
471
U.S. civil-negligence court opinions with more than
12,000
expert-annotated highlights. These highlights identify legally significant passages across five categories: Application of Law to Facts (ALF), Procedural History, Relevant Precedents, Facts, and Outcome. Related benchmarks are discussed in Appendix
A
. Our main
contributions
are:

•

Expert-annotated legal benchmark.
We present AnnoCaseLaw, a densely annotated benchmark capturing legally significant passages, including the application of law to facts and relevant precedents, for systematic evaluation of legal annotation reproduction (Section
2
).

•

Comprehensive model evaluation.
We compare proprietary frontier, open-weight, legal-specialised, and decision models across five annotation categories. Models perform better on Facts and Outcome than on more nuanced categories, including ALF, Procedural History, and Relevant Precedents (Table
1
); even frontier models miss about a quarter of expert-marked ALF passages (Figure
2
).

•

Annotation quality and cost trade-offs.
We evaluate annotation performance alongside estimated inference cost. Proprietary frontier models achieve the strongest balance of precision and recall on ALF, while Gemma 4 achieves comparable Macro-F1 to proprietary frontier models at a fraction of the estimated inference cost (Figures
2
and
3
).

2.1
Dataset Construction

The court opinions were retrieved from the Caselaw Access Project
(
Harvard Law School Library, 2018
)
. The dataset includes
471
U.S. civil-negligence cases from three states: Arkansas, Illinois, and New Mexico. Cases were identified from the repository and checked for relevance by the annotators. It includes decisions from state appeals courts, state supreme courts, and the Illinois Claims Court, a specialised tribunal handling negligence claims against the state. Further details on jurisdictional coverage, opinion lengths, and annotation statistics are provided in Appendix
B
.

2.2
Expert Annotation Protocol

The annotations were produced by three legally trained annotators (final-year law students) under the supervision of an experienced legal scholar, following a shared annotation protocol. Each opinion was annotated by one student. The full annotation instructions are provided in Appendix
E.1
.

The protocol distinguishes five categories of passages in judicial opinions, illustrated in Figure
1
.
Application of Law to Facts (ALF)
,
Procedural History (PH)
, and
Relevant Precedents
capture specific aspects of the court’s legal analysis, covering only passages concerning duty of care, breach of duty, and contributory negligence, three central issues in civil negligence law. ALF identifies passages explaining how the court applies the law to the facts, selecting only the reasoning necessary to explain its decision. PH records the proceedings, including earlier courts’ decisions and reasons, and the standard the deciding court uses to review them. Relevant Precedents identifies citations to earlier cases and other legal sources directly relevant to the court’s reasoning, excluding those used only as background; only cited cases are evaluated in this category (Section
2.3
).
Facts
captures events and circumstances before litigation, including facts the court accepts or assumes to be true. Facts are marked comprehensively, including within the court’s reasoning.
Outcome
identifies passages stating the present court’s disposition, such as affirming, reversing, remanding, or dismissing.

2.3
Benchmark Task

The benchmark evaluates sentence selection for ALF, PH, Facts
and Outcome, and cited-case selection for Relevant Precedents. For sentence selection, each opinion is split into numbered sentences, and models select sentence numbers for each category. Expert highlights are mapped to the same sentences for evaluation, allowing a sentence to belong to multiple categories. For Relevant Precedents, models receive the opinion text and a list of cited cases extracted automatically using GPT-5.6 Sol, and select those relevant under the annotation protocol. Expert-marked cases missing from the candidate list are outside the recall calculation. Appendix
B.3
provides implementation details for sentence alignment and citation-candidate construction.

2.4
Experimental Setup

We evaluate twelve generative language models and four decision
models, including proprietary frontier, open-weight, and
legal-specialised models. All models receive the full judicial
opinions and instructions adapted from the expert annotation
protocol, without labelled examples. Generative models return
structured JSON, jointly selecting sentences across the four
sentence categories and selecting relevant precedent candidates
in a separate request. Decision models answer a yes/no question
for each sentence–category pair and each precedent candidate;
items are selected when the probability of “yes” is at least
0.5. To examine how the question format affects the comparison,
we also evaluate a generative model using yes/no questions on a subset of
opinions (Appendix
D.2
). Model settings
and prompts (Appendices
C

and
E
).

2.5
Evaluation Metrics

We compare model and expert annotations using precision, recall
and F1. Scores are calculated per opinion and averaged
within each category, excluding the opinions without reference
annotations for that category. Macro-F1 is the mean of the five
category F1 scores. We estimate 95% percentile bootstrap confidence
intervals by resampling opinions; Table
1
shows each
score
\pm
half the interval width as a symmetric approximation.
We also compare the total number of ALF sentences selected by
each model with the expert total, since similar F1 scores can
correspond to different amounts of selected text. Scoring details
are provided in Appendix
C.3
.

Inference Cost.

We estimate inference costs for all 471 opinions from API token
usage and prices or, for self-hosted models, GPU time and hourly
rental rates. Appendix

Within

Annotation Performance and Cost.

These differences in annotation performance also have cost implications (Figure
3
). Gemma achieves Macro-F1 close to that of proprietary frontier models at substantially lower inference cost. For the full benchmark, its estimated inference cost is $1.07, compared with $84.46 for Claude Opus 5.

Decision models.

Generative models select annotations jointly, whereas decision
models answer yes/no questions about individual items. To examine
whether this difference affects their comparison, we also test
Gemma with yes/no questions on a subset of 40 opinions. On this sample, Jev,
Kev and Clef achieve higher Macro-F1 than Gemma’s yes/no version,
but all decision models remain below its joint selection
(Appendix
D.2
), suggesting that the
question format contributes to the observed differences between
models.

4
Discussion

Current models struggle to reproduce expert legal annotations, particularly the precise reasoning supporting judicial decisions. Models that recover more expert-marked reasoning often select additional text, while more precise models may miss important passages, making aggregate F1 an incomplete measure of annotation quality. Model choice also involves a cost trade-off: smaller open-weight models such as Gemma offer competitive overall performance at substantially lower inference cost, but may recover less legal reasoning. The appropriate model therefore depends on the annotation task, the relative importance of missed and unnecessary selections, and the available budget. These findings suggest that models should be evaluated as potential aids to expert annotation rather than assumed replacements for expert judgment.

Thomson Small improves F1 for reasoning annotation by 7.3 points over its parent, Snowdon Small, gaining precision at the cost of some recall (Appendix
D.3
). This suggests that specialisation can improve annotation performance, but since Thomson’s training also includes non-legal material, we cannot attribute the improvements to legal adaptation alone.

5
Conclusion

AnnoCaseLaw provides a benchmark for evaluating the reproduction of legally significant annotations in U.S. civil-negligence opinions (Section
2
). Frontier models perform better on Facts and Outcome than on more nuanced categories, particularly Application of Law to Facts and Relevant Precedents (Table
1
). These findings highlight the need to assess annotation coverage alongside estimated inference cost (Figures
2
and
3
).

Limitations

AnnoCaseLaw provides a densely annotated benchmark of U.S. civil-negligence opinions spanning three states and multiple court types. However, we do not claim that these results will generalise to other jurisdictions, areas of law, annotation protocols, or languages. As the underlying opinions are publicly available, we cannot rule out that models encountered them during training, potentially confounding model comparisons. Thomson 1.0 Small also received training beyond the legal domain, including tax and journalism material, as well as further post-training, so its improvement over Snowdon 1.1 Small cannot be attributed to legal training alone. Decision and generative models used different inference settings, and some generative models required more retries than others. Finally, three final-year law students produced the annotations under the supervision of an experienced legal scholar and following a shared, documented protocol. Each opinion was annotated by one student. Given the scope of this paper, independently annotating opinions to assess inter-annotator agreement was beyond the scope of this study; this remains an important direction for future work.

Future Work

Our results highlight the promise of language models for annotation in high-stakes domains such as law. Promising directions for further developing AnnoCaseLaw include fine-tuning smaller open-weight and legal-specialised models, including decision models, to improve annotation coverage while maintaining precision and cost efficiency. Further work should also evaluate whether model-assisted annotation reduces human effort compared with annotation from scratch.

Ethical considerations

AnnoCaseLaw contains people’s names and details of their injuries and disputes. These details can be sensitive even though the court opinions are public. The goal of this dataset is to enable research on legal annotation, and performance on this benchmark does not establish whether a model can provide reliable legal advice or make legal decisions.

B.3
Reference construction

Sentence labels.

We remove annotator summaries, appended lower-court opinions and
separate concurring or dissenting opinions from the input text.
A deterministic splitter uses punctuation and paragraph boundaries,
with rules for legal abbreviations, citations and footnotes.
Expert-highlighted excerpts are matched to the cleaned text,
allowing for differences in whitespace, page markers and OCR errors.
Repeated excerpts were aligned using the original annotated documents
or, for 46 highlights, agreement between two language models. The
original annotation categories were retained.

A sentence receives a category when highlights in that category cover
at least half of its non-whitespace characters. Shorter highlights
also qualify when at least half of one highlight lies within the
sentence, with an overlap of at least 15 non-whitespace characters.
For Outcome, the minimum is six characters to retain short decisions
such as “Affirmed.” Categories are assigned independently, so a
sentence can receive more than one label.

Of the 9,204 highlights across the four sentence categories, 251 are
omitted from alignment because they are very short, occur frequently,
fall outside the retained text or cannot be located.
Separately, 472 sentence units with highlights that meet neither rule,
or whose labels depend on unresolved repeated excerpts, are excluded
from scoring for every model and all four sentence categories. They
remain in the model input without exclusion markers. Unhighlighted
sentences remain available for evaluation.

Precedent candidates.

A separate GPT-5.6 Sol pass, with medium reasoning effort, extracts
case names and citations from each cleaned opinion. The mentions
are checked against the text, allowing for OCR errors, and grouped
by case identity. Candidates are aligned with expert highlights to
identify the reference selections. Every model receives the same
candidate list without reference labels.

This task covers cited cases only; statutes and other legal sources
included in the original annotations are not evaluated. Scores
measure relevance selection from the supplied candidates, so an
expert-marked case missing from the candidate list is outside the
recall calculation.

C.3
Scoring and confidence intervals

Precision, recall and F1 compare model selections with the reference
items after excluded sentence IDs are removed. Empty selections
receive zero for all three metrics when the opinion has reference
items for that category. Each metric is averaged over opinions with
at least one reference item, giving them equal weight. Macro-F1 is
the mean of the five category-level F1 scores.

For ALF, we also divide the total number of model selections by the
total number of expert selections across the same opinions:

\sum_{i}|S_{i}|/\sum_{i}|G_{i}|
, where
S_{i}
and
G_{i}
are the model and
reference sets for opinion
i
.

We estimate 95% percentile confidence intervals using 10,000
bootstrap resamples of opinions
(
Efron and Tibshirani, 1993
)
, using the same
draws for all models. For Macro-F1, each draw samples
from the union of eligible opinions, and each category is averaged
over its eligible sampled opinions. Model differences are calculated
within each draw. Table
1
displays the score with
\pm

half the interval’s width, a symmetric approximation to the percentile
interval.

These intervals describe variation across opinions with the
annotations and model outputs fixed. They do not measure variation
across repeated inference runs and are not adjusted for multiple
comparisons.

Protocols for Annotating Cases

Iteration #5, 12 March 2021

Coding Protocols for Iteration #4

We will tag six types of text in the judgements, and add two elements.

#1 Facts: these should be stated comprehensively early on in the judgement. Tag only text about facts that happened "in the world" before the litigation began (ie the facts alleged to have give rise to the legal consequences in the plaintiff’s suit). Highlight these in yellow. Include any assumed/conceded "facts" as we are interested in all assumed facts on which the reasoning is based.

#2 Procedural history: highlight in pink statements about the procedural status of the case ("this is an appeal ….") and ("plaintiff succeeded at first instance") and what standard of review is applied ("de novo" vs "review") etc, and (where available) what the lower court’s reasons for its decision were.

Turning to the actual decision in the case, we will focus on three specific aspects:

#3 Application of law to facts: highlight in green the minimum necessary statements of the application of law to the facts to get the court to the decision. (Exclude discussion of points which are not necessary to the conclusion). Include contextual statements from the text that help to explain the application of the law to the facts. Include "judicial cues" for application of the law.

#4 Relevant precedents – highlight in blue the names of relevant prior cases (precedents) referred to which are salient for the application of law to facts in this case. The scope of this will be driven by the scope of what is tagged in green as "application of law to the facts". Include the citation information (court, year, law report). This annotation can exclude cases cited by way of background, or in relation to issues which are not determinative. Please also include relevant pieces of legislation, including section numbers. (But no need to highlight statutory text)

#5 Outcome: highlight in red at the end the court’s statement of the outcome ("I find for the defendant..") Fine to highlight multiple times if it appears more than once.

In addition, we will also code a seventh factor, once we have completed the breakdown of rules of the restatement:

#6 Plain English summary – account in plain English of what happened in the case.

#7 Restatement: indicate which rule(s) of the Restatement of Torts are applicable to the decision in the case. This will be based on a comparison of a table of Restatement provisions and our reading of the case.

Additional Protocols Agreed During RA Meetings

1.
Our focus is on the following elements of tort law: existence/scope of duty, breach of duty (i.e., whether the defendant was negligent), and the existence of contributory negligence (i.e., whether the claimant was negligent) (the ’Relevant Issues’).

2.
Pink and green highlights should only relate to the Relevant Issues. For example, if the appeal concerns (i) whether there was a duty; (ii) matters of causation; and (iii) damages, we should only highlight the procedural facts and any applications of the law to the case which relate to issue (i).

\bullet

Moreover, if the case primarily concerns issues (ii) and (iii), but issue (i) is mentioned very briefly (and what is mentioned would be useful to our model), annotate the case anyway, but mark it in the tagging sheet as a ’Marginal case’ and add a comment to explain why you have marked it as such.

Figure 4:
Expert annotation protocol.

3.
Contributory negligence:

\bullet

We are interested in whether the court finds the claimant to be guilty of contributory negligence. However, if the court goes on to discuss whether the claimant’s contributory negligence caused his/her injury, then this does not need to be highlighted.

\bullet

Similarly, we can highlight by what percentage the court finds the claimant to have contributed to his/her injury, but we do not need to highlight any discussion of damage reduction on account of the claimant’s negligence.

4.
Special liability rules:

\bullet

Where a case concerns occupiers’ liability, employer’s liability, strict liability, or other special rules governing liability, the discussions relating to these issues need only be highlighted if they are generalizable to common law negligence. For example:

(a)
If the case exclusively concerns an employee’s attempt to claim under the New Mexican Workers’ Compensation Act, then this should not be highlighted.

(b)
If, however, the case discusses an occupier’s duty of care to visitors and does so in general tort law of negligence terms, this should be highlighted (even if the case refers to some specific rules under the Restatement).

\bullet

In the case (b), we should mark the column ’Potentially other sections of the Restatement relevant’ in the tagging sheet.

5.
If the claim is against a state entity, and the discussion of the court merely concerns whether that state entity is immune from liability under the Tort Claims Act, this should not be highlighted.

6.
We do not highlight the cases related to the standard of review.

7.
If the court makes it clear that through its judgment it is making ’new law’, mark the ’Makes new law’ column in the tagging sheet.

8.
Mark the column ’special concurrence’ in the tagging sheet if the case includes a special concurrence after the judgment. The contents of the special concurrence should not be annotated.

9.
Do not annotate dissenting opinions.

10.
Treat witness testimony which is relied on by the court for its reasoning as fact.

\bullet

Where some witnesses disagree, but the court makes it clear that a majority of witnesses agree on particular facts, do not treat ’minority’ witness testimony as fact.

11.
Highlight the whole case name, including all the citation references after it (so the blue highlights will sometimes be very long).

12.
The various tort law Restatements should be highlighted blue even though they are not technically statutory.

13.
If the court mentions claims made by the claimant against parties that are not party to the case at hand, this information does not form part of the procedural facts and therefore should not be highlighted pink.

14.
Square brackets and other characters/symbols can be deleted or ignored (the model will delete them anyway).

15.
Add any other comments in the ’Comments’ section of the tagging sheet that you think are necessary.

16.
Where new factual information is recounted by the court in the course of its application of the law to the facts, tag that information as (yellow) fact notwithstanding that it is part of the court’s reasoning, to ensure that all facts are captured.

17.
Where the judgement includes a detailed factual description of the life, behaviour or practices of one of the parties (such as psychiatric or employment history) that occurs prior to the factual situation in which the tortious acts are alleged to have occurred, this should be annotated as (yellow) fact.

Figure 5:
Expert annotation protocol (continued).

18.
Where facts are recounted by the court that pertain exclusively to issues that are out of scope, they should nonetheless be annotated.

\bullet

For example, see the case of Trujillo v Chavez. In this case, the court recounts facts that relate exclusively to the question of whether or not the plaintiff was a fare-paying passenger (in which case the plaintiff would not be statutorily barred from seeking compensation). Notwithstanding that those facts do not go to the negligence of the defendant, they should be annotated.

19.
Where a case is heavily based on State-specific law (for example Delgado Claims in New Mexico), it should still be tagged if it is a duty and breach case.

20.
Where the court’s statement of facts takes the form of (a) restatement of witness testimony (apparently uncontradicted, and ostensibly in support of conclusions that such testimony establishes the facts); or (b) a claim that ’the evidence establishes’ the facts recounted, exclude prefatory remarks that refer to the basis for the court’s conclusion such as, ’The evidence establishes that …’, and ’The plaintiff testified that …’.

\bullet

Where the court’s statement of facts includes an excerpt from examination-in-chief or cross-examination of a witness, the useful, unambiguous portions of those excerpts (that is, excluding exclamations such as, ’oh’ and ’um’ which accompany verbatim records of witness examination) should generally be annotated as (yellow) fact, EXCEPT where necessary to give proper meaning to the court’s application of law to the facts on the point in support of which the excerpt is included, in which case such testimony should be highlighted as (green) application of the law to the facts.

21.
Where the court’s application of law to the facts refers to a relevant case name or statute, highlight that case name or statute in blue (keeping the rest of the statement in green).

\bullet

If, however, the procedural facts refer to a case name or statute, do not highlight this blue: the procedural facts should be highlighted pink only.

22.
Where the court states that a piece of evidence included in an affidavit and excerpted in the judgement is not admissible, and therefore that it does not form a basis for the court’s decision, that evidence should not be highlighted as fact.

\bullet

EXAMPLE: Consider the case of Cowan v. Ellison Enterprises, Inc., in which the court states the following in relation to part of the plaintiff’s affidavit:

In reviewing the motion for summary judgment, we do not consider appellant’s statement, "friends and relatives . . . have seen loose grapes on the floor in the produce section." That statement was not based on personal knowledge, and it is nothing more than inadmissible hearsay. As such, it should not be accepted as the basis for finding a genuine issue of material fact to deny entry of summary judgment.

23.
Where the court discusses whether a particular piece of evidence has probative value as regards the tort elements of duty or breach, and the discussion provides a signal as to the court’s reasoning on those elements, this discussion should be annotated.

24.
Where facts appear in a dissenting opinion, those facts should ordinarily not be annotated unless there is no effective statement of the facts in the majority opinion.

25.
Where facts appear in a specially concurring separate opinion that do not appear in the majority opinion, they should be annotated.

26.
Cases dealing with damage to property should be annotated where they turn on the same elements of negligence considered for the purposes of cases involving physical injury and they do not concern pure economic loss.

27.
Where possible, do not annotate references to the party type (such as ’defendant’, ’plaintiff’, or ’appellant’) in the court’s restatement of the facts.

\bullet

For example: if the court refers to ’defendant John’ in its statement of the facts, highlight only ’John’ in yellow.
