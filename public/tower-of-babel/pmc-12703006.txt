Severe Hypothyroidism‐Induced Rhabdomyolysis in the Absence of Triggering Factor
Authors: Mohamed Haris Mohamed Azmi, Nur ‘Aini Binti Eddy Warman, Fatimah Zaherah Mohamed Shah, Aimi Fadilah Mohamad, Mohd Hazriq Awang, Nur Aisyah Zainordin, Rohana Abdul Ghani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRhabdomyolysis is a rare but serious complication of hypothyroidism that can occur even in the absence of external triggers or classical symptoms. Early recognition through creatinine kinase screening in high‐risk or atypical presentations is essential to prevent complications such as acute kidney injury and thus enable timely management.

Introduction

Rhabdomyolysis is a clinical syndrome characterized by the rapid breakdown of the skeletal muscle tissue leading to the release of intracellular contents into the circulation. It is a rare but serious complication of hypothyroidism, typically occurring in the presence of precipitating factors such as statin use, trauma, or excessive exertion. However, it can rarely arise in the absence of such triggers, making early recognition challenging.

While muscle‐related symptoms and mildly elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels are frequently seen in hypothyroid patients [1, 2], progression to rhabdomyolysis is uncommon [3], especially in the absence of precipitating factors. Given the nonspecific nature of early symptoms, diagnosis can be delayed, increasing the risk of adverse outcomes such as acute kidney injury (AKI). In this report, we present a unique case of severe rhabdomyolysis secondary to previously undiagnosed hypothyroidism in a patient without identifiable external triggers, highlighting the importance of clinical vigilance in atypical presentations.

Case History and Examination

A 32‐year‐old man with no known medical illness presented with a one‐month history of lethargy and 10 kg weight gain, followed by a two‐week history of facial puffiness. Further history revealed associated cold intolerance. There was no muscle pain or weakness, dark‐colored urine, or vomiting. There was also no frothy urine. He was previously healthy with no known medical illness. He was not on any medications such as statins or supplements, and denied recent physical exertion or exercise, trauma, alcohol use, or family history of thyroid or autoimmune diseases.

On examination, he was alert and oriented. His vital signs were stable, with a blood pressure of 112/69 mmHg and a pulse rate of 66 beats/min. Physical findings include mild facial puffiness, without peripheral oedema, dry skin, or goiter. Neurological examination revealed no muscle wasting, proximal myopathy, and normal deep tendon reflexes. Other systemic examinations were unremarkable.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigation and Treatment

Laboratory investigations confirmed overt hypothyroidism, with a free T4 level of 7.60 pmol/L (normal range (NR): 7.86–14.41) and a high TSH level of > 49.40 mIU/L (NR: 0.38–5.33). There were also transaminitis and acute kidney injury (AKI), with aspartate aminotransferase (AST) 130.3 U/L, alanine aminotransferase (ALT) 118.1 U/L, urea 7.3 mmol/L, creatinine 182 μmol/L, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 37.6 mL/min. Further investigations confirmed Hashimoto's thyroiditis, with a positive anti‐thyroid peroxidase (anti‐TPO) antibody of 178 IU/mL (NR: < 35). Given the presence of acute kidney injury (AKI), serum creatine kinase (CK) was assessed and was found to be markedly elevated at 2527 U/L (normal range: 22–198). The constellation of biochemical abnormalities in the absence of other identifiable causes pointed to hypothyroidism as the underlying driver of rhabdomyolysis, which subsequently contributed to AKI. He received intravenous fluids and was started on oral levothyroxine 75 mcg daily, titrated to 100 mcg based on repeat thyroid function tests.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

Over the course of the hospitalization, his renal function and CK levels gradually improved, with full normalization by the fourth week post‐discharge. However, the transaminitis was slow to improve during the admission; hence, further work‐up was sent. Hepatitis screen and autoimmune hepatitis workup, including anti‐LKM, anti‐SMA, anti‐mitochondrial antibodies, immunoglobulins, were unremarkable. Hepatobiliary ultrasound was normal.

Discussion

Hypothyroidism is a common endocrine disorder with a broad clinical spectrum, ranging from mild symptoms to life‐threatening complications. Among its systemic manifestations, musculoskeletal involvement is common due to the pivotal role of thyroid hormone in skeletal muscle function, which regulates mitochondrial function, oxidative metabolism, and muscle fiber composition [4]. In hypothyroidism, diminished mitochondrial activity, reduced ATP production, and fiber‐type switching contribute to impaired muscle function and structural integrity [5]. Although muscle symptoms in hypothyroidism are common, they are frequently underrecognized [6]. Muscle symptoms are reported in up to 79% of hypothyroid patients, but these symptoms often lack specificity and may be overlooked in clinical practice [2].

Elevated serum creatine kinase (CK) levels are frequently observed in hypothyroidism, typically < 10 times the upper limit of normal [7, 8]. A previous study demonstrated that 57% of patients with overt hypothyroidism and 10% of those with subclinical hypothyroidism exhibited elevated CK levels, often in the absence of muscular complaints [7]. A significant positive correlation between CK and TSH (r = 0.432, p = 0.04) and negative correlations between CK and both FT3 (r = −0.556, p = 0.002) and FT4 (r = −0.448, p = 0.04) have been reported [7]. These abnormalities normalized with thyroid hormone replacement therapy.

While mild, asymptomatic elevations of creatine kinase (CK) are frequently observed in hypothyroidism [8], progression to rhabdomyolysis is rare and typically occurs in the presence of additional stressors such as statin use, trauma, or intense exertion [3, 9] Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a recognized complication of rhabdomyolysis, resulting from mechanisms such as myoglobin‐induced nephrotoxicity, renal vasoconstriction, and tubular obstruction by heme pigment casts [10] However, AKI secondary to hypothyroidism‐induced rhabdomyolysis without any precipitating factors is exceedingly rare, with only a few cases reported [11].

Our case highlights an important clinical lesson. The patient presented without the classic triad of rhabdomyolysis: no myalgia, muscle weakness, or dark urine, highlighting the importance of maintaining high clinical suspicion even in atypical presentations [12]. The diagnosis was made purely on biochemical grounds: markedly elevated CK and AKI, in the setting of hypothyroidism. The absence of other contributing factors, such as medication use, trauma, or strenuous exertion, further implicates severe hypothyroidism as the sole precipitant of rhabdomyolysis in this case. Remarkably, the patient achieved complete clinical and biochemical resolution following vigorous hydration and thyroid hormone replacement. This case highlights the potential role of routine CK monitoring in overt hypothyroid patients, especially those presenting with fatigue, muscle weakness, or unexplained renal impairment. Moreover, it also emphasizes the importance of considering thyroid function evaluation in patients presenting with unexplained CK elevation or rhabdomyolysis, particularly when classical triggers are absent [3].

In hypothyroidism‐induced rhabdomyolysis, treatment entails both aggressive fluid resuscitation to mitigate renal injury and timely thyroid hormone replacement [11, 13]. Electrolyte monitoring is essential during the acute phase. Prompt initiation of thyroid hormone therapy is equally critical [3]. CK levels should be monitored until normalization, and renal function should be closely followed during hospitalization and early outpatient care. Our patient responded well to a relatively conservative levothyroxine titration protocol, with resolution of both CK elevation and hypothyroid symptoms within 1 month. These findings reinforce the notion that severe hypothyroidism, even in the absence of external triggers, can independently precipitate rhabdomyolysis and AKI [14].

Conclusion

This case highlights rhabdomyolysis as an uncommon complication of severe hypothyroidism, and its presence without any external precipitating factors is even rarer. While muscle symptoms and mild elevation of CK are common in hypothyroidism, progression to rhabdomyolysis typically requires additional triggers such as trauma or infections. Our patient's presentation was atypical, with no classic symptoms of rhabdomyolysis or identifiable risk factors. Diagnosis was based on elevated CK level, high creatinine, and confirmed severe hypothyroidism. The patient responded well to intravenous hydration and thyroid hormone replacement, with complete recovery of renal function and normalization of CK levels. Early diagnosis and prompt treatment are essential to prevent complications and ensure optimal patient outcomes.
