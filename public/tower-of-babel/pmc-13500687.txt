VerbaFake, EchoFake, and PixelFake: lightweight architectures with advanced augmentation for unimodal deepfake detection
Authors: Usman Ali, Muhammad Rizwan Shoukat, Mahjbeen Rajput, Saeed Ali Omar Bahaj, Muhammad I Khan, Tariq Mahmood
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rapid evolution of deepfake technology, driven by deep learning and generative models such as Generative Adversarial Networks, has revolutionized digital content creation while simultaneously introducing serious threats to privacy, security, and digital trust. This study addresses the increasing risks posed by deepfakes, synthetically generated images, audio, and text, by developing a comprehensive unimodal deepfake detection framework. The system leverages a range of deep learning models including CNNs, attention mechanism (NLP), GRU and BiLSTM (RNNs), and capsule networks. For image detection, models such as MobileNetV2, EfficientNetV2-S, and the PixelFake (scratch-built) model achieved high accuracy, with MobileNetV2 and EfficientNetV2-S reaching 97%. Text-based detection models, including a VerbaFake (scratch-built), Efficient-CapsNet, and Attention-based BiLSTM, consistently achieved 99% accuracy. Audio deepfake detection models, such as Xception, DenseNet201, and the EchoFake (scratch-built) model, attained accuracies of 96%, 97%, and 98%, respectively.The framework integrates specialized pipelines for each modality, ensuring unimodal consistency and enhancing detection reliability.Publicly available datasets from Kaggle and additional curated sources were used, comprising over 26,000 image samples, 20,000 audio clips, and 31,429 text entries.Experimental results confirm the system’s effectiveness, especially when employing CNN-based models.This research contributes a lightweight, real-time-capable solution with practical applications in journalism, cybersecurity, and law enforcement. While also raising awareness about the ethical implications of deepfakes and informing policy development. Introduction

Although the expression “deepfakes” is just a few years old in 2017, the underlying technology has been developing for years. Early attempts at creating realistic synthetic media can be traced back to the 1990s, with researchers using CGI to create human-like images1. However, it was the development of deep learning methods, particularly GANs in 2010, that significantly advanced the capabilities of deepfake technology. Deepfakes rely on swapping faces, changing facial expressions, or even generating entirely new faces, using complex algorithms, such as Generative Adversarial Networks (GAN) or other forms of advanced neural networks2. Deepfakes can be used to spread misinformation about a particular subject, defame people, infringe on someone’s privacy, and even aid cybercrime3. For example, clips of public figures lying may spread false information in public while heavily influencing mass opinion in society4. A troubling report from New Hampshire revealed that an AI-generated fake voice resembling President Joe Biden was used in robocalls aimed at discouraging Democratic voters from participating in the primary election5.

In the UK in 2020, criminals used AI to simulate the voice of a German CEO, misleading an employee into shifting 243,000 dollars to a deceitful account. This is one of the first known cases where fake audio was used in a financial scam6. In America in March 2023, deepfakes were generated that showed that Trump was arrested by police, which was initially posted on Twitter. In May 2019, two artists collaborating with the company Cannyai created a deepfake video of Mark Zuckerberg talking about controlling data from billions of people7. Different techniques/methods are used to detect different types of deepfakes, such as text, audio, image, and video, to mitigate the concern of misuse of anyone’s identity, face, voice, or any email, news, etc. Deepfake text is written using LLMs such as Longformer, RoBERTa, and GPT. These human-like text styles make it difficult to differentiate between AI-generated and real content8,9. Fast models utilize factual and semantic consistency graphs to detect deepfake text. Combined approaches of TF-IDF and BPE are used for text detection. BPE tokenizes text into sub-parts, and TF-IDF converts the text into numeric features; both use Bayesian classifiers. By combining the predicted results of both approaches, the classification is improved10. DetectGPT focuses on perplexity metrics to classify whether the text is AI-generated or human-written11. Fake audio creation utilizes TTS systems and voice cloning to make a human-like voice. Techniques like Tacotron and WaveNet make it possible to differentiate synthetic voices from real ones12. Some models, like ResNet2 and ResNet, use CQCCS and MFCCs to check pitch variations13. The MFCC technique is used to extract features from audio. The audio is segmented into smaller parts for better results. Most relevant and important features are selected, and many machine learning algorithms are implemented to predict the performance14,15. Self-supervised models use raw waveforms to give good accuracy for detection. Using raw waveforms, it improves the detection efficiency even under noise16. Deepfake images are generated by changing facial expressions or the complete face. Particularly, GANs are used to generate synthetic images, and architectures like XceptionNet and EfficientNet are also used for deepfake creation. CNN-based architectures are used to detect manipulated images like Mesonet, which are first trained on fake and real data. Other architectures are ResNet and EfficientNet, which are used for the same purpose10,17. Researchers are now focusing on sophisticated detection techniques that leverage inconsistencies in the way deepfake algorithms process images and videos. A real-time deepfake gaze and blink pattern detection framework using TimeSformer and a hybrid Transformer-CNN is used for deepfake video detection18, and a hybrid deep learning model, which is the combination of ResNet-101 and MesoNet4, with the analysis of eye movement19.

In this research, lightweight deepfake detection frameworks for text, image, and audio are presented using CNNs, pretrained models, RNNs with attention, custom scratch-built models, and Capsule Networks. By using the curated datasets and optimized preprocessing, the system obtained 99% accuracy in text, 97% in image, and 98% in audio, making it a reliable and source-effective solution for real-world applications.

Research challenges

In previous research, many limitations have been found, such as a lack of the latest models. The faceforensics++ dataset is mostly used. Even recent models have not produced satisfactory results. Accuracy declines when the model is tested on the unseen dataset. Lack of real-time applications due to the high and complex computations of models. Absence of datasets reflecting real-world conditions, such as social media platforms. Previous research shows a lack of data generalization and the dangers of adversarial attack detection. Minimal use of lightweight models. Implementing a multi-model deepfake detection system is very complex and difficult to apply in real-time due to high computational requirements.

Achieved 97.6% accuracy on FF++ and emphasized fine-grained feature extraction. Alfarouk et al.31 introduced a new technique for deepfake detection using a hybrid architecture combining CNNs and LSTMs. Trained in DFDC and Ciplab datasets, it achieved 98.24% accuracy with very low error rates. Patel et al.32 proposed a novel approach that includes training CNN models on a large dataset of deepfakes and fine-tuning the model on a smaller dataset by using transfer learning. M.Javed et al.33 introduced a multimodal deepfake detection architecture that merges CNN and Vision Transformer with Diffusion Models to enhance robustness and process audio-visual.

With some methods, it acquired an accuracy of over 90%. They evaluated eight models with an ensemble model achieving 0.902 AUC,0.87 pAUC, and 0.192. Pu et al.38 evaluate deepfake text detection models, finding semantic feature-based models like FAST more resilient to adversarial evasion and propose DFTFooler for low-cost evasion. Methodology

In this research, the proposed framework leverages a unimodal model architecture to detect deepfakes across text, image, and audio domains, ensuring robustness and cross-domain consistency. For textual analysis, models including Attention-based BiLSTM, Efficient-CapsNet, and a scratch-built CNN-GRU hybrid were applied to the DAIGT “Catch the AI” dataset (31,429 samples). Preprocessing steps such as tokenization, stopword removal and One-Hot encoding were performed to convert raw text into meaningful numerical representations. These representations were then used to train models that effectively distinguished human-written text from AI-generated counterparts with accuracies up to 99%. For image and audio detection, the system integrated lightweight yet powerful architectures. A custom image dataset of 26,000 real and fake samples was preprocessed through grayscale conversion, resizing, and normalization before being fed into models such as EfficientNetV2-S, MobileNetV2, and a scratch-built CNN. Similarly, 20,000 audio samples were resampled to 16 kHz, converted to Mel spectrograms, and augmented with noise and masking techniques before being passed into Xception, DenseNet, and custom CNN models, achieving accuracies as high as 98%. By combining specialized pipelines for each modality, the framework ensures reliable, real-time detection across diverse multimedia content. The proposed model codes and associated data are publicly available at GitHub repository, with DOI: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.19232320.

Data pre-processing

It involves structuring raw data into a format suitable for machine learning and deep learning models. Improves data quality, removes irrelevant information, and feeds only relevant data to models to learn meaningful patterns. It is a very important step in data analysis and model training45.

Pre-processing of text dataset: The preprocessing step converts raw data into a suitable format for model training. It removes unnecessary text elements that are not meaningful for training. Tokenization breaks text into smaller units called characters, sub-words, words, or tokens, making the data structured for model processing. Stopwords like is, are, and in are removed to focus on meaningful text. Since ML and DL models can’t process text directly, text vectorization converts it into numerical form using methods such as One-Hot Encoding, Count Vectorization, and TF-IDF. Labels are also encoded for classification tasks, as most models require numerical input.

Comparison analysis based on classification report

All of the models demonstrated outstanding performance, with high levels of consistency and precision across a variety of domains. In the text category, both Efficient-CapsNet and the VerbaFake (scratch-built) model performed exceptionally well, surpassing the Attention-based BiLSTM, which received 98% of the ratings, with 99% of the metrics. The PixelFake (scratch-built) image model achieved 92% accuracy in the picture domain, while MobileNetV2 achieved 97%, indicating that there is room for progress. Generally, all models exhibited excellent precision and recall, making them dependable options for deepfake identification across diverse data kinds. Although PixelFake achieved good accuracy, it shows poor performance compared to other models. There are many cases of PixelFake’s failure. The architectural depth of PixelFake is insufficient to detect the complex patterns present in images. It consists of only a few convolutional layers, which makes it unable to extract high semantic features. Another reason for PixelFake’s failure is the absence of attention mechanisms, which limit the model’s power to get localized artifacts that are present in manipulated parts of images. Too complex dataset features for a simple CNN are also a failure case. PixelFake accepts only grayscale inputs, and it loses important chromatic information. These are the failure cases of PixelFake, which contribute to the model’s poor performance. In addition, the absence of feature aggregation or attention makes it less robust against localized manipulations and results in a relatively higher misclassification rate against complex samples. Performance comparison analysis

In deepfake text detection, three different models are evaluated: the performance of all text models is shown in Fig. 8(a) and Table 7, an Attention-based BiLSTM, an Efficient-CapsNet, and a VerbaFake (scratch-built) model. The Attention-based model has achieved 99% accuracy, 98% recall, 99% F1-score, and 99% precision. It slightly improved contextual comprehension by using the attention mechanism to model dependencies in long text sequences. The Efficient-Capsnet also achieves 99% accuracy, while all other matrices achieve 99% due to its ability to model spatial hierarchies in the feature representation using capsule layers. Surprisingly, the scratch-built model has performed outstandingly well, obtaining 99% across all evaluation metrics. This means that a well-structured, lightweight architecture can perform as well as other complex models without increasing computational complexity. Figure 8(a) and Table 7 show the comparison of all text models, which shows that all models performed outstandingly, but the scratch-built model is more effective than other models due to its less complexity in architecture. In deepfake audio detection, models are trained on 20,000 real and fake audio samples. We used two pre-trained models, Xception and DenseNet201, and an EchoFake (scratch-built) model. Xception has obtained 96% accuracy, 96% F1-score, and 98% recall. Although it struggled with precision, which is 95%. DenseNet201 has achieved an overall performance of 97% accuracy and F1-score, 96% precision, and 98% recall. This advancement is due to its densely connected architecture. However, the scratch-built model has performed surprisingly better than the two pre-trained models at 98% in all evaluation measures. This model is lightweight and simpler than the other two pre-trained models. This model’s achievement can be attributed to efficient design and effective preprocessing, by the use of SpecAugment and Gaussian noise for data augmentation. These methods enhanced generalization by making the model less sensitive to variations in the input. In particular, ablation-style experiments showed that the elimination of SpecAugment and Gaussian noise resulted in a considerable drop in recall, showing that these augmentations play crucial role in enhancing robustness to spectral variations. These effects were found to be most pronounced in less complex architecture. Figure 8(b) and Table 7 show the dominance of the scratch-built model despite its simplicity. In image-based deepfake detection, we have analyzed the performance of two pre-trained models, EfficientNetV2-S and MobileNetV2, and a PixelFake (scratch-built) model. The EfficientNetV2-S model has achieved an accuracy and F1-score of 97%, and a recall and precision of 96%, proving to be a potent compound scaling model with efficient convolutional blocks. Figure 8(c) and Table 7 show that the MobileNetV2 model has obtained 97% of all evaluation metrics, making it a better option for real-time or mobile applications due to its lighter architecture. Though optimal in training time, the scratch-built model has a lower accuracy of 92%, and all other evaluation metrics are the same, as mentioned in Table 7, and is not successful at detecting subtle image manipulation. This model could not perform as well as the other two pre-trained models. MobileNetV2 has performed outstandingly well among all three models. An interesting finding was that converting images to grayscale and then re-encoding them as RGB enhanced classification metrics by 1–2%, particularly the recall, as it helped the models to focus on structure and texture instead of color-derived artifacts. However, PixelFake showed a limited benefit from this input variation because of its shallow feature hierarchy and lack of multiscale feature aggregation. The comparison results clearly show that MobileNet-V2 has an optimal balance between performance and resource usage.

Comparison with state-of-the-art models

The state-of-the-art deepfake detectors often achieve strong benchmark accuracy, but real-world deployment demands a balance between accuracy, model size and inference speed as highlighted in Tables 8 and 13. In text domain all evaluated models are compact(around 2M parameters and under 35MB).Efficient-CapsNet is slightly heavier and slower while scratch built VerbaFake model achieves the fastest inference time (103 ms) and still delivers near state of the art performance showing that high textual detection accuracy does not require deeper or larger architectures. Conversely, GPT-2-based detectors10 have a small footprint but show weaker performance (85% F1), underscoring the classic trade-off between efficiency and accuracy when using LLM-based models. For audio, large pretrained models like Xception and DenseNet201 offer strong detection (96–97% F1/AUC) but with high latency (>300ms) and storage (>70 MB). MobileNetV2 offers a lightweight architecture with good accuracy(97%). Overall, three trade-off patterns emerge: large SOTA models maximize accuracy but cost the most, lightweight fine-tuned backbones offer the best balance, and scratch-built models provide strong efficiency with only small accuracy compromises, making them practical for real-world deepfake detection. Discussion

Scratch-built models across all modalities have achieved high competitive accuracy while being less complex. This renders them extremely suitable for real-time applications and on resource-limited devices. Every model is optimized with effective pre-processing, MFCC and specAugment for audio, gray-scale to RGB conversion for image modality, and tokenization with TF-IDF/BPE for text-boosting their performance and generalizability. The detection models performed consistently high: 99% in text, 98% in audio, and 97% in image domains, aligning closely with or surpassing established benchmarks. The balanced dataset creation and usage across modalities improved model fairness and reduced bias toward any specific class, real or fake. A comparison with the related studies is shown in the Table 13.

Limitations and failure causes

Current research analyzes each modality separately. Cross-modal inconsistencies in real-world deepfakes need to be jointly analyzed. Despite high validation accuracy, the models may underperform on deepfakes generated by new techniques, as examined in other recent works. Although lightweight models are efficient, they may not have the depth required to detect very subtle manipulations compared to deep architectures, such as the Transformer and GAN discriminators. These models may also struggle with low-resolution images.

Conclusion & future work

This research emphasizes that highly effective deepfake detection in text, audio, and image modalities can be made highly effective by using carefully curated and preprocessed datasets along with lightweight deep learning models. The scratch-built models provided excellent performance, with up to 99% accuracy in text, 98% in audio, and 92% in images, while maintaining computational efficiency acceptable for real-time deployment. This research shows that simpler models can perform better than complex architectures. When they are properly designed and well-trained. The study further emphasizes the value of data preprocessing like tokenization in text, Mel-spectrogram with augmentation in audio, and normalization to grayscale in images, which collectively contribute to increasing model performance and generalizability.

The possible real-world applications of this research are immense. This system can be used for the authentication of any news, photo, or audio in the media or journalism to avoid misinformation. This detection approach can also be used in cybersecurity for fraud prevention for individuals or organizations against phishing attacks, identity fraud, and financial scams with deepfakes. Social media can use this technique to detect manipulated content, thereby improving user safety and trust. Digital forensic labs and law enforcement teams can utilize it to authenticate the multimedia evidence, which would improve the integrity of investigations and the judicial process. Finally, this technology can be used in educational and digital rights management to maintain content authenticity and safeguard intellectual property rights.

Building on these results, several future lines for advancements are given. Enabling more efficient multimodal deepfake detection by the use of advanced fusion techniques, such as attention-based or transformer-based cross-modal fusion, may rely on leveraging complementary information across data types. Further expanding the datasets to include larger and more diverse samples developed by the latest generative models. Incorporating XAI frameworks is also advisable to improve model prediction ability, thereby fostering confidence and transparency, particularly in sensitive applications like journalism, law enforcement, digital forensic labs, and cybersecurity.

The text dataset can be downloaded from https://www.kaggle.com/datacoll-datacoll-data-for-competition, image datasets from https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/manjilkarki/deepfake-and-realimages and https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/ciplab/real-and-fake-face-detection, and the audio dataset from https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/birdy654/deep-voice-deepfake-voice-recognition.

The custom code used for this study is publicly available on GitHub at https://github.com/UsmanAlExp/DeepFake-Detection. To ensure long-term preservation and versioned access, the version of the code corresponding to this study has been archived in Zenodo and is available at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.19232320. The repository includes documentation for installation and use.
