A contrastive reconstruction vision transformer model for intelligent diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder
Authors: Lumin Xing, Zhengyin Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Automated assessment through the analysis of facial expressions in autism can assist in early screening, providing strong support for timely intervention and contributing to the healthy development of patients. However, current deep learning models still face several challenges in the practical application of intelligent diagnosis for Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD). First, traditional models struggle to fully capture subtle facial expression changes and local fine-grained features. Second, existing models primarily rely on manually annotated facial expression images for supervised training, often overlooking unlabeled facial expression data. Therefore, in this study, supported by the Internet of Medical Things, we propose a Contrastive Reconstruction Vision Transformer (CREViT) model for the intelligent diagnosis of ASD. The design concept of the CREViT model integrates contrastive learning, autoencoder, and Vision Transformer (ViT). Its core advantage lies in the combination of ViT and autoencoders, enabling the model to more accurately capture subtle changes in facial expression images and learn richer and higher-quality image representations. Furthermore, by leveraging contrastive learning, the CREViT model effectively reduces its dependence on labeled data while fully utilizing unlabeled data, thereby improving the model’s generalization capability. We conducted comprehensive experiments on a real-world Autism Spectrum Disorder facial expression dataset. The results reveal that the CREViT model performs exceptionally well in intelligent ASD diagnosis, significantly enhancing prediction accuracy and model generalization ability.

Introduction

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD)1–4 is a neurodevelopmental condition marked by social difficulties and repetitive behaviors. In recent years, the prevalence of ASD has been increasing annually5–7. Due to the challenges in improving core symptoms, many individuals with ASD struggle to integrate into mainstream society, making this issue a pressing social concern.

Currently, educational intervention8,9 is the primary approach to promoting ASD rehabilitation. Early detection of autism is crucial for maximizing the effectiveness of interventions. However, traditional scale-based assessment methods have limitations, including strong subjectivity and low efficiency, highlighting the urgent need for improvement through intelligent technologies. In recent years, deep learning-based intelligent diagnosis methods for autism have made significant progress. Among them, automated assessment through the analysis of facial expressions in autism has proven to be a valuable tool for early screening, providing strong support for timely intervention and contributing to the healthy development of patients.

With advances in artificial intelligence and biomedical imaging, recent studies have increasingly applied machine and deep learning techniques to the automated detection of ASD10,11. These approaches combine diverse data sources, including facial images, functional MRI, EEG signals, and eye-tracking data, to improve diagnostic accuracy.

First, Khosla et al.12 employed deep learning models to detect ASD using facial images. Their study analyzed facial features, such as the distance and arrangement of the eyes, nose, and lips, to determine whether they had ASD. Compared to traditional brain imaging-based diagnostic methods, this approach incorporates denoising preprocessing, making it more suitable for real-world applications. Mittal et al.13 proposed the DL-ASD framework for dynamically analyzing and predicting whether aged 1-10 have ASD. Their study utilized a Kaggle dataset and trained an improved convolutional neural network (CNN) for ASD classification. This method incorporated feature computation and optimization techniques such as dropout and batch normalization to enhance model performance while accurately predicting six specific emotions and four common emotions. Lai et al.14 utilized machine learning methods to analyze retinal images to improve ASD classification accuracy.

Additionally, Yin et al.15 proposed a novel approach that combines machine learning and neuroimaging techniques for ASD diagnosis. Their study constructed brain functional networks using functional magnetic resonance imaging data and employed an autoencoder to extract high-level features, followed by deep neural networks (DNN) for classification. After incorporating a pre-trained autoencoder, the accuracy improved to 79.2%, and the AUC increased to 82.4%. The results demonstrated that this deep learning approach outperformed existing techniques. Ghazal et al.16 classified ASD using facial features such as lack of eye contact, uncommon gestures, or unusual body movements. They developed a deep transfer learning model, AlexNet, based on the Kaggle dataset. Experimental results showed that this model achieved a detection accuracy of 87.7%, surpassing other existing ASD detection models and showing promise for clinical early screening. Gary et al.17 proposed a hybrid approach that integrates deep learning with explainable artificial intelligence to identify the most critical features, enhancing ASD prediction accuracy while providing explanations and recommendations for diagnostic results, thereby aiding in the early clinical screening of ASD symptoms.

Similarly, Ahmed et al.18 pointed out the limitations of existing deep learning models in utilizing data richness. They were the first to attempt ASD classification using single-volume brain images, designing an image generator to extract single-volume images from whole-brain scans. They evaluated four deep learning methods and their ensemble classifier. Using five-fold cross-validation, their study tested four preprocessing pipelines on the large-scale, multi-site ABIDE brain imaging dataset and demonstrated that this approach outperformed existing techniques, exhibiting robustness and consistency.

Han et al.19 proposed a multimodal diagnostic framework that integrates EEG and eye-tracking (ET) data to enhance identification accuracy. Their study employed a deep learning-based stacked denoising autoencoder (SDAE) for two-step feature learning and fusion: first, two separate SDAE models were trained for EEG and ET data to extract features; then, a third SDAE model was used to fuse the features from both modalities, capturing the correlations and complementarity between behavioral and neurophysiological characteristics to improve classification performance. The study collected multimodal data from 40 children with ASD and 50 typically developing children for evaluation. Experimental results showed that this method outperformed single-modality and simple feature fusion approaches, demonstrating high diagnostic accuracy and providing an objective and reliable clinical diagnostic support tool.

However, the practical application of deep learning models in intelligent ASD diagnosis still faces numerous challenges. A key issue that urgently needs to be addressed is how to improve the model’s reliability, stability, and generalization performance.

First, traditional models typically use a CNN model as a feature extraction module to extract features from facial expression images. However, CNN-based models have limitations in modeling long-range dependencies within facial regions, making it difficult to fully capture subtle facial expression changes and local fine-grained features. This, in turn, affects the precise recognition of autism-related facial expression characteristics.

Second, existing models mainly rely on manually annotated facial expression images for supervised training, often neglecting unlabeled facial expression data. In the field of computer vision, making full use of unlabeled data can improve a model’s ability to learn image features, allowing it to obtain richer and more efficient representations. By leveraging unsupervised learning methods, models can reduce their dependence on manually labeled data, improve generalization ability, and better adapt to the complex and diverse nature of facial expression features.

Therefore, in this study, we propose a Contrastive Reconstruction Vision Transformer (CREViT) model for intelligent ASD diagnosis, as shown in Fig. 1. The design concept of the CREViT model integrates contrastive learning20–22, autoencoder23–25, and Vision Transformer (ViT) based encoder26–29. Its core advantage lies in the combination of a ViT-based encoder and autoencoders, enabling the model to more accurately capture subtle changes in facial expression images, thereby learning richer and higher-quality image representations. Additionally, by leveraging contrastive learning, the CREViT model effectively reduces its dependence on labeled data while fully utilizing unlabeled data, enhancing its generalization capability.Fig. 1The network architecture of the CREViT model.

The network architecture of the CREViT model.

Specifically, for a given facial expression image, the CREViT model follows this processing workflow: First, an image augmentation strategy is applied to generate two augmented versions of the facial expression image. Then, these two augmented images are separately fed into the same ViT-based autoencoder model to learn their respective image representations. Next, the CREViT model optimizes the learned representations using contrastive learning, ensuring that different augmented views of the same image are drawn closer together while views from different images are pushed apart, thereby improving representation learning capability. Finally, the extracted features of the target image are input into a prediction function to obtain the corresponding ASD diagnosis category.

The computation steps of the ViT-based autoencoder model are as follows: The input image is processed by the ViT-based encoder to learn its representation, which is then fed into a decoder to reconstruct the image. The error between the reconstructed image and the original image is calculated, and by minimizing this error, the model learns higher-quality image features. Moreover, reconstruction loss, contrastive loss, and cross-entropy loss are optimized simultaneously to ensure their contribution to the parameters within the CREViT model. We conducted comprehensive experiments on a real-world ASD facial expression dataset. The results reveal that the CREViT model performs exceptionally well in intelligent ASD diagnosis, significantly improving prediction accuracy and model generalization capability.

Results

First, we compare CREViT with several representative deep learning architectures to demonstrate its superiority in classification performance. Third, we conduct ablation studies to investigate the contributions of different components within the framework. Finally, we analyze the impact of key hyperparameters, including the learning rate, on the model performance.

Among the CNN-based baselines, DenseNet121 achieves competitive results with an AUC of 0.962 and an Accuracy of 0.900, while ResNet18 and MobileNetV3-Large exhibit relatively lower performance, likely due to limited representational capacity. Modern CNN architectures such as EfficientNet-B0, RegNet-Y, and ConvNeXt-Tiny further improve performance; notably, ConvNeXt-Tiny achieves an Accuracy of 0.923 and an F1-Score of 0.923, yet still remains slightly inferior to CREViT in overall classification effectiveness.

Transformer-based models, including ViT-B/16, DeiT-Small, and Swin Transformer variants, demonstrate strong representation learning ability but show relatively reduced recall and F1-Score. For instance, ViT-B/16 achieves an AUC of 0.924 but suffers from lower recall (0.767) and Accuracy (0.830). Swin-based models improve performance compared with ViT, yet they do not surpass CREViT in terms of Accuracy or F1-Score.

Overall, these results indicate that the CREViT model can more effectively distinguish children with autism spectrum disorder from neurotypical controls while maintaining high classification stability and generalization ability. Compared with a wide range of CNN-based and transformer-based architectures, CREViT consistently achieves superior or comparable performance across multiple evaluation metrics, highlighting its robustness in the ASD intelligent diagnosis task.

The performance advantage of CREViT can be attributed to its integrated framework that combines ViT representations with an autoencoder structure. This design enables the model to capture subtle and fine-grained variations in facial expression images, leading to richer and more discriminative feature representations. Furthermore, the incorporation of contrastive learning allows CREViT to effectively leverage unlabeled data, reducing reliance on large-scale annotations and further enhancing generalization performance.

Ablation study

We first investigate the impact of encoder architecture on the performance of the proposed framework. To this end, we evaluate three representative encoder backbones–ResNet, ViT, and MaxViT–under identical training settings. All models are trained for 30 epochs on the same dataset, with all other components of the framework kept unchanged.

The quantitative results indicate a clear performance gap among different encoders. After identifying MaxViT as the most effective encoder, we further examine the contribution of contrastive learning within the CREViT framework. Specifically, we compare the complete CREViT model, which incorporates contrastive loss, with a variant in which the contrastive loss is removed. Both models are trained for 30 epochs using the same encoder, dataset, and optimization strategy.

The results shown in Table 2 and Fig. 5 demonstrate that the inclusion of contrastive learning leads to consistent performance improvements across multiple evaluation metrics. The full CREViT model achieves an AUC of 0.968, an Accuracy of 0.927, and an F1-score of 0.925, whereas the model without contrastive loss attains an AUC of 0.965, an Accuracy of 0.900, and an F1-score of 0.896. These findings suggest that contrastive learning provides complementary benefits beyond encoder selection alone. By encouraging feature-level consistency across augmented views and enhancing inter-sample separability, the contrastive loss improves the quality of learned representations, thereby strengthening the overall robustness of the CREViT model.

The impact of learning rate

To determine an appropriate learning rate, we perform a grid search while keeping all other hyperparameters fixed. Specifically, the learning rate is varied across four representative values: 1 × 10−5, 1 × 10−4, 3 × 10−4, and 1 × 10−3. All models are trained for 30 epochs under identical experimental settings, and performance is evaluated on the validation set.

The learning rate comparison results are illustrated in Fig. 6. Among the evaluated settings, a learning rate of 1 × 10−4 achieves the best overall performance, with an AUC of 0.968, an Accuracy of 0.927, and an F1-score of 0.925. In contrast, a smaller learning rate of 1 × 10−5 results in slower convergence and inferior final performance, yielding an Accuracy of 0.860 and an F1-score of 0.853. When the learning rate is increased to 3 × 10−4, the model exhibits degraded performance, particularly in recall and classification stability, with an Accuracy of 0.837 and an F1-score of 0.827. A further increase to 1 × 10−3 leads to similarly suboptimal results, with an AUC of 0.915 and an Accuracy of 0.833.Fig. 6Impact of learning rate on model performance.

Impact of learning rate on model performance.

These results reveal a clear trade-off between convergence speed and representation quality. Smaller learning rates promote stable optimization but may converge slowly, whereas larger learning rates accelerate convergence at the cost of reduced discriminative performance. The learning rate of 1 × 10−4 provides the most favorable balance between optimization stability and final classification accuracy.

Discussion

In this work, we propose CREViT for the intelligent diagnosis of ASD. The design of the CREViT model integrates contrastive learning, autoencoders, and a ViT-based encoder. Its core advantage lies in the combination of a ViT-based encoder and autoencoders, which enables the model to capture subtle variations in facial expression images more accurately, thereby learning richer and higher-quality image representations. Additionally, leveraging contrastive learning, the CREViT model effectively reduces reliance on labeled data while fully utilizing unlabeled data, enhancing the model’s generalization ability.

We conducted comprehensive experiments on a real ASD facial expression dataset, and the results demonstrate that the CREViT model excels in the intelligent diagnosis of autism, significantly improving prediction accuracy and generalization performance.

However, the CREViT model has certain limitations. Currently, it primarily relies on facial expression images for diagnosis and does not fully utilize multimodal information (such as speech and behavioral patterns), which may lead to an incomplete characterization of autism-related features. In future work, we plan to incorporate multiple modalities to enhance the accuracy of ASD diagnosis.

The design concept of the CREViT model integrates contrastive learning, autoencoders, and ViT. Its core advantage lies in the combination of ViT and autoencoders, enabling the model to more accurately capture subtle changes in facial expression images, thereby learning richer and higher-quality image representations.

Additionally, by leveraging contrastive learning, the CREViT model effectively reduces dependence on labeled data while fully utilizing unlabeled data, enhancing the model’s generalization capability.

Dataset

We collected facial expression images of autism and non-autistic children as the dataset for this section. The data was sourced from the well-known competition platform Kaggle. Specifically, there are 1,468 facial expression images of children with autism and 1,468 images of non-autistic children, with a resolution of 224 × 224.

To enhance the training effectiveness of the model, we applied standardized preprocessing to the dataset and split it into the training set, validation set, and test set in a specific ratio to ensure the model’s generalization capability.
