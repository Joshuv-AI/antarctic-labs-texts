Spherical quadrilateral with three right angles and its application for diameter of extreme points of a convex body


Abstract




Marek Lassak

Abstract
.
We prove a theorem on the relationships between the lengths of sides of a spherical quadrilateral with three right angles.
They are analogous to the relationships in the Lambert quadrilateral in the hyperbolic plane.
We apply this theorem in the proof of our second theorem that if
C
is a two-dimensional spherical convex body of diameter
\delta\in(\frac{1}{2}\pi,\pi)
, then the diameter of the set of extreme points of
C
is at least
2\arccos\big(\frac{1}{4}(\cos\delta+\sqrt{\cos^{2}\delta+8})\big)
.
This estimate cannot be improved.

Mathematical Subject Classification (2010).
52A55.

Keywords:
diameter, extreme point, Lambert quadrilateral, spherical convex body, spherical quadrilateral

Introduction

Denote by
S^{n}
be the unit sphere of the
(n+1)
-dimensional Euclidean space
E^{n+1}
.
In this article we mostly deal with
S^{2}
.

Let us recall some needed notions from the spherical geometry.
By a
great circle
of
S^{2}
we mean the intersection of
S^{2}
with any two-dimensional linear subspace of
E^{3}
.
By a pair of
antipodes
of
S^{2}
we understand any pair of points being the intersection of
S^{2}
with a one-dimensional subspace of
E^{3}
.
Observe that if two different points
p,q\in S^{2}
are not antipodes, there is exactly one great circle passing through them.
By the
arc

pq
connecting these points we mean the shorter part of the great circle through
p
and
q
, and
by the
distance

|pq|
of
p
and
q
we understand the length of the arc
pq
.

A subset of
S^{2}
is called
convex
if it does not contain any pair of antipodes of
S^{2}
and if together with every two points it contains the arc connecting them.
A closed convex set
C\subset S^{2}
with non-empty interior is called a
convex body
.
Its boundary is denoted by
{\rm bd}(C)
.
A point of
C
is called
extreme
if it does not belong to the relative interior of any arc with end-points in
C
.
The set of extreme points of
C
is denoted by
E(C)
.
Let us add that convexity in
S^{n}
is considered in very many papers and monographs (see [1–12]).

The set of points of
S^{2}
in the distance at most
\rho
, where
0<\rho\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
, from a fixed point of
S^{2}
is called a
spherical disk
of
radius

\rho
.
Spherical disks of radius
\frac{\pi}{2}
are called
hemispheres
.
Two hemispheres whose centers are antipodes are called
opposite hemispheres
.
The set of points of a great circle of
S^{2}
which are at a distance at most
\frac{\pi}{2}
from a fixed point
p
of this great circle is called a
semicircle
.
We call
p
the
center
of this semicircle.

If non-opposite hemispheres
G
and
H
are different, then
L=G\cap H
is called a
lune
.
As in
[
6
]
and
[
7
]
, the semicircles bounding
L
and contained in
G
and
H
, respectively, are denoted by
G/H
and
H/G
.
The
thickness

\Delta(L)
of
L
is defined as the distance between the centers of
G/H
and
H/G
.
The two common points of the semicircles bounding
L
are called
corners
of
L
.

Recall a lemma from
[
10
]
:

for every convex body
C\subset S^{2}
of diameter at most
\frac{\pi}{2}
we have
{\rm diam}(E(C))={\rm diam}(C)
.

In the present paper we consider an analogous property for every convex body
C\subset S^{2}
of diameter over
\frac{\pi}{2}
.
Namely, it is proved in Theorem 2 that
{\rm diam}(E(C))\geq 2\arccos\big(\frac{1}{4}(\cos\delta+\sqrt{\cos^{2}\!\delta+8})\big)
for every convex body
C\subset S^{2}
of diameter over
\frac{\pi}{2}
.
In order to overcome some troubles in its proof, it is necessary to find a length of a side of a quadrilateral with three right angles.

We perform the above task in Theorem 1, where we present some relationships between the lengths of sides of a spherical quadrilateral with three right angles, similar to the relationships in the Lambert quadrilateral in the hyperbolic plane.

2
Some properties of lunes

The following function, where
\delta\in(\frac{1}{2}\pi,\pi)
, plays an important role in our research:

\varphi(\delta)=\arccos\big(\frac{1}{4}(\cos\delta+\sqrt{\cos^{2}\!\delta+8})\big).

Lemma 1
.

The function
\varphi(\delta)
is increasing and thus one-valued in the interval
(\frac{1}{2}\pi,\pi)
.
The set of its values is the interval
(\frac{1}{4}\pi,\frac{1}{3}\pi)
.
We have
\cos\delta=\frac{2\cos^{2}\!\varphi-1}{\cos\varphi}
for
\varphi
from this interval, where

\varphi(\delta)
is shortly denoted by
\varphi
.

Proof.

The derivative of
\;\cos\delta+\sqrt{\cos^{2}\!\delta+8}\;
is
\;-\sin\delta\;\!(1+\cos\delta/\!\sqrt{\cos^{2}\delta+8})
.
Since
\sqrt{\cos^{2}\!\delta+8}>-\cos\delta\;
for
\;\delta\in(\frac{1}{2}\pi,\pi)
, we conclude that this derivative is negative in the interval
(\frac{1}{2}\pi,\pi)
.
Hence
\cos\delta+\sqrt{\cos^{2}\!\delta+8}
is a decreasing function of
\delta
in the interval
(\frac{1}{2}\pi,\pi)
.
So
\varphi(\delta)
is increasing there.
Hence it is one-valued.
Moreover, from
\varphi(\frac{1}{2}\pi)=\frac{1}{4}\pi
and
\varphi(\pi)=\frac{1}{3}\pi
, by the continuity of
\varphi(\delta)
we get the second thesis.
The third one results by a simple calculation.
∎

Here is a lemma analogous to Lemma from
[
10
]
, where a lune of thickness at most
\frac{\pi}{2}
was considered.
We keep the same notation.

Lemma 2
.

Let
L\subset S^{2}

For every

|qv|\geq\min\{|qu|,|qz|\}

Proof.

Clearly, the farthest point

to

Observe that for

decreases as the distance
|px|
increases.
This easily implies the assertion of our lemma.
∎

a lune of thickness

Denote by

this corner of

this one closer to

Of course,

Observe that

Hence there exists a unique point

Also on

Denote

From

3
Quadrilaterals with three right angles

Let
Q=abcd
be a spherical quadrilateral with right angles at
a,b
and
c
.
Put
\kappa=|ab|
,
\lambda=|bc|
,
\mu=|cd|
and
\nu=|da|
.
Recall that for the alike hyperbolic Lambert quadrilateral
abcd
we have
we have
{\rm sinh\,}\mu={\rm sinh\,}\kappa\;{\rm cosh\,}\nu
,
\tanh\mu={\rm cosh\,}\lambda\tanh\kappa
,
{\rm sinh\,}\nu={\rm sinh\,}\lambda\;{\rm cosh\,}\mu
,
\tanh\nu={\rm cosh\,}\kappa\tanh\lambda
.
Here is a theorem in which we present some relationships in the quadrilateral
Q
analogous to the ones in the hyperbolic Lambert quadrilateral.

Theorem 1.

In the spherical quadrilateral
Q
we have

(1)
\sin\mu=\sin\kappa\cos\nu,

(2)
{\tan\mu}=\tan\kappa\cos\lambda,

(3)
\cos\nu=\sqrt{\cos^{2}\!\mu\cos^{2}\!\lambda+\sin^{2}\!\mu},

(4)
\cos\nu=\frac{\cos\lambda}{\sqrt{1-\sin^{2}\!\lambda\sin^{2}\!\kappa}}.

Proof.

Denote
|bd|
by
\xi
(see Figure 1).
From the right triangle
bcd
we get
\cos\xi=\cos\mu\cos\lambda
.
From the right triangle
dab
we get
\cos\xi=\cos\nu\cos\kappa
.
Hence
\cos\mu\cos\lambda=\cos\nu\cos\kappa
.

Fig. 1.
Spherical quadrilateral with three right angles

Prolong the arcs
cd
and
ba
up to the intersection point
e
(we mean that
d\in ce
and
a\in eb
).
From the right triangle
ead
we see that
\cos|de|=\cos|ea|\cos|ad|
.
Moreover, since
ce
and
ba
are perpendicular to the great circle containing
bc
, we have
|ce|=90^{\circ}
and
|be|=90^{\circ}
.
Consequently, from
\cos|de|=\cos(90^{\circ}-\mu)=\sin\mu
and
\cos|ea|=\cos(90^{\circ}-\kappa)=\sin\kappa
we obtain (1).

The preceding two paragraphs imply (2).
Still it is sufficient to divide left and right sides of
\sin\mu=\cos\nu\sin\kappa
by left and right sides of
\cos\mu\cos\lambda=\cos\nu\cos\kappa
, respectively, which gives
\frac{\tan\mu}{\cos\lambda}=\tan\kappa
.
This is equivalent to (2).

Let us show (3).
From (2) we get
\tan^{2}\!\mu=\tan^{2}\!\kappa\cos^{2}\!\lambda

which equals
\frac{\sin^{2}\!\kappa}{1-\sin^{2}\!\kappa}\cos^{2}\lambda
.
Applying (1) we substitute here
\sin\kappa
by
\frac{\sin\mu}{\cos\nu}
.
We get
\tan^{2}\!\mu=\frac{\sin^{2}\!\mu}{\cos^{2}\!\nu-\sin^{2}\!\mu}\cos^{2}\!\lambda
.
Consequently,
\cos^{2}\!\nu=\cos^{2}\!\mu\cos^{2}\!\lambda+\sin^{2}\!\mu
, which gives (3).

Finally, we intend to show (4).
We start from the squared (3).
Since
\cos^{2}\!\mu=1-\sin^{2}\!\mu
, we have

\cos^{2}\!\nu=(1-\sin^{2}\!\mu)\cos^{2}\!\lambda+\sin^{2}\!\mu=\sin^{2}\!\mu(1-\cos^{2}\!\lambda)+\cos^{2}\!\lambda=\sin^{2}\!\mu\sin^{2}\!\lambda+\cos^{2}\!\lambda
.
Having in mind (1), let us substitute here
\sin^{2}\!\mu=\sin^{2}\!\kappa\cos^{2}\!\nu
.
We get
\cos^{2}\!\nu=\sin^{2}\!\kappa\cos^{2}\!\nu\sin^{2}\!\lambda+\cos^{2}\!\lambda
.
Hence
\cos^{2}\!\nu(1-\sin^{2}\!\kappa\sin^{2}\!\lambda)=\cos^{2}\!\lambda
, which implies (4).
∎

4
Comparison between the diameters of
C
and
E(C)

Theorem 2.

For every convex body
C\subset S^{2}
of diameter
\delta\in(\frac{\pi}{2},\pi)
we have

{\rm diam}(E(C))\geq 2\arccos\big(\frac{1}{4}(\cos\delta+\sqrt{\cos^{2}\!\delta+8})\big).

This inequality cannot be improved for every
\delta
.

Shortly saying, the inequality in our theorem is
{\rm diam}(E(C))\geq 2\varphi(\delta)
.

By compactness arguments, the diameter
\delta
of

.
Clearly,

By Lemma 7 of
[

at

.
Analogously, the semicircle

\delta
centered at

and orthogonal to
gj
at

supports

.
Denote by

the center of the lune

, this is the midpoint of
gj
.
Clearly,
C\subset L
and

has thickness
|gj|=\delta
.
Consider three cases.

Case 1, when

Since
|gj|=\delta
, by Claim we get the thesis of our theorem.

Case 2, when

and

(or analogously, when

).

Since

different from

such that

.
Clearly, these two points belong to the semicircle

.
We intend to show that
\max\{|jf|,|jh|,|fh|\}\geq 2\varphi(\delta)
.
This will imply
{\rm diam}(E(C))\geq 2\varphi(\delta)

Observe that for every position of

in

In each we obtain a consequence.

(2.1) Assume that
|gf|\leq\varphi(\delta)
.
Then

(2.2) Assume that
|gh|\leq\varphi(\delta)
.
Then

(2.3) Assume that

As explained earlier in Case 2, from the obtained consequences we conclude that

in the particular worst situation when
|gh|=2\varphi(\delta)-\frac{\pi}{2}
.
Since the quadrangle
hmog
has right angles at
m,o
and

, let us apply (3) from Theorem 1, where
m=a,o=b,g=c
and

.
So in the terms of Theorem 1 we assume that
\mu=2\varphi-\frac{\pi}{2}

and we intend to show that
\nu\geq\varphi(\delta)
.
In other words, we intend to show that
\cos\nu\leq\cos\varphi(\delta)
.
Having in mind (3) from Theorem 1, we intend to show that

\sqrt{\cos^{2}(2\varphi(\delta)-\frac{\pi}{2})\cos^{2}\!\lambda+\sin^{2}\!(2\varphi(\delta)-\frac{\pi}{2})}\leq\cos\varphi(\delta).

In other words, we intend to show that

\sqrt{\sin^{2}(2\varphi(\delta))\cos^{2}\!\lambda+\cos^{2}(2\varphi(\delta))}\leq\cos\varphi(\delta).

We deal here with an arbitrary
\delta
such that
\frac{1}{2}\pi<\delta<\pi
, so with
\lambda\in(\frac{1}{4}\pi,\frac{1}{2}\pi)
and
\varphi(\delta)\in(\frac{1}{4}\pi,\frac{1}{3}\pi)
, what Lemma
1
says.
Since
\lambda=\frac{1}{2}\delta
, we are planning to show that

\sqrt{\sin^{2}2\varphi\cos^{2}\!\frac{\delta}{2}+\cos^{2}\!2\varphi}\leq\cos\varphi,

where for simplicity we write
\varphi
in place of

.
This is equivalent to

\sin^{2}2\varphi\cos^{2}\!\frac{\delta}{2}+\cos^{2}\!2\varphi\leq\cos^{2}\varphi

This is equivalent to

\sin^{2}2\varphi\cdot\frac{1+\cos\delta}{2}+\cos^{2}2\varphi\leq\cos^{2}\varphi

and by Lemma
1

(1-\cos^{2}2\varphi)\cdot\frac{1+\frac{2\cos^{2}\varphi-1}{\cos\varphi}}{2}+\cos^{2}2\varphi\leq\cos^{2}\varphi
.
Having in mind that
\cos 2\varphi=2\cos^{2}\varphi-1
we see that this is equivalent to

\big(1-(2\cos^{2}\varphi-1)^{2}\big)\cdot\frac{1+\frac{2\cos^{2}\varphi-1}{\cos\varphi}}{2}+(2\cos^{2}\varphi-1)^{2}\leq\cos^{2}\varphi
.

Putting

, we get
(1-(2x^{2}-1)^{2})\cdot(1+\frac{2x^{2}-1}{x})/2+(2x^{2}-1)^{2}\leq x^{2}
, i.e., that
(2x-1)(x+1)(x-1)(1-2x^{2})\leq 0
.
In particular, this inequality is true for

.
In other words, for

.
Hence (***) holds true for
\varphi\in[\frac{\pi}{3},\frac{\pi}{4}]
what we promised.

Consequently (***) remains true if we lessen

.
Still
|hm|

decreases.

Final remarks

Formulating and proving an
n
-dimensional version of Theorem 2 seems to be very difficult.
Even formulating a conjecture does not seem to be easy.

From Theorem 2 we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary.

For every convex body
C\subset S^{2}
with
{\rm diam}(C)\in(\frac{\pi}{2},\pi)
we have

{\rm diam}(E(C))>\frac{2}{3}\cdot{\rm diam}(C).

Proof.

By Theorem 2 it is sufficient to show that
\arccos\big(\frac{1}{4}(\cos\delta+\sqrt{\cos^{2}\!\delta+8})\big)>\frac{1}{3}\delta
for every
\delta\in(\frac{1}{2}\pi,\pi)
.
This is we have to show that

\cos\delta+\sqrt{\cos^{2}\!\delta+8}<4\cos\frac{1}{3}\delta

[formula]

for every such a
\delta
.

Substituting
3\alpha=\delta
into the well known formula
\cos 3\alpha=4\cos^{3}\alpha-3\cos\alpha
, we get
y=4z^{3}-3z
, where
y=\cos\delta
and
z=\cos\frac{\delta}{3}
(clearly,
z>0
).
Consequently, (****) is equivalent to
4z^{3}-3z+\sqrt{(4z^{3}-3z)^{2}+8}<4z
, this is to
4z^{4}-5z^{2}+1<0
, which holds true for
\frac{1}{2}<z<1
.
Hence (****) holds true if
\cos\frac{1}{6}\pi<\cos\frac{1}{6}\pi<\cos 0
, this is if
0<\delta<\pi
.
In particular this is true when
\frac{1}{2}\pi<\delta<\pi
.
∎

The inequality in Corollary cannot be improved.
In order to see this consider the limit of regular triangles whose heights tend to
\pi
from below.
The author expects that the following generalization of Corollary (where a convex body and its extreme points are defined as in
S^{2}
) holds true:
for every convex body
C\subset S^{n}
of diameter
\delta\in(\frac{\pi}{2},\pi)
we have

{\rm diam}(E(C))>\frac{2}{\pi}(\arcsin\sqrt{\frac{n+1}{2n}})\cdot\delta

This value cannot be lessened for the family of the regular simplexes inscribed in the ball of radius
r
