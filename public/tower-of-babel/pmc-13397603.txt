Association between hip abductor muscle strength and mobility limitation (locally defined as locomotive syndrome) in community-dwelling adults undergoing osteoporosis screening: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Yasuhiro Morita, Kayo Miura, Tomoyuki Arai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMobility limitation is associated with an increased risk of disability in older adults. In Japan, mobility limitation is commonly assessed using the concept of locomotive syndrome. Hip abductor muscle strength plays a key role in gait stability; however, its independent association with mobility limitation remains unclear, partly because previous studies have focused primarily on general muscle strength or muscle mass rather than frontal-plane hip stability. This study aimed to investigate the association between hip abductor muscle strength and mobility limitation in community-dwelling adults undergoing osteoporosis screening.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 325 community-dwelling adults undergoing osteoporosis screening. Hip abductor muscle strength was measured isometrically in a seated position using a standardized dynamometer and normalized to body weight. Mobility limitation was assessed using the locomotive syndrome risk test, and participants with a Locomotive score ≥ 1 were classified as having mobility limitation. Logistic regression analyses were performed to examine the association between hip abductor muscle strength and mobility limitation. Odds ratios (ORs) were calculated per 1 standard deviation decrease in muscle strength, with adjustment for age, sex, body mass index, skeletal muscle mass index, and bone density.ResultsAmong 325 participants (mean age 74.1 ± 5.4 years; 70 men and 255 women), 250 (76.9%) were classified as having mobility limitations. Participants with mobility limitations showed significantly lower hip abductor muscle strength than those without. In logistic regression analysis, reduced hip abductor muscle strength was significantly associated with mobility limitation. This association remained significant after adjustment for age, sex, body mass index, skeletal muscle mass index, and bone density (odds ratio per 1 standard deviation decrease: 2.40, 95% confidence interval: 1.71–3.38, p < 0.001).ConclusionsReduced hip abductor muscle strength was independently associated with mobility limitation, regardless of muscle mass and bone density. Assessment of hip abductor muscle strength may provide useful information for identifying individuals at risk of mobility limitation during community-based screenings.

Background

Mobility limitation is a major consequence of musculoskeletal impairment and a key determinant of disability and loss of independence in aging populations. To identify individuals at high risk of mobility disability due to musculoskeletal system disorders, the Japanese Orthopedic Association proposed the concept of locomotive syndrome [1, 2]. This concept broadly overlaps with internationally used constructs, such as mobility limitation and functional decline, and has been widely applied in clinical and community-based research [3–5]. Locomotive syndrome was originally proposed by the Japanese Orthopaedic Association to identify individuals at high risk of requiring nursing care due to musculoskeletal disorders. It is classified into stages based on functional decline using a combination of performance-based tests and self-reported assessment. Unlike sarcopenia, which primarily focuses on muscle mass and strength, locomotive syndrome encompasses a broader concept of mobility function, including balance, gait ability, and activities of daily living. The locomotive syndrome risk test integrates multiple domains of mobility, including performance-based measures and self-reported functional status, whereas single tests such as gait speed or chair stand performance assess more specific aspects of physical function.

Hip abductor muscles play a critical role in stabilizing the pelvis during gait and maintaining balance during daily activities [6, 7]. Weakness of these muscles adversely affects lower-extremity biomechanics and postural control, and has been associated with impaired balance, functional limitations, and an increased risk of falls, particularly in older adults [7–9]. Clinically, hip abductor weakness is frequently observed after proximal femoral fractures and is a major target of rehabilitation aimed at restoring gait and functional independence [10].

Muscle and bone are functionally interconnected through mechanical loading, a concept known as the bone–muscle unit [11]. Previous studies have reported associations between muscle strength, such as grip strength or quadriceps strength, and bone density [12, 13]. However, muscle strength reflects not only muscle quantity but also neuromuscular coordination and functional performance. Current consensus definitions of sarcopenia emphasize the importance of muscle strength and physical performance in addition to muscle mass [14, 15].

Despite the recognized importance of hip abductor muscles for locomotion and balance, the association between hip abductor muscle strength and mobility limitation has not been fully understood. Specifically, it remains unclear whether hip abductor muscle strength is associated with mobility limitation independently of age, sex, body composition, muscle mass, and bone density. Compared with single performance-based measures such as gait speed or chair stand tests, the locomotive syndrome risk test provides a more comprehensive assessment of mobility by integrating multiple dimensions of physical function and self-reported disability. While previous studies have demonstrated associations between general muscle strength and mobility outcomes, it remains unclear whether hip abductor muscle strength reflects a distinct functional aspect of mobility beyond muscle mass and bone health. Incorporating skeletal muscle mass index and bone density into the analytical models allows a more comprehensive evaluation of this relationship. This study aimed to investigate the association between hip abductor muscle strength and mobility limitation, as defined by locomotive syndrome criteria, in community-dwelling adults undergoing osteoporosis screening.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted as part of an osteoporosis screening program for community-dwelling adults in City A, Saitama Prefecture, Japan. The screening was conducted between April 2022 and March 2023. Participants underwent assessments of bone density, body composition, physical function, and muscle strength. Inclusion criteria were the ability to ambulate independently with or without an assistive device and completion of all required assessments, including bone density measurement, body composition analysis, mobility assessment, and hip abductor muscle strength testing. All participants were community-dwelling individuals and were not residing in nursing homes or long-term care facilities. Participants who used assistive devices, such as canes, were included to reflect real-world variability in mobility among community-dwelling adults. Exclusion criteria included a history of neurological disorders affecting lower-limb function, severe musculoskeletal conditions that interfered with reliable muscle strength measurement, acute illness at the time of assessment, or missing data in key variables.

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Saitama Medical University (approval number: U 2022-024), and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Assessment of mobility limitation

Mobility limitation was assessed using the Locomotive Syndrome Risk Test proposed by the Japanese Orthopedic Association, which consists of three components: the two-step test, the stand-up test, and the 25-item Geriatric Locomotive Function Scale. Participants were classified as having mobility limitation if they met the criteria for locomotive syndrome stage ≥ 1 according to the Locomotive Syndrome Risk Test [5].

Assessment of hip abductor muscle strength

Hip abductor muscle strength was measured using a dedicated hip abduction/adduction dynamometer (T.K.K.3367b; Takei Scientific Instruments Co., Ltd., Niigata, Japan), as previously described [16]. This device enables standardized isometric assessment of hip abductor muscle strength with minimal compensatory movements. It has been widely used in epidemiological and clinical studies in Japan, demonstrating acceptable validity and high reliability for assessing lower-limb muscle strength [16, 17]. Participants were seated upright with the pelvis positioned against the backrest of the chair and the knees flexed at 90°. Both thighs were secured using a belt connected to the dynamometer system. Maximal isometric hip abduction force was measured during simultaneous bilateral hip abduction to minimize compensatory trunk and pelvic movements. This approach has been used in previous studies employing similar dynamometer systems, supporting its reproducibility and feasibility in clinical and epidemiological settings [16, 17]. Participants were familiarized with the testing procedure prior to measurement. Measurements were performed twice with brief rest intervals, and the higher value was used for analysis. Standardized verbal encouragement was provided during each trial to ensure maximal effort. Muscle strength was normalized to body weight (N/kg). In this study, muscle strength assessment was limited to the hip abductor muscles using this device.

Bone density and body composition

Bone density was assessed at the calcaneus using quantitative ultrasound, and results were expressed as T-scores. Calcaneal quantitative ultrasound was used as a practical, non-invasive, and widely applicable method suitable for large-scale community-based screening settings. Body composition, including skeletal muscle mass index (SMI), was measured using bioelectrical impedance analysis. SMI was calculated as appendicular skeletal muscle mass divided by height squared (kg/m²). Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight divided by height squared (kg/m²).

Physical function and clinical variables

Physical function was evaluated using the five-times sit-to-stand test, and the time required to complete five consecutive sit-to-stand movements was recorded as the outcome measure. Information on fracture and fall histories in the past year was obtained using a self-administered questionnaire developed for the screening program. Falls were defined as events in which participants unintentionally came to rest on the ground or a lower level. Fall history was recorded as a binary variable (presence or absence).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as numbers and percentages. Group differences were examined using independent t-tests or chi-square tests. Logistic regression analyses were performed to examine the association between hip abductor muscle strength and mobility limitation. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated per 1 standard deviation decrease in hip abductor muscle strength.

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0.10 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Among the 325 participants, 250 (76.9%) were classified as having mobility limitations.

Participants with mobility limitations were older (72.1 ± 8.4 years vs. 67.5 ± 9.7 years) and exhibited lower skeletal muscle mass index, reduced hip abductor muscle strength, and poorer performance on the five-times sit-to-stand test than those without mobility limitations. The five-times sit-to-stand test was included as a simple and feasible measure of lower-limb function in a screening setting. This test provides complementary information to the locomotive syndrome classification by capturing specific aspects of lower-limb strength and functional performance. There were no significant differences in body mass index or bone density, whereas fall history was more frequent among participants with mobility limitations (Table 1).

Discussion

This study demonstrated that reduced hip abductor muscle strength was independently associated with mobility limitation in community-dwelling adults undergoing osteoporosis screening. This association remained robust after adjustment for age, sex, body composition, and bone density, indicating that hip abductor muscle strength reflects a functional dimension of mobility that is not fully captured by muscle mass or bone health alone. These findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating that impaired physical performance is closely associated with mobility limitation, which in Japan is commonly assessed using the concept of locomotive syndrome, in community-dwelling older adults [18]. However, due to the cross-sectional design, the direction of this association cannot be determined, and reverse causation or bidirectional relationships are possible.

Hip abductor muscle strength was selected as the primary muscle strength indicator because it plays a unique and non-redundant role in locomotion. The hip abductors, particularly the gluteus medius, are essential for frontal-plane pelvic stability during single-leg support, which constitutes most of the gait cycle [6, 7]. Impairment of this function leads to increased trunk sway, reduced gait efficiency, and compromised balance control [7, 9]. Unlike muscle groups primarily responsible for propulsion, hip abductors function as postural stabilizers that integrate neuromuscular coordination, balance, and functional loading.

From a musculoskeletal perspective, dysfunction of the hip abductor muscles alters frontal-plane load distribution across the hip and lower extremity, potentially contributing to abnormal joint mechanics and cumulative musculoskeletal stress. These biomechanical characteristics may help to explain the observed association between hip abductor muscle strength and mobility limitation [8, 12, 13].

Although isokinetic dynamometry is considered the gold standard for muscle strength assessment, the present method provides a practical and valid alternative for large-scale community-based screening, where feasibility, safety, and reproducibility are essential. The use of a portable, standardized dynamometer enables efficient assessment of muscle strength without imposing excessive burden on participants or examiners [16, 17]. The use of simultaneous bilateral hip abduction may reduce compensatory trunk and pelvic movements; however, this method does not allow assessment of side-to-side differences and may limit comparability with studies using unilateral measurements. Nevertheless, this approach has been applied in previous studies using similar measurement systems, supporting its feasibility in screening contexts.

Several limitations should be noted. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Second, muscle strength was assessed using a single measurement modality. In addition, muscle strength assessment was limited to the hip abductor muscles, and it remains unclear whether the observed weakness reflects muscle-specific deficits or generalized muscle weakness. Future studies should examine the relationship between hip abductor strength and other lower extremity muscle groups. In addition, bone density was assessed using quantitative ultrasound rather than dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, which may limit comparability with studies using DXA. Although body composition was assessed using bioelectrical impedance analysis, fat-related indices such as body fat percentage were not included in the present analyses. In this study, body mass index was used as a proxy for adiposity because of its widespread clinical use and applicability in screening settings. However, the lack of direct measures of adiposity may represent a limitation. Participants were recruited from an osteoporosis screening program, which may have introduced selection bias. The study population may include a higher proportion of women and individuals who are more health-conscious, and may also include individuals at increased risk of osteoporosis or fracture. These factors may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader community-dwelling population. Third, unmeasured factors such as physical activity, joint pain, and neurological conditions may have influenced the observed associations and represent potential residual confounding. Nonetheless, the strengths of this study include the relatively large sample size and the comprehensive assessment of musculoskeletal and functional parameters.

Conclusions

This study showed that hip abductor muscle strength was independently associated with mobility limitation in community-dwelling adults. Thus, assessment of hip abductor muscle strength may provide clinically meaningful information to help identify individuals at risk of early mobility decline in community-based musculoskeletal screening programs.
