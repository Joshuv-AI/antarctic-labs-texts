Hybrid system design on reverse total shoulder arthroplasty glenoid components
Authors: Süleyman Semih Dedeoğlu, Mustafa Buğra Ayaz, Yavuz Şahbat, Yunus İmren
Published: 2026

Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) has become a definitive and effective treatment for patients with complex proximal humeral fractures, irreparable rotator cuff tear arthropathy, osteonecrosis, or glenohumeral arthritis caused by tumors or inflammatory conditions.13,16 rTSA aims to restore shoulder function by bypassing the deficient rotator cuff and employing a non-anatomic biomechanical design. Among the mechanical complications associated with rTSA, glenoid component loosening has been reported as the most common cause of implant failure.5,19

Regarding central fixation, conical hydroxyapatite-coated pegs have demonstrated the most favorable primary stability in biomechanical studies.11 Nevertheless, no clear biomechanical superiority has been identified among fixation methods such as a central screw, single peg, dual peg, or fin-type central keel in reverse baseplate designs.10 Subsequent research has shown that baseplates with an oval or pear-shaped geometry, which better conform to native glenoid anatomy, can increase the contact area and enhance fixation strength.17 However, in patients with compromised bone quality due to conditions such as posterior glenoid bone loss, central erosion in rheumatoid arthritis, severe retroversion, osteoporosis, tumor-related defects, or previous radiotherapy, baseplate fixation remains challenging.15

In this study, we introduce a novel glenoid baseplate design that utilizes a wedge mechanism through the glenoid via the anterior cortex. The key concept is to achieve more rigid fixation with anterior scapular cortex engagement, thus reducing reliance on trabecular bone stock. This enhanced stability allows for greater design flexibility without compromising implant fixation, especially in patients with insufficient glenoid bone stock or those undergoing revision surgery. Furthermore, due to its minimally invasive application technique, the design is expected to facilitate implantation in tight or stiff shoulders with less soft tissue dissection.

The aim of this study is to present the fundamental design elements and functional characteristics of a newly developed glenoid baseplate system registered under patent number PCT/TR2025/050476 and to evaluate its feasibility and stability through computational and cadaveric analyses. We hypothesized that engagement of the anterior scapular cortex through a wedge-based fixation mechanism would improve load transfer and reduce shear stress at the bone–implant interface compared with conventional baseplate fixation strategies.

Material and methods

Ethical approval

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the institutional review board of Metin Sabancı Baltalimanı Bone Diseases Training and Research Hospital on June 26, 2024 (Decision No: 157).

Design of the hybrid glenoid component

The hybrid glenoid component system is a novel design aimed at minimizing anterior-to-posterior capsular dissection during rTSA. The most distinctive feature of the hybrid baseplate is the presence of a central wedge rather than a conventional central peg. The wedge features sharp edges, facilitating insertion into the prepared bone bed via gentle impaction after osteotomies (Fig. 1).

Materials included in the study

This computational finite element study was based on a single fresh-frozen cadaveric specimen and a single clinical computed tomography (CT) dataset. The study included CT Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) data of the right shoulder of a healthy 18-year-old male and a fresh-frozen right shoulder specimen from a 52-year-old male cadaver.

The scapula was reconstructed from the DICOM dataset using Materialise Mimics software, with cortical and trabecular regions segmented separately. The cadaveric specimen was obtained from a body donor program, stored at −20 °C, and thawed at room temperature for 24 hours prior to preparation. The proximal humerus was transected 20 cm distal to the head, and all soft tissues were preserved medially up to 15 cm from the joint line. The scapula was fixed medially using a vise grip. Through a deltopectoral approach, the humerus and acromion dorsal rim, together with the deltoid muscle, were resected.

Three-dimensional modeling and prototype manufacturing

A right-sided baseplate, pin insertion guide, and cutting guide were designed in SolidWorks Premium 2022 SP4.0 (Dassault Systèmes SolidWorks Corp., Waltham, MA) based on the scapular reconstruction. These components were manufactured using a direct metal laser sintering three-dimensional (3D) printer with aluminum powder. Surface optimization for osseointegration was not performed, as the prototype was developed solely for geometric and fixation-related evaluation.

Design of classical and hybrid comparative models

To evaluate the biomechanical performance of the hybrid component, 2 implantation variants were created in SolidWorks: the native and alternative hybrid configurations, as described in the device specification. The hybrid glenosphere component was modeled as a 41 mm diameter hemisphere with a 2 mm inferior offset, mounted on the baseplate via a sliding mechanism and fixed with a locking screw.

For comparison, a conventional reverse shoulder arthroplasty baseplate was modeled based on the Biomet Comprehensive Reverse Shoulder System technical specifications.1 This standard design included a 28 mm baseplate with a central peg and 4 peripheral screw holes, compatible with 6.5 mm and 4.75 mm locking screws. Both concentric (neutral offset) and eccentric (4.5 mm inferior offset) configurations were modeled. Two screw fixation patterns were simulated: standard, defined as fixation with 2 divergent screws, and saturated, defined as fixation with all 4 peripheral screws. Combining offset and screw configurations produced 4 classical variants: concentric standard, concentric saturated, eccentric standard, and eccentric saturated (Fig. 3).

Offset adjustments were applied to match the glenosphere articular surface positions between the classical and hybrid models, ensuring objective comparison. Eccentric classical designs were medialized to minimize the biomechanical disadvantages associated with lateralization while preserving deltoid tension. These eccentric configurations were primarily intended to reduce the risk of inferior glenoid notching, consistent with the biomechanical findings of Gutiérrez et al, who demonstrated that inferior tilt and glenosphere eccentricity decrease baseplate–bone interface forces in reverse shoulder arthroplasty.9 Baseplate inclination of 10°, considered biomechanically optimal in standard models, was excluded to maintain identical conditions with the hybrid design.

Finite element model preparation

All models were imported into Ansys 2022 R2 for finite element analysis (FEA). Cortical and trabecular bones were modeled as separate linear elastic materials, with properties defined as follows: trabecular bone Young's modulus 1 Gigapascal (GPa), Poisson's ratio 0.35, ultimate tensile strength 6 MPa; cortical bone Young's modulus 9 GPa, Poisson's ratio 0.3, and ultimate tensile strength 98 MPa. Implant materials included cobalt–chromium alloy (Young's modulus 220 GPa, Poisson's ratio 0.3, ultimate tensile strength 515 MPa) and titanium alloy (Young's modulus 110 GPa, Poisson's ratio 0.3, ultimate tensile strength 220 MPa).18

Mesh generation utilized 10-node second-order tetrahedral elements (SOLID187). Cortical bone, trabecular bone, and implants were modeled as fully integrated volumes. Convergence testing was performed by refining mesh density until changes in maximum cortical bone stress and implant–bone interface micromotion were <3%. A final mesh containing approximately 305,000 elements and 468,000 nodes was selected. Cortical bone consisted of ∼184,386 elements, trabecular bone ∼57,727 elements, and implant components ∼63,214 elements.

Interface definitions and boundary conditions

Implant–bone interfaces were defined as frictional (coefficient of friction 0.64) to simulate non-osteointegrated conditions,2 while implant–implant interfaces were defined as bonded. The analysis was based on ASTM F2028-08 dynamic evaluation standards for glenoid loosening.8,12

Loading scenarios

The scapula was fixed along its medial border, and a load of approximately 560 Newton (∼0.7 body weight) was applied to the glenosphere under the following conditions:

Neutral position: Scapular plane 0° inclination.

Inclined positions: 30° and 60° inclination in the scapular plane.

Scaption: 90° forward flexion with 30° abduction in the axial plane.

Reverse plank: 90° extension with 30° abduction in the axial plane.

These loading scenarios were adopted from validated computational and mechanical models. The applied load of approximately 560 N represents joint reaction forces reported during moderate shoulder loading conditions in previous biomechanical studies and has been used in validated computational simulations of reverse shoulder arthroplasty3,4 (Fig. 4).

Outcome measures

Primary outcomes included von Mises max equivalent stress on the bone and implant, as well as shear stress at the implant–bone interface. The finite element models were validated through mesh convergence testing and by comparing predicted stress distribution patterns with those reported in previous experimental and computational studies.12,18

Cadaver study

In the cadaveric study, the feasibility of the hybrid glenoid component and its associated application instruments was evaluated. The placement principle of the hybrid component was inspired by the bone block procedure performed through the anterior glenoid rim. For comparison purposes, the central peg position for the conventional baseplate was first determined with a K-wire, and the peg socket was created using a 7.5 mm cannulated reamer.

Subsequently, 2 pins were inserted using the pin guide of the hybrid glenoid system. Through the cutting guide placed over these pins, anterior, lateral, and transverse cuts were made using a fine oscillating saw. Following the transverse cuts, the exposed bone segment was removed with a clamp, and the resulting groove was flattened using a bone saw. The hybrid baseplate was then inserted into the groove via its central wedge and impacted into place. Post-implantation, the baseplate was observed to fit tightly and securely into the prepared site. Fixation was completed with 2 K-wires inserted through the plate portions of the implant.

Upon removal of the implants, the components and instruments were found to be consistent with the anatomical 3D reconstruction model analysis. The reference points for glenoid cuts were determined using the pin guide with the glenoid fossa, anterior rim, posterior cortex, or inferior rim as anatomical landmarks. During cutting, the posterior cortex was preserved. The location of the transverse cut was noted to be superior and in a more cortical bone–dominant position compared to the central location of the fossa in conventional implants (Fig. 2D).

Finite element analysis results

FEA under neutral vectoral loading demonstrated that the peak equivalent stress was located on the implant in both standard and hybrid models, whereas in saturated models it was located at the medial edge of the scapula, which is the region that study models are fixed (Fig. 5). Evaluation of the maximum equivalent stress in bone revealed higher values in saturated models compared to standard models and in the alternative hybrid model compared to the native hybrid model (Fig. 6A). Overall, across all loading conditions, the native hybrid model exhibited lower maximum equivalent bone stress than the saturated models, but higher than the standard model. Under reverse plank loading, however, both the standard concentric and hybrid models demonstrated lower bone stress.

Maximum equivalent implant stress was generally lower in saturated models and higher in the standard model under all loading conditions (Fig. 6B). In hybrid models, the maximum implant stress was distinctly lower than in other models under reverse plank loading, while higher values were recorded under 60° inclination and scaption-directed loads. Under 60° inclination loading, the stress peak was concentrated on the inferior screw, whereas under scaption-directed loading, the peak stress was located at the edge of the central wedge (Fig. 7).

Analysis of implant shear stress revealed consistently lower values in saturated models and higher values in standard models (Fig. 6C). In hybrid models, shear stress under neutral and 30° inclination forces in the scapular plane was similar to that of saturated models. Under 60° inclination loading, values approached those of the standard model, while under scaption-directed loading, results were similar to the standard model. Under reverse plank loading, hybrid models outperformed even the saturated models, demonstrating lower shear stress values.

Stress distribution analysis

Previous studies have reported that medialized offsets provide greater stability compared to lateralized offsets, larger diameter baseplates (28 mm) outperform smaller ones (25 mm), concentric placement is superior to eccentric placement, and fixation with 4 screws offers better stability than with 2 screws.6 In the present study, the success criterion was defined as achieving biomechanical stability comparable to conventional designs fixed with 4 screws excluding the central peg screw. Accordingly, following FEA, it was deemed essential to perform biomechanical testing before any clinical application. Osseointegration of the implant was deliberately excluded in the model, simulating conditions closest to the immediate postoperative period. Since the central wedge surfaces and the joint-facing surface of the hybrid glenoid component are coated with hydroxyapatite as per the design specifications, excluding osteointegration may have placed the implant at a disadvantage in this analysis. The 4-screw fixation version of the hybrid system and the hybrid revision prototype were excluded from the study.

The FEA results indicated that the hybrid component demonstrated mechanical performance comparable to the four-screw configuration at best and to the two-screw configuration at worst. Nonetheless, certain perspectives revealed better or worse outcomes depending on the loading condition. In saturated models, stress analysis consistently showed lower implant stress compared to bone stress across all loading conditions, suggesting more effective transfer of load from the implant to the bone. This may be advantageous in terms of load sharing, although long-term effects such as stress shielding would need to be evaluated via strain analysis. Conversely, in standard models, equivalent stress in the implant was markedly higher than in bone, raising concerns about potential component fatigue failures over time. In the hybrid model, implant and bone stresses were more closely aligned.

When examined by loading direction, neutral loading exhibited higher stresses in the implant, 30° inclination exhibited higher stresses in bone, 60° inclination resulted in higher bone stress in the native hybrid model but higher implant stress in the alternative hybrid model, scaption loading generated higher stresses in the implant, and reverse plank loading led to higher bone stresses. The mechanical response discrepancies observed under axial loads (scaption and reverse plank) were attributed to the anterior position of the baseplate, which required a 2 mm posterior off-axis placement of the glenosphere to match the intended joint surface position in this study. Similarly, to ensure alignment of glenosphere surfaces between models, the central wedge of the baseplate was positioned 2 mm inferior to the original design, which may also explain the elevated stresses under 60° inclination loading. These positional adjustments should be considered in future implant design refinements.

Shear stress, a parameter associated with early loosening, was consistently lowest in saturated models across all loading conditions. Hybrid models demonstrated shear stress responses similar to saturated models under neutral and 30° inclination loads but higher under 60° inclination and scaption loads. Under reverse plank loading, however, hybrid models outperformed saturated models by exhibiting lower shear stress values. In contrast, standard models exhibited high shear stress in all loading directions.

Discussion

The most important finding of the present study is that the proposed hybrid glenoid baseplate engaging the anterior scapular cortex demonstrated biomechanical performance comparable to conventional fixation methods across multiple loading conditions in this computational model. In addition, the cadaveric feasibility assessment confirmed that the implant could be inserted reproducibly using the dedicated cutting guides while achieving stable seating within the prepared glenoid slot.

In conventional baseplate implantation, an acceptable threshold for baseplate–glenoid contact surface is considered to be 80%. If this value falls below 50%, grafting or augmentation techniques are typically required.15 The hybrid baseplate design engages the glenoid in a more superior region with greater cortical bone content. This feature supports expectations that it could offer a more stable alternative in revision procedures or in cases with trabecular bone loss or severe retroversion where conventional fixation is insufficient.14 However, the principle of superior positioning also raises concerns about potential glenoid notching. Conventional designs have described concentric and eccentric glenosphere positioning relative to the baseplate. Eccentric placement has been shown to reduce notching and improve adduction and abduction9 but may also increase off-axis loading and thereby contribute to loosening.18 In the hybrid system, the component is positioned more superiorly and laterally, potentially avoiding notching while maintaining deltoid tension. Although lateralization is generally associated with biomechanical disadvantage,7 sufficient cortical engagement with osteointegration is expected to offset these drawbacks.

Technical considerations and potential applications

The hybrid system was developed to address several limitations of conventional implants. In this study, no marked biomechanical superiority over standard components was observed. However, the exclusion of osteointegration in the present analysis inherently placed the new design at a disadvantage. Furthermore, the 3D-reconstructed bone model was assigned the properties of healthy young bone. In an osteoporotic model, the higher cortical engagement of the hybrid component could be expected to yield better mechanical performance. Similarly, in arthritic, defective, or deformed bone models or in revision surgeries, it may prove to be a valuable alternative. Four-screw fixation is not feasible in every case, further supporting the potential utility of this design.

Intraoperative challenges

Several issues may arise during implantation. One is the risk of intraoperative fracture, which is related to preserving the posterior glenoid rim. Preserving this cortical segment allows the central wedge of the impacted baseplate to achieve a press-fit, contributing to long-term implant success. Even if perforation occurs, implantation and screw fixation are generally possible, though early postoperative rehabilitation may need to be limited. Prevention may be achieved through meticulous preoperative planning or by revising the central wedge's technical specifications.

Intraoperative fracture, however, represents a more serious complication and may require additional procedures. In such cases, the implant's capacity for four-screw fixation can be supplemented with other fixation methods, including reconstruction plates. Another concern is the potential risk to neurovascular structures from the stop element that limits advancement and regulates version in this anterior approach. This warrants further cadaveric studies; if necessary, design modifications to the stop element could mitigate the risk. Nonetheless, in the hands of a surgeon experienced in anterior bone block procedures, it is highly likely that safe retraction and successful implantation can be achieved. An important limitation of this design is that the presented implant may be problematic in type B3 or C glenoids and in revision settings.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the FEA was based on CT data derived from a single anatomical model obtained from a healthy young individual. Because patients undergoing reverse shoulder arthroplasty are typically older and may present with osteoporotic bone quality, the model may overestimate fixation stability compared with clinical scenarios. Second, only static loading conditions were simulated, whereas clinical glenoid loosening is often associated with cyclic fatigue loading. Future studies incorporating cyclic loading simulations would therefore be valuable. Third, simplified linear elastic material properties were assumed for bone and implant components, which may not fully replicate the complex mechanical behavior of biological tissues. Finally, the cadaveric validation consisted of a single feasibility specimen and did not include mechanical testing. Consequently, the present study should be interpreted as an early-stage biomechanical feasibility evaluation of a novel implant concept.

Conclusion

The hybrid glenoid baseplate design demonstrated promising biomechanical characteristics and surgical feasibility in this preliminary evaluation. Engagement of the anterior scapular cortex through a wedge-based mechanism may represent a potential fixation strategy for challenging glenoid conditions. However, further biomechanical testing, larger anatomical simulations, and clinical investigations are necessary to determine the true clinical value of this concept.
