Pathogenicity study of ascomycetous opportunistic yeasts from fresh feces of pigeons using Tenebrio molitor larvae as a host model
Authors: Anna Glushakova, Andrei Berezhnov, Aleksey Kachalkin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe choice of model to assess host–pathogen interactions is crucial for the analysis of microbial pathogenicity. Insects are increasingly chosen as model hosts for the study of microbial infections. Although the immune system responses of insects are not specific, they have a complex, evolutionarily conserved innate immune system with cellular and humoral elements. In this study, we investigated in vivo the virulence of potentially pathogenic ascomycetous yeasts found in fresh feces of street pigeons using the larval host model of Tenebrio molitor. The results were arranged in survival curves according to the Kaplan–Meier method. The yeasts Arxiozyma bovina showed the highest pathogenicity, resulting in a significantly shortest median survival time — 24 h, followed by Nakaseomyces glabratus (4 days), Candida albicans and C. tropicalis (5 days), Clavispora lusitaniae (6 days), C. parapsilosis and Pichia kudriavzevii (7 days). The T. molitor model demonstrated the pathogenic potential and virulence differences of the ascomycetous yeasts observed in fresh pigeon droppings in the city. It provided a valuable, swift and cost-effective tool for preliminary virulence screening of clinically important yeasts spread by synanthropic birds in the anthropophized areas. Its detection is of key importance for immunocompromised people and children and is in line with the One Health approach.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-26115-9.Keywords: Arxiozyma bovina, Bird droppings, Candida, Survival analysis, Virulence, WHO fungal priority pathogens listSubject terms: Ecology, Microbiology, Ecology, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Among the birds that inhabit cities, there are so-called full synanthropes, i.e., sedentary birds that live permanently in populated areas. A “classic” example of such birds is the street pigeon — a cosmopolitan bird that lives on all continents. Under the influence of intensive urbanization and global warming, which is stronger in the Arctic regions than on the entire planet, it has also spread to some cities in the polar regions (Labytnangi, Nadym, Vorkuta)1,2. Adaptive behavior, omnivory3 and the ability to nest directly on buildings4 contribute to the active spread of these birds and the barely controlled increase in their numbers in cities.

As the bird that lives near humans in anthropophized areas worldwide, the street pigeon is the best-studied synanthropic bird as a reservoir and vector for the spread of human pathogens, including yeasts via feces5–13. Our recent study of potentially pathogenic ascomycetous yeasts in the feces of synanthropic birds in Moscow also showed that of all the birds examined (Rock Pigeon, European Starling, White Wagtail, Great Tit and House Sparrow), the pigeon had the most pathogens in its feces (7 species)14. These were 5 out of 6 ascomycetous species from the list of critical, high and medium-important yeast pathogens published in the World Health Organization (WHO) fungal list: Candida albicans, Nakaseomyces glabratus (Candida glabrata), C. tropicalis, C. parapsilosis and Pichia kudriavzevii (Candida krusei)15 and two pathogens not yet included in the WHO fungal list, namely Arxiozyma bovina and Clavispora lusitaniae. Only Candidozyma auris (Candida auris) from the WHO fungal list critical group of pathogens was not observed by us in pigeon droppings. But in the study based on metagenomic analysis, the pigeon feces collected in Uppsala were shown to contain C. auris13. This was the first detection of C. auris, a multidrug-resistant fungal pathogen that poses a serious threat to human health in bird droppings worldwide.

The fact that street pigeons generally live in close proximity to humans makes the transmission of zoonotic fungal diseases via their feces possible16. In a recent clinical study in France, two patients with invasive fungal infections caused by the potentially pathogenic yeast A. bovina were found to have had previous contact with pigeons. A strain of A. bovina was also isolated from the feces of one of the pigeons with which one of the two patients had had contact17. All these yeasts are not harmful to healthy people. But in people with a weakened immune system (cancer, radiation damage, antibiotic therapy, corticosteroids, various weakened conditions) they can cause the development of an infection18. In this context, it seems important to evaluate the virulence of strains of potentially pathogenic yeasts isolated directly from substrates in the urban environment (in our case from fresh pigeon droppings), where the probability of interaction with humans is high. Dispersal can be of particular importance in anthropophized areas where humans and synanthropic species interact closely and share space19. Contamination from bird droppings can affect topsoil, various surfaces such as street furniture or playground equipment, and surface waters used for irrigation, drinking and recreation20,21. They can also pose a risk of contamination after rainfall in sewage systems22.

Invertebrates (Drosophila melanogaster, Caenorhabditis elegans, Galleria mellonella, Tenebrio molitor) are frequently used as an alternative host-model to study bacterial and fungal infections and to evaluate the efficacy of antimicrobial drugs23–29. In this study, we decided to use the larval model of T. molitor to study yeast infections because it offers a number of important advantages for assessing the pathogenicity of yeast strains in vivo. The main advantages of the model system with T. molitor larvae include the possibility of using a large number of animals for the experiment, the simplicity of their maintenance under laboratory conditions and incubation at 37 °C, which makes it possible to study human pathogens using this model30. Despite the lack of acquired immunity in insects (they do not produce antibodies), the innate immunity of T. molitor larvae shows considerable similarities with the immune response of vertebrates, including humans: the humoral response in T. molitor involves the production of melanin and antimicrobial peptides with bactericidal and antifungal activity; cellular responses are coordinated by hemocytes, which perform functions such as phagocytosis, encapsulation and nodule formation31. This host model has proven valuable for the study of infections caused by different yeast species such as C. albicans, Cryptococcus neoformans30, Malassezia furfur32 and C. auris31. The use of T. molitor larvae is in line with proposals based on the principle of the three Rs (Refinement, Reduction and Replacement) presented to the scientific community in 1959 by William Moy Stratton Russell and Rex Leonard Burch in the book “Principles of Humane Experimental Technique”33.

The aim of this study was therefore to investigate the pathogenicity of strains of clinically important ascomycetous yeasts (C. albicans, N. glabratus, C. tropicalis, C. parapsilosis, P. kudriavzevii, Cl. lusitaniae and A. bovina) isolated from fresh feces of street pigeons in vivo using the host-model T. molitor.

Materials and methods

Study sites, sampling and processing

Study sites, sampling, and processing have been described in detail in our recent study focused on comparing the diversity of ascomycetous, potentially pathogenic yeast species in feces of five synanthropic birds (Rock Pigeon, European Starling, White Wagtail, Great Tit and House Sparrow). The most diverse pathogen–yeast complexes were found in the fresh feces of pigeons14. Here we briefly present the key information and important details. Fresh pigeon droppings were collected in May 2024 in the city of Moscow (55°44′24″N 37°36′36″E) in parks, playgrounds, hospital areas, near metro stations, large supermarkets, near garbage containers and on sidewalks with green areas along highways. The required samples were taken from fresh droppings found on the soil, in the grass, on leaves of shrubs and young trees, on asphalt paths and curbs, on wooden and iron parts of benches, swings and on the rubberized surface of playgrounds, taking care not to harm the birds. The collection took place during the day from 11 am to 4 pm. The average daily air temperature during the sampling period was + 24 °C and the average humidity was 35% (https://world-weather.ru/pogoda/russia/moscow/may-2024/). The temperature of fresh feces measured with a DT-8865 pyrometer (Sem Test Instrument, China) within 5 min after defecation of the individual bird and before sampling was in the range of + 32 to + 35 °C.

We waited for cases of defecation. Samples were collected using sterile gloves and sterile viscose swabs (1.8 ± 0.5 mm). Each swab was immersed in 3 mL of sterile saline (0.9% NaCl) supplemented with 0.5 g/L chloramphenicol transport medium. In this way, 48 samples (feces from 48 pigeons) were collected and delivered to the laboratory for analysis. Each sample was assigned a unique identifier by origin and date. All microbiological manipulations in the laboratory, including plating, were performed in a sterile laminar box (Esco Airstream Gen 3 LVG-AG-F, Singapore) with sterile gloves. In addition, the hands of the operators were pre-tested for the absence of opportunistic yeasts on the skin to prevent contamination of the material to be tested. The samples were processed within 6 h of collection. Each swab was shaken in transport medium on a Multi Reax Vortexer (Heidolph Instruments, Schwabach, Germany) for 15 min at 2000 rpm and then allowed to sediment for 15 min at 22 °C. After the swab was removed, the supernatant was transferred to a sterile conical tube. Subsequently, 100 µL of the supernatant was transferred to the chromogenic medium “Brilliance Candida Agar” (Thermo Scientific™, Waltham, MA, USA) for rapid isolation and presumptive identification of clinically important Candida species. The chromogenic color reactions on an opaque background allow a quick and easy differentiation of Candida spp. especially in the presence of different complexes. Grown colonies that did not show characteristic coloration were also counted. Representative colonies of all yeast colony phenotypes (with/without characteristic coloration were isolated in pure culture and then identified using molecular methods (see below). The plates were incubated aerobically at 30 °C. Colony growth was examined after 24, 48, 72, 96 and 120 h. “Brilliance Candida Agar” has shown the highest level of sensitivity after 72 h of incubation time. No further changes were observed after 96 and 120 h of incubation. Candida albicans ATCC 10231, Candida parapsilosis ATCC 22019 and Candida tropicalis ATCC 750 were used for media quality positive controls and Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 for media quality negative control. Each sample was plated in three replicates.

Molecular identification of pure cultures

For molecular identification the ITS rDNA region was used as a universal DNA-barcoding for fungi34. The nuclear ribosomal ITS1-5.8 S-ITS2 region was amplified with ITS1f (5’-CTT GGT CAT TTA GAG GAA GTA A-3’) and LR5 (5’-TCC TGA GGG AAA CTT CG-3’) primers, then sequenced using the ITS5 primer (5’-GGA AGT AAA AGT CGT AAC AAG G-3’)35. The criteria described in Vu36 were used to separate the yeast species. DNA isolation and PCR were performed according to the previously described procedure35,37. DNA sequencing was performed using the Big Dye Terminator V3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA) with subsequent analysis of reaction products on an Applied Biosystems 3130xl Genetic Analyzer at the facilities of Evrogen (Moscow). For species identification, the nucleotide sequences were compared with those in public databases using the BLAST NCBI (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov (accessed on January 20, 2025)) and the MycoID (http://www.mycobank.org (accessed January 20, 2025)) tools. The ITS regions of the strains studied were 99.8–100% similar to the type strains. Nine yeast species were detected and identified in fresh pigeon feces (C. albicans, N. glabratus, C. tropicalis, C. parapsilosis, P. kudriavzevii, A. bovina,Cl. lusitaniae, Debaryomyces hansenii and D. fabryi). Sequences for representatives of all identified yeast species were deposited in the GenBank database (PP905608; PV089567–PV089574)14. Of the 9 species observed, 7 were potentially pathogenic (C. albicans, N. glabratus, C. tropicalis, C. parapsilosis, P. kudriavzevii, A. bovina and Cl. lusitaniae) and two were less clinically relevant species with wide environmental distribution (Debaryomyces hansenii and D. fabryi)38. In this study, strains of yeast species with high clinical relevance (C. albicans, N. glabratus, C. tropicalis, C. parapsilosis, P. kudriavzevii, A. bovina and Cl. lusitaniae) were used for the in vivo pathogenicity assessment. Only strains for which molecular identification was performed were used in pathogenicity tests.

Tenebrio molitor larvae infection and survival curves

An invertebrate host model of T. molitor larvae was used to evaluate the virulence of strains of potentially pathogenic ascomycetous yeasts isolated from fresh feces of pigeons. The selected larvae were inoculated with 35 strains (5 strains for each of the 7 pathogenic yeast species). Each strain was tested on three groups of larvae from different batches. Each group consisted of 10 animals. Thus, each yeast strain was tested on 30 larvae.

We used the methodology described in the work of de Souza30, which was successfully applied to pathogenic yeasts for the first time, with slight modifications. The larvae were purchased from the specialized store Antplanet.ru (Moscow), where they were kept in a cool place at plus 4 °C on a diet of apples and bran. Immediately before survival studies, the selected larvae were incubated for 48 h at 22 °C, followed by 24 h at 37 °C. For the survival studies, we selected larvae weighing between 100 and 200 mg that had a clear, uniform color without dark spots or grayish marks. These larvae were inoculated using a Hamilton syringe (701 SN, 26s, 10 µL capacity) (Hamilton Bonaduz AG, Switzerland). Inoculation was performed by injecting the inoculum into the hemocoel, at the second or third visible sternite above the legs, in the ventral portion. Each larvae received 5 µL of the suspension containing the pathogen. The strains of pathogens were grown in YEPD, a complete medium for yeast growth (yeast extract (1%), peptone (2%), dextrose (2%), ddH2O) with agitation for 24 h at 37 °C. The cells were then centrifuged at 2000 x g for 10 min using a medical laboratory centrifuge (TAGLER SM-12, Russia), the supernatant was discarded and the inoculum was washed 3 times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS pH 7.4 ± 0.1, Laboway, Russia). Cell density was determined in an Olympus cell counter model R1 (Evident, Japan) and suspensions of 1 × 105 CFU/mL for each strain were used for inoculation. This concentration of suspension for the infection of T. molitor larvae was chosen based on the results of de Souza30. As well as on our own preliminary studies. The dose that caused 100% lethality of larvae within 24 h after inoculation with a type strain of C. albicans VKM Y-2994 (= ATCC 18804), a clinical isolate from the skin of a patient with a fungal infection, was taken as the infectious dose of a strain with strong pathogenicity. This strain was used as a positive control in the study. A reference strain of C. albicans VKM Y-2994 (= ATCC 18804) that had previously been inactivated by heating (65 °C for 30 min) was used as an additional control. For comparison, the strain of the baker’s yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae was tested. Phosphate-buffered saline was used as a negative control. All larvae inoculated with yeasts and phosphate-buffered saline were incubated at 37 °C in Petri dishes with rearing feed (sterile bran) and sawdust. The number of dead larvae was recorded at 24-hour intervals for 14 days. To determine the death of the larvae, we visually checked their melanization and their reaction to physical stimuli by gently touching them. First, we compared the pathogenicity of the strains within each yeast species (five strains for each of the seven yeast species). Then we compared the different yeast species based on the most virulent strain (the strain that killed the first larvae fastest after inoculation).

Statistical data analyses

All statistical analyses were performed in PAST.5.02. (Oslo, Norway). The results were arranged in a survival curve according to the Kaplan–Meier method. Differences in survival rate between T. molitor larvae groups inoculated with strains of different potentially pathogenic yeasts were assessed using the Log-rank Test (chi-square, p-value). A statistical significance cut-off value of p ≤ 0.05 was used. Microsoft Excel 365 (Redmond, WA, US) and PAST.5.02. (Oslo, Norway) were used to visualize the Kaplan–Meier plots.

Results

Survival analysis (time-to-event analysis) using the Kaplan–Meier method and the Log-rank Test showed a high convergence of results between strains within each of the species studied — the differences in survival curves between strains were not significant (p > 0.05) (Figure S1). However, all 35 strains tested showed virulence — statistical difference to PBS, inactivated C. albicans and non-virulent baker’s yeasts S. cerevisiae, p ≤ 0.05. The time to death of the first animals inoculated with the strain of the baker’s yeast S. cerevisiae was 10 days and 80% of the animals survived after an observation period of 14 days (Figure S2). We found no dead animals inoculated with a reference strain of C. albicans that had previously been inactivated by heating during the 14-day observation period, confirming the correlation between mortality and viability of the yeast strains.

The yeasts A. bovina exhibited the highest pathogenicity of all seven species tested, resulting in a significantly shorter median survival time — the median survival time of T. molitor larvae inoculated with A. bovina strains was 24 h (Fig. 1A). This result was considered statistically significant (p ≤ 0.05, Log-rank Test) (Figure S3). The yeasts N. glabratus after 4 days (Fig. 1B), the yeasts C. albicans (Fig. 1C) and C. tropicalis (Fig. 1D) after 5 days, Cl. lusitaniae after 6 days (Fig. 1E), C. parapsilosis (Fig. 1F) and P. kudriavzevii (Fig. 1G) after 7 days followed in order of increasing median survival time. The median survival of all species in the Candida group differed significantly from the median survival of A. bovina. The differences between the species in the Candida group were not significant (Figure S3).

Kaplan–Meier survival plots show T. molitor survival curves after infection with strains of A. bovina (A), N. glabratus (C. glabrata) (B), C. albicans (C), C. tropicalis (D), Cl. lusitaniae (C. lusitaniae) (E), C. parapsilosis (F) and P. kudriavzevii (C. krusei) (G). Results are expressed as % survival in comparison to uninfected (PBS-treated) larvae (negative control), larvae treated with heat-inactivated strain of C. albicans VKM Y-2994 (additional negative control) and active strain of C. albicans VKM Y-2994 (positive control). Values obtained per group are presented: three groups of 10 larvae from different batches were infected with the test strain of each pathogen, replicated three times and pooled to generate a survival curve with 30 individuals per strain. Photo of the larvae was taken from https://antplanet.ru/catalog/view/213.

Discussion

Our results show that strains of all seven yeast species isolated from fresh feces of street pigeons display pathogenicity in the T. molitor invertebrate model. Notably, A. bovina exhibited exceptional pathogenicity, with a median survival time of only 24 h, significantly shorter than that of all other tested species. The high pathogenicity of A. bovina strains in our study is particularly noteworthy, as this species is not currently included in the WHO priority fungal pathogen list. Our finding that A. bovina caused mortality comparable to the positive control strain C. albicans VKM Y-2994 (= ATCC 18804) indicates that this species warrants greater attention as an emerging pathogen. This result is consistent with recent increases in clinical isolations of A. bovina17,39 and supports the hypothesis that rare yeast species may pose under-recognised health risks.

Among the WHO-listed priority pathogens found in our pigeon samples, we observed a hierarchy of virulence that partially corresponds to WHO priority classifications. The high-priority pathogen N. glabratus (4-day median survival), the critical-priority pathogen C. albicans (5-day median survival), and the high-priority pathogen C. tropicalis (5-day median survival) demonstrated greater pathogenicity than the medium-priority pathogen P. kudriavzevii (7-day median survival). Although these differences were not significant in our study, they can be considered and tested on a larger number of strains from different sources.

The thermotolerance of our A. bovina strains provides further context for their high pathogenicity. Our isolates grew at 43 °C14, consistent with previous reports of A. bovina growth at 45 °C40,41. This thermophilic capacity may contribute to successful infection at mammalian body temperatures and supports the clinical relevance of our pathogenicity findings.

Our validation controls confirmed the reliability of the T. molitor model for pathogenicity assessment. The heat-inactivated C. albicans strain caused no mortality over 14 days, while the non-pathogenic baker’s yeast S. cerevisiae showed minimal virulence (10-day onset, 80% survival). These results establish clear baselines for distinguishing pathogenic from non-pathogenic responses in this model.

The detection of multiple WHO priority fungal pathogens in street pigeon feces, combined with their confirmed pathogenicity in our invertebrate model, supports the hypothesis that synanthropic birds serve as reservoirs and dispersal agents for opportunistic fungal pathogens. The presence of highly pathogenic species such as A. bovina alongside established pathogens suggests that environmental surveillance programmes should expand beyond currently recognised priority species.

These findings have implications for public health surveillance and risk assessment. The T. molitor model proved effective for rapid pathogenicity screening of environmental yeast isolates, providing quantitative virulence data that could inform clinical decision-making and epidemiological monitoring25,31,42. Standardising this approach could enhance the ability to identify emerging fungal threats in urban environments where human–pigeon contact is frequent.

The present study has several limitations. First, only a small number of strains from a single substrate and region were examined. Second, additional tests of the virulence properties of the strains, such as hydrolytic activity and sensitivity to antifungal agents, were not performed. Third, no control groups of larvae were included to assess adaptation to abrupt temperature changes and the effects of thermal stress on viability.
