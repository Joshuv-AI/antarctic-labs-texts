Relationships among Helicobacter pylori seropositivity, the triglyceride-glucose index, and cardiovascular disease: a cohort study using the NHANES database
Authors: Chunlin Tang, Qian Zhang, Chunmei Zhang, Xue Du, Zhongyan Zhao, Wenqian Qi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHelicobacter pylori (H. pylori), a widely prevalent pathogen that can be cured through relatively simple medical methods, is thought to be potentially associated with the risk of cardiovascular diseases (CVD), although controversy remains. Currently, it is unclear whether the triglyceride-glucose index (TGI), a classic indicator of insulin resistance, influences the relationship between H. pylori infection and CVD. The present work explored the relationships between H. pylori seropositivity, the TGI and CVD, and the potential effect of TGI in this association.MethodsIn this cross-sectional and cohort study, data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) III (1988–1994) and NHANES (1999–2000) were used. The effects of the TGI, H. pylori seropositivity, and their interaction on the risk of CVD were assessed using logistic regression models. The hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for all-cause mortality (ACM) were calculated using the Cox proportional hazards model. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) curves were employed to investigate potential non-linear or linear relationships among the TGI, H. pylori seropositivity, occurrence of CVD, and ACM. Mediation analyses were employed to assess the potential effects of H. pylori seropositivity and TGI on the risk of CVD and mortality.ResultsOf the 9,399 participants, 4,488 (47.75%) were H. pylori-immunoglobulin G (IgG)-positive, and 3,934 (41.86%) were diagnosed with CVD. In the general population, participants with a TGI ≥ 75th percentile who were positive for H. pylori-IgG antibody had the highest risk of developing CVD (odds ratio = 1.487; 95% CI: 1.088–2.033). Among patients with CVD, those with a TGI ≥ 75th percentile & positive for H. pylori-IgG antibody were at a higher ACM risk (HR = 1.227; 95% CI: 1.009–1.491). H. pylori exhibited a significant mediating effect on CVD occurrence (Pindir = 0.004) and mortality (Pindir = 0.004) via the TGI.ConclusionsH. pylori seropositivity may indirectly elevate the risk of CVD and mortality via the TGI. Combining the patient’s H. pylori serological status with their TGI could enhance the predictive ability for CVD occurrence and related mortality. Therefore, the clinical practice of screening for and eradicating H. pylori in CVD patients may be anticipated.Graphical abstract

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) encompass stroke, coronary artery disease, and peripheral arterial disease [1–4]. Insulin resistance is a well-recognized independent risk factor for CVD development [5]. Recently, the triglyceride-glucose index (TGI) has emerged as a readily accessible and reliable indicator for insulin resistance, exhibiting strong predictive capability in clinical settings. A research team in the United States reported a U-shaped relationship between the baseline TGI, all-cause mortality (ACM), and CVD risk among individuals with diabetes or prediabetes [6]. Moreover, high TGI has been identified as a predictor for poor outcomes in populations with both hypertension and coronary heart disease (CHD) [7]. The TGI also serves as a risk factor for CHD progression and atherosclerosis severity in individuals with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) [8].

Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori), a human-specific pathogen recognized for its carcinogenic properties, infects more than 50% of the global population in 2015 [9]. In addition to its established roles in gastrointestinal diseases such as gastric cancer, chronic gastritis, and peptic ulcers, H. pylori infection (HPI) has been implicated in several extra-gastric conditions, including CHD [10, 11]. A recently published meta-analysis demonstrated that positive HPI markers (e.g., anti-H. pylori immunoglobulin G [IgG] antibody, anti-Cag A antibody, and fecal H. pylori antigens) are associated with increased CHD risk [1]. A large study of 21,251 gastric biopsy patients found a significant association between the severity of histological chronic inflammation due to HPI and elevated cardiovascular risk scores in a Korean population [12], Currently, H. pylori may contribute to the onset and progression of cardiovascular diseases (CVD) through various mechanisms, including insulin resistance [4, 11, 13]. However, no significant difference in mortality risk has been found between H. pylori-positive and H. pylori-negative CVD patients [14]. A study by Epameinondas et al. found no association between HPI and cardiovascular diseases [4]. Recent evidence has revealed that the TGI is closely associated with H. pylori seropositivity, thus implicating the involvement of H. pylori in insulin resistance [15]. The same study also observed increased overall mortality in individuals with higher TGI levels and positive H. pylori. However, this relationship was absent in H. pylori-negative individuals. These results suggest a potential interaction between H. pylori seropositivity and the TGI in influencing cardiovascular outcomes. Nevertheless, few studies have thoroughly explored the interaction between H. pylori seropositivity and the TGI in CVD patients or evaluated their influence on long-term outcomes.

In this study, we aimed to conduct a cross-sectional and cohort study using data from the NHANES database to analyze the potential relationships among H. pylori seropositivity, TGI, and the risk of CVD and mortality.

Materials and methods

Data source

This cross-sectional and cohort study evaluated data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) III (1988–1994) and NHANES (1999–2000). NHANES, an ongoing survey that employs a multistage probability sampling design to create a nationally representative pool of the United States population, assesses the nutritional and health statuses of both children and adults. The survey gathers extensive information, including demographic data, medical examination data, laboratory results, and questionnaire responses. Approval of the use of NHANES was obtained from the Research Ethics Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics. Written informed consent was provided by all subjects prior to participation.

Data were obtained from participants (age ≥ 18) who had undergone H. pylori serological testing and provided fasting blood samples. After excluding individuals without records on the TGI, CVD diagnosis, covariate data, or follow-up information, a total of 9,399 participants were enrolled (Fig. 1).

Covariates

Demographic information, including age, sex, race (i.e., Mexican American, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, other Hispanic, other races), poverty–income ratio, education level (i.e., > high school, high school or equivalent, < high school), and smoking status (i.e., current, former, never smoker) were collected from all participants. Households with a poverty–income ratio below 1.3 were classified as low-income. Fasting blood samples were collected during the participants’ visits to the mobile examination center under controlled conditions and sent to the National Center for Environmental Health for analysis. Laboratory measurements included hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), fasting blood glucose (FBG), insulin, C-reactive protein (CRP), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and total cholesterol. Categorical variables were sex, race, age, education level, low household income, diabetes status, and smoking status. Body mass index (BMI), HbA1c, FBG, insulin, total cholesterol, TG, HDL, and CRP were treated as continuous variables [16, 17].

The BMI was obtained by dividing each subject’s weight (kg) by the square of his/her height (m²) [18]. BMI categories were defined as follows: < 25.0 kg/m²; underweight or normal weight; 25.0–30.0 kg/m², overweight; and ≥ 30 kg/m², obese. Diabetes were defined if participants met at least one of the following criteria: (1) HbA1c levels ≥ 6.5%, (2) FBG ≥ 126 mg/dL, or (3) self-reported use of antidiabetic medications. Prediabetes was defined as HbA1c between 5.7% and 6.4% or FBG between 100 and 125 mg/dL. Metabolic syndrome status was determined in accordance with the National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III criteria, requiring the presence of three or more of the following factors: (1) blood pressure ≥ 130/85 mmHg, self-reported hypertension, or current use of antihypertensive medications; (2) HDL cholesterol < 40 mg/dL (1.0 mmol/L) in men or < 50 mg/dL (1.3 mmol/L) in women; (3) waist circumference > 88 cm for women or > 102 cm for men; (4) fasting TG ≥ 1.7 mmol/L (150 mg/dL); and (5) FBG ≥ 5.6 mmol/L (100 mg/dL), self-reported diabetes, or current use of antidiabetic medications [19]. Based on the smoking status, participants were classified as current smokers, former smokers, or non-smokers. Current smokers were those who reported smoking > 100 cigarettes in their lifetime and were currently smoking. Former smokers were those who reported smoking > 100 cigarettes in their lifetime but were not smoking at the time of the survey. Non-smokers were those who smoked < 100 cigarettes in their lifetime.

Exposure variables and outcomes

The primary exposure variables were the TGI and H. pylori seropositivity, which was determined using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay with an anti-H. pylori-IgG antibody (Wampole Laboratories, Cranbury, NJ) [20]. This method shows reproducibility, sensitivity, and specificity comparable to other serological antibody tests, such as complement fixation, radioimmunoassay, immunofluorescence, and hemagglutination [17, 21]. In NHANES III (1988–1994), H. pylori data were classified as IgG antibody positive or negative. In NHANES (1999–2000), participants were categorized as seronegative (optical density < 1.1) or seropositive (optical density ≥ 1.1). TGI = ln (fasting TG [mg/dL] × FBG [mg/dL] / 2) [22].

The primary outcomes of this study were CVD, including CHD, myocardial infarction, stroke, congestive heart failure (CHF), angina, and hypertension, as well as ACM. The diagnosis of CVD was made if they answered “yes” to the question, “Has a doctor or other health professional ever told you that you had CHD, angina, stroke, CHF, or heart attack?“. Hypertension was determined if any of the following criteria were met: (1) currently taking antihypertensive medications; (2) answered “yes” to the questions, “Has a doctor or other health professional ever told you that you had high blood pressure?“; or (3) diastolic blood pressure was ≥ 90 mmHg or systolic blood pressure was ≥ 140 mmHg.

To examine whether the TGI and H. pylori seropositivity are associated with ACM risk in CVD patients, we performed a survival analysis by grouping participants based on their TGI, H. pylori serological status, and the combination of both factors. First, subjects were categorized into two groups using the third quartile (Q3) of the TGI as the cutoff: those with a TGI ≥ 75th percentile and those with a TGI < 75th percentile. Participants were then further categorized as H. pylori-negative or H. pylori-positive based on their H. pylori-IgG antibody status. Finally, participants were divided into four groups based on the combination of the TGI and H. pylori-IgG antibody status: TGI < 75th percentile & H. pylori-negative, TGI < 75th percentile & H. pylori-positive, TGI ≥ 75th percentile & H. pylori-negative, and TGI ≥ 75th percentile & H. pylori-positive.

We then established three models for analysis: Model 1 was univariate; Model 2 was adjusted for sex, race (non-Hispanic White, other Hispanic, Mexican American, non-Hispanic Black, or other races) and age (continuous); and Model 3 was further adjusted for additional variables, including education level (> high school, high school or equivalent, or < high school), smoking status (never, former, or current smoker), BMI (continuous), diabetes status (yes or no), and HDL (continuous).To assess risk factors for CVD, particularly the TGI and H. pylori-IgG antibody, weighted univariate and multivariate logistic regression was used to estimate the associations between the TGI, H. pylori-IgG antibody, their combination, and CVD. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were obtained. Furthermore, based on multivariate logistic regression models, RCS curves were plotted to investigate the potential nonlinear relationships among the TGI, H. pylori-IgG antibody, and CVD.

A survival analysis was performed to assess the relationships among the TGI, H. pylori seropositivity, and ACM in CVD patients. Patients were divided into four groups based on TGI values (≥ 75th percentile vs. < 75th percentile) and H. pylori serostatus (positive vs. negative). The log-rank test and Kaplan–Meier curves were employed to compare the survival rates among these groups. The hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs for mortality risk were obtained using the Cox proportional hazards models. RCS curves based on the Cox proportional hazards regression model were applied to further investigate potential nonlinear relationships among the TGI, H. pylori seropositivity, and ACM in CVD patients.

A subgroup analysis was utilized by stratifying the population according to prediabetes, diabetes, obesity, and metabolic syndrome. Within each subgroup, a weighted multivariate Cox regression analysis was applied to estimate the relationships among the TGI, H. pylori seropositivity, and mortality risk in CVD patients.

Mediation analyses were performed to explore the indirect and direct relationships between H. pylori seropositivity and CVD via the TGI. In this analysis, H. pylori positivity was designated as the predictor variable (X), a TGI ≥ 75th percentile as the mediator variable (M), and CVD occurrence or mortality in CVD patients as the outcome variable (Y). The mediation process encompassed four steps: (1) The association between X and Y (Model Y = βTot X) was verified, where βTot represents the total effect; (2) the association between X and M (Model M = β1 X) was established, where β1 indicates the indirect effect; (3) the portion of Y explained by adjusting for X (Model Y = β2 M + βDir X) was determined, where β2 represents the indirect effect and βDir denotes the direct effect; and (4) the percentage of the mediation effect was calculated: mediation effect (%) = (β1 × β2 / βTot) × 100%. This method has been extensively applied in previous research to assess the mediation effects [23].

The mortality outcomes and follow-up duration were obtained through a linkage with the National Death Index public access files (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data-linkage/mortality.htm), which was updated on December 31, 2019. Mortality status was represented by the “MORTSTAT” variable, and the follow-up period was captured using the “PERMTH_EXM” variable. The average follow-up duration was 259 ± 106 months for participants who were negative for H. pylori and 235 ± 114 months for H. pylori-positive participants.

Statistical analysis

The characteristics of participants with different H. pylori-IgG antibody statuses (positive vs. negative) at baseline were compared. Continuous data were tested for normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed variables were expressed as means ± standard deviations and compared using the t-test, while those that were not normally distributed were described as medians with interquartile ranges (25–75%) and compared by the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables were shown as frequencies (percentages) and analyzed using Pearson’s Chi-squared test. All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA (17.0). RCS, and mediation interaction analyses were performed using R, with the ‘rms’ package for RCS and the ‘mediation’ package for mediation analysis. NHANES sampling weights were applied to all statistical evaluations. For more details on the survey design and weighting methodology, please refer to the NHANES documentation. Covariates for the models were selected based on established associations with outcomes [6, 24]. A two-sided P-value of < 0.05 was set as statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics at baseline

A total of 9,399 participants (4,748 women and 4,651 men) were included. The characteristics at baseline, stratified by H. pylori serostatus, are presented in Table 1. Among them, 4,488 (47.75%) participants were H. pylori-IgG positive. These participants were more likely to be male, older, non-Hispanic Black, or Mexican American, with higher rates of household poverty and lower education levels (all P < 0.05). They also exhibited higher levels of total cholesterol, TG, FBG, HbA1c, insulin, CRP, and BMI, as well as a higher TGI and increased prevalence of diabetes and CVD (all P < 0.05).

Associations among TGI, H. Pylori serological statuses, and CVD risk

After adjusting for confounding factors, participants with a TGI ≥ 75th percentile had a higher risk of CVD (OR = 1.307; 95% CI: 1.053–1.622) compared to those with a TGI < 75th percentile (Table 2). When combining the TGI and H. pylori seropositivity, participants with a TGI ≥ 75th percentile & H. pylori seropositivity exhibited the highest risk of CVD (OR = 1.487; 95% CI: 1.088–2.033). No significant difference in the risk of CVD was observed between H. pylori-negative and H. pylori-positive participants.

Associations among TGI, H. Pylori serological statuses, and mortality in CVD

Among the total of 3,934 CVD patients, 2,476 (62.9%) died (Table 3). CVD patients with a higher TGI and positive H. pylori-IgG antibody were associated with an elevated rate of ACM (Fig. 3A, B). When evaluating the combined effect of the TGI and H. pylori seropositivity, participants in the TGI ≥ 75th percentile & H. pylori-IgG antibody positive group had the highest ACM rate in comparison with those in the other three groups (Fig. 3C). In adjusted Model 3, CVD patients with a TGI ≥ 75th percentile & positive H. pylori-IgG antibody had the highest ACM risk (HR = 1.227; 95% CI: 1.009–1.491), followed by patients with a TGI ≥ 75th percentile & negative H. pylori-IgG-antibody, and those with a TGI < 75th percentile & positive H. pylori-IgG antibody. However, when the TGI or H. pylori-IgG antibody positivity was evaluated individually, neither showed a statistically significant correlation with ACM. The RCS curves in Fig. 4 indicate a notable linear relationship between the TGI and mortality risk among CVD participants (P for nonlinearity = 0.052). There was no link between H. pylori-IgG antibody levels and the risk of CVD-related mortality.

Discussion

Our findings indicated that H. pylori seropositivity indirectly affects the occurrence and mortality of CVD through the TGI. Specifically, participants exhibiting both a high TGI and positive H. pylori-IgG were at the highest risk of CVD occurrence. Similarly, among individuals with CVD, those with a high TGI and positive H. pylori-IgG exhibited the greatest ACM risk. These associations remained significant after adjusting for confounding factors. These results imply that H. pylori seropositivity may indirectly contribute to CVD risk through mechanisms involving insulin resistance, and combining H. pylori serostatus with the TGI improves the predictive capacity for both CVD occurrence and mortality.

Insulin resistance has been confirmed to play a significant role in the occurrence and progression of cardiovascular diseases. The TGI, as a novel marker of insulin resistance, has also been shown in multiple studies to significantly increase the risk of CVD, including CHD [25], ischemic stroke [26], heart failure [27], myocardial infarction [28]. Additionally, Zhao et al. observed a positive correlation of the TGI with CHD risk and the severity of coronary artery atherosclerosis in patients with NAFLD [8]. Several studies mentioned above suggest that an elevated TGI significantly increases the risk of CVD and may also exacerbate the severity of coronary atherosclerosis. Additionally, existing research has confirmed that elevated TGI can serve as a reliable predictor of increased mortality in various cardiovascular diseases, including hypertension in middle-aged and elderly individuals [29], CHD [30], ischemic stroke in critically ill patients [31], chronic heart failure [32] and acute myocardial infarction [33, 34]. Our data corroborate these findings, confirming the positive impact of the TGI on CVD risk and the association between elevated TGI levels and increased ACM among CVD patients.

The relationship between HPI and increased CVD risk remains controversial. A meta-analysis has demonstrated that HPI is associated with a slight increase in CVD risk [35]. H. pylori seropositivity may increase the risk of several cardiovascular factors, including hypertension [36], dyslipidemia [37], and NAFLD [38], all of which were well-established contributors to CVD development. However, few studies have examined the relationship between H. pylori seropositivity and CVD risk. The study by Epameinondas et al. concluded that there is no association between HPI and CVD [4]. One cohort study reported no significant difference in mortality risk between H. pylori-negative and H. pylori-positive patients with CVD [14]. Our logistic regression and Cox regression analyses revealed that H. pylori-IgG positivity, after adjusting for confounding factors, did not elevate the risk of either CVD occurrence or ACM. Additionally, RCS analysis did not reveal any significant correlation between H. pylori-IgG antibody titers and the risk of CVD or mortality. However, Kaplan–Meier survival curves showed that CVD patients with H. pylori seropositivity had a significantly higher cumulative mortality rate than their H. pylori-negative counterparts. This result suggests that although H. pylori seropositivity may not directly influence CVD occurrence and mortality, it may exert an indirect impact in this population.

Then, we stratified the population based on H. pylori serological status and further grouped the patients using the TGI for additional analysis. A recent study reported that elevated TGI levels are associated with an increased H. pylori seropositivity titer [15]. However, no research to date has examined the combined effect of H. pylori seropositivity and TGI on the risk of CVD and mortality. In the present study, we found that participants with both a high TGI and positive H. pylori-IgG antibody were at the highest risk of CVD. Similarly, among patients with CVD, those with both high TGI levels and H. pylori-IgG positivity were at the highest ACM risk. This trend persisted even after stratifying the CVD patients by metabolic conditions, such as diabetes, prediabetes, obesity, and metabolic syndrome. These findings suggest that the combined effect of H. pylori seropositivity and elevated TGI plays an essential role in CVD risk and mortality.

We further conducted mediation analysis to elucidate the role of the TGI in mediating the relationship between H. pylori seropositivity and CVD occurrence and mortality. Consistent with previous findings, we did not observe a direct association between H. pylori seropositivity and either CVD risk or mortality. However, our analysis revealed a significant indirect effect of H. pylori seropositivity on CVD risk and mortality, as mediated by the TGI. It indicates that H. pylori seropositivity may contribute to increased CVD risk and mortality via its impact on insulin resistance. A previous study established a linear relationship between the TGI and H. pylori-IgG positivity, with higher TGI levels being associated with an increased risk of HPI [15]. A meta-analysis also suggested a potential link between HPI and insulin resistance [39]. HPI may influence insulin resistance through multiple mechanisms, including inflammation, oxidative stress, and adipokine regulation. One such mechanism is inflammation [40], where H. pylori stimulates the production of IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α, leading to the phosphorylation of serine/threonine residues on the insulin receptor. This disrupts receptor activation and ultimately reduces insulin sensitivity, resulting in insulin resistance. Another mechanism is oxidative stress, as HPI induces the production of reactive oxygen species and platelet activation, both of which play a crucial role in insulin resistance [41]. Additionally, H. pylori can affect levels of leptin, monocyte chemoattractant protein-1, growth hormone-releasing peptide, and lipopolysaccharide, which can influence insulin resistance sensitivity [40–43].

H. pylori, as a widely prevalent pathogen, has a relatively high eradication success rate [44]. Being a pathogen that can be cured through relatively simple medical methods, studying the association between H. pylori and other high-incidence, high-disease-burden extra-gastrointestinal diseases may provide new therapeutic targets for clinical treatment. The conclusion of this paper may provide a basis for screening H. pylori serological status in populations for CVD and for implementing clinical practices aimed at changing H. pylori serological status through eradication. It also supports preventive strategies to reduce the infection rate of H. pylori, such as improving public health measures. However whether H. pylori eradication could further reduce CVD risk remains to be explored in further prospective studies.

The limitations of the present work are as follows: First, despite adjusting for multiple confounding factors, the observed associations between the TGI, H. pylori seropositivity, and CVD may still be influenced by residual or unmeasured confounders. In addition, due to the inherent limitations of the NHANES database, the method of H. pylori detection does not include stool tests, urea breath tests, or gastric samples. H. pylori serological antibody status does not directly represent the current infection status of H. pylori. Therefore, this study cannot directly prove the impact of active HPI on CVD, nor can it address whether this effect would disappear after H. pylori eradication. These questions require further validation through prospective cohort studies. Moreover, the lack of H. pylori detection and data on whether H. pylori eradication therapy was administered in subsequent NHANES surveys makes it difficult to overcome the limitations of cross-sectional studies. Particularly, it is challenging to obtain direct evidence to confirm the causal relationship between H. pylori infection status, TGI, and CVD. Finally, our study is based on the NHANES database, which is representative of the United States population. Therefore, the applicability of our results to other regions or other ethnic remains uncertain.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study suggests that H. pylori seropositivity may indirectly elevate the risks of CVD occurrence and mortality through its influence on the TGI. The combination of H. pylori seropositivity and the TGI enhances the predictive capability for both CVD occurrence and related mortality, highlighting the significant extra-gastrointestinal impact of H. pylori seropositivity and its broad pathological effects through metabolic pathways. The results may provide a basis for screening H. pylori serological status in populations for CVD and for implementing clinical practices aimed at changing H. pylori serological status through eradication. It also supports preventive strategies to reduce the infection rate of H. pylori, such as improving public health measures.
