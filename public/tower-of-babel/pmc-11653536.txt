Increasing financial incentives can lower the cost of trial recruitment
Authors: Alan Schwartz, Saul J Weiner, Molly Harrod
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMonetary incentives are commonly used to help recruit trial participants. Some studies have found greater recruitment with larger incentives, while others have found smaller incentives more cost-effective in terms of cost per participant. As part of an implementation study, we compared the impact of four approaches to recruitment, three of which involved phone recruitment with varying financial incentives. Adding modest financial incentives reliably increased the recruitment ratio, and greater incentives increased recruitment more than smaller incentives. However, recruiters required less time to obtain agreement to participate when the greater incentive was offered, and these time savings made the greater incentive cost-saving relative to the smaller incentive and cost-effective relative to no incentive. Our results suggest the possibility of a “sweet spot” for financial incentives, and that trial designers should consider pilot-testing incentive levels in the context of their other recruitment costs to determine whether paying participants more may be cost-saving for trial sponsors.Keywords: Financial incentive, Recruitment, Cost-effectiveness, Clinical trial

Background

Trials are critical to advancing our answers to scientific and medical questions. As a result, strategies for efficiently recruiting trial participants are important [1]. Monetary incentives for recruitment have been the subject of several recent reviews [2, 3, 4, 5]. In a 2014 systematic review, Huynh and colleagues concluded that “monetary incentives, direct contact, and medical referral are cost-effective strategies for recruiting participants to health research studies” [3]. However, a 2018 Cochrane review of strategies to improve recruitment to trials included two studies reporting six trials of comparing financial incentives to no incentive and found no significant pooled difference in the rate of recruitment [4].

Parkinson et al.’s [5] scoping review on designing incentives suggested that greater incentives should generally result in a higher rate of participant recruitment [5]. However, some studies have found higher impact from smaller incentives [6]. Huynh et al. concluded that more research was needed on the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) of incentives. The ICER is the difference in total study costs under two incentive strategies divided by the change in the proportion of invited individuals who participate under each strategy. For example, if one strategy costs $10,000 more than another but yields an additional 10% participation, its ICER is $1000/1% recruitment.

This study responds to the need for additional research on the incremental cost-effectiveness of incentives. Secondary to an implementation study of a patient-collected audio recording program to improve the physician–patient communication, we compared the impact of four sequential approaches to recruitment designed for that study, three of which involved varying financial incentives. We hypothesized that incentives would increase participation and could be cost-effective if they reduced time spent by our paid recruiters to obtain participants.

Methods

We employed in-person waiting room recruiting at the time of a scheduled visit with no incentive, followed by three variations of recruiting by phone in advance of the visit: with no incentive, with a $10 gift card incentive, and with a $20 gift card incentive. There were no other differences in the recruiting scripts or consent materials.

For each approach, we recorded the number of participants invited and the number who participated, as well as the costs of the recruiter’s time and any financial incentives provided to the participant. Recruiters were paid $30.50 per hour throughout the study. Our primary outcome was the cost per participant recruited; a secondary outcome was Parkinson et al.’s ICER. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Jesse Brown VA Medical Center.

Results

Table 1 shows the results of the approaches. Although many patients were approached in the in-person waiting room method, very few (4%) agreed to participate. In contrast, the non-incentive phone-based strategy yielded significantly higher participation (15%; p < 0.001) and required only 3.4 recruiter hours per participant recruited. Phone calls without incentives cost $105 per patient recruited.

Costs and effects of recruiting strategies

Offering a $10 incentive increased the rate of recruitment from 15 to 22% (p = 0.12) at a cost of $153 per patient recruited (ICER $1660/1% recruitment relative to no incentive). Of the $153, $10 was the incentive itself, and the remaining additional cost stemmed from recruiters spending about 30 more minutes per patient contacted, as compared with calls without an incentive.

Offering a $20 incentive, however, not only further increased the rate of recruitment from 22 to 35% (p < 0.001), but reduced the cost per patient recruited to $76 (cost-saving relative to a $10 incentive, ICER $303/1% recruitment relative to no incentive). Although more was spent on incentives, recruiters spent, on average, only about 6 min more per patient contacted than when there was no incentive.

Conclusions

In this embedded study, we found that phone-based recruitment strategies in advance of clinic visits were superior to in-person recruitment at the time of visit. We speculate that this may be because patients were focused on their clinical care at the time of visit and an invitation to participate in research, which requires consideration, imposed cognitive load that was most easily reduced by declining; this requires further study. In contrast, when patients were recruited in advance, they could focus their attention on the recruitment decision and were more likely to agree to participate (and then follow through at the time of their visit).

Adding modest financial incentives to the call-based strategies reliably increased the recruitment ratio, and greater incentives increased recruitment more than smaller incentives. Incentives overall increased recruitment time, likely because of the time required to explain the incentive. However, recruiters required less time to obtain agreement to participate when the greater (vs. smaller) incentive was offered, and these time savings made the greater incentive cost-saving relative to the smaller incentive and cost-effective relative to no incentive, falling within the range of ICERs reported by Huynh et al.’s review (running from cost-saving to $465/1%) [1].

Because the incentives were introduced sequentially, some of the benefit of the greater vs. smaller incentive could have resulted from recruiters becoming more efficient in phone recruiting simply through practice. Despite this limitation, our results suggest the possibility of a “sweet spot” for financial incentives, and that trial designers should consider pilot-testing incentive levels in the context of their other recruitment costs to determine whether paying participants more may be cost-saving for trial sponsors. All else being equal, faster recruitment could also result in completing trials in a shorter timeframe, accelerating the pace of scientific and medical knowledge.
