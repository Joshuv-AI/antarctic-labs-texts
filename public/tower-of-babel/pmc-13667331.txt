Primary Percutaneous Coronary Intervention Guided by High‐Definition Intravascular Ultrasound (The SPECTRUM Study)
Authors: Frederik T W Groenland, Annemieke C Ziedses des Plantes, Tara Neleman, Jurgen M R Ligthart, Karen T Witberg, Rutger‐Jan Nuis, Jeroen M Wilschut, Roberto Diletti, Wijnand K den Dekker, Isabella Kardys, Paul Cummins, Karim D Mahmoud, Nicolas M Van Mieghem, Joost Daemen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundLimited data are available on the use of intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) in patients presenting with ST‐segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI).AimsThis study aimed to assess the impact of IVUS‐guided primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI).MethodsSPECTRUM (NCT05007535) was a prospective, single‐center, observational cohort study. STEMI patients presenting within 12 h after symptom onset with stable hemodynamics and undergoing primary PCI of a native culprit lesion (angiographic reference diameter ≥ 2.25 mm) were eligible. Pre‐intervention, post‐intervention and post‐optimization (if applicable) HD‐IVUS pullbacks were obtained per protocol. The primary endpoint was target vessel failure (TVF) at 12 months, a composite of cardiovascular death, target vessel myocardial infarction, and target vessel revascularization. The secondary endpoint was presence of IVUS‐guided optimization.ResultsA total of 200 STEMI patients were included. Mean age was 62.1 (±12.5) years, 69% were male, 11.0% had diabetes and the left anterior descending was the culprit vessel in 44.0%. IVUS‐guided optimization was performed in 41.1%. Optimal PCI, based on predefined IVUS criteria, increased from 52.1% post intervention to 65.5% post optimization (p < 0.001). A stent‐edge plaque burden > 50% was the main reason for not achieving optimal PCI results after IVUS‐guided optimization (94.4%). TVF at 12 months occurred in 3.0% (95% CI 1.1%−6.4%).ConclusionsSPECTRUM is the first dedicated prospective study demonstrating the potential of IVUS to optimize outcomes in a primary PCI setting. IVUS guidance in primary PCI was safe, significantly increased the number of STEMI patients with an optimal PCI result and resulted in low rates of TVF at 12 months.Keywords: acute coronary syndrome, intravascular ultrasound, percutaneous coronary intervention, ST elevation myocardial infarction

Introduction

In patients with coronary artery disease undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) facilitates tailored lesion preparation, accurate stent sizing and optimization guidance, resulting in superior clinical outcome as compared to angiography‐guided PCI [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7].

A wealth of data supporting the use of intravascular imaging resulted in a class IA recommendation to use either IVUS or optical coherence tomography in patients with chronic coronary syndrome [8]. However, studies supporting the use of IVUS in a primary PCI setting are limited to a single randomized trial in a more general acute coronary syndrome (ACS) population and a meta‐analysis of observational studies suggesting that the beneficial impact of IVUS‐guided PCI may also be extended to patients with acute myocardial infarction [9, 10, 11, 12].

As primary PCI can be challenged by spasm, (spontaneous) dissection, more complex lesion morphology (e.g. plaque ruptures, calcified nodules and thrombus), stent thrombosis, and no‐reflow, patients presenting with ST‐segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) may derive particular benefit from IVUS‐guided procedures [13, 14, 15, 16]. In addition to its general advantages, recent data demonstrated that high‐definition IVUS (HD‐IVUS, i.e., 40−60 MHz) can be used for culprit lesion plaque characterization and thrombus assessment in these patients [17, 18].

Nevertheless, the impact of IVUS‐guided primary PCI on optimization strategies and clinical outcome remains unclear. “Tissue characterization and primary PCI guidance using IVUS” (SPECTRUM) is the first dedicated prospective study assessing the safety and efficacy of IVUS guidance in patients presenting with STEMI [19].

Methods

Study Design and Patient Population

The design of the study has been described previously [19]. SPECTRUM was a prospective, investigator‐initiated, single‐center, observational cohort study performed at the Erasmus University Medical Center, Rotterdam, the Netherlands (NCT05007535). The study was approved by the local ethics committee on October 7, 2020, and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Conference on Harmonization Good Clinical Practice. All patients provided written informed consent.

Adult STEMI patients undergoing primary PCI for a native coronary artery culprit lesion with an angiographic vessel reference diameter ≥ 2.25 mm were eligible. STEMI was defined according to the fourth universal definition of myocardial infarction [20]. Cardiogenic shock (i.e., systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg or the need for vasopressor or mechanical circulatory support) and presentation ≥ 12 h after symptom onset were exclusion criteria.

Study Procedure, Study Materials and Imaging Analysis

Medical therapy, including the use of antithrombotic therapy, occurred per current clinical practice guidelines [13]. PCI was performed using standard techniques at the discretion of the operator.

Intravascular imaging was intentionally performed with the multifrequency high‐definition (40–60 MHz) IVUS system (Kodama, ACIST Medical Systems, Eden Prairie, Minnesota, USA), while stenting was performed with the Firehawk Sirolimus Target Eluting Coronary Stent System (Microport, Shanghai, China). Pullbacks were conducted at an automated pullback speed of 2.5 mm/s, acquiring 60 frames per second (24 frames per mm).

In brief, each patient underwent the following IVUS assessments: (1) A “pre‐intervention IVUS pullback” directly after vessel wiring (i.e., before any lesion preparation, including aspiration thrombectomy); (2) a “post‐intervention IVUS pullback” following PCI (preferably after angiographically successful results, starting at least 2 cm distal to the most distal stent edge or treated segment); (3) a “post‐optimization IVUS pullback“ in case PCI optimization was performed. The predefined criteria for IVUS‐guided optimization were as follows: (1) a residual focal lesion proximal or distal of the stented segment with either a percentage area stenosis ≥ 75% or a percentage area stenosis of 50%−75% accompanied by a minimal lumen area (MLA) of < 2.5 mm2; (2) edge dissections involving the media with a length of >3.0 mm; (3) underexpansion with a minimal stent area (MSA) < 5.0 mm2 and < 90% stent expansion (based on the MLA of the distal stent edge reference segment [5 mm]); (4) an average plaque burden > 50% at the proximal or distal stent edge reference segments (5 mm). The PCI result was considered “optimal” if all IVUS criteria were met. Given the uncertain clinical relevance of stent malapposition and thrombus protrusion and their association with subsequent adverse events, these findings were systematically recorded but were not included among the predefined IVUS criteria for PCI optimization. A detailed description of the protocolized use of IVUS, all quantitative and qualitative intravascular imaging definitions, as well as the results of the pre‐intervention IVUS pullbacks (i.e., culprit lesion findings) have been published previously [18, 19].

Quantitative and qualitative IVUS measurements were performed systematically by the Erasmus University Medical Center academic corelab, using dedicated software (QCU‐CMS, Leiden University Medical Center, LKEB, Division of Imaging Processing, V.4.69) and according to a previously defined methodology [19, 21].

Study Endpoints

The primary endpoint was TVF at 12 months, defined as a composite of cardiovascular death, target vessel myocardial infarction (TVMI) and clinically‐driven target vessel revascularization (TVR) [22]. The secondary outcome of interest was the presence of IVUS‐guided PCI optimization, defined as additional treatment (including balloon dilatation, stent placement or aspiration thrombectomy) following the “post‐intervention IVUS pullback.”

Other endpoints included TVF at 30 days, as well as major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) and stent thrombosis at 30 days and 12 months [19, 22]. MACE was a composite of all‐cause mortality, any myocardial infarction and repeat revascularization (not including a staged PCI or coronary artery bypass graft surgery for multivessel disease).

Data Management and Follow‐Up

All study data were stored in a dedicated local research database. Follow‐up was obtained using an in‐house developed dedicated platform (CathSuite), facilitating online patient‐reported outcome measurements, renouncing the need for sending printed questionnaires, standardized telephone calls, or outpatient clinic visits [19]. All events were adjudicated by an independent clinical event committee.

Sample Size

With 400 to 500 STEMI patients undergoing primary PCI annually at our hospital, and assuming that approximately 40% of patients would be eligible for inclusion (due to exclusion criteria, lack of informed consent, or participation in other registered cardiovascular trials), the sample size of 200 patients was chosen as a pragmatic target allowing both calculation of an acceptably narrow confidence interval (CI) for the primary endpoint, as well as completion of study inclusion within 12−18 months. Assuming a 12‐month TVF rate of 10% as a conservative estimate, a sample size of 200 patients would allow for a 95% CI ranging from 6% to 14% [19].

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages and compared using Pearson's Chi‐Square test or Fisher's exact test (as appropriate). Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed variables and as medians with 25th−75th percentiles for non‐normally distributed variables. The Shapiro−Wilk test was used to investigate whether continuous variables were normally distributed. Changes in continuous and categorical IVUS measurements between post‐intervention and post‐optimization IVUS pullbacks were investigated using paired t‐tests (normally distributed variables), Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests (non‐normally distributed variables) and the McNemar test (binary variables) as appropriate.

TVF is presented as a count with percentage, including the Clopper–Pearson 95% CI for a binomial proportion. Univariable Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was performed to assess the association between (sub)optimal PCI based on the predefined IVUS criteria and TVF.

SPSS version 28 was used for statistical analysis. A 2‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

PCI optimization was performed in 29/99 (29.3%) patients despite an IVUS‐defined optimal PCI result on post‐intervention assessment, and consisted of postdilatation in 25/29 patients (86.2%). Conversely, PCI optimization was performed in 49/91 (53.8%) patients who did not meet the IVUS‐defined optimal stenting criteria on post‐intervention assessment. In these cases, optimization was achieved by means of postdilatation in 45/49 patients (91.8%) and additional stenting in 32/49 patients (65.3%) (Figure 2).

Of interest, PCI optimization was performed despite angiographically successful PCI (i.e., TIMI flow grade 3 with <30% residual stenosis) in 37/78 (47.4%) patients, after angiographically unsuccessful PCI (e.g., no TIMI flow grade 3) in 21/78 (26.9%) patients, and without knowledge of the angiographic result (purely based on post‐intervention IVUS) in 20/78 (25.6%) patients. In 24/37 (64.9%) patients with an angiographically successful PCI result but suboptimal IVUS findings, triggers for PCI optimization were a residual focal lesion in 25.0%, a submedial edge dissection > 3 mm in 4.2%, underexpansion in 20.8%, and edge plaque burden > 50% in 79.2%. Optimization was achieved by means of postdilatation in 91.7% and additional stenting in 58.3%.

No reflow (TIMI flow grade 0 or 1) assessed during the final angiographic imaging was present in four patients (2.0%), and vessel perforations were observed in two patients (1.0%) (Supporting Information S1: Table 2).

Discussion

The SPECTRUM study provides a dedicated evaluation of the protocolized use of IVUS to guide primary PCI in patients presenting with STEMI. The main findings can be summarized as follows: (1) IVUS‐guided PCI was safe and resulted in low TVF rates at 12 months (3%); (2) IVUS‐guided optimization was performed in 41.1%; (3) IVUS‐guided optimization significantly increased the number of cases with an optimal PCI result (from 52.1% to 65.5%) (Central Illustration 1).

The SPECTRUM study investigated the protocolized use of IVUS‐guided primary PCI in patients presenting with STEMI. IVUS‐guided PCI was safe and resulted in low TVF rates at 12 months (3%). Furthermore, IVUS‐guided optimization was performed in 41.1% and significantly increased the number of cases with an optimal PCI result (52.1% to 65.5%; p < 0.001). The 2023 ESC guidelines for the management of ACS provide a class IIa indication for the use of intravascular imaging to guide PCI of a clear culprit lesion in patients presenting with ACS [13]. However, data to support the use of IVUS in this subset of patients are mostly derived from subgroup analyses of large trials in which STEMI patients were vastly underrepresented or excluded. Several studies have proposed that the beneficial impact of IVUS‐guided PCI can be extended to patients with acute myocardial infarction, and more specifically to STEMI [9, 10, 11]. In the recently published IVUS‐ACS trial, which compared IVUS‐ versus angiography‐guided PCI in 3505 ACS patients, the beneficial impact of IVUS guidance on 12‐month TVF persisted in a subgroup analysis including 973 STEMI patients (6.0% vs. 11.5% [HR 0.54, 95% CI 0.34−0.85]) [12]. Although the trial represents a stronger dataset in terms of design and statistical power, it unfortunately refrained from providing detailed insights into the actual mechanisms by which IVUS improved outcomes.

Primary PCI has been recognized as one of the most challenging PCI scenarios for several reasons: (1) presence of spasm with an inherent risk of stent undersizing; (2) presence of (spontaneous) dissection; (3) high thrombus burden and no‐reflow; (4) presence of complex lesion morphology, including plaque ruptures, calcified nodules and stent thrombosis [13, 14, 15, 16, 18]. The latter strengthens the rationale for IVUS guidance to assure optimal PCI results. More specifically, IVUS allows detailed culprit lesion characterization, including the visualization of both calcium and plaque rupture, and the assessment of thrombus [17, 18]. These insights allow tailored use of aspiration thrombectomy, plaque preparation and calcium modification, ensure true lumen stenting, and could potentially guide the use of per‐procedural glycoprotein IIb/IIIa receptor antagonists. Additionally, post‐intervention pullbacks allow PCI optimization specifically tailored to a primary PCI setting, including restrictive bailout stenting and a less aggressive postdilatation approach in case of much thrombus protrusion or no‐reflow.

In the present study, IVUS‐guided optimization was performed in 41.1% of patients. In almost 3 out of 4 patients, additional treatment occurred despite an angiographically successful PCI or without knowledge of the angiographic result. This finding clearly demonstrates how IVUS augments angiography by enabling the identification of optimization targets and reducing contrast use. Several remarkable imaging findings and subsequent optimization strategies should be discussed. First, post‐intervention optimal stenting criteria were not achieved in 47.9% of patients, mostly due to a remaining plaque burden > 50% at the stent edges (84.6%). In over half of these cases (53.8%), further optimization was performed, resulting in a final optimal stenting result as defined by IVUS in 65.6%. Conversely, it remains intriguing that IVUS guidance did not prevent geographic miss in 84.6% of patients with a suboptimal post‐intervention result, and operators refrained from performing optimization in 46.2%. The latter is in line with previous work from our group in more stable patients enrolled within the randomized FFR REACT trial, in which optimal IVUS criteria following optimization were not met in 80.2%, largely due to a remaining plaque burden > 50% at the edges in 75.6% [23]. A potential explanation could lie in the evaluation of edge plaque composition, which, regrettably, was not included in the scope of this study. Second, in patients who did meet all the predefined IVUS criteria following initial PCI, additional PCI optimization was still performed in 29.3%. Optimization mostly consisted of postdilatation only, in cases in which the mean MSA was 5.72 mm2, which may have been considered suboptimal in patients in whom larger stent sizes were used. This finding is not surprising given the fact that IVUS guidance is known to result in higher postdilatation rates [3, 24]. Third, direct stenting rate was as high as 57.5%, aspiration thrombectomy was performed in 7.7% of patients following initial PCI, and in 24.0% of patients glycoprotein IIb/IIIa antagonists were used. The latter, in part driven by IVUS‐derived findings, could have contributed to a final TIMI flow grade 0 or 1 in merely 2.0% of cases. Larger studies are needed to investigate the association between non‐calcified plaque amenable to direct stenting, high thrombus burden, thrombus protrusion, malapposition, and subsequent no reflow, in relation to IVUS‐guided treatment strategies.

As mentioned, in the present study, the rate of IVUS‐defined optimal stenting criteria significantly augmented to 65.5% following PCI optimization. This final number is significantly lower as compared to the 79.9% of patients with an optimal PCI result within the IVUS‐ACS trial, a difference that may be explained by the different threshold for stent‐edge plaque burden (50% vs. 55%) [12]. Although this difference may seem trivial, at least 25 patients within the present study ended with an average stent‐edge plaque burden between 50% and 55%, indicating that the proportion of patients with an optimal PCI result would have been higher if a less strict threshold had been applied. Conversely, within our study, IVUS triggered treatment of residual focal lesions which were not readily apparent on angiography in 26.5% of cases. Hypothetically, this finding may have contributed to the lower‐than‐expected TVF rates observed. However, this study was not powered to assess whether IVUS‐guided optimization of specific predefined targets was associated with improved clinical outcomes.

Acknowledging the limited sample size of our study, the TVF rate at 12 months was merely 3.0% whereas TVMI and TVR were observed in only 2.0% and 1.5%, respectively, with no major procedural safety events noted. Of note, these results should be interpreted in light of primary PCI care in an urban Dutch area with streamlined logistics resulting in a median ischemia time of 124 min. Substantiated by an analysis of Sharp et al. demonstrating that IVUS guidance in ACS is highly cost‐effective, and the eagerly awaited results of the ongoing iSTEMI trial (NCT04775914), future guidelines may further strengthen the recommendations of IVUS in STEMI patients [25].

Limitations

The main limitation of our study is its single‐arm observational design, precluding a direct comparison with angiography‐guided primary PCI. All potential types of bias inherent to this design should be considered. In particular, not all consecutive eligible patients were included, which might limit the generalizability of our findings due to a potentially selected population. Furthermore, although institutional research support was provided by the manufacturers of the IVUS system and stent used in this study, the absence of an angiography‐guided comparator limited our ability to distinguish the independent contribution of IVUS‐guided optimization from potential effects related to the stent platform or institutional expertise.

Second, study procedures were performed at the Erasmus University Medical Center, a high‐volume, “level 3,” catheterization laboratory with extensive expertise in intravascular imaging. We acknowledge that the implementation of IVUS‐guided primary PCI, particularly when including multiple IVUS pullbacks, may be challenging in routine clinical practice due to factors such as limited operator expertise, procedural time constraints, and resource limitations. Developments of AI‐based technologies are currently being implemented in widely used imaging systems and may enhance the feasibility of careful examination of multiple pullbacks.

Third, in certain cases operators deviated from the protocolized use of IVUS, and adherence to the predefined criteria for PCI optimization was not uniform. We believe this reflects real‐world clinical practice.

Fourth, since the use of nitroglycerine was not protocolized, vasospasm may have influenced vessel and lumen parameters with a potential impact on subsequent stent sizing and IVUS‐defined results.

Finally, although clinically reassuring, the observed TVF rate of 3.0% was substantially lower than the anticipated 10% used for sample size calculation, resulting in reduced statistical power. The low number of events precluded subgroup analyses as well as multivariable adjustment to assess the association between IVUS findings and TVF. The in‐house CathSuite follow‐up platform, based on online patient‐reported outcome measurements without standardized telephone calls or clinical visits, may have resulted in underreporting of clinical outcomes.

Conclusions

SPECTRUM is the first dedicated prospective study demonstrating the potential of IVUS to optimize outcomes in a primary PCI setting. IVUS guidance in primary PCI was safe, significantly increased the number of STEMI patients with an optimal PCI result, and resulted in low rates of TVF at 12 months.
