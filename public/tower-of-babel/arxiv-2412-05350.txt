Lorenz gauge and the Poisson bracket in canonical electromagnetism
D. Jaffino Stargen

Abstract
In treatments of electromagnetism, it is often tacitly assumed that the vector potentials of the field and their conjugate momenta satisfy the canonical Poisson bracket relations, despite the fact that the components of the vector potential are constrained by gauge conditions. Here I explicate how this comes about by imposing Poisson bracket relations on the independent field variables remaining after the Lorenz gauge constraint is accounted for. The naively assumed Poisson brackets happens to be correct even after gauge fixing, owing to a conspiracy between the gauge conditions and the principle of relativity.



I
Introduction

In the Hamiltonian view of classical systems

[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
, the state of a system is
specified by a set of
n
generalized coordinates
\mathbf{q}=\{q_{i}\}
and their
conjugate momenta
\mathbf{p}=\{p_{i}\}
, assumed to be unconstrained and satisfying
the Poisson bracket relation
\{q_{i},p_{j}\}=\delta_{ij}
; time evolution of
the state is described by Hamilton’s equations:
\dot{q}_{i}=\{q_{i},H\}
and

\dot{p}_{i}=\{p_{i},H\}
. Thus, in this scheme, the Poisson bracket between
the
n

independent
pairs of variables (“degrees of freedom”) plays
a crucial role.

If the degrees of freedom are not independent, i.e. if they are constrained,

\phi(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})=0
, then the Hamiltonian formalism has to be
amended
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
:
either the Poisson bracket has to be modified, or, if they are retained by
treating the variables as independent, then the constraints
\phi=0
need to be
imposed as auxiliary conditions.

The classical theory of the electromagnetic field naturally presents a constrained
problem. If the system is to be treated in an explicitly Lorentz covariant manner,
then the appropriate field coordinates are the vector potentials
\{A^{\mu}\}
.
However, not all configurations of the potentials are physically distinct,
i.e. a gauge constraint of the form
\phi(A^{\mu})=0
appears between them.
Paralleling the discussion above, one of two possibilities can address these
constraints. The Poisson bracket suitable, had the field variables been independent,

\{A^{\mu}(t,{\bf x}),\pi^{\nu}(t,{\bf x}^{\prime})\}=\eta^{\mu\nu}\delta({\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\prime}),

(1)

needs to be modified to account for the gauge constraints, where the signature
of the Minkowski
metric is
\eta=(-1,1,1,1)

[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
.
Or, if they are to be retained as above, the gauge constraint needs to be imposed
separately, and further, it must be ensured that once imposed, they remain valid
for all times, i.e.
\{\phi(A^{\mu}),H\}=0
. Both these routes can be explored by using
Dirac’s theory of constrained Hamiltonian mechanics

[
7
,
8
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
.

A less sophisticated, and arguably more transparent, approach is conceivable:
eliminate the gauge constraint explicitly in order to identify the true
independent degrees of freedom of the field, and impose Poisson brackets on those.
Although not apriori obvious, such a procedure, if performed consistently, agrees
with
eq.

1
. The primary purpose of our exposition is precisely to demonstrate
how this comes about, i.e., how the Poisson bracket structure of electromagnetism
[
eq.

1
] is upheld
after
the gauge constraint is accounted for.
As it happens, this relies on the principle of relativity
in an essential manner.

The exposition is organized as follows: in
Section

II
we describe the
appropriate degrees of freedom corresponding to the electromagnetic field system;

Section

III
explicates how the Poisson bracket corresponding to the field
variables,
A^{\mu}
and
\pi^{\mu}
, is modified due to the gauge constraint,
and indeed how the Poisson bracket preserves its structure even after the gauge
constraint is taken into consideration; and we conclude in
Section

IV
.

II
Canonical electromagnetism

We start with the Lagrangian density of the electromagnetic field as

[
16
,
10
]

{\cal L}=-\frac{1}{2}{\partial}_{\mu}A_{\nu}{\partial}^{\mu}A^{\nu},

(2)

where the four-potential,
A^{\mu}
, are taken to be the (generalized) coordinates,
whose canonically conjugate momentum,
\pi_{\mu}
, is

\pi_{\mu}\equiv\frac{{\partial}{\cal L}}{{\partial}({\partial}_{0}A^{\mu})}={\partial}_{0}A_{\mu}.

(3)

However, due to the gauge constraints involved, the field coordinates,
A^{\mu}
,
are not independent of each other. The gauge constraint of our choice is of the
Lorenz form,
i.e.
,

\partial_{\mu}A^{\mu}=0.

(4)

In order to render the subsequent discussion as simple as possible,
we decompose the field coordinate,
A^{\mu}
, as

A^{\mu}(\mathbf{x},t)=\sum_{\lambda=0}^{3}\int{\rm d}^{3}{\bf k}\varepsilon^{\mu}_{(\lambda)}q^{(\lambda)}_{{\bf k}}(t)e^{-{\mathrm{i}}{\bf k}\cdot{\bf x}},

(5)

where
\varepsilon^{\mu}_{(\lambda)}
are polarization vectors, with
\mu
and

\lambda
signifying the spacetime and polarization indices, respectively;
the variables,
q^{(\lambda)}_{{\bf k}}
, capture the dynamical degrees of freedom
corresponding to the field, and are complex variables in general.
Since the vector potential,
A^{\mu}
, is real, the dynamical variables,

q^{(\lambda)}_{{\bf k}}
,
and its complex conjugate,
q^{*(\lambda)}_{{\bf k}}
, are related by

q^{*(\lambda)}_{{\bf k}}=q^{(\lambda)}_{-{\bf k}}
.

Therefore, the field Lagrangian,
L
, in terms of the dynamical
variables,
q^{(\lambda)}_{{\bf k}}
, can be obtained from the Lagrangian density,

{\cal L}
, of the field [
eq.

2
] as

L\equiv\int{\rm d}^{3}{\bf x}~{\cal L}(t,{\bf x})=\int{\rm d}^{3}{\bf k}~L_{{\bf k}},

(6)

where

L_{{\bf k}}\equiv\eta_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\biggl({\dot{q}}_{{\bf k}}^{(\lambda)}{\dot{q}}_{-{\bf k}}^{(\lambda^{\prime})}-|{\bf k}|^{2}q_{{\bf k}}^{(\lambda)}q_{-{\bf k}}^{(\lambda^{\prime})}\biggr).

(7)

Since the dynamical variables,
q^{(\lambda)}_{{\bf k}}
, are independent of each
other prior to taking gauge constraint into consideration, the Lagrangian,

L_{{\bf k}}
, corresponding to a field mode, labelled by
{\bf k}
, is composed of four

independent
dynamical systems corresponding to the dynamical variables,

q^{(\lambda)}_{{\bf k}}
. The momentum variables canonically conjugate to the
variables,
q^{(\lambda)}_{{\bf k}}
, can be found to be

p_{{\bf k}(\lambda)}\equiv\frac{{\partial}L_{{\bf k}}}{{\partial}{\dot{q}}^{(\lambda)}_{{\bf k}}}={\dot{q}}_{-{\bf k}(\lambda)},

(8)

p_{-{\bf k}(\lambda)}\equiv\frac{{\partial}L_{{\bf k}}}{{\partial}{\dot{q}}^{(\lambda)}_{-{\bf k}}}={\dot{q}}_{{\bf k}(\lambda)}.

(9)

Therefore, the field Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle\equiv\int{\rm d}^{3}{\bf k}~\eta_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\biggl(p_{{\bf k}}^{(\lambda)}{\dot{q}}_{{\bf k}}^{(\lambda^{\prime})}+p_{-{\bf k}}^{(\lambda)}{\dot{q}}_{-{\bf k}}^{(\lambda^{\prime})}-L_{{\bf k}}\biggr)

(10)

\displaystyle=\int{\rm d}^{3}{\bf k}~\eta_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\biggl(p_{{\bf k}}^{(\lambda)}p_{-{\bf k}}^{(\lambda^{\prime})}+|{\bf k}|^{2}q_{{\bf k}}^{(\lambda)}q_{-{\bf k}}^{(\lambda^{\prime})}\biggr).

(11)

Using this canonical setup of classical electromagnetism,
we eliminate the redundant variables due to the gauge constraint [
eq.

4
],
and analyze the effects of incorporating this redundancy in the functional form
of the Poisson bracket corresponding to the field variables,
(A^{\mu},\pi^{\mu})
.

III
Lorenz gauge and Poisson bracket

The Lorenz gauge condition in
eq.

4
, when written in terms of the
dynamical variables,

(q_{\pm\mathbf{k}}^{(\lambda)},p_{\pm\mathbf{k}}^{(\lambda)})
, becomes

\left({\rm k}_{i}\varepsilon^{i}_{(\lambda)}\right)q^{(\lambda)}_{\pm{\bf k}}\pm i\varepsilon^{0}_{(\lambda)}p^{(\lambda)}_{\mp{\bf k}}=0,

(12)

which are a set of algebraic constraints between the variables

q_{\pm\mathbf{k}}^{(\lambda)}
and
p_{\pm\mathbf{k}}^{(\lambda)}
.

In the Hamiltonian view the gauge constraints in
eq.

12

are imposed at one instant of time; it is then necessary to ensure that
they remain true under the time evolution generated by the Hamiltonian
H
.
That is, one needs to demand that

\{{\partial}_{\mu}A^{\mu},H\}=0,

(13)

with the expression for Poisson bracket (see
appendix

A
) as

\displaystyle\{f_{{\bf k}},g_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}\}

\displaystyle\equiv\eta^{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\biggl(\frac{{\partial}f_{{\bf k}}}{{\partial}q^{(\lambda)}_{{\bf k}}}\frac{{\partial}g_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}}{{\partial}p^{(\lambda^{\prime})}_{{\bf k}}}-\frac{{\partial}g_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}}{{\partial}q^{(\lambda)}_{{\bf k}}}\frac{{\partial}f_{{\bf k}}}{{\partial}p^{(\lambda^{\prime})}_{{\bf k}}}

\displaystyle\hskip-7.0pt+\frac{{\partial}f_{{\bf k}}}{{\partial}q^{(\lambda)}_{-{\bf k}}}\frac{{\partial}g_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}}{{\partial}p^{(\lambda^{\prime})}_{-{\bf k}}}-\frac{{\partial}g_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}}{{\partial}q^{(\lambda)}_{-{\bf k}}}\frac{{\partial}f_{{\bf k}}}{{\partial}p^{(\lambda^{\prime})}_{-{\bf k}}}\biggr)\delta({\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}),

(14)

and it will generate an independent set of algebraic constraints in terms
of the dynamical variables,

(q_{\pm\mathbf{k}}^{(\lambda)},p_{\pm\mathbf{k}}^{(\lambda)})
.

Using these expressions, and the field Hamiltonian,
H
,
[
eq.

11
], to compute the Poisson bracket in

eq.

13
implies the algebraic constraints

|{\bf k}|^{2}\varepsilon^{0}_{(\lambda)}q^{(\lambda)}_{\pm{\bf k}}\pm i\left({\rm k}_{i}\varepsilon^{i}_{(\lambda)}\right)p^{(\lambda)}_{\mp{\bf k}}=0.

(15)

One can further check whether the condition imposed before, i.e.,

\{{\partial}_{\mu}A^{\mu},H\}=0
, too remains enforced in time, which means demanding

\{\{{\partial}_{\mu}A^{\mu},H\},H\}=0
. Direct computation yields

\begin{split}\{\{{\partial}_{\mu}A^{\mu},H\},H\}&=\int{\rm d}^{3}{\bf k}~e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\\
&\times\left[\left({\rm k}_{i}\varepsilon^{i}_{(\lambda)}\right)q^{(\lambda)}_{\pm{\bf k}}\pm i\varepsilon^{0}_{(\lambda)}p^{(\lambda)}_{\mp{\bf k}}\right].\end{split}

(16)

Comparing the expression in the square bracket with the algebraic equation
in
eq.

12
, it is evident
that the condition,
\{\{{\partial}_{\mu}A^{\mu},H\},H\}=0
, does not need to be
demanded separately, since the algebraic equation
in
eq.

12
makes it inevitable.

Since the Lorenz gauge constraint [
eq.

4
] is manifested as two
pairs of algebraic equations [
eqs.

12
and
15
],
eliminating the gauge constraint [
eq.

4
] amounts to solving
these algebraic equations
to eliminate the redundant variables,
say,
(q^{(0)}_{\pm{\bf k}},p^{(0)}_{\pm{\bf k}})
. Therefore, the redundant variables,

(q^{(0)}_{\pm{\bf k}},p^{(0)}_{\pm{\bf k}})
,
can be expressed in terms of the independent ones,

(q^{(i)}_{\pm{\bf k}},p^{(i)}_{\pm{\bf k}})
, as

\displaystyle q^{(0)}_{\pm{\bf k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{(i^{\prime})}q^{(i^{\prime})}_{\pm{\bf k}}\mp i\beta_{(i^{\prime})}p^{(i^{\prime})}_{\mp{\bf k}}}{\gamma},

(17a)

\displaystyle p^{(0)}_{\mp{\bf k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pm i|{\bf k}|^{2}\beta_{(i^{\prime})}q^{(i^{\prime})}_{\pm{\bf k}}+\alpha_{(i^{\prime})}p^{(i^{\prime})}_{\mp{\bf k}}}{\gamma},

(17b)

where

\displaystyle\alpha_{(i)}

\displaystyle\equiv-\left({\rm k}_{\ell}\varepsilon^{\ell}_{(0)}\right)\left({\rm k}_{m}\varepsilon^{m}_{(i)}\right)+|{\bf k}|^{2}\varepsilon^{0}_{(0)}\varepsilon^{0}_{(i)},

(18a)

\displaystyle\beta_{(i)}

\displaystyle\equiv\left({\rm k}_{\ell}\varepsilon^{\ell}_{(0)}\right)\varepsilon^{0}_{(i)}-\varepsilon^{0}_{(0)}\left({\rm k}_{m}\varepsilon^{m}_{(i)}\right),

(18b)

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle\equiv\left({\rm k}_{\ell}\varepsilon^{\ell}_{(0)}\right)^{2}-|{\bf k}|^{2}\left(\varepsilon^{0}_{(0)}\right)^{2}.

(18c)

Therefore, the gauge constraint in
eq.

4
gets manifested
into four algebraic relations between the redundant variables,

(q^{(0)}_{\pm{\bf k}},p^{(0)}_{\pm{\bf k}})
, and
the independent variables,
(q^{(i)}_{\pm{\bf k}},p^{(i)}_{\pm{\bf k}})
,
as shown in
eq.

17
.
These four algebraic relations [
eq.

17
] reduce the total
number of degrees of freedom,

(q^{(\lambda)}_{\pm{\bf k}},p^{(\lambda)}_{\pm{\bf k}})
, from sixteen
(when gauge constraints are not imposed),
to twelve. Since the variables,
(q^{(i)}_{\pm{\bf k}},p^{(j)}_{\pm{\bf k}})
,
are supposed to combine in describing
the six components of
electric and magnetic fields,
({\bf E},{\bf B})
, it may appear
that the (twelve) independent variables,

(q^{(i)}_{\pm{\bf k}},p^{(j)}_{\pm{\bf k}})
, over-describe the system, and
it can be shown straightforwardly that this
is not the case (see appendix
B
).

Therefore, the linearly independent canonical variables,

(q^{(i)}_{\pm{\bf k}},p^{(i)}_{\pm{\bf k}})
,
that are left after taking the gauge constraint [
eq.

4
]
into consideration, possess a
Poisson bracket expression in a reduced form as

\displaystyle\{f_{{\bf k}},g_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}\}_{\mathbf{r}}

\displaystyle\equiv\delta^{ij}\biggl(\frac{{\partial}f_{{\bf k}}}{{\partial}q^{(i)}_{{\bf k}}}\frac{{\partial}g_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}}{{\partial}p^{(j)}_{{\bf k}}}-\frac{{\partial}g_{{\bf k}}^{\prime}}{{\partial}q^{(i)}_{{\bf k}}}\frac{{\partial}f_{{\bf k}}}{{\partial}p^{(j)}_{{\bf k}}}

\displaystyle+\frac{{\partial}f_{{\bf k}}}{{\partial}q^{(i)}_{-{\bf k}}}\frac{{\partial}g_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}}{{\partial}p^{(j)}_{-{\bf k}}}-\frac{{\partial}g_{{\bf k}}^{\prime}}{{\partial}q^{(i)}_{-{\bf k}}}\frac{{\partial}f_{{\bf k}}}{{\partial}p^{(j)}_{-{\bf k}}}\biggr)\delta({\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}),

(19)

which implies

\displaystyle\{q^{(i)}_{{\bf k}},p^{(j)}_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}\}_{\mathbf{r}}

\displaystyle=\delta^{ij}\delta({\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}),

(20a)

\displaystyle\{q^{(i)}_{{\bf k}},p^{(j)}_{-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\}_{\mathbf{r}}

\displaystyle=\{q^{(i)}_{-{\bf k}},p^{(j)}_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}\}_{\mathbf{r}}=0,

(20b)

\displaystyle\{q^{(i)}_{\pm{\bf k}},q^{(j)}_{\pm{\bf k}^{\prime}}\}_{\mathbf{r}}

\displaystyle=\{p^{(i)}_{\pm{\bf k}},p^{(j)}_{\pm{\bf k}^{\prime}}\}_{\mathbf{r}}=0,

(20c)

where we call
\{\cdot,\cdot\}_{\mathbf{r}}
as the reduced–Poisson
bracket corresponding to the canonical variables
(q^{(i)}_{\pm{\bf k}},p^{(i)}_{\pm{\bf k}})
.
This is also evident from
14
, since any general
observable, after taking the gauge constraint
[
eqs.

12
and
15
] into consideration, are functions
only of the dynamical variables
(q^{(i)}_{\pm{\bf k}},p^{(i)}_{\pm{\bf k}})
.

Since the (redundant) variables,
(q^{(0)}_{\pm{\bf k}},p^{(0)}_{\pm{\bf k}})
,
depend on the variables,
(q^{(i)}_{\pm{\bf k}},p^{(i)}_{\pm{\bf k}})
, as given
by
eq.

17
, the reduced-Poisson bracket of the former can be evaluated to be

\{q^{(0)}_{-{\bf k}},p^{(0)}_{-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\}_{\mathbf{r}}=\{q^{(0)}_{{\bf k}},p^{(0)}_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}\}_{\mathbf{r}}=\left(\frac{2|{\bf k}|^{2}}{\gamma}+1\right)\delta({\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}).

(21)

The Poisson brackets of these dynamical variables [i.e.
eqs.

20
and
21
]
can be used explicitly to compute the Poisson bracket of the field variable

A^{\mu}
, and its conjugate momentum variable
\pi^{\mu}
[
eq.

3
]:

\displaystyle\{A^{\mu}(t,{\bf x}),\pi^{\nu}(t,{\bf x}^{\prime})\}

\displaystyle=\int{\rm d}^{3}{\bf k}~{\rm d}^{3}{\bf k}^{\prime}~{\rm e}^{-i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf x}}~{\rm e}^{i{\bf k}^{\prime}{\bf x}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\times\varepsilon^{\mu}_{(\lambda)}\varepsilon^{\nu}_{(\lambda^{\prime})}\{q^{(\lambda)}_{{\bf k}},p^{(\lambda^{\prime})}_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}\}_{\mathbf{r}},

(22)

which is evaluated to be

\displaystyle\{A^{\mu}(t,{\bf x}),\pi^{\nu}(t,{\bf x}^{\prime})\}=\int{\rm d}^{3}{\bf k}~{\rm e}^{-i{\bf k}\cdot({\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\times\biggl\{\eta^{\mu\nu}+\frac{1}{\gamma}\biggl[-k_{\ell}\left(\eta^{\ell\mu}\varepsilon^{\nu}_{(0)}+\eta^{\ell\nu}\varepsilon^{\mu}_{(0)}\right)\left(k_{m}\varepsilon^{m}_{(0)}\right)

\displaystyle+|{\bf k}|^{2}\varepsilon^{0}_{(0)}\left(\varepsilon^{\mu}_{(0)}\eta^{\nu 0}+\varepsilon^{\nu}_{(0)}\eta^{\mu 0}\right)+2|{\bf k}|^{2}\varepsilon^{\mu}_{(0)}\varepsilon^{\nu}_{(0)}\biggr]\biggr\}.

(23)

III.1
What preserves the Poisson bracket?

Though the Lorenz gauge constraint [
eq.

4
] apparently modifies the
Poisson bracket of the field and its conjugate momentum in a nontrivial manner
[
23
], it turns out that the principle of relativity reduces
the Poisson bracket expression in
23
to the simpler form as
in
eq.

1
. According to the principle of relativity, there cannot exist a
rest frame for electromagnetic fields, since it is a massless (gauge) field;
implying that the polarization vector
\varepsilon^{\mu}_{(0)}
is

[
10
,
16
]

\varepsilon^{\mu}_{(0)}=\left(-1,0,0,0\right).

(24)

Remarkably, making use
only
of this fact,
23

reduces nicely to

\{A^{\mu}(t,{\bf x}),\pi^{\nu}(t,{\bf x}^{\prime})\}=\eta^{\mu\nu}\delta({\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\prime}).

(25)

Therefore, the principle of relativity plays an essential role in ensuring
the validity of the Poisson bracket relation in
eq.

1
, even
after

the Lorenz gauge constraint [
eq.

4
] is imposed.

IV
Conclusion

If the field variables,
A^{\mu}
, of classical electromagnetism were
dynamically independent, then the functional form of the Poisson bracket
between the field,
A^{\mu}
, and its conjugate momentum,
\pi^{\mu}
, would
have been of the form given in
eq.

1
. However, since the field variables,

A^{\mu}
, are related by a gauge constraint [
eq.

4
], the Poisson
bracket is modified to the form in
23
. But, remarkably,
the principle of special relativity — which obviates a rest frame for
electromagnetic fields and consequently implies that the polarization vector
satisfies
eq.

24
— suffices to ensure that the Poisson bracket
in
23
takes the oft-assumed form in
eq.

1
. This state
of affairs is a result of the careful interplay between relativity, gauge
invariance, and the Hamiltonian structure of classical electromagnetism
— an analysis of which seems to be missing in the extant literature,
to the best of our knowledge, and which we have provided in a pedagogical fashion.
