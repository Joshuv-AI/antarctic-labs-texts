Evaluation of vinpocetine in an acute doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity model in rats
Authors: Abdullah Ercan, Mehmet Ediz Sarihan, Onural Ozhan, Necip Ermis, Azibe Yildiz, Nigar Vardi, Merve Durhan, Yilmaz Cigremis, Cemil Colak, Hakan Parlakpinar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDoxorubicin (DOX) is an effective antineoplastic agent whose clinical use is limited by cardiotoxicity. This study aimed to evaluate the potential protective effects of vinpocetine (VINPO) in an acute DOX-induced cardiotoxicity model in rats. Thirty-two male Wistar albino rats were randomly assigned to four groups: Control, DOX (20 mg/kg, single intraperitoneal dose), VINPO (10 mg/kg for 3 days), and VINPO + DOX. Forty-eight hours after DOX administration, electrocardiography (ECG), heart rate (HR), blood pressure (BP), and oxygen saturation were recorded. Serum troponin-I, creatin kinase (CK), and CK-MB mass levels were measured. Myocardial and vascular tissues were analyzed for malondialdehyde (MDA), superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and reduced glutathione (GSH). Histopathological and caspase-3 immunohistochemical evaluations were also performed. DOX administration resulted in significant alterations in vascular antioxidant enzyme activities and increased myocardial histopathological injury scores compared with controls. VINPO partially modulated certain oxidative stress parameters, particularly vascular SOD and CAT activities. However, serum cardiac biomarkers, ECG parameters, and caspase-3 immunoreactivity did not differ significantly among groups. Histopathological myocardial injury was not significantly improved in the VINPO + DOX group compared with DOX alone. VINPO demonstrated modest modulatory effects on selected oxidative stress parameters in acute DOX-induced cardiotoxicity. However, these effects were not consistently supported by functional or histopathological findings. Further studies using more severe or longer-term cardiotoxicity models are warranted to clarify its cardioprotective potential.Keywords: Doxorubicin, Vinpocetine, Cardiotoxicity, Malondialdehyde, Caspase-3 immunostainingSubject terms: Cardiology, Drug discovery, Medical research

Introduction

Anthracyclines are widely used and highly effective antineoplastic agents; however, their clinical use is limited by dose-dependent cardiotoxicity, which may progress to irreversible cardiomyopathy and heart failure (HF)1. Doxorubicin (DOX), one of the most commonly prescribed anthracyclines, induces cardiotoxicity through multiple interconnected mechanisms, including oxidative stress (OS), mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammation, and apoptosis2.

Among these mechanisms, excessive generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and impairment of endogenous antioxidant defense systems are considered central contributors to DOX-induced myocardial injury. OS disrupts the balance between oxidants and antioxidants, leading to lipid peroxidation (LP), cellular damage, and cardiomyocyte death3. Consequently, antioxidant-based strategies have been investigated as potential therapeutic approaches to attenuate DOX-related cardiac injury4.

Vinpocetine (VINPO) is a semi-synthetic derivative of the vinca alkaloid vincamine, which is isolated from the plant Vinca minor5. Chemically, VINPO is an ethyl apovincaminate compound with a complex indole-based structure that enables lipophilic membrane permeability and central nervous system penetration6. It has been widely used in several countries for the treatment of cerebrovascular disorders and cognitive impairment7.

Pharmacologically, VINPO exerts multiple biological effects. It is known to inhibit phosphodiesterase type 1 (PDE1), modulate voltage-dependent sodium channels, and enhance cerebral blood flow6. In addition, VINPO has demonstrated anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, partly through suppression of nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling and attenuation of OS–related pathways8,9. Experimental studies have also suggested that VINPO may reduce apoptosis and mitochondrial dysfunction under conditions of cellular stress10.

Beyond its neuroprotective effects, increasing evidence indicates that VINPO may exert cardioprotective actions11. OS and inflammation are central mechanisms in various forms of myocardial injury, including drug-induced cardiotoxicity12–15. Among cardiotoxic agents, DOX, a widely used anthracycline chemotherapeutic agent, is well known to induce dose-dependent cardiac injury characterized by OS, mitochondrial dysfunction, and apoptosis16–18.

In the present study, we employed an acute DOX-induced cardiotoxicity model rather than a myocardial ischemia model. Although both conditions result in myocardial injury, DOX-induced cardiotoxicity and ischemic injury differ substantially in their underlying pathophysiological mechanisms19. DOX cardiotoxicity is primarily associated with OS, mitochondrial dysfunction, and cardiomyocyte injury, whereas ischemic heart injury is mainly driven by hypoxia and reperfusion-related mechanisms19–21. Therefore, the present experimental design specifically aims to investigate acute DOX-induced cardiotoxicity and its pharmacological modulation.

Given the central role of OS in DOX-induced cardiotoxicity and the reported antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties of VINPO, the present study aimed to investigate the potential protective effects of VINPO in an acute DOX-induced cardiotoxicity model in rats using biochemical, electrophysiological, and histopathological parameters.

Materials and methods

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Inonu University, Faculty of Medicine (Protocol No. 2017/A-43) and was supported by the Scientific Research Projects Fund of Inonu University (Project No. 2016/164). All experimental procedures were performed at the Experimental Animal Research Laboratory of the Inonu University, Faculty of Medicine.

A total of 32 male Wistar albino rats, weighing 300–400 g, were obtained from the Experimental Animal Research Laboratory of Inonu University. The animals had comparable biological and physiological characteristics and were randomly assigned to four experimental groups (n = 8 per group). The rats were housed under standard laboratory conditions (22 ± 2 °C, 60% relative humidity, and a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle) with free access to standard pellet chow and water.

Animal care, including cage cleaning, waste removal, and the provision of food and water, was performed by the facility’s veterinarian and trained staff. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the Principles for the Care of Laboratory Animals published by the National Health Research Community and the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals issued by the Institute of Laboratory Animal Resources and the National Institutes of Health22. In addition, the study was designed, conducted, and reported in compliance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines to ensure transparency, rigor, and reproducibility of the animal research23.

Experimental groups

The rats were randomly assigned to four experimental groups, each consisting of eight animals. Group 1 (Control, n = 8) received the vehicle solution [dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO): saline] intraperitoneally once daily for three days. Group 2 (DOX, n = 8) was administered a single intraperitoneal (i.p.) dose of DOX (20 mg/kg) on the first day of the study, followed by i.p. administration of the vehicle solution (DMSO: saline) once daily for the subsequent three days. Group 3 (VINPO, n = 8) received VINPO intraperitoneally at a dose of 10 mg/kg once daily for three consecutive days. Group 4 (VINPO + DOX, n = 8) initially received VINPO (10 mg/kg, i.p.), followed 30 min later by a single i.p. dose of DOX (20 mg/kg); VINPO administration (10 mg/kg, i.p.) was then continued once daily for an additional 2 days. VINPO was dissolved in a DMSO:saline solution prior to i.p. administration. The DMSO:saline vehicle was used in both the VINPO (Group 3) and VINPO + DOX (Group 4) groups.

The experimental protocol was designed to model acute DOX-induced cardiotoxicity and does not represent an ischemia–reperfusion or myocardial ischemia model. At 48 h after DOX administration, electrocardiography (ECG), heart rate (HR), blood pressure (BP), and oxygen saturation were recorded using the Biopac MP100 Data Acquisition System. After completion of these measurements, the rats were euthanized, and blood as well as myocardial and vascular tissue samples were collected for subsequent biochemical, histopathological, and immunohistochemical analyses.

Surgical procedure

Prior to surgery, the rats were anesthetized by i.p. injection of xylazine hydrochloride (8 mg/kg; Rompun®, Bayer) and ketamine hydrochloride (75 mg/kg; Ketalar®, Pfizer). After adequate anesthesia was achieved, the animals were placed in the supine position on the operating table, and the surgical field was disinfected with 10% povidone–iodine. A 3-cm midline incision was then made through the skin, subcutaneous tissue, and fascia.

Mean arterial BP was measured via carotid artery cannulation, and oxygen saturation was continuously monitored. Blood samples were collected from all groups by intracardiac puncture for biochemical analyses. The collected blood samples (5 mL) were centrifuged, and the obtained serum was stored at − 80 °C until analysis.

Upon completion of all experimental procedures, the rats were maintained under deep anesthesia with ketamine (75 mg/kg, i.p.) and xylazine (8 mg/kg, i.p.). After confirmation of a surgical plane of anesthesia, euthanasia was performed by exsanguination via intracardiac blood collection.

Myocardial and vascular tissues were excised, sectioned both vertically and horizontally, and immediately fixed in 10% formalin. The tissue samples were then stored at 4 °C until histopathological processing.

Serum biochemical evaluation

Blood samples obtained from the rats were collected into gel vacuum tubes (BD Vacutainer SST, UK) and centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 10 min. The resulting serum was analyzed using an automated analyzer system at the Central Laboratory of Inonu University Turgut Ozal Medical Center. Serum levels of troponin-I, creatine kinase (CK), and creatine kinase–MB isoenzyme (CK-MB mass) were measured.

Tissue biochemical evaluation

Malondialdehyde (MDA), an indicator of LP, was measured according to the method described by Uchiyama and Mihara24. Reduced glutathione (GSH) levels were determined using Ellman’s method25. Tissue superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was assessed according to the method of Sun et al.26, and tissue catalase (CAT) activity was measured following Lück’s method27. Protein concentrations in tissue samples were determined using the Biuret protein assay, with bovine serum albumin used as the standard28.

Histopathological methods

At the end of the experiment, myocardial and vascular tissues were fixed in 10% formaldehyde. Following fixation, the tissues were processed through routine dehydration and clearing procedures and embedded in paraffin blocks. Sections 4–5 μm in thickness were cut from the paraffin blocks and stained with hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) for general histological evaluation.

Histopathological assessment of myocardial sections focused on the presence of congestion–hemorrhage and cardiomyocyte degeneration, including cytoplasmic hypereosinophilia and pyknotic nuclei. For each specimen, ten randomly selected microscopic fields were evaluated and scored according to the severity of histopathological changes as follows: 0, no change; 1, mild; 2, moderate; and 3, severe.

In vascular tissue sections, histopathological alterations such as thinning of the elastic lamellae and loss of myofibrils in smooth muscle cells were evaluated using the same semiquantitative scoring system. The semiquantitative histopathological scoring was performed in a blinded manner by an independent histologist who was unaware of the experimental group assignments.

All analyses were performed using a Leica DFC-280 research microscope in conjunction with the Leica Q Win Image Analysis System (Leica Micros Imaging Solutions Ltd., Cambridge, UK).

Immunohistochemical analysis

For immunohistochemical analysis, tissue sections were first deparaffinized and rehydrated. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating the sections in 0.01 M citrate buffer (pH 6.0) in a pressure cooker for 15–20 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched by incubation with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 12 min. After washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), sections were incubated with a protein blocking solution (Ultra V Block) for 5 min to reduce nonspecific binding.

The sections were incubated with a rabbit polyclonal anti–caspase-3 antibody (Caspase-3 (CPP32) Ab-4, Cat. #RB-1197-P1, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Fremont, CA, USA) at a dilution of 1:200 at 37 °C for 60 min. Following PBS washing, a biotinylated secondary antibody was applied for 10 min at 37 °C. Sections were subsequently incubated with streptavidin–peroxidase for 10 min at 37 °C. Immunoreactivity was visualized using a chromogen, after which the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin and mounted with a water-based mounting medium.

The degree of positive staining was evaluated using a semiquantitative scoring system based on staining intensity (0: 0–25%; 1: 26–50%; 2: 51–75%; 3: 76–100%) and distribution (0: negative; +1: mild; +2: moderate; +3: severe), as previously described29. Immunohistochemical scoring was also conducted under blinded conditions.

Statistical analysis

As the data did not conform to a normal distribution, quantitative variables were expressed as median (minimum–maximum). Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (SPSS for Windows, version 26). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Comparisons among groups were conducted using the Kruskal–Wallis H test for nonparametric data. When statistically significant differences were detected, pairwise comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

DOX is a broad-spectrum and highly effective chemotherapeutic agent that has been used in the treatment of various malignant neoplasms30. However, its clinical use is commonly associated with several side effects, including irreversible cardiotoxicity that can progress to congestive HF31.

The prevention of cardiotoxicity has become increasingly important due to the growing number of patients receiving anthracycline therapy. Numerous studies have been conducted for this purpose, and several strategies have been shown to partially mitigate toxicity. Approaches to reduce cardiotoxicity include modifying anthracycline administration to limit myocardial exposure, using anthracycline analogs with lower cardiotoxic potential, and co-administering preventive or cardioprotective agents32.

Various cardioprotective drugs may be combined to reduce DOX-induced cardiotoxicity or to increase efficacy at lower doses33. Therefore, adjuvant drugs are co-administered with DOX to reduce DOX-related cardiotoxicity or to increase therapeutic effects34. Although studies have mostly yielded useful results, strategies developed against DOX-related complications remain weak35.

This study aimed to investigate the protective and therapeutic effect of VINPO is a potent antioxidant and antiinflammatory agent in previous studies on cardiotoxicity experimentally induced by DOX or isoproterenol (ISO) in rats36,37. The effects of VINPO were evaluated using biochemical, electrophysiological, and histopathological parameters.

The selection of the 48-hour time point represents another important aspect of the experimental design. DOX-induced cardiotoxicity is known to manifest early biochemical and cellular alterations within the first few days after administration, including OS, mitochondrial dysfunction, and initial histopathological changes38. Previous acute cardiotoxicity studies have demonstrated that these early events typically become detectable within a 24–72 h window following DOX exposure39,40. Therefore, the 48-h evaluation was chosen to capture the early phase of DOX-induced myocardial injury, while minimizing the confounding effects of longer-term remodeling processes.

According to some recent studies, DOX increases the release of CK-MB mass and CK in myocardial tissues. This is significant in the diagnosis of DOX-induced cardiotoxicity41,42. In the present study, no statistically significant differences were observed among the groups with respect to CK levels, and CK-MB mass values remained within the normal range in all experimental groups. This finding may be explained by the rapid increase in circulating CK and CK-MB levels following acute myocardial injury, followed by their normalization within a relatively short time period. In other words, although CK and CK-MB mass levels may transiently increase after DOX administration due to cardiac damage, they may have returned to near-normal levels by the time the measurements were performed.

Previous studies have shown that in cases of minimal myocyte damage, troponin levels are increased and can be used as a marker of cardiotoxicity42,43. In the present study, cardiac troponin I (cTnI) levels were measured in all groups. Although no statistically significant differences were observed among the groups, cTnI levels tended to be higher in the DOX group compared with the control group and lower in the VINPO + DOX group than in the DOX group. This pattern may suggest that DOX induced cardiac injury and that VINPO exerted a potential protective effect, although this interpretation should be made cautiously given the lack of statistical significance.

Although no statistically significant differences were observed, HR tended to be higher in the DOX and VINPO + DOX groups. This increase may reflect a reflex (compensatory) tachycardia, which is thought to occur secondary to anthracycline-induced inhibition of contractile protein synthesis. Such a pattern of tachycardia is consistent with findings reported in previous studies. Meta‑analysis in breast cancer (37 studies, 5705 patients) found heart‑rate changes in 12% of patients, with tachycardia in ~ 7% during anthracycline therapy44. Pediatric Holter study showed increased supraventricular tachycardia risk and QT changes after anthracyclines, linked to increased sympathetic activity45. A large pediatric ALL cohort reported persistent tachycardia and other ECG abnormalities during daunorubicin‑containing chemotherapy46. These data support that tachycardia during anthracycline treatment is common and clinically recognized.

There were no statistically significant differences among the groups with respect to ECG parameters, including PR interval, QRS duration, and QT interval. These findings differ from those reported in some studies on DOX-induced cardiotoxicity. This discrepancy may indicate that, in the acute phase, cardiotoxicity primarily manifests as reflex tachycardia, whereas other ECG abnormalities have not yet developed. Therefore, it is plausible that longer-term studies might reveal additional ECG alterations not captured within the present experimental time frame. Acute/high‑dose DOX in animals frequently alters repolarization without large changes in PR or QRS. Rat studies of acute DOX (days 5–10) show significant QT/corrected QT (QTc) prolongation and ST‑segment changes, while RR, PR and QRS are often unchanged or only mildly affected47,48. Another acute/chronic rat study found QRS and QT/QTc increased in both acute and chronic groups, but HR, P amplitude and PR could remain unchanged acutely49. Reviews describe acute toxicity (within days) as mainly transient ECG abnormalities and arrhythmias, often repolarization changes, with dose, species and anesthesia strongly modifying ECG patterns31,50,51. Importantly, some acute models also report no significant change in several intervals early on, despite biochemical and structural myocardial injury48,52. Importantly, some acute models also report no significant change in several intervals early on, despite biochemical and structural myocardial injury.

When ECG abnormalities were examined, T-wave negativity was observed in one rat in the control group, four rats in the DOX group, and three rats in the VINPO + DOX group. The higher incidence of T-wave negativity in the DOX group supports the presence of developing cardiotoxicity. In a retrospective series of lymphoma patients with chronic DOX‑related cardiac dysfunction, the most prominent ECG change at the time DOX‑related cardiac dysfunction was diagnosed was T‑wave flattening in V3–V6 (80%), alongside QT interval changes. These T‑wave and QT apex interval abnormalities were already detectable in a milder form months before the echo‑defined left ventricle dysfunction appeared, and were absent at baseline, supporting their role as early indicators of DOX cardiotoxicity53. A proof‑of‑concept human myocardial slice study found that patients treated with anthracyclines showed acute and chronic alterations in T‑, P‑, and R‑wave morphology, paralleling ex vivo evidence of DOX‑induced myocardial injury and inter‑patient susceptibility54. In patients receiving DOX, new T‑wave flattening or negativity, especially in lateral/precordial leads, is a plausible early sign of cardiotoxicity, particularly when it appears after treatment and progresses. However, it should be interpreted together with QT metrics, troponin/N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), and imaging [global longitudinal strain (GLS) or cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR)] rather than used alone to diagnose cardiotoxicity.

When BP values were compared, systolic, diastolic, and mean BP levels were higher in the DOX and VINPO + DOX groups. This elevation in BP does not fully align with the findings reported in the majority of previous studies on DOX-induced cardiotoxicity. Effects on systolic, diastolic and mean BP are dose‑, time‑ and model‑dependent, and are not consistently higher than control. Animal and human studies show mixed BP responses rather than a simple increase. In a rat model, intermediate and high cumulative doses of DOX (16–20 mg/kg) caused significant hypotension, with lower systolic, diastolic and mean BP versus controls (e.g., mean BP ~ 129 vs. 95–87 mmHg, p < 0.05)55. Another rat study reported increased systolic, diastolic and mean arterial BP with DOX, alongside QT prolongation and reduced HR; exenatide partially reversed these BP changes56. In women with breast cancer receiving DOX/cyclophosphamide, 24‑h ambulatory monitoring showed a general trend toward decreased mean 24‑h systolic and diastolic BP after chemotherapy, not higher values, while arterial stiffness indices increased57. In Hodgkin lymphoma patients on adriamycin (DOX), bleomycin, vinblastine, and dacarbazine (ABVD), carotid‑femoral pulse‑wave velocity decreased over treatment, while changes in systolic and diastolic BP were small and statistically insignificant (≈ 4/1 mmHg decrease)58. However, changes in HR, BP and ECG due to DOX administration continued in the VINPO + DOX group, meaning that the expected improvement was not achieved in this experimental protocol.

As antioxidative system and OS markers in myocardial ve vascular tissue samples; MDA, SOD, CAT and GSH were assayed. MDA is one of the most known secondary products of LP, and it can be used as a marker of cell membrane injury59. SOD, which is one of the endogenous enzymatic antioxidants SOD, provides the conversion of superoxide to oxygen and hydrogen peroxide. SOD appears to be the first line of defense against oxygen-induced free radicals and can be rapidly induced when exposed to OS60. CAT is an antioxidant enzyme that plays a significant role in cellular protection against oxidative damage by reducing hydrogen peroxide to oxygen and water61. GSH is the principal intracellular antioxidant, which may act directly by scavenging reactive oxygen and nitrogen species or indirectly supporting enzymatic activity as a cofactor62.

According to our results, CAT activity in myocardial tissue was higher in the DOX and VINPO + DOX groups compared with the control group, and it was also higher in the VINPO + DOX group than in the DOX group. This pattern may suggest a possible modulatory effect of VINPO on the antioxidant defense system rather than a robust stimulatory action. In addition, myocardial MDA, SOD, and reduced GSH levels tended to be higher in the DOX group compared with the control group, whereas these values showed a tendency to move toward control levels in the VINPO + DOX group. The elevations in myocardial MDA, SOD, CAT, and GSH levels may reflect a compensatory response to DOX-induced OS. Moreover, a reduction in glutathione peroxidase (GPx) activity could have contributed to increased GSH levels by limiting its oxidation. Pre-treatment with VINPO before DOX administration was associated with MDA, GSH, and SOD levels approaching those of the control group; however, this observation should be interpreted cautiously given the modest magnitude of the changes. Notably, the increases in GSH and CAT levels following DOX administration are in line with some previous reports. Breast cancer patients on DOX showed higher CAT and GPx activity than healthy controls, suggesting an adaptive up‑regulation of antioxidant enzymes in blood cells63. In rat liver, DOX (5 mg/kg/week) increased catalase and GPx, along with SOD, compared to control64. In leukemia cell lines treated with DOX–transferrin conjugate, CAT activity increased, with coordinated up‑regulation of other GSH‑related enzymes65. In several tumor or normal cell models, DOX exposure was associated with increased intracellular GSH as part of a stress response or resistance phenotype66,67. An increase in GSH and CAT after DOX is biologically plausible and documented in several models, especially peripheral blood cells and some tumor or liver settings. However, many toxicity‑focused studies show depletion instead, so any statement should specify the tissue, dose, and timing when claiming consistency with previous work.

The increase in MDA levels after DOX administration, together with the values approaching those of the control group in the VINPO + DOX group, may be attributed to enhanced LP induced by DOX and a potential protective effect of VINPO through attenuation of LP. However, the observation of increased MDA levels following VINPO administration alone, despite its known antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, is unexpected. This finding suggests that the elevation of MDA cannot be fully explained within the scope of the present study. Therefore, this result should be interpreted with caution, and further investigations are warranted to clarify the underlying mechanisms. In toxic demyelination induced by ethidium bromide in rats, VINPO given without other drugs showed a dose‑dependent, bidirectional effect on brain MDA. Low doses VINPO (1.5–3 mg/kg) decreased MDA in cortex and striatum, consistent with antioxidant action. High dose VINPO (6 mg/kg) increased MDA in cortex and hippocampus and lowered total antioxidant capacity, indicating a pro‑oxidant shift at higher dosing68. In an aluminum chloride neurotoxicity model, low‑dose VINPO (10 mg/kg) reduced brain MDA and had neuroprotective effects, whereas higher doses of related nootropics showed pro‑oxidant and pro‑inflammatory properties, supporting a “low‑dose protective, high‑dose harmful” pattern for this class69. Across animal data, VINPO is generally antioxidant and lowers MDA, but high doses in some brain models can paradoxically increase MDA and reduce antioxidant capacity. This suggests a narrow therapeutic window and that any observed rise in MDA after VINPO alone is most plausibly dose‑ or context‑dependent rather than a universal effect.

In addition, GSH, SOD, and CAT levels were also assessed in vascular tissue. GSH levels were significantly higher in the DOX group compared with the control group. This increase in GSH may reflect a compensatory defense response of the vascular tissue to DOX-induced stress. Moreover, suppression of GPx activity could have contributed to the elevation of GSH levels by limiting its oxidation. In line with our findings, increases in GSH levels following DOX administration have also been reported in previous studies. Endothelial (SVEC4‑10) and tumor cell lines exposed to DOX plus the GSH‑synthesis inhibitor showed marked GSH modulation, demonstrating that DOX‑related toxicity is tightly linked to GSH levels in vascular/tumor cells67. However, no difference in terms of GSH level in the DOX group and VINPO + DOX group attributed that VINPO was unable to return DOX-induced cardiotoxicity.

SOD and CAT enzyme activities decreased significantly after DOX administration. This result is consistent with previous studies and can be explained by the inhibitory effect of DOX on the antioxidant system70. In contrast, both SOD and CAT levels in the VINPO + DOX group were close to the control group, unlike the DOX group. This is a finding that supports the antioxidant activity of VINPO.

Myocardial tissue exhibited a normal histopathological appearance in both the control and VINPO groups, suggesting that VINPO did not induce histopathological cardiotoxicity under the conditions of the present study. In the DOX group, congestion–hemorrhage and cardiomyocyte degeneration were observed in myocardial sections stained with H&E. However, no significant differences were detected between the DOX and VINPO + DOX groups in the histological evaluation. These histopathological alterations observed in the DOX group are consistent with DOX-induced cardiotoxicity and are in line with findings reported in the literature40,71. No histopathological differences were observed among the groups in vascular tissue sections stained with H&E. The absence of overt histopathological changes despite alterations in tissue markers may be attributed to the possibility that DOX-induced toxicity caused segmental or focal vascular injury that was not captured in the examined sections.

In addition, caspase-3 immunoreactivity was evaluated in both myocardial and vascular tissues. Although no statistically significant differences were observed among the groups, caspase-3 immunoreactivity tended to be higher in the DOX group. Caspase‑3 is a key executioner caspase in apoptosis. Increased caspase‑3 immunoreactivity or activity in a DOX group generally indicates that DOX is triggering more apoptotic cell death in that tissue or cell type. This condition supports apoptosis due to DOX treatment and this result in accordance with the literature72,73. However, no statistically significant difference was observed in caspase-3 immunoreactivity between the DOX and VINPO + DOX groups. Accordingly, under the conditions of the present study, VINPO did not appear to produce a clear protective effect against DOX-induced injury with respect to caspase-3 immunoreactivity and histopathological alterations.

Although VINPO is known for its potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, it did not reverse the DOX-induced histopathological changes under the conditions of the present study. This lack of effect may be related to an insufficient dose of VINPO or to the possibility that its protective effects require a longer time to become apparent. In contrast to our findings, Güven et al.37 reported that VINPO exerted protective effects against ISO-induced cardiotoxicity. In that study, VINPO was administered at a dose of 20 mg/kg, and the animals were sacrificed four days after the first dose of VINPO. Differences in dosing and experimental timing may partly explain the discrepancy between the results. Moreover, it is possible that the affected myocardial regions were not fully represented in the examined sections due to the focal nature of the injury. Therefore, further studies using different doses and longer follow-up periods are warranted to more accurately delineate the cardioprotective potential of VINPO.

A major limitation of the present study is that the degree of cardiac injury induced by DOX was relatively mild, and most of the assessed parameters did not reach statistical significance. In the current experimental design, an acute DOX administration protocol with a limited duration was used, which may have resulted in a less severe cardiac phenotype compared with chronic cardiotoxicity models. In contrast, several previous studies employing longer treatment durations, repeated dosing regimens, or higher cumulative doses of DOX have reported more pronounced biochemical, functional, and histopathological alterations, allowing clearer detection of cardioprotective interventions. For example, in experimental settings using either repeated dosing or longer observation periods, DOX has been shown to induce marked biochemical derangements together with severe histopathological changes, including inflammatory cell infiltration, myocardial fibrosis, and cardiomyocyte disorganization, thereby providing a more robust window to detect cardioprotective interventions74. Similarly, studies evaluating antioxidant-based strategies, such as melatonin, have reported pronounced increases in MDA levels accompanied by clear morphological damage, including fibrosis and vacuolization of cardiomyocytes, which were partially reversed by the intervention, indicating a substantially more severe injury phenotype than that observed in the present model75. Moreover, in models employing therapeutic modulation after DOX exposure (e.g., molsidomine-based protocols), histopathological scoring systems and longer treatment schedules have been used to capture structural myocardial injury more comprehensively, again facilitating clearer detection of treatment effects76. Moreover, when compared with the clinical phenotype observed in patients receiving anthracycline-based chemotherapy, the extent of myocardial injury in the present model appears substantially milder. Clinical studies and comprehensive reviews indicate that anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity in patients encompasses a broad and often severe spectrum, ranging from asymptomatic left ventricular dysfunction to overt dilated cardiomyopathy and congestive HF, frequently associated with persistent reductions in left ventricular ejection fraction, myocardial remodeling, and adverse long-term outcomes. In this context, symptomatic HF has been reported in a substantial proportion of high-risk patients, and a significant number of individuals fail to recover baseline cardiac function once impairment has occurred, underscoring the clinical severity and chronic nature of anthracycline-related cardiac injury77. Moreover, contemporary clinical data emphasize that anthracycline cardiotoxicity represents a disease spectrum that includes both symptomatic HF and subclinical but prognostically relevant declines in systolic function, with incidence rates increasing markedly with cumulative dose and patient-related risk factors78. Consistent with these clinical observations, recent pathophysiological analyses highlight that DOX-induced cardiotoxicity in humans is frequently associated with progressive myocardial dysfunction, structural remodeling, and, in a subset of patients, irreversible HF, a phenotype that is clearly more severe than the modest myocardial alterations observed in the present experimental model19. This modest degree of injury may have limited our ability to fully demonstrate the cardioprotective potential of VINPO, as robust protective effects are inherently more difficult to detect in models characterized by only mild damage. Therefore, the absence of consistent and statistically significant changes in several endpoints may, at least in part, reflect the limited severity of the DOX-induced injury model rather than a true lack of pharmacological efficacy. Future studies using longer-term or more severe DOX-induced cardiotoxicity protocols may provide a clearer and more definitive assessment of the cardioprotective effects of VINPO.

Another important limitation of the present study is the relatively mild nature of the histopathological findings. The histological injury scores were low overall, and although some trends were observed, the differences in caspase-3 did not reach statistical significance between the experimental groups. While these results were acknowledged, the modest degree of structural myocardial damage likely limited our ability to detect clear and robust treatment-related effects. In experimental settings characterized by only mild tissue injury, it is inherently more difficult to demonstrate significant improvements in both histological and apoptotic markers. Early molecular/apoptotic changes often precede visible structural damage. In mild injury, histology may appear almost normal while gene/protein markers are already altered, so histology underestimates true injury and protection79. Therefore, the absence of significant differences in caspase-3 and the low histopathological scores should be considered a major limitation of the present study and taken into account when interpreting the cardioprotective potential of the intervention.

Another methodological limitation of the present study is the relatively short duration of ECG recordings. Although standard ECG parameters were assessed, brief recording periods may have limited our ability to detect transient arrhythmias or subtle electrophysiological abnormalities. Therefore, the absence of significant differences in ECG findings should be interpreted with caution, and longer or continuous ECG monitoring in future studies may provide a more comprehensive evaluation of DOX-induced electrophysiological alterations and the potential effects of VINPO.

Conclusion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the potential protective and therapeutic effects of VINPO, a compound with anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and neuroprotective properties, in a DOX-induced cardiotoxicity model. The present findings are consistent with the literature regarding the cardiotoxic effects of DOX. Based on certain biochemical parameters and tissue markers, VINPO appeared to partially attenuate some of the unfavorable changes induced by DOX, suggesting a possible cardioprotective and therapeutic potential. However, these effects were not supported by hemodynamic parameters, electrocardiographic findings, or histopathological evaluations. Taken together, these results indicate that the observed effects were modest, and further studies including larger sample sizes, additional experimental groups, and different dosing regimens are warranted to more clearly define the protective and therapeutic potential of VINPO in cardiotoxicity. In conclusion, although VINPO modestly reversed some biochemical aspects of DOX-induced cardiotoxicity, the relatively mild degree of cardiac injury observed in this model represents a major limitation of the study and should be taken into account when interpreting the cardioprotective potential of VINPO.
