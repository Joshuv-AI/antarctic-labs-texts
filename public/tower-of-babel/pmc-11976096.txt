Evaluation of salivary flow rate, pH and anti-CCP antibodies in relation to oral manifestations in patients with Rheumatoid Arthritis: A matched case-control study in Sulaimaniyah, Iraq
Authors: Hevi Nihad Mohammed Fadhil, Khadija Mohammed Ahmed
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRheumatoid arthritis (RA) affects the oral cavity, mainly through exocrine salivary gland dysfunction, leading to reduced salivary flow rate. Recently, salivary antibodies against cyclic citrullinated peptide (CCP) have been suggested as diagnostic and prognostic tools in patients with RA.ObjectivesTo assess salivary flow rate, pH, and oral manifestations in patients with RA and evaluate salivary anti-CCP antibody levels as biomarkers of inflammation.Subjects and methodsA matched case-control study was conducted at the Rheumatology Centre in Sulaimaniyah, Iraq, on 90 subjects (50 patients with RA and 40 healthy controls) between January 2021 and January 2022. The oral cavity was examined for signs of oral and temporomandibular joint (TMJ) involvement along with the assessment of the Decayed, Missed, Filled Teeth (DMFT) index and oral health status. Saliva samples were collected from each subject to measure the flow rate, pH, and anti-CCP antibodies using ELISA.ResultsThe salivary flow rate was substantially reduced in patients with RA. The main oral manifestations include oral ulcerations, tongue redness, dry/cracked lips, buccal mucosal dryness, taste disturbances, angular cheilitis, and candidiasis. TMJ involvement and higher DMFT scores were also more frequent in patients with RA. However, there was no significant difference between the groups regarding the oral hygiene index or salivary anti-CCP antibody levels.ConclusionsPatients with RA exhibit decreased salivary flow rates and generally poorer oral health status. Salivary anti-CCP antibody levels were deficient but without a significant difference between the RA and control groups.Keywords: Autoimmune disease, Blood biomarker, Salivary variables, Case-control study

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic, inflammatory, autoimmune, systemic illness characterised by joint inflammation and cartilage/bone damage, with significant consequences and multiple organ involvement, leading to oedema, pain, stiffness, and loss of function (Ediz et al, 2011). RA also presents with systemic manifestations, including haematologic, neurologic, cardiovascular, and lacrimal/salivary gland damage (Zalewska et al, 2013).

Additionally, RA is associated with various oral manifestations such as temporomandibular joint (TMJ) disorders, gingivitis, periodontitis, xerostomia, secondary Sjögren's syndrome, and bacterial or fungal infections. Studies have shown that treating oral diseases in patients with severe RA can alleviate some RA symptoms (Silvestre-Rangil et al., 2016, Tar et al., 2021).

Patients with RA often experience xerostomia, which has a detrimental effect on their overall oral hygiene (Vineet and Nayak, 2015). Consequent hyposalivation presents as a furrowed or erythematous tongue, atrophic mucosa, food retention, and cervical caries (Osailan et al., 2012). TMJ involvement is commonly associated with pain, masticatory muscle tenderness, joint sounds, and restricted joint movement (Aliko et al., 2011). Moreover, oral health issues related to RA and its management may lead to additional complications owing to immunosuppressive effects, promoting conditions such as candidiasis and oral ulceration, exacerbated by saliva deficiency (de Souza et al., 2016).

Frequently used diagnostic tests for RA include the assessment of antinuclear antibodies (ANA), C-reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), rheumatoid factor (RF), and anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (CCP) antibodies. Citrullinated proteins are abundant in inflammatory tissues (Tar et al., 2021). Patients with RA develop anti-CCP antibodies that attack healthy tissues. Anti-CCP antibodies are important for the diagnosis and prognosis of RA because of their specificity and sensitivity (van der Helm-van Mil et al., 2005, Dionello et al., 2016). Owing to their anti-inflammatory and immunological properties, salivary immunoglobulin (Ig) A antibodies may protect the oral cavity from mucosal citrullinated antigens and correlate with a milder form of RA (Svärd, 2020; Mortazavi et al., 2022).

This study was designed to evaluate the main oral complications affecting patients with RA and highlight the significance of salivary anti-CCP antibodies as biomarkers of inflammation using the ELISA technique.

Subjects and methods

Study design and setting

A case-control study was performed at the Rheumatology Centre, Sulaimaniyah, Iraq, on 50 patients with established RA (6 males and 44 females) and 40 healthy age- and sex-matched controls (4 males and 36 females) from January 2021 to January 2022.

Questionnaire

A validated, self-administered questionnaire was designed to include the sociodemographic characteristics of the patients including, sex and age, occupation, smoking status, information on medications used, and oral complaints, specifically dry mouth.

Study protocol

Extensive clinical examination of the oral mucosa was conducted to detect signs of ulceration, redness, and dryness. To assess xerostomia (salivary gland hypofunction), patients were scored as follows: a score of 0 was given to patients who experienced no feeling of dry mouth, a score of 1 for mild, 2 for pronounced, and 3 for severe dry mouth affecting speech (Moen et al., 2005) Salivary flow rate and pH were also measured using standard procedures (Mahesh et al., 2014).

TMJ involvement was assessed by evaluating joint sounds (clicking or crepitus), tenderness over the TMJ and the masticatory muscles, pain during jaw opening, maximal mouth opening (inter-incisal distance < 40 mm), and mandibular deviation (Anderson et al., 2010). The dental status of the participants was determined using the Decayed, Missed, Filled Teeth (DMFT) index, and oral hygiene was assessed using the oral hygiene index-simplified (OHI-S).

Salivary anti-CCP IgA was measured using the Sandwich-ELISA method with a human anti-CCP antibody ELISA kit (Elabscience, USA). A standard curve was constructed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, version 25) was used for data analysis. Significant differences between the groups with respect to different quantitative variables were assessed using independent t-tests. The chi-square test was used to compare categorical data between groups. Linear regression showed an association between DMFT and salivary flow rate. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant while a p-value of < 0.001 was deemed highly significant.

Results

Sociodemographic features of participants

Most participants in both groups were females (44 in the RA group and 37 in the control group). The mean age of patients in the RA group was 48.04 ± 13.07 years (range 25–76), while it was 42.93 ± 9.21 (range 25–66) years in the control group. Most patients in the RA (72 %) and control groups (38 %) were housewives. Additionally, the most commonly used drug for patients with RA was methotrexate (72 %), followed by prednisolone (18 %), and 12 % used other drugs such as hydroxychloroquine and sulfasalazine.

Subjects' feeling of oral dryness

There was a significant difference (p < 0.05) in the feeling of oral dryness between the RA and control groups. Patients with RA experienced more severe dry-mouth-related difficulties, and their oral functions were more compromised than those of healthy controls (Fig. 1).

There was a significant (p < 0.05) increase in the level of oral manifestations in the RA group compared to healthy controls. The DMFT index was significantly higher in the RA group than in the control group (p = 0.04), especially the index of missing and decayed teeth. In the RA group, 24 % of the patients were completely edentulous and 6 % were partially edentulous. In the control group, 12.5 % of the patients were completely edentulous and 10 % were partially edentulous. Discussion

Results of this study indicate that women are significantly more likely to be affected by RA than men of the same age, consistent with the findings of Pedersen et al. (2006), who suggested that sex hormones may be involved in RA. Additionally, the results revealed a significant decrease in salivary fluid secretion in the RA group compared to that in the control group, with four patients experiencing xerostomia (no saliva secretion at all) This could be attributed to secondary Sjögren syndrome. Zalewska et al. (2013) reported xerostomia in patients with RA, accompanied by a substantial decrease in salivary flow in the control group. This is in agreement with the results of other studies (Silvestre-Rangil et al., 2016, Nagler et al., 2003, Aliko et al., 2010).

The current study revealed a significant difference in salivary pH between the two groups. The mean pH value of the control group was 6.63, whereas that of the experimental group was 6.39. These results confirm that when the physiological salivary pH range increases, the flow rate of saliva increases, and vice versa. This finding is compatible with that reported by Bardow et al. (2000), who concluded that salivary pH became significantly more alkaline with increasing flow rates. However, this is contrary to the results of Farsi (2007), who found no significant changes in salivary flow rate.

The feeling of oral dryness was higher in the RA group than in the control group. Regarding xerostomia related to oral function, with a sum score range of 0–4, most patients reported sipping liquids to aid in swallowing dry foods, and many reported having no difficulty in consuming dry food. Notably, none of the subjects reported whether the saliva in their mouth seemed to be too little or too much. These results are consistent with those reported by Uhlig et al. (1999), who demonstrated a high incidence of oral dryness in patients with RA. Wolfe and Michaud (2008) indicated that oral dryness is particularly common in RA and is influenced by the severity of the disease, as well as the effects of drug therapy. Russell and Reisine (1998) indicated that the symptoms of dry mouth in patients with RA may or may not correlate with actual salivary gland dysfunction. Conversely, Zalewska et al. (2013) suggested that xerostomia in patients with RA may reflect a compromised salivary immune system.

Regarding the oral manifestations observed in RA, we demonstrated a significant increase in the RA group compared to healthy controls, especially taste disturbance (34 %) and mouth ulcers (42 %). Although one patient in the control group had angular cheilitis and another had oral candidiasis, this could be attributed to inadequate oral hygiene. These outcomes follow that of Al- Yassin, (2013), who identified oral ulceration to be the most prominent manifestation in patients with RA.

Our findings were identical to those of Zalewska et al. (2011) who confirmed that the DMFT index was 1.229 times higher in patients with RA than in healthy controls. Likewise, Zalewska et al. (2013) reported a significantly higher DMFT index in the RA group than in healthy controls. However, Yamakawa et al. (2002) found no significant difference in the number of decayed or filled teeth between the two groups but found significantly more missing teeth in the RA group. Overall, the DMFT indices were substantially more numerous. However, Silvister-Rangil et al. (2016) and Pischon et al. (2008) disagree with our findings, as they could not establish a significant difference in the DMFT indices between the two groups. Additionally, while the percentage of completely edentulous subjects was higher in the RA group, the difference was not statistically significant. Conversely, the percentage of partially edentulous subjects were higher in the control group. These findings are incompatible with those of Yamakawa et al. (2002) who reported significantly higher percentages of both partial and complete edentulism in the RA group than in the healthy control group.

This study found a significantly higher debris index in the RA group, likely due to limitations in manual dexterity. However, no significant differences were observed in the calculus index between groups resulting in an overall OHI-S score that was insignificant. These findings are consistent with those of Madikeri, (2006), who reported no substantial difference in OHI-S between RA and the control group. In terms of TMJ involvement, patients with RA exhibited significantly more clinical signs and symptoms. Joint sounds were the most frequent symptom in patients with RA, aligning with the findings of Aliko et al., 2011, Garib and Qaradaxi, 2011, and Abdulla et al. (2014), who also identified joint sounds as the most frequent TMJ findings in patients with RA. In the present study, 46 % of the patients presented with TMJ clicks.

In comparison, only 6 % had crepitus, which might be ascribed to the fact that most patients received medical treatment, such as corticosteroids or disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs, which cause less crepitation, as the drugs can reduce or prevent damage to the joint (Sidebottom and Salha,2013). This was reported by Aliko et al. (2011), who observed that sounds were mainly presented as clicks, whereas the occurrence of crepitations on clinical examination was 14 %. These results contrast with those of other studies (Yamakawa et al., 2002, Bessa-Nogueira et al., 2008), which observed a higher frequency of crepitus in the TMJ area in patients with RA (36 % and 29.5 %, respectively).

This study also revealed that tenderness in the TMJ and masticatory muscles was significantly higher in the RA group than in the healthy controls, which supports the findings of Moen et al. (2005), who found a higher frequency of symptoms and dysfunction related to the TMJ and masticatory system in patients with RA. This is also compatible with a study by Yamakawa et al. (2002), who reported more frequent TMJ tenderness in subjects with RA than in non-RA control groups. Moreover, pain during mouth opening was significantly higher in the RA group in the present study, which disagrees with Yamakawa et al. (2002), who observed no differences in pain between the two groups. This is also inconsistent with Pischon et al. (2008), who reported no significant differences in TMJ symptoms between patients with RA and healthy controls.

Our results revealed deficient levels of salivary anti-CCP, without a significant difference between the RA and control groups, consistent with a previous study (Svärd et al., 2013). Moreover, the presence of salivary IgA did not differ significantly between patients with and without anti-CCP IgA in the serum, supporting the notion that salivary IgA is primarily derived from salivary gland plasma cells and does not reflect the systemic compartment (Svärd,2014). Hence, any potential leakage from the circulation appears to be of minor importance.

However, Kidhir and Al-Jubouri (2013) reported that the mean concentration of salivary anti-CCP was significantly higher in the RA group than in the healthy group. Additionally, a positive correlation exists between serum and salivary anti-CCP concentrations in patients with chronic RA (Khidhir and Al-Jubouri, 2013). One plausible reason for these negative results could be the use of the Elabscience® ELISA kit, which is designed for research purposes only. This ELISA kit employed the Sandwich-ELISA method, whereas other studies used modified anti-CCP2 kits and polyclonal rabbit IgG anti-human α-chain antibodies conjugated with HRP as the secondary antibody (Svärd et al., 2013, Svärd, 2014, Svärd et al., 2020).

Lastly, it is important to note that this study has some limitations. The small sample size, short study period, and the inclusion of patients with established RA for over 12 months may have impacted the findings. Future large-scale studies involving both recent and chronic patients with RA could provide more insights into the presence and/or absence of anti-CCP antibodies in relation to disease duration and severity, potentially yielding different results.

Conclusions

RA negatively affects oral health, predominantly causing decreased salivary flow rate, which in turn causes oral discomfort. A significant proportion of patients with RA exhibit signs and symptoms of TMJ involvement. Therefore, it is essential for oral healthcare professionals to recognise and diagnose the oral manifestations associated with RA.
