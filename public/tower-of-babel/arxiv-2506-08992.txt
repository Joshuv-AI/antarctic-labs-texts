Optimal hedging of an informed broker facing many traders
Philippe Bergault, Pierre Cardaliaguet, Wenbin Yan

Abstract
This paper investigates the optimal hedging strategies of an informed broker interacting with multiple traders in a financial market. We develop a theoretical framework in which the broker, possessing exclusive information about the drift of the asset’s price, engages with traders whose trading activities impact the market price. Using a mean-field game approach, we derive the equilibrium strategies for both the broker and the traders, illustrating the intricate dynamics of their interactions. The broker’s optimal strategy involves a Stackelberg equilibrium, where the broker leads and the traders follow. Our analysis also addresses the mean field limit of finite-player models and shows the convergence to the mean-field solution as the number of traders becomes large. Keywords: market making, algorithmic trading, externalization, mean field games, Stackelberg equilibrium

Introduction

Liquidity provision is a cornerstone of financial markets, with a significant portion of this activity occurring in over-the-counter (OTC) markets where brokers play a pivotal role in managing order flow. A key challenge for brokers arises when they possess private information about the price process, specifically the drift, and must interact with a large number of uninformed traders. This creates a complex environment where the broker seeks to exploit his informational advantage while managing the risks associated with inventory and market impact. The core of the problem lies in the broker’s ability to externalize risk in the market or internalize it to optimize his position without fully revealing his informational edge to the uninformed traders.

The study of externalization-internalization strategies has gained increasing attention in financial markets research. Externalization refers to the act of hedging or offloading a position in the market, while internalization involves holding the risk internally in the hope of favorable price movements or the arrival of offsetting trades. Several recent studies have focused on brokers’ strategies for balancing these two approaches in various markets. For instance,

In contrast to these works, our paper examines a Stackelberg game in which a broker, possessing private information about the price drift, faces a continuum of uninformed traders. The goal is to determine how the broker can strategically externalize order flow while withholding key information from the market. The choice of a Stackelberg game is motivated by several applications. First, although in theory the broker’s activity in an interbank market is not perfectly observable by traders, some participants can still detect recurring execution patterns and infer information about the broker’s strategy, particularly if the broker plays a major role in providing liquidity. Moreover,

Finally, an obvious and increasingly relevant application is that of Automated Market Makers (AMMs) in decentralized finance. Indeed,
Milionis et al. 2022

[

demonstrate that, in order to limit losses for liquidity providers, an AMM benefits from hedging through an externalization strategy on a centralized exchange (or potentially another decentralized exchange). These protocols operate under publicly predefined rules that are accessible to all (via smart contracts): the fact that the strategy is observable by all agents is an inherent characteristic of the decentralized system. An AMM deciding to adjust its externalization strategy based on private information must therefore carefully consider the information it is about to reveal.

Unlike

In particular, we establish the existence of a deterministic critical time at which the broker should fully disclose their private information, while ensuring it remains completely hidden until that moment. These results highlight the impact of private information on market dynamics, particularly with respect to liquidity provision and consumption.

While the most ideal model would involve a closed-loop formulation for all players, a detailed analysis of such a problem, incorporating the master equation and addressing a Nash equilibrium for asymmetric players with incomplete information, remains challenging and poorly understood. Advancing our understanding in this direction could be highly valuable for future research.

2
Mathematical Model and Main Results

In this section, we aim to establish the mathematical model of an informed broker interacting with multiple traders in a financial market. The discussion will focus on constructing the mathematical framework for the corresponding financial problem, which involves determining the optimal hedging strategies for an informed broker interacting with multiple uninformed traders. The discussion will not be fully rigorous at this stage. A rigorous formalization of the problem, both in the mean-field setting and in the finite case, will be presented in sections
3
and
5
, respectively.

2.1
Market Setup and Dynamics

We consider a trading horizon
\displaystyle T>0
and a probability space
\displaystyle\big(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}\big)
, under which all stochastic processes are defined (see Remark
2.1
). Let
\displaystyle N\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}
denote the number of traders in a market consisting of a single asset whose price process is denoted by
\displaystyle S
. We denote by
\displaystyle\nu_{t}^{n}
and
\displaystyle\nu_{t}^{B}
, respectively, the execution rates of trader
\displaystyle n
and the broker on the stock. The price process of the stock
\displaystyle(S_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
evolves according to the following dynamics:

\mathrm{d}S_{t}=\left(b\,\bar{\nu}_{t}+\mu\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}W_{t},

where
\displaystyle\sigma>0
is a constant,
\displaystyle\mu
is a real-valued random variable with finite second moment, and
\displaystyle\bar{\nu}_{t}:={\scriptstyle\frac{1}{N}\sum\nolimits_{n=1}^{N}}\nu_{t}^{n}
represents the average execution rate of the traders. In this setup, the traders have a linear permanent impact on the price, which is observable by all market participants.

The inventory and cash processes of trader
\displaystyle n
are denoted by
\displaystyle(Q^{n}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
and
\displaystyle(X^{n}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
, respectively. Trader
\displaystyle n
interacts with the broker at rate
\displaystyle(\nu_{t}^{n})_{t\in[0,T]}
, where
\displaystyle\nu^{n}_{t}>0
corresponds to buying and
\displaystyle\nu^{n}_{t}<0
corresponds to selling. The inventory dynamics and cash dynamics are given by:

\mathrm{d}Q^{n}_{t}=\nu^{n}_{t}\mathrm{d}t,\quad\mathrm{d}X^{n}_{t}=-\nu^{n}_{t}\big(S_{t}+\eta\nu^{n}_{t}\big)\mathrm{d}t,

where
\displaystyle\eta>0
is the transaction cost charged by the broker (identical for all traders). The initial inventories
\displaystyle(Q_{0}^{n})_{1\leq n\leq N}
, the random variable
\displaystyle\mu
, and the Brownian motion
\displaystyle W
are assumed to be mutually independent.

We denote by
\displaystyle(NQ^{B}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
and
\displaystyle(NX^{B}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
the inventory and cash processes of the broker, respectively. The scaling factor
\displaystyle N
is introduced to facilitate the mean-field limit analysis later. This adjustment is particularly useful when introducing the mean-field limit. The broker, who executes the trades of the traders, can also externalize trades in a lit market at rate
\displaystyle(N\nu^{B}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
. The broker’s inventory and cash process evolve according to the dynamics:

\mathrm{d}Q^{B}_{t}=\left(\nu^{B}_{t}-\bar{\nu}_{t}\right)\mathrm{d}t,\quad\mathrm{d}X^{B}_{t}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\nu^{n}_{t}\big(S_{t}+\eta\nu^{n}_{t}\big)\mathrm{d}t-\nu^{B}_{t}\big(S_{t}+\eta^{B}\nu^{B}_{t}\big)\mathrm{d}t,

where
\displaystyle\eta^{B}>0
is the execution cost associated with trading in the lit market.

Remark 2.1
.

Specifically, we would consider the problem on the probability space

\displaystyle\left(\Omega\times\mathbb{R},\mathcal{F}\otimes\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}),\mathbb{P}\otimes\mathbb{Q}\right)
, where
\displaystyle(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
is rich enough to support the independent random variables
\displaystyle\mu
,
\displaystyle(Q_{0}^{n})_{1\leq n\leq N}
, and
\displaystyle W
, while
the probability measure
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
could also be part of the broker’s control. This would allow the broker to enrich the value of
\displaystyle\mu
without changing the problem to be solved, especially when
\displaystyle\mu
is a discrete random variable. Thus, to simplify our notation and without loss of generality, we assume that
\displaystyle\sigma(\mu)
is already rich enough to ensure the problem is non-trivial.

2.2
Optimization Problems

Knowing the responses of the broker and other traders, the
\displaystyle n
-th trader aims to maximize the following objective function:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[X^{n}_{T}+Q^{n}_{T}S_{T}-a\,\left(Q^{n}_{T}\right)^{2}-\phi\int_{0}^{T}\left(Q^{n}_{t}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right],

over their set of admissible controls
\displaystyle(\nu^{n}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
, where
\displaystyle a,\phi>0
correspond to the risk aversion of the informed trader, and are identical for all traders.
By Ito’s formula, it is easy to see that this is equivalent to maximizing

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left\{Q^{n}_{t}\left(b\,\bar{\nu}_{t}+\mu\right)-\eta\left(\nu^{n}_{t}\right)^{2}-2\,aQ^{n}_{t}\nu^{n}_{t}-\phi\left(Q^{n}_{t}\right)^{2}\right\}\mathrm{d}t\right].

(2.1)

Knowing the responses of the traders, the broker seeks to maximize the following objective function:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[X^{B}_{T}+Q^{B}_{T}S_{T}-a^{B}\left(Q^{B}_{T}\right)^{2}-\phi^{B}\int_{0}^{T}\left(Q^{B}_{t}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right],

over his set of admissible controls
\displaystyle(\nu^{B}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
, where
\displaystyle a^{B},\phi^{B}>0
correspond to the broker’s risk aversion.
Again, by Ito’s formula, it is easy to see that this is equivalent to maximizing

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left\{Q^{B}_{t}\left(b\bar{\nu}_{t}+\mu\right)+\eta\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(\nu^{n}_{t}\right)^{2}-\eta^{B}\left(\nu^{B}_{t}\right)^{2}-2\,a^{B}Q^{B}_{t}\left(\nu^{B}_{t}-\bar{\nu}_{t}\right)-\phi^{B}\left(Q^{B}_{t}\right)^{2}\right\}\mathrm{d}t\right].

(2.2)

Remark 2.2
.

At time 0, the drift
\displaystyle\mu
is revealed only to the broker. Consequently, traders can infer the drift term solely based on the information leaked through the broker’s execution rate. Mathematically, this is expressed as
\displaystyle\mu_{t}:=\mathbb{E}[\mu|\sigma(\nu^{B}_{s}\,;\penalty\ 0\leq s\leq t)]
, which forms a martingale.

2.3
Main Results

To obtain an approximate solution for the finite game, we propose a mean field game approach. Note that the propagation of chaos suggests that, in the limit, the execution rates of individual traders become independent, conditional on
\displaystyle\nu^{B}
. In this framework, the broker no longer interacts with
\displaystyle N
individual traders but rather the distribution infinitely many traders.

Our main results can be stated informally as follows. We have identified the broker’s optimal information-leaking strategy and the corresponding optimal control in the mean field setting. Furthermore, we have proven that this strategy remains approximately optimal in the finite case, as detailed in Theorem
4.3
, Remark
4.2
, and Theorem
5.1
. Here we give the informal version of them.

Result 1
.

Assume that the initial inventories of the traders are i.i.d. random variables with finite fourth moments, and that the linear permanent impact of the traders’ execution rates on the stock price
\displaystyle b
is sufficiently small.

In the case of infinitely many traders, there exists a critical time
\displaystyle t_{c}\in[0,T]
such that the broker’s optimal information-leaking strategy is that no information about the drift term
\displaystyle\mu
is revealed by the broker before the critical time, and all information is disclosed at the critical time. The broker’s optimal control is given by an explicit formula, piecewise analytic on
\displaystyle[0,t_{c}]
and
\displaystyle(t_{c},T]
.

Moreover, in the case of finitely many traders, there exists
\displaystyle N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
\displaystyle N\geq N_{0}
, the optimal control in the infinite case is a
\displaystyle C/\sqrt{N}
-optimal one for the broker, where
\displaystyle C
is a constant independent of
\displaystyle N
.

3
Mean Field Game and Stackelberg Problem

In this section, we formalize the Stackelberg problem between the broker and the representative trader in the mean field setting. The original game, which involves one broker and many traders, simplifies to a game between the broker and a single representative trader, thereby significantly reducing the problem’s complexity. We will present the available information for both the representative trader and the broker, define their respective optimization problems, and outline the game they participate in, to properly introduce the corresponding Stackelberg framework.

3.1
MFG Equilibrium of the Traders

In this section, we suppose that the execution rate of the broker,
\displaystyle(\nu^{B}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
, is fixed. We first consider the admissible strategies for the representative trader, which are defined as follows:

\mathcal{A}^{r}=\left\{\nu=(\nu_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\nu_{t}\text{ is }\mathcal{F}^{r}_{t}\text{-progressively measurable, and }\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\nu_{t}^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]<+\infty\right.\right\},

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{r}
denotes the filtration generated by the initial condition of the representative trader’s inventory process
\displaystyle Q_{0}
and the broker’s execution rate
\displaystyle(\nu^{B}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
.

To introduce the mean field game of traders, given the broker’s execution rate
\displaystyle\nu^{B}
, for
\displaystyle\nu\in\mathcal{A}^{r}
, we consider the associated inventory process
\displaystyle(Q_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
of the representative trader as

Q_{t}=Q_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\nu_{u}\,\mathrm{d}u,

and the mean field execution rate
\displaystyle(\bar{\nu}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
of the traders as

\bar{\nu}_{t}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\,m_{t}(\mathrm{d}x),

where the process
\displaystyle(m_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
takes values in
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, representing the distribution of the other traders’ execution rates at time
\displaystyle t
, conditional on
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{t}:=\sigma(\{\nu^{B}_{s};0\leq s\leq t\})
.

Hence, given the broker’s execution rate
\displaystyle\nu^{B}
, the representative trader should try to maximize the objective function as follow, which is the mean field version of (
2.1
),

\displaystyle\displaystyle H[\nu^{B}](\nu)=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left\{Q_{t}\left(b\,\bar{\nu}_{t}+\mu\right)-\eta\left(\nu_{t}\right)^{2}-2\,aQ_{t}\nu_{t}-\phi\left(Q_{t}\right)^{2}\right\}\mathrm{d}t\right],

(3.1)

with
\displaystyle b>0
representing the market impact of the traders,
\displaystyle\eta
representing the transaction costs charged by the broker to the traders, and
\displaystyle a,\phi>0
corresponding to the risk aversion of the informed traders.

Finally, we can give the following definition,

Definition 3.1
.

Given an admissible control
\displaystyle\nu^{B}
of the broker, an MFG equilibrium associated with
\displaystyle\nu^{B}
is a pair
\displaystyle(\nu^{\nu^{B}},m^{\nu^{B}})
of processes where
\displaystyle\nu^{\nu^{B}}\in\mathcal{A}^{r}
and
\displaystyle m^{\nu^{B}}
is an
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{r}_{t}
-progressively measurable random process taking values in
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, and

\displaystyle(i)

\displaystyle H[\nu^{B}](\nu^{\nu^{B}})=\underset{\nu\in\mathcal{A}^{r}}{\sup}H[\nu^{B}](\nu);

\displaystyle(ii)

\displaystyle m^{\nu^{B}}_{t}
is the distribution of
\displaystyle\nu^{\nu^{B}}_{t}
conditional on
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma\left(\{\nu^{B}_{s};s\leq t\}\right)
for Lebesgue-almost every
\displaystyle t\in[0,T]
.

3.2
Stackelberg Equilibrium of the Informed Broker

The set of admissible strategies for the informed broker is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{B}=\left\{\nu^{B}=(\nu^{B}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}\ \middle|\ \begin{array}[]{l}\nu^{B}_{t}\text{ is independent of }Q_{0}\text{ and }\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}(\nu^{B}_{t})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right]<+\infty.\end{array}\right\}.

and we write
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{B}
for the filtration generated by the
\displaystyle\nu^{B}
, chosen by the broker in the mean field game. Note that if
\displaystyle\mu
is a constant, the problem is trivial and not considered here. If
\displaystyle\mu
takes only discrete values, as in
Bergault et al. 2024

[
10
]
, the broker would then be allowed to enlarge the probability space as we mentioned in Remark
2.1
. Although the independence of the
\displaystyle\nu^{B}
with
\displaystyle Q_{0}
may seem strange at the beginning, it is saying nothing but that the broker is playing an open-loop game, since he knows nothing about the conditions of the traders throughout the time.

To introduce the Stackelberg game of the broker, given the representative trader’s execution rate
\displaystyle\nu
, for
\displaystyle\nu^{B}\in\mathcal{A}^{B}
, we define the associated inventory process of the informed broker as

Q^{B}_{t}=Q^{B}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\nu^{B}_{u}-\bar{\nu}_{u}\right)\mathrm{d}u.

Hence, given the distribution
\displaystyle(m_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
of the traders, taking the mean field version of problem (
2.2
), the broker would try to optimize the following objective function,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} H^{B}[m](\nu^{B})=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left\{Q^{B}_{t}\left(b\,\bar{\nu}_{t}+\mu\right)+\eta\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{2}m_{t}(\mathrm{d}x)-\eta^{B}\left(\nu^{B}_{t}\right)^{2}-2\,a^{B}Q^{B}_{t}\left(\nu^{B}_{t}-\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\,m_{t}(\mathrm{d}x)\right)-\phi^{B}\left(Q^{B}_{t}\right)^{2}\right\}\mathrm{d}t\right].\end{aligned}

(3.2)

with
\displaystyle\eta^{B}>0
representing the transaction costs in the lit market, and
\displaystyle a^{B},\phi^{B}>0
the risk aversion parameters for the broker.

Finally, we can give the following definition,

Definition 3.2
.

The problem of the broker consists of solving

\underset{\nu^{B}\in\mathcal{A}^{B}}{\sup}\quad\underset{(\nu^{\nu^{B}},m^{\nu^{B}})}{\inf}H^{B}[m^{\nu^{B}}](\nu^{B}),

where the infimum is taken over all MFG equilibria
\displaystyle(\nu^{\nu^{B}},m^{\nu^{B}})
associated with
\displaystyle\nu^{B}
.

We will find in the next section that in such an MFG the equilibrium is in fact unique, so that no infimum is actually required in the definition.

4
Optimal Information Leaking Strategy

In this part, we aim to determine the optimal strategies for both the representative trader and the broker. We achieve this by solving the backward stochastic Hamilton-Jacobi equations associated with the optimization problems in Definition
3.1
and Definition

, which are both linear-quadratic optimal stochastic control problems with random coefficients.

To this end, we will introduce a specific form of the Hamilton-Jacobi equation and find its unique solution in Subsection
4.1
: this part is largely inspired by
Bismut 1976

[
14
]
.
With this result in mind, and by applying the maximum principle, we will derive the unique optimal control for the representative trader in problem (
3.1
) (Lemma
4.1
), given the broker’s execution rate process
\displaystyle\nu^{B}
, in Subsection
4.2
.
Subsequently, we will identify the optimal information-leaking strategy and the broker’s associated unique optimal control (Theorem

) in Subsection

.

4.1
Explicit Computation for a Solution to a Backward HJ Equation

We investigate the solution to an HJ equation of the form

\begin{array}[]{l}du_{t}=H_{t}(x,Du_{t})\,dt+dM_{t}(x)\qquad\text{in }(0,T)\times\mathbb{R},\\
u_{T}(x)=g_{T}(x)\qquad\text{in }\mathbb{R},\end{array}

(4.1)

where

H_{t}(x,\xi)=A_{t}+B_{t}x+\frac{C_{t}x^{2}}{2}+D_{t}\xi+E_{t}x\xi+\frac{F_{t}\xi^{2}}{2},

(4.2)

and

g_{T}(x)=G_{1}+G_{2}x+\frac{G_{3}x^{2}}{2},

with
\displaystyle(A_{t},B_{t},C_{t},D_{t},E_{t},F_{t})
adapted to a filtration
\displaystyle(\mathcal{F}_{t})
(satisfying the usual assumptions) and bounded, and
\displaystyle(G_{1},G_{2},G_{3})
being
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{T}
-measurable and bounded. According to previous research (see
Bismut 1976

[
14
]
), we know that there exists a unique solution for such an equation. We look for
\displaystyle(u,M)
in the form

u_{t}(x)=\alpha_{t}+\beta_{t}x+\frac{\gamma_{t}x^{2}}{2},

and

M_{t}(x)=Z_{\alpha,t}+Z_{\beta,t}x+\frac{Z_{\gamma,t}x^{2}}{2},

where
\displaystyle(\alpha_{t},\beta_{t},\gamma_{t})
and
\displaystyle(Z_{\alpha},Z_{\beta},Z_{\gamma})
are adapted to
\displaystyle(\mathcal{F}_{t})
, and
\displaystyle(Z_{\alpha},Z_{\beta},Z_{\gamma})
form an
\displaystyle(\mathcal{F}_{t})
-martingale. In view of the equation,
\displaystyle(\alpha_{t},\beta_{t},\gamma_{t})
and
\displaystyle(Z_{\alpha},Z_{\beta},Z_{\gamma})
must satisfy:

\displaystyle\displaystyle d\alpha_{t}+xd\beta_{t}+\frac{x^{2}}{2}d\gamma_{t}=\left(A_{t}+B_{t}x+\frac{C_{t}x^{2}}{2}+D_{t}(\beta_{t}+\gamma_{t}x)+E_{t}x(\beta_{t}+\gamma_{t}x)+\frac{F_{t}(\beta_{t}+\gamma_{t}x)^{2}}{2}\right)dt

\displaystyle\displaystyle+dZ_{\alpha,t}+xdZ_{\beta,t}+\frac{x^{2}}{2}dZ_{\gamma,t}.

This yields the system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(i)&d\gamma_{t}=\left(C_{t}+2E_{t}\gamma_{t}+F_{t}\gamma_{t}^{2}\right)dt+dZ_{\gamma,t},\qquad\gamma_{T}=G_{3},\\
(ii)&d\beta_{t}=\left(B_{t}+D_{t}\gamma_{t}+E_{t}\beta_{t}+F_{t}\beta_{t}\gamma_{t}\right)dt+dZ_{\beta,t},\qquad\beta_{T}=G_{2},\\
(iii)&d\alpha_{t}=\left(A_{t}+D_{t}\beta_{t}+\frac{F_{t}\beta_{t}^{2}}{2}\right)dt+dZ_{\alpha,t},\qquad\alpha_{T}=G_{1}.\end{array}\right.

We assume that
\displaystyle C_{t}
,
\displaystyle E_{t}
,
\displaystyle F_{t}
, and
\displaystyle G_{3}
are deterministic and such that the Riccati equation

\frac{d}{dt}\gamma_{t}=C_{t}+2E_{t}\gamma_{t}+F_{t}\gamma_{t}^{2},\qquad\gamma_{T}=G_{3}

has a unique solution. Then
\displaystyle Z_{\gamma}=0
. For
\displaystyle\beta_{t}
we find

\beta_{t}=e^{-\int_{t}^{T}(E_{s}+F_{s}\gamma_{s})ds}G_{2}-\int_{t}^{T}e^{-\int_{t}^{s}(E_{r}+F_{r}\gamma_{r})dr}(B_{s}+D_{s}\gamma_{s})ds-\int_{t}^{T}e^{-\int_{t}^{s}(E_{r}+F_{r}\gamma_{r})dr}dZ_{\beta,s}.

Taking the conditional expectation with respect to
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{t}
, we obtain

\beta_{t}=e^{-\int_{t}^{T}(E_{s}+F_{s}\gamma_{s})ds}\mathbb{E}[G_{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]-\int_{t}^{T}e^{-\int_{t}^{s}(E_{r}+F_{r}\gamma_{r})dr}\left(\mathbb{E}[B_{s}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]+\mathbb{E}[D_{s}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]\gamma_{s}\right)ds.

4.2
Optimal Control of the Representative Trader

Throughout this subsection, we suppose that the filtration
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{t}
, representing the information disclosed by the informed broker, is fixed, and we discuss the optimal control of the representative trader in this case, that is, to consider,

\max_{\nu_{t}}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left\{Q_{t}\left(b\,\bar{\nu}_{t}+\mu_{t}\right)-\eta\left(\nu_{t}\right)^{2}-2\,aQ_{t}\nu_{t}-\phi\left(Q_{t}\right)^{2}\right\}\mathrm{d}t\right],

where

dQ_{t}=\nu_{t}\,dt,

and where
\displaystyle\mu_{t}=\mathbb{E}[\mu\ |\mathcal{F}^{r}_{t}]=\mathbb{E}[\mu\ |\mathcal{F}_{t}]
, and
\displaystyle(\nu_{t})
is
\displaystyle(\mathcal{F}^{r}_{t})
-progressively measurable. In this case, the value function
\displaystyle u
of the representative trader satisfies an HJ equation of the form (
4.1
), with

\displaystyle\displaystyle H_{t}(q,\xi)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\min_{\nu\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{-\nu\xi-q\left(b\,\bar{\nu}_{t}+\mu_{t}\right)+\eta\nu^{2}+2\,aq\nu+\phi q^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=-q\left(b\,\bar{\nu}_{t}+\mu_{t}\right)+\phi q^{2}-\frac{1}{4\eta}|\xi-2aq|^{2},

where the minimum is reached for
\displaystyle\tilde{\nu}
, given by

\tilde{\nu}=\tilde{\nu}(q,\xi)=\frac{1}{2\eta}(\xi-2aq).

With the notation of the previous subsection, we have

A_{t}=0,\;B_{t}=-\left(b\,\bar{\nu}_{t}+\mu_{t}\right),\;C_{t}=\phi-\frac{a^{2}}{\eta},\;D_{t}=0,\;E_{t}=\frac{a}{\eta},\;F_{t}=-\frac{1}{2\eta},\qquad g_{T}\equiv 0.

Note that, aside from
\displaystyle B_{t}
, all the coefficients are deterministic. This leads to the system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(i)&d\gamma_{t}=\left(\phi-\frac{a^{2}}{\eta}+2\frac{a}{\eta}\gamma_{t}-\frac{1}{2\eta}\gamma_{t}^{2}\right)dt,\qquad\gamma_{T}=0,\\[4.30554pt]
(ii)&d\beta_{t}=\left(-\left(b\,\bar{\nu}_{t}+\mu_{t}\right)+\frac{2a-\gamma_{t}}{2\eta}\beta_{t}\gamma_{t}\right)dt+dZ_{\beta,t},\qquad\beta_{T}=0,\\[4.30554pt]
(iii)&d\alpha_{t}=\left(-\frac{1}{4\eta}\beta_{t}^{2}\right)dt+dZ_{\alpha,t},\qquad\alpha_{T}=0.\end{array}\right.

(4.3)

The first equation has a (deterministic) analytic solution defined on
\displaystyle[0,T]
, as guaranteed by classical results on ordinary differential equations. On the other hand,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\beta_{t}=-\int_{t}^{T}\Gamma_{s,t}\mathbb{E}\left[-\left(b\,\bar{\nu}_{s}+\mu_{s}\right)\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]ds=b\int_{t}^{T}\Gamma_{s,t}\mathbb{E}[\bar{\nu}_{s}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]ds+\mu_{t}\int_{t}^{T}\Gamma_{s,t}ds,

where

\Gamma_{s,t}=\exp\left(\int_{s}^{t}\frac{\gamma_{r}-2a}{2\eta}\,dr\right).

Hence, the optimal dynamics for the representative trader are

d\hat{Q}_{t}=\hat{\nu}_{t}dt=\frac{1}{2\eta}\left((\gamma_{t}-2a)\hat{Q}_{t}+\beta_{t}\right)dt,\qquad\hat{Q}_{0}=Q_{0},

(4.4)

where
\displaystyle Q_{0}
(the initial distribution of the traders’ inventories) is a random variable independent of all the others. Thus,

\hat{Q}_{t}=\Gamma_{0,t}\cdot Q_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{2\eta}\Gamma_{s,t}\beta_{s}ds.

(4.5)

Moreover, we know that at the equilibrium,
\displaystyle\bar{\nu}
satisfies
\displaystyle\bar{\nu}_{t}=\mathbb{E}[\hat{\nu}_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]
for all
\displaystyle t\in[0,T]
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bar{\nu}_{t}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2\eta}\left((\gamma_{t}-2a)\hat{Q}_{t}+\beta_{t}\right)\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{t}-2a}{2\eta}\Gamma_{0,t}\cdot\bar{Q}_{0}+b\frac{\gamma_{t}-2a}{4\eta^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\Gamma_{s,t}\int_{s}^{T}\Gamma_{r,s}\mathbb{E}[\bar{\nu}_{r}|\mathcal{F}_{s}]dr\,ds

\displaystyle\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\gamma_{t}-2a}{4\eta^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\Gamma_{s,t}\mu_{s}\int_{s}^{T}\Gamma_{r,s}dr\,ds

\displaystyle\displaystyle\quad+b\int_{t}^{T}\frac{1}{2\eta}\Gamma_{s,t}\mathbb{E}[\bar{\nu}_{s}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]ds+\mu_{t}\int_{t}^{T}\frac{1}{2\eta}\Gamma_{s,t}ds.

On the normed space
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}
of
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{t}
-progressively measurable processes
\displaystyle\xi
with finite norm

\|\xi\|_{\mathcal{D}}:=\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|\xi_{t}|^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}},

we define the bounded linear operators
\displaystyle L,\tilde{L}:\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{D}
such that for any
\displaystyle\xi\in\mathcal{D}
and
\displaystyle t\in[0,T]
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle L\xi_{t}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{s}^{T}A_{r,s,t}\mathbb{E}[\xi_{r}|\mathcal{F}_{s}]drds+\int_{t}^{T}B_{s,t}\mathbb{E}[\xi_{s}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]ds,

(4.6)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tilde{L}\xi_{t}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\tilde{L}^{C,D}\xi_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}C_{s,t}\xi_{s}ds+\xi_{t}D_{t},

(4.7)

where

A_{r,s,t}=\frac{\gamma_{t}-2a}{4\eta^{2}}\Gamma_{r,s}\Gamma_{s,t},\penalty\ B_{s,t}=\frac{1}{2\eta}\Gamma_{s,t},\penalty\ C_{s,t}=\frac{\gamma_{t}-2a}{4\eta^{2}}\Gamma_{s,t}\int_{s}^{T}\Gamma_{r,s}dr,\penalty\ D_{t}=\frac{1}{2\eta}\int_{t}^{T}\Gamma_{s,t}ds.

Assuming that
\displaystyle b
is small, i.e.,
\displaystyle 0<b<\|L\|_{B(\mathcal{D})}^{-1}
, the fixed point
\displaystyle\bar{\nu}
exists and is unique in
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}
, and can be written as

\bar{\nu}_{t}=(I-bL)^{-1}(z\cdot\bar{Q}_{0}+\tilde{L}\mu_{\cdot}),

(4.8)

where
\displaystyle z\in C^{\omega}
is defined by

z_{t}=\frac{\gamma_{t}-2a}{2\eta}\Gamma_{0,t},\qquad t\in[0,T].

Given a pair of continuous maps
\displaystyle(E,F)=(E_{s,t},F_{t})
, we note that

\displaystyle\displaystyle L\circ\tilde{L}^{E,F}\mu_{t}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{s}^{T}A_{r,s,t}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{r}E_{u,r}\mu_{u}du+\mu_{r}F_{r}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]drds+\int_{t}^{T}B_{s,t}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{s}E_{u,s}\mu_{u}du+\mu_{s}F_{s}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]ds

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{s}^{T}A_{r,s,t}\left(\int_{0}^{r}E_{u,r}\mu_{u}du+\mu_{s}\left(\int_{s}^{r}E_{u,r}du+F_{r}\right)\right)drds

\displaystyle\displaystyle\quad+\int_{t}^{T}B_{s,t}\left(\int_{0}^{t}E_{u,s}\mu_{u}du+\mu_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{s}E_{u,s}du+F_{s}\right)\right)ds

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\mu_{u}\left(\int_{u}^{t}\int_{s}^{T}A_{r,s,t}E_{u,r}drds\right)du+\int_{0}^{t}\mu_{s}\left(\int_{s}^{T}\int_{s}^{r}A_{r,s,t}\left(E_{u,r}du+F_{r}\right)dudr\right)ds

\displaystyle\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\mu_{u}\left(\int_{t}^{T}B_{s,t}E_{u,s}ds\right)du+\mu_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}B_{s,t}\left(\int_{t}^{s}E_{u,s}du+F_{s}\right)ds\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\tilde{L}^{\tilde{E},\tilde{F}}\mu_{t},

with

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\tilde{E}_{s,t}&=\int_{s}^{t}\int_{u}^{T}A_{r,u,t}E_{s,r}drdu+\int_{s}^{T}A_{r,s,t}\left(\int_{s}^{r}E_{u,r}du+F_{r}\right)dr+\int_{t}^{T}B_{u,t}E_{s,u}du,\\
\tilde{F}_{t}&=\int_{t}^{T}B_{s,t}\left(\int_{t}^{s}E_{u,s}du+F_{s}\right)ds.\end{array}\right.

(4.9)

Let
\displaystyle\hat{L}:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}
be the operator such that
\displaystyle(\tilde{E},\tilde{F})=\hat{L}[E,F]=(\hat{L}_{1}[E,F],\hat{L}_{2}[E,F])
, where
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
is a normed space of function pairs
\displaystyle(E,F)
in
\displaystyle C^{0}([0,T]^{2})\times C^{0}([0,T])
with finite norm
\displaystyle\|E\|_{C^{0}}\vee\|F\|_{C^{0}}
. Restricting
\displaystyle b
further if necessary, i.e.,
\displaystyle 0<b<\min\{\|L\|_{B(\mathcal{D})}^{-1},\|\hat{L}\|_{B(\mathcal{C})}^{-1}\}
, we find that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\beta_{s}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=b\int_{s}^{T}\Gamma_{r,s}\mathbb{E}[\bar{\nu}_{r}|\mathcal{F}_{s}]dr+\mu_{s}\int_{s}^{T}\Gamma_{r,s}dr

\displaystyle\displaystyle=b\int_{s}^{T}\Gamma_{r,s}(I-bL)^{-1}z_{r}dr\cdot\bar{Q}_{0}+\mu_{s}\int_{s}^{T}\Gamma_{r,s}dr+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b^{k+1}\int_{s}^{T}\Gamma_{r,s}\mathbb{E}\left[L^{k}\circ\tilde{L}\mu_{r}|\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]dr

\displaystyle\displaystyle=b\int_{s}^{T}\Gamma_{r,s}(I-bL)^{-1}z_{r}dr\cdot\bar{Q}_{0}+\mu_{s}\int_{s}^{T}\Gamma_{r,s}dr+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b^{k+1}\int_{s}^{T}\Gamma_{r,s}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{r}\hat{L}_{1}^{k}[C,D]_{u,r}\mu_{u}du+\hat{L}_{2}^{k}[C,D]_{r}\mu_{r}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]dr

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\beta_{s}^{1}\cdot\bar{Q}_{0}+\int_{0}^{s}\beta^{2}_{u}\mu_{u}du+\beta_{s}^{3}\mu_{s},

where

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\beta_{s}^{1}&=b\int_{s}^{T}\Gamma_{r,s}(I-bL)^{-1}z_{r}dr,\\
\beta_{s}^{2}&=b\int_{s}^{T}\Gamma_{r,s}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b^{k}\hat{L}_{1}^{k}[C,D]_{u,r}dr,\\
\beta_{s}^{3}&=\int_{s}^{T}\Gamma_{r,s}\left[1+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b^{k+1}\left(\int_{s}^{r}\hat{L}_{1}^{k}[C,D]_{u,r}du+\hat{L}_{2}^{k}[C,D]_{r}\right)\right]dr.\end{array}\right.

(4.10)

Hence, by combining the equation above with equations (
4.5
) and (
4.8
), we have the expression for
\displaystyle\hat{\nu}
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{\nu}_{t}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{t}-2a}{2\eta}\left(\Gamma_{0,t}\cdot Q_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{2\eta}\Gamma_{s,t}\beta_{s}ds\right)+\frac{1}{2\eta}\beta_{t}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{t}-2a}{2\eta}\left(\Gamma_{0,t}\cdot Q_{0}+\bar{Q}_{0}\cdot\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{2\eta}\Gamma_{s,t}\beta_{s}^{1}ds+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{2\eta}\Gamma_{s,t}\int_{0}^{s}\beta_{u}^{2}\mu_{u}duds+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{2\eta}\Gamma_{s,t}\beta_{s}^{3}\mu_{s}ds\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt+\frac{1}{2\eta}\left(\bar{Q}_{0}\beta^{1}_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}\beta_{s}^{2}\mu_{s}ds+\beta_{t}^{3}\mu_{t}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\bar{A}_{t}\bar{Q}_{0}+\bar{B}_{t}Q_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\bar{C}_{s,t}\mu_{s}ds+\bar{D}_{t}\mu_{t},

where

\left\{\begin{aligned} \bar{A}_{t}&=\frac{\beta_{t}^{1}}{2\eta}+\frac{\gamma_{t}-2a}{4\eta^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\Gamma_{s,t}\beta_{s}^{1}ds,\\
\bar{B}_{t}&=\frac{\gamma_{t}-2a}{2\eta}\Gamma_{0,t},\\
\bar{C}_{s,t}&=\frac{\gamma_{t}-2a}{4\eta^{2}}\left(\beta_{s}^{2}\int_{s}^{t}\Gamma_{r,t}dr+\Gamma_{s,t}\beta_{s}^{3}\right)+\frac{\beta_{s}^{2}}{2\eta},\\
\bar{D}_{t}&=\frac{\beta_{t}^{3}}{2\eta}.\end{aligned}\right.

(4.11)

Lemma 4.1
.

Assuming
\displaystyle b>0
is small enough, there exists
\displaystyle(\bar{A},\bar{B},\bar{C},\bar{D})\in C^{\omega}([0,T]\times[0,T],\mathbb{R}^{4})
, as defined in (
4.11
), such that for any martingale
\displaystyle(\mu_{t})
, the mean field control
\displaystyle\hat{\nu}=\hat{\nu}(Q_{0},\mu_{\cdot})
is given by

\hat{\nu}_{t}=\hat{\nu}(\mu_{\cdot})_{t}=\bar{A}_{t}\bar{Q}_{0}+\bar{B}_{t}Q_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\bar{C}_{s,t}\mu_{s}ds+\mu_{t}\bar{D}_{t},\qquad t\in[0,T].

(4.12)

Hence, we also have the conditional expectation of the first and second moments of
\displaystyle\hat{\nu}
with respect to
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{t}
:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \bar{\nu}_{t}&=\mathbb{E}[\hat{\nu}_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]=(\bar{A}_{t}+\bar{B}_{t})\bar{Q}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\bar{C}_{s,t}\mu_{s}ds+\mu_{t}\bar{D}_{t},\\
\bar{M}_{t}&:=\mathbb{E}[\hat{\nu}_{t}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]=\left(\bar{A}_{t}^{2}+2\bar{A}_{t}\bar{B}_{t}\right)\cdot\bar{Q}_{0}^{2}+\bar{B}_{t}^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}[Q_{0}^{2}]\\
&\qquad\qquad+2\left(\bar{A}_{t}+\bar{B}_{t}\right)\cdot\left(\int_{0}^{t}\bar{C}_{s,t}\mu_{s}ds+\mu_{t}\bar{D}_{t}\right)\cdot\bar{Q}_{0}+\left(\int_{0}^{t}\bar{C}_{s,t}\mu_{s}ds+\mu_{t}\bar{D}_{t}\right)^{2},\end{aligned}\right.\qquad t\in[0,T].

(4.13)

Remark 4.2
.

By "
\displaystyle\,b>0
small enough", we mean precisely that,

0<b<\exp\left(-2T\frac{a\vee\sqrt{\eta\phi}}{\eta}\right)\cdot\frac{\eta^{2}\,a^{-1}\wedge\eta^{\frac{3}{2}}\,\phi^{-\frac{1}{2}}\wedge\eta}{T^{2}+T}.

(4.14)

Proof.

Recall the definition of
\displaystyle L
in formula (
4.6
).
Note that by the explicit formula of
\displaystyle\gamma
, we have

\|A\|_{C^{0}([0,T]^{3})}\leq\frac{a\vee\sqrt{\eta\phi}}{2\eta^{2}}\exp\left(2T\frac{a\vee\sqrt{\eta\phi}}{\eta}\right),\qquad\|B\|_{C^{0}([0,T]^{2})}\leq\frac{1}{2\eta}\exp\left(2T\frac{a\vee\sqrt{\eta\phi}}{\eta}\right).

Hence, by Doob’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\|L\xi\|_{\mathcal{D}}^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left|L\xi_{t}\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{s}^{T}A_{r,s,t}\mathbb{E}\left[\xi_{r}|\mathcal{F}_{r}\right]drds\right|^{2}\right]+2\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left|\int_{t}^{T}B_{s,t}\mathbb{E}\left[\xi_{s}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]ds\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq 2\|A\|_{C^{0}}^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{s}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\xi_{r}\right|\Big|\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]drds\right)^{2}\right]+2\|B\|_{C^{0}}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\xi_{s}\right|\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]ds\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq 2\|A\|_{C^{0}([0,T]^{3})}^{2}\cdot\frac{T^{4}}{4}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq s\leq T}\left|\xi_{s}\right|\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]^{2}\right]+2\|B\|_{C^{0}}\cdot T^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq s\leq T}\left|\xi_{s}\right|\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{a^{2}\vee(\eta\phi)}{4\eta^{4}}\cdot T^{4}+\frac{1}{\eta^{2}}\cdot T^{2}\right)\cdot\exp\left(4T\frac{a\vee\sqrt{\eta\phi}}{\eta}\right)\cdot\|\xi\|_{\mathcal{D}}^{2}.

Thus, giving the best bound for the operator
\displaystyle L
:

\|L\|_{B(\mathcal{D})}\leq\exp\left(2T\frac{a\vee\sqrt{\eta\phi}}{\eta}\right)\cdot\sqrt{\frac{a^{2}\vee(\eta\phi)}{4\eta^{4}}\cdot T^{4}+\frac{1}{\eta^{2}}\cdot T^{2}}.

(4.15)

Similarly, recall the definition of
\displaystyle\hat{L}
in formula (
4.9
). We have

\sup_{0\leq s,t\leq T}\left|\hat{L}_{1}\left[E,F\right]_{s,t}\right|\leq\exp\left(2T\frac{a\vee\sqrt{\eta\phi}}{\eta}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{a\vee\sqrt{\eta\phi}}{2\eta^{2}}\cdot T^{2}\cdot\|E\|_{C^{0}}+\frac{a\vee\sqrt{\eta\phi}}{2\eta^{2}}\cdot T\cdot\|F\|_{C^{0}}+\frac{1}{2\eta}\cdot T\cdot\|E\|_{C^{0}}\right),

and

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left|\hat{L}_{2}\left[E,F\right]_{t}\right|\leq\exp\left(2T\frac{a\vee\sqrt{\eta\phi}}{\eta}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{1}{4\eta}\cdot T^{2}\cdot\|E\|_{C^{0}}+\frac{1}{2\eta}\cdot T\cdot\|F\|_{C^{0}}\right).

Thus, giving the best bound for the operator
\displaystyle L
:

\|\hat{L}\|_{B(\mathcal{C})}\leq\exp\left(2T\frac{a\vee\sqrt{\eta\phi}}{\eta}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{1}{4\eta}T^{2}+\frac{1}{2\eta}T\right)\vee\left(\frac{a\vee\sqrt{\eta\phi}}{2\eta^{2}}(T^{2}+T)+\frac{1}{2\eta}T\right).

(4.16)

In total, we can derive the best bound for
\displaystyle b
,

0<b<\exp\left(-2T\frac{a\vee\sqrt{\eta\phi}}{\eta}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{a^{2}\vee(\eta\phi)}{4\eta^{4}}\cdot T^{4}+\frac{1}{\eta^{2}}\cdot T^{2}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\wedge\left(\frac{1}{4\eta}T^{2}+\frac{1}{2\eta}T\right)^{-1}\wedge\left(\frac{a\vee\sqrt{\eta\phi}}{2\eta^{2}}(T^{2}+T)+\frac{1}{2\eta}T\right)^{-1}

which, by taking a convenient version of it, gives (
4.14
).

4.3
Optimal Control of the Informed Broker

The problem of the broker consists in maximizing over
\displaystyle\nu^{B}
(adapted to
\displaystyle(\mathcal{F}_{t})
) and then over
\displaystyle(\mathcal{F}_{t})
(or equivalently over
\displaystyle\mu_{\cdot}
) the quantity:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left\{Q^{B}_{t}\left(b\,\bar{\nu}_{t}+\mu\right)+\eta\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{2}m_{t}(\mathrm{d}x)-\eta^{B}\left(\nu^{B}_{t}\right)^{2}-2\,a^{B}Q^{B}_{t}\left(\nu^{B}_{t}-\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\,m_{t}(\mathrm{d}x)\right)-\phi^{B}\left(Q^{B}_{t}\right)^{2}\right\}\mathrm{d}t\right]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left\{Q^{B}_{t}\left(b\,\bar{\nu}_{t}+\mu_{t}\right)+\eta\mathbb{E}[\hat{\nu}_{t}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]-\eta^{B}\left(\nu^{B}_{t}\right)^{2}-2\,a^{B}Q^{B}_{t}\left(\nu^{B}_{t}-\bar{\nu}_{t}\right)-\phi^{B}\left(Q^{B}_{t}\right)^{2}\right\}\mathrm{d}t\right],

where
\displaystyle Q_{0}^{B}
is the deterministic initial inventory of the broker, and

\mathrm{d}Q^{B}_{t}=\left(\nu^{B}_{t}-\bar{\nu}_{t}\right)\mathrm{d}t,\qquad Q^{B}_{0}=Q^{B}_{0}.

In the first step, we fix the filtration
\displaystyle(\mathcal{F}_{t})
(and thus the process
\displaystyle(\mu_{\cdot})
), and in view of Lemma
4.1
, the process
\displaystyle(\hat{\nu})
is optimized with respect to the control
\displaystyle\nu^{B}
. The value function of the broker is then given by

u^{B}(t,q)=\alpha^{B}_{t}+\beta^{B}_{t}q+\gamma^{B}_{t}q^{2}/2,

which satisfies a backward Hamilton-Jacobi equation of the form

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathrm{d}u^{B}_{t}=H^{B}_{t}(q,Du^{B}_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}M^{B}_{t}(q)\qquad\text{in }(0,T)\times\mathbb{R},\\
u^{B}_{T}(q)=0\qquad\text{in }\mathbb{R},\end{array}

where

\displaystyle\displaystyle H^{B}_{t}(q,\xi)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\min_{\nu^{B}\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{-(\nu^{B}-\bar{\nu}_{t})\xi-\left(q\left(b\,\bar{\nu}_{t}+\mu_{t}\right)+\eta\mathbb{E}[\hat{\nu}_{t}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]-\eta^{B}\left(\nu^{B}\right)^{2}-2\,a^{B}q\left(\nu^{B}-\bar{\nu}_{t}\right)-\phi^{B}q^{2}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=-q\left(b\,\bar{\nu}_{t}+\mu_{t}\right)-\eta\mathbb{E}[\hat{\nu}_{t}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]-2\,a^{B}q\bar{\nu}_{t}+\phi^{B}q^{2}-\frac{|\xi-2\,a^{B}q|^{2}}{4\eta^{B}}+\bar{\nu}_{t}\xi,

7
Conclusion

We have studied a Stackelberg game in which an informed broker interacts with a continuum of uninformed traders, aiming to externalize inventory while concealing private information about the price drift. The model combines an open-loop control for the broker and a closed-loop mean field game for the traders, yielding explicit characterizations of optimal strategies. Our results show that the broker should initially behave as if uninformed and strategically delay the revelation of information. This delayed disclosure allows the broker to benefit from his informational advantage without inducing adverse reactions from the market too early.

The numerical analysis illustrates how traders adjust their inventories in response to the broker’s actions, and how the broker’s strategy induces a gradual alignment of the traders’ positions. These simulations confirm the theoretical insights and reveal the collective dynamics of inventory adjustment and risk management under asymmetric information.
