Sex differences in blood pressure variability during critical illness: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Pauline Yeung Ng, Hazel Yu Yan Hui, April Ip, Jethro Lai, Yuki Yiu Ki Leung, Andrew Kei-Yan Ng, Simon Wai Ching Sin, Kai Hang Yiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRationaleHigher blood pressure variability (BPV) has been associated with adverse cardiovascular events in various clinical settings, but its effects during critical illness remain unclear. There have been observed disparities in risk-adjusted mortality between females and males in the intensive care unit (ICU) and it is possible that BPV contributes to the underlying mechanistic physiology.ObjectivesWe aimed to examine whether BPV differs between females and males in the ICU and whether sex modifies the association between BPV and clinical outcomes.MethodsThis retrospective study included all adult patients admitted to the ICU between January 2013 and December 2023. Exposure was the biologically-assigned sex. Main outcomes were BPV measured by normalised average real variability (ARV) and coefficient of variation during the first 7 days of ICU admission. Exploratory outcomes included ICU mortality. Multivariable regression models were used to adjust for patient characteristics.Main resultsA total of 11 153 admissions were included, of which 4390 (39.4%) were females. Females had increased variability in mean arterial pressure (MAP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) compared with males (normalised ARV of MAP: 8.2% vs 8.2%, adjusted coefficient 0.15, 95% CI 0.03 to 0.26 and normalised ARV of DBP: 9.3% vs 9.1%, adjusted coefficient 0.41, 95% CI 0.27 to 0.55). In the separate multivariable logistic regression analysis, BPV was associated with increased ICU mortality in a dose dependent manner (p<0.001). Each percentage increase in MAP variability was associated with 12% higher odds of ICU mortality, an effect that was modified by sex (p for interaction=0.024).ConclusionsFemales have higher variability in MAP and DBP compared to males during critical illness, which results in association with higher risks of short-term mortality. A thorough understanding of physiological differences, risk stratification and potential treatment strategies are crucial for mitigating sex-related disparities in the ICU.Keywords: Hypertension; Biostatistics; Translational Medical Research; Outcome Assessment, Health Care

Introduction

There has been a growing interest in the intraindividual variability of physiological measures as a mechanism to explain different clinical outcomes between patients. Higher blood pressure variability (BPV) in individuals has been associated with increased incidence of all-cause mortality, cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events,1 end-stage renal disease2 and stroke.3 4 In a population including individuals with and without baseline hypertension, it was found that patients with the highest quartile of visit-to-visit BPV have 80% increase in all-cause mortality compared with the lowest quartile.4 Among patients requiring haemodialysis, greater predialysis BPV was associated with all-cause mortality and major adverse cardiovascular events.5 However, most studies on BPV had been performed in the outpatient setting, and it is unclear whether the same physiological effects persist during acute illness.

Various sex-related differences have been recently identified in the intensive care unit (ICU), ranging from implicit biases in triage decisions to differences in outcome.6 For example, females were found to have higher risk-adjusted mortality than males at ICU discharge and at 1 year.7 8 Also, females were more likely to experience hypoactive delirium while in the ICU and have worse functional outcomes after discharge.9 However, the understanding of the biological processes that underlie these sex-related differences in critical illness remains limited.6 9

In this retrospective observational study, we examined the association between sex and BPV in patients requiring critical care, in an attempt to better understand physiological differences that exist between females and males. We hypothesised that females have higher BPV in the ICU. We also hypothesised that the observed increased BPV during critical illness was associated with worse clinical outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient population

This retrospective cohort study included information from a hospital-wide electronic health record system in Hong Kong between 1 January 2013 and 31 December 2023. All patients who were ≥18 years at the time of ICU admission at a tertiary-referral centre, and had at least two arterial line systolic and diastolic blood pressure (BP) measurements recorded during their ICU stay were included. Readmissions to the ICU during or beyond the same hospital stay were considered as discrete episodes. Patients with missing Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation IV (APACHE IV) scores were excluded.

Study exposure

Patients were divided into two groups according to their biologically-assigned sexes, that is, females and males.

Study outcomes

All BP readings obtained from the arterial line during the first 7 days of ICU admission were included. BP measurements that were biologically implausible and probably attributed to erroneous data recording were truncated based on prespecified cut-offs. The primary and secondary outcomes were computed for systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP) and mean arterial pressure (MAP), respectively.

The primary outcome was BPV quantified by normalised average real variability (ARV),10 which is calculated by ARV divided by the mean of all BP measurements of the patient within the ICU stay. Data collection

Patient baseline characteristics and clinical variables were gathered using the Clinical Data Analysis and Reporting System, which is a centralised repository of de-identified electronic health records from all public hospitals in Hong Kong. Information on patient demographics, comorbidities, hospitalisations, diagnoses, laboratory test results, prescriptions and outcomes were collected. Comorbidities were identified based on the recorded codes from the International Classification of Diseases, ninth revision, Clinical Modification (see online supplemental section E1.1 in the online data supplement). Previous validation of the data demonstrated a high level of accuracy in coding.14 15 Physiological data that were specific to the ICU stay were collected from the ICU-specific Clinical Information System. All data were de-identified to protect patient privacy.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were described as frequencies with percentages (%), and continuous variables as median with IQR. Categorical variables were compared with Fisher’s exact test or Pearson’s χ2 test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were compared with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Demographics and clinical variables that were significantly different between the exposure groups with a p value<0.05 and other confounders selected a priori based on biological plausibility were included in multivariable linear regression. The variables included age at the time of ICU admission, source of admission to ICU (accident and emergency room/general ward/postoperation/other ICU), chronic health conditions (respiratory failure, renal disease, hepatic failure, cirrhosis, metastases, leukaemia or myeloma, lymphoma), underlying cardiovascular comorbidities (atrial fibrillation or other supraventricular tachycardia, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, ischaemic heart disease, heart failure, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease), APACHE IV score,16 principal diagnosis category (sepsis, cardiovascular, neurological, gastrointestinal, haematological, metabolic, renal/genitourinary, respiratory, trauma) and medications prescribed at least 3 months before hospital admission (beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, diuretics, angiotensin converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers, vasodilating agents, inotropes, vasopressors). Details of medication classes are included in online supplemental section E1.2 in the online data supplement. All variables included in the regression model were tested for multicollinearity using the variance inflation factor.

Subgroup analyses were performed to assess whether the association between sex and BPV differed across subgroups, including patients aged older than 55, admitted due to sepsis, who have a history of hypertension, or a history of ischaemic heart disease, respectively. Interaction terms were added to the regression model to examine for the presence of effect modification on the association between sex and BPV across these subgroups.

In sensitivity analysis, the primary outcome of BPV, measured by normalised ARV, was divided into four quartiles, with each quartile representing 25% of the distribution: the first quartile includes the lowest 25% BPV, followed by the second (25–50%), third (50–75%) and fourth quartiles (highest 25%). A logistic regression model was then used to re-examine whether sex was associated with increased risk of being in a certain quartile of BPV. Furthermore, we confirmed the primary findings by additionally adjusting for the total number of BP measurements for each patient in the multivariable linear regression model.

In exploratory analyses, multivariable logistic regression models were used to examine the relationship between BPV, measured by normalised ARV and adverse clinical outcomes of ICU mortality, 7-day mortality and 30-day mortality. The date of admission to ICU was defined as day 1. A simplified confounder model including age at the time of ICU admission, sex and APACHE IV score was used. The presence of effect modification of the BPV-mortality association by sex was tested by including a sex-by-quartiled BPV interaction term, where BPV was categorised into four equally-sized variability groups, as detailed in sensitivity analysis above.

All analyses were performed with two-tailed tests and a p value<0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were developed and executed in both Stata MP, V.18.0 and RStudio, V.2024.04.2.

Primary outcome

A total of 2 667 126 BP measurements were included in the computation of the primary outcome. There were less hourly BP measurements in females within the 7-day period of data collection (52 vs 63, p<0.001). The median MAP was 81 (74–89) mm Hg for females and 83 (75–92) mm Hg for males; the median DBP was 61 (54–68) mm Hg for females and 62 (55–70) mm Hg for males; and the median SBP was 120 (108–136) mm Hg for females and 124 (111–139) mm Hg for males (all p<0.001).

Unadjusted and adjusted outcomes are shown in table 2. There was no statistically significant difference in the unadjusted variability in MAP between females and males (8.2% vs 8.2%, p=0.70). The unadjusted DBP variability was higher in females than males (9.3% vs 9.1%, p<0.001). The unadjusted SBP variability was lower in females than males (8.8% vs 9.0%, p=0.001) (figure 2). After adjusting for confounders in the multivariable linear regression model, the variabilities in MAP and DBP, measured by normalised ARV, were greater in females compared with males (MAP: coefficient 0.15, 95% CI 0.03 to 0.26, p=0.011; and DBP: coefficient 0.41, 95% CI 0.27 to 0.55, p<0.001, respectively). There was no statistically significant difference in SBP variability between females and males (SBP: coefficient −0.03, 95% CI −0.17 to 0.10, p=0.63). Except that of age, the variance inflation factor for all variables included in the regression model was less than 9, indicating an acceptable degree of collinearity (see online supplemental table E1). Detailed coefficients of the multivariable linear regression models are presented in online supplemental tables E2–E4.

Sensitivity analysis

Multivariable logistic regression was performed for the outcome defined by four quartiles of BPV. The results were consistent with that of multivariable linear regression in the primary analysis: there were greater MAP and DBP variability in females compared with males. Females were associated with an increased risk of being in the higher quartile 4 of BP variability compared with quartile 1 (MAP: adjusted OR (aOR) 1.14, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.29, p=0.040; and DBP: aOR 1.26, 95% CI 1.11 to 1.42, p<0.001, respectively). There was no association between sex and SBP variability across quartiles. These results are presented in table 4.

Exploratory analysis

After adjusting for patient characteristics and disease severity, increasing variability in MAP was significantly associated with all adverse clinical outcomes in a dose-dependent manner. This included ICU mortality, with higher odds observed across quartiles of BPV (quartile 2: aOR 1.51, 95% CI 1.20 to 1.90, p<0.001; quartile 3: aOR 1.73, 95% CI 1.38 to 2.16, p<0.001; quartile 4: aOR 3.02, 95% CI 2.43 to 3.74, p<0.001). Similar associations were found between BPV and 7-day mortality (quartile 2: aOR 1.32, 95% CI 1.03 to 1.69, p=0.027; quartile 3: aOR 1.41, 95% CI 1.11 to 1.79, p=0.005; quartile 4: aOR 2.66, 95% CI 2.13 to 3.33, p<0.001), and between BPV and 30-day mortality (quartile 2: aOR 1.17, 95% CI 0.99 to 1.39, p=0.065; quartile 3: aOR 1.18, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.40, p=0.051; quartile 4: aOR 1.63, 95% CI 1.38 to 1.93, p<0.001). Quartile 1 served as the reference group in all analyses. When analysed as linear variables, each percentage increase in normalised ARV of MAP, DBP and MAP were associated with 12%, 3% and 15% increase in risk of ICU mortality, respectively. These results are presented in table 4.

Importantly, sex significantly modified the association between BPV and ICU mortality (p for interaction for ‘sex-by-quartile 4 of BPV’=0.024), meaning that females experience excess risks of ICU mortality attributable to BPV compared with males (see online supplemental tables E6–E8).

Discussion

This is one of the few studies in literature that have examined detailed differences in BPV between female and male patients during critical illness in the ICU. We found that females have greater BPV in MAP and DBP than males during critical illness, especially in the age group >55 years. These findings were robust across different measures of BPV and in sensitivity testing. Importantly, short-term increases in BPV during critical illness were associated with increased risk of adverse outcomes including ICU and 7-day mortality, even after adjusting for the severity of acute illness and differences in chronic health, with females experiencing an exaggerated risk of mortality attributable to BPV. By examining detailed physiological data, we provided insights into intraindividual BPV as a possible mechanistic explanation of disparities in outcomes between the sexes during critical illness.

Over the recent years, sex and gender differences in management and outcomes have become increasingly scrutinised in the practice of clinical medicine. Multiple studies have shown that the treatment for various clinical conditions should be adjusted for sex due to intrinsic biological differences.6 7 17 For example, females are more prone to developing torsade de pointes arrhythmias while on QT-prolonging drugs due to the effect of oestrogens on lengthening the cardiac repolarisation time.18 In other clinical contexts, such as by not specifying sex-specific thresholds of creatinine or urea, a systematic bias in clinical decision-making and risk prediction may contribute to an underestimation of renal impairment, and potentially, more delayed assignment to renal replacement therapy in females.19 20

Isolating the effect of sex on outcomes in critical care is particularly challenging given the heterogeneity of patients, diagnoses and complex disease processes in the ICU.21 Some studies have reported increased mortality in women compared with men at ICU discharge (OR 1.25) and at 1 year or beyond (OR 1.08), even after adjusting for differences in severity of illness.8 It is possible that these outcomes are contributed to by implicit biases in decisions relating to triage and care practices.6 In addition, the female sex has consistently been identified as an independent risk factor of ICU-acquired weakness with an effect on long-term functional outcomes.22 With a view of these reported differences between sexes, we hypothesised that there may be additional physiological phenomena during critical illness that explain the observed between-sex differences in clinical outcomes.

BPV refers to intraindividual variability in BP that occurs as part of the normal physiological response. In the ambulatory setting, studies have consistently indicated that increasing BPV is associated with adverse clinical outcomes.23 24 For example, in a population of 52 387 individuals free from myocardial infarction and stroke, each 4.68% increase in BPV was associated with a 13% increase in the risk of mortality and 7% increase in risk of cardiovascular disease.1 Other studies showed that patients with a history of transient ischaemic attack and increased visit-to-visit variability in SBP have a significantly increased risk of stroke (HRs between 6 and 15).3 In patients with newly diagnosed untreated hypertension, BPV in the short term adversely impacted left ventricular systolic dysfunction.3 25 Other BPV-related negative effects on microalbuminuria and neuronal injury have also been reported.26–28 Taken together, the adverse downstream effects of increased BPV on systemic microcirculation across organ systems are highly-relevant to patients with critical illness who already have a myriad of organ dysfunction.24 Our data confirmed that the adverse effects associated with BPV are magnified in the critical care setting—that each 1% increase in variability of MAP was associated with 12% higher odds of ICU mortality.

Across different measures of variability, we showed that females in the ICU had increased BPV compared with males—they had a 14% and 26% increase in risk of being in the highest quartile of variability in MAP and DBP, respectively. These findings are consistent with ambulatory studies.29 In a large Italian cohort, females were shown to have increased variability in ambulatory BP during the daytime.10 While it remains possible that the effects of vasopressors and inotropes on vascular tone and cardiac contractility affect BPV in the ICU, there are fundamental differences in arterial stiffness between the sexes that are persistent regardless of acute illness. BPV is mostly contributed to by impaired elastic recoil of arteries, leading to less buffering of pressure changes and wider oscillation for any given change in stroke volume.30 31 Arterial pressure-volume index as an oscillometric index of arterial stiffness has been demonstrated to be lower in younger females than males.32 33 However, females experience a rapid increase in arterial stiffening after the onset of menopause due to lower oestrogen levels.34–36 Second, age-related onset of endothelial dysfunction, which was defined by declining flow-mediated dilation of the brachial artery, has been associated with increasing BPV with age.37 38 The overall hormonal implications on arterial stiffness may explain the exaggerated difference in BPV across females and males observed in our subgroups >55 years, and provide a mechanistic hypothesis for a targetable subpopulation.

In describing differences in BPV between sexes and their association with adverse outcomes, we provided baseline measures to explore targeted interventions to mitigate these disparities during critical illness. Notably, an increase in BPV was independently associated with important short-term outcomes such as ICU and 7-day mortality across a large cohort of mixed medical and surgical ICU patients. It remains speculative and worthy of further investigation whether these outcomes could be improved with clinical practices that moderate BPV. For example, subgroups of high-risk patients could benefit from closer titration of vasopressors or use of short-acting anti-hypertensive medication to achieve tighter BP targets even during periods of critical illness. Based on our findings, it is possible that females, especially above 55 years, could be targets of clinical trials and more closely monitored to mitigate the risks attributable to increased BPV.

The observational nature of our study limited our report to associations and we could not make inferences about the causality of BPV on mortality and other clinical outcomes. Although we adjusted extensively for numerous patient characteristics, we cannot exclude the effects of potential unmeasured confounders, especially if there were unmeasured inequalities related to sex in BP management and intensive care processes. Second, the optimal method for examining BPV is not standardised in published literature,39 and can be especially challenging for the ICU cohort in whom the baseline BP could be shifted during acute illness. We therefore focused our analyses on measures of BPV adjusted by the mean BP. Our results were also consistent across different measures of variability. Finally, it remains to be examined whether the observed data, which originated from patients largely of Asian ethnicity, translate across heterogeneous populations.

Conclusions

Females have increased BPV compared with males during critical illness, particularly in older patients above 55 years. Higher BPV was associated with increased risk of short-term mortality in ICU. It is important for healthcare providers and researchers to agree on standardised measures of BPV, perform risk stratification and trial targeted treatment strategies to level sex-related differences in the ICU.
