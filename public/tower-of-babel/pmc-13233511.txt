Symptoms and Knowledge of Dental Erosion Among Hong Kong Adults
Authors: Ivy Guofang Sun, Xinyu Liu, Iris Xiaoxue Yin, Jane Jing Zhang, Xuliang Deng, Chun Hung Chu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study investigates self-reported symptoms and knowledge of dental erosion among adults in Hong Kong.MethodsA 2-week online self-administered closed-ended questionnaire was conducted in 2025, with pre-verified panel members aged 25 to 45 years invited via the Hong Kong Public Opinion Research Institute’s online platform. The questionnaire collected demographic data, medical and dental history, self-reported symptoms, dietary habits, dental visits, management strategies for dental hypersensitivity, and knowledge about dental erosion. Multivariable binary logistic regression was performed to identify independent risk factors for high symptom burden (≥3 symptoms).ResultsOf the 2311 participants, 96% reported self-reported symptoms potentially related to dental erosion, with 1452 valid questionnaires analyzed. Among these, 50% were middle-aged (35–45 years) and 55% were female. The 2 most common symptoms were yellowing teeth (76%) and temperature or acid sensitivity (65%), with 36% reporting at least 3 of the 5 symptoms. Many (44%) had experienced symptoms for at least 3 years, but only 38% sought dental consultation. Almost half (41%) of participants had never heard of dental erosion, and among those aware, only 22% correctly answered all 3 knowledge questions. The median daily eating/drinking frequency was 6, with 20% reporting ≥9 episodes daily. Multivariable analysis identified middle age (aOR = 1.608, p < .001), acid reflux (aOR = 1.655, p = .001), frequent sour snack consumption (aOR = 4.658, p < .001), and frequent juice/fruit vinegar intake (aOR = 1.429, p = .041) as independent risk factors for high symptom burden.Clinical SignificanceThis study highlights the need for increased public awareness and early intervention strategies to prevent dental erosion and its associated complications among Hong Kong adults.Keywords: Dental erosion, Adults, Dietary habits, Self-reported symptom, Questionnaire

Introduction

Dental erosion, an increasing concern in modern dentistry, refers to the irreversible loss of tooth structure caused by the chemical dissolution of enamel and dentin due to acid exposure.1 Unlike caries (cavities) induced by bacterial activity, dental erosion is driven by non-bacterial acids originating from foods, beverages, or bodily fluids. If left unmanaged, it can progress to erosive tooth wear, a broader condition characterized by the synergistic effect of chemical erosion and mechanical forces such as abrasion or attrition.2 This interplay between chemical and physical factors highlights the importance of early recognition and management of dental erosion to preserve oral health.3

The primary causes of dental erosion are intrinsic and extrinsic acids. Intrinsic acids originate within the body, most notably gastric acid that reaches the oral cavity in conditions such as gastroesophageal reflux disease, chronic vomiting, or regurgitation. Extrinsic acids, on the other hand, come from external sources, including acidic foods and beverages such as citrus fruits, carbonated drinks, and sports drinks, as well as environmental exposures like occupational contact with acidic fumes.4 Repeated exposure to these acids softens the enamel surface, leaving teeth more susceptible to subsequent damage.5

The progression from dental erosion to erosive tooth wear occurs when acid-weakened enamel is further damaged by mechanical forces, leading to progressive tooth structure loss.6 Such mechanical factors include abrasion from aggressive toothbrushing or using abrasive toothpaste, attrition caused by teeth grinding or clenching (bruxism), and normal chewing forces.7,8 For example, brushing immediately after consuming acidic foods or beverages can remove the softened enamel layer, accelerating erosion. Over time, this synergistic chemical-physical effect results in significant structural damage, increased tooth sensitivity, and aesthetic issues, such as yellowing, translucency, and irregular edges. These changes not only compromise oral function but also affect the appearance of the smile, impacting self-confidence. Early intervention and proper oral hygiene practices are thus crucial to preventing this progression and preserving tooth function and aesthetics.9

Early signs of dental erosion include smooth, shiny lesions on tooth surfaces, cupping or dents on molars and rounded edges on anterior teeth.10 These subtle changes are often initially unnoticed but become more prominent as erosive tooth wear advances, accompanied by heightened sensitivity to temperature and sweet foods, visible cracks, chips, and further tooth structure loss. Long-term progression can alter bite alignment and oral function (e.g., chewing efficiency, speech), while aesthetic deterioration—particularly of anterior teeth—negatively impacts self-confidence and social interactions.11 Regular dental check-ups and prompt attention to unusual changes are key to early detection, preventing severe damage and maintaining oral health and a confident smile.

Dental erosion acts as the chemical catalyst for erosive tooth wear, a complex condition shaped by both acid exposure and mechanical stress. Recognizing acid sources, modifying daily habits, and seeking professional care are essential to halting this process.7,12 Understanding the significance of dental erosion enables individuals to take proactive measures to protect their teeth and ensure long-term oral health. This study thus aims to investigate self-reported symptoms and knowledge of dental erosion among adults in Hong Kong.

Method

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study used an online questionnaire survey. The Hong Kong Public Opinion Research Institute (www.pori.hk) invited pre-verified panel members aged 25–45 years to complete the survey through its online platform over a 2-week period. This approach helped ensure high data integrity and professional rigor. Participation was voluntary. Ethical approval was obtained from the local Institutional Review Board, and all responses were anonymized to protect confidentiality.

Participants

Eligible participants were Chinese adults aged 25 to 45 years residing in Hong Kong who provided informed consent. Individuals with incomplete responses related to the primary outcomes (self-reported symptoms and knowledge about dental erosion) or inconsistent data (e.g., mismatched age or residency) were excluded from the analysis.

Questionnaire design

The self-administered questionnaire was adapted with permission from a previously validated instrument used in population-based oral health surveys.10 The adapted questionnaire was pilot tested for clarity before distribution.

It comprised 6 sections, including a 4-item module assessing dental erosion knowledge.

The knowledge assessment instrument was adapted from a previously validated questionnaire used in a population-based telephone survey of Hong Kong Chinese adults.10 The original tool was developed and administered by the Public Opinion Program (POP) of the Social Science Research Centre, The University of Hong Kong, using standardized protocols and trained interviewers. Given its established validity for the target population, no additional reliability or validation testing was performed in the present study. The questionnaire was administered in its original Chinese version with consistent item wording and structure.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the proportion of participants reporting 3 or more self-reported symptoms potentially related to dental erosion (out of 5 predefined items). Secondary outcomes were (1) levels of knowledge about dental erosion and (2) factors associated with the presence of a high symptom burden.

Sample size calculation

The minimum required sample size was 385 participants, calculated using a standard formula for prevalence studies, assuming a 20% anticipated prevalence of dental erosion risk, a 95% confidence level, and a 4% margin of error. Allowing for an estimated 30% of incomplete responses, the target recruitment was increased to 550 participants.

Statistical analysis

Survey data were exported to Microsoft Excel for cleaning and de-identification, and then analysed in SPSS version 28.0 (IBM Corp.). Descriptive statistics (percentage, mean, and standard deviation) were used to summarise participant characteristics and outcome variables.

Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages, and differences between participants with high and low symptom burden were tested using the chi-squared test. Continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations and compared using independent-samples t-tests.

Variables with a p -value <.1 in bivariate analyses, together with clinically relevant confounding factors including age, sex, education level, and history of dental scaling, were entered into a multivariable binary logistic regression model to identify independent factors associated with high self-reported symptom burden. Adjusted odds ratios (aOR) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported. To assess the robustness of the findings, sensitivity analyses were conducted using alternative thresholds of ≥2 and ≥4 symptoms. The same set of covariates was used in all sensitivity models. A 2-sided p-value < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

The study adhered to ethical guidelines, emphasizing voluntary participation, informed consent, and strict data anonymity.

Results

Survey response

A total of 2311 adults in Hong Kong accessed the online survey. Of these, 2219 (96%) reported at least one symptom suggestive of dental erosion (Figure 1). Overall, 1452 participants completed all relevant items and were included in the analysis (completion rate 65%; 767 excluded due to incomplete response). The estimated margin of error was ±2.6% at a 95% confidence level, under the maximum variability assumption (p = 50%).

Risk factors for dental erosion

Baseline characteristics and bivariate analyses

Baseline characteristics stratified by symptom burden are presented in Table 1. Compared with participants who reported fewer than 3 symptoms, those with high symptom burden (≥ 3 symptoms) were more likely to be middle-aged, have a history of acid reflux, report more frequent daily eating and drinking episodes, consume sour snacks and juice or fruit vinegar more frequently, replace sweet foods with sour alternatives, and engage in multiple dentin hypersensitivity management behaviors including using desensitizing toothpaste, switching toothpaste, and avoiding irritant foods and beverages(all p < .05). No significant intergroup differences were found in sex, education level, dental scaling history, consumption of fruit, lemon tea, wine, carbonated drinks, lactic acid beverages, or dental consultation.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis

Independent correlates of high symptom burden (≥3 symptoms) from multiple logistic regression are shown in Table 3. After adjustment for potential confounders, middle age (36-45 years) (aOR = 1.608, 95% CI: 1.270-2.035, p < .001), history of acid reflux (aOR = 1.655, 95% CI: 1.217-2.250, p = .001), higher daily eating/drinking frequency (aOR = 1.058, 95% CI: 1.008–1.110, p = .021), frequent sour snack consumption (aOR = 4.658, 95% CI: 1.347-2.774, p < .001), and frequent juice or fruit vinegar intake (aOR = 1.429, 95% CI: 1.018-2.014, p = .041) remained significant risk factors. Use of desensitizing toothpaste (aOR = 1.430, 95% CI: 1.128-1.813, p = .003) and switching to a different toothpaste (aOR = 1.060, 95% CI: 1.014-1.109, p = .010) were also associated with increased odds of high symptom burden, likely reflecting behavioral responses to more severe symptoms.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses using alternative thresholds of ≥2 and ≥4 symptoms were conducted to evaluate the stability of the main findings (Table 3). For the threshold of ≥2 symptoms, the direction and magnitude of associations were broadly similar to the primary analysis, with middle age, acid reflux, sour snack consumption, daily eating frequency, and toothpaste-related behaviours all positively associated with symptom burden. For the stricter threshold of ≥4 symptoms, some associations did not reach conventional statistical significance due to fewer cases; however, the direction of effects for all key variables was unchanged, and all adjusted odds ratios remained >1. Collectively, these results indicate consistent patterns across different symptom cutoffs.

Discussion

Dental erosion, the progressive loss of tooth structure due to chemical dissolution by intrinsic (e.g., gastric acid) or extrinsic (e.g., dietary acids) agents, is a critical yet often overlooked oral health condition.13 Unlike mechanical wear (attrition or abrasion), erosion involves a complex interplay between acid exposure and subsequent physical wear, rendering teeth vulnerable to hypersensitivity, discoloration, and structural compromise.2 As an irreversible condition, prevention through behavioral modification and early diagnosis is paramount. Globally, rising consumption of acidic beverages and snacks, coupled with lifestyle changes, has increased erosion prevalence, particularly in urban populations.14,15 Hong Kong, with its fast-paced lifestyle and dietary preferences for sugary/acidic foods, provides a compelling context to study erosion patterns and public awareness.10

Methodological rationale: online vs. telephone surveys

This study employed an online survey to assess self-reported symptoms and knowledge of dental erosion among Hong Kong adults, following a telephone-based survey conducted 17 years prior.10 The shift from telephone to online methodology reflects evolving communication trends: declining response rates to unsolicited calls (now averaging <10% in urban areas)16 and the widespread adoption of digital platforms. We initially planned to recruit 520 participants over 2 weeks, but unexpectedly received 2,300 logins (340% above target). However, approximately one third of attrition occurred (767 incomplete responses), potentially due to impatient participants disengaging mid-survey, and digital interface challenges. Notably, 91% of completers with valid responses held university degrees, suggesting platform complexity as a potential accessibility barrier.

Online surveys offer cost efficiency, rapid data collection, and anonymity, which may enhance honesty in reporting sensitive health behaviors.17 However, these benefits come with the risk of excluding older adults and individuals with limited digital literacy, thereby introducing potential selection bias. In contrast, previous telephone surveys, while more resource-intensive, provided broader demographic representation.10 The opportunity for interviewer-participant interaction allowed clarification of complex terminology, such as “dental erosion,” thereby reducing ambiguity. Nevertheless, telephone surveys are subject to interviewer bias and often underrepresent younger adults who are less likely to respond to calls.

Our online approach was designed to maximize accessibility for tech-savvy adults aged 25–45 years, consistent with Hong Kong’s high internet penetration rate (97% in 2024).18 However, the resulting sample overrepresented highly educated individuals (90% with tertiary qualifications vs. 64% in the general young age group), suggesting that the findings may not fully generalize to lower socioeconomic groups.19 This limitation highlights the need for mixed-method approaches in future studies to balance both reach and inclusivity.

Symptom distribution and temporal trends

Notably, the distribution of self-reported symptoms potentially related to dental erosion was similar to that in the 17-year prior Hong Kong population survey: yellowing teeth (76%), thermal sensitivity (68%), and edge cracks (52%) remained the most common complaints. Direct interpretation of temporal trends is limited by differences in survey mode (telephone vs. online) and sampling frames, precluding definitive conclusions about changes over this period.10 However, notable dietary shifts were observed: daily eating frequency increased from 5.4 to 6.3 episodes, and soft drink consumption rose from 20% to 26%. These shifts can also be observed in other studies, reporting more frequent snacking and increased intake of sugar-sweetened beverages—both recognized risk factors for dental erosion.14,20 The rising consumption of soft drinks has drawn international public health concern, as highlighted by the World Health Organization, due to its association with dental erosion and other health risks.21,22

Given increased exposure to dietary risk factors, public awareness is critical for prevention. Awareness of dental erosion has doubled since the prior survey, likely reflecting recent oral health campaigns; however, 41% of respondents remain uninformed, indicating a persistent need for targeted education and intervention.

Age and lifestyle: differential risk profiles

Middle-aged adults reported a higher risk of dental erosion and longer symptom duration compared to young adults, likely reflecting cumulative acid exposure over time and delayed preventive measures. These findings align with prior research linking increased age to enamel thinning and greater dentin exposure.23,24 Importantly, dental erosion is not directly caused by age: even young individuals may develop erosion with frequent acidic food/beverage consumption.25,26 A key misconception persists: while 59% of participants had heard of dental erosion, half of them regarded it as a normal aging process rather than a pathological condition.

Beyond age, several lifestyle factors exacerbated erosion risk: frequent juice/fruit vinegar consumption, acid reflux, and replacing sweets with sour alternatives—behaviors often perceived as healthier (e.g., fruit vinegar for weight management), highlighting the need for nuanced dietary guidance.9,27 Recent systematic reviews confirm that frequent intake of natural fruit juices and acidic snacks/sweets is significantly associated with increased erosive tooth wear, with researchers recommending that dentists advise patients to track dietary frequency and types to develop individualized preventive strategies.28 Thus, public education on healthy dietary habits is crucial for both general and oral health, and for erosion prevention.

Clinical and diagnostic challenges

Consistent with the European Organization for Caries Research (ORCA)and theInternational Association for Dental, Oral, and Craniofacial Research (IADR) ORCA/IADR 2020 consensus, we used the term “dental erosion” (chemical tooth surface loss from non-bacterial acids), which aligns with the acid-related signs/symptoms reported by participants. In contrast, “erosive tooth wear” describes a combined chemomechanical clinical condition requiring professional examination, making it unsuitable for a self-reported, population-based survey.

Diagnosing dental erosion remains complex: clinically, it presents as smooth, glossy enamel lesions often confused with abrasion or attrition. The Basic Erosive Wear Examination (BEWE) index, while widely used, lacks sensitivity for early-stage lesions and standardized training protocols,29,30 with only 5% of dentists routinely using such tools—leading to underdiagnosis.31 Self-reported data, while practical for large samples, introduce limitations: laypersons may misattribute discoloration or sensitivity to erosion without clinical confirmation. Nevertheless, self-reported symptoms provide valuable insights into perceived oral health burdens and behavioral motivators (e.g., about two-thirds of at-risk participants adopted desensitizing toothpaste).32

Implications for public health and education

The rise in soft drink consumption and eating frequency underscores the urgency of dietary interventions. Public health campaigns should emphasize not only sugar reduction but also acid exposure management—for example, rinsing with water after acidic meals and avoiding immediate post-acid brushing (which accelerates enamel loss). Tailored education is critical: young adults preferred dentist-delivered advice supplemented by written materials, identifying clinical settings as ideal intervention points.32 Schools and workplaces could further disseminate visual aids demonstrating erosion progression and preventive techniques.

Multilevel preventive strategies are warranted to improve public awareness: (1) population-based campaigns clarifying that dental erosion is an acid-induced pathological condition, distinct from caries and physiological aging; (2) dietary guidance reducing frequent acidic intake, promoting post-acid water rinsing, and avoiding brushing within 30 minutes of acid consumption; (3) integrating routine erosion screening into dental visits; (4) delivering educational messages via social media, community centers, workplaces, and clinics to reach diverse sociodemographic groups; and (5) clear labeling of acidic products to support healthier choices.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, the online methodology limited participation to highly educated, employed individuals, potentially underestimating risks in marginalized groups. Second, self-reported symptoms—while practical for large samples—cannot replace clinical validation, as symptoms are non-specific and may reflect other conditions (e.g., caries, abrasion, hypersensitivity). Third, the ≥3-symptom threshold, though supported by sensitivity analyses, is not clinically validated and may overestimate risk. Fourth, the highly educated sample (91% tertiary) differs from the general Hong Kong population, limiting generalizability. Fifth, there was a discrepancy between the anticipated (20%) and observed (36%) prevalence of dental erosion risk, likely due to the anticipated prevalence being referenced from prior telephone surveys with different sampling frames.10 Notably, our valid sample size (1,452) far exceeded requirements, ensuring sufficient statistical power, and sensitivity analyses confirmed result robustness, mitigating the impact of this discrepancy.

Future research should integrate clinical exams with surveys to correlate symptoms with objective measures (e.g., BEWE). Longitudinal studies tracking dietary habits and erosion progression could clarify causal relationships, while randomized trials might test the efficacy of educational interventions.

Conclusion

This survey highlights 2 critical challenges in managing dental erosion among Hong Kong adults: a persistent public knowledge gap and underprioritized clinical detection. With two-fifths of participants unaware of dental erosion and over half of informed individuals misperceiving it as a natural aging process, widespread educational initiatives are urgently needed to clarify the pathological nature and modifiable risk factors of dental erosion. Clinically, the strong association between frequent acidic beverage consumption, high meal frequency, and self-reported symptoms underscores the need for dentists to systematically screen for erosion, particularly in middle-aged adults. This study demonstrates that dental erosion in Hong Kong adults stems from modifiable dietary risks and knowledge gaps, emphasizing the importance of clinical screening and patient education to prevent acid-related tooth structure loss.
