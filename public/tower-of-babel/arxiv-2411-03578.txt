Abstract
. In this paper, we study stability properties of solutions to scalar conservation laws with a class of non-convex fluxes. Using the theory of a a -contraction with shifts, we show L 2 L^{2} -stability for shocks among a class of large perturbations, and give estimates on the weight coefficient a a in regimes where the shock amplitude is both large and small. Then, we use these estimates as a building block to show a uniqueness theorem under minimal entropy conditions for weak solutions to the conservation law via a modified front tracking algorithm. The proof is inspired by an analogous program carried out in the 2 × 2 2\times 2 system setting by Chen, Golding, Krupa, & Vasseur.



1.
Introduction & main results

We consider a
1-
d scalar conservation law:

u_{t}+(f(u))_{x}=0\indent t>0,x\in\mathbb{R},

(1)

where
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}
are time and space and
u\in\mathcal{I}_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}
is the unknown. The state space
\mathcal{I}_{0}
is assumed to be convex and bounded. Without loss of generality, we may assume
\mathcal{I}_{0}=[-M,M]
for some
M>0
. We assume that
f\in C^{4}(\mathcal{I}_{0})
. In this paper, we will consider the case where
f
is concave-convex. This means
f
verifies the following properties:

\begin{cases}uf^{\prime\prime}(u)>0&(u\neq 0),\\
f^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)\neq 0,\\
\lim_{|u|\to+\infty}f^{\prime}(u)=+\infty.\end{cases}

(2)

In particular,
f
has only one inflection point, normalized to be
f^{\prime\prime}(0)=0
. Our guiding example is
f(u)=u^{3}
. We will consider classes of solutions to (
1
) that are entropic for one (or more) convex entropies
\eta
, i.e. solutions which verify additionally:

(\eta(u))_{t}+(q(u))_{x}\leq 0\indent t>0,x\in\mathbb{R},

(3)

in the sense of distributions. More specifically, we ask that for all
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R})
verifying
\psi\geq 0
:

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\bigl(\psi_{t}(t,x)\eta(u(t,x))+\psi_{x}(t,x)q(u(t,x))\bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\geq 0.

Notation 1.1
.

Throughout, we work with a single fixed
C^{3}
strictly convex entropy, denoted
\eta
, and a few Kružkov entropies, denoted
\eta_{k}
, where
\eta_{k}(u):=|u-k|
. A weak solution to (
1
) will be denoted “
\eta
-entropic” if it is entropic for the entropy
\eta
, and will be denoted “
\eta_{k}-
entropic” if it is entropic for
\eta_{k}
.

It is well known that weak solutions to (
1
) need not be unique. Kružkov showed that solutions which are
\eta_{k}
-entropic for all
k
are contractive against one other in
L^{1}
, and thus one recovers uniqueness if all the entropy conditions are imposed
[
25
]
. The main question we explore in this work is whether uniqueness can be recovered by imposing less entropy conditions.

Dafermos
[
10
]
and DiPerna
[
14
]
showed a weak/strong stability result for Lipschitz solutions to (
1
) by studying the evolution of the relative entropy:

\eta(v|w)=\eta(v)-\eta(w)-\eta^{\prime}(w)(v-w),

where
w
is the strong solution, and
v
is a
\eta
-entropic weak solution. Note that in this case,
v
is assumed to be entropic for merely one entropy. Notice that if
u
is a weak solution of (
1
), (
3
), then
u
verifies a full family of entropy inequalities. Indeed, we define
q(a;b)
, the relative entropy flux, as:

q(a;b)=q(a)-q(b)-q^{\prime}(b)(f(a)-f(b)).

Then, for any
b\in\mathcal{I}_{0}
constant, each
\eta(\cdot|b)
is an entropy for (
1
), and each
\eta
-entropic
u
satisfies:

(\eta(u|b))_{t}+(q(u;b))_{x}\leq 0,

(4)

in the sense of distributions. Similar to Kružkov’s theory
[
25
]
, (
4
) provides a family of entropies measuring the distance of the solution to any constant state. Thus, the relative entropy method may be considered an analog of Kružkov’s theory applicable to systems of conservation laws (although we treat the scalar case here, where Kružkov’s methods are available). The main difference is that the relative entropies are
L^{2}
-based rather than
L^{1}
-based.

Recently, the study of the time evolution of the relative entropy has been extended to the setting in which
w
is a shock, under a genuine nonlinearity condition. In the scalar case, this corresponds to
f
being strictly convex. Leger showed
L^{2}
-contraction for shocks among a class of solutions verifying merely one entropy condition
[
28
]
. More recently, a weighted
L^{2}
-stability property for shocks, the so-called “a-contraction” property, has been shown, which can be seen as an extension of the result of Leger to systems (see
[
35
]
and
[
20
]
. See also
[
22
]
for extensions to balance laws). We also briefly mention the extension of the
a
-contraction theory to systems of conservation laws with viscosity, which was recently used to prove the uniqueness of solutions to the isentropic Euler equations in the class of inviscid limits from Navier-Stokes
[
19
]
,
[
4
]
. Another result of note is the recent work of Huang, Wang, & Zhang, who prove the
a
-contraction property for the cubic flux in the viscous setting, which is the chief example in the class of fluxes we address in this article
[
18
]
.

In this work, we adapt the so-called “relative entropy” program to two novel scenarios. Primarily, we consider for the first time at the hyperbolic level a flux which is not genuinely nonlinear. Secondly, we construct the modified front tracking algorithm in a large-BV setting. The first main result is to show the
a
-contraction property for shocks via the relative entropy method in the case where the flux
f
is concave-convex, among a class of weak solutions verifying minimal entropy conditions.

We restrict our study to solutions verifying the so-called Strong Trace property, which is now classical for the
a-
contraction theory:

Definition 1.1
.

Let
u\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R})
. We say that
u
verifies the Strong Trace property if for any Lipschitzian curve
t\mapsto h(t)
, there exists two bounded functions
u_{-},u_{+}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
such that for any
T>0

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\sup_{y\in(0,\frac{1}{n})}|u(t,h(t)+y)-u_{+}(t)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\sup_{y\in(-\frac{1}{n},0)}|u(t,h(t)+y)-u_{-}(t)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=0.

Notation 1.2
.

For convenience, we will use later the notation
u_{+}(t)=u(t,h(t)+)
, and
u_{-}(t)=u(t,h(t)-)
.

Now, we can define the wildest spaces of solutions that we consider in the paper. The space of weak solutions that a fixed shock
(u_{L},u_{R})
has stability in will depend on

. To this end, for
\epsilon>0

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\text{weak},u_{L},\epsilon}=\{u\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}:\mathcal{I}_{0})|\text{weak solution to }\eqref{cl},\eta,\eta_{\phi^{\natural}(u_{L})}-\text{entropic, }

\displaystyle\text{ verifying Definition \ref{strongtrace}, and }(u_{-}(t),u_{+}(t))\not\in B(\phi_{0}^{\flat}(u_{L}),\epsilon)\times B(u_{L},\epsilon)

\displaystyle\text{ for almost every $t>0$ for all Lipschitz curves $h(t)$}\}.

For definitions of the auxiliary functions
\phi^{\natural}

\phi_{0}^{\flat}
, see (
10

12

Theorem 1.1
.

Consider (
1
), with
f
verifying (

a_{1}(\dot{h}(t)\eta(u_{-}(t)|u_{L})-q(u_{-}(t);u_{L}))-a_{2}(\dot{h}(t)\eta(u_{+}(t)|u_{R})-q(u_{+}(t);u_{R}))\leq 0,

(5)

for almost every

,

In the above, and in all that follows, “dependence on the system” will be interpreted as a dependence on

As a corollary, we obtain the following contraction property:

Corollary 1.1
.

Consider (
1
), with
f
verifying (
2

u_{L}>0,u_{R}\in\mathcal{V}_{0}

(u_{L},u_{R})

E_{t}(u)=a_{1}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\eta(u(t,x+h(t))|u_{L})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+a_{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\eta(u(t,x+h(t))|u_{R})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

Assuming the contraction property holds, the stability in

Lemma 1.1

(from
[

]

[

]

For any fixed compact set

The constants

As an application of these estimates, we prove a uniqueness theorem for solutions to (
1
) verifying minimal entropy conditions. In the case where the flux is convex, it is well known that if a weak solution
u
is entropic for one
C^{1}
strictly convex entropy, then it is in fact the Kružkov solution. This was first proven by Panov
[
31
]
, and later by De Lellis, Otto, & Westdickenberg
[
13
]
using the correspondence between entropy solutions of the conservation law and viscosity solutions of the integrated equation (see also
[
3
]
, in which the results of the aforementioned articles were recently improved). Later, it was again proven by Krupa & Vasseur
[
23
]
, who introduced the ideology that
L^{2}
-stability for shock waves can be extended to stability results for more general solutions, via a strategy of showing that a
\eta
-entropic weak solution
u
verifies Oleĭnik’s “condition E”, which is known to imply that
u
is then in fact the Kružkov solution
[
30
]
,
[
32
]
.

Much less is known when the flux is not convex. In some cases, traveling waves have been shown to converge in the diffusive-dispersive limit to “non-classical” or “undercompressive” shock waves, which are not the Oleĭnik shock but are nevertheless entropic for at least one entropy
[
16
]
,
[
21
]
. Uniqueness of Riemann solutions in the class of piecewise smooth functions verifying a single entropy condition and an appropriate kinetic relation was shown by LeFloch
[
27
]
, while uniqueness of weak solutions in a class of piecewise smooth solutions verifying a certain regularity property was shown by Ballou
[
1
]
. Regularity of the Kružkov solution was studied by Cheng, Dafermos, & Hoff
[
11
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
17
]
. However, to the best of our knowledge, there are no uniqueness or regularity results under minimal entropy conditions or for less regular solutions. This is what we aim to address in our last theorem.

The strategy will be to use the
L^{2}
-stability result of Theorem
1.2
to control the
L^{2}
distance between a weak solution and a BV function which is to be determined. This allows for the transfer of desirable properties of the BV function onto the weak solution. The discretization scheme of Krupa & Vasseur used in
[
23
]
fails for the non-convex case, so we use a modified front tracking algorithm introduced in the
2\times 2
system setting by Chen, Krupa, & Vasseur
[
5
]
. The key difference is that as we are in the scalar case, the front tracking algorithm is valid for large-
BV
initial data
[
9
]
. One of the main contributions of this work is to construct the weight
a
in the modified front tracking algorithm in a large-BV setting. The last main result we prove is the following stability theorem:

Theorem 1.3
.

Consider (
1
) where
f
verifies (
2
). Let
u^{0}\in BV(\mathbb{R})
and

Range(u^{0})\subset[\underline{b},\overline{b}]
, where
0<\underline{b}<\overline{b}
. Let
u
be the Kružkov solution to (
1
) with initial data
u^{0}
. Let

be a sequence of wild solutions, uniformly bounded in

, with initial values
u^{0}_{n}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. If
u^{0}_{n}
converges to

in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, then for every

,
u_{n}
converges to

in

.

As any
BV
function verifies the Strong Trace property (Definition
1.1
), we obtain the following theorem as a consequence:

Theorem 1.4
.

Consider (
1
) where
f
verifies (
2
). Let
u^{0}\in BV(\mathbb{R})
and

Range(u^{0})\subset[\underline{b},\overline{b}]
, where

. Then, any solution with initial value

is unique in the class:

\{v\in L^{\infty}([0,T];BV(\mathbb{R}))\text{ for all }T>0\text{ and }\eta,\eta_{\phi^{\natural}(\underline{b})}-entropic\}

This shows that in certain cases, less than the complete set of entropy conditions need to be imposed in order to obtain uniqueness, even in the case when the flux is not convex. This is sharp in the sense that one
C^{3}
convex entropy is not enough to grant uniqueness for concave-convex fluxes, even in the case where the initial data is localized to the convex region (see Appendix
A
). Finally, we note that the methods in this article can be used to prove analogous results for Oleĭnik shocks with left-hand states in the concave region of the flux, and solutions with initial data localized to the concave region, although we do not address that here.

The article is structured as follows. In Section
2
, we prove the most general result on
L^{2}
-stability for shock waves, Theorem
1.1
. As a corollary, we obtain that the weight

may be picked uniformly among shocks localized to a compact set in the convex region of the flux, the first half of the finer estimates needed in Section

. In Section

1.2
, providing the other half of the finer estimates needed. Finally, in Section
4

2.
Dissipation rates in the large shock regime

2.1.
Outline of the proof of Theorem
1.1

We fix a shock of interest
(u_{L},u_{R})
with size
s_{0}=|u_{L}-u_{R}|>0
, which we wish to establish stability for. We desire to show (
5
):

\dot{h}(t)(a_{1}\eta(u_{-}(t)|u_{L})-a_{2}\eta(u_{+}(t)|u_{R}))-a_{1}q(u_{-}(t);u_{L})+a_{2}q(u_{+}(t);u_{R})\leq 0,

\Pi_{a}:=\{u\in\mathcal{I}_{0}|\frac{a_{1}}{a_{2}}\eta(u|u_{L})-\eta(u|u_{R})\leq 0\}.

(7)

If the weak solution

where

Proposition 2.1
.

Consider (

In the above, it is assumed that

(u_{-},u_{+},\sigma_{\pm})

D_{RH}(u_{-},u_{+},\sigma_{\pm})=q(u_{+};u_{R})-\sigma_{\pm}\eta(u_{+}|u_{R})-\frac{1}{a}(q(u_{-};u_{L})-\sigma_{\pm}\eta(u_{-}|u_{L})).

(9)

Notice that
D_{RH}(u_{-},u_{+},\sigma_{\pm})

in the case when
u_{-}=u_{+}=u
and
\sigma_{\pm}=\lambda(u)
. The first proposition will control this quantity when

.

Consider (
1
), where
f
verifies (
2
). Let
u_{L}>0
and
u_{R}\in\mathcal{I}_{0}
such that
(u_{L},u_{R})
verifies the Oleĭnik condition. There exists
a^{*}>0
such that for any
a<a^{*}
, and any
\eta,\eta_{\phi^{\natural}(u_{L})}

2.2.
Preliminaries

2.2.1.
Auxiliary functions

In this section, we introduce some important functions related to entropy dissipation for the concave-convex flux
f
. A detailed exposition of most of these functions may be found in
[
27
]
, so we omit the proofs of results that may be found therein.

Definition 2.1
.

We define the tangent function
\phi^{\natural}
as follows:

\phi^{\natural}(u)=\begin{cases}v\text{ such that }v\neq u,f^{\prime}(v)=\frac{f(u)-f(v)}{u-v}&u\neq 0,\\
0&u=0.\end{cases}

(10)

The function
\phi^{\natural}
is uniquely defined owing to the concave-convex nature of the flux, and
\phi^{\natural}\in C^{1}({\mathcal{I}_{0}})
because
f\in C^{4}(\mathcal{I}_{0})
, via the implicit function theorem. It is also monotone decreasing and thus invertible. We denote the inverse
\phi^{-\natural}
.

Recall that for a convex entropy
\eta
, a shock wave
(u_{-},u_{+})
is
\eta
-entropic if and only if
E_{\eta}(u_{-},u_{+})\leq 0
, where
E_{\eta}
is the following entropy dissipation function:

E_{\eta}(u_{-},u_{+}):=-\sigma_{\pm}(\eta(u_{+})-\eta(u_{-}))+q(u_{+})-q(u_{-}),

(11)

where
\sigma_{\pm}=\frac{f(u_{-})-f(u_{+})}{u_{-}-u_{+}}
and
q
is the entropy flux associated to
\eta
. We reference
[
27
]
for the following theorem regarding
E_{\eta}
:

Lemma 2.1

(Theorem 3.1 in
[
27
]
)
.

Let
\eta
be
C^{1}
. For any left state
u_{-}>0
, the function
E_{\eta}(u_{-},\cdot)
is monotone decreasing in
(-\infty,\phi^{\natural}(u_{-})]
and monotone increasing in
[\phi^{\natural}(u_{-}),\infty)
. Further,
E_{\eta}(u_{-},u_{-})=0
.

Notice that for a
C^{1}
entropy
\eta
, this implies there are exactly two points where
E_{\eta}(u,\cdot)
equals zero, one of which equals
u
. We will use this to implicitly define a zero entropy dissipation function:

Definition 2.2
.

We define the zero entropy dissipation function as follows:

\phi_{0}^{\flat}(u)=\begin{cases}v\text{ such that }v\neq u,\text{ and }E_{\eta}(u,v)=0&u\neq 0,\\
0&u=0.\end{cases}

(12)

Again,
\phi_{0}^{\flat}
is monotone decreasing and
\phi_{0}^{\flat}\in C^{1}({\mathcal{I}_{0}})
.

Finally, we define the companion function to a fixed point of reference:

Definition 2.3
.

Define the companion function to
k
as follows:

\phi_{k}^{\sharp}=\begin{cases}v\text{ such that }v\neq u,k,\frac{f(v)-f(u)}{v-u}=\frac{f(k)-f(u)}{k-u}&u\neq\phi^{-\natural}(k),\\
k&u=\phi^{-\natural}(k).\end{cases}

Remark 2.1
.

We will most often use the companion function
\phi_{\phi^{\natural}(u_{L})}^{\sharp}
, for some
u_{L}>0
:

\phi_{\phi^{\natural}(u_{L})}^{\sharp}(u)=\begin{cases}v\text{ such that }v\neq u,\phi^{\natural}(u_{L}),\text{ and }\frac{f(v)-f(u)}{v-u}=\frac{f(\phi^{\natural}(u_{L}))-f(u)}{\phi^{\natural}(u_{L})-u}&u\neq u_{L},\\
\phi^{\natural}(u_{L})&u=u_{L}.\end{cases}

For later purposes, we need a Lipschitz bound on
\phi_{\phi^{\natural}(u_{L})}^{\sharp}
at
u_{L}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
u_{L}>0
. Then, there exists
C>0
such that for any
u\in\mathcal{I}_{0}
with
u\geq u_{L}
and
\phi_{\phi^{\natural}(u_{L})}^{\sharp}(u)\in\mathcal{I}_{0}
:

|\phi_{\phi^{\natural}(u_{L})}^{\sharp}(u)-\phi_{\phi^{\natural}(u_{L})}^{\sharp}(u_{L})|\leq C|u-u_{L}|.

Here,
C
depends on the system and continuously on
d(u_{L},0)
.

Proof.

Fix
k:=\frac{\phi^{\natural}(u_{L})}{2}<0
. By (
2
), there exists
c<0
such that
f^{\prime\prime}\leq c
on
[-M,k]
(note that
c:=\max_{x\in[-M,k]}f^{\prime\prime}(x)
depends on
d(u_{L},0)
). So, for any
x\in[-M,k]
, we obtain:

f(x)\leq\frac{c}{2}(x-\phi^{\natural}(u_{L}))^{2}+f^{\prime}(\phi^{\natural}(u_{L}))(x-\phi^{\natural}(u_{L}))+f(\phi^{\natural}(u_{L}))=:p(x).

Next, for
u>u_{L}
, define
h
as the companion function to
\phi^{\natural}(u_{L})
associated to the barrier function
p
:

h(u)=\begin{cases}v\text{ such that }v\leq\phi^{\natural}(u_{L}),\text{ and }\frac{p(v)-f(u)}{v-u}=\frac{f(\phi^{\natural}(u_{L}))-f(u_{L})}{\phi^{\natural}(u_{L})-u_{L}}&u\neq u_{L},\\
\phi^{\natural}(u_{L})&u=u_{L}.\end{cases}

Note that for
u>u_{L}
,
|\phi_{\phi^{\natural}(u_{L})}^{\sharp}(u)-\phi^{\natural}(u_{L})|\leq|h(u)-\phi^{\natural}(u_{L})|
, so it suffices to show the bound
|h(u)-\phi^{\natural}(u_{L})|\leq C|u-u_{L}|
. We can compute
h(u)
explicitly using the quadratic formula and find:

h(u)=\phi^{\natural}(u_{L})-\frac{2|\sigma(u,\phi^{\natural}(u_{L}))-\sigma(u_{L},\phi^{\natural}(u_{L}))|}{|c|},

where
\sigma(u,v)=\frac{f(u)-f(v)}{u-v}
. So, we obtain:

|h(u)-\phi^{\natural}(u_{L})|\leq\frac{2}{|c|}|\sigma(u,\phi^{\natural}(u_{L}))-\sigma(u_{L},\phi^{\natural}(u_{L}))|\leq C|u-u_{L}|,

where
C
may again be taken to depend continuously on
d(u_{L},0)
by property
(g)
(see Section
2.2.3
).
∎

2.2.2.
Entropy dissipation for Kružkov entropies

In this section, we give useful necessary and sufficient conditions for a shock
(u_{-},u_{+},\sigma_{\pm})
to be entropic for a Kružkov entropy
\eta_{k}(u)=|u-k|
. Lemma
2.1
holds only for
C^{1}
entropies, which the Kružkov entropies fail to verify. Recall that the associated entropy-flux is
q_{k}(u)=sgn(u-k)(f(u)-f(k))
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
f
verify (
2
). Let
(u_{-},u_{+})
be a shock with
u_{-}>0,u_{+}
. Let
k\leq\phi^{\natural}(u_{-})
. Then,
(u_{-},u_{+})
is
\eta_{k}
-entropic if and only if
u_{+}\geq k
.

Proof.

Let
u_{+}\geq k
. Then, one may compute:

E_{\eta_{k}}(u_{-},u_{+})=0,

so
(u_{-},u_{+})
is
\eta_{k}-
entropic.

On the other hand, let
k>u_{+}
. It suffices to show that
E_{\eta_{k}}(u_{-},u_{+})>0
. We compute:

E_{\eta_{k}}(u_{-},u_{+})=\sigma_{\pm}(u_{+}-k)+f(k)-f(u_{+})+\sigma_{\pm}(u_{-}-k)+f(k)-f(u_{-}).

Now,
\sigma_{\pm}(u_{+}-k)+f(k)-f(u_{+})>0\iff\sigma_{\pm}<\sigma(k,u_{+})
However, this is clear geometrically as both
k,u_{+}\leq\phi^{\natural}(u_{-})
. For the other term, we see
\sigma_{\pm}(u_{-}-k)+f(k)-f(u_{-})>0\iff\sigma_{\pm}>\sigma(u_{-},k)
. This is also clear as both
k,u_{+}\leq\phi^{\natural}(u_{-})
. So, both terms are positive and we see
E_{\eta_{k}}(u_{-},u_{+})>0
.
∎

The situation is more delicate when
k>\phi^{\natural}(u_{-})
. The following proof is a modification of the proof of Theorem 3.1 in
[
27
]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
f
be concave-convex. Let
(u_{-},u_{+})
be a shock with
u_{-}>0,u_{+}
. Let
k\in(\phi^{\natural}(u_{-}),u_{-}]
. Then,
(u_{-},u_{+})
is
\eta_{k}
-entropic if and only if
u_{+}\geq\phi_{k}^{\sharp}(u_{-})
.

Proof.

Firstly, by the Oleĭnik condition,
(u_{-},u_{+})
is entropic for any entropy for
u_{+}\geq k
, so it suffices to check what happens for
u_{+}<k
. In this region,
\eta_{k}
is smooth, so we differentiate the entropy dissipation with respect to
u_{+}
and find:

\partial_{u_{+}}E_{\eta_{k}}(u_{-},u_{+})=b(u_{-}u_{+})\partial_{u_{+}}\sigma_{\pm},

where:

\displaystyle b(u_{-},u_{+})

\displaystyle:=\eta_{k}(u_{-})-\eta_{k}(u_{+})-\eta^{\prime}(u_{+})(u_{-}-u_{+})=2(u_{-}-k)>0,

\displaystyle\partial_{u_{+}}\sigma_{\pm}

\displaystyle=\frac{f^{\prime}(u_{+})-\sigma_{\pm}}{u_{+}-u_{-}}.

As the flux is concave-convex, it is clear that:

\displaystyle\partial_{u_{+}}\sigma_{\pm}

\displaystyle<0\text{ for }u_{+}<\phi^{\natural}(u_{-}),

\displaystyle\partial_{u_{+}}\sigma_{\pm}

\displaystyle>0\text{ for }u_{+}>\phi^{\natural}(u_{-}).

Thus, we see that
u_{+}\mapsto E(u_{-},u_{+})
attains exactly one zero for
u_{+}<k
and will be positive for all
u_{+}
less than this value. It remains to determine what this decisive value of
u_{+}
is. In the region
u_{+}<k
, we compute:

\displaystyle E_{\eta_{k}}(u_{-},u_{+})

\displaystyle=-\sigma_{\pm}(2k-u_{-}-u_{+})+2f(k)-f(u_{+})-f(u_{-})

\displaystyle=(k-u_{+})(\sigma(k,u_{+})-\sigma_{\pm})+(k-u_{-})(\sigma(k,u_{-})-\sigma_{\pm}).

We see that
E_{\eta_{k}}(u_{-},u_{+})=0
for
u_{+}
such that
\sigma_{\pm}=\sigma(u_{+},k)=\sigma(u_{-},k)
, or exactly
u_{+}=\phi_{k}^{\sharp}(u_{-})
.
∎

2.2.3.
Properties of the system

In this section, we list some abstract properties satisfied by the system.

Properties 1.
Recall that we assume that the set of states

(a)

For any

(b)

\sigma_{u_{L}}(0)=\lambda(u_{L})
. We assume that these curves are defined globally in
\mathcal{I}_{0}
for every

.

(c)

There exist functions

1)

For

,

is an Oleĭnik shock. Further,
\sigma_{u_{L}}^{\prime}(s)<0
for
0\leq s<s^{u_{L}}_{\phi^{\natural}}
,
\sigma_{u_{L}}^{\prime}(s)=0
for
s=s^{u_{L}}_{\phi^{\natural}}
, and
\sigma_{u_{L}}^{\prime}(s)=0
for
s=s^{u_{L}}_{\phi^{\natural}}
, and
\sigma_{u_{L}}^{\prime}(s)>0
for

.

2)

For

\eta-
entropic Rankine-Hugoniot discontinuity. Further, if

, then
(u_{L},S_{u_{L}}(s))
fails to be
\eta
-entropic.

(d)

If
(u,v)
is a
\eta
-entropic Rankine-Hugoniot discontinuity with velocity

. More generally, for any

-entropic Rankine-Hugoniot discontinuity, we have

(e)

If
(u,v)

-entropic Rankine-Hugoniot discontinuity, then

.

(f)

For

(the shock strengthens with

).

(g)

We have the following regularity:

1)

is smooth on
\mathcal{I}_{0}\setminus\{0\}
.

2)

on

.

Notation 2.1
.

For

fixed, we will denote

to be such that

. If the subscript is a function

instead of a point

.

Remark 2.2
.

As we are in the scalar setting, the shock curves are simple and explicit. Let
u_{L}>0
. Then,
S_{u_{L}}(s)=u_{L}-s
, and
\sigma_{u_{L}}(s)=\frac{f(u_{L}-s)-f(u_{L})}{-s}
. Computing the derivative, we see:

\sigma^{\prime}_{u_{L}}(s)\approx-\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(u_{L}-s)^{2},

for

small. In other words, the negativity of

degenerates for

(crucially, in the proofs of Propositions

.

2.2.4.
Study of the set
\Pi_{a}

An important part of the analysis in both the large and small shock regimes is to study the structure of the set
\Pi_{a}
(defined in (
7
)). Note that it is a closed interval, as for
a<1
, the function
\frac{1}{a}\eta(u|u_{L})-\eta(u|u_{R})
is strictly convex, and the function is clearly positive near both
\pm\infty
and negative at
u_{L}
. Here, we give a lemma that illuminates the structure in the large shock regime. It is comparable to Lemma 3.2 in
[
22
]
, although the proof is based on Lemma 3.4 in
[
15
]
. It gives precise asymptotics on the diameter of the set
\Pi_{a}
as
a\to 0
.

Lemma 2.5
.

For
a
sufficiently small,
\Pi_{a}
is compactly contained within
\mathcal{I}_{0}
and
diam(\Pi_{a})\leq C\sqrt{a}
, where the implicit constant depends only on the system and continuously on
s_{0}
.

Proof.

Let
u\in\Pi_{a}
. Then,

\displaystyle\eta(u|u_{L})

\displaystyle\leq a\left(\eta(u|u_{L})+\int_{u_{L}}^{u_{R}}\partial_{v}\eta(u|v)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v\right)

\displaystyle\leq a(\eta(u|u_{L})+s_{0}(|u-u_{L}|+|u-u_{R}|)).

where
\partial_{v}\eta(u|v)=-\eta^{\prime\prime}(u)(u-v)
. By Lemma
1.1
, this implies:

\displaystyle\frac{(1-a)}{a}|u-u_{L}|^{2}-Cs_{0}|u-u_{L}|-Cs_{0}^{2}\leq 0,

where
C
depends only on the system. Using Young’s inequality, we find that
|u-u_{L}|\leq C\sqrt{a}
for
a
sufficiently small, where
C
now also depends on
s_{0}
continuously.
∎

Lemma 2.7

Now, assume that

\displaystyle D_{RH}(u_{-},u_{+},\sigma_{\pm})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{a}(-q(u_{-};u_{L})+v\eta(u_{-}|u_{L}))

\displaystyle+q(S_{u_{-}}(s);u_{R})-\sigma_{u_{-}}(s)\eta(S_{u_{-}}(s)|u_{R}).

(19)

for

sufficiently small, where

,

q(S_{u_{-}}(s);u_{R})-\sigma_{u_{-}}(s)\eta(S_{u_{-}}(s)|u_{R})=\int_{s^{u_{-}}_{u_{R}}}^{s}\sigma_{u_{-}}^{\prime}(\tau)(\eta(u_{-}|S_{u_{-}}(\tau))-\eta(u_{-}|u_{R}))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau.

2.5.
Proof of Proposition
2.3

Firstly, let

. Recall from (

) that the entropy dissipation is defined as:

E_{\eta}(u_{-},u_{+})=-\sigma_{\pm}(\eta(u_{+})-\eta(u_{-}))+q(u_{+})-q(u_{-}).

For
u_{+}=u_{L}
, we see from (
9
):

D_{RH}(u_{-},u_{+},\sigma_{\pm})\leq C-\frac{1}{a}E_{\eta}(u_{L},u_{-}),

where
C
depends only on the system. Now,
u_{-}\leq\phi_{0}^{\flat}(u_{L})-\epsilon
, so by Lemma
2.1
, there exists
\kappa
such that
E_{\eta}(u_{L},u_{-})\geq\kappa
, where
\kappa
depends continuously on
\epsilon,d(u_{L},0)
. So, we obtain:

D_{RH}(u_{-},u_{+},\sigma_{\pm})\leq-\frac{1}{a}\kappa+C.

Taking
a
sufficiently small, we obtain
D_{RH}\leq 0
. Now, let
u_{+}\in\Pi_{a}
. Then,

\sigma_{\pm}\eta(u_{-}|u_{L})-q(u_{-};u_{L})=\sigma(u_{-},u_{L})\eta(u_{-}|u_{L})-q(u_{-};u_{L})+C|u_{+}-u_{L}|,

where

depends only on the system. Now, invoking Lemma

, we obtain:

\sigma_{\pm}\eta(u_{-}|u_{L})-q(u_{-};u_{L})\leq-E_{\eta}(u_{L},u_{-})+C\sqrt{a}\leq-\kappa+C\sqrt{a}.

So, in total:

D_{RH}(u_{-},u_{+},\sigma_{\pm})\leq C+\frac{1}{a}(-\kappa+C\sqrt{a})\leq 0,

for

sufficiently small.

3.
Dissipation rates in the small shock regime

3.1.
Outline of the proof of Theorem
1.2

The approach taken in the proof of Theorem
1.2
is vastly different than in the large shock regime. In particular, we need much finer control on the weight
a=\frac{a_{2}}{a_{1}}
, which we can only hope to obtain by working locally around a degenerate shock (constant state). To this end, we will first work with shocks localized around a fixed state
d\in[\underline{b},\overline{b}]
, and extend to a global statement via a Lebesgue number argument. The local work is a recasting of
[
15
]
in the scalar setting, where many of the proofs are simplified. However, we have the added complication that the characteristic field is not globally genuinely nonlinear. The significant change is that we must take care to show that all the computations take place in the region where

does not degenerate (see Lemma
3.8
, and the remark beforehand). The macroscopic structure of the proof is the same as in Section
2
. We need analogs of Propositions

and

(Proposition

is now unimportant as we are working in the more restrictive class of weak solutions

). In order to obtain the finer control on

, we will parameterize

where

, consider shocks

with strength

\displaystyle\tilde{\eta}(u)

\displaystyle=(1+Cs_{0})\eta(u|u_{L})-\eta(u|u_{R}),

The analogs of

\displaystyle D_{cont}(u)

\displaystyle D_{RH}(u_{-},u_{+},\sigma_{\pm})

\displaystyle=q(u_{+};u_{R})-\sigma_{\pm}\eta(u_{+}|u_{R})-(1+Cs_{0})(q(u_{-};u_{L})-\sigma_{\pm}\eta(u_{-}|u_{L})).

The first proposition needed is an analog of Proposition

.

Consider (

) with

. Fix

(2)

We use Propositions

and

anywhere Propositions

(3)

In case 2, we use (

∎

Now, we prove Theorem

3.4.
Proof of Proposition
3.1

In this section, we prove Proposition
3.1
. For this section, we fix a state
d
, and a single Oleĭnik shock
(u_{L},u_{R})
with
u_{R}=S_{u_{L}}(s_{0})
, and both
u_{L},u_{R}\in[\underline{b},\overline{b}]
. Firstly, note that
\Pi_{C,s_{0}}
is compactly contained in
(0,\infty)
(
\tilde{\eta}(u_{R})>0
, so
\Pi_{C,s_{0}}
must be contained in
(u_{R},\infty)
). We recall that the continuous entropy dissipation is:

\displaystyle D_{cont}(u)=-\tilde{q}(u)+\lambda(u)\tilde{\eta}(u),

(24)

where
\tilde{\eta}(u)=(1+Cs_{0})\eta(u|u_{L})-\eta(u|u_{R})
and
\tilde{q}(u)=(1+Cs_{0})q(u;u_{L})-q(u;u_{R})
. It suffices to show
D_{cont}(u)\lesssim-s_{0}^{3}
for
C
sufficiently large,
s_{0}
sufficiently small, and
u\in\Pi_{C,s_{0}}
.

The dissipation rate is motivated by the scalar case with convex flux, in which the rate is computed explicitly for Burger’s equation (see
[
33
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
24
]
). The dissipation rate was proven in the system case in
[
15
]
. Although we are working in the scalar case, the fact that the flux is not genuinely nonlinear introduces some system-like qualities (we need to use a weight
a
, and there are in some sense two distinct characteristic fields separated by the inflection point). For this reason, we follow the proof of
[
15
]
, although many of the proofs are greatly simplified as a consequence of being in the scalar setting. The strategy is to show that there is a unique maximizer
u^{*}
for
D_{cont}
in
\Pi_{C,s_{0}}
, and then show that
D_{cont}(u^{*})\lesssim-s_{0}^{3}
. Firstly, we show that a maxima cannot occur in the interior of
\Pi_{C,s_{0}}
:

Lemma 3.5

(Lemma 5.1 in
[
15
]
)
.

Let
u
be a critical point of
D_{cont}
. Then,
u\in\partial\Pi_{C,s_{0}}
.

Proof.

We compute:

\displaystyle D_{cont}^{\prime}(u)=\tilde{\eta}(u)f^{\prime\prime}(u).

As
u
is a critical point:

\tilde{\eta}(u)f^{\prime\prime}(u)=0

As the characteristic field is genuinely nonlinear in
\Pi_{C,s_{0}}
by property
(a)
, we see
\tilde{\eta}(u)=0
.
∎

As
\Pi_{C,s_{0}}
is a closed interval, there are exactly two points which may be maximizers of
D_{cont}
(for existence, note that
D_{cont}
is a continuous function on the compact set
\Pi_{C,s_{0}}
, so it must attain a maximum). The next lemma shows which one of the two points on the boundary of
\Pi_{C,s_{0}}
is the maximizer.

Lemma 3.6

(Lemma 5.2 in
[
15
]
)
.

Let
u^{*}
be a maximizer of
D_{cont}
on
\Pi_{C,s_{0}}
. Then,
u^{*}<u_{L}
.

Proof.

We compute:

D_{cont}^{\prime\prime}(u^{*})=f^{\prime\prime}(u^{*})\tilde{\eta}^{\prime}(u^{*}).

As
u^{*}
is a maximizer, we must have
D_{cont}^{\prime\prime\prime}(u^{*})\leq 0
. So, if
u^{*}
is the right endpoint of
\Pi_{C,s_{0}}
, then
\tilde{\eta}^{\prime}(u^{*})>0
, a contradiction.
∎

The previous two lemmas show that it suffices to compute
D_{cont}(u^{*})
, where
u^{*}
is the left endpoint of
\partial\Pi_{C,s_{0}}
, and show that
D_{cont}(u^{*})\lesssim-s_{0}^{3}
. To compute this, we introduce some notation. We define
D_{R}(u)
, and
D_{L}(u)
as follows:

\displaystyle D_{R}(u)

\displaystyle:=q(u;u_{R})-\lambda(u)\eta(u|u_{R}),

\displaystyle D_{L}(u)

\displaystyle:=-q(u;u_{L})+\lambda(u)\eta(u|u_{L}).

Both
D_{R}
and
D_{L}
are approximately half of the entropy dissipation, i.e. they roughly sum to
D_{cont}
:

D_{cont}(u)=D_{R}(u)+(1+Cs_{0})D_{L}(u).

So, it suffices to show that actually,
D_{L}(u^{*})\lesssim-s_{0}^{3}
and
D_{R}(u^{*})\lesssim-s_{0}^{3}
. This is precisely the content of the following lemma.

Lemma 3.7

(Lemma 5.7 in
[
15
]
)
.

For
C
sufficiently large and
s_{0}
sufficiently small:

\displaystyle D_{R}(u^{*})

\displaystyle\lesssim-s_{0}^{3},

\displaystyle D_{L}(u^{*})

\displaystyle\lesssim-s_{0}^{3}.

Proof.

Firstly, we show that
|u^{*}-u_{L}|\sim s_{0}
. As
u^{*}>u_{R}
,
|u^{*}-u_{L}|\lesssim s_{0}
, so it suffices to show the reverse inequality. We have:

s_{0}^{2}=|u_{L}-u_{R}|^{2}\sim\eta(u_{L}|u_{R})=-\tilde{\eta}(u_{L})\lesssim s_{0}d(u_{L},\partial\Pi_{C,s_{0}}),

by Lemmas
1.1
and
3.3
. Dividing by
s_{0}
gives the result.

Next, we estimate
D_{L}
as follows. Firstly, we compute:

D_{L}^{\prime}(u)=f^{\prime\prime}(u)\eta(u|u_{L})\sim|u-u_{L}|^{2}.

Then:

D_{L}(u^{*})=\int_{u_{L}}^{u^{*}}D_{L}^{\prime}(u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\lesssim-\int_{u^{*}}^{u_{L}}|u-u_{L}|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\lesssim-|u^{*}-u_{L}|^{3},

so we obtain:

D_{L}(u^{*})\lesssim-s_{0}^{3},

since
|u^{*}-u_{L}|\sim s_{0}
. The same argument applied to
D_{R}
grants the result.
∎

This completes the proof of Proposition
3.1
.

3.5.
Proof of Proposition
3.2

In this section, we prove Proposition
3.2
. Again, we follow the same proof outline pioneered in
[
15
]
. This section is more delicate than the proof of Proposition
3.1
, and in fact the negativity computed in Proposition
3.1
is needed to show Proposition
3.2
. For convenience, we now give a brief recount of the strategy taken in
[
15
]
, which we also follow here.

Firstly, for any state
u\in\Pi_{C,s_{0}}
, we determine that there is a “maximal” shock
(u,u_{+})
, such that:

D_{max}(u):=\max_{s\leq s^{u}_{\phi^{\natural}(\underline{b}))}}D_{RH}(u,S_{u}(s),\sigma_{u}(s))=D_{RH}(u,u_{+},\sigma_{\pm}).

This is proved in Lemma
3.8
. Recall that by the definition of
\mathcal{S}_{\text{weak}}
, we need only consider shocks with both states bounded from below by
\phi^{\natural}(\underline{b})
, which explains the maximum over the restricted range in the above definition. Next, we decompose
\Pi_{C,s_{0}}
into multiple sets and show
D_{max}(u)\leq 0
on each set separately. We divide the proof into two parts based on the length scale of sets in each part:

Part 1:

D_{max}(u)\leq 0
for
u
in the set
\Pi_{C,s_{0}}\setminus R_{C,s_{0}}
, which has length scale
diam(\Pi_{C,s_{0}})\sim C^{-1}
.

Part 2:

D_{max}(u)\leq 0
for
u
in the set
R_{C,s_{0}}
, which has length scale
s_{0}
.

Within part 1, we decompose the proof into two steps.

Step 1.1:
Firstly, we show that
D_{max}(u)\leq 0
for
u
outside of a set
Q_{C,s_{0}}
. In this case, we use that
u
is sufficiently far from
u^{*}
, the unique maximizer of
D_{cont}
in
\Pi_{C,s_{0}}
, to gain an improvement of Proposition
3.1
(see Proposition
3.5
).

Step 1.2:
Secondly, we show that
D_{max}(u)\leq 0
for
u
in the set
Q_{C,s_{0}}\setminus R_{C,s_{0}}
.

Within part 2, we decompose the proof into four steps, corresponding to a decomposition of
R_{C,s_{0}}
into three parts:

Step 2.1:
We show
D_{max}(u)\leq 0
in a set
R_{C,s_{0}}^{bd}
, via methods similar to Step 1.1.

Step 2.2:
We show
D_{max}(u)\leq 0
is a set
R_{C,s_{0}}^{0}
, a set of states close to
u_{L}
, by showing that
u_{L}
is a local maximizer of
D_{max}
, and
D_{max}(u_{L})=0
.

Step 2.3:
We show
D_{max}(u)\leq 0
on the remaining region
R_{C,s_{0}}\setminus(R_{C,s_{0}}^{bd}\cup R_{C,s_{0}}^{0})
via an ODE argument.

Before we begin part 1, we justify the definition of
D_{max}(u)
, i.e. we show that the function:

s\mapsto\mathcal{F}(s,u):=D_{RH}(u,S_{u}(s),\sigma_{u}(s))

has a unique maximum
s^{*}(u)
, with
s^{*}(u)\lesssim C^{-\frac{1}{2}}s_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}
. The proof is based on Lemma 6.1 in
[
15
]
, although the lack of genuine nonlinearity makes things slightly more subtle here.

Lemma 3.8
.

For
C
sufficiently large and
s_{0}
sufficiently small, the function
s\mapsto\mathcal{F}(s,u)
has a unique maximum, denoted
s^{*}
, for
s\in[0,s^{u}_{\phi^{\natural}(\underline{b})}]
, which is uniquely defined by the equation:

\eta(u|S_{u}(s^{*}))=-\tilde{\eta}(u).

Further, for
u^{+}=S_{u}(s^{*}),|u-u_{+}|\lesssim C^{\frac{-1}{2}}s_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}
, so that for
C
sufficiently large and
s_{0}
sufficiently small,
\mathcal{M}
is compactly contained in
(0,\infty)
.

Proof.

We split into two cases, depending on whether
\Pi_{C,s_{0}}
is contained in the interval
[\underline{b},\overline{b}]
or not.
Firstly, suppose
u>\underline{b}
for all
u\in\Pi_{C,s_{0}}
. In this case, we also have
\phi^{\natural}(u)<\phi^{\natural}(\underline{b})
, as
\phi^{\natural}
is monotonically decreasing. Then, we expand
\mathcal{F}(s,u)
as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(s,u)

\displaystyle=\left(q(u;u_{R})-\sigma_{u}(s)\eta(u|u_{R})+\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}^{\prime}(\tau)\eta(u|S_{u}(\tau))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\right)

\displaystyle-(1+Cs_{0})(q(u;u_{L})-\sigma_{u}(s)\eta(u|u_{L}),

by Lemma
2.9
. Taking a derivative:

\derivative{\mathcal{F}}{s}=\sigma_{u}^{\prime}(s)\left(\tilde{\eta}(u)+\eta(u|S_{u}(s))\right).

Note that
\sigma_{u}^{\prime}(s)<0
for all
s\in[0,s^{u}_{\phi^{\natural}(\underline{b})})
by property
(c)
. So, for
s\in[0,s^{u}_{\phi^{\natural}(\underline{b})})
, we obtain
\mathcal{F}(s,u)
is decreasing in
s
if and only if:

g(s)=\tilde{\eta}(u)+\eta(u|S_{u}(s))\geq 0.

Now,
g(0)=\tilde{\eta}(u)\leq 0
, as
u\in\Pi_{C,s_{0}}
. Further, using property
(f)
, we see
g^{\prime}(s)>0
. So, there is a unique point
s^{*}
such that
g(s^{*})=0
for
s\in[0,s^{u}_{\phi^{\natural}(\underline{b})})
. It follows that
s^{*}
is the unique maximum for
s\mapsto\mathcal{F}(s,u)
on the interval
[0,s^{u}_{\phi^{\natural}(\underline{b})})
, and is characterized by
g(s^{*})=0
. It remains to compare
\mathcal{F}(s^{*},u)
and
\mathcal{F}(s^{u}_{\phi^{\natural}(\underline{b})},u)
. Using property
(c)
again, and the fact that
g(s)>0
for all
s\in(s^{*},s^{u}_{\phi^{\natural}(\underline{b})}]
:

\mathcal{F}(s^{u}_{\phi^{\natural}(\underline{b})},u)-\mathcal{F}(s^{*},u)=\int_{s^{*}}^{s^{u}_{\phi^{\natural}(\underline{b})}}\sigma_{u}^{\prime}(s)\left(\tilde{\eta}(u)+\eta(u|S_{u}(s))\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s<0,

so
s^{*}
is the unique maximizer. Further, by letting
u_{+}=S_{u}(s^{*})
, we estimate:

|u-u_{+}|^{2}\lesssim\eta(u|u_{+})=-\tilde{\eta}(u)\lesssim s_{0}d(u,\partial\Pi_{C,s_{0}})\lesssim C^{-1}s_{0}

by using Lemmas
1.1
,
3.3
, and
3.1
.

The case when there exists
u\in\Pi_{C,s_{0}}
with
u<\underline{b}
is more delicate. The main issue is that for
u<\underline{b}
,
\phi^{\natural}(u)<\phi^{\natural}(\underline{b})
, and so the derivative does not have only one critical point as was the case before. We deal with this as follows. Making the same considerations as above and using property
(c)
, we see that
\mathcal{F}(s,u)
has critical points at
s^{*}
and
s^{u}_{\phi^{\natural}}
, and the derivative has the following sign in between:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\derivative{\mathcal{F}}{s}\geq 0&s\in[0,s^{*}],\\
\derivative{\mathcal{F}}{s}\leq 0&s\in[s^{*},s^{u}_{\phi^{\natural}}],\\
\derivative{\mathcal{F}}{s}\geq 0&s\in[s^{u}_{\phi^{\natural}},s^{u}_{\phi^{\natural}(\underline{b})}].\end{cases}

So it suffices to once again compare
\mathcal{F}(s^{*},u)
and
\mathcal{F}(s^{u}_{\phi^{\natural}(\underline{b})},u)
. Firstly,
\phi^{\natural}(\underline{b})<0
, so there exists
K
such that
\phi^{\natural}(\underline{b})<-\frac{2\underline{b}}{K}
. Take
C
sufficiently large and
s_{0}
sufficiently small so that
S_{u}(s^{*})\geq\frac{\underline{b}}{K}
for all
u\in\Pi_{C,s_{0}}
,
\phi^{\natural}(u)<-\frac{\underline{b}}{K}
, and also
u>\frac{\underline{b}}{2}
for all
u\in\Pi_{C,s_{0}}
.
We check:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(s^{u}_{\phi^{\natural}(\underline{b})},u)-\mathcal{F}(s^{*},u)

\displaystyle=\int_{s^{*}}^{s^{u}_{\phi^{\natural}(\underline{b})}}\sigma_{u}^{\prime}(s)\left(\tilde{\eta}(u)+\eta(u|S_{u}(s))\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{s^{*}}^{s^{u}_{\phi^{\natural}}}\sigma_{u}^{\prime}(s)\left(\tilde{\eta}(u)+\eta(u|S_{u}(s))\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\int_{s^{u}_{\phi^{\natural}}}^{s^{u}_{\phi^{\natural}(\underline{b})}}\sigma_{u}^{\prime}(s)\left(\tilde{\eta}(u)+\eta(u|S_{u}(s))\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{s^{u}_{\frac{\underline{b}}{K}}}^{s^{u}_{\frac{-\underline{b}}{K}}}\sigma_{u}^{\prime}(s)\left(\tilde{\eta}(u)+\eta(u|S_{u}(s))\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+|u-\underline{b}|

\displaystyle\lesssim-1+|u-\underline{b}|

\displaystyle\lesssim-1+(C^{-1}+\tilde{s_{0}}),

where we have used the smoothness of
\phi^{\natural}
, Lemma
3.1
, and
|u_{L}-d|<\tilde{s_{0}}
. So, taking
C
larger and
\tilde{s_{0}}
smaller as needed, we obtain that
s^{*}
is the unique maximizer in this case as well.
∎

.

Lemma 3.9

(Lemma 5.3 in
[

)

D_{cont}^{\prime\prime}(u^{*})\lesssim-s_{0}.

We compute:

The second lemma is an estimate on

Lemma 3.10

(Lemma 5.9 in
[

]
)

Lemma 3.11

(Lemma 6.2 in
[
15
]
)

\displaystyle D_{cont}(u)

\displaystyle=D_{cont}(u^{*})+D_{cont}^{\prime}(u^{*})(u-u*)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}D_{cont}^{\prime\prime}(u^{*})(u-u^{*})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{u^{*}}^{u}D_{cont}^{\prime\prime\prime}(u^{*})(u-\tau)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\lesssim-s_{0}^{3}-(s_{0})(C^{\frac{1}{6}}s_{0})^{2}+(Cs_{0})(C^{\frac{1}{6}}s_{0})^{3}

\displaystyle\lesssim-C^{\frac{1}{3}}s_{0}^{3},

and

∎

The next step is to prove the improved dissipation rate at

(recall that

Lemma 3.12

(Lemma 6.5 in

\displaystyle D_{cont}(\tilde{u})

\displaystyle=D_{cont}(u)+\int_{0}^{\tilde{u}-u}D_{cont}^{\prime}(v)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\lesssim-s_{0}^{3}+\int_{0}^{\tilde{u}-u}\tilde{\eta}(v)f^{\prime\prime}(v)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\lesssim-s_{0}^{3}+\int_{0}^{\tilde{u}-u}\tilde{\eta}(v)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v,

(26)

where we have used Proposition
3.1
. Now, we leverage Lemma
3.2
, which implies
|\tilde{\eta}^{\prime}(\tilde{u})|\sim s_{0}
(where we have also used Lemma
3.1
), to show that in fact, for
v
sufficiently close to
\tilde{u}
,
\tilde{\eta}(v)\lesssim-s_{0}(\tilde{u}-v)
. Indeed, as
\tilde{\eta}
is increasing at
\tilde{u}
, we see:

-\tilde{\eta}^{\prime}(\tilde{u})\sim-s_{0}.

So, by the mean value theorem, there exists
s
with
|s-\tilde{u}|\leq|v-\tilde{u}|
such that:

\displaystyle-\tilde{\eta}^{\prime}(v)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\eta}^{\prime}(\tilde{u})-\tilde{\eta}^{\prime\prime}(s)(s-\tilde{u})

\displaystyle\lesssim-s_{0}+Cs_{0}|v-\tilde{u}|

\displaystyle\lesssim-s_{0},

by taking
|v-\tilde{u}|\lesssim C^{-1}
. So, for such
v
, we obtain:

\tilde{\eta}(v)=-\int_{0}^{\tilde{u}-v}\tilde{\eta}^{\prime}(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\lesssim-s_{0}(\tilde{u}-v).

Substituting this into (
3.5.1
), we see:

D_{cont}(\tilde{u})\lesssim-s_{0}^{3}+\int_{0}^{\tilde{u}-u}\tilde{\eta}(v)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v\lesssim-s_{0}^{3}-C^{-2}s_{0}\lesssim-C^{2}s_{0},

for
C
sufficiently large and
s_{0}
sufficiently small.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

Note that for
C
sufficiently large and
s_{0}
sufficiently small
-C^{-2}s_{0}\lesssim-C^{\frac{1}{3}}s_{0}^{3}
.

This completes the proof of Proposition
3.5
.

Proof of Proposition
3.4
:

Now, we give the proof of Proposition
3.4
. We split into two cases, depending on whether
u_{+}=S_{u}(s^{*})
, the unique maximum of
D_{max}
established in Lemma
3.8
, is inside
\Pi_{C,s_{0}}
or not. Firstly, we prove a lemma that shows that if
u_{+}\in\Pi_{C,s_{0}}
, there is no need for the improved estimate Proposition

.

Lemma 3.13

(Lemma 6.6 in
[
15
]
)
.

Suppose that the maximal shock
(u,u_{+})
satisfies
u,u_{+}\in\Pi_{C,s_{0}}
. Then:

D_{RH}(u,u_{+},\sigma_{\pm})\leq D_{cont}(u_{+})\lesssim-s_{0}^{3}.

Proof.

The entropy dissipation is bounded by:

\displaystyle D_{RH}(u,u_{+},\sigma_{\pm})

\displaystyle\leq(q(u^{+};u_{R})-\sigma_{\pm}\eta(u^{+}|u_{R}))-(1+Cs_{0})(q(u_{+};u_{L})-\sigma_{\pm}\eta(u_{+}|u_{L})),

by Lemma
2.9
, as
(u,u_{+})
is
\eta
-entropic. Then, adding and subtracting
\lambda(u_{+})\eta(u_{+}|u_{R})
and
\lambda(u_{+})(1+Cs_{0})\eta(u_{+}|u_{L})
, we obtain:

\displaystyle D_{RH}(u,u_{+},\sigma_{\pm})

\displaystyle\leq D_{cont}(u_{+})+(\sigma_{\pm}-\lambda(u_{+}))(\eta(u_{+}|u_{R})+(1+Cs_{0})\eta(u_{+}|u_{L}))

\displaystyle\leq D_{cont}(u_{+})\lesssim-s_{0}^{3},

where in the second to last inequality we used
u_{+}\in\Pi
, and
\sigma_{\pm}>\lambda(u_{+})
by property
(d)
. The last inequality follows from Proposition
3.1
.
∎

Now, recall the notation
\mathcal{F}(s,u)=D_{RH}(u,S_{u}(s),\sigma_{u}(s))
. By Lemma
3.8
, it suffices to show that
\mathcal{F}(s^{*},u)
satisfies the estimate
\mathcal{F}(s^{*},u)\lesssim-s_{0}^{3}
. By Lemma
3.13
, we may assume that
u_{+}\not\in\Pi_{C,s_{0}}
. Then, via the fundamental theorem of calculus:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(s^{*},u)

\displaystyle=D_{cont}(u)+\int_{0}^{s^{*}}\sigma_{u}^{\prime}(\tau)(\tilde{\eta}(u)+\eta(u|S_{u}(\tau)))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\lesssim-C^{\frac{1}{3}}s_{0}^{3}-\int_{0}^{s^{*}}\sigma_{u}^{\prime}(\tau)(\tilde{\eta}(u)+\eta(u|S_{u}(\tau)))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\lesssim-C^{\frac{1}{3}}s_{0}^{3}-\int_{0}^{s^{*}}\left(|u-S_{u}(\tau)|^{2}-s_{0}d(u,\partial\Pi_{C,s_{0}})\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\lesssim-C^{\frac{1}{3}}s_{0}^{3}-(s^{*})^{3}+s_{0}s^{*}d(u,\partial\Pi_{C,s_{0}}),

(27)

where we have used Proposition
3.5
,
\sigma_{u}^{\prime}\sim-1
, Lemma
3.3
, and Lemma
1.1
. Next, since
u_{+}\not\in\Pi_{C,s_{0}}
, we have
d(u,\partial\Pi_{C,s_{0}})\lesssim|u-u_{+}|\lesssim s^{*}
. Further, we see:

(s^{*})^{2}\lesssim|u-u_{+}|^{2}\lesssim\eta(u|u_{+})=-\tilde{\eta}(u)\lesssim s_{0}d(u,\partial\Pi_{C,s_{0}})\lesssim s_{0}s^{*},

which grants
d(u,\partial\Pi_{C,s_{0}})\lesssim s^{*}\lesssim s_{0}
. Substituting this into (
3.5.1
), we see:

\mathcal{F}(s^{*},u)\lesssim-s_{0}^{3},

for
C
sufficiently large.

3.5.2.
Step 1.2: Shocks in
Q_{C,s_{0}}\setminus R_{C,s_{0}}

In this section, we show that there exists a set
R_{C,s_{0}}
of order
s_{0}
around

such that

except on
R_{C,s_{0}}

Remark 3.5
.

Note that for

sufficiently large, and

The main proposition to be proved in this step is the following:

Proposition 3.6

(Proposition 6.3 in
[
15
]
)
.

There exists
K_{h}>0
depending only on the system such that for
C
sufficiently large,
s_{0}
sufficiently small, and

Informally, in this step, we prove
D_{max}(u)\lesssim-s_{0}^{3}
for

at a sort of “medium” distance from

; states in
Q_{C,s_{0}}\setminus R_{C,s_{0}}
are at a distance more than of order
s_{0}
from

in some sense. A quantitative justification for this is the following lemma:

Lemma 3.14

(Lemma 6.7 in
[
15
]
)
.

There exists a universal constant
K^{*}
such that for
C
sufficiently large,
s_{0}
sufficiently small, and any

satisfying:

-\tilde{\eta}(u)\geq K^{*}s_{0}^{2},

u_{+}\in\Pi_{C,s_{0}}
.

Proof.

Let
-\tilde{u}\geq K^{*}s_{0}^{2}
, and assume
u_{+}\not\in\Pi_{C,s_{0}}
. Then, letting
K_{1}
be the universal constant from Lemma

, we see:

K^{*}s_{0}^{2}\leq-\tilde{\eta}(u)\leq K_{1}s_{0}d(u,\partial\Pi_{C,s_{0}})\leq K_{1}s_{0}|u-u_{+}|.

Letting
K_{2}
be the universal constant from Lemma

, Lemma
3.8

K_{2}|u-u_{+}|^{2}\leq\eta(u|u_{+})=-\tilde{\eta}(u)\leq K_{1}s_{0}|u-u_{+}|.

Combining these, we see:

K^{*}s_{0}\leq\frac{K_{1}^{2}}{K_{2}}s_{0}.

Taking
K^{*}
strictly larger than
\frac{K_{1}^{2}}{K_{2}}
gives a contradiction, so we must have

.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

By Lemma
3.13
, we then see that if
-\tilde{\eta}(u)\geq K^{*}s_{0}^{2}
, then
D_{max}(u)\lesssim-s_{0}^{3}
. By Lemma

, this roughly says that if
s_{0}\lesssim d(u,\partial\Pi_{C,s_{0}})
, then
D_{max}(u)\lesssim-s_{0}^{3}
holds.

Proof of Proposition
3.6
:
Now, we prove Proposition
3.6
. The strategy is to show that for
u\in Q_{C,s_{0}}
, there exists a univeral constant
K_{h}
such that for
b>K_{h}s_{0}
,
C
sufficiently large, and
s_{0}
sufficiently small, one has
-\tilde{\eta}(u)\geq K^{*}s_{0}^{2}
, where
K^{*}
is the universal constant from Lemma
3.14
. Combined with Lemma
3.13
, this will complete the proof of Proposition
3.6
.

So, suppose
u\in Q_{C,s_{0}}
, with
K_{h}s_{0}<b\leq C^{\frac{1}{6}}s_{0}
(where
K_{h}
is yet to be determined). Then, using the fundamental theorem of calculus, we write:

-\tilde{\eta}(u)=-\tilde{\eta}(u^{*})+\int_{u}^{u^{*}}\tilde{\eta}^{\prime}(v)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v.

(28)

Using
|u-u_{L}|\leq C^{\frac{1}{6}}s_{0}
and Lemma
3.1
, we obtain:

\tilde{\eta}^{\prime}(u)=Cs_{0}(\eta^{\prime}(u)-\eta^{\prime}(u_{L}))+(\eta^{\prime}(u_{R})-\eta^{\prime}(u_{L}))=-s_{0}\eta^{\prime\prime}(u_{L})+\mathcal{O}(C^{\frac{7}{6}}s_{0}^{2}).

(29)

So, writing

as
v(t)=tu^{*}+(1-t)u
, we have
v^{\prime}(t)=-b
. Re-parameterizing the integral in (
28
) and using (
29
):

\displaystyle-\tilde{\eta}(u)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left(-s_{0}\eta^{\prime\prime}(u_{L})+\mathcal{O}(C^{\frac{7}{6}}s_{0}^{2})\right)(-b)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left(bs_{0}\eta^{\prime\prime}(u_{L})+\mathcal{O}(C^{\frac{4}{3}}s_{0}^{3})\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=bs_{0}\eta^{\prime\prime}(u_{L})+\mathcal{O}(C^{\frac{4}{3}}s_{0}^{3})).

Since
\eta^{\prime\prime}(u_{L})\geq K_{1}>0
, and

, we can write this as:

-\tilde{\eta}(u)\geq K_{1}K_{h}s_{0}^{2}-K_{2}C^{\frac{4}{3}}s_{0}^{3}.

So, taking
s_{0}
sufficiently small (depending on

), we may obtain:

-\tilde{\eta}(u)\geq\frac{K_{1}K_{h}}{2}s_{0}^{2}.

Choosing
K_{h}=\frac{2K^{*}}{K_{1}}
grants the result.

3.5.3.
Step 2.1: Shocks close to
\partial\Pi_{C,s_{0}}

In this step, we prove that there exists a constant
K_{bd}>0
such that if
|u-u^{*}|\leq K_{bd}s_{0}
, then

. Consequently, if we define the set:

R_{C,s_{0}}^{bd}:=\{u\in R_{C,s_{0}}||u-u^{*}|\leq K_{bd}s_{0}\},

then we have

on

. This is contained in the following proposition:

Proposition 3.7
.

There exists a universal constant
K_{bd}>0
such that for
C
sufficiently large,
s_{0}
sufficiently small, and any
u\in R_{C,s_{0}}^{bd}
,
D_{max}(u)\lesssim-s_{0}^{3}
.

Proof.

Following the proof of Proposition
3.4
, we use the fundamental theorem of calculus to estimate:

D_{max}(u)\lesssim D_{cont}(u)+s_{0}^{2}d(u,\partial\Pi_{C,s_{0}}).

(30)

The key difference is that in this region, we cannot use Lemma

to blow up the first term (cf. (

)). So, we need a bound on the second. By definition, there are universal constants
K_{bd},K>0
such that for

, then

(via Proposition

). Finally, for
u\in R_{C,s_{0}}
,

implies
|u-u^{*}|\leq K_{bd}s_{0}
for

sufficiently large and
s_{0}
sufficiently small.
∎

3.5.4.
Step 2.2: Shocks close to

Now, we prove that states near

satisfy
D_{max}(u)\leq 0
. In this region, we cannot hope to obtain

, because
u_{+}(u_{L})=u_{R}
, and so

. However, we can still show

by showing that

is a local maximum for

. We begin by stating some algebraic identities and estimates. The proofs of the following lemmas are straightforward and follow from taking derivatives of the defining equations for
u^{+}
, and using local asymptotics on

(cf. Lemma 3.2 in
[
15
]
, and the references to
[

cited therein), so we simply refer to

Lemma 3.15

(Lemma 6.8 in
[
15
]
)
.

Let
u_{+}=u_{+}(u)=S_{u}(s^{*})
be such that
D_{max}(u)=D_{RH}(u,u_{+},\sigma_{\pm})
. Then, the following identities are valid:

\displaystyle(f^{\prime}(u_{+})-\sigma_{\pm})u_{+}^{\prime}=f^{\prime}(u)-\sigma_{\pm}+(u_{+}-u)\sigma_{\pm}^{\prime},

(31)

\displaystyle\eta^{\prime}(u_{R})-\eta^{\prime}(u_{+})+(1+Cs_{0})(\eta^{\prime}(u)-\eta^{\prime}(u_{L}))=\eta^{\prime\prime}(u_{+})u_{+}^{\prime}(u-u_{+}),

(32)

\displaystyle D_{max}^{\prime}(u)=(\eta^{\prime}(u_{+})-\eta^{\prime}(u_{R})-(1+Cs_{0})(\eta^{\prime}(u)-\eta^{\prime}(u_{L})))(f^{\prime}(u)-\sigma_{\pm}).

(33)

Lemma 3.16

(Lemmas 6.9, 6.10 in
[
15
]
)
.

For
C
suficiently large,
s_{0}
sufficiently small, and any
u\in R_{C,s_{0}}\setminus R_{C,s_{0}}^{bd}
:

\displaystyle|u-u_{+}|

\displaystyle\sim s_{0},

\displaystyle\lambda(u)-\sigma_{\pm}

\displaystyle\sim s_{0},

\displaystyle\sigma_{\pm}-\lambda(u_{+})

\displaystyle\sim s_{0},

\displaystyle|u_{+}^{\prime}|+|\sigma_{\pm}^{\prime}|

\displaystyle\lesssim 1,

\displaystyle|u_{+}^{\prime\prime}|+|\sigma_{\pm}^{\prime\prime}|

\displaystyle\lesssim s_{0}^{-1},

\displaystyle|D_{max}^{\prime\prime\prime}(u)|

\displaystyle\lesssim 1.

Now, we state and prove the main proposition of this step.

Proposition 3.8

(Proposition 6.5 in
[
15
]
)
.

There exists a universal constant
K_{0}>0
such that for
C
sufficiently large
s_{0}
sufficiently small, and any

, where:

R_{C,s_{0}}^{0}:=\{u\in R_{C,s_{0}}||u-u_{L}|\leq K_{0}s_{0}\},

D_{max}(u)\leq 0.

Proof.

Firstly
u_{+}(u_{L})=u_{R}
, which implies
D_{max}(u_{L})=D_{RH}(u_{L},u_{R},\sigma(u_{L},u_{R}))=0
. Secondly, evaluating (
33
) at
u_{L}
:

D_{max}^{\prime}(u_{L})=0.

So,
u_{L}
is a critical point of
D_{max}
. Next, we compute
D_{max}^{\prime\prime}(u_{L})
, and obtain:

D_{max}^{\prime\prime}(u_{L})=(\eta^{\prime\prime}(u_{R})u_{+}^{\prime}-(1+Cs_{0})\eta^{\prime\prime}(u_{L}))(f^{\prime}(u_{L})-\sigma(u_{L},u_{R})).

(34)

We show that
D_{max}^{\prime\prime}(u_{L})\lesssim-s_{0}
. Indeed, evaluating (
32
) at
u_{L}
, we obtain:

s_{0}^{-1}(u_{R}-u_{L})\eta^{\prime\prime}(u_{R})u_{+}^{\prime}(u_{L})=0,

implying
u_{+}^{\prime}(u_{L})=0
. Returning to (

D_{max}^{\prime\prime}(u_{L})=-(1+Cs_{0})\eta^{\prime\prime}(u_{L})(f^{\prime}(u_{L})-\sigma(u_{L},u_{R}))

(35)

By Lemma
3.16
, we see
D_{max}^{\prime\prime}(u_{L})\lesssim-s_{0}
. In other words, there exists a constant
K_{1}>0
such that for

sufficiently large, and
s_{0}
sufficiently small:

Finally, we construct

for

sufficiently large and

∎

3.5.5.
Step 2.3: Shocks in
R_{C,s_{0}}^{+}
and

Finally, we show that

for

. For any

Then,

\displaystyle R_{C,s_{0}}^{+}

\displaystyle:=\{u\in R_{C,s_{0}}\setminus(R_{C,s_{0}}^{bd}\cup R_{C,s_{0}}^{0})|b(u)>0\},

\displaystyle R_{C,s_{0}}^{-}

\displaystyle:=\{u\in R_{C,s_{0}}\setminus(R_{C,s_{0}}^{bd}\cup R_{C,s_{0}}^{0})|b(u)<0\}.

In both regions, the proof follows via an ODE argument. The strategy is to show that there are no critical points for
D_{max}
in either region. We start with a characterization of critical points which will let us define the relevant ODE:

Lemma 3.17

(Lemma 6.11 in
[
15
]
)
.

Let

be in the interior of
\Pi_{C,s_{0}}
. Then,
u
is a critical point of
D_{max}
if and only if:

\eta^{\prime}(u_{+})-\eta^{\prime}(u_{R})-(1+Cs_{0})(\eta^{\prime}(u)-\eta^{\prime}(u_{L}))=0.

(36)

Proof.

We use (
33
), which grants:

D_{max}^{\prime}(u)=(\eta^{\prime}(u_{+})-\eta^{\prime}(u_{R})-(1+Cs_{0})(\eta^{\prime}(u)-\eta^{\prime}(u_{L})))(f^{\prime}(u)-\sigma_{\pm}).

So, if
(

holds, then
D_{max}^{\prime}(u)=0
. On the other hand, if
D_{max}^{\prime}(u)=0
, and

is in the interior of
\Pi_{C,s_{0}}
, we claim

, and the result follows. Indeed, since
u
is in the interior of
\Pi_{C,s_{0}}
,
u^{+}\neq u
, and by property
(d)
,
\sigma_{\pm}<\lambda(u)
.
∎

Now, we define the ODE that will track the evolution of the quantities we want to estimate:

Lemma 3.18

(Lemma 6.12 in
[
15
]
)
.

Let
u\in R_{C,s_{0}}\setminus R_{C,s_{0}}^{bd}
, and define
u(t)=tu+(1-t)u_{L}
. Define the functions
F,G,e:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
as:

\displaystyle F(t)

\displaystyle:=-(1+Cs_{0})(\eta^{\prime}(u(t))-\eta^{\prime}(u_{L}))sgn(b),

\displaystyle G(t)

\displaystyle:=-(\eta^{\prime}(u_{+}(t)))-\eta^{\prime}(u_{R}))sgn(b),

\displaystyle e(t)

\displaystyle:=\frac{|u_{+}(t)-u(t)|}{b}.

\displaystyle-e(t)G^{\prime}(t)+G(t)

\displaystyle=F(t),

(37)

\displaystyle G(0)

\displaystyle=0.

(38)

Proof.

Evaluating (

and multiplying by

G(t)-F(t)=-sgn(b)(u_{+}(t)-u(t))\eta^{\prime\prime}(u_{+}(t))u_{+}^{\prime}(t).

Computing

, we obtain:

G^{\prime}(t)=-sgn(b)\eta^{\prime\prime}(u_{+}(t))u_{+}^{\prime}(t)(u-u_{L}),

because
u^{\prime}(t)=u-u_{L}
. So,
G
solves:

\displaystyle-e(t)G^{\prime}(t)+G(t)

\displaystyle=F(t),

\displaystyle G(0)

\displaystyle=0.

∎

A consequence of Lemmas
3.17
and
3.18
is that
u
is a critical point of

if and only if
F(1)=G(1)
. We will show this is not the case for
u
in both remaining regions in the following analysis.

Lemma 3.19

(Lemma 6.13 in
[
15
]
)
.

For
C
sufficiently large,
s_{0}
sufficiently small, and any
u\in R_{C,s_{0}}^{+}
,
u
is not a critical point of
D_{max}
.

Proof.

Let
u\in R_{C,s_{0}}^{+}
, and define
u,F,G
, and
e
as in Lemma

. Solving (
37
), we obtain:

G(t)=\frac{-1}{E(t)}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{E(s)}{e(s)}F(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s,\text{ where }E(t)=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}-e(s)^{-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right).

We will show that

sufficiently small, and all

. So, it suffices to show that

. Indeed, we have:

F(1)=-(1+Cs_{0})(\eta^{\prime}(u)-\eta^{\prime}(u_{L})),

in
R_{C,s_{0}}^{+}
. Taylor expanding and using the fact that
u\in R_{C,s_{0}}^{+}
, so
K_{0}s_{0}\leq b\leq K_{h}s_{0}
:

\displaystyle F(1)

\displaystyle=-(1+Cs_{0})(-\frac{1}{2}\eta^{\prime\prime}(u_{L})(u-u_{L})+\mathcal{O}((u-u_{L})^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{(1+Cs_{0})}{2}\eta^{\prime\prime}(u_{L})b+\mathcal{O}(K_{h}s_{0}^{2})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\eta^{\prime\prime}(u_{L})K_{0}s_{0}+\mathcal{O}(K_{h}s_{0}^{2}),

which is strictly positive for
s_{0}
sufficiently small.
∎

Lemma 3.20

(Lemma 6.14 in
[
15
]
)
.

For
C
sufficiently large,
s_{0}
sufficiently small, and any
u\in R_{C,s_{0}}^{-}
,
u

D_{max}
.

Proof.

Let
u\in R_{C,s_{0}}^{-}
, and define
u,F,G
, and

as in Lemma

4.4.
Modified front tracking algorithm

For an introduction to the front tracking algorithm in the scalar case, we refer to Chapter 6 of Bressan’s book
[
2
]
, Chapter 14 of Dafermos’ book
[
12
]
or Chapter 4 of LeFloch’s book
[
27
]
. Informally, the idea is to take a piecewise-constant approximation of the initial data, solve the Riemann problems produced, and then let the solution propagate until a time when two waves interact. Then, the Riemann problems produced are again solved, and the waves are allowed to propagate again. This is repeated ad infinitum (although one can prove the process eventually terminates in a finite number of interactions). As we change the velocity of the shocks to move at a shifted speed determined by Proposition
4.2
rather than the Rankine-Hugoniot speed, and we are in a somewhat simplified setting due to the initial data being localized to the convex region of the flux, we will give a quick introduction to the types of wave interactions we will encounter below.

4.4.1.
The Riemann solver

Fix a parameter
h>0
with
h<\frac{\epsilon}{2}
, where
\epsilon
is the threshold constant from Proposition
4.2
. We distinguish between three types of waves: “big” shocks, “small” shocks, and rarefaction shocks, which we will henceforth simply refer to as rarefactions. The initial classification of waves in these three buckets will be discussed in Section
4.4.2
. Now, assume that at a positive time
\overline{t}
, there is an interaction at the point
\overline{x}
between two waves:
(u_{L},u_{M})
with strength
\sigma_{1}
and
(u_{M},u_{R})
with strength
\sigma_{2}
. We distinguish between so-called “monotone” interactions, in which
(u_{M}-u_{L})(u_{R}-u_{M})\geq 0
, and “non-monotone” interactions, in which
(u_{M}-u_{L})(u_{R}-u_{M})<0
.

A: Monotone interactions.

(1)

Big shock-big shock: if two big shocks interact, the solution of the Riemann problem will be the following big shock:

v_{a}(t,x)=\begin{cases}u_{L}&x<h_{1}(t),\\
u_{R}&x>h_{1}(t),\end{cases}

where
h_{1}(t)
is the shift function with initial data
h_{1}(\overline{t})=\overline{x}
from Proposition
4.2
.

(2)

Rarefaction-rarefaction: as all rarefactions will travel at the characteristic speed of the right-hand state, and all the computations are localized to the region where the flux is convex, this wave interaction is not actually possible.

(3)

Big shock-small shock: in this case, the solution to the Riemann problem will be a big shock:

v_{a}(t,x)=\begin{cases}u_{L}&x<h_{1}(t),\\
u_{R}&x>h_{1}(t).\end{cases}

(4)

Small shock-small shock: in this case, there are two possible solutions. If
\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}\geq\epsilon
, then the solution will be a big shock:

v_{a}(t,x)=\begin{cases}u_{L}&x<h_{1}(t),\\
u_{R}&x>h_{1}(t).\end{cases}

On the other hand, if
\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}<\epsilon
, the solution will be classified as a small shock, and will move with the speed
h_{2}
.

v_{a}(t,x)=\begin{cases}u_{L}&x<h_{2}(t),\\
u_{R}&x>h_{2}(t).\end{cases}

B: Non-monotone interactions.

(1)

Rarefaction-big shock: in this case, there are two possible solutions. Let
\sigma_{1}
be the strength of the rarefaction, and let
\sigma_{2}
be the strength of the big shock. If
\sigma_{2}-\sigma_{1}>\frac{\epsilon}{2}
, then the solution will be a big shock:

v_{a}(t,x)=\begin{cases}u_{L}&x<h_{1}(t),\\
u_{R}&x>h_{1}(t).\end{cases}

On the other hand, if
\sigma_{2}-\sigma_{1}<\frac{\epsilon}{2}
, the solution will be classified as a small shock, and will move with the speed
h_{2}
.

v_{a}(t,x)=\begin{cases}u_{L}&x<h_{2}(t),\\
u_{R}&x>h_{2}(t).\end{cases}

(2)

Rarefaction-small shock: in this case, there are two possible solutions. Let
\sigma_{1}
be the strength of the rarefaction, and let
\sigma_{2}
be the strength of the small shock. If
\sigma_{1}>\sigma_{2}
, then the result will be a rarefaction with strength
\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}
:

v_{a}(t,x)=\begin{cases}u_{L}&x<\overline{x}+(t-\overline{t})\lambda(u_{R}),\\
u_{R}&x>\overline{x}+(t-\overline{t})\lambda(u_{R}).\end{cases}

On the other hand, if
\sigma_{2}>\sigma_{1}
, the result will be a small shock with strength
\sigma_{2}-\sigma_{1}
:

v_{a}(t,x)=\begin{cases}u_{L}&x<h_{2}(t),\\
u_{R}&x>h_{2}(t).\end{cases}

4.4.2.
Construction of the approximate solutions

Fix a function
u^{0}\in BV(\mathbb{R})
with
Range(u^{0})\subset[\underline{b},\overline{b}]
. Note that
f
is strictly convex in this region. Then, fix a piecewise constant initial data
\psi_{h}(0)
that verifies the following properties:

\displaystyle\psi_{h}(0,\cdot)\text{ has at most $\frac{1}{h}$ jump discontinuities},

\displaystyle TV(\psi_{h}(0,\cdot))\leq TV(u^{0}),

\displaystyle\inf u^{0}\leq\psi_{h}(0,\cdot)\leq\sup u^{0}.

Then, at each jump discontinuity of
\psi_{h}(0,\cdot)
, we can solve (locally in time) the Riemann problem. This works as follows. Let
u_{L},u_{R}
be two states on either side of a jump discontinuity at
\overline{x}
. If
u_{L}>u_{R}
, the Riemann solution is a shock. We will assign all shocks with size
|\sigma|>\epsilon
to be “big”, and all shocks with size
|\sigma|<\epsilon
to be “small”. We let big shocks propagate with speed
h_{1}(t)
and small shocks propagate with speed
h_{2}(t)
. On the other hand, if
u_{L}<u_{R}
, the classical Riemann solution is a rarefaction fan, which we must replace with several “rarefaction shocks” each of strength
<h
, i.e. the Riemann solution will be:

v_{a}(t,x)=\begin{cases}u_{L}&x<\overline{x}+t\lambda(w_{1}),\\
w_{j}&\overline{x}+t\lambda(w_{j})<x<\overline{x}+t\lambda(w_{j+1})\indent(1\leq j\leq N-1),\\
u_{R}&x>\overline{x}+t\lambda(u_{R}),\end{cases}

where
N=\lceil\frac{\sigma}{h}\rceil
, and:

w_{j}=u_{L}+\frac{j}{N}(u_{R}-u_{L})\indent\text{ for }1\leq j\leq N-1.

Then, we let the waves propagate. By slightly perturbing the speed of a wave if necessary, we can assume that at any time
t>0
, there is at most one wave interaction. At the first interaction point between two waves, we solve the Riemann problem as dictated in Section
4.4.1
. Then, we continue recursively. The following lemma shows this process will produce a function
\psi_{h}
with the following properties:

Lemma 4.3

(Chapter 4, Theorem 2.1 in
[
27
]
)
.

The wave front tracking approximations
\psi_{h}
are well-defined globally in time. In particular, the total number of waves in
\psi_{h}
is uniformly bounded in
t
(but tends to infinity when
h
tends to
0
). Further, the approximations satisfy the uniform estimates:

\displaystyle TV(\psi_{h}(t,x))\leq TV(u^{0}),

\displaystyle\inf u^{0}\leq\psi_{h}(t,x)\leq\sup u^{0},

\displaystyle||\psi_{h}(t,\cdot)-\psi_{h}(s,\cdot)||_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\leq TV(u^{0})\hat{\lambda}|t-s|,\text{ for }0<s<t<T.

We omit the proof, as using the shifts instead of the Rankine-Hugoniot speeds only affects the Lipschitz constant in time, where we must make sure that the maximum speed of any wave is bounded. This is guaranteed by Proposition
4.2
, where we show that the speed of all waves is bounded by
\hat{\lambda}
. All other aspects of the proof are identical. For completeness, a proof will be given in the author’s PhD thesis
[
6
]
.

4.4.3.
The weight function
a

Now, we define the function
a
that gives stability of a weak solution
u\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{weak}}
and the function
\psi_{h}

Lemma 4.4

Let

Firstly, we will show the following:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}<1\implies\prod_{i=1}^{N}(1-a_{i})\geq 1-\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}.

We proceed by induction. Assume that

. Then:

\prod_{i=1}^{1}(1-a_{i})=1-a_{i}=1-\sum_{i=1}^{1}a_{i},

so (
44

, and let

. Then:

\prod_{i=1}^{k+1}(1-a_{i})=(1-a_{k+1})(\prod_{i=1}^{k}(1-a_{i}))\geq(1-a_{k+1})(1-\sum_{i=1}^{k}(1-a_{i})\geq 1-\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}(1-a_{i}).

Now, we prove the main lemma. Firstly, assume that
\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}<\frac{1}{4}
. Then:

\prod_{i=1}^{N}(1-a_{i})\geq 1-\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}\geq\frac{3}{4}\geq\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{4K}.

Otherwise, partition

into

, with

, such that:

\frac{1}{4}\leq\sum_{i}a_{i,j}\leq\frac{1}{2}\text{ for all }1\leq j\leq 4K.

Then, applying (

\prod_{i=1}^{N}(1-a_{i})=\prod_{j=1}^{M}\prod_{i}(1-a_{i})\geq\prod_{j=1}^{M}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\geq\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{M}\geq\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{4K}.

∎
