Discoid Lupus Erythematosus along the Lines of Blaschko in an Adult: A Case Report
Authors: Aishwarya Muddebihal, Sandip Mohanty, Meenakshi Batrani
Published: 2023

Dear Editor,

Discoid lupus erythematosus is an inflammatory auto-immune disease. Classical lesions present as discoid erythematous atrophic hyperkeratotic dyschromic plaques involving the scalp, head, and neck area.[1] Rarely, the lesions follow the lines of Blaschko, representing linear discoid lupus erythematosus (LDLE).[2] Here, we present a case of 19-year-old male LDLE on the trunk.

A 19-year-old male presented with asymptomatic erythematous lesions over the abdomen and trunk in a linear fashion for the past 1 year. The lesions started over the lower abdomen and progressed along the midline up to the chest and laterally at the level of left nipple in a duration of 3 months. He had no history of photosensitivity or systemic symptoms. His past, family, drug, and medical history were unremarkable. Cutaneous examination showed multiple well-defined erythematous indurated plaques of varying size with adherent scales and central scarring [Figure 1a and b]. The lesions followed the Blaschko lines extending from the lower abdomen along the midline to the left thoracic area at the level of nipples. Dermoscopic examination showed follicular plugging, yellow scales, arborizing vessels, and white structureless areas [Figure 1c]. Mucosa, nails, scalp, and systemic examination were unremarkable. Haematological parameters, liver, kidney function tests, and chest radiograph were normal. Anti-nuclear antibodies were negative by indirect immunofluorescence. Histopathology showed an atrophic epidermis with hyperkeratosis, follicular plugging, lichenoid infiltrate in the papillary dermis, abutting basal layers showing vacuolisation, and basal–parabasal apoptotic keratinocytes, along with perivascular and perieccrine lymphocytic infiltrate in the upper to deep dermis [Figure 2a and b]. PAS stain highlighted basement membrane thickening, and Alcian Blue stain showed mucin deposition in the reticular dermis [Figure 3a and b]. Direct immunofluorescence showed granular bands of IgM, C3, and fibrinogen along the dermo-epidermal junction [Figure 3c]. Thus, based on the above findings, a diagnosis of LDLE was made. The patient showed good response to oral prednisolone, hydroxychloroquine 200 mg twice daily, and topical mometasone furoate cream.

Linear involvement in cutaneous lupus erythematosus is rare. It may present as LDLE, lupus panniculitis, tumid LE, SCLE, and bullous LE, with LDLE being the most common presentation.[3] The first case of linear DLE was reported by Umber and Winkelmann et al. in 1978 in a patient with concomitant scleroderma.[4] The term ‘Blaschko-linear LE’ was proposed by Jin et al.[3] Linear cutaneous LE is due to genetic mosaicism, which results in abnormal clone of cells proliferating and migrating along the Blaschko lines. The mosaic cells have neo-antigens which stimulate local immune response.[3] Environmental factors like sunlight, ultraviolet light, infections, drugs, pesticides, and heavy metals can also trigger LDLE.[5] Keratinocyte apoptosis through p53, tumor necrosis factor, and Fas/Fas ligand is an important event in initiating the cutaneous lesions of DLE. Another proposed mechanism is that the genetically variant keratinocytes are deficient of proteins crucial for the regulation of apoptosis or they are immunologically distinct with abnormal major histocompatibility complex expression.[3]

Linear LE compared to systemic lupus erythematosus occurs at a younger age with equal sex distribution, less photosensitivity, rare systemic involvement, and less ANA positivity. The majority of the lesions are seen on the face, followed by upper limbs, trunk, and neck. Histopathology is identical to classical DLE.[3]

Linear DLE should be differentiated from lichen planus, lichen striatus, linear granuloma annulare, linear psoriasis, morphea, or inflammatory linear verrucous epidermal nevus.[3]

Linear DLE mimicking linear lichen planus/lichen planus pigmentosus has also been reported.[6]

Our case is unique as our patient had lesions only on photo-protected sites without head and neck involvement, and there are only a handful cases of LDLE reported on the trunk in adults.
