Borderline resectable pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma: a narrative review
Authors: Rupesh Thapa Chhetri, Krisha Maharjan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Borderline resectable (BR) pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is a well-recognized entity with specific characteristics. Previously considered inoperable, these tumors are now increasingly resectable due to advances in neoadjuvant chemotherapy. This review synthesizes current knowledge on diagnostic, therapeutic, and management approaches for pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, especially for BR disease. It covers the definition, criteria, and surgical management, along with various clinical challenges in the diagnosis of borderline disease. Neoadjuvant therapy is the treatment of choice before surgery, but there are still limited data on the selection of optimal therapy and on the early identification of non-responders. Multidetector computed tomography, along with CA19-9 and positron emission tomography, are being used to evaluate resectability after neoadjuvant therapy. Pancreatic resection for PDAC is one of the most complex procedures in abdominal surgery due to the technical and oncological challenges associated with its aggressive growth. Despite these options, management remains challenging.

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains a significant global health challenge and is ranked as the third-leading cause of cancer deaths in the Western world[1]. Management is challenging due to its biological characteristics, its anatomical location, and its generally late presentation, with minimal early symptoms. PDAC can be clinically categorized as resectable (R), borderline resectable (BR), and unresectable (UR), which is further divided into locally advanced and metastatic[2,3]. Specific anatomical, biological, and clinical features characterize BR-PDAC. Because of the effectiveness of oncologic treatments and increasing surgical experience, especially in vascular reconstruction, many patients have successfully undergone surgical management[4,5]. Despite modern management techniques, significant challenges to management remain. This article aims to summarize current insights into the management of PDAC, especially the borderline-resectable variety.

Methodology

Literature for this narrative review was identified through PubMed, Scopus, and web searches using keywords such “Pancreatic Ductal Adenocarcinoma,” “Borderline Resectable,” “Neoadjuvant therapy,” “Radiotherapy,” and “Recent trials.” Publications from 2020 to 2025 were prioritized, although important landmark studies were also included. Publications in English with relevant content were selected. Duplicate publications and conference abstracts were excluded. The literature search flow diagram the selection process (Fig. 1). Definition

Borderline resectability of PDAC is based on the extent of venous and arterial involvement in the absence of distant metastasis[6]. The International Association of Pancreatology in 2017 proposed expanding the preoperative staging criteria by redefining BR PDAC using biological and conditional criteria, suggesting that resectability needs to be assessed beyond anatomical parameters[7]. The anatomical factors are represented by the size and location of the tumor, along with the tumor’s relationship with the surrounding vascular structures[8]. This defines a tumor that is high-risk for margin-positive resection when undergoing upfront surgery. The biological or biochemical factors are represented by an elevated preoperative serum carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA19-9) level above 500 IU/mL and biopsy-proven regional lymph node metastasis[7]. This is defined when there are findings that raise the possibility of extra-pancreatic metastatic disease. The conditional criteria are represented by the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status[7]. This is defined when the patient is at high risk of mortality or complications after surgery related to performance status and comorbidities. PDAC, based on anatomical parameters, has been classified by many systems, and the most common is the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) classification (Table 1). There was further refinement that maintained an anatomical focus while introducing subclassifications based on vascular involvement. BR-PDAC was further divided into BR-PV and BR-A, which can be defined as:

BR-A (arterial invasion): Tumor contact with the SMA and/or CA of less than 180 degrees, without stenosis or deformity. Tumor abutment of the CHA without tumor contact with the proper hepatic artery and/or CA.

BR-PV (SMV/PV invasion alone): Tumor contact of 180 degrees or greater, or invasion of the SMV/PV, not exceeding the inferior border of the duodenum[6].

Management of BR-PDAC

The proper definition of BR-PDAC was developed to identify tumors for which preoperative neoadjuvant therapy (NAT) could offer an advantage to treating clinicians. An observational multicenter study of patients with pancreatic head carcinoma who were treated with upfront surgery was performed to evaluate the validity of the preoperative resectability status. The results showed that the overall survival of the BR-PV and BR-A groups was significantly poorer than that of the resectable group[10]. In addition, most other retrospective studies showed that the neoadjuvant treatment group had significantly better outcomes than the upfront surgery group[11,12]. NAT provides certain benefits: early systemic treatment for undetected micrometastases; an increase in the R0 resection rate; and a reduction in complications (Post-operative pancreatic fistula)[13,14]. On the other hand, some argue that the reduced chance of surgery due to disease progression represents a significant detriment to disease management[15,16]. Currently, NCCN guidelines recommend neoadjuvant treatment rather than upfront surgery for BR-PDAC[17]. The debate about the best management approach for BR-PDAC remains open due to the lack of high-level evidence.

Neoadjuvant therapy in BR-PDAC

Many major guidelines recommend mFOLFIRINOX (modified fluorouracil, leucovorin, irinotecan, and oxaliplatin) and gemcitabine-based regimens (e.g., nanoparticle albumin-bound-paclitaxel) as acceptable options, depending on performance status, but none recommend a single preferred regimen due to insufficient comparative evidence[3,18]. The use of NAT is considered in cases where tumor shrinkage is necessary to achieve resectability. In a meta-analysis conducted by Yang et al, 50 studies were analyzed, and it included 117 254 patients with both resectable and BR-PDAC[19]. NAT was associated with improved overall survival, higher R0 resection rates in BR-PDAC, and lower rates of nodal involvement. In addition, NAT did not increase postoperative morbidity, supporting its use in BR-PDAC[19].

Furthermore, the ESPAC-5 trial compared four treatments: immediate surgery, neoadjuvant gemcitabine plus capecitabine (GemCap), neoadjuvant FOLFIRINOX, and neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (radiotherapy at a total dose of 50.4 Gy with capecitabine). R0 resection was achieved in 14% of patients after immediate surgery, and in 23% of those who received any NAT before surgery. One-year survival rates were 39% for immediate surgery and 76% for the combined NAT groups. Among the NAT groups, survival rates were 84% for FOLFIRINOX, 78% for GemCap, and 60% for CRT. Adding radiotherapy to neoadjuvant chemotherapy did not improve outcomes. No serious side effects that would disable further treatment were observed in any NAT group. This was the first randomized clinical trial to confirm the favorable effect of NAT on survival in BR-PDAC compared to immediate surgery[20]. Jang et al conducted a randomized clinical trial involving patients with BR-PDAC, comparing neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy with immediate surgery, and demonstrated significant differences in favor of the NAT group[21] (Table 2). The authors of PREOPANC-2, a multicenter, randomized phase III study, aimed to evaluate the impact of neoadjuvant FOLFIRINOX chemotherapy versus neoadjuvant gemcitabine-based CRT on survival in patients with R-PDAC and BR-PDAC. The FOLFIRINOX arm received FOLFIRINOX every 14 days for eight cycles, followed by surgery. In contrast, the CRT arm received three cycles of neoadjuvant gemcitabine with hypo-fractionated radiotherapy (36 Gy in 15 fractions) during the second cycle, followed by surgery and four cycles of adjuvant gemcitabine. Although neoadjuvant FOLFIRINOX did not demonstrate a survival advantage over gemcitabine-based CRT, the latter remains a valid and effective alternative, particularly for patients with suboptimal performance status who may not tolerate intensive multi-agent chemotherapy[25] (Table 2).

The NUPAT-01 trial compared the efficacy of two neoadjuvant therapies recommended by the NCCN for BR-PDAC: FOLFIRINOX and gemcitabine with nanoparticle albumin-bound paclitaxel (GEM/nab-PTX). The R0 resection rate in the intention-to-treat population was 73.1% in the FOLFIRINOX group and 56.0% in the GEM/nab-PTX group. Among patients who underwent surgical resection, the 3-year OS rate was 62.6% in the FOLFIRINOX group and 55.1% in the GEM/nab-PTX group, which did not differ significantly. According to the authors, the NUPAT-01 trial demonstrated that neoadjuvant chemotherapy with either FOLFIRINOX or GEM/nab-PTX is feasible, well-tolerated, and associated with a high rate of R0 resections, providing favorable survival outcomes in BR-PDAC[26] (Table 2).

NAT is a treatment strategy for BR-PDAC, the principal benefit of which lies in its ability to achieve tumor downstaging. The clinical evidence consistently shows that NAT reduces tumor size and vascular involvement, thereby increasing the likelihood of R0 resections and improving surgical outcomes. Challenges include treatment-related toxicity, the risk of disease progression during therapy, and the fact that a large proportion of patients may never proceed to surgery. Assessing treatment response is another major limitation, as the utility of CT imaging and CA 19-9 remains uncertain.

Resectability after NAT

It is important to prevent an unnecessary and risky R1 pancreatic resection, which is the main goal of restaging after NAT. MDCT (Multidetector Computed Tomography) is commonly used to assess the initial resectability of PDAC, with an accuracy of 73–97% for predicting R0 resection in patients undergoing upfront surgery[27]. Alternatively, an MDCT assessment of R0 resectability after NAT may underestimate pathological responses[28,29]. Yang et al showed that Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) and CT have similar performance in assessing PDAC tumor size before and after NAT[30]. There are several reasons why conventional imaging is unreliable in accurately predicting an R0 resection after NAT. Firstly, the inflammatory and fibrotic reaction of the perivascular tissue, or its sterile necrosis (following a pathological response) induced by NAT, can be indistinguishable from neoplastic infiltration. Secondly, there is the possibility that an imaging-clean adipose plane can be restored by NAT, indicating a possible R0 resection that turns out to be incorrect due to the presence of residual microscopic infiltration of the perivascular region[31]. The NCCN guidelines have introduced customized criteria for PDAC resection after NAT, where radiographic findings, unless demonstrating clear disease progression, are no longer the only criteria used to propose exploratory surgery in the presence of other patient data showing clinical improvement and at least stable or decreased CA19-9[3]. A histopathological assessment of post-NAT pancreatectomy specimens to determine the diagnosis, tumor grade, margin status, tumor size, lymph node status, presence of lymphovascular or perineural invasion, as well as the tumor’s response to NAT, is important in predicting the prognosis of PDAC patients. In particular, the results of the pathological assessment of tumor response to NAT were reported based on five different systems: the College of American Pathologists system, the Evans grading system, the MD Anderson Cancer Center scheme, the Royal North Shore system, and the integrated pathologic score[32,33].

Despite many differences in the grading of tumor response, a significant proportion of patients achieve little or no response to NAT, with survival after surgical resection not significantly different from that of similar unoperated patients. There is a need for a more objective evaluation of the usefulness of potential surgical resection with respect to further neoadjuvant treatment. Currently, CA19-9 is the most important marker of PDAC biology[3,34,35]. Unfortunately, approximately 10% of the population cannot express CA19-9[36].

Despite the variability in the cut-off values reported by several authors and the need for their possible standardization, CA19-9 is a very useful tool to evaluate the response to NAT in PDAC and could also be used to guide the overall duration of neoadjuvant treatment[29,37–39]. However, it cannot be the only tool used for evaluation, as a significant proportion of patients with PDAC may not secrete CA19-9 or may have normal CA19-9 values[36,40]. The 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (18F-FDG-PET) has been increasingly used in the differential diagnosis and prognostic evaluation of PDAC since the early 1990s[41]. The NCCN consensus guidelines state that 18F-FDG-PET/CT may be considered after the formal pancreatic CT protocol in high-risk patients to detect extra-pancreatic metastases[3]. According to the recently revised Clinical Practice Guidelines for Pancreatic Cancer of the Japan Pancreas Society, “Positron emission tomography is not recommended as a diagnostic or qualitative diagnostic tool in patients with suspected pancreatic cancer,” but it “is recommended in patients with suspected distant metastasis, as PET is more specific than CT for the diagnosis of distant metastasis”[42]. Furthermore, several authors have recently evaluated the role of 18F-FDG-PET/CT in the management of PDAC[43,44].

The staging challenges of PDAC, even in the current era of high-quality imaging, are also evident in the results of a staging laparoscopy performed in 1004 patients between 2017 and 2021, which led to a change in management for 1 in 5 patients[45]. Tumor-specific tracers can provide alternative solutions for more accurate staging and better monitoring of therapeutic response. The Fibroblast Activation Protein inhibitor (FAPI) is a tumor-specific tracer on which important clinical studies have been conducted. More than 90% of the tumor volume consists of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) that are associated with the promotion of tumor growth, tissue invasion, metastasis development, immune escape, and resistance to therapy[46,47]. Considering the high expression of FAP on the cell surfaces of CAFs and its limited expression in normal tissues, PET/CT imaging of CAFs with radiolabeled FAP inhibitors is an active field in nuclear medicine[48].68Ga- and 18F-radiolabeled FAPI variants (including FAPI-04, FAPI-46, and FAPI-74) have produced promising results in the diagnosis of PDAC[49,50]. The measurement of the intracellular levels of phosphorylated thymidine has been postulated to be an accurate method for estimating cellular growth. Fluorinated thymidine analogues, such as 18F-fluorothymidine (18F-FLT), are trapped as radiolabeled phosphates within the cell, allowing uptake of the radiolabeled thymidine analogues to serve as a surrogate measure of cellular proliferation. 18F-FLT-PET/CT imaging is potentially superior to 18F-FDG-PET/CT, as 18F-FLT uptake is not affected by inflammation or hyperglycemia[51,52]. In conclusion, the evaluation of resectability after NAT is mainly based on NCCN guidelines. Despite the increased efficacy of NAT after the introduction of FOLFIRINOX and gemcitabine-based chemotherapy, better determination and monitoring of the pathological response to NAT remain limited.

Surgery

Depending on the location and extent of the tumor, a pancreaticoduodenectomy, distal pancreatectomy, or total pancreatectomy may be performed. The surgical procedure involves removing the tumor with clear margins and at least twelve lymph nodes (necessary for staging). In recent years, minimally invasive procedures, particularly robotic procedures, have been implemented based on the assumption of a potential benefit in terms of faster recovery and the possibility for patients to proceed more quickly and more frequently to adjuvant therapy[53,54]. For a proper surgical procedure, it is important to know the vascular anatomy around the tumor (Fig. 2). In these cases, since venous resections have become the standard of care, the crucial point for surgical decision-making has become the arterial assessment. To date, whether to perform an arterial resection remains an individualized decision to be considered only in well-selected cases. Therefore, it is essential to assess whether a radical surgery can be performed without arterial resection. For this purpose, different artery-first procedures have been proposed, and each of them has a specific indication depending on tumor location[55]. The criteria for surgery were good performance status, significant tumor shrinkage after NAT, a decrease in tumor marker levels (CA19-9), negative cytology results, and absence of peritoneal deposits on staging laparoscopy[56]. Open surgery remains the standard of surgical care for pancreatoduodenectomy. Although the PLOT and PADULAP trials initially showed potential benefits for laparoscopic pancreatoduodenectomy, these results were challenged by the Dutch LEOPARD 2 study, which reported an increase in mortality in the laparoscopic group and was therefore discontinued prematurely[57–59]. Choi et al also found the minimally invasive approach to be inferior in terms of disease-free survival for stage III pancreatic head tumors[60]. Conversely, Giani et al demonstrated comparable oncologic outcomes between open and minimally invasive techniques, and they found that the minimally invasive technique was associated with better lymph node harvesting[61].

Surgical oncologic quality metrics emphasize adequate lymphadenectomy to prevent understaging and ensure clearance of locoregional disease. Surgical resection includes en bloc extirpation of the regional lymph nodes. Examination of 12 lymph nodes may be sufficient for accurate American Joint Committee on Cancer staging of PDAC[62]. Alternatively, the International Study Group for Pancreatic Surgery (ISGPS) recommends examining at least 15 lymph nodes for staging. These guidelines suggest that the minimum number of lymph nodes examined should be between 12 and 17 for a pancreatic head tumor[63]. Furthermore, the lymph node ratio (LNR), defined as the ratio of positive lymph nodes to the total number of examined lymph nodes, has prognostic importance and is influenced by both the number of positive and the number of harvested lymph nodes[62,63].

Adjuvant chemotherapy

Another aspect of management that requires further clarity is adjuvant therapy in patients with BR-PDAC who received NAT. It is a matter of debate whether adjuvant treatment is necessary. In theory, it is reasonable to assume that adjuvant therapy should improve survival in patients with persistent micro-metastatic disease even after NAT and surgery. The American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) guidelines recommend that adjuvant therapy be offered for 6 months, including the preoperative course[64]. The NCCN suggests that this can be considered in this context[3]. Ivey et al found that postoperative chemotherapy was associated with improved median overall survival, especially in node-positive disease[65]. In a multicentre AGEO-FRENCH cohort of 80 patients who underwent surgery for BR-PDAC after neoadjuvant FOLFIRINOX, 54% received adjuvant chemotherapy, and the authors failed to find an association with improved survival[66]. Conversely, Barnes et al conducted a study that examined 234 patients with resectable and BR/LA-PDAC who had undergone NAT; 59% received adjuvant therapy, and it was associated with a significantly decreased risk of death among patients with nodal metastatic disease[67]. There are very limited data to guide recommendations on this topic, and it will require further study. It would seem reasonable to consider adjuvant chemotherapy in BR-PDAC patients post-NAT with node-positive disease.

Supportive treatment

A multidisciplinary approach is an essential part of every oncological therapy. It includes the treating surgeon, radiologist, medical oncologist, and anesthesiologist. In addition, mental health has a significant impact on surgical care, especially in oncological settings in which patients are anxious. Recently, evidence-based interventions have been integrated. These include proper psychological screening, cognitive behavioral therapy, and mindfulness-based stress reduction, which are proven strategies[68]. These are targeted interventions for anxiety and depression, which can impair surgical recovery. These therapies bring about important changes in management. Due to this approach, there is a reduction in complications, a shorter recovery duration, and decreased long-term care needs that influence treatment[69]. It is high time that the integration of psychological management into oncosurgical care be considered essential for truly holistic management.

Use of artificial intelligence

Use of artificial intelligence (AI) can aid in transforming big data into clinically actionable insights more efficiently, reducing inevitable errors, improving diagnostic accuracy, and making real-time predictions. Cai et al established a PDAC risk prediction model by analyzing 138 chronic pancreatitis patients with focal mass lesions. A scoring method based on logistic regression was employed, which included five variables: sex, mass number, mass location, bilirubin, and CA19-9 (AUC: 0.72)[70]. Furthermore, Appelbaum et al used a logistic regression model and developed a prediction model for PDAC using electronic health record data. A total of 18 risk factors, i.e., age, gender, race, abdominal pain, angina pectoris, asthma, atherosclerotic heart disease, gallbladder calculus, chest pain, chronic pancreatitis, coronary heart disease, diabetes mellitus, emphysema, essential hypertension, family history of pancreatic cancer, jaundice, stroke, and ulcer, were used, and their prediction model displayed an AUC of 0.71[71].

Moreover, AI-based tissue biomarker analysis can be used to identify a multiple-gene expression signature for the detection of early PDAC. Zhang et al designed a novel AI method based on relative gene expression data and RNA-seq data collected from databases that could distinguish PDAC from non-PDAC[72].

In addition to these findings, Liu et al reported that the AUC of an AI (convolutional neural network) platform for CT-assisted diagnosis of PDAC was 0.963. Using AI to analyze thousands of images on a pixel-by-pixel basis aids radiologists in diagnosis[73].

Though AI has recently become an up-and-coming tool in management protocols, there is a lack of adequate standardization, and it requires further research.

Conclusion

BR-PDAC is a recognized entity in the pancreatic surgical community. Despite several changes to the exact definition of BR-PDAC, it remains necessary to clarify exactly what it means and how management protocols for this entity should be applied. The various definitions need to be considered, including anatomical, biological, and conditional factors. Neoadjuvant treatment followed by surgery has become the gold standard for BR-PDAC. The management protocols for the use of NAT, which offers several advantages, need to be incorporated into primary institutions, and further study of the optimal chemotherapeutic agents and radiation therapy is needed. In addition, restaging following NAT and the use of adjuvant chemotherapy should be emphasized for accurate diagnosis and management in all centers.
