Multiscale damage evolution and chemo-mechanical constitutive modeling of acid-corroded sandstone
Authors: Li Hao, Zeng Yifan, Wu Qiang, Cheng Yifan, Yang Donghui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThis study investigated time-dependent chemo-mechanical deterioration of fine-grained sandstone exposed to dilute sulfuric acid representing the proton-sulfate component of acid mine drainage. Specimens were corroded for 0–20 days and examined by X-ray diffraction, scanning electron microscopy, endpoint solution chemistry, and triaxial compression at 20, 30, and 40 MPa. Results showed cumulative carbonate dissolution, cement degradation, microstructural deterioration, and mechanical weakening. After 20 days, Ca2+ and Mg2+ concentrations reached 156 and 25.3 mg/L, while peak strength decreased by 33.3%, 26.6%, and 29.9%, respectively. A nested Mohr-Coulomb comparison yielded F(4,5) = 1.284 and p = 0.388, supporting downward envelope translation but not statistically detectable slope variation. A time-dependent damage model reproduced pre-peak responses across all 15 conditions, with R2 = 0.767–0.961. The framework links mineral alteration and microstructural damage to mechanical degradation but requires further validation for multicomponent field acid mine drainage and long-term engineering service.

Introduction

High-sulfur coal is widely distributed in China (Figure 1). Oxidation of sulfur-rich components in coal seams releases sulfate (SO42−) and hydrogen ions (H+), which are the main drivers of acid mine drainage (AMD) formation.1 AMD is commonly characterized by a pH below 4.5 and high concentrations of sulfate and heavy-metal ions. Such corrosive water not only accelerates underground equipment corrosion and induces water-inrush hazards but also damages the structural integrity of coal-rock water-resisting strata through seepage, further aggravating pore expansion and mechanical degradation of the surrounding rock, ultimately threatening the long-term stability and ecological safety of deep underground engineering.2Figure 1Geographic distribution of high-sulfur coal resources in China

Geographic distribution of high-sulfur coal resources in China

After acidic fluids react chemically with rock mineral grains and cements, the original pore and fracture structures inside the rock mass can be substantially modified. This issue has been studied from multiple perspectives by researchers worldwide.3 Existing studies on fluid-rock interaction have confirmed that acidic aqueous solutions produce pronounced selective dissolution in sedimentary rocks containing carbonate components such as calcite and dolomite. This mineral dissolution at the mesoscale directly weakens the macroscopic load-bearing skeleton of the rock.4,5 Using nondestructive testing methods such as nuclear magnetic resonance and micro-computed tomography (CT), subsequent studies further found that acidic fluids preferentially dissolve soluble matrices and intergranular cementing materials, thereby promoting the initiation and propagation of subcritical cracks and ultimately causing obvious decreases in crack initiation stress and damage stress.6,7,8 Macroscopic mechanical tests have yielded consistent findings: as solution acidity increases, rock density, elastic modulus, compressive strength, and related parameters decrease to varying degrees.9,10 For example, Chen et al.11 used CT scanning to clarify the mesoscopic mechanism of sandstone damage evolution under chemical corrosion, and the long-term immersion tests conducted by Luo et al.12 showed that the uniaxial compressive strength of sandstone can decrease by approximately 43% in a strongly acidic environment with pH = 1.25.

In summary, existing studies have clarified many effects of solution acidity, ionic composition, and immersion duration on rock mineralogy, pore structure, and mechanical properties, and several chemical-damage models have been proposed. Three issues nevertheless merit further study. First, fewer studies have followed damage accumulation through a controlled corrosion-duration series. Second, the cross-scale link among mineral dissolution, changes in particle contacts, characteristic stress thresholds, and macroscopic strength attenuation remains incompletely quantified. Third, whether acidic corrosion primarily translates the strength envelope or also changes confining-pressure sensitivity is difficult to determine without explicit nested statistical testing, wider pressure coverage, and replicate specimens. Recent reaction-chemo-mechanical studies suggest that mineral mass loss and microcrack propagation may interact, but these mechanisms and their transfer to sandstone under AMD-relevant acidic exposure require further verification. To address these research gaps, fine-grained sandstone was exposed to a fixed dilute sulfuric acid solution for 0–20 days and characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD), SEM, endpoint ion analysis, and triaxial compression. The study retains three linked aims: to document the measured time-series mineralogical, microstructural, and mechanical changes; to compare time-specific, common-slope, and full interaction Mohr-Coulomb models using the 15 measured peak strengths; and to develop a time-dependent chemo-mechanical constitutive description with clearly stated calibration and extrapolation boundaries.

Evolution of mineral composition and ions in the immersion solution

Main mineral reaction pathways

The reactions described here are presented as possible stoichiometric pathways consistent with the identified minerals and the endpoint solution composition. Because solution chemistry was measured only initially and after 20 days, the data cannot resolve stage-specific reaction rates or establish a kinetic ranking among minerals.

Possible ion-release pathways during the reaction of the constituent minerals with dilute sulfuric acid are summarized here. Carbonate dissolution is consistent with the large cumulative increases in Ca2+ and Mg2+, but the present endpoint measurements do not determine when or how rapidly each reaction occurred.

The dissolution reaction of calcite (CaCO3) under dilute sulfuric acid corrosion is:CaCO3+H2SO4→Ca2++SO42−+CO2↑+H2O

The dissolution reaction of dolomite [CaMg(CO3)2] under dilute sulfuric acid corrosion is:CaMg(CO3)2+2H2SO4→Ca2++Mg2++2SO42−+2CO2↑+2H2O

Feldspar minerals (microcline) undergo slow hydrolysis under acidic conditions, releasing Na+ and K+ into pore water with groundwater seepage and generating secondary clay minerals such as kaolinite simultaneously.2KAlSi3O8+2H2SO4+9H2O→2K++Al2Si2O5(OH)4+2SO42−+4H4SiO4

Interlayer Na+ in montmorillonite is substituted by H+, raising the Na+ concentration in aqueous solution, while Al3+ and Mg2+ ions get leached out from the mineral octahedral sheets, and such cation exchange together with metal ion dissolution proceeds synchronously.Na0.3(Al,Mg)2Si4O10(OH)2+H2SO4→Na++Al3++Mg2++SO42−+silicagel

Mineral contents and ion responses in the immersion solution

As shown in Figure 2, the relative contents of dolomite and calcite decreased markedly, consistent with the ion-release mechanism controlled by preferential dissolution of carbonate minerals. Microcline and montmorillonite also showed decreasing trends, but the magnitudes were smaller. The change in microcline is consistent with progressive hydrolysis of silicate minerals, whereas the change in montmorillonite may be related to interlayer cation exchange and sheet leaching. Table 2 shows that after 20 days, the concentrations of Ca2+, Mg2+, and Na+ increased from 0.36, 0.038, and 0.45 mg/L to 156, 25.3, and 15.0 mg/L, respectively, providing independent evidence for carbonate dissolution and ion exchange in clay minerals. It should be noted that XRD phase proportions are semi-quantitative results; in this study, they are mainly used to identify relative change trends and are not interpreted as strict mass-conservation results. Microstructural evolution

Figure 3 presents the available SEM fields obtained after 0, 5, 10, 15, and 20 days of acid exposure. The ×200 fields are shown in Figures 3A–3E, and an additional ×500 view of the 20-day specimen is shown in Figure 3F. The uncorroded sandstone specimen at 0 days (Figure 3A) showed a comparatively intact granular texture in the selected SEM field. Grain edges were distinct and surfaces were relatively smooth, with no obvious dissolution pits. Dark surface regions were generally small and visually isolated in this field. The visible cement-like material appeared comparatively continuous, and no obvious large cracks or grain detachment were observed. After 5 days of acid exposure (Figure 3B), grain edges appeared more rounded, and local surface depressions were more evident. Dark regions appeared larger and more irregular, and some cement-like material was locally discontinuous. These 2D features are visually consistent with local mineral dissolution and possible cement loss, but they do not establish bulk pore enlargement, seepage channels, or connectivity changes. At 10 days (Figure 3C), surface depressions appeared deeper or more numerous and locally formed honeycomb-like or sponge-like textures. Some grain boundaries appeared less clearly cemented. These observations may indicate continued surface alteration and local weakening of grain contacts; they do not demonstrate increased bulk porosity or a semi-suspended granular skeleton. At 15 and 20 days (Figures 3D and 3E), the selected fields showed more extensive irregular dark regions, local detachment-like features, and less continuous cement-like material at some grain contacts. The surfaces appeared less compact than the 0-day field. Figure 3F provides a higher magnification view of the 20-day surface morphology. Because each duration is represented by a single field acquired under nonidentical imaging conditions, these observations are qualitative and should not be interpreted as complete cement disappearance or bulk structural loosening.Figure 3SEM images of sandstone after different acid-corrosion durations(A) 0 days at ×200.(B) 5 days at ×200.(C) 10 days at ×200.(D) 15 days at ×200.(E) 20 days at ×200.(F) 20 days at ×500.Scale bars, 300 μm.

Stress-strain behavior and evolution of characteristic stresses

Stress-strain response stages

The stress-strain curves at confining pressures of 20, 30, and 40 MPa are shown in Figures 7A–7C, respectively. All specimens displayed obvious brittle characteristics, and the curves can be roughly divided into four stages: pore and fissure compaction, elastic deformation, yield deformation (stable crack propagation), and post-peak deformation and failure. No clearly independent unstable crack propagation stage was observed. Stage 1: pore and fissure compaction: in this stage, the stress-strain curve is concave upward, and axial strain increases relatively rapidly with stress. This response is commonly associated with closure of initial microcracks and voids under loading. The selected SEM fields show surface depressions consistent with chemical alteration, but they do not directly identify the internal features being compacted. The weakly expressed confinement effect on the initial nonlinear segment may be related to exposure-induced changes in local cementation and grain-contact conditions, although this mechanism was not measured directly. Stage 2: elastic deformation: in this stage, the stress-strain relation is approximately linear, and the curve slope (elastic modulus) remains essentially stable, indicating that the specimen response is dominated by elastic deformation. The upper limit of this stage corresponds to the crack initiation stress. The lower crack-initiation stresses observed after exposure may be associated with chemically altered grain boundaries, cement degradation, and pre-existing defects, but the present measurements do not isolate their individual contributions. Stage 3: stable microcrack propagation: the curve gradually becomes concave downward and axial deformation accelerates, indicating a response dominated by stable microcrack propagation. Possible weakening of local cementation and grain-contact boundaries may facilitate crack growth along altered interfaces, increasing the deformation rate and flattening the curve. Some long-duration specimens exhibit double-peak behavior, especially at 20 days. The upper limit of this stage is the crack damage stress, which represents the characteristic state before unstable crack growth. Stage 4: post-peak deformation and failure: after reaching peak strength, the stress of each specimen dropped rapidly, showing typical brittle failure characteristics and indicating that a complete brittle-ductile transition did not occur within the range of this study. Brief plastic flow may occur after the peak, after which the stress remains at a certain residual level.Figure 7Stress-strain responses of acid-corroded sandstone at different confining pressures(A) 20 MPa.(B) 30 MPa.(C) 40 MPa.

Under conventional triaxial compression, the peak strength was described by the linear Mohr-Coulomb relation in Equation 1, where k is the slope of the strength envelope and σcs is the intercept extrapolated to σ3 = 0. The internal friction angle and cohesion were calculated as φ = arcsin[(k−1)/(k + 1)] and c = σcs/(2k0.5), respectively. Because the lowest confining pressure was 20 MPa, σcs is an extrapolated intercept and should not be equated with the measured uniaxial compressive strength.(Equation 1)σ1=σcs+kσ3

To test whether the available data support a systematic change in confining-pressure sensitivity, corrosion duration was treated as a five-level categorical factor. For time group i and observation j, the reduced model M0 used time-specific intercepts and one common slope, whereas the full model M1 included time-specific intercepts and time-by-confining-pressure interaction terms (time-specific slopes), as given in (Equation 2), (Equation 3):(Equation 2)M0:σ1,ij=αᵢ+kσ3,ij+εᵢⱼ(Equation 3)M1:σ1,ij=αᵢ+kᵢσ3,ij+εᵢⱼ

M0 contained six parameters (five intercepts and one common slope), with a residual sum of squares (RSS) of 473.129 and 9 residual degrees of freedom. M1 contained ten parameters (five intercepts and five slopes), RSS = 233.423, and 5 residual degrees of freedom. The nested comparison gave F(4,5) = 1.284 and p = 0.388. Thus, the additional interaction terms did not produce a statistically detectable improvement at α = 0.05, but this failure to reject M0 does not prove that the slopes are identical.

The reduced-model results are summarized in Table 6, and Figure 13 compares the uncorroded and 20-day strength envelopes. The reduced model gave a pooled common slope k = 3.0972 (SE = 0.2293; 95% confidence interval [CI], 2.5785–3.6159), corresponding conditionally to a pooled apparent internal friction angle of 30.8°. The five intercept estimates were 248.581, 213.821, 204.264, 194.974, and 146.551 MPa; each had SE = 8.052 MPa and the respective 95% CIs were reported in Table 7. Under this simplification, apparent cohesion decreased from 70.6 to 41.6 MPa (41.0%) from 0 to 20 days. The more strongly supported conclusion is therefore a downward change in the overall load-bearing level. Figure 13 shows that after 20 days of acid corrosion, the peak strength at each confining-pressure level decreased markedly, and the strength envelope shifted as a whole toward the lower stress region. Failure modes and peak strain

Macroscopic failure morphology

The macroscopic failure characteristics of sandstone specimens under different acid-corrosion durations and confining pressures are shown in Figure 14. At each acid-corrosion duration, increasing confining pressure generally made specimen failure less violent and more distributed, although no complete brittle-to-ductile transition was identified (Figures 14A–14O). Morphologically, the failure pattern changed from a single dominant macroscopic crack to a more distributed dendritic crack cluster. This suggests that higher confining pressure dispersed internal stress concentrations from a single local direction to multiple directions, and the failure mechanism gradually shifted from being dominated by axial stress toward joint control by axial stress and confining pressure. Evolution of peak strain

Figure 15 shows the peak strains of sandstone specimens under different confining pressures and acid-corrosion durations. Physical meaning of model parameters and calibration boundaries

The model parameters are condition dependent. kc is influenced by solution chemistry, temperature, transport conditions, and soluble-mineral content; m and ε0 depend on confining pressure, lithology, and the initial-defect population; and ν affects the confinement contribution through 2νσ3. In this study, kc was fitted to the available 0- to 20-day modulus-retention trend, whereas m and ε0 were calibrated from the uncorroded 20 MPa pre-peak curve. Sensitivity expressions and the calibration formulas are provided in STAR Methods. These parameters must be recalibrated for other rocks, solutions, pressure ranges, or long-term field conditions.

Discussion

Comparison with previous studies and contributions of this study

Current research on acid-corroded rocks mainly follows three mainstream lines. The first compares rock strength degradation and energy evolution by controlling variables such as solution pH, environmental temperature, and corrosion-cycle number. The second focuses on fractured rock masses and constructs damage constitutive models coupling macroscopic mechanical responses and mesoscopic damage evolution. The third improves the theoretical framework of rock chemo-mechanical coupling from the perspectives of mineral reaction, solute transport, and fracture propagation mechanisms. Existing conclusions indicate a basic consensus: mineral dissolution caused by acid solutions leads to different degrees of decrease in the elastic modulus and peak strength of rocks, lowers the crack initiation stress level, and advances crack initiation. Comparison between recent studies on acid-corroded rocks and the present work

Compared with previous studies, the core academic contribution of this work is not merely to verify again the weakening effect of acid corrosion on the mechanical properties of sandstone. Instead, under test conditions with constant acid type and target pH, this study systematically reveals the time-evolution path of rock properties over a 0- to 20-day corrosion period and connects XRD phase analysis, solution ion concentration testing, SEM mesoscopic observation, characteristic stress evolution, measured peak strength, and constitutive model construction into an integrated analytical framework. In particular, a common-slope model and a nested F-test statistical method are introduced to quantitatively distinguish two evolution modes of the strength envelope: overall downward translation and systematic change in confining-pressure sensitivity. This avoids the common research pitfall of overinterpreting the time evolution of internal friction angle and cohesion solely on the basis of high coefficients of determination obtained from three-point linear regressions.

Evidence-bounded interpretation of multiscale damage evolution

Figure 17 summarizes the evidence-bounded multiscale interpretation. Together, the observations support a bounded multiscale interpretation rather than a directly demonstrated damage sequence. The endpoint increases in Ca2+ and Mg2+, and the XRD trends are consistent with carbonate dissolution. In the selected SEM fields, larger and more irregular dark regions and locally less continuous cement-like material are consistent with surface alteration and possible local cement loss. These features could reduce local contact stiffness and facilitate crack initiation, but the present data do not measure particle-contact area, three-dimensional connectivity, or force-chain topology. Reaction-chemo-mechanical models reported in previous studies provide a plausible conceptual basis for coupling mineral removal and microcrack development; applying that mechanism to the present sandstone remains a hypothesis. Terms such as “pore-connectivity transition,” “degradation of load-transfer paths,” and “critical instability” are therefore used only as hypotheses, linking the observed surface morphology with the mechanical response. The available SEM images cannot establish bulk-connected porosity, three-dimensional contact area, or force-chain topology. Verification would require multiple independent fields, micro-CT or NMR, pore-network measurements, differential interference contrast (DIC), and/or grain-scale numerical analysis.Figure 17Conceptual illustration of the possible multiscale alteration pathways in acid-exposed sandstone

Conceptual illustration of the possible multiscale alteration pathways in acid-exposed sandstone

The quantitative 2D SEM descriptors suggest a possible two-part morphological path within the selected fields. From 0 to 5 days, the dark-area fraction increased by about 55%, the number of connected regions decreased by about 75%, and the area contribution of regions ≥200 px2 exceeded 96%, consistent with coalescence or enlargement of surface depressions. From 5 to 20 days, the area fraction varied within a narrower range while D90, D95, and the large-diameter tail generally increased. A plausible interpretation is the coexistence of continuing enlargement of large dissolution depressions, local wall detachment, surface rearrangement, and secondary small-pit formation. This interpretation remains conditional because the images are single fields acquired under nonidentical imaging conditions. The specimen-level peak-strength data do not establish statistically distinct rapid-slow-accelerated degradation stages. Differences between adjacent exposure durations may reflect cumulative chemical alteration together with specimen heterogeneity. Endpoint chemistry and single-field SEM cannot resolve time-dependent reaction kinetics or a connectivity threshold. Accordingly, the 0- to 20-day results are interpreted as an overall deterioration trend with condition-specific fluctuations; any staged mechanism remains a hypothesis requiring time-resolved chemistry, replicated mechanical tests, and three-dimensional pore characterization.

Overall, the decreases in calcite and dolomite and the increases in dissolved Ca2+ and Mg2+ are consistent with cumulative carbonate dissolution during 0–20 days of acid exposure. The SEM observations and quantitative 2D descriptors indicate enlargement and redistribution of dark surface regions, but they do not establish three-dimensional pore connectivity or degradation of load-transfer paths.

Possible competition between confinement and acid-exposure weakening

Confinement and acid exposure have opposing observed effects on sandstone strength. Confining pressure tends to close cracks, increase normal contact forces, and restrict dilatancy, thereby increasing peak strength. Acid exposure is associated with the dissolution of susceptible minerals and weakening of cemented contacts. After 20 days, peak strength decreased by 33.3%, 26.6%, and 29.9% at confining pressures of 20, 30, and 40 MPa, respectively, while characteristic stresses and elastic moduli also showed overall deterioration. Intermediate differences remain specimen-level trends because only one specimen was tested per condition and should not be interpreted as statistically proven degradation stages. The present measurements do not directly quantify contact stiffness or internal load-transfer paths. The nested comparison gave F(4,5) = 1.284 and p = 0.388; under the reduced-model common-slope simplification, the slope was 3.0972 (95% CI, 2.5785–3.6159), and apparent cohesion decreased by 41.0%. These results support an overall downward shift of the strength envelope but do not demonstrate slope equality or a constant internal friction angle. Possible changes in confining-pressure sensitivity remain unresolved because of the limited pressure range, low residual degrees of freedom, and lack of replicates. The observed macroscopic failure patterns are consistent with a qualitative competition between confinement and exposure-related weakening. Confinement can suppress crack opening and promote more distributed failure, whereas chemically altered interfaces may reduce local crack-initiation resistance. Because internal crack paths and contact forces were not measured in situ, the relative roles of these processes cannot be quantified from the present tests and should not be converted directly into an equivalent confining pressure or burial depth.

Model applicability and uncertainty

Advantages and limitations of the constitutive model

Most existing constitutive studies of chemically corroded rocks use solution pH, porosity, or mass-loss ratio as quantitative indicators of final-state damage. In this study, the elastic modulus degradation process is formulated as an explicit time function and coupled with a mechanical damage model based on the Weibull distribution, enabling separate characterization of chemical damage accumulated before loading and mechanical damage induced during loading. Chen et al.16 and Liu et al.17 verified the feasibility of combining the Lemaitre equivalent strain assumption with a statistical damage framework under thermo-chemical coupling conditions and in fractured sandstone specimens, respectively. Tang and Hu18,19 emphasized, from the mechanistic perspective of reactive transport, the bidirectional feedback between chemical dissolution and microcrack development. In comparison, the advantage of the present model is that it uses only a small number of physically meaningful parameters to describe the influence of corrosion duration on pre-peak stiffness and strength evolution. However, the model does not explicitly solve reactant transport, pore-connectivity evolution, or the feedback effect of the stress field on reaction rate.

Across all 15 tested conditions, the time-dependent chemo-mechanical model reproduced the principal pre-peak responses, with R2 values of 0.767–0.961 and NRMSE values of 6.67%–12.30%. Its present scope is limited to pre-peak stiffness degradation, progressive damage, and peak-strength attenuation. Post-peak instability, residual strength, other lithologies and solutions, and long-term field prediction require additional experiments and recalibration.

The applicability limitations of the model should also be recognized. First, the Weibull formulation and the assumption that failed microelements immediately lose all load-bearing capacity provide an idealized representation of post-peak behavior; actual specimens can retain strength through confinement and friction along rough fracture surfaces. Second, the chemical damage function was fitted over 0–20 days and does not include acid depletion, diffusion control, or secondary precipitation, so it cannot be extrapolated without constraint to multi-year corrosion. The parameters were calibrated mainly from the 20 MPa data and assessed in-sample across the available pressure range; parameter identification should be repeated and independently tested for cross-pressure applications. Long-term monitoring, independent validation, and back analysis remain necessary for reliable time-dependent parameters.20

To improve the model description of post-peak behavior, residual friction parameters can be introduced in future work, and a friction-degradation function related to plastic strain can be added to the effective-stress expression in Equation 20:(Equation 20)σ=(1−D)Eε+σrexp(−βεpε0)where, σr is the residual strength parameter, β is the friction degradation coefficient, and εp is the plastic strain. This form can realize a gradual post-peak stress decrease without disrupting the underlying damage-evolution framework. The influence of acid immersion duration on residual strength can also be further quantified, for example, by establishing an empirical relation between σr and time, so that the model can completely describe the whole mechanical behavior of acid-corroded sandstone from pre-peak damage to the post-peak residual stage.

Limitations of the data and statistical interpretation

The nested model has limited power: each time-specific regression uses three pressure points and one residual degree of freedom, and the full interaction model has five residual degrees of freedom. A nonsignificant interaction can therefore reflect either genuinely small slope differences or insufficient information to detect them. Future designs should include additional low and intermediate pressures (for example, 0, 5, 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 MPa) and at least three replicate specimens per condition. In this study, only three confining-pressure levels, 20, 30, and 40 MPa, were set for each corrosion duration, and the residual degree of freedom of the time-specific Mohr-Coulomb regression was only 1. According to the current matrix, each corrosion duration-confining pressure combinations had only one representative specimen. Therefore, a high R2 only indicates approximate linearity of the data within the range of 20–40 MPa and does not imply high statistical precision for c and φ. The nested F-test and Monte Carlo intervals can restrict overinterpretation, but they cannot replace parallel tests.

In addition, the Mohr-Coulomb intercept is extrapolated from the 20–40 MPa confining-pressure range to σ3 = 0 and should be referred to as a Mohr-Coulomb (MC) extrapolated intercept or apparent uniaxial strength. It cannot be equated with uniaxial compressive strength (UCS) obtained from direct uniaxial tests. Future studies should add low and intermediate confining-pressure points and set no fewer than three parallel specimens for each condition to identify the time evolution of c, φ, and confining-pressure sensitivity more reliably. Recent studies on chemically corroded rocks also commonly emphasize that uncertainties in the inverse estimation of c and φ should be reduced through multiple confining-pressure points, repeated tests, and comparisons among multiple strength criteria.16,21 Therefore, in this study, the Mohr-Coulomb parameters are positioned as apparent parameters within a given confining-pressure range rather than as the only criterion for long-term rock-mass strength.

Engineering implications

The directly supported engineering conclusion is limited to the tested fine-grained sandstone, dilute sulfuric acid exposure, 0- to 20-day duration, and 20–40 MPa confining-pressure range. Closed mines affected by acidic mine water and acid-exposed sandstone formations are the closest application settings, but quantitative use requires site-specific calibration for mineralogy, water chemistry, seepage and recharge, temperature, in situ stress, and exposure history. CO2 storage and geothermal development are considered only as analogy-based chemo-mechanical settings because their fluids, temperatures, pressures, transport processes, and precipitation reactions differ substantially. The present 20-day immersion results should not be linearly scaled to multi-year service life. Based on the aforementioned conclusions, in engineering analysis, the confining pressure in laboratory triaxial tests should not be directly equated with formation burial depth or support resistance, and the degradation laws obtained from short-term tests should not be simply extrapolated linearly to cover the entire service life of a mine. In practical applications, corresponding scale conversion and parameter correction should be completed in combination with actual conditions such as in situ stress level, acid-solution recharge rate, seepage conditions, and formation temperature.

High-sulfur coalfields are widely distributed in China. Long-term acidification of water accumulated in abandoned goafs can both increase the risk of mine water hazards and continuously corrode roof sandstone water-resisting strata. The findings of this study indicate that, for mining areas affected by AMD, long-term stability evaluations such as overburden movement after mine closure and safety assessment of water-resisting strata should not directly use rock mechanical parameters measured at the early exploration stage.

When conducting engineering stability analysis, it is necessary to incorporate the time dimension and comprehensively characterize the long-term evolution of rock mechanical parameters in combination with acid-solution recharge conditions, seepage characteristics, environmental temperature, and in situ stress state. The constitutive model established in this study can provide theoretical support for stability analysis of water-resisting strata in closed mines, rock-mass assessment in areas affected by acidic groundwater, and time-dependent stability calculation of deep roadways. However, long-term parameter extrapolation still requires calibration using longer duration laboratory tests and field monitoring data. Such structural-integrity problems induced by chemo-mechanical coupling also exist in engineering fields, such as geothermal development and CO2 geological storage. The acidic environment formed when CO2 dissolves in formation water, together with salt precipitation, can significantly change the elastic parameters and local stress distribution of sandstone.22 This also indicates that engineering risks arising from the coupling among chemical environment, pore structure, and mechanical response have common features across different underground engineering scenarios.

Overall, acid corrosion significantly changes the deformation laws and failure modes of underground engineering rock masses. For long-term stability evaluation of rock masses in acidic-water environments, the traditional purely stress-controlled analytical framework should be expanded to a time-dependent chemo-mechanical coupled analysis system. At the same time, engineering applications should pay attention to applicability boundaries and avoid unconstrained extrapolation beyond the corrosion duration and confining-pressure range of the present tests.

Experimental model and study participant details

The experimental material was bluish-gray fine-grained sandstone obtained from intact rock blocks. No human participants, animals, cell lines, or biological model organisms were involved.

Method details

Test materials and specimen preparation

Sandstone is one of the main lithologies in coal-measure overburden. Bluish-gray fine-grained sandstone was cored from intact blocks and processed into right circular cylinders with nominal diameter 50 mm and height 100 mm (height-to-diameter ratio 2.0; Figure S4). Diameter and height deviations were controlled within ±0.3 mm, maximum end-face unevenness within ±0.05 mm, and end-face perpendicularity deviation below 0.25°. Specimens with visible macroscopic fractures or anomalous ultrasonic velocities were excluded. Pre-test P-wave velocity, S-wave velocity, and density were recorded for consistency assessment (Table 1).

Test equipment

The tests were performed using a YSC-1000 computer-controlled servo rock triaxial testing machine. The equipment is located at the National Engineering Research Center of Coal Mine Water Hazard Prevention and Control and Water Resource Protection and is a comprehensive testing platform capable of simulating various environmental conditions and conducting multiphysics-coupled rock tests.

Acid corrosion treatment and microstructural characterization

The immersion solution was prepared from sulfuric acid (H2SO4) and distilled water, with a nominal target pH of 3.6; an independently measured initial value of 3.8 is reported in Table 2. The simplified solution was selected to isolate proton-sulfate attack and did not reproduce the Fe, Al, Mn, ionic-strength, redox, complexation, or precipitation processes of multicomponent field AMD. Specimens were exposed for 0, 5, 10, 15, or 20 days. The available historical record did not document solution volume, water/rock ratio, replacement schedule, temperature, sealing condition, or intermediate pH monitoring; these missing metadata are reported rather than inferred.

Microstructural characterization included XRD and SEM tests, which were used to analyze changes in mineral composition and surface morphology under different corrosion durations. In addition, inorganic ion analysis was conducted on the initial acid solution and the 20 d immersion solution to assist in identifying the main mineral reaction pathways.

SEM image processing

Each of the five available ×200 SEM images was linearly stretched over its 1st-99th grayscale percentile range to 0–255 and denoized with a 3 × 3 Gaussian filter. A black-hat transform using a 31 × 31-pixel elliptical structuring element was used to extract locally dark features. Pixels were classified as dark regions when the black-hat response was ≥25 and the normalized grayscale was ≤90. Connected components smaller than 16 px2 were removed. The remaining regions were used to calculate 2D dark-area fraction, count, equivalent-diameter percentiles, CCDFs, and large-region area contribution.

Parameter sensitivity was assessed over black-hat thresholds 18–30, absolute-darkness thresholds 80–100, and minimum component areas 8–64 px2. The principal sequence (lower at 0 day, higher at 5 days, followed by smaller fluctuations) remained stable within this range. Mean gray level, P95-P5 contrast, and Laplacian variance were also calculated (Table 3). Because one field was available per time and the 15 and 20 days images had lower quality, the analysis is descriptive and two-dimensional.

Triaxial compression test scheme

Five corrosion durations (0, 5, 10, 15, and 20 days) and three confining pressures (20, 30, and 40 MPa) formed 15 conditions (Figure S5). One representative specimen was assigned to each condition. Before loading, the platen was brought close to the specimen and a 1 kN preload was applied at 0.1 kN/s. After sealing the chamber, confining pressure was increased at 0.05 MPa/s to the target value. Following stabilization, axial displacement was applied at 0.02 mm/min until failure. Axial load/stress, axial and radial displacement/strain, circumferential strain, and confining pressure were recorded at 1 Hz. The one-specimen design supports descriptive trend analysis but not within-condition uncertainty estimation.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The crack initiation stress (σci) and crack damage stress (σcd) were determined using the volumetric strain method; that is, the corresponding stress thresholds were identified from characteristic turning points on the volumetric strain-axial strain curves. The peak strength was taken as the maximum axial stress of each stress-strain curve.

The peak strength was described using the linear Mohr-Coulomb relation in Equation 1, where k is the slope of the envelope and σcs is the intercept extrapolated to σ3 = 0. The internal friction angle and cohesion were calculated as φ = arcsin[(k - 1)/(k + 1)] and c = σcs/(2k0.5), respectively. Because the lowest confining pressure was 20 MPa, σcs is only an MC extrapolated intercept and is not equivalent to the measured uniaxial compressive strength.

To distinguish between the overall translation and slope change of the envelope caused by acid corrosion, time-specific independent regression models, a common-slope model for all times, and a time-confining pressure interaction model were established. A nested F test was used to determine the significance of the interaction term (significance level α = 0.05). Parameter intervals were estimated through Monte Carlo propagation based on the pooled residual variance and parameter covariance.
