Improving Top-Down Sequence Coverage with Targeted Fragment Matching
Authors: Matthew
T. Robey, Kenneth R. Durbin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Top-down mass spectrometry (TDMS) of intact proteins and antibodies enables direct determination of truncations, sequence variants, post-translational modifications, and disulfides without the need for any proteolytic cleavage. While mass deconvolution of top-down tandem mass spectra is typically used to identify fragment masses for matching to candidate proteoforms, larger molecules such as monoclonal antibodies can produce many fragment ions, making spectral interpretation challenging. Here, we explore an alternative approach for proteoform spectral matching that is better suited for larger protein analysis. This workflow uses direct matching of theoretical proteoform isotopic distributions to TDMS spectra, avoiding drawbacks of mass deconvolution such as poor sensitivity and problems differentiating overlapping distributions. Using a data set that analyzed an intact NIST monoclonal antibody across different fragmentation modes, we show that this isotope fitting strategy increased the sequence coverage of both light and heavy chain sequences >3-fold. We further found that isotope fitting is particularly amenable to identifying large fragments, including those near the hinge region that have been traditionally difficult to analyze by top-down methods. These advances in proteoform spectral matching can greatly increase the power of top-down analyses for intact biotherapeutics and other large molecules.

Introduction

Top-down mass spectrometry (TDMS) has become a powerful tool for characterizing intact proteins and antibodies and is becoming increasingly important in academia and the biopharmaceutical industry. In contrast to the widespread peptide mapping approach, TDMS allows for precise determination and quantitation of the specific proteoforms (i.e., sequence and modification variants) present in samples. Despite these advantages, TDMS fragmentation spectra can be substantially more complex, making data interpretation challenging.

Interpreting TDMS spectra typically involves mass deconvolution, which simplifies spectra containing multiple isotopes and charge states into neutral fragment masses for comparison to potential proteoforms. The ProSight search engine1 uses Xtract (Thermo Fisher Scientific) or the THRASH algorithm2 before matching fragment masses to theoretical fragments of proteoforms populated with possible PTMs and sequence variants. MSPathFinder3 similarly uses an algorithm based on THRASH for deconvolution, followed by a sequence graph approach for proteoform matching. MS-Align+4 and TopPIC5 perform mass deconvolution using MS-Deconv, then use spectral alignment to match fragment masses to candidate proteoforms.

While these deconvolution approaches have been useful for TDMS, deconvolution can fail to detect fragments that are low abundance or whose isotopes are obscured by overlapping signals in complex spectra. An alternative strategy to mass deconvolution is to directly compare theoretical ions from candidate proteoforms to fragmentation spectra, avoiding pitfalls of an untargeted deconvolution approach (Figure 1). We recently introduced an isotope fitting-based spectral matching software for single spectra in TDValidator6 that uses candidate proteoforms to create theoretical fragment isotope distributions, which are then compared to experimental distributions using a least-squares regression model. Though additional tools have also been introduced for visualizing a fragmentation spectrum with theoretical proteoform isotope distributions,3,7,8 the isotope fitting approach has not previously been compared side-by-side with mass deconvolution or applied to higher-throughput intact monoclonal antibody (mAb) workflows.

Comparison of conventional deconvolution versus isotope fitting approaches for spectral matching. (A) In the deconvolution approach, a raw spectrum is converted to neutral fragment masses before being matched to candidate proteoforms. (B) In the isotope fitting approach, theoretical isotope distributions from candidate proteoforms are directly compared to experimental spectra without deconvolution.

Here, we evaluate isotope fitting as an alternative to conventional mass deconvolution for targeted TDMS analyses. By analyzing an existing data set of intact NIST mAb with a variety of fragmentation techniques,9 we show that isotope fitting can triple sequence coverage. While ensemble TDMS experiments typically yield fragments <10 kDa,10 we show that isotope fitting can improve detection of large fragments spanning the interior heavy chain region. These improvements to TDMS spectral matching sharply increase the value that can be obtained from top-down spectra.

Experimental Section

Top-Down Data Set

For the algorithmic comparisons, we used an existing data set of intact NIST monoclonal antibody fragmentation spectra (MASSIVE MSV000086331).9 This data set includes targeted MS2 direct infusion data files, each containing different fragmentation settings from HCD, EThcD, ETD, or activated ion electron transfer dissociation (AI-ETD) methods. For consistency across comparisons, we only included files targeting the 48+ charge state, resulting in a total of 128 data files.

Proteoform Spectral Matching Using Mass Deconvolution

Data files were processed using a new workflow in ProSight Native1 that batch processes top-down fragmentation spectra. For every data file, each targeted MS2 centroid spectrum was used for spectral matching. For deconvolution, spectra (adjusted by the calibration routine below) were processed using a modified THRASH algorithm,2 allowing for fragment masses from 200 to 50 000 Da with a minimum signal-to-noise of 3 and a 0.55 minimum reliability value quality score. Deconvolved masses were matched to candidate proteoforms using the existing ProSight Lite spectral matcher with a 5 ppm tolerance. We considered the full-length light and heavy chain NIST mAb sequences with half-cystines to account for disulfide bonds.

Isotope Fitting-Based Spectral Matching

After mass deconvolution and ProSight Lite spectral matching, proteoform candidates were directly compared to spectra using isotope fitting. We calculated theoretical isotope distributions for the light and heavy chain using the Mercury7 algorithm,11 considering only the 99.99% most intense peaks from each distribution. Theoretical isotope distributions were first compared to each spectrum by identifying peaks that matched the two most abundant theoretical peaks within an m/z tolerance of 10 ppm. If matching peaks were found, the theoretical isotope distribution was adjusted by the average m/z error of the two peaks to allow for relative calibration within the distribution. Additional matches were identified with a 3-ppm tolerance. We used the residual sum of squares to represent the error between experimental and theoretical intensities for matched isotope peaks. One minus the square root of this error was reported as the final score. After this initial isotope fitting, we calibrated the spectrum by shifting it to account for the average m/z error of matched fragments. A final fitting step on this calibrated spectrum was performed using narrower tolerances of 5 ppm for the two most abundant peaks and 2.5 ppm for other matches. For both mass deconvolution and isotope fitting, we required spectral matches to have ≥ ten matching fragments.

Results and Discussion

Spectral Matching Using Isotope Fitting

We investigated an alternative top-down fragment matching strategy that uses isotope fitting to directly compare candidate proteoforms to fragmentation spectra without mass deconvolution. To benchmark isotope fitting, we processed 27,000 fragmentation spectra from an intact NIST mAb data set across 128 fragmentation settings. Each spectrum was processed with isotope fitting and the existing ProSight Lite deconvolution approach. To estimate an initial false discovery rate (FDR) for isotope fitting, we examined the data file with the most matching fragments (AI-ETD with 30 ms ETD reaction time and 36W laser power). Using a decoy distribution of 100 randomly scrambled light and heavy chain sequences, we observed a higher proportion of real matching fragments than decoy matching fragments at higher isotope fit scores (Figure 2a). At a score threshold of 0.5 (the default for TDValidator), we observed 15 ± 11 and 19 ± 14 matching fragments from decoy light and heavy chains, respectively. In comparison, the same 0.5 score threshold for the actual light and heavy chain proteoforms produced 82 ± 15 and 155 ± 26 matching fragments, respectively.

Benchmarking an isotope fit score for proteoform spectral matching without deconvolution. (a) Distribution of matching fragments versus a decoy distribution for a single fragmentation spectrum. Decoys were generated by random shuffling of light and heavy chain residues before isotope fit-based matching to the spectrum. Spectral matches with average fit scores >0.68 represent a ∼5% false discovery rate (FDR), with the FDR improving as the score threshold approaches 1. (b) Examples of matching fragment ions at various fit scores. At low scores, experimental isotope distributions poorly match theoretical distributions. At higher scores (0.87), experimental isotope distributions closely match the shape of theoretical distributions.

To estimate the false discovery rate (FDR) for isotope fitting, we also considered decoy versus actual distributions at the spectral match level (Figure S1). For this analysis, we used a fit score threshold of 0.5 for individual fragment ions and computed the mean fit score of resulting matching fragments across each spectral match. This resulted in average fit scores of 0.60 ± 0.04 and 0.61 ± 0.04 for light and heavy chain decoy spectral matches, respectively, compared to 0.69 ± 0.01 and 0.74 ± 0.02 for actual light and heavy chain spectral matches, respectively. For this analysis an average fit score of 0.68 for a spectral match corresponded to a 5% FDR. Thresholds of 0.5 fit score for fragment ions and 0.68 mean fit score for spectral matches were thus used for the remainder of this study. The FDR at these thresholds was slightly higher when separately considering c- and z- ions (14% FDR) or b- and y- ions (8%), which lends additional evidence that b-, y-, c-, and z- ions were all present (Figure S2).

Building on these observations, we also looked at internal ions to determine their prevalence and assess their potential contribution to higher FDR values. Using the TDValidator module in ProSight Native, we found that 52% and 19% of matching fragments were internal ions in the top HCD and ETD spectral matches, respectively (Figure S3). Though further assessments of internal ions were beyond the scope of this study, this finding further suggests that many of the unmatched peaks within each spectrum could be explained by including internal fragment ions.

In comparison, when this decoy analysis was performed using mass deconvolution, we observed 4 ± 3 matching fragments across decoy light and heavy chains, compared to 44 ± 12 and 92 ± 22 matching fragments for the correct light and heavy chains, respectively. To allow side-by-side comparisons to isotope fitting, we also controlled the FDR at 5% for spectral matching via mass deconvolution (Figure S1). Scrambling sequences proved to be a conservative approach, as FDR values for both isotope fitting and mass deconvolution improve to <1% when considering completely randomized decoy sequences instead of sequence scrambling (Figure S4).

We used top-down fragmentation tools in ProSight Native1 for the AI-ETD spectrum above to also visualize matching fragments at various score thresholds (Figure 2b). At lower scores, ions such as the +5 charge state of z95 (fit score of 0.3) had few matching peaks and a shape that poorly fit the theoretical distribution. For ions with midrange scores, such as the 6+ charge state of z94, there are more matching peaks and a closer fit to the theoretical shape. At high scores, such as the c92 4+ charge state (fit score of 0.87), the experimental and theoretical distributions are well-aligned.

Isotope Fitting Increases Sequence Coverage

We used heatmaps to visualize the sequence coverage from isotope fitting versus deconvolution. Each heatmap row represents the top spectral match from the 128 different fragmentation settings and each column indicates an amino acid along the sequences (Figure 3a and 3b). We observed a stark difference with isotope fitting versus deconvolution, with several regions of both chains more densely colored due to increased sequence coverage. Across these spectral matches, the sequence coverage using mass deconvolution was only 13 ± 5% and 13 ± 4% for the light and heavy chain, respectively. Decreasing the reliability value quality score for THRASH mass deconvolution from 0.55 to the minimum allowable 0.01 threshold resulted in a ∼ 30% increase in the average number of detected fragment masses per spectrum, but only marginally increased the sequence coverage by ∼1%. This suggests that lower reliability value thresholds largely increase the number of false positive fragment masses without significant increases to sequence coverage.

Comparing (a) deconvolution versus (b) isotope fitting spectral matching across 27 000 fragmentation spectra and four fragmentation types. While the two approaches show similar fragmentation hotspots, isotope fitting produced much denser sequence coverage. Several fragments were observed across many spectral matches (visible as vertical lines), validating these new fragments observed with isotope fitting. As an example, the top heavy chain match (acquired using AI-ETD with a 120 ms reaction time) showed a 3-fold improvement to sequence coverage in (c) deconvolution versus (d) isotope fitting. Most of this increase in sequence coverage was at the termini.

In comparison, sequence coverage from isotope fitting was increased ∼2-fold to 28 ± 12% and 25 ± 8% for the light and heavy chain, respectively. For the heavy chain spectral match with the highest sequence coverage, we observed only 13% coverage using deconvolution (Figure 3c) but a 3-fold increase to 39% using isotope fitting (Figure 3d). Isotope fitting likewise increased light chain sequence coverage 2.5-fold for this spectrum (Figure S5). Using isotope fitting, we noted similar >1.5-fold improvements in sequence coverage when comparing the isotope fitting results to Xtract (Thermo Fisher Scientific), FLASHDeconv,12 or MS-Deconv13 determined masses (Figures S6 and S7).

We additionally observed improvements to the N-terminal variable regions (residues 1–110) which are important for antigen binding. Using mass deconvolution, we observed sequence coverage of 21 ± 9% and 23 ± 8% for the light and heavy chain variable regions, respectively. Using isotope fitting, this sequence coverage was improved to 42 ± 21% and 39 ± 17% coverage for light and heavy chain variable regions, respectively.

Isotope Fitting Improves Detection of Large Fragments

For intact antibody chains, detecting large fragments is crucial for obtaining sequence coverage across the entire proteins. However, large fragments can be difficult to deconvolve due to multiple charges that decrease the intensity. Using isotope fitting, we observed an increase in the number of large matching fragments (Figure S8). Across the 128 top spectral matches described above, the mass of matching fragments was 6,917 ± 5016 Da using mass deconvolution, compared to 8,478 ± 4688 Da using isotope fitting.

Isotope fitting is beneficial for gentle fragmentation settings that favor large fragments. For one heavy chain spectral match (AI-ETD using 20 W activation and a minimal 5 ms reaction time), out of 125 total matching fragments by isotope fitting, 77 matches (62%) had a mass >10 kDa (Figure S9). At one residue pair in the hinge region (Arg258-Thr259), we observed matching fragments in both y- and z- fragmentation channels (Figure S9). While both fragments were low intensity compared to other fragments (9.5 signal-to-noise for z191 and 18.2 signal-to-noise for y191), these two fragments were verified across multiple charge states (Figure S10).

Conclusions

As TDMS becomes routinely used for large molecule characterization, algorithmic refinement is needed to ensure that the most information can be obtained from each fragmentation spectrum. We thus present an alternative approach for spectral matching that forgoes the typical mass deconvolution step, instead directly comparing candidate proteoforms to spectra via their theoretical isotopic distributions. We show that isotope fitting can significantly increase the sequence coverage of large proteins such as antibodies without increasing the FDR. Furthermore, we show isotopic fitting can be used for more than single spectra analysis by leveraging the method in batch mode for high-throughput analyses on larger top-down data sets. Altogether, these and future improvements to protein fragmentation data analyses will serve to better establish the role of TDMS in defining proteoform landscapes for biotherapeutics.
