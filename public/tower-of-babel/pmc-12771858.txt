Transient orbital apex syndrome with magnetic resonance imaging evidence following sub-Tenon’s ropivacaine anesthesia during combined cataract and macular surgery: a case report
Authors: Xiang Cao, Zhencheng Ge, Yiqing Gong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough sub-Tenon’s anesthesia (STA) is a widely accepted regional anesthesia technique in ophthalmic surgery, we report a rare case of transient orbital apex syndrome (OAS) following its administration using ropivacaine during combined cataract and macular surgery.Case presentationWe describe the clinical course of a 71-year-old male who developed acute postoperative vision loss, ptosis, ophthalmoplegia, and mydriasis in the operated eye following sub-Tenon’s ropivacaine injection. Orbital MRI revealed mild perineural fluid surrounding the optic nerve and localized edema of the retrobulbar fat, without evidence of compressive lesions or optic nerve thickening. The patient received supportive therapy, including oxygen, neurotrophic agents, and osmotic and microcirculation enhancers. Remarkably, all neurological signs resolved within 24 h, and visual acuity improved to 0.15.ConclusionAlthough STA is considered a safer alternative to needle-based techniques, this case highlights the potential for rare but reversible complications such as OAS. Early recognition and timely, supportive management contributed to the patient’s favorable recovery, although outcomes may vary in other cases. In addition, thorough preoperative counseling and vigilant postoperative monitoring are essential, particularly in elderly or high-risk patients, to ensure prompt detection and management of any unexpected complications.Keywords: Sub-Tenon’s anesthesia, Ropivacaine, Orbital apex syndrome, Transient vision loss

Introduction

Sub-Tenon’s anesthesia (STA) is a widely accepted regional anesthesia technique in ophthalmic surgery, particularly for cataract and vitreoretinal procedures, due to its efficacy and lower risk profile compared to retrobulbar or peribulbar injections [1, 2]. Orbital apex syndrome (OAS), characterized by simultaneous dysfunction of cranial nerves II, III, IV, VI, and the ophthalmic division of V, is most commonly associated with tumors, inflammation, or trauma [3, 4]. OAS secondary to STA has not been well documented and remains a theoretical but plausible complication due to its proximity to orbital apex structures. We present a case of transient orbital apex-like syndrome following sub-Tenon’s ropivacaine anesthesia, with direct evidence of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

Case presentation

A 71-year-old male with a history of hypertension, bilateral epiretinal membrane (ERM), senile cataract, and high myopia was admitted for scheduled right eye phacoemulsification combined with pars plana vitrectomy for ERM and internal limiting membrane (ILM) peeling. Preoperative best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) in the right eye was 0.2.

STA was performed by an experienced consultant ophthalmologist. Following topical anesthesia, a small conjunctival incision (approximately 2 mm) was made in the inferonasal quadrant, approximately 5 mm from the limbus. A blunt, curved metal cannula was introduced through the incision and advanced gently along the scleral surface to the equatorial region of the globe. A total of 4 mL of 0.75% ropivacaine (without hyaluronidase) was injected into the sub-Tenon’s space without ocular compression afterward. No unusual resistance or complications were encountered during the block.

The surgery proceeded uneventfully. However, one hour after surgery, the patient reported a sudden and complete loss of vision in the operated (right) eye. Clinical examination revealed no light perception, accompanied by complete ptosis of the right upper eyelid. The pupil was fixed, dilated to approximately 5 mm, and nonreactive to light. Ocular motility was absent in all directions, indicating total ophthalmoplegia. Slit-lamp examination showed mild subconjunctival hemorrhage. Fundus examination revealed a flat retina without hemorrhages or detachment. Intraocular pressure was within normal limits at 13 mmHg (1 mmHg = 0.133 kPa). These findings suggested an acute orbital process involving the optic nerve and multiple cranial nerves. The MRI demonstrated subtle perineural hyperintensity along the optic nerve sheath complex on T2-weighted sequences, consistent with transient perioptic fluid accumulation. Focal edema was observed in the retrobulbar adipose tissue, manifesting as a hyperintense signal in the superolateral quadrant of the right posterior orbital space (Fig. 1). There was no evidence of optic nerve thickening, mass lesion, or intracranial abnormalities. The absence of radiological signs of compressive pathology or direct optic nerve damage supports a transient, non-structural etiology—most likely toxic or ischemic neuropathy secondary to inadvertent posterior diffusion of local anesthetic.

Supportive treatment was initiated immediately after recognition of the OAS, including high-flow oxygen, intravenous mannitol to reduce orbital edema, Danshen (Salvia miltiorrhiza Burge) to improve microcirculation [5, 6], and neurotrophic agents (mecobalamin and mouse nerve growth factor) to support neural recovery [7, 8]. Topical dexamethasone was applied to control local inflammation. These interventions were implemented without delay, and an MRI was performed in parallel, ensuring that imaging did not compromise the timeliness of treatment.

Approximately 16 h postoperatively, the patient reported subjective improvement, initially describing the return of light perception in the right eye. This was followed by progressive restoration of visual function. On examination, upper eyelid ptosis had resolved, the pupil exhibited a normal reactive response to light, and full ocular motility had been regained. These findings indicated a rapid and near-complete neurological recovery, suggesting a transient and reversible neuropraxic event likely caused by local anesthetic diffusion or orbital apex pressure. At 20 h postoperatively, BCVA had improved to 0.15, with no residual ptosis or extraocular muscle restriction observed.

Discussion

Compared to intraoperative transient LP loss [9, 10], prolonged postoperative NLP is far less reported in the literature. Our case is distinct in that the patient developed complete vision loss, ptosis, ophthalmoplegia, and non-reactive mydriasis postoperatively-yet recovered within 24 h, indicating a transient neuropraxia rather than permanent nerve injury. This constellation resembles transient OAS, though structural causes were excluded by MRI.

Selecting an appropriate anesthetic technique and agent for ophthalmic surgery is essential for ophthalmologists, anesthesiologists, and patients, as it directly influences surgical outcomes, patient safety, and perioperative experience [11]. STA is considered safer and more widely preferred compared to peribulbar and retrobulbar blocks [11]. Ropivacaine, although generally considered less neurotoxic than bupivacaine or lidocaine, may still pose a risk when used at high doses or with prolonged exposure [12, 13]. Acute postoperative vision loss is most commonly associated with complications such as retrobulbar hemorrhage, central retinal artery occlusion (CRAO), or optic nerve infarction, all of which typically result in irreversible visual impairment [1, 14, 15]. In contrast, the transient vision loss observed in this case suggests an alternative mechanism—likely posterior diffusion of local anesthetic into the optic canal or orbital apex, resulting in transient cranial nerve compression or ischemia. Though Sub-Tenon’s block avoids sharp needles, improper cannula placement or excessive injection volume may still lead to deep orbital spread. This may lead to temporary blockade of the optic nerve and cranial nerves III, IV, and VI, thereby producing a reversible OAS-like presentation [4]. The presence of mildly increased perineural fluid signals on orbital MRI further supports this hypothesis.

Although MRI is not routinely required in every case, in this instance, it was performed promptly and alongside supportive therapy, ensuring no delay in the initiation of treatment. The imaging findings were crucial in ruling out compressive or vascular etiologies, such as retrobulbar hemorrhage or central retinal artery occlusion, and provided radiological evidence consistent with transient anesthetic diffusion [16].

Nevertheless, other contributing factors-including the patient’s age, underlying vascular comorbidities, high myopia, perioperative circulatory changes, the complexity of the surgical procedure, and intraoperative fluctuations in intraocular pressure-may also have played a role [9]. Therefore, this complication should be regarded as rare and multifactorial in nature, rather than attributed solely to the anesthetic technique.

Although sub-Tenon anesthesia is generally considered safer than retrobulbar or peribulbar anesthesia, improper injection into the sub-Tenon space may still affect the optic nerve, potentially causing visual disturbances in rare cases [9]. The use of adjuvants, such as hyaluronidase, has been shown to enhance the diffusion of local anesthetics, reduce orbital pressure, and improve the overall quality of anesthesia [17, 18]. However, in this case, hyaluronidase was not incorporated. Its use in STA has been well-documented to accelerate the onset of anesthesia and akinesia, while allowing for lower volumes of anesthetic agents, thereby minimizing complications such as ocular muscle paresis and improving safety during the procedure [17].

While conservative management proved effective in this patient, more complex cases with significant neurological deficits may require a more cautious, individualized approach. This outcome highlights the importance of vigilant postoperative monitoring, particularly in elderly or vasculopathically vulnerable patients. Given the increased susceptibility in elderly patients with underlying cerebrovascular changes, anesthetic volume and technique should be carefully optimized [11]. The use of ropivacaine, which promotes anterior diffusion, and careful injection technique may help minimize posterior spread and reduce the risk of complications.

Conclusion

Sub-Tenon’s ropivacaine anesthesia may, in rare instances, induce transient dysfunction of the optic and cranial nerves, which can mimic OAS. However, this complication should be regarded as rare and multifactorial, likely involving multiple preoperative, intraoperative, and procedural factors. Early recognition and appropriate supportive management contributed to the patient’s favorable recovery, although outcomes may differ in other cases. Clinicians should provide thorough preoperative counseling and maintain close postoperative monitoring to facilitate early detection and management of such complications. Moreover, as such transient events can cause significant anxiety for patients, it is crucial to inform those scheduled for ophthalmic surgery under local anesthesia about these rare but possible intraoperative phenomena to reduce distress and enhance perioperative confidence.
