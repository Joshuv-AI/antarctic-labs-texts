Existence and asymptotic stability of a generic Lotka-Volterra system with nonlinear spatially heterogeneous cross-diffusion
Tianxu Wang, Jiwoon Sim, Hao Wang

Abstract
(Part 1) This article considers a class of Lotka-Volterra systems with multiple nonlinear cross-diffusion, commonly known as prey-taxis models. The existence and stability of classic solutions for such systems with spatially homogeneous sources and taxis have been studied in one- or two-dimensional space, however, the proof is non-trivial for a more general setting with spatially heterogeneous predation functions and taxis coefficient functions in arbitrary dimensions. This study introduces a new weighted L ϵ p L_{\epsilon}^{p} -norm and extends some classical inequalities within this normed space. Coupled energy estimates are employed to establish initial bounds, followed by applying heat kernel properties and an advanced bootstrap process to enhance solution regularity. For stability analysis, we extend LaSalle’s invariance principle to a general L ∞ L^{\infty} setting and utilize it alongside Lyapunov functions to analyze the stability of each possible constant equilibrium. All results are achieved without introducing an extra logistic growth term for predators or imposing smallness conditions on taxis coefficients.

Introduction

In predator-prey interactions, alarm pheromones are crucial in communication between different species. Predators are attracted by signals generated by prey, which may manifest as chemical, acoustic, visual, or other detectable changes
Chivers et al. [1996]
. In ecosystems, non-random foraging strategies are often observed, with populations of predators moving towards areas with rapidly increasing prey density, guided by these signals
Bräucker et al. [1994]
. Such movement is commonly referred to as “prey-taxis".

Kareiva and Odell
Kareiva and Odell [1987]
first proposed the following prototypical Lotka-Volterra system with prey-taxis:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}X=r(X)-f(X,Y)+\delta_{X}\Delta X,

\displaystyle(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,\infty),

(1.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}Y=ef(X,Y)-dY-\nabla\left(H(X,Y)\nabla X\right)+\delta_{Y}\Delta Y,

\displaystyle(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,\infty),

\displaystyle\partial_{\nu}X(x,t)=\partial_{\nu}Y(x,t)=0,

\displaystyle(x,t)\in\partial\Omega\times(0,\infty),

\displaystyle(X(x,0),Y(x,0))=(X_{0}(x),Y_{0}(x)),

\displaystyle x\in\Omega,

where
X
and
Y
represent the population density of prey and predators, respectively. This model has been widely studied in recent years.
Here,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a bounded domain, and
\partial_{\nu}
is the outer normal derivative. The function
r(X)
denotes the growth function for prey,
f(X,Y)
represents predation on prey, and
H(X,Y)
characterizes attraction towards food sources or the inclination towards prey for predators. The parameter
e
is the production efficiency,
\delta_{X}
,
\delta_{Y}
are diffusion coefficients, and
d
is the death rate.

The global existence of solutions to (
1.1
) has been studied under different assumptions regarding the taxis sensitivity function
H
. For example, when
H(X,Y)=h(X)
and
h
has bounded support,
Ainseba et al. [2008]
established the existence and uniqueness of weak solutions using the Schauder fixed point theorem and a duality technique (see also
Bendahmane [2008]
for multi-species case). Classical solutions were further considered in
Tao [2010]
for
n\leq 3
via the contraction mapping principle, alongside
L^{p}
and Schauder estimates. On the other hand, the form
H(X,Y)=\chi h(Y)
is also widely used, where
\chi
is a constant and
h(Y)\leq CY
. With additional logistic growth terms for predators, Wang and Wang
Wang and Wang [2019]
established global existence for
n=2
. When
n\geq 3
, the result holds as long as the carrying capacity for predators and the taxis coefficient
\chi
are sufficiently small. Wu et al.
Wu et al. [2016]
proved global existence and uniform persistence in arbitrary dimensions under a smallness condition on the taxis coefficient. Jin and Wang
Jin and Wang [2017]
achieved global existence and stability of the coexistence steady state without requiring additional logistic growth terms or imposing the aforementioned smallness condition. However, their findings were limited to the case where
n=2
.

As we have mentioned, the global existence of solutions under
H(X,Y)=\chi Y
in (
1.1
) needs extra assumptions, such as introducing logistic growth term on predators, imposing smallness condition on parameters or restricting the dimension
n
. Without these assumptions, blow-up behavior does occur. Existing results along this line mainly focus on the classical Keller-Segel system, which shares the same cross-diffusion term with system (
1.1
):

\displaystyle\partial_{t}X=\Delta X-X+Y,

\displaystyle(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,\infty),

(1.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}Y=\Delta Y-\nabla\left(\chi Y\nabla X\right),\qquad

\displaystyle(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,\infty).

Nagai
Nagai [1995]
proved the existence of blow-up solutions in two dimensions when
||Y_{0}||_{L^{1}(\Omega)}
is above a threshold. This is also supported in
Jin and Wang [2016]
and generalized into higher dimensions in
Winkler [2010]
. To prevent the occurrence of blow-up solutions, sublinear taxis sensitivity functions are considered, namely, the term
\chi Y
is replaced by
\chi h(Y)
in (
1.2
), where
h(z)=O(z^{\alpha})
as
z\to\infty
and
\alpha\leq 1
. Horstmann and Winkler
Horstmann and Winkler [2005]
proved the global existence of the solution when
\alpha<\frac{2}{n}
, and for the case
\alpha>\frac{2}{n}
, blow-up solutions were found with
L^{1}
bounded initial values. When the initial values are allowed to be
C^{1}
functions, the global existence was established when
\alpha<\frac{4}{n+2}

Tao [2013]
. It remains open whether blow-up solutions exist for
\alpha>\frac{4}{n+2}
.

Typically, a three-compartment prey-taxis model is more challenging compared to a one-prey, one-predator model. Guo et al.
Guo and Wang [2019]
studied a relatively simple case where two predators consume a single prey species, with the taxis term depending solely on the gradient of prey. They used semigroup theory to investigate the global existence and boundedness of solutions under the condition of small taxis coefficients. There are also numerous studies focusing on the chemical signal model with one prey taxis term
Ahn and Yoon [2020]
,
Li [2023]
,
Geng et al. [2024]

A more commonly used three-compartment model in ecology is the trophic cascade model which can be seen as an extension of the two-species Lotka-Volterra system described in (
1.1
). Compared to the prey-taxis systems explored in previous studies, the trophic cascade model exhibits more complex coupling structures.
The basic predator-prey dynamics across three trophic levels with a general setting where the predation strategy is allowed to connect with spatial heterogeneity, can be described by the following equations on
\Omega\times(0,\infty)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}X=r(X)-f(X,Y,x)+\delta_{X}\Delta X,

(1.3)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}Z=e_{2}g(Y,Z,x)-d_{2}Z-\nabla(\chi_{2}(x)h(Z)\nabla Y)+\delta_{Z}\Delta Z,

\displaystyle\partial_{\nu}X(x,t)=\partial_{\nu}Y(x,t)=\partial_{\nu}Z(x,t)=0,\qquad(x,t)\in\partial\Omega\times(0,\infty),

In model (

Predation behaviors vary according to spatial influences. For example, within sheltered zones where consumers can evade predators, predators have fewer opportunities and less motivation to search for prey. Therefore, it is necessary to model the taxis coefficient as a function

rather than a constant.

The functions
f(X,Y,x)
and
g(Y,Z,x)
represent predation on producers by consumers and predation on consumers by predators, respectively. These predation functions are inversely proportional to handling time. Since handling time varies significantly across the region, these functions should also take spatial heterogeneity into consideration. Here,

\tau_{2}(x)
represents the handling time for predators on consumers. In this study,

We proceed via Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality. Take

\theta=\frac{n(q-1)(2-\alpha)}{2q+nq(1-\alpha)}\geq 0,

then
\theta<1
is equivalent to

\left(1-\frac{2}{n}\right)q<2-\alpha.

The above inequality holds when
n\leq 2
or when
1\leq q\leq 2-\alpha
. When
n\geq 3
, we have

q<\frac{2-\alpha}{1-\frac{2}{n}}=\frac{4}{3}+\frac{\frac{2}{3}+\frac{2}{n}-\alpha}{1-\frac{2}{n}}+\frac{2}{3n-6}.

We observe that the above inequality holds for
1\leq q<\frac{4}{3}+\nu_{0}
. Now the Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality implies

||\widetilde{H}(\phi)||_{L^{\frac{2q}{2-\alpha}}(\Omega_{1})}^{\frac{2q}{2-\alpha}}\leq c_{2}||\nabla\widetilde{H}(\phi)||_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}^{\frac{2q}{2-\alpha}\theta}||\widetilde{H}(\phi)||_{L^{\frac{2}{2-\alpha}}(\Omega_{1})}^{\frac{2q}{2-\alpha}(1-\theta)}+c_{2}||\widetilde{H}(\phi)||_{L^{\frac{2}{2-\alpha}}(\Omega_{1})}^{\frac{2q}{2-\alpha}}.

By direct calculation, the relation

\frac{2q}{2-\alpha}\theta=\frac{2n(q-1)}{2+n(1-\alpha)}<2

is equivalent to

q<\frac{2}{n}+(2-\alpha)=\frac{4}{3}+\left(\frac{2}{3}+\frac{2}{n}-\alpha\right).

This is true whenever
q\in[1,2-\alpha]\cup[1,\frac{4}{3}+\nu_{0})
. Therefore, by Young’s inequality, there exists
\theta^{\prime}>0
such that

||\widetilde{H}(\phi)||_{L^{\frac{2q}{2-\alpha}}(\Omega_{1})}^{\frac{2q}{2-\alpha}}\leq\epsilon||\nabla\widetilde{H}(\phi)||_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}^{2}+c_{3}||\widetilde{H}(\phi)||_{L^{\frac{2}{2-\alpha}}(\Omega_{1})}^{\theta^{\prime}}+c_{2}||\widetilde{H}(\phi)||_{L^{\frac{2}{2-\alpha}}(\Omega_{1})}^{\frac{2q}{2-\alpha}}.

This implies equation (
2
), and we conclude the proof of the lemma.
∎

Now we introduce a family of weighted
L^{p}
norms, which appears naturally in the a priori estimates.

Definition 2.1
.

For
p\in[1,\infty]
,
t\in[0,\infty)
and
\epsilon>0
, define
L^{p}_{\epsilon}(0,t)
to be the Banach space consisting of all functions in
L^{p}(0,t)
and equipped with the norm

||f||_{L^{p}_{\epsilon}(0,t)}:=e^{-\epsilon t}||e^{\epsilon\cdot}f||_{L^{p}(0,t)}.

For
t=\infty
, we define

||f||_{L^{p}_{\epsilon}(0,\infty)}:=\limsup\limits_{t\to\infty}||f||_{L^{p}_{\epsilon}(0,t)}

and set

L^{p}_{\epsilon}(0,\infty)=\left\{f\in L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(0,\infty):||f||_{L^{p}_{\epsilon}(0,\infty)}<\infty\right\}.

Remark 2.1
.

It’s easy to verify the Hölder’s inequality for
L_{\epsilon}^{p}
norm, i.e.

||fg||_{L^{p}_{\epsilon}(0,t)}\leq||f||_{L^{p_{1}}_{\epsilon_{1}}(0,t)}||g||_{L^{p_{2}}_{\epsilon_{2}}(0,t)},

where
\epsilon=\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}
,
\frac{1}{p}=\frac{1}{p_{1}}+\frac{1}{p_{2}}
, and
t\in[0,\infty]
.

We now show the boundedness of a class of convolution operators on the space
L^{p}_{\epsilon}(0,t)
based on Young’s convolution inequality.

Proposition 2.1
.

Define an operator
T_{\rho,\lambda}
by

T_{\rho,\lambda}\phi(t)=\int_{0}^{t}(1+(t-s)^{-\rho})e^{-\lambda(t-s)}\phi(s)\mathrm{d}s,

(2.2)

where
\rho\in(0,1)
and
\lambda>0
. Suppose

1\leq p\leq q\leq\infty\quad\text{and }\quad\frac{1}{p}+\rho<\frac{1}{q}+1,

(2.3)

then for any
t\in[0,\infty]
and
0<\epsilon<\lambda
,
T_{\rho,\lambda}
is a bounded operator from
L_{\epsilon}^{p}(0,t)
to
L^{q}_{\epsilon}(0,t)
, and the bound is uniform in
t
.

We first prove that the operator
T_{\rho,\lambda}
is uniformly bounded from
L^{p}(0,t)
to
L^{q}(0,t)
. Fix
t\in[0,\infty]
and
\phi\in L^{p}(0,t)
. For
s\in\mathbb{R}
, define
\widetilde{\phi}(s)=\phi(s)\text{1}_{(0,t)}(s)
and
\widetilde{\psi}(s)=(1+s^{-\rho})e^{-\lambda s}\text{1}_{(0,t)}(s)
. Then we have
T_{\rho,\lambda}\phi(s)=\widetilde{\phi}*\widetilde{\psi}(s)
for
s\in(0,t)
. Let
r
be a real number such that

\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{r}=\frac{1}{q}+1,

then from (
2.3
) we know that
1\leq r<\frac{1}{\rho}
. It follows that

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\psi}\|_{L^{r}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle\leq\|e^{-\lambda s}\|_{L^{r}(0,t)}+\|s^{-\rho}e^{-\lambda s}\|_{L^{r}(0,t)}

\displaystyle\leq(r\lambda)^{-\frac{1}{r}}+(r\lambda)^{\rho-\frac{1}{r}}\Gamma(1-\rho r)^{\frac{1}{r}}=:c<\infty.

By Young’s convolution inequality, we get

\|T_{\rho,\lambda}\phi\|_{L^{q}(0,t)}\leq\|\widetilde{\phi}*\widetilde{\psi}\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\|\widetilde{\phi}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}\|\widetilde{\psi}\|_{L^{r}(\mathbb{R})}\leq c\|\phi\|_{L^{p}(0,t)}.

(2.4)

Now, suppose
t<\infty
and
\phi\in L^{p}_{\epsilon}(0,t)
. By definitions of the operator
T_{\rho,\lambda}
and
L^{q}_{\epsilon}
norm, we have

\displaystyle||T_{\rho,\lambda}\phi||_{L^{q}_{\epsilon}(0,t)}

\displaystyle=e^{-\epsilon t}\left\|e^{\epsilon r}\int_{0}^{r}(1+(r-s)^{-\rho})e^{-\lambda(r-s)}\phi(s)\mathrm{d}s\right\|_{L^{q}(0,t)}

\displaystyle=e^{-\epsilon t}\left\|\int_{0}^{r}(1+(r-s)^{-\rho})e^{-(\lambda-\epsilon)(r-s)}e^{\epsilon s}\phi(s)\mathrm{d}s\right\|_{L^{q}(0,t)}

\displaystyle=e^{-\epsilon t}||T_{\rho,\lambda-\epsilon}(e^{\epsilon\cdot}\phi)||_{L^{q}(0,t)}.

By (
2.4
), we obtain that

||T_{\rho,\lambda}\phi||_{L^{q}_{\epsilon}(0,t)}\leq ce^{-\epsilon t}||e^{\epsilon\cdot}\phi||_{L^{p}(0,t)}=c||\phi||_{L^{p}_{\epsilon}(0,t)}.

(2.5)

Finally, we take the upper limit of
t\to\infty
in equation (
2.5
) and deduce that (
2.5
) is also true for
t=\infty
.
∎

Next, we prove some connections between the original
L^{p}
norm and the new norm in this article.

Proposition 2.2
.

For any
t\in[0,\infty]
and
\epsilon>0
, we have

||\phi||_{L^{\infty}_{\epsilon}(0,t)}\leq||\phi||_{L^{\infty}(0,t)}\leq\sup_{s\in(0,t)}||\phi||_{L^{\infty}_{\epsilon}(0,s)}.

(2.6)

We first consider
t<\infty
. The first inequality is obvious as

\displaystyle||\phi||_{L^{\infty}_{\epsilon}(0,t)}

\displaystyle=\left\|e^{\epsilon(\cdot-t)}\phi\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,t)}\leq\left\|\phi\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,t)}.

For any
0<r\leq s<t
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\phi\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{\epsilon}(0,s)}

\displaystyle=e^{-\epsilon s}\left\|e^{\epsilon\cdot}\phi\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,s)}

\displaystyle\geq e^{-\epsilon s}\left\|e^{\epsilon\cdot}\phi\right\|_{L^{\infty}(s-r,s)}

\displaystyle\geq e^{-\epsilon r}\left\|\phi\right\|_{L^{\infty}(s-r,s)}.

Choose
r=t/N
for
N\in\mathbb{N}
, then we have

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in(0,t)}||\phi||_{L^{\infty}_{\epsilon}(0,s)}

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{1\leq k\leq N}||\phi||_{L^{\infty}_{\epsilon}(0,kr)}

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{1\leq k\leq N}e^{-\epsilon r}\left\|\phi\right\|_{L^{\infty}((k-1)r,kr)}

\displaystyle=e^{-\epsilon r}\left\|\phi\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,t)}.

Now we let
N\to\infty
to prove the second inequality in (
2.6
). Finally, we take
t\to\infty
to deduce that (
2.6
) is also true for
t=\infty
.
∎

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
1\leq p\leq q\leq\infty
,
t\in[0,\infty]
and
\epsilon>0
. Then there exists a constant
c
independent of
t
so that

||\phi||_{L^{p}_{\epsilon}(0,t)}\leq c\left\|\phi\right\|_{L^{q}(0,t)}.

(2.7)

Assume that
\phi\in L^{q}(0,t)
, otherwise the inequality is trivial.
By Hölder’s inequality,

||\phi||_{L^{p}_{\epsilon}(0,t)}=||e^{\epsilon(\cdot-t)}\phi||_{L^{p}(0,t)}\leq||e^{\epsilon(\cdot-t)}||_{L^{\frac{pq}{q-p}}(0,t)}||\phi||_{L^{q}(0,t)}=c||\phi||_{L^{q}(0,t)},

where

c=||e^{-\epsilon\cdot}||_{L^{\frac{pq}{q-p}}(0,\infty)}=\left(\frac{q-p}{\epsilon pq}\right)^{\frac{q-p}{pq}}.

Finally we take
t\to\infty
to deduce that (
2.7
) is also true for
t=\infty
.
∎

Proposition 5.1

(Generalized LaSalle’s invariance principle)
.

Let
U:[0,\infty]\to W^{1,\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
satisfy
\sup_{t\geq 0}

\|U(t)\|_{W^{1,\infty}}<\infty
. Suppose there is a function
\mathcal{L}\in C^{1}([0,\infty);[0,\infty))
with
\mathcal{L}(t)=\mathcal{L}^{(0)}(U(t))
and
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{L}(t)=\mathcal{L}^{(1)}(U(t))
.

(a)

Suppose there is a closed subset
S\subset L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
so that
U(t)\in S
and
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{L}(t)\leq 0
for all
t\geq 0
, then we have
\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}d_{\infty}(U(t),M)=0
, where
M
is the largest invariant set in
E=\left\{V\in S:\mathcal{L}^{(1)}(V)=0\right\}
, and
d_{\infty}
is the distance under
L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
.

(b)

Let
U^{*}=(u_{1}^{*},u_{2}^{*},u_{3}^{*})\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
, and let
S
be a closed neighbourhood of
U^{*}
in
L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Suppose the set
S
satisfies the following conditions:

(i)

If
U(t)\in S
, then
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{L}(t)\leq 0
;

(ii)

There exist
c_{1}
,
c_{2}>0
and
p_{1}
,
p_{2}
,
p_{3}\in[1,\infty)
so that

c_{1}d_{p}(V,U^{*})\leq\mathcal{L}^{(0)}(V)\leq c_{2}d_{p}(V,U^{*}),\ \forall V\in S,

where

d_{p}(V,U^{*}):=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\|v_{i}-u_{i}^{*}\|_{L^{p_{i}}}^{p_{i}},\ V=(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}).

Then there exists
\epsilon>0
, so that if
d_{p}(U_{0},U^{*})<\epsilon
, then
\lim_{t\to\infty}d_{\infty}(U(t),M)=0
, where
M
is the same as in part (a). Here
\epsilon
depends on
S
,
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
and
K:=\sup_{t\geq 0}\|U(t)\|_{W^{1,\infty}}
.

(a)
Since
\|U(t)\|_{W^{1,\infty}}
is uniformly bounded for all

, the Sobolev Embedding Theorem implies that
\{U(t):t\geq 0\}
is a (pre)compact subset of
L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
. We can verify that the proof of LaSalle’s invariance principle (Theorem 1,
LaSalle [1960]
) is valid under the metric of
L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Now part (a) follows immediately.

(b)
Denote

. By Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality, there exists

so that

||u_{i}(t)-u_{i}^{*}||_{L^{\infty}}\leq c_{3}||u_{i}(t)-u_{i}^{*}||_{W^{1,\infty}}^{\frac{n}{n+p_{i}}}||u_{i}(t)-u_{i}^{*}||_{L^{p_{i}}}^{\frac{p_{i}}{n+p_{i}}}

for all
t\geq 0
. Denote
c_{4}=\max\limits_{1\leq i\leq 3}c_{3}(K+||u_{i}^{*}||_{W^{1,\infty}})^{\frac{n}{n+p_{i}}}
, then we have

||u_{i}(t)-u_{i}^{*}||_{L^{\infty}}\leq c_{4}||u_{i}(t)-u_{i}^{*}||_{L^{p_{i}}}^{\frac{p_{i}}{n+p_{i}}}

(5.17)

for
1\leq i\leq 3
and
t\geq 0
.

Choose
\epsilon_{0}>0
so that
B(U^{*},\epsilon_{0})\subset S
. Let
U_{0}
be any initial value so that

\displaystyle d_{p}(U_{0},U^{*})<\min\left\{1,\left(\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{c_{4}}\right)^{p^{\prime}},\,\frac{c_{1}}{c_{2}c_{4}}\epsilon_{0}^{p^{\prime}}\right\}=:\epsilon,

where

. Then by (

\|U_{0}-U^{*}\|_{L^{\infty}}<c_{4}\max\limits_{1\leq i\leq 3}\epsilon^{1/(n+p_{i})}\leq c_{4}\epsilon^{1/p^{\prime}}\leq\epsilon_{0}.

It follows that
U_{0}\in S
. By condition (iii) and
\epsilon\leq\frac{c_{1}}{c_{2}c_{4}}\epsilon_{0}^{p^{\prime}}
, we further get

\mathcal{L}(0)\leq c_{2}d_{p}(U_{0},S_{0})<\frac{c_{1}}{c_{4}}\epsilon_{0}^{p^{\prime}}.

Define

t^{*}=\sup\big\{T\in[0,\infty):\|U(t)-U^{*}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\epsilon_{0},\ \forall t\in[0,T]\big\}\in[0,\infty],

(5.18)

then

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{3}\|u_{i}(t)-u_{i}^{*}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{n+p_{i}}\leq c_{4}d_{p}(U(t),U^{*})\leq\frac{c_{4}}{c_{1}}\mathcal{L}(t)\leq\frac{c_{4}}{c_{1}}\mathcal{L}(0)<\epsilon_{0}^{p^{\prime}}=\min_{1\leq i\leq 3}\epsilon_{0}^{n+p_{i}}.

f_{2}(z)=\left(1+\tau_{1}z\right)\left(1-\frac{z}{K}\right)
. Given that
Y^{*}\leq\frac{d_{2}}{e_{2}\mu_{2}}
, we have
\frac{Y^{*}}{1+\tau_{2}Y^{*}}\mu_{2}-\frac{d_{2}}{e_{2}}<0
. It follows that
\mathcal{L}_{2}^{(1)}\leq 0
if
J(X,X^{*})\leq 0
. To ensure
J(X,X^{*})\leq 0
, it is necessary that
f_{1}(X)-f_{1}(X^{*})
and
f_{2}(X)-f_{2}(X^{*})
have opposite signs. Since
f_{1}(X)
is monotonically increasing,
f_{1}(X)-f_{1}(X^{*})
and
X-X^{*}
share the same sign.

(a)
Suppose
X^{*}>\frac{1}{2}\left(K-\frac{1}{\tau_{1}}\right)
, then
\mathcal{L}_{2}^{(1)}\leq 0
holds for all
U
in a closed neighbourhood
S
of
U^{*}=(X^{*},Y^{*},0)
(in the norm of
L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
). By a similar argument as in Lemma
5.4
, we can prove from Taylor expansion that there exist
c_{1}
,
c_{2}>0
, such that

c_{1}d_{p}(V,U^{*})\leq\mathcal{L}_{2}^{(0)}(V)\leq c_{2}d_{p}(V,U^{*}),\ \forall V\in S,

where

d_{p}(V,U^{*})=||X-X^{*}||_{L^{2}}^{2}+||Y-Y^{*}||_{L^{2}}^{2}+||Z||_{L^{1}},\ V=(X,Y,Z).

By equations (
1.3
), we have

E:=\{V\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3}):\mathcal{L}_{2}^{(1)}(V)=0\}=\{(X^{*},Y,0)\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3}):Y\text{ is a nonnegative constant}\}.

The maximal invariant set in
E
is
M=\{(X^{*},Y^{*},0)\}
.
Then part (a) follows from Proposition
5.1
(b) and Remark
5.2
.

(b)
If
X^{*}>K-\frac{1}{\tau_{1}}
, then
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{L}_{2}(t)\leq 0
for all
t\geq 0
. Part (b) then follows from Proposition
5.1
(a).

(c)
When
X^{*}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(K-\frac{1}{\tau_{1}}\right)
, we assert that
(c)
holds. Suppose, for contradiction, that there exists
\epsilon>0
such that for all solutions
U(t)
with initial values satisfying
\|U_{0}-U^{*}\|_{L^{\infty}}<\epsilon
, equation (
5.24
) holds. This implies
\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{L}_{2}(t)=0
.

Now, consider a spatially homogeneous solution
(X,Y,0)\neq(X^{*},Y^{*},0)
with an initial condition in this neighborhood. The Lyapunov function then satisfies

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}_{2}}{\mathrm{d}t}(t)=r|\Omega|J(X,X^{*}).

When
X^{*}<\frac{1}{2}\left(K-\frac{1}{\tau_{1}}\right)
, we have
J(X,X^{*})>0
in a punctured neighborhood of
X^{*}
, implying
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{L}_{2}(t)>0
for all
t
. When
X^{*}=\frac{1}{2}\left(K-\frac{1}{\tau_{1}}\right)
,
J(X,X^{*})>0
for all
X<X^{*}
. Thus, if
X_{0}<X^{*}
, then
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{L}_{2}(t)>0
for any
t\geq 0
. Combined with the fact that
\mathcal{L}_{2}(t)>0
for all
U\neq U^{*}
, this contradicts the assumption that
\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{L}_{2}(t)=0
. Therefore,
(c)
holds.
∎

Lemma 5.6

(Stability of coexistence steady state)
.

Let
U(t)=(X(t),Y(t),Z(t))
be the solution of (
1.3
) – (
1.4
), and
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
are constant functions.

(a)

if

\frac{1}{\delta_{X}}\chi_{1M}^{2}h^{2}(Y^{*})+\frac{1}{\delta_{Z}}\chi_{2M}^{2}h^{2}(Z^{*})<4\delta_{Y}

(5.26)

and
D\Phi(U^{*})
is negative definite, then for any
R>0
, there exists
\epsilon=\epsilon(R)>0
, so that if the terms in (
5.16
) are bounded by
R
and
||U_{0}-U^{*}||_{L^{2}}<\epsilon
, then
\lim_{t\to\infty}||U(t)-U^{*}||_{L^{\infty}}=0
.

(b)

if
D\Phi(U^{*})
has an eigenvalue with positive real part, then there exists
R>0
, so that for any
\epsilon>0
, we can choose an initial value
U_{0}
with
||U_{0}-U^{*}||_{L^{\infty}}<\epsilon
, but
||U(\tau)-U^{*}||_{L^{\infty}}>R
for some
\tau>0
.

(a)
Define a Lyapunov function by

\mathcal{L}_{3}(t)=\frac{1}{2}||U(t)-U^{*}||_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(5.27)

Since
U^{*}
is a constant function and
\Phi(U^{*})=0
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{L}_{3}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(U-U^{*})^{T}\big(\nabla(\mathbb{A}(x,U)\nabla U)+\Phi(U)\big)\,\mathrm{d}x

(5.28)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(U-U^{*})^{T}\nabla(\mathbb{A}(x,U)\nabla U)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}(U-U^{*})^{T}(\Phi(U)-\Phi(U^{*}))\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\nabla U^{T}\mathbb{A}(x,U)\nabla U\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}(U-U^{*})^{T}(\Phi(U)-\Phi(U^{*}))\,\mathrm{d}x.

Denote

\mathbb{B}(x):=\mathbb{A}(x,U^{*})+\mathbb{A}(x,U^{*})^{T}=\begin{pmatrix}2\delta_{X}&-\chi_{1}(x)h(Y^{*})&0\\
-\chi_{1}(x)h(Y^{*})&2\delta_{Y}&-\chi_{2}(x)h(Z^{*})\\
0&-\chi_{2}(x)h(Z^{*})&2\delta_{Z}\end{pmatrix}.

We can check that
\mathbb{B}(x)
has positive eigenvalues if and only if

\frac{1}{\delta_{X}}\chi_{1}^{2}(x)h^{2}(Y^{*})+\frac{1}{\delta_{Z}}\chi_{2}^{2}(x)h^{2}(Z^{*})<4\delta_{Y}.

(5.29)

Therefore, (
5.26
) implies the matrix
\mathbb{A}(x,U^{*})
is positive definite for all
x\in\overline{\Omega}
. It follows that, there exists
\epsilon_{1}>0
, such that for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
|z-U^{*}|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon_{1}
, the matrix
\mathbb{A}(x,z)
is positive definite. Here
|\cdot|_{p}
is the
l^{p}
norm of vectors. Besides, we denote the largest eigenvalue of
\big(D\Phi(U^{*})+D\Phi(U^{*})^{T}\big)/2
to be
-\lambda_{0}<0
. We can also choose
\epsilon_{2}>0
such that for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
|z-U^{*}|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon_{2}
, we have

\big|\Phi(z)-\Phi(U^{*})-D\Phi(U^{*})(z-U^{*})\big|\leq\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{0}|z-U^{*}|_{2}.

It follows that

\displaystyle(z-U^{*})^{T}(\Phi(z)-\Phi(U^{*}))

(5.30)

\displaystyle=(z-U^{*})^{T}\big(\Phi(z)-\Phi(U^{*})-D\Phi(U^{*})(z-U^{*})\big)+(z-U^{*})^{T}D\Phi(U^{*})(z-U^{*})

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{0}|z-U^{*}|_{2}^{2}.

Combining (
5.27
)-(
5.30
), we obtain

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{L}_{3}(t)\leq-\lambda_{0}\mathcal{L}_{3}(t)

(5.31)

as long as
||U(t)-U^{*}||_{L^{\infty}}\leq\min\{\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}\}
. Now we can prove part (a) by applying Proposition
5.1
and Remark
5.2
with
S=\{V\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3}):||V-U^{*}||_{L^{\infty}}\leq\min\{\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}\}\}
and
E=M=\{U^{*}\}
.

(b)
We consider any spatially homogeneous solution
U
with an initial value
U_{0}
. In this case, the equation (
1.3
) reduces to the ODE
\frac{\mathrm{d}U}{\mathrm{d}t}=\Phi(U)
.
If
D\Phi(U^{*})
has an eigenvalue with positive real part, then
U^{*}
is an unstable equilibrium regarding the ODE, that is, there exists
R>0
so that for any
\epsilon>0
, we can choose an initial value
U_{0}
with
|U_{0}-U^{*}|_{\infty}<\epsilon
, but
|U(\tau)-U^{*}|_{\infty}>R
for some
\tau>0
. Now we see that the definition of instability as described in item (b) is satisfied.
∎

Theorem
1.2
follows directly from Lemmas
5.4
,
5.5
, and
5.6
.
∎
