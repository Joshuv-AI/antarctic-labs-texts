Lower oxidative balance score is linked to increased miscarriage risk: findings from a case–control study
Authors: Amir Hossein Faghfouri, Samira Gholami, Amin Mokari-Yamchi, Negar Karimi, Shabnam Vazifekhah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOxidative stress (OS) may play a role in adverse pregnancy outcomes, including spontaneous miscarriage. This study aimed to assess oxidative balance score (OBS), ranging from 0 to 38, and related characteristics in women with spontaneous miscarriage compared to healthy pregnant women.MethodsA case–control study was conducted at Kowsar Women’s Hospital in Urmia, Iran, between June 2024 and March 2025. The case group included 152 women with spontaneous miscarriage, and the control group included 300 healthy pregnant women beyond 20 weeks' gestation. OBS, combining dietary and lifestyle antioxidant/pro-oxidant factors, was calculated and analyzed across tertiles.ResultsTotal OBS was significantly lower in the case group compared to controls (20.25 ± 7.19 vs. 22.45 ± 6.67; p = 0.023), while dietary OBS did not differ significantly (p = 0.15). Lifestyle OBS was lower in the miscarriage group (p < 0.001), with reduced physical activity and higher smoking prevalence. Magnesium, selenium, and vitamin B6 intakes were also significantly lower in the case group (p-values = 0.047, 0.034, 0.042, respectively); However, after applying Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (adjusted α = 0.0026), none of the nutrient-level associations remained statistically significant. Higher tertiles of total and lifestyle OBS were associated with lower odds of miscarriage after adjustment for confounders (OR = 0.46, 95%CI (0.21- 0.99) and OR = 0.41, 95%CI(0.19- 0.88), respectively), while dietary OBS remained non-significant.ConclusionLower total and lifestyle OBS are associated with an increased risk of spontaneous miscarriage. OBS may serve as a useful marker for miscarriage risk assessment.Keywords: Miscarriage, Oxidative stress, Antioxidants, Risk assessment, Case–control studies

Introduction

Miscarriage, defined by the American Society for Reproductive Medicine as the spontaneous loss of a clinical pregnancy before 20 weeks of gestation, poses a significant obstetric challenge, with a pooled risk estimate of 15.3% in Europe and North America [1, 2]. Based on data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2019 study, a regional analysis revealed that the incidence rate of miscarriage was 2,409.61 per 100,000 population in the North Africa and Middle East (NAME) region, compared with 2,172.36 per 100,000 globally [3].

The etiology of miscarriage is complex and multifactorial, involving genetic, anatomical, hormonal, and environmental factors [4]. Key contributors include body mass index (BMI), smoking, physical activity, diet, and alcohol consumption, all of which have been associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes [5]. A balanced diet rich in antioxidants may mitigate the risk of miscarriage by reducing oxidative stress (OS), whereas diets high in processed foods and proinflammatory nutrients may increase this risk [6].

OS, characterized by an imbalance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) and antioxidant defenses, is heightened during pregnancy due to increased metabolic demands and physiological changes [7, 8]. Studies on miscarriage have shown an increase in specific oxidative biomarkers during pregnancy, such as 8-hydroxydeoxyguanosine, malondialdehyde, and total oxidant status [9]. The placenta generates physiological ROS levels, with dysregulation potentially contributing to pathology, and disruptions in its function due to oxidative imbalance can impair oxygen and nutrient delivery to the fetus, contributing to adverse pregnancy outcomes such as miscarriage, fetal developmental abnormalities, preterm birth, and low birth weight [10]. Compromised antioxidant enzyme activity has been implicated in these outcomes, highlighting the detrimental effects of OS on maternal physiology and fetal development [11].

To quantify the cumulative impact of dietary and lifestyle factors on OS, the oxidative balance score (OBS) has been developed, with higher scores indicating lower OS [12, 13]. The OBS integrates dietary components (e.g., dietary fiber, carotene, vitamins, minerals) and lifestyle factors (e.g., physical activity, alcohol consumption, BMI, serum cotinine levels), offering a practical tool for assessing OBS with significant public health implications [12]. Higher OBS has been associated with reduced risks of chronic diseases, including type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, cancers, hypertension, and obesity [14–18]. Despite its established utility, few studies have explored the relationship between maternal OBS and pregnancy outcomes, and none have specifically investigated its association with miscarriage [13, 19, 20].

This study aims to evaluate the oxidative balance score and characteristics of women who experienced spontaneous miscarriage compared to healthy pregnant women beyond 20 weeks of gestation. By examining these associations, we seek to elucidate the role of OS in miscarriage risk and contribute to the evidence base for targeted interventions to improve maternal and fetal health outcomes.

Method

Study subjects and setting

This 1:2 hospital-based case–control study was conducted at Kowsar Women’s Hospital in Urmia, Iran, from June 2024 to March 2025. The case group consisted of 152 women who experienced spontaneous miscarriage, and were admitted to the hospital. The control group consisted of 300 healthy pregnant women beyond 20 weeks' gestation, who attended the outpatient clinics of hospital for routine prenatal care or had successfully delivered a healthy child. Participant Flow Diagram (STROBE) [21]. Diagram follows STROBE guidance to report numbers at each stage, reasons for non-participation, and numbers included in the final analysis

Consistent with international guidance, WHO defines miscarriage (spontaneous abortion) as the spontaneous loss of a pregnancy before 24 weeks’ gestation [22]. Eligibility criteria included: 1) singleton pregnancy, 2) age ≥ 18 years, 3) pregnancy achieved without assisted reproductive technology (ART), 4) no vaginal bleeding in the first trimester for controls, and 5) no fetal malformations in the current pregnancy. Women with known genetic disorders, recurrent pregnancy loss, or chronic diseases like diabetes and hypertension were excluded to minimize confounding. Power calculations indicated that with 152 cases and 300 controls (1:2 matching), the study would have greater than 80% power to detect an odds ratio of 0.54 or greater, assuming an exposure prevalence of 30% in controls and a two-sided alpha of 0.05 [23].

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Urmia University of Medical Sciences, Iran (Approval ID: IR.UMSU.REC.1402.023). All participants received detailed information about the purpose and procedures of study, provided written informed consent, and were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without consequence.

Assessment of OBS

The OBS was constructed based on 19 components, including 16 dietary nutrients and 3 lifestyle factors [24]. Pro-oxidants (n = 4) smoking status, BMI, total iron intake, and total dietary fat. Antioxidants (n = 15) included dietary selenium, fiber, zinc, copper, calcium, magnesium, vitamin C, vitamin E, vitamin B12, total folate, niacin, riboflavin, vitamin B6, beta-carotene, and vitamin A (retinol activity equivalents). The 168‑item semi‑quantitative FFQ used in this study was previously validated in Tehranian adults against twelve 24‑h dietary recalls (24hDR) and selected biomarkers, and its test–retest reliability was evaluated over one year. Energy‑adjusted and deattenuated Pearson correlations between the FFQ and the mean of twelve 24‑h recalls for nutrients ranged 0.24–0.71 in men (mean r = 0.53) and 0.11–0.60 in women (mean r = 0.39), while for food groups the age‑adjusted, deattenuated Spearman correlations ranged 0.03–0.77 in men (median 0.44) and 0.12–0.79 in women (median 0.37). It includes168 food items, with participants indicating intake frequency (daily, weekly, monthly, or yearly) and portion sizes [25, 26]. Physical activity was evaluated using the short-form International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), which assesses the frequency and duration of vigorous-intensity, moderate-intensity, and walking activities over the past seven days. Each activity was assigned a corresponding metabolic equivalent task (MET) value (8.0 for vigorous, 4.0 for moderate, and 3.3 for walking), and total physical activity was expressed in MET-hours per week (MET-h/week) [27]. Smoking was assessed using appropriate questionnaire. Body weight and height were measured using a calibrated digital scale and stadiometer, respectively, with participants wearing light clothing and no shoes. BMI was calculated by dividing weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2).

The dietary and lifestyle variables included in the OBS were classified into tertiles according to their distribution among the participants. For antioxidant factors, individuals falling into the lowest tertile were given 0 points, those in the middle tertile received 1 point, and the highest tertile earned 2 points. The scoring was inverted for pro-oxidant factors. Participants in the highest tertile were assigned 0 points, the middle tertile 1 point, and the lowest tertile 2 points. Smoking status assessed via self-reported data and those who never smoked were scored 2 points, former smokers received 1 point, and current smokers were assigned 0 points. BMI categories followed WHO guidelines, with normal weight (< 25 kg/m2) receiving 2 points, overweight (25–29.9 kg/m2) assigned 1 point, and obesity (≥ 30 kg/m2) given 0 points. Physical activity was evaluated by total weekly MET minutes and participants in the highest quartile received 2 points, those in the middle two quartiles received 1 point, and the lowest quartile received 0 points [24]. The overall OBS was determined by adding the points assigned to each component, producing a total score ranging from 0, indicating the highest pro-oxidant exposure, to 40, representing the greatest antioxidant exposure.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Independent samples t-tests were used to compare baseline characteristics, OBS, and dietary and lifestyle components between the case and control groups. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test.

Logistic regression analyses were conducted to evaluate the association between OBS tertiles and the risk of miscarriage. In the unadjusted model (Model 1), odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated comparing the highest tertile (T3) to the lowest tertile (T1) for total OBS, dietary OBS, and lifestyle OBS. Adjusted logistic regression (Model 2) controlled for confounders including age, gestational age, BMI, occupation, education level, total energy intake, and parity. The calibration of logistic models was assessed by the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-the-fit test. To account for potential inflation of Type I error due to multiple comparisons across 19 individual OBS components, Bonferroni correction was applied. The adjusted significance threshold was set at α/19 = 0.0026. P-values below this threshold were considered statistically significant in sensitivity analyses. A p-value < 0.05 (two-sided) was considered significant before correction.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The baseline characteristics of the case (n = 152) and control (n = 300) groups are presented in Table 1. Women in the control group had a mean gestational age of 30 ± 3.11. Women in the case group, who experienced spontaneous miscarriage, were significantly older (p = 0.001) compared to the control group. However, no significant difference was observed in BMI between the case and control groups (p = 0.11). Education level and occupational status were comparable between groups (p = 0.081 and p = 0.18, respectively). Income distribution differed significantly (p = 0.045), with the case group reporting a higher proportion of individuals earning 15–20 million Iranian Rial (IRR) per month (52.9% vs. 38.8%) and a lower proportion earning 8–15 million (27.9% vs. 40.3%) compared to controls.

Association between OBS and miscarriage

Table 2 summarizes the comparison of OBS and its components between the case and control groups. The total OBS was significantly lower in the case group compared to the control group (p = 0.023). While dietary OBS did not differ significantly between groups (p = 0.15). Regarding dietary intakes, vitamin B6 (p = 0.042), magnesium (p = 0.047), and selenium (p = 0.034) were significantly lower in the case group. Other dietary components, including dietary fiber, carotene, riboflavin, niacin, folate, vitamins B12, C, E, calcium, zinc, copper, total fat, and iron, showed no significant differences (p > 0.05). Lifestyle OBS was significantly lower in the case group (p < 0.001), with lower physical activity (p = 0.008) and a higher prevalence of smoking (p = 0.021) compared to controls. BMI, as a lifestyle OBS component, showed no significant difference (p = 0.11). However, after applying Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (adjusted α = 0.0026), none of the nutrient-level associations remained statistically significant.

The association between tertiles of OBS components and miscarriage risk is presented in Table 3. In the unadjusted model (Model 1), the highest tertile (T3) of total OBS was associated with the reduced OR for miscarriage (OR 0.43, 95% CI 0.22–0.87) compared to the lowest tertile (T1). Similarly, the highest tertile of lifestyle OBS was associated with a lower miscarriage risk (OR 0.39, 95% CI 0.19–0.78). Dietary OBS showed a non-significant trend toward reduced risk in the highest tertile (OR 0.53, 95% CI 0.26–1.06). After adjustment for confounders in Model 2, the associations remained significant for total OBS (OR 0.46, 95% CI 0.21–0.99) and lifestyle OBS (OR 0.41, 95% CI 0.19–0.88), while dietary OBS remained non-significant (OR 0.53, 95% CI 0.24–1.15). The calibration of the adjusted logistic regression models (Model 2) was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, indicating a good model fit (p = 0.927), and the explanatory power of the model was assessed by Nagelkerke R2 = 0.034.

Discussion

The findings of this study reveal a significant association between lower OBS and increased risk of spontaneous miscarriage, with women in the case group (miscarriage) exhibiting lower total OBS and lifestyle OBS compared to controls. Notably, specific dietary components such as vitamin B6, magnesium, and selenium, as well as lifestyle factors including physical activity were significantly lower in the case group, suggesting their potential role in modulating OS-related miscarriage risk. Moreover, a higher prevalence of smoking was reported in the case group compared to the control group. The adjusted odds ratio analysis further confirmed that higher total OBS and lifestyle OBS were associated with reduced miscarriage risk, while dietary OBS showed a non-significant trend. These results indicate that lifestyle factors, more than dietary components alone, may play a critical role in mitigating OS and improving pregnancy outcomes, highlighting the need for targeted interventions to address modifiable risk factors in women at risk of miscarriage.

These findings align with prior research linking maternal lifestyle and dietary patterns to pregnancy outcomes. A meta-analysis reported that diets rich in fruits, vegetables, seafood, dairy, eggs, and grains were associated with reduced miscarriage odds [28], while another found that higher dietary total antioxidant capacity (TAC) was linked to lower risks of miscarriage, gestational diabetes mellitus, and pre-eclampsia [29]. Our results extend these observations by demonstrating that OBS, which integrates multiple dietary and lifestyle factors to reflect cumulative OS [13], serves as a valuable indicator of miscarriage risk. Specifically, lower intakes of vitamin B6, magnesium, and selenium in the case group are consistent with studies associating deficiencies in these nutrients with adverse pregnancy outcomes, such as recurrent abortions and pre-eclampsia [30–33].

Regarding lifestyle factors, the higher smoking prevalence and lower physical activity in our case group align with evidence that smoking increases OS via ROS production and can decrease fertility in women [34]; and that regular physical activity reduces miscarriage risk [35]. A retrospective study in China supports these observations, reporting a lower miscarriage risk among women engaging in light to moderate regular physical activity, defined as sports lasting more than 30 min at least twice per week [36].Additionally, de Meirelles et al. demonstrated that 30 min of moderate-intensity exercise, including treadmill, resistance, and stretching activities, significantly improves OS status by increasing catalase activity and platelet superoxide dismutase levels [37].

Obesity is an established risk factor for spontaneous miscarriage, with both elevated and low BMI compared to the normal range associated with increased risk, forming a U-shaped risk curve [38]. Although the underlying mechanisms are not fully elucidated, the inflammatory state associated with adiposity may disrupt metabolism and sex hormone secretion, potentially leading to reduced oocyte quality and impaired embryo implantation [39–41].

However, unlike studies linking dietary OBS to conditions such as infertility, rheumatoid arthritis, biological aging, [42–44], our study found no significant association between dietary OBS and miscarriage, suggesting a stronger influence of lifestyle factors. This may be attributed to limitations in dietary OBS assessment, such as reliance on dietary recall or food frequency questionnaires, which are susceptible to measurement error, or the relatively small case group sample size (n = 152), which may limit statistical power to detect significant associations.

Additionally, prior research has shown that OBS is inversely associated with chronic inflammation, prediabetes, impaired glucose tolerance, obesity, polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), infertility, and menopausal status [45–49], indicating that OBS may serve as a broader indicator of OS status for lifestyle interventions across reproductive and health outcomes [48]. To our knowledge, this is the first study to assess OBS in relation to miscarriage, highlighting its potential as a novel tool for identifying at-risk populations.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the observational nature of the study precludes establishing causality between OBS and miscarriage risk, as unmeasured confounders may influence the observed associations. Second, dietary and lifestyle data were based on self-reported measures, which are subject to recall bias and may not fully capture long-term patterns. Third, the study population may not be representative of broader demographics, as socioeconomic and regional factors, such as income disparities observed between groups, could influence generalizability. Fourth, while the OBS integrates multiple dietary and lifestyle factors, it may not fully account for other environmental or genetic factors contributing to OS and miscarriage risk. Future studies with larger, more diverse cohorts and prospective designs are needed to address these limitations.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that a higher overall OBS, particularly driven by lifestyle factors such as physical activity and smoking status, is associated with a significantly reduced risk of spontaneous miscarriage. The overall dietary OBS did not significantly impact miscarriage risk. Importantly, the protective association of total and lifestyle OBS with miscarriage risk remained significant after adjusting for potential confounders. These findings highlight the importance of lifestyle-related oxidative balance in pregnancy outcomes and suggest that interventions targeting modifiable behaviors may help reduce miscarriage risk.
