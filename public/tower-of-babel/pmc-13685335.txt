Comorbidity patterns and inpatient expenditure among patients with coronary heart disease in China
Authors: Xiali Chen, Zhixin Liu, Pan Han, Rui Dong, Hongjie Zhang, Xing Lin Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoronary heart disease (CHD) often co-occurs with other chronic conditions. This increases care complexity and hospital costs. Most previous studies have relied on single diseases or simple comorbidity counts. Less is known about whether different multimorbidity patterns are associated with different levels and structures of inpatient expenditure. We aimed to identify comorbidity patterns among middle-aged and older inpatients with CHD and to examine their associations with hospital costs.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective study using electronic health record data from a tertiary hospital in Shanxi Province, China, from 2020 to 2024. Patients aged 45 years or older with a principal discharge diagnosis of CHD (ICD-10: I20–I25) were included. The outcome was total inpatient expenditure per admission. Latent class analysis was applied to 14 comorbidities with prevalence of at least 1%. We then used generalised linear models with a gamma family and log link to examine the association between latent classes and inpatient expenditure, adjusting for sociodemographic and clinical covariates.ResultsAmong 33,044 patients used for pattern identification, 85.16% (28,140) had at least one included comorbidity. Five classes were identified: low comorbidity (39.89%), vascular disease (5.20%), hypertension-dominant (42.27%), diabetes-renal (3.16%), and cardiac dysfunction (9.48%). Compared with the low-comorbidity class, all other classes were associated with higher inpatient expenditure, with the largest increases observed in the cardiac dysfunction class (ER = 1.33, 95% CI: 1.30–1.36) and the diabetes-renal class (ER = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.23–1.32). Predicted mean expenditure was highest in the cardiac dysfunction class ($2,615.77; 95% CI: 2,562.30–2,669.24) and the diabetes-renal class ($2,502.99; 95% CI: 2,421.49–2,584.49), compared with $1,964.85 (95% CI: 1,943.54–1,986.16) in the low-comorbidity class. Medical consumables accounted for the largest share of expenditure in all groups, while the cardiac dysfunction class had the highest proportions of diagnostic and medication costs.ConclusionsMultimorbidity was highly prevalent among middle-aged and older inpatients with CHD. Distinct comorbidity patterns were associated with different levels and structures of inpatient expenditure. Introduction

Coronary heart disease (CHD) remains a leading cause of morbidity, mortality, and healthcare expenditure worldwide [1]. Its burden is particularly substantial in China, where population ageing and the long-term accumulation of cardiometabolic risk have increased the number of people living with chronic cardiovascular disease [2, 3]. According to the Report on Cardiovascular Health and Diseases in China 2023, the number of individuals living with CHD in China increased from about 11 million in 2018 to 11.39 million in 2020 [4]. In routine clinical practice, CHD rarely occurs in isolation. Middle-aged and older patients admitted with CHD often have multiple coexisting conditions, including hypertension, diabetes, heart failure, renal dysfunction, arrhythmia, and cerebrovascular disease [5, 6]. These conditions can complicate treatment, increase monitoring needs, and change the way hospital resources are used [7–9].

The clinical and economic importance of multimorbidity in CHD is increasingly recognised. However, many previous studies have relied on simple comorbidity counts or examined individual diseases in isolation [10–13]. This approach is useful for describing disease burden, but it may miss important differences between patients who have the same number of conditions but different disease combinations. In practice, a patient with CHD plus hypertension may have a very different care pathway from a patient with CHD plus heart failure and renal impairment [14–16]. A pattern-based approach may therefore provide more clinically meaningful information than a count-based approach alone. Although recent studies have begun to explore multimorbidity patterns in cardiovascular populations, most have focused on clinical outcomes such as mortality, readmission, and length of stay rather than the economic implications of different phenotypes [6, 17–19]. Evidence remains limited on how specific comorbidity patterns are associated with inpatient expenditure and with the internal composition of hospital costs.

China is rapidly expanding DRG- and DIP-based provider payment reform, and these systems are expected to improve cost control and reimbursement efficiency [20]. However, current grouping logic still relies mainly on principal diagnoses and broad severity categories [21]. If multimorbidity is not adequately captured, patients with different clinical complexity may still be grouped too broadly, which may weaken the alignment between reimbursement and actual resource use. This issue is relevant not only to total expenditure, but also to expenditure composition, because different phenotypes may generate costs through different combinations of diagnostics, medications, and consumables [22, 23]. Better evidence on the expenditure patterns associated with different multimorbidity phenotypes may therefore help inform both hospital management and payment refinement.

We therefore used electronic health record data from a large tertiary hospital in Shanxi Province, China, from 2020 to 2024 to provide a more detailed picture of cost heterogeneity among hospitalised patients with CHD in a tertiary-care setting and to generate evidence relevant to both clinical management and payment policy. We applied latent class analysis to identify multimorbidity phenotypes among hospitalised middle-aged and older adults with CHD. We then examined how these phenotypes were associated with total inpatient expenditure and the composition of hospital costs.

Methods

Study design and data source

This retrospective observational study used admission-level electronic health record (EHR) data from Yuncheng Central Hospital, a large tertiary hospital in Shanxi Province, China. Yuncheng is a regional hub with a population of 4.72 million, and its 2023 per capita GDP was US$6,939, indicating a mid-to-high level of economic development in North China. The hospital is a provincial cardiovascular research centre with 2,200 beds, and its cardiology department consistently ranks among the top three in Shanxi Province in terms of discharge and procedural volume. According to local cardiovascular quality-control statistics, the hospital accounted for more than 50% of CHD hospitalisations and over 70% of cardiovascular interventional procedures in Yuncheng during the study period. As a tertiary referral centre, it mainly reflects inpatient resource use among CHD patients treated in a higher-level hospital setting.

Data were extracted from the hospital information system in December 2025 and included demographic characteristics, diagnoses, procedures, admission and discharge details, clinical outcomes, and itemised inpatient expenditure. We included all hospitalisations between 1 January 2020 and 31 December 2024 with a principal discharge diagnosis of CHD (ICD-10: I20–I25). The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Yuncheng Central Hospital (No. YXLL2025120). All records were de-identified before analysis and were handled using anonymised identifiers.

Study participants

Eligible records were hospital admissions of patients aged 45 years or older with a principal discharge diagnosis of coronary heart disease (CHD; ICD-10 codes I20–I25) and a length of stay of at least one day. We initially identified 40,860 hospitalisation records that met these criteria. Of these, 33 records were excluded because of missing key information, including patient identity, sex, or itemised expenditure data. The final analytic sample therefore included 40,827 admissions from 33,044 unique patients.

To address the different requirements of phenotype identification and cost analysis, we constructed two analytic datasets from the cleaned records. For latent class analysis, we used a patient-level dataset and retained only the index admission, defined as the first eligible hospitalisation during the study period, in order to satisfy the assumption of independent observations. This yielded a final cohort of 33,044 unique patients. For expenditure analysis, we used an admission-level dataset that included all 40,827 eligible hospitalisations recorded between 2020 and 2024. Repeated admissions from the same patient were retained to capture variation in resource use across hospitalisations and to align the analysis with the per-admission reimbursement logic of China’s DRG/DIP payment system.

Outcome

The outcome was total inpatient expenditure per admission. We also examined expenditure composition according to the national discharge billing framework, including comprehensive medical service fees, diagnostic fees, treatment fees, medication fees, blood and blood-product fees, consumables fees, and other charges. All costs were adjusted to 2024 Chinese yuan using the Consumer Price Index published by the National Bureau of Statistics of China. Selected summary estimates were also presented in US dollar equivalents using the average 2024 exchange rate.

Comorbidity measurement

Comorbidities were identified primarily using the Elixhauser framework [24], based on the principal diagnosis and up to ten secondary diagnoses coded with ICD-10. To avoid overlap with the index condition, ischaemic cardiomyopathy (I25.5) was excluded from the coding process. We additionally included stroke, dyslipidaemia, and venous thromboembolism because of their recognised clinical relevance to coronary heart disease (CHD) and their inclusion in prior related studies [5, 25, 26]. A full list of all screened comorbidities, together with the corresponding ICD-10 codes and prevalence in the patient-level cohort, is provided in Supplementary Table 1. For latent class analysis, we retained conditions with a prevalence of at least 1.0% to reduce instability from very sparse indicators and to focus on common, clinically meaningful multimorbidity patterns [27, 28]. This procedure resulted in 14 binary comorbidity indicators for the final class models, covering cardiovascular, metabolic, and other systemic conditions.

Covariates

Covariate selection was guided by Andersen’s Behavioural Model of Health Services Use and prior health services research [29–31]. The variables were grouped into predisposing, enabling, and clinical need factors to capture the multidimensional correlates of inpatient expenditure. Predisposing factors included sex, age group (45–59, 60–74, or ≥ 75 years), ethnicity (Han vs. other), marital status (with vs. without a spouse), and occupation (formal employment, including civil servants and public institution staff; farmer; or other). Enabling factors were represented by insurance or payment type, categorised as Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance (UEBMI), Urban-Rural Resident Basic Medical Insurance (URRBMI), or other schemes. Clinical need and service-related factors included length of stay (1–7, 8–14, or ≥ 15 days), admission year (2020–2024), interval since the previous admission during the study period (single admission only, 1–30 days, 31–180 days, or > 180 days), ICU transfer during admission, and surgical intervention. Discharge status was categorised as recovered, improved, not improved, died, or other, reflecting the clinical outcome of the admission episode.

Statistical analysis

First, we described the baseline characteristics of the 33,044 unique patients at their index admission using frequencies and percentages, with results stratified by admission year.

Second, latent class analysis (LCA) was performed in Mplus version 8.3 using the patient-level dataset based on index admissions to identify distinct multimorbidity phenotypes. Models with two to ten classes were estimated. The final class solution was selected by jointly considering multiple criteria: the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and sample-size adjusted BIC to assess relative model fit; entropy to evaluate class separation; the Lo-Mendell-Rubin (LMR) and Vuong-Lo-Mendell-Rubin (VLMR) likelihood ratio tests to assess whether adding one more class improved fit compared with the model with one fewer class [32]; and clinical interpretability, including whether the resulting classes showed distinct and clinically meaningful comorbidity patterns. To further assess internal robustness, we performed an internal 5:5 random split-sample validation, in which the full sample was randomly divided into discovery and validation sets and LCA models were re-estimated in each subsample. Each patient was then assigned to the latent class for which they had the highest posterior probability. The demographic and clinical characteristics of patients were compared across the identified classes, and between-group differences were assessed using chi-square tests for categorical variables.

Third, to examine the association between multimorbidity patterns and inpatient expenditure, we analysed admission-level mean expenditure (i.e., expenditure per hospitalisation episode) using the full admission-level dataset. We fitted generalised linear models (GLM) with a gamma family and log link because inpatient expenditure was strictly positive and markedly right-skewed (Supplementary Fig. 1), and the gamma-log model directly estimates mean costs on the original scale [33–35]. To support the choice of the gamma specification, we additionally compared it with a log-normal alternative and examined residual diagnostics (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3; Supplementary Fig. 2). Patient-level cluster-robust standard errors were used to account for repeated hospitalisations within the same patient. The low-comorbidity class served as the reference group, and exponentiated coefficients were interpreted as expenditure ratios (ERs).

Fourth, beyond total admission-level expenditure, we analysed the itemised composition of inpatient costs across the latent classes. We also estimated adjusted predicted mean expenditure per admission for each multimorbidity class. In addition, class-stratified GLMs were fitted to examine whether the associations of demographic and clinical factors with per-admission expenditure differed across multimorbidity patterns.

Finally, two supplementary analyses were conducted to assess model robustness. We re-estimated the main expenditure models using generalised estimating equations (GEE) and mixed-effects models as sensitivity analyses to examine whether the associations of multimorbidity phenotypes with per-admission expenditure were sensitive to alternative ways of accounting for within-patient correlation due to repeated hospitalisations.

All analyses, except for the LCA, were conducted in Stata version 16.0. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The patient-level dataset included 33,044 unique individuals. The number of index admissions increased from 3,027 in 2020 to 9,969 in 2024. The cohort was predominantly male (64.39%) and mainly aged 60–74 years (49.32%). Most patients were farmers (68.31%) and married (90.76%). URRBMI was the main payment type (71.81%). Over time, short stays of 1–7 days became more common, the proportion undergoing surgery increased, and ICU transfers decreased. Most patients were discharged as improved, and in-hospital mortality remained low (Table 1).

Latent class analysis

Among the 33,044 patients, 28,140 (85.16%) had at least one of the 14 included comorbidities. The most common comorbidities were hypertension (53.39%), dyslipidaemia (23.65%), diabetes (21.88%), stroke (19.13%), and arrhythmia (15.89%). Models with two to ten classes were evaluated. Although AIC, BIC, and adjusted BIC continued to decline with additional classes, the reduction became smaller after the five-class model (Supplementary Fig. 3). Although the LMR and VLMR tests remained significant up to the seven-class model, the five-class solution showed the highest entropy (0.69), and entropy declined in the six- and seven-class models (Supplementary Table 4). These statistical results, together with review by five clinicians with relevant expertise, supported the five-class solution. The clinicians considered this solution to provide the clearest and most clinically meaningful phenotype structure, because each class showed a distinct pattern of coexisting conditions that could be readily recognised in practice, including low comorbidity, vascular disease, hypertension-dominant, diabetes-renal, and cardiac dysfunction profiles. Internal 5:5 split-sample validation showed acceptable reproducibility of this structure across subsamples (Supplementary Tables 5 and 6; Supplementary Fig. 4).

The five classes showed distinct multimorbidity profiles (Fig. 1). The low-comorbidity class had low probabilities for most conditions, with dyslipidaemia (25.86%) as its only notable feature. The vascular disease class was defined by peripheral vascular disease (89.47%), often with stroke (52.88%) and hypertension (67.71%). The hypertension-dominant class, the largest in the cohort, was characterised by universal hypertension (100%) with a moderate prevalence of dyslipidaemia (25.05%). The diabetes-renal class showed very high probabilities of uncomplicated diabetes (96.26%), hypertension (74.50%), and heart failure (54.46%), and it was the only class with a notable prevalence of complicated diabetes (40.17%) and renal failure (24.83%). The cardiac dysfunction class was distinguished by near-universal heart failure (96.68%), often with arrhythmia (53.13%) and valvular disease (9.10%) (Supplementary Table 7).

The classes also differed in demographic and clinical characteristics (Table 2). The low-comorbidity class was the youngest, with 44.30% aged 45–59 years. The cardiac dysfunction class was the oldest, with 35.66% aged 75 years or older and a higher proportion of patients without a spouse. The vascular disease class had the highest proportion of formal employment and UEBMI coverage. The cardiac dysfunction and vascular disease classes had the longest hospital stays, and the cardiac dysfunction class had the highest ICU transfer rate, the lowest surgery rate, and the worst discharge outcomes.

Associations between multimorbidity patterns and inpatient expenditure

In the adjusted GLM, all multimorbidity classes had higher per-admission expenditure than the low-comorbidity class (Table 3). The largest increases were observed in the cardiac dysfunction class (ER = 1.33, 95% CI: 1.30–1.36) and the diabetes-renal class (ER = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.23–1.32), whereas the hypertension-dominant (ER = 1.05, 95% CI: 1.03–1.06) and vascular disease classes (ER = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.00–1.06) showed smaller increases. Adjusted predicted mean expenditure showed the same pattern (Table 4). The highest mean per-admission costs were observed in the cardiac dysfunction class ($2,615.77; 95% CI: 2,562.30–2,669.24) and the diabetes-renal class ($2,502.99; 95% CI: 2,421.49–2,584.49), followed by the hypertension-dominant class ($2,054.74; 95% CI: 2,033.07–2,076.41), the vascular disease class ($2,022.59; 95% CI: 1,960.84–2,084.33), and the low-comorbidity class ($1,964.85; 95% CI: 1,943.54–1,986.16).

Predicted mean inpatient expenditure by multimorbidity patterns

Medical consumables accounted for the largest share of expenditure in all classes, ranging from 28.17% to 41.78%, with the highest proportions in the hypertension-dominant (41.78%) and low-comorbidity (40.08%) classes. By contrast, the cardiac dysfunction and diabetes-renal classes showed a larger contribution from non-consumable components. Medication costs accounted for 16.92% of expenditure in the cardiac dysfunction class and 14.01% in the diabetes-renal class, compared with 7.22% in the low-comorbidity class. The cardiac dysfunction class also had the highest share of diagnostic fees (24.33%), while the diabetes-renal class showed a similarly high diagnostic share (22.88%) (Fig. 2).

Inpatient expenditure composition by multimorbidity patterns

Several covariates were also associated with per-admission expenditure (Table 3). Compared with men, women had lower costs (ER = 0.82, 95% CI: 0.81–0.83), and ethnic minority patients had lower costs than Han patients (ER = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.85–0.96). By contrast, older age (≥ 75 years: ER = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.07–1.12) and URRBMI coverage (ER = 1.06, 95% CI: 1.04–1.09) were associated with higher expenditure. Clinical factors showed the strongest associations: compared with stays of 1–7 days, hospitalisation for 8–14 days was associated with a 77.8% increase in expenditure (ER = 1.78, 95% CI: 1.75–1.81), and stays of ≥ 15 days were associated with a more than fourfold increase (ER = 4.45, 95% CI: 4.25–4.67). In addition, admissions without surgery (ER = 0.34, 95% CI: 0.33–0.34) and those without ICU transfer (ER = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.76–0.80) had lower expenditure. Relative to 2020, expenditure in 2021–2024 remained consistently lower (ERs ranging from 0.68 to 0.70), indicating a marked temporal decline in per-admission costs.

Influencing factors of inpatient expenditure by multimorbidity patterns

Class-stratified GLMs showed that the correlates of per-admission expenditure differed across the five multimorbidity phenotypes (Supplementary Table 8). Length of stay and surgery were associated with expenditure in all classes, but the magnitude varied. Compared with stays of 1–7 days, hospitalisation for ≥ 15 days was associated with the largest increase in expenditure in the low-comorbidity (ER = 4.85, 95% CI: 4.40–5.35) and hypertension-dominant (ER = 4.89, 95% CI: 4.45–5.38) classes, and a smaller increase in the cardiac dysfunction class (ER = 3.61, 95% CI: 3.32–3.93). Admissions without surgery were associated with lower expenditure across all phenotypes.

Demographic and enabling factors appeared to matter more in the lower-burden phenotypes. Female sex was associated with lower expenditure in most classes, but the association was weaker in the cardiac dysfunction group. Older age (≥ 75 years) and URRBMI coverage were associated with higher expenditure in the low-comorbidity and hypertension-dominant classes, but not in the diabetes-renal or cardiac dysfunction classes.

A temporal decline in expenditure relative to 2020 was observed across most phenotypes, but it was more marked in the low-comorbidity and hypertension-dominant classes than in the cardiac dysfunction class. Readmissions within 180 days were also associated with higher expenditure in several classes.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses using GEE and mixed-effects models produced findings that were highly similar to those of the main gamma GLM (Supplementary Tables 9 and 10). In both alternative models, all multimorbidity phenotypes remained associated with higher per-admission expenditure than the low-comorbidity class, with the largest effects again observed in the cardiac dysfunction and diabetes-renal phenotypes. The direction and magnitude of the associations for key covariates, including sex, age, insurance type, length of stay, admission year, ICU transfer, surgery, and time between admissions, were also largely unchanged. These results indicate that the main findings were robust to alternative approaches for handling repeated hospitalisations.

Discussion

This study examined the association between multimorbidity phenotypes and inpatient expenditure among patients with CHD in a large tertiary hospital in North China. We identified five distinct phenotypes: low comorbidity, vascular disease, hypertension-dominant, diabetes-renal, and cardiac dysfunction. Among them, the cardiac dysfunction and diabetes-renal phenotypes were associated with the highest per-admission expenditure. We also found clear differences in expenditure composition across phenotypes. In addition, the associations of demographic and clinical factors with expenditure varied by phenotype.

The multimorbidity structure identified in this study was broadly consistent with previous research showing that hypertension, dyslipidaemia, and diabetes often cluster in patients with cardiovascular disease [36–38]. However, the identification of vascular disease and cardiac dysfunction as independent phenotypes in this study provides greater phenotypic granularity than community-based research. In a tertiary referral hospital, patients are more likely to present with advanced disease and greater clinical complexity than those managed in lower-level facilities. This may partly explain why vascular disease and cardiac dysfunction emerged as distinct phenotypes in our cohort. By contrast, musculoskeletal or pain-related patterns, which are often reported in population-based studies [38, 39], were not prominent in our data, possibly because inpatient records tend to capture acute and management-relevant conditions more completely than chronic but non-acute disorders.

Different phenotypes also showed clear differences in expenditure structure. The cardiac dysfunction and diabetes-renal groups had the highest adjusted mean cost per admission, whereas the low-comorbidity and hypertension-dominant groups had lower costs. Medical consumables accounted for the largest share of expenditure in every group, but this share was highest in the lower-burden phenotypes. By contrast, the cardiac dysfunction and diabetes-renal groups had larger shares of diagnostic and medication costs. Our data showed that these two groups also had longer hospital stays, higher ICU transfer rates, and worse discharge outcomes. This pattern suggests that expenditure in the more complex phenotypes depended less on standardised procedural consumables and more on close monitoring, repeated diagnostic assessment, and ongoing medical management [40]. In other words, their higher costs seem to reflect a different pattern of clinical complexity and resource use, not simply a greater number of comorbidities.

Per-admission expenditure fell markedly after 2020. This trend coincided with several major changes in China. National coronary stent volume-based procurement was formally implemented in late 2020 [41], and the DRG/DIP payment reform was further expanded through the three-year action plan issued in late 2021 [42]. These policy changes provide important context, but our data cannot show that they directly caused the decline. Other factors may also have contributed. For example, more standardised peri-procedural care and greater use of transradial percutaneous cardiovascular interventions (PCI) may shorten hospital stay and reduce procedural costs. Previous studies have shown that transradial PCI is associated with shorter stays and lower hospital costs [43, 44]. COVID-19 may also have changed admission patterns, admission thresholds, and case mix during the early part of the study period. A Beijing inpatient database study reported a significant decline in acute coronary syndrome admissions during the pandemic period [45]. Changes in coding, billing, or hospital workflow may also have played a role. The decline was larger in the low-comorbidity group than in the cardiac dysfunction group, which suggests that the change was not uniform across phenotypes. A possible explanation is that the lower-burden groups depended more on standardised procedures and consumables, whereas the more complex phenotypes depended more on diagnostic monitoring and medical management and may therefore have been less responsive to consumable-focused reforms. These explanations remain hypotheses, and future studies using quasi-experimental designs, such as difference-in-differences analyses, are needed to test them more directly.

From the perspective of Andersen’s Behavioural Model, our findings suggest that the relative importance of expenditure-related factors differs by multimorbidity phenotype. In the lower-burden phenotypes, older age and URRBMI coverage were still associated with higher costs. In the cardiac dysfunction and diabetes–renal phenotypes, these associations were no longer statistically significant. This pattern suggests that, as clinical complexity increases, inpatient expenditure becomes more closely related to disease severity and treatment needs than to socio-demographic characteristics. At the same time, the higher expenditure observed among URRBMI-covered patients in the lower-burden groups may point to differences in care pathways before admission, including less effective primary care management or delayed hospital presentation [46, 47]. This may reflect broader inequities in access to and continuity of CHD care.

This interpretation also helps explain why the low-comorbidity phenotype should not be viewed as clinically unimportant. Although this group had the lowest overall expenditure, it included a relatively young subgroup of patients with CHD. A low burden of recorded inpatient comorbidity does not necessarily indicate low cardiovascular risk. Rather, it may reflect the presence of important risk factors that were not captured in our dataset, such as smoking, obesity, physical inactivity, or family history [48, 49]. From a clinical and public health perspective, this finding highlights the value of earlier risk detection and prevention in patients who appear relatively uncomplicated at the time of admission, before progression to more complex and costly phenotypes.

These findings have several practical implications for CHD management, although they still need to be confirmed in larger multicentre longitudinal studies. First, the clear heterogeneity in expenditure across phenotypes suggests that DRG/DIP payment systems may need to better account for multimorbidity patterns. In particular, complex phenotypes such as cardiac dysfunction and diabetes-renal disease had greater diagnostic and medication intensity, which may not be fully captured by grouping systems based mainly on principal diagnosis. Incorporating multimorbidity information may therefore help improve the alignment between reimbursement and actual resource use. Second, the high-cost phenotypes were characterised by multi-organ involvement and more complex care needs. This suggests that fragmented management may be insufficient for these patients. More integrated and multidisciplinary care models may help improve coordination, streamline diagnostic pathways, and reduce the risks associated with polypharmacy [50]. Third, our results suggest that the role of socio-demographic factors may differ by phenotype. In lower-burden groups, expenditure was still associated with age and insurance status, whereas in more complex phenotypes, expenditure appeared to be more closely related to clinical severity and treatment needs. This pattern may indicate that inequities in care exert their strongest effects before patients reach the most severe stage of disease. From a public health perspective, this supports earlier risk identification and intervention in vulnerable populations, with the aim of reducing progression to more complex and costly multimorbidity phenotypes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was based on data from a single tertiary referral hospital. The findings therefore more likely reflect inpatient resource use among CHD patients treated in a tertiary-care setting than the full spectrum of CHD care across healthcare levels. Referral bias and greater case-mix severity are therefore likely. Second, comorbidities were identified from inpatient discharge coding rather than from outpatient history, laboratory data, or medication records. This may have led to under-recording of some chronic but non-acute conditions, including dyslipidaemia and musculoskeletal disorders. During CHD hospitalisation, discharge coding is likely to prioritise acute cardiovascular events and other admission-relevant diagnoses, which may reduce the capture of chronic upstream risk factors such as dyslipidaemia. Third, for latent class analysis we retained only conditions with a prevalence of at least 1.0%. Although this was done to reduce instability from sparse indicators and to focus on common multimorbidity patterns, it may have excluded rare but clinically important conditions and may therefore have influenced the exact composition of the identified phenotypes. Fourth, some classes, particularly the vascular disease and diabetes-renal groups, accounted for a relatively small proportion of the sample. Although they remained clinically interpretable and showed acceptable internal reproducibility, estimates for these groups may still be less precise. Fifth, we did not have data on important behavioural and outpatient risk factors, including smoking, obesity, medication adherence, and health literacy. Residual confounding therefore remains possible. Finally, this was an observational study, and all findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects.

Conclusion

Among middle-aged and older inpatients with CHD, multimorbidity was highly prevalent and formed five distinct phenotypes. The cardiac dysfunction and diabetes-renal phenotypes were associated with the highest per-admission expenditure, compared with the low-comorbidity group. These high-cost phenotypes also showed a different expenditure structure, with relatively greater contributions from diagnostic and medication costs, whereas lower-burden phenotypes had a larger share of medical consumables. Together, these findings show that multimorbidity patterns are associated not only with the level of inpatient expenditure but also with how hospital resources are used. This supports the value of moving beyond simple comorbidity counts when examining hospital costs in CHD. Further multicentre studies are needed to validate these phenotypes and assess their relevance across different levels of care and provider payment settings.
