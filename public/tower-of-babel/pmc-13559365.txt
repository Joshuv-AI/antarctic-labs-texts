Development of clinical-radiological prediction models to prioritize ALK, ROS1, and RET fusion testing in major driver alteration-negative non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Yike Wang, Yingbo Xie, Xuan Yao, Kelan Ke, Pei Ma, Fengnan Wang, Qin Liu, Xiaohong Xie, Ming Liu, Xinqing Lin, Chengzhi Zhou, Zhanhong Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLung cancer is the leading cause of cancer mortality worldwide. Anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), c-ros oncogene 1 (ROS1), and rearranged during transfection (RET) fusions occur in 1–3% of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and require prompt targeted therapy; however, universal next-generation sequencing (NGS) is often delayed or unavailable. This study aimed to develop clinical-radiological models to prioritize fusion testing.The aim of this study was to explore the association between combining radiological and clinical features and ALK, ROS1 and RET fusion in NSCLC.MethodsA retrospective analysis was carried out on 130 patients diagnosed with fusion gene-positive NSCLC. Of these patients, 85 were ALK-positive, 23 were ROS1-positive, and 22 were RET-positive. Furthermore, 318 cases that tested major driver alteration-negative controls were included. Clinical, serological, and radiological features were compared. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed, and models were internally validated using 1,000-bootstrap resampling.ResultsMultivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that among ALK fusion gene-positive patients, utilizing a combination of age, carbohydrate antigen 153 (CA153), pericardial effusion, and vertebral metastasis resulted in an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.803 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.748–0.858, P<0.001]. For ROS1 fusion gene-positive patients, combining smoking status, thrombotic events, D-dimer (DD2), supraclavicular lymph node metastasis, and vertebral metastasis led to an AUC of 0.894 (95% CI: 0.839–0.949, P<0.001). The RET model (thrombosis and esophageal invasion) demonstrated poor discriminative performance (AUC 0.617, 95% CI: 0.479–0.756), precluding reliable clinical application.ConclusionsIn major driver alteration-negative lung adenocarcinoma, particularly among younger adults, elevated DD2 and specific metastatic patterns (vertebral metastasis, pericardial effusion, supraclavicular lymph node involvement) enable risk-stratified prioritization of ALK and ROS1 fusion testing. This exploratory triage strategy may guide timely utilization of immunohistochemistry (IHC), or fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) when comprehensive (NGS) is delayed or unavailable, optimizing treatment initiation in advanced disease. However, these models require prospective external validation before clinical implementation and should not replace guideline-recommended comprehensive molecular testing. The RET findings are hypothesis-generating only and cannot support clinical decision-making.

Highlight box

Key findings

• In major driver alteration-negative non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), multivariate models achieved areas under the curve (AUCs) of 0.803 for anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) [age ≤50.5 years, carbohydrate antigen 153 (CA153) ≥42.83 U/mL, pericardial effusion, and vertebral metastasis] and 0.894 for c-ros oncogene 1 (ROS1) [smoking status, thrombosis, D-dimer (DD2) ≥1,203 ng/mL, supraclavicular and vertebral metastasis]. DD2 was significantly elevated in both ALK-positive and ROS1-positive cohorts. The rearranged during transfection (RET) model showed poor discrimination (AUC 0.617) and is not suitable for clinical use.

What is known and what is new?

• It is known that ALK, ROS1, and RET fusions occur in 1–5% of NSCLC, are associated with rapid progression, and require prompt targeted therapy; however, universal next-generation sequencing is often delayed or unavailable in resource-limited settings.

• This study is the first to construct fusion-specific, clinically applicable triage models within a unified major driver alteration-negative control cohort, quantifying distinct metastatic patterns and identifying CA153 and DD2 as accessible biomarkers. Reliable RET-specific prediction cannot be achieved with small sample sizes.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• When comprehensive next-generation sequencing is delayed or unavailable, clinicians may use this risk-stratified approach (combining younger age, elevated DD2, and specific metastatic patterns) to prioritize rapid immunohistochemistry or fluorescence in situ hybridization testing for ALK and ROS1 fusions, thereby accelerating treatment initiation. This exploratory strategy requires prospective external validation and should not replace guideline-recommended comprehensive molecular testing.

Introduction

Based on the 2022 GLOBOCAN report, there were 2.48 million new cases of lung cancer and 1.81 million fatalities globally, establishing it as a prevalent cancer worldwide and a primary contributor to cancer-related mortality (1). Pathologically, lung cancer is classified into small cell lung cancer (SCLC) and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) (2). Around 85% of lung cancers belong to the category of NSCLC, which encompasses variations like lung adenocarcinoma and lung squamous cell carcinoma. Within this classification, lung adenocarcinoma stands out as the predominant subtype (3). In individuals with non-smoking lung adenocarcinoma, there is a notably elevated incidence of EGFR fusions, reaching 74.5% (4). In recent years, advances in genetic testing technology have led to a steady increase in the number of patients identified with fusion genes such as anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), rearranged during transfection (RET), and c-ros oncogene 1 (ROS1) (5). As molecular medicine evolves, a growing array of targeted therapies has entered clinical practice, marking a transition in lung cancer treatment toward precision medicine and personalized care (6-8). The implementation of targeted therapies significantly enhances the quality of life for patients with advanced lung cancer and prolongs their survival. Consequently, driver gene testing has become an essential prerequisite for treatment in lung cancer patients, especially those with advanced disease (9,10).

ALK, a receptor tyrosine kinase with the typical structure of such receptors, is predominantly found in the central and peripheral nervous systems, with its precise physiological role still not fully understood (11,12). Nevertheless, studies have demonstrated a correlation between ALK gene fusions and the development and advancement of various tumors (13). In 1994, Morris et al. initially detected the nucleophosmin (NPM)-ALK fusions fusion in anaplastic large cell lymphoma (14). Subsequent studies have revealed more than 20 different ALK gene fusions in various types of tumors. The prevalence of ALK fusions is 3.0% in NSCLC and 3.9% in adenocarcinoma (15-17). At present, lorlatinib stands out as a primary therapy for individuals diagnosed with ALK-positive NSCLC, showcasing notably higher effectiveness than crizotinib in various trials, substantially prolonging progression-free survival (PFS) (18-20). The ROS1 gene translocation is recognized as an additional driver gene in NSCLC (21). This translocation constitutes a distinct molecular subtype within NSCLC, occurring in approximately 1–2% of cases (22). Its prevalence is notably higher in patients with EGFR and ALK double-negative lung adenocarcinoma (23). Moreover, ROS1, a receptor tyrosine kinase belonging to the insulin receptor family, shows considerable amino acid homology with the kinase domain of ALK (24). As a result, crizotinib, an ALK tyrosine kinase inhibitor, exhibits strong efficacy against ROS1-translocated NSCLC (25). The activation mechanism of RET fusion proteins resembles that of ALK fusion proteins; however, their amino acid homology with the kinase domain of ALK is lower than that of ROS1 (26). RET fusions are observed in 1-3% of lung cancer patients (27).

Substantial advancements have been achieved in the development of specific treatments for uncommon driver gene fusions like ALK, ROS1, and RET, leading to a considerable extension of median PFS in individuals with advanced NSCLC (28). The effectiveness of these medications relies significantly on molecular subtyping, necessitating patients to undergo precise genetic testing to validate the existence of relevant targets (29,30). Fusion gene-positive tumors are marked by rapid progression and a poor prognosis (31-33). Consequently, it is essential to ascertain the fusion status and promptly initiate appropriate targeted therapy upon diagnosis. Neglecting this may lead to patients missing the optimal treatment window due to swift disease deterioration. Traditional next-generation sequencing (NGS) testing is expensive and frequently limited by economic constraints. In critically ill patients, obtaining adequate tissue samples may be impractical, and utilizing peripheral blood samples can result in false-negative outcomes due to the technical challenges associated with detecting fusion genes through NGS. Additionally, the limited availability of tissue specimens in certain cases complicates the ability to conduct repeated or multi-platform validation. Consequently, the rapid identification of patients with fusion genes, while maintaining accuracy, presents a significant challenge that necessitates urgent attention in clinical practice (34-36).

Tumor markers and imaging features play a crucial role in the diagnosis, treatment, and monitoring of recurrence in NSCLC (37,38). Nevertheless, limited research has examined the relationship between imaging characteristics and other clinical features, including ALK, ROS1, and RET fusions in NSCLC. Consequently, we undertook this retrospective study to investigate these parameters and assess their association with ALK, ROS1, and RET fusions in NSCLC.

Despite international guidelines recommending universal fusion gene testing in advanced NSCLC, real-world implementation is frequently constrained by tissue inadequacy, financial limitations, and turnaround time. Current algorithms lack evidence-based triage tools to identify patients at highest likelihood for fusion positivity among major driver alteration-negative cohorts. This study addresses this gap by integrating routinely available clinical, serological, and radiological parameters to construct predictive models for ALK, ROS1, and RET fusions, facilitating precision diagnostic pathways in resource-limited settings. We present this article in accordance with the STARD reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1400/rc).

Methods

Study population and inclusion criteria

We collected clinical data from all 2,458 patients with NSCLC who underwent NGS testing at The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University between June 2015 and October 2024. A total of 2,110 patients were excluded for several reasons: lack of pathological and cytological confirmation; genetic testing indicating fusions in EGFR, KRAS, MET, BRAF, HER2, and others; presence of concomitant malignancies; prior antitumor treatment through surgery or other modalities; and incomplete clinical data. This study ultimately enrolled 448 patients, comprising 85 ALK-positive, 23 ROS1-positive, 22 RET-positive, and 318 major driver alteration-negative cases (Figure 1). Patients who had never smoked or had smoked fewer than 100 cigarettes in total were classified as non-smokers, while the remainder were categorized as former smokers. Clinical staging was conducted using computed tomography (CT) or other imaging modalities. Tumor lymph node metastasis (TNM) staging adhered to the International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer (IASLC) 9th Edition TNM Classification for Lung Cancer. All cases underwent centralized pathological review by two board-certified pathologists. Pulmonary lymphoepithelioma-like carcinoma (LELC) was diagnosed according to World Health Organization (WHO) classification criteria, characterized by syncytial growth patterns, prominent lymphoid stroma, and undifferentiated morphology. Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) status was assessed by Epstein-Barr virus-encoded RNA in situ hybridization (EBER-ISH) in all 27 LELC cases, with positive nuclear staining confirming EBV association. Among the 318 driver-negative controls, 27 cases (8.5%) were confirmed as EBV-positive LELC, reflecting the regional epidemiology of southern China. Blood samples were collected within 7 days prior to any antitumor therapy and before invasive procedures (biopsy, thoracentesis, catheterization). For patients with thrombosis, the DD2 value used was the pre-anticoagulation baseline obtained within 24 hours before treatment initiation. The median interval between blood sampling and NGS testing was 5 days. For patients with multiple measurements, the first diagnostic baseline value was used. From 2015 to 2017, a 56-gene DNA-based NGS panel was used; from 2018 to 2024, a 425-gene panel (OncoScreen Plus, Burning Rock Biotech, Guangzhou, China) was uniformly applied. Both panels included ALK, ROS1, and RET rearrangement detection. ALK immunohistochemistry (IHC) (Ventana D5F3, Tucson, AZ, USA) and FISH were used as confirmatory assays for all ALK-positive cases. ROS1 and RET were assessed uniformly by NGS across the entire study period. All imaging findings were retrospectively reviewed by two independent thoracic radiologists who were blinded to molecular status. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University (Medical Research Ethics Review No. 2022-164) and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

Clinical data collection

Collect the clinical characteristics and baseline imaging features of patients with ALK-positive, ROS1-positive, RET-positive, and major driver alteration-negative NSCLC. The imaging examinations encompass CT of the chest, upper abdomen, and lower abdomen; positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT); cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI); emission computed tomography (ECT); and lower extremity venous color Doppler ultrasound. All patients underwent the aforementioned imaging examinations to evaluate primary tumor size, thrombosis, metastases (including bilateral multiple, bronchial, vascular, esophageal, pleural, pleural effusion, pleural metastases, pericardial effusion, and pulmonary lymphangitis), as well as lymph node involvement (ipsilateral hilar, ipsilateral mediastinal, contralateral hilar, contralateral mediastinal, and supraclavicular), distant lymph node metastasis, and metastases in non-vertebral bones, vertebrae, brain, liver, adrenal glands, kidneys, spleen, pancreas, skin, and muscles. Esophageal invasion was defined as direct tumor infiltration or compression of the esophageal wall by the primary tumor or adjacent metastatic lymph nodes on CT, characterized by loss of the fat plane, esophageal wall thickening, or mucosal irregularity. Blood samples were collected and analyzed in the clinical laboratory of our hospital for D-dimer (DD2), neuron-specific enolase (NSE), carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), carbohydrate antigen 125 (CA125), carbohydrate antigen 153 (CA153), cytokeratin 19 fragment antigen (CYFRA21-1), and squamous cell carcinoma antigen (SCC).

Statistical analysis

Univariate logistic regression was performed to identify factors associated with ALK, ROS1, and RET fusion positivity. Variables with P<0.05 in univariate analysis underwent multicollinearity assessment using variance inflation factor (VIF); variables with VIF >5 were excluded. Backward stepwise multivariable logistic regression with entry P<0.05 and stay P<0.05 was applied to identify independent predictors. For each final model, we calculated the EPV ratio as the number of fusion-positive events divided by the number of predictors. The discriminative performance of each final model was assessed by the area under the curve (AUC). All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

To address potential confounding by histologic heterogeneity, we performed a sensitivity analysis restricted to lung adenocarcinoma. After excluding non-adenocarcinoma histologies (squamous cell carcinoma, lymphoepithelial carcinoma, large cell carcinoma, and undefined NSCLC) from the control group, the adenocarcinoma-only cohort comprised 82 ALK-positive, 20 ROS1-positive, 21 RET-positive, and 222 pan-negative controls. Univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were repeated using the same variable selection protocol as the primary analysis. Serum marker comparisons (CEA, CA125, CA153, CYFRA21-1, SCC, NSE, DD2) were also repeated in this histology-homogeneous subset for the ALK cohort; similar histology-restricted serum marker comparisons were not performed for ROS1 (n=20) and RET (n=21) due to insufficient statistical power.

To assess whether identified predictors were merely surrogates for advanced disease stage, we restricted the analysis to patients with stage IIIB–IVB disease (ALK: n=78, ROS1: n=23, RET: n=17, controls: n=271). Univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were repeated using the same variable selection protocol.

To assess model stability and quantify overfitting, we performed 1,000-bootstrap resampling with replacement for each final multivariable model across all three analytic scenarios (full cohort, adenocarcinoma-only, and stage IIIB–IVB). This approach provides a conservative estimate of model performance that accounts for overfitting inherent to small sample sizes. Bootstrap analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1 (packages pROC and boot).

Results

This study assessed the general characteristics and various clinical and imaging features associated with molecular diagnosis in 2,458 patients with NSCLC who underwent NGS testing at our hospital from 2015 to 2024. Among these patients, 139 cases (5.67%) were identified as ALK-positive, 46 cases (1.87%) as ROS1-positive, and 33 cases (1.34%) as RET-positive. These proportions are consistent with the overall fusion gene detection rates documented in both domestic and international literature. Association between radiological characteristics and other clinical features and ALK status

A total of 85 ALK-positive patients were included in the study. It was observed that ALK fusion-positive NSCLC patients tended to be younger {50 [interquartile range (IQR), 42–60.5] vs. 63 (IQR, 53–68) years; P<0.001], with no significant variations in terms of gender or smoking history. NSE levels [22.00 (IQR, 15.76–30.05) vs. 17.70 (IQR, 14.15–23.38) ng/mL; P=0.001], CA125 levels [77.2 (IQR, 34.47–154.76) vs. 37.71 (IQR, 15.41–94.98) U/mL; P<0.001], and CA153 levels [54.67 (IQR, 21.47–110.8) vs. 18.04 (IQR, 10.67–38.44) U/mL; P<0.001] were notably elevated in ALK fusion patients compared to major driver alteration-negative patients. Conversely, no significant differences were noted for CEA levels [8.38 (IQR, 3.11–45.48) vs. 5.79 (IQR, 2.91–29.03) ng/mL; P=0.34], CYFRA21-1, or SCC. The DD2 levels in the ALK fusion-positive cohort [1,092 (IQR, 540–3,097) vs. 710 (IQR, 366.5–1,287.5) ng/mL fibrinogen equivalent unit (FEU); P=0.003] were significantly higher than those in the major driver alteration-negative group, irrespective of the presence or absence of thrombosis. Pathological diagnosis of lung adenocarcinoma was confirmed in 82 cases (96.5%) within the ALK fusion-positive group and in 222 cases (69.8%) within the major driver alteration-negative group, indicating a statistically significant difference between the two groups (P<0.001). Furthermore, the ALK-positive group exhibited notable differences in tumor staging when compared to the major driver alteration-negative group. Specifically, the ALK-positive group presented with more advanced tumor staging, as evidenced by N staging (P=0.009), M staging (P=0.02), and overall TNM staging (P=0.01). In terms of imaging findings, patients in the ALK-positive group demonstrated significantly higher rates of ipsilateral pulmonary multiple metastases (51.8% vs. 37.7%, P=0.02), contralateral pulmonary multiple metastases (36.5% vs. 31.8%, P<0.001), pericardial effusion (29.4% vs. 11.9%, P<0.001), ipsilateral mediastinal lymph node metastasis (85.9% vs. 71.7%, P=0.008), contralateral mediastinal lymph node metastasis (42.4% vs. 26.4%, P=0.004), contralateral hilar lymph node metastasis (40.0% vs. 24.2%, P=0.004), supraclavicular lymph node metastasis (37.6% vs. 23.0%, P=0.006), vertebral metastasis (27.1% vs. 4.1%, P<0.001), and skin or muscle metastasis (8.2% vs. 1.6%, P=0.004). No statistically significant differences were found in other imaging findings.

Association between radiological characteristics and other clinical features and ROS1 status

Twenty-three patients with ROS1 positivity were enrolled in the study. Among these, ROS1 fusion patients were predominantly non-smokers, with a prevalence of 82.6% compared to 58.5% in the control group (P=0.02). The ROS1-positive cohort showed a higher occurrence of thrombosis (13.0% vs. 2.8%; P=0.04) and notably elevated DD2 levels [3,182 (IQR, 1,301–6,500) vs. 710 (IQR, 366.5–1,287.5) ng/mL FEU; P<0.001]. Additionally, levels of CA125 [138.5 (IQR, 82.7–326.6) vs. 37.71 (IQR, 15.41–94.98) U/mL; P<0.001], CA153 [58.39 (IQR, 47.27–81.15) vs. 18.04 (IQR, 10.67–38.44) U/mL; P<0.001], and SCC [1.7 (IQR, 0.8–4.4) vs. 1.00 (IQR, 0.6–1.98) ng/mL; P=0.02] were significantly higher in ROS1-fusion patients compared to major driver alteration-negative patients. However, no significant variances were noted for CEA [12.5 (IQR, 2.9–66.51) vs. 5.79 (IQR, 2.91–29.03) ng/mL; P=0.40], NSE, and CYFRA21-1. In tumor staging, the ROS1-positive group exhibited a higher proportion of N3-stage patients compared to the major driver alteration-negative group, with rates of 78.3% and 39.0%, respectively (P<0.001). Imaging findings indicated that ROS1-positive patients had increased rates of contralateral pulmonary metastases (39.1% vs. 31.8%, P=0.004), pleural effusion (65.2% vs. 35.8%, P=0.005), pericardial effusion (39.1% vs. 11.9%, P=0.001), carcinomatous lymphangitis (39.1% vs. 19.2%, P=0.04), ipsilateral mediastinal lymph node metastasis (100.0% vs. 71.7%, P=0.003), contralateral mediastinal lymph node metastasis (60.9% vs. 26.4%, P<0.001), contralateral hilar lymph node metastasis (56.5% vs. 24.2%, P=0.001), supraclavicular lymph node metastasis (56.5% vs. 23.0%, P<0.001), and vertebral metastasis (30.4% vs. 4.1%, P<0.001).

Association between radiological characteristics and other clinical features and RET status

Twenty-two RET-positive patients were enrolled in the study. Among these, patients with RET fusions were more frequently female (59.1% vs. 33.6%; P=0.01) and non-smokers (81.8% vs. 58.5%; P=0.03). CA125 levels were significantly elevated in RET fusion patients compared to major driver alteration-negative patients, with values of [127.2 (IQR, 32.10–504.7) vs. 37.71 (IQR, 15.41–94.98) U/mL; P=0.005]. In contrast, CEA levels [10.2 (IQR, 1.80–45.31) vs. 5.79 (IQR, 2.91–29.03) ng/mL; P=0.92], as well as NSE, CA153, CYFRA21-1, and SCC, did not exhibit significant differences. The DD2 levels were also significantly higher in the RET fusion-positive group than in the major driver alteration-negative group, recorded at [2,797 (IQR, 1,041–6,538) vs. 710 (IQR, 366.5–1,287.5); P<0.001]. Furthermore, the RET-positive group demonstrated a greater propensity for thrombosis (18.2% vs. 2.8%; P=0.006). RET-positive patients, compared to the major driver alteration-negative group, showed a higher prevalence of adenocarcinoma (P=0.03). In terms of radiological findings, RET-positive patients had a higher occurrence of esophageal invasion (13.6% vs. 1.3%; P=0.007) and vertebral metastases (18.2% vs. 4.1%; P=0.01). Conversely, major driver alteration-negative patients were more prone to developing contralateral pulmonary metastases (68.2% vs. 27.3%, P<0.001). There were no other significant differences in clinical characteristics between the two groups.

Comparison radiological characteristics and other clinical features with ALK, ROS1, and RET fusions

Sensitivity analysis in adenocarcinoma-only cohort

Given the histologic imbalance between fusion-positive groups (predominantly adenocarcinoma) and the mixed-histology control group, we performed a sensitivity analysis restricted to lung adenocarcinoma. After exclusion of 96 non-adenocarcinoma cases from the control group, the final adenocarcinoma-only cohort comprised 82 ALK-positive, 20 ROS1-positive, 21 RET-positive, and 222 major driver alteration-negative controls. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were repeated with 1,000-bootstrap internal validation (Table S1). The optimism-corrected AUCs were 0.790 (95% CI: 0.720–0.836) for the ALK model (age, CA153, vertebral metastasis), 0.881 (95% CI: 0.779–0.926) for the ROS1 model (thrombosis, DD2, supraclavicular and vertebral metastasis), and 0.631 (95% CI: 0.539–0.744) for the RET model (thrombosis, esophageal invasion). Notably, the ALK and ROS1 models showed minimal AUC change compared with the full cohort, indicating robustness to histologic restriction. In contrast, the RET model’s poor performance persisted, confirming that its near-random discrimination was attributable to small sample size rather than histologic confounding.

Sensitivity analysis in advanced-stage (IIIB–IVB) cohort

To address potential confounding by disease stage, we restricted the analysis to patients with stage IIIB-IVB disease (ALK: n=76, ROS1: n=23, RET: n=17, controls: n=271). In this advanced-stage subset, the optimism-corrected AUCs were 0.819 for ALK, 0.868 for ROS1, and 0.601 for RET (Table S2). The direction and magnitude of ORs for key predictors remained consistent with the main analysis, although CIs were wider due to reduced sample sizes. These findings suggest that the identified radiological features for ALK and ROS1 are not merely surrogates for advanced disease stage, but may reflect fusion-specific biological behavior. The RET model again demonstrated poor discrimination, reinforcing its unsuitability for clinical triage.

Histology-restricted serum marker analysis in adenocarcinoma-only cohort

To assess whether serum marker differences were confounded by the mixed-histology control group, we restricted the ALK analysis to lung adenocarcinoma (ALK: n=82, controls: n=222). In this histology-homogeneous comparison, CA153 (OR =1.01, 95% CI: 1.006–1.015, P<0.001) and DD2 (P=0.003) remained elevated in ALK-positive patients. NSE also remained significant (OR =1.021, 95% CI: 1.005–1.037, P=0.009). In contrast, SCC (P=0.91) and CYFRA21-1 (P=0.21) showed no significant differences, confirming that their apparent associations in the full cohort were driven by the inclusion of squamous cell carcinoma and other non-adenocarcinoma histologies in the control group. CEA remained non-significant (P=0.75). Due to insufficient sample sizes, similar histology-restricted analyses were not performed for ROS1 (n=20) or RET (n=21) (Tables S3,S4).

Discussion

In this study, ALK fusion gene-positive patients with NSCLC were more prevalent among younger individuals, aligning with prior studies (37). Multiple studies have validated that heightened serum levels of NSE and CA125 serve as autonomous prognosticators of ALK fusion positivity (39,40). This study further demonstrated that CA153 levels were significantly elevated in ALK-positive patients compared to negative controls. While CA153 has traditionally been considered a marker for breast cancer, its encoding gene, MUC1, can be directly upregulated by EML4-ALK signaling in lung adenocarcinoma (41). This discovery may offer a novel auxiliary indicator for the initial clinical screening of ALK-positive NSCLC. Patients with ALK-positive tumors demonstrated significantly elevated levels of DD2 compared to those with major driver alteration-negative tumors, indicative of the high-coagulation state associated with ALK-driven tumors, as reported by Roopkumar et al. (42). Previous research indicates that the cumulative incidence of venous thromboembolism (VTE) in ALK-rearranged NSCLC ranges from 16.7% to 47.1%, substantially higher than the incidence in the overall NSCLC population (6.6–24.7%) (43-45). Another study corroborated this finding, reporting a similar range of 17.1% to 41.8% (46,47). However, this cohort did not replicate the disparity between the two groups, likely because most enrolled patients were in the pre-treatment phase and had not yet commenced therapy. Conversely, the persistence of CA153 and DD2 elevations after restricting to adenocarcinoma supports their potential as ALK-specific biomarkers independent of histologic confounding. Previous literature has documented a higher incidence of lymph node, pleural, and brain metastases in cases with ALK fusions. Gao et al. conducted a review of multiple studies and found that patients with ALK fusions exhibited an increased susceptibility to lymph node, pleural, and brain metastases (48). Woo et al., in a study involving 1,056 postoperative adenocarcinoma cases, reported that EML4-ALK positivity was linked to a greater likelihood of mediastinal lymph node and pleural recurrence and metastasis (49). In our study, vertebral metastasis emerged as the most significant predictor of ALK positivity (OR =7.56, P<0.001), occurring in 27.1% of ALK-positive cases compared to only 4.1% in the negative group. This finding suggests that the vertebrae may serve as the primary and predominant site of bone metastasis within the ALK-positive subgroup. The ALK model retained an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.819 in stage IIIB–IVB disease, with identical predictors as the full cohort and adenocarcinoma-only analyses. The biological rationale for these stage-independent associations is compelling.

Patients with ROS1 fusion genes are strongly associated with non-smoking status, a correlation that has been validated in multiple large-scale studies (37). This study confirms that elevated serum CA125 levels are significantly correlated with ROS1 fusion positivity, aligning with previous findings (50). These results further support the use of CA125 as a reliable initial screening indicator for ROS1-positive NSCLC. This study is the first to show that CA153 levels are notably elevated in patients with ROS1 fusions, suggesting a potential serological marker for this subtype. Our results reveal that ROS1-positive patients have significantly higher DD2 levels than the major driver alteration-negative group, indicating a procoagulant state in these patients. Several studies have reported a substantially increased thrombosis risk in ROS1 fusion patients (51,52). One proposed mechanism suggests that ROS1 fusions stimulate excessive mucin secretion by tumor cells, which in turn promotes the extrinsic coagulation cascade by activating platelets and damaging vascular endothelium (53,54). This process leads to a hypercoagulable state in ROS1-positive NSCLC. Multivariate logistic regression analysis in this study indicated that supraclavicular lymph node metastasis (OR =4.064, 95% CI: 1.23–13.43) and vertebral metastasis (OR =8.173, 95% CI: 2.088–31.992) serve as independent predictors in patients with ROS1 fusion gene positivity (P=0.02), thereby quantifying their association strength for the first time within a major driver alteration-negative control cohort. While prior studies have suggested that ROS1-positive patients exhibit a higher propensity for supraclavicular and vertebral lesions (55), direct statistical comparisons with the major driver alteration-negative group have been absent. This study offers evidence-based support for such comparisons; however, external validation in large-scale cohorts is still required. The ROS1 model achieved an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.868 in the advanced-stage cohort, with consistent predictor retention. These features demonstrated robustness to major confounders and support their potential utility as triage tools in advanced-stage disease. The RET model’s consistent failure across all scenarios confirms that reliable prediction cannot be achieved with the available sample size, regardless of stage distribution or histologic restriction.

To date, large-scale cohort studies investigating the clinical characteristics of RET fusion-positive NSCLC remain scarce. Findings from a single-center, large-sample study indicate that RET fusion-positive NSCLC is more frequently observed in female patients and non-smokers, suggesting unique epidemiological and biological features. Chen et al. conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis, revealing that RET fusions primarily occur in younger, non-smoking Asian females diagnosed with lung adenocarcinoma (56). Additionally, this study was the first to show that serum levels of CA125 and CA153 were significantly elevated in RET fusion-positive patients when compared to major driver alteration-negative controls. This finding necessitates validation in larger external cohorts. Large-scale studies assessing the true incidence of thromboembolism in RET fusion-positive NSCLC are limited. Among the published Phase I/II clinical trials, only one case of thromboembolism (2.9%) was reported among 34 patients treated with alectinib (57), while no thromboembolic events were observed in the prunatinib Phase II trial (58). Conversely, a recent retrospective cohort study revealed a thromboembolism incidence as high as 48% in RET-positive patients (59), indicating significant heterogeneity in the existing literature. To clarify the actual thrombotic risk in this population, expanded sample sizes and standardized diagnostic criteria are essential. In our study, the RET fusion-positive group demonstrated significantly elevated levels of DD2 compared to the major driver alteration-negative group, which was associated with a markedly increased incidence of clinical thrombotic events. Notably, our study is the first to quantify this association within a unified major driver alteration-negative control framework, revealing that RET fusion-positive patients exhibited the highest thrombotic risk (18.2% vs. 2.8%, P=0.006) despite their small sample size. This study found a higher incidence of esophageal and vertebral invasion in RET fusion-positive NSCLC patients compared to major driver alteration-negative controls, although external validation with a larger sample size is necessary. Current research indicates that RET fusion-positive patients often present with brain metastases, with a 25% incidence at diagnosis among stage IV patients (60). Chinese patients demonstrate similar patterns to those observed in Western populations, with brain metastasis rates at diagnosis ranging from 17.7% to 27.8% (61,62). In addition to the brain, vertebral bodies are also susceptible to metastasis. While RET-positive patients do not exhibit significant differences in overall bone metastasis rates compared to ALK and ROS1 groups, vertebral involvement is the most prevalent once metastasis occurs (63).

Fusion gene fusions in NSCLC are closely linked to younger age, female gender, non-smoking status, and adenocarcinoma histology; however, differences exist among various genes. Digumarthy et al. (64) reported that patients with ROS1 fusions were older than those with ALK fusions (55 vs. 50 years, P=0.01). In contrast, Wu et al. (65) found no significant age difference between ROS1-positive and ROS1-negative patients, although ROS1-positive patients exhibited a younger median age (53 vs. 62 years). Additionally, Digumarthy et al. (64) noted a higher proportion of females (72%) in the ROS1-rearranged group compared to the ALK-rearranged group (45%). Conversely, Song et al. (66) reported similar proportions of female patients in both the ALK- and ROS1-rearranged groups (52.8% and 53%, respectively). Digumarthy et al. (63) observed that the RET group was significantly older than the ALK group (64 vs. 51 years, P<0.001), with no differences in gender, smoking status, or bone metastases. These discrepancies in findings may arise from the low incidence of the disease and small sample sizes across studies. Notably, this study found that DD2 was significantly elevated in all three groups compared to the major driver alteration-negative group; however, the RET-positive group exhibited the highest thrombotic risk (18.2% vs. 2.8%, P=0.006).

A critical limitation of our study is the small number of events for ROS1 (n=23) and RET (n=22) relative to the number of predictors, resulting in events-per-variable (EPV) ratios of 4.6 and 11.0, respectively. The ROS1 EPV of 4.6 substantially falls below the conventional threshold of 10:1 recommended for stable logistic regression, substantially increasing the risk of overfitting, parameter inflation, and optimistic performance estimates. While the RET EPV of 11.0 marginally exceeds this threshold, the overall event number of 22 remains precarious for reliable model development. The wide CIs for ROS1 and RET ORs further underscore small-sample imprecision. These intervals encompass both null effects and extreme associations, rendering the point estimates clinically uninterpretable. We explicitly caution that these estimates should not guide patient management or testing prioritization decisions.

Research indicates that for ALK fusions, an age of ≤50.5 years and a CA153 level of ≥42.83 U/mL are associated with ALK fusions in NSCLC. When the four factors of age, CA153, pericardial effusion, and vertebral metastasis were combined, the AUC was 0.803 (95% CI: 0.748–0.858, P<0.001). In the case of ROS1 fusions, a DD2 level of ≥1,203 ng/mL FEU correlated with ROS1 fusions in NSCLC. When the five factors of smoking status, thrombotic status, DD2, supraclavicular lymph node metastasis, and vertebral metastasis were combined, the AUC was 0.894 (95% CI: 0.839–0.949, P<0.001). For RET fusions, the combination of thrombotic status and esophageal invasion yielded an AUC of 0.617 (95% CI: 0.479–0.756, P=0.07).

DD2 levels were significantly elevated across all three groups: ALK [1,092 (IQR, 540–3,097) vs. 710 (IQR, 366.5–1,287.5) ng/mL; P=0.003], ROS1 [3,182 (IQR, 1,301–6,500) vs. 710 (IQR, 366.5–1,287.5) ng/mL; P<0.001], and RET [2,797 (IQR, 1,041–6,538) vs. 710 (IQR, 366.5–1,287.5) ng/mL; P<0.001]. Multivariate logistic regression analysis further validated that ROS1 fusions (OR =6.466, 95% CI: 1.129–37.039, P=0.04). DD2 is a dynamic biomarker influenced by acute thrombosis, infection, and inflammation; our single baseline measurement may not fully capture its temporal variability.

The RET AUC is 0.617, and the adenocarcinoma-only sensitivity analysis yielded an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.629 (95% CI: 0.485–0.773). The near-identical performance between the full mixed-histology cohort (AUC 0.617) and the adenocarcinoma-restricted cohort (AUC 0.629) carries an important methodological implication: histologic heterogeneity was not the primary driver of the RET model’s poor performance. Rather, the model inherently lacks discriminative power due to the very small number of RET-positive events (n=22), resulting in an EPV ratio of approximately 11 for a two-factor model. This falls well below the conventional threshold of 10 events per predictor variable recommended for stable logistic regression. Consequently, the wide CIs and the near-random discrimination reflect fundamental statistical instability rather than confounding by histologic subtype. We therefore conclude that the RET findings in this study are strictly hypothesis-generating and cannot support clinical triage. Future development of RET-specific prediction models will require substantially larger, multicenter cohorts to achieve adequate statistical power and reliable external validation.

Our control group included 27 cases (8.5%) of pulmonary LELC, all of which were EBV-positive by EBER-ISH. This proportion substantially exceeds the <1% prevalence of LELC in unselected NSCLC cohorts from Western populations, reflecting the well-documented endemicity of EBV-associated pulmonary LELC in southern China. While our adenocarcinoma-only and LELC-exclusion sensitivity analyses confirmed that this enrichment did not drive the primary ALK and ROS1 findings, the high proportion of LELC may limit the generalizability of our control group characteristics to non-Asian or non-endemic populations. Future multicenter studies incorporating geographically diverse cohorts are needed to validate whether our triage models remain applicable in regions where LELC is rare.

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design introduces two distinct types of bias. Information bias arises from variable timing of biomarker sampling, incomplete clinical records, and lack of standardized prospective data collection; we partially mitigated this by applying uniform inclusion criteria, centralized laboratory analysis, and standardized imaging protocols. Selection bias arises from the potential for non-random patient enrollment; we mitigated this by consecutive inclusion of all NGS-tested patients from June 2015 to October 2024 and rigorous application of pre-defined exclusion criteria, but residual selection bias cannot be fully excluded. Second, treatment heterogeneity and insufficient follow-up duration precluded a comprehensive analysis of patient survival outcomes; prospective clinical trials are essential to further explore this. Third, although this study was conducted at a relatively large single center with standardized imaging protocols and centralized laboratory analysis, the low prevalence of fusion genes resulted in limited cohort sizes for ROS1 (n=23) and RET (n=22) subgroups. These predictive models therefore require prospective validation in independent multicenter cohorts. Fourth, the control group included a regionally enriched proportion of EBV-positive pulmonary LELC (27/318, 8.5%), reflecting the epidemiology of our Southern Chinese population; this may limit generalizability to non-Asian or non-endemic regions. Fifth, serological testing utilized various analytical instruments over the 9-year study period; despite internal quality control measures, inter-batch variability cannot be entirely eliminated.

Conclusions

In this exploratory retrospective cohort, several clinical, serological, and radiological features were associated with ALK and ROS1 fusion positivity in driver-negative lung adenocarcinoma. These findings are hypothesis-generating and require prospective validation in appropriately matched advanced lung adenocarcinoma cohorts before they can be used to guide testing prioritization. They should not replace guideline-recommended comprehensive molecular testing. When comprehensive NGS is delayed or unavailable, a risk-stratified approach combining younger age, elevated DD2, and specific metastatic patterns may help prioritize rapid IHC, or FISH testing for ALK and ROS1 fusions, but this is an exploratory strategy requiring external validation.
