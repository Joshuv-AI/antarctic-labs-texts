Structure of non-trivial intersecting families
Andrey Kupavskii

Abstract
. We say that a family of k k -subsets of an n n -element set is intersecting , if any two of its sets intersect. In this paper, we study the structure of large intersecting families. Several years ago, Han and Kohayakawa (Proc. AMS, 2017), and then Kostochka and Mubayi (Proc. AMS, 2017) obtained certain structural results concerning large intersecting families. In this paper, we extend and generalize their results, giving them a conclusive form.



1.
Introduction

For positive integer
n
we use the standard notation
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
and, more generally,
[a,b]:=\{a,a+1,\ldots,b\}
for integers
a,b
, with the convention
[a,b]=\emptyset
for
a>b
. For a set
X
, denote by
2^{X}
its power set and, for an integer
k\geq 0
, denote by
{X\choose k}
the collection of all
k
-element subsets (
k
-sets
) of
X
. A
family
is a collection of sets. We call a family
intersecting
if any two of its sets intersect. An intersecting family is
trivial
, or a
star
if all its sets contain a given element. Otherwise, it is called
non-trivial
. The family of all
k
-sets containing a given element is called a
full star
.

One of the oldest and most famous results in extremal combinatorics is the Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem
[
2
]
, which states that for
n\geq 2k
the largest intersecting families in
{[n]\choose k}
are full stars. Hilton and Milner
[
8
]
found the size and structure of the largest non-trivial intersecting families of
k
-sets for
n>2k
. They have size
{n-1\choose k-1}-{n-k-1\choose k-1}+1
and for
k\geq 5
have an element that intersects all but one set. That is, they have the form
\mathcal{H}_{k}
, where for integer
2\leq u\leq k

(1.1)

\mathcal{H}_{u}:=\ \Big\{A\in{[n]\choose k}\ :\ [2,u+1]\subset A\Big\}\cup\Big\{A\in{[n]\choose k}\ :\ 1\in A,[2,u+1]\cap A\neq\emptyset\Big\}.

We remark that
|\mathcal{H}_{3}|=|\mathcal{H}_{2}|
.

For any set
X
, family
\mathcal{F}\subset 2^{X}
and
i\in X
, we use the following standard notation

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\bar{i}):=

\displaystyle\ \{F\ :\ i\notin F,F\in\mathcal{F}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(i):=

\displaystyle\ \{F\setminus\{i\}\ :\ i\in F,F\in\mathcal{F}\}.

For a family
\mathcal{F}\subset 2^{[n]}
and
i\in[n]
, the
diversity

\gamma(\mathcal{F})
of
\mathcal{F}
is
\min_{i\in[n]}|\mathcal{F}(\bar{i})|
. The following theorem due to Zakharov and the author is a slight strengthening of a result due to Frankl
[
3
]
. It provides a far-reaching generalization of the Hilton–Milner theorem.

Theorem 1.1

(Kupavskii and Zakharov
[
12
]
)
.

Let
n>2k>0
and
\mathcal{F}\subset{[n]\choose k}
be an intersecting family. If
\gamma(\mathcal{F})\geq{n-u-1\choose n-k-1}
for some real
3\leq u\leq k
, then

(1.2)

|\mathcal{F}|\leq{n-1\choose k-1}+{n-u-1\choose n-k-1}-{n-u-1\choose k-1}.

This theorem implies the Hilton–Milner theorem if one substitutes
u=k
. It is sharp for integer values of
u
, as is witnessed by the example (
1.1
). On a high level, it provides us with an essentially sharp upper bound on
|\mathcal{F}|
in terms of its distance to the closest trivial family.

Numerous authors aimed to determine precisely, what are the largest intersecting families in
{[n]\choose k}
with certain restrictions. One of such questions was studied by Han and Kohayakawa
[
7
]
, who determined the largest family of intersecting families that is neither contained in the Erdős–Ko–Rado family, nor in the Hilton–Milner family. In our terms, the question can be restated as follows: what is the largest intersecting family with
\gamma(\mathcal{F})\geq 2
? For
i\in[k]
let us put
I_{i}:=[i+1,k+i]
and

\mathcal{J}_{i}:=\ \{I_{1},I_{i}\}\cup\Big\{F\in{[n]\choose k}\ :\ 1\in F,F\cap I_{1}\neq\emptyset,F\cap I_{i}\neq\emptyset\Big\}.

We note that
\mathcal{J}_{i}\subset{[n]\choose k}
and that
\mathcal{J}_{i}
is intersecting for every
i\in[k]
. Moreover,
\gamma(\mathcal{J}_{i})=2
for
i>1
and
\mathcal{J}_{1}
is the Hilton–Milner family. It is an easy calculation to see that
|\mathcal{J}_{i}|\geq|\mathcal{J}_{i+1}|
for every
k\geq 4
and
i\in[k-1]
. More precisely,
|\mathcal{J}_{1}|>|\mathcal{J}_{2}|
if
n\geq 2k+1
, and
|\mathcal{J}_{i}|>|\mathcal{J}_{i+1}|
for
n\geq 2k+i-1
and
i\geq 2
. For
n<2k+i-1
we have
|\mathcal{J}_{i}|=|\mathcal{J}_{i+1}|=\ldots=|\mathcal{J}_{k-1}|
.
1
1

1

Indeed, the difference
|\mathcal{J}_{i}|-|\mathcal{J}_{i+1}|
is
{n-k-2\choose k-2}-1
for
i=1
and
{n-k-i\choose k-1}-{n-k-i-1\choose k-1}={n-k-i-1\choose k-2}
for
i\geq 2
. The last expression is strictly positive whenever
n\geq 2k+i-1
and is
0
if
2k\leq n<2k+i-1
.

Theorem 1.2

(Han and Kohayakawa
[
7
]
)
.

Let
n>2k
,
k\geq 4
. Then any intersecting family
\mathcal{F}
with
\gamma(\mathcal{F})\geq 2
satisfies

(1.3)

|\mathcal{F}|\leq{n-1\choose k-1}-{n-k-1\choose k-1}-{n-k-2\choose k-2}+2,

moreover, for
k\geq 5
the equality is attained only on the families isomorphic to
\mathcal{J}_{2}
.

We note that Han and Kohayakawa also treated the case
k=3
, as well as described the cases of equality for
k=4
. These cases are more tedious and we omit them. Actually, a slightly weaker version of the theorem above (without uniqueness) is a consequence of the main result in the paper by Hilton and Milner
[
8
]
.

Given
l\geq 2
, denote by
\mathcal{E}_{l}
an inclusion-maximal
k
-uniform intersecting family with
|\mathcal{E}_{l}(\bar{1})|=l
,
|\bigcap_{E\in\mathcal{E}_{l}(\bar{1})}E|=k-1
(note that the family is defined up to isomorphism). Note that
\mathcal{J}_{2}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{E}_{2}
.
The following theorem is one of the main results due to Kostochka and Mubayi
[
10
]
(they have also treated the cases
k=3
and
4
, which we omit here).

Theorem 1.3

(Kostochka and Mubayi
[
10
]
)
.

Let
k\geq 5
and
n=n(k)
be sufficiently large. If
\mathcal{F}\subset{[n]\choose k}
is intersecting and
|\mathcal{F}|>|\mathcal{J}_{3}|
then
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{E}_{l}
for some
l\in\{0,\ldots,k-1,n-k\}
.

It is not difficult to see that for
k-1<l<n-k
we have
\mathcal{E}_{l}\subset\mathcal{E}_{n-k}
: we have
\mathcal{E}_{l}(1)=\mathcal{E}_{n-k}(1)
for this range. That is why these families are missing from the statement of the theorem.
The authors of
[
10
]
used the Delta-systems method of Frankl.
2
2

2

The goal of their paper, was, in a way, to draw the attention of the researchers to this method.

Many results in extremal set theory are much easier to obtain once one assumes that
n
is sufficiently large compared to
k
. (The possibility to apply the Delta-systems method being one of the reasons.) In particular, the bound on
n
in Theorem
1.3
is doubly exponential in
k
.
3
3

3

Here we should note that a more efficient delta-system type approach and a more careful analysis can improve these dependencies to polynomial.

Below, we state a result that is much more general than Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
and, additionally, holds without any restriction on
n
.

We say that a family
\mathcal{M}
is
minimal w.r.t. common intersection
if, for any
M^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}
, we have
|\bigcap_{M\in\mathcal{M}\setminus\{M^{\prime}\}}M|>|\bigcap_{M\in\mathcal{M}}M|
.
The following theorem is the main result of this paper.

Theorem 1.4
.

Assume that
n>2k\geq 8
. Consider an intersecting family
\mathcal{F}\subset{[n]\choose k}
with
\gamma(\mathcal{F})=|\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})|
.
Take a subfamily
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})
, which is minimal w.r.t. common intersection and such that
|\bigcap_{M\in\mathcal{M}}M|=t
. Take the (unique) inclusion-maximal intersecting family
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\subset{[n]\choose k}
, such that
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(\bar{1})=\mathcal{M}
. If
t\geq 3

then we have

(1.4)

|\mathcal{F}|\leq|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}|,

and, for
k\geq 5
, equality is possible if and only if
\mathcal{F}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
. If
k=4
and
t=3
then the equality is possible if and only if
\mathcal{F}
is isomorphic to one of
\mathcal{E}_{n-k},\mathcal{J}_{2}
.

Moreover, if
\mathcal{F}
is as above and
|\bigcap_{F\in\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})}F|\leq t
for some
t\geq 3
, then

(1.5)

|\mathcal{F}|\leq|\mathcal{J}_{k-t+1}|,

and, for
k\geq 5
and
n\geq 2k+(k-t+1)-1=3k-t
, equality is possible only if
\mathcal{F}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{J}_{k-t+1}
. If
k\geq 5
and
2k+1\leq n<3k-t
, then equality holds only if
\mathcal{F}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{J}_{i}
with
i\geq k-t+1
.

This theorem generalizes Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
and gives a reasonable classification of
all
large intersecting families.
We also note that we cannot in general replace the condition
t\geq 3

by
t\geq 2
.
Indeed, one can see that the family
\mathcal{H}_{2}
(cf. (
1.1
)) is larger than
\mathcal{J}_{k-1}
for large
n
.
4
4

4

The size of
\mathcal{J}_{k-1}
can be upper bounded by
{n-2\choose k-2}+{n-3\choose k-2}+2+(k-2)\big({n-4\choose k-2}-{n-k-2\choose k-2}\big)<{n-2\choose k-2}+2{n-3\choose k-2}
for, say,
n>2k^{3}
.

Corollary 1.5
.

Let
n>2k\geq 10
and
\mathcal{F}\subset{[n]\choose k}
be an intersecting family.

(1)

If
|\mathcal{F}|>|\mathcal{J}_{3}|
then
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{E}_{l}
for some
l\in\{0,\ldots,k-1,n-k\}
.

(2)

Assume that
|\mathcal{F}|=|\mathcal{J}_{3}|
and
\mathcal{F}
is not a subfamily of any
\mathcal{E}_{\ell}
. Then, if
n\geq 2k+2
, the family
\mathcal{F}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{J}_{3}
. If
n=2k+1
then
\mathcal{F}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{J}_{i}
for some
i\geq 3
.

Proof of Corollary
1.5
.

1. Fix any
\mathcal{F}
with
|\mathcal{F}|>|\mathcal{J}_{3}|
and assume that
\gamma(\mathcal{F})=|\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})|
. First assume that
|\bigcap_{F\in\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})}F|\leq k-2
. We are in position to apply the second part of Theorem
1.4
to
\mathcal{F}
, and get a contradiction with
|\mathcal{F}|>|\mathcal{J}_{3}|
.
Therefore,
|\bigcap_{F\in\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})}F|=k-1
and thus
\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{E}_{l}(\bar{1})
for
l=|\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})|
.
2. The condition that
\mathcal{F}
is not a subfamily of any
\mathcal{E}_{\ell}
guarantees that
|\bigcap_{F\in\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})}F|\leq k-2
. Since
|\mathcal{F}|=|\mathcal{J}_{3}|
and
k\geq 5
, we can apply the uniqueness part of the second part of Theorem
1.4
.
∎

The deduction of Theorem
1.2
from Theorem

with

Proof of Theorem
1.4

2.1.
Preliminaries

Recall the definition of shifting. For a given pair of indices
1\leq i<j\leq n
and a set
A\subset[n]
, define the
(i,j)
-shift

S_{ij}(A)
as follows. If
i\in A
or
j\notin A
, then
S_{ij}(A)=A
. If
j\in A,i\notin A
, then
S_{ij}(A):=(A-\{j\})\cup\{i\}
.
The
(i,j)
-shift
S_{ij}(\mathcal{A})
of a family
\mathcal{A}
is defined as follows:

S_{ij}(\mathcal{A}):=\{S_{ij}(A)\ :\ A\in\mathcal{A}\}\cup\{A\ :\ A,S_{ij}(A)\in\mathcal{A}\}.

Shifting preserves sizes of sets and families. Moreover, it preserves the property of a family to be intersecting. We refer tje reader to a survey of Frankl
[
4
]
. When applied directly, it does not, however, preserve different non-triviality related properties. One of the challenges that we overcome in this paper is to carefully apply shifting so that the non-trivial structure is preserved.

We will need a two-family generalization of Theorem
1.1
. We say that the families
{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}
are
cross-intersecting
if any set from
{\mathcal{A}}
intersects any set from
{\mathcal{B}}
. The following result, modulo uniqueness, appeared in
[
12
]
.

Corollary 2.1

(
[
12
]
, Theorem 1)
.

Let
a,b>0
,
n>a+b
. Let
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{[n]\choose a},\ {\mathcal{B}}\subset{[n]\choose b}
be a pair of cross-intersecting families.

If
b<a
, or
b\geq a
and
|{\mathcal{B}}|\leq{n-b-1+a\choose a-1}
, then

(2.1)

|{\mathcal{A}}|+|{\mathcal{B}}|\leq{n\choose a}.

Moreover, the displayed inequality is strict unless
|{\mathcal{B}}|=0
.

If
b<a
and
|{\mathcal{B}}|\geq{n-j\choose b-j}
for integer
j\in[b]
, then

(2.2)

|{\mathcal{A}}|+|{\mathcal{B}}|\leq{n\choose a}+{n-j\choose b-j}-{n-j\choose a}.

Moreover, if the inequality on
{\mathcal{B}}
is strict, then the inequality in the displayed formula above is also strict, unless
b=a-1
,
j=1
, and
|{\mathcal{B}}|={n\choose b}
.

Note that the roles of
a,b
are interchanged, as compared to
[
12
, Theorem 1]
. Also, in Corollary
2.1
we write stronger upper bounds on
|{\mathcal{B}}|
for simplicity. The result in
[
12
]
did not explicitly treat the equality case. The strictness part of (
2.1
) for
b<a
follows by comparing the bound with the bound in (
2.2
) for the case
j=b
(then, the condition on
|{\mathcal{B}}|
is
|{\mathcal{B}}|\geq 1
). Strictness in (
2.1
) for
b\geq a
follows by comparing the same bounds (
j=b+1
and
j=b
) from
[
12
, Theorem 1, part 2]
. Finally, the equality case in (
2.2
) follows from
[
11
, Theorem 2.12 part 3]
.

The following lemma is crucial for the proof of the second part of the main theorem. It allows us to compare the sizes of different
\mathcal{F}
with
\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})
being different minimal families. Given integers
m>2s
, let
\mathcal{T}_{2}^{s}:=\{[s],[s+1,2s]\}
. Let
\mathcal{F}_{2}^{s}\subset{[m]\choose k-1}
stand for the largest family that is cross-intersecting with
\mathcal{T}_{2}^{s}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
k,s
and
m\geq k+s
be integers,
k\geq 4
. Given a family
\mathcal{H}\subset{[m]\choose s}
with
\tau(\mathcal{H})=2
and minimal w.r.t. this property, consider the maximal family
\mathcal{F}\subset{[m]\choose k-1}
that is cross-intersecting with
\mathcal{H}
. Then the unique maximum of
|\mathcal{F}|+|\mathcal{H}|
is attained when
\mathcal{H}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{T}_{2}^{s}
and
\mathcal{F}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{F}_{2}^{s}
.

Proof.

Let us first express
|\mathcal{F}_{2}^{s}|
. It is not difficult to see that

\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}_{2}^{s}|\ =\

\displaystyle{m-1\choose k-2}-{m-s-1\choose k-2}+

\displaystyle{m-2\choose k-2}-{m-s-2\choose k-2}+

\displaystyle\cdots

(2.3)

\displaystyle{m-s\choose k-2}-{m-2s\choose k-2}.

Indeed, in the first line we count the sets containing
1
that intersect
[s+1,2s]
, in the second line we count the sets not containing
1
, containing
2
and intersecting
[s+1,2s]
etc.

Rather surprisingly, we can bound the size of
\mathcal{F}
for any
\mathcal{H}
in a similar way. Suppose that
z:=|\mathcal{H}|
and
\mathcal{H}=\{H_{1},\ldots,H_{z}\}
. Since
\mathcal{H}
is minimal, for each
l\in[z]
there exists an element
i_{l}
such that
i_{l}\notin H_{l}
and
i_{l}\in\bigcap_{j\in[z]\setminus\{l\}}H_{j}
. (All
i_{l}
are of course different.) Applying Bollobas’ set-pairs inequality
[
1
]
to
\mathcal{H}
and
\{i_{l}\ :\ l\in[z]\}
, we get that
|\mathcal{H}|\leq{s+1\choose s}=s+1
.

For each
l=2,\ldots,z
, we count the sets
F\in\mathcal{F}
such that
F\cap\{i_{2},\ldots,i_{l}\}=\{i_{l}\}
. (Cf. the displayed inequality below.) Such sets must additionally intersect
H_{l}\setminus\{i_{2},\ldots,i_{l-1}\}
. Note that
H_{1}\supset\{i_{2},\ldots,i_{z}\}
. This covers all sets from
\mathcal{F}
that intersect
\{i_{2},\ldots,i_{z}\}
and gives the first
z-1
lines in the displayed inequality below. Next, we have to deal with sets from
\mathcal{F}
that do not intersect
\{i_{2},\ldots,i_{z}\}
. Firstly, they must intersect
H_{1}
.
Assuming that
H_{1}\setminus\{i_{2},\ldots,i_{z}\}=\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{s+1-z}\}
, for each
l=1,\ldots,s+1-z
we further count the sets
F\in\mathcal{F}
such that
F\cap\{i_{2},\ldots,i_{z},j_{1},\ldots,j_{l}\}=\{j_{l}\}
. Since the intersection of
\mathcal{H}
is empty, there must exist
i\geq 2
such that
j_{l}\notin H_{i}
. Thus, these sets must additionally intersect
H_{i}\setminus\{i_{2},\ldots,i_{z}\}
. Note that the set
H_{i}\setminus\{i_{2},\ldots,i_{z}\}
has size
s-z+2
. This count gives the last
s+1-z
lines in the displayed inequality below, in which we upper bound
|H_{i}\setminus\{i_{2},\ldots,i_{z}\}|
by
s-z+2
.
Since
F\cap H_{1}\neq\emptyset
for any
F\in\mathcal{F}
and given that the classes for different
l
are disjoint, we clearly counted each set from
\mathcal{F}
exactly once. (However, we may also have counted some sets that are not in
\mathcal{F}
.) Doing this count, we get the following bound on
\mathcal{F}
.

\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}|\ \leq\

\displaystyle{m-1\choose k-2}-{m-s-1\choose k-2}+

\displaystyle{m-2\choose k-2}-{m-s-1\choose k-2}+

\displaystyle\cdots

\displaystyle{m-z+1\choose k-2}-{m-s-1\choose k-2}+

\displaystyle{m-z\choose k-2}-{m-s-2\choose k-2}+

\displaystyle\cdots

(2.4)

\displaystyle{m-s\choose k-2}-{m-2s-2+z\choose k-2}\ =:\ f(z).

Note that (
2.4
) coincides with (
2.3
) when substituting
z=2
.
We have
f(z-1)-f(z)\geq{m-s-1\choose k-2}-{m-s-2\choose k-2}={m-s-2\choose k-3}>1
(here we use that
m\geq s+k
and
k\geq 4
). Therefore, for any
z\geq z^{\prime}
,

|\mathcal{H}|+|\mathcal{F}|\leq f(z^{\prime})+z^{\prime},

and the inequality is strict unless
z=|\mathcal{H}|=z^{\prime}
.

At the same time, we have
|\mathcal{F}_{2}^{s}|+|\mathcal{T}_{2}^{s}|=f(2)+2
. Since, up to isomorphism, there is only one family
\mathcal{H}\subset{[m]\choose s}
of size
2
with
\tau(\mathcal{H})=2
, we immediately conclude that the lemma holds.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.4

Let us deal with the first part of the statement first. Choose
\mathcal{F}
,
t
,
\mathcal{M}
, and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
satisfying the requirements of the theorem, and assume that
|\mathcal{F}|\geq|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}|
, and
\mathcal{F}

\mathcal{M}

|\mathcal{F}|=|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}|\leq|\mathcal{H}_{3}|=|\mathcal{H}_{2}|
then we can w.l.o.g. replace
\mathcal{F}
by
\mathcal{H}_{3}
(cf. (
1.1
)). Indeed,
|\cap_{H\in\mathcal{H}_{3}(\bar{1})}H|=3
and
\mathcal{H}_{3}(\bar{1})
contains a copy of any minimal family
\mathcal{M}
with
|\cap_{M\in\mathcal{M}}M|\geq 3
as in the statement of the theorem. If
|\mathcal{F}|\geq|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}|\geq|\mathcal{H}_{3}|
then, by Theorem
1.1
,
\gamma(\mathcal{F})\leq{n-4\choose k-3}
. Thus, in what follows, we may suppose that
\gamma(\mathcal{F})=|\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})|\leq{n-4\choose k-3}
. We also suppose that
\bigcap_{M\in\mathcal{M}}M=[2,t+1]
.

We need to show that
|\mathcal{F}|\leq|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}|
and equality for
k\geq 5
is only possible if
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
. To do so, we shall gradually transform
\mathcal{F}
into
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
. We will preserve all key properties and, moreover, after each exchange operation, the size of
\mathcal{F}
shall strictly increase if
\mathcal{F}
changes. In what follows, arguing indirectly, we assume that
\gamma(\mathcal{F})>\gamma(\mathcal{F}^{\prime})
.

First, for all
i=2,\ldots,t+1
and
j>i
, we consecutively apply all the
S_{ij}
-shifts to
\mathcal{F}
. Note that the family
\mathcal{M}
stays intact under these shifts, and the diversity and size of
\mathcal{F}
is not affected. Thus, we may assume that
\mathcal{F}
is invariant under these shifts. For any
j\geq 2
and
S\subset[2,j]
, define

\mathcal{F}(S,[j]):=\big\{F\subset[j+1,n]\ :\ F\cup S\in\mathcal{F}\big\}

and, for any family
\mathcal{R}\subset{[n]\choose k}
, let
\partial\mathcal{R}:=\bigcup_{R\in\mathcal{R}}{R\choose k-1}
denote the
shadow
of
\mathcal{R}
.

An important consequence of the shifts we made is that, for any
i\in[2,t+1]
and
S\subset[2,i-1]
, we have

(2.5)

\big|\mathcal{F}(S\cup\{i\},[i])\big|\geq\big|\partial\mathcal{F}(S,[i])\big|.

Actually, we have
\mathcal{F}(S\cup\{i\},[i])\supset\partial\mathcal{F}(S,[i])
. Indeed, for any
F^{\prime}\in\partial\mathcal{F}(S,[i])
, there exist
j\in[i+1,n]
and
F\in\mathcal{F}(S,[i])
, such that
F^{\prime}\cup\{j\}=F
, and, since we performed the
(i,j)
–shift, we have
F^{\prime}\cup\{i\}\cup S\in\mathcal{F}
. That is,
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}(S\cup\{i\},[i])
.

Recall that we have
|\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})|\leq{n-4\choose k-3}
.

Proposition 2.3
.

For any
i\in[2,4]
and with the convention
[2,1]=\emptyset
, we have

(2.6)

|\mathcal{F}([2,i-1],[i])|\leq{n-5\choose k-3}.

Proof.

Assume that (
2.6
) does not hold. Let us show that

|\partial\mathcal{F}([2,i-1],[i])|>{n-5\choose k-4}.

Recall that the Kruskal–Katona theorem in Lovasz’ form
[
13
]
states that if
\mathcal{K}
is a family of
m
-element sets and, for some real
x\geq m
, we have
|\mathcal{K}|\geq{x\choose m}
, then
|\partial\mathcal{K}|\geq{x\choose m-1}
. In our case,
\mathcal{K}:=\mathcal{F}([2,i-1],[i])\subset{[i+1,n]\choose k-i+2}
and, by assumption,
|\mathcal{K}|>{n-5\choose k-3}
. Select
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{K}
such that
|\mathcal{K}^{\prime}|={n-5\choose k-3}
. Define
x
by the following equality:
{x\choose k-i+2}={n-5\choose k-3}
. Let us upper bound the value of
x
. We claim that
x\leq n-4
for any
i=2,3,4
. First,
{n-4\choose k-i+2}\geq{n-4\choose k-3}>{n-5\choose k-3}
for any
i=2,3,4
, where the first inequality holds since
n\geq 2k+1
. Second,
{y\choose k-i+2}
is an increasing function of
y
, and thus the value of
x
defined above is at most
n-4
.

By the Kruskal–Katona theorem in Lovasz’ form, we have
|\partial\mathcal{F}([2,i-1],[i])|\geq|\partial\mathcal{K}^{\prime}|\geq{x\choose k-i+1}
.
We have

\displaystyle{x\choose k-i+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{k-i+2}{x+1-(k-i+2)}{x\choose k-i+2}=\frac{k-i+2}{x+1-(k-i+2)}{n-5\choose k-3}

\displaystyle\overset{(\text{since }i\leq 4)}{\geq}\frac{k-2}{x+1-(k-2)}{n-5\choose k-3}\overset{(\text{since }x\leq n-4)}{\geq}\frac{k-2}{n-3-(k-2)}{n-5\choose k-3}

\displaystyle>\frac{k-3}{n-k-1}{n-5\choose k-3}={n-5\choose k-4}.

From the computation above, we have
|\partial\mathcal{F}([2,i-1],[i])|>{n-5\choose k-4}
, and thus

\big|\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})\big|\geq\big|\mathcal{F}([2,i-1],[i])\big|+\big|\mathcal{F}([2,i],[i])\big|\overset{\eqref{eqcard}}{\geq}\big|\mathcal{F}([2,i-1],[i])\big|+\big|\partial\mathcal{F}([2,i-1],[i])\big|>{n-4\choose k-3},

a contradiction.
∎

For each
i=2,\ldots,k
, consider the following bipartite graph
G_{i}
. The parts of
G_{i}
are

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{a}^{i}:=\

\displaystyle\Big\{P\ :\ P\in{[2,n]\choose k-1},\ P\cap[2,i]=\{i\}\Big\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{b}^{i}:=\

\displaystyle\Big\{P\ :\ P\in{[2,n]\choose k},\ P\cap[2,i]=[2,i-1]\Big\},

and edges connect disjoint sets. We identify
\mathcal{P}_{a}^{i}
with
{[i+1,n]\choose k-2}
and
\mathcal{P}_{b}^{i}
with
{[i+1,n]\choose k-i+2}
.

For each
i=2,\ldots,t+1
we will apply (
2.1
) to

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}_{i}:=\mathcal{F}(1)\cap\mathcal{P}_{a}^{i}\ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ \ {\mathcal{B}}_{i}:=\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})\cap\mathcal{P}_{b}^{i},

and conclude that
|{\mathcal{A}}_{i}|+|{\mathcal{B}}_{i}|\leq{n-i\choose k-2}
, and that the inequality is strict unless
{\mathcal{B}}_{i}=\emptyset
. Put
X=[i+1,n]
. In order to apply (
2.1
), we need, first,
n-i=|X|>k-2+k-i+2=2k-i
(this holds since
n>2k
). Most importantly, we need
|{\mathcal{B}}_{i}|\leq{|X|-(k-i+2)-1+(k-2)\choose k-3}={n-5\choose k-3}
for
i\in[2,4]
. This holds because of (
2.6
). For
i\geq 5
the uniformity of
{\mathcal{B}}_{i}
(thought of as a subset in
{[i+1,n]\choose k-i+2}
is smaller than the uniformity of
{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
. Thus, we may replace
{\mathcal{A}}_{i},\ {\mathcal{B}}_{i}
with
{\mathcal{B}}^{\prime}_{i}:=\emptyset
and
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}_{i}:={X\choose k-2}
. The resulting family
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i}
is cross-intersecting since all sets in
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i}(\bar{1})
contain
[2,i]
and sets in
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i}(1)\setminus\mathcal{F}(1)
intersect
[2,i]
. Moreover, if
|{\mathcal{B}}_{i}|\neq 0
then
|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i}|>|\mathcal{F}|
. Thus, by maximality of
|\mathcal{F}|
we may assume that all sets in
\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})
must contain
[2,i]
. Applying this argument for each
i=2,\ldots,t+1
, we conclude that all sets in
\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})
must contain
[2,i]
.

Put
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{1},\ldots,M_{z}\}
. Since
\mathcal{M}
is minimal, for each
M_{l}\in\mathcal{M}
,
l\in[z]
, there is

(2.7)

j_{l}\in\Big(\bigcap_{M\in\mathcal{M}\setminus\{M_{l}\}}M\Big)\setminus\Big(\bigcap_{M\in\mathcal{M}}M\Big).

We assume that
j_{l}=t+l+1
,
l\in[z]
. In particular,
\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{z}\}=[t+2,t+z+1]
.

Next, for
i=t+2,\ldots,t+z+1
we shall show by induction that all sets in
\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})\setminus\{M_{i-t-1}\}
contain
[2,i]
. This is true for
i=t+1
. Fix some
i
and consider the bipartite graph
G_{i}
, defined above. We can apply (
2.2
) with
n-i,k-2,k-i+2,k-i+2
playing roles of
n,a,b,j
, respectively. First, note that
b<a
. Second, we know that
|{\mathcal{B}}_{i}|\geq{n-i\choose(k-i+2)-(k-i+2)}=1
since
M_{i-t-1}\in\mathcal{P}_{b}^{i}
(note that
M_{l}\notin\mathcal{P}_{b}^{i}
for
l\neq i-t-1
, since all of them contain
i_{l}
due to the definition of
i_{l}
). Therefore,
|\mathcal{A}_{i}|+|\mathcal{B}_{i}|\leq{n-i\choose k-2}-{n-k-i\choose k-2}+1
. For
k\geq 5
, the inequality is strict unless
{\mathcal{B}}_{i}=\{M_{i-t-1}\}
. Replace
{\mathcal{A}}_{i},\ {\mathcal{B}}_{i}
with
{\mathcal{B}}^{\prime}_{i}:=\{M_{i-t-1}\}
and
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}_{i}:=\{A\in{[i+1,n]\choose k-2}\ :\ A\cap M_{i-t-1}\neq\emptyset\}
. Let us denote the resulting family
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i}
.

Let us briefly discuss the case
k=4
. If
k=4
then each set in
\mathcal{F}([2,4],[4])
is a singleton, so
|\mathcal{M}|=2
and we apply the argument above for
i=5
only. Thus
n-5,2,1,1
play the roles of
n,a,b,j
, respectively, and so the inequality is strict unless
|{\mathcal{B}}_{i}|={n-5\choose 1}
, i.e.,
{\mathcal{B}}_{i}
contains all possible
1
-element sets. This means that
\mathcal{F}
is (a subfamily of) the exceptional family
\mathcal{E}_{n-k}
for
k=4
. If
|{\mathcal{B}}_{i}|<{n-5\choose 1},
then after the replacement we will have
|\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})|=2
(i.e.,
\mathcal{F}
will turn out to be isomorphic to a subfamily of
\mathcal{J}_{2}
).

Let us return to the analysis. The family
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i}
is cross-intersecting. Indeed, any set in
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i}(1)\setminus\mathcal{F}(1)
contains
i
and intersects
M_{i-t-1}
. At the same time, all sets in
\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})\setminus\mathcal{M}
contain
[2,i-1]
by induction, and those of
\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})\setminus\mathcal{M}
that do not contain
i
belong to
\mathcal{P}_{b}^{i}
. But they were removed after the replacement. Therefore, the only set not containing
i
in
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i}(\bar{1})
is
M_{i-t-1}
, which shows the cross-intersecting property. Since
|\mathcal{F}|
is maximal, we must have
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i}
. Repeating this argument for
i=t+2,t+3,\ldots,t+z+1
, we may assume that any set in
\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})\setminus\mathcal{M}
must contain the set
[2,t+z+1]
.

Put
t^{\prime}:=t+z+1
. If
\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})\setminus\mathcal{M}
is non-empty then, for each
l\in[z]
, the set
M_{l}\setminus[2,t^{\prime}]
must be non-empty: otherwise,
t^{\prime}-1>k
. If
\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})\setminus\mathcal{M}
is empty then
\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})=\mathcal{M}
, which contradicts
\gamma(\mathcal{F})>\gamma(\mathcal{F}^{\prime})
. For each
l\in[z]
, select
one element
i_{l}\in M_{l}\cap[t^{\prime}+1,n]
. Note that
i_{l}
may coincide. Put
I:=\{i_{l}\ :\ l\in[z]\}
. Consider the bipartite graph
G(t^{\prime},I)
with parts

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{a}(t^{\prime},I):=\

\displaystyle\Big\{P\ :\ P\in{[2,n]\choose k-1},\ I\subset P,\ [2,t^{\prime}]\cap P=\emptyset\Big\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{b}(t^{\prime},I):=\

\displaystyle\Big\{P\ :\ P\in{[2,n]\choose k},\ [2,t^{\prime}]\subset P,\ I\cap P=\emptyset\Big\},

and edges connecting disjoint sets. Put
Y=[t^{\prime}+1,n]\setminus I
,
|Y|=n-t^{\prime}-|I|
. We identify
\mathcal{P}_{a}(t^{\prime},I)
with
{Y\choose k-|I|-1}
,
|I|+1\leq z+1
, and
\mathcal{P}_{b}(t^{\prime},I)
with
{Y\choose k-t^{\prime}+1}
, where
t^{\prime}-1\geq t+z-1\geq z+3
. We note
|Y|=n-t^{\prime}-|I|>k-|I|-1+k-t^{\prime}+1
. By the choice of
I
, we have
|\mathcal{M}\cap\mathcal{P}_{b}(t^{\prime},I)|=\emptyset
. Let

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}:=\mathcal{F}(1)\cap\mathcal{P}_{a}(t^{\prime},I)\ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ \ {\mathcal{B}}:=\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})\cap\mathcal{P}_{b}(t^{\prime},I).

We have
k-|I|-1>k-t^{\prime}+1
, and, therefore, we may apply (
2.1
) with
a:=k-|I|-1,\ b:=k-t^{\prime}+1
. We have
b<a
in this case. We conclude that
|{\mathcal{A}}|+|{\mathcal{B}}|\leq{|Y|\choose k-|I|-1}
, and that the inequality is strict unless
{\mathcal{B}}=\emptyset
.
As before, replacing
{\mathcal{A}}
with
\mathcal{P}_{a}(t^{\prime},I)
and
{\mathcal{B}}
with
\emptyset
does not decrease the sum of sizes of the families and preserves the cross-intersecting property. Thus, by the choice of
\mathcal{F}
, we must have
{\mathcal{B}}=\emptyset
.

Repeating this for all possible choices of
I
, we arrive at the situation when any set from
\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})\setminus\mathcal{M}
must intersect
any
such set
I
. Clearly, this is only possible for a set
F
if
F\supset M_{l}\cap[t^{\prime}+1,n]
for some

. But then
|F|>|M_{l}|
, which is impossible. Thus
\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})=\mathcal{M}
, which contradicts our assumption
\gamma(\mathcal{F})>\gamma(\mathcal{F}^{\prime})
, so the proof of (
1.4
) with uniqueness is complete. Indeed, uniqueness follows from the fact that the inequalities (
2.1
), (
2.2
) are strict unless the family
{\mathcal{B}}
has sizes
0
and
1
, respectively, or
k=4
(we mentioned it at every application, and treated the case
k=4
separately above).

Let us now prove the moreover part of the statement of Theorem
1.4
. First, if there is no family
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})
, minimal w.r.t. common intersection and such that
|\bigcap_{M\in\mathcal{M}}M|=t
, then we apply
(i,j)
-shifts to
\mathcal{F}
for
2\leq i<j\leq n
until such family appears. Since common intersection of any subfamily of
\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})
may change by at most one after any shift, either we obtain the desired
\mathcal{M}
or we arrive at a shifted family
\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})
without such
\mathcal{M}
. But the latter is impossible. Indeed, for a shifted intersecting family
\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})
, we have
|\bigcap_{F\in\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})}F|=[2,j]
for some
j\geq 2
, and in that case
[2,j]\cup[j+2,k+2]\in\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})
. But then the set
F_{j^{\prime}}:=[2,j^{\prime}]\cup[j^{\prime}+2,k+2]\in\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})
, where
j\leq j^{\prime}\leq k+1
. It is clear that the sets
F_{j},\ldots,F_{k-t+j+1}
form a (minimal) subfamily that has common intersection of size
t
.

Fix
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})
, minimal w.r.t. common intersection, such that
|\bigcap_{M\in\mathcal{M}}M|=t
. Applying the first part of Theorem
1.4
, we may assume that
\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})=\mathcal{M}
. Clearly, the number of sets in
\mathcal{F}(1)
passing through
\bigcap_{M\in\mathcal{M}}M
is always the same, independently of the form of
\mathcal{M}
. Thus, we need to analyze the sum of sizes of the family
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}:=\big\{F\in\mathcal{F}(1)\ :\ F\cap\bigcap_{M\in\mathcal{M}}M=\emptyset\big\}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}:=\big\{M\setminus\bigcap_{M\in\mathcal{M}}M\ :\ M\in\mathcal{M}\big\}.
Note that
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
are cross-intersecting, moreover,
\tau(\mathcal{M}^{\prime})=2
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
is minimal w.r.t. this property.

Now we can apply Lemma
2.2
with
[2,n]\setminus\bigcap_{M\in\mathcal{M}}M
playing the role of

and

playing the role of

. Note that

, and so the condition on

from the lemma is satisfied. This proves (
1.5
), moreover, we get that for

the inequality was strict unless
|\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})|=2

|\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})|=2

\mathcal{F}
is isomorphic to a subfamily of

, and the equality is possible only if
\mathcal{F}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{J}_{i}
. Finally, we have seen in the introduction that if

then
|\mathcal{J}_{k-t+1}|>|\mathcal{J}_{i}|
for

.
This concludes the proof of Theorem

.
