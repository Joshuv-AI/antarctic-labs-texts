Medication Discrepancy‐Related Medication Safety Incidents in Surgical Patients: A Register Study Based on Medication Safety Incident Reports
Authors: Saralotta Vähälä, Miia Tiihonen, Esa Jämsen, Ville Valkonen, Terhi Toivo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsSurgical patients are at high risk of experiencing medication errors, particularly those related to the lack of accurate medication history. This study aimed to determine the number and the nature of reported medication safety incidents in surgical patients in a Finnish University Hospital.MethodsIn this retrospective study, the medication safety incidents (n = 902) reported in 7 surgical units of a Finnish University Hospital during 2021–2022 were quantitatively analyzed. Incident reports concerning medication discrepancy errors (i.e.a mismatch between the documented medication list and the intended medication regimen) were further assessed with an inductive and data‐driven analysis based on their free text sections.ResultsThere was a total of 902 incidents. Of these, 70.6% were classified as patient involved incidents and 29.4% near misses. The most common incidents were administration errors (45.8%, n = 413) and prescribing errors (20.7%, n = 187). The most reported medicines were antithrombotics (24.1%, n = 140) and opioids (14.3%, n = 83). Approximately (39% n = 350) were related to errors in up‐to‐date medication information, such as in the documentation of the patient's electronic medication list (46.0%, n = 161) and the lack of medication reconciliation (30.3%, n = 106). Most of these discrepancy incidents caused no clinical consequences to the patient (69.9%), while some resulted in treatment delays (7.7%), psychological/cognitive symptoms (6.4%) and cardiorespiratory symptoms (5.1%). The majority of the incidents did not affect the duration of hospitalization (83.3%) but some required extra work or monitoring (9.8%).ConclusionMedication discrepancy‐related incidents accounted for approximately 39% of the medication safety incidents reported from the surgical units. Improving adherence to electronic medication list protocols, communication among care personnel, and clarity of professional responsibilities may support medication safety in surgical care.

Introduction

Surgical patients are the one of the most vulnerable group of patients to experience adverse drug events (ADEs): World Health Organization (WHO) has estimated that 24% of all medication‐related patient harm occurs in surgical settings [1]. A lack of accurate medication history leading to errors in the medication charts is a significant factor compromising medication safety in surgical patients [2, 3, 4, 5], and it has been estimated that 55%–59% of surgical patients experience some form of medication error (ME) due to inaccurate medication history during their hospital stay [3, 5].

Medication discrepancy refers to situations where the medication list used in clinical decision‐making is outdated or inaccurate [6]. Actions taken to ensure that a patient's medication list is up‐to‐date include medication reconciliation from two different sources at, reviewing the medication list when new medicines are prescribed, and updating the medication list during the hospitalization and at the transitions of care. ADEs caused by inaccurate medication history can lead to serious drug interactions, drug omissions, surgical complications, and deterioration of the patient's underlying condition [3, 4]. In elective surgery, the time between the surgical evaluation and the day of surgery can create an additional risk such that the medication list has become outdated prior to surgery [2]. In addition to causing patient harm, morbidity and mortality, MEs represent a significant burden and cost to the health care system as a result of prolonged and more complex hospital stays [1].

Although patient safety in surgery has received increasing attention in recent years through global initiatives, such as WHO's Safe Surgery Program [7], the incidence of surgical adverse events (AE) remains high [8, 9]. As surgical patients have been identified as a significant patient population to experience ADEs due to a lack of accurate medication history [10], research is needed to improve patient safety in surgical patients. Reporting of medication safety incidents during the perioperative period is considered an important strategy for identifying risk and reducing patient harm [11]. The aim of this study was to determine the number and type of medication safety incidents in surgical patients reported to a web‐based reporting system, with particular focus on incidents related to medication discrepancy errors.

Methods

Study Design and Data Collection

This study was conducted in the second largest University Hospital in Finland, which provides tertiary medical care to nearly one million Finns and performs more than 29,000 surgical procedures every year. As a way of ensuring medication safety, the hospital has defined and harmonized processes as well as guidelines related to patient's accurate medication history. According to the guidelines, a patient's medications and risk factors/information (e.g., allergies, medical conditions), must be checked and documented in the electronic medication list by the attending physician, nurse or pharmacist upon arrival to the unit. New medications (e.g., antibiotics, antithrombotics) initiated during the perioperative period should be documented in the electronic medication list by the treating anaesthesiologist and/or surgeon. When the patient is discharged, the attending physician should review the patient's electronic medication list and discontinue medications that are no longer necessary. The patient should then receive a printout of an updated home medication list and the prescriptions needed for outpatient care.

In Finland, patient safety incident reporting is widely used in social and health care units, with HaiPro being the most commonly used reporting system [12]. HaiPro is a voluntary, anonymous, structured, and web‐based reporting system that has been widely used for almost 20 years to report and investigate patient safety incidents [13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. In this retrospective study, the medication safety incidents reported between January 1, 2021 and December 31, 2022 in the hospital's HaiPro system were collected from seven units, i.e. wards for soft tissue surgery, musculoskeletal surgery, neurosurgery, otorhinolaryngology surgery, ophthalmic surgery, gynecological surgery, urological surgery, gastrointestinal surgery, and vascular surgery.

Eligibility Criteria

The dataset included incident reports documented in the selected units during the study period. As information of the duration of the hospitalization was not available in the reports; therefore, incidents from the different types of surgical care episodes were included. Pediatric surgical units were excluded in the study.

Data Analysis

This study used descriptive design, including both quantitative analysis and a theory‐guided qualitative content analysis.

Quantitative Analysis of All Medication Safety Incident Reports

In the first part of the study, a quantitative analysis of all medication safety incident reports was performed to examine the structured numerical information readily available from the reports, such as the number of reported different medication safety incidents, their location, the nature of the incident, harm to the patient and problems for the unit, reporter's profession, possible contributing factors, and the 10 most commonly reported medicines, based on their Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical class (ATC class) [18].

Descriptive statistics, including direct distributions and percentages of the variables, were analyzed and calculated in Microsoft Excel (version 2403 Build 16.0.17425.20124, 64‐bit, Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, Washington) using basic statistical functions.

Qualitative Content Analysis of Incidents Related to Medication Discrepancies

In the second part of the study, an inductive and data‐driven content analysis method was applied to further characterize the information recorded in the free text sections of the safety incident reports (Figure 1). The data analysis and interpretation framework are based on the principles of the Human Error Theory [19]. The reports were first screened by the researcher (SLV) to identify reports related to medication discrepancy errors. In this study, medication discrepancies were defined as content‐level differences between medication lists, whereas medication reconciliation errors were defined as process‐level failures to perform or complete the reconciliation step [20]. Medication discrepancy errors were defined a difference between the medication information documented in the medication list and the medication regimen that should have been in place at the time of care, with the potential to contribute to a medication error or inappropriate medication administration. In accordance with Finnish guidelines, intentional discrepancies (i.e. planned and clinically justified modifications to a patient's medication regimen that have been appropriately documented) are not reported to the HaiPro system and are therefore not included in the data. For this reason, the term "medication discrepancy" refers exclusively to unintentional discrepancies in this study. Because the HaiPro data did not include a separate structured field for intentionality, this classification was based on the incident type and free‐text descriptions reviewed by the research team. The included reports were categorized into three phases of hospitalization: admission, those occurring during hospital‐stay, and finally at discharge. The consequences of the medication safety incidents were categorized as clinical consequences for the patient and consequences related to the hospitalization. These categories were formed through the data‐driven analysis derived during the course of the study and were not predetermined. The analysis was conducted by a multidisciplinary research team (SLV, TT, MT, VV) with expertise in content analysis, medication safety, risk management, and qualitative research. The study design and reporting followed RECORD Statement (REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely‐collected health Data) for transparent reporting of the use of routinely‐collected health care data [21]. A completed RECORD checklist is provided as Supporting Information.

Summarized process flowchart of the quantitative and qualitative analysis of the medication safety incident reports in the surgical units of University Hospital between 2021 and 2022.

The free text sections of the reports were analyzed multiple times to ensure the consistency of the error classifications. Reports that did not mention consequences in the free text sections were classified according to the quantitative data, relying on the reporter's perception of harm to the patient and the problems it posed for the unit. If the reports did not mention consequences for the hospitalization, they were included in the category of additional work and monitoring in situations when the researchers could reliably assess that the reported serious clinical consequence (e.g., anaphylactic shock) must have affected the duration of the hospital stay.

Some incident reports included several patient cases. These were divided into separate incident reports. Some incident reports concerning the case of an individual patient, contained several errors. These were categorized according to the error type during the classification process. The free text sections of the reports revealed situations related to medication reconciliation errors, even when the report had not been classified as such. For these reasons, the number of errors is higher than the number of incident reports.

Ethical Considerations

Approval for the study was obtained from the hospital. No names or other identifying information of patients or health care professionals were included in the reports, and no information about the reporter or the reporter's unit was recorded. According to the Finnish research ethics guidelines [22] and the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki [23], a pre‐evaluation of the study protocol by an ethics committee was not required since the study contained no medical intervention to patients and no identifiable patient health data were collected for the study. No informed consents was needed for this register‐based study. The data security and privacy were ensured throughout the study in accordance with the European Union's General Data Protection Regulation and the Finnish data security legislation.

Results

Results of the Quantitative Analysis

A total of 902 medication safety incidents were reported during the study period. The majority of the incidents actually happened to the patient (70.6%, n = 637) and the rest were near misses (Table 1). The most common errors were administration errors (45.8%, n = 413) and prescribing errors (20.7%, n = 187). Medication reconciliation errors accounted for 6.8% (n = 61) of the incidents. In nearly half (46.8%, n = 422) of the cases, the patient experienced no harm, whereas the harm was considered to be mild in 22.9% (n = 207), moderate in 6.5% (n = 59), and only exceedingly rarely were they designated as severe, that is, a mere 0.1% (n = 1) of the cases. Almost all incidents were reported by nursing staff (93.3%, n = 842). Background conditions behind the incidents and contributing factors were mentioned in 651 reports (72.2%). The contributing factors related most commonly to communication and information transfer, or handling of procedures (Figure 2).

Results of the Qualitative Analysis

Altogether, 38.8% (n = 350) of all medication safety incident reports were related to medication discrepancies. The majority of medication discrepancy‐related incidents occurred during the time when the patient was in the hospital (62.0%, n = 217) or during admission (34.3%, n = 120), with a smaller proportion at discharge (7.4%, n = 26). Some reports included multiple errors which took place at different phases of the hospitalization. A total of 424 errors were identified in these 350 reports.

Of the medication discrepancy‐related incidents (n = 350), almost half (46.0%, n = 161) were related to errors in the electronic medication list documentation (Table 2). Most commonly medicines were not recorded in the electronic medication list (n = 121), they were unscheduled or incorrectly scheduled in the electronic medication list (n = 26), or some discontinued medication remained in the electronic medication list (n = 22). The second most common error was the lack of medication reconciliation or inadequate medication reconciliation at the patient's first admission to the ward (30.3%, n = 106), followed by using outdated printout of the medication lists (15.4%, n = 54). Discussion

The quantitative analysis revealed that the most frequently medication safety incidents reported in surgical patients were administration and prescribing errors. However, most incidents caused no or only minor harm to the patient. Notably, more than every third of all medication safety incidents was associated with medication discrepancy. Two major types of discrepancy errors were identified; 1) errors in the electronic medication list documentation and 2) the lack of medication reconciliation. Most of these discrepancy errors caused no consequences, while one third resulted in harm to the patient and one‐fifth affected hospitalization either as additional work or even as consequence for the patient such as reoperations. Medication discrepancy‐related reports included incidents with clinically important consequences, such as treatment delays, psychological or cognitive symptoms, cardiorespiratory symptoms, metabolic disturbances, bleeding or hematoma, and rare life‐threatening events. Because this study was descriptive and based on voluntary incident reports, no conclusions can be drawn about causality, relative risk, or differences in harm between medication discrepancy‐related incidents and other medication safety incident types. Future studies with appropriate denominators and inferential analyzes are needed to examine whether specific incident types are associated with different levels of patient harm.

Nearly half of all medication discrepancy‐related incidents were associated with errors in the electronic medication list. The most common challenge encountered in our study was that medications initiated during surgery were documented in other locations, such as the anesthesia information management system, instead of the electronic medication list. Should this have occurred, it increased the risk that some medication would remain unnoticed, possibly resulting in drug omissions and other MEs in the patient's postoperative medication. Although the safe medication practice guidelines of the hospital state that the physician is responsible for updating the electronic medication list, there appear to be practical challenges in allocating the responsibilities and duties of different staff members and protocols, particularly in the postoperative prescribing and updating of the electronic medication list. This was also evident in the results of the quantitative analysis, in which errors in communication, information transfer and challenges in following protocols accounted for 80% of the reported contributing factors. These findings are consistent with previous studies [24, 25]. Future studies could include more detailed analysis about the contributing factors leading to difficulties in allocation of staff responsibilities and interprofessional work processes.

The most commonly reported ATC groups emerging from the study data were antithrombotics, opioids, beta‐lactam antibacterials and insulins and analogs, again in agreement with previous studies [3, 26, 27, 28, 29], which have noted that these are high‐risk drugs that affect medication safety in surgical patients. It is evident that these medications can cause serious ADEs in surgical patients. Accordingly, it has been reported that the most common consequences of AEs observed in surgical patients are related to the central nervous system [26], bleeding [29, 30, 31] and arterial or venous thrombosis [26], which were also observed in the data of this study.

Previous studies have linked medication‐related problems and medication discrepancies with hospital readmissions [31] and surgery cancellations [32, 33]. Previously, it has been shown that 1.0% to 2.4% of canceled surgeries are due to incorrect preoperative medication management, such as a failure to conduct a preanaesthesia evaluation, the patient not being informed to withhold medicines prior to surgery or not following instructions [32, 33]. In the present descriptive incident‐report data, some medication discrepancy‐related reports described consequences for hospitalization, including additional monitoring, reoperation, canceled surgery, and readmission. However, because the number of such reports was small and the data were not linked to surgical episode denominators, these findings should be interpreted descriptively. The contribution of preoperative medication management to surgical cancellations is considered important, as medication reconciliation and medication management errors could be prevented relatively easily in comparison to other errors in the surgical setting [32].

Because medication discrepancy‐related errors represented a notable proportion of the reported medication safety incidents, actions are needed to improve the accuracy and use of electronic medication lists in surgical care. An accurate reporting activity enables better utilization of safety incident reporting systems and improvement of patient safety. Patient‐involved incidents constituted 70.6% of the reports, whereas near misses constituted 29.4%. From a safety culture perspective, this distribution may reflect reporting practices in the voluntary reporting system and highlights the importance of encouraging near‐miss reporting as part of patient safety work. This is relatively well in line with other incident reporting studies conducted in Finland [13, 15, 16]. In addition, in previous studies conducted from incident reporting systems in different countries, the reporting activity has varied widely among the different professionals: nursing staff report the majority of the safety incidents (83%–90%), whereas there are few reports made by physicians (0.4%–5%) [15, 34]. We found that the proportion of incidents described by physicians in the surgical units was very low (3%), considering that physicians most frequently report operation‐related incidents as compared to other types of incidents [35]. Increasing the reporting activity overall, especially that by physicians, would be important, as the factors contributing to safety incidents may be better identified by these health care professionals [36].

Methods to improve medication safety in surgical patients include the use of the WHO and SURPASS Surgical Safety Checklists, which have a checking protocol of medications and allergies prior to the induction of anesthesia. The use of perioperative safety checklists has been shown to reduce surgical complications, reoperations, and hospital readmissions [37, 38]. Furthermore, according to several studies, a pharmacist‐led medication reconciliation during the perioperative period can reduce medication errors [39, 40, 41]. In addition to medication reconciliation, it is also important to increase each patient's awareness of the importance of an accurate and up‐to‐date medication list. According to a survey conducted by the Finnish Medicines Agency, only 20% of Finns have an up‐to‐date medication list [42]. Missing or outdated medication lists are also common among the older adults: in recent studies, 43% of older adults did not have an up‐to‐date medication list [43] and 22% did not understand the meaning of a medication list [44]. Therefore, increasing patients' and their caregivers' active involvement in maintaining accurate and up‐to‐date medication lists and making them available to health care providers could facilitate medication reconciliation and to contribute to improved medication safety.

An important strategy for reducing MEs in hospital setting is the implementation of a closed‐loop electronic medication management system (EMMS), in which manual processes are replaced by automated and electronic versions [45]. Based on these principles, after our data was collected, the study hospital has now fully transitioned to the use of electronic medication lists instead of printing them out. This is expected to reduce the MEs associated with the use of outdated medication lists. However, as seen in the study results, transitioning to the fully electronic systems does not eliminate the risk of medication discrepancies during the perioperative period. Therefore, education, orientation, and defined protocols and responsibilities of health care professionals are needed to ensure accurate and up‐to‐date electronic medication lists and support medication safety in surgical patients.

The strength of this study was the large number of medication safety incident reports. The study data provided extensive and diverse medication‐related information from seven different surgical units from the second largest University Hospital in Finland. In particular, the use of the qualitative analysis method allowed us to gather more information than could be obtained only from the quantitative data available in the reports. For example, in the structured data, medication reconciliation errors accounted for 7% of all reported incidents. In the qualitative review of the free‐text sections, reconciliation‐related issues were identified in 30% of medication discrepancy‐related reports, corresponding to approximately 12% of all reported incidents. This indicates that many of the reports that were primarily classified as other error types actually involved errors related to medication reconciliation and they took place already when the patient first arrived in the unit. Therefore, the number of medication reconciliation errors is higher than shown in the results of the quantitative analysis. This could be mostly in the situations involving multiple errors, where it may be technically easier or more practical to report only a single error. Error occurrence and reporting practices are influenced by an open and blame‑free organizational reporting culture, which is associated with reporting rates [46].

Study Limitations

This study has several limitations. It was conducted in a single center setting, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. The analysis relied on a voluntary incident reporting system, making the data vulnerable to underreporting and reporting bias—serious incidents are more likely to be reported than minor errors or near misses.

The incident reports were not linked to the total number of surgical episodes, admissions, discharges, medication orders, or medication reconciliation processes during the study period. Therefore, incidence rates could not be calculated. Terms such as ‘common’ should be interpreted as referring to the proportion of reported medication safety incidents, not to the occurrence of these events among all surgical patients.

The incident reports did not include information on length of stay or distinguish between short‐stay/day‐surgery pathways and admitted inpatient episodes. Therefore, the dataset may combine care episodes with different medication reconciliation requirements and practices. This may affect the generalizability of the reported proportions of medication discrepancy‐related incidents, because medication reconciliation may be performed differently in short‐stay surgical care.

The free text sections of the reporting system also leave room for differences in interpretation as the information presented in the reports was limited or incomplete, particularly with regard to the consequences of the incidents. Although the classification consistency criteria were carefully revised during the study by the research team, differences in interpretation may still have occurred. No quantitative inter‐coder reliability measure, such as Cohen's kappa, was calculated. Classification consistency relied on repeated review of the reports and consensus discussions within the multidisciplinary research team. This should be considered when interpreting the qualitative categorization of incident types and consequences. Consequently, other complementary methods could be useful if one wished to evaluate the impact of medication safety incidents on patients and their hospitalization. Nevertheless, the reporting of clinical consequences in our study appears to be consistent with a recent systematic review evaluating the reporting of incidents in different countries and the frequency of reported clinical consequences. The review indicated that approximately 56% of the reports stated that the mistake did have actual consequences for the patient [34].

Conclusions

Medication discrepancy errors accounted for approximately 39% of all medication safety incidents reported in the surgical units of a Finnish University Hospital. The findings highlight documentation of medications in electronic medication lists and incomplete medication reconciliation as important areas for improving medication safety in surgical care. To support medication safety in surgical care, the findings suggest the need for: 1) better adherence to protocols for electronic medication lists and medication reconciliation processes; 2) more effective communication among personnel involved in patient care; and 3) clearer allocation of responsibilities among healthcare professionals.
