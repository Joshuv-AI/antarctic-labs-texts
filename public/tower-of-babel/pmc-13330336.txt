Comparison of modified thoracolumbar interfascial plane and anterior quadratus lumborum blocks for postoperative analgesia and recovery after lumbar transpedicular fixation surgery: a prospective randomized clinical study
Authors: Tuba Yaman, Ahmet Murat Yayik, Mehmet Kursat Karadag, Mehmet Hakan Sahin, Elif Oral Ahiskalioglu, Erkan Cem Celik, Muhammed Enes Aydin, Ali Ahiskalioglu, Nagihan Si̇msek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEffective postoperative analgesia after lumbar transpedicular fixation (TPF) surgery remains challenging because of extensive tissue trauma and high nociceptive burden. Ultrasound-guided fascial plane blocks have emerged as important components of multimodal analgesia in spinal surgery. This study aimed to compare the analgesic efficacy, opioid consumption, technical performance, and quality of recovery associated with the modified thoracolumbar interfascial plane (mTLIP) block versus the anterior quadratus lumborum block (QLB) in patients undergoing two- or three-level lumbar TPF surgery.MethodsIn this prospective, randomized, single-center trial, 100 patients (ASA I–II) scheduled for two- or three-level lumbar TPF surgery were randomly assigned to receive bilateral ultrasound-guided mTLIP (n = 50) or anterior QLB (n = 50) in addition to standardized general anesthesia and multimodal postoperative analgesia. The primary outcome was cumulative fentanyl consumption during the first 24 postoperative hours. Secondary outcomes included interval opioid consumption, postoperative pain scores, time to first analgesic request, block performance characteristics, opioid-related adverse effects, and quality of recovery assessed using the QoR-15 questionnaire.ResultsBaseline demographic and surgical characteristics were comparable between groups. Fentanyl consumption during the first 4 postoperative hours was significantly lower in the mTLIP group compared with the QLB group (75 [50–125] µg vs. 125 [75–175] µg; p = 0.017). Opioid consumption during the 4–8 h and 8–24 h intervals, as well as total 24-hour fentanyl consumption, did not differ significantly between groups. Postoperative pain scores at rest and during movement, time to first analgesic request, additional analgesic requirements, QoR-15 scores, and opioid-related adverse effects were similar. The mTLIP block demonstrated significantly shorter performance time and superior needle visualization compared with QLB (p < 0.001).ConclusionsIn patients undergoing multi-level lumbar TPF surgery, the mTLIP block provided superior opioid-sparing analgesia in the early postoperative period compared with anterior QLB, while overall analgesic efficacy and quality of recovery were comparable thereafter. Improved technical performance and procedural efficiency may favor mTLIP as a practical regional analgesia option in extensive lumbar spinal surgery.

Introduction

Transpedicular screw fixation (TPF) surgery is one of the fundamental stabilization techniques used in the treatment of conditions characterized by spinal instability, deformity, and severe structural disruption [1]. Severe postoperative pain is commonly encountered following TPF surgery [2]. Extensive skin incision, dissection, prolonged retraction of the paraspinal muscles, manipulation of posterior osseous structures, including the lamina and facet joints, and periosteal stimulation during pedicle screw placement generate substantial nociceptive input, thereby contributing significantly to the postoperative pain [2, 3, 4].

Effective pain control is essential for facilitating early mobilization, preventing thromboembolic complications, and reducing the length of hospital stay [3, 4, 5, 6]. To achieve effective analgesia following spinal surgery, multimodal analgesic strategies incorporating nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, opioids, and ultrasound-guided fascial plane blocks have gained increasing prominence [7, 8, 9].

The thoracolumbar interfascial plane (TLIP) block is a lumbar paraspinal regional anaesthetic technique initially described by Hand et al. [10]. This approach was subsequently refined by Ahiskalioglu et al. into the modified thoracolumbar interfascial plane (mTLIP) block, in which local anaesthetic is injected within the fascial plane between the longissimus and iliocostalis muscles [11]. This block selectively targets the dorsal rami of the thoracolumbar spinal nerves by delivering local anaesthetic within defined paraspinal fascial compartments and provides effective analgesia in lumbar spinal surgery [10, 12, 13].

Quadratus lumborum block (QLB) is a posterior abdominal wall block developed as an advanced modification of the transversus abdominis plane (TAP) block. The technique involves injecting local anesthetic into the thoracolumbar fascia enveloping the quadratus lumborum muscle. The thoracolumbar fascia extends posteriorly and converges toward the paravertebral region. In QLB, the local anesthetic spreads along this fascial plane to the paravertebral space, providing analgesia by affecting both the dorsal and ventral rami [14]. This block is primarily used in abdominal surgery but has also been applied in lumbar spinal surgery and provides effective analgesia [14, 15]. Both the mTLIP block and the QLB block have been effectively utilized in various spinal surgeries, and their efficacy has been demonstrated [13]. However, to the best of our knowledge, there is no study comparing these two techniques specifically in the context of transpedicular fixation surgery.

The primary objective of this prospective, randomized study is to compare the effects of mTLIP and anterior QLB block on postoperative opioid consumption in patients undergoing two- or three level lumbar TPF surgery. Secondary objectives include assessing the effects of postoperative pain scores, quality-of-recovery scores (QoR-15), first analgesic requirement time, the need for additional analgesic interventions and block performance data, such as needle visibility and performance time.

Methods

Study design

This single-center, prospective, randomized clinical trial was conducted at the Ataturk University Medical Faculty Hospital, Erzurum, Türkiye between April 2022 and July 2023. This study was approved by Institutional Review Board of Ataturk University Faculty of Medicine Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Approval No: B.30.2.ATA.0.01.00/295, Chairperson Prof. Prof. Mustafa Gul) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. The trial was prospectively registered and followed the CONSORT statement.

Eligible participants were aged 18–65 years with American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status I or II scheduled for any two- or three-level lumbar fixation procedures between L1 and L5 under general anesthesia. Patients with a history of bleeding diathesis, chronic opioid usage, those receiving anticoagulant therapy, individuals with allergies to the pharmacological agents used in the study (local anesthetics, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and opioids), those with local skin infection at the block site, pregnant patients, and those who declined participation were excluded.

Randomization and blinding

Randomization was performed in a 1:1 ratio using a computer-generated sequence created by an independent statistician before surgery. To maintain allocation concealment, group assignments were placed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes, which were only opened by the anesthesiologist upon patient admission to the block room. The anesthesiologist performing the blocks was not involved in postoperative assessments or data collection. Additionally, outcome assessors remained blinded to group allocation throughout the perioperative period.

Block technique

All patients were transferred to the block room 30 min before surgery, and standard monitoring was applied. Bilateral blocks were performed by the same anesthesiologist experienced in regional anesthesia, using the same ultrasound device and a 22-G, 10-cm sonovisible needle. During block administration, block performance data and any complications were recorded. Needle visibility was assessed using two separate parameters: needle tip visibility and needle shaft visibility. Needle tip visibility was graded on a five-point scale as follows: ‘very good’ when the needle tip was visualized as a completely bright point; ‘good’; ‘fair’; ‘poor’; and ‘very poor’ when only the shadow of the needle tip within the tissue was visible. Needle shaft visibility was also evaluated on a five-point scale and categorized as: ‘not visualized,’ ‘only a small segment visualized,’ ‘less than half visualized,’ ‘almost all visualized,’ and ‘all visualized from the puncture site. Twenty minutes after the block, a different anesthesiologist conducted a bilateral dermatomal assessment using a cold–warm sensation test, and the findings were documented.

Anterior quadratus lumborum block technique

After placing the patient in the lateral decubitus position, the block site and the convex ultrasound probe were sterilized. The probe was positioned at the mid-axillary line between the iliac crest and the ribs (Fig-1 A). The transverse processes of the vertebrae, erector spinae, quadratus lumborum, and psoas muscles were identified. Once an adequate image was obtained, an in-plane approach was used to advance the needle into the fascial plane between the quadratus lumborum and psoas muscles, and negative aspiration was performed (Fig-1B). After confirming the absence of blood or air, 2 mL of saline was injected to verify correct needle placement. Subsequently, 30 mL of 0.25% bupivacaine solution was administered into this plane to perform the anterior quadratus lumborum block. The procedure was then repeated on the contralateral side following repositioning of the patient.

Modified thoracolumbar interfacial plane block technique

After positioning the patients in the prone position, a linear ultrasound probe was placed perpendicular to the spine at the level of the L3 vertebra (Fig-1 C). Following visualization of the spinous process, the probe was moved laterally to identify the paraspinal muscles (multifidus, longissimus, and iliocostalis). Using an in-plane approach, the needle was advanced into the fascial plane between the longissimus and iliocostalis muscles (Fig-1D). After confirming negative aspiration for blood and air, 2 mL of saline was injected to verify correct needle placement. Subsequently, 30 mL of 0.25% bupivacaine solution was administered into this plane to perform the mTLIP block. The same procedure was then repeated on the contralateral side while maintaining the prone position.

General anesthesia

All patients in both groups were monitored using standard monitoring, including electrocardiography, non-invasive blood pressure, and pulse oximetry. Midazolam (2 mg) was administered intravenously for premedication. For induction of anesthesia, intravenous propofol (2–2.5 mg/kg), fentanyl (1–1.5 µg/kg), and rocuronium bromide (0.6 mg/kg) were administered. Following endotracheal intubation, patients were positioned in the prone position. Anesthesia was maintained with sevoflurane in a mixture of 50% air and 50% oxygen. For intraoperative analgesia, remifentanil was administered as a continuous infusion at a rate of 0.01–0.1 µg/kg/min. The infusion rate was titrated based on the patient’s hemodynamic responses, with adjustments made to maintain heart rate and blood pressure within physiological limits and to ensure hemodynamic stability throughout the procedure.

All patients underwent two- or three-level lumbar transpedicular fixation surgery, which was performed by the same surgical team using a standardized and consistent surgical approach throughout the study period. Incision length, fixation level, intraoperative blood loss, surgical duration, and total anesthesia time were recorded. At the end of surgery, neuromuscular blockade was reversed using sugammadex at a dose of 2–4 mg/kg. Patients who met the criteria for extubation were subsequently extubated and transferred to the post-anaesthesia care unit for close monitoring. Once a modified Aldrete score of ≥ 9 was achieved, patients were discharged from the PACU and transferred to the ward.

Postoperative analgesia

All patients received the same postoperative analgesic regimen. For postoperative pain management, 50 mg of intravenous dexketoprofen and 1 g of intravenous paracetamol were administered 30 min before the end of surgery. Postoperatively, paracetamol was repeated every 6 h and dexketoprofen every 12 h via the intravenous route.

Postoperative analgesia was provided using an intravenous patient-controlled analgesia system containing fentanyl at a concentration of 10 µg/mL. The PCA was programmed without a loading dose or basal infusion, delivering a 25 µg bolus with a lockout interval of 15 min, and was continued for 24 h. In the recovery room, patients with a VAS score of 4 or higher were administered 25 mg of intravenous meperidine as rescue analgesia.

Postoperative follow-up

Patients were assessed in the PACU at 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h postoperatively. Pain scores were recorded at each assessment. In addition, opioid consumption was documented for the intervals of 0–4, 4–8, and 8–24 h, as well as cumulatively over 24 h. The time to first analgesic request, defined as the first activation of the PCA device, was also recorded.

Opioid-related adverse effects, including nausea, vomiting, need for antiemetic therapy, constipation, pruritus, urinary retention, and dry mouth, were documented. Antiemetic treatment was administered when patients reported nausea requiring intervention or experienced at least one episode of vomiting, in line with routine clinical practice. Postoperative constipation was assessed within the first 24 h after surgery. Accordingly, postoperative constipation was defined as the absence of bowel movement (stool passage) and passage of flatus within the first 24-hour postoperative period, accompanied by clinical symptoms such as abdominal discomfort and distension.

At 24 h postoperatively, the quality of recovery was assessed using Turkish version of the quality of recovery-15 (QoR-15) questionnaire [16].

Statistical analysis and sample size calculation

The primary outcome of the study was fentanyl consumption during the first 24 h after surgery. According to the results of a pilot study conducted in 20 patients, mean 24-hour fentanyl consumption was 332 ± 142 µg in the QLB group (n = 10) and 271 ± 115 µg in the mTLIP group (n = 10).

Based on 24-hour fentanyl consumption, a total sample size of 92 patients (46 in each group) was calculated using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.2; Düsseldorf, Germany), with an alpha level of 0.05, a power of 0.90, and a medium effect size (0.47). To account for potential dropouts, it was decided to include at least 50 patients in each group.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of data distribution. Descriptive data are presented as median [interquartile range] or number (percentage), as appropriate. The Mann–Whitney U test was applied for between-group comparisons of non-normally distributed continuous variables. The chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables (such as nausea and constipation) between the groups. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 111 patients were assessed for eligibility, of whom 11 were excluded—three due to possible inability to complete the QoR-15 assessment in Turkish and eight due to chronic opioid use. The remaining 100 patients were randomized equally to the QLB (n = 50) and mTLIP (n = 50) groups. All randomized patients received the allocated intervention; none were lost to follow-up, and all were included in the final analysis, ensuring complete outcome data for both groups (Fig-2).

Discussion

Our study demonstrated that the modified thoracolumbar interfascial plane block was shown to reduce opioid consumption in the early postoperative period compared with the anterior quadratus lumborum block in patients undergoing two- to three-level TPF surgery; however, opioid consumption was comparable between the two groups at later postoperative time points. In addition, no significant differences were observed between the mTLIP and QLB groups with respect to pain scores at rest and during movement, time to first analgesic request, or the requirement for rescue analgesia. Compared with QLB, the mTLIP block was associated with a shorter block performance time, superior needle visualization, and greater technical ease of application.

The early superiority of the mTLIP block over QLB is consistent with the anatomical targets of these techniques and their respective patterns of local anaesthetic spread. In the mTLIP technique, local anaesthetic is spread within the fascial plane between the longissimus and iliocostalis muscles, resulting in a distribution that is closer to the dorsal rami and therefore more segmental and predictable. The modification described by Ahıskalıoğlu et al. may further support this early analgesic effect by allowing easier sonographic identification and more reliable separation of the relevant fascial planes compared with the classical TLIP approach [11, 13]. Although in our study all patients have 100% coverage in the posterior dermatomes in the L1-L5 range, in anterior QLB, the injectate may spread variably along the extensive thoracolumbar fascia, leading to a more heterogeneous temporal and anatomical effect on the dorsal rami. Although QLB has been shown to provide broad-field analgesia in abdominal surgery, the segmental sensory blockade required for spinal surgery may not always be achieved with sufficient consistency [17].

The results of randomized studies evaluating single-shot QLB are more heterogeneous. Indeed, a randomized comparison of posterior QLB and TLIP reported no significant differences between the two techniques in terms of pain scores or opioid consumption [15]. This discrepancy may be largely attributable to the inclusion of single-level disc surgery in that study, a procedure typically associated with a lower postoperative pain burden. In contrast, our study included two- to three-level instrumented spinal surgeries requiring wider tissue dissection and associated with higher postoperative pain intensity. In this context, the superior early analgesic efficacy of the mTLIP block, which more directly targets the dorsal rami and paraspinal components, appears clinically meaningful. However, opioid consumption was significantly lower only in the early postoperative period (0–4 h), which may partly reflect the predefined time interval used for analysis. Beyond this period, the absence of a significant difference suggests that the analgesic effects of the two techniques may converge over time. Furthermore, the time to first analgesic request was comparable between groups and did not reach statistical significance, indicating a similar overall duration of effective analgesia. Therefore, while mTLIP may provide a modest advantage in the immediate postoperative phase, this benefit should be interpreted with caution in terms of its clinical magnitude and sustainability.

Our findings further indicate that mTLIP and anterior QLB provide comparable analgesia during the mid-to-late postoperative period. Consistent with this observation, a randomized controlled study by Alver et al. in microendoscopic disc surgery demonstrated that mTLIP was associated with lower pain scores and improved QoR-40 in the early postoperative hours, whereas analgesic efficacy converged between the two techniques at 24 h [17]. Importantly, our results suggest that this convergence persists even in stabilization surgeries involving larger surgical incisions and a greater nociceptive burden. This pattern may be explained by the similar duration of action of local anesthetics when administered as single-shot mTLIP and QLB techniques.

In addition to analgesic efficacy, postoperative quality of recovery was assessed using the QoR-15 score at 24 h, with no significant difference observed between the groups. This finding supports our overall results, indicating that although early postoperative opioid between the two block techniques. Given that the QoR-15 is a sensitive, multidimensional instrument encompassing physical comfort, emotional state, perceived psychological support, and pain control, the absence of a between-group difference suggests that the overall clinical impact of analgesia was similar with both mTLIP and QLB.

This observation is consistent with similar findings reported in block comparison studies in literature. A study comparing TLIP and posterior QLB reported that when pain scores and opioid consumption were comparable, QoR scores likewise did not differ significantly between the two techniques [15]. In contrast, Alver et al. reported higher QoR-40 scores with mTLIP compared with posterior QLB in microendoscopic disc surgery; however, this difference emerged in a surgical context characterized by more limited tissue trauma, where the segmental selectivity of the block may play a more pronounced role [17]. In our study, involving a higher nociceptive burden model such as two- to three-level instrumented spinal surgery, the impact of single-shot block techniques on overall recovery perception appeared comparable. These findings support the notion that, in extensive spinal procedures, postoperative recovery is shaped by multiple perioperative factors beyond the choice of regional block technique alone.

One of the key findings of our study is the markedly greater ease of performance of the mTLIP block compared with QLB. Our block performance data demonstrated significant differences between the two techniques in terms of both imaging quality and procedure time. Needle tip and shaft visibility were significantly superior with the mTLIP block than with QLB, facilitating more precise needle guidance and potentially increasing procedural safety.

In addition, the mTLIP block can be performed without the need to reposition the patient, which represents a substantial practical advantage in the peroperative setting. This not only shortens the overall procedure time but also improves workflow efficiency and reduces the burden on both the patient and the clinical team. Furthermore, the mTLIP block is technically simpler to perform, making it more reproducible and accessible, particularly in routine clinical practice. Indeed, although a modest difference in fentanyl consumption was observed within the first 4 postoperative hours, the clinical significance of this finding appears limited when considered alongside the aforementioned advantages. Taken together, these results suggest that the mTLIP block may be a more practical and preferable option for lumbar spine surgery, offering sufficient analgesia while providing important procedural and functional benefits.

In the mTLIP group, the vast majority of cases were rated as having “good” or “very good” visualization of both the needle tip and shaft, whereas imaging quality in the QLB group was more heterogeneous, with the needle shaft only partially visualized in more than half of the cases.The deeper anatomical plane required for anterior QLB, together with the challenges associated with accessing the thoracolumbar fascia and quadratus lumborum muscle, may contribute to suboptimal needle visualization. In contrast, the mTLIP block is performed within a more superficial interfascial plane between the paraspinal muscles, which is more clearly defined and more readily distinguishable on ultrasound. This finding is consistent with previous reports indicating that mTLIP allows easier sonographic identification than the classical TLIP technique. Ahıskalıoğlu et al. demonstrated that the longissimus–iliocostalis plane can be more reliably delineated than the multifidus–longissimus plane, suggesting that this modified approach may enhance both procedural safety and block success [11, 13]. This anatomical advantage was also reflected in the improved ease of application observed in our study.

Although the number of needle insertion attempts was similar in both groups, suggesting comparable technical success rates, the markedly superior imaging quality observed with mTLIP indicates a block profile with a shorter learning curve and greater reproducibility. Previous studies have reported that anterior QLB may be technically more challenging, particularly in obese patients, owing to greater depth and heterogeneity of tissue layers; our findings are consistent with these observations [18].

In this context, although mTLIP and QLB yielded comparable analgesic efficacy during the mid to late postoperative period, mTLIP may be regarded as a clinically more advantageous option owing to its shorter performance time, superior sonographic imaging quality, and more predictable anatomical targets. Particularly in extensive spinal stabilization procedures such as two- to three-level instrumentation, the ability to perform the block in a rapid and reliable manner represents a key factor that may favorably influence perioperative workflow.

This study has several limitations. First, no data were available regarding preoperative pain scores, which may have influenced postoperative analgesic requirements. Second, the absence of a control group precluded direct assessment of the absolute analgesic efficacy of either block beyond their contribution within a multimodal analgesia regimen; comparison with a control group might have affected the observed outcomes. Third, intraoperative opioid consumption and was not recorded. Intraoperative opioid use may influence early postoperative pain scores and opioid-related adverse effects. Fourth, the QoR-15 questionnaire was administered at 24 h postoperatively; assessments at 48 and 72 h might have revealed differences between groups. Finally, the sample size was calculated based on the primary outcome of opioid consumption. With a relatively limited sample size, block-related adverse events may not have been fully captured. Larger, adequately powered studies are therefore warranted to more comprehensively evaluate safety outcomes.

Conclusion

In patients undergoing two- to three-level instrumented spinal stabilization surgery, the mTLIP block provided superior opioid-sparing analgesia in the early postoperative period compared with anterior QLB, while both techniques demonstrated comparable analgesic efficacy in the mid to late postoperative phases. Although overall pain scores, rescue analgesic requirements, and quality of recovery were similar between groups, the mTLIP block was associated with a shorter performance time, improved sonographic needle visualization, and greater technical reproducibility. These findings suggest that, beyond analgesic equivalence, procedural feasibility and anatomical predictability may favor mTLIP as a practical regional analgesia option in extensive spinal surgery.
