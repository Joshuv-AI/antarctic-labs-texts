Global existence and optimal time-decay rates of the compressible Navier-Stokes equations with density-dependent viscosities
Jie Fan, Xiangdi Huang, Anchun Ni a. State Key Laboratory of Mathematical Sciences, Academy of Mathematics and Systems ScienceChinese Academy of Sciences, Beijing 100910, P.R.China;

Abstract
This paper is devoted to studying the Cauchy problem for the three-dimensional isentropic compressible Navier-Stokes equations with density-dependent viscosities given by μ = ρ α , λ = ρ α ​ ( α > 0 ) \mu=\rho^{\alpha},\lambda=\rho^{\alpha}(\alpha>0) . We establish the global existence and optimal decay rates of classical solutions under the assumptions of small initial data in L 1 ​ ( ℝ 3 ) ∩ L 2 ​ ( ℝ 3 ) L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}) and the viscosity constraint | α − 1 | ≪ 1 |\alpha-1|\ll 1 . The key idea of our proof lies in the combination of Green’s function method, energy method and a time-decay regularity criterion. In contrast to previous works, the Sobolev norms of the spatial derivatives of the initial data may be arbitrarily large in our analysis. Keywords: compressible Navier-Stokes equations; global well-posedness; optimal decay rates; density-dependent viscosities Mathematics Subject Classifications (2010): 76N10, 35B45.



1
Introduction

We consider the isentropic compressible Naiver-Stokes equations with density-dependent viscosities in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
:

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\rho+\div(\rho u)=0,\\
\partial_{t}(\rho u)+\div(\rho u\otimes u)+\nabla P=\div\mathbb{T},\\
\rho|_{t=0}=\rho_{0},u|_{t=0}=u_{0},\end{cases}

(1.1)

Here
\rho(t,x)
and
u(t,x)
denote the density and velocities of the fluid respectively. The pressure
P
and the viscous stress tensor
\mathbb{T}
are given by:

\begin{split}&P=\frac{1}{\gamma}\rho^{\gamma}\text{ and }\mathbb{T}=2\mu(\rho)Du+\lambda(\rho)\div u\mathbb{I}_{3},\end{split}

(1.2)

where we assume
\gamma>1
and
Du=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla u+(\nabla u)^{T})
is the deformation tensor and
I_{3}
is the three-dimensional identity matrix. The shear and bulk viscosities
\mu(\rho),\lambda(\rho)
are dependent with the density and satisfy the following hypothesis:

\mu(\rho)=\mu\rho^{\alpha},\lambda(\rho)=\lambda\rho^{\alpha}\text{ and }\alpha>0,\mu>0,2\mu+3\lambda\geq 0.

(1.3)

The investigation of the compressible Navier–Stokes equations with density-dependent viscosities
\mu=\mu(\rho)
and
\lambda=\lambda(\rho)
is complicated due to the strong nonlinearity and possible degeneracy. Vasseur-Yu
[
17
]
, and Li-Xin
[
11
]
independently proved the global existence of weak solutions in the density-degenerate case, based on the significant a priori estimates developed by Bresch-Desjardins
[
2
]
, and Mellet-Vasseur
[
15
]
. The global well-posedness of classical solutions under sufficiently large initial density was established by Yu
[
18
]
and Huang–Li–Zhang
[
5
]
, using a method adapted from Huang–Li–Xin
[
6
]
, which was originally developed for small-energy classical solutions in the constant-viscosity case.

The behavior of solutions near non-vacuum equilibrium for the compressible Navier–Stokes equations was first studied by Matsumura-Nishida
[
13
]
, where they proved the global existence of classical solutions and derived decay rates under the assumption of small initial data in Sobolev space
H^{s}
. Zeng
[
19
]
was the first to adopt Green’s function analysis for the one-dimensional isentropic compressible Navier–Stokes equations. Hoff-Zumbrun
[
7
,
8
]
studied multi-dimensional compressible flows with artificial viscosity and established uniform decay rates for diffusive waves. Furthermore, Liu-Wang
[
12
]
and Li
[
9
]
carefully investigated the properties of Green’s functions for the isentropic and full Navier–Stokes systems, respectively, and presented optimal time-decay rates in odd dimensions, where the weaker Huygens’ principle appears due to stronger dispersion effects in odd-dimensional spaces. Guo-Wang
[
4
]
also developed a general framework to study the optimal decay rates of higher-order spatial derivatives for dissipative systems, including the compressible Navier–Stokes equations.
It should be noted that all of the above decay estimates are derived under the assumption of small initial data in Sobolev norms:

\left\lVert(\rho_{0}-\tilde{\rho},u)\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\ll 1,

where
\tilde{\rho}
is the equilibrium density. Nevertheless, few studies have investigated the decay rates of solutions when the viscosities are density-dependent.
Luo-Yang
[
10
]
obtained the optimal decay rates for higher-order derivatives in the case where viscosity depends on density (
\mu(\rho)=\alpha\rho,\lambda(\rho)=\beta\rho
), under the following conditions:

\sum_{l=0}^{N}\Big(\|\nabla^{l}(\rho-1)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\nabla^{l}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\Big)\ll 1.

In this paper, our goal is to remove the restriction of requiring small derivatives and to establish the global existence and optimal decay rates of solutions to (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) under the following partial smallness condition:

\left\lVert(\rho_{0}-\tilde{\rho},u)\right\rVert_{L^{1}\cap L^{2}}\ll 1,

and a proper restriction to the parameter
\alpha
.

To formulate our problem, we consider solutions to the compressible Navier–Stokes equations (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) with density-dependent viscosities, near the equilibrium state
(\tilde{\rho},0)
. For convenience, we set
\tilde{\rho}=1
and take the replacement:

\rho\to\varrho+\tilde{\varrho}=\varrho+1,u\to u.

Then we obtain the following linearized compressible Naiver-Stokes equations:

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\varrho+\nabla\cdot u=N_{\varrho}(\varrho,u),\\
\partial_{t}u+\nabla\varrho-(\mu\Delta u+(\mu+\lambda)\nabla(\nabla\cdot u))=N_{u}(\varrho,u),\\
\varrho|_{t=0}=\varrho_{0}=\rho_{0}-1,u|_{t=0}=u_{0},\end{cases}

(1.4)

where the nonlinear terms
N_{\varrho},N_{u}
are given by:

N_{\varrho}(\varrho,u)=-\nabla\cdot(\varrho u),\quad N_{u}(\varrho,u)=N_{1}+N_{2}+N_{3}+N_{4},\\

(1.5)

with

\begin{split}N_{1}(\varrho,u)=&-u\cdot\nabla u,\\
N_{2}(\varrho,u)=&-H_{\gamma}(\varrho)\nabla\varrho,H_{\gamma}(\varrho)=(1+\varrho)^{\gamma-2}-1,\\
N_{3}(\varrho,u)=&G_{\alpha}(\varrho)\left(2\mu\nabla\varrho\cdot Du+\lambda\nabla\varrho\div u\right),G_{\alpha}(\varrho)=\alpha(1+\varrho)^{\alpha-2},\\
N_{4}(\varrho,u)=&H_{\alpha}(\varrho)\left(\mu\Delta u+(\mu+\lambda)\nabla\div u\right),H_{\alpha}(\varrho)=(1+\varrho)^{\alpha-1}-1.\\
\end{split}

(1.6)

The main result of this paper can be stated as follows:

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume that the initial data
(\varrho_{0},u_{0})\in H^{4}(\mathbb{R}^{3}).
There exists sufficiently small constants
C_{0},\delta>0
such that if

\begin{split}\left\lVert(\varrho_{0},u_{0})\right\rVert_{L^{1}\cap L^{2}}\leq C_{0},\end{split}

(1.7)

and

|\alpha-1|\leq\delta,

(1.8)

then the Cauchy problem (
1.1
) has a unique global classical solution in
\mathbb{R}^{3}\times(0,\infty)
satisfying

(\varrho,u)\in C([0,\infty);H^{4}),\nabla u\in L^{2}([0,\infty);H^{4}).

Furthermore, we have the following optimal uniform decay estimate:

\begin{split}&\left\lVert(\varrho,u)(t)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq C(1+t)^{-\frac{3}{2}},\\
&\left\lVert\nabla(\varrho,u)(t)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq C(1+t)^{-2},\\
\end{split}

(1.9)

and the optimal higher-order decay estimates:

\left\lVert\nabla^{k}(\varrho,u)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C(1+t)^{-\frac{3}{4}-\frac{k}{2}},k=0,1,2.

(1.10)

For
2\leq q\leq\infty,
it also holds

\|\nabla^{k^{\prime}}(\varrho,u)\|_{L^{p}}\leq C(1+t)^{-\frac{3}{2}(1-\frac{1}{p})-\frac{k^{\prime}}{2}},

(1.11)

with
k^{\prime}=0,1.

Remark 1.1
.

It should be noted that
L^{1}
-smallness is necessary to derive the optimal decay estimates (
1.9
) and (
1.10
), although the solution may not preserve in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
due to the possibility of negative perturbation of density.

Remark 1.2
.

Unlike classical results
[
4
,
7
,
8
]
that require smallness in the
H^{N}
-norm (with
N\geq 2
), our framework imposes smallness only on the
L^{1}\cap L^{2}
-norm while allowing the derivatives to remain bounded. Our results permits the Sobolev norms of the spatial derivatives of the initial data to be arbitrarily large.

Remark 1.3
.

The condition (
1.8
) is essential for closing the high-order estimates, which suggests that our method might fail in the constant viscosity case. Moreover, our analysis covers the case
\alpha=1
.

Remark 1.4
.

We first obtain the uniform decay estimates (
1.9
) via the continuity argument, upon which the optimal decay estimates (
1.10
)-(
1.11
) is subsequently established.

We now outline the proof for Theorem
1.1
and highlight the key challenges and techniques involved. Our main strategy combines the linear analysis via the Green function (see Section
2
), and a dedicated nonlinear analysis which couples conditional energy estimates with a regularity criterion, which is formulated as uniform optimal decay estimates (see Section
3
).

For the linear analysis, we have leveraged the advantages of the Green function of the linearized Navier-Stokes equations, and give out the following decomposition for the Green function
G
according to the frequency analysis:

G=G_{L}+G_{HR}+G_{HS}.

Here
G_{L}
denotes the low-frequency part, which behaves similarly to the heat kernel.
G_{HR}
is the regular part related to the high-frequency regime, and it can accommodate one derivative in regularity estimates.
G_{HS}
represents the singular part of the Green’s function; it acts like a Dirac delta function at the high-frequency level, but fortunately only appears in the continuity equation. The part is standard and can be checked in many other works concerning compressible Naiver-Stokes equation and related models (see
[
3
,
7
,
8
,
16
]
).

Our main innovation focuses on the proof of nonlinear stability, which is sketched through the following bootstrap process:

\text{regularity criterion}\Longrightarrow\text{conditional energy estimates}\Longrightarrow\text{improved regularity criterion}.

The regularity criterion is designed in accordance with the linear stability and optimal uniform decay estimates (see (
1.9
)):

\left\lVert(\varrho,u)(t)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq C(1+t)^{-\frac{3}{2}},\\
\left\lVert\nabla(\varrho,u)(t)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq C(1+t)^{-2}.

We first attempt to establish conditional energy estimates under the above time-decay regularity criterion, together with the
L^{2}
-smallness assumption on the initial data (
1.7
) and the viscosity constraint (
1.8
). It is notable that the classical energy estimate is proved by constructing a family of energy functionals coupled with each other, due to the lack of a dissipative estimate for the density as the continuity equation is hyperbolic (see, e.g. Guo-Wang
[
4
]
). Consequently, it requires the smallness of the initial data in the Sobolev norm (
H^{[\frac{N}{2}]+2}
for
N>3
).

In contrast to the classical approach, our conditional energy estimate needs only
L^{2}
-smallness of the initial data, with no smallness condition is imposed on the derivatives. This is because the time-decay regularity criterion enables us to close energy inequality at each
l-
th level. In this process, the following nearly-linear condition

|\alpha-1|\leq\delta(\mu),

for the viscosity
\mu(\rho)=\mu\rho^{\alpha},\lambda(\rho)=\lambda\rho^{\alpha}

also plays a significant role in absorbing the higher-order dissipative terms for the velocity, since the linear expansion of
H_{\alpha}(\rho)\propto(\alpha-1)
, which acts as extra coefficients on the dissipation terms.

With the conditional energy estimates at hand, we are able to close the time-decay regularity criterion by the Duhamel formula (
2.1
), using the regularity estimates (see Lemma
2.2
) of the Green function as well as the a priori optimal decay estimates. Careful decomposition and interpolation techniques are utilized in this process. Particularly, a
L^{1+}
axuiliary estimate (where we choose as
L^{\frac{4}{3}}
) is introduced to obtain the optimal uniform decay estimate (
1.9
), since the
L^{1}
estimate is not expected as we addressed in the Remark
1.1
. It is also noteworthy that to close the a priori assumption (
3.2
), the singular part of the Green’s function requires the estimates of
\|\nabla^{2}(\varrho,u)\|_{L^{1}L^{\infty}}
for the solution, while the second-order nonlinear terms involving
\nabla^{2}u
additionally demand higher regularity of the solution.

Finally, based on the uniform decay estimates established in Section
3
, we obtain the global existence and optimal decay rates of the solution by employing the standard continuity argument and the Green’s function method in Section
4
.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.

In Section
2
, we formulate the problem using the Green’s function and provide a frequency analysis of its different components. In Section
3
, we establish both basic and higher-order energy estimates, design a bootstrap scheme, and close it using these estimates along with the regularity properties of the Green’s function. In Section
4
, we verify the global well-posedness and derive the
H^{2}
-optimal decay estimates, thereby completing the proof of the main theorem.

2
Preliminary

For convenience in the nonlinear analysis, we express system (
1.4
) in operator form:

\begin{split}\partial_{t}V+LV=N(V).\end{split}

Here we denote

V=\begin{pmatrix}\varrho\\
u\end{pmatrix},L=\begin{pmatrix}0&\div\\
\nabla&-(\mu\Delta+(\mu+\lambda)\nabla\div)\Id_{n\times n}\end{pmatrix},N(V)=\begin{pmatrix}N_{\varrho}(V)\\
N_{u}(V)\end{pmatrix}.

Clearly,
L
is a self-adjoint operator and can be characterized by its symbol. According to the Duhamel principle, the solution can be expressed as follows:

\begin{split}V(t)=&e^{-tL}V_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-s)L}N(V)(s)ds=G(t)*V_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}G(t-s)*N(V)(s)ds.\end{split}

(2.1)

Here we have
\mathcal{F}({e^{-tL}f})=\hat{G}(t)\hat{f}.

For completeness, we provide a sketch of the analysis of the Green function
G(t,x)
. We aim to compute its Fourier transform
\hat{G}(t,\xi)
and derive regularity estimates via frequency analysis. For more details on the linear analysis, please refer to
[
3
,
7
,
8
,
16
]
.

1.

Explicit form of Green function.

To analyze the density component of the Green function, a key observation is that we can derive the following wave-type equation from the linear part of (
1.4
):

\varrho_{tt}-\nabla\varrho-(2\mu+\lambda)\Delta\varrho_{t}=0.

In the frequency form, it can be written as the following second-order ODE:

\frac{d}{dt}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\varrho}\\
\hat{\varrho}_{t}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-|\xi|^{2}&-(2\mu+\lambda)|\xi|^{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\varrho}\\
\hat{\varrho}_{t}\end{pmatrix}.

(2.2)

The the general solution of (
2.2
) is

\begin{split}\hat{\varrho}(t)=A(\xi)e^{\lambda_{-}(\xi)t}+B(\xi)e^{\lambda_{+}(\xi)t},\end{split}

where the eigenvalues
\lambda_{\pm}
is determined by the following characteristic equation

\begin{split}&\lambda^{2}+(2\mu+\lambda)|\xi|^{2}\lambda+|\xi|^{2}=0.\\
\end{split}

We can explicitly solve for the eigenvalues as

\lambda_{\pm}(\xi)=\frac{-\nu|\xi|^{2}\pm\sqrt{\nu^{2}|\xi|^{4}-4|\xi|^{2}}}{2},

where
\nu=(2\mu+\lambda)
. And the coefficients
A(\xi),B(\xi)
are determined by the initial data:

\begin{cases}A(\xi)+B(\xi)=\hat{\varrho}_{0},\\
\lambda_{-}A(\xi)+\lambda_{+}(\xi)B(\xi)=-i\xi\hat{u}_{0},\end{cases}

which can be solved as

\begin{cases}A(\xi)=\frac{\lambda_{+}\hat{\varrho}+i\xi\hat{u}_{0}}{\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}},\\
B(\xi)=-\frac{\lambda_{-}\hat{\varrho}+i\xi\hat{u}_{0}}{\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}}.\end{cases}

By combining the explicit form of coefficients and eigenvalues, we obtain the following expression for the density:

\begin{split}\hat{\varrho}(t)=&\frac{\lambda_{+}e^{\lambda_{-}t}-\lambda_{-}e^{\lambda_{+}t}}{\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}}\hat{\varrho}_{0}+i\xi\frac{e^{\lambda_{-}t}-e^{\lambda_{+}t}}{\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}}\hat{u}_{0}.\end{split}

(2.3)

Moreover, after the Fourier transform, the linear part of momentum equation becomes

\hat{u}_{t}+i\xi\hat{\varrho}+(\mu|\xi|^{2}I+(\mu+\lambda)\xi\xi^{T})\hat{u}=0.

(2.4)

Since it is a multi-dimensional ODE, we decompose the momentum equation orthogonally with respect to the frequency direction. Denote
\xi=|\xi|\xi^{\prime}
, then we have

\hat{u}=(\hat{u}\cdot\xi^{\prime})\xi^{\prime}+(\hat{u}-(\hat{u}\cdot\xi^{\prime})\xi^{\prime})\coloneqq a\xi^{\prime}+b,a\xi^{\prime}\parallel\xi,b\perp\xi.

The first and third terms are parallel to
\hat{u}
, which justifies the use of the above decomposition. The second and fourth terms are parallel to
\xi
.

\begin{split}i\xi\varrho=i|\xi|\varrho\xi^{\prime},\quad(\mu+\lambda)\xi\xi^{T}\hat{u}=(\mu+\lambda)|\xi|^{2}a\xi^{\prime}.\end{split}

As a result, the multi-dimensional ODE can be decomposed into two scalar ODEs:

\begin{cases}\tilde{\varrho}a_{t}+(2\mu+\lambda)|\xi|^{2}a+i|\xi|\hat{\varrho}=0,\\
\tilde{\varrho}b_{t}+\mu|\xi|^{2}b=0,\\
a_{0}=\hat{u}_{0}\cdot\xi^{\prime},b_{0}=\left(I-\frac{\xi\xi^{T}}{|\xi|^{2}}\right)\hat{u}_{0}.\end{cases}

Using the above results and standard ODE techniques, we can derive that

\begin{cases}a=-i|\xi|\frac{e^{\lambda_{+}t}-e^{\lambda_{-}t}}{\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}}\hat{\varrho}_{0}+\frac{\lambda_{+}e^{\lambda_{+}t}-\lambda_{-}e^{\lambda_{-}t}}{\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}}(u_{0}\cdot\xi^{\prime}),\\
b=e^{-\mu|\xi|^{2}t/\tilde{\varrho}}\left(I-\frac{\xi\xi^{T}}{|\xi|^{2}}\right)\hat{u}_{0}.\end{cases}

The solution of momentum equation (
2.4
) can then be expressed as

\hat{u}=-i\frac{e^{\lambda_{+}t}-e^{\lambda_{-}t}}{\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}}\hat{\varrho}_{0}\xi+\left(e^{-\mu|\xi|^{2}t}I-\left(\frac{\lambda_{+}e^{\lambda_{+}t}-\lambda_{-}e^{\lambda_{-}t}}{\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}}-e^{-\mu|\xi|^{2}t}\right)\xi^{\prime}\xi^{\prime T}\right)\hat{u}_{0}.

(2.5)

Summarizing the above results (
2.3
) and (
2.5
), we derive the explicit form of the Green function in the frequency level.

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose the Green function
G(t,x)
of the linearized Navier–Stokes equations (
1.4
) is given by formula (
2.1
). Then its Fourier transform
\hat{G}(t,\xi)
can be expressed as follows:

\begin{split}\hat{G}(t,\xi)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\lambda_{+}e^{\lambda_{-}t}-\lambda_{-}e^{\lambda^{+}t}}{\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}}&-i\frac{e^{\lambda_{+}t}-e^{\lambda_{-}t}}{\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}}\xi^{T}\\
-i\frac{e^{\lambda_{+}t}-e^{\lambda_{-}t}}{\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}}\xi&\ \ \ e^{-\mu|\xi|^{2}t}(I-\xi^{\prime}\xi^{\prime T})+\frac{\lambda_{+}e^{\lambda_{-}t}-\lambda_{-}e^{\lambda_{+}t}}{\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}}\xi^{\prime}\xi^{\prime T}\end{pmatrix},\end{split}

(2.6)

where the eigenvalues
\lambda_{\pm}
is defined by

\lambda_{\pm}(\xi)=\frac{-\nu|\xi|^{2}\pm\sqrt{\nu^{2}|\xi|^{4}-4|\xi|^{2}}}{2},\nu=(2\mu+\lambda).\\

(2.7)

2.

Frequency analysis of Green function.
To fully exploit the stability mechanism of the linear operator, we consider the low-frequency and high-frequency parts separately.

If
|\xi|\ll 1
, we expand the Green function around
|\xi|=0
. Then the eigenvalues
\lambda_{\pm}
given by (
2.7
) have the following asymptotic expansion:

\begin{split}\lambda_{+}(\xi)=i|\xi|-\frac{\nu}{2}|\xi|^{2}+O(|\xi|^{3}),\lambda_{-}(\xi)=-i|\xi|-\frac{\nu}{2}|\xi|^{2}+O(|\xi|^{3});\\
e^{\lambda_{+}t}(\xi)=e^{i|\xi|t}e^{-\frac{\nu}{2}|\xi|^{2}t}e^{O(|\xi|^{3})t},e^{\lambda_{+}t}(\xi)=e^{-i|\xi|t}e^{-\frac{\nu}{2}|\xi|^{2}t}e^{O(|\xi|^{3})t};\\
\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}(\xi)=2i|\xi|+O(|\xi|^{3}),\frac{1}{\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}}(\xi)=-\frac{i}{2}|\xi|^{-1}+O(|\xi|);\\
\frac{\lambda_{+}}{\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}}(\xi)=\frac{1}{2}+iO(|\xi|),\frac{\lambda_{-}}{\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}}(\xi)=-\frac{1}{2}+iO(|\xi|).\end{split}

Utilizing the above asymptotic analysis and explicit form (
2.6
) of Green function
G(t,x)
, we find that it behaves like the heat kernel in the low-frequency regime.

If
|\xi|\gg 1
, we now expand the Green function around
|\xi|^{-1}\to 0
. Then

\begin{split}\lambda_{+}(\xi)=-1+O(|\xi|^{-2}),\lambda_{-}(\xi)=-|\xi|^{-2}+1+O(|\xi|^{-2});\\
\frac{\lambda_{+}(\xi)}{\lambda_{+}(\xi)-\lambda_{-}(\xi)}e^{\lambda_{+}(\xi)t}=e^{-t}O(|\xi|^{-2});\\
\frac{\lambda_{-}(\xi)}{\lambda_{+}(\xi)-\lambda_{-}(\xi)}e^{\lambda_{+}(\xi)t}=e^{-t}(-1+O(|\xi|^{-2}));\\
\frac{1}{\lambda_{+}(\xi)-\lambda_{-}(\xi)}e^{\lambda_{+}(\xi)t}=e^{-t}O(|\xi|^{-2}).\end{split}

We can see that the singularity occurs in the first term of the Green function:

\begin{split}\frac{\lambda_{+}(\xi)e^{\lambda_{-}(\xi)t}-\lambda_{-}(\xi)e^{\lambda^{+}(\xi)t}}{\lambda_{+}(\xi)-\lambda_{-}(\xi)}\sim{e^{-ct}}-|\xi|^{-2}e^{-c^{\prime}|\xi|^{2}t}.\end{split}

Inspiring by the above analysis, we take decomposition as follows:

G=G_{L}+G_{HR}+G_{HS}.

Here
G_{L}
denotes the low frequency part of the Green function, defined by

G_{L}=\chi(D)G,\quad\chi(\xi)\coloneqq\begin{cases}1,&|\xi|\leq\frac{1}{2},\\
0,&|\xi|>1,\end{cases}

(2.8)

and
G_{HR}
and
G_{HS}
denote the regular and singular parts of the Green function in the high-frequency regime:

G_{HS}=e^{-ct}\delta(x)\begin{pmatrix}1-\chi(D)&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad G_{HR}=(1-\chi(D))G-G_{HS}.

(2.9)

We have following standard estimates for the Green function. More details of the proof can be found in
[
3
,
8
,
16
]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose
G(x,t)
is the Green function of the linearized Navier–Stokes equations (
1.4
), and that
G_{L}
,
G_{HR}
, and
G_{HS}
are defined by (
2.8
) and (
2.9
), respectively.

1.

For any
k\geq 0
and
p\in[1,\infty]
, we have:

\begin{split}\left\lVert D^{k}G_{L}(\cdot,t)\right\rVert_{L^{p}}\leq&C(1+t)^{-\frac{3}{2}(1-\frac{1}{p})-\frac{k}{2}}.\\
\end{split}

(2.10)

2.

For any
k=0,1
and
p\in[1,\infty],
we have:

\left\lVert D^{k}G_{HR}(\cdot,t)\right\rVert_{L^{p}}\leq Ce^{-Ct}.

(2.11)

3.

For any
p\in[1,\infty]
and
f\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, we have:

\left\lVert G_{HS}*f\right\rVert_{L^{p}}\leq Ce^{-Ct}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{p}}.

(2.12)

Remark 2.1
.

Compared to the singular part,
G_{HR}
can bear a first derivative.

3
A priori estimates

This section establishes uniform a priori estimates for the system (
1.1
). Fix
T>0
, and let
(\varrho,u)
be a smooth solution on
\mathbb{R}^{3}\times(0,T]
with initial data
(\varrho_{0},u_{0})
satisfying the prescribed regularity conditions. Below, we derive essential a priori estimates for this solution.

Proposition 3.1
.

Assume that the conditions of Theorem
1.1
hold. There exists a positive constant
\eta
depending on
\lambda,\mu,\gamma
such that if
(\varrho,u)
is a smooth solution to the system (
1.1
) on
\mathbb{R}^{3}\times(0,T]
satisfying

\begin{gathered}\|(\varrho,u)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\frac{1}{2}(1+t)^{-\frac{3}{2}},\quad\|\nabla(\varrho,u)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\eta(1+t)^{-2},\end{gathered}

(3.1)

then the following estimates hold:

\begin{gathered}\|(\varrho,u)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\frac{1}{4}(1+t)^{-\frac{3}{2}},\quad\|\nabla(\varrho,u)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\frac{\eta}{2}(1+t)^{-2}.\end{gathered}

(3.2)

In the following discussion, we will first establish conditional energy estimates, and then use them to close the regularity criterion.

3.1
Conditional energy estimate

In this subsection, under the assumptions of time-decaying regularity criteria and small initial data in
L^{2}
, we derive uniform energy estimates for the solution and its derivatives. To ensure the global existence of smooth solutions, it suffices to establish the following a priori estimates.

The following lemma is necessary to handle the nonlinear terms involving the density:

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume that
\|\varrho\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 1
. Suppose
\varrho\in H^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
and
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth function with bounded derivatives up to order
k\geq 1
, then for any
2\leq p\leq\infty
, the following estimate holds:

\|\nabla^{k}f(\varrho)\|_{L^{p}}\leq C\left\lVert\nabla^{k}\varrho\right\rVert_{L^{p}},

(3.3)

where the constant
C>0
depends on
\|f\|_{C^{k}},p
and
k.

Proof.

Notice that for
k\geq 1
,

\nabla^{k}(f(\varrho))=\text{a sum of products }g^{\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n}}(\varrho)\nabla^{\gamma_{1}}\varrho\cdots\nabla^{\gamma_{n}}\varrho,

where the functions
f^{\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n}}(\varrho)
are some derivatives of
f(\varrho)
and
1\leq\gamma_{i}\leq k
,
i=1,\ldots,n
with
\gamma_{1}+\cdots+\gamma_{n}=k
. We then use the Sobolev interpolation inequality and Hölder inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{k}f(\varrho)\|_{L^{p}}\leq

\displaystyle C\|\nabla^{\gamma_{1}}\varrho\cdot\cdot\cdot\nabla^{\gamma_{n}}\varrho\|_{L^{p}}

(3.4)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\|\nabla^{\gamma_{1}}\varrho\|_{L^{p_{1}}}\cdot\cdot\cdot\|\nabla^{\gamma_{n}}\varrho\|_{L^{p_{j}}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\|\nabla^{k}\varrho\|_{L^{p}}^{\frac{p}{pk-3}\Big((\gamma_{1}+\cdot\cdot\cdot+\gamma_{j})-3(\frac{1}{p_{1}}+\cdot\cdot\cdot+\frac{1}{p_{j}})\Big)}\|\varrho\|^{\zeta}_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\|\nabla^{k}\varrho\|_{L^{p}}\|\varrho\|^{\zeta}_{L^{\infty}},

where
\frac{1}{p_{1}}+\cdot\cdot\cdot+\frac{1}{p_{j}}=\frac{1}{p}
and
\zeta>0
is a positive constant.
Consequently, the lemma holds provided that
\|\varrho\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 1
.
∎

We also state the classical commutator estimate below, which will be used in the higher-order energy estimates.

Lemma 3.2
.

[

,

]

Let

be an integer, then we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{k}(gh)\|_{L^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\leq C\|g\|_{L^{r_{1}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\|\nabla^{k}h\|_{L^{r_{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}+C\|h\|_{L^{r_{3}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\|\nabla^{k}g\|_{L^{r_{4}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})},

(1\leq i\leq 4)

\frac{1}{r_{1}}+\frac{1}{r_{2}}=\frac{1}{r_{3}}+\frac{1}{r_{4}}=\frac{1}{r}.

Let
k\geq 1
be an integer and define the commutator

\displaystyle[\nabla^{k},f]g\overset{\text{def}}{=}\nabla^{k}(fg)-f\nabla^{k}g,

(3.6)

Then we have

\displaystyle\|[\nabla^{k},f]g\|_{L^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\leq C\|\nabla g\|_{L^{r_{1}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\|\nabla^{k-1}h\|_{L^{r_{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}+C\|h\|_{L^{r_{3}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\|\nabla^{k}g\|_{L^{r_{4}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})},

(3.7)

with

\frac{1}{r_{1}}+\frac{1}{r_{2}}=\frac{1}{r_{3}}+\frac{1}{r_{4}}=\frac{1}{r}.

Lemma 3.3
.

Under the assumption (

Lemma 3.4

),

\begin{split}\left\lVert\nabla^{k}G(t)*V_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq&\left\lVert\nabla^{k}G_{L}(t)*V_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert\nabla^{k}G_{HR}(t)*V_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert\nabla^{k}G_{HS}(t)*V_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\\
\leq&C\left\lVert\nabla^{k}G_{L}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\left\lVert V_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{1}}+C\left\lVert\nabla G_{HR}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\left\lVert\nabla^{k-1}V_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+Ce^{-Ct}\left\lVert\nabla^{k}V_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\\
\leq&C(1+t)^{-\frac{3}{4}-\frac{k}{2}}+Ce^{-Ct}+Ce^{-Ct}\leq C(1+t)^{-\frac{3}{4}-\frac{k}{2}}.\end{split}

(4.4)

The
L_{2}
can be splited into the following two term:

\displaystyle L_{2}\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\frac{t}{2}}\|\nabla^{k}G_{L}(t-s)\|_{L^{2}}\|N(V)(s)\|_{L^{1}}ds+\int_{\frac{t}{2}}^{t}\|\nabla G_{L}(t-s)\|_{L^{1}}\|\nabla^{k-1}N(V)(s)\|_{L^{2}}ds

(4.5)

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle L_{21}+L_{22}.

For
L_{21}
, we have

\displaystyle L_{21}\leq

\displaystyle C\int_{0}^{\frac{t}{2}}\|\nabla^{k}G_{L}(t-s)\|_{L^{2}}\|N(V)(s)\|_{L^{1}}ds

(4.6)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int_{0}^{\frac{t}{2}}(1+t-s)^{-\frac{3}{4}-\frac{k}{2}}(1+s)^{-\alpha_{1}}ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(1+t)^{-\frac{3}{4}-\frac{k}{2}},

where
\alpha_{1}>1
is ensured by Section
3.2
.
The term
L_{22}
requires careful analysis and will be discussed in detail using (
3.40
), (
3.34
), (
3.35
), (
3.43
), (
3.49
):

\displaystyle L_{22}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int_{\frac{t}{2}}^{t}\|\nabla G_{L}(t-s)\|_{L^{1}}\||\nabla^{k-1}N_{\varrho}|+|\nabla^{k-1}N_{1}|+|\nabla^{k-1}N_{2}|+|\nabla^{k-1}N_{3}|)(s)\|_{L^{2}}ds

(4.7)

\displaystyle+C\int_{\frac{t}{2}}^{t}\|\nabla G_{L}(t-s)\|_{L^{\frac{4}{3}}}\|\nabla^{k-1}N_{4}(s)\|_{L^{\frac{4}{3}}}ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int_{\frac{t}{2}}^{t}(1+t-s)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\|V\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla^{k}V\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle+\|\nabla V\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla^{k-1}V\|_{L^{2}}+\|\nabla V\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla^{k}V\|_{L^{2}}+\|\nabla V\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla^{k-1}V\|_{L^{2}}\Big)(s)ds

\displaystyle+\int_{\frac{t}{2}}^{t}(1+t-s)^{-\frac{7}{8}}\Big(\|\varrho\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla^{k+1}u\|_{L^{\frac{4}{3}}}+\|\nabla^{k-1}\varrho\|_{L^{2}}\|\nabla^{2}u\|_{L^{4}}\Big)ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(1+t)^{-\frac{3}{4}-\frac{k}{2}},

where we have used

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{3}u\|_{L^{\frac{4}{3}}}\leq

\displaystyle C(1+t)^{-\frac{21}{20}}.

(4.8)

Regarding high-frequency nonlinear estimates, we obtain

\displaystyle L_{3}\leq

\displaystyle C\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla^{k}(G_{HR}(t-s)*N(V)(s))\|_{L^{2}}ds

(4.9)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla G_{HR}(t-s)\|_{L^{1}}\|\nabla^{k-1}N(V)(s)\|_{L^{2}}ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int_{0}^{t}e^{-C(t-s)}\Big(\|V\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla^{k}V\|_{L^{2}}+\|\nabla V\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla^{k-1}V\|_{L^{2}}+\|\nabla V\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla^{k}V\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle+\|\varrho\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla^{k+1}u\|_{L^{2}}+\|\nabla^{2}u\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla^{k-1}\varrho\|_{L^{2}}\Big)(s)ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(1+t)^{-\frac{3}{4}-\frac{k}{2}},

where we have used (
3.56
),(
3.34
), (
3.35
), (
3.43
) and

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{3}V\|_{L^{2}}\leq

\displaystyle C\|\nabla^{4}V\|^{\frac{2}{5}}_{L^{2}}\|V\|^{\frac{3}{5}}_{L^{\infty}}\leq C(1+t)^{-\frac{9}{10}}.

(4.10)

We establish the following nonlinear bound for the singular part

\displaystyle L_{4}\leq

\displaystyle C\int_{0}^{t}\|G_{HS}(t-s)*\nabla^{k}\text{div}(\varrho u)(s)\|_{L^{2}}ds

(4.11)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int_{0}^{t}e^{-C(t-s)}\|\nabla^{k}\text{div}(\varrho u)(s)\|_{L^{2}}ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int_{0}^{t}e^{-c(t-s)}\Big((\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\varrho\|_{L^{\infty}})(\|\nabla^{k+1}\varrho\|_{L^{2}}+\|\nabla^{k+1}u\|_{L^{2}})

\displaystyle+(\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\nabla\varrho\|_{L^{\infty}})(\|\nabla^{k}\varrho\|_{L^{2}}+\|\nabla^{k}u\|_{L^{2}})\Big)(s)ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int_{0}^{t}e^{-C(t-s)}\Big(\|V\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla^{k+1}V\|_{L^{2}}+\|\nabla V\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla^{k}V\|_{L^{2}}\Big)(s)ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(1+t)^{-\frac{3}{4}-\frac{k}{2}},

where we have used (
3.34
), (
3.35
), (
4.10
), (
3.40
), (
3.49
).

Putting together (
4.4
) -(
4.11
) yields

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{k}V\|_{L^{2}}\leq C(1+t)^{-\frac{3}{4}-\frac{k}{2}}.

(4.12)

Thus, we have completed the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Remark 4.1
.

When handling the singular terms, we need to distribute all derivatives to the nonlinear terms. As a result, we can only obtain the optimal decay for
\|\nabla^{k}u\|_{L^{2}}
in this setting.
