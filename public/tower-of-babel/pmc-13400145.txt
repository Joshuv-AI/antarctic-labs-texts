HIV tri-specific killer engagers directly enhance NK cell function and safely expand NK cells in vivo
Authors: Ross T Cromarty, Julien A Clain, Yvette Soigner, Tumpa Dasgupta, Kate Dixon, Kevin Nguyen, Hannah Flores, Emma Eungard, Joshua Walker, Josh Bjorgen, Martin Felices, Justin Harper, Timothy W Schacker, Zachary Davis, Mirko Paiardini, Jeffrey S Miller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAn HIV cure remains elusive, and many individuals develop comorbidities; therefore, new synergistic strategies to combat HIV are needed. Enhanced natural killer (NK) cell function associates with HIV elite control, primarily through antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC). We generated tri-specific killer engagers (TriKEs) to enhance NK cell responses against HIV-infected cells. HIV TriKEs, one using the VRC01/07 antibody domains (VRC TriKE) and one using the CD4 ectodomain 1 (CD4 TriKE), promote NK cell function and killing of HIV target cells. NK cells expressing both CD38 and CD57 are major mediators of this function. We performed a dose-escalation study in SIV-uninfected rhesus macaques to assess the safety and pharmacodynamics of our CD4 TriKE. The TriKE is safe and effective at expanding peripheral and lymphoid NK cells in vivo. These findings support TriKEs as a promising safe immunotherapy to recognize and kill HIV-infected cells, with potential for combination cure strategies.Keywords: HIV, immunotherapy, NK cells, ADCC, tri-specific killer engagers

Introduction

HIV remains a major health burden despite the advent and improvement of antiretroviral therapy (ART) for successful viral control. Compared to people without HIV (PWOH), people with HIV (PWH) on long-term ART have an increased incidence of developing comorbidities related to continued inflammation and immune activation,1 highlighting the need for additional therapies to target persistent HIV reservoirs. Elite controllers naturally control HIV replication without ART, highlighting the potential of the immune system to combat HIV,2,3,4 making immunotherapeutic approaches an attractive modality choice.

Natural killer (NK) cells are a unique component of the immune system, with both supportive5,6 and cytotoxic effector7,8,9 functions, allowing them to have a dual beneficial impact. Despite HIV-mediated immune evasion mechanisms,10 NK cells still recognize and kill HIV-infected cells.11,12,13,14 Antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), an important mechanism of HIV control,15,16,17,18,19,20,21 can overcome HIV immune evasion.22,23,24 Broadly neutralizing antibodies (bNAbs) can mediate viral control25 and are both treatment and prevention strategies.26,27 However, despite effective neutralization, most bNAbs exhibit poor ADCC28,29,30 and are susceptible to viral escape.31,32,33 All HIV variants must utilize the CD4 receptor for initial gp120 binding to the cell surface.34 Previous studies identified the CD4 ectodomain 1 molecule as a promising engager, with potent HIV neutralization and minimal MHC class II interaction.35,36,37,38 IL-15 is an important cytokine that enhances both NK and T cell function.39,40,41 Although HIV infection impairs NK cell mitochondrial function,42 IL-15 reprogrammed this function and enhanced activity through CD16,43 highlighting the potential synergy of IL-15 stimulation and NK cell ADCC function mediated through CD16.

We employed the tri-specific killer engager (TriKE) construct, currently being investigated for cancer,44,45,46 to target HIV. TriKEs (Figures 1A–1C) consist of (1) an anti-CD16 molecule that binds CD16 at higher affinity than conventional antibodies,47 (2) an antigen-engaging molecule to target the antigen of interest, which in this case is the gp120 protein of the HIV envelope, and (3) an IL-15 molecule between binding domains, which enhances NK cell survival and function.48 We generated two human HIV-specific TriKEs, one based on the VRC01 and VRC07 bNAbs, termed the VRC TriKE (Figure 1A), and another utilizing the ectodomain 1 of CD4, termed the CD4 TriKE (Figure 1B), and assessed their ability to direct and mediate NK cell function against HIV-infected target cells.

Results

HIV TriKEs bind to both NK cells and HIV target cells

We first evaluated TriKE binding to both NK cells and HIV target cells. TriKEs are designed with a 10× His tag,49 allowing identification of cell-bound TriKEs by flow cytometry with an anti-His antibody. We found comparable levels of CD16 expression (∼90%) and TriKE binding (∼90%) to NK cells in both PWOH and PWH (Figures 1D and S1), suggesting that all NK cells that expressed CD16 also bound TriKEs. We found similar binding between the VRC and CD4 TriKEs (Figures 1D and S1). Three target cell lines were used: an HIV-uninfected CD4 T cell line, A3.01 (Figures S2A, S2E, and S2I); a productively infected A3.01 line termed 8E5 (Figures S2B, S2F, and S2J); and a latently infected A3.01 line ACH-2 (Figures S2C, S2G, and S2K), which can be reactivated with PMA (phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate) (Figures S2D, S2H, and S2L).50 We found specific TriKE binding to the 8E5 (VRC: 8.87%, CD4: 15.2%) and reactivated ACH-2 (VRC: 5.82%, CD4: 38.6%) cells, with minimal binding to unstimulated ACH-2 cells (VRC: 0.89%, CD4: 2.5%) and no binding to the uninfected A3.01 cells, indicating a lack of surface expression of the HIV envelope. These results show successful engagement of our TriKEs with both NK cells and target cells expressing the HIV envelope.

The CD4 TriKE mediates enhanced function of NK cells against HIV target cells

Initial development of the CD4 TriKE, used at a concentration of 60 nM with enriched NK cells and unstimulated or PMA-activated A3.01 and ACH-2 cells, showed significant NK cell function, with increased CD107a (p < 0.0001, Figure S4A), IFN-γ (p < 0.0001, Figure S4B), and TNF-α expression (p < 0.0001, Figure S4C) compared to conditions without TriKE. Significant killing (70%) of reactivated ACH-2 cells was observed (p = 0.0023) compared to the control without TriKE (Figure S4D). Similarly increased NK cell responses were observed when comparing the PMA-stimulated ACH-2 condition to the unstimulated ACH-2 and PMA-stimulated A3.01 conditions (Figures S4A–S4D), highlighting that reactivation of these latent cells leads to better TriKE-mediated NK cell function. We next titrated both TriKEs and identified an effective concentration of 10 nM (Figure S4E) to use in subsequent assays utilizing whole peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) as a more biologically representative mixed-cellular model encountered in vivo.

HIV-targeting TriKEs induce specific proliferation and activation of NK cells

To assess the proliferative and activating potential of TriKEs on immune cell populations within PBMCs from PWOH and PWH, we co-cultured CellTrace-labeled PBMCs with 10 nM of IL-15 and VRC or CD4 TriKEs for 1 week. We focused on CD56+CD3− NK cells, as well as CD4 and CD8 T cells. Both TriKEs induced proliferation of NK cells (Figure 1E) and activated NK cells with increased HLA-DR (Figure 1G) and CD69 (Figure 1H) expression. More pronounced responses were observed in PBMCs from PWH than in those from PWOH. IL-15 can also activate CD8 T cells.51 Consistent with this, IL-15, but not TriKE treatment, resulted in a ≥2-fold increase in CD8 T cell proliferation (Figure S5A). Furthermore, only IL-15 increased CD8 T cell expression of CD38 (Figure S5B), HLA-DR (Figure S5C), and CD69 (Figure S5D). Similar but less pronounced results were observed with CD4 T cells (Figures S5F–S5J). These results highlight that TriKEs preferentially deliver IL-15 to NK cells, promoting their proliferation and activation, while exerting less direct impact on T cell populations.

HIV TriKEs mediate enhanced NK cell function against HIV-infected targets

To assess TriKE-mediated NK cell function against HIV-infected target cells, we used PBMCs from PWOH and PWH and compared NK cell function to the IL-15+VRC01 bNab control. We found that both PWH (Figure 2A) and PWOH (Figure 2B) exhibited ≥2-fold increased NK cell degranulation with both the VRC and CD4 TriKEs compared to IL-15+bNab, with the highest magnitude of response against the productively infected 8E5 targets (PWOH, VRC: p = 0.0096, CD4: p = 0.0015; PWH, VRC: p = 0.0027, CD4: p = 0.0107). Similarly increased degranulation was observed against the latently infected ACH-2 targets, showing induction of NK cell function even with little TriKE engagement (Figures S2C, S2G, and S2K). To assess the specificity of our TriKEs toward HIV-infected targets, we compared NK cell degranulation in infected target cell conditions to the uninfected A3.01 control. The CD4 TriKE significantly increased NK cell degranulation against both HIV-infected target cells with PBMCs from PWOH (Figure 2A; 8E5: p = 0.0079, ACH-2: p = 0.021) and PWH (Figure 2B; 8E5: p = 0.046, ACH-2: p = 0.029).

NK cells also exert effector functions through the production of cytokines IFN-γ and TNF-α, which further modulate the immune response.52,53 PBMCs from PWOH (Figure 2C) and PWH (Figure 2D) showed significantly increased NK cell IFN-γ production against the productively infected 8E5 targets with the VRC TriKE (PWOH: 8E5: p = 0.0055; PWH: p = 0.0224) and CD4 TriKE (PWOH: 8E5: p = 0.0045) compared to IL-15+bNab. Similar findings were observed against the latently infected ACH-2 targets (Figures 2C and 2D). TNF-α production by NK cells from PWOH (Figure 2E) but not PWH (Figure 2F) had a significant ≥2-fold increase with the CD4 TriKE against the productively HIV-infected 8E5 (p = 0.0046) and the latently HIV-infected ACH-2 (p = 0.0026) targets compared to the IL-15+bNab control. Compared to the uninfected A3.01 controls (Figures 2E and 2F), the CD4 TriKE induced significant TNF-α production against the 8E5 targets (PWOH: p = 0.022; PWH: p = 0.032). Comparable results were observed against the latently infected ACH-2 targets.

We next assessed whether this increased function led to target cell killing (Figures 2G and 2H). Target cells were CellTrace labeled, and target cell viability was evaluated by measuring the proportion of live cells within the CellTrace-labeled population. With PBMCs from PWOH (Figure 2G), the VRC TriKE mediated significant killing of the productively infected 8E5 targets compared to the uninfected A3.01 controls (∼10%, p = 0.028). Similarly, the CD4 TriKE mediated killing of both 8E5 (∼15%, p = 0.006) and ACH-2 (∼15%, p = 0.036) cells compared to the uninfected A3.01 control (Figure 2G). PBMCs from PWH displayed similar findings (Figure 2H), with significant killing of both HIV-infected targets by the VRC TriKE (8E5: ∼10%, p = 0.021, ACH-2: ∼12%, p = 0.0011) and the CD4 TriKE (8E5: ∼11%, p = 0.0034; ACH-2: ∼12%, p = 0.0039) compared to the uninfected A3.01 control (Figure 2H).

To confirm that increased NK cell functionality was associated with killing of target cells, we performed simple linear regression analysis to assess the relationship between NK cell function and target cell viability. We found, with PWOH PBMCs, that all three markers of NK cell function, CD107a (p ≤ 0.0001), IFN-γ (p < 0.0025), and TNF-α (p < 0.0084), significantly negatively associated with target cell viability, indicating that enhanced function leads to reduced viability (Figures S6A–S6C). With PBMCs from PWH, only NK cell degranulation (CD107a, p < 0.026) was significantly negatively associated with target cell viability (Figures S6D–S6F). These results show that in a mixed-cell population, as encountered in vivo, HIV-targeting TriKEs can successfully mediate NK cell function, cytokine production, and killing of HIV-infected target cells.

HIV-targeting TriKEs mediate enhanced polyfunctional responses in CD38- and CD57-expressing NK cells

We next sought to understand whether CD38 and CD57 activation markers were co-expressed with functional markers on NK cells (Figure 3). CD38 acts as an activation and metabolic marker,54 while activation through CD38 has been associated with cytotoxic functions.55,56 CD57 is a maturation marker,57 and CD57+ NK cells displayed enhanced ADCC function against HIV.58,59 Therefore, to assess TriKE-mediated responses within NK cells expressing either or both CD38 and CD57, we performed Simplified Presentation of Incredibly Complex Evaluations (SPICE) analysis60 to create Figure 3 (8E5), Figures S7, S8, and S9 (PB alone, A3.01, and ACH-2), and Tables S2, S3, S4, and S5. Each slice of the pie represents a different NK phenotype based on the expression of CD38, CD57, CD107a, IFN-γ, and TNF-α, which are shown by the colored radial arcs surrounding each pie slice.60 We found, with PBMCs from PWOH against productively infected 8E5 targets, that both HIV TriKEs significantly increased the polyfunctional response of NK cells that were CD38+CD57+ compared to the IL-15+bNab control (Figures 3D and 3E; Table S2). PBMC NK cells from PWH displayed similar but more pronounced phenotypic changes in response to both HIV TriKEs, with increased function within CD38+CD57+ NK cells compared to the IL-15+bNab control (Figures 3I and 3J; Table S2). We found comparable results against the latently infected ACH-2 cells (Figure S9), with CD38+CD57+ NK cells displaying the greatest increase in function. These analyses indicate that TriKE therapy potentiates NK cell function primarily within NK cells that express both CD38 and CD57 (Figures 3K–3N).

Recombinant human IL-15 has greater biological activity than TriKE-contained IL-15

To assess the relative biological activity of the recombinant human (rH) IL-15 and TriKE-contained IL-15, we utilized the HEK-Blue IL-2/IL-15 reporter system. We ran each TriKE and respective IL-15 control on a separate plate due to space limitations. For the VRC TriKE plate, we found that the EC50 was achieved at 0.008 nM for the rH IL-15 and 0.244 nM for the VRC TriKE, indicating that the biological activity of the rH IL-15 was 28.2 times higher than that of the VRC TriKE (Figure S10A). In the CD4 TriKE plate, the EC50 was achieved at 0.01 nM for the rH IL-15 and 0.096 nM for the CD4 TriKE, indicating that the biological activity of the rH IL-15 was 8.6 times higher than that of the CD4 TriKE (Figure S10B) in this HEK-Blue cellular model. At the 10 nM concentration, the biological activity of the rH IL-15 was 0.95 and 0.94 times higher than the IL-15 contained in the VRC and CD4 TriKEs, respectively. These results highlight that the rH IL-15 had greater biological activity than the TriKE-contained IL-15.

The 3G8-CD4 TriKE is safe and well tolerated in rhesus macaques

For translational testing in non-human primate (NHP) models, we “rhesusized” the TriKE platform by substituting the anti-CD16 scFv clone 3G8 (Figure 1C), which can engage rhesus macaque (RM) CD16.61 We focused on the CD4 TriKE as the primary translational modality due to the potential of immune escape described with VRC01 bNAbs.33 The 3G8-CD4 TriKE bound to NK cells with lower occupancy than the cam16-CD4 TriKE (Figure S11A) but still induced comparable NK cell degranulation against PMA-stimulated ACH-2 cells (Figure S11B). To evaluate the safety and tolerability of the CD4 RM TriKE, three SIV-uninfected RMs received subcutaneous injections five times per week for 3 weeks. The dosing followed a stepwise escalation, starting at 100 μg/kg/day (week 1), then 200 μg/kg/day (week 2), and finally 300 μg/kg/day (week 3) (Figure 4A). Animals maintained stable weights throughout the study (Figure S12A). White and red blood cell counts remained stable and within normal reference ranges (Figures S12B and S12C). We also monitored organ-specific serum chemistry parameters, including aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and total protein. Total protein levels remained stable and within normal ranges, showing no evidence of vascular leak (Figure S12D), and hepatocellular enzymes remained within normal limits (Figures S12E and S12F). These data show that even at the highest TriKE doses tested, therapy was well tolerated and showed no organ toxicity in healthy RMs.

The 3G8-CD4 TriKE potently induces NK cell expansion, proliferation, and activation in blood and lymph nodes of RMs

We next assessed the in vivo pharmacodynamic impact by performing flow cytometric analysis of whole blood and lymph node (LN) cells. As previously described in NHP models,62,63 NK cells are defined as CD8+NKG2A+ cells within live CD3−CD20− cells, as illustrated by the gating strategy in Figure S13. In tissues, NK cells can have variable CD8 expression64; however, we did not observe a significant proportion of NK cells that lacked CD8 in this study and thus excluded these from our analyses. In peripheral blood, NK cell frequencies and fold change from pre-treatment decreased 6 h after the first dose of the administration cycle (Figure 4C). Following this initial drop in NK cells, an increased proportion of NK cells was observed at days 7, 14, and 22 (Figure 4C). In the LNs, a variable expansion of the NK cell population was observed (Figure 4D), with one animal responding particularly well, as seen by the fold change increase (Figure S14B). This expansion peaked at day 11, coinciding with the final administration of the second dosing cycle (200 μg/kg/day) (Figures 4B, top; and 4D). These changes were also reflected in an increase in absolute NK cell numbers in two of three animals (Figure S14C). These findings suggest that TriKE administration dynamically modulates NK cell distribution in both blood and lymphoid tissues, reflecting a potent and temporally coordinated in vivo response.

To further characterize the in vivo effects of TriKE on NK cell function, we assessed activation through HLA-DR and CD69 co-expression (HLA-DR+CD69+) and proliferation via Ki-67 expression across time points (Figures 4E–4H). Peripheral NK cell proliferation increased sharply after the intervention (Figure 4E), peaking between days 7 and 14 before gradually declining by day 32. Activation of peripheral NK cells was modest, with transient elevation following the intervention (Figure 4G). In LNs, the frequencies of NK cells expressing Ki-67 (Figure 4F) and HLA-DR+CD69+ (Figure 4H) increased markedly during the first two dosing cycles, reaching a peak at day 11 (Figure 4B, bottom 4) and returned to near-baseline levels by day 32.

To evaluate NK cell cytolytic potential, we monitored granzyme B expression. In peripheral NK cells, granzyme B remained consistently high throughout therapy (Figure S15A). However, in LNs, a modest increase in granzyme B-expressing NK cells was observed, peaking at day 11 (Figure S15B). Although PD-1 is more commonly studied on T cells, its expression on NK cells has been associated with activation-induced regulatory mechanisms.65,66 Notably, PD-1+ NK cells increased by day 4 in both blood and LNs, coinciding with the end of the initial 100 μg/kg/day dosing week (Figures S15C and S15D), but returned to baseline by day 11. In blood, a second more modest increase in PD-1+ NK cells was observed during the third dosing week (Figure S15C). These findings show that, in vivo, the 3G8-CD4 TriKE effectively promotes blood and LN NK cell expansion, proliferation, activation, and cytolytic potential.

The 3G8-CD4 TriKE transiently enhances proliferation and activation of memory CD8 and CD4 T cells in RMs

To determine the TriKE impact on T cells, we assessed CD8 and CD4 T cell proliferation and activation of total (Figure S16) and memory (Figure S17) populations from blood and LNs. We found little impact of TriKE treatment on circulating numbers of CD4 and CD8 T cells (Figures S16A and S16B) but did observe increased Ki-67 expression in CD8 and, less so, in CD4 T cells, in both compartments (Figures S16C–S16F). Similarly, TriKE treatment increased the frequency of Ki-67+ memory CD8 and CD4 T cells, with the greatest response observed during week 2 in both blood and LNs (Figures S17B, S17C, and S17F). The proliferative response was more pronounced in memory CD8 T cells than in memory CD4 T cells in both compartments, consistent with other studies.51 In contrast, HLA-DR+CD69+ expression in memory T cells showed a more modest and heterogeneous pattern over time (Figures S17D, S17E, and S17G), with transient increases in memory CD8 T cells at day 11. As observed in NK cells, both Ki-67 and HLA-DR+CD69+ expression declined after day 11 (Figures S17F and S17G). Together, these findings suggest that TriKE treatment elicits a transient burst of memory T cell proliferation and activation.

Discussion

We demonstrate that two HIV-targeting TriKEs, a VRC TriKE utilizing VRC01 and VRC07 binding domains and a CD4 TriKE utilizing the CD4 ectodomain 1 molecule, direct and mediate NK cell function. We found significant NK cell degranulation (CD107a) and cytokine (IFN-γ and TNF-α) production mediated by TriKEs when co-cultured with both productively and latently HIV-infected cell lines. TriKEs also induced preferential proliferation and activation of NK cells, with less impact on T cells. These results are consistent with targeted TriKE observations from our lab in the cancer setting.44,45,46,49,67,68,69,70,71,72,73,74 Our data also revealed that NK cells expressing both CD38 and CD57 exhibited the best TriKE-mediated polyfunctional responses. Tomescu et al. found that CD57+ NK cells were the main subset that mediated bNAb-induced ADCC in PWH,59 consistent with our results. Many preclinical and clinical trials show that ADCC responses are associated with lower viral loads18,75,76,77,78 and infection prevention,79,80 highlighting the beneficial role of effective ADCC responses in combating HIV.

Despite the promise of bNAb therapy, complications remain, such as poor Fc-effector functions, which hinder successful ADCC responses,28,29,30 and potential viral escape, which diminishes the continued effectiveness of bNAbs.31,32,33,81,82,83 However, all HIV variants utilize the CD4 receptor for binding and infection of CD4 T cells,34 and thus it represents a promising antigen engager to use that should overcome issues with antigen escape. Small CD4 mimetics have been of interest as they increase the susceptibility of HIV-infected cells to ADCC,84,85 likely by stabilizing the HIV envelope on the cell surface in its open conformation.86,87,88 A previous study utilized this approach by generating a CD4−CD16 bi-specific NK cell engager without IL-15, which successfully induced NK cell degranulation and killed HIV target cells,89 similar to our results. However, although CD16 ligation can prime NK cells to cytokine-mediated proliferation, alone it does not induce NK cell proliferation.90 Our rhesusized CD4 TriKE was well tolerated in RMs, with no adverse safety concerns. TriKE treatment induced robust in vivo NK cell expansion and activation in both peripheral and lymphoid tissue, similar to other IL-15 therapies.91,92,93,94 We observed an expansion of NK cells after the weekend rest period (days 4–7 and days 11–14), suggesting a lingering impact of TriKE on NK cells despite a break in therapy. These results are consistent with an early phase 1 clinical trial testing a CD33-targeting TriKE in acute myeloid leukemia (NCT03214666). This trial demonstrated safety across all 6 dose cohorts (5–150 μg/kg), robust NK cell activation and expansion in the blood and bone marrow,91 and consistency of TriKE response between preclinical and clinical models. We also observed a progressive increase in NK cells expressing granzyme B within LNs, suggesting enhanced cytotoxic potential of NK cells at the primary site of HIV reservoirs.95,96 Whether this reflects increased granzyme B expression in resident NK cells or infiltration of peripheral blood NK cells remains to be determined. NK cells that express CXCR5, a lymphoid homing marker, have also been shown to express PD-1 in the periphery97 and LNs.98 While we did not assess chemokine receptors in this study, we did observe an early increase in PD-1 expression in both compartments, possibly indicating that TriKE therapy may increase the homing capacity of NK cells; however, this needs to be further explored. Our findings align with preclinical and clinical trials of IL-15 modalities, which are of major interest in combating HIV.39 In African green monkeys, NK cell migration into LN follicles, containing high IL-15 levels, was associated with control of SIV replication, while anti-IL15 therapy reversed these effects.99 In SIV-infected RMs, N-803 expanded peripheral NK cells, similar to our results, and decreased viral loads.100 In PWH, N-803 alone induced activation and proliferation of NK cells and reductions in measures of HIV-infected cells in both the periphery92 and LNs93 while combining N-803 with haploidentical NK cell therapy decreased vRNA+ cells in LNs.94 Collectively, these data highlight the role of IL-15-induced NK cell responses in helping to control and clear HIV reservoirs. TriKEs, while providing NK cell activation and proliferation responses similar to other IL-15 modalities, have the added benefit of engaging CD16 and the HIV envelope, thereby creating an immunological synapse to specifically promote an ADCC response against HIV-infected cells.

Importantly for translation purposes, TriKEs were shown to overcome cancer-induced immune suppression67 and hypoxia within the tumor environment,74,101 both of which are relevant to lymphoid compartments.102,103 Combinations of different immunotherapies are likely to be the most successful strategy to combat HIV, and TriKEs can be combined with other modalities such as latency-reversal agents,104 immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy,105 and adoptive NK transfer strategies.94 This study provides evidence for the use of TriKEs to target HIV-infected cells. These results highlight that TriKEs enhance specific NK cell function against productively and latently HIV-infected cells, leading to killing of these targets. Our in vivo study highlights the potential translation of this platform, with no adverse events noted, and strong expansion and activation of the NK cell compartment. Together, these results highlight the TriKE as a potential component of future combination HIV cure strategies.

Limitations of the study

This study should be interpreted within its limitations. We used cell lines as targets for the in vitro testing for consistency of these models. However, these cell lines are susceptible to NK cell killing by natural cytotoxicity due to their immortalized nature and thus may limit definitive extrapolation to infection of primary targets, a caveat of this study. Future follow-up studies need to be performed that employ autologous ex vivo infection models, using primary PBMCs and clinical HIV isolates that are either bNab sensitive or resistant to further clarify the specificity of TriKEs in mediating ADCC NK cell killing of primary reservoir targets. These types of autologous ex vivo infection assays and infected large animal studies are important and necessary before moving the HIV TriKE into further clinical translation. Further, we used an equimolar concentration for our IL-15 controls, while the biological activity of the rH IL-15 was higher than that of the TriKE-contained IL-15. This likely also contributes to the NK cell function and particularly killing of target cells, as these are also susceptible to natural cytotoxicity in the IL-15 and IL-15+bNab controls. We did not include measurements of CD16 in the functional and proliferation experiments due to potential competitive binding to CD16. Future studies could include baseline measurements of CD16 to assess the relationship between baseline CD16 expression and TriKE-mediated NK cell function. We assessed NK cell function by the induction of degranulation (CD107a) and cytokine production (IFN-γ and TNF-α); however, these are not direct measurements of ADCC. NK cells are also known to mediate killing by other mechanisms such as induction of apoptosis.8 Measurements of these mechanisms should be included in future studies diving deeper into TriKE-mediated mechanisms. We did not assess CMV serostatus of donors and related adaptive NK cell populations that expand in response to CMV. These adaptive NK cells are known to have enhanced cytotoxic functions.106 Future studies should include measurement of these unique and important cells. Further, our initial in vivo study included uninfected RMs, and while the results were promising, future studies in SIV/SHIV-infected RMs are required to confirm these findings in the context of infection and to assess anti-viral efficacy. We performed daily subcutaneous dosing throughout the week based on an early clinical trial (NCT03214666). Daily dosing is not optimal in the clinical setting of HIV cure. We did, however, observe an impact of TriKE over the weekend rest period (days 4–7 and days 11–14), suggesting a lingering biologic impact of the TriKEs in vivo. We also observed a waning effect of TriKE therapy, particularly during the 3rd and highest dose. This could be due to daily dosing and escalating TriKE therapy, leading to NK cell exhaustion and less responsiveness to IL-15 signaling by TriKEs.107 Another possibility for this effect may be the induction of anti-drug antibodies against the TriKE molecule at these later time points. The occurrence of NK exhaustion, development of anti-drug antibodies, and optimization of dosing regimens need further exploration in future efficacy studies. We did not include analyses to determine the influence of sex or gender in this study. Future studies should include donors and animals to investigate the influence of sex and gender on the observed outcomes.

Method details

TriKE plasmid design and protein production

The VRC TriKE contains the light variable and conserved sequences of VRC01 and the heavy variable and conserved regions of VRC07 bNAbs,108 which together form the antibody fragment (Fab) antigen targeting domain of the VRC TriKE. The CD4 TriKE contains the ectodomain 1 of the CD4 molecule which has minimal binding to MHC class 2, and strong binding to HIV gp-120 Envelope.38 Each targeting domain sequence was synthesized into the backbone of a TriKE expression plasmid (Azenta Life Sciences) to generate fusion proteins consisting of an anti-CD16 engager, an IL-15 moiety, a C-terminal antigen targeting domain, and a 10x His tag, as previously described.49 Further, for the Rhesus CD4 TriKE, we utilized the anti-CD16 clone 3G8, which can cross react with rhesus CD16,61 albeit at a lower occupancy than the camelid nanobody, which is unable to bind rhesus CD16. Human IL-15 is cross-reactive with Rhesus Macaques,109 and so we retained the human IL-15 in these Rhesusized TriKEs. TriKE expression plasmids were transfected into Expi293F cells (Gibco, Cat# A14527) using Expifectamine transfection reagent (Gibco, Cat# A14524) and the protein was purified by immobilized metal ion affinity column using the ÄKTA Avant 150 chromatography system (Cytiva, Cat# 28976337). The 10x His-tag at the C-terminus of the TriKE molecule allows for protein purification His-Trap excel columns (Cytiva, Cat# 17371206) and downstream detection via flow cytometry staining with PE anti-His antibody (Biolegend, Cat#362603). Desalting steps were carried out using the HiPrep 26/10 Desalting kit (Cytiva, Cat# 17508702). The purity of the TriKE protein was validated using SDS PAGE gel stained with One-Step Blue (Biotium, Cat# 21003-1L), and protein was quantified using Qubit Protein Assay kit (Invitrogen, Cat# Q33211).

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells and NK cell isolation

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) were isolated from Leukopaks for PWOH (n = 5) and EDTA tubes for PWH (n = 5), using density-gradient centrifugation with Ficoll-Paque Premium (GE Healthcare, Cat. No. GE17-5442-03). NK cells were enriched from PBMCs using negative selection magnetic beads from EasySep Human NK Cell Enrichment kit (STEMCELL Technologies, Cat. No. 19055). Cryopreserved PBMCs were thawed and rested overnight at 37°C. Enriched NK cells were isolated from thawed PBMC stocks and rested overnight at 37°C prior to use.

Media and cell culture

We used two medias throughout this study which were sterile filtered through 0.22 μm steritop filters (Millipore, SCGPS05RE). Standard R10, composed of RPMI 1640 (Corning, Cat#10-041-CV), 10% fetal bovine serum (Cytiva, Cat#SH30088.03HI) and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin (Gibco, Cat#15140-122). We further added 1% non-essential ammino acids (ThermoFischer, Cat#11140050) and 1% sodium pyruvate (ThermoFischer, Cat#11360070) to create C10 media, which was used throughout for all cell culture. Cell lines A3.01 (Cat#166), 8E5 (Cat#95) and ACH-2s (Cat#349) were maintained in C10 media and split every two days. Cultures were not maintained for longer than 1 month. All cell culture was done at 37°C in 5% CO2 humidified incubators. Cell cultures were negative for Mycoplasma contamination.

Binding assays

To assess TriKE binding to target cells and primary NK cells, we utilize the 10x His-Tag on the TriKEs for detection via the anti-His antibody (Biolegend, Cat#362603). Target cells were maintained as described above. Whole PBMCs from PWOH and PWH were thawed from frozen stocks and rested overnight in C10 media. The following day target cells or PBMCs were collected, washed and resuspended in fresh C10 media and incubated at 37°C in 5% CO2 for 30min with 10 nM TriKE. After incubation, cells were collected, washed to remove excess TriKE, stained with the binding assay cocktail (Table S1A) and fixed.

Activation and proliferation

In addition to the strong NK cell priming that CD16 ligation mediates by TriKEs, the inclusion of IL-15 modulates the NK cell response by two mechanisms. The first is activation of NK cells through binding to IL-15RA, which lowers the signal threshold required to induce a functional response. Secondly, IL-15 is a potent activator of NK cell proliferation. PBMCs from both PWOH and PWH were labeled with CellTrace Violet dye (Invitrogen, Cat. No. C34557), plated at 400,000 PBMCs/mL in a 48 deep-well plate (Nest Scientific, Cat No. 504162) and incubated with 10 nM rH IL-15 or 10 nM of either TriKE at 37°C in 5% CO2 for 7 days. Following incubation, cells were collected and stained with the proliferation surface staining cocktail (Table S1C) and fixed.

Functional assay

Whole PBMCs from PWOH and PWH were thawed from frozen stocks and rested overnight in C10 media. We used PBMCs as effector cells in these experiments as a more biologically representative mixed cell model than enriched NK cells and is what would be encountered in vivo. The next day target cells (A3.01, 8E5 and ACH-2s) were labeled with CellTrace Violet dye (Invitrogen, Cat. No. C34557) as per manufactures instructions. ACH-2 cells are a model of latent HIV which do express HIV envelope at low-levels, but can be reactivated to induce high levels of HIV envelope expression.50 We did not reactivate the ACH-2 cells in these experiments to maintain the latent HIV model. Targets were then co-cultured with PBMCs at an effector to target (E:T) ratio of 2:1 and treated with 10 nM recombinant human (rH) IL-15 (NCI), 10 μg/mL VRC01 bNAb (BEI-RRP, Cat#ARP-12033), both rH IL-15 and bNAb or 10 nM of VRC or CD4 TriKE, then stained with FITC anti-human CD107a (BioLegend, Cat# 328606). After an hour incubation, GolgiStop (BD Biosciences, Cat# 554724) and GolgiPlug (BD Biosciences, Cat# 555029) were added 1:100 and 1:150 respectively. After another 4 h incubation, cells were collected and stained with the surface antibody cocktail (Table S1B), fixed, permeabilized and stained with the intracellular cocktail (Table S1B).

IL-15 bioassay

To quantify biological activity of the recombinant human (rH) and TriKE contained IL-15, HEK-Blue IL-2/IL-15 cells (InvivoGen, Cat# hkb-il2-2) were utilized as reporter cells. On Day 1, cells were harvested by rinsing twice with pre-warmed phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and detached using trypsin at room temperature for 2–3 min. Following detachment, cells were resuspended in pre-warmed DMEM with 10% FBS to a density 2.8 × 105 cells/mL. rH IL-15 and VRC and CD4 TriKEs were diluted as indicated, ranging from 0.000153 to 20 nM in a final volume of 20 μL. 180 μL of the HEK blue cells (50,000 cells/well) were added to each well and incubated overnight at 37°C in 5% CO2. On day 2, QUANTI-blue Solution (InvivoGen, Cat# rep-qbs3) was prepared by mixing 100ul of QUANTI-blue and 100 μl of QB buffer in 9.8 mL of sterile H2O. In a new flat bottom plate, 20 μL of the HEK Blue cell supernatant was added with 180 μL of resuspended QUANTI-Blue Solution. Plates were incubated at 37°C for 20–30 min. IL-15 levels were determined by measuring the optical density (OD) at 620–655 nm using a ClarioStar microplate reader. Dose-response curves and EC50 values were calculated using GraphPad Prism.

NHP study

All animals received five subcutaneous doses of the rhesusized TriKE following a stepwise dose escalation: 100 μg/kg during the first week, 200 μg/kg during the second, and 300 μg/kg during the third. We chose this dosing regimen based on an early clinical trial (NCT03214666) in the cancer setting where TriKE half-life was under 3 h and patients were required to be dosed by continual intravenous infusion for a 96 h period. Continual infusions are not feasible in NHPs or in the clinical setting of HIV cure where most patients are living a near-normal life, thus for this early study we decided on daily subcutaneous dosing. Daily dosing is still not optimal in the clinical setting of HIV cure, and the dosing regimen will need to be further optimized. During the study, whole blood was collected in Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)-coated tubes, and LN cells were obtained via LN biopsy or fine needle aspirates (FNA). Blood samples were used for complete blood counts and serum chemistry panels. Plasma was separated from EDTA-anticoagulated blood by centrifugation. LN biopsy tissues were mechanically processed and passed through a 70-μm cell strainer.

Flow cytometry

All stained samples from in vitro assessments were acquired on the custom BD Fortessa H0081 instrument, housed by the University of Minnesota Flow Cytometry core and analyzed by flow cytometry using the gating strategy depicted in Figure S3. For the NHP study, whole blood collected from EDTA-coated tubes and fresh LN cells from RMs were stained with monoclonal antibodies, as previously described.110 The fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies used are provided in Table S1D. Event acquisition was performed on a Cytek Aurora using the SpectroFlo software, and data were collected from at least 100,000 CD3+ events. Data were analyzed using the gating strategy depicted in Figures S3 and S13 using FlowJo v10.8.1 Software (BD Life Sciences).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical assessment for in vitro functional and proliferation assays was performed with a matched repeated measures two-way ANOVA with a Geisser-Greenhouse correction between conditions compared to the IL-15 control (functional) or No treatment (proliferation). Multiple comparisons were controlled for by a Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test. GraphPad Prism (La Jolla, California) was used to analyze data and generate data graphs with error bars showing mean ± SEM. SPICE60 was used to analyze and generate the radial pie plots of the polychromatic flow cytometry data and Wilcoxon rank-Sum test was used to analyze differences in phenotypes between conditions compared to the IL-15 control. No statistical analysis was performed in the in vivo rhesus macaque study due to the low sample size.
