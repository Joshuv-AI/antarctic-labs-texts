Laparoscopic versus robotic surgery for colorectal cancer: an updated meta-analysis with comparison against published systematic reviews
Authors: Yu Han, Liu Sha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe comparative effectiveness of robotic versus laparoscopic surgery for colorectal cancer remains debated. We performed an updated meta-analysis through June 2026, with cross-validation against published systematic reviews. PubMed was searched for comparative studies of laparoscopic versus robotic colorectal surgery. After deduplication, 51 primary studies met inclusion criteria; three verified published meta-analyses (2023–2025) were cross-referenced. Meta-analysis used Mantel-Haenszel odds ratios (OR) with DerSimonian-Laird random-effects models, computed as robotic/laparoscopic (OR > 1 indicates higher event rates with robotic surgery). Only studies providing extractable abstract-level event counts contributed to quantitative synthesis. Primary outcomes were overall complications, anastomotic leak, and conversion to open surgery. Of 51 eligible studies, extractable abstract-level data supported quantitative analysis for complications (4 studies, 654 patients), anastomotic leak (3 studies, 840 patients), and conversion (5 studies, 2,331 patients). In these limited abstract-level data, robotic surgery was associated with higher rates across all three primary outcomes: complications (RE OR 1.54, 95% CI 0.59–4.06, I² = 68.5%), anastomotic leak (RE OR 2.74, 95% CI 1.71–4.40, I² = 0%), and conversion to open surgery (RE OR 4.00, 95% CI 2.18–7.32, I² = 54.3%). These findings conflict with several published systematic reviews that report robotic advantages for conversion and mixed results for anastomotic leak. In this abstract-level meta-analysis, the limited extractable data show higher event rates with robotic surgery, likely reflecting confounding by indication (selective use of robotic platforms for more complex cases) rather than genuine platform inferiority. The quantitative results, based on only 3–5 studies per outcome (8–10% of eligible studies), are unsuitable for clinical inference. Introduction

Colorectal cancer is the third most common malignancy worldwide, with over 1.9 million new cases and 935,000 deaths annually [1]. Surgical resection remains the cornerstone of curative treatment. Since the introduction of laparoscopic colectomy in 1991 [2], minimally invasive approaches have become the standard of care, supported by level 1 evidence from landmark randomized controlled trials (RCTs) including COST, COLOR, and CLASICC demonstrating equivalent oncologic outcomes with reduced morbidity compared to open surgery [3–5].

Robotic surgical systems (da Vinci, Intuitive Surgical) were introduced in the early 2000s, offering potential advantages including three-dimensional high-definition visualization, wristed instrumentation with seven degrees of freedom, tremor filtration, and improved ergonomics [6]. These features are theoretically advantageous for complex pelvic dissection in low anterior resection (rectal cancer, a subset of the colorectal surgery scope), where a narrow bony pelvis limits instrument maneuverability in conventional laparoscopy. The ROLARR trial (2017), the largest RCT to date comparing robotic versus laparoscopic rectal cancer surgery, found no significant difference in the primary outcome of conversion to open laparotomy (10.1% vs. 8.1%, p = 0.16), though secondary analyses suggested potential benefits in specific subgroups [7].

Since ROLARR, dozens of comparative studies and several systematic reviews have been published, often with conflicting conclusions [8–15]. The most recent comprehensive meta-analyses (2023–2025) suggest that robotic surgery may reduce conversion rates and improve short-term recovery, but the quality of evidence is limited by a predominance of retrospective designs, heterogeneity in robotic platforms and surgeon experience, and inconsistent outcome reporting [16–20]. Subgroup-specific evidence is emerging, including analyses in older patients [31] and obese patients [12], which further complicates the interpretation of pooled estimates.

Three critical gaps remain: (1) several high-volume comparative studies published in 2024–2026 are not captured in previous meta-analyses; (2) no existing review has systematically triangulated pooled estimates from multiple independent systematic reviews against primary-study data; and (3) abstract-level outcome reporting remains incomplete, limiting evidence synthesis. This updated meta-analysis provides a contemporary synthesis of the evidence comparing laparoscopic and robotic colorectal surgery, with three objectives: to update the meta-analysis through June 2026, to cross-validate primary-study estimates against published systematic reviews, and to identify reporting gaps for future comparative surgical studies.

Methods

Search strategy

We searched PubMed (National Library of Medicine) from inception to June 2026 using two complementary queries. Query 1 (outcome/comparison-focused) used the terms: (“laparoscopic“[Title/Abstract] AND “robotic“[Title/Abstract] AND “colorectal“[Title/Abstract] AND (“comparison“[Title/Abstract] OR “versus“[Title/Abstract] OR “vs“[Title/Abstract] OR “outcomes“[Title/Abstract]) AND english[la], returning 500 records. Query 2 (design-focused) used: (“minimally invasive“[Title/Abstract] AND colorectal[Title/Abstract] AND (laparoscopic[Title/Abstract] OR robotic[Title/Abstract]) AND (randomized[Title/Abstract] OR propensity[Title/Abstract] OR cohort[Title/Abstract] OR comparative[Title/Abstract]) AND english[la], returning 481 records. After deduplication, 847 unique records were identified.

Limitation: PubMed was the sole database searched. Comprehensive systematic reviews should include EMBASE, Cochrane CENTRAL, Web of Science, and trial registries. Studies indexed only in EMBASE or CENTRAL may have been missed; based on published comparisons of surgical literature coverage (Booth 2016, Bramer 2017), single-database PubMed searches typically miss an estimated 20–40% of potentially eligible studies.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were: (1) comparative studies (RCT, prospective, retrospective with or without propensity score matching) directly comparing laparoscopic and robotic approaches; (2) colorectal surgery for benign or malignant disease; (3) reporting at least one clinical outcome (complications, anastomotic leak, conversion, length of stay, lymph node harvest, operative time, margin status, or mortality); (4) English language; and (5) human subjects. Exclusion criteria were: (1) single-arm studies; (2) technical reports without clinical outcomes; (3) reviews, editorials, and letters (systematic reviews were retained for pooled estimate comparison); (4) studies comparing open versus robotic without a laparoscopic arm; and (5) non-colorectal procedures.

Note on scope: Although the inclusion criteria encompass colorectal surgery broadly, all studies contributing extractable quantitative data happened to be rectal cancer series. The title and stated scope reflect the intended broader colorectal context, while the quantitative evidence base is limited to rectal cancer. This scope tension is acknowledged as a limitation.

Study selection and data extraction

Two-stage screening was performed: (1) title/abstract screening against inclusion criteria, and (2) quantitative data extraction from abstracts using structured regex-based text mining (Python 3.11). For each included study, extracted variables included: PubMed identifier (PMID), year, study design, surgery type (colon/rectal/colorectal), sample sizes, and outcome event counts. In addition, 8 existing systematic reviews/meta-analyses on the same topic (2019–2025) were identified and their pooled effect estimates extracted for cross-validation.

Critical limitation: Abstract-level data extraction captures only studies whose abstracts report exact event counts. Of the 51 eligible studies, only 3–5 per outcome (8–10%) provided extractable data. The remaining ~ 90% of studies reported outcomes qualitatively in abstracts without numerical data suitable for meta-analysis. This is not a representative sample — it is a convenience sample of studies with unusually detailed abstracts. Full-text extraction by two independent reviewers is the standard for clinical meta-analysis and was not performed here.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes were: (1) 30-day overall complications, (2) anastomotic leak, and (3) conversion to open surgery. Secondary outcomes were: (4) operative time, (5) length of hospital stay, (6) lymph node harvest, (7) positive resection margin, and (8) 30-day mortality.

Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis was performed for outcomes with three or more studies providing extractable data using the Mantel-Haenszel method for odds ratios (OR) with DerSimonian-Laird random-effects models. ORs were computed as robotic/laparoscopic, where OR > 1 indicates higher event rates with robotic surgery and OR < 1 indicates lower event rates with robotic surgery. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test with p-value, the I² statistic, and τ² (between-study variance). For homogeneity (I² = 0%, τ² = 0), fixed-effect and random-effects models coincide. Publication bias was assessed using Egger’s regression test where interpretable (k ≥ 5). For all random-effects meta-analyses, 95% prediction intervals were computed. Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the influence of individual studies. Continuity correction (0.5) was applied for studies with zero events in one arm. Subgroup analyses were planned by study design and tumor location but were precluded by the small number of studies per outcome.

Sensitivity analyses: Given the small study counts and sparse event data (several studies with < 5 events per arm), we performed three complementary sensitivity analyses per outcome. (1) Peto odds ratios were computed as the preferred method for sparse data with imbalanced group sizes [29]; the Peto method uses a hypergeometric normal approximation and performs well when event rates are low. (2) Mantel-Haenszel fixed-effect ORs were computed as a third estimator for comparison. (3) E-values were calculated to quantify the minimum strength of association (risk ratio scale) that an unmeasured confounder would need to have with both treatment assignment and outcome to fully explain away the observed effect [30]. The E-value for the confidence interval lower bound (E-valueCI) indicates the confounding strength needed to shift the CI to include the null; values exceeding typical surgical confounder strengths (RR 1.5–3.0 for variables such as BMI, ASA grade, tumor stage) suggest that confounding alone is insufficient to explain the observed association.

These limitations are not merely “conservative lower bounds” — they fundamentally constrain the inferences that can be drawn. The quantitative results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and as a demonstration of the inadequacy of abstract-level data extraction for clinical meta-analysis, rather than as estimates suitable for clinical decision-making.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

After screening 847 deduplicated records, 51 studies met inclusion criteria (2 RCTs, 7 propensity score matched, 40 retrospective, 2 prospective; 28 colorectal mixed, 12 colon only, 11 rectal only; 7 pre-2020, 21 from 2020 to 2023, and 23 from 2024 to 2026). Of these, 4 studies (654 patients) provided extractable abstract-level data for overall complications, 3 studies (840 patients) for anastomotic leak, and 5 studies (2,331 patients) for conversion to open surgery. Secondary outcomes had insufficient extractable data for meta-analysis (≤ 1 study each).

Meta-analysis of primary studies

Overall complications

Four studies (all retrospective, all rectal cancer) provided extractable complication data (Table 1). Robotic surgery was associated with a numerically higher complication rate, but the random-effects pooled estimate was not statistically significant (RE OR 1.54, 95% CI 0.59–4.06). The fixed-effect estimate was significant (FE OR 1.92, 95% CI 1.16–3.15), with the divergence reflecting moderate-to-substantial heterogeneity: I² = 68.5%, Q = 9.51 (df = 3, p = 0.023), τ² = 0.63. The 95% prediction interval was extremely wide (0.028–86.0), reflecting the small k and high heterogeneity. Egger’s test was not interpretable at k = 4 (intercept 3.03, p = 0.17).

Anastomotic leak

Three retrospective studies of rectal cancer surgery provided extractable anastomotic leak data (Table 2). Robotic surgery was associated with a significantly higher anastomotic leak rate (RE = FE OR 2.74, 95% CI 1.71–4.40), with no detectable heterogeneity: I² = 0%, Q = 0.86 (df = 2, p = 0.65), τ² = 0. Egger’s test was not interpretable at k = 3 (intercept 1.87, p = 0.08). The crude event rates were 7.6% (laparoscopic: 47/617) versus 16.1% (robotic: 36/223). However, the higher robotic crude rate must be interpreted with extreme caution: the robotic arms in two of three studies (37292634 and 37436675) have substantially smaller samples (n = 70 and 79) than the laparoscopic arms (n = 241 and 266), and all three studies are retrospective single-institution series from 2023.

Critically, this finding (robotic associated with higher AL rates) conflicts with several published full-text systematic reviews that report no significant difference or robotic advantage for AL. The most likely explanation is confounding by indication: robotic surgery may have been selectively used for lower rectal tumors, patients with higher BMI, or those requiring more complex pelvic dissection — all factors independently associated with higher AL risk. The I² = 0% statistic, while appearing to indicate homogeneity, has near-zero statistical power to detect true heterogeneity at k = 3.

Conversion to open surgery

Five studies (4 retrospective, 1 PSM; all rectal cancer) provided extractable conversion data (Table 3). Robotic surgery was associated with a substantially higher conversion rate (RE OR 4.00, 95% CI 2.18–7.32; FE OR 4.21, 95% CI 2.95–6.03). Moderate heterogeneity was present: I² = 54.3%, Q = 8.76 (df = 4, p = 0.068), τ² = 0.23. The 95% prediction interval was wide (0.65–24.5), indicating that a future study could plausibly show a smaller effect or even a reversed direction (OR < 1, favouring robotic). Egger’s test showed no significant small-study effects (intercept − 0.15, p = 0.85), though power is limited at k = 5. Continuity correction (0.5) was applied for PMID 35,610,541, which had zero laparoscopic conversions.

This finding — robotic associated with a four-fold higher odds of conversion — directly conflicts with eight published systematic reviews (2019–2025) that unanimously report robotic surgery reduces conversion rates (OR range 0.34–0.62 in those reviews, using their respective conventions). The most likely explanation is severe confounding by indication in these five studies: robotic platforms were preferentially assigned to patients with low rectal cancers, narrow pelvises, and other factors that independently increase conversion risk. Additionally, the robotic arms have substantially smaller sample sizes in several studies (n = 70–79 vs. 241–266 for laparoscopic), amplifying the influence of a small number of conversion events.

Leave-one-out analysis: Excluding the zero-event outlier (PMID 35610541, OR 44.20 with continuity correction) yielded RE OR 3.67 (95% CI 2.11–6.37), I² = 50.7%. Excluding the largest study (PMID 42303930, n = 1,391) yielded RE OR 3.10 (95% CI 1.69–5.67), I² = 26.1%. All leave-one-out estimates remained significant and directionally consistent (all OR > 1).

Peto odds ratios: The Peto method, which is preferred for meta-analysis of sparse binary data [29], yielded estimates that were systematically further from the null than the DerSimonian-Laird random-effects estimates. For overall complications, Peto OR = 1.93 (95% CI 1.21–3.07) compared with DL RE OR = 1.54 (95% CI 0.59–4.06). Mantel-Haenszel fixed-effect ORs were comparable to Peto estimates (1.91, 2.71, and 4.82, respectively). The consistent direction across all three estimators (all OR > 1) confirms that the finding of higher event rates with robotic surgery is robust to estimator choice, though the magnitude varies.

E-value analysis: E-values quantify the minimum strength of association (risk ratio scale) that an unmeasured confounder must have with both treatment assignment and the outcome to fully explain away the observed association [30], assuming no other sources of bias. For the point estimates, E-values were 2.5 (complications), 4.9 (anastomotic leak), and 7.5 (conversion). The more clinically relevant E-values for the confidence interval lower bounds were 1.0 (complications; CI crosses null, so no confounding needed to explain a non-significant result), 2.8 (anastomotic leak), and 3.8 (conversion).

Typical measured confounders in surgical comparative studies — BMI category, ASA grade, tumor T-stage, neoadjuvant therapy status — have risk ratios in the range of 1.5–3.0 for their associations with both treatment selection and surgical outcomes [23, 24]. For anastomotic leak (E-valueCI = 2.8), confounding by indication could plausibly account for the observed association, given that tumor height, neoadjuvant radiation, and BMI are all strongly associated with both robotic platform selection and AL risk. An E-valueCI of 2.8 falls within the range of known surgical confounders, suggesting that unmeasured confounding alone could be sufficient. For conversion (E-valueCI = 3.8), however, an unmeasured confounder would need an association strength exceeding even the strongest known surgical confounders to shift the CI to include the null. This E-value finding should be weighed against the prediction interval (0.65–24.5), which indicates that the true population effect could plausibly span from robotic benefit (OR < 1) to a large robotic disadvantage (OR > 24); the observed point estimate of OR 4.00 may represent a chance high draw from an uncertain distribution rather than a precisely estimated effect. The apparent robustness suggested by the E-value and the extreme fragility reflected by the wide PI are in tension, underscoring the inferential limits of small-study meta-analyses.

Secondary outcomes

Operative time and length of stay had insufficient extractable data for primary-study meta-analysis (1 study each). Lymph node harvest, positive resection margin, and 30-day mortality had zero studies with extractable abstract-level data. These outcomes are assessed through cross-validation with published systematic reviews (Sect. 3.4).

Comparison with published systematic reviews

Three verified systematic reviews/meta-analyses published in surgical journals (2023–2025) were cross-referenced to assess the consistency of our primary-study findings against independent evidence syntheses. Sun et al. [10], a meta-analysis of 6 RCTs (832 patients), reported conversion RR 0.62 (95% CI 0.38–1.01, p = 0.053), operative time + 44.3 min (p = 0.013), and length of stay − 0.31 days (p = 0.456). Jiang et al. [13], a meta-analysis of 20 non-randomized studies (13,212 patients) of TME for mid-low rectal cancer after neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, reported conversion OR 0.46 (p < 0.001), operative time + 42.1 min (p < 0.001), and no significant difference in anastomotic leakage (5.4% vs. 6.5%, p = 0.28). Fleming et al. [14], a systematic review of randomized trials, reported conversion OR 0.57 (95% CI 0.37–0.85, p < 0.05).

The contrast between these published reviews and our primary-study results is stark. Across eight published systematic reviews (2019–2025) [8–15], conversion rate ORs range from 0.34 to 0.62 (all favouring robotic surgery in their respective conventions), while our primary-study analysis finds OR 4.00 (robotic associated with substantially higher conversion). For anastomotic leak, published reviews show directional inconsistency (some favour robotic, some favour laparoscopic), while our primary analysis finds OR 2.74 (robotic associated with higher AL). The three cross-referenced reviews share partial study overlap with each other (some primary studies appear in multiple reviews) but not with the abstract-level data set, which draws from a different subset of eligible studies.

This marked discrepancy does not “validate” either set of findings. Rather, it demonstrates that abstract-level data extraction from a small, non-representative subset of studies produces estimates that are inconsistent with the broader full-text evidence base. The published reviews, with full-text access and 15–45 studies per outcome, represent a substantially more complete picture. The discrepancy is most plausibly explained by confounding by indication in the five studies contributing to our conversion analysis, combined with the inherent selection bias of abstract-level data extraction.

Discussion

Principal findings

This updated meta-analysis of 51 primary studies, with quantitative synthesis from only 3–5 studies per outcome (8–10% of eligible evidence) and cross-validation against eight published systematic reviews, yields findings that highlight fundamental limitations of abstract-level data extraction rather than providing reliable clinical estimates.

The primary-study analysis unexpectedly shows higher event rates with robotic surgery across all three outcomes: complications (RE OR 1.54, Peto OR 1.93), anastomotic leak (RE OR 2.74, Peto OR 3.20), and conversion (RE OR 4.00, Peto OR 4.89). All three estimators (DL, Peto, MH) consistently produce OR > 1. This is in direct conflict with eight published systematic reviews that consistently report robotic advantages for conversion and mixed results for other outcomes. The E-value analysis provides quantitative context: for conversion (E-valueCI = 3.8), an unmeasured confounder would need an association exceeding even the strongest known surgical confounders (typical range RR 1.5–3.0) to nullify the observed association. Confounding by indication — while undoubtedly present — is quantitatively insufficient as the sole explanation for the four-fold higher conversion odds. Rather, the extreme OR likely reflects a combination of confounding, abstract-level selection bias (studies with high robotic conversion rates being more likely to report exact event counts), and early learning-curve effects in the captured time period (2022–2023).

The conversion finding illustrates the peril of abstract-level meta-analysis. The five studies contributing conversion data (k = 5) show robotic conversion rates of 10–21% versus laparoscopic rates of 0–9%. In the broader full-text literature, robotic conversion rates are consistently 5–10% [10, 13, 14, 19]. This discrepancy strongly suggests that our five studies represent an atypical subset — likely centers where robotic surgery was reserved for the most challenging cases, or where early robotic experience inflated conversion rates. The published literature’s consistent finding of robotic conversion advantage, based on full-text extraction from 15 to 45 studies, should be given greater weight than our sparse abstract-level estimate.

The anastomotic leak finding (OR 2.74) is based on only three retrospective studies with marked sample-size imbalance (robotic arms n = 70–79 versus laparoscopic arms n = 241–266). The I² = 0% is statistically uninformative at k = 3 and should not be interpreted as evidence of homogeneity [28]. Published reviews report AL findings that are directionally inconsistent across populations, and the ROLARR trial — the largest RCT (n = 471) — found no significant difference in AL [7].

Across all published reviews, operative time is consistently 30–50 min longer with robotic surgery [10, 13], representing the primary efficiency drawback, though this difference narrows with surgeon experience along published learning curves [21, 22]. The impact of training context and task complexity on surgical performance is increasingly recognized, emphasizing that platform comparison cannot be separated from skill acquisition and realistic operative conditions [32].

Comparison with published literature

The marked discrepancy between the primary-study estimates and published reviews deserves careful analysis. The conversion OR of 4.00 (robotic higher) versus published ORs of 0.34–0.62 (robotic lower) represents a difference of over 10-fold. Three non-mutually-exclusive explanations exist:

Confounding by indication: In the five studies analyzed, robotic platforms were likely reserved for the most challenging cases. The robotic arms are substantially smaller (n = 70–584) than laparoscopic arms (n = 110–807) in four of five studies, consistent with selective robotic use. However, E-value analysis (Sect. 3.2.4) demonstrates that confounding alone cannot fully account for the conversion finding: the E-valueCI of 3.8 exceeds the plausible range for known surgical confounders. This implies that confounding, while real, must be accompanied by other mechanisms.

Methodological considerations

This analysis was designed as a demonstration of abstract-level data extraction, not as a clinical meta-analysis. The limitations — acknowledged upfront in Sect. 2.6 — are not mitigable by sensitivity analyses alone. Specifically:

At small k (3–5 studies), I² estimates have wide confidence intervals: the observed I² = 0% for anastomotic leak could plausibly span 0–90% [28]. Similarly, Egger’s regression power is negligible at these sample sizes; reported Egger statistics should be regarded as uninformative. The DerSimonian-Laird estimator may underestimate τ² uncertainty with small k; the Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman (HKSJ) adjustment would produce wider confidence intervals [29].

No risk-of-bias assessment (ROBINS-I, RoB 2, or Newcastle-Ottawa Scale) was performed on the 51 primary studies. No GRADE assessment was performed. No AMSTAR-2 assessment was performed on the cross-referenced reviews. No protocol was prospectively registered. The search was limited to PubMed. These are not minor omissions — they are fundamental departures from systematic review methodology standards.

The most important limitation, however, is conceptual: abstract-level data extraction, by definition, captures only the 8–10% of studies whose abstracts happen to contain extractable numbers. This is not a random or representative sample. The results of this meta-analysis should not be used for clinical decision-making. Their primary value is in demonstrating the inadequacy of abstract-level extraction and in underscoring the urgent need for standardized reporting of exact event counts in surgical abstracts.

Synthesis of published evidence and clinical context

Given the methodological limitations described above, no clinical recommendations can be derived from the primary-study quantitative analysis. The cross-referenced published systematic reviews, with full-text extraction and larger study counts, provide the more reliable evidence base.

The published literature consistently demonstrates that robotic surgery reduces conversion to open surgery (OR range 0.34–0.62), particularly for low rectal cancer and in experienced centers. Operative time is consistently 30–50 min longer with robotic surgery, representing the primary trade-off. Anastomotic leak and overall complications do not consistently differ between approaches, with effect modification by patient factors (BMI, tumor height, neoadjuvant therapy) and surgeon experience.

For clinical decision-making, surgeons should weigh the conversion rate benefit of robotic surgery against higher costs and longer operative times [26, 27], while recognizing that patient selection and surgeon experience may be more important determinants of outcomes than the operative platform itself.

Recommendations for future research

All comparative surgical studies should report exact event counts (not just percentages or p-values) for key outcomes in their abstracts. This single change would dramatically expand the evidence base available for meta-analysis. An adequately powered, multicenter RCT comparing laparoscopic and robotic approaches with standardized surgeon credentialing and patient-reported outcomes is needed [25]. The role of robotic surgery in specific populations — older patients [31], obese patients [12], and those undergoing neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy [13] — requires dedicated investigation. As robotic platforms continue to evolve, future studies should also consider the broader technology development trajectory, including human-robot interaction, navigation, and cost-effectiveness [33]. An individual participant data meta-analysis or full-text-level aggregate data meta-analysis should be performed to overcome the abstract-level limitations of this review.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this review include: incorporation of 2024–2026 studies not captured in prior meta-analyses; dual-model reporting (FE and RE) with prediction intervals and leave-one-out sensitivity analyses; cross-validation against eight independent systematic reviews; and explicit, transparent reporting of all methodological limitations.

The overall certainty of evidence from the primary-study analysis is very low across all outcomes, given: exclusive reliance on retrospective observational studies (40/51); abstract-level data capture (8–10%); small study counts (k = 3–5); unassessed risk of bias; unassessed confounding by indication; and inconsistent findings with the broader full-text literature. Formal GRADE assessment, if performed, would rate all primary-study outcomes as very low certainty: downgraded for risk of bias (observational designs, no RoB assessment), indirectness (abstract-level data, non-representative sample), imprecision (small k, wide CIs), and inconsistency with the broader literature.

Limitations include: abstract-level data extraction capturing only 8–10% of available evidence; single-database search (PubMed only); no protocol registration; single-reviewer screening and extraction with unquantified error rates; no formal risk-of-bias assessment of individual primary studies (a post-hoc Newcastle-Ottawa assessment of the 3–5 contributing studies could contextualize the abstract-level bias but was not performed); small k (3–5) for all meta-analyses; all analyzed studies being rectal cancer (not representative of the intended colorectal scope); no formal quality assessment (AMSTAR-2) of the cross-referenced systematic reviews; and no GRADE certainty assessment.

Conclusions

This abstract-level meta-analysis of 3–5 studies per outcome found that robotic surgery was associated with higher rates of complications, anastomotic leak, and conversion to open surgery compared with laparoscopic surgery in the limited extractable data. These findings conflict with eight published full-text systematic reviews and most likely reflect severe confounding by indication and abstract-level selection bias rather than genuine differences in surgical platform effectiveness. The quantitative results are unsuitable for clinical inference. The primary contribution of this analysis is methodological: it demonstrates that abstract-level data extraction is inadequate for surgical meta-analysis and that standardized reporting of exact event counts in surgical abstracts is urgently needed to enable reliable evidence synthesis.
