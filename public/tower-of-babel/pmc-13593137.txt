Real-time Quality Assessment of Donor Kidneys During Normothermic Machine Perfusion Using Perfusate and Urinary Biomarkers
Authors: Julia S. Slagter, Agni Papamanoli, Farhan Akram, Muhammad Adeel Azam, Andrew Stubbs, Marian C. Clahsen-van Groningen, Robert J. Porte, Robert C. Minnee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background.Objective, real-time criteria for kidney graft quality during normothermic machine perfusion (NMP) are lacking, contributing to subjective acceptance decisions and organ discard. We evaluated real-time perfusate/urinary biomarkers and perfusion parameters during NMP and developed an exploratory predictive model for delayed graft function (DGF).Methods.This predefined secondary analysis used the NMP arm of a randomized trial (n = 41). Primary nonfunction cases (n = 3) were excluded a priori. The remaining immediate graft function/DGF cohort (n = 38) was split 70/30 into training and test sets; all screening and feature selection were restricted to the training set. Variables without missing data were grouped according to their correlation structure and physiological interpretability, and the feature with the largest absolute effect size within each group was selected. An L2-penalized logistic regression model was evaluated using cross-validation and a held-out test set (n = 12).Results.DGF occurred in 20 of 41 (49%) and primary nonfunction in 3 of 41 (7%) cases. In the training set (immediate graft function n = 12; DGF n = 14), DGF was associated with higher venous Po2 at 60 min and lower total urine production >0–120 min. Urinary phosphate at 120 min met the prespecified threshold for practical relevance and was retained as a complementary model feature. The resulting 3-feature regression model achieved a test set area under the curve of 0.81 and an accuracy of 0.83.Conclusions.Lower urine output and higher venous Po2 during NMP were associated with DGF. The regression model showed preliminary internal discrimination and warrants validation in larger independent cohorts.

INTRODUCTION

Kidney transplantation faces persistent donor-organ scarcity, yet approximately 8%–25% of procured kidney grafts are not used for transplantation.1,2 The actual discard rate is likely even higher if organs unsuitable before procurement are included.1 Current discard criteria remain largely subjective, leading to unnecessary organ discard and substantial variability between transplant centers.3 This underscores the urgent need for objective and reliable criteria to assess kidney graft quality before transplantation.

Normothermic machine perfusion (NMP) has emerged as a novel, promising platform for real-time organ quality assessment during preservation. During NMP, metabolically active grafts enable dynamic viability assessment before transplantation. In liver transplantation, NMP has increased the utilization of suitable donor livers in the Netherlands by 20%,4 using biochemical analysis of the perfusate and bile as biomarkers to assess hepatobiliary function.5 Unfortunately, unlike liver transplantation, no reliable biomarkers for kidney viability have been identified so far. The Cambridge group developed a preliminary assessment score based on NMP parameters (flow, urine production, and macroscopic appearance of the kidney graft),6 enabling the successful transplantation of 5 grafts previously deemed unsuitable for transplantation.7 However, this score failed to discriminate kidneys with delayed graft function (DGF) in a larger cohort from the same group.8

Within this context, the primary objective of this study was to develop a hypothesis-generating statistical model using 2-h NMP measurements to predict DGF. Secondary objectives included exploring associations with primary nonfunction (PNF), acute rejection, graft loss, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design and Ethics

This is a predefined secondary analysis of a randomized controlled trial9,10 evaluating 2 h of end-ischemic NMP versus hypothermic machine perfusion (HMP) alone in kidney transplantation. The parent trial was powered to detect a 30% difference in the incidence of immediate graft function (IGF) between HMP + NMP and HMP alone. This analysis includes only the kidneys in the NMP arm of the trial (n = 41). All patients provided written informed consent for participation in the study. The trial was approved by the Medical Ethics Review Committee of Erasmus MC (MEC-2020-0366).

Preservation and Sampling

donation after circulatory death (DCD) kidneys aged 50 y or older were preserved on oxygenated HMP; all other kidneys were preserved on nonoxygenated HMP. HMP was performed with Belzer Machine Perfusion Solution (Bridge to Life, IL).

Kidneys allocated to the NMP arm of the trial subsequently underwent 2 h of end-ischemic NMP immediately before transplantation. The 2-h regimen was chosen based on the encouraging results of our pilot study.11 NMP was performed using a red blood cell-based perfusate, the composition of which is detailed in the Supplementary Material (Table S1, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A906). The Kidney Assist device (XVIVO, Göteborg, Sweden) was used for perfusion, providing pulsatile arterial flow. During NMP, arterial flow and vascular resistance were continuously monitored.

Perfusate and urine samples were collected every 30 min. Arterial and venous blood samples were also taken every 30 min and analyzed immediately after collection. Blood gas analysis was performed using the ABL90 FLEX PLUS blood gas analyzer (Radiometer Medical ApS, Brønshøj, Denmark). Urine samples were collected at baseline and at 2 h, stored at –80°C, and subsequently analyzed.

Biomarkers

During NMP, hemodynamic measurements and biomarkers that were available in real time were examined. Arterial and venous blood gas analysis of the perfusate included hemoglobin, pH, Po2, Pco2, lactate, potassium, sodium, calcium, and glucose. Urinary biomarkers included potassium, sodium, phosphate, glucose, and total protein. Arterial flow (mL/min/100 g) and renal resistance (per 100 g of kidney weight) were recorded every 15 min. Urine was collected over a 30-min interval, after which urine output was measured, and the urine was recirculated into the circuit.

Outcome Parameters

Baseline characteristics, including donor age, sex, body mass index, cold ischemia time (CIT), donor and recipient warm ischemia time, and final serum creatinine levels, were collected. The primary objective was a statistical model to distinguish DGF from IGF. Secondary objectives were to explore associations with PNF, eGFR at 30 and 90 d posttransplant (dichotomized as eGFR ≥30 versus <30 mL/min/1.73 m2), acute rejection within 2 wk, and 12-mo death-censored graft loss.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables are presented as percentages. Continuous variables are reported as mean ± SD or median (interquartile range), depending on normality assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Approximately normal variables were compared using Welch t test, otherwise, the Mann-Whitney U test was used. Because the primary inferential focus was the predictive model, P-values were reported unadjusted, and values <0.05 were regarded as nominally significant rather than confirmatory. Exploratory comparisons used available-case analysis; therefore, denominators vary slightly by variable/timepoint and are reported in the Results section. Exploratory analyses and plotting were performed using R (v4.5.1). Logistic regression modeling was performed in Python (v3.13.9) using scikit-learn (v1.7.2).

Cohort and Data Split

For predictive modeling, PNF cases (n = 3) were excluded a priori, leaving an IGF/DGF cohort (n = 38). This cohort was split 70/30 into training and test sets, stratified by outcome to preserve class balance. Because of sample size constraints, multivariable adjustment for donor/recipient confounders (eg, CIT, donor age, and DCD/donation after brain death status) was not performed. All exploratory analyses comparing IGF and DGF were restricted to the training set to avoid data leakage, whereas those for PNF, acute rejection, graft loss, and eGFR outcomes were performed on the entire dataset.

Exploratory Analyses

In exploratory analyses, NMP-derived measurements were compared across transplant outcomes. These analyses were hypothesis-generating, and no correction for multiple testing was applied.

Logistic Regression Model

Population

The IGF and DGF grafts (n = 38; 18 IGF, 20 DGF), excluding the 3 PNF samples, were used for model development. Features with any missing values in this 2-class cohort were excluded. As detailed above, all screening, feature selection, correlation analysis, threshold definition, and model fitting were performed exclusively on the training set. The test set was used solely for the final evaluation.

Feature Selection

Collinearity assessment (training set only)

Pearson correlation coefficients were computed among the features in the training set. Variables with an absolute correlation ≥0.7 were considered collinear and were excluded from simultaneous use in multivariable models. After examination of the training set correlation structure, correlated variables were grouped based on the observed correlation patterns and physiological interpretability.

Univariate analysis (training set only)

The real-time NMP features were compared between the DGF and IGF groups. The effect sizes were expressed as Hedges’ g or Cliff’s delta, signed so that positive values indicated higher values in the DGF group. “Practical” relevance was defined as |Hedges’ g| ≥ 0.40 or |Cliff’s delta| ≥ 0.20. The univariate receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve area under the curve (AUC) for DGF versus IGF was computed for each feature.

Within each resulting group, the feature with the largest absolute effect size was selected for multivariable modeling.

Multivariable Model Development and Validation

Features were standardized within each training fold using a pipeline-embedded StandardScaler (with mean-centering tuned). An L2-penalized logistic regression was fitted (liblinear, random_state=42). The regularization strength (C) was tuned using a RepeatedStratifiedKFold cross-validation strategy (3 folds, 10 repeats) to optimize ROC-AUC. Training performance was summarized using out-of-fold (OOF) predictions from 3-fold stratified cross-validation. The final model hyperparameters were locked, and its performance was evaluated on the 30% held-out test set.

DGF occurred in 20 of the 41 transplanted kidneys (49%), PNF in 3 (7%), and acute rejection within the first 2 wk after transplantation in 5 (12%; Table 2).

IGF Versus DGF (Training Set Only)

In the 70% training cohort (IGF n = 12, DGF n = 14), venous Po2 measured at 60 min (Figure 1A) was higher in the DGF group than the IGF group (median, 40.7 mm Hg [35.43–44.87 mm Hg] versus 32.0 mm Hg [21.98–35.70 mm Hg]; Cliff’s delta = 0.54; P = 0.022; AUC = 0.77). A similar pattern was observed with venous Po2 at 90 min, but this was excluded from the predictive model because of a missing value. Other perfusate markers (arterial/venous potassium, calcium, glucose, lactate, pH, and Pco2) did not differ significantly between groups.

Among the urinary biomarkers, urinary phosphate at 120 min (Figure 1B) was numerically higher in the IGF group than the DGF group (4 mg/dL [2.63–4.45 mg/dL] versus 3.1 mg/dL [2.30–3.77 mg/dL]; Cliff’s delta = –0.33; P = 0.16; AUC = 0.66), but the difference did not reach statistical significance. Urinary total protein at 120 min was higher in the DGF group in the unadjusted analysis (P = 0.01) but was excluded from modeling because of missing values. Other urinary solutes (glucose, potassium, and sodium) did not show significant group separation.

No significant differences were observed in arterial flow or vascular resistance per 100 g between the IGF and DGF groups.

Total urine production during NMP from 0 to 120 min (Figure 1C) was higher in the IGF group (547.6 ± 328.9 versus 292.1 ± 156.6 mL; Hedges’ g = –0.99; P = 0.026; AUC = 0.77). Urine production was also higher in the IGF group at 90 min (160 ± 90.3 versus 86.6 ± 58.3 mL; Hedges’ g = –0.95; P = 0.026; AUC = 0.77) and 120 min (127.5 mL [88.25–211.25 mL] versus 63.5 mL [35.25–113.75 mL]; Cliff’s delta = –0.62; P = 0.008; AUC = 0.81). The difference at 60 min showed the same direction but did not reach statistical significance (124 ± 82.7 versus 73.7 ± 41 mL; Hedges’ g = –0.77; P = 0.074; AUC = 0.70).

IGF Versus PNF (Exploratory)

In the full dataset (IGF n = 18, PNF n = 3), PNF kidneys showed numerically lower arterial flow and higher resistance per 100 g, although these differences did not reach statistical significance. Arterial flow showed a progressive decline after 60 min in PNF kidneys, whereas there was a continued increase in IGF kidneys (Figure S1, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A906).

Among the perfusate biomarkers, arterial lactate at 120 min was lower in PNF kidneys in the unadjusted comparison (8.2 ± 0.6 versus 9.6 ± 1.8 mmol/L; P = 0.026), while venous lactate showed a similar numerical difference that did not reach statistical significance (Figure S2, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A906).

Urine production was lower in PNF kidneys both as a total measurement (112.6 ± 133.7 versus 516.4 ± 344.7 mL; P = 0.007; Figure S3A, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A906) and at specific timepoints (90 min: 21 ± 20.52 versus 148.67 ± 102.59 mL; P = 0.00017 and 120 min: 23.6 ± 19.7 versus 161.9 ± 116.2 mL; P = 0.00018; Figure S3B, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A906).

No clear differences were observed between IGF and PNF kidneys regarding urinary biomarkers.

Acute Rejection (Exploratory)

Regarding acute rejection (no rejection: n = 35; rejection: n = 5; 1 missing sample), the flow per 100 g was lower in the rejection group at several timepoints in the unadjusted comparison (Figure S4, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A906; 30 min: 157.5 ± 51.3 versus 114.5 ± 29.9 mL/min/100 g; P = 0.028; 45 min: 185.7 ± 59.9 versus 125.8 ± 36.9; P = 0.016; 60 min: 195.4 ± 73.1 versus 142.6 ± 37.2; P = 0.033; and 105 min: 213.1 ± 79.3 versus 148.5 ± 51.6; P = 0.047).

No perfusate biomarkers reached statistical significance. Venous Na+ was numerically lower in the rejection group, but the difference did not reach statistical significance (60 min: 142 mmol/L [138–144 mmol/L] versus 139 mmol/L [138–140 mmol/L]; P = 0.068; Figure S5A, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A906).

No significant differences were detected for urinary biomarkers. Urinary phosphate at 120 min was numerically higher in the rejection group, but the difference did not reach statistical significance (3.1 mg/dL [2.35–4.0 mg/dL] versus 4.6 mg/dL [3.2–4.6 mg/dL]; P = 0.153; Figure S5B, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A906).

Urine production was numerically lower in the rejection group, but the difference did not reach statistical significance (Figure S6, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A906).

Graft Loss at 12 mo (Exploratory)

Regarding graft loss (no graft loss: n = 32; graft loss: n = 7; 2 missing samples), arterial flow per 100 g was lower in kidneys with graft loss at multiple early timepoints in the unadjusted comparisons (Figure S7A, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A906), including at 15 min (91.6 ± 30.0 versus 134.3 ± 53.9 mL/min/100 g; P = 0.031), 30 min (115.0 ± 30.3 versus 154.5 ± 48.9 mL/min/100 g; P = 0.042), 45 min (124.9 ± 35.9 versus 181.1 ± 59.4 mL/min/100 g; P = 0.020), and 60 min (141.5 ± 35.5 versus 190.4 ± 73.0 mL/min/100 g; P = 0.040). Renal vascular resistance was numerically higher in kidneys with graft loss, but the difference did not reach statistical significance (Figure S7B, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A906).

Similarly, regarding perfusate biomarkers, venous Po2 was numerically lower in kidneys with graft loss across several timepoints, with only the 30-min comparison reaching statistical significance (30 min: 23.9 ± 11.5 versus 37.1 ± 13.3 mm Hg; P = 0.024; Figure S8, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A906).

eGFR at 30 and 90 d (Exploratory)

Regarding the 30-d eGFR group (<30 mL/min/1.73 m2 n = 17; ≥30 n = 22; 2 samples missing), perfusate biomarker venous glucose was higher in the low-eGFR group (60 min: 10.26 ± 0.93 versus 9.65 ± 0.81; P = 0.036; Figure S9A [SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A906] and 90 min: 10.52 ± 0.95 versus 9.73 ± 1.05; P = 0.020). Venous ionized calcium was also higher in the low-eGFR group at 30 min (2.16 ± 0.25 versus 1.99 ± 0.20; P = 0.026; Figure S9B, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A906).

For the 90-d eGFR group (<30 mL/min/1.73 m2 n = 15; ≥30 n = 25; 1 missing sample), none of the measures differed significantly between the <30 and ≥30 mL/min/1.73 m2 groups.

Logistic Regression Model

Feature Selection

Collinearity assessment (training set only)

The pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients for all candidate predictors are presented in Table S7 and Figure S10 (SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A906). Correlated variables were grouped based on the observed correlation structure and physiological interpretability, resulting in 3 distinct physiological axes: urine production, oxygenation/perfusion, and solute handling.

Univariate analysis (training set only)

After variables with missing values were excluded, the feature with the highest absolute effect size within each physiological axis was selected for multivariable analysis. This process yielded total urine production (0–120 min), venous Po2 at 60 min, and urinary phosphate at 120 min. The univariate analysis results (effect sizes, AUCs, and unadjusted P-values) are available in Table S8 (SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A906).

Train set

In the training set, the model achieved a cross-validated AUC of 0.78 (with the best configuration being C = 1, without centering). The OOF AUC was 0.76, with an accuracy of 0.69, balanced accuracy of 0.69, sensitivity of 0.71, and specificity of 0.67, as evaluated at the 0.5 threshold. The Brier score was 0.20.

Test set

Applied to the held-out test set (n = 12; 6 IGF, 6 DGF), the model yielded an AUC of 0.81 and an accuracy of 0.83 (balanced accuracy = 0.83; sensitivity for DGF = 0.83; specificity for IGF = 0.83; positive predictive value = 0.83; negative predictive value = 0.83; Brier score = 0.20). Bootstrap resampling of the test predictions confirmed moderate-to-good discriminatory performance (AUC 0.81, with a wide 95% confidence interval reflecting the small sample size).

DISCUSSION

This study investigated the associations between real-time perfusion parameters and posttransplant outcomes. In this exploratory analysis, lower urine production and higher venous Po2 during NMP were associated with subsequent DGF, while lower urinary phosphate had a practically relevant effect size and was retained for the statistical model.

Our results are not entirely consistent with findings from the Oxford group.12 In their study, urine production was lower and sodium excretion was higher in the DGF group, although neither difference reached statistical significance. In our study, urinary sodium excretion did not show a consistent pattern, whereas urine production was lower in the DGF group at multiple time points and when calculated as a cumulative total, with the comparisons reaching nominal significance. Urine production was also lower in kidneys that developed PNF. Together, these findings reinforce the importance of urine production as a predictive biomarker and are consistent with the scoring system described by Hosgood et al,6 although our cutoff value was slightly higher.

Venous Po2 may provide complementary information on renal oxygen utilization during NMP. When arterial oxygenation, hemoglobin concentration, and renal blood flow are comparable, a higher venous Po2 is consistent with reduced oxygen extraction. Several nonexclusive mechanisms could explain this finding. Firstly, impaired filtration could decrease solute load and, by extension, ATP-dependent reabsorption, leading to reduced oxygen demand.13 Alternatively, impaired mitochondrial capacity could reduce oxygen consumption coupled to ATP production.14 Finally, heterogeneous intrarenal perfusion could leave some renal parenchyma inadequately supplied despite adequate overall renal blood flow,15 potentially reflecting microvascular dysfunction. These mechanisms provide a plausible interpretation of the observed association with DGF, although venous Po2 alone cannot distinguish among reduced metabolic demand, impaired cellular capacity to use oxygen, and heterogeneous tissue oxygen delivery.

Urinary phosphate was retained as the third multivariable analysis feature because of its effect size and, although it may provide information on renal solute handling, its physiological interpretation is more indirect. Phosphate is filtered at the glomerulus and predominantly reabsorbed in the proximal tubule.16 Because proximal-tubular cells and their membrane transporters are particularly susceptible to ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI), tubular dysfunction could reduce phosphate reabsorption and increase fractional phosphate excretion.17 Conversely, IRI can also reduce renal blood flow, the filtration coefficient, and GFR,18 potentially reducing the amount of phosphate delivered to the tubular lumen and ultimately reaching the urine. In our study, urinary phosphate concentrations were numerically lower in DGF kidneys, alongside substantially reduced urine production, a pattern that could be consistent with reduced filtered phosphate delivery in DGF kidneys. However, urinary concentration alone cannot distinguish the contributions of glomerular filtration, tubular reabsorption, and urine-volume-related concentration effects. Considering that the concentration differences between IGF and DGF kidneys were not statistically significant, this interpretation should remain cautious.

The Hosgood score also incorporates arterial flow. Although the prognostic value of flow and renal resistance during NMP is understudied, a recent large study in HMP-preserved kidneys found that the trajectory of renal resistance was not associated with DGF development.19 In our study, arterial flow and renal resistance trajectories did not differ significantly between kidneys with IGF and those with DGF, but arterial flow showed a notable decline in kidneys with PNF. While the limited number of PNF cases and lack of statistical significance precludes broader conclusions, the potential predictive value of declining flow may warrant further investigation.

A key strength of this study is that perfusion parameters were directly correlated with posttransplant outcomes, as all kidneys that underwent NMP were ultimately transplanted. Whereas most previous research on viability assessment has been conducted in preclinical porcine models or studies of discarded human kidneys,7,20 this investigation represents the largest series to date assessing viability in transplanted human kidney grafts. Another strength is the exclusive focus on real-time measurements. Although research has been done on genetic and hormonal biomarkers,21,22 these typically require complex detection methods and long processing times, often taking days to weeks to yield results. As such, they are currently limited to research settings aimed at understanding renal physiology during NMP. In contrast, real-time perfusion parameters offer immediate clinical applicability, supporting real-time decision-making for kidney acceptance.

Limitations

The most significant limitation of the study pertains to the small cohort size, restricting the ability to detect associations between perfusion parameters and posttransplant outcomes. While the modeling approach was appropriate for the available sample size, several key aspects need to be highlighted.

Firstly, while DGF was selected as the primary outcome because of its relatively high incidence in the sample, it is not an ideal surrogate for viability assessment, as kidneys with DGF may still function well in the long term. This is especially true for DCD donors, for whom DGF is not necessarily linked to poorer long-term graft outcomes.23,24 Accordingly, the model’s utility for organ-acceptance decisions requires validation against longer term outcomes. Because of the small numbers of cases with PNF (n = 3) and 12-mo graft loss (n = 7), these outcomes could not support the development of separate predictive models; they were therefore only explored descriptively in our study. In larger datasets, similar modeling approaches could potentially be developed for these endpoints. However, identifying robust associations between perfusion parameters and PNF is likely to remain difficult given its low incidence (1%–2%).25

Secondly, the cohort size and number of DGF cases constrained both feature selection and model development. Because feature selection was confined to the training set to prevent data leakage, the effective sample size was further reduced to 26 cases, resulting in lower power for univariable analyses. Multivariable modeling was therefore limited to 2–3 predictors and to variables without missing data. This approach preserved all 38 kidneys in the overall modeling cohort while avoiding imputation, which can introduce bias and instability in small datasets. However, limiting the model to 2–3 predictors meant that relevant risk factors for DGF, such as donor age, donor type, and CIT,26 could not be included as covariates. These factors may therefore confound the observed associations, and the apparent predictive contribution of the selected features may be overestimated. Moreover, despite penalization and restricted model complexity, the small sample size may still affect model stability and risk overfitting. This limitation extends to model evaluation, and, while the model’s internal performance was promising (OOF AUC, 0.76; test AUC, 0.81), the small size of held-out test set reduces the precision of the performance estimate and makes it more sensitive to the train-test split.

Another important limitation is the potential protocol dependence of the measured variables, particularly with respect to urine recirculation and perfusate composition. Previous studies have shown that urine recirculation supports perfusate homeostasis and hemodynamic stability during NMP,20 which led to its inclusion in this study. However, by returning excreted solutes to the perfusion circuit, urine recirculation may alter subsequent solute concentrations and hemodynamic measurements.20,27 Moreover, perfusate composition, which varies between NMP protocols, has also been shown to significantly affect measured perfusion and injury parameters.28 Because these choices may influence the variables included in the model, its coefficients and thresholds should be considered specific to the protocol used in this study. Application in other centers would therefore require prospective external validation and may require protocol-specific recalibration or refitting. Nevertheless, the physiological axes represented by these predictors—urine production, oxygen handling, and solute handling—may remain relevant for prognostic assessment across protocols.

Exploratory comparisons involving additional features and transplant outcomes should be interpreted cautiously, with the main inferential focus of the article being the predictive model development. As no correction for multiple testing was applied, positive findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Additionally, because these comparisons used the available NMP cohort without a formal sample-size calculation, limited statistical power may have obscured genuine associations.

Finally, inflammatory activation in IRI may be more directly captured by tissue-based approaches and molecular profiling20-22 than by urinary solute markers alone. While these approaches are not available as real-time readouts during NMP, they could aid in biomarker validation.

CONCLUSIONS

In this study, higher venous Po2 at 60 min and lower cumulative urine production after 2 h of NMP were associated with DGF and used as real-time perfusion readouts. Urinary phosphate met the prespecified threshold for practical relevance and was retained as a complementary feature for the development of a 3-feature penalized logistic regression model. The model showed preliminary internal discrimination and warrants further evaluation as a candidate decision-support approach. However, the small sample, lack of confounder adjustment, and protocol dependence of the measurements mean that clinical application is not yet supported. External validation in larger cohorts and clinical decision-impact testing are needed to determine whether this approach could contribute to objective real-time kidney graft assessment during NMP.
