GDROM v2: An Inventory of Operation Variables Time Series and Rules for 2,017 Large Reservoirs across the CONUS
Authors: Zihan Zheng, Ximing Cai, Linshui Zhang, James Li, Yanan Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Reservoir operation plays a central role in water management, satisfying diverse human demands and simultaneously influencing hydrologic processes. Yet for most reservoirs, the lack of complete daily operation records and realistic operation rules constrains their representation in large-scale hydrological models. To address this, we present GDROM v2, a nationwide dataset of 2,017 reservoirs across the Contiguous United States (CONUS), building upon the original GDROM of 452 reservoirs. GDROM v2 provides (1) daily time series of inflow, release, and storage variables, integrated through data fusion of existing datasets and reconstructed estimates; (2) operation rules adopted from original GDROM for data-rich reservoirs and derived for data-limited reservoirs using transfer learning. Validation results show that the reconstructed variable time series reasonably agree with observations; derived operation rules outperform existing benchmark models across diverse data availability conditions. GDROM v2, publicly available via an open-data platform (Hydroshare.org), offers the largest collection of reservoir operation variables time series and realistic operation rules across the CONUS, providing a useful resource for hydrological modeling and water management studies.

Background & Summary

Reservoir operation serves multiple water management purposes such as water supply, flood control, hydropower generation, recreation, and navigation, while it also poses a major human interference to hydrologic processes via altering the streamflow regime1,2. Realistic representation of reservoir operation characterized by operation rules has become increasingly important in large-scale hydrological and land surface models, both for improving streamflow simulation, and enhancing reservoir operation purposes such as flood control, water supply, and hydro-energy production3–5. Nowadays, with the advances in machine learning, data-driven models have gained prominence in reservoir operation studies6–9. These models learn generalizable patterns from historical records and capture complex operator behaviors, often achieving higher accuracy than traditional physically-based models10. While effective models have been developed11,12, they require long-term, high-quality operation records with fine temporal resolutions (e.g., daily).

However, high-quality daily operation records are often fragmented, inconsistent, or entirely unavailable, which hinders the derivation of consistent rules. To handle the issue, two major datasets have been developed to date in the Contiguous United States (CONUS). ResOpsUS provides daily inflow, outflow, storage, and evaporative losses for 689 reservoirs spanning 1974–2020, which assembled from multiple federal, state, and local agencies including U.S. Geological Survey (USGS), U.S. Army Corps of Engineers (USACE), U.S. Bureau of Reclamation (USBR), Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA), California Data Exchange Center (CDEC), and others13. While providing invaluable first-hand records of reservoir operations, the dataset is subject to some limitations: many reservoirs lack complete records of storage, inflow, and release; for those with all three variables available, the values sometimes do not satisfy the basic water balance relation. The second dataset, Generic Data-Driven Reservoir Operation Models (GDROM) offers high-quality 15+ year daily inflow, release, and storage data for 452 reservoirs from agencies such as USACE, USBR, CDEC14. Since these two datasets were compiled for overlapping time periods using similar sources, they share a substantial number of reservoirs.

Due to the scarcity and inconsistency of daily operation records, it is difficult to derive a dataset of reservoir operation rules, which are required to guide reservoir routing in large-scale hydrological models and Earth system models. At present, two such datasets exist. The Inferred Storage Targets and Release Functions for CONUS (ISTARF-CONUS) provides operation rules for 1,930 reservoirs using a 19-parameter harmonic model; rules in ISTARF-CONUS emphasize its simplicity and scalability but may have limited accuracy and insufficient capacity to represent site-specific operations15. In contrast, GDROM provides reliable, generalizable, interpretable operation rules for 452 reservoirs that are compatible with rainfall-runoff processes14. These rules are derived by machine learning and classification tree techniques, and formulated as transparent if–then rules. Therefore, GDROMs are relatively highly data-demanding, restricting their coverage to reservoirs with sufficient long-term, high-quality records.

In summary, the major limitation of existing reservoir operation datasets lies in the limited number of reservoirs with long-term, high-quality daily operation records, which constrains the training of robust reservoir operation rules. To address this limitation, we present GDROM v2, a nationwide dataset covering daily operation data (when available) and derived operation rules for 2,017 reservoirs across the CONUS, building upon the original GDROM of 452 reservoirs. To overcome the scarcity of daily operation records, we apply a data fusion method to compile inflow, storage, and release using multiple data sources such as streamflow records from U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) gage stations16, remote sensing observation of reservoir surface area17 and reservoir bathymetric storage estimates18. To improve the representation of operation rules for data-limited reservoirs, we further extend the established GDROM framework with a transfer learning strategy by identifying reference data-rich reservoirs and inferring operation rules for target data-limited reservoirs.

GDROM v2 advances reservoir research and modeling on two complementary fronts. First, it provides the most comprehensive collection of reservoir operation variables time series currently available for the CONUS, approximately covering all reservoirs with a surface area larger than 1 km2, thereby enabling the development and training of more robust models. Second, by extending operation rules from data-rich to data-limited reservoirs, GDROM v2 expands the applicability of data-driven reservoir modeling, providing a realistic alternative to the oversimplified reservoir schemes widely used in large-scale hydrological models. Together, these contributions establish GDROM v2 as a critical resource for hydrological modeling, water management studies, and future extensions to reservoirs around the world.

Methods

The construction of GDROM v2 follows a two-step methodology: (1) integrating daily time series of inflow, release, and storage variables through data fusion of existing datasets and reconstructed estimates; (2) deriving reservoir operation rules for data-limited reservoirs using transfer learning based on the rules derived from reference data-rich reservoirs that are analogous to the target data-limited reservoirs. The detailed procedures are described in the following sections.

Step 1: Daily operation variables time series compilation

Reliable reservoir operation modeling requires long-term, high-quality daily inflow, release, and storage records. In this step, we compile daily inflow, release, and storage time series using a data fusion approach that integrates multiple data sources into a unified dataset. Data fusion methods are widely used in hydrological and water resource studies to combine ground-based observations, remote sensing data, and model outputs, providing a more comprehensive and consistent representation of system states and processes19–21. In this study, our data fusion framework integrates two types of data: (1) observations collected from existing public datasets and agency records; and (2) reconstructed variables derived by combining streamflow gage observations, remote sensing reservoir surface area, and bathymetric profiles.

Existing dataset merging

We first adopt data from the original GDROM14 (452 reservoirs), ResOpsUS13 (679 reservoirs), and USACE WM23 (about 489 reservoirs accessible). The original GDROM compiles high-quality daily inflow, release, and storage records for 452 CONUS reservoirs with long-term observations (over 15 years). ResOpsUS provides inflows, outflows, changes in storage, and evaporation losses, spanning 1974–2020 and assembled directly from reservoir operators. In addition, we incorporate 288 reservoirs from USACE WM23, which extends the data length of existing GDROM and ResOpsUS reservoirs and adds new reservoirs that were not included in either dataset. To ensure consistency, we adopt GRanD_ID24 as common reservoir identifiers given that both the original GDROM and ResOpsUS already use the identifiers. While USACE WM does not use GRanD_ID, we assign the USACE reservoirs GRanD_ID by matching reservoirs in the two data sources via a geographic information system (GIS) program. Reservoirs not listed in GRanD are assigned placeholder IDs beginning with 10000, including 43 reservoirs from the original GDROM, and 68 reservoirs from USACE WM. Further, we compile a comprehensive metadata table to keep key attributes of all reservoirs, including geographic location, storage capacity, and primary operation purposes, etc. Most of these attributes are obtained from GRanD v1.324, while for reservoirs not listed in GRanD, supplementary information is retrieved from the NID database25.

Inflow, release, and storage reconstruction

Reservoir inflow and release are reconstructed by linking USGS streamflow gages to the primary inlet and outlet streams of each reservoir. Figure 1a provides a schematic diagram illustrating how USGS gage records are mapped to reservoirs: streamflow at the nearest upstream gage (e.g., gage A) is taken as reservoir inflow, while streamflow at the nearest downstream gage (e.g., gage E) is taken as reservoir release. If multiple upper/down streams exist, the streamflow records from all the corresponding gages are aggregated as the total inflow/release of the target reservoirs. In addition, some inflow streams can be monitored by multiple gages (e.g., gages B and C for Inlet 3) and the inflow will be aggregated over the multiple flow records. For release estimation, if a downstream gage receives discharges from the target reservoir, as well as an intermediate tributary (e.g., Outlet 7 with gages E and F, the reservoir release will be set to the difference between the streamflow values (e.g., gage E flow minus gage F flow). If no gage station can be found for a minor inlet/outlet stream (e.g., Inlet 4 or Outlet 8), the associated inflow/release is neglected; if a reservoir’s major inlet/outlet is ungauged, the reservoir’s inflow/release is treated as missing. For some cases, an upstream reservoir (Ru) or a downstream reservoir (Rd) exists within the proximity of a target reservoir, and hydrological connection exists between them, the release/inflow from the upstream/downstream reservoir will be taken as part of inflow/release of the target reservoir.Fig. 1The framework for reconstructing reservoir inflow and release based on upstream and downstream gage configurations. To support the above reconstruction procedures, a GIS-based reservoir-stream-gage system is developed to detect stream-reservoir connectivity by refining the Lake-TopoCat with NHDPlusV2, and mapping USGS streamflow gage stations (parameter code: 00060) to their corresponding streams. Figure 1b illustrates an example of the Red Bluff Dam (GRanD_ID: 1242) and its surrounding streams and gage stations. The primary inlet and outlet locations and USGS streamflow gages in the proximity are marked in the diagram. This diagram highlights the spatial relationship between the reservoir and adjacent gage stations, which are used to derive estimates of inflow and release. Particularly, to map USGS streamflow records to inflow or release of a target reservoir, we conduct numerous trial-and-error tests, and finally take a 10-kilometer proximity threshold between the USGS gage stations and the inflow/release streams of the reservoir. For cases where the stream network contains dense branching tributaries, a more conservative threshold (e.g., <5 km) is applied.

Meanwhile, daily storage time series is reconstructed by integrating remote sensing lake area data from SARAH-CONUS17 and the bathymetric profiles from the GRDL dataset18. Sub-weekly surface area time series from SARAH-CONUS are first converted into storage time series using GRDL bathymetric profiles; the resulting sub-weekly storage data are then resampled to daily values using linear interpolation. It is reasonable to assume that the sub-weekly storage series are smooth and continuous, and the linear interpolation is a robust choice for reconstructing daily values.

Via the reconstruction procedures stated above, we compile daily inflow and release time series from 1980 to 2024 for 1002 reservoirs, among which 199 have both variables, 695 release only, and 108 inflow only. In addition, daily storage time series are compiled for 1,590 reservoirs from 2016 to 2024. Some of these reconstruction time series overlapping with existing datasets are used for validation, while the non-overlapping ones provide complementary information to expand overall data coverage.

Data cleaning and merging

The quality of data is critical for building reliable reservoir operation models. However, any existing datasets may contain errors, missing entries, or unrealistic patterns due to sensor malfunctions, reporting issues, or data processing errors. To address these issues, we begin with essential preprocessing across all data sources, including formatting standardization, error checking, and outlier removal, ensuring consistency and completeness across variables. In particular, for reservoirs with abnormal values (e.g., extreme outliers, prolonged constant values, or unreasonable negative values) or inconsistencies in the same variable across multiple data sources (i.e., the same variable appears in different datasets but shows substantial disagreement), we first conduct visual inspection of time series by generating diagnostic plots for each reservoir. We prioritize reviewing time series that show sudden shifts (assessing whether such changes align with seasonal or historical flow patterns) or periods of abnormally constant values. Notably, constant inflow values are generally considered abnormal since natural runoff processes inherently fluctuate, while constant release or storage values may be reasonable due to human-controlled operations. Negative flow or storage values are replaced with zero if such instances are rare; otherwise, the entire time series for that variable will be excluded. Further, we conduct visual comparisons across multiple data sources to assess consistency and reliability. For variables with data available from multiple sources, we evaluate their temporal patterns and select the most reliable records. If two sources provide non-overlapping segments with consistent trends, we merge them to extend the length of the time series.

Mass balance check and reconstruction

Theoretically, for any reservoirs, inflow, release, and storage variable must satisfy the mass balance relation at any period. This fundamental relationship can be used to conduct further data processing. In real-world practices, measured inflow data are often unavailable or unreliable due to seepage to or discharge from groundwater, precipitation to and evaporation from reservoir surface, or incomplete measurements6. Due to these limitations, the reconstructed inflow time series often fails to satisfy the mass balance relationship. To avoid this issue, we first check whether the mass balance is matched for reservoirs with all inflow, release and storage time series. Reservoir categorization

A comprehensive inventory of available daily reservoir operation variables time series is established for 2,017 reservoirs across the CONUS. Based on data availability and quality, all reservoirs are classified into three categories:Data-Rich Reservoir (Res-R): This category includes 748 reservoirs with more than five years of continuous daily records with high-quality standards, specifically with minimal missing values, consistent structure, and availability of inflow, release, and storage data.Data-Limited Reservoir (Res-L): This category includes 174 reservoirs with shorter record durations (one to five years) or lower data quality, such as discontinuous time series or the presence of negative values that have been cleaned.Data-Missing Reservoir (Res-M): This category includes 1,095 reservoirs for which only one of the three variables (i.e., inflow, release, and storage) is available; or two variables are available but do not overlap temporally; or none of the variables has a continuous time series of at least one year. In such cases, reconstruction via the water balance equation is not feasible. Nevertheless, any available reservoir data, even only with one variable, can still be useful for the assessment of streamflow alterations and reservoir operation modeling.

Step 2: Reservoir operation rule training

To handle varying data availability, the original GDROM is modified to support rule extraction for the three categories (Res-R, Res-L, Res-M) tailored to different data availability conditions. For Res-Rs, which have high-quality, long-duration daily records, operation rules are directly trained using the primary GDROM derivation framework6; each GDROM consists of (1) Operation modules derived from Hidden Markov Decision Tree (HMDT) for release simulation, and (2) Module application conditions derived from the Classification and Regression Tree (CART). In contrast, for Res-Ls and Res-Ms, direct training of GDROM models is infeasible, as short or incomplete historical records fail to capture the full range of hydrological and operation conditions required for extracting representative rule sets. Consequently, key operation behaviors—such as responses to drought and flood events, or seasonal transitions—may be absent in these records. To address this limitation, transfer learning is well-suited for situations with heterogeneous data availability because it leverages knowledge from data-rich sources to enhance model performance in data-poor target reservoirs26,27. In our application (Fig. 2), each Res-L or Res-M (defined as a target reservoir) is matched to a similar Res-R (defined as an analogous reservoir) through an analogous reservoir identification method as detailed below. Both trained modules and application conditions of the analogous reservoir are used as learning sources for the derivation of GDROMs for Res-Ls and Res-Ms.Fig. 2Schematic of the transfer learning framework for deriving GDROMs for Res-L and Res-M (target reservoir). Analogous reservoir identification

To enable the GDROM application to Res-Ls and Res-Ms, we develop an Analogous Reservoir Identification Method that systematically matches each Res-L or Res-M to a unique data-rich Res-R from 300 reference reservoirs with long-term, high-quality operation variables time series, as well as acceptable performance in the original GDROM. The matching is based on a composite similarity score that integrates three components: operation purpose, geographical distance, and storage capacity, normalized to the range of 0–1. We test four weight schemes for the three components (i.e., 33% each; 50%:25%:25%; 25%:50%:25%; and 25%:25%:50%) and find that the 50%:25%:25% scheme performs slightly better. This is consistent with the fact that reservoir operation rules are associated with operation purpose. We select the Res-R with the highest similarity score as the analogous reservoir. Transfer learning for Res-L

As shown in Fig. 2 (left), we apply a transfer learning strategy in which each target Res-L is matched to the most similar reference Res-R using the Analogous Reservoir Identification Method described above. Then, the pretrained GDROM model of the matched Res-R, including both the HMDT and CART, is transferred and fine-tuned for the target Res-L.

For HMDT transfer, all HMDT modules of the reference reservoirs are represented using five basic types of operation modules28, as defined below:Constant: The release remains constant regardless of inflow or storage conditions.Inflow-Constant: The release follows a piecewise constant relationship based on inflow thresholds.Inflow-Linear: The release follows a linear relationship with inflow.Storage-Constant: The release follows a piecewise constant relationship based on storage thresholds.Storage-Nonlinear: The release is nearly constant at a low storage level and then increases approximately in a linear form when the storage exceeds a threshold.

These five basic types of operation modules serve as assembling “Lego pieces” in the transfer process; each module type retains a fixed structure and only a few parameters require calibration, including (1) threshold values (e.g., inflow or storage thresholds) and (2) release parameters (e.g., constant values or slope coefficients). In this way, short-term time series (typically 1 to 5 years) available for Res-Ls can be used to estimate reasonable values of these parameters via calibration.

For CART transfer, the process is composed of (1) transferring the original CART of a reference Res-R to a target Res-L, and (2) fine-tuning CART threshold parameters that define decision-making conditions (e.g., from Storage ≤ 0.48 to Storage ≤ 0.52). The overall tree structure, including its topology, splitting variables, and branching structure is preserved, and only the threshold parameters are adjusted by using the proportion of samples (i.e., the data points assigned to a certain decision-making node) for each input variable (storage, inflow, PDSI, or DOY) that falls into each branch of the original CART. For example, as shown in Fig. 3b, if 2000 samples with condition Storage ≤ 0.48 and 1650 with condition Storage > 0.48, the sample proportion with the threshold is calculated as 2000/(2000 + 1650) ≈ 0.55. This proportion is adopted to the target Res-L to determine a new threshold of a variable.Fig. 3Transfer process of CART structures from Res-R to Res-L and Res-M. Technical Validation

To evaluate the quality of the GDROM v2 dataset including the reconstructed input variables and derived operation rules, we conduct two validation tasks: (1) assessing the consistency between reconstructed input variables (inflow, release, and storage) and corresponding observations; (2) evaluating the performance of GDROM operation rules derived from different data availability conditions. The detailed validation results are presented below.

Quality assessment of reconstructed time series

For robust comparison, we selected only reservoirs with overlapping records, including 36 reservoirs with inflow data, 265 with release data, and 267 with storage data. The results of the validation are displayed in Fig. 6. Reconstructed release series demonstrate excellent performance across all three evaluation metrics. Over 90% of reservoirs show KGE values greater than 0.75, indicating accurate alignment in not only correlation but also mean and variability. Similarly, PCC exceeds 0.9 for most cases, confirming a strong agreement in temporal trends; NSE values are above 0.8, reflecting the model’s ability to capture overall system dynamics. This high level of accuracy is mainly attributed to two reasons: (1) most reservoirs just have one outlet and only one release value needs to be reconstructed, thereby reducing reconstruction uncertainty; (2) USGS gage stations are typically located at or nearby those outlets, i.e., the reservoir releases are well measured.Fig. 6Performance of release, inflow, and storage reconstruction using gage-matching against ResOpsUS records, evaluated by KGE, PCC, and NSE.

Performance of release, inflow, and storage reconstruction using gage-matching against ResOpsUS records, evaluated by KGE, PCC, and NSE.

For inflow reconstruction, the model also demonstrates satisfactory performance, though with slightly lower accuracy compared to release. Approximately 80% of reservoirs achieve PCC values greater than 0.8, indicating reliable reproduction of temporal patterns. Meanwhile, around 60% of reservoirs exceed the threshold of KGE and NSE (0.5 is set for the threshold of both metrics), suggesting moderate agreement in both magnitude and variability. Compared to release reconstruction, low performance can be primarily attributed to two issues: (1) Reservoirs often receive inflows from multiple, spatially dispersed inlets, which increases the complexity and difficulty of the reconstruction process; (2) USGS gage stations are less frequently installed at upstream inlets, particularly for small tributaries. Thus, many of these inflow sources lack direct measurements, leading to data gaps that introduce significant errors and uncertainty into the inflow reconstruction process.

For storage reconstruction, the model demonstrates strong consistency in temporal trends, with Pearson’s correlation coefficients (PCC) generally exceeding 0.8, indicating that the reconstructed storage series effectively capture the seasonal and interannual trends. However, KGE and NSE values are more dispersed across reservoirs, reflecting greater uncertainty in reproducing the absolute magnitude of observed storage, an issue also reported in the previous study6. This uncertainty can be attributed to several factors: (1) Data source errors, including inaccuracies in satellite-derived lake surface area estimates and area-to-storage conversions based on bathymetric profiles that are simplified and/or involve strong assumptions; (2) interpolation errors, as the use of linear interpolation between irregular observations can introduce temporal artifacts; (3) systematic discrepancies in storage definitions, which represent the most significant source of uncertainty. For example, some records include dead storage while others do not, leading to inconsistencies in the baseline storage values across datasets. Despite these limitations, the consistently high correlation values confirm that the seasonal dynamics of storage are well represented, and the reconstructed series remain sufficiently reliable for estimating inflow, storage and release.

Quality assessment of operation rules

The performance of GDROMs (representing operation rules) generated for Res-R, Res-L, and Res-M reservoirs is compared against two widely used benchmark models: HANA5 and WISS38. To ensure consistency, the same 300 reference Res-Rs used in GDROM transfer are selected for evaluation. To evaluate the feasibility of GDROMs for Res-L and Res-M, we deliberately imposed data-limited conditions: GDROMs for Res-L are fine-tuned using only two-year (common data length for Res-L); while GDROMs for Res-M are generated solely based on reference reservoirs, with no time series data involved in training. GDROMs for Res-R, HANA, and WISS under data-rich conditions are trained using the first 80% of each reservoir’s time series; the remaining 20% is reserved as a common validation set to evaluate model performance across different methods.

The evaluation is conducted under two simulation settings: (1) Simulating with real storage (i.e. observed storage) (realS), where daily releases are simulated using real storage, and Release_realS is used as a baseline to assess the model’s release simulation accuracy; (2) simulating with iterated storage (iterS), where releases are simulated by GDROMs. This setting includes both Release_iterS and Storage_iterS to evaluate the model’s robustness when all variables are simulated dynamically. As shown in Fig. 7, GDROMs for Res-R perform the best, achieving low errors and tight distributions across both simulation settings (realS and iterS) and over all three evaluation metrics (nRMSE, nTRMSE High, nTRMSE Low). This demonstrates that the model can stably and accurately emulate reservoir operations when rich data is available. While the overall performance is strong, slightly higher errors are observed in nTRMSE High for release simulation (both Release_realS and Release_iterS), which reflect the challenge of simulating high-release conditions (e.g. flood events). Nevertheless, GDROMs for Res-R still significantly outperform the benchmark models (HANA and WISS) under the two settings, showing the advantages of data-driven rule extraction and underscoring the potential of data-driven models for representing reservoir operations in large-scale hydrological models.Fig. 7Comparison of GDROM performance across data availability levels and simulation settings using three normalized error metrics against benchmarking models.

For Res-Ls, although GDROMs are fine-tuned using only two-year data and rely on five basic types of “Lego” pieces for operation modules, they still deliver consistently strong performance under both the realS and iterS settings and generally outperform the benchmark models. Compared to GDROMs for Res-R, the performance gap is smallest during high-flow periods (nTRMSE High), likely because the operations during the high flow periods follow prescribed rules across reservoirs. However, slight declines are observed in overall accuracy (nRMSE) and for low-flow periods (nTRMSE Low), when reservoir operation behaviors tend to be more site-specific and strongly influenced by local drought-management practices. Further, Storage_iterS tends to exhibit higher errors, which is expected due to the accumulation of simulation errors over time. Overall, although the performance with Res-L declines compared to Res-R GDROMs, the results confirm the effectiveness of the similarity-based transfer learning strategy and demonstrate the practicality of the “Lego” pieces operation modules under data-limited conditions.

GDROMs for Res-M achieve performance that is generally comparable to the benchmark models. Notably, the benchmark models are trained on the complete time series of each reservoir, whereas the Res-M GDROMs are derived entirely from their reference Res-R reservoirs via transfer learning without using any site-specific time series. This means the framework and parameters transferred from analogous reservoirs can effectively play a role like those derived directly from historical data. To be specific, compared to HANA and WISS, GDROMs for Res-M under iterS achieve slightly better results than the benchmark models, whereas under realS their performance is slightly worse. Although counterintuitive, this may be because the simulated storage series under the iterS setting have strong temporal continuity, while the real storage in realS setting may not, which can lead to more stable and internally consistent simulations. A similar phenomenon was also shown with STARF-CONUS15, where the extrapolated method (STARFITx) achieved better performance when using simulated storage (S-SIM) compared to observed storage (S-OBS). Meanwhile, across the three evaluation metrics, the best results are observed for normal hydrologic conditions, which suggests that under extreme conditions (either high or low flow), the similarity in operation behavior between reservoirs tends to weaken. Notably, error distributions for Res-M are more dispersed compared to the benchmarking models. This variability highlights the dependence of Res-M performance on how well a target reservoir matches its reference reservoir especially in terms of operation purpose, location, and capacity.

Overall, the derived operation rules (i.e., GDROMs) show robust generalization capability under different data availability conditions. For Res-L and Res-M, their GDROMs underscore both potential and limitation of using a transfer learning method, pointing to future needs in better matching between target and reference reservoirs matching and exploring improved transfer learning approaches under data-scarce conditions.

Usage Notes

Before using GDROM v229, users are encouraged to review the Methods section of this paper to understand how daily inflow, release, and storage time series are compiled, and how reservoir operation rules are derived using a transfer learning method, which helps users understand the rationale and limitations of the datasets (i.e., reservoir inflow, storage, and release time series and operation rules represented by GDROMs).

Users may also review the readme.md file, which provides detailed guidance on dataset organization, available data versions, and the application of the operation rules. We provide three forms of operation records: the collected data retains original records, the cleaned data provides quality-controlled series for analysis, and the normalized data are prepared for model training. The operation rules are stored as plain-text files, making them readily integrable into standard analysis workflows (e.g., Python, R). In addition, GDROM v2 includes Python scripts for training and applying GDROMs. Via these scripts, users can customize reservoirs of their interest and automatically download the relevant data, which eases the procedures to couple GDROMs into their hydrological models; via the scripts, users can also compare GDROM outputs against other observational or modeled datasets.
