Paired Explicit Relaxation Runge-Kutta Methods: Entropy-Conservative and Entropy-Stable High-Order Optimized Multirate Time Integration Journal: ??
Daniel Doehring, Hendrik Ranocha, and Manuel Torrilhon

Abstract
We present novel entropy-conservative and entropy-stable multirate Runge-Kutta methods based on Paired Explicit Runge-Kutta (P-ERK) schemes with relaxation for conservation laws and related systems of partial differential equations. Optimized schemes up to fourth-order of accuracy are derived and validated in terms of order of consistency, conservation of linear invariants, and entropy conservation/stability. We demonstrate the effectiveness of these P-ERRK methods when combined with a high-order, entropy-conservative/stable discontinuous Galerkin spectral element method on unstructured meshes. The Paired Explicit Relaxation Runge-Kutta methods (P-ERRK) are readily implemented for partitioned semidiscretizations arising from problems with equation-based scale separation such as non-uniform meshes. We highlight that the relaxation approach acts as a time-limiting technique which improves the nonlinear stability and thus robustness of the multirate schemes. The P-ERRK methods are applied to a range of problems, ranging from compressible Euler over compressible Navier-Stokes to the visco-resistive magnetohydrodynamics equations in two and three spatial dimensions. For each test case, we compare computational load and runtime to standalone relaxed Runge-Kutta methods which are outperformed by factors up to four. All results can be reproduced using a publicly available repository.

Introduction

The concept of entropy is of pivotal importance in both analysis and numerical discretization of hyperbolic conservation laws and related systems of partial differential equations.
This insight dates back to the seminal works by Oleĭnik
Oleĭnik 1959
, Lax

Lax 1957
,
and Kružkov
Kružkov 1970
who realized that entropy provides the sought guidance to resolve the non-uniqueness issue caused by shocks.
Employing entropy principles allowed the aforementioned authors to answer questions originally raised by Riemann, Rankine, and Hugoniot which remained open for more than 70 years.
Besides being an indispensable tool for the analysis of conservation laws
Lax 1973
,
Dafermos 2005
, entropy is at its heart a physical, thermodynamic quantity.
All physical processes are irreversible, i.e., carry an inherent direction and thus lead to an increase of the physical entropy.
A prominent example from hydrodynamics is the entropy increase across shocks
Courant and Friedrichs 1976
.
Another notoriously complicated hydrodynamic feature are scale-spanning turbulent flows which continue to challenge various branches of science and engineering.
Recent studies on entropy-conservative/entropy-stable numerical schemes
Gassner et al. 2016
,
Sjögreen and Yee 2018
,
Manzanero et al. 2020
,
Rojas et al. 2021
,
Chan et al. 2022
have shown how entropy-conservative/entropy-stable schemes can be used to obtain more robust simulations for under-resolved turbulent flows.
Thus, to accurately simulate such and even more intricate phenomena, entropy can and should be employed as a guiding principle in addition to the traditional balance laws governing mass, momentum, and energy.

The construction of entropy-conservative/entropy-stable finite volume schemes has been pioneered by Tadmor
Tadmor 1986
,
Tadmor 1987
and was since then extended to a variety of high-order accurate discretization schemes, see for instance
Fjordholm et al. 2012
,
Fisher and Carpenter 2013
,
Gassner 2013
,
Carpenter et al. 2016
and references therein.
In this work we focus on the discontinuous Galerkin spectral element method method, i.e., a nodal Discontinuous Galerkin method
Hesthaven and Warburton 2007
with spatially collocated interpolation/quadrature points
Black 1999
,
Kopriva 2009
.
These in principle arbitrarily high-order accurate schemes achieve entropy conservation/stability by computing the volume integral with summation-by-parts operators and flux-differencing techniques
Gassner et al. 2016
,
Fisher and Carpenter 2013
,
Gassner 2013
,
Chen and Shu 2017
,
Chan 2018
.
In addition, to conserve entropy, inviscid surface flux functions
Chandrashekar 2013
,
Winters and Gassner 2016
,
Ranocha 2018
,
Ranocha 2020
must be used.
Entropy stability however, i.e., monotone decay of the mathematical entropy, is guaranteed for instance by standard dissipative Lax-Friedrichs type surface fluxes
Tadmor 2003
.

In the previous paragraph we briefly outlined the building blocks of an entropy-conservative/entropy-stable
spatial
discretization.
To obtain a (fully) discrete entropy-conservative/entropy-stable scheme for an unsteady problem, it remains to discretize the
temporal
derivative in a way that preserves this property.
Examples of entropy-conservative/entropy-stable space-time methods can be found in
Hiltebrand and Mishra 2014
,
Friedrich et al. 2019
,
Gaburro et al. 2023
.
If the method of lines technique
Cockburn and Shu 2001
is employed, different techniques to ensure the conservation of nonlinear functionals of the ordinary differential equation system (such as the mathematical entropy) need to be employed.
Options for this are projection-based methods
[
Hairer et al. 2006
, Chapter IV.4]
,
Calvo et al. 2010
,
Kojima 2016
,
Najafian and Vermeire 2025
, the incremental direction technique
Calvo et al. 2006
, or the relaxation methodology developed in
Ketcheson 2019
,
Ranocha et al. 2020
.
As pointed out in
Kang and Constantinescu 2022
,
Ranocha et al. 2020a
,
Ranocha et al. 2020b
, a relaxation approach to ensure conservation of energy and other quadratic invariants was already proposed in
Sanz-Serna 1982
,
Sanz-Serna and Manoranjan 1983
.
Therein, the authors consider a modified Leapfrog scheme and apply it to the Korteweg–de Vries and nonlinear Schrödinger equation.
The relaxation idea was also presented in
Dekker and Verwer 1984
.

In this work we combine the relaxation methodology
Ketcheson 2019
,
Ranocha et al. 2020
,
Ranocha et al. 2020b
and the Paired Explicit Runge-Kutta methods
Vermeire 2019
,
Nasab and Vermeire 2022
,
Doehring et al. 2024
to obtain an optimized multirate time integration scheme that is entropy-conservative/entropy-stable, which we accordingly label Paired Explicit Relaxation Runge-Kutta.
The closest related work to the present study is
Kang and Constantinescu 2022
, where the authors extend the second-order accurate multirate Runge-Kutta method from
Constantinescu and Sandu 2007
with the relaxation methodology.
Therein, however, the authors consider besides pure ordinary differential equations only the Burgers equation and do not quantify speed-up of the relaxed multirate methods over the baseline relaxed time integration methods.
In contrast, we demonstrate in this work speed-up and reduced computational effort for up to fourth-order discretizations of the compressible Navier-Stokes-Fourier, visco-resistive Magnetohydrodynamics and inviscid Euler equations.

The paper is organized as follows:
We begin by briefly sketching out the considered partial differential equation systems, Paired Explicit Runge-Kutta methods, the notion of the (global) solution entropy, and the relaxation approach in
Section

2
.
Then, we proceed to the construction of Paired Explicit Relaxation Runge-Kutta methods in
Section

3
which includes a detailed discussion on how the relaxation approach can act as a time-limiting technique to improve the robustness of the multirate scheme.
The obtained methods are thoroughly validated in
Section

4
where conservation of entropy and linear invariants is demonstrated without impairing the accuracy, i.e., order of consistency, of the schemes.
In
Section

5
, the Paired Explicit Relaxation Runge-Kutta schemes are applied to a laminar, effectively incompressible flow around the SD7003 airfoil, a Mach
0.5

For each test case, we demonstrate improved performance of the Paired Explicit Relaxation Runge-Kutta schemes over single-rate relaxed Runge-Kutta methods.
Furthermore, we showcase increased robustness of the relaxed multirate Paired Explicit Relaxation Runge-Kutta schemes over the standard Paired Explicit Runge-Kutta methods, especially for under-resolved simulations.
We conclude the paper in
Section

6
.

2.2
Paired Explicit Runge-Kutta Methods

Paired Explicit Runge-Kutta methods have been developed in
Vermeire 2019
and consecutively extended to third-
Nasab and Vermeire 2022
and fourth-order
Doehring et al. 2024
of consistency.
Furthermore, an embedded scheme based on the second-order method has been proposed in
Vermeire 2023
which enables error-based timestep control.

The central idea of the Paired Explicit Runge-Kutta methods is to pair Runge-Kutta schemes with different domains of stability and computational cost in an efficient way.
These schemes belong to the class of Partitioned Runge-Kutta methods
Rentrop 1985
,
Bruder et al. 1988
,
Günther et al. 2001
and are naturally applicable to semidiscretizations (
2.2
) which may be partitioned according to different characteristic timescales:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{U}(t_{0})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{U}_{0},

(2.4a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{U}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{U}^{(1)}(t)\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{U}^{(R)}(t)\end{pmatrix}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{F}^{(1)}\big(t,\boldsymbol{U}^{(1)}(t),\dots,\boldsymbol{U}^{(R)}(t)\big)\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{F}^{(R)}\big(t,\boldsymbol{U}^{(1)}(t),\dots,\boldsymbol{U}^{(R)}(t)\big)\end{pmatrix}=\boldsymbol{F}\big(t,\boldsymbol{U}(t)\big)\>.

(2.4b)

Here,
R
denotes the number of
partitions/levels
.
Based on the local characteristic speed

\nu(\boldsymbol{x}_{m})\sim\max_{i=1,\dots,d}\frac{(k+1)\cdot\rho_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}_{m})}{h(\boldsymbol{x}_{m})}>0,\quad m=1,\dots,M

(2.5)

the unknowns
\left\{U_{m}\right\}_{m=1,\dots,M}
are assigned to different partitions
r=1,\dots,R
which are integrated with different schemes.
In (
2.5
)

denotes the number of spatial dimensions,
h
is a measure for the smallest characteristic cell size,

is the Discontinuous Galerkin solution polynomial degree, and
\rho_{i}
is the spectral radius of the Jacobian of the directional flux
\boldsymbol{f}_{i}
:

J_{i}\coloneqq\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{f}_{i}}{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}

(2.6)

cf. (
2.1b

In the following, the superscript

denotes quantities corresponding to the
r

These may be a subset of unknowns

\boldsymbol{F}^{(r)}
.
Note that for the sake of readability we oftentimes pad the vectors

of the full system (
2.2

Evaluations of the corresponding right-hand side

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{F}^{(r)}\Biggl(\underbrace{t_{n}+c_{i}^{(r)}\Delta t}_{\eqqcolon t_{n,i}},\underbrace{\boldsymbol{U}_{n}+\Delta t\sum_{j=1}^{S}\sum_{k=1}^{R}a_{i,j}^{(k)}\boldsymbol{K}_{j}^{(k)}}_{\eqqcolon\boldsymbol{U}_{n,i}}\Biggr),\quad i=1,\dots,S;\>\>r=1,\dots,R

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{U}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{U}_{n}+\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{S}\sum_{r=1}^{R}b_{i}^{(r)}\boldsymbol{K}_{i}^{(r)}\>,

When integrating (
2.2

2.3
The Notion of Entropy

In the context of this work, we are interested in ordinary differential equation systems (
2.2
) with states
\boldsymbol{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
with standard Euclidian inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and norm.
Additionally, we assume a smooth convex global entropy functional

H:\mathbb{R}^{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

(2.13)

whose temporal evolution is tied to a physical meaningful solution of (
2.2
) and thus also (
2.1
).
By the chain rule, the evolution over time of
H(\boldsymbol{U})
is given by

\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}H\big(\boldsymbol{U}(t)\big)=\left\langle\frac{\partial H(\boldsymbol{U})}{\partial\boldsymbol{U}},\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\boldsymbol{U}(t)\right\rangle\overset{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:PartitionedODESys2}}{=}\left\langle\frac{\partial H(\boldsymbol{U})}{\partial\boldsymbol{U}},\boldsymbol{F}\big(t,\boldsymbol{U}(t)\big)\right\rangle\>.

(2.14)

Entropy-conservative systems (e.g. appropriately discretized Euler equations) thus satisfy

\forall\>\boldsymbol{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{M},\>t\in[0,T]:\quad\left\langle\frac{\partial H(\boldsymbol{U})}{\partial\boldsymbol{U}},\boldsymbol{F}(t,\boldsymbol{U})\right\rangle=0

(2.15)

with discrete equivalent

H(\boldsymbol{U}_{n+1})=H(\boldsymbol{U}_{n})=H(\boldsymbol{U}_{0})\quad\>\forall\>n\in\mathbb{N}\>.

(2.16)

For entropy-dissipative systems (e.g. correspondingly discretized Navier-Stokes-Fourier equations) these equalities are replaced by inequalities, usually by the convention that the (mathematical) entropy decreases over time, i.e.,

\left\langle\frac{\partial H(\boldsymbol{U})}{\partial\boldsymbol{U}},\boldsymbol{F}(t,\boldsymbol{U})\right\rangle\leq 0\quad\Rightarrow\quad H(\boldsymbol{U}_{n+1})\leq H(\boldsymbol{U}_{n})\leq H(\boldsymbol{U}_{0})\quad\>\forall\>n\in\mathbb{N}\>.

(2.17)

In this work we are concerned with the compressible flows of ideal gases described by the Euler, Navier-Stokes-Fourier, or magnetohydrodynamics equations.
For these systems, the mathematical entropy
s
as a function of the continuous conserved variables
\boldsymbol{u}
is given by

s(\boldsymbol{u})\coloneqq-\underbrace{\rho}_{\equiv u_{1}}\cdot s_{\text{therm}}(\boldsymbol{u})=-\rho\cdot\log\left(\frac{p(\boldsymbol{u})}{\rho^{\gamma}}\right)\>,

(2.18)

which is then integrated over the spatial domain
\Omega
to obtain the total entropy
\eta
:

\eta(t)\coloneqq\eta\big(\boldsymbol{u}(t,\boldsymbol{x})\big)=\int_{\Omega}s\big(\boldsymbol{u}(t,\boldsymbol{x})\big)\,\text{d}\boldsymbol{x}\>.

(2.19)

Note that we denote with
\eta:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
the continuous equivalent of the discrete global entropy
H:\mathbb{R}^{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.

2.4
Relaxation Runge-Kutta Methods

The term Relaxation Runge-Kutta method was coined in
Ketcheson 2019
for Runge-Kutta methods whose final update step (
2.7c
) is scaled by the
relaxation parameter

\gamma_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
:

\boldsymbol{U}_{n+1}(\gamma_{n})=\boldsymbol{U}_{n}+\Delta t\gamma_{n}\sum_{i=1}^{S}b_{i}\boldsymbol{K}_{i}\>.

(2.20)

Recall that
\boldsymbol{K}_{i}
denotes the
i
-th stage of the Runge-Kutta method, i.e.,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{K}_{i}=\boldsymbol{F}\left(t_{n,i},\boldsymbol{U}_{n,i}\right),\quad i=1,\dots,S

(2.21)

with intermediate state
\boldsymbol{U}_{n,i}=\boldsymbol{U}_{n}+\Delta t\sum_{j=1}^{S}a_{i,j}\boldsymbol{K}_{j}
and time
t_{n,i}=t_{n}+c_{i}\Delta t
, cf.
Eq.

2.7b
.

The introduction of this relaxation parameter allows to satisfy exact entropy conservation, i.e.,

H\big(\boldsymbol{U}_{n+1}(\gamma_{n})\big)\overset{!}{=}H(\boldsymbol{U}_{n})+\gamma_{n}\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{S}b_{i}\left\langle\frac{\partial H(\boldsymbol{U}_{n,i})}{\partial\boldsymbol{U}_{n,i}},\boldsymbol{K}_{i}\right\rangle\>.

(2.22)

It is worthwhile to examine (
2.22
) for a trivial function
\boldsymbol{H}
, for instance
\boldsymbol{H}(\boldsymbol{U})=I\boldsymbol{U}
where
I
is the identity matrix
I\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
.
Thus, for a single component
k
of
\boldsymbol{U}
we have
H_{k}(\boldsymbol{U})=U^{(k)}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\delta_{km}U^{(m)}
with Kronecker delta
\delta_{km}
.
In this case (
2.22
) corresponds to the well-known linear-invariant (i.e., mass) conservation property of Runge-Kutta methods, as for every component
k
of
\boldsymbol{U}
we have

\displaystyle H_{k}\big(\boldsymbol{U}_{n+1}(\gamma_{n})\big)=U_{n+1}^{(k)}(\gamma_{n})

\displaystyle=U_{n}^{(k)}+\gamma_{n}\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{S}b_{i}\left\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{k},\boldsymbol{K}_{i}\right\rangle

(2.23)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:RRKM_Update}}{\Leftrightarrow}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\delta_{km}\left[\boldsymbol{U}_{n}+\Delta t\gamma_{n}\sum_{i=1}^{S}b_{i}\boldsymbol{K}_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=U_{n}^{(k)}+\gamma_{n}\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{S}b_{i}K_{i}^{(k)}

(2.24)

where
\boldsymbol{e}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
is the
k
-th (Euclidian) unit vector.
Additionally, we immediately see that arbitrary linear invariants are conserved for every choice of
\gamma_{n}
.
The same holds of course also for linear functions resulting in a scalar entropy, such as
H(\boldsymbol{U})=\boldsymbol{q}^{T}\boldsymbol{U}
where
\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
could resemble a vector corresponding to quadrature weights.

For nonlinear functions
H(\boldsymbol{U})
such as the (mathematical) entropy (
2.22
) does not trivially hold, however.
In this scenario, we search for a relaxation parameter
\gamma_{n}
such that the true change in entropy

\Delta H_{n}\coloneqq\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{S}b_{i}\left\langle\frac{\partial H(\boldsymbol{U}_{n,i})}{\partial\boldsymbol{U}_{n,i}},\boldsymbol{K}_{i}\right\rangle

(2.25)

matches the entropy of the relaxed update state
\boldsymbol{U}_{n+1}(\gamma_{n})
:

H\big(\boldsymbol{U}_{n+1}(\gamma_{n})\big)=H\left(\boldsymbol{U}_{n}+\Delta t\gamma_{n}\sum_{i=1}^{S}b_{i}\boldsymbol{K}_{i}\right)\overset{!}{=}H(\boldsymbol{U}_{n})+\gamma_{n}\Delta H_{n}(\boldsymbol{U}_{n})\>.

(2.26)

For entropy-conservative systems the the scalar product in
Eq.

2.25
is thus exactly zero, i.e.,

\left\langle\frac{\partial H(\boldsymbol{U}_{n,i})}{\partial\boldsymbol{U}_{n,i}},\boldsymbol{K}_{i}\right\rangle\overset{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:StageSingleEq}}{=}\left\langle\frac{\partial H(\boldsymbol{U}_{n,i})}{\partial\boldsymbol{U}_{n,i}},\boldsymbol{F}\big(t_{n,i},\boldsymbol{U}_{n,i}\big)\right\rangle=0

(2.27)

Note that we demand the equality
Eq.

2.26
also for entropy diffusive systems, i.e., systems with
\langle\partial_{\boldsymbol{U}}H,\boldsymbol{F}\rangle\leq 0
to suppress erronous additional changes in entropy due to the time integration method.

We can rewrite (
2.25
) by introducing the
entropy variables

Tadmor 2003

\boldsymbol{w}(\boldsymbol{u})\coloneqq\frac{\partial s(\boldsymbol{u})}{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{V}

(2.28)

with (
2.19
) as

\Delta H_{n}=\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{S}b_{i}\left\langle\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{W}_{n,i}\,\text{d}\Omega\,,\boldsymbol{K}_{i}\right\rangle

(2.29)

which will be used in the remainder.
The vector of discretized entropy variables
\boldsymbol{W}
is obtained by evaluating the entropy variables at every node of the mesh and then re-assembling them into a vector of dimension
\mathbb{R}^{M}
.
Note that integration of discrete integrands is denoted by the differential form
\text{d}\Omega
, while for integration of continuous quantities we employ
\text{d}\boldsymbol{x}
, cf. (
2.19
).

It is well-known that the first order condition for a Runge-Kutta method is that
\sum_{i=1}^{S}b_{i}=1
.
Thus, we interpret
\gamma_{n}
as acting on the timestep
\Delta t
rather than on the weights
\boldsymbol{b}^{T}
.
Consequently, the obtained update
\boldsymbol{U}_{n+1}(\gamma_{n})
is understood as an approximation at time
t_{n+1}=t_{n}+\gamma_{n}\Delta t

Ketcheson 2019
,
Ranocha et al. 2020
.

Conversely, when interpreting the relaxed result
\boldsymbol{U}_{n+1}(\gamma_{n})
as an approximation to
t_{n+1}=t_{n}+\Delta t
the Relaxation Runge-Kutta method can be viewed as a projection method
[
Hairer et al. 2006
, Chapter IV.4]

Ketcheson 2019
.
In that case, the relaxation mechanism can be interpreted as an instance of the incremental direction technique (IDT)
Calvo et al. 2006
,
Ketcheson 2019
,
Ranocha et al. 2020
.

At this point, numerous questions arise, concerning for instance existence and uniqueness of
\gamma_{n}
, order of the method, and stability properties.
Here, we briefly state the main results obtained in
Ketcheson 2019
,
Ranocha et al. 2020
,
Ranocha et al. 2020b
which settle these concerns.

1.

Existence and uniqueness of
\gamma_{n}
:
Clearly (
2.26
) is trivially fulfilled for
\gamma_{n}=0
.
A positive (i.e., nontrivial) unique solution which is close to unity exists for
\gamma_{n}
for strictly convex entropy functionals
H
coupled with at least second-order accurate schemes for sufficiently small
\Delta t
.

2.

Accuracy:
Given a Runge-Kutta method of order
p
, the relaxed update
\boldsymbol{U}_{n+1}(\gamma_{n})
is an order
p
approximation of the true solution at time
t_{n+1}=t_{n}+\gamma_{n}\Delta t
for relaxation parameters
\gamma_{n}=1+\mathcal{O}\left(\Delta t^{p-1}\right)
(which follows from the existence and uniqueness of
\gamma_{n}
).
We validate this property in
Section

4.3
.

3.

Linear Stability:
For
\gamma_{n}\leq 1
the relaxed method is linearly stable for a given timestep
\Delta t
if the standard method is linearly stable for this
\Delta t
.
We provide a brief illustration on this manner by means of a concrete example in
Section

3.2
.

4.

Nonlinear Stability:
We remark that also e.g. strong stability preserving methods
Shu and Osher 1988
,
Gottlieb et al. 2001
can be relaxed while maintaining their nonlinear stability properties.

We are now equipped with the necessary tools to introduce the Paired Explicit Runge-Kutta methods with relaxation.

3
Paired Explicit Relaxation Runge-Kutta Methods

The generalization of the relaxation methodology to Paired Explicit Runge-Kutta schemes is straightforward.
Due to the shared weight vector cf. (
2.12
) all stages
i
with
b_{i}\neq 0
are evaluated across all partitions
r=1,\dots,R
.
Thus, the
direction

\boldsymbol{d}_{n}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{S}b_{i}\boldsymbol{K}_{i}

(3.1)

and entropy change
\Delta H
(cf. (
2.26
)) are computed completely analogous to standard Runge-Kutta methods.
As we are only concerned with Paired Explicit Runge-Kutta methods of order two and higher, the conditions for existence and uniqueness of a solution
\gamma_{n}
are fulfilled.
Furthermore, the Paired Explicit Runge-Kutta schemes considered in
Vermeire 2019
,
Nasab and Vermeire 2022
,
Doehring et al. 2024
,
Doehring et al. 2024
have nonnegative weights
b_{i}\geq 0
which ensures that the relaxed update is dissipation preserving for such problems
Ranocha et al. 2020
and avoids stage downwinding
Gottlieb and Shu 1998
.

The sparsity in the weight vector
\boldsymbol{b}^{T}
renders Paired Explicit Runge-Kutta methods especially attractive for the relaxation methodology.
The vanilla second-order method as proposed in
Vermeire 2019
has only
b_{S}=1
with all other entries being zero.
The third-order method proposed in
Nasab and Vermeire 2022
has non-zero
b_{S-1}=0.75,\allowbreak b_{S}=0.25
.
The third-order variant proposed in
Doehring et al. 2024
adds
b_{1}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{6}}
to
b_{S-1}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{6}},\allowbreak b_{S}=\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}
which avoids a negative entry in Butcher array of the original scheme
Nasab and Vermeire 2022
, thus there is no downwinding of stages
Gottlieb and Shu 1998
.
Finally, the fourth-order method developed in
Doehring et al. 2024
has
b_{S-1}=b_{S}=0.5
.
Archetypes for the Butcher tableaus of these methods are provided in
A
.

This sparsity in
\boldsymbol{b}^{T}
is a very attractive feature as this reduces the number of integrations involved in a sequential computation of
\Delta H
, cf. (
2.25
).
Of course, with the usual computation-storage tradeoff one can always reduce (
2.25
) to a single integration if all intermediate approximations
\boldsymbol{U}_{n,i}
and stages
\boldsymbol{K}_{i}
are stored, even though they are not required for the actual update (
2.7c
).
We remark that the transform from conserved
\boldsymbol{U}_{n,i}
to entropy variables
\boldsymbol{W}_{n,i}
would also be required for this approach.
In our implementation of the Paired Explicit Relaxation Runge-Kutta methods we opt for the sequential computation of (
2.25
) to avoid unnecessary memory overhead.

In practice, we compute (
2.29
) as

\Delta H_{n}=\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{S}b_{i}\sum_{j}\int_{\Omega_{j}}\sum_{l}\big\langle\boldsymbol{w}_{n,i,j,l},\boldsymbol{k}_{i,j,l}\big\rangle\,\text{d}\Omega_{j}

(3.2)

which follows from interchanging the scalar product and the integral (a quadrature rule on the discrete level).
Thus, for every element
\Omega_{j}
in the mesh we compute the dot product
\big\langle\boldsymbol{w}_{n,i,j,l},\boldsymbol{k}_{i,j,l}\big\rangle
for each node
l
in the element.
Note that on every node we have exactly
V
variables, thus the inner product in
Eq.

3.2
is a scalar product on
\mathbb{R}^{V}
.

3.1
Solving the Relaxation Equation

To ensure an entropy-stable time update the scalar nonlinear equation

r(\gamma_{n})\coloneqq H\left(\boldsymbol{U}_{n}+\gamma_{n}\Delta t\boldsymbol{d}_{n}\right)-H(\boldsymbol{U}_{n})-\gamma_{n}\Delta H_{n}\overset{!}{=}0

(3.3)

needs to be solved for
\gamma_{n}
.
In the preparation of this work we considered three main approaches to solve (
3.3
).
These are a standard bisection procedure which is quite robust and easy to implement.
The drawback of this method is, however, that one needs to supply a bracketing interval
[\gamma_{\text{min}},\gamma_{\text{max}}]
.
As the bisection method converges only linearly, the choice of this bracketing interval heavily influences the number of iterations taken to solve (
3.3
) to some desired accuracy.
Second, we considered the secant method which can be seen as a middle-ground between the bisection method and Newton-Raphson method.
In particular, the secant method is of similar computational complexity as the bisection method, but offers a higher convergence rate.
As for bisection, however, the secant method also requires two initial guesses
\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}
which are used to compute the first update.
The presence and significance of these hyperparameters render the secant method only a fallback option for our purposes.

Thus, we mainly considered variants of the Newton-Raphson method which converge quadratically near the root.
The required derivative of
\frac{\text{d}r}{\text{d}\gamma_{n}}
is given by
Ranocha et al. 2020

r^{\prime}(\gamma_{n})=\int_{\Omega}\big\langle\boldsymbol{w}(\boldsymbol{U}_{n}+\gamma_{n}\Delta t\boldsymbol{d}_{n}),\Delta t\boldsymbol{d}_{n}\big\rangle\,\text{d}\Omega-\Delta H_{n}\>.

(3.4)

To determine the stepsize in the Newton-Raphson procedure we experimented with different line-search methods.
In practice (see
Section

4.1
) we found that the vanilla Newton-Raphson procedure with trivial stepsize
1
converges extremely fast (
1
to
3
iterations) and additional line-search methods are not necessary.
In the very first timestep we set
\gamma_{1}=1
while in subsequent timesteps we use the previously obtained value for the relaxation parameter as the initial guess for the Newton-Raphson iteration.
As stopping criteria we employ the residual of the relaxation equation (
3.3
), the magnitude of the update step and a maximum number of iterations to prevent iterating divergent sequences.

3.2
Entropy Relaxation as Time Limiting

The traditional discretization of a hyperbolic partial differential equation involves a spatial discretization that is total variation diminishing/total variation bounded or offers at least some form of control over oscillations at discontinuities.
This is then paired with an strong stability preserving time integration scheme to prevent the introduction of spurious oscillations due to the time marching mechanism
Gottlieb et al. 2001
,
Gottlieb and Shu 1998
.
The latter scheme may be explicit or implicit, however, even for unconditionally linearly stable (A-stable) implicit methods of order
p>1
, the nonlinearly stable, i.e., stability-preserving timestep is finite
Gottlieb et al. 2001
.
In order to render implicit schemes more efficient than their explicit counterparts timesteps beyond the strong stability preserving stability limit are often employed.
Since in this case global non-oscillarity can no longer be guaranteed, localized
time-limiting
strategies have been studied
Forth 2001
,
Duraisamy et al. 2003
,
Duraisamy and Baeder 2007
with some recent contributions
Arbogast et al. 2020
,
Puppo et al. 2023a
,
Puppo et al. 2023b
.
The central idea in the referenced works is to blend a high-order implicit scheme with the first-order, unconditionally nonlinearly stable implicit/backward Euler method.

In order to determine the limiting factor/blending coefficient different criteria based e.g. on temporal derivatives
Duraisamy et al. 2003
,
Puppo et al. 2023a
have been employed.
Recently, an indicator based on the local numerical entropy production

\Delta s_{n,j}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\Delta t}\left[s\left(\boldsymbol{U}_{n+1,j}\right)-s\left(\boldsymbol{U}_{n,j}\right)-\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\sum_{i=1}^{S}b_{i}\left(\Psi_{i,j-\frac{1}{2}}-\Psi_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right]

(3.5)

was proposed in
Puppo et al. 2023b
.
Derived in the context of one-dimensional finite-volume schemes, (
3.5
) denotes the entropy production on cell
j
due to numerical entropy fluxes
\Psi_{i,j\pm\frac{1}{2}}
exchanged from time
t_{n}
to
t_{n+1}
.
The numerical entropy fluxes are required to be consistent with the entropy flux
\psi(\boldsymbol{u})

Tadmor 2003
which corresponds to the entropy
s(\boldsymbol{u})
in the sense that
\left(\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}\right)^{T}=\boldsymbol{w}^{T}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{f}}{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}

Tadmor 2003
.
Equation (
3.5
) can be understood as a localized version of the relaxation equation (
2.22
) as both determine the change in entropy due to the numerical scheme.
In fact, by integrating (
3.5
) over all cells and time interval
[t_{n},t_{n+1}]
we recover (
2.22
) for a suitable choice of the numerical entropy flux function
\Psi
.

We would like to remark that the relaxation methodology can also be applied to local entropies, i.e., convex functionals that are defined on subdomains
\Omega_{j}\subset\Omega

Ranocha et al. 2020a
.
In the extreme, these subdomains could be reduced to single cells, resulting in local entropy changes
\Delta H_{j}
which can be computed analogous to
Eq.

3.5
.

In
Puppo et al. 2023b
, the authors employ (
3.5
) as an indicator if the timestep needs to be reduced to avoid oscillations.
Similarly, the relaxation mechanism as discussed above can act as a limiter for monotonicity violations due to discontinuities in the partitioned Runge-Kutta scheme.
This is particularly attractive as we have demonstrated in
Doehring et al. 2024
that at the intersection of partitions spurious oscillations may occur.
These are intensified for larger differences in active stage evaluations
E^{(1)}-E^{(2)}
, larger timesteps, and reduced grid resolution
Doehring et al. 2024
.
In the next section we follow up on this insights and demonstrate in what sense the relaxation methodology can act as a time-limiting mechanism for Paired Explicit Runge-Kutta methods.

which is the linear equivalent of

.
The update matrix

can now be used to confirm linear stability by assessing whether all eigenvalues of

are inside the unit circle.
This is indeed the case for both the standard Paired Explicit Runge-Kutta method, i.e.,

and the relaxed method with

, which confirms the discussion on linear stability from

.

for all times, i.e., the update matrix remains constant.
For the Paired Explicit Relaxation Runge-Kutta methods, however,

is in general not identical among timesteps due to the relaxation parameter

.

.

D
to examine the nonlinear stability of the time integration methods.

if and only if

(3.9)

) to hold.

and

to see if the relaxation mechanism has a stabilizing effect.
For the standard Paired Explicit Runge-Kutta method we have
\min_{i,j}D_{i,j}(1)\approx-0.370
and
\max_{i,j}D_{i,j}(1)\approx 1.115
.

and
\max_{i,j}D_{i,j}(\gamma_{1})\approx 0.86
.
Clearly, the relaxation mechanism nudges the entries of

closer to the

Additionally, we would like to remark that for both cases the row-sums equal one, i.e.,

3.2.2
Kelvin-Helmholtz Instability with Adaptive Mesh Refinement

Motivated by these insights we reconsider a numerical example conducted in
Doehring et al. 2024
, wherein we studied the applicability of the standard Paired Explicit Runge-Kutta schemes to the Kelvin-Helmholtz instability on an adaptively refined mesh.
We employ the setup from
Rueda-Ramírez and Gassner 2021
with initial condition at
t_{0}=0
given by

\begin{pmatrix}\rho\\
v_{x}\\
v_{y}\\
p\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}+\frac{3}{4}b\\
\frac{1}{2}(b-1)\\
\frac{1}{10}\sin(2\pi x)\\
1\end{pmatrix},\quad b\coloneqq\tanh\left(15y+7.5\right)-\tanh\left(15y-7.5\right)

on periodic domain

The compressible Euler equations are discretized using the discontinuous Galerkin spectral element method with

, subcell shock-capturing

.
Initially, the domain is discretized with

.

In

4.3.2
Viscous Shock Propagation

To demonstrate that the Paired Explicit Relaxation Runge-Kutta methods with relaxation maintain their designed order of accuracy for entropy diffusive problems we consider the viscous shock propagation testcase.
This testcase is constructed by balancing shock-formation due to the hyperbolic nonlinearity through diffusion, resulting in a time-invariant shock profile.
Becker
Becker 1922
was the first one to derive the a solution for the shock profile of the 1D Navier-Stokes-Fourier equations, i.e., taking both viscosity and heat conduction into account.
This requires setting the Prandtl number
\text{Pr}=\frac{3}{4}=c_{p}\frac{\mu}{\kappa}=\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}\frac{\mu}{\kappa}
which is not too far from physical values for air and other gases.
Becker’s work was revisited by Morduchow and Libby
Morduchow and Libby 1949
which showed that some of Becker’s assumptions could be relaxed.

For a general parametrization, the solution for the 1D shock profile involves a nonlinear equation which needs to be solved numerically to obtain a solution to the Becker equation of motion
Morduchow and Libby 1949
,
Margolin et al. 2017
.
In
Margolin et al. 2017
, however, the authors show that for the Mach number
\text{Ma}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{3-\gamma}},\allowbreak 1\leq\gamma\leq\frac{5}{3}
an explicit solution is available.
Here, we set
\gamma=\frac{5}{3}
(monatomic ideal gas), dynamic viscosity
\mu=0.15
, reference density
\rho_{0}=1
, and shock speed
v=1
.
From this, we compute the Mach number, speed of sound ahead of the shock
c_{0}=\frac{v}{\text{Ma}}
, and the reference pressure
p_{0}=c_{0}^{2}\frac{\rho_{0}}{\gamma}
which is computed from the fact that the enthalpy is constant across the shock.
Furthermore, we have the length scale
l=\frac{\mu}{\gamma-1}\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma-1}\frac{1}{\rho_{0}v}

Margolin et al. 2017
.
Since the shock profile is moving with velocity
v
, the solution at any time
t
can be obtained by tracing back to the solution at initial position
x_{0}(x,t)\coloneqq x-vt
.
With characteristic position

\chi(x)\coloneqq 2\exp\left(\frac{x}{2l}\right)

(4.11)

and "effective momentum"

w(\chi)\coloneqq 1+\frac{1}{2\chi^{2}}\left(1-\sqrt{1+2\chi^{2}}\right)

(4.12)

the primal variables are given by
Margolin et al. 2017

\displaystyle\rho(t,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{0}}{w}

(4.13a)

\displaystyle u(t,x)

\displaystyle=v(1-w)

(4.13b)

\displaystyle p(t,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{0}}{w}\left(1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}\text{Ma}^{2}\left(1-w^{2}\right)\right)

(4.13c)

where we abbreviate
w\equiv w(\chi(x_{0}(x,t)))
.
The solution
Eq.

4.13
at initial time is provided by
Fig.

21
in the appendix.

We solve this problem on
\Omega=[-2,2]
up to
t_{f}=0.5
.
For the hyperbolic boundary conditions, i.e., the values for the inviscid fluxes, we use inflow conditions obtained from the analytical solution at the left boundary and (free) outflow conditions at the right boundary.
The parabolic boundary conditions required for the viscous gradient fluxes are realized as moving isothermal, no-slip walls where velocity and temperature are obtained from the analytical solution.
The surface fluxes are computed using the Harten-Lax-Van Leer-Einfeldt
Einfeldt 1988
flux and the viscous terms are discretized using the Bassi-Rebay 1 scheme
Bassi and Rebay 1997
,
Gassner et al. 2018
.
Since this problem is diffusion-dominated we also tested the local Discontinuous Galerkin (LDG) method
Cockburn and Shu 1998
with zero penalty parameter
Cockburn and Dong 2007
which yields similar results.
For the volume integral we use the standard weak form.

In contrast to the previous example we now refine simultaneously in space and time.

5
Applications

In this section we apply the Paired Explicit Relaxation Runge-Kutta methods to a range of different flow regimes of increasing complexity.
We begin by considering three two-dimensional examples.
First, we study the laminar, viscous, effectively incompressible flow over the SD7003 airfoil in
Section

5.1
.
This problem can be seen as an extended testcase as it allows validating the Paired Explicit Relaxation Runge-Kutta methods by comparing aerodynamic coefficients to reference data and is not characterized by complicated flow features.
Next, we consider the flow of a magnetized, viscous, compressible fluid around a cylinder in
Section

5.2
, which is described by the visco-resistive Magnetohydrodynamics equations
Bohm et al. 2020
,
Warburton and Karniadakis 1999
.
Then, we turn our attention to the inviscid, transonic flow around the NACA0012 airfoil.
The testcase discussed in
Section

5.3
corresponds to case C1.2 of the third international workshop on high-order computational fluid dynamics methods held in 2015
Huynh 2015
.
We apply adaptive mesh refinement to this problem to resolve the steady standing shock on the airfoil top surface.
Coming to three-dimensional problems, we consider the classic ONERA M6 wing
Schmitt and Charpin 1979
,
Slater 2002
in
Section

5.4
for an inviscid, compressible gas, i.e., also a steady setup.
Additionally, we investigate the robustness of the relaxed multirate schemes by means of a viscous transonic flow across the NASA Common Research Model on a very coarse grid in
Section

5.5
.

All computations are performed using the Trixi.jl
Ranocha et al. 2022
,
Schlottke-Lakemper et al. 2021a
,
Schlottke-Lakemper et al. 2021b

5.1.2

To begin, we demonstrate performance improvements of the Paired Explicit Runge-Kutta scheme with relaxation over the plain Paired Explicit Runge-Kutta scheme.
For the given configuration, the Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy number can be increased by roughly

We attribute this observation to the increased robustness of the Paired Explicit Relaxation Runge-Kutta scheme compared to the standard scheme, cf.

Here, some code optimization could be possible e.g. by embodying this integration in the Discontinuous Galerkin solution process.

Second, we compare the performance of the entropy-conservative, multirate scheme with entropy-stable, standalone schemes.
Besides the natural comparison with the optimized

5.2.1
Setup

The flow of a magnetized fluid past a cylinder has been studied both numerically and experimentally
Crawford and Karniadakis 1995
,
Weier et al. 1998
,
Kanaris et al. 2013
.
The configuration employed here is loosely based on the setup described in
Warburton and Karniadakis 1999
.
Most importantly, we also set the Mach number
\text{Ma}=0.5
, rendering the flow truly compressible.
The ratio of specific heats is set to
\gamma=\frac{5}{3}
and the Reynolds number based on the diameter of the cylinder
D=1
is set to
\text{Re}=\frac{\rho_{\infty}v_{\infty}D}{\mu}=200
.
Following
Warburton and Karniadakis 1999
, the Alfvénic Mach number
A=\frac{v_{A}}{v_{\infty}}
is set to
A=0.1
.
This defines the magnitude of the magnetic field
B_{\infty}=v_{A}\sqrt{\rho_{\infty}}
.
With this Alfvénic Mach number the viscous Lundquist number is
S_{\mu}=A\cdot\text{Re}=\frac{\rho_{\infty}v_{A}D}{\mu}=50
.
We choose
v_{\infty}=p_{\infty}=1
, thus
\rho_{\infty}
follows from the speed of sound
c=\frac{v_{\infty}}{\text{Ma}}
of an ideal gas as
\rho_{\infty}=p_{\infty}\frac{\gamma}{c^{2}}
.
Again, as in
Warburton and Karniadakis 1999
we use the same values for the viscous and resistive Lundquist numbers
S_{\mu}=S_{\eta}
.
Then, the magnetic resistivity is given by
\eta=\frac{v_{A}D}{S_{\eta}}
.

The cylinder is placed at
(0,0)
in the rectangular domain
\Omega=[-5D,60D]\times[-10D,10D]
.
The computational mesh is generated using
HOHQMesh

Kopriva et al. 2024
which enables the generation of conforming high-order, i.e., curved-boundary quadrilateral meshes.
In this case, the mesh uses fourth-order boundaries, consists of
2258
elements and is symmetric around the
y=0
axis, cf.
Fig.

27
.

The hyperbolic boundary conditions imposed at
\partial\Omega
are weakly enforced Dirichlet conditions
Mengaldo et al. 2014
based on the initial/freestream condition.
At the cylinder surface a slip-wall boundary condition
van der Vegt and van der Ven 2002
with zero magnetic field is imposed.
The parabolic boundary conditions of the cylinder are no-slip, adiabatic, and isomagnetic
\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{0}
walls.
At the inlet, i.e., left boundary, the parabolic boundary condition imposes a moving, adiabatic, isomagnetic fluid with velocity
v_{x}=v_{\infty}
and purely horizontal magnetic field
B_{x}=B_{\infty}
.
On the other boundaries, i.e., top, bottom and outlet/right boundary the gradients are just copied which corresponds to domain-extending boundary conditions.
The simulation is initialized with the uniform inflow state.

The solution fields are approximated with
k=3
Discontinuous Galerkin solution polynomials.
The Riemann problems due to the inviscid fluxes (
4.15
) are approximately solved with the Harten-Lax-Van Leer two-wave solver
Harten et al. 1983
with Davis-type wave speed estimates
Davis 1988
.
As for the previous example, the viscous fluxes are computed with the BR1 scheme
Bassi and Rebay 1997
,
Gassner et al. 2018
.
The nonconservative Powell term (
4.16
) is solved with the specialized numerical flux described in
Derigs et al. 2018
,
Bohm et al. 2020
.
Opposed to the standard Navier-Stokes-Fourier equations the simulation of a magnetized fluid requires stabilization.
To this end, we employ the entropy-stable schock-capturing technique by means of the first-order finite volume method on the subcells of the Discontinuous Galerkin scheme
Hennemann et al. 2021
,
Rueda-Ramírez et al. 2021
.
The required volume fluxes are the entropy conservation and kinetic energy preserving flux
Hindenlang and Gassner 2019
which extends the flux by Ranocha
Ranocha 2020
to the MHD equations.
The nonconservative volume fluxes are treated with the same numerical flux as the nonconservative surface fluxes.

We simulate the flow until
t_{f}=120
such that the vortex street is fully developed and reaches the outlet boundary.
Upon inspection of the mesh (cf.
Fig.

27
) it becomes apparent that roughly four base cell sizes (minimum edge length) are present: Cells with
h\approx 0.25
at the cylinder,
h\approx 0.5
in the near wake,
h\approx 1
in the far wake, and
h\approx 2
at the top and bottom boundary.
Thus, we seek to find a four-member Paired Explicit Relaxation Runge-Kutta family for which the admissible timestep roughly doubles for each family member.
For the fourth-order methods the
E=\{5,\allowbreak 6,\allowbreak 8,\allowbreak 13\}
scheme fulfills this requirement and is thus used.

To solve the relaxation equation
Eq.

3.3
we employ the Newton-Raphson procedure which stops at
3
iterations or if the residual tolerance is less or equal
10^{-14}
.
For this simulation we employ a linear ramp-up of the

), from a small value

which is required to overcome the naive initial condition to a maximum value for the developed flow

(5.11)

where the ramp-up time

The maximum CFL numbers

5.2.2
Performance Comparison

For this example, the Paired Explicit Relaxation Runge-Kutta does not come with improved Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy numbers compared to the standard Paired Explicit Runge-Kutta.

of the total runtime.
Additionally, the multirate Paired Explicit Relaxation Runge-Kutta scheme requires one more timestep (

Across schemes, we observe that on average a single Newton iteration is required to solve the relaxation equation

Turning to the comparison to the standalone relaxed entropy-stable schemes we observe that these require at least

6
Conclusions

In this work we presented a novel class of entropy conservation/stable high-order, optimized, multirate time integration schemes suitable for the integration of convection-dominated compressible flow problems.
These schemes are based on the Paired Explicit Runge-Kutta methods and allow for the same non-intrusive implementation based on a coefficient-based partitioned semidiscretization.
The Paired Explicit Relaxation Runge-Kutta methods maintain the same order of accuracy as the corresponding Paired Explicit Runge-Kutta methods, preserve linear invariants such as mass, momentum, and energy, and introduce no defects in the global entropy.
We have shown that the Paired Explicit Runge-Kutta schemes benefit significantly from the relaxation approach, as it enhances robustness of the multirate schemes.
In particular, this is demonstrated for underresolved viscous flows, where the schemes with relaxation maintain positivity for longer time intervals or are stable for larger Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy numbers.
This is due to the relaxation parameter
\gamma
acting as an adaptive timestep controller, which in turn has a limiting effect.

We have shown that the Paired Explicit Relaxation Runge-Kutta schemes outperform standard Relaxation-Runge-Kutta methods for a collection of problems, ranging from low Mach and low Reynolds number flow regimes to transonic inviscid and viscous flows.
We highlight here the flow of a magnetized fluid around a cylinder governed by the visco-resistive Magnetohydrodynamics equations, the transonic inviscid flow over a NACA0012 airfoil with adaptive mesh refinement and the simulation of the ONERA M6 wing.
Depending on the problem, right-hand side evaluations are reduced by factors up to four and speedups in excess of three are observed.

In future work we will explore the application of the Paired Explicit Relaxation Runge-Kutta schemes to flows involving explicit turbulence modeling.
In addition, the construction of implicit-explicit (IMEX) Paired Explicit Runge-Kutta/Paired Explicit Relaxation Runge-Kutta schemes is of interest, for instance for simulations involving chemical reactions.

A.2

The archetype of the third-order Paired Explicit Runge-Kutta schemes as presented in

is given by

\begin{array}[]{c|c|c c c c c c}i&\boldsymbol{c}&&&A^{(r)}&&&\\
\hline\cr 1&0&&&&&&\\
2&c_{2}&c_{2}&&&&\\
3&c_{3}&c_{3}-a_{3,2}&a_{3,2}&&&\\
