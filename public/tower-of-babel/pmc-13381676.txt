Attentional negativity and emotional symptoms as serial mediators linking parental verbosity to non-suicidal self-injury in Chinese pre-adolescents
Authors: Xiaojuan Cui, Guoli Yan, Xiaohui Dai, Minghui Li, Tengfen Yang, Qiuping Yan, Xiaofei Hou, Ling Sun, Jiansong Zhou, Huifang Yin, Guangming Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNon-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) in pre-adolescents represents an escalating clinical concern, with early onset being a predictor of persistent self-harm and future suicidal behavior. Despite growing recognition of the role of family dynamics in youth NSSI, the specific mechanisms through which parental communication behaviors contribute to early-onset NSSI remain poorly characterized, particularly within the Chinese cultural context. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between a specific parental communication behavior—parental verbosity (excessive, repetitive verbal reasoning during discipline)—and pre-adolescent NSSI, testing a mediation pathway involving the Attentional Negativity Index (ANI: a self-reported measure of perceived attention to negative versus positive information) and emotional symptoms (EM). A total of 1,452 pre-adolescents (aged 10 - 12 years) and their parents were recruited from eight elementary schools in Tianjin, China. A multi-informant approach was employed specifically for emotional symptoms (EM), collecting data through both student-report and parent-report to enhance measurement validity and reduce single-informant bias. Parental verbosity was assessed via parent-report, while ANI and NSSI were assessed via student-report, as these constructs reflect children’s subjective internal experiences that are most accurately captured by self-report. Serial mediation analyses using Hayes’ PROCESS macro were employed to examine the proposed cognitive-emotional mechanisms. The prevalence of NSSI in this sample was 23.8%. Serial mediation analyses revealed a significant pathway from parental verbosity to NSSI via sequential mediation of ANI and emotional symptoms (indirect effect = 0.024, 95% CI [0.004, 0.049]); however, the total indirect effect across all pathways was non-significant (Effect = 0.026, 95% CI [-0.047, 0.103]). Specifically, parental verbosity was positively associated with self-reported ANI, which in turn predicted higher levels of emotional symptoms, ultimately linking to increased NSSI frequency. This serial mediation pathway remained significant in sensitivity analyses using parent-reported emotional symptoms (indirect effect = 0.008, 95% CI [0.001, 0.017]), providing preliminary evidence consistent with the proposed pathway, pending replication in independent longitudinal samples. The present findings are consistent with a cognitive-emotional cascade model in which parental verbosity, self-reported ANI, and EM are sequentially associated with NSSI in pre-adolescents. While these findings identify a potential mechanism linking a specific parental communication behavior to NSSI, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Introduction

Non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) in pre-adolescents (ages 10-12) has emerged as a critical public health concern and a significant predictor of subsequent suicidal behavior. Characterized by the deliberate destruction of one’s own body tissue without suicidal intent—commonly manifesting as behaviors such as cutting, burning, or striking oneself1—NSSI during this early developmental stage marks a pivotal window of vulnerability2,3. Research indicates that while NSSI behavior often increases significantly during the pre-adolescent years4, individuals who begin self-harming at age 12 or younger are more likely to display increasingly persistent patterns throughout their lives and experience more physically dangerous incidents compared to peers who develop these behaviors later in adolescence5,6. Because the risk of future suicide attempts escalates with the frequency and severity of such early self-harming behaviors7–9, identifying proximal risk factors in the immediate family environment during pre-adolescence is crucial for preventing the escalation into more severe clinical patterns10.

Extensive research has established the family environment as a distal risk factor for adolescent NSSI10,11. Macro-level parenting dimensions such as parental conflict, harsh discipline, psychological control, and high expressed emotion—particularly criticism and hostility — are well-documented predictors12–14. Recent evidence from a cross-cultural study comparing Chinese and European mothers underscores that parental verbosity — a dysfunctional discipline pattern involving excessive, repetitive verbal explanation that persists even after the child has already received the relevant information — serves as a predictor of internalized symptoms in the Chinese subgroup in prior work, although the prevalence of parental verbosity is lower in China compared to Eastern European contexts15.

This heightened significance in the Chinese context may reflect the distinctive demands of the contemporary educational and family environment. China’s highly competitive educational system16, characterized by intense academic pressure and college entrance examination stakes, frequently drives parental anxiety regarding educational achievement. Under such high-pressure circumstances, parents often resort to excessive verbal reasoning and repetitive explanation17— operationalized here as verbosity—as a disciplinary strategy intended to enhance compliance and academic success. Thus, parental verbosity may function less as a cultural tradition and more as a maladaptive parental response to external performance pressures18,19. This suggests that understanding the mechanisms linking parental verbosity to child psychopathology is particularly relevant and urgent for Chinese families.

Notably, these macro-level parenting styles are often manifested through more subtle and frequent daily verbal interactions. As a daily interpersonal stressor, parental verbosity manifests through excessive, repetitive, and over-inclusive verbalizing during discipline rather than providing direct consequences — for example, repeatedly explaining rules after the child has already heard them, delivering extended lectures following minor misbehavior, or demanding that the child justify their actions verbally17. Verbosity is characterized by an over-reliance on talking itself as a disciplinary tool — persisting with verbal explanation and reasoning even when such strategies have proven ineffective—and thus differs from Overreactivity (which centers on displays of anger, harsh reprimands, and loss of emotional control), Laxness (which reflects a failure to enforce consequences consistently), and psychological control (which involves manipulative or guilt-inducing communication)20. Crucially, verbosity functions as an aversive stimulus not through explicitly hostile or threatening content, but through two interrelated structural features: (1) temporal unpredictability—the child cannot determine when the verbal episode will conclude or what behavioral consequence, if any, will follow; and (2) informational redundancy—the repetitive content provides little new corrective guidance while continuously demanding the child’s attentional resources, creating a sustained cognitive burden without clear resolution17,21. Having established that parental verbosity operates as a structural stressor in the family environment, we now turn to the theoretical frameworks that explain how such chronic verbal exposure may shape children’s cognitive and emotional development.

To understand how these daily verbal stressors translate into psychological risks, the current study draws upon Patterson’s Coercive Family Process model21 and experience-dependent learning theory22. Consistent with Patterson’s framework, chronic exposure to the structurally aversive features of verbose discipline described above — its unpredictability and redundancy — is proposed to shape children’s information-processing in a manner that heightens sensitivity to negative environmental cues21,22. For pre-adolescents whose executive function capacities are still developing3, this sustained cognitive demand may be particularly burdensome, as they must allocate substantial mental resources to track the flow of verbal input rather than processing its content. Within this framework, chronic exposure to such unpredictable and cognitively demanding interactions creates an experiential learning context wherein children develop heightened attention to potential negative environmental cues as an adaptive — though ultimately maladaptive — response strategy22. Pollak’s model of experience-dependent affective learning posits that children repeatedly exposed to aversive interpersonal contexts develop sensitized threat-detection systems, manifesting as increased attention to negative information22. In the case of parental verbosity, we propose that the unpredictable and cognitively taxing nature of verbose interactions trains children to maintain heightened awareness of potential negativity — not because the verbal content is explicitly threatening, but because the interaction style itself requires constant monitoring for negative outcomes (e.g., When will this end? What punishment might follow? What exactly am I supposed to do? ). Importantly, this learned pattern may manifest as a stable cognitive tendency that children can metacognitively report — specifically, an awareness of their propensity to attend more readily to negative than positive information in their environment23,24.

We operationalize this metacognitive tendency via the Attentional Negativity Index (ANI), which reflects children’s metacognitive awareness of their attentional tendencies — namely, their subjective perception that they notice and attend to negative information more readily than positive information23,24— rather than the automatic, pre-conscious attentional deployment captured by behavioral paradigms such as dot-probe tasks. This measurement approach is theoretically and clinically meaningful in its own right: individuals’ beliefs about their attentional tendencies shape how they interpret and respond to environmental stimuli25. Via the affect-as-information mechanism25, children who perceive heightened negative attentional focus are more likely to interpret ambiguous situations negatively, potentially generating a self-reinforcing cycle — perceived negative focus → negative interpretation → negative affect → further reinforcement—that manifests as elevated emotional symptoms (EM) including persistent sadness, worry, and general distress. Consistent with functional models of NSSI1,26, we further propose that these amplified emotional symptoms may prompt engagement in NSSI as a maladaptive emotion regulation strategy, particularly among pre-adolescents whose adaptive regulation capacities are still developing3.

In summary, this study tests a serial mediation model (Verbosity → self-reported ANI → EM → NSSI) in a sample of Chinese pre-adolescents (ages 10-12), examining whether self-reported ANI and EM sequentially mediate the association between parental verbosity and NSSI. By focusing on a specific parental communication behavior and the cognitive-emotional mechanisms associated with it, this research aims to clarify a theoretically relevant pathway and generate hypotheses for future longitudinal and intervention-oriented research in this vulnerable population.

Methods

Participants

A school-based cross-sectional survey was conducted in December 2021 in Dongli District, Tianjin, China. Participants were recruited from a sample of eight primary schools via a convenience sampling method. Students in grades 5 to 6 were eligible to participate. Inclusion criteria included: (1) enrollment as a primary school student in Tianjin; (2) the ability to understand and complete the questionnaire; and (3) informed assent from the child and written consent from a parent. Exclusion criteria included: (1) pronounced reading impairments or uncooperative behavior; (2) a documented severe psychiatric illness or organic brain syndrome; or (3) incomplete or invalid questionnaires with critical data omissions or implausible responses. The final sample consisted of 1,452 parent-child pairs. The mean age of the pre-adolescent participants was 11.10 years (SD = 0.67). The sex distribution was nearly even, with 735 males (50.6%) and 717 females (49.4%). Regarding socioeconomic status, the majority of families (77.9%) reported a middle-level economic status, and most mothers (60.3%) had completed junior high school.

Procedure

Prior to the formal investigation, we contacted school directors and counselors from eight selected primary schools in Tianjin to obtain institutional authorization. The headteachers of each participating class were then trained in standardized survey procedures to ensure the consistency and quality of data collection across different sites. Recruitment was conducted via a hybrid communication approach. Parents were first informed of the study’s scientific objectives and procedures by teachers through “WeChat” (a widely used social media platform in China). Subsequently, students brought home paper-based informed consent forms for their parents to sign. Only those students who provided their own informed assent and returned their parents’ written consent were eligible to participate.

The survey utilized paper-based questionnaires to collect data separately:

Student Section: Students completed their assessments independently in their classrooms during designated school hours. This process was supervised by trained teachers and investigators to ensure confidentiality and to prevent mutual discussion among students.

Data entry and quality control

To ensure data integrity, each parent-child dyad was assigned a unique identification (ID) code for accurate matching and anonymity. All paper-based raw data were double-entered by two independent researchers into IBM SPSS 26.0 to minimize transcription errors. Questionnaires were strictly screened for quality: those with > 10% missing data or response patterns inconsistent with scale norms (e.g., endorsing all maximum or minimum responses) were excluded, resulting in 1,452 matched parent-child pairs for the final analysis.

Measures

Demographic and psychosocial assessment

Parents provided demographic and family background information, including the child’s age, sex, and school grade. They also reported the family economic status, education level and family structure (marital status and cohabitation patterns). Students independently completed student-report assessments of psychosocial factors.

Deliberate self-harm inventory–short version (DSHI-s)

The DSHI-s is a 16-item scale that measures the frequency of non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) over the past six months27,28. Respondents rate the occurrence of specific behaviors, such as cutting or burning, on a 4-point scale ranging from 0 (“never”) to 3 (“many times”). The scale has demonstrated good reliability (α = 0.82) and validity, including its ability to differentiate clinical factors like lower self-esteem among individuals who engage in repeated self-harm28. In the current sample, the Cronbach’s α for the DSHI-s was 0.803, indicating good internal consistency.

The attention to positive and negative information scale (APNI)

The APNI, first developed by Noguchi and colleagues, consists of 22 items measured on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“very inconsistent”) to 5 (“very consistent”)24. This tool evaluates an individual’s subjective tendencies to focus on either positive or negative stimuli, with higher scores indicating greater attentional focus. A traditional Chinese adaptation was subsequently produced by researchers at the University of Hong Kong29. For this study, we utilized the Attentional Negativity Index (ANI) subscale, derived from the Chinese revision developed by Lv Yaodi30. The ANI specifically measures self-reported perceptions of attending more readily to negative than positive information in one’s environment. Previous studies have confirmed its strong reliability in child populations, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.8330. In the current sample, the Cronbach’s α for the ANI subscale was 0.775, demonstrating acceptable internal consistency for this measure.

The strengths and difficulties questionnaire (SDQ)

The SDQ is a comprehensive 25-item tool developed by Goodman to assess emotional and behavioral challenges in children and teenagers31. This tool consists of five subscales: emotional symptoms, conduct problems, hyperactivity, peer relations, and pro-social behavior. The first four subscales make up the Difficulties Questionnaire, reflecting negative problems, while the Pro-social Behavior subscale serves as the Strengths Questionnaire. Each subscale has five items, and a three-point Likert scale was used. The three versions of the SDQ have essentially the same item content, with only minor differences in wording, and all have good reliability32,33. The age range of the SDQ has been extended to 2–17 years (see http://www.sdqinfo.com for details). In this study, the student-reported and parent-reported versions of the SDQ were mainly used. Official permission for the use of the SDQ in PDF format, which was administered using the pen-and-paper method, was obtained from Youth in mind Limited and is included as Supplementary File 2. For the current sample, the Cronbach’s α coefficients for the emotional symptoms subscale were 0.629 for the student version and 0.596 for the parent version. Although these values are relatively low, they are consistent with the well-documented tendency for brief subscales with only five items to yield lower internal consistency estimates, particularly in community samples34, and are nonetheless considered acceptable for construct-level measurement in large-scale developmental research32,33.

The Parenting scale (PS)

The PS measures ineffective discipline across three key dimensions: Laxness (11 items), Over-Reactivity (10 items), and Verbosity (7 items), with an additional four items that span multiple dimensions17. In the current study, we focused on the Verbosity subscale, which assesses parents’ use of excessively detailed or convoluted verbal reasoning instead of direct consequences when a child misbehaves. Although the Verbosity subscale showed moderate internal consistency (α = 0.63), the overall scale maintained strong reliability (α = 0.84)17. In the current sample, the Cronbach’s α for the Verbosity subscale was 0.609. Although this value falls below the conventional threshold of 0.70, it is consistent with the lower internal consistency of the Verbosity subscale reported in the original validation study and in subsequent cross-cultural adaptations17, and is attributable to the heterogeneous yet conceptually coherent nature of verbose disciplinary behaviors assessed across only seven items. Importantly, the construct validity of the Verbosity subscale is supported by its theoretically grounded item content and its demonstrated predictive associations in prior research17. In the current study, the Verbosity subscale was completed by parents or primary caregivers.

Statistical analyses

The statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS 26.0 and Hayes’ PROCESS macro (Model 6). Graphical visualization of the path models was conducted using the R programming language (version 4.5.2) with the ‘DiagrammeR’ package to ensure high-resolution graphical representation. The indirect effects were assessed using bias-corrected bootstrap 95% confidence intervals, with 5,000 resamples. We first calculated descriptive statistics for all variables. To examine the relationships between variables, we performed a Pearson correlation analysis. To test our proposed model, we performed a serial mediation analysis to examine whether ANI and EM played mediating roles in the relationship between parental verbosity and NSSI. This analysis included both student-reported and parent-reported EM data. All models were adjusted for age, sex, grade, family socioeconomic status, parental education level, marital status, and living arrangements. The statistical significance was set at p < .05. To examine the potential influence of common method bias, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted using all 39 items from the four primary study measures.

Bivariate correlations

Pearson correlation analyses (Table 2) revealed that the NSSI total score was significantly and positively correlated with self-reported ANI (r = .236, p < .01), student-reported EM (r = .374, p < .01), and parent-reported EM (r = .237, p < .01). Parental verbosity showed a significant positive correlation with self-reported ANI (r = .056, p < .05) and parent-reported EM (r = .074, p < .01). The zero-order correlation between parental verbosity and NSSI was near zero and not statistically significant (r = .001), underscoring the critical importance of examining indirect pathways rather than relying solely on bivariate correlations to understand the mechanisms linking parenting behaviors to youth self-injury. Given the theoretical importance of indirect pathways, subsequent path analyses were performed to examine the hypothesized indirect effects.

Primary serial mediation analysis

To test the hypothesized cognitive-emotional cascade, a serial mediation analysis (Model 6) was performed using self-reported ANI and student-reported EM (Table 3; Fig. 1). The direct effect of parental verbosity on NSSI was not statistically significant (B = -0.038, SE = 0.089, p = .674). However, the indirect effect analysis revealed a significant serial mediation pathway: parental verbosity was positively associated with self-reported ANI (B = 0.067, SE = 0.030, p = .025), which predicted higher EM (B = 0.777, SE = 0.056, p < .001), ultimately linking to increased NSSI total score (B = 0.464, SE = 0.037, p < .001). The serial indirect effect (Verbosity → ANI → EMs → NSSI) was statistically significant (Effect = 0.024, 95% CI [0.004, 0.049]). The total indirect effect was non-significant (Effect = 0.026, 95% CI [-0.047, 0.103]), indicating that the overall indirect influence of parental verbosity on NSSI through all pathways combined did not reach statistical significance in the primary model. In contrast, the simple indirect pathway through student-reported EM alone (Ind2: Verbosity → EM → NSSI) yielded a non-significant negative indirect effect (Effect = -0.024, 95% CI [-0.089, 0.039]). The negative sign of this indirect effect can be understood as follows: when we include ANI in the model, it captures the dominant pathway through which Verbosity influences children’s emotional symptoms as perceived by the children themselves. Once ANI is accounted for, there is little additional direct association between Verbosity and student-reported EM (B = -0.051, p = .422). In other words, parental verbosity appears to influence how children report their own emotional symptoms primarily by first affecting their attentional patterns (ANI), rather than having a separate direct effect. This finding is consistent with the possibility that children’s cognitive processing of their attentional tendencies (captured by ANI) may represent one important mechanism linking parental verbal discipline to children’s self-reported emotional distress. Additionally, a significant simple indirect effect through ANI alone was identified (Effect = 0.026, 95% CI [0.003, 0.054]).

Sensitivity analyses

To assess the consistency of our findings across different informants, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by replacing student-reported EM with parent-reported EM (Table 4; Fig. 2). The results were largely convergent with the primary model in their core findings, with one notable difference in the aggregate indirect effect. The direct effect of parental verbosity on NSSI remained non-significant (B = -0.106, SE = 0.092, p = .249). Unlike the primary model, in which the total indirect effect was non-significant due to the suppression effect of Ind2 (see above), the total indirect effect in the sensitivity model was significant (Effect = 0.095, 95% CI [0.030, 0.170]). This difference is attributable to the resolution of the suppression effect: with parent-reported EM, the Verbosity → EM direct path was positive and significant (B = 0.151, SE = 0.063, p = .017), rendering Ind2 positive (+ 0.045) and thus allowing all three indirect pathways to contribute in the same direction to the significant total. Specifically, the serial mediation pathway through ANI and parent-reported EM was statistically significant (Effect = 0.008, 95% CI [0.001, 0.017]). The simple mediation pathway through self-reported ANI alone remained significant (Effect = 0.043, 95% CI [0.006, 0.085]). These findings are broadly consistent with the proposed cognitive-emotional cascade model, although the modest effect sizes warrant cautious interpretation.

We tested an alternative mediator sequence by reordering the mediators (Verbosity → student-reported EM → self-reported ANI → NSSI). This path yielded a non-significant serial indirect effect (Effect = 0.000, 95% CI [-0.009, 0.008]), which was not supportive of the reordered mediator sequence examined here. Specifically, parental verbosity did not directly relate to EM (p = .991) when the preceding role of ANI was excluded. To address concerns regarding reverse causality, an alternative model switching the independent and dependent variables (NSSI → self-reported ANI → student-reported EM → Verbosity) was examined. This reverse serial pathway also failed to reach significance (Effect = 0.000, 95% CI [-0.002, 0.001]). Specifically, student-reported EM did not significantly predict parental verbosity (p = .529).

These empirical checks are inconsistent with the two alternative orderings tested here — specifically, the reversed mediator sequence (EM → ANI) and the reverse causality model (NSSI → EM → ANI → Verbosity). However, it is important to note that non-significant indirect effects in cross-sectional alternative models do not establish temporal precedence. The observed data remain compatible with bidirectional effects, unmeasured common causes, or third-variable confounds not captured in the current design. These tests should therefore be interpreted as not supportive of the specific alternative orderings examined, rather than as confirmatory evidence for the proposed sequence. Longitudinal designs will be necessary to establish the temporal ordering and directionality of these associations.

To address competing explanations, we additionally tested parallel mediation models (Verbosity → ANI → NSSI, Verbosity → EM → NSSI) in which ANI and emotional symptoms were specified as simultaneous mediators. Across both student- and parent-reported models, the indirect pathway through ANI (Verbosity → ANI → NSSI) remained statistically significant. The pathway through emotional symptoms (Verbosity → EMs → NSSI) was not significant (Effect = 0.001, 95% CI [-0.064, 0.063]), but was significant in the pathway Verbosity → EMp → NSSI (Effect = 0.052, 95% CI [0.009, 0.114]). The direct pathway (Verbosity → NSSI) was not statistically significant. We further tested whether ANI moderated the association between parental verbosity and emotional symptoms. However, the interaction term Verbosity × ANI was not significant for either student-reported emotional symptoms (B = 0.013, SE = 0.082, p = .872, 95% CI [-0.148, 0.175]) or parent-reported emotional symptoms (B = 0.083, SE = 0.081, p = .306, 95% CI [-0.076, 0.243]). Therefore, ANI did not significantly moderate the association between parental verbosity and emotional symptoms. Taken together, these results indicate that the serial mediation specification (Verbosity → ANI → EM → NSSI) was more consistent with the observed data than either a parallel mediation or moderation-based alternative, though the cross-sectional design precludes definitive model comparison. Common method bias assessment

To further examine the potential influence of common method bias, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted across all 39 items from the four primary study measures. The first unrotated factor accounted for 15.02% of the total variance, which was well below the conventional 50% threshold for problematic common method bias. This finding suggests that shared method variance is unlikely to fully account for the observed associations. Nevertheless, this result does not fully eliminate the possibility of common method variance, particularly because ANI, student-reported emotional symptoms, and NSSI were all assessed via child self-report.

Discussion

This study identified a significant serial mediation pathway through which parental verbosity was associated with increased NSSI frequency among Chinese pre-adolescents by sequentially affecting self-reported ANI and EM. The observed NSSI prevalence of 23.8% is consistent with recent prevalence estimates among Chinese pre-adolescent samples35. Regarding the proposed cognitive-emotional mechanism, similar patterns were observed across informants, although the magnitude and significance of specific pathways differed between the student- and parent-reported models. In the parent-reported model, the indirect pathway through ANI alone remained significant, and the serial indirect pathway through ANI and parent-reported emotional symptoms was also statistically significant, although relatively small in magnitude (indirect effect = 0.008, 95% CI [0.001, 0.017]). By contrast, the indirect pathway through parent-reported emotional symptoms alone was not statistically significant. These findings suggest that ANI may represent a relatively more stable indirect pathway linking parental verbosity to NSSI across informants, whereas the role of emotional symptoms may vary depending on the reporter. The effect sizes remained modest, and these findings should therefore be regarded as preliminary and in need of replication in longitudinal studies.

Although the bivariate correlation between parental verbosity and self-reported ANI was significant but modest in magnitude (r = .056, p = .025), the path analysis revealed a statistically significant indirect pathway from verbosity to NSSI through serial mediation of ANI and EM (95% CI [0.004, 0.049]). This pattern is consistent with a predominantly indirect association pattern36,37, wherein the distal family stressor was linked to NSSI primarily through indirect pathways involving cognitive and emotional processes. This finding underscores the importance of examining indirect pathways in developmental research, as relying solely on zero-order correlations may mask theoretically meaningful mechanisms38.

The present findings are broadly consistent with the hypothesized model linking parental communication behavior to child psychopathology, although the evidence should be interpreted cautiously. Drawing from experience-dependent learning theory22, we posit that chronic exposure to verbose discipline may condition children to develop heightened self-reported ANI. This mechanism aligns with Patterson’s Coercive Family Process21, suggesting that persistent verbal admonition with excessive reasoning functions as an aversive stressor rather than an effective corrective tool. Repetitive exposure to verbal discipline may be associated with the development of a cognitive style characterized by heightened metacognitive awareness of, and hyper-vigilance toward, negative cues in the environment39. Importantly, our results showed that self-reported ANI significantly mediated the association between verbosity and NSSI frequency both independently (Effect = 0.026) and as part of the serial sequence (Effect = 0.024).

Furthermore, our results are broadly consistent with the affect-as-information model25. In this pathway, self-reported ANI functions as a cognitive mechanism that is associated with amplified emotional distress. A child with a heightened self-reported negativity index is more likely to interpret ambiguous or even neutral situations as threatening39, potentially generating or exacerbating emotional symptoms (EMs). Consistent with functional models of NSSI26, these unmanageable emotional states may then be associated with children engaging in self-injurious behavior as a maladaptive emotion regulation strategy40. This pathway is consistent with one possible route through which a subtle parental communication pattern may be associated with internal cognitive-emotional processes linked to NSSI frequency. Common method variance also remains a consideration, particularly in the primary model, because ANI, student-reported emotional symptoms, and NSSI were assessed via child self-report. At the same time, parental verbosity was assessed via parent-report, and we additionally conducted a sensitivity analysis using parent-reported emotional symptoms to partially reduce reliance on a single informant. Nevertheless, as noted above, shared method variance cannot be fully excluded, and future studies should incorporate multi-method and longitudinal designs41.

Two distinct indirect pathways were identified: (1) a simple mediation pathway through self-reported ANI alone, and (2) a serial mediation pathway through self-reported ANI and EM in series.

Primary mediation: self-reported ANI as a simple (independent) pathway

Consistent with experience-dependent learning theory22, chronic exposure to verbose, repetitive verbal stimuli is associated with a heightened self-reported ANI. This self-reported cognitive tendency, characterized by a perceived difficulty disengaging from threat-related stimuli39, is associated with increased youth NSSI frequency independently of EM, which is consistent with the possibility that self-reported ANI represents one simple mediating pathway (Effect = 0.026). This pathway may be relevant for understanding some impulsive or automatic forms of NSSI40 that are associated with an overwhelming state of cognitive hyperarousal.

Serial mediation: cognitive-emotional cascade (verbosity → ANI → EM → NSSI)

Parental verbosity was associated with higher self-reported ANI, which in turn was associated with increased EM. These elevated emotional symptoms were subsequently associated with greater NSSI frequency, which is broadly consistent with affect-as-information theory25 and with functional models emphasizing the emotion-regulation role of NSSI26,40. This pathway may reflect one possible cognitive-emotional route associated with more deliberate, though maladaptive, coping processes.

A noteworthy difference emerged between the two models. In the primary model, the direct path from parental verbosity to student-reported EM was negligible and non-significant (B = -0.051, p = .422), resulting in a non-significant negative indirect pathway through EM alone (Ind2 = -0.024). This pattern suggests that, from the child’s perspective, the emotional impact of verbose discipline may be experienced mainly through its effect on attentional appraisal. In other words, children’s reports of emotional distress may be shaped more by their tendency to attend to negative information than by the parenting behavior itself. This is consistent with a cognitive mediation account in which ANI serves as a more proximal factor in self-reported distress, whereas verbosity operates as a more distal antecedent. This interpretation remains provisional and should not be taken as evidence of full mediation or temporal ordering. In contrast, in the sensitivity model using parent-reported EM, parental verbosity retained a significant direct positive association with EM (B = 0.151, p = .017). This difference may reflect informant-specific perspectives: parents, as both the agents of verbal discipline and observers of their child’s emotional presentation, may be more sensitive to the direct behavioral manifestations of EM associated with their own parenting behavior, whereas children may experience the emotional impact of verbosity more through the mediating cognitive pathway of heightened attentional negativity. These patterns are consistent with the informant differences noted above, and should be interpreted cautiously given the modest effect sizes.

One finding warrants specific discussion. In the parallel mediation model (Parental Verbosity → ANI / EM → NSSI), the indirect pathway through parent-reported EM was significant, whereas the student-reported pathway was not. This asymmetry may partly reflect a shared reporter effect: parental verbosity and parent-reported EM were both assessed by the same parent respondent, potentially inflating their covariance through common method variance. As reported above, shared method variance is unlikely to account for the overall pattern of associations, yet informant-specific inflation of particular paths cannot be excluded. The consistency of the ANI pathway across informants suggests that this cognitive intermediary may be relatively robust to reporter source, though longitudinal and multi-method designs are needed to disentangle reporter effects from substantive informant differences.

The prevalence of verbosity in this Chinese sample may be consistent with the highly competitive educational environment characteristic of contemporary Chinese society, although direct cultural measurement was beyond the scope of this study. Critically, the present findings extend beyond this contextual observation: the identification of a significant cognitive-emotional cascade (Verbosity → ANI → EM → NSSI) suggests that, regardless of its cultural or situational origins, parental verbosity carries potential psychological consequences through the specific mechanism of heightened attentional negativity. This finding implies that the harm of verbose discipline may lie not in cultural specificity but in its structural properties — properties that are, in principle, worth examining in future longitudinal and cross-cultural research, and only subsequently in parent-focused intervention studies if supported by stronger causal evidence across diverse cultural settings. One interpretation is that parental verbosity may function less as a cultural trait and more as a maladaptive response to external performance pressures, though this interpretation requires direct empirical testing in future research. Future cross-cultural comparisons—especially with Western contexts that emphasize autonomy and direct communication—would be valuable to clarify how different parenting communication patterns are associated with child psychopathology.

Limitations

While this research provides new insights into a specific parental communication pattern and its psychological consequences in pre-adolescence, several issues warrant consideration in future research. First, although the cross-sectional design precludes direct causal inference, our findings identify important statistical associations and possible mechanisms that merit further investigation. Although we tested reverse causality and found no significant pathway from student-reported emotional symptoms to parental verbosity, longitudinal studies tracking these variables throughout pre-adolescence would help clarify temporal ordering and support causal interpretation42. Second, our reliance on self-report measures is a conventional approach in developmental research. The self-reported ANI captures metacognitive awareness of attentional tendencies, which is theoretically meaningful for understanding how children interpret and respond to their environment. Future research could enhance measurement precision by integrating objective behavioral assessments (e.g., dot-probe, eye-tracking) to complement these subjective measures43. Additionally, the Verbosity subscale primarily measures the frequency and duration of verbal discipline, and future studies could incorporate observational coding of parent-child interactions to capture additional dimensions of parental communication. Relatedly, the internal consistency of the Verbosity subscale (α = 0.609) and the SDQ emotional symptoms subscales (α = 0.629 student-reported; α = 0.596 parent-reported) were below the conventional threshold of 0.70, likely reflecting the small number of items and the conceptual heterogeneity of the constructs. Although these values are consistent with those reported in the original validation studies and are within ranges commonly observed in community-based developmental research, future studies should consider using measures with higher internal consistency or supplementing with additional items to improve reliability. Third, although we controlled for demographic variables, we did not control for other dimensions of dysfunctional parenting (e.g., laxness, over-reactivity) due to potential multicollinearity and model complexity. Future studies should examine the unique contribution of verbosity while accounting for these co-occurring parenting behaviors. Fourth, the modest effect sizes (e.g., Effect = 0.024) reflect the multifactorial nature of NSSI etiology. Parental verbosity represents one meaningful mechanism among multiple influences, consistent with developmental psychopathology research where significant indirect effects through serial pathways capture important etiological processes. While larger-scale studies may strengthen precision in estimating these subtle effects, the present findings should be regarded as preliminary and theory-consistent rather than as strong evidence for a confirmed cognitive-emotional mechanism, particularly given the small effect sizes and the non-significant total indirect effect in the primary model. Fifth, the sample was drawn from a single urban district (Dongli District) in Tianjin; thus, findings may not generalize to rural areas, other geographic regions of China with different economic development levels or educational pressures, or regions with different cultural norms regarding parenting and education. Although the urban Chinese context provides theoretical salience for examining parental verbosity given its educational pressures, this very specificity limits the scope of inference. Sixth, common method bias is a notable limitation. Three of the four key variables — ANI, student-reported emotional symptoms, and NSSI — relied on child self-report, raising the possibility that shared method variance inflated the observed associations. Although parental verbosity was assessed via parent-report and Harman’s single-factor test results (reported above) did not indicate severe common method bias, these measures do not fully eliminate shared method variance concerns. Notably, the significant indirect pathway through parent-reported emotional symptoms in the parallel mediation model (Effect = 0.052, 95% CI [0.009, 0.114]) may partly reflect a shared reporter effect, given that parental verbosity and parent-reported emotional symptoms were assessed by the same respondent. Future studies should incorporate multi-method assessments, including objective or physiological measures, to reduce reliance on self-report.

Conclusion

The present findings are consistent with a cognitive-emotional cascade model linking parental verbosity to pre-adolescent NSSI through a sequential pattern of associations involving self-reported ANI and emotional symptoms. These associations should be interpreted cautiously: the observed indirect effects were modest, one model yielded a non-significant total indirect effect, and the cross-sectional design does not permit conclusions about temporal or causal ordering. This research shifts the focus from broad parenting style dimensions toward identifying how a specific, well-defined parental communication behavior is associated with a sequence of cognitive and emotional processes.
