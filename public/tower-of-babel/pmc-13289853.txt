Diagnostic strategies in necrotizing soft tissue infections: from clinical scores to multi-omics and machine learning
Authors: Xiaolin Ji, Xinze Li, Zhongqiu Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNecrotizing soft tissue infections (NSTIs) represent a group of rapidly progressing, life-threatening infections characterized by widespread tissue necrosis, systemic inflammation, and multiorgan failure. Early diagnosis remains a clinical challenge because of nonspecific initial manifestations and overlapping symptoms with other soft tissue infections. Diagnostic scoring systems such as the Laboratory Risk Indicator for Necrotizing Fasciitis score and its variants have been widely utilized to facilitate early recognition but are limited by variable sensitivity and insufficient predictive value across diverse clinical populations. Recent advances in multi-omics technologies and machine learning approaches have enabled the identification of molecular biomarkers and predictive patterns associated with NSTI onset and progression. Integration of high-dimensional omics data with clinical and imaging parameters holds potential for dynamic, real-time diagnostic support, and individualized risk stratification in the intensive care setting. This review summarizes the evolution of diagnostic strategies for NSTIs, critically appraises the limitations of conventional clinical scoring systems, and examines emerging omics-based and ML-driven approaches. Finally, we propose an integrated diagnostic roadmap that aligns clinical assessment, imaging, microbiologic evaluation, host-response biomarkers, and multi-omics data to guide future research and clinical translation.

Background

Necrotizing soft tissue infections (NSTIs) are fulminant, rapidly progressive infections that often culminate in septic shock and multiorgan failure requiring intensive care management [1–3]. They encompass a spectrum of clinical entities, including necrotizing fasciitis (NF), necrotizing cellulitis, and necrotizing myositis. Among these, NF is the most aggressive phenotype, with extensive tissue injury and widespread necrosis leading to persistently high mortality [2, 4–9]. Reported in-hospital mortality rates for NF vary widely, ranging from 10% to 75%, and are influenced by factors such as delayed surgical intervention, advanced age, hypotension, acidosis, bacteremia, renal dysfunction, hyponatremia, peripheral vascular disease, and the presence of myonecrosis or myositis [10].

Clinically, NSTIs are characterized by severe inflammation, progressive necrosis, and toxin-mediated injury. Without timely treatment, these features can rapidly progress to sepsis, multiorgan dysfunction, and death [11, 12]. Early manifestations—including erythema, swelling, and pain—are frequently nonspecific and often mimic benign conditions such as cellulitis or abscesses, thereby complicating timely recognition [13]. Despite advances in clinical and critical care, a substantial diagnostic gap remains. Only 15%–34% of NF patients receive an accurate diagnosis at the time of admission [1, 14], and overall mortality for NSTIs continues to range between 20% and 40%, largely because of delayed recognition and the fulminant progression of the disease [15–20].

To improve early identification, the Laboratory Risk Indicator for Necrotizing Fasciitis (LRINEC) score was developed as a diagnostic screening tool [21, 22]. However, subsequent validation studies have shown that its accuracy was initially overestimated. Reported sensitivities for a threshold of ≥6 range from 43.2% to 80.0%, and for ≥8, they range from 28.6% to 68.4% across different populations and clinical settings. Several studies have also highlighted its limited specificity [23, 24]. For example, Bennett (2008) reported a positive predictive value (PPV) for mortality of only 21% at the recommended threshold (≥6), with substantial mortality still observed in patients below this cut-off, suggesting that the LRINEC reflects generalized inflammation rather than pathophysiological changes specific to NSTIs [25]. The score performs particularly poorly in high-risk populations, such as patients with diabetes, immunosuppression, or intravenous drug use, and its utility in patients with hematological malignancies is especially limited [6, 26–30]. Collectively, these data raise concerns about the incremental value of the LRINEC beyond careful clinical assessment in critically ill populations [23, 31–33]. Imaging modalities [computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS)] can assist in decision-making, but none are definitive, and each may be constrained by clinical instability or resource availability in the intensive care unit (ICU).

Because NSTIs may evolve within hours, immediate surgical exploration and debridement—together with broad-spectrum antibiotics and aggressive sepsis management—remain the cornerstone of care, and each 24-hour delay is associated with a significant increase in mortality [2, 3, 9, 34, 35]. Accordingly, there is a pressing need for diagnostic strategies that accelerate triage and improve specificity without sacrificing sensitivity at the bedside [2, 35].

Recent advances in molecular diagnostics, particularly multi-omics technologies, have substantially improved the diagnostic and prognostic assessment of NSTIs [36, 37]. Integrative approaches combining transcriptomics, immunomics, and metabolomics have identified pathogen-specific and host-response biomarkers, providing valuable insights into NSTI pathophysiology [31, 36, 38]. Moreover, ML techniques are being increasingly applied to multi-omics datasets to enhance early detection, refine risk prediction, and support personalized treatment [39, 40]. The application of artificial intelligence (AI) to multi-omics data holds promise for closing current diagnostic gaps in NSTIs, particularly in the ICU, where early recognition is crucial. This review outlines the transition from conventional scores to AI and omics-based tools and considers their future integration into critical care workflows.

In this narrative review, we summarize the evolution and performance of existing diagnostic tools, critically appraise their applicability to critically ill patients, and outline an ICU-oriented framework that integrates clinical assessment, imaging, and multi-omics/ML approaches to close the diagnostic gap in NSTIs (Figure 1).

Microbiological classification and pathogen-specific epidemiology of necrotizing soft tissue infections

The diverse microbiological spectrum and pathogen-specific epidemiology of NSTIs present significant challenges for clinical diagnosis and management. NSTIs constitute a heterogeneous group of rapidly progressive infections with varying microbial etiologies, degrees of anatomical involvement, and clinical severity. NSTIs are traditionally classified into four types, polymicrobial (Type I), monomicrobial (Type II), and less common Types III and IV, which are associated with marine bacteria and fungi, respectively (Table 1) [10, 41]. Among these, Type I (polymicrobial) and Type II (monomicrobial) are the most prevalent. Type I infections are typically caused by a mix of aerobic and anaerobic organisms, whereas Type II infections are most often due to a single pathogen, commonly Streptococcus pyogenes [11, 42, 43]. Accurate and timely identification of the microbial etiology is crucial for guiding targeted antibiotic therapy and improving patient outcomes [9, 26]. However, overlapping clinical features among NSTI subtypes, difficulties in early pathogen detection, and the potential for rapid disease progression create substantial diagnostic challenges. Thus, prompt recognition of NSTI subtypes remains essential to minimize the morbidity and mortality associated with diagnostic delays.

Classification and characteristics of necrotizing soft tissue infections

Necrotizing soft tissue infections are classified into four types on the basis of their microbiological etiology: Type I, Type II, Type III, and Type IV. Each type differs in terms of risk factors, clinical setting, and disease severity. Data are summarized from previously published studies as cited in references [10, 11, 41–54]

Type I NSTIs represent the predominant subtype and are observed in 70%–80% of clinical cases [44–47]. They usually arise from polymicrobial infections involving both aerobic and anaerobic organisms and commonly originate in the gastrointestinal or genitourinary tract [48]. Patients with diabetes mellitus, peripheral vascular disease, or immunosuppression—especially those who have undergone recent surgical procedures—are at increased risk of developing this form of NSTI [48, 49]. Type II NSTIs are characterized by monomicrobial infections and are usually caused by gram-positive organisms, most commonly Group A Streptococcus (GAS) or Staphylococcus aureus, in ~20%–30% of cases (including methicillin-resistant S. aureus [MRSA]) [43–46, 50, 51]. Given the prominent role of GAS in Type II NSTIs, recent epidemiological surveillance has attracted considerable attention. Notably, a recent analysis revealed that invasive GAS infections significantly increased across 10 U.S. states between 2013 and 2022, with the incidence increasing from 3.6 to 8.2 per 100 000 persons [52]. This form can affect individuals across all age groups, particularly previously healthy patients, particularly after trauma or surgical interventions, and may occur even in the absence of immunosuppression. Type III NSTIs are caused primarily by gram-negative monomicrobial pathogens, most notably marine-related Vibrio species such as Vibrio vulnificus [53] and Vibrio damsela [15, 42, 54–56]. These infections are predominantly reported in warm coastal regions and are strongly associated with seawater exposure or the ingestion of contaminated seafood [55, 57–60]. Although less common, Clostridium species have also been implicated in Type III infections [61]. Despite timely diagnosis and aggressive treatment, Type III NSTIs have a high mortality rate of 30%–40% [53, 60, 62]. Infections caused by marine Vibrio species are being increasingly recognized worldwide, with V. vulnificus NSTIs—manifesting as septicemia or necrotizing wound infections—being especially lethal, with case fatality rates exceeding 50% [63]. Kuehl et al. (2019) further proposed classifying all monomicrobial gram-negative NF as Type III, citing its higher mortality compared with that of Types I and II infections, particularly when due to Enterobacterales [42, 64]. These infections typically affect older patients with comorbidities such as liver cirrhosis or chronic kidney disease and are associated with poor outcomes [64, 65]. Type IV NF is attributed to fungal pathogens, which typically occur after traumatic injuries or burns, with Aspergillus and zygomycetes being the most frequently isolated organisms [45, 66, 67]. Candida-associated NF is exceedingly rare and is usually observed in immunocompromised patients [67].

Microbiological confirmation and diagnostic limitations of necrotizing soft tissue infections

Microbiological confirmation plays a central yet inherently limited role in the diagnosis of NSTIs [68]. The diagnosis of nonpurulent cellulitis is inherently difficult because of nonspecific clinical features and the lack of a validated gold-standard test, which in turn complicates early differentiation from NSTIs [69]. Confirmatory cultures and histopathology therefore play a complementary role in excluding sterile necrotizing mimickers, despite limitations related to delayed availability, low yield, and skin contamination [70–72]. Interpretation is especially challenging when common colonizers such as S. aureus are isolated and require the integration of sampling depth, histopathology, organism burden, and specimen concordance rather than a reliance on culture results alone, underscoring the need for standardized diagnostic reference criteria [73].

Beyond microbiologic ambiguity, NSTI diagnosis is further complicated by early clinical overlap with sterile inflammatory dermatoses such as pyoderma gangrenosum (PG), Sweet’s syndrome, and complex purpuric disorders, all of which may closely mimic necrotizing infection in terms of both inflammatory severity and skin necrosis-like appearance [74]. PG is a type of neutrophilic dermatosis characterized by severe pain and rapidly progressive ulceration with undermined borders and can closely resemble early NSTIs in terms of clinical presentation [75–77]. Sweet’s syndrome may present with acute inflammatory skin lesions and systemic inflammatory features but is distinguished by the absence of deep tissue necrosis or invasive infection and by its characteristic response to immunosuppressive therapy [78]. Cutaneous vasculitic disorders may also manifest with purpura, bullae, or ulceration suggestive of NSTIs; however, these findings reflect primary vascular inflammation or occlusion, and skin biopsy remains critical for establishing the diagnosis [79]. Importantly, reliance on microbiologic results or isolated clinical findings alone is insufficient and may lead to inappropriate surgical intervention or delayed immunomodulatory treatment [69, 80, 81].

Limitations of traditional scoring systems

Inherent limitations of the Laboratory Risk Indicator for Necrotizing Fasciitis score

The nonspecific early presentation of NSTIs, frequently mimicking cellulitis or abscess, contributes to diagnostic delay and high mortality, prompting the development of scoring systems to assist in distinguishing necrotizing from non-NSTIs [42]. The LRINEC score, proposed by Wong et al. in 2004, integrates six laboratory parameters [C-reactive protein (CRP), white blood cell (WBC) count, hemoglobin (Hb), sodium (Na), creatinine, and glucose] into a composite index, with a cut-off of ≥6 points (Table 2) [21]. In the original cohorts, it demonstrated high sensitivity (89.9% and 92.9%) and high predictive performance [PPV 92.0%, negative predictive value (NPV) 96.0%]. These findings position the LRINEC as a potentially valuable tool for differentiating necrotizing from non-necrotizing infections in the emergency setting. However, its reliance solely on nonspecific laboratory data—without accounting for clinical presentation or imaging findings—raised concerns about its applicability in diverse patient populations and atypical cases [19]. Subsequent studies revealed substantial variability in LRINEC performance across populations and clinical settings (Table 3). While moderate accuracy was reported in some cohorts, multiple studies from Europe, Southeast Asia, and the United States reported substantially reduced sensitivity and frequent misclassification of high-risk NSTI patients, with limited improvement even at high cut-off values [82–85]. These discrepancies suggest that demographic, geographic, and pathogen-related factors significantly influence LRINEC performance, although relatively better accuracy has been reported in select populations [86]. These inconsistencies highlight a fundamental limitation of the LRINEC score: it relies exclusively on nonspecific laboratory values and does not incorporate clinical presentation, imaging features, or immune response dynamics. As a result, the LRINEC score often fails to capture early pathophysiological changes unique to NSTIs, reducing its sensitivity in high-risk subgroups and pathogen-specific settings. For example, in Vibrio-associated NF, only 11% of patients exceeded the LRINEC threshold despite high mortality [87]. Similarly, Hansen et al. (2017) reported no correlation between the LRINEC score and key inflammatory cytokines [interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α)], underscoring its inability to reflect host immune activation [31]. Importantly, multiple studies have demonstrated that the LRINEC has limited prognostic value and was not designed to serve as a mortality prediction tool. Large cohort and contemporary studies have repeatedly demonstrated poor discriminatory ability for mortality, despite the identification of other independent prognostic factors, such as age, comorbid cardiovascular disease, lactic acidosis, bacteremia, radiographic gas, and multiorgan dysfunction [29, 88–90].

Clinical consequences of diagnostic delays

Delayed diagnosis and surgical intervention are the principal determinants of mortality in NSTIs [91, 92], with reported amputation rates ranging from 21% to 60% [16, 93, 94]. Surgical delay beyond 12 hours is consistently associated with increased mortality, underscoring the critical importance of early and aggressive surgical management [42, 95, 96]. Cohort data demonstrate that initial nonsurgical management or interhospital transfer usually prolongs the time to surgical intervention, while case-based evidence shows that low or falsely reassuring LRINEC scores may delay exploration despite the development of necrotizing infection [97–99]. Collectively, these findings reinforce that clinical judgement and early surgical consultation must take precedence over scoring systems when NSTI is suspected [97–99].

Taken together, these findings underscore that the LRINEC should remain an adjunctive screening tool and must not delay surgical consultation or exploration when NSTI is clinically suspected.

Modified scoring systems and imaging for diagnosis

Borschitz et al. (2015) refined the LRINEC by adding clinical features, particularly ‘pain out of proportion’, and incorporating discriminative markers such as elevated CRP levels, thereby improving its PPV without compromising specificity [13]. Wu et al. (2021) further refined the LRINEC by adding diabetes, kidney disease, and high-sensitivity CRP, resulting in a modified version, the modified LRINEC (m-LRINEC), demonstrating improved discrimination compared with the original LRINEC score [94]. A similar modification, the MLRINEC, developed by Wu P. H. et al. (2021), incorporated serum lactate levels and liver disease, improving sensitivity (91.8%) and specificity (88.4%) by accounting for comorbidities and dynamic clinical parameters [100]. Several alternative scoring systems have been developed to overcome the limitations of the LRINEC, including the Site of Infection, Immunosuppression, Age, Renal function, and Inflammatory markers score (SIARI) and the Laboratory Risk Indicator for Necrotizing Fasciitis score (LARINF), which integrate clinical context and inflammatory markers and demonstrate improved diagnostic or prognostic performance in selected populations [102,104]. For Fournier’s gangrene (FG), Fournier’s Gangrene Mortality Index (FGMI) includes age, creatinine level, albumin level, lymphocyte percentage, and the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), achieving an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.88 and outperforming the LRINEC in predicting mortality risk [105]. In addition to modified LRINEC-based models, mortality-focused tools such as the Necrotizing Soft Tissue Infection Mortality Score (NECROSIS) incorporate early clinical signs and laboratory abnormalities to support risk stratification, although further validation is needed [106]. Consistent findings from subsequent studies indicate that hyponatremia, elevated WBC count, low hematocrit, advanced age, anemia, and multiple wound sites are stronger predictors of mortality and amputation risk in NSTIs than the LRINEC score is [109,110]. Although advanced tools such as the Japanese Laboratory Risk Indicator for Necrotizing Fasciitis (J-LRINEC) have demonstrated improved diagnostic performance, their clinical adoption remains limited because of complexity, while additional indices, including the Systemic Immune–Inflammation Index (SII), Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), and elevated international normalized ratio (INR >1.5), have been identified as independent predictors of mortality in patients with NF and sepsis [107,111,112]. Importantly, pathogen-specific studies further highlight the prognostic heterogeneity of NSTIs, which cannot be adequately captured by laboratory-only scoring systems. In patients with Aeromonas- and Vibrio-associated NF, mortality has been linked to bloodstream infection, shock, skin necrosis, ineffective antimicrobial therapy, seawater exposure, hyperlactatemia, and low fibrinogen levels, underscoring the influence of microbial etiology and the host response on disease severity and outcome [113,114]. Imaging plays a crucial role in diagnosing NSTIs, especially when laboratory scores fail to provide definitive differentiation. Studies have demonstrated that fascial involvement, a lack of fascial enhancement, and fluid along the deep fascia on CT are strongly associated with NF, outperforming the LRINEC score in early diagnosis [115–117]. The MRINEC algorithm, which incorporates MRI findings such as T2 hyperintensity of the deep fascia, has achieved 94% sensitivity in distinguishing NF from cellulitis, even among patients with low LRINEC scores [103]. POCUS enables rapid bedside assessment of fascial thickening and fluid collection with high sensitivity [118], whereas near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) can be used to differentiate cellulitis from NF on the basis of significantly lower oxygen saturation in NF patients [119]. Collectively, these findings suggest that while modified scoring systems and imaging can support clinical assessment, timely surgical evaluation cannot be replaced when NSTI is suspected.

Multi-omics biomarkers and molecular signatures in necrotizing soft tissue infections

Transcriptomics in necrotizing soft tissue infections diagnosis and prognosis

Recent transcriptomic studies have highlighted both pathogen- and host-specific signatures that hold diagnostic value in NSTIs. Imöhl et al. (2017) analysed S. pyogenes isolates in Germany and reported associations of specific emm types (e.g. emm1 and emm28) and superantigen genes (speA, speG, and speJ) with severe clinical phenotypes such as NF and sepsis [38]. In a murine NF model, Hirose et al. (2019) reported significant transcriptional reprogramming involving 483 genes, including virulence determinants such as sagA (streptolysin S precursor), speB (cysteine protease), and spd (secreted DNase), reflecting pathogen adaptation to the inflammatory microenvironment [120]. Similarly, Jahagirdar et al. (2022) demonstrated that monomicrobial GAS infections were enriched in host cytokine-related genes (e.g. IL1R2 and IL18R1), whereas polymicrobial infections upregulated the expression of chemokine-associated genes (e.g. STAT1 and CXCL9). These findings indicate that transcriptomic profiling can distinguish etiological subtypes of NSTIs and provide early stratification in clinical practice [40].

Further mechanistic insights underscore the role of transcriptomics in revealing host–pathogen interactions. The soluble M1 protein of S. pyogenes, particularly its B-repeat domain, was shown to activate the NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain–containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome through potassium efflux and clathrin-mediated uptake, driving caspase-1-dependent IL-1β release and pyroptotic cell death [121]. In murine models, extracellular M1 protein alone was sufficient to trigger IL-1β secretion, underscoring its dual function as an early immune signal and mediator of hyperinflammation in invasive infections such as NF and streptococcal toxic shock syndrome [121]. Complementary genomic and transcriptomic studies have further revealed mechanisms of persistence and virulence regulation, including fibrinolysis-driven immune evasion [122], fabT mutations, and rocA variable-number tandem repeat (VNTR) deletions [123,124], as well as unique genomic islands and phage elements in virulent S. aureus strains [125]. Beyond prognostication, transcriptomic and immunologic profiling may also aid early differentiation of true bacterial SSTIs from inflammatory mimics (pseudocellulitis), a clinically common source of misdiagnosis; however, evidence remains preliminary and is limited by the lack of a gold standard reference [71]. Collectively, these data highlight the diagnostic utility of transcriptomic profiling for identifying high-risk strains, stratifying infection subtypes, and guiding early therapeutic intervention.

Metabolomics in necrotizing soft tissue infection diagnosis and prognosis

Metabolomic profiling is a powerful tool for identifying biomarkers that capture both physiological and pathogenic processes in NSTIs (Figure 2). Afzal et al. (2020) applied untargeted gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) profiling and network analysis to identify metabolites associated with NSTIs, including ornithine, ribose, urea, and glucuronic acid. These metabolites not only showed differential abundance but also modulated GAS growth and biofilm formation, highlighting their potential as both diagnostic biomarkers and mechanistic mediators of NSTI pathogenesis [36]. Additionally, metabolic biomarkers have been linked to disease severity in NSTI patients. Hansen et al. (2018) reported a reduced L-arginine/asymmetric dimethylarginine (ADMA) ratio as an independent predictor of mortality, demonstrating the prognostic significance of nitric oxide pathway dysregulation in NSTIs [37].

Subsequent studies have consistently reinforced the prognostic importance of renal and metabolic dysfunction in NSTIs. Elevated creatinine levels and a reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) have been identified as independent predictors of in-hospital mortality [126]. Building on this, Liu et al. (2022) developed a multivariable mortality risk model that incorporates renal function (creatinine), muscle injury (CK), coagulation parameters (PT), and invasive ventilation and achieved excellent discrimination (AUC 0.94) [127].

Immunomics and proteomics in necrotizing soft tissue infection prognosis

Immunomic profiling, particularly the measurement of acute-phase reactants, has gained attention as a powerful tool for diagnosing and prognosticating NSTIs. Procalcitonin (PCT) is a biomarker that has shown superior diagnostic performance compared with the LRINEC score. A study by Kishino et al. (2021) demonstrated that serum PCT, with a cut-off of 1.0 ng/ml, achieved 88% sensitivity, 89% specificity, and an NPV of 93%, establishing PCT as a valuable rule-out biomarker for low-risk NF in the emergency setting [128]. In odontogenic NSTIs and FG, simple hematologic and inflammatory indices such as the SII, CRP + NLR, NLR, and CRP/prealbumin (PAB) ratio have shown strong diagnostic and prognostic accuracy and often outperform the LRINEC in early risk stratification [129, 130]. These findings underscore the role of biomarker profiling in early diagnosis and highlight the importance of host immune responses in NSTI progression. In a large-scale prospective study, Hansen et al. (2017) reported that patients with streptococcal NSTI exhibited significantly elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-α, with IL-6 being the most strongly correlated with disease severity [31]. This study demonstrated the utility of cytokine profiling for identifying systemic inflammation and assessing the severity of NSTIs, identifying IL-6 and TNF-α as key diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers [31]. Notably, high baseline pentraxin 3 (PTX3) levels are associated with increased mortality risk, further emphasizing the importance of immune and inflammatory biomarkers [131]. In addition to these immune markers, Chella Krishnan et al. (2016) reported that host genetic variations, including multiple quantitative trait loci, influence the outcomes of NSTIs, with IL-1β acting as a critical mediator [132]. Building on this, LaRock et al. (2020) demonstrated that GAS exploits SpeB-mediated IL-1β activation to facilitate neutrophil-driven bacterial colonization and infection, demonstrating pathogen-mediated manipulation of host inflammatory pathways [133]. These findings are consistent with those of Saijo et al. (2024), who reported that early GAS-NSTI patients often exhibited disproportionately elevated CRP levels despite normal WBC counts, suggesting that discordant biomarker patterns may represent early diagnostic indicators [134]. Understanding these genetic and immune mechanisms not only provides insight into the pathophysiology of NSTIs but also offers a promising avenue for developing more precise diagnostic tools and targeted therapies. In a large prospective multicenter cohort, Palma Medina et al. profiled 36 circulating immune and endothelial proteins and identified thrombomodulin as a highly accurate biomarker for NSTI detection (AUC = 0.95) [135]. Distinct cytokine and tissue-remodeling signatures (IL-2, IL-10, IL-22, CXCL10, Fas ligand, and MMP-9), together with shared shock-associated markers (G-CSF, S100A8, and IL-6), differentiated monomicrobial from polymicrobial NSTIs and were associated with septic shock risk, supporting proteomics-based phenotypic and prognostic stratification [135]. In patients with Group A streptococcal NSTIs, Bergsten et al. demonstrated a dose–response relationship between IVIG administration and plasma neutralization of streptococcal superantigens, indicating measurable functional immune modulation [136]. Such immune readouts are unlikely to support rapid bedside diagnosis but may enable diagnostic refinement through biological phenotyping, helping identify subgroups with distinct host–pathogen interactions and differential therapeutic responsiveness.

However, coagulation abnormalities constitute a pivotal determinant of disease progression. Coagulopathy, in particular, has emerged as a significant marker of systemic organ failure and worsened prognosis. For instance, Ling et al. (2021) demonstrated that an elevated international normalized ratio (INR >1.5) was a strong independent predictor of mortality in patients with NSTIs, highlighting its role as a prognostic marker for severe disease [112]. Furthermore, Johnson et al. (2023) proposed an immunocoagulopathy-based model that incorporated platelet count, neutrophil count, and age and achieved an impressive AUC of 0.829 for in-hospital mortality prediction [137]. This model outperformed the LRINEC score and emphasized the value of the routine complete blood count as an accessible tool for risk stratification. Moreover, thrombocytopenia, another hallmark of coagulation dysfunction, has been identified as a powerful independent predictor of mortality [137]. Together, these findings highlight that both immune markers and coagulation abnormalities serve as critical components for understanding the pathophysiology of NSTIs and improving risk prediction, paving the way for more refined, multidimensional diagnostic models.

Translational applications and machine learning integration

The integration of multi-omics data, including transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic profiles, into machine learning (ML)-based diagnostic platforms is a promising avenue for enhancing early diagnosis and risk prediction in NSTIs. The functional annotation of the core genes of S. pyogenes, such as scfA and scfB, has provided key molecular signatures that can be incorporated into multi-omics and AI diagnostic strategies targeting severe soft tissue infections [138]. Integrating multi-omics data with AI-driven analytics offers the potential to generate personalized diagnostic and prognostic models, thereby enhancing early detection, patient stratification, and treatment optimization in NSTIs.

Machine learning in necrotizing soft tissue infection diagnosis

ML is being increasingly applied to optimize the diagnosis and risk stratification of NSTIs. Jahagirdar et al. (2022) applied unsupervised clustering and random forest models to host–pathogen transcriptomic data to differentiate monomicrobial from polymicrobial NSTIs, thereby capturing etiologically and biologically distinct disease phenotypes rather than performing NSTI versus non-NSTI classification [40]. In the large INFECT cohort (n = 409), Katz et al. (2022) developed a random forest-based decision support system that identified 16 early sepsis-related variables (e.g. lactate, urine output, and blood pressure) to predict in-hospital mortality in NSTI patients, achieving an AUC of 0.91 and outperforming the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, even when limited to variables available within the first hour of admission, highlighting the potential of ML for accurate early mortality risk prediction in NSTIs [39]. HBOmic studies have used transcriptomic profiling and ML to evaluate responses to hyperbaric oxygen therapy, identifying molecular endotypes and biomarkers (e.g. NAT10, DNAJA3, and MTCO2P12) associated with immune restoration and therapeutic responsiveness [139, 140]. Importantly, this approach provides a framework for individualized adjunctive therapy in NSTI-associated sepsis. Similarly, Chang and Wu (2024) applied an interpretable Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) model to distinguish NF from cellulitis and to further classify NF into V. vulnificus and non-Vibrio subtypes, achieving an AUC of 0.86, with SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis identifying key clinical and exposure-related predictors [141]. Challenges and limitations of multi-omics and machine learning in necrotizing soft tissue infection diagnosis

Despite the promising insights provided by multi-omics and ML studies in NSTIs, several methodological limitations must be acknowledged. Most available omics datasets are derived from small, single-center cohorts with substantial heterogeneity in patient characteristics, sampling time points, and analytical platforms, which limits their reproducibility and generalizability. In addition, many studies remain exploratory in nature and lack independent validation cohorts or prospective clinical integration, thereby constraining their immediate translational impact.

Importantly, the current generation of multi-omics platforms is constrained by turnaround time, technical complexity, and cost, which precludes their routine use in real-time bedside diagnosis or determination of surgical timing in acute NSTIs. While these approaches may improve biological understanding and retrospective diagnostic accuracy, it remains unrealistic to expect most omics-based assays to meaningfully accelerate first debridement decisions within current clinical workflows. This limitation represents a critical barrier that must be clearly recognized when interpreting their clinical potential. Rather than serving as immediate diagnostic tools, emerging evidence suggests that multi-omics approaches may be better positioned to support post-diagnostic risk stratification and treatment individualization after NSTI has been confirmed. Biomarker-guided immune and molecular profiling may help identify biologically distinct patient subgroups with differential responses to adjunctive therapies. For example, in Group A streptococcal NSTIs, Bergsten et al. demonstrated a dose–response relationship between intravenous immunoglobulin administration and plasma neutralization of streptococcal superantigens, supporting the concept that functional immune readouts could be leveraged to identify patients most likely to benefit from IVIG and to inform dosing strategies [136].

From an ML perspective, progress in NSTI research will likely require moving beyond isolated single-center models toward larger, multi-institutional efforts. Promising directions include multimodal models that combine clinical variables with microbiology and omics signals, as well as collaborative training strategies (e.g. federated learning) that allow models to be developed across sites while keeping patient-level data within local governance frameworks. Such approaches could help address data scarcity, improve robustness across settings, and support the development of clinically generalizable tools.

Challenges and future directions of necrotizing soft tissue infection diagnosis in critical care settings

NSTIs remain diagnostically challenging, especially in the ICU setting, as their early clinical manifestations are often nonspecific and overlap with those of benign soft tissue infections, leading to frequent misdiagnosis, delayed surgical intervention, and worse clinical outcomes [142, 143]. In parallel, recent multidisciplinary guideline efforts, such as the 2025 Consensus on the Diagnosis and Treatment of Adult Necrotizing Fasciitis, emphasize the lack of standardized diagnostic and treatment protocols and provide evidence-informed recommendations to support clinical standardization [144]. Traditional laboratory-based risk stratification tools, particularly the LRINEC score and its derivatives (e.g. m-LRINEC, MLRINEC, SIARI, and LARINF), demonstrate limited sensitivity, specificity, and external validation in heterogeneous ICU populations, with their performance further compromised by the confounding effects of critical illness and concurrent therapies. Advanced imaging modalities such as CT and MRI may aid in diagnosis but often incur delays because of logistical constraints, patient instability, and interobserver variability. Together with the marked etiological heterogeneity of NSTIs and diverse host factors, including diabetes, immunosuppression, and hematologic malignancy, these limitations substantially hinder timely diagnosis and accurate risk stratification in real-world clinical settings.

Conclusions

Despite ongoing advances in diagnostic methodologies, NSTIs continue to pose significant challenges in critical care settings. Traditional scoring systems such as the LRINEC score demonstrate considerable variability in sensitivity and specificity across diverse populations, limiting their utility, particularly among immunocompromised or comorbid patients. While imaging modalities and laboratory indices provide some diagnostic support, their practical application in the ICU is frequently limited by clinical instability and resource limitations. The emergence of multi-omics technologies—encompassing transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, and immunomics—combined with ML algorithms offers a promising avenue for more accurate, timely, and individualized diagnosis. These integrative approaches enable the identification of pathogen-specific and host–response biomarkers that may facilitate early recognition and risk stratification. Moving forwards, large-scale prospective validation and seamless integration into clinical workflows—particularly DSS within electronic health records—will be essential to translate these innovations into improved outcomes. A paradigm shift toward data-driven, precision diagnostics represents a critical step in narrowing the current diagnostic gap and reducing the high morbidity and mortality associated with NSTIs.
