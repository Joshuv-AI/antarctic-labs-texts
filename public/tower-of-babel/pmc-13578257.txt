Yield stability of groundnut genotypes across contrasting environments in Niger using GGE biplot analysis
Authors: Abdoul-Razak Oumarou Mahamane, Zeinabou Sadissou Souley, Mamadou Coulibaly Adama, Seyni Boureima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGroundnut (Arachis hypogaea L.) productivity in Niger remains low despite its potential, mainly due to erratic rainfall, poor soil fertility, and disease and pest pressure. This study aimed to quantify the effects of genotype, environment, and genotype × environment interaction (G × E), evaluate yield performance and stability, and identify superior genotypes and optimal test environments. A total of 21 groundnut varieties were evaluated across four environments (two locations over two years) during the 2021 and 2022 cropping seasons. Genotype plus genotype × environment interaction (GGE) biplot analysis was used to assess stability and adaptability. Analysis of variance showed significant genotype × environment interaction for pod yield (p < 0.01), while genotype and environment effects were highly significant (p < 0.001) for most agronomic traits. Mean pod yield ranged from 1915.22 kg/ha for Diankadapé to 2990.10 kg/ha for ICGV 93,305, with a grand mean of 2517.26 kg/ha. The highest-yielding genotype, ICGV 93,305, exceeded the grand mean by 18.78% and the lowest-yielding genotype by 56.12%. Other high-yielding genotypes included ICGV-IS 13,989 (2945.88 kg/ha), ICGV-IS 14,857 (2846.22 kg/ha), Pyr-370 (2774.10 kg/ha), and Rafeet Car (2772.55 kg/ha). The GGE biplot explained 80.85% of the total G + GE variation, with PC1 and PC2 accounting for 58.52% and 22.33%, respectively. The mean-versus-stability view identified ICGV-IS 13,989 and SH470P as the most stable genotypes, while the ideal-genotype ranking indicated Rafeet Car, ICGV 93,305, ICGV-IS 13,989, and ICGV-IS 14,857 as desirable genotypes. Environment E1 was the most discriminating and representative test environment. These findings identify stable and high-performing groundnut varieties for varietal recommendation and future breeding in Niger.Keywords: Groundnut, Genotype × environment interaction, Yield stability, GGE biplot, Multi-environment trialsSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Genetics, Plant sciences

Introduction

Groundnut (Arachis hypogaea L.) is an important grain legume and oilseed crop grown widely in tropical and subtropical regions1. It contributes to food security, nutrition, livestock feed, edible oil production, and household income, especially among smallholder farmers2. Groundnut seed is rich in oil (43–55%) and protein (25–28%) and also provides essential vitamins and minerals, making it important for both dietary energy and nutritional quality3,4. Global groundnut production exceeds 50 million tons annually, with Asia and Africa accounting for the largest share5. Recent studies have further emphasized the importance of groundnut in Africa, where production is mainly rainfed and strongly affected by rainfall variability, soil fertility, pests, diseases, and crop management practices5,6.

In West Africa, groundnut serves as both a subsistence and commercial crop, supporting rural livelihoods and local markets7,8. In Niger, it is the second most important legume crop after cowpea in terms of cultivated area and economic contribution, occupying more than one million hectares9. However, national yields remain low, commonly around 600–800 kg/ha10, which is below the regional average of about 1007 kg/ha11. This low productivity is mainly associated with erratic rainfall, poor soil fertility, drought stress, disease pressure, and limited use of improved varieties. These constraints are particularly important in Sahelian production systems, where rainfall is unpredictable and most farmers operate under low-input conditions.

Improving groundnut productivity in Niger requires both long-term breeding and short-term varietal deployment strategies. The development of locally adapted, high-yielding, disease-tolerant, and stable varieties remains essential for sustainable yield improvement. Because varietal development is time-intensive, the introduction and evaluation of high-yielding varieties from diverse agro-ecological backgrounds offer a more rapid pathway to increase productivity, particularly when such materials possess broad adaptation or specific suitability to target environments. Such evaluation can also broaden the genetic base available to national breeding programs, support evidence-based varietal recommendation, and accelerate varietal replacement.

A major challenge in varietal selection is genotype × environment interaction (GEI), where genotype performance changes across locations, years, or management conditions12. GEI complicates selection because a genotype that performs well in one environment may not perform similarly in another. Environment and GEI strongly contribute to yield variation in groundnut. Several studies have confirmed that environment is the largest source of variation of groundnut pod and seed yield across different agroecological zones13–15.

Multi-environment trials are therefore essential for identifying genotypes with high mean performance, broad stability, or specific adaptation. These trials allow breeders to distinguish genotypes that perform consistently across environments from those that are better suited to particular locations. The genotype plus genotype × environment interaction (GGE) biplot is widely used for this purpose because it provides a practical visual framework for identifying stable genotypes, winning genotypes in specific environments, mega-environments, and informative test locations16,17. Recent studies continue to demonstrate the value of GGE biplot analysis in groundnut stability studies, particularly for identifying high-yielding stable genotypes and discriminating test environments14,15,18.

Despite the importance of groundnut in Niger, limited information is available on the yield stability and adaptability of diverse groundnut varieties evaluated across contrasting Nigerien environments. This creates a gap in varietal recommendation because selection based only on mean yield may overlook stability, specific adaptation, and the usefulness of test environments. Accordingly, the present study evaluated 21 groundnut varieties from Niger, Senegal, Mali, Burkina Faso, and ICRISAT-Mali across four environments in Niger. The objectives were to (i) quantify the effects of genotype, environment, and genotype × environment interaction on pod yield and related traits; (ii) assess yield performance and stability using GGE biplot analysis; and (iii) identify superior genotypes and informative test environments for groundnut improvement and varietal recommendation in Niger.

Study location

The experiment was conducted at two locations which include Tarna and Bengou stations of the National Agricultural Research Institute of Niger during the 2021 and 2022 cropping seasons. This resulted in a total of four environments. The Tarna research station is located at 13°28′ North latitude and 7°10′ East longitude. The annual rainfall varies from 230 to 630 mm, which is characteristic of the Sahelian climate. The soils are predominantly sandy, characterized by low water-holding capacity and inherently low fertility. The experimental site has a soil pH of 6.5 and consists of 90% sand, 8% clay, and 2% organic matter. During the experimental period, Tarna recorded total annual rainfall of 503.2 mm in 2021 and 669.6 mm in 2022. The Bengou research station is located at 11°58′45″ North latitude and 3°33′29″ East longitude (Fig. 6). The station consistently records annual rainfall exceeding 1200 mm. The soil is sandy-loam in texture, highly leached, and has a pH of 5.9. During the experimental period, the site received a total annual rainfall of 796.9 mm in 2021 and 1081.2 mm in 2022.

Relationship among environments view of the GGE biplot for the test environments.

Field establishment

The experiment was laid out using a randomized complete block design with three replications (Montgomery,19. At both sites, ploughing was carried out using a tractor prior to sowing. This operation was essential to ensure proper seedbed preparation, improve water infiltration, and promote optimal root development. Each plot measured 1 m × 4 m (4 m²) and consisted of three rows with a total of 27 planting hills, spaced 0.5 m between rows and 0.15 m between hills. The spacing was 0.75 m between plots and 1.5 m between blocks. Sowing was conducted on 10 July 2021 and 6 July 2022 at Tarna, and on 18 July 2021 and 14 July 2022 at Bengou. Throughout the experimental period, standard agronomic and crop management practices recommended for groundnut production in the respective regions were followed to ensure optimal crop establishment and growth.

Data collection

Data were collected from all plants within 1 m2 yield quadrat from each experimental plot. Days to first flowering (DFF) was recorded as the number of days from sowing to the appearance of the first flower in each plot, and days to 50% flowering (D50F) as the number of days from sowing until 50% of plants within a plot had flowered. Maturity date (MAD) was determined as the number of days from sowing to pod maturity, representing the total crop cycle duration for each variety. Dry biomass (BM) was measured as the average dry weight of haulms and pods of plants from the yield quadrat. Pod yield (PY) was determined as the average dry weight of pods of plants from the yield quadrat. Harvest index (HI) was calculated as ratio of seed weight to total biomass weight. Hundred-seed weight (SW) was measured as the weight (g) of 100 randomly selected seeds. Late leaf spot (LLS) severity was assessed 75 days after sowing using a 1–9 scale, where 1 indicated high resistance and 9 indicated high susceptibility.

Data analysis

All analyses were carried out using R software version 4.5.2. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was carried out to evaluate the effects of genotype, environment, and their interaction on pod yield and other agronomic traits. The combined ANOVA model enabled partitioning of total variation and identification of significant interaction effects across the four test environments, following standard procedures for multi-environment trials20. Statistical significance was assessed at probability levels of α = 0.05, 0.01, and 0.001. Differences were considered significant at p ≤ 0.05, highly significant at p ≤ 0.01, and very highly significant at p ≤ 0.001. To further investigate genotype performance and stability patterns, the genotype plus genotype × environment interaction (GGE) biplot methodology was conducted as described by Yan et al.,16 and Yan and Tinker17. The GGE biplot was constructed using the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) derived from singular value decomposition of the environment-centered G + GE matrix. Graphical outputs generated from the GGE analysis included: (i) the Mean vs. Stability view to evaluate average yield performance and stability of genotypes; (ii) the Which-won-where polygon view to identify winning genotypes and delineate possible mega-environments; (iii) the Discriminativeness vs. Representativeness view to assess the testing efficiency of each environment; (iv) the Ranking of Environments relative to an ideal test environment; (v) the Relationship among Environments based on vector angles; and (vi) the Ranking of Genotypes relative to an ideal genotype combining high mean yield and stability. These graphical analyses provided a comprehensive assessment of genotype adaptability and test environment effectiveness across Niger conditions.

Results

Analysis of variance

The combined analysis of variance across environments showed significant genetic variability among the genotypes for most of the traits under study (Table 1). Genotypes had highly significant effects (p < 0.001) on DFF, D50F, LLS, SH, SW, BM and PY, while a significant effect (p < 0.01) was observed for HI. Only MAD showed non-significant variation among the genotypes. The environment exerted a powerful influence on trait expression, with highly significant effects (p < 0.001) detected for DFF, D50F, MAD, SW, and BM. Significant effects (p < 0.01) were also found for LLS, HI, and PY. The interaction between genotype and environment was highly significant (p < 0.001) for D50F, LLS, and SW, while PY showed significant GEI (p < 0.01).

Mean performance

The combined mean performance across the different environments revealed substantial genotypic variability for all traits (Table 2). Pod yield ranged from 1915.22 kg/ha (V3) to 2990.10 kg/ha (V7) with a mean of 2517.26 kg/ha. The highest-yielding genotype was V7 (2990.10 kg/ha), followed closely by V11 (2945.88 kg/ha), V12 (2846.22 kg/ha), V15 (2774.10 kg/ha), and V16 (2772.55 kg/ha). Biomass production had a mean of 4605.28 kg/ha with V15 (6714.19 kg/ha), V7 (6166.46 kg/ha), V16 (5953.76 kg/ha), and V12 (5784.03 kg/ha) producing the highest biomass. Days to first flowering (DFF) had a mean of 22.01 days and days to 50% flowering (D50F) had a mean of 24.73 days. Shelling percentage averaged 70.78% (CV = 10.44%). Higher shelling percentage values were recorded for V9 (74.83%), V1 (74.25%), and V5 (74.17%). Harvest index (HI) averaged 0.34 with V19 (0.44) and V9 (0.40) exhibiting comparatively higher values. Seed weight (SW) showed moderate variability (CV = 13.35%), with V11 (52.25 g), V10 (51.50 g), and V15 (50.00 g) producing the largest seeds.

The GGE biplot indicated that PC1 accounted for 58.52% of the total G + GE variation, while PC2 explained 22.33%. Together, PC1 and PC2 accounted for 80.85% of the total variation, indicating that the biplot adequately represented the performance of the 21 groundnut varieties across the different environments (Fig. 1).

Mean vs. stability

The Mean vs. Stability view of the GGE biplot showed that varieties V15 (Senegal), V7 (Burkina Faso), V12 (ICRISAT-Mali), V16 (Senegal), and V11 (ICRISAT-Mali) were located furthest to the right of the AEC ordinate, indicating they had the highest mean yields across the four environments in Niger (Fig. 2). Regarding stability, V11 (ICRISAT-Mali) and V18 (Burkina Faso) showed the shortest vertical projections to the AEC abscissa, suggesting high stability across years and locations. In contrast, the high-yielding V15 (Senegal) exhibited a long projection, indicating significant instability and high sensitivity to environmental fluctuations between Bengou and Tarna (Table 3).

Which-won-where

The which-won-where (polygon) view of the GGE biplot was used to identify the best-performing genotypes for grain yield across the four test environments and to delineate potential mega-environments (Fig. 3). The polygon view showed V6, V10, V7, V15, V19, and V3 as the vertex varieties. These varieties represent those with the greatest response to environmental interaction, acting as either the highest or poorest yielders in specific environments. The equality (ray) lines drawn from the origin perpendicular to the sides of the polygon divided the biplot into several sectors. Based on the distribution of environment markers, the test environments were grouped into three mega-environments. Mega-environment 1 was represented by E1 and E2, which fell into the sector where V19 and V15 were the vertex genotypes, indicating superior performance in these environments. Mega-environment 2 was represented by E4, where V7 was the vertex genotype, suggesting superior performance in this location. Mega-environment 3 was represented by E3, which did not fall within any sector.

Polygon view of GGE biplot (which–won–where) for 21 groundnut varieties.

Discriminativeness vs. representativeness

The discriminating ability of each environment is assessed by the environment vector, which is the length of the line connecting the biplot origin to the environment marker. A longer vector implies a stronger ability of the environment to distinguish or “discriminate” among the genotypes. In the biplot, E1 had the longest vector length, indicating it was the most discriminating environment where genotypes exhibited the most diverse yield performance (Fig. 4). This was followed by E3, which also showed a strong discriminating force. In contrast, E2 and E4 had relatively shorter vectors, with E2 being the shortest, suggesting it was the least distinguishing environment where genotypes showed similar yield levels. The representativeness of an environment is measured by the angle between the environment’s vector and the AEC axis; smaller angles indicate higher representativeness. E4 and E1 had the smallest angles, signifying that they were the most representative of the average environment. Conversely, E3 had a very large angle (nearly perpendicular) to the AEC axis, indicating that it was a non-representative environment despite its high discriminating power.

Discriminativeness vs. representativeness view of the GGE biplot for the test environments.

Ranking of environments

The GGE biplot was further used to rank the test environments by simultaneously considering their discriminating ability and representativeness (Fig. 5). In this view, the ideal environment is depicted at the center of the concentric circles (indicated by the small circle on the average environment axis). An ideal test environment is characterized by a long vector (high discriminating power) and a small projection onto the AEA ordinate (high representativeness). In the present analysis, E1 was positioned closest to the center of the concentric circles, indicating that it most closely approximated the ideal test environment by combining good discriminating ability with high representativeness. Conversely, E2 was positioned farthest from the center of the concentric circles and closest to the biplot origin, suggesting it has the least discriminating power and deviates most from the ideal environment.

GGE biplot view of the ranking of environments for the 21 groundnut varieties evaluated for pod yield.

Relationship among environments

The relationships among the four test environments are presented in the environment-vector view (Fig. 6). The vectors for E3 and E4 formed a small angle, indicating a positive correlation in genotype ranking during the second year. Similarly, E1 and E2 showed a moderate correlation. However, an obtuse angle was observed between E3 and E2, indicating that genotypes performed in a nearly opposite manner between these two environments.

Ranking of genotypes

An ideal genotype is one located at the center of the concentric circles, combining high mean yield with minimal GEI (high stability). Accordingly, V16 was found to be very close to the center of the concentric circles and is therefore considered the ideal genotype for this set of environments (Fig. 7). Similarly, V7, V11, and V12, which were relatively closer to the ideal genotype (V16), were considered desirable genotypes due to their high and consistent performance. While V15 showed high yield potential (positioned far to the right), its distance from the ideal center suggests it may be more specifically adapted rather than broadly stable. On the other hand, the low-yielding genotypes V6, V21, V13, and V3 were positioned far from the ideal genotype and were considered to be the most unsuitable for production across the tested environments. Selecting genotypes like V16 and V11 would provide the most reliable productivity across varying environmental conditions.

Discussion

The observed variation among genotypes indicates the presence of exploitable genetic diversity, which is essential for effective selection in groundnut breeding4. This aligns with findings from Gangadhara et al.,21, who reported moderate to high genetic variability and high heritability for yield-related traits, suggesting that selection can lead to meaningful genetic improvement. Similarly, Pobkhunthod et al.,15 demonstrated that genotype effects were significant for yield traits, confirming that genetic differences among lines can be effectively utilized in breeding programs.

The strong influence of environmental conditions on trait expression highlights the complexity of yield as a quantitative trait. Yield performance in groundnut is highly dependent on environmental factors such as rainfall, temperature, and soil conditions22. This interpretation is consistent with several studies. Pobkhunthod,15 reported that environment accounted for more than half of the total variation in both pod and seed yield, indicating that environmental conditions are the dominant driver of yield expression. Similarly, Kebede and Tana ,23 found that environment explained up to 84.7% of variation in dry pod yield, while Gangadhara et al.,21 also identified environment as the largest contributor to yield variation. These consistent findings confirm that environmental effects are not only significant but often outweigh genetic effects, which has important implications for breeding.

The presence of genotype × environment interaction suggests that genotypes respond differently across environments, making selection more complex. This pattern is widely reported in groundnut research. Daudi,13 observed highly significant genotype × environment interaction across multiple agroecological zones, indicating that genotype ranking changed across environments. Similarly, Savemore et al.24 reported strong crossover interaction, where no single genotype consistently performed best across all environments. Gangadhara,21 also demonstrated that different genotypes were superior in different locations, confirming the presence of crossover interaction. Genotype × environment interaction is a key constraint in groundnut improvement and must be explicitly addressed in breeding strategies.

Seasonal variation further contributes to differences in genotype performance, reflecting strong genotype × year interaction. Differences between Bengou and Tarna and between years likely reflect variation in rainfall amount and distribution, temperature patterns, soil characteristics, and disease pressure. This agrees with Daudi et al.,13, who reported weak correlations between environments from different years, indicating that seasonal conditions significantly influenced genotype ranking. Similarly, Oteng-Frimpong et al.,25 found significant year × location × genotype interaction, confirming that genotype performance is not stable across seasons. These findings emphasize the need for multi-year evaluation to ensure reliable selection of superior genotypes.

The relationship between biomass production and yield suggests that yield improvement depends more on the efficiency of assimilate partitioning than on total biomass accumulation. This interpretation is supported by Oteng-Frimpong et al.,25, who showed that traits such as plant height and number of pods alone were not sufficient to explain yield differences, emphasizing the importance of physiological efficiency in determining final productivity. Similarly, Pobkhunthod et al.,15 found that yield was more strongly associated with reproductive traits such as number of seeds per plant rather than structural traits like seed size. These findings indicate that breeding programs should focus not only on increasing biomass but also on improving the efficiency of assimilate allocation to pods.

The use of multivariate approaches such as GGE biplot analysis provides a robust framework for understanding genotype performance and interaction patterns. The high proportion of the total variation explained by the first two principal components from the GGE biplot analysis in this study indicates that they adequately described genotype performance and interaction patterns. Previous studies consistently show that the first two principal components capture a large proportion of the total variation, allowing reliable interpretation of genotype stability and adaptability. For example, Oteng-Frimpong et al.,25 reported that the first two components explained up to 92% of the variation, while Pobkhunthod et al.,15 reported values above 60%. Kebede and Tana,23 also reported that the first two components captured more than 80% of the interaction pattern. Navrood et al.,26 reported a higher proportion (94.56%) in a study evaluating ten advanced lines across six environments. GGE biplot analysis is therefore an effective tool for evaluating genotype performance and interaction across environments.

The identification of genotypes that combine high yield with stability is critical for breeding programs, particularly in environments characterized by high variability17. Stability is essential because farmers require consistent performance across seasons rather than occasional high yields. This is consistent with Daudi et al.,13, who identified genotypes that combined high yield and stability across diverse environments and emphasized their importance for wide adaptation.

The evidence of both broad and specific adaptation indicates that different genotypes may be suitable for different production environments. The presence of specific adaptation suggests that genotype recommendation should consider target environments. This pattern is consistent with findings from Kebede and Tana23, who identified genotypes that are specifically adapted to particular environments. Similarly, Pobkhunthod et al.,15 and Gangadhara et al.,21 showed that some genotypes performed consistently across environments, while others were superior only under specific conditions. These findings suggest that breeding programs should adopt a dual strategy, targeting both wide adaptation and specific adaptation depending on the production context.

The evaluation of testing environments in terms of their discriminating ability and representativeness is essential for improving selection efficiency. In this study, the environment that combined strong discriminating power with good representativeness (E1) was the most useful for selecting broadly adapted genotypes, because it could separate genotypic performance while still reflecting the average target environment. This agrees with Pobkhunthod et al.,15, who reported that representative and discriminating locations are ideal for selecting superior peanut genotypes across multilocation trials. Similarly, Kona et al.,14 emphasized that test environments with both high discriminating ability and good representativeness provide greater selection efficiency than environments that are either weakly discriminating or highly atypical. However, environments that are highly discriminating but poorly representative should not be ignored, because they can help detect crossover interaction and identify genotypes with specific adaptation. Therefore, the present findings suggest that future groundnut testing networks in Niger should retain environments that support broad-adaptation selection while also using contrasting environments to capture specific adaptation and improve recommendation accuracy.

Conclusion

The present study revealed significant variation among the 21 groundnut varieties evaluated across four environments in Niger. Genotype effects were highly significant (p < 0.001) for pod yield, biomass, shelling percentage, 100-seed weight, days to first flowering, days to 50% flowering, and late leaf spot severity, while genotype × environment interaction was significant for pod yield (p < 0.01). Mean pod yield ranged from 1915.22 kg/ha for Diankadapé to 2990.10 kg/ha for ICGV 93,305, with an overall mean of 2517.26 kg/ha. The highest-yielding varieties were ICGV 93,305 (2990.10 kg/ha), ICGV-IS 13,989 (2945.88 kg/ha), ICGV-IS 14,857 (2846.22 kg/ha), Pyr-370 (2774.10 kg/ha), and Rafeet Car (2772.55 kg/ha). The GGE biplot explained 80.85% of the total G + GE variation, with PC1 accounting for 58.52% and PC2 for 22.33%. The mean-versus-stability view identified ICGV-IS 13,989 and SH470P as the most stable varieties, while the ideal-genotype ranking showed Pyr-370, ICGV 93,305, ICGV-IS 13,989, and ICGV-IS 14,857 as the most desirable genotypes. The which-won-where analysis separated the environments into three mega-environments, and E1 was identified as the closest to the ideal test environment because it combined discriminating ability and representativeness. These results indicate that Pyr-370, ICGV 93,305, ICGV-IS 13,989, and ICGV-IS 14,857 are promising materials for further evaluation and possible varietal release or recommendation in Niger, while E1 can be prioritized as an informative environment for future groundnut yield trials.
