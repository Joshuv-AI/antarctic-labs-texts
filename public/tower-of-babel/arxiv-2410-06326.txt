Convex Estimation of Gaussian Graphical Regression Models with Covariates
Ruobin Liu, Guo Yu

Abstract
Gaussian graphical models (GGMs) are widely used to recover the conditional independence structure among random variables. Recent work has sought to incorporate auxiliary covariates to improve estimation, particularly in applications such as co-expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) studies, where both gene expression levels and their conditional dependence structure may be influenced by genetic variants. Existing approaches to covariate-adjusted GGMs either restrict covariate effects to the mean structure or lead to nonconvex formulations when jointly estimating the mean and precision matrix. In this paper, we propose a convex framework that simultaneously estimates the covariate-adjusted mean and precision matrix via a natural parametrization of the multivariate Gaussian likelihood. The resulting formulation enables joint convex optimization and yields improved theoretical guarantees under high-dimensional scaling, where the sparsity and dimension of covariates grow with the sample size. We support our theoretical findings with numerical simulations and demonstrate the practical utility of the proposed method through a reanalysis of an eQTL study of glioblastoma multiforme and an analysis of diet on the human gut microbiome.



1
Introduction

Graphical models use graphs to represent conditional independence relationships among components of a random vector
\bm{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{p})
.
In the Gaussian case, this leads to Gaussian graphical models (GGMs), where conditional independences correspond to zeros in the precision matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{-1}
.
Specifically, for a Gaussian random vector
\bm{X}
and
i\neq j
, the variables
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
are conditionally independent given
\bm{X}_{\{1,\dots,p\}\setminus\{i,j\}}
if and only if
\Omega_{ij}=0

[
Lauritzen, 1996
]
.
There has been extensive work on the estimation of GGMs in high-dimensional settings
[
Yuan and Lin, 2007
,
Friedman et al., 2007
,
Meinshausen and Bühlmann, 2006
]
, where sparsity is typically induced on the precision matrix to improve interpretability and estimation accuracy.

A major application of GGMs arises in genomics, where the goal is to infer gene interaction networks from gene expression data.
Such problems are inherently high-dimensional, with the number of genes often exceeding the number of samples
[
Schäfer and Strimmer, 2005
]
.
A prominent example is provided by co-expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) studies, in which gene expression measurements are analyzed jointly with external genetic markers.
These markers may confound gene expression levels, either through local genetic effects or through associations with unmeasured factors influencing gene expression.
This setting motivates the development of Gaussian graphical models that incorporate covariate information, enabling adjustment for such confounding in network estimation.

Specifically, let
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
be the vector of responses and
\bm{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
the associated vector of covariates.
We consider the general model

\bm{X}\>|\>\bm{U}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}\sim N(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}),\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})),

(1)

which allows both the mean vector and the precision matrix of
\bm{X}
to depend on a realization of covariates
\bm{U}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}
.
A commonly used specification assumes that only the mean depends on
\bm{U}
, while the precision matrix remains constant. This specification is also known as the conditional Gaussian graphical model
[
Yin and Li, 2011
]
:

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Gamma}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}},\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{0}.

(2)

Estimation of the unknown parameters in (
2
) is challenging, as the negative log-likelihood associated with (
1
) is not jointly convex in
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Gamma}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{0})
.
Existing approaches typically rely on alternating optimization
[
Rothman et al., 2010
,
Yin and Li, 2011
,
Chen et al., 2018
]
or two-stage procedures
[
Cai et al., 2013
,
Chen et al., 2016
]
.
Despite this nonconvexity, minimax-optimal rates for joint estimation under (
1
) with (
2
) have been established
[
Chen et al., 2018
,
Lv et al., 2022
]
.
Several works have sought to convexify the problem. For instance,
Wang [2015]
propose a convex formulation based on an initial estimate of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
, while
Zhu [2020]
introduce a reparametrization that yields a jointly convex negative log-likelihood.

Model (
1
) with specification (
2
) can also be viewed as a multivariate response regression model, where the primary goal is to improve estimation of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
by leveraging structural assumptions on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{0}
.
In contrast, the covariate-adjusted GGM framework focuses on recovering the sparsity pattern of the precision matrix while accounting for covariate effects through
\bm{U}
.
Several methods in this setting incorporate structure-inducing penalties into the likelihood of (
1
) associated with the specification (
2
); see
Liu and Yu [2025]
for a recent review highlighting connections and distinctions between these closely related lines of work.

While substantial progress has been made in modeling heterogeneous means, relatively little work allows the graph structure itself to vary with covariates; most existing approaches instead assume a common precision matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{0}
across subjects.
However, subject-specific graph structures are plausible in applications such as eQTL studies.
For example, gene C may mediate the co-expression of genes A and B only in the presence of a single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) local to gene C.
In such cases, genes A and B may be conditionally independent given the rest of the network unless a genetic variant is present near gene C
[
Fehrmann et al., 2011
,
Kolberg et al., 2020
,
Rockman and Kruglyak, 2006
]
.

A small number of works have considered models in which both the mean and precision depend on covariates.
Bayesian approaches, for example, allow subject-specific precision matrices by imposing regression or clustering structures on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}

[
Ni et al., 2022
,
Niu et al., 2024
,
Wang et al., 2022
,
Zeng et al., 2025
]
.
However, these methods do not directly address joint estimation of both the mean and precision as functions of covariates within a unified framework.

This work is closely related to
Zhang and Li [2023]
, which extends (
2
) to allow both the mean and the network structure of the responses to depend on covariates.
Specifically, their model specifies (
1
) by taking

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Gamma}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}},\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{0}+\sum_{h=1}^{q}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{h}u_{h}

(3)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p},h=0,1,\dotsc,q
are sparse, symmetric matrices.
As in conditional GGMs, joint estimation of the parameters
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Gamma}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{0},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{1},\dotsc,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{q})
under (
3
) leads to a nonconvex objective. To address this,
Zhang and Li [2023]
adopt a two-stage estimation procedure.
Building on this framework,
Zhang and Li [2025]
propose a multi-task estimator with a cross-task group penalty, while
Meng et al. [2025]
develop debiased nodewise estimators for inference on edge–covariate effects.

Wang and Gao [2025]
introduce a parametrization for covariate-adjusted graphs where the estimated precision matrices are guaranteed to be positive definite. However, their work is developed for small, fixed numbers of covariates
q
and their theoretical guarantees do not immediately allow for
q
to scale with
n
.

In this work, we develop
Covariate-adjusted Sparse Precision with Natural Estimation
(
cspine
), a method for estimating covariate-adjusted GGMs with heterogeneous network effects based on a jointly convex formulation of (
1
).
Like
Zhang and Li [2023]
, our method uses nodewise regression
[
Meinshausen and Bühlmann, 2006
]
to estimate the covariate-adjusted graph structure.
However, by adopting a natural parametrization of the Gaussian likelihood, we obtain a formulation in which each nodewise regression reduces to a convex optimization problem, thereby avoiding the nonconvexity inherent in the existing approaches.

Our main contributions and the organization of the paper are as follows.
In Section
2
, we review the framework of
Zhang and Li [2023]
and introduce the proposed natural parametrization, under which the likelihood in (
1
) is jointly convex in all unknown parameters.
Moreover, the natural parametrization also carries an interpretable mean structure whereby the average level of the responses is mediated by the covariance matrix. This property is detailed in Section
2.4
.
Building on this formulation, Section
3
presents the proposed model and estimation procedure with sparsity-inducing penalties.
Section
4
establishes theoretical guarantees for the proposed estimator, showing that the convex formulation leads to improved scaling of the ambient dimensions
p
and
q
relative to the sample size
n
under assumptions comparable to those in
Zhang and Li [2023]
.
These theoretical advantages are further supported by extensive simulations in Section
5
. Finally, in Section
6
, we illustrate the practical utility of the proposed approach through an analysis of glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) gene expression data and human gut microbiome data.
Proofs of all theoretical results are provided in Appendix
A
.

Throughout this paper, we adopt the following notational conventions unless otherwise specified. Scalars are denoted by regular (non-bold) letters, vectors by bold lowercase letters, and matrices by bold uppercase letters.
For any positive integer
m
, let
[m]=\{1,\ldots,m\}
, and for
j\in[m]
, let
-j
denote the index set
[m]\setminus\{j\}
. For a matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}
and index sets
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
, we write
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}_{\mathcal{I},}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}_{\mathcal{J}}
, and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}
for the submatrices of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}
consisting of rows indexed by
\mathcal{I}
, columns indexed by
\mathcal{J}
, and both rows indexed by
\mathcal{I}
and columns indexed by
\mathcal{J}
, respectively.

2
Model Formulation and Natural Parametrization

In this section, we first review the model formulation of
Zhang and Li [2023]
for estimating covariate-adjusted GGMs in (
1
) via a nodewise regression approach, and discuss the challenges of their two-stage estimation procedure arising from the nonconvexity of the formulation.
We then introduce a convex formulation of (
1
) based on a natural parametrization.

2.1
Nodewise Regression in Covariate-Adjusted GGMs

Suppressing the dependence of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}
on the covariates
\bm{U}
for notational simplicity, the Gaussian model in (
1
) implies that, for each
j\in[p]
,

X_{j}-\mu_{j}\>|\>\bm{X}_{-j},\bm{U}\sim N\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{j,-j}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{-1}_{-j,-j}(\bm{X}_{-j}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}_{-j}),\,\Sigma_{jj}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{j,-j}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{-1}_{-j,-j}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{-j,j}\right).

(4)

By the block matrix inversion formula, (
4
) is equivalent to the linear model with Gaussian errors

X_{j}=\mu_{j}-\Omega^{-1}_{jj}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}_{j,-j}(\bm{X}_{-j}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}_{-j})+\varepsilon_{j},\quad\varepsilon_{j}\sim N(0,\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon_{j}}),\quad\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon_{j}}=1/\Omega_{jj},

(5)

where the expression is understood conditional on
(\bm{X}_{-j},\bm{U})
.
Based on this representation, nodewise regression methods for estimating GGMs
[
Meinshausen and Bühlmann, 2006
]
can be naturally extended to incorporate covariate adjustments, where the regression coefficients and error variance are used to recover the
j
-th column of the precision matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}
.

2.2
Non-Convexity of the Nodewise Regression Under (
3
)

Write
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Gamma}}=(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{1},\dotsc,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{p})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
. Under the specification (
3
) of
Zhang and Li [2023]
, the nodewise regression model (
5
) becomes

X_{j}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{j}+\sum_{k\neq j}^{p}\eta_{jk0}(X_{k}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{k})+\sum_{k\neq j}^{p}\sum_{h=1}^{q}\eta_{jkh}u_{h}(X_{k}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{k})+\varepsilon_{j},

(6)

where
\eta_{jkh}=-[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{h}]_{jk}/\Omega_{jj}
for
k\in[p]\backslash\{j\}
and
h\in\{0\}\cup[q]
; this model is termed
Gaussian graphical regression
in
Zhang and Li [2023]
.
Indeed, the least squares criterion based on (
6
) is not jointly convex in the unknown parameters, due to the product terms
\eta_{jkh}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{k}
.

To address this difficulty,
Zhang and Li [2023]
separate the estimation of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{j}
from that of
\eta_{jkh}
.
Specifically, if
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
were known, the responses may first be centered via
Z_{j}=X_{j}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{j}
, reducing the model to the oracle form

Z_{j}=\sum_{k\neq j}^{p}\eta_{jk0}Z_{k}+\sum_{k\neq j}^{p}\sum_{h=1}^{q}\eta_{jkh}u_{h}Z_{k}+\varepsilon_{j},

(7)

for which the least squares criterion is convex in the
\eta
’s.

In practice, however,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
is not known, and a two-stage procedure is employed.
In the first stage, each
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{j}
is estimated under a simplified working model

X_{j}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{j}+\varepsilon_{j}.

Comparing with the full model (
6
), this working model imposes a strong assumption on the specification (
3
), effectively treating the precision matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{0}
as diagonal and all other matrices
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{h}
as zero matrices, thereby assuming conditional independence among all pairs of responses, which is furthermore not dependent on covariates.
As a result, the first-stage estimator does not account for the network structure and its potential dependence on covariates, and thus may incur non-negligible bias when such dependencies are present.

Given the resulting estimate of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
, the second stage proceeds by forming centered responses
Z_{j}
and estimating the coefficients
\eta_{jkh}
using the oracle model (
7
).
This stage treats the estimate of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
as fixed, thereby inheriting any estimation error from the first stage.

The two-stage procedure introduces additional estimation error, as inaccuracies in estimating
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{j}
propagate to the estimation of
\eta_{jkh}
.
As shown in the theoretical analysis of
Zhang and Li [2023]
, the concentration guarantees are weakened relative to the oracle setting in which
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
is known, since the overall error probability depends on the tuning parameters from the first stage. We revisit these limitations in Section
4.1
.

2.3
A Convex Formulation via Natural Parametrization

These limitations motivate the development of a formulation that enables joint convex estimation without relying on stagewise procedures. Specifically, we reparametrize the Gaussian likelihood using its natural parameters, which decouple the interaction between regression and precision parameters in (
6
).
Recall that the density of a
p
-dimensional Gaussian distribution can be expressed in terms of its natural parameters
(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Theta}}})
, where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Theta}}}=-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}
, as

L(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Theta}}}\>|\>\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})=\exp\left\{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Theta}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}-A(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Theta}}})\right\}.

(8)

Our formulation is motivated by the fact that the cumulant function
A
is jointly convex in the natural parameter
(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Theta}}})
and therefore so is
-\log L(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Theta}}}\>|\>\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})
.
Specifically, define

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}=\diag(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Theta}}=-\diag(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}},

(9)

where
\diag(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}})
is the
p\times p
diagonal matrix formed from
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}
.
Under this parametrization, the nodewise regression model in (
5
) can be written as

X_{j}=\mu_{j}-\Omega^{-1}_{jj}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}_{j,-j}(\bm{X}_{-j}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}_{-j})+\varepsilon_{j}=\theta_{j}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Theta}}_{j,-j}\bm{X}_{-j}+\varepsilon_{j},

which yields a least squares criterion that is jointly convex in
\theta_{j}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Theta}}_{j,-j}
.

In contrast to (
3
), we model covariate dependence through
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Theta}})
rather than
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}})
by specifying

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Gamma}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}},\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Theta}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{0}+\sum_{h=1}^{q}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{h}u_{h},

(10)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
represents the baseline graph structure and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
captures the effect of covariate
u_{h}
on the graph structure.
Writing
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Gamma}}=(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{1},\dotsc,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{p})^{\top}
and letting
\beta_{jkh}=[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{h}]_{jk}
for
k\in[p]\setminus\{j\}
and
h\in\{0\}\cup[q]
,
this specification leads to the following nodewise regression model

\displaystyle X_{j}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{j}+\sum_{k\neq j}^{p}\beta_{jk0}X_{k}+\sum_{k\neq j}^{p}\sum_{h=1}^{q}\beta_{jkh}u_{h}X_{k}+\varepsilon_{j},\quad\varepsilon_{j}\sim N(0,\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon_{j}}),\quad\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon_{j}}=1/\Omega_{jj}

(11)

for
j\in[p]
.
That is,
X_{j}
is regressed on the covariates
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}
, all other responses
X_{k}
for
k\neq j
, and the pairwise interactions between the covariates and the other responses.
Remarkably, the least squares criterion associated with (
11
) is jointly convex in
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{j}
and
\beta_{jkh}
, enabling simultaneous estimation of all parameters and avoiding the nonconvexity inherent in the formulation of
Zhang and Li [2023]
.

The parametrization (
9
) implicitly imposes that
\Theta_{jj}=-1
for all
j\in[p]
.
Under the specification (
10
), this is enforced by setting
[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{0}]_{jj}=-1
and
[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{h}]_{jj}=0
for all
h\in[q]
and
j\in[p]
.
Consequently, the residual variance
\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon_{j}}=1/\Omega_{jj}
, that is, the conditional variance, does not depend on covariates, an assumption also adopted by
Zhang and Li [2023]
.
Moreover,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Theta}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}
share the same sparsity structure, so that estimating the sparsity pattern of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Theta}}
is equivalent to that of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}
.
Accordingly, we focus on recovering the sparsity pattern of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Theta}}
in what follows.

Finally, we note that the proposed parametrization is closely related to, yet distinct from, existing convex formulations.
The transformation in (
9
) is conceptually related to the natural parametrization in
Yu and Bien [2019]
for univariate-response regression and its multivariate extension in
Zhu [2020]
.
However, the scaling by
\diag(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}})^{-1}
in (
9
) is crucial for ensuring that the nodewise regression model in (
11
) remains jointly convex in the presence of covariate adjustments.
This feature distinguishes our approach from the reparametrizations in
Yu and Bien [2019]
and
Zhu [2020]
, which do not accommodate covariate-dependent variance or covariance structures.

2.4
Effect of Covariates and Network Structure on the Mean

Let
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})
denote the natural parameter as in (
8
).
Combining with (
9
) and (
10
) gives the mean as
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})
, where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})
is the covariance of
\bm{X}
given
\bm{U}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}
. The matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})
acts as a propagation operator for the natural parameter
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})
so that the product aggregates the contributions of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}
across co-varying responses to produce
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})
.
Explicitly, the conditional mean of
X_{j}
is decomposed as

\mu_{j}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})=\Sigma_{jj}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})\,\Omega_{jj}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}+\sum_{k\neq j}\Sigma_{jk}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})\,\Omega_{kk}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}.

(12)

The first term in (
12
) captures the contribution of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}
to
\mu_{j}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})
through node
j
itself.
The product
\Sigma_{jj}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})\,\Omega_{jj}
is the variance inflation factor
1/(1-R^{2}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}))
, where
R^{2}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})
is the
R^{2}
of regressing
X_{j}
on
\bm{X}_{-j}
conditional on
\bm{U}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}
.
The intercept
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}
in (
11
) is therefore amplified by a factor that grows with how predictable
X_{j}
is from its network neighbors.
The second term in (
12
) aggregates contributions from all other nodes
k\neq j
.

The decomposition (
12
) allows covariates to have cascading effects on the mean through the estimated network, a plausible phenomenon in many domains.
For instance, recent advances in the understanding of gene regulatory networks point toward network-mediated effects of eQTLs on mean expression levels.
Empirical evidence demonstrates that many eQTLs may alter the expression of distant genes by percolating local perturbations through shared pathways and co-expression networks
[
Boyle et al., 2017
,
Albert et al., 2018
,
Võsa et al., 2021
,
Zhou and Cai, 2021
,
Ružičková et al., 2024
]
.
In the context of the human gut microbiome, the decomposition in (
12
) allows host factors such as diet to influence the abundance of a given taxonomic unit both directly and indirectly, as perturbations to keystone species propagate through cross-feeding and competitive exclusion relationships to reshape the overall microbial community
[
Culp and Goodman, 2023
]
.

3
Estimation

For each
j\in[p]
, define the concatenated parameter vector

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{j}=(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{\top}_{j,0},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{\top}_{j,1},\dotsc,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{j,q}^{\top})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{(p-1)(q+1)}
,
where the
h
-th block collects the parameters

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{j,h}=(\beta_{j1h},\dotsc,\beta_{j,j-1,h},\beta_{j,j+1,h},\dotsc,\beta_{jph})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p-1}.

Under this notation, the nodewise regression model (
11
) can be written compactly as

X_{j}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{j}+\bm{X}_{-j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{j,0}+\sum_{h=1}^{q}(u_{h}\bm{X}_{-j})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{j,h}+\varepsilon_{j},\quad\varepsilon_{j}\sim N(0,\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon_{j}}).

(13)

Figure
1
illustrates how each block
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{j,h}^{\top}
corresponds to the
j
-th row (and, by symmetry, the
j
-th column) of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Theta}}
in (
10
).

Suppose we collect
n
independent observations
\{(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{(i)},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{n}
of responses and covariates from the joint distribution in (
1
).
Let
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}=\left[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{(1)},\dotsc,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{(n)}\right]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times q}

and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}=\left[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{(1)},\dotsc,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{(n)}\right]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
denote the covariate and response matrices, respectively. Let
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denote the
h
-th and
k
-th columns of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}
.
For
j\in[p]
, define the design matrix

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}_{-j}=\left[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}_{-j,0},\ \boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}_{-j,1},\ \dotsc,\ \boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}_{-j,q}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(p-1)(q+1)},

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}_{-j,0}&=\left[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\dotsc,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j-1},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j+1},\dotsc,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{p}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(p-1)},\\
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}_{-j,h}&=\left[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1}\odot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{h},\ \dotsc,\ \boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j-1}\odot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{h},\ \boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j+1}\odot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{h},\ \dotsc,\ \boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{p}\odot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{h}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(p-1)}\;\text{for $h\in[q]$},\end{split}

and
\odot
denotes the elementwise product.

We consider estimating
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{j}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{j}
in (
13
) by solving the following penalized least squares problem

\minimize_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{j},\,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{j}}\,\frac{1}{2n}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}_{-j}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{j}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+g_{j}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{j},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{j}),

(14)

where
g_{j}
is a convex penalty function.
In this paper, we focus on penalties that induce sparsity in
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{j}
, promoting interpretable graph estimates.
By construction, sparsity in
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{j}
corresponds to sparsity in the
j
-th row (and, by symmetry, the
j
-th column) of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Theta}}

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}
via (

).

3.1
Sparse-Group Penalized Estimation

Motivated by applications to eQTL studies, we consider a structured sparsity-inducing penalty for
g_{j}
in (
15
).
Our goal is to capture both elementwise and groupwise sparsity in the coefficients
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{j}
.
Elementwise sparsity within each group
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{j,h}
allows for the possibility that a covariate
u_{h}
affects the conditional dependence between
X_{j}
and only a subset of the remaining
\{X_{k}:k\neq j\}
.
In contrast, groupwise sparsity, where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{j,h}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}}
, indicates that the covariate
u_{h}
has no effect on any conditional dependence structure involving
X_{j}
.

To this end, we consider the convex optimization problem

\minimize_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{j},\,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{j}}\,\frac{1}{2n}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}_{-j}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{j}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{j}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{j}\rVert_{1}+\lambda_{j}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{j}\rVert_{1}+\lambda_{g,j}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{j,-0}\rVert_{1,2}

(15)

where
\lambda_{j}\geq 0
and
\lambda_{g,j}\geq 0
are tuning parameters, and

\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{j,-0}\rVert_{1,2}=\sum_{h=1}^{q}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{j,h}\rVert_{2}

denotes the group lasso penalty
[
Yuan and Lin, 2006
]
.
To ease notation and with the understanding that the nodewise regressions (
15
) are independent, we will suppress the notational dependence of
\lambda
and
\lambda_{g}
on the node
j
.
Together, the
\ell_{1}
and group penalties on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{j}
form a sparse-group lasso penalty
[
Simon et al., 2013
]
.
In contrast, only an
\ell_{1}
penalty is applied to
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{j,0}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{j}
, allowing for unrestricted elementwise sparsity in the baseline precision matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{0}
and in the covariate effects on the mean vector.

To estimate the diagonal entries of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}
, an estimate of the noise variance
\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon_{j}}
is required. While several estimators are available in the literature on error variance estimation in high-dimensional linear regression
[
Reid et al., 2016
,
Yu and Bien, 2019
]
, we adopt

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{\varepsilon_{j}}=\frac{\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}_{-j}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}_{j}\rVert_{2}^{2}}{n-\widehat{\mathrm{nnz}}},

(16)

where
\widehat{\mathrm{nnz}}
denotes the number of nonzero entries of
(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}}_{j}^{\top},\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}_{j}^{\top})^{\top}
as in
Zhang et al. [2020]
.

3.2
Optimization

Following
Zhang and Li [2025]
, we use the augmented Lagrangian method with semismooth Newton subproblem solvers of
Zhang et al. [2020]
to solve (
15
). This method achieves superlinear local convergence by exploiting the second-order sparsity of the sparse-group proximal operator and we have found it to outperform first order methods in our experimentation.
Let
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}_{j}=[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}},\,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}_{-j}]
,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{c}}_{j}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j}
, and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{j}=(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{j}^{\top},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{j}^{\top})^{\top}
, so (
15
) can be written as

\min_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{j}}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}_{j}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{c}}_{j}\rVert_{2}^{2}/2n+g(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{j})
,
where
g(\cdot)
is the sparse-group lasso penalty.
Its dual problem is

\min_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}},\,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{s}}}\;\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{c}}_{j},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\rangle+\frac{n}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\|_{2}^{2}+g^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{s}}),\qquad\text{s.t.}\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{s}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}},

where
g^{*}
is the convex conjugate of
g
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{s}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}
are dual variables.
This dual problem is solved via the augmented Lagrangian method (ALM), with the subproblem in each ALM update solved using a semismooth Newton step, which is outlined inAlgorithm
1
.

Specifically, consider the proximal operator
\operatorname{prox}_{g}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}})=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}\{g(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}})+\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}\rVert_{2}^{2}/2\}
and set
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}^{k}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}})=\tau_{k}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{j}^{k}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}
.
The ALM subproblem at iteration
k
amounts to minimizing

\psi_{k}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}})=\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{c}}_{j},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\rangle+\frac{n}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\|_{2}^{2}+g^{*}\!\bigl(\operatorname{prox}_{g^{*}/\tau_{k}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}^{k}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}))\bigr)+\frac{\tau_{k}}{2}\bigl\|\operatorname{prox}_{g}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}^{k}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}))\bigr\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{2\tau_{k}}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{j}^{k}\|_{2}^{2}.

(17)

Step 1 in Algorithm
1
solves
\min_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}}\psi_{k}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}})
via semismooth Newton iterations using the generalized Jacobian of
\operatorname{prox}_{g}
.
The proximal map decomposes as
\operatorname{prox}_{g}=\operatorname{prox}_{\lambda_{g}\|\cdot\|_{1,2}}\circ\operatorname{prox}_{\lambda\|\cdot\|_{1}}
, and only groups
G_{h}
satisfying
\|[\operatorname{prox}_{\lambda\|\cdot\|_{1}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}})]_{G_{h}}\|_{2}>\lambda_{g}
contribute to the Jacobian.
This second-order sparsity means each Newton solve operates on a dense system whose size is determined by the solution’s active set. We refer the reader to
Zhang et al. [2020]
for details.

3.3
Implementation Details

The penalized nodewise regression problem (
15
) involves two tuning parameters:
\lambda
controlling elementwise sparsity and
\lambda_{g}
controlling groupwise sparsity. We select
(\lambda,\lambda_{g})
via
k
-fold cross-validation.
Equivalently, we reparametrize the penalty using a global sparsity penalty
\lambda_{0}>0
and a mixing parameter
\alpha_{s}\in[0,1]
,
so that the penalty in (
15
) becomes

g_{j}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{j},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{j})=\alpha_{s}\lambda_{0}(\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{j}\rVert_{1}+\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{j}\rVert_{1})+(1-\alpha_{s})\lambda_{0}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{j,-0}\rVert_{1,2}.

For fixed
\alpha_{s}
, we compute solutions along a regularization path in
\lambda_{0}
. Following standard practice, we standardize the columns of the design matrix within each nodewise regression to ensure even penalization of the parameters.

As in standard nodewise regression approaches to GGMs, each row of the precision matrix is estimated separately, and the resulting estimate is not necessarily symmetric
[
Meinshausen and Bühlmann, 2006
]
.
In our setting, each nodewise regression in (
15
) estimates one row of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Theta}}
, and asymmetry arises due to both independent row estimation and the different row scaling factors in (
9
).
To enforce symmetry, we apply a post hoc symmetrization step after performing all nodewise regressions.
Specifically, with a solution
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}_{j}
to (
15
) and the corresponding error variance estimate in (
16
), set

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}_{j}=-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}_{j}/\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{\varepsilon_{j}}

and construct symmetric matrices
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}}_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
for all
h=0,1,\dotsc,q
with entries

[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}}_{h}]_{jk}=[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}}_{h}]_{kj}=\tilde{\beta}_{jkh}\mathds{1}\left[\lvert\tilde{\beta}_{jkh}\rvert<\lvert\tilde{\beta}_{kjh}\rvert\right]+\tilde{\beta}_{kjh}\mathds{1}\left[\lvert\tilde{\beta}_{jkh}\rvert>\lvert\tilde{\beta}_{kjh}\rvert\right],

where the expression
\mathds{1}\left[P\right]
is the indicator function of the argument
P
: it equals
1
if
P
is true and
0
otherwise.
This corresponds to the “and-rule” symmetrization, under which
[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}}_{h}]_{jk}
is nonzero only if both
\tilde{\beta}_{jkh}
and
\tilde{\beta}_{kjh}
are nonzero.
An alternative is the “or-rule,” defined by

[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}}_{h}]_{jk}=[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}}_{h}]_{kj}=\tilde{\beta}_{jkh}\mathds{1}{\left[\lvert\tilde{\beta}_{jkh}\rvert\geq\lvert\tilde{\beta}_{kjh}\rvert\right]}+\tilde{\beta}_{kjh}\mathds{1}{\left[\lvert\tilde{\beta}_{jkh}\rvert\leq\lvert\tilde{\beta}_{kjh}\rvert\right]},

which retains an edge if either coefficient is nonzero.
We adopt the more conservative “and-rule”, noting that both approaches are asymptotically equivalent
[
Meinshausen and Bühlmann, 2006
]
and have been widely used in the GGM literature
[
Cai et al., 2011
,
Liu et al., 2017
,
Li and Maathuis, 2021
,
Tran and Yu, 2022
,
Cai et al., 2013
,
Zhang and Li, 2023
]
.

Finally, the mean vector and precision matrix are recovered via (
9
) as

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{(i)})

\displaystyle=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}}_{0}+\sum_{h=1}^{q}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}}_{h}u^{(i)}_{h}\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{(i)})=(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{(i)}))^{-1}\diag(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}})\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Gamma}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{(i)}.

(18)

As with other graphical model methods based on independent neighborhood selection, the resulting estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{(i)})
in (
18
) may fail to be positive definite. One consequence is that
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{(i)})
is not immediately estimable. In such cases, a post-hoc rescaling may be applied to ensure positive definiteness as described in
Zhang and Li [2023]
.

We present theoretical guarantees for the estimator defined in (

Assumption 1

Suppose

Define the population Gram matrix

There exist constants

4.1
Understanding the Theoretical Guarantees

We conclude this section with a discussion of the
\ell_{2}
estimation error and a comparison with the results of
Zhang and Li [2023]
.
The same insights apply to the support recovery guarantees in Theorem
2
.

In
Zhang and Li [2023]
, two rates of estimation error are established: an
oracle
rate and a
two-stage
rate.
The oracle rate is achieved when the true
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
in (
3
) is known, so that the centered responses can be constructed exactly and the estimation in (
7
) can be carried out directly.
The resulting rate is

\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\eta}}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\eta}}_{j}\rVert_{2}^{2}\precsim\frac{\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon_{j}}}{n}(s_{\beta}\log(ep)+s_{\beta,g}\log(eq/s_{\beta,g})),

(20)

which holds with probability at least
1-C_{1}\exp[-C_{2}\{s_{\beta}\log(ep)+s_{\beta,g}\log(eq/s_{\beta,g})\}]
for some positive constants
C_{1},C_{2}
.

In practice, however,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
is unknown and must be estimated. As reviewed in Section
2.2
,
Zhang and Li [2023]
employ a two-stage procedure in which
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
is estimated via the misspecified lasso regression

\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}}_{j}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{j}}\frac{1}{2n}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{j}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{1}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{j}\rVert_{1},

(21)

where
\lambda_{1}\geq 0
,
and the resulting estimate is used to construct centered responses
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{j}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}}_{j}
, which are then used in the second-stage regression (
7
).

Although the resulting two-stage estimator can attain the oracle rate (
20
), the associated probability bound depends critically on the tuning parameter
\lambda_{1}
in (
21
).
Specifically, suppose that
\lambda_{1}\propto\sqrt{\kappa_{1}\log q/n}
for some
\kappa_{1}>0
.
Standard lasso results imply that the first-stage estimation error satisfies
\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{j}\rVert_{2}^{2}/n\precsim s_{\gamma}\cdot\kappa_{1}\log q/n
with probability at least
1-3\exp(-\kappa_{1}\log q)
.
To ensure that this error does not dominate the second-stage estimation, a union bound must be applied over all
p\times q
columns of the design matrix. This leads to the overall success probability of at least
1-C_{3}\exp[C_{4}\{\log p-(\kappa_{1}-1)\log q\}]
for positive constants
C_{3},C_{4}
.

This dependence introduces a nontrivial tradeoff: if
\kappa_{1}
is too small, the concentration probability deteriorates. If
\kappa_{1}
is too large, the first-stage penalty becomes overly aggressive, increasing the bias in the first-stage estimator and potentially inflating the overall error. In addition, when
\log p
dominates
\log q
, the success probability may no longer decay to zero, further weakening the theoretical guarantees.

By contrast, our estimator matches the oracle rate without requiring a separate centering stage, thereby avoiding error propagation from the first stage and eliminating the need to tune an additional penalty parameter.
Furthermore, the oracle rate in
Zhang and Li [2023]
is established under the scaling conditions
\log(pq)=O(n^{1/6})
and
s_{\gamma}+s_{\beta}=o(n^{1/6})
. In comparison, Assumption
4
is strictly weaker and allows for a wider range of scaling regimes. It permits

\log(pq)
and
s_{\gamma}+s_{\beta}
to grow at faster rates relative to
n
, e.g.,

\log(pq)=O(n^{1/4}/\log(n))
and
s_{\gamma}+s_{\beta}=o(n^{1/4})
, while still ensuring consistency.

These theoretical advantages in our framework stem from the joint convexity of (
15
), which enables simultaneous estimation of
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}})
without intermediate approximation steps. In contrast, the two-stage procedure in
Zhang and Li [2023]
requires additional control over the first-stage estimation error, leading to more restrictive conditions.
We provide additional empirical evidence of these advantages in Section
5
, particularly in settings where accurate first stage estimation in
Zhang and Li [2023]
is challenging.

0.1
.
We generate the baseline precision matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{0}
using a preferential attachment algorithm
[
Barabási and Albert, 1999
]
with power parameter equal to
1
. Among the
q
covariates, half are drawn from
\Bernoulli(0.5)
and half from
\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)
. The continuous covariates are then centered and standardized to have unit variance. To introduce covariate-dependent effects, we select
q_{e}=5
covariates to have nonzero influence and construct the matrices
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{h}
for
h\in[q_{e}]
as Erdős–Rényi graphs
[
Erdős and Rényi, 1959
]
with edge probability
v_{e}=0.01
. This choice ensures that the covariate-dependent graphs are sparser than the population graph
[
Clauset et al., 2009
]
.

Given the graph structure, the nonzero entries of
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{h}}
are generated independently from the distribution
\mathrm{Unif}([-0.5,-0.35]\cup[0.35,0.5])
. To ensure diagonal dominance of the resulting precision matrix, each row
j
is scaled by

\sum_{h}\sum_{k\neq j}\lvert\beta_{jkh}\rvert\times 1.5
followed by symmetrization of each
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{h}
via averaging
\beta_{jkh}
and
\beta_{kjh}
. This construction mirrors the data-generating mechanism in
Zhang and Li [2023]
.

5.2
Model Specification and Experimental Setup

For each observation
i\in[n]
, we construct the precision matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{(i)}
via

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{0}+\sum_{h=1}^{q}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{h}u_{h}^{(i)}
, setting all entries of
\diag(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{(i)})
to be
1
, so that
\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon_{j}}=1
.
To construct the mean, we introduce
\delta\in[0,1]
and set

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{(i)})=(1-\delta)\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Gamma}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{(i)}+\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{(i)})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Gamma}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{(i)}.

(22)

The parameter
\delta
controls the relative influence of direct covariate effects versus covariance-mediated network effects. Note that
\delta=0
results in data sets where
RegGMM
is correctly specified while
\delta=1
means
cspine
is correctly specified. Both models are misspecified for intermediate values of
\delta
.
Finally we generate responses via
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{(i)}\sim N_{p}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}^{(i)},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{(i)})
for
i\in[n]
.

We fix
p=25
responses and consider
q=50
and
q=100
covariates.
For each setting, we generate
100
independent data sets with sample sizes
n=200
and
n=400
.
We compare against the nodewise regression method of
Zhang and Li [2023]
(referred to as
RegGMM
) as well as the multi-task extension in
Zhang and Li [2025]
(
mt-RegGMM
).
We found that the method of
Wang and Gao [2025]
has intractable runtime in the large
q
setting and therefore exclude it from comparison.
When
\delta=0
in (
22
), we additionally compare against an oracle estimator (
RegGMM-oracle
) where the true
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
is plugged into
RegGMM
; the performance of
RegGMM-oracle
reflects the baseline oracle rate in (
20
).
The tuning parameters for all methods were selected using
5
-fold cross-validation over a path of
100
penalties.
For
RegGMM
and
mt-RegGMM
, the stage 1 step uses
10
-fold cross-validation to select
\lambda_{1}
over a path of
100
penalties in (
21
).

We report the mean and standard error of the following performance metrics:

TPR
(true positive rate of detected edges across all
\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{h}\}
),
FPR
(false positive rate of detected edges across all
\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{h}\}
),
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{\mathrm{err}}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{j}\rVert_{2}
(nodewise estimation error), and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}_{\mathrm{err}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}}^{(i)}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{(i)}\rVert^{2}_{F,\mathrm{off}}/n
(precision matrix estimation error over off-diagonal entries).

6
Data Application

We demonstrate our method on two real data sets. The first is a glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) eQTL study, where we reanalyze data from
Zhang and Li [2023]
to illustrate how
cspine
recovers SNP-modified gene co-expression networks. The second is a dietary survey study from the American Gut Project, where we examine how dietary factors affect the conditional dependence structure of gut microbiome abundances.

6.1
GBM eQTL Analysis

Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) is the most malignant type of brain cancer and patient prognosis is typically very poor.
Although there has been research on the genetic signaling pathways involved in the proliferation of GBM, it remains largely incurable; see
Hanif et al. [2017]
for a survey.
It is important to understand the conditional independence structure of genes involved in GBM in order to discover new drug therapies
[
Kwiatkowska et al., 2013
]
.
Our estimated graphs describe the conditional independence of co-expressions in a gene network; hence, we refer to estimated networks and effects of SNPs on this network.

We reanalyze a GBM eQTL data set that was reported in

6.2
AGP Microbiome Analysis

The human gastrointestinal tract is an extraordinarily dense and dynamic microbial ecosystem, hosting trillions of microorganisms that have co-evolved with the host to perform essential physiological, metabolic, and immunological functions.
These microorganisms are intimately linked by cross-feeding, where metabolites produced by one microbe are consumed by another, resulting in interdependent microbial communities; see
Culp and Goodman [2023]
for a review.
Moreover, recent research suggests that diet plays a major role in the composition of this ecosystem; see for example
Conlon and Bird [2014]
,
Klimenko et al. [2022]
,
Snodgrass and Velayudhan [2026]
among many others.
It is of clinical and biological interest to understand how dietary factors may affect the complex interrelationships of human gut bacteria.

We analyze data from the American Gut Project (AGP), a large, crowd-funded study of the human microbiome
[
McDonald et al., 2018
]
. AGP participants submit samples and fill out detailed lifestyle and diet surveys. 16S rRNA sequencing is applied to the samples, operational taxonomic units (OTUs) are identified, and the OTU abundances are recorded.
Estimating the inverse covariance of OTU abundances has been applied to AGP data before in
Kurtz et al. [2015]
. Here we provide a brief analysis of the effect of diet on the OTU abundance network using subject-level dietary survey responses as covariates.
We selected
p=20
OTUs to analyze, representing the top
20
most abundant OTUs in our data.
The
q=11
covariates are binarized responses indicating the frequency of consuming various types of grains, seafood, fruits, vegetables, meats, sugar, and alcohol, with responses “rarely” and “never” mapped to
0
and all other responses to
1
.
Subsetting to “omnivores” only, our final data set consisted of
n=367
subjects with no missing data.
For each subject we followed the standard procedure of converting raw OTU counts into relative abundances followed by a center log ratio transform
[
Kurtz et al., 2015
]
.

Figure

The OTU 4478125 is highly connected in this graph. It corresponds to the species
Faecalibacterium prausnitzii
, which is one of the most abundant bacteria in the human microbiome
[
Sabater et al., 2025
]
.
We find that it is highly connected to OTUs in the genus
Bacteroides
.
This is an interesting finding as
F. prausnitzii
has been shown to stabilize the gut environment and benefit the physiology of the host
[
Snodgrass and Velayudhan, 2026
]
. The cross-feeding relationships between
F. prausnitzii
and members of different genera, including
Bacteroides
, have also been observed before
[
Rios-Covian et al., 2015
,
Wrzosek et al., 2013
]
.
Another interesting observation is the high connectivity of OTU 4365130, identified with the species
Parabacteroides distasonis
, which recent research suggests to play a key role in human health
[
Ezeji et al., 2021
]
.

7
Discussion

In this work, we propose a new framework for covariate-adjusted Gaussian graphical models based on a natural parametrization that yields a jointly convex formulation.
This approach enables unified estimation of both the covariate-dependent mean and covariate-dependent precision matrix, in contrast to the two-stage procedure of
Zhang and Li [2023]
.
Furthermore, the natural parametrization is more than a computational convenience. The resulting mean structure
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})\,\diag(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}})\,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Gamma}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}
describes an interpretable mechanism in which covariates influence the mean response through broader network effects.
Our theoretical analysis shows that the convex formulation permits more relaxed scaling conditions on the sparsity level of the true parameter relative to the sample size
n
. These advantages are supported by our simulation results, which demonstrate improved performance in regimes where stagewise methods are challenged by error propagation.

The proposed framework is flexible and does not impose restrictions on the form of the penalty functions
g_{j}
in (
14
), allowing for a wide range of sparsity structures. Incorporating more structured penalties is a promising direction for future work. For instance, one may consider hierarchical penalties that reflect the relationship between covariate effects on the mean and precision matrix, where a covariate influencing the precision matrix is expected to also affect the mean, but not necessarily vice versa.
Another direction is to develop tuning-free variants of the proposed method, for example by adapting ideas from the square-root lasso
[
Belloni et al., 2011
]
.
Additionally, methods for statistical inference could be developed by applying debiasing techniques to each nodewise regression
[
Meng et al., 2025
]
. From a theoretical perspective, it would be of interest to establish minimax optimality of the estimator in (
15
). While our results achieve the same rate as in
Zhang and Li [2023]
, the optimality of this rate remains an open question.

Zhang and Li [2023]
, with modifications made to accommodate our concatenated design matrix
[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}]
.
We will first recall several lemmas from the literature and then prove some supporting lemmas before moving onto the main proofs.

A.1
Technical Lemmas

Lemma 1

(
Bellec et al. 2018
Lemma 1)
.

Let
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be any convex function and let

\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}\in\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\rVert_{2}^{2}+g(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}})\right\}

where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then for all
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\frac{1}{2n}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}\rVert_{2}^{2}+g(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}})+\frac{1}{2n}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}})\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2n}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\rVert_{2}^{2}+g(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}).

Lemma 2

(
Graybill and Marsaglia 1957
Theorem F)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\sim N_{p}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}},\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}_{p})
and let
A
be a
p\times p
idempotent matrix with rank
r\leq p
.
Then
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}^{\top}A\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}/\sigma^{2}\sim\chi^{2}_{r}
.

Lemma 3

(
Laurent and Massart 2000
Lemma 1)
.

Suppose that
U\sim\chi^{2}_{r}
. For any
x>0
it holds that

\PP(U-r\geq 2\sqrt{rx}+2x)\leq e^{-x}.

Lemma 4

(
Vershynin 2011
Proposition 5.16)
.

Let
X_{1},\dotsc,X_{n}
be independent, mean zero sub-exponential random variables. Let
v_{1}=\max_{i}\lVert X_{i}\rVert_{\psi_{1}}

where
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\psi_{1}}
is the sub-exponential norm. Then there exists a constant
c
such that for any
t>0
we have

\PP\left(\left\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right\rvert\geq t\right)\leq 2\exp\left\{-c\min\left(\frac{t^{2}}{v_{1}^{2}n},\frac{t}{v_{1}}\right)\right\}.

Lemma
5
comes from Theorem 4.1 in
Kuchibhotla and Chakrabortty [2022]
applied to marginally sub-exponential random vectors.

Lemma 5

(
Kuchibhotla and Chakrabortty 2022
Theorem 4.1)
.

Let
\bm{Z}_{1},\dotsc,\bm{Z}_{n}
be independent random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
and let
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}=[\bm{Z}_{1},\dotsc,\bm{Z}_{n}]^{\top}
.
Assume each element of
\bm{Z}_{i}
is sub-exponential with bounded sub-exponential norm for all
i\in[n]
;

\lVert Z_{ij}\rVert_{\psi_{1}}=\sup_{d\geq 1}d^{-1}(\EE\lvert Z_{ij}\rvert^{d})^{1/d}\leq K_{z}
for all
i\in[n]
and
j\in[p]
for some constant
K_{z}>0
.
Let
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}/n
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}}=\EE[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}/n]
.
Define

\Upsilon_{n}=\max_{j,k}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Var(Z_{ij}Z_{ik}).

Then we have

\sup_{\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\rVert_{0}\leq k,\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\rVert_{2}\leq 1}\left\lvert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\right\rvert\precsim k\sqrt{\frac{\Upsilon_{n}\log p}{n}}+K_{z}^{2}\frac{k(\log n\log p)^{2}}{n}

with probability at least
1-O(1/p)
.

With Assumptions
1
and
2
, we may apply Lemma
5
to our design matrix
[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}]
because
it is elementwise sub-exponential, each entry being the product of a sub-Gaussian and a bounded random variable.
Furthermore, we have by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality that

\max_{\ell,k}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Var\left([\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}]_{i\ell}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}]_{ik}\right)\leq\max_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3},\ell_{4}}\EE\left(X_{\ell_{1}}^{(1)^{2}}X_{\ell_{2}}^{(1)^{2}}U_{\ell_{3}}^{(1)^{2}}U_{\ell_{4}}^{(1)^{2}}\right)=O(1)

since the entries of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}^{(1)}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}^{(1)}
have bounded moments.
Thus
\Upsilon_{n}=O(1)
in our setting.

Lemma 6

(
Loh and Wainwright 2012
Lemma 12)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
be a symmetric matrix such that
\lvert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\rvert\leq\delta_{1}
for all
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
with
\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\rVert_{2}=1
and
\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\rVert_{0}\leq 2s
.
It holds for all
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
that

\lvert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\rvert\leq 27\delta_{1}\left(\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{s}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\rVert_{1}^{2}\right).

Treating
j
as fixed and suppressing the dependence of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}_{-j}
on
j
, recall that the sample and population Gram matrices are defined as

\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}=\frac{1}{n}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}]^{\top}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}],\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}=\EE\left(\frac{1}{n}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}]^{\top}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}]\right).

Lemma 7
.

For a set of indices
S\subset[p(q+1)-1]
,
denote by
[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}]_{S}
the submatrix of
[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}]
with columns indexed by
S
.
Under Assumptions
1
–
4
,
there exist constants
M_{uw}
and
C_{0}
such that with probability at least
1-C_{0}\exp(-\log(pq))
we have

\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}]_{S}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}\leq M_{uw}

for all
S
satisfying
\lvert S\rvert\leq\hat{s}^{\mathrm{max}}_{\gamma}+\hat{s}^{\mathrm{max}}_{\beta}
, provided that
(\hat{s}_{\gamma}^{\mathrm{max}}+\hat{s}_{\beta}^{\mathrm{max}})\log(pq)=O(\sqrt{n})
, as assumed in Theorem
1
.

Proof.

Letting
k=\lvert S\rvert
, it suffices to show that

\sup_{\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\rVert_{0}\leq k,\,\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\rVert_{2}\leq 1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}

is bounded with the provided probability. We may write

\displaystyle\sup_{\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\rVert_{0}\leq k,\,\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\rVert_{2}\leq 1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\rVert_{0}\leq k,\,\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\rVert_{2}\leq 1}\left\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}\right)\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\right\}.

The second term is bounded by Assumption
3
.
For the first term, by Lemma
5
we have

\sup_{\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\rVert_{0}\leq k,\,\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\rVert_{2}\leq 1}\left\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}\right)\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\right\}\precsim k\left(\frac{\log(pq)}{n}\right)^{1/2}+\frac{k\log(pq)^{2}}{n/(\log n)^{2}}

with probability at least
1-C_{0}\exp(-\log(pq))

and we see that the right-hand side is
o(1)
by Assumption
4
.
∎

A.2
Proof of Theorem
1

For ease of notation, we will drop the dependence of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{j}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{j}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}_{j}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}_{-j}
on
j
.
Let
S_{\beta},S_{\gamma},\hat{S}_{\beta},\hat{S}_{\gamma}
be the support sets of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}}
, respectively.
Let
S_{\beta,g}=\{h\in[q]:\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{h}\neq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}}\}
index the blocks
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{h}
of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
that are not identically zero and let
\hat{S}_{\beta,g}
be the corresponding block indices for
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}
.
For any vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}
and set of block indices
S
, let
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{(S)}
denote the sub-vector containing blocks in
S
.
Let
s_{\beta},s_{\gamma},s_{\beta,g},\hat{s}_{\beta},\hat{s}_{\gamma},\hat{s}_{\beta,g}
be the number of elements in

S_{\beta},S_{\gamma},S_{\beta,g},\hat{S}_{\beta},\hat{S}_{\gamma},\hat{S}_{\beta,g}
, respectively.
Our proof occurs in three steps.

Step 1:

In this step we bound the error
\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}\rVert_{2}^{2}/n
by the stochastic term
\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}\rangle/n
, which is then bounded by a projection of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}
onto the columns of
[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}]
.

Since our penalty function

g(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}})=\lambda\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}\rVert_{1}+\lambda\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\rVert_{1}+\lambda_{g}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{-0}\rVert_{1,2}

is convex, by Lemma
1
we have

\frac{1}{2n}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}\rVert_{2}^{2}+g(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}})+\frac{1}{2n}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2n}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\rVert_{2}^{2}+g({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}})

where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
.
Since
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
we may write

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2n}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2n}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2n}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\rVert_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{n}\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}\rangle+\frac{1}{2n}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Plugging this into the previous expression and substituting the penalty expression then yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lambda\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}}\rVert_{1}+\lambda\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}\rVert_{1}+\lambda_{g}\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}_{-0}\rVert_{1,2}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\frac{1}{n}\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}\rangle+\lambda\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}\rVert_{1}+\lambda\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\rVert_{1}+\lambda_{g}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{-0}\rVert_{1,2}.

Using the triangle inequality for the
\ell_{1}
norm (and analogously for the group norm) yields

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{n}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lambda\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}_{S_{\gamma}^{c}}\rVert_{1}+\lambda\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{S_{\beta}^{c}}\rVert_{1}+\lambda_{g}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{(S_{\beta,g}^{c})}\rVert_{1,2}\\
&\qquad\leq\frac{1}{n}\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}\rangle+\lambda\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}_{S_{\gamma}}\rVert_{1}+\lambda\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{S_{\beta}}\rVert_{1}+\lambda_{g}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{(S_{\beta,g})}\rVert_{1,2}.\end{split}

(23)

Now let
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
be arbitrary index sets of the columns of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}
respectively.
Denote by
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}
the orthogonal projection onto the columns of
[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}]
indexed by
(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J})
.
Let
\mathcal{I}_{0}=S_{\gamma}\cup\hat{S}_{\gamma}
and
\mathcal{J}_{0}=S_{\beta}\cup\hat{S}_{\beta}
denote the unions of the true and estimated support sets of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
.
We seek to bound the stochastic term

\begin{split}\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\varepsilon}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}\rangle&=\langle\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{I}_{0},\mathcal{J}_{0}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}),\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}\rangle\\
&\leq\lVert\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{I}_{0},\mathcal{J}_{0}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})\rVert_{2}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}\rVert_{2}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{2a_{1}}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\frac{a_{1}}{2}\lVert\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{I}_{0},\mathcal{J}_{0}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})\rVert_{2}^{2}.\end{split}

(24)

Step 2:

Following
Zhang and Li [2023]
, we first bound the term
\lVert\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{I}_{0},\mathcal{J}_{0}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})\rVert_{2}^{2}
with a counting argument.
For fixed
s^{\prime}_{\gamma}
,
s^{\prime}_{\beta}
, and
s^{\prime}_{\beta,g}
, let

\mathcal{H}(s^{\prime}_{\gamma},s^{\prime}_{\beta},s^{\prime}_{\beta,g})=\{(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J})\subset[q]\times[(p-1)(q+1)]:\lvert\mathcal{I}\rvert=s^{\prime}_{\gamma},\,\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert=s^{\prime}_{\beta},\,\lvert g(\mathcal{J})\rvert=s^{\prime}_{\beta,g}\}

where
g(\mathcal{J})
is the number of nonzero groups of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{\mathcal{J}}
.
We show that

\log\lvert\mathcal{H}\rvert\leq s^{\prime}_{\gamma}\log\frac{eq}{s^{\prime}_{\gamma}}+s^{\prime}_{\beta,g}\log\frac{eq}{s^{\prime}_{\beta,g}}+s^{\prime}_{\beta}\log(ep).

Define
k_{0}
to be the exponential of the right-hand side, so that
\lvert\mathcal{H}\rvert\leq k_{0}
.
For any
(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J})\in\mathcal{H}
, Lemmas
2
and
3
yield, after a union bound,

\PP\left(\sup_{(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J})\in\mathcal{H}}\lVert\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})\rVert_{2}^{2}\geq 5\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}{\left[s^{\prime}_{\gamma}\log\frac{eq}{s^{\prime}_{\gamma}}+s^{\prime}_{\beta,g}\log\frac{eq}{s^{\prime}_{\beta,g}}+s^{\prime}_{\beta}\log(ep)\right]}+\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}t\right)\leq e^{-t/3}.

(25)

Bounding
\lVert\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{I}_{0},\mathcal{J}_{0}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})\rVert_{2}^{2}
and
r=\sup r(s^{\prime}_{\gamma},s^{\prime}_{\beta},s^{\prime}_{\beta,g})
via the union bound and the KKT conditions gives the concentration bound

\PP\left\{r\geq\tilde{M}\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}(s_{\gamma}\log(eq/s_{\gamma})+s_{\beta}\log(ep)+s_{\beta,g}\log(eq/s_{\beta,g}))\right\}\leq c_{1}\exp\left\{-c_{2}E_{j}/\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}\right\}.

(26)

Using the KKT optimality conditions and Lemma
7
,

\lambda^{2}\hat{s}_{\gamma}+\lambda^{2}\hat{s}_{\beta}+\lambda_{g}^{2}\hat{s}_{\beta,g}\leq\frac{2}{n}M_{uw}\left\lVert\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{I}_{0},\mathcal{J}_{0}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\varepsilon}})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\frac{2}{n}M_{uw}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

(27)

Combining (
25
)–(
27
) and plugging into (
23
)–(
24
) gives

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{n}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lambda\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}_{S^{c}_{\gamma}}\rVert_{1}+\lambda\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{S^{c}_{\beta}}\rVert_{1}+\lambda_{g}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{S^{c}_{\beta,g}}\rVert_{1,2}\\
&\quad\leq\frac{5a_{1}a_{2}}{2(a_{2}-2)}E_{j}+\frac{a_{1}a_{2}}{2(a_{2}-2)}\cdot\frac{r}{n}+\lambda\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}_{S_{\gamma}}\rVert_{1}+\lambda\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{S_{\beta}}\rVert_{1}+\lambda_{g}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{S_{\beta,g}}\rVert_{1,2}\end{split}

(28)

where
E_{j}=(\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}/n)\cdot\left(s_{\gamma}\log(eq/s_{\gamma})+s_{\beta}\log(ep)+s_{\beta,g}\log(eq/s_{\beta,g})\right)
.

Step 3:

We proceed to bound the penalty term by
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}^{1/2}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}\end{pmatrix}\right\rVert
.
Standard
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}
norm inequalities along with Assumption
3
lead to

\displaystyle\lambda\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}_{S_{\gamma}}\rVert_{1}+\lambda\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{S_{\beta}}\rVert_{1}+\lambda_{g}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{S_{\beta,g}}\rVert_{1,2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2C}{\sqrt{\phi_{0}^{\ast}}}\sqrt{E_{j}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}^{1/2}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}\end{pmatrix}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq a_{3}\frac{C^{2}}{\phi_{0}^{\ast}}E_{j}+\frac{1}{a_{3}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}^{1/2}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}\end{pmatrix}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

for any
a_{3}>0
.
We then bound the difference between
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}/n
and
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}^{1/2}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}\end{pmatrix}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
using Lemmas

and
6

Choosing

a_{2}=6
,
a_{3}=6

\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}\rVert_{2}^{2}\precsim\frac{\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}}{n}\left(s_{\gamma}\log(eq/s_{\gamma})+s_{\beta}\log(ep)+s_{\beta,g}\log(eq/s_{\beta,g})\right)

with probability at least

for some positive constants

A.3
Proof of Theorem
2

The proof occurs in three steps.

Step 1:

We show that with high probability

\left\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}\end{pmatrix}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{3}{2}(\lambda+\lambda_{g}).

(29)

By the KKT conditions we know that an optimizer
(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}})
satisfies

\left\lvert\left([\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}]^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}})/n\right)_{\ell}\right\rvert\leq\lambda+\lambda_{g}

for all
\ell\in[p(q+1)-1]
.
By the triangle inequality, to show (
29
) it suffices to show that with high probability

\left\lVert\frac{1}{n}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}]^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{\lambda+\lambda_{g}}{2}.

(30)

The products
U_{\ell}\varepsilon
and
X_{\ell}U_{h}\varepsilon
are sub-exponential; by Lemma
4
and a union bound with
\log p\asymp\log q
,

\PP\left(\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}]^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\varepsilon}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\geq\frac{\lambda+\lambda_{g}}{2}\right)\leq 2\exp(-c_{3}\log p)

for some
c_{3}>0
.

We also establish the cone condition

\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}_{S_{\gamma}^{c}}\rVert_{1}+\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{S_{\beta}^{c}}\rVert_{1}\leq 4\tau_{j}(\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}_{S_{\gamma}}\rVert_{1}+\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{S_{\beta}}\rVert_{1})

(31)

using the optimality of
(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}})
and Hölder’s inequality on the stochastic term, where
\tau_{j}=1+\sqrt{(s_{\gamma}+s_{\beta})/s_{\beta,g}}
as in Assumption
5
.

Step 2:

We bound the diagonal and off-diagonal entries of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}
.
By Lemma
4
and Assumption
3
,

\PP\left(\frac{\phi^{\ast}_{0}}{2}\leq\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}(\ell,\ell)\leq 2\phi^{\ast}_{1}\right)\geq 1-2\exp(-c_{6}n).

(32)

By Assumption
5
,

\PP\left(-\frac{1}{c_{0}(1+8\tau_{j})(s_{\beta}+s_{\gamma})}\leq\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}(k,\ell)\leq\frac{3}{c_{0}(1+8\tau_{j})(s_{\beta}+s_{\gamma})}\right)\geq 1-2\exp(-c_{7}n).

(33)

Step 3 and Final Step:

Define
\tilde{S}=S_{\gamma}\cup\{q+i\mid i\in S_{\beta}\}
.
Using the bounds from Steps 1–2, we establish the restricted eigenvalue condition

\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}]\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}\end{pmatrix}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\geq\left(\frac{\phi^{\ast}_{0}}{2}-\frac{1}{c_{0}}\right)\left\lVert\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}\end{pmatrix}_{\tilde{S}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

(34)

Combining (
29
), (
31
), and (
34
) yields

\left\lVert\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}\end{pmatrix}_{\tilde{S}}\right\rVert_{2}\leq 3(\lambda+\lambda_{g})(1+4\tau_{j})\left(\frac{c_{0}}{c_{0}\phi^{\ast}_{0}-2}\right)\sqrt{s_{\gamma}+s_{\beta}}.

Finally, applying the
\ell_{\infty}
bound from (
29
) and the matrix inequality

\left\lVert\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}\end{pmatrix}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{2}{\phi^{\ast}_{0}}\left\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}\end{pmatrix}\right\rVert_{\infty}+\frac{6}{c_{0}\phi^{\ast}_{0}(1+8\tau_{j})(s_{\gamma}+s_{\beta})}\left\lVert\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}\end{pmatrix}\right\rVert_{1},

we obtain

\left\lVert\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\nu}}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}\end{pmatrix}\right\rVert_{\infty}=\frac{3}{\phi^{\ast}_{0}}(\lambda+\lambda_{g})\left(1+\frac{6(1+4\tau_{j})^{2}}{(1+8\tau_{j})(c_{0}\phi^{\ast}_{0}-2)}\right)

as desired.
\blacksquare

Appendix B
Sufficient Conditions for Theoretical Results

While Assumptions
1
–
4
are enough to prove Theorem
1
and the addition of Assumption
5
for Theorem
2
, it is desirable to present a set of interpretable sufficient conditions.
The key difference in the theoretical analysis of our proposed method versus that of
Zhang and Li [2023]
is that, due to the stage 1 centering in (
21
), the columns of the design matrix in
Zhang and Li [2023]
are centered. By contrast, the design matrix
[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}]
of our proposed method contains the terms
x^{(i)}_{j}u^{(i)}_{h}
where it does not necessarily hold that
\EE(x^{(i)}_{j}\>|\>\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{(i)})=0
, but does obviate the need to bound a noisy design matrix.

One consequence of the above is that the matrix
\EE(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}_{\mathrm{ZL}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}_{\mathrm{ZL}}/n)
is a covariance matrix whose spectrum is easily bounded via bounds on the eigenvalues of
\Cov(\bm{U})
and
\Cov(\bm{X}\>|\>\bm{U})
; these are Assumptions 1 and 2 in
Zhang and Li [2023]
.
Meanwhile, our analogous matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}
is an (uncentered) Gram matrix. Nevertheless, Theorems
1
and
2
follow from interpretable bounds on the eigenvalues of
\Cov(\bm{U})
and
\Cov(\bm{X}\>|\>\bm{U})
, plus additional assumptions that control the mean term.

B.1
Sufficient Conditions for Theorem
1

Assumption 6
.

The covariates
\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are i.i.d. mean zero random vectors with covariance matrix satisfying

\phi_{0}\leq\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\Cov(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{(i)}))\leq\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\Cov(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{(i)}))\leq\phi_{1}

for some constants
0<\phi_{0}\leq\phi_{1}<\infty
.

Assumption 7
.

Recall that
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})=\Cov(\bm{X}\>|\>\bm{U}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})
.
Suppose for constants
\psi_{0},\psi_{1}>0
we have

\psi_{0}\leq\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{(i)}))\leq\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{(i)}))\leq\psi_{1}.

Assumptions
6
and
7
are analogous to Assumptions 1 and 2 in
Zhang and Li [2023]
respectively, and are standard for results in the high-dimensional setting.

Next, as our design matrix is the concatenated
[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}]
, we must restrict the cross-covariance between
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}
.

Assumption 8
.

For any vectors
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(p-1)(q+1)}
define the correlation of projections

\rho^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}})=\operatorname{Corr}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{(i)},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}^{(i)}\right)

and let
\rho^{(i)}_{\min}=\min_{\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}\rVert_{2}=1,\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}\rVert_{2}=1}\rho^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}})
denote the minimal canonical correlation between
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{(i)}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}^{(i)}
.
We assume there exists a constant
\rho_{0}
satisfying
0\leq\rho_{0}<1
such that
\rho^{(i)}_{\min}>-\rho_{0}
for all
i\in[n]
.

Assumption 9
.

There exist constants
M_{\mu},M_{u,1}>0
such that
\EE\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})\rVert_{2}^{4}\leq M_{\mu}
and
\EE\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}\rVert_{2}^{4}\leq M_{u,1}
.

Assumption 10
.

There exists a constant
M_{\infty}>0
such that
\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})\rVert_{\infty}\leq M_{\infty}
.

Together, Assumptions
6
through
10
are sufficient to imply Assumptions
3
and
2
, as summarized in the following propositions.

Proposition 1
.

Assumptions
1
and
6
–
9
imply Assumption
3
.

Proposition 2
.

Assumptions
1
,
7
, and
10
imply Assumption
2
.

B.2
Sufficient Conditions for Theorem
2

With Assumption
10
we have

\displaystyle\max_{k\neq\ell}\lvert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}(k,\ell)\rvert\leq\max_{\ell_{1}\neq\ell_{2}}\left\lvert\left[\Cov(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{(1)})\right]_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}\right\rvert\times\left\lvert\max_{\ell_{3}\neq\ell_{4}}\sup_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}\left[\Cov(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{(1)}\>|\>\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})\right]_{\ell_{3},\ell_{4}}+M_{\infty}^{2}\right\rvert.

Therefore, Assumption
5
is satisfied when the covariances of
\bm{X}
and
\bm{U}
are small and the mean of
\bm{X}
is small.

B.3
Proof of Proposition
1

We wish to prove that Assumptions
1
and
6
–
9
imply Assumption
3
.
It suffices to upper and lower bound
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}
for all unit vectors
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}
.
Express the block matrix form as

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}}&\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}^{(12)}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}^{(12)\top}&\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}\end{pmatrix}

and let
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}=(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},u_{1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},\dotsc,u_{q}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})\in\mathbb{R}^{(p-1)(q+1)}
be a row of the data matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}
.
Express an arbitrary unit vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}
as
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}})
where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(p-1)(q+1)}
.
Further express
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}
as
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}_{0},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}_{1},\dotsc,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}_{q})
where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{p-1}
,
h=0,\dotsc,q
.
Let
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}=[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}_{1},\dotsc,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}_{q}]\in\mathbb{R}^{(p-1)\times q}
and define
c(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}_{0}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}
.

Lower bound:

Since
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}
is centered,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}\geq\phi_{0}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}\rVert_{2}^{2}
by Assumption
6
.
By Assumptions
6
and
7
and the law of total variance,

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}\geq\psi_{0}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}_{0}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\psi_{0}\phi_{0}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}\rVert_{F}^{2}\geq\phi_{0}^{\prime}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}\rVert_{2}^{2}

where
\phi_{0}^{\prime}=\min(\psi_{0},\psi_{0}\phi_{0})
.
By Assumption
8
,

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}^{(12)}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}\geq-\rho_{0}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}\right)^{1/2}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}\right)^{1/2}.

Completing the square yields

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\geq(1-\rho_{0}^{2})\frac{\phi_{0}\phi_{0}^{\prime}}{\phi_{0}+\phi_{0}^{\prime}}=:\phi_{0}^{\ast}.

Upper bound:

By Assumption
6
,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}\leq\phi_{1}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}\rVert_{2}^{2}
.
By Assumptions
6
,
7
, and
9
,

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}\leq(\phi_{1}^{\prime}+\phi_{2}^{\prime})\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}\rVert_{2}^{2}

where
\phi_{1}^{\prime}=\max(\psi_{1},\psi_{1}\phi_{1})
and
\phi_{2}^{\prime}=\sqrt{8}M_{\mu}^{1/2}(1+M_{u,1}^{1/2})
.
By the AM-GM inequality and all in all,

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\leq 2\max(\phi_{1},\phi_{1}^{\prime}+\phi_{2}^{\prime})=:\phi^{\ast}_{1}.\quad\blacksquare

B.4
Proof of Proposition
2

We show that Assumptions
1
,
7
, and
10
imply Assumption
2
.
Write
X_{i}-\EE X_{i}=(X_{i}-\mu_{i}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}))+(\mu_{i}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})-\EE[\mu_{i}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})])
.
Conditionally on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}
,
X_{i}-\mu_{i}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})\sim\mathcal{N}(0,[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})]_{ii})
, which is sub-Gaussian with norm bounded by

by Assumption
7
.

\lvert\mu_{i}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})-\EE[\mu_{i}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})]\rvert\leq 2M_{\infty}

\blacksquare
