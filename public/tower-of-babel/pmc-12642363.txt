Untangling pre-residency and residency determinants of career choice among internal medicine residents: an observational study
Authors: Diana C Bouhassira, Cancan Zhang, Neal Biddick, Jason A Freed, Nikki DeMelo, Kenneth J Mukamal, C Christopher Smith
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFor most physicians trained in internal medicine (IM), residency represents the last opportunity to compare and contrast generalist versus subspecialty careers. Decisions about subspecialty practice impact physician supply and distribution in the United States. This study tested the association between randomly assigned intern year rotation duration and career choice and assessed the importance of resident characteristics versus residency experiences in predictive modeling.MethodsThis retrospective observational study included all categorical residents graduating from a university-affiliated IM program between 2017 and 2023 (N = 338). The association between randomly assigned intern year rotation duration and career choice was evaluated with univariable and multivariable log-link Poisson regression models. “Career choice” was defined as the field in which residents were practicing one year after residency. Predictive models were built using random forest modeling to compare the relative importance of resident characteristics and residency experiences.ResultsPre-residency career interests and ultimate career choice were highly concordant (P < 0.001, Cramer’s V 0.50 (95% CI: 0.41–0.51)), particularly among residents pursuing cardiology, gastroenterology, and hematology/oncology. Rotation duration and career choice were not consistently associated; in multivariable analyses, weeks on service in the intensive care unit were associated with a career in pulmonary/critical care (RR 1.40, 95% CI 1.23–1.56), but there was no association between rotation duration and career in hematology/oncology, cardiology, gastroenterology, or general medicine. Internally cross-validated predictive models revealed that pre-residency demographics and interests were highly discriminative of career choice (AUC 0.824). The addition of residency experiences did not improve model performance (AUC 0.829).ConclusionsPre-residency career interests are a key determinant of career choice and hold greater weight in predictive models than intern year clinical exposure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08240-1.Keywords: Graduate medical education, Career choice, Internal medicine

Background

Internal Medicine (IM) and its subspecialties constitute the largest proportion of medical providers in the United States; in 2023, 21% of all medical residents trained in an IM residency program [1]. Thus, the career choices made by IM residents have direct impacts on important facets of American healthcare including the availability and geographic distribution of subspecialists and primary care physicians [2]. Career decisions are contingent on a variety of factors, but one pivotal element is whether to pursue a career in general or subspecialty medicine; the drivers of this component of IM residents’ decision-making are critical to understand but remain understudied.

A growing body of literature has evaluated individual factors hypothesized to contribute career field of choice. For example, prior work has described associations between career field of choice and resident-specific characteristics such as sex, educational debt, exam scores, or country of medical school training [3–5]. Research using the nationally administered IM in-training examination has suggested that residency may be a formative time during which more than half of IM residents change their career plans [6]. The timing of career decision-making has been found to vary depending on subspecialty interest, with those selecting a career in general medicine coming to that decision later than those pursuing highly competitive subspecialty fellowships [7–9].

Leveraging a large, prospectively created dataset containing resident demographic characteristics, stated career interests prior to residency, and detailed accounting of intern year clinical exposure and mentorship for 338 categorical IM residents graduating from a single medical center between 2017 and 2023, we sought to extend the findings of this foundational literature in two ways. First, the randomly assigned nature of intern year core inpatient clinical rotations at our institution allowed us to directly test the hypothesis that more weeks of exposure to a clinical service would increase the likelihood of choosing that field as a career across multiple subspecialties. Second, we evaluated the relative importance of pre-residency resident characteristics versus residency exposures in the prediction of final career outcomes. With these analyses, we sought to shed light on important questions faced by IM residency program leaders such as “How will clinical exposure impact my residents’ career trajectories?” and “Which residents are likely to benefit most from fellowship-focused career development opportunities, such as additional research time or sub-specialty clinic time?”

Methods

Setting and participants

We analyzed prospectively collected data from a single, university-affiliated IM residency program in the northeastern U.S. The study population included all categorical residents who graduated from the program between 2017 and 2023. We excluded preliminary residents enrolled for only one year of IM residency, residents who completed only two years of residency on the American Board of Internal Medicine (ABIM) research (i.e., “short-track”) pathway, and residents who transferred programs. There are no other tracks which impact intern year scheduling at our program.

Exposures of interest

As part of routine program operations, all medical students who were interviewed for residency positions reported up to four career interests on an electronic survey. Interests were reported using free text, which we categorized into ten categories: cardiology/vascular medicine, gastroenterology, general medicine/primary care, hematology/oncology, hospital medicine, infectious disease/global health, nephrology, pulmonary/critical care, other fields, and unknown (due to non-response). Additional resident characteristics available at the start of intern year were self-reported gender, age at the start of intern year, United Stated Medical Licensing Examination (USMLE) Step 1 score, presence of a second non-M.D. graduate level degree, first choice subspecialty elective, and the calendar year in which intern year began.

For each resident, we extracted the number of assigned weeks on each core inpatient, primary clinical service (general medicine (wards), medical intensive care (ICU), gastroenterology/hepatology (GI), cardiology, and hematology/oncology), assigned peer mentoring group (“firm”), and assigned advisor specialty. Core rotations make up a total of 32 weeks; the remaining 20 weeks of the year are distributed between outpatient primary care clinic (9 weeks), elective (3 weeks), Jeopardy back-up coverage (3 weeks) and vacation (5 weeks). Program leaders make core rotation and firm assignments without consulting specialty interests or requested electives, creating a natural experiment with quasi-randomization.

Outcome of interest

Our primary outcome of interest was “career choice,” defined in this study as the field in which residents were practicing or training one year after residency, assessed from direct correspondence with former residents one year following completion of residency. For residents who accepted temporary positions following residency (e.g., a single year of hospital medicine), we assigned career choice as the fellowship that they subsequently entered. During the time of our study, all graduates were able to match into the specialty of their choice. Data on career choice was missing for 7 residents, who were excluded.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables and are reported as median (IQR) for continuous variables and frequencies (N%) for categorical variables. Initial exploratory data analysis using box plots and contingency tables was performed to evaluate univariable associations between career choice and exposures of interest. Normally distributed continuous variables were compared using one-way ANOVA, non-normal continuous variables were compared using Kruskal-Wallis tests and categorical variables using chi-square tests. To quantify the concordance between stated pre-residency interest, we calculated Cramer’s V and its 95% confidence interval using bootstrapping after the addition of 10− 16 to avoid empty cells.

To answer our primary research question regarding whether the number of randomly assigned weeks on a clinical service increased the likelihood of choosing that field as a career, we first confirmed pseudo-randomization by testing that there was no association between pre-residency interest and number of weeks assigned using Kruskal-Wallis tests. To mitigate small cells, we collapsed career choice into six categories: cardiology, hematology/oncology, gastroenterology, pulmonary/critical care, general internal medicine (including hospital medicine and primary care), and other (including allergy/immunology, endocrinology, geriatrics, global health, industry, infectious disease, nephrology, palliative care, physiatry and medical rehabilitation, rheumatology, and vascular medicine). We tested the association between number of weeks on each core clinical service and choosing the corresponding field as a career using univariable and multivariable log-link Poisson regression with robust standard errors to mitigate convergence issues associated with log-binomial regressions and estimate relative risks. We also tested for an interaction between number of weeks on service and pre-residency interest in that subspecialty. Multivariable analyses were adjusted for number of weeks on service for each subspecialty rotation, pre-residency interest in that career, requested subspecialty elective, firm, advisor specialty, age, gender, Step 1 score, and presence of a second degree.

To answer our second research question regarding the relative importance of pre-residency preferences and demographics compared with residency experiences in the prediction of career choice at the start of residency, we utilized random forest modeling to develop a predictive model for career choice based on pre-residency preferences and demographics alone. We randomly split our data into a training (75%) set and a test (25%) set using stratified sampling based upon final career outcomes. In the training dataset, we performed 5-fold cross-validation using grid search to tune the hyperparameters of random forests, including the number of variables randomly sampled for splitting, the minimum terminal node size, and the number of trees. Optimal hyperparameters were selected by maximizing accuracy. We then trained the model with the optimal hyperparameters on the training dataset. In the test data, we evaluated model accuracy, plotted receiver operator characteristics (ROC) curves for each outcome, and reported associated areas under the curve (AUCs). By repeating the same hyperparameter tuning procedure, we subsequently incorporated residency experience data to evaluate their impact on the model and ranked variable importance based upon the mean decrease in Gini index.

This study was determined not to be human subjects research by the institutional IRB. This study is reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline [10]. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software, version 4.2.2 (R Foundation). We utilized the randomForest R package to implement random forest algorithms.

Pre-Residency career interests, demographics and career choice

Of 289 residents who completed the voluntary pre-residency career interest survey, 163 (56.4%) reported 1 interest, 93 (32.2%) reported 2 interests, 33 (11.4%) reported 3 interests, and 3 (1.0%) reported 4 interests. There was moderate to strong concordance between first stated pre-residency career interest and career choice (P < 0.001, Cramer’s V 0.50 (95% CI: 0.41–0.51), Fig. 1). Of the 289 residents, 141 (48.8%) ultimately pursued a career that matched their first stated pre-residency interest, 57 (19.7%).

Observed Relationship between Pre-Residency Career Interest and Final Career Choice. Of those with a stated interest in cardiology at the start of residency, 72.5% ultimately pursued a career in cardiology; the corresponding percentages for gastroenterology, general medicine, hematology/oncology, and pulmonary critical care were 62.5%, 53.3%, 57.7%, and 50% respectively. Of those who stated a career interest outside of those five specialties, 45.3% went on to a career outside of those specialties. pursued a career they had listed as a secondary interest and 91 (31.5%) pursued a career not listed in their pre-residency interests.

The greatest concordance between pre-residency interest and career choice occurred in cardiology (72.5%), gastroenterology (62.5%), hematology/oncology (57.7%), and general internal medicine (53.3%). Concordance was 50% or less between early interest and career choice amongst those who expressed early interest in pulmonary and critical care or other fields (defined as endocrinology, ID, global health, rheumatology, allergy & immunology, nephrology, palliative care, addiction medicine, and geriatrics). Expressed differently, the relative risk of matching into cardiology amongst residents who stated cardiology as their primary career interest prior to residency was 6.94 (95% CI: 4.80–10.3); the comparable relative risks were 11.0 (95% CI: 6.54–19.7) for hematology/oncology and 7.76 for gastroenterology (95% CI: 4.95–12.4).

No statistically significant associations were found for the overall relationship between career choice and resident gender, age at intern year, presence of a second degree, Step 1 score, or calendar year residency began.

Intern year clinical exposure and career choice

We next examined whether randomly assigned number of weeks on core clinical services during intern year influenced entry into those fields. Kruskal-Wallis testing confirmed that, as expected, number of weeks assigned was not associated with pre-residency interests (Supplemental Table 1).

In univariable analysis, more assigned weeks on the inpatient hematology/oncology service was associated with a greater likelihood of pursuing a career in hematology/oncology (RR per additional week 1.18; 95% CI 1.00–1.39) (Table 2). This finding held true in the subgroup of residents without a previously stated interest in hematology/oncology (RR 1.48; 95% CI: 1.12–1.96) but was not significant in those who had a preceding interest in hematology/oncology (RR of 0.94; 95% CI 0.80–1.11). In the multivariable model, the association between weeks on hematology/oncology service and final career was no longer statistically significant. There was an association between weeks on service in the ICU and a career in pulmonary/critical care in the multivariable model (RR 1.40, 95% CI 1.23–1.56) but not the univariable model. For cardiology, wards, and GI services, we did not find any association between number of assigned weeks on service and final career field. In exploratory analysis, there was no significant association between weeks on any clinical service and the collapsed “other” career category. In univariable analysis, we did not find association between assigned advisor’s specialty or assigned firm and career choice.

Relative contribution of pre-residency and residency factors in career choice

To answer our second research question, we developed a random forest model to predict career choice using baseline resident characteristics (gender, age, second degree status, Step 1 score, stated pre-residency interest, requested intern elective, and calendar year residency began). This model had an overall accuracy of 45.2%. It performed best in those who pursued a career in cardiology, gastroenterology, and hematology/oncology (one-vs-rest class accuracy rate of 70.0%, 67.7%, and 59.9% respectively), less well for those who pursued careers in GIM or other fields (class accuracy rate of 27.0% and 40.0%, respectively), and particularly poorly in those who pursued pulmonary and critical care (class accuracy rate of 0.0%). On the test data, this model had an overall AUC of 82.4%, with career-specific AUC as high as 94.9% for hematology/oncology and as low as 63.1% for general internal medicine (Fig. 2). The most important predictors in the model were requested intern elective specialty and stated pre-residency interest, followed by Step 1 score.

We subsequently fit an extended random forest model with the resident baseline characteristics described in the first model and, additionally, residency-specific exposures (weeks on service, advisor specialty, and firm) to evaluate whether this would demonstrate improved performance. This model had similar overall accuracy (46.0%), and was similarly most effective at predicting cardiology, gastroenterology, and hematology oncology (one-vs-rest class accuracy rate of 66.0%, 69.4%, and 52.9%, respectively). On the test data, this model had an AUC of 82.9%. Again, amongst all factors, the most important predictors were requested intern elective and stated pre-residency interest (Fig. 3, Supplemental Fig. 1).

Discussion

Using six years of data prospectively collected at a large IM residency program, we evaluated the relationship between career field one year after residency and resident demographic characteristics, stated preferences, and intern year experiences. We found that, generally, residents who plan to specialize in cardiology, hematology/oncology, and gastroenterology have made that decision prior to residency, and their career outcome can be predicted with good accuracy using their initial stated career interest. In contrast, pre-residency career interests and demographics are less predictive of outcome in individuals considering careers in general internal medicine and pulmonary/critical care. We did not find a significant impact of intern year clinical experience or advisor specialty on choice of career field, with the possible exception of increased ICU exposure.

Our work complements existing literature. Similar to others, we found high concordance between pre-residency career interests and career choice in those pursuing careers in cardiology and gastroenterology [11] and additionally found this to be true for those interested in hematology/oncology and general medicine. Beyond describing this association, this study was novel in demonstrating that data available at the start of residency can be used to predict career choice. We found that, at our program, pre-residency interests carried greater weight in a predictive model than intern year clinical rotation duration; whether this is the case across institutions requires external validation. While our model demonstrated good accuracy in predicting career choice in those interested in cardiology, hematology/oncology, and gastroenterology, it was less successful at predicting careers in general medicine and pulmonary/critical care, suggesting that our measured factors may not fully capture determinants of interest in those fields. Given small sample sizes for the less commonly pursued specialties combined into an “Other” category in this study, we could not draw conclusions regarding those fields. Our data was drawn from routine program operations not specifically dedicated to understanding career decision making; future research in this area should consider factors outside traditional demographics and clinical exposure.

Considering the impact of specific clinical exposures, adjusting for a limited set of resident demographic characteristics and other intern year exposures, we found an association between ICU rotation length and a career in pulmonary/critical care. However, we found no adjusted association between duration of inpatient rotations in cardiology, hematology/oncology, gastroenterology and general medicine, and final career choice. Differences between our findings and those of others investigating this question [11, 12] may be attributable to differences in measured confounders, or may represent real differences in effect as clinical rotations across programs differ in terms of structure, patient population, and educational opportunities. Further, rotation duration may not be the most influential characteristic of a rotation. Importantly, less commonly pursued IM subspecialties such as endocrinology and nephrology do not have a dedicated inpatient rotation at our institution, complicating the measurement of exposure to these fields, and are only pursued by a handful of residents annually, limiting our power to draw conclusions related to these specialties.

This study has implications for those involved in IM residency curriculum design and raises questions about how IM programs can best support residents who train in the same program but ultimately differentiate into a broad assortment of career professionals. The pedagogical and professional value of residency experiences is not limited to their utility in preparing residents for fellowship. A well-rounded residency experience involving engagement with patients with diverse conditions is likely to generate subspecialists with a greater understanding and appreciation for conditions outside of their direct purview. However, there is limited empirical evidence to guide those creating curricula and schedules on the appropriate balance between such broad exposure and focused training for those who plan to apply for competitive fellowships such as research time, subspecialty clinic time, or sub-subspecialty consult electives. Our work suggests that additional inpatient clinical exposure during intern year is not likely to significantly alter career trajectories in those who begin residency with a plan to apply for competitive subspecialty fellowships, however, further work is needed to understand the impact of varied residency clinical exposure on these individuals and to understand what drove their career decisions prior to residency.

Strengths and limitations should be considered when evaluating the results of our analyses. Among its strengths, our study closely followed almost all residency graduates (98%) over multiple years with minimal missing data. Because core clinical rotation time in our program is randomly assigned, we could determine its effect free of substantial confounding. However, core rotations only comprise 32 weeks of intern year, and it is possible that exposures occurring during elective, Jeopardy back-up coverage, or primary care clinic time could influence our outcome. Additionally, this was a single-center study, which limits generalizability. For context, the studied IM program is one of the largest in the U.S., housed in a research-intensive institution in an urban environment, and 75% of graduates pursue fellowship training. Finally, all residents included in this study graduated from MD-granting medical schools (as opposed to DO-granting schools), 4% of which were located outside of the U.S. Thus, we did not assess the impact of medical school type or U.S. versus international medical school training on our outcomes.

Conclusions

Cumulatively, these results highlight the importance of residents’ pre-residency interests in determining their final career field of choice and identified that these stated interests hold greater weight in predictive models compared to demographic factors or intern year clinical exposure. Overall, we did not find consistent evidence that career field of choice is strongly influenced by clinical rotation duration; the relationship between clinical exposure during residency and career choice requires further study.
