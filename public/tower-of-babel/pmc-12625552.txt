Effects of foot rotation on ankle control and injury risk during badminton lunge landings
Authors: Jun Wen, Datao Xu, Xiangli Gao, Xiuye Qu, Zanni Zhang, Huiyu Zhou, Zsolt Radak, Zixiang Gao, Yaodong Gu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe badminton lunge is a decisive yet injury-prone action that places substantial loads on the ankle, and toe-in, neutral, or toe-out orientations may alter joint mechanics and influence injury risk; however, evidence in sport-specific lunge contexts is scarce. This study quantified the biomechanical effects of different toe-orientations in badminton lunge landings using integrated motion capture, force measurement, electromyography, and finite element modeling. By uniquely combining EMG and FEA, the study provides evidence-based guidance for performance optimization and injury prevention.MethodsThirty male amateur badminton players performed right-lunge landings under three toe-orientations. Kinematic and kinetic data were collected using motion capture and force platforms. Muscle activation was assessed through surface electromyography and musculoskeletal modeling. A three-dimensional finite element model of the foot simulated Von Mises stress distribution. Statistical analyses included repeated measures ANOVA and statistical parametric mapping.ResultsToe-out landings significantly reduced ankle eversion angles, eversion moments, anterior-posterior shear forces, and joint impulse (p < 0.001, p = 0.025, p = 0.047, p = 0.038). They also promoted smoother power output and greater anteroposterior center-of-mass displacement. Stress shifted from the rearfoot to the forefoot, accompanied by increased activation of the peroneal muscles and triceps surae. In contrast, toe-in landings produced higher eversion loading, stress concentration in the rearfoot, greater tibialis anterior activation, and restricted center-of-mass displacement.ConclusionToe-out positioning lowered ankle load and rearfoot stress, improved stabilizing muscle activation and shock absorption, and facilitated center-of-mass control. Toe-in positioning elevated stress concentration and injury risk. Moderate toe-out angles were recommended to optimize footwork efficiency and protect joint health.

Introduction

Badminton is a sport that integrates both agility and competitiveness, requiring frequent rapid stops, starts, directional changes and jumps, all of which place considerable stress on the lower limbs [1]. Among its footwork patterns, the lunge is one of the most common, accounting for over 15% of total movements and playing a key role in front-court play such as net shots, saves and tempo control [2]. The lunge involves a forward step into single-leg support while the trail leg simultaneously pushes off to initiate the next action. Previous studies have shown that abrupt stops generate eccentric forces that result in ground reaction loads several times body weight [3], especially on the dominant leg, which increases ankle injury risk. Although badminton is a non-contact sport, injury incidence remains high at approximately seven injuries per 1,000 h of play, with landing identified as a key risk phase [4]. In competitive settings, players often lunge under unexpected shuttle trajectories or when fatigued, both of which compromise lower limb control [5]. The ankle, as a crucial shock absorber and stabilizer, is particularly vulnerable to inversion or eversion sprains that can lead to chronic instability [6]. Critically, toe-orientation at landing—whether toe-in, neutral, or toe-out—affects impact absorption strategies [7, 8], ankle mechanics, and ligament loading patterns [9]. These biomechanical changes influence joint stability, balance control, and power generation, making toe orientation a key factor in movement efficiency and injury risk [10].

Foot-orientation angle at landing plays a pivotal role in shaping joint kinematics and load transmission throughout the lower extremity [11]. Changes in toe angle can alter ankle and knee kinematics, affecting impact force absorption and potentially increasing injury risk [12, 13]. Previous studies have demonstrated that toe-in landings are associated with increased ankle dorsiflexion and eversion angles [14], which may compromise joint alignment and elevate injury risk due to excessive ligament loading and instability in the frontal plane. Conversely, toe-out postures have been linked to reduced eversion angles and greater plantarflexor engagement [15, 16], potentially promoting more stable landing mechanics. Notably, toe-orientation can also influence knee mechanics, suggesting potential ankle–knee interactions. In particular, toe-orientation can affect knee valgus angles, as excessive internal rotation of the foot has been shown to increase medial knee displacement, a known risk factor for ACL injuries [8, 11]. During sport-specific movements such as the badminton lunge, where rapid deceleration occurs on a single supporting limb, such variations in angular joint behavior can have amplified consequences [17]. Therefore, understanding how toe orientation modifies biomechanical mechanisms at landing is crucial for improving movement efficiency and preventing injury in high-impact, multidirectional sports [18].

We observed that most existing studies investigating the effects of toe-orientation on human movement have primarily focused on low-intensity daily activities such as walking [12, 15], with particular attention given to proximal joints like the knee [8]. Although interest in the biomechanics of the badminton lunge has increased in recent years [2, 19, 20], there is still a notable lack of research specifically examining how toe-orientation during the landing phase of the lunge influences lower limb injury mechanisms. Given the rapid changes in gameplay and the dynamic, unpredictable nature of landing scenarios, athletes often exhibit individual variability in toe angle upon contact [5]. Therefore, understanding how these variations influence lower limb biomechanics, particularly ankle joint control and injury risk, is essential for enhancing movement efficiency and informing preventive strategies. Comparing the biomechanical impacts of toe-in, toe-out, and neutral foot positions during badminton lunge landings is the goal of this study, focusing specifically on ankle joint response and potential injury mechanisms (Fig. 1b and d). We hypothesized that ankle eversion injury risk would decrease progressively from toe-in to toe-out positions, and that mechanical load on the ankle would be greatest under the toe-in condition.

Foot-orientation at landing plays a critical role in modulating joint loading and neuromuscular control. In badminton, where landing conditions vary unpredictably, understanding how toe-in, neutral, and toe-out positions affect lower limb biomechanics is essential for reducing injury risk. To test this hypothesis, we employed a novel multimodal approach combining kinematic, kinetic, and electromyographic (EMG) analysis with finite element modeling (FEA) to investigate the ankle’s biomechanical response during different landing postures. While previous studies have applied EMG or FEA separately in badminton biomechanics, integrating these modalities allows for simultaneous analysis of muscle activation patterns and stress distribution, providing a more comprehensive insight into injury mechanisms than past approaches. Kinematic and kinetic parameters were essential for understanding joint control, with muscle activation supporting the stabilization of the joints [21]. The gastrocnemius and soleus aided in impact absorption, while the peroneus muscles controlled eversion/inversion, and the tibialis anterior decelerated motion [16, 22]. Joint moment impulse, the cumulative rotational force applied at a joint over time, reflects the joint load experienced during dynamic movements, while center-of-mass trajectory indicates overall body balance [23]. FEA allowed detailed assessment of bone stress. Von Mises stress, representing combined stress intensity, was used to identify potential overload and injury risk in the foot [24]. This study used a 3D FEA model to analyze the distribution and propagation trends of Von Mises stress during lunge landings, aiming to inform technique optimization and injury prevention strategies in badminton.

Materials and methods

Participants

Using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7; Heinrich Heine University of Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany), the necessary sample size for this investigation was established [25]. A one-way repeated measures ANOVA (effect size f = 0.25, α = 0.05, power = 0.80) indicated that a minimum of 28 participants was required. This study focused exclusively on male amateur badminton players to minimize variability from sex and training level, though this limits the generalizability of the findings to female or professional athletes. Therefore, to guarantee accurate data collection, 30 male amateur badminton players from Ningbo University were enlisted for the experiment. Their ages were 21.7 ± 1.2 years, their heights were 175.67 ± 2.53 cm, and their weights were 65.72 ± 4.08 kg. To maintain consistency, the inclusion criteria listed below were used: (1) every participant was a male university student in good health who played badminton on a regular basis; (2) they played badminton three to four times a week for at least two hours each time; (3) no ailments or injuries to the lower limbs that potentially impair performance were reported within the previous six months; (4) all subjects were dominant with their right leg and right hand [26]. Every participant gave written informed permission after being fully informed of the study’s goals, methods, and requirements prior to data collection. The institutional ethics committee gave its approval for the project (approval number: TY 2025051).

Experimental protocol and procedures

Experimental setup

This investigation was carried out at Ningbo University’s Sports Biomechanics Laboratory. Before testing, the participants’ height and weight were measured and recorded as baseline anthropometric data. The right leg’s length, which was measured from the lateral malleolus to the anterior superior iliac spine, was also measured in order to establish the starting and landing positions [17], enabling a realistic simulation of a maximal right-lunge motion typically seen on a badminton court. As shown in Figs. 1A and 38 spherical reflective markers (diameter: 12.5 mm) were affixed to significant anatomical landmarks in accordance with prior methods to enable precise motion tracking [27]. Motion capture data were collected using a Vicon system (Oxford Metrics Ltd., Oxford, UK) equipped with eight high-speed cameras, each operating at a sampling rate of 200 Hz [28]. During right-lunge landings, ground reaction forces were measured at a 1000 Hz sample rate utilizing a force platform (AMTI, Watertown, MA, USA) [29]. To guarantee data integrity, the force and motion capture data were completely synced. Participants wore tight-fitting sportswear throughout the testing session. All tests were conducted under standard laboratory environmental conditions (temperature approximately 25 °C, relative humidity approximately 60%), and all players wore the same model of Yonex badminton shoes to ensure consistency. Surface electromyography (EMG) sensors were attached according to the SENIAM (Surface Electro Myo Graphy for the Non-Invasive Assessment of Muscles) guidelines [30]. To track muscle activity, six EMG sensors (Delsys Inc., Boston, MA, USA) were positioned over the muscle bellies of the tibialis anterior (TA), medial gastrocnemius (MG), lateral gastrocnemius (LG), soleus (SOL), peroneus longus (FL), and peroneus brevis (FB) [16]. As seen in Fig. 1A, every sensor was meticulously positioned in relation to its corresponding anatomical landmark.

Experimental procedure

Before formal data collection, participants performed a standardized 15-minute warm-up session consisting of three components: dynamic stretching, sport-specific activation, and cardiovascular stimulation [31]. This included stretching exercises targeting the neck, shoulders, and hip joints, along with high-knee runs, lateral shuffles, and simulated racket swings. Short sprints and multidirectional movements were also included to fully activate the musculoskeletal system. To ensure data reliability, participants also performed familiarization trials of the maximal right-lunge motion following the warm-up. The experimental protocol required participants to execute a standardized right-lunge. They lunged from point A, landed at point B, and returned to point A with a right-leg push-off. To replicate real-game conditions and control for technical variables, three distinct toe-orientation angles were tested during landing: neutral (0°), toe-in (20° internal rotation), and toe-out (20° external rotation) [8]. These angles were clearly marked on the force plate using adhesive tape to ensure accurate execution.

A randomized testing design was implemented. Prior to each trial, a research assistant used a random number generator to determine the landing angle [32], minimizing potential order effects. Each participant completed seven valid trials under each of the three toe orientation conditions, with a two-minute rest between trials to reduce fatigue. It was emphasized that participants must ensure their right landing foot made full contact with the force platform during each trial; otherwise, the trial was considered invalid and excluded from analysis [33].

Data processing and analysis

Kinematic, Kinetic, and musculoskeletal modeling

The principal aim of this research was to examine the biomechanical properties of the lower limbs under various toe orientations during the landing phase of the badminton lunge. Data processing was conducted under blinded conditions to ensure that analysts were unaware of the assigned toe-orientation. The first point of contact was determined to be the point at which the vertical ground reaction force (VGRF) surpassed 10 N [34]. The data collection window was defined from that moment until the VGRF dropped below 10 N. Vicon Nexus software (v1.8.5, Vicon Metrics Ltd., Oxford, UK) was used to extract kinematic and kinetic data in C3D format. Custom scripts were then used to transcode the C3D files into TRC and MOT formats in MATLAB (R2024a, The MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) [35]. Dynamic residual analysis was used to improve the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), and the filtering frequencies were chosen in accordance with Winter’s previous study standards. VGRF data was cut off at 20 Hz, whereas kinematic data was cut off at 10 Hz using a fourth-order zero-lag Butterworth low-pass filter. The processed datasets were loaded into OpenSim (Sim TK, Stanford, USA) for sophisticated parameter computation and biomechanical analysis [36]. A subject-specific static model was created by scaling a generic musculoskeletal model to the anthropometric measurements of each participant. Anatomical correctness was ensured by adjusting the muscle origin and insertion locations according to limb length. The inverse kinematics tool in OpenSim was used to calculate joint kinematics, and motion files (MOT) were created to record the lunge landing phase. By aligning the model with observed motion and optimizing center of mass (COM) representation by decreasing residual forces in the frontal and transverse planes, a Residual Reduction Algorithm (RRA) was used to increase the accuracy of kinematic data. In order to minimize the difference between the model and experimental marker data, joint moments were computed for each degree of freedom using inverse dynamics and a weighted least squares optimization technique. Joint moments were then multiplied by angular velocities at each time interval to determine joint power. Static optimization was used to estimate muscle activation patterns [37].

EMG processing and joint reaction force estimation

A fourth-order Butterworth band-pass filter (10–400 Hz) was applied to the surface electromyography (EMG) signals, and then full-wave rectification and low-pass filtering were performed at a cut-off frequency of 6 Hz afterwards [38]. The EMG data was collected at 1000 Hz to guarantee precise capture within the relevant frequency range. Muscle activation levels were compared by first normalizing EMG signal amplitudes to the maximal root mean square (RMS) value and then to maximum voluntary contraction (MVC). The accuracy and reliability of the musculoskeletal model were evaluated by comparing simulated muscle activation outputs with the recorded EMG signals [39]. As shown in Fig. 1E, the observed EMG data and the model-predicted muscle activation levels did not differ statistically significantly.

Statistical analysis

Before statistical analysis, the Shapiro-Wilk test was used to determine whether the data were normal [40]. The impact of varying toe orientations during lunge landings on the ankle and knee joints was investigated using a one-way repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) for data that satisfied the assumptions of normal distribution. Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM) was used to examine the time-series kinematic and kinetic data collected during the landing phase. All data were interpolated into 101 time points, which correspond to 0% to 100% of the landing phase, using a specially designed MATLAB algorithm [41]. The preprocessed data were run through the open-source SPM1d script, and the multiple comparisons problem that arises in time-series studies was addressed using corrections based on Random Field Theory (RFT). For ANOVA, Greenhouse-Geisser correction and Bonferroni adjustment were applied where appropriate, and effect sizes (partial η²) were interpreted as small (0.01), medium (0.06), or large (0.14) [42]. SPSS 27.0 for Windows was used for all statistical calculations, and p < 0.05 was chosen as the significance level. Origin 2024 software was used to plot and visualize the data.

Finite element analysis of ankle integration model

Acquisition and reconstruction of geometric data

The participant’s right foot underwent CT and MRI scans (tube voltage: 120 kV; current: 125 mA; slice thickness: 2 mm; no interslice gap) [43]. The 2D medical images were segmented using Mimics 21.0 (Materialise, Leuven, Belgium) [44]. Geometric refinement of bones, plantar fascia, and soft tissues was conducted in Geomagic Studio 2021 (Geomagic, Inc., Research Triangle Park, NC, USA). The anatomical components were assembled and solidified using SolidWorks 2021 (SolidWorks Corp., Waltham, MA, USA). Cartilage was simulated by filling the space between adjacent bones with a solid elastic material. The final foot model comprised 29 bones, 1 plantar fascia, 66 ligaments, 33 articular cartilage structures, and an encapsulating soft tissue layer. Three foot models represented toe-in, neutral, and toe-out landing orientations.

Material Properties, Boundary, and loading conditions

In order to simulate the interaction between the surfaces of bone and cartilage, direct surface-to-surface contact was defined and finite element analysis was performed using ANSYS Workbench 2024. A frictionless connection was applied at the interfaces between bones and cartilage. The surrounding soft tissue served to constrain and stabilize both bone and cartilage structures [44]. A coefficient of friction of 0.6 was used to describe the contact interaction between the ground and the plantar surface, as illustrated in Fig. 1D. Biomechanical loading conditions applied to the model are also summarized in Fig. 1D. The ground plate was fixed to serve as the reference base, and the foot model was positioned based on the desired landing configuration. To ensure uniform alignment, the global coordinate system was aligned with the original coordinate framework used in OpenSim, and the angle between the tibial axis and the foot’s longitudinal axis in the sagittal plane was adjusted to define the ankle angle. Mesh convergence was achieved by iteratively refining the global element size by approximately 10% until the variation of key output parameters was below 5%, ensuring stable and reproducible finite element results. Lastly, boundary constraints were defined within the model using the displacements of the three main foot parts. All materials were assumed to be linearly elastic, homogeneous, and isotropic [45]. The mechanical properties of each component were defined by two parameters: Young’s modulus (E) and Poisson’s ratio (ν), as detailed in Table 1 [46].

Model validation

Ankle fluoroscopic pictures were taken during landing using a high-speed dual fluoroscopic imaging system (DFIS). To verify the precision of the computational model, the ankle joint displacement findings from DFIS and finite element analysis (FEA) were compared [8]. Simultaneously, using the VICON motion analysis system and force platform, motion capture and ground reaction force data were gathered to act as loading and boundary conditions for the FEA simulation. The DFIS acquired ankle joint images from two orthogonal views. Following geometric calibration and image alignment, the relative displacement between the talus and tibia was calculated using the coordinate calculator plugin in Rhino software (version 6.16, McNeel and Associates, USA), as shown in Fig. 1E. The outcomes confirmed the validity and reliability of the built finite element model by showing a high degree of agreement between the ankle joint displacements observed by DFIS and those predicted by FEA in three-dimensional space.

Results

Joint kinematics and kinetics

Figure 2 illustrates the differences in kinematic and kinetic characteristics during the lunge landing phase under three toe orientation conditions in badminton. As participants transitioned from toe-in to toe-out orientation upon landing, the ankle dorsiflexion angle showed a significant decreasing trend, with statistically significant differences among the three conditions. Effect size was large (η² = 0.42), indicating a substantial increase in anterior ankle load with toe-in landings. This suggests that toe-in landings may increase anterior ankle load, potentially raising injury risk. In the frontal plane, the ankle eversion angle also differed significantly throughout the landing phase. Specifically, the eversion angle was greatest under the toe-in condition, followed by the neutral position, and was lowest in the toe-out condition. Effect size was large (η² = 0.39), indicating a meaningful impact on lateral ankle stability. Greater eversion may challenge lateral ankle stability. For the knee joint, there were no significant differences in sagittal plane angles across the three toe orientations during landing. However, in the frontal plane, the knee abduction angle was greatest in the toe-out condition and smallest in the toe-in condition, with statistically significant differences observed during the early (0% – 4.3%) and late (79.1% – 96.5%) phases of the landing movement. Effect size was moderate (η² = 0.17), suggesting a meaningful influence of toe orientation. Figure 2 also summarizes the differences in peak ankle and knee joint angles across the three toe orientation conditions. Significant differences were observed in the peak dorsiflexion, eversion, and inversion angles of the ankle, as well as in the peak flexion, abduction, and adduction angles of the knee.

In Fig. 2, the kinetic differences among the three foot orientation conditions during the landing phase of the badminton lunge are presented. As the foot orientation shifted from toe-in to toe-out, a decreasing trend was observed in ankle plantarflexion moments. Significant differences were found between conditions during the early landing phase (3.2% – 20.3%) and the pre-takeoff phase (84% – 91%). Effect sizes were moderate to large (η² = 0.18–0.35), with toe-out producing lower plantarflexion moments than toe-in and neutral orientations. In the frontal plane, ankle moments also exhibited significant differences during the initial phase of landing (0% – 10%). Effect size was moderate (η² = 0.22), with toe-in showing the highest eversion moment, indicating greater demand on lateral ankle stabilizers.

Regarding ankle joint power in the sagittal plane, significant differences were observed between the three foot orientations during 7.8%, – 9.4%, 11.9%, – 19.1%, and 20.8% – 24.5% of the landing phase (η² = 0.17–0.31). During these periods, dorsiflexion power was higher in the toe-out condition early and higher in the toe-in condition later, suggesting that toe-in landings may increase ankle load and potential injury risk. In the frontal plane, ankle joint power also showed significant differences across multiple phases (0% – 5.5%, 11.1% – 19%, 26% – 33.5%, 68.5% – 85.4%). Effect sizes were moderate to large (η² = 0.19–0.34), with toe-in producing the highest inversion power early and highest eversion power later, indicating greater demands on ankle stabilizing muscles and potential lateral or medial ankle stress.

Table 2 summarizes the differences in peak ankle joint moments and powers among the three foot orientation conditions during the lunge landing phase. At the ankle, toe-out increased peak dorsiflexion moment from 0.03 ± 0.19 to 0.14 ± 0.17 Nm/kg (η² = 0.320) while slightly reducing plantarflexion from − 1.27 ± 0.27 to −1.20 ± 0.24 Nm/kg and eversion moment from 0.52 ± 0.27 to 0.45 ± 0.30 Nm/kg (approximately 13% reduction). Inversion moment decreased substantially from 0.04 ± 0.53 to −0.08 ± 0.06 Nm/kg (η² = 0.541). Corresponding ankle powers declined, notably eversion from 0.92 ± 0.36 to 0.54 ± 0.45 W/kg (approximately 41% reduction) and inversion from − 0.98 ± 0.67 to −0.46 ± 0.39 W/kg. At the knee, toe-out increased flexion moment from − 0.35 ± 0.14 to −0.47 ± 0.27 Nm/kg but reduced abduction and adduction moments from 0.54 ± 0.24 to 0.38 ± 0.18 and from − 0.54 ± 0.21 to −0.82 ± 0.31 Nm/kg (η² = 0.251–0.531), with consistent decreases in knee flexion and adduction powers. Overall, toe-out orientation reduced ankle frontal-plane loading by up to 41% and altered knee moments, indicating meaningful biomechanical adaptations during landing.

Muscle activation

Figure 3 illustrates the changes in lower limb muscle activation during the landing phase of the badminton lunge under three different foot orientation conditions (toe-in, neutral, and toe-out). Overall, muscle activation levels varied with foot orientation throughout the landing process. For the medial and lateral gastrocnemius muscles, the highest activation was observed under the toe-out condition, whereas the lowest occurred in the neutral position. Significant differences were found during 66.7% – 80.5% of the landing phase for the medial gastrocnemius (η² = 0.23) and 61% – 78.5% for the lateral gastrocnemius (η² = 0.28), indicating moderate to large effects. Higher gastrocnemius activation in toe-out may improve ankle push-off and shock absorption. Regarding the soleus, significant differences were observed during 53.5% – 79.6% and 89.8% – 93.5% of the landing phase. Effect sizes were moderate to large (η² = 0.21 and 0.18, respectively), with activation highest under toe-out in the first phase and highest under toe-in in the second phase, suggesting greater calf support in toe-out landings and compensatory control in toe-in. The peroneus longus exhibited significant differences during 7% – 27.2%, 58.9% – 76.5%, and 94% – 100% of the movement cycle. Effect sizes were moderate (η² = 0.19–0.25), with the highest activation under toe-out and the lowest under toe-in. Similarly, the peroneus brevis showed significant activation differences during 9.8% – 25.4%, 57.9% – 76.5%, and 96% – 100%. Effect sizes were moderate (η² = 0.18–0.24), following a similar trend as the peroneus longus—greater activation in the toe-out condition and lower activation in toe-in. For the tibialis anterior, significant differences were observed at 0% – 11.9% and 51.5% – 53.6% of the landing phase. Effect sizes were moderate (η² = 0.17 and 0.16), with the highest activation under the toe-in condition. Interestingly, the activation level was lowest in the toe-out condition at the beginning of the movement, whereas in the mid-to-late phase, the lowest activation occurred under the neutral foot position.

Joint Impulse, joint reaction Force, shear Force, and center of mass displacement

Figure 4 presents the results of joint impulse, joint reaction forces, shear forces, and center of mass (COM) displacement under three different foot orientation conditions during the landing phase of the badminton lunge. For ankle joint moment impulse in the sagittal plane, the neutral foot position yielded the highest impulse, whereas the toe-out condition produced the lowest. Significant differences were observed between neutral and toe-out (η² = 0.17), suggesting greater load on the ankle in neutral landings. Regarding vertical ankle joint reaction forces, significant differences were observed during 20.2% – 74.5% of the landing phase. The neutral foot orientation resulted in the lowest vertical reaction force, while the toe-out condition showed the highest. Effect size was moderate to large (η² = 0.21), with toe-out producing the highest vertical force, indicating increased work for impact absorption. For anteroposterior shear forces at the ankle joint, a significant difference was detected in the early phase (0% – 3.5%). Effect size was moderate (η² = 0.16), with the toe-out condition generating the greatest shear force and the toe-in condition the least. More shear in toe-out could challenge ankle stability early in landing. In the frontal plane, mediolateral shear forces showed significant differences at the onset of landing (0% – 3%). The toe-in condition resulted in the highest mediolateral shear force. Effect size was moderate (η² = 0.18), higher side-to-side shear in toe-in may raise the risk of ankle sprains. As for COM displacement in the anteroposterior direction, significant differences were identified during 16.8–88.7% of the movement phase (p = 0.020), with the toe-out condition exhibiting the largest displacement and the neutral condition the smallest. However, no significant differences were found in COM displacement in the frontal plane among the three foot orientation conditions.

The distribution of the Von-Mise stress

Figure 5 illustrates the distribution of von Mises stress in the calcaneus, talus, navicular, and the first to third metatarsals during the landing phase of the badminton lunge under three different foot orientation conditions. Stress in the calcaneus was relatively high under the toe-in and neutral conditions, showing a pronounced concentration in the medial rearfoot region, whereas the overall stress level was lower under the toe-out condition. This corresponds with higher ankle eversion angles and moments in toe-in landings, linking medial rearfoot stress to frontal-plane ankle loading. For the talus, the highest stress was observed under the neutral condition, followed by toe-out, with the lowest under toe-in. Localized stress peaks were consistently present in the anterior region across all conditions. The navicular, a critical structure on the medial side of the midfoot, exhibited the greatest stress in the neutral position, followed by toe-in, with the lowest stress occurring in the toe-out condition. The first metatarsal showed the highest stress level under the toe-in condition, which significantly decreased under toe-out. Toe-in landings may increase forefoot load, especially on the first metatarsal. For the second metatarsal, stress gradually increased as foot orientation transitioned from toe-in to toe-out. The third metatarsal followed a similar trend, with von Mises stress rising progressively from toe-in to toe-out. Overall, as foot orientation changed from toe-in to toe-out, von Mises stress in the calcaneus, talus, navicular, and first metatarsal tended to decrease, whereas stress in the second and third metatarsals progressively increased. These FEA results integrate with kinematic and kinetic findings, illustrating how foot orientation redistributes load and influences injury risk patterns.

Discussion

Joint kinematics and kinetics

The primary objective of this study was to investigate the effects of different foot orientation angles (toe-in, neutral, and toe-out) on lower limb biomechanical characteristics during the landing phase of the badminton lunge, with a particular focus on ankle joint motor control and injury risk under varying postural conditions. It was hypothesized that as the foot orientation shifted from toe-in to toe-out, the risk of ankle eversion sprain would decrease, and the mechanical load on the ankle would be highest under the toe-in condition. Our findings support this hypothesis and further reveal that toe-in orientation not only elevates the risk of ankle injury but may also reduce force generation efficiency, as indicated by observed power fluctuations. In contrast, toe-out orientation effectively reduces ankle eversion angles and loading levels, while also optimizing muscle activation patterns and redistributing stress across foot bones more favorably.

Previous studies have suggested that a moderate toe-out angle during landing may promote increased ankle plantarflexion and decreased inversion, thereby offering protective effects against ankle sprains [7, 14, 49]. Building on this foundation, our study found that toe-in orientation produced the highest dorsiflexion and eversion angles, deviating from physiological norms and disrupting proper landing mechanics. This was accompanied by greater compensatory eversion moments, consistent with prior reports [10, 50], and may consequently elevate the risk of ankle injury. The literature has consistently indicated that excessive inversion during lateral movements or insufficient plantarflexion during landings can increase such risks [10], further supporting our findings. Furthermore, our data showed that as foot orientation transitioned from toe-in to toe-out, both plantarflexion and eversion joint moments progressively decreased. This suggests that toe-out landings require less active compensatory control. Power analysis revealed that toe-in conditions caused large frontal-plane power fluctuations (initial eversion followed by mid-phase inversion), reflecting instability and elevated joint stress. In contrast, toe-out landings maintained a steadier power profile that facilitated smoother energy absorption and improved dynamic stability [51].

Muscle activation

Muscle activation results showed that changes in foot orientation significantly [52]. Among the [53], the medial and lateral gastrocnemius displayed significantly higher activation during the late landing phase (66% – 80%) under the toe-out condition, with the neutral position showing the lowest values. Prior studies have shown that insufficient activation of these muscles impairs lower limb control and elevates ankle sprain risk [8, 53]. Therefore, we suggest that during backward shifting of the center of mass for push-off, the toe-out posture may facilitate more effective activation of the calf extensors [54], potentially supporting dorsiflexion and eversion control and contributing to postural stability [55]. Additionally, earlier badminton lunge research indicated that higher soleus activation enhances elastic energy storage and performance [56]. In this study, soleus activation peaked under toe-out and was lowest under toe-in, supporting its role in impact resistance. However, at the movement’s end phase (89.8% – 93.5%), toe-in showed greater soleus activity, possibly as a compensation for postural instability. Previous work noted that peroneus longus activation resists inversion torque [57], and toe-out can promote its pre-activation to reduce injury risk. Consistent with this, our findings showed peroneus longus and brevis had highest activation under toe-out across multiple time windows, contributing to lateral stability and sprain prevention [58]. Although toe-out facilitates synergistic activation around the ankle, we observed significantly higher tibialis anterior activity under toe-in during early (0% – 11.9%) and mid (51.5% – 53.6%) landing, likely as a compensatory response to increased instability or inversion tendency [59]. This compensatory activation may reduce control efficiency and increase fatigue risk [60]. Overall, toe-out promotes proactive stabilizing muscle engagement and more efficient neuromuscular strategies, while toe-in relies more on reactive compensations, potentially elevating injury risk.

Joint Impulse, joint reaction Force, shear Force, and center of mass displacement

This study examined ankle loading under different foot orientations through joint moment impulse, vertical and overall joint reaction forces, shear forces, and COM displacement, to assess their effects on impact absorption and stability. In the sagittal plane, toe-out showed the smallest joint moment impulse, while neutral showed the largest. Previous studies on knee injury have suggested that reduced joint impulse is linked to lower joint loading and injury risk [61]. Thus, the smaller impulse in toe-out may reflect improved ankle energy absorption and reduced cumulative stress. Regarding vertical joint reaction force, toe-out produced the highest, and neutral the lowest. While greater force may imply heavier loading, it may also suggest enhanced support during deceleration, aligning with prior reports that forehand lunges with higher joint force are associated with faster stops [20, 36, 62]. Shear forces offered further insight: in the sagittal plane, early-phase shear was highest in toe-out and lowest in toe-in, indicating possibly stronger push-off. Similarly, frontal plane mediolateral shear was highest under toe-in, suggesting increased lateral instability and sprain risk, which aligns with findings in sport-specific lunge biomechanics [63, 64]. Previous studies also linked COM displacement to task performance and control strategy [65]. Consistent with this, toe-out showed greater COM displacement, which may indicate a more proactive forward-shift strategy, potentially supporting stability and motion continuity. In contrast, toe-in may restrict COM mobility, leading to compensatory muscle activation and instability [66].

Surprisingly, the results revealed no significant differences in frontal-plane COM displacement, contradicting our initial hypothesis. Although toe-in landings demonstrated substantially increased frontal-plane ankle shear forces and reaction forces - factors typically associated with elevated sprain risk - the corresponding COM displacement in this plane failed to show proportional increases. This unexpected finding suggests the presence of compensatory control mechanisms at the knee, hip [67], and trunk levels that effectively maintain COM stability despite ankle instability. Notably, while sagittal plane knee motion remained consistent across conditions, the observed increase in frontal-plane knee adduction during toe-in landings provides direct evidence for this proximal compensation strategy. However, such compensatory adjustments may paradoxically increase stress concentration at the ankle joint, potentially exacerbating injury risk. Comparative analysis clearly shows that toe-out landings provide superior shock absorption and more efficient COM regulation [68], reflecting more stable and adaptable biomechanics. In contrast, toe-in landings display less optimal load distribution characteristics and delayed COM control responses [69], biomechanical patterns that may increase susceptibility to lower extremity injuries. These findings have important implications for understanding injury mechanisms and developing preventive strategies in sports involving frequent landing movements.

The distribution of the Von-Mise stress

We also conducted finite element analysis of the foot, revealing that as the foot orientation gradually shifted from toe-in to toe-out, von Mises stress in the hindfoot and midfoot structures — including the calcaneus, talus, and navicular — consistently decreased, while stress in the second and third metatarsals progressively increased. This pattern reflects a redistribution of plantar load from medial to lateral and from posterior to anterior regions. Notably, under the toe-in and neutral conditions, significant medial stress concentrations were observed in the calcaneus and navicular, indicating greater loading on the stabilizing structures of the hindfoot and a potential risk for stress-related injuries [70]. Integrating these FEA results with the joint kinetics and muscle activation findings, we observed that toe-out landings, which exhibited lower ankle joint impulses, reduced frontal-plane shear forces, and more efficient activation of the gastrocnemius, soleus, and peroneal muscles, also corresponded to more favorable stress redistribution at the skeletal level. This synergy between neuromuscular control and structural load transfer likely contributes to improved impact absorption and overall landing stability. Conversely, toe-in landings, associated with higher eversion moments, greater tibialis anterior compensatory activation, and elevated shear forces, coincided with medial hindfoot stress concentrations, highlighting a mechanistic link between altered muscle coordination, joint loading, and focal bone stress, which may increase the risk of ankle sprain and chronic injuries.

Limitations and future directions

However, this study has several limitations. First, only healthy male amateur badminton players were included, which restricts the generalizability of the findings to other genders, competitive levels, or populations with previous injuries. Second, the experimental task involved a simulated lunge, which, while representative of actual play, does not fully capture the fatigue accumulation, unpredictable shuttle trajectories, and decision-making demands of real competition. Third, the analysis primarily focused on the ankle joint, with limited consideration of the kinetic and kinematic interactions involving proximal joints such as the knee and hip. Future research should therefore examine more diverse participant groups, including female athletes, elite players, and individuals undergoing rehabilitation after ankle injuries. Moreover, incorporating competitive scenarios with multi-directional steps, varying shuttle placements, and fatigue-inducing conditions would provide a more ecologically valid understanding of lower limb biomechanics. In addition, exploring hip-knee-ankle coordination and its role in mitigating injury risk could further strengthen preventive and performance-oriented strategies.

Conclusion

This study highlights the critical role of foot orientation in lower-limb biomechanics during badminton lunge landings. Toe-out positioning reduced ankle and rearfoot load, enhanced stabilizing muscle activation and shock absorption, and improved center-of-mass control, promoting overall ankle stability. In contrast, toe-in positioning increased stress concentration, compensatory demands, and potential injury risk. These findings have broader implications for training and injury prevention. Athletes and coaches may benefit from emphasizing foot orientation awareness in drills, incorporating toe-out landing practices, and considering fatigue management to maintain safe movement patterns. It should also be noted that the study was conducted on male amateur players under controlled laboratory conditions without fatigue protocols, which may limit direct application to other populations or competitive scenarios. Future research is encouraged to explore these effects across different skill levels, genders, and dynamic gameplay conditions.
