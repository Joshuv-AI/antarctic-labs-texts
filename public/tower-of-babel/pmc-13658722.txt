Dengue Vodcast: Insights from Recent Infectious Disease Congresses
Authors: Marco Aurélio P. Sáfadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dengue is the most common vector-borne viral disease. Dengue virus belongs to the Flaviviridae family, and it is predominantly transmitted by Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus mosquitoes. Dengue is a global threat to public health, with hundreds of millions of infections each year. Not only is the incidence of dengue increasing, but the geographic areas at risk are also expanding, with outbreaks in areas that have never reported dengue cases in the past, such as North America and Europe. In this vodcast, Professor Marco Aurélio Sáfadi, a pediatric infectious diseases specialist and associate professor at Santa Casa de São Paulo, Brazil, discusses key highlights of current dengue research from selected congresses held in 2025—the World Congress of the World Society for Pediatric Infectious Diseases, the American Society of Tropical Medicine and Hygiene, and the Latin American Congress of Pediatric Infectious Disease. The aim of the vodcast is to communicate the latest scientific findings and provide insights into their clinical significance. The vodcast covers a wide range of topics relating to dengue, including vaccination, surveillance and control, prophylaxis, diagnosis, management, predicting disease progression, and immune responses during dengue infection. Data from these congresses highlight the key role dengue vaccines play in disease prevention, and how vector control methods, like Wolbachia, can help reduce the spread of the disease. They also highlight the need for an integrated approach to targeting dengue, bringing together advanced diagnostic and prognostic tools, vector control techniques, and novel therapeutic and disease management strategies.Vodcast available for this article. Dengue Vaccines

I didn’t mention, but associated with the 6,000,000 cases we reported in Brazil in 2024, we have approximately 6000 deaths associated with dengue [1]. Vaccine Overview

I'll start just briefly mentioning an overview of the available vaccines. We currently have three already-licensed dengue vaccines. These are all live attenuated vaccines [2, 3], and we also have some vaccines in late-stage development, and I will briefly mention that.

The first of these vaccines, licensed years ago, is CYD-TDV. CYD-TDV has a yellow fever backbone genetically modified to express two key membrane and envelope proteins—these are key elements to induce antibodies and immune responses. However, this vaccine proved to be associated with increased risk of severity in persons who were vaccinated when naΪve to the virus (seronegative). So, the need to prescreen populations to administer the vaccine clearly jeopardized the possibility of using this vaccine on a large scale, and the vaccine is now being discontinued. The schedule associated with this vaccine was three doses administered every 6 months [2, 4].

The second vaccine that was licensed is TAK-003. TAK-003 consists of a live attenuated dengue 2 strain. So, we have a dengue 2 strain attenuated, and the three other components of the vaccine are three chimeric viruses consisting of the same attenuated backbone of the dengue virus 2 but genetically modified to express the membrane and envelope proteins from the respective serotype 1, serotype 3, and serotype 4 viruses. Two doses of the vaccine is the schedule of this vaccine, and these two doses should be administered 3 months apart [2, 5, 6].

The third vaccine to be licensed in Brazil is the Butantan-DV vaccine. It is administered as a single dose and combines three attenuated wild-type dengue virus serotypes—dengue virus types 1, 3, and 4—with a chimeric virus consisting of an attenuated dengue virus type 4 backbone expressing the premembrane and envelope proteins of dengue virus type 2 [7].

In late-stage development, we have a very similar vaccine, very analogous to the Butantan-DV, which is the TV003 and also TV005. That is already in development and there are other vaccines that are also in the pipeline [3, 8].

Evaluating a TAK-003 Booster Dose in Healthy Children and Adolescents in Endemic Regions: Results from DEN-301

So, here we see some key data that was presented associated with these vaccines. Now we start with the TAK-003 booster data. This is very new data, not yet published, but was presented in the congress. So, what they showed here is the long-term and the booster data for TAK-003—presented from two double-blind placebo control phase 3 studies.

Here we see the preliminary findings showing that TAK-003 protected against symptomatic dengue. [In the main trial,] the efficacy was 61% and also protected against dengue hospitalization (84% vaccine efficacy) [5]. These efficacy estimates were observed up to 4.5 years after the primary vaccination. The age group of these efficacy data were children and adolescents [5]. Among children (4–11 years old at time of randomization in the main trial) that received previously the primary vaccination, a booster dose was given 4–4.5 years after the primary vaccination [9]. So, what they identified in this study was that continued protection against virologically confirmed dengue (VCD) as well as protection against dengue hospitalization was observed; 74% vaccine efficacy against VCD and 90% efficacy against hospitalization. These estimates were observed in a period of 25 months after the booster. So, clearly showing the importance of these recent data [9].

Regarding safety, no TAK-003-related serious adverse events or deaths were observed in the booster data. All severe dengue and dengue hemorrhagic fever cases that occurred were observed in the placebo group, and the safety profile was largely consistent with that seen after primary vaccination [9]. So, as a summary of the findings of this booster data, dengue protection continued during the booster phase, the TAK-003 booster was well tolerated, and the data, in our opinion, is not yet clear for supporting the need of booster dose.

Evaluating a TAK-003 Booster Dose in Healthy Adults and Adolescents: Results from DEN-303

So, here we see the evaluations from the DEN-303 trial. This is a follow-up study of two phase 3 trials, DEN-315 and DEN-304, and here the presenters were able to show the immunogenicity of TAK-003 against all four serotypes. The importance of these data is that the data were collected in a non-endemic setting, meaning it’s probably a naive population. The study was done in Mexico City, which is clearly a non-endemic setting of dengue, as well as parts of the United States. The observations were acceptable long-term safety over up to 69 months of follow-up in adolescents, which is the Mexican data, and as well as up to 42 months of follow-up in adults, which is the US data [10, 11].

Effectiveness of TAK-003 in Adolescents in São Paulo, Brazil, During the 2024 Outbreak

Well, let's move on to my region. This is real-world data from São Paulo, from Brazil, during our huge 2024 outbreak – this is a case control study during this outbreak. The outbreak was associated predominantly with dengue 1 and dengue 2, and TAK-003 protected against both symptomatic dengue and hospitalization due to dengue in adolescents aged 10–14 years. This is the age group that was included initially in the vaccination program of TAK-003 here in Brazil, specifically here showing the data from São Paulo. So, following the first dose, as you can see here, the effectiveness of the vaccine against symptomatic dengue was 67% in a period of 14 to 27 days, so it's short-term effectiveness data, and 49% after 90 days. So, this is preliminary data, short-term vaccine effectiveness in a real-world setting [1, 12].

[NOTE: At the 43rd Annual Meeting of the European Society for Paediatric Infectious Diseases (ESPID), held in Bologna, Italy, from 26–30 May 2026, investigators presented 1-year follow-up data from a large real-world effectiveness study of TAK-003 conducted during the 2024–2025 dengue outbreak in São Paulo, Brazil. The study showed sustained protection against symptomatic dengue and dengue-related hospitalization in adolescents aged 10–14 years, with effectiveness of 59.2% and 73.1% against symptomatic dengue after one and two doses, respectively, and 74.6% and 87.9%, respectively, against hospitalization. These findings provide important real-world evidence supporting the effectiveness of TAK-003 in reducing dengue burden in endemic, high-transmission settings and during outbreak response [13].

Other TAK-003 Studies

So, these are a couple of messages from other TAK-003 studies. Post marketing surveillance – this is a review of safety data conducted 2 years after launch. Although some new safety signals were identified post marketing, which includes anaphylactic reactions, this was seen here in Brazil, and some transient vaccine viremia-associated events, the important message is that the overall risk-benefit profile of TAK-003 remains positive [14]. Now we have some new general recommendations pertaining to anaphylaxis that were included in the label for the TAK-003 vaccine.

A postauthorization study (DEN-401) is ongoing in children and adolescents in Asia to further assess the effectiveness of TAK-003 in preventing hospitalization due to dengue [15]. This will be key data because it will be data collected in Asia, with other serotypes circulating compared to the serotypes that circulated at similar periods here in Latin America.

A number of presentations in this congress also analyzed immune responses to TAK-003. In summary, TAK-003 elicited immediate humoral and cellular immune responses in adults, regardless of whether they were naΪve or already exposed to dengue [16]. The humoral immune responses elicited by TAK-003 showed unique non-overlapping parameters, with differences being driven mainly by dengue serotype and time since vaccination [17]. Although the fact that humoral responses induced by TAK-003 are different from those observed by natural infection, they were serotype balanced and associated with high-quality immune responses [18].

5-Year Efficacy and Safety Data for the Butantan-DV Vaccine

Now let's share the data that was recently presented regarding the Butantan-DV vaccine. This is data after 5 years. This is a placebo-controlled phase 3 trial conducted in Brazil [19]. It’s overall efficacy of 5 years of data from the Butantan-DV single-dose study that includes children, adolescents and adults up to 59 years of age. All these age groups were included in this efficacy evaluation. The efficacy was 77% against symptomatic VCD, 77% in participants with prior exposure, and 58% in those participants that were naΪve when they were included in the study. The serotypes that circulated during the study were only dengue 1 and dengue 2, so there is not yet available data against dengue 3 and dengue 4, and the efficacy estimates against symptomatic VCD was 73% against dengue 1 and 55% against dengue 2. The efficacy against dengue with warning signs and severe dengue, which was also one of the objectives, was 80%, and a post-hoc analysis then indicated 100% efficacy in the prevention of hospitalization due to dengue. So far, no safety concerns have been observed [19].

Surveillance and Control

So, let's move to the next topic which is vector surveillance and control. This is another key pillar to prevention. This is clearly very important together with vaccines—the control of the mosquito and improvement in surveillance methods.

Approaches to Improve Vector Surveillance and Control

So, these are some new approaches that are being considered to improve dengue surveillance and also to improve control of the mosquito.

Some molecular techniques were presented. The idea is to improve mosquito identification via molecular techniques. This is a labor- and time-intensive, and also costly, technique, and reliable electronic systems to manage vector data have been lacking so far. These are new techniques that are being developed. Some of the approaches being considered to improve dengue surveillance include the possibility of extraction of virus and vector RNA/DNA—that could be performed in the field without the need for a lab [20]. Other approaches include artificial intelligence-based identification of mosquito species [21], the vector-borne disease platform for data recording and analysis [22], and machine learning pipelines [23]. The idea is an open-source pipeline supporting vector screening and trying to link vectors and patterns in screening data to predict disease risk factors, and areas that would be at increased risk of having outbreaks of dengue.

There were also very interesting presentations regarding vector control. Taking into account the continued expansion of dengue geographically, as I mentioned previously in this presentation, approaches to improve control of the dengue vector are being evaluated to reduce transmission: mosquito traps [24]; techniques to limit vector breeding (fertilization interference [25], sterile insects—males sterilized using X-rays [26] or males and females genetically modified via CRISPR to achieve functional sterility of these mosquitoes [27]); insecticides (attractive toxic sugar baits [28] and very interesting transcriptomic profiling of mosquitoes to identify mechanisms underlying insecticide resistance [29], which could be informative for public health measures); and also limiting viral infections in mosquitoes [30, 31].

Impact of Releasing Wolbachia-Infected Mosquitoes on Dengue Incidence in Campo Grande, Brazil

One of the techniques that was presented in this congress was Wolbachia, which is a bacteria, with Wolbachia-infected mosquitoes. This is a study performed in Campo Grande here, also in Brazil. This approach was shown to be a very effective intervention for daily control in an urban setting in a Brazilian city, in which more than 100 million Wolbachia-infected mosquitoes were released in the environment between December 2020 and December 2023 [32, 33]. The intervention, as presented here, greatly reduced the magnitude of the dengue peaks in the outbreaks, even with Wolbachia prevalence as low as 40%. Even in this prevalence of Wolbachia in the community of mosquitoes, this significantly reduced dengue incidence. In this study, the incidence of dengue dropped by 63% in fully treated areas [32, 33], which is very compelling, very reassuring data of these techniques that should be incorporated in the long-term to really control dengue.

Improving Dengue Virus Surveillance

So now let's move to dengue surveillance. There are some new approaches that are being considered for dengue surveillance. Dengue-specific low-cost serological tests, novel diagnostic tests. There is a clear need to develop rapid and cost-effective diagnostic tests that can differentiate between antigenically related viruses [34]. It's important to mention that we have other arboviruses circulating in this area, which sometimes can induce cross reactivity. So, we clearly need more accurate and rapid tests to help clinicians in the field.

Also, broad monitoring for dengue infection, in addition to new diagnostic tests aimed at identifying infections in individual people (which can be resource intensive and challenging). Techniques that more broadly monitor the presence of dengue infection across a wider cross-section of our population. For example, building on innovation made during the COVID pandemic in the US, they were able to monitor wastewater samples for the presence of dengue virus—these would help to predict outbreaks, as well as to identify sequences of the virus that are circulating in different regions of the world [35]. Also, development of national surveillance systems to inform on the epidemiology of travel-related infections and local spread of the virus [36].

Prophylaxis, Diagnosis, Management, and Predicting Disease Progression

Regarding dengue prophylaxis, dengue diagnosis and dengue management, we have some new approaches that are currently being considered to try to improve dengue diagnosis and particularly dengue management.

Dengue Prophylaxis with Daily Oral Mosnodenvir: Results from a Phase 2a Study

So, regarding prophylaxis, a study using a new antiviral in development, named mosnodenvir, was presented [37]. In summary, the data collected among adults between 18 and 55 years showed it to be a promising intervention. It was a randomized controlled human infection model, and this study with mosnodenvir prophylaxis showed that the antiviral was associated with the possibility of reduced dengue 3 infection risk in naΪve individuals that were included in this trial [37].

New Dengue Diagnosis and Management Methods

Regarding diagnosis, new dengue diagnosis, as well as new management methods, were presented. Regarding diagnosis, it is important to remember that the clinical manifestations of dengue are very similar to the clinical manifestations of other arboviruses, like zika, like chikungunya, which is always an issue for clinicians [38]—to be able to distinguish a patient with these symptoms. So, improvement in diagnosis methods is clearly needed. They presented some hints in terms of clinical manifestations that may help differentiate dengue from the other arboviral infections. Among these clinical manifestations, they highlighted that the presence of abdominal pain, leukopenia, basophilia, vomiting, and nausea were all clinical manifestations more associated with dengue [39].

Also, some new serological assays that could help to distinguish, with accuracy, dengue from other flavivirus [34]. Some saliva and urine basic testing, which are less invasive testing which would help compared with the standard serum tests [40].

Regarding management for patients whose dengue infection is severe—associated with plasma leakage, severe bleeding, impaired organ function—restoring plasma volume is the standard treatment. If I could address one key message in terms of management, it is the importance of early recognition of shock. This is crucial to decrease complications and to decrease case fatality rates. So, clinicians should be able to detect shock early and implement vigorous strategies to really manage and address this issue. The World Health Organization (WHO) currently recommends crystalloid solutions for initial replacement and colloids for persistent shocks [41]. Adding an infusion of 25% albumin solution to standard WHO treatment may improve survival in children with fluid-refractory non-responder severe dengue, according to a small study that was performed in India. The addition of albumin improved survival by 20% and decreased the need for other treatments [42].

Low-cost wearable devices that monitor hemodynamic instability have shown promise for real-time non-invasive assessment of pediatric patients with dengue shock. This is also very important to predict disease progression [43].

Predicting Disease Progression

So, being able to predict disease progression has proven to be crucial to reduce complications and severe disease. There are some clinical and demographic factors that may help to predict progression to severe dengue and these include abnormal mental activity [44], the presence of pleural infusion or pulmonary infiltration [44], low platelet count [44], and being elderly [45]. In Brazil, we have experience showing that two in every three deaths associated with dengue during the 2024 outbreak were reported in elderly persons [1]. So, being elderly is clearly a risk for increased severe complications and death associated with dengue. Other risk factors include the presence of diabetes [45], hypertension [45], and obviously the presence of secondary dengue infection [45]. For context, the identification of warning signs of severe disease, never forget, this is also clearly important, among these warning signs are the presence of persisting abdominal pain, vomiting, bleeding (nose and gums), increased hematocrit levels, fluid accumulation, liver enlargement, lethargy alternated with restlessness, and also the presence of blood in vomit or stools [46, 47]. Also notably important to mention that severe dengue has been shown in a study to be associated with socioeconomic vulnerability—lower education and higher poverty were elements associated with increased severity of dengue [48].

And finally, the point of care ultrasound. Point-of-care ultrasound of the abdomen, the lungs and heart may also be useful in predicting dengue severity in pediatric patients [49].

Immune Responses During Dengue Infection

Regarding immune responses during dengue infection, we saw an overview of new findings pertaining to immune responses to infection and immune-mediated enhancement.

Immune Responses to Dengue Infection

Investigating immune responses to dengue helps to understand the landscape and the quality of protection following infection or following vaccination. So, whether immune responses are serotype specific, which is long-term, or cross reactive, which is usually short-term, and the mechanism behind this phenomenon. So, antibodies from a primary infection—this is also a very important message. Antibodies from primary infection, from the first infection of a person, may worsen the risk of having a secondary infection from a different serotype and increase presentation. Very interestingly, this increased risk is observed only in the secondary infections but are not observed after the third or even the fourth infection [50]. The increased risk of severity is associated with secondary infections with dengue, which is the phenomenon of antibody-dependent enhancement, a process in which non-neutralizing dengue-virus specific antibodies to a serotype, instead of helping, will facilitate viral entry into cells via Fc gamma receptors. This is not yet fully understood, although new research has identified a potentially critical role for cell-signaling intermediates, such as SHP-1, in this process [51].

So, immune responses to dengue infection—a number of presentations discussed new findings pertaining to immune responses to dengue infection, including: the magnitude and long-term persistence of dengue virus antibodies [52]; characterization of cross-reactive antibodies [53]; the proteomic characterization of antibody responses to secondary infections [54]; mathematical modelling of these antibody responses to help identify secondary infections [55]; and the use of antibody responses and viremia to safely model primary, secondary and tertiary infections using attenuated dengue virus strains [56]. So, these were also some of the new data that were presented.

Conclusions

So, as a summary of these new very interesting research findings that were presented in these congresses, I can highlight in the summary that dengue is clearly expanding, and expanding not only in incidence, but also in the geographic areas at risk. We are seeing outbreaks of dengue in places that never reported dengue in the past. We have seen outbreaks of dengue in North America, we have seen outbreaks of dengue in Europe, so dengue is clearly an expanding disease.

Vaccination is a key pillar tool for prevention. We have seen data with different vaccines, with TAK-003 supporting meaningful protection and real-world data in adolescents. Recent long-term safety data showing acceptable safety up to approximately 5–6 years in adolescents and approximately 3–4 years in adults in non-endemic settings. A booster dose was well tolerated, although the totality of the data is not yet sufficient to clearly recommend or not a booster dose in those individuals. We have seen very recent data with a single dose of the Butantan-DV vaccine that proves it to be efficacious against symptomatic VCD—against virologically confirmed dengue caused by dengue 1 and dengue 2, which were the only dengue serotypes that circulated during the study—regardless of dengue serostatus at baseline. With an acceptable safety profile up to 5 years after vaccination.

We have seen also integrated vector control measures like Wolbachia, a very interesting element to help with vector control. Improved surveillance, which can deliver sustained community level risk reduction and the need to optimize dengue outcomes. This requires an integrated approach, advancing diagnostics, prognostic tools to help clinicians, and the development of therapeutics. This could help to better distinguish dengue from other arboviruses and to identify those at higher risk, and to improve the management of these patients.

So, thank you very much for your attention.
