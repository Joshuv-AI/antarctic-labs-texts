Independent association between relative fat mass and total bone mineral density in U.S. adolescents: a cross-sectional study adjusting for Appendicular Lean Mass Index
Authors: Penghui Lv, Chenglong Zhang, Shuaiwei Wei, Jincheng Bai, Lu Li, Jianjun Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe relationship between adiposity and bone health, often referred to as the “obesity paradox,” remains a subject of debate. Relative fat mass (RFM) has recently emerged as a more accurate surrogate for body fat percentage than the Body Mass Index (BMI). However, the independent association between RFM and bone mineral density (BMD) in the adolescent population, particularly after rigorously accounting for lean mass, remains ambiguous, particularly concerning the independent metabolic influence of adiposity. This cross-sectional study evaluated 3,933 adolescents (aged 12–19 years) from the 2011–2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). Multiple imputation was applied to manage missing covariates. We employed weighted multivariate linear regression to assess the association between RFM and total BMD. Appendicular Lean Mass Index (ALMI) was integrated as a core covariate to isolate the metabolic influence of adiposity from its mechanical loading effects. In the unadjusted model, RFM showed no significant correlation with total BMD (β = 0.001, P = 0.122). However, after adjusting for ALMI and other potential confounders, a significant negative association emerged. In the fully adjusted model, each one-unit increase in RFM was associated with a 0.005 g/cm2 decrease in total BMD (β = -0.005, P < 0.001). Furthermore, sex-specific non-linear relationships were identified. Threshold effect analysis revealed inflection points at an RFM of 21.28 for males and 39.44 for females. Beyond these respective thresholds, the decline in bone mineral accrual significantly accelerated (P < 0.001). After accounting for the mechanical loading effects of ALMI, higher RFM is independently associated with reduced total BMD in adolescents. These findings reveal observational sex-specific inflection points (21.28 for males, 39.44 for females) beyond which lower BMD was observed. Introduction

Osteoporosis (OP) is a systemic skeletal disorder characterized by low bone mass and microarchitectural deterioration, leading to increased bone fragility and fracture susceptibility1,2. While traditionally considered a geriatric condition, adolescence represents a critical “window of opportunity” for lifelong skeletal health, as approximately 40%–50% of peak bone mass is accrued during this period3. Recent epidemiological shifts indicate that suboptimal bone mass accrual in youth is becoming increasingly prevalent, potentially predisposing this population to early-onset osteoporosis and fragility fractures in later life4–6. Consequently, elucidating accurate indicators to monitor bone mineral density (BMD) during this developmental phase is essential for timely intervention and long-term fracture prevention7.

The escalating global obesity epidemic among youth has introduced complex challenges to pediatric bone health8–10. Historically, a “positive correlation” between obesity and BMD was widely accepted, primarily attributed to the increased mechanical loading that excess weight exerts on the skeleton, which stimulates osteoblastic bone formation in accordance with Wolff’s Law11–14. However, this “obesity paradox” is increasingly being scrutinized. Emerging evidence suggests that once the mechanical advantages of muscle mass and total weight are partitioned out, excess adiposity may exert detrimental metabolic effects on bone via chronic low-grade inflammation and dysregulated adipokine secretion15,16.

Traditional anthropometric indices, such as Body Mass Index (BMI) and Waist Circumference (WC), are limited by their inability to distinguish between lean mass and fat mass, or to differentiate subcutaneous from visceral adipose tissue17,18. This lack of phenotypic specificity is particularly confounding during adolescence, a period marked by rapid and sexually dimorphic changes in body composition. To address these limitations, Woolcott and Bergman proposed Relative Fat Mass (RFM) as a novel adiposity index19. Validated against dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), RFM has demonstrated superior accuracy in estimating whole-body fat percentage compared to BMI across diverse ethnic and age groups. Furthermore, RFM has been robustly associated with metabolic derangements, including insulin resistance and dyslipidemia20–22.

While a negative association between RFM and BMD was recently reported in adults23, this relationship remains poorly understood in adolescents, where bone-muscle-fat interactions are highly dynamic. Critically, most prior research has failed to decouple the metabolic harm of adiposity from the mechanical protection afforded by muscle mass. In this study, we utilized data from the 2011–2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to systematically investigate the association between RFM and total BMD in U.S. adolescents. By integrating the Appendicular Lean Mass Index (ALMI) as a pivotal covariate and employing multiple imputation to ensure data integrity, we aimed to clarify the independent impact of relative adiposity on adolescent skeletal development.

Methods

Study population

Data were derived from NHANES 2011–2018, a series of biennial cross-sectional surveys designed to evaluate the health and nutritional status of the non-institutionalized U.S. population. The study protocols were approved by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) Research Ethics Review Board, and all participants provided written informed consent. As depicted in the recruitment flowchart (Fig. 1), we initially screened 39,156 participants across four cycles. Individuals outside the 12–19 age range (n = 33,941) were excluded. Among the remaining adolescents, we further excluded those with missing data for total BMD (n = 1,033), RFM (n = 31), or sample weights (n = 218). To mitigate selection bias and preserve statistical power, missing covariates—including physical activity (15.4%), dietary intake (11.1%), biochemical markers (approx. 10.0%), family poverty income ratio (8.6%), and ALMI (0.8%)—were addressed using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE). We generated 5 imputed datasets. The imputation model included all analytical variables, with biochemical markers utilized solely as auxiliary variables to enhance predictive accuracy while remaining excluded from the final regression models to avoid over-adjustment bias.

Flowchart of participant selection from NHANES 2011–2018.

Exposure and outcome measures

The primary exposure of interest was RFM. Anthropometric measurements, including height and waist circumference, were performed by trained technicians following standardized protocols. RFM was calculated using the sex-specific equation: RFM = 64 − (20 × height/waist) + (12 × sex), where sex was assigned as 0 for males and 1 for females. The primary outcome was total BMD, quantified via DXA using a Hologic QDR-4500 A fan-beam densitometer (Hologic, Inc., Bedford, MA, USA)24. The whole-body DXA scans were performed to partition the body into several anatomical areas of interest, including the head, trunk, left/right arms, and left/right legs. Total BMD was defined as the integrated bone mineral density (g/cm²) of these collective regions. For this study, we utilized the whole-body BMD value (Total Body) as the primary outcome measure, as it provides a comprehensive assessment of the skeletal status during the rapid growth phase of adolescence. DXA is recognized as the gold standard for pediatric bone assessment due to its exceptional precision and minimal radiation exposure.

Covariates

Covariates were selected based on biological plausibility and established literature, encompassing demographic variables such as age, sex, race/ethnicity (Mexican American, Other Hispanic, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, and Other Race), Family Poverty Income Ratio (PIR), and the education level of the household reference person. Body composition parameters included standing height and ALMI, defined as (Appendicular Lean Mass [kg]) / (height [m])2, to specifically partition out the mechanical loading effects of skeletal muscle. Furthermore, we adjusted for dietary factors, specifically the mean intake of calcium (mg) and vitamin D (mcg) from two 24-hour dietary recalls, as well as lifestyle factors including physical activity status. Biochemical markers, including serum calcium, phosphorus, uric acid, creatinine, and liver enzymes such as alkaline phosphatase (ALP), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST), were also evaluated. However, to avoid over-adjustment bias, these variables were intentionally excluded from the final multivariable regression models, as they likely act as intermediate mediators on the causal pathway between adiposity and bone metabolism.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in accordance with the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) guidelines for the analysis of complex NHANES data. To account for the inclusion of 24-hour dietary recall variables, we utilized the dietary day 2 sampling weights (WTDR2D). For the combined four cycles (2011–2018), new multi-year sample weights were constructed by dividing the individual cycle dietary weights by four (WTDR2D / 4) to ensure national representativeness and maintain statistical integrity.

Baseline characteristics across RFM quartiles (Q1–Q4) were compared using weighted chi-square tests for categorical variables and weighted linear regression for continuous variables. The association between RFM and total BMD was assessed using weighted multivariate linear regression with progressive adjustment across three models: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for age, sex, and race/ethnicity; and Model 3 (the fully adjusted model) further incorporated education level, PIR, height, ALMI, physical activity, and dietary intake. Notably, biochemical markers were intentionally excluded from Model 3 to prevent over-adjustment bias, as they likely act as intermediate mediators on the causal pathway. Furthermore, given that sex is integrated into the RFM equation, sex-stratified analyses were conducted to address potential collinearity and evaluate the consistency of the association across genders. Additionally, sex was retained as a covariate in the overall models to account for sex-specific physiological differences in bone metabolism that are independent of adiposity.

To explore potential non-linear dose-response relationships, we employed generalized additive models (GAM) with smooth curve fitting. Upon detection of non-linearity, a two-piecewise linear regression model was utilized to determine the sex-specific inflection points and the threshold effects. Subgroup analyses and forest plots were conducted to ensure the consistency of the association across age and sex strata. All statistical procedures were performed using R (version 4.2.2) and EmpowerStats, with a two-sided P < 0.05 defining statistical significance.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The baseline characteristics of the 3,933 participants, stratified by RFM quartiles, are detailed in Table 1. The quartiles were categorized as follows: Q1 (4.10–20.09, n = 983), Q2 (20.10–29.26, n = 983), Q3 (29.27–35.49, n = 983), and Q4 (35.49–56.54, n = 984). Significant heterogeneities were observed across quartiles for most baseline variables (all P < 0.05), with the exception of age (P = 0.100), education level (P = 0.095), and total BMD (P = 0.095). Notably, participants in the highest RFM quartile (Q4) exhibited significantly lower biochemical markers compared to those in the Q1 group, including AST (21.31 ± 0.28 U/L vs. 24.75 ± 0.44 U/L), ALP (98.81 ± 2.54 U/L vs. 182.55 ± 5.51 U/L), phosphorus (4.13 ± 0.03 mg/dL vs. 4.53 ± 0.04 mg/dL), and creatinine (0.67 ± 0.01 mg/dL vs. 0.78 ± 0.01 mg/dL) (all P < 0.001). Furthermore, nutritional factors such as calcium and vitamin D intake demonstrated a progressive decline from Q1 to Q4 (P < 0.001).

Association between RFM and adolescent BMD

The weighted multivariate linear regression analysis (Table 2) demonstrated a complex relationship between adiposity and bone health. In the unadjusted model (Model 1), RFM exhibited a non-significant trend with total BMD (β = 0.001, 95% CI: -0.001 to 0.001, P = 0.122). Upon adjusting for age, sex, and race in Model 2, a significant positive correlation emerged (β = 0.002, 95% CI: 0.002 to 0.003, P < 0.001).

However, in the fully adjusted model (Model 3)—which rigorously accounted for ALMI to decouple the metabolic influence of adiposity from muscle-driven mechanical loading—the association underwent a significant inversion. Each one-unit increase in RFM was associated with a 0.005 g/cm2 decrease in total BMD (β = -0.005, 95% CI: -0.005 to -0.004, P < 0.001). When treated as a categorical variable, participants in Q4 demonstrated a markedly lower total BMD compared to those in Q1 (β = -0.076, 95% CI: -0.095 to -0.057, P < 0.001), with a significant P for trend (< 0.001). Sex-stratified analysis further confirmed that this significant inverse association was robustly maintained in both males (β = -0.005, P < 0.001) and females (β = -0.004, P < 0.001), as shown in Table 2.

Nonlinearity and threshold effect analysis

GAM smooth curve fitting revealed significant non-linear relationships between RFM and total BMD for both sexes (Fig. 2). As visualized, the RFM distribution ranges differed notably between sexes, primarily reflecting the constant adjustment factor in the RFM formula (+ 12 for females). Specifically, males predominantly occupied the lower RFM spectrum (approx. 5–40), whereas females were distributed across a higher range (approx. 22–55). Furthermore, the log-likelihood ratio tests confirmed that the two-piecewise linear regression models provided a significantly superior fit compared to the standard linear models for both males and females (both P < 0.001).

As detailed in Table 3, distinct sex-specific inflection points were identified. For males, the inflection point was 21.28. Below this threshold, the association was not significant (β < 0.001, 95% CI: -0.002 to 0.002, P = 0.955); however, once RFM exceeded 21.28, a steep decline in BMD was observed (β = -0.008, 95% CI: -0.009 to -0.007, P < 0.001). For females, the inflection point was substantially higher at 39.44. Similarly, variations below this threshold showed no significant impact (β = -0.001, 95% CI: -0.002 to 0.001, P = 0.267), but exceeding 39.44 precipitated an accelerated compromise of bone mass (β = -0.009, 95% CI: -0.012 to -0.007, P < 0.001).

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses confirmed the stability of the negative association between RFM and total BMD across most strata, including sex, race/ethnicity, physical activity, and education level (Fig. 3). Notably, a highly significant interaction effect was identified for age (P for interaction < 0.001). In contrast, no significant interaction effects were observed for sex (P = 0.217), race (P = 0.219), physical activity (P = 0.124), or education level (P = 0.138). This suggests that while the detrimental impact of RFM on BMD is consistent across most demographic and lifestyle strata, the magnitude of this effect is significantly modulated by the developmental stage of the adolescent.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is the first to systematically elucidate the association between RFM and total BMD in a nationally representative U.S. adolescent population. Our findings demonstrate a robust, non-linear inverse association between RFM and total BMD. Notably, a “reversal effect” was observed across regression models: a positive association emerged after basic demographic adjustment (Model 2), but shifted to a significant negative association after further adjustment for ALMI. This finding suggests that, after accounting for the mechanical contribution of muscle mass, adiposity exerts an adverse metabolic effect on skeletal development.

A long-standing debate surrounds the “obesity paradox” in bone metabolism25,26. Higher BMI has traditionally been associated with increased BMD, largely explained by Wolff’s Law27. In the present study, the crude model showed no significant association, while Model 2 demonstrated a positive relationship (β = 0.002, P < 0.001), seemingly supporting this paradigm. However, indices such as BMI and RFM do not distinguish between lean and fat mass28. After adjusting for ALMI in Model 3, the association reversed (β = -0.005, P < 0.001), indicating that the apparent protective effect is largely attributable to muscle mass rather than adiposity itself29.

ALMI serves as a surrogate for mechanical loading, with skeletal muscle promoting bone formation through both biomechanical stimulation and secretion of myokines such as insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1)30–33. Once this factor was accounted for, the detrimental metabolic effect of adiposity became evident. Methodologically, biochemical markers were excluded from the final model to avoid over-adjustment bias, as they may act as intermediates on the causal pathway. Although these biochemical variables were not included in the final regression models, their distribution across RFM quartiles provides supportive descriptive evidence rather than causal inference. Importantly, the inverse association remained robust after adjusting for lifestyle and dietary factors, suggesting an independent metabolic effect of adiposity.

Baseline characteristics further indicated that individuals in the highest RFM quartile had poorer nutritional and biochemical profiles, suggesting a potential “double-hit” effect: chronic low-grade inflammation combined with suboptimal nutritional status may synergistically impair bone accrual. These observations should be interpreted as associative patterns rather than evidence of causal mediation. This highlights that the skeletal impact of adiposity is likely exacerbated by clustered metabolic and nutritional disadvantages.

Sex-specific analyses revealed distinct non-linear relationships and inflection points. In males, BMD declined markedly when RFM exceeded 21.28, whereas females maintained relative stability up to 39.44. This sexual dimorphism is biologically plausible, as females require higher essential fat for pubertal development, and adipose-derived aromatase contributes to estrogen production, potentially offering transient skeletal protection.

These findings suggest that the identified inflection points may serve as observational reference values for risk stratification, rather than definitive clinical intervention thresholds. The observed inflection points may represent a physiological “tipping point,” beyond which pro-inflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress override the beneficial effects of mechanical loading34–36.

The inverse association between RFM and BMD may be explained by mechanisms within the bone-fat axis37,38. Adipose tissue secretes pro-inflammatory cytokines that promote osteoclastogenesis and inhibit osteoblast activity39. Additionally, mesenchymal stem cells may preferentially differentiate into adipocytes rather than osteoblasts, impairing bone formation. Dysregulated adipokines, including elevated leptin and reduced adiponectin, further disrupt bone remodeling40.

Subgroup analyses also identified a significant interaction with age (P for interaction < 0.001), suggesting modulation by pubertal development. Although physical activity did not show a significant interaction (P = 0.124), the consistently negative associations across activity levels indicate that excess adiposity remains an independent risk factor that may not be fully offset by mechanical loading during adolescence.

Clinical implications and limitations

Clinically, RFM offers a cost-effective, non-invasive, and radiation-free surrogate for adiposity that outperforms traditional BMI in identifying adolescents at risk of suboptimal skeletal health. Our findings underscore that RFM levels below these observed inflection points were associated with more favorable bone outcomes; however, these values should be interpreted cautiously and not as definitive clinical targets given the cross-sectional nature of the study.

Despite its strengths (e.g., a large nationally representative cohort and rigorous multi-dimensional adjustments), several limitations exist. First, the cross-sectional design precludes definitive causal inferences. Second, the RFM equation was originally developed for adults. Although it demonstrates superior correlation with DXA-derived fat than BMI, its accuracy in adolescents—who undergo rapid sexually dimorphic transitions—is not as extensively validated. Consequently, absolute RFM values in this cohort should be interpreted with caution.

Third, the lack of precise biological maturity markers (e.g., Tanner staging or bone age) in the NHANES 2011–2018 cycles forced a reliance on chronological age. Given the high inter-individual variability during puberty, this may introduce residual confounding, even with our robust adjustment for ALMI.

Fourth, the relatively high obesity prevalence in the NHANES dataset may limit the generalizability of our findings to populations with lower baseline adiposity. Finally, while DXA is the gold standard for assessing areal BMD, it cannot capture volumetric density or bone microarchitecture. Future longitudinal studies are warranted to track RFM kinetics and their long-term skeletal impact. Therefore, the absolute RFM values and identified thresholds should be interpreted with caution in adolescent populations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study reveals a significant independent and non-linear negative association between RFM and total BMD in U.S. adolescents. By adjusting for ALMI, we decoupled the mechanical loading of muscle from the metabolic impacts of adiposity, unmasking excess fat mass as a potent risk factor for suboptimal skeletal development. We identified critical sex-specific inflection points at an RFM of 21.28 for males and 39.44 for females; beyond these thresholds, the decline in bone mineral accrual significantly accelerates. These findings challenge the traditional “obesity paradox” and emphasize the necessity of sex-specific clinical targets for maintaining adolescent skeletal health. These thresholds should be interpreted as observational inflection points rather than causal or clinically actionable targets.
