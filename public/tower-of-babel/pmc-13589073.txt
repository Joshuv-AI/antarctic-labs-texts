Impact of Respiratory Mechanics on Venous Return in Single Ventricle Physiology
Authors: Markus A. Horvath, Jean Bonnemain, Andreas Escher, David Van Story, Bryce Starr, Mulan Jiang, Emilia Herdes, David Annese, Breanna Piekarski, Stephanie Agudelo, Kinsey Brassaw, Maria Valenza, Andrew Powell, Pedro J. del Nido, Ellen T. Roche
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Visual Abstract

The Fontan procedure is a stepwise surgical intervention performed in participants with congenital heart disease not suitable for biventricular repair.1 It involves diverting the venous return directly to the pulmonary circulation, creating the total cavopulmonary connection (TCPC) and resulting in an effective single ventricle physiology. Owing to major progress in surgical techniques and clinical management, the 30-year survival rate among Fontan-palliated participants is now higher than 80%, and the number of Fontan patients is expected to double over the next 20 years.2 However, the complication rate remains unacceptably high and affects outcomes and quality of life.

In the absence of a subpulmonary ventricle, the venous return to the pulmonary circulation is passive and depends on the gradient between the mean venous systemic and the pulmonary artery pressures. This alteration of the driving force results in systemic venous hypertension and low cardiac output.3 Associated aberrant univentricular hemodynamics represent the hallmarks of Fontan physiology and trigger a cascade of pathophysiological consequences, ultimately leading to a condition where the circulatory system no longer meets the metabolic need—the “failing Fontan.”3

Advances in the understanding of Fontan physiology suggest that the root cause of circulatory dysfunction lies in compromised venous return and chronic venous congestion.4 The latter increases the afterload on abdominal organs and plays an important role in the development of Fontan-associated liver disease5,6 or protein-losing enteropathy.7

In Fontan circulation, venous return is greatly affected by respiratory mechanics.8 Although inspiration increases venous return, the opposite occurs during expiration; in the absence of a valvular apparatus, retrograde flow in the inferior vena cava (IVC) up to 30% has been reported, which may be associated with detrimental consequences.9 It has been suggested that retrograde flow increases portal pressure, which, in turn, may negatively affect abdominal organs.10 Furthermore, retrograde flow increases venous pulsatility, which is associated with flow inefficiencies.11

Although the effect of respiration on global venous return during effort or at rest has gained increasing attention,12,13 only a handful of clinical studies exist14,15 and there remains a paucity of understanding about the driving mechanisms of flow reversal. Previous studies showed conflicting results about the role of heart and respiration on the total driving force,16,17 whereas recent studies showed that respiration governs flow characteristics but does not affect mean flow.8 Of note, the distinct effect of respiratory mechanics, characterized by respiratory effort and rate, on retrograde flow has not been studied previously. A mechanistic understanding of the interaction between the respiratory system and venous return may improve participant management and help develop new therapies.

Methods

Study group

Between April 2021 and February 2022, Fontan patients who were scheduled for routine MRI follow-up at Boston Children's Hospital were screened. Inclusion criteria were participants with Fontan physiology who 1) were 15 years of age or older, 2) did not have a Fontan fenestration or had previously had the fenestration closed, and 3) had a resting O2 saturation >85%. The study was approved by the local ethics committee at Boston Children's Hospital (IRB-P00037103). Written informed consent was obtained and stored for all participants.

Study design

Eligible participants were contacted prior to their routinely scheduled MR imaging appointment. Following participant consent, the participant and their family were provided with a detailed explanation of the study. Remaining questions were addressed, and a rehearsal of the study was performed. Upon completion of the clinically indicated routine MRI, the in-scanner breathing study was conducted. During this study, the participant was guided through a breathing protocol using an audiovisual guide streamed with the available headphones and video screen.

MRI breathing protocol

The breathing protocol consisted of sequentially varying either respiratory effort or rate during the MR image acquisition, while maintaining the other one constant. Respiratory efforts were classified as shallow, baseline, and deep, whereas RRs were classified as slow, baseline, and fast. The protocol, represented in Figure 2, was divided into 5 segments: 1) baseline breathing at 14/min; 2) shallow breathing at 14/min; 3) deep breathing at 14/min; 4) slow breathing at 9/min; 5) fast breathing at 28/min. For each segment, the acquisition time was adjusted to ensure that 6 full breathing cycles were recorded for each participant; this corresponded to approximately 30 seconds for all conditions except slow breathing, which required a longer acquisition time. For analysis, the last 5 cycles of each segment were used for averaging. Each segment was separated by a 1-minute rest phase during which the participant could breathe freely to recover from the preceding breathing pattern. The participants were encouraged to synchronize their breathing with the audio-visual guide (see Video 1).Figure 2Breathing ProtocolThe protocol is subdivided into 5 sequential breathing patterns: baseline, shallow, deep, slow, and fast. Image acquisition protocol

MR imaging was performed on a 1.5-Tesla MR system (Philips Medical Systems) using a 28-channel surface coil array. Recordings were made in 2-D phase-contrast (PC-MRI) mode to acquire real-time flow measurements at a temporal resolution of 54 ms. Sensitivity encoding (SENSE 5) and echo planar imaging level 11 were applied during image acquisition. A velocity encoding (VENC) of 180 cm/s was chosen based on preliminary measurements to avoid velocity aliasing during respiratory maneuvers associated with transient peak velocities. Peak IVC velocities observed during the study supported the selected VENC (see Supplemental Table 1).

The voxel size was set to 2.5 × 2.73 mm with a slice thickness of 10 mm. The imaging plane of 300 mm × 300 mm was positioned perpendicular to the IVC, 20 mm above the entrance of the hepatic veins into the IVC, as shown in Figures 3A to 3C. In cases where respiratory artifacts were detected because of diaphragm motion into the imaging plane, the imaging plane was adjusted, and the measurements were repeated. Accordingly, the IVC remained within the imaging plane even during exaggerated breathing, with the descending aortic flow serving as internal control. Respiratory patterns quantification

Respiratory patterns were assessed and quantified using image segmentation. MRI data were exported in DICOM format, and a motion-tracking software (Tracker Video Analysis and Modeling Tool, 2022) was used to measure the displacement of the chest wall on a frame-by-frame basis. The image plane was the same as described in the previous section. The most anterior point of the right chest wall (external surface of the rib) was identified and tracked during all the respiratory sequences (Supplemental Figure 1). For each respiratory segment, displacement was averaged over 5 respiratory cycles, and end-inspiration and end-expiration were identified as the maximal and minimal displacement values, respectively (MATLAB, MathWorks).

Amplitude was defined as the difference between the end-inspiratory and end-expiratory distance and was used as a surrogate of the respiratory effort (RE). The change in respiratory effort for each breathing pattern was calculated as the percentage change in chest wall amplitude from baseline to shallow and deep breathing, resulting in normalized data relative to the baseline chest wall motion. This nondimensional number could then be compared between all study participants. Respiratory rate (RR, 1/min) was calculated as the average over 5 respiratory cycles.

Blood flow quantification

Blood flow quantification was performed in the commercially available software cvi42 (Circle Cardiovascular Imaging Inc) from velocity maps in the recorded through planes. The cross-sectional area of the inferior vena cava (IVC) and descending aorta was segmented in each frame as shown in Figure 3D. The quantified flow information was extracted and combined with the chest motion data on a frame-by-frame basis in MATLAB. The heart rate during each respiratory segment was calculated from the peak-to-peak distance of each stroke volume in the descending aorta. Characteristics of the IVC flow data were interpreted during the 5 respiratory cycles, which were previously segmented. The following variables were extracted: 1) retrograde IVC flow rate; 2) forward IVC flow rate; 3) retrograde flow ratio (RFR); 4) forward IVC flow volume; 5) retrograde IVC flow volume; 6) pulsatility index.

Flow volumes were obtained by integrating the instantaneous flow rates over a single respiratory cycle. Retrograde flow ratio was calculated for each respiratory pattern as follows:=∑i=1nvol.i−vol.i+∗1nwith vol.i− and vol.i+ denoting the total negative (retrograde) and positive (forward) flow measured in the Fontan IVC during one respiratory cycle and n = 5 representing the number of respiratory cycles averaged.

The pulsatility index was calculated as shown below:PulsatilityIndex=(maximumflowrate−minimumflowrate)meanflowwhere respective flow rates were averaged over n = 5 respiratory cycles.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented using the mean, SD, median, IQR, or range, as appropriate. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to evaluate if continuous data were normally distributed. As data deviated from normality, nonparametric statistical methods were used. Overall hemodynamic differences across breathing conditions were assessed using the Friedman test. When the Friedman test was significant, post hoc pairwise comparisons vs baseline were performed using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm correction for multiple comparisons. Associations between 2 continuous variables were evaluated using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient. Analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software), and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Subjects

Among the initially included 26 patients, 2 participants were excluded because of implanted foreign material that prevented flow analysis, and an additional 2 participants were excluded because of implanted foreign material that prevented analysis of the breathing protocol. Accordingly, the final study population consisted of 22 participants. The participant population had congenital heart disease including hypoplastic left heart syndrome (n = 9), double inlet left ventricle (n = 5), tricuspid atresia (n = 3), double outlet right ventricle (n = 3), right-dominant atrioventricular canal (RdAVC, n = 1), and single right ventricle (n = 1). Further details are provided in Supplemental Table 2.

Although the analysis of baseline IVC flow was conducted in all 22 participants, the respiratory study was conducted in 20 participants because 1 participant could not follow the breathing protocol and 1 participant terminated the study prematurely because of fatigue. Of the 20 participants, atrioventricular regurgitation was not significant in 11 participants, whereas in 9 participants atrioventricular leaks (4 mild, 3 mild-moderate, 2 moderate-severe) were reported. No adverse effects, discomfort, pain, or other issues related to the MRI procedure or breathing protocol were noted in any other participants during the study.

Mean age was 22.7 ± 6.7 years (range: 16.4-42.2) with completed TCPC 19.4 ± 5.9 years on average before the study, and an average body surface area and body mass index of 1.8 ± 0.2 m2 and 24.4 ± 4.5 kg/m2, respectively.

Discussion

Whereas the heartbeat is the primary driver of mean systemic venous return in the Fontan circulation,9 the role of respiration in Fontan hemodynamics remains under debate. Some studies suggest minimal effects of respiration on net venous return,17 whereas others attribute an important auxiliary pumping function to respiratory mechanics.9,16,18,19 In this work, we explored the impact of key respiratory mechanics—RE and RR—on venous return to improve our understanding of cardiorespiratory interactions in Fontan patients.

Respiration as a driver of venous flow reversal in the Fontan circulation

In the absence of a subpulmonary pump or valve, the Fontan circulation is uniquely sensitive to intrathoracic–abdominal pressure gradients. During inspiration, the diaphragm moves downward, lowering intrathoracic pressures, which helps draw blood forward in the IVC and lungs. Conversely, during expiration, the diaphragm rises, which increases intrathoracic pressure while lowering abdominal pressures. In the Fontan circulation, which lacks a subpulmonary pump or valve, blood is then pushed down through the IVC into the abdomen, where pressures are lowered. Although heartbeat-induced retrograde flow has been described in intra-atrial Fontan configurations,20 the extent of respiration-driven flow reversal in TCPC anatomies has remained less well characterized.

Our findings underscore the high prevalence of retrograde flow caused by respiration in Fontan patients with TCPC. Across breathing patterns, all participants showed flow reversal, whereas 19 and 13 participants (20) exceeded 10% and 20% RFR, respectively. Furthermore, our findings show that the peak reverse flow ratio may reach 45.4%, surpassing the previously reported 30%.9

Distinct mechanisms of respiratory effort and RR

To contribute to a greater understanding of the underlying mechanisms, we examined the unique effects of RE and RR on venous IVC flow separately. Our findings show that an increase in RE and RR causes an increase in RFR. Concomitantly, we note an increase in pulsatility with both RE and RR, which marks flow inefficiencies. Our results reveal that the characteristics of RE and RR in modulating RF differ, suggesting different underlying mechanisms.

Specifically, with greater respiratory effort, the increased transdiaphragmatic pressures drive both forward and retrograde flow. Of note, there is a more pronounced increase in retrograde flow compared to forward flow. This is because of a positive baseline flow, which causes an increase in both forward and backward flow, and subsequently a larger increase in retrograde flow volumes and retrograde peak flow rates.

In contrast, RR effects retrograde flow by causing a substantial decrease in forward flow volume while only moderately increasing RF volume. Thus, although RF increases with RR, the major driver is a fall in forward flow during each respiratory cycle. Analyzing cardiac output in our study did not identify variations in heart rate as a contributing factor to the described respiratory causes of Fontan flow reversal.

Baseline flow as a determinant of susceptibility to flow reversal

Our study found a positive association of baseline IVC flow rates in the Fontan circulation with flow reversal. Participants with lower baseline flow rate in the IVC showed elevated pulsatility and increased retrograde flow. Possibly, this can be attributed to flow inertia, as a higher mean flow needs greater energy induced by the transdiaphragmatic pressure gradients to reverse its direction in the IVC. Conversely, a participant with low mean IVC flow rates, potentially caused by impaired cardiac output, would be more susceptible to flow reversal, potentially exacerbating hemodynamic flow inefficiencies.

Hemodynamic efficiency rather than global output

Given that the heartbeat, rather than respiration, is the primary driver of mean systemic venous return in the Fontan circulation,9 it is expected that our study shows overall mean flow in the IVC and descending aorta remaining relatively consistent across different breathing patterns. Importantly, however, preservation of mean flow does not imply physiological equivalence across respiratory conditions. Our findings demonstrate that respiration can cause a substantial shift in blood volume, particularly in the retrograde direction without altering net systemic output.

This respiration-driven shift in flow directionality enhances venous pulsatility and turbulence, which reduces flow efficiency in this already low-energy-flow circulation21,22 and potentially elevates the thromboembolic risk.23,24 Moreover, the augmented retrograde and peak flow may amplify the strain on abdominal organ tissue, such as the liver, caused by increased shear forces and peak pressures, which has been demonstrated in the setting of Fontan flow reversal in a clinically validated in vitro model.25 Prior research also suggests that flow reversal and peak pressure are more pronounced in an upright body posture, which would exacerbate the hemodynamic findings of this work.25

The role of the respiratory pump in Fontan physiology

Although respiratory mechanics of increased breathing effort augment cardiac output in biventricular physiology, our findings suggest a different role of the respiratory pump in the Fontan circulation. Respiratory mechanics fail to increase effective forward flow and primarily modulate venous flow profiles, dissipating energy into retrograde flow and pulsatility. In the absence of a subpulmonary pump and valve, respiration-induced flow energy appears to be lost to venous inefficiency rather than translated into increased cardiac return. This mechanism may contribute to the well-described exercise intolerance observed in Fontan patients despite preserved or stable resting cardiac output.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. Although lateral tunnel and extracardiac Fontan anatomies were included, the study was not powered to assess anatomy-specific differences, and analyses were therefore performed on the combined cohort. Our participant population exhibits a certain degree of heterogeneity, which may have influenced the results. The validity of these findings cannot be generalized to the pediatric patient, for instance. Various measurements have demonstrated that for most tests, the instructions were adequately followed. However, instructions are always subject to interpretation, particularly regarding the depth of respiration. Hence, we could not completely control respiratory effort, which might have impacted results. The study was conducted in the supine position, which could affect cardiopulmonary interactions and therefore affect results. In addition, although respiratory maneuvers altered venous flow profiles, the net effects on gas exchange, ventilatory efficiency, symptoms, and long-term outcomes were not assessed.

Real-time 2D phase-contrast MRI is inherently limited to a single imaging plane per acquisition. Given the central role of venous congestion and impaired venous return in Fontan pathophysiology—particularly with respect to abdominal organ hemodynamics and Fontan-associated liver disease—we therefore prioritized assessment in a plane perpendicular to the IVC and descending aorta. Acquiring additional planes to measure superior vena cava (SVC) flow, total cardiac output, and aortopulmonary collateral flow would require separate sequential scans and would therefore prevent simultaneous measurements of flow in those vessels during identical respiratory cycles and breathing maneuvers. Importantly, although hyperventilation-induced hypocapnia may influence cerebral vasomotor tone and potentially affect SVC return, existing literature reports inconsistent respiratory modulation of SVC flow in Fontan patients16 and generally lower respiratory-induced variability in the SVC compared with the IVC.15,26 Future studies employing 4D flow MRI may overcome the current technical constraints of 2D acquisitions and enable comprehensive, simultaneous assessment of the Fontan circulation during controlled respiratory maneuvers.PerspectivesCLINICAL COMPETENCIES IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: In patients with a single ventricle, inferior vena cava flow reversal is ubiquitous and modulated by respiratory mechanics. CLINICAL COMPETENCIES IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: In patients with a single ventricle, inferior vena cava flow reversal is ubiquitous and modulated by respiratory mechanics. Increases in respiratory effort and rate alter venous return through various mechanisms, thereby exacerbating flow reversal. However, systemic output remains unaffected, suggesting an impairment in hemodynamic efficiency rather than a primary reduction in cardiac output. The implementation of a dedicated breathing protocol during follow-up magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) allows for in-depth investigation and a comprehensive understanding of patient-specific hemodynamics.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: The translational implications of these findings extend across biological, clinical, and interventional domains. First, the marked respiration-dependent increases in retrograde flow and pulsatility provide a plausible hemodynamic mechanism linking abnormal venous flow to end-organ injury, particularly Fontan-associated liver disease. Future studies integrating respiration-resolved flow waveforms derived from clinical imaging into liver-on-chip platforms may enable systematic investigation of how oscillatory shear stress, flow reversal, and pressure transients drive endothelial dysfunction, stellate cell activation, and fibrosis.

Second, the present study was cross-sectional and conducted among clinically stable Fontan patients; therefore, it was not designed to establish prognostic associations. Longitudinal investigations in participants with progressive Fontan failure, advancing hepatic disease, or declining exercise capacity are needed to determine whether exaggerated retrograde flow or pulsatility precedes clinical deterioration and may serve as an early marker of hemodynamic decompensation.

Finally, these findings have important implications for intervention testing and device development. Breathing-based interventions should be evaluated not solely by their effects on cardiac output, but by their impact on venous flow efficiency, directionality, and pulsatility. Notably, although prior work has proposed increasing respiratory effort as a therapeutic strategy, our data suggest that shallow and slow breathing—at least in the supine position—may be more favorable.27 Moreover, realistic respiration-dependent venous flow conditions should always be incorporated into preclinical testing of Fontan assist devices. Prior concepts, such as valve-based strategies, may warrant renewed consideration but must be balanced against the heightened thrombotic risk inherent to low-pressure venous circuits. Integrating high-fidelity clinical flow measurements with benchtop and computational models will be essential to guide safe and effective device design.

From a clinical standpoint, determination of risk factors for failing Fontan and early identification of hemodynamic aggravation before symptoms occur is of critical importance to improve participant outcomes. Cardiopulmonary interactions, especially venous return, are likely one of the major determinants. Currently, cardiac-gated MRI to measure inferior vena cava flow is part of the continuous follow-up and management of Fontan patients. Our findings suggest that incorporating venous flow analysis, which enables evaluation of the entire respiratory cycle, into routine MRI follow-up may provide complementary insights into cardiorespiratory coupling and venous inefficiency. Longitudinal analysis of retrograde flow or pulsatility could therefore help identify risk factors for increased venous congestion causing tissue and end-organ damage.
