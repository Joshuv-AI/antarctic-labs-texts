Plasma metagenomic cfDNA sequencing identifies pathogens in culture-negative sepsis following urinary pouch rupture
Authors: Katharina Hoeter, Linda Marriott, Elmo W I Neuberger, Urantschimeg Dagwadordsch, Radhe Shantha Kumar, Perikles Simon, Marc Bodenstein, Maïwenn Kersaudy-Kerhoas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryA patient with a complex urological history presented with abdominal pain and respiratory distress after catheter dysfunction. She underwent emergency surgery for a ruptured urinary pouch. Sepsis was later diagnosed based on clinical deterioration, including tachycardia, fever, an elevated respiratory rate and raised inflammatory markers, but blood cultures remained negative. A metagenomic microbial cell-free DNA (cfDNA) assay (iSEP-SEQ), performed early from plasma as part of a research protocol, identified Klebsiella and Pseudomonas at the genus level. Results were obtained retrospectively and were not available in real time; therefore, they did not alter immediate management. These findings were confirmed by cultures from drainage fluid and urine. Broad-spectrum antibiotic treatment led to clinical improvement. This case highlights the limitations of conventional microbiological methods in culture-negative sepsis and illustrates the role of cfDNA-based metagenomic testing as an adjunctive and complementary diagnostic tool for early, accurate pathogen detection. Early use of such tools may support timely and targeted management in complex infectious disease presentations.

Background

Sepsis is a life-threatening syndrome caused by the immune system’s dysregulated response to infection, leading to organ damage, septic shock, and death. The condition is characterised by an increase of two or more points in the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score.1 Globally, sepsis accounts for approximately 20% of deaths, as highlighted by the WHO Sepsis Report 2017.2

Current guidelines recommend immediate empirical broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy following blood culture collection, with transition to pathogen-specific de-escalation based on microbiological results.1

However, conventional culture techniques often require 2–7 days for pathogen identification3 and may yield false negatives in 40%–60% of cases with suspected sepsis, particularly in patients with prior antibiotic exposure or complex infections.35 This hampers the initiation of targeted therapy and contributes to the growing threat of antimicrobial resistance (AMR).

Advances in molecular diagnostics, such as plasma metagenomics microbial cell-free DNA (mcfDNA) sequencing, offer a novel approach to early pathogen detection. This ‘liquid biopsy’ technology can identify a broad range of pathogens directly from blood samples, including atypical organisms, and may provide clinically actionable results even when standard cultures are negative.6 7

This case illustrates its potential clinical utility.

Case presentation

A woman in her early 30s under regular urological follow-up presented directly to a urology outpatient clinic of a tertiary care hospital in Germany, with severe lower abdominal pain and progressive dyspnoea following catheter dysfunction. Her relevant medical history included a lumbosacral meningomyelocele, which required multiple prior neurosurgical procedures, including spinal untethering, placement of a ventriculoperitoneal shunt and posterior spinal stabilisation. She had also developed low bladder compliance, which was managed with ileocaecal augmentation and an appendix stoma.

During follow-up, she developed transitional cell carcinoma of the augmented bladder, which progressed to keratinising squamous cell carcinoma. This was managed with radical cystectomy and conversion to a continent urinary diversion (MAINZ pouch I, a continent ileocaecal urinary reservoir with catheterisable stoma), with preservation of the appendix stoma. She was monitored through regular oncological evaluations and managed bladder emptying via clean intermittent catheterisation.

In the weeks prior to admission, the patient reported increasing difficulty with catheterisation. A pouchoscopy revealed mucosal swelling at the stoma site, and a catheter was placed for continuous drainage for 3 weeks without immediate complications.

The night before presentation, catheter function deteriorated, with reduced urine output followed by acute respiratory symptoms. On admission, she showed signs of cardiopulmonary decompensation, and emergency computer tomography (CT) demonstrated a large rupture of the urinary reservoir. Emergency lower abdominal laparotomy confirmed an iatrogenic pouch rupture, which was surgically repaired. A catheter fixation device (Pouchofix, Teleflex) and a nasogastric tube were placed. Postoperatively, the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU). On admission, she was alert (Glasgow Coma Scale score 14), with pupils equal and reactive, tachycardia (120 beats per minute), systolic blood pressure of 110 mm Hg, and oxygen saturation of 98% on 3 L/min oxygen via nasal cannula. The abdomen was soft and moderately distended without peritonism; surgical drains showed minor haemorrhagic output. Piperacillin–tazobactam was initiated intraoperatively and continued postoperatively.

On Day 2 of ICU admission, due to ongoing clinical instability, antimicrobial therapy was escalated to meropenem and fluconazole. Shortly after administration, the patient developed pruritus, flushing, wheeze and agitation, consistent with an acute hypersensitivity reaction; therapy was discontinued and managed with antihistamines (dimetindene, cimetidine) and corticosteroids (methylprednisolone), with rapid symptom improvement. Reintroduction of antimicrobial therapie triggered a second hypersensitivity reaction, prompting a switch to ceftriaxone, which was well tolerated and continued as definitive therapy. The subsequent ICU course (Days 2–9) was characterised by persistent tachycardia, fluctuating infection markers, evolving pulmonary findings, and negative blood cultures. Investigations

Imaging studies

Contrast-enhanced abdominal CT was performed on admission. During the excretory phase, following retrograde contrast instillation via an indwelling pouch catheter, significant contrast leakage into the abdominal cavity was identified, originating from a defect in the left laterodorsal aspect of the urinary pouch. Free intraperitoneal fluid was detected in the perisplenic, perihepatic and pelvic spaces.

CT of the chest excluded pulmonary embolism and right heart strain.

Abdominal ultrasound confirmed a small volume of free fluid in the pelvic cavity (Koller pouch) with no further abnormalities.

Supine chest radiography demonstrated an enlarged cardiac silhouette and a left lower lobe consolidation with a positive bronchopneumogram, consistent with pneumonia, as well as a right-sided pleural effusion.

Transthoracic echocardiography showed a volume-depleted state with kissing ventricles and a collapsible inferior vena cava, supporting the need for aggressive fluid management.

Continuous physiological monitoring was maintained throughout the patient’s ICU stay, including 5-lead electrocardiography, invasive and non-invasive blood pressure, respiratory rate and body temperature. These parameters are summarised infigures2 3.

Serum β-d-glucan levels measured on Days 2 and 6 were within borderline or non-diagnostic ranges.

Metagenomic cfDNA Analysis

Following the clinical diagnosis of sepsis on Day 3 (see the Definition box), plasma was collected for research-based metagenomic analysis with informed consent (approved by the regional ethics committee; study ID 2020–15535). Plasma was processed through a standardised protocol: centrifugation, cryopreservation and shipment on dry ice to a research facility in the United Kingdom (UK). The plasma sample was processed using a proprietary microfluidic extraction method and sequenced on the MinION Mk1C platform (Oxford Nanopore Technologies) for 2 hours. Details of this approach (iSEP-SEQ) are available in DOI: 10.1101/2024.12.29.24319315. Bioinformatic analysis using the EPI2ME WIMP workflow identified Klebsiella (160 reads per million) and Pseudomonas (53 reads per million) at the genus level. All other classifications were below interpretative thresholds (<30 RPM), consistent with background signal or contamination. The complete workflow took less than 6 hours. Results were not available in real time but were obtained blindly, shared with the clinical team, and were later found to correlate with culture results from drainage fluid and urine samples.

Interpretation and Limitations

While standard blood cultures remained negative, cfDNA metagenomic sequencing yielded early identification of Klebsiella and Pseudomonas, later confirmed by conventional cultures of the abdominal drain fluid (table 3). This case highlights both the diagnostic potential of cfDNA-based sequencing and the current limitations related to real-time availability and clinical integration.

Differential diagnosis

Differential diagnoses considered during the ICU course included hypovolaemia, postoperative pain-related respiratory compromise, pulmonary embolism, antibiotic-induced hypersensitivity reactions, and sepsis.

Initial tachycardia and echocardiographic findings of kissing ventricles and a collapsible inferior vena cava supported hypovolaemia and prompted aggressive fluid resuscitation. Partial heart rate improvement suggested volume responsiveness, but persistent tachycardia and respiratory distress indicated an additional process.

Pulmonary embolism was excluded by contrast-enhanced chest CT and normal D-dimer levels. A temporal association between β-lactam administration and respiratory symptoms, with rapid resolution after withdrawal, supported antibiotic-induced hypersensitivity.

Despite these considerations, progressive organ dysfunction, rising inflammatory markers, hyperlactataemia and a ∆SOFA ≥ 2 fulfilled Sepsis-3 criteria, leading to the clinical diagnosis of sepsis despite persistently negative blood cultures.

Outcome and follow-up

Following her transfer from the ICU, the patient underwent early mobilisation and respiratory therapy as part of standard postintensive care rehabilitation. The surgical wound healed per primam intentionem, and drainage was removed as planned.

During the remainder of her hospital stay, the patient remained afebrile, and inflammatory markers declined steadily. She completed a 7-day course of intravenous ceftriaxone without adverse effects. She was discharged on Day 14, in stable condition, with ongoing urinary drainage via the Pouchofix system and a catheter.

At 4-week follow-up, the patient reported no symptoms of infection or complications. Laboratory parameters, including white cell count and C-reactive protein, had normalised. The surgical site remained fully healed, and she had resumed most activities of daily living.

The patient remains under regular outpatient surveillance. At the latest follow-up (15 months after discharge), she was alive, clinically stable and had experienced no recurrence of infection or hospital readmission.

Discussion

This case underscores the diagnostic and therapeutic complexity of sepsis in patients with altered anatomy following complex urological reconstruction. Structural modifications, such as continent urinary diversions, complicate physiological drainage, sample acquisition and localisation of infection and may delay diagnosis. Similar diagnostic complexities and the need for early imaging in patients with complicated urinary diversions support the findings of this case.8 9 Urinary conduit (pouch) rupture should be suspected in patients presenting with acute abdominal pain, reduced catheter output or systemic decompensation, and early imaging is critical for timely surgical management.

While structural complications may be identified promptly with imaging, microbiological confirmation of sepsis-causing pathogens is frequently delayed despite early sampling and empirical therapy.10 11 According to Sepsis-3 guidelines, early empirical antibiotic therapy should be initiated within the first hour of recognition, with de-escalation guided by microbiological data.1 In practice, delayed or absent pathogen identification frequently limits timely de-escalation, as illustrated in this case.

Initial treatment focused on volume resuscitation and empiric antimicrobial therapy with piperacillin–tazobactam. Clinical deterioration prompted escalation to meropenem and fluconazole, while hypersensitivity reactions necessitated an urgent switch to ceftriaxone.12 Despite ongoing clinical deterioration, standard microbiological diagnostics remained negative for the first 7 days. This is not uncommon in urinary tract infections.13 14 Subsequent identification of Klebsiella oxytoca and Pseudomonas aeruginosa in drainage fluid occurred only after clinical improvement, rendering culture results confirmatory rather than actionable.

A metagenomic microbial cfDNA assay performed on Day 3 samples detected both Klebsiella and Pseudomonas several days before culture confirmation on drainage fluid. However, as the test was not part of routine clinical diagnostics, results were not used to guide therapy. Published data suggest that plasma metagenomic cfDNA assays can achieve sensitivities of approximately 75%–90% with analytical specificities >99% and clinical specificities of 62%–97% depending on the interpretative thresholds applied and the specific context.1518 Plasma cfDNA sequencing has demonstrated superior detection of Gram-negative pathogens compared with conventional blood cultures, including Enterobacterales and Pseudomonas.19 In a recent prospective study, plasma cfDNA mNGS detected 78% of Gram-negative rods in bloodstream infections, surpassing traditional culture performance.20 Several medical case reports have reported the use of cfDNA sequencing for the detection of pathogens. Table 4 provides an overview of cases in which cfDNA sequencing revealed pathogens not identified by initial culture-based methods.2125 Our case complements other published reports to support the notion that metagenomics could improve outcomes by enabling earlier targeted therapy and subsequent de-escalation and reduction of antimicrobial exposure. Klebsiella and Pseudomonas are common Gram-negative uropathogens, and their detection by cfDNA is biologically expected in severe postoperative infection. This case is distinguished from previous reports by the use of real-time nanopore sequencing for pathogen detection directly from cfDNA extracted by a novel sample preparation platform, CNASafe. Most existing cases have relied on large-scale laboratory infrastructure, whereas this approach demonstrates the feasibility of decentralised diagnostics. In addition, this case uniquely demonstrates the detection of uropathogens from plasma cfDNA sequencing.

Compared with standard cultures, cfDNA sequencing offers earlier, culture-independent pathogen detection, but cannot distinguish viable from non-viable organisms and does not reliably localise the site of infection. In addition, limitations of cfDNA sequencing include susceptibility to contamination, variable reporting thresholds and heterogeneity in laboratory workflows and bioinformatic pipelines.26 27 Accordingly, cfDNA assays should be regarded as complementary to conventional diagnostics in diagnostically complex or culture-negative cases. The metagenomic cfDNA assay used in this case was performed within a research framework. The Oxford Nanopore Technologies MinION Mk1C platform was selected for its on-demand sequencing capability and potential applicability in near-patient settings. Although the complete analytical workflow required less than 6 hours, results were not available in real time and did not inform clinical decision-making.

It should be noted that the Oxford Nanopore workflow offers technical capability for concurrent AMR gene detection.28 While our analysis focused on species-level identification, incorporating resistance profiling would provide a more comprehensive diagnostic tool. This is particularly relevant in regions with higher prevalence of AmpC or carbapenemase-mediated resistance,29 30 expanding the potential for rapid pathogen and resistance profiling in critical settings.

Clinical improvement was multifactorial, driven by surgical source control, empirical antimicrobial therapy and supportive intensive care. The cfDNA findings correlated with abdominal drainage fluid and urine culture results, which identified causative pathogens. The added value of this case lies in the diagnostic context and timing: cfDNA sequencing was applied in a patient with complex postoperative anatomy and persistently negative blood cultures, with pathogen detection preceding culture confirmation by several days.

Current European Association of Urology guidelines recommend empirical broad-spectrum therapy for urosepsis with subsequent de-escalation based on microbiological results.31 This case illustrates the diagnostic gap created when culture results are delayed or absent. These developments align with broader public health initiatives, such as the UK metagenomic pathogen surveillance programme.26 Until supported by prospective validation studies and adopted into clinical guidelines, metagenomic cfDNA assays should be regarded as valuable adjuncts rather than replacements for conventional diagnostics in sepsis care.

Our case complements other published reports describing delayed pathogen detection in clinically altered patients and supports the notion that molecular diagnostics could improve outcomes by enabling earlier targeted therapy and subsequent de-escalation and reduction of antimicrobial exposure.
