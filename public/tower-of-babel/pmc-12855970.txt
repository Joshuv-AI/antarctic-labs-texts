Highly efficient heterogeneous thermal catalysis for noble-metal-free hydrogen production from formic acid
Authors: Liang Qiu, Lin Yao, Ping Wang, Zhiwei Jiang, Jinglin Li, Ying Li, Yixin Li, Zhaosong Wu, Muhammad Salman Nasir, Ding Wang, Xinqiang Wang, Zhen Huang, Baowen Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Hydrogen extraction from liquid hydrogen carriers is a promising strategy to address hydrogen storage and transportation challenges for a hydrogen economy. We report a novel heterogeneous catalytic architecture, Ni(Mn)-O-P/GaN nanowires, for efficient, selective, and ultra-stable hydrogen evolution from formic acid (FA). The catalyst achieves a high activity of 29.92 mol H2·gcat−1·h−1 with nearly 100% selectivity and a high turnover frequency (TOF) of 31,019.2 h−1 at 150 °C. It exhibits exceptional stability over 4000 hours under fluctuated temperatures (55-75 °C) with a turnover numbers (TONs) of 5,023,060, integrable with low-grade industrial waste heat. In-situ characterizations, isotope experiments, and density functional theory calculations collectively reveal that the synergy between Ni(Mn)-O-P and GaN are favorable for the O-H dissociation of FA with an interesting H-exchange mechanism with H2O while inhibiting the undesired FA dehydration and coking formation. An industrial prototype test validates practical on-demand hydrogen production using waste heat.

Introduction

Green hydrogen (H2) is emerged as a disruptive renewable energies carrier owing to its high energy density (142 MJ/kg) and zero emission1. However, the unique physicochemical properties of H2, such as low boiling point (−253 °C), low molecular weight (2 g/mol), and inflammability, pose insurmountable challenges for its storage, transportation, and distribution2. Conventional gas compression and cryogenic liquefaction methods can’t meet the massive demand for H2 from different economic sectors, remaining the bottleneck of the eventual success in establishing an H2 economy3. Liquid hydrogen carriers (LHCs) present a revolutionary strategy to address the issues above by employing well-established infrastructures of liquid fuels4,5. Critically, the thermal catalysis of on-demand hydrogen extraction from LHCs shows great promise for global decarbonization by synergizing industrial waste heat (which accounts for nearly 60% of the total global energy consumption) with renewable energies.

Among a variety of LHCs, formic acid (FA) has attracted global attention due to its non-toxicity, low flammability, and high hydrogen density6,7. Moreover, FA can be produced from CO2 hydrogenation or partial oxidation of biomass, thus promising a sustainable loop for green H28–11. Since the pioneering study in the 1960s12, homogeneous catalysts have been extensively explored for FA dehydrogenation with high activity, some of which even delivered high turnover numbers (TONs) of over a million13–15. However, organic solvents or base additives are often indispensable to accelerate the reaction kinetics, which increases the complexity and cost of the system16. Meanwhile, homogeneous catalysts are not industrially conducive to producing H2 from FA owing to their vulnerability and limited reusability. In stark contrast, heterogeneous catalysis holds grand promise for practically producing H2 from FA owing to its superior reusability, robustness, and scalability17,18. Thus far, a number of supported metals heterogeneous catalysts, including single atom catalysts19–21, metal nanoparticles (NPs)22–25, a combination of single atoms and clusters26, and multi-metallic alloy27,28 have been used for FA decomposition. However, although some studies have reported the use of earth-rich metals as catalysts in both homogeneous and heterogeneous systems29, H2 production from FA is highly dependent on noble metals, which are limited by their high cost and low abundance13,30. Meanwhile, noble metals are easily deactivated because of their CO sensitivity and severe coke formation during thermal catalysis of LHCs31–34. In addition, the undesired dehydration reaction would limit the practical application of thermal catalysis of FA decomposition due to the resultant toxic impurities (HCOOH → CO↑+ H2O)35,36. In this context, it is imperative to explore a noble-metal-free heterogeneous catalytic architecture for highly efficient, selective, and stable FA decomposition into H2. The pursuit of an ideal solid support to work in synergy with rational catalytic centers is critical yet remains a grand challenge.

1-dimensional (1D) GaN nanowires (NWs) are an emerging support for assembling a new-generation heterogeneous catalytic architecture because of the unique geometric and electronic properties37,38. First, the well-defined 1D morphology with ultrahigh surface area is geometrically favorable for efficient exposure of active sites. Second, the surface of GaN with polar variability can be precisely engineered at the atomic level to tailor chemical reactions by cooperating with appropriate co-catalysts39,40. In this study, an unprecedented heterogeneous catalytic architecture was proposed for thermal catalysis of FA decomposition by rationally coupling Ni(Mn)–O–P with GaN NWs (Fig. 1). The as-prepared heterogeneous catalyst shows a considerable activity of 29.92 mol H2·gcat−1 · h−1 with a high selectivity of nearly 100% from FA decomposition under 150 °C. Benefitting from the high atom efficiency of the catalyst, a high turnover frequency (TOF) of 31019.2 h−1 was obtained for Ni(Mn)–O–P/GaN NWs. Of note, the catalyst exhibits an ultra-long-term operation over 4000 hours with high TONs of 5,023,060 mole H2 under fluctuating temperatures ranging from 55 to 75 °C. It thus shows desired flexibility to utilize different industrial waste heat for H2 extraction from FA on demand, which is verified by an industrial-scale pilot system in the scenario of steam power generation. As studied by the comprehensive in situ spectroscopic characterizations, isotope labeling experiments, and density functional theory (DFT) calculations, the integrated Ni(Mn)-O-P/GaN NWs is beneficial for water-assisted activation of the O–H bond of FA to lower the energy barrier of FA dehydrogenation through an interesting H-exchange mechanism (HCOO-HFA + HO-Hwater ⇋ HCOO-Hwater + HO-HFA) while suppressing the undesired FA dehydration (HCOOH → H2O + CO↑), thus simultaneously enhancing the activity and selectivity. Furthermore, the introduction of Ni(Mn)-O-P over GaN NWs imparts the catalyst with the distinctive CO-insensitive and anti-coking properties, thus resulting in superior stability. Overall, this work illustrates the scientific and technical feasibility of on-demand H2 extraction from FA powered by low-grade industrial waste heat, showing significant potential for employing LHCs to address the growing demand for green H2 across multiple industrial sectors.Fig. 1Schematic diagram of the research strategy.Scheme of heterogeneous catalytic H2 production from FA decomposition over Ni(Mn)-O-P/GaN NWs powered by industrial waste heat.

Results

Materials growth and characterizations

The epitaxial GaN NWs were employed as scaffolds to assemble a new heterogeneous catalytic architecture for H2 evolution from FA by coupling with Mn-doped Ni-O-P (Please see “Methods” Section and Figs. S1–S3 in Supplementary Information). As characterized by scanning electron microscope (SEM), GaN NWs exhibited lengths ranging from 500 to 700 nm with diameters in the range of 50 to 80 nm (Fig. S4). The 1D morphology of GaN NWs can maximize the number of catalytic sites. As seen from Figs. 2a, S5, the decoration of Ni(Mn)-O-P nanoclusters did not alter the overall morphology of 1D NWs. High-Angle Annular Dark-Field Scanning Transmission Electron Microscopy (HAADF-STEM) image (Fig. S6) showed that Ni(Mn)-O-P species were randomly distributed on the GaN NWs surface with a diameter ranging from 27.4–92.9 nm. High-Resolution Scanning Transmission Electron Microscopy (HR-STEM) image (Fig. 2b) showed the lattice spacing of 0.26 nm, which was attributed to the (002) plane of GaN41,42, indicating the growth direction of the nanowires along the c-axis. It is consistent with the X-ray Diffraction (XRD) patterns (Fig. S7) and Raman spectra43 (Fig. S8). The lattice spacing of 0.24 nm and 0.206 nm could be indexed to the (111) plane of NiO and the (212) plane of NiP. However, no XRD diffraction peaks of Ni(Mn)-O-P were observed, possibly due to its low loading content (0.23 μmol·cm−2 as measured by Inductively Coupled Plasma Atomic Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-AES)). Energy-dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) mapping images (Figs. 2c, S9) and Time of Flight Secondary Ion Mass Spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) (Fig. 2d, e) further confirmed the success in anchoring Ni(Mn)-O-P nanoclusters onto the GaN NWs surface. The assembled Ni(Mn)-O-P was further investigated by X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) characterizations (Fig. S10). A positive binding energy shift of 0.23 eV was observed for Ga 2p over Ni(Mn)-O-P/GaN NWs compared to pristine GaN NWs (Fig. S11b). Additionally, the N 1s peak of pristine GaN at 397.26 eV is shifted to 397.56 eV upon the decoration of Ni(Mn)-O-P (Fig. S11c). These results evidenced the electronic interaction between GaN NWs and Ni(Mn)-O-P. The existence of Ni-O-P was further validated with a clear indicator of Ni-O, Ni-P and P-O interaction (Figs. 2f, S11d, Table S1). Of note, the doping of Mn leads to a substantial decrease in the binding energy of Ni and O. It is indicative of the electron redistribution from the doped Mn sites to the Ni and O sites (Fig. 2f, g), which significantly affects FA decomposition as studied below.

The catalytic activity of formic acid decomposition

The reaction was conducted in a sealed stainless temperature-programmed chamber under atmospheric argon (Fig. S12). 1 mL of neat FA ( > 99.9%) was used as the feedstock if not specifically noted. As seen from Figs. 3a, S13, the influence of co-catalysts on FA dehydrogenation is significant. Particularly, the H2 activity of Ni-O/GaN NWs reaches 0.88 mol·gcat−1 · h−1. The introduction of phosphorus can further improve the activity up to 3.20 mol·gcat−1 · h−1, even surpassing some noble metals tested (Table S2, Figs. S14, S15). More interestingly, the doping of secondary transition metals, i.e., Cr, Fe, Cr, Mn further enables a significant enhancement in the activity of FA dehydrogenation. Among them, Ni(Mn)-O-P/GaN NWs deliver an optimal activity of 11.55 mol·gcat−1 · h−1, which is nearly 2 orders of magnitude higher than that of the commercial Pt/C catalyst (Fig. S16). The activity is closely related to the doping amount of Mn in the Ni(Mn)-O-P, showing a volcano trend as the Mn content increases (Fig. 3b). Compared with pure GaN NWs, the selectivity of H2 was sharply improved from 49% up to 99% by loading Ni(Mn)-O-P nanoclusters (Fig. 3c). Without GaN, Ni(Mn)-O-P was nearly inactive for FA decomposition, suggesting the critical synergy of GaN with Ni(Mn)-O-P in H2 production from FA (Fig. S17). More interestingly, compared to neat FA decomposition, the introduction of water (1 mL HCOOH + 200 μL H2O) resulted in an observable enhancement in H2 activity with a high value of 29.92 mol·gcat−1 · h−1 (Figs. 3d, S18), the underlying cause will be studied below. Of note, CO2 was produced from formic acid dehydrogenation at a stoichiometric H2/CO2 ratio of 1/1. This finding indicates negligible formic acid dehydration (Fig. S19). The temperature-dependent activity of Ni(Mn)-O-P/GaN NWs for water-assisted FA decomposition was next studied (Fig. 3e). The reaction can readily occur under 60 °C with H2 activity of 3.79 mol·gcat−1 · h−1, which is superior to most of the reported catalysts under comparable conditions (Table S3, Fig. 3f). A considerable TOF of 3,932.68 h−1 was obtained due to the combined effect of high intrinsic activity and high atom efficiency of the catalyst. As expected, the activity was rapidly enhanced by increasing the reaction temperature, reaching 29.92 mol·gcat−1·h−1 with a high TOF of 31,019.2 h−1 at 150 °C. Long-term stability was tested to assess the viability of using Ni(Mn)-O-P/GaN NWs for heterogeneous catalysis of H2 production from FA over fluctuating temperatures. As shown in Fig. 3g, the optimized Ni(Mn)-O-P/GaN NWs demonstrated an exceptional stability of over 4000 h with benchmark TONs of 5,023,060 mole H2. After the 4000 h test, the hydrogen activity only decreased by ~10% (Fig. S20). The Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM) images and XPS spectra show that the morphological and valence state of the nanoclusters remain stable and do not undergo significant change during the reaction. (Fig. S21). In sharp contrast, noble metals such as Ir, Pd, and Pt showed obvious activity degradation after 200 h of operation under identical conditions (Fig. S22). Herein, the exceptional stability of Ni(Mn)-O-P/GaN NWs can be attributed to their CO insensitivity and superior anti-coking property as revealed by spectroscopic characterizations and density functional theory calculations below.

Reaction mechanism

To unravel the reaction mechanism at the molecular level, in situ Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR), and electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) characterizations were collectively carried out to investigate the key intermediates of FA decomposition. As shown in Fig. S23, the in situ FTIR characteristic peaks of HCOO* at 1363 cm−1, 1585 cm−1, and 2940 cm−1 were observed for both Ni(Mn)-O-P/GaN NWs and bare GaN NWs, which results from the O–H dissociation of FA (HCOOH → HCOO* + *H)44–48. It should be noted that the accumulation rate of HCOO* over Ni(Mn)-O-P/GaN far outperforms that over pure GaN (Fig. S24), corroborating the synergistic effect between Ni(Mn)-O-P and GaN in promoting the O–H dissociation of FA, which aligns with the in situ EPR characterizations49,50 (Fig. 4a). Notably, upon the addition of water, in situ FTIR spectral shifts were observed during the reaction (Fig. 4b). Typically, the asymmetric stretching vibration of HCOO* in HCOOH/H2O system exhibited an obvious red shift, which is indicative of the water-assisted stabilization of the HCOO* intermediate arising from the O-H dissociation of HCOO-H51,52. By contrast, the carbonyl stretching band of HCOOH was shifted from 1742 cm−1 downward to 1780 cm−1 when water was introduced into neat FA, further suggesting the activation of HCOOH with H2O51,52. Meanwhile, as studied by Nuclear Magnetic Resonance Spectroscopy (NMR) characterizations, the adsorbed water was dissociated into H* + *OH (in a terminal hydroxyl configuration) over the Ni(Mn)-O-P/GaN interface53,54 (Fig. 4c). It is energetically favorable for FA dehydrogenation through an interesting H-exchange mechanism while inhibiting the undesired FA dehydration, which was verified by Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS) measurement (Figs. 4d, e, S25), both HD and D2 were produced from when DCOOD and H2O were used as feedstock. Meanwhile, trace amounts of DCOOD and DCOOH/HCOOD were detected in the mixture of HCOOH and D2O. These results validated the H exchange between FA and H2O (HCOO-HFA + HO-Hwater ⇋ HCOO-Hwater + HO-HFA). The kinetic isotope effect (KIE) was then studied. As shown in Fig. 4f, the KIE ratios of (HCOOH + H2O)/(DCOOD + H2O) and (HCOOH + H2O)/(HCOOH + D2O) are greater than 1, indicating that H2O can promote the dehydrogenation of FA in the kinetics. Temperature-programmed H2 desorption (TPD) characterizations were further conducted to gain more insights into the reaction mechanism. As demonstrated in Fig. 4g, h, the desorption temperature of H2 can be reduced from 289 °C over bare GaN NWs downward to 279 °C over Ni(Mn)-O-P/GaN NWs. It is indicative of the readily desorption of H2, which is favorable for H2 extraction from FA over the Ni(Mn)-O-P/GaN interface. Meanwhile, the decorated Ni(Mn)-O-P facilitates the desorption of CO from the GaN surface, which is a clear indicator of the CO-insensitivity property of the catalyst, thus facilitating stability. Moreover, during the long-term heterogeneous catalysis of H2 extraction from FA, no carbon species were obviously detected on the surface of Ni(Mn)-O-P/GaN NWs after stability testing, as characterized by the Raman spectroscopy technique (Fig. 4i). In contrast, the progressive changes in the C 1s spectra (Figure S26), combined with Raman spectroscopy (Fig. 4i), demonstrate the evolution of coking over Pd/GaN NWs after 200 h of thermal catalysis of FA decomposition. The results above reveal the superiority of Ni(Mn)-O-P/GaN NWs for highly efficient and ultra-stable H2 production from FA.

Theoretical calculations

DFT calculations were performed to study the origin of the outstanding activity, selectivity, and stability (Fig. S27). The electronic properties of Ni(Mn)-O-P/GaN are first elucidated. As indicated by charge transfer analysis (Figs. 5a, S28), Ni(Mn)-O-P clusters deliver 0.623 e to GaN, which is much less than that Ni-O and Ni-O-P and the presented pattern is consistent with the XPS results (Fig. 2f). Density of states (DOS) calculations revealed that the introduction of phosphorus and manganese helps to increase the electron occupation near the Fermi level, and the significant interfacial charge redistribution was mediated through the Ni 3d orbitals (Figs. 5b,S29). Meanwhile, the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) orbitals on the Ni(Mn)-O-P surface are concentrated near Mn atoms, suggesting that Mn sites are most likely active sites for efficient FA dehydrogenation (Fig. S30). DFT calculations were further conducted to gain more insights into water-assisted FA decomposition at the molecular level. The OH adsorption energies over different catalytic interfaces were first calculated (Fig. S31). As shown in Fig. 5c, Ni(Mn)-O-P/GaN exhibits stronger *OH affinity at POx moieties with a relatively high ΔEads of −4.19 eV compared to Ni-O-P/GaN (−1.92 eV). The oxygen center in the adsorbed OH on Ni(Mn)-O-P/GaN shows a reduced charge compared to the Ni-O-P/GaN counterpart, indicating the introduction of Mn has enhanced the electronegativity of the nanoparticles. (Fig. S32). Furthermore, compared to Ni-O/GaN and Ni-O-P/GaN, HCOOH is thermodynamically favored to be absorbed on Ni(Mn)-O-P*OH/GaN (Fig. S33). It is noteworthy that the O–H bond of HCOOH is extended over Ni(Mn)-O-P while the C–O bond is shortened, indicating the activation of the O–H bond as well as the stabilization of C–O bond (Table S4). Hence, the Ni(Mn)-O-P/GaN interface facilitates the selective FA dehydrogenation into H2 while inhibiting the undesired dehydration.

The dehydrogenation step of FA decomposition over Ni-O/GaN, Ni-O-P/GaN and Ni(Mn)-O-P/GaN was then calculated (Figs. 5d, S34–S36). It was discovered that, for Ni-O/GaN, the O–H cleavage of HCOO-H (HCOOH → HCOO* + *H) was identified to be RDS in the intermediate (IM1) with a high energy barrier of 2.56 eV, which can be significantly lowered to 0.96 eV by doping with phosphorous. More critically, upon the incorporation of Mn in Ni-O–P, the detached H atoms readily migrated to the Mn sites with a further reduced energy barrier of 0.89 eV. It is thus rationalized that Ni(Mn)-O-P works in synergy with GaN to promote the O–H dissociation of HCOOH, which is in excellent agreement with in situ FTIR and EPR spectroscopic characterizations above. Consequently, the RDS was switched from the O–H dissociation over Ni-O/GaN into H* + H* → *H2 over Ni(Mn)-O-P/GaN with a significantly lowered energy barrier of 1.12 eV. For comparison, the energy state of *HCOOH → HCO* + *OH, a key step in FA dehydration, was also investigated. As seen from Figs. 5e, S37, the energy barrier of *HCOOH → HCO* + *OH at the Ni(Mn)-O-P/GaN interface is as high as 2.02 eV. It is much higher than that of *HCOOH → HCOO* + H* (0.89 eV), thus preventing the undesired FA dehydration. As a consequence, the synergy between Ni(Mn)-O-P and GaN not only enhances the activity by lowering the energy barrier of FA dehydrogenation but also improves the selectivity by inhibiting the undesired dehydration. Moreover, the key step of coking formation, i.e., *CO → *C conversion, is thermodynamically disfavored with an exceptionally high Gibbs free energy (ΔG) of 7.68 eV over the Ni(Mn)-O-P/GaN interface (Fig. 5f), suggesting the superior anti-coking property. The results above provide solid computational evidence to support the exceptional activity, selectivity, and stability of hydrogen production from FA over Ni(Mn)-O-P/GaN, which is well consistent with spectroscopic characterizations.

Industrial prototype test

To evaluate the eventual viability of using industrial waste heat to extract H2 from FA over Ni(Mn)-O-P/GaN NWs, an industrial-scale pilot system was designed for FA decomposition using simulated industrial exhaust gas by heating air (Fig. 6a). As shown in Fig. 6b, H2 activity can reach 3.06 mol·gcat−1·h−1 by purging with hot air at 60 °C. As calculated by the software of ChemSep, in the steam turbine power generation scenario, with the output of 13 kW of useful work, 24.3 kW of low-grade waste heat can be utilized for thermal catalysis of H2 production from FA. 47.52 kmol of H2 per hour can be achieved (equal to 95.42 kg/h). Discussion

In summary, a noble-metal-free heterogeneous catalytic architecture of Ni(Mn)-O-P/GaN NWs was explored for utilizing industrial waste heat to extract H2 from FA. The as-designed Ni(Mn)-O-P/GaN NWs demonstrated high TONs of 5,023,060 mole H2 per mole Ni(Mn)-O-P over a benchmark long-term operation of >4000 h under fluctuating temperatures. In situ spectroscopic characterizations, isotope labeling experiments, and density functional theory calculations collectively revealed that the synergy between Ni(Mn)-O-P and GaN energetically facilitates FA dehydrogenation through an interesting H-exchange mechanism with H2O at the molecular level. Meanwhile, it is disclosed that the excellent CO insensitivity and anti-coking property of Ni(Mn)-O-P/GaN NWs enabled the achievement of the exceptional stability, far outperforming state-of-the-art noble metals. An industrial-scale pilot system in the scenario of steam power generation, in combination with theoretical energy flow calculation, has demonstrated the technical feasibility of on-demand H2 extraction from FA using low-grade industrial waste heat. This study shows great promise of achieving carbon neutrality by synergizing industrial waste heat with renewable energies through an LHCs-mediated hydrogen storage system.

Methods

Assembly of GaN-supported metals

MBE growth of GaN onto a 4-inch Si wafer

GaN nanowires (NWs) were epitaxially synthesized on a 4-inch Si (111) substrate using a SVTA molecular beam epitaxy (MBE) system. The setup included a dual-filament Knudsen cell for the gallium source (Ga purity: 99.99999%), a standard Knudsen cell for magnesium (Mg purity: 99.9999%), and a Veeco Unibulb radio frequency (RF) nitrogen plasma source (N2 purity: 99.9999%). During deposition, the nitrogen plasma was maintained with a steady N2 flow rate of 1.0 sccm and an RF power of 400 W, yielding a GaN NW growth rate of 300 nm/h. All nanowires were fabricated under nitrogen-rich conditions, with a III/V ratio fixed at 0.2. Prior to growth, the silicon wafer was degassed in the chamber at 900 °C for 30 min. This was followed by a 60-s surface nitridation at 800 °C to form a thin SixNy layer that promotes subsequent GaN nucleation. An unintentionally doped n-type GaN NW layer was first deposited at 700 °C, after which Mg-doped p-GaN NWs were grown at 600 °C. To modulate the nanowire diameter during p-GaN deposition, the temperature of the Mg cell was systematically increased from 230 °C to 270 °C. The final structure consists of a p–n double-layer GaN nanowire array.

Deposition of metals

The deposition of metals onto 1D GaN was conducted in a sealed quartz chamber (0.44 L) under illumination by a 300 W Xenon lamp. GaN NWs supported by a Si wafer were placed at the bottom of the chamber. Characterization

XRD analysis was performed using a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer. The Ni(Mn)-O-P content on 1D GaN supported by Si was quantified through ICP-AES using an AGILENT 730 instrument. Raman spectra were recorded using a HORIBA LabRAM HR Evolution spectrometer (Japan) with a spectral range of 200–2100 nm and a focal length of 800 mm. XPS measurements were conducted on an ESCALAB 250xi system equipped with a non-monochromatic aluminum anode, with the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV serving as the calibration reference. STEM images were performed on a Thermo Fisher Scientific Talos F200X S/TEM system operating at 200 kV, which was also equipped with a Super-X EDS detector. TEM images were obtained using a JEOL 2100 F microscope. TPD measurements were carried out on a Quantachrome Autosorb-iQ-C chemisorption analyzer. FTIR spectroscopy was conducted on a PerkinElmer Frontier FTIR spectrometer equipped with an MCT detector and a detachable 10 cm gas cell. EPR spectroscopy was conducted on a Bruker A300 instrument (Germany), EPR spectras were obtained on solution samples (100 mM), and the quantitative results of free radicals were obtained using the standard DPPH. GC-MS measurements were obtained using TRACE 1300, ISQ 7000, Thermo Scientific. The NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker AVANCE III 400 WB spectrometer.

Computational methods

DFT calculations

Density functional theory (DFT)55 calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab Initio Simulation Package 6.1.0 (VASP 6.1.0)56,57. The interaction between valence electrons and ionic cores was treated with the projector-augmented wave (PAW) method58. Exchange-correlation effects within the Kohn-Sham framework59 were described using the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional under the generalized gradient approximation (GGA)60. A plane-wave basis set with a kinetic energy cutoff of 400 eV was used to expand the wavefunctions. For slab calculations, a 2 × 2 × 1 Gamma scheme k-point grid was employed to sample the Brillouin zone61. Geometry optimization was carried out with convergence thresholds set to 0.02 eV/Å for atomic forces and 10−4 eV for total energy.

Based on experimental identification of the m-plane of GaN, a GaN (100) surface was modeled using a 4 × 3 supercell consisting of four atomic layers to represent pristine GaN accurately. Ni-O/GaN, Ni-O-P/GaN, Ni(Mn)-O-P/GaN configurations were then generated by placing Ni6O9, Ni6O6P3, Ni5Mn1O6P3 clusters on the GaN (100) surface. To prevent interference from periodic images, a vacuum layer of at least 25 Å was applied perpendicular to the surface for all slab models, and in all three dimensions for nanoclusters. During structural relaxation, the bottom two layers of GaN were fixed in their bulk positions for slab simulations.
