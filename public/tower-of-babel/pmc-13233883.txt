Efficacy of steroid-omitting antiemetic therapy in patients with advanced pancreatic cancer receiving nano-liposomal irinotecan plus fluorouracil and leucovorin
Authors: Yurie Tokito, Kazunaga Ishigaki, Katsuhiko Nara, Go Endo, Hiroto Nishio, Koshiro Fukuda, Tatsunori Suzuki, Yasuyoshi Sato, Naminatsu Takahara, Narikazu Boku, Mitsuhiro Fujishiro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNano-liposomal irinotecan plus 5-fluorouracil and leucovorin (Nal-IRI/FL) is an established second-line chemotherapy for advanced pancreatic cancer (APC). Standard antiemetic prophylaxis for this moderately emetogenic regimen includes a 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT3) receptor antagonist and dexamethasone (Dex). However, steroid use may worsen glucose intolerance in patients with diabetes mellitus (DM), and evidence supporting Dex-omitting strategies remains limited.MethodsWe retrospectively compared prophylactic antiemetic efficacy between Dex-omitting (no steroid use) and Dex-sparing (day 1 only) regimens combined with a 5-HT3 receptor antagonist and neurokinin-1 (NK1) receptor antagonist in patients with APC who received Nal-IRI/FL between October 2020 and August 2024. The primary outcome was total control (TC), defined as no nausea, vomiting, or rescue medication use within 120 h.ResultsPatients were categorized into Dex-sparing (n = 61) and Dex-omitting (n = 13) groups. Baseline characteristics were similar except for a higher prevalence of DM in the Dex-omitting group (31% vs. 100%, P < 0.01). TC rates were comparable (80% vs. 69%, P = 0.46). Relative dose intensity was slightly higher in the Dex-omitting group (Nal-IRI, 55% vs. 62%; 5-FU, 59% vs. 68%). Progression-free survival (5.2 vs. 5.0 months, P = 0.84) and overall survival (10.1 vs. 9.4 months, P = 0.56) were also similar.ConclusionsSteroid-omitting antiemetic therapy in combination with a 5-HT3 receptor antagonist and NK1 receptor antagonist appears to be a feasible option for patients with APC receiving Nal-IRI/FL, particularly those with diabetes mellitus. However, larger prospective studies are needed to validate these findings.

Introduction

Chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting (CINV) is one of the most common and burdensome adverse events (AEs), potentially compromising treatment intensity and deteriorating patients’ quality of life and nutrition status [1–3]. Appropriate antiemetic prophylaxis according to the emetogenic risk of each chemotherapy regimen is essential for symptom control and chemotherapy continuation. CINV is generally classified into two types depending on the timing of onset [4, 5]. Acute CINV, which occurs within 24 h, is mainly triggered by serotonin released from enterochromaffin cells in the gastrointestinal tract, and thus, 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT3) receptor antagonist can play a key role for its prevention [6]. In contrast, delayed CINV, which occurs during 24–120 h after chemotherapy, is primarily mediated by substance P activating neurokinin-1 (NK1) receptors in the medullary vomiting center, for which NK1 receptor antagonists are effective [7]. Steroid shows antiemetic efficacy in both acute and delayed phases in addition to 5-HT3 receptor antagonist and NK1 receptor antagonist, respectively.

Nano-liposomal irinotecan plus fluorouracil and leucovorin (Nal-IRI/FL) is a standard second-line chemotherapy for patients with advanced pancreatic cancer (APC) [8, 9], which is classified as a moderate emetic chemotherapy (MEC). Japanese guidelines recommend a prophylactic doublet antiemetic therapy with a 5-HT3 receptor antagonist and 3-days Dex for the MEC regimen [10]. It has been reported that Dex can be spared from day 2 onward when palonosetron, a second-generation 5-HT3 receptor antagonist, is used because of its prolonged efficacy [11]. However, repeated use of Dex bi-weekly for Nal-IRI/FL raises safety concerns such as impaired glucose tolerance or exacerbation diabetes mellitus (DM), both of which are prevalent in patients with APC. Therefore, steroid-omitting antiemetic therapy is preferable for such patients [11–14].

Recently, NK1 receptor antagonists have been reported to enhance antiemetic efficacy in some MEC regimens. In addition, use of NK1 receptor antagonists allows reduction of steroid use.

Previous studies suggested the feasibility of the steroid-omitting antiemetic strategies using a second-generation 5-HT 3 receptor antagonist and an NK1 receptor antagonist. However, it is unclear whether Dex can be completely omitted without deteriorating adequate control of CINV in patients with APC receiving the Nal-IRI/FL. Therefore, we evaluated the efficacy of prophylactic steroid-omitting antiemetic therapy with a second-generation 5-HT3 receptor antagonist and an NK1 receptor antagonist in this cohort.

Patients and methods

Study design

This is a single-center retrospective study conducted at the University of Tokyo Hospital. We collected clinical data from our prospectively maintained database and supplemented it with a review of electronic medical records. Given the retrospective design of the study, informed consent for the use of patient’s information was obtained on an opt-out basis. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was approved by the ethics committee of the University of Tokyo Hospital (Approval No. 2058).

Patients and antiemetic prophylaxis

Consecutive patients with APC who received at least one cycle of Nal-IRI/FL between October 2020 and August 2024 were selected in this study, excluding those without sufficient clinical data on treatment responses and AEs. Because a second-generation 5-HT3 receptor antagonist was used, no patient in either group received oral Dex on days 2 and 3. The Dex-sparing group received 9.9 mg Dex and 0.75 mg of palonosetron on day 1 of each cycle. Fosnetupitant (235 mg) or aprepitant (125 mg on day 1, followed by 80 mg on days 2 and 3) was added depending on the severity of nausea in prior chemotherapy. In contrast, the Dex-omitting group received only 0.75 mg of palonosetron without Dex in combination with either fosnetupitant or aprepitant. The criteria for NK1 receptor antagonist selection was not pre-specified, and the choice was based on the treating physician’s clinical judgment, referring to the severity of nausea experienced during prior chemotherapy.

Chemotherapy

Nal-IRI at a dose of 70 mg/m2 and leucovorin at 200 mg/m2 were administered intravenously over 90 and 120 min, respectively, on day 1, followed by a continuous infusion of 5-FU at 2400 mg/m2 over 46 h, repeated every 2 weeks. UGT1A1 genotyping was performed before the treatment [15], and patients with homozygous or double heterozygous for *6 and *28 alleles, or those with unavailable results, received a reduced Nal-IRI dose at 50 mg/m2. In addition, for patients who had experienced severe AEs, such as ≥ grade 3 myelosuppression during prior chemotherapy, doses of Nal-IRI and 5-FU were also reduced to 50 mg/m2 and 1800 mg/m2, respectively. In response to treatment-related AEs, the treatment interval was extended to 3–4 weeks if required.

Clinical outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was a total control (TC) rate of nausea and vomiting. TC was defined as the absence of CINV without rescue antiemetics within 120 h after the first administration of chemotherapy. Secondary outcomes included safety during the entire course of Nal-IRI/FL treatment, tumor response, progression-free survival (PFS), and overall survival (OS). The tumor response was assessed according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1 [16]. PFS was defined as the time from initiation of Nal-IRI/FL to documented disease progression or death. OS was defined as the time from initiation of Nal-IRI/FL to death from any cause. In addition, dose intensity (DI) and relative DI (RDI) were evaluated for both Nal-IRI and 5-FU. DI was calculated as the actually delivered dose per unit time (mg/m2/week), and RDI was defined as the ratio of the actual DI to the planned DI. For Nal-IRI, the planned dose was set at 70 mg/m2 for patients with wild-type or single heterozygous UGT1A1 polymorphisms and 50 mg/m2 for those with double heterozygous or homozygous UGT1A1 polymorphisms and for those with unknown UGT1A1 genotype.

Assessment of CINV

Because the initial cycle of Nal-IRI/FL was administered during hospitalization for approximately 1 week, AEs within 120 h were monitored precisely. CINV was evaluated according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0 [17]. Conversely, nausea and vomiting unrelated to chemotherapy but attributable to other reasons such as opioid use or gastrointestinal obstruction were excluded from the events of CINV.

Blood glucose assessment

Blood glucose level was checked regularly before each meal and at bedtime during hospitalization specifically in patients with DM. As chemotherapy was administered in the afternoon, pre-dinner and bedtime glucose levels on day 1 were compared between the two groups, because blood glucose levels were controlled by adjusting antidiabetic therapy, including insulin, from day 2 onward.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables, presented as numbers and percentages, were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables, presented as medians with ranges, were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. PFS and OS were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and compared between groups using the log-rank test. Statistical significance was set at a two-sided P-value of < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed with EZR (Jichi Medical University, Tochigi, Japan), which is a graphical user interface for R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). More precisely, it is a modified version of R commander designed to add statistical functions frequently used in biostatistics [18].

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 74 patients with APC who received NAL-IRI/FL were enrolled in this study: 61 in the Dex-sparing group and 13 in the Dex-omitting groups. As shown in Table 1, baseline patient characteristics were comparable between the two groups including age, sex, ECOG performance status (PS), disease status, the presence of metastases, and the distribution of UGT1A1 polymorphisms. In contrast, the prevalence of DM was significantly higher in the Dex-omitting group than in the Dex-sparing group (100% vs. 31%, P < 0.01), with insulin used more frequently in the Dex-omitting group (85% vs. 26%, P < 0.01). The dose of Nal-IRI during entire treatment course was reduced in 49% of patients in the Dex-sparing group compared with 15% in the Dex-omitting group (P = 0.03). Safety and TC rate of nausea and vomiting

TC of CINV was achieved in 49 patients (80%) in the Dex-sparing group and in 9 patients (69%) in the Dex-omitting group, with no significant difference between the two groups (P = 0.46). Notably, all patients in the Dex-omitting group received an NK1 receptor antagonist, whereas only 10% of those in the Dex-sparing group did (P < 0.01).

As shown in Table 3, grade ≥ 3 neutropenia was observed more frequently in the Dex-sparing group (36% vs. 8%, P = 0.05), whereas other hematological toxicities were comparable. The incidences of grade ≥ 3 nausea (3 vs. 8%, P = 0.45) and diarrhea (2 vs. 15%, P = 0.08) were low both in the Dex-sparing and Dex-omitting groups without significant difference. Tumor response, PFS, and OS

The median follow-up duration was 9.9 months (range, 1.1–55.3) in the Dex-sparing group and 9.5 months (range, 1.4–20.0) in the Dex-omitting group. Tumor response did not differ significantly between the two groups, with response rates (RR) of 5% and 0% (P > 0.99) and disease control rates (DCR) of 57% and 70% (P = 0.54) in the Dex-sparing group and the Dex-omitting group, respectively (Table 5). The median PFS was 5.2 months (95% confidence interval [CI] 3.8–6.0) and 5.0 months (95% CI 1.9–8.3), respectively (P = 0.84; Fig. 3). Similarly, the median OS was 10.1 months (95% CI 8.9–11.4) and 9.4 months (95% CI 3.8–12.8), respectively (P = 0.56; Fig. 4). Discussion

Previous studies have evaluated the possibility of sparing Dex in both the MEC and highly emetogenic chemotherapy (HEC) regimens [11–13]; however, these antiemetic therapies included Dex on day 1. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate the feasibility and potential utility of completely omitting Dex from day 1 combined with a second-generation 5-HT3 receptor antagonist and an NK1 receptor antagonist in Nal-IRI/FL.

In this study, clinical outcomes were compared between the Dex-sparing group and the Dex-omitting group. However, because 3-day dexamethasone administration is recommended as a standard component of antiemetic prophylaxis for MEC and HEC regimens according to the current antiemetic guidelines [3], it is concerned that efficacy of the Dex-sparing anti-emetic therapy might be inferior to the standard 3-day dexamethasone administration. A meta-analysis comparing day-1-only Dex (corresponding to our Dex-sparing approach) with Dex on days 1–3 for MEC regimens found no significant decrease in complete response (no emesis and no rescue medication) rates in acute and delayed-phases, which ranged from 64.9% to 67.4% [11]. Compared to the overall incidence of nausea being 27% according to real-world data from Japan on the Nal-IRI/FL regimen, the TC rate of the Dex-sparing group being 80% in this study seemed compatible [9]. It is considered that the TC rate in the Dex sparing group even with a low proportion of patients receiving NK1 receptor antagonists in this study represented the real-world status of CINV induced by the Nal-IRI/FL regimen.

Another concern in this study was the initial dose reduction of Nal-IRI in more than two-thirds of the patients in both groups. However, because proportions of the patients who initiated the Nal-IRI/FL at the reduced dose were similar between the two groups, it is considered that comparability of the clinical outcomes could be preserved. In this setting, the TC rate was 80% in the Dex-sparing group and 69% in the Dex-omitting group, showing a trend toward a higher TC rate in the Dex-sparing group without significant difference. Additional prospective studies are warranted to validate the efficacy of steroid-free antiemetic strategies in this population. Moreover, as younger patients showed a lower TC rate (Table 4), careful monitoring may be warranted for subgroups with high emetic risk. One possible explanation is that younger patients may report nausea more acutely or have less tolerance for CINV; alternatively, age-related differences in drug metabolism or receptor sensitivity may contribute. This observation warrants further investigation in prospective studies.

In patients with APC who are frequently complicated with comorbid DM, one of the most notable concerns associated with the use of corticosteroids is steroid-induced hyperglycemia. In Japan, the prevalence of DM among patients with PC is approximately 31% [19]. In our study, patients in the Dex-sparing group had substantially higher blood glucose levels in the evening and at bedtime on day 1 of chemotherapy than those in the Dex-omitting group. These elevations in blood glucose levels may require medical interventions, such as insulin dose adjustments, on time. This issue is important especially in older patients, for whom stable glycemic control is difficult to maintain at home. Therefore, a steroid-omitting approach may offer practical advantages especially for APC patients with DM.

Although the frequency of initial dose reduction of Nal-IRI/FL was comparable between the Dex-sparing and Dex-omitting groups, dose reduction during the treatment course occurred more frequently in the Dex-sparing group, resulting in a lower RDI of both Nal-IRI and 5-FU. In contrast, patients in the Dex-omitting group tended to receive reduced doses from the initial administration, which may have contributed to the lower frequency of subsequent dose reductions during treatment. In this study, the median PFS and OS in the Dex-omitting group were 5.0 months (95% CI, 1.9–8.3) and 9.4 months (95% CI, 3.8–12.8), respectively, which were comparable to those reported in the NAPOLI-1 trial (PFS 3.1 [95% CI, 2.7–4.2] months; OS 6.2 [95% CI, 4.8–8.4] months) [8]. No significant differences in PFS or OS were observed between the Dex-sparing and omitting groups, indicating comparable treatment outcomes.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective and single-center design might include selection and information biases and dose reduction of Nal-IRI in more than half of the subjects, limiting the generalizability of the findings. Second, the Dex-omitting group included only 13 patients, which substantially limits statistical power and increases the risk of type II error. The absence of statistically significant differences between groups should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence between the two antiemetic strategies. Third, all patients in the Dex-omitting group had DM, whereas only 31% of the Dex-sparing group did. Antiemetic regimen was chosen by treating physicians referring to patient background such as DM status. These stark imbalances which might confound both efficacy and safety outcomes. Importantly, all patients in the Dex-omitting group received an NK1 receptor antagonist, whereas only 10% of those in the Dex-sparing group did. As NK1 receptor antagonists are known to enhance antiemetic efficacy particularly in delayed CINV, it is considered that the comparable TC rates were partly attributed to more use of NK1 receptor antagonists in the Dex-omitting group. Thus, the use of NK1 receptor antagonists can be considered in combination with the omission of dexamethasone. Furthermore, non-glycemic steroid-related adverse effects, such as insomnia or osteoporosis, were not systematically evaluated, and the economic implications of substituting NK1 receptor antagonists for Dex were not assessed. These limitations warrant cautious interpretation of the results and underscore the need for larger prospective studies to validate the safety and efficacy of Dex-omitting antiemetic strategies in this setting.

In conclusion, this hypothesis-generating retrospective study suggests that Dex-omitting antiemetic in combination with a 5-HT3 receptor antagonist and an NK1 receptor antagonist appears to be a feasible option for patients with APC receiving Nal-IRI/FL, particularly those with diabetes mellitus for whom the Dex-omitting strategy was associated with improved glycemic control without apparent compromise of treatment intensity or efficacy. However, because the confounding effects of NK1 receptor antagonist use and patient selection must be acknowledged in this study, larger prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings.
