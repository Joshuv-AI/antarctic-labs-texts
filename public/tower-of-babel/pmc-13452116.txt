Multi-generational effects of salicylic acid on Lipaphis erysimi and induced defense in Brassica juncea
Authors: Muhammad Wajid Javed, Muhammad Imran Jhammat, Ammara Riaz, Muhammad Umair Gulzar, Dilbar Hussain, Asad Aslam, Nazih Y. Rebouh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Turnip aphid, Lipaphis erysimi Kalt. (Hemiptera: Aphididae), is an insecticide-resistant pest of Brassica spp. SA-salicylic acid, a hormonal regulator of systemic acquired resistance in plants, might prove to be a better option against resistant pests. Therefore, SA was applied exogenously in the four treatments with the concentrations of 0, 0.5, and 1 mM 24-hours before infesting the Brassica juncea (Chinese leaf mustard) plants with L. erysimi. Responses of aphid were noted for two generations (F1 and F2) on reproductive fitness parameters. Kaplan-Meier survivorship, developmental period, reproduction time, effective fecundity (Md), intrinsic rate of increase (rm), relative growth rate, generation time (Mgt), total progeny, proportion of nymphs reaching adulthood, and nymph survival were significantly affected at the concentrations of 1- and 0.5-mM SA over the control and 0 mM treatments. Plant chlorophyll, nitrogen, and crude proteins were greatest in 0.5 mM while 1 mM SA reduced it significantly. Flavonoid (myricetin and quercetin), hydrocinnamic acid (caffeic, ferulic, and sinapic acid), and phenolic acid (chlorogenic, gallic, and syringic acid) were activated in response to aphid attack which were enhanced in 1 mM SA followed by 0.5 mM. Therefore, SA could be a better tool in managing L. erysimi but field level studies are suggested to assess its efficacy in diversified environment. This study could help in reducing pest abundance in organic agriculture and improving plant biochemistry to breed resistant crops to ensure global food security.

Introduction

Chinese leaf mustard, Brassica juncea (L.) Czern. & Coss. (Brassicales: Brassicaceae), is an important source of edible oil in the world having 38–42% oil content [1, 2]. In Pakistan, the oilseed crop from Brassica family ranks second after cottonseed crop [3]. It is used as an essential culinary component in several famous cuisines [2, 4]. Economically it is feeding a large chunk of population in the world [3]; however, its production is largely hindered by insect infestation specifically Lipaphis erysimi and Brevicoryne brassicae [5–7].

Turnip aphid, Lipaphis erysimi Kalt. (Hemiptera: Aphididae), is a cosmopolitan and notorious pest of brassica crops and causes a yield loss of 35–75% [7, 8]. It feeds on leaves, shoots, flowers, and pods to reduce plant vigor and quality [7]. It also transmits several plant viruses [8]. Several synthetic insecticides are used to control L. erysimi; however, insecticides may not be a viable option due to edible nature of crop (leaf, shoot, seed meal, and oil) [2, 3]. Moreover, increasing demand of organic food necessitates alternative pest management techniques rather than insecticides [9–11].

In the present situation, salicylic acid (SA)-mediated induced defense is an important pest management tool, which can reduce pest population [11–13]. SA is a bioactive hormone, which may act as a defense-signaling molecule while inducing systemic acquired resistance and convene hypersensitive (HR) response through exogenous application [14]. SA-signaling network can be activated due to upregulation of PR-pathogenesis-related genes [15]. This is a practical approach for farming community to improve plant vigor to generate more yield components on one side and protects the plants against pests, on the other [11, 13]. Plants are protected through increasing the number of beneficial insects for biological control and reducing pest pressure through enhanced plant defense [14, 15].

Several studies have shown SA effectiveness to reduce plant pests [5, 6, 11, 13, 16, 17]; however, for a specialist herbivore, e.g., L. eryismi, studies are limited specifically on B. juncea. This is the first study where multi-generational host mediated effects of SA were recorded on B. juncea against L. erysimi. Current research was based on the hypothesis that the application of SA could not only reduce aphid reproductive fitness but also improve plant chlorophyll, nitrogen, crude proteins, and phenolic defense. The objective of the research was to determine the effect of SA on aphid reproductive attributes, plant chlorophyll, nitrogen, protein, and phenolic contents. Eight treatments were followed in a sequence: non-infested, infested, 0 mM, 0.5 mM, 1 mM, 0 mM + Infested, 0.5 mM + Infested, 1 mM+ Infested. The grouping logic behind this combination was to assess the independent impact of aphid infestation and SA treatments on plant. While combined treatments of SA + aphid infestations were studied to determine SA impact on plants in the presence of aphids. This study could help in reducing pest abundance in organic agriculture and improving plant biochemistry to breed resistant crops to ensure global food security.

Results

Reproduction fitness of Lipaphis erysimi

Kaplan-Meier survivorship curves revealed a statistically significant difference (χ2 = 24.59, df = 3, P < 0.001) between control and SA treatments. Lowest survival was recorded in 1 mM SA followed by 0.5 mM. The results of control were not different from 0 mM (Fig. 1). Aphid pre-reproductive period was delayed in the higher concentration to lengthen its development period (F (3, 36) = 479.1, P < 0.0001), as a result of this, number of aphids in the upcoming generation were reduced. Maximum delay in aphid development was noted in 1 mM SA followed by 0.5 mM. Whereas control and 0 mM showed similar effects (7.61 vs. 7.59 days) (Fig. 2a). Mean reproduction time (F (3, 36) = 818.1, P < 0.0001) was found to be the greatest in control treatments whereas SA treatments reduced it in the order of 1 mM SA > 0.5 mM SA > 0 mM SA (Fig. 2b).

Effective fecundity (Md) was also suppressed significantly (F (3, 36) = 33.10, P < 0.0001) both in 0.5- and 1-mM SA while results of control were similar to that of 0 mM (Fig. 2c). Intrinsic rate of natural increase (rm) was also affected (F (3, 36) = 48.48, P < 0.0001) where control had greatest improvement (0.32 mean number of offsprings per female per day) while SA reduced it in a concentration dependent manner (Fig. 2d). Lower rm in SA treatments was linked with low Md values where female aphids produced lower number of offspring. Relative growth rate was slightly improved in SA-deprived treatment followed by the control/0 mM. Results were statistically suppressive (F (3, 36) = 95.79, P < 0.0001) for 1 mM followed by 0.5 mM SA (Fig. 2e).

Mean generation time (Mgt) in days, was also statistically different (F (3, 36) = 87.67, P < 0.0001) in SA treatment where 0.5 mM showed less impact while 1 mM had the greatest (Fig. 2f). This could be associated with delayed development time and low relative growth which ultimately prolonged the aphid mean generation time. Total progeny production (F2 generation) was highest in the control treatments while both concentrations of SA reduced it significantly (F (3, 36) = 29.74, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2g). Number of immature becoming adults out of produced offspring were significantly lower (F (3, 36) = 70.67, P < 0.0001) in 1 mM SA followed by 0.5 mM and control treatments (Fig. 2h). Progeny survival of F2 nymphs was the number of aphids’ offspring which remained alive in F2 generation. These values were statistically significant (F (3, 36) = 226.7, P < 0.0001) and almost two-folds highest in the control and 0 mM treatments while a 50% reduction was observed in 0.5- and 1-mM concentrations (Fig. 2i) which was linked to the higher number of mortalities.

Effect on nitrogen and crude proteins of Brassicajuncea

Percentage nitrogen contents were fluctuating under SA treatments and SA + insects. The highest contents were found in 0.5 mM SA-treated plants that were free from insect infestation (Fig. 3a). Similar results were recorded for crude proteins (Fig. 3b). Other treatments had lower values either due to toxic effects of L. erysimi or phytotoxic response of higher SA concentration. The 1 mM SA reduced crude protein and nitrogen the most indicating deteriorated plant quality (Fig. 3a, b).

Effect on chlorophyll content of Brassicajuncea

Chlorophyll contents (SPAD values) affected significantly under aphids and SA treatments (Fig. 3c). Infested plants had the lowest chlorophyll revealing a drastic effect of aphid feeding on plants. Application of SA improved chlorophyll contents in infested as well as non-infested plants. The highest chlorophyll was found in plants treated with 0.5 mM. However, increasing the concentration to 1 mM, declined its contents which indicated a stress on plants (Fig. 3c).

Activation of defense chemicals

Aphid infested plants revealed activation of various defense chemicals including flavonoids, hydrocinnamic acid, and phenolic acids. Among flavonoids, myricetin and quercetin were activated in aphid infested plants (Fig. 4). Myricetin was greatest in 1 mM SA treatment on aphid infested plants followed by 1 mM SA on non-infested plants (Fig. 4a). Quercetin was activated the most in 1 mM treatment on both infested and non-infested plants followed by 0.5 mM on infested plants (Fig. 4b). Hydrocinnamic acid included caffeic, ferulic, and sinapic acid (Fig. 5). Although aphid feeding activated caffeic acid but 1 mM SA followed by 0.5 mM enhanced its contents the most over the infested and non-infested plants (Fig. 5a).

Discussion

Foliar treatment of salicylic acid reduced different reproductive fitness parameters of L. erysimi where highest concentration, i.e., 1 mM was more effective over the others. This was also stated in the findings of Yali & Sattari-Nassab [18] along with Javed et al. [5, 6] where 1 mM concentration of both SA and CA were efficient over 0.5 mM to reduce reproductive and biological fitness of Brevicoryne brassicae. Our findings were also in agreement with Khoshfarman-Borji [19] where application of SA along with a rhizobacteria (Pseudomonas putida) improved the plant defense system to reduce B. brassicae reproductive fitness. Studies of Javed et al. [5, 6] along with Yali & Sattari-Nassab [17] showed a decrease in development period, reproduction time, effective fecundity, intrinsic rate, relative growth rate, immature becoming adults, and mean generation time of B. brassicae on Brassica napus. Researchers attributed this reduced aphid fitness to improvement in plant chemical defense, e.g., phenolics, glucosinolates, and nutrient chemicals [11, 13, 16].

More recently, Javed et al. [11, 13] indicated that the application of SA was effective to reduce the population and reproduction indices of B. brassicae on Brassica napus under field conditions that was basically attributed to a significant enhancement in a number of nutrient and phenolic defense chemicals. RP- (reverse phase) HPLC revealed activation of defense compounds specifically myricetin, quercetin, gallic, syringic, caffeic, ferulic, sinapic, and chlorogenic acid which were recorded playing an important role in SA-induced defense against brassica aphids [11]. Improvement in myricetin and quercetin was dependent on SA concentration. Thiruvengadam et al. [20] noted SA-dependent improvements in myricetin, quercetin along with hydrocinnamic and phenolic acids in B. oleracea. A report from Park et al. [21] also revealed SA-mediated activation of gallic, caffeic, sinapic, chlorogenic, ferulic and several other flavonoids. Javed et al. [11] also recorded a concentration-dependent improvement in several phenolic defense compounds (quercetin, myricetin, caffeic, ferulic, chlorogenic, sinapic, cinnamic, coumaric, and benzoic acids) in SA-treated B. napus fields infested with B. brassicae.

SA has been demonstrated to improve the concentrations of various defense chemicals through elicitation and upregulation of PAL-phenyl ammonia lyase genes [22]. Moreover, SA also synthesizes phenolic defense compounds through phenylpropanoid pathway [23]. The phenylpropanoid pathway is a vital plant metabolic route converting phenylalanine into diverse phenolic compounds, including lignin, flavonoids, and coumarins. It bridges primary metabolism (shikimate pathway) and secondary metabolism, providing structural integrity (lignin) and environmental protection (defense, signaling, UV protection) [24]. In addition, SA-based improvement in the uptake of macro- and micronutrients also plays a role in enhanced activation and biosynthesis of phenolic defense chemicals [11].

Application of different inducers on different plants have shown to reduce reproductive fitness of different aphid species due to improvement in plant flavonoid biosynthesis and physiological defense chemicals [16]. Similar results were recorded under jasmonic acid-induced defense in B. juncea against sucking and chewing pest where the function of secondary defense metabolites was important [17]. SA has also known to produce different secondary metabolites that could influence aphid phloem uptake and reduce nutrient digestion [13, 16].

Chlorophyll contents were reduced in L. erysimi-infested plants. It was stated that feeding of Lipaphis and other aphids on plants could destroy leaf chlorophyll either by puncturing or by inducing cellular toxiphemia [14]. Application of SA significantly improved plant chlorophyll especially in 0.5 mM SA. Treatment with SA elicited plant defense to divert photosynthesis products towards leaf [25]. Another mechanism indicates that SA suppress reactive oxygen species in plant tissues, thereby, assisting the plants to recover its lost chlorophyll. More recently, Setotaw et al. [17] delineated the SA-mediated mechanism of chlorophyll and defense production in plants in response to insects (Spodoptera litura, S. frugiperda, and Mythimna separata) which indicated an intricate pattern of connection of SA to genetic transcriptions of ERF, WRKY, and bHLH in maize.

In chloroplast developing cells, SA was recorded to repair aphid-injured chlorophyll. This repair is regulated indirectly where SA-mediated uptake of nitrogen and magnesium play an important role. Both nutrients worked as the building blocks of chlorophyll [11]. Consequently, plants treated with 0.5 mM SA showed a higher quantity of chlorophyll compared to the infested plants. Conversely, increasing the concentration to 1 mM SA reduced the chlorophyll substantially which is showing toxic effects of higher SA concentration on plant chlorophyll [25].

Feeding of L. erysimi reduced nitrogen in plant tissues. Foliar application of SA improved nitrogen, and hence, crude proteins in both non-infested and infested plants. The value-added improvement in nitrogen and crude proteins thought to occur due to higher uptake of nitrogen in plants under improved root surface area [13]. This SA-mediated improvement in the levels of nitrogen and proteins was also confirmed in previous studies of Javed et al. [11] and [13]. SA may enhance plant root nodules to uptake more atmospheric nitrogen by improving the performance of nitrogen-fixing bacteria [26]. Exogenous application also improved root length and nutrient absorption area across the soil layer enabling the plants to acquire more nutrients especially nitrogen, sulphur, zinc, and magnesium [11, 13]. Improvement in plant sulphur also help to increase plant nitrogen, and hence, crude proteins via increasing the sulphur-based amino acids and phenolics which contribute in enhancing plant protein profile [22]. This was also deciphered in the recent study of Javed et al. [27].

The results of the study also showed that 0.5 mM SA optimizes chlorophyll and nitrogen contents, while 1 mM SA inhibits them which may be attributed to phytotoxicity. This has serval mechanistic explanations. A high concentration (1 mM) usually triggers systemic acquired resistance which is energetically expensive, diverting plant photosynthetic products and nutrients away from growth processes (chlorophyll biosynthesis and nitrogen assimilation) towards the defense production (e.g., phenolic compounds, glucosinolates, pathogenesis-related proteins) [28–30]. High SA concentration antagonizes the biosynthesis of crucial growth and photosynthetic hormones especially auxin which promotes growth and nutrient allocation. A high concentration of SA dose can induce an oxidative burst (H₂O₂) causing oxidative stress which can damage the photosynthetic apparatus (chlorophyll degradation) and inhibit key enzymes involved in nitrogen metabolism (e.g., nitrate reductase) [31]. There may be a downregulation of genes encoding chlorophyll (e.g., glutamyl-tRNA reductase, magnesium chelatase) followed by stomatal closure [30]. Nitrogen metabolism may also be interfered by inhibiting nitrate uptake through transporters’ activities or suppression of nitrogen assimilation pathway (nitrate reductase and glutamine synthetase) [32]. It may shift nitrogen away from photosynthetic proteins (Rubisco) towards alkaloids and PR-proteins etc [30, 33, 34].

Critically stating, the primary novelty of this research revealed from its multi-generational investigation of salicylic acid (SA) as an inducer against a pesticide-resistant pest, moving beyond simple mortality counts to a comprehensive analysis of the pest’s entire life cycle and the plant’s physiological response. While the use of SA as an elicitor is not entirely new, the study innovates by precisely tracing its population-level consequences across consecutive generations (F1 and F2) of L. erysimi. This implies that the actual efficacy of SA is not merely in killing aphids but in effectively disrupting the aphid reproductive potential in a sustainable way. The negative effect persisting into the F2 generation is important, suggesting that the induced resistance mechanism has a lasting, systemic effect within the plant, providing a longer pest protection.

Interpreting the results more critically, inducer application supported the first part of our hypothesis that it could reduce aphid biological fitness; however, the rest of hypothesis was not supported fully where higher concentration (1 mM) was not improving chlorophyll, nitrogen, and crude proteins showing phytotoxic results. Some researchers have supported this finding while other presented a contrary approach having positive impacts whereby inducers were applied under field conditions governed by several edaphic and ecological factors. This study was limited in a sense as it was conducted under semi-controlled conditions. It is suggested to extend these experiments in the field with multiple sowing seasons under alternating eco-graphic and geological factors.

Furthermore, the research uncovers a potentially novel trade-off in plant physiology triggered by different SA concentrations. It found that while 0.5 mM SA improved chlorophyll content, the more effective dose (1 mM) actually reduced it. The same 1 mM concentration simultaneously reduced plant nitrogen and crude proteins. This dissociation between concentrations for chlorophyll and nitrogenous compounds is an intriguing physiological insight, suggesting that SA-induced resistance, at higher concentrations, may prioritize the allocation of resources towards defense-related compounds, which is a strategic shift that ultimately enhances the plant’s defensive capability despite a potential cost to chlorophyll and proteins.

It shifts the paradigm from directly targeting the pest with insecticides to manipulating the crop’s physiology to make it a hostile and unsuitable host for sustainable pest management. However, it needs a field-level validation under variable environments towards translating these findings into a viable integrated pest management strategy. The plant-insect interaction should be studied on the molecular levels where different plant metabolites should be estimated either colorimetrically or using Real Time PCR (RT-qPCR for PR genes). Studying plant resistance/defense against the insect by examine the expression of the defensive genes against the insect infestation will be an important contribution. Soluble protein determination using SDS-PAGE will enrich the data in future studies.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that SA effectively primes an induced defense response in Brassica juncea, which significantly impairs key reproductive parameters of the turnip aphid (Lipaphis erysimi) across two consecutive generations (F1 and F2) evidenced by reduced fecundity and intrinsic rates of increase, highlights the potential of SA as a durable option for managing this resistant insect. The 0.5 mM improved nitrogen, chlorophyll, and crude proteins; however, 1 mM caused plant stress. It reflects dose-dependent trade-offs where 0.5 mM improved the plant physiology, while 1 mM imposed physiological costs. This must be ensured that only those treatments should be selected which maximize defense benefits and minimize any negative effects on plant growth to establish a firm foundation for successful field application.

Materials and methods

Plants and insects

Seeds of Brassica juncea cultivar “AARI Canola” were procured from Oilseed Research Institute, Faisalabad. This variety has a medium stature, dark green glossy leaves, and semi-erect posture. The seeds of the plant were sown in sterilized pots (8 kg, 25 cm diameter), under screen house (21 ± 3 °C temperature, 65 ± 3% relative humidity, and 10:14 h light/dark period). Properties of soil and water were determined before the experiment (Tables 1 and 2). Fertilizer nutrients, i.e., nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium were applied at the rates of 90, 60, and 50 kg ha− 1 respectively, using Urea, DAP (Di Ammonium Phosphate), and SOP (Sulphate of Potash).

Quality of irrigation water

L. eryismi were identified morphologically through Blackman and Eastop [35] where pale green colour, presence of triangular cauda, short siphunculi, white powdery covering, dark marking on abdominal dorsum were stated as the key identity marks. Homogenized L. erysimi population was obtained by sustaining aphids for more than 18 generations on B. juncea plants.

Treatments’ evaluation

Initially, four different concentrations (0.5, 1, 1.5, and 3 mM) were screened while only 0.5 and 1 mM were selected as treatments, as the higher concentrations i.e., 1.5 and 3 mM possessed phytotoxicity in terms of damage to plants. Lab grade SA (CAS Number: 69-72-7) was purchased from SigmaAldrich and 0.1% V/V ethanolic solutions of SA with the concentrations 0.5 and 1mM were produced respectively [11]. Solutions were applied using a manual handheld sprayer (Top-gun, 1 L) at a volume of 30 ml per plant. Accumulatively, 2 plants per pot and 10 replications per concentration per treatment were followed for aphid reproduction studies in a completely randomized design. The initial study had higher number of clip cages per treatment. However, due to unavoidable experimental attrition, i.e., primarily from aphid escape or squash, compromised replicates were intentionally excluded and reported data only from intact cages to ensure data integrity. The experiment was proceeded on 65–70 days old plants.

Bioassay for reproductive fitness of Lipaphis erysimi

After 24 h of foliar application of inducers, homogenous group of apterous parthenogenetic Lipaphis female aphids were introduced on plants and then confined under clip caging (1.5 cm depth, 3 cm diameter) with one female per clip cage. Clip cages were placed on abaxial leaf surface and one clip cage was maintained per plant. Only F1 generation was allowed to grow within a clip cage by removing the mother aphids after laying the eggs. Kaplan-Meier survivorship function was recorded on the number of nymphs survived on the treated plants. If a female aphid was escaped accidently or squashed then its readings were shown as barbed points on survivor curves [6].

The pre-reproductive period defines the time when female aphid produced its first offspring while development period was the time taken by nymphs to reach their reproduction ability. Likewise, reproduction time was termed as the number of days taken by aphids to lay the first until the last nymph. Effective fecundity (Md) was the number of nymphs produced in a time equal to pre-reproductive period while the ratio of nymph development time to pre-reproductive period (rdp) was also measured [36]. The intrinsic rate (rm) was calculated from the formula;\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_{m}=0.738 (\mathrm{ln}\ Md)/r_{dp}$$\end{document}

While relative growth rate was estimated as rm/0.86 and mean generation time as Mgt = rdp/0.738. Total progeny production was the total number of nymphs (F2 generation) that were produced out of F1 generation. Immature becoming adults were the number of immature (nymphs) of F2 generation that became mature. Their nymph survival was calculated from the number of nymphs that were found alive to the total number of nymphs laid by female, and then multiplying it with 100.

Chlorophyll, nitrogen, and crude protein measurements

Chlorophyll contents of the plant leaves were measured by chlorophyll metering device (SPAD 502, Minolta, Japan). Soil Plant Analysis Development (SPAD) meter was placed on the middle part of the fourth fully expanded leaf from the top of the plant. Furthermore, nitrogen was measured from leaves using Kjeldal apparatus, following wet acid digestion of 0.5 g of dried samples. Later on, crude proteins were calculated by the acid-based titration method. The obtained nitrogen contents were multiplied with 6.2 to estimate crude proteins [26].

Quantification of defense chemicals (RP-HPLC)

Samples were prepared as per protocols mentioned by Javed et al. [11] in order to estimate the phenolic defense compounds. According the procedure, 40 g of leaf sample was homogenized using 200 ml ethanol (80%) followed by ultrasonication (1 h, 4℃) and the pellet was extracted. This pellet was re-extracted over-night (200 rpm) using extraction buffer (100 ml) followed by alkaline hydrolysis using 2 N sodium hydroxide under 17–18 h of constant nitrogen atmosphere. The final mixture was filtered through a Teflon membrane and analyzed using RP-HPLC.

Phenolic compounds were identified after equating the retention time and UV-visible peak spectra of established standards while the quantification of these compounds was done using comparison method. The area obtained [mV.s] was multiplied with correction factors. Signals were processed in DataApex (Data Handling Application for Windows OS, Version 2001, Prague, The Czech Republic) for final results.

Statistical analysis

Prior to analysis of variance, data were checked for normality of distribution and homoscedasticity using Shapiro-Wilk and Bartlett test, respectively. Later on data were analyzed following Fisher statistical variance analysis (ANOVA). Treatment means were compared by applying Tukey test at level of significance 0.05. Data, which were not following the assumptions of ANOVA, were log transformed. Insect survival was calculated using Kaplan-Meier survivorship followed by Mantel–Cox test at P < 0.05. All test and statistical comparisons were carried out using GraphPad Prism, version 10.4.1 (627) (GraphPad Inc., CA, US).
