Surgical Success in Obstructive Sleep Apnea Patients Using Preoperative Drug‐Induced Sleep Endoscopy and Awake Endoscopy Considering Disease Severity
Authors: Nafil Arimbrathodi, Arif Nambillath, Noora Alhail, Labiba Abughazzah, Waheed Rahman, Abhishek Menon, Yousef Hudaib, Majid Alabdulla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundObstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a prevalent disorder characterized by recurrent episodes of airway obstruction during sleep, leading to fragmented sleep and oxygen desaturation. Identifying the precise site and pattern of obstruction is essential for surgical success. Awake endoscopy with Müller's maneuver (AEMM) has long been used, but drug‐induced sleep endoscopy (DISE) offers dynamic assessment under sleep‐like conditions.ObjectiveTo retrospectively compare surgical outcomes of OSA patients evaluated with DISE versus AEMM, stratified by disease severity, and to determine whether the comparative value of DISE over AEMM differs according to OSA severity as measured by AEMM (AHI).MethodsRecords of 102 OSA patients undergoing sleep surgery between January 2022 and December 2024 at Hamad Medical Corporation were reviewed. Patients were grouped into AEMM‐only (n = 54) and DISE (n = 48). Surgical success was defined as postoperative AHI < 15 events/hour and at least 50% reduction from baseline. Statistical analysis included independent and paired t‐tests, chi‐square tests, and the Johnson–Neyman procedure to identify AHI‐based thresholds of DISE benefit.ResultsDISE group had higher surgical success compared to AEMM (62.5% vs. 40.7%, p = 0.114). Preoperative and postoperative AHI changes were significant in both groups (p < 0.001). Stratification revealed that moderate‐to‐severe OSA patients (AHI > 16.7) had significantly better outcomes when surgery was based on DISE (p < 0.001). In patients with mild OSA (AHI < 16.7), DISE conferred no additional benefit over AEMM.ConclusionDISE‐based surgical planning improves outcomes in moderate‐to‐severe OSA, while AEMM remains adequate for mild OSA cases. Disease severity should guide the choice of preoperative airway assessment method.

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is one of the most common sleep‐related breathing disorders, with prevalence estimates ranging from 9% to 38% depending on age and sex [1]. It is characterized by recurrent episodes of upper airway collapse during sleep, leading to hypoxemia, sleep fragmentation, and excessive daytime sleepiness [1]. Untreated OSA is associated with increased cardiovascular morbidity, metabolic dysfunction, neurocognitive impairment, and reduced quality of life [2, 3, 4]. The public health and socioeconomic impact of OSA is therefore substantial.

Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) therapy remains the first‐line treatment, but long‐term adherence is suboptimal, with nearly half of patients discontinuing use within the first year [5]. As a result, surgical interventions targeting upper airway collapse represent an important therapeutic alternative for CPAP‐intolerant patients [6].

For surgical planning, polysomnography (PSG) provides diagnostic and severity data but does not localize anatomical sites of obstruction. Awake nasopharyngoscopy with Müller's maneuver (AEMM), first described by Borowiecki et al. [7], is widely used but limited in its ability to replicate natural airway collapse during sleep. Drug‐induced sleep endoscopy (DISE), developed by Croft and Pringle [8], permits dynamic visualization of obstruction patterns under pharmacologically induced sleep. Several studies have shown DISE can alter surgical planning compared with AEMM, often by identifying tongue base or multilevel obstruction [9, 10, 11, 12].

The role of OSA severity has not been adequately considered in most comparative studies. Severity not only influences treatment outcomes but also correlates with the anatomical level of collapse, with more severe OSA often involving the tongue base and hypopharynx [13, 14]. As AEMM has limited diagnostic power at these levels, DISE may offer significant advantages in patients with moderate‐to‐severe OSA. Chen et al. attempted to include disease severity as a consideration, but stratification by severity was not the primary focus of their study [15]. Similarly, Askar et al. demonstrated the comparative value of positional awake endoscopy versus DISE in OSA assessment [11] and subsequently described pattern‐based surgical decision making for retropalatal obstruction [12].

The central research question of this study is: Does the benefit of DISE over AEMM in guiding sleep surgery outcomes vary according to OSA disease severity? We hypothesized that DISE would confer greater benefit in moderate‐to‐severe OSA, where multilevel collapse is more prevalent, while AEMM may remain sufficient for mild disease. This stratification makes our study distinctive, as it integrates disease severity into the comparison of DISE and AEMM, providing a perspective for optimizing surgical decision‐making.

Methods

This is a single‐institution retrospective case series. We reviewed records of OSA patients treated at the Ambulatory Care Center, Hamad Medical Corporation, between January 2022 and December 2024. Inclusion criteria were: age ≥ 18 years, AHI ≥ 5 events/hour on type I or type II PSG or type III portable sleep monitoring as per AASM guidelines, CPAP refusal or intolerance, preoperative endoscopy (AEMM ± DISE), sleep surgery (palatal, multilevel, with or without nasal procedures), and follow‐up PSG ≥ 3 months postoperatively. Exclusion criteria were BMI > 35 kg/m2, prior oropharyngeal or hypopharyngeal surgery, incomplete records, or loss to follow‐up. A total of 118 patients were screened; 16 were excluded. Thus, 102 patients were analyzed.

OSA severity was classified according to AHI: mild (5–15 events/hour), moderate (15–30 events/hour), and severe (> 30 events/hour). Based on their preoperative evaluation, patients were divided into two groups. Fifty‐four patients evaluated exclusively with AEMM were included in the AEMM group; 48 patients evaluated with both AEMM and DISE were included in the DISE group. DISE was recommended in the following situations: (1) when AEMM provided insufficient or inconsistent data (e.g., in cases of severe OSA without tongue base blockage on AEMM); (2) patients who had not responded to CPAP therapy; or (3) patients with clinical symptoms and no correlating AHI on PSG. DISE was only undertaken after obtaining informed patient consent, given the procedural risks of sedation and additional costs.

Awake endoscopy was performed in the outpatient clinic with the patient seated. The VOTE classification was used to assess and record the upper airway's obstructive tendencies. The obstructive patterns in the upper airway were evaluated and documented in the patient's electronic health record.

All DISE procedures were carried out in the operating room with the patient supine and monitored by the bispectral index (BIS). No decongestant or intranasal topical anesthetic was used. Patients were sedated with target‐controlled propofol infusion, commencing at 2.0 µg/mL and increased by 0.2 µg/m: every 2 minutes until optimal sedation was achieved. The BIS score was maintained between 50 and 70. A flexible nasopharyngoscope was inserted once the patient was asleep (BIS ≤ 70) and the examination was recorded on video. The DISE was performed by the same surgeon who assessed the patient in the outpatient clinic.

Results of DISE and AEMM were recorded using the VOTE classification system. The velum, oropharynx, tongue base, and epiglottis were identified as anatomical sites of obstruction. Obstruction configuration was evaluated as concentric, lateral, or anteroposterior. Zero points were awarded for no or mild obstruction (< 50%), one point for partial obstruction (50%–75%), and two points for severe or complete obstruction (> 75%). Surgery was proceeded with when the degree of obstruction in a particular upper airway region exceeded one point (> 50% obstruction).

Sleep surgery for OSA was performed on all patients, involving one or more of the following techniques: (1) barbed relocation pharyngoplasty; (2) coblation tongue base reduction; (3) nasal surgery (septoplasty with submucosal diathermy of inferior turbinates); (4) epiglottoplasty using CO2 laser. Surgical decisions were based on AEMM and/or DISE results, patient complaints, and clinical examination findings. The number of observers involved in endoscopic assessment was two per patient—the operating surgeon (who performed all evaluations) and a trained clinical fellow who independently reviewed each video recording.

Surgical success was defined as a postoperative AHI of less than 15 events per hour and a 50% reduction from baseline AHI value, or a postoperative AHI of less than 5 events per hour.

Statistical Analysis

SPSS software version 30 was used for data analysis. Continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. The chi‐square test was used for categorical variables; independent or paired t‐tests were used to compare continuous data between groups. To identify the threshold AHI at which DISE‐based surgical planning produces significantly better outcomes than AEMM, the Johnson‐Neyman procedure was employed. This method identifies regions of significance in moderated regression analyses—specifically, the range of the moderator variable (preoperative AHI) within which the relationship between the predictor (evaluation method: DISE vs. AEMM) and the outcome (surgical success) is statistically significant at p < 0.05. It avoids the limitation of arbitrary AHI cut‐points by directly computing the boundary values from the data. A p‐value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all other analyses.

Results

The analysis comprised 102 patients in total. There were 54 patients in the AEMM group (44 men and 10 women) and 48 patients in the DISE group (36 men and 12 women). Table 1 summarizes the demographic characteristics. Patients in the AEMM group were 44.3 ± 14.2 years old compared with 46.8 ± 8.7 years in the DISE group (p = 0.342). Males outnumbered females in both groups (AEMM 81.0%, DISE 75%). Mean BMI was 29.2 ± 3.7 kg/m2 in the AEMM group versus 28.4 ± 4.2 kg/m2 in the DISE group (p = 0.448). There was no statistically significant difference in Modified Mallampati Stage between groups (p = 0.756).

The DISE group's success rate was higher than the AEMM group's (62.5% vs. 40.7%), although the difference was not statistically significant overall (p = 0.114). Discussion

This single‐institution retrospective case series examined whether the benefit of DISE over AEMM in guiding sleep surgery differs according to OSA disease severity. The principal finding is that DISE‐based surgical planning significantly improves outcomes in patients with moderate‐to‐severe OSA (AHI > 16.7 events/hour), while outcomes in mild OSA patients are equivalent between the two assessment modalities. These findings provide a practical, evidence‐based rationale for stratified use of DISE in clinical practice.

DISE identified multilevel obstructions, particularly at the tongue base and hypopharynx, which were frequently underestimated by AEMM. This explains the superior outcomes observed when preoperative AHI exceeded 16.7 events/hour, a threshold above which multilevel surgery was required in the majority of DISE‐guided cases. The proportion of multilevel procedures in the DISE group (72.9%) was more than double that in the AEMM group (35.2%), directly reflecting the greater diagnostic yield of DISE at hypopharyngeal and tongue base levels.

Several published series support these observations. Rodriguez‐Bruno et al. and Kezirian et al. reported that DISE alters surgical planning in 40%–50% of patients compared with awake assessment [9, 10]. Ha et al. demonstrated particular utility of DISE in selecting tongue base procedures [16], while Askar et al. [11] performed a direct comparative study of positional awake endoscopy versus DISE, reporting that DISE provided superior anatomical information in a proportion of patients with moderate‐to‐severe disease. Their subsequent work established pattern‐based surgical algorithms for retropalatal obstruction guided by positional endoscopy findings [12], and most recently demonstrated that hypopharyngeal surgical decisions based on differential endoscopic patterns are associated with improved outcomes in moderate‐to‐severe OSA [17]. Our findings are consistent with this evolving body of evidence, which collectively argues for DISE as the preferred investigation when hypopharyngeal involvement is clinically suspected—predominantly in moderate‐to‐severe disease.

Conversely, Certal et al. [18] and Viana et al. [19] found no improvement or even poorer outcomes with DISE‐based planning. Our Johnson–Neyman analysis reconciles these apparently conflicting results by demonstrating that the benefit of DISE is not uniform across all OSA severities. Studies that do not stratify by disease severity may dilute a real benefit in moderate‐to‐severe patients with a null effect in mild disease, producing inconclusive aggregate findings. Stratification by AHI‐defined severity groups should therefore be considered a methodological standard in future comparative DISE studies.

OSA severity correlates with anatomical involvement. Severe OSA is frequently associated with multilevel collapse, including the hypopharynx and tongue base [13, 14]. AEMM, conducted with the patient awake and seated, is limited in its ability to replicate sleep‐state pharyngeal dynamics, particularly at subpalatal levels [7, 11]. DISE, by pharmacologically mimicking natural sleep, captures obstruction patterns that may be entirely absent on awake examination. This mechanistic advantage is most consequential when surgical targeting of the hypopharynx or tongue base is needed—a situation much more common in moderate‐to‐severe disease.

For OSA severity classification, our study used AHI derived from attended type I PSG or type III portable monitoring as per AASM criteria. OSA was classified as mild (AHI 5–15 events/hour), moderate (15–30 events/hour), and severe (> 30 events/hour). AHI alone does not fully capture OSA burden, and the lowest oxygen saturation (LO2Sat) is an important complementary index. In our cohort, preoperative LO2Sat was 76.4 ± 9.8% in the AEMM group versus 80.1 ± 3.9% in the DISE group (p = 0.094), reflecting comparable baseline oxygenation impairment between groups. Postoperatively, LO2Sat improved to 79.6 ± 6.4% in the AEMM group and 85.8 ± 3.4% in the DISE group (p = 0.072). The greater absolute improvement in the DISE group (+5.7 percentage points vs. +3.2) is consistent with more comprehensive surgical targeting enabled by DISE. While these differences did not reach conventional statistical significance—likely due to sample size—the trend parallels the AHI outcome data and supports the clinical benefit of DISE in moderate‐to‐severe disease. Oxygen desaturation index (ODI) and cumulative time spent below 90% SpO2 (T90) were not systematically available in this retrospective cohort; prospective studies should incorporate the full oxygen impairment profile alongside AHI.

The practical implication of these findings is a cost‐effective, stratified approach: AEMM is sufficient for mild OSA, being less invasive and avoiding the risks and costs of sedation, while DISE should be the standard preoperative evaluation for moderate‐to‐severe OSA to maximize surgical success. This is consistent with the pattern‐based surgical decision frameworks described by Askar et al. [12, 17], which emphasize tailoring procedural selection to endoscopic phenotype.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. The retrospective single‐center design introduces selection bias, and the indication for DISE was not randomized. The sample size is modest. Variability in sedation depth and interobserver interpretation may have influenced DISE findings, although the requirement for dual‐observer review mitigated this. Follow‐up PSG was performed at a minimum of 3 months, which may not reflect longer‐term stability of outcomes. Despite these limitations, the stratified Johnson‐Neyman analysis substantially strengthens the internal validity of the severity‐specific conclusion.

Conclusion

DISE enhances surgical planning for OSA patients with moderate‐to‐severe disease, improving outcomes compared with AEMM alone. For mild OSA, AEMM remains adequate and cost‐effective. Disease severity, as measured by preoperative AHI, should be considered when selecting the preoperative airway evaluation method for individualized patient management. Prospective, multicentre studies with standardized sedation protocols and inclusion of comprehensive oxygen indices are needed to validate these findings.
