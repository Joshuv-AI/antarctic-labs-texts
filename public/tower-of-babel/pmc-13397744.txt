Restricted cubic spline analysis of the association between the third-trimester triglyceride-glucose index and neonatal birthweight in pregnant women with gestational diabetes mellitus: a multicenter cohort study
Authors: Tao Yu, Kairong Lyu, Yuanfei Wang, Yamei Yang, Li Gou, Jing He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, a surrogate indicator of insulin resistance, has been linked to adverse pregnancy outcomes. However, evidence among women with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) remains limited, especially concerning the relationship between the third-trimester TyG index and the risk of delivering a large for gestational age (LGA) infant. This study aimed to investigate this association and assess whether pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) modifies the relationship.MethodsThis retrospective multicenter cohort study included women with GDM who delivered singleton live births at four tertiary hospitals in Wuhan, China, between January 2022 and December 2023. Eligible participants had available third-trimester fasting triglyceride and fasting plasma glucose measurements for TyG index calculation. Multivariable logistic regression models were used to estimate odds ratios for LGA risk per 1-unit increase in the TyG index. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses were performed to evaluate potential nonlinear associations, while stratified analyses examined differences across pre-pregnancy BMI categories. Crude LGA rates were calculated across TyG quartiles to illustrate absolute risk differences.ResultsAmong 2,754 women with GDM, 283 (10.28%) delivered LGA infants. Each 1-unit increase in the third-trimester TyG index was associated with a significantly greater risk of LGA (adjusted OR 1.80, 95% CI 1.44–2.27). RCS analysis suggested a modest nonlinear association (P for nonlinearity = 0.046). The crude incidence of LGA increased from 7.6% in the lowest TyG quartile (Q1) to 16.3% in the highest quartile (Q4), corresponding to an absolute risk difference of 8.7 percentage points. The associations remained directionally consistent across BMI subgroups, with no significant interaction observed between TyG index and pre-pregnancy BMI (P for interaction > 0.05).ConclusionsAn elevated third-trimester TyG index was independently associated with a higher risk of LGA in women with GDM. Although a potential nonlinear relationship was identified, the evidence supporting nonlinearity was limited. These findings support a statistical association rather than established clinical applicability. Further prospective studies are required to determine whether the TyG index can contribute to risk stratification or clinically meaningful risk assessment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09338-0.

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) refers to glucose intolerance of varying severity that develops or is first identified during pregnancy in women without previously recognized abnormalities in glucose metabolism [1]. GDM is associated with substantial short- and long-term adverse outcomes for both mothers and their offspring [2, 3], including increased risks of eclampsia and excessive fetal growth [4, 5]. Peng et al. [6] reported that women with GDM have a three- to four-fold greater likelihood of delivering a large for gestational age (LGA) infant compared with normoglycemic pregnant women. Even when glycemic control is considered adequate, approximately 15–20% of women with GDM still give birth to LGA neonates. These infants face a higher risk of perinatal complications, including shoulder dystocia and neonatal hypoglycemia, as well as a significantly increased susceptibility to obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and cardiovascular disease later in life [7–9].

The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index has emerged as a practical surrogate marker of insulin resistance. It reflects the metabolic effects of elevated triglyceride levels, which promote the transfer of free fatty acids from adipose tissue to non-adipose tissues, contributing to insulin resistance and influencing fetal growth through alterations in maternal metabolism [10]. Previous studies have shown that the TyG index may outperform the homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) in identifying insulin resistance and may also predict the occurrence of LGA among women with GDM [11, 12]. The third trimester represents a particularly important stage of pregnancy, characterized by accelerated fetal adipose tissue accumulation and peak physiological insulin resistance [13]. During this period, the maternal metabolic environment exerts a substantial influence on fetal growth and development. Therefore, clarifying the independent association and dose–response relationship between the TyG index and LGA risk during late pregnancy may facilitate more precise identification of women at increased risk.

Several recent cohort studies have explored this relationship. In a preliminary Chinese cohort, a higher first-trimester TyG index demonstrated modest ability to discriminate subsequent LGA risk (AUC = 0.64), with women in the highest TyG tertile exhibiting nearly three-fold higher odds of delivering an LGA infant compared with those in the lowest tertile [14]. Similarly, a prospective birth cohort from Southeast China found that elevated first-trimester TyG index levels were independently associated with an increased risk of LGA. However, the TyG index alone showed limited predictive performance (AUC < 0.60) [15]. Comparable findings were reported in an Iranian cohort, where women in the highest TyG tertile had up to a 5.3-fold greater risk of delivering LGA newborns [16]. Recently, a large population-based study involving over 46,000 pregnancies demonstrated that elevated TyG index levels across any trimester, as well as persistently high TyG trajectories throughout pregnancy, were positively associated with LGA risk [17]. These findings highlight the potential value of longitudinal TyG monitoring, although trimester-specific thresholds for women with GDM, particularly during late pregnancy, remain unclear.

Despite growing interest in the TyG index, clinically validated cut-off values specifically applicable to third-trimester management in women with GDM have not been clearly established. Therefore, the primary aim of this study was to characterize the distribution of the third-trimester TyG index among women with GDM and to investigate its association with LGA risk. In particular, we sought to examine the shape of the dose–response relationship to better illustrate how LGA risk varies across the observed range of TyG index values.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective multicenter cohort study used electronic medical record (EMR) data from four tertiary hospitals in Wuhan, China, between January 2022 and December 2023. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (No. TJ-IRB20220611). Given the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived. The study was reported in compliance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [18].

Study population

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosis of GDM based on the Chinese Guidelines for Maternal and Child Co-management of Gestational Diabetes Mellitus [19]; (2) availability of third-trimester fasting triglyceride (TG) and fasting plasma glucose (FPG) measurements; and (3) singleton live births. Participants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) pre-existing type 1 or type 2 diabetes mellitus; (2) presence of major fetal congenital anomalies or other severe congenital disorders; (3) missing third-trimester TG or FPG data; (4) multiple gestations or stillbirths; or (5) fewer than three recorded gestational weight gain (GWG) measurements during pregnancy or unavailable pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) data. Data collection and definitions

Across all participating centers, routine clinical management generally adhered to guideline-recommended care pathways for GDM, including dietary and exercise interventions combined with regular glucose monitoring, with insulin therapy initiated when glycemic targets were not adequately met. Data were collected using a standardized extraction protocol and were primarily obtained from electronic medical records, structured obstetric databases, and neonatal registries. The extracted information included metabolic indicators, anthropometric data, pregnancy-related characteristics, and neonatal outcomes.

The third-trimester TyG index was derived from a single fasting laboratory measurement of triglycerides and fasting plasma glucose obtained during late pregnancy rather than from averaged measurements. The TyG index was calculated as ln[TG (mg/dL) × FPG (mg/dL)/2]. TG and FPG values measured in mmol/L were converted to mg/dL before calculation. Maternal height and weight were measured by trained healthcare personnel. Pre-pregnancy BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2) and classified according to the established criteria for Chinese adults as underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5–23.9 kg/m2), overweight (24.0–27.9 kg/m2), and obese (≥ 28.0 kg/m2) [20].

The primary outcome was LGA, defined as a birth weight exceeding the 90th percentile according to Chinese neonatal birthweight reference standards stratified by sex and gestational age [21]. Birth weight, gestational age, and fetal sex were obtained from obstetric databases and neonatal medical records at each participating center. To enhance diagnostic consistency, LGA status was further confirmed by study physicians using standardized birthweight reference charts and corresponding birth records.

Other covariates included sociodemographic characteristics such as maternal age, educational level (junior high school or below, senior high school or vocational school, college/undergraduate, or postgraduate or above), and place of residence (urban versus rural). To better characterize longitudinal patterns of maternal weight change during pregnancy, GWG trajectories were modeled using latent class trajectory analysis and categorized into fast-, medium-, and slow-growth groups (Supplementary Figures S1 and S2). This method was adopted to capture dynamic patterns of weight gain throughout pregnancy rather than relying solely on a single cumulative GWG measure.

Glucose metabolism-related variables included plasma glucose concentrations measured at 0, 1, and 2 h during the 75-g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). Laboratory parameters included total cholesterol (TC) and TG, both measured using standardized enzymatic assays. Neonatal variables comprised fetal sex, neonatal hyperbilirubinemia, and neonatal hypoglycemia. Neonatal hyperbilirubinemia was identified based on pre-discharge total bilirubin measurements together with clinical diagnostic records, while neonatal hypoglycemia was defined as an initial postnatal blood glucose level < 2.6 mmol/L. Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP) and polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) were identified from clinical diagnoses documented in the EMR. Pregnancies conceived through in vitro fertilization and embryo transfer were classified as embryo transfer (ET) pregnancies.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.2). Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using Student's t-test for normally distributed continuous variables, the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed continuous variables, and the χ2 test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

Third-trimester triglyceride and fasting plasma glucose measurements were complete for all participants in the analytical cohort because these variables were required for study inclusion, and LGA status was obtained directly from delivery records. Consequently, missing data were confined to covariates and accounted for less than 8% of observations. Although Little’s test did not indicate evidence against the assumption of missing completely at random (P = 0.12), the test may have limited sensitivity to detect subtle deviations when the proportion of missing data is relatively small. Therefore, multiple imputation by chained equations was performed under the more conservative missing-at-random assumption. The imputation model incorporated all variables included in the analyses. Regression analyses were conducted separately within each imputed dataset, and pooled estimates were generated according to Rubin's rules.

To identify potential risk factors, baseline characteristics were compared between the LGA and non-LGA groups using univariable analyses, with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05. Continuous variables, including maternal age, pre-pregnancy BMI, OGTT glucose values, and lipid parameters, were analyzed as continuous measures. Categorical variables, such as residence, delivery mode, and maternal comorbidities, were evaluated using clinically relevant reference categories, including vaginal delivery and absence of disease. Ordinal variables, including educational attainment, were analyzed for trends across categories.

Candidate covariates for multivariable logistic regression were selected based on clinical relevance, previous evidence, and potential confounding effects on the association between the third-trimester TyG index and LGA risk. Univariable analyses were used only as supplementary guidance during model construction rather than as the sole basis for variable selection. Pre-pregnancy BMI and GWG were included because of their established associations with maternal metabolic status and fetal growth. Mode of delivery, parity, and fetal sex were additionally incorporated owing to their recognized relationships with birth weight outcomes. The 1-h OGTT glucose level was further included to account for maternal glycemic status, a major contributor to fetal overgrowth in pregnancies complicated by GDM.

Candidate predictors were further evaluated using multivariable logistic regression analyses. Three sequential models were developed based on findings from univariable analyses (P < 0.05) together with clinical relevance. Model 1 was unadjusted for potential confounders. Model 2 was adjusted for GWG trajectory, pre-pregnancy BMI, mode of delivery, parity, and fetal sex. Model 3 additionally incorporated the 1-h OGTT glucose level in addition to the covariates included in Model 2. These sequential models were constructed to assess the stability and robustness of the observed association under progressively greater levels of covariate adjustment. The relationship between the third-trimester TyG index and the risk of LGA was expressed as crude odds ratios (ORs) and adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for each 1-unit increase in the TyG index.

To investigate potential nonlinear relationships between the third-trimester TyG index and the risk of LGA, as well as to characterize the dose–response pattern, the TyG index was modeled using restricted cubic spline (RCS) functions within multivariable logistic regression analyses [22]. This method was chosen because it enables flexible, continuous evaluation of the relationship between a continuous exposure variable (TyG index) and the odds of a binary outcome (LGA) across the observed TyG distribution, without assuming a strictly linear association or relying on arbitrary categorical cut-offs. Odds ratios for LGA relative to the reference value were estimated throughout the observed range of TyG values, allowing visualization of the continuous dose–response relationship. Three knots were positioned at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of the TyG distribution in accordance with commonly recommended practices for RCS modeling in epidemiological research [23]. This knot configuration was chosen to achieve an appropriate balance between model flexibility and parsimony, while ensuring sufficient data representation across the observed TyG range and reducing potential estimation instability at the extreme ends of the distribution. Wald χ2 tests were applied to assess both the overall association (P for overall) and deviation from linearity (P for nonlinearity) through joint testing of the spline coefficients. A P-value for nonlinearity < 0.05 was considered indicative of a nonlinear dose–response relationship; otherwise, the association was interpreted as approximately linear across the observed TyG range.

Potential effect modification by pre-pregnancy BMI was evaluated using two complementary approaches. First, separate RCS models were fitted within individual BMI strata, and the resulting dose–response curves were visually compared. Second, an interaction term between the TyG index and BMI category was included in the fully adjusted RCS models. Statistical interaction on the multiplicative scale was assessed using a global Wald χ2 test for interaction; P for interaction < 0.05 was considered evidence of significant effect modification. To further describe the absolute risk of LGA across the TyG distribution, the TyG index was additionally categorized into quartiles based on its distribution, and crude LGA incidence rates were calculated within each quartile.

Results

Population characteristics

A total of 2,754 pregnant women with GDM were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1), among whom 283 (10.28%) delivered infants classified as LGA. Compared with women in the non-LGA group, those in the LGA group had significantly higher third-trimester TyG index values (9.75 ± 0.62 vs. 9.54 ± 0.58, P < 0.001). The distribution of pre-pregnancy BMI also differed significantly between the two groups (P < 0.001), with a greater proportion of women in the LGA group categorized as overweight (31.45% vs. 23.55%) or obese (13.43% vs. 8.29%). In analyses stratified by BMI category, the fully adjusted association was most pronounced among women with overweight status (aOR 2.20, 95% CI 1.45–3.39), followed by those with obesity (aOR 1.92, 95% CI 1.01–3.76) and normal-weight women (aOR 1.54, 95% CI 1.14–2.10). In comparison, no statistically significant association was observed in the underweight subgroup, which may reflect the limited sample size and low number of LGA events within this category; therefore, findings for this subgroup should be interpreted as exploratory.

Analysis of nonlinear associations between TyG index and the risk of LGA

In the overall cohort, RCS logistic regression analyses suggested a potential nonlinear relationship between the third-trimester TyG index and the risk of LGA, although the evidence supporting nonlinearity was modest (P for nonlinearity = 0.046; Fig. 2). Across the observed TyG range, the probability of LGA remained relatively stable at lower TyG levels and increased more noticeably at higher values. This trend was generally consistent with the quartile-based analysis, in which the crude incidence rates of LGA were 7.6%, 7.9%, 9.7%, and 16.3% across Q1 (TyG 7.2–9.2), Q2 (TyG 9.2–9.6), Q3 (TyG 9.6–9.9), and Q4 (TyG 9.9–11.7), respectively. In BMI-stratified RCS analyses (Fig. 3), the estimated associations between TyG index values and LGA were generally positive across BMI categories. No statistically significant evidence of nonlinearity was identified within individual BMI strata (all P for nonlinearity > 0.05). The observed associations were broadly consistent with linear dose–response patterns, although estimates became less precise at the extremes of the TyG distribution due to greater uncertainty in areas with lower data density. Discussion

This study demonstrated that a higher third-trimester TyG index was independently associated with an increased risk of LGA among women with GDM. The observed association remained consistent across different BMI categories, and within each stratum, the relationships were generally compatible with linear trends. Furthermore, no significant interaction between TyG and BMI was identified. These results suggest that the third-trimester TyG index may represent a valuable research marker for investigating elevated LGA risk in pregnancies complicated by GDM.

Although the RCS analyses provided marginal statistical evidence of nonlinearity (P for nonlinearity = 0.046), closer evaluation indicated that the association was largely continuous. The apparent nonlinearity appeared to be driven mainly by limited data density at the lower end of the TyG distribution, rather than by a biologically meaningful threshold effect. Therefore, the observed curve should be interpreted primarily as a descriptive, data-driven pattern. Any potential clinical application based on specific TyG cut-off values should therefore be approached cautiously and would require robust external validation in independent study populations.

Our findings are generally consistent with previous studies reporting a positive association between the TyG index and the risk of LGA [24, 25]. However, results across the literature have not been entirely consistent [26, 27]. Several studies conducted in Chinese populations demonstrated that a higher first-trimester TyG index was associated with a significantly increased risk of LGA, with women in the highest TyG tertile or quartile exhibiting approximately 2- to threefold greater odds of delivering an LGA infant compared with those in the lowest TyG category [14, 15]. Similarly, Pazhohan et al. [16] observed a significant positive relationship between first-trimester TyG index and LGA risk in an Iranian cohort, further supporting the potential relevance of this association across different populations. Most previous investigations focused on early pregnancy. In comparison, this study specifically evaluated the third trimester, a period characterized by rapid fetal adipose tissue accumulation and peak physiological insulin resistance. As a result, the third-trimester TyG index may better reflect the maternal metabolic environment most directly linked to fetal overgrowth. It may therefore represent a biologically more relevant time point for assessing LGA risk in women with GDM.

In comparison, Gurza et al. [27] did not observe a significant association between a TyG index > 8.6 and adverse pregnancy outcomes, including LGA and preterm birth. These inconsistencies may be attributable to differences in the timing of TyG assessment, study populations, outcome definitions, and analytical methods across studies. The median TyG index in our cohort (9.6) was considerably higher than the cut-offs used in some previous reports, suggesting that underlying population-level metabolic differences may also contribute to the discrepant findings. Triglyceride levels are known to increase progressively after 6–8 weeks of gestation and typically peak around 36 weeks, indicating that hypertriglyceridemia predominantly develops during the mid- to late stages of pregnancy [28].

Calculated from fasting triglyceride and glucose concentrations, the TyG index is considered a practical surrogate marker of insulin resistance (IR). It has been associated with adverse maternal and fetal outcomes, particularly among women with GDM during the third trimester. Elevated TyG index values generally reflect more pronounced IR. From a physiological perspective, maternal IR contributes to hyperglycemia and increased transplacental glucose transfer. This excess nutrient supply stimulates fetal pancreatic beta-cell hyperplasia and accelerates maturation of the fetal insulin secretory response, leading to compensatory hyperinsulinemia. Fetal insulin functions as a potent anabolic and growth-promoting hormone, directly enhancing fetal growth and adipose tissue deposition. The combined effects of fetal hyperinsulinemia and hyperglycemia further promote fat and protein accumulation, ultimately contributing to excessive fetal growth and the development of LGA [29, 30].

The TyG index has emerged as a novel biomarker of insulin resistance and is associated with the risk of various metabolic disorders. In several studies, the TyG index has been considered a more informative surrogate marker of insulin resistance than fasting blood glucose or 1-h OGTT glucose levels alone [24, 31]. Its simplicity, low cost, and good reproducibility support its use in metabolic research and future validation studies [32].

Birth weight is a key determinant of both short- and long-term neonatal health outcomes. LGA not only increases the risk of immediate neonatal complications, including fetal distress, shoulder dystocia, brachial plexus injury, hyperbilirubinemia, and hypoglycemia [33–35], but may also negatively influence long-term health by increasing susceptibility to metabolic disorders such as obesity, cardiovascular disease, and diabetes later in life [36, 37]. Well-established risk factors for LGA include pre-pregnancy overweight or obesity, previous history of LGA, multiparity, male fetal sex, and abnormalities in glucose and lipid metabolism [38, 39]. Consistent with previous evidence, this study found that LGA was associated with pre-pregnancy overweight or obesity, excessive GWG, cesarean delivery, multiparity, male fetal sex, elevated 1-h OGTT glucose levels, and a higher third-trimester TyG index. After adjustment for established confounding factors, including pre-pregnancy BMI, GWG, and maternal glycemic status, each 1-unit increase in the third-trimester TyG index was associated with an 80% increase in the odds of LGA.

GWG trajectories were included to reflect longitudinal patterns of maternal weight gain throughout pregnancy rather than relying solely on a single cumulative measure. Both GWG trajectories and 1-h OGTT glucose levels were incorporated as clinically relevant covariates because of their close relationships with maternal metabolic status and fetal growth. However, these variables may also partially represent intermediates along the causal pathway linking maternal metabolic dysfunction to fetal overgrowth. As a result, the possibility of partial overadjustment cannot be completely ruled out, which may have attenuated the observed associations. Furthermore, given the observational nature of the study, residual confounding from unmeasured variables remains possible. Therefore, the fully adjusted effect estimates should be interpreted with caution, with greater emphasis placed on the overall consistency and stability of the association across the sequential regression models.

Obesity is a major contributor to the development and progression of GDM, and pre-pregnancy BMI is widely recognized as an important indicator of maternal metabolic health [40]. In the present BMI-stratified analyses, the positive association between the TyG index and the odds of LGA remained generally consistent across BMI categories. Although no statistically significant association was identified in the underweight subgroup, this result should be interpreted cautiously due to the small sample size and limited number of events in this category. No significant TyG × BMI interaction was observed, suggesting that pre-pregnancy BMI did not substantially modify the association between the TyG index and LGA risk in our cohort. These findings support an association between the TyG index and LGA risk, while also underscoring the relevance of maternal weight status in women with GDM [19]. Future studies involving larger cohorts with sufficient representation of underweight women are needed to generate more reliable subgroup-specific estimates [41].

From a clinical standpoint, the TyG index can be easily calculated from fasting triglyceride and fasting plasma glucose measurements routinely obtained during prenatal care. However, the current findings support a statistical association rather than confirmed clinical applicability. Although the third-trimester TyG index may have potential value in assessing metabolic risk during late pregnancy, its utility for risk stratification or routine clinical decision-making remains uncertain and requires further validation in prospective studies. Therefore, the TyG index should not be considered a standalone tool for guiding clinical management at present. Interpretation of these findings should also consider the magnitude and precision of the observed effect estimates, the absolute risk differences across TyG quartiles, and the inherent limitations associated with the observational study design.

Several limitations of this study should be considered. First, the retrospective observational design does not permit causal inference; therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Selection bias may also be present because study inclusion required the availability of third-trimester metabolic measurements and sufficiently complete clinical data, which may have preferentially included women with more comprehensive follow-up. Similarly, residual confounding from unmeasured variables such as lifestyle factors, treatment adherence, socioeconomic conditions, and differences in management practices across centers cannot be completely excluded and may have influenced the estimated associations.

Second, the TyG index was calculated from a single fasting measurement obtained during late pregnancy. Therefore, the potential effects of within-individual biological variability, measurement error, and inter-laboratory variation cannot be ruled out. Because repeated metabolic assessments were unavailable, longitudinal changes and trajectories in the TyG index during pregnancy could not be evaluated.

Third, neonatal outcomes were restricted to routinely documented clinical measures, and the lack of detailed neonatal metabolic or adiposity-related data limited further mechanistic interpretation. Finally, the study population was derived from tertiary hospitals within a single region of China, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations or healthcare settings. Future prospective studies involving more diverse populations are needed to validate these findings and to clarify further the potential role of the TyG index in late-pregnancy risk stratification.

Conclusion

In conclusion, an elevated third-trimester TyG index was independently associated with a higher risk of LGA among women with GDM. Although the TyG index may reflect maternal metabolic status during late pregnancy, the current findings support a statistical association rather than established clinical applicability. Further prospective studies are required to determine whether the TyG index can contribute to risk stratification or clinically meaningful risk assessment.
