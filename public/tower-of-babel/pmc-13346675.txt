Dynamic Bayesian network-based risk assessment for gas treatment station
Authors: Hamza Zerrouki, Samer Mekatel, Basem A. Alkhaleel, Ashu Yadav, Shatrudhan Pandey, Hemant Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Risk assessment in gas processing plants plays a critical role in preventing hazardous events that may escalate into catastrophic accidents and severe economic losses. Conventional approaches, such as Event Tree (ET) analysis, have long been employed to identify potential outcomes and design preventive or protective barriers. Despite their usefulness, ET models remain limited by their static structure and their inability to manage uncertainty adequately. To overcome these drawbacks, this study proposes the use of a Dynamic Bayesian Network (DBN) framework, which enables the explicit representation of event dependencies and the continuous updating of probabilities, features not achievable with traditional ET methods. Moreover, the DBN approach can model the temporal progression of sequential events while incorporating safety barriers into a unified analytical framework. This dynamic framework provides greater flexibility and depth in risk analysis. Its applicability is demonstrated through a case study of a gas processing treatment unit, highlighting the model’s effectiveness in representing complex accident scenarios and supporting more reliable risk-informed decision-making. The case study shows that implementing this framework holds advantages in managing safety barriers in a unified way, considering the interplay among safety barriers, making continuous risk-treatment adaptations to sustain the safety and security of the gas processing system. This study provides guidance to practitioners for evaluating the performance of safety barriers in gas processing units.

Introduction

The rapid advancement of modern industries has introduced a variety of emerging risks that, if not properly managed, may result in serious harm to people, assets, and the environment. This industrial progress represents a double-edged aspect: while it contributes to improving living standards through enhanced production and services, it simultaneously increases the likelihood of severe accidents and large-scale losses1. Among the various industrial sectors, the chemical industry occupies a central position due to its significant influence on modern technological development. These facilities integrate multiple engineering disciplines, including mechanical, civil, and safety engineering, and operate under conditions that often involve elevated pressures and temperatures. The chemical sector encompasses a wide range of production activities, extending from food processing and detergent manufacturing to the oil and gas industry2. Moreover, Chemical plants encompass all transport, storage, and manufacturing industries operating under high-pressure and/or high-temperature conditions. In addition to the above, the pharmaceutical production and manufacturing industries are also a big part of chemical plants.

Natural gas stations are exposed to several major risks due to the hazardous nature of the processed gas and the complexity of the operations. The most critical risks include fire and explosion hazards resulting from the high flammability of natural gas3, often triggered by leaks caused by equipment failure, corrosion, or operational errors, leading to possible scenarios such as jet fires, flash fires, and vapor cloud explosions (VCE)4. The presence of toxic components such as hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) poses serious health risks, including fatal exposure. In addition, operations under high pressure and temperature increase the likelihood of ruptures and sudden releases. The strong interconnection between process units can lead to cascading failures or domino effects, where an initial incident escalates into a large-scale accident5. Furthermore, the effectiveness of safety barriers is crucial, as their failure can significantly worsen accident consequences. Finally, the invisible and highly dispersive nature of gas can delay detection, increasing the severity and impact of potential incidents.

The accelerated pace of industrial development, particularly within the chemical and process industries, has led to a considerable increase in the likelihood of industrial accidents. These sectors are characterized by the handling of a wide variety of substances, many of which possess hazardous properties such as toxicity, flammability, and explosiveness, often under extreme pressure and temperature conditions. Among these, the petrochemical sector, encompassing oil and gas processing, represents one of the most critical and high-risk domains due to the nature and scale of its operations.

Although the frequency of accidents in such industrial sectors remains relatively low, their potential consequences are often severe and can result in catastrophic outcomes. A notable example is the incident that occurred at the Skikda LNG liquefaction complex (GL1/K) in Algeria on January 19, 2004. At approximately 18:40, a massive explosion, followed by an extensive fire, destroyed three out of six liquefaction units within the facility. The accident led to substantial human and material losses, resulting in 23 fatalities, 74 injuries, and significant economic damage, in addition to serious environmental impacts6.

The continuous evolution of industrial systems highlights the essential need for reliable methods and analytical tools to assess potential risks and ensure adequate safety levels within process plants. Typically, such analyses are carried out by safety engineers in collaboration with multidisciplinary teams comprising mechanical, instrumentation, and process engineering specialists. Over the past few decades, numerous methodologies have been developed to support decision-making in the domains of safety management and risk assessment. These approaches are generally grouped into two main categories: qualitative and quantitative methods. Qualitative techniques encompass tools such as Hazard and Operability Study (HAZOP), Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA), and What-if analysis. Conversely, quantitative approaches include Event Tree (ET), Fault Tree (FT), and Bow-Tie (BT) analyses.

Despite their wide application, these traditional quantitative techniques exhibit certain limitations, particularly when new system information becomes available or when interdependencies among events must be represented dynamically. To address these challenges, Bayesian Networks (BNs) have been increasingly employed over the past three decades, offering a flexible probabilistic framework capable of updating system knowledge and capturing complex event relationships. The integration of BNs with conventional techniques has thus emerged as a powerful and systematic approach for enhancing decision-making in risk analysis and human reliability assessment7.

Performed a comparative assessment between Fault Tree (FT) and Bayesian Network (BN) methodologies for safety evaluation in process industries8,9. Subsequently, the work was extended by transforming the Bow-Tie model into a Bayesian framework to enable dynamic safety analysis of process systems10,11. The application of Bayesian Networks has demonstrated significant effectiveness in analyzing safety and risk within complex systems, offering a robust and flexible alternative capable of addressing the inherent limitations of traditional analytical methods.

This study presents the transformation of a traditional Event Tree (ET) into a Dynamic Bayesian Network (DBN) as a means to address the inherent limitations of conventional ET analysis. Unlike static ET models, the DBN framework captures event interdependencies and allows for the continuous updating of probability distributions over time. Through this transformation, sequenced events and safety barriers are integrated within a unified probabilistic graphical structure, enabling the real-time monitoring of both accident progression and barrier performance. To demonstrate the applicability of the approach, a case study of a gas release in a treatment unit adjacent to a processing facility is conducted. The findings highlight the capacity of the DBN model to enhance process safety by dynamically updating the network structure and incorporating additional preventive barriers, ultimately providing a more flexible and robust tool for risk-informed decision-making.

Table 1 shows that modern risk assessment has evolved into a hybrid discipline where Event Trees (ET) provide the logical backbone for accident sequences while Bayesian Networks (BN) manage complex dependencies and multi-state variables. These combined methods, such as the EDIB and STPA-FBN frameworks15,18, bridge qualitative system-theoretic analysis with quantitative fuzzy logic to address data scarcity in emerging sectors like green hydrogen. However, significant limitations persist, most notably the “state explosion” problem, where increased model granularity leads to exponential computational costs, necessitating optimization techniques like Bayesian pruning17. Furthermore, a structural tension exists between Unified BNs, which offer superior diagnostic inference, and Object-Oriented BNs (OOBNs), which favor modularity and computational speed at the risk of missing cross-object dependencies20. The reliance on Expert Elicitation in fuzzy models18 also introduces potential subjectivity, requiring structured validation to maintain scientific rigor. Integration with existing Probabilistic Safety Assessments (PSA) remains a hurdle, as surrogate BN models must be validated against traditional regulatory standards while managing increased visual complexity. Ultimately, while hybrid BNs offer a more realistic representation of “domino effects” and temporal dynamics13,16, their implementation requires a careful balance between model fidelity and practical calculability.

Gas treatment process

Natural gas treatment involves the purification of raw gas produced at the wellhead by removing undesirable components, such as water, acid gases (CO₂, H₂S, etc.), and heavy hydrocarbons (condensates), to meet commercial and pipeline specifications. These operations are essential to ensure safe transportation, prevent corrosion, and enhance the market value of the processed gas. The Hudson process involves cooling natural gas through heat exchange, followed by a series of pressure expansions. A key component of this process is the expansion through a dynamic machine known as a turboexpander, which enables the gas to reach very low temperatures. The condensed hydrocarbons are subsequently separated through fractionation in two rectification columns, namely a deethanizer and a debutanizer. The remaining non-condensable components are discharged as vent gas.

The Pritchard process, also referred to as mechanical refrigeration, is one of the simplest and most direct methods for liquid hydrocarbon recovery. It relies on an external refrigeration cycle, typically using propane as the refrigerant. The refrigeration loop includes a centrifugal or reciprocating compressor that raises the refrigerant from low to high pressure. Before refrigeration, the gas passes through a gas–gas heat exchanger to improve thermal efficiency22.

An effective alternative consists of combining the advantages of both the Hudson and Pritchard processes to enhance liquid recovery performance. This integrated configuration is commonly known as the mixed process. The mixed process incorporates both a propane refrigeration loop and a turboexpander to meet cooling requirements. Methods and process

Event Tree (ET) technique

The Event Tree (ET) methodology provides a structured graphical framework for representing the potential consequences that may arise from a predefined initiating event (IE), such as a system malfunction or process failure. Following the occurrence of the IE, a chain of subsequent developments may unfold, giving rise to different accidental scenarios. Within ET analysis, dichotomous states (e.g., success versus failure, yes versus no, or true versus false) are systematically applied at each branching node to model the occurrence or non-occurrence of intermediate events, the effectiveness of protective barriers, and the resulting outcomes23. The event tree architecture thus maps accident sequences as logical progressions of binary outcomes, thereby illustrating multiple possible accident trajectories. The probability of occurrence of a particular consequence, referred to as the Probability of Outcome Event (POE), is derived by computing the product of the probabilities associated with each event along a selected branch, under the assumption of statistical independence among the events24. Proposed framework

The transformation of an Event Tree (ET) into a Bayesian Belief Network (BBN) is performed in accordance with the approach introduced by Bearfield & Marsh12. This transformation entails the systematic conversion of the ET elements, namely the initiating event, subsequent developments, and safety barriers, into corresponding nodes within the BBN architecture. In the BBN model, initiating and intermediate events, along with safety barriers, are represented as parent (or root) nodes, whereas the potential consequences are modeled as child (or leaf) nodes. Each root node is characterized by a Conditional Probability Table (CPT), which specifies its probabilistic behavior.

Figure 2 presents an integrated, structured methodology for dynamic risk assessment of an industrial process that combines classical hazard identification techniques with probabilistic and learning-based models. The framework progressively transforms qualitative safety knowledge into a quantitative, adaptive risk model capable of supporting both predictive and diagnostic analyses. The process begins with system definition (Step 1), which establishes the physical boundaries, operating conditions, and functional characteristics of the studied installation. This step ensures that the subsequent analyses are performed within a clearly defined and consistent system context, as required by international risk assessment standards. Based on the system definition, accident scenarios are identified. In parallel, a HAZOP analysis is conducted (Step 2) to systematically identify potential deviations from normal operating conditions, their causes, and possible hazardous outcomes. The results of the HAZOP serve as a structured input for defining credible accident scenarios, initiating events, and failure modes of safety barriers. The identified scenarios are then decomposed into three fundamental elements:

(i)the critical (initiating) event,(ii)the associated safety barriers, and.(iii)the possible accident consequences.

These elements form the logical foundation for constructing an Event Tree Analysis (ETA), which explicitly models the sequential success or failure of safety barriers following an initiating event.

To overcome the static nature of conventional ETA (Step 3), the event tree structure is subsequently converted into a Dynamic Bayesian Network (DBN). This conversion in step 4 preserves the causal logic of the event tree while enabling the explicit representation of probabilistic dependencies and temporal evolution. Within the DBN framework, safety barriers and consequences are represented as stochastic nodes whose states evolve.

A quantitative analysis is then performed using the DBN (Step 5), allowing the computation of time-dependent probabilities of barrier failure and accident consequences. Unlike static QRA approaches, this framework supports the continuous updating of probabilities as new operational or inspection data become available. The resulting DBN enables four complementary types of analysis:

Predictive analysis (Step 5.1), to estimate the likelihood of future accident scenarios and consequences under evolving system conditions;Diagnostic analysis (Step 5.2), to infer the most probable causes and barrier failures given observed outcomes or incidents;Safety barrier performance assessment (Step 5.3), to evaluate the effectiveness, degradation, and reliability of individual or combined barriers over time;Sequential learning (Step 5.4), whereby posterior probabilities are iteratively updated using operational feedback, inspection results, or incident data, allowing the model to reflect the actual safety performance of the system throughout its lifecycle.

Description of the process

The Module Processing Plant 2 (MPP2), situated in the southern region of Algeria, consists of multiple process units such as distillation columns, pumps, a turbo-expander, and heat exchangers, as illustrated in Fig. 3. The plant’s main function is the extraction of heavy hydrocarbons, including condensate and liquefied petroleum gas (LPG), from untreated natural gas, as well as the production of gas streams designated for both sale and reinjection purposes. Raw biphasic gas from 39 wells is collected through 9 gathering lines and directed to manifolds before entering the Boosting section, where compression from 93 to 120 kg/cm² enhances liquid recovery. The compressed gas is then distributed across three parallel processing trains (A, B, and C), each with a design capacity of 20 million m³/day. At diffuser D001, the gas enters at 120 kg/cm² and 58 °C, then is cooled in tower E101 to ~ 40 °C before entering separator D101. Here, gas, water, and hydrocarbons are separated: water is discharged to an evaporation basin, while the gas stream (118 kg/cm²) is routed through exchangers E103 and E102, cooled to − 9 °C, and subsequently expanded through valve PRCV108 to 100 kg/cm² and − 13 °C before reaching high-pressure separator D102. To mitigate hydrate formation, 80% mono-ethylene glycol (MEG) is injected into the exchangers. In D102, gas is separated from condensate and water absorbed into MEG, which is then recycled to the regeneration unit. The gas stream undergoes isentropic expansion in turbo-expander K101 (from 118 to 67 kg/cm², − 35 °C), producing energy recovery before entering cold separator D103. After heat exchange with the raw gas stream, the product is reheated to ~ 43 °C, recompressed to 74 kg/cm², and delivered to the sales gas network. Meanwhile, condensates separated in D101, D102, and D103 are collected in low- and medium-pressure separators (D104 and D105) for further handling.

For this study, emphasis is placed on the admission separator D101, which represents the first separation stage of raw gas entering MPP2. The downstream units are beyond the scope of this work.

Application of the methodology

In order to determine potential accident scenarios, a Hazard and Operability Study (HAZOP) was conducted. HAZOP is a qualitative methodology standardized in reference27, commonly employed for the systematic identification and assessment of hazards. It focuses on analyzing deviations from normal operating conditions to identify possible abnormal situations, thereby enabling a structured evaluation of safety and operability issues. The detailed HAZOP analysis for the investigated facility is documented in reference28.

Results and discussion

The results of the DBN model are presented in Figs. 6 and 7, which include safety barriers and the potential consequences. Based on the HAZOP analysis, three major hazardous consequences were identified: (i) pressure increase in the D101, (ii) gas release into the atmosphere, and (iii) fire and explosion. In addition, a safe operating state was defined as a Situation under control. The probabilities of these four possible outcomes were estimated using the DBN model and are reported in Figs. 6 and 7. As shown in Fig. 6, consequence C2 (Pressure increase in the D101) exhibits the highest probability of occurrence compared with the other two hazardous consequences. To mitigate this scenario, two high-level alarms (LICAH101 and LICAH102) are implemented in the process. Their function is to alert the operator in the control room, who can then take corrective action by opening either the liquid line bypass valve LICV101A (diverting flow from D101 to D105) or LICV101B (diverting flow to D003A). These actions effectively reduce the pressure in D101 and help maintain safe operating conditions.

In this framework, safety barriers (safety valves, alarms, and operator response) are modeled as probabilistic nodes whose failure probabilities are iteratively updated based on observed accident occurrences and associated consequences (C2, C3, and C4).

For C2, multiple observations are recorded across the operational timeline, particularly during the early and later years. Within the Bayesian updating process, these recurrent events increase the posterior probability of barrier failure, reflecting accumulated operational evidence and gradual degradation effects. This behavior highlights the sensitivity of moderate-severity consequences to variations in barrier performance and operational conditions.

C3 exhibits fewer and more sporadic occurrences, resulting in a slower update of posterior failure probabilities. Nonetheless, the presence of C3 events indicates that combinations of partial barrier failures and adverse operational states remain plausible within the network structure. These observations contribute to refining conditional probability tables (CPTs) associated with higher-severity outcomes.

No events corresponding to C4 are observed during the ten years. From a Bayesian perspective, this leads to posterior probabilities that remain close to prior estimates for catastrophic scenarios, suggesting effective risk mitigation by existing layers of protection. However, the Bayesian framework inherently preserves residual uncertainty, ensuring that the absence of observations does not imply zero probability.

Figure 8 demonstrates the capability of sequential Bayesian learning to dynamically update safety barrier reliability based on operational feedback, enabling time-dependent risk assessment and improved prediction of accident consequences throughout the lifecycle of the gas treatment unit.

The near-perfect synchronization between the initiating event (PIC139) and the safety barriers (PCV139 and PIC1AH139) is a direct result of probabilistic dependency and evidence propagation within the network topology. Because these components function as an integrated pressure protection loop, the model treats them as a coupled subsystem; thus, improved reliability data for the controller (PIC139) naturally propagates through the causal chain to the associated valve and alarm nodes. Over-extended operational lifecycles, safety barriers like the PCV139 and PIC1AH139 are subjected to harsh process conditions, including high-pressure fluctuations, thermal cycling, and exposure to corrosive sour gas components, which lead to phenomena such as hydrogen embrittlement, seal wear, and mechanical fatigue. The primary causes of this deterioration in gas units are often internal corrosion from H2S or CO2 contaminants and fouling of valve seats, which increase the likelihood of “fail-to-danger” scenarios. Consequently, a comprehensive dynamic assessment must balance the optimistic updates of Bayesian learning with the physical constraints of material degradation to ensure that maintenance intervals are triggered before the physical PFD begins its inevitable ascent due to aging infrastructure.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the transformation of the Event Tree (ET) technique into a Dynamic Bayesian Network (DBN) framework to overcome the key limitations of traditional ET analysis. Unlike static ET models, DBNs are capable of representing event dependencies, multi-state variables, and probabilistic updates, thereby providing a more realistic estimation of accident frequencies and consequences. Through this approach, cascading events and safety barriers can be integrated into a unified, flexible structure that supports continuous data updating and real-time assessment of barrier effectiveness.

The methodology is applied to a case study of a gas treatment unit at Hassi R’Mel (Sonatrach), illustrating the model’s ability to capture complex accident scenarios and improve risk-informed decision-making. Results show that DBNs offer significant advantages over conventional risk assessment methods by adopting a probability-based approach that prioritizes critical consequences and enables proactive process safety management.

Nonetheless, several challenges remain. The accuracy of DBN predictions depends on the availability and quality of probabilistic input data, while simplifying assumptions during model construction may introduce uncertainty. Although computational requirements are manageable for medium-scale systems, scalability to larger and more complex facilities may present practical limitations. Despite these challenges, advances in inference algorithms and computing capabilities make DBNs increasingly feasible for industrial applications.

From a regulatory standpoint, the proposed DBN approach is intended to complement, rather than replace, existing risk acceptance criteria. By moving beyond static frequency, consequence matrices, DBNs provide a dynamic and quantitative tool that strengthens traditional frameworks, offering a more detailed representation of accident propagation and enhancing the reliability of safety-related decision-making in high-hazard industries.

From a practical perspective, the proposed DBN framework offers a powerful decision-support tool for safety managers and engineers in gas processing plants. It enables real-time monitoring of accident progression and continuous evaluation of safety barrier performance. The approach supports proactive risk management by facilitating the identification of critical scenarios and prioritization of risk mitigation actions. The application to the Hassi R’Mel gas treatment unit demonstrates its effectiveness in improving risk-informed decision-making and enhancing process safety management strategies.

Despite its advantages, the proposed approach presents several limitations. The reliability of the DBN model is highly dependent on the availability and quality of input data, particularly probabilistic parameters. In addition, certain simplifying assumptions made during model development may introduce uncertainties in the results. While computational performance remains acceptable for medium-scale systems, scalability to large and highly complex industrial installations may require significant computational resources and advanced inference techniques.

Future work could focus on improving data acquisition processes and integrating real-time operational data to enhance model accuracy and robustness. The development of automated or learning-based methods for DBN structure and parameter estimation represents another promising direction. Moreover, extending the framework to large-scale industrial systems and integrating it with other advanced techniques, such as machine learning or digital twins, could further improve its applicability. Finally, future studies may explore the incorporation of human and organizational factors to provide a more comprehensive assessment of industrial risks.
