Rerupture and Total Complication Rate After Single-Incision Power Optimizing Cost-Effective Distal Biceps Repair
Authors: Charles Z Jiang, Peter Howard, Jacob Weinberg, Zach Spears, Thomas Hughes, Mark E Baratz, John R Fowler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe single-incision power optimizing cost-effective (SPOC) technique for distal biceps tendon repair reattaches the tendon to its native anatomical site, maximizing supination strength while minimizing surgical morbidity. Long-term follow-up data on the SPOC technique remain limited, and debate remains surrounding the optimal fixation strategy for distal biceps tendon repair. This study aims to evaluate the complication rates of patients undergoing SPOC distal biceps tendon repair.MethodsA retrospective chart review was performed on all patients who underwent SPOC distal biceps repair between 2016 and 2024 by two surgeons at the same center. Inclusion criteria included primary distal biceps repair with the SPOC technique and greater than 12 weeks of follow-up. Exclusion criteria included revision surgery, allograft use, non-SPOC repair technique, and less than 12 weeks of follow-up. Complete or partial rupture of the distal biceps tendon was recorded based on intraoperative findings. The resulting cohort of 84 arms was analyzed with rerupture as the primary endpoint. Secondary endpoints were nonrerupture complications, reoperation, operative time, and tourniquet time.ResultsOut of 84 arms, 52 (61.9%) were complete ruptures and 32 (38.1%) were partial ruptures. There were no documented reruptures and a total complication rate of 22/84 (26.2%). The mean operative time was 78.2 ± 21.4 minutes with a mean tourniquet time of 66.9 ± 19.2 minutes. Seventeen arms had nonoperatively managed lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve paresthesias, one had nonoperatively managed heterotopic ossification, and one had persistent tendinitis. The return to OR rate was 3.6%, with one heterotopic ossification excision, one lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve neurolysis, and one tenolysis for soft tissue tethering.ConclusionsThis study supports the use of SPOC distal biceps repair for both partial and complete biceps ruptures, with a low rerupture rate, similar complication rate to other commonly used techniques, and low return to OR rate.Type of study/level of evidencePrognostic IV.Key words: Complication, Distal biceps, Rupture, Single-incision, Surgical outcome

The single-incision power optimizing cost-effective (SPOC) technique for distal biceps tendon repair employs a single anterior incision to reattach the distal biceps tendon to the posterior and ulnar surface of the radial tuberosity, preserving its native anatomical site. Introduced by Tanner et al1 in 2013, this technique has been shown to maximize supination strength while minimizing surgical morbidity through a single-incision approach that eliminates the need for implants or anchors.2,3 By drilling smaller holes and using transosseous bone tunnels and suture fixation, the SPOC technique preserves bone stock and reduces cost. However, debate remains surrounding the optimal techniques for tendon fixation in distal biceps tendon repair, and as a result, technique choice largely depends on surgeon preference.4, 5, 6

Conventional fixation techniques include cortical suture buttons, interference screws, suture anchors, and bone tunnels, the last of which includes the SPOC technique.7 The other techniques outside of bone tunnels often require large cortical drill holes or bicortical drilling. For example, cortical suture button fixation, though biomechanically strong and conducive to tensioning the graft,8 risks posterior interosseous nerve entrapment and necessitates either a large bicortical tunnel or placement into the medullary canal—both of which compromise bone stock and may result in nonanatomic tendon placement.9, 10, 11, 12 Although a double-incision approach mitigates these risks, it can lead to a greater chance of heterotopic ossifications and radioulnar synostosis.13,14 Interference screws can augment fixation strength but require even larger drill holes and introduce the potential for radial tuberosity osteolysis and iatrogenic proximal radius fractures due to high insertional stress.15 Anchor fixation, although technically straightforward, is costly and can result in suboptimal tendon-to-bone contact, especially when placed in a nonanatomic position.9,16 Furthermore, a systematic review by Watson et al6 showed that bone tunnel and cortical button fixation had significantly lower complication rates compared with suture anchors and intraosseous screw fixation.

Bone tunnel fixation techniques such as SPOC involve drilling smaller, unicortical holes and passing suture through the radius to secure the tendon directly to its anatomic footprint on the ulnar and posterior aspect of the radial tuberosity. This avoids many of the complications associated with implant-based techniques while restoring native tendon biomechanics, and the SPOC technique offers the theoretical benefits of improved healing through bone-to-tendon contact, decreased risk to neurovascular structures, reduced implant cost, and preservation of bone stock.

Despite these potential advantages, limited data exist regarding long-term outcomes following SPOC repair. A single institutional study demonstrated satisfactory patient-reported outcomes with a total complication rate of 21.1% and a major complication rate of 5.9%, comparable to rates reported with other techniques.17 The goal of the present study is to evaluate the long-term outcomes and complication rates associated with the SPOC technique for distal biceps tendon repair. We hypothesize that SPOC will demonstrate complication rates similar to or lower than those of other fixation techniques while achieving full restoration of forearm motion and function.

Materials and Methods

Charts were reviewed retrospectively for all patients who underwent primary distal biceps tendon repair using the SPOC technique at a single academic tertiary care center between 2016 and 2024. Procedures were performed by two fellowship-trained upper extremity surgeons. The treating surgeons diagnosed the distal biceps injury based on patient history, physical examination, and review of the preoperative magnetic resonance imaging. Patients with a full-thickness distal biceps rupture were offered repair. Patients with partial distal biceps tears on magnetic resonance imaging were offered both surgery and nonoperative treatment. Electronic medical records, operative reports, and postoperative clinic documentation were examined to obtain demographic information, injury characteristics, operative details, and postoperative outcomes. Operative time and tourniquet time were extracted from anesthesia and operating room records.

Inclusion criteria were primary SPOC distal biceps repair and a minimum of 12 weeks of postoperative follow-up. Exclusion criteria included revision distal biceps surgery, use of allograft tissue, non-SPOC repair technique, and less than 12 weeks of follow-up. Complete or partial rupture of the distal biceps tendon was recorded for each case based on intraoperative findings. The primary outcome was rerupture, defined as clinical or imaging-confirmed loss of fixation documented by the treating surgeon. Secondary outcomes included nonrerupture complications, reoperation, operative time, and tourniquet time. All analyses were descriptive; continuous variables were summarized using means and standard deviations or medians and interquartile ranges, and categorical variables were summarized using counts and percentages.

This study received approval from the University of Pittsburgh Institutional Review Board (STUDY20070308), with waiver of informed consent and HIPAA authorization due to its retrospective cohort design. The investigation was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.

Surgical technique

The SPOC technique was performed as originally described by Tanner et al.1 Patients were positioned supine with a tourniquet applied to the upper arm. A single anterior longitudinal incision of approximately 3–4 cm was made over the antecubital fossa, centered over the bicipital tuberosity. The lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve (LABCN) was identified and protected throughout the procedure. The distal biceps tendon was identified and, in the setting of a complete rupture, retrieved proximally. For partial ruptures, the degenerative portion of the tendon was debrided at the footprint prior to repair. The forearm was fully supinated to expose the radial tuberosity. The tendon was then secured using two running Krackow whipstitches with #2 FiberWire sutures. Two ∼2.5-mm drill holes were placed along the posterior ulnar surface of the radial tuberosity approximately 10–15 mm apart to recreate the tendon’s native anatomical footprint. A spinal needle was passed through each drill hole, and a right-angle clamp was positioned posteriorly between the radius and ulna to retrieve a 0-Prolene shuttle suture. The shuttle sutures were used to pass the repair sutures posteriorly through the radius and back out anteriorly. With the forearm in full supination and the elbow at approximately 90° of flexion, the tendon was reduced onto the posterior surface of the radial tuberosity, and the sutures were tied over the anterior cortical bone bridge between the two drill holes. Postoperative protocol was varied; generally, all patients were immobilized in a splint or sling immediately postoperatively for approximately 2 weeks, then changed to a sling or hinged elbow brace with physical therapy or occupational therapy referrals as needed. No heterotopic ossification prophylaxis was given.

Results

A total of 264 patients who underwent distal biceps tendon repair during the study period were initially identified. Of these, 105 patients underwent repair using the SPOC technique. After excluding patients who underwent revision surgery, required allograft augmentation, underwent a non-SPOC repair, or had fewer than 12 weeks of follow-up, 84 arms remained for final analysis.

The cohort consisted of 78 males (92.9%) and 6 females (7.1%), with a mean age of 48.4 years (range, 25.6–71.2 years). Median follow-up was 28.9 weeks (interquartile range [IQR] 16.1–57.5 weeks). Among the included cases, 52 repairs (61.9%) were performed for complete ruptures, and 32 repairs (38.1%) were for partial ruptures (Table 1). There were no documented reruptures during the follow-up period.

A total of 22 complications occurred (22/84, 26.2%). When subgrouping by complete or partial rupture, those with complete ruptures had a complication rate of 15.4% (8/52) while those with partial ruptures had a complication rate of 43.8% (14/32). The most common complication was LABCN paresthesia, occurring in 18 patients, 6 of whom had complete rupture versus 12 with partial rupture. Two patients developed heterotopic ossification (HO), 1 of whom required operative excision. Additional complications included one case of persistent tendinitis and one case of soft tissue tethering resulting in restricted motion and requiring tenolysis. Discussion

The results of this study support the use of the SPOC technique for the repair of both complete and partial distal biceps tendon ruptures. In our cohort of 84 cases, there were no reruptures, and the overall complication rate was 26.2%, with 3.6% of patients requiring a return to the operating room—rates consistent with those reported in the current literature. In a meta-analysis of complications after distal biceps tendon repair by Amin et al,13 the overall complication rate and rerupture rate in the single-incision group were 28.3% and 2.1%, respectively, compared with 20.9% and 0.6%, respectively, in the double-incision group. A more recent systematic review by Amarasooriya et al18 found an overall complication rate of 25% with a 4.6% rate of major complications and a 20.4% rate of minor complications. They further analyzed complications by fixation technique, with bone tunnels resulting in a rate of 3.8% major complications and 16% minor complications; however, this was not further stratified by single- or double-incision approach. When looking at the single-incision approach, this study found a 2.9% rate of major complications and 16.3% rate of minor complications. The minor complication rate in our SPOC cohort (22.6%) was slightly higher than that reported in this systematic review, potentially attributable to the tendency to underreport complications—particularly subjective minor complications such as numbness—in the literature, which the authors acknowledge.18

The most common complication observed in our SPOC cohort was LABCN paresthesia, occurring in 21.4% of cases, which is also consistent with previous literature.19 This is a known complication following distal biceps tendon repair due to the nerve’s close proximity to the anterior approach and likely reflects traction or manipulation during dissection. Importantly, all but one case was transient or managed nonoperatively, suggesting that although common, this complication is typically self-limited. Heterotopic ossification was rare, with two cases and only one case requiring excision. This aligns with prior evidence suggesting that single-incision techniques reduce the risk of HO compared with double-incision repairs.13

Notably, our study found a higher complication rate observed in patients undergoing SPOC repair for partial ruptures (43.8%) compared with those undergoing repair for complete ruptures (15.4%). The majority of complications in both groups were LABCN paresthesias, accounting for 12 of 14 complications (85.7%) in the partial rupture group versus 6 of 8 (75.0%) in the complete rupture group. This disparity is consistent with current literature, as a retrospective review by Sane et al19 found significantly higher rates of LABCN paresthesia between patients with partial biceps tendon tear (42%) versus complete biceps tendon rupture (22%). This is likely due to degenerative, inflammatory changes and fibrosis in close proximity to the LABCN associated with partial ruptures,20 which require more extensive dissection in the vicinity of the LABCN and traction on the nerve, and the delayed repair of partial ruptures, which has been shown to increase the rate of LABCN paresthesia.21,22 Overall, the results of our study are consistent with previously reported complication rates in the literature, indicating that the SPOC technique is a safe and effective option for primary distal biceps tendon repair while reducing surgical cost. From a value-based care perspective, SPOC provides important cost advantages. By avoiding implants, the technique eliminates a substantial portion of direct operative costs associated with anchors, buttons, or screws. Coupled with the low reoperation rate observed in this cohort, SPOC offers a cost-conscious option without compromising safety or effectiveness.

An important consideration in evaluating the cost-effectiveness of the SPOC technique is whether it is associated with longer operative or tourniquet times compared with other distal biceps repair techniques. In our cohort, the mean operative time was 78.2 ± 21.4 minutes with a mean tourniquet time of 66.9 ± 19.2 minutes. Lang et al23 found average operative times of 79.8 ± 19.6 minutes for single-incision suture anchor fixation, 91.5 ± 13.7 minutes for single-incision cortical bone fixation, and 76.5 ± 22.8 minutes for double-incision transosseous suture repair fixation, with the price of cortical buttons being a third higher than the price of two suture anchors and far exceeding the price of transosseous sutures. Cognetti et al24 performed a cost analysis between single-incision repairs using either cortical button or suture anchor fixation and double-incision repairs using transosseous suture fixation. They reported a median tourniquet time and procedure length of 31 minutes (IQR 27–40) and 44 minutes (IQR 39–54), respectively, for single-incision repairs and 68 minutes (IQR 61–75) and 110 minutes (IQR 103–113), respectively, for double-incision repairs, with a significantly lower total surgical cost for double-incision repairs. Although the SPOC technique does not fall squarely into either of these categories, as it represents a single-incision transosseous suture fixation technique, these studies suggest that it has operative times comparable to other techniques. Given our cohort’s shorter operative time compared with the double-incision repairs reported in Cognetti et al’s24 cost analysis, the SPOC technique may offer even greater cost savings relative to other single-incision techniques.

This study has several limitations. The retrospective design and reliance on chart review introduce inherent biases, including underreporting of minor complications. Functional outcomes such as the disabilities of the arm, shoulder and hand outcome questionnaire or Mayo Elbow Performance Score were not routinely collected, limiting our ability to formally quantify return to function. The study was performed at a single institution with two surgeons, which may affect generalizability. Finally, the median follow-up period was just over half a year, and longer-term tendon healing and durability of repair remain to be established. Future studies should incorporate prospective, multicenter designs with standardized functional outcome measures and cost analyses. Randomized comparisons between SPOC and anchor- or button-based fixation would further clarify relative efficacy, particularly with respect to tendon healing, strength restoration, and long-term patient-reported outcomes.
