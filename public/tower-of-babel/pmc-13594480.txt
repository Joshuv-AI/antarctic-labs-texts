Impact of end-stage renal disease on stroke risk in patients with atrial fibrillation
Authors: Abdallah El Bizri, Hadi Itani, Ahmad Mustafa, John A. Afif, Michel El Khoury, Chapman Wei, Suzanne El-Sayegh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD) and atrial fibrillation (AF) face paradoxical risks of both thrombosis and bleeding, creating uncertainty regarding stroke risk and optimal anticoagulation (AC) strategies.ObjectivesThis study evaluated the impact of ESRD on stroke risk and mortality in patients with AF using a large national cohort.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted using the National Inpatient Sample database for 2018–2019. Adult patients (≥18 years) with AF were stratified by ESRD status. Propensity score matching (1:1) was performed using baseline demographic and comorbidity variables. Primary outcomes included ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, transient ischemic attack (TIA), and all-cause mortality.ResultsOf 1,749,172 patients with AF, 89,741 (5.1%) had ESRD. After propensity matching (68,653 pairs), patients with ESRD showed significantly lower rates of ischemic stroke (1.8% vs 3.1%; P < .001), hemorrhagic stroke (0.3% vs 0.8%; P < .001), and TIA (0.5% vs 0.6%; P < .001) than patients without ESRD. Patients with ESRD had lower odds of ischemic stroke (odds ratio [OR], 0.59; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.55–0.63), hemorrhagic stroke (OR, 0.37; 95% CI, 0.31–0.43), and TIA (OR, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.65–0.88) (all P < .001). These findings persisted despite lower rates of oral AC use among patients with ESRD (24.7% vs 29.5%; P < .001). All-cause mortality was substantially higher in patients with ESRD (8.6% vs 4.6%; P < .001; OR, 1.95; 95% CI, 1.87–2.04).ConclusionPatients with AF with ESRD had lower cerebrovascular event rates despite lower AC use, suggesting that uremic platelet dysfunction and intradialytic heparin exposure may confer protective AC effects. These findings challenge traditional stroke risk assumptions and highlight the need for ESRD-specific risk-stratification tools and individualized shared decision making.

Key Findings ▪Patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD) and atrial fibrillation (AF) had nearly double the all-cause in-hospital mortality compared with the matched sample, suggesting a major competing risk that may mask the actual stroke events.▪Even with fewer strokes, the markedly higher mortality in ESRD suggests that many patients may die of cardiovascular or other causes before realizing long-term benefits from anticoagulation, complicating net clinical benefit calculations.▪Current CHA2DS2-VASc–based stroke risk assessment is insufficient for patients with AF with ESRD, highlighting the need for ESRD-specific risk-stratification tools and individualized shared decision making, including consideration of left atrial appendage closure in patients with a high risk of bleeding.

Introduction

Patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD) are at a higher risk of cardiovascular complications, which remain the highest cause of mortality in these patients.1 Patients with ESRD have a particularly high risk of cerebrovascular incidents, which is further intensified by the presence of atrial fibrillation (AF).1 Almost 25% of patients with ESRD have AF.2 This is attributable to shared risk factors, including age, hypertension (HTN), diabetes mellitus, and peripheral vascular disease (PVD).1,2 AF is known to cause a hypercoagulable state through endothelial dysfunction, platelet aggregation, and stasis of blood in the left atrium.3 In a study by Providência et al,3 markers of left atrial stasis, including left atrial thrombus, dense spontaneous echocardiographic contrast, and low flow velocities, were more pronounced with decreasing estimated glomerular filtration rate. AF, particularly permanent AF, has been shown to confer a higher risk of cerebrovascular accidents in patients with ESRD, even when the CHA2DS2-VASc score is 0, with approximately a 5-fold risk. Stroke carries an elevated mortality rate in patients with ESRD, with a more than 33% mortality rate owing to the event and a larger rate of mortality in 1 year.4, 5, 6, 7

Although patients with ESRD are at a high risk of thrombotic events, they also have a high risk of bleeding episodes owing to several factors.8 Bleeding occurs in approximately 50% of patients, ranging from minor bruises to major gastrointestinal bleeding, retroperitoneal bleeding, perioperative bleeding, and detrimental intracranial hemorrhage.8 This elevated risk can be attributed to platelet dysfunction in uremia and impaired interaction between platelets and the vessel wall. In addition, platelet activation from continuous contact with artificial surfaces and from the use of heparin or heparin-related products during hemodialysis increases the risk of bleeding.9, 10, 11

With the high risk of thrombosis incurred in patients with ESRD and AF and the simultaneous bleeding risk owing to ESRD, the use of anticoagulation (AC) in these patients is challenging. Although the American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology guideline recommends AC in these patients, the European Society of Cardiology guideline notes the lack of convincing evidence for the benefit of oral AC (OAC) in patients with ESRD.12,13 Pokorney et al14 demonstrated that the use of OACs in patients with AF and ESRD showed an elevated risk of bleeding without a lower risk of stroke or mortality.

Given that ESRD puts patients at risk of bleeding and thrombosis simultaneously, it is critical to identify patients with ESRD with AF who are at a higher risk of strokes or bleeding. Although the CHA2DS2-VASc score is recommended for assessing AC need, it does not include ESRD or kidney disease in its scoring system.15 Other scoring systems, such as the Anticoagulant and Risk Factors in AF, the Global Anticoagulant Registry in the FIELD AF, and the Outcomes Registry for Better Informed Treatment, demonstrated better discrimination and calibration for major bleeding events.15, 16, 17

Methods

A retrospective cohort study was conducted using the National Inpatient Sample (NIS) database for 2018–2019. The NIS is the largest publicly available all-payer inpatient health care database in the United States, designed to produce national estimates of hospitalizations and outcomes.18

The study cohort included adult patients (≥18 years of age) with a primary or secondary diagnosis of AF. Patients were stratified based on the presence or absence of ESRD. The study also included their complete demographic and clinical data. Demographic variables collected included age, sex, and race/ethnicity. Clinical comorbidities and baseline characteristics were identified using International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, diagnosis codes (Supplemental Table 1). They included the following: diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, coronary artery disease (CAD), chronic heart failure (HF), carotid artery disease, PVD, HTN, dyslipidemia (DLD), smoking history, morbid obesity (body mass index ≥40 kg/m2), obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), and chronic liver disease. In addition, baseline medications were documented, including use of OACs (warfarin or direct OACs), aspirin, other antiplatelet agents, and previous venous thromboembolism (deep vein thrombosis/pulmonary embolism) status.

The primary outcomes of interest were (1) ischemic stroke, (2) hemorrhagic stroke, (3) transient ischemic attack (TIA), and (4) all-cause mortality during the follow-up period. Outcomes were identified using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, diagnosis and procedure codes. Secondary outcomes included stratified analyses by AC status, dialysis modality (for patients with ESRD), and cardiovascular mortality.

Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Baseline characteristics were compared between the ESRD and non-ESRD cohorts using independent-samples t tests for continuous variables and χ2 tests for categorical variables. P < .05 was considered to be statistically significant. Those with P < .2 were included in the multivariate analysis.

To account for confounding and reduce selection bias, propensity score matching (PSM) was performed using logistic regression. The propensity score was estimated as the probability of having ESRD conditional on baseline covariates, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, CAD, chronic HF, carotid artery disease, PVD, HTN, DLD, smoking status, morbid obesity, OSA, chronic liver disease, OAC use, aspirin use, other antiplatelet use, and previous deep vein thrombosis/pulmonary embolism. Patients with ESRD were matched 1:1 with patients without ESRD using nearest-neighbor matching with a caliper width of 0.20 standard deviations of the logit of the propensity score.

After PSM, both univariate and multivariable analyses were performed. Univariate comparisons between matched ESRD and non-ESRD groups were conducted using χ2 tests for categorical variables. Multivariable binary logistic regression was performed to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for each outcome, adjusting for all baseline covariates included in the propensity score.

The NIS contains deidentified discharge-level data. In accordance with institutional policy and federal regulations, this study was considered exempt from institutional review board review and informed consent requirements.

All analyses were performed using SPSS version 28.

Discussion

This retrospective observational study shows a paradoxical finding regarding stroke risk in patients with AF and ESRD. Despite ESRD being an established independent risk factor for cerebrovascular accidents and AF conferring additional thrombotic risk, propensity-matched analysis revealed that patients with AF with ESRD had significantly lower odds of ischemic stroke (OR, 0.59; 95% CI, 0.55–0.63), hemorrhagic stroke (OR, 0.37; 95% CI, 0.31–0.43), and TIA (OR, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.65–0.88) than patients with AF without ESRD. This finding persisted despite lower rates of OAC use in the ESRD cohort (24.7% vs 29.5%; P < .001), suggesting that factors inherent to ESRD management may confer stroke protection that outweighs the combined prothrombotic effects of ESRD and AF.1,17

Although patients with ESRD and AF are considered to be at higher thromboembolic risk than the general population, this study’s findings suggest a more complex relationship between prothrombotic and AC forces in this subpopulation.19 The present study stratified AF as a binary diagnosis only without information on AF type (ie, paroxysmal, persistent, permanent) or quantitative AF burden. Emerging evidence suggests that AF burden carries significant prognostic value beyond the presence or lack of AF, influencing the risk of stroke, HF, and mortality. Current evidence shows that AF burden can fluctuate substantially over time and may more accurately reflect arrhythmic risk than a single diagnostic label.20,21 Thus, the present findings should be interpreted as relating to clinical AF diagnosis rather than to burden-specific risk in ESRD.

In the present cohort of 1,749,172 patients with AF, those with ESRD (n = 89,741; 5.1%) had markedly lower rates of ischemic stroke (1.8% vs 3.1%; P < .001), hemorrhagic stroke (0.3% vs 0.8%; P < .001), and TIA (0.5% vs 0.6%; P < .001) than propensity-matched patients with AF without ESRD in the univariate analysis, despite significantly lower rates of OAC use in the ESRD cohort (24.7% vs 29.5%; P < .001). These findings reflect the combined effects of intrinsic ESRD-related hemostatic abnormalities, dialysis-related AC, and the high competing risk of cardiovascular death, rather than a simple predominance of AC over prothrombotic mechanisms.22

According to the United States Renal Data System, hemodialysis remains the mainstay of renal replacement therapy, and routine use of heparin during dialysis to prevent extracorporeal circuit clotting may provide intermittent systemic AC exposure in a substantial proportion of patients with ESRD.23 However, most AF-related ischemic strokes are presumed embolic from large left atrial or left atrial appendage thrombi and are primarily thrombin mediated, which is why vitamin K antagonists and direct OACs are the standard of care for stroke prevention in AF rather than antiplatelet therapy.24,25 The NIS dataset did not allow adjudication of ischemic strokes as embolic vs thrombotic in etiology, thereby limiting the detection of the mechanisms underlying these strokes.

In this context, the lower ischemic stroke rate observed in our ESRD cohort (1.8% vs 3.1%) despite lower OAC use (24.7% vs 29.5%) should be interpreted as hypothesis generating. 1 possible explanation is that uremic platelet dysfunction, combined with intermittent heparin exposure during dialysis, may contribute to a partially AC state in some patients, thereby modifying stroke risk.9 The 41% reduction in ischemic stroke odds (OR, 0.59; 95% CI, 0.55–0.63) and 63% reduction in hemorrhagic stroke odds (OR, 0.37; 95% CI, 0.31–0.43) in the multivariable logistic regression analysis after propensity matching are consistent with a change in the thrombotic and bleeding balance, but do not prove a protective effect of uremia against embolic stroke or justify withholding guideline-directed AC. Rather, these data underscore the need for ESRD-specific risk stratification and for cautious, individualized decision making when weighing stroke prevention against bleeding risk in this high-risk population.

The ESRD population is markedly heterogeneous, and clinical outcomes may vary substantially based on renal replacement modality.26 Although our study did not stratify outcomes by dialysis modality, the demographic characteristics of our ESRD cohort showed significant differences from patients without ESRD that may influence stroke risk. Patients with ESRD were younger (mean age 69.06 vs 74.30 years; P < .001) and had higher rates of diabetes mellitus (62.6% vs 35.1%; P < .001), HTN (94.7% vs 81.5%; P < .001), CAD (48.9% vs 38.6%; P < .001), and chronic HF (41.0% vs 38.1%; P < .001), yet showed lower cerebrovascular event rates. Existing evidence suggests essential differences between hemodialysis and peritoneal dialysis. Meta-analyses have shown that peritoneal dialysis patients experience a 22% lower hemorrhagic stroke rate than hemodialysis patients (hazard ratio, 0.78; 95% CI, 0.69–0.88), potentially attributable to the absence of routine AC and to better blood pressure control with peritoneal dialysis.27 However, some studies report higher ischemic stroke rates in peritoneal dialysis patients, particularly in non-Asian populations, possibly related to altered glucose metabolism, hypervolemia, and exposure to glycated end products from dialysate.28 Future analyses should stratify ESRD cohorts by dialysis modality to better characterize stroke risk profiles and inform personalized management strategies.

Another factor that may influence the net AC milieu in ESRD is the temporal clustering of AF episodes around hemodialysis sessions. Implantable device studies have shown that AF onset is more frequent on hemodialysis days and particularly during the dialysis procedure itself, when systemic heparin is routinely administered.29,30 Although the administrative dataset used in this study did not capture the timing of AF relative to dialysis or heparin dosing, it should be considered that this temporal association, together with uremic platelet dysfunction, may contribute to a lower stroke risk.

The CHA2DS2-VASc score does not account for renal function, despite chronic kidney disease being an independent predictor of mortality and bleeding.31 Our ESRD cohort had higher rates of stroke risk factors, including diabetes (62.6% vs 35.1%), HTN (94.7% vs 81.5%), CAD (48.9% vs 38.6%), and chronic HF (41.0% vs 38.1%), yet experienced lower stroke rates (1.8% vs 3.1%; P < .001).32 Modified scores such as R2CHADS2-VASc, which award 2 additional points for renal dysfunction, demonstrate superior predictive value for mortality compared with standard CHA2DS2-VASc.33 However, Li et al32 demonstrated that renal dysfunction may not independently predict thromboembolism and is, in fact, associated with major bleeding and mortality. Alternative bleeding risk scores, including the Anticoagulant and Risk Factors in AF, the Global Anticoagulant Registry in the FIELD AF, and the Outcomes Registry for Better Informed Treatment, incorporate renal function and have better discrimination for bleeding events; however, their predictive performance remains modest in patients with advanced CKD.34

Evidence suggests that cerebral microbleeds are highly prevalent in hemodialysis patients and are a strong independent predictor of future intracerebral hemorrhage (hazard ratio, 26.53; 95% CI, 2.88–244.90), but not of cerebral infarction.35 Patients with ≥10 microbleeds experience a higher risk of intracranial hemorrhage (adjusted OR, 14.56; 95% CI, 2.86–74.16).36 The presence of cerebral microbleeds may help explain why AC in ESRD populations causes bleeding complications without commensurate stroke reduction, and may partially account for the dramatically lower hemorrhagic stroke rate observed in our ESRD cohort (0.3% vs 0.8%; P < .001; OR, 0.37; 95% CI, 0.31–0.43), given that patients at highest bleeding risk may have been selectively excluded from AC therapy.37 This is likely explained by selection and competing risk rather than by the protection conferred by microbleeds. Clinicians may have preferentially avoided or discontinued AC in patients with ESRD perceived to be at the highest bleeding risk (such as those with known cerebral microbleeds, previous intracranial hemorrhage, or severe uremic bleeding diathesis), whereas patients with lower bleeding risk were more likely to receive and continue AC.38

Our study showed that only 24.7% of patients with ESRD with AF received OAC compared with 29.5% of patients without ESRD (P < .001), reflecting clinicians’ hesitancy and underscoring the need for alternative approaches. Left atrial appendage closure (LAAC) has emerged as a viable option for stroke prevention in patients at high bleeding risk.25,39 Recent studies specifically in ESRD populations demonstrate that LAAC is associated with significantly lower rates of the composite outcome of stroke, systemic embolism, major bleeding, and mortality than both warfarin and apixaban.25,39 The reduction is primarily driven by decreased rates of major and gastrointestinal bleeding, with potentially fewer ischemic events as well.40,41 Although procedural complications may be twice as common in patients with ESRD compared with those without renal disease, LAAC remains a relatively safe procedure with acceptable complication rates.40,41

Despite the lower cerebrovascular event rates observed in our ESRD cohort (ischemic stroke 1.8% vs 3.1%; hemorrhagic stroke 0.3% vs 0.8%; TIA 0.5% vs 0.6%; all P < .001), all-cause mortality was substantially higher (8.6% vs 4.6% in the matched analysis; P < .001; OR, 1.95; 95% CI, 1.87–2.04), consistent with the well-established excess mortality burden in ESRD populations.42,43 Cardiovascular disease accounts for approximately 48% of deaths in ESRD, with the 5-year survival rate for patients with ESRD estimated at only 35%.42,43 This high mortality risk complicates stroke-prevention strategies, given that patients may die from cardiovascular or other causes before achieving the long-term benefits of AC. The nearly 2-fold rise in mortality observed in our ESRD cohort underscores this risk. It raises questions about the net clinical benefit of AC in a population with limited life expectancy. Another explanation is that the nearly 2-fold higher all-cause in-hospital mortality in patients with ESRD with AF represents a major competing risk that masks the observed incidence of stroke. That is, patients with ESRD may die from cardiovascular or other causes before experiencing a cerebrovascular event, so the lower stroke rate in our analysis may reflect survival bias and competing risk rather than a true protective effect of AF or ESRD on stroke. In addition, given the conflicting guideline recommendations, that is, the American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology endorses AC whereas the European Society of Cardiology notes insufficient evidence for benefit in ESRD, and recent network meta-analyses suggesting that no AC may be a noninferior alternative with vitamin K antagonists associated with the worst outcomes, a shared decision-making approach is essential.12,13,44,45

Limitations

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, as a retrospective observational study using administrative data, our findings require validation in prospective cohorts. Although PSM was used to balance baseline characteristics, unmeasured confounding cannot be ruled out. Second, the study did not stratify OAC by agent type (warfarin vs direct OACs), dosage, or duration of therapy, which may significantly affect outcomes. The quality of AC control, as measured by time in therapeutic range for warfarin users, was not assessed. Third, patients with ESRD represent a heterogeneous population encompassing hemodialysis (in-center and home-based), peritoneal dialysis, and pre-emptive transplant recipients, each with distinct hemostatic profiles. Our analysis did not stratify by dialysis modality, vascular access type, dialysis vintage, or adequacy parameters, all of which may influence cerebrovascular outcomes. Fourth, critical clinical variables such as cerebral microbleed burden, left atrial appendage thrombus, and biomarkers of thrombosis and bleeding were not captured in the database, which potentially introduces residual confounding. Fifth, the study period and follow-up duration were not specified, limiting assessment of temporal trends and long-term outcomes. Furthermore, the lower event rates in the ESRD group may partially reflect the competing risk of mortality, given that patients with ESRD may die before experiencing cerebrovascular events. Finally, the present study did not identify or adjust for LAAC procedures. As a result, findings may underestimate the use and potential impact of the procedure in this population. The reliance on adjusted and propensity-matched analyses may not fully reflect the absolute, unadjusted risk profile of patients with ESRD with AF that clinicians must consider in practice. Although these methods help reduce confounding, they cannot capture the full complexity of competing risks, multimorbidity, and frailty that shape real-world net clinical benefit and should therefore be interpreted as complementary to, rather than a replacement for, the observed crude risk in this high-risk population.

Conclusion

In this large propensity-matched cohort study, patients with AF with ESRD demonstrated significantly lower rates of ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, and TIA than patients with AF without ESRD, despite lower OAC use. These findings challenge traditional assumptions about stroke risk in this population and suggest that the AC effects of uremic platelet dysfunction and routine intradialytic heparin exposure may outweigh the combined prothrombotic effects of ESRD and AF. However, the substantially elevated mortality in patients with ESRD underscores the complexity of clinical decision making in this vulnerable population. Furthermore, the lower event rates in the ESRD group may partially reflect the competing risk of mortality, given that patients with ESRD may die before experiencing cerebrovascular events. Finally, the present study did not identify or adjust for LAAC procedures. As a result, findings may underestimate the use and potential impact of the procedure in this population. The reliance on adjusted and propensity-matched analyses may not fully reflect the absolute, unadjusted risk profile of patients with ESRD with AF that clinicians must consider in practice. Although these methods help reduce confounding, they cannot capture the full complexity of competing risks, multimorbidity, and frailty that shape real-world net clinical benefit and should therefore be interpreted as complementary to, rather than a replacement for, the observed crude risk in this high-risk population.

Prospective randomized controlled trials, ESRD-specific risk-stratification tools, and comparative effectiveness research on stroke-prevention strategies are critically needed to establish evidence-based guidelines and optimize outcomes for this high-risk population.
