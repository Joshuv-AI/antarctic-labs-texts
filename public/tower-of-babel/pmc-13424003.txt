The effect of an indirect anchorage reinforcement device on maxillary anterior intrusion with clear aligners: a three-dimensional finite element analysis and exploratory clinical validation
Authors: Zexuan Yang, Qiong Liu, Zhijie Yang, Yirong Hu, Bingguo Kou, Yue Hui, Siyu Lan, Zexu Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnterior intrusion is commonly incorporated into clear aligner protocols to correct deep overbite and maintain overbite control during anterior retraction. However, anterior intrusion remains difficult to achieve predictably, potentially due to insufficient vertical anchorage of the posterior segment. This study proposed an indirect anchorage reinforcement (IAR) device to guide occlusal contact and indirectly reinforce posterior vertical anchorage, and evaluated its biomechanical effects and clinical performance.MethodsA two part study was conducted. First, three-dimensional finite element analysis simulated initial responses in anterior intrusion (Model A) and combined anterior intrusion and retraction with first premolar extraction (Model B). Each model included a Control and an IAR condition (A-Control, A-IAR, B-Control, B-IAR). A 0.2 mm activation was applied for intrusion, and Model B additionally included a 0.2 mm activation for retraction. A 200 g vertical load was applied to the IAR device to simulate occlusal contact. Outcomes included initial tooth displacement, sagittal axial inclination, the sagittal force–moment system, and aligner displacement and von Mises stress. Second, a matched exploratory clinical study with a historical control evaluated a maxillary anterior intrusion protocol. The planned total intrusion was 2 mm, delivered in 10 aligners at 0.2 mm per aligner, with no other programmed tooth movements during these steps. Aligners alone were compared with aligners incorporating the IAR device. Intrusion accuracy was quantified by three-dimensional model superimposition and calculated as achieved divided by planned intrusion. Propensity score overlap weighting and overlap-weighted regression were used to compare the groups.ResultsIn finite element simulations, the IAR design increased anterior intrusion and suppressed posterior extrusion, with smaller axial inclination changes and a reduced moment arm d, consistent with a resultant force acting closer to the center of resistance. Clinically, the IAR group achieved higher intrusion accuracy. In overlap-weighted analyses, the weighted mean difference (IAR minus control) was 18.96% points for incisors (95% confidence interval 9.01–28.91; P < 0.001) and 33.56% points for canines (95% confidence interval 24.65–42.46; P < 0.001). Intra-examiner reliability was good (intraclass correlation coefficient = 0.85).ConclusionAcross both non-extraction and extraction scenarios, the IAR concept improved the achievement and predictability of anterior intrusion while suppressing extrusion of the posterior anchorage teeth, indicating improved vertical anchorage control. The exploratory clinical findings supported its clinical feasibility, with a more pronounced improvement in canine intrusion.

Background

Maxillary protrusion often requires substantial anterior retraction to improve the facial profile [1]. Because orthodontic forces are typically applied occlusal to the tooth center of resistance, anterior retraction is frequently accompanied by overbite deepening, which may lead to premature anterior contacts [2]. Such contacts can increase anterior tooth mobility and contribute to traumatic periodontitis and alveolar bone resorption, thereby limiting further retraction [3]. Deep overbite is also a major challenge in Class II correction [4]. Therefore, opening the bite is often a prerequisite for retraction, and anterior intrusion is a key step in deep overbite management.

With advances in materials science and computer-aided technologies, clear aligner therapy has been increasingly used to treat more complex malocclusions [5]. However, deep bite correction and substantial anterior retraction remain challenging with clear aligners [6]. Due to limitations in force delivery and anchorage control, anterior retraction with clear aligners may be accompanied by unwanted tooth movements, including overbite deepening, distal tipping of the canines, and canine extrusion [7]. Haouili et al. [8] reported intrusion accuracies of 33.4% and 44.6% for the maxillary central and lateral incisors, respectively. Khosravi et al. [9] reported that clear aligner therapy achieved only 1.5 mm of overbite reduction, and that the contributions of maxillary and mandibular incisor intrusion to overbite improvement were only 30% and 0%, respectively. Importantly, many previous evaluations were evaluated under treatment plans where multiple movements occurred concurrently rather than being assessed in isolation. Because clear aligners cover the entire dentition, the dentition functions as a three-dimensional interactive anchorage system, and movement of one segment may influence the others. In this context, the apparent accuracy of anterior intrusion may be overestimated when premolar extrusion is incorporated. When anterior intrusion is difficult to express, the programmed vertical discrepancy between the anterior teeth and premolars may be expressed predominantly on the premolars, manifesting as premolar extrusion. Consequently, although a vertical “step” consistent with the animation may be observed at follow-up, it may be fully attributable to premolar extrusion (Fig. 1). Clinically, extrusion of the maxillary posterior teeth may induce occlusal plane and mandibular rotation, potentially compromising facial harmony or offsetting the profile improvement achieved by anterior retraction [4].

In clinical practice, anterior intrusion is often planned together with retraction to counteract overbite deepening during retraction. Zhu et al. [10] and Zhang et al. [11] compared the biomechanical effects of adding intrusion overcorrection during retraction and reported reduced tendencies for extrusion and tipping of the canines and second premolars. Although some compromise in incisor inclination control may be clinically acceptable in protrusion cases, preventing overbite deepening during retraction remains essential. Therefore, improving the efficiency of anterior intrusion and stabilizing the vertical position of the anterior teeth remain clinically relevant. Haouili et al. [8] further suggested that the low accuracy of anterior intrusion may be attributable to insufficient posterior anchorage. Ideally, the posterior segment bearing most of the reaction force from anterior intrusion should remain stable. However, premolar extrusion compromises vertical anchorage during anterior intrusion and may reduce intrusion accuracy. Based on these considerations and clinical observations, we proposed a novel auxiliary device. An occlusal pad with a preset height was added to the middle segment of the clear aligner to guide occlusal contact and indirectly reinforce posterior anchorage, thereby improving the accuracy of anterior intrusion (Fig. 2A, B).

This study combined three-dimensional finite element analysis and a matched exploratory clinical study to provide complementary evidence. The finite element analysis characterized the biomechanical effects of the device in non-extraction and extraction scenarios. The clinical study provided clinical support for these findings by comparing actual anterior intrusion accuracy between the control and indirect anchorage reinforcement (IAR) groups, thereby informing a strategy to improve the predictability of anterior intrusion with clear aligners.

Methods

This study consisted of two parts. First, a three-dimensional finite element analysis was performed to simulate the initial biomechanical responses of anterior intrusion (Model A) and combined anterior intrusion and retraction (Model B). For each model, a control condition and an indirect anchorage reinforcement (IAR) condition were established to compare the biomechanical effects with and without the IAR design. Second, a matched exploratory clinical study was conducted using the loading scenario of Model A as the reference. Clinical outcomes were used to corroborate the finite element findings and to evaluate the clinical accuracy of anterior intrusion with the IAR device.

Finite element analysis

Establishment of the FE model

Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) data from one adult female volunteer were used as the source dataset. The participant had a complete dentition with no obvious abnormalities in tooth size or morphology and no periodontal disease, and showed slight proclination of the maxillary anterior teeth (U1–NA = 27°).

The DICOM-format CBCT data were imported into Mimics (Materialise, Belgium) to extract the maxillofacial bone and dentition. Geomagic Studio (3D Systems, USA) and SolidWorks (Dassault, USA) were then used to construct geometric models of the alveolar bone, teeth, periodontal ligament (PDL), clear aligner, attachments, and the indirect anchorage reinforcement (IAR) device (Fig. 2C). The alveolar bone was modeled as a layered structure, comprising an outer cortical shell (1.5 mm) and an inner cancellous core. The PDL was generated as a uniform layer by offsetting each root surface by 0.3 mm [12]. The clear aligner was created by outwardly offsetting the crowns and attachments by 0.75 mm [13]. The assembled model was meshed with tetrahedral elements in HyperMesh (2019, Altair, USA). The teeth and attachments were meshed with 0.3 mm first order tetrahedral elements, the cancellous bone with first order tetrahedral elements with a maximum element size of 5.0 mm, and the PDL and aligner with 0.3 mm modified quadratic tetrahedral elements. The model was then imported into ANSYS (19.0, ANSYS, USA) for analysis.

Except for the PDL, all materials were assumed to be homogeneous, isotropic, and linearly elastic. The PDL was modeled as a homogeneous and isotropic hyperelastic material to better reproduce its mechanical response under the small-displacement assumption [14, 15]. The elastic modulus and Poisson’s ratio of the teeth, PDL, alveolar bone, attachments, and aligner were adopted from previous studies [16, 17] (Table 1). Material parameters of the IAR device were set to be the same as those of the clear aligner (TPU). The IAR is designed with a thickness of 3 mm, which is consistent with the interocclusal rest space (freeway space).

The posterior maxilla was fully constrained in all degrees of freedom. The tooth–PDL and PDL–alveolar bone interfaces were defined as bonded contacts. The tooth–aligner interface was defined as sliding surface-to-surface contact with a friction coefficient of 0.2 (µ = 0.2) [18]. The base of the IAR device was rigidly coupled to the bonded aligner surface. For each tooth, a local coordinate system was established with the origin at the root geometric center. The x-axis was mesiodistal with the mesial direction defined as positive. The y-axis was labiolingual and buccolingual with the labial and buccal direction defined as positive. The z-axis was vertical with the gingival direction defined as positive (Fig. 2F).

Working conditions and loading method

Two three-dimensional finite element models were constructed (Fig. 2D): a full-arch anterior intrusion model (Model A) and an anterior intrusion–retraction model with first premolar extraction (Model B). Within each model, a Control condition and an indirect anchorage reinforcement (IAR) condition were defined, resulting in four simulation conditions: A-Control, A-IAR, B-Control, and B-IAR.

In Model A, A-Control was designed with 0.2 mm of anterior intrusion. A-IAR applied the same intrusion design (0.2 mm) and additionally incorporated an occlusal pad (bite pad) on the aligner at the first premolar region. In Model B, B-Control was designed with 0.2 mm of anterior intrusion and 0.2 mm of anterior retraction. B-IAR applied the same intrusion and retraction designs (0.2 mm each) and additionally incorporated an occlusal pad at the second premolar region. To simulate occlusal contact under intercuspation, a vertical load of 200 g was applied to the IAR device in both A-IAR and B-IAR [7, 19]. Loading was achieved via an interference-fit approach, using nonlinear contact with a penalty-based formulation [20]. The solution was computed under the initial interference condition between the teeth and the aligner until a converged mechanical equilibrium state was reached.

Outcome measures

After convergence, the results were imported into HyperView (2019, Altair, USA) for post-processing. The following outcomes were extracted: (1) initial displacement of the maxillary teeth, (2) sagittal axial inclination, (3) the sagittal force system at the center of resistance (CoR), including force, moment, and the moment arm (d), and (4) displacement and stress distributions of the clear aligner. To quantify tooth displacement, reference markers were defined at the center of the incisal edge for incisors, the cusp tip for canines, and the center of the occlusal surface for posterior teeth. The CoR was defined along the tooth long axis within the cervical to middle third of the root for single root teeth and in the furcation region for multirooted teeth.

Exploratory clinical analysis

Study design

This study was an exploratory ambidirectional cohort study using a historical control (non-concurrent cohort) design [21, 22]. The control group was retrospectively derived from the control arm of a previously conducted prospective randomized trial at our center (approval No.IRB-REV-2022003), which compared two clear aligner protocols for maxillary anterior intrusion (simultaneous intrusion and staged intrusion). The experimental group (IAR group) was enrolled prospectively using identical eligibility criteria, intervention procedures, and outcome assessment protocols. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the School of Stomatology, the Fourth Military Medical University (Approval No. KQ-YJ-2024-244).

Because the mandible lacks stable anatomical structures suitable for model superimposition, this study focused on the maxillary anterior teeth. The intervention consisted of clear aligner-based intrusion of the maxillary anterior teeth. In the control group, intrusion was performed with clear aligners alone. In the IAR group, an indirect anchorage reinforcement (IAR) device was added to assist anterior intrusion. The thickness of the IAR for each patient is adjusted to create a posterior tooth separation of 2 ~ 3 mm, which corresponds to the interocclusal rest space (freeway space). A total intrusion of 2 mm was planned and delivered in 10 aligners, with 0.2 mm of intrusion per aligner. To minimize the influence of concurrent tooth movements on the intrusion outcome, only anterior intrusion was programmed during these 10 steps, with no additional planned movements. In patients with anterior crowding, the intrusion protocol was initiated only after alignment and relief of crowding had been achieved. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age 18–45 years; (2) mild maxillary crowding with a planned need for maxillary anterior intrusion; (3) intact palatal rugae and palatal vault; (4) no obvious incisal wear of the anterior teeth; and (5) confirmed good compliance with aligner replacement every 7 days. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) missed appointments or poor compliance; (2) morphological changes of the observed teeth during treatment due to trauma or pulpal/endodontic disease; (3) damage to the palatal rugae or palatal vault during treatment; and (4) use of medications affecting bone metabolism (e.g., bisphosphonates). A total of 12 patients were included in the historical control group. A total of 16 patients were enrolled in the IAR group but 2 were excluded(1 patient had incisor trauma during treatment, and 1 patient did not complete aligner wear). The total number of teeth measured was 145 (104 incisors and 41 canines).

Intrusion accuracy was assessed by three-dimensional model superimposition between the initial (T1) and final (T2) models. The T1 model was imported into Geomagic Studio (3D Systems, USA) to establish a coordinate system, in which the occlusal plane was defined as the horizontal plane (XY plane) and the gingival direction was defined as positive. Under this convention, tooth intrusion was quantified as the displacement along the Z-axis [23, 24]. T1 and T2 were then imported into ProPlan CMF (3.0, Materialise, Belgium), and T2 was registered to T1 using the palatal vault as the reference. The registered models were subsequently re-imported into Geomagic Studio for landmark identification and measurements. According to previously reported three-dimensional measurement approaches [23, 25–27], the landmarks were defined as the occlusal point (O), mesial point (M), and distal point (D) for each anterior tooth. Specifically, the occlusal point was defined as the midpoint of the incisal edge for incisors and as the cusp tip for canines. For each tooth, the mean change in the Z-coordinate across these three landmarks was calculated and defined as the intrusion amount. The primary outcomes were maxillary incisor and canine intrusion accuracy. Intrusion accuracy was calculated as the ratio of achieved intrusion to planned intrusion, expressed as a percentage: intrusion accuracy (%) = (achieved/planned) × 100. The occlusal plane used to define the horizontal plane was constructed from the mesiobuccal cusps of the bilateral maxillary first molars and the incisal edge of the maxillary central incisor [23]. The palatal reference region comprised the second and third palatal rugae and the posterior palatal vault, extending to a level close to the distal contact lines of the bilateral maxillary first molars. These palatal structures have been reported to remain stable during orthodontic tooth movement and were therefore used as a stable reference for maxillary superimposition [28, 29].

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or number (percentage). Covariate balance was evaluated using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with |SMD| < 0.20 indicating acceptable balance [30]. Propensity scores were estimated by logistic regression using sex, age, overbite, FMA, and U1–NA as covariates, and overlap weighting (average treatment effect in the overlap population, ATO) was applied [9, 31, 32]. Incisor and canine intrusion accuracy were compared between groups using overlap weighted linear regression, and the results were reported as weighted mean differences (IAR minus control) with 95% confidence intervals and two-sided P values. Analyses were conducted in R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). To assess intra-examiner reliability, 25% of the samples were randomly selected and remeasured after 3 weeks.

In the A Control group, the central incisors, lateral incisors, and canines exhibited intrusive displacement of 0.0623 mm, 0.0740 mm, and 0.0951 mm, respectively. The maxillary first and second premolars showed extrusive displacement of 0.0525 mm and 0.0289 mm, respectively. The mean intrusion of the maxillary anterior teeth was 0.077 mm, whereas the posterior teeth showed a mean extrusion of 0.027 mm, indicating that part of the reaction force from anterior intrusion was transferred to the posterior teeth and induced passive extrusion of the anchorage teeth. In the A IAR group, the central incisors, lateral incisors, and canines exhibited increased intrusion of 0.0763 mm, 0.0905 mm, and 0.1230 mm, corresponding to absolute increases of 0.0140 mm, 0.0170 mm, and 0.0280 mm compared with the A Control group. The gain in canine intrusion was the most pronounced, approximately 29%. Premolar extrusion was markedly reduced to 0.0043 mm for the first premolar and 0.0024 mm for the second premolar, representing reductions of 92% and 93% compared with the A Control group.

In the B Control group, the central incisors showed slight extrusion of 0.0050 mm, whereas the lateral incisors and canines showed mild intrusion of 0.0265 mm and 0.0620 mm, respectively. In the B IAR group, the central incisors shifted from extrusion to intrusion of 0.0021 mm, and intrusion increased to 0.0353 mm for the lateral incisors and 0.0723 mm for the canines, representing increases of 30% and 17% compared with the B Control group.

Sagittal force moment system and axial inclination of the maxillary dentition

Given the curvature of the dental arch, both sagittal displacement/inclination metrics and the sagittal force-moment system were evaluated in a tooth-specific sagittal plane: labio-lingual for anterior teeth and mesio–distal for canines and posterior teeth (Fig. 4A). Considering the potential shift of the center of rotation during tooth movement, the axial inclination of each tooth was derived from tooth length, its initial horizontal projection, and the changes in the coordinates (displacements) of the root and cusp landmarks, thereby avoiding the uncertainty inherent in inclination calculations based on a predefined initial rotation radius (Fig. 4B).

As shown in Fig. 4, when only intrusion was designed for the anterior teeth (Model A), the anterior teeth experienced a labially directed force and a labial crown moment, indicating a tendency toward labial tipping. The canines and premolars experienced a mesially directed force and a mesial crown moment, indicating a tendency toward mesial tipping. In the A IAR group, the direction of the force moment system remained consistent with that in the A Control group. Both the sagittal force (F) and moment (M) decreased. The moment arm ratio d, calculated as M divided by F, decreased in the incisors but increased in the canines and premolars. A smaller d suggests a shorter effective moment arm, with the line of action acting closer to the center of resistance.

Overall, the anterior teeth showed a labial tipping tendency and the posterior teeth showed a mesial tipping tendency. Changes in axial inclination were smaller in the A IAR group, suggesting a trend toward more bodily intrusion.

In Model B, the anterior teeth experienced a lingually directed retraction force and a lingual crown moment, whereas the posterior teeth experienced a mesially directed force and a mesial crown rotational moment. In the B IAR group, the retraction force on the anterior teeth increased, while the mesial force on the posterior teeth decreased. In addition, the moments on both the anterior and posterior teeth decreased, and d also decreased across the maxillary dentition. The axial inclination pattern in Model B was consistent with the force direction. B IAR showed slightly smaller changes in anterior axial inclination, whereas the reduction was more pronounced in the posterior teeth. Together, these findings are consistent with the resultant force acting closer to the center of resistance. In the anterior teeth, this pattern was accompanied by axial inclination changes that remained close to those observed in the B Control group despite the greater retraction force. Meanwhile, the posterior teeth experienced smaller mesial force and moment, with the resultant force acting closer to the center of resistance, resulting in reduced mesial axial inclination changes.

Aligner displacement and von mises stress

The initial displacement pattern and von Mises stress distribution of the aligner are shown in Fig. 5. Peak aligner displacement was mainly concentrated in the anterior and premolar regions. Compared with the A Control group, the B Control group showed higher maximum displacement and higher maximum von Mises stress, indicating greater mechanical demand on the aligner under the extraction design. During anterior intrusion (Model A), addition of the IAR device led to stress concentration between the canine and first premolar. The maximum aligner displacement decreased, whereas the maximum von Mises stress increased, suggesting more effective transfer of intrusive load to the anterior teeth and reduced unintended displacement of the maxillary dentition. A similar pattern was observed in Model B. Relative to the B Control group, the B IAR group showed little change in maximum von Mises stress but a decrease in maximum aligner displacement. These findings were consistent with the tooth displacement results, indicating reduced unwanted movements, including lingual tipping of the anterior teeth and mesial tipping of the posterior teeth.

Discussion

Deep overbite is a common malocclusion, and anterior intrusion is often incorporated into clear aligner protocols to correct overbite and maintain overbite control during anterior retraction [33]. However, previous clinical studies have reported limited accuracy and predictability of anterior intrusion with clear aligners. The vertical response of the posterior anchorage segment during intrusion, particularly unwanted extrusion, has also received relatively little attention. In our study, incorporating the IAR device was associated with improved vertical stability of the posterior segment and enhanced expression of maxillary anterior intrusion in both the finite element simulations and the clinical analysis. In the anterior intrusion scenario, the control condition showed anterior intrusion accompanied by premolar extrusion. By contrast, the IAR condition markedly suppressed premolar extrusion while increasing anterior intrusion. It also produced smaller changes in axial inclination, suggesting a shift toward more bodily intrusion of the anterior teeth. During anterior retraction, the indirect anchorage reinforcement design was associated with improved overbite control and less mesial tipping of the posterior teeth. Clinically, intrusion accuracy remained higher with the IAR device than with aligners alone, with a larger improvement observed for canines. Taken together, these findings suggest that improving posterior vertical anchorage may enhance the predictability of anterior intrusion with clear aligners, and the IAR concept may represent a clinically useful adjunct in anterior intrusion and retraction protocols.

For anterior intrusion, the ideal three dimensional movement is to achieve intrusion while maintaining the three dimensional positional stability of the remaining teeth [4]. In the control condition (A Control), the anterior teeth exhibited intrusion accompanied by labial crown inclination. The anchorage teeth exhibited mesial inclination, buccal expansion, and vertical extrusion, and the magnitude of vertical displacement decreased toward the posterior region. Vertically, the reaction to anterior intrusion was concentrated mainly on the premolar segment, leading to an extrusive displacement tendency of the premolars. Clear aligner therapy primarily delivers orthodontic forces through initial misfit and interference contact between the aligner and teeth, thereby driving the teeth gradually toward the target positions. Clinically, horizontal rectangular attachments or deep overbite optimized attachments are often placed on premolars to ensure retention in the premolar region. Once premolar extrusion occurs, the programmed vertical discrepancy between the anterior teeth and premolars is reduced, which can weaken the effective intrusive action on the anterior teeth and may even become difficult to express. The difference in force distribution in the anterior region between A C and A IAR supports this interpretation. With the IAR design, the extrusive tendency of premolars was markedly suppressed; meanwhile, force in the anterior teeth increased relative to the A control, and the anterior vertical displacement also increased.

Notably, during anterior intrusion, both the anterior and posterior teeth showed an anteriorly directed displacement tendency in the sagittal dimension. The anterior teeth were subjected to a labial force with labial crown inclination, whereas the posterior teeth experienced a mesial force with mesial crown inclination. This pattern may be related to the intrusion design along the tooth long axis, which places the aligner in a relatively shortened state in the sagittal dimension. Under this condition, the anterior teeth may be subjected to a lingual force and the posterior teeth to a mesial force, contributing to a mesial inclination tendency. Meanwhile, the intrusive force is primarily applied on the crown, and its point of action lies labial to the center of resistance, generating a labial crown rotation effect. This effect may offset or exceed the lingual action associated with aligner shortening, resulting in an overall labial inclination tendency of the anterior teeth.

Li et al. [34] used finite element analysis to investigate the biomechanics of mandibular anterior intrusion under different initial IMPA conditions. They reported that when the initial IMPA was < 105°, the force on the anterior teeth was directed toward the lingual and intrusive directions. When the initial IMPA was > 105°, the force on the central incisor shifted from lingual to labial and was accompanied by a labial crown moment, which is consistent with the present observation. The efficiency of tooth intrusion tends to be higher when the long axis of the tooth is aligned with the alveolar bone. With the IAR design, anterior intrusion increased while the labial inclination tendency decreased. The posterior teeth showed a marked reduction in vertical extrusion as well as a reduced mesial inclination tendency. The force moment analysis was consistent with the displacement results, in A IAR, both the labial force and moment on the anterior teeth decreased, and the calculated d also decreased, suggesting that the intrusive direction of the aligner was closer to the tooth long axis passing through the center of resistance. This may suggest that aligners assisted by the IAR enable nearly bodily intrusion of anterior and make the long axis of teeth approximately parallel to the alveolar bone. In the clinical study, the mean U1-NA angle of our subjects was 27.46 ± 6.08°, slightly higher than the normative mean value of 22.8 ± 5.7°, while still within the 95% normal reference range. This indicated that our enrolled patients presented mild maxillary incisor labial inclination, which is the most common clinical anatomical feature in patients requiring anterior intrusion for Class II malocclusion. Meanwhile, the vertical extrusive action on premolars was counteracted, thereby maintaining their vertical position. Mesial force and moment on the posterior teeth decreased, whereas d increased. When considered together with the displacement patterns and inclination outcomes, the anchorage teeth showed an overall reduction in inclination, suggesting that the IAR design also strengthened sagittal anchorage in the posterior segment and made the anchorage teeth more stable, closer to the ideal condition for anterior intrusion.Meanwhile, the vertical extrusive action on premolars was counteracted, thereby maintaining their vertical position. In the corresponding clinical study, baseline differences in age, sex, overbite, vertical facial pattern, and anterior inclination were assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), as these factors may influence intrusion accuracy. The initial maximum absolute SMD was 0.57. To reduce confounding due to baseline differences, a propensity score model was constructed using these covariates, followed by propensity score overlap weighting targeting the average treatment effect in the overlap population. Covariate balance before and after weighting was evaluated using standardized mean differences and Love plots. After weighting, baseline imbalance was substantially reduced(all |SMD|<0.20). Importantly, unweighted intrusion accuracy results were presented descriptively only, whereas between group inference was based on regression estimates after overlap weighting.

Under a purified movement design in which only anterior intrusion was programmed with a planned total intrusion of 2 mm, intrusion accuracy for both incisors and canines were higher in the IAR group than in the control group. Descriptively, incisor intrusion accuracy was 26.36 ± 15.00% in the control group and 42.52 ± 11.32% in the IAR group, and canine intrusion accuracy was 21.46 ± 12.59% and 53.28 ± 10.70%, respectively. After overlap weighting, between group differences remained and were directionally consistent. The weighted mean difference in incisor intrusion accuracy was 18.96% points with a 95% confidence interval of 9.01 to 28.91 (P < 0.001), and the corresponding difference for canines was 33.56% points with a 95% confidence interval of 24.65 to 42.46 (P < 0.001). These findings support that the IAR design improves the predictability and effectiveness of anterior intrusion with clear aligners, with a more pronounced improvement observed for canines. Meade and Weir retrospectively compared the predicted versus achieved overjet and overbite changes generated by ClinCheck for Invisalign treatment and found significant discrepancies. Planned increases in overbite could be up to 222.72% greater than predicted, whereas in extraction cases the planned reduction in overbite achieved only 8.69% of the predicted decrease [35]. Using cone-beam computed tomography, Maher Al-balaa et al. [36] evaluated the predicted versus actual anterior intrusion with Invisalign and likewise reported significant inconsistencies across multiple teeth, including maxillary and mandibular canines and incisors. The overall true intrusion accuracy was approximately 51.19%, which was comparable to the intrusion accuracy observed in the A-IAR group in the present study. However, these studies evaluated intrusion under concurrent multi movement protocols, in which different planned movements may interact. In addition, differences in the planned total amount of intrusion may influence intrusion accuracy and comparability across studies. Overall, the finite element and clinical findings in the present study were directionally consistent. Compared with the control protocol, the IAR protocol showed better maxillary anterior intrusion and improved predictability. Based on the present results, the IAR device may upgrade anterior intrusion from low predictability to moderate predictability, providing mutually supportive evidence for its biomechanical rationale and clinical performance.

To date, direct measurements of occlusal contact forces at the middle segment under specific bite opening heights remain limited. Available evidence indicates that the initial forces generated by clear aligners vary with the amount of activation and decay substantially after insertion. Li et al. [37] reported that aligners fabricated from 1 mm sheets generated initial forces of approximately 8.05 to 12.24 N under 0.2 to 0.6 mm activation when seated on resin models, which decayed to about 2.36 to 2.62 N by day 4 to day 5. Jin et al. [38] reported that their finite element model produced a peak force of approximately 250 g, which was higher than the range typically considered “light” forces. Because finite element studies in orthodontics typically focus on instantaneous initial responses and are commonly used to simulate the initial force system generated by clear aligners, a vertical load of 200 g was applied in the present study to simulate occlusal contact force between the arches. Furthermore, in finite element model, the aligner was assigned a uniform thickness of 0.75 mm. Clinically, aligners are usually fabricated by thermoforming a 0.75 mm thermoplastic sheet against a positive cast. This manufacturing procedure frequently leads to greater thickness in the posterior aligner segment relative to the anterior region, rather than a uniform thickness across the entire appliance. Such dimensional variation may overestimate posterior tooth forces and periodontal ligament stresses in our finite element simulation when compared with actual clinical conditions.

Although the finite element model was simplified relative to clinical reality and reflects instantaneous initial responses, it is theoretically unable to capture the cumulative effect of wear time on tooth movement [39, 40]. Accordingly, finite element analysis is more suitable for elucidating biomechanical mechanisms and for relative comparisons between conditions. Notably, the finite element simulated mean accuracy for incisors and canines in Model A Control was 34.08% and 47.55%, respectively. The incisor result was close to the clinical measurement, with a difference of 7.72% points, whereas the difference for canines was larger at 20.1%. In Model A IAR, the finite element simulated accuracy was 41.7% for incisors and 61.55% for canines, with differences of 0.82 and 8.27% points from the corresponding clinical measurements. On the one hand, the observed agreement between the finite element simulations and the clinical measurement to some extent supports the validity of finite element analysis as a widely used approach in orthodontics for exploring biomechanical mechanisms and comparing biomechanical effects. On the other hand, it indirectly supports the choice of applying a 200 g load to simulate occlusal contact force in the middle segment.

In extraction cases treated with clear aligners using a combined “retraction–intrusion” design, an ideal movement path requires the anterior teeth to move palatally (distally) along the alveolar envelope while achieving mild intrusion or at least maintaining vertical stability. Because clear aligner function both as a force-delivery unit and as a guiding “track,” balancing material stiffness and elasticity remains challenging [38]. As a guiding structure, sufficient rigidity is needed to maintain shape and control tooth position; as a force source, the material should deliver relatively gentle and sustained elastic forces to support safe and effective tooth movement. Over recent decades, reductions in activation per step and improvements in material properties have enabled clear aligners to deliver clinically acceptable force levels in many situations. Although the initial forces may be relatively high, they can decay rapidly after insertion, and the subsequent force levels may meet clinical requirements in most cases [41, 42]. Although the current evidence regarding orthodontically induced inflammatory external apical root resorption (OIIRR) with clear aligners remains limited, available studies overall suggest that, compared with fixed appliances, clear aligner therapy may be associated with a lower risk of OIIRR [43]. However, limitations in material and structural stiffness may still compromise control during long range retraction, leading to unwanted movements such as distal tipping of the anterior teeth, deepening of overbite, and mesial tipping of the posterior teeth [7, 38]. In our simulations, under the B Control condition, the central incisors showed an extrusion tendency with lingual displacement and lingual tipping, accompanied by a lingual crown moment. The lateral incisors and canines exhibited mild intrusion and lingual (distal) displacement, together with lingual and distal crown moments. The anchorage teeth experienced mesially directed forces and mesial crown moments, showing a tendency for mesial tipping while remaining nearly unchanged vertically, collectively resembling the “roller-coaster effect” commonly observed during extraction space closure with clear aligners. This pattern is consistent with the iterative analysis reported by Zhang et al. [11]. In addition, under a design combining 0.15 mm of anterior intrusion and 0.2 mm of anterior retraction, Zhang also observed a tendency for premolar extrusion after the sixth iteration.

In the B IAR condition, the central incisors shifted from extrusion to mild intrusion, and intrusion of the lateral incisors and canines also increased. Regarding axial control, the B IAR condition showed increased retraction forces on the anterior teeth, whereas moments decreased or remained unchanged, with a concomitant reduction in d. Correspondingly, labiolingual inclination changes were smaller, indicating more controlled tipping. Together, these findings suggest that the IAR design enhanced anterior retraction while improving axial control toward a more bodily retraction pattern, which may be related to more effective expression of intrusive forces. The crown morphology of anterior teeth resembles a “watermelon seed” shape, being narrower incisally and wider cervically, which may have two implications. First, although the aligner fully covers the crown, the “watermelon seed” crown shape makes it difficult for the intrusive force to pass along the tooth long axis through the center of resistance, thereby making bodily intrusion difficult to achieve [44]. Second, without sufficient retention, the aligner may be more prone to occlusal disengagement during movements such as rotation or retraction, thereby reducing the effective delivery of intrusive forces. This phenomenon has been described in vitro and has been referred to as the “watermelon-seed effect” [45–48]. In this study, the IAR device incorporated occlusal contact forces to indirectly reinforce posterior vertical anchorage, allowing greater elastic to rebound of the aligner in the anterior segment. This may suppress occlusal disengagement and facilitate the expression of intrusive forces. This may suggest that increasing the overall stiffness of the entire aligner is not the only strategy; enhancing structural stiffness in the posterior segment may also be a feasible option to improve control. A previous study increased the gingival thickness in the posterior segment to reinforce posterior stiffness and, in both finite element and experimental models, observed improved posterior anchorage with reduced mesial tipping tendency [38]. In the present study, the B IAR condition also showed smaller mesially directed forces and moments on the anchorage teeth than the control condition, together with reductions in d and in mesial displacement and inclination. One possible explanation is that incorporating the IAR device locally thickens the occlusal portion of the aligner, which may increase local stiffness. These findings suggest that the IAR design can enhance sagittal anchorage of the posterior segment during anterior retraction, thereby reducing mesial displacement and tipping of the posterior teeth.

Limitations

Despite the wide use of finite element analysis in orthodontics to explore biomechanical effects and potential mechanisms, it cannot fully reproduce the complex intraoral biological environment or time-dependent factors [39]. Accordingly, the finite element findings in this study primarily reflect initial mechanical responses, and further refinement of model settings is warranted in future work. In addition, although the exploratory clinical study provided cross-validation for the finite element observations and lends support to the potential value of this approach in improving the predictability of anterior intrusion and mitigating movement patterns associated with the roller-coaster effect, the limited sample size may affect the robustness of the estimates. Moreover, the historical control (non-concurrent cohort) design cannot fully eliminate time related confounding. Future studies with larger samples and higher level evidence, ideally randomized controlled trials, are needed to further validate and generalize these findings.

Conclusion

Using finite element analysis and an exploratory clinical study, we found that adding an indirect anchorage reinforcement device to the middle segment of the aligner may improve the predictability of anterior intrusion and the biomechanical performance during intrusion and retraction. By mediating occlusal contact forces, the IAR device may partly mitigate the influence of the vertical reactive force generated by anterior intrusion, thereby helping stabilize the posterior anchorage teeth and reducing unintended tipping during intrusion and retraction, which may support overbite control. Overall, this study proposes a strategy to improve the predictability of anterior intrusion while stabilizing posterior anchorage, with potential as an adjunct for anterior intrusion and overbite control in clear aligner therapy.
