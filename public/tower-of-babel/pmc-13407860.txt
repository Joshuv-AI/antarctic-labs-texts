Association between body roundness index and frailty among middle aged and older adults in China
Authors: Jintao Wu, Ziming Cai, Bo Liu, Jinzhu Liang, Chongyang Zhang, Daming Feng, Gongpeng Xiong, Wenping Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Previous cross-sectional studies have indicated an association between the Body Roundness Index (BRI) and frailty. However, longitudinal evidence supporting this relationship remains scarce. Therefore, this study employs a longitudinal analysis to investigate the correlation between BRI and the risk of frailty in a Chinese population, and to evaluate the potential utility of BRI in the early identification and risk prediction of frailty. This study utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). A total of 4924 participants who completed follow-up from 2011 to 2018 were included. The BRI was calculated based on physical measurements and questionnaire data, and participants were categorized into quartiles according to BRI values. Multivariable-adjusted weighted regression models and trend tests were employed to examine the association between BRI and frailty. Furthermore, subgroup and sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the findings. After 7 years of follow-up, a total of 1242 participants (25.22%) developed frailty. The analysis revealed a positive association between the BRI and the risk of frailty (OR = 1.23, 95% CI: 1.15–1.33, p < 0.0001), with a significant increasing trend in frailty risk across ascending BRI quartiles (p for trend < 0.0001). Subgroup analyses and interaction tests indicated that the positive association between BRI and frailty risk remained consistent across various demographic, lifestyle, and clinical subpopulations (all p for interaction > 0.05). This study demonstrates that an elevated BRI is significantly associated with an increased risk of frailty. These findings suggest that while elevated BRI is an independent risk factor for frailty, its standalone predictive value is modest. Introduction

Frailty represents a multifaceted clinical syndrome marked by a progressive decline in multiple physiological systems, substantially increasing the vulnerability to adverse health outcomes such as falls, disability, hospitalizations, and premature mortality1,2. It is widely regarded as a pivotal risk factor for functional decline in older adults3. Against the backdrop of a rapidly aging global population, frailty has emerged as a pressing public health issue worldwide4. A recent meta-analysis involving community-dwelling adults from 62 countries estimated the pooled prevalence of frailty to be 24%5. In China, the world’s most populous nation, the prevalence of frailty has been rising in tandem with demographic aging and shifts in lifestyle6. Epidemiological studies reveal that the occurrence of frailty among Chinese older adults climbed from 16.87% in 2011 to 22.54% in 2020, with projections suggesting it may reach 32.46% by 20307. This upward trend carries profound implications for healthcare systems and societal productivity8. Despite the escalating burden of frailty, effective predictive indicators remain scarce. In recent years, growing scholarly attention has been directed toward the relationship between various obesity-related indices and frailty9. The body mass index (BMI), a conventional metric derived from height and weight, is commonly employed to evaluate obesity and associated health risks10,11. However, BMI fails to differentiate fat distribution or account for variations in body composition, and its utility is particularly limited in pediatric and adolescent populations12,13. Consequently, there is a critical need for more precise and comprehensive assessment tools to better predict health risks associated with frailty14.

The Body Roundness Index (BRI), introduced by Thomas, which is a novel anthropometric indicator that quantifies abdominal adiposity and overall body fat by integrating the geometric relationship between waist circumference and height15. This metric is applicable to individuals of different sexes and age groups, exhibits stronger correlations with various metabolic disorders, accommodates variations in body morphology, and offers superior risk stratification compared to the BMI, thereby overcoming several limitations associated with the traditional BMI16. Owing to these strengths, BRI has been increasingly utilized in the context of preventive medicine17,18. Previous research has established significant associations of BRI with several health conditions, including metabolic syndrome, insulin resistance, and cardiovascular diseases—each of which represents a major risk factor for frailty19–21. Recent advancements have further validated BRI’s crucial role in cardiometabolic and physical risk stratification, emphasizing its clinical utility in accurately reflecting visceral adiposity compared to traditional metrics22–24. Despite these findings, the evidence linking BRI directly to the frailty remains limited. Although several cross-sectional analyses, mainly based on the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), have indicated a positive correlation between BRI and frailty, longitudinal evidence derived from Chinese populations is notably scarce25. This gap is especially pronounced in certain demographic or clinical subgroups, where relevant investigations are largely lacking. Therefore, a comprehensive evaluation of the utility of BRI in the diagnosis and risk prediction of frailty is warranted.

The China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) offers high-quality micro-level data collected from individuals aged 45 years and above, spanning 28 provinces, 150 county-level units, and 450 village-level units across China26. CHARLS is designed to facilitate interdisciplinary research addressing challenges related to population aging. Data acquisition was conducted primarily through structured survey instruments, encompassing key domains such as demographic characteristics, health status, physical measurements, employment status, retirement patterns, and pension systems. Based on the CHARLS database, the present study examines the association between BRI and frailty, with the objective of establishing a scientific basis for early identification, targeted prevention, and strategic intervention of frailty.

Methods

Data collection and study population

This study adhered to the guidelines and protocols of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS)26. The dataset is publicly available at http://charls.pku.edu.cn. We included participants from the 2011 survey wave who underwent physical examinations and provided complete baseline data. A set of exclusion criteria was applied to remove ineligible individuals, including: (1) Those under 45 years of age at the time of the 2011 survey; (2) Missing data on the Body Roundness Index (BRI); (3) Incomplete information on key frailty-related variables; (4) Individuals diagnosed with frailty in 2011; (5) Participants lost to follow-up by 2018. Since the CHARLS study had already obtained ethical approval from the Peking University Institutional Review Board and all participants provided written informed consent, no additional ethical approval was required for this study.

BRI assessment

The Body Roundness Index (BRI) was developed by Thomas using three independent databases, encompassing a total of 7,166 participants15. The index was validated through analyses incorporating variables such as height, weight, ethnicity, and body fat percentage. The predictive formula, derived from rigorously measured waist circumference and height, is defined as follows: BRI = 364.2–365.5 × √[1 - (WC (m) / 2π)² / (0.5 × height (m))²]. All measurements were collected by trained personnel following standardized protocols to ensure data reliability.

Frailty assessment

The Frailty Index (FI) was constructed from 36 health-related items encompassing cognitive function, activities of daily living, common chronic diseases, mental health, and sensory impairments(Additional file 1: Table S1)27. Detailed descriptions of these items are provided in Supplementary Table 1. Each item was assigned a score of 1 if the health criterion was met, and 0 otherwise. The FI was calculated by dividing the total deficit score by 36, yielding a value ranging from 0 to 1. Based on previous studies, frailty was defined as an FI ≥ 0.25, with higher values indicating greater severity of frailty28,29.

Covariates

To mitigate the potential influence of confounding factors, we further adjusted for covariates that may affect both BRI and frailty risk. Beyond fundamental sociodemographic and lifestyle factors, we incorporated specific clinical indicators. The TyG index was strictly included as a validated surrogate marker for insulin resistance. Because metabolic dysfunction is a crucial pathophysiological mechanism linking central obesity (captured by BRI) to skeletal muscle wasting and subsequent frailty, adjusting for the baseline TyG index helps to isolate the independent association of body roundness from concurrent metabolic dysregulation. Furthermore, baseline memory and cognitive statuses were included because cognitive decline frequently co-occurs with physical frailty (forming cognitive frailty), sharing common vascular and inflammatory pathways, and can significantly accelerate functional deterioration.

The adjusted variables included the following categories: (1) socio-demographic characteristics: age, sex, educational level, marital status, and residence; (2) questionnaire-derived measures: memory status, cognitive status, smoking status, drinking status, hypertension, cardiovascular disease (CVD), diabetes, coronary heart disease (CHD), stroke, and emotional impairment (defined as an affirmative response to the diagnosis of emotional, nervous, or psychiatric problems); (3) laboratory indicators: triglyceride-glucose index (TyG)30.

Statistical analysis

Regarding the handling of missing data, we employed a complete case analysis strategy. Given that the missingness primarily occurred in the main exposure variable (components of the BRI) and key physiological biomarkers (such as the TyG index) during the 2011 baseline survey, we opted against multiple imputation. Methodologically, imputation of the primary exposure itself is generally not advisable, particularly when it accounts for a large proportion of the missing data. The comparison of baseline characteristics between the included and excluded participants is provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Since the data (including age, TyG index, memory, and cognition scores) did not follow a normal distribution, they are expressed as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR). Based on the normality of data distribution, the chi-square test, Kruskal–Wallis H test, or analysis of variance (ANOVA) were employed. A weighting scheme was applied in the analyses, with results for continuous and categorical variables reported as weighted means and weighted proportions, respectively. Weighted multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to examine the association between BRI and the incidence of frailty at the 7-year follow-up. Logistic regression was selected because the outcome variable (incident frailty) was treated as a binary status (yes/no). Covariates were adjusted sequentially to ensure robustness.

In all weighted multivariable logistic regression models, specific handling of covariates was predefined. Age, TyG index, memory scores, and cognitive scores were treated as continuous variables to preserve data variance and detect dose-response relationships. Conversely, sociodemographic parameters (sex, educational level, marital status, residence), lifestyle factors (smoking and drinking status), and the presence of specific chronic diseases (hypertension, CVD, diabetes, CHD, stroke, and depressive status) were incorporated as categorical variables.

Three models were constructed: Model 1 assessed the crude association between BRI and frailty risk; Model 2 was further adjusted for age, sex, educational level, marital status, and residence; Model 3 included comprehensive adjustments for demographic and health-related variables. The reliability of regression outcomes was verified using trend tests, while potential linear associations were evaluated via smooth curve fitting and weighted generalized additive models. Subgroup analyses stratified by age, sex, residence, and health status were conducted to examine the consistency of the BRI–frailty relationship. Sensitivity analysis was performed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves to compare the predictive efficacy of BRI and BMI. Additionally, to address potential residual confounding from physical activity, a targeted sensitivity analysis was conducted in a sub-cohort with complete physical activity data. Physical activity was derived from the IPAQ module and categorized into three levels (no activity, low activity, and high activity) following the methodology established in previous literature to assess the robustness of the fully adjusted model31. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS, R software, and the stats package, with a two-sided p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, as well as subsequent follow-up assessments, the following participants were excluded: those under 45 years of age at the time of the 2011 survey, individuals with missing BRI data, those lacking information on potential frailty-related variables, participants diagnosed with frailty in 2011, and individuals lost to follow-up by 2018. Among the initial 17,705 participants surveyed in 2011, 151 under the age of 45 and 4,518 with incomplete BRI data were excluded. To minimize confounding effects, further exclusions were made: 6,253 participants were due to missing essential covariates, and 978 individuals with a current or previous diagnosis of frailty at the 2011 survey. An additional 881 participants lost to follow-up before 2018 were also excluded. Ultimately, 4,924 participants were included in the final analysis, the detailed flowchart is shown in Fig. 1.

The included cohort comprised 4,924 participants (3,250 females and 1,674 males) with a mean age of 58 years. During the 7-year follow-up period, 1,242 individuals (25.22%) developed frailty. When stratified by BRI quartiles, the incidence of frailty exhibited a progressive increase across groups (Q1: 19.74%; Q2: 21.53%; Q3: 26.42%; Q4: 33.20%; p < 0.001). The quartile ranges were as follows: Q1: 0.17 ≤ BRI < 3.35 (n = 1,231); Q2: 3.35 ≤ BRI < 4.19 (n = 1,231); Q3: 4.19 ≤ BRI < 5.22 (n = 1,230); Q4: 5.22 ≤ BRI ≤ 12.28 (n = 1,232). Significant differences (p < 0.05) were observed across quartiles in terms of age, sex, educational level, marital status, residence, smoking status, drinking status, hypertension, CVD, diabetes, stroke, depressive status, and TyG, as detailed in Table 1.

Association between the BRI and Frailty status

The results demonstrated a significant positive association between increased BRI and the risk of frailty (Table 2), with an odds ratio (OR) of 1.38 (95% CI: 1.29–1.47; p < 0.0001) in the unadjusted model. This association remained statistically significant in subsequent partially adjusted models (Model 2: OR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.20–1.38; p < 0.0001) and the fully adjusted model (Model 3: OR = 1.23, 95% CI: 1.15–1.33; p < 0.0001). A dose-response relationship was observed across BRI quartiles, with progressively elevated frailty risk corresponding to higher quartiles (p for trend < 0.0001). Furthermore, restricted cubic spline regression analysis confirmed a linear relationship between BRI and frailty risk (Fig. 2).

Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis was performed to account for the potential confounding effect of baseline physical activity. As shown in Supplementary Table S5, after further adjusting for physical activity levels, the significant positive association between continuous BRI and incident frailty remained remarkably robust (OR = 1.23, 95% CI: 1.15–1.33; p < 0.0001), indicating that the association is independent of daily physical activity.

Evaluation of the prediction model

The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was 0.585 (95% CI: 0.566–0.604), indicating modest discriminatory ability. Both BRI and BMI demonstrated predictive capacity for frailty risk, with BRI showing a significantly higher AUC compared to BMI (0.585 vs. 0.528, p < 0.001), as detailed in Fig. 3 and Table S3.

To assess the incremental predictive value of BRI beyond standard clinical factors, we compared a baseline clinical model (incorporating age, sex, and selected comorbidities) with an augmented model that additionally included BRI. The baseline model achieved a C-statistic (AUC) of 0.684. The integration of BRI into the baseline model significantly increased the C-statistic to 0.691 (DeLong test, P = 0.002). Furthermore, the inclusion of BRI resulted in a significant improvement in risk reclassification, yielding a continuous Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) of 0.115 (95% CI: 0.051–0.179, P < 0.001) and an Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI) of 0.005 (95% CI: 0.002–0.007, P < 0.001). The comparative ROC curves are presented in Supplementary Figure S1. These comprehensive metrics demonstrate that while the standalone discriminative ability of BRI is modest, it provides modest incremental value for frailty risk stratification at the population level.

Subgroup analysis

To account for potential variations in the positive association between BRI and frailty, subgroup analyses and interaction tests were conducted. Stratification was performed based on age, sex, education level, residence, smoking status, marital status, alcohol consumption, hypertension, cardiovascular disease (CVD), diabetes, coronary heart disease (CHD), stroke, emotional status. All subgroup models were adjusted for the following covariates: age, sex, education level, residence, smoking status, marital status, alcohol consumption, TyG index, hypertension, CVD, diabetes, CHD, stroke, emotional status, memory, and cognitive function.

As detailed in Supplementary Table S4, stratum-specific analyses revealed a statistically significant association between elevated BRI and increased frailty risk within several subgroups, including non-smokers, non-drinkers, and females. However, it is crucial to note that the interaction tests did not yield statistical significance across any of the stratification variables (all p for interaction > 0.05; Fig. 4). This confirms that there is no significant effect modification, indicating that the positive association between elevated BRI on frailty is highly robust and consistent across different populations.

Discussion

Analysis of the 7-year follow-up data from 4,924 participants revealed a significant positive association between increased Body Roundness Index (BRI) and the prevalence of frailty. This association remained consistent across all adjusted models and subgroup analyses, indicating that BRI serves as an independent predictor of frailty. Consistent with conventional body mass index (BMI), BRI may function as a discriminative indicator of frailty risk, suggesting its potential utility as a supplementary indicator for assessing frailty risk alongside other established clinical metrics.

By integrating waist circumference and height, BRI provides a precise quantification of visceral fat distribution, thereby overcoming limitations of BMI in predicting body fat percentage and adiposity patterning32. The pathophysiological mechanisms underlying the BRI-frailty association likely involve a complex interplay of chronic inflammation and metabolic dysfunction. Visceral fat accumulation, accurately captured by elevated BRI, is known to stimulate pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α) that can directly induce muscle protein degradation and accelerate sarcopenic obesity. This biological plausibility is strongly supported by the most current literature. A recent comprehensive analysis elucidated that visceral adiposity, precisely indexed by BRI, drives physical deterioration and biological aging through a triad of mechanisms: metabolic dysfunction, heightened oxidative stress, and the critical depletion of systemic protective factors such as high-density lipoprotein (HDL) and vitamin D24. Our longitudinal findings perfectly align with this specific mechanistic framework. In our cohort, the persistent cardiometabolic burden stemming from central adiposity—as tracked by an elevated BRI—likely acts through these exact systemic inflammatory and oxidative pathways to accelerate the progressive functional decline and muscle wasting characteristic of the frailty cascade. Importantly, our analytical models provide specific empirical insights into these pathways within our cohort. In our study, we explicitly adjusted for the TyG index, a validated surrogate marker for insulin resistance, in the final multivariable model. While the odds ratio for frailty risk decreased from the unadjusted Model 1 to the fully adjusted Model 3, the association between BRI and frailty remained highly significant (OR = 1.23). This pattern critically suggests that while insulin resistance and metabolic dysregulation partially mediate the relationship between central adiposity and frailty, they do not entirely account for it. The sustained independent predictive value of BRI in our final model indicates that other mechanisms—such as the direct lipotoxic effects of visceral fat on skeletal muscle, persistent low-grade inflammation not fully captured by metabolic markers, or the sheer mechanical burden of severe abdominal obesity—are independently and profoundly associated with the frailty cascade. Therefore, within this specific cohort, BRI demonstrates its value by capturing a broader spectrum of physiological vulnerability beyond standard metabolic parameters.

This study demonstrated that while BRI showed a statistically significantly higher AUC (0.585) compared to BMI (0.528; P < 0.001) in predicting frailty, the absolute discriminatory ability of both indices remains modest. We explicitly acknowledge that the absolute magnitude of incremental discrimination (ΔC-statistic = 0.007) is small and may not translate into meaningful clinical utility at the individual level. Therefore, the practical positioning of BRI is as a low-cost epidemiological screening signal at the population level rather than a clinically actionable predictor.

Although interaction tests indicated no significant effect modification across any subgroups (all p > 0.05), these subgroup-specific observations should be presented as hypothesis-generating and exploratory only. For instance, the nominal significance observed in females might hypothetically relate to postmenopausal estrogen decline and associated central fat redistribution33. Studies indicate that the menopausal transition is accompanied by increased central adiposity and reduced peripheral fat, with greater visceral deposition correlating with higher cardiometabolic risk34. For a given BRI value, women typically have lower muscle mass and higher fat mass than men, rendering them more susceptible to frailty under the influence of lipotoxicity35. Additionally, the decline in resting energy expenditure occurs more rapidly in women, potentially contributing to their higher vulnerability36. Similarly, the nominally significant risk observed in the 50–59 age group might reflect peak career and family responsibilities, midlife weight gain, and higher prevalence of metabolic syndrome. Although marriage provides emotional support, increased family duties—such as childcare and domestic tasks—may reduce time for physical activity, coupled with more frequent family meals and higher caloric intake, potentially amplifying the BRI–frailty association37.

Interestingly, stratum-specific analyses revealed that the association between BRI and frailty was statistically significant among non-smokers and non-drinkers, but did not reach statistical significance among smokers and drinkers. However, because all interaction tests were non-significant (p > 0.05), we cannot definitively conclude that the association between BRI on frailty fundamentally differs by smoking or drinking status. The apparent lack of significance within the smoking and drinking strata might simply be due to insufficient statistical power resulting from smaller subgroup sample sizes, or it could be hypothetically related to the overwhelming, competing detrimental effects of tobacco and alcohol on the aging trajectory might partially mask the independent contribution of adiposity. Overall, the lack of significant statistical interaction implies that managing central adiposity, as reflected by BRI, remains a universally applicable strategy for frailty prevention, regardless of background lifestyle factors. Future studies with larger sample sizes are required to further elucidate these complex relationships.

Moreover, participants without hypertension, CVD, diabetes, CHD, stroke, or depressive symptoms generally exhibited longer healthspan, likely engaging in more physical and social activities. The intricate interplay between specific health conditions and functional decline is crucial for understanding the frailty trajectory. For instance, recent evidence from the CHARLS cohort has demonstrated a robust association between cardiovascular disease and an increased risk of functional dependence38. This aligns with our observation that the accumulation of cardiometabolic burden—which is closely linked to central adiposity and elevated BRI—can precipitate a cascade of physical and functional impairments, ultimately exacerbating the overall risk of frailty. Conversely, those with these chronic conditions may exhibit lower weight due to catabolic effects, indirectly attenuating frailty risk.

Although previous studies have reported a positive correlation between BRI and frailty in Chinese populations, our study further delineates this association across BRI intervals and provides robust confirmation of the BRI–frailty relationship39. However, several limitations should be acknowledged.

First, while BRI is a reliable anthropometric proxy for body fat, waist circumference cannot distinguish between subcutaneous and visceral adipose tissue; thus, future research should incorporate imaging techniques for enhanced validation40. Additionally, as our sample was primarily drawn from specific regions in China and focused on individuals aged ≥ 45 years, the generalizability of these findings to other ethnic or younger populations may be limited. Multicenter, multinational studies are therefore necessary to confirm these associations globally. Furthermore, although our Frailty Index (FI) cutoff of ≥ 0.25 aligns with established literature41, it is important to note that alternative thresholds exist, and frailty prevalence estimates may vary depending on the specific cutoff applied.

Second, while this study provides valuable prospective insights, the 7-year follow-up may not fully capture the long-term, lifelong dynamics between BRI and frailty. Relying on a single baseline BRI measurement to predict incident frailty seven years later is also a notable limitation. Although we excluded individuals with prevalent frailty at baseline, the risk of reverse causality cannot be entirely ruled out. The insidious, prodromal stages of physical decline can induce profound alterations in body composition—such as sarcopenia or sarcopenic obesity—well before a formal frailty diagnosis is made42,43.

Critically, the substantial exclusion of participants due to missing data necessitated a complete case analysis, which inherently carries the risk of selection bias. As detailed in Supplementary Table S2, the baseline differences between included and excluded populations suggest that our analytic sample may not perfectly represent the original cohort. Finally, while residual confounding from unmeasured variables remains a possibility, we conducted a sensitivity analysis specifically adjusting for physical activity levels to mitigate this concern. The results clearly demonstrated that the association between BRI and frailty remained highly consistent and independent of baseline physical activity, thereby further strengthening the robustness of our longitudinal findings. Consequently, our findings should be interpreted as a robust longitudinal association rather than a definitive causal relationship.

Conclusion

This study provides further support for the utility of the Body Roundness Index (BRI) as a supplementary indicator for assessing the risk of frailty. These findings suggest that BRI may serve as a cost-effective epidemiological tool for population-level screening to identify individuals at increased risk of frailty. Future research should investigate the utility of BRI in conjunction with other body composition indicators for predicting FI, with a focus on developing integrated models for more accurate identification of high-risk populations and the implementation of targeted preventive strategies.
