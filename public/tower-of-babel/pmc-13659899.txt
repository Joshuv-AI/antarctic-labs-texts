Environmental contamination with canine soil-transmitted helminths in New Zealand urban areas: A nationwide molecular, spatial, and risk factor analysis survey
Authors: Madisson Nam, Patsy A. Zendejas-Heredia, Sara Ashby, Sze-Fui Hii, Ushani Atapattu, Lucas Huggins, Anke Wiethoelter, Carl Eden, Maureen Forsyth, Frédéric Beugnet, Neil D. Young, Robin B. Gasser, Rebecca Traub, Vito Colella
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Urban parks and recreational areas are frequent gathering sites for dogs and their owners, where dog waste creates sources for pathogens of veterinary and zoonotic concern. Canine soil-transmitted helminths (cSTHs) such as hookworms (Ancylostoma spp. and Uncinaria stenocephala), roundworms (Toxocara canis and Toxascaris leonina), whipworms (Trichuris vulpis), and threadworms (Strongyloides stercoralis), are recognised for their ability to cause gastrointestinal disease and other health issues in dogs. In addition, some species are known for being zoonotic and cause potentially life-threatening conditions in humans. In an exploratory investigation performed in 2023, canine faecal samples were collected from 16 areas across five regions in New Zealand and screened using antigen-based tests, revealing that overall, 31.3% (95% CI 14.2–55.6) of areas were contaminated with at least one parasite, with 7.1% (95% CI 3.3–14.6) and 3.5% (95% CI 1.2–9.9) of individual samples testing positive for roundworms and whipworms, respectively. In a subsequent nationwide survey in 2024, 739 canine faecal samples were collected from 123 urban areas across New Zealand and analysed using multiplex real-time PCRs. Positive samples were further genetically characterised by Sanger and nanopore sequencing. Overall, 24.4% (95% CI 17.7–32.7) of areas tested positive for at least one cSTH, most commonly Ancylostoma caninum (12.2%, 95% CI 7.5–19.1), followed by Uncinaria stenocephala (9.8%, 95% CI 5.7–16.3), T. canis (3.3%, 95% CI 1.3–8.1), and T. vulpis (1.6%, 95% CI 0.4–5.7), whilst Ancylostoma ceylanicum and S. stercoralis were not detected. Logistic regression analyses identified dog density, proximity to city centres, and bioclimatic zone, as predictors of cSTHs contamination in urban areas. This study provides in depth information on the molecular identity and spatial distribution of cSTHs in New Zealand's urban areas and contributes to understanding environmental exposure risk to support parasite surveillance and control in companion animals.

Introduction

Canine soil-transmitted helminths (cSTHs) are gastrointestinal nematodes that require environmental development in soil, where eggs or larvae mature into infective stages prior to host transmission [1]. Infection occurs primarily via ingestion of infective stages or larval skin penetration, and can result in anaemia, gastrointestinal signs, poor growth, and reduced wellbeing in dogs [2], [3], [4]. Several cSTHs, including canine hookworms, Strongyloides stercoralis, and Toxocara canis, are zoonotic and may cause cutaneous, visceral, or ocular larva migrans in humans [5], [6]. Canine hookworms (e.g., Uncinaria stenocephala and Ancylostoma spp.) are typically acquired through percutaneous penetration or ingestion of infective larvae and can have significant clinical impacts in dogs [7]. In temperate regions, human infections are most commonly associated with cutaneous larva migrans, while eosinophilic enteritis caused by A. caninum is rarely reported [8], [9]. Toxocara canis is of particular concern due to the high number of eggs shed by infected dogs, especially puppies, and the prolonged environmental persistence of these eggs under favourable conditions [10], [11]. Human toxocariasis can result in visceral, ocular, neural, or covert larva migrans, highlighting the public health importance of cSTH control in companion animals [12], [13], [14], [15].

In New Zealand, data on cSTHs remain limited. In 1973, prevalences of 33% for T. canis, 31% for U. stenocephala, and 24% for Trichuris vulpis were recorded in a small necropsy-based survey of 51 pet and working dogs from Palmerston North [16]. In 1981, similar prevalences of 38.2% for T. canis, 36.4% for U. stenocephala, and 36.4% for T. vulpis were recorded in a study of 55 dogs from eleven regions across the country that used necropsy and microscopic examination of intestinal contents [17]. More recently, a 2015 survey of 56 pet and shelter dogs, detected hookworms (4%), T. vulpis (6%) and T. canis (13%) in faecal samples using faecal floatation [18]. In addition, a 2019 study collected 171 faecal samples from working dogs and detected hookworms in 12%, T. vulpis in 5%, and T. canis in 5% of faecal samples, also using floatation [19]. While U. stenocephala is considered endemic in New Zealand, A. caninum has only been detected sporadically (in 1975, 2018, 2023) and to date, exclusively in greyhounds [20], [21], [22]. However, previous studies have been constrained by small sample sizes, dogs belonging to specific risk categories, and diagnostic methods with limited sensitivity and specificity, restricting the detection of low-intensity or uncommon infections. Advances in molecular diagnostics, particularly multiplex quantitative PCR (qPCR), enable sensitive and specific detection of multiple parasite species within a single sample [23], [24]. These approaches have been applied internationally to establish robust epidemiological data and inform veterinary and public health interventions [25], [26], [27], [28], [29]. However, no nationwide molecular survey of cSTHs has been conducted in New Zealand, leaving critical gaps in understanding their occurrence, distribution, and zoonotic risk.

Urban areas represent important interfaces between canine and human activity and may act as transmission hotspots. Environmental contamination with canine faeces can create persistent reservoirs of infective stages, posing risks to both animal and human health [30], [31]. Despite this, the extent of cSTH contamination in New Zealand urban areas remains largely unexplored. The present study aimed to characterise environmental contamination with cSTHs across New Zealand harnessing the capability of an antigen-based assay to detect adult cSTH and the sensitivity and specificity of a multiplex qPCR to detect and identify cSTH eggs in stool. Further, we integrated qPCR with Sanger and nanopore sequencing to determine species-specific (i) frequency and spatial distribution of cSTHs and (ii) socio-economic and bioclimatic variables as potential risk factors associated with their environmental contamination.

Materials and methods

Exploratory antigen-based environmental survey

An exploratory survey was conducted between August and October 2023 to assess the frequency of detection of adult roundworms, whipworms, and hookworms using an antigen-based diagnostic approach. Fresh canine faecal samples (estimated less than 24 h old) were collected from 16 urban parks across five New Zealand regions (Auckland, Hamilton, Rotorua, Christchurch, and Wellington), with 2 to 10 samples per park (n = 85). Samples were collected only after the dog owner had left the area, targeting abandoned faecal deposits contributing to environmental contamination. Samples were tested for hookworms (Ancylostoma spp., Uncinaria spp.), roundworms (Toxocara spp., Toxascaris spp., Bayliscaris spp.), and whipworms (Trichuris spp.) using a commercial coproantigen assay (IDEXX, New Zealand).

Study area and collection of samples for nationwide environmental survey

A nationwide cross-sectional survey of cSTHs in urban areas was conducted between January and April 2024 across New Zealand's north and south islands. A total of 123 areas were sampled including off-leash dog parks, dog friendly play spaces, sporting grounds, walkways, domains, and beaches. For simplicity, all sampled areas will be referred to from hereon as parks. Minimum sample size was calculated using previous Australian estimates of cSTH contamination (44%) [30] with a 95% confidence level and 5% margin of error, with a design effect of 2 applied to account for clustering within selected parks. Selected areas were divided into sub-tropical and temperate bioclimatic zones based on a modified Köppen classification system [32]. Between 1 and 13 fresh faecal samples per park (median 7) were collected from abandoned faecal deposits within each park. Individual samples were transferred into 50 mL screw cap containers with approximately 25 mL of 70% non-denatured ethanol. Faecal samples were kept at room temperature during transport to the University of Melbourne, Australia, and stored at 4 °C until further processing for parasitological screening.

Molecular methods

DNA extraction

Faecal samples were centrifuged at 3750 ×g for 5 mins and ethanol was removed. Faecal material was rehydrated (1:1 with water), homogenised, and incubated overnight at 4 °C. DNA was extracted from 250 μL of rehydrated faeces using a modified protocol for the Maxwell® RSC PureFood GMO and Authentication Kit (Promega). Mechanical lysis was performed using bead-beating with CTAB buffer and zirconia-silica beads, followed by proteinase K digestion (60 °C, 2 h). DNA was eluted in 50 μL. See Appendix A for further details.

Multiplex qPCR for canine soil-transmitted helminths

Each DNA extract was analysed in duplicate using three multiplex TaqMan qPCR assays targeting: (i) A. caninum and A. ceylanicum internal transcriber spacer I (ITS-1) [33], (ii) Trichuris vulpis (18S rRNA gene) and Uncinaria stenocephala (ITS-1) [26], [33], and (iii) Toxocara canis (ITS-2) and Strongyloides spp. (18S rRNA gene) [26], [34]. All assays included an internal reaction control (EHV4 gB gene) [35], with positive synthetic gene fragment controls and nuclease-free water negative controls. Reactions (20 μL) contained GoTaq Probe Master Mix, template DNA, and controls. Cycling conditions were 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min (BioMolecular Systems MIC platform). Detailed assay protocols, including the primer and probe sequences, corresponding gene targets, and references for each assay, are provided in Appendix B.

Sanger and nanopore DNA sequencing

Sanger sequencing was performed for T. canis and U. stenocephala (ITS and cox1 loci) [36], [37], [38], [39], while Oxford Nanopore Technologies MinION sequencing was performed for A. caninum, T. vulpis and S. stercoralis (ITS1–5.8S-ITS2 or 18S rRNA regions) [40], [41], [42]. Libraries were prepared using ONT PCR Barcoding Expansion and Ligation Sequencing Kit V14, as described in [41]. All sequences, regardless of the sequencing method were processed using consistent quality control and alignment procedures to ensure comparability for downstream phylogenetic analyses. See Appendix C and Appendix D for detailed protocols and information on primers and probes.

Hookworm phylogenetic analysis

Sequences were quality-checked, trimmed, and aligned in Geneious Prime® (v2025.2.2, Biomatters Ltd., New Zealand) using the ClustalW algorithm. Species identity was confirmed using BLASTn (≥98% identity and high query coverage). Representative sequences from GenBank of closely related species were included for phylogenetic context, with Bunostomum spp. used as the outgroup. The best-fitting nucleotide substitution model was determined using the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) in MEGA 12, and phylogenetic inference was performed in MrBayes (K2P + I model; 1,000,000 generations, 25% burn-in), and validated with maximum likelihood analysis in MEGA 12 with 1000 bootstrap replicates. Final visualisation was performed in TreeViewer (v2.2.0). Further details for this analysis are described in Appendix E.

Statistical analysis

Sample-level detection frequencies and park-level contamination rates

Environmental contamination outcomes were assessed as park-level contamination rate (proportion of urban parks with ≥1 positive samples), and sample-level detection frequencies (proportion of faecal samples testing positive). Sample-level detection frequencies were calculated for each cSTH species. Corresponding 95% confidence intervals were calculated using the Wilson method [43]. All analyses were conducted using Epitools (https://epitools.ausvet.com.au/). Detection frequencies and corresponding confidence intervals for the antigen-based survey were calculated using the same approach.

Risk factor analysis

Univariable and multivariable logistic regression models assessed associations between park contamination and environmental and socio-demographic factors. These included temperature, rainfall, socio-economic deprivation, species richness, distance to city centre, dog density, and bioclimatic zone. Rainfall (mm) and mean maximum temperature (°C) were obtained from the National Institute of Water and Atmospheric Research (NIWA). Socio-economic deprivation was assessed using the New Zealand Index of Deprivation (NZDep), categorised as low (1–3), medium (4–6), or high (7–10). Species richness was obtained from Environment Aotearoa 2022 and categorised as low, medium, or high according to the regional estimates. Distance to the nearest city centre was calculated in ArcGIS using sampling coordinates derived from park addresses and the coordinates of the corresponding city centre. Dog density was calculated as the number of registered dogs per 10,000 people using data from the New Zealand Department of Internal Affairs (2023). Bioclimatic zone was classified as subtropical or temperate based on topography and the modified Köppen classification used by the New Zealand Meteorological Service. Variables with a p-value ≤0.25 were considered for multivariable analyses. Multicollinearity was assessed using VIF and correlation coefficients (> 0.6). Model selection was guided by forward stepwise minimisation of Akaike's Information Criterion (AIC) [44] and fit was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. Analysis of the results was performed using Excel 2021 (Microsoft Corp., WA, USA), R (R Core Team, 2025), RStudio (v4.5.1, Posit Team, 2025), and GraphPad Prism (v10.4.2, Boston, MA, USA). Further details on risk factor analysis are found in Appendix F.

Spatial distribution mapping

Georeferenced park locations were mapped, and their spatial distribution was visualised across New Zealand in ArcGIS Online and ArcMap (Esri ArcGIS®, 2021). Environmental layers derived from topography (altitude and landform) and a modified Köppen climate classification system were overlaid onto maps to investigate regional patterns of parasite distribution.

Park-level contamination with cSTHs

In the nationwide survey, a total of 739 dog faecal samples from 123 urban parks in New Zealand were collected. Fifty-one parks were sampled in the sub-tropical bioclimatic zone (269 faecal samples across 9 locations), and 72 parks in the temperate zone (470 faecal samples across 37 locations) (Appendix G).

Toxocara canis (3.3%, 4/123) and Trichuris vulpis (1.6%, 2/123) were less frequently detected. For A. caninum univariable analysis showed higher odds in temperate zones (OR 5.4, 95% CI 1.4–35.59, p = 0.012), increasing dog density (OR 1.001, 95% CI 1.00–1.002, p = 0.010), decreasing with distance (OR 0.84, 95% CI 0.69–0.98, p = 0.018) and monthly average maximum temperature (OR 0.83, 95% CI 0.68–1.02, p = 0.07). The final model retained bioclimatic zone and dog density, with similar trends but no statistical significance.

For U. stenocephala, monthly average maximum temperature produced the lowest AIC (79.3) among the continuous variables, suggesting a positive, though non-significant, association with parasite detection (OR = 1.30, 95% CI: 0.98–1.87, p = 0.068). Due to the small number of positive detections in the lowest NZDep Index category, NZDep Index was collapsed into two levels (“Low–Medium” and “High”) to improve model convergence and stability. NZDep Index showed a strong univariable relationship with U. stenocephala presence with significantly lower odds in low–medium deprivation areas (OR = 0.07, 95% CI: 0.004–0.37, p < 0.001). No multivariable model improved fit sufficiently for retention.

Overall, these results suggest that cSTH occurrence is influenced by climatic, environmental, and host-related factors, with higher dog density and proximity to urban centres increasing contamination risk.

Ten U. stenocephala ITS sequences (PX426507 – PX426516) and four cox1 sequences (PX426646 - PX426649) showed ≥99% identity to published isolates. An 831 bp T. vulpis 18S rDNA sequence (PX980586) similarly showed 99–100% identity with previously published T. vulpis isolates. Discussion

This study presents the first large-scale molecular survey of environmental contamination with cSTHs in urban and recreational areas across New Zealand. We used antigen-based diagnostics, coupled with multiplex qPCR and targeted sequencing to establish a baseline of area-level contamination rates and sample-level detection frequencies, as well as spatial distribution, and associated socio-economic and bioclimatic risk factors. Approximately one-quarter of tested areas in New Zealand were found to be contaminated with at least one cSTH species. This contamination rate is in line with previous reports from Australia, Europe, and North America, suggesting that environmental exposure risk in New Zealand is meaningful and warrants attention [26], [30], [31], [45], [46], [47].

The overall sample-level detection frequency of cSTHs in faeces (4.6%) was comparable to that reported from temperate regions in Australia (5.4%) [26], with similar detection frequencies observed within New Zealand temperate regions (5.3%). The lower detection frequency observed compared with previous dog-based studies in New Zealand [16], [17], [18], [19] likely reflects differences in sampled populations. Dogs attending urban parks are more likely to receive regular veterinary care, including parasite prevention [48], [49] whereas working and shelter dogs often experience higher parasite burdens due to management and exposure factors [50], [51]. Similarly, the low detection frequency of T. canis mirrors findings from Australian parks [26] and likely reflects demographic factors, as puppies, the primary shedders, are often restricted from public areas until fully vaccinated [52]. Despite its relatively low occurrence, T. canis remains of public health importance because its eggs can persist for several years in the environment and become infective after development in soil, allowing environmental contamination and infection risk to accumulate over time [53]. Given that only faecal samples were assessed, overall environmental contamination, and therefore public health risk, may be underestimated [45].

The differing detection profiles between the antigen-based survey and the nationwide molecular survey likely reflect differences in study design, parasite biology, and diagnostic approach. The antigen-based survey occurred in spring (August–October 2023), whereas the molecular survey was conducted in summer (January–April 2024). The two approaches differ fundamentally, with antigen testing detecting prepatent infections and molecular assays detecting DNA from eggs [54], [55]. This distinction is particularly relevant for T. vulpis which has been consistently reported in previous New Zealand surveys [16], [17], [18], [19] and has a long prepatent period (11–12 weeks), intermittent egg shedding, and low faecal egg counts [56]. These traits reduce detection probability of its eggs in cross-sectional molecular surveys. The detection of whipworm antigen in spring (3.5%) alongside low molecular detection in summer (0.4%) is therefore consistent with known parasite biology. Given that T. vulpis can cause chronic gastrointestinal disease, and its thick-shelled eggs can persist in soil for extended periods, continued surveillance coupled with anthelmintic protocols are of relevance for appropriate control [57]. Conversely, hookworms showed the opposite pattern, with no antigen detection in spring, but were the most frequently detected taxa in summer, with A. caninum predominating. This may reflect seasonal biology, as the development and survival of environmental stages of A. caninum are strongly influenced by temperature and moisture, with warmer conditions generally favouring larval development and transmission [58], and/or potentially false-negative results due to low level of faecal antigens in some animals. Together, these findings highlight the complementary nature of diagnostic approaches and the value of integrated surveillance.

From a veterinary and public health perspective, the occurrence of A. caninum is particularly relevant due to its pathogenicity and zoonotic potential [59]. Contrary to expectations, A. caninum was more frequently detected in temperate regions, while U. stenocephala showed no climatic associations. However, microclimatic conditions in urban areas and factors such as dog density and movement may facilitate transmission. Socioeconomic associations observed for U. stenocephala suggest that human-related factors, including veterinary care access and ownership practices, may influence parasite distribution [60], [61]. These findings highlight the importance of local ecological and socio-economic drivers in shaping environmental contamination and increase exposure risk of cSTHs to animal and people.

Despite A. ceylanicum and S. stercoralis being frequently found in dogs in the Asia–Pacific region [62], [63] and in Australia [26], [64], the lack of detection in this survey may reflect climatic constraints, historical isolation or environmental factors being less conducive for their transmission in the country. However, increasing pet movement and climate change could alter risk profiles [61], [65], reinforcing the importance of ongoing molecular surveillance.

Molecular diagnostics provided greater taxonomic resolution than previous microscopy-based studies in New Zealand [16], [17], [18], [19], which were limited in their ability to discriminate closely related species. DNA sequencing supported qPCR detections, providing confidence in species-level assignments and frequency estimates. For example, U. stenocephala sequences from New Zealand were clearly differentiated from other Uncinaria species (U. lucasi, U. hamiltoni, U. sanguinis), confirming species identity and supporting the robustness of molecular characterisation applied in this study [66], [67]. While this study provides a comprehensive overview of environmental contamination of cSTHs, these data are not directly informative for host-level prevalence due to the nature of the study. Additionally, extrapolation of these findings should be limited to the urban recreational environments.

Overall, these findings highlight the importance of One Health parasite surveillance in shared urban environments. Responsible waste disposal, routine anthelmintic treatment, and public education remain key strategies to reduce environmental contamination. Continued application of molecular diagnostic tools will be essential for monitoring parasite distributions and safeguarding both human and animal health.

Conclusion

This study provides the first molecular baseline of cSTHs in New Zealand, demonstrating that environmental contamination of urban areas is common. Across the two complementary surveys, detection frequencies varied among cSTH groups, with hookworms generally detected more frequently than whipworms and roundworms, highlighting the value of integrating diagnostic approaches that capture different biological features of cSTHs. Given that eggs of species such as T. canis and T. vulpis can remain viable in soil for months to years, environmental contamination may accumulate over time and likely exceeds that captured in single cross-sectional surveys. Future research should incorporate longitudinal sampling, host-level data, and spatial risk modelling to improve understanding of parasite ecology, transmission dynamics and emerging parasite risks in the New Zealand dog population. Addressing risk-based parasite control remains essential to protecting companion animal welfare and ensuring the health and safety of shared environments within a One Health framework.
