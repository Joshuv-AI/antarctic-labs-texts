Precision diagnosis of preoperative infection in urolithiasis: integrating targeted next-generation sequencing for enhanced accuracy—a multicenter cohort study
Authors: Zhenglin Chang, Jierong Chen, Jiakai Chen, Yanyun Zhu, Yuexin Zhang, Jialin Liu, Jiwang Deng, Haojie Wu, Lai Bin, Huaming Lin, Ying Liang, Qianjun Li, Baoqing Sun, Guohua Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreoperative infection in urolithiasis patients poses significant risks for surgical complications. Traditional urine culture methods have limitations in detection speed and accuracy, necessitating more precise and rapid diagnostic approaches for preoperative infection assessment.MethodsThis multicenter cohort study evaluated urolithiasis patients with suspected preoperative infections (discovery cohort: n = 114; validation cohort: n = 64), comparing the diagnostic performance of traditional urine culture, metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS), and targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS).ResultsIn urolithiasis patients with suspected preoperative infections, both NGS methods demonstrated 100% sensitivity and negative predictive values (NPV) for pathogen detection, with tNGS showing superior specificity and positive predictive values (PPV) compared to mNGS. tNGS significantly reduced detection time (13 h vs. 48–72 h for traditional culture). mNGS detected 43 distinct pathogens while tNGS identified 42, both revealing previously undetected microorganisms including viruses and fungi. Among culture-positive cases, Escherichia coli (E. coli) was the predominant pathogen with higher prevalence in female patients. In polymicrobial infection patterns, tNGS identified bacterial-viral combinations as the dominant pattern (50.79%), followed by bacterial-mycoplasma/chlamydia (17.46%), and bacterial-viral-fungal (12.70%) combinations. These findings were validated in the second cohort, where bacterial-viral co-infections remained predominant (73.68%). Both NGS methods maintained 100% concordance with culture-positive results in clear and turbid urine samples, with tNGS demonstrating fewer false positives.ConclusionsFor urolithiasis patients with suspected preoperative infections, tNGS emerges as a superior diagnostic tool due to its higher specificity, faster turnaround time, and ability to detect complex pathogen patterns. While mNGS offers advantages in broad-spectrum screening, tNGS provides a more practical approach for precise preoperative infection assessment, potentially improving surgical timing decisions and patient outcomes.Clinical trialNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12119-3.Keywords: Urinary tract infections, Urolithiasis, Targeted next-generation sequencing, Pathogen detection, Clinical infection diagnosis

Introduction

Urolithiasis is one of the most common urinary system diseases worldwide, which carries a high burden of infectious complications that critically impact surgical outcomes [1]. Recent epidemiological studies report UTIs in 20–30% of urolithiasis patients, with struvite and calcium phosphate stones showing the highest infection rates due to biofilm-enhanced bacterial colonization [2]. The consequences of undiagnosed infections are severe: 5–10% of cases progress to urosepsis—accounting for 8.6–30.6% of all sepsis episodes—with mortality risks exceeding 20% [3, 4]. Additional morbidity includes postoperative systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), chronic kidney disease and acute kidney injury [5–7].

Urinary tract infections (UTIs), primarily caused by uropathogenic Escherichia coli (UPEC), are common, costly, and complicated by increasing antibiotic resistance, with E. coli resistance accounting for 48.1% of bloodstream infections in 2020, linked to widespread antibiotic use (2023 World Health Satatics) [8–11]. Current European guidelines emphasize the importance of proper infection control before urological procedures, yet existing diagnostic methods often fall short in providing comprehensive pathogen identification [12, 13]. Traditional culture methods typically require 48–72 h for results and may miss important pathogens, particularly viruses, fungi, and fastidious bacteria [14]. This limitation is particularly problematic in the context of urolithiasis [15], where mixed infections are common but frequently underdiagnosed. Furthermore, the emergence of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) and the increasing recognition of polymicrobial infections have complicated the preoperative assessment of urolithiasis patients, highlighting the need for more sophisticated diagnostic approaches [16–18].

In this multicenter cohort study, we aim to evaluate the clinical utility of two advanced molecular diagnostic approaches - mNGS and tNGS - for detecting preoperative infections in urolithiasis patients. Through parallel comparison with traditional urine culture in both discovery and validation cohorts, we seek to assess their detection efficiency, accuracy, and ability to identify complex pathogen patterns. This study aims to establish whether these next-generation sequencing (NGS) approaches can provide more comprehensive and timely diagnostic information in order to mitigate the high burden of infection-related complications.

Methods

Study protocol and urine sample collection for pathogen detection

All patients were clinically suspected of UTI at the time of enrollment, though systematic symptom data were not uniformly collected. Based on the previously designed tNGS panel for pathogen identification in UTIs, a study was conducted to further evaluate the performance of this panel in real-world clinical settings. To assess the clinical utility of this method, we prospectively evaluated a discovery cohort comprising 400 consecutively enrolled inpatients with suspected UTIs from the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University between December 2023 and April 2024. Samples were first excluded if they had a volume of less than 2 mL, had been stored for more than 7 days, were contaminated, or had unsuccessful DNA extraction. Subsequently, samples without urolithiasis and those that were negative for UTIs culture were excluded. Ultimately, 76 urine culture-positive and 38 urine culture-negative specimens were included for analysis. Urine samples were collected using the clean-catch midstream morning method in sterile dry tubes following standard procedures. Immediately after collection, all samples underwent microbiological culture, antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST), as well as mNGS and tNGS analyses. Concurrently, we collected the clinical information of the patients.

To further evaluate the tNGS panel’s performance in various clinical settings, we gathered an additional 200 urine samples from two distinct cohorts at separate medical centers, which served as validation sets. This study received approval from the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University (Approval number: [ES-2023-229-02]). All specimens were obtained from patients who had signed broad informed consent for the use of their clinical samples in research. Given that only residual specimens from routine clinical examinations were utilized and the study posed minimal risk to patients, the Ethics Committee waived the requirement for specific individual informed consent. The data collection protocol complies with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Microbiological analysis and antibiotic susceptibility testing

Urine samples were inoculated onto Columbia blood agar plates using a 10 µL calibrated loop and the quadrant streaking method. Cultivation was conducted under microaerophilic conditions (5–10% CO₂) at 37 °C for 24–48 h. For the determination of urinary tract infection (UTI), if the bacterial count exceeded 1 × 10^4 CFU/mL, the sample was regarded as positive; otherwise, it was considered negative. When detecting fungi, the positive result was defined by a colony count of ≥ 1 × 10^3 CFU/mL. Isolates with bacterial counts exceeding 1 × 10^4 CFU/mL underwent species identification using matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS). Antimicrobial susceptibility was evaluated using the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method. The results were interpreted according to the guidelines of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) (https://clsi.org/shop/standards/m100/) [19, 20].

Metagenomic pathogen identification workflow

A total of 800 µL urine samples were collected in sterile screw-cap tubes and transferred to centrifuge tubes. Microbial DNA was extracted using the Universal DNA Extraction Kit (Guangzhou Heyuan Biotech, China) on a Smart32 Nucleic Acid Extraction Instrument (Guangzhou Daan Co., Ltd, China). RNA was extracted using the RNA Extraction Kit (Guangzhou Heyuan Biotech, China), and subsequent reverse transcription was performed using the RNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Guangzhou Heyuan Biotech, China). DNA and RNA concentrations were measured with a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer.

For library construction, 5 to 30 ng of DNA/cDNA was fragmented to 200–300 bp using transposase. Libraries were then prepared with the Universal DNA Library Construction Kit for MGI (Guangzhou Heyuan Biotech, China) and sequenced on the MGISEQ-200 platform (MGI, China) using 50-bp single-end reads and the MGISEQ-200RS Reagent Kit (FCL SE50/FCS SE100).

Following sequencing, low-quality reads were removed using fastp-v0.21.0. The remaining reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38/hg38) using SNAP-v1.0.3, and uniquely mapped reads were excluded. Non-human reads were further aligned to a microbial genome database, consisting of approximately 16,000 genomes (including bacteria, viruses, fungi, protozoa, and other eukaryotic pathogens) sourced from NCBI RefSeq, NCBI nt, and the FDA Reference Viral Database, using kraken2-v2.1.1.

Antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) were analyzed by aligning the non-host reads to the Comprehensive Antibiotic Research Database (CARD) (https://card.mcmaster.ca/) using Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA) with parameters: bwa mem -t thread -Y -M -k 17 -T.

Candidate pathogens were identified by removing background contamination from reagent, engineering, and laboratory microorganisms, then comparing the remaining reads to a negative control sample. The reliability of candidate pathogens was confirmed through sequence alignment quality assessment by bioinformatics tools. A comprehensive evaluation of suspected pathogens was made based on the patient’s epidemiological history, clinical diagnosis, and other laboratory results.

Targeted metagenomic sequencing analysis

The principle of pathogen detection is consistent with that of previous studies. Urine samples (800 µL) were collected in sterile screw-cap freezing tubes and transferred to centrifuge tubes. Total nucleic acids (DNA and RNA) were extracted using the Universal DNA Extraction Kit (Guangzhou Heyuan Biotech, China) with the Smart32 Nucleic Acid Extraction Instrument (Guangzhou Daan Co., Ltd, China). Subsequently, RNA reverse transcription was carried out with the RNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Guangzhou Heyuan Biotech, China), utilizing the total nucleic acid obtained earlier. The concentration of the resulting DNA/cDNA was measured with a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer.

Targeted library preparation was performed using the Multiplex DNA Library Construction Kit for 232 microorganisms (Guangzhou Heyuan Biotech, China) with 1 to 100 ng of DNA/cDNA per sample. Sequencing was performed on the DNBSEQ-G99 platform (MGI, China) with 50-bp single-end reads, using the DNBSEQ-G99 SM Reagent Kit (FCL SE100/PE50).

Bioinformatic processing included quality filtering (fastp v0.21.0) followed by alignment to a curated pathogen database containing 173 clinically relevant targets (126 bacterial, 19 viral, 28 fungal species) using BWA alignment. Antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) were predicted by aligning tNGS-generated reads to the CARD (https://card.mcmaster.ca/) using the BWA with the same parameters. Data interpretation and clinical reporting were automated through the DAMicrob analysis platform, an in-house developed bioinformatics pipeline incorporating epidemiological and diagnostic parameters for pathogen confirmation.

Data processing and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software and IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 25.0). One-way ANOVA was applied to assess group differences, followed by post hoc comparisons using the least significant difference (LSD) test or Dunnett’s T3 test when appropriate. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance. For data visualization, the ‘ggplot2’ package in R was used, with final adjustments to color schemes and layout made in Adobe Illustrator, as outlined previously [21–23].

Results

Traditional urine culture results in urolithiasis patients with concurrent infections

From an initial collection of 400 urine samples in the discovery cohort, 286 samples were excluded based on predefined criteria, yielding 114 cases of urolithiasis with concurrent UTIs for final analysis (Figure S1). Among these specimens, 76 samples demonstrated positive culture results, while 38 samples were culture-negative (Fig. 1A). Demographic analysis of culture-positive cases revealed a predominant distribution among middle-aged and elderly populations, with significantly higher prevalence in female patients compared to males (Fig. 1B). In the culture-positive specimens, bacterial pathogens were predominant, with E. coli identified as a key pathogen. Gender-based comparative analysis revealed significant differences in E. coli distribution between male and female patients with urolithiasis (Figs. 1C, D).

Several comorbidities and clinical characteristics showed significant gender-based differences (Table 1). Female patients had a higher prevalence of urinary tract hydronephrosis and effusion (UTHE) (71.1% vs. 46.9%, p = 0.002) and renal insufficiency (RI) (27.8% vs. 13.6%, p = 0.033). In contrast, male patients showed higher rates of hyperuricemia (HUA) (21.0% vs. 9.3%, p = 0.047), renal cysts (RC) (32.1% vs. 13.4%, p = 0.005), and cardiovascular conditions including coronary heart disease (CHD) (17.3% vs. 6.2%, p = 0.036) and heart disease (HD) (25.9% vs. 8.2%, p = 0.003). Analysis of mNGS and tNGS results

We performed both mNGS and tNGS analyses on the selected 114 samples. In the mNGS analysis (Fig. 2A, B), Escherichia coli emerged as the predominant pathogen, with notably higher detection rates in female patients, consistent with traditional culture findings. Interestingly, Enterococcus faecalis ranked as the second most frequently detected pathogen, exhibiting higher detection rates compared to conventional culture methods. The mNGS approach demonstrated superior pathogen detection breadth, identifying previously undetected microorganisms including human polyomavirus, human herpesvirus, mycoplasma, and other pathogens. Additionally, mNGS showed enhanced detection of fungal species, including Candida glabrata, C. tropicalis, and C. albicans, particularly among female patients, surpassing the detection rates of traditional culture methods. The tNGS analysis (Fig. 3A, B) similarly revealed high detection rates for E. coli and E. faecalis, with increased prevalence in female patients. Consistent with mNGS findings, tNGS demonstrated superior detection of fungal species, particularly C. glabrata, compared to traditional culture methods. Notably, tNGS exhibited enhanced sensitivity for high-abundance pathogens, viruses, mycoplasma, and chlamydia. Of particular interest was the significantly higher detection rate of human polyomavirus 2 compared to mNGS analysis, with elevated prevalence observed in female patients.

Comparative performance analysis of mNGS and tNGS technologies

We conducted a comprehensive comparison of pathogen detection capabilities between mNGS and tNGS technologies. In the discovery cohort, both sequencing methods demonstrated excellent sensitivity and NPV (100%) compared to traditional urine culture. However, tNGS exhibited superior specificity and PPV compared to mNGS, indicating enhanced accuracy in excluding non-target pathogens and reduced false-positive rates (Table 2). The higher true positive rate of tNGS suggested greater reliability in confirming the presence of actual pathogens (Fig. 4A). Notably, tNGS (13 h) demonstrated significantly shorter detection times compared to both mNGS (17 h) and traditional culture (48–72 h) methods, highlighting its potential for improving clinical diagnostic efficiency (Fig. 4B). The validation cohort further confirmed these findings, with tNGS maintaining 100% detection rate for culture-positive samples while significantly reducing detection time compared to conventional methods (13 h vs. 48–72 h for traditional culture) (Fig. 4C, D; Table 3). Regarding pathogen identification scope, mNGS detected 43 distinct pathogens compared to 42 by tNGS, demonstrating slightly broader identification potential (Figs. 2A and 3A). In the discovery cohort (n = 114), both NGS methods showed excellent sensitivity with culture-positive results. mNGS achieved 100% concordance in both clear (72/72) and turbid (23/23) urine samples, with tNGS showing identical detection rates. However, tNGS demonstrated superior specificity, with fewer false-positive results compared to mNGS in both clear and turbid samples. These findings were further validated in the validation cohort (n = 64), where both clear and turbid urine samples showed 100% concordance with culture-positive results (Tables 4 and 5).

In conclusion, while mNGS proves optimal for comprehensive screening and broad pathogen identification, tNGS emerges as the preferred choice for precise diagnosis and non-target pathogen exclusion, offering enhanced specificity and PPV. This complementarity suggests distinct but valuable roles for each technology in clinical diagnostics. Despite the limitations, our study provides strong evidence for the clinical utility of tNGS as a rapid and accurate diagnostic tool for preoperative infection assessment in urolithiasis patients.

Pathogen co-occurrence patterns

While traditional urine culture methods were unable to detect co-existing pathogens, both tNGS and mNGS successfully identified various polymicrobial infections. Analysis of both sequencing methods revealed bacterial infections and pathogen co-existence as predominant patterns. In the discovery cohort, mNGS analysis identified bacterial-fungal (35.48%) and bacterial-viral (35.48%) combinations as the most prevalent co-occurrence patterns, followed by bacterial-viral-fungal (19.35%), bacterial-fungal-mycoplasma/chlamydia (6.45%), and bacterial-mycoplasma/chlamydia (3.23%) combinations. In contrast, tNGS analysis revealed bacterial-viral combinations as the dominant pattern (50.79%), with additional patterns including bacterial-mycoplasma/chlamydia (17.46%), bacterial-viral-fungal (12.70%), bacterial-viral-mycoplasma/chlamydia (11.11%), bacterial-fungal-mycoplasma/chlamydia (4.76%), and bacterial-fungal and viral-fungal combinations (1.59% each). Compared to traditional culture methods, both NGS approaches demonstrated superior capabilities in comprehensive pathogen detection. Notably, tNGS identified a broader spectrum of co-existence patterns than mNGS, indicating enhanced detection capabilities (Fig. 5A, B). The validation cohort corroborated these findings (Fig. 5C, D), with bacterial-viral co-infections emerging as the predominant pattern (73.68%), consistent with the discovery cohort results.

Antibiotics resistance prediction

Among the 114 urolithiasis cases with concurrent infections, 95 samples demonstrated bacterial positivity with available AST results. AST analysis focused on Gram-negative bacteria (Enterobacteriaceae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter baumannii, Klebsiella pneumoniae) and Gram-positive bacteria (Staphylococcus and Enterococcus species). For Gram-negative bacteria, resistance to penicillins and third-generation cephalosporins was predominant, with resistance rates of 60% (39/65) and 41.54% (27/65), respectively, while carbapenem resistance remained relatively low (3.07%, 2/65) (Table S1-1). Among Staphylococcus aureus isolates (n = 2), no methicillin resistance was observed. For Enterococcus faecalis (n = 3) and Enterococcus faecium (n = 7), only one exhibited vancomycin resistance (Table S1-2).

ARG analysis was performed to predict susceptibility to antibiotics commonly used for UTIs, leveraging the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD). ARG detection results were compared with AST outcomes to validate predictive accuracy. For Gram-negative bacteria (n = 65), Extended-Spectrum β-Lactamase (ESBL) genes (blaCTX−M, blaTEM, blaSHV) and carbapenem-hydrolyzing class D β-lactamase (CHDL) genes (blaKPC, blaNDM, blaOXA−23−like, blaOXA−48−like, blaOXA−51−like) were analyzed to assess resistance to penicillins, cephalosporins, and carbapenems. tNGS demonstrated higher ARGs detection rates than mNGS (64.62% vs. 47.69%, p = 0.077), with more samples showing ≥ 2 ESBLs or CHDLs via tNGS (Table S1-1). For cephalosporin resistance prediction, tNGS exhibited superior sensitivity (96.15%, 95% CI: 81.11–99.32%) compared to mNGS (77.78%, 95% CI: 59.24–89.39%), though specificity was lower for tNGS (55.26%, 95% CI: 39.71–69.85%) versus mNGS (76.32%, 95% CI: 60.79–87.01%). Similar trends were observed for penicillin resistance prediction, with tNGS showing higher sensitivity (73.17%, 95% CI: 58.07–84.31%) but lower specificity than mNGS (Table 6). For carbapenem resistance, both tNGS and mNGS showed high specificity, though limited sample size precluded robust sensitivity assessment (Table 6).

Subgroup analysis revealed that blaCTX−M alone provided sufficient sensitivity (81.48%, 95% CI: 63.30–91.82%) and specificity (84.21%, 95% CI: 69.58–92.56%) for predicting third-generation cephalosporin resistance via tNGS. Incorporating blaSHV marginally improved sensitivity, while adding blaTEM significantly reduced specificity despite further sensitivity gains (Table S1-1, Table S1-3). Combining blaCTX−M, blaSHV, and CHDL genes optimized predictive accuracy for cephalosporin resistance (Table S1-3, Table 6).

For Gram-positive bacteria, both tNGS and mNGS successfully predicted vancomycin resistance in one case of Enterococcus faecium. However, the predictive accuracy of these methods requires validation with larger sample sizes due to the limited number of resistant cases (only 1 case) in this cohort(Table S1-2).

In the validation cohort, tNGS demonstrated consistent ARGs detection rates for ESBLs, CHDLs, mecA, and vanA/B/C compared to the discovery cohort, confirming stable sensitivity and specificity across populations (Table S2).

Discussion

This multicenter cohort study presents compelling evidence for the superiority of tNGS in diagnosing preoperative infections among urolithiasis patients [24]. Our findings demonstrate that tNGS achieves optimal diagnostic performance with 100% sensitivity and enhanced specificity compared to both traditional culture methods and mNGS, while significantly reducing detection time from the conventional 48–72 h to just 13 h [25, 26]. The comprehensive pathogen detection capability revealed complex polymicrobial infection patterns, with bacterial-viral combinations emerging as the predominant pattern, challenging traditional culture-based diagnostic paradigms [27]. Furthermore, our gender-specific analysis unveiled distinct differences in pathogen distribution and clinical characteristics, providing new insights into infection patterns in urolithiasis patients. By incorporating antibiotic resistance gene (ARG) analysis, we validate the significance of advanced molecular diagnostic methods in clinical settings moreover, especially in the context of escalating AMR.

The enhanced diagnostic accuracy of tNGS addresses a critical clinical need in urolithiasis management. Recent studies have highlighted the increasing challenges in diagnosing complicated UTIs, particularly in patients with underlying urological conditions [28, 29]. The limitations of diagnostic tools and methods have increased the difficulty of diagnosis, along with the high recurrence rate of diseases [30–32]. Our findings extend this understanding by demonstrating that tNGS can detect a broader spectrum of pathogens while maintaining higher specificity than mNGS. This is particularly relevant given the increasing recognition of polymicrobial infections in urolithiasis, as documented by Li et al. who found complex microbial communities associated with infectious stones [16]. The ability to accurately identify multiple pathogens simultaneously represents a significant advancement over traditional culture methods [33, 34], which often fail to detect polymicrobial infections [12, 13].

The rapid turnaround time of tNGS (13 h) represents a significant advancement in preoperative assessment. This aligns with recent guidelines emphasizing the importance of timely infection control before urological procedures [8, 10]. The ability to quickly identify potential pathogens, including viruses and fungi that are typically missed by traditional culture, enables more informed decision-making regarding surgical timing and antibiotic selection. From a clinical perspective, this means that tNGS could facilitate earlier initiation of targeted therapy, reduce surgical delays, and ultimately improve perioperative safety and patient outcomes. This is particularly crucial given the rising concerns about AMR in urological infections [35], Escherichia coli has a high degree of resistance to multiple antibiotics [36] with recent data showing E. coli resistance rates exceeding more than 50% in some populations [37, 38]. Notably, our study observed a high prevalence of bacterial infections among urolithiasis patients, with Escherichia coli being the predominant pathogen, particularly among female patients. The resistance of Gram-negative bacteria to third-generation cephalosporins exceeding 40% among urolithiasis patients necessitates an urgent reevaluation of empirical treatment strategies. The speed and accuracy of tNGS could potentially reduce unnecessary antibiotic use and improve patient outcomes by enabling more targeted antimicrobial therapy [39, 40].

This study has limitations. The primary limitation is the relatively small cohort size (Discovery Cohort: n = 114; Validation Cohort: n = 64), which may affect the statistical power of subgroup analyses and the generalizability of the findings. Future larger multicenter studies are needed to confirm our results and to correlate tNGS findings with clinical outcomes such as surgical timing and postoperative infection rates. Furthermore, the absence of systematically collected clinical symptom data introduces a potential for selection bias. Another consideration is that while we demonstrated the ability to detect ARGs, the clinical correlation with phenotypic resistance for all pathogen-drug combinations requires further validation in larger samples.

Our observation of distinct polymicrobial infection patterns, particularly the prevalence of bacterial-viral co-infections (50.79% in discovery cohort, 73.68% in validation cohort), provides new insights into the complexity of UTIs in urolithiasis patients. This finding builds upon recent work on bacteria-stone associations [2], but extends beyond by revealing the significant role of viral co-infections. The gender-specific differences in pathogen distribution and clinical characteristics align with current understanding of UTI pathogenesis [41, 42], and provide additional granularity regarding the impact of gender on infection patterns in urolithiasis. These findings highlight that current empirical treatment approaches may be insufficient and support the clinical utility of adopting preoperative urine NGS as a complementary diagnostic tool.

The inclusion of antibiotic resistance gene (ARG) analysis enhances the clinical utility of NGS technologies in our study. AMR among uropathogens has emerged as a significant global concern, with increasing resistance to conventional antibiotics posing challenges in treatment decision-making [35]. Our data indicate that among Gram-negative bacteria, resistance to penicillins and third-generation cephalosporins was 77.35% and 41.53%, respectively, while carbapenem resistance remained relatively low at 3.07%. The presence of ESBL encoding genes (such as bla_CTX-M, bla_TEM, bla_SHV) was particularly prominent, especially among E. coli isolates, consistent with reports of rising resistance rates in previous studies. Comparative analysis between mNGS and tNGS for ARG detection revealed a higher detection rate with tNGS (64.62%) compared to mNGS (47.69%). This finding suggests that targeted sequencing enhances resistance prediction capabilities. Additionally, tNGS was more effective in detecting high-abundance genes associated with carbapenem resistance, such as class D β-lactamase (CHDL) genes bla_KPC, bla_NDM, and bla_OXA variants. Notably, while tNGS exhibited a higher false-positive rate in detecting certain antibiotic resistance genes, it demonstrated greater sensitivity and predictive accuracy in identifying third-generation cephalosporin resistance genes like bla_CTX-M. This reinforces its value in guiding individualized antimicrobial therapy prior to surgery.

The identification of previously undetected pathogens through NGS methods has important clinical implications [43]. While recent advances in urolithiasis treatment have been documented, our findings suggest that current diagnostic and treatment approaches may need significant revision. The high prevalence of bacterial-viral co-infections raises questions about the adequacy of current antibiotic-focused treatment strategies. This aligns with recent calls for more personalized approaches to UTI management [44], which integrates antibiotic resistance prediction, patient data and guidelines to provide personalized suggestions for UTI management. As highlighted by emerging evidence of complex host-pathogen interactions in UTIs, the new evidence calls for the development of personalized treatment strategies [45]. The ability of tNGS to detect these complex pathogen patterns could enable more targeted therapeutic approaches, potentially improving treatment outcomes while reducing the risk of AMR. Future studies that integrate large-scale clinical and compositionaI data, such as those we reported before [46], could lead to powerful predictive models for personalized patient care and improved surgical outcomes.

In summary, our preliminary findings suggest that the implementation of tNGS in the preoperative assessment of urolithiasis patients can improve diagnostic accuracy, assist in formulating personalized antimicrobial treatment plans, and reduce the incidence of postoperative infections, with future gene-targeted anti-fibrotic strategies—already explored in polycystic kidney disease—potentially breaking the infection-stone-CKD cycle [47]. We propose further exploration of the potential of preoperative urine NGS as an adjunct to clinical decision - making. However, its widespread adoption is currently hampered by several practical limitations, including high costs, the need for specialized sequencing equipment, challenges in data interpretation, and the lack of unified clinical guidelines. Therefore, future research endeavors should not only focus on validating its clinical efficacy through larger - scale prospective studies but also evaluate cost - effectiveness and establish standardized frameworks to facilitate its incorporation into routine clinical practice. A promising direction is integrating tNGS infection data, with renal risk factors—aligning with Yu et al., who built an MRD prediction nomogram (AUC = 0.705) using factors like hypertension. This combined model could let clinicians assess both infection and renal risk, optimizing personalized care [48].
