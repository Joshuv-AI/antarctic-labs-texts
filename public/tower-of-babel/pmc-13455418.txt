Influence of simulated gastric acid exposure on surface characteristics and color stability of flowable and packable bulk-fill composites
Authors: Meltem Tekbaş-Atay, Mediha Büyükgöze-Dindar, Refik Poyraz Dindar, Melike Güler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the effects of simulated gastric acid exposure on the surface roughness, gloss, and color stability of flowable and packable bulk-fill composite resins.MethodsFive composite materials were evaluated: a universal composite—Estelite Σ Quick (Tokuyama Dental, Japan) [EQ]; two packable bulk-fill composites—Filtek One Bulk Fill Restorative (3M ESPE, USA) [FR] and Tetric N-Ceram Bulk-Fill (Ivoclar Vivadent, Liechtenstein) [TN]; and two flowable bulk-fill composites—Estelite Bulk-Fill Flow (Tokuyama Dental, Japan) [EF] and Hi-Fill Bulk-Flow (FSM Dental, Türkiye) [HF]. Standardized disc-shaped specimens (n = 12 per group) were fabricated using Mylar strips; no finishing or polishing procedures were performed on the specimen surfaces. Baseline measurements of surface roughness (Ra), gloss (GU), and color parameters (for ΔE₀₀ calculation) were obtained prior to exposure using a contact profilometer, glossmeter, and spectrophotometer, respectively. The specimens were then subjected to simulated gastric acid (pH 1.2) for durations corresponding to 1 year (3 h) and 5 years (15 h) of cumulative exposure. Following each exposure period, all measurements were repeated. Color change (ΔE₀₀) were calculated according to CIE2000 formula.ResultsSimulated gastric acid exposure resulted in a significant increase in surface roughness and color change, along with a reduction in gloss values in most materials (p < 0.05). Surface roughness increased significantly in all groups except EQ (p > 0.05), with the highest values observed in the TN group in a 5-year simulation (0.43 ± 0.25 μm; p = 0.037). Gloss values significantly decreased in all materials (p ≤ 0.007), although FR exhibited a more limited reduction, reaching significance only after prolonged exposure (p = 0.017), and maintained the highest gloss after 5 years of simulation (74.9 ± 8.9 GU). ΔE₀₀ increased significantly in all groups over time (p < 0.001), with the greatest change observed in EQ (3.18 ± 0.7).ConclusionSimulated gastric acid exposure compromised the surface and optical properties of all tested composites, with flowable bulk-fill materials demonstrating greater susceptibility than packable counterparts. FR exhibited superior performance, preserving gloss and maintaining color changes within clinically acceptable limits (ΔE₀₀≤1.8). These findings highlight the pivotal influence of filler content, resin matrix composition, and photoinitiator system on composite resistance to acid-induced surface degradation and optical instability.

Background

The long-term success and clinical durability of resin-based composite restorations are closely linked to their physical, mechanical, and esthetic performance [1]. These properties, however, can be significantly compromised by the complex and often aggressive conditions within the oral cavity. Among these challenges, the acidic environment of the oral cavity plays a critical role-particularly in cases of dental erosion, which may arise from either extrinsic or intrinsic sources [2]. Extrinsic erosion is typically associated with the frequent intake of acidic foods and beverages, whereas intrinsic erosion is primarily linked to medical conditions such as gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) and bulimia nervosa [3].

GERD is characterized by the involuntary relaxation of the upper esophageal sphincter, resulting in the regurgitation of gastric contents into the oral cavity [4, 5]. In contrast, bulimia nervosa, an eating disorder marked by cycles of binge eating followed by compensatory behaviors such as self-induced vomiting, also exposes dental structures to acidic insults [6]. In both cases, the oral environment is intermittently subjected to gastric fluid rich in hydrochloric acid (HCl), a highly corrosive agent with a pH typically ranging from 0.9 to 1.5 [7]. Although the buffering capacity of saliva and esophageal secretions can raise this pH slightly—often not below 1.5 after emesis—the repeated exposure to such a low pH poses a serious threat to both dental hard tissues and restorative materials, facilitating the initiation and progression of erosive lesions [8].

In response to clinical demands for more efficient restorative procedures, bulk-fill composite resins have been introduced. These materials enable the placement of increments up to 4–5 mm in a single layer, thereby reducing the number of clinical steps and chair time compared to traditional incremental techniques [2]. Moreover, bulk-fill composites are designed to minimize polymerization shrinkage, which in turn reduces cuspal deflection and the potential for marginal leakage [1]. To achieve adequate depth of cure, manufacturers have employed various strategies, including increasing filler particle size, reducing filler content, and incorporating enhanced photo-initiator systems [9]. Bulk-fill composites are commonly divided into two main categories: high-viscosity (packable) and low-viscosity (flowable) types, each exhibiting distinct rheological and mechanical characteristics due to differences in their filler content and resin matrix composition [10].

Despite their advantages, limited data exist on the chemical resilience of bulk-fill composites under clinically relevant acidic conditions, particularly those simulating intrinsic erosion caused by gastric acid exposure. Acid-induced degradation can adversely affect their surface characteristics and optical stability [11]. Exposure to low pH environments may lead to hydrolysis of the polymer network, increased porosity at the filler-matrix interface, and subsequent deterioration in gloss, surface smoothness, and color stability [4]. These changes not only compromise the esthetic outcome but may also contribute to greater bacterial adherence, particularly when surface roughness exceeds the critical threshold of 0.2 μm [12]. Considering the clinical significance of acid-induced erosion in individuals affected by conditions such as GERD and bulimia nervosa, evaluating the performance of restorative materials under such acidic challenges is of paramount importance. Addressing this gap, the present study aimed to investigate the effects of simulated gastric acid exposure on the surface roughness, gloss, and color stability of packable and flowable bulk-fill composite resins.

The tested null hypothesis was that simulated gastric acid exposure would have no significant effect on the surface roughness, gloss, or color stability of either packable or flowable bulk-fill composite resins.

This in vitro study investigated the effects of simulated gastric acid exposure on five resin-based restorative materials: two flowable bulk-fill composites, two packable bulk-fill composites, and one universal composite resin used as a control. A power analysis was conducted based on previous literature [13], using a significance level of 0.05, a statistical power of 80%, and an effect size of 0.63. As a result, 60 specimens were prepared (n = 12 per material group).

Disc-shaped specimens (8 mm diameter × 2 mm thickness) were fabricated using cylindrical plexiglass molds. Uncured composites were placed into the molds, and Mylar polyester strips were applied to both surfaces to minimize the formation of oxygen inhibition layer and standardize the surface texture. A glass slide was gently pressed to ensure a flat surface and remove excess material. No finishing or polishing procedures were performed, and the Mylar-formed surfaces were preserved for all measurements. This approach was adopted to standardize baseline surface conditions across all materials.

Polymerization was carried out using an LED curing unit (Elipar DeepCure-S, 3M ESPE, USA) operating in standard mode with an output intensity of approximately 1470 mW/cm², applied for 20 s with the light tip positioned perpendicularly and in close proximity to the specimen surface. Light intensity was periodically verified using a radiometer (SDI LED Radiometer, Bayswater, Australia) to ensure consistency.

Following polymerization, specimen thickness was verified using a digital caliper (Powerfix Electronic Digital Caliper, Padget Services, London, UK) to ensure standardization. Each specimen was labeled on the top surface using a permanent marker for consistent orientation in repeated measurements. All samples were stored in distilled water at 37 °C in an incubator for 24 h to complete initial polymerization and simulate oral conditions prior to baseline testing.

Surface roughness, gloss, and color were assessed at three time points: baseline (after 24 h of water storage), following 1 year, and after 5 years of simulated gastric acid exposure [14].

Surface roughness (Ra) was evaluated using a contact profilometer (Surtronic S128, Taylor Hobson, Leicester, UK) with a 0.8 mm cutoff and a traverse speed of 0.6 mm/s. The device was calibrated before each measurement session using a reference calibration block (Ra = 5.81 μm). Three non-overlapping scans were performed on each specimen, and the mean Ra value was recorded.

Surface gloss was quantified using a glossmeter (Novo-Curve, Rhopoint Instruments, UK) operating at a 60° geometry, consistent with international standards for dental materials. Calibration was performed using a manufacturer-provided black glass standard (93.3 Gloss Units). For each specimen, three measurements were taken, with the sample rotated 90° between readings [15].

Color stability was assessed using a spectrophotometer (VITA Easyshade V, Vita Zahnfabrik, Germany). All measurements were performed by a single operator at a consistent location, using the same device and a standardized white background. To reduce potential diurnal variation, readings were consistently taken at the same time of day. Three readings were obtained from each specimen, and the mean values were recorded. Where the terms ΔL′, ΔC′, and ΔH′ represent the perceived differences in lightness, chroma, and hue between two color samples, RT known as the rotation function, introduces a correction for the interplay between chroma and hue variations, particularly in the blue region of the color space. To account for the non-uniformity of the CIELAB color space, weighting functions (SL, SC and SH) are employed, adjusting the influence of lightness, chroma, and hue differences based on their location in the L′a′b′ space. Additionally, parametric factors (KL, KC and KH) are included to accommodate variations in viewing conditions and experimental settings.

Simulated gastric acid was prepared by dissolving 0.113% hydrochloric acid in deionized water and adjusting the pH to 1.2 using a calibrated pH meter (Hanna HI 83141, USA). In accordance with the protocol established by Backer et al. [14], 3 h of immersion at 37 °C was deemed equivalent to one year of clinical exposure to gastric reflux or emesis. Specimens were thus immersed in the simulated gastric solution for 3 h in an incubator (Nüve, Turkey). Subsequent to this initial exposure, surface roughness, gloss, and color measurements were repeated (Fig. 1).

To simulate five years of cumulative exposure, the specimens were further immersed for an additional 12 h (yielding a total of 15 h of acid challenge), after which all surface assessments were repeated to monitor progressive degradation.

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 23.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Shapiro–Wilk test was employed to evaluate the normality of data distribution. For variables conforming to a normal distribution, parametric analyses were employed. Intergroup comparisons involving more than two groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple pairwise comparisons. When comparisons were restricted to two independent groups, the independent samples t-test was utilized. Intragroup comparisons across time points were performed using paired samples t-tests. For variables that violated the assumption of normality, non-parametric methods were applied. Intergroup comparisons at each time point were carried out using the Kruskal–Wallis test, with Mann–Whitney U tests employed for post hoc pairwise analyses where appropriate. Intragroup comparisons were assessed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Intragroup comparisons, however, revealed a general trend toward increased surface roughness over time, although the extent and statistical significance of these changes varied among materials. EQ exhibited minimal variation across all time points, with no statistically significant differences (p > 0.05). Similarly, EF showed a gradual increase in median Ra values, with statistically significant differences observed between baseline and 1-year (p = 0.044) and between baseline and 5-year (p = 0.008), while the change between 1 and 5 years was not significant (p > 0.05).

In contrast, FR, TN, and HF demonstrated more pronounced increases in surface roughness over time. FR showed statistically significant increases between baseline and 5 years (p = 0.011) and between 1 and 5 years (p = 0.042), indicating progressive surface degradation. TN also demonstrated a significant increase in Ra between 1 and 5 years, while earlier comparisons did not yield statistically significant differences. Similarly, HF demonstrated significant increases between baseline and 5 years (p = 0.004) and between 1 and 5 years (p = 0.007), reflecting a time-dependent deterioration in surface characteristics.

At baseline, surface gloss values ranged from 69.5 ± 8.4 GU (EF) to 78.9 ± 7.5 GU (FR), with significant intergroup differences observed (p = 0.028). FR demonstrated the highest initial gloss and was significantly different from EF, indicating inherent variability in the optical properties of the materials prior to acidic exposure. After 1 year of immersion in simulated gastric acid, the intergroup variation in gloss remained statistically significant (p = 0.001). While FR retained its superior gloss level (77.6 ± 8.7 GU), a marked reduction was observed in TN (60.8 ± 11.8 GU), EF (60.2 ± 14.7 GU), and HF (64.1 ± 6.9 GU). By the 5-year evaluation, gloss values had further declined across all groups, and the differences between materials were statistically significant (p = 0.000). FR continued to show the highest gloss retention (74.9 ± 8.9 GU), whereas all other materials demonstrated substantial reductions.

EQ experienced a significant and progressive decline in gloss from 71.4 ± 9.1 GU at baseline to 63.4 ± 7.4 GU at 1 year (p = 0.006) and 57.4 ± 6.7 GU at 5 years (p = 0.000). HF followed a similar degradation pattern, decreasing from 75.1 ± 6.1 GU to 64.1 ± 6.9 GU at 1 year (p = 0.000) and 57.4 ± 5.5 GU at 5 years (p = 0.000), indicating high susceptibility to acid-induced surface deterioration. TN and EF also showed significant gloss loss from baseline to 1 year (p = 0.003 and p = 0.007, respectively) and to 5 years (p = 0.001 and p = 0.003), though no further significant change was observed between 1 and 5 years, indicating that most surface changes occurred early in the exposure period. In contrast, FR presented the highest initial gloss value (78.9 ± 7.5 GU) and demonstrated the greatest retention over time, with only a modest yet significant reduction observed at 5 years (78.9 ± 7.5 to 74.9 ± 8.9 GU; p = 0.017), suggesting superior gloss retention under acidic conditions.

Discussion

This study tested the hypothesis that prolonged exposure to simulated gastric acid adversely affects the surface roughness, gloss, and color stability of bulk-fill composite resins. The findings partially support this hypothesis: although all materials exhibited measurable alterations in optical and surface properties over time, only certain composites, notably FR, maintained parameters within clinically acceptable limits after five years, whereas EF and TN displayed perceptible or clinically relevant changes. These outcomes underscore the influence of material composition—particularly filler content, filler morphology, and resin matrix chemistry—on resistance to acidic challenges.

A principal strength of this investigation is its rigorous and clinically oriented methodology, which aimed to replicate intrinsic erosive conditions relevant to patients with GERD or bulimia nervosa. Although simulating gastric acid exposure in vitro is clinically relevant, no universally accepted protocol exists [3]. Previous studies have used varying immersion durations and acidic media, with continuous 24-hour exposure representing a worst-case scenario [16], 45 min approximating one month of real-life exposure [17], and 6–24 h corresponding to 2–8 years of clinical exposure [14]. In line with the protocol established by Backer et al. [14], the present study adopted immersion durations of 3 h and 15 h at 37 °C to simulate cumulative exposure over 1 year and 5 years, respectively. The use of hydrochloric acid adjusted to pH 1.2 [18]—reflective of actual gastric fluid—further enhances the clinical relevance of the simulation. This methodological approach offers a structured and evidence-based means of evaluating the long-term chemical and optical stability of restorative materials under highly acidic conditions.

Another key strength of the present study is the standardization of specimen fabrication and polymerization procedures, which minimized operator-dependent variability and ensured reproducible surface characteristics. All specimens were prepared using identical molds and polymerized by the same operator with a single light-curing unit, the intensity of which was periodically monitored to ensure consistency throughout the study. To eliminate the formation of an oxygen-inhibited layer and to achieve an initially smooth and glossy surface, Mylar strips were applied during polymerization. This approach is supported by recent literature indicating that the smoothest and glossiest surfaces are achieved with Mylar strips, and that subsequent polishing procedures typically yield comparable results [19]. Therefore, no additional finishing or polishing was applied to the specimens. This decision was further justified by the inherent variability in polishing procedures, where factors such as the type and abrasiveness of the polishing system, as well as operator-dependent variables like applied force and duration, can significantly influence the outcome [20].

The long-term behavior of resin-based composite materials under simulated gastric acid exposure appears to be closely related to their filler content, filler morphology, and resin matrix composition [21]. In this study, the universal nanohybrid composite EQ was selected as the control group due to its exceptionally high filler content (82 wt%, 71 vol%) and uniformly distributed supra-nano spherical fillers. These characteristics likely contributed to its superior resistance to surface degradation, as reflected in minimal changes in surface roughness over time. Conversely, EF, despite sharing a similar filler morphology and manufacturer with the control material EQ, exhibited the highest degree of color change and marked reductions in surface gloss. This disparity may be attributed to EF’s comparatively lower filler content (70 wt%, 56 vol%) and the inclusion of more hydrophilic monomers, such as Bis-MPEPP, within its resin matrix—factors that likely increased its susceptibility to hydrolytic degradation and pigment uptake under acidic conditions. This finding is consistent with previous research by Meniawi et al., which reported greater discoloration in Bis-MPEPP-containing composites due to their chemical composition and moisture sensitivity [22]. Nonetheless, surface roughness values for EF remained comparable to those of the control group throughout the exposure period, suggesting that its surface topography was less affected despite pronounced optical deterioration.

The findings observed in the present in vitro investigation align with the anticipated behavior of resin-based composite materials subjected to acidic environments, owing to the inherent chemical vulnerability of their polymer matrices. As previously demonstrated, monomers such as triethylene glycol dimethacrylate (TEGDMA) possess greater hydrophilic potential compared to alternatives such as bisphenol-A-glycidyl dimethacrylate (Bis-GMA), urethane dimethacrylate (UDMA), and hexanediol dimethacrylate (HDMDMA), a phenomenon attributed to the higher density of polar functional groups (e.g., hydroxyl, ether, and ester linkages) within their molecular structure [23]. Increased hydrophilicity has been associated with greater water sorption, which can in turn accelerate hydrolytic degradation and promote the leaching of filler particles—processes that may compromise surface integrity and optical performance over time [14].

In light of this, the markedly higher color change and significant reduction in gloss observed in the EF and HF groups may be attributed to their inclusion of TEGDMA-based monomer systems, which render them more susceptible to hydrolytic breakdown and pigment penetration under acidic conditions. Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that both materials maintained comparatively low surface roughness values throughout the observation period. This outcome may be attributed to the flowable nature of these materials, which allows for enhanced adaptability to the Mylar strip during specimen fabrication, thereby promoting the formation of smoother and more homogeneous surfaces through the minimization of surface irregularities. From a clinical perspective, this may support their applicability in esthetically demanding regions, although their hydrophilic nature necessitates caution when used in moisture-prone environments, such as subgingival areas, where increased water exposure could potentially impair long-term esthetic outcomes.

Tetric N-Ceram Bulk-Fill (TN), exhibiting a comparatively lower filler load (75–77 wt%, 53–55 vol%), demonstrated statistically significant increases in surface roughness and color instability over time. Notably, despite the absence of TEGDMA in its formulation, the TN group presented the highest surface roughness values accompanied by significant alterations in gloss. These findings may be attributable to its relatively reduced filler content and the incorporation of Bis-GMA within its monomeric matrix, which potentially contains residual unreacted monomers. Given that Bis-GMA is known to exhibit a greater degree of hydrophilicity compared to UDMA, its presence may contribute to the observed susceptibility to surface degradation and color change [24]. In addition, previous research has suggested that the photoinitiator system of TN, particularly the presence of Ivocerin-a germanium-based photoinitiator unique to this material-may also influence its susceptibility to staining due to its effect on polymer network formation and the degree of conversion [25]. Consistent with these results, Arnez et al. reported that TN exhibited the most pronounced color changes following exposure to hydrochloric acid among the materials tested [7].

By contrast, the FR material demonstrated superior performance in terms of gloss retention and exhibited the least degree of color change over the five-year simulation period. Notably, this composite does not contain TEGDMA, but instead incorporates less hydrophilic monomers such as UDMA, along with advanced monomer technologies including addition–fragmentation monomers (AFM) and aromatic urethane dimethacrylate (AUDMA). These components are designed to enhance polymer network stability by reducing water sorption, minimizing polymer relaxation, and improving resistance to acidic degradation. The presence of such advanced monomer systems likely contributed to the improved esthetic and surface stability of FR, underscoring the relevance of monomer selection in the long-term clinical performance of bulk-fill composites exposed to chemically aggressive environments.

An important consideration in interpreting the findings of the present study lies in the thresholds used to determine the clinical relevance of changes in surface properties, particularly gloss, roughness, and color stability. The perceptibility and acceptability limits commonly adopted in the literature are not universally standardized, leading to potential ambiguity regarding the clinical impact of observed variations. For instance, surface gloss evaluation is subject to variability based on the observer’s visual perception, measurement instrumentation, and the coordinate system employed. According to the American Dental Association (ADA), a surface gloss level between 40 and 60 gloss units (GU) is considered clinically acceptable [26]. Furthermore, Cook and Thomas [27] proposed that gloss values below 60 GU are generally regarded as poor, whereas values between 60 and 80 GU are deemed acceptable. More recently, studies investigating the perceptual thresholds for gloss variation have reported that gloss differences as small as approximately 6 GU can be perceived by only trained observers, while changes exceeding 35 GU are often deemed unacceptable in clinical terms [28]. In the current study, despite prolonged exposure to simulated gastric acid, all materials maintained gloss values close to or within the ADA-defined acceptable range after five years, indicating that the optical deterioration observed was unlikely to compromise clinical esthetics.

Similarly, the evaluation of surface roughness should be contextualized using clinically relevant thresholds. A surface roughness (Ra) value below 0.2 μm has been proposed in the literature as a critical limit to minimize bacterial adhesion and subsequent plaque accumulation, which can lead to secondary caries [29]. When the present results are considered within this framework, it appears that the TN and FR groups approached or exceeded this threshold after five years of acidic challenge, potentially posing a higher risk for biofilm formation. In contrast, the other tested materials remained within or near the clinically acceptable range, suggesting more favorable surface integrity under erosive conditions.

With regard to color stability, previous studies have reported a CIE Lab ΔE threshold of 3.3 for clinically perceptible color differences [30], whereas some sources suggest a ΔE of 3.7 as the level at which 50% of observers would consider the change acceptable [31]. In the present study, color stability was evaluated using the CIEDE2000 formula (ΔE₀₀) in accordance with Paravina et al. [32, 33], applying an acceptability threshold (AT) of 1.8. Using this criterion, all materials remained within clinically acceptable limits after 1 year. However, after 5 years, only FR maintained color changes at the threshold of clinical acceptability, while all other materials exhibited perceptible discoloration, with the most pronounced change observed in EF.

Despite the rigorous methodology employed, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, as an in vitro investigation, the experimental conditions cannot fully replicate the dynamic and multifactorial environment of the oral cavity, where salivary flow, enzymatic activity, mechanical wear, and thermal fluctuations interact with restorative materials. Although the simulated gastric acid exposure was standardized and based on established protocols, continuous immersion does not accurately reflect the intermittent nature and buffering effects of physiological reflux episodes. Moreover, the absence of mechanical forces, such as mastication and toothbrushing, may have led to an underestimation of the overall degradation of material surfaces. A further limitation is that all measurements were performed directly on Mylar-formed surfaces without polishing, which does not fully reflect clinical practice; consequently, the reported surface roughness, gloss, and color stability may represent an idealized scenario and could differ from values obtained on polished restorations in vivo. Finally, while this methodological choice enhanced inter-sample standardization, it may affect the generalizability of the findings. Future in vivo or long-term clinical studies are warranted to validate these results and assess their applicability under real-world conditions.
