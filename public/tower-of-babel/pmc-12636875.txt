Access to emergency care in primary healthcare system in Tanzania: a mixed-method community-based study in a resource-limited setting
Authors: Gimbo Hyuha, Manase Kilonzi, Nathanael Sirili, Notikela Nyamle, George Kiwango, Juma Athumani Mfinanga, Raya Mussa, Gasto Frumence, Hendry R Sawe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTimely access to emergency care services (ECS) is vital for reducing morbidity and mortality from acute conditions. While global barriers to ECS are well documented, little attention has been paid to access within primary healthcare (PHC) in low- and middle-income countries. This study assessed access to ECS at PHC in Tanzania, offering insights relevant to similar contexts.MethodsWe conducted a community-based, mixed-methods study in December 2021 across four villages in three Tanzanian regions. A structured questionnaire based on WHO-ECS indicators was administered to 673 randomly selected community members, covering healthcare access, awareness and barriers. Additionally, 12 in-depth interviews were conducted with village leaders and healthcare providers, and 4 focus group discussions with village health committee members explored systemic issues. Quantitative data were summarised using descriptive statistics, while qualitative data were analysed thematically. The primary outcome was the proportion accessing ECS, assessed across availability, acceptability, physical accessibility and affordability. Secondary outcomes included general healthcare access and perceived challenges, analysed using both quantitative and qualitative methods.ResultsAmong 673 participants, median age was 43 years (IQR 31–55); 55% were female, 68% had primary education and 89% were self-employed, mostly subsistence farmers. While 91% sought healthcare, only 27% sought ECS; of those, 73% obtained it. Most found ECS available (79%), affordable (64%), acceptable (59%) and accessible (57%). Barriers included long travel distances (48%), long waiting times (74%), medication shortages (64%), poor customer service (56%) and inability to pay (56%). Qualitative data revealed systemic issues such as poor infrastructure, low trust in facilities and preference for traditional healers.ConclusionOur findings underscore significant barriers to access to ECS at the PHC level. These barriers are systemic, infrastructural and sociocultural. Addressing them requires coordinated, multisectoral interventions involving policy makers, healthcare providers, decision-makers at national and local levels, and communities.Keywords: Public Health, Health Services Accessibility, Emergencies, Community-Based Participatory Research

Introduction

Emergency conditions significantly contribute to global mortality and disability rates.1 2 Many emergencies go undocumented, particularly in resource-limited countries with poor documentation systems.3 Residents of low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) are at heightened risk for emergency conditions due to limited access to routine health check-ups, hazardous environments, a high incidence of infectious diseases and inadequate health systems. The WHO’s Emergency Care Systems Framework (2019) defines emergency care services (ECS) as a set of health system components, encompassing prehospital and facility-based care, that are organised to provide timely recognition, treatment and referral of all acute illnesses and injuries across the life course.4 Conditions requiring ECS include traumatic injuries, burns, falls, myocardial infarction, stroke and infections.5 These emergencies not only threaten individual lives but also lead to societal losses in productivity and increased reliance on care, adversely affecting overall population health.6 Emergency conditions contribute a substantial burden worldwide, with median disability-adjusted life years per 100 000 population of 47 728 in low-income countries, 25 186 in middle-income countries and 15 691 in high-income countries, reflecting a disproportionate burden in resource-limited settings.2 Despite the higher incidence of emergencies, access to ECS remains much lower in low-income countries, with only 8 visits per 1000 population compared with 264 visits per 1000 in high-income countries.2

Access is defined as the ability to seek, reach and utilise healthcare services, measured across five dimensions: approachability, acceptability, availability, accommodation, affordability and appropriateness.7 Recognising the importance of ECS access, the World Health Assembly passed a resolution in 2018 to ensure ECS are integrated into all health systems.8 9 The WHO developed an emergency care system framework outlining essential functions at the scene of illness, during transportation and within healthcare facilities.4 WHO emphasises that ECS are vital for population health and well-being. Consequently, member states are mandated to ensure ECS are accessible to all, regardless of cost.5

Evidence indicates that over 50% of preventable annual mortality in LMIC could be averted through the establishment and strengthening of high-quality emergency care systems.10 Key building blocks to improve access include human resources, essential medical products, information technology, governance and financing.6 Barriers to accessing ECS in LMICs encompass unavailability of services, lack of awareness, poor communication, inadequate road conditions, limited transport and mechanical failures.11 12 Cultural factors, public distrust, equipment shortages and a lack of skilled personnel further hinder access in resource-limited settings.10 13

In Tanzania, the public health system is organised in a pyramidal structure that facilitates progressive access to more specialised services.14 15 At the base of this pyramid are primary healthcare (PHC) facilities, which include dispensaries, health centres and district hospitals. These facilities primarily provide outpatient services, basic diagnostic care (like malaria test and urine pregnancy tests), maternal and child health services, and treatment for common illnesses. PHC facilities serve as the first point of contact for the majority of Tanzanians, particularly those residing in rural and underserved areas.

As patients progress upward through the system, they are referred to regional referral hospitals (secondary level) and subsequently to zonal and national hospitals (tertiary level), where more specialised and complex services, including advanced emergency and surgical care, are available. An online supplemental figure illustrating Tanzania’s healthcare system has been included for better understanding.

Emergency medicine is a relatively new specialty in Tanzania, having been formally introduced approximately 14 years ago at the national referral facility, Muhimbili National Hospital.14 Since then, the government has expanded ECS to many secondary-level public hospitals, especially in response to the systemic vulnerabilities highlighted during the COVID-19 pandemic. Despite these efforts, emergency services still remain limited in PHC facilities.

A large proportion of the Tanzanian population still relies on PHC facilities, as accessing secondary or tertiary care often involves significant logistical challenges. These include long travel distances, poor road infrastructure, limited availability of ambulances and financial constraints. For much of the population, particularly those living in remote or rural areas, PHC facilities remain the most accessible and commonly used entry point into the health system. Rural populations, where large segments of the population reside and many rely on livestock keeping or subsistence farming, are especially vulnerable due to the limited availability of timely emergency care.3 12 This vulnerability is further exacerbated by the rising burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) in the country, which demand early detection and prompt intervention for effective management.16 17 Despite the increasing demand for emergency care, there is limited empirical evidence on how ECS are delivered and accessed at the PHC level in LMIC, including Tanzania. To address this gap, the present study was conducted across four villages located in three different regions of Tanzania. It aims to assess the availability and accessibility of ECS within the PHC system, with a particular focus on identifying current capacities, systemic limitations and opportunities for strengthening emergency care delivery in rural settings, where such services are often most critically needed.

Methods

Study design

A sequential mixed-method community-based study, starting with quantitative data collection followed by qualitative data collection, was conducted in December 2021 across three selected regions in mainland Tanzania. This study assessed the access to ECS within the PHC system. The quantitative component focused on measuring the extent of access to ECS, while in-depth interviews (IDI) and focus group discussions (FGD) provided further insights into the limitations and challenges faced by community members when seeking ECS.

Study settings

The study was conducted in four villages across three regions of mainland Tanzania: Bantu (Tanga Region), Kikombo and Ihumwa (Dodoma Region) and Lukata (Mbeya Region). These sites were primarily selected to include sites that had ongoing interventions to enhance economic capabilities, enabling the community to access general healthcare services such as the Abbott Fund’s ‘Dairy Cow Project’.18 Abbott Fund Tanzania, which also supported this study, implements these initiatives to promote community self-reliance and better health outcomes. By involving the same sites, we wanted to complement the efforts not only in general healthcare access but also with a special attention to ECS. We aimed to capture a range of social, cultural and economic contexts in relation to access to ECS across three different zones of Tanzania.

The selected villages represent distinct geographic and administrative zones of Tanzania. Bantu village, located in Tanga Region in the northeast along the Indian Ocean coast, is part of Mkinga District. It lies approximately 60 km from the Tanga Regional Referral Hospital, which houses a well-established emergency department staffed with an emergency medicine specialist.19

Kikombo and Ihumwa are situated in Dodoma Region, centrally located and home to the capital of Tanzania. Dodoma Region has evolved over time, from its establishment in 1963 as part of the Central Province to its current administrative structure comprising seven districts, including the urban district where Ihumwa is located.20 21 Kikombo, another peri-urban area in Dodoma Region, is located near Ihumwa. Both villages are approximately 27 km from the Dodoma Regional Referral Hospital, which serves as the main referral centre for the region and has an operational emergency department staffed by an emergency physician.22

Lukata village is located in Mbeya Region, in the southern highlands of Tanzania. The region spans 63 420 km2 and comprises 6.4% of the total land area of the United Republic of Tanzania.23 Lukata is situated about 60 km from the Mbeya Zonal Referral Hospital, which offers tertiary-level care to the region.

In terms of healthcare infrastructure, Ihumwa and Lukata each have a public dispensary located within the village. In contrast, Bantu and Kikombo lack in-village PHC facilities. Residents of Bantu typically access care from a facility in nearby Mkinga village, while those in Kikombo rely on Chilolo Dispensary, located in a neighbouring village. This regional and infrastructural diversity provided an opportunity to examine how geographic location, administrative context and health facility availability shape community access to ECS within PHC settings in Tanzania.

Study participants and outcomes

Community members aged 18 years and older were enrolled to respond to quantitative questions assessing access to ECS in nearby healthcare facilities. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant before the interviews. Village administrators, village health leaders and the village health committee (VHC) members participated in IDIs and FGDs.

The primary outcome was the proportion of people who accessed ECS measured across four dimensions: availability, affordability, acceptability and physical accessibility. Secondary outcomes included utilisation of ECS at PHC, challenges and barriers faced, awareness of ECS and general healthcare access.

Sample size and sampling technique

Quantitative component

A multistage cluster sampling approach was employed. The selected villages represented three distinct geo-political zones of Tanzania, each with varying cultural and economic characteristics. These differences were expected to influence community readiness and access to emergency healthcare services, and therefore justified the use of cluster sampling to account for heterogeneity across sites. In calculating the sample size, we assumed a prevalence of 50% (p=0.5) for community readiness to emergency health services, as no prior estimates exist for Tanzania. Using p=0.5 yields the maximum of the minimum sample size required, thereby improving the statistical precision of the study. Following the Standardized Monitoring and Assessment of Relief and Transitions methodology,24 a design effect (DEFF) of 1.5 was applied in the absence of known cluster design effects.

The sample size calculation was based on the formula: n=DEFF × Z2 × p(1−p) / E2, where Z=1.96 for a 95% CI and E=0.05 (desired precision).

This yielded an initial sample size of 576. After accounting for a 10% non-response rate, the final adjusted sample size was 634.

To ensure proportional representation, this sample was distributed across the four villages according to population data obtained from the Village Executive Officers. Each village further subdivided the sample across its hamlets, and households were selected systematically. Within each household, two adults aged 18 years or older were interviewed. If more than two eligible adults were present, the head of household was prioritised, and the second participant was selected using simple random sampling. Ultimately, a total of 673 respondents were enrolled.

Qualitatively

IDIs were conducted with the Village Executive Officer, Village Chairperson and health facility in charge, while one FGD was held per village with members of the VHC; these are non-medical and usually community members who work closely with health facility in the village. In total, 12 IDIs were conducted (4 Village Executive Officers, 4 Village Chairpersons and 4 health facility in-charges), and 4 FGDs were conducted across the sites, each consisting of 8 to 10 participants.

Data collection procedure

Quantitative

An interviewer-administered questionnaire was developed in English and translated into Kiswahili to capture indicators related to emergency healthcare service provision from the demand side (online supplemental file 1). This tool was not limited to questions about access but included overall access-related indicators, such as potential for emergency service establishment, issues of access, barriers and challenges. The tool incorporated selected components from the WHO Hospital Emergency Unit Assessment Tool,25 to assess community awareness of basic emergency care standards, with adaptations made to ensure contextual relevance to the Tanzanian PHC setting. In addition, the questionnaire was pretested with 50 community members in Dar es Salaam to refine clarity, sequencing and language. The final version was deployed electronically using Research Electronic Data Captur :(REDCap) (V.9.1.21, Vanderbilt University, Nashville, Tennessee, USA), and data collection was conducted via tablets by three trained data collectors and one investigator per village, all of whom received detailed training on study objectives and tool administration.

Qualitative

Semistructured guides for IDIs and FGDs (online supplemental file 2) were developed in English and translated into Kiswahili. The guides aimed to explore perceptions of and access to ECS within the PHC system, focusing on barriers, limitations and local service realities. IDIs were conducted with key local administrative leaders, including the Village Executive Officer, Village Chairperson and the health facility in-charge. FGDs were conducted with members of the VHC, including 10-cell leaders (the lowest tier of community representation). The inclusion of 10-cell leaders in FGDs provided grassroots perspectives and community-level insights, complementing the more administrative viewpoints obtained through the IDIs. Each IDI lasted 30–45 min and FGDs ranged from 60 to 90 min. Interviews were led by a senior qualitative researcher, with assistance from a junior data collector responsible for informed consent and demographic data collection. Each participant in the IDIs and FGDs received 10 000 Tanzanian shillings (≈US$4.06) as compensation for their time. All discussions were audio-recorded with prior consent. Data collection continued until thematic saturation was reached, that is, when no new information emerged during subsequent interviews or discussions.

Data analysis

Quantitative

The data extracted from REDCap and stored in excel spreadsheet will be analysed with the aid of Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS V.22.0, IBM, Ltd, Carolina, USA). Descriptive findings are summarised using frequencies and percentages, with some results presented using bar charts for clarity.

Qualitative

Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim. Coding was conducted using Microsoft Excel, with initial coding conducted in Kiswahili to ensure the accuracy of the original meaning. These codes and corresponding quotes were later translated into English. To familiarise themselves with the data, all authors thoroughly reviewed the transcripts and field notes multiple times before starting the analysis. The analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s six-step thematic approach.26 First, familiarised with the data, the team conducted line-by-line coding of the transcripts and field notes. Second, they grouped the codes into descriptive and analytical themes. Finally, overarching themes were identified. The coding process involved breaking down the data into manageable parts to uncover deeper insights and meanings. The initial codes were organised into descriptive categories, which were further refined into abstract analytical themes. The findings were presented as themes and subthemes, supported by key quotes. Although the process is described sequentially, the actual analysis was iterative, with themes being revisited and refined throughout the process.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of the study participants

A total of 673 participants were enrolled in the study. The median age was 43 years (IQR 31–55), and the majority were female (55.1%, n=371). Most participants (68.4%) had attained primary education, with none from Ihumwa or Kikombo reporting college-level education. A significant proportion (91.9%) were employed, primarily as subsistence farmers (72.5%). Access to healthcare services at primary healthcare facilities

Most participants (91.2%) reported that they typically seek care from a healthcare facility when unwell. Cultural beliefs were not widely seen as a barrier to healthcare access, with 93.0% of respondents disagreeing that traditional or cultural practices interfered with their care-seeking behaviour. However, awareness of emergency medical conditions was relatively low; only 47.9% of participants were able to correctly identify one or more health conditions that require urgent or emergency care.

Despite this limited awareness, nearly all participants (96.3%) viewed the availability of emergency services at nearby health facilities as positive. Nonetheless, concerns about healthcare system capacity were evident: 49.5% disagreed that their local facility had sufficient healthcare workers, 49.8% felt the infrastructure was inadequate and 52.3% believed diagnostic and treatment equipment was lacking (table 2).

Utilisation of emergency care services (ECS) and challenges and barriers to ECS

Only 27% (n=182) of participants reported seeking ECS. Among them, 132 (72.5%) successfully accessed services, with the highest rate observed in Lukata village (91.3%) (figure 1).

Proportion of participants who sought and received emergency care services.

Conversely, 50 participants reported being unable to access ECS when needed. The most common barrier was unavailability of services (39.4%). Other barriers included limited laboratory services, long distances to health facilities and unaffordability. Notably, approximately 85% of respondents in Lukata who failed to access ECS attributed it to financial constraints (figure 2).

Reported reasons for failure to access emergency health services among participants (overall N=50).

Among the 132 participants who accessed ECS, the majority agreed that services were available (78.8%), affordable (63.6%), acceptable (58.5%) and accessible (56.8%). However, nearly half (47.7%) reported needing to travel long distances to reach services (table 2).

Participants also reported multiple challenges while seeking ECS. In total, 162 challenge-related responses were recorded (participants could select more than one). The most frequently reported issue was long waiting times to see a provider (23%), especially in Bantu village. Other barriers included lack of medicines (20%), poor customer service (17%) and inability to pay for services (17%). These issues were more commonly reported by residents of Bantu village (table 2).

Existing trust in traditional healers

A deep-rooted trust in traditional healers, coupled with scepticism toward modern medicine, was evident. Several respondents emphasised that community members often turn to traditional medicine, especially when facility-based care fails or is perceived as ineffective.

There are some people who, after failing in the hospital, go to traditional medicine, and that cannot be disputed because many people do that. (IDI, participant 1, Bantu)

And

That is a problem in this community, it’s a very big problem, meaning a large percentage of people go to these traditional medicine practices, and there are times when someone asks to take a patient out of the hospital to where? To traditional medicine. (IDI, participant 3, Bantu)

Inadequate facility resources

Respondents highlighted frequent medication stock-outs, shortages of diagnostic equipment and laboratory tests, and insufficient emergency treatment options.

The lack of medicine is one of the challenges. The reason why they don’t meet the demand is because they receive so many people. This means that the medicines don’t arrive on time, and when they do arrive, they don’t last. (IDI, participant 2, Bantu)

And

The problem in this neighbourhood is that some tests are missing at the clinic. Therefore, my advice is for the government to ensure that all tests are available at the facility (mentions the name), and also to increase the number of specialists. As I mentioned, one “specialist” may be overwhelmed. (FGD, participant 6, Kikombo)

Human resource constraints

Health facilities were often understaffed and lacked specialised providers. Some staff reported being forced to perform duties beyond their training due to staff shortages. Patient care was also criticised as sometimes inattentive or dismissive.

According to the staffing norm, we are supposed to have 15 staff members in this facility, but as of now, we are only seven. This shortage has forced us to share tasks, including responsibilities that we are not trained for, among ourselves. (IDI, participant 3, Ihumwa)

And

For example, I went with my housemaid; she was very sick and had fainted. When I got there, the doctors were just passing by, ignoring the patient until some gentlemen helped me to carry the girl to the doctor. You see, the services there are good, but the patient care is not very good. (IDI, participant 2, Bantu)

Discussion

This study aimed to assess access to ECS within PHC settings in Tanzania, highlighting both the strengths and limitations of service delivery in rural contexts. While most community members rely on PHCs for general health needs, emergency care utilisation remains low. The findings highlight how systemic, geographic, financial and sociocultural factors intersect to shape care-seeking behaviour and overall access to ECS. Dissatisfaction with emergency services was common, driven by long distances, waiting times, medicine shortages and financial barriers. Although participants recognised the importance of emergency care, limited awareness, low trust in formal health facilities and preference for traditional healers contributed to delayed care-seeking. Our findings are consistent with findings across other settings, which report that limited knowledge and low public awareness contribute to low uptake of ECS, particularly in rural settings.27,30 Similar previous studies in Tanzania have shown that poor understanding of what constitutes emergency and misconceptions about emergency care in rural areas contributed to the underutilisation of ECS.28 31

Geographic barriers were evident in villages lacking nearby healthcare facilities, emphasising the role of distance to care in shaping access. Such barriers are associated with poorer outcomes in other contexts, including delayed cancer diagnoses and increased mortality from acute conditions like asthma.32 33 The disparity of healthcare utilisation between urban and rural areas suggests disparities in health promotion campaigns between urban and rural areas.34 35 This calls for community-based health promotion campaigns in rural areas targeting raising awareness of emergencies necessitating ECS. These findings highlight the disconnect to the national policy efforts such as the Tanzania Community Health Strategy 2019–2023 and the National eHealth Strategy, both of which emphasising community level.36

Nearly half of participants in this study perceived their local PHC facilities as lacking sufficient staff, infrastructure and capacity to deliver emergency care. These perceptions echo prior findings from rural Tanzania and many LMICs, where systemic shortages of trained personnel, diagnostics and essential medicines undermine PHC performance.2837,39 Although Tanzania has ongoing initiatives aimed at improving healthcare access, such as the Primary Healthcare Services Development Programme and the Health Sector Strategic Plan V (2021–2026), our findings suggest a persistent misalignment between yet again these national policy goals and the lived experiences of rural communities.36 39 Concerted efforts are needed among stakeholders to ensure synergistic effects from multiple parallel policy efforts in place.

Among those who received services, most described them as available, affordable and culturally acceptable. However, significant barriers remained including long travel distances, extended waiting times and inability to pay. These barriers were compounded by the dominance of out-of-pocket payments in Tanzania’s healthcare system, with families reporting an average annual expenditure of approximately US$100. The absence of widespread health insurance coverage contributes to delayed or forgone care. Such financial barriers are well documented across sub-Saharan Africa and disproportionately affect rural and low-income populations compared with high-income settings.3040,42 Consistent with our findings, the authors concluded that expanding health insurance coverage and developing emergency medical systems could significantly enhance timely access to ECS in similar settings.43

When formal healthcare is financially or logistically inaccessible, rural residents often turn to traditional healers, who are perceived as more affordable and accessible.44 45 This trend was echoed in our findings, where some participants expressed strong cultural trust in traditional healers. These patterns of underutilisation align with broader evidence from LMIC, where healthcare access is shaped by a combination of supply-side factors (eg, facility availability, geographic accessibility and quality of care) and demand-side factors (eg, socioeconomic status, cultural beliefs and financial constraints).6 27 32 39 46 47

The relatively low utilisation of ECS observed in our study (only 27%) likely reflects these intersecting structural and systemic barriers mentioned above. Inadequate facility capacity, the limited availability of emergency services at the PHC level and the absence of widespread health insurance coverage all contribute to suboptimal care. Additionally, it is plausible that some emergencies are either managed at home or not recognised as requiring formal medical intervention. This is supported by findings from a study by Shayo et al in Northern Tanzania, which identified substantial gaps in community understanding of emergency conditions. Consistent with our findings, the authors concluded that the developing emergency medical systems could significantly enhance timely access to ECS in similar settings.43

Contrary to reports from some participants in our study that affordability is acceptable, national household surveys indicate the existence of catastrophic health expenditures in many households in rural Tanzania.48 Perhaps the discrepancy noted here may be due to sampling bias (from selection of those that never needed care), social desirability bias or variations in the perceived value of health and healthcare costs. Although Tanzania has implemented key policy instruments such as the Health Financing Strategy (2016–2026), the Community Health Fund and, more recently, the Universal Health Insurance Act (2024), our findings revealed persistently low enrolment in health insurance schemes. This has resulted in continued reliance on out-of-pocket payments. Given that over 75% of Tanzanians are employed in the informal sector, strategies aimed at increasing health insurance enrolment in these populations are urgently needed.36 49 It is also important to scrutinise these schemes further by acknowledging that, while they are designed to offer financial protection, they do not automatically guarantee improved health outcomes. As Das and Do argue, behavioural responses by healthcare providers can undermine the intended benefits of such schemes.50 Their evaluation across LMIC settings found that, although insurance increased utilisation, health outcomes did not necessarily improve. This highlights the need for a multisectoral and multilayered approach to ensure accessible and affordable emergency healthcare in Tanzania and similar contexts.

A notable contrast emerged between community members’ self-reported perceptions and administrators’ views regarding the role of cultural beliefs in healthcare-seeking behaviour. Over 90% of community respondents stated that cultural beliefs did not influence their decisions to seek care. In contrast, several local administrators emphasised that a strong public reliance on traditional healers continues to hinder timely access to formal healthcare services. This discrepancy suggests a potential disconnect between provider assumptions and actual community perspectives. It is possible that community members under-reported the influence of cultural beliefs due to social desirability bias during structured interviews. Conversely, administrators may overestimate these cultural barriers based on anecdotal observations or outdated assumptions. This inconsistency underscores the need for deeper qualitative exploration to better understand how both community members and health providers perceive and navigate healthcare options. A recent study on childhood cancer in Tanzania found similar patterns whereby many community members attributed illness to witchcraft, resulting in delayed presentation to formal health services.51 This highlights the ongoing relevance of cultural narratives in shaping health-seeking behaviour and the importance of aligning provider perceptions with evolving community norms. Health-seeking behaviour in many sub-Saharan African contexts navigates both systems in pragmatic and context-sensitive ways. Studies have shown that people frequently alternate between traditional and biomedical services depending on factors such as symptom severity, chronicity, perceived cause and treatment efficacy.52,54 For instance, Kamat (2001) illustrated how patients in Mumbai make strategic rather than purely rational choices, seeking care from informal, traditional or formal providers based on affordability, urgency and accessibility, rather than strict adherence to medical ideology.55 56 Similarly, in southwestern Uganda, researchers proposed a conceptual model of therapeutic itineraries shaped by interactions at multiple levels (including provider trust, access to health systems and peer testimony), all influencing whether individuals choose traditional care, biomedical care or a combination of both.54 In Northern Tanzania, community members were likewise observed to fluidly switch between systems depending on context, needs and access.52 In Mozambique, even where biomedical care is available, traditional healers remain a primary point of contact for illnesses believed to have spiritual or social origins.57 This phenomenon, often referred to as medical pluralism, reflects a flexible, layered model of care-seeking behaviour that should be recognised and accommodated in both research and policy design.58,60

Limitations and generalisability

This study employed a descriptive, cross-sectional design, which limits causal inference. Nevertheless, the relatively large sample size, inclusion of geographically and culturally diverse rural sites, and the use of a mixed-methods approach enhance the robustness and generalisability of the findings in Tanzania. The qualitative data added important contextual depth, helping to interpret and validate quantitative results and offering a more comprehensive understanding of both supply-side and demand-side barriers to ECS access.

We acknowledge that data collection occurred during daytime hours, typically from early morning to late afternoon. In the early hours, more female participants were available, likely reflecting traditional gender roles and household responsibilities. As the day progressed, more male participants returned and were subsequently interviewed. Despite efforts to ensure broad representation, this timing may have introduced some selection bias. Individuals who were unavailable during these hours, primarily men engaged in fishing, farming or small-scale business activities, may be under-represented. Additionally, those interviewed later in the day may have experienced fatigue, potentially influencing the quality of their responses. Nonetheless, 44.9% of respondents were male, contributing meaningful insights that supported a balanced analysis and strengthened the validity of the study’s conclusions.

Impact

This study provides timely insights into the availability, accessibility and utilisation of ECS within Tanzania’s PHC system. By examining four rural villages across diverse regions, it captures the complex interplay of geographic, financial, cultural and systemic barriers shaping community access to emergency care. Importantly, it challenges assumptions about the influence of cultural beliefs and exposes gaps in community awareness and system readiness for emergency conditions.

Emergency care spans infectious diseases and NCDs; yet, in many LMICs including Tanzania, ECS remains underprioritised at the primary care and rural levels. Our findings contribute to addressing this evidence gap, emphasising the urgent need for health system strengthening, improved health literacy and expanded insurance and referral mechanisms. These insights can inform national strategies and stimulate global discourse on achieving equitable, timely access to life-saving emergency services.

Conclusion

Access to ECS in PHC settings is suboptimal, and addressing the identified barriers is essential to improving service delivery. To achieve Universal Health Coverage by 2030, it is essential to address key barriers to healthcare access. Strategies should focus on promoting health insurance schemes, subsidising healthcare services in rural areas, offering incentives to healthcare workers in PHC settings and enhancing provider training. Strengthening ECS in these settings will significantly reduce the morbidity and mortality burden, improving overall health outcomes in LMICs. Synergistic efforts on improving access to ECS in rural settings that involve developing contextualised interventions with a sector-wide approach including health, education, infrastructure, financing and communication are needed. Large-scale research is needed to unveil and test contextualised modes for improving access to emergency healthcare services in under-resourced rural settings.
