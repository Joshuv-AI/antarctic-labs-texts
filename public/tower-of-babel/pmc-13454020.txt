Clinicopathological features and impact on surgical axillary management of encapsulated papillary carcinoma of the breast
Authors: Yasmine Hany Abdel Moamen Elzohery, Ahmed Orabi, Galal Ghaly, Neveen Gamal Fathy Ali, Eman Ali Khalaf, Hazem Abdelazim, Sherif Nasser Taha, Mohamed Fathy Abdelfattah Abdelrahman Elithy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEncapsulated papillary carcinoma (EPC) is a rare breast malignancy accounting for approximately 0.5–1% of all breast cancers. Although generally associated with favorable outcomes, the presence of invasive carcinoma may influence axillary nodal involvement, treatment decisions, and prognosis.ObjectiveTo evaluate the clinicopathological and imaging characteristics of EPC, assess the frequency of associated invasive disease and axillary lymph node involvement, and determine their impact on surgical axillary management and oncological outcomes.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included all consecutive patients diagnosed with EPC and treated surgically at Baheya Center for Early Detection and Treatment of Breast Cancer, Egypt, between 2017 and 2024. Clinical, radiological, pathological, treatment, and follow-up data were reviewed. Patients were classified as pure EPC, EPC associated with ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), or invasive EPC. Survival outcomes were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method.ResultsA total of 113 patients with EPC were included. Histopathological evaluation demonstrated pure EPC in 22 patients (19%), EPC associated with DCIS in 17 (15%), and invasive EPC in 74 (65%). Most patients were postmenopausal, and the majority of tumors exhibited favorable biological characteristics, including high hormone receptor positivity and low HER2 expression. Axillary metastases were identified in 15 patients (13.2%) and occurred exclusively in tumors associated with invasion. Sentinel lymph node biopsy was performed in 82 patients (72.5%), axillary dissection in 11 (9.7%), and no axillary surgery in 20 (17.6%). Invasive EPC was associated with larger tumor size, higher pathological stage, and increased nodal involvement compared with non-invasive EPC. During follow-up, loco-regional recurrence occurred in four patients and distant metastases in three patients, all within the invasive EPC subgroup. Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated excellent overall and disease-free survival.ConclusionsEPC is an uncommon breast malignancy with an overall favorable prognosis, but it remains a diagnostically and therapeutically challenging entity because of its heterogeneous pathological spectrum and the variable presence of associated invasive carcinoma. It needs individualized axillary management, with routine axillary staging potentially unnecessary in carefully selected patients with pure EPC, while invasive EPC warrants more comprehensive surgical assessment.

Introduction

Encapsulated papillary carcinoma (EPC) is a rare subtype of breast cancer, accounting for approximately 0.5–1% of all breast malignancies. It belongs to a heterogeneous group of papillary breast lesions characterized by fibrovascular cores lined by neoplastic epithelial cells. The presence of an intact myoepithelial cell layer within the fibrovascular cores distinguishes benign papillary lesions, such as intraductal papilloma, from malignant papillary neoplasms, which typically lack a continuous myoepithelial layer [1].

Papillary breast lesions encompass a spectrum ranging from benign papillomas to atypical proliferative and malignant lesions. Atypical epithelial proliferations arising within an intraductal papilloma may manifest as atypical ductal hyperplasia (< 3 mm) or ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) (≥ 3 mm). EPC is characterized by a well-circumscribed papillary neoplasm arising within a cystically dilated duct, composed of delicate fibrovascular cores and surrounded by a thick fibrous capsule. Histologically, EPC lacks myoepithelial cells both within the papillary structures and, in most cases, at the lesion periphery. Invasive papillary carcinoma is diagnosed when malignant cells infiltrate beyond the fibrous capsule into the surrounding breast stroma while maintaining papillary architectural features [2].

According to the 2019 World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Breast Tumours, EPC without conventional stromal invasion should be staged as carcinoma in situ because of its generally indolent biological behavior. Several studies have demonstrated that EPC is associated with excellent clinical outcomes, including low rates of axillary lymph node metastasis, local recurrence, and distant dissemination [3, 4].

Clinically, EPC predominantly affects postmenopausal women, with the highest incidence occurring during the seventh decade of life, although younger patients may occasionally be affected. Patients commonly present with a palpable breast mass or bloody nipple discharge, while a subset of lesions are asymptomatic and detected incidentally through screening mammography [5].

Radiological evaluation plays a pivotal role in the diagnosis and preoperative assessment of EPC. On mammography, EPC typically appears as an oval or round, high-density or isodense mass with circumscribed margins. Focal margin indistinctness may be observed in lesions associated with invasive carcinoma, whereas spiculated margins are uncommon unless a desmoplastic stromal reaction accompanies frank invasion. Unlike other breast malignancies, EPC is infrequently associated with suspicious microcalcifications [6].

Ultrasonography commonly demonstrates EPC as a complex cystic-solid lesion or, less frequently, a predominantly solid hypoechoic mass. Characteristic findings include mural nodules projecting into a cystically dilated space, internal septations, posterior acoustic enhancement, and prominent vascularity within the solid component on Doppler interrogation. When associated with invasive carcinoma, lesions may exhibit irregular contours and indistinct margins. Intralesional hemorrhage may occasionally occur, resulting in fluid-debris levels detectable on ultrasound or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [7].

Advanced contrast-enhanced imaging modalities, including MRI and contrast-enhanced spectral mammography (CESM), provide additional information regarding lesion morphology and disease extent. EPC typically appears as a complex cystic lesion with enhancing mural nodules or solid components. Areas of intrinsic T1 hyperintensity may be observed on MRI due to intralesional hemorrhage. Following contrast administration, the cyst wall, septations, and mural nodules usually demonstrate avid enhancement, while the solid components may exhibit either homogeneous or heterogeneous enhancement patterns, occasionally accompanied by washout kinetics [8].

Aim of the study.

The present study aimed to evaluate the clinicopathological characteristics of encapsulated papillary carcinoma, determine the frequency of associated invasive disease and axillary lymph node involvement, and assess the impact of these findings on surgical axillary management.

Patients and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at Baheya Center for Early Detection and Treatment of Breast Cancer, Egypt. All consecutive patients diagnosed with encapsulated papillary carcinoma who underwent surgical treatment between 2017 and 2024 were identified from the institutional database and included in the study. Clinical, radiological, pathological, treatment, and follow-up data were retrospectively retrieved from electronic medical records and pathology reports. During the study period, 113 patients were diagnosed with EPC and fulfilled the study eligibility criteria.

The study protocol was approved by the Baheya Research Ethics Committee (IRB protocol No. 202210240050). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Eligibility criteria

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they had a histopathologically confirmed diagnosis of EPC, underwent definitive surgical treatment at our institution, and had complete clinicopathological data available for analysis. Patients with incomplete pathological records, those who received treatment outside the institution, or those with missing essential treatment or follow-up data were excluded from the study.

Clinical and radiological evaluation

All patients underwent comprehensive preoperative clinical assessment, including detailed history taking and physical examination. Radiological evaluation consisted of bilateral mammography and breast ultrasonography. Additional imaging using contrast-enhanced spectral mammography or magnetic resonance imaging was performed when further characterization of the lesion or assessment of disease extent was required.

Preoperative tissue diagnosis was established using ultrasound-guided core needle biopsy. Axillary nodal status was routinely assessed by ultrasonography. Fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) was performed in patients with indeterminate or suspicious axillary lymph nodes to aid preoperative staging and surgical planning.

Surgical management

All patients underwent definitive surgical treatment according to institutional multidisciplinary recommendations. Breast surgery consisted of either breast-conserving surgery or mastectomy, depending on tumor extent, breast size, multifocality, and patient preference.

Axillary management was individualized according to preoperative imaging findings and the suspected presence of invasive disease. Sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) was generally performed in patients undergoing mastectomy, patients with imaging or pathological suspicion of invasion, or when axillary staging was considered clinically indicated. Axillary lymph node dissection (ALND) was reserved for patients with confirmed nodal metastases or when recommended by the multidisciplinary team.

Histopathological assessment

All surgical specimens were examined by dedicated breast pathologists. Routine histopathological evaluation was performed using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained sections. The diagnosis of EPC was established based on characteristic morphological features, including a papillary neoplasm surrounded by a thick fibrous capsule, papillary fronds containing delicate fibrovascular cores, epithelial cells demonstrating low- to intermediate-grade cytological atypia, and absence of a continuous myoepithelial cell layer along the papillary structures.

Data collected from pathological reports included tumor size, histological subtype, tumor grade, lymphovascular invasion, associated ductal carcinoma in situ, nodal status, estrogen receptor (ER) status, progesterone receptor (PR) status, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) status, and Ki-67 proliferative index.

Histopathological classification was performed according to the 2019 World Health Organization Classification of Breast Tumours. Tumors were categorized as pure EPC, EPC associated with ductal carcinoma in situ, EPC with microinvasion, or EPC associated with invasive carcinoma based on the presence and extent of invasion beyond the fibrous capsule and the associated epithelial component identified in the surgical specimen.

Follow-up and outcome assessment

Patients were followed according to institutional surveillance protocols, including regular clinical and radiological examinations. Follow-up data were collected from outpatient records and imaging reports. Outcome measures included locoregional recurrence, distant metastasis, disease-free survival (DFS), overall survival (OS), and cause-specific mortality.

Overall survival was defined as the interval between the date of diagnosis and death from any cause or the date of last follow-up. Disease-free survival was defined as the interval between definitive surgical treatment and the first documented locoregional recurrence, distant metastasis, or the date of last follow-up for patients without evidence of recurrence.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, whereas continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (range), as appropriate. Comparisons between categorical variables were performed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when expected cell counts were less than five. Continuous variables were compared using the independent-samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U test according to data distribution. Survival outcomes were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05.

Results

A total of 113 patients with histopathologically confirmed encapsulated papillary carcinoma of the breast were included in the present study. The clinicopathological, radiological, surgical, and oncological outcomes of the study cohort were analyzed.

As regards the demographic characteristics of the study population, EPC predominantly affected older women, with a mean age of 63.09 ± 10.82 years. The majority of patients were postmenopausal (89.4%), while only 10.6% were premenopausal. A positive family history of breast cancer was documented in 15% of patients, reflecting the generally sporadic nature of this uncommon breast malignancy (Table 1).

According to axillary ultrasonographic assessment, the majority of patients demonstrated non-specific lymph nodes, while pathological lymphadenopathy was detected in only a small subset of cases. Indeterminate axillary lymph nodes were identified in 10.6% of patients and subsequently underwent fine-needle aspiration cytology for further evaluation. Pure EPC accounted for 19% of cases, whereas 15% were associated with ductal carcinoma in situ. Microinvasive carcinoma was identified in 11.5% of patients, while stromal invasion was present in 44%. Additional invasive histological subtypes included invasive cribriform carcinoma, mucinous carcinoma, and invasive micropapillary carcinoma. Pathological data of the studied patients

In terms of pathological tumor characteristics, the mean pathological tumor size was 3.40 ± 2.36 cm, while the mean invasive component size measured 1.49 ± 1.15 cm. Most lesions were unifocal and exhibited negative surgical margins. Low- and intermediate-grade tumors predominated, whereas high-grade lesions were uncommon. Lymphovascular invasion was identified in a minority of patients. Furthermore, associated DCIS was frequently encountered, emphasizing the complex pathological spectrum of EPC (Table 3).

According to immunohistochemical analysis, EPC demonstrated a favorable biological profile. Hormone receptor positivity was observed in the vast majority of patients, with only eight ER-negative tumors identified, all of which were associated with invasive carcinoma. HER2 overexpression was rare and was exclusively encountered in tumors with an invasive component. These findings support the generally luminal-like biological behavior of EPC (Table 3).

As regards axillary staging, combined axillary ultrasonography and FNAC demonstrated high diagnostic performance for the detection of nodal metastases. Comparison with final pathological findings after axillary surgery revealed a sensitivity of 66.7%, specificity of 98.7%, positive predictive value of 90.9%, negative predictive value of 93.9%, and overall diagnostic accuracy of 93.6%. These findings highlight the value of preoperative radiological axillary assessment in selecting patients for surgical nodal staging (Table 4).

According to the pathological classification adopted in the present study, patients were categorized into pure EPC, EPC associated with DCIS, and invasive EPC, including microinvasive disease. Comparative analysis demonstrated significant differences in tumor stage and nodal status among these subgroups. Pure EPC and EPC associated with DCIS were uniformly classified as in situ disease, whereas invasive EPC encompassed a broad spectrum ranging from microinvasion to T3 tumors. Notably, axillary nodal metastases were observed exclusively among patients with invasive EPC, indicating that stromal invasion is the principal determinant of nodal spread in this disease entity. Furthermore, invasive EPC was associated with larger tumor size and more advanced pathological stage compared with non-invasive EPC (Table 7).

In terms of diagnostic-pathological concordance, upstaging from a non-invasive intraductal papillary lesion diagnosed on core needle biopsy to malignant EPC on final surgical pathology occurred in 24 patients (21%). Among these cases, 14 patients were found to harbor invasive carcinoma, while the remaining patients demonstrated microinvasive disease. Consequently, 16 patients underwent delayed sentinel lymph node biopsy during a second surgical procedure. Figure 6 illustrates the pattern of pathological upstaging and its subsequent impact on surgical management.

As regards reoperation rates, 26 patients required additional surgical intervention. Wider excision was performed in three patients because of positive margins associated with invasive disease. Completion mastectomy was required in five patients due to extensive residual DCIS, postoperative MRI findings suggestive of residual disease, or recurrence during follow-up. In addition, two patients underwent delayed axillary clearance after developing locoregional recurrence.

According to survival analysis, the mean follow-up duration was 30 ± 19 months. Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated favorable overall survival outcomes throughout the study period, with an estimated mean overall survival of 64.3 months (95% CI: 58.0–70.6 months). The corresponding Kaplan–Meier overall survival curve is presented in Fig. 7.

In terms of disease-free survival, Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated excellent outcomes, with an estimated mean disease-free survival of 65.5 months (95% CI: 58.9–72.1 months). Recurrence events were uncommon and occurred exclusively among patients with invasive EPC. The corresponding Kaplan–Meier disease-free survival curve is presented in Fig. 8.

As regards oncological outcomes, loco-regional recurrence occurred in four patients, all of whom had invasive EPC. Recurrences developed at 6, 9, and 10 months after surgery, while one patient was lost to follow-up and subsequently re-presented with recurrence two years later. Distant metastases were identified in three patients with invasive EPC, involving the bone, lung, and liver. Importantly, no breast cancer-related deaths were observed during follow-up, while eight deaths were attributed to causes unrelated to breast cancer. These findings further support the excellent prognosis of pure EPC and EPC associated with DCIS, while highlighting the relatively higher risk associated with invasive EPC (Table 8).

Discussion

Encapsulated papillary carcinoma is a rare breast malignancy characterized by distinctive pathological features and generally favorable clinical outcomes. The present study evaluated the clinicopathological characteristics, imaging findings, axillary nodal involvement, and surgical management of 113 patients with EPC treated at a specialized breast cancer center.

Consistent with previous reports, EPC predominantly affected older women, with most patients in our cohort being postmenopausal at the time of diagnosis [9]. This demographic distribution supports the well-recognized association between EPC and advanced age compared with other forms of breast cancer.

Regarding tumor location, nearly half of the lesions were located in the upper outer quadrant, whereas approximately one-fifth were centrally located. Although some studies have described EPC as frequently arising in the retroareolar region because of its origin within large central ducts, our findings demonstrate that EPC may occur throughout the breast parenchyma and should not be considered exclusively a centrally located lesion [10].

The radiological appearance of EPC has been reported as variable but often demonstrates characteristic features that may facilitate diagnosis. Previous studies described EPC as a well-circumscribed, oval or lobulated mass, typically lacking spiculated margins [11]. Similar findings were observed in the present study, where most lesions appeared as circumscribed masses with cystic and solid components. Furthermore, lesions presenting as complex cystic masses with mural nodules on ultrasonography were frequently confirmed as EPC on final pathological examination.

Importantly, our imaging-pathological correlation demonstrated that lesions exhibiting non-mass enhancement, extensive contrast uptake, indistinct margins, or spiculated contours were more likely to harbor associated DCIS or invasive carcinoma. These findings highlight the value of multimodality imaging in the preoperative assessment of EPC and suggest that imaging characteristics may provide useful clues regarding the likelihood of associated invasive disease.

Ultrasonographically, EPC has classically been described as a cystic or complex cystic-solid lesion containing mural papillary projections arising from the cyst wall [7]. This characteristic appearance was observed in 64.6% of our patients. Although the present study was not designed to compare the diagnostic performance of individual imaging modalities, ultrasonography was particularly useful for identifying complex cystic lesions and mural nodules, whereas CESM and MRI provided valuable information regarding lesion extent, associated ductal involvement, and areas of non-mass enhancement. Consequently, a multimodality imaging approach appears beneficial for surgical planning and disease characterization.

The optimal management of EPC remains controversial, particularly with regard to axillary staging. Current evidence suggests that pure EPC behaves similarly to ductal carcinoma in situ and can often be managed adequately with complete surgical excision and negative margins. However, the necessity of routine axillary surgery remains debated because of the low reported incidence of nodal metastasis. Previous studies have reported lymph node involvement rates of approximately 5.5% [12, 13].

In the present study, axillary metastases were identified in 13.2% of patients. Notably, nodal involvement was confined to tumors associated with stromal invasion, whereas no nodal metastases were observed in patients with pure EPC or EPC associated with DCIS. These findings support a selective rather than routine approach to axillary staging. Sentinel lymph node biopsy may be omitted in carefully selected patients with pure EPC, whereas axillary evaluation appears justified when invasive disease is suspected preoperatively or confirmed on final pathology.

Breast-conserving surgery was the most commonly performed procedure in our cohort, accounting for 58.4% of cases. Mastectomy was reserved for selected patients according to disease extent, breast size, multifocality, or patient preference. These findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating breast-conserving surgery rates ranging from 29% to 93% among patients with EPC [14].

Similarly, the frequency of sentinel lymph node biopsy in our study (72.5%) falls within the wide range reported in the literature, which varies between 30% and 100% [15]. This variability likely reflects differences in institutional practice patterns and the ongoing uncertainty regarding the optimal axillary management of EPC.

From a biological perspective, EPC is generally characterized by a favorable immunophenotype, including strong hormone receptor expression and rare HER2 overexpression. Previous studies have reported extremely low HER2 positivity rates, with some series documenting no HER2-positive tumors [16]. Our findings support this observation, as 92.9% of tumors were hormone receptor-positive and only three patients demonstrated HER2 positivity. Notably, all ER-negative tumors were associated with invasive carcinoma, further emphasizing the biological distinction between pure and invasive EPC.

The presence of stromal invasion represents one of the most clinically relevant factors influencing prognosis and treatment decisions in EPC. A study from Pakistan reported invasive disease in 44% of EPC cases [17]. Similarly, more than half of the patients in our cohort demonstrated an invasive component. Histopathological classification revealed substantial heterogeneity, including pure EPC, EPC associated with DCIS, microinvasive EPC, and EPC associated with invasive carcinoma.

These findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating that EPC is frequently associated with adjacent conventional DCIS, invasive breast carcinoma of no special type (IBC-NST), or both [13]. Importantly, our comparative analysis demonstrated that invasive EPC was associated with larger tumor size, higher pathological stage, and increased axillary nodal involvement compared with non-invasive EPC. These observations highlight the biological heterogeneity of EPC and underscore the importance of accurately identifying associated invasive disease when planning surgical and adjuvant treatment.

The challenge of preoperative diagnosis is further illustrated by the substantial rate of pathological upstaging observed in the present study. An international multicenter review reported an upgrade rate of 14.4% following excision of papillary lesions diagnosed on core needle biopsy, with invasive carcinoma identified in 3.7% of cases [18]. Another study reported upgrade rates as high as 28% in papillary lesions with atypia [19]. In our cohort, pathological upstaging occurred in 21% of patients, with invasive carcinoma identified in 12% and microinvasive disease in the remainder. These findings emphasize the limitations of core needle biopsy in papillary lesions and support careful pathological assessment of excision specimens.

The prognosis of EPC is generally excellent. Previous studies have reported 10-year survival rates approaching 100% [20]. In the largest population-based analysis of EPC, Grabowski et al. demonstrated favorable long-term outcomes, with similar cumulative survival rates among patients with in situ and invasive EPC [21]. Likewise, Solorzano et al. reported a disease-specific survival rate of 100% and a recurrence rate of only 12.5% [14].

Our findings are consistent with these observations. Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated excellent overall and disease-free survival, with recurrence events occurring exclusively in patients with invasive EPC. Although loco-regional recurrence and distant metastases were observed, these events were uncommon and limited to tumors harboring an invasive component. Furthermore, no breast cancer-related deaths were recorded during follow-up.

Previous reports have suggested that invasive papillary carcinomas exhibit less aggressive biological behavior and a more favorable prognosis than conventional invasive breast carcinoma of no special type (IBC-NST) [22]. Similarly, the present study demonstrated excellent oncological outcomes across all EPC subgroups under contemporary multidisciplinary management. Nevertheless, invasive EPC remained associated with a higher risk of recurrence and nodal metastasis than non-invasive EPC.

From a clinical perspective, our findings suggest that EPC should not be regarded as a uniform disease entity. Rather, management decisions should be guided by the presence or absence of associated invasion. While pure EPC appears to behave similarly to DCIS and may be safely managed with limited surgery and selective omission of axillary staging, invasive EPC warrants a more comprehensive surgical and oncological approach because of its increased metastatic potential.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective single-center design may introduce selection and information bias. Second, despite representing one of the larger EPC series reported from the region, the relatively small number of recurrence events and nodal metastases limited the ability to perform robust multivariable analyses. Consequently, independent predictors of invasion and axillary nodal involvement could not be reliably identified without a substantial risk of model overfitting.

In addition, interpretation of survival outcomes should be approached with caution because of the limited number of events and the relatively short follow-up period for a disease characterized by excellent long-term prognosis. Subgroup survival comparisons were therefore statistically underpowered.

Finally, because the institutional database included only patients diagnosed with EPC, the prevalence of EPC among all breast cancer cases treated during the study period could not be accurately determined.

Conclusion

Encapsulated papillary carcinoma is an uncommon breast malignancy that predominantly affects postmenopausal women and generally exhibits favorable biological characteristics and excellent oncological outcomes. However, associated invasive carcinoma was identified in a substantial proportion of patients and was associated with larger tumors, higher pathological stage, axillary nodal involvement, and recurrence events. These findings emphasize the importance of meticulous pathological evaluation and individualized axillary management. While pure EPC appears to behave similarly to ductal carcinoma in situ and may not routinely require axillary staging, the presence of invasion should strongly influence decisions regarding axillary surgery and adjuvant treatment.
