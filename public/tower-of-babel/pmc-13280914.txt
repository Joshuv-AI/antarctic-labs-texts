Photochemistry of Halogen-Bonded Complexes: Light-Initiated Electron Transfer from Halide Anions to Bromo-Substituted Electrophiles
Authors: Md Mahiuddin Sarker, Maison Hardin, Sergiy V. Rosokha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Light-initiated reactions involving halogen-bonded (HaB) complexes have attracted increasing attention in recent years as a platform for developing metal-free synthetic procedures. However, mechanistic details and quantitative data on the quantum yields (efficiency) of these photochemical processes remain scarce. Halogen-bonded complexes formed between R–Br electrophiles (R–Br = CBr3NO2, CBr4, CBr3H, or CBr3F) and halide anions, X– (X– = I–, Br–, or Cl–), provided convenient model systems to address these questions. The R–Br···X– complexes exhibit distinct charge-transfer (CT) absorption bands in the 250–400 nm region, which are well separated from the higher-energy intramolecular transitions of the individual HaB donors and acceptors. Irradiation at 365, 302, and 254 nm leads to the formation of X3 – anions with quantum yields ranging from about 0.2 to 0.8. Analysis of the wavelength dependence of the quantum yields demonstrates that photochemical reactivity is initiated by excitation of the CT bands rather than by intramolecular transitions. These results highlight the crucial role of the charge-transfer component of halogen bonding in enabling photochemical transformations. In general, higher quantum yields are observed for systems containing stronger electron acceptors (CBr4 or CBr3NO2) and/or stronger electron donors (I–). Since complexes formed by the stronger donors and acceptors show lower-energy CT bands, these findings open a pathway toward the rational design of “green” photochemical processes operating under visible or near-UV irradiation.

Introduction

Halogen bonding has emerged over the last two decades as one of the central themes in supramolecular chemistry. − Owing to its strong directionality and tunability through the choice of halogen and substituents, halogen bonding has demonstrated broad potential in fields ranging from crystal engineering and molecular recognition to catalysis and rational drug design. Since halogen-bonded (HaB) complexes share structural features with hydrogen-bonded systems, the participating species are described similarly, i.e., halogen-containing electron-poor molecules (electron acceptors, Lewis acids) are referred to as HaB donors, while electron-rich partners (electron donors, Lewis bases) serve as HaB acceptors. ,

Halogen bonding is commonly attributed to the electrostatic attraction of electron-rich species to σ-holes, i.e., regions of positive electrostatic potential on the surface of a covalently bonded halogen atom. , However, the earliest examples of associations between dihalogens or other halogen-based electrophiles and electron donors were interpreted within the framework of Mulliken’s theory as charge-transfer (CT) complexes. − One of the hallmark features of such associations is the appearance of new absorption bands absent in the spectra of the isolated components. These bands originate from an electronic transition between a HOMO largely localized on the electron donor and a LUMO primarily located on the electron acceptor. Irradiation within the region of these bands therefore promotes charge transfer from the donor to the acceptor. In complexes formed by closed-shell molecules, this excitation produces reactive ion-radical pairs capable of initiating cascades of subsequent chemical transformations. , These photoinitiated processes can proceed under mild conditions without external catalysts and can lead to radical addition, borylation, cross-coupling, cyclization, degradation, and other synthetically valuable reactions.

Recognizing HaB complexes as a subset of donor–acceptor associations and identifying their characteristic CT absorption bands suggests that they too can engage in light-induced reactivity. , Indeed, the photoinduced electron-transfer between perfluoroiodides and heterocyclic compounds to give perfluoroalkylated products were reported even before associations of these molecules were recognized as halogen-bonded complexes. , The photochemistry of HaB complexes has received growing attention in recent years. − Notably, photoinduced electron transfer to halogenated electrophiles (HaB donors) generates radical anions, which commonly undergo rapid C–X bond cleavage to yield organic radicals and halide anions. , This fast fragmentation suppresses back electron transfer to the oxidized donor, thereby increasing the yield of downstream products. Yet, despite the growing number of reports demonstrating that irradiation of HaB complexes can generate diverse products in high yields, quantitative data on the quantum yields (i.e., the number of product molecules formed per photon absorbed) remain scarce.

A further complication arises from the spectral overlap between the CT bands of many HaB complexes and the intramolecular absorption bands of their constituents. As such, irradiation of their solutions can lead to excitation of the individual reactants. , These excited species are typically much stronger electron donors or acceptors than their ground-state parent molecules and may initiate electron transfer independently. , This complicates mechanistic analysis and hinders optimization of experimental procedures (e.g., the choice of the irradiation wavelength).

Clarifying the mechanisms of photoactivation and determining quantum yields (i.e., light-based efficiencies) of photochemical reactions are essential for guiding their rational use in synthesis. Moreover, halogenated species are common pollutants that could be remediated via thermal, electrochemical, or photochemical reductive cleavage of carbon–halogen bonds. − As such, quantitative information on their photochemical reactions is important for understanding their environmental transformation and for designing effective mitigation pathways. Accordingly, to clarify these aspects of the photochemistry of HaB complexes, we investigate in this work light-induced processes involving associations formed by bromo-substituted electrophiles R–Br (Chart 1) and halide anions, X– (X– = I–, Br–, or Cl–).

Bromo-Substituted Electrophiles (Y = H, F, Br, or NO2)

Earlier solution-phase and X-ray crystallographic studies demonstrated that the interaction of halides with R–Br electrophiles results in the formation of HaB complexes, R-Br·X–. , Their formation constants are in 0.3 M–1 to 10 M–1 range and the halogen-bond lengths in their X-ray structures are about 10–15% shorter than the van der Waals separations. In the absence of light, these complexes are persistent, except for the complex of the strongest electrophile, e.g., CBr3NO2, with the strongest electron donor, e.g., iodide. Such HaB association undergoes fast redox processes with formation of I3 – at room temperature, and therefore can be observed only at low temperature. In general, the R-Br·X–complexes are characterized by distinct absorption bands in the 250–400 nm range (Table ). The Mulliken correlation between the energies of these bands and the differences in redox potentials (paralleling their HOMO–LUMO gaps) of the corresponding electron donors, X–, and acceptors, R–Br, confirms their CT nature. Most importantly, these bands are well separated from the absorptions of the individual donors and acceptors, which exhibit absorption bands at shorter wavelengths (Table ).

Tuning the redox potentials of the HaB donors and acceptors enables modulation of the charge-transfer excitation energies and allows comparison of photochemical efficiencies at different wavelengths. Finally, oxidation of halide anions yields trihalide species, X3 –, with intense and well-defined absorption bands (Table ), facilitating determination of their yields. Taken together, these characteristics make complexes of R–Br electrophiles with halides an ideal model system for elucidating both the mechanisms and efficiencies of photochemical processes in HaB assemblies.

Experimental Section

Commercially available CBr3H and CBr4 were purified by distillation and sublimation, respectively; tetra-n-butylammonium (Bu4NX) and tetrapropylammonium (Pr4NX) salts of chloride, bromide and iodide were purified by recrystallization; and CBr3F was used without additional purification. CBr3NO2 was synthesized by bromination of nitromethane as described previously.

Photochemical reactions were performed in acetonitrile solutions containing 0.5–1.0 mM of R-Br and excess (0.1–0.3 M) of halide salts (the results obtained with Bu4N+ or Pr4N+ salts were within the error limit). At these concentrations, the intensities of the CT absorption bands of HaB complexes (at their maxima) were in 0.5 to 2.0 range in a quartz cell with l = 1 cm. Stirred solutions were irradiated with UPV 3UV lamp (low-pressure mercury fluorescent tubes with bandpass filters from AnalitikJena). It delivers light at shortwave (λirr = 254 nm, narrow emission line with bandwidth of less than 3 nm), midrange (λirr = 302 nm, bandwidth of about 10 nm), or longwave (λirr = 365 nm, bandwidth of about 30 nm centered at 365 nm), wavelengths. Intensity of the incident light at each wavelength were determined using a standard ferrioxalate actinometry. Light intensity were 9.6 × 1015 photons/s at λirr = 254 nm, 17 × 1015 photons/s at λirr = 302 nm, and 14 × 1015 photons/s at λirr = 365 nm.

UV–vis spectra were measured in the same cell using CARY 5000 UV–vis spectrophotometer. The absorption of the solution upon irradiation can be expressed as the sum of the absorption of the HaB complex and X3 – formed upon irradiation: Abs = (cX3×εX3 + (cC – cX3)×εc)l, where cc is initial concentration of the complex (before irradiation) and cX3 is a concentration of trihalide; while εc and εX3 are extinction coefficients of these species at a given wavelength and l = 1 cm. As such, concentration of the X3 – anions formed upon irradiation can be determined as cX3 = ΔAbs/ (εX3 - εc) l, where ΔAbs is the differential absorption at the absorption bands maxima of X3 – anions (determined by subtraction of intensity of absorption at this wavelength of the solution before irradiation from the intensity of absorption after irradiation). The absorption maxima observed upon irradiation of solutions of R–Br in the presence of I–, Br–, or Cl– anions were similar to those measured in solutions containing Br2 (0.1–0.3 mM) and a large excess (100 mM) of the corresponding halide. The interaction of Br2 with halides in such mixtures leads to the formation of Br2X–, followed by X3 – (due to rapid halide exchange). Furthermore, the same absorption bands were observed in solutions containing halides, Br2 and 0.25 mM of R–Br (comparable to that in the solutions under irradiation). These results support the assignments of the absorption bands appearing upon irradiation of R–Br solutions with halide anions to X3 – species.

Quantum yields were obtained by dividing the rates of formation of X3 – during irradiation by the intensities of the absorbed light (obtained by multiplying the overall light intensity by the ratio of the light absorbed, i.e. I/Io, where I/Io was determined from the initial absorbance of the complex at λirr (since Abs­(complex)=log (Io /I)). The essentially linear increase of the concentration of X3 – during 4–6 initial irradiation intervals (vide infra) indicated that product did not affected rates of photochemical reactions at this stage. The values of the quantum yields were obtained typically as an average of 3–4 experiments for each HaB complex at each wavelength (and each experiment included measurements of the spectral changes upon 4–6 irradiation intervals).

Geometries of the complexes were optimized without constraints in acetonitrile (using a polarizable continuum model) at the M06–2X/def2-TZVPP level with the Gaussian 09 suit of programs. − The absence of imaginary frequencies confirmed that the optimized structures correspond to true minima. Time-dependent DFT calculations were performed at the same level using the optimized geometries of the complexes. Previous computational benchmarks have shown that the M06–2X functional is among the most accurate and reliable DFT methods for modeling HaB associations. , The triple-ζ def2-TZVPP basis set includes two sets of polarization functions for heavy atoms and one set for hydrogen atoms. Inner-shell electrons are treated using effective core potentials, which reduce basis-set size and account for scalar relativistic effects, important for systems containing iodine. Our previous studies also demonstrated that M06–2X/def2-TZVPP calculations reproduce experimental features of HaB complexes. The calculations with B3LYP-D3 functional produced complexes with very close binding energies (mean unsigned deviations, MUD, of 0.45 kcal/mol) and halogen-bond Br···X bond lengths (MUD= 0.08 Å). Energies and atomic coordinates of the optimized complexes are provided in the Supporting Information.

Results and Discussion

UV–Vis Study of the Photoreactions of R-Br·X ‑ Complexes

Irradiation of solutions of the complexes formed by R–Br electrophiles and halide anions in the UV range resulted in the appearance and gradual increase of intensity of absorption bands of X3 – anions, indicating the occurrence of a photoinduced redox process. For example, addition of a large excess of iodide (taken as a salt with tetrabutylammonium counterions) to a solution of CBr3F in acetonitrile led to the appearance of a broad absorption band at λmax = 302 nm, indicating formation of the CBr2F–Br···I– complex. Irradiation of this solution with light at λirr = 302 nm resulted in the appearance of two bands with maxima at 293 and 365 nm (Figure ), which are characteristic features of I3 – anions.

UV–vis spectral changes upon irradiation of solutions containing 0.87 mM CBr3F and 0.22 M I– at λirr = 302 nm. Inset: Dependence of the differential absorption (ΔAbs) at 365 nm on irradiation time.

The intensities of these bands (obtained by subtracting the absorption spectrum of the solution before irradiation from that after irradiation) increase at the initial stage almost linearly with irradiation time. The time dependences of this differential absorption, ΔAbs, allowed for determination of the rate of increase in the concentration of I3 – anions during irradiation using the extinction coefficients of the I3 – bands. From these data, the quantum yield for I3 – formation was calculated to be 0.31 ± 0.04 (see the Experimental Section for details).

Irradiation of similar solutions containing CBr3F and I– at λirr = 365 or 254 nm also resulted in the appearance of absorption bands characteristic of I3 – anions. Analogous formation of I3 – was observed upon irradiation of solutions containing I– and CHBr3 or CBr4 at 254, 302, or 365 nm (see the Supporting Information, note that measurements of the photochemical reactions in solutions of I– and CBr3NO2 were hindered by fast thermal electron transfer between these species). Computational Analysis of the Electronic Transitions in the R-Br· X – Complexes

The interaction of the R-Br electrophiles (Chart 1) with halides gives rise to new absorption bands in the UV–Vis spectra. Previous analysis relating the energies of these bands to the HOMO levels of the halides and the LUMO levels of the halogenated molecules suggested that they originate from charge-transfer transitions. To confirm this assignment, we carried out quantum-chemical calculations on the relevant complexes. Consistent with earlier studies, DFT optimization of the R–Br/X– pairs revealed energy minima corresponding to halogen-bonded (HaB) complexes. It is noteworthy that optimization of the dyads involving bromoform also yielded hydrogen-bonded (HyB) complexes. Such results are consistent with the earlier NMR studies indicating formation of HyB complexes between halides and CHBr3. , More recent combined UV–Vis and NMR studies, together with quantum-chemical calculations, showed that the HyB and halogen-bonded (HaB) complexes of bromoform with halides have comparable formation constants (0.87 M–1 and 0.52 M–1 in acetonitrile, respectively) and binding energies (Table S1, Supporting Information). However, TD-DFT calculations indicate that the lowest-energy absorption bands of the HyB complexes are 4–5 times less intense than those of the HaB complexes (Table ), confirming that the absorption of the HaB complexes dominates in the irradiation range.

The energies of the HaB absorption bands correlate with the HOMO–LUMO gaps of the complexes (Table ). Visualization of the molecular orbitals shows that the HOMOs are primarily localized on the halide anions, whereas the LUMOs reside on the brominated electrophiles (Figure ). TD-DFT analysis further indicates that the lowest-energy transitions with significant oscillator strength are dominated by HOMO → LUMO excitations. Together, these results confirm the charge-transfer nature of the observed bands, analogous to those in classical donor–acceptor complexes.

Mechanistic Analysis of the Photo-Redox Reactions

Irradiation of solutions containing halides and R–Br electrophiles at λirr = 254, 302, or 365 nm resulted, in most cases, in formation of the corresponding trihalide anions. The absence of such reactions upon irradiation of chloride solutions at 365 nm (and, for the weaker electrophiles, at 302 nm) is clearly related to the fact that the charge-transfer and intramolecular transitions in complexes with Cl– anions (and in their individual components) are characterized by higher energies. As a result, these solutions are nearly transparent at these wavelengths. The same reasoning explains the essentially complete absence of photoreaction upon irradiation at 365 nm of solutions containing bromide and weaker electron acceptors, i.e., CHBr3 or CFBr3.

In contrast, irradiation at λirr = 365 nm of solutions containing bromide and CBr4 or CBr3NO2, as well as solutions containing R–Br molecules and iodide, resulted in the formation of Br3 – or I3 – anions, respectively. It is important to note that neither the individual halides nor the R–Br electrophiles absorb above 350 nm; thus, solutions of the isolated reactants are transparent at λirr = 365 nm. The only species that absorb in this region are the HaB complexes. Therefore, photochemical processes initiated by irradiation in this spectral region can be attributed exclusively to excitation of the CT bands.

Furthermore, the quantum yields of the photoreactions initiated by irradiation of the same solutions at 302 or 254 nm are, in most cases, close to those obtained upon irradiation at 365 nm. Although the individual R–Br electrophiles do absorb at 254 nm, the weak dependence of the quantum yields on the irradiation wavelength suggests that CT absorption represents the dominant pathway for these photoreactions (with the likely exception of the solutions with I– anions irradiated at 254 nm, since these anions absorb strongly at this range, vide infra). Overall, these data demonstrate that photoinduced charge transfer, rather than intramolecular excitation, constitutes the first step of the most of photochemical processes.

Previous studies of thermal electron transfer from iodide to R–Br electrophiles mediated by HaB complexes make it possible to identify two important features relevant to the photochemical reactions. First, thermal electron transfer to the R–Br electrophiles considered in the current work adds an electron to the antibonding σ* orbital. This addition is accompanied by concerted (barrierless) dissociation of the R–Br bond with release of a bromide anion, which was halogen-bonded to the halide donor. Second, in the HaB complex, the electron donor (halide) is arranged close to the bromine atom, with the C–Br···I angle close to 180°. The Br– anion detached from the electrophile is attracted to the halogen atom X formed upon electron transfer, and this attraction results in formation of the BrX– · radical anion. In essence, electron transfer from the halide to R–Br within the HaB complex leads to elongation of the C–Br bond and shortening of the Br···X separation, which results in cleavage of the C–Br bond and formation of the corresponding Br–X bond (eq ). 1

In a similar manner, absorption of a photon in the region of the CT band results in the transfer of an electron from the HOMO (located primarily on the halide) to the LUMO (which represents the antibonding σ* orbital of R–Br, Figure ). This leads to formation of the same R· radical and BrX–· radical anion (eq 2) as in the thermally initiated process in eq . 2

Furthermore, the R· radical is a stronger oxidant than its parent R–Br electrophile. Accordingly, it can oxidize an additional halide anion (which binds X– to form X2 –·) and/or BrX–·, resulting in formation of the R– anion, may extract a hydrogen atom from the tetraalkylammonium cations or participate in radical recombination reactions. The R– anions may abstract a proton from the solvent to form R–H. A detailed analysis of the pathways involving organic radicals and anions, as well as the characterization of the organic photoproducts, however, is beyond the scope of the present study. For the systems in which photoreactions occur, the quantum yields of X3 – anions listed in Table vary from about 0.2 to over 0.8. It is noticeable that the trihalide yields are generally higher in systems containing stronger electron acceptors (CBr4 and CBr3NO2) and stronger donors (I–). This trend is likely related to the fact that the radicals produced upon photodissociation of the C–X bonds in CBr4 and CBr3NO2 are stronger oxidants, which facilitates the forward reactions in eqs and .

It should also be noted that in solutions containing a large excess of iodide, as in the present study, the absorption at ∼ 254 nm is dominated by the iodide anion, which exhibits a strong band in this region (see Table ). Consequently, irradiation at 254 nm primarily leads to photoexcitation of I–: I−+hν→I−* 9

The resulting photoexcited iodide (I–*) is a much stronger reducing agent than ground-state I– (since Eox* = Eox – Eexc) and can, in principle, readily reduce R–Br molecules. I−*+R−Br→I·+R−Br− 10

However, it is more likely that the actual reducing species are solvated electrons. Indeed, photoexcitation of iodide is known to populate charge-transfer-to-solvent (CTTS) states that undergo ultrafast electron detachment, producing solvated electrons on subpicosecond time scales. The latter are also very strong reducing agents, which reduce R-Br electrophiles. I−*→I·+e−solv 11 e−solv+R−Br→R−Br− 12

In either case, photon absorption ultimately leads to efficient, albeit indirect, reduction of the R–Br electrophiles (accompanied by dissociation of the C–Br) and formation of iodine atoms, analogous to the pathway initiated by excitation of the charge-transfer transition in the complex. The overall efficiencies of these processes are likely governed by similar subsequent reaction steps, which accounts for the comparable quantum yields observed upon irradiation at different wavelengths. In the same way, the photoexcited I3 –* anions (resulting from the absorption light by I3 – anions) are very strong reducing agents comparable to that of I– *. As such, the photoexcitation of these anions leads to the processes analogous to that described in eqs –. Thus, although I3 – anions are characterized by strong band near 365 nm, the increase of its absorption at this wavelength upon irradiation does not affect significantly efficiency of the photoredox processes (which explains essentially linear dependence of the absorption at 360 nm on irradiation time).

Overall, the data obtained in the present work demonstrate that the photochemical processes involving halogen-bonded complexes are initiated, in most cases, by the excitation of the charge-transfer bands, rather than intramolecular transitions of the individual components. This finding underscores the vital role of the charge-transfer component of halogen bonding in enabling the use of this intermolecular interaction in photochemical synthetic procedures. Moreover, because complexes formed by the strongest electron donors and acceptors are characterized by the highest quantum yields and lowest-energies CT absorption bands, such pairs provide the most efficient platforms for designing “green” photochemical processes using visible light, including sunlight.
