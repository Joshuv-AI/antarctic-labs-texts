Early Distant Metastasis in Synchronous Bilateral Breast Cancer including a Small Low-Grade Carcinoma with Osteoclast-Like Giant Cells: A Case Report
Authors: Mayuko Hirata, Tamami Morisaki, Yuki Asaka, Hanae Matsuda, Saeko Henmi, Yuko Kikukawa, Asuka Kochi, Chika Watanabe, Koji Takada, Yukie Tauchi, Kana Ogisawa, Haruhito Kinoshita, Kenichi Kohashi, Shinichiro Kashiwagi

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTINTRODUCTIONBreast carcinoma with osteoclast-like giant cells (OGCs) is an uncommon morphological finding, and its clinical significance remains unclear. We report a case of synchronous bilateral hormone receptor-positive breast cancer including a small, low-grade carcinoma with OGCs, followed by early distant recurrence after surgery.CASE PRESENTATIONA 47-year-old woman presented with a palpable mass in the left breast. Imaging revealed synchronous bilateral breast tumors without evidence of nodal or distant metastasis. She underwent bilateral mastectomy and bilateral sentinel lymph node biopsy. The right breast tumor was a carcinoma with OGCs measuring 6 mm in diameter; it was estrogen receptor (ER)-positive, progesterone receptor (PgR)-positive, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-negative, had a Ki-67 labeling index of 10%, was nuclear grade 1/histological grade 1, and showed no lymphovascular invasion or nodal metastasis. The left breast tumor was an invasive ductal carcinoma (so-called scirrhous type), measuring 25 mm in diameter; it was ER-positive, PgR-positive, HER2-negative, had a Ki-67 labeling index of 10%, was nuclear grade 2/histological grade 2, showed lymphatic invasion, and had an Oncotype DX recurrence score of 16. Tamoxifen was initiated. CT performed 6 months after surgery as postoperative imaging follow-up for this patient revealed multiple pulmonary nodules, and PET-CT showed pulmonary and lumbar vertebral lesions. Pathological confirmation was not performed; therefore, the precise origin could not be determined. Fulvestrant plus abemaciclib was started for clinically suspected recurrent breast cancer.CONCLUSIONSThis case illustrates that early distant recurrence can occur in synchronous bilateral hormone receptor-positive breast cancer even when available clinicopathological and genomic findings appear relatively favorable. However, because the left conventional invasive ductal carcinoma had more plausible recurrence-risk features than the small right-sided carcinoma with OGCs, and because the metastatic lesions were not pathologically confirmed, this case should not be interpreted as evidence that the OGC-containing carcinoma caused the recurrence. Rather, it highlights the diagnostic and risk-assessment complexity of synchronous bilateral breast cancer that includes a rare stromal-rich morphological pattern.

INTRODUCTION

Breast carcinoma with OGCs is an uncommon morphological pattern of invasive breast carcinoma characterized by the presence of multinucleated giant cells within the tumor stroma.1–3) In the current World Health Organization classification, breast carcinoma with OGCs is not regarded as an independent histological subtype, but rather as a special morphological pattern that may accompany invasive carcinoma, most commonly invasive carcinoma of no special type.1,4) Because of its rarity, the clinicopathological features, biological significance, and prognostic implications of this pattern remain incompletely understood.2–5)

OGCs in breast carcinoma are generally considered non-neoplastic stromal cells rather than carcinoma cells themselves. Immunohistochemical and ultrastructural studies have suggested that these cells are derived from the monocyte–macrophage lineage, typically expressing histiocytic markers such as CD68 and lacking epithelial marker expression.6–8) Earlier functional studies also demonstrated that osteoclast-like multinucleated giant cells isolated from breast carcinoma could resorb bone in vitro, suggesting osteoclast-like functional properties despite their macrophage lineage.6) More recent studies have further suggested that OGCs may belong to the spectrum of TAMs, particularly M2-like macrophages, and they may be linked to an activated tumor immune microenvironment and RANK-L-related biology.8–10) These findings indicate that the presence of OGCs may reflect a distinct stromal and immune microenvironment rather than a purely incidental histological finding.

The clinical behavior of breast carcinoma with OGCs remains controversial. Some studies have reported that these tumors frequently show a luminal phenotype and may behave similarly to conventional invasive breast carcinoma, whereas others have described recurrence or metastasis, suggesting biological heterogeneity.4,5,10) In addition, when breast carcinoma with OGCs occurs in the setting of synchronous bilateral breast cancer, interpretation of metastatic origin can be challenging, because bilateral breast tumors may represent either independent primary tumors or clonally related disease, and histological assessment alone may be insufficient to distinguish these possibilities.11,12)

Herein, we report a case of synchronous bilateral hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer that developed early distant metastasis after surgery, in which one tumor was a small, low-grade carcinoma with OGCs. Because the patient had bilateral primary tumors and the metastatic lesions were not pathologically confirmed, the recurrence cannot be definitively attributed to the OGC-containing carcinoma. This case therefore highlights the diagnostic complexity of synchronous bilateral breast cancer and the potential limitations of conventional clinicopathological risk assessment when rare stromal-rich morphological patterns are present.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 47-year-old woman presented to a local clinic with a palpable mass in the left breast and was referred to our hospital for further evaluation and treatment. She had a medical history of bronchial asthma and no notable family history of breast or ovarian cancer. On physical examination, no definite mass was palpable in the right breast, whereas a mass was palpable near the left areola. No clinically apparent axillary lymphadenopathy was observed. Laboratory tests showed no elevation of tumor markers.

Breast ultrasonography revealed a lobulated hypoechoic mass measuring 8.7 × 5.1 × 5.6 mm in the upper outer area of the right breast (Fig. 1A). In the left breast, an irregular hypoechoic mass measuring 22.5 × 12.5 × 17.8 mm was detected in the periareolar to lower inner area (Fig. 1B). Core needle biopsy of the right breast tumor showed carcinoma with OGCs. The tumor was ER-positive, PgR-positive, HER2-negative, and had a Ki-67 labeling index of 20%. Core needle biopsy of the left breast tumor showed invasive ductal carcinoma, which was ER-positive, PgR-positive, HER2-negative, and had a Ki-67 labeling index of 25%. The higher Ki-67 values in the biopsy specimens than in the surgical specimens were considered to reflect intratumoral heterogeneity and sampling differences between core needle biopsy and the resected specimens.

Preoperative imaging findings of synchronous bilateral breast tumors. (A) Ultrasonography of the right breast showing a lobulated hypoechoic mass measuring 8.7 × 5.1 × 5.6 mm. (B) Ultrasonography of the left breast showing an irregular hypoechoic mass measuring 22.5 × 12.5 × 17.8 mm. (C) Preoperative chest CT showing no evidence of pulmonary metastasis.

Chest and abdominal CT showed no apparent lymph node or distant metastasis (Fig. 1C). Bone scintigraphy revealed no evidence of bone metastasis. Breast MRI was not performed because of bronchial asthma. The preoperative clinical stage was cT1bN0M0, cStage I for the right breast cancer and cT2N0M0, cStage IIA for the left breast cancer.

The patient underwent bilateral mastectomy and bilateral sentinel lymph node biopsy. Histopathological examination of the right breast tumor revealed a carcinoma with OGCs measuring 6 mm in diameter. Hematoxylin and eosin staining showed invasive carcinoma accompanied by multinucleated OGCs in the tumor stroma (Fig. 2A). Immunohistochemically, the giant cells were positive for CD68, supporting their histiocytic/macrophage lineage (Fig. 2B). The carcinoma cells were ER-positive, PgR-positive, and HER2-negative. The Ki-67 labeling index was 10%. The tumor was classified as nuclear grade 1 and histological grade 1, with no lymphatic or venous invasion. Sentinel lymph node metastasis was not detected, and the final pathological stage was pT1N0M0.

The left breast tumor was an invasive ductal carcinoma (so-called scirrhous type), measuring 25 mm in diameter. In summary, the left tumor was ER-positive, PgR-positive, and HER2-negative, with a Ki-67 labeling index of 10%, nuclear grade 2, histological grade 2, lymphatic invasion, no venous invasion, and an Oncotype DX recurrence score of 16. Thus, compared with the right-sided 6-mm grade 1 carcinoma with OGCs, the left tumor had several clinicopathological features that made it at least an equally plausible, if not more plausible, source of subsequent recurrence. Based on the clinicopathological findings and genomic recurrence score, adjuvant endocrine therapy with tamoxifen was initiated.

Six months after surgery, follow-up CT was performed as postoperative imaging follow-up for this patient rather than in response to a specific documented symptom or tumor-marker elevation; this imaging revealed multiple pulmonary nodules suspicious for metastases (Fig. 3A). PET-CT performed at that time demonstrated abnormal FDG uptake in the pulmonary lesions and lumbar vertebra, consistent with distant metastases (Fig. 3B). Biopsy or cytological examination of the pulmonary or bone lesions was not performed. Therefore, because the patient had synchronous bilateral breast cancers, the precise origin of the metastatic lesions could not be definitively determined. Although another primary malignancy, including primary lung cancer, cannot be completely excluded based on imaging alone, the imaging pattern and clinical context were considered compatible with recurrent breast cancer. First-line systemic therapy for recurrent disease with fulvestrant plus abemaciclib was initiated. At the time of manuscript revision, the follow-up period after treatment initiation was too short to reliably evaluate radiological response; therefore, treatment-response data are not presented.

DISCUSSION

Breast carcinoma with OGCs is a rare morphological pattern of invasive breast carcinoma, and its clinicopathological significance remains controversial.1–5) The present case is noteworthy because early distant recurrence occurred after surgery for synchronous bilateral hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative breast cancers, despite apparently favorable clinicopathological and genomic findings. However, the metastatic origin must be interpreted cautiously. The right-sided OGC-containing carcinoma measured only 6 mm, was grade 1, and showed no lymphovascular invasion or nodal metastasis, whereas the left conventional invasive ductal carcinoma measured 25 mm, was grade 2, and showed lymphatic invasion. Therefore, the left breast cancer is at least an equally plausible, and possibly more plausible, source of recurrence. This case should thus be interpreted as early systemic recurrence in synchronous bilateral breast cancer that included an OGC-containing carcinoma, not as evidence that the OGC-containing carcinoma caused the recurrence.

OGCs in breast carcinoma are generally regarded as non-neoplastic stromal multinucleated giant cells derived from the monocyte–macrophage lineage.6–8) In the present case, CD68 positivity supported their histiocytic/macrophage lineage. Previous studies have suggested that OGCs may represent a specialized macrophage population within the TME, including an M2-like or immunosuppressive TAM phenotype.8,9) Molecular characterization has also suggested possible involvement of RANK-L-related biology in breast carcinomas with OGCs.5) These findings support the concept that OGCs may reflect a distinctive stromal and immune microenvironment. Nevertheless, the presence of OGCs alone does not establish aggressive behavior or metastatic tropism, and any association with the lumbar vertebral metastasis observed in this patient remains speculative.

The unexpectedly rapid recurrence also illustrates the limitations of recurrence-risk assessment based on a single prognostic system. The 21-gene recurrence score is a validated tool in hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative early breast cancer,13,14) and the left tumor had a recurrence score of 16. However, this result should be interpreted as one component of risk assessment rather than definitive evidence of biologically negligible risk. Other prognostic systems may provide complementary information. EPclin integrates the EndoPredict molecular score with clinicopathological variables such as tumor size and nodal status, whereas CTS5 integrates age, tumor size, grade, and nodal status to estimate late distant recurrence after 5 years of endocrine therapy.15,16) In the present case, EndoPredict was not performed and EPclin could not be calculated; CTS5 is also not optimized to predict recurrence within 6 months after surgery. Surrogate models incorporating ER, PgR, Ki-67, and TILs have recently been proposed to estimate the Oncotype DX recurrence score.17) However, TILs were not quantified in this case, and such models cannot determine metastatic origin or fully capture the significance of rare OGC-rich stromal patterns.

A key diagnostic limitation is the absence of pathological confirmation of the metastatic lesions. Because biopsy or cytological examination of the pulmonary or bone lesions was not performed, the metastatic origin could not be confirmed by histology, immunohistochemistry, or molecular comparison. In synchronous bilateral breast cancer, histology alone may be insufficient to determine whether tumors are independent primary cancers or clonally related disease, and genomic comparison between bilateral primaries and metastatic lesions can be useful.11,12,18) Moreover, because the pulmonary lesions were diagnosed radiologically, another primary malignancy, including primary lung cancer, cannot be completely excluded based on imaging alone. The follow-up period after initiation of fulvestrant plus abemaciclib was also too short to evaluate treatment response at the time of manuscript revision. These limitations are important and should temper the interpretation of this case.

In summary, this case highlights the complexity of interpreting early distant recurrence in synchronous bilateral hormone receptor-positive breast cancer when one tumor contains OGCs. The case does not prove that OGCs were responsible for recurrence; rather, it emphasizes that conventional clinicopathological and genomic factors may not fully explain all patterns of early systemic relapse, particularly in rare stromal-rich morphological settings. Further accumulation of cases with metastatic-site biopsy, molecular comparison between primary and metastatic lesions, immune microenvironment analysis, and standardized recurrence-risk assessment is needed to clarify the clinical significance of OGCs in breast carcinoma.

CONCLUSIONS

We reported a case of synchronous bilateral hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer that developed early distant metastasis after surgery, in which one tumor was a small, low-grade carcinoma with OGCs. Because the metastatic lesions were not pathologically confirmed and the left conventional invasive ductal carcinoma had more plausible recurrence-risk features, the recurrence cannot be definitively attributed to the OGC-containing carcinoma. This case therefore emphasizes the limitations of conventional clinicopathological and genomic risk assessment, as well as the diagnostic difficulty of determining metastatic origin in synchronous bilateral breast cancer. Further clinicopathological and molecular studies are warranted to clarify whether OGCs represent a biologically relevant tumor microenvironmental feature or merely an associated rare morphological finding.
