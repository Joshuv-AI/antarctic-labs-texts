Zero-fluoroscopy cardioneuroablation in a pregnant woman with cardioinhibitory neurally mediated syncope
Authors: Massimo Saviano, Giulio Pio Federico Mallardi, Francesco Moda, Stefano Carugo
Published: 2026

Introduction

Cardioneuroablation (CNA) is an interventional therapy aimed at modulating cardiac autonomic function through the selective ablation of parasympathetic ganglionated plexi (GP). By modulating vagal inputs to the sinus and atrioventricular nodes, CNA has emerged as a potential treatment for selected patients with cardioinhibitory neurally mediated syncope (NMS), particularly when symptoms are recurrent and disabling, in the absence of structural heart disease.

Despite increasing clinical experience, CNA remains a nonroutine therapy: robust randomized evidence is limited, and procedural end points and patient-selection criteria remain to be standardized.1 These uncertainties are even more relevant during pregnancy, when therapeutic options may be limited and minimizing procedural risk, including avoidance of ionizing radiation, is mandatory. Here, we report a case of zero-fluoroscopy CNA performed in a pregnant woman with recurrent traumatic cardioinhibitory NMS and a documented prolonged pause, yielding symptom resolution through delivery and during the postpartum period.

Case report

A woman aged 24 years at 26 weeks’ gestation presented to the emergency department after a syncopal episode complicated by head and abdominal trauma.

After acute obstetric–gynecologic conditions were excluded, the patient experienced a further syncopal event during observation. Continuous electrocardiogram (ECG) monitoring documented a 10-second pause preceded by progressive sinus bradycardia. The patient also reported frequent episodes of typical prodromal symptoms, such as blurred vision, palpitations, and an impending sense of loss of consciousness.

Her medical history revealed 2 prior syncopal episodes during the current pregnancy, with clinical features fully overlapping those previously described. No other relevant medical history was reported.

On the basis of the clinical presentation and reassuring fetal status, the patient was admitted to the cardiology unit for monitoring. Structural heart disease was excluded, and first-line tests were performed; baseline 12-lead ECG showed sinus rhythm at approximately 100 beats per minute (bpm), with normal atrioventricular and intraventricular conduction, normal repolarization, and a normal QTc interval. Routine blood tests, including electrolytes, were within normal limits. Neurologic assessment was negative. Telemetry during admission did not reveal additional clinically significant arrhythmic events or hypotension. Therefore, we performed a bedside vagal challenge with left carotid sinus massage, documenting profound bradycardia (heart rate ∼20 bpm), reproducing clinical manifestations.

The syncopal episodes were therefore attributed to a cardioinhibitory neurally mediated mechanism. According to these findings, we proposed a zero-fluoroscopy CNA avoiding ionizing radiation.2

The gynecologists administered antenatal corticosteroid therapy to promote fetal lung maturation and performed serial ultrasound monitoring to safeguard fetal well-being, eventually prompting intervention if needed.

We targeted parasympathetic GP under deep sedation, with continuous support from gynecologists and anesthesiologists throughout the procedure. We entirely performed the procedure without fluoroscopy, thus completely avoiding ionizing radiation. Hemodynamic stability was monitored using invasive radial arterial blood pressure and end-tidal carbon dioxide. We introduced the mapping catheter (HD-Grid Abbott Laboratories, Chicago, IL) through left subclavian venous access and used the EnSite X system to create a sinus rhythm local activation time map to delineate the sinoatrial node, minimizing the risk of inadvertent injury.

Three primary approaches for identifying GPs have been described: anatomical localization, high-frequency stimulation to elicit a vagal response, and electrogram (EGM)-guided identification based on characteristic signals.2

In our center, GP localization is EGM based because this approach is considered more sensitive and time efficient. Typical low voltage, multiphasic, fractionated potentials were used to identify parasympathetic GP, including the right atrial superior GP, located near the junction of the superior vena cava and right atrium. The target signals of EGM were characterized by >3 deflections, each lasting ≥2 ms, with a frequency between 560 and 980 Hz showing >0.06 mV and <1.5 mV of amplitude. We considered a minimum threshold of 0.06 mV to exclude noise or low-amplitude signals related to myocardial scar. The maximum threshold was set as 1.5 mV to increase specificity, given GP signals typically have lower amplitude than does healthy atrial myocardium. Using this approach, we identified target signals corresponding to the superior and inferior right atrial GP (Figure 1).

We used a TactiFlex (Abbott) ablation catheter through the left subclavian vein access. After phrenic nerve localization using electrical stimulation in the area of superior vena cava/crista terminalis, we delivered up to 38 W radiofrequency (RF) to the right atrial superior and inferior GP, achieving abolition of local EGM.

In particular, we performed 5 RF pulses at the right superior GP and 1 pulse at the right inferior GP, each lasting approximately 30 seconds. Our procedural end point was the abolition of the characteristic GP potential.

The ablation increased the heart rate (by ∼25%), without eliciting any vagal reflex. An atropine test (2 mg intravenous) performed at the end of the procedure showed no chronotropic response, consistent with effective parasympathetic attenuation.

Postprocedural fetal ultrasound examinations confirmed fetal well-being.

In the days after the procedure, the patient’s condition remained stable and asymptomatic. Her heart rate stabilized at approximately 110‒115 bpm, consistent with intraprocedural findings. She was therefore discharged and completed the remainder of the pregnancy uneventfully.

The patient spontaneously delivered after 39 weeks’ gestation, with no further syncopal episodes through 6 months of follow-up. In addition, no bradycardia or related symptoms occurred during labor or delivery. A 48-hour Holter ECG showed a mean heart rate of 95 bpm, with no significant pauses detected.

Discussion

This case illustrates a pragmatic application of zero-fluoroscopy CNA in a pregnant patient with cardioinhibitory NMS. Recurrent episodes complicated by trauma, documentation of a progressive sinus bradycardia preceding a prolonged pause, and reproduction of the patient’s typical symptoms together with marked bradycardia during a bedside vagal challenge supported the indication.

After earlier syncopal episodes during pregnancy, conservative measures, including adequate hydration, had already been recommended, but these failed to prevent recurrence. From a pathophysiological perspective, neurally mediated syncope may result either from increased vagal tone acting on the vasculature and leading to vasodilation or from heightened vagal activity affecting heart rate, potentially culminating in sinus arrest, as observed in our patient. Therefore, pharmacologic therapy with agents such as midodrine was not pursued, given the absence of features suggestive of orthostatic hypotension. In contrast, in light of the predominant cardioinhibitory mechanism, CNA was considered a potentially definitive strategy to prevent recurrent neurally mediated syncopal episodes. The patient’s vagal hyperactivity may be related to pregnancy-associated anatomical and mechanical factors, with the enlarging uterus compressing and enhancing vagal afferent ganglia.4

Although some reports suggest that more extensive approaches may provide higher long-term efficacy, considering the risk of ionizing radiation to the fetus and the left atrial instrumentation and their associated risks (bleeding, systemic embolization, and the technical challenges in emergency pericardiocentesis), the team opted for a right-atrium‒only ablation strategy. Moreover, given the transient nature of the pregnancy related neuromediated syncope, the durability of the result was relatively insignificant.

The single subclavian venous access minimized the number of vascular punctures and consequently the risk of access-related complications. A subclavian approach was also preferred because the uterine pelvic compression, during pregnancy, may lead to venous tortuosity and augmented venous pressure, increasing the risk of hemorrhages.

This report is limited to a single case without long-term follow-up. Further data are needed to define standardized procedural endpoints, durability, and patient selection criteria for CNA, particularly in pregnancy, when specific safety considerations must guide procedural planning and strategy.

Conclusion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the only reported case of zero-fluoroscopy CNA performed during pregnancy.

In this patient with recurrent traumatic cardioinhibitory NMS and a documented prolonged pause, CNA was feasible and appeared effective in the short term, allowing progression to delivery without maternal or fetal complications.5 Notably, the patient remained completely asymptomatic for the rest of pregnancy.

Although limited by the single-case nature and short follow-up, this report supports considering zero-fluoroscopy CNA as a device-free option in carefully selected pregnant patients when conservative measures fail.
