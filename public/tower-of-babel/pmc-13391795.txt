Systemic Inflammation Triggers Motor Axon Degeneration Via Macrophage IL-1β-Mediated P53/SIRT1/Autophagy Pathway
Authors: Bowen Sun, Jie Li, Xuexin Li, Rui Tu, Xinyu Yao, Fei He, Yun Wang, Li Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Axonal degeneration of motor neurons is a hallmark pathological feature of neurodegenerative diseases, culminating in motor dysfunction in advanced stages. Systemic inflammation exacerbates motor dysfunction, yet the relationship between systemic inflammation and motor axon degeneration remains poorly understood. We investigated whether macrophages impair motor axon morphology during systemic inflammation and the underlying mechanisms. Methods: A zebrafish systemic inflammation model was established using CuSO₄ exposure. In vivo confocal imaging examined spatiotemporal relationships between macrophages and motor axon morphology in transgenic zebrafish. Motor function was assessed using behavioral analysis. RAW264.7 macrophage-like cells stimulated with lipopolysaccharide were co-cultured with NSC-34 motor neurons to investigate molecular mechanisms. Results: CuSO₄ treatment induced elevated inflammatory cytokines and motor dysfunction in zebrafish. Histological analysis revealed increased macrophage infiltration around motor axons with concurrent reductions in axon number, length, and diameter. Macrophage depletion or IL-1Ra pretreatment significantly ameliorated motor axon damage and dysfunction. In vitro, LPS activated the NLRP3 inflammasome in RAW264.7 macrophage-like cells, releasing IL-1β, which was accompanied by disruption of the SIRT1-autophagy axis in motor neurons. Increased IL-1β release from macrophages was accompanied by upregulated P53 expression in motor neurons, which paralleled reduced SIRT1 levels, impaired autophagy, and decreased axon-associated protein expression. Pharmacological inhibition of NLRP3 with MCC950, IL-1Ra treatment, or P53 knockdown restored the SIRT1-autophagy axis and axon protein expression. Conclusions: Systemic inflammation activates macrophage NLRP3 inflammasome and promotes the release of inflammatory mediators including IL-1β, with findings suggestive of a model in which elevated IL-1β paralleled increased motor neuronal P53 expression and disruption of the SIRT1-autophagy balance, potentially contributing to motor axon morphological damage and dysfunction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10753-026-02530-6.

Introduction

Neurodegenerative diseases pose a significant and growing challenge to global healthcare systems [1]. Epidemiological studies indicate that more than 10 million people worldwide are affected by Parkinson’s disease (PD), with its prevalence having doubled over the past 25 years [2]; the carrier frequency of spinal muscular atrophy (SMA)-associated genetic variants approaches 3% of the global population [3]; while amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) remains relatively rare, its incidence rates are exhibiting an alarming upward trend [4]. These neurodegenerative disorders profoundly impact patients’ quality of life and impose substantial socioeconomic burdens on healthcare systems and society. Consequently, the development of effective therapeutic interventions has emerged as a pressing global health priority.

Structural and functional integrity of motor axons holds considerable significance for maintaining normal motor function [5, 6]. Neurofilaments, comprising light (NFL), medium (NFM), and heavy (NFH) chains, are essential cytoskeletal components primarily expressed in mature axons [7]. Motor axon degeneration, a hallmark of neurodegenerative diseases, is characterized by progressive loss of neurofilament expression, axonal swelling and fragmentation, and impaired nerve conduction, ultimately resulting in severe motor dysfunction [8, 9]. Systemic inflammation is a critical factor accelerating the onset and progression of neurodegenerative diseases, with its causal relationship to motor dysfunction being well-documented across multiple pathological conditions [10–12]. Systemic inflammation represents a complex pathophysiological state involving multiple inflammatory mediator release and immune cell activation. Macrophages, key orchestrators of immune responses, become activated during systemic inflammation and secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines (including TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β), whose abnormally elevated expression levels correlate with disease progression and motor dysfunction severity [13, 14]. While current research has established a close link between systemic inflammation and motor dysfunction development in neurodegenerative diseases, with abnormally increased inflammatory factors potentially affecting motor neurons through direct or indirect mechanisms, further investigation is needed to determine whether morphological damage to motor axons leads to motor dysfunction in the context of systemic inflammation and to understand the underlying mechanisms.

Injury to motor axons induces Wallerian degeneration and disintegration of axons and myelin, yielding substantial debris and inhibitory myelin fragments, which hinder axonal regeneration and repair [15, 16]. Macrophages, with their robust phagocytic capabilities, rapidly accumulate in degenerated axonal regions, actively engulfing degenerated and necrotic tissue debris, inhibitory myelin, and axonal fragments [17]. Beyond their role as key immune system components, macrophages influence the microenvironment through cytokine and inflammatory factor secretion. During systemic inflammation, macrophages initially serve beneficial functions through debris clearance and initiation of repair mechanisms. However, in chronic or dysregulated inflammatory states, macrophages can further damage motor axons by releasing harmful cytokines, ROS, and enzymes while impeding axonal regeneration [18, 19]. Our previous research revealed extensive macrophage presence around sciatic nerves in septic mice, with infiltrating macrophages causing sciatic nerve edema and subsequent neuromuscular dysfunction [20]. Nevertheless, the relationship between systemic inflammation-induced macrophage activation and motor axon morphological changes remains to be further delved into.

SIRT1, a histone deacetylase, is a key regulator of multiple biological processes. Through interactions with multiple downstream pathways, SIRT1 exerts broad regulatory control over cellular stress responses, metabolic homeostasis, and aging-associated processes [21]. Among its numerous downstream pathways, autophagy emerges as an exceptionally important mechanism through which SIRT1 exerts protective effects, particularly in neurons. It is crucial for maintaining cellular homeostasis and preventing neurodegeneration in neurons. Autophagy protects neurons and axons from injury during axon degeneration by degrading damaged or excess organelles and macromolecules, and reducing intracellular oxidative stress and inflammatory responses [22, 23]. SIRT1 can regulate various autophagy-related proteins and signaling pathways through deacetylation, promoting autophagy initiation and progression [24]. However, it remains unclear whether inflammatory factors secreted by macrophages under systemic inflammation can mediate motor axon degeneration by damaging the SIRT1-autophagy axis and reducing intracellular autophagy levels.

Zebrafish have emerged as powerful models for investigating microglia and macrophage functions in neurodegeneration. The optical transparency of zebrafish larvae, coupled with advanced transgenic labeling techniques, enables real-time visualization of macrophage-neuron interactions in vivo [25]. Live imaging studies have demonstrated that microglia rapidly respond to neuronal injury through morphological changes and phagocytic activation, with microglia processes converging toward damaged neurons within minutes [26]. Importantly, studies using zebrafish have revealed that inflammatory cytokines play critical regulatory roles in tissue regeneration, with both pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory phases being essential for proper repair [27, 28]. The zebrafish model thus provides unique opportunities to dissect the temporal dynamics of macrophage activation and their interactions with motor neurons during systemic inflammation.

Therefore, it was hereby hypothesized that systemic inflammation activates and recruits macrophages to infiltrate around motor axons, where they secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines that subsequently disrupt the SIRT1-autophagy axis in motor neurons, ultimately leading to axonal degeneration and motor dysfunction. Using fluorescent transgenic zebrafish and in vivo confocal microscopy, this study aims to investigate whether macrophages impair motor function through structural damage to motor axons under systemic inflammatory conditions. The potential molecular mechanisms underlying macrophage-mediated disruption of the neuronal SIRT1-autophagy axis were explored utilizing Transwell co-culture techniques. Understanding these relationships may provide valuable insights into potential therapeutic strategies for systemic inflammation-related motor dysfunction in neurodegenerative diseases.

Materials and Methods

Zebrafish Husbandry and Maintenance

Wild-type (AB) zebrafish, along with transgenic lines Tg(mnx1:eGFP) and Tg(mpeg1:mCherry), were procured from the National Zebrafish Resource Center in Wuhan, China, and subsequently maintained at the Zebrafish Technology Platform of Southwest Medical University. The fish were maintained in a controlled environment at 28 °C with a 14:10 light-dark photoperiod. Embryos were produced by crossing three adult breeding pairs and sustained in E3 medium, composed of 5 mM NaCl, 0.17 mM KCl, 0.33 mM CaCl2, 0.33 mM MgSO4, and 0.00001% methylene blue, at a constant temperature of 28 °C. All procedures involving zebrafish were performed in compliance with the laboratory animal ethics committee of Southwest Medical University (License N. 20230812-007). Clinical trial number: not applicable.

CuSO₄ Exposure

Zebrafish larvae at five days post-fertilization (dpf) were randomly allocated into 6-well plates, with 30 in each well. These larvae were subjected to treatments with either a vehicle (control) or varying concentrations of CuSO₄ (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at 5, 10, 20, or 40 µM. Following a 3 d exposure period, the larvae were harvested and rinsed three times with embryo culture water for subsequent analyses.

Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) Detection

The intracellular levels of ROS were quantified utilizing the fluorescent probe 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA; MCE, USA). Post CuSO₄ exposure, the larvae were incubated with 10 µM DCFH-DA for 1 h at room temperature under dark conditions. Following rinsing with culture water, fluorescence imaging was conducted using an Olympus fluorescence microscope.

Analysis of Macrophage Distribution and Motor Axon Morphology

Quantification of macrophages and morphological assessments were performed in accordance with established protocols [29]. Double transgenic zebrafish Tg(mnx1:eGFP; mpeg1:mCherry) were generated by crossing Tg(mnx1:eGFP) and Tg(mpeg1:mCherry) lines. At 5 dpf, embryos with normal morphology and developmental stage were identified and randomly selected for treatment groups. Following completion of the 3 d CuSO₄ exposure at 8 dpf, larvae were anesthetized with tricaine mesylate and embedded in low-melting point agarose. Z-stack images were captured using a Nikon laser scanning confocal microscope with a 20× objective lens, focusing on somites 8–12. Three-dimensional reconstructions were generated using Imaris software, and maximum intensity projections were exported as TIFF files. The shortest 3D Euclidean distance between each mpeg1⁺ macrophage centroid and the nearest motor axon surface was measured using the Distance Transformation function in Imaris, with motor axon surfaces and macrophage centroids rendered via the Surface and Spots modules, respectively, with a minimum of 100 mpeg1⁺ cells analyzed per group. To ensure unbiased analysis, all samples were coded by an independent researcher not involved in image acquisition or quantification. All image analyses were performed blinded by sample coding using ImageJ, with the treatment identity revealed only after data collection was complete. Following scale calibration, motor axon number, extension length (from spinal cord to ventral myotome), and diameter were measured. For each larva, measurements from 5 motor axons within somites 8–12 were averaged to generate a single per-animal value, which served as the biological replicate for all statistical analyses, with 15–18 larvae per treatment group, and data are reported as mean ± s.d. Macrophage quantification was performed as the number of mCherry⁺ cells within the standardized region of interest.

Macrophage Morphological Assessment

Macrophage morphology was assessed from confocal microscopy images using ImageJ software. Based on cellular morphology observed in mpeg1:mCherry⁺ cells, macrophages were categorized according to their shape characteristics: Amoeboid morphology: Compact, spherical cell body with minimal cellular processes. This morphology is associated with pro-inflammatory activation, as McWhorter et al. demonstrated that macrophages of this shape express significantly elevated iNOS levels and reduced arginase-1, consistent with M1 polarization; Intermediate morphology: Oval to slightly elongated cell shape with moderate process extension, representing a transitional state; Ramified morphology: Extended cell body with prominent, branching cellular processes. This morphology is associated with a prohealing phenotype, as elongated macrophages have been shown to express higher levels of arginase-1, CD206, and YM-1, consistent with M2 polarization [30].

Motor Behavior Analysis

Motor activity was evaluated utilizing the DanioVision behavioral tracking system. Larvae were randomly allocated to treatment groups prior to CuSO₄ exposure, and those exhibiting gross morphological abnormalities or spontaneous death prior to testing were excluded. Each experimental group consisted of 36 larvae total: 12 larvae per trial (12 technical replicates) across 3 independent trials (3 biological replicates). Subsequent to CuSO₄ exposure, individual larvae were placed into 48-well plates, each containing 1 ml of embryo medium. Following a 10-minute acclimation period at 28 °C, locomotor activity was recorded under alternating light and dark conditions (10 min dark/10 min light/10 min dark/10 min light/10 min dark; total 50 min). Behavioral recording and subsequent data analysis using EthoVision XT were performed by an investigator blinded to group allocation.

Macrophage Depletion

To achieve selective depletion of macrophages, 3 dpf Tg(mnx1:eGFP; mpeg1:mCherry) larvae were administered yolk sac injections of either 2 nl of clodronate liposomes (Lipo-clodronate; YEASEN, China) or control phosphate-buffered saline liposomes (Lipo-PBS; YEASEN, China). Post-injection, the larvae were incubated in embryo medium for 48 h before imaging with an Olympus fluorescence microscope, quantitative assessment and statistical analysis of macrophage density in the mid-trunk region with high motor axon density. The efficiency of macrophage depletion was subsequently quantified utilizing ImageJ software.

Cell Culture

The RAW264.7 macrophage-like cell line and the NSC-34 motor neuron cell line were procured from Fenghui Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Hunan, China). These cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (SPERIKON, China), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/ml penicillin, and streptomycin (Beyotime, China), at 37 °C with 5% CO₂.

Preparation of Conditioned Medium and Cell Viability Detection

RAW264.7 macrophage-like cells were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured to 70%–80% confluence. Subsequently, the cells were treated with a standard culture medium supplemented with LPS (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at concentrations of 100, 200, 500, 1000, and 2000 ng/ml for 6 h, followed by a 12 h incubation in fresh medium. The culture supernatants were then collected and centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min to remove any sediment, yielding conditioned medium. Concurrently, NSC-34 cells were cultured in 96-well plates to 70%–80% confluence and then randomly assigned to six groups: control, 100 ng/ml LPS, 200 ng/ml LPS, 500 ng/ml LPS, 1000 ng/ml LPS, and 2000 ng/ml LPS. Each group was treated with the corresponding conditioned medium and incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, 10 µl of CCK-8 reagent (TargetMol, China) was added to each well and incubated in the dark for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. The LPS concentration that reduced neuronal viability by 50% was selected for establishing the subsequent Transwell co-culture system.

Establishment of Transwell Co-Culture System

RAW264.7 macrophage-like cells were seeded in 6-well plates and treated with the optimal LPS concentration previously determined for 6 h before collection for index detection. In the Transwell system, RAW264.7 macrophage-like cells were seeded in the upper chamber, while NSC-34 cells were placed in the lower chamber, enabling separate cultivation of the two cell types. Following a 6 h LPS treatment of RAW264.7 macrophage-like cells, the two cell layers were combined and co-cultured for an additional 24 h. Subsequently, the NSC-34 cells from the lower chamber were harvested for further analysis. To inhibit macrophage NLRP3, RAW264.7 macrophage-like cells were pretreated with MCC950 (10 µM; TargetMol, China) for 2 h before LPS exposure, followed by subsequent experimental procedures.

IL-1β-Treated Motor Neurons

The optimal concentration of IL-1β was determined using the CCK-8 assay according to the manufacturer’s instructions. NSC-34 cells were cultured in 96-well plates and subjected to various concentrations of IL-1β (0, 10, 20, 50, and 100 ng/ml; TargetMol, USA) for a duration of 24 h. Subsequently, 10 µl of CCK-8 was added to each well, and the plates were incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader, following the manufacturer’s protocol. To inhibit IL-1β, IL-1Ra (50 ng/ml; TargetMol, USA) was administered to the NSC-34 cells during IL-1β treatment, and the cells were analyzed after 24 h.

Enzyme Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

The supernatant from RAW264.7 cell cultures was subjected to centrifugation and subsequently collected for enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) analysis. The pro-inflammatory cytokine interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) was quantified utilizing a mouse-specific ELISA kit (Mlbio, China). Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader, following the manufacturer’s protocol.

LV Infection and siRNA Transfection

The SIRT1 plasmid was inserted into a lentiviral (LV) vector and subsequently overexpressed in NSC-34 cells, with an empty plasmid containing shRNA utilized as a negative control. Infection experiments were performed following the manufacturer’s protocol (LeapWalBio, China). At 48 h post-infection, the medium was replaced with fresh cell culture medium containing 2 µg/ml puromycin (YEASEN, China). After an additional 3 d, stably infected cells were selected for further experiments. For siRNA-mediated knockdown, NSC-34 cells were transfected with siRNA targeting P53 (sequence provided in Supplementary Table 1; Obiosh, China) using a transfection reagent. Western blot analysis was conducted 48 h post-transfection to assess the knockdown efficiency. Successfully transfected NSC-34 cells were then utilized for subsequent experiments.

Real-Time Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (Rt-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from zebrafish and cellular samples using TRIzol (Vazyme, China). Subsequently, reverse transcription was performed employing the First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Seq-hunt, China). Quantitative real-time PCR (Rt-qPCR) analysis was conducted utilizing the SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Seq-hunt, China). Supplementary Table 1 delineates the primers utilized for the zebrafish genes EF1a, Tnf-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, alongside the mouse genes Beta-actin, P53, E2F1, c-myc, HIC1, and FOXO3a. The average cycle threshold (Ct) values for each target gene were determined from three replicates and normalized to the average Ct of the housekeeping gene.

Acetylcholine (Ach) Content Detection

A sample comprising one hundred zebrafish from each experimental group was homogenized in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) under ice-cold conditions. The homogenate was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C to obtain the supernatant, which was subsequently analyzed for acetylcholine content. Cell culture supernatants (500 µl) were collected after drug treatment, centrifuged to remove cellular debris, and analyzed for acetylcholine content using the Njjcbio kit (China).

Western Blotting Analysis

A 30 µg sample of denatured cellular protein homogenate was analyzed by SDS-PAGE, with electrophoresis conducted at 100 V for 1.5 h. Subsequently, proteins were transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane using a constant current of 220 mA, with the transfer duration tailored according to the molecular weight of the proteins. The membrane was blocked using a 5% skimmed milk solution in TBST for 2 h at room temperature, and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The primary antibodies utilized included NLRP3 (mouse monoclonal antibody, Adipogen, Switzerland, Cat# AG-20B-0014, 1:2000), Caspase-1 (rabbit monoclonal antibodies, Abcam, UK, Cat# ab179515, 1:1000), IL-1β (rabbit monoclonal antibodies, Abcam, UK, Cat# ab234437, 1:1000), NFL (rabbit monoclonal antibodies, Abcam, UK, Cat# ab223343, 1:1000), NFM (rabbit monoclonal antibodies, Abcam, UK, Cat# ab254348, 1:1000), P62 (rabbit monoclonal antibodies, Abcam, UK, Cat# ab109012, 1:10000), LC3 (rabbit monoclonal antibodies, Abcam, UK, Cat# ab192890, 1:1000), KIF5A (rabbit polyclonal antibodies, Abcam, UK, Cat# ab5628, 1:1000), Syn (rabbit monoclonal antibodies, Abcam, UK, Cat# ab32127, 1:1000), Beta-actin (mouse monoclonal antibody, Proteintech, China, Cat# 66009-1, 1:5000), NFH (rabbit monoclonal antibodies, HUABIO, China, Cat# ET1702-72, 1:1000), P53 (mouse monoclonal antibodies, HUABIO, China, Cat# EM1701-91, 1:1000), and SIRT1 (rabbit monoclonal antibody, CST, USA, Cat# 9475, 1:1000). Subsequently, the samples were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (goat anti-mouse or anti-rabbit, Proteintech, China, Cat# RGAR001 or RGAM001, 1:5000) for 1 h at room temperature. Additionally, immunoblotting signals were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate (Biosharp, China). To ensure normalization against a loading control from the identical membrane, membranes were subsequently stripped using stripping buffer (Beyotime, China) and reprobed with the anti-β-actin antibody. The band intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence

Polylysine-coated cell slides were placed into 6-well plates, followed by the seeding of NSC-34 cells and subsequent Transwell co-culture, as previously described. Following co-culture, the slides were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 20 min, washed with PBST, and permeabilized with 0.05% Triton X-100 for an additional 20 min. The slides were then blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h. Primary antibodies, including SIRT1 (mouse monoclonal antibody, HUABIO, China, Cat# M1506-3, 1:200) and LC3 (rabbit monoclonal antibody, Abcam, UK, Cat# ab192890, 1:500), were applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The following day, the slides were washed thrice with PBST and incubated with an appropriate Alexa-Fluor conjugated secondary antibody (Proteintech, China, Cat# RGAM002 or RGAR004, 1:1000) for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, the slides were mounted with an anti-fade mounting medium containing DAPI, and images were captured using an Olympus fluorescence microscope.

Flow Cytometry

Cell death was evaluated by PI staining and flow cytometry. Larvae (5 dpf) exposed to 10 µM CuSO₄ for 0–72 h were dissociated into single-cell suspensions, filtered (40 μm), and stained with PI (1 µg/mL, 15 min). Analysis was performed on a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer (≥ 10,000 events/sample). PI-positive cells were quantified using FlowJo software.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were hereby conducted utilizing GraphPad Prism version 9.5 (GraphPad Software, USA), with the results expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\stackrel{-}{x}$$\end{document}±s). A t-test was employed to assess differences between two groups, whereas one-way ANOVA was utilized for comparisons among multiple groups. When comparing multiple experimental groups to a single control group, Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test was applied to control family-wise error rate. When comparing all groups in pairwise fashion, Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used. A p-value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Establishment of Zebrafish Systemic Inflammation Model

In order to establish a zebrafish systemic inflammation model, 5 dpf zebrafish were cultured in E3 medium containing varying concentrations of CuSO₄ (0, 5, 10, 20, and 40 µM) for 3 d. Subsequently, mortality, ROS production, morphological changes, and expression of inflammatory factors were detected. The findings indicated a dose-dependent increase in zebrafish mortality and ROS production with increasing CuSO₄ concentrations (Fig. 1a-b). CuSO₄ exposure for 3 d (5–8 dpf) induced morphological alterations in zebrafish, such as pericardial and yolk sac edema, spinal curvature, yolk sac rupture, and sometimes resulted in mortality (Fig. 1c). The mRNA expression levels of inflammatory factors IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 were also analyzed. The study demonstrated a dose-dependent upregulation of mRNA expression for three inflammatory factors following CuSO₄ treatment, with IL-1β exhibiting the most pronounced change (p < 0.05, Fig. 1d-f).

CuSO₄ Exposure Decreases Motor Function in Zebrafish

Zebrafish motor function was evaluated following exposure of 5 dpf larvae to CuSO₄ (0, 5, 10, and 20 µM) for 3 d. Analysis of motion trajectories and heat maps indicated a progressive decline in zebrafish locomotion as CuSO₄ concentration increased (Fig. 2a-b). Software analysis and data visualization revealed a significant dose-dependent decrease in zebrafish movement speed and total distance traveled at 50 min, with increasing CuSO₄ concentration in both light and dark environments (p < 0.05, Fig. 2c-e).

CuSO₄ Exposure Increases Macrophage Infiltration at Motor Axons of Zebrafish and Induces Morphological Changes of Motor Axons

The 10 µM concentration was selected for subsequent studies because it induced significant inflammatory responses and motor dysfunction without causing significant cell death (Supplementary Fig. 1a-b), with approximately 60% of larvae maintaining normal morphology (Supplementary Fig. 1c); all subsequent experiments exclusively used morphologically normal larvae. Laser confocal in vivo imaging was employed to investigate the interaction between macrophages and motor axons during CuSO₄ exposure, with fluorescence changes observed in Tg(mnx1:eGFP; mpeg1:mCherry). A significant increase in macrophage infiltration around the zebrafish motor axon was identified following modeling, accompanied by morphological damage to the motor axon (Supplementary Video 1, Fig. 3a). As demonstrated by quantitative analysis, compared to the control group, the modeled group exhibited a significant reduction in the length, diameter, and number of motor axons, along with an over twofold increase in macrophage infiltration around motor axons, with a predominance of the amoeboid morphology (p < 0.05, Fig. 3b-e). Additionally, the three-dimensional reconstruction analysis of Z-axis scans revealed a decreased shortest distance between macrophages and motor axons post-modeling (p < 0.05, Fig. 3f-g).

Partial Macrophage Depletion and IL-1Ra Ameliorate Motor Axon Morphological Damage During CuSO₄ Exposure

To investigate whether macrophage infiltration into motor axons during CuSO₄ exposure induces morphological damage, 2 nl of intervention agents was microinjected into the yolk sac of 3 dpf zebrafish to partially deplete macrophages. After 2 d, the zebrafish were exposed to CuSO₄, and index detection was then performed (Fig. 4a). Given the prominent elevation of IL-1β in previous experiments and its documented origin from macrophages [31], 2 nl (50 ng/ml) of IL-1Ra was injected into the yolk sac of 3 dpf zebrafish for intervention experiments. Two days post Lipo-clodronate microinjection, a reduction in the overall macrophage count was observed. Statistical analysis revealed a decrease of approximately 60% in the macrophage population within the designated area (p < 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 2a-b). Initial assessment of motor axon morphology following macrophage depletion without inflammatory challenge revealed no significant alterations (p > 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 2c-e). Subsequently, preconditioning was performed before CuSO₄ exposure. The experimental findings indicated that pre-treatment with Lipo-clodronate or IL-1Ra prior to modeling effectively reversed the reduction in the length, diameter, and number of motor axons associated with CuSO₄ exposure. Additionally, this intervention partially restored the levels of the critical neurotransmitter Ach secreted by axon terminals (p < 0.05, Fig. 4b-f). However, macrophage phenotyping revealed that macrophages in the model retained a predominantly amoeboid morphology following Lipo-clodronate and IL-1Ra pretreatment (Fig. 4g).

Partial Clearance of Macrophages and IL-1Ra Improve Motor Dysfunction Caused by CuSO₄ Exposure

Following the observed improvements in motor axon morphology with Lipo-clodronate and IL-1Ra treatment, motor function was assessed in various groups of zebrafish. Initial evaluations indicated no significant alterations in motor function following partial macrophage clearance in the absence of CuSO₄ exposure modeling (p > 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 3a-b). Subsequently, pretreatment protocols were implemented before CuSO₄ exposure. Analysis of trajectory and heat maps revealed that Lipo-clodronate pretreatment mitigated the deterioration of motor function attributable to CuSO₄ exposure. Extended evaluation of movement speed over a 50-minute duration, including statistical analyses of average movement speed and total movement distance, demonstrated that motor function-related metrics were partially restored with incomplete macrophage clearance (p < 0.05, Fig. 5a-e). Additionally, experimental findings with IL-1Ra indicated that IL-1Ra pretreatment similarly ameliorated the decline in motor function associated with CuSO₄ exposure (p < 0.05, Fig. 5f-g).

Antioxidant NAC Treatment Fails to Ameliorate Motor Axon Damage and Motor Dysfunction Induced by CuSO₄ Exposure

Given that CuSO₄ exposure induces both inflammatory responses and oxidative stress, the contribution of oxidative stress to motor axon damage and macrophage infiltration was investigated. To test this hypothesis, zebrafish larvae at 5 dpf were co-treated with CuSO₄ (10 µM) and the antioxidant N-acetylcysteine (NAC, 100 µM) for 3 d. NAC effectively reduced ROS generation induced by CuSO₄ exposure (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Confocal imaging of motor axons and macrophages revealed that NAC treatment did not significantly ameliorate motor axon morphological damage (Supplementary Fig. 4b). Quantitative analysis demonstrated that despite successful ROS reduction, motor axon length, diameter, and number showed no significant improvement in the NAC+CuSO₄ group compared to the CuSO₄-only group (p > 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 4c-e). Furthermore, macrophage infiltration around motor axons remained elevated following NAC treatment, with the amoeboid morphology macrophage phenotype distribution showing no statistically significant changes (p > 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 4f).

To further evaluate whether oxidative stress contributes to motor dysfunction, locomotor activity in NAC-treated zebrafish was assessed. Analysis of motion trajectories and heat maps revealed that NAC co-treatment did not restore the motor impairment caused by CuSO₄ exposure (Supplementary Fig. 5a-b). Quantitative analysis of total distance traveled and average swimming speed demonstrated that CuSO₄ exposure significantly reduced locomotor activity compared to the control group, while NAC supplementation failed to produce significant improvement in either parameter (p > 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 5c-d).

LPS Treatment Activates the NLRP3 Inflammasome and Induces IL-1β Release in RAW264.7 Macrophage-Like Cells

The molecular mechanism of macrophage-induced motor axon damage under systemic inflammation was investigated using an in vitro model. A Transwell co-culture system was established, positioning RAW264.7 macrophage-like cells in the upper chamber and NSC-34 cells in the lower chamber. Prior to co-culture, RAW264.7 macrophage-like cells were pre-treated with LPS for 6 h to mimic an inflammatory environment (Fig. 6a). Subsequently, the compartments were combined to examine the underlying mechanisms. Initial experiments using conditioned medium established optimal LPS concentration, with NSC-34 cell viability decreasing approximately 40% at 1 µg/mL LPS (p < 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 6). This concentration was selected for subsequent co-culture experiments. Based on the aforementioned animal experimental results, it was hypothesized that macrophages might induce morphological damage to motor axons through the release of IL-1β during systemic inflammation. NLRP3 inflammasome activation has been reported as a predominant signaling pathway responsible for macrophage-mediated IL-1β secretion [31]. The present observations revealed NLRP3 inflammasome activation in RAW264.7 macrophage-like cells following a 6 h exposure to LPS. These findings suggested that LPS stimulation significantly enhanced the expression of NLRP3 inflammasome-associated proteins in RAW264.7 macrophage-like cells and increased IL-1β levels in the culture medium (p < 0.05, Fig. 6b-f).

SIRT1 Activator SRT1720 Ameliorates CuSO₄-Induced Motor Axon Injury and Motor Dysfunction

To further investigate whether SIRT1 activation could ameliorate motor axon injury in vivo, zebrafish embryos were pretreated with the SIRT1-specific activator SRT1720 (5 µM) for 12 h before CuSO₄ exposure. Confocal imaging revealed that SRT1720 pretreatment improved motor axon morphological damage (Supplementary Fig. 10a). Quantitative analysis demonstrated that compared with the CuSO₄-only group, the SRT1720 + CuSO₄ group showed restoration of motor axon length, diameter and number (p < 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 10b-d). Motor function analysis further validated the protective effects of SIRT1 activation. Movement trajectory plots and heatmaps showed that SRT1720 pretreatment improved the decline in locomotor activity induced by CuSO₄ (Supplementary Fig. 10e-f). Quantitative data indicated that the total distance traveled and average swimming speed in the SRT1720 + CuSO₄ group were higher than those in the CuSO₄-only group (p < 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 10g-h).

Chloroquine and Rapamycin Treatments Identify Defective Autophagy Initiation in NSC-34 Cells

To distinguish impaired autophagic flux from defective autophagy initiation, NSC-34 cells were treated with the lysosomal inhibitor chloroquine (CQ, 20 µM, 6 h) or pretreated with the mTOR inhibitor rapamycin (Rapa, 5 µM, 12 h) prior to Transwell co-culture with LPS-treated RAW264.7 macrophage-like cells. CQ treatment significantly elevated P62 levels and increased the lipidated form of LC3 in both control and LPS co-cultured NSC-34 cells, while further reducing NFL expression (p < 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 11a-d), confirming that downstream lysosomal degradation remained intact and that autophagic flux was preserved. Conversely, rapamycin pretreatment significantly increased the lipidated form of LC3, reduced P62 accumulation, and partially restored the expression of NFL, KIF5A, and Syn under inflammatory conditions (p < 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 11e-j). These results demonstrate that autophagy dysfunction in inflammation-exposed NSC-34 cells stems from impaired initiation rather than defective flux, and that restoring autophagy induction is sufficient to attenuate neuronal protein loss.

Recent research has identified key transcription factors that modulate SIRT1 transcription within the inflammatory settings [34]. Notably, E2F1, FOXO3a, and c-myc have been shown to upregulate SIRT1 transcription, whereas P53 and HIC1 serve as negative regulators. To assess the effects of IL-1β on SIRT1 expression in NSC-34 cells, the mRNA levels of pertinent transcription factors were hereby evaluated. Among these, P53 demonstrated the most pronounced alteration, exhibiting an over 8-fold increase in expression (p < 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 12a-e). The results suggested that P53 knockdown could ameliorate the disruption of the SIRT1-autophagy axis and the reduction in axon-related protein expression in NSC-34 cells (p < 0.05, Fig. 11e-l).

Discussion

In the later stages of neurodegenerative diseases, patients often experience motor dysfunction, which is accelerated by two critical factors: motor axon degeneration and systemic inflammation [35]. While previous studies have observed axonal damage in the brains of patients with systemic inflammatory conditions such as sepsis, the specific effects of systemic inflammation on motor axon morphology and the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood [19]. In this study, a zebrafish model of systemic inflammation was established using CuSO₄ exposure to thoroughly investigate whether systemic inflammation could induce morphological alterations in motor axons, subsequently leading to motor dysfunction. Furthermore, the potential molecular mechanisms involved were explored, thereby providing a preliminary perspective for understanding motor dysfunction in neurodegenerative diseases.

Systemic inflammation models in zebrafish are predominantly established through lipopolysaccharide (LPS) injection or CuSO₄ exposure [36]. To avoid the potential confounding effects of invasive procedures on macrophage infiltration patterns, we utilized CuSO₄ exposure. Although CuSO₄ exposure induces both inflammatory responses and oxidative stress via ROS production, rescue experiments using the antioxidant N-acetylcysteine (NAC) showed that while NAC effectively reduced ROS, it did not significantly improve motor axon morphological damage, macrophage infiltration, or motor dysfunction. Beyond inflammatory responses and oxidative stress, copper ions may also induce general cytotoxicity through metal ion displacement and disruption of cellular homeostasis. However, flow cytometric analysis revealed no significant cell death at 10 µM CuSO₄, and the significant axonal rescue by macrophage depletion and IL-1Ra indicates that inflammatory signaling, rather than direct copper cytotoxicity, is the principal pathogenic driver of motor axon degeneration in this model. These results highlight the specificity of inflammatory pathways in mediating motor axon morphological damage, but the potential synergistic effects between oxidative stress and inflammation, as well as validation in additional inflammatory models, warrant further investigation [37, 38].

The association between macrophages and neuronal injury has consistently been a key focus of research. Recent studies have demonstrated that PM2.5 exposure increases microglial infiltration in the brain of experimental animals, where aberrantly activated microglia create a chronic neuroinflammatory environment, exacerbating neuronal damage and synaptic loss [39]. In this study, a systemic inflammation model was successfully established by treating transgenic zebrafish larvae with CuSO₄. In vivo confocal imaging revealed significant mpeg1⁺ cell infiltration and morphological damage at motor axon sites. These findings aligned with the previous observations in the sciatic nerves of septic mouse models conducted by the present research group, further confirming the detrimental effects of systemic inflammation on motor axons. Notably, under systemic inflammatory conditions, macrophages exhibited marked infiltration around motor axons, suggesting their crucial role in motor axon injury. Partial macrophage depletion experiments further suggested that macrophage infiltration is a key factor driving morphological damage to motor axons. Together, these findings suggest that macrophage infiltration contributes to motor axon morphological damage and, consequently, to the locomotor deficits observed in this model; the extent to which axonal pathology alone drives the behavioral phenotype, however, remains to be directly verified.

Recent zebrafish studies have illuminated the complex, dual roles of macrophages in neuronal injury and regeneration. Transgenic zebrafish models reveal that macrophages rapidly infiltrate degenerating motor neurons and dynamically phagocytose neuronal debris [40, 41]; however, their functional impact on neuronal outcomes depends critically on their activation states. Aberrantly activated macrophages, particularly those adopting an amoeboid pro-inflammatory phenotype (analogous to the M1-like state), can exacerbate neuronal damage, as observed in motor axon injury. Conversely, macrophages can promote neural regeneration, as evidenced by their secretion of Vegf-A facilitating neuromuscular junction regeneration in peripheral nerve injury models [42]. This paradoxical behavior may be attributed to macrophage heterogeneity and temporal dynamics. During the early stages of neuronal injury, pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages predominantly accumulate at the injury site, amplifying the inflammatory cascade by secreting pro-inflammatory mediators and consequently aggravating neuronal damage [43]. As the disease progresses, macrophages may transition to an M2 phenotype, secreting anti-inflammatory factors promoting tissue repair and neural regeneration [44]. In this study, macrophages infiltrating motor axon sites were found to predominantly exhibit M1 characteristics, providing new insights into macrophage function during systemic inflammation. Contemporary understanding of macrophages has evolved beyond the traditional M1/M2 dichotomy, with numerous macrophage subpopulations identified based on their spatial distribution and distinctive gene expression profiles [45]. Future studies should further investigate the specific roles and mechanisms of these diverse macrophage subpopulations in neuronal injury.

It is important to note that mpeg1⁺ cells in zebrafish larvae include both peripheral macrophages and central nervous system (CNS)-resident microglia [46]. Microglia colonize the spinal cord by 3 dpf and establish intimate contacts with motor neuron cell bodies and proximal axons through dynamic surveillance processes [41, 47]. Under systemic inflammatory conditions, these CNS-resident cells may autonomously initiate spinal inflammatory responses, potentially contributing to central axon pathology independently of peripheral macrophage infiltration [19, 28]. While our imaging included both regions, our analysis focused on mpeg1⁺ cells within the peripheral myotomes that appeared to spatially correlate with distal motor axon changes; however, given that the mpeg1 promoter labels both peripheral macrophages and microglia, the concurrent involvement of CNS-resident microglia interacting with neuronal cell bodies or proximal axonal segments cannot be excluded, and the in vivo cell-type attribution therefore remains incomplete. Furthermore, in vitro data using RAW264.7 cells, a peripheral macrophage-derived line, suggest that macrophage-sourced factors such as IL-1β may potentially contribute to axonal protein alterations; this finding, however, does not preclude a parallel contribution from microglia-derived signals in vivo, and the present study cannot definitively attribute the observed effects to peripheral macrophages alone. Disentangling the relative spatial and temporal contributions of these distinct mpeg1⁺ populations—for example, through microglia-specific promoters such as P2ry12 or cx3cr1, or through peripheral macrophage-specific depletion strategies that spare the microglial compartment—remains an important goal for future mechanistic studies.

IL-1β, as a crucial pro-inflammatory mediator, plays a pivotal role in neurodegenerative diseases. Studies have demonstrated that systemic inflammation can trigger aberrant activation of innate immune responses in the central nervous system, causing activated microglia to release increased levels of IL-1β [48–51]. This process can compromise the morphology and function of central axons and synapses. In this study, significant upregulation of IL-1β expression was observed in zebrafish under systemic inflammatory conditions, and administration of IL-1Ra attenuated both motor axon damage and motor dysfunction induced by systemic inflammation. These findings support the role of IL-1β in motor axon injury and motor dysfunction, while also identifying a potential therapeutic target. Nevertheless, whether axonal pathology alone, rather than concurrent NMJ or muscle changes, accounts for the behavioral phenotype remains to be directly verified. NLRP3, a sophisticated inflammasome primarily present in macrophages, becomes aberrantly activated in inflammatory environments, leading to the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-18, a mechanism considered crucial in accelerating neuromuscular disease progression [52, 53]. In this study, pharmacological inhibition of NLRP3 with MCC950 in the Transwell co-culture system suggested that NLRP3 inflammasome activation in RAW264.7 macrophage-like cells contributes to IL-1β release and subsequent alterations in motor axonal protein expression, though genetic validation remains needed to exclude potential off-target effects. This was consistent with our observations in the zebrafish model. Notably, the present zebrafish experiments revealed differential protective effects between partial macrophage depletion and IL-1Ra pretreatment on motor axon morphology and motor function. The partial macrophage depletion group exhibited slightly superior protective effects compared to the IL-1Ra pretreatment group, suggesting that inflammatory mediators beyond IL-1β might contribute to motor axon injury.

Macrophages, a principal source of inflammatory mediators, secrete multiple inflammatory factors including IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-18 to mediate immune responses [54]. In periventricular white matter injury models, TNF-α produced by activated microglia interacts with TNFR1 on oligodendrocytes, inducing oligodendrocyte apoptosis and ultimately leading to neurological dysfunction [55]. Within the central nervous system, elevated levels of IL-6 can activate microglia and astrocytes, promoting the release of additional pro-inflammatory cytokines and neurotoxic molecules, thereby exacerbating neuroinflammation in neurodegenerative diseases [56]. In this study, elevated expression levels of both TNF-α and IL-6 in zebrafish were observed under systemic inflammatory conditions. Moreover, in the cellular model, NSC-34 cells in the Transwell co-culture system exhibited a more pronounced decrease in axon-associated protein expression compared to the IL-1β treatment group, further confirming the potential role of other inflammatory mediators beyond IL-1β in morphological damage to motor axons.

SIRT1 acts as a crucial deacetylase, playing a vital role in neuroprotection by promoting autophagy activation through deacetylation of autophagy-related genes. The axon-protective effects of the SIRT1-autophagy axis have been validated in various disease models. SIRT1 may delay disease progression in AD patients by promoting mitochondrial autophagy in the central nervous system [23]. SRT2104 is known as a specific SIRT1 agonist, which enhances autophagy in dopaminergic neurons of PD model mice, thereby attenuating axonal degeneration [57]. Recent studies on neuroprotective agents have also confirmed the SIRT1-autophagy axis as a crucial target for their therapeutic effects [58, 59]. In this study, both Transwell co-culture and IL-1β treatment were observed to impair the SIRT1-autophagy axis in NSC-34 cells, which could be ameliorated by MCC950 and IL-1Ra. Rapamycin pretreatment and chloroquine flux assay experiments further demonstrated that autophagy dysfunction in inflammation-exposed NSC-34 cells stemmed from impaired initiation rather than defective flux, and that restoring autophagy induction could partially protect axon-associated proteins. In vivo, SRT1720 pretreatment also ameliorated motor axon injury in CuSO₄-exposed zebrafish. This suggested that IL-1β secreted by macrophages under systemic inflammatory conditions may be linked to motor axon morphological damage, potentially through impairment of the SIRT1-autophagy axis.

To further explore potential upstream regulators of SIRT1 in this context, the expression levels of five key transcription factors potentially influencing SIRT1 transcription in inflammatory environments were examined. Results showed increased expression of P53 and HIC1, which negatively regulated SIRT1 transcription, while the positive regulators E2F1 and FOXO3a exhibited varying degrees of reduction. These findings provide preliminary evidence suggesting a potential role of P53 in this context; however, functional validation of these transcription factors warrants further investigation. Beyond transcription factors, SIRT1 expression is also influenced by DNA methylation and post-transcriptional regulation. Studies have shown that decreased SIRT1 levels in gestational diabetes rat embryos affect embryonic development. Besides, treatment with DNA methylation inhibitors can increase embryonic SIRT1 protein expression, thereby alleviating developmental abnormalities [60]. Regarding post-transcriptional regulation, SIRT1-AS-lncRNA directly binds to SIRT1’s 3’UTR, promoting post-transcriptional translation. The Human antigen R (HuR) RNA-binding protein regulates SIRT1 mRNA stability and translation through its three RNA recognition motifs [61]. Therefore, further investigation should be conducted to determine whether macrophages influence motor neuron SIRT1 expression through additional pathways under systemic inflammatory conditions.

P53 serves as a potential therapeutic target in neurodegenerative diseases, which maintains a critical expression balance in neurons essential for neural stem cell self-renewal and differentiation [62]. Studies have demonstrated that upregulated P53 expression promotes neuronal death in neurodegenerative diseases including AD, while intervention in P53-induced apoptotic signaling pathways can restore neuronal vitality and cognitive function in AD patients [63]. Increased P53 expression has also been observed in affected tissues of PD patients, closely correlating with dopaminergic neuron degeneration and death in the substantia nigra [64]. During the investigation of potential associations between macrophage-secreted IL-1β and SIRT1 expression in motor neurons, significantly elevated P53 expression was observed in NSC-34 cells following IL-1β treatment. Under physiological conditions, SIRT1 maintained P53 expression balance by deacetylating P53 at lysine 382, thereby modulating its transcriptional activity. However, systemic inflammation appears to be associated with disruption of this balance. Increased P53 expression coincided with reduced SIRT1 levels in inflamed motor neurons, suggesting a correlative relationship between these two molecules, though the precise transcriptional mechanism underlying this association remains to be clarified.

Previous studies have also reported relationships between IL-1β and P53. Activated microglia-derived IL-1β can induce cell cycle arrest and apoptosis by upregulating P53 expression in neural progenitor cells [65]. The binding of IL-1β to IL-1R1 on the cell membrane activates multiple neuronal injury-related signaling pathways, including JNK, NF-κB, and GSK3β [66–68]. While direct evidence linking these signaling pathways to P53 expression regulation remains elusive, studies have shown that NF-κB can promote P53-mediated apoptosis, while both JNK and GSK3β signaling pathways can modulate P53 activity [69–72]. In the present study, the co-occurrence of IL-1β elevation and P53 upregulation in motor neurons, based on expression changes and pharmacological intervention, provides indirect support for a potential role of P53 in this context. Targeting P53-induced injury-related signaling pathways may restore neuronal vitality and delay disease progression.

Our combined use of zebrafish and murine cell lines provided complementary advantages: real-time visualization of cellular interactions in vivo and detailed molecular dissection in vitro. Although prior literature has reported conservation of core inflammatory and autophagic pathways across vertebrates [73, 74], caution is warranted when extrapolating the proposed SIRT1-autophagy-P53 working model from murine cell lines to zebrafish motor neurons and human disease, and validation in mammalian models and chronic inflammatory environments remains necessary. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, observations were limited to a single time point; dynamic analyses across multiple stages would better characterize disease progression. Second, while P53 knockdown restored SIRT1 levels, direct evidence of P53 binding to the SIRT1 promoter requires validation via ChIP or reporter assays. Third, the acute inflammation model and reliance on pharmacological tools in vivo may not fully replicate the chronic, multifactorial pathology of human neurodegenerative diseases; future studies incorporating genetic manipulation approaches, chronic models, and human biospecimens are warranted to further validate causal inference and translational relevance. Finally, as NMJ integrity and skeletal muscle histology were not directly assessed, the locomotor deficits observed here are interpreted as associated with, rather than exclusively caused by, motor axon morphological damage; future studies incorporating these assessments would help delineate the relative contributions of axonal, synaptic, and muscular compartments.

Conclusions

In this study, a zebrafish model of systemic inflammation was constructed using CuSO₄ exposure to investigate motor axon morphological damage and its underlying molecular mechanisms during systemic inflammation. As indicated by the results, under systemic inflammatory conditions, macrophages significantly infiltrate the periphery of motor axons and transform into an amoeboid morphology. Macrophages secrete inflammatory mediators including IL-1β; elevated IL-1β paralleled increased neuronal P53 expression, reduced SIRT1 levels, and impaired autophagy, collectively accompanying motor axon morphological damage and motor dysfunction. Based on the current associative and pharmacological evidence, we propose a working model in which macrophage-derived IL-1β, P53 upregulation, and SIRT1-autophagy impairment are functionally linked to motor axon degeneration. Future research should elucidate the roles and mechanisms of different macrophage subpopulations in motor axon injury, clarify the contributions of broader cytokine networks to neuronal dysfunction, and identify novel neuroprotective agents targeting the SIRT1-autophagy axis and P53 signaling pathway (Fig. 12).
