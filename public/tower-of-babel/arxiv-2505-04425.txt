𝕄 ∗ \M^{*} , ℕ ∗ \N^{*} , and ℍ ∗ \HH^{*} Thanks: The first author is supported in part by NSF grant DMS-2154229.
Will Brian, Alan Dow, Klaas Pieter Hart

Abstract
. Let 𝕄 = ℕ × [ 0 , 1 ] \M=\mathbb{N}\times[0,1] . The natural projection π : 𝕄 → ℕ \pi:\M\rightarrow\mathbb{N} , which sends ( n , x ) (n,x) to n n , induces a projection mapping π ∗ : 𝕄 ∗ → ℕ ∗ \pi^{*}:\M^{*}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}^{*} , where 𝕄 ∗ \M^{*} and ℕ ∗ \mathbb{N}^{*} denote the Čech-Stone remainders of 𝕄 \M and ℕ \mathbb{N} , respectively. We show that 𝖢𝖧 \mathsf{CH} implies every autohomeomorphism of ℕ ∗ \mathbb{N}^{*} lifts through the natural projection to an autohomeomorphism of 𝕄 ∗ \M^{*} . That is, for every homeomorphism h : ℕ ∗ → ℕ ∗ h:\mathbb{N}^{*}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}^{*} there is a homeomorphism H : 𝕄 ∗ → 𝕄 ∗ H:\M^{*}\rightarrow\M^{*} such that π ∗ ∘ H = h ∘ π ∗ \pi^{*}\circ H=h\circ\pi^{*} . This complements a recent result of the second author, who showed that this lifting property is not a consequence of 𝖹𝖥𝖢 \mathsf{ZFC} . Combining this lifting theorem with a recent result of the first author, we also prove that 𝖢𝖧 \mathsf{CH} implies there is an order-reversing autohomeomorphism of ℍ ∗ \mathbb{H}^{*} , the Čech-Stone remainder of the half line ℍ = [ 0 , ∞ ) \mathbb{H}=[0,\infty) .

1.
Introduction

Let
\I
denote the unit interval
[0,1]
, and let
\M=\N\times\I
.
Let
\pi:\M\to\N
denote the natural projection
(n,x)\mapsto n
.
Moving to the Čech-Stone compactifications,
\pi
extends to a continuous surjection
\beta\pi:\beta\M\to\beta\N
.
Because
\pi
is surjective and
\pi^{\leftarrow}(n)
is compact for every
n
,
the map
\beta\pi
restricts to a surjection
from
\M^{*}=\beta\M\setminus\M
onto
\N^{*}=\beta\N\setminus\N
.
Let
\pi^{*}=\beta\pi\restr\M^{*}
.
In other words,
\pi^{*}
is the continuous surjection
\M^{*}\to\N^{*}

induced by
\pi
.

This paper is organized around two main theorems.
The first states that, assuming the Continuum Hypothesis (henceforth
\mathsf{CH}
),
every autohomeomorphism of
\N^{*}
can be “lifted” through
\pi^{*}
to an
autohomeomorphism of
\M^{*}
:

Theorem 1
.

Assuming
\mathsf{CH}
, if
h
is an autohomeomorphism
of
\N^{*}
, then there is an autohomeomorphism
H
of
\M^{*}

such that
\pi^{*}\circ H=h\circ\pi^{*}
.

This theorem complements a recent result of the second author in
[
4
]
,
where he shows that the conclusion of Theorem
1
fails
consistently.
Specifically, this lifting property fails in the model of Veličković
from
[
15
]
in which
\mathsf{MA}_{\aleph_{1}}
holds and
\N^{*}
has a
nontrivial autohomeomorphism.
Theorem
1
and the main theorem of
[
4
]
, taken together,
answer Question 2.4 in
[
3
]
.

It is not difficult to see that all trivial autohomeomorphisms of
\N^{*}
lift through
\pi^{*}
. (We give the easy argument in Section
2
.)
Thus the conclusion of Theorem
1
holds if all autohomeomorphisms
of
\N^{*}
are trivial, which is implied by forcing axioms like
\mathsf{PFA}
, or even just
\mathsf{OCA}_{T}
.
Thus this lifting property provides a rare example of a statement about Čech-Stone remainders that follows from forcing axioms and from
\mathsf{CH}
, but not from
\mathsf{ZFC}
.

Our second theorem concerns yet another Čech-Stone remainder. Let
\HH=[0,\infty)
denote the space of nonnegative real numbers, and let
\HH^{*}=\beta\HH\setminus\HH
.
The order on
\HH
induces a quasiorder on certain subsets of
\HH^{*}
.
This is explained further in Section
4
below (see also the survey of
\HH^{*}
by the third author,
[
7
]
).
An old folklore question about
\HH^{*}
is whether there is an autohomeomorphism of
\HH^{*}
that reverses this order. Our second theorem shows that, consistently, there is.

Theorem 2
.

\mathsf{CH}
implies there is an order-reversing autohomeomorphism of
\HH^{*}
.

On the other hand, a recent result of Vignati
(see
[
16
]
*Theorem C) states that
\mathsf{OCA}_{T}+\mathsf{MA}
implies all autohomeomorphisms of
\HH^{*}
are trivial. An easy argument (which is given in Section
4
below) shows that all trivial autohomeomorphisms of
\HH^{*}
are order-preserving. Thus the existence of an order-reversing autohomeomorphism of
\HH^{*}
is independent of
\mathsf{ZFC}
.

Theorem
2
could really be called a corollary. It follows relatively easily from two other theorems: Theorem
1
stated above, and a recent result of the first author in
[
1
]
, which states that, assuming
\mathsf{CH}
, the shift map and its inverse are conjugate in the autohomeomorphism group of
\N^{*}
.

The next two sections are devoted to
\M^{*}
and
\N^{*}
, and the proof of Theorem
1
. The fourth and final section of the paper contains some background material on
\HH^{*}
and a proof of Theorem
2
.

2.
More on
\M^{*}
and
\N^{*}

The aim of this section is to introduce some ideas and notation concerning
\M^{*}
and
\N^{*}
, and to prove two relatively easy positive results similar to Theorem
1
. These two results form part of the motivation for proving Theorem
1
.

An
almost permutation
of
\N
is a bijection from one co-finite subset of
\N
to another. A
trivial autohomeomorphism
of
\N^{*}
is a homeomorphism
h
induced by an almost permutation
f
of
\N
, in the sense that
h=\beta f\restr\N^{*}
, or equivalently, the action of
h
on the clopen subsets of
\N^{*}
is simply

h[A^{*}]=(f[A])^{*}
, for all
A\subseteq\N
.
Similarly, if
f
is a homeomorphism between two co-compact subsets of
\M
then
H_{f}=\beta f\restr\M^{*}
is an autohomeomorphism of
\M^{*}
, and any such autohomeomorphism of
\M^{*}
is called
trivial
.

Proposition 3
.

If
h
is a trivial autohomeomorphism
of
\N^{*}
, then there is a trivial autohomeomorphism
H
of
\M^{*}

such that
\pi^{*}\circ H=h\circ\pi^{*}
.

Proof.

Let
h
be a trivial autohomeomorphism of
\N^{*}
. Fix an almost permutation
f
of
\N
such that
h=\beta f\restr\N^{*}
. Define
g:\mathrm{dom}(f)\times\I\to\M
by setting

g(n,x)\,=\,(f(n),x).
Observe that
g
is a homeomorphism from one co-compact subset of
\M
to another, and therefore
H_{g}=\beta g\restr\M^{*}
is a trivial autohomeomorphism of
\M^{*}
.
Because
\pi\circ g=f\circ\pi
, we have
\beta\pi\circ\beta g=\beta f\circ\beta\pi
, and then restricting to the Čech-Stone remainders,
\pi^{*}\circ H_{g}=h\circ\pi^{*}
.
∎

The existence of a nontrivial autohomeomorphism of
\N^{*}
is independent of
\mathsf{ZFC}
. On the one hand, Walter Rudin proved in
[
11
]
that
\mathsf{CH}
implies there are
2^{\mathfrak{c}}
autohomeomorphisms in total, though of course only
\mathfrak{c}
of them can be trivial. On the other hand, Shelah proved in

[
13
]
*Chapter 4, via an oracle-c.c. iteration, that it is consistent to have all autohomeomorphisms trivial. Building on Shelah’s work, Shelah and Steprāns showed in
[
12
]
that
\mathsf{PFA}
implies all autohomeomorphisms are trivial. Veličković showed in
[
15
]
that
\mathsf{OCA}_{T}+\mathsf{MA}
suffices, though it is consistent with
\mathsf{MA}_{\aleph_{1}}
to have nontrivial autohomeomorphisms. (Here
\mathsf{OCA}_{T}
denotes Todorčević’s Open Coloring Axiom, defined in
[
14
]
, now sometimes called
\mathsf{OGA}
.) Building on work of Moore in
[
10
]
, DeBondt, Farah, and Vignati showed in
[
2
]
that
\mathsf{OCA}_{T}
alone implies all autohomeomorphisms of
\N^{*}
are trivial.
Combined with Proposition
3
, this shows Theorem
1
remains true when
\mathsf{CH}
is replaced with
\mathsf{OCA}_{T}
.

Corollary 4
.

Assuming
\mathsf{OCA}_{T}
, if
h
is an autohomeomorphism
of
\N^{*}
, then there is an autohomeomorphism
H
of
\M^{*}

such that
\pi^{*}\circ H=h\circ\pi^{*}
. ∎

In light of this, the main point of Theorem
1
is not simply that the conclusion is consistent, but specifically that it follows from
\mathsf{CH}
. This is good to know for two reasons: because it contributes to the longstanding program of understanding the behavior of Čech-Stone remainders under
\mathsf{CH}
, and because it enables us to prove Theorem
2
. Note that the conclusion of Theorem
2
is not implied by
\mathsf{OCA}_{T}
(see Proposition
12
below), and indeed, we do not currently know how to obtain an order-reversing autohomeomorphism of
\HH^{*}
except via
\mathsf{CH}
.

For each
n\in\N
, let
\I_{n}=\pi^{\leftarrow}(n)=\{n\}\times\I
. These are the connected components of
\M
.
Analogously, for each
u\in\N^{*}
let

\I_{u}=(\pi^{*})^{\leftarrow}(u)
.
Equivalently,

\I_{u}\,=\,\bigcap_{A\in u}(\pi^{*})^{\leftarrow}\bigl[\operatorname{cl}_{\beta\N}A\bigr]\,=\,\bigcap_{A\in u}\operatorname{cl}_{\beta\M}(\pi^{\leftarrow}[A]).

These are the connected components of
\M^{*}
(see
[
7
]
*Corollary 2.2).

In particular, if
H
is an
autohomeomorphism of
\M^{*}
, then
H
permutes the set
\{\I_{u}:u\in\N^{*}\}
of connected components of
\M^{*}
.
Let
\rho_{H}
denote the corresponding permutation of
\N^{*}
, so that
H(\I_{u})=\I_{\rho_{H}(u)}
for all
u\in\N^{*}
.

If
B^{*}
is clopen in
\N^{*}
, then
(\pi^{*})^{\leftarrow}[B^{*}]
is clopen in
\M^{*}
, by the continuity of
\pi^{*}
. But the converse is also true: if
C
is clopen in
\M^{*}
, then
C=(\pi^{*})^{\leftarrow}[B^{*}]
for some clopen
B^{*}\subseteq\N^{*}
. To see this, note that if
C
is clopen then
C
and
\M^{*}\setminus C
are both compact, so
\pi^{*}[C]
and
\pi^{*}[\M^{*}\setminus C]
are both compact as well. But these sets are disjoint, because
(\pi^{*})^{\leftarrow}(u)=\I_{u}
is connected for each
u\in\N^{*}
, which means either
\I_{u}\subseteq C
or
\I_{u}\subseteq\M^{*}\setminus C
.
Hence
\pi^{*}[C]
and
\pi^{*}[\M^{*}\setminus C]
are complementary closed sets, hence clopen, and
C=(\pi^{*})^{\leftarrow}\left[\pi^{*}[C]\right]
and
\M^{*}\setminus C=(\pi^{*})^{\leftarrow}\left[\pi^{*}[\M^{*}\setminus C]\right]
.

Proposition 5
.

If
H
is an autohomeomorphism of
\M^{*}
, then
\rho_{H}
is an autohomeomorphism of
\N^{*}

such that
\pi^{*}\circ H=\rho_{H}\circ\pi^{*}
.

Proof.

That
\pi^{*}\circ H=\rho_{H}\circ\pi^{*}
follows from the definition of
\rho_{H}
, so we need only show
\rho_{H}
is an autohomeomorphism of
\N^{*}
. Because
\rho_{H}
is bijective (again, by definition) and
\N^{*}
is compact, it suffices to show
\rho_{H}
is continuous.
Let
A\subseteq\N
, so that
A^{*}
is a basic clopen subset of
\N^{*}
. Then
(\pi^{*})^{\leftarrow}[A^{*}]
is a clopen subset of
\M^{*}
. Because
H
is a homeomorphism,
H^{\leftarrow}\bigl[(\pi^{*})^{\leftarrow}[A^{*}]\bigr]
is clopen as well. By the paragraph preceding this proposition, this means there is some clopen
B^{*}\subseteq\N^{*}
such that
H^{\leftarrow}\bigl[(\pi^{*})^{\leftarrow}[A^{*}]\bigr]=(\pi^{*})^{\leftarrow}[B^{*}]
. But

H^{\leftarrow}\bigl[(\pi^{*})^{\leftarrow}[A^{*}]\bigr]=(\pi^{*}\circ H)^{\leftarrow}[A^{*}]=(\rho_{H}\circ\pi^{*})^{\leftarrow}[A^{*}]=(\pi^{*})^{\leftarrow}\bigl[\rho_{H}^{\leftarrow}[A^{*}]\bigr],

so
(\pi^{*})^{\leftarrow}\bigl[\rho_{H}^{\leftarrow}[A^{*}]\bigr]=(\pi^{*})^{\leftarrow}[B^{*}]
, which implies
\rho_{H}^{\leftarrow}[A^{*}]=B^{*}
.
∎

In other words, this proposition states that autohomeomorphisms of
\M^{*}
project downward through
\pi^{*}
to autohomeomorphisms of
\N^{*}
.
That is, Theorem
1
remains true, without even needing to assume
\mathsf{CH}
, if we switch the roles of
\M^{*}
and
\N^{*}
.
The opposite direction, lifting upward through
\pi^{*}
rather than projecting downward, is more subtle, and this more difficult direction is the content of Theorem
1
.

3.
A proof of Theorem
1

Proof of Theorem
1
:

Let
h
be an autohomeomorphism of
\N^{*}
.
Using
\mathsf{CH}
, we aim to construct an autohomeomorphism
H
of
\M^{*}
such
that
\pi^{*}\circ H=h\circ\pi^{*}
.
Our construction of
H
needs a few ingredients.

The first is a map
h^{+}:\mathcal{P}(\N)\to\mathcal{P}(\N)
with the property that for all
subsets
A
of
\N
we have
h[A^{*}]=h^{+}(A)^{*}
.

The second is a suitable base for the closed sets of
\M^{*}
that is a
distributive lattice with respect to
\cup
and
\cap
.
We shall describe
H
dually by specifying an automorphism of that base.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a countable distributive lattice base for the closed sets
of
\I
, say the lattice generated by the family of closed intervals with
rational end points.
We identify members of
\mathcal{B}^{\N}
with closed subsets of
\M
in the obvious way:
if
B\in\mathcal{B}^{\N}
then

F_{B}=\bigcup\bigl\{\{k\}\times B(k):k\in\N\bigr\}

In this way the power
\mathcal{B}^{\N}
determines a base for the closed sets
of
\M
, and hence also for the closed sets of
\beta\M
, as the following
lemma implies.

For the ring-theoretic approach one could take the subring
R
of the
ring
C(\I)
generated by the constant functions and the functions
d_{B}

for
B\in\mathcal{B}
, where
d_{B}(x)=d(x,B)
.
The power
R^{\N}
represents a subring of
C(\M)
and the bounded elements
of it would be the analogue of
\mathcal{B}^{\N}
in what follows.

Lemma 6
.

If
F
and
G
are closed and disjoint subsets of
\M
then there
are members
B
and
C
of
\mathcal{B}^{\N}
such that

F\subseteq F_{B}
,
G\subseteq F_{C}
and
F_{B}\cap F_{C}=\emptyset
. ∎

Because for closed subsets
F
and
G
of
\M
we
have
\operatorname{cl}_{\beta\M}F\cap\M^{*}=\operatorname{cl}_{\beta\M}G\cap\M^{*}
if and only if

\{n:F\cap\I_{n}\neq G\cap\I_{n}\}
is finite, we see that the reduced power

\mathcal{B}^{\N}/\mathit{fin}
determines a base for the closed sets of
\M^{*}
:
the family
\{F_{B}^{*}:B\in\mathcal{B}^{\N}\}
.
We have
F_{B}^{*}\subseteq F_{C}^{*}
if and only if
\{k:B(k)\subseteq C(k)\}
is cofinite.
The latter condition also defines the partial order of
\mathcal{B}^{\N}/\mathit{fin}
.

It follows that if we let
B^{*}
denote the equivalence class of
B\in\mathcal{B}^{\N}

in
\mathcal{B}^{\N}/\mathit{fin}
we get the equivalence

B^{*}\le C^{*}\text{ \quad if and only if \quad}F_{B}^{*}\subseteq F_{C}^{*}

for
B,C\in\mathcal{B}^{\N}
.

The algebraic structure of the lattice
\mathcal{B}^{\N}/\mathit{fin}
is determined completely
by its partial order, so it will suffice to define an automorphism
of the partially ordered set
(\mathcal{B}^{\N}/\mathit{fin},\le)
.

We define this automorphism by defining a partial map

\varphi:\mathcal{B}^{\N}\to\mathcal{B}^{\N}
with the following properties. (The construction is detailed below.)

(1)

If
B\in\mathcal{B}^{\N}
then there are unique
C\in\operatorname{dom}\varphi

and
D\in\operatorname{ran}\varphi

such that
B=^{*}C
and
B=^{*}D
; this uniqueness ensures that
\varphi

determines a well-defined surjection from
\mathcal{B}^{\N}/\mathit{fin}
to itself.

(2)

If
B
and
C
are in
\operatorname{dom}\varphi
, and if

A_{1}=\{k:B(k)\subseteq C(k)\}
and

A_{2}=\{k:C(k)\subseteq B(k)\}
,
then
\{k:\varphi(B)(k)\subseteq\varphi(C)(k)\}=^{*}h^{+}(A_{1})

and
\{k:\varphi(C)(k)\subseteq\varphi(B)(k)\}=^{*}h^{+}(A_{2})
.

In condition (2) the set
A_{3}=\N\setminus(A_{1}\cup A_{2})
is the set of
k

where
B(k)
and
C(k)
are incomparable.
It follows that
h^{+}(A_{3})
is mod finite equal to the set of
k

where
\varphi(B)(k)
and
\varphi(C)(k)
are incomparable.

Lemma 7
.

The conditions above ensure that
\varphi
induces an automorphism
of the partially ordered set
(\mathcal{B}^{\N}/\mathit{fin},\le)
.

Proof.

Since
\operatorname{dom}\varphi
and
\operatorname{ran}\varphi
intersect every equivalence class in
exactly one point we automatically obtain a surjective map
\varphi^{*}

from
\mathcal{B}^{\N}/\mathit{fin}
to itself.

In order to see that
\varphi^{*}
is injective assume
\varphi^{*}(B^{*})=\varphi^{*}(C^{*})
.
This means that
\{k:\varphi(B)(k)=\varphi(C)(k)\}
is co-finite,
hence so are
\{k:\varphi(B)(k)\subseteq\varphi(C)(k)\}
and

\{k:\varphi(B)(k)\subseteq\varphi(C)(k)\}
.
But this means, with the notation as in condition (2) above,
that
h^{+}(A_{1})
and
h^{+}(A_{2})
are co-finite too.
Because
h^{+}
represents
h
the sets
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
must then be co-finite
as well, and we conclude that
\{k:B(k)=C(k)\}
is co-finite and hence
that
B=C
even.

Similarly, using that
A_{1}
is cofinite if and only if
h^{+}(A_{1})
is co-finite, we obtain
that
B^{*}\le C^{*}
iff
\varphi(B^{*})\le\varphi^{*}(C^{*})
,
and so
\varphi^{*}
is an automorphism.
∎

Given
A\subseteq\N
, define
\M^{*}_{A}=\left(\bigcup_{n\in A}\I_{n}\right)^{*}=\operatorname{cl}_{\beta\M}\left(\bigcup_{n\in A}\I_{n}\right)\setminus\M
.

Lemma 8
.

The autohomeomorphism
H
of
\M^{*}
determined by
\varphi^{*}

satisfies
h\circ\pi^{*}=\pi^{*}\circ H
.

Proof.

To show that
\pi^{*}\circ H=h\circ\pi^{*}
we let
A\subseteq\N
and show that

\pi^{*}\bigl[H[\M^{*}_{A}]\bigr]=h\bigl[\pi^{*}[\M^{*}_{A}]\bigr].

Define
B
and
I
in
\mathcal{B}^{\N}
by

B(k)=\begin{cases}\I&\text{ if }k\in A\\
\emptyset&\text{ if }k\notin A\end{cases}

and
I(k)=\I
for all
k
;
in our construction we shall have
I\in\operatorname{dom}\varphi
and
\varphi(I)=I
.

Then
A=\{k:I(k)\subseteq B(k)\}
and so

\{k:\I\subseteq\varphi(B)(k)\}=\{k:\varphi(I)(k)\subseteq\varphi(B)(k)\}=^{*}h^{+}(A).

Likewise
\N\setminus A=\{k:B(k)\subseteq\emptyset\}
and we obtain

\{k:\varphi(B)(k)=\emptyset\}=^{*}h^{+}(\N\setminus A)=^{*}\N\setminus h^{+}(A).

We deduce that
H[\M^{*}_{A}]=\M^{*}_{h^{+}(A)}
and so

\pi^{*}\bigl[H[\M^{*}_{A}]\bigr]=\pi^{*}[\M^{*}_{h^{+}(A)}]=h^{+}(A)^{*}=h[A^{*}]=h\bigl[\pi^{*}[\M^{*}_{A}]\bigr].\qed

The construction

Using
\mathsf{CH}
, fix an enumeration
\langle B_{\alpha}:\alpha\in\omega_{1}\rangle

of
\mathcal{B}^{\N}
.
In a recursion of length
\omega_{1}
we construct two sequences

\langle C_{\alpha}:\alpha\in\omega_{1}\rangle
and

\langle D_{\alpha}:\alpha\in\omega_{1}\rangle

of members of
\mathcal{B}^{\N}
.
These will be such that

\varphi=\bigl\{\langle{C_{\alpha}},{D_{\alpha}}\rangle:\alpha\in\omega_{1}\bigr\}

is the map that we seek.

To begin the construction we let

•

C_{0}=D_{0}=\langle\emptyset:n\in\N\rangle
, and

•

C_{1}=D_{1}=\langle\I:n\in\N\rangle
.

•

C_{2}=D_{2}=\langle[0,\frac{1}{2}]:n\in\N\rangle
.

The first two conditions take care of the maximum and minimum of
\mathcal{B}^{\N}/\mathit{fin}
and facilitate the proof of Lemma
8
.
The third condition ensures that our autohomeomorphism
H
will preserve the order on each of the
\I_{u}
. We have not yet said what this order is, and that is because one does not need to worry about it yet.
This third condition does not affect the rest of the proof in this section, but we include it because it will be useful in Section
4
when finding an order-reversing autohomeomorphism of
\HH^{*}
.

Next let
\alpha\ge 2
and assume we have

\varphi_{\alpha}=\bigl\{\langle{C_{\beta}},{D_{\beta}}\rangle:\beta\in\alpha\bigr\}
such that

\varphi_{\alpha}
satisfies conditions (1) and (2) above up to
\alpha
,
that is

(1)

If
\gamma<\beta<\alpha
then

\{k:C_{\gamma}(k)\neq C_{\beta}(k)\}
and
\{k:D_{\gamma}(k)\neq D_{\beta}(k)\}

are infinite; this ensures the uniqueness clause in (1).

(2)

If
\gamma<\beta<\alpha
and

A_{1}=\{k:C_{\gamma}(k)\subseteq C_{\beta}(k)\}
and

A_{2}=\{k:C_{\gamma}(k)\subseteq C_{\beta}(k)\}

then
\{k:D_{\gamma}(k)\subseteq D_{\beta}(k)\}=^{*}h^{+}(A_{1})

and
\{k:D_{\gamma}(k)\subseteq D_{\beta}(k)\}=^{*}h^{+}(A_{2})
.

We extend
\varphi_{\alpha}
to
\varphi_{\alpha+1}
, as follows.

•

If
\alpha
is even let
C_{\alpha}
be the first term of the
sequence
\langle B_{\alpha}:\alpha\in\omega_{1}\rangle
that satisfies

B_{\alpha}\neq^{*}C_{\beta}
for all
\beta<\alpha
.
We show how to determine
D_{\alpha}
so as to satisfy the conditions above
up to and including
\alpha
.

•

If
\alpha
is odd let
D_{\alpha}
be the first term of the
sequence
\langle B_{\alpha}:\alpha\in\omega_{1}\rangle
that satisfies

B_{\alpha}\neq^{*}D_{\beta}
for all
\beta<\alpha
.
We show how to determine
C_{\alpha}
so as to satisfy the conditions above
up to and including
\alpha
.

We shall only deal with the even case; the argument in the odd case is
the mirror image of that in the even case.

We stop before we start, however.
It turns out that the second assumption on the recursion is too weak.

To illustrate this assume that there are
\gamma
and
\beta
below
\alpha

such that the set
A
of
k

C_{\gamma}(k)\subseteq C_{\alpha}(k)\subseteq C_{\beta}(k)

To stay with our example we note that “

We strengthen condition (2) so that it covers all formulas of lattice theory

Remark 9
.

The proof above is based on Wallman’s representation theorem for
distributive lattices, see
[
17
]
.
That paper established a many-valued duality between certain distributive
lattices and compact spaces: to every distributive lattice there corresponds
a compact space, and to every compact space there may correspond various
lattices, e.g., the full family of closed sets, or any base for its closed
sets that forms a lattice.

A true duality for compact spaces is due to Gelfand and Kolomogorov
in
[
6
]
: here the ring of continuous functions is the
algebraic counterpart of the compact space.
One can give a version of our proof based on this duality, where the
ring
C(\M^{*})
of continuous functions on
\M^{*}
is represented
as the quotient of the ring
C^{*}(\M)
by the ideal of functions that
have limit zero at infinity.
One can give a version of our proof that constructs an automorphism
of
C^{(}\M^{*})
whose dual is an autohomeomorphism as desired.

An order-reversing autohomeomorphism of
\HH^{*}

We begin this section with a description of the standard quasiorder on the connected components
\I_{u}
of
\M^{*}
.

Given a sequence
\bar{x}=\langle x_{n}:\,n\in\N\rangle\in\I^{\N}
and

, define

\bar{x}_{u}=u\mathchar 45\relax\!\lim\nolimits_{n}(n,x_{n}),

where the limit is taken in
\beta\M
. Equivalently, this is the unique element of the set
\bigcap_{A\in u}\operatorname{cl}_{\beta\M}\{(n,x_{n}):\,n\in A\}
.
Let
C_{u}\subseteq\I_{u}
denote the set of all points of this form:

C_{u}=\{\bar{x}_{u}:\,\bar{x}\in\I^{\N}\}.

This is a proper subset of
\I_{u}
(see
[
7
]
).
Observe that
C_{u}
has a natural linear order:

\bar{x}_{u}\leq_{u}\bar{y}_{u}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\{n:\,x_{n}\leq y_{n}\}\in u.

The fact that this is a total order of
C_{u}
can be seen as an instance of Łoś’ Theorem, because

is really just the ultrapower
\I^{\N}/u
.
Observe that
C_{u}
has a least element
\bar{0}_{u}
and a greatest element
\bar{1}_{u}
, where
\bar{0}
denotes the constant sequence
\langle 0,0,0,\dots\rangle
and
\bar{1}

\langle 1,1,1,\dots\rangle
.

Proposition 10
.

The set
C_{u}\setminus\{\bar{0}_{u},\bar{1}_{u}\}
is dense in
\I_{u}
, and its subspace topology is the same as the order topology induced by
\leq_{u}
.
Furthermore,

(1)

is irreducible between
\bar{0}_{u}
and
\bar{1}_{u}
, and if

and the other

.

(2)

If

and

, then every subcontinuum of
\I_{u}
that contains

and
\bar{0}_{u}
also contains
x
, and inversely, every subcontinuum of
\I_{u}
that contains

and
\bar{1}_{u}
also contains

.

See
[
7
]
*Section 2 for a proof.
This proposition implies that

is a topologically definable subset of

. In particular, any autohomeomorphism

must map

Part

: for any

that contains

, if and only if every subcontinuum of
\I_{u}
that contains
x
and
\bar{1}_{u}
also contains

.

Let us note that
\leq_{u}
is not a partial order.
Let us write

to mean that
x\leq_{u}y
and

.
For each

, the set

\I_{u}

If

.
For points

There are always

The quotient of

An autohomeomorphism

\text{if $x,y\in\I_{u}$ and $x\leq_{u}y$, then $H(x)\leq_{\rho_{H}(u)}H(y)$},

\text{if $x,y\in\I_{u}$ and $x\leq_{u}y$, then $H(y)\leq_{\rho_{H}(u)}H(x)$}.

Equivalently,

H(\bar{0}_{u})=\bar{0}_{\rho_{H}(u)}

and

.

Observe that an autohomeomorphism

\M^{*}
induced by

is co-finite, and it is neither order-preserving nor order-reversing if

is the ultrafilter generated by
\{A+1:\,A\in u\}
. Next, like with
\M
and
\HH
, our quotient mapping
\M^{*}\to\HH^{*}
is defined by gluing the rightmost point of each connected component
\I_{u}
to the leftmost point of the “next” component,
\I_{\sigma(u)}
. More precisely, define an equivalence relation
\sim
on
\M^{*}
by setting
\bar{1}_{u}\sim\bar{0}_{\sigma(u)}
for all
u\in\N^{*}
(and of course
x\sim x
for all
x\in\M^{*}
). Then the quotient space
\M^{*}/\!\sim
is
\HH^{*}

(see
[
7
]
*Theorem 2.4).
Let
Q:\M^{*}\to\HH^{*}
denote this quotient mapping.

For each
u\in\N^{*}
, our quotient mapping
Q:\M^{*}\to\HH^{*}
restricts to an injection on
\I_{u}
.
Because
\I_{u}
is compact and
Q
continuous, this means
Q\!\restriction\!\I_{u}
is an embedding
\I_{u}\to\HH^{*}
.
In other words, we may (and do) think of the
\I_{u}
as subspaces of
\HH^{*}
.
For each
u\in\N^{*}
, let
\I^{Q}_{u}=Q[\I_{u}]
denote this copy of
\I_{u}
in
\HH^{*}
.
Alternatively,

\I^{Q}_{u}=\bigcap_{A\in u}\operatorname{cl}_{\beta\HH}\bigcup\{[n,n+1]:\,n\in A\}.

Via this identification of
\I_{u}
with
\I^{Q}_{u}
, each
\I^{Q}_{u}\subseteq\HH^{*}
has a natural quasi-order, the push-forward of the quasi-order
\leq_{u}
on
\I_{u}
, which we still denote by
\leq_{u}
in
\I^{Q}_{u}
.

Let us note that the
\I^{Q}_{u}
are examples of what are called
standard subcontinua
of
\HH^{*}
. These are special connected subsets of
\HH^{*}
whose structure and interrelationships determine much about the topology of
\HH^{*}
(see
[
7
]
Section 5). It is not difficult to see that the ordering
\leq_{u}
described above for
\I^{Q}_{u}
matches the usual quasi-order defined on a standard subcontinuum of
\HH^{*}
.

Let us say that a homeomorphism
H:\HH^{*}\to\HH^{*}
is
order-reversing
if there is a permutation
\rho
of
\N^{*}
such that

\circ

H[\I^{Q}_{u}]=\I^{Q}_{\rho(u)}
for all
u\in\N^{*}
, and

\circ

if
x\leq_{u}y
in
\I^{Q}_{u}
, then
H(y)\leq_{\rho(u)}H(x)
in
\I^{Q}_{\rho(u)}
.

In other words, an order-reversing autohomeomorphism of
H
is one that permutes the
\I^{Q}_{u}
while reversing their order.

Proposition 12
.

No trivial autohomeomorphism of
\HH^{*}
is order-reversing.

Proof.

Let
H
be a trivial autohomeomorphism of
\HH^{*}
. We aim to show
H
is not order-reversing. Because
H
is trivial, there is a homeomorphism
f:C\to D
, where
C
and
D
are co-compact subsets of
\HH
, such that
H=\beta f\!\restriction\!\HH^{*}
.
Observe that
f
must be order-preserving on a tail of
\HH
: i.e., if
a<b
and
a,b
are sufficiently large, then
f(a)<f(b)
.

For each
n\in\N
, fix
a_{n},b_{n}
such that
n\leq a_{n}<b_{n}\leq n+1
.
Let
\bar{a}=\langle a_{n}:\,n\in\N\rangle
and
\bar{b}=\langle b_{n}:\,n\in\N\rangle
, and let

f(\bar{a})=\langle f(a_{n}):\,n\in\N\rangle
and
f(\bar{b})=\langle f(b_{n}):\,n\in\N\rangle
.

Fix
u\in\N^{*}
and suppose
H[\I^{Q}_{u}]=\I^{Q}_{v}
for some
v\in\N^{*}
. (Otherwise
H
is not order-reversing.)
Let
x=Q(\bar{a}_{u})
and
y=Q(\bar{b}_{u})
. Because
a_{n}<b_{n}
for all
n
, we have
\bar{a}_{u}<_{u}\bar{b}_{u}
in
\I_{u}
, which means
x=Q(\bar{a}_{u})<_{u}Q(\bar{b}_{u})=y
in
\I^{Q}_{u}
.

Now
H(x)=\beta f(Q(\bar{a}_{u}))=Q(f(\bar{a})_{v})
, and similarly
H(y)=Q(f(\bar{b})_{v})
.
But because
f
is order-preserving on a tail, we have
f(a_{n})<f(b_{n})
for all sufficiently large
n
, and therefore
f(\bar{a})_{v}\leq_{v}f(\bar{b})_{v}
. Hence
H(x)=Q(f(\bar{a})_{v})\leq_{v}Q(f(\bar{b})_{v})=H(y)
in
\I^{Q}_{v}
, and this means that
H
is not order-reversing.
∎

Corollary 13
.

It is consistent that no autohomeomorphism of
\HH^{*}
is order-reversing.

Proof.

As we mentioned already in Section 2, a recent result of Vignati in
[
16
]
states that
\mathsf{OCA}_{T}+\mathsf{MA}
implies all autohomeomorphisms of
\HH^{*}
are trivial. The corollary follows from this and the previous proposition.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2
.

As before, let
\sigma
denote the trivial autohomeomorphism of
\N^{*}
induced by the successor function
n\mapsto n+1
on
\N
.
By a recent theorem of the first author (the main theorem of
[
1
]
),
\mathsf{CH}
implies
\sigma
and
\sigma^{-1}
are conjugate in the autohomeomorphism group of
\N^{*}
. In other words,
\mathsf{CH}
implies there is an autohomeomorphism
f
of
\N^{*}
such that
f\circ\sigma=\sigma^{-1}\circ f
.
Fix some such
f
and, using
\mathsf{CH}
again and applying Lemma
11
, fix an order-preserving autohomeomorphism
F
of
\M^{*}
such that
\pi^{*}\circ F=f\circ\pi^{*}
.

Recall the homeomorphism
\mathsf{flip}_{\N}:\M\to\M
is defined by

\mathsf{flip}_{\N}(n,x)\,=\,(n,1-x)

for all
(n,x)\in\M
. Let
\mathsf{flip}_{\N}^{*}=\beta\,\mathsf{flip}_{\N}\restr\M^{*}
denote the trivial autohomeomorphism of
\M^{*}
induced by
\mathsf{flip}_{\N}
.
Clearly
\mathsf{flip}_{\N}^{*}
is order-reversing on each
\I_{u}
, and in particular,

\mathsf{flip}_{\N}^{*}(\bar{1}_{u})=\bar{0}_{u}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathsf{flip}_{\N}^{*}(\bar{0}_{u})=\bar{1}_{u}

for every
u\in\N^{*}
.

Because
\mathsf{flip}_{\N}^{*}
is an order-reversing autohomeomorphism of
\M^{*}
and
F
is an order-preserving autohomeomorphism of
\M^{*}
, their composition

H=F\circ\mathsf{flip}_{\N}^{*}

is an order-reversing autohomeomorphism of
\M^{*}
.
In particular, observe that

H(\bar{1}_{u})=F\circ\mathsf{flip}_{\N}^{*}(\bar{1}_{u})=F(\bar{0}_{u})=\bar{0}_{f(u)}

and

H(\bar{0}_{u})=F\circ\mathsf{flip}_{\N}^{*}(\bar{0}_{u})=F(\bar{1}_{u})=\bar{1}_{f(u)}

for every
u\in\N^{*}
.

Recall the equivalence relation
\sim
on
\M^{*}
described earlier in this section, whose non-singleton equivalence classes are the sets of the form
\{\bar{1}_{u},\bar{0}_{\sigma(u)}\}
for
u\in\N^{*}
.
By our choice of
f
, for any given
u\in\N^{*}
we have

H(\bar{1}_{u})=\bar{0}_{f(u)}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad H(\bar{0}_{\sigma(u)})=\bar{1}_{f(\sigma(u))}=\bar{1}_{\sigma^{-1}(f(u))}.

In particular,
H
maps the
\sim
-equivalence class
\{\bar{1}_{u},\bar{0}_{\sigma(u)}\}
to the
\sim
-equivalence class
\{\bar{1}_{\sigma^{-1}(f(u))},\bar{0}_{f(u)}\}
(i.e., the class
\{\bar{1}_{v},\bar{0}_{\sigma(v)}\}
where
v=\sigma^{-1}(f(u))
). In other words,
H
respects the equivalence classes of the relation
\sim
. Consequently,

[x]_{\sim}\mapsto[H(x)]_{\sim}

is a well-defined mapping
\M^{*}/\!\sim\ \to\M^{*}/\!\sim
. Let
h
denote this mapping. Recalling that
Q
denotes the quotient mapping from
\M^{*}
to
\M^{*}/\!\sim
, we have
Q\circ H=h\circ Q
.

We claim
h
is an order-reversing autohomeomorphism of
\HH^{*}\cong\M^{*}/\!\sim
.
The fact that
h
is an autohomeomorphism follows from the fact that
H
is, and that
h\circ Q=Q\circ H
.
Next, recall that for every
u\in\N^{*}
, the quotient mapping
Q
restricts to a homeomorphism
\I_{u}\to\I^{Q}_{u}
.
Because the sets of the form
\I_{u}
are the connected components of
\M^{*}
, the homeomorphism
H
maps each
\I_{u}
homeomorphically to
\I_{\rho_{H}(u)}=\I_{f(u)}
.
Thus, for each
u\in\N^{*}
, the map
h=Q\circ H\circ Q^{-1}
is a composition of homeomorphisms
\I^{Q}_{u}\to\I_{u}\to\I_{f(u)}\to\I^{Q}_{f(u)}
. Thus, setting
\rho=f
,
h
satisfies the first clause in the definition of an order-reversing autohomeomorphism of
\HH^{*}
.

Finally, we wish to show that
if
x,y\in\I^{Q}_{u}
and
x\leq_{u}y
then
h(y)\leq_{f(u)}h(x)
.
Fix
u\in\N^{*}
and
x,y\in\I^{Q}_{u}
with
x\leq_{u}y
.
As in the previous paragraph,

h=Q\circ H\circ Q^{-1}
is a composition of homeomorphisms
\I^{Q}_{u}\to\I_{u}\to\I_{f(u)}\to\I^{Q}_{f(u)}
. Both
Q
and
Q^{-1}
preserve the order of
x
and
y
, while
H
reverses it, so
h(y)\leq_{f(u)}h(x)
.
∎

Remark 14
.

Peter had a knack of coming up with colourful descriptions of various
constructions.
We thought it apt to give a Peter-esque description of the order-reversing
homeomorphism in this fashion.

One can think of
\M
as a sequence of domino tiles.
One obtains the map
Q:\M\to\HH
, described above by tipping all tiles over
to the right so that for every
n
the top of
\I_{n}
touches the
bottom of
\I_{n+1}
; after some welding the map
Q
is done.

If one tips all tiles over to the left and again does some
welding to join the bottom of
\I_{n}
and the top of
\I_{n+1}
one obtains
another map from
\M
onto
\HH
: the composition
Q\circ\mathsf{flip}
.

Both maps yields maps from
\M^{*}
onto
\HH^{*}
that may be interpreted
as tipping over the domino tiles
\I_{u}
, either all to the right, or all to
the left, and doing the analogous welding.

Our results show that under
\mathsf{CH}
there is a autohomeomorphism of
\HH^{*}

that rearranges the components
\I_{u}
and flips them all over.

Clearly there is no autohomeomorphism of
\HH
itself that does this for the
tiles
\I_{n}
, hence this autohomeomorphism is non-trivial.
