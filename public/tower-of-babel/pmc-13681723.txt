Recurrent trigeminal neuralgia caused by an anomalous cerebellar artery arising from the persistent primitive trigeminal artery: illustrative case
Authors: Hiroki Narita, Nobuyuki Watanabe, Kentaro Watanabe, Ryoto Wachi, Kohei Ishikawa, Michihiro Tanaka, Yuichi Murayama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDPersistent primitive trigeminal artery (PTA) and its related variants—the PTA family—are rare vascular anomalies that can cause trigeminal neuralgia through atypical neurovascular compression patterns, requiring careful anatomical evaluation. The authors describe a rare case of recurrent trigeminal neuralgia caused by an anomalous cerebellar artery arising from a PTA and further complicated by a long-standing Teflon granuloma.OBSERVATIONSA 79-year-old man developed recurrent trigeminal neuralgia 19 years after microvascular decompression. Imaging revealed an anomalous anterior inferior cerebellar artery (AICA) arising from a PTA and compressing the trigeminal nerve, along with a Teflon granuloma obscuring the vessel’s course. Three-dimensional multimodal reconstruction clarified the neurovascular conflict. During surgery, dense adhesions due to the granuloma were encountered, and maximal granuloma removal enabled successful transposition of the anomalous AICA.LESSONSPTA family anomalies can produce trigeminal neuralgia through atypical patterns of neurovascular compression. In complex or recurrent cases, preoperative 3D reconstruction imaging can enhance understanding of aberrant vascular anatomy. Maximal safe removal of a Teflon granuloma is crucial to create sufficient space and facilitate adequate decompression of the offending vessel. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE2699

Persistent carotid-vertebrobasilar anastomoses represent variant arterial communications between the anterior and posterior circulations and include four well-recognized types: the trigeminal, optic, hypoglossal, and proatlantal arteries. Among them, the persistent primitive trigeminal artery (PTA) is the most common and largest channel, typically connecting the internal carotid artery (ICA) to the basilar artery (BA) through the medial aspect of Meckel’s cave or the dura mater of the clivus.1,2 The angiographic anatomy of the PTA and its classification based on the site of junction with the BA were first characterized by Saltzman in 1959 (Saltzman types 1 and 2).1 Subsequent anatomical and angiographic studies have described a PTA variant, designated as Saltzman type 3, in which an anomalous cerebellar artery arises directly from the ICA without interposition of the BA.3,4 Additional reports have described other PTA variants involving cerebellar arteries branching from the PTA itself.4–6

Although PTA and its variants have been implicated in trigeminal neuralgia, their occurrence is exceedingly rare—reported in only 0.2%–0.6% of all TN cases—and therefore no consensus has been established regarding the typical course of the offending vessel or the site of neurovascular conflict.5 We encountered a rare case of recurrent trigeminal neuralgia caused by an anomalous cerebellar artery branching from a PTA. Interpretation of the vascular anatomy was further complicated by a granuloma that had formed around Teflon placed during a microvascular decompression (MVD) performed 19 years earlier. This report highlights key techniques to delineate complex vascular anatomy that differs markedly from routine MVD scenarios and to manage severe adhesions associated with a long-standing Teflon granuloma, which together obscure identification of the true offending artery.

Illustrative Case

History and Examination

A 79-year-old man had undergone MVD for left trigeminal neuralgia 19 years earlier at another institution. His facial pain initially resolved but recurred nearly 2 decades later. Despite escalation of carbamazepine to 800 mg/day, his symptoms persisted, and he was referred to our hospital for further evaluation. MR angiography (Fig. 1A) and CT angiography demonstrated a left-sided PTA connecting the cavernous segment of the ICA to the BA. In addition, an anomalous artery branched dorsally from the PTA and coursed toward the lateral cerebellar surface (Fig. 1B). This anomalous artery was partially obscured by a mass in the cerebellopontine cistern, which appeared as a mildly hypodense lesion on CT (Fig. 1C). Constructive interference in steady state (CISS) imaging revealed that the left trigeminal nerve was laterally displaced by the anomalous artery (Fig. 1D). Based on its origin and trajectory, the anomalous artery was presumed to be an anomalous anterior inferior cerebellar artery (AICA) arising from the PTA and compressing the trigeminal nerve. To better delineate the complex anatomy, multimodal imaging datasets—CT, CT angiography, MR angiography, and CISS—were imported into a 3D visualization and measurement software (Amira 5.6, FEI Company). The DICOM data were merged, and the bone, cerebellum, trigeminal nerve, vasculature, and Teflon granuloma were segmented using both automated and manual techniques (Fig. 1E and F). The preoperative diagnosis was recurrent trigeminal neuralgia caused by an anomalous AICA originating from a PTA and complicated by a Teflon granuloma. Accordingly, surgical planning focused on transposing the offending artery and removing the granuloma.

Surgical Procedure

A retrosigmoid approach was performed using the ORBEYE 4K 3D exoscope (Sony Olympus Medical Solutions) with the patient in the lateral decubitus position. The skin incision and craniotomy were slightly enlarged compared with the prior surgery. After opening the dura and dissecting the intradural connective tissue, the trigeminal nerve was identified. A Teflon granuloma was located caudal to the nerve and was firmly adherent to the surrounding structures (Fig. 2A). As the granuloma was carefully excised in a piecemeal fashion, the anomalous AICA gradually came into view, forming a loop along the medial aspect of the trigeminal nerve and then projecting toward the lateral cerebellar surface (Fig. 2B). Following maximal removal of the Teflon granuloma, sufficient space was created between the anomalous AICA and the trigeminal nerve. A single new Teflon sling was wrapped around both the proximal and distal limbs of the AICA loop, and the entire loop was transposed toward the tentorium. The sling was then secured to the tentorium using 6-0 Prolene sutures and reinforced with fibrin glue. Additionally, a Teflon ball was placed between the distal limb of the AICA loop and the brainstem as an interposition.

Postoperatively, the patient’s facial pain resolved immediately without the need for medication. No complications were observed in the clinical course or on postoperative MRI. The patient has remained symptom free for 2 years following the procedure.

Discussion

Observations

This case involved an anomalous AICA branching from a PTA and compressing the trigeminal nerve. Saltzman classified the PTA into two primary types. In type 1, the PTA inserts into the BA between the origins of the AICA and the superior cerebellar artery (SCA). In this configuration, the BA proximal to the insertion may be hypoplastic and the posterior communicating artery (Pcom) may be absent, indicating that the PTA supplies the distal BA, posterior cerebral artery (PCA), and SCA territories.1,5 In type 2, the PTA inserts into the BA and supplies the SCAs bilaterally, whereas a patent Pcom predominantly supplies the PCAs. Saltzman type 3 has been described as a PTA variant in which a cerebellar artery arises directly from the ICA, without interposition of the BA.3,4 Saad et al. subclassified this type into three groups based on the terminus of the anomalous artery: type 3a (SCA), type 3b (AICA), and type 3c (posterior inferior cerebellar artery [PICA]).4 Other authors have reported a combination type in which a Saltzman type 1 PTA coexists with a Saltzman type 3–like anomalous cerebellar artery arising from the PTA itself.4–6

The PTA family—including classical PTA, PTA variants, and combination types—is a recognized but rare cause of trigeminal neuralgia, accounting for only 0.2%–0.6% of all trigeminal neuralgia cases.7 As depicted schematically in Fig. 3, the classical PTA (Fig. 3A) typically courses along the medial aspect of Meckel’s cave, whereas PTA variants (Fig. 3B) and combination types (Fig. 3C) are more likely to compress the trigeminal nerve in the cisternal portion or root entry zone (REZ) because of their trajectory toward the lateral cerebellar surface.7 Three cases caused by PTA combination types have been reported.5,6,8 The anomalous cerebellar artery involved was identified as the AICA in one case and the PICA in another, and was unspecified in the remaining case. In all reports, the main trunk of the PTA itself was not the offending structure; rather, the anomalous artery compressed the trigeminal nerve in the REZ or cisternal segment. Symptom improvement was achieved by transposition or interposition of the anomalous vessel. As in standard MVD, care must be taken to avoid injury to perforators and cranial nerves during transposition.8 Our case also involved a Teflon granuloma placed during MVD 19 years earlier. Prior studies of recurrent trigeminal neuralgia have shown that recurrence is most often due to a small artery or vein,9 whereas Teflon granuloma accounts for approximately 24% of cases.10 Hyperdense lesions on CT scans11,12 and gadolinium-enhancing lesions on T1-weighted MRI13 are suggestive of Teflon granuloma, although negative imaging does not exclude the diagnosis.13 Removal of Teflon granuloma has been reported to alleviate symptoms.11,13,14 Because recurrence may occur even after long intervals—up to 120 months—and adhesions can be dense13 or exhibit neovascular supply,9 redo operations require meticulous dissection. Lessons

In routine MVD, the course of the offending vessel and the anticipated site of compression can often be identified readily on MRI. However, cases involving the PTA family exhibit abnormal vascular architecture and may even present neurovascular conflict within Meckel’s cave. Furthermore, a Teflon granuloma can distort or displace the offending vessel, complicating interpretation of preoperative imaging. In such complex situations, preoperative 3D reconstruction may provide substantially greater anatomical clarity than conventional imaging alone.

Intraoperatively, the offending vessel in this case was firmly adherent to the Teflon granuloma, making delineation of its course challenging. Adequate transposition required removal of as much Teflon as safely possible to release these adhesions. However, dense fibrous adhesions and bleeding from granuloma-associated neovascularization can make complete mobilization difficult, requiring the creation of a small working space sufficient to insert a Teflon sling for suspending the offending vessel.

With respect to the technical nuances of the Teflon sling ­technique used in this case, because the anomalous AICA loop could not be completely dissected free from the Teflon granuloma, our primary objective was to maximize the distance between the AICA loop and the trigeminal nerve. To achieve this, the sling was wrapped around both the proximal and distal limbs of the loop simultaneously to pull the entire complex toward the tentorium. Because of the severe adhesions to the Teflon granuloma, there was a significant elastic recoiling force pulling the vessel back toward its original position. Therefore, we anchored the Teflon sling to the tentorium using 6-0 Prolene sutures to ensure secure mechanical fixation. In such a highly adherent redo surgical field, we reasoned that using fibrin glue alone would provide insufficient holding power to resist this strong recoiling force.
