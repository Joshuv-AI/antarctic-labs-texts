Multiligament ankle instability: Case report and therapeutic perspectives
Authors: Yassine Ben Bouzid, Moulay Omar Lamrani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSevere ankle sprains involving complex ligament injuries represent a significant challenge in sports traumatology. These injuries often include damage to the syndesmosis, lateral and medial ligaments, and associated intra-articular structures. Early diagnosis and appropriate surgical management are crucial to prevent chronic instability and long-term functional impairment.Case presentationWe report the case of a 19-year-old male football player who sustained a severe ankle injury during a match. The mechanism of injury involved forced external rotation with weight-bearing. Initial examination revealed significant swelling, extensive bruising, and tenderness around the ankle. Imaging studies confirmed syndesmotic injury with external rotation of the fibula, anterior widening of the syndesmotic gap, and osteochondral fragments in the ankle joint. Surgical management included dynamic fixation of the syndesmosis with four suture buttons, anatomical ligament repair using anchors, and removal of osteochondral fragments. Postoperative rehabilitation involved immobilization followed by progressive mobilization and proprioceptive strengthening. At twelve months, the patient had regained full, pain-free ankle stability and returned to sports activities.DiscussionThe management of complex ankle injuries requires a multidisciplinary approach integrating advanced surgical techniques such as dynamic syndesmotic fixation and ligament reinsertion. Compared to rigid screws, suture buttons offer enhanced physiological micromotion and eliminate the need for implant removal. Intra-articular pathologies, frequently associated with such injuries, further complicate treatment and may benefit from arthroscopic techniques. Rehabilitation protocols focusing on early mobilization and proprioceptive training are essential for optimal recovery.ConclusionThis case highlights the importance of comprehensive surgical and rehabilitative management in treating severe combined ankle injuries. Advances in dynamic fixation and arthroscopic repairs hold promise for improving functional outcomes and minimizing long-term complications.Keywords: Ankle sprain, Syndesmotic injury, Ligament repair, Dynamic fixation

Introduction

Ankle sprains, among the most common injuries in sports traumatology, are often considered benign. However, approximately 30 % of patients develop chronic complications, such as chronic ankle instability (CAI), particularly when appropriate initial treatment is not administered [1,2]. This CAI is generally associated with ligamentous insufficiency resulting from traumatic injury, mainly involving the lateral, medial, and syndesmotic ligament complexes [3,4]. Among these structures, the lateral ligaments are most frequently affected, especially in supination injuries that place significant strain on the anterior talofibular ligament [5,6].

Syndesmotic injuries, although less common, present significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. They often occur during forced external rotation or hyperdorsiflexion trauma, particularly in athletes participating in high-impact activities such as skiing or ice hockey [7,8]. These injuries, frequently overlooked during initial evaluations, are a common cause of persistent pain and prolonged functional impairment [9,10]. Their management depends on the stability of the injury: while stable injuries can be treated conservatively, unstable injuries almost always require surgical intervention to prevent complications such as early osteoarthritis [11,12].

The diagnosis of ligament injuries relies on both clinical and imaging tools. Radiographs are essential for excluding fractures, but their utility in detecting complex ligament injuries remains limited. MRI, with its high sensitivity, has become the gold standard for assessing the extent of ligament injuries and identifying concomitant damage, such as osteochondral lesions [12,13]. Additionally, arthroscopy has emerged as an indispensable tool, not only for confirming suspected injuries but also for addressing associated intra-articular damage [13,14].

The treatment of ligament instabilities has evolved, with an emphasis on surgical and arthroscopic approaches. Arthroscopic repair techniques, inspired by the Broström-Gould procedure, allow for the restoration of joint stability with outcomes comparable to open techniques while reducing operative morbidity and recovery times [15,16]. For complex cases, particularly those involving combined injuries or poor tissue quality, ligament reconstructions using tendon grafts or artificial ligaments are considered, although their long-term efficacy still requires further validation [17,18].

In this report, we present the clinical case of a 19-year-old male patient who sustained a severe sprain of the talocrural and syndesmotic joint during a football match. This injury, classified among severe sprains, highlights the importance of timely and appropriate management to prevent potential complications. The case also illustrates the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges associated with this type of sports trauma.

Case presentation

A 19-year-old male patient was admitted to the emergency department for right ankle trauma sustained during a football match. The patient reported that at the moment of injury, his ankle was subjected to forced external rotation while weight-bearing. He also mentioned that his ankle was dislocated on the field and reduced on-site by emergency responders. Clinical examination revealed significant ankle swelling and bruising on the anterior aspect of the ankle and over the lateral malleolus (Fig. 1). No deformity was observed. Pulses were palpable, and no neurological deficit was noted.

clinical aspect of the ankle showing extensive bruising and significant swelling.

Radiographic evaluation of the ankle and knee in two standard views showed no fractures (Fig. 2). There was no syndesmotic diastasis or widening of the medial talo-malleolar space. The talar tilt was normal, and the fibula was intact along its entire length (Fig. 2). The patient was immobilized in a posterior plaster splint and prescribed analgesics.

Ankle radiograph showing a congruent tibio-talar joint, no widening of the medial malleolar space, and normal tibio-fibular overlap. No fractures were noted, including at the proximal fibula.

Five days later, the patient presented to the outpatient clinic with refractory pain despite level 2 analgesics. Bruising had spread throughout the ankle, and ankle movement was limited due to pain. An anterior drawer test revealed instability. Palpation of both the medial and lateral malleoli was painful. A CT scan of the ankle revealed a 4.5° external rotation of the lateral malleolus relative to the fibular notch. The syndesmosis showed posterior compression and anterior widening (Fig. 3). The talus was externally rotated by 5° within the mortise. Osteochondral fragments were also identified along the anterior edge of the tibial plafond (Fig. 4). MRI (Fig. 5) was performed to further evaluate the injury and revealed the following:

•Lateral collateral ligament complex: Complete rupture of the anterior talofibular ligament and a probable partial rupture of the posterior talofibular ligament, with a hematic effusion in the sheaths of the peroneal and flexor tendons (Fig. 5A). Heterogeneous hematoma infiltration was noted within the flexor hallucis longus, flexor digitorum longus, and soleus muscles.

A postoperative radiograph was systematically performed for verification (Fig. 7). The patient was immobilized in a posterior plaster splint for two months, with strict non-weight-bearing and thromboprophylaxis. Passive rehabilitation and muscle strengthening were initiated on day 15 after suture removal.

Postoperative radiograph demonstrating the restored alignment and fixation.

At the 6-month follow-up, the patient had resumed walking without pain, demonstrated stable ankle joint function, and returned to sports activities.

At the 12-month follow-up, clinical examination confirmed complete functional recovery, with full range of motion, no residual pain, and no signs of instability. Follow-up radiographs demonstrated a congruent ankle joint, appropriate alignment of the distal tibiofibular syndesmosis, and the absence of deformity or degenerative changes (Fig. 8).

Functional assessment using the AOFAS (American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society) Ankle–Hindfoot Score yielded 92 out of 100, indicating an excellent functional outcome.

Discussion

Severe ankle sprains, involving complex ligament injuries, pose a significant challenge in terms of diagnosis and management. The case of our 19-year-old patient highlights these situations, with an injury affecting the syndesmosis, the talofibular ligament, and the medial collateral ligament. A comprehensive surgical approach was adopted, including ligament reinsertion using anchors and dynamic fixation of the syndesmosis with four suture buttons. This multidisciplinary management reflects current principles of treatment for combined ankle injuries as described in the literature.

Severe syndesmotic injuries, such as the one observed in our patient, are often associated with a mechanism of forced external rotation of the joint [7,8]. Surgical fixation is required to restore tibiofibular stability and prevent chronic instability. Suture buttons, as used in our case, offer several advantages over rigid screws, including allowing physiological micromotion and reducing the risk of complications such as implant breakage or loosening [19,20]. These devices also eliminate the need for removal procedures, thus improving functional recovery and reducing indirect costs related to prolonged immobilization [21].

Regarding the lateral and medial ligaments, their involvement in multidirectional ankle stability necessitates specific management. In our intervention, anatomical reinsertion was performed, following Broström and Gould's recommendations for lateral lesions and Hintermann's recommendations for medial lesions [15,16,22]. Reinsertion of the medial collateral ligament, in particular, is crucial for preventing valgus deformities of the hindfoot, which may occur if the ligament remains insufficient [23,24].

The complexity of combined injuries, as in the presented case, is often compounded by the frequent presence of associated intra-articular injuries. While we opted for an open approach, this article underscores the growing role of arthroscopy in such contexts. In addition to its diagnostic utility, arthroscopy allows simultaneous treatment of intra-articular injuries, such as osteochondral lesions and loose bodies [13,14]. In the future, integrating arthroscopic techniques into ligament repairs could offer outcomes comparable to open techniques, with reduced morbidity and accelerated recovery [18,25].

This case also highlights the importance of postoperative rehabilitation to ensure optimal recovery. The literature recommends early mobilization protocols combined with proprioceptive strengthening exercises to prevent recurrence and improve long-term functional outcomes [26,27].

Conclusion

The management of complex ankle injuries, such as those encountered in our case, relies on a multidimensional surgical approach integrating modern fixation and ligament repair techniques. The promising results of dynamic devices and arthroscopic repairs described in recent literature open new perspectives for the treatment of these injuries, while improving functional outcomes and reducing long-term complications.
