Genetically determined heterogeneous treatment effects of Helicobacter pylori eradication for targeted gastric cancer prevention
Authors: Zong-Chao Liu, Yu-Xin Wang, Heng-Min Xu, Xuan Han, Wen-Jing Zhao, Hui Ma, Yang Zhang, Jing-Ying Zhang, Tong Zhou, Wei-Cheng You, Kai-Feng Pan, Wen-Qing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPopulation-based Helicobacter pylori screen-and-treat is a priority for gastric cancer (GC) prevention, yet the preventive effect varies across individuals, and the determinants of this heterogeneity remain insufficiently understood. As large-scale prevention efforts expand globally, identifying individuals most likely to benefit is increasingly important. We analyzed two randomized trials, the Mass Intervention Trial in Linqu, Shandong Province (MITS; ChiCTR-TRC-10000979; n = 1,740), and the Shandong Intervention Trial (SIT; NCT00339768; n = 1,811), to characterize genetically determined heterogeneous treatment effects of H. pylori eradication for GC prevention. We identified 11 genetic variants that modified the preventive efficacy of eradication and integrated their genotype-specific conditional average treatment effects into a polygenic treatment benefit score (PTBS). We further compared a benefit-based strategy prioritizing individuals predicted to derive greater benefit for GC prevention with a risk-based strategy defined by a polygenic risk score and assessed whether benefit-based subgroups exhibited differential residual GC risk post-eradication. With the PTBS, targeting the high-treatment-benefit individuals conferred stronger protection against GC (MITS, hazard ratio [HR] = 0.44, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.35–0.54, number needed to treat [NNT] = 56; SIT, HR = 0.20, 95% CI 0.10–0.41, NNT = 26) than treating all H. pylori-positive participants or those of high genetic risk. PTBS further stratified residual GC risk among individuals post-treatment (MITS, HR = 0.65, 95% CI 0.52–0.81; SIT, HR = 0.27, 95% CI 0.13–0.56, for high-treatment-benefit individuals) and was associated with slower precancerous lesion progression. Our findings suggest that host genetic factors modulate the preventive efficacy of H. pylori eradication and that incorporating genotype-specific heterogeneous treatment effects provides a framework for enhancing the precision of population-based screen-and-treat programs while supporting post-eradication risk management.Keywords: gastric cancer, Helicobacter pylori, heterogeneous treatment effect, targeted prevention

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) remains a significant global health challenge,1 with its burden projected to persist without effective intervention strategies.2 Chronic infection with Helicobacter pylori is the primary etiological factor for GC, particularly non-cardia GC. Extensive evidence from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and meta-analyses establishes that H. pylori eradication significantly reduces GC incidence and mortality.3 The International Agency for Research on Cancer of the World Health Organization (IARC/WHO) Working Group issued global guidance aimed at reducing the global burden of GC, suggesting a prioritization of H. pylori “screen-and-treat” interventions through population-wide strategies in high-risk regions and targeted approaches for intermediate- and high-risk individuals in areas with otherwise low GC incidence.4

Despite the proven efficacy of H. pylori eradication for GC prevention, its preventive effectiveness varies substantially among individuals due to a complex interplay of bacterial, environmental, and host factors.5 Not all infected individuals develop GC, and some may develop the disease even after successful eradication.5,6,7 These variations, known as heterogeneity of treatment effects (HTE), complicate the direct implementation of screen-and-treat programs and highlight the need for a more refined, cost-effective pathway. Emerging biomarkers such as DNA methylation profiles demonstrate that risk stratification remains possible after H. pylori eradication, suggesting that preventive benefits are not uniform across individuals, even within well-defined risk groups.8 Traditionally, HTE is investigated through prespecified subgroup analyses in RCTs,9 which are often limited by unexpectedly low statistical power and a focus on relative risk (RR) scales that are less directly applicable to decision-making.10,11 In addressing this, advanced causal inference approaches have been developed to more validly estimate HTE, typically by estimating conditional average treatment effects (CATEs), allowing for a crucial distinction to be made between an individual’s baseline risk of developing a disease and the potential benefit derived from an intervention.9 Such distinction is especially critical in light of the fact that high baseline GC risk does not necessarily imply proportionally greater benefit from H. pylori eradication6,12 as well as the growing challenge of antibiotic resistance in GC prevention and the need to avoid unnecessary antibiotic use.13 It has been advocated that treating patients with a high estimated treatment benefit (“high-benefit approach”), instead of treating patients with a high baseline risk of disease (“high-risk approach”), has the potential to maximize the effectiveness of intervention and better improve population health outcomes.12,14 Accordingly, identifying who truly benefits most, and who remains at exceptionally high residual risk even after eradication, is essential for precision implementation of H. pylori screen-and-treat strategies.4

Genetic variants within key signaling pathways, such as the NF-κB inflammatory pathway, which acts as a major effector in H. pylori-mediated gastric carcinogenesis, and the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, a critical driver activated by H. pylori that promotes early initiation and subsequent progression of GC, can shape the trajectory from gastric lesions to malignancy.15 The variants can provide stable, directional, and biologically relevant predictors that can be measured before intervention,16 making them ideal biomarkers for identifying individuals most likely to benefit from H. pylori eradication. Indeed, recent findings suggest that individuals with high genetic risk of GC derive greater preventive benefit from H. pylori eradication,17,18 but important gaps remain in weak causal evidence for gene-by-treatment interactions, limited cost-effectiveness evaluation, and the lack of calibrated absolute-risk frameworks.19,20

Characterizing the HTE of H. pylori eradication for GC prevention is necessary for refining long-term post-treatment surveillance and ensuring the sustainability of screen-and-treat programs. In this post hoc analysis using data from two RCTs,6,7 we examined genetic variants related to GC development and H. pylori infection to identify genetically determined HTE of the eradication therapy, aiming to formulate a treatment-benefit-based framework for targeted GC prevention. The primary objective was to elucidate the varying degrees of long-term protection for GC achieved within the treated population and, crucially, to offer a complementary precision tool to identify individuals retaining elevated residual GC risk, thereby enhancing the practicality and readiness of implementing H. pylori screen-and-treat programs.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

The overall study design is illustrated by Figure 1. The study involved a two-stage discovery and validation design leveraging two independent large-scale RCTs that evaluated the preventive effects of H. pylori eradication on GC. Participants of the discovery stage were drawn from the Mass Intervention Trial in Linqu, Shandong Province (MITS; ChiCTR-TRC-10000979),7 a cluster-randomized trial initiated in March 2011 that enrolled 180,284 residents ages 25–54 years after population-based H. pylori screening using the 13C urea breath test. Details of the MITS were described previously.7 In the MITS, H. pylori-positive participants (n = 102,330) were randomized to receive a 10-day standard quadruple or low-dose symptom-alleviation regimen, while H. pylori-negative individuals (n = 77,954) received no treatment. Over 11.8 years of follow-up until December 2022, a total of 1,035 cases of incident GC were documented by linkage to the cancer registry, death reporting system, and active clinical follow-up. For analytical purposes, a representative subset population (n = 2,804) for the entire MITS was derived, which employed a nested case-control design to include 935 incident GC cases and 1,869 matched controls (1:2 ratio). These participants had qualified genotype data, and inverse probability weights were applied for the subsequent analyses.18 Among them, 1,740 H. pylori-positive participants were included as a discovery dataset (“MITS dataset”) to identify genetic profiles underlying the HTE of H. pylori eradication for GC prevention in the current study.

Study design for identifying genetically determined HTE of H. pylori eradication for GC prevention

This post hoc analysis of two randomized trials comprised a discovery stage based on the MITS (ChiCTR-TRC-10000979; n = 1,740) and a validation stage based on the SIT (NCT00339768; n = 1,811). Genotype-specific CATEs were estimated for variants showing effect modification in both trials and integrated into a composite benefit score to predict individualized preventive benefit, which was subsequently evaluated in the validation trial for reproducibility of treatment-benefit stratification and post-treatment risk stratification. We formulated a benefit-based strategy that prioritized individuals predicted to derive greater preventive effect from H. pylori eradication and compared this strategy with a risk-based strategy, which defined high-risk individuals using a polygenic risk score. We further evaluated whether benefit-defined subgroups demonstrated stratified risk of GC and the progression of precancerous gastric lesions following H. pylori eradication therapy.

Findings derived from the discovery stage were subsequently validated using the Shandong Intervention Trial (SIT; NCT00339768), a randomized, double-blinded, placebo-controlled trial initiated in 1995. A total of 3,365 trial participants ages 35–64 years had undergone endoscopic examination, histopathological evaluation, and H. pylori serology. Among them, in July 1995, H. pylori-positive participants (n = 2,258) were randomized in a 2 × 2 × 2 factorial design to eradication therapy, vitamin supplementation, and garlic supplementation or their placebos. H. pylori-negative individuals (n = 1,107) entered a 2 × 2 design for vitamin and garlic supplementation only. Longitudinal follow-up was conducted through repeated endoscopic screening and other approaches of passive and active follow-up similar to the MITS (Method S1). Of the 2,924 participants with peripheral blood DNA samples for genotyping, 2,755 passed quality control. Among them, 1,811 H. pylori-positive participants were included in the validation stage of the current study (“SIT dataset”), 122 participants developed GC during the follow-up ending in December 2024.

In our study, candidate genetic variants were first prioritized for treatment-genotype interactions in the discovery trial (MITS) and subsequently confirmed in the validation trial (SIT). We characterized the genotype-specific HTE by estimating the CATEs using the MITS dataset. A composite polygenic treatment benefit score (PTBS) reflecting the individualized preventive effect of H. pylori treatment was then derived in the MITS dataset and applied to assess average preventive effects and post-eradication GC risk stratification. We further used the SIT dataset to evaluate whether the PTBS could reproducibly identify subgroup differences in preventive effects and stratify post-eradication GC risk in an independent trial population.

Both the original trials and the current study were approved by the institutional review board of Peking University Cancer Hospital & Institute, and all participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

Genome-wide variant detection and quality control

Peripheral blood leukocyte DNA samples were collected from study participants. Samples with concentrations >10 ng/μL and total yields >300 ng with clear electrophoresis bands were deemed as meeting quality control requirements. Participants from the MITS were genotyped using the Illumina Asian Screening Array and those from the SIT were genotyped using the Illumina Global Screening Array following similar principles and workflows.18 Individual genotypes were phased with SHAPEIT2 and imputed using IMPUTE2 with the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 reference panel. To enhance comparability across the two trials, identical post-imputation quality control principles were applied, and only high-quality variants passing the same filtering criteria in both datasets were retained for downstream analyses. Post-imputation filters excluded variants with call rate <95%, Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium p value < 10−6 or minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.1%, and imputation information score (INFO) score ≤0.3. Individuals were excluded if they had sex mismatches, duplicates, or outlier heterozygosity. Quality control was conducted using PLINK v.1.9.

Selection of candidate genomic regions and genetic variants

We prioritized candidate genomic regions based on complementary biological knowledge and existing study findings. First, we curated gene sets guided by a priori biological hypotheses, including 1,168 genes involved in GC- and H. pylori infection-related signaling pathways from the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database and 27 known cancer-predisposing genes (Table S1).21 Candidate single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) were then identified either by extracting all variants within these genomic regions or by selecting variants that act as significant expression or splicing quantitative trait loci (eQTLs/sQTLs) for these genes in stomach tissues from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) Project v.8 (p < 5 × 10−8).22 In parallel, we incorporated existing evidence from prior genome-wide association studies (GWASs) by extracting genome-wide SNVs with suggestive significance (p < 5 × 10−4) from: (1) GWASs of GC and precancerous gastric lesion progression17,23 and (2) a newly conducted GWAS meta-analysis of GC in East Asian populations pooling estimates from BioBank Japan, the Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study, and the China Kadoorie Biobank (Method S1). Independent variants were defined by pairwise pruning with a 50-kb sliding window and an r2 threshold of 0.5. A total of 47,203 variants identified from these streams of evidence were combined to form the candidate set of variants for subsequent analyses.

Identification of genetic variants modifying the effect of H. pylori treatment on GC risk

To identify the genetic profiles underlying HTE of H. pylori treatment for GC prevention, we first evaluated each SNV from the established candidate variant set for its statistical interaction with H. pylori treatment on GC risk. The variant’s interaction with H. pylori treatment in relation to GC risk was assessed by fitting Cox proportional hazards models on GC incidence, including the SNV coded additively by copies of the effect allele, the treatment indicator, and their interaction term, while adjusting for age, sex, smoking status, and alcohol consumption. The analyses were initially conducted in the MITS dataset using inverse probability weighting to account for potential confounding and accommodate for nested case-control designs.18,24 SNVs showing significant interaction after false discovery rate (FDR) adjustment with the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure (FDR-adjusted p < 0.05) were taken forward for validating the interaction effects in the SIT dataset, where the same modeling approach and covariate adjustments were applied. To characterize the potential regulatory effects and functional relevance of these variants, we performed comprehensive functional annotation analyses including cis-eQTL annotations from stomach tissue in the GTEx Project v.8 using FUMA v.1.8.2, regulatory element annotations from HaploReg v.4.2, RegulomeDB v.2.2, and ENCODE Phase 3 and sequence-level regulatory predictions using AlphaGenome v.0.1.0. In addition, differential expression analyses were performed for their nearby candidate genes in GC versus normal gastric tissues using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and GTEx Project v.8 data (Method S1). The SNVs showing both statistically significant interaction (p < 0.05) with H. pylori treatment and consistent effect direction as estimated in the MITS and SIT datasets were prioritized for subsequent CATE estimation.

Estimation of CATEs for the prioritized genetic variants

Based on H. pylori-positive participants in the MITS dataset, marginal CATEs for H. pylori treatment in reducing GC risk were estimated by each prioritized variant, averaged over the distributions of other prioritized SNVs, age, sex, and status of smoking and alcohol consumption. The CATEs were estimated using a doubly robust, machine-learning-based approach designed to flexibly capture non-linear interactions between genetic variants and the preventive effect of H. pylori treatment on GC risk.25 The approach integrates two main components: (1) pseudo-outcome modeling, which estimates the risk of developing GC under treatment and no treatment for each individual, and (2) effect modeling, which directly models variation in treatment effects as a function of genetic variants. The components employed flexible neural networks with two hidden layers containing two nodes and weight decay regularization to accommodate potential non-linearities. The estimated CATEs represent the expected absolute GC risk reduction attributable to H. pylori treatment for individuals carrying a specific genotype. In this context, a negative CATE indicates a greater predicted preventive benefit (i.e., higher reduced risk after treatment) from H. pylori treatment. SNVs with statistically significant CATEs (p < 0.05) were retained to construct the final genetic profile.

Benefit-based assessment on the preventive effect of H. pylori eradication

Based on the SNVs with significant CATEs, we developed the PTBS leveraging H. pylori-positive individuals in the MITS dataset to quantify individualized preventive benefit of H. pylori treatment. For each individual, the PTBS was calculated as the negative of the sum of genotype-specific CATEs (BenefitScorei=−∑j=1mCATEij, where i denotes the ith individual and j indexes the jth SNV), with higher score values indicating greater anticipated reductions in GC risk and enhanced treatment benefits for GC prevention. Given that CATEs estimate the absolute risk difference (treatment risk minus control risk), negative values signify a reduction in disease incidence. We therefore inverted the sign so that higher PTBS values intuitively correspond to a greater magnitude of preventive efficacy. All individuals were subsequently dichotomized into two groups using the median PTBS as the cutoff: individuals with scores at or above the median were assigned to the “high-treatment-benefit” group and those with scores below the median to the “moderate-treatment-benefit” group. The same genotype-specific CATEs were applied as the scoring weights to the SIT participants to predict the individualized preventive benefit, using the identical cutoff value derived from the MITS dataset to define the benefit groups. To facilitate individualized decision-making for H. pylori treatment, a web application was developed using Streamlit for easy calculation of the PTBS.

We compared the preventive effects when H. pylori treatment was allocated according to two hypothetical strategies, namely the benefit-based and the risk-based approaches. In the benefit-based approach, H. pylori-positive individuals of the high-treatment-benefit group would be considered for treatment. In the risk-based approach, participants were stratified into high- and low-genetic-risk groups based on their polygenic risk score (PRS) for GC, which was calculated using meta-analyzed GWAS summary statistics and previously established panels (Method S1). Individuals with PRS at or above the 50th percentile were classified as high genetic risk and would be considered eligible for H. pylori treatment. The preventive effects of H. pylori treatments were then examined respectively for H. pylori-positive participants of the high-treatment-benefit group (based on the PTBS) and the high-risk group (based on the PRS). In the high-treatment-benefit group, we further examined if the preventive effect differed by the individual’s genetic risk. The multivariate-adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) and the corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using Cox proportional hazards models to quantify the preventive effect of H. pylori treatment, adjusting for age, sex, smoking, and alcohol consumption. The proportional hazard assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals. To further compare the two approaches, we calculated the number needed to treat (NNT) with 95% CIs, using the formula NNT = 1/(assumed control risk [ACR] × (1 − RR)),3 where the ACR was estimated from local cancer registry data from the trial regions.7

In addition to the overall treatment effect, we examined the effect of successful H. pylori eradication by comparing individuals who achieved confirmed eradication with the reference group. We also determined the effect of H. pylori treatment stratified by baseline age, sex, smoking, and alcohol consumption. Outcome-specific analyses were conducted to examine the effects for cardia and non-cardia GC as well as early-onset GC (diagnosed at age <50 years).

Benefit-based risk stratification of GC after H. pylori eradication

For both the MITS and the SIT datasets, we evaluated whether different benefit subgroups, as determined by the PTBS, could further stratify GC risk among H. pylori-positive participants who received eradication therapy, including those already considered at high or low genetic risk. Cumulative incidence curves were plotted to illustrate the differences in GC risk by benefit subgroups among individuals who received H. pylori treatment. HRs of GC incidence with 95% CIs were calculated for the high-treatment-benefit group, with the moderate-treatment-benefit group as the reference, using Cox regression models adjusting for age, sex, smoking, and alcohol consumption.

Based on the SIT dataset, we further performed trajectory analyses to assess whether predicted individualized preventive benefit modified the progression of gastric lesions over time following H. pylori treatment. Lesion-stage progression was modeled using linear mixed-effects models (LMMs), with the outcome defined as transitional changes in gastric lesion severity during longitudinal endoscopic follow-up (Method S1). In the LMMs, we modeled follow-up time since baseline endoscopy and its interaction with benefit groups to test for differences in lesion progression rates. A subject-specific random intercept was included to capture individual-level variation across repeated measurements, and age, sex, and baseline histopathology were adjusted for as covariates. A significant interaction term (p < 0.05) was considered as evidence that progression rates varied over time between benefit groups.

Sensitivity analyses

We conducted sensitivity analyses in two domains to test the robustness of our findings. First, we varied thresholds for defining high-treatment-benefit groups (based on PTBS) and high-risk groups (based on PRS values) from the 50th to the 95th percentile to assess whether targeting high-treatment-benefit individuals consistently outperformed targeting high-risk individuals under alternative subgroup definitions. Second, in analyses of precancerous lesion progression, we varied the PTBS thresholds (50th to 90th percentile) to distinguish high- from moderate-treatment-benefit subgroups and repeated these analyses within strata of low- and high-genetic-risk individuals to evaluate whether differences in lesion progression remained stable across genetic risk backgrounds.

Results

Study participants

Baseline characteristics for all H. pylori-positive participants in the MITS (n = 1,740) and SIT (n = 1,811) datasets are summarized in Table S2. In the MITS, 869 participants were randomized to H. pylori eradication therapy, with 512 (58.9%) achieving successful eradication, and 871 to the reference group (symptom-alleviation treatment). The mean (standard deviation, SD) baseline age was 44.7 (7.4) years for GC cases and 44.8 (7.4) years for non-cases, with males accounting for 77.1% and 48.6%, respectively. In the SIT, 877 participants received H. pylori eradication therapy, of whom 645 (73.5%) achieved successful eradication. GC cases had a mean ± SD age of 48.7 ± 9.7 years, with 72.1% male, while non-cases were on average 46.2 ± 8.9 years of age and 46.5% male.

Preventive effect of H. pylori treatment in the evaluated datasets

After 11.8 years of follow-up in the MITS dataset, H. pylori treatment was associated with a 20% reduction in GC incidence compared with the symptom-alleviation treatment group (HR = 0.80, 95% CI 0.69–0.93). In the SIT dataset, after 29.4 years of follow-up, H. pylori treatment was associated with a 34% reduction in GC incidence (HR = 0.66, 95% CI 0.46–0.95). Generally consistent effect estimates were also yielded in the analyses stratified by age, sex, smoking, and alcohol consumption (Table S3). No significant heterogeneity was observed for the risk estimates between the included participants of the current study and their respective parent trials (Cochran’s Q test for heterogeneity, p-het = 0.497 for the MITS and p-het = 0.285 for the SIT; Table S4), supporting the representativeness and robustness of the dataset-specific estimates.

Genetic profiles underlying the HTE of H. pylori eradication in preventing GC

We identified 2,045 variants showing significant interaction with H. pylori treatment in reducing GC risk (FDR-adjusted p < 0.05; Table S5) in the MITS dataset. Functional annotation of these variants, together with transcriptional evidence for the corresponding candidate genes, suggested their potential involvement in gastric carcinogenesis (Table S6). For instance, variants (rs76394620 and rs62387050) at the BMP6 locus were mapped to a non-coding regulatory region enriched for enhancer-associated chromatin features, as well as open chromatin signals detected by DNase I hypersensitive sites sequencing (DNase-seq) (rs76394620). Several variants (rs76394620, rs62387050, and rs1743638) also overlapped with predicted transcription factor binding motifs (e.g., YY1, FOXP1, MEF2, and RXRA), suggesting potential effects on transcriptional regulation. In addition, a downregulation of BMP6 expression was observed in GC tissues compared with normal gastric tissues in TCGA and GTEx v.8 datasets.

Eleven of the 2,045 variants were further replicated in the SIT dataset with significant and consistent interaction effects (p < 0.05; Table 1; Figure 2). We found no substantial inflation of the test statistics for the association analyses in the MITS (λGC = 1.07) and SIT datasets (λGC = 0.97) (Figure S1). Most of these replicated variants were mapped to gene loci within GC-related pathways (KEGG pathway ID: hsa05226), including the Wnt (hsa04310), PI3K-Akt (hsa04151), and MAPK (hsa04010) signaling pathways, which are implicated in the transition from intestinal metaplasia to dysplasia during the development of intestinal-type GC, progression from dysplasia to adenocarcinoma in intestinal-type GC, and development of diffuse-type gastric adenocarcinoma, respectively. Among the 2,045 variants identified in the discovery stage, multiple ones were located in regions with complex linkage disequilibrium, including the HLA and VEGFA regions of chromosome 6 (Figure S2); however, none of these variants were validated in the SIT dataset.

The marginal CATEs for these variants were estimated according to the individual genotypes in both the MITS and the SIT datasets, and the effect estimates were meta-analyzed by random-effect models (Figure S3). Among them, three variants (rs10762496, rs4969266, and rs9975448) displayed CATE values that switched from negative to positive in both datasets, indicating a transition from predicted benefit of H. pylori treatment to loss of benefit with increasing dosage of the reference allele. In contrast, five variants (rs7860304, rs12806363, rs368403298, rs7894516, and rs877447) demonstrated a greater preventive effect as the dosage of the reference allele increased. These patterns highlight genotype-dependent heterogeneity in the preventive effect of H. pylori treatment.

PTBS and PRS for evaluating the preventive effect of H. pylori treatment

We derived individuals’ PTBS based on CATEs of the replicated 11 variants. The PTBS was used to identify high-benefit individuals (defined as PTBS at or above the median, 0.806 in both datasets) in whom the preventive effect of H. pylori treatment was subsequently evaluated. Additionally, we quantified the individual genetic risk of GC using the PRS calculated from 16 independent variants identified through meta-analysis together with 124 variants from previously established panels (Method S1). Both the PTBS and the PRS demonstrated similar distribution patterns across the MITS and SIT datasets (Figure S4). Additionally, no significant differences in baseline characteristics were observed between the high- and the moderate-benefit subgroups in either dataset.

Preventive effect among individuals with high-treatment-benefit

Compared with conventional treatment on all H. pylori-positive individuals, targeting those in the high-treatment-benefit subgroup yielded stronger preventive effects against GC, with an HR of 0.44 (95% CI 0.35–0.54; NNT = 56, 95% CI 44–83) in the MITS after 11.8-year follow-up (p-het < 0.001) and 0.20 (95% CI 0.10–0.41; NNT = 26, 95% CI 23–36) in the SIT participants after 29.4-year follow up (p-het = 0.020) (Table 2). When restricting the treatment group to individuals with confirmed successful H. pylori eradication, a greater protective effect was found for individuals of the high-treatment-benefit subgroup than for the overall H. pylori-positive population (p-het < 0.001 for both MITS and SIT; Figures 3A and 3B). Specifically, individuals in the high-treatment-benefit group had higher baseline GC risk when allocated to the symptom-alleviation treatment or placebo group, compared with the overall H. pylori-positive population, which partly translated into greater absolute risk reductions than treating all individuals. This suggests that the PTBS may complement conventional high-risk definitions by identifying those whose elevated risk is particularly amenable to intervention. Such similar effect patterns were consistently observed in the subgroup analyses stratified by baseline age, sex, smoking, and alcohol use (Table S7).

Findings remained robust when restricting the treatment group to those with successful eradication (data not shown).

Preventive efficacy by treatment benefit versus by baseline GC susceptibility

We also compared the differences in preventive effect between the benefit-based H. pylori treatment approach and the risk-based approach. Compared with the risk-based approach, the benefit-based approach demonstrated superior performance in identifying individuals who derived stronger preventive effects from H. pylori eradication. In the MITS dataset, H. pylori treatment reduced GC risk more substantially (p-het < 0.001) in the high-treatment-benefit individuals (HR = 0.44, 95% CI 0.35–0.54; NNT = 56, 95% CI 44–83) than in the high-genetic-risk individuals (HR = 0.73, 95% CI 0.59–0.91; NNT = 117, 95% CI 67–991). These findings were replicated in the SIT dataset after 29.4 years of follow-up; the HR of H. pylori treatment was 0.20 (95% CI 0.10–0.41; NNT = 26, 95% CI 23–36) in the high-treatment-benefit targeting approach and 0.58 (95% CI 0.37–0.93; NNT = 47, 95% CI 32–181) in the high-genetic-risk targeting approach (p-het = 0.026; Table 2). Concordant results were derived when restricting the treatment group to individuals with successful H. pylori eradication (p-het < 0.001 for both MITS and SIT, Figures 3C and 3D).

Importantly, the benefit-based approach improved the identification of individuals likely to benefit regardless of their overall genetic risk. In the MITS, among the individuals targeted by the high-treatment-benefit approach, H. pylori treatment reduced GC risk to similar extents in both low- (HR = 0.47, 95% CI 0.34–0.64) and high-genetic-risk subgroups (HR = 0.38, 95% CI 0.28–0.51; p-het = 0.339). Comparable results were observed in SIT (HR = 0.17, 95% CI 0.07–0.45 for high genetic risk; HR = 0.25, 95% CI 0.08–0.76 for low genetic risk, p-het = 0.606; Figure 4).

Stratification of post-treatment residual risk of GC and gastric lesion progression

When restricting our analysis to individuals who received H. pylori treatment, we found that the PTBS also effectively stratified the subsequent risk of GC among these treated individuals. Compared with individuals with a moderate-treatment-benefit score, those classified as with high treatment benefit had significantly lower cumulative GC incidence post-treatment in the MITS (HR = 0.65, 95% CI 0.52–0.81) and the SIT cohorts (HR = 0.27, 95% CI 0.13–0.56; Figures 5A and 5B). This stratification was evident across genetic backgrounds, as high-treatment-benefit individuals had lower GC incidence than moderate-treatment-benefit individuals in both high- (HR = 0.63, 95% CI 0.46–0.86 in MITS; HR = 0.25, 95% CI 0.10–0.67 in SIT; Figures 5C and 5D) and low-genetic-risk (HR = 0.66, 95% CI 0.49–0.91 in MITS; HR = 0.27, 95% CI 0.09–0.81 in SIT; Figures 5E and 5F) groups, showing comparable effects between genetic-risk strata in both the MITS (p-het = 0.836) and the SIT (p-het = 0.920). The patterns remained robust when the treatment group was restricted to participants with confirmed successful eradication (Figure S6).

Beyond GC incidence, we examined whether benefit scores could capture the dynamics of precancerous lesion progression after individuals received H. pylori treatment. Using longitudinal endoscopic follow-up data from the SIT (Figure S7; Table S9), we found that high-treatment-benefit individuals experienced significantly slower progression of gastric lesions over 29.4 years of follow-up post-treatment compared with moderate-treatment-benefit individuals (coefficient for the interaction term = −0.161, p-for-interaction < 0.001; Figure 6A), with homogeneous patterns (p-het = 0.090) observed across the strata of both high-genetic-risk (coefficient for the interaction term = −0.194, p-for-interaction < 0.001, Figure 6B) and low-genetic-risk (coefficient for the interaction term = −0.124, p-for-interaction < 0.001, Figure 6C) individuals. Discussion

In this post hoc analysis of two RCTs, we identified genetic variants that modify the effectiveness of H. pylori treatment on GC prevention. Leveraging this genotype-specific HTE, we developed a composite PTBS to quantify individual-level preventive benefit in the discovery trial (MITS), with its performance evaluated in an independent validation trial (SIT) to assess reproducibility and generalizability. Our findings demonstrate that a benefit-based prevention strategy is potentially more effective than a risk-based approach in guiding targeted GC prevention. Furthermore, among individuals who have already received eradication therapy, the PTBS effectively distinguished individuals’ residual GC risk and the trajectories of precancerous gastric lesion progression, even for those already at high genetic risk, thus offering a novel framework for personalized post-treatment surveillance. Importantly, such a scoring system is not intended to replace existing guidelines but rather to provide a complementary framework for informing policy prioritization in settings with resource constraints or major concerns such as antibiotic resistance, as well as to support individual decision-making. Its primary value also lies in refining post-eradication risk stratification by identifying individuals who remain at substantial residual GC risk and who may benefit from intensified surveillance or additional preventive measures.

Our study implies that host genetic variants modulate the preventive effect of H. pylori treatment. Among these variants, several corresponding gene loci have already been linked to H. pylori infection and gastric carcinogenesis. Specifically, TRAF2 (9q34.3) encodes a required adaptor for H. pylori-induced NF-κB activation in gastric epithelial cells,26 and FGF18 (5q35.1) encodes an oncogenic ligand in GC through FGFR2-ERK-c-Jun-YAP1 signaling,27,28 a pathway that is activated during H. pylori infection. The variant rs9975448 in the locus of IFNAR2 (21q22.11) functions as a cis-eQTL, regulating IFNAR2 expression in gastric tissues and participating in the type I interferon pathway that is activated during H. pylori-induced gastric carcinogenesis.29,30 Additionally, H. pylori can trigger the Wnt/β-catenin signaling cascade in gastric epithelium. Although CSNK1A1L (13q13.3) itself lacks GC-specific studies, its parent family (casein-kinase-1) is a core regulator of Wnt signaling, often dysregulated in GC.31 Besides, several loci lacking direct evidence of association with H. pylori infection appear to exert broader effects through GC-related pathways, suggesting that the PTBS captures a wider spectrum of carcinogenic processes and thereby justifies its utility in stratifying GC risk, including residual risk post-eradication. For instance, CACNA2D3 (3p14.3) is often silenced by promoter methylation in GC and has been linked to a poorer prognosis.32 MCC (5q22.2) shows frequent loss in GC and is implicated early in neoplastic progression.33,34 RPTOR (17q25.3) functions as an mTORC1 scaffold, and germline variants at this locus have been associated with GC prognosis.35 In addition, signaling kinases such as PRKCA (17q24.2) and RPS6KA4 (11q13.1) contribute to the MAPK and PI3K pathways, which are commonly altered in GC.36,37

The progressive implementation of H. pylori screen-and-treat programs worldwide signifies significant international efforts to prevent GC. The MITS, a large-scale cluster RCT conducted by our team, confirmed the feasibility and long-term efficacy of population-based H. pylori screen-and-treat strategies.7 Public health initiatives are increasingly adopting H. pylori screen-and-treat strategies in GC high-risk settings such as the Matsu Islands,38,39 Japan,40 and Bhutan.41 Meanwhile, momentum has built in Europe following the adoption of Europe’s Beating Cancer Plan,2 with pilot projects such as EUROHELICAN (“accelerating gastric cancer reduction in Europe through H. pylori eradication”) and TOGAS (“towards gastric cancer screening implementation in the European Union”) continuously evaluating the feasibility, cost-effectiveness, and implementation pathways of population-based H. pylori eradication strategies across European countries.42 Compared with universal screening in all settings, the Maastricht VI/Florence Consensus report recommended population-based H. pylori intervention strategies for countries with intermediate-to-high GC incidence.43 The IARC 2025 Working Group report further advocated for a comprehensive assessment on readiness before implementing an H. pylori screen-and-treat program for GC prevention.4 These and other consensus reports have thereby recommended a population-targeted risk stratification strategy for H. pylori intervention,4,43,44,45 emphasizing the need for cost-effectiveness analysis to inform policy decisions in high-risk population subgroups. Since risk heterogeneity for H. pylori infection and GC exists across population subgroups, a risk-stratified intervention strategy targeting high-risk groups may better optimize resource allocation than universal intervention.4

While risk-based strategies for H. pylori screen-and-treat hold promise for guiding GC prevention, universally accepted criteria for defining the optimal target populations remain to be established, and evidence on long-term effectiveness across specific subgroups remains unclear.4 Subgroup analyses from RCTs suggest that factors such as age, sex, histopathology, and lifestyle exposures may influence preventive effects, considering that individuals have different baseline risks of GC.6,7,46,47 However, these factors are often prone to inconsistent findings due to limited statistical power and provide only modest value for tailoring interventions.10,11 Emerging evidence indicates that genetic predisposition can influence an individual’s baseline risk of GC and modify the preventive efficacy of H. pylori eradication.17,18,21 Nevertheless, past approaches using conventional polygenic scores share limitations, including restricted transferability across populations, reliance on relative rather than absolute risk, and limited interpretability at the individual level, which may hinder their application to targeting individuals most likely to benefit from H. pylori treatment in real-world scenarios.48 Moreover, their limited accuracy in identifying truly high-risk individuals may dilute the protective benefit achievable as expected within risk-based frameworks.49 To address these, we developed a treatment-benefit-based approach that aggregates genotype-specific CATEs to capture the anticipated benefits of H. pylori eradication. Crucially, this approach reflects both baseline disease risk and relative treatment efficacy, favoring individuals with more “room” for risk reduction through intervention,12 thereby providing a complementary perspective to conventional high-genetic-risk classifications.

We observed that the subgroup characterized by high treatment benefit demonstrated a more pronounced magnitude of risk reduction compared with the unstratified infected population or the high-genetic-risk subgroup. Importantly, this enhanced preventive efficacy was evident regardless of the established underlying genetic susceptibility, implying that the PTBS captured a dimension of intervention outcome distinct from baseline risk. In the benefit-based strategy, the NNT to prevent one GC case dropped significantly compared with that for the risk-based approach and that estimated from the recent meta-analyses on the RCTs,3,50 and the advantage strengthened with longer follow-up, with NNT declining from 56 at 11.8 years (MITS) to 24 at 29.4 years (SIT). These patterns were consistent across baseline age, sex, lifestyle, and age at GC onset subgroups and were particularly pronounced for non-cardia GC. Collectively, these results suggest that a benefit-based approach could refine the target population definition in H. pylori screen-and-treat programs, particularly by identifying those at intermediate baseline risk who may also achieve substantial preventive benefit yet are often overlooked by the genetic-risk-based approaches focusing on relative risk estimates.51 This approach can also support asymptomatic infected individuals without a family history of GC by offering individualized expectations of preventive benefit and helping them better understand what eradication may achieve and how to plan their follow-up care.

Within the PTBS framework, baseline risk and treatment effect modification are treated as distinct components, with baseline risk representing an individual’s underlying susceptibility to GC and treatment effect modification capturing inter-individual differences in the expected absolute risk reduction conditional on receiving eradication therapy. Although individuals at higher baseline risk of GC often achieve larger absolute risk reductions simply because more events are preventable,52 baseline risk alone does not fully determine treatment benefit. Substantial treatment effect heterogeneity can exist among individuals with similar underlying risks,12 reflecting differences in response to eradication therapy. The PTBS therefore serves a role complementary to the PRS, allowing individuals with comparable baseline susceptibility to be further stratified according to differential risk reductions rather than conflating treatment response with disease risk. In this context, a dual PRS-PTBS approach would help refine clinical decision-making when risk and benefit classifications do not align. Specifically, high-risk individuals with low PTBS may require intensive post-treatment surveillance after H. pylori eradication due to high residual risk, whereas moderate-risk individuals with high PTBS could be prioritized for primary H. pylori treatment to maximize the efficiency and absolute benefit of the intervention.

The benefit-based framework using the PTBS may be most relevant in GC high-incidence settings, where the absolute burden of GC is substantial but prevention resources are relatively limited.13 In such settings, phased implementation of the H. pylori screen-and-treat strategy could be considered under this framework to prioritize limited resources, particularly when genetic data are already being collected in existing screening programs or research cohorts. Of note, the PTBS is developed based on germline genome-wide genetic variants, which can be utilized for multiple purposes and across diverse settings. Moreover, the cost of genome-wide genotyping has declined markedly in recent years,53 making PTBS highly cost-efficient when embedded within broader genomic or GC screening initiatives.54 Indeed, a cost-effectiveness modeling study demonstrated that integrating polygenic risk evaluation into organized screening programs can improve efficiency particularly in the context of declining genotyping costs.55 Nonetheless, formal cost-effectiveness and implementation studies will be essential, particularly prior to the use of the benefit-based framework as a stand-alone strategy.55

A noteworthy finding from our study is that the clinical utility of the PTBS extends beyond predicting the magnitude of preventive efficacy to informing post-eradication risk stratification and surveillance triage. This observation further highlights the value of integrating this novel system into broader GC prevention and control scenarios. In clinical practice, successful H. pylori eradication does not reduce GC risk to zero, particularly for individuals whose gastric mucosa has passed a “point of no return.”4,56 This aligns with recent evidence that patients with elevated RIMS1 methylation levels in gastric mucosa had markedly increased residual GC risk after eradication, pointing to a subgroup that may require intensive endoscopic surveillance post-treatment.8 While a tiered prevention framework has been proposed to connect primary prevention of GC through H. pylori eradication with secondary prevention by endoscopic screening, practical tools to refine individual risk profiles subsequent to primary interventions remain limited.4 Accordingly, the PTBS serves as an effective stratification tool for the residual risk of GC after eradication therapy. Among treated individuals, those classified as moderate-treatment-benefit had a higher subsequent GC risk than high-treatment-benefit individuals, a difference that persisted even within the subgroup already at high genetic risk. This was further substantiated by longitudinal analysis of precancerous lesion trajectories, which revealed that high-treatment-benefit individuals experienced significantly slower lesion progression after eradication. Together, our findings highlight the clinical importance of benefit-based risk stratification for the expected tiered GC prevention, suggesting that individuals with moderate treatment benefit from H. pylori eradication, particularly those already with advanced gastric lesions, may require more intensive and frequent endoscopic surveillance after H. pylori treatment.

To our knowledge, this study is among the first to introduce a genetic-based, benefit-oriented stratification approach supported by two RCTs with long-term follow-up, offering a novel framework to tailor H. pylori screen-and-treat strategies and enhance cost-effectiveness for GC prevention. To date, while eight RCTs evaluating H. pylori treatment in GC prevention have released findings,5,39 our analysis uniquely incorporates two of these landmark trials.6,7 The PTBS provides absolute rather than relative thresholds. This feature would enhance transferability of the score across populations and increase practical utility for public health decision-making. While we applied the median threshold in this study as an exploratory choice to illustrate benefit heterogeneity, our sensitivity analyses confirmed that the superiority of the benefit-based over the risk-based approach was robust to different threshold definitions. Importantly, PTBS may also help connect primary and secondary GC prevention by informing both the prioritization of H. pylori eradication among individuals most likely to benefit and the identification of treated individuals who may remain at elevated residual risk and therefore warrant more intensive post-eradication surveillance. However, rather than serving as a prescriptive rule for practice, real-world implementation would require refinement and calibration of thresholds to population-specific characteristics. Several limitations merit consideration. First, the two trials, MITS and SIT, used by this study differed in study design, treatment regimens, and follow-up duration, which could affect the magnitude of effect estimates. Because the regimens differed, we could not determine whether the identified SNVs modify the efficacy of specific drug regimens. Second, the SIT was originally designed and powered for gastric lesion progression as its primary outcome rather than GC incidence, which may limit the efficiency of risk estimates in stratified analyses. Third, we prioritized candidate genomic regions to ensure biological plausibility, explicitly acknowledging that this hypothesis-driven approach implies a trade-off that may exclude potentially predictive variants in non-candidate regions compared with hypothesis-free screening. Fourth, as our analysis is restricted to GC prevention, a prediction of moderate-treatment-benefit does not diminish the immediate clinical value of eradication for conditions like peptic ulcers or dyspepsia.2 Finally, while the two trials were confined to Asian populations in a high-risk region, the identified variants are mostly common SNVs across diverse ancestries, supporting potential transferability but necessitating recalibration for population-specific linkage disequilibrium.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that host genetic variation modifies the preventive effect of H. pylori treatment on GC. Integrating genotype-specific heterogeneous treatment effects can enhance the precision of H. pylori screen-and-treat strategies by optimizing intervention efficiency and identifying individuals requiring intensive post-treatment surveillance. This approach may be useful in real-world scenarios where prevention resources are limited and prioritization of individuals most likely to benefit is needed.
