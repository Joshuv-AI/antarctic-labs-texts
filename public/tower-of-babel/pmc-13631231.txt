Increased maximum carbon release offsets half of the growth trend in annual land carbon sink
Authors: Yu Bai, Zheng Fu, Josep Peñuelas, Philippe Ciais, Fangyue Zhang, Paul C Stoy, Alessandro Cescatti, Stephen Sitch, Mirco Migliavacca, William K Smith, Weize Tang, Yiqi Luo, Yulin Shangguan, Kai Wang, Michael O’Sullivan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe terrestrial carbon sink has strengthened in recent decades through enhanced maximum CO2 uptake (MCU) and a longer carbon uptake period (CUP), whereas maximum CO2 release (MCR) remains less quantified. Using atmospheric inversion estimates and 16 terrestrial biosphere models, we show that MCU and MCR both increased significantly from 1993 to 2022, with MCU rising faster globally (0.18 ± 0.04 versus 0.07 ± 0.01 g C m−2 yr−1). MCU increases were widespread, whereas MCR increases were strongest at mid- to high latitudes. Enhanced MCU and CUP increased annual net ecosystem productivity by 0.31 ± 0.09 g C m−2 yr−1, but rising MCR offset 44 ± 20% of this gain, leaving a net increase of 0.18 ± 0.06 g C m−2 yr−1. Elevated CO2 dominates long-term increases, while climate variability contributes to spatial heterogeneity. These findings highlight the importance of accounting for carbon release in projections of future terrestrial sink strength.Subject terms: Climate-change ecology, Ecosystem ecology, Ecosystem ecology

Introduction

Terrestrial ecosystems act as major carbon sinks, offsetting approximately 30% of anthropogenic CO2 emissions1,2. The long-term increase in global terrestrial carbon uptake has mostly been attributed to enhanced peak photosynthetic activity and lengthened growing seasons, but the stability of this sink under ongoing climate change remains uncertain, particularly because the processes of both carbon uptake and release are being altered simultaneously3–9. How processes of carbon release have changed over recent decades and how these concurrent changes interact with processes of carbon uptake to influence the long-term dynamics and stability of terrestrial carbon sinks remain insufficiently quantified.

Previous studies have predominantly focused on metrics of carbon uptake, showing that increases in the maximum CO2 uptake (MCU, defined as the annual maximum of monthly net ecosystem productivity) and the carbon uptake period (CUP, Fig. S1a) have strongly contributed to the long-term enhancement of annual net ecosystem productivity (NEPannual)10–12. Even small relative changes in MCU are often indicative of substantial changes in annual net carbon uptake13–16. Short-term peak values in daily or weekly fluxes (e.g., net ecosystem productivity [NEP] or gross primary productivity [GPP]) correlate closely with their annual totals13. NEPannual covaries with peak light-saturated GPP at long-term European observational sites17. Similarly, many recent studies have also advanced our knowledge of how ecosystem phenology and thereby CUP influence the terrestrial carbon sinks4,11,18,19. Collectively, these studies consistently reported higher photosynthetic peaks and longer periods of carbon assimilation in many regions worldwide, both of which have contributed to strengthening the terrestrial carbon sink in recent decades20–22.

In contrast, trends in extreme carbon release, particularly the maximum CO2 release (MCR, defined as the magnitude of the annual minimum monthly NEP), remain insufficiently quantified, despite their potential importance in modulating long-term NEPannual dynamics (Fig. S1b). Carbon uptake and release processes are intrinsically linked, as increases in photosynthetic carbon assimilation can also stimulate ecosystem respiration through enhanced substrate supply23–25. Accordingly, rising vegetation productivity may enhance MCR, as higher GPP increases the supply of readily decomposable carbon, including root-derived compounds and dissolved organic carbon, which stimulate ecosystem respiration (ER)23–25. Meanwhile, intensifying warming and extreme events can amplify ER and thus MCR, especially through enhanced heterotrophic respiration and intensified disturbance regimes, especially in boreal and semi-arid ecosystems26–28. Moreover, increasing climatic variability, such as more frequent extreme heatwaves and droughts, has also been linked to intensified carbon release in many ecosystems29–31. These previous findings suggest that MCR magnitude may have increased in recent decades, potentially offsetting the positive effects of increases in MCU and the extension of CUP on NEPannual. However, large-scale, observation-constrained assessments of the global spatiotemporal patterns of MCR and its contribution to NEPannual trends remain limited. Likewise, the spatial heterogeneity and relative influence of MCU, CUP, and MCR on long-term carbon balance, as well as the evolving balance between carbon uptake and release processes, have yet to be systematically quantified.

Atmospheric inversions and dynamic global vegetation models now allow large-scale estimation of terrestrial carbon dynamics30,32–34. NEP is the balance between GPP and ER, both of which are regulated by environmental factors such as light, temperature, precipitation, and atmospheric CO2 concentration ([CO2])35–37. Positive NEP indicates a carbon sink, whereas negative NEP indicates a carbon source. Physiology-related (MCU, MCR) and phenology-related (CUP) indicators provide a framework for characterizing changes in NEPannual, as environmental drivers affect NEPannual indirectly by regulating these indicators15,38. Understanding the long-term trajectories of MCU, MCR, and CUP, and their environmental controls, is thus essential for predicting the stability of terrestrial carbon sinks.

Here, we integrate global monthly NEP from multiple sources: two atmospheric-inversion NEP datasets (Jena CarboScope [Jena] and Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service [CAMS]) and an ensemble of simulations from 16 terrestrial surface models participating in the TRENDY v12 project. Using these datasets, we assess recent global trends in MCU, CUP, and MCR, and their relative impacts on NEPannual. Specifically, we aim to: (1) quantify global trends in MCU, CUP, and MCR, (2) evaluate the influence of MCR trends on NEPannual, (3) determine the relative contributions of MCU, CUP, and MCR to NEPannual trends, and (4) identify the roles of key climatic factors and atmospheric CO2 in regulating MCU and MCR dynamics.

Results

Global trends in MCU, CUP and MCR

All three datasets consistently show increasing MCU from 1993 to 2022, at rates of 0.12 ± 0.003 (± standard error), 0.25 ± 0.010, and 0.16 ± 0.002 g C m−2 yr−1 for Jena, CAMS, and the TRENDY ensemble (all p < 0.001; Fig. 1a). Positive MCU trends were widespread, covering approximately 75 ± 9% of vegetated land surface, particularly in southeastern China and northern South America (Fig. 1d, g, j). A significant lengthening trend was also observed for CUP, increasing by 0.005–0.009 months yr−1 across the three datasets (p < 0.05; Fig. 1b, e, h, k). NEPannual showed a consistent increasing trend across datasets, with rates of 0.13 ± 0.024, 0.40 ± 0.044, and 0.37 ± 0.009 g C m−2 yr−1 for Jena, CAMS, and TRENDY, respectively (Fig. S2a). This increase was spatially widespread, occurring in 61 ± 5% of terrestrial grid cells, ranging from 55% for Jena to 71% for TRENDY (Fig. S2b–d).

In parallel to MCU increase and CUP lengthening, the magnitude of MCR also increased significantly over the last three decades, at rates of 0.08 ± 0.004, 0.09 ± 0.005, and 0.05 ± 0.001 g C m−2 yr−1 for Jena, CAMS, and TRENDY, respectively (all p < 0.001; Fig. 1c). Positive MCR trends dominated 59, 61, and 77% of terrestrial grid cells for Jena, CAMS, and TRENDY, respectively (Fig. 1f, i, l), with significant increases (p < 0.05) in 39, 34, and 31% of grid cells. In contrast, significant decreases in MCR were limited to only 10 ± 4% of the pixels. The strongest increases occurred north of 40°N in Europe and northern Asia, while decreases were concentrated in northern South America and central Africa (Fig. 1f, i, l). We noted differences in the absolute magnitudes of MCU and MCR trends among the three NEP datasets, with MCU trends ranging from 0.12 to 0.25 g C m−2 yr−1 and MCR trends from 0.05 to 0.09 g C m−2 yr−1. Despite these differences, the strong consistency across the three independent datasets (Jena, CAMS, and TRENDY) strengthens confidence in our conclusions. We further performed the same analysis using long-term monthly eddy covariance observations (Methods; Table S1). The magnitudes of MCU, CUP, and MCR trends were generally comparable across site observations and Jena, CAMS, and TRENDY (Kruskal–Wallis test, p > 0.05; Fig. S3). In addition, we repeated the analysis using daily eddy covariance observations to further test the consistency of our results. Among sites exhibiting statistically significant trends, both MCU and MCR were predominantly characterized by increasing trends (Fig. S4), reinforcing the agreement across datasets and temporal scales.

To examine whether these trends have shifted over time, we divided the 30-year period into two equal halves (1993–2007 and 2008–2022). MCU trends were stronger in the first half, whereas MCR trends intensified in the second (Fig. S5a, b), indicating contrasting temporal trajectories and relative rates of change between carbon uptake- and release-related processes. This temporal divergence suggests a recent slowdown in the increase of NEPannual. Quadratic fitting further showed declining MCU trends across mid- to high-latitude regions of the Northern Hemisphere and accelerated MCR trends in central Africa and South America (Fig. S5c–h), further highlighting spatial heterogeneity in the relative rates of change between MCU- and MCR-related processes. Such divergence likely reflects differing sensitivities of uptake and release processes to environmental drivers such as [CO2] and water availability, as well as broader differences in the temporal dynamics of photosynthesis and respiration processes. Multi-year means values of MCU, CUP, MCR, and NEPannual over 1993–2022 showed distinct spatial patterns (Figs. S6 and S7). Mean MCU was consistently higher in mid- to high-latitudes of the Northern Hemisphere (Fig. S6a, d, g), whereas mean MCR was elevated in Europe, central South America, and central Africa and lower in Australia and Russia (Fig. S6b, e, h). NEPannual largely mirrored the spatial distribution of MCU, with higher values at mid- and high latitudes regions (Fig. S7). We further analyzed the spatial and temporal patterns of the MCU-to-MCR ratio to characterize the balance between uptake- and release-related changes (Figs. S8 and S9). The ratio was higher in mid- and high-latitude regions and lower in the tropics. While the global median remained stable over time, regional trends diverged, decreasing in North America and increasing in Central Africa, indicating a spatially heterogeneous but broadly stable carbon balance.

Rising MCR partly offsets NEPannual gains from increased MCU and CUP

Our analysis shows that increasing magnitude of MCR substantially reduces the magnitude of the positive NEPannual trend driven by increases in MCU and the extension of CUP. To quantify the compensation effect, we established two scenarios using a decomposition framework: a control scenario, where NEPannual was reconstructed based on all three components (MCU, MCR, and CUP), and a treatment scenario, where MCR was held constant at its value in the first year to isolate its contribution (Methods). The reconstructed NEPannual trends closely matched the original datasets (r = 0.92–0.97, p < 0.001; Fig. 2a, d, g and S2b–d; S10), demonstrating that the three components (MCU, MCR, and CUP) effectively capture the trends in NEPannual. NEPannual trends were consistently higher when MCR held constant, indicating a pronounced offsetting effect of carbon release (Fig. 2c, f, i). Globally, median NEPannual trends decreased from 0.28, 0.18, and 0.48 g C m−2 yr−1 (treatment) to 0.12, 0.12, and 0.29 g C m−2 yr−1 (control) for Jena, CAMS, and TRENDY, respectively (Fig. 2). On average, increasing magnitude of MCR offset 0.11 ± 0.03 g C m−2 yr−1 (0.06, 0.15, and 0.13 g C m−2 yr−1 for Jena, CAMS, and TRENDY), accounting for 44 ± 20% of potential increase in NEPannual trend driven by MCU and CUP. The offsets were more pronounced in northern mid- to high latitudes (Fig. 2c, f, i). The magnitude of MCU and MCR trends were positively correlated across all three datasets (Fig. S11), but their relative rates of change varied substantially across regions and time periods, highlighting heterogeneous responses of carbon uptake and release processes. Consistent with these gridded analyses, an independent decomposition analysis based on flux tower observations also showed that increasing magnitude of MCR partially offset the enhancement of NEPannual driven by increasing MCU and CUP (Fig. S12).

Spatial contributions of MCU, CUP and MCR to NEPannual trends

We also quantified the relative contributions of the three components to NEPannual trends using an attribution analysis (Methods, Fig. 3). Trends in MCU exerted a positive influence on NEPannual trends in 75 ± 7% of terrestrial ecosystems, particularly across the Northern Hemisphere (Fig. 3a, d, g). MCU- dominated regions largely coincided with areas showing increasing NEPannual (81, 70, and 81% for Jena, CAMS, and TRENDY, respectively; Fig. 3j–l). In contrast, rising MCR drove NEPannual declines in 66 ± 5% of grid cells, especially at mid- to high-latitudes (Fig. 3b, e, h). Within MCR-dominated regions, NEPannual decreased in more than 60% of the grid cells (Fig. 3j–l), showing the substantial offsetting effects of MCR on the annual land carbon sink. The corresponding absolute trends in NEPannual attributable to MCU, MCR, and CUP further revealed that MCU-driven increases in NEPannual were strongest across northern high-latitude ecosystems, whereas MCR-driven reductions in NEPannual were particularly pronounced in many mid-latitude and semi-arid regions (Fig. S13). To further illustrate the combined influences of the three components, we used a composite visualization to simultaneously represent the relative contributions of MCU, MCR, and CUP to long-term NEPannual trends (Fig. S14). The composite maps show widespread MCU dominance across northern high-latitude ecosystems, whereas stronger MCR influences emerge in many semi-arid and mid-latitude regions. CUP generally plays a secondary role globally but exhibits regionally important contributions in tropical and Southern Hemisphere ecosystems.

When stratified by vegetation type, our analysis identified MCU as the leading factor behind positive NEPannual trends in 67% of forests, 65% of grasslands, and 61% of croplands (Figs. 3j–l and S15). MCR contributed 17%, 20%, and 21%, while CUP accounted for the remaining 16%, 15%, and 19% of positive trends in these vegetation types. Conversely, in regions where NEPannual declined, MCR was the dominant contributor, responsible for roughly half of the decreases (50, 51, and 48% of the grid cells in forests, grasslands, and croplands, respectively; Fig. 3j–l). MCU and CUP contributed the remaining portions, accounting for 31% and 19% of the NEPannual decreases in forests, 35% and 14% in grasslands, and 34% and 18% in croplands, respectively (Fig. 3j–l).

Elevated [CO2] dominates MCU and MCR trends, while climate variability governs regional heterogeneity

We further attributed trends in MCU and MCR to environmental factors using ridge regression and TRENDY factorial experiments (Methods). Across all three datasets, rising [CO2] emerged as the dominant driver of long-term increases in both MCU and MCR, accounting for the largest fraction of grid cells for uptake (49 ± 8%) and release trends (48 ± 1%) (Fig. 4a, b). In contrast, climatic variables contributed more comparably and heterogeneously. For MCU trends, precipitation, temperature, and solar radiation explained 20 ± 10, 18 ± 2, and 12 ± 1% of the dominant areas, respectively. Similar contributions were observed for MCR trends, averaging 17 ± 9, 20 ± 3, and 14 ± 1% for precipitation, temperature, and solar radiation, respectively.

Factorial experiments with TRENDY confirmed these findings. Comparing simulations with varying [CO2] only (S1) versus no forcing (S0), and varying [CO2] plus climate (S2) versus S1, showed that [CO2] exerted a much stronger influence on MCU and MCR trends than climate (Fig. 4c–g). MCU trends were slightly negative (–0.02 ± 0.02 g C m−2 yr−1) in S0, whereas in S1, [CO2] alone strongly enhanced MCU trends (0.10 ± 0.02 g C m−2 yr−1). Adding climate change (S2) raised the trend to 0.13 ± 0.01 g C m−2 yr−1, with minimal additional effects from including land-cover change (S3: 0.16 ± 0.01 g C m−2 yr−1; Fig. 4c). MCR showed a similar but distinct pattern: trends were 0.003 ± 0.01 g C m−2 yr−1 under S0, 0.03 ± 0.01 under S1, 0.06 ± 0.01 under S2, and 0.05 ± 0.01 g C m−2 yr−1 under S3 (Fig. 4c). Comparisons across scenarios demonstrated that [CO2] had a much stronger effect on MCU and MCR trends (Figs. 4d–g and S16). Specifically, global median MCU trends were 0.12 g C m−2 yr−1 under [CO2]-only forcing versus 0.03 g C m−2 yr−1 under climate-only forcing; corresponding values for MCR were 0.03 and 0.02 g C m−2 yr−1, respectively (Fig. 4d–g). All median trends were significantly different from zero (p < 0.001, Wilcoxon signed-rank test), and the [CO2]-driven trends were significantly larger than those driven by climate forcing (p < 0.001, Kruskal–Wallis test). Spatially, [CO2] effects on MCU were positive almost globally, particularly across the mid- to high latitudes of the Northern Hemisphere, whereas climate effects were more heterogeneous, positive in western Russia and northern Canada but negative in central North America and southern Europe (Fig. 4d, f). For MCR, [CO2]-driven trends remained predominantly positive across Northern Hemisphere mid- to high latitudes, while negative trends emerged in northern South America, central Africa, and Southeast Asia; in contrast, climate change contributed positively in southern Europe and South America (Fig. 4e, g). Together, these results suggest that rising [CO2] primarily governs long-term increases in MCU and MCR, whereas regional climate variability contributed more strongly to spatial heterogeneity in MCU and MCR trends. These patterns were broadly consistent across TRENDY model with different nutrient-cycle representations (Figs. S17–S19). Our analyses suggested that phosphorus-enabled model (E3SM) showed stronger [CO2]-driven MCR responses than models without explicit phosphorus constraints, especially in tropical rainforests (Figs. S17–S20). This difference may reflect the role of nutrient–carbon interactions in regulating ecosystem responses to elevated CO2, as phosphorus availability can influence carbon assimilation, allocation, and biomass accumulation in phosphorus-limited ecosystems39–43. However, because only one model explicitly represents both nitrogen and phosphorus cycling, these differences may also reflect other structural characteristics among ecosystem models. Additional sensitivity analyses, in which environmental variables were detrended and climate conditions were re-evaluated based on the peak month of MCU or MCR and its adjacent months, revealed an enhanced apparent influence of climate variability, particularly temperature, on MCU and MCR trends (Figs. S21 and S22). This suggests a stronger role of climate variability in regulating interannual and intra-seasonal fluctuations of MCU and MCR.

Discussion

We assessed long-term trends in MCU, MCR, and CUP over the past three decades. By quantifying their relative contributions to NEPannual and attributing changes to climatic drivers and rising atmospheric CO2, we reveal how concurrent shifts in carbon uptake and release processes regulate the dynamics of terrestrial carbon sinks in a warming world. We found a pronounced global intensification of both MCU and MCR, accompanied by a widespread extension of the CUP (Fig. 5a). The increases in MCU and CUP enhanced NEPannual, with MCU primarily determining the magnitude of carbon uptake and CUP extending the duration over which ecosystems act as carbon sinks, yet these gains were substantially offset by a concurrent rise in MCR, which reduced the net strengthening of land carbon sinks (Fig. 5b). Decomposition analysis confirmed MCU as the primary positive driver of NEPannual (Fig. 5c), highlighting the central role of peak vegetation activity in sustaining terrestrial carbon sequestration, consistent with previous findings emphasizing the importance of maximum photosynthetic capacity and phenological extension in reinforcing carbon sinks14,20,44,45.

However, substantial regional increases in MCR, particularly across mid- and high-latitude ecosystems, indicate a growing counterforce to enhanced MCU. Multiple mechanisms likely underpin these trends. First, carbon uptake and release processes are tightly coupled: higher GPP supplies more labile substrates, such as root exudates and dissolved organic carbon, that fuel microbial respiration23–25. Second, warming-induced acceleration of heterotrophic respiration could amplify MCR, though this effect can be constrained by soil moisture limitations46–48. Moreover, autumn cooling across northern ecosystems has reduced GPP more than respiration, enhancing net carbon release49. In addition, other factors that could be driving non-linear increases in MCR include increasing climate variability and intensified disturbances. Increasing climatic variability, such as more frequent extreme heatwaves and droughts, has promoted carbon release in many ecosystems29–31. Boreal and humid ecosystems could be particularly vulnerable to these events50–52. Human-driven land-use changes, including cropland expansion, biomass harvesting53,54, and fire disturbance55,56, further exacerbate MCR by depleting soil carbon and accelerating erosion57,58. These processes may also interact with phenological dynamics, as shifts in the timing and CUP can modulate the seasonal patterns of carbon uptake and release.

The ecological implications of rising MCR are profound. Globally, the magnitude of MCR increases offset up to 44% of the NEPannual gains driven by enhanced MCU and CUP. Across the Northern Hemisphere, trends in MCU have generally weakened relative to the intensifying MCR, further exacerbating the reduction of terrestrial carbon sinks. Consequently, the benefits of CO2 fertilization could be overestimated if assessments consider only increases in GPP, MCU, or CUP without accounting for concurrent rises in MCR. Intensified peaks of carbon release are thus becoming a key driver of declining net ecosystem carbon uptake, as warming intensifies respiration and disturbances become more frequent50,59–61. This dynamic suggests that ecosystems may approach a quasi-equilibrium, where long-term net carbon fluxes stabilize rather than continue to rise or decline62,63. Yet this equilibrium will be uneven: regional variation in climate, disturbance regimes, and land management ensures divergent carbon sink trajectories. Some ecosystems, such as boreal forests in Siberia and temperate forests in central Europe, could shift from net sinks to sources if MCR outpaces MCU and CUP1,64. Anticipating these transitions will require integrating long-term observations with process-based models that explicitly represent climate–microbe–vegetation interactions, thereby improving constraints on carbon sink trajectories and identifying early warning signals of sink-to-source shifts.

Because our data were available only at monthly resolution, CUP was assessed in months rather than days, which precluded detailed temporal analyses typically conducted at daily scales. Our analysis indicated that elevated [CO2] was the dominant factor associated with increases in MCU and MCR (Fig. 5d). Elevated [CO2] enhances plant growth through two primary mechanisms: (1) stimulating carboxylation while reducing photorespiration65–67, and (2) reducing stomatal conductance and improving water-use efficiency68. Elevated [CO2] directly stimulates MCU through enhanced photosynthesis, leading to increased plant productivity and an indirect promotion of MCR by providing greater substrate availability for respiration. This [CO2]-driven effect on MCR, however, is increasingly less important than the intensifying impacts of climatic extremes, particularly higher temperatures, prolonged droughts, and variable precipitation. These stressors have become the dominant regulators of carbon release in regions where carbon sinks are weakening, especially across boreal, temperate, and semi-arid ecosystems46–48. While high temperature and drought could suppress photosynthesis, they can induce delayed respiration surges through decomposition of accumulated organic matter69. Other unquantified factors, including nitrogen deposition and land management practices (e.g., biomass harvesting), can also contribute to long-term changes in MCU and MCR and warrant further investigation. We also noted that atmospheric inversion datasets carry inherent uncertainties, particularly in regions with sparse observational coverage (Fig. S23). These uncertainties, together with atmospheric transport effects and the spatial integration of CO2 signals, may introduce biases19. Nevertheless, the strong consistency across the three independent datasets (Jena, CAMS, and TRENDY) enhances confidence in the robustness of our conclusions.

The varying MCU and MCR sensitivities to elevated [CO2] across TRENDY models further suggest that nutrient-cycle representation may influence simulated CO2 fertilization effects. E3SM, the only model in our ensemble representing both nitrogen and phosphorus cycling, showed stronger CO2-driven increases in MCR than models without explicit phosphorus cycling, particularly in tropical regions. This difference may reflect phosphorus-mediated regulation of carbon allocation, biomass accumulation, and ecosystem carbon storage under elevated CO240–42. However, because this behavior is represented by a single model, it cannot be attributed uniquely to phosphorus cycling and may also reflect other model-specific structural characteristics. In contrast, models incorporating nitrogen cycling alone did not show consistently different CO2 responses from models without explicit nitrogen constraints. Although nitrogen limitation can constrain CO2 fertilization by increasing nutrient demand under enhanced carbon uptake, its effects are likely to depend on interactions with other model formulations, including carbon allocation and nutrient-feedback structures70–72.

Collectively, our findings demonstrate that while much attention has been disproportionally paid to the strengthening of carbon uptake and phenology, the parallel accelerating rise in MCR underscores that carbon release dynamics are also increasingly governed by the balance and coupling between uptake and release processes. Accurately representing these release processes, and their sensitivity to CO2 fertilization, climate extremes, and disturbances, will be essential for improving model predictions of carbon–climate feedbacks and for developing effective strategies to preserve and enhance terrestrial carbon sinks under accelerating global change.

Methods

Atmospheric inversion NEP datasets

We used two global atmospheric inversion NEP datasets. The first dataset was the Jena CarboScope atmospheric CO2 inversion (version v2023, hereafter Jena), a widely used observation-based estimate of NEP73. Jena estimates spatiotemporal fluxes of CO2 exchange by linking surface CO2 fluxes to atmospheric CO2 mole fractions through the TM3 atmospheric transport model, driven by the National Centers for Environmental Prediction reanalysis data74,75. Flux estimates are optimized to minimize the mismatch between simulated and observed CO2 concentrations via a quadratic cost function. The resulting NEP dataset provides daily fluxes at a 2.5° × 2° spatial resolution for 1993–2022. For consistency with other datasets, daily Jena NEP values were aggregated to monthly scale.

The second dataset was the Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service product (version v22r1_surface, hereafter CAMS). CO2 fluxes were estimated using the LMDZ (Laboratoire de Météorologie Dynamique Zoom) atmospheric transport model, driven by ERA5 meteorological fields from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts, and optimized using a Bayesian inversion framework76,77. CAMS provides monthly CO2 fluxes from 1993 to 2022 at a 2.5° × 1.27° spatial resolution.

Although both Jena and CAMS are widely used to estimate total land carbon fluxes, inversion-derived NEP represents the net land-atmosphere CO2 exchange74,76. It integrates both biological fluxes and non-biological components such as biomass burning and lateral carbon transfers (e.g., riverine export, trade-related carbon flows)78. Thus, NEP from inversion datasets reflects total net carbon exchange between land and atmosphere, encompassing both ecosystem and anthropogenic contributions79.

NEP from TRENDY models

We also used monthly NEP outputs from 16 Dynamic Global Vegetation Models participating in the TRENDY project (v12), including CABLE-POP, CLASSIC, CLM5.0, E3SM, EDv3, IBIS, ISAM, ISBA-CTRIP, JSBACH, JULES, LPJwsl, LPX-Bern, OCN, ORCHIDEE, VISIT and YIBs80. The TRENDY ensemble includes models with differing representations of key processes, including nutrient limitations (e.g., nitrogen and phosphorus cycles), plant allocation, turnover, and disturbances, which can affect the magnitude of simulated CO2 fertilization effects (Supplementary Table 2). To further assess the potential influence of nutrient limitations on simulated MCU and MCR responses, TRENDY models were additionally separated into three groups according to their nutrient-cycle schemes: models including both nitrogen and phosphorus cycling, models including nitrogen cycling only, and models without explicit nitrogen or phosphorus constraints. These NEP simulations correspond to the TRENDYv12 S3 scenario, which includes time-varying CO2, climate change, and land-cover change. NEP from the TRENDY ensemble were standardized to a 1° × 1° spatial resolution. To isolate the effects of different environmental factors, three additional TRENDY scenarios were also analyzed: S0 (no forcing), S1 (varying CO2 only), and S2 (varying CO2 and climate change).

Definitions and calculations for MCU, MCR, CUP, and NEPannual

At each grid, we decomposed NEPannual into three components: maximum CO2 uptake (MCU), maximum CO2 release (MCR), and carbon uptake period (CUP, Fig. S1a). Positive NEP values denote ecosystem carbon uptake (carbon sink), whereas negative values indicate carbon release (carbon source). NEPannual was calculated as the sum of monthly NEP for each calendar year. MCU and MCR are defined as the maximum and minimum monthly NEP values within a year, respectively, with MCR calculated as the negative of the minimum NEP (i.e., multiplied by −1) to represent the magnitude of carbon release as a positive value. CUP was defined as the number of months per year when NEP remained positive. MCU reflects the highest level of ecosystem carbon uptake in a year, while MCR represents the maximum ecosystem carbon loss. The use of monthly data ensures consistency across global atmospheric inversion products and multi-model ensembles. For each year from 1993 to 2022, we calculated MCU, MCR, CUP, and NEPannual at each grid cell using monthly NEP from Jena, CAMS, and the TRENDY ensemble. In the mid-to-high latitudes of the Northern Hemisphere, MCR generally occurs around September, while in the Amazon basin and central Africa, it typically occurs between January and April (Fig. S24). We note that MCU and MCR represent integrated properties of ecosystem carbon balance and should not be interpreted as direct measures for gross primary productivity (GPP) or ecosystem respiration (ER).

Eddy covariance observations

We used long-term eddy covariance observations from FLUXNET Shuttle, which provides standardized flux data across multiple regional networks, including AmeriFlux, the Integrated Carbon Observation System (ICOS), OzFlux, and AsiaFlux, to perform the same analysis83–86. Only high-quality records with NEE_VUT_REF_QC ≥ 0.8 were retained, and sites with at least 15 years of valid observations were included in the analysis, resulting in a total of 42 sites (Supplementary Table 1). MCU, MCR, and CUP were calculated following the same definitions and procedures as those applied to the gridded datasets. For each site, long-term trends in MCU, MCR, and CUP were estimated using Sen’s slope estimator and the Mann–Kendall significance test, consistent with the approach used for the gridded datasets. To further test the independence of our findings, we additionally quantified maximum gross primary productivity (GPPmax) and maximum ecosystem respiration (ERmax) from daily observations (GPP_NT_VUT_REF and RECO_NT_VUT_REF). We found that GPPmax and ERmax were significantly correlated with MCU and MCR, respectively, and exhibited consistent positive trends across sites (Fig. S25). We noted that available long-term flux-tower observations are predominantly located in temperate and boreal regions of North America and Europe, whereas observations in tropical regions remain limited. Therefore, this validation provides observational support for the applicability of MCU and MCR at available flux sites, but further observations from tropical regions are needed to assess their robustness across a broader range of biomes.

Attribution of MCU and MCR trends to environmental factors

We used ridge regression to quantify the effects of four key environmental variables, atmospheric CO2 concentration ([CO2]), precipitation, temperature, and solar radiation (via cloud cover), on MCU and MCR trends. Given the potential collinearity among environmental variables and the presence of shared long-term trends, ridge regression was employed to mitigate multicollinearity among covariates and to enable a more reliable evaluation of the relative importance of each factor87,88. The selected predictors were chosen to represent the primary first-order controls on MCU and MCR trends at the global scale, including atmospheric CO2 concentration (CO2 fertilization), precipitation (water supply), temperature (thermal regulation), and solar radiation (energy availability). These variables provide an effective representation of major environmental constraints on MCU and MCR trends. Temperature, precipitation, and cloud-cover data (0.5° × 0.5°) were obtained from the Climate Research Unit (CRU TS 4.08) dataset89, and monthly [CO2] was derived from the Mauna Loa Observatory. All predictors were resampled using bilinear interpolation to match the resolution of Jena, CAMS, and TRENDY data. Variables were standardized (z-score normalization) before regression to ensure comparability. Standardized coefficients were used to quantify the relative importance of each driver.

We also used TRENDY factorial experiments to quantify the effects of [CO2] and climate change on MCU and MCR trends. Four simulation scenarios were analyzed: S0 (no forcing), S1 (varying CO2 only), S2 (varying CO2 and climate change), and S3 (varying CO2, climate change, and land-cover change). Trends in MCU and MCR were first calculated for each scenario. The impact of [CO2] was isolated by comparing S1 with S0, whereas the effect of climate change was isolated by comparing S2 with S1. These comparisons allowed attribution of MCU and MCR trends to individual environmental drivers.
