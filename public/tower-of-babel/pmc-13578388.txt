Thoracic endovascular aortic repair for iatrogenic descending aortic pseudoaneurysm after aborted transcatheter aortic valve implantation
Authors: Federico Bargagna, Marta Casula, Marcello Ravani, Anees Al Jabri, Sergio Berti, Antonio Rizza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Iatrogenic aortic injury is a rare but life-threatening complication of transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI). Descending thoracic aortic pseudoaneurysms are particularly uncommon and challenging to manage. We report an 88-year-old woman with prior type A dissection treated with a Bentall procedure and Carpentier-Edwards Perimount bioprosthesis, who developed severe valve stenosis. An initial transfemoral TAVI failed due to unfavorable descending thoracic aortic anatomy. One year later, angiography revealed a descending thoracic aortic pseudoaneurysm, which was likely related to the prior procedure. Given the high surgical risk, staged management was performed: thoracic endovascular aortic repair followed by successful transapical valve-in-valve TAVI.

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has become the standard of care for patients with severe aortic stenosis who are aged >70 years.1 Despite continuous improvements in device technology and procedural techniques, iatrogenic aortic injury (IAI) remains a rare but potentially catastrophic complication. Although recent studies of TAVI in patients with a low surgical risk do not report this complication, the incidence rate in major registries ranges from 0.02% to 1.9%.2,3 Most TAVI-related aortic injuries involve the aortic root or ascending aorta (Stanford type A), typically resulting from direct trauma from the delivery system, aggressive oversizing, or manipulation of the guidewire.4 In contrast, injuries to the descending thoracic aorta (DTA, Stanford type B), particularly those presenting as pseudoaneurysm formation, are extremely rare and may manifest late.5 The management of these complications is particularly challenging in patients with prior aortic surgery or chronic aortic disease, in whom altered anatomy and intrinsic aortic wall fragility further complicate treatment. Here, we report a case of a delayed DTA pseudoaneurysm that was likely related to catheter manipulation during an aborted transfemoral TAVI attempt, which was successfully managed with a staged, fully endovascular strategy.

Written informed consent for publication was obtained from the patient.

Case presentation

An 88-year-old woman was referred for the management of the severe structural deterioration of a surgical aortic bioprosthesis. In 2007, she had undergone emergency surgery for Stanford type A aortic dissection, which was treated with a Bentall procedure using a Carpentier-Edwards Perimount aortic bioprosthesis. Her medical history included permanent atrial fibrillation on chronic anticoagulation, hypertension, obesity, and a history of breast cancer treated with chemotherapy and radiotherapy. In June 2024, the patient underwent a transfemoral TAVI attempt using a self-expanding Medtronic Evolut FX 29-mm valve. Advancement of the delivery system was unsuccessful because of severe tortuosity of the DTA and unfavorable horizontal orientation of the neo-aortic plane. Valve implantation was aborted, and balloon aortic valvuloplasty was performed during the same hospitalization as a palliative measure. No postprocedural computed tomography angiography (CTA) of the thoracic aorta was obtained, as the patient remained clinically stable and no signs of aortic injury were suspected at that time. The patient was discharged with planned clinical follow-up. In October 2025, the patient was re-evaluated for worsening dyspnea (New York Heart Association functional class III) in the setting of severe aortic stenosis due to the degeneration of the surgical bioprosthesis. Considering the patient’s clinical status and the high surgical risk (Society of Thoracic Surgeons Predicted Risk of Mortality: 14.5%), the Heart Team recommended to repeat TAVI. In January 2026, the preprocedural CTA revealed proximal DTA dissection associated with a large pseudoaneurysm measuring 56 × 57 mm, consistent with a contained rupture located in Ishimaru zone 4 (Fig 1). The findings were highly suggestive of delayed IAI occurring during the previous transfemoral TAVI attempt. A retrospective review of the preprocedural CT scan performed before the first TAVI attempt (June 2024) showed no evidence of dissection, pseudoaneurysm, or focal lesion of the DTA. Given the patient’s high surgical risk and the unstable nature of the lesion, on the following day, the patient underwent thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) as the first step of a staged transcatheter strategy (Fig 2). A thoracic stent-graft (Terumo Aortic, 40 × 36 × 200 mm) was successfully deployed in the DTA (zones 3 and 4) via the right femoral artery, achieving complete exclusion of the pseudoaneurysm. Completion angiography confirmed accurate device positioning without endoleak. Following clinical stabilization, transapical valve-in-valve TAVI was performed a few days later under general anesthesia through a left mini-thoracotomy. An Edwards Sapien 3 Ultra 23-mm balloon-expandable valve was implanted within the degenerated surgical prosthesis (Fig 3). The transapical approach was intentionally chosen to avoid further manipulation of the DTA and the recently treated segment. Final angiographic and echocardiographic assessments demonstrated optimal valve function. Discussion

IAI during TAVI is rare, with the reported incidence rate ranging from 0.02% to 1.9% in large registries.2,3 Most injuries involve the ascending aorta or aortic root, whereas involvement of the DTA is distinctly uncommon and only sporadically reported, particularly when presenting as delayed pseudoaneurysm formation. The present case illustrates a rare instance of delayed DTA pseudoaneurysm formation following an aborted transfemoral TAVI attempt. The novelty lies not in the use of TEVAR, which is an established treatment for complicated type B aortic lesions, but in the mechanism and timing of presentation. The absence of detectable aortic injury at the time of the index procedure, followed by late pseudoaneurysm formation, suggests a delayed evolution of an initially unrecognized iatrogenic wall injury. This delayed presentation of IAI likely reflects a mechanism of subintimal or contained aortic wall disruption that may initially remain clinically silent. Progressive weakening of the aortic wall over time can subsequently lead to pseudoaneurysm formation or contained rupture, particularly in patients with chronically diseased or surgically altered aortas. From a clinical perspective, this case highlights that apparent stability after technically challenging or unsuccessful transfemoral TAVI may be misleading. In such scenarios, especially in the presence of hostile aortic anatomy or prior aortic surgery, early postprocedural CTA may be considered to detect occult aortic injury and prevent delayed, potentially life-threatening complications. Once a pseudoaneurysm or contained rupture is identified, conservative management is inappropriate because of the high risk of rupture. According to the 2024 European Society of Cardiology Guidelines, TEVAR is the recommended treatment for complicated type B aortic injuries, offering an effective and minimally invasive alternative to open surgery in high-risk patients.6 In the present case, TEVAR enabled prompt exclusion of the lesion and stabilization of the aorta. To minimize the risk of further aortic trauma, definitive valve implantation was subsequently performed through a transapical approach, thereby completely avoiding instrumentation of the DTA and the recently treated segment. This staged, fully transcatheter strategy enabled the safe completion of the procedure in a patient with a high surgical risk. Previous reports have demonstrated the feasibility of endovascular repair in the setting of IAI during TAVI. For example, Kilic et al7 previously described the feasibility of concomitant endografting during TAVI for iatrogenic dissections, emphasizing that immediate endovascular sealing can reverse a potentially fatal outcome. In contrast, the present case emphasizes that aortic injury may also manifest in a delayed manner, reinforcing the need for vigilance even after an apparently uneventful or aborted procedure. A limitation of the present report is the lack of follow-up CTA, which precludes radiological assessment of the long-term durability of the endovascular repair. Nevertheless, the patient remained clinically stable during the available clinical follow-up.

Conclusions

A delayed DTA pseudoaneurysm is a rare but severe complication of transfemoral TAVI attempts. TEVAR offers an effective and minimally invasive strategy for stabilizing complex IAI, thereby enabling subsequent transcatheter valve treatment in selected high-risk patients and demonstrating the feasibility of a staged, fully transcatheter approach. Routine consideration of postprocedural aortic imaging after aborted TAVI attempts may help prevent delayed, life-threatening complications.
