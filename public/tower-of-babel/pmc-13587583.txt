Dual antidiabetic potential of arjunglucoside I and arjunetin from Terminalia chebula via modulation of insulin- and gluconeogenesis-related responses
Authors: Ye-Seul Song, Chen Huo, Alexander Blöchle, Niranjan Parajuli, Ki Hyun Kim, Hyo-Jong Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA deciduous tree belonging to the Combretaceae family, Terminalia chebula Retz. has a long history of traditional use in diabetes management. However, it is not fully understood how the extract of T. chebula exerts the antidiabetic effects. To identify the constituents responsible for this activity, we investigated the bark of T. chebula and determined the structures of compounds exhibiting antidiabetic potential. Using molecular networking, LC-MS, and NMR analysis, we identified five phytochemical compounds (1–5): arjunglucoside I (1), arjunetin (2), arjungenin (3), (–)-catechin (4), and (–)-epicatechin (5). We found that arjunglucoside I and arjunetin selectively inhibited α-glucosidase, but not α-amylase, suggesting that they may regulate postprandial glucose levels with few gastrointestinal side effects. Both compounds significantly increased the gene expression of insulin (ins) but decreased that of pck1, which encodes phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK), a key enzyme in gluconeogenesis. These effects were also observed under metabolic stress induced by cAMP/dexamethasone, indicating feedback regulation between insulin and gluconeogenesis pathways. Arjunglucoside I and arjunetin protected zebrafish from oxidative damage induced by alloxan, modulated calcium signaling-related gene expression under insulin-resistant conditions, and increased glucose uptake in insulin-resistant C2C12 myotubes. Collectively, these results provide insights into the use of arjunglucoside I and arjunetin for diabetes treatment and the development of novel antidiabetic agents derived from the promising phytochemicals.KEYWORDS: Terminalia chebula, arjunglucoside I, arjunetin, α-glucosidase, PEPCK, insulin resistance

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus has become one of the fastest-growing health challenges in the world with an enormous burden on public health and healthcare systems (Farzaei et al. 2015; Ong et al. 2023; Yang et al. 2019). Although important advances in the field of anti-diabetic therapy have been achieved, several currently available drugs induce side effects undermining long-term compliance and limiting therapeutic efficacy (Lee et al. 2023). In this light, there is growing interest in developing safer alternatives that can control blood glucose whilst causing minimum treatment-related complications. Diabetes is not only characterized by incomplete insulin secretion but also several metabolic perturbations like increased hepatic glucose production, chronic osmotic stress and insulin resistance. Due to the close interconnection of these pathological processes effective therapies should target multiple mechanisms regulating glucose homeostasis (Cao et al. 2023; Hatting et al. 2018).

Postprandial hyperglycemia is one of the major therapeutic targets in diabetes treatment or management (Tian et al. 2023). After a meal, dietary carbohydrates hydrolyze into absorbable sugars leading to an increase in the concentration of glucose present within blood. In this process, α-amylase and α-glucosidase are predominantly involved, so these enzymes are important targets for glycemic control. α-Amylase breaks down complex polysaccharides into smaller oligosaccharides through hydrolysis in saliva and the pancreas, while α-glucosidase located at the intestinal brush border subsequently converts them into absorbable monosaccharides. Inhibiting these enzymes can delay carbohydrate digestion and glucose absorption, thereby preventing an increase in postprandial blood glucose (Kwon et al. 2017). However, excessive inhibition of α-amylase may lead to gastrointestinal distress. Thus, for people on a diet rich in carbohydrates α-glucosidase inhibitors may be safer and more effective postprandial glycemic control than other inhibitors (Liu et al. 2019).

The use of the zebrafish (Danio rerio) as a model organism for metabolic diseases, including diabetes mellitus has become popular in vertebrate research (Zang et al. 2018). They are genetically similar to humans and have conserved metabolic pathways, as well as being optically transparent during early developmental stages allowing the disease's progression and responses towards treatment to be observed in vivo (Angom and Nakka 2024; Bournele and Beis 2016). Zebrafish are also an ideal model for high-throughput screening, allowing rapid assessment of efficacy and safety of putative antidiabetic agents (Bugel et al. 2014). The model is especially useful for natural products that are difficult to isolate or only available in limited quantities since these experiments need very little test material (Roohi et al. 2024). In addition, zebrafish reproduce key features of diabetes including pancreatic dysfunction, insulin resistance and metabolic disturbances, offering a helpful tool to investigate the mechanisms involved in regulating glucose homeostasis (Covington and Chen 2024; Yashaswini et al. 2025). Collectively, these benefits uniquely position zebrafish as a feasible in vivo model to assess candidate antidiabetic agents early on in their development.

Medicinal plants remain a valuable source of structurally diverse bioactive compounds with potential therapeutic applications for the treatment of diabetes (Tundis et al. 2010). Among them, Terminalia chebula Retz. (Combretaceae), commonly known as harro in Nepal, has been widely used in traditional medicine across South Asia and has received growing scientific interest because of its reported antidiabetic and antioxidant properties (Bag et al. 2013; Gupta 2012; Kolla et al. 2017). In Ayurvedic medicine, T. chebula has traditionally been used for Prameha, which is a disorder analogous to diabetes mellitus (Kumar et al. 2006; Peterson et al. 2017). Classical Ayurvedic literature, including the Charaka Samhita, describes its use for restoring metabolic balance. This traditional use is supported by experimental studies demonstrating the α-glucosidase inhibitory activity of T. chebula. T. chebula extracts have been shown to improve glycemic control, enhance insulin sensitivity, attenuate oxidative stress, and protect against diabetes-associated neuronal damage (Agrawal and Kulkarni 2023; El-Shamarka et al. 2024). Although these pharmacological effects have been documented extensively, relatively little is known about the individual phytochemicals responsible for the observed antidiabetic activity.

As part of our ongoing efforts to discover novel bioactive phytochemicals from diverse natural resources (Cho et al. 2024; Gil et al. 2024; Lee et al. 2024; Bridget et al. 2025; Kim et al. 2025), we used zebrafish-based in vivo assays, enzymatic assays, and insulin-resistant cell models to investigate the antidiabetic potential of T. chebula and its bioactive constituents. Comprehensive phytochemical and biological investigations were conducted to identify the bioactive constituents responsible for the antidiabetic activity of T. chebula and to provide pharmacological support for its traditional medicinal use. Guided by tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS)-based molecular networking, five compounds (1–5) were isolated from the bark extract of T. chebula. All these compounds were evaluated for their antidiabetic effects using enzyme assays together with cellular and zebrafish models of pancreatic injury, metabolic stress, and insulin resistance. The carbohydrate-digesting enzymes, pancreatic function, insulin signaling- and gluconeogenesis-related genes as well as glucose uptake in both the control and diabetic C2C12 myotubes were analyzed. Using these strategies, we sought to obtain more mechanistic insights into the traditional application of T. chebula in diabetes control as well as practical natural products with promise for further development towards novel antidiabetic compounds.

Materials and methods

General experimental procedure

All spectroscopic and chromatographic analyses were performed using standard instrumentation in natural product chemistry, with slight modifications of previously reported procedures (Jeong et al. 2026). High-field NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker AVANCE III HD 850 MHz spectrometer equipped with a 5 mm TCI CryoProbe. Chemical shifts were referenced to residual solvent signals and are quoted in ppm. UV–Vis absorption spectra were obtained on an Agilent 8453 diode-array spectrophotometer and specific optical rotations were measured at 25°C on a JASCO P-2000 digital polarimeter. LC–MS analyses were performed on an Agilent 1200 Series HPLC system coupled to a 6130 ESI mass detector. High-resolution mass measurements were obtained using an Agilent 6545 Q-TOF instrument. Sephadex LH-20 column chromatography was performed, and TLC monitoring employed silica gel F254 and RP-C18 plates visualized under UV light or by anisaldehyde–sulfuric acid staining.

Plant material

Terminalia chebula was collected in July 2020 from Dang district in Nepal, and a voucher specimen was deposited in the Central Herbarium of the Central Botany Department at Tribhuvan University, under voucher no. TUCH 210018. The plant material was authenticated by Dr. Niranjan Parajuli, co-author of this manuscript. The collected plant materials were shade-dried and crushed to a fine powder before extraction.

Extraction and separation of compounds 1–5

Dried T. chebula bark (1.2 g × 2) was separately extracted with methanol and ethyl acetate to yield a methanolic extract (125.0 mg, TC1) and an ethyl acetate (EtOAc) extract (80.2 mg, TC2), respectively. The EtOAc extract (80.2 mg) was fractionated on a Sephadex LH-20 column eluted with 100% MeOH to yield five fractions (EAF1–EAF5). Each fraction was initially analyzed by thin-layer chromatography (TLC) and then profiled by LC–MS. Fractions EAF1 and EAF3 were chosen using LC–MS profiles as well as GNPS molecular networking for further purification by semi-preparative reversed-phase HPLC. Fraction EAF1 (18.6 mg) was purified by semi-preparative HPLC using gradient elution with MeOH/H2O (50–70% MeOH) at a flow rate of 2.0 mL/min, yielding compounds 1 (1.3 mg, tR = 31.6 min), 2 (0.5 mg, tR = 39.8 min), and 3 (0.6 mg, tR = 44.1 min). Similarly, fraction EAF3 (25.9 mg) was purified by semi-preparative HPLC using gradient elution with MeOH/H2O (25–45% MeOH) at a flow rate of 2.0 mL/min, affording compounds 4 (3.9 mg, tR = 25.0 min) and 5 (2.8 mg, tR = 41.3 min).

Global natural products social molecular networking (GNPS)-based molecular networking analysis

A Q-TOF mass spectrometer was utilized to perform high-resolution LC–MS/MS analysis in the positive ion mode to generate a comprehensive metabolite profile of the ethyl acetate extract (TC2). The raw files obtained were converted to mzXML using MSConvert (ProteoWizard). In addition, the mzXML files were uploaded to the GNPS database for processing, as part of the feature-based molecular networking (FBMN) workflow (http://gnps.ucsd.edu). Precursor and fragment ion mass tolerances of 0.02 Da were used to build molecular networks. Network edges were kept if the cosine similarity score was greater than 0.7 with at least six matched fragment ions. The resulting networks were exported in GraphML format and imported into Cytoscape (v3.9.1) for visualization. The analytical strategy adhered to designated GNPS-based metabolomic approaches with modifications that were developed and established through its application to this extract (Thapa et al. 2024).

α-Glucosidase inhibitory activity

Rat intestinal acetone powder was homogenized in 0.9% NaCl solution and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 30 min. The supernatant containing α-glucosidase was transferred to a 96-well microplate and preincubated with acarbose (10−2, 10−1, 100, 101, or 102 μM) or the test compounds (10−4, 10−3, 10−2, 10−1, 100, 101, or 102 μM) at 37°C for 10 min. Then, the substrate p-nitrophenyl-α-D-glucopyranoside (pNPG, 1 mM) in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) was added and incubated at 37°C for another 5 min. The absorbance of the samples was measured at 405 nm using a microplate reader (Tecan Group Ltd., Switzerland).

Subsequently, 25 μL of soluble starch solution (0.5% w/v) was added as the substrate and incubated for another 10 min. Maintenance of zebrafish

All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Sungkyunkwan University, Republic of Korea (SKKUIACUC2025-06-54-2, SKKUIACUC2026-04-60-1, and SKKUIACUC2026-04-110-1). AB wild-type zebrafish and the transgenic lines Tg(lfabp:dsRED; elastase:GFP) and Tg(ins:GFP) were obtained from the Korea Zebrafish Organogenesis Mutant Bank (ZOMB). Zebrafish were maintained at 28.5°C under a 14 h light/10 h dark photoperiod in accordance with standard protocols (Ali et al. 2017). Adult male and female zebrafish were housed together overnight in breeding tanks to allow for natural spawning. Fertilized eggs were collected the following morning and used for subsequent experiments.

Zebrafish survival and developmental toxicity assay

At 1 day post-fertilization (dpf), AB wild-type zebrafish embryos were exposed to compounds 1 and 2 at concentrations of 1 or 10 μM for 24 h. Following exposure, the embryos were transferred to fresh E3 medium, and the chorions were manually removed using a fine needle. Embryo survival was recorded to evaluate potential effects on viability. In addition, heart rate was measured, and morphological abnormalities were examined using a stereomicroscope (Macrotech, Daejeon, Republic of Korea). These assessments were performed to evaluate the potential developmental toxicity of the test compounds in zebrafish embryos.

Pancreatic protective assay in alloxan-treated zebrafish

From the day of fertilization, Tg(lfabp:dsRED; elastase:GFP) embryos were treated with 0.2 mM 1-phenyl-2-thiourea (PTU) for 6 days to suppress pigmentation and enhance optical transparency. At 6 days post-fertilization (dpf), the larvae were transferred to a 96-well plate and exposed to compounds 1 and 2 at concentrations of 1 or 10 μM for 24 h. Following compound treatment, the larvae were transferred to fresh E3 medium with or without alloxan (500 μM) and incubated for 30 min. All the larvae were subsequently fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at room temperature for 50 min. The fixed larvae were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and imaged using an Eclipse Ts2 microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) to capture pancreatic fluorescence images and quantify enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) intensity. Fluorescence intensity was quantified relative to untreated control larvae and expressed as a percentage.

Analysis of ins and pck1 mRNA expression in zebrafish larvae

From 96 h post-fertilization (hpf), zebrafish larvaewere cultured in E3 medium containing compounds 1 and 2 at concentrations of 1 or 10 μM for 48 h. In parallel, another batch of the same group was treated with dexamethasone (1 μM) along with cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP, 100 μM), in the presence or absence of the test compounds for the indicated duration. Larvaewere then homogenized using a needle with a 1 mL insulin syringe following treatment. Total RNA was obtained from samples using TRIzol™ reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using M-MLV Reverse Transcriptase (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) was conducted to measure the mRNA expression levels of preproinsulin (ins) and phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (pck1) under both basal and cAMP/Dex-induced gluconeogenic stress conditions using TOPreal™ SYBR Green qPCR PreMIX (Enzynomics, Daejeon, Republic of Korea) on a Rotor-Gene® Q real-time PCR system (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). All reactions were conducted in triplicate. Gene expression levels were normalized to 18S ribosomal RNA (18S rRNA), which served as an internal control. The primer sequences used were as follows: ins forward, 5′-TGGTCGTGTCCAGTGTAAGC-3′; ins reverse, 5′-GAAGGGGCTCAACGTCTCTC-3′; pck1 forward, 5′-GGCCCGTGTGGAGAGTAAAA-3′; pck1 reverse, 5′-ACGTACATCACACGCCCTTT-3′; 18S rRNA forward, 5′-TCGCTAGTTGGCATCGTTTATG-3′; and 18S rRNA reverse, 5′-CGGAGGTTCGAAGACGATCA-3′.

Assessment of pancreatic islet recovery and calcium-signaling-associated gene expression in INS-resistant zebrafish

To establish an insulin-resistant zebrafish model, 3 dpf Tg(ins:GFP) zebrafish larvae were exposed to 10 μM insulin for 48 h, and subsequently incubated with compounds 1 and 2 at concentrations of 1 or 10 μM for an additional 48 h. Pancreatic islet morphology was observed under a fluorescence microscope, and the pancreatic islet size was quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). For gene expression analysis, RT-qPCR was performed as described above. The primer sequences used were as follows: cacna1c forward, 5′-TACTGCTGCTCTTCCTCTTC-3′; cacna1c reverse, 5′-ACATCACAGAGTTCCAGTCC-3′; cacna1d forward, 5′-AGGTCACCAAAGAGAAGACTGC-3′; and cacna1d reverse, 5′-GAGGAAGATCGTCTTGACTGCT-3′. The primer sequences for 18S rRNA were identical to those described above.

Culture of C2C12 cells

Mouse C2C12 myoblasts were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). Cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C (Kim et al. 2025b).

Cell viability assay in C2C12 cells

C2C12 myoblasts were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 103 cells/well and incubated for 24 h, then treated with compounds 1 and 2 (diluted in DMSO to reach desired concentrations of 2.5–40 μM) for an additional 24 h. Cell viability was measured by a colorimetric assay (CellTiter 96® AQueous One Solution Reagent, Promega, Madison, WI, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a Sunrise™ microplate reader (Tecan Group Ltd., Männedorf, Switzerland).

Measurement of glucose uptake in insulin-resistant C2C12 myotubes

C2C12 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well. Upon reaching confluence, myogenic differentiation was induced by replacing the growth medium with DMEM supplemented with 2% horse serum (HS; Gibco). After 6 days of differentiation, fully differentiated myotubes were used for glucose uptake experiments. To induce insulin resistance, differentiated C2C12 myotubes were pretreated with compounds 1 and 2 (1 or 10 μM) for 1 h, followed by treatment with palmitic acid (PA, 750 μM) for 24 h in the continued presence of the compounds. After treatment, cells were washed with PBS and incubated in serum-free medium for 1 h. The cells were then incubated with insulin (1 μg/mL) and 50 μM 2-NBDG for an additional 1 h at 37 °C. Glucose uptake was quantified by measuring the fluorescence intensity using a microplate reader at excitation and emission wavelengths of 485 and 540 nm, respectively.

Statistical analysis

All measurements were conducted under blinded conditions. Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three independent experiments. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 6 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Differences among groups were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by an appropriate multiple-comparison test, and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Unless otherwise indicated, each experiment was performed in triplicate and independently repeated at least three times to confirm reproducibility.

Results

Selective inhibitory effects of T. chebula extracts on key carbohydrate-metabolizing enzymes in vitro

The methanolic (TC1) and ethyl acetate (TC2) extracts of T. chebula bark were preliminarily screened for their inhibitory activities against α-glucosidase and α-amylase. Both extracts produced marked inhibition of α-glucosidase activity (Figure 1A), whereas neither affected α-amylase activity under the experimental conditions (Figure 1B). Since α-amylase inhibition is often associated with intestinal adverse effects such as abdominal distension and pain (Kwon et al. 2017), selective α-glucosidase inhibition may serve the purpose of achieving desired postprandial glucose levels that are better tolerated. This led to the identification of TC2 with stronger α-glucosidase inhibitory ability than TC1 for further phytochemical analysis.

Effects of Terminalia chebula bark extracts on carbohydrate-digesting enzymes. (A) Inhibitory effects of the methanolic (TC1) and ethyl acetate (TC2) extracts on intestinal α-glucosidase activity using rat intestinal acetone powder as the enzyme source. Activity is shown as the percentage of inhibition relative to the vehicle control. (B) Effects of TC1 and TC2 extracts on porcine α-amylase activity. Acarbose (100 μM) was used as a positive control. Values are presented as the mean ± SD of at least three independent experiments (n = 3). Statistical differences were determined by one-way ANOVA, and *** indicates p < 0.001 compared with the control group.

GNPS-based molecular networking analysis and isolation of compounds

Ethyl acetate (TC2) extract was characterized using high-resolution tandem mass spectrometry (HRMS/MS)-based metabolomic profiling to identify the chemical constituents of this bioactive extract. The MS/MS data acquired were subjected to analysis using GNPS molecular networking according to previously reported workflows (Huo et al., 2023; Lee et al. 2024; Schalk et al. 2020). Overall, the molecular network contained 160 molecular features as nodes and was interconnected by 185 edges (Figure 2A). The GNPS molecular network revealed a distinct cluster corresponding to triterpenoids, including features putatively annotated as madecassic acid (m/z 487.343), arjunic acid (m/z 471.349; Figure 2B), and their putative structural congeners. These congeners vary by the degree of hydrogenation, oxidation, methylation, or glycosylation. Another cluster corresponding to flavonoid derivatives was observed, including features putatively annotated as epicatechin (m/z 291.087), epigallocatechin (m/z 307.082), catechin 3-gallate (m/z 443.099), and epigallocatechin gallate (m/z 459.096; Figure 2C). The assignment of these clusters provided an overview of the major metabolite classes in the TC2 extract.

Global natural products social molecular networking (GNPS) molecular network-based visualization of the chemical composition of the ethyl acetate extract (TC2) of Terminalia chebula bark. (A) Overview of the resulting molecular network illustrating the distribution of metabolite classes. (B) Enlarged view of the molecular cluster corresponding to the triterpenoid derivatives and (C) flavonoid derivatives.

Subsequent molecular networking-guided fractionation of the TC2 extract using column chromatography and semi-preparative HPLC isolated five major metabolites with potential bioactivity. These included three triterpenoid derivatives, arjunglucoside I (1) (Gossan et al. 2016), arjunetin (2) (Zhang et al. 2022), and arjungenin (3) (Gossan et al. 2016), as well as two flavonoid derivatives, (–)-catechin (4) (Abd El-Razek 2007) and (–)-epicatechin (5) (Chao et al. 2010) (Figure 3). Selective inhibition of α-glucosidase by isolated compounds (1–5) in vitro

The inhibitory effects of the five isolated compounds (1–5) on key carbohydrate-metabolizing enzymes were evaluated in vitro. All compounds selectively inhibited α-glucosidase activity without affecting α-amylase activity at the tested concentration, indicating enzyme-selective inhibitory profiles (Figure 4A). Among the isolated compounds, arjunglucoside I (1) and arjunetin (2) showed the most potent α-glucosidase inhibitory activity. Compounds 1 and 2 were subsequently tested over a broad concentration range. Both compounds exhibited concentration-dependent inhibition of α-glucosidase (Figure 4B), whereas no significant inhibition of α-amylase was measured at any investigated concentrations (Figure 4C). These results demonstrate that both compounds selectively inhibit α-glucosidase under the present experimental conditions.

Developmental toxicity of compounds 1 and 2 in vivo

Developmental toxicity of compounds 1 and 2 was then investigated using zebrafish embryos. At 24 h post-fertilization (hpf), embryos were incubated for a further 24 h with each compound, and survival together with developmental outcomes was monitored. No treatment-related morphological abnormalities were observed during organogenesis (Figure 5A). As noted, no embryos exposed to either compound were positive for hemorrhage or yolk sac malformations (Figure 5B). Heart rate remained comparable to that of the control group throughout the experiment (Figure 5C), suggesting developmental toxicity was not detectable under the tested conditions.

Pancreatic protective effects and metabolic gene regulation by compounds 1 and 2

Therefore, we next examined the protective effects of compounds 1 and 2 in an alloxan-induced pancreatic injury model using Tg(lfabp:dsRED; elastase:GFP) zebrafish (Wan et al. 2006). With this model, pancreatic ectopic GFP expression was driven by elastase and only allowed for qualitative as well as quantitative assessment of the targeted tissue. Zebrafish larvae were treated with compounds 1 and 2 for 24 h at 6 dpf, followed by alloxan exposure to induce acute pancreatic oxidative stress. Cytotoxic glucose analogue alloxan caused strong decreases in GFP fluorescence, which is a distinctive marker for pancreatic injury mediated by reactive oxygen species and its uptake via GLUT2 transporter (Lenzen 2008; Banda et al. 2018). Remarkably, compounds 1 and 2 considerably protected against alloxan-induced pancreas injury in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 6A and B). These results indicate that both compounds 1 and 2 exert protective effects against pancreatic damage caused by acute oxidative stress.

We next investigated whether compounds 1 and 2 have an ins expression-promoting effect in zebrafish larvae. Treatment with both compounds resulted in a dose-dependent increase in ins mRNA levels as assessed via RT-qPCR (Figure 6C). The effects of compounds 1 and 2 on ins expression were additionally assessed in zebrafish larvae subjected to Dex and cAMP (cAMP/Dex), which generates metabolic stress related to aberrant glucose metabolism. Compounds 1 and 2 restored ins mRNA expression under these conditions (Figure 6D).

We also examined the effects of compounds 1 and 2 on phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (pck1) mRNA expression, an indicator relating to gluconeogenesis (Yoo et al. 2023). PEPCK, encoded by pck1, catalyzes the rate-limiting step in gluconeogenesis and is involved in glucose metabolic regulation. Reduction of pck1 mRNA levels following treatment with compounds 1 and 2 was shown by RT-qPCR analysis (Figure 6E), with compound 1 exhibiting the greatest effect. Concerning stress-induced pck1 upregulation, a comparable trend was evident with cAMP/Dex treatment, where both compounds significantly decreased pck1 expression in response to these conditions (Figure 6F).

A direct causal relationship between the compound-induced upregulation of ins and suppression of pck1 could not be clearly established. However, further analysis conducted in cAMP/Dex-induced metabolic stress conditions revealed that restoration of the expression of ins and suppression of pck1 were detected concomitantly. These results indicate that the combined actions of compounds 1 and 2 are effective in gene expression related to insulin- and gluconeogenesis induction under metabolic stress. This supports the antidiabetic potential of compounds 1 and 2 across multiple diabetes-related responses.

Effects of compounds 1 and 2 on pancreatic islet GFP signal and calcium signaling-related gene expression under insulin-resistant conditions

We developed an insulin-resistant zebrafish model with type 2 diabetes-like metabolic and pancreatic β-cell dysfunction after treatment with insulin to evaluate the antidiabetic potential of compounds 1 and 2 for hyperinsulinemia-induced insulin resistance. We then assayed the pancreatic islet status of Tg(ins:GFP) zebrafish larvae in which GFP fluorescence serves as a readout for signals related to those produced by insulin-secreting β-cells (Nam et al. 2021). This indicated pancreatic dysfunction under metabolic dysregulation because chronic exposure to insulin significantly reduced GFP-positive pancreatic islet size compared with vehicle-treated controls (Figure 7A and B). GFP-positive pancreatic islet size was restored following treatment with compounds 1 and 2, suggesting protective effects against chronic metabolic stress-associated pancreatic impairment.

Since calcium signaling is closely associated with pancreatic β-cell function and insulin secretion, we further investigated the expression of genes related to calcium signaling under insulin-resistant conditions. Wild-type AB zebrafish larvae cultured under the same conditions were analyzed for gene expression. To determine the effect of these two compounds on calcium signaling, we examined whether they could regulate genes associated with this pathway through RT-qPCR analysis in pretreated insulin-treated zebrafish larvae (Figure 7C). These findings suggest that compounds 1 and 2 protect against the loss of pancreatic islet-dependent responses during the development of insulin resistance.

Effects of compounds 1 and 2 on glucose uptake under PA-induced insulin resistance in C2C12 myotubes

To ascertain whether the antidiabetic effects of compounds 1 and 2 were extended to peripheral glucose utilization, we evaluated the effects on C2C12 myoblasts and differentiated myotubes. To exclude any nonspecific cytotoxic effects, cell viability was first evaluated. Both compounds were not found to affect C2C12 cell viability at concentrations of up to 20 μM (Figure 8A). We then investigated glucose uptake in PA-treated C2C12 myotubes, an in vitro model of insulin resistance in skeletal muscle cells. One of the most well-studied sites for insulin-mediated glucose disposal is skeletal muscle, where defective glucose uptake in myotubes has been directly linked to metabolic derangement typically seen in type 2 diabetes (Yang et al. 2013; Kwak et al. 2017). Treatment with PA reduced the 2-(N-(7-nitrobenz-2-oxa-1,3-diazol-4-yl)amino)−2-deoxyglucose (2-NBDG) uptake under both basal and insulin-stimulated conditions (Figure 8B). Compounds 1 and 2 increased basal uptake of the glucose analog, 2-NBDG, as well as potentiated the insulin-induced increase in uptake of this tracer. These findings suggest that compounds 1 and 2 improve glucose uptake in insulin-resistant C2C12 myotubes.

Discussion

Since a large volume of extract was not available, a GNPS-guided molecular networking approach for prioritizing bioactive constituents was employed using the ethyl acetate extract of T. chebula bark. Molecular networking provided a comprehensive overview of the chemical composition by grouping metabolites based on MS/MS spectral similarity and allowing numerous structurally related compounds to be identified simultaneously. This method avoids the repeated separation of known components and can estimate the bioactive compounds with a minimal amount of sample. Five compounds (1–5) were identified from T. chebula bark as representative constituents guided by GNPS analysis.

Among the isolated compounds, compounds 1 and 2 exhibit selective inhibitory effects on α-glucosidase while having negligible effects on α-amylase, indicating their selective action on carbohydrate digestion (Asano 2003; Dirir et al. 2022). This selectivity may have advantages, as potent α-amylase inhibitors are often associated with gastrointestinal symptoms such as bloating and abdominal pain (Kumari et al. 2024; Kwon et al. 2017). By targeting the final stage of carbohydrate digestion while largely preserving the activity of α-amylase, compounds 1 and 2 may help to limit post-meal blood sugar elevation without significantly interfering with the digestion of starch (Kumar et al. 2011).

The initial safety of compounds 1 and 2 was inferred from these zebrafish experiments as well. Under the test conditions, neither compound had detectable effects on embryonic development or morphology. These findings warrant an expanded investigation of both compounds as potential lead candidates in antidiabetic drug discovery; however, their safety should first be assessed using toxicity assessment models. Notably, compounds 1 and 2 affected several biological processes associated with diabetes. The dose-dependent upregulation of ins mRNA expression induced by both compounds suggests a potential role in enhancing pancreatic β-cell function. This represents a much sought-after benefit of antidiabetic strategies aimed at restoring or augmenting endogenous insulin secretion (Oh 2015). Concurrently, the reduced pck1 mRNA expression indicated a second possible pathway involved in their antidiabetic potential (Wang et al. 2022). PEPCK encoded by pck1 catalyzes a rate-limiting step in gluconeogenesis and therefore the repression of pck1 expression might correlate with decreased gluconeogenesis, which is one of the key pathogenic processes contributing to diabetic hyperglycemia. Further analyses under cAMP/Dex-induced hepatic metabolic stress support the association between the observed changes in gene expression related to insulin and gluconeogenesis. However, this evidence did not support a direct causal relationship between the upregulation of ins and the suppression of pck1. By contrast, cAMP/Dex-based analyses of the overlapping and nonoverlapping gene lists showed that compounds 1 and 2 exerted complementary effects on insulin- and gluconeogenesis-related genes under metabolic stress. Nevertheless, these findings should not be simply regarded as support for direct mediation of pck1 suppression by the compound-induced upregulation of ins. However, the effects of compounds 1 and 2 on ins and pck1 expression might represent antagonistic and independent pharmacological actions. Additional studies incorporating inhibition of insulin signaling or the insulin receptor and downstream analyses are necessary to answer whether these two responses are mechanistically connected. The results of this study indicate that compounds 1 and 2 exert multiple pharmacological effects relevant to diabetes rather than modulating a single mechanism. Of the two compounds, compound 1 inhibits gluconeogenic regulation and also exerts a greater inhibitory effect on hepatic insulin-related gene expression by reducing pck1 expression.

The two compounds also showed protective effects against alloxan-induced pancreatic injury in zebrafish, which suggested biological activity under acute oxidative stress conditions. Since alloxan-induced β-cell damage is strongly associated with oxidative injury, the protection observed may include some antioxidant effect and/or stimulation of endogenous cytoprotective pathways. While the respective mechanisms remain to be elucidated, our results show that both compounds 1 and 2 have biological effects beyond the inhibition of carbohydrate-digesting enzymes.

The alloxan model is primarily characterized by the destruction of pancreatic β-cells and insulin deficiency, both features of early type 1 diabetes. This makes the observed effects in this model better seen as protection of pancreatic function from acute injury rather than improving broader metabolic disturbances associated with diabetes. The insulin-resistant zebrafish model, on the other hand, mimics chronic metabolic stress and less effective action of circulating blood glucose regulators associated with type 2 diabetes. In this model, compounds 1 and 2 altered the expression of genes involved in calcium signaling, a pathway closely linked to β-cell function and insulin secretion. Their effects could range from protection of the pancreas against acute injury to modulation of responses elicited under insulin-resistant conditions.

In addition, we tested compounds 1 and 2 in palmitic acid–induced insulin-resistant C2C12 myotubes, a widely used model of impaired peripheral glucose utilization. Since skeletal muscle is responsible for most insulin-stimulated glucose uptake, responses in this model are directly applicable to insulin resistance. Both compounds increased basal and insulin-stimulated glucose uptake, indicating that these compounds affect peripheral as well as pancreatic and hepatic activities. Taken together, the findings across these experimental models suggest that compounds 1 and 2 act on multiple processes involved in glucose homeostasis rather than on a single diabetes-related pathway.

In this study, arjunglucoside I (1) and arjunetin (2) were identified as the major antidiabetic components of T. chebula bark guided by GNPS molecular networking. Both phytochemicals specifically inhibited α-glucosidase while maintaining the permissible function of α-amylase. They also demonstrated biological synergy that included the induction of ins transcription; suppression of pck1 during glucocentric stress; protection against excess nutrient-induced impairment of pancreatic mass and function; promotion of enhanced glucose uptake by insulin-resistant skeletal muscle. The data support not a single mechanism, but rather the concept that both compounds coregulate various pathways controlling glucose homeostasis. Collectively, these results provide pharmacological evidence supporting the traditional use of T. chebula and offer a foundation for additional studies to elucidate their mechanisms of action and develop therapeutics.
