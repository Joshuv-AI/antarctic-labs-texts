Serum electrolytes and blood urea nitrogen changes in end-stage renal disease patients undergoing contemporary haemodialysis: A multi-center cross-sectional study
Authors: Wastina Bitewlign, Mohammed Jemal, Enyew Fenta Mengistu, Hiwot Tezera Endale, Tadesse Asmamaw Dejenie, Kibur Hunie Tesfa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCorrection of electrolyte imbalance and adequate haemodialysis (HD) minimizes disease complications and hospitalization. Despite the problem, there is a paucity of data regarding the assessment of serum electrolytes and blood urea nitrogen changes (BUN) in end-stage renal disease patients undergoing contemporary HD in Ethiopia.ObjectiveThe objective of this study was to assess serum electrolytes and BUN changes in patients with End-Stage Renal Disease undergoing contemporary HD in Ethiopia.MethodsA multi-center cross-sectional study was conducted from June 1 to August 10, 2021. The levels of serum electrolytes and BUN before and immediately after HD were measured using an ion-selective electrode and a spectrophotometry analyzer, respectively. Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 26. Descriptive statistics were performed for descriptive variables, and continuous variables were compared as the mean ± SD using a paired t-test. Pearson's correlation coefficient with a 95 % confidence level was used to measure the strength of the association. P-values < 0.05 were declared statistically significant.ResultsThe mean ± SD age of the participants was 44.19 ± 15.25 years. The mean ± SD duration of HD was 1.71 ± 1.65 years, and 52 (72.2 %) patients received dialysis twice per week. Compared with immediate postdialysis concentration, a statistically significant decrease was noted for potassium (1.74 ± 1.11 mmol/L, p < 0.001), magnesium (0.42 ± 0.35 mmol/L, p < 0.001), phosphorus (0.62 ± 0.49 mmol/L, p < 0.001), and BUN (31.9 ± 10.4 mmol/L, p < 0.001). This study showed that 43 (59.7 %) end-stage renal disease patients received adequate HD.ConclusionThis study found a significant decrease in serum potassium, magnesium, phosphorus, and BUN following dialysis. The urea reduction ratio revealed a minimum adequate HD session among end-stage renal disease patients on maintenance haemodialysis (MHD).Keywords: Haemodialysis (HD), serum electrolyte, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), Renal disease, ESRD

Background

There were 697.5 million cases of chronic kidney disease (CKD) (the prevalence of 9.1 %), and 1.2 million people died from CKD worldwide. Globally, patients with chronic renal failure who were on haemodialysis (HD) accounted for 70 % until the end of 2014 (Grassmann et al., 2005). Treatment options for CKD were not readily found in most countries in Sub-Saharan Africa (Kobayashi and Hidaka, 2020). It is estimated that approximately 4000 patients with end-stage kidney disease (ESRD) are on HD in sub-Saharan Africa, which is less than 1 % of the global cases on maintenance haemodialysis (MHD) (Naghavi et al., 2015). The region contributes to less than 5 % of patients on renal replacement therapy worldwide (Krzesinski et al., 2007; Barsoum, 2006; Naicker, 2003). More than 70 % of ESRD patients are estimated to live in developing countries such as sub-Saharan Africa by 2030 (Ogundele, 2018).

HD patients are exposed to 18,000–36,000 L of water per year, along with the biochemical composition of the dialysate. The electrolyte changes caused by the contact of the blood with the dialysate via the semipermeable membrane of the dialyzer can trigger the immediate or long-term effects of dyselectrolytemia. In our dialysis units, dialysate solution commonly contains six electrolytes: sodium (Na+ = 138 mmol/L), potassium (K+ = 2 mmol/L), calcium (Ca2+ = 1.5 mmol/L), magnesium (Mg2+ = 0.5 mmol/L), chlorine (Cl− = 109 mmol/L), and bicarbonate (HCO3– = 35 mmol/L), which is uniformly used for all patients. The nonelectrolyte component, glucose or dextrose, is usually present within the dialysate. It is considered a ‘drug’ administered to all dialysis patients; hence, its composition is important. Another component of HD, the dialyzer, is a semipermeable membrane whose surface area is 1.6 m2. High quality corrosion-resistant conductivity meter is used to determine the proper water-to-concentrate ratio of the dialysate. The conductivity was determined by the ionic constitute of the dialysate. In our setup the HD machine normal conductivity range is 12.5–13.5 mS/cm. The electrolytic changes caused by contact of the blood with the dialysate through the dialyzer can cause immediate or long-term effects. Electrolyte disturbances commonly occur in patients undergoing dialysis and increase the risk of morbidity and mortality (Timofte et al., 2021).

Electrolyte disturbances such as hyperkalaemia, hypocalcemia, hyperphosphatemia, and hyperuricemia have common associations with ESRD (Chaudhari et al., 2017). The Secondary Analysis of Monitoring in Dialysis study reported that among 66 ESRD patients on HD, 40 % hypokalaemia and 67 % hyperphosphatemia were present in immediate HD samples (Correa et al., 2021). In a study from the United States, of 27,544 MHD patients, 4729 (17.2 %) and 6133 (22.3 %) had hypomagnesemia and hypermagnesemia, respectively (Lacson et al., 2015). Moreover, a study conducted in Palestine reported a significant increase in serum calcium concentration following haemodialysis,with mean ± SD values of (2.22 ± 0.07 mmol/L) pre-dialysis and (2.30 ± 0.07 mmol/L) post-dialysis (Alhamss et al., 2021).

Achievement of dialysis adequacy is important to improve quality of life, minimize health care costs, and decrease disease complications and mortality rates in HD patients (Abideen et al., 2018; Adas et al., 2014). This can be mathematically measured by the urea reduction ratio (URR). A study in Nigeria showed that out of 74 ESRD patients on HD, the mean URR was 40 %, indicating a significantly high prevalence of inadequate HD (Batubo et al.). Assessment of HD adequacy dose at a Palestinian hospital among 64 ESRD patients on MHD 22 (34.4 %) had target URR ≥ 65 % (Adas et al., 2014). The adequacy dose calculation enables nephrologists and clinicians to define ESRD patients with inadequate dialysis who need the collaboration of efforts to minimize disease complications and mortality.

Despite familiarity with the maintenance HD system from over seventy years of clinical use (Gottschalk and Fellner, 1997) descriptions of the importance of changes in serum electrolytes with immediate practice remain understudied. Online tracking of blood concentrations of critical electrolytes through dialysis can enable such an individualized and tailor-made dialysis prescription (Palmer, 2001) thereby stopping or at the very least mitigating the complications. Therefore, the study will provide comprehensive information about the reputation of serum electrolytes among ESRD patients by analyzing the most important biological electrolytes earlier and at once after HD. In addition, this study aimed to evaluate dialysis adequacy, which is a predictor of morbidity and mortality in patients with MHD (Kim et al., 2021). To the best of our knowledge, there is no published data on the assessment of serum electrolytes and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) changes in contemporary HD in Ethiopia. Therefore, the study will offer additional information. In addition, the study will be used as a baseline for upcoming studies with comparable motives.

Methods

Study design and participants

An institutional-based cross-sectional study was conducted from June 1 to August 10, 2021, at the University of Gondar Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (UoGCSH) and Felege Hiwot Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (FHCSH), in the towns of Gondar and Bahar Dar, respectively. Those were the only public hospitals that provide HD therapy in Amhara Region, Ethiopia. The study participants were ESRD patients who were receiving HD at the UoGCSH and FHCSH during the study period. The study participants were selected based on a convenience sampling technique, and a total of 72 study participants were included. The inclusion criteria were: ESRD patients aged ≥18 who were undergoing HD at the UoGCSH and FHCSH during the study period. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with comorbid illness such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, HIV, and chronic heart failure; (2) medication-related history (diuretics, antidiabetics, ACE-inhibitors, calcium channel blockers, and antiacids); (3) critically ill patients; and (4) patients with a current infection that resulted in any fluid loss.

Data collection and variables

The data were collected based on an interviewer-administered semi-structured questionnaire. The questionnaire was prepared in English and then translated to Amharic and back-translated to English to see its consistency. Data on sociodemographic (sex, age, marital status, occupation, educational status, and residence), clinical (body weight, blood pressure), and dialysis-related (frequency of dialysis per week, duration of dialysis, duration of the single dialysis session, history of calcium tablet supplementation) data were collected directly from the patients using a structured questionnaire. Predialysis weight was measured before dialysis therapy, and postdialysis weight was taken from the patient's record of the previous dialysis session. Blood pressure was measured before and after dialysis. Blood sample analyses were performed by following the standard operation protocol. Data were collected by trained health professionals.

Bodyweight and blood pressure measurement

The bodyweight of the patients was measured after the subjects were asked to take off heavy clothes and shoes before dialysis, and after-dialysis value was taken from the record of the previous dialysis session. Interdialytic weight gain (IDWG) was defined as the predialysis weight of the current session minus the postdialysis weight of the prior dialysis sessions (Sobhi et al., 2021). Blood pressure was measured digitally using a mercury sphygmomanometer in a sitting position before and after dialysis on the arm devoid of vascular accesses after 10 min of rest. Intradialytic hypertension was defined as an increase in systolic blood pressure (SBP) during a dialysis session of greater than 10 mmHg above the predialysis SBP (Nongnuch et al., 2015). In addition, Intradialytic hypotension was defined as less than 20 mmHg decrease in post-dialysis SBP from predialysis SBP in contemporary HD with associated clinical symptoms (Nongnuch et al., 2015; Park et al., 2013).

Blood sample collection and biochemical analysis

Five milliliters (5 mL) of blood sample were collected by using a serum separator tube from a fistula needle, catheter, or graft, avoiding any anticoagulant or dialysate infusion before the samples were collected and dispensed into a serum separator tube at the beginning and at once before the end of dialysis. To avoid HD vascular access recycling, the following methods of postdialysis blood sample collection were used: (1) set the ultrafiltration speed to 100; (2) then the dialysate was bypassed; (3) extracorporeal circulation was maintained for 3–5 min at normal speed; and (4) blood samples were extracted from the extracorporeal pipeline. Blood samples in tubes were clearly labelled and transferred to the laboratory in an ice box. The blood sample was left for 30 min, and serum was separated from the collected blood sample by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min. Then the serum was taken and stored at −20 °C until the time of biochemical analysis (Yang et al., 2021a). The level of serum electrolytes and BUN was determined by using the ion-selective electrode principle and spectrophotometry of the Beckman-Coulter ISE analyzer, respectively.

The expected normal values of electrolytes: were serum sodium > 136 mmol/L to < 145 mmol/L, serum potassium > 3.5 mmol/L to < 4.5 mmol/L, serum chloride > 98 mmol/L to < 108 mmol/L, serum calcium (ionized) > 1.15 mmol/L to < 1.31 mmol/L, and total calcium > 4 mmol/L to < 5 mmol/L, serum magnesium > 0.70 mmol/L to < 1.15 mmol/L, serum phosphorus >0.81 mmol/L to < 1.4 mmol/L, and serum BUN > 5.00 mmol/L to < 13.9 mmol/L. Electrolyte disorders were defined as follows: below the expected normal values, they were defined as hypo, and above the expected cut-off point of these electrolytes, they were defined as hyper. The URR is a measure of the adequacy of dialysis. The URR is calculated with the formula 100 × (1 - [Ct/Co]), in which Ct is the BUN measured post-dialysis and Co is the pre-dialysis BUN (Batubo et al.; Owen et al., 1993).

Statistical analysis

The data were coded and entered into the computer using Epi-data software version 4.6 and, exported into the statistical package for social science (SPSS) version 26 for analysis. Descriptive statistics, including frequency distributions, means, standard deviations, and percentages, were carried out. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots. Most variables were approximately normally distributed, and the sample size sufficiently large (n ≥ 30) supporting the use of paired sample t-tests to compare pre-and post-dialysis measurements. The mean difference was calculated pre-dialysis value minus post-dialysis value, with negative values indicating post-dialysis increase and positive values indicating decreases. Pearson's correlation coefficient with a 95 % confidence level was used to measure the strength of the association. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

This study was conducted at the UoGCSH and FHCSH to assess the effect of HD on serum electrolytes and BUN changes among ESRD patients receiving maintenance HD. To understand the changes in serum electrolytes and BUN, pre- and postdialysis values were analyzed and quantified. Based on the BUN change, dialysis adequacy was also evaluated using the URR.

In the present study, the average time of a single HD session was 4 h. The mean ± SD of the duration of HD was 1.71 ± 1.65 years, with a minimum of one month and a maximum of twelve years. Regarding the frequency of dialysis, the majority of patients (72.2 %) underwent dialysis twice weekly, and while only 4.2 % received it three times per week. This pattern is consistent with reports from Pakistan (79 %), Sri Lanka (90 %), and Bangladesh (66 %) where majority of ESRD patients received HD twice per week (Abideen et al., 2018; Rodrigo et al., 2010; Rashid, 2004). A study in Russia reported that 95 % of ESRD patients underwent three dialysis sessions per week (Bikbov et al., 2017), a figure mirrored in the United Kingdom (Pyart et al., 2020) and Brazil, where 92.3 % followed a thrice-weekly schedule (Sesso and Lugon, 2020). Although recent data on dialysis adequacy are limited in some regions, most developing countries offer less frequent dialysis, largely due to insufficient public health funding.

Among 72 MHD patients, 23.6 % experienced intradialytic hypertension (ΔSBP ≥ 10 mmHg), 15.3 % had intradialytic hypotension (ΔSBP ≤ −20 mmHg), and the remainder had ΔSBP between −20 mmHg and 10 mmHg. Jongha Park et al. (2013) reported 10.2 % had ΔSBP < −30 mmHg, 79.2 % between −30 mmHg and 10 mmHg, and 10.6 % had ΔSBP ≥ 10 mmHg at the end of the dialysis session. In another study conducted by David A. Jaquens et al. among 647 ESRD patients on HD, 32.2 % of patients had intradialytic hypotension, 19.5 % had intradialytic hypertension, and the rest had stable SBP at the end of the HD session (Jaques and Davenport, 2021). Unlike the previous studies, the present study noted a higher presence of intradialytic hypertension than intradialytic hypotension following dialysis. This may be due to changes in vascular tone, increased renin-angiotensin release and sympathetic nervous system activation in response to ultrafiltration, increased viscosity and serum-sodium gradient, or expanded extracellular volume in response to dialysis treatment (Inrig et al., 2007; Fellner, 1993). The current study noted that the serum potassium level post-HD was significantly lower than the mean serum potassium level before dialysis. Our results clearly showed a greater magnitude of the decline in serum potassium, with 58.3 % of sessions having immediate post-HD potassium below the reference limit. Similarly, this is supported by a study conducted in Iraq where the mean serum potassium level pre-HD (4.48 ± 0.83 mmol/L) was changed to 3.69 ± 0.65 mmol/L post-HD (Ajam, 2020). Other studies also showed a significant decrease in serum potassium levels following HD (Correa et al., 2021; Alhamss et al., 2021; Al-Saffar, 2011). This may be due to ESRD patients relying on HD for potassium removal during each dialysis session to prevent hyperkalaemia by modifying the dialysate potassium concentration, prescribing potassium binders, and modifying dietary potassium intake (Kovesdy et al., 2007).

In the present study, on average, serum sodium appeared to increase from 117.56 ± 16.53 mmol/L to 121.69 ± 20.58 mmol/L, (p = 0.086) following a single HD session. Wafaa H. Ajam reported that the mean Na + concentration post-HD was slightly higher than the pre-HD (Ajam, 2020), which is consistent with our study. In the current study, 65.3 % patients had serum Na+ concentrations < 136 mmol/L following dialysis. Similarly, a secondary analysis of the Mid study described that 18.5 % of patients had immediate post-HD hyponatremia. In our study, serum sodium and chlorine concentrations were unaffected by HD. Gonella Geetha Meenakshi (2016) reported higher pre-HD sodium levels, which disagrees with our study. The difference may be due to sodium profiling in addition to the heterogeneity of the inpatient population (genetic variation, lifestyle).

In the current study, the mean ± SD of IDWG was 2.43 ± 1.06 kg. The predialytic SBP and DBP were 145 ± 21 mmHg and 85 ± 17 mmHg, respectively. Serum predialysis sodium had a significant correlation with IDWG (r = −0.90, p = <0.001) and with predialysis SBP (r = −0.12, p = 0.322). Hence, sodium and extracellular fluid balance play a major role in IDWG and blood pressure. In our study, the single dialysate sodium concentration (138 mmol/L) for all patients ignored inter- and intraindividual variability of sodium levels, which can result in unnecessary IDWG and blood pressure instability. Similarly, an Egyptian study reported a positive correlation between the sodium gradient and IDWG (Kora et al., 2020), while research from Iran found that sodium concentration variability was associated with blood pressure fluctuations in HD patients (Ebrahimi et al., 2017), aligning with our results. Our findings are supported by studies demonstrating a correlation between the sodium gradient and fluid overload (Munoz Mendoza et al., 2011; Hecking et al., 2012).

According to the current study, 91.7 % patients were noted to have hypocalcemia following HD therapy. Hypocalcemia is a well-known metabolic abnormality in patients with advanced kidney diseases. A study conducted in Iraq (Nathir et al., 2020) and China (Sun et al., 2017) reported that 41 % and 34 % of ESRD patients on regular HD had hypocalcemia, respectively, which is consistent with our study. The mean ionized calcium level in our study showed an increase in its concentration after HD of 1.36 ± 0.52 mmol/L when compared to pre-HD of 1.31 ± 0.62 mmol/L (p = 0.505). Similarly, Abdul-Majeed. H reported that there was a significant mean increase in Ca2+ from pre-HD to post-HD concentrations of 1.98 ± 0.17 mmol/L to 2.22 ± 0.15 mmol/L, respectively (p = 0.003) (Al-Saffar, 2011). The result obtained is consistent with various reports that stated mean serum calcium levels in post-HD were slightly higher than pre-HD concentrations (Correa et al., 2021; Alhamss et al., 2021).

In this study, hypermagnesemia were observed in 54.2 % of patients before HD therapy. This finding aligns with previous studies on MHD patients, which reported elevated serum magnesium levels, commonly attributed to the loss of residual renal function (Yang et al., 2021b; Azem et al., 2020). Postdialysis hypomagnesemia was noted in 41.7 % patients on MHD, with mean serum magnesium levels decreasing significantly from 1.18 ± 0.28 mmol/L before HD to 0.76 ± 0.23 mmol/L after HD (p < 0.001). Similar reductions have been documented in other studies (Correa et al., 2021; Mogal et al., 2021). For instance, an Iranian study of 58 HD patients reported a significant reduction in serum magnesium in 39.7 % of participants during the HD session (p = <0.001) (Pakfetrat et al., 2008). Another study conducted in China on continuous observation of serum total magnesium levels in patients undergoing HD reported a significant reduction of serum magnesium after HD (Yang et al., 2021b). HD patients may have a normal, increased, or decreased level of serum magnesium (Elsharkawy et al., 2006).

Our findings noted a mean ± SD decrease in phosphorus levels before dialysis from 1.61 ± 0.55 mmol/L to 0.99 ± 0.46 mmol/L after dialysis, p < 0.001. In Our study, 62.5 % of patients had predialysis phosphorus levels > 1.4 mmol/L, while 40.3 % had postdialysis values < 0.81 mmol/L. These findings align with previous research showing significantly higher phosphorus levels before than after dialysis (Meenakshi, 2016) and with a report from Iraq identifying hyperphosphatemia as the most common mineral abnormality in ESRD patients (Hassen et al., 2018). Numerous studies have highlighted that hyperphosphatemia contributes to renal mineralization and secondary hyperparathyroidism, potentially accelerating the progression of renal disease (Kalantar-Zadeh et al., 2010; Freethi et al., 2016). This can be explained by the fact that a decline in kidney excretory function, regarding dietary habits, ethnic factors, dialysis quality, and economic factors affects the use of more expensive phosphate binders. This is probably the reason for hyperphosphatemia among HD patients (Mogal et al., 2021).

Studies on dialysis patients have shown that morbidity and mortality are highly affected by the delivered dialysis dose (Lozano et al., 2012). The present study was looking for the prevalence of adequate HD in patients with ESRD. Among the participants, 59.7 % achieved a URR ≥ 65 %, with a mean URR of 66.69 %. These findings suggest that a majority of ESRD patients in our study met the target HD adequacy recommended by the KDOQI 2006 guidelines, which define a minimally adequate dialysis dose as a URR of at least 65 % (Foundation, 2002). In a study conducted in India among 50 ESRD patients with MHD, 28 % were found to be adequate (Chauhan and Mendonca, 2015). Moreover, studies were conducted by DOPPS and the United States, and the URR ranged from 60 to 90 %, which referred to better achievement of dialysis adequacy (Owen et al., 1998; Tzamaloukas et al., 2006). In the United States, the dialysis market can be credited with institutionalizing the urea reduction index as the leading measure of dialysis adequacy. It is taken as a performance target, and units meet it by increasing the blood flow rate and dialyzer size in addition to increasing the frequency of dialysis per week (six times per week) (Fuller and Robinson, 2017; Group, 2010). However, findings from countries such as Nigeria and Tanzania reported mean URRs of 42 % and 60.9 %, respectively (Batubo et al.; Somji et al., 2020). Furthermore, in Morocco, one single center dialysis unit reported that only 59 % received adequate dialysis, and in the university hospitals of Egypt, 60 % of prevalent HD patients receive inadequate HD (Maoujoud et al., 2012; El-Sheikh and El-Ghazaly, 2016). The increased prevalence of dialysis inadequacy may be due to the principle of conventional HD, in which the majority of patients receive HD twice or three times per week, low blood flow rates, and reduced single dialysis sessions. Dialysis adequacy parameters in most countries in sub-Saharan Africa are infrequently assessed and underreported (Ashuntantang et al., 2017). In addition, the income level and commitment of nephrologists and dialysis care in the country highly depend on the government's policies on reporting and meeting dialysis quality indicators and public health spending on dialysis practice. In developing countries, due to low public health care spending and a lack of quality standards for dialysis care, poor survival is commonly reflected among patients on regular HD (Ashuntantang et al., 2017; van der Tol et al., 2019).

Strength and limitation of the study

The study was conducted at two centers providing dialysis therapy; hence, it could help to notice those clinical problems in different dialysis settings, which was the main strength of the study. Whereas this is a comparative cross-sectional study; hence, it cannot associate causal relationships between the factors and outcome, and it was conducted on small study participants. Therefore, it was difficult to draw conclusion about the general population, which was also another limitation of the study. Some variables exhibited relatively large standard deviation, reflecting high inter-patient variability. Although parametric paired t-tests were used, we acknowledge that non-parametric methods such as the Wilcoxon signed-rank test could provide additional validity for variables with non-normal distributions. In addition, because of its simplicity and clear concept, the present study evaluated dialysis adequacy using only URR. Due to financial constraints, the study did not include all biologically significant electrolytes that do have effects on dialysis treatment. In addition, other factors that can cause dyselectrolytemia were also not investigated.

Conclusions and recommendation

This study provides a preliminary assessment of the changes in electrolytes and BUN from pre-to post-contemporary HD therapy. The results of the study found that there was a significant decrease in serum potassium, magnesium, phosphorus, and BUN following dialysis treatment. Although serum calcium, chlorine, and sodium were not significantly affected after dialysis, there was an increase in postdialysis measures. Furthermore, the study revealed that a minimum adequate HD session among ESRD patients receiving HD compared with the recommended guidelines of the kidney disease outcome and quality initiative guidelines. The frequency of dialysis had a significant positive correlation with the adequacy of dialysis. It also had a significant correlation with predialysis potassium, predialysis magnesium, and postdialysis phosphorus. Predialysis sodium also had a correlation with IDWG and pre-dialysis SBP. Overall, the findings of the present study suggest that individualizing the dialysate prescription and increasing the frequency of dialysis will improve dialysis outcomes. Regular monitoring of key electrolytes is crucial, as dyselectrolytemia is a common complication in HD patients and contributes to increased mortality primarily through cardiovascular events. In addition, we suggest that researchers conduct a large-scale prospective cohort study to better understand the incidence and causal factors of dyselectrolytemia in this population.
