Quantum channel coding: Approximation algorithms and strong converse exponents
Aadil Oufkir, Mario Berta

Abstract
We study relaxations of entanglement-assisted quantum channel coding and establish that non-signaling assistance and a natural semi-definite programming relaxation — termed meta-converse — are equivalent in terms of success probabilities. We then present a rounding procedure that transforms any non-signaling-assisted strategy into an entanglement-assisted one and prove an approximation ratio of ( 1 − e − 1 ) (1-e^{-1}) in success probabilities for the special case of measurement channels. For fully quantum channels, we give a weaker (dimension dependent) approximation ratio, that is nevertheless still tight to characterize the strong converse exponent of entanglement-assisted channel coding [Li and Yao, IEEE Tran. Inf. Theory (2024)]. Our derivations leverage ideas from position-based coding, quantum decoupling theorems, the matrix Chernoff inequality, and input flattening techniques.

The meta-converse is a versatile linear programming bound that implies many converse results for channel coding

In the same spirit, using non-signaling correlations to design approximation algorithms of the success probability has been applied to coding over classical-quantum channels

In

In that work, two approximations of the success probabilities are proven: one is multiplicative, depending logarithmically on the size of the channel, and the other is additive, which is only non-trivial when the sizes of the codes differ. Motivated by these approximation results and their powerful implications for large deviation refinements, we pose the same questions in the quantum setting:

Are similar one-shot approximation algorithms possible for quantum channel coding?

If so, are they sufficient to establish large deviation refinements for quantum channels?

In this work, we consider the problem of channel coding over quantum-classical and quantum channels with entanglement assistance in the one-shot setting. From an algorithmic perspective, as discussed in

1.2
Overview of findings

Following the works of
[
41
,
35
,
36
,
3
,
21
]
, we consider a natural SDP relaxation for channel coding over quantum channels known as the meta-converse. This relaxation is closely related to coding with non-signaling assistance
[
55
]
. We then investigate the relation between the meta-converse, non-signaling and entanglement assistance in the one-shot setting with a focus on the success probability. Moreover, we apply our one-shot findings along with known results for the hypothesis testing problem to deduce optimal strong converse exponents.

Non-signaling assistance and meta-converse.

The meta-converse (MC) reflects some constraints that any coding scheme should satisfy. The meta-converse success probability is closely related to that of coding when the sender and receiver share non-signaling (NS) correlations. For coding over a quantum channel
\mathcal{N}
with a fixed number of messages
M
, the non-signaling success probability, denoted
\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M)
, is the solution to the following SDP program
[
36
,
55
]
:

\begin{split}\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M)\!=\!\sup_{\rho_{R},\Lambda_{RB}}\quad&\frac{1}{M}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\Lambda_{RB}\cdot(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{RB}\right]\\
\text{subject to}\quad&\rho_{R}\in\mathcal{S}(R),\\
&\Lambda_{B}=\mathbb{I}_{B},\\
&0\preccurlyeq\Lambda_{RB}\preccurlyeq M\rho_{R}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B}.\end{split}

(1)

The MC success probability, denoted
\suc^{\rm{MC}}(\mathcal{N},M)
, is the solution to a similar program to (
1
), except that it has the constraint
\Lambda_{B}\preccurlyeq\mathbb{I}_{B}
instead of
\Lambda_{B}=\mathbb{I}_{B}
. Classically, both programs are equal
[
35
]
and thus non-signaling correlations provide an operational interpretation of the meta-converse introduced by
[
41
]
. However, in the quantum setting (even for classical-quantum channels), these programs are in general not equal. Our first result is a rounding inequality between the MC and NS success probabilities.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be a quantum channel and
M\geq 1
. We have that

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{MC}}(\mathcal{N},M)

\displaystyle\geq\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M)\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right)\cdot\suc^{\rm{MC}}(\mathcal{N},M).

(2)

This proposition shows that, as the number of messages increases, the optimal success probabilities for MC and NS strategies become increasingly similar. Since the approximation is multiplicative in terms of success probabilities, this rounding has a direct application on the strong converse exponents. In words, the strong converse exponent is the rate at which the success probability approaches
0
when the transmission rate is strictly above the channel capacity. From Proposition
1
, we deduce that characterizing the strong converse exponent of non-signaling strategies reduces to analyzing the meta-converse. Moreover, the meta-converse admits inherently a formulation in terms of the hypothesis testing problem. The strong converse exponent of the (composite) hypothesis testing problem can be derived from known results in the literature. More precisely, we can show using Proposition
1
and the results of
[
22
,
27
]
:

Corollary 2
.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be a quantum channel and
r\geq 0
. We have that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n},e^{nr})

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\suc^{\rm{MC}}(\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n},e^{nr})

(3)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\alpha\geq 0}\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\left(r-\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{1+\alpha}(\mathcal{N})\right),

(4)

where
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{N})
denotes the sandwiched mutual information of the channel
\mathcal{N}
and order
\alpha
, formally defined in (
28
).

In the following we show that the natural NS relaxation (
1
) provides an approximation of the entanglement-assisted success probability. We observe that because of Proposition
1
, similar results can be phrased in terms of MC instead of NS.

Quantum-classical setting.

We begin our approximation results by the quantum-classical setting as the classical and classical-quantum settings were studied by
[
3
]
and
[
21
]
respectively. Moreover, the entanglement-assisted capacity of quantum-classical channels differs from that of the shared randomness and unassisted case
[
6
,
5
,
28
]
. For this reason, we compare non-signaling assistance directly with entanglement assistance. It turns out that in this setting, we are able to obtain nice approximation results that generalize exactly the optimal classical approximation results of
[
3
]
and relate the EA success probability, denoted
\suc^{\rm{EA}}
, with its NS counterpart. Namely we obtain:

Proposition 3
.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be a quantum-classical channel and
M,M^{\prime}\geq 1
. We have that

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{EA}}(\mathcal{N},M^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{M}{M^{\prime}}\left(1-\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right)^{M^{\prime}}\right)\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M).

(5)

In particular, when
M^{\prime}=M
, we obtain

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{EA}}(\mathcal{N},M)\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{e}\right)\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M).

(6)

The proof of this proposition utilizes the position-based coding of
[
1
]
along with a sequential decoder similar to
[
57
]
. The main advantage of the quantum-classical setting is that the output system is classical, allowing the sequential decoder operators to be significantly simplified, which enables a tight evaluation of the approximation error.

Specifically, the NS program of the success probability (
1
) provides a quantum state
\rho_{R}
and an observable
O_{RB}=\frac{1}{M}\rho_{R}^{-1/2}\Lambda_{RB}\rho_{R}^{-1/2}
such that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left[O_{RB}\cdot\sigma_{RB}\right]

\displaystyle=\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{Tr}\left[O_{RB}\cdot\sigma_{R}\otimes\sigma_{B}\right]=\frac{1}{M},

(7)

where
\sigma_{RB}=\rho_{R}^{1/2}(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{RB}\rho_{R}^{1/2}=\mathcal{N}_{A\rightarrow B}(\phi_{RA})
and
\phi_{RA}
is a purification of
\rho_{R}
. We take inspiration from the position-based coding of
[
1
]
and encode the message using its corresponding position within a shared entangled state. The equalities in Eq. (
7
) will be used to decode the message. More precisely,

M^{\prime}
copies of
\phi_{RA}
are shared between Alice (holding
A_{1}\dots A_{M^{\prime}}
systems) and Bob (holding
R_{1}\cdots R_{M^{\prime}}
systems). To send the message
m\in[M^{\prime}]
, Alice applies the channel
\mathcal{N}_{A_{m}\rightarrow B}
to the
m^{\rm{th}}
copy of her part of the shared entanglement. To decode the message, Bob performs some measurements on systems
R_{1}\cdots R_{M^{\prime}}B
. By Eq. (
7
), measuring the systems
R_{m}B
with the observable
O_{R_{m}B}
successfully detects it (outcome ‘
0
’) with a success probability
\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M)
, while measuring the systems
R_{l}B
with the observable
O_{R_{l}B}
for
l\neq m
falsely detects (outcome ‘
0
’) the message with probability
\frac{1}{M}
. A natural strategy is to measure systems
R_{l}B
for
l=1,\dots,M^{\prime}
sequentially and return the first detection (outcome ‘
0
’). To analyze this scheme, we use crucially the fact that the operators
\{O_{R_{l}B}\}_{l=1}^{M^{\prime}}
do commute when
B
is a classical system. In general (
B
can be quantum), the operators
\{O_{R_{l}B}\}_{l=1}^{M^{\prime}}
do not commute, and we are unable to prove an approximation with a multiplicative error using the sequential decoder.

A direct application of Proposition
3
is that EA and NS strategies achieve the same strong converse exponent for quantum-classical channels. This along with the characterization of the strong converse exponent of NS in Corollary
2
gives the EA strong converse exponent in the quantum-classical setting.

Corollary 4
.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be a quantum-classical channel and
r\geq 0
. We have that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\suc^{\rm{EA}}(\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n},e^{nr})

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n},e^{nr})

(8)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\alpha\geq 0}\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\left(r-\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{1+\alpha}(\mathcal{N})\right).

(9)

We will further generalize this result to the quantum setting in Corollary
7
. However, we are unable to achieve the same approximation ratios from Proposition
3
in the fully quantum setting. The main challenge is that the analysis of the sequential decoder becomes cumbersome when the channel output system is quantum and the sequential measurement operators do not commute.

Quantum setting - Additive error.

In the fully quantum setting, we can still use the position-based coding from
[
1
]
. However, the sequential decoder is no longer easy to analyze. To address the issue of non-commutativity of the operators, we can apply the well-known decoder and inequality of Hayashi-Nagaoka
[
26
]
, which lead to an approximation with an additive error. Note that this decoder and inequality were used by
[
21
]
to prove a similar approximation result in the classical-quantum setting, where a non-signaling strategy is rounded to a shared-randomness one.

Proposition 5
.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be quantum channel and
M,M^{\prime}\geq 1
. We have the following inequality between the entanglement-assisted and non-signaling success probabilities

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{EA}}(\mathcal{N},M^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M)-5\sqrt{\frac{M^{\prime}}{M}}.

(10)

This proposition shows that NS correlations do not help to increase the EA capacity of a quantum channel
[
31
]
.
Moreover,
the additive error in (
10
) implies that EA and NS have the same second-order asymptotics in the small deviation regime (which remains not fully characterized in the quantum setting
[
15
]
, even for NS strategies). However, this approximation error is insufficient to conclude that EA and NS have the same error or strong converse exponents.

Even in the classical-quantum setting, it is unclear whether EA and NS should have the same error exponent. This is because NS (specifically, with activation) corresponds to the sphere-packing bound
[
14
,
11
,
39
]
, while achievability results (such as those for rounded strategies) so far seem to only imply the random coding bound at best
[
4
,
32
,
44
]
, which is only known to be optimal above a critical rate
[
32
,
44
]
. Therefore, we focus on investigating whether EA and NS share the same strong converse exponent. As we have seen in the quantum-classical setting, a simple approach to compare EA and NS strong converse exponents is by designing an EA approximation algorithm to the NS success probability that achieves a (small) multiplicative error.

Quantum setting - Multiplicative error.

Recall that the NS program of the success probability (
1
) provides a quantum state
\rho_{R}
and an observable
O_{RB}=\frac{1}{M}\rho_{R}^{-1/2}\Lambda_{RB}\rho_{R}^{-1/2}
such that

\mathrm{Tr}\left[O_{RB}\cdot\mathcal{N}_{A\rightarrow B}(\phi_{RA})\right]=\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M)

(11)

where
\phi_{RA}
is a purification of
\rho_{R}
. In the quantum-classical setting, we can use the position-based coding
[
1
]
and the sequential decoder
[
57
]
. However in the quantum setting, the operators
\{O_{R_{l}B}\}_{l=1}^{M}
do not commute and the sequential decoder is hard to analyze. To circumvent this difficulty, we draw inspiration from
[
21
]
and attempt to use the following measurement device:

\displaystyle\left\{\Xi_{l}=\frac{1}{Z}O_{R_{l}B}\right\}_{l=1}^{M}\;\text{where}\;\;Z=\left\|\sum_{l=1}^{M}O_{R_{l}B}\right\|_{\infty}\!.

(12)

The normalization
Z
ensures that
\sum_{l=1}^{M}\Xi_{l}\preccurlyeq\mathbb{I}
— a condition necessary for valid measurements. Moreover, this normalization factor will contribute to the approximation error as follows

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{EA}}(\mathcal{N},M)\geq\frac{1}{Z}\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M).

(13)

The authors of
[
21
]
employ the matrix Chernoff inequality
[
53
]
to control a similar normalization factor. In the quantum setting, however, applying the matrix Chernoff inequality is insufficient to control
Z
with high probability. The reason is that it is unclear how to utilize the constraint

\mathrm{Tr}_{R}\left[\rho_{R}\cdot O_{RB}\right]=\frac{1}{M}\mathrm{Tr}_{R}\left[\Lambda_{RB}\right]=\frac{1}{M}\mathbb{I}_{B};

(14)

as it does not appear in this form in the standard matrix Chernoff inequality. To overcome this obstacle, we consider a simplified setting where
\rho_{R}=\frac{1}{|R|}\mathbb{I}_{R}
is the maximally mixed state. Under this assumption, the constraint

\mathrm{Tr}_{R}\left[\rho_{R}\cdot O_{RB}\right]=\frac{1}{M}\mathbb{I}_{B}
simplifies to

\mathrm{Tr}_{R}\left[O_{RB}\right]=\frac{|R|}{M}\mathbb{I}_{B}.

(15)

Furthermore, it can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U_{R}\sim\mathcal{L}(R)}\left[U_{R}O_{RB}U_{R}^{\dagger}\right]=\frac{1}{|R|}\mathbb{I}_{R}\otimes\mathrm{Tr}_{R}\left[O_{RB}\right]=\frac{1}{M}\mathbb{I}_{RB},

(16)

where
\mathcal{L}(R)
is any unitary
1
-design probability distribution on
\mathbb{U}(R)
. Consequently, the constraint
\mathrm{Tr}_{R}\left[\rho_{R}\cdot O_{RB}\right]=\frac{1}{M}\mathbb{I}_{B}
implies

\left\|\mathbb{E}_{U_{R}\sim\mathcal{L}(R)}\left[U_{R}O_{RB}U_{R}^{\dagger}\right]\right\|_{\infty}=\frac{1}{M},

(17)

which is one of the key factors of the matrix Chernoff inequality. Conjugating the observable
O_{RB}
by the unitary
U_{R}
leads to the same success probability if the state
\mathcal{N}_{A\rightarrow B}(\phi_{RA})
is appropriately conjugated (see Eq. (
11
)). Since
\phi_{RA}
is the maximally entangled state (recall that we assume
\rho_{R}
is the maximally mixed state and
\phi_{RA}
is a purification of it), we can conjugate
\mathcal{N}_{A\rightarrow B}(\phi_{RA})
with the unitary
U_{R}
by conjugating
\phi_{RA}
with
U_{A}^{\top}
(a property of the maximally entangled state) which can be performed by Alice before applying the channel. Note that as opposed to the position-based coding, we only need one shared copy of the maximally entangled state. This is crucial for bounding the dimension of the measurement operators as this latter appears in the error probability of the matrix Chernoff inequality. Applying a random coding with a unitary
1
-design formed with Pauli operators was used previously for the problem of entanglement-assisted channel coding (see e.g.,
[
30
,
32
]
).

In order to handle the case of an arbitrary state
\rho_{R}
we utilize the input flattening technique from
[
32
]
. The idea is to decompose the input state as

\rho_{R}=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{v}p_{j}\cdot\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{H}_{j}}|}\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{H}_{j}},

(18)

where
v
is the number of distinct eigenvalues of
\rho_{R}
,
\{p_{j}\}_{j=1}^{v}
is a probability distribution, and
\{\mathcal{H}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{v}
are orthogonal subspaces of
R
.
With this in hand, we can sample from the probability distribution
j\sim\{p_{j}\}_{j=1}^{v}
and consider the problem as if we have
\rho_{R}=\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{H}_{j}}|}\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{H}_{j}}
, a maximally mixed state. Our approximation strategy builds on these ideas and chooses the shared entanglement based on shared-randomness samples from
\{p_{j}\}_{j=1}^{v}
. Random unitaries sampled from a unitary
1
-design are applied depending on the corresponding subspaces. Finally, a measurement device is formed depending on the shared-randomness and random unitaries. This EA strategy is described in more details in Section
4.2
and achieves the following performance.

Proposition 6
.

Let
\mathcal{N}_{A\rightarrow B}
be a quantum channel.
Let
\rho_{R}\in\mathcal{S}(R)
be an optimal quantum state maximizing the NS program (
1
).
Let
v
be the number of distinct eigenvalues of
\rho_{R}
. Assume that
\log|A|\geq e^{2}
.
We have

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{EA}}(\mathcal{N},M)\geq\frac{1}{2v\log\left(2ve^{v}M(\dim A)^{v}\dim B\right)}\cdot\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M).

(19)

In the finite block-length regime, we then find

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{EA}}(\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n},e^{nr})\geq\frac{1}{\operatorname{poly}(n)}\cdot\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n},e^{nr}),

(20)

which also explains why this multiplicative error is not harmful is terms of strong converse exponent. To obtain the previous inequality, we use symmetry arguments and show that a near-optimal quantum state maximizing the NS program (
1
) is the de Finetti state
[
12
]
for which we have
v_{n}=\operatorname{poly}(n)

[
27
]
. This allows us to deduce the EA strong converse exponent from the NS one (Corollary
2
).

Corollary 7
.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be a quantum channel and
r\geq 0
. We have that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\suc^{\rm{EA}}(\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n},e^{nr})

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n},e^{nr})

(21)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\alpha\geq 0}\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\left(r-\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{1+\alpha}(\mathcal{N})\right).

(22)

This tight characterization of the strong converse exponent in the quantum setting was first established by
[
33
]
. Moreover,
[
33
]
was the first to introduce the flattening technique for this problem. While our proof also employs this technique, its structure is significantly different from that of
[
33
]
. In particular, our proof relies on the relationship between EA, NS, and MC success probabilities, providing deeper insights into the connection between composite hypothesis testing and entanglement-assisted coding.

Notation

The set of integers between
1
and
n
is denoted by

.
Finite dimensional Hilbert spaces are denoted by
A,B,\dots
and their corresponding dimensions are written as
|A|,|B|,\dots
. The set of unitary operators on the space

\mathbb{U}(A)
.
A positive semi-definite operator is denoted by
\rho\succcurlyeq 0
. Moreover,
\rho\succcurlyeq\sigma

\rho-\sigma\succcurlyeq 0
. For a Hermitian operator
\rho

\rho=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\Pi_{i}
, we denote by

\rho^{-1}=\sum_{i:\lambda_{i}\neq 0}\lambda_{i}^{-1}\Pi_{i}

Quantum states are positive semi-definite operators with trace
1
. The set of quantum states on a Hilbert space
A
is denoted by
\mathcal{S}(A)
. Let
\{\ket{i}\}_{i=1}^{|A|}
be the canonical orthonormal basis of
A
. The maximally mixed state over
A
is
\mathbf{1}_{A}=\frac{1}{|A|}\mathbb{I}_{A}
. The maximally entangled state over
A
is

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi}_{RA}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|A|}}\ket{w}_{RA}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|A|}}\sum_{i=1}^{|A|}\ket{i}_{R}\otimes\ket{i}_{A},

(23)

where
R\simeq A
.

A quantum channel
\mathcal{N}_{A\rightarrow B}
is a completely-positive, trace-preserving map from
\mathcal{S}(A)
to
\mathcal{S}(B)
.
The Choi matrix of
\mathcal{N}_{A\rightarrow B}
is

\displaystyle(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{RB}=\mathcal{N}_{A\rightarrow B}(|w\rangle\!\langle w|_{RA})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{|A|}\ket{i}\bra{j}_{R}\otimes\mathcal{N}(\ket{i}\bra{j})_{B}.

(24)

A quantum-classical or measurement channel
\mathcal{N}_{A\rightarrow X}
is a quantum channel where the output system
X

A measurement device is a POVM (positive operator-valued measure), consisting of a set of positive semi-definite operators that sum to the identity operator
\mathbb{I}
. Measuring a quantum state
\rho
with a POVM
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{x}\}_{x\in\mathcal{X}}
returns the outcome
x\in\mathcal{X}
with probability

.

The sandwiched Rényi divergence
[
37
,
58
]
between
\rho\succcurlyeq 0
and
\sigma\succcurlyeq 0
of order
\alpha>1
is defined as follows

\displaystyle\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}\left(\rho\middle\|\sigma\right)=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\mathrm{Tr}\left[\left(\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}\rho\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}\right)^{\alpha}\right]

(25)

if
\rho\ll\sigma
(i.e., the support of
\rho
is included in the support of
\sigma
). Otherwise, we set
\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}\left(\rho\middle\|\sigma\right)=+\infty
. It extends continuously for
\alpha=1
to the Umegaki relative entropy, given by

D\left(\rho\middle\|\sigma\right)=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho(\log\rho-\log\sigma)\right].

(26)

The quantum hypothesis testing relative entropy is defined as

\displaystyle D_{H}^{\varepsilon}(\rho\|\sigma)=-\log\min\Big\{\mathrm{Tr}\left[\sigma O\right]\Big|\,0\preccurlyeq O\preccurlyeq\mathbb{I},\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho O\right]\geq 1-\varepsilon\Big\}.

(27)

For a quantum channel
\mathcal{N}_{A\rightarrow B}
, the channel’s sandwiched Rényi mutual information of order
\alpha\geq 1
is defined as
[
22
]

\displaystyle\widetilde{I}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{N}_{A\rightarrow B})

\displaystyle=\sup_{\rho_{R}\in\mathcal{S}(R)}\inf_{\sigma_{B}\in\mathcal{S}(B)}\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}\left(\rho_{R}^{1/2}(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{RB}\rho_{R}^{1/2}\middle\|\rho_{R}\otimes\sigma_{B}\right).

(28)

Let
\sigma
be a Hermitian operator with spectral projections
\Pi_{1},\dots,\Pi_{v}
, where
v
is the number of distinct eigenvalues of
\sigma
. The pinching channel associated with
\sigma
is defined as:

\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}(\cdot)=\sum_{i=1}^{v}\Pi_{i}(\cdot)\Pi_{i}.

(29)

The pinching inequality
[
25
,
51
]
states that for any positive semi-definite operator
\rho
,

\rho\preccurlyeq v\cdot\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}(\rho).

(30)

2
Quantum channel coding

In this section, we define the task of quantum channel coding using entanglement-assisted or non-signaling strategies. Then we study the relation between non-signaling assistance and the meta-converse.

2.1
Entanglement-assisted channel coding

We follow
[
56
]
and define an
(n,M)
entanglement-assisted code as follows. Alice and Bob share a pure quantum state
\ket{\Psi}_{E_{A}E_{B}}
where Alice holds
E_{A}
and Bob holds
E_{B}
. Let
[M]
be the set of messages to be transmitted. To send a message
m\in[M]
, Alice applies an encoding channel
\mathcal{E}^{m}_{E_{A}\rightarrow A^{\prime n}}
to her part of the shared entangled state
|\Psi\rangle\!\langle\Psi|_{E_{A}E_{B}}
depending on the message
m
. Then the global state is

\mathcal{E}^{m}_{E_{A}\rightarrow A^{\prime n}}\left(|\Psi\rangle\!\langle\Psi|_{E_{A}E_{B}}\right).

(31)

Alice transmits
A^{\prime n}
over
n
iid copies of the channel
\mathcal{N}_{A^{\prime}\rightarrow B}
, leading to the state

\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n}_{A^{\prime n}\rightarrow B^{n}}\left(\mathcal{E}^{m}_{E_{A}\rightarrow A^{\prime n}}\left(|\Psi\rangle\!\langle\Psi|_{E_{A}E_{B}}\right)\right).

(32)

Upon receiving systems
B^{n}
, Bob performs a POVM
\{\Xi^{m}_{B^{n}E_{B}}\}_{m=1}^{M}
on the channel output
B^{n}
and his part of the shared entanglement
E_{B}
in order to decode the message
m
that Alice sent. Bob returns the outcome of this measurement. Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=\Big(\ket{\Psi}_{E_{A}E_{B}},\{\mathcal{E}^{m}_{E_{A}\rightarrow A^{\prime n}}\}_{m=1}^{M},\{\Xi^{m}_{B^{n}E_{B}}\}_{m=1}^{M}\Big).

(33)

The probability of Bob correctly decoding Alice’s message
m
is

\displaystyle\suc_{\mathcal{C}}(\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n},M,m)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\Xi^{m}_{B^{n}E_{B}}\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n}_{A^{\prime n}\rightarrow B^{n}}\left(\mathcal{E}^{m}_{E_{A}\rightarrow A^{\prime n}}\left(|\Psi\rangle\!\langle\Psi|_{E_{A}E_{B}}\right)\right)\right].

(34)

The average success probability for the coding scheme
\mathcal{C}
is

\displaystyle\suc_{\mathcal{C}}(\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n},M)

\displaystyle=\!\frac{1}{M}\!\sum_{m=1}^{M}\!\mathrm{Tr}\!\left[\Xi^{m}_{B^{n}E_{B}}\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n}_{A^{\prime n}\rightarrow B^{n}}\!\left(\!\mathcal{E}^{m}_{E_{A}\rightarrow A^{\prime n}}\!\left({\Psi}_{E_{A}E_{B}}\right)\!\right)\!\right]\!.

(35)

The rate
r
of communication is

r=\frac{1}{n}\log M,

(36)

and the code
\mathcal{C}
has
\varepsilon
error if
\suc_{\mathcal{C}}(\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n},M)\geq 1-\varepsilon
. A rate
r
of entanglement-assisted classical communication is achievable if there is an
(n,e^{n(r-\delta)})
entanglement-assisted classical code achieving a success probability
1-\varepsilon
for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
\delta>0
, and sufficiently large
n
.
The entanglement-assisted classical capacity
C^{\rm{EA}}(\mathcal{N})
of a quantum channel
\mathcal{N}
is equal to the supremum of all achievable rates.

Finally, the one-shot entanglement-assisted success probability is given as the optimal average success probability over all EA
(1,M)
codes

\suc^{\rm{EA}}(\mathcal{N},M)=\sup_{\mathcal{C}}\suc_{\mathcal{C}}(\mathcal{N},M).

(37)

The one-shot entanglement-assisted error probability can be defined as follows

\varepsilon^{\rm{EA}}(\mathcal{N},M)=1-\suc^{\rm{EA}}(\mathcal{N},M).

(38)

2.2
Non-signaling assisted channel coding

The computation of the optimal entanglement-assisted (EA) success probability in (
37
) is notoriously difficult, as it requires optimization over shared entangled states, encoding channels, and decoding measurement devices, all of which can involve systems with unbounded dimension. A natural relaxation of this optimization is obtained by allowing more powerful correlations known as non-signaling
[
36
]
. Strategies using this assistance encompass entanglement-assisted strategies, though communication cannot be achieved using non-signaling correlations alone.

A non-signaling assisted code corresponds to a superchannel which is non-signaling (does not permit communication by itself) from the encoder to the decoder and vice-versa. More precisely, a non-signaling channel between Alice and Bob whose input systems are
A_{i},B_{i}
and output systems are
A_{o},B_{o}
has a positive semi-definite Choi matrix
J_{A_{i}A_{o}B_{i}B_{o}}
satisfying (e.g.,
[
17
]
):

\displaystyle J_{A_{i}B_{i}B_{o}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}_{A_{i}}\otimes J_{B_{i}B_{o}},

\displaystyle(A\nrightarrow B),

(39)

\displaystyle J_{A_{i}A_{o}B_{i}}

\displaystyle=J_{A_{i}A_{o}}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{B_{i}},

\displaystyle(B\nrightarrow A).

(40)

Let
\mathcal{N}\!\mathcal{S}
be the set of non-signaling superchannels. The optimal non-signaling success probability of a quantum-channel
\mathcal{N}
for sending
M
messages is

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\Pi\in\mathcal{N}\!\mathcal{S}}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\bra{m}\Pi(\mathcal{N})(|m\rangle\!\langle m|)\ket{m},

(41)

where
\Pi(\mathcal{N})
is the effective channel.
The non-signaling error probability of a quantum-channel
\mathcal{N}
for sending
M
messages can be defined as follows

\varepsilon^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M)=1-\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M).

(42)

It is known that the success probability (
41
) is the solution of the following SDP program
[
36
,
55
]

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M)=\sup_{\rho_{R},\Lambda_{RB}}\quad

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\Lambda_{RB}(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{RB}\right]

subject to

\displaystyle\rho_{R}\in\mathcal{S}(R),

\displaystyle\Lambda_{B}=\mathbb{I}_{B},

\displaystyle 0\preccurlyeq\Lambda_{RB}\preccurlyeq M\rho_{R}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B},

(43)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\rho_{R},\Lambda_{RB}}\quad

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left[\Lambda_{RB}(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{RB}\right]

subject to

\displaystyle\rho_{R}\in\mathcal{S}(R),

\displaystyle\Lambda_{B}=\frac{1}{M}\mathbb{I}_{B},

\displaystyle 0\preccurlyeq\Lambda_{RB}\preccurlyeq\rho_{R}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B}.

(44)

Unlike the entanglement-assisted success probability (
37
), the non-signaling success probability is computationally accessible. Furthermore, since entanglement-assisted strategies are non-signaling, we have the obvious inequality

\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M)\geq\suc^{\rm{EA}}(\mathcal{N},M).

(45)

One of the goals of this paper is to design approximation algorithms that achieve a reverse type inequality of (
45
) with as small an (additive or multiplicative) error as possible.

2.3
Non-signaling value and meta-converse

In the previous section, we observed that non-signaling strategies are useful for approximating the entanglement-assisted success probability of coding over quantum channels. It turns out that the non-signaling success probability is closely related to the well-known meta-converse
[
41
,
35
,
36
,
55
]
. In fact, they are even equal in the classical setting
[
35
]
.
The meta-converse error probability for one-shot coding over a quantum channel
\mathcal{N}
and with a communication size
M
is
[
36
]

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\rm{MC}}(\mathcal{N},M)=\inf\Big\{\varepsilon\in[0,1]\;\Big|\;\sup_{\rho_{R}\in\mathcal{S}(R)}\inf_{\sigma_{B}\in\mathcal{S}(B)}\!D_{H}^{\varepsilon}\!\left(\rho_{R}^{1/2}(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{RB}\rho_{R}^{1/2}\big\|\rho_{R}\otimes\sigma_{B}\right)\geq\log M\!\Big\}.

(46)

It is known that the meta-converse success probability is the solution of the following SDP program
[
36
,
55
]

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{MC}}(\mathcal{N},M)=\sup_{\rho_{R},\Lambda_{RB}}\quad

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\Lambda_{RB}(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{RB}\right]

subject to

\displaystyle\rho_{R}\in\mathcal{S}(R),

\displaystyle\Lambda_{B}\preccurlyeq\mathbb{I}_{B},

\displaystyle 0\preccurlyeq\Lambda_{RB}\preccurlyeq M\rho_{R}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B}.

(47)

Observe that the difference between the meta-converse program (
47
) and the non-signaling program (
43
) for the success probability lies in the constraint
\Lambda_{B}\preccurlyeq\mathbb{I}_{B}
versus
\Lambda_{B}=\mathbb{I}_{B}
. This implies that
\suc^{\rm{MC}}(\mathcal{N},M)\geq\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M)
, since the meta-converse allows for a looser constraint on the measurement operator
\Lambda_{B}
. The authors of
[
55
]
show that the meta-converse corresponds to non-signaling correlations assisted by
1
-bit of perfect communication (known as activation). In the following we provide a reverse inequality between the meta-converse and non-signaling success probabilities, achieving this with a multiplicative error and without the need for activation.

Proposition 8
.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be a quantum channel and
M\geq 1
.
We have that

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{MC}}(\mathcal{N},M)

\displaystyle\geq\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M)\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right)\cdot\suc^{\rm{MC}}(\mathcal{N},M).

(48)

Proof.

The NS and MC programs are stated in (
43
) and (
47
) respectively.
The inequality
\suc^{\rm{MC}}(\mathcal{N},M)\geq\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M)
is clear. Let us prove the remaining inequality
\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M)\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right)\cdot\suc^{\rm{MC}}(\mathcal{N},M).
To this end, we consider
(\rho_{R},\Lambda_{RB})
an optimal solution of (
47
) with size
M-1
. We construct
\Lambda^{\prime}_{RB}=\Lambda_{RB}+\rho_{R}\otimes(\mathbb{I}_{B}-\Lambda_{B})
that satisfies:

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\prime}_{RB}

\displaystyle\succcurlyeq\Lambda_{RB}\succcurlyeq 0,

(49)

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\prime}_{B}

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{B}+(\mathbb{I}_{B}-\Lambda_{B})=\mathbb{I}_{B},

(50)

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\prime}_{RB}

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{RB}+\rho_{R}\otimes(\mathbb{I}_{B}-\Lambda_{B})

(51)

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq(M-1)\rho_{R}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B}+\rho_{R}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B}

(52)

\displaystyle=M\rho_{R}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B}.

(53)

So
(\rho_{R},\Lambda^{\prime}_{RB})
is a feasible solution to the program (
43
) of size
M
. Hence, we have

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{M}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\Lambda^{\prime}_{RB}\cdot(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{RB}\right]

(54)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{M}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\Lambda_{RB}\cdot(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{RB}\right]

(55)

\displaystyle=\frac{M-1}{M}\cdot\suc^{\rm{MC}}(\mathcal{N},M-1)

(56)

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right)\cdot\suc^{\rm{MC}}(\mathcal{N},M),

(57)

where we used in the last inequality
\suc^{\rm{MC}}(\mathcal{N},M-1)\geq\suc^{\rm{MC}}(\mathcal{N},M)
that can be easily checked with the following formulation obtained from (
47
) by making the change of variable
\Lambda_{RB}\leftarrow\frac{1}{M}\Lambda_{RB}
:

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{MC}}(\mathcal{N},M)=\sup_{\rho_{R},\Lambda_{RB}}\quad

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left[\Lambda_{RB}(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{RB}\right]

subject to

\displaystyle\rho_{R}\in\mathcal{S}(R),

\displaystyle\Lambda_{B}\preccurlyeq\frac{1}{M}\mathbb{I}_{B},

\displaystyle 0\preccurlyeq\Lambda_{RB}\preccurlyeq\rho_{R}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B}.

(58)

∎

Proposition
8
demonstrates that as the number of messages grows, the optimal success probabilities for MC and NS strategies converge. Since the approximation is multiplicative with respect to success probabilities, this rounding result has a direct implication in terms of the strong converse exponent. The strong converse exponents for the meta-converse and non-signaling error probabilities are defined as follows:

\displaystyle E^{\rm{MC}}(\mathcal{N},r)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\suc^{\rm{MC}}(\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n},e^{nr}),

(59)

\displaystyle E^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},r)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n},e^{nr}).

(60)

Using Proposition
8
we immediately deduce that the meta-converse and non-signaling strategies have the same strong converse exponent:

Corollary 9
.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be a quantum channel. For all
r\geq 0
, we have that

\displaystyle E^{\rm{MC}}(\mathcal{N},r)=E^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},r).

(61)

The meta-converse (
46
) corresponds to the problem of composite hypothesis testing. We deduce the achievability strong converse exponent of the meta-converse from the one established for composite hypothesis testing by
[
27
]
:

Proposition 10
.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be a quantum channel. For all
r\geq 0
, we have that

\displaystyle E^{\rm{MC}}(\mathcal{N},r)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\suc^{\rm{MC}}(\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n},e^{nr})

(62)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\alpha\geq 0}\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\left(r-\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{1+\alpha}(\mathcal{N})\right).

(63)

Proof.

We have that from Proposition
19
:

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{MC}}(\mathcal{N},M)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\rho_{R}\in\mathcal{S}(R)}\sup_{0\preccurlyeq O\preccurlyeq\mathbb{I}}\Big\{\,\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho_{R}^{1/2}(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{RB}\rho_{R}^{1/2}\cdot O\right]\;\Big|\;\sup_{\sigma_{B}\in\mathcal{S}(B)}\mathrm{Tr}\left[(\rho_{R}\otimes\sigma_{B})O\right]\leq\frac{1}{M}\Big\}.

So, we have for
n\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{MC}}(\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n},e^{nr})

\displaystyle=\sup_{\rho_{R^{n}}\in\mathcal{S}(R^{n})}\sup_{0\preccurlyeq O\preccurlyeq\mathbb{I}}\Big\{\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho_{R^{n}}^{1/2}(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{R^{n}B^{n}}^{\otimes n}\rho_{R^{n}}^{1/2}\cdot O\right]\Big|\;\sup_{\sigma_{B^{n}}\in\mathcal{S}(B^{n})}\mathrm{Tr}\left[(\rho_{R^{n}}\otimes\sigma_{B^{n}})O\right]\leq e^{-nr}\Big\}

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{\rho_{R}\in\mathcal{S}(R)}\sup_{0\preccurlyeq O\preccurlyeq\mathbb{I}}\Big\{\mathrm{Tr}\left[\big(\rho_{R}^{1/2}(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{RB}\rho_{R}^{1/2}\big)^{\otimes n}\cdot O\right]\Big|\;\sup_{\sigma_{B^{n}}\in\mathcal{S}(B^{n})}\mathrm{Tr}\left[(\rho_{R}^{\otimes n}\otimes\sigma_{B^{n}})O\right]\leq e^{-nr}\Big\}

(64)

where we choose the state to be iid, i.e.,
\rho_{R^{n}}=(\rho_{R})^{\otimes n}
. From the strong converse result for the composite hypothesis testing problem of
[
27
, Theorem 14]
we deduce that:

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{n}\log\sup_{0\preccurlyeq O\preccurlyeq\mathbb{I}}\left\{\mathrm{Tr}\left[\big(\rho_{R}^{1/2}(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{RB}\rho_{R}^{1/2}\big)^{\otimes n}\cdot O\right]\,\middle|\;\sup_{\sigma_{B^{n}}\in\mathcal{S}(B^{n})}\mathrm{Tr}\left[(\rho_{R}^{\otimes n}\otimes\sigma_{B^{n}})O\right]\leq e^{-nr}\right\}

(65)

\displaystyle\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}\sup_{\alpha>0}\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\left(r-\inf_{\sigma_{B}\in\mathcal{S}(B)}\widetilde{I}_{1+\alpha}(\rho_{R}^{1/2}(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{RB}\rho_{R}^{1/2}\|\rho_{R}\otimes\sigma_{B})\right).

(66)

Hence, we get

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\suc^{\rm{MC}}(\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n},e^{nr})

(67)

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{\rho_{R}\in\mathcal{S}(R)}\sup_{\alpha\geq 0}\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\left(r-\inf_{\sigma_{B}\in\mathcal{S}(B)}\widetilde{I}_{1+\alpha}(\rho_{R}^{1/2}(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{RB}\rho_{R}^{1/2}\|\rho_{R}\otimes\sigma_{B})\right)

(68)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\alpha\geq 0}\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\left(r-\widetilde{I}_{1+\alpha}(\mathcal{N})\right),

(69)

where the last equality can be proven using Sion’s minimax theorem (see
[
33
, Eqs. (100)–(102)]
).

∎

On the other hand, the converse strong converse exponent is proven by
[
22
]
for entanglement-assisted strategies.

Proposition 11
.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be a quantum channel. For all
r\geq 0
, we have that

\displaystyle E^{\rm{MC}}(\mathcal{N},r)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\suc^{\rm{MC}}(\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n},e^{nr})

(70)

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{\alpha\geq 0}\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\left(r-\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{1+\alpha}(\mathcal{N})\right).

(71)

This result is stated in
[
22
]
for EA strategies. By inspection, the proof applies for the meta-converse as well. For the reader’s convenience, we include a detailed proof of the converse result of the meta-converse strong converse exponent in Appendix
C
. By combining Corollary
9
, Proposition
10
, and Proposition
11
, we deduce the NS and MC strong converse exponent.

Corollary 12
.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be a quantum channel. For all
r\geq 0
, we have that

\displaystyle E^{\rm{MC}}(\mathcal{N},r)

\displaystyle=E^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},r)=\sup_{\alpha\geq 0}\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\left(r-\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{1+\alpha}(\mathcal{N})\right).

(72)

3
Approximation algorithms in the quantum-classical setting

In this section, we consider the restricted class of quantum-classical channels with classical output system. We are able to prove approximation results that relate the entanglement-assisted and non-signaling success probabilities.

Proposition 13
.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be a quantum-classical channel and
M,M^{\prime}\geq 1
. We have the following inequality between the entanglement-assisted and non-signaling success probabilities

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{EA}}(\mathcal{N},M^{\prime})\geq\frac{M}{M^{\prime}}\left(1-\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right)^{M^{\prime}}\right)\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M).

(73)

In particular, when
M^{\prime}=M
, we obtain

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{EA}}(\mathcal{N},M)

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right)^{M}\right)\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M)

(74)

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{e}\right)\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M).

(75)

Moreover, when
M^{\prime}=o(M)
, we have that

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{EA}}(\mathcal{N},M^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\frac{M^{\prime}}{2M}+o\Big(\frac{M^{\prime}}{M}\Big)\right)\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M).

(76)

This result extends the classical rounding results from
[
3
]
to the quantum-classical setting with entanglement assistance while maintaining the same approximation guarantees. Additionally,
[
3
]
proved that similar approximation ratio (between shared randomness and non-signaling success probabilities) is optimal for classical channels. Since
\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M)\geq\suc^{\rm{EA}}(\mathcal{N},M)\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{e}\right)\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M)
, we conclude that EA and NS have the same strong converse exponent. Therefore, by Corollary
12
, we obtain the following characterization of the EA strong converse exponent.

Corollary 14
.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be a quantum-classical channel. For all
r\geq 0
, we have that

\displaystyle E^{\rm{EA}}(\mathcal{N},r)

\displaystyle=E^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},r)=\sup_{\alpha\geq 0}\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\left(r-\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{1+\alpha}(\mathcal{N})\right).

(77)

Proof of Proposition
13
.

Let
(\Lambda_{RB},\rho_{R})
be an optimal solution to the NS success probability program (
44
).
Let
M^{\prime}
be the size of the messages to be transmitted with an entanglement-assisted strategy.
We consider the following scheme inspired from the position-based coding of
[
1
]
and the sequential decoder of
[
57
]
:

Shared entanglement.

Let
\ket{\phi}_{RR^{\prime}}=\rho_{R}^{1/2}\ket{w}_{RR^{\prime}}
be a purification of
\rho_{R}
where
R^{\prime}\simeq R
. The shared entanglement state is

\bigotimes_{m=1}^{M^{\prime}}\phi_{R_{m}R_{m}^{\prime}},

(78)

where
R^{\prime}_{1}\cdots R^{\prime}_{M^{\prime}}
systems are held by Alice while
R_{1}\cdots R_{M^{\prime}}
systems are held by Bob.

Encoding.

To send the message
m\in[M^{\prime}]
, Alice places the system
R^{\prime}_{m}
in
A^{\prime}
and traces out the rest of her part of the shared entanglement. In other words, she applies the map
\mathrm{id}_{R^{\prime}_{m}\rightarrow A^{\prime}}\otimes\bigotimes_{l\neq m}\mathrm{Tr}_{R^{\prime}_{l}}\left[\cdot\right]
. The resulting state is

\displaystyle\sigma_{R_{1}R_{2}\cdots R_{M^{\prime}}A^{\prime}}(m)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{id}_{R^{\prime}_{m}\rightarrow A^{\prime}}\otimes\bigotimes_{l\neq m}\mathrm{Tr}_{R^{\prime}_{l}}\left[\textstyle\bigotimes_{l=1}^{M^{\prime}}\phi_{R_{l}R_{l}^{\prime}}\right]=\phi_{R_{m}A^{\prime}}\otimes\bigotimes_{l\neq m}\rho_{R_{l}}.

(79)

Transmission.

Alice transmits
A^{\prime}
over the channel
\mathcal{N}_{A^{\prime}\rightarrow B}
. The global state is then

\displaystyle\zeta_{R_{1}R_{2}\cdots R_{M^{\prime}}B}(m)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}_{A^{\prime}\rightarrow B}(\sigma_{R_{1}R_{2}\cdots R_{M^{\prime}}A^{\prime}}(m))=\mathcal{N}_{A^{\prime}\rightarrow B}\left(\phi_{R_{m}A^{\prime}}\right)\otimes\bigotimes_{l\neq m}\rho_{R_{l}}.

(80)

Decoding.

Since
(\Lambda_{RB},\rho_{R})
is a solution of the program (
44
), they satisfy
0\preccurlyeq\Lambda_{RB}\preccurlyeq\rho_{R}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B}
, thus we can define the observable

\displaystyle 0\preccurlyeq O_{RB}=\rho_{R}^{-1/2}\Lambda_{RB}\rho_{R}^{-1/2}\preccurlyeq\mathbb{I}_{RB}.

(81)

Bob performs the measurement:

\displaystyle\left\{\Xi_{R_{1}R_{2}\cdots R_{M^{\prime}}B}(m)\right\}_{m=1}^{M^{\prime}}\bigcup\left\{\Xi_{R_{1}R_{2}\cdots R_{M^{\prime}}B}^{\star}\right\},\quad\text{with }

(82)

\displaystyle\Xi_{R_{1}R_{2}\cdots R_{M^{\prime}}B}(m)=\sqrt{\mathbb{I}-O_{R_{1}B}}\cdots\sqrt{\mathbb{I}-O_{R_{m-1}B}}O_{R_{m}B}\sqrt{\mathbb{I}-O_{R_{m-1}B}}\cdots\sqrt{\mathbb{I}-O_{R_{1}B}},

(83)

\displaystyle\quad\;\;\Xi_{R_{1}R_{2}\cdots R_{M^{\prime}}B}^{\star}=\mathbb{I}_{R_{1}R_{2}\cdots R_{M^{\prime}}B}-\sum_{m=1}^{M^{\prime}}\Xi_{R_{1}R_{2}\cdots R_{M^{\prime}}B}(m).

(84)

This measurement corresponds to sequentially measuring
R_{t}B
using the POVM
\{O_{R_{t}B},\mathbb{I}-O_{R_{t}B}\}
for
t=1,2,\dots
and returning the first index
t
for which we get the first outcome (corresponding to the observable
O_{R_{t}B}
).
Note that the last operator in (
82
) can be written as

\displaystyle\Xi_{R_{1}\cdots R_{M^{\prime}}B}^{\star}=\mathbb{I}\!-\!\sqrt{\mathbb{I}\!-\!O_{R_{1}B}}\cdots\sqrt{\mathbb{I}\!-\!O_{R_{M^{\prime}-1}B}}(\mathbb{I}\!-\!O_{R_{M^{\prime}}B})\sqrt{\mathbb{I}\!-\!O_{R_{M^{\prime}-1}B}}\cdots\sqrt{\mathbb{I}\!-\!O_{R_{1}B}},

(85)

which shows that it is indeed an observable (using
0\preccurlyeq O_{RB}\preccurlyeq\mathbb{I}_{RB}
) and the set (
82
) is a valid POVM.
If we observe an outcome corresponding to the operator
\Xi_{R_{1}R_{2}\cdots R_{M^{\prime}}B}^{\star}
we return
0
.
Since
B
is a classical system we have that

\forall l\neq l^{\prime}:\quad O_{R_{l}B}O_{R_{l^{\prime}}B}=O_{R_{l^{\prime}}B}O_{R_{l}B},

(86)

thus the measurement operators can be written as

\displaystyle\Xi_{R_{1}R_{2}\cdots R_{M^{\prime}}B}(m)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\mathbb{I}-O_{R_{1}B}}\cdots\sqrt{\mathbb{I}-O_{R_{m-1}B}}O_{R_{m}B}\sqrt{\mathbb{I}-O_{R_{m-1}B}}\cdots\sqrt{\mathbb{I}-O_{R_{1}B}}

(87)

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{I}-O_{R_{1}B})\cdots(\mathbb{I}-O_{R_{m-1}B})O_{R_{m}B}.

(88)

Analysis of the scheme.

The success probability of decoding the
m^{\text{th}}
message can be calculated as follows:

\displaystyle\suc(\mathcal{N},M^{\prime},m)

(89)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\zeta_{R_{1}R_{2}\cdots R_{M^{\prime}}B}(m)\cdot\Xi_{R_{1}R_{2}\cdots R_{M^{\prime}}B}(m)\right]

(90)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\mathcal{N}_{A^{\prime}\rightarrow B}\left(\phi_{R_{m}A^{\prime}}\right)\otimes\bigotimes_{l\neq m}\rho_{R_{l}}\cdot(\mathbb{I}-O_{R_{1}B})\cdots(\mathbb{I}-O_{R_{m-1}B})O_{R_{m}B}\right]

(91)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho_{R_{m}}^{1/2}\mathcal{N}_{A^{\prime}\rightarrow B}\left(w_{R_{m}A^{\prime}}\right)\rho_{R_{m}}^{1/2}\otimes\bigotimes_{l\neq m}\rho_{R_{l}}\cdot(\mathbb{I}-O_{R_{1}B})\cdots(\mathbb{I}-O_{R_{m-1}B})O_{R_{m}B}\right]

(92)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\left[(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{R_{m}B}\cdot(\rho_{R_{1}}-\Lambda_{R_{1}B})\cdots(\rho_{R_{m-1}}-\Lambda_{R_{m-1}B})\Lambda_{R_{m}B}\right]

(93)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\left[(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{R_{m}B}\cdot(\mathbb{I}_{B}-\Lambda_{B})\cdots(\mathbb{I}_{B}-\Lambda_{B})\Lambda_{R_{m}B}\right]

(94)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right)^{m-1}\mathrm{Tr}\left[(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{R_{m}B}\cdot\Lambda_{R_{m}B}\right]

(95)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right)^{m-1}\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M),

(96)

where we used
\Lambda_{B}=\frac{1}{M}\mathbb{I}_{B}
and
\mathrm{Tr}\left[(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{RB}\cdot\Lambda_{RB}\right]=\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M)
.

Hence, the average success probability of this scheme can be calculated as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M^{\prime}}\sum_{m=1}^{M^{\prime}}\suc(\mathcal{N},M^{\prime},m)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M^{\prime}}\sum_{m=1}^{M^{\prime}}\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right)^{m-1}\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M)

(97)

\displaystyle=\frac{M}{M^{\prime}}\left(1-\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right)^{M^{\prime}}\right)\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M).

(98)

Finally we deduce the desired bound on the EA success probability:

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{EA}}(\mathcal{N},M^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{M^{\prime}}\sum_{m=1}^{M^{\prime}}\suc(\mathcal{N},M^{\prime},m)

(99)

\displaystyle=\frac{M}{M^{\prime}}\left(1-\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right)^{M^{\prime}}\right)\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M).

(100)

∎

The optimality of Proposition
13
remains unclear, as entanglement-assisted strategies are more challenging to manage compared to shared-randomness strategies (e.g.,
[
40
,
9
,
8
]
). Moreover, we are unable to establish similar approximations as in Proposition
13
in the fully quantum setting. The main challenge is that, in the quantum setting, we can no longer simplify the measurement operators as done in (
88
). Consequently, the analysis of the sequential decoder (
82
) becomes non-trivial and does not yield the exact success probabilities as in (
96
). In the next section, we present alternative approximation results for channel coding in the quantum setting.

4
Approximation algorithms in the quantum setting

In this section, we explore approximation algorithms for channel coding in the general quantum setting. Our objective is to design algorithms that approximate the entanglement-assisted success probability. We begin by presenting a simple approximation with an additive error in Section
4.1
, followed by a more involved approximation that achieves a multiplicative error in Section
4.2
.

4.1
Approximation with an additive error

Our first attempt at designing approximation algorithms uses the same shared entanglement and the position-based coding
[
1
,
43
]
, similar to what we have done in the quantum-classical setting in Section
3
. However, to deal with the non-commutative difficulty that arises in the quantum setting, we use the well-known decoder and inequality of Hayashi-Nagaoka
[
26
]
.

Proposition 15
.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be quantum channel and
M,M^{\prime}\geq 1
. We have the following inequality between the entanglement-assisted and non-signaling error probabilities

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\rm{EA}}(\mathcal{N},M^{\prime})\leq 2\varepsilon^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M)+4\frac{M^{\prime}-1}{M}.

(101)

Moreover, we have the following inequality between the entanglement-assisted and non-signaling success probabilities

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{EA}}(\mathcal{N},M^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M)-5\sqrt{\frac{M^{\prime}}{M}}.

(102)

This proposition is similar to what was achieved for classical-quantum channels using shared-randomness strategies by
[
21
]
. Additionally, it implies that non-signaling correlations do not allow to improve the entanglement-assisted classical capacity of a quantum channel
[
31
]
. Finally, for an approximation in terms of error probabilities, the additive error
O(\frac{M^{\prime}}{M})
seems to be unavoidable. This can be seen by the optimality proof in the classical setting of
[
3
]
. In terms of success probabilities, the additive error
O\big(\sqrt{\frac{M^{\prime}}{M}}\big)
is not sufficient enough to deduce optimal strong converse exponents. For this reason, in the next section, we explore more involved approximation algorithms that achieve small multiplicative errors.

Proof of Proposition
15
.

Let
(\Lambda_{RB},\rho_{R})
be an optimal solution of the NS success probability program (
44
). Let
M^{\prime}
be the number of the messages to be transmitted with an entanglement-assisted strategy.
We consider the following scheme inspired by the position-based coding of
[
1
]
and Hayashi-Nagaoka decoder
[
26
]
:

Shared entanglement.

Let
\ket{\phi}_{RR^{\prime}}=\rho_{R}^{1/2}\ket{w}_{RR^{\prime}}
be a purification of
\rho_{R}
where
R^{\prime}\simeq R
. The shared entanglement state is

\bigotimes_{m=1}^{M^{\prime}}\phi_{R_{m}R_{m}^{\prime}},

(103)

where
R^{\prime}_{1}\cdots R^{\prime}_{M^{\prime}}
systems are held by Alice while
R_{1}\cdots R_{M^{\prime}}
systems are held by Bob.

Encoding.

To send the message
m\in[M^{\prime}]
, Alice places the system
R^{\prime}_{m}
in
A^{\prime}
and traces out the rest of her part of the shared entanglement. In other words, she applies the map
\mathrm{id}_{R^{\prime}_{m}\rightarrow A^{\prime}}\otimes\bigotimes_{l\neq m}\mathrm{Tr}_{R^{\prime}_{l}}\left[\cdot\right]
. The resulting state is

\displaystyle\sigma_{R_{1}R_{2}\cdots R_{M^{\prime}}A^{\prime}}(m)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{id}_{R^{\prime}_{m}\rightarrow A^{\prime}}\otimes\bigotimes_{l\neq m}\operatorname{Tr}_{R^{\prime}_{l}}\bigg[\bigotimes_{l=1}^{M^{\prime}}\phi_{R_{l}R_{l}^{\prime}}\bigg]=\phi_{R_{m}A^{\prime}}\otimes\bigotimes_{l\neq m}\rho_{R_{l}}.

(104)

Transmission.

Alice transmits
A^{\prime}
over the channel
\mathcal{N}_{A^{\prime}\rightarrow B}
. The global state is then

\displaystyle\zeta_{R_{1}R_{2}\cdots R_{M^{\prime}}B}(m)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}_{A^{\prime}\rightarrow B}(\sigma_{R_{1}R_{2}\cdots R_{M^{\prime}}A^{\prime}}(m))=\mathcal{N}_{A^{\prime}\rightarrow B}\left(\phi_{R_{m}A^{\prime}}\right)\otimes\bigotimes_{l\neq m}\rho_{R_{l}}.

(105)

Decoding.

Since
(\Lambda_{RB},\rho_{R})
is a solution of the program (
44
), they satisfy
0\preccurlyeq\Lambda_{RB}\preccurlyeq\rho_{R}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B}
thus we can define the observable

\displaystyle 0\preccurlyeq O_{RB}=\rho_{R}^{-1/2}\Lambda_{RB}\rho_{R}^{-1/2}\preccurlyeq\mathbb{I}_{RB}.

(106)

Bob performs the measurement
[
26
]

\displaystyle\left\{\Xi_{R_{1}R_{2}\cdots R_{M^{\prime}}B}(m)=\left(\textstyle\sum_{l=1}^{M^{\prime}}O_{R_{l}B}\right)^{-1/2}O_{R_{m}B}\left(\textstyle\sum_{l=1}^{M^{\prime}}O_{R_{l}B}\right)^{-1/2}\right\}_{m=1}^{M^{\prime}}.

(107)

Analysis of the scheme.
We rely on the following inequality
[
26
]
valid for all
0\preccurlyeq A\preccurlyeq\mathbb{I}
,
B\succcurlyeq 0
and
c>0
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}-(A+B)^{-1/2}A(A+B)^{-1/2}\preccurlyeq(1+c)(\mathbb{I}-A)+(2+c+c^{-1})B.

(108)

Using this inequality, the error probability of decoding the
m^{\text{th}}
message can be controlled as follows:

\displaystyle\varepsilon(\mathcal{N},M^{\prime},m)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\zeta_{R_{1}\cdots R_{M^{\prime}}B}(m)\cdot(\mathbb{I}-\Xi(m))_{R_{1}R_{2}\cdots R_{M^{\prime}}B}\right]

(109)

\displaystyle\leq(1+c)\mathrm{Tr}\left[\zeta_{R_{1}\cdots R_{M^{\prime}}B}(m)\cdot(\mathbb{I}-O_{R_{m}B})\right]

(110)

\displaystyle\;\;+\!(2+c+c^{-1})\mathrm{Tr}\left[\zeta_{R_{1}\cdots R_{M^{\prime}}B}(m)(\textstyle\sum_{l\neq m}O_{R_{l}B})\right]

(111)

Recall that
\ket{\phi}_{RA^{\prime}}=\rho_{R}^{1/2}\ket{w}_{RA^{\prime}}
.
The first term (
110
) can be simplified as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left[\zeta_{R_{1}R_{2}\cdots R_{M^{\prime}}B}(m)\cdot(\mathbb{I}-O_{R_{m}B})\right]

\displaystyle=1-\mathrm{Tr}\left[\mathcal{N}_{A^{\prime}\rightarrow B}\left(\phi_{R_{m}A^{\prime}}\right)\otimes\bigotimes_{l\neq m}\rho_{R_{l}}\cdot\frac{\Lambda_{R_{m}B}}{\rho_{R_{m}}}\right]

(112)

\displaystyle=1-\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho_{R_{m}}^{1/2}\mathcal{N}_{A^{\prime}\rightarrow B}\left(w_{R_{m}A^{\prime}}\right)\rho_{R_{m}}^{1/2}\cdot\frac{\Lambda_{R_{m}B}}{\rho_{R_{m}}}\right]

(113)

\displaystyle=1-\mathrm{Tr}\left[(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{R_{m}B}\cdot\Lambda_{R_{m}B}\right]

(114)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M).

(115)

The second term (
111
) can be calculated as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left[\zeta_{R_{1}R_{2}\cdots R_{M^{\prime}}B}(m)\cdot(\textstyle\sum_{l\neq m}O_{R_{l}B})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\mathcal{N}_{A^{\prime}\rightarrow B}\left(\phi_{R_{m}A^{\prime}}\right)\otimes\bigotimes_{l\neq m}\rho_{R_{l}}\cdot(\textstyle\sum_{l\neq m}\frac{\Lambda_{R_{l}B}}{\rho_{R_{l}}})\right]

(116)

\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{l\neq m}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\mathcal{N}_{A^{\prime}\rightarrow B}\left(\rho_{A^{\prime}}\right)\cdot\Lambda_{B}\right]

(117)

\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{l\neq m}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\mathcal{N}_{A^{\prime}\rightarrow B}\left(\rho_{A^{\prime}}\right)\cdot\frac{1}{M}\mathbb{I}_{B}\right]

(118)

\displaystyle=\frac{M^{\prime}-1}{M}.

(119)

Hence from (
110
), (
111
), (
115
) and (
119
) we deduce that the average error probability is bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\rm{EA}}(\mathcal{N},M^{\prime})\leq\frac{1}{M^{\prime}}\sum_{m=1}^{M^{\prime}}\varepsilon(\mathcal{N},M^{\prime},m)\leq(1+c)\varepsilon^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M)+(2+c+c^{-1})\frac{M^{\prime}-1}{M}.

(120)

In particular for
c=1
we deduce

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\rm{EA}}(\mathcal{N},M^{\prime})\leq 2\varepsilon^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M)+4\frac{M^{\prime}-1}{M}.

(121)

In terms of success probability we have that from (
120
):

\displaystyle 1-\varepsilon^{\rm{EA}}(\mathcal{N},M^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq 1-\varepsilon^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M)-c\varepsilon^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M)-(2+c+c^{-1})\frac{M^{\prime}-1}{M}.

(122)

Then, if
M^{\prime}\leq M
, we choose
c=\sqrt{\frac{M^{\prime}}{M}}\leq 1
and obtain

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{EA}}(\mathcal{N},M^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M)-2\sqrt{\frac{M^{\prime}}{M}}-3\frac{M^{\prime}}{M}

(123)

\displaystyle\geq\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M)-5\sqrt{\frac{M^{\prime}}{M}}.

(124)

If
M^{\prime}>M
, this inequality holds trivially.
∎

4.2
Approximation with a multiplicative error

In this section, we focus on obtaining an approximation of the entanglement-assisted success probability with a small multiplicative error in the quantum setting, as similar results in the classical, classical-quantum, and quantum-classical settings have already been established in
[
3
,
21
]
, and in Section
3
, respectively. To this end, we round a non-signaling strategy to an entanglement-assisted one. The program of the non-signaling success probability (
44
) can be written as

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\rho_{R}\in\mathcal{S}(R)}\sup_{\Lambda_{RB}}\left\{\mathrm{Tr}\left[\Lambda_{RB}\cdot(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{RB}\right]\,\middle|\,\Lambda_{B}=\tfrac{1}{M}\mathbb{I}_{B},\,0\preccurlyeq\Lambda_{RB}\preccurlyeq\rho_{R}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B}\right\}

(125)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\rho_{R}\in\mathcal{S}(R)}\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M,\rho),

(126)

where we introduce
\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M,\rho)
for
\rho_{R}\in\mathcal{S}(R)
defined as:

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M,\rho)=\sup_{\Lambda_{RB}}\quad

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left[\Lambda_{RB}(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{RB}\right]

subject to

\displaystyle\Lambda_{B}=\frac{1}{M}\mathbb{I}_{B},

\displaystyle 0\preccurlyeq\Lambda_{RB}\preccurlyeq\rho_{R}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B}.

(127)

We have the following rounding result of the non-signaling success probability in the quantum setting.

Proposition 16
.

Let
\mathcal{N}_{A\rightarrow B}
be a quantum channel,
\rho_{R}\in\mathcal{S}(R)
be a quantum state and
M\geq 1
.
Let
v
be the number of distinct eigenvalues of
\rho_{R}
. Assume that
\log|A|\geq e^{2}
.
We have that

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{EA}}(\mathcal{N},M)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2v\log(2vMe^{v}|A|^{v}|B|)}\cdot\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M,\rho).

(128)

This approximation of the success probability is not as clean as the one established for quantum-classical channels in Proposition
13
. However, since the multiplicative error in Eq. (
128
) depends logarithmically on the size of the channel
\mathcal{N}
, the communication size, and the number of distinct eigenvalues of the state
\rho_{R}
, we will see in Section
4.3
that in terms of the strong converse exponent, this approximation is largely sufficient.

To prove Proposition
16
, we combine ideas from the classical-quantum rounding result of
[
21
]
, coding using the decoupling technique
[
30
,
33
]
, position-based coding
[
1
]
, and the flattening input state technique
[
33
]
.

Proof of Proposition
16
.

Let
A^{\prime}\simeq R\simeq A
. Write
R=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{v}R_{j}
and

\displaystyle\rho_{R}=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{v}\lambda_{j}\Pi^{j}_{R}=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{v}p_{j}\mathbf{1}_{R_{j}},

(129)

where
\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j}
are the distinct eigenvalues of
\rho_{R}
,
\{\Pi^{j}_{R}\}_{j}
are orthogonal projectors onto the Hilbert spaces
\{R_{j}\}_{j}
,
\mathbf{1}_{R_{j}}
is the maximally mixed state on
R_{j}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{1}_{R_{j}}=\frac{1}{|R_{j}|}\Pi^{j}_{R}
and
p_{j}=|R_{j}|\cdot\lambda_{j}
.

Let
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
be any unitary
1
-design probability measure on
\mathbb{U}(\mathcal{H})
, i.e, for all
X_{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{K}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})}\left[U_{\mathcal{H}}X_{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{K}}U_{\mathcal{H}}^{\dagger}\right]=\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{H}}\otimes X_{\mathcal{K}}.

(130)

Sampling uniformly at random from the set of Pauli operators is an example of a unitary
1
-design.

Denote by
\Psi_{AA^{\prime}}=|\Psi\rangle\!\langle\Psi|_{AA^{\prime}}
the maximally entangled state on
AA^{\prime}

\{\ket{x_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{|A|}

\ket{\Psi}_{AA^{\prime}}
is defined as:

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi}_{AA^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|A|}}\sum_{i=1}^{|A|}\ket{x_{i}}_{A}\otimes\ket{x_{i}}_{A^{\prime}}.

(131)

Consider the following rounding scheme illustrated in Figure

\displaystyle=\sup_{\Lambda_{RB}}\left\{\mathrm{Tr}\left[\Lambda_{RB}\cdot(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{RB}\right]|\Lambda_{B}=\tfrac{1}{M}\mathbb{I}_{B},\,0\preccurlyeq\Lambda_{RB}\preccurlyeq\rho_{R}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B}\right\}.

Bob performs the measurement

\displaystyle\bigg\{\left\|\textstyle\sum_{l=1}^{M}O^{\bm{j},\,\bm{U}}(l)\right\|_{\infty}^{-1}\cdot\,O^{\bm{j},\,\bm{U}}(m)\bigg\}_{m=1}^{M}\;\bigcup\;\bigg\{\mathbb{I}-\textstyle\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left\|\textstyle\sum_{l=1}^{M}O^{\bm{j},\,\bm{U}}(l)\right\|_{\infty}^{-1}\cdot\,O^{\bm{j},\,\bm{U}}(m)\bigg\}\,,

(144)

\displaystyle\qquad\text{with }\,O^{\bm{j},\,\bm{U}}_{B^{1}\cdots B^{v}B}(m)=\frac{|B_{j_{m}}^{j_{m}}|}{p_{j_{m}}}\cdot(U_{m})_{B_{j_{m}}^{j_{m}}}\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\Lambda_{B^{j_{m}}B}\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}(U_{m}^{\dagger})_{B_{j_{m}}^{j_{m}}}\otimes\bigotimes_{s\in[v]\setminus j_{m}}\mathbb{I}_{B^{s}}.

Recall that, for
s\in[v]
,
B^{s}=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{v}B^{s}_{j}
, and
\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}
is the orthogonal projector onto the Hilbert subspace
B_{j_{m}}^{j_{m}}
of
B^{j_{m}}

B^{1}B^{2}\cdots B^{v}B
so the dimension of
O^{\bm{j},\,\bm{U}}(m)

|R|^{v}\cdot|B|
. Note that
\textstyle\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left\|\textstyle\sum_{l=1}^{M}O^{\bm{j},\,\bm{U}}(l)\right\|_{\infty}^{-1}\cdot\,O^{\bm{j},\,\bm{U}}(m)\preccurlyeq\mathbb{I}
which confirms that (
144

’, corresponding to the measurement operator

m^{\prime}

0
.

Let us analyze this scheme. Denote by
Z

Z={\left\|\sum_{m=1}^{M}O^{\bm{j},\,\bm{U}}(m)\right\|_{\infty}}
. The success probability of sending the message
m\in[M]
conditioned on
(j_{1},\dots,j_{M})
and
(U_{1},\dots,U_{M})
is:

\displaystyle\suc(\mathcal{N},M,m)

(146)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\tfrac{1}{Z}\cdot O^{\bm{j},\,\bm{U}}_{B^{1}\cdots B^{v}B}(m)\cdot\zeta_{B^{1}\cdots B^{v}B}(m)\right]

(147)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{|B_{j_{m}}^{j_{m}}|}{Zp_{j_{m}}}\mathrm{Tr}\left[(U_{m})_{B_{j_{m}}^{j_{m}}}\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\Lambda_{B^{j_{m}}B}\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}(U_{m}^{\dagger})_{B_{j_{m}}^{j_{m}}}\!\cdot\!(U_{m})_{B_{j_{m}}^{j_{m}}}\mathcal{N}_{A^{\prime}\rightarrow B}\!\circ\!\iota_{A^{j_{m}}\rightarrow A^{\prime}}\!\left(\!\Psi_{B_{j_{m}}^{j_{m}}A_{j_{m}}^{j_{m}}}\!\right)\!(U_{m}^{\dagger})_{B_{j_{m}}^{j_{m}}}\right]

(148)

(149)

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\tfrac{1}{Zp_{j_{m}}}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\Lambda_{B^{j_{m}}B}\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\cdot\mathcal{N}_{A^{\prime}\rightarrow B}\circ\iota_{A^{j_{m}}\rightarrow A^{\prime}}\left(|B^{j_{m}}|\cdot\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\Psi_{B^{j_{m}}A^{j_{m}}}\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\right)\right]

(150)

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{=}\tfrac{1}{Zp_{j_{m}}}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\Lambda_{B^{j_{m}}B}\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\cdot\mathcal{N}_{A^{\prime}\rightarrow B}\circ\iota_{A^{j_{m}}\rightarrow A^{\prime}}\left(|B^{j_{m}}|\cdot\Psi_{B^{j_{m}}A^{j_{m}}}\right)\right]

(151)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{Zp_{j_{m}}}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\Lambda_{B^{j_{m}}B}\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\cdot(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{B^{j_{m}}B}\right],

(152)

where in
(a)

|B_{j_{m}}^{j_{m}}|\cdot\Psi_{B_{j_{m}}^{j_{m}}A_{j_{m}}^{j_{m}}}=|B^{j_{m}}|\cdot\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\Psi_{B^{j_{m}}A^{j_{m}}}\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}

(b)
we used that
\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}

\mathcal{N}_{A^{\prime}\rightarrow B}\circ\iota_{A^{j_{m}}\rightarrow A^{\prime}}

To prove
|B_{j_{m}}^{j_{m}}|\Psi_{B_{j_{m}}^{j_{m}}A_{j_{m}}^{j_{m}}}=\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}|B^{j_{m}}|\Psi_{B^{j_{m}}A^{j_{m}}}\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}

\ket{x}

\rho

\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\ket{x}=0

\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\ket{x}=\ket{x}

\displaystyle|B^{j_{m}}|\cdot\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\Psi_{B^{j_{m}}A^{j_{m}}}\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{|R|}\ket{x_{i}}\bra{x_{j}}_{B^{j_{m}}}\otimes\ket{x_{i}}\bra{x_{j}}_{A^{j_{m}}}\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}

(153)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in[|R|]:\\
x_{i},x_{j}\in B_{j_{m}}^{j_{m}}\end{subarray}}\ket{x_{i}}\bra{x_{j}}_{B_{j_{m}}^{j_{m}}}\otimes\ket{x_{i}}\bra{x_{j}}_{A_{j_{m}}^{j_{m}}}

(154)

\displaystyle=|B_{j_{m}}^{j_{m}}|\cdot\Psi_{B_{j_{m}}^{j_{m}}A_{j_{m}}^{j_{m}}}.

(155)

Let
\mathcal{G}=\{Z\leq\log(2vMe^{v}|R|^{v}|B|)\}
denotes the ‘good’ event that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{j}\sim p,\bm{U}\sim\mathcal{L}}\left[\suc(\mathcal{N},M,m)\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{j}\sim p,\bm{U}\sim\mathcal{L}}\left[\suc(\mathcal{N},M,m)\cdot\mathbf{1}(\mathcal{G})\right]

(157)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{j}\sim p,\bm{U}\sim\mathcal{L}}\left[\tfrac{1}{Zp_{j_{m}}}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\Lambda_{B^{j_{m}}B}\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\cdot(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{B^{j_{m}}B}\right]\cdot\mathbf{1}(\mathcal{G})\right]

(158)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{j}\sim p,\bm{U}\sim\mathcal{L}}\left[\frac{1}{p_{j_{m}}}\cdot\frac{1}{\log(2vMe^{v}|R|^{v}|B|)}\cdot\mathrm{Tr}\left[\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\Lambda_{B^{j_{m}}B}\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\cdot(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{B^{j_{m}}B}\right]\cdot\mathbf{1}(\mathcal{G})\right]

(159)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{j}\sim p,\bm{U}\sim\mathcal{L}}\left[\frac{1}{p_{j_{m}}}\cdot\frac{1}{\log(2vMe^{v}|R|^{v}|B|)}\cdot\mathrm{Tr}\left[\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\Lambda_{B^{j_{m}}B}\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\cdot(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{B^{j_{m}}B}\right]\right]

(160)

\displaystyle\quad-\mathbb{E}_{\bm{j}\sim p,\bm{U}\sim\mathcal{L}}\left[\frac{1}{p_{j_{m}}}\cdot\frac{1}{\log(2vMe^{v}|R|^{v}|B|)}\cdot\mathrm{Tr}\left[\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\Lambda_{B^{j_{m}}B}\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\cdot(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{B^{j_{m}}B}\right]\cdot\mathbf{1}(\mathcal{G}^{c})\right].

(161)

The expectation (
160

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{j}\sim p,\bm{U}\sim\mathcal{L}}\left[\frac{1}{p_{j_{m}}}\cdot\frac{1}{\log(2vMe^{v}|R|^{v}|B|)}\cdot\mathrm{Tr}\left[\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\Lambda_{B^{j_{m}}B}\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\cdot(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{B^{j_{m}}B}\right]\right]

(162)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{v}p_{j}\frac{1}{p_{j}}\cdot\frac{1}{\log(2vMe^{v}|R|^{v}|B|)}\cdot\mathrm{Tr}\left[\Pi^{j}_{R}\Lambda_{RB}\Pi^{j}_{R}\cdot(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{RB}\right]

(163)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\log(2vMe^{v}|R|^{v}|B|)}\cdot\operatorname{Tr}\bigg[\sum_{j=1}^{v}\Pi^{j}_{R}\Lambda_{RB}\Pi^{j}_{R}\cdot(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{RB}\bigg]

(164)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\log(2vMe^{v}|R|^{v}|B|)}\cdot\frac{1}{v}\cdot\mathrm{Tr}\left[\Lambda_{RB}\cdot(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{RB}\right]

(165)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\log(2vMe^{v}|R|^{v}|B|)}\cdot\frac{1}{v}\cdot\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M,\rho),

(166)

where we used
B^{j_{m}}\simeq R
and in the inequality we used the pinching inequality (
30

v\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{v}\Pi^{j}_{R}\Lambda_{RB}\Pi^{j}_{R}=v\cdot\mathcal{P}_{\rho}(\Lambda_{RB})\succcurlyeq\Lambda_{RB}.

(167)

To bound the expectation (
161

[
21
]
. Since
\Lambda_{RB}
is a solution to the program
(
143
), we have
\Lambda_{RB}\preccurlyeq\rho_{R}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B}
so
\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\Lambda_{B^{j_{m}}B}\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\preccurlyeq p_{j_{m}}\mathbf{1}_{B^{j_{m}}_{j_{m}}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B}
thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{j}\sim p,\bm{U}\sim\mathcal{L}}\left[\frac{1}{p_{j_{m}}}\cdot\frac{1}{\log(2vMe^{v}|R|^{v}|B|)}\cdot\mathrm{Tr}\left[\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\Lambda_{B^{j_{m}}B}\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\cdot(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{B^{j_{m}}B}\right]\cdot\mathbf{1}(\mathcal{G}^{c})\right]

(168)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\log(2vMe^{v}|R|^{v}|B|)}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\bm{j}\sim p,\bm{U}\sim\mathcal{L}}\left[\mathrm{Tr}\left[\mathbf{1}_{B^{j_{m}}_{j_{m}}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B}\cdot(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{B^{j_{m}}B}\right]\cdot\mathbf{1}(\mathcal{G}^{c})\right]

(169)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\log(2vMe^{v}|R|^{v}|B|)}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\bm{j}\sim p,\bm{U}\sim\mathcal{L}}\left[\mathrm{Tr}\left[\mathbf{1}_{B^{j_{m}}_{j_{m}}}\cdot\mathbb{I}_{B^{j_{m}}}\right]\cdot\mathbf{1}(\mathcal{G}^{c})\right]

(170)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\log(2vMe^{v}|R|^{v}|B|)}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{\bm{j}\sim p,\bm{U}\sim\mathcal{L}}\left[\left\|\textstyle\sum_{m=1}^{M}O^{\bm{j},\,\bm{U}}(m)\right\|_{\infty}>\log(2vMe^{v}|R|^{v}|B|)\right],

(171)

where we recall the definition of the random matrix
O^{\bm{j},\,\bm{U}}_{B^{1}\cdots B^{v}B}(m)

\displaystyle O^{\bm{j},\,\bm{U}}_{B^{1}\cdots B^{v}B}(m)=\frac{|B_{j_{m}}^{j_{m}}|}{p_{j_{m}}}\cdot(U_{m})_{B_{j_{m}}}\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\Lambda_{B^{j_{m}}B}\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}(U_{m}^{\dagger})_{B_{j_{m}}}\otimes\bigotimes_{s\in[v]\setminus j_{m}}\mathbb{I}_{B^{s}}.

(172)

Moreover,
\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\Lambda_{B^{j_{m}}B}\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\preccurlyeq p_{j_{m}}\mathbf{1}_{B_{j_{m}}^{j_{m}}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B}
implies

\displaystyle 0\preccurlyeq O^{\bm{j},\,\bm{U}}_{B^{1}\cdots B^{v}B}(m)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{|B_{j_{m}}^{j_{m}}|}{p_{j_{m}}}\cdot(U_{m})_{B_{j_{m}}}\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\Lambda_{B^{j_{m}}B}\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}(U_{m}^{\dagger})_{B_{j_{m}}}\otimes\bigotimes_{s\in[v]\setminus j_{m}}\mathbb{I}_{B^{s}}

(173)

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq|B_{j_{m}}^{j_{m}}|\cdot(U_{m})_{B^{j_{m}}_{j_{m}}}\mathbf{1}_{B^{j_{m}}_{j_{m}}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B}(U_{m}^{\dagger})_{B^{j_{m}}_{j_{m}}}\otimes\bigotimes_{s\in[v]\setminus j_{m}}\mathbb{I}_{B^{s}}

(174)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}_{B_{j_{m}}^{j_{m}}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B}\otimes\bigotimes_{s\in[v]\setminus j_{m}}\mathbb{I}_{B^{s}}

(175)

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq\mathbb{I}_{B^{j_{m}}}\otimes\bigotimes_{s\in[v]\setminus j_{m}}\mathbb{I}_{B^{s}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B}

(176)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}_{B^{1}\cdots B^{v}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B}\,,

(177)

and thus
0\leq\lambda_{\min}(O^{\bm{j},\,\bm{U}}(m))
as well as
\lambda_{\max}(O^{\bm{j},\,\bm{U}}(m))\leq 1=L
. Furthermore, the expectation of the random matrix

can be controlled as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{j}\sim p,\bm{U}\sim\mathcal{L}}\left[O^{\bm{j},\,\bm{U}}_{B^{1}\cdots B^{v}B}(m)\right]

(178)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j_{m}=1}^{v}p_{j_{m}}\mathbb{E}_{U_{m}\sim\mathcal{L}(B_{j_{m}}^{j_{m}})}\left[\tfrac{|B^{j_{m}}_{j_{m}}|}{p_{j_{m}}}\cdot(U_{m})_{B_{j_{m}}^{j_{m}}}\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\Lambda_{B^{j_{m}}B}\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}(U_{m}^{\dagger})_{B_{j_{m}}^{j_{m}}}\otimes\bigotimes_{s\in[v]\setminus j_{m}}\mathbb{I}_{B^{s}}\right]

(179)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j_{m}=1}^{v}\mathbf{1}_{B^{j_{m}}_{j_{m}}}\otimes\mathrm{Tr}_{B^{j_{m}}_{j_{m}}}\left[{|B^{j_{m}}_{j_{m}}|}\cdot\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\Lambda_{B^{j_{m}}B}\Pi_{B^{j_{m}}}^{j_{m}}\otimes\bigotimes_{s\in[v]\setminus j_{m}}\mathbb{I}_{B^{s}}\right]

(180)

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq\sum_{j_{m}=1}^{v}\mathbb{I}_{B^{j_{m}}_{j_{m}}}\otimes\mathrm{Tr}_{B^{j_{m}}}\left[\Lambda_{B^{j_{m}}B}\otimes\bigotimes_{s\in[v]\setminus j_{m}}\mathbb{I}_{B^{s}}\right]

(181)

(182)

\displaystyle=\frac{v}{M}\mathbb{I}_{B^{1}\cdots B^{v}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B}.

(183)

Hence, we deduce that

\mu_{\max}=\lambda_{\max}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{j}\sim p,\bm{U}\sim\mathcal{L}}\left[O^{\bm{j},\,\bm{U}}_{B^{1}\cdots B^{v}B}(m)\right]\right)\leq\lambda_{\max}\left(v\mathbb{I}_{B^{1}\cdots B^{v}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B}\right)=v
.
Therefore by the matrix Chernoff inequality (
L=1
and
\mu_{\max}\leq v
) (see
[
53
, Theorem 5.1.1]
reproduced in Theorem
22
) we have for
\gamma=(1+\delta)\mu_{\max}=\log(2vM|R|^{v}|B|)
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\bm{j}\sim p,\bm{U}\sim\mathcal{L}}\left[\big\|\textstyle\sum_{m=1}^{M}O^{\bm{j},\,\bm{U}}(m)\big\|_{\infty}>(1+\delta)\mu_{\max}\right]

\displaystyle\leq|B^{1}\cdots B^{v}B|\cdot\left(\frac{e^{\delta}}{(1+\delta)^{1+\delta}}\right)^{\mu_{\max}}

(184)

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}|R|^{v}|B|\exp(-\gamma)

(185)

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\leq}\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{v}\cdot\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M,\rho),

(186)

where we used in
(a)

\mu_{\max}\leq v
and the inequality
\gamma\log\gamma-\gamma-\gamma\ln(v)\geq\gamma
valid for all
\gamma\geq e^{2}v
(this is implied by the choice of
\gamma
and the assumption
\log|A|\geq e^{2}
); in
(b)
we used
\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M,\rho)\geq\frac{1}{M}
and we choose
\gamma
such that

\displaystyle|R|^{v}|B|\exp(-\gamma)\leq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{v}\cdot\frac{1}{M}\Leftrightarrow\gamma\geq\log(2vM|R|^{v}|B|).

Consequently, from (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{j}\sim p,\bm{U}\sim\mathcal{L}}\left[\suc(\mathcal{N},M,m)\right]

(188)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{v\log(2vMe^{v}|R|^{v}|B|)}\cdot\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M,\rho)-\frac{1}{2v\log(2vMe^{v}|R|^{v}|B|)}\cdot\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},M,\rho)

4.3
Application: strong converse exponent for coding over quantum channels

In this section, we apply the approximation result with multiplicative error obtained in Section
4.2
to establish the entanglement-assisted strong converse exponent in the quantum setting.
To this end, we show that the coding strong converse exponent is the same for entanglement-assisted and non-signaling strategies.

Proposition 17
.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be a quantum channel. For all
r\geq 0
, we have that

\displaystyle E^{\rm{EA}}(\mathcal{N},r)=E^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N},r).

(193)

As a direct corollary of this proposition and the exact characterization of the non-signaling strong converse exponent in Corollary
12
, we can conclude the entanglement-assisted strong converse exponent.

Corollary 18
.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be a quantum channel. For all
r\geq 0
, we have that

\displaystyle E^{\rm{EA}}(\mathcal{N},r)=\sup_{\alpha\geq 0}\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\left(r-\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{1+\alpha}(\mathcal{N})\right).

(194)

We note that
[
33
]
is the first to prove this result. Our proof strategy differs significantly and may be of independent interest.

Proof of Proposition
17
.

We first reduce the problem to a permutation invariant state
\rho_{R}
in the non-signaling program of
\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n},M,\rho_{R^{n}})
(
127
). Next, we reduce the problem to the case where
\rho_{R^{n}}=\rho_{R^{n}}^{\rm{dF}}
is the de Finetti state
[
12
]
. Finally we apply the rounding result proven in Proposition
16
.

Reduction to permutation invariant states.

By Lemma
21
, the optimization in

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n},M)=\sup_{\rho_{R^{n}},\Lambda_{R^{n}B^{n}}}\quad

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left[\Lambda_{R^{n}B^{n}}\cdot(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{RB}^{\otimes n}\right]

(195)

subject to

\displaystyle\rho_{R^{n}}\in\mathcal{S}(R^{n}),

(196)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{B^{n}}=\tfrac{1}{M}\mathbb{I}_{B^{n}},

(197)

\displaystyle 0\preccurlyeq\Lambda_{R^{n}B^{n}}\preccurlyeq\rho_{R^{n}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B^{n}}.

(198)

can be restricted to permutation invariant states
\rho_{R^{n}}
. We denote the set of permutation invariant states over
R^{\otimes n}
by
\mathcal{S}_{n}(R)
. We note that similar symmetry arguments were used by
[
36
,
31
]
to reduce the NS programs of the coding size and fidelity.

Reduction to the de Finetti state.
Let
g(n,d)=\binom{n+d^{2}-1}{n}\leq(n+1)^{d^{2}-1}
. Any permutation invariant state
\rho_{R^{n}}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}(R)
satisfies
\rho_{R^{n}}\preccurlyeq g(n,|R|)\rho_{R^{n}}^{\rm{dF}}
where
\rho_{R^{n}}^{\rm{dF}}
is the de Finetti state from
[
12
]
and stated later on in (
208
). So, we find

\displaystyle\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n},M)

(199)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\rho_{R^{n}}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}(R)}\sup_{\Lambda_{R^{n}B^{n}}}\left\{\mathrm{Tr}\left[\Lambda_{R^{n}B^{n}}\cdot(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{RB}^{\otimes n}\right]\;\middle|\;\Lambda_{B^{n}}=\tfrac{1}{M}\mathbb{I}_{B^{n}},\,0\preccurlyeq\Lambda_{R^{n}B^{n}}\preccurlyeq\rho_{R^{n}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B^{n}}\right\}

(200)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\Lambda_{R^{n}B^{n}}}\left\{\mathrm{Tr}\left[\Lambda_{R^{n}B^{n}}\cdot(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{RB}^{\otimes n}\right]\;\middle|\;\Lambda_{B^{n}}=\tfrac{1}{M}\mathbb{I}_{B^{n}},\,0\preccurlyeq\Lambda_{R^{n}B^{n}}\preccurlyeq g(n,|R|)\rho_{R^{n}}^{\rm{dF}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B^{n}}\right\}

(201)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\Lambda_{R^{n}B^{n}}}\left\{g(n,|R|)\mathrm{Tr}\left[\Lambda_{R^{n}B^{n}}\cdot(J_{\mathcal{N}})_{RB}^{\otimes n}\right]\;\middle|\;\Lambda_{B^{n}}=\tfrac{1}{g(n,|R|)M}\mathbb{I}_{B^{n}},\,0\preccurlyeq\Lambda_{R^{n}B^{n}}\preccurlyeq\rho_{R^{n}}^{\rm{dF}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B^{n}}\right\}

(202)

\displaystyle=g(n,|R|)\cdot\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n},g(n,|R|)M,\rho^{\rm{dF}})

(203)

\displaystyle\leq g(n,|R|)\cdot\sup_{\rho_{R^{n}}\in\mathcal{S}(R^{n})}\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n},g(n,|R|)M,\rho)

(204)

\displaystyle\leq g(n,|R|)\cdot\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n},g(n,|R|)M).

(205)

Hence, the non-signaling success probability
\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n},M)
is comparable to
\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n},M,\rho^{\rm{dF}})
:

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{g(n,|R|)}\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n},M)

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{g(n,|R|)}\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n},\tfrac{M}{g(n,|R|)})

(206)

\displaystyle\leq\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n},M,\rho^{\rm{dF}})\leq\suc^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n},M).

(207)

5
Conclusion

We explored entanglement-assisted quantum channel coding from an algorithmic point of view. We developed approximation algorithms for the entanglement-assisted success probability using non-signaling relaxation in the quantum-classical and in the quantum setting. As an application, we provided an alternative proof for the characterization of the entanglement-assisted strong converse exponent. The achievability proof proceeds by connecting the entanglement-assisted (EA), non-signaling (NS), and meta-converse (MC) strong converse exponents. The latter is then deduced from the inherent relation between the meta-converse and composite hypothesis testing. While this technique worked effectively for strong converse exponents, it remains unclear whether the EA, NS, and MC error exponents are equal. Moreover, obtaining tight approximation bounds in the fully quantum setting remains an open problem.

Theorem 22
.

Consider a finite sequence

Define the maximum eigenvalue

\mu_{\max}=\lambda_{\max}(\mathbb{E}\left[Y\right])=\lambda_{\max}\left(\sum_{k}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{k}\right]\right).
