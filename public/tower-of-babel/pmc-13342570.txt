Nutritional profiling, bioactive phytochemicals, and functional food components of wild edible fruits from the Indo-Burma biodiversity hotspot
Authors: Rody Ngurthankhumi, Tridip Kumar Hazarika, Zothansiama, Esther Lalruatsangi, Lalramchhana, Lalnunzawma, Panthor Debbarma, G Karunakaran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNortheast India constitutes a substantial portion of the Indo-Burma biodiversity hotspot and harbours a wide array of wild edible fruits. These fruits significantly contribute to the nutritional security, traditional medicine, and sustainability of the region’s indigenous populations. Though they hold ethno medicinal importance and are widely used traditionally, detailed information on nutritional properties, bioactive compounds, and functional food elements is quite limited. Accordingly, this study has been conducted to evaluate the nutritional profile, bioactive phytochemicals, mineral content, and functional food attributes of seven chosen wild edible fruits from Mizoram, north-east India, specifically Antidesma bunius, Carallia brachiata, Emblica officinalis, Embelia subcoriacea, Elaeocarpus lanceifolius, Spondias pinnata, and Terminalia chebula. Standard analytical methods were utilized to assess proximate composition, vitamins, dietary fiber components, pigments, phytochemical constituents, and mineral elements. Significant differences were noted among the species, highlighting the extraordinary diversity present in the fruits analyzed. Terminalia chebula reported high concentrations of carotenoids, alkaloids, carbohydrates, proteins, and energy value, as well as minerals such as potassium and cobalt. Carallia brachiata exhibited elevated levels of chlorophyll, anthocyanin, and saponin, along with significant amounts of nitrogen, magnesium, and manganese. Antidesma bunius exhibited notable amounts of vitamin E, dietary fiber, lignin, and minerals such as calcium, copper, and iron. At the same time, Emblica officinalis displayed particularly elevated levels of vitamin C, total sugars, dry matter, and zinc. The research offers novel comparative perspectives on the nutritional and phytochemical abundance of wild edible fruits from the Indo-Burma hotspot, emphasizing their potential as significant sources of natural bioactive substances and health-enhancing functional foods. These recognized nutritionally enhanced species could be valuable options for future functional food innovation and agricultural enhancement initiatives. The result highlights the significance of preserving wild edible fruit species for dietary diversification and sustainable food systems. Future investigations focusing on metabolomic profiling and the clinical validation of these bioactive compounds will further reveal their therapeutic and industrial potential.Keywords: Biodiversity hotspot, Functional foods, Mizoram, Nutritional composition, Wild edible fruitsSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Chemical biology, Plant sciences

Introduction

The WHO has acknowledged the significance of nutrition as a metric in the Millennium Development Goal (MDG) aimed at decreasing maternal and under-5-year death rates, particularly in developing nations1. An individual’s well-being and overall health are determined by their nutritional status, which depends on their nutrient intake and utilisation. Optimal nutritional status influences energy levels, immune function, and disease prevention. Unfortunately, there is a worrisome global trend in nutrition, with millions of people suffering from malnutrition, leading to numerous health issues and contributing to the overall burden of diseases2. Individuals must be mindful of their nutritional intake and make informed decisions to meet their daily nutrient requirements for optimal health3.

Despite India’s immense economic growth over the decade, the nutritional status of its population has not seen the desired transformation. Recent research findings indicate that India, despite making significant progress in reducing stunting growth rates over the past decade, continues to have the highest proportion of undernourished individuals globally. India’s position in the Global Hunger Index (GHI) stood at 103 out of 119 countries in 2018, shedding light on the alarming issue of under nutrition. However, incorporating several varieties of fruits, whole grains, vegetables, and proteins into one’s diet and ensuring the availability of these nutritional sources, even in remote rural areas, can effectively address this issue.

Regarding this matter, emphasising the significance of fruits as essential elements of the human diet for providing essential nutrition is crucial. Fruits have been recognised as an important and nourishing nutrient source vital to promote human well-being. In addition to vitamins and minerals, they also offer carbohydrates, proteins, fibres, organic acids, and bioactive phytochemicals like secondary metabolites4. Nutritional research has identified wild edible fruits offer nutritious diet and potential for treating various ailments as a source of bioactive compounds and functional food component5. These fruits possess remarkable therapeutic potential due to their bioactive components, such as flavonoids and anthocyanin. Additionally, they are crucial in calories and nutrition as they are available in the wild and are easily accessible even for the local rural communities, which, in several cases, are their primary source of traditional healing6.

Wild edible fruits have deep cultural roots in specific regions and are cherished as traditional crops. These fruits play a pivotal role in the livelihood generation of local and rural communities, serving as a critical source of essential macronutrients, vitamins and micronutrients and use in traditional medicine7,8. These fruits are highly valued in the areas where access to commercially available nutrient-rich fruits is limited and often unaffordable. Upon conducting a thorough nutritional analysis of these wild fruits, it becomes evident that their nutritional quality often matches and, in certain instances, surpasses that of domesticated varieties of fruits that require extensive cultivation. They provide affordable yet nutritious options for many individuals living in rural regions9. Though they possess significant nutritional benefits, numerous wild edible fruits remain undiscovered or underutilised.

The north-east India is renowned for its exceptional biodiversity, vibrant ethnic diversity, and traditional culture10. The indigenous communities in the region rely on forests for sustenance, including food, fibre, and animal feed, as well as for the traditional medicinal resources used in their healthcare practices.

Mizoram, situated in India’s north-east region has the largest indigenous population. The tribal people of the state depend on forests for food, fibre, and fodder, and ethno medicines used in traditional healthcare system. In addition to other edible and non-edible plants in the forests, several wild fruits are also present, which the tribal people have been used since ancient times in their traditional medicinal practices to combat various diseases11. Within the rural tribal populations, these wild fruits serve as a valuable means of generating sustainable income and ensuring livelihood security12. In Mizoram, in addition to other wild edible fruits, fruits such as Terminalia chebula, Emblica officinalis, Elaeocarpus lanceifolius along with Antidesma bunius, Carallia brachiata, Spondias pinnata and Embelia subcoriacea are also available abundantly in natural populations which have long been relied upon for their nutritional and medicinal benefits. Despite their historical usage in traditional medicine, due to lack of insufficient documentation of these species, there may be chance of losing significant information about the ethno medicinal qualities of these fruits. There is need for scientific exploration, documentation, domestication and conservation of these valuable natural resources. It is crucial to study thoroughly and document the nutritional values of these fruits and promote these untamed fruits for food and nutritional security.

In light of the aforementioned details, the present research is aimed to document the nutritional composition, functional food components and bioactive phytochemicals of indigenous wild edible fruits in Mizoram, India.

Materials and methods

Collection of sample

Seven unique wild edible fruits including bignay berries (Antidesma bunius L. Spreng.), freshwater mangrove (Carallia brachiata (Lour.) Merr.), amla (Emblica officinalis Gaertn), poimur tenga (Embelia subcoriacea (C.B.Clarke) Mez), lanceleaf marble tree (Elaeocarpus lanceifolius Roxb), Indian hog plum (Spondias pinnata L.) and chebulic myrobalan (Terminalia chebula Retz.) were collected during 2021–2022 from the forests of villages viz., north Vanlaiphai, Khawlailung, new Champhai, Tanhril, Melthum, Saikhawthlir, Bualpui village of Serchhip, Champhai, Aizawl, Lawngtlai and Lunglei districts of Mizoram, north-east India (Fig. 1). As these are not endangered/ threatened species, so no geographical coordinates were taken for the samples. The survey and collection of samples was conducted in the primary and secondary forests as well as home gardens. On the basis of preliminary survey, the above mentioned seven wild edible fruits were collected from these villages. Before collecting the fruits, verbal consent were taken from the forests officials as well as the village head, and orchard owners. Since these fruits are very common in north-east India, and local people know it from plant canopy, architecture of the plant, very peculiar fruit shape. But their formal identification and authentication was done by Prof. Tridip Kumar Hazarika of department of Horticulture, Aromatic and Medicinal Plants, Mizoram University. For all the collected species, herbarium was prepared and the voucher specimens were deposited in the department of Horticulture, Aromatic and Medicinal Plants, Mizoram University having deposition numbers viz., MZU/HAMP/22/AB; MZU/HAMP/22/CB; MZU/HAMP/22/EO; MZU/HAMP/22/ES; MZU/HAMP/22/EL; MZU/HAMP/22/SP and MZU/HAMP/22/TC.

Preparation of the sample

After collection, the fruits were washed with ethanol and subsequently removing the contaminants present on the fruit’s surface with distilled water. The fruits were then dehydrated using absorbent paper. Afterwards, the fruits were partitioned into smaller segments and dehydrated at 50–60˚C. Subsequently, the specimens were pulverised into powder, filtered through a 20 μm screen, and placed in a tightly sealed container. The methanol extracts were acquired via a Buchi R-300 rotary evaporator. Subsequently, the samples were analysed to assess their qualities.

Functional food components analysis

Determination of pigments

To quantify the total carotenoid content, 100 mg of fresh samples were ground in mortar and pestle with 5 ml of 80% acetone, which was then centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 5 min and retrieved the supernatant. The total carotenoid content was determined by measuring the supernatant’s absorbance at wavelengths of 664, 648 and 470 nm using a UV vis spectrophotometer13.

The anthocyanin content was estimated using procedure14. The fresh fruit sample (1 g) was mixed with 10 ml of ethanolic Hydrochloric acid (HCl) and then moved to a 100 ml volumetric flask to reach the desired volume through dilution. The mixture was chilled at a temperature of 4 °C overnight. Afterwards, a 0.2 ml portion was taken from the mixture and then diluted to a final volume of 10 ml. The optical density (O.D.) was subsequently determined at a wavelength of 535 nm using a UV vis spectrophotometer.

For estimation of total chlorophyll, 1gm fresh sample was ground using 20 ml extractant solvent of 80% acetone after which it was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C and the supernatant was collected. The same process was repeated till the sample became colourless. Subsequently, it was brought up to the final volume of 100 ml using 80% acetone and analysed using a UV-visible spectrophotometer at 663 and 645 nm wavelengths15.

Determination of vitamins

To determine the vitamin E content of the fruits, 10 ml of 0.1 N H2SO4 was added to grounded 0.5 g fresh fruit sample and left overnight. The extract obtained was then filtered from which 1.5 ml of tissue extract was taken along with the standard solution, and water individually transferring them into three separate test tubes using a pipette. 1.5 ml of ethanol was introduced into the test and blank samples, while 1.5 ml of water was added to the standard sample. After centrifugation, 1.5 ml of xylene was added to each tube which was vortexed and centrifuged. The xylene layer was carefully transferred into a separate tube, where 1 ml of the 2,2’-dipyridyl reagent was added, after which it was analysed at a wavelength of 460 nm compared to a blank solution. A volume of 0.33 ml of ferric chloride was introduced into each test tube designated as the blank after 15 min. Subsequently, the resulting liquid on the top was measured using a UV-visible spectrophotometer at 520 nm, with the blank solution as the reference16.

The quantification of ascorbic acid in the fruit sample was determined using a spectrophotometric technique described by15. The fresh fruit samples were blended in a water bath with metaphosphoric acid, comprised of a 5% concentration of metaphosphoric acid in a 10% acetic acid solution. After filtration, the resulting mixture was treated with a solution containing 85% H2SO4 and 2,4-dinitrophenyl-hydrazine. Afterwards, the samples were placed in an incubator at 60 °C for 60 min. The measurement was performed using a UV-visible spectrophotometer at a wavelength of 520 nm.

Determination of bioactive-alkaloid and saponin compounds

The approach outlined by17 was utilised to estimate the total alkaloid content of the fruits. The dehydrated sample was soaked in 80% ethanol, evaporating at 72 °C to prepare a concentrated solution. Plant extract (1 mg) was dissolved in dimethyl sulphoxide (DMSO), and 1 ml of 2 N HCl was added. Then, the solution was filtered and put into a separate funnel. Subsequently, equal amounts of bromocresol green (5 ml) and phosphate buffer (5 ml) were introduced. Chloroform was introduced and agitated before being placed into a 10 cc flask, and the volume was made up by using chloroform. Multiple reference standard solutions were created, each with varying doses of atropine. The clear liquid was analysed using a UV-visible spectrophotometer at 470 nm, with the reagent blank as the reference.

The total saponin content was determined using the method described by18 based on the vanillin-sulphuric acid colorimetric reaction with slight modifications. 50 ml of methanolic plant extract was mixed with 250 ml of distilled water, followed by the addition of vanillin reagent (250 µl), where 800 mg of vanillin was dissolved in 10 ml of 99.5% ethanol. A 2.5 ml aliquot of 72% sulphuric acid was introduced into the mixture and then homogeneously blended. In a water bath, the solution was kept at 60 °C for 10 min and chilled for 10 min in cold water, and the reading was taken at 544 nm. The results were quantified by using diosgenin equivalent (mg DE/g extract) using diosgenin as the standard.

Mineral contents

For estimation of minerals, 1 g of finely ground dried fruit samples were taken into a small beaker. A di-acid combination of HNO3 and 70% HClO4 was added to the solution in a 4:1 ratio (10 ml) which was left undisturbed for 24 h. The extract was then heated using controlled heating with a hot plate until the red NO2 vapours stopped and became colourless. The final extract was then transferred to a 50 ml beaker and filtered using Whatman No. 1 filter paper and the volume made up was done. The minerals Ca, Co, Na, Fe, Cu, Mg, Mn, K, and Zn were estimated using Atomic Absorption Spectroscopy (AAS) from the extracted sample solution. Automatic N2 digestion, distillation Kjeldahl apparatus was used to estimate the N, and the spectrophotometric vanadium phosphomolybdate technique was employed to assess P.

Nutritional component analysis

The fruits’ moisture, ash, total sugar, crude fibre, reducing sugar, dry matter, non-reducing sugar, fat, hemicellulose, lignin, cellulose, carbohydrate and starch content were measured by using19.

To estimate the titratable acidity of the fruits juice, 0.1 N NaOH solution was used for titration. The indicator phenolphthalein identified a pale pink colour, indicating the end point and the acidity was expressed as a percentage in relation to the citric acid concentration. The measurement of TSS content in the fresh fruit was done using a digital refractometer with a range of 0–85 °Brix. A small amount of juice from the fruit was dropped to the prism surface, and the measurement was recorded.

The protein content was evaluated using the method20. A total of 0.1 ml and 0.2 ml of dried samples were collected in a test tube. Ensuring that the volume in all test tubes is precisely calibrated to 1 ml. Add 5 ml of reagent C to every tube, including the control tube. Thoroughly mix the components and let the mixture rest for 10 min. Next, add 0.5 ml of Folin reagent to the mixture, ensuring it is mixed well. Then, incubate the solution at room temperature in a light-protected setting for 30 min. The optical absorbance at a wavelength of 660 nm was used to measure the blue hue.

The energy value was calculated using the following conversion parameters specified by21.

Quantification of anti-nutrients

The approach published by15 was used to quantify phytic acid by using the dry samples. The fruits were extracted by using 5% H2SO4 solution and 1 ml of the supernatant was combined with an equal volume of ferric (III) chloride, which was kept in water bath for 30 min at 100 °C, then kept for 15 minutes in ice-cold water, and then centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 30 min. Then 1 ml of supernatant was carefully placed into a cuvette, followed by the addition of a small quantity of 2,2’bipyridine solution until the appearance of a vivid pink hue. The measurement of absorbance for the reaction mixture was assessed at 519 nm wavelength by using distilled water as reference.

Titrimetric technique of22 was used for estimation of oxalate. A 2 g dry sample was subjected to digestion by using 10 ml of 6 M HCl for one hour and them the mixture was allowed to cool. It is subjected to filtration after being transferred into a volumetric flask (250 ml). In a beaker, 125 ml of filtrate was added 3 to 4 drops of methyl red. NH4OH was slowly added to the solution until the colour changes from salmon pink to pale yellow. Afterwards, the solution was measured for the pH level and heated at 90 °C, followed by a cooling process and filtration to eliminate the residue. Once again, the filtered liquid was heated at 90 °C. Subsequently, a 10 ml solution of 5% calcium chloride (CaCl2) was added while stirring. The decantation procedure effectively separated the solution, leaving behind a solid substance dissolved in 20% (v/v) H2SO4 (10 ml). The filtrate volume made up to 300 ml, out of which 125 ml of this filtrate was heated near the boiling point. The heated filtrate was titrated against 0.05 M potassium tetraoxomanganate KMNO4 solution. A persistent pink colouration for 30 s was observed at end point.

The tannin was evaluated using the Folin-Denis technique developed by15. 75 ml of water was added to 0.5 g dried sample in a conical flask, boiled for 30 min, and centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 20 min. The supernatant was carefully poured into a volumetric flask, diluted with distilled water to make a final volume of 100 ml, of which 1 ml was taken in a volumetric flask containing 75 ml of water. Folin-Denis reagent (5 ml) and sodium carbonate (10 ml) were added and then diluted to a final volume of 100 ml. After 30 min using UV-visible spectrophotometer, the absorbance was taken at 700 nm; the blank solution was used for reference.

Data analysis

The data were analysed statistically for ANOVA using SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp., 2017). The data were represented as the average (mean) and the measure of variability (standard deviation). The Duncan Multiple Range Test (DMRT) was used to differentiate treatment means with a significance threshold of 5% (p < 0.05).

Results and discussion

Functional food components of seven wild edible fruits

Functional food components are biologically active chemicals in fruits and vegetables that provide benefits beyond essential nutrition. They possess the capacity to provide further health advantages. These compounds, like vitamins, dietary fibres, minerals, probiotics, and phytochemicals, are naturally present in vegetables and fruits which are essential for maintaining optimal health and avoiding illnesses.

Carotenoids are vital for human health because they are advantageous as potent antioxidants, safeguarding cells from harm produced by free radicals23. Certain carotenoids, notably β-carotene, serve as essential pro-vitamin A substances that are enzymatically transformed into retinol (vitamin A) in the human body and are crucial for preserving normal vision, boosting immune function, aiding cellular growth and differentiation, and controlling reproductive and developmental activities. Moreover, carotenoids possess strong antioxidant properties that help neutralize reactive oxygen species, which diminishes oxidative stress and aids in the prevention of several chronic and degenerative diseases24. From Table 1, it is revealed that among all the studied wild edible fruits, the carotene as most significant in Terminalia chebula (1.75 ± 0.005 mg 100 g–1, followed by Antidesma bunius (1.48 ± 0.004 mg/100 g) and Elaeocarpus lanceifolius (1.141 ± 0.006 mg/100 g), while the lowest was observed in Spondias pinnata (0.195 ± 0.005 mg/100 g). Carotenoid levels directly correlate with chlorophyll levels. The dynamic ratio between these two pigment groups dictates how effectively the plant catches light and regulates its energy pathways25. The significant presence of carotenoides in Terminalia chebula corresponds to its well-documented pharmacological characteristics, such as its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects26. The carotenoid pigment observed in Antidesma bunius is slightly lower as compared to the findings (1.70 ± 0.29 mg 100 g–1 of27 might be due to the difference in the environmental condition.

Chlorophyll, the green pigment found in plants, has significant effects on human nutrition due to its many health benefits, such as its antioxidant qualities, detoxification abilities, promotion of wound healing, improvement of blood health, and enhancement of digestive health28. The chlorophyll content was highest in Carallia brachiata (0.378 ± 0.003 mg/100 g), followed by Elaeocarpus lanceifolius (0.279 ± 0.001 mg/100 g) and Terminalia chebula (0.119 ± 0.001 mg/100 g). Spondias pinnata had the lowest chlorophyll levels (0.033 ± 0.001 mg/100 g) and were statistically similar to the levels found in Emblica officinalis (0.034 ± 0.001 mg/100 g) and Embelia subcoriacea (0.035 ± 0.001 mg/100 g) (Table 1). These variations might be due to the genetic traits and environmental factors like light and temperature. Elaeocarpus lanceifolius and Terminalia chebula has a high chlorophyll concentration which may be due to their high carotenoid content as these two parameters are closely related enabling them to effectively carry photosynthesis. Terminalia chebula and Spondias pinnata showed chlorophyll content in the range reported by27.

Anthocyanins are class of flavonoids that give rise to different colours in many fruits29. The anthocyanins escalate the darkening of the fruit. Among the studied fruits, Carallia brachiata had the maximum anthocyanin of 816.67 ± 5.28 mg/100 g. Terminalia chebula (521.67 ± 7.64 mg/100 g) and Spondias pinnata (484.17 ± 0.76 mg/100 g) followed it. Embelia subcoriacea had the lowest anthocyanin of 231.67 ± 2.89 mg/100 g (Table 1). The deep purple and blue colouration of Carallia brachiata may be attributed to their high anthocyanin and chlorophyll concentrations as these two parameters are closely related thereby enhancing dark colour of the fruits30,31. The anthocyanin concentration in Antidesma bunius is almost similar to the range of anthocyanin reported by32.

Vitamin C is essential for bolstering the body’s capacity to fight against disease and improving white blood cell generation and performance, which are crucial for combating infections33. Among the studied fruits, Emblica officinalis exhibited maximum vitamin C (756.00 ± 4.00 mg/100 g). It was followed by Spondias pinnata (644.00 ± 8.00 mg/100 g) and Terminalia chebula (154.00 ± 4.00 mg/100 g). Among the studied fruits, the lowest vit. C was observed in Elaeocarpus lanceifoliu (42.00 ± 14.00 mg/100 g) (Table 1). The variation may be attributed to the environment and climatic differences in their growth and development. The recorded vitamin C values in Emblica officinalis are greater than the range (400–570 mg 100 g[–1 reported by34. Emblica officinalis is distinguished by its exceptional concentration of vitamin C which underscores its role as a potent antioxidant that protects cells from oxidative damage and reduces the risk of chronic diseases such as heart disease and cancer35.

Vitamin E has neuroprotective properties, which help to prevent neurodegenerative disorders like Alzheimer’s, preserving the integrity of structure in cell membranes and promoting brain well-being36. The maximum vitamin E was found in Antidesma bunius (36.69 ± 0.35 mg/100 g), followed by Emblica officinalis (28.02 ± 0.93 mg/100 g), Spondias pinnata (27.15 ± 0.28 mg/100 g) and Terminalia chebula (27.01 ± 0.28 mg/100 g). In comparison, the least was found in Elaeocarpus lanceifolius (22.88 ± 0.65 mg/100 g) but it was statistically at par with Carallia brachiata (23.24 ± 0.65 mg/100 g) (Table 1). As Vitamin E is localized in the thylakoid membranes, vitamin E shares biosynthetic precursors with other plastidic isoprenoids such as carotenoids thus explaining the higher concentration of vitamin E in Antidesma bunius and Terminalia chebula which also possessed higher concentration of carotenoids37. The vitamin E concentration in Antidesma bunius, Spondias pinnata and Terminalia chebula was within the range already reported by27.

A fibre-rich diet has been proven to reduce the risk of gastrointestinal disorders such as diverticulitis, irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), and colorectal cancer38. The highest crude fibre levels were found in Antidesma bunius (24.27 ± 1.43%). This was followed by Emblica officinalis (21.10 ± 1.16%) and Embelia subcoriacea (16.07 ± 1.11%), while the lowest was in Spondias pinnata (5.60 ± 0.71%), but it was statistically similar to Terminalia chebula (5.73 ± 0.37%) (Table 1). The high crude fiber content in Antidesma bunius, Emblica officinalis and Embelia subcoriaceae is mainly contributed by their high levels of dry matter percentage as observed in the current studies and further indicates its potential as a valuable dietary source of fiber, which is known to promote digestive health by improving digestion and facilitating regular bowel movements39. The crude fibre content observed in Spondias pinnata was marginally higher than the values reported by40.

Alkaloids are found primarily in plants and have significant roles in human health due to their varieties of biological activities. These compounds are recognised for their large range of pharmacological effects, such as anti-inflammatory, pain-relieving, anticancer, and antimicrobial properties41. Morphine and codeine are well-known for their pain-relieving properties and are used as analgesics in medical settings to manage severe pain42. Vinblastine and vincristine an important alkaloids known for their anticancer properties43. The alkaloid content in various wild fruits revealed significant differences among them. Among the studied fruits, Terminalia chebula (192.84 ± 4.84 mg/100 g) had the highest alkaloid, followed by Elaeocarpus lanceiofolius (164.50 ± 5.01 mg/100 g) and Carallia brachiata (147.44 ± 4.57 mg/100 g). Conversely, Emblica officinalis (89.94 ± 4.58 mg/100 g) exhibited the lowest alkaloid, followed by Spondias pinnata (95.36 ± 3.36 mg/100 g) (Table 1). The significant amounts of alkaloids in Terminalia chebula indicate its promise as a valuable reservoir of these bioactive substances.

Saponins can bind to cholesterol in the digestive system, thus inhibiting its absorption and promoting excretion. This mechanism aids in reducing blood cholesterol levels and lowering cardiovascular disease risk44. Additionally, saponins display cytotoxic activity against cancer cells and have shown promise in treating various cancers45. In our investigation, among the 7 wild edible fruits, Carallia brachiata exhibited the maximum saponin (13.70 ± 0.50 mg/100 g), followed by Terminalia chebula (11.93 ± 1.08 mg/100 g) and Antidesma bunius (9.78 ± 0.15 mg/100 g). In contrast, the lowest saponin content was exhibited in Embelia subcoriacea (3.45 ± 0.73 mg/100 g (Table 1). The saponin content was slightly lower than the values recorded by46. These variations in saponin content are influenced by the specific plant species, the particular plant part, and various environmental factors. The high saponin content in Carallia brachiata, Terminalia chebula and Antidesma bunius reveals their significant potential in promoting human health and highlights their importance in drug and pharmaceutical formulations.

Composition of mineral in wild edible fruits

Minerals are crucial constituent of our meals. They serve diverse purposes, supporting our bones, affecting the functioning of muscles and nerves, and maintaining the body’s water equilibrium47. Additionally, they serve as constituents of enzymes, hormones, and other physiologically active substances. Certain minerals also play a crucial role in ensuring the immune system functions well. This pertains to both the intrinsic defence system and the adaptive immune response. The availability of minerals may impact both infection susceptibility and chronic illness development48. Micronutrient deficiencies pose a substantial public health issue in several developing nations, particularly endangering new borns and pregnant women49.

Nitrogen is a crucial constituent of amino acids, which serve as the foundational units of proteins. Proteins are crucial in developing, restoring, and maintaining bodily tissues. Nitrogen is an essential component of nucleotides, constituting DNA and RNA’s building blocks. These molecules are crucial in storing and transmitting genetic information50. The research revealed that Carallia brachiata had the maximum nitrogen content (5.90 ± 0.09%), followed by Antidesma bunius (4.87 ± 0.21%) and Elaeocarpus lanceifolius (4.65 ± 0.05%). Embelia subcoriacea had the lowest nitrogen content (1.89 ± 0.09%) (Table 2). The diversity in nitrogen composition across various fruits may be ascribed to the quantities of nitrogen in the soil. The nitrogen concentration in Antidesma bunius was greater than the values reported by46. These findings highlight the importance of Carallia brachiata and other species with high nitrogen content in contributing to dietary nitrogen intake and potentially enhancing soil fertility through organic matter decomposition.

Phosphorus is a pivotal compound of adenosine triphosphate (ATP), the chemical responsible for storing and transferring energy inside cells. This is crucial for the creation of energy and metabolism. Being a constituent of DNA and RNA, phosphorus is crucial for storing and transmitting genetic information. When combined with calcium, phosphorus is essential for developing and maintaining strong bones and teeth. Around 85% of phosphorus in our body is located inside the skeletal system in bones and teeth51. The results displayed that Antidesma bunius (0.26 ± 0.01%) had the highest P content, followed by Carallia brachiata (0.25 ± 0.005%). Conversely, Emblica officinalis (0.08 ± 0.001%) displayed the lowest P levels (Table 2). The amount of phosphorus present in Emblica officinalis was higher compared to the values reported by34. The high phosphorus content in Antidesma bunius and Carallia brachiata indicates their potential nutritional benefits, particularly in regions where phosphorus deficiency is a concern. Conversely, the lower phosphorus content in Emblica officinalis suggests it may be less beneficial in phosphorus contribution but could offer other nutritional benefits.

Potassium aids in counterbalancing the impact of sodium and mitigates tension in the walls of blood vessels, facilitating blood pressure reduction. Increased consumption of potassium is linked to a decreased likelihood of experiencing a stroke or cardiovascular disorder52. This is crucial for maintaining regular digestive and muscle functioning. The findings demonstrate that Terminalia chebula (4.37 ± 0.10%) had the highest potassium, Elaeocarpus lanceifolius (4.00 ± 0.03%) had the second highest K. Antidesma bunius exhibited the lowest potassium level (1.36 ± 0.01%), followed by Embelia subcoriacea (1.71 ± 0.02%) (Table 2). The high potassium concentration of Terminalia chebula highlights its significance in regulating several metabolic processes, such as glucose and protein metabolism and maintaining fluid balance in the body. This is crucial for cellular function, general homeostasis, and several physiological activities, such as regulating blood pressure, muscular function, and nerve signalling53.

Calcium maintains robust skeletal structures and dental enamel and is essential to release neurotransmitters54. In the present investigation, the highest Ca was reported in Antidesma bunius (376.10 ± 4.88 mg/100 g), followed by Spondias pinnata (202.15 ± 3.27 mg/100 g) and Carallia brachiata (136.00 ± 4.32 mg/100 g). The significant amount of calcium in Antidesma bunius and Spondias pinnata suggests it is importance for neuromuscular function and bone health. The relatively low calcium level of Terminalia chebula indicates that it may not provide as much calcium, but it might still give other nutritional benefits55.

Magnesium is a cofactor for various enzyme activities, including energy generation, DNA, and protein synthesis. Approximately 60% of the body’s magnesium is kept inside the skeletal system. It contributes to bone formation and affects osteoblasts and osteoclast functions, which are responsible for constructing and deconstructing bone tissue56. Among the studied fruits, The highest Mg was recorded in Carallia brachiata (373.67 ± 4.78 mg/100 g), followed by Emblica officinalis (321.33 ± 4.91 mg/100 g) and Embelia subcoriacea (293.33 ± 3.13 mg/100 g). Sodium plays a pivotal role in regulating the body’s fluid balance by controlling the movement of water in cells, which is essential for proper hydration and cellular function. Sodium regulates fluid balance, nerve function, and muscle contraction. In our present investigation, Elaeocarpus lanceifolius (59.37 ± 0.08 mg/100 g) had the maximum Na, followed by Carallia brachiata (59.05 ± 0.93 mg/100 g) and Emblica officinalis (57.82 ± 4.48 mg/100 g). While fruits like Elaeocarpus lanceifolius, Carallia brachiata, and Emblica officinalis can provide valuable sodium, it is essential to take into account individual dietary requirements and health conditions when including these fruits in the diet60.

The fruits’ dry matter content (DMC) is a critical indicator of various quality attributes and holds significant implications for consumers and producers. It is commonly used to evaluate the quality and ripeness of fruits. Understanding the dry matter content in fruits is crucial for making well-informed decisions about their nutritional value, shelf life, and appeal to consumers64. Higher dry matter content generally suggests better quality, as it signifies the accumulation of sugars, starches, proteins, and other nutrients. This is particularly crucial for fruits such as apples, pears, and avocados, where DMC is a reliable indicator of ripeness and the ideal time for harvesting65. Among the studied fruits, the maximum dry matter content was observed in Emblica officinalis (37.84 ± 0.78%) and Antidesma bunius (30.19 ± 0.73%). Spondias pinnata (12.93 ± 2.76%) recorded the least dry matter (Table 3). These findings indicate that Emblica officinalis and Antidesma bunius are likely to possess higher nutrient, sugar, and other beneficial compounds, making them more nutrient-dense and potentially more flavourful to include in nutrition diet. Additionally, the higher dry matter content in these fruits suggests a longer shelf life due to lower water content, which reduces the risk of microbial growth and spoilage.

The ash content of fruits is crucial in nutritional labelling and dietary assessments. In general, higher ash concentration is associated with increased mineral content, thereby contributing to the nutritional value and flavour of the fruit66. The study revealed that Spondias pinnata (5.44 ± 0.08%) exhibited maximum ash content, followed by Elaeocarpus lanceifolius (4.18 ± 0.09%). However, Emblica officinalis (1.16 ± 0.12%) reported the lowest ash content (Table 3). These compelling results highlight the rich mineral content in Spondias pinnata and Elaeocarpus lanceifolius, underscoring their significant role in promoting crucial physiological functions such as bone health, enzyme activity, and overall metabolic processes67. The results reported are slightly higher than the range reported by40. The data obtained aligns with the findings documented in the study in the eastern Himalayas by31.

Lignin plays a critical role in providing structural support and strength to cell walls, which is essential for maintaining the firmness and texture of fruits. This is critical in determining fruit’s marketability and consumer acceptance68. The highest levels of lignin was observed in Antidesma bunius (12.63 ± 0.57%), followed by Embelia subcoriacea (10.64 ± 0.01%) and Emblica officinalis (9.66 ± 0.01%), while the lowest was identified in Terminalia chebula (2.06 ± 0.01%) (Table 3). The lignin concentration in wild fruits typically increases as they mature or grow over time. Comprehending lignin’s function in fruit lignification is crucial to enhance postharvest storage techniques. The lignification process encompasses lignin deposition and can impact fruit ripening and softening69.

Cellulose is essential for preserving the firmness and texture of fruits, which directly impacts their marketability and consumer appeal. Knowledge of the cellulose content in fruits is essential for their processing and preservation as it influences the texture and stability of processed fruit products like jams, jellies, and dried fruits70. The study revealed that Embelia subcoriacea (75.43 ± 2.61 mg/100 g) had the highest cellulose content followed by Carallia brachiata (66.85 ± 1.63 mg/100 g) and Emblica officinalis (65.87 ± 0.001 mg100 g–1, while Elaeocarpus lanceifolius (39.02 ± 0.54 mg100 g–1 displayed the lowest cellulose content (Table 3). The findings indicate that Embelia subcoriacea, Carallia brachiata, and Emblica officinalis are having abundance cellulose, which is crucial for maintaining the structural integrity of fruits and serving as dietary fibre. Furthermore, as a dietary fibre, cellulose promotes a healthy gut microbiome by acting as a prebiotic71.

Hemicellulose is vital in providing structural support to plant cell walls. It aids in bonding cellulose fibres, thereby enhancing the overall strength and rigidity of the cell wall. Unlike cellulose, hemicellulose is more flexible and readily undergoes hydrolysis. This flexibility is essential for accommodating cell wall expansion and growth, particularly during fruit development and ripening71. In our observation, maximum hemicellulose was recorded in Elaeocarpus lanceifolius (6.36 ± 0.01%), followed by Spondias pinnata (5.97 ± 0.01%) and Terminalia chebula (5.82 ± 0.02%). Conversely, the least hemicellulose content was in Carallia brachiata (1.87 ± 0.01%) (Table 3). The higher hemicellulose in Elaeocarpus lanceifolius and Spondias pinnata may prove to be an essential source of dietary fibre which can be broken down through fermentation by gut bacteria and promoting gut health and lowering the risk of certain diseases72.

Furthermore, the sugars in fruits provide a readily available source of quick energy, as they can be swiftly converted into glucose, which is vital for both brain function and physical activity74.

The total soluble solids (TSS) encompass the aggregate concentration of all substances such as sugars, acids, proteins, and minerals that can dissolve in fruit. Anti-nutrients in the wild edible fruits

Phytic acid is a natural substance commonly present in fruits. It is often considered an anti-nutrient as it can bind to essential minerals like iron, zinc, calcium, and magnesium, thus lowering their bioavailability and absorption in the human body77. The presence of phytic acid, in the form of phytate, was highest in Embelia subcoriacea (7.06 ± 0.16 mg/100 g), followed by Antidesma bunius (5.64 ± 0.10 mg/100 g) and Spondias pinnata (3.85 ± 0.03 mg/100 g). The lowest phytate level was observed in Elaeocarpus lanceifolius (3.41 ± 0.02 mg 100 g–1(Table 5). In our study, the phytate values obtained in Antidesma bunius and Spondias pinnata are slightly lower than the findings of27. The variation in phytic acid content among these fruits highlights the importance of considering anti-nutrient levels when evaluating their nutritional value. High-phytic acid fruits like Embelia subcoriacea and Antidesma bunius may require processing methods such as soaking, sprouting, or fermentation to reduce phytic acid levels and enhance mineral bioavailability82. On the other hand, fruits with lower phytic acid content, like Elaeocarpus lanceifolius, can be consumed with minimal processing, providing a more readily available source of essential minerals89.

Oxalates, or oxalic acid, are naturally found in many plant-based foods, including fruits. These compounds are considered anti-nutrients as they bind to minerals like calcium, forming insoluble complexes hindering mineral absorption and, in some cases, contributing to the formation of kidney stones in susceptible individuals90. Carallia brachiata (11.44 ± 1.32 mg/100 g) reported the largest concentration of oxalate, followed by Embelia subcoriacea (6.16 ± 0.44 mg/100 g) and Spondias pinnata (5.50 ± 1.10 mg/100 g). In contrast, Antidesma bunius (2.42 ± 0.66 mg/100 g) had the lowest value (Table 5). This high oxalate content may affect the absorption of minerals and could pose a risk for individuals susceptible to kidney stones91. The elevated levels of oxalate in Embelia subcoriacea indicate that while these fruits are nutritious, their ability to deliver minerals to the body may be diminished due to the binding effect of oxalates89.

Tannins are natural polyphenol compounds, commonly present in various plants, including fruits. These compounds are classified as anti-nutrients due to their ability to interact with proteins and other macromolecules, leading to decreased digestibility and hindered nutrient absorption92. The study revealed that Terminalia chebula (483.78 ± 7.49 mg/100 g) had the highest tannin but it was statistically at par with Emblica officinalis (317.55 ± 4.91 mg/100 g) and Spondias pinnata (211.55 ± 1.91 mg/100 g). The least tannins was observed in Antidesma bunius (48.22 ± 1.68 mg/100 g) (Table 5). The highest tannin in Terminalia chebula may impact its protein digestibility and overall nutrient absorption93. It is essential to note that tannin compounds are not toxic while consuming quantities below 560 mg/100 g, which is considered a safe daily intake for individuals94.

The presence of a notable variety of bioactive phytochemicals, dietary fibres, vitamins, and mineral nutrients highlights their potential for the development of value-added functional foods, nutraceuticals, fortified drinks, therapeutic dietary supplements, and clean-label food items. Fruits like Terminalia chebula and Emblica officinalis, which show significant levels of carotenoids, vitamin C, proteins, and vital minerals, can act as valuable raw materials for antioxidant-enriched products designed to support immune health and alleviate disorders linked to oxidative stress. Additionally, species abundant in fiber and minerals, such as Antidesma bunius, might be investigated for creating foods fortified with dietary fiber and micronutrient supplements. In public health nutrition, integrating these nutrient-rich fruits into nutrition programs for school children and community dietary diversification efforts could play a crucial role in reducing micronutrient deficiencies and non-communicable diseases. Furthermore, advancing sustainable farming, value addition, agro-processing, and market integration of these wild fruit resources could generate new livelihood prospects for local communities in the Indo–Burma biodiversity hotspot.

Conclusion

The current study emphasizes the extraordinary nutritional and phytochemical properties of wild edible fruits from Mizoram, India, highlighting their potential as functional foods with significant health benefits. The studied species showed unique nutritional profiles, highlighting their combined importance in enhancing dietary variety, increasing micronutrient content, and meeting the rising need for natural bioactive substances. Among the fruits assessed, Terminalia chebula emerged as the most nutritionally exceptional species, showing remarkably high levels essential functional and nutritional components, such as carotenoids, alkaloids, carbohydrates, proteins, energy, potassium, and cobalt, while maintaining lower levels of antinutritional tannins. Similarly, Carallia brachiata, Antidesma bunius, Emblica officinalis, Embelia subcoriacea, Elaeocarpus lanceifolius, and Spondias pinnata showcased species-specific enhancement of beneficial phytochemicals.

Together, these results identify wild edible fruits from north-east India as an overlooked source of functional biomolecules with significant potential for use in nutraceutical, pharmaceutical, and functional food sectors. Their consistent inclusion in the diet could play a substantial role in addressing micronutrient deficiencies and lowering the likelihood of chronic degenerative diseases through natural antioxidant and bioactive processes. In addition to their nutritional significance, these species serve as a vital aspect of indigenous food systems and customary ethnobotanical knowledge.

The research emphasizes the need for systematic conservation, scientific evaluation, and sustainable use of these overlooked bioresources. Strategic initiatives focused on community awareness, domestication, value addition, and commercialization could improve rural livelihoods, bolster regional food and nutritional security, and aid in biodiversity conservation within the Indo-Burma biodiversity hotspot. Ultimately, these findings underscore the critical importance of conserving and utilizing local wild flora to combat hidden hunger and support sustainable food security initiatives in the region. Subsequent research concentrating on bioavailability, pharmacological effectiveness, metabolomic analysis, and clinical verification of these bioactive compounds will further reveal the therapeutic and industrial capabilities of these wild edible fruits, facilitating their incorporation into evidence-supported functional nutrition and sustainable health frameworks.
