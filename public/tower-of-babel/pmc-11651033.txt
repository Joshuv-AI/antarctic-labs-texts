Implementation of ultrasound-guided cannulation training for foundation doctors
Authors: Mia Pham, Robert Aldous, Stephan Brincat
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeripheral intravenous cannulation (PIVC) is an essential skill for newly qualified foundation doctors. It has high failure rates, resulting in care delays, pain and infection. We explored the perceived impact of ultrasound-guided PIVC (US-PIVC) training on confidence when performing difficult-access PIVC. We surveyed 88 foundation doctors to evaluate their perceptions of US-PIVC. Each cohort of participants was given two sessions over a 2-month period. Confidence was assessed using electronic questionnaires through 5-point Likert scales and free text responses. Confidence when performing PIVC with difficult access, regardless of US, increased significantly following the course (p < 0.01). This was also true regarding confidence when performing US-PIVC (p < 0.01.). Key themes identified included technical skills, confidence and clinical utility.Keywords: Ultrasound, Ultrasound-guided, Peripheral intravenous cannulation, Foundation doctors, Audit, Simulation

Background

Peripheral intravenous cannulation (PIVC) is an essential skill that newly qualified foundation doctors are tasked with regularly in both emergency and acute medical unit settings. It is considered a core competency within the foundation programme curriculum (UK)1 and is also outlined as a key outcome by the General Medical Council (UK) for provisionally registered doctors (postgraduate year 1).2 Despite this, it has high failure rates with up to 26% of patients requiring multiple insertion attempts, which can result in pain, infection and delays in patient care.3

Traditionally, PIVC has been carried out using landmark-driven palpation. However, intravenous access can be challenging in intravenous drug users, oedematous or frail patients.4 In these circumstances, central venous access is often the only alternative. The procedure has significant risks including infection, pneumothorax and mechanical complications.5 Ultrasound-guided PIVC (US-PIVC) has been shown in multiple studies to increase overall success rates to 90% when directly compared to traditional ‘blind’ techniques, in addition to reducing complications such as skin breakage, pain and incorrect placement.6, 7, 8 Other added benefits include the reduction in unnecessary central venous access, the decrease in junior doctor reliance on seniors, and improved patient satisfaction. In addition, timely cannulation can reduce dose omission or delays, paramount to patient safety.9,10

US-PIVC training has yet to be incorporated into foundation programme curricula. There has been growing interest in incorporating ultrasound-based competencies within undergraduate curriculums, with some medical schools opting to integrate ultrasound teaching in all aspects of their curriculums.11 The Royal College of Radiologists in the UK recommended that ultrasound competencies be assessed within the foundation or undergraduate curriculum.12

We aimed to assess foundation doctors’ experience of US-PIVC teaching within a large tertiary centre to evaluate whether it would be a valuable addition to the foundation and undergraduate curriculum in the UK.

Methods

Initial pre-course survey

We surveyed 98 trainees: 46 foundation year 1 (FY1), 44 foundation year 2 doctors and eight trust-grade doctors working at St George's Hospital (SGH) to evaluate their perceptions of US-PIVC. An internet-based electronic survey available on Google Forms (See Appendix 1 and 2) was sent to each participant. Pre- and post-course confidence was assessed using 6-point Likert scales and free text questions. The questionnaire was accessible for a 2-month period from September 2023 to October 2023. Eligible participants were foundation doctors (year 1 or 2) currently in training at SGH. Demographic information regarding respondent training grade was also collected. 65 foundation doctors recorded responses for analysis.

Teaching course structure

The US-PIVC training consisted of a total of four sessions (n = 16, n = 20, n = 22, n = 25). Each cohort of foundation doctors was given two sessions over a 2-month period from October 2023 to January 2024. Teaching was delivered by one core anaesthetic trainee and three internal medicine trainees. Teaching sessions consisted of two sections, which were 15 and 45 min long respectively:

•Section 1 – An introductory lecture on basic principles of ultrasound and its application to identify veins and arteries.

•Section 2 (students were split into small groups) – Walk through and live practice of US-PIVC on a model.*

Each trainer facilitated a small group for each session. Each cohort of foundation doctors then had a repeat teaching session a month following the first, to enable the trainees to further hone the US-PIVC skill.

Post-course evaluation

A post-course electronic questionnaire was distributed via a QR code after the sessions. Email reminders were sent periodically post-teaching session to ensure completion. Each participant was assigned a unique response number to correlate pre- and post-course responses for anonymity. Pre- and post-course Likert scores were compared using the Wilcoxon signed rank test. Box plots using the ‘ggplot2’ function on R were used to graph the data for FY1 and FY2 doctors respectively. All analyses were performed using R statistical software (v4.2.3 2021) (See Appendix 3 for full R script.) Free text responses were grouped according to themes identified. We set an alpha level of significance of 0.01.

Results

Pre-training questionnaires

About 65 of the 98 trainees (66%) responded to the initial pre-course questionnaire. 30 of these were FY1 doctors (46.2%), 27 of these were FY2 doctors (41.5%) and eight were trust-grade doctors (12.3%). About 38.5% of participants reported that they had not had any previous ultrasound teaching and 53.8% had never had specific US-PIVC teaching. When asked about their confidence levels, respondents reported a median score of 2/5 (interquartile range, IQR = 2) for dealing with patients with difficult venous access. Specifically for US-PIVC, the median score was 1/5 (IQR = 1). 100% of those surveyed agreed that US-PIVC should be included within the foundation programme curriculum.

Post-training questionnaire

About 55 trainees of the initial 65 surveyed completed the post-training questionnaire. Those that did not complete the post-training questionnaire were excluded from Likert-scale analysis. Participants reported a median score of 5/5 for usefulness of the teaching sessions (IQR = 0). Confidence when performing PVC on patients with difficult access, regardless of ultrasound, increased significantly following the session (p < 0.001) (See Fig. 1). This was also true for confidence when performing US-PIVC (p < 0.001). Likert scale analysis was supported by free text responses. Key themes identified from feedback highlighted technical skills, improved confidence when cannulating, clinical utility and future use. Participants praised the interactivity of the sessions, and the opportunity to practise the skill multiple times.

Discussion

Outcomes of course

Various literature has shown that US-PIVC is associated with fewer failed procedures and reduces complications associated with landmark-driven palpation.13,14 A recent meta-analysis (1,860 patients, 10 studies) demonstrated that US-PIVC was associated with a two-times higher percentage of first cannulation (OR: 2.1, 95% CI), and higher patient satisfaction (95% CI, p < 0.001).6 Nonetheless, factors such as practitioner experience and clinician confidence are key confounding factors.15 Our study demonstrates the positive impact of the pilot US-PIVC course for foundation doctors, and was on a large enough scale to have a significant effect size when reporting confidence immediately post-training (p < 0.01). These outcomes reflect Kirkpatrick's model level 1 (reaction) and level 2 (learning) outcomes.16 Trainees reported that the training was engaging and relevant to their role as foundation doctors, and the repeat session enabled trainees to practise and apply the knowledge gained.

It is interesting to note that post-course confidence when approaching difficult PIVC improved regardless of ultrasound, suggesting an improved understanding of vascular anatomy post-course. Our study demonstrates that US-PIVC improves clinician confidence, which has been shown to positively influence insertion success (p < 0.0001) in a recent cohort study.17 Similar studies to ours ran training programmes – albeit on a smaller level (n = 20, n = 18) – and corroborated our finding that US-PIVC increases trainees’ confidence in both landmark-based methods and ultrasound-guided approaches.18,19

Simulation-based medical education provides a safe learning environment for both teacher and trainer.20 Our study utilised this approach and has demonstrated that trainees developed not only technical skills but also awareness of personal limitations, which is essential for further professional development.

Limitations

Key limitations of this study include the lack of any structured objective assessment of US-PIVC competence both immediately following the course, and in the post-training period. The use of self-reported scores to measure confidence effect size post-training also has limitations. Trainees may have over-reported confidence, which could have misrepresented the quality of training assessed. Literature has shown that there is poor correlation between self-confidence ratings and objective assessment, and these should not be used as a standalone tool.21,22

Our results are also limited by a small sample size of 65 doctors. Seven doctors had to be excluded due to teaching non-attendance and three doctors did not fill out the post-teaching questionnaire. We were unable to therefore assess the clinical teaching fellows / trust-grade doctors due to not having a set weekly teaching session. Nonetheless, from the preliminary data, self-reported confidence levels were higher in this group at baseline. We did not follow up data on whether participating trainees found the use of ultrasound reduced the likelihood of reliance on senior colleagues. Specific data on whether trainees found that they had to escalate to the anaesthetic/ICU teams for difficult cannulation would be useful.

Implications for future practice

Key strengths of our teaching sessions include small group sessions which enabled students to feel more confident in practising US-PIVC for the first time, with the opportunity for individual teacher–student interaction and feedback. The sessions were of greatest benefit to the FY1 trainee group, many of whom had not ever had the opportunity to practise this skill. The course could realistically be expanded to final-year medical students with adequate time, resources and facilitators. Nonetheless, there runs the risk that trainees may ‘deskill’ if they become solely dependent on US-PIVC.

Conclusion

Our data support various other literature that trainees find US-PIVC training enjoyable and that it increases their confidence. Nonetheless, we need more evidence on the clinical impact of the training; specifically on patient safety, medication delays and reduction on senior reliance. There is scope for a larger study correlating confidence scores with assessment scores immediately post-course, and longer-term analysis on impact on failure rates when cannulating in the acute medical unit. We hope to extend this training to final-year medical students at St George's University, and for it to become a permanent feature of the undergraduate medical curriculum.
