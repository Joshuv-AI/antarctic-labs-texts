Knot complements decomposing into prisms
Jason DeBlois, Arshia Gharagozlou, Neil R Hoffman

Abstract
. We describe four hyperbolic knot complements in 𝕊 3 \mathbb{S}^{3} , each of which covers a prism orbifold : the quotient of ℍ 3 \mathbb{H}^{3} by the action of a discrete group generated by reflections in the faces of a polyhedron that has the combinatorial type of a triangular prism. The prism orbifolds are rigid-cusped and contain compact, totally geodesic hyperbolic triangle sub-orbifolds; as a result, the knot complements covering them have hidden symmetries and contain closed, embedded, totally geodesic surfaces.

1.
Introduction

Three hyperbolic knot complements in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
are known to cover a
tetrahedral reflection orbifold
: the quotient of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
by a discrete group generated by reflections in the four faces of a tetrahedron. For each of these knot complements, the cover to a tetrahedral orbifold is obtained from a
platonic
decomposition, into isometric copies of a single non-compact, finite-volume polyhedron in the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{3}
whose isometry group acts transitively on “flags” of the form (face, edge, ideal vertex). They are the figure-eight knot complement, which decomposes into two regular ideal tetrahedra as showed in Thurston’s Notes
[
56
]
, and the two ‘‘dodecahedral knots’’ attributed to Aitchison--Rubinstein
1

1

The dodecahedral knot with a fibered complement appears as Figure 1b in
[
54
]
. Riley refers to it there as ‘Thurston’s knot’, based on a 1981 lecture in which, according to Riley’s account, Thurston labeled it as a “totally asymmetric knot”. However as showed by Aitchison–Rubinstein
[
7
, §12]
, it is not in fact totally asymmetric. SnapPy
[
18
]
computes the full symmetry group of its complement as
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
.

[
6
]
.

Those listed above are the only platonic knot complements in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
, by works of A.W. Reid, who proved that the only arithmetic hyperbolic knot complement is that of the figure-eight
[
52
]
, and of the third author concerning the dodecahedral knots
[
27
]
.
After the tetrahedron, the next least-complicated three-dimensional polyhedron having all trivalent vertices is a triangular prism.
We will say that a
prism orbifold
is the quotient of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
by the action of a discrete group generated by reflections in the five faces of such a prism.
Our main result is:

Theorem 1.1
.

There exist hyperbolic knot complements in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
that cover prism orbifolds.

In fact we exhibit four such knot complements, see

—or eight, if one distinguishes handedness, since they are chiral (

gives counterexamples to two conjectures from the early-to-mid 1990’s, on the non-existence of hyperbolic knot complements having hidden symmetries or closed, embedded, totally geodesic surfaces. A commensurability classification of the relevant prism orbifolds supplied in

also shows that

1.1.
Hidden symmetries of knot complements

A
hidden symmetry
of a space
X
is a homeomorphism between finite-degree covers of
X
that does not lift a self-homeomorphism of
X
. Among all hyperbolic manifolds, the arithmetic ones are characterized by having infinitely many hidden symmetries up to a natural equivalence. Non-arithmetic hyperbolic 3-manifolds having
some
hidden symmetries are also not uncommon, though. For instance, any manifold that non-normally covers an orbifold has a hidden symmetry.

However,
hyperbolic knot complements in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
with hidden symmetries seem exceedingly rare: until now, only the three platonic knot complements were known to have them, and many more are known not to. The reasons for this go back to a landmark paper of Neumann–Reid from the early 1990’s
[
48
]
. Proposition 9.1 there implies that a hyperbolic knot complement in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
has hidden symmetries if and only if it covers a rigid-cusped orbifold. Here we pause to recall that it follows from Margulis’s Lemma that cusp cross-sections of hyperbolic
3
-orbifolds are Euclidean
2
-orbifolds (see eg.
[
20
, §2]
). We call an orbifold
rigid-cusped
if any such cusp cross-section is a
rigid
Euclidean orbifold: one of the
(2,3,6)
,
(2,4,4)
, or
(3,3,3)
-triangle orbifolds, or their orientable double covers (“turnovers”).

Proposition 9.1 of
[
48
]
implies that the dodecahedral knot complements have hidden symmetries, since they cover rigid-cusped tetrahedral orbifolds. Having observed this, and in light of Reid’s result (recently proved at that time) that the only arithmetic knot complement is that of the figure-eight, Neumann–Reid asked if there exist knot complements with hidden symmetries other than those of the figure-eight and dodecahdral knots
[
48
, Q. 1, p. 307]
. A few years later, with no new examples having emerged in the meantime, they conjectured that in fact there are no others
[
21
, Problem 3.64(a)]
.

Since this conjecture was posed, multiple authors have contributed to a now-substantial literature showing that various classes of hyperbolic knot complements satisfy it: those of the two-bridge knots
[
53
]
and of all “highly twisted” knots
[
30
]
, for instance, and many others
[
26
]
,
[
40
]
,
[
46
]
,
[
16
]
,
[
15
]
. These all rely on the criterion given in
[
48
, Prop. 9.1]
. One can apply the same criterion, using rigorous computations with SnapPy within Sage
[
19
]
, to rule out eg. all at-most-fifteen-crossing knots in the census besides the figure-eight.

However since the prism orbifolds have rigid cusps (cf.
Remark

2.1
),
Theorem

4.1
yields:

Corollary
4.2
.

In addition to the figure-eight and dodecahedral knot complements, there are at least four more hyperbolic knot complements in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
that have hidden symmetries.

In Conjecture 1.3 of their 2015 paper
[
11
]
on commensurability and hidden symmetries of knot complements, Boileau-Boyer-Cebanu-Walsh conjectured that every knot complement with hidden symmetries covers an orbifold with a
(2,3,6)
-cusp. Our new examples are also counterexamples to this conjecture.

Corollary
4.3
.

There are at least four hyperbolic knot complements in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
that have hidden symmetries and do not cover an orbifold with a
(2,3,6)
-cusp.

This follows from
3.5
, which shows that the
(3,3,3)
-cusped prism orbifolds covered by our examples do not cover any other orbifold. That result also allows us to organize our examples of knot complements with hidden symmetries into
commensurability classes
: collections of manifolds that pairwise share a finite-degree cover.

Corollary 1.2
.

There are at least four distinct commensurability classes of hyperbolic 3-orbifolds that contain knot complements with hidden symmetries.

For this result, our four new examples divide into two new commensurability classes, containing two each—see
Theorem

4.1
—adding to those of the figure-eight and of the dodecahedral knot complements (which are commensurable with each other but not the figure-eight).

1.2.
The Menasco-Reid conjecture

In the same collection as
[
48
]
,
Topology ’90
, Menasco–Reid published a paper in which they conjectured that no hyperbolic knot complement in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
contains a closed, embedded totally geodesic surface
[
45
, Conjecture 1]
, and established this for the complements of
alternating
knots and of tunnel number one knots (which includes the two-bridge knots). This conjecture is also in the Kirby list
[
21
, Problem 1.76]
. It is now known to hold for many more families of knots, including almost alternating knots
[
4
]
, toroidally alternating knots
[
3
]
, knots of braid index
3

[
39
]
and
4

[
44
]
, Montesinos knots
[
49
]
, and three-bridge and double-torus knots
[
32
]
.

In the other direction, Leininger showed in
[
38
]
that ‘one can get “as close as possible” to a counter-example’, by constructing a sequence of hyperbolic knot complements in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
containing embedded surfaces whose principal curvatures approach
0
. We go all the way: in
2.8
below, we observe that every manifold cover of a prism orbifold contains a closed, embedded totally geodesic surface, hence obtaining the following from
Theorem

4.1
.

Corollary 1.3
.

There are at least four hyperbolic knot complements in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
that each contain a closed embedded totally geodesic surface.

In fact, widening the lens to closed, not-necessarily-embedded, totally geodesic surfaces in hyperbolic knot complements does not grow the list of already-known examples much. The figure-eight
[
41
]
and dodecahedral knot
[
7
]
complements contain closed, immersed (not embedded) totally geodesic surfaces, but these are all that we know of. Thus:

Corollary 1.4
.

In addition to the figure-eight and dodecahedral knot complements, there are at least four more hyperbolic knot complements in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
that have closed immersed totally geodesic surfaces.

To motivate our next corollary, we note that the three previously known knots in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
whose complements have hidden symmetries are each alternating. But since the knot complements of
Corollary

1.3
have hidden symmetries, by
[
45
]
they are not alternating. More broadly, from the other works cited in this subsection’s first paragraph we obtain:

Corollary 1.5
.

There exist knots in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
with complements admitting hidden symmetries that are neither alternating, almost alternating, toroidally alternating, nor Montesinos.

We finally mention the following consequence of the more-technical
Theorem

6.7
.

Corollary 1.6
.

There exist distinct knot complements in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
that are related by mutation along a closed, embedded totally geodesic surface.

Here,
mutation
refers to cutting along the surface and re-gluing the resulting pieces along their boundaries by a self-isometry. Like the well-known mutation along four-punctured spheres (cf.
[
55
]
), hyperbolic knot complements related in this way have the same volume.

1.3.
Computational Tools

In
Section

4
we present the main examples of this paper in such a way that their properties can in principle be extracted by human computation. We carry this out for one of our four examples in
Section

5
, culminating in
Theorem

5.14
which implies
Theorem

1.1
. Hence this paper does contain a complete by-hand proof of
Theorem

1.1
and the existence of counterexamples to the conjectures mentioned below it. The proof of
Theorem

4.1
in
Section

4
appeals at points to machine computation, however, using both standard tools of computational topology (SnapPy
[
18
]
and Regina
[
13
]
) and custom-coded Python scripts. These computations are combinatorial in nature and fully rigorous.

These examples were also
produced
with the aid of machine computation, and it is fair to say that we could not have produced them without it. We give a detailed description of this process in
Section

7
. Here is a high-level overview of its steps.

1.3.1
Pre-filtering.

For each member of an initial list of 54 individual prism orbifolds and 8 infinite families, we tested a sequence of necessary conditions for being covered by a knot complement. This process was expedited by custom-coded Python scripts and the use of Sage
[
19
]
. Twelve individual prism orbifolds, and two infinite sub-families, survived all tests.

1.3.2
Enumeration.

The degree of any manifold cover of an orbifold is at least the least common multiple of its vertex group orders. The smallest of these values, among the prism orbifolds surviving pre-filtering, is
24
. For each of the three (3,3,3)-cusped prism orbifolds attaining this bound we used the
low-index
Python method coded by Culler et al
[
17
]
, which implements a computational group theory algorithm due to C.C. Sims, to enumerate all subgroups of its orbifold
\pi_{1}
up to index
24
, yielding
32\,425
,
29\,432
, and
306\,552
subgroups.

1.3.3
Filtering.

Each subgroup from
1.3.2
is given in terms of a right-permutation representation, which we converted to a polyhedral decomposition of the corresponding cover (cf.
Lemma

4.9
below). Guided by this, we custom-coded Python scripts to test each cover for having one cusp, being a manifold, and having first homology
\mathbb{Z}
. These yielded
20
,
22
, and
12
subgroups, respectively, corresponding to covers having all three properties.

1.3.4
Lens space recognition.

For each subgroup remaining after
1.3.3
, we converted the corresponding cover’s cell decomposition to a triangulation and used methods of Regina
[
13
]
and SnapPy
[
18
]
to test for lens space fillings. These tests were successful for eight covers, belonging to four orientation-reversing-isometric pairs, which yield the four examples of
Theorem

4.1
(eight, up to orientation-preserving isometry) after passing to further covers.

In this paper,

\mathbf{e}=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{9})
of integers greater than 1 specifies a hyperbolic prism as pictured on the left in Figure

Remark 2.1
.

For each nine-tuple
\mathbf{e}
considered in this paper, we require the following:

•

The triple
(a_{1},a_{2},a_{5})
must equal one of
(2,3,6)
,
(3,3,3)

(2,4,4)
up to permutation of entries, so that the “vertex” shared by the edges labeled
a_{1}
,
a_{2}
, and
a_{5}
(circled in the Figure) is ideal in the prism’s geometric realization.

•

For each other vertex, shared by edges labeled
a_{i}
,
a_{j}
and
a_{k}

\frac{1}{a_{i}}+\frac{1}{a_{j}}+\frac{1}{a_{k}}>1
, so that the corresponding vertex of the geometric realization belongs to
\mathbb{H}^{3}
.

These ensure that each prism orbifold considered here has exactly one cusp, which is rigid.

As the ultimate goal of this paper is to discuss examples of prism orbifolds covered by knot complements, the one-cusped case is the only one relevant for this investigation.

The unique orientable double cover of the prism orbifold specified by a tuple
\mathbf{e}
as above is the quotient

of

\widetilde{\Pi}(\mathbf{e})
generated by rotations around the sides of one of the prism

\bar{P}(\mathbf{e})

,
a_{4}
,
a_{5}
, and

(1)

Definition 7.1
.

Let

The Lemma below is a version of
[
10

[

Lemma 7.2

(Boileau-Boyer-Cebanu-Walsh, Hoffman)
.

Let

be an orientable hyperbolic 3-orbifold with a non-torus cusp covered by knot complement. Let

denote the cusp killing homomorphism. Then,

is trivial. Additionally,
|Q|\cong B^{3}

is connected to the cusp, and the fundamental group is generated by peripheral torsion elements (
|Q|
denotes the underlying topological space of

Each orientable prism orbifold has underlying space

, and its isotropy graph’s sole component—the prism’s one-skeleton—is connected to the cusp. The following algorithm checks the effect of cusp-killing on the orbifold fundamental group; cf.
[

First, erase the edges corresponding to peripheral torsion. Next check any degree 2 vertices in the isotropy graph and resolve the labels via the gcd of the two orders of torsion connected to that vertex. If the gcd is 1, prune these two edges and repeat the process above until either the group goes away or there are not there are two degree two vertices and no edges labeled 1. In the latter case the image under cusp-killing is non-trivial; hence the orbifold is not covered by a knot complement.

7.3.
Enumeration

Having significantly cut down the number of prism orbifolds that might be covered by knot complements, we turn to the problem of identifying which of these orbifolds’ covers might be knot complements. We begin with a general observation about the degrees of manifold covers of orbifolds.

Lemma 7.13
.

For a finite-degree cover
p\colon\thinspace M\to\widetilde{O}
from a manifold
M
to an orientable hyperbolic
3
-orbifold
\widetilde{O}=\mathbb{H}^{3}/\widetilde{\Pi}
, and any
\tilde{x}\in\mathbb{H}^{3}
, the order of the stabilizer
G_{\tilde{x}}
of
\tilde{x}
in
\widetilde{\Pi}
divides the degree of
p
.

Proof.

Let
M=\mathbb{H}^{3}/\Gamma
for a subgroup
\Gamma
of
\widetilde{\Pi}
. Taking
\tilde{x}\in\mathbb{H}^{3}
and
G_{\tilde{x}}<\widetilde{\Pi}
as above, for any non-identity element
g\in G_{v}
and any left coset
\gamma_{i}\Gamma
of
\Gamma
in
\widetilde{\Pi}
, if
g\gamma_{i}\Gamma=\gamma_{i}\Gamma
then
\gamma_{i}^{-1}g\gamma_{i}
is in
\Gamma
. But this can’t happen because
\gamma_{i}^{-1}g\gamma_{i}
is also of finite order, and
\Gamma
is torsion-free since
M
is a manifold. It follows that each orbit of the
G_{\tilde{x}}
-action by left multiplication on the left cosets of
\Gamma
has size
|G_{\tilde{x}}|
. Since the set of all left cosets is a disjoint union of
G_{\tilde{x}}
-orbits,
|G_{\tilde{x}}|
divides
[\widetilde{\Pi}:\Gamma]
.
∎

We apply
Lemma

7.13
to the
vertex groups
of the prism orbifolds, meaning the stabilizers of vertices of geometric realizations of prisms in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
in their associated rotation groups.

Corollary 7.14
.

For a finite-degree manifold cover
p\colon\thinspace M\to\widetilde{O}
, where
\widetilde{O}
is an orientable prism orbifold, and a non-ideal vertex
v
of the associated prism
P
at the intersection of edges labeled
a_{i}
,
a_{j}
and
a_{k}
, the order
|\widetilde{G}_{v}|=2/n_{v}
of the vertex group
\widetilde{G}_{v}
divides the degree of
p
, where
n_{v}=\frac{1}{a_{i}}+\frac{1}{a_{j}}+\frac{1}{a_{k}}-1
.

Proof.

Referring again by
P
to a geometric realization of
P
in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
given by Andreev’s theorem, the vertex
v
is a triple intersection of faces that pairwise meet at dihedral angles of
\pi/a_{i}
,
\pi/a_{j}
, and
\pi/a_{k}
. Therefore the set of unit tangent vectors in
T_{v}\mathbb{H}^{3}
that point into
P
is a spherical triangle with the same set of dihedral angles, hence with area
\pi n_{v}
, for
n_{v}
as above, by the Gauss-Bonnet formula. This triangle is a fundamental domain for the action of
G_{v}
on
T_{v}\mathbb{H}^{3}
, where
G_{v}
is the stabilizer of
v
in the group generated by reflections in the faces of
P
. So since the unit tangent sphere has area
4\pi
,
|G_{v}|=4/n_{v}
. The stabilizer
\widetilde{G}_{v}
of
v
in the orientable prism group has index
2
in
G_{v}
, hence order
2/n_{v}
. The result now follows from
Lemma

7.13
.
∎

The prism orbifolds satisfying all tests of Sections
7.1
and

8.
Further questions

While we have exhibited new hyperbolic knot complements with hidden symmetries, it remains unclear how many there are, or how they are distributed among the collection of all hyperbolic knot complements. For instance, the following is open.

Question 8.1
.

Are there infinitely many hyperbolic knot complements in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
with hidden symmetries?

Even if there are infinitely many, the following still-open conjecture postulates that they are sparsely distributed.

Conjecture 8.2

(
[
16
]
, Conjecture 0.1)
.

For any
R>0
, at most finitely hyperbolic knot complements in
\mathbb{S}^{3}

have hidden symmetries and volume less than
R
.

The search for knot complements with hidden symmetries is also related to the following conjecture of Reid–Walsh.

Conjecture 8.3

(
[
53
]
, Conjecture 5.2(i))
.

A hyperbolic knot complement in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
has at most three knot complements in its commensurability class.

Here we recall from above
Corollary

3.6
that the
commensurability class
of a manifold
M
refers to the collection of manifolds commensurable to
M
, where two spaces are
commensurable
if they have a common cover of finite degree. For instance, it follows from their construction as covers of
O^{333}_{2}
that
\widetilde{M}_{2,1}
and
\widetilde{M}_{2,2}
from
Theorem

4.1
are commensurable; as are
\widetilde{M}_{3,1}
and
\widetilde{M}_{3,2}
. But the
\widetilde{M}_{2,j}
are not commensurable to the
\widetilde{M}_{3,j^{\prime}}
, by
Corollary

3.6
.

Boileau–Boyer–Cebanu–Walsh proved in
[
10
]
that the Reid–Walsh conjecture holds for hyperbolic knot complements
without
hidden symmetries. The case of knot complements with hidden symmetries remains open. In particular, we do not even know answers to:

Question 8.4
.

Does there exist a commensurability class containing infinitely many hyperbolic knot complements in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
?

Question 8.5
.

How many knot complements in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
belong to the commensurability class of
\widetilde{M}_{2,1}
? Of
\widetilde{M}_{3,1}
?

The corresponding question to
8.5
has been answered for the previously-known hyperbolic knot complements with hidden symmetries: recall that the figure-eight knot complement is the only knot complement in its commensurability class, by
[
52
]
; and the known dodecahedral knot complements are the only two in their commensurability class, by
[
27
]
.

Turning to the subject of totally geodesic surfaces in knot complements, we recall that Adams-Schoenfeld constructed the first examples of properly embedded totally geodesic surfaces in hyperbolic knot complements, in
[
2
]
. Their examples are
Seifert surfaces
—non-compact and single-cusped—and they remark that “one could ask whether there are embedded totally geodesic surfaces in knot complements other than Seifert surfaces” (
[
2
, §1]
, final sentence). Our examples show that there are, but the following remains open:

Question 8.6
.

Does there exist a hyperbolic knot complement in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
containing a properly embedded,
non-compact
totally geodesic surface that is not a Seifert surface?

There is also a rich literature on
immersed
totally geodesic surfaces in hyperbolic knot complements, including algebraic obstructions to their existence—
[
42
, Th. 5.3.1, Cor. 5.3.2]
for closed surfaces, and
[
14
]
for all surfaces—and counting results
[
36
]
,
[
37
]
. These are lent additional import by the recent, spectacular characterization of arithmeticity in terms of totally geodesic surfaces
[
9
]
. As mentioned above
Corollary

1.4
, the list of known knot complements containing immersed
closed
totally geodesic surfaces is short. Comparing that result with
Corollary

4.2
sheds light on the following perhaps surprising questions that Genevieve Walsh has asked:

Question 8.7
.

Does there exist a hyperbolic knot complement in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
without hidden symmetries that contains a
closed
, immersed totally geodesic surface?

Question 8.8
.

Conversely, does every hyperbolic knot complement with hidden symmetries contain a closed, immersed totally geodesic surface?

We close with a challenge: to overcome one aspect of the present paper which may not be entirely satisfying—the indirect way in which we know the knot complements
\widetilde{M}_{i,j}
. Specifically:

(1)

Draw a diagram of a knot
K_{i,j}
such that
\widetilde{M}_{i,j}\cong\mathbb{S}^{3}-K_{i,j}
, for
i\in\{2,3\}
and
j\in\{1,2\}
, in a way that makes visible the totally geodesic surface
S_{i,j}
and/or the covering transformation of its projection to a knot in a lens space.

(2)

Compute knot invariants of the
K_{i,j}
that would typically depend on a diagram, for instance their Jones and/or HOMFLY-PT polynomials.

Toward item (1), we recall that Adams proved that for a knot
K\subset\mathbb{S}^{3}
the volume of
\mathbb{S}^{3}-K
is at most
(c-5)v_{8}+4v_{3}

[
1
]
, improving an estimate of D. Thurston, where
c
is the crossing number of
K
and
v_{3}\approx 1.015
and
v_{8}\approx 3.664
are respectively the volumes of an ideal tetrahedron and octahedron. This implies that
\widetilde{M}_{2,1}
and
\widetilde{M}_{3,1}
are the complements of at-least-
159
-crossing knots, and
\widetilde{M}_{2,2}
and
\widetilde{M}_{3,2}
of at-least-
270
-crossing knots, cf.
Remark

4.5
. (We know of no reason to believe that these bounds are near sharp.)

Should (1) prove too challenging, we note that the non-compact side
M_{2,1}^{-}
of
S_{2,1}
in
M_{2,1}
is itself a knot in a handlebody, by
5.12
, with volume “only”
\approx 32.293
, cf.
Corollary

4.4
. It would already be interesting to give a diagram for this knot in a standardly-embedded handlebody.
