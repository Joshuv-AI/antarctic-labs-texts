On a conjecture of Terry and Wolf
V. Gladkova

Abstract
. This paper shows that the VC 2 \mathrm{VC}_{2} -dimension of a subset of 𝔽 p n \mathbb{F}_{p}^{n} known as the ‘quadratic Green-Sanders example’ is at least 3 and at most 501. Bounded VC 2 \mathrm{VC}_{2} -dimension is a model-theoretic tameness property, whose effect on higher-order arithmetic regularity lemmas was recently studied by Terry and Wolf, as an analogue to bounded VC \mathrm{VC} -dimension in the linear setting. It is in that line of work that the quadratic Green-Sanders example was introduced, and the upper bound of 501 confirms a conjecture of Terry and Wolf. The techniques used in the proof are purely combinatorial and exploit a result in bipartite Ramsey theory. Additionally, the paper presents a much simplified proof of the fact that the (linear) Green-Sanders example has VC \mathrm{VC} -dimension at most 3.

1.
Introduction

This paper concerns the so-called
Green-Sanders examples
, a term coined by Terry and Wolf
[
15
]
to describe certain subsets of
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
which are known to be counterexamples to some strengthenings of the arithmetic regularity lemma. An
arithmetic regularity lemma
is a group-theoretic analogue of Szemerédi’s regularity lemma for graphs
[
12
]
: given a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
, it provides a polynomially-structured partition of the space such that on most parts of the partition,
A
is ‘uniform’ in a certain sense. A ‘linear’ version of the arithmetic regularity lemma, proved by Green
[
7
]
, states that for any
\epsilon>0
and any
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
, there is a subspace
H\leqslant\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
such that
A
is Fourier-uniform on all but an
\epsilon
-proportion of the cosets of
H
, with the codimension of
H
depending only on the choice of
\epsilon
. Green
[
7
]
further showed that the codimension of
H
can be as large as a tower of height
\Omega(\log_{2}\epsilon^{-1})
, which was later strengthened to a tower of height
\epsilon^{-1}
by Hosseini, Lovett, Moshkovitz and Shapira
[
9
]
.

This arithmetic regularity lemma cannot be improved to produce a subspace for which
A
is Fourier-uniform on all of its cosets, as demonstrated by Green and Sanders
[
6
]
via a construction which originated in the work of Bergelson, Deuber and Hindman
[
3
]
.

Definition 1.1

(Linear Green-Sanders example)
.

Let
w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}
denote the standard basis of
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
. The
linear Green-Sanders example
in
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
is defined as

\mathrm{GS}(p,n)=\{x\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}:\text{ }\exists i\text{ }w_{j}^{T}x=0\text{ for }j<i,w_{i}^{T}x=1\}.

However, Terry and Wolf
[
13
]
(see also Conant
[
4
]
) showed that such an improvement of the arithmetic regularity lemma can be obtained for a class of sets known as ‘stable’ sets
[
13
, Definition 2]
. In fact, they proved that not only can one find a subspace
H
such that a stable set
A
is Fourier-uniform on all of its cosets, but in fact
A
has near-trivial density (that is, almost 0 or almost 1) on all cosets; moreover, one can ensure that the codimension of
H
is at most polynomial in
\epsilon^{-1}
– a drastic improvement on the tower-type bounds required for general sets. Alon, Fox and Zhao
[
1
]
and Sisask
[
11
]
subsequently generalised this to sets of bounded
\mathrm{VC}
-dimension, which is defined as follows in the context of abelian groups.

Definition 1.2

(
\mathrm{VC}
-dimension of a subset)
.

Given
k\geq 1
, a finite abelian group
G
and a subset
A\subseteq G
,
A
is said to have
\mathrm{VC}
-dimension at least
k
if there exist elements
\{x_{i}:i\in[k]\}\cup\{y_{S}:S\subseteq[k]\}
in
G
such that
x_{i}+y_{S}\in A
if and only if
i\in S
. The
\mathrm{VC}
-dimension of
A
is the largest
k
such that
A
has
\mathrm{VC}
-dimension at least
k
.

The arithmetic regularity lemma for sets of bounded
\mathrm{VC}
-dimension likewise achieves polynomial bounds on the codimension of
H
as well as the near-trivial density of
A
on most of the cosets, but does not guarantee it for all cosets. In this way, it can be thought of as a halfway point between the stable regularity lemma of Terry and Wolf
[
13
]
, and the arithmetic regularity lemma of Green
[
7
]
.

Theorem 1.3

(Arithmetic regularity lemma for sets of bounded
\mathrm{VC}
-dimension
[
1
,
11
]
)
.

Fix
k\geq 1
and
\epsilon>0
. Suppose that
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
has
\mathrm{VC}
-dimension of at most
k
. Then there is a subspace
H\leqslant\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
of index at most
\epsilon^{-k-o(1)}
such that for all but an
\epsilon
-proportion of
c\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
, either
|S\cap(H+c)|\geq(1-\epsilon)|H|
or
|S\cap(H+c)|\leq\epsilon|H|
.

Indeed, exceptional cosets in Theorem
1.3
are unavoidable since the linear Green-Sanders example has bounded
\mathrm{VC}
-dimension: specifically, Terry and Wolf showed that it has
\mathrm{VC}
-dimension at most
3

[
14
, Corollary A.10]
. Their argument for
p>3
was rather technically involved, and Section
2
of this paper presents a simpler proof, as well as establishing the following stronger theorem for
p\geq 5
.

Theorem 1.4
.

For all primes
p\geq 5
and all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the
\mathrm{VC}
-dimension of
\mathrm{GS}(p,n)
is 2.

In
[
15
]
and
[
16
]
, Terry and Wolf undertook the study of strengthenings of the arithmetic regularity lemma in the quadratic setting. Here the place of stable sets is taken by so-called ‘
\mathrm{NFOP}_{2}
’ sets
[
15
, Definition 1.14]
and
\mathrm{VC}
-dimension is replaced by
\mathrm{VC}_{2}
-dimension, a generalisation of
\mathrm{VC}
-dimension introduced by Shelah in
[
10
]
and defined by Terry and Wolf
[
15
, Definition 1.11]
in the context of finite abelian groups as follows.

Definition 1.5

(
\mathrm{VC}_{2}
-dimension of a subset)
.

Given
k\geq 1
, a finite abelian group
G
and a subset
A\subseteq G
,
A
is said to have
\mathrm{VC}_{2}
-dimension at least
k
if there exist elements

\{x_{i}:i\in[k]\}\cup\{y_{i}:i\in[k]\}\cup\{z_{S}:S\subseteq[k]^{2}\}\subseteq G

such that
x_{i}+y_{j}+z_{S}\in A
if and only if
(i,j)\in S
. The
\mathrm{VC}_{2}
-dimension of
A
is the largest
k
such that
A
has
\mathrm{VC}_{2}
-dimension at least
k
.

Terry and Wolf established arithmetic regularity lemmas for sets of bounded
\mathrm{VC}_{2}
-dimension
[
15
, Theorem 1.12]
and for
\mathrm{NFOP}_{2}
sets
[
15
, Theorem 1.19]
. They also showed that a quadratic generalisation of
\mathrm{GS}(p,n)
does not satisfy the conclusions of the latter
[
15
, Theorem 1.21]
.

Definition 1.6

(Quadratic Green-Sanders example)
.

Let
M_{1},\ldots,M_{n}
be a basis for the space of symmetric
n\times n
matrices of rank
n
over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
, and let
Q_{i}
denote the quadratic polynomial
Q_{i}(x)=x^{T}M_{i}x
. The
quadratic Green-Sanders example
in
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
is defined as

\mathrm{QGS}(p,n)=\{x\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}:\text{ }\exists i\text{ }Q_{1}(x)=\ldots=Q_{i-1}(x)=0,Q_{i}(x)=1\}.

As observed in
[
15
]
, a suitable basis for the space of symmetric
n\times n
matrices of rank
n
exists for odd
n
by
[
5
, Lemma 3]
.

Terry and Wolf further conjectured
[
14
, Conjecture 5.24]
that the quadratic Green-Sanders example has bounded
\mathrm{VC}_{2}
-dimension. If true, it would show that the arithmetic regularity lemma for sets of bounded
\mathrm{VC}_{2}
-dimension must be strictly weaker than that for
\mathrm{NFOP}_{2}
sets. This paper confirms that this is indeed the case.

Theorem 1.7
.

For all primes
p>2
and all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the
\mathrm{VC}_{2}
-dimension of
\mathrm{QGS}(p,n)
is at most
\text{br}(5;3,3)
.

Here,
br(r;s,t)
is the
bipartite
r
-colour Ramsey number
, which is the least integer
N
such that for any
r
-colouring of the edges of the complete bipartite graph
K_{N,N}
, there is a monochromatic copy of
K_{s,t}
. A result of Conlon
[
2
]
implies the bound
br(r;3,3)\leq 4r^{3}+1
(see Appendix
A
), which in particular means that the
\mathrm{VC}_{2}
-dimension of
\mathrm{QGS}(p,n)
is at most
501
.

Finally, Section
4
exhibits a non-trivial lower bound on the
\mathrm{VC}_{2}
-dimension of the quadratic Green-Sanders example.

Theorem 1.8
.

For all primes
p>2
and all
n\geq 31
, the
\mathrm{VC}_{2}
-dimension of
\mathrm{QGS}(p,n)
is at least 3.

It seems likely that the lower bound is closer to the truth, but the precise value of the
\mathrm{VC}_{2}
-dimension of
\mathrm{QGS}(p,n)
remains to be determined.

2.
The
\mathrm{VC}
-dimension of the linear Green-Sanders example

The concept of
\mathrm{VC}
-dimension may be more familiar to the reader in the following form, where
\mathcal{P}(V)
denotes the power set of a given set
V
.

Definition 2.1

(
\mathrm{VC}
-dimension of a family of sets)
.

Let
V
be a set, and let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(V)
. A set
S\subseteq V
is said to be
shattered by
\mathcal{F}
if
\{S\cap F:\text{ }F\in\mathcal{F}\}=\mathcal{P}(S).
The
\mathrm{VC}
-dimension of
\mathcal{F}
is the largest integer
k
(if it exists) such that
\mathcal{F}
shatters some set of size
k
.

It is easy to verify that this definition coincides with Definition
1.2
upon taking
V
to be the finite abelian group
G
, and
\mathcal{F}=\{A-x:x\in G\}
to be the family of translates of the set
A
. It will be convenient to bear this correspondence in mind as we proceed. In a slight abuse of language,
A
will be said to
shatter
a set
S
when the family of its translates does.

It is not difficult to see that the set
\{(0,0,0),(0,1,-1),(0,-1,1)\}
is shattered by translates of
\mathrm{GS}(3,n)

[
15
]
, and thus the
\mathrm{VC}
-dimension of
\mathrm{GS}(3,n)
is at least 3. This section gives a short proof showing that the
\mathrm{VC}
-dimension of
\mathrm{GS}(p,n)
is at most 3 for any
p\geq 3
, meaning that the
\mathrm{VC}
-dimension of
\mathrm{GS}(3,n)
is equal to 3.

Theorem 2.2
.

For all primes
p>2
and all
n
,
\mathrm{GS}(p,n)
has
\mathrm{VC}
-dimension at most 3.

There are two key observations that make the proof possible. Firstly, if a set
S
is shattered by translates of
A
, then so is any translate of
S
. In particular, we may assume without loss of generality that
0\in S
. The second observation is that any set of size 4 must have at least two non-zero elements that both lie in
A
or both lie in
A^{C}
, by the pigeonhole principle. In Section
3
, the pigeonhole principle will be replaced by a more sophisticated version of Ramsey’s Theorem.

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

Let
\langle x,y\rangle
denote the dot product
x^{T}y
, and let
\mathrm{fnz}:\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}\rightarrow[n+1]
be the function indicating the position of the first non-zero coordinate of a vector, if it exists, defined by

\mathrm{fnz}(x)=\begin{cases}\min\{k:\text{ }\langle x,w_{k}\rangle\neq 0\}&\text{ if }x\neq\underline{0};\\
n+1&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Then
A
can be rewritten as

A=\{x\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}:\text{ }\mathrm{fnz}(x)<n+1\text{ and }\langle x,w_{\mathrm{fnz}(x)}\rangle=1\}.

Suppose there is a set
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
of size at least 4 such that
S
is shattered by translates of
A=\mathrm{GS}(p,n)
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
0\in S
. Moreover, since
|S|\geq 4
, there must be two non-zero elements
a_{1},a_{2}\in S
such that either
a_{1},a_{2}\in A
or
a_{1},a_{2}\in A^{C}
.

Case 1:
a_{1},a_{2}\in A
.
Since
A
shatters
S
, there exists some
y\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
such that
y=0+y\in A
and
a_{1}+y,a_{2}+y\notin A
.

Fix
j\in\{1,2\}
and suppose that
\mathrm{fnz}(a_{j})<\mathrm{fnz}(y)
. Then for all
i\leq\mathrm{fnz}(a_{j})
,

\langle a_{j}+y,w_{i}\rangle=\langle a_{j},w_{i}\rangle+\langle y,w_{i}\rangle=\langle a_{j},w_{i}\rangle\in\{0,1\},

since
a_{j}\in A
. However, this implies that
a_{j}+y\in A
, contradicting the choice of
y
. Similarly,
\mathrm{fnz}(y)<\mathrm{fnz}(a_{j})
is impossible, for then
\langle a_{j}+y,w_{i}\rangle=\langle a_{j},w_{i}\rangle+\langle y,w_{i}\rangle=\langle y,w_{i}\rangle
for all
i\leq\mathrm{fnz}(y)
, and thus
a_{j}+y\in A
as before. Therefore
\mathrm{fnz}(y)=\mathrm{fnz}(a_{j})
for any
j\in\{1,2\}
, so in particular,
\mathrm{fnz}(a_{1})=\mathrm{fnz}(a_{2})
.

There must also exist a different
y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
such that
y^{\prime},a_{1}+y^{\prime}\in A
and
a_{2}+y^{\prime}\notin A
. Arguing as in the preceding paragraph, it must be the case that
\mathrm{fnz}(y^{\prime})=\mathrm{fnz}(a_{2})
, which implies
\mathrm{fnz}(a_{1})=\mathrm{fnz}(y^{\prime})
. But then

\langle a_{1}+y^{\prime},w_{\mathrm{fnz}(a_{1})}\rangle=\langle a_{1},w_{\mathrm{fnz}(a_{1})}\rangle+\langle y^{\prime},w_{\mathrm{fnz}(a_{1})}\rangle=2\notin\{0,1\}.

Since
\mathrm{fnz}(a_{1})=\mathrm{fnz}(y^{\prime})
implies
\mathrm{fnz}(a_{1})\leq\mathrm{fnz}(a_{1}+y^{\prime})
, the above equation shows that
a_{1}+y^{\prime}\notin A
, which is again a contradiction.

Case 2:
a_{1},a_{2}\notin A
.
Since
A
shatters
S
, there exists some
y\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
such that
y\notin A
and
a_{1}+y,a_{2}+y\in A
.

Fix
j\in\{1,2\}
. If
\mathrm{fnz}(a_{j})>\mathrm{fnz}(y)
, then
\langle a_{j}+y,w_{\mathrm{fnz}(a_{j}+y)}\rangle=\langle y,w_{\mathrm{fnz}(y)}\rangle\notin\{0,1\}
, contradicting the choice of
y
. Similarly, we cannot have
\mathrm{fnz}(y)>\mathrm{fnz}(a_{j})
, as that would imply
\langle a_{j}+y,w_{\mathrm{fnz}(a_{j}+y)}\rangle=\langle a_{j},w_{\mathrm{fnz}(a_{j})}\rangle=1
. Therefore,
\mathrm{fnz}(y)=\mathrm{fnz}(a_{j})
for any
j\in\{1,2\}
, so in particular,
\mathrm{fnz}(a_{1})=\mathrm{fnz}(a_{2})
.

There must further exist a
y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
such that
y^{\prime},a_{1}+y^{\prime}\in A
and
a_{2}+y^{\prime}\notin A
. Since
\mathrm{fnz}(a_{2})>\mathrm{fnz}(y^{\prime})
would imply
\langle a_{2}+y^{\prime},w_{\mathrm{fnz}(a_{2}+y^{\prime})}\rangle=\langle y^{\prime},w_{\mathrm{fnz}(y^{\prime})}\rangle=1
, it must be the case that
\mathrm{fnz}(y^{\prime})\geq\mathrm{fnz}(a_{2})=\mathrm{fnz}(a_{1})
. Similarly,
\mathrm{fnz}(y^{\prime})>\mathrm{fnz}(a_{1})
would mean that
\langle a_{1}+y^{\prime},w_{\mathrm{fnz}(a_{1}+y^{\prime})}\rangle=\langle a_{1},w_{\mathrm{fnz}(a_{1})}\rangle\notin\{0,1\}
, so we must have
\mathrm{fnz}(y^{\prime})=\mathrm{fnz}(a_{1})
, and therefore
\mathrm{fnz}(a_{1}+y^{\prime})\geq\mathrm{fnz}(a_{1})
.

However,
\mathrm{fnz}(a_{1}+y^{\prime})=\mathrm{fnz}(a_{1})
would imply

1=\langle a_{1}+y^{\prime},w_{\mathrm{fnz}(a_{1}+y^{\prime})}\rangle=\langle a_{1},w_{\mathrm{fnz}(a_{1})}\rangle+1\neq 1,

which forces us to conclude that
\mathrm{fnz}(a_{1}+y^{\prime})>\mathrm{fnz}(a_{1})
. As a consequence,
\langle a_{1},w_{\mathrm{fnz}(a_{1})}\rangle=-\langle y^{\prime},w_{\mathrm{fnz}(y^{\prime})}\rangle=-1.
By a symmetric argument, choosing
y^{\prime}
to be so that
y^{\prime},a_{2}+y^{\prime}\in A
and
a_{1}+y^{\prime}\notin A
, we must have
\langle a_{2},w_{\mathrm{fnz}(a_{2})}\rangle=-1
also.

Finally, there exists
y^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
such that
y^{\prime\prime},a_{1}+y^{\prime\prime}\notin A
and
a_{2}+y^{\prime\prime}\in A
. It is once again the case that
\mathrm{fnz}(a_{2})=\mathrm{fnz}(y^{\prime\prime})=\mathrm{fnz}(a_{1})
and therefore

\langle a_{1}+y^{\prime\prime},w_{\mathrm{fnz}(y^{\prime\prime})}\rangle=\langle a_{2}+y^{\prime\prime},w_{\mathrm{fnz}(y^{\prime\prime})}\rangle=-1+\langle y^{\prime\prime},w_{\mathrm{fnz}(y^{\prime\prime})}\rangle\neq 0.

However, this is impossible, as
\mathrm{fnz}(a_{1}+y^{\prime\prime}),\mathrm{fnz}(a_{2}+y^{\prime\prime})\geq\mathrm{fnz}(y^{\prime\prime})
and the first non-zero coordinates of
a_{1}+y^{\prime\prime}
and
a_{2}+y^{\prime\prime}
must be different.

As both cases lead to a contradiction,
S
cannot have size greater than 3.
∎

When
p\geq 5
, it turns out that a contradiction can be forced even when
a_{1}\in A,a_{2}\notin A
which rules out the possibility of
|S|\geq 3
. This results in Theorem
1.4
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Let
p\geq 5
, and suppose that
S
is a set of size 3 shattered by
A
. Without loss of generality, it may be assumed that
S=\{0,a_{1},a_{2}\}
for some non-zero
a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
. Moreover, we must have
a_{1}\in A,a_{2}\notin A
as a consequence of the proof above.

Since
A
shatters
S
, there exists some
y\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
such that
y,a_{1}+y\notin A
and
a_{2}+y\in A
. Each of
\mathrm{fnz}(a_{2})<\mathrm{fnz}(y)
and
\mathrm{fnz}(y)<\mathrm{fnz}(a_{2})
would imply
\langle a_{2}+y,w_{\mathrm{fnz}(a_{2}+y)}\rangle\neq 1
, so we must have
\mathrm{fnz}(a_{2})=\mathrm{fnz}(y)
. On the other hand, if
\mathrm{fnz}(a_{1})<\mathrm{fnz}(y)
, then

\langle a_{1}+y,w_{\mathrm{fnz}(a_{1}+y)}\rangle=\langle a_{1},w_{\mathrm{fnz}(a_{1})}\rangle=1,

contradicting the choice of
y
, so
\mathrm{fnz}(a_{1})\geq\mathrm{fnz}(y)=\mathrm{fnz}(a_{2})
.

There also exists some
y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
such that
y^{\prime},a_{2}+y^{\prime}\in A
and
a_{1}+y^{\prime}\notin A
. Similarly to the previous paragraph, this yields the conclusion
\mathrm{fnz}(a_{2})\geq\mathrm{fnz}(y^{\prime})=\mathrm{fnz}(a_{1})
. Therefore,
\mathrm{fnz}(a_{1})=\mathrm{fnz}(a_{2})
and, in particular, we must have

\langle a_{2},w_{\mathrm{fnz}(a_{2})}\rangle+\langle y^{\prime},w_{\mathrm{fnz}(y^{\prime})}\rangle=\langle a_{2}+y^{\prime},w_{\mathrm{fnz}(y^{\prime})}\rangle\in\{0,1\},

which implies
\langle a_{2},w_{\mathrm{fnz}(a_{2})}\rangle=-1
.

Finally, there exists a
y^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
such that
y^{\prime\prime}\notin A
and
a_{1}+y^{\prime\prime},a_{2}+y^{\prime\prime}\in A
. As in the case of
y
, this leads to
\mathrm{fnz}(a_{2})=\mathrm{fnz}(y^{\prime\prime})
, which in turn means that
\mathrm{fnz}(a_{1})=\mathrm{fnz}(y^{\prime\prime})
. Then
\mathrm{fnz}(a_{j})\leq\mathrm{fnz}(a_{j}+y^{\prime\prime})
for each
j\in\{1,2\}
, and hence

\langle a_{j},w_{\mathrm{fnz}(a_{j})}\rangle+\langle y^{\prime\prime},w_{\mathrm{fnz}(y^{\prime\prime})}\rangle=\langle a_{j}+y^{\prime\prime},w_{\mathrm{fnz}(a_{j})}\rangle\in\{0,1\}.

However, this only holds if
\langle y^{\prime\prime},w_{\mathrm{fnz}(y^{\prime\prime})}\rangle=-1
and
\langle a_{2},w_{\mathrm{fnz}(a_{2})}\rangle=2
, a contradiction.

To complete the proof, observe that
A
does indeed shatter a set of size 2: for example,
S=\{0,w_{2}\}
can be shattered by taking
y
from the set
\{0,w_{1},-w_{1},w_{2}\}
.
∎

3.
The quadratic Green-Sanders example has bounded
\mathrm{VC}_{2}
-dimension

For convenience, the definition of
\mathrm{VC}_{2}
-dimension is restated below in terms of a function that assigns to each
(i,j)\in[0,k]^{2}
a value from
\{A,A^{C}\}
. Here
[0,k]
denotes
\{0,1,2,\dots,k\}
, as opposed to the standard
[k]=\{1,2,\dots,k\}
.

Definition 3.1

(Containment map)
.

For a set
A
and an integer
k\geq 1
, a function
\phi:[0,k]^{2}\rightarrow\{A,A^{C}\}
is called a
containment map on
[0,k]^{2}
. If
\phi
is defined only on a subset of
[0,k]^{2}
, then
\phi
is a
partial containment map on
[0,k]^{2}
.

Definition 3.2

(
\mathrm{VC}_{2}
-dimension, containment map form)
.

Given an integer
k\geq 1
, a finite abelian group
G
and a subset
A\subseteq G
,
A
is said to have
\mathrm{VC}_{2}
-dimension at least
k
if there exist sets
X=\{x_{0},\ldots,x_{k-1}\}
and
Y=\{y_{0},\ldots,y_{k-1}\}
in
G
such that the following holds.
For any containment map
\phi
on
[0,k-1]^{2}
, there exists
z\in G
such that
x_{i}+y_{j}+z\in\phi(i,j)
. If this is the case, we say that
z
realises
\phi
.

The tuple
(X,Y)
is said to be
quadratically shattered by
A
if every containment map on
[0,k-1]^{2}
can be realised.

As in the linear case, it may be assumed without loss of generality that
x_{0}=y_{0}=0
: if
(X,Y)
is quadratically shattered by
A

Throughout this section and the rest of the paper, let

(1)

Q_{t}(x+y+z)=Q_{t}(x+z)+Q_{t}(y+z)-Q_{t}(z)+2x^{T}M_{t}y.

In the previous section, the proof of Theorem

Example 3.3

Let

Proposition 3.4

(ii)

4.
A lower bound on the
\mathrm{VC}_{2}
-dimension of the quadratic Green-Sanders example

Considering that the
\mathrm{VC}
-dimension of
\mathrm{GS}(p,n)
is at most
3
, it is tempting to conjecture that the true
\mathrm{VC}_{2}
-dimension of
\mathrm{QGS}(p,n)
is much smaller than
br(5;3,3)
. This section gives a construction which shows that the
\mathrm{VC}_{2}
-dimension of
\mathrm{QGS}(p,n)
is at least
3
for any
p>2
. The goal is to exhibit a pair of sets
X,Y
of size
|X|=|Y|=3
such that
A=\mathrm{QGS}(p,n)
quadratically shatters
(X,Y)
.

Given a sequence
i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\in[n]
, let
Q_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}}
denote the joint function
(Q_{i_{1}},\ldots,Q_{i_{k}})
taking values in
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{k}
. Note that if
x\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
and
Q_{1,\ldots,k}(x)
is non-zero for some
k<n
, then
Q_{1,\ldots,k}(x)
completely determines whether
x\in\mathrm{QGS}(p,n)
. This simple observation suggests that it may be sufficient to consider only a small subset of
\{Q_{i}\}
in order to quadratically shatter a pair of sets.

Additionally, in order to have control over whether an element of
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
is in
A
, it is useful to consider
quadratic factors
, the type of partition produced by the quadratic arithmetic regularity lemma.

Definition 4.1

(Quadratic Factor)
.

A
quadratic factor
\mathcal{B}
of complexity
D
is a partition of
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
into the level sets of some polynomials
P_{1},\ldots,P_{D}:\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{p}
of degree at most
2
. In other words, the parts of
\mathcal{B}
have the form
B=\{x\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}:\text{ }(P_{1}(x),\ldots,P_{D}(x))=\underline{c}\}
, where
\underline{c}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{D}
. Such a part is called an
atom of
\mathcal{B}
and
\underline{c}
is referred to as its
label
.

By definition, an atom
B
with label
\underline{c}
is non-empty if and only if there is an element
x\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
that simultaneously satisfies
P_{1}(x)=c_{1},\ldots,P_{D}(x)=c_{D}
. It turns out that when the quadratic polynomials inducing
\mathcal{B}
are defined by matrices of sufficiently high rank, we can ensure that all atoms are non-empty via the following lemma
[
8
, Lemma 4.2]
.

Lemma 4.2

(Size of atoms)
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a quadratic factor defined by polynomials
L_{1},\ldots,L_{l},P_{1},\ldots,P_{q}
, where
\{L_{i}\}
are linearly independent linear polynomials, and
\{P_{i}\}
are quadratic polynomials of the form
x^{T}M_{i}x
for some matrix
M_{i}
. Then for any
\underline{c}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{D}
, the atom
B
with label
\underline{c}
satisfies
\big||B|-p^{-(l+q)}|G|\big|\leq p^{-r/2}|G|
, where

r=\min({\mathrm{rank}(\lambda_{1}M_{1}+\ldots+\lambda_{q}M_{q}):\text{ }\underline{\lambda}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{q}\backslash\{0\}}).

In particular, if the quadratic polynomials in Lemma
4.2
are taken to be a subset of
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{n}
, then
r=n
so all atoms of
\mathcal{B}
are non-empty as long as the complexity of
\mathcal{B}
is less than
n/2
.

While the goal of this section is to prove a lower bound of
3
, the general approach will be demonstrated by first obtaining a weaker bound of
2
. The main steps of the proof below are labelled and highlighted in bold to serve as a blueprint for the proof of the eventual stronger result.

Theorem 4.3

For all primes

so that all cross-terms have the same value (taken to be zero throughout this section), then the proof of Theorem
1.7
implies that
(X,Y)
is
not
quadratically shattered by
A
. It may still be possible to construct suitable
X
and
Y
using the method outlined here, but the cross-terms
\mu_{i,j}^{(t)}
would have to have different values. On the other hand, the
\mathrm{VC}_{2}
-dimension of
\mathrm{QGS}(p,n)
may be exactly
3
. Whether or not this is the case remains an interesting open question.

Appendix A
Multicolour bipartite Ramsey numbers

In
[
2
]
, Conlon proved that
br(2;t,t)\leq(1+o(1))2^{t+1}\log(t)
. The same proof shows that for any
r\geq 2
,
br(r;t,t)\leq(1+o(1))r^{t+1}\log(t)
(the only modification needed is to replace the subgraph density of
\frac{1}{2}
by
\frac{1}{r}
throughout the argument). The proof relies on a lemma which, given an edge density
\rho
, guarantees a copy of
K_{q,s}
in any large enough bipartite graph of density
\rho
. The version stated here is easily extracted from the proof of
[
2
, Lemma 1]
before an asymptotic bound is applied.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
G
be a subgraph of
K_{m,n}
with edge density
\rho
, where
0<\rho\leq 1
. Then, provided that
m>(q-1)\rho^{-1}
and

n>\frac{\binom{m}{q}}{\binom{\rho m}{q}}(s-1),

G
must contain a copy of
K_{q,s}
.

The bound on
m
is necessary to ensure that the function
F(x)=\binom{x}{q}
is convex so as to allow an application of Jensen’s inequality in the proof. We now verify that in the case
q=s=3
, Lemma
A.1
guarantees the following.

Proposition A.2
.

For any
r\geq 2
,
\text{br}(r;3,3)\leq 4r^{3}+1
.

Proof.

Fix an
r
-colouring of
K_{n,n}
, where
n=4r^{3}+1
. Denote the two vertex classes of
K_{n,n}
by
X
and
Y
, respectively. For each vertex
v\in X
, there is a colour that appears on at least
4{r}^{2}+1
edges incident with
v
. Call such a colour ‘dominant’. By averaging, there is a colour
c\in[r]
which is dominant for
m\geq 4r^{2}+1
vertices in
X
. Let
X^{\prime}
denote the set of these vertices, and let
H
be the graph on
X^{\prime}\cup Y
consisting only of the edges coloured
c
. Note that
H
is a subgraph of
K_{m,n}
.

The average degree of the vertices of
Y
in
H
is

\frac{(4r^{2}+1)^{2}}{4r^{3}+1}=\frac{16r^{4}+8r^{2}+1}{4r^{3}+1}=4r+\frac{8r^{2}-4r+1}{4r^{3}+1}.

In particular, there must be a vertex
y\in Y
of degree at least
q=4r+1
and, therefore, there is a copy of
K_{q,1}
in
H
.

Let
X^{\prime\prime}\subseteq X^{\prime}
denote the
q
vertices of this instance of
K_{q,1}
, and
Y^{\prime}=Y\backslash\{y\}
. Let
H^{\prime}
be the induced subgraph of
H
on
(X^{\prime\prime},Y^{\prime})
. The total number of edges in
H^{\prime}
is at least
4r^{2}q
, which means its edge density
\rho^{\prime}
in
K_{q,n-1}
satisfies
\rho^{\prime}\geq{4r^{2}}/(n-1)=1/r.

By Lemma
A.1
,
H
has a copy of
K_{3,2}
so long as
q>2\rho^{\prime-1}
(which indeed holds since
q>4r
) and additionally

n-1>\frac{\binom{q}{3}}{\binom{\rho^{\prime}q}{3}}

To see that the latter holds, observe that
\rho^{\prime}q>4
, and therefore

\displaystyle\frac{\binom{q}{3}}{\binom{\rho^{\prime}q}{3}}

\displaystyle<\frac{q(q-1)(q-2)}{4\times 3\times 2}=\frac{4r(16r^{2}-1)}{24}<4r^{3}=n-1.

Hence,
H^{\prime}
contains a copy of
K_{3,2}
.

On the other hand, all vertices of
X^{\prime\prime}
were part of a copy of
K_{q,1}
of the same colour, so adding
y
to
K_{3,2}
gives a monochromatic
K_{3,3}
, as required.
