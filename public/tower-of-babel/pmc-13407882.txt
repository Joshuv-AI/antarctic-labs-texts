Relief of GbDEAH12G repression on the GbNAC072C-GbHOX3 axis improves lint percentage in Gossypium barbadense
Authors: Bin Li, Nan Zhao, Kaiyun Jiang, Jie Kong, Meng Wang, Baoliang Wang, Weiran Wang, Suen Liu, Huijing Li, Zhuanxia Pan, Jiahui Zhu, Junyi Geng, Beibei Lv, Zhanghao Xia, Alifu Aierxi, Jingrou Zhang, Baosheng Guo, Pengbo Li, Daojun Yuan, Anhui Guo, Jinping Hua
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Improving lint percentage (LP) is a key breeding target for Sea Island cotton (Gossypium barbadense), yet its genetic basis remains poorly understood. Here, we identify GbNAC072C as a positive regulator of LP that promotes fiber cell protrusions by directly activating the transcription of GbHOX3. We find that GbNAC072C is inhibited by GbDEAH12G, and crucially, the strength of this interaction is haplotype-dependent. The GbDEAH12G protein binds GbNAC072C with higher affinity than the GbDEAH12C variant, leading to stronger suppression of GbHOX3 activation and consequently lower LP. This explains why the GbDEAH12C-GbNAC072C allele combination gives improved LP. Our work elucidates how haplotype-specific protein interactions modulate a key transcriptional cascade and proposes a disinhibition strategy for breaking yield limits in Sea Island cotton breeding.

Introduction

Cotton (Gossypium spp.) is the most important natural fibre crop in the world. Sea Island cotton (G. barbadense) is renowned for its superior fibre quality, making it a crucial raw material for premium cotton textiles1. However, its low yield greatly limits its production and applications. It is therefore vital to increase the yield of Sea Island cotton in order to develop high-yield potential cotton varieties. Cotton yield is influenced by several components such as boll number (BN), single boll weight (SBW), lint percentage (LP), seed index (SI) and lint index (LI)2. LP, defined as the percentage of lint weight to seed cotton weight, is a critical yield component in cotton. While G. barbadense is renowned for its superior fibre quality, it generally suffers from lower yield compared to G. hirsutum. This yield limitation is partly attributable to its typically lower LP. Therefore, improving LP is particularly crucial for G. barbadense cultivation, as it represents a key pathway to enhance its yield potential without compromising its elite fibre qualities. Enhancing LP has become a major breeding objective in Sea Island cotton improvement programmes, aiming to bridge the yield gap while preserving its high-value fibre properties. Additionally, LP is a genetically stable intrinsic trait of cotton that is closely related to fibre quantity, length, diameter and seed weight of the fibre-bearing seed, thus making it a key economic indicator of cotton varieties3,4. Numerous studies have demonstrated a significant positive correlation between LP and lint yield, highlighting its importance in improving cotton yield potential5,6. Therefore, identifying the functional genes underlying LP is essential to improve the yield of G. barbadense.

Fibre yield is closely related to the process of fibre development. Cotton fibres originate from the differentiation of specific protodermal cells of the ovule and develop through four continuous yet overlapping stages: initiation, elongation, secondary cell wall thickening (SCW) and maturation7,8. Fibre initiation plays a decisive role in yield formation. Cotton fibres are classified into two types: lint and fuzz9. Fibre cells (25–30%) initiated during the early developmental stage (−1 to 3 DPA) develop into lint fibres, while those initiated between 4 and 10 DPA differentiate into fuzz fibres10,11. The initiation of cotton fibres is a rapid process involving sequential differentiation, diffuse growth and tip-biased diffuse growth: at −1.5 DPA, fibre cell differentiation has not yet been observed; by −1 DPA, fibre cell differentiation begins at the chalazal end of the ovule, but no apparent protrusions have formed; by −0.5 DPA, fibre cells exhibit diffuse growth and differentiation; by 0 DPA, tip-biased diffuse growth, diffuse growth, and differentiation occur simultaneously10,12. Fibre yield depends on the number of epidermal cells that have the potential to differentiate into fibre cells during the fibre initiation stage13, and thus increasing the proportion of epidermal cells that differentiate into fibres holds significant potential for enhancing fibre yield.

Transcription factors (TFs) play crucial roles in fibre initiation. HOX3 was highly expressed in the 0 DPA fibres, and its loss of function prevented the transition to tip-biased diffuse growth10. GhHOX3 and GhHOX4 positively regulated cotton fibre elongation14–16. Moreover, a lot of other TFs have been identified to positively regulate fibre initiation, for example, MYB (i.e. GhMYB10917, GhMYB2518, GhWER19, GhLPF120, GhMML321 and GhMYB25-like11,22,23), HD-ZIP (i.e. GhHD-124, GhHDZ7625 and GbPDF126), TCP (i.e. GbTCP27 and GhTCP1428,29) and WRKY (i.e. GhWRKY1630). However, few studies have addressed the role of NAC TFs in fibre initiation, with the exception of one NAC gene, GhFSN1, which acted as a positive regulator of SCW biosynthesis and fibre modification31.

The availability of Sea Island cotton genomes is crucial to unveil the genetic mechanisms of key agronomic traits32–36, and lays the foundation for cotton genomics-to-breeding (G2B)37. Based on resequencing of Sea Island cotton genomes, numerous loci/genes related to yield were successfully identified by genome-wide association studies (GWAS)38–40, offering valuable gene resources for cotton improvement. Research on cotton LP has primarily focused on the identification of LP-related genes through high-density genetic mapping41–43 or GWAS44,45, while the functional dissection and regulatory mechanisms of LP-related genes remain limited.

In this study, we identify an LP-associated NAC TF, GbNAC072C, that can enhance LP by promoting fibre cell protrusion during fibre initiation. Further analysis reveals that GbNAC072C activates the transcription of a key fibre initiation factor, GbHOX3, thereby increasing LP. Additionally, GbNAC072C and GbDEAH12G exhibit an antagonistic effect on LP regulation. Overexpression of GbDEAH12G inhibits fibre cell protrusion and reduces LP. Furthermore, GbDEAH12G inhibits the transcriptional activation of GbHOX3 by GbNAC072C, whereas GbDEAH12C does not interfere with this activation. Simultaneous overexpression of GbNAC072C and knocking out of GbDEAH12G promotes fibre cell protrusions and accelerates fibre growth. We further show that the combination of GbDEAH12C and GbNAC072C alleles leads to improved LP. These findings highlight the critical role of allele-specificity in regulation of LP and provide genetic resources for enhancing Sea Island cotton yield.

Results

Identification of the LP-associated gene GbNAC072 in G. barbadense

To identify the genes controlling LP of G. barbadense, a genome-wide association study was performed using multi-year, multi-point LP phenotypic data from 336 G. barbadense accessions in our previous study38. A single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) signal at position 5,162,009 on chromosome A04 exceeded the genome-wide significance threshold (−log₁₀P = 6.05) and was significantly associated with LP (Fig. 1a). Within the linkage disequilibrium decay region of this association signal, a non-synonymous SNP site (5,461,049, T/C) was found in the third exon of gene Gbar_A04G002550 that encodes a NAC transcription factor named GbNAC072 (Fig. 1b). This T-to-C mutation resulted in an amino acid substitution from leucine to serine (Fig. 1c) and a significant alteration in the predicted protein structure (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 1a). Analysis of transcriptome data from G. barbadense cv. Hai7124 revealed that GbNAC072 expression was highest in ovules at 0–5 DPA, with markedly lower levels in vegetative tissues, later-stage ovules and developing fibres (Fig. 1f). Furthermore, comparison of transcriptome data from ovules (0–5 DPA) and fibres (10–25 DPA) of one HapC accession (GbYH9) and three HapT accessions (XH33, XH37 and XH58) showed that GbNAC072 expression was significantly higher in GbYH9 specifically in 0 DPA ovules (P < 0.05), but not in fibres, consistent with a positive regulatory role in LP during fibre initiation (Fig. 1g). Finer temporal expression profiling during fibre initiation (−2 DPA to 5 DPA) in GbYH9 (HapC) and GbC352 (HapT) accessions indicated that GbNAC072 expression peaked at 3 DPA in GbYH9 and 2 DPA in GbC352, suggesting a critical role during early fibre development (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Moreover, we observed that the GbNAC072 protein was localised in the nucleus (Fig. 1h), and displayed transcriptional activation activity (Fig. 1i, j).

Evolutionary analysis indicated that GbNAC072 was more closely related to homologous genes of At subgenomes in tetraploid cotton species (AD1, AD3-AD7) and A genomes (A1, A2), which are associated with higher lint yield, than with the D5 genome46,47 (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Haplotype distribution analysis revealed that the low-LP HapT allele was present in specific G. barbadense (3-79 and Hai7124), G. tomentosum, and G. darwinii (AD5-32), whereas the high-LP HapC allele was predominant in G. barbadense (Pima90), all examined G. hirsutum accessions (TM-1, ZM24, NDM8, GhJin668, PSC355, TX1000 and B713), and other AD-genome species, as well as in the A-genome and D-genome diploid progenitors. The results suggested that the low-LP HapT of GbNAC072 emerged after polyploidization and subsequently differentiated within G. barbadense (Supplementary Fig. 2c).

GbNAC072C enhances LP by promoting fibre cell protrusion

To validate the function of GbNAC072, we first performed virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) in two G. barbadense cultivars, GbYH9 and GbC352. The VIGS system was confirmed to be effective using CHLI as a positive control, which produced the expected yellowing phenotype in leaves, floral buds and cotton bolls (Supplementary Fig. 3a–l). Silencing of GbNAC072 led to a significant reduction in LP by 10.10% in GbYH9 and 7.90% in GbC352 compared with wild type (Fig. 2a; Supplementary Fig. 3m). SBW decreased by 0.55 g and 1.92 g in the silenced lines, respectively (Fig. 2b). FL was reduced by 4.8 mm in GbYH9 and 11.2 mm in GbC352 (Fig. 2c, d). SI was significantly lower in the GbC352-silenced lines but unchanged in GbYH9 (Supplementary Fig. 3n), while seed length and width were unaffected in both backgrounds (Supplementary Fig. 3o–q). To explore the application potential of GbNAC072 in LP regulation of G. hirsutum, three independent knockout lines of the GhNAC072 homologues were generated in G. hirsutum cv. GhJin668 (Ghnac072-KO1, Ghnac072-KO2 and Ghnac072-KO3) (Supplementary Fig. 4a; Supplementary Fig. 5a). Considering the potential functional redundancy between At and Dt subgenomes in the tetraploid cotton, we aligned the protein sequences of the At and Dt homologues of GbNAC072 and found a high similarity of 95.32% (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Using sgRNAs targeting conserved regions, we simultaneously edited both homologues, introducing indels in the second exon of GhNAC072 that caused frameshift mutations and premature translation termination (Supplementary Fig. 5b; Supplementary Fig. 7a, b). In parallel, GbNAC072 overexpression G. hirsutum (cv. GhJin668) lines were also created, including two GbNAC072C-overexpressed (GbNAC072C-OE1 and GbNAC072C-OE2) and three GbNAC072T-overexpressed (GbNAC072T-OE1, GbNAC072T-OE2 and GbNAC072T-OE3) lines (Supplementary Fig. 4c, e; Supplementary Fig. 5c). At −1 DPA, no protrusions were observed on the ovule epidermal cells. At -0.5 DPA, protrusions began to form, but the protrusion number was significantly reduced in Ghnac072-KO lines compared to GhJin668, while it was significantly increased in GbNAC072C-OE and GbNAC072T-OE lines, with more protrusions in GbNAC072C-OE than in GbNAC072T-OE lines (Fig. 2e, f). A similar trend was observed in epidermal cells at 0 DPA (Fig. 2e, g). At 1–3 DPA, the growth rate of ovule epidermal cells in Ghnac072-KO lines slowed down compared with that in GhJin668, while that in GbNAC072C-OE lines significantly increased compared with GhJin668, but it was comparable in GbNAC072T-OE and GhJin668 (Fig. 2e). These results suggested that GbNAC072C could promote epidermal cell protrusion in cotton ovule. Additionally, LP in Ghnac072-KO lines decreased significantly compared with GhJin668, and increased significantly in GbNAC072C-OE lines but not in GbNAC072T-OE lines (Fig. 2h). These results suggested that GbNAC072C enhanced LP by promoting the protrusion of cotton ovule epidermal cells. Similarly, FL and SBW in Ghnac072-KO lines also decreased but increased significantly in GbNAC072C-OE lines, with no significant change in the GbNAC072T-OE lines (Fig. 2i, j; Supplementary Fig. 5d). Meanwhile, there were no significant differences in SI of Ghnac072-KO, GbNAC072C/T-OE and GhJin668 lines (Supplementary Fig. 5e). These results indicate that GbNAC072C promotes ovule epidermal cell protrusion and contributes to increased LP, fibre length and boll weight in upland cotton.

To establish breeding materials carrying the GbNAC072-edited locus, GhNAC072-edited G. hirsutum lines (GhJin668-Cas9) were crossed with two G. barbadense cultivars, GbYH9 and GbXin78, leveraging the high amino acid sequence similarity between GbNAC072 in G. barbadense and its homologue in G. hirsutum (99.41%; Supplementary Fig. 8a). Sequencing of the F1 progeny confirmed frameshift mutations at the GbNAC072 locus (Supplementary Fig. 9a; Supplementary Fig. 10a). Due to the interspecific cross, these progeny lines exhibit a heterozygous genetic background. SEM analysis of ovule epidermal cells from −1 to 5 DPA in GbYH9-derived progeny and 0–1 DPA in GbXin78-derived progeny revealed protrusion formation and growth dynamics (Supplementary Fig. 9b–d; Supplementary Fig. 9j–l), but these observations serve only as evidence that the mutated locus is present in breeding materials, rather than providing functional validation. Fibre-related traits, including LP, FL, SI and seed phenotype, were measured in these interspecific lines. Across the two genetic backgrounds, these progeny lines exhibited variation in LP, FL, SI and seed phenotype relative to the corresponding G. barbadense parental lines and GhJin668 knockout controls (Supplementary Fig. 9e–i; Supplementary Fig. 9m–q), demonstrating that these progeny can be used as breeding materials carrying the GbNAC072-edited locus, providing a foundation for the development of homozygous mutant lines in the future.

To test functional conservation, we overexpressed GbNAC072C and GbNAC072T in Arabidopsis (Supplementary Fig. 11a). Trichome number was significantly higher in both overexpression lines compared to WT, and greater in GbNAC072C-OE than in GbNAC072T-OE plants (Supplementary Fig. 11b, c), indicating that GbNAC072C also promotes trichome formation in a heterologous system.

GbNAC072 interacts with the LP negative regulator GbDEAH12

We previously identified an LP-related gene (Gbar_A05G014160) on chromosome A05, which encodes an ATP-dependent RNA helicase DEAH12 (GbDEAH12)38. A non-synonymous C/G mutation in the first exon of GbDEAH12 altered its three-dimensional protein structure (Supplementary Fig. 1b; Supplementary Fig. 12a). Transcript analysis showed that GbDEAH12 expression was higher in 1 DPA ovules of GbC352 (HapC) than in GbGiza81 (HapG), suggesting that GbDEAH12 may negatively regulate LP of G. barbadense (Supplementary Fig. 12b). Consistent with this, broader expression profiling across eight HapC materials (XH38, XH53, XH37, XH52, XH58, XH47, XH41 and XH33) and three HapG materials (LuoSaiNa, Mo1431 and Ashi) revealed that GbDEAH12 transcript levels were generally higher in HapC ovules (0–5 DPA) and early fibres (10 DPA) but declined during later stages (10–25 DPA), while HapG materials exhibited overall lower expression (Supplementary Fig. 12c), further supporting its potential negative role in fibre development. Additionally, we observed that GbDEAH12 was localised in the nucleus (Supplementary Fig. 12d).

To trace the evolutionary origin of the GbDEAH12 haplotypes (HapG/C) during cotton polyploidization, we compared the haplotypes of GbDEAH12 homologues in tetraploid cotton species and their diploid progenitors. The results revealed that HapG was present in tetraploids G. barbadense (Hai7124), G. hirsutum (TM-1, ZM24, NDM8, GhJin668, PSC355 and TX1000), and G. stephensii (AD701), whereas HapC existed in tetraploids G. barbadense (3-79 and Pima90), G. hirsutum (B713), G. tomentosum, G. mustelinum, G. darwinii, and G. ekmanianum (AD602), as well as in diploid progenitor species G. herbaceum (ZhongCao1), G. arboreum (SXY1), and G. raimondii (D502) (Supplementary Fig. 12e). This distribution suggested that the HapG allele (associated with high LP) may have arisen after polyploidization, with subsequent divergence in G. barbadense and G. hirsutum. Phylogenetically, GbDEAH12 grouped closely with A-subgenomes homologues from tetraploid AD species and diploid A-genome species (Supplementary Fig. 12f).

Structural modelling predicted that GbNAC072 and GbDEAH12 could interact via salt bridges and hydrogen bonds (Supplementary Fig. 12g). Subsequently, we validated their physical interaction by a series of assays, including yeast two-hybrid (Y2H), bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC), luciferase complementation imaging (LCI) assays, co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and pull-down (Fig. 3a–e). Given that both proteins exist in two haplotypes with distinct structural conformations, we further investigated whether these variations affect their binding affinity. Using quantitative β-galactosidase assays and LCI to systematically test all four allelic combinations, we consistently observed that the interaction between GbNAC072C and GbDEAH12G was the strongest, significantly exceeding that of the GbNAC072T-GbDEAH12G pair and all other combinations (Fig. 3f–h). By contrast, GbNAC072T showed only weak binding to both GbDEAH12G and GbDEAH12C. To unravel the role of GbDEAH12 in LP regulation of upland cotton, we generated knockout lines (Ghdeah12-KO1, Ghdeah12-KO2 and Ghdeah12-KO3) targeting the homologous genes of GbDEAH12 in the G. hirsutum cultivar GhJin668 using the CRISPR/Cas9 system (Supplementary Fig. 4b; Supplementary Fig. 5f). Considering the functional redundancy between At and Dt subgenomes in the tetraploid cotton, we aligned the protein sequences of the At and Dt homologues of GbDEAH12 and identified a high similarity of 94.68% (Supplementary Fig. 6b). We therefore designed sgRNAs targeting conserved regions to simultaneously edit both homologues. The resulting edited plants exhibited deletions in the first exon of GhDEAH12 that introduced frameshift mutations, leading to premature translation termination in both subgenomes (Supplementary Fig. 5g; Supplementary Fig. 7c, d). Additionally, we also created GbDEAH12G- and GbDEAH12C-overexpressed G. hirsutum (cv. GhJin668) lines (Supplementary Fig. 4d, f; Supplementary Fig. 5h). At −0.5 DPA, the number of protrusions was significantly higher in Ghdeah12-KO lines than in GhJin668, while reduced in GbDEAH12G/C-OE lines with lower in GbDEAH12G-OE than that in GbDEAH12C-OE (Fig. 4a, b). This pattern held at 0 DPA (Fig. 4a, c). GbNAC072C activates the transcription of GbHOX3

To investigate the downstream pathway of GbNAC072C, we performed RNA-seq analyses on 3-DPA ovules of the C-haplotype line GbYH9 and the T-haplotype line GbC352. Given that GbYH9 and GbC352 are distinct natural varieties rather than near-isogenic lines (NILs), the 4220 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including 2664 up-regulated and 1556 down-regulated genes (Fig. 6a, Supplementary Data 1), reflect both haplotype-specific differences and broader genome-wide variation beyond GbNAC072 allelic effects. DAP-seq analysis was performed to identify potential direct targets of GbNAC072, revealing 149,380 binding peaks associated with 60,235 genes (Supplementary Fig. 16a, b). Peak distribution analysis showed that GbNAC072 preferentially bound near the TSSs, with significant enrichment in the promoter region within 500 bp upstream of the TSS (Supplementary Fig. 16c). Specifically, 12.0 % (17,711) of GbNAC072’s binding peaks were located in the promoter regions of 16,473 genes (Fig. 6b; Supplementary Data 2). Intersection of the DAP-seq and RNA-seq data identified 441 down-regulated DEGs harbouring NAC binding peaks as potential direct targets (Fig. 6c; Supplementary Data 3). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed three biological processes potentially related to cotton ovule epidermal cell protrusion, namely, plant epidermal development, trichome differentiation, and trichome morphogenesis (Fig. 6d; Supplementary Data 4), involving a total of 15 genes (Fig. 6e). Among them, Gbar_A12G028460 (GbHOX3) caught our attention as it is homologous to Ghir_A12G028530 (HOX3_At) in G. hirsutum, a known key regulator of fibre initiation10. Consistent with a direct regulatory role, DAP-seq identified two prominent GbNAC072 binding peaks (P1 at −53 bp and P2 at −2411 bp) in the GbHOX3 promoter (Fig. 6f, g), both containing the typical NAC-binding CACG motifs (Supplementary Fig. 16d). We confirmed this direct binding through chromatin immunoprecipitation quantitative PCR (ChIP-qPCR) (Fig. 6h), yeast one-hybrid (Y1H) (Fig. 6i), and electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) (Fig. 6j). Natural variations of GbNAC072 and GbDEAH12 improve the LP of G. barbadense

To further explore the application potential of the superior allele of GbNAC072 in G. barbadense breeding, the resequencing data from 1118 G. barbadense accessions were used for haplotype analysis (Supplementary Fig. 17a; Supplementary Data 5). This population clustered into three subgroups (G1, G2 and G3) (Supplementary Fig. 17b–d). The subgroup G1 included 135 wild accessions, mainly from South America and the Caribbean. The subgroup G2 comprised 397 early improved and introduced accessions from Egypt, Central Asia and China (Xinjiang). The subgroup G3 consisted of 586 modern cultivars and breeding lines, primarily from the former Soviet Union, Central Asia, Egypt, the USA and China (Xinjiang).

A total of 23 SNPs and 11 InDels were identified in the 2000 bp upstream promoter and the coding region of GbNAC072, including 5 SNPs and 6 InDels in the promoter region, 3 SNPs in the exons, 2 InDels in the introns, and 15 SNPs and 3 InDels in the 3’UTR region. These variations formed four main haplotypes, Hap1-Hap4, involving 663 accessions. Hap1 contained 83 accessions with GbNAC072C, while Hap2-Hap4 included 241, 253 and 86 accessions with GbNAC072T and other differences in the promoter and 3’UTR regions. Hap1 exhibited significantly higher LP than Hap2-Hap4 (Fig. 8a), and was prevalent in G1 (24.4%) (Fig. 8b), indicating its origin in wild germplasm. Analysis of fibre traits across breeding stages revealed that LP increased significantly from G2 to G3, while FL, fibre uniformity (FU), and fibre strength (FS) declined significantly, with an obvious increase in fibre elongation (FE) and no significant change in fibre micronaire (FM) (Fig. 8c–h; Supplementary Data 6). We speculated that, in the early breeding stages (G2) of G. barbadense, efforts prioritised fibre quality traits and neglected LP selection, so Hap1 had not been widely utilised. In contrast, in modern breeding stages (G3) of G. barbadense, breeders have consciously selected high-LP varieties, even at the cost of slightly compromising fibre quality. High-LP Hap1 was primarily distributed in the USA (25.3%), Central Asia (16.9%) and Peru (13.3%) and China (9.6%); in contrast, low-LP Hap2-Hap4 were most prevalent in China, accounting for 31.5%, 35.5%, and 46.5% of their respective distributions (Supplementary Fig. 18a). Critically, we calculated the interactive effect of different GbNAC072 and GbDEAH12 alleles on LP, and found that the GbDEAH12C-GbNAC072C combination exhibited significantly higher LP than the other combinations, and explained 19.71% of phenotypic variance (Fig. 9a; Supplementary Data 7). From this, we saw that ‘unfavourable-favourable’ (C-C) allele combination contributed to the highest LP in G. barbadense. This aligns with our molecular finding that GbDEAH12G, but not GbDEAH12C, suppresses GbNAC072C-mediated activation of GbHOX3 (Fig. 6k–n). To functionally test this interaction, we generated double overexpression lines for all four allele combinations (Supplementary Fig. 21a–d). At −0.5 DPA, fibre cell protrusions were exclusive to the GbDEAH12C-GbNAC072C line (Fig. 9b, c). By 0 DPA, obvious fibre cell protrusions were observed in all materials, but the number and growth rate of protrusions in the GbDEAH12C-GbNAC072C overexpression material were significantly higher than those in the other three combinations (Fig. 9b, d). From 1 to 3 DPA, the growth rate of the GbDEAH12C-GbNAC072C overexpression material was notably faster than that of the other three allele combinations (Fig. 9b). Taken together, the ‘unfavourable-favourable’ (GbDEAH12C-GbNAC072C) allele combination outperformed the other three combinations in terms of fibre cell initiation and growth rate, which may be the reason for LP increase. The LP, FL, SBW and SI were compared in the double overexpression lines of the four allele combinations mentioned above during the maturation stage of the fibres. The results showed that the GbDEAH12C-GbNAC072C combination mutant exhibited significantly higher LP than the other combinations, while the GbDEAH12G-GbNAC072T combination mutant showed the lowest LP (Fig. 9e). The trends in FL (Fig. 9f, g) and SBW (Fig. 9h) were consistent with those observed in LP. In addition, no significant differences in seed index were observed among the four allele combination mutants (Fig. 9i). Discussion

LP is a key factor determining cotton yield. To date, more than 1439 QTLs controlling LP have been reported, and more than 60 stable major-effect QTLs have been available for marker-assisted selection4. In our previous research, an SNP (Gbar_A04: 5,162,009) on chromosome A04 was identified to be significantly associated with LP of G. barbadense by GWAS38. Within the 1 Mb region flanking this SNP, two additional significant SNPs, Gbar_A04: 5,151,663 and Gbar_A04: 5,162,034, were identified. These three SNPs defined a candidate LP QTL with the physical interval of 4,651,663-5,662,034 on chromosome A04 (Supplementary Data 8), which overlapped with the LP-related QTLs (qLP-C04-1, A04: 3,989,679-9,512,326; qLP-C04-2, A04: 4,161,499-58,361,308) previously identified using IF2 population and recombinant inbred lines (RILs) in G. hirsutum48. This overlap suggested that LP-associated loci might have undergone convergent selection during the domestication of G. barbadense and G. hirsutum. Similarly, the KRN2 gene in maize increased kernel row number and kernel number per ear to enhance maize yield, while its homologue OsKRN2 in rice regulated secondary branch number and grain number to improve rice yield49.

LP is closely related to fibre development50. Cotton fibre is a single cell that differentiates from the ovule epidermis, and the number of initial fibre cells determines the final cotton fibre yield51. In G. hirsutum, the fibre density at 0 DPA and 1 DPA was positively correlated with LP52. In this study, we identified a NAC TF encoding gene, GbNAC072, which was highly expressed at the fibre initiation stage (Fig. 1g). The T-to-C nonsynonymous SNP in GbNAC072 significantly correlated with LP of G. barbadense (Fig. 1e). Both GbNAC072C-OE and GbNAC072T-OE promoted fibre cell protrusion, but only GbNAC072C-OE significantly increased LP (Fig. 2e–h). In contrast, GhMYB25-like_AthapT, as the dominant negative mutation of GhMYB25-like, not only lost the ability to bind to the promoter of the downstream genes, but also inhibited the activity of the GhMYB25-like proteins during the formation of lint and fuzz fibres, ultimately leading to the lint reduction and fuzzless phenotype; however, its negative effects were regulated by the expression level of GhMYB25-like11. Furthermore, overexpression of GbNAC072C/T promoted trichome protrusion in Arabidopsis, but GbNAC072C-OE had more trichomes than GbNAC072T-OE, further demonstrating the distinct roles of different haplotypes in trichome formation (Supplementary Fig. 11a–c). Previous study in Arabidopsis has shown that a membrane-associated NAC transcription factor, NTL8, negatively regulated trichome formation by directly activating the expression of TRY and TCL153.

TFs play a critical role in fibre initiation. A fibre-initiation regulatory network consisting of transcription factors MYB25-like_At, HD1_At, and HOX3 was identified by single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) from G. hirsutum Xu142_LF at −1.5, −1, −0.5 and 0 DPA ovules10. The transcription factor GhWRKY16 was preferentially expressed in the early developing stages of ovules and fibres, and silencing GhWRKY16 significantly reduced the number of fibre cell protrusions on the ovules and shortened the fibre length30. The phosphorylation of GhWRKY16 by GhMPK3-1 directly activated the transcription of GhMYB25, GhHOX3, GhMYB109 and GhCesA6D-D11, thereby promoting early fibre development30. In this study, we revealed that GbNAC072C regulated the expression of GbHOX3 by binding to the promoter of GbHOX3, whereas GbNAC072T did not affect the expression of GbHOX3 (Fig. 6f–n). In G. barbadense, silencing GbHOX3 reduced the number of fibre cell protrusions and decreased LP and FL (Fig. 7a–i). In contrast, in G. hirsutum cv. GhJin668, the knockout of HOX3 had no effect on fibre cell protrusion but inhibited further elongation, ultimately leading to fuzzless seeds10. Moreover, in G. hirsutum cv. R15, GhHOX3 co-suppression lines delayed fibre cell protrusion and significantly reduced FL, producing nearly fuzzless seeds14. However, in our study, the fibre cells could further elongate in GbHOX3-silenced G. barbadense, providing new insights into its regulatory role in cotton fibre development.

Genetic interactions are widespread in crops, and the relationship between gene interactions and phenotypes is highly complex, with most quantitative traits controlled by polygenic coordination54. Recently, 96 high-confidence candidate genes were identified to be associated with 16 agronomic traits using an 18K-rice ‘hybrid’ population and a genetic interaction network comprising 19 hub genes was constructed to uncover 170 ‘masking-type’ epistatic interactions55. In this study, we identified the interaction between GbNAC072 and GbDEAH12 (Fig. 3a–e) and demonstrated that different allele combinations significantly affect binding affinity, with GbNAC072C-GbDEAH12G showing the strongest interaction (Fig. 3f–h). Interestingly, despite their strong physical interaction, the GbDEAH12C-GbNAC072C allele combination exhibited the highest LP (Fig. 9a–e). This apparent paradox can be explained by our finding that GbDEAH12C has no apparent inhibiting effect on GbNAC072C-mediated activation of GbHOX3, while GbDEAH12G strongly suppresses this activation (Fig. 6k–n). These results suggested that GbDEAH12G exerted an epistatic effect on GbNAC072C. Moreover, the two superior alleles controlling LP in G. barbadense did not enhance LP through simple gene pyramiding but rather exhibited functional antagonism (Fig. 10). GbDEAH12C (Hap2) accounted for 59.0% of G. barbadense accessions in China (Supplementary Fig. 20c), while the high-LP haplotype of GbNAC072 (Hap1) constituted only 9.6% (Supplementary Fig. 18a), suggesting that the latter had substantial application potential. Taken together, our results highlight that allele-specific interactions between GbDEAH12 and GbNAC072 modulate GbHOX3 transcription and contribute to LP variation in G. barbadense. GbDEAH12 exhibited a negative regulatory pattern (Supplementary Fig. 12b), and GbDEAH12G exhibited a significantly higher LP than GbDEAH12C. In cotton, GbDEAH12G-OE had less fibre cell protrusion and lower LP than GbDEAH12C-OE (Fig. 4a–d). In Arabidopsis, GbDEAH12G-OE, not GbDEAH12C-OE, significantly reduced the number of trichomes (Supplementary Fig. 11e, f). Functional loss of GbDEAH12 increased the fibre cell protrusion and LP in cotton (Fig. 4a–d). Therefore, we suggested that the HapG variant of GbDEAH12 might partially attenuate gene expression, thereby reducing its negative regulatory effect and ultimately enhancing yield. However, the forced overexpression of HapG appeared to repress yield-related genes, leading to a drop in LP. In contrast, HapC was already highly expressed in low-yield materials, and the additional overexpression did not further enhance its negative regulatory effect and thus had no significant impact on LP.Fig. 10The proposed model showing allele-specific interactions regulating fibre cell initiation and LP in G. barbadense.The transcription factor GbNAC072C significantly enhances LP by promoting fibre cell protrusion during the fibre initiation stage. The differences in fibre yield between G. barbadense and G. hirsutum are closely related to the distinct processes of fibre development. Although fibre development has been extensively studied in G. hirsutum10,56, comparisons between G. barbadense and G. hirsutum remain limited. The fibres are linearly elongated during the 5–15 DPA in G. hirsutum acc. TM-1, while up to 20 DPA in G. barbadense acc. Hai7124; the maximum fibre elongation rate dropped sharply at 25 DPA in TM-1, while it persisted until 30 DPA in Hai7124; compared to TM-1 (elongation period: 5–25 DPA), Hai7124 exhibited a significantly prolonged elongation period (5–30 DPA), which might be the reason for the longer fibre of G. barbadense57. A comparative transcriptomic analysis was performed on 0 DPA ovules and fibre samples from 5, 10, 15, 20, 25 and 30 DPA of G. barbadense (Pima90-53 and Hai7124) and G. hirsutum (HY405 and ND13), resulting in forty-two candidate genes associated with fibre elongation58. This study examined the ovule epidermal cells at −1–3 DPA of high-LP G. barbadense (GbYH9) and high-LP G. hirsutum (GhJin668) to elucidate the dynamical fibre initiation process in G. barbadense and G. hirsutum. At −1 DPA, no fibre cell protrusions were observed in either cotton species. At 0 DPA, fibre cell protrusions began to appear in both G. barbadense and G. hirsutum; however, the fibre cells exhibited larger protrusions in G. barbadense than those in G. hirsutum. At 1–3 DPA, fibre cells of G. barbadense elongated rapidly, whereas those of G. hirsutum exhibited a relatively slower growth rate (Fig. 5a; Supplementary Fig. 9b), a trend similar to that observed in G. hirsutum ZM2459. Expression analysis revealed that GbNAC072 was highly expressed in the ovules of GbYH9 at 3 DPA but exhibited minimal expression in fibre tissues (Supplementary Fig. 2a). In contrast, in GhJin668, GbNAC072 was highly expressed in the ovules at −1 DPA and reached its highest expression level at 25 DPA fibres (Supplementary Fig. 22a). Similarly, GbDEAH12 exhibited high expression in the ovules of GbGiza81 at 1 DPA but showed minimal expression in fibre tissues (Supplementary Fig. 12b). However, in GhJin668, GbDEAH12 was highly expressed in the ovules at 0 DPA, with expression level peaked at 25 DPA fibres (Supplementary Fig. 22b). These results suggested that GbNAC072 and GbDEAH12 exhibited distinct expression patterns, leading to different protrusion growth rate during fibre initiation, ultimately affecting the LP divergence in G. barbadense and G. hirsutum.

Functional genomic studies in G. barbadense remain technically challenging due to its low regeneration efficiency, strong genotype dependence and prolonged transformation cycle, which have long constrained stable genetic transformation and functional validation in this species. To partially circumvent these limitations, we adopted an interspecific hybridisation-based strategy by crossing G. barbadense with G. hirsutum materials carrying the Cas9 construct, thereby introducing edited alleles of GbNAC072 and GbDEAH12 into a G. barbadense-related genetic background, as molecularly confirmed by HI-TOM sequencing (Supplementary Fig. 9a; Supplementary Fig. 13a). The resulting F1 and BC1F1 progeny serve primarily as proof that the edited alleles are successfully incorporated into a G. barbadense-enriched genetic background and can be used as breeding materials, rather than providing definitive functional validation of the candidate genes. It should also be noted that the RNA-seq analysis supporting downstream target identification, such as GbHOX3, is based on comparisons between two distinct natural varieties rather than near-isogenic lines, and thus includes substantial genome-wide variation; the definitive assignment of GbHOX3 as a target relies primarily on DAP-seq binding peaks and biochemical validation. Given the persistent technical barriers to direct transformation, the generation of stable, homozygous gene-edited lines in G. barbadense remains an ongoing, long-term effort that will require further backcrossing, fixation, and validation. Despite these limitations, our findings indicate that interspecific hybridisation combined with genome editing can serve as a feasible exploratory strategy for functional analysis under transformation-recalcitrant conditions, offering a complementary and transitional framework for functional genomics and molecular breeding rather than a replacement for stable transformation systems.

Methods

Plant materials and growing conditions

The G. barbadense materials included three high-LP lines (GbYH9, GbGiza81 and AShi) and one low-LP line (GbC352), whose LP phenotypes listed in Supplementary Data 9. Additionally, an approved G. barbadense cultivar, GbXin78 (LP: 33.2%), was included. GbYH9 and GbC352 were used for the VIGS experiment and the development of backcross populations carrying different GbNAC072C/T haplotypes, while GbGiza81 and GbC352 were used to construct backcross populations carrying different GbDEAH12G/C haplotypes.

Due to the underdeveloped transgenic system in G. barbadense, the G. hirsutum line GhJin668 has been used as a recipient of genetic transformation to generate gene-edited and overexpressed lines carrying different GbNAC072C/T and GbDEAH12G/C haplotypes. Hybridisation between overexpression and gene-editing lines was performed to create materials that overexpressed GbNAC072C/T while knocking out GbDEAH12, and those that overexpressed GbDEAH12G/C while knocking out GbNAC072. Additionally, the overexpression lines of different GbNAC072C/T and GbDEAH12G/C haplotypes were crossed to generate double overexpression lines. Gene-editing materials for GbNAC072 and GbDEAH12 were developed by validating CRISPR/Cas9 constructs in G. hirsutum. The confirmed edited lines were then hybridised with G. barbadense accessions (GbYH9, GbGiza81, AShi and GbXin78). After identifying F1 progenies carrying the desired edits via sequencing, selected individuals were backcrossed with the corresponding G. barbadense parents. The resulting BC1F1 generation primarily serves as proof that the edited alleles are successfully incorporated into a G. barbadense-enriched genetic background and can be used as breeding materials, rather than providing definitive functional validation of GbNAC072 or GbDEAH12.

All cotton materials were cultivated following standard agronomic practices at the Guoxin Cotton Base in Hejian, Cangzhou, China (38°38′N, 116°13′E), the Economic Crop Research Institute of the Xinjiang Academy of Agricultural Sciences in Korla, China (41°48′N, 86°6′E) and the Sanya Breeding Base in Sanya, China (18°21′N, 109°11′E).

The Arabidopsis thaliana variety used in this study was Columbia-0 (Col-0). Seeds were sown on 1/2MS medium and transferred to a light incubator after 2 days of vernalisation in the dark at 4 °C. The incubator was maintained at 22 °C with a photoperiod of 16 h of light and 8 h of dark, and a relative humidity of 80%. Arabidopsis seedlings were then transplanted into pots with a 1:1 mixture of nutrient soil and vermiculite. Overexpression vectors containing the coding sequences of GbNAC072C/T and GbDEAH12G/C were constructed, transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101, and employed to transform Arabidopsis inflorescences via the floral dip method. Positive transgenic plants from generations T0 to T3 were screened, and leaf trichomes were observed in the T3 progeny.

RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cotton ovules and fibres, and Arabidopsis leaves using the RNA Easy Fast Plant Tissue Kit (TIANGEN, Beijing, China) and reverse transcribed into first-strand cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using SuperReal PreMix Plus (SYBR Green) (TIANGEN, Beijing, China) on a CFX96 system. The relative expression of the corresponding genes was calculated using the 2-ΔΔCT method. Three independent biological replicates were performed for each gene. For normalisation, GhUBQ7 was used as the internal control for cotton genes, and Actin2 was used as the internal control for Arabidopsis genes. The primers used for qRT-PCR are listed in Supplementary Data 10.

Subcellular localisation

The full-length cDNAs of GbNAC072 and GbDEAH12, without stop codons, were cloned into the vector pCAMBIA 1300 to generate 35S::GbNAC072-GFP and 35S::GbDEAH12-GFP fusions, respectively. The plasmids, including empty 35S::GFP, 35S::GbNAC072-GFP, 35S::GbDEAH12-GFP, nuclear marker 35S::HY5-RFP, and membrane marker 35S::CBLn-RFP, were each transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101. Cultures of Agrobacteria carrying each GFP fusion construct were co-infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves along with the nuclear marker 35S::HY5-RFP and the membrane marker 35S::CBLn-RFP. At 2–4 days after infiltration, the fluorescent signals in the leaves were detected using a laser confocal microscopy (Carl Zeiss, LSM780, Germany). The primers are listed in Supplementary Data 10.

Dual-luciferase transcriptional activity assay in cotton protoplasts

To verify the transcriptional activation activity of GbNAC072, the full-length GbNAC072 was cloned into an effector vector containing the GAL4 DNA-binding domain (GAL4BD) to generate a GAL4BD-GbNAC072 fusion construct driven by the CaMV35S promoter. The effector construct (GAL4BD-GbNAC072), the negative control (GAL4BD-None) and the positive control (GAL4BD-VP16) were independently co-transfected into cotton protoplasts with a reporter plasmid containing GAL4-responsive firefly luciferase (fLUC) and an internal control plasmid carrying Renilla luciferase (rLUC) driven by the Arabidopsis UBIQUITIN3 promoter, at a ratio of 6:6:1 (effector: reporter: internal control). The transfected protoplasts were incubated at 23 °C in darkness for 12 h, and luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

To investigate the transcriptional regulation of the target gene GbHOX3 by GbNAC072C/T and the effect of GbDEAH12G/C-GbNAC072C/T interaction on the transcriptional activity of GbHOX3, a 3-kb sequence of the GbHOX3 promoter was cloned into the pGreen II 0800-LUC (reporter) vector. The CDS of GbDEAH12G/C and GbNAC072C/T were cloned into the pGreen II 62-SK (effector) vector. The experimental approach was performed in two systems:

For the transient expression assay in cotton protoplasts, the effector and reporter constructs were co-transfected into the protoplasts of G. hirsutum cv. YZ1. The transformed protoplasts were incubated at 23 °C in darkness for 12 h prior to luciferase activity measurement.

To further verify the findings in planta, the same constructs were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101. The bacterial cultures were resuspended in an infiltration buffer (10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM MES (pH 5.7), and 150 µM acetosyringone) and infiltrated into leaves of Nicotiana benthamiana. After incubation for 48–64 h, the leaves were sprayed with 0.8 mM D-luciferin, and the LUC signals were captured using a plant in vivo imaging system (LB985 NightSHADE, Germany).

Luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The primers used are listed in Supplementary Data 10.

Virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) assay

Based on multi-year and multi-location phenotypic data for LP in the G. barbadense population, two accessions with extreme phenotypes, GbYH9 (highest LP) and GbC352 (lowest LP) were selected for VIGS experiments. A 442 bp fragment from the GbNAC072 coding region and a 416 bp fragment from the GbHOX3 coding region were inserted into the pCLCrVA vector and transformed into the Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105. GbYH9 and GbC352 plants were grown, and once cotyledons were fully expanded, Agrobacterium tumefaciens EHA105 cultures containing pCLCrVA-GbNAC072, pCLCrVA-GbHOX3, pCLCrVA-CHLI (positive control) and pCLCrVA (negative control) were grown to the exponential phase. The cultures were resuspended in infiltration buffer (10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM MES (pH 5.7), and 200 μM acetosyringone), and Agrobacterium tumefaciens EHA105 containing the helper vector pCLCrVB was treated similarly. After 3 h of incubation, the suspensions were mixed at a ratio of 1:1.

The abaxial side of the cotyledons was gently scratched with a syringe needle without penetrating the leaf surface, and the suspension was infiltrated using a syringe. In the positive control group, VIGS silencing of the CHLI resulted in chlorotic leaves in cotton plants60. Once the chlorotic phenotype appeared, leaves from all plants were collected to assess the silencing efficiency using qRT-PCR. For plants confirmed to be silenced, ovules at −1 DPA to 5 DPA were collected individually during flowering for SEM observations. Phenotypes for LP, SBW, SI and FL were evaluated at maturity. The primers are listed in Supplementary Data 10.

Vector construction and cotton transformation

CRISPR/Cas9 technique was used to generate GbNAC072 and GbDEAH12 mutants in cotton. To target GbNAC072, two sgRNAs were designed (GATAAAGTTATCACGACCGA; GTCTTCAACGAGTTCATCCG), and for GbDEAH12, two sgRNAs were designed (ATCTGACGACAAGTATGCCG; TTAACCGTAGTAAGGTGCCC). Using the pGTR vector as a template, the tRNA-sgRNA cassette was amplified by PCR and then cloned into the Bsa I-digested pRGEB32-GhU6.9 expression vector61. The primers used for PCR are listed in Supplementary Data 10. The transformed pRGEB32-GhU6.9 expression vector was introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101, and the positive strains were subsequently transferred into G. hirsutum cv. GhJin668. Gene editing at the sgRNA target sites in transgenic plants was detected using the High-Throughput Mutation Tracking (Hi-TOM) workflow62.

To generate overexpression transgenic lines carrying different haplotypes of GbNAC072C/T and GbDEAH12G/C, the full-length coding sequences of GbNAC072C/T (Supplementary Fig. 23a) and GbDEAH12G/C (Supplementary Fig. 23b) were amplified from the materials of different haplotypes, respectively, and cloned into the pCAMBIA2301 vector to obtain 35S::GbNAC072C, 35S::GbNAC072T, 35S::GbDEAH12G, and 35S::GbDEAH12C. These constructs were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101, and the positive strains were subsequently introduced into G. hirsutum cv. GhJin668, and the expression of the transgenic progeny was confirmed using qRT-PCR.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

At the flowering stage, ovules at fibre initiation stages (−1, 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 DPA) were collected from GbYH9 and GbC352 silenced plants and their corresponding controls. Similarly, ovules at fibre initiation stages (−1, −0.5, 0, 1, 2 and 3 DPA) were collected from GbNAC072 and GbDEAH12 gene knockout lines, different haplotype overexpression lines and their respective controls. The epidermal cells of the ovules were examined using a Hitachi TM4000 scanning electron microscope.

Yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) screening

Y2H screening was performed using the Matchmaker Gold Yeast Two-Hybrid System (Clontech). To verify the interaction between GbNAC072C and GbDEAH12G in yeast, the CDS of GbNAC072C was cloned into the pGBKT7 vector, and the CDS of GbDEAH12G was cloned into the pGADT7 vector. Then, pGBKT7-GbNAC072C and pGADT7-GbDEAH12G were co-transformed into yeast strain Y2H Gold and grown on SD/-Leu/-Trp medium. Positive transformants were further tested on SD/-Leu/-Trp/-His/-Ade medium as described in the Yeast Protocol Manual (Clontech, PT3024-1). The interaction of pGADT7-T7 and pGBKT7-53, and the interaction of pGADT7-T7 and pGBKT7-Lam were considered as a positive and negative control, respectively. The primers used for the construction are described in Supplementary Data 10.

Bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay

The full-length coding sequences of GbNAC072C and GbDEAH12G were amplified and inserted into YFPn and YFPc plasmids, respectively, to generate the YFPn-GbNAC072C and GbDEAH12G-YFPc fusion constructs. These fusion constructs were first transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101. The bacterial strains containing these constructions were collected by centrifugation and resuspended in infiltration buffer (10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM MES (pH 5.7), and 150 µM acetosyringone) to a final OD600 of 1.0. The suspensions were then mixed in a 1:1 ratio and injected into the epidermal cells of N. benthamiana leaves. YFP fluorescence resulting from BiFC was detected 48 h after infiltration using a laser scanning confocal microscope (LSM880; Zeiss). The primers used for BiFC detection are listed in Supplementary Data 10.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

To investigate whether GbNAC072 interacts with GbDEAH12 in N. benthamiana leaves, Myc-GbNAC072C was co-expressed with GbDEAH12G-GFP or GFP alone by Agrobacterium-mediated transient transformation. Total soluble proteins were extracted by grinding the infiltrated leaves in the protein extraction buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 1× Protease inhibitor cocktail, 1 mM PMSF, and 100 μM MG132)63, followed by centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C to remove debris. The supernatants were collected, and 100 μL was reserved as input control. The remaining supernatants were incubated with anti-GFP magnetic beads (Lablead, Cat. No.: GNA-25-500) for 1 h at 4 °C with gentle rotation. Beads were washed three times with washing buffer (10 mM Na2HPO4, 1.8 mM KH2PO4, 390 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, and 0.2% Triton X-100 (pH 7.4)) to remove nonspecifically bound proteins. Co-immunoprecipitated proteins were eluted and analysed by immunoblotting using anti-GFP (1:5000; Lablead, Cat. No.: G1002) and anti-Myc (1:5000; Lablead, Cat. No.: M1002) antibodies. The primer sequences used for vector construction are listed in Supplementary Data 10.

Pull-down assay

The coding sequences of GbNAC072 and GbDEAH12 were cloned into the pMAL-c5x and pGEX-6p-1 vectors, respectively, to generate MBP-GbNAC072 and GST-GbDEAH12 fusion constructs. MBP and MBP-GbNAC072 proteins were expressed in Escherichia coli Rosetta (DE3) cells (Weidi, Shanghai, China) and purified using amylose resin (New England Biolabs, Beijing, China). GST-GbDEAH12 was expressed in E. coli Rosetta (DE3) and purified using ProteinIso® GST Resin (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). The primers used for vector construction are provided in Supplementary Data 10.

For the pull-down assays, purified GST-GbDEAH12 was incubated with either MBP (negative control) or MBP-GbNAC072 in lysis buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 50 mM NaCl, and 1 mM DTT) together with amylose resin at 4 °C for 2 h with gentle rotation. After incubation, the resin was thoroughly washed three times with lysis buffer to remove unbound proteins. The retained proteins were eluted and analysed by immunoblotting using anti-GST (1:2000; AE001, ABclonal) and anti-MBP (1:2000; AE016, ABclonal) antibodies to detect GST-GbDEAH12 and MBP-GbNAC072, respectively.

Luciferase complementation imaging (LCI) assay

The split firefly LUC complementation assay was performed to examine the interaction between GbNAC072 and GbDEAH12 using constructions JW771 (nLuc) and JW772 (cLuc)64. The full-length coding sequences of GbNAC072C/T and GbDEAH12G/C were cloned into JW771 and JW772 vectors, respectively, to generate GbNAC072C/T-nLuc and cLuc-GbDEAH12G/C fusion constructs. The fusion constructs were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101, resuspended in infiltration buffer (10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM MES (pH 5.7), and 150 µM acetosyringone) and infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. After 48–64 h of incubation, 0.8 mM D-luciferin was sprayed onto the surface of N. benthamiana leaves, and the LUC signals were detected using a plant live imaging system (LB985 NightSHADE, Germany). The primers are listed in Supplementary Data 10.

Yeast β-galactosidase assay

To quantitatively assess the protein-protein interaction between GbNAC072C/T and GbDEAH12G/C in yeast, a β-galactosidase assay was performed. The coding sequences of GbNAC072C/T and GbDEAH12G/C were cloned into the pGBKT7 and pGADT7 vector, respectively. The resulting constructs, pGBKT7-GbNAC072C/T and pGADT7-GbDEAH12G/C, were co-transformed into the yeast strain Y2H-Gold. Transformants were selected on SD/-Leu/-Trp solid medium and incubated at 30 °C until colonies appeared.

A single colony for each interaction pair was inoculated in liquid SD/-Leu/-Trp medium and cultured to mid-log phase (OD660 between 0.5 and 1.0). The exact OD660 of each culture was recorded. Then, 70 µL of cell suspension was mixed with an equal volume of a 1:1 mixture of Y-PER™ Yeast Protein Extraction Reagent and β-Galactosidase Assay Buffer (Thermo Scientific, #75768). After a brief incubation, the enzymatic reaction was initiated by adding o-nitrophenyl-β-D-galactopyranoside (ONPG). Hydrolysis of ONPG to o-nitrophenol (ONP) and galactose in a mildly alkaline solution resulted in the development of a yellow colour. The reaction time was recorded, and the absorbance at 420 nm was measured.

RNA sequencing and data analysis

Total RNA was extracted from 3-DPA ovules of the C-haplotype line GbYH9 and the T-haplotype line GbC352 using TRIzol reagent, and further purified using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). Reverse transcription and cDNA library construction were performed according to Illumina standards, and sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform (Bluescape Hebei Biotech Co. Ltd., Baoding, China). The raw sequencing data were quality controlled using the tool fastp65 and mapped to the 3-79 reference genome using the STAR software66. Transcripts were reconstructed and gene expression levels were estimated using the programme StringTie (v.1.3.3b). Reads were calculated for each gene using the programme HTSeq (v.0.6.1; https://pypi.org/project/HTSeq/). DEGs were identified using DEseq2 (http://www.bioconductor.org/packages/devel/bioc/html/DESeq2.html) with P < 0.05, |log2(fold change)| ≥ 0.5. Three biological replicates were included for each sample. DEGs are shown in Supplementary Data 1.

DNA affinity purification sequencing analysis (DAP-seq)

DAP-seq analyses were performed using a DNA affinity purification sequencing kit (Cat. No.: D202009, Bluescape Hebei Biotech Co. Ltd.) according to the manufacturer’s instructions67. Fresh ovules of G. barbadense cv. GbHY9 were used for genomic DNA extraction. The fragmented gDNA was constructed into libraries using the NEXTFLEX Rapid DNA Seq Kit (PerkinElmer, Austin, TX, USA). The coding sequence of GbNAC072C was cloned into the pFN19K HaloTag T7 SP6 Flexi expression vector and expressed using the TNT SP6-coupled wheat germ extraction system (Promega). Expressed proteins were captured directly using MagneHalo Tag Beads (Promega). The GbNAC072C-bound beads were incubated with an adaptor-ligated gDNA library. Eluted DNA was sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq6000 with two technical replicates (Supplementary Fig. 16a). Beads without added proteins were used as the input for the negative control DAP library. Raw data were filtered using the default parameters of the fastp software to obtain high-quality clean data for downstream analysis. Clean reads were mapped to the 3–79 genome sequence66 (http://www.cottongen.org/) using BWA-MEM to obtain uniquely mapped reads68. Peaks were obtained using MACS software69. Technical duplicate samples were merged using IDR software with Q < 0.0570. Typical motifs in the peak regions were analysed using MEME-ChIP software71. The peak positions were annotated using ChIPseeker72. Promoter regions were identified as those binding peaks located within 2000 bp upstream of the transcription start site. Binding peaks were visualised using IGV (Integrative Genomics Viewer) (http://software.broadinstitute.org/software/igv/download).

Yeast one-hybrid (Y1H) assay

Y1H assays were performed using the MatchmakerTM Gold Yeast Monohybrid System (Clontech). The full-length CDS of GbNAC072C was cloned into the pGADT7 vector as prey. For bait construction, two fragments of the GbHOX3 promoter, P1 (−426 to −108) and P2 (−2571 to −2011), were inserted into the pAbAi vector. The linearised pAbAi bait plasmids were transformed into the Y1H Gold yeast strain, and the minimal inhibitory concentrations of aureobasidin A (AbA) were determined for each bait strain. The prey construct or an empty pGADT7 vector was then transformed into the recombinant bait-reporter strains. The interaction between pGADT7-p53 and pAbAi-p53 served as a positive control. Yeast cells were cultured on SD/-Leu medium, with or without AbA, at 30 °C for 3–5 days. The primers are shown in Supplementary Data 10.

Electrophoretic mobility shift (EMSA) assay

The full-length CDS of GbNAC072C was amplified and cloned into the pMAL-c5x expression vector. The resulting pMAL-GbNAC072C construct was transformed into Escherichia coli Rosetta cells. Recombinant expression of MBP-GbNAC072C was induced with 0.5 mM IPTG in LB medium at 16 °C with shaking for 24 h. MBP-GbNAC072C protein was purified from bacterial lysates using amylose resin affinity chromatography (NEB, E8021V) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA probes corresponding to the putative GbNAC072C binding motifs in the GbHOX3 promoter (probes P1 and P2) were designed and synthesised. For each motif, a matched probe carrying mutations in the core binding sequence was synthesised and used as a negative control. Purified MBP-GbNAC072C protein and DNA probes were incubated together at room temperature for 20 min to allow complex formation. DNA-protein complexes were resolved and detected using the EMSA Gel Shift Kit (Beyotime, GS009) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Chemiluminescent signals were captured using a Fusion X7 chemiluminescence imaging system. The probes used in this study are listed in Supplementary Data 10.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-qPCR

Leaves were collected from 35S::GbNAC072C-Myc transgenic plants and crosslinked in 1% formaldehyde to preserve protein-DNA interactions. Crosslinking was quenched with glycine, and the fixed tissue was immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground to a fine powder. Chromatin was isolated from the powdered tissue and subjected to sonication to shear it into 200–500 bp fragments. After removal of debris and contaminants, the soluble chromatin fraction was incubated with an anti-Myc antibody (Lablead, Cat. No.: M1002) for immunoprecipitation of GbNAC072C-bound DNA-protein complexes. Immune complexes were washed, reverse crosslinked, and the DNA was subsequently purified. Primers were designed according to GbNAC072C binding sites identified by DAP-seq. Enrichment of target genomic regions was quantified by qPCR using ChIP and input DNA samples. All primers used in the ChIP-qPCR assay are listed in Supplementary Data 10.

Haplotype and evolutionary analyses

In the analysis of haplotype distribution for GbNAC072 and GbDEAH12, resequencing data from 1118 G. barbadense accessions worldwide (336 from our own materials and 782 from public databases) were used38–40,73–76 (Supplementary Data 5). The sequencing data were aligned to the reference genome of 3–7966, and SNPs and InDels were identified. Using five G. mustelinum accessions as outgroups75,76, population structure analysis was performed using the detected high-quality SNPs. This was supplemented with phylogenetic analysis, principal component analysis (PCA) and geographic distribution to classify G. barbadense accessions into different subgroups, which were marked on the maps using R software. For the haplotype analysis of GbNAC072 and GbDEAH12, SNPs and InDels detected within the gene sequences, including the 2-kb promoter regions, were used for haplotype classification. Of the 1,118 G. barbadense accessions, 666 with phenotypic data of yield and fibre quality were analysed. The LP phenotypes of different haplotypes of GbNAC072 and GbDEAH12 were compared, as were the LP and fibre quality phenotypes of accessions from different subgroups.

The combined haplotypes of GbNAC072-GbDEAH12 were analysed in 336 proprietary G. barbadense accessions. Four major combined haplotypes were identified by classification, and the LP of the combined haplotypes was analyzed for comparison.

Construction of near-isogenic lines (NILs) for GbNAC072C/T and GbDEAH12G/C

To verify the functionality of the high-lint-percentage haplotypes of GbNAC072C and GbDEAH12G, materials containing the GbNAC072C/T haplotypes (GbYH9CC and GbC352TT) and the GbDEAH12G/C haplotypes (GbGiza81GG and GbC352CC) were selected for the construction of NILs, with GbC352 used as the recurrent parent. After three generations of backcrossing and selfing, the high-lint-percentage haplotypes of GbYH9CC and GbGiza81GG were introgressed into GbC352 to generate the corresponding NILs. These NILs were then evaluated for the phenotypic traits, including LP, FL and SI.

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance between groups was determined using a two-tailed Student’s t-test, a one-way ANOVA or a two-way ANOVA, followed by a Duncan’s multiple range test. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (http://www.graphpad.com/).
