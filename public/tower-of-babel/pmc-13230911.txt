Comparative analysis of deep learning algorithms for rolling element bearing fault classification under variable loads and speeds
Authors: Pratyush Vishal, Rohith Nair, Jonathan Jacob, Narendiranath Babu T, M Pandiyan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper presents a comparative study of deep learning methods used to classify roller bearing faults. We obtained experimental vibration data by conducting controlled tests on roller bearings with various faults under different load conditions to simulate real-world industrial conditions. The methodology first evaluates four different time-frequency techniques-STFT, CWT, WPT and CQ-NSGT, establishing CQ-NSGT as the superior method for feature visualization. A novel hybrid transfer learning architecture that integrates pre-trained backbones (EfficientNetB0, MobileNetV2, InceptionV3) with custom residual blocks is proposed. These CNNs are compared to a baseline CNN and a Vision Transformer (ViT). Their performance was rigorously evaluated using fivefold cross-validation and tested against additive white Gaussian noise at Signal-to-Noise Ratios (SNR) of 1 dB, 3 dB, and 5 dB. The EfficientNetB0 hybrid model was able to reach the highest baseline accuracy of 99.83% while also exhibiting excellent robustness-maintaining 98.8% accuracy even at 1 dB SNR. MobileNetV2 is the most computationally efficient model with a training time of only 121.4 s and 0.50 GFLOPS making it perfect for edge deployment. ViT has potential but is less noise stable and lacks the inductive bias of CNNs which is important for this particular application. These results can be used as a guide for trading off accuracy of diagnosis and computational cost in industrial predictive maintenance.Keywords: Bearing fault diagnosis, Deep learning, EfficientNetB0, Transfer learning, Hybrid architecture, CQ-NSGT, Vibration analysis, Predictive maintenance, Condition monitoring, SpectrogramsSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Industries heavily emphasize the reliability of rotating machinery since component failures not only halt production but also lead to costly repairs. One of the most crucial parts is rolling element bearings whose failure can be due to wear, fatigue, fracture, and corrosion. Such problems are sometimes caused by assembly errors, misuse, and lack of maintenance1. Deep learning (DL) models for fault diagnosis have become quite popular recently. One hybrid approach first transforms the signal into a time-frequency image using Continuous Wavelet Transform (CWT), afterward the transformed image is processed by a 1D convolutional residual network(ResNet), a multi-head self-attention (MHSA) mechanism, and a bidirectional long short-term memory (BiLSTM) network to simultaneously extract spatial, temporal, and hierarchical features2.

A big challenge in developing these models is data scarcity. To tackle “few-shot learning”, an innovative method uses CWT images but applies a Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model (DDPM) to create synthetic, high-quality data. The enhanced dataset was thus fed to a hybrid KANs-CNN model which leveraged the unique learnable activation functions of Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks3. One more significant challenge is environmental noise. For a noise-robust model, a lightweight 1D-CNN was developed that operated in the frequency domain and uniquely used both the magnitude and phase parts of a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) as input. It was able to keep a very high level of accuracy even at extremely low signal-to-noise ratios (-10 dB)4 by training the model with noise additions. Advanced architectures such as the MLSCA-CW, on the other hand, are intended for extreme noise situations as they combine soft thresholding modules for filtering with a multi-location fusion module to integrate data from different sensor positions5.

Through its use of vibration envelope spectrum data which extracts peak frequencies and peak intensities as features and classifies them with a basic Naive Bayes algorithm, the method achieves 99% accuracy6. A team of scientists developed a complex parallel-path model which unites 1D-LSTM time-series analysis with 2D-CNN CWT image processing to extract data from both temporal and frequency domains7. Researchers concentrate their efforts on enhancing one specific metric through their work to improve hybrid CNN-Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) models by first applying envelope analysis to preprocess signals and then using adaptive mean filtering to reduce noise8.

Identifying fault severity is also important. A two-layered diagnostic scheme was introduced to address this. A hybrid feature pool was created from time-domain, envelope spectrum, and wavelet energy features and then fed into a sparse stacked autoencoder (SSAE) to classify the specific crack size9. The “signal-to-image” pipeline enables the use of powerful, pre-trained 2D-CNNs. Previous work has used 2D-CNN based methods for this task. In one method, the Constant-Q Nonstationary Gabor Transform (CQ-NSGT) is used to create time–frequency images. An Inception-ResNet-V2 model is then applied to these images10. In another study, spectrograms were used as input to AlexNet. The model achieved 100% accuracy and required fewer resources compared to newer deep learning architectures11.

Deep feature extraction can be used together with classical ML techniques. In one approach, a CWT image is fed to a deep CNN such as ResNet50 just to extract features. These features are then classified using Support Vector Machines or Random Forests12. A few black-box deep learning models are being re-examined. A researcher applied Hamiltonian Neural Networks to classify a system’s total energy signature while enforcing physics-based constraints13.

The conversion of signals into images is an important research area. One approach classifies faults under different operating conditions. In the method, one-dimensional motor current signals are first transformed into two-dimensional pictures via Gramian Angular Field technology. A simple two-layer CNN is subsequently adapted for classification14. EfficientNetV2 showcases model flexibility as it was architected to harmonize accuracy, training speed, and parameter efficiency15. Some use even less complicated techniques like the wavelet envelope power spectrum16 or add specific modules, such as a multi-feature block, in a custom CNN17.

Some very recent work has focused on the development of simpler models that can be interpreted more easily. One feature engineering study has demonstrated that frequency band separation techniques, when used together with a Random Forest classifier, yield good results. The method managed to attain very high accuracy at a very low computational cost18. Thus deep learning models, which are more complex and involved, may not always be necessary when it is possible to use domain-specific methods. Unfortunately, the majority of these methods have been tested only on clean benchmark datasets, which do not present the same level of complexity as real-world scenarios. A factory-based vibration dataset collected from a pulp mill has revealed the real challenge. Real-world data, in fact, come with variations in symptoms of a failure, changes in the operational conditions and external disturbances, which are not the case with controlled laboratory data19.

This real challenge from the world has been confirmed by research records that demonstrate simple CNNs alone sometimes reach over 98% accuracy just by being directly applied to raw accelerometer data20. CNN-BiLSTM-MHSA hybrid models are needed to effectively deal with the speed variability feature of the air vehicles (UAVs) application21.

In order to address the issues of limited data and environmental noise, Siddique et al.22 proposed a hybrid deep learning framework that combines Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) with Vision Transformers (ViTs) for milling machine fault diagnosis. Their technique starts with the extraction of clean time-frequency features from vibration signals using logarithmic Continuous Wavelet Transform (log-CWT) scalograms and Canny edge filtering. The researchers then combined the local spatial details captured by ResNet-18 with the global contextual information modeled by ViT and employed a validation-guided stacking ensemble classifier to enhance the accuracy of the diagnosis. This method with two branches demonstrated a high level of robustness against variations in conditions and achieved a classification accuracy of 99.78% on real-world milling datasets, outperforming conventional single-model designs22.

As a solution to the problem of both accuracy and speed in the diagnosis of centrifugal pump faults, Zaman et al.23 came up with a hybrid deep learning model that integrates Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) with Principal Component Analysis (PCA). The vibration signals of the pump were converted to Stockwell Transform and Continuous Wavelet Transform (CWT) scalograms that were later filtered by the Sobel operator to highlight the fault signature transients. After each individual CNN branch got its essential features and these were combined into a hybrid feature pool, the authors applied PCA to reduce the feature space and therefore, a two-layer Artificial Neural Network (ANN) was trained for the classification of the pump states with high accuracy. The authors’ multi-modal approach outperformed the best state-of-the-art baseline models in terms of noise immunity, reaching a classification accuracy of 95.60% and showing significant improvements in precision and recall metrics23.

Ullah et al.24 in their study addressed data efficiency problems and varying operating pressure issues in centrifugal pump fault diagnosis essentially by coming up with a multi-sensor framework based on autoregressive (AR) residual learning. First, the AR observer in the study generates fault-sensitive residuals from vibration signals, which are then statistically and spectrally characterized, followed by a refinement of the features using an Auto-Permutation Feature Importance (Auto-PFI) tool. By utilizing these refined features, the authors constructed a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) that provided a powerful probabilistic classification system, capable of handling class imbalance and overlapping fault signatures in an efficient manner. Comparison experiments at different pressure levels demonstrated that the new method outperformed existing deep learning and entropy-based methods in generalization capability, with classification accuracies of more than 99%24.

Umar et al.25 developed a burst-informed framework for milling machines that aimed to solve problems related to interpretation of non-stationary acoustic emission (AE) signals as well as a lack of explainability in current fault diagnosis models. Burst-Informed Wavelet Denoising (BIWD) together with adaptive segmentation was employed by the researchers to extract high-energy transients, from which multi-domain features were obtained and eventually combined through an attention-guided fusion mechanism. The combination of a Performance-Weighted Stacked Ensemble (PWSE) classifier with SHAP and LIME interpretability tools enabled the authors to build a fault diagnosis system that is not only physically consistent but also interpretable at the burst level. Experiments on datasets at various operating speeds demonstrated superior generalization performance of the proposed method over state-of-the-art deep learning models as evidenced by the diagnostic accuracy of 99.21%25.

Fallahy and Rezazadeh26 demonstrated that one can avoid deep learning’s high resource and time overhead for retraining when one freezes the backbone of the model and only allows a few parameters like batch normalization statistics to be updated during Test-time Entropy Minimisation. They argued that a process like Exponential Moving Average that essentially looks at recent history can help prevent the model from getting confused and changing drastically as it gets data from new domains. Also, a set of methods could be made more trustworthy by applying Test-time Augmentation which is essentially a technique that removes noise and makes it easier to identify particular anomalies. Creating different versions of an image and forcing the model to be consistent across them, the approach effectively reduces the model’s hesitation that is unavoidable in distinguishing heterogeneous masonry textures. To sum up, the technologies mentioned above can be combined to conduct high-level structural health monitoring without any specialized high-performance computing equipment26.

Dataset description

The sampling rate of the sensor used is crucial for diagnosing faults. It sets the limit on the frequency range content that we can analyze from the signal and it also impacts the time resolution we can get. We picked a sampling rate of 12,800 Hz after a series of preliminary experiments. The rates below this did not really succeed in capturing the high-frequency components indicative of bearing defects in a reliable manner. Meanwhile, 12, 800 Hz is strikes a balance where it is adequate enough to pick up the components we need, while also being feasible for a real-time implementation.

Since real machines run in changing environments, we conducted tests at three rotational speeds: 1000 RPM correlates with a low-speed operation, 1500 RPM refers to a medium-speed, and 2000 RPM depicts a high-speed scenario. Besides that, three different loading conditions were considered, no load at 0 g, a light load at 6 g, and a heavy load at 12 g. Such testing in different speed and load scenarios allows us to observe how the characteristics of the fault features change with varying operating conditions.

Our data records a total of five different health states of a bearing: Healthy Baseline (HB), Ball Fault (BF), Inner Race Fault (IRF), Outer Race Fault (ORF), and Combined Fault (CF). A data snapshot for every single combination of fault type, rotational speed, and load level was obtained and that resulted in 45 distinct operating conditions altogether. Each of them is accompanied by a separate time-series file with roughly 420, 000 samples to it.

In total, the data files hold 18.9 million plus data points. This allowed for the extraction of necessary diversity from this volume of data when training models capable of handling the variability between different conditions. It is also very beneficial for us in terms of the trustworthiness of the performance tests, especially when we evaluate the generalization ability of the model to unseen speed and load conditions.

In order to scale the dataset and enhance the model’s robustness we used window splitting to cut the vibration signal into segments of a fixed length. We tried both overlapping and non-overlapping windows, so that each segment could represent a short part of the signal with potentially fault-related patterns. In this way, by training on a large number of segments, the model is enabled to recognize faults as they gradually develop over time instead of depending on isolated snapshots only.

Along with window splitting, we used a number of augmentation techniques aimed at improving model robustness against real-world variations. For instance, horizontal flipping was used to produce signal versions that are mirror images of the original ones, while time-domain rotation was intended to simulate the phase change, which could easily take place in real operating conditions. Additionally, we applied the injection of a very small amount of noise to the data so that the model can still perform well even when the quality of the sensor data is not ideal.

For model evaluation, we chose scikit-learn’s train-test split function to separate the augmented dataset into training (80%) and validation (20%) sets. The random split of the data ensured that all fault types and operating conditions were naturally distributed in both sets, so we are quite sure that the validation performance will indicate how good the model is at generalizing to unseen data.

The dataset offers some significant benefits. Data was recorded at three different rotation speeds. This enables the model to deal with the variation in speed. The sampling frequency is 12, 800 Hz. Such a high sampling rate allows for detecting minute fault-related changes that would otherwise have been overlooked at lower frequencies. Physics based validation of fault signatures

Roller bearings have different parts. Defects in any of these parts produce vibrations at certain frequencies. The main frequencies are BPFO, BPFI and BSF.

BPFO is the frequency at which the rolling elements pass over a defect on the outer race. As the outer race is fixed, thus, the frequency is directly proportional to the shaft speed and produces relatively stable spectral peaks under steady operating conditions. BPFI signifies the frequency at which the rolling elements hit the inner race defect. Since the inner race turns with the shaft, this part usually shows amplitude modulation and sidebands at the shaft rotational frequency. BSF is related to the defects on the rolling elements. It depicts the rate at which a ball spins on its axis and usually comes with harmonics because of periodic slip and changing contacts.

Methodology

Signal-to-image transformation

The first stage converts one-dimensional vibration signals into two-dimensional images. These images are used as input for deep learning models. This step is required because such models work better with image data.

Vibration signals from rotating machines are non-stationary. Their characteristics change over time. Because of this, analysis only in the time domain or only in the frequency domain is not sufficient. A time–frequency analysis method is needed to capture how frequency content changes over time.

Several signal-to-image methods were examined for this purpose. However, some limitations were observed. The Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) uses a fixed window size. This trade-off between time and frequency resolution mainly arises. The Continuous Wavelet Transform (CWT) allows for multi-resolution analysis. On the other hand, it is computationally very expensive and does not ensure perfect signal reconstruction. As a result, the accuracy of the signal representation can be lower10

We used the raw, unfiltered one-dimensional vibration signals and applied Constant-Q Non-stationary Gabor Transform (CQ-NSGT) as per Eq. (3) to get two-dimensional time-frequency representations of the signals, i.e. spectrograms. These spectrograms serve as visualizations that illustrate where vibration energy is located in frequency and how it changes over time simultaneously. This conversion is especially useful in detecting faults in bearings as each fault will leave a unique signature in frequency pattern that change over time, thus, those signals from the raw source will be almost impossible to detect.

To avoid the model becoming too fitted to the training set, we carried out data augmentation on the spectrograms to increase the diversity of patterns which the network would see during the training. Next, we resized the images to 224 × 224 pixels so our models could process them properly10.

Transfer learning

A transfer learning approach is used in this study. Rather than building a model from the ground up, pre-trained models are fine-tuned for the task of bearing fault classification. This shortens the training period and eliminates the requirement for extremely large labeled datasets.

A custom hybrid framework was made instead of just using pre-trained models. The purpose of this framework is to convert general features into vibration fault patterns.

Here, a pre-trained model is used as a feature extraction backbone (Refer Fig. 7). On top of that, custom residual blocks are added. These blocks assist in the refinement of the features extracted for the task of bearing fault classification.

Proposed model architecture

This work investigates three deep learning models to classify roller bearing faults. The models utilize vibrational signals transformed into spectrogram images. All the models rely on transfer learning. Custom hybrid architectures incorporate pre-trained networks to function as feature extractors. Furthermore, to make the features suitable for fault classification, custom residual blocks are added.

MobileNetV2, InceptionV3, and EfficientNetB0 are the three fundamental models used in this study. The reason behind the selection of these models was their frequent use in image classification as well as their being different in architectural characteristics. In the proposed method, each model is used as a backbone for feature extraction. After that, the extracted features are processed with the help of custom residual blocks for bearing fault classification.

Three different backbones were experimented with in this work. Each backbone was assessed separately to compare the results under the same testing conditions.

MobileNetV2: lightweight efficiency for resource-constrained applications

Lightweight and efficient network. It is can be run on low power devices without any problems. It’s architecture (Fig. 8) is based on inverted residual blocks and linear bottlenecks which help to keep it small and fast.

MobileNetV2 architecture.

InceptionV3: multi-scale feature extraction through parallel processing

The Inception architecture (Fig. 9) addresses the issue of feature extraction at various scales by performing 1 × 1, 3 × 3, and 5 × 5 convolutional filtering simultaneously within each module. Hence, the model can respond to tiny details and large spatial patterns at the same time. To limit the computational cost, the architecture uses factorized convolutions, i.e., splitting a large filter into a series of small ones, which gives the structural power that is maintained while the number of parameters and operations is hugely decreased.

InceptionV3 architecture.

EfficientNetB0: compound scaling for optimal resource utilization

EfficientNet (Fig.10) uses compound scaling that gradually and systematically scales depth, width, and resolution of the network by using a fixed set of coefficients; thus, it ensures these dimensions are balanced and not independently tuned. The architecture is comprised of Mobile Inverted Bottleneck Convolution (MBConv) blocks which add Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) modules to recalibrate channel-wise feature responses—basically the network is enabled to highlight the features it finds most useful and at the same time, it suppresses those less relevant, based on the global context.

EfficientNetB0 architecture.

Unique contribution: the hybrid residual architecture

The‍ core idea of this research is to utilize a hybrid deep learning framework. Preliminary trials revealed that the direct application of a pre-trained model for bearing fault classification did not yield satisfactory results. Such models have been trained on huge image datasets and are very good at extracting general features. Nevertheless, they are not geared towards the identification of specific patterns in bearing fault spectrograms.

In order to fix this, the pre-trained base model is frozen and retained only for feature extraction. In addition, custom residual blocks are appended to the base model. These blocks allow the model to fine-tune the features extracted for the bearing fault classification task. Thus, the model can retain general feature information and at the same time obtain task-specific patterns from the vibration data.

Residual blocks (Refer Fig. 11) are implemented primarily for training stability. When deep networks are built, they can easily have the problem of the gradient becoming smaller and smaller. Shortcuts, or skip connections, in residual blocks give the network this ability of reducing the gradient problem. This allows for a deeper network while making the training process more reliable. The deeper network is capable of extracting more complex features from spectrogram data since shallow models have limited capacity.

Figuratively, the hybrid model that comprises frozen pre-trained backbone and trainable custom layers generalizes better than the conventional model. In this way, the benefits of transfer learning are preserved and at the same time the model is adapted to bearing fault ​‍data.

Adaptive optimization strategy for bearing fault detection

Model training was done with the Adam optimizer. Adam was chosen since it adjusts the learning rate during training. This is quite handy for spectrogram data, which has variations over time and frequency. Additionally, bearing fault signals can be noisy and may give sparse gradients. Thus, Adam allowed stable training when fine-tuning the custom residual blocks. The pre-trained base model was not altered during this phase.

Robustness testing using noise injection

Robustness testing was conducted to ensure that the model’s performance is not degraded in noisy situations. Additive White Gaussian Noise (AWGN) was created with the help of the Numpy library. This noise was superimposed on the original vibration signals to imitate industrial interference. Three different signal-to-noise ratios were examined. Namely, these were 5 dB, 3 dB, and 1 dB. The performance of the model was measured independently at each noise ​‍​level.

Cross-validation strategy

We employed a stratified fivefold cross-validation method. The data set was split into five equal parts. Each part had a balanced distribution of all fault classes. Among these were Ball Fault, Inner Race Fault, Outer Race Fault, Combined Fault, and Healthy conditions. The models were each trained five times, with the validation fold being changed each time. The average and standard deviation of the results across all folds are provided as the final performance results.

Samples of different operating conditions (loads and speeds) in whole were combined into a single dataset so that samples were classified based on the type of fault rather than the condition of acquisition. The aim of the current research is to test how well deep learning models can classify faults at the window-level when the operating regimes are mixed, instead of looking at generalization across conditions (e.g., training on one load and testing on another).

Therefore, a stratified k-fold cross-validation method was used to maintain class balance in each fold of the dataset while also ensuring that each fold contains samples that are representative of the entire range of operating conditions.

In addition, window segmentation was conducted without overlap, thus each sample is a separate time segment of the vibration signal and duplicated or almost identical signal fragments are not allowed to occur across the folds. This lowers the extent to which redundancy-driven performance has been artificially increased and, at the same time, allows one to comfortably estimate the performance of the window-level classifier under different loading and speed conditions.

Four of such techniques are Constant-Q Non-Stationary Gabor Transform (CQ-NSGT), Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT), Continuous Wavelet Transform (CWT), and Wavelet Packet Transform (WPT). Checking these methods includes almost all fault states. The vibration signals for these spectrograms were recorded while the instrument was operating at 1500 RPM and a load of 6 g. It is noted that using the same conditions of the experiment allows direct comparability of methods as differences in the spectrograms can be attributed to changes of the transformation technique, not confounded by experimental variation. Each method displays some variations in what features of the fault pattern are more visible. Compared with other methods, CQ-NSGT excels in time–frequency resolution. Consequently, harmonic components related to faults can be more easily detected, especially to the inner and outer race of the fault. Moreover, the constant-Q aspect aids in keeping the frequency resolution at both low and high frequency ranges.

STFT generates an even time–frequency distribution. Thus it provides reliable results and can be considered as a standard method for observing general fault patterns. Despite this, the limitation of the fixed window size is to not be able to represent at the same time short transient events and long-duration signal components.

CWT quite accurately records the transient events due to its variable resolution. This results in impact-related features being more visible. Such features are typically related to ball faults and combined fault cases.

WPT lets the vibration signal be broken down into several frequency bands. From this, one can get a closer look as to how the energy of the signal is spread across frequencies. The differences between healthy and faulty bearings are made quite clear in these representations due to changes in spectral content.

The level of success is judged qualitatively based on how well the visualization of the separation of fault-related harmonic components under identical operating conditions can be done. Among several cases, CQ-NSGT is more clearly separating fault related components. The spectrograms thus created are what is given as inputs to convolutional neural networks. These inputs allow the feature extraction process to focus on fault-related features under different operating conditions.

Quantitative spectrogram analysis

Objective quantitative evaluation was used to further examine the feature- discriminating ability and the structural consistency of the different time, frequency representations. A spectral kurtosis based class separability analysis was executed to mathematically validate how each transformation can keep the fault impulse characteristics which are the bearing defect fingerprints. Spectral kurtosis has become very popular in the field of rotating machinery diagnostics because of its capability of identifying non-Gaussian and transient components of vibration signals, thus allowing the fault-induced impulsive behavior buried in the complex mechanical responses to be effectively detected and characterized27.

Kurtosis feature vectors were extracted from the spectrograms corresponding to each time, frequency representation. Class separability was evaluated by the ratio of the between-class and within-class scatter measures obtained from these feature vectors. The between-class scatter indicates how far different fault categories are scattered in the feature space, while the within-class scatter is how closely samples of the same class are clustered. A good representation should be able to maximize the separation between classes and at the same time minimize the variability within each class, hence leading to a better discriminative structure in the transformed domain.

In addition to evaluating the separability based on scatter, we also separately assessed the intra-class variance of the kurtosis features to quantify the consistency and structural stability of the representation of samples under various operating conditions. When the intra-class variance is low, it means that the transformation has preserved the fault signatures in a consistent and reliable manner, thus, giving less uncertainty to the subsequent classification steps and higher robustness.

The comparative analysis reveals CQ-NSGT as the most effective method in terms of class separability among all the techniques that were evaluated, exhibiting a score which falls very close to CWT, which demonstrated the highest score. Besides that, it also demonstrates the minimum intra-class variance that is in line with the physically interpretable preservation of fault-induced impulsive structures. The numeric results convincingly demonstrate that CQ-NSGT yields a significantly tighter and more organized distribution of discriminative features mapped in the kurtosis domain, relative to STFT, CWT, and WPT. In general, the findings statistically and empirically justify the decision of CQ-NSGT being the most suitable time, frequency transformation for the present fault diagnosis framework.

The quantitative results in Table 5 give a comparative assessment of time-frequency transformation techniques using spectral kurtosis-based class separability and intra-class variance. Out of all the methods tested, CWT obtains the greatest class separability score (0.0630), with CQ-NSGT (0.0585) almost on par, whereas STFT (0.0408) and WPT (0.0170) show a lower level of separability. CWT only exceeds CQ-NSGT slightly in separability, but separability is not the only factor that can represent the stability and robustness of a feature set.

Training configuration

The CNN-based models EfficientNet, InceptionNet and MobileNet were trained using the same hyperparameters. This was done to ensure a fair comparison. All three models used the Adam optimizer. Sparse categorical cross-entropy was used as the loss function. Accuracy was used as the evaluation metric.

Early stopping was applied to reduce overfitting. A patience value of three epochs was used based on validation loss. A learning rate scheduler was also applied. The learning rate was reduced by a factor of 0.5 after five epochs without improvement. The minimum learning rate was set to 1 × 10−5. Training was limited to a maximum of 100 epochs. Training logs were saved using CSV callbacks for future analysis.

The Vision Transformer (ViT) model used a different training setup. Input images of size 224 × 224 were divided into patches of size 16 × 16. This resulted in 196 patches per image. The model consisted of six transformer encoder blocks. Each block used six attention heads. The embedding dimension was set to 384. The MLP expansion ratio was set to 2. The AdamW optimizer was used along with a cosine annealing learning rate schedule. Training was performed for 50 epochs. Weight decay was set to 0.01 and dropout was set to 0.1 to improve generalization.

Discussion on partial fine-tuning of pre-trained model backbone

Partial fine-tuning of the deeper backbone layers was experimentally evaluated during preliminary studies. In particular, the later convolutional blocks of the pre-trained backbone were unfrozen to assess whether additional task-specific adaptation would improve classification performance.

This modification substantially increased the number of trainable parameters, which lead to a higher computation cost, longer training time and increased GPU memory usage. It is important to note that while fine-tuning does not alter total parameter count, it increases the number of trainable parameters. In the MobileNet V2 based hybrid model, unfreezing 25% of the model for fine-tuning resulted in a 30–40% increase in training time. Similarly in EfficientNet, the training time increased by 50% and doubled in the case of InceptionV3. Subsequently, if a higher percentage of the backbone layers are unfrozen, the time and computational penalty is compounded.

The proposed model architecture employing a frozen backbone achieved a classification accuracy of 99.83% (in the case of EfficientNet), demonstrating that the pretrained feature representations were sufficiently discriminative for the target fault categories. In our testing, when partial fine-tuning with 25% of the layers were unfrozen, it resulted in marginal improvements of the training accuracy with no improvement in the validation accuracy indicating that the model was overfitting.

This behaviour can primarily be attributed to the limited size of the available dataset. This constrains the model’s ability to reliably update millions of additional trainable parameters without memorizing training samples. Therefore, opening up more layers to training essentially raised the limit of what the model could do and made finding the optimum more challenging.

Thus, the definitive ensemble architecture constitutes a deliberate and well supported empirical decision to choose robustness and efficiency over excessive expansion through parameters.

Brief analysis of model performance under speed fluctuations

Purely additive stochastic noise cannot be regarded as an accurate representation of the full range of mechanically induced disturbances in industrial bearing systems, such as load torque ripple, rotational speed fluctuation, structural resonance, or shaft misalignment. These phenomena can introduce non-stationary modulation, frequency drift, harmonic amplification, and impulsive components that deviate substantially from the assumption of idealized additive noise.

However, the robustness analysis in this study was aiming at evaluating the stability of the feature vectors derived by deep neural networks under controlled and standardized degradation of input signals, rather than replicating a mechanical failure mechanism from the surface. Here, additive stochastic disturbance is used as a generic signal stress-testing tool that disrupts the signal in all frequency bands, thus preventing the model from depending on very localized or fragile signal artifacts. A deep neural network that keeps a high classification performance even when the signal is corrupted broadband is indicative of the learned features representing the real fault-related structural characteristics rather than the incidental or narrowband ones.

In mathematical terms, a lot of the disturbances caused by the machine can be broken down into additive components that are combined with modulation effects. Hence, being robust against additive perturbations is basically a minimum requirement of diagnostic model resilience that a model should meet before being tested under complicated, scenario-specific noise conditions. In this way, additive noise testing offers a lower-bound robustness guideline that is the foundation for more specialized disturbance models to be developed later on.

Apart from noise intensity variation, robustness behavior under mechanically motivated disturbance is also revealed by the results of speed fluctuation simulation. According to Table 13, EfficientNet essentially keeps the performance level, a very slight drop in performance from 99.90% to 99.83% is hardly detectable, thus it can be said that its stability under temporal variability is quite remarkable. MobileNet shows a moderate decrease in performance from 99.00% to 98.23%, whereas Inception suffers a more significant loss of performance from 98.20% to 95.07%. The comparative results of Table 13 thus indicate that scale-adaptive feature extractors have the ability to preserve at a much higher level the output of the model even in the condition of speed-induced non-stationarity. The few differences in the performance of EfficientNet can therefore be seen as additional evidence that the model is representing the fault signatures in a physically meaningful way rather than fixed temporal patterns.

Realism in this study could be improved by using more physics based disturbance models, for example, amplitude modulation as a way of simulating torque ripple, time-warping for speed variation, or the amplification of a narrowband resonance. However, these methods would necessitate more parameters and assumptions about the specific conditions of the machine operation, which can differ quite a bit from one installation to another.

In this paper we propose a fixed, reproducible robustness benchmark and we recognize that the future publication of the work may involve the combination of condition-specific disturbance modeling to not only introduce a higher degree of ecological validity but also an increased level of application relevance.

InceptionV3 has the longest training time with 230.5 s. The longer training time is a reflection of the higher computational cost per epoch of the model compared to the other models.

Computational complexity analysis with latency and memory footprint

The study establishes that there are marked differences in computational performance between different architectural configurations and this factor plays a major role when one is deciding to deploy AI models on limited-resource devices.

The computational experiments extended beyond mere training time and GFLOPs and thus explicitly identified inference latency and memory usage. Steady-state inference latency was measured for each of the proposed models, with 600 single image forward passes of the resolution 224 × 224, excluding warm-up iterations so that the latency measure was untainted by the compilation overhead and the measured steady-state result was accurate. A comprehensive suite of statistical tools for descriptive statistics, such as the floor latency, measure of dispersion, quartiles, interquartile range, and 95% confidence intervals for latency, were computed to ensure that the measure is statistically reliable. Also, the consistency of latency in real-time during lengthy executions was investigated to ensure stability.

Memory usage was monitored by tracking model size and recording the peak memory usage of RAM during inference. After every model, to ensure that tests stayed independent of each other and memory was not retained for the previous model, the TensorFlow sessions were cleared.

The testing was done on a MacBook Air with an M4 chip and 16GB RAM using the CPU only to provide a realistic scenario for the deployment.

Discussion

Out of the tested models, EfficientNetB0 was the most accurate across the validation and noise conditions. The accuracy scores showed negligible deviation when 5 dB, 3 dB, and 1 dB noise levels were applied. These findings point to the robustness of the model’s performance across different signal conditions and only moderate computational requirements of the model.

InceptionV3 performed well under the baseline condition. With the accuracy scores staying consistent across the different folds. However, this model was more computationally expensive than the lightweight architectures.

MobileNetV2 was able to achieve a high level of accuracy under normal conditions while also keeping the computational cost low. Such a combination of factors can be beneficial in scenarios with limited computational resources.

The Vision Transformer (ViT) had a low baseline accuracy and a high variability across the folds. Its performance was inconsistent under the various conditions indicating that it was sensitive to data splits and noise levels. Overall, ViT’s performance might be reliant on the nature of the dataset and the training configuration.

Interpretation of results and implications

EfficientNetB0 achieved the highest accuracy of all models tested for various noise conditions. Accuracy was more than 98.8% at 1 dB SNR and the accuracy performance didn’t drop significantly as the noise levels increased. The model was less affected by the noise and signal degradation than other models (Refer Fig. 21).

Comprehensive visual comparison of all five models under noise-free conditions.

The variation of the performance between the models reflects the impact of the different architectural design choices. MobileNetV2 reached a baseline accuracy of 99.0% while still having a relatively low computational load. InceptionV3 scored a baseline accuracy of 98.2% and demonstrated consistency across the different folds. These findings suggest that convolutional design choices impact the trade-off between accuracy and computational cost differently.

Vision Transformer (ViT) achieved an average accuracy of only 82.98% and showed larger variability across the different folds with a coefficient of variation of 5.93. The detailed class-wise evaluation indicated that the model was more prone to confuse some fault classes with one another than others. For example, from a total of 450 samples, 380 Inner Race Fault cases were correctly identified, which is a very large number. However, along with that, there was also a mix-up between Ball Fault and Inner Race Fault, as a misclassification was noticed in the results.

Among the models, the accuracy of EfficientNetB0 displayed the smallest variation under noise corrupted conditions. The decrease in accuracy caused by noise, going from the baseline to 1 dB SNR, was about 1% point. The model’s performance was not significantly affected by changes in signal quality.

To rule out prejudiced comparisons, the models’ performances were evaluated in a comprehensive manner using the same architectural setup. Each model’s architecture consisted of a pre-trained backbone, hybrid residual blocks, and a common classification head. The rationale behind such an arrangement is that the differences in performance metrics are mainly due to the feature extractors rather than the classification layers.

Conclusion

This research work investigated a hybrid deep-learning framework for bearing fault diagnosis mainly focused on the aspects of accuracy, robustness, and computational efficiency. Five different model architectures, among them EfficientNetB0, MobileNetV2, and Vision Transformer (ViT), were compared by utilizing time-frequency analysis-generated spectrograms.

Out of the signal-processing techniques under consideration, the Constant-Q Non-Stationary Gabor Transform (CQ-NSGT) was found to be capable of producing clear representations of fault-related frequency components under various operating conditions.

The hybrid model approach fused frozen pre-trained backbones and custom residual blocks. Using this combination the model performances were notably better than when simply pre-trained models were used. EfficientNetB0 was the best in terms of baseline accuracy reaching 99.86% and was able to keep the performance above 98.8% at 1 dB SNR. MobileNetV2 gave a baseline accuracy of 99.0% at the same time it required much less computational resources, with 0.50 GFLOPs and a training time of roughly 121 s.

The Vision Transformer (ViT) recorded a lower accuracy mean and a higher variation in the set of validation folds. The decrease in the performance was found in the noisy condition leading to the conclusion that it is affected by the kind of data and the noise levels. In comparison with CNN-based models, ViT in the same application showed less consistent behavior.

The comparatively lower performance of the Vision Transformer (ViT) model in our experiments can be attributed to the known sensitivity of transformer-based architectures to model scale and data volume. As demonstrated in An Image is Worth 16 × 16 Words: Transformers for Image Recognition at Scale28, Vision Transformers achieve competitive or superior performance only when trained on very large datasets and with sufficiently large model configurations. When trained on smaller datasets without large-scale pretraining, ViTs underperform well-established CNN architectures such as ResNet28.

The authors explicitly demonstrate that the performance of transformers is very dependent on the size of the model and the amount of training data, and that small ViT variants trained on limited datasets do not perform at the level of CNN baselines. This is mainly explained by the absence of strong spatial inductive biases (e.g., locality and translation equivariance) that CNNs naturally encode. On the other hand, small ViT models have to get such structural priors from data through learning, hence they become more and more dependent on scale for effective representation learning.

In this research, a somewhat small ViT model was deliberately chosen so that the computational resources would be equivalent and the results comparable to the CNN baselines. Nevertheless, in line with the scaling trend reported by Dosovitskiy et al., it is very probable that the combination of the model with limited capacity and the moderate dataset size has prevented the transformer from acquiring sufficiently strong global dependencies thereby causing the model to achieve lower accuracy and higher variance.

This observed performance gap is in line with previous empirical findings reported in the literature and simply mirrors known scaling characteristics of Vision Transformers rather than representing a fundamental architectural limitation.

Therefore, based on the findings of this research, EfficientNetB0 can be considered a suitable solution when extremely high accuracy and stability are paramount, whereas MobileNetV2 may serve as a performance and computational efficiency trade-off option in cases of limited computational resources. To ensure that the variations revealed were mainly attributable to the feature extraction backbones, a standardized evaluation framework was used.

The dataset was collected from a controlled laboratory test rig that researchers used to conduct the experiment. In order to maintain the repeatability and experimental consistency, the researchers carefully controlled the operating conditions, fault types, and acquisition parameters. However, real industrial environments present additional challenges, gradually evolving fault severity, changes in lubrication, contamination from the environment, differences in sensor mounting stiffness and placement, etc.

These are all factors that can greatly affect the vibration signatures and can change the statistical characteristics of the measured signals in such a way that is not fully demonstrated by the laboratory conditions. Hence, thorough validation under various industrial operating scenarios is still an important task for future research.

Therefore, future research may broaden this work by gathering long-term field data from various installations and different operating regimes to assess cross-domain generalization. Adding multi-sensor data, like temperature, acoustic emission, or current signals, can give additional diagnostic information and thus improve the system’s tolerance to environmental variations. Moreover, addressing real-time deployment issues and model interpretability, chiefly through the use of explainable AI methods, would greatly facilitate their application in practice for industrial monitoring systems by offering an increase in transparency, trust, and operational reliability.
