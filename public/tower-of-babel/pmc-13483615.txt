Spatial and telehealth accessibility to eating disorder treatment in the United States: evidence from registry and LLM-augmented data
Authors: Lingbo Liu, Chuying Huo, Ariel L. Beccia, Tracy K. Richmond, S. Bryn Austin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdequate geographic access to eating disorder treatment is essential for timely care. Yet the distribution of in-person and telehealth programs, and their accessibility across urban and rural settings and across communities with different socioeconomic conditions, remains unclear.MethodsWe combined an official registry of eating disorder programs from the National Alliance for Eating Disorders with an AI-augmented web corpus built from U.S. domains using ISTARI.AI and large language models. After cleaning and linkage, we analyzed 328 registry centers for in-person care and an augmented set of 2,045 physical sites for proximity checks; telehealth programs were mainly limited to intensive outpatient and partial hospitalization. In-person accessibility was estimated at the census tract level with the two-step floating catchment area method (2SFCA) using program-based capacity. Telehealth accessibility used an unbounded two-step virtual catchment area (u2SVCA) that discounts demand by internet subscription. We also derived tract-level proximity indicators within 30 miles, namely nearest distance and the best available facility size. Population and covariates came from the American Community Survey and the 2020 Census. Multivariable ordinary least squares models related accessibility to median household income, rurality, education, insurance, and race or ethnicity. Correlation-based sensitivity analyses compared metrics across data sources and service regimes that either permit cross-state care or restrict care to within-state.ResultsLarge language models classified employee size from web summaries with an accuracy of 0.641 and Cohen’s kappa of 0.176. In-person access concentrates in metropolitan corridors, with longer distances and lower accessibility in rural tracts; allowing cross-state care improves proximity near many borders, while within-state constraints reduce reachable capacity across interior states. Telehealth per capita availability varies by state, and effective telehealth access declines after discounting by subscription, with lower values across parts of the South and interior West and the longest mean distances in Alaska. Regression models show strong rural and income gradients. Higher income and urban residence are associated with shorter distance and higher accessibility, while rurality is associated with poorer access for both in-person and telehealth measures. Conditional associations for Black and Latine population shares are small once socioeconomic factors are included. Proximity metrics from the AI-augmented set are moderately correlated with registry-based 2SFCA scores, and telehealth and in-person accessibility show lower but nonzero correlation, suggesting that telehealth and in-person measures capture partially distinct dimensions of potential access, while cross-source differences may also reflect coverage and measurement differences.ConclusionsEating disorder treatment access remains uneven across the United States. In-person capacity is sparse outside metropolitan regions, and telehealth expands potential reach but remains constrained by broadband subscription and state-based administrative boundaries. Combining registry data with AI-augmented provider information and paired in-person and telehealth accessibility models can help identify communities with limited access. These findings support policy efforts to expand cross-state practice pathways, improve broadband affordability and availability, and increase transparency in program capacity reporting.

Introduction

Eating disorders are common, impairing, and life-threatening psychiatric conditions that require timely, multi-level care [1]. Meta-analytic evidence shows elevated mortality across eating disorder diagnoses, with anorexia nervosa carrying the highest standardized mortality ratio [2]. Recent decision-analytic modeling suggests that by age 40 nearly one in five women and one in seven men in the United States will have experienced an eating disorder, and that expanding treatment coverage could avert deaths at the population level [3]. Despite this burden, access to specialty eating disorder care remains uneven across intensive outpatient (IOP), partial hospitalization (PHP), residential, and inpatient settings, where multidisciplinary teams including psychiatrists, therapists, physicians, and dietitians provide evidence-based services. Long travel distances and a persistent maldistribution of clinicians disadvantage many communities, particularly rural areas where a fifth of the population resides yet a small fraction of physicians practice [4]. Telehealth expanded rapidly during the COVID-19 era and may reduce some geographic barriers, but effective use depends on broadband availability, internet subscription, and digital readiness, which vary by income, rurality, and race or ethnicity [5]. Telehealth refers to the remote delivery of clinical services through video, telephone, or other digital communication technologies, including psychotherapy, psychiatric care, nutrition counseling, follow-up care, and selected intensive outpatient or partial hospitalization services. Telehealth can reduce travel burdens, expand the feasible reach of specialized providers, and support continuity of care, particularly for patients in rural or underserved communities. Telehealth was used in mental health care before COVID-19 and is now a routine modality for psychotherapy and medication management. For higher-acuity eating disorder services, however, telehealth often functions as a complement or partial substitute. It can support hybrid care, continuity of care, and follow-up, but it does not fully replace intensive in-person programs.

In health services research and health geography, access is commonly conceptualized as the fit between population needs and health-system resources across multiple dimensions, including availability (supply relative to need), spatial accessibility (travel separation), affordability (financial and time costs), accommodation (service organization), and acceptability (social and cultural fit) [6]. Building on these frameworks, we focus on spatial accessibility for in-person services and on a telehealth-specific affordability constraint (internet subscription) that conditions effective virtual access, while treating other contextual factors (e.g., insurance coverage) as correlates of disparities rather than as the access construct itself. Spatial accessibility measures are essential for quantifying and comparing in-person and virtual care. The two-step floating catchment area (2SFCA) method family is widely used to estimate geographic access to health services by linking supply, demand, and travel impedance [7–10]. Related work has adapted these ideas to telehealth. The two-step virtual catchment area (2SVCA) framework and its enhanced variants incorporate constraints for broadband availability and affordability to estimate effective virtual access [11–13]. However, data incompleteness remains a central challenge in mapping eating disorder services, since official registries are heterogeneous and many provider attributes are unknown or inconsistently reported.

Recent work in large language models (LLMs) and web-scale data extraction suggests that organizational information can be collected from public websites to complement registry data [14]. LLMs and related foundation models have been applied to clinical knowledge tasks, medical reasoning, information extraction, cohort phenotyping, and telehealth-oriented data curation [15, 16]. These applications remain subject to important concerns about accuracy, bias, transparency, and domain-specific validation, particularly when they are used in health equity or telehealth settings [17]. This motivates our use of LLMs to enrich provider attributes and classify employee size from web content, while explicitly evaluating classification performance [18, 19].

We therefore combined the National Alliance for Eating Disorders registry with an AI-augmented web dataset to improve national coverage of treatment sites and attributes, developed a simplified unconstrained 2SVCA variant tailored to ED telehealth, and assessed disparities by rurality and sociodemographic characteristics. We also evaluated the use of LLMs for provider-data enrichment and compared AI-augmented and registry-based accessibility estimates. These analyses address equity questions in the eating disorders literature, including differences in detection and treatment by race, ethnicity, insurance, and socioeconomic position.

Literature review

Disparities in eating disorders and their sociodemographic and rural correlations

Adequate geographic access to eating disorder (ED) treatment is important for timely care and recovery. Only a minority of those affected ultimately receive specialty care, and treatment seekers report structural and financial barriers that vary by identity and geography [20]. Studies among college populations document disparities in detection and treatment by race or ethnicity, sex, socioeconomic background, and weight status, pointing to referral and recognition biases that disadvantage minoritized groups [21]. Retrospective cohort data further indicate lower access and receipt of recommended care among publicly insured youth and youth of color [22]. The COVID-19 era saw sharp increases in adolescent ED admissions, highlighting the vulnerability of younger patients and stressors that exacerbate care gaps [23]. Authoritative reviews emphasize that EDs are disabling, deadly, and costly, and that outcomes depend on timely, multi-level care that is unevenly distributed [1]. Rurality compounds the problem because patients often travel long distances to scarce services, reflecting longstanding workforce maldistribution and travel-related barriers to mental health care [24, 25]. Socioeconomic position is also implicated, with a growing body of evidence challenging the notion that EDs are “diseases of affluence” and documenting burdens among lower-income groups [26]. These findings support equity analyses that consider rurality, income, insurance, and race or ethnicity together.

Measuring accessibility to eating disorder care

Spatial accessibility methods integrate supply, demand, and travel impedance to complement simpler, widely used, and interpretable metrics such as nearest-facility distance [27, 28]. The 2SFCA method family operationalizes availability as the ratio of facility capacity to surrounding demand and then accumulates these ratios at population locations within travel catchments, with numerous variants addressing distance decay and edge effects [29]. Recent reviews in health geography and global health underscore the method’s broad utility for heterogeneous health systems and its sensitivity to network and raster travel time assumptions [30, 31]. As telehealth expanded, broadband-aware extensions adapted the 2SFCA logic to virtual services. In this framing, telehealth can relax travel-impedance constraints for some services, but it remains contingent on administrative eligibility (e.g., licensure/service footprint), organizational capacity, and patient-level feasibility and preferences, which can preserve or reshape spatial inequities. The 2SVCA incorporates broadband availability and affordability at supply and demand nodes, acknowledging that effective telehealth requires adequate connectivity and paid subscription [32]. Enhanced formulations (E2SVCA) refine broadband functions and demographic adjustments to better approximate video visit requirements and heterogeneity in uptake. Evidence from U.S. oncology and national utilization studies shows that telehealth can reduce, but not eliminate, geographic inequities because digital divides in broadband coverage and subscription mirror existing socioeconomic and rural disparities [33]. Policy and research initiatives now emphasize rigorous, equity-aware evaluation of telehealth’s geographic benefits, with cancer telehealth research centers and federal guidance calling for scalable, reproducible measures of access [34]. For eating disorder services, these advances are directly relevant because levels of care differ in intensity, registries incompletely capture capacity, and administrative telehealth service areas are defined at the state level, requiring tailored modeling choices and sensitivity analyses.

Large language models for health data curation and augmentation

LLMs encode broad clinical knowledge and have shown encouraging performance on multi-dataset medical question-answering tasks, while human evaluations reveal limits that require domain-specific assessment [15]. Work on generalist medical AI has also argued that foundation models can support multiple modalities and tasks with limited supervision, including the curation and synthesis of health information when paired with governance and evaluation frameworks [16]. In diagnostic reasoning, GPT-4 achieves similar accuracy on complex clinical cases relative to physicians reading case challenges, illustrating potential to augment, not replace, expert workflows [18]. For data curation, LLM can accelerate electronic health record phenotyping by drafting cohort definitions and extracting variables, and they show promise in structured extraction from semi-structured notes, although calibration and error modes warrant caution [35, 36]. At the same time, comparative studies caution that current LLM may underperform purpose-built predictors on clinical forecasting tasks, especially when fairness, calibration, and privacy-aware transformations are considered [17]. Global guidance urges responsible use of large multimodal models in health, emphasizing transparency, safety, and mitigation of bias, particularly where telehealth intersects with infrastructure constraints and equity goals [37]. Recent applications also use LLM to summarize medical records and surface overlooked patterns, again highlighting opportunities for quality improvement coupled with the need for rigorous validation against ground truth [38]. These studies support the use of LLMs to enrich provider attributes from public websites and to estimate employee size as a capacity proxy in sparse registries, provided that performance is evaluated with confusion matrices, agreement statistics, and error audits.

Data and methodology

Figure 1 shows the workflow for measuring geographic access to eating disorder treatment in the United States. We assembled supply from the National Alliance registry (www.findEDhelp.com) and from an AI-augmented web dataset built with LLM; tagged programs as in person or telehealth; linked the two sources; validated and imputed missing attributes such as employee size; and created an integrated set of physical sites and telehealth service areas. For in-person care, census tract-level population defines demand, and accessibility is estimated with the 2SFCA model. Telehealth accessibility is evaluated at the state level, because clinician licensure and service areas are recorded by state, using a simplified 2SVCA with a broadband affordability adjustment. We then evaluated accessibility scores by rurality and by sociodemographic strata.

Data

National alliance registry (findEDhelp) as supply data

We used the National Alliance for Eating Disorders registry as the official reference for both in-person and telehealth programs. Each entry was tagged as telehealth, physical, or hybrid. Entries were tagged as telehealth when the center name or the registry label indicated virtual or remote delivery. Program counts by level of care were retained for capacity modeling. The registry included three age groups: children (0–12 years), adolescents (13–17 years), and adults (18 years or older). We used intensive outpatient programs (IOP) and partial hospitalization (PHP) for telehealth as they are most consistently reported for virtual delivery, although some institutions also offer outpatient care. The registry listed 387 centers in total, of which 328 had in-person programs and 160 offered telehealth programs. Centers may appear in both categories. On the supply side, this study considers the eating disorder treatment centers with brick-and-mortar sites recorded in the National Alliance for Eating Disorders Treatment Center & Practitioner Directory for each state (National Alliance for Eating Disorder, 2022). Data on the levels of care for eating disorder treatment available and patient volume capacity were extracted from this same directory. To compute the overall capacity of a treatment center offering multiple levels of care, we used an hour-based weighting system that reflects the relative intensity of each level. To derive an hour-based weight for each level of care, we calculated the full-time-equivalent contribution of each treatment level by dividing the average number of treatment hours provided per day by the 24 h in a day. The levels of eating disorder treatment covered in this study were Residential treatment (full-day), Partial hospitalization (6–11 h per day), and Intensive outpatient treatment (3 h per day). Residential treatment was weighted as 1.00, reflecting 24 h of care per day. Partial hospitalization was weighted as 0.375, based on the midpoint of 6–11 h of care per day divided by 24 h. Intensive outpatient treatment was weighted as 0.125, based on 3 h of care per day divided by 24 h. The total capacity of each treatment center was then calculated by multiplying the maximum number of patients allowed at each level of care by the corresponding weight and summing these weighted capacities across all available levels of care. Figure 2a maps the 328 in-person centers by level of care. Figure 2b visualizes cross-state telehealth service for IOP and partial hospitalization program (PHP). For programs that serve multiple states, capacity is divided evenly across served states.

WebAI data with LLM for augmented supply data

To improve national coverage beyond the registry, we used a WebAI platform (ISTARI.AI) to collect website text from domains registered in the United States and to identify organizations whose pages contained the phrase “eating disorder treatment center.” For each candidate record we extracted domain, organization name, latitude, longitude, address, and an LLM-generated summary. The initial harvest yielded 3,505 records. We cleaned the corpus by removing entries outside the United States, removing entries with missing names or insufficient text, and deduplicating locations by retaining the record with the largest employee class. We then used LLM (Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite) to classify each record’s relevance to eating disorder treatment and to assign the same employee size classes as the known values, 0 to 10, 11 to 50, 51 to 250, or 250 or more. Predicted sizes were checked against known values in the corpus. After linkage to the registry and removal of duplicates, the augmented database contained approximately 2,045 unique physical sites. Population, distance, and sociodemographic covariates

Population counts were obtained at the census tract level from the American Community Survey in 2020. Using the 2020 Census Demographic and Housing Characteristics (DHC) file, we constructed age specific populations to compute accessibility for distinct groups. The DHC file included three age groups: children aged 0 to 12 years, adolescents aged 13 to 17 years, and adults aged 18 years or older. We calculated accessibility separately for each group. We assembled tract-level covariates that are used for disparity analyses, including racial and ethnic composition, median household income, health insurance coverage, educational attainment, and internet subscription and affordability. Rurality was defined using the 2020 Census housing-density thresholds in the U.S. Census Bureau Urban Area Criteria: urban areas had ≥ 425 housing units per square mile, low-density areas had 200–424 units per square mile, and rural areas had < 200 units per square mile. These categories were used to characterize the rurality gradient.

Methodology

LLM application for estimating employee size from web content

We applied Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite to organization summaries generated by ISTARI.AI and required a strict JSON response with two classification fields and one reasoning field: an eating disorders treatment (EDT) involvement flag that indicates whether the entity provides mental health or eating disorder services, and an employee size class chosen from 0 to 10, 11 to 50, 51 to 250, or 250 or more. Outputs were parsed and standardized; invalid JSON triggered a single requery; and contradictory text such as disclaimers that no care is provided led to exclusion from the supply set. For records that were relevant but missing employee size, the predicted size class was used as the imputed value that feeds capacity construction.

Results

Spatial accessibility based on AI-augmented dataset

LLM-based employee size estimation

We evaluated the LLM on 1,769 organizations with known employee-size labels across four classes. Overall accuracy was 0.641, and Cohen’s kappa was 0.176, indicating only slight agreement beyond chance under substantial class imbalance. Figure 3 visualizes the mapping between true and estimated classes with a Sankey diagram. The model identifies the extremes reasonably well: class 1 achieved high recall and precision (recall 0.809, precision 0.828), and class 4 showed high recall but low precision (recall 0.846, precision 0.162), reflecting frequent over-assignment into class 4. In contrast, class 2 was rarely predicted at all (29 predictions for 353 true cases), resulting in very low recall of 0.025 despite high specificity of 0.986. Class 3 exhibited low precision of 0.056 and modest recall of 0.295, with substantial spillovers from other classes. These statistics are summarized in Table 1 and explain the gap between nominal accuracy and low kappa: the model tended to assign many medium-sized organizations to class 1 or to larger classes, while using class 2 infrequently.

We report both measures under two service regimes: cross-state travel permitted and within-state only.

Sensitivity analyses and sociodemographic associations of accessibility

Correlation-based sensitivity analysis

To assess robustness across methods and data sources, we examined Pearson correlations among proximity measures derived from the AI-augmented dataset and gravity-based accessibility measures computed from the National Alliance registry in Fig. 8a. A star denotes the within-state scenario; unstarred metrics allow cross-state care.

Figure 8a shows that same-metric correlations between cross-state and within-state variants are high. Nearest distance has r = 0.932; the best available size within 30 and 45 miles has r from 0.830 to 0.966; 2SFCA at 30 and 45 miles has r from 0.664 to 0.915. Telehealth metrics for IOP and PHP are nearly identical with r = 0.983. Proximity measures from the AI-augmented supply show moderate correlation with 2SFCA from the National Alliance registry, r from 0.275 to 0.436, indicating shared but non-identical spatial patterns. Correlations between 2SVCA and 2SFCA are lower, r from 0.175 to 0.258, suggesting that telehealth reflects a partially distinct access dimension; however, differences may also arise from coverage, reporting, and measurement differences between modalities and data sources. Nearest distance is negatively correlated with the best available size, reflecting that tracts closer to centers tend to reach larger facilities.

Socioeconomic gradients are consistent across indicators as shown in Fig. 8b. Higher median household income and higher educational attainment are associated with shorter distances and higher accessibility scores, with r from − 0.278 to − 0.124 for distance and r from 0.214 to 0.265 for the other indicators. Rurality correlates with poorer access, with r = 0.399 for distance and r from − 0.363 to − 0.155 for the other indicators. Correlations by race and ethnicity are small. White share is weakly associated with longer distance and slightly lower accessibility; Latine share shows small positive associations for some indicators; Black share is near zero or slightly negative. These results support the use of 2SFCA and telehealth 2SVCA as complementary measures, with proximity indicators serving as robustness checks.

Regression results linking accessibility to sociodemographic factors

The models show a strong and consistent rural gradient (Table 4). Relative to urban tracts, low-density (LD) and rural-area (RA) tracts have longer travel distances and lower accessibility across all outcomes. For Nearest Distance the coefficients are 0.336 and 0.793 in the Black model and 0.345 and 0.824 in the Latine model. For Nearest Max-30 Miles, the coefficients are − 0.261 and − 0.729 and for 2SFCA 30 Miles they are − 0.323 and − 0.508. Telehealth access shows the same pattern with 2SVCA-IOP at − 0.190 and − 0.309. Income gradients are also clear. Higher median household income is associated with shorter distances (− 0.185 and − 0.167) and higher access for the other three outcomes, with coefficients from 0.151 to 0.219. Education generally moves in the same direction, most consistently for shorter distance in the Black model (− 0.106) and for higher 2SVCA in both models (0.016 to 0.071). Insurance has mixed but mainly positive associations, especially for 2SVCA-IOP in the Latine model (0.158).

Conditional on income, education, insurance, and rurality, the race and ethnicity terms are small in magnitude. Black share is associated with slightly shorter distance (− 0.080) and small changes in the other outcomes, including a modest negative association with 2SVCA IOP (− 0.040). Latine share shows slightly shorter distance (− 0.099) and small positive associations with Nearest Max 30 Miles, 2SFCA 30 Miles, and 2SVCA IOP (0.060, 0.116, and 0.074). Taken together, these models indicate that rurality and income are the dominant correlates of accessibility, while racial and ethnic composition has smaller conditional effects once socioeconomic factors are included.

Discussion

Using national registry and web-derived provider data, we examined how in-person and telehealth access to eating disorder treatment varies across the United States. The results show that access is shaped by provider location, rurality, socioeconomic conditions, internet subscription, and state-based service boundaries. Telehealth broadens potential reach, but it does not remove these barriers; instead, it operates alongside in-person care as a separate and still uneven form of access.

The in-person results show that EDT capacity is concentrated in metro areas, producing a clear rural gradient in proximity-based and 2SFCA accessibility. This pattern is consistent with longstanding evidence that rural residents face longer travel and more sparse specialty supply [4, 25]. Previous studies have documented barriers related to socioeconomic position, insurance status, race or ethnicity, and treatment-seeking experiences, but fewer have mapped the national geography of treatment supply itself [20–22, 26]. Cross-state travel assumptions matter: allowing patients to seek care across borders improves proximity metrics and increases the maximum available capacity within 30 miles for many tracts, while within-state constraints reduce effective choice sets in border and interior states. These patterns are relevant for referral networks and point to the value of interstate licensure flexibility and provider compacts [5, 13].

The telehealth results also show substantial interstate variation, even among programs that advertise multi-state coverage. After discounting for internet subscription, parts of the South and interior West have lower effective virtual access, consistent with evidence that broadband infrastructure and subscription shape telemedicine use at the community level [11, 13, 32–34]. Telehealth therefore does not erase geographic disparities. It shifts part of the access problem from travel distance to digital access and administrative eligibility. Policy responses could include broadband investment, affordability supports, digital-navigation services, and cross-state practice pathways for low-access communities.

A further contribution is the combined use of an official treatment registry and an LLM-augmented web corpus. Provider registries often lack complete information on site locations, service types, and capacity-related attributes. Web-based data and LLM-assisted extraction can help fill some of these gaps, but they require validation and careful interpretation, especially when used in equity-focused analyses [17–38]. In this study, the AI-assisted corpus increased the number of geocoded in-person sites and allowed us to compare proximity measures from the augmented dataset with 2SFCA scores from the registry. The moderate correlations suggest that the two data sources capture related but non-identical aspects of access. The modest agreement in LLM-based employee-size classification also supports a cautious approach: LLM-derived information should supplement registry data rather than replace it.

The equity patterns are clear. Higher median household income and urban residence are associated with better access, whereas rurality is associated with poorer access across both in-person and telehealth measures. These results are consistent with prior work documenting disparities in eating disorder diagnosis and treatment by race or ethnicity, insurance coverage, and socioeconomic position [21, 22]. Our analysis adds a geographic explanation for these disparities by showing how provider distribution, licensing boundaries, and digital affordability shape the set of providers available before clinical triage begins.

Several limitations should be noted. Distances are approximated using census-tract centroids rather than population-weighted centroids; this can introduce positional error, particularly in large and internally heterogeneous rural tracts, and may modestly over- or under-estimate travel distances for some communities. Registry capacity data are more complete for certain levels of care and for intensive outpatient telehealth, and our full-time-equivalent weighting scheme, while transparent, may not fully capture throughput differences across programs. In addition, the registry data are self-reported, and some treatment centers, including academic medical centers, may be underrepresented. The u2SVCA abstraction treats program supply as statewide while discounting on the demand side by internet subscription; finer service geographies and clinician licensing data could improve these estimates. LLM classification depended on public web content and a single model configuration. Because our LLM inclusion prompt was intentionally broad and did not require explicit mention of eating disorders, the augmented provider set may be over-inclusive and may include some general mental health facilities. Future work should compare models, integrate few-shot examples, and test domain-specific small language models. Finally, our cross-sectional design quantifies access rather than realized utilization. Linking these measures to claims or appointment data will be critical for causal interpretation and for evaluating policy changes in licensure and broadband.

Conclusion

Geographic access to eating disorder treatment in the United States remains uneven. In-person capacity is concentrated in metropolitan areas and remains limited in many rural areas. Telehealth expands potential reach, but its benefits are constrained by broadband subscription and state-based administrative boundaries. By combining an official registry with an AI-augmented provider corpus and applying paired in-person and telehealth accessibility models, this study offers a national workflow for measuring physical and virtual access. The results point to several priorities: expanding cross-state practice pathways for mental health providers, improving broadband availability and affordability, and increasing transparency in program capacity reporting. LLMs may help address gaps in provider data, but their outputs need validation, auditing, and clear documentation before being used in equity-sensitive health research.
