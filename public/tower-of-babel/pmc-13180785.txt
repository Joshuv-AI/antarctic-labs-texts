An Aberrant Internal Carotid Artery Mimicking a Pharyngeal Mass: A Rare Cause of Dysphagia
Authors: Sushil Raj Shrestha, Abhisek Jha, Shiva Bhusan Pandit, Prabha Jha, Manash Jha, Aastha Bhattarai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAberrant course of the internal carotid artery (ICA) within the pharyngeal wall is an uncommon vascular anomaly that can present with symptoms mimicking structural or neoplastic lesions. Recognition of this variation is critical to avoid catastrophic complications during diagnostic or therapeutic interventions. Case Presentation: An 89‐year‐old woman presented with a one‐year history of gradually progressive, painless, and persistent dysphagia. Clinical examination was unremarkable. Flexible laryngoscopy revealed a bulge in the left lateral pharyngeal wall just above the level of the vocal cords with pooling of secretions in the vallecula and the left pyriform sinus. Contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CECT) of the neck demonstrated an aberrant ICA causing the submucosal protrusion. Given the high risk of hemorrhage, surgical intervention was deferred. The patient was managed conservatively with counseling and ergotherapy focused on swallowing rehabilitation. This case underscores the importance of considering rare vascular anomalies, including aberrant ICA, in the differential diagnosis of dysphagia. Preoperative imaging evaluation is crucial before attempting any invasive procedure in the pharyngeal region to prevent potentially fatal vascular injury.

Introduction

Clinically, dysphagia is defined as an impairment in swallowing resulting from abnormal delay in transit of either a solid or a liquid bolus from the oral cavity to the stomach. The underlying etiologies can be broadly classified into mechanical obstruction and motility disorders. Mechanical obstruction usually results from lesions obstructing the lumen or causing external compression of the pharyno‐esophageal tract. Common lesions include Zenker diverticulum, esophageal webs, oropharyngeal tumors, abscesses, and goiters. Rarely, vascular anomalies such as aberrant internal carotid artery (ICA) or subclavian artery, aorto‐esophageal fistula, C3‐C4 cervical osteophytes, esophageal tears, aortic aneurysm, called dysphagia aortica, may also cause mechanical dysphagia [1, 2]. Dysphagia lusoria, most commonly caused by aortic arch abnormalities such as aberrant right subclavian artery or common carotid arteries, represents the most frequent vascular cause leading to mechanical dysphagia. In contrast, secondary dysphagia due to aberrant or tortuous ICA—whether coiled, kinked, or medially displaced—is exceedingly rare [3]. Here, we present a case of a 89‐year‐old woman with progressive dysphagia caused by an aberrant ICA presenting as a submucosal bulge in the left lateral pharyngeal wall.

Case History/Examination

An 89‐year‐old woman presented to our otolaryngology clinic with a one‐year‐long history of gradually progressive, painless and persistent dysphagia that had begun to cause significant distress and social embarrassment. She reported frequent incidents of food sticking to her throat, occasionally leading to choking, especially with solid meals. She had not experienced regurgitation of undigested food, significant and unintentional weight loss, odynophagia, heartburn, abdominal pain, limb weakness, diplopia, or dysarthria. Additionally, she denied any co‐morbidities, smoking or alcohol addiction, as well as past head and neck surgeries. Focused head and neck examination revealed no abnormalities, and systemic review was unremarkable.

Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment)

Based on the history of persistent and progressive dysphagia along with an unremarkable physical examination, a mechanical cause of dysphagia was speculated. A flexible laryngoscopic evaluation was performed, which revealed a bulge in the left lateral pharyngeal wall just above the level of the vocal cords along with pooling of secretions in the vallecula and left pyriform sinus, confirming the mechanical nature of obstruction. (Figure 1) To further delineate the cause, a contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CECT) of the neck was ordered. It demonstrated a tortuous medially displaced left internal carotid artery. The vessel coursed medially into the parapharyngeal/retropharyngeal space, producing a submucosal protrusion of the left lateral pharyngeal wall. The carotid origin from the great vessels was otherwise preserved. No aneurysm or dissection was noted. (Figure 2) As surgical intervention posed a significant risk of catastrophic hemorrhage due to the aberrant vascular anatomy, conservative management was deemed appropriate.

Results and Conclusions (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

Comprehensive counseling was provided regarding the nature of her condition and the possible risks of interventions. The patient was advised to pursue ergotherapy aimed at improving swallowing function and coping strategies. The patient expressed relief upon learning the benign but anatomically significant cause of her symptoms. She appreciated the conservative approach and reported gradual improvement in swallowing function following ergotherapy.

Discussion

The ICA develops from the combination of third pharyngeal arch branches and the cranial segment of paired dorsal aortae around the 24th day of gestation [4]. ICA, a terminal branch of the common carotid artery, originates at the level of the upper border of the lamina of thyroid cartilage and has a relatively straight course in the neck. Congenital variations—including kinks, coils, loops, S or C‐shaped tortuosities—are frequent, often asymptomatic, and may occur in about 10%–40% of individuals. Rarely, an aberrant ICA can produce clinical symptoms such as globus sensation in the throat, dysarthria, hoarseness, upper respiratory distress or progressive dysphagia—a finding similar to our case [5]. Therefore, a detailed understanding of ICA anatomy is essential before undertaking any procedures to avoid potentially catastrophic complications.

Endoscopy of the upper aero‐digestive tract remains the primary investigative modality in assessing pharyngeal pathology. Videofluroscopy and functional endoscopic evaluation of swallowing are considered the diagnostic gold standard for oropharyngeal dysphagia owing to their ability to provide detailed visualization of anatomical structures and accurately assess bolus penetration and aspiration [6]. In contrast, due to the unavailability of advanced imaging modalities at our center, we proceeded with flexible laryngoscopy, which revealed a submucosal protrusion in the left lateral pharyngeal wall just above the level of the vocal cords, thereby demonstrating a mechanical cause of dysphagia. Cross‐sectional imaging modalities such as CECT, Magnetic Resonance Imaging, or angiography are essential for characterizing atypical bulges in the laryngeal or pharyngeal wall as they provide excellent and detailed information regarding vascular structures and surrounding anatomy [7, 8]. Given its cost‐effectiveness, we selected CECT, which allowed us to confirm the diagnosis of an aberrant ICA presenting as a submucosal bulge in the lateral pharyngeal wall. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of an aberrant ICA showcasing as a submucosal protrusion in the left lateral pharyngeal wall [9]. Several hybrid surgical techniques such as endovascular exclusion of aberrant artery and re‐establishment of arterial continuity, mediastinoscopic aberrant artery ligation and clipping have been described for management of dysphagia lusoria [10]. However, conservative management is generally preferred even for symptomatic aberrant ICA, considering the high risk of catastrophic hemorrhages. Additionally, patient counseling regarding the potential risk of vessel injury even with routine upper airway procedures such as nasogastric tube insertion, intubation, as well as with more complex surgeries such as tonsillectomy remains cornerstone of conservative management in the cases of symptomatic aberrant ICA [11]. Deglutition therapy—consisting of effortful swallow, Mendelsohn maneuver, tongue pressure resistance exercises, chin tuck against resistance and neuromuscular electrical stimulation– has been shown to be beneficial in management of dysphagia, particularly in inoperable cases [12, 13]. In a similar manner, considering the patient preference and the inoperability of the condition, our patient underwent ergotherapy and subsequently reported substantial improvement in swallowing function, demonstrating the effectiveness of conservative management.

Conclusion

An aberrant ICA should be considered in patients presenting with unexplained dysphagia and pharyngeal submucosal bulges. Recognition of this vascular variant is vital to prevent fatal complications during diagnostic or therapeutic procedures. Noninvasive imaging remains the cornerstone for diagnosis, while conservative management in the form of ergotherapy is generally safe and effective.
