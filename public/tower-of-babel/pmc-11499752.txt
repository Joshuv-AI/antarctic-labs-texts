In-utero transfer, survival-focused care and survival to 28-days at 22-24 weeks’ gestation pre- and post- implementation of an extreme prematurity management guideline in Victoria, Australia
Authors: Hannah G Gordon, Alexis Shub, Michael J Stewart, Stefan C Kane, Jeanie LY Cheong, Calum T Roberts, James Holberton, Rosemarie A Boland
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCare for infants born at 22–24 weeks’ gestation varies globally, with an increasing willingness to provide survival-focused (‘active’) care for infants born at 22 weeks’ gestation in recent years. This study aims to report changes in care for infants born at 22–24 weeks before and after the introduction of a statewide guideline for extreme prematurity (EP).MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted, including all live births at 22–24 weeks in tertiary perinatal centres from 1 January 2015 to 31 December 2022 in Victoria, Australia. Medical records were reviewed to obtain antenatal history and perinatal outcomes. Data on in utero referral and transfer to tertiary perinatal centres were sourced from the statewide perinatal emergency retrieval service (Paediatric Infant Perinatal Emergency Retrieval, PIPER) database. Changes in PIPER referrals and transfers, survival-focused care and survival at 28 days preguideline and postguideline were assessed using logistic regression.ResultsFollowing the guideline, at 22 weeks’ gestation, there was an increase in referrals to PIPER and a 3.31 (95% CI 1.84 to 5.95) higher likelihood of in utero transfer to tertiary centres.Following the guideline, infants had a 6.67 (95% CI 1.40 to 31.72) higher likelihood of receiving survival-focused care at 22 weeks, and a 5.57 (95% CI 1.22 to 25.44) higher likelihood at 23 weeks. All 24-week live births received survival-focused care at birth. The 28-day survival for infants who received survival-focused care was unchanged preguideline and postguideline.ConclusionFollowing the publication of the EP guideline in Victoria, in utero referrals and transfers at 22 weeks’ gestation have increased, as has survival-focused management of inborn live births at 22–24 weeks.Keywords: Neonatology, Resuscitation, Infant, Health services research, Mortality

Introduction

Births at 22–24 weeks’ gestation represent a small proportion of all births (0.3% in Australia in 2021),1 yet they pose significant challenges in obstetric and neonatal care. Across high-income countries, there is wide variation in the approach to the management of births at 22–24 weeks’ gestation, and hence there is wide variation in infant outcomes.2 3

In recent years, many countries have developed guidelines for obstetric and neonatal care of births at these gestations, with the aim of improving infant survival while balancing the risks of major neurosensory disability.4 5

In 2006, a consensus statement was published in New South Wales and the Australian Capital Territory, defining the lower limit for survival-focused care (‘active management’) to be 23+0 weeks’ gestation.6 However, as infant outcomes continue to improve in high-income countries,3 the lower gestational age limit for providing survival-focused care has shifted to include births at 22 weeks. In 2019, the British Association of Perinatal Medicine published their guideline for the management of births prior to 27 weeks’ gestation, defining 22+0 as the lower limit for survival-focused care, changed from 23+0 weeks previously.4

In December 2020, Safer Care Victoria published the first statewide guideline for the management of extremely preterm births at 22–24 weeks’ gestation in Victoria, Australia.7 In the guideline, survival-focused care can be considered from 22+0 weeks’ gestation onwards, with 22+0 and 23+6 weeks’ gestation falling within the Zone of Parental Discretion, where joint parent and clinician decision-making is recommended to guide care for births at these gestations.7 Survival-focused care is generally recommended for births at 24+0 weeks’ gestation onwards.

The impact of the extreme prematurity (EP) guideline in changing obstetric and neonatal practice in Victoria has not yet been evaluated. Therefore, we aimed to report changes in in utero referrals and transfers to tertiary perinatal centres, as well as rates of survival-focused care of live births in tertiary centres (‘inborn’) before and after the publication of the EP guideline. We also sought to determine whether there has been a change in 28-day survival in inborn infants who received survival-focused care prepublication and postpublication of the guideline.

Methods

This was a retrospective population-based cohort study using data sourced from the Paediatric Infant Perinatal Emergency Retrieval (PIPER) database, the Victorian Perinatal Data Collection (VPDC) and all tertiary perinatal centres in Victoria. PIPER provide a statewide perinatal consultation and obstetric and neonatal bed-finding service for all non-tertiary public and private hospitals in the state. The VPDC held at the Victorian Government Department of Health collects data on all births in the state of Victoria and is used for local and National reporting of perinatal outcomes.1 8

Inclusion criteria

Births were eligible for inclusion if they occurred in Victoria between 22+0 and 24+6 weeks’ gestation. The two study cohorts were births between 1 January 2015 and 31 December 2020 (pre-EP guideline) and 1 January 2021 and 31 December 2022 (post-EP guideline).

Exclusion criteria

Infants born alive following a termination of pregnancy were excluded. Live births from two private hospitals colocated with a tertiary perinatal centre were not included, nor were births from one hospital that became a tertiary centre in 2019 and commenced caring for infants born prior to 25 weeks’ gestation after the study period.

Participants and data collection

In utero referrals and transfers

The cohort included all women referred to PIPER, of whom some were subsequently transferred to a tertiary perinatal centre at 22–24 weeks.

Victorian birth data

The denominators for all 22–24 weeks’ live births in the state of Victoria until 2022 were obtained from the VPDC. These included births in non-tertiary (‘outborn’ births) and all tertiary (‘inborn’ births) hospitals.

Survival-focused care and survival to 28 days

Perinatal data for all live births between 22+0 and 24+6 weeks’ gestation between 1 January 2015 and 31 December 2022 were sourced from three tertiary perinatal centres in Victoria: Mercy Hospital for Women, Royal Women’s Hospital and Monash Health. Maternal demographics and clinical care details were sourced from medical records by a single investigator (HG). Infant demographics, delivery room resuscitation interventions, survival to neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission and survival to 28 days were sourced from medical records.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed by using Stata V.18 (StataCorp). Characteristics of study participants are presented as mean (SD) and n (%). Changes in PIPER referrals and transfers, survival-focused care and survival at 28 days were assessed preguideline and postguideline using logistic regression.

Ethics was approved by the Royal Children’s Hospital Ethics Committee (HREC 83543), with Site Specific Approval and a Research Collaboration Agreement in place between all participating sites. A waiver of consent was approved for this study. The use and publication of Victorian birth data in table 1 was approved by the Consultative Council on Obstetric and Paediatric Mortality and Morbidity (ID 5732). This research adheres to the RECORD statement for the reporting of observational studies using routinely collected health data (online supplemental table S1).

Patient and public involvement

Although patients were not involved in the design of the study, qualitative research indicates that parents place a high value on the anticipated outcomes for extremely preterm infants.9

Results

There were 705 live births overall at 22–24 weeks’ gestation between 2015 and 2022 in the state of Victoria (table 1). This included 427 infants liveborn in three tertiary perinatal centres from 365 pregnancies. Among these, there were 60 sets of twins and 3 sets of triplets.

In utero referrals and transfers

Over the study period, there was an increase in referrals to PIPER at 22 weeks’ gestation from 19 referrals in 2015 to 40 in 2022 (table 2, online supplemental appendix figure S1). Following the introduction of the guideline, referrals at 22 weeks’ gestation represented 3.1% of all referrals to PIPER, compared with 1.7% preguideline (OR 1.82 (95% CI 1.38 to 2.41)). The proportion of referrals at 23 and 24 weeks’ gestation was similar preguideline and postguideline.

Following publication of the guideline, women referred to PIPER at 22 weeks’ gestation had 3.31 (95% CI 1.84 to 5.95) higher likelihood of subsequent transfer to a tertiary centre compared with preguideline (online supplemental appendix figure S2). Comparatively, women referred to PIPER at 24 weeks’ gestation were less likely to be transferred to a tertiary centre compared with 2015–2019 (OR 0.59 (95% CI 0.38 to 0.92)). There was no change in the likelihood of transfer at 23 weeks’ gestation before and after the publication of the guideline.

Maternal demographic and clinical characteristics are described in table 3. Most women in the cohort presented with spontaneous onset of labour, with or without preterm rupture of membranes. Prior to publication of the EP guideline, 45% of women gave birth by caesarean section, compared with 59% following the publication of the guideline.

Infant demographics

Following the guideline, infants born at 22 weeks’ gestation had a 6.67 (95% CI 1.40 to 31.72) higher likelihood of receiving survival-focused care than those born prior to publication of the guideline (table 2; online supplemental appendix figure S3). This increase was also observed at 23 weeks’ gestation (OR 5.57 (95% CI 1.22 to 25.44)), and at 24 weeks’ gestation, all live births received survival-focused care following the guideline.

There was no significant change in infant survival to 28 days prepublication and postpublication of the guideline (table 2; online supplemental appendix figure S4). At 22 weeks’ gestation, one infant (33%) survived to 28 days prior to publication of the guideline, compared with three infants (38%) post (table 4). All four infants born at 22 weeks’ gestation and surviving to 28 days were exposed to at least two doses of antenatal corticosteroids and magnesium sulfate prior to birth.

At 23 weeks’ gestation, 51% of infants survived to 28 days prior to publication of the guideline, compared with 60% postguideline, and at 24 weeks’ gestation, 82% of infants survived to 28 days prior to publication of the guideline, compared with 84% postguideline.

Discussion

In recent years, there has been an increase in the rates of survival-focused management of infants born at 22–24 weeks’ gestation across high-income countries.10,12 This study has found similar trends in Victoria, Australia.

We found the number of referrals to the PIPER statewide perinatal emergency retrieval service at 22 weeks’ gestation more than doubled over the study period, from 19 referrals in 2015 to 40 in 2022. There was more than a sixfold increase in the number of subsequent transfers in utero at 22 weeks’ gestation to tertiary centres facilitated by PIPER, from 4 transfers in 2015 to 25 in utero transfers in 2022. The same increase was not seen at 23 or at 24 weeks’ gestation. This may be due to variations in the proportion of accepted referrals across individual years. Specifically, 2021 was characterised by an unusually low proportion of referrals accepted for transfer (58%), perhaps in an attempt to minimise patient transfer during the COVID-19 pandemic. Of note, however, there were no formal COVID-19-related changes in guidelines around timing or gestation of PIPER transfers in Victoria.

We observed a significant increase in the proportion of inborn 22-week liveborn infants who received survival-focused care following publication of the EP guideline, from 13% in 2015–2020 to 50% in 2021–2022. Similar trends were also seen at 23 and at 24 weeks’ gestation. This is in keeping with recent population-based data from England and Wales, where a threefold increase in survival-focused care at 22 weeks’ gestation was observed following the publication of the British Association of Perinatal Medicine consensus guideline.4 11

Although there was an increase in the proportion of inborn live births who received survival-focused care, there was no change in infant survival to 28 days following the introduction of the guideline. This may be related to the lack of change over time in the proportion of infants exposed to two doses of antenatal corticosteroids. The benefit of antenatal corticosteroids at these early gestations is well described.13 14 However, the administration of a complete course may not always be feasible in unanticipated or precipitate preterm labour. In our cohort, 78% of infants born at 24 weeks’ gestation received a complete course of antenatal corticosteroids following the guideline, compared with 53% of infants at 22–23 weeks’ gestation. Concerted efforts to administer antenatal corticosteroids should, therefore, be a priority in at-risk pregnancies at these early gestations where survival-focused care is sought.

Recent population-based data from Sweden compared outcomes across two time points (2004–2007 and 2014–2016) and also noted no change in the proportion of extremely preterm infants exposed to antenatal corticosteroids.15 This study did, however, observe an increase in survival to 1 year. A longer follow-up of the Victorian cohort to characterise long-term survival and outcomes for these infants in currently underway.

Given that there were only four infants born at 22 weeks’ gestation who survived to 28 days, the study is underpowered to identify a significant change over time in this cohort. When considering infants born alive at 23 and 24 weeks’ gestation, 28-day survival rates are consistent with outcome data published at comparable centres in England and France.2 16

A key finding of our study was that no infant born at 22 weeks’ gestation survived to 28 days unless they were exposed to at least two doses of antenatal corticosteroids (a ‘complete course’) and magnesium sulfate prior to birth. Although magnesium sulfate is associated with reduced rates of cerebral palsy in infants born extremely preterm, it does not confer a survival benefit.17 It may be that magnesium sulfate in our cohort reflects concurrent measures associated with improved survival, such as having received a complete course of corticosteroids prior to birth.

The Safer Care Victoria Extreme Prematurity Guideline proposes that joint parent and clinician decision-making is recommended when considering survival-focused care for births between 22+0 and 23+6 weeks’ gestation.7 Following the publication of the guideline, 95% of inborn live births at 23 weeks’ gestation received survival-focused care compared with 78% preguideline. It is possible that establishing a Zone of Parental Discretion that begins at 22+0 weeks’ gestation creates a greater sense of parental obligation to elect for survival-focused care for infants born at 23 weeks’ gestation, and influences counselling by the care team.18 This requires further investigation of factors influencing parental decision-making at these early gestational ages.

Study strengths and limitations

This is the first study examining temporal trends in rates of in utero transfer, survival-focused care and 28-day infant survival in Victoria prior to and following the publication of the Safer Care Victoria Extreme Prematurity guideline. It includes all live births in three tertiary perinatal centres across the state and provides contemporaneous data for 28-day survival at these gestations if birth conditions are optimised (inborn, antenatal corticosteroids, magnesium sulfate, resuscitation at birth and NICU admission). Files were reviewed for each patient to ensure resuscitation intent was accurately described in the cohort, rather than inferred based on the presence or absence of interventions.

However, inherent in database-based research is the potential for error in data entry. This was minimised by additional file review for each mother and infant in the cohort. Additionally, the denominators for live births statewide were sourced from the VPDC.

A further limitation of our study was that we did not collect data reporting the proportion of women transferred by PIPER who remained undelivered following in utero transfer at 22–24 weeks or undelivered after 24 weeks’ gestation. We also did not collect data on stillbirths and acknowledge that survival-focused care may have been intended for some stillborn infants.

Additionally, our study did not include infants born outside a tertiary perinatal centre, nor those admitted to NICU following ex utero transfer. Thus, there is the likelihood of an overestimate of 28-day survival, as 22–24 weeks’ infants born outside a tertiary centre have higher mortality rates despite receiving survival-focused care.18 19 These data and outcomes will be captured in planned future research.

Conclusion

Following the introduction of the EP guideline in Victoria, Australia in December 2020, there has been an increase in in utero referrals and transfers of women at 22 weeks’ gestation to tertiary perinatal centres. Concurrently, there has been an increase in the proportion of inborn live births at 22–24 weeks receiving survival-focused care. Further studies are needed to report the long-term outcomes for these infants. Parental perspectives regarding antenatal counselling and shared decision-making at these early gestational ages also warrant further investigation.
