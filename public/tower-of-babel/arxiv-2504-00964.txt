Random cliques in random graphs revisited
Robert Morris, and Oliver Riordan

Abstract
We study the distribution of the set of copies of some given graph H H in the random graph G ⁡ ( n , p ) G(n,p) , focusing on the case when H = K r H=K_{r} . Our main results capture the ‘leading term’ in the difference between this distribution and the ‘independent hypergraph model’, where (in the case H = K r H=K_{r} ) each copy is present independently with probability π = p ( r 2 ) \pi=p^{\binom{r}{2}} . As a concrete application, we derive a new upper bound on the number of K r K_{r} -factors in G ⁡ ( n , p ) G(n,p) above the threshold for such factors to appear. We will prove our main results in a much more general setting, so that they also apply to random hypergraphs, and also (for example) to the case when p p is constant and r = r ⁡ ( n ) ∼ 2 ​ log 1 / p ⁡ ( n ) r=r(n)\sim 2\log_{1/p}(n) .

Introduction

Our aim in this paper is to study the distribution of the copies of some given graph
H
in a binomial random graph
G(n,p)
. Intuitively, if we do not expect to see any pairs of copies of
H
sharing edges, then not only will the number of copies of
H
be asymptotically Poisson, but the random set of copies will be close to a random subset of all possible copies of
H
in which each appears independently with probability
\pi=p^{e(H)}
. As a warm-up for our main result, we formalise this in Section
3
. We then consider the case in which intersecting pairs are likely to appear, but larger ‘clusters’ are unlikely, or there are not too many of them. Here we prove a result capturing the effect of intersecting pairs on the distribution of the set of copies, with an error term involving the expectation of larger clusters. Finally, we will give an application of this result, bounding the number of
K_{r}
-factors in
G(n,p)
for
r\geqslant 3
constant.

Throughout, we consider the random
set
of copies of
H
, not just the number of such copies. This paper is in some sense inspired by
[
22
]
, where the second author studied copies of
K_{r}
in
G(n,p)
via a coupling argument, but the technical approach we take here will be very different, arguing not via coupling, but by directly estimating the probability of each ‘plausible’ outcome.

We will state and prove our main results in a more general setting (so that they also apply, for example, to hypergraphs), which makes them a little hard to absorb quickly (see Theorems
4.1
and
5.1
). We will therefore begin by giving two relatively simple consequences for the case of copies of
K_{r}
in
G(n,p)
, first for cliques of constant size, and then for larger cliques when
p
is constant.

1.1
Constant-size cliques in

For any graph

Our first theorem bounds the probability that

(for ‘most’ choices of

\mu_{r}\leqslant n^{1+\varepsilon}

\mu_{r}={n\choose r}p^{{r\choose 2}}

\Lambda(n,r)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s=2}^{r-1}\binom{n}{r}\binom{r}{s}\binom{n-r}{r-s}p^{2\binom{r}{2}}\Big(p^{-\binom{s}{2}}-1\Big)

(1.1)

13
]

[
22
]
and Heckel
[
6

There has been significant interest in recent years in the problem of bounding the number of

1.4
Relationship to previous work

In
[
22
]
, the second author studied, as here, the hypergraph
H_{r}(G(n,p))
formed by the copies of
K_{r}
in
G(n,p)
, for
r\geqslant 4
and
p\leqslant n^{-2/r+\gamma}
for some
\gamma=\gamma(r)>0
. The main result there (extended to
r=3
by Annika Heckel in
[
6
]
) is that for some
\pi^{\prime}\sim\pi=p^{\binom{r}{2}}
, there is a coupling of
H_{r}(G(n,p))
and
H_{r}(n,\pi^{\prime})
such that

H_{r}(n,\pi^{\prime})\subset H_{r}(G(n,p))

with high probability; as noted in
[
22
]
, there is no similar coupling in the other direction, precisely due to fact that
H_{r}(G(n,p))
typically contains many more pairs of intersecting hyperedges than
H_{r}(n,\pi)
does.

To compare the two results: the coupling result of
[
22
]
is useful for showing that certain structures exist in
G(n,p)
(see, e.g.,
[
2
,
7
,
12
,
16
,
21
]
), and it is also relatively easy to apply, since it gives a direct comparison to a simple distribution. However, it is only ‘one-way’, and loses something in that
\pi^{\prime}
is not equal to
\pi
. In contrast, Theorem
1.1
is two-way, and in a sense sharper, giving fairly precise bounds on the actual probability of all outcomes within some ‘typical’ set. On the other hand, while in some sense it answers the distribution question for
H_{r}(G(n,p))
almost completely, it is not at first clear how to apply it; we will illustrate one method in Section
7
(see in particular Lemma
7.6
), which we expect to have further applications. The earlier coupling result definitely does not imply Theorem
1.1
; the reverse implication could perhaps hold, but seems hard to prove – it is often difficult to go from probabilities of individual outcomes to coupling results. In summary, we believe that the two results are complementary.

Mousset, Noever, Panagiotou and Samotij
[
19
]
proved results that are loosely related to those given here. They consider, for example, the probability that
G(n,p)
contains no copies of some given graph
F
(their actual context is much more general, as ours will be). For
F=K_{r}
, this is asking for the probability that
H_{r}(G(n,p))
has no edges. They manage to give a very precise answer to this question in many cases. Here, in contrast, we consider not one possible outcome, but the entire set of ‘typical’ outcomes. This complicates the situation considerably, and correspondingly our results are less precise.
We will briefly discuss the connection between the proof techniques used here and in
[
19
]
once we have outlined our method (see Remark
2.1
).

The rest of this paper is organized as follows: in Section
2
we describe the abstract set-up for our general results, and the basic method of proof. In Section
3
, we prove the simple ‘warm-up’ result alluded to earlier. Then in Section
4
we prove the main part of our main result, Theorem
4.1
, giving an upper bound on the probability of suitable outcomes. In Section
5
we prove a corresponding lower bound with additional conditions. In Section
6
we consider the specific case of copies of
K_{r}
, in particular deducing Theorems
1.1
and
1.4
. Finally, in Section
7
we turn to applications, proving Theorem
1.2
via a general lemma that may be useful for applying Theorem
1.1
in other contexts.

The following basically trivial lemma will be used in the proof below.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
D
be a down-set, and define
d(A)=\mathbb{P}(A)-\mathbb{P}(A\mid D)
for any event
A
. Then, if
U_{1},\ldots,U_{k}
are up-sets, we have

0\leqslant d(U_{1}\cup\cdots\cup U_{k})\leqslant d(U_{1})+\cdots+d(U_{k}).

Proof.

The first inequality is just Harris’ Lemma. For the second,
starting with the identity
\mathbb{P}(U_{1}\cup U_{2})=\mathbb{P}(U_{1})+\mathbb{P}(U_{2})-\mathbb{P}(U_{1}\cap U_{2})
and subtracting the same identity for probabilities conditioned on
D
, we obtain

d(U_{1}\cup U_{2})=d(U_{1})+d(U_{2})-d(U_{1}\cap U_{2})\leqslant d(U_{1})+d(U_{2}),

since
U_{1}\cap U_{2}
is an up-set. This proves the case
k=2
. The general case now follows easily by induction on
k

∎

\prod_{j\text{ simple}}(1-\pi_{j})\leqslant\exp\bigg(-\frac{2|Y|}{\mu}\cdot\Delta_{2}+O\Big(\Delta_{3}+\phi\cdot Q_{2}(Y)+Q_{3}(Y)+Q_{4}(Y)\Big)\bigg),

4.23
), we obtain

\prod_{j\text{ neutral or simple}}(1-\pi_{j})\leqslant\exp\bigg(-\mu-\bigg(\frac{2|Y|}{\mu}-1\bigg)\big(\Delta_{2}-\Delta_{2}^{0}\big)+\eta(Y)\bigg),

where

\eta(Y)=O\Big(\Delta_{3}+\phi\cdot Q_{2}(Y)+Q_{3}(Y)+Q_{4}(Y)+\pi^{2}N\Big).

Finally, using the trivial bound
\prod_{j\text{ complex}}(1-\pi_{j})\leqslant 1
, and noting that

(1-\pi)^{N-|Y|}=\exp\big(-\pi N+\pi|Y|+O(\pi^{2}N)\big)=\exp\big(-\mu+O(\pi^{2}N)\big),

and that
p^{|R(Y)|}=\pi^{|Y|}p^{-t(Y)}
, the bound in Theorem
4.1
follows from (
2.1
), completing the proof of the theorem.
∎

5
The lower bound

In this section we prove a lower bound on the probability
\mathbb{P}(I=Y)
that a certain subset of our events
E_{i}
hold and the rest do not (corresponding, in the standard setting, to a specific set of copies of
H
being the ones that are present in
G(n,p)
). This bound will roughly match the upper bound in Theorem
4.1
, up to the error term. Here there is a surprising annoyance, caused by the ‘complex’ terms
j
. Recall that
j\in Y^{\mathrm{c}}
is
complex
if there are two or more elements
i\in Y
such that
i\sim j
. Fixing
Y\subset[N]
, and defining
A_{j}^{\prime}=A_{j}^{\prime}(Y)
as before, let

C(Y)=\sum_{j\text{ complex}}\mathbb{P}(A_{j}^{\prime})=\sum_{j\text{ complex}}p^{|E_{j}\setminus R(Y)|}.

(5.1)

Then
C(I)
is a random variable, and its expectation counts certain clusters. Unfortunately, we do not in general have good control on their size. Roughly speaking, we expect the main contribution to come from cases where there are exactly two elements
i\in Y
with
i\sim j
; note that the expectation of the sum of such terms is
O(\Delta_{3})
. However, we can’t simply ignore additional intersections between
E_{j}
and sets
E_{i}
with
i\in Y
, since they may increase the conditional probability of
A_{j}^{\prime}
.

We first state a result with an error term involving
C(Y)
. Then we shall discuss methods of bounding
C(Y)
. We need a further very mild assumption, namely that
Y
is
possible
, meaning that
\mathbb{P}(I=Y)>0
. Note that this is equivalent to there not existing any
j\in Y^{\mathrm{c}}
with
E_{j}\subset R(Y)
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose that
1-p=\Omega(1)
,
\Delta_{2}^{0}\leqslant\mu/4
, and
\phi\Delta_{2}=O(\Delta_{3})
. Then, for every possible
Y\subset[N]
with
|Y|=O(\mu)
, we have

\mathbb{P}(I=Y)=\pi^{|Y|}(1-\pi)^{N-|Y|}p^{-t(Y)}\exp\bigg(-\bigg(\frac{2|Y|}{\mu}-1\bigg)\big(\Delta_{2}-\Delta_{2}^{0}\big)+\eta^{\prime}(Y)\bigg),

where

\eta^{\prime}(Y)=O\Big(\Delta_{3}+\phi\cdot Q_{2}(Y)+Q_{3}(Y)+Q_{4}(Y)+\pi^{2}N+C(Y)\Big).

Note that the only differences from Theorem
4.1
are the additional (mild) assumptions that
Y
is possible,
1-p=\Omega(1)
and
\Delta_{2}^{0}\leqslant\mu/4
, the two-way rather than one-way bound, and the appearance of
C(Y)
in the error term
\eta^{\prime}(Y)
. We remark that a corresponding two-way version of Corollary
4.4
also follows.

To prove Theorem
5.1
, we simply go through the proof of Theorem
4.1
, noting that almost all of the bounds proved there are two-way, and showing how to give lower bounds in the remaining places.

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

The first place in the proof of Theorem
4.1
where we gave a one-way bound was (

). We claim that, using (
2.5
), we can replace this by

\pi_{j}=\frac{\pi-\varepsilon_{j}}{1-\delta_{j}}=(\pi-\varepsilon_{j})(1+\delta_{j})+O(\pi\delta_{j}^{2}).

This will follow if we can show that
\delta_{j}\leqslant 1/2
for each neutral
j\in Y^{\mathrm{c}}
. To see why this is true, note that if
j
is neutral, then

\delta_{j}\leqslant\hat{\delta}_{j}=\sum_{i\to j}\,\mathbb{P}(A_{i}^{\prime})=\sum_{i\to j}\pi\leqslant\sum_{i\sim j}\pi=\frac{2\Delta_{2}^{0}}{\pi N}=\frac{2\Delta_{2}^{0}}{\mu}\leqslant\frac{1}{2},

where the first two steps follow from (
2.6
) and Harris’ Lemma, the third holds because
i
is neutral for every
i\prec j
, and hence
\mathbb{P}(A_{i}^{\prime})=\mathbb{P}(A_{i})=\pi
, the fifth holds by (
4.5
) and since
\mathcal{H}
is symmetric, and the last holds by assumption.

It follows that the loss in (
4.14
) is
O(\pi\delta_{j}^{2})
for each neutral
j
, and by (
2.8
) and Claim
4.9
, the sum of these terms is at most

\sum_{j\textup{ neutral}}\pi\delta_{j}^{2}\leqslant\sum_{j\textup{ neutral}}\pi\hat{\delta}_{j}^{2}\leqslant\sum_{j\textup{ neutral}}\hat{\varepsilon}_{j}\hat{\delta}_{j}=O(\Delta_{3}).

The only place in the proof of Claim
4.6
in which we gave a one-way bound was in (
4.15
); the loss here is due to terms
j
that we have overcounted because either
j\in Y
and there is some
i\in Y
with
i\sim j
, or
j\in Y^{\mathrm{c}}
and there is more than one
i\in Y
with
i\sim j
. The loss is therefore at most

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in Y\\
i\sim j\end{subarray}}\pi+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,k\in Y\\
i\neq k\end{subarray}}\sum_{i\sim j\sim k}\pi=O\big(\phi\cdot Q_{2}(Y)+Q_{3}(Y)\big),

(5.2)

by (
4.2
) and (
4.3
), and this is already included in the error term
\eta(Y)
. Since Claims
4.7
–
4.10
all give two-way bounds, the only other loss for the neutral and simple terms is in the inequality
1-\pi_{j}\leqslant e^{-\pi_{j}}
, which we used twice, in (
4.23
) and in the final calculation. In both cases, we can replace it by the two-way bound

\log(1-\pi_{j})=-\pi_{j}+O(\pi_{j}^{2}),

which holds because
\pi_{j}=\pi
for every neutral
j
, and
\pi_{j}\leqslant\phi=\max_{i\neq j}\mathbb{P}\big(A_{j}\mid A_{i}\big)
for every simple
j
, and
1-\phi\geqslant 1-p=\Omega(1)
, by assumption. The total loss in the inequality is therefore at most

O\big(\pi\mu+\phi\Delta_{2}+\phi\eta(Y)\big)=O\big(\pi^{2}N+\Delta_{3}+\eta(Y)\big)=O(\eta(Y)),

since
|Y|=O(\mu)
and
\phi\Delta_{2}=O(\Delta_{3})
, and also
\phi\leqslant 1
.

What remains is to deal with complex
\pi_{j}
, where we previously just used the trivial inequality
\pi_{j}\geqslant 0
. Here we simply note that from the first inequality in (
2.7
) (which is simply Harris’ Lemma), we have

\sum_{j\text{ complex}}\pi_{j}\leqslant\sum_{j\text{ complex}}\mathbb{P}(A_{j}^{\prime})=C(Y),

by (
5.1
). Now, since we only consider possible sets
Y
, we have
E_{j}\not\subset R(Y)
for every
j\in Y^{\mathrm{c}}
, and therefore
\pi_{j}\leqslant\mathbb{P}(A_{j}^{\prime})=p^{|E_{j}\setminus R(Y)|}\leqslant p
, which is bounded away from

, by assumption. It follows that
\log(1-\pi_{j})=O(\pi_{j})
,
so

\prod_{j\text{ complex}}(1-\pi_{j})=e^{O(C(Y))},

completing the proof.
∎

This result is of course rather a cop-out: we have expressed the final error term simply as what comes out of the proof. We now show that it is indeed useful, by giving two approaches to bounding
C(Y)
. The first is most useful for the case of subgraphs of constant size. Recall that
\mathcal{H}
is
s
-uniform.

Lemma 5.2
.

Suppose that
s=O(1)
. Then

\mathbb{E}\big[C(I)\big]=O\big(\Delta_{3}+\cdots+\Delta_{s+1}\big).

The key to the proof is the following definition.

Definition 5.3
.

A
star-cluster

\mathcal{S}
is a pair
(j,T)
where
j\in[N]
and
T\subset[N]
with
|T|\geqslant 2
satisfy

E_{j}\not\subset R(T),

and if
|T|\geqslant 3
, then

E_{i}\cap E_{j}\not\subset R(T\setminus\{i\})

for every
i\in T
.

In words, the centre of the star
E_{j}
is not contained in the union of the leaves
\{E_{i}:i\in T\}
, and the set of leaves is minimal given
E_{j}\cap R(T)
, meaning that if
|T|>2
, then each leaf intersects
E_{j}
in some element that is not contained in any of the other leaves. Note that the definition implies that
|T|\leqslant|E_{j}|
.

We will say that the star-cluster
\mathcal{S}=(j,T)
is
pre-present
in
Y
if
T\subset Y
, and
present
in
Y
if also
j\in Y
. Define

\pi_{0}(\mathcal{S})=p^{|R(T)|},\qquad\pi_{1}(\mathcal{S})=p^{|E_{j}\cup R(T)|}\quad\text{and}\quad\pi_{c}(\mathcal{S})=p^{|E_{j}\setminus R(T)|},

so
\pi_{0}(\mathcal{S})
is the probability that
\mathcal{S}
is pre-present in
I
,
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{S})
is the probability that
\mathcal{S}
is present in
I
, and
\pi_{c}(\mathcal{S})
is the probability that
\mathcal{S}
is present in
I
, conditioned on the event that
\mathcal{S}
is pre-present in
I
. Let us also define

{\widehat{C}}(Y)=\sum_{\mathcal{S}}\mathbbm{1}\big[\mathcal{S}\text{ is pre-present in }Y\big]\cdot\pi_{c}(\mathcal{S}),

(5.3)

and make the following simple but key observation.

Observation 5.4
.

C(Y)\leqslant{\widehat{C}}(Y)
for every possible
Y\subset[N]
.

Proof.

Fix
Y\subset[N]
, let
j
be a complex term, and let
T
be a minimal subset of
Y
such that
E_{j}\cap R(Y)\subset R(T)
and
|T|\geqslant 2
. Then
\mathcal{S}=(j,T)
is a star-cluster, since
E_{j}\not\subset R(Y)
(because
Y
is possible and
i\in Y^{\mathrm{c}}
), and if
|T|\geqslant 3
, then
E_{i}\cap E_{j}\not\subset R(T\setminus\{i\})
for every
i\in T
, by the minimality of
T
. Moreover,
\mathcal{S}
is pre-present in
Y
, since
T\subset Y
. Since
\mathbb{P}(A_{j}^{\prime})=\pi_{c}(\mathcal{S})
, it follows that

C(Y)=\sum_{j\text{ complex}}\mathbb{P}(A_{j}^{\prime})\leqslant\sum_{\mathcal{S}}\mathbbm{1}\big[\mathcal{S}\text{ is pre-present in }Y\big]\cdot\pi_{c}(\mathcal{S})={\widehat{C}}(Y),

as claimed.
∎

We can now easily deduce the claimed bound on
\mathbb{E}\big[C(I)\big]
.

Proof of Lemma
5.2
.

Observe first that

\mathbb{E}\big[{\widehat{C}}(I)\big]=\sum_{\mathcal{S}}\pi_{0}(\mathcal{S})\pi_{c}(\mathcal{S})=\sum_{\mathcal{S}}\pi_{1}(\mathcal{S}),

and that each
\mathcal{S}
corresponds to a
3
-cluster if
s=2
, and a cluster of size between
3
and
s
if
s\geqslant 3
. By Observation
5.4
, and since
s=O(1)
, it follows that

\mathbb{E}\big[C(I)\big]\leqslant\mathbb{E}\big[{\widehat{C}}(I)\big]=\sum_{\mathcal{S}}\pi_{1}(\mathcal{S})=O\big(\Delta_{3}+\cdots+\Delta_{s+1}\big),

as claimed.
∎

At this point we only outline our second method for bounding
C(Y)
, since it is probably best illustrated by the concrete example in Section
6.2
. The idea is to consider a set
\mathcal{F}
of ‘forbidden configurations’, which we will take to be certain types of cluster that are not likely to appear. Then we may define a ‘legal star-cluster’
(j,T)
to be a star-cluster whose leaves do not form any configuration in
\mathcal{F}
. In place of Observation
5.4
, we obtain

C(Y)\leqslant{\widehat{C}}_{\mathcal{F}}(Y):=\sum_{\mathcal{S}\text{ legal}}\mathbbm{1}\big[\mathcal{S}\text{ is pre-present in }Y\big]\cdot\pi_{c}(\mathcal{S})

for every
\mathcal{F}
-free possible set
Y\subset[N]
.
If
I
is with high probability
\mathcal{F}
-free and
\mathbb{E}\big[{\widehat{C}}_{\mathcal{F}}(I)\big]
is small, then we obtain good control on
C(I)
with high probability.

such that each after the first shares at least one edge of

with the union of the previous ones. For each

be the

, and let

denote the expected number of labelled copies of
\mathcal{C}_{i}

. Thus

, and

where

that

and

Let us say that step

. Note that step 2 is always ordinary; if there are at least two ordinary steps, then

since
\mu_{r}\leqslant n^{r}p^{r\choose 2}\leqslant n^{1+O(\gamma)}
and recalling that

and
\gamma
is sufficiently small.

This leaves the case where

is the only ordinary step. Let

, and suppose first that

. Then there are
r+1
vertices in total and (since

. On the other hand, if

, then

does not contain a copy of
K_{r}^{-}
, so

must contain at least two edges not in

, and hence

N_{k}=O(N_{3})=O(p^{2}N_{2})=O\big(p^{2}\cdot\mu_{r}\cdot n^{2/r-1+O(\gamma)}\big)=n^{-\Omega(1)},

since

,
\mu_{r}\leqslant n^{1+O(\gamma)}
and
\gamma
is sufficiently small.
∎

Before continuing, let’s quickly note the following immediate corollary of Lemma

Corollary 6.8

If

and

By Lemma

∎

We can now prove the claimed bound on

.

Lemma 6.9
.

\Delta_{3}=O(\xi)
.

Proof.

If

, then any

-cluster is either a copy of

or has exactly

vertices and

edges, so in this case

as claimed, since

. If

, then one can easily check that any
3
-cluster is either a copy of

, or has

8

We will deduce Theorem

If

Recall from Lemma

If

Remark 6.15
.

Note that Lemma

In order to prove (

, and those that are not. For each

, let us say that a

Lemma 6.16
.

If
k\in\{3,4\}
, then the expected number of normal

-clusters is

O^{*}(n^{3\theta-1})+n^{-\Omega(1)}.

In the proof of Lemma

{\hat{\theta}}=\max\{\theta,0\}.

We will also need the following simple observation.

Observation 6.17

If
\mu_{r}=n^{O(1)}
, then

.

Proof.

Observe that

\mu_{r}={n\choose r}p^{{r\choose 2}}=n^{O(1)}\bigg(\frac{n}{r}\cdot p^{r/2}\bigg)^{r},

and hence

∎

The proof of the lemma is somewhat similar to that of Lemma
6.7
.

Proof of Lemma
6.16
.

Observe first that the number of isomorphism classes of
k
-clusters is
O^{*}(1)
, so it will suffice to prove the claimed bound for a fixed
k
-cluster
\mathcal{C}
. Let

be the cliques in
\mathcal{C}
, ordered so that each after the first shares an edge of
K_{n}
with the union of the earlier ones.
The complication in this setting is that, since

. Indeed, a crude bound on

, the number of choices in step

, where the implicit constant depends only on

, since

and

.

To deal with this issue, let us say that two cliques have a

\lambda

\lambda

N_{i}
denote the expected number of labelled copies of
\mathcal{C}_{i}
(the

) present in

.
If two cliques in
\mathcal{C}
have a strange intersection of size

N_{2}\leqslant\mu_{r}\cdot{n\choose r-t}p^{\binom{r}{2}-\binom{t}{2}}\leqslant n^{-\lambda/2},

, by Observation
6.17
. Now, observe that

N_{i}=O^{*}\big(m_{i}N_{i-1}\big),

as in the proof of Lemma

E_{i}

with

\mathbb{P}\big(H_{r}(G(n,p))=H\big)\leqslant\pi^{e(H)}(1-\pi)^{N-e(H)}p^{-t(H)}e^{-(\frac{2e(H)}{\mu_{r}}-1)(\Delta_{2}-\Delta_{2}^{0})+\eta(H)},

where
N={n\choose r}
and

\eta(H)=O\Big(\Delta_{3}+\phi\cdot Q_{2}(H)+Q_{3}(H)+Q_{4}(H)+\pi^{2}N\Big).

Moreover, by Lemma
6.13
, and since
\pi^{2}N=n^{-\Omega(1)}
, we have

\eta(H)=O^{*}\big(\Delta_{3}+\Delta_{4}+n^{-\Omega(1)}\big)=O^{*}(n^{\theta})+n^{-\Omega(1)},

and by (

), (
6.13
) and (
6.21
) we have

\Delta_{2}=\nu_{2}+O^{*}(n^{\theta})+n^{-\Omega(1)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Delta_{2}^{0}=\sum_{k=2}^{r-1}\nu_{k}^{0}=\nu_{2}^{0}+n^{-\Omega(1)}.

By (
6.16
), and observing that
n^{1-\delta}\cdot\Delta_{2}/\mu_{r}=O^{*}(n^{\theta})+n^{-\Omega(1)}
for every
\delta>0
, since
\Delta_{2}=O^{*}(n^{2\theta}+n^{\theta})
, it follows that

\bigg(\frac{2e(H)}{\mu_{r}}-1\bigg)\big(\Delta_{2}-\Delta_{2}^{0}\big)=\nu_{2}-\nu_{2}^{0}+O^{*}(n^{\theta})+n^{-\Omega(1)},

(6.22)

and hence we obtain the claimed bound. Finally, recall from Lemma
6.18
that
H_{r}(G(n,p))
is plausible with high probability.
∎

Lower bound for
p
constant

In this subsection we will complete the proof of Theorem

. To do so we will use Theorem

, as in Section

, but here we will need to work harder to bound the term

Our first task is to define the family

of hypergraphs for which we will prove the claimed lower bound; doing so will require a little preparation. The first step is to describe the set of configurations of copies of
K_{r}
that typically appear in

when

.

Definition 6.19
.

A cluster is
legal
if it is either contained in a copy of
K_{r+1}
, consists of a pair of copies of
K_{r}
sharing exactly
2

3
-cluster formed by cliques whose vertex sets

satisfy

|S_{1}\cap S_{2}|=|S_{1}\cap S_{3}|=2\qquad\text{and}\qquad\big(S_{2}\cap S_{3}\big)\setminus S_{1}=\emptyset.

We say that a graph

, or the corresponding
r

, is

We will show below that, if

and

are chosen as in Theorem
1.4
, then

is legal with high probability (see Lemma

).

Next, recall from Definition
5.3
that a star-cluster is a collection of at least three copies of

, one of which (the ‘centre’) is not contained in the union of others (the ‘leaves’), and if there are at least three leaves, then each leaf intersects the centre in some edge that is not contained in any of the other leaves. Here we will need the following slightly more restrictive notion.

Definition 6.20
.

We say that a star-cluster
\mathcal{S}
is

if the graph formed by the union of its leaves is legal.

As in Section
5
, we say that the star-cluster
\mathcal{S}
is

if all of its leaves correspond to edges of

, and

if the centre also corresponds to an edge of

. Now, define

{{\widehat{C}}_{\mathrm{L}}}(H)=\sum_{\mathcal{S}\text{ legal}}\mathbbm{1}\big[\mathcal{S}\text{ is pre-present in }H\big]\cdot\pi_{c}(\mathcal{S}),

(6.23)

where
\pi_{c}(\mathcal{S})
denotes the probability that
\mathcal{S}
is present in
H_{r}(G(n,p))
, conditioned on the event that
\mathcal{S}
is pre-present in
H_{r}(G(n,p))
. The following variant of Observation
5.4
follows from the same proof.

Observation 6.21
.

C(H)\leqslant{{\widehat{C}}_{\mathrm{L}}}(H)
for every possible and legal hypergraph
H
.

Proof.

To deduce the claimed inequality from the proof of Observation
5.4
, note that if
H
is legal, then any star-cluster that is pre-present in
H
is also legal.
∎

We can now define the family of hypergraphs
\mathcal{G}
for which we will prove the lower bound in Theorem
1.4
.

Definition 6.22
.

Let

be an arbitrary constant. An

-uniform hypergraph

with

H
is plausible,
H
is legal, and

{{\widehat{C}}_{\mathrm{L}}}(H)\leqslant(\log n)^{C}\,\mathbb{E}\big[{{\widehat{C}}_{\mathrm{L}}}\big(H_{r}(G(n,p))\big)\big].

Theorem
1.4
is an immediate consequence of the following theorem.

Theorem 6.23
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be constant, let
-1+\varepsilon\leqslant\theta=\theta(n)\leqslant 1/2-\varepsilon
, and suppose that
p=p(n)\in(\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon)
and

are chosen so that
\mu_{r}=\Theta(n^{1+\theta})
. Then

\mathbb{P}\big(H_{r}(G(n,p))=H\big)=\pi^{e(H)}(1-\pi)^{{n\choose r}-e(H)}p^{-t(H)}e^{-\nu_{2}+\nu_{2}^{0}+O^{*}(n^{\theta})+n^{-\Omega(1)}}

for every reasonable
H
, and
H_{r}(G(n,p))
is reasonable with high probability.

We will deduce Theorem
6.23
from Theorem
5.1
. Let us assume that
\varepsilon>0
is fixed, and that
p\in(\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon)
,
r\in\mathbb{N}
and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
satisfy the assumptions of the theorem, and recall from the previous section that the conditions of Theorem
4.1
are satisfied. Note also that
1-p\geqslant\varepsilon
by assumption, and that

\Delta_{2}^{0}=O^{*}\bigg(\frac{\mu_{r}^{2}}{n^{2}}\bigg)=o(\mu_{r}),

(6.24)

since
\mu_{r}=O(n^{3/2})
. We may therefore apply Theorem
5.1
to the
{r\choose 2}
-uniform hypergraph encoding copies of
K_{r}
in
E(K_{n})
. Since, by Definition
6.22
, every reasonable hypergraph
H
is plausible, it follows from the proof of Theorem
6.12
(in particular from Lemma
6.13
and (
6.22
)) that the claimed bound holds up to a factor of
\exp\big(O(C(H))\big)
. By Observation
6.21
and Definition
6.22
, it will therefore suffice to prove the following lemma. Recall that
{\hat{\theta}}=\max\{\theta,0\}
.

Lemma 6.24
.

\mathbb{E}\big[{{\widehat{C}}_{\mathrm{L}}}\big(H_{r}(G(n,p))\big)\big]=O^{*}\big(n^{\theta}+n^{3{\hat{\theta}}-1}\big).

We say that a star-cluster
\mathcal{S}
is
special
if it consists of exactly three cliques, all of which are contained in a copy of
K_{r+1}
, and otherwise we say that
\mathcal{S}
is
ordinary
. The first term in Lemma
6.24
comes from special legal star-clusters, and the second from ordinary ones. Note that in an ordinary legal star cluster, no two of the leaves can be contained in a copy of
K_{r+1}
that also contains the centre of
\mathcal{S}
, since otherwise these two leaves would already cover all but one edge of the centre, so by the definition of a star-cluster there can be no further leaves in
\mathcal{S}
, and hence
\mathcal{S}
is special.

Proof of Lemma
6.24
.

Observe first that

\mathbb{E}\big[{{\widehat{C}}_{\mathrm{L}}}\big(H_{r}(G(n,p))\big)\big]=\sum_{\mathcal{S}\text{ legal}}\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{S}\text{ is present in }G(n,p)\big).

The contribution to the sum from special legal star-clusters is

O^{*}(\mu_{r+1})=O^{*}(n^{\theta}),

by (
6.20
), and since the union of the cliques in
\mathcal{S}
is a copy of
K_{r+1}
, and there are
O^{*}(1)
ways to choose the centre and the leaves inside a given copy of
K_{r+1}
. From now on we consider only ordinary legal star-clusters.

As usual, we will partition the sum according to the isomorphism type of the ordinary legal star-cluster
\mathcal{S}
. For a fixed type, we partition the leaves of
\mathcal{S}
into groups: any set of leaves contained in a copy of
K_{r+1}
forms a group, and every other leaf forms a group of size
1
. Note that each group consists of at most three cliques, since no leaf can be contained in the union of two or more other leaves, by the minimality of
\mathcal{S}
(see Definition
5.3
). Observe also that any group shares at most two edges with the union of all the other groups, since the non-singleton groups are edge-disjoint from the others, and each singleton group shares an edge (and no more) with at most two other singleton groups.

We now bound the expectation in the usual way, starting by choosing the centre,
S
, and then adding leaves one by one. Each time we add a singleton group, it forms a
2
-cluster with
S
, and conditioning on the other leaves being present only increases the probability by a factor of at most
p^{-2}=O(1)
, so we multiply the expectation by a factor of

O\bigg(\frac{\Delta_{2}}{\mu_{r}}\bigg)=O(n^{{\hat{\theta}}-1}),

where the final inequality follows from (
6.21
). Adding a non-singleton group is slightly more complicated; we claim that each time we add such a group, we multiply the expectation by a factor of

O^{*}\bigg(\frac{\Delta_{2}}{\mu_{r}}\cdot np^{r}\bigg)=O^{*}(n^{{\hat{\theta}}-2}),

(6.25)

where the final inequality holds by Observation
6.17
. To see this, let
U
be the vertex set of a non-singleton group, and note that (as we observed before the proof)
V(S)
is not contained in
U
, since
\mathcal{S}
is ordinary. It follows that at most
r-1
vertices of
U
are shared with the centre of
\mathcal{S}
, and hence
U
has at least two vertices outside
V(S)
. Let
u\in U\setminus V(S)
, and let
T
be the copy of
K_{r}
with vertex set
U\setminus\{u\}
. Note that
T
is not necessarily a leaf of
\mathcal{S}
; however, if
\mathcal{S}
is present in

then at least
{r\choose 2}-1
edges of

are contained in
G(n,p)
. Moreover, if
\mathcal{S}
is present in
G(n,p)
then at least
r-1
edges between

and
V(T)
are contained in
G(n,p)
, and none of these edges are contained in
S\cup T
, or in any of the cliques in different groups. Since there are only
O^{*}(1)
choices for the leaves of
\mathcal{S}
given

, we obtain (
6.25
).

Finally, note that since
\mathcal{S}
has at least two leaves, we either have at least two singleton groups, or at least one non-singleton group. It follows that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{S}\text{ legal}\\
\text{and ordinary}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{S}\text{ is present in }G(n,p)\big)=O^{*}\big(\mu_{r}\cdot n^{2{\hat{\theta}}-2}\big)=O^{*}(n^{3{\hat{\theta}}-1}),

(6.26)

as required.
∎

It only remains to show that
H_{r}(G(n,p))
is reasonable with high probability. We have already shown that
H_{r}(G(n,p))
is plausible with high probability, and the final condition of reasonableness in Definition
6.22
holds by Markov’s inequality. We therefore only need to prove the following lemma.

Lemma 6.25
.

G(n,p)
is legal with high probability.

Proof.

Recall first from the proof of Lemma
6.18
that

\nu_{3}+\cdots+\nu_{r-2}=o(1),

so with high probability the only pairs of copies of
K_{r}
in
G(n,p)
that share at least one edge share either
2
or
r-1
vertices. Let
\mathcal{C}
be a cluster, and suppose that
\mathcal{C}
contains two copies of
K_{r}
sharing
r-1
vertices, say
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
. Either
\mathcal{C}
is contained in a copy of
K_{r+1}
, or it contains a third copy of
K_{r}
that shares between
2
and
r-1
vertices with
E_{1}\cup E_{2}
. The expected number of these latter configurations is at most

O^{*}\bigg(\binom{n}{r+1}p^{\binom{r+1}{2}}\cdot\frac{\Delta_{2}}{\mu_{r}}\bigg)=O^{*}\bigg(\frac{\Delta_{2}}{n}\bigg)=o(1),

by Observation
6.17
and (

), so with high probability no such clusters exist.

It remains to consider clusters
\mathcal{C}
in which every pair of copies of

share at most two vertices. If
\mathcal{C}
has size 2 then it is legal, and otherwise there exist three cliques in
\mathcal{C}
whose vertex sets

satisfy

|S_{1}\cap S_{2}|=|S_{1}\cap S_{3}|=2.

The expected number of such configurations with

is

O^{*}\bigg(\frac{\mu_{r}^{3}}{n^{5}}\bigg)=O^{*}(n^{3\theta-2})=o(1),

and the expected number of

is

O^{*}\bigg(\frac{\mu_{r}^{4}}{n^{6}}\bigg)=O^{*}(n^{4\theta-2})=o(1),

so with high probability these also do not occur in

, as required.
∎

Finally, let’s put the pieces together and prove Theorem

.

Proof of Theorem
6.23
.

The proof of Theorem
6.12
, together with the assumption that

and (

), which allows us to apply Theorem

in place of Theorem

, and the fact that every reasonable

.

To bound this extra error term, we use Observation

. Indeed, since

, and hence

C(H)\leqslant{{\widehat{C}}_{\mathrm{L}}}(H)=O^{*}\Big(\mathbb{E}\big[{{\widehat{C}}_{\mathrm{L}}}\big(H_{r}(G(n,p))\big)\big]\Big)=O^{*}\big(n^{\theta}+n^{-\Omega(1)}\big)

7
Clique factors in
G(n,p)

One of the main motivations for comparing the hypergraph
H_{r}(G(n,p))
with the binomial random hypergraph
H_{r}(n,\pi)
is to study
K_{r}
-factors in
G(n,p)
, by relating them to matchings in
H_{r}(n,\pi)
, or in the model
H_{r}(n,m)
where the number of hyperedges is fixed. The main focus of research has been on the threshold for such a factor/matching to exist, for
r\geqslant 3
constant. While the original (extremely influential) paper of Johannson, Kahn and Vu
[
11
]
established the (coarse) threshold simultaneously in both models, the more recent extremely precise results of Kahn
[
13
,
14
]
were only proved directly for the hypergraph matching question (‘Shamir’s problem’); corresponding results for
K_{r}
-factors are deduced using the coupling of
[
22
]
(for the sharp threshold for
r\geqslant 4
) and the extension due to Heckel
[
6
]
for
r=3
. Remarkably, despite the apparent loss in the coupling,
Heckel, Kaufmann, Müller and Pasch
[
7
]
managed to transfer even Kahn’s hitting time result
[
14
]
to the
K_{r}
-factor setting.

For both Shamir’s problem and the
K_{r}
-factor problem, the threshold for existence is now extremely well understood. Here we turn to a question that has received less attention: once hypergraph matchings/clique factors exist, how many are there? For simplicity, we will focus on the case where
p=p(n)
or
m=m(n)
is a (possibly small) constant factor above the threshold.

Our main aim in this subsection is to prove Theorem
1.2
; however, we will begin by discussing matchings in random hypergraphs. Let
M(H)
denote the number of perfect matchings in an
r
-uniform hypergraph
H
, and recall from Section
1.2
that we write

\Sigma(n,m)=\frac{n!}{r!^{n/r}(n/r)!}\frac{(m)_{n/r}}{(N)_{n/r}}

for the expected number of such matchings in the random hypergraph
H_{r}(n,m)
if
r\,|\,n
, where as usual
N=\binom{n}{r}
is the number of possible hyperedges and
(m)_{k}=m(m-1)\cdots(m-k+1)
. Let us also write

\Sigma(n,\pi)=\frac{n!}{r!^{n/r}(n/r)!}\pi^{n/r},

for the corresponding expectation in the binomial random hypergraph
H_{r}(n,\pi)
, and let

m_{r}(n)=\frac{n\log n}{r}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\pi_{r}(n)=\frac{m_{r}(n)}{N}

be the thresholds for a perfect matching to exist in the two models when
r\in\mathbb{N}
is constant (note also that
\pi_{r}(n)\sim q_{r}(n)^{r\choose 2}
, where
q_{r}(n)
was defined in (
1.3
)). Observe that if
\pi=m/N
, then

\frac{\Sigma(n,\pi)}{\Sigma(n,m)}=\frac{m^{n/r}}{N^{n/r}}\frac{(N)_{n/r}}{(m)_{n/r}}=\exp\left(\frac{n^{2}}{2r^{2}m}+O(n^{3}/m^{2})+O(n/m)\right),

(7.1)

which is
\exp(\Theta(n/\log n))
when
m=\Theta(m_{r}(n))
and
r=O(1)
.

Kahn
[
13
, Theorem 1.2]
gives a counting result for Shamir’s problem, which can be strengthened to the following; see Section
7.2
.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
r\geqslant 3
and
\varepsilon>0
be fixed. If
m\geqslant(1+\varepsilon)m_{r}(n)
, then

M(H_{r}(n,m))=e^{o(n/\log n)}\,\Sigma(n,m)

with high probability.

Only the lower bound here is interesting; the upper bound is just Markov’s inequality. An immediate corollary is the corresponding result for
H_{r}(n,\pi)
.

Corollary 7.2
.

Let
r\geqslant 3
and
\varepsilon>0
be fixed. If
\pi\geqslant(1+\varepsilon)\pi_{r}(n)
, then

M(H_{r}(n,\pi))=e^{o(n/\log n)}\,\Sigma(n,m)

with high probability, where
m=\big\lfloor\binom{n}{r}\pi\big\rfloor
.

Proof.

A standard argument: if we take
m_{+}=(1+n^{-1/2})m
and
m_{-}=(1-n^{-1/2})m
, then
H_{r}(n,\pi)
with high probability has between
m_{-}
and
m_{+}
edges, and conditional on having
m
edges, it has the distribution of
H_{r}(n,m)
. The result now follows from Theorem
7.1
, since the dependence of
\Sigma(n,m)
on
m
scales (roughly) as
m^{O(n)}
, so
\Sigma(n,m_{+})
and
\Sigma(n,m_{-})
are both within a factor of
\exp(O(n^{1/2}))
of
\Sigma(n,m)
.
∎

As we see here, translating between
H_{r}(n,m)
and
H_{r}(n,\pi)
is essentially trivial in either direction, since the property of containing a perfect matching is monotone. Note, however, that the statement for
H_{r}(n,\pi)
involves the expectation in the model
H_{r}(n,m)
. By (
7.1
), the expected number of perfect matchings in
H_{r}(n,\pi)
is larger by a factor of order
\exp\big(\Theta(n^{2}/m)\big)
, and this difference cannot be absorbed into the error term when
m=\Theta(n\log n)
.

This was for counting matchings in hypergraphs. What can we say about the number of
K_{r}
-factors in
G(n,m)
or
G(n,p)
? As outlined above, the coupling results of
[
22
]
and Heckel
[
6
]
give the following corollary of Theorem
7.1
. Here we need some upper bound on
p
for the coupling to work well; presumably it is not needed for the result to be true. Recall that we write
F_{r}(G)
for the number of
K_{r}
-factors in a graph
G
.

Corollary 7.3
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
r\geqslant 3
be fixed. There exists
\gamma=\gamma(r)>0
such that if
p=p(n)
satisfies
(1+\varepsilon)q_{r}(n)\leqslant p\leqslant n^{-2/r+\gamma}
, then

F_{r}(G(n,p))\geqslant e^{o(n/\log n)}\,\Sigma(n,m)

with high probability, where
m=\big\lfloor\binom{n}{r}p^{\binom{r}{2}}\big\rfloor
.

Proof.

This is essentially immediate from Corollary
7.2
and
[
22
, Theorem 1]
for
r\geqslant 4
, or
[
6
, Theorem 2]
for
r=3
: the latter results show that there is a coupling of
G(n,p)
and
H_{r}(n,\pi^{\prime})
with
\pi^{\prime}\sim p^{\binom{r}{2}}
such that with high probability the set of copies of
K_{r}
in the former contains all hyperedges of the latter. As noted in both papers (see Remark 4 in
[
22
]
), we may in fact take
\pi^{\prime}=(1-n^{-\delta})\pi
for some constant
\delta>0
. Then the corresponding
m
and
m^{\prime}
agree within a factor of
1-n^{-\delta}
, so
\Sigma(n,m^{\prime})=\exp(O(n^{1-\delta}))\cdot\Sigma(n,m).

∎

The discussion above suggests an obvious question: can we bound the number of
K_{r}
-factors in
G(n,p)
from above? Of course, the expectation gives an upper bound. However, writing
\pi=p^{\binom{r}{2}}
, we have

\mathbb{E}\big[F_{r}(G(n,p))\big]=\Sigma(n,\pi),

(7.2)

which by (
7.1
) is significantly larger than
\Sigma(n,m)
for
m=\lfloor\mu_{r}\rfloor
, and so does not match the lower bound in Corollary
7.3
. One might expect that switching to
G(n,m)
would help, but this is not the case. In the hypergraph setting, the expectation in
H_{r}(n,\pi)
is ‘too large’ (larger than the typical value) because when
H_{r}(n,\pi)
has more hyperedges than typical, it has a lot more matchings. However, the expectation in
G(n,p)
is not too large due to extra edges, but rather due to extra copies of
K_{r}
.

This suggests a strategy: condition on the total number of copies of
K_{r}
in
G(n,p)
. This thinking (in the different context of studying colourings of
G(n,1/2)
, which we will return to in future work) is in fact what motivated the present paper; using Theorem
6.2
we are able to prove the following result.

Theorem 7.4
.

Let
r\geqslant 3
and
\varepsilon>0
be constants. There exists
\gamma=\gamma(r)>0
such that if
p\leqslant n^{-2/r+\gamma}
, then

F_{r}(G(n,p))\leqslant e^{O^{*}(\sqrt{n})}\,\Sigma(n,m)

with high probability, where
m=\big\lfloor\binom{n}{r}p^{\binom{r}{2}}\big\rfloor
.

Together, with Corollary
7.2
, this result establishes Theorem
1.2
, illustrating how our present results here complement the couplings of
[
22
]
and
[
6
]
. Note that the result above is (of course) only interesting when
p
is at least (or is close to) the threshold
q_{r}(n)
, since otherwise
F_{r}(G(n,p))=0
with high probability.

7.1
A general lemma

The main ingredient in the proof of Theorem

given by Theorem

Throughout this subsection, let us fix

and

denote the number of 3-clusters in

\Delta_{3}=\mathbb{E}\big[W_{3}\big(H_{r}(G(n,p))\big)\big],

cf. (

\omega=\omega(n)\to\infty
as

arbitrarily slowly.

Definition 7.5
.

An
r
-uniform hypergraph
H
is
well behaved
if it is good and

W_{3}(H)\leqslant\omega\Delta_{3}.

Recall from Lemma
6.3
that
H_{r}(G(n,p))
is good with high probability, and observe that therefore, by Markov’s inequality,
H_{r}(G(n,p))
is well behaved with high probability. Let
\mathcal{W}
denote the event that
H_{r}(G(n,p))
is well behaved, and for each
m\in\mathbb{N}
with
|m-\mu_{r}|\leqslant\omega\sqrt{\mu_{r}}
, consider the event

\mathcal{W}_{m}=\big\{H_{r}(G(n,p))\text{ is well behaved and }e(H_{r}(G(n,p)))=m\big\}.

Recall also that
\xi
was defined in (
6.4
).

The following technical lemma is the main result of this subsection.

Lemma 7.6
.

For each
r\geqslant 3
, there exists
\gamma>0
such that if
p\leqslant n^{-2/r+\gamma}
and
\mu_{r}\to\infty
, then the following holds. Let
E_{1},\ldots,E_{k}
be distinct copies of
K_{r}
in
K_{n}
, and let
F=E_{1}\cup\cdots\cup E_{k}
. For any
m\in\mathbb{N}
with
|m-\mu_{r}|\leqslant\omega\sqrt{\mu_{r}}
, we have

\frac{\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{W}_{m}\cap\{F\subset G(n,p)\}\big)}{\mathbb{P}\big(\mathrm{Bin}(N,\pi)=m\big)}\leqslant\frac{(m)_{k}}{(N)_{k}}\cdot p^{-t(F)}\cdot e^{-\binom{k}{2}\binom{m}{2}^{-1}(\Delta_{2}\,-\,\Delta_{2}^{0})\,+\,O^{*}(\xi)\,+\,n^{-\Omega(1)}},

where
N=\binom{n}{r}
and

t(F)=k\binom{r}{2}-e(F)

(7.3)

is the number of repeated graph edges in
E_{1},\ldots,E_{k}
.

We remark that the error terms in the lemma depend on
r
,
\gamma
and
\omega
, but not on
k
. To help motivate the bound in the lemma, notice that

\frac{(m)_{k}}{(N)_{k}}\cdot\mathbb{P}\big(\mathrm{Bin}(N,\pi)=m\big)

is the probability that
H_{r}(n,\pi)
has exactly
m
edges, including
E_{1},\ldots,E_{k}
.

Recall from Section
6.2
that if
H
is a hypergraph, then we write
t_{s}(H)
for the number of pairs of hyperedges of
H
sharing exactly
s
vertices, and from (
1.2
) that
t(H)
is the analogue of (
7.3
) for hypergraphs. In the proof of Lemma
7.6
, we will need the following bounds on
t(H)
in terms of
t_{2}(H),\ldots,t_{r-1}(H)
.

Lemma 7.7
.

For any
r
-uniform hypergraph
H
we have

0\leqslant\sum_{s=2}^{r-1}\binom{s}{2}t_{s}(H)-t(H)\leqslant\binom{r-1}{2}W_{3}(H).

Proof.

Both
\sum_{s=2}^{r-1}\binom{s}{2}t_{s}(H)
and
t(H)
count graph edges that appear in at least two hyperedges of
H
, with certain multiplicities. Edges that appear in exactly
k
hyperedges have multiplicities
\binom{k}{2}
and
k-1
, which are equal when
k=2
, and for
k\geqslant 3
the former multiplicity is larger, so this proves the lower bound.

For the upper bound, note that each edge in
k
hyperedges contributes zero to the central term if
k=2
, and at most
{k\choose 2}-k+1\leqslant{k\choose 3}
if
k\geqslant 3
. Moreover, each such edge is contained in the common intersection of at least
{k\choose 3}
different
3
-clusters. The central term is therefore at most the sum over all
3
-clusters
\mathcal{C}
of the number of graph edges contained in the common intersection of the hyperedges in
\mathcal{C}
, which is at most the claimed upper bound.
∎

We will also need a similar bound for the following variant of
t_{s}(H)
.

Definition 7.8
.

Given an
r
-uniform hypergraph
H
and
2\leqslant s\leqslant r-1
, we write
t_{s}^{-}(H)
for the number of unordered pairs
\{e,f\}
of hyperedges of
H
such that

(i)

e
and
f
share exactly
s
vertices, and

(ii)

neither
e
nor
f
intersects any other hyperedge of
H
in two or more vertices.

In other words,
t_{s}^{-}(H)
counts the number of ‘isolated’
2
-clusters with intersection size
s
. In particular, note that
t_{s}^{-}(H)\leqslant t_{s}(H)
.

Lemma 7.9
.

For any
r
-uniform hypergraph
H
we have

0\leqslant\sum_{s=2}^{r-1}\binom{s}{2}t_{s}(H)-\sum_{s=2}^{r-1}\binom{s}{2}t^{-}_{s}(H)\leqslant 3\binom{r-1}{2}W_{3}(H).

Proof.

The lower bound is trivial, since
t_{s}^{-}(H)\leqslant t_{s}(H)
by definition. Now, observe that
\sum_{s=2}^{r-1}\binom{s}{2}t_{s}(H)
counts all pairs
(e,\mathcal{C})
, where
\mathcal{C}
is a
2
-cluster and
e
is a graph edge in the intersection of the two hyperedges of
\mathcal{C}
. If the pair
(e,\mathcal{C})
does not contribute to the sum with
t_{s}^{-}
, then there must be some hyperedge of
H
that forms a 3-cluster with
\mathcal{C}
. On the other hand, for each 3-cluster in
H
there are at most three choices for the
2
-cluster
\mathcal{C}
, and then at most
\binom{r-1}{2}
choices for the edge
e
in the intersection of these two hyperedges.
∎

We are now ready to prove our technical lemma.

Proof of Lemma
7.6
.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{m}
be the set of
r
-uniform hypergraphs on
[n]
that contain the
k
hyperedges corresponding to
E_{1},\ldots,E_{k}
and satisfy the conditions of
\mathcal{W}_{m}
, and observe that

\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{W}_{m}\cap\{F\subset G(n,p)\}\big)=\sum_{H\in\mathcal{F}_{m}}\mathbb{P}\big(H_{r}(G(n,p))=H\big).

(7.4)

Every
H\in\mathcal{F}_{m}
is good by Definition
7.5
, so by Theorem
6.2
, the right-hand side of (
7.4
) is equal to

\sum_{H\in\mathcal{F}_{m}}\pi^{m}(1-\pi)^{N-m}p^{-t(H)}\exp\bigl(-\Delta_{2}+\Delta_{2}^{0}+O^{*}(\xi)+n^{-\Omega(1)}\bigr).

(7.5)

Note that
p^{-t(H)}
is the only term that depends on
H
, so our aim is to bound

\sum_{H\in\mathcal{F}_{m}}p^{-t(H)}.

Roughly speaking, the idea is to build a random hypergraph
H
starting with the
k
given edges and adding
m-k
new random edges one-by-one, considering how the
p^{-t(H)}
term evolves as we add edges, which we can conveniently do working in terms of expectations.

Let
h_{1},\ldots,h_{k}
be the hyperedges corresponding to
E_{1},\ldots,E_{k}
, and let the remaining edges
h_{k+1},\ldots,h_{m}
be chosen independently and uniformly at random from all
N=\binom{n}{r}
possible hyperedges. For
k\leqslant i\leqslant m
let
\tilde{H}_{i}
be the random hypergraph with edge-set
\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{i}\}
. Since
H\in\mathcal{F}_{m}
implies that
H
has
m
distinct hyperedges, there are exactly
(m-k)!
outcomes leading to
\tilde{H}_{m}=H
for any given
H\in\mathcal{F}_{m}
. Thus, in this probability space,

\mathbb{P}(\tilde{H}_{m}=H)=\frac{(m-k)!}{N^{m-k}},

and hence

\displaystyle\sum_{H\in\mathcal{F}_{m}}p^{-t(H)}

\displaystyle\,=\frac{N^{m-k}}{(m-k)!}\sum_{H\in\mathcal{F}_{m}}p^{-t(H)}\cdot\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{H}_{m}=H\big)

\displaystyle\,=\frac{N^{m-k}}{(m-k)!}\cdot\mathbb{E}\mathopen{\lx@scalerel@obj{\big[}}p^{-t(\tilde{H}_{m})}\mathbbm{1}\big[\tilde{H}_{m}\in\mathcal{F}_{m}\big]\mathclose{\lx@scalerel@obj{\big]}},

(7.6)

where the expectation is over the random choice of
h_{k+1},\ldots,h_{m}
.

To bound the expectation, we will first replace
t(\tilde{H}_{m})
by a simpler quantity which is bounded from above and below by the sums in Lemmas
7.7
and
7.9
. To do so, for each
2\leqslant i\leqslant m
and
2\leqslant s\leqslant r-1
let us write
A_{i,s}
for the event that
h_{i}
shares exactly
s
vertices with one previous
h_{j}
, and does not meet any other
h_{j^{\prime}}
with
j^{\prime}<i
in two or more vertices. Now, for each
2\leqslant\ell\leqslant m
, define

Z_{\ell}=\sum_{i=2}^{\ell}\sum_{s=2}^{r-1}\mathbbm{1}\big[A_{i,s}\big]\binom{s}{2},

and observe that

\sum_{s=2}^{r-1}{s\choose 2}t_{s}^{-}(\tilde{H}_{\ell})\leqslant Z_{\ell}\leqslant\sum_{s=2}^{r-1}{s\choose 2}t_{s}(\tilde{H}_{\ell}),

since
Z_{\ell}
counts the graph edges in a subset of the intersections between pairs of hyperedges of
\tilde{H}_{\ell}
(this proves the upper bound), and this subset includes all isolated
2
-clusters (this proves the lower bound). By Lemmas
7.7
and
7.9
, it follows that

t(\tilde{H}_{\ell})=Z_{\ell}+O(W_{3}(\tilde{H}_{\ell})).

Applying this for
\ell=k
and
\ell=m
, and noting that
W_{3}(\tilde{H}_{k})\leqslant W_{3}(\tilde{H}_{m})
, we obtain

t(\tilde{H}_{m})=t(\tilde{H}_{k})+Z_{m}-Z_{k}+O(W_{3}(\tilde{H}_{m})).

Now, by Definition
7.5
and Lemma
6.9
, if
H
is well behaved, then

W_{3}(H)\leqslant\omega\Delta_{3}=O(\omega\xi)=O^{*}(\xi).

Thus, on the event
\tilde{H}_{m}\in\mathcal{F}_{m}
, we have

t(\tilde{H}_{m})=t(\tilde{H}_{k})+Z_{m}-Z_{k}+O^{*}(\xi).

Recalling (
7.6
), this implies that

\sum_{H\in\mathcal{F}_{m}}p^{-t(H)}\leqslant e^{O^{*}(\xi)}\frac{N^{m-k}}{(m-k)!}\cdot p^{-t(F)}\cdot\mathbb{E}\mathopen{\lx@scalerel@obj{\big[}}p^{-Z_{m}+Z_{k}}\mathclose{\lx@scalerel@obj{\big]}},

(7.7)

since
\log(1/p)=O^{*}(1)
and
t(\tilde{H}_{k})=t(F)
, by definition.

To complete the proof, we will inductively bound
\mathbb{E}\big[p^{-Z_{\ell}+Z_{k}}\big]
. To be precise, we claim that for each
k<\ell\leqslant m
, we have

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{\lx@scalerel@obj{\big[}}\,p^{-Z_{\ell}+Z_{k}}\,\big|\,\tilde{H}_{\ell-1}\mathclose{\lx@scalerel@obj{\big]}}\leqslant\big(1+c(\ell)\big)\cdot p^{-Z_{\ell-1}+Z_{k}},

(7.8)

where

c(\ell)=(\ell-1)\sum_{s=2}^{r-1}\binom{r}{s}\binom{n-r}{r-s}{n\choose r}^{-1}\big(p^{-\binom{s}{2}}-1\big).

To show this, let us write

q(s):=\binom{r}{s}\binom{n-r}{r-s}{n\choose r}^{-1}

for the probability that a random
r
-set shares exactly
s
vertices with a given
r
-set, and observe that

\mathbb{P}\big(A_{\ell,s}\mid\tilde{H}_{\ell-1}\big)\leqslant(\ell-1)q(s)

for each
2\leqslant s\leqslant r-1
, by the union bound over edges of
\tilde{H}_{\ell-1}
. It follows that

\mathbb{P}\bigg(Z_{\ell}-Z_{\ell-1}={s\choose 2}\;\Big|\;\tilde{H}_{\ell-1}\bigg)\leqslant(\ell-1)q(s)

for each
2\leqslant s\leqslant r-1
, and otherwise
Z_{\ell}=Z_{\ell-1}
.
This proves (
7.8
), and it follows that

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{\lx@scalerel@obj{\big[}}p^{-Z_{m}+Z_{k}}\mathclose{\lx@scalerel@obj{\big]}}\leqslant\prod_{\ell=k+1}^{m}\big(1+c(\ell)\big)\leqslant\exp\bigg(\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{m}c(\ell)\bigg).

(7.9)

It only remains to bound the sum in (
7.9
). To do so, observe first that

\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{m}c(\ell)=\bigg(\binom{m}{2}-\binom{k}{2}\bigg)\sum_{s=2}^{r-1}q(s)\big(p^{-\binom{s}{2}}-1\big),

(7.10)

since
\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{m}(\ell-1)=\binom{m}{2}-\binom{k}{2}
. Note also that

\nu_{s}^{0}=\frac{q(s)}{2}\binom{n}{r}^{2}p^{2\binom{r}{2}}=q(s)\cdot\frac{\mu_{r}^{2}}{2},

by (
6.12
) and (
6.13
), and the definition of
q(s)
, and hence

q(s)\cdot\binom{m}{2}=\big(1+O^{*}(1/\sqrt{\mu_{r}})\big)\cdot\nu_{s}^{0},

since
|m-\mu_{r}|\leqslant\omega\sqrt{\mu_{r}}
. Since
\nu_{s}=p^{-\binom{s}{2}}\nu_{s}^{0}
, it follows from (
7.10
) that

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{m}c(\ell)

\displaystyle\,=\big(1+O^{*}(1/\sqrt{\mu_{r}})\big)\bigg(1-\binom{k}{2}\binom{m}{2}^{-1}\bigg)\sum_{s=2}^{r-1}\big(\nu_{s}-\nu_{s}^{0}\big)

\displaystyle\,=\bigg(1-\binom{k}{2}\binom{m}{2}^{-1}\bigg)\big(\Delta_{2}-\Delta_{2}^{0}\big)+O^{*}\bigg(\frac{\Delta_{2}}{\sqrt{\mu_{r}}}\bigg),

(7.11)

since
\Delta_{2}=\sum_{s=2}^{r-1}\nu_{s}
and
\Delta_{2}^{0}=\sum_{s=2}^{r-1}\nu_{s}^{0}
.

We are finally ready to put the pieces together. First, by (
7.4
) and (
7.5
),

\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{W}_{m}\cap\{F\subset G(n,p)\}\big)\leqslant\pi^{m}(1-\pi)^{N-m}e^{-\Delta_{2}+\Delta_{2}^{0}+O^{*}(\xi)+n^{-\Omega(1)}}\sum_{H\in\mathcal{F}_{m}}p^{-t(H)}.

Next, by (
7.7
), (
7.9
) and (
7.11
), we have

\sum_{H\in\mathcal{F}_{m}}p^{-t(H)}\leqslant\frac{N^{m-k}}{(m-k)!}\cdot p^{-t(F)}\cdot e^{(1-\binom{k}{2}\binom{m}{2}^{-1})(\Delta_{2}-\Delta_{2}^{0})+O^{*}(\xi)+O^{*}(\Delta_{2}/\sqrt{\mu_{r}})}.

Noting that

{N\choose m}^{-1}\frac{N^{m-k}}{(m-k)!}=e^{O(m^{2}/N)}\frac{(m)_{k}}{(N)_{k}}=e^{O(\xi)}\frac{(m)_{k}}{(N)_{k}},

since
k\leqslant m
and
m^{2}/N=O(n^{2-r+O(\gamma)})=O(\xi)
, and recalling from (
6.11
) that

\frac{\Delta_{2}}{\sqrt{\mu_{r}}}=O(\xi)+n^{-\Omega(1)},

it follows that

\frac{\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{W}_{m}\cap\{F\subset G(n,p)\}\big)}{\mathbb{P}\big(\mathrm{Bin}(N,\pi)=m\big)}\leqslant\frac{(m)_{k}}{(N)_{k}}\cdot p^{-t(F)}\cdot e^{-\binom{k}{2}\binom{m}{2}^{-1}(\Delta_{2}-\Delta_{2}^{0})+O^{*}(\xi)+n^{-\Omega(1)}},

as required.
∎

It is straightforward to deduce Theorem
7.4
from Lemma
7.6
.

Proof of Theorem
7.4
.

We may assume that
p\geqslant q_{r}(n)/2
, since otherwise we would have
F_{r}(G(n,p))=0
with high probability. In particular, it follows from this assumption that
p\geqslant n^{-2/r}
, and thus
\mu_{r}\to\infty
and
\xi=\Omega(1)
. Applying Lemma
7.6
to each of the
K_{r}
-factors in
K_{n}
, and setting
k=n/r
, we obtain

\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[F_{r}(G(n,p))\mathbbm{1}[\mathcal{W}_{m}]\big]}{\mathbb{P}\big(\mathrm{Bin}(N,\pi)=m\big)}\leqslant F_{r}(K_{n})\cdot\frac{(m)_{k}}{(N)_{k}}\cdot e^{-\binom{k}{2}\binom{m}{2}^{-1}(\Delta_{2}-\Delta_{2}^{0})\,+\,O^{*}(\xi)},

for each
m
with
|m-\mu_{r}|\leqslant\omega\sqrt{\mu_{r}}
, since if
F
is a
K_{r}
-factor then
t(F)=0
.

Now, since
\Delta_{2}\geqslant\Delta_{2}^{0}
, and noting that

\Sigma(n,m)=F_{r}(K_{n})\cdot\frac{(m)_{n/r}}{(N)_{n/r}},

it follows that

\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[F_{r}(G(n,p))\mathbbm{1}[\mathcal{W}_{m}]\big]}{\mathbb{P}\big(\mathrm{Bin}(N,\pi)=m\big)}\leqslant e^{O^{*}(\xi)}\,\Sigma(n,m).

Summing over all
m
in the range, it follows that

\mathbb{E}\big[F_{r}(G(n,p))\mathbbm{1}[\mathcal{W}]\big]\leqslant e^{O^{*}(\xi)}\,\Sigma(n,m^{+}),

where
\mathcal{W}
is the event that
H_{r}(G(n,p))
is well behaved (which in particular implies that
|m-\mu_{r}|\leqslant\omega\sqrt{\mu_{r}}
), and
m^{+}=m+\omega\sqrt{m}
.
Since

\frac{\Sigma(n,m^{+})}{\Sigma(n,m)}=\bigg(\frac{m^{+}}{m}\bigg)^{O(n)}=\exp\bigg(O^{*}\bigg(\frac{n}{\sqrt{m}}\bigg)\bigg)=e^{O^{*}(\sqrt{n})}

and
\xi\leqslant n^{1/3+O(\gamma)}
, it follows that

\mathbb{E}\big[F_{r}(G(n,p))\mathbbm{1}[\mathcal{W}]\big]\leqslant e^{O^{*}(\sqrt{n})}\,\Sigma(n,m).

Since
\mathcal{W}
holds with high probability, by Markov’s inequality the right-hand side above is an upper bound on
F_{r}(G(n,p))
that holds with high probability, completing the proof.
∎

Replacing the trivial bound
\Delta_{2}\geqslant\Delta_{2}^{0}
with a simple calculation gives a stronger result for fixed
m
. To be precise, one can easily check that

\Delta_{2}=\big(1+o(1)\big)\frac{\mu_{r}^{2}}{p}\binom{r}{2}^{2}\binom{n}{2}^{-1}

and
\Delta_{2}^{0}\sim p\Delta_{2}=o(\Delta_{2})
, and hence, for
k=n/r
and
m\sim\mu_{r}
, we have

\binom{k}{2}\binom{m}{2}^{-1}\big(\Delta_{2}-\Delta_{2}^{0}\big)\sim\frac{(r-1)^{2}}{2p}.

It therefore follows from the proof above that

\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[F_{r}(G(n,p))\mathbbm{1}[\mathcal{W}_{m}]\big]}{\mathbb{P}\big(\mathrm{Bin}(N,\pi)=m\big)}\leqslant\exp\bigg(-(1+o(1))\frac{(r-1)^{2}}{2p}\,+\,O^{*}(\xi)\bigg)\cdot\Sigma(n,m)

for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
with
|m-\mu_{r}|\leqslant\omega\sqrt{\mu_{r}}
. This bound illustrates an interesting phenomenon: in
G(n,p)
, given that there are exactly
m
copies of
K_{r}
, the conditional expected number of
K_{r}
-factors is noticeably (around
\exp(-\Theta(p^{-1}))
) smaller than the expected number of matchings in
H_{r}(n,m)
. Intuitively, the reason for this is that
H_{r}(G(n,p))
prefers intersecting pairs of copies of
K_{r}
over disjoint ones, so given that there are
m
copies of
K_{r}
, we expect more intersecting pairs than in
H_{r}(n,m)
, and so fewer factors.

7.2
The lower bound

In this section we outline the proof of Theorem
7.1
, which as discussed earlier is a slight modification of Kahn’s proof of Theorem 1.2 in
[
13
]
.

Proof of Theorem
7.1
.

As in
[
13
]
, start with the complete
r
-uniform hypergraph and remove hyperedges one by one uniformly at random, letting
\Phi_{t}
be the number of matchings after
t
steps, so the random variable we are interested in is
\Phi_{N-m}
where
N=\binom{n}{r}
. Let
\xi_{t}
be the random fraction of matchings removed in step
t
, and
\gamma_{t}
its expectation, which is simply
(n/r)/(N-t+1)
, since each of the
n/r
edges in some particular matching has probability
1/(N-t+1)
of being chosen for removal in step
t
. Note that

\Phi_{m}=\Phi_{0}\prod_{t=1}^{N-m}(1-\xi_{t}),

(7.12)

and, considering the probability that any particular matching survives,

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}{}}[\Phi_{m}]=\Phi_{0}\prod_{t=1}^{N-m}(1-\gamma_{t}).

One key point (of many!) in
[
13
]
is that
\alpha_{t}=\xi_{t}-\gamma_{t}
is a martingale difference sequence, and furthermore that (off some bad event, on which he freezes the martingale)
\xi_{t}=O(\gamma_{t})
(see (16) in
[
13
]
), so
\alpha_{t}=O(\gamma_{t})
. It follows that
\sum_{t=1}^{N-m}\alpha_{t}=O(\sqrt{S})
with high probability, where
S=\sum_{t=1}^{N-m}\gamma_{t}^{2}=O(n/\log n)
. To evaluate the product in (
7.12
), one considers the log. Since
\xi_{t}=O(\gamma_{t})=o(1)
, we have
\log(1-\xi_{t})=-\xi_{t}+O(\gamma_{t}^{2})
, and this is where the final error in
[
13
]
comes from: it is written as
o(n)
, but is in fact
O(S)=O(n/\log n)
.

We can improve on this by considering the variance of the
\xi_{t}
. Although not essential, it is cleaner to consider the product

\frac{\Phi_{m}}{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}{}}[\Phi_{m}]}=\prod_{t=1}^{N-m}\frac{1-\xi_{t}}{1-\gamma_{t}}=\prod_{t=1}^{N-m}\left(1-\frac{\xi_{t}-\gamma_{t}}{1-\gamma_{t}}\right).

Let
\alpha_{t}^{\prime}=(\xi_{t}-\gamma_{t})/(1-\gamma_{t})=\alpha_{t}/(1-\gamma_{t})
. This is again a martingale difference sequence, and each term is
O(\gamma_{t})
. So we again have that
\sum_{t=1}^{N-m}\alpha_{t}^{\prime}=O(\sqrt{S})
with high probability. Moreover, since
\alpha_{t}^{\prime}=o(1)
for all
t\in[N-m]
, we have

\log\frac{\Phi_{m}}{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}{}}[\Phi_{m}]}=-\sum_{t=1}^{N-m}\alpha_{t}^{\prime}-\frac{1+o(1)}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{N-m}(\alpha_{t}^{\prime})^{2}.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{t}
denote the
\sigma
-algebra corresponding to the information revealed after
t
steps, and define

V_{t}=\mathbb{E}\big[(\alpha_{t}^{\prime})^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]=(1-\gamma_{t})^{-2}\Var\big(\xi_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big).

Since
\alpha_{t}^{\prime}=O(\gamma_{t})
we have that
(\alpha_{t}^{\prime})^{2}-V_{t}
is a martingale difference sequence with each term of size
O(\gamma_{t}^{2})=o(\gamma_{t})
, and it follows that the sum of these terms is with high probability
o(\sqrt{S})
, so it just remains to bound
\sum_{t=1}^{N-m}V_{t}
. If we only use the bound
V_{t}=O(\gamma_{t}^{2})
effectively used in
[
13
]
, we are still left with an
O(n/\log n)
error term. However, although it is not stated there,
[
13
]
contains all the ingredients to prove a stronger bound. The key is that, in addition to the deterministic bound
\xi_{t}=O(\gamma_{t})
, the property
\mathfrak{E}
defined just below (62) implies that (off the same bad event)
\xi_{t}\sim\gamma_{t}
with conditional probability
1-o(1)
. Together, these two bounds imply that
\Var\big(\xi_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big)=o(\gamma_{t}^{2})
, so
V_{t}=o(\gamma_{t}^{2})
. Hence we have
\sum_{t=1}^{N-m}V_{t}=o(S)
, giving the claimed improvement.
∎

Note that, by the same argument, a stronger result would follow from any improvement of this ‘approximate’ flatness of the distribution of
\xi_{t}
given
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
.
