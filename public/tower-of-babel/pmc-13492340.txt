Primary cardiac angiosarcoma mimicking minoxidil-related pericarditis: a case report
Authors: Akinori Takahashi, Motoki Nakao, Toshiyuki Nagai, Suguru Ishizaka, Toshihisa Anzai, Julio Echarte, Moisés Jiménez Santos, Mattia Alberti, Deepti Ranganathan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiac angiosarcoma, a rare and highly malignant primary cardiac tumour with poor prognosis, often presents diagnostic challenges owing to nonspecific clinical manifestations, with some cases initially presenting with acute pericarditis. Minoxidil, a commonly used treatment for androgenetic alopecia (AGA), has been associated with drug-induced pericarditis.Case summaryA 23-year-old man with no significant medical history presented with chest pain at rest and pericardial effusion. The patient had been taking 5 mg/day of oral minoxidil for AGA; thus, drug-induced pericarditis was initially suspected. His symptoms improved with aspirin and colchicine. However, he experienced recurrent episodes of pericarditis. On the third presentation, echocardiography revealed cardiac tamponade, and pericardiocentesis showed haemorrhagic pericardial effusion with elevated tumour markers. Imaging studies supported the suspicion of a primary cardiac malignancy. Surgical biopsy confirmed the diagnosis of cardiac angiosarcoma. The patient underwent extensive tumour resection followed by adjuvant chemotherapy and proton beam therapy and was discharged without evidence of recurrence. However, metastatic recurrence subsequently developed, and he died 30 months after surgery.DiscussionThis case highlights the importance of considering malignancy in the differential diagnosis of recurrent pericarditis in young adults. Although minoxidil-related pericarditis was initially suspected, multimodal imaging and histopathological evaluation led to the diagnosis of cardiac angiosarcoma. Introduction

Cardiac angiosarcoma is a rare, highly malignant primary cardiac tumour with a poor prognosis. Owing to its nonspecific clinical presentation, diagnosis is often delayed; however, some cases present with acute pericarditis.1 Low dose oral minoxidil, used for androgenetic alopecia (AGA),2 has been associated with drug-induced pericarditis.3

Here, we present a case of cardiac angiosarcoma mimicking minoxidil-related pericarditis.

Case presentation

A 23-year-old man presented to the emergency department of our hospital with chest pain at rest worsened by deep inspiration. He had no relevant medical or family history but had been taking oral minoxidil (5 mg/day) for androgenetic alopecia for 1 year. Electrocardiography (ECG) showed diffuse ST-segment elevation. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) showed pericardial effusion without cardiac tamponade. Chest computed tomography (CT) revealed small pericardial effusion, with no pleural effusion, ascites, mass, lymphadenopathy, or hepatosplenomegaly. Thus, minoxidil-related pericarditis was initially diagnosed. He was treated with oral aspirin (3000 mg/day) and colchicine (0.5 mg/day).

After the pericardial effusion decreased, the patient was discharged, and aspirin was tapered during outpatient follow-up. Two months after his initial visit, he presented again with non-exertional chest pain and was diagnosed with recurrent pericarditis. His symptoms improved after increasing the aspirin dose, which was tapered again.

Six months after his initial visit, the patient presented to our hospital with chest pain at rest, exacerbated by deep breathing. Upon admission, the patient was hypotensive and mildly tachycardic. He appeared tachypnoeic with cold, diaphoretic skin, with no signs of heart failure. Given the recurrence of pericarditis even after discontinuation of minoxidil, minoxidil-induced pericarditis was considered less likely, prompting hospitalization for further evaluation.

Chest radiography showed no cardiomegaly or pulmonary congestion. Electrocardiography demonstrated sinus rhythm with ST-segment elevation in leads II, III, aVF, V5, and V6. Tumour markers were as follows: hyaluronic acid (HA) 33.0 ng/mL (normal <50.0 ng/mL), carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) 1.7 ng/mL (normal <5.0 ng/mL), cytokeratin 19 fragments (CYFRA) 1.2 ng/mL (normal <32.1 ng/mL), and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) antigen 2.0 ng/mL (normal <2.0 ng/mL). Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 71% and no findings of a mass around the heart. Pericardial effusion of approximately 10 mm was observed around both ventricles, along with dilated inferior vena cava (IVC) (Figure 1A). Echocardiographic findings, including sonographic pulsus paradoxus and early-diastolic right ventricular collapse, together with hypotension, supported the diagnosis of cardiac tamponade despite the absence of clinical pulsus paradoxus or elevated jugular venous pressure. Pericardiocentesis was performed via a subxiphoid approach, leading to immediate haemodynamic stabilization, and 270 mL of bloody pericardial fluid was aspirated. Analysis of the pericardial fluid revealed red blood cells 3 840 000/μL, haemoglobin 11.0 g/dL, lactate dehydrogenase 323 U/L, protein 5.7 g/dL, adenosine deaminase 14.8 U/L, HA 318 000 ng/mL, CEA 1.7 ng/mL, CYFRA 75.1 ng/mL, and SCC antigen 6.6 ng/mL. Markedly elevated pericardial fluid HA and CYFRA with low CEA raised suspicion for malignant pericardial mesothelioma. Although pericardial-fluid cut-offs have not been validated, pleural-fluid data suggest that high HA is highly specific, but it lacks sensitivity for mesothelioma.4 However, these markers are insufficient to establish a definitive diagnosis, and further evaluation was required. Cytological examination of the pericardial fluid revealed no malignant cells. Bacterial cultures and smear microscopy for Mycobacterium tuberculosis were negative. Based on recurrent pericarditis, haemorrhagic pericardial effusion, and elevated tumour markers in the pericardial fluid, malignant pericardial disease was suspected. Multimodality imaging was therefore conducted to investigate the aetiology of the recurrent pericarditis.

Although TTE was useful for detecting pericardial effusion and haemodynamic compromise, it did not reveal a definite pericardial mass. Electrocardiography -gated cardiac CT provided anatomical information by identifying focal thickening and a 10 mm mass at the roof of the right atrium (Figures 1B, 2A), a region that was difficult to evaluate adequately on standard TTE views. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) provided additional tissue characterization, demonstrating diffuse pericardial thickening and a solid mass surrounding the right atrium (Figure 2B). These findings suggested an infiltrative malignant process rather than isolated recurrent pericarditis, raising suspicion for cardiac lymphoma or malignant pericardial mesothelioma. Transoesophageal echocardiogram (TEE) clearly delineated a 29 × 33 mm irregular mass on the anterior wall of the right atrium, extending from the superior vena cava (Figure 3A) to the vicinity of the IVC (Figure 3B). Blood flow signals were detected within the tumour (Figure 3C), suggesting a vascular cardiac tumour. Coronary angiography (CAG) showed multiple tumour feeding vessels from the right coronary artery to the suspected lesion in the right atrium (Figure 4), indicating a hypervascular cardiac tumour. 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography revealed increased FDG uptake on the anterior surface of the right atrium, with no abnormal uptake in other organs, suggesting a primary cardiac malignant tumour. There was no evidence of distant metastasis. On the basis of the CT and CMR findings, malignant lymphoma and malignant pericardial mesothelioma were considered as differential diagnoses. Findings from TEE suggested cardiac angiosarcoma. Chemotherapy is recommended for malignant lymphoma and pericardial mesothelioma, whereas extended surgical resection is the first-line therapy for cardiac angiosarcoma (see Supplementary material online, Figure S1). Since the treatment strategy depends on the tumour type and extensive resection is not appropriate without a definitive diagnosis, partial tumour resection for biopsy was planned in accordance with the guidelines (class IIa recommendation).5

The patient underwent biopsy of the right atrial tumour via surgical sternotomy. The mass was adherent to the right atrial wall. Intraoperative rapid diagnosis revealed a proliferation of spindle-shaped cells, which was highly suspicious for sarcoma. Immunohistochemical staining showed strong reactivity for cluster of differentiation (CD) 31 (Figure 5), CD34, and ETS-related gene, consistent with angiosarcoma. Extended surgical resection of the tumour was considered the first-line therapy for angiosarcoma. Follow-up chest CT demonstrated increased tumour size, prompting surgery. Extended tumour resection was performed, with the 55 × 30 mm mass excised grossly with clear margins. A large patch of bovine pericardium was used to reconstruct the right atrium and atrial septum. Histopathology confirmed the diagnosis of cardiac angiosarcoma. Postoperatively, he was treated with adjuvant chemotherapy using paclitaxel (40 mg/m2 on days 1, 8, and 15) for two cycles and proton beam therapy (70 Gy equivalent in 35 fractions over 7 weeks). The patient was discharged without evidence of tumour recurrence or distant metastasis. Four months after surgery, new pulmonary nodules were detected, suggestive of lung metastases. Although the patient was enrolled in a clinical trial, the disease progressed, and standard chemotherapy was subsequently administered. Hepatic dysfunction later developed because of progression of liver metastases, leading to a transition to best supportive care. The patient died 30 months after surgery.

Discussion

Primary cardiac malignant tumours are extremely rare,6 and cardiac angiosarcoma is the most common type. Owing to its nonspecific presentation, early diagnosis is challenging, although some cases present with pericarditis.1 Several reports have described young patients with unexplained recurrent pericardial effusions eventually diagnosed with cardiac angiosarcoma.7,8 Therefore, malignancy should be considered in young adults with unexplained recurrent pericardial effusion, particularly when it is haemorrhagic.9

The present case shared several typical features, including right atrial involvement, haemorrhagic pericardial effusion, and cardiac tamponade.10 However, very young age, transient response to anti-inflammatory treatment, and initial suspicion of minoxidil-related pericarditis complicated the diagnosis. The initial response to aspirin and colchicine was nonspecific and may have reflected transient improvement in tumour-associated pericardial inflammation. Negative pericardial fluid cytology was also a diagnostic pitfall rather than evidence against malignancy, as cytology can be negative in cardiac angiosarcoma despite pericardial effusion, possibly because of limited exfoliation of tumour cells into the pericardial space.11,12

Minoxidil, originally introduced as a vasodilator for resistant hypertension,13 is widely used at low doses for AGA.2 It has been associated with drug-related pericarditis and is listed as a potential cause in the ESC guidelines.3,5 Initially, minoxidil-related pericarditis was suspected; however, recurrence after discontinuation of minoxidil and haemorrhagic pericardial effusion prompted evaluation for underlying malignancy. Because multimodality imaging and cytological analysis were insufficient for a definitive diagnosis, a pericardial biopsy was performed, confirming cardiac angiosarcoma. Our diagnostic approach remained consistent with the revised 2025 ESC recommendations for pericardial diseases.14 While minoxidil is known to cause sodium and water retention,13 no clinical or mechanistic link has been established between minoxidil and cardiac angiosarcoma. Thus, minoxidil was most likely an apparent alternative explanation that delayed recognition of the underlying malignancy rather than a proven contributor to tumour formation.

The prognosis of primary cardiac angiosarcoma remains poor. A systematic literature review reported a median overall survival of approximately 12 months, although multimodality treatment including surgery and chemoradiotherapy may improve survival.10 In the present case, despite extended tumour resection followed by adjuvant chemotherapy and proton beam therapy, pulmonary and hepatic metastases subsequently developed, and the patient died 30 months after surgery. This clinical course highlights the aggressive nature of cardiac angiosarcoma and the importance of early suspicion and histological confirmation in patients with unexplained recurrent haemorrhagic pericardial effusion.

Conclusion

This case highlights the importance of considering cardiac tumour in recurrent pericarditis, even in young adults with suspected drug-related pericarditis. Pericardial biopsy helps to identify the aetiology of pericardial effusion and should be considered to confirm the presence of a malignant cardiac tumour.
