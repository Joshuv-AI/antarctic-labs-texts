Assessing climate change effects on streamflow and paddy production in the Bharathapuzha Basin Kerala
Authors: Gulja S. Nair, D. Karunanidhi, Berihu Abadi Berhe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In humid tropical regions, where paddy production is heavily reliant on monsoon rainfall and regulated streamflow, climate variability remains a major threat to agricultural sustainability. Using projections from three CMIP6 climate models under two shared socioeconomic pathways and the Soil and Water Assessment Tool (SWAT), this study examines how climate variations may affect hydrological processes and paddy yield in the Bharathapuzha River watershed in Kerala, India. The SWAT model was calibrated and validated against observed streamflow and district-level yield statistics, achieving NSE = 0.74 (monthly) for streamflow and NSE = 0.80 (RSR = 0.45) for Palakkad and NSE = 0.68 (RSR = 0.57) for Alathur for paddy yield. .Results indicate significant increases in rainfall variability and temperature across the CMIP6 models, leading to altered streamflow regimes characterized by intensified flood peaks and heightened dry-season stress. Projected paddy yields show consistent declines, ranging from 23 to 33% by 2035 to as much as 68–74% by 2100 under SSP5-8.5. The Alathur block exhibits greater near-term sensitivity, while the Palakkad block faces stronger late-century declines. These results highlight the vulnerability of Kerala’s paddy cultivation systems to changing climatic conditions and underscore the importance of adaptive measures—including climate-resilient crop varieties, optimized planting schedules, and improved irrigation management—to ensure long-term regional food security.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52356-3.

Introduction

Climate variation poses severe risks to crop and water sustainability, especially in humid tropical regional reliant on monsoon rainfall. In India, where rice is the staple for millions, changes in temperature, precipitation, and extreme events directly threaten both hydrology and availability of food1,2. Important contributors to climate change have been determined to be carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions. Since the anthropological interventions have greatly increased the amount of CO2 in the atmosphere1. In 2024, atmospheric CO₂ levels surged by 3.75 ppm, the highest annual increase on record, according to NOAA analysis of globally averaged marine surface data3. Since climate change directly affects resource availability in terms of time and place, it has the greatest impact on agriculture and water resources. India has recently witnessed an increase in natural calamities, such as floods, droughts etc. Numerous studies investigated and comprehend these phenomena and their consequences4. These climate changes have the potential to worsen food insecurity, lower agricultural production, and increase competition for water resources if they are not handled. In tropical and subtropical areas like India, where smallholder farming predominates and agriculture is highly reliant on monsoon rains, the effects are anticipated to be especially severe. Therefore, region-specific modeling and climate forecasting are necessary for mitigation and adaptation plans to be successful.

The primary climatic variables projected to change under CMIP6 scenarios include precipitation and temperature, both of which exert strong control on hydrological processes. Variations in rainfall influence surface runoff generation, groundwater recharge, and streamflow regimes, while rising temperatures increase evapotranspiration demand and alter the soil–water balance. In monsoon-dependent basins such as the Bharathapuzha, these combined effects intensify hydrological extremes, leading to increased flood peaks during the wet season and reduced baseflows during the dry season. Increased rainfall variability further amplifies these effects by enhancing surface runoff, reducing groundwater recharge, and creating pronounced seasonal imbalances in streamflow. This results in significant alterations in streamflow regimes, including shifts in seasonal discharge patterns and increased frequency of hydrological extremes.

The IPCC’s 6th Assessment Report (AR6, 2021–2023), projects that over pre-industrial levels, the global temperatures are increasing by 1.5 °C by 2030s under majority of emission scenarios5. It is anticipated that this warming will increase the frequency of droughts and floods in South Asia, change monsoon circulation patterns, and amplify extreme precipitation events. These changes make agricultural systems more vulnerable, especially in areas that depend on the monsoon. According to international estimates, rice yields in tropical regions may decrease in the range of 3–10% for each one degree Celsius rise in temperature6–8. Moreover, it has been demonstrated that increasing nighttime temperatures shorten grain filling times, speed up rice phenology, and decrease yields9–11. Although adaptation strategies like better irrigation scheduling, genetically modified cultivers, and climate-informed cropping calendars are being investigated globally, it is still unclear how beneficial they will be at the basin level without a location-specific analysis.

The India Meteorological Department (IMD) reports that in recent decades, India has undergone marked climate changes, including higher average temperatures, shifts in monsoon timing, and a rise in catastrophic weather incidents’ both intensity and frequency. A warming trend of about 0.7 °C per century is shown by long-term records, with recent decades seeing faster rises12–15. Significant drops in streamflow and rainfall dependability have been documented in a number of studies throughout large river basins, which may have consequences for agriculture that depends on irrigation16. Potential yield losses for staple crops, including rice, under future warming scenarios have been emphasized by research conducted in various agroclimatic zones of India11,17,18. Spatial variation in climate impacts, however, emphasizes the significance of basin-scale studies, especially in areas like Kerala where monsoon dependence is strong and paddy farming is a major source of income19–21.

Kerala’s agricultural environment revolves around paddy agriculture. In the 1970s, Kerala had more than 8,80,000 ha of paddy fields; however, because of land conversion, labor shortages, and difficulties with water management, the area under cultivation has decreased to less than 2,00,000 ha in recent years22–24.Paddy, which is mostly grown during the Virippu and Mundakan seasons, continues to be the main staple crop in spite of this reduction.

Although the state is ideal for paddy due to its high rainfall and humid tropical environment, such regions are highly vulnerable to climate variability due to their strong dependence on monsoon rainfall patterns. Paddy cultivation is water-intensive and closely synchronized with seasonal rainfall, making it particularly sensitive to variations in rainfall timing, intensity, and distribution. Even minor shifts in monsoon onset or prolonged dry spells can disrupt key growth stages such as transplantation and grain filling. Due to its heavy reliance on monsoon rainfall and controlled river flows, Kerala’s paddy production is vulnerable to both seasonal droughts and floods. Unseasonable rainfall, unpredictable monsoon start, and protracted dry spells have caused planting schedule disruptions and output reductions in a number of districts in recent years. Furthermore, rising temperatures and evapotranspiration rates brought on by climate change may make irrigation more necessary and increase rivalry for water among the residential, commercial, and agricultural sectors. The interaction of decreasing streamflow, changing rainfall patterns, and rising temperatures presents a serious threat to maintaining paddy yield, especially in the study location. In addition, intensified flood events during the monsoon can cause waterlogging and crop damage, while reduced water availability during dry periods affects critical growth stages such as flowering and grain filling. The combined effect of these hydrological extremes can significantly reduce paddy yield under climate variability.

Bharathapuzha river basin is home to a vast rice field that is mostly reliant on regulated streamflow and monsoon rainfall. The Soil and Water Assessment Tool (SWAT) is a physically based, semi-distributed model designed to simulate the interactions between climate, hydrology, and crop growth processes at the watershed scale25–29. It utilizes daily climate inputs such as precipitation and temperature to simulate key hydrological components including surface runoff, evapotranspiration, soil moisture, and groundwater flow, while also modelling crop growth and yield responses. It is intended for long-term, continuous simulations. Surface runoff, evapotranspiration, soil moisture dynamics, groundwater recharge, and agricultural production may all be simulated thanks to its modular design. SWAT has been used extensively around the world to evaluate diverse components of a watershed including effects of climate variability30–33. It has been utilized in India to assess crop yield as well as streamflow fluctuations in river basins such the Godavari, Mahanadi, and Periyar6,21,34–41. SWAT is an effective tool for investigations of the consequences of global warming, because it can incorporate downscaled climatic estimates. Owing to the Bharathapuzha basin’s diverse land use patterns, noticeable wet-dry seasonality, and close hydrological ties to agricultural output, SWAT is especially appropriate for the Bharathapuzha basin due to its ability to simulate the interactions between hydrological processes and irrigation-dependent paddy cultivation under varying climatic conditions21,42,43.Concerns about water supply and crop production in the basin have increased as a result of recent trends in decreasing discharge, changed rainfall patterns, and rising temperatures39,44,45. Being a humid tropical watershed, it is especially vulnerable to changes in evapotranspiration, seasonal variability, and precipitation intensity. Despite numerous SWAT-based studies across India, comprehensive assessments that simultaneously evaluate hydrology and paddy yield under CMIP6 projections remain scarce for Kerala’s humid tropical basins.

Numerous studies employing the Soil and Water Assessment Tool have examined the impacts of global climate variations on crop yields and hydrological processes across diverse climatic regions28,46–49. Under high-emission scenarios, SWAT simulations and CMIP5 predictions in the Mahanadi basin suggested a probable decline in monsoon streamflow and rice yield50. In Southeast Asia’s humid tropical basins, where integrated hydrology–crop modeling showed that temperature increases and changed rainfall patterns could lower rice yield by as much as 15% by the middle of the century, SWAT has been effectively implemented outside of India51.These studies offer useful methodological insights, but because of Kerala’s distinct monsoon regime, land use patterns, and paddy cultivation practices, their conclusions cannot be applied directly to the state’s humid tropical climate.

Although previous studies in Kerala21,48 have independently examined rainfall–streamflow trends or crop-yield responses, integrated climate-impact assessments combining hydrological modelling and crop-yield simulation for the Bharathapuzha basin remain limited. This study contributes to the existing knowledge by providing a basin-scale, SWAT-based analysis using three contrasting CMIP6 models, offering block-level (Palakkad and Alathur) paddy-yield projections across multiple future horizons, and identifying the agro-climatic mechanisms driving differences in climate sensitivity between the two blocks. The combined assessment of hydrological extremes, crop productivity, and adaptation strategies provides new insights that are directly relevant for regional water and agricultural planning.

By integrating the Soil and Water Assessment Tool with CMIP6 climatic projections, the present work aims to understand existing deficiencies in knowledge by examining the implications of climate variation on streamflow and paddy yield of Bharathapuzha basin. The specific objectives of the research are to: (i) quantify the projected variations in streamflow and paddy yield under mid-21st-century climate scenarios; (ii) utilize CMIP6 climate models namely EC-Earth3-Veg, INM-CM5-0, and MPI-ESM1-2-HR under two shared socio economic pathways, SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5, to forecast the climate impacts; (iii) train and test the SWAT model for the basin; and (iv) propose adaptive strategies, including climate-resilient paddy cultivation practices and sustainable water management approaches, for Kerala’s humid tropical regions.

Methodology

Study area location

The Bharathapuzha watershed spans the districts of Palakkad, Thrissur, and Malappuram and is roughly 6,186 km² in size, with lesser areas extending into Tamil Nadu. The river, called Nila locally, rises 1,965 m above MSL in the Anamalai Hills in the Western Ghats. It travels west for around 209 km until it joins Arabian Sea at Ponnani. The basin has distinct wet and dry seasons with a humid tropical climate. The Northeast monsoon (October–November) complements the Southwest monsoon (June–September) as the main source of rainfall.

The Thuthapuzha, Gayathripuzha, Kalpathipuzha, and Kannadipuzha rivers are among the basin’s numerous tributaries, which are essential for irrigation and residential water supply. There are low-lying coastal plains in the west, rolling midlands in the middle, and highlands in the east. The basin is particularly noteworthy for its heavy reliance on controlled irrigation systems and flow regimes fed by the monsoon. Figure 1 illustrates where the study basin is located.

Present research focuses on Palakkad and Alathur blocks within the Palakkad district, which form part of the core agricultural zone of the Bharathapuzha Basin. These blocks are located in the mid-to-lowland stretches of the basin, where extensive paddy fields are supported by canal irrigation from major projects such as Malampuzha.Palakkad Block, known as the “granary of Kerala”, benefits from fertile alluvial soils, relatively flat terrain, and the availability of irrigation water from both river systems and canals. The predominant cropping pattern includes paddy in the Virippu (Kharif) and Mundakan (Rabi) seasons, often followed by pulses or vegetables as third crops where water permits.Alathur Block, located downstream of Palakkad town, has mixed cropping systems but remains heavily dependent on paddy cultivation. The block’s agriculture is supported by both major and minor irrigation canals, as well as local tanks. Alathur also experiences a stronger influence of erratic rainfall patterns and has comparatively less reliable irrigation support than Palakkad, making it more sensitive to water availability fluctuations. Its downstream location within the basin, combined with variations in soil properties and water retention capacity, further contributes to its higher sensitivity to climate variability.

The basin exhibits a humid tropical climate with marked interannual variability. Annual rainfall during 1998–2017 ranged between ~ 700 mm (2003) and over 2100 mm (2007), while mean annual temperature was ~ 24.2 °C with a gradual warming trend, particularly in minimum temperatures. Streamflow is strongly monsoon-dependent with prominent flows across June–November and lesser flows in summer, consistent with previous analyses39.

Data used

For hydrological and crop yield simulations in the Bharathapuzha River Basin, this work used observed hydro-meteorological, crop yield, spatial, and climate prediction datasets. From 1998 to 2017, the India Meteorological Department provided precipitation and maximum/minimum temperature data on a daily basis. The Central Water Commission provided the discharge data for the Kuttipuram station over the same time period. Annual statistics on paddy yields from 2008 to 2017 were gathered from the Kerala government’s Agricultural Statistics Board, and were used to validate simulated crop yields, thereby improving the reliability and regional relevance of the model outputs. Land use maps from the National Remote Sensing Centre, soil maps from the FAO’s Land and Water Development Division, and a Digital Elevation Model of 30 m resolution from NASA’s Earth Observing System Data Information System were among the spatial resources. Bias-corrected CMIP6 Global Climate Model (GCM) projections for 2018–2100 were sourced from the Indian Institute of Technology Gandhinagar. Model configuration

The Soil and Water Assessment Tool (SWAT) is a semi-distributed, process-based hydrological model developed to simulate the impacts of land management and climate variability on water, sediment, and agricultural processes at the watershed scale52,53. SWAT divides the watershed into sub-basins and further into Hydrologic Response Units (HRUs) based on unique combinations of soil, land use, and slope. It simulates major hydrological components such as surface runoff, infiltration, evapotranspiration, percolation, plant growth, and groundwater flow using physically based equations. The model is particularly suitable for long-term climate impact assessments because it allows integration of climate projections, crop growth processes, and streamflow responses within a single modelling framework. In this study, SWAT was used to evaluate future changes in rainfall, temperature, streamflow, and paddy yield under multiple CMIP6 climate scenarios. Its ability to integrate climate projections with hydrological processes and crop growth within a single modelling framework makes it particularly suitable for assessing the combined impacts of climate change on streamflow and agricultural productivity at the watershed scale.

SWAT was employed to model hydrological processes and crop yields in the Bharathapuzha watershed. The model configuration was based on a previously calibrated and validated setup developed for the basin, which included watershed delineation, subbasin generation, and Hydrological Response Unit (HRU) definition. A DEM (30 m resolution), vegetation maps, and soil data was used for delineation purpose. The basin outlet was set at the Kuttipuram gauging station. The Kuttipuram gauging station was selected as the watershed outlet because it is the primary long-term CWC discharge monitoring site in the basin, receiving flow from all major tributaries. This location provides reliable observed streamflow data for model calibration and validation, thereby enhancing hydrological accuracy in SWAT simulations. Daily streamflow records from 1998 to 2017 were employed for model training and testing. To ensure adequate representation of wet, dry, and average years, the training duration was considered from 1998 to 2008, while the testing duration covered 2009 to 2017. The parameters of the model were optimized by means of the Sequential Uncertainty Fitting (SUFI-2) algorithm within the SWAT-CUP framework. SUFI-2 performs parameter optimization by generating multiple parameter sets through Latin Hypercube sampling and evaluating model performance for each iteration. The algorithm progressively narrows parameter ranges based on their sensitivity and contribution to model accuracy. Uncertainty is assessed through the p-factor, representing the percentage of observed data bracketed by the 95% prediction uncertainty (95PPU), and the r-factor, which indicates the average width of the prediction band. Together, these metrics ensure that both parameter uncertainty and model error are accounted for during calibration.

Model performance was assessed using two standard hydrological accuracy metrics: Nash–Sutcliffe Efficiency (NSE) and the coefficient of determination (R²). These indicators were applied at both daily and monthly time steps during calibration and validation to evaluate the correspondence between simulated and observed streamflow.

Following validation, a baseline historical simulation was run for the period 1998–2017 using observed climate and land use data to establish a reference representation of current hydrological and crop-yield conditions. This baseline serves as a framework against which future climate projections (2035, 2060, 2080, and 2100) are compared, enabling quantification of deviations in streamflow and paddy yield and facilitating assessment of climate-driven changes over time. This baseline provides a calibrated and observation-driven representation of current watershed behavior, allowing future climate impacts to be quantified as deviations from existing hydrological and crop-yield conditions. It captures natural variability in rainfall, streamflow, evapotranspiration, and soil moisture over two decades, thereby offering a robust benchmark for assessing changes under future climate scenarios. Where required, revalidation was performed to confirm model stability under the updated input datasets before proceeding to future scenario simulations. During revalidation, model performance was reassessed using NSE and R² at both daily and monthly time steps to ensure consistency with the original calibration. Simulated streamflows generated with updated climate and land-use inputs were compared against observed discharge to verify model stability. Additionally, calibrated parameter ranges were checked for robustness, ensuring that no major parameter adjustments were required when new datasets were introduced.

Modeling climate-induced changes

Climate projections (bias-corrected) from the CMIP6 were employed to forecaast the consequences of future climate variations on discharge and paddy yield in the Bharathapuzha watershed. Three Global Climate Models (GCMs), EC-Earth3-Veg, INM-CM5-0, and MPI-ESM1-2-HR, were selected for the region. Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) are standardized scenarios that describe alternative future trajectories of socioeconomic development, greenhouse gas emissions, and climate change. In this study, two SSPs were considered. SSP2-4.5 represents a ‘middle-of-the-road’ pathway characterized by moderate population growth, mixed energy use, and stabilization of emissions, leading to intermediate climate impacts. In contrast, SSP5-8.5 represents a fossil fuel-intensive pathway with high greenhouse gas emissions and strong radiative forcing, resulting in greater warming, increased rainfall variability, and more frequent extreme events.

Bias correction is necessary when using CMIP6 climate model outputs because raw model simulations often contain systematic errors in representing observed climate variables such as precipitation and temperature. These biases can significantly affect hydrological and crop model simulations if not corrected.

Precipitation, maximum and minimum temperature data, already bias-corrected and statistically downscaled to a 0.25° × 0.25° spatial resolution, were obtained from the Indian Institute of Technology Gandhinagar. These datasets follow the empirical quantile-mapping bias-correction framework developed by IIT Gandhinagar, as documented in54, and provide daily bias-corrected CMIP6 precipitation and temperature projections for South Asia. The CMIP6 climate data provided by IIT Gandhinagar are bias-corrected using a quantile-mapping approach and statistically downscaled to a 0.25° × 0.25° spatial resolution. Quantile mapping adjusts the statistical distribution of model outputs to match observed climatology by aligning their cumulative distribution functions. This approach corrects not only the mean but also the variability and extreme values of climate variables such as precipitation and temperature, while preserving long-term climate change signals. As a result, it improves the reliability of climate inputs used for hydrological and crop simulations at the watershed scale. The selected 0.25° × 0.25° spatial resolution is widely used in watershed-scale studies as it provides an optimal balance between spatial detail and computational efficiency. This resolution is sufficiently fine to capture spatial variability in rainfall and temperature patterns across the basin, which are critical for simulating hydrological processes such as runoff, evapotranspiration, and soil moisture dynamics in SWAT, while remaining computationally manageable for long-term simulations.

The three CMIP6 GCMs were selected based on multiple criteria, including their demonstrated skill in representing key characteristics of the Indian Summer Monsoon system such as the timing of monsoon onset and withdrawal, seasonal rainfall magnitude, spatial distribution of precipitation, and the frequency and intensity of extreme rainfall events, the availability of daily bias-corrected precipitation and temperature datasets, and their contrasting climate sensitivities to capture a range of possible future climate conditions. The use of multiple GCMs allows the study to capture inter-model variability and associated uncertainty in climate projections, thereby providing a range of possible future rainfall and temperature responses rather than relying on a single model outcome. Recent evaluation studies over South Asia have also identified EC-Earth3-Veg, INM-CM5-0, and MPI-ESM1-2-HR as among the more reliable CMIP6 models for monsoon-driven basins, making them suitable for hydrological and agricultural impact assessment in the Bharathapuzha watershed.

The selected SSP pathways capture contrasting global development and emission futures. SSP2-4.5 represents a ‘middle-of-the-road’ trajectory characterized by moderate population growth, balanced energy use, and gradual implementation of climate policies, leading to stabilization of greenhouse gas emissions after mid-century. In contrast, SSP5-8.5 assumes rapid economic growth driven by fossil fuel–intensive development, high energy demand, and limited climate mitigation efforts, resulting in continuously increasing greenhouse gas emissions and high radiative forcing by the end of the century. These two pathways were selected because they represent widely used moderate and high-emission scenarios in climate impact research, effectively bracketing a realistic range of future climate conditions. Their adoption enables assessment of both intermediate and worst-case impacts, while also ensuring consistency and comparability with previous studies and IPCC assessments.

To represent different future horizons, simulations were conducted for four time slices: 2035 (near-term), 2060 (mid-century), 2080 (late-century), and 2100 (end of century). These time slices were selected in line with IPCC assessment timelines to capture short-, medium-, and long-term climate change impacts. The 2035 horizon reflects near-term climate conditions that are directly relevant for immediate agricultural adaptation planning and decision-making, while 2060 and 2080 represent progressive mid- and late-century transitions, and 2100 captures long-term end-century impacts under sustained emission scenarios. This temporal stratification enables systematic assessment of how hydrological processes and paddy yield evolve over time and facilitates comparison of the rate and magnitude of climate-induced changes under different SSP pathways.

The monthly calibrated SWAT model, configured with crop management practices for paddy, was used for scenario simulations. For each SSP, GCM, and time slice, the downscaled climate data replaced historical weather inputs in the model. All non-climatic inputs such as land use, soil properties, irrigation assumptions, crop parameters, and management schedules were kept constant to isolate the effects of climate variability on hydrological processes and crop yield. This approach ensures that changes in streamflow and paddy yield can be attributed solely to variations in climate variables, avoiding confounding influences from changes in land management or agricultural practices.

This approach provided a consistent framework for quantifying future changes in streamflow and crop yield under multiple climate scenarios, enabling direct comparison with the historical baseline period. Using the historical baseline (1998–2017) as a reference enhances the reliability of projected impacts by anchoring future simulations to conditions that the model has already reproduced accurately, thereby enabling deviations in streamflow and paddy yield to be attributed specifically to climate-driven changes in rainfall and temperature. This comparison allows deviations in streamflow and yield to be interpreted relative to observed hydrological behavior and historical crop performance. It also helps distinguish true climate-driven changes from model uncertainties, supports clearer detection of long-term trends across future horizons, and ensures that projected impacts are evaluated within the context of past variability in the Bharathapuzha watershed.

Hydrological and crop yield simulation

The adjusted and verified SWAT model was adopted to generate hydrological dynamics as well as paddy production in the study area under historical and future climate scenarios. Daily climate variables such as precipitation and temperature were used to drive key hydrological processes, including surface runoff, evapotranspiration, infiltration, and soil moisture dynamics within the SWAT model.

For paddy cultivation, crop parameters (e.g., base temperature, optimum temperature, maximum leaf area index, and harvest index) were adopted directly from the default SWAT crop database for rice. These default parameters were not altered but were verified against observed yield data for Palakkad district to ensure that the model outputs reasonably represented local conditions. Crop management practices, including sowing dates, fertilizer application, and irrigation schedules, were incorporated based on agricultural statistics and field reports from the Kerala Department of Agriculture, as these factors influence crop growth stages, nutrient availability, and soil moisture conditions, thereby affecting simulated paddy yield in SWAT. Irrigation water supply was assumed to be primarily from surface canals, supplemented by rainfall, reflecting the existing agricultural system in Palakkad and Alathur blocks, as accurate representation of canal irrigation is essential for capturing water availability, soil moisture dynamics, and crop water stress, which directly influence simulated paddy yield.

Although the land-use map classified cultivated areas under a generalized “Agricultural land – row crops” category, crop simulation in SWAT is performed at the Hydrological Response Unit (HRU) level. During HRU definition, agricultural HRUs corresponding to Palakkad and Alathur blocks were explicitly assigned rice (paddy) as the crop type using the SWAT crop database. Therefore, the simulated yield outputs represent paddy production rather than aggregated agricultural land productivity.

Simulations were carried out for the baseline period (1998–2017) and for four future time slices (2035, 2060, 2080, and 2100) under the two socio economic pathways using three selected CMIP6 GCMs (EC-Earth3-Veg, INM-CM5-0, and MPI-ESM1-2-HR). Model outputs included monthly and seasonal streamflow at the Kuttipuram outlet and paddy yield at the block level, as analysis at these temporal scales helps capture intra-annual variability, including peak monsoon flows and dry-season water availability, which are critical for understanding hydrological processes and irrigation support.

Performance metrics and uncertainty examination

Calibration and validation are essential steps in SWAT modelling to ensure that the model accurately represents observed hydrological processes. Calibration involves adjusting model parameters to match observed streamflow, while validation evaluates the model’s predictive capability using an independent dataset. Model performance was assessed using standard statistical metrics, including the Nash–Sutcliffe Efficiency (NSE) and the Ratio of the Root Mean Square Error to the Standard Deviation of observed data (RSR). These indices were applied to both streamflow and paddy yield simulations. NSE ranges from −∞ to 1, where values approaching 1 signify superior model performance. Values greater than 0.50 are widely considered satisfactory for hydrological applications, as they indicate that the model provides reasonably accurate predictions compared to observed data.RSR55 standardizes RMSE by means of the standard deviation of observed data. For crop yield, the SWAT default parameters for rice were applied without site-specific calibration. Accordingly, the same performance indicators were applied to validate concordance among the real and model generated yields for Palakkad and Alathur blocks. Although absolute yield estimates may be affected by the use of default crop parameters, this approach allows reliable assessment of relative changes in yield.

Results and discussion

Model calibration and validation

According to Nair et al., the SWAT model for Bharathapuzha watershed was calibrated and verified for streamflow at the Kuttipuram gauging station39. While the hydrological model configuration follows Nair et al. (2025), the present study additionally incorporates crop-specific HRU management to simulate paddy yield under climate change scenarios. Model predictions were found satisfactory, with NSE values of 0.67 (calibration) and 0.62 (validation) at the daily scale, and 0.74 (calibration) and 0.70 (validation) at the monthly scale. Corresponding RSR values ranged from 0.51 to 0.62, verifying the accuracy of the model for hydrological forecasts. According to SWAT performance guidelines55, RSR values between 0.5 and 0.7 indicate good model performance, and the obtained range of 0.51–0.62 therefore reflects reliable hydrological prediction capability of the model. Detailed calibration and validation plots, including daily and monthly hydrographs and observed–simulated plots, are provided in Nair et al. (2025) and are not repeated here to avoid duplication. The present study employs the same validated model configuration.

The calibration and validation hydrographs (Figs. 3 and 4) show that the SWAT model successfully reproduces the seasonal dynamics of streamflow at the Kuttipuram outlet. Peak monsoon flows and low-flow conditions are captured reasonably well, with slight underestimation during extremely high-flow years (e.g., 2005, 2013) and minor overestimation in some recession periods. The model achieved NSE values of 0.67 (calibration) and 0.62 (validation) at the daily scale, and 0.74 (calibration) and 0.70 (validation) at the monthly scale, confirming satisfactory predictive skill. The close alignment between observed and simulated hydrographs reinforces the model’s suitability for conducting future climate-impact simulations in the Bharathapuzha basin.

For paddy yield, validation was performed by comparing SWAT-simulated yields with observed district-level agricultural statistics for both Palakkad and Alathur blocks. The model achieved NSE = 0.80 (RSR = 0.45) in Palakkad and NSE = 0.68 (RSR = 0.57) in Alathur indicating satisfactory skill in reproducing interannual variations in paddy production. Validation results showed slightly better agreement in Palakkad, where irrigation support is more stable, while Alathur exhibited greater variability due to higher sensitivity to rainfall fluctuations. These findings confirm the model’s capability to simulate spatial differences in yield response across the basin. Since detailed calibration and validation results are published elsewhere, the present paper focuses on future projections of climate, streamflow, and paddy yield using the validated model framework. Providing full calibration and validation details again would be redundant and beyond the scope of this paper, whose primary contribution is the assessment of long-term climate-change impacts using a previously validated and robust model setup.

A full crop-yield calibration could not be undertaken because cultivar-specific physiological parameters and field-level yield observations were not available for the Bharathapuzha basin. Therefore, similar to practices recommended for ungauged agricultural watersheds56,57, district-level agricultural statistics were used for model validation rather than calibration. This approach allows reliable assessment of interannual variability and long-term yield trends, even though absolute magnitudes may retain some uncertainty. The validated model performance (NSE = 0.80 for Palakkad; NSE = 0.68 for Alathur) supports the robustness of subsequent climate-impact assessments.

Impact on hydrology

Rainfall projections (Figs. 5, 6 and 7) by the three CMIP6 GCMs under SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 indicate increasing variability, with more frequent extremes under SSP5-8.5. This increased variability under SSP5-8.5 is driven by stronger radiative forcing, which enhances atmospheric moisture capacity and intensifies the hydrological cycle, resulting in more frequent and intense precipitation extremes compared to SSP2-4.5. EC-Earth3-Veg projects the highest late-century rainfall peaks (Fig. 5), indicating strong monsoon intensification and a greater likelihood of extreme runoff and flood events in the Bharathapuzha basin, INM-CM5-0 shows moderate but fluctuating increases (Fig. 6), while MPI-ESM1-2-HR suggests relatively stable mid-century patterns followed by sharp late-century rises (Fig. 7). A comparative assessment of the three GCMs indicates distinct rainfall behaviours across the basin. EC-Earth3-Veg consistently projects stronger monsoon intensification and higher late-century peaks, whereas INM-CM5-0 shows the smallest deviation from historical rainfall, with moderate increases under both SSP pathways. MPI-ESM1-2-HR displays the greatest variability, including amplified extremes and sharper fluctuations under SSP5-8.5. These divergent patterns reflect the range of uncertainty within CMIP6 projections and justify the use of multiple models to capture inter-model variability and associated uncertainty, thereby providing a more robust assessment of future rainfall patterns and their hydrological impacts.

Temperature simulations (Figs. 8, 9 and 10) display steady increases in both minimum and maximum values, with SSP5-8.5 consistently higher than SSP2-4.5. EC-Earth3-Veg (Fig. 8) shows gradual warming with stronger increases under high emissions. INM-CM5-0 (Fig. 9) suggests moderate increases, while MPI-ESM1-2-HR (Fig. 10) projects the strongest late-century warming, which is expected to significantly increase evapotranspiration demand, accelerate soil moisture depletion, and thereby raise irrigation requirements for sustaining paddy cultivation. Temperature projections show distinct differences across the three GCMs. EC-Earth3-Veg indicates gradual but steady warming, INM-CM5-0 shows moderate increases with less variability, while MPI-ESM1-2-HR projects the highest late-century temperature rise under both SSP pathways. These varying warming intensities directly influence evapotranspiration rates and soil-water dynamics. Higher temperatures—particularly under MPI-ESM1-2-HR—are expected to substantially increase evapotranspiration demand, accelerate soil-moisture depletion, and intensify dry-season water stress. The comparatively moderate warming in INM-CM5-0 implies lower evapotranspiration pressures, whereas EC-Earth3-Veg suggests intermediate impacts. These distinctions highlight how model-specific temperature sensitivities contribute to uncertainty in hydrological predictions, as variations in warming intensity directly affect evapotranspiration rates, soil moisture dynamics, groundwater recharge, and streamflow behavior under future climate scenarios.

Streamflow predictions at the Kuttipuram outlet (Figs. 11, 12 and 13) reflect these climatic shifts. EC-Earth3-Veg (Fig. 11) projects moderate increases under SSP2-4.5 but sharp flood peaks under SSP5-8.5, driven by stronger monsoon intensification and higher rainfall extremes associated with increased radiative forcing under the high-emission scenario. INM-CM5-0 (Fig. 12) shows relatively stable flows until mid-century before sharp rises in SSP5-8.5, while MPI-ESM1-2-HR (Fig. 13) indicates modest mid-century increases with marked late-century amplification. The three GCMs show contrasting streamflow responses at the Kuttipuram outlet under the SSP pathways. EC-Earth3-Veg projects moderate increases under SSP2-4.5 but pronounced flood peaks under SSP5-8.5, reflecting its strong monsoon intensification signal. INM-CM5-0 indicates relatively stable flows under SSP2-4.5 with only moderate increases, but displays sharp late-century rises under SSP5-8.5. In contrast, MPI-ESM1-2-HR shows more uniform increases across time slices, with modest changes in the mid-century period and stronger amplification toward 2100. Collectively, these models reveal a wide spread in future streamflow behavior, highlighting substantial uncertainty in peak-flow magnitudes and dry-season deficits under high-emission scenarios. Unlike previous studies in the basin that examined rainfall or streamflow trends independently, the present work integrates multi-GCM climate projections with hydrological and agricultural dynamics, providing a more comprehensive assessment of how climate-induced hydrological extremes translate into crop-production risks. These projected streamflow changes are driven by the combined effects of increased rainfall intensity, which enhances surface runoff and peak discharge, and rising temperatures, which elevate evapotranspiration and reduce baseflow contributions during the dry season.

Projected increases in rainfall variability and temperature contribute simultaneously to more intense monsoon floods and dry-season water shortages. High-intensity monsoon rainfall events exceed soil infiltration capacity, thereby reducing infiltration, limiting groundwater recharge, and generating rapid surface runoff, and sharply increase peak discharge, thereby amplifying flood risks in the basin. Conversely, rising temperatures elevate evapotranspiration demand, increasing water loss from soil and plant surfaces, accelerating soil-moisture depletion, potentially contributing to soil quality degradation under changing land-use and climate conditions58 and reducing groundwater recharge, and thereby intensifying dry-season water stress. These processes lead to lower baseflows and heightened summer water scarcity. The combined impact of erratic rainfall distribution and continuous warming explains the basin’s increasing susceptibility to both monsoon-season flooding and dry-season hydrological deficits under future climate scenarios.

The three CMIP6 models used in this study exhibit noticeable differences in their climate projections, which introduce uncertainty into future hydrological and agricultural assessments. EC-Earth3-Veg shows higher late-century rainfall peaks and stronger warming, resulting in more pronounced increases in runoff and larger projected yield reductions. INM-CM5-0 presents more moderate rainfall changes and smoother mid-century patterns, while MPI-ESM1-2-HR indicates relatively stable mid-century conditions followed by sharp increases toward the end of the century. These inter-model differences create a range of possible future outcomes, introducing uncertainty in projected agricultural productivity by influencing evapotranspiration, soil moisture availability, and crop growth conditions, as well as the magnitude and timing of climate impacts. Consequently, agricultural planning must consider flexible adaptation approaches that remain effective across varying climate trajectories.

Overall, the projections point to heightened risks of hydrological extremes—floods during peak monsoon and potential dry-season water stress. Adaptive irrigation planning, soil conservation, and integrated water management will be critical to safeguard crop productivity in the basin.

Existing water-management practices such as canal irrigation, tank storage, and groundwater extraction provide only partial relief from dry-season water stress in the Bharathapuzha basin. Canal flows decline sharply during summer, tank systems are constrained by monsoon-dependent recharge, and falling groundwater levels reduce pumping reliability. Consequently, these strategies offer limited buffering capacity, and dry-season water shortages remain common during key crop growth stages.

The projected intensification of hydrological extremes further exacerbates these agricultural vulnerabilities. Enhanced monsoon rainfall and higher flood peaks can disrupt early crop growth stages, including nursery establishment and transplantation by causing waterlogging, seedling damage, and delays in field preparation. During the vegetative phase, excess moisture may limit soil aeration and nutrient uptake. In contrast, reduced baseflows and increased evapotranspiration during the dry season intensify soil moisture stress during critical reproductive stages, particularly flowering and grain filling, leading to reduced yield and crop stability across the basin.

Given the projected increase in both flood intensity and dry-season water scarcity, a combination of structural and management-based adaptation measures is essential to reduce climate risks to paddy production in the Bharathapuzha basin. Enhancing canal and tank rehabilitation, expanding decentralized water-storage structures, and promoting watershed-level soil and water conservation measures can improve monsoon water retention and reduce flood peaks. During the dry season, strategies such as regulated groundwater extraction, micro-irrigation, and efficient canal scheduling can help manage rising irrigation demand. At the farm scale, adopting short-duration and flood-tolerant paddy varieties, adjusting sowing and transplanting windows to avoid peak hazard periods, and implementing alternate wetting and drying (AWD) irrigation can substantially reduce vulnerability to climate-induced water stress. Collectively, these measures offer a robust adaptation pathway for managing future hydrological extremes in the watershed, highlighting the importance of adaptive crop management strategies for maintaining yield stability under water-stressed conditions59.

Despite using three GCMs and two SSP pathways, mid-century projections appear relatively similar because SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 do not diverge strongly until after 2060, and all three models show comparable warming signals in the early periods. As a result, rainfall, temperature, and streamflow patterns exhibit only moderate differences before late-century. Impact on paddy yield

Historical yield simulations using the calibrated SWAT model demonstrated good agreement with observed data for both Alathur and Palakkad blocks (Figs. 14a-c and 15a-c). The value of performance indicators were within acceptable ranges (NSE > 0.50; RSR < 0.70), confirming its reliability for yield projections. Although slight overestimations were noted in some years, the model successfully reproduced the overall declining trend in observed paddy yields from 2008 to 2017, consistent with earlier findings that highlight the sensitivity of rice production to climatic variability in Kerala. Unlike earlier studies that evaluated crop-yield sensitivity without explicitly linking it to basin-scale hydrological dynamics, the present work offers an integrated assessment that connects multi-GCM climate projections, streamflow variability, and block-level paddy production responses, thereby providing a more process-based understanding of climate impacts. Overall, climate projections indicate that paddy yields in both Alathur and Palakkad may decline by approximately 60–74% by the end of the century under high-emission scenarios, highlighting the severe long-term vulnerability of the crop to future warming and rainfall variability. Model-wise patterns are evident in EC-Earth3-Veg (Figs. 14a and 15a), INM-CM5-0 (Figs. 14b and 15b), and MPI-ESM1-2-HR (Figs. 14c and 15c).

Variations in streamflow strongly influence paddy production because irrigation availability and soil-moisture levels depend on seasonal flow conditions. Lower dry-season flows reduce water supply during sensitive crop stages, whereas intensified monsoon flows increase waterlogging and delay planting. These hydrological disruptions contribute to reduced yield performance across both blocks.

Future projections up to 2100 under the two shared socio economic pathways consistently showed declines in paddy yield across both study blocks (Tables 2 and 3). In the Alathur block (Fig. 14a-c), projected yield reductions range from 26.17% to 41.85% by 2035, intensifying to as much as 73.86% by 2100 under SSP5-8.5 (MPI-ESM1-2 h; Fig. 14c). EC-Earth3-Veg and INM-CM5-0 models also indicate progressively worsening impacts, particularly beyond mid-century.

A projected 68–74% reduction in paddy yields by 2100 would have major socioeconomic implications for the region. Such losses would significantly reduce farm income, increase vulnerability among small and marginal farmers, and may accelerate livelihood transitions away from agriculture. This scale of production decline could also lead to increased food-price volatility and reduced affordability for low-income households, thereby intensifying regional food-security risks. At the state level, Kerala’s dependence on imported rice would deepen further, heightening exposure to external price fluctuations and supply uncertainties. These anticipated impacts underscore the urgency of adopting climate-resilient agricultural practices and strengthening long-term adaptation planning.

Basin-scale water management and climate-resilient agricultural practices are essential to reduce projected declines in paddy production under future climate scenarios, as increasing rainfall variability, dry-season water stress, and temperature-driven evapotranspiration will challenge the stability of paddy cultivation across the basin. Several adaptive strategies may help counter future paddy yield reductions in the Bharathapuzha basin. The use of climate-resilient and short-duration paddy varieties can reduce exposure to late-season moisture stress. Optimizing sowing and transplanting schedules to avoid peak monsoon floods and late-summer dry spells can also stabilize productivity. Improved water-management approaches, such as alternate wetting and drying, efficient canal water distribution, and strengthened tank storage, further enhance resilience under future climate scenarios. In addition, basin-scale water management strategies, including optimized reservoir operations and flood control measures, are essential to reduce hydrological risks. Effective implementation of these strategies requires coordinated efforts among key stakeholders, including farmers, irrigation authorities, agricultural extension agencies, policymakers, and research institutions, to enhance climate resilience in the Bharathapuzha basin.

However, under the severe end-century declines projected under SSP5-8.5 (≥ 70%), incremental measures such as improved varieties and irrigation efficiency may not be adequate, and more transformative strategies including crop diversification and shifts to less water-demanding crops may be required to sustain farmer livelihoods.

For the Palakkad block (Fig. 15a-c), yield reductions are relatively less severe in the near term, with declines of 23–33% by 2035, but reach up to 68.17% by 2100 under SSP5-8.5, with the highest decline projected by the MPI-ESM1-2-HR model (Fig. 15c), primarily due to stronger late-century warming and increased rainfall variability leading to higher evapotranspiration and moisture stress. Compared to Alathur, Palakkad shows more gradual mid-century decreases; however, under SSP2-4.5, all three GCMs indicate a progressive increase in yield reduction from 2035 to 2100, reflecting the cumulative effects of warming and rainfall variability over time, with long-term losses eventually converging across models. Figures 14a–c and 15a–c enable direct comparison of GCM-specific responses across the two blocks.

Differences in climate sensitivity between Palakkad and Alathur blocks arise from variations in agro-climatic conditions, irrigation reliability, and soil properties. Alathur exhibits higher near-term sensitivity due to greater rainfall variability, dependence on canal-fed irrigation with less consistent supply, and soils with lower moisture-retention capacity. In contrast, Palakkad benefits from relatively stable irrigation support and more fertile soils, which help buffer early impacts but do not prevent significant long-term yield declines under increasing temperature and hydrological stress.

The similarity in mid-century yield projections across scenarios arises because differences between SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 remain modest until late-century. Hence, temperature and rainfall-driven stress levels are comparable in the early periods, leading to similar yield reductions. More pronounced divergence appears only toward 2100, as SSP5-8.5 produces substantially stronger warming, higher evapotranspiration demand, and greater rainfall variability and hydrological extremes compared to SSP2-4.5, with the most significant increase in yield decline occurring between mid-century and late-century periods (2060–2100), indicating an accelerating, non-linear intensification of climate impacts on paddy production.

A comparison between the two blocks highlights that Alathur exhibits greater near-term sensitivity, especially under the EC-Earth3-Veg projections, whereas Palakkad faces more pronounced late-century declines, particularly under high-emission scenarios.
