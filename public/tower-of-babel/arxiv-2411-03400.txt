On Dedekind’s problem, a sparse version of Sperner’s theorem, and antichains of a given size in the Boolean lattice
Matthew Jenssen, Alexandru Malekshahian, Jinyoung Park

Abstract
Dedekind’s problem, dating back to 1897, asks for the total number ψ ⁡ ( n ) \psi(n) of antichains contained in the Boolean lattice B n B_{n} on n n elements. We study Dedekind’s problem using a recently developed method based on the cluster expansion from statistical physics and as a result, obtain several new results on the number and typical structure of antichains in B n B_{n} . We obtain detailed estimates for both ψ ⁡ ( n ) \psi(n) and the number of antichains of size β ​ ( n ⌊ n / 2 ⌋ ) \beta\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor} for any fixed β > 0 \beta>0 . We also establish a sparse version of Sperner’s theorem: we determine the sharp threshold and scaling window for the property that almost every antichain of size m m is contained in a middle layer of B n B_{n} .

Introduction

An
antichain
of a poset
P=(X,\leq)
is a subset of
X
in which any two elements are incomparable in the partial order
\leq
. Dedekind’s problem
[
18
]
, dating back to 1897, asks for the total number
\psi(n)
of antichains contained in the
n
-dimensional Boolean lattice
B_{n}=(2^{[n]},\subseteq)
where
[n]:=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. Equivalently,
\psi(n)
is the number of elements of the free distributive lattice on
n
generators, or the number of monotone Boolean functions
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
. The exact value of
\psi(n)
is known only for
n\leq 9
: the case
n=9
recently came within reach of modern computing power
[
30
,
72
]

more than 30 years after the determination of
\psi(8)

[
73
]
.

Over the past several decades, a large amount of research has gone into establishing bounds for
\psi(n)
for large
n
. Dedekind’s problem is closely related to the classical extremal problem of finding the
largest
antichain in
B_{n}
. Sperner’s theorem from 1928
[
70
]
, a cornerstone of extremal combinatorics, solves this problem: the largest antichain in
B_{n}
has size
\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
. Moreover, the only antichains to achieve this bound are the middle layer(s) of
B_{n}
: the families consisting of all sets of size
\lfloor n/2\rfloor
and of all sets of size
\lceil n/2\rceil
, respectively. Taking all subsets of such an antichain gives the simple lower bound
\log_{2}\psi(n)\geq\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
.

Kleitman
[
41
]
– following bounds of Gilbert
[
26
]
, Korobkov
[
45
]
and Hansel
[
28
]
– was the first to estimate the logarithm of
\psi(n)
up to a
(1+o(1))
factor, showing that
\log_{2}\psi(n)=\left(1+O\left(\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right)\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}.
The error term was subsequently improved to
O\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)
by Kleitman and Markowsky
[
42
]
. A few years later Korshunov
[
46
]
determined
\psi(n)
itself up to a
(1+o(1))
factor, thereby ‘asymptotically solving’ Dedekind’s problem. A shorter proof was later given by Sapozhenko
[
66
]
. Henceforth, we let
N=\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
.

Theorem 1.1

(Korshunov
[
46
]
, Sapozhenko
[
66
]
)
.

As
n\rightarrow\infty

\psi(n)=(1+o(1))2^{N}\exp\left[\binom{n}{n/2+1}\left(2^{-n/2}+\frac{n^{2}-2n-16}{32}\cdot 2^{-n}\right)\right],

while for
n
odd

\displaystyle\psi(n)=(2+o(1))2^{N}\times

\exp\left[\displaystyle\binom{n}{(n+1)/2}\left(2^{-(n+1)/2}+\frac{3n^{2}-19}{32}\cdot 2^{-(n+1)}\right)+\binom{n}{(n+3)/2}\left(\frac{1}{2}\cdot 2^{-(n+1)/2}+\frac{n+5}{8}\cdot 2^{-(n+1)}\right)\right].

Dedekind’s problem remains a testbed for modern tools and techniques in combinatorics. Kahn
[
39

showed that the Kleitman-Markowsky bound
[

In this paper we approach Dedekind’s problem using a recently developed method based on tools from statistical physics (see Section

Theorem 1.2

Let

Proving sparse analogues of classical extremal theorems has been a recent and fruitful trend in combinatorics. This line of research has led to several breakthroughs and the development of powerful and general combinatorial techniques such as the ‘transference principle’ of Conlon and Gowers

Another way in which an extremal problem can be ‘sparsified’ is to solve the extremal problem relative to some sparse random set. The literature on such problems is vast and so we give an example most relevant to our setting and refer the reader to the excellent survey

Theorem 1.3

There exist sequences of rational functions

1.1
Methods and related work

Here we give an overview of the proofs of the results from the previous section. The intuition for our approach comes from statistical physics. We build on tools from
[
66
,
32
,
33
,
35
,
36
,
37
]
, and develop some new tools along the way.

Let
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{n}
denote the set of all antichains in
B_{n}
.
Our starting point is to write a partition function for antichains:

Z(\lambda)=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\lambda^{|A|}\,

along with the corresponding (Gibbs) measure
\mu_{\lambda}
on
\mathcal{A}
given by

\displaystyle\mu_{\lambda}(A)=\frac{\lambda^{|A|}}{Z(\lambda)}\,.

We note in particular, that
Z(1)
is equal to
\psi(n)
, the total number of antichains in
B_{n}
and
\mu_{1}
is the uniform distribution on antichains. For us, the combinatorial relevance of studying
Z(\lambda)
and
\mu_{\lambda}
at general
\lambda>0
comes from the following identity: for
m\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mu_{\lambda}(|A|=m)=\frac{\lambda^{m}\psi(n,m)}{Z(\lambda)}\,.

(1.1)

If
\lambda
is chosen carefully so that the expected size of an antichain sampled from
\mu_{\lambda}
is close to
m
, then we can hope to estimate
\mu_{\lambda}(|A|=m)
via a local limit theorem. By the identity (
1.1
), estimating
\psi(n,m)
then reduces to the problem of estimating the partition function
Z(\lambda)
.

Classical statistical physics is largely concerned with understanding partition functions like
Z(\lambda)
and their associated Gibbs measures. From this perspective, a central motivating question is the following: how does the typical structure of a sample from
\mu_{\lambda}
change with
\lambda
? As
\lambda
increases, the measure
\mu_{\lambda}
becomes more biased toward larger antichains, and so we might expect samples to increasingly ‘correlate’ with a maximum antichain (a middle layer). Many powerful methods have been developed to rigorously study such ‘emergence of structure’ or ‘phase transition’ phenomena. The tool most relevant to us is the cluster expansion
[
64
,
60
,
13
,
49
]
(see Section
2
). Recently, the cluster expansion and related tools have been applied outside the context of classical statistical physics, to algorithmic and combinatorial problems of enumeration and sampling
[
10
,
62
,
59
,
11
,
29
,
32
,
33
,
35
,
36
,
37
,
52
,
3
,
38
]
. Particularly relevant to our current context are the papers of the first author and Perkins
[
35
]
, and the first author, Perkins and Potukuchi
[
36
]
: here the cluster expansion is applied to study in detail the number and typical structure of independent sets in the
d
-dimensional hypercube
Q_{d}
.

In our context, we use the cluster expansion to understand the measure
\mu_{\lambda}
as a perturbation of the ‘ground state’ measure which selects a middle layer uniformly at random and then takes a
\lambda/(1+\lambda)
-random subset of that layer. This allows for a very precise understanding of the measure
\mu_{\lambda}
and its associated partition function
Z(\lambda)
. In particular, this heuristic shows that for a given value of
\lambda
, the expected size of an antichain chosen from
\mu_{\lambda}
should be close to
\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}N
. Returning to (
1.1
), we see that in order to estimate
\psi(n,m)
, where
m=\beta N
, we should expect the suitable choice of
\lambda
to be close to
\frac{\beta}{1-\beta}
.

It is natural to rephrase the problem of counting antichains in terms of counting independent sets in a graph. Abusing notation slightly, we let
B_{n}
denote the graph whose vertex set is
2^{[n]}
, where
u
is adjacent to
v
if and only if
u\subset v
or
v\subset u
. An antichain in
B_{n}
is then simply an independent set in this graph.

The
container method
has proven to be an essential tool in the study of independent sets in graphs and hypergraphs. In the graph setting, its roots can be traced back to work of Kleitman and Winston
[
43
]
and Sapozhenko
[
65
]
. These tools were generalized to the context of hypergraphs and developed into a powerful method in the seminal works of Balogh, Morris and Samotij
[
6
]
and Saxton and Thomason
[
67
]
. The container method has also proven to be an essential tool in applications of the cluster expansion to problems in combinatorics
[
32
,
33
,
35
,
36
,
37
,
52
,
3
]
. Very broadly speaking, the container method allows one to efficiently bound the probability of seeing large deviations from a ground state. This in turn allows one to prove the convergence of cluster expansions of partition functions which encode these perturbations.

In this paper we build on Sapozhenko’s approach to Dedekind’s problem
[
66
]
, another early application of the (graph) container method. Following a recent development of the authors
[
34
]
, we prove a new container result (Theorem

1.3
A note on asymptotic notation

All asymptotic notation is to be understood with respect to the limit
n\to\infty
. All implicit constants in the asymptotic notation
O,\Omega
etc. will be absolute constants unless specified otherwise. For two functions
f,g:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
we write
f(n)\sim g(n)
to denote that
\lim_{n\to\infty}f(n)/g(n)=1
, and
f(n)\ll g(n)
to denote that
\lim_{n\to\infty}f(n)/g(n)=0
. We write
f(n)=\tilde{O}(g(n))
if there exists
C>0
such that
|f(n)|\leq(\log n)^{C}|g(n)|
for
n
sufficiently large. We use
\tilde{\Omega}
analogously.
We write that
f(n)=O(g(n))

for
n
even (odd)
if
f(2k)=O(g(2k))
(
f(2k+1)=O(g(2k+1))
) as
k\to\infty
respectively. We extend this convention to other asymptotic notation analogously.

For two functions
f,g:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
we understand
f(n)\leq g(n)
to mean that the inequality holds for
n
sufficiently large.

We say a sequence of events
A_{n}
holds ‘with high probability’ (abbreviated ‘whp’) if
\mathbb{P}(A_{n})=1-o(1)
.

. Recall that we let
B_{n}
denote the Boolean lattice on the ground set
[n]
. Abusing notation slightly, we also let
B_{n}
denote the graph with vertex set
2^{[n]}
where two sets are joined by an edge if and only if one is strictly contained in the other (the comparability graph of the poset
B_{n}
). Similarly if
U\subseteq B_{n}
, we identify
U
with the subgraph of this comparability graph induced by the elements of
U
. We note that an antichain
A\subseteq B_{n}
is precisely an
independent set
in the comparability graph. For
U\subseteq B_{n}
, the measure
\mu_{U,\lambda}
introduced at (

) can therefore be viewed as the
hard-core measure at activity
\lambda
on independent sets in the graph
U
.

Throughout this paper, we will use lowercase letters to denote vertices of
B_{n}
, uppercase letters to denote sets of vertices, and cursive letters to denote collections of sets.

Given a graph
G
and a vertex
v\in G
we write
d(v)
for the degree of
v
and
N(v)
for the neighbourhood of
v
. Given
U\subseteq V(G)
, we write
G[U]
for the induced subgraph of
G
on vertex set
U
and
N_{U}(v)
for
N(v)\cap U
. We define the
square graph

G^{2}
to have the same vertex set as
G
and setting
u
and
v
to be adjacent in
G^{2}
if and only if the distance between
u
and
v
in
G
is at most 2. We call
U\subseteq V(G)

2
-linked
if
G^{2}[U]
is connected.

The following useful result is standard in the literature - see e.g.
[
25
, Lemma 2.1]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Given a graph
G
of maximum degree
\Delta
, the number of
2
-linked subsets of
G
of size
t
that contain a given vertex

is at most
(e\Delta^{2})^{t-1}
.

Throughout the paper, we write
\log
for
\log_{2}
and
\ln
for
\log_{e}
. We employ a common abuse of notation by often omitting floor and ceiling symbols for notational convenience.

2.1
Polymer models and cluster expansion

We will also require the notion of polymer model and cluster expansion from statistical physics which we introduce now.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a finite set, called the set of
polymers
. Let
w:\mathcal{P}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
a weight function and
\sim
a symmetric and antireflexive relation on
\mathcal{P}
which we refer to as the
compatibility
relation. We also say two polymers
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
are incompatible if
\gamma_{1}\nsim\gamma_{2}
. We refer to the triple
(\mathcal{P},\sim,w)
as a
polymer model
.

Let
\Omega=\Omega(\mathcal{P},\sim)
be the collection of all sets consisting of pairwise compatible polymers, including the empty set of polymers. We define the
partition function

\Xi=\Xi(\mathcal{P},\sim,w)
as

\Xi=\sum\limits_{\Lambda\in\Omega}\prod\limits_{\gamma\in\Lambda}w(\gamma),

(2.1)

where by convention, we take the contribution from the empty set to be
1
.

Let
\Gamma=(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{k})
be an (ordered) tuple of polymers. The
incompatibility graph
of
\Gamma
is the graph
G_{\Gamma}
on vertex set
[k]
where
i
is adjacent to
j
if and only if
\gamma_{i}\nsim\gamma_{j}
.
We call
\Gamma
a
cluster
if
G_{\Gamma}
is connected.
We define the
size
of
\Gamma
to be
\|\Gamma\|\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\gamma_{i}|
. We define the weight of a cluster
\Gamma
to be

\displaystyle w(\Gamma)=\phi(G_{\Gamma})\prod\limits_{{\gamma}\in\Gamma}w(\gamma),

(2.2)

where
\phi
is the Ursell function of a graph
G=(V,E)
, defined as

\displaystyle\phi(G):=\frac{1}{|V|!}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subseteq E:\\
(V,A)\text{ connected}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|A|}.

Let
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{P},\sim)
be the set of all clusters. Then the
cluster expansion
of
\ln\Xi
is the formal power series

\ln\Xi=\sum\limits_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}}w(\Gamma).

(2.3)

This turns out to be the multivariate Taylor series of
\ln\Xi
- see
[
19
,
69
]
. A powerful tool that gives a sufficient condition for the convergence of the cluster expansion, as well as tail bounds, is the following:

Theorem 2.2

(Kotecký and Preiss
[
49
]
)
.

Let
f,

g:\mathcal{P}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
be two functions. If for all
{\gamma}\in\mathcal{P}
we have

\sum\limits_{{\gamma^{\prime}}\nsim{\gamma}}|w({\gamma}^{\prime})|e^{f({\gamma}^{\prime})+g({\gamma}^{\prime})}\leq f({\gamma}),

(2.4)

then the cluster expansion (
2.3
) converges absolutely.

Moreover, setting
g(\Gamma):=\sum_{{\gamma}\in\Gamma}g({\gamma})
and writing
\Gamma\nsim{\gamma}
if there exists some
{\gamma}^{\prime}\in\Gamma
such that
\gamma^{\prime}\nsim{\gamma}
, we then have that, for all polymers
{\gamma}
,

\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}\\
\Gamma\nsim{\gamma}\end{subarray}}|w(\Gamma)|e^{g(\Gamma)}\leq f({\gamma}).

(2.5)

2.2
Antichains of a given size

We will make repeated use of the following general lemma which will be useful for dealing with antichains of a given size.

Lemma 2.3

(
cf.

[
36
, Lemma 15]
)
.

Let
\lambda=\lambda(n)>0
. Let
\mathcal{F}_{1}
and
\mathcal{F}_{2}
be families of independent sets in a graph
G=G_{n}
with
\mathcal{F}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{2}
. For
i\in\{1,2\}
, let
Z_{i}=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{F}_{i}}\lambda^{|A|}
, and let
\mu_{i}
be the probability measure given by

\mu_{i}(A)=\begin{cases}\lambda^{|A|}/Z_{i}&\text{ if $A\in\mathcal{F}_{i}$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

If
Z_{2}=(1+f(n))Z_{1}
where
f(n)=o(1)
, then

\left\|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\right\|_{TV}\leq f(n)\,.

(2.6)

Moreover, with
i_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{i})
the number of independent sets of size
m
contained in
\mathcal{F}_{i}
, if
\lambda
and
m
are such that

i_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{1})\lambda^{m}/Z_{1}\gg f(n),

(2.7)

then

i_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{2})=(1+o(1))i_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{1})\,.

Proof.

Recall that

\left\|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\right\|_{TV}=\sum_{A\subseteq V(G),\,\mu_{1}(A)>\mu_{2}(A)}\mu_{1}(A)-\mu_{2}(A)\,.

We may assume that
\mathcal{F}_{1}\subsetneq\mathcal{F}_{2}
, in which case
\mu_{1}(A)>\mu_{2}(A)
if and only if
A\in\mathcal{F}_{1}
. We therefore have

\left\|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\right\|_{TV}=\sum\limits_{A\in\mathcal{F}_{1}}\frac{\lambda^{|A|}}{Z_{1}}-\frac{\lambda^{|A|}}{Z_{2}}=\frac{f(n)}{1+f(n)}\times\sum\limits_{A\in\mathcal{F}_{1}}\frac{\lambda^{|\mathcal{A}|}}{Z_{1}}\leq f(n)\,.

For the second part, let
\mathcal{I}_{i}
be the collection of independent sets of size
m
contained in
\mathcal{F}_{i}
. Note that
\mu_{1}(\mathcal{I}_{2})=\mu_{1}(\mathcal{I}_{1})
(since
\mu_{1}(A)=0
if
A\notin\mathcal{F}_{1}
), therefore,

\displaystyle|\mu_{1}(\mathcal{I}_{1})-\mu_{2}(\mathcal{I}_{2})|=|\mu_{1}(\mathcal{I}_{2})-\mu_{2}(\mathcal{I}_{2})|\leq\|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\|_{TV}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{TVD.bd}}}{{\leq}}f(n)\,.

(2.8)

On the other hand,

\left|\mu_{1}(\mathcal{I}_{1})-\mu_{2}(\mathcal{I}_{2})\right|=\left|\frac{i_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{1})\lambda^{m}}{Z_{1}}-\frac{i_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{2})\lambda^{m}}{Z_{2}}\right|=\frac{\lambda^{m}}{Z_{1}}|i_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{1})-(1+o(1))i_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{2})|\,.

The result now follows from (
2.7
) and (
2.8
).
∎

2.3
Isoperimetry

Recall that for
i\in[n]
we let
L_{i}
denote the
i^{\text{th}}
layer
\binom{[n]}{i}
of
B_{n}
. For
i\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
and
v\in L_{i}
, we write
N^{+}(v)
for the set of neighbours of
v
in
B_{n}
that belong to the layer
L_{i+1}
.
We extend this notation to
N^{+}(S)
for a set
S\subseteq B_{n}
by setting
N^{+}(S):=\cup_{v\in S}N^{+}(v)
. For
i\in\{1,\dots n\}
and

we similarly define
N^{-}(v)
to be the set of neighbours of

in
B_{n}
that lie in
L_{i-1}
, and we extend this notation to
N^{-}(S)
in the obvious way.

We will make frequent use of the following result that is an easy consequence of the classical Kruskal-Katona theorem
[
40
,
51
]
.

Proposition 2.4
.

If
i\leq\lceil n/2\rceil
and
S\subseteq L_{i-1}
, then:

1.

if
|S|\leq n/10
, then
|N^{+}(S)|\geq n|S|/2-|S|^{2}
;

2.

if
|S|\leq n^{4}
, then
|N^{+}(S)|\geq n|S|/50
;

3.

furthermore, if
i\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor
, then
|N^{+}(S)|\geq(1+1/n)|S|
; and

4.

if

and
|S|\leq|L_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}|/2,
then
|N^{+}(S)|\geq(1+1/n)|S|
.

3
Reducing to central layers

In this section we prove Theorem
1.6
, showing that the dominant contribution to the partition function
Z(\lambda)
comes from antichains contained in the central layers
C_{n}\subseteq B_{n}
.

We prove Theorem
1.6
by iterating an argument which says that the weighted number of antichains in three consecutive layers
L_{[k-2,k]}
,
k\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor
, of
B_{n}
is very close to the weighted number in
L_{[k-1,k]}
, the larger two of the three layers. Given
X,Y\subseteq B_{n}
, let
Y^{X}=\{v\in Y\mid v\not\subset w\ \forall w\in X\}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

There exists
C>0
such that if
\lambda\geq C\log^{2}n/\sqrt{n}
then the following holds.
For any
2\leq k\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor
and
X\subseteq L_{[k+1,n]}
, we have

Z(L^{X}_{[k-2,k]},\lambda)=\left(1+o\left(\frac{1}{n\sqrt{N}}\right)\right)Z(L^{X}_{[k-1,k]},\lambda)\,.

(3.1)

We need the factor of
n
in the denominator of the error term since we will iterate this lemma approximately
n
times. Before turning to the proof of Lemma
3.1
, we show how it implies Theorem
1.6
. This step also illuminates the need for the set
X
in the statement of Lemma
3.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.6
assuming Lemma
3.1
.

Let
1\leq k\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1
. Partitioning antichains according to their intersection with
L_{[k+2,n]}
we see that

\begin{split}Z(L_{[k-1,n]},\lambda)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\subseteq L_{[k+2,n]}\\
X\in\mathcal{A}\end{subarray}}\lambda^{|X|}\cdot Z(L^{X}_{[k-1,k+1]},\lambda)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{3to2}}}{{=}}\left(1+o\left(\frac{1}{n\sqrt{N}}\right)\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\subseteq L_{[k+2,n]}\\
X\in\mathcal{A}\end{subarray}}\lambda^{|X|}\cdot Z(L^{X}_{[k,k+1]},\lambda)\\
&\ =\left(1+o\left(\frac{1}{n\sqrt{N}}\right)\right)Z(L_{[k,n]},\lambda)\,.\end{split}

Iterating the above we conclude that

Z(\lambda)=\left(1+o\left(\frac{1}{n\sqrt{N}}\right)\right)^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1}Z(L_{[\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1,n]},\lambda)\,.

Observe that by symmetry
Z(L_{[\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1,n]},\lambda)=Z(L_{[0,\lceil n/2\rceil+1]},\lambda)
, and again for any
1\leq k\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1
,

\begin{split}Z(L_{[k-1,\lceil n/2\rceil+1]},\lambda)&\ =\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\subseteq L_{[k+2,\lceil n/2\rceil+1]}\\
X\in\mathcal{A}\end{subarray}}\lambda^{|X|}\cdot Z(L^{X}_{[k-1,k+1]},\lambda)\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{3to2}}}{{=}}\left(1+o\left(\frac{1}{n\sqrt{N}}\right)\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\subseteq L_{[k+2,\lceil n/2\rceil+1]}\\
X\in\mathcal{A}\end{subarray}}\lambda^{|X|}\cdot Z(L^{X}_{[k,k+1]},\lambda)\\
&\ =\left(1+o\left(\frac{1}{n\sqrt{N}}\right)\right)Z(L_{[k,\lceil n/2\rceil+1]},\lambda).\end{split}

Therefore,

\displaystyle Z(\lambda)=\left(1+o\left(\frac{1}{n\sqrt{N}}\right)\right)^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1}Z(L_{[0,\lceil n/2\rceil+1]},\lambda)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+o\left(\frac{1}{n\sqrt{N}}\right)\right)^{n}Z(L_{[\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1,\lceil n/2\rceil+1]},\lambda)

\displaystyle=\left(1+o\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\right)\right)Z(C_{n},\lambda)\,.

∎

In order to prove Lemma
3.1
, we shall make use of an ‘embedding trick’ that appears in the work of Hamm and Kahn
[
27
]
and later in the work of Balogh and Krueger
[
5
]
. This trick allows us to reduce the proof of Lemma
3.1
to the special case
k=\lfloor n/2\rfloor
below:

Lemma 3.2
.

There exists
C>0
such that if
\lambda\geq C\log^{2}n/\sqrt{n}
then the following holds.
For any
X\subseteq L_{[\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1,n]}
, we have

Z(L^{X}_{[\lfloor n/2\rfloor-2,\lfloor n/2\rfloor]},\lambda)=\left(1+o\left(\frac{1}{n\sqrt{N}}\right)\right)Z(L^{X}_{[\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1,\lfloor n/2\rfloor]},\lambda)\,.

Proof of Lemma
3.1
assuming Lemma
3.2
.

For
k=\lfloor n/2\rfloor
, this is precisely Lemma
3.2
. Fix
k<\lfloor n/2\rfloor
and
X\subseteq L_{[k+1,n]}
.
Given
w\in B_{n}
, let
w^{+}=w\cup[n+1,2n-2k]
considered as an element of the larger lattice
B_{2n-2k}
.
Given
S\subseteq B_{n}
, let
S^{+}=\{w^{+}:w\in S\}\subseteq B_{2n-2k}
. For
i\in[2n-2k]
, write
\overline{L}_{i}\coloneqq\binom{[2n-2k]}{i}
, the
i^{\text{th}}
layer in
B_{2n-2k}
, and for
S\subseteq[2n-2k]
let
\overline{L}_{S}=\cup_{i\in S}\overline{L}_{i}
. Let
X^{\prime}=X^{+}\cup\bigcup\limits_{j=n+1}^{2n-2k}([2n-2k]\backslash\{j\})
.
Observe that if
v\in\overline{L}_{[n-k-1,n-k]}^{X^{\prime}}
then
[n+1,2n-2k]\subseteq v
and
v=w^{+}
for some
w\in{L}_{[k-1,k]}^{X}
.
Moreover,
A\subseteq L_{[k-1,k]}^{X}
satisfies
|A^{+}|=|A|
and
A
is an antichain if and only if
A^{+}\subseteq\overline{L}_{[n-k-1,n-k]}^{X^{\prime}}
is an antichain. It follows that
Z\left(\overline{L}_{[n-k-1,n-k]}^{X^{\prime}},\lambda\right)=Z\left(L_{[k-1,k]}^{X},\lambda\right)
. Similarly
Z\left(\overline{L}_{[n-k-2,n-k]}^{X^{\prime}},\lambda\right)=Z\left(L_{[k-2,k]}^{X},\lambda\right)
.
Finally, by Lemma
3.2
,

Z\left(\overline{L}_{[n-k-2,n-k]}^{X^{\prime}},\lambda\right)=\left(1+o\left(\frac{1}{n\sqrt{N}}\right)\right)Z\left(\overline{L}_{[n-k-1,n-k]}^{X^{\prime}},\lambda\right)
.
∎

We will in fact prove a result a little more general than
Lemma

3.2
. We state the result first and then explain the motivation for it. We first need a little notation.

First let
r\in\{\lfloor n/2\rfloor,\lceil n/2\rceil\}
and let
\mathcal{A}_{r}
denote the set of all antichains
S\subseteq B_{n}
such that each
2
-linked component
A
, where the 2-linkedness is defined with respect to the comparability graph of
B_{n}
induced on
L_{r-1}\cup L_{r}
, of
S\cap L_{r-1}
satisfies
|N^{+}(A)|\geq(1+1/n)|A|
.

For
U\subseteq B_{n}
, let

Z_{r}(U,\lambda)=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{r},\ A\subseteq U}\lambda^{|A|}\,.

Lemma 3.3
.

There exists
C>0
such that if
\lambda\geq C\log^{2}n/\sqrt{n}
then the following holds. If
r\in\{\lfloor n/2\rfloor,\lceil n/2\rceil\}

and
X\subseteq L_{[r+1,n]}
, we have

Z_{r}(L^{X}_{[r-2,r]},\lambda)=\left(1+o\left(\frac{1}{n\sqrt{N}}\right)\right)Z_{r}(L^{X}_{[r-1,r]},\lambda)\,.

We note that by Proposition
2.4
, if
r=\lfloor n/2\rfloor
then
|N^{+}(A)|\geq(1+1/n)|A|
for all subsets

and so
\mathcal{A}_{r}=\mathcal{A}
and so
Z_{r}\equiv Z
. Thus,
Lemma

3.2
is precisely the
r=\lfloor n/2\rfloor
case of
Lemma

3.3
.

We now attempt to motivate the

case of
Lemma

3.3
. When

is odd and
r=\lceil n/2\rceil
,
Theorem

1.6
shows that
Z(\lambda)
is very close to

where

, the union of four central layers.
Roughly speaking, we will show that the dominant contribution to

comes from antichains that are ‘close’ to a subset of

of the middle layers

,
L_{r-1}
. We may therefore separate these contributions and estimate them individually. If

, then the expression
\lambda^{|X|}Z_{r}(L^{X}_{[r-2,r]},\lambda)
captures the contribution from antichains

that are close to a subset of

and which satisfy

. As we will see, the expansion property in the definition of

allows us to approximate

by

.

3.1
Overview of the proof of Lemma
3.3

For the remainder of this section, fix
r\in\{\lfloor n/2\rfloor,\lceil n/2\rceil\}

and
X\subseteq L_{[r+1,n]}
.

Our strategy will be to interpret the two partition functions in the statement of Lemma
3.3
as the partition functions of two related polymer models which we introduce now.

Recall that for
i\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
and
A\subseteq L_{i}
we let
N^{+}(A)
denote the set of neighbours of
A
in the layer
L_{i+1}
. Let
\mathcal{P}_{X}=\mathcal{P}_{X,r}
denote the set of all
2
-linked (again, in the graph
L_{r-1}\cup L_{r}
)
subsets
A\subseteq L^{X}_{r-1}
such that
|N^{+}(A)|\geq(1+1/n)|A|
. We call
\mathcal{P}_{X}
the set of polymers. Note that if
A\subseteq L^{X}_{r-1}
, then
N^{+}(A)\subseteq L^{X}_{r}
.
Two polymers
A_{1}
,
A_{2}
are
compatible
, written
A_{1}\sim A_{2}
, if and only if the union
A_{1}\cup A_{2}
is
not

2
-linked. For a set
A\subseteq L^{X}_{r-1}
, we write
{\rm{Int}}(A)
for
\{v\in L^{X}_{r-2}:N^{+}(v)\subseteq A\}
. Define the weight of
A
by

\displaystyle w(A)=\lambda^{|A|}(1+\lambda)^{-|N^{+}(A)|}\sum_{B\subseteq{\rm{Int}}(A)}\lambda^{|B|-|N^{+}(B)|}\,.

(3.2)

The polymer model
(\mathcal{P}_{X},\sim,w)
comes with its associated partition function
\Xi=\sum\limits_{\Lambda\in\Omega}\prod\limits_{A\in\Lambda}w(A)
where, as before,
\Omega
denotes the collection of all sets of pairwise compatible polymers. Recall that
\mathcal{A}_{r}
denotes the set of all antichains
S\subseteq B_{n}
such that each
2
-linked component
A
of
S\cap L_{r-1}
satisfies
|N^{+}(A)|\geq(1+1/n)|A|
. The set
\Omega
is therefore in one-to-one correspondence with the set
\{S\cap L^{X}_{r-1}:S\in\mathcal{A}_{r}\}
.

The following lemma motivates the study of this polymer model in the context of Lemma
3.3
.

Lemma 3.4
.

(1+\lambda)^{M}\cdot\Xi=Z_{r}(L^{X}_{[r-2,r]},\lambda)\,,

where
M=|L^{X}_{r}|
.

Proof.

Given an antichain
S\subseteq L^{X}_{[r-2,r]}
, let

\rho(S)=(S\cap L^{X}_{r-1})\cup N^{+}(S\cap L^{X}_{r-2})\,.

We partition the collection of antichains
S
in
L^{X}_{[r-2,r]}
according to the value of
\rho(S)
. Note that if
\rho(S)=A
, then
S\cap L^{X}_{r}
can be any subset of
L^{X}_{r}\backslash N^{+}(A)
. Moreover,
S\cap L^{X}_{r-2}
can be any subset of
{\rm{Int}}(A)
and if
S\cap L^{X}_{r-2}=B
then
S\cap L^{X}_{r-1}=A\backslash N^{+}(B)
. We conclude that

\displaystyle Z_{r}(L^{X}_{[r-2,r]},\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subseteq L^{X}_{r-1}\\
A\in\mathcal{A}_{r}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq L^{X}_{[r-2,r]}\\
S\in\mathcal{A}_{r},\,\rho(S)=A\end{subarray}}\lambda^{|S|}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subseteq L^{X}_{r-1}\\
A\in\mathcal{A}_{r}\end{subarray}}(1+\lambda)^{M-|N^{+}(A)|}\sum_{B\subseteq{\rm{Int}}(A)}\lambda^{|B|}\cdot\lambda^{|A|-|N^{+}(B)|}

\displaystyle=(1+\lambda)^{M}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subseteq L^{X}_{r-1}\\
A\in\mathcal{A}_{r}\end{subarray}}w(A)\,.

The result follows by noting that if
A
decomposes into the collection of mutually compatible polymers
\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{\ell}\}
, then
w(A)=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}w(A_{i})
.
∎

We also consider a polymer model
(\mathcal{P}_{X},\sim,w^{\prime})
where
w^{\prime}
is the simplified weight

w^{\prime}(A)=\lambda^{|A|}(1+\lambda)^{-|N^{+}(A)|}.

We let
\Xi^{\prime}=\sum_{\Lambda\in\Omega}\prod_{A\in\Lambda}w^{\prime}(A)
the associated partition function and note that (a simplified version of) the proof of Lemma
3.4
shows that

(1+\lambda)^{M}\cdot\Xi^{\prime}=Z_{r}(L^{X}_{[r-1,r]},\lambda)\,,

where again
M=|L^{X}_{r}|
.
Therefore, to prove Lemma
3.3
, it suffices to show that

\Xi/\Xi^{\prime}=1+o(1/(n\sqrt{N}))\,.

(3.3)

and
a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we now let
\mathcal{G}^{1}_{j,\ell,a_{1},a_{2}}
denote the set of all clusters
\Gamma
containing
v_{1}
, with
\|\Gamma\|=j
,
|V(\Gamma)|=\ell
and with the set of
v_{1}
-active coordinates equal to
[a_{1}]\cup[k,k+a_{2}-1]
. Define
\mathcal{G}^{2}_{j,\ell,a_{1},a_{2}}
in the analogous way, where we instead require that the clusters
\Gamma
contain
v_{2}
and that
[a_{1}]\cup[k+2,k+a_{2}+1]
is the set of
v_{2}
-active coordinates. Note that if
\Gamma
is a cluster with
|V(\Gamma)|=\ell
containing
v_{r}
, then
\Gamma
has at most
2(\ell-1)

v_{r}
-active coordinates. Thus
\mathcal{G}^{r}_{j,\ell,a_{1},a_{2}}=\emptyset
unless
a_{1}+a_{2}\leq 2(\ell-1)
. Note also that
\|\Gamma\|\geq|V(\Gamma)|
, so
\mathcal{G}^{r}_{j,\ell,a_{1},a_{2}}=\emptyset
unless
\ell\leq j
.

Now by vertex transitivity within the parts
L_{k-1},L_{k+1}
of
G_{C}
and symmetry of coordinates, we have

\begin{split}&\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}\\
\|\Gamma\|=j\end{subarray}}w_{C}(\Gamma)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}\frac{1}{\ell}\times\\
&\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(a_{1},a_{2})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2}\\
a_{1}+a_{2}\leq 2(l-1)\end{subarray}}\left(\binom{n}{k-1}\binom{k-1}{a_{1}}\binom{n-k+1}{a_{2}}\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{G}^{1}_{j,\ell,a_{1},a_{2}}}\mkern-24.0muw_{C}(\Gamma)+\binom{n}{k+1}\binom{k+1}{a_{1}}\binom{n-k-1}{a_{2}}\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{G}^{2}_{j,\ell,a_{1},a_{2}}}\mkern-24.0muw_{C}(\Gamma)\right).\end{split}

The factors
\binom{n}{k-1},\binom{n}{k+1}
come from choosing a root vertex
v_{1}\in L_{k-1}
or root vertex
v_{2}\in L_{k+1}
. The
1/\ell
comes from the fact that each cluster
\Gamma
with
|V(\Gamma)|=\ell
is counted by precisely
\ell
choices of root vertex. The factors of
\binom{k-1}{a_{1}}\binom{n-k+1}{a_{2}}
and
\binom{k+1}{a_{1}}\binom{n-k-1}{a_{2}}
come from the choice of active coordinates relative to the root vertex.

Given
\Gamma\in\mathcal{G}^{1}_{j,\ell,a_{1},a_{2}}\cup\mathcal{G}^{2}_{j,\ell,a_{1},a_{2}}
, we can write

w_{C}(\Gamma)=\phi(G_{\Gamma})\lambda^{j}(1+\lambda)^{-(k+1)j+\alpha(\Gamma)}

with
\alpha(\Gamma)\coloneqq(k+1)j-\sum_{A\in\Gamma}|\partial(A)|
. Observe that
0\leq\alpha(\Gamma)\leq j(j+1)/2
: indeed, each
A\in\Gamma
is a subset of either
L_{k-1}
or
L_{k+1}
. Moreover, any pair
u,v\in L_{k-1}
has at most one common neighbour in
\mathcal{U}
and similarly for pairs
u,v\in L_{k+1}
. It follows that
k|A|-|A|(|A|-1)/2\leq|\partial(A)|\leq(k+1)|A|
.

Since
|\mathcal{G}^{r}_{j,\ell,a_{1},a_{2}}|
is independent of
n
for
r\in\{1,2\}
, we have that

\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{G}^{r}_{j,\ell,a_{1},a_{2}}}w_{C}(\Gamma)(1+\lambda)^{j(k+1)}=\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{G}^{r}_{j,\ell,a_{1},a_{2}}}\phi(G_{\Gamma})\lambda^{j}(1+\lambda)^{\alpha(\Gamma)}

is independent of
n
and is a polynomial in
\lambda
of degree at most
j+j(j+1)/2\leq 2j^{2}
.
It follows that we may choose
S^{1}_{j},S^{2}_{j}
in
(
4.6
) such that,
\deg_{n}S_{j}^{r}\leq 2(j-1)
and
\deg_{\lambda}S_{j}^{r}\leq 2j^{2}
.

It remains to show that the coefficients of
S_{j}^{r}(n,\lambda)
can be computed efficiently. We split this into two steps.

Claim 4.3
.

For
r\in\{1,2\}
and
j,\ell,a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
, the set
\mathcal{G}^{r}_{j,\ell,a_{1},a_{2}}
has size
e^{O(j\log j)}
and can be generated in time
e^{O(j\log j)}
.

Proof.

We assume that
r=1
(the proof for
r=2
is identical).
Recall that we may assume
a_{1}+a_{2}\leq 2(\ell-1)
and
\ell\leq j
else
\mathcal{G}^{1}_{j,\ell,a_{1},a_{2}}=\emptyset
.
For each
m\leq\ell
, we first generate the set
\mathcal{L}_{m}
of all
2
-linked sets (in
G_{C}
)
A
with
|A|=m
,
v_{1}\in A
and whose set of active coordinates is a subset of
[a_{1}]\cup[k,k+a_{2}-1]
. We do this by iterating over
1\leq m\leq\ell
.

For
m=1
this is trivial. For
m\geq 2
, suppose we have already generated
\mathcal{L}_{m-1}
. For each
A\in\mathcal{L}_{m-1}
and
v\in A
, run through the list of all vertices
w
at distance 2 from
v
and with set of active coordinates contained in
[a_{1}]\cup[k,k+a_{2}-1]
. Then, for each such
w
not in
A
, add
A\cup\{w\}
to
\mathcal{L}_{m}
. There are
m-1
choices for
v
, and given a choice of
v
there are at most
\binom{a_{1}+a_{2}}{2}\leq 2\ell^{2}
choices of
w
. This shows that
|\mathcal{L}_{m}|\leq 2m\ell^{2}|\mathcal{L}_{m-1}|
and that
\mathcal{L}_{m}
can be generated in time
O(m\ell^{2}|\mathcal{L}_{m-1}|)
given
\mathcal{L}_{m-1}
. In particular,
|\mathcal{L}_{\ell}|\leq 2^{\ell}\ell!\ell^{2\ell}=e^{O(j\log j)}
.

Now let
\mathcal{L}_{\ell}^{a_{1},a_{2}}
denote the subset of
\mathcal{L}_{\ell}
consisting of all
2
-linked sets
A
whose set of active coordinates is precisely
[a_{1}]\cup[k,k+a_{2}-1]
. We can generate
\mathcal{L}_{\ell}^{a_{1},a_{2}}
in time
e^{O(j\log j)}
by checking each element of
\mathcal{L}_{\ell}
in turn.

Finally, we generate
\mathcal{G}^{1}_{j,\ell,a_{1},a_{2}}
. For each
A\in\mathcal{L}_{\ell}^{a_{1},a_{2}}
in turn, we will generate the list of all clusters
\Gamma
with
\|\Gamma\|=j
and
V(\Gamma)=A
. We do this in time
e^{O(j\log j)}
. Recall that a cluster
\Gamma
is an ordered tuple of polymers
(A_{1},\dots,A_{t})
with
\sum_{i=1}^{t}|A_{i}|=j
. Now, there are precisely
2^{j}
ordered (integer) partitions of
j
of the form
(b_{1},\dots,b_{t})
with
\sum_{i=1}^{t}b_{i}=j
. For each element of
\binom{A}{b_{1}}\times\dots\times\binom{A}{b_{t}}
(a set of size at most
e^{O(j\log j)}
), we may check if it constitutes a cluster.

This shows that
|\mathcal{G}^{1}_{j,\ell,a_{1},a_{2}}|=e^{O(j\log j)}
and that we may generate the whole list
\mathcal{G}^{1}_{j,\ell,a_{1},a_{2}}
in time
e^{O(j\log j)}
.

∎

Claim 4.4
.

For
r\in\{1,2\}
and
\Gamma\in\mathcal{G}^{r}_{j,\ell,a_{1},a_{2}}
, we can calculate
w_{C}(\Gamma)
in time
e^{O(j)}
.

Proof.

Given some
\Gamma\in\mathcal{G}^{r}_{j,\ell,a_{1},a_{2}}
and
A\in\Gamma
, we can calculate
|\partial(A)|
in time
O(j^{2})
.
Moreover, the Ursell function
\phi(G_{\Gamma})
is an evaluation of the Tutte polynomial of
G_{\Gamma}
, and thus by a result of Björklund, Husfeldt, Kaski and Koivisto
[
12
, Theorem 1]
it may be computed in time
e^{O(|V(G_{\Gamma})|)}=e^{O(j)}
. Thus we can compute
w(\Gamma)
in time
e^{O(j)}
.

∎

The above two claims conclude the proof of
Theorem

4.2
.
∎

5
Antichains in three central layers of a given size

In the previous section we saw how to obtain detailed asymptotics for the partition function
\Xi_{C}
. As we will see in later sections (Lemmas
6.1
and
7.2
), this will translate to detailed asymptotics for
Z(C_{n},\lambda)
.

For the proofs of Theorems
1.2
,
1.3
, and
1.5
, we will use
Lemma

2.3
to obtain information about antichains in
B_{n}
given our knowledge of antichains in
C_{n}
. Let
i_{m}(C_{n})
denote the number of antichains of size
m
in
C_{n}
. Our next aim is twofold. First, we wish to find a suitable value of
\lambda
such that
i_{m}(C_{n})\lambda^{m}/Z(C_{n},\lambda)=\Omega(1/\sqrt{N})
(cf. (
2.7
) and
Theorem

1.6
). Secondly, we wish to estimate
i_{m}(C_{n})
itself. It turns out that the first of these objectives will in fact be just one step on the way to establishing the second one, so we will focus on the latter. We achieve this by again studying the polymer model
(\mathcal{P}_{C},\sim,w_{C})
. Let
\nu
denote the measure on
\Omega=\Omega(\mathcal{P}_{C},\sim)
given by

\nu(\Lambda)=\frac{\prod_{A\in\Lambda}w_{C}(A)}{\Xi_{C}}\,.

(5.1)

Recall that we let
k=\lceil n/2\rceil
. Let
\mu^{C}_{\lambda}
denote the measure on antichains in
C_{n}^{\prime}=L_{[k-1,k+1]}
defined by the following process.

1.

Select
\Lambda
according to
\nu
and let
\bar{\Lambda}=\bigcup_{A\in\Lambda}A
.

2.

Let
U
be a
\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}
-random subset of
L_{k}\backslash\partial(\bar{\Lambda})
.

3.

Output the antichain
S=\bar{\Lambda}\cup U
.

We let
i_{m}^{C}
denote the number of antichains
A
of size
m
such that
\mu^{C}_{\lambda}(A)>0
(note that
i_{m}^{C}
is independent of
\lambda
). We will see, in
Lemma

6.1
, that when
n
is even
\mu^{C}_{\lambda}
is simply the hard-core model on
C_{n}
at activity
\lambda
(i.e.
\mu_{C_{n},\lambda}
defined at (
1.2
)) and so
i_{m}(C_{n})=i_{m}^{C}
. When
n
is odd we will show in
Lemma

7.2
that
i_{m}(C_{n})=(2+o(1))i_{m}^{C}
.

In the remainder of this section we develop the tools needed to estimate
i_{m}^{C}
. The main aim of this section is to prove the following.

Theorem 5.1
.

For
r\in\{0,1\}
,
there exists a sequence of rational functions
R^{r}_{j}(n,\beta)
,
j\in\mathbb{N},
such that the following holds. Given
\epsilon>0,\ t\in\mathbb{N}
and
m\in[N]
such that

\beta\coloneqq\frac{m}{N}\in[1-4^{-1/t}+\epsilon,1]
we have

i_{m}^{C}=(1+o(1))\binom{N}{m}\exp\left[N\sum_{j=1}^{t-1}R^{r}_{j}\left(n,\beta\right)\cdot\left(1-\beta\right)^{jk}\,\right]

if
n\equiv r\pmod{2}
.
Moreover, the coefficients of
R^{r}_{j}
can be computed in time
e^{O(j\log j)}.

At a high level, we follow the approach taken in
[
36
]
to enumerate independent sets of a fixed size in the hypercube.

Throughout this section we assume that
\lambda\geq C\log^{2}n/\sqrt{n}
for
C
sufficiently large. We also assume that
\lambda=O(1)
; for concreteness we will take
\lambda\leq 10
. We will later treat the easy case where
\lambda
is unbounded separately (in Sections
6.4
and
7.6
), using a more direct argument.

Let
\mathbf{A}
denote an antichain sampled according to
\mu^{C}_{\lambda}
. Note that by the definition of
\mu^{C}_{\lambda}
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|\mathbf{A}|=m)=\frac{i_{m}^{C}\lambda^{m}}{(1+\lambda)^{N}\Xi_{C}}\,.

(5.2)

Given our detailed knowledge of
\Xi_{C}
obtained from the previous section, it therefore suffices to estimate
\mathbb{P}(|\mathbf{A}|=m)
in order to estimate
i_{m}^{C}
. We are at liberty to choose
\lambda
for this task (provided
\lambda\geq C\log^{2}n/\sqrt{n}
and
\lambda=O(1)
) and so we choose
\lambda
so that the expectation of
\mathbf{A}
is close to
m
. Our strategy for estimating
\mathbb{P}(|\mathbf{A}|=m)
is then as follows. Let
\mathbf{\Lambda}
be sampled according to
\nu
(as in Step
1
in the definition of
\mu^{C}_{\lambda}
). First we show that
\mathbf{\bar{\Lambda}}
is, with very high probability, ‘small’ in some appropriate sense (see Lemma
5.3
). We then show that
|\mathbf{\bar{\Lambda}}|
and
|\partial(\mathbf{\bar{\Lambda}})|
are concentrated around their (small) means (Lemma
5.6
). We may therefore think of
|\mathbf{A}|
as a random variable that is very close to
\text{Bin}\left(N,\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}\right),
and by the Local Central Limit Theorem we may (asymptotically) write down the probability that
|\mathbf{A}|=m.

We will encounter several technicalities along the way. For instance, in order to prove Theorem
1.5
we may (and will) choose
\lambda
so that
\mathbb{E}[|\mathbf{A}|]=m,
but this value of
\lambda
is not explicit (its existence simply follows by continuity of the expectation). For our proof of Theorem
1.3
, we instead wish to find a value of
\lambda
that can be computed efficiently. We therefore have to relax our condition to just
\left|\mathbb{E}[|\mathbf{A}|]-m\right|=o(\sqrt{N})
; a suitable
\lambda
for which this holds is given by Lemma
5.11
below.

It will be useful to consider the modified polymer model
(\mathcal{P}_{C},\sim,\tilde{w}_{C})
where
\tilde{w}_{C}(A)\coloneqq w_{C}(A)e^{|A|/n^{3}}
. Let
\tilde{f}(A)\coloneqq|A|/(2n^{3})
and let
g
be as in (
3.6
). We observe that the Kotecký-Preiss condition (
2.4
) for the polymer model
(\mathcal{P}_{C},\sim,\tilde{w}_{C})
with the functions
\tilde{f},

g:\mathcal{P}_{C}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
becomes precisely the inequality (
4.2
) that we have already established. Recall that we let
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{P}_{C},\sim)
denote the set of clusters for this polymer model, and we write
g(\Gamma)=\sum_{{\gamma}\in\Gamma}g({\gamma})
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\alpha\in[0,1]
,
M>0
and let
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
. If
g(\Gamma)\geq M
for all
\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
then

\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}|\tilde{w}_{C}(\Gamma)|e^{\alpha g(\Gamma)}\leq 2^{n}e^{-(1-\alpha)M}\,.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.2

e^{(1-\alpha)M}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\\
\Gamma\nsim\{v\}\end{subarray}}|\tilde{w}_{C}(\Gamma)|e^{\alpha g(\Gamma)}\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}\\
\Gamma\nsim\{v\}\end{subarray}}|\tilde{w}_{C}(\Gamma)|e^{g(\Gamma)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{kpbounds}}}{{\leq}}\tilde{f}(\{v\})\leq 1\,.

Summing this inequality over all
v\in L_{k-1}\cup L_{k+1}\subseteq B_{n}
completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

If
\mathbf{\Lambda}
is sampled according to
\nu
, then

|\mathbf{\bar{\Lambda}}|\leq N/n^{2},

with probability at least
1-\exp\{\Omega(N/n^{5})\}
.

Proof.

By Markov’s inequality, we have
\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathbf{\bar{\Lambda}}|>N/n^{2}\right)\leq e^{-N/n^{5}}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{|\mathbf{\bar{\Lambda}}|/n^{3}}\right]
.

Let
\tilde{\Xi}_{C}=\Xi(\mathcal{P}_{C},\sim,\tilde{w}_{C})
be the polymer model partition function, and note that

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{|\mathbf{\Lambda}|/n^{3}}\right]=\tilde{\Xi}_{C}/\Xi_{C}\leq\tilde{\Xi}_{C}\,.

Observing that
g(\Gamma)\geq\frac{n}{3}\ln(1+\lambda)
for all
\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}
, we have, by
Lemma

5.2
(with
\alpha=0
),

\ln\tilde{\Xi}_{C}=\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}}\tilde{w}_{C}(\Gamma)\leq\frac{2^{n}}{(1+\lambda)^{n/3}}=o\left(\frac{N}{n^{5}}\right),

which yields

\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathbf{\bar{\Lambda}}|>N/n^{2}\right)\leq e^{-N/n^{5}}\tilde{\Xi}_{C}=e^{-\Omega(N/n^{5})}.

∎

For a cluster
\Gamma
, recall we write
\|\Gamma\|
for
\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}|\gamma|
. Let us also write
\|\partial(\Gamma)\|
for
\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}|\partial(\gamma)|
, so that we have
w_{C}(\Gamma)=\phi(G_{\Gamma})\lambda^{\|\Gamma\|}(1+\lambda)^{-\|\partial(\Gamma)\|}.

Lemma 5.4
.

For any
t,\ell\geq 1
fixed, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}\\
\|\Gamma\|\geq t\end{subarray}}|w_{C}(\Gamma)|\|\Gamma\|^{\ell}=O\left(\frac{2^{n}n^{100t+\ell}}{(1+\lambda)^{\frac{n}{2}t}}\right).

(5.3)

Proof.

First, for
t\leq\|\Gamma\|\leq n/10,
note that
g(\Gamma)\geq\ln(1+\lambda)\left(\frac{n-2}{2}t-t^{2}\right)-100t\ln n
. By Lemma
5.2
(with
\alpha=0
), noting that
|w_{C}(\Gamma)|\leq|\tilde{w}_{C}(\Gamma)|
for all
\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}
, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}:\\
t\leq\|\Gamma\|\leq n/10\end{subarray}}|w_{C}(\Gamma)|\|\Gamma\|^{\ell}\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}:\\
t\leq\|\Gamma\|\leq n/10\end{subarray}}|w_{C}(\Gamma)|n^{\ell}\leq\frac{2^{n}n^{100t+\ell}}{(1+\lambda)^{\frac{n-2}{2}t-t^{2}}}=O\left(\frac{2^{n}n^{100t+\ell}}{(1+\lambda)^{\frac{n}{2}t}}\right)

(recall that
\lambda\leq 10
).
For
n/10<\|\Gamma\|\leq n^{4},
we have
g(\Gamma)\geq\frac{n\|\Gamma\|}{100}\ln(1+\lambda)\geq\frac{n^{2}}{1000}\ln(1+\lambda),
and so
\|\Gamma\|^{\ell}\leq n^{4\ell}\leq e^{g(\Gamma)/2}
. Thus by Lemma
5.2
(with
\alpha=1/2
),

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}\\
\frac{n}{10}<\|\Gamma\|\leq n^{4}\end{subarray}}|w_{C}(\Gamma)|\|\Gamma\|^{\ell}\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}\\
\frac{n}{10}<\|\Gamma\|\leq n^{4}\end{subarray}}|w_{C}(\Gamma)|e^{g(\Gamma)/2}\leq\frac{2^{n}}{(1+\lambda)^{\frac{n^{2}}{2000}}}.

Finally, for
\|\Gamma\|>n^{4},
observe that
g(\Gamma)\geq\frac{\|\Gamma\|}{n^{2}}\ln(1+\lambda)\geq n^{2}\ln(1+\lambda)
, and so
\|\Gamma\|^{\ell}\leq e^{g(\Gamma)/2}
. Therefore, as above,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}\\
\|\Gamma\|>n^{4}\end{subarray}}|w_{C}(\Gamma)|\|\Gamma\|^{\ell}\leq\frac{2^{n}}{(1+\lambda)^{\frac{n^{2}}{2}}}.

Summing the above three bounds we get the result.
∎

We now introduce the
cumulants
of a random variable
X
. First, the
cumulant generating function
of
X
is
h_{t}(X)\coloneqq\ln\mathbb{E}[e^{tX}].
The
\ell^{\text{th}}
cumulant of
X
is then

\kappa_{\ell}(X)\coloneqq\left.\frac{\partial^{\ell}h_{t}(X)}{\partial t^{\ell}}\right|_{t=0}.

The first two cumulants are
\kappa_{1}(X)=\mathbb{E}[X]
and
\kappa_{2}(X)=\text{var}(X)
. Note that for constants
a,b>0
we have

\kappa_{\ell}((X-a)/b)=\kappa_{\ell}(X)/b^{\ell}.

(5.4)

As before, let
\mathbf{\Lambda}
be sampled according to
\nu
.
The cumulants of
|N(\mathbf{\Lambda})|
can be expressed in terms of the cluster expansion as follows.
For
t\geq 0
, consider the polymer model
(\mathcal{P}_{C},\sim,w_{t})
with modified weights
w_{t}(A)=w(A)e^{t|\partial(A)|}
and let
\Xi_{t}=\Xi(\mathcal{P}_{C},\sim,w_{t})
denote the corresponding partition function. We then have

\displaystyle h_{t}(|\partial(\mathbf{\bar{\Lambda}})|)=\ln\Xi_{t}-\ln\Xi_{0}\,.

Applying the cluster expansion to
\ln\Xi_{t}
(for
t
sufficiently small), taking derivatives, and evaluating at
t=0
shows that

\displaystyle\kappa_{\ell}(|\partial(\mathbf{\bar{\Lambda}})|)=\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}}w_{C}(\Gamma)\|\partial(\Gamma)\|^{\ell}\,.

(5.5)

Similarly

\kappa_{\ell}\left(|\mathbf{\bar{\Lambda}}|\right)=\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}}w_{C}(\Gamma)\|\Gamma\|^{\ell}.

(5.6)

We use the following standard fact (see e.g.
[
31
]
).

Fact 5.5
.

Let
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of real-valued random variables such that
\kappa_{1}(X_{n})\rightarrow 0,

\kappa_{2}(X_{n})\rightarrow 1
and
\kappa_{\ell}(X_{n})\rightarrow 0
for all
\ell\geq 3.
Then the sequence
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
converges in distribution to a standard Gaussian.

Lemma 5.6
.

If
\mathbf{\Lambda}
is sampled according to
\nu
, then

\mathbb{P}\left(|\partial(\mathbf{\bar{\Lambda}})|=\mathbb{E}(|\partial(\mathbf{\bar{\Lambda}})|)+o(\sqrt{\lambda N})\right)=1-o(1)

and

\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathbf{\bar{\Lambda}}|=\mathbb{E}(|\mathbf{\bar{\Lambda}}|)+o(\sqrt{\lambda N})\right)=1-o(1).

Proof.

We present only the proof for
|\partial(\mathbf{\bar{\Lambda}})|
; the concentration bound for
|\mathbf{\bar{\Lambda}}|
follows similarly. Note that

\mathbb{E}[|\partial(\mathbf{\bar{\Lambda}})|]=\kappa_{1}(|\partial(\mathbf{\bar{\Lambda}})|)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{cumeq}}}{{=}}\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}}w_{C}(\Gamma)\|\partial(\Gamma)\|\leq n\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}}|w_{C}(\Gamma)|\|\Gamma\|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{cmbound}}}{{=}}O\left(\frac{2^{n}n^{102}}{(1+\lambda)^{\frac{n}{2}}}\right).

We split our proof into two cases, depending on how large
\lambda
is.

More precisely, let
\epsilon>0
be a sufficiently small constant.
If
\lambda\geq 3-\epsilon
, then
(1+\lambda)^{\frac{n}{2}}\geq 2^{n}(1-\epsilon/4)^{\frac{n}{2}}=2^{n}e^{-\epsilon^{\prime}n},
where
\epsilon^{\prime}>0
can be made arbitrarily small by choosing
\epsilon
suitably small. Hence the right-hand side of the above bound is
o(\sqrt{\lambda N}).
The conclusion then follows from Markov’s inequality.

Now assume that
\lambda\leq 3-\epsilon
. In this case, we will use
5.5
. For all
\ell\geq 2
, we have

\kappa_{\ell}(|\partial(\mathbf{\bar{\Lambda}})|)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{cumeq}}}{{=}}\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}}w_{C}(\Gamma)\|\partial(\Gamma)\|^{\ell}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{cmbound}}}{{=}}O\left(\frac{2^{n}n^{100+2\ell}}{(1+\lambda)^{\frac{n}{2}}}\right)

(5.7)

and

\displaystyle\text{var}(|\partial(\mathbf{\bar{\Lambda}})|)=\kappa_{2}(|\partial(\mathbf{\bar{\Lambda}})|)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{cumeq}}}{{\geq}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}\\
\|\Gamma\|=1\end{subarray}}w_{C}(\Gamma)\|\partial(\Gamma)\|^{2}-n^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}\\
\|\Gamma\|\geq 2\end{subarray}}|w_{C}(\Gamma)|\|\Gamma\|^{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{cmbound}}}{{=}}2\binom{n}{n/2-1}\frac{\lambda(n/2+1)^{2}}{(1+\lambda)^{\frac{n}{2}+1}}-O\left(\frac{2^{n}n^{204}}{(1+\lambda)^{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\ =\Omega\left(\frac{2^{n}}{(1+\lambda)^{\frac{n}{2}}}\right).

Setting
Z=(|\partial(\mathbf{\bar{\Lambda}})|-\mathbb{E}|\partial(\mathbf{\bar{\Lambda}})|)/\sqrt{\text{var}(|\partial(\mathbf{\bar{\Lambda}})|)},
we have, for fixed
\ell\geq 3
,

\kappa_{\ell}(Z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{cumlin}}}{{=}}\text{var}(|\partial(\mathbf{\bar{\Lambda}})|)^{-\ell/2}\kappa_{\ell}(|\partial(\mathbf{\bar{\Lambda}})|)=O\left(\frac{n^{100+2\ell}((1+\lambda)^{\frac{n}{2}})^{\frac{\ell}{2}-1}}{(2^{n})^{\frac{\ell}{2}-1}}\right)=o(1)\,,

where for the final equality we used the assumption
\lambda\leq 3-\epsilon
.
Hence by Fact
5.5
,
Z
converges in distribution to a unit Gaussian. Finally, note that setting
\ell=2
in (
5.7
) we have
\text{var}(|\partial(\mathbf{\bar{\Lambda}})|)=o(\lambda N).
The conclusion follows.
∎

Recall that
\mathbf{A}
is an antichain sampled from the distribution
\mu^{C}_{\lambda}
, and
|\mathbf{A}|=|\mathbf{\bar{\Lambda}}|+\text{Bin}\left(N-|\partial(\mathbf{\bar{\Lambda}})|,\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}\right).
Taking expectations and using (
5.5
) and (
5.6
), we obtain the following lemma.

Lemma 5.7
.

\mathbb{E}|\mathbf{A}|=\mathbb{E}|\mathbf{\bar{\Lambda}}|+\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}\left(N-\mathbb{E}|\partial(\mathbf{\bar{\Lambda}})|\right)=\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}N+\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}}w_{C}(\Gamma)\left(\|\Gamma\|-\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}\|\partial(\Gamma)\|\right).

In particular, this gives the following estimate for
\mathbb{E}|\mathbf{A}|
. Recall that
k=\lceil n/2\rceil
.

Corollary 5.8
.

For
r\in\{0,1\}
,
there exists a sequence of polynomials
F^{r}_{j}(n,\lambda)
,
j\in\mathbb{N},
such that for any fixed
t\geq 0
we have

\mathbb{E}|\mathbf{A}|=\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}N\left[1+\frac{1}{(n-k+1)(k+1)}\sum_{j=1}^{t}F^{r}_{j}(n,\lambda)(1+\lambda)^{-j(k+1)}\right]+O\left(\frac{2^{n}n^{100(t+1)+2}}{(1+\lambda)^{(t+1)k}}\right)

(5.8)

if
n\equiv r\pmod{2}
.
Moreover
\deg_{n}F^{r}_{j}\leq 2j+1
and
\deg_{\lambda}F^{r}_{j}\leq 2j^{2}
, and the coefficients of
F^{r}_{j}
can be computed in time
e^{O(j\log j)}.

In particular,

\mathbb{E}|\mathbf{A}|=(1+o(1))\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}N\,.

(5.9)

Proof.

We prove this only in the case of
n
odd; the case of
n
even is similar but simpler. By Lemmas
5.4
and
5.7
, noting that
\|\partial(\Gamma)\|\leq n\|\Gamma\|
, we have

\mathbb{E}|\mathbf{A}|=\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}N+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}\\
\|\Gamma\|\leq t\end{subarray}}w_{C}(\Gamma)\left(\|\Gamma\|-\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}\|\partial(\Gamma)\|\right)+O\left(\frac{2^{n}n^{100(t+1)+2}}{(1+\lambda)^{(t+1)k}}\right).

Recalling that
w_{C}(\Gamma)=\phi(G_{\Gamma})\lambda^{\|\Gamma\|}(1+\lambda)^{-\|\partial(\Gamma)\|}
and taking the partial derivative with respect to
\lambda
on both sides of (
4.6
) we obtain, for fixed
j\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\binom{n}{k-1}\left[\frac{\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}S_{j}^{1}}{(1+\lambda)^{j(k+1)}}-\frac{j(k+1)S_{j}^{1}}{(1+\lambda)^{j(k+1)+1}}\right]+\binom{n}{k+1}\left[\frac{\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}S_{j}^{2}}{(1+\lambda)^{j(k+1)}}-\frac{j(k+1)S_{j}^{2}}{(1+\lambda)^{j(k+1)+1}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}\\
\|\Gamma\|=j\end{subarray}}w_{C}(\Gamma)\left(\|\Gamma\|-\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}\|\partial\Gamma\|\right).

Noting that
N=\binom{n}{k}
,
\binom{n}{k-1}/N=k/(n-k+1)
,
\binom{n}{k+1}/N=(n-k)/(k+1)
and setting

F_{j}^{1}=k(k+1)\left[(1+\lambda)\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}S_{j}^{1}-j(k+1)S_{j}^{1}\right]+(n-k)(n-k+1)\left[(1+\lambda)\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}S_{j}^{2}-j(k+1)S_{j}^{2}\right]

we obtain (
5.8
). By
Theorem

4.2
,

\deg_{n}F^{1}_{j}\leq 2(j-1)+3=2j+1
and
\deg_{\lambda}F^{1}_{j}\leq 2j^{2}
.
Moreover the coefficients of
F^{1}_{j}(n,\lambda)
can be computed in time
e^{O(j\log j)}
since the same is true for
S_{j}^{1}(n,\lambda),S_{j}^{2}(n,\lambda)
.

Finally note that we obtain (
5.9
) by setting
t=0
in (
5.8
).
∎

Lemma 5.9
.

For
p=p(n)
bounded away from 1 with
pn\rightarrow\infty
, let
X=X(n)\sim\text{Bin}(n,p).
Then, for any
m=np+o(\sqrt{np}),
we have as
n\rightarrow\infty

\mathbb{P}(X=m)=\frac{1+o(1)}{\sqrt{2\pi np(1-p)}}.

Proof.

\mathbb{P}(X=m)=\binom{n}{m}p^{m}(1-p)^{n-m}
, and the result follows from Stirling’s formula.
∎

Lemma 5.10
.

Suppose
m\in[N]
is such that

|\mathbb{E}|\mathbf{A}|-m|=o(\sqrt{{\lambda}N}).

(5.10)

Then

i_{m}^{C}=(1+o(1))\frac{(1+\lambda)^{N+1}\Xi_{C}}{\lambda^{m}\sqrt{2\pi N\lambda}}.

(5.11)

Proof.

We have
i_{m}^{C}=\mathbb{P}(|\mathbf{A}|=m)\lambda^{-m}(1+\lambda)^{N}\Xi_{C}.
Hence it suffices to show that

\mathbb{P}(|\mathbf{A}|=m)=(1+o(1))\frac{1+\lambda}{\sqrt{2\pi N\lambda}}.

As before we let
\mathbf{\Lambda}
denote a sample from
\nu
so that

\mathbb{P}(|\mathbf{A}|=m)=\sum_{\Lambda\in\Omega}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{\Lambda}=\Lambda)\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\text{Bin}\left(N-|\partial(\bar{\Lambda})|,\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}\right)=m-|\bar{\Lambda}|\right).

(5.12)

By Lemma
5.3
, we may condition on the event
|\partial(\bar{\Lambda})|\leq N/n
while only incurring an additive error of
o(1/\sqrt{\lambda N})
. The binomial probabilities in
(
5.12
)
are then bounded uniformly by
O(1/\sqrt{\lambda N})
, so it suffices to show that they are in fact, with high probability, equal to
(1+o(1))\frac{1+\lambda}{\sqrt{2\pi N\lambda}}
. By Lemma
5.9
, it suffices to show that with high probability in the choice of
\mathbf{\Lambda}
, we have

\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}\left(N-|\partial(\bar{\Lambda})|\right)=m-|\bar{\Lambda}|+o(\sqrt{\lambda N}).

But this follows immediately by recalling our assumption that
m=\mathbb{E}|\mathbf{A}|+o(\sqrt{\lambda N})
together with the identity given by Lemma
5.7
and the concentration bounds of Lemma
5.6
.
∎

Lemma 5.11
.

For
r\in\{0,1\}
, there exists a sequence of rational functions
B_{j}^{r}(n,\beta),\ j\in\mathbb{N},
such that the following holds. Given
\epsilon>0
and
t\in\mathbb{N}
, let
q=\lceil t/2\rceil-1.
If
n\equiv r\pmod{2}
and
m\in[N]
is such that
\beta\coloneqq\frac{m}{N}\in[1-4^{-1/t}+\epsilon,1-\epsilon]
, then taking

\lambda\coloneqq\frac{\beta}{1-\beta}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}B_{j}^{r}(n,\beta)(1-\beta)^{j(k+1)}

we have

\left|\mathbb{E}|\mathbf{A}|-m\right|=o(\sqrt{\lambda N}).

Moreover, the coefficients of
B_{j}^{r}
can be computed in time
e^{O(j\log j)}
.

Proof.

With
q=\lceil t/2\rceil-1
, set

\lambda=\frac{\beta}{1-\beta}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}B^{r}_{j}(n,\beta)(1-\beta)^{j(k+1)}

where the functions
B^{r}_{j}
are rational functions in
n,\beta
of constant degree (independent of
n
) to be determined later. Note that by the assumption on
\beta
we have
\lambda=\Theta(1)
.

By
Corollary

5.8
we have

\mathbb{E}|\mathbf{A}|=\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}N\left[1+\frac{1}{(n-k+1)(k+1)}\sum_{j=1}^{q}F_{j}^{r}(n,\lambda)(1+\lambda)^{-j(k+1)}\right]+O\left(\frac{2^{n}n^{100(q+1)+2}}{(1+\lambda)^{(q+1)k}}\right)\,,

(5.13)

where
F_{j}^{r}(n,\lambda)
is a polynomial with
\deg_{n}F_{j}^{r}\leq 2j+1
and
\deg_{\lambda}F_{j}^{r}\leq 2j^{2}
. We now show that our choice of
q=\lceil t/2\rceil-1
makes the error in the above expression equal to
o(N^{1/2})
.

Let
X\coloneqq\lambda(1-\beta)-\beta=\sum_{j=1}^{q}B^{r}_{j}(1-\beta)^{j(k+1)+1}
and note that
X=o(1)
. Since
(1+\lambda)^{-1}=(1-\beta)/(1+X)=(1+o(1))(1-\beta)
and
\beta\geq 1-4^{-1/t}+\epsilon
, it follows that

\displaystyle(1+\lambda)^{-(q+1)k}\leq e^{o(n)}(1-\beta)^{(q+1)k}=O\left(e^{-\Omega(n)}\cdot 4^{-\frac{(q+1)k}{t}}\right)=O\left(e^{-\Omega(n)}N^{-1/2}\right)\,.

(5.14)

Returning to (
5.13
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|\mathbf{A}|=\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}N\left[1+\frac{1}{(n-k+1)(k+1)}\sum_{j=1}^{q}F_{j}^{r}(n,\lambda)(1+\lambda)^{-j(k+1)}\right]+o(N^{1/2})\,.

(5.15)

Our goal is to find a suitable choice of
B^{r}_{1},\dots B^{r}_{q}
that makes (
5.15
) equal to
m+o(N^{1/2})
. Our first step is to expand the RHS of (
5.15
) as a function of
n,\beta,B^{r}_{1},\dots B^{r}_{q}
.

Note that
X=O(n^{O(1)}(1-\beta)^{n/2})
. Since
\beta\geq 1-4^{-1/t}+\epsilon
, it follows that
X^{q+1}=O(e^{-\Omega(n)}N^{-1/2})
.

First we expand
(1+\lambda)^{-\ell}
as function of
\beta
and
X
: for
\ell=O(n)
,

\displaystyle(1+\lambda)^{-\ell}=\frac{(1-\beta)^{\ell}}{(1+X)^{\ell}}

\displaystyle=(1-\beta)^{\ell}\left[\sum_{i=0}^{q}\binom{-\ell}{i}X^{i}+O\left((\ell X)^{q+1}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=(1-\beta)^{\ell}\sum_{i=0}^{q}\binom{-\ell}{i}X^{i}+O\left(e^{-\Omega(n)}N^{-1/2}\right)\,.

(5.16)

Next we rewrite
F_{j}^{r}(n,\lambda)
as a function of
n,\beta
and
X
. Since
\deg_{\lambda}F_{j}^{r}\leq 2j^{2}
and
\lambda=\frac{\beta+X}{1-\beta}
, we may write
F_{j}^{r}(n,\lambda)=(1-\beta)^{-c_{j}}G_{j}^{r}(n,\beta,X)

for some integer
0\leq c_{j}\leq 2j^{2}
and
G_{j}^{r}
a polynomial in
n,\beta,X
with
\deg_{n}G_{j}^{r}\leq 2j+1
. Plugging this into (
5.15
) along with (
5.16
) we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}|\mathbf{A}|=

\displaystyle(\beta+X)\sum_{i=0}^{q}(-X)^{i}\left[1+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\frac{G_{j}^{r}(n,\beta,X)}{(n-k+1)(k+1)}(1-\beta)^{j(k+1)-c_{j}}\sum_{i=0}^{q}\binom{-j(k+1)}{i}X^{i}\right]+o(N^{-1/2}).

(5.17)

Recalling that
X=\sum_{j=1}^{q}B^{r}_{j}(1-\beta)^{j(k+1)+1}
, we rewrite (
5.17
) as a polynomial in
(1-\beta)^{k+1}
and note that by (
5.14
),
n^{O(1)}(1-\beta)^{s(k+1)}=o(N^{-1/2})
for
s\geq q+1
. This yields

\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}_{C_{n},\lambda}|\mathbf{A}|=\beta+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\frac{H^{r}_{j}(n,\beta,B^{r}_{1},\dots B^{r}_{j})}{(n-k+1)(k+1)(1-\beta)^{c_{j}}}\cdot(1-\beta)^{j(k+1)}+o(N^{-1/2})

(5.18)

where
H^{r}_{j}
is a polynomial in
n,\beta,B^{r}_{1},\dots B^{r}_{j}
. Moreover, note that each
H^{r}_{j}
is linear in
B^{r}_{j}
, with nonzero coefficient. By induction, we conclude that there is a choice of
B^{r}_{1},\dots B^{r}_{j}
such that
H^{r}_{1}=\dots=H^{r}_{q}=0
, with each
B^{r}_{j}
a rational function of
n,\beta
of constant degree (depending on
j
but not on
n
). Plugging this choice of
B^{r}_{j}
into (
5.18
) and recalling that
\lambda=\Theta(1)
we conclude that

\mathbb{E}|\mathbf{A}|=\beta N+o(\sqrt{\lambda N})\,.

Finally, note that the above proof also gives an algorithm for computing the functions
B^{r}_{j}
. Indeed, the coefficients of
F^{r}_{j}
can be computed in time
e^{O(j\log j)}
(by
Corollary

5.8
), and therefore so can the coefficients of
G^{r}_{j}
and
H^{r}_{j}
. Having computed
B^{r}_{1},\ldots,B^{r}_{j-1}
, we can compute
B^{r}_{j}
by solving a linear equation.
∎

We are now in a position to prove

5.1
. The final ingredient that we require is the following simple reformulation of
Lemma

5.9
: for
m=m(n)\in[N]
such that
\beta:=m/N
is bounded away from
0
and

, we have, with

\binom{N}{m}=(1+o(1))\frac{(1+\lambda_{0})^{N+1}}{\lambda_{0}^{m}\sqrt{2\pi m\lambda_{0}}}\,.

(5.19)

Proof of

.

Once again, we present only the proof for

odd. The case of even

\lambda
as in

5.11
and let

. Note that

.

By

\displaystyle i_{m}^{C}=

\displaystyle(1+o(1))\binom{N}{m}\left(\frac{1+\lambda}{1+\lambda_{0}}\right)^{N}\left(\frac{\lambda_{0}}{\lambda}\right)^{m}\Xi_{C}\,,

(5.20)

and by

\displaystyle\Xi_{C}=(1+o(1))\exp\left[\sum_{j=1}^{t-1}\binom{n}{k-1}S^{1}_{j}(n,\lambda)(1+\lambda)^{-j(k+1)}+\binom{n}{k+1}S^{2}_{j}(n,\lambda)(1+\lambda)^{-j(k+1)}\right]\,.

Taking logarithms and dividing by

, it remains to prove that we can find

for all

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{1+\lambda}{1+\lambda_{0}}\right)-\beta\ln\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{0}}\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{t-1}\frac{k}{n-k+1}S^{1}_{j}(n,\lambda)(1+\lambda)^{-j(k+1)}+\frac{n-k}{k+1}S^{2}_{j}(n,\lambda)(1+\lambda)^{-j(k+1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{t-1}R_{j}^{1}(n,\beta)(1-\beta)^{jk}+o(N^{-1}).

Now for

To compute the coefficients of

We now consider the case

We have:

Recall that

(the hard-core measure on

at activity

and one on antichains in

will concentrate on antichains that are close to a subset of
L_{k-1}
. The measure on antichains in

will be precisely the measure

introduced in
Section

5
. The measure on antichains

will equivalent under the symmetry
B_{n}\to B_{n}
,

.

We note that a similar strategy was carried out in
[
35
]
to analyse independent sets in the hypercube: there it is shown that the hard-core measure on the discrete hypercube

is well-approximated by a mixture of measures that favour odd/even vertices of
Q_{n}
. This idea is also present in the earlier work of Korshunov and Sapozhenko
[
47
]
and Galvin
[
23
]
on independent sets in

.

In this section, the

Recall that

The construction of the pairs of containers partially adapts a recent improvement on the graph container method by the authors
[

to increase the valid range of

to

(it would have been
\tilde{\Omega}(n^{-1/3})
without the new idea in
[
34
]
). Our analysis for the reconstruction steps is inspired by Galvin
[
22
]
, who also considered a pair of containers in bipartite graphs (as opposed to a poset).

For future reference, we record the basic binomial estimate

{n\choose\leq k}\leq\exp\left(k\log\frac{en}{k}\right)

(8.1)

where
\binom{n}{\leq k}:=\sum_{i\leq k}\binom{n}{i}.

with

\log|\mathcal{V}_{B}|=O(t^{max}\log^{2}n/n).

(8.15)

(8.16)

In sum, putting together the costs (

), (

) and (

(8.17)

8.2.2
Bounding
\sum_{(A,B)\in\mathcal{H}_{r}(a,b,g,h)}\lambda^{|A|+|B|-h}
using containers

We always write
(F,S)
for an approximating pair for
A\in\mathcal{A}
and
(P,Q)
for an approximating pair for
B\in\mathcal{B}
. Let
\gamma
be the absolute constant from
Lemma

8.5
. Recall that we write (
cf.
(
8.8
))
\kappa=\max\{i:2^{i}\leq\sqrt{l}\}
and
\kappa^{\prime}=\max\{j:2^{j}\leq\sqrt{l^{\prime}}\}
.

As we sketched at the beginning of
Section

8
, we apply different reconstruction strategies depending on the interplay of the quadruple
(F,S,P,Q)
. To this end, we define
F
to be
tight
if
|F|\geq g-t/2^{\kappa}~(\geq g-2t/\sqrt{l})
, and
slack
otherwise.
Similarly, say
P
is
tight
if
|P|\geq h-t^{\prime}/2^{\kappa^{\prime}}~(\geq h-2t^{\prime}/\sqrt{l^{\prime}})
, and
slack
otherwise.

We note that if
(F,S)\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{A}}(F^{\prime})
for some
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{A}}
then

|S|-a\leq 2t.

(8.18)

Indeed, by definition
(F,S)
is a
(l/(2^{i}\gamma),u/\gamma)
-approximating pair for some
A\in\mathcal{G}(a,g)
and some
i\in[0,\kappa]
. By
Proposition

8.3
and the inequality
u\leq l
, we have (writing
f=|F|
and
s=|S|
) that
s\leq f+(t-f+s)/\gamma,
which yields
s\leq f+t/(\gamma-1)
. So (recalling that
\gamma\geq 2
), we have
s-a\leq f-a+t\leq g-a+t=2t.

Similarly, if
(P,Q)\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{B}}(P^{\prime})
for some
P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{B}}
then

|Q|-b\leq 2t^{\prime}.

(8.19)

We write
(A,B)\sim_{i,j}(F,S,P,Q)
if
(F,S)\in 2^{L_{r}}\times 2^{L_{r-1}}
is a
(l/(2^{i}\gamma),u/\gamma)
-approximating pair for
A
and
(P,Q)\in 2^{L_{r}-1}\times 2^{L_{r-2}}
is a
(l^{\prime}/(2^{j}\gamma),u^{\prime}/\gamma)
-approximating pair for
B
.

Our goal is to establish the inequality below for any given
(i,j)\in[0,\kappa]\times[0,\kappa^{\prime}]
and
(F,S,P,Q)
:

\displaystyle\sum_{(A,B)\sim_{i,j}(F,S,P,Q)}\lambda^{|A|+|B|-h}\leq(1+\lambda)^{g-\Omega(t^{max}/\sqrt{n})}.

(8.20)

This, combined with (
8.17
) (the total cost for
(F,S,P,Q)
and
i,j\in[0,\kappa]\times[0,\kappa^{\prime}]
) and the assumption that
\lambda\geq C\log^{2}n/\sqrt{n}
(and that we can choose a large enough
C
), will yield the conclusion of
Theorem

3.6
.

We consider four cases to bound the sum in (
8.20
). In what follows, we use
G
for
N^{+}(A)
and
H
for
N^{+}(B)
for simplicity. We also use lower case letters to denote the cardinality of sets denoted by upper case letters, e.g.
p:=|P|,q:=|Q|
, etc.

Case 1.

F,P
slack

In this case, we have

g-f>t/2^{\kappa}\geq t/\sqrt{l},

(8.21)

and

h-p>t^{\prime}/2^{\kappa^{\prime}}\geq t^{\prime}/\sqrt{l^{\prime}}.

(8.22)

By
Proposition

8.3
and the fact that
u\leq l
, we have that

s\leq f+\frac{1}{l}[(g-f)l/\gamma+(s-a)2^{-i}l/\gamma],

so

\begin{split}g-s&\geq g-f-(g-f)/\gamma-(s-a)2^{-i}/\gamma\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{F.slack}}}{{\geq}}(g-f)(1-1/\gamma)-(s-a)(g-f)/(t\gamma)\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{s.t.bd}}}{{\geq}}(g-f)(1-1/\gamma-2/\gamma)=\Omega(g-f)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{F.slack}}}{{=}}\Omega(t/\sqrt{n}).\end{split}

(8.23)

Similarly, we also have

h-q=\Omega(t^{\prime}/\sqrt{n}).

(8.24)

Now, we specify
B
as a subset of
Q
. This specifies
H=N^{+}(B)
, and then we specify
A\setminus H
as a subset of
S\setminus H
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{(A,B)\sim_{i,j}(F,S,P,Q)}\lambda^{|A|+|B|-h}\leq\sum_{B\subseteq Q}\lambda^{|B|}\sum_{A\backslash H\subseteq S\backslash H}\lambda^{|A\backslash H|}\leq(1+\lambda)^{q}(1+\lambda)^{s-h}~

\displaystyle=(1+\lambda)^{g}(1+\lambda)^{-(g-s)-(h-q)}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\lambda)^{g-\Omega(t^{max}/\sqrt{n})}.

Case 2.

F
tight,
P
slack

We first specify
G
by specifying
G\setminus F
as subset of
N(S)
. Note that, by
Proposition

8.3
,
|N(S)|\leq|S|l\leq 2gl
. Also, by the assumption that
F
is tight,
|G\setminus F|\leq t/2^{\kappa}\leq 2t/\sqrt{l}
, so the cost for
G
is

\log\binom{2gl}{\leq 2t/\sqrt{l}}=O(t\log n/\sqrt{n}).

(8.25)

The specification of
G
gives
[A]
for free (but not
A
).

Next, we specify
B
as a subset of
Q
, then specify
A\setminus H
as a subset of
[A]\setminus H
. Noting that
|Q|=q
and
[A]\setminus H=a-h
we therefore have

\displaystyle\sum_{(A,B)\sim_{i,j}(F,S,P,Q)}\lambda^{|A|+|B|-h}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left[O(t\log n/\sqrt{n})\right](1+\lambda)^{q}(1+\lambda)^{a-h}~

\displaystyle=\exp\left[O(t\log n/\sqrt{n})\right](1+\lambda)^{g}(1+\lambda)^{-t-(h-q)}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\lambda)^{g-\Omega(t^{max}/\sqrt{n})},

where the last inequality uses the facts that
t\log n/\sqrt{n}\ll t\lambda
and (
8.24
).

Case 3.

F
slack,
P
tight

We first specify
H
by specifying
H\setminus P
as a subset of
N(Q)
. Note that again by
Proposition

8.3
, we have
|N(Q)|\leq|Q|l^{\prime}\leq 2hl^{\prime}
and
|H\setminus P|\leq 2t^{\prime}/\sqrt{l^{\prime}}
(since

is tight), and so the cost for
H
is

\log\binom{2hl^{\prime}}{2t^{\prime}/\sqrt{l^{\prime}}}=O(t^{\prime}\log n/\sqrt{n}).

(8.26)

Then (with
[B]
given by

) specify
B
as a subset of
[B]
. Finally, specify
A\setminus H
as a subset of

. Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{(A,B)\sim_{i,j}(F,S,P,Q)}\lambda^{|A|+|B|-h}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left[O(t^{\prime}\log n/\sqrt{n})\right](1+\lambda)^{b}(1+\lambda)^{s-h}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left[O(t^{\prime}\log n/\sqrt{n})\right](1+\lambda)^{g}(1+\lambda)^{-(g-s)-t^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\lambda)^{g-\Omega(t^{max}/\sqrt{n})},

where the last inequality uses the facts that
t^{\prime}\log n/\sqrt{n}\ll t^{\prime}\lambda
and (
8.23
).

Case 4.

F,P
tight

We first specify
H
by specifying
H\setminus P
as a subset of

whose cost is as in (

), then specify

as a subset of

. Next, we specify

as a subset of

\displaystyle\sum_{(A,B)\sim_{i,j}(F,S,P,Q)}\lambda^{|A|+|B|-h}

9
Concluding remarks

In this paper we proved several results on the number and typical structure of antichains in
B_{n}
.
Some of our results (e.g.
Theorem

1.5
) are limited by our application of the container tools in the previous section. We believe that the following strengthening of
Theorem

1.5
holds:

Conjecture 9.1
.

There exists a function
\beta^{*}(n)=\tilde{\Theta}(1/n)
such that if

\beta\geq\beta^{*}(n)
, then almost all antichains of size
\beta N
in
B_{n}
are contained in three central layers.

Conjecture
9.1
would follow if one could prove a version of
Theorem

3.6
that holds for
\lambda=\tilde{\Omega}(1/n)
. Moreover for
\beta
in the relevant range, a heuristic calculation suggests that the probability a vertex
v\in L_{n/2-2}\cup L_{n/2+2}
(assuming
n
is even for simplicity) is contained in a uniformly random antichain of size
\beta N
is approximately
\beta(1-\beta)^{n^{2}/4}
. The factor of
(1-\beta)^{n^{2}/4}
comes from the fact that
v
blocks approximately
n^{2}/4
vertices in the middle layer
L_{n/2}
.
\beta=\Theta(1/n)
is therefore the threshold at which one might expect to see vertices in layers
L_{n/2-2}\cup L_{n/2+2}
in a uniformly random antichain of size
\beta N
.

In fact, we conjecture the following sequence of thresholds generalising
9.1
.

Conjecture 9.2
.

Given
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a function
\beta^{*}(n)=\tilde{\Theta}(1/n^{k})
such that if

\beta\geq\beta^{*}(n)
, then almost all antichains of size
\beta N
in
B_{n}
are contained in
2k+1
central layers.

A somewhat different direction of research would be to study the number and typical structure of antichains of size
\beta N
in the (vertex) percolated lattice
B_{n,p}
for some
p\in(0,1)
.
Such questions might help to elucidate the connection between the sparsification of Dedekind’s problem/ Sperner’s theorem considered here and those considered in
[
5
,
15
,
27
,
44
,
8
,
56
,
63
]
. In a similar spirit, Kronenberg and Spinka
[
50
]
study the hard-core model on the (edge) percolated hypercube
Q_{d}
and it would be interesting to see if parts of their approach could be adapted to this context.
