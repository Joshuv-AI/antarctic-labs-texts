The formation and function of intercellular bridges in male germlines
Authors: Jian Cui, Jiaxuan Cheng, Bingbing Wu, Andong He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Intercellular bridges (ICBs) are fundamental and evolutionarily conserved structures in spermatogenesis, with roles that extend far beyond simple cytoplasmic connections. These stable conduits, which connect synchronously developing germ cells, are crucial for cytoplasmic sharing and intercellular communication. This review highlights the core regulatory machinery governing ICBs dynamics, including their formation and function, in mouse and Drosophila melanogaster. It further explores the distribution patterns of ICBs and their molecular components across various animal species. Lastly, it identifies key challenges and future directions necessary to fully elucidate the regulatory mechanisms and broader biological significance of ICBs in male germ cell development.

Introduction

Spermatogenesis, a highly orchestrated process essential for male fertility, primarily takes place within the testes. It encompasses three critical phases: the mitotic proliferation and differentiation of spermatogonia, the meiotic division of primary spermatocytes, and the morphological maturation of spermatids into fully differentiated spermatozoa1. This intricate process initiates with diploid spermatogonia, progresses through successive rounds of mitosis and meiosis, and culminates in the production of haploid sperm. The precise regulation of each stage is vital to ensure successful sperm production and male reproductive function. In this process, cell–cell communication plays an indispensable role2. Intercellular bridges (ICBs), as cytoplasmic structures connecting germ cells, are key to enabling this communication.

ICBs, also referred to as cytoplasmic bridges (CBs) or ring canals (RCs) in Drosophila melanogaster, differ significantly from other intercellular connections due to their unique structural characteristics3,4. These bridges are typically described as micron-scale bridges (usually > 0.5 μm) to permit the exchange of genetic materials, even organelles, between connected cells5,6. They are evolutionarily conserved from invertebrates to humans and are formed during cell division in both male and female gametogenesis7,8. Numerous molecules and organelles have been observed to traverse ICBs, and these structures have been correlated with normal spermatogenesis and male fertility9.

Recent years have seen a surge of interest in ICBs, driven by their potential to serve as stable conduits for short-range cell–cell communication3. Accumulating evidence suggests that ICBs play a multifaceted role in coordinating cellular activities during spermatogenesis, including facilitating the exchange of gene products post-meiosis and ensuring synchronous development of germ cells10,11. This review seeks to consolidate current understanding of ICBs’ structure and formation mechanisms, with a particular focus on mouse and Drosophila melanogaster spermatogenesis. We then discuss the potential functional roles of ICBs, emphasizing their contributions to genetic exchange and cellular coordination during spermatogenesis. We also explore the phylogenetic distribution of ICBs and their components across animal species. Finally, we outline the key challenges and future directions in elucidating the precise regulatory mechanisms and broader biological significance of ICBs in male germ cell development.

The male germ cell cyst and ICBs’ structure

Enrico Sertoli was the first to use the term “intercellular bridges” to describe the cytoplasmic connection between spermatogonia12, as translated by C. Geyer13. Von Ebner used drawings to also indicate that the germ cells had cytoplasmic bridges14. However, it took many years before this observation was more broadly recognized in reproductive biology15. The reason that protoplasmic bridges among germ cells remained a scarcely discussed topic for a long time was likely because they were generally regarded as transient structures, analogous to the spindle bridges observed during the telophase of mitotic division in many other cell types, until a pivotal breakthrough occurred in 1955, when electron microscopy revealed that ICBs in cat spermatids were stable cytoplasmic channels connecting neighboring cells, thereby challenging the transient nature hypothesis16.

ICBs form via incomplete cytokinesis during germ cell divisions. These structures are maintained between spermatogonia, primary spermatocytes, and secondary spermatocytes, and persist to connect spermatids until a late stage of maturation (Fig. 1)17. A germ cyst is a group of cells that are connected to each other via ICBs19. The spatial organization of male germ cell cysts, determined by the number of ICBs per cell and the spatial arrangement of germ cells within the cyst, serves as a record of the pattern of divisions within a cyst20,21. It has been categorized into several types: (a) Linear: in this configuration, cells form unbranched chains, with each germ cell containing no more than two ICBs. This arrangement is frequently observed in mammalian testes22,23. (b) Branched: in this arrangement, cells have a number of ICBs corresponding to the number of divisions they have undergone, forming a branched structure within branched cysts. For example, in rats20, bulls24, and Drosophila melanogaster25, germ cell cysts adopt a branched topology in which individual cells may possess up to four ICBs, corresponding to the number of mitotic divisions they have undergone. Subsequent meiotic divisions further increase the number of ICBs per cell and the total number of interconnected cells within the cyst. (c) Spherical: this type features cysts with a spherical, anuclear central mass of cytoplasm, where each cell is connected to the central cytoplasm by a single ICB22. This configuration is observed in annelids, such as earthworms22,26.

ICBs in mammals are dynamic entities that undergo a series of morphological transformations, including alterations in size and structure, throughout the process of spermatogenesis27. It is generally observed that the diameter of ICBs increases as cells advance through spermatogenesis27,28, such as in mice, the diameter of spermatogonial ICBs ranges from approximately 1 to 1.3 µm in spermatogonia. In spermatocytes, the diameter of ICBs slightly increases, reaching approximately 1.4 to 1.7 µm27. During spermiogenesis, ICBs have a diameter of approximately 3 µm, before slightly constricting to a reduced size prior to spermiation17,18. Additionally, studies demonstrated the thickness of ICBs in rats exhibits variability18. ICBs are approximately 30 nm thick prior to the completion of the first meiotic division, around 50 to 60 nm thick with somewhat uneven height in secondary spermatocyte ICBs, and about 40 nm thick in spermatid ICBs before the conclusion of spermiogenesis18.

The most prominent feature identified in all ICBs analyzed through transmission electron microscopy is the presence of an electron-dense layer, termed the bridge density, which is associated with the plasma membrane on the cytoplasmic side of the ICBs (Fig. 1B, C)18,29,30. The ICBs consist of two distinct rims: an outer rim that appears electron-dense in micrographs and an inner, thicker rim that is less electron-dense, this reduced electron density in the inner rim likely stems from its reduced concentration of structural proteins, such as actin31. This structure was initially described by Burgos and Fawcett as a “dark ring” in cat spermatids16. Generally, this “dense material” spans the entire length of the ICBs but seldom extends beneath the plasma membrane of the conjoined cells32. The exact composition and function of the “dark ring” remain unclear, though studies suggest that the dense material may primarily serve to reinforce and impart rigidity to the bridges during cellular division18.

Additionally, studies have demonstrated that the thickened cytoplasmic membrane margins of the ICBs exhibit an affinity for uranyl stain (used for nucleic acids), which has been interpreted as a condensation of RNA33. A deeper understanding of the formation, composition, and functions of the electron-dense bridge rim could provide crucial insights into the regulation of the ICBs formation process. In addition, the near-universality of male germline cysts, and their remarkable similarity across disparate taxa, mean that the cytological machinery and developmental processes required to build cysts in the germline are available in essentially all animals.

The bridge-partitioning complex (BPC) is a transient structure that regularly appears within the lumen of pre-existing ICBs during successive divisions of connected spermatogonial and spermatocyte cells in some mammalian and nonmammalian male germ cells34. It is characterized by a series of parallel cisternae traversing the bridge lumen and separated by regular layers of densely stained material. Acting as a barrier, the BPC may serve two potential yet not mutually exclusive functions. It could prevent the spread of hypothetical degenerative signals throughout a germ‑cell clone, or it might help maintain the integrity of individual germ cells by blocking the loss of nuclear material or spindle components during mitosis. However, whether BPCs additionally contribute to stabilizing ICBs as cells undergo mitotic and meiotic divisions and specifically function to inhibit the completion of cleavage remains uncertain18,34.

The formation of ICBs in the mouse and Drosophila melanogaster spermatogenesis

The molecular composition of ICBs in the male germline has been extensively studied, particularly in Drosophila melanogaster and mouse35. ICBs are composed of numerous cytokinesis proteins, which reveals an intimate relationship between cytokinesis and the formation of ICBs36. However, the mechanism underlying the formation of ICBs remains incompletely understood18,34. A recent study showed that ICBs formation is strikingly conserved in mouse and Drosophila melanogaster, and proposed a unified model of ICBs formation37. It involves four distinct stages: (1) central spindle formation and cleavage furrow ingression; (2) midbody formation; (3) midbody reorganization; and (4) nascent ICBs formation.

The formation of ring canals (RCs) in Drosophila melanogaster

During Drosophila melanogaster spermatogenesis, germline stem cells (GSCs) located at the hub of the testis undergo asymmetric division, generating one new GSC and one spermatogonial cell38. This spermatogonial cell then undergoes four rounds of mitotic division to form a cluster of 16 primary spermatocytes interconnected by RCs39. Mature spermatocytes synchronously enter meiosis, resulting in the formation of 64 spermatids that remain interconnected by RCs. Spermatids stay connected through RCs during the subsequent processes of tail elongation and individualization4.

The contractile ring and central spindle proteins are implicated in the formation of RCs40. Anillin serves as a master regulator of cytokinesis, capable of scaffolding key regulators of the contractile ring40. Anillin interacts with the cytoskeletal scaffolding protein Cindr, recruiting it to the plasma membrane, thereby contributing to the spatial organization of protein complexes associated with the contractile ring during cytokinesis40. In addition, the centralspindlin complex, a heterotetramer comprised of a dimer of the kinesin-6 motor protein MKLP1 and a dimer of a Rho-GTPase activating protein MgcRacGAP, is highly conserved and is best known for its role in crosslinking the microtubule cytoskeleton and facilitating the formation and activation of the actomyosin contractile ring41,42. The kinesin-6 Pavarotti (MKLP1 in mouse) and Tumbleweed (MgcRacGAP in mouse) represent conserved components of all stable ICBs types with known molecular compositions, suggesting their potential role in stabilizing RCs36,41. Moreover, Nessun Dorma (Nesd), an evolutionarily conserved partner of the centralspindlin complex, accumulates at the midbody ring during late cytokinesis and may function as a scaffold protein essential for RCs formation43.

Citron kinase/Sticky represents another pivotal regulator in the formation of RCs. Sticky mediates the transition from the contractile ring to the midbody ring and contributes to the formation of the midbody in GCs37,44. Citron kinase/Sticky interacts with several contractile ring proteins45,46 and central spindle components, thereby stabilizing the constricting contractile ring and retaining Anillin at the mature midbody ring. In the absence of Citron, defects in midbody organization and dynamics occur, leading to a delayed or failed transition from the midbody to RCs44.

In addition to Citron, other crucial components involved in the formation of RCs include Septins and Orbit/CLASP and Mucin-D4,40,47,48. RCs in male germline of Drosophila melanogaster are characterized by a cytoskeleton enriched with septins, specifically at least three types: Septin 1, 2, and the septin Peanut4. At the initiation of cytokinesis, septins localize to and delineate the site of the nascent cleavage furrow49. Orbit, a microtubule-binding protein, facilitates RCs formation by mediating the transport of necessary components towards the cleavage furrow47. Mucin-D, a glycoprotein localized to nascent RCs in the apical region of larval testes during primary spermatocyte division, likely contributes to the initial stabilization of contractile rings into RCs and may serve as a structural component throughout RC development, supporting growth and maturation by anchoring key proteins to the plasma membrane48.

One of the earliest signs of RCs formation is the appearance of phosphotyrosine epitopes (PTyr) at the site of the constricting cleavage furrow in dividing male germ cells, and this persists to mark the RCs walls after both mitotic and meiotic divisions38. The rings of phosphotyrosine-containing material were not static in structure during spermatogenesis; they increased in diameter as the spermatocytes expanded in volume38. Proper organization of PTyr in the RCs formation requires Src64 and four-wheel drive (fwd)50,51. Src64 is the primary tyrosine kinase essential for RCs tyrosine phosphorylation, controlling phosphorylation on and around RCs50. Additionally, SRC64 can promote the formation of an actin network around RCs, which depends on Abl tyrosine kinase and the Arp2/3 pathway to maintain the correct RC diameter in Drosophila melanogaster male germline cysts50. Mutations in Src64 result in the complete loss of PTyr staining on RCs at the testis tip50. Furthermore, PTyr accumulation also relies on fwd51. The fwd gene encodes a predicted phosphatidylinositol 4-kinase (PI4K), the enzyme responsible for the initial step in the synthesis of the crucial membrane phospholipid phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2)51. Rings of PTyr failed to accumulate even in fwd mutant dividing spermatocytes with tightly constricted contractile rings, despite successful formation of RCs during preceding mitotic divisions in the mutants51.

Different RC components display distinct spatial and temporal localization patterns40. Some RCs proteins, such as Anillin and Pavarotti, localized on all RCs4,40. By contrast, Cindr is located in mitotic RCs and then lost from RCs at the point of mitotic completion (Fig. 2)38,40. The formation of ICBs in mouse

As germ cells progress through mouse spermatogenesis, the composition of ICBs undergoes significant changes (Fig. 2). ICBs connecting spermatogonia and spermatocytes feature an outer ring comprising the centralspindlin complex (MgcRacGAP and MKLP1) and three septins (SEPT2, SEPT7, and SEPT9), excluding Anillin52. Surrounded by this outer ring is an inner ring containing Testis-expressed gene14 (TEX14), Centrosomal 55 kDa protein (CEP55), and pericentrin52,53. Heat shock factor 2 (HSF2), the only known non-cytoskeletal ICB component, colocalizes with TEX14 at ICBs; however, Hsf2-null mice remain fertile with intact ICBs, indicating that its precise role at the bridge remains to be elucidated9. As these bridges mature, the inner ring expands and eventually merges with the outer ring, with septins being progressively removed52. Additionally, Spermatogenic cell/sperm-associated keratin of 57 kDa (Sak 57), a keratin 5 homolog, forms a complex with β-tubulin within ICBs connecting late pachytene-diplotene spermatocytes, potentially serving as an intercellular ligament that reinforces bridge structural integrity54. The observed association of ARF6 with MKLP1 during spermatogenesis suggests the existence of a molecular mechanism that dissociates ARF6 from MKLP1 in ICBs after the completion of meiosis55. Additionally, Protocadherin 3 is exclusively localized at ICBs between elongating spermatids17. Other proteins associated with ICBs during mouse spermatogenesis include δ-tubulin, RBM44, CENP-E, KIAA1210, TOP2B and ZO-111,56–58. However, the specific roles these proteins play in the formation or stabilization of the ICBs remain poorly understood.

TEX14 plays a crucial role in promoting the formation of ICBs by blocking abscission59,60. During the late stages of cytokinesis in somatic cells, the centralspindlin complex binds and recruits the centrosomal protein CEP55 to the midbody. Here, CEP55 regulates midbody structure and membrane fusion to complete cytokinesis. TSG101 and ALIX are recruited to the midbody by CEP55, and their interaction is essential for somatic cell abscission. In contrast, in male germ cells, the centralspindlin complex (consisting of MKLP1 and MgcRacGAP) and CEP55 are stable components of ICBs61. They colocalize with TEX14 to form a ring-like structure. TEX14 binds to CEP55 via a conserved GPPX3Y motif, competitively inhibiting the interaction of CEP55 with the GPPX3Y motifs of ALIX and TSG101. This prevents the recruitment of ALIX and TSG101 to the midbody, thereby inhibiting cytokinesis60. Additionally, MKLP1 binds TEX14 in vitro and localizes to the midbody of germ cells, where it presumably recruits TEX14 to the midbody52. In the germ cell conditional MgcRacGAP knockout testis, germ cells, which lack this essential component of centralspindlin, do not form TEX14 ring structures, leading to the failure of ICB formation and the production of multinucleated cells61.

In the male mouse, ICBs in the germline have an actin-rich cytoskeleton, unlike the septin-rich ICBs observed in male Drosophila melanogaster4. Actin plays a critical role in maintaining these structures, particularly in the later stages of spermatogenesis62. Studies using cytochalasin D, a specific inhibitor of actin filaments, have shown that a single intratesticular injection can disrupt certain ICBs in spermatids within 3 h of administration, leading to the formation of multinucleated cells63.

Emerging evidence suggests that several specialized lipids play crucial roles in stabilizing meiotic germ cell ICBs62,64–66. The high degree of membrane curvature required for ICB stabilization necessitates lipid components with specific biophysical properties67. After crossing the blood-testis barrier, male germ cells initiate the synthesis of specialized glycosphingolipids containing ultra-long chain polyunsaturated fatty acids (ULC-PUFAs), characterized by 26–32 carbon atoms and 4–6 double bonds68. These distinctive fatty acids likely facilitate the formation of the highly convex plasma membrane that encloses ICBs in spermatids69. For example, the synthesis of ULC-PUFA complex glycosphingolipids is essential for ICB stabilization, supported by the observation that mutations in CerS3, a gene encoding an enzyme involved in ultra long-polyunsaturated sphingolipids synthesis, lead to disorganized ICBs and the subsequent death of prophase I spermatocytes, akin to the phenotype seen in Tex14 knockout mice69–71. Notably, TEX14 continues to localize to disorganized ICBs in CerS3 knockout spermatocytes, indicating that lipid rearrangement is critical for ICB stability69. Furthermore, sulfo-galactosylglycerolipid (SGG), an organized lipid and lipid raft component, is vital for ICB membrane integrity72,73. In the absence of SGG, ICBs exhibit structural abnormalities, leading to multinucleated primary spermatocytes and meiotic division arrest72. Furthermore, syntaxin2 (STX2) colocalizes with SGG, and the STX2-mediated intracellular transport and localization mechanism for SGG is implicated in ICB maintenance. In Stx2 mutant germ cells, STX2 deficiency disrupted ICBs and produced multinucleated spermatocytes. TEX14 was found on the multinucleated giant cell membrane in an aberrant patchy pattern62,74,75.

Mitotic cells have been shown to accumulate classical sphingolipids with long to very long acyl chains at ICBs, whereas male germ cells accumulate complex sphingolipids containing very long/ultra-long chain polyunsaturated fatty acids (VLC-PUFAs)64,65,69. These differences in sphingolipid composition, with distinct biophysical properties, suggest differing underlying mechanisms between the ICB compositions or formation of mitotic and male meiotic cells67. Furthermore, scaffolding molecules and central spindle components have been observed to localize at ICBs. The precise coordination between these lipid molecules and other essential proteins in the dynamic regulation of ICBs remains poorly understood. Thus, it is important in future studies to tease out, using in vivo models as well as in vitro systems, how lipids coordinate with other essential proteins to dynamically regulate ICBs.

The functions of male germline ICBs

The phylogenetic conservation of ICBs in mammalian germ cells suggests the preservation of a structured entity that plays a fundamental role in germ cell development. Although the structure and protein composition of ICBs have been relatively well-studied, their physiological functions remain incompletely understood. Perhaps the most directly demonstrated function of ICBs is the intercellular sharing of cytoplasmic contents between connected germ cells. In Drosophila, live imaging with photoactivatable GFP (PA-GFP) has demonstrated that cytoplasmic proteins move through RCs at all stages of spermatogenesis, from diploid spermatogonia to post-meiotic haploid spermatids, although this sharing is selective rather than indiscriminate4. Earlier work in Drosophila oogenesis had established that the fusome, a germline-specific organelle extending through RCs, is essential for coordinating cytoplasmic sharing among germ cells within a cyst76. Similarly, in rat spermatids, organelle traffic through ICBs has been directly visualized, including the intercellular transfer of Golgi-derived vesicles and the chromatoid body27. Protein equilibration through RCs has also been demonstrated in Drosophila somatic follicle cells77. This direct cytoplasmic sharing likely underpins the downstream functions discussed below.

Chromosome dosage compensation in haploid cells after meiosis

Due to the two meiotic divisions in testicular germ cells, haploid spermatids contain either an X or Y chromosome. It is highly probable that the products of sex chromosomes are effectively transported between spermatids, rendering them phenotypically diploid and ensuring the synchronous development of post-meiotic haploid germ cells27,78.

Studies indicate that proteins such as A-kinase anchoring proteins (AKAPs), which are encoded by X-linked genes, are crucial components of all spermatozoa and are essential for sperm motility and fertility in the mouse79. This necessitates the transport of either proteins or mRNAs between spermatids bearing the X and Y chromosomes79. Moreover, in Drosophila melanogaster, active biosynthesis of crucial mRNAs and proteins occurs during the 3-day growth phase of primary spermatocytes prior to meiosis. A subset of genes, termed “cup” and “comet” based on the localization of mRNAs at one end of spermatid bundles, is transcribed only after meiosis during spermatid tail elongation4. The timing of “cup” and “comet” gene expression suggests their involvement in spermatid development. Mutations in one comet gene, scotti, which is involved in spermatid individualization, result in male sterility4. However, heterozygous scotti mutants remain fertile, implying that products generated by haploid spermatids with the wild-type allele are likely transferred to spermatids with the mutant allele4.

Mechanistically, several studies have demonstrated that many male germ cell mRNAs, such as Prm1 and AKAP4 transcripts, can be transported between connected cells by a DNA- and RNA-binding protein called testis-brain-RNA-binding protein (TB-RBP)79–81. Additionally, the chromatoid body, an mRNA-containing organelle found in haploid spermatids, has been shown to move across male germline ICBs in a microtubule-dependent manner (Fig. 3)10,27. Although the significance of these movements remains unclear, it is hypothesized that these parallel movements over the haploid nucleus may be required to collect gene products from various chromosome territories within the haploid nucleus.

A critical question remains: How do spermatids first recognize and then transport RNA/proteins through ICBs? Given that other post-meiotically transcribed, X-linked genes have been identified, it is plausible that a common mechanism for this movement exists. While simple diffusion is a potential explanation, it may not guarantee the transport of sufficient RNA/protein to generate functional spermatids79. Moreover, certain transcripts, such as the t-complex responder mRNA, might not be shared between spermatids79. There is also compelling evidence for functionally distinct sperm within a male, leading to transmission ratio distortion (TRD), as seen in the mouse with different alleles at the t-complex82. The TRD observed with t-haplotypes has been attributed to unequal sharing of post-meiotic products, although no direct evidence supports this mechanism. Additionally, gene product sharing may not be universal for all spermatid-expressed genes, particularly those encoding membrane proteins10,83.

It is noteworthy that mutations in germline cells differ from those in somatic cells, as germline mutations can be passed on to the next generation, potentially affecting the fitness of the entire population. In males, spermatogenic cysts persist through and beyond meiosis, allowing for gene product sharing between haploid spermatocytes. This compensatory mechanism may offset the absence of hemizygous genes carried on the sex chromosomes in species where males are the heterogametic sex. More broadly, sharing could buffer against stochastic variations in gene expression and possibly protect against the generation of sperm carrying selfish genes that lead to meiotic drive or segregation distortion84,85.

Maintaining synchronous development within cysts of germ cells

Most early evidence for the involvement of ICBs in maintaining synchrony comes from observations in the mouse, where dividing chains of undifferentiated spermatogonia frequently show that all cells are at the same stage of mitosis86,87. Alternatively, successive mitotic stages may appear sequentially from one end of the chain to the other88. Additionally, germ cells linked by ICBs exhibit identical morphological features, further supporting the role of ICBs in synchronizing cell division and differentiation (Fig. 3)88.

Germ cells often develop in synchrony, undergoing mitosis and entering meiosis in a coordinated manner, which may be enabled by the intercellular sharing of signals via ICBs. Recent research evidence supports this hypothesis in spermatogonial cells89. Studies show that in the absence of ICBs in Tex14−/− mouse mutants, the synchrony of spermatogonial cell differentiation is impaired, with some cells exhibiting delayed differentiation compared to their behavior in a normal seminiferous epithelium cycle89. Additional evidence comes from species such as Hutchinsoniella90, Dicyema typus91, and Balanophyllia europaea92, where spermiogenic cells develop without ICBs, resulting in unsynchronized spermiogenesis91. Interestingly, a recent study in fetal mouse ovaries, cytoplasmic sharing via ICBs is also required for the coordinated transition to meiosis within cysts7.

However, synchronization cannot fully explain the functions of ICBs/RCs, and there are limitations to the evidence mentioned above. For example, the fundamental significance of spermatogenic cell synchronization remains unclear. Furthermore, although ICBs/RCs can transport cytoplasmic products, this provides only a partial explanation for the synchronization of differentiation. These limitations highlight the need for further investigation to understand how ICBs/RCs contribute to germ cell development across different species.

In addition, the presence or absence of ICBs/RCs exhibit significantly different response patterns to cell death across different species93,94. Research in Drosophila has revealed that even if only a subset of cells within a spermatogonial cluster exhibit detectable DNA damage, the entire cluster still undergoes synchronized cell death as a whole; however, mutations that disrupt cyst structure compromise synchronous spermatogonial death and reduce overall germ cell death. These findings suggest that cellular connectivity is a mechanism conferring heightened sensitivity to DNA damage94. In contrast, studies on fetal germ cells in mice have demonstrated a different pattern, the frequency of primordial germ cell (PGC) apoptosis in Tex14−/− mice which lack cytoplasmic sharing showed no difference compared to their wild-type littermates. This indicates that shared intrinsic cellular properties, rather than intercellular connectivity, act as the primary determinant of apoptosis in this context93. These differing outcomes may stem from differences in the protein composition of ICBs across species. Therefore, when elucidating the core functions of ICBs, species-specific physiological contexts and evolutionary backgrounds must be taken into consideration.

Evolution of the male germ cell ICBs/RCs and their components

An examination of the eukaryotic tree of life reveals that ICBs are remarkably widespread in animals in spermatogenesis (Fig. 4)95. The preponderance of evidence suggests that ICBs/RCs have a long evolutionary history in animal spermatogenesis, maintaining a consistent form throughout 700 million years of metazoan evolution (Fig. 4A). They recur as a trait in numerous metazoan lineages, including Porifera96,97, Coelenterata98, Platyhelminthes99,100, Nematoda101–103, Tardigrada104,105, Arthropoda40,106, Mollusca107–109, Annelida19,110, Sipuncula111,112, Echinodermata113, and Vertebrata114. The widespread occurrence of germ cell ICBs implies that they play a crucial role in male germ cell development. However, not all male germ cell development universally requires ICBs. In certain instances, the products of each division are distinctly separated, and no ICBs are formed, as observed in species such as Balanophyllia europaea92, Dicyema typus91, Macrobrachium rosenbergii115, Hutchinsoniella90. Notably, even in some vertebrates such as the blue fox, ICBs appear to be rare, as mitotic divisions during spermatogenesis generally result in complete cytokinesis116, although this remains to be definitively established. This suggests that ICBs/RCs are a plastic trait that has been lost and regained in various branches of the animal tree. This raises an interesting question: how does this observation challenge the long-held assumption that ICBs/RCs are necessary for X/Y dosage compensation? If ICBs/RCs are absent in these species, how do they bypass what was thought to be an “essential” requirement? We selected TEX14, CEP55, MKLP1, MgcRacGAP as core components of the ICB formation and then identified their orthologs based on sequence similarity and domain scan in 15 representative animals’ genomes (Fig. 4B). The phylogenetic tree depicts evolutionary relationships among the included species. Among the selected core components of ICB, MKLP1 is the most conserved component present in all animals98,118. MgcRacGAP can be traced to earlier porifera stages but is absent from Fasciola hepatica (Platyhelminthes). TEX14 and CEP55 can be traced to vertebrata stages, implying that vertebrates have evolved new molecules to participate in ICB formation.

Conclusions and future perspectives

Germline ICBs interconnecting cells during gametogenesis have fascinated researchers for many years. It is widely assumed that ICBs provide open channels of communication between germ cells, and such communication is required for the maturation of male germ cells. However, the molecular control of their formation remains elusive, and much is yet to be discovered about ICBs functions. We highlight several key avenues for future investigation below.

Little is known about how and why ICBs are formed. Recent studies have established that ICBs are derived from the midbody during incomplete cytokinesis37, yet the molecular mechanisms governing this transformation, including the subsequent transport of organelles, proteins, and RNAs through the resulting channels, remain poorly understood. Some insights may emerge from studying viral RNA transfer and in vitro organelle/protein transport6. Since cytokinesis is the prerequisite precursor to ICBs formation, it is often difficult to distinguish whether the proteins identified at ICBs actively regulate their differentiation or simply establish the necessary cleavage furrow architecture. The challenge of uncoupling essential cytokinetic machinery from specific ICBs/RCs differentiation without causing lethality represents a major obstacle in the field. Moreover, the discussion regarding mechanisms of abscission inhibition in germ cells focuses almost exclusively on Tex14. A study showed that down-regulation of Usp8 in Drosophila egg chambers leads to complete abscission in cystocytes, resulting in germline cysts with fewer than 16 cells, whereas overexpression of Usp8 in cells that normally undergo complete cytokinesis blocks abscission and causes ectopic cyst formation119. Whether additional proteins or mechanisms in the male reproductive system contribute to this process warrants further investigation.

Several methodological advances offer new opportunities to address these questions. Researchers have leveraged video imaging systems, now widely employed to investigate the key mechanisms underlying ICBs formation37,120,121. A recent study reported an ex vivo method for live imaging of male meiotic cytokinesis in intact adult testes using gas-permeable membrane chambers, which could facilitate exploration of RC formation and characteristics in germ cells122. Furthermore, cryo-electron tomography (cryo-ET) has recently been used in plants to reconstruct the three-dimensional ultrastructure of plasmodesmata, a functionally analogous intercellular channel123,124. Applying correlative light microscopy or cryo-ET to examine ICBs at high resolution could prove invaluable for elucidating their detailed architecture and biogenesis.

Although numerous studies have explored the possible roles of ICBs during spermatogenesis85, comprehensive functional models remain limited, primarily due to the scarcity of genetic tools available to specifically disrupt ICBs formation. Several outstanding questions merit attention: (1) The prevailing explanation for ICBs function is that they permit synchronous germ cell development, yet it remains unclear why spermatogenic cells cannot mature independently84, and why ICBs have been preserved through evolution. (2) Given their midbody origin, do ICBs also retain certain midbody functions, such as serving as sites for spatiotemporally regulated translation?125 (3) Recent studies have shown that ICBs protect germline genome integrity during spermatogenesis through transposon repression126; understanding how ICBs facilitate this suppression will be crucial for defining their broader functional roles. (4) With the continuous advancement of cutting-edge technologies, new tools integrating multi-omics information offer promising approaches to infer ICBs function127. Investigating ICBs across both spatial and temporal dimensions will be essential for gaining deeper insights. To make further progress, tools that enable acute and targeted protein depletion offer a promising avenue for disrupting ICBs function in vivo. Notably, several such systems have already been established in Drosophila, including the deGradFP system, which harnesses the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway to achieve direct depletion of GFP-tagged proteins128, and the auxin-inducible degron (AID) system, which permits rapid, reversible, and spatiotemporally controlled degradation of target proteins129. Given the extensive genetic toolkit available in Drosophila, including GAL4/UAS-driven spatial control and the large collections of endogenously tagged protein lines, this organism represents a particularly powerful system for dissecting ICBs function through targeted disruption of their molecular components during spermatogenesis.

Finally, it is important to recognize that our current knowledge of ICB formation and function is limited to a few species that serendipitously became model systems. However, eukaryotic tree surveys have revealed closely related lineages to animals that may also possess ICBs (Fig. 4A). Given that these lineages could provide additional insights into the origins and functions of ICBs, efforts to characterize them should continue.
