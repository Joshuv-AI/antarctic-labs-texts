Vaccine side effects after vaccination against COVID-19 in employees at an University Hospital in Austria
Authors: Christine Maria Schwarz-Martelanz, Magdalena Hoffmann, Andreas Wedrich, Andrea Berghold, Eva Reininghaus, Sabine Reinisch, Andrea Borenich, Gerald Sendlhofer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe objective of this study was to investigate symptoms after vaccination with different COVID-19 vaccines reported from employees in an Austrian University hospital. A paper-based survey was conducted among adult employees of an Austrian University Hospital after receiving a first, second or booster dose of a COVID-19 vaccine during January 2021 and January 2022 to identify any side effects reported by participants. The survey further inquired about the participants’ demographic information, sick leave or hospitalisation due to the symptoms. Out of the approximately 5000 persons who have been vaccinated, 1093 participants completed the survey. A total of 818 of 1029 employees (79%) reported symptoms after the first, 613 of 1007 employees (61%) reported symptoms after the second and a total of 814 of 974 employees (84%) reported symptoms after booster vaccination. There were differences in the type of vaccination in relation to and number of different symptoms, sick leave as well as anxiety. In conclusion, according to the COVID-19 vaccine type, different side effects might occur following the first, second and booster doses of vaccination. Background

Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus—type 2 (SARS-CoV-2) is the causative agent of the infectious disease Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) and a strain of the coronaviridae, which causes several symptoms that are predominantly respiratory in nature1. There are numerous COVID-19 vaccines available and administered worldwide2.

As with most vaccinations, vaccination reactions and side effects can occur after the vaccination. These side effects and reactions usually occur shortly after vaccination and last for a few days. Numerous side effects and reactions are triggered by COVID-19 vaccines (e.g. swelling, redness, pain, fatigue, diarrhea, nausea, headache, shivering, and fever)3,4. Most of these side effects usually diminish in a few days and serious side effects after vaccinations are generally rare. In these severe cases, a strong inflammation of the respiratory tract and other organs occurs. Thrombotic events and anaphylaxis were reported5–7. People are often hesitant to take COVID-19 vaccines due to uncertainty about side effects and reactions after vaccination8.

The vaccine Corminaty has been developed and manufactured by BioNTech and Pfizer. It is a nucleoside-modified mRNA vaccine that encodes for the viral spike glycoprotein of SARS-CoV-29. The vaccine demonstrated local and systemic reactions in adults. The most common side effect reported was pain at the injection site. The most common systemic events were fatigue, chills, muscle pain, headaches and joint pain. Some patients have also reported experiencing a fever after receiving the first and second doses of the vaccine. However, this typically resolved within a day10,11. A multicenter cross-sectional study by Haider et al. showed that after the first dose, pain at the injection site and fever were the most frequently reported side effects, occurring in 70.4% and 70.0% of participants, respectively12.

The vaccine Spikevax (Moderna) is an mRNA-based vaccine developed by the National Institutes of Health and Moderna. The most common side effects were pain at the injection site and tiredness, headache, muscle ache, joint ache and chills. Swollen lymph nodes in the arm where the vaccine was injected were also reported, but these generally resolved within a day or two. Nausea, vomiting and fever were also reported13,14.

The Janssen Pharmaceuticals Company of Johnson & Johnson developed the vaccine Ad26.COV2.S (adenovirus serotype 26) that expresses full-length spike glycoprotein of SARS-CoV-2 in host cells after vaccination15. The most common reported local reaction was injection site pain and the most frequent systemic reactions were headache, fatigue, myalgia, and nausea16.

Vaxzeria, is a non-replicating viral vector vaccine developed by the University of Oxford and the pharmaceutical company AstraZeneca. Both local and systemic reactions were reported by participants after vaccination. The most common local reaction was pain at the injection site. Other systemic reactions included fever, muscle aches, headaches, chills, fatigue, malaise and nausea. These reactions were primarily mild and occurred 4–5 days after vaccination13. These reactions were more prevalent in younger adults than in older adults (≥ 56 years)17. In an international, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase 3 trial (included 19,630 SARS-CoV2-negative adult participants (18–59 years of age and 60 years of age or older) who received the vaccine) injection site pain was the most common local reaction, occurring in 48.6% of the participants; the most common systemic reactions were headache (38.9%), fatigue (38.2%), myalgia (33.2%), and nausea (14.2%)15. The vaccine was paused in several European countries due to reports of thromboembolic events in vaccinated individuals18.

Healthcare workers can experience a range of COVID-19 symptoms, similar to the general population, but with some unique considerations. Healthcare workers have been prioritized for vaccination at the beginning of the pandemic for two main reasons. Firstly, they are at high risk of infection. Secondly, significant infection transmission among healthcare workers and their subsequent absence from work could lead to a shortage of qualified staff.

The purpose of the following survey was to determine retrospectively how many healthcare employees developed symptoms in response to each vaccine, how long the symptoms lasted and whether the employees had been on sick leave or had been hospitalized due to the symptoms.

Materials and methods

Reporting

The research and reporting methodology followed the Checklist for Reporting Of Survey Studies (CROSS Checklist), recommended by the EQUATOR network (Checklist see Supplemental material 1).

Study design and study setting

In January 2021, the Quality and Risk Department of the University Hospital in Graz was instructed to offer a vaccination to employees and, subsequently, high-risk patients. Four different vaccines against COVID-19 were administered in the University Hospital in 2021 and 2022 (during this present study), the vector-based vaccine Vaxzevria (Oxford/AstraZeneca), the mRNA vaccine Comirnaty from BioNTech/Pfizer and the mRNA vaccine Spikevax (Moderna). In addition, in this present study, one person was vaccinated with the vector-based vaccine from Johnson & Johnson (Janssen-Cilag Pharma, Germany). All vaccines were also administered to individuals who may have already been infected with the SARS-CoV-2. For a complete immunization two repeated doses of the vaccine were required and a booster vaccination 4–6 months after the last vaccination19.

The study was conducted among employees at the University Hospital of Graz and the Medical University of Graz. The paper-based survey was handed out directly after the third (booster) vaccination. The survey was designed to identify side effects reported after receiving a COVID-19 vaccination (first, second and booster vaccination). A box for anonymous survey collection was provided and participants also had the opportunity to return the survey internally by post. Participation was on a voluntary basis. Each potential participant was informed that collected data was going to be stored at the Department of Quality and Risk Management and that the Medical University of Graz will perform the data analysis.

Study participants

Employees at the University Hospital of Graz and the Medical University of Graz from all nationalities, sex, age, and organizational units who had at least received one booster vaccination and who were willing to participate were included in the survey. Only individuals for whom the date/vaccine of the booster was known were included.

Survey

The survey was implemented in the program EvaSys© and underwent pretesting for comprehensibility. Employees were invited to participate in the paper-based survey. On the first page of the survey, an introduction about the aim of the study was given. Demographic information such as age, sex, organizational unit was then queried as well as when the first, second and booster vaccination was administered. The type of vaccine was also requested. Then, employees were asked about possible symptoms and side effects that had occurred in connection with the vaccinations. The side effects were classified into local and systemic side effects. The local side effects e.g. included injection site reactions like pain at the site of vaccination, redness, swelling/lymph node enlargement, and itch. The systemic side effects included e.g. fever, headache, and muscle pain. In addition, a free text field was added in which the participants had the opportunity to enter other experienced symptoms. Moreover, it was asked how long the symptoms did last (1 day, 2 days, 3 or more days) and whether the employees had been on sick leave or had been hospitalized due to the symptoms. Finally, the participants were asked how afraid they were of the vaccinations (Likert scale 1 (no anxiety)—10 (very great anxiety)) (Supplemental material 1).

Statistical analysis

Demographic data and symptoms experienced after COVID-19 vaccination are presented as absolute numbers and percentages. The number of symptoms experienced after COVID-19 vaccination and anxiety are reported as median (IQR). Comparisons between groups were made using Pearson’s Chi-squared test, Kruskal–Wallis test, and Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Univariable and multivariable logistic regressions were performed to identify risk factors for symptoms. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were done using R version 4.3.3.

Ethical considerations

The ethics committee of the Medical University of Graz approved the study (34-326ex 21/22). All participants provided written informed consent before taking part in the study. All methods are carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Results

Demographic data

Out of the approximately 5000 persons who have been vaccinated at the above-described setting, 1093 participants completed the survey. Of these, 1043 employees provided information on the received booster vaccination (at least date or vaccine) and were therefore included for analysis. Of these, 204 (20%) were up to 34 years, 231 (22%) 35–44 years old, 353 (34%) were 45–54 years old, 246 (24%) 55 years or older. Of all participants 235 (24%) were male and 753 (76%) were female. Self-reported symptoms after the first vaccination

A total of 1043 people received the first vaccine. 13 of these did not specify a vaccine. 726 (70%) received the vaccine Vaxzeria from AstraZeneca, 233 (23%) received the mRNA vaccine Corminaty from Pfizer-BioNTech and 70 (7%) the mRNA vaccine Spikevax from Moderna. There was only one person who was vaccinated with Janssen-Cilag/Johnson & Johnson (Janssen). The following analysis of the first vaccination excludes the vaccine with Janssen-Cilag/Johnson & Johnson (Janssen) and unspecified vaccine and therefore data of 1029 vaccines are included.

A total of 818 employees (79%) retrospectively reported symptoms after the first vaccination. The detailed list of self-reported symptoms after the vaccinations divided into the respective vaccines can be found in Fig. 1 and Supplement Fig. 1 and Supplemental material 1. The median (IQR) number of symptoms reported after vaccination was 4 (1.0–7.0). The median number of symptoms reported after vaccination with Vaxzeria was 5.0 (2.0; 7.0); with Corminaty 1.0 (0.0, 2.0) and with Spikevax 2.0 (0.0, 4.0). The number of reported symptoms by sex after vaccination is presented in Supplement Fig. 2.

Symptoms after first, second and booster vaccination (%).

After first vaccination 284 of 1029 employees (28%) were on sick leave. Most employees that were on sick leave were vaccinated with Vaxzeria (Vaxzeria: 276 (38%), Spikevax: 4 (5.7%), Comirnaty: 4 (1.7%)). Only two (0.2%) people had to be hospitalized after the first vaccination. Overall anxiety for the first vaccination was quite low 1 (1, 3) [0 (no anxiety) to 10 (very great anxiety)]. There were differences in the type of vaccination in relation to different symptoms, number of symptoms, sick leave as well as anxiety (see Supplemental material 1).

Logistic regression was used to analyse the presence of symptoms after the first vaccination, with vaccine, age and sex as the independent variables. Vaxzeria recipients had a higher likelihood of reporting symptoms compared to Comirnaty recipients (OR 5.38 (3.83, 7.59), p < 0.001). Spikevax recipients showed a trend towards a higher likelihood of reporting symptoms compared to Comirnaty recipients (OR 1.64 (0.94, 2.94), p = 0.087). Moreover, there was a lower percentage of employees 55 years or older that reported vaccine symptoms (irrelevant of the type of vaccine) than employees of the youngest age group (up to 34 years) (OR 0.45 (0.28, 0.70), p < 0.001). More women reported symptoms after the first vaccination than men [615 (83%) vs. 164 (71%); OR 1.93, (1.37, 2.71), p < 0.001] (see Table 2).

Self-reported symptoms after the second vaccination

A total of 1043 employees received a second vaccine, 36 of them did not specify a vaccine. Therefore, 1007 were included in the analysis. A total of 613 employees (61%) reported symptoms after the second vaccination. The median number of symptoms after the second vaccination was lower than for the first vaccination [1 (0–4)]. The detailed list of self-reported symptoms per vaccine can be found in the following Fig. 1 and Supplemental material 1.

After second vaccination 96 of 1007 employees (9.5%) were on sick leave. Most people vaccinated with Spikevax were on sick leave (Comirnaty: 22 (9.7%), Spikevax: 15 (21%), Vaxzeria: 59 (8.3%)). No vaccine recipient had to be hospitalized after the second vaccination. As with the first vaccination, general anxiety about the second vaccination was low [1 (1, 2)] (0 (no anxiety) to 10 (very great anxiety)]. There were differences between the type of vaccination and the various symptoms, the number of symptoms, sick leave, and anxiety (see Supplemental material 1).

More symptoms after second vaccination were reported by women than by men [491 (65%) vs. 109 (46%); OR 2.17, (1.61, 2.92), p < 0.001]. Individuals who had symptoms after the first vaccination also had symptoms after the second vaccination [555 (67.2%) vs. 271 (32.8%); OR 3.88, (2.84, 5.33), p < 0.001] (see Table 3).

Discussion

Reactogenicity to vaccines represents various local and systemic manifestations due to the inflammatory response to vaccination. It depends on various factors such as the characteristics of the recipient (age, sex, etc.), the type of vaccine, the composition, the route of administration and many other factors20. It is therefore likely that most people would have a vaccine reaction after COVID-19 vaccination. The reported symptoms were overall consistent with the vaccine product information. This present survey study aims to provide an insight into the actual side effects experienced by recipients of the COVID-19 vaccine in a university hospital in Austria.

In total, more than 1000 employees completed the survey. The results show that around 79% of the study participants experienced some side effects due to the first COVID-19 vaccination. The number of symptoms after the second vaccination was slightly lower. This could also be related to the vaccinations offered during this period. Of the employees, 61% reported symptoms after the second vaccination. Similar to our findings, Kaur et al. found that side effects are more common after the first vaccination compared to the second vaccination3. The percentage of reported symptoms was higher after booster vaccination than after the second vaccination. Most employees who had been vaccinated with Vaxzeria (first vaccine) and Spikevax (second vaccination) reported vaccine-related symptoms (87%). Most frequently reported symptoms were injection site pain, fatigue, and headache. This can be explained because, in most cases, the immune system has already been exposed to the viral antigen, either through vaccination or infection. Consequently, stronger reactions to the vaccination are possible. Similar findings were reported in other studies; most common side effects after vaccination were pain, swelling at the vaccination site, fatigue, muscle and joint pain, lethargy, dizziness, fever and headaches21,22. Symptoms can be explained because the immune system produces inflammatory mediators, such as cytokines, after vaccination, which have inflammatory effects on the body’s organs. For this reason the side effects of the coronavirus vaccination can persist for days after the vaccination23. Menni et al. showed that most vaccine side effects start during the first 24 h following vaccination and persist for 1–2 days24. Interestingly, more systemic side effects to the vaccine were observed after first vaccination with viral vector-based vaccines (e.g. Vaxzeria) than mRNA based vaccines (e.g. Corminaty)25. Vector vaccines carry both the antigen and the viral vector and can therefore both elicit an immune response. This may explain the higher reactogenicity of vector vaccines26. In addition, the high prevalence of adenovirus diseases in the population and the associated pre-existing immune mechanisms could also play a role27.

Moreover, it is crucial to evaluate the consequences of post vaccine myocarditis after administration of mRNA vaccines, particularly in young people. Studies have shown that the prognosis at hospital discharge after post vaccine myocarditis was generally favorable with short hospital stays and seldom cases of death22,23. The mechanisms producing myocardial injury after administration of a COVID-19 mRNA vaccine are not well understood, with various hypotheses such as an altered gene expression, direct immune activation by mRNA, molecular mimicry, immune dysregulation, or aberrant cytokine expression. These elements should all be taken into account for ongoing and future mRNA vaccine recommendations30.

Another important factor is the effect of previous SARS-CoV-2 infections on vaccine side effects. Previous infections could significantly influence the type and frequency of reported symptoms. We have also analysed if there is a trend that people, who had no symptoms after the first vaccination also had no symptoms after the second and third vaccination. Of the individuals who did not show any symptoms after the first vaccination, more than half remained symptom-free after the second vaccination. In contrast, nearly 90% of individuals who had symptoms after the first vaccination also had symptoms after the booster vaccination.

Studies have shown that vaccine recipients with a history of COVID-19 infection reported more adverse reactions than those without a history of COVID-19. The severity of COVID-19 infection was also associated with the frequency of adverse reactions31. It is thought that the increased reactogenicity is directly related to increased immunogenicity, as people with previous infections have higher COVID-19 antibody titres than those who have not been previously infected24.

Reactogenicity of coronavirus disease vaccines can result in inability to work. Sick leave after COVID-19 vaccination is frequent and associated with the vaccine applied. Our results showed that 284 of 1029 employees (28%) were on sick leave after the first vaccination. The sick leave rate was lower (9.5%) after second vaccination and was again higher after booster vaccination. Our study showed that more employees were on sick leave after the first vaccination with Vaxzeria than with Corminaty or Moderna. Other studies have also shown similar results. Especially after administration of Vaxzeria, symptoms were common and also age dependent: 84.3% of 18–29 year olds were temporarily unable to work, 50.3% for two or more days even32. A study of Bauernfeind et al. also showed high rates of sick leave after first/second vaccination. Sick leave occurred in 239 (10.4%) and 539 (23.3%) participants after the first/ second vaccination. Older age groups aged 50 years and above took significantly less sick leave after any COVID-19 vaccination compared with those aged < 30 years33. This can also be explained by the fact that the immune system no longer reacts as strongly to the respective vaccine components with increasing age. Age and sex might affect the reactogenicity to COVID-19 vaccination.

The presence of symptoms after vaccination (regardless of the vaccine) was higher in women than in men in our present survey study (for the first, second and booster vaccination). Other studies have also observed similar results. In a study of Nachtigall et al. females reported more side effects compared to males in the cohort34. In general, females tend to have an enhanced immune response compared to males because of a more intense cellular and humoral immune response to vaccinations35,36. These effects should be considered when interpreting the results of similar studies investigating reactogenicity after COVID-19 vaccination.

Furthermore, large staff shortages after the respective vaccinations in acute care were a major problem. In any case, the rate of potential adverse events and high rates of sick leave following immunization must be considered when planning an immunization campaign. A strategic focus is essential here for the future.

Interestingly, our results show that the fear of vaccination was low. Another study of Badami et al. showed that non-vaccinated healthcare professionals had severe anxiety, whereas, in vaccinated participants, anxiety levels were low. As the immunizations were implemented, healthcare workers felt more secure than before, however, the vaccine did not entirely remove anxiety37. The large number of symptoms reported in the free text field of the survey also showed that the employees showed great interest and provided detailed feedback on how they felt after the vaccinations.

To sum up, vaccines play a vital role in preventing deaths and hospitalizations caused by infectious diseases. They help to control the spread of disease and significantly impact infection and serious illness. Vaccination has proven to be an essential tool in reducing the transmission of SARS-CoV-2, as well as severe illness and mortality related to the virus. Vaccines are generally safe to administer, with serious side effects occurring only in very rare cases. The possible consequences of a SARS-CoV-2 infection far outweigh these side effects.

Limitations

The first limitation of this study is that it was a single center using convenience sampling, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Second, the study population consisted mainly of women. In Austria, most health services and social care workers are women. Therefore, the sex distribution reflects the population under study. The side effect profile was based on self-reported data, which introduces the potential for recall bias and subjective interpretation. Participants may not accurately recall or may perceive side effects differently. It should be noted that the survey was conducted after the third vaccination, which may have resulted in some recall bias. It is also possible that the survey was mostly completed by people who have experienced symptoms. Participants were not asked in advance whether they already had a confirmed infection or not. This is a potential source of bias. One or more previous COVID-19 infections could have an influence on severity and type of symptoms. Another bias is the non-response bias. The participants might be unwilling or unable to respond to a survey question or the entire survey. Perhaps participants do not want to answer the question about fear of vaccination or do not want to describe all the symptoms after the vaccinations. The study did not evaluate the severity of side effects, limiting our understanding of the intensity and impact of symptoms. Assessing the severity could provide a more comprehensive picture of the overall safety profile of the vaccines. In addition, the vaccine’s latent effects were not investigated or included in this study.

Conclusions

The type of COVID-19 vaccine administered appears to influence the occurrence and nature of side effects following vaccination. Our study indicates that the side effects associated with COVID-19 vaccines were common. Notably, after the initial vaccination, vaccination resulted in a higher incidence of side effects, while the second dose elicited more side effects. Furthermore, side effects were reported more frequently after the second dose when an mRNA vaccine was administered after a vector-based vaccine. Significant more women reported symptoms after vaccination than men. Large staff shortages were a significant issue following vaccinations in acute care settings, particularly given current staffing deficits. It is crucial to consider the rate of potential adverse events, such as sick leave, when planning immunization campaigns. These findings contribute valuable insights to address the persistent challenges of vaccination hesitancy, which stem from widespread concerns about the safety profile of COVID-19 vaccines.
