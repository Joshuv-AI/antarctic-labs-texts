Investigating the relationship between asthma symptoms and severity with television watching, computer use, and internet access among 13-14-year-old children in Yazd, Iran
Authors: Nasrin Behniafard, Zahra Nafei, Foad Forghani, Masoud Faisal, Mehran Karimi, Marzie Vaghefi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAsthma is a prevalent chronic respiratory disease that affects individuals from childhood to adulthood. This study aimed to investigate the association between symptoms and severity of asthma with television viewing, computer and Internet use in adolescents, considering the lifestyle changes in recent decades.MethodsThis study was conducted in early 2020 as part of the Global Asthma Network 2020 survey, among 13–14-year-olds in Yazd, Iran. Participants were classified into case and control groups based on the presence and severity of asthma symptoms. The data was collected and analyzed using SPSS-20 software to compare the participants based on their screen time.ResultsThe analysis of data collected from a total of 5141 students produced noteworthy results. A significant correlation was identified between the occurrence of wheezing in the past year, the presence of severe asthma, and the amount of time spent watching television, using a computer, or internet. The unadjusted model revealed that screen time of more than three hours, pet ownership, current smoking, and ever smoking were all significant predictors of wheezing and severe asthma. Smoking was the strongest predictor of asthma and its severity. After adjusting for confounding factors, the multiple logistic regression model confirmed that screen time of more than three hours remained a significant predictor of both asthma and its severity.ConclusionThis study finds a significant association between media usage and asthma symptoms and severity. However, this association is influenced by lifestyle factors such as physical activity, obesity, diet, and exposure to indoor allergens. Further research is needed to better understand the complex interplay between media usage and asthma, considering the impact of these factors.Keywords: Asthma, Computer, Internet, Television, Children

Background

Asthma is the most prevalent chronic respiratory condition, affecting individuals from childhood through adulthood [1]. According to a 2023 report by the Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA), the prevalence of asthma is steadily increasing in multiple countries. This upward trend has instigated a multitude of epidemiological investigations aimed at elucidating the fundamental factors underlying the surge in asthma incidence [2, 3].

Furthermore, based on Global Asthma Network (GAN) 2020, the mean global prevalence of wheezing in the past 12 months in adolescents (13–14 years) and children (6–7 years) was reported to be approximately 13% and 11%, respectively [4]. Asthma is a heterogeneous chronic disease that results from complex interactions between genetic and environmental factors [5]. Many environmental factors have been identified to be involved in the onset or exacerbation of asthma, such as exposure to allergens, irritants, infections, and antibiotics [6, 7].

The recent dramatic increase in asthma incidence has coincided with a dramatic increase in media use over the past decade, suggesting the need to investigate this relationship in society. The widespread availability and appeal of media, particularly among children and adolescents, has led to increased sedentary behavior, which is associated with negative health outcomes, such as obesity, stress, anxiety, sleep disturbances, reduced outdoor activity, and sunlight exposure [8–12]. Evidence from various studies strongly suggests that adolescents who engage in sedentary behaviors are more likely to experience a higher prevalence of asthma symptoms [13, 14].

A study conducted in Greece in 2021, utilizing the GAN questionnaire in adolescents, revealed that excessive television watching (more than three hours per day per week) and extensive engagement in computer activities such as computer or video games (more than three hours per day per week) were linked to a higher prevalence of current asthma and current rhinitis among the subjects [15].

Given the importance of this subject and the limited research in this area, the current study aimed to investigate the relationship between asthma symptoms and the time spent watching television (TV), using a computer, or accessing the Internet among adolescents in Yazd.

Methods

Study design and setting

The study was conducted as part of the GAN 2020 survey, which was carried out in Yazd from February to June 2020. The GAN survey is a standardized, cross-sectional epidemiological study conducted across multiple centers and countries using a standardized questionnaire derived from the ISAAC questionnaire [4]. The GAN questionnaire was translated from English to Persian and tested on a sample of 100 randomly selected adolescents for reliability and validity. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the Persian version of the questionnaire was 0.862, indicating good internal consistency.

Study size and participants

Participants were selected through cluster sampling from 48 public and private schools in two educational districts based on the GAN recommendation to gather at least 3000 samples. All 13–14-year-old students enrolled in these schools were invited to complete a questionnaire. Unacceptable data were reviewed by phone and corrected where necessary. As a result of school closures during the COVID-19 pandemic, an electronic questionnaire was designed and made available to virtual classrooms for the participating schools.

Variables and data sources

The study population was stratified into two groups: case and control. The case group comprised all participants who reported wheezing in the past year or physician-confirmed asthma. Students who did not meet the criteria for the case group were allocated to the control group. The variables measured in this study were: wheezing in the past year, physician-confirmed asthma, asthma severity, and screen time (hours per day). The GAN questionnaire was used to assess these variables through questions 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 32, and 33.

Screen time was defined as the total number of hours spent on television viewing, computer use, and internet activities during a typical 7-day week, expressed as hours per day.

Severe asthma defined by four or more wheezing attacks, one or more wheezing-induced sleep disturbances per week, or speech impairment due to wheezing in the past year.

It is important to note that, in addition to the standard questions on the GAN questionnaire for 13–14-year-old participants, two supplementary questions related to bird ownership and second-hand smoke exposure from other family members were added to capture potential confounders of asthma symptoms and severity.

In order to classify obesity within our sample population, we utilized the body mass index (BMI) growth charts developed by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) for children. For boys and girls aged 13–14 years old, BMI values exceeding 26 and 27, respectively, were considered indicative of obesity.

Statistical methods

Data were analyzed using SPSS software version 20 for Windows (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). The Chi-square test, simple and multiple logistic regression analyses were used to compare data between the case and control groups. Statistical significance was defined as P values less than 0.05 for all analyses.

Results

The survey was completed by 5141 Iranian students out of 7214 students, resulting in a response rate of 71.3%. Of the total sample, the gender distribution was 40.3% male and 59.7% female, with 4.8% of students having asthma confirmed by a physician. Additionally, 9% of the students reported experiencing wheezing in the previous year, while 2% reported severe asthma symptoms.

Among the participants, 35% reported owning a cat, dog or bird, with birds being the most common (88%). Additionally, 1.8% of them were current cigarette smokers, and 4.5% had ever smoked. 9.6% met the study’s criteria for obesity, while 36.4% and 31% reported excessive screen time (> 3 h/day) for television and computer use, respectively.

Male participants had significantly higher rates of severe asthma and physician-confirmed asthma. Additionally, cigarette smoking, both past and present, was found to be significantly associated with wheezing in the past year and severe asthma in both male and female participants (P < 0.05). Discussion

This study identified a significant association between media use and asthma symptoms and severity, which persisted after controlling for potential confounding factors. The Singapore/Malaysia Genetic Cross-sectional Epidemiology Study, following ISAAC guidelines, is an ongoing epidemiological investigation that explores the relationship between socioeconomic factors, lifestyle choices, co-morbidities, family history of atopic diseases, and current asthma. This study found that more than one hour of daily television or computer use is associated with increased odds of current asthma [16]. In addition, a prospective longitudinal cohort study demonstrated a significant association between prolonged television watching in children without wheezing symptoms at age 3.5 years and the subsequent development of asthma later in childhood [17].

Several previous studies have reported significant associations between increased television viewing time, higher BMI in children, and the development of asthma symptoms [18–20]. However, our study failed to find a significant relationship between obesity and asthma symptoms or severity, which contradicts some previous findings [21–23]. These discrepancies highlight the need for further investigation to better understand the relationship between obesity and asthma.

Reviewing previous studies, a positive correlation between pet ownership and smoking with childhood asthma incidence and severity has been reported [24]. Our study also revealed this relationship, as pet ownership was associated with a 35% and 73% increased likelihood of asthma and its severity, respectively.

A significant association has been reported between prenatal and postnatal exposure to second-hand smoke and the risk of childhood asthma, asthma-like syndrome, and wheezing, demonstrating the potential adverse effects of environmental tobacco smoke on respiratory health during development [25]. The current study demonstrated a significant correlation between ever smoking, current smoking, and second-hand smoking exposure and the presence of asthma symptoms, with the first two also associated with increased asthma severity.

To account for potential confounding factors, this study considered smoking and pet ownership as risk factors for asthma, as suggested by previous research [26]. Even after adjusting for these factors, a significant association was found between media use (more than three hours of TV and Internet use) with asthma symptoms and severity.

Studies indicate that excessive screen time, which includes prolonged TV viewing and overuse of computers, mobile phones, and the Internet, can lead to reduced physical activity levels in children, contributing to a sedentary lifestyle with potential negative effects on lung development and overall respiratory health. While the precise relationship between physical activity and asthma is still under investigation, some studies have suggested that exercise may reduce exhaled nitric oxide levels and reduce total cells and eosinophils in the sputum of individuals with asthma [27–29]. Nevertheless, other research suggests that physical activity can exacerbate asthma symptoms, particularly in individuals with lower levels of physical activity, due to exercise-induced bronchospasm [30, 31].

While our study did not directly measure vitamin D levels, it is plausible that prolonged screen time among children correlates with reduced exposure to sunlight and an increased risk of vitamin D deficiency. Consequently, vitamin D deficiency can affect the immune system regulation and increase the likelihood of developing allergic disorders and asthma in children. Moreover, extended periods spent indoors could lead to greater contact with indoor allergens, potentially exacerbating the effect of low vitamin D levels on allergic diseases [32, 33].

One study showed that physical activity can cause a significant shift in cytokine production from T-helper 2 to T-helper 1 in asthmatic patients. This shift in cytokine balance has important implications for the immune response, especially when encountering different pathogens, such as viruses, which leads to an improved ability to combat infections. Given that viral diseases have the potential to trigger asthma exacerbations and severe asthma, this study corroborates our prior findings indicating a significant correlation between extended periods of sedentary activity, such as watching TV or spending time on computers or the Internet, and an increased prevalence of severe asthma [34].

Some limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. Firstly, the retrospective nature of the study design may have introduced recall bias, which could potentially influence the results. Secondly, the lack of data on participants’ family history precluded the inclusion of this important variable as a potential confounder in the analysis. Despite these limitations, this study has several notable strengths, including the use of an electronic questionnaire and web interface design, which minimized the likelihood of data entry errors, and the ability to obtain a larger sample size through the electronic approach.

Conclusion

This study identified a significant relationship between screen time and the presence and severity of asthma symptoms, even when controlling for confounding factors. However, it appears that the relationship between media usage time and asthma is influenced by a complex interplay of lifestyle factors such as physical activity, obesity, diet, indoor allergen exposure, and other unidentified variables. To better understand the relationship between screen time and allergic and immunological diseases, further comprehensive prospective research is needed that considers potential genetic and environmental factors.
