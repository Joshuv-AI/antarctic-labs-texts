An unusual case of burst suppression electrographic pattern following intrathecal baclofen overdose in critical care: a case report
Authors: Nory Elhadjene, Hugo Soulier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBaclofen is a gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA)-B receptor agonist used to treat neurological spasticity. Several cases of baclofen intoxication have been reported with neurological symptoms. While numerous reports describe oral baclofen toxicity, the neurological consequence of intrathecal overdose remain poorly characterised. We describe an electroencephalogram (EEG) post-anoxic encephalopathy pattern following intrathecal BACLOFEN injection to treat spasticity in an intensive care unit in September 2024.

Background

Baclofen is a gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA)-B receptor agonist used to treat neurological spasticity. By reducing the release of excitatory neurotransmitters (glutamate and aspartate) in the spinal cord, and inhibiting calcium influx, it reduces neuronal excitability and muscle spasticity.1 Its pharmacokinetics include good oral absorption but limited blood-brain barrier penetration,2 resulting in modest central nervous system (CNS) effects at standard doses. Several cases of baclofen intoxication have been described in the literature, particularly related to intrathecal pump catheter failure or oral overdose.3,4 We report a case of electroencephalogram (EEG) post-anoxic encephalopathy pattern following intrathecal BACLOFEN injection to treat spasticity in an intensive care unit.

Case presentation

A 41-year-old female patient with no significant medical or surgical history was admitted to the ICU for diabetic ketoacidosis secondary to left submandibular sialadenitis. Septic shock ensued, requiring vasopressor support and mechanical ventilation for 16 days. After 4 weeks in ICU, she developed ICU-acquired weakness resulting in a fixed equinovarus deformity of both feet. For spasticity management an intrathecal injection of baclofen 50 μg (0.05 mg) was planned: unfortunately, 50 mg was administered. Following the injection, the patient became unconscious [Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 3] with haemodynamic failure requiring fluid resuscitation and vasopressor support, and respiratory insufficiency requiring mechanical ventilation via the tracheostomy tube. The haemodynamic failure was rapidly managed and the patient remained haemodynamically stable with no evidence of major cerebral hypoperfusion or severe hypotension. A non-contrast brain CT scan was performed 15 min after the intrathecal baclofen injection and showed no abnormalities. A lumbar cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) drainage of 30 ml was performed 15 min after the CT scan, but did not result in any clinical neurological improvement. Unfortunately, no CSF baclofen levels were measured to compare with plasma levels. Plasma baclofen, measured at this time was 0.246 mg l−1, with a second level obtained 19 h later of 0.035 mg l−1. Although plasma baclofen levels above 1 mg l−1 are associated with toxicity, it is important to understand that in our patient's case, plasma baclofen levels cannot be correlated with the clinical severity of the presentation due to the intrathecal route of administration, rather than oral or intravenous administration. An EEG performed 4 h after the injection showed a discontinuous pattern interspersed with high amplitude activity, consistent with a burst suppression pattern (Fig. 1a). Nine hours after the intrathecal injection, the patient exhibited tonic–clonic movements, necessitating the initiation of antiepileptic therapy with clonazepam (bolus of 1 mg) followed by levetiracetam (1 g per day). Serial EEG monitoring was performed: a second EEG at 24 h post-injection showed persistent burst suppression patterns (Fig. 1b). The patient remained comatose with a GCS score of 3. A third EEG on day 2 showed a continuous pattern of theta/delta activity in response to nociceptive and auditory stimuli (Fig. 1c). The patient's level of consciousness improved, with a GCS score of 10. The final EEG on day 3 showed a continuous pattern with a well-modulated alpha rhythm, predominantly in the posterior regions, in response to eye opening and closing (Fig. 1d). The patient regained normal consciousness concurrently with the normalisation of the EEG findings. The patient was transferred to a rehabilitation unit several weeks later to continue her recovery and rehabilitation care. At follow up, the patient had no residual neurological deficits related to the baclofen intoxication. Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first report of a burst-suppression EEG pattern following an intrathecal baclofen overdose due to a dosing error. We emphasise the favourable spontaneous evolution, its prognostic relevance for clinicians, and the essential role of the EEG in the diagnosis of drug-induced encephalopathies. Baclofen overdose is a real diagnostic and therapeutic challenge in the ICU because of the wide range of neurological symptoms, from lethargy to coma, seizures or abnormal movements.5 In severe baclofen overdose, loss of brainstem reflexes and coma may mimic brain death with a burst-suppressed electrographic pattern.6 Baclofen is not included in standard toxicological panels and its levels are not routinely available in practice, leading to a potential diagnostic challenge in unexplained coma with areflexia. In these situations, EEG findings such as diffuse slowing or burst suppression patterns can support the diagnosis and guide further evaluation; while diffuse slowing is the most common EEG finding, less typical patterns such as burst suppression can provide valuable diagnostic clues. Indeed, these patterns are most often associated with metabolic or toxic encephalopathies or even post-anoxic encephalopathy.6 Periodic complexes have been described in lithium toxicity, cephalosporin poisoning and chronic bismuth exposure. Our understanding of the dose-dependent central nervous system effects following intrathecal baclofen remains limited. Experimental data in rats indicates that high-dose baclofen can induce early-onset, severe encephalopathy with EEG changes progressing from deepening sleep to generalised epileptiform discharges and burst-suppression.7 Prior repeated exposure appears to attenuate both the severity and duration of these effects, suggesting the presence of tolerance mechanisms and a potential role for the blood–brain barrier. However, EEG alone cannot differentiate between toxic-metabolic encephalopathy and post-anoxic injury, and interpretation requires integration with clinical context, imaging findings and the patient's temporal evolution.4

Moreover, baclofen's toxic plasma concentration typically ranges from 1.1 to 3.5 mg l−1 with coma or fatal outcomes occurring at levels between 6 and 9.6 mg l−1.8 Our patient had relatively low plasma levels (0.246 mg l−1) because of the direct intrathecal injection: misunderstanding this could be a trap in the diagnosis. The absence of rapid plasma levels complicates the diagnosis and requires other tools for diagnosis. Notably, as drug elimination consists of approximately 15% metabolised by the liver and 85% excreted through kidney, patients with renal dysfunction could have more toxic adverse effects. Although haemodialysis may be interesting for baclofen toxicity in end stage renal disease, the Extracorporeal Treatments in Poisoning (EXTRIP) workgroup did not use haemodialysis for acute baclofen intoxication.9 Finally, the absence of monitoring of CSF baclofen levels is a limitation, as a more precise correlation analysis could have been achieved through the simultaneous sampling of CSF and EEG recording.

Conclusion

In conclusion, in cases where standard toxicology has failed to identify the causative agent, EEG monitoring can guide diagnosis and management, demonstrating the importance of its utility in toxicological evaluation. This case also illustrates a rare iatrogenic intrathecal baclofen overdose in which deep coma with a burst suppression EEG pattern was fully reversible, supporting the notion that, in this context, burst suppression may primarily reflect a drug effect rather than irreversible brain injury.
