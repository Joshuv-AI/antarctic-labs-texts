Documenting traditional knowledge of the Trans-Himalaya: ethnic uses of plants in Drass valley, Ladakh
Authors: Sakina Banoo, Aijaz Hassan Ganie, Anzar Ahmad Khuroo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn Indian Trans-Himalaya, Drass is one of the coldest high-altitude areas which remain cut off from rest of the world for many months. In this geographically isolated region, a treasure trove of ethnobotanical knowledge has accumulated through generations of interaction between the local residents and rich floristic diversity, resulting in a profound understanding of the plant uses in nutritional, medicinal, cultural and ritual contexts. Thus, aim of the study was to gather, analyse and document the indigenous knowledge of plant utilization of this unique region.MethodsData on ethnomedicinal and ethnobotanical knowledge was obtained through semi-structured interviews using a pre-prepared questionnaire. A total of 60 informants were interviewed in local languages (Balti, Sheena and Pahadi) among three ethnic communities. Use value (UV), fidelity level (FL%) and Jaccard's similarity index (JI%) were employed to quantitatively analyse the data.ResultsWe documented 121 plant species, of which 35 had medicinal properties and 86 species were used for other ethnobotanical purposes. Most of the plant species used in ethnomedicines was herbaceous. Leaves were the most used plant part to treat a variety of human diseases. The formulations were administered orally in most cases, while few were applied externally. The FL% of the plant species ranged from 12 to 100%, and the highest UV was found for Rumex patentia. Ethnic uses not previously known were recorded for 13 plant species.ConclusionsWe documented diversity of plant species with traditional uses in the study region. These plant species were used as medicine as well as for other ethnobotanical purposes. Our study recommends further phytochemical investigations and pharmacological validations of 13 plant species that were documented for the first time for their ethnomedicinal uses. Also the plants used as food, fodder, firewood can be utilized for further studies to ensure nutritional and energy security with due benefit sharing among local stakeholders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13002-025-00785-1.Keywords: Ethnomedicinal knowledge, Informants, Use value, Fidelity level, New use reports

Introduction

People living in remote regions of the world are critically dependent on the local biodiversity for their survival and livelihood [1]. In the Himalaya, the highest mountain range of the world, the ethnic communities rely on the diverse range of plants to meet their basic needs of food, fuel, medicine, fodder for livestock and a host of other economic and ecological benefits [2]. Over generations, the people residing in far-flung areas of the Himalaya have amassed a huge wealth of traditional knowledge about local use of biodiversity [3]. However, in recent times, an ever-increasing push for the physical infrastructure and the resultant land-use and socio-cultural transformations have put the biodiversity and associated traditional knowledge in the Himalaya under a high risk of erosion [4]. The precious knowledge of traditional uses of plants is fast fading among the young population in the region and now remains confined to only few elderly people in remote areas. To safeguard the traditional knowledge from further erosion in the Himalaya, it is important to scientifically document this valuable information for guiding resilience of indigenous communities and achieving goals of sustainable development [5].

Ladakh, a region falling in the Indian Trans-Himalaya, harbours a unique floristic diversity, and represents a rich repository of traditional knowledge, especially use of plants for medicinal purposes [6]. The region often remains isolated from the rest of the world for many months during winter. The remoteness of the region, harsh climatic conditions and ruggedness of the terrain have led the people to search for nature-based solutions to meet basic livelihood and healthcare needs [7].The traditional medicinal system of the region is popularly known as the ‘Amchi’ system of medicine [8], and this system is prevalent among the Buddhist community and practised in the Leh district. In contrast, people in the Muslim-dominated Kargil district usually practise the ‘Hakeem’ system of medicine and the experts in this system are locally known as ‘Akhone Aba’ who mostly use wild-growing plants to treat various human diseases across the region.

Wild edible plants (WEPs) used in mountainous areas serve as primary food source or a dietary supplement. The WEPs have huge scope to function as primary economic resource through commercialization [9]; and are also an important genetic resource for cultivation of novel crops and the identification of new germplasms [10]. The use of WEPs will not only meet the local demand as a crucial element of a global sustenance strategy, but may also aid in the preservation of local cultural traditions [11]. There is a global consensus to conduct research on WEPs used by ethnic groups so as to validate these plants through advanced modern food science and technology for their nutritional composition. This has established a new research direction in the ethnobotany of WEPs [12].

To date, several ethnobotanical studies have been carried out in different parts of the Ladakh region [13–19]. However, majority of these studies have focussed on the ‘Amchi’ system of medicine, and other systems of traditional medicine and traditional knowledge have been largely neglected. In Kargil district, the Drass valley represents a typical transition zone between temperate Kashmir and cold-arid Ladakh. It is one of the coldest areas inhabited by humans in the world [20]. The valley comprises nearly less than one per cent of the area of the Ladakh region; but still includes almost half (48%) of the total floristic diversity known from this Trans-Himalayan region [21]. The people residing here still follow the old customs and traditions and most of them belong to low income groups, and depend on farming and livestock rearing. Access to modern health care is often a problem in remote areas of Drass due to the poor access through roads and also to the prevailing climatic conditions. Furthermore, because of their accessibility, cost, familiarity, personal experiences and perceived efficacy over time, people in the region view plants as a sustainable and self-sufficient source for healthcare and other sources of wild food and energy. Being the transition zone, the valley harbours a rich floristic diversity [21] but has not been explored for its medicinal plant wealth and other resources of ethnobotanical importance due to its remoteness, harsh environment and rugged terrain that restrict researchers’ access to the area. The people residing in these far-flung mountainous areas have adapted to these conditions and have gained the valuable traditional knowledge associated with plants used for medicine, food, and energy. Therefore, it is imperative to document the traditional knowledge of Drass valley from the perspective of the mutual relationship between humans and plants. Till date, no study is available on the traditional knowledge associated with plants (medicine, food and energy) in Drass valley. Therefore, the objective of present study was to (i) identify and document plant species and parts used in traditional medicine and associated knowledge, and prevailing ethnic practices including methods of preparation and administration of the traditional medicine in the study region, (ii) identify and document medicinal plants with highest use value and the most frequently used medicinal plants to treat a particular human disease in the region, (iii) identify and document plant species with first-time claim of ethnomedicinal uses, (iv) document plant species used as vegetables, fuel, fodder, edibles and spices in the Drass valley.

We expect that the results from this study can provide basic information on ethnobotanical and traditional knowledge of local people in the area, which is useful for primary healthcare promotion and benefits of regional flora. Also the study may provide insights to guide the discovery of new plant-based formulations, and inform the policies to achieve the food, nutritional and energy security in this Himalayan region, with learnings for elsewhere.

Materials and methods

Study area

Drass region of district Kargil in Ladakh (India) is located at 74°.13′ to 74°.42′ N latitude and 34°.03′ to 34°.10′ E longitude with altitude ranging from 2700 to 5000 m above the sea level. Lying between Kashmir and Ladakh, the study region is about 60 km away from main Kargil town (Ladakh) and 120 km away from Srinagar (Kashmir) from the other side of Zojila mountain pass (Fig. 1). The study area remains isolated from the rest of world for about six months due to heavy snowfall in the Zojila pass. Locally, the area is also known as ‘Hemm babz’, ‘Hemm’ means snow, and ‘babz’ means place. Two ethnic communities inhabit the Drass area: the Brokpa and the Purigpa, the former speaks ‘Sheena’ language and the latter ‘Balti’ language, also nomads temporarily inhabit two places speak ‘Pahadi’. During the winter season, the study area witnesses severe cold and a pleasant weather during summer season. The temperature ranges between minus −15 and −35 °C in winter and 5°C to 25 °C in summer [22].

Demographic data

During the present study, a total of 60 informants aged between 15 and 75 years old were interviewed to record the traditional ethnic knowledge about plant species (Table 1). The total population of the study area is about 20,000 (sparcely populated) residing at 23 locations which include 21 villages and two temporary settlements (Meenamarg and Gumri). In these two localities, the climate is temperate with diverse habitats like grasslands, rocky slopes, streams with vegetation along banks and high alpine mountains, and in rest of the localities (villages) climate is cold desert, with habitats that include cultivated land, grasslands, streams with aquatic to mesophytic vegetation and sub-alpine to alpine mountains (Table 1). Among them, female informants (n = 24) demonstrated a greater level of knowledge sharing experience than male informants (n = 36). Also in each village, interview was usually conducted with elderly and experienced members, locally known as ‘Akhone Aba’. Throughout the survey, a continuous cordial relationship with the local population was maintained to ensure the validity of the information presented related to the informants’ traditional knowledge.

Data collection

The practice of ethnomedicinal uses to cure various human diseases is a widely accepted among the Brokpa and Purigpa communities of Drass valley. We conducted extensive surveys in the study area during different seasons from 2019 to 2022 for the collection of data on plants and the associated traditional knowledge. The information about traditional knowledge was obtained through structured and semi-structured interviews. The aims and objectives of the study were explained to each prospective respondent in order to solicit their consent and cooperation before the data collection, following Ganie et al. [23]. The ‘Akhone Abas’ and elderly experienced people irrespective of gender were often accompanied to the field for validation of the plant species used as medicine/other uses and authentication of the plant specimens collected from wild habitats. Information about the local name of the plant species, method of preparation, dosage and use to treat human diseases were recorded. The interviews were conducted in local languages (Balti and Sheena) among the two different communities of the study area. The first author (Sakina Banoo) conducted interview in ‘Balti’ and for ‘Sheena’ language the assistance was taken from local educated members of the ‘Brokpa’ community. We adhered to The International Society of Ethnobiology Code of Ethics in interviews (International Society of Ethnobiology Code of Ethics with 2008 additions http://ethnobiology.net/code-of-ethics/).

Taxonomic identification

The plant species of the study area with ethnic uses were collected in consultation with ‘Akhone Abas’ and elderly experienced people. The photographs of some medicinal plants growing in their natural habitats of study area are also provided (Fig. 2). The plant specimens were identified by perusing relevant taxonomic literature [24–27] and were also cross-checked with the previously deposited voucher specimens at University of Kashmir Herbarium (KASH). The voucher specimens of collected plant specimens were deposited at KASH.

Data analysis

The data collected during this study was analysed using three different quantitative indices, namely, fidelity level, use value and Jaccard's similarity index.

Fidelity level

Fidelity level (FL%) was employed to determine which plant species was the most frequently used to treat a particular human disease [28]. It was calculated by the formula, FL (%) = Ip/Iu × 100, where ‘Ip’ is the number of use reports in each of the use categories, while ‘Iu’ is the total number of use reports. It provides relative importance of a particular plant species for medicinal purposes [29]. Plants that are frequently used have higher FL values, and vice versa [30].

Categorization of diseases

The present study attempted to develop a uniform framework for categorization of different diseases, and the diseases recorded were grouped into 19 categories according to International classification of primary care-2 (ICPC-2) classification [34, 35]. Diseases like ‘head ache’, ‘body ache’, ‘fever’ and ‘chest pain’ did not match any disease category and they were therefore placed under unspecified and general category (OTH-A) (Appendix I and Table 2).

Results

We recorded 121 plant species from the study area that were used for ethnobotanical purposes. Of the total plant species recorded, 35 species were used for medicinal purposes (Appendix I) and 86 plant species were used as fodder, fuel, vegetables, timber, raw edible and spices (Appendix II).

Diversity of ethnomedicinal flora

The ethnomedicinal flora of the study area comprises 35 species belonging to 32 genera in 20 angiosperm families (Appendix I). Polygonaceae with five species was the dominant family, followed by Apiaceae with four species. There are 11 families, namely, Asphodelaceae, Brassicaceae, Crassulaceae, Fabaceae, Gentianaceae, Malvaceae, Onagaraceae, Orchidaceae, Plumbaginaceae, Scrophulariaceae and Solanaceae that contribute a single species each.

Growth form of medicinal plants

Most of the documented medicinal plant species were herbs (30 spp., 80%), followed by shrubs (3 spp., 14.28%) and subshrubs (2 spp., 5.72%) (Appendix I).

Habitat and status (wild or cultivated)

Most of the species were growing in dry rocky slopes (37%), followed by moist grassland (14%), dry places along roadsides (11%), open dry places (8%), moist slopes (6%) and fewer species grew on stabilized slopes, rocky moist places and shady places (Appendix I). Only one species (Fagopyrum esculentum) was cultivated and the rest of the medicinal plant species were wild-growing. Most of these species were growing along an elevational gradient of 3000–4000 m asl and some species grew in the alpine zones (Appendix I).

Part used

The leaves of 11 species were the most commonly used plant part/s to treat various diseases, followed by roots (8 spp.), flowers (8 spp.), seeds (6 spp.), stem (5 spp.), rhizomes (3 spp.), fruits (2 spp.) and aerial parts (1 sp.) The leaves of Dysphania botrys, Cicer microphyllum, Eremurus himalaicus, Malva neglecta, Lactuca macrorhiza, Ranunculus laetus, Rheum tibeticum, Rheum webbianum, Rumex patientia, Taraxacum officinale and Mentha longifolia were the most commonly used plant species for medicinal purposes.

Diseases treated

These plant species in the study area were used to treat 27 human diseases, and some plant species were used to treat more than one disease and some diseases are treated by using more than one plant species (Table 2).

Method of preparation and administration

The preparation of crude drugs involved the formation of decoction in most cases (37%), followed by powdering the dried plants (17%), eaten raw or as vegetable (11%) and crushing the plant parts on a stone with water and then licking the stone (6%). In addition flour, smoke and ash of plant parts were used for the preparation of herbal remedies (Appendix I).

In the study area, there were 25 plant species used for internal application and 11 were applied externally to treat various diseases (Fig. 3).

Use value

The use value ranged from 0.01 to 0.2 (Appendix I). Rumex patentia had the highest use value of 0.2 and the lowest use value of 0.01 was for Eremurus himalaicus (Appendix I).

Fidelity value

The fidelity value of medicinal plants ranged from 12 to 100%. Plant species such as Solenanthus circinnatus, Fagopyrum esculentum, Prangos pabularia, Rumex paientia, Dyspharia botrys, Heracleum pinnatum, Dactylorhiza hetageria, Berberis orthobotrys, Epilobium angustifolium, Hyoscymus niger and Cicer microphylum had the highest fidelity value (100%) for some particular diseases. The second highest fidelity value, i.e. 83%, was recorded for Aconitum vialeceum, A. heterophyllum, Rosa foetida, R. webbiana and Thlaspi arvensis each, while the lowest fidelity level (12%) was found in Eremurus himalaica (Table 2).

Jaccard's similarity index (JI%)

A comparison with previous studies on ethnobotanical data in India as well as in other countries was performed by using the Jaccard index. The traditional medicinal uses of plants, as detailed in Appendix I, were compared with 30 published ethnomedicinal sources at both regional and national levels (Table 3). The JI ranged from 0.26 to 17.24. The top three highest degrees of similarities were recorded from various areas of Ladakh with studies conducted by Hadi et al. [45] with a JI of 17.24, followed by Bashir et al. [6] with a JI of 16.67 and Angmo et al. [17] with a JI of 13.34 (Table 3). Among neighbouring states of India, the highest degree of similarity was recorded from Himachal Pradesh with a JI of 5.21. It was also recorded that from neighbouring countries highest degree of similarity was recorded from Gilgit-Baltistan of Pakistan with a JI of 5.21 (Table 3).

New records of ethnomedicinal uses

We report for the first-time new ethnomedicinal uses for 13 plant species, which include: Aconitum violaceum, Berberis orthobotrys, Blitum virgatum, Epilobium angustifolium, Eremurus himalaicus, Fagopyrum esculentum, Malva neglecta, Melanoseris macrorhiza, Podophyllum hexandrum, Prangos pabularia, Ranunculus bulbosus, Rheum tibeticum and Solenanthus circinnatus (Appendix I). All these species grow in wild except Fagopyrum esculentum which is cultivated in the study region. Some species show narrow distribution range to the Wesstern Himalaya (Aconitum violaceum, Eremurus himalaicus, Rheum tibeticum).

Diversity of plants used as vegetables, fuel, fodder, edibles and spices

We found 86 plant species belonging to 57 genera and 30 families that were used for different purposes (other than medicinal) in Drass valley (Appendix II). Asteraceae with 10 species was the dominant family, followed by Salicaceae and Fabaceae with nine species each, and Apiaceae and Rosaceae with eight species each. There were 13 families (Amaranthaceae, Betulaceae, Boraginaceae, Campanulaceae, Caprifoliaceae, Elaegnaceae, Cupressaceae, Gentianaceae, Papaveraceae, Iridaceae, Pinaceae, Solanaceae and Urticaceae) with a single species used for ethnobotanical purposes in the study area.

In terms of growth form, herbs contribute 52 species (61%), followed by 16 shrub species (18%), 11 trees (13%) and seven subshrubs (8%).

The aerial parts were utilized most frequently (29%), followed by leaves (17%), wood (14%), branchlets (12%), seeds (8%), fruits (6%), twigs (5%), the whole plant (4%), bark (3%), flowers (2%) and stems (> 1%) for ethnobotanical purposes. In addition, there were 53 species used as fodder (26%), followed by fuel (26 spp.; 22%), economic purposes (14 spp.; 12%), vegetables (11 spp.; 9%), edible (8 spp.; 9%), timber (4 spp.; 39%) and spices (1 sp.; > 1%) in the study area.

Discussion

The people of the Drass valley have a close relationship with the local flora, and there is a rich tradition of using the plants for their own needs. We collected data concerning plants that play a role in the lives of local people in the Drass valley—a remote area of Ladakh. The traditional usage of 121 plant species has been recorded and these plant species are used for ethnomedicinal properties and ethnobotanical purposes.

Diversity of ethnomedicinal flora

In the study area, 35 plant species were used as medicine belonging to various families. Polygonaceae was the dominant family with five species; our results are in contrary to the results of previous workers who reported Asteraceae as the dominant family with ethnomedicinal properties [6, 18, 19, 40, 43, 48, 49]. The members of the family Polygonaceae are traditionally used to treat cardiovascular diseases, hypertention and cancer; [50]. Apiaceae was the second largest family with plant species like Carum carvi, Heracleum pinnatum, Hymenolaena candollei and Prangos pabularia used as medicine in the study area. Tariq et al. [51] also found that Apiaceae was the most dominant family with ethnomedicinal uses.

Growth form of medicinal plants

Most of the plant used as medicine were herbaceous and our results are in conformity with previous studies [52–56]. Herbaceous plants were the most dominant group of plants used in the preparation of local remedies, which may be due to their relative abundance, easy availability and effective treatment when compared to other life forms [57, 58].

Habitat of medicinal plants

Ladakh being the mountainous region with undulating terrain and a rain-shadow area have plant species which grow in dry conditions and most of them grow in dry rocky habitats. Most species (97%) grow in the wild and therefore these plants should be used in a sustainable manner [34].

Parts used as medicine

Leaves were predominantly used plant part as medicine in the study area may be due to their abundance and accessibility. Also leaves contain bioactive chemicals that can be easily extracted and used for treatment of health disorders [56, 59]. Leaves are also considered safer than other organs, such as roots and rhizomes [60, 61]. The judicious use of leaves does not pose any threat to existence of a plant species.

Method of preparation and administration

Decoction was commonly used herbal remedies in the study area and it corroborates with previous findings from the Indian Himalayan region [62, 63]. The herbal remedies were taken orally in majority of the cases and external applications of herbal remedies were second prevalent form of administration. Similar to our observations, the oral administration was commonly used method in other parts of India [19, 34] In some case, the plant/plant parts were crushed with water on a stone and then the stone was licked, the reasons why medicine is taken by this method needs further investigation.

Use value

The most important and most common medicinal plant species used was Rumex patentia as demonstrated by its high use value index. It may be due to the availability of plant species in the lower elevation. This plant species is also used widely in China for treatment of ailments like haemostasis, and also have antibacterial and anti-infammatory action in vivo [64]. Acantholimon lycopodioides was also one of the most commonly used species in the study area, and previous studies have also found the plant species with ethnomedicinal uses in Ladakh (India) and Shigar valley of Pakistan [65]. The reason for highest use value may also be due to the fact that the plant species grow abundantly in the study area and people of the region have experienced its medicinal uses over the period of time. The highest use value was also recorded in Arnebia euchroma, the plant species has not only been used for treatment of various diseases traditionally but recently important phytochemical constituents have been isolated from this plant species which are used for curing various harmful diseases [54].

Fidelity value

The fidelity level shows the extent to which the informants agreed about the preferred use categories for each species. Prangos pabularia is popular as a traditional treatment for arthritis, and this species has wide usage in Indian system of medicine [64]. The high fidelity level in the study region for the use of arthritis can also be further used for discovery of potential molecules. Heracleum pinnatum is most preferred plant species for treatment of digestive disease. The genus Heracleum has broad pharmacological activities which include anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, anticholinesterase, anti-oxidant, antiviral, cytotoxic and anticarcinogenic properties [66].

Comparative analysis of medicinal plants species in Drass and other areas

Various ethnobotanical studies have documented the traditional uses of plants in different communities, such as the study conducted by Rinchen [38], which identified 35 medicinal plants in the Suru and Zanskar valleys of Ladakh, and another study by Kumar et al. [39] recorded 54 medicinal plants utilized by the people Singay-lalok region. A synthesis study from the Jammu and Kashmir region of western Himalaya of India documented 1123 medicinal plant species used by locals to treat various diseases, highlighting the value of traditional knowledge [4]. To enable a clear comparison of our findings with previous research, we adopted a systematic framework that includes species identification, traditional uses and cultural context. Our systematic studies revealed that in previous studies, some plant species were reported to be used in treatment of a particular disease; however, our study for the first time reported novel ethnomedicinal claim of the particular plant species.

The high Jaccard's similarity index (JI%) was observed between the current study and other parts Ladakh can be attributed to the close geographical proximity of these neighbouring regions with close cultural, geographical and vegetation types [17]. The highest degree of similarities was recorded from Kargil [6] and Suru valley (17). Suru valley of Kargil share the cultural proximity with the Drass as both the regions follow the same religion and the ‘Aba Akhoons’ are the herbalist consulted to treat diseases, likewise both the regions have temperate-to-cold desert type of climatic conditions and both these regions have similar type of vegetation [6, 17, 45]. The gradual decline in Jaccard’s similarity index from northern to north-east areas likely reflects the impact of distance and geographical barriers (high mountain passes) that hinder the exchange of information regarding the use of ethnomedicinal plants [4]. The other regions of western Himalaya of India like Jammu and Kashmir, Himachal Pradesh and Uttarakhand have less similarity due to geographical barriers, different cultures and vegetation types and temperate to sub-tropical climatic conditions. As one moves towards the south of India Punjab plains [73], the dissimilarity increases due to prevalent tropical type of climatic conditions, differences in socio-cultural, religious and proximity to urban centres. These findings revealed that traditional knowledge of plants to be used as medicine tends to be more uniform in regions that are geographically close and culturally similar, while differences increase with greater distances and geographical barriers. This underscores the importance of considering regional and cultural factors when examining traditional plant-based healthcare practices. The plant species commonly used in traditional medicine highlights a shared cultural heritage in Ladakh and India, whereas the unique practices identified in Drass indicate localized knowledge that merits further exploration. The variations of usage of medicinal plants reflect adaptations to local environmental conditions and cultural customs.

Plant species with first-time ethnomedicinal claims

We recorded 13 medicinal plants with first-time claims of ethnobotanical uses, e.g. in previous studies, Aconitum violaceum (endemic to this region) was reported to be used in treatment of boils [67, 68], asthma, high fever [67–70], gastric troubles [67–70], sciatic pain [40, 69], intestinal worms [40, 67, 71], renal pain, snakebite and scorpion bites [64, 69, 70]; however, our study for the first-time reports the ethnomedicinal claim that rhizome of the species was used to treat jaundice and stomach pain. This species is also rich in various chemical compounds that make it important in the field of medicine. Historically, Berberis orthobotrys (native to Western Himalaya) was used for the treatment of wounds, cancer (breast cancer and osteosarcoma), gastrointestinal disorders and several other diseases [72, 73]. As well as treating internal pain, the root powder of this species is applied externally to cuts and wounds, also used to treat gastrointestinal diseases [74, 75]. According to our study, the plant was used to treat the skin diseases and this ethnomedicinal claim has been reported for the first time. Prangos pabularia (native to Western Himalaya) root extract was used to treat stomach ulcers; the plant as a whole seems to have a wide range of pharmacological effects, including antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, analgesic, diuretic, abortifacient, allelopathic and anticancer properties, that have been used in traditional medical systems since ancient times [76, 77], but present study reports that the species can be used for the treatment of arthritis as well. In the previous studies, it was reported that Podophyllum hexandrum can be used to cure ailments like constipation, fever, jaundice, liver disorders, syphilis, diseases of lymph glands, etc. [77]. The ethnomedicinal use of treating joint crack by P. hexandrum (native to Western Himalaya) has been recorded for the first time. Fagopyrum esculentum is reported to be used to treat painful urination, prevent heart disease, manage diabetes, boost immune system, prevent asthma, great source of protein and used to treat diarrhoea [40]; however, in present study it was found that the plant species can also be used to treat appendicitis. Previous studies revealed that Malva neglecta (native to Western Himalaya) can be used to treat pain, inflammation, haemorrhoids, renal stones, constipation and infertility [78], but present study revealed that this plant species can also be used to treat muscle spam and stomach pain—the first-time ethnomedicinal claim. The other species with new use reports include: Blitum virgatum, the species was previously reported to treat urinary disorders and acidity [79]; however, the traditional use to treat heart diseases has been reported for the first time. In the present study, it was reported that Epilobium angustifolium (native to study region) was used to treat stomach pain; however, the species was previously reported to be used for treatment of insomnia, anaemia, delirium tremens, infections and colds [80, 81]. Eremurus himalaicus in previous studies was reported to be used for the treatment of anaemia, and inducing lactation in post-partum period [82—85}; however, to treat the ear pain is its first ethnomedicinal claim recorded in present study. Rheum tibeticum recorded for the first time to be used to treat abdomen pain; previously it was reported to be used for rheumatism [85]. Melanoseris macrorhiza is reported in this study to be used against diabetes for first time and previously reported to treat headache [86], stomach-ache [87] and jaundice [88]. Ranunculus bulbosus was previously reported to be used as tonic [89]; however, in present study it was reported to be used to treat arthritis. Solenanthus circinnatus earlier reported to be used in bone fractures [90], treatment of bone contusion, tendon damages and to reduce joint inflammation in Persian folk medicine [91]; however, we first-time report that the species is used for treatment of boils. Recent studies suggest that the historic ethnomedicinal uses of plant-based medicines as a preliminary screening tool can prove beneficial in the identification of bioactive compounds and discovery of new drugs [92].

Diversity of plants used as vegetables, fuel, fodder, edibles and spices

Asteraceae was the dominant family with 10 plant species used in different ethnobotanical purposes. This may be attributed to dominance of this family in the mountainous region of Ladakh [21], so local people use most of its members in their day to day life in Drass region. The herbs were usually used for ethnobotanical purpose; herbaceous habit is most dominant life form in mountainous cold desert regions of the Himalaya [93] and in neighbouring regions [94]. Plants in the study area are perennial, so people like to use the aerial parts of the plants for multiple purposes. Keeping in view the sparse vegetation and harsh environment, the people use the resources in a sustainable manner and the perennating organs like roots and rhizomes are not used so as to maintain the plant populations for sustainable utilization. The study area is second coldest place in the world; therefore, people have knowledge of various plants that can be used as firewood to counter the harsh winter conditions.

In present study, 11 plant species are reported to have use as wild vegetables, these species are mostly consumed during their early vegetative phase when there is shortage of food due to long winters in the study area and also they remain cut off from rest of India. Therefore, the availability of these plants during early spring was used as a substitute to fill the gap of food scarcity that occurs until the new staple food is ready and the main supply line is restored after harsh winters and heavy snowfall. This implies that wild edible plants have fundamental roles in food security. The wild edible plants are important resources to combat food insecurity in developing countries. The ethnobotanical knowledge of plants used as edibles (wild vegetables) is useful during crisis time such as pandemics and wars [95]. Also, ethnobotanical knowledge about food plants and particularly that of wild plants, semi-domesticated taxa or minor, underutilized or neglected crops is of great relevance in food security and sovereignty, comprising both the right of any person to have a sufficient and healthy nutrition and also the right of people to have culturally adequate food [96]. The nutrient rich underutilized plants of far-flung areas like Ladakh can be used for food fortification—a novel approach to decrease hunger, improve the situation of food security and a sustainable and cost-effective method to overcome micronutrient deficiencies for a large population [97]. These plants are important for ecosystem services, basic livelihood and relatives of cultivated crops, including major crop that can be used for further breeding programmes [98, 99], and as a reservoir of genes not present in domesticated taxa or races that could be useful for new crop developments, e.g. the Wild Lens and Cicer growing in extreme weather conditions [100] could also be used as alternative crops in the changing global climate scenario. These plants in current scenario face cultural erosion and also face conservation problems, thus making such studies highly desirable for sustainable utilization [101]. Likewise many plant species are used as fodder in the study area and can be utilized for climate-resilient agriculture in near future.

The emerging energy crisis can also be mitigated in a sustainable manner by using plant biomass as alternative energy. The present study can also provide clues about biofuel and bioenergy crops. The people of the region use some shrubs (Cotoneaster integerrimus, Hippophae rhamnoides) to meet energy needs in sustainable way. The information collected in this study can also prove helpful in the identification and commercialization of wild fruits (Sorbus tianschanica, Ribes orientale) and other plant spices (Carum carvi) of the region.

Implications of utilizing plant resources in the study area

Our study on the traditionally used plant species in the Drass valley has significant implications for environmental sustainability, food security and public health. The study region harbours a unique diversity of plants which highlights the importance of traditional knowledge systems in maintaining ecological balance and promoting sustainable practices. However, anthropogenic factors and climate change pose serious threats to local flora [102] and the livelihoods that depend on these resources. Our study brought in focus the vital role of traditional knowledge in utilizing plants for health care, food and nutritional security. The loss of this prized knowledge could jeopardize food security, local healthcare system and utilization of biodiversity in sustainable manner. Therefore, this valuable knowledge can play a crucial role in addressing health issues, malnutrition and promoting dietary diversity. The sustainable harvesting and cultivation of these plants can also enhance resilient livelihoods and reduce dependence on external food sources [4]. The unique biodiversity of Drass valley underscores the region’s ecological richness, and conserving the plant diversity of the region can play a significant role in habitat restoration, aiding in soil stabilization (Berberis orthobotrys, Bistorta vivipara) and providing shelter for diverse insect fauna (Eremurus himalaicus), thereby enhancing overall ecosystem health. The rich floral diversity of the region offers essential ecosystem services, such as attracting pollinators (Rosa webbiana, Gentianella moorcroftiana) that are vital for the productivity of both wild and cultivated crops and improving soil health through processes like nitrogen fixation (Cicer microphyllum, Chesneya cuneata) and the enhancement of organic matter. From a public health perspective, this study emphasizes the importance of integrating traditional medicine into formal healthcare systems. The people of this mountainous region rely on these phyto-medicines to address various health issues, with their effectiveness often rooted in centuries of empirical knowledge. However, threats to these resources could increase dependence on synthetic pharmaceuticals, which may be less accessible or culturally not accepted in these communities. Protecting and promoting the sustainable use of traditional medicines can enhance public health outcomes by providing affordable and culturally appropriate healthcare options. This study thus highlights the interconnectedness of environmental integrity, food security and public health in the study region.

Limitation of the study

Present study encountered some challenges as well which include, the dosage of different formulations have not been standardized and also accessing a few areas was very difficult because of security reasons. Also, there are concern of loss of this valuable knowledge due to socio-cultural transformation and ever-increasing push for economic development based on ecologically hazardous practices, as well as ethical issues surrounding intellectual property rights and informed consent. Despite these challenges affecting the research process, we have achieved the research objectives by implementing alternative strategies, such as encouraging informants to participate and taking help from local administration to reach the inaccessible areas of the study region. Our research recommends further studies on validation of medicinal plants and also for further investigation on plants used for food and energy purposes.

Conclusion

We recorded 121 plant species with traditional use in Drass valley of Ladakh, of these 35 species had medicinal properties and 86 species were used by the people of area in their day-to-day life. The plant species used as medicine were mostly herbaceous and found in rocky habitats. The medicinal plants were used to treat 27 disease categories. Twenty-two medicinal plant species had high use values, these species were considered to have potential for use in public herbal medicine. The fidelity level values identified the species which were commonly used and which diseases were the most treated. We have recorded 13 plant species with first-time ethnomedicinal claims. The traditional knowledge of plants used as food, fuel, timber, spices iwas also recoded, including sustainable harvesting methods. In contemporary world, this traditional wisdom could be leveraged to promote biodiversity conservation at local level, management of diseases during crisis times, identification of nutritious diets, alternative energy resources and economic welfare of the far-flung areas, such as Drass valley. The valuable knowledge, as generated in this study, needs to be validated scientifically in the future with due benefit sharing among local stakeholders.
