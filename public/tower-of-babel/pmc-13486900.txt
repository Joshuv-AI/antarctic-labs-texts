Accelerated biological aging predicts poor outcomes in frail ICU patients
Authors: Laptin Ho, Frederick B. Rogers, Sumeet Rai, Henrique Nicola, Wilson F. Käyser-Abdo, Abdulrahman Al-Fares, Antonios Katsounas, Nicholas Larson, Mandy H.M. Chu, Henry M.K. Wong, Esther Y.K. Cham, Kwok M. Ho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryChronological age often fails to reflect biological vulnerability in critical illness. In this prospective multinational prevalence study, we assessed whether accelerated biological aging, measured by the Levine PhenoAge model, improves prognostication among frail adults (clinical frailty scale ≥4) admitted to eight ICUs across six countries. Accelerated aging (PhenoAgeAccel) was defined as PhenoAge exceeding chronological age after regression adjustment. Among 784 ICU bed-days, 327 bed-days (41.7%) were occupied by frail patients, of whom 208 (63.6%) showed PhenoAgeAccel. Biological age outperformed chronological age for predicting 30-day mortality (AUROC 0.66 vs. 0.54). PhenoAgeAccel was associated with longer ICU stay, higher 30-day mortality (30% vs. 11%; relative risk [RR] 2.70), and greater risk of death or ICU readmission (RR 2.48), remaining significant after adjustment for frailty. Accelerated biological aging is common in frail ICU patients and may improve short-term risk stratification beyond chronological age alone.

Introduction

The Levine PhenoAge model is a biological age metric derived from nine routine clinical blood tests. Introduced in 2018,1 it has been validated as a predictor of 10-year all-cause survival, cognitive decline, and diabetes mellitus—independent of chronological age, education, socioeconomic status, and smoking habits—even in individuals over 80 years old.2,3 Recent evidence suggests that accelerated biological aging—commonly defined as biological age exceeding chronological age—is a stronger predictor of adverse outcomes in acute care settings than chronological age.4 These outcomes include mortality,5,6 ICU readmission,7 and postoperative complications after major surgery,8,9 even after adjustment for illness severity and the clinical frailty scale (CFS).10 However, these findings are limited by retrospective designs and single-country cohorts.

Frailty is common and independently associated with increased risk of long-term mortality and disability.11 Emerging evidence indicates that accelerated biological aging is strongly linked to frailty and may contribute to its development.12,13,14,15,16 Yet, the prevalence and prognostic impact of accelerated biological aging in frail ICU patients remain poorly described.13,16

In this prospective multinational study, we aimed to quantify the prevalence of accelerated biological aging in frail ICU patients using the Levine PhenoAge model.1 Accelerated biological aging (PhenoAgeAccel) was defined as a positive PhenoAge residual—that is, biological age exceeding chronological age—after calibration using PhenoAge data from the entire study cohort.6 We then examined its association with 30-day mortality. Additionally, we explored whether plasma globulin levels—a marker of chronic inflammation17,18,19—correlate with PhenoAge in critically ill patients.

Results

Prevalence of PhenoAgeAccel

Among 784 patient-bed days evaluated, 327 (41.7%; 95% confidence interval [CI], 38.2–45.3) were occupied by frail ICU patients. Of these, 208 bed days (63.6%; 95% CI, 58.1–68.8) were attributable to 95 frail ICU patients with PhenoAgeAccel (Figure S1), corresponding to prevalence of PhenoAgeAccel of 63.6% among frail ICU patient-bed days. At the patient level, 52.6% of frail ICU patients exhibited PhenoAgeAccel (95% CI, 42.1–62.7).

PhenoAge versus chronological age in predicting outcomes

Chronological age, analyzed as a continuous variable, was not associated with 30-day mortality (p = 0.559). In contrast, PhenoAge demonstrated numerically better discrimination for 30-day mortality than chronological age (area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve [AUROC] 0.66 vs. 0.54). Similarly, for the composite outcome of 30-day mortality or ICU readmission, chronological age was not predictive (AUROC: 0.53), whereas both PhenoAge (AUROC: 0.64, p = 0.022) and PhenoAge residuals (AUROC: 0.63, p = 0.035) were significant predictors. There was no significant difference in predictive performance between PhenoAge and PhenoAge residuals (p = 0.891). Incorporating PhenoAge residuals into a model including chronological age improved the net correct classification by 24% based on the net reclassification index.

Survivors exhibited minimal accelerated biological aging (median PhenoAge residuals −1.5 years, interquartile range [IQR] −14.5 to 11.6) compared with non-survivors (median +7.9 years, IQR −1.5 to 13.8; p = 0.042). Similarly, survivors without ICU readmission were biologically younger (median −2.3 years, IQR −14.6 to 8.6) than patients who died within 30 days or required ICU readmission (median +7.9 years, IQR −1.2 to 13.4; p = 0.022).

When PhenoAge residuals were dichotomized into PhenoAgeAccel versus non-PhenoAgeAccel, PhenoAgeAccel was more prevalent among males (66% vs. 44%, p = 0.035) and was associated with a greater burden of comorbidities, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (24% vs. 7%, p = 0.021), heart failure (32% vs. 7%, p = 0.002), and end-stage renal disease (14% vs. 2%, p = 0.039). Sensitivity analyses

All sensitivity analyses showed consistent direction and magnitude of association. Restricting our analysis to patients who had CFS >4 (N = 37) did not change the results materially; PhenoAgeAccel showed a trend toward an association with an increased risk of the composite of 30-day mortality or ICU readmission (aOR 8.82, 95%CI, 0.94–83.1; p = 0.057).

Using Cox proportional hazards regression while adjusting for CFS in assessing time to death or ICU readmission did not substantially change the results (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR]: 2.22, 95% CI: 0.98–5.02; p = 0.056); and CFS itself was not significant (aHR: 1.02, 95% CI: 0.64–1.62; p = 0.943). When adjusted for SOFA instead of CFS in the Cox regression, the direction and magnitude of the association between PhenoAgeAccel and death or ICU readmission remained similar (aHR: 2.05, 95% CI: 0.86–4.92; p = 0.108), while SOFA score was not significant (aHR: 1.02, 95% CI: 0.93–1.12; p = 0.629) (Figures S2 and S3).

Restricting the analysis to patients aged >65 years also did not materially change our results (CFS aHR: 0.85, 95% CI: 0.47–1.56; p = 0.607; PhenoAgeAccel aHR: 2.66, 95% CI: 0.88–7.99; p = 0.082).

Discussion

In this multinational ICU study, accelerated biological aging—measured by PhenoAgeAccel—was highly prevalent among frail ICU patients, particularly those with a greater burden of comorbidities such as heart failure and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, reinforcing its biological relevance in line with prior studies.12,13,14,15,16

Notably, chronological age alone did not distinguish between patients with and without accelerated aging (p = 0.338) and was not associated with survival (p = 0.559). In contrast, PhenoAge and related measures were significantly associated with 30-day mortality, prolonged ICU length of stay, and the composite outcome of death or readmission. The attenuation of the association with the composite outcome after adjustment for SOFA score suggests residual confounding by acute illness severity, although the association remained apparent after adjustment for infection or sepsis. Mortality risk in most living organisms increases exponentially with time; in humans, this age-associated acceleration in hazard is well described by the Gompertz-Makeham law.20,21,22 Within this framework, overall mortality risk comprises an age-independent component (A) and an age-dependent component (B·eCx), where x denotes age, B scales the baseline level of age-related mortality, and the product Cx represents the age-scaled exponent that governs the exponential increase in hazard with advancing age. Thus, larger values of C amplify the effect of each additional year of age, producing a steeper rise in mortality risk across the lifespan.

The Levine PhenoAge model is derived from a Gompertz-based proportional hazards modeling approach but does not explicitly incorporate the age-independent term of the Gompertz-Makeham law.23 Instead, PhenoAge integrates biomarker-based indicators of physiological aging to quantify inter-individual variation in age-related mortality risk beyond chronological age alone. Accelerated biological aging, as captured by PhenoAgeAccel, can therefore be conceptualized as an upward shift in the age-dependent hazard—manifesting as higher vulnerability at a given age (effectively increasing B) and/or an amplified age effect (a larger Cx), corresponding to a steeper hazard trajectory with advancing age (Figure 3).Figure 3Hypothetical impact of accelerated biological aging, measured as PhenoAgeAccel using the Levine PhenoAge model, on the Gompertz-Makeham mortality curveThe Levine PhenoAge model uses a Gompertz-based proportional hazards framework but omits the age-independent term. Consistent with this conceptualization, our findings demonstrate that biological age—but not chronological age—was associated with short-term mortality in critically ill patients. Together with prior studies linking PhenoAge to long-term health span and survival,1,2 these results support PhenoAgeAccel as a biologically meaningful marker of vulnerability that modifies both baseline risk and the pace of mortality risk acceleration. This theoretical framework provides a useful lens for interpreting how biological aging metrics, such as PhenoAgeAccel, relate to mortality risk.

If PhenoAgeAccel reflects heightened baseline vulnerability and accelerated hazard escalation, then biological processes that erode physiological reserve are likely contributors. One such process is immunosenescence—age-associated remodeling of the immune system characterized by depletion of naive lymphocyte pools, expansion of highly differentiated or exhausted T cell subsets, and impaired immune plasticity in response to stressors.19,24,25 From this mechanistic perspective, aging is accompanied by shifts in the balance of naive and exhausted CD8+ T cells, phenotypes linked to diminished immune adaptability and adverse outcomes in both aging populations and critically ill patients.26,27 Recent evidence further suggests that persistent infections, particularly cytomegalovirus (CMV), may accelerate biological aging through DNA methylation changes and immunosenescent pathways.28 Together, these processes link inflammaging, chronic subclinical infection, immune dysfunction, accelerated biological aging, and diminished physiologic resilience and bioenergetic reserve.19,25 It is therefore plausible that these interrelated mechanisms are more prevalent in individuals with PhenoAgeAccel—even when chronologically young13,16—thereby increasing both baseline mortality risk and the pace at which risk escalates during acute physiological stress such as critical illness.

The findings of this study have important implications for critical care practice. First, the biomarkers required for PhenoAge calculation are routinely measured in ICU patients (Tables 2 and 3), making its integration into clinical workflows both feasible and cost-neutral. Current prognostic models in critical care largely rely on chronological rather than biological (or “effective”) age.21 Although chronological age remains a cornerstone of ICU admission decisions and resource allocation,29,30 it is an imperfect predictor of outcomes in older adults.31 Frailty adds prognostic value; however, it can also be present in younger patients,13,15 leading to discordant risk signals when chronological age and frailty diverge.16 In addition, accelerated biological aging may be present even in non-frail individuals.16 Taken together, these observations suggest that incorporating accelerated biological aging may improve prognostic accuracy beyond chronological age alone in frail ICU patients.

Second, although our findings are observational, they suggest that biological age may represent a potentially modifiable risk factor. PhenoAgeAccel may reflect reduced physiological reserve or resilience, thereby limiting recovery from critical illness or major surgery. Unlike chronological age, which is fixed, biological age is dynamic: it may accelerate in response to disease or adverse lifestyle factors and, conversely, improve following recovery32 or targeted interventions such as exercise, nutritional support, or microbiome-based therapies.33,34,35 In surgical populations, multicomponent prehabilitation has been shown to enhance physiological reserve prior to major elective procedures, potentially reducing postoperative complications by mitigating accelerated biological aging.8,9 Whether such approaches translate into improved patient-centered outcomes remains uncertain and warrants evaluation in randomized controlled trials—an urgent priority in aging populations.

Third, contrary to our hypothesis, we did not observe a positive correlation between plasma globulin levels and PhenoAge. Prior studies have linked systemic inflammation—captured by biomarkers such as C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-10 (IL-10), interleukin-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1RA), insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), and tumor necrosis factor receptor-1 (sTNFR-1)—to epigenetic clocks, including PhenoAge, that predict long-term outcomes.36 In contrast, plasma globulin reflects total immunoglobulins and related proteins and is influenced by diverse factors, including liver disease and polyclonal immune responses. As such, it appears insufficiently specific to capture low-grade inflammaging and is unlikely to serve as a surrogate marker of biological aging in critically ill patients.

In summary, our findings indicate that accelerated biological aging, measured by PhenoAgeAccel, is prevalent among frail ICU patients and strongly associated with short-term mortality, whereas chronological age alone has limited predictive power. These results highlight the limitations of chronological age as a marker of physiological resilience and position biological age as a dynamic, clinically relevant metric. Integrating biological aging measures with frailty assessment could improve risk stratification in acute care and advance understanding of how aging biology influences critical illness outcomes. Future multicenter studies incorporating diverse aging clocks and molecular biomarkers are needed to validate these findings and explore how biological age can inform precision medicine interventions in critical care research.

Limitations of the study

Although this study spanned multiple countries, enhancing generalizability, the sample of frail ICU patients was modest. This limited statistical power to determine whether PhenoAgeAccel independently predicts mortality and ICU readmission beyond CFS or SOFA scores. We used the CFS—a simple but subjective measure of frailty—alongside a second-generation biological clock to estimate biological age. While pre-admission CFS is widely used as a routine risk parameter in many ICUs,16,31 variability in its assessment across different centers and continents may have introduced heterogeneity.

Additionally, blood tests were analyzed locally at each ICU, and measurement methods could not be standardized, potentially increasing variability in biomarker values. Given the limited number of centers and low event rate, we lacked sufficient power to include study site as a random effect in multivariable models to formally account for this heterogeneity. We used a 30-day mortality endpoint; however, a longer follow-up period might better capture between-group differences.

Finally, because PhenoAgeAccel was not assessed in non-frail ICU patients, the prevalence of accelerated biological aging across the broader critically ill population remains uncertain. Future larger studies including all critically ill patients and incorporating multiple frailty and biological aging measures could improve both internal and external validity, although feasibility in this population remains challenging.

Experimental model and study participant details

This was a prospective, multinational prevalence study involving eight ICUs across Hong Kong, Australia, Germany, the Netherlands, the USA, and Kuwait. The study was approved by the institutional review board or ethics committee at each participating center. Data collection spanned five consecutive days, beginning on 22 September 2025.

Method details

Screening, enrollment, and study outcomes (Figure S1)

All patients admitted during the study period (Monday 08:00 to Saturday 08:00), as well as those already present at 08:00 on day 1, were screened for eligibility by site investigators or their delegates. A CFS score of 4 corresponds to being vulnerable or very mildly frail, 5 to mildly frail, 6 to moderately frail, 7 to severely frail, 8 to very severely frail, and 9 to terminally ill.37 The primary outcome was the prevalence of PhenoAgeAccel among frail ICU patients.

Secondary outcomes included identifying risk factors for PhenoAgeAccel and determining whether PhenoAgeAccel was associated with an increased risk of poor hospital outcomes, such as prolonged ICU stay, ICU readmission, discharge to a nursing home, or death within 30 days. Additionally, plasma globulin levels (or total protein levels) were measured to assess their association with PhenoAgeAccel. Because this study had no external funding, accelerated biological aging was assessed exclusively using the Levine PhenoAge model, as all required biomarkers are routinely measured in ICU patients. PhenoAge was estimated using blood tests obtained on ICU admission, or at the time of enrolment if admission laboratory data were unavailable. All samples were processed locally at each participating center.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Sample size and statistical analysis

Our previous work demonstrated that accelerated biological aging was present in over 55% of critically ill frail adults and in more than 65% of non-survivors.6,16 To ensure adequate precision under a potentially lower prevalence in this ICU cohort, we conservatively assumed a prevalence of 40%, corresponding to a 95% CI of 30% to 50%. Achieving a narrower CI would have required a substantially larger sample size than was feasible.

The prevalence of PhenoAgeAccel was primarily estimated using the proportion of ICU bed-days occupied by frail patients with PhenoAgeAccel over the 5-day study period. ICU bed-days are a relevant health economic metric; a proportion attributable to patients with PhenoAgeAccel, may indicate potential benefits of pre-ICU interventions targeting biological aging. Because patient admissions and discharges occurred throughout the study period, resulting in daily fluctuations in patient counts, we also reported the proportion of frail ICU patients who exhibited PhenoAgeAccel.

A two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant, without adjustment for multiple comparisons.

The discriminative ability of chronological age and PhenoAge for predicting 30-day mortality was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC). Improvement in risk classification was assessed using the net reclassification index (NRI), comparing a base-model including chronological age alone with a model incorporating chronological age and PhenoAge residuals. The mean predicted risk of 30% for the composite outcome of death or ICU readmission was used as the cut point.

In this study, PhenoAgeAccel was defined as the positive residual from a linear regression of PhenoAge on chronological age, thereby identifying individuals who were biologically older than expected for their chronological age. By design, this approach mitigates the influence of acute illness–related biomarker perturbations and recalibrates the Levine PhenoAge model for the ICU setting (Figures S4 and S5).6

For example, the mean plasma creatinine concentration in the cohort was 144 μmol/L, elevated creatinine alone did not result in PhenoAgeAccel unless it exceeded the level expected for this population. Thus, patients with creatinine values above the normal range (105 μmol/L) were not necessarily classified as having PhenoAgeAccel; rather, classification required deviation beyond the cohort-expected level (Figure S6). Similarly, upper interquartile range values for several PhenoAge components among patients without PhenoAgeAccel (e.g., white cell count, C-reactive protein, glucose, and albumin) exceeded standard reference ranges, reflecting the use of PhenoAge residuals to define PhenoAgeAccel (Table 3).

We then assessed the associations between PhenoAgeAccel and 30-day mortality or ICU readmission during the same hospitalization using logistic regression models. Models were adjusted separately for the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS),37 sepsis/infection, or the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score to minimize overadjustment and collinearity.38

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the findings. Because the CFS may be less reliable in patients younger than 65 years, we performed an analysis restricted to patients aged ≥65 years (N = 62, 65.3%). Additional analyses were limited to patients with a CFS score >4. Finally, multivariable analyses were repeated using Cox proportional hazards models to evaluate consistency when accounting for time to the first adverse event.
