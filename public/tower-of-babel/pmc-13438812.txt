Metabolic stress exacerbates Parkinson’s disease pathology through mitochondrial dysfunction and ferroptosis
Authors: Yanyan Zheng, Huan Huang, Sisi Wang, Feiyu Ding, Wenyuan Wang, Wenjia Wang, Chen Gao, Quan Zhang, Minyan Ye, Qiang Li, Zhongpeng Dai, Liuxi Chu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEpidemiological studies have linked type 2 diabetes to Parkinson’s disease (PD), but causality and mechanisms remain unclear. Here, using Mendelian randomization, we identify genetically elevated 2‑hour post‑load glucose as a causal risk factor for PD. To investigate the underlying biology, we developed a mouse model combining glucose intolerance with MPTP‑induced dopaminergic injury. Glucose intolerance markedly exacerbated motor and cognitive deficits, dopaminergic neuron loss, and α‑synuclein accumulation. Mechanistically, hyperglycemia synergized with MPTP to impair mitochondrial respiration, collapse membrane potential, and disrupt cristae architecture. These changes were accompanied by iron overload, glutathione depletion, lipid peroxidation, downregulation of the ferroptosis suppressor GPX4, and upregulation of the pro‑ferroptosis enzyme ACSL4–a signature of ferroptosis. The same pathway was activated in human dopaminergic neurons exposed to high glucose and MPP⁺. Our findings establish that glucose intolerance directly aggravates PD pathology via mitochondrial dysfunction and ferroptosis, suggesting that targeting this cell death pathway or improving glycemic control could offer therapeutic opportunities for PD, especially in patients with metabolic comorbidities.Subject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder worldwide, characterized by progressive loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra and pathological accumulation of α‑synuclein1–5. Despite decades of research, the etiology of PD remains incompletely understood, and no disease‑modifying therapy is currently available. Epidemiological studies have identified a wide range of putative risk factors for PD, including lifestyle behaviors (e.g., smoking, alcohol consumption, physical inactivity), dietary patterns, and metabolic conditions such as obesity, dyslipidemia, and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM)6–8. Among these, disturbances in glucose metabolism have attracted particular interest because they are common, potentially modifiable, and linked to multiple neurodegenerative processes7,9,10.

Observational studies have reported inconsistent associations between glycemic traits and PD risk11–13. Some large‑scale cohort studies suggest that T2DM or elevated blood glucose levels increase the risk of developing PD11–13, whereas others have found no significant association or even inverse relationships14,15. These discrepancies may arise from confounding, reverse causation, and measurement errors inherent to conventional observational designs. Therefore, whether abnormal glucose metabolism plays a causal role in PD pathogenesis remains an open question.

To address this gap, we first leveraged the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2023 study to characterize the contemporary epidemiological landscape of PD, including temporal trends, regional disparities, and projections to 2050. The GBD analysis provides a comprehensive picture of the rising global burden of PD, underscoring the urgent need to identify modifiable risk factors that could inform prevention strategies. Subsequently, we performed two‑sample Mendelian randomization (MR) using genetic variants as instrumental variables to evaluate the causal effects of a broad spectrum of metabolic and lifestyle exposures on PD risk. MR is less susceptible to confounding and reverse causation than observational studies, thus offering stronger evidence for causal inference16. Among the traits examined, genetically predicted higher 2‑hour post‑load glucose (2hGlu) emerged as a significant risk‑enhancing factor for PD, whereas several adiposity‑related phenotypes showed inverse associations.

Based on this causal clue from MR, we hypothesized that chronic hyperglycemia or glucose intolerance directly exacerbates PD pathology. To test this hypothesis and explore underlying mechanisms, we established a mouse model combining high‑fat diet/streptozotocin‑induced glucose intolerance with MPTP‑induced subacute PD. In this model, we assessed motor and cognitive behaviors, dopaminergic neuron integrity, and mitochondrial function. Furthermore, we conducted in vitro experiments using SH‑SY5Y cells exposed to high glucose and MPP⁺ to validate the direct effects of hyperglycemia on dopaminergic neurons. Our results demonstrate that glucose intolerance aggravates PD‑like phenotypes through mitochondrial dysfunction and ferroptosis, a newly recognized form of regulated cell death driven by lipid peroxidation.

In summary, this study integrates epidemiological burden analysis, genetic causal inference, and mechanistic validation in vivo and in vitro. By focusing on 2hGlu as a modifiable risk factor, we provide convergent evidence that impaired glucose homeostasis contributes to PD pathogenesis via mitochondria‑dependent ferroptosis. These findings may inform future preventive strategies targeting glycemic control and open new avenues for neuroprotective interventions.

Results

Global burden analysis

From 1990 to 2023, the global burden of PD increased markedly (Table 1). The total number of DALYs and deaths attributable to PD approximately tripled over this period, reflecting population growth and population aging. Although age-standardized DALY and mortality rates rose more modestly, they still showed a consistent upward trend (EAPC for DALYs: 0.35% [95% CI: 0.31–0.38]; EAPC for deaths: 0.34% [95% CI: 0.29–0.38]). Incidence and prevalence also increased substantially in both absolute and age-standardized terms, indicating that PD is becoming more common globally. By contrast, age-standardized YLD and YLL rates were relatively stable or slightly declining, despite large increases in absolute YLDs and YLLs, suggesting that part of the observed growth in burden is driven by demographic change rather than worsening rates alone. Differences in PD burden across age and sex

Across the study period, the burden of PD rose steeply with age and was concentrated in middle-aged and older adults (Fig. 2A, B). The greatest absolute numbers of DALYs, incidence, and prevalent cases were observed in individuals aged 50–59 years, with a continued high burden into older age groups. Age-standardized rates in these age bands showed modest but consistent increases over time, with small positive EAPCs, indicating that PD burden in middle-aged populations has grown beyond what would be expected from demographic change alone. Mortality rates were highest in older age groups and were generally stable or slightly declining, with some age bands showing small negative EAPCs for death rates.

Sex-stratified analyses showed that men consistently experienced a higher PD burden than women across all metrics and years (Table 2). Differences in PD across SDI levels

When stratified by Socio-demographic Index (SDI), high-SDI countries bore the largest absolute burden of PD throughout the study period, with the highest DALYs and prevalence (Fig. 3A, B). However, their age-standardized DALY rates showed only modest increases over time (EAPC around 0.3), suggesting relatively stable rates in the context of aging populations.

Distribution of PD -related indicators across SDI regions in GBD 2023 data.

A and B show the variation in PD burden across different SDI regions, with A illustrating age-standardized rates (DALYs deaths, and incidence) and B displaying the number of cases.

In contrast, low- and middle-SDI regions, although starting from lower absolute levels of PD burden, experienced more rapid relative growth. Low-SDI countries showed the highest EAPC in age-standardized DALY rates (around 0.9), followed by middle-SDI regions (EAPC approximately 0.5), indicating accelerating PD burden in settings with fewer resources and more limited capacity for diagnosis and management. High-middle SDI regions exhibited substantial increases in absolute DALYs but a slight decline in age-standardized rates, reflecting complex interactions between demographic change and evolving risk profiles.

Overall, these patterns highlight a dual challenge: sustained high burden in high-SDI settings and faster proportional growth in low- and middle-SDI regions, emphasizing the need for context-specific prevention and care strategies across the SDI spectrum.

Forecasted risk factors for PD

The ARIMA forecasts for PD risk factors from 2024 to 2050 indicate a steady increase in disease burden for both sexes. For females, DALYs are projected to rise significantly from approximately 3,550,610 in 2024 to 6,738,893 by 2050. Similarly, males are expected to experience an increase in DALYs, from about 4,637,450 to 8,426,923 during the same period (Fig. 4A). Prevalence is forecasted to escalate significantly, with female cases increasing from about 5,674,828 to 12,370,300, and male cases rising from 6,373,155 to 11,644,996 between 2024 and 2050 (Fig. 4B). Projected death counts also demonstrate an upward trend, with female deaths expected to rise from around 191,913 in 2024 to 355,152 in 2050. Males are projected to see an increase in deaths from approximately 248,561 to 444,534 (Fig. 4C). YLDs are also projected to rise, with females expected to increase from 786,078 to 1,700,675, and males from about 896,370 to 1,635,250 over the forecast period (Fig. 4D). Incidence rates for PD are anticipated to grow as well, with females increasing from roughly 632,453 cases in 2024 to nearly 1,429,697 cases by 2050, while males are expected to rise from about 788,239 to over 1,402,637 cases (Fig. 4E). Similarly, YLLs are predicted to rise, with females increasing from roughly 2,769,447 to 5,170,949, and males from about 3,742,082 to 6,850,862 (Fig. 4F). These forecasts highlight a concerning trend in the growing burden of PD, emphasizing the need for enhanced public health strategies to address the anticipated increases in both incidence and prevalence across gender.

Influence of risk factors on the PD burden

Mendelian randomization (IVW, fixed effects) was employed to evaluate causal effects of various exposures on Parkinson’s disease (PD) risk (Fig. 5). Notably, 2hGlu (2‑hour glucose) emerged as the strongest positive risk factor (Beta = 0.428, OR = 1.534, 95% CI: 1.055–2.232, p = 0.025), suggesting that postprandial hyperglycemia may substantially increase PD susceptibility. Regarding smoking-related factors, smoking initiation (OR = 1.380) and lifetime smoking (OR = 1.194) were associated with increased PD risk, whereas smoking cessation showed a protective effect (OR = 0.688). For anthropometric measures, waist circumference (OR = 1.333) and visceral adiposity (OR = 1.359) were positively associated with PD risk, while adjusted BMI unexpectedly exhibited an inverse association (OR = 0.789). Collectively, these findings highlight 2hGlu as a key metabolic indicator and reveal complex, non‑uniform relationships between smoking, body composition, and PD etiology. Establishment and validation of impaired glucose tolerance and subacute Parkinson’s disease models

Given the MR findings that 2hGlu was the strongest positive risk factor for PD, To investigate the impact of impaired glucose tolerance on PD, we next sought to experimentally validate the causal impact of impaired glucose tolerance on PD pathogenesis using animal models.

To investigate the impact of impaired glucose tolerance on PD, we first established a stable mouse model of type 2 diabetes–like metabolic dysfunction. As illustrated in Fig. 6A, C57BL/6J mice were fed a high-fat diet (HFD) for 4 weeks, followed by intraperitoneal administration of low-dose streptozotocin (STZ, 40 mg/kg) for five consecutive days to induce impaired glucose tolerance. Seven days after the final STZ injection, an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) was performed. As shown in Fig. 6B, C, compared with control mice, the HFD + STZ group exhibited significantly elevated fasting blood glucose levels as well as markedly increased glucose levels at all time points during OGTT. Notably, the 2 h post-load glucose level exceeded 16.7 mmol/L (p < 0.001), indicating successful establishment of impaired glucose tolerance.

Metabolic cage analysis further revealed that both total energy expenditure and nocturnal energy expenditure were significantly increased in HFD + STZ mice compared with controls (Fig. 6D, E, p < 0.01), suggesting a compensatory elevation in metabolic rate. In parallel, serum insulin levels and the homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) were markedly elevated in the HFD + STZ group (Fig. 6F, G, p < 0.01), confirming the presence of hyperinsulinemia and insulin resistance.

In parallel, a subacute PD model was established by intraperitoneal injection of MPTP (20 mg/kg) for five consecutive days. The experimental design and behavioral assessment workflow are shown in Fig. 6H. Behavioral tests conducted 7 days after the final MPTP injection demonstrated that MPTP-treated mice exhibited a significantly reduced latency to fall in the rotarod test (p < 0.001), decreased total locomotor activity in the open field test (p < 0.01), and prolonged descent time in the pole test (p < 0.001) compared with controls (Fig. 6I–K), indicating robust motor dysfunction. Immunohistochemical analysis further showed a marked reduction in tyrosine hydroxylase (TH)-positive neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta, along with decreased TH-positive fiber density in the striatum in MPTP-treated mice (Fig. 6L, p < 0.001), confirming dopaminergic neurodegeneration.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that HFD + STZ and MPTP treatments successfully establish models of impaired glucose tolerance and subacute PD, respectively, thereby providing a robust experimental foundation for subsequent mechanistic investigations.

Motor function assessments revealed that, compared with the Control group, PD mice exhibited significantly reduced rotarod latency, decreased total locomotor activity in the open field test, and prolonged descent time in the pole test (all p < 0.01). Notably, these motor deficits were further aggravated in DM + PD mice, as evidenced by shorter rotarod latency (p < 0.01), reduced total distance traveled (p < 0.05), and prolonged pole test time (p < 0.05) compared with the PD group (Fig. 7C, E, G), indicating that diabetic status markedly exacerbates MPTP-induced motor dysfunction.

Cognitive performance was also impaired. In the Y-maze test, PD mice showed reduced time spent and percentage of distance traveled in the novel arm relative to controls (p < 0.05), while DM + PD mice exhibited further declines compared with PD mice (Fig. 7I–J, p < 0.05). Consistently, in the novel object recognition test, the discrimination index was significantly decreased in PD mice versus controls (p < 0.01) and further reduced in DM + PD mice (p < 0.05) (Fig. 7L, M). Pearson correlation analysis revealed a significant negative correlation between 2 h glucose levels and discrimination index (r = −0.54, p < 0.01; Fig. 7N), suggesting that the severity of glucose intolerance is directly associated with cognitive impairment.

Pathological analysis of dopaminergic neurodegeneration showed that TH-positive neurons in the substantia nigra were markedly reduced in PD mice compared with controls (p < 0.001), and further decreased in DM + PD mice (p < 0.01) (Fig. 7O). Immunoblot analysis confirmed a reduction in TH protein levels in PD mice (p < 0.01), with a further decline in DM + PD mice (p < 0.05). In contrast, total α-synuclein (α-Syn) levels were significantly elevated in DM + PD mice compared with PD mice (p < 0.05), indicating enhanced α-Syn accumulation under diabetic conditions (Fig. 7P, Q).

To elucidate the underlying mechanisms, we examined ferroptosis- and mitochondria-related pathways. Heatmap analysis showed that, relative to PD mice, DM + PD mice exhibited further downregulation of ferroptosis-suppressive genes (Gpx4, Slc7a11) and mitochondrial biogenesis regulators (Ppargc1a, Nrf1, Tfam), alongside upregulation of the pro-ferroptotic gene Acsl4 (Fig. 7R). Consistently, immunoblotting demonstrated reduced GPX4 expression (p < 0.05) and increased ACSL4 expression (p < 0.01) in DM + PD mice compared with PD mice (Fig. 7S, T).

Transmission electron microscopy revealed progressive mitochondrial damage across groups: mitochondria in control mice displayed intact morphology and well-defined cristae, whereas PD mice showed mild swelling and cristae disruption. In contrast, DM + PD mice exhibited severe mitochondrial abnormalities, including cristae loss and outer membrane rupture, accompanied by characteristic features of ferroptosis (Fig. 7U). Biochemical assays further demonstrated increased iron and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels (both P < 0.05), along with decreased glutathione (GSH) content (p < 0.01) in DM + PD mice compared with PD mice (Fig. 7V–X).

Collectively, these findings indicate that diabetic status markedly exacerbates MPTP-induced PD phenotypes, at least in part by promoting ferroptosis and mitochondrial dysfunction.

Molecular mechanisms underlying diabetes-aggravated Parkinson’s disease: mitochondrial dysfunction and ferroptosis

The in vivo findings above indicate that diabetic status exacerbates PD pathology by promoting ferroptosis and inducing mitochondrial dysfunction. To further determine the direct effects of hyperglycemia on dopaminergic neurons while minimizing the influence of systemic complexity, we established an in vitro model using human neuroblastoma SH-SY5Y cells. As illustrated in Fig. 8A, cells were assigned to four groups: control, high glucose (25 mM), MPP⁺ (500 μM; the active metabolite of MPTP), and high glucose combined with MPP⁺ (HG + MPP⁺), to mimic the diabetic PD microenvironment.

Mitochondrial morphology analysis (Fig. 8B) showed that control cells exhibited elongated mitochondria with intact cristae. In contrast, both high-glucose and MPP⁺ treatment induced mild mitochondrial swelling and partial cristae disruption. Notably, the HG + MPP⁺ group displayed the most severe structural damage, characterized by marked cristae loss, outer membrane rupture, vacuolization, and classic ferroptotic features, including mitochondrial shrinkage and disappearance of cristae.

Consistently, mitochondrial respiratory function (Fig. 8C) demonstrated that maximal respiratory capacity was significantly reduced in both high-glucose and MPP⁺ groups compared with controls (both p < 0.05), and was further decreased in the HG + MPP⁺ group relative to the MPP⁺ group (p < 0.01), indicating a synergistic impairment of oxidative phosphorylation.

Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) was assessed using JC-1 staining. As shown in Fig. 8D–F, control cells predominantly exhibited red fluorescence (J-aggregates), indicative of high membrane potential. Both high-glucose and MPP⁺ treatments significantly reduced the red/green fluorescence ratio (both p < 0.05), while the HG + MPP⁺ group showed a further decline compared with the MPP⁺ group (p < 0.01), suggesting aggravated mitochondrial depolarization under hyperglycemic conditions.

Ferroptosis-associated biochemical analyses (Fig. 8G–J) revealed that, compared with the MPP⁺ group, HG + MPP⁺ treatment significantly increased intracellular malondialdehyde (MDA) levels and iron accumulation (both p < 0.05), while markedly decreasing glutathione (GSH) content and the GSH/GSSG ratio (both p < 0.05). Immunoblotting further confirmed that GPX4 expression was significantly downregulated (p < 0.05), whereas ACSL4 expression was upregulated (p < 0.01) in the HG + MPP⁺ group compared with the MPP⁺ group (Fig. 8K, L).

Collectively, these in vitro results are highly consistent with the in vivo findings and demonstrate that a hyperglycemic environment directly aggravates MPP⁺-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and ferroptosis in dopaminergic neurons, thereby exacerbating PD-like pathological injury.

Discussion

In this study, we combined global epidemiological burden analysis, Mendelian randomization, and experimental validation to investigate the role of glucose intolerance in Parkinson’s disease pathogenesis. Our MR analysis identified genetically elevated 2‑hour post‑load glucose (2hGlu) as a potential causal risk factor for PD. Guided by this finding, we established a mouse model of glucose intolerance combined with MPTP‑induced subacute PD and demonstrated that diabetic status significantly exacerbates motor and cognitive deficits, dopaminergic neuron loss, and α‑synuclein pathology. Mechanistically, we found that hyperglycemia aggravates PD through mitochondrial dysfunction and ferroptosis, as evidenced by impaired mitochondrial respiration, loss of membrane potential, ultrastructural damage, and increased lipid peroxidation together with downregulation of GPX4 and upregulation of ACSL4. These observations were further corroborated in SH‑SY5Y cells exposed to high glucose and MPP⁺, confirming that hyperglycemia directly promotes dopaminergic neuron injury via the same pathways.

In this comprehensive analysis of PD from 1990 to 2023, complemented by forecasts to 2050 and MR of putative risk factors, we demonstrate a substantial and continuing escalation in the global PD burden, alongside marked regional, demographic, and socioeconomic heterogeneity. Overall DALYs and deaths have increased roughly threefold since 1990, and age-standardized rates have also risen, indicating that this expansion cannot be explained by population aging alone17–19. While longer life expectancy remains a major driver, the positive EAPCs for DALYs and mortality suggest additional contributions from changing environmental and lifestyle exposures, improved recognition and diagnosis, and potential diagnostic drift20. These trends collectively point to PD as an increasingly important contributor to global neurological disability and mortality.

At the same time, the rise in PD burden is unevenly distributed across world regions and SDI strata. High-SDI settings still carry the largest absolute burden, largely due to older population structures and greater diagnostic capacity, whereas many low- and middle-SDI regions are experiencing the fastest proportional increases in PD incidence, prevalence, and disability21–24. This pattern likely reflects converging demographic transitions, evolving exposure profiles (e.g., urbanization and industrialization), and gradual improvements in detection. The combination of sustained high burden in affluent settings and accelerating growth where specialist services and dopaminergic therapies are least available underscores a widening equity gap and signals mounting pressure on health systems worldwide25. These findings highlight the urgency of context-specific strategies for prevention, earlier detection, and capacity-building for long-term PD care.

Our MR results add to a growing body of genetic evidence supporting a causal relationship between disordered glucose metabolism and PD risk26,27. Unlike conventional observational studies that are susceptible to confounding and reverse causation, MR uses germline genetic variants as instrumental variables, thereby providing more robust estimates of causal effects. The positive association between genetically predicted 2hGlu and PD risk is consistent with large‑scale meta‑analyses linking type 2 diabetes to increased PD incidence11–13 and with clinical observations that poor glycemic control is associated with faster motor progression28,29. Nevertheless, the magnitude of the effect observed in our MR analysis was modest, and the confidence intervals were relatively wide, which may reflect the polygenic nature of glucose metabolism traits and the complexity of their influence on neurodegeneration. Importantly, MR estimates reflect lifelong exposure to elevated glucose levels, offering a distinct advantage over snapshot measurements in observational studies.

The translational relevance of this genetic finding is strongly supported by our in vivo experiments. Mice with glucose intolerance induced by a high‑fat diet and low‑dose STZ showed markedly worse MPTP‑induced Parkinsonian phenotypes compared with normoglycemic PD mice. Notably, the exacerbation was not limited to motor dysfunction but also extended to cognitive deficits, as assessed by Y‑maze and novel object recognition tests. These results align with clinical evidence that diabetic PD patients experience faster cognitive decline and a higher risk of dementia30. The correlation between 2hGlu levels and discrimination index further suggests a dose‑dependent relationship between glycemic severity and cognitive impairment.

At the mechanistic level, our study provides the first experimental evidence, to our knowledge, that hyperglycemia exacerbates PD pathology by triggering mitochondrial dysfunction and ferroptosis. Ferroptosis is an iron‑dependent form of regulated cell death driven by lethal lipid peroxidation, and it has recently been implicated in various neurodegenerative diseases, including PD31–34. However, whether and how diabetic metabolic stress promotes ferroptosis in dopaminergic neurons has remained unclear. Our data show that in both mouse substantia nigra and SH‑SY5Y cells, high glucose combined with MPTP/MPP⁺ treatment leads to severe mitochondrial damage: reduced oxygen consumption rate, decreased membrane potential, and ultrastructural abnormalities such as cristae loss and outer membrane rupture. These mitochondrial alterations are accompanied by iron accumulation, GSH depletion, MDA elevation, downregulation of GPX4, and upregulation of ACSL4–a classic ferroptosis signature. The fact that these changes were more pronounced in the DM + PD group than in the PD group, and were recapitulated in vitro, strongly suggests that hyperglycemia sensitizes dopaminergic neurons to ferroptotic cell death.

The interplay between mitochondrial dysfunction and ferroptosis is likely bidirectional. Mitochondria are both major sources of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and critical regulators of iron homeostasis. Impaired electron transport chain activity, as observed in our Seahorse assays, leads to increased mitochondrial ROS production, which can initiate lipid peroxidation. Simultaneously, loss of mitochondrial membrane potential may trigger release of labile iron from iron‑sulfur clusters and heme, fueling Fenton chemistry and amplifying oxidative damage. The downregulation of GPX4, a phospholipid hydroperoxidase that requires glutathione to reduce lipid peroxides, represents a final common pathway that permits ferroptosis execution. In the context of hyperglycemia, chronic oxidative stress and advanced glycation end products may further compromise GPX4 expression or activity, creating a permissive environment for ferroptosis.

Our findings have potential clinical implications. First, they support the notion that strict glycemic control might be beneficial not only for preventing diabetic complications but also for modifying PD progression in patients with coexisting glucose intolerance. Although large randomized trials are lacking, observational studies have suggested that metformin use is associated with reduced PD risk in diabetic populations35. Interestingly, in a preliminary arm of our study (data not shown), metformin treatment partially reversed the exacerbated phenotypes in DM + PD mice, consistent with a protective effect via improvement of mitochondrial function and suppression of ferroptosis. Second, our results highlight GPX4 and ACSL4 as potential therapeutic targets for breaking the ferroptosis‑mitochondrial dysfunction vicious cycle in PD, particularly in patients with metabolic comorbidities.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Although MR provides evidence for causality, it cannot fully exclude horizontal pleiotropy, and our findings on 2hGlu require replication in larger, multi‑ancestry GWAS datasets. The animal model used – HFD/STZ combined with MPTP – recapitulates some features of glucose intolerance and acute dopaminergic injury, but does not fully reflect the chronic, progressive nature of human PD or the long‑term effects of diabetes. Additionally, MPTP primarily damages the nigrostriatal pathway without inducing α‑synuclein aggregation in the same manner as genetic or aging‑related models. Future studies using α‑synuclein preformed fibril injection or transgenic models (e.g., A53T) in diabetic backgrounds would be valuable. The in vitro experiments used SH‑SY5Y cells, a neuroblastoma line that differs from primary human dopaminergic neurons; confirmatory studies in human induced pluripotent stem cell‑derived neurons are warranted. Finally, our mechanistic focus on ferroptosis does not exclude other pathways, such as neuroinflammation, impaired autophagy, or insulin signaling deficits, which may also contribute to the observed phenotype.

In summary, this study provides convergent genetic and experimental evidence that glucose intolerance causally exacerbates Parkinson’s disease pathology. Mendelian randomization identifies elevated 2‑hour post‑load glucose as a risk factor for PD. Mechanistically, hyperglycemia aggravates dopaminergic neuron loss, motor and cognitive deficits, and α‑synuclein accumulation by inducing mitochondrial dysfunction and ferroptosis, both in vivo and in vitro. These findings establish a direct link between disordered glucose metabolism and PD, and suggest that targeting ferroptosis or improving glycemic control may offer new therapeutic avenues for PD, particularly in patients with metabolic comorbidities.

Methods

Data source

This study draws upon data from the GBD 2023 Study. The GBD 2023 database encompasses cross-sectional global data collected from 1990 to 2023, covering 204 countries and territories. It evaluates the burden of 371 diseases and injuries, including PD. For these conditions, data are categorized by sex (male, female, total population), age groups (40 years and older), geographic region (204 countries and territories), and sociodemographic index (SDI). The ICD-10 codes used for PD are G20. Data sources include census records, household surveys, vital registration systems, hospital records, and disease registries, all compiled and stored within the Global Health Data Exchange (GHDx).

Estimated risk factors

The temporal trends of disease rates were quantified using the Estimated Annual Percent Change (EAPC), which provides a summary measure of the average rate of change over the study period. The EAPC was calculated based on log-linear regression models fitted to the age-standardized rates (e.g., incidence, prevalence, mortality, Disability-Adjusted Life Years [DALYs]), with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). This approach allows for assessing the direction and magnitude of trends over time.

Socio-demographic index

The Socio-Demographic Index (SDI) is a composite measure that captures the social and economic factors influencing health outcomes across different regions36. It is calculated as the geometric mean of three components: the total fertility rate among individuals under 25 years old (TFU25), the average education level of those aged 15 and older (EDU15 + ), and the lag-distributed income per capita (LDI). In this GBD 2023 study, the SDI values were multiplied by 100 to produce a scale that ranges from 0 to 100.

Auto Regressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) analysis

In this study, we utilized the ARIMA model to project future DALYs related to PD from 2022 to 2050. ARIMA is a widely used time series analysis method that models data based on its own past values and past errors, providing reliable short- and long-term forecasts. These projections offer valuable insights for public health planning and the development of preventive strategies.

Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis

We employed a two-sample MR approach to evaluate the causal associations between a broad spectrum of risk factors and PD. Summary statistics for GWAS were sourced from large-scale European-ancestry studies. These datasets encompassed traits related to education, intelligence, smoking behaviors (e.g., age of initiation, lifetime smoking, peer smoking, cessation, initiation), alcohol consumption (frequency, use disorder, drinks per week), coffee intake, sleep characteristics (chronotype, insomnia, sleep duration), physical activity (moderate to vigorous activity, strenuous exercises, sedentary behavior), dietary factors (e.g., cereal, vegetable, fish intake, processed meat, cheese, fruit intake), obesity-related traits (body mass index [BMI], birth weight, childhood BMI, waist and hip circumference, waist-to-hip ratio, body fat percentage, visceral adiposity), serum biomarkers (ferritin, transferrin, iron, zinc, magnesium, glucose), and other exposures.

Instrumental variables were selected as genome-wide significant (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸) SNPs associated with each exposure, pruned for linkage disequilibrium (r² < 0.01 within 10,000 kb). The number of variants used per trait is specified in the results tables. MR analyses primarily employed the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method with both fixed-effects and random-effects models to account for heterogeneity, evaluated via Cochran’s Q test. In cases of significant heterogeneity, median-based methods (weighted median and simple median) were used for robustness. Pleiotropy was assessed through the MR-Egger intercept test, with a non-significant intercept indicating no directional pleiotropy. To further detect and correct for horizontal pleiotropy and outliers, MR-PRESSO was applied. Multiple testing correction was performed using the Bonferroni method, with significance thresholds adjusted accordingly.

The causal direction was validated using the MR Steiger test, which compares the variance explained in the exposure versus the outcome, supporting the assumed causal pathway. All analyses were implemented in R using the Two Sample MR package and supplementary functions for pleiotropy and heterogeneity assessments. Effect estimates are scaled per standard deviation increase in genetically predicted risk factors. This integrated approach, combining multiple sensitivity analyses, ensured robust and reliable inference regarding the causal effects of these risk factors on PD.

Animals and housing

Male C57BL/6J mice (8 weeks old, weighing 20–22 g) were obtained from Weitong Lihua Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions at 22 ± 2 °C with a 12 h light/dark cycle (lights on at 7:00 AM) and free access to standard chow and water. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Wenzhou Medical University (No: SYXK(Zhe)-2021-0020) and conformed to the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Induction of glucose intolerance (Type 2 Diabetes-like model)

Mice were fed a high-fat diet (60% kcal from fat, D12492, Research Diets) for 4 weeks, followed by intraperitoneal injection of low-dose streptozotocin (STZ, 40 mg/kg, freshly dissolved in 0.1 M citrate buffer, pH 4.5) for 5 consecutive days. Control mice received an equal volume of citrate buffer. One week after the last STZ injection, an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) was performed. After 6 h fasting, blood glucose levels were measured at 0, 30, 60, and 120 min following oral glucose administration (2 g/kg). Mice with fasting blood glucose ≥ 11.1 mM or 2 h blood glucose ≥ 16.7 mM were considered glucose-intolerant and used for subsequent experiments.

MPTP-induced subacute Parkinson’s disease model

Mice received intraperitoneal injections of 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP, 20 mg/kg, Sigma-Aldrich) once daily for 5 consecutive days. Control mice received an equal volume of saline. Behavioral tests were performed at days 7 and 21 after the last MPTP injection. For the diabetes + PD group (DM + PD), mice were first subjected to the glucose intolerance protocol as described above and then received MPTP injections.

Experimental groups

Mice were randomly assigned to the following groups (n = 10–12 per group for behavioral and histological analyses; n = 6 per group for molecular and biochemical assays): Control: normal diet + citrate buffer + saline, PD: normal diet + citrate buffer + MPTP, and DM + PD: HFD + STZ + MPTP.

Metabolic cage analysis

Energy expenditure and respiratory exchange ratio (RER) were measured using a comprehensive laboratory animal monitoring system (CLAMS, Columbus Instruments). Mice were individually housed in metabolic cages for 48 h after a 24 h acclimation period. Data were collected every 15 min, and average values for light and dark phases were calculated.

Serum Insulin and HOMA-IR

Blood samples were collected from the tail vein after 6 h of fasting. Serum insulin levels were measured using a mouse insulin ELISA kit (Crystal Chem). Homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) was calculated as: fasting blood glucose (mmol/L) × fasting insulin (mU/L) / 22.5.

Behavioral tests

All behavioral tests were conducted during the light phase (9:00 AM–2:00 PM) by an investigator blinded to group allocation. Mice were habituated to the testing room for at least 1 h before each test.

Rotarod test

Mice were placed on a rotating rod (4–40 rpm over 5 min, Ugo Basile). The latency to fall was recorded. Each mouse underwent three trials, and the average latency was calculated.

Open field test

Mice were placed in a square arena (40 × 40 × 40 cm) and allowed to explore freely for 10 min. Total distance traveled was recorded and analyzed using EthoVision XT (Noldus).

Pole test

A rough-surfaced vertical pole (50 cm height, 1 cm diameter) was placed in the home cage. Mice were placed head-up near the top, and the time to turn completely downward (turn time) and to descend to the base (descent time) were recorded.

Y-maze test

The maze consisted of three arms (40 × 10 × 20 cm each) at 120° angles. Mice were placed in the center and allowed to explore freely for 8 minutes. The percentage of spontaneous alternation (consecutive entries into three different arms) was calculated.

Novel object recognition (NOR) test

The test consisted of a habituation phase (5 min exploration of empty arena), a training phase (10 min exposure to two identical objects), and a test phase (5 min exposure to one familiar and one novel object). The discrimination index was calculated as (time with novel − time with familiar) / total exploration time.

Immunohistochemistry

Mice were deeply anesthetized and transcardially perfused with ice-cold PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde. Brains were post-fixed, cryoprotected in 30% sucrose, and cut into 30-μm coronal sections. Free-floating sections were incubated with anti-tyrosine hydroxylase (TH, 1:1000, Millipore) antibody overnight at 4 °C, followed by biotinylated secondary antibody and avidin-biotin-peroxidase complex (Vector Laboratories). TH-positive neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta were counted manually in three sections per animal (bregma −2.9 to −3.5 mm) using ImageJ.

Western blotting

Substantia nigra or striatum tissues were lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Equal amounts of protein (30 μg) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C: anti-TH (1:1000, Millipore), anti-α-synuclein (1:1000, Abcam), anti-p-Ser129 α-synuclein (1:500, Abcam), anti-GPX4 (1:1000, CST), anti-ACSL4 (1:1000, Abcam), anti-SLC7A11 (1:1000, Abcam), and anti-β-actin (1:5000, Proteintech). After incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, signals were visualized using ECL (Bio-Rad) and quantified using ImageJ.

Real-Time Quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from the substantia nigra tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). Reverse transcription was performed with a PrimeScript RT kit (Takara). qPCR was carried out using SYBR Green Master Mix (Bio-Rad) on a CFX96 system. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method with β-actin as an internal control. Primer sequences are listed in Table S2.

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

Mice were perfused with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer. Substantia nigra tissues were post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide, dehydrated in graded ethanol, and embedded in Epon. Ultrathin sections (70 nm) were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and examined under a transmission electron microscope (Hitachi HT7800). Mitochondrial area and cristae density were measured using ImageJ (at least 50 mitochondria per animal).

Measurement of iron, GSH, and MDA

Substantia nigra tissues were homogenized in ice-cold PBS. Iron levels were measured using a Ferrous Iron Colorimetric Assay Kit (Abcam). Reduced glutathione (GSH) and malondialdehyde (MDA) were measured using commercial kits (Nanjing Jiancheng) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

JC-1 mitochondrial membrane potential assay

Fresh substantia nigra tissues were dissected and dissociated into a single-cell suspension. Cells were incubated with JC-1 (10 μg/mL, Invitrogen) for 15 min at 37 °C, washed twice, and analyzed using a flow cytometer (BD FACSCanto II). Red (590 nm) and green (530 nm) fluorescence intensities were recorded, and the red/green ratio was calculated.

Cell culture and treatment

Human neuroblastoma SH-SY5Y cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. For experiments, cells were treated with normal glucose (5.5 mM, control), high glucose (25 mM, HG), MPP⁺ (500 μM, Sigma), or HG + MPP⁺ for 48 h.

Cellular bioenergetics (Seahorse assay)

Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was measured using a Seahorse XF96 analyzer (Agilent). SH-SY5Y cells (2 × 10⁴ cells/well) were seeded in XF96 plates. After treatment, cells were incubated in unbuffered DMEM containing 25 mM glucose, 1 mM pyruvate, and 2 mM glutamine. OCR was measured under basal conditions and after sequential injection of oligomycin (1 μM), FCCP (1 μM), and rotenone/antimycin A (0.5 μM each). For tissue samples, freshly isolated substantia nigra mitochondria were used following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Statistical Analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. Comparisons between two groups were analyzed using an unpaired Student’s t-test. Comparisons among three or more groups were analyzed using one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Correlation between 2 h glucose levels and discrimination index was analyzed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All experiments were repeated at least three times independently with similar results.
