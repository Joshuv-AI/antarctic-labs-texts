Spin characteristics of organic radicals from a single-molecule junction perspective
Authors: Yangyang Pan, Lingyu Zeng, Tianfang Shi, Jie Li, Hongyang Zhang, Han Shu, Yupeixuan Zheng, Bo Han, Hanjun Zhang, Juan Casado, Dongsheng Wang, Yonghao Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Organic radicals contain unpaired electrons and represent important open-shell systems for studying molecular spin effects and charge transport. However, conventional spectroscopic and magnetic measurements mainly yield signals, making it difficult to directly resolve intrinsic changes in orbital distribution, exchange interactions and spin state evolution. Single-molecule junctions address this limitation by translating local electronic and spin-related variations into measurable transport responses. In this perspective, we summarize the stable radical scaffolds and radical form tuning strategies relevant to single-molecule junctions. We then discuss how spin distribution, spin coupling and spin states govern orbital structure, molecule–electrode coupling and transport channels, and how these effects are read out electrically. Finally, we highlight opportunities in radical–electrode orbital hybridization, spin-selective transport, control of multiple spin states and radical-based single-molecule functional devices, with the aim of providing mechanistic guidance for molecular spintronic devices.

Introduction

Organic radicals are open-shell molecules containing unpaired electrons and play important roles in both reactive intermediates and functional molecular materials.1,2 In reaction chemistry, radicals are widely involved in organic synthesis,3,4 photochemical transformations,5 polymerization,6 and biologically relevant redox processes,7 where they serve as key intermediates in bond cleavage, bond formation, and electron-transfer reactions. In functional materials, unpaired electrons endow radicals with distinctive redox activity, spin characteristics, and chemical reactivity, making them of sustained interest in molecular magnets,8,9 organic conducting materials,10 energy-storage materials,11 and optoelectronic systems,12 while also providing an important molecular basis for molecular spintronics. However, radicals usually possess high reactivity and short lifetimes, and have long been regarded as transient species that are difficult to isolate and study systematically. Since Gomberg first reported the triphenylmethyl radical in 1900,13 stable radical chemistry has advanced through strategies such as steric protection and electronic structure regulation, enabling selected radicals to be isolated, characterized and studied systematically.14–17 Consequently, radical research has expanded from the generation, trapping, and stabilization of radicals to the broader question of how unpaired electrons govern molecular electronic structure, spin configuration, and functional response.18,19

In this context, open-shell frontier orbitals, spin distribution, and spin interactions have become central to understanding the structure property relationships of organic radicals.20–22 Unlike closed-shell molecules, organic radicals possess singly occupied molecular orbital associated with unpaired electrons. These open-shell orbitals can be energetically accessible for electron transport, while their spatial distribution governs spin delocalization and coupling to the molecular backbone or electrodes.20,23,24 Such delocalization is often accompanied by changes in resonance contributions and bond-length alternation, thereby reshaping the overall electronic structure.21,25 In systems containing two or more radical center, exchange interactions further regulate the relative stability of singlet, triplet, and higher-spin states.22,26 These open-shell features are commonly characterized by a combination of spectroscopic and magnetic techniques. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) is used to detect unpaired electrons and, through hyperfine and exchange-coupling parameters, provides information on spin-density distribution and spin interactions. SQUID-based magnetic susceptibility measurements probe paramagnetic behavior, magnetic exchange interactions, and spin state characteristics.14–17 Vibrational spectroscopy (IR/Raman) and time-resolved spectroscopy, particularly transient absorption spectroscopy (TA), can further track structural changes, charge-transfer processes, and transient species.27,28 Together, these methods have established a fundamental understanding of radical systems in terms of electronic structures, magnetic properties, and dynamic processes. However, conventional characterizations usually reflect ensemble-averaged responses, making it difficult to directly resolve state changes of individual radical molecules under specific local environments, interfacial contact, or external perturbation.

The development of nanotechnology has enabled the direct probing of electronic structures at the molecular scale and dynamic processes.29,30 With the maturation of scanning tunneling microscopy break junctions (STM-BJ), mechanically controllable break junctions (MCBJ), and nanogap electrodes (NGEs), individual molecules can be controllably bridged between two nanoscale electrodes and studied as independent charge transport channels.31–35 Compared with conventional ensemble methods, single-molecule junctions reduce the obscuring effects of intermolecular interactions, aggregation states, and ensemble averaging. In combination with conductance statistics, differential conductance spectroscopy, and theoretical transport calculations, they enable correlations to be established among molecular structure, interfacial coupling, and electrical transport response,36–39 and allow molecular conformational changes, redox transformations, and chemical reactions to be analyzed.40–43 For organic radicals, the singly occupied molecular orbital (SOMO) or related open-shell orbitals may lie close to the electrode Fermi level and participate in electron transport, thereby introducing transport resonances or spin dependent transmission features that are reflected in the low bias conductance of the junction.44–46 As a result, radical generation, retention, quenching, and redox transformation can be converted into distinguishable electrical signals, making single-molecule junctions an effective platform for in situ readout of individual radical forms and their evolution.47,48

In this perspective, we focus on the spin characteristics of organic radicals at the single-molecule scale, with particular emphasis on spin distribution, spin coupling, and the relationship between spin states and electrical transport. Also we aim to clarify how unpaired electrons regulate molecular structure and transport response (Fig. 1).34,35,49–55 Therefore, we first summarize the structural features and stabilization strategies of organic radicals, as well as the design principles that enable them to serve as functional units in single-molecule junctions. Secondly, we discuss how single-molecule junctions can serve as a molecular-scale electrical readout platform to probe changes in radical electronic structure and spin states that are difficult to resolve by conventional measurements. In radical junctions, spin properties changes can reshape frontier orbitals, energy-level alignment and molecule–electrode interactions. These changes may also be accompanied by bond rearrangement or resonance structure evolution, and can ultimately be reflected in measurable transport responses. From this perspective, single-molecule junctions provide a way to relate the spin characteristics of organic radicals to molecular structural changes, interfacial reactions and charge transport, offering mechanistic insight into molecular spintronics.

Stable and tunable organic radicals for single-molecule junction studies

Radical-based single-molecule junctions generally refer to systems in which a single radical molecule bridges two electrodes, forming an electrode–radical–electrode charge-transport pathway.56 Because radicals are intrinsically reactive, they can undergo deactivation, coupling, or spin quenching during device fabrication, interfacial contact formation, and electrical measurements. Therefore, the primary prerequisite for reliable studies is to obtain radicals that can retain their open-shell character over the timescale of junction formation and measurement.57 For single-molecule junctions, “stability” does not merely mean that a radical can be isolated or detected; rather, it also requires that the radical withstand perturbations from electrode coupling, local electric fields, mechanical stretching, and the surrounding environment.

Classification of radicals

Radicals can either be prepared before junction fabrication or generated in situ under device operating conditions.58–60 The former relies on the intrinsic chemical stability of the molecule. For example, phenoxyl radicals can be produced from corresponding precursors through oxidative dehydrogenation, while bulky substituents such as tert-butyl groups suppress side reactions.61,62 The latter approach uses light irradiation, electrochemical control, or bias-induced changes in molecular charge state and open-shell character; for instance, viologen derivatives can form radical forms under electrochemical conditions.63,64 Preformed radicals are useful for establishing structure-transport relationships, whereas in situ generated radicals are better suited for tracking radical formation, reaction, and transformation processes.

Organic radicals are commonly classified as persistent radicals and stable radicals.20,65 Persistent radicals possess sufficiently long lifetimes under specific conditions to be detected by conventional methods. Stable radicals further require sufficient robustness to be isolated or handled while retaining their open-shell character under air, moisture, thermal perturbation, or chemical environments. For single-molecule junction studies, this distinction has practical significance: whether a radical can withstand device fabrication and measurement conditions directly determines whether it can be reliably measured as an active single-molecule unit.

According to charge state, radicals can be classified into neutral radicals and radical ions.66 Radical ions are typically formed through single-electron oxidation or reduction of a molecule: loss of one electron produces a radical cation, whereas gain of one electron produces a radical anion. Compared with neutral radicals, radical ions possess both a net charge and an unpaired electron. As a result, their charge state and open-shell electronic structure are more readily modulated by external electric fields or electrochemical gating, and they are more sensitive to charge transfer processes at molecule–electrode interfaces. Typical examples include polaron states in conducting polymers, such as poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):poly(styrenesulfonate) (PEDOT:PSS),67 and π-conjugated donor systems represented by tetrathiafulvalene (TTF).68

According to the number of radical center, organic radicals can be classified as monoradicals, diradicals, and polyradicals.69 Monoradicals contain only one unpaired electron and usually correspond to a total spin quantum number of S = 1/2 and a doublet state; they represent the most fundamental and common open-shell structures in stable radical chemistry, luminescent radicals, and molecular electronics. In contrast, diradicals contain two unpaired electrons, and their electronic structures usually require consideration of both open-shell singlet and triplet states. The singlet–triplet energy gap (ΔES–T) and the diradical character index (y0) are key parameters for describing the electronic structure, spin coupling, and reactivity of diradicals.21 Among them, ΔES–T reflects the relative stability of different spin states, whereas y0 characterizes the degree to which a system evolves from the closed-shell limit toward the open-shell diradical limit. Finally, polyradicals contain three or more unpaired electrons, typically exhibiting higher spin multiplicities and more complex spin arrangements, and provide an important foundation for developing high-spin molecules, molecular magnets, and quantum functional materials.22

From the perspective of electronic structure, radicals can also be classified as delocalized or localized radicals according to the spatial distribution of spin density within the molecular framework.69 In delocalized radicals, the unpaired electron can extend over a large π-conjugated framework, multiple resonance structures, or several aromatic/heteroaromatic units; their stability and functional properties are therefore closely related to spin distribution.70,71 On the other hand, in localized radicals the unpaired electron is mainly confined to specific local units, such as N–O˙ radical fragments or localized heterocyclic frameworks.72 This distinction is not a strict binary classification, but rather describes relative differences in the extent of spin density delocalization among different radical systems. For single-molecule junction studies, spin density distribution not only affects radical stability and electronic structure, but also determines how spin is distributed within the molecular framework, how spin center couple to each other or to the electrodes, and which spin state is stabilized under the junction environment.

Delocalized radicals: spin distribution across extended π-frameworks

Triphenylmethyl radicals and their fused aryl derivatives are among the most representative stable carbon-centered organic radicals.1,13,73 The persistence of the classical triphenylmethyl radical mainly arises from delocalization of the central unpaired electron over the triaryl framework, together with steric protection of the central carbon radical site by the aryl substituents (Fig. 2a). This class of radicals is typically generated by reduction of triphenylmethyl halides, single-electron reduction of triarylmethyl cations, or conversion of triarylmethanol-type precursors.74 It should be noted that although triphenylmethyl radicals are highly persistent, they are not chemically inert and may still undergo dimerization, oxidation, or halogenation reactions.75 Their stability, spectroscopic properties, and redox behavior can be further tuned by aryl substitution, heteroaryl replacement, or the introduction of bulky substituents (S1, S2).76,77 Among them, polychlorinated triarylmethyl radicals, such as perchlorotriphenylmethyl radical (PTM) and tris(2,4,6-trichlorophenyl)methyl radical (TTM), effectively suppress radical dimerization and side reactions through the electronic effects and steric shielding of chlorine substituents, and have become important scaffolds in studies of stable radicals, luminescent radicals, and molecular electronic materials.78,79 In single-molecule junctions, PTM/TTM derivatives can serve as stable spin units, and their sterically bulky frameworks help reduce spin quenching caused by electrode contact. At the same time, because their spin density is relatively localized, their direct contribution to the main charge-transport pathway may be limited, making these systems more suitable for studying radical–electrode exchange coupling and Kondo transport.

From the perspective of structural extension, fluorenyl radicals can be regarded as carbon-centered open-shell π-systems derived from triarylmethyl radicals through fusion and rigidification of the aryl framework; they can also be described as dibenzo-extended cyclopentadienyl radicals.80 Compared with conformationally more flexible triarylmethyl radicals, the fluorene framework enhances π-conjugation and molecular rigidity through its fused-ring structure, allowing the unpaired electron to further delocalize over the fluorene core and attached π-conjugated fragments. As a result, the stability, frontier orbital energy levels, redox behavior, and reactivity of fluorenyl radicals typically show stronger structural dependence. Through covalent linkage, fused-ring extension, or incorporation into larger π-conjugated frameworks, fluorenyl radicals can be further used to construct diradicals, polyradicals, or diradicaloid systems, whose optical, electrochemical, and magnetic properties can be tuned through intramolecular spin coupling.81–83 Systems represented by indeno[1,2-b]fluorene and related fused frameworks further exploit extended conjugation and backbone rigidity to regulate frontier orbital arrangements, aromatic/antiaromatic contributions, and the balance between closed-shell quinoidal and open-shell diradical structures.26,84–86 In single-molecule junctions, fluorenyl and related fused aryl radicals can be used to examine how open-shell orbital reconstruction, bond-length alternation, and molecular conformation reshape charge-transport pathways.

Phenalenyl radicals are typical delocalized π-radicals with non-bonding molecular orbitals and can undergo reversible interconversion among cationic, neutral radical, and anionic redox states.87–90 In neutral phenalenyl radicals, the unpaired electron is not confined to a single carbon atom (Fig. 2b), but is mainly distributed over alternating carbon sites along the periphery of the aromatic framework (S3),91 giving rise to pronounced spin-distribution features and multistage redox activity. Because the parent phenalenyl radical readily undergoes dimerization, sterically demanding substituents are usually introduced to improve its kinetic stability (S4). Meanwhile, phenalenyl radicals can form different aggregated states through σ-dimerization or face-to-face π–π overlap, leading to tunable spin coupling and magnetic behavior.92 Phenalenyl radicals provide useful models for discussing nonbonding frontier orbitals, reversible redox states, and spin-related transmission, although their tendency toward dimerization requires steric protection and junction-compatible anchoring groups for single-molecule junction studies.

Phenoxyl radicals are typically formed from phenolic compounds through single-electron oxidation accompanied by deprotonation.93 Their unpaired electrons can delocalize from the oxygen atom to the ortho and para positions of the aromatic ring (Fig. 2c), giving them more pronounced resonance stabilization than ordinary alkoxyl radicals. Simple phenoxyl monoradicals remain highly reactive and readily undergo coupling, disproportionation, or further oxidation; their persistence can be significantly improved by introducing bulky substituents at the ortho and para positions.94 In larger π-conjugated systems, phenoxyl units often serve as key spin-bearing motifs for constructing open-shell diradicals and high-spin molecules. For example, some classical phenoxyl-based high-spin systems achieve triplet ground states or strong spin-coupling behavior through the cooperation between quinoidal polarized structures and radical resonance structures (S5).95 In phenoxyl-based diradical systems, the diradical character and ΔES–T can be further tuned by varying the heteroatom effect and the nature of the bridging units (S6).96,97 Phenoxyl radicals are more often used as spin-bearing units in diradical and high-spin molecular systems, and may provide design principles for studying how radical resonance structures and spin delocalization influence molecular transport.

Blatter radicals are a class of stable nitrogen-containing heterocyclic radicals-based on the 1,2,4-benzotriazin-4-yl core.98 Their unpaired electrons can delocalize over the benzotriazine framework and attached aryl groups (Fig. 2d), their stability mainly arises from electronic delocalization and resonance stabilization within the heterocyclic framework, rather than relying solely on bulky steric protection.99 The distribution of the unpaired electron between the benzotriazine core and peripheral aryl groups can be altered via chemical modification (S7, S8).100–103 Therefore, Blatter radicals are not only important models for studying the electronic structure and spin coupling of heterocyclic delocalized radicals, but also functional molecular scaffolds with near-infrared response and electrochemical reversibility.104,105 Blatter radicals feature stable heterocyclic open-shell frameworks and tunable spin distributions, making them suitable for studying spin-related transport under the combined influence of radical structure, molecular conformation, and electrode contact.

p-Quinodimethane and its derivatives are typical Kekulé-type diradicaloid systems for understanding the closed-shell/open-shell balance, and can also be regarded as fundamental models for many delocalized diradical structures.106,107 Their electronic structures usually involve competition between closed-shell quinoidal and open-shell diradical configurations: the former is stabilized by quinoidal conjugation and π-bond pairing, whereas the latter can be stabilized through aromaticity recovery and spin delocalization. Because antiferromagnetic coupling usually exists between the two spin center, these systems often exhibit open-shell singlet ground states. Since the reports of Thiele's and Chichibabin's hydrocarbon,108,109p-quinodimethane and its extended structures have served as important models for studying diradical character, narrow-gap π-systems, near-infrared absorption, and spin state regulation. By introducing aryl substituents, electron-withdrawing units, or extended quinoidal conjugated frameworks, the properties of closed-shell/open-shell contributions, degree of delocalization, frontier orbital energy levels, and charge transport properties can be systematically tuned.110–112 These Kekulé-type diradicaloids provide a direct framework for relating closed-shell/open-shell balance, diradical character, frontier-orbital narrowing, and long-range single-molecule charge transport.

Overall, delocalized radicals share the common feature that unpaired electrons can extend over large π-conjugated frameworks. Spin delocalization can, reduce the local reactivity of radical center and enhance molecular persistence. On the other hand, it also provides broader tunability for redox behavior, frontier orbital energy levels, and spin coupling. Therefore, these systems are not only important models for understanding open-shell electronic structures and spin interactions, but also provide an important molecular basis for constructing radical-based single-molecule junctions and spin functional materials.

Localized radicals: well-defined spin center

Nitronyl nitroxide radicals are representative locally delocalized radicals, in which the unpaired electron is mainly distributed over the two N–O units and the adjacent imidazoline framework (Fig. 3a). As a result, they exhibit relatively well-defined spin distribution, good chemical stability, and tunable magnetic interactions.113,114 This class of radicals can be synthesized from corresponding precursors through condensation and oxidation, and has long been used as building blocks for molecular magnets, metal–radical complexes, and high-spin molecules. Their magnetic coupling can be tuned through substituent effects, bridging modes, crystal packing, and coordination with transition-metal or lanthanide ions (S9, S10).115,116 Nitronyl nitroxide radicals can serve as localized spin centers for probing magnetic exchange interactions with metal centers, neighboring radicals, or electrode electrons, although their coupling to the main π-transport backbone depends on molecular connectivity.

Verdazyl radicals use nitrogen-rich heterocyclic frameworks as the main spin-bearing units, and their spin density is usually concentrated within localized heterocyclic structures (Fig. 3b).117 Compared with highly reactive radical intermediates, these systems exhibit good thermal stability, chemical persistence, and reversible redox behavior.118 By tuning substituent positions, π-conjugation length, or molecular planarity, their spin distribution, molecular packing, electrochemical properties, and intermolecular exchange interactions can be modified.117,119 Verdazyl and oxoverdazyl derivatives have been used to construct diradicals, triradicals, and organic magnetic solids, and serve as important platforms for studying the coupling between local spin center and molecular frameworks (S11, S12).117,120 Verdazyl and oxoverdazyl radicals can be used to investigate redox-controlled open-shell/closed-shell switching in single-molecule junctions, where changes in charge and spin states are converted into conductance responses.

In TEMPO radicals, the spin density is mainly localized around the N–O˙ unit (Fig. 3c). The 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine framework suppresses dimerization and related side reactions through steric hindrance, endowing TEMPO with good air stability and chemical persistence.121,122 Because the nitroxide N–O˙ unit in TEMPO is relatively localized and can undergo reversible nitroxide/oxoammonium redox transformations, TEMPO and its derivatives can serve as redox-active units in radical polymers and organic energy-storage materials; they are also used as radical traps and spin bearing building blocks for high-spin molecular systems (S13, S14).123,124 In single-molecule junctions, TEMPO radicals are better viewed as localized side-chain spin probes for examining how a pendant nitroxide spin affects magnetotransport without necessarily dominating the through-bond conductance channel.

Overall, localized radicals bear spin center within relatively well-defined local structural units, which facilitates the distinction of different contributions from local spins, π-transport backbones, and molecule electrode interfaces to single-molecule electrical transport. Compared with delocalized radicals, these systems are better suited for analyzing exchange coupling and through-space interactions between spin center and electrode electrons, bridging π segments, or neighboring radical center.

Single-molecule junction perspective: spin characteristics of organic radicals

The spin characteristics of organic radicals can be understood in terms of a hierarchical relationship among spin delocalization, spin coupling and spin states: spin delocalization describes the spatial distribution of unpaired electrons, spin coupling reflects exchange interactions between spin centers, and spin states reflect the energy ordering of different spin arrangements (Fig. 4a). Taking Kekulé-type diradicals as an example, unpaired electrons can delocalize through π-conjugated pathways or through-space interactions within the molecular backbone, accompanied by bond-length alternation and changes in the balance between quinoidal and diradical resonance structures. The resulting spin-density distribution further affects the mode of orbital overlap, exchange pathway, and the magnitude and sign of the magnetic exchange constant J between two radical centers. In general, antiferromagnetic coupling favor an open-shell singlet state, whereas ferromagnetic coupling favors a triplet or higher-spin state. Accordingly, the spin state can be regarded as the energetic consequence of spin coupling, with its relative stability commonly described by ΔES–T. When the singlet and triplet states are close in energy, temperature can alter the relative populations of different spin states by modulating their thermal occupation. However, an external magnetic field itself should not generally be regarded as directly inducing singlet–triplet mixing. Its primary physical effect is to split the spin sublevels through the Zeeman interaction. In radical-pair or diradical systems, singlet–triplet mixing usually relies on additional spin-mixing mechanisms, including hyperfine coupling, dipolar interactions, and coupling between molecular spins and electrode electron spins. Whether these spin-dynamic processes can be converted into observable changes in spin-state populations or charge-transport responses further depends on the singlet–triplet energy gap, the exchange coupling strength, and the matching of timescales among spin mixing, spin relaxation, and charge transport. Therefore, magnetic-field modulation of spin-state populations and spin-related transport is not a universal behaviour of all radical systems, but should be understood as a system-dependent effect that can occur only under specific energy-scale and spin-dynamic conditions.

Single-molecule junction studies have shown that open-shell orbital distributions, exchange coupling between spin center, and interactions between localized spins and electrode electrons can be characterized by transport signals such as conductance, dI/dV spectra, Kondo resonances, and magnetic-field responses.56,125–127 In a two-terminal molecular junction, the conductance G reflects charge transmission through a molecule bridging two electrodes; in the low-bias limit, it is commonly written as G = dI/dV|V→0 and reported in units of the conductance quantum G0 = 2e2/h. In STM-BJ and MCBJ measurements, conductance traces and statistical histograms are used to identify reproducible molecular conductance features. Bias-dependent differential conductance (dI/dV) spectra provide further information on molecular resonances, inelastic excitations and spin-related transport features. For open-shell radicals, exchange coupling between a localized molecular spin and conduction electrons in the electrodes can produce a Kondo resonance, typically observed as a zero-bias anomaly in low-temperature dI/dV spectra and examined by temperature dependence or magnetic-field-induced Zeeman splitting. Magnetic-field-dependent transport, including magnetoresistance and field-dependent changes in dI/dV spectra, can further help identify spin-mediated transport.

These transport observables reflect different types of physical information in radical-based single-molecule junctions and should therefore be interpreted separately. Conductance enhancement, conductance suppression and length-dependent transport are commonly associated with changes in energy-level alignment, conjugation topology, molecule–electrode coupling and the participation of open-shell frontier orbitals. However, conductance changes alone are insufficient to assign a specific spin process. By contrast, zero-bias Kondo resonances, magnetic-field-induced Zeeman splitting, IETS spin excitations and magnetoresistance provide more direct information on exchange coupling involving localized spins. Gate- or redox-dependent conductance switching may indicate changes in charge state or spin state, but such assignments generally require support from magnetic-field-dependent measurements, spectroscopic characterization or theoretical transport calculations. Accordingly, the following discussion mainly uses conductance-based signals and related transport features to discuss spin distribution, Kondo resonances, IETS spin excitations, magnetic-field responses and related measurements to analyze spin coupling, and gate- or redox-controlled transport changes and related switching behavior to examine spin-state control. Accordingly, this section discusses the spin-related transport behavior of organic radicals in single-molecule junctions, focusing on spin distribution, spin coupling, and spin state control.

Spin distribution

Spin distribution describes the spatial extension of spin density associated with unpaired electron(s) from a local radical centre into the molecular framework, and is an important parameter for understanding the open-shell electronic structure of organic radicals.128 In single-molecule junctions, spin distribution not only affects the frontier orbital gap and the spatial distribution of open-shell frontier orbitals (Fig. 4b),129 but also regulates the main molecular transport pathway through resonance-structure rearrangement, bond-length alternation, and changes in conjugation topology (Fig. 4c).25 Therefore, spin distribution is not merely a spin-density map, but a structural variable that connects open-shell orbitals, the molecular backbone, and charge transport. Single-molecule conductance measurements provide an experimental route to analyze this relationship. By combining electrical characteristics such as conductance magnitude, length dependence, and bias response with theoretical transmission-function calculations, it is possible to further determine whether open-shell orbitals participate in the main transport channel and how their participation modifies electron transmission near the Fermi level.125

In on-site radical systems, the radical centre is directly embedded in the main transport backbone, making the open-shell orbitals more likely to participate in charge transport. Liu et al. generated phenothiazine (PTZ) radical cations at room temperature through acid/oxidant triggering, and compared the single-molecule conductance of the neutral and radical forms using MCBJ measurements (Fig. 5a). The results showed that the conductance of the radical form increased by approximately 200-fold. Theoretical calculations indicated that the PTZ radical form has a reduced HOMO–LUMO gap and exhibits enhanced transmission near the HOMO dominated energy region, thereby lowering the tunneling barrier.49 Subsequently, Gao et al. extended the radical effect to supramolecular junctions using redox-active TMB (Fig. 5b). In EC-STM-BJ measurements, neutral TMB at 0 V formed single-molecule and π-stacked supramolecular junctions, whereas one-electron oxidation at 0.35 V generated mixed-valence radical species and produced supramolecular radical junctions with higher conductance. Further oxidation at 0.5 V gave diimine-type TMB species with a distinct conductance response. These results indicate that SOMO-involved intermolecular interactions can enhance through-space electronic coupling and promote charge transport.130 The neutral fluorene-based radical 36-FR reported by Zheng et al. further showed that a delocalized half-filled orbital can introduce Breit–Wigner type resonant transport features, opening a new transport pathway for an originally low conducting closed-shell precursor (Fig. 5c).45

When the systems under investigation are extended from monoradicals to diradicals or diradicaloid molecules, the contribution of open-shell resonance forms to the overall electronic structure becomes more pronounced, significantly affecting bond-length distribution, frontier orbital gaps, and length-dependent transport behavior. Li et al. reported oligophenylene bridged bis(triarylamine) molecular wires that can be oxidized to form monoradical cation and diradical dications.46,50 In contrast to the exponential conductance decay with increasing length observed for neutral molecules (Fig. 6a), the oxidized species exhibited reversed conductance decay; for example, the 2.6 nm-long B32+ junction showed a conductance of 0.13G0, approximately 5400-times higher than that of the corresponding neutral state. Sil et al. reported a class of neutral acene-like benzoquinoidal diradicals (Fig. 6b), whose open-shell character originates from resonance contributions between closed-shell quinoidal and diradical structures. As the conjugated backbone was extended, the diradical character index y0 increased and the frontier orbitals gradually approached the electrode Fermi level, leading to anti-ohmic transport behavior.131,132 Furthermore, Shen et al. reported a class of open-shell donor–acceptor macromolecules (Fig. 6c) that maintained high conductance close to 1G0 over lengths exceeding 20 nm, indicating that extended π-conjugation, narrow band gaps, and diradical character can cooperatively support long-range resonant transport.133

Unlike on-site radical systems, off-site radical designs structurally separate the radical delocalization pathway from the main charge transport channel, making them particularly suitable for analyzing how spin distribution regulates molecular electronic structure, resonance forms, and conjugation topology. By controlling the radical injection sites, Zheng et al. systematically revealed the effects of spin distribution, bond-length alternation, and linear/cross-conjugation relationships on single-molecule conductance. In the cyclopentadienone derivative systems, the pyridine-linked backbone serves as the main transport channel, whereas the radical injection pathway is designed to be cross-conjugated with the main channel in the PCP series and linearly conjugated in the FCF series (Fig. 7a). STM-BJ measurements showed that radical injection turns on cross-conjugation and decreases conductance in the PCP series; in contrast, it turns off cross-conjugation, strengthens the linear through bond transport channel, and increases conductance in the FCF series (Fig. 7a).51 Subsequently, 9,9′-bifluorenylidene derivatives further demonstrated that stepwise radical injection and spin removal can continuously tune the extent of spin distribution along the non-conducting pathway, thereby transforming an originally linearly conjugated main transport pathway into a cross-conjugated one. With increasing spin delocalization, the electronic structure evolves from a linear-conjugated radical form to a hypervalent sulfur resonance form and then to a hypervalent/quinoidal resonance form. This resonance transformation introduces intragap states near the Fermi level and opens an additional linear conjugation pathway, thereby enhancing single-molecule conductance.135

Beyond through-bond conjugation topology, radical-modulated conductance can also be discussed in the context of quantum interference in coherent molecular transport. In single-molecule circuits with parallel paths, conductance does not necessarily follow a simple conductance superposition law, because transmission amplitudes through different molecular pathways may interfere.136,137 In molecular ensemble junctions, intermolecular quantum interference can occur when electrons coherently probe multiple molecules, with each molecule acting as an interference path through its tunneling phase. By changing conjugation topology or through-space electronic coupling, radical formation may alter the relative phases of transmission amplitudes along different molecular pathways, thereby modulating conductance through quantum interference.

In summary, the influence of spin distribution on single-molecule transport does not simply depend on the extent of spin delocalization, but rather on how it reshapes the relationship among open-shell orbitals, molecular resonance structures, and the main transport pathway. When delocalized open-shell orbitals are effectively coupled to the electrodes, electron transmission near the Fermi level can be enhanced. Conversely, when spin distribution alters the conjugation topology or introduces unfavorable quantum interference, conductance may also be suppressed. Therefore, spin distribution should be understood as a structure dependent electronic factor that modulates transport, rather than a simple conductance-enhancing mechanism.

Spin coupling

In organic radical systems, spin coupling is usually quantified by the relative energies of different spin states, and essentially reflects the magnitude and sign of exchange interactions between unpaired electrons.22,138 The spin coupling discussed in this section is distinct from spin–orbit coupling (SOC): SOC describes the interaction between electron spin angular momentum and orbital angular momentum, whereas the present discussion focuses on two types of exchange processes, namely the exchange coupling between localized radical spins and conduction electrons in the electrodes, and magnetic exchange coupling within diradicals or among multiple radical center.139–142 Single-molecule junctions provide transport spectroscopic tools to distinguish these coupling mechanisms. For example, Kondo resonance appears as a zero-bias anomaly in low temperature dI/dV spectra and can be verified by temperature dependence and magnetic-field-induced Zeeman splitting. For diradical systems containing two spin center, magnetic exchange interactions are commonly described by the Heisenberg spin Hamiltonian Ĥ = −2JŜAŜB where ŜA and ŜB are the spin angular momentum operators at sites A and B, respectively, and J is the magnetic coupling constant. Under the sign convention used here, J > 0 indicates that the two interacting spins tend to align parallel, corresponding to ferromagnetic coupling, whereas J < 0 indicates that the two spins tend to align antiparallel, corresponding to antiferromagnetic coupling.22,138,143 The magnitude and sign of the coupling constant depend not only on the distance between the two radical center, but also on the spatial distribution of SOMOs, the orbital overlap pattern, connection topology, conjugation pathway, and molecular conformation.

Early studies of spin coupling in radical-based single-molecule junctions mainly focused on the exchange interaction between localized spins in monoradicals and conduction electrons in metal electrodes. Frisenda et al. investigated neutral, stable polychlorotriphenylmethyl (PTM) radical single-molecule junctions using MCBJ (Fig. 8a), and observed a zero-bias Kondo anomaly at low temperature.52 This signal gradually weakened with increasing temperature and underwent Zeeman splitting under an applied magnetic field, whereas the corresponding closed-shell PTM-αH molecule did not show a similar response. These results indicate that the signal originates from exchange coupling between the unpaired electron in the SOMO of the PTM radical and the conduction electrons in the electrodes. This work demonstrates that the localized magnetic moment of an organic radical can be preserved in solid-state single-molecule devices and read out through electrical transport signals. In addition to Kondo resonance, magnetoresistance responses can also reflect the modulation of transport processes by localized radical spins. Hayakawa et al. studied the magnetotransport behavior of TEMPO-OPE monoradical junctions at 4.2 K (Fig. 8b).144 The OPE conjugated backbone serves as the main transport channel, while the TEMPO radical unit is nearly decoupled from the OPE π orbitals. Experiments showed that TEMPO-OPE molecular junctions exhibit pronounced positive magnetoresistance under an external magnetic field, significantly larger than that of the corresponding closed-shell OPE molecule. Analysis of I–V characteristics and IETS suggested that the magnetic field can modify the effective coupling between the transport orbital and the electrodes that the magnetic field, indicating that even when the radical is located on a side chain and partially decoupled from the main π channel, its localized spin can still influence single-molecule magnetotransport through spin-related mechanisms.

For diradical and polyradical systems, the research focus further shifts to magnetic exchange coupling among multiple spin center within a molecule. Gaudenzi et al. incorporated an all organic triradical molecule into single-molecule junctions (Fig. 9a) and analyzed its spin excitation behavior using low temperature transport spectroscopy.34 In the crystalline state, this molecule usually exhibits a high-spin quartet ground state, whereas in single-molecule junctions, local environment induced conformational distortion can change the dihedral angle between the radical center and the central aryl ring. This conformational change can modulate SOMO overlap and exchange pathways, thereby changing the sign of the magnetic exchange coupling and switching the system between ferromagnetic and antiferromagnetic regimes. This result shows that spin coupling in single-molecule junctions is determined not only by the intrinsic molecular structure, but also by electrode contacts and local conformation. Subsequently, Baum et al. further highlighted the decisive role of the current pathway in spin readout using bianthracene-based radical systems.145 Low-temperature MCBJ studies showed that both the monoradical 1-OS and diradical 2-OS can exhibit an S = 1/2 Kondo resonance, but only the diradical 2-OS shows an IETS signal arising from spin flip processes (Fig. 9b). This signal corresponds to singlet–triplet excitation between the two radical center, with an exchange energy of approximately 10 meV. These results indicate that spin readout in diradical molecules depends not only on the number of radical center, but also on whether the current pathway couples simultaneously to both spin center. Gate control further demonstrates that intramolecular magnetic exchange can change dynamically with molecular charge state.

In addition to intramolecular coupling, intermolecular radical interactions can also generate new exchange channels. In a study of Blatter radical single-molecule junctions, Mitra et al. showed (Fig. 9c) that some junctions without conventional zero-bias Kondo peaks can exhibit pronounced negative magnetoresistance.53 Combined distance dependent and magnetic-field-dependent measurements, together with theoretical calculations, showed that this phenomenon can be explained by a singlet–triplet Kondo model, indicating that through space interactions, π-stacking, or lateral coupling under different molecular configurations may also participate in spin-related transport. These results suggest that in radical-based single-molecule junctions, spin coupling is not limited to the interior of a single molecule; intermolecular configurations near the interface may also modify magnetic exchange pathways.

Overall, spin coupling in single-molecule junctions can be divided into three categories: exchange coupling between localized radical spins and electrode conduction electrons, intramolecular magnetic exchange coupling among multiple radical center, and additional exchange channels introduced by intermolecular contacts or through space interactions. Kondo resonance, magnetoresistance responses, and IETS spin-excitation spectra reveal these coupling processes from different perspectives. Because the relevant transport signals are highly sensitive to molecular conformation, contact geometry, charge state, and current pathway, future studies should combine temperature-dependent measurements, magnetic-field control, and theoretical calculations to quantitatively distinguish the contributions of different exchange mechanisms to single-molecule transport behavior.

Spin state

Spin states describe the spin arrangement and total spin multiplicity of open-shell electrons. They are key descriptors for understanding the electronic structure, magnetic response, and reactivity of organic radicals.22,69,138,146 Compared with molecules that usually adopt closed-shell singlet states, organic radicals can exhibit from doublet to higher-spin states, depending on the number of unpaired electrons, orbital occupation, and interactions between spin center. Their relative stability is jointly regulated by exchange interactions, orbital occupation, and molecular conformation. In single-molecule junctions, spin states are further coupled to charge state, interfacial bonding, and transport channels, and can therefore give rise to distinguishable electrical responses.147–149 In radical chemistry, spin states are not only descriptors of open-shell electronic structures, but are also closely associated with the formation and transformation of radical intermediates. Radical generation, atom transfer, radical addition, coupling and recombination are often accompanied by changes in electronic structure, spin configuration and bonding configuration. In single-molecule junctions, these changes can further modify molecular orbitals, molecule–electrode coupling and transport channels, thereby converting spin-state evolution during radical reactions and interfacial bonding into measurable transport responses.

Single-molecule junctions can identify reaction transformations from closed-shell precursors to open-shell radical states. Zhang et al. used STM-BJ to study C–O bond cleavage in alkoxyamines, showing that the interfacial electrostatic field can promote bond breaking and release the nitroxide radical 4-amino-TEMPO together with a carbon-centered radical. Theoretical analysis further indicated that stabilization of charge-separated resonance structures by the electric field helps lower the bond-cleavage barrier (Fig. 10a).150 Feng et al. combined UV irradiation with bias control to realize C–N radical coupling reactions in single-molecule junctions. These results show that single-molecule junctions can map radical generation and reaction transformations into statistically analysable electrical signals (Fig. 10b). Transient radicals can also be captured at molecule–electrode interfaces and converted into stable, strongly coupled contacts.55 Zhang et al. used single-electron transfer from the electrodes to Katritzky salts in STM-BJ to induce C–N bond cleavage, releasing benzyl-type carbon radicals and further forming benzyl-type Au–C contacts (Fig. 10c).151 These studies demonstrate that open-shell intermediates can not only be captured in situ as reactive species, but also act as interfacial bonding units that modify molecule–electrode coupling and transport channels.

External fields can induce spin-state transitions that are directly reflected in single-molecule device responses. Naghibi et al. showed that junctions of the persistent 6-oxoverdazyl radical 1 retain open-shell character at room temperature and exhibit a conductance nearly one order of magnitude higher than that of its closed-shell analogue (Fig. 11a). Electrochemical gating reversibly converts radical 1 into a closed-shell anionic state, leading to decreased conductance, transistor-like switching, and rectification behavior.152 Yang et al. used graphene–molecule–graphene single-molecule junctions to achieve direct electrical detection and control of spin states in the donor–acceptor diradical molecule NTCPhN. By varying temperature, bias voltage, and external magnetic field, they effectively regulated the interconversion of its electronic structure among closed-shell, open-shell singlet, and open-shell triplet states, and further clarified the corresponding thermodynamic and kinetic features (Fig. 11b).35 Changes in spin state can also affect spin-selective transport by modulating quantum interference effects, thereby enabling spin filtering. Chelli et al. found that the doublet and singlet states of the open-shell π-radical TPM-B correspond to different interference patterns: in the doublet state, a meta-connected radical can exhibit spin-dependent constructive or destructive spin interference, whereas after conversion to the singlet state, it recovers the conventional quantum-interference characteristics of closed-shell molecules, leading to conductance changes over several orders of magnitude (Fig. 11c).54

Overall, single-molecule junction techniques can convert radical reaction processes and interfacial state changes into distinguishable electrical responses, thereby revealing how molecular orbitals participate in radical reactions, as well as the orbital hybridization and interfacial coupling features involved in radical–metal electrode bonding. At the same time, different spin arrangements or spin channels can alter the selectivity of electron transmission, giving rise to spin filtering and magnetic responses. Thus, in radical-based single-molecule junctions, the significance of spin states lies not only in distinguishing different open-shell configurations, but also in revealing the intrinsic relationships among spin states, reaction pathways, interfacial coupling, and electrical transport.

Conclusions and outlook

Above discussion shows that radical-based single-molecule junctions can translate unpaired electron related orbital reconstruction, spin state evolution and interfacial interactions into electrical transport signatures. This capability can be understood at three levels. First, within the molecular framework, spin delocalization can induce bond-length alternation, changes in resonance structures and reconstruction of open-shell orbitals, thereby modifying transport channels near the Fermi level. Second, spin centre coupling and radical electrode exchange interactions can tune magnetic exchange strengths and spin excitation energies, which are reflected in Kondo resonances, IETS features and magnetic-field responses. Third, radical generation and spin state switching can be monitored through conductance traces and related transport signals. Thus, single-molecule junctions are not only tools for comparing molecular conductance values, but also provide a means to resolve microscopic radical processes that are difficult to distinguish in ensemble measurements.

Radical-based single-molecule junctions have already demonstrated their ability to probe open-shell spin states. Future studies should further clarify how interfacial coupling, spin control and device function are connected. Several directions are particularly important. (1) Interfacial bonding and orbital hybridization. The bonding modes and orbital hybridization at radical electrode interfaces, as well as their effects on transport channels near the Fermi level, remain to be clarified. Such understanding will guide the design of radical anchoring units and efficient molecular wires. (2) Spin selective transport. Spin selective transport under room temperature and realistic operating conditions remains a key direction. Rational molecular design is expected to advance spin filtering functions in radical-based single-molecule junctions. (3) Spin state control. In diradical and polyradical systems, different spin states are often close in energy and sensitive to temperature, magnetic field, and local environment. Controlling their relative stability and correlating it with conductance responses will help reveal the relationship among spin state, orbital structure and transport response. (4) Functional device construction. The reversible redox behavior, tunable spin states and structural responsiveness of radicals provide opportunities for single-molecule memristors, switches and multistate logic devices.

Within these functional directions, achieving stable spin readout under room-temperature conditions and capturing fast radical-related dynamics are two key challenges for advancing radical-based single-molecule junctions toward practical devices and mechanistic analysis. Kondo resonances, inelastic spin excitations and magnetic-field-dependent differential conductance features usually involve low-energy scales and are therefore mostly measured at low temperature. At room temperature, thermal broadening and spin relaxation may weaken these signals. Unstable radical–electrode contacts or overly strong molecule–electrode coupling may also reduce measurement reproducibility or induce spin quenching. Therefore, room-temperature radical-based single-molecule spin devices require progress at the molecular, interfacial and device levels: improving radical stability, controlling radical–electrode contact geometry and enhancing the reproducibility of single-molecule junction measurements.

Temporal resolution is mainly used to probe fast processes such as radical generation, quenching, redox conversion and transient interfacial species. Previous studies have shown that nanosecond conductance measurements can resolve fast two-state conductance fluctuations in low-temperature STM junctions,153I–V measurements with kHz time resolution can track frontier-orbital parameters during junction elongation,154 microsecond graphene–molecule–graphene junction measurements can monitor single-molecule conformational changes or reaction intermediates,155 and nanosecond differential-conductance spectroscopy can resolve excited-state dynamics in single-molecule radical transistors.156 Further progress will require longer junction lifetimes, improved mechanical stability, higher amplifier bandwidth and sampling rates, better signal-to-noise ratios, and synchronization of transport measurements with optical, electrochemical, electrostatic or magnetic stimuli.

Radical-based single-molecule junctions are therefore expected to advance from electrical readout platforms for open-shell spin phenomena to molecular-scale systems for probing how spin distribution, exchange coupling and spin state evolution shape charge transport, providing an experimental basis for future radical-based molecular spintronic devices.
