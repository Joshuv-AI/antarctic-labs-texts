Efficacy and safety of GW117 tablets in major depressive disorder: a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase 2 dose-finding study

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGW117 (N-(2-(6-chloro-7-deuteromethoxy-naphthalen-1-yl) ethyl) acetamide) is a dual-acting agent (MT1/MT2 agonist, 5-HT2C antagonist) with prior evidence of antidepressant efficacy and favourable safety.AimsTo preliminarily evaluate the efficacy and safety of GW117 in major depressive disorder (MDD) and to explore the optimal dosing.MethodsA total of 280 eligible patients aged 18–65 years with MDD were randomly assigned (1:1:1:1) to 8 weeks of double-blind treatment with fixed doses of GW117 tablets (20, 40, 60 mg/day) or placebo. The primary endpoint was the change from baseline to Week 8 in the total score of the Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression-17 item (HAMD-17). Although differences versus placebo did not reach statistical significance in the overall population, GW117 20 mg monotherapy showed significant improvements in multidimensional depressive symptoms, including insomnia, in the optimal response subgroup. Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a prevalent psychiatric disorder characterised by chronic progression and high recurrence risk, which can lead to occupational disability and suicidal behaviour, posing a significant challenge to global public health systems.1 2 Studies have demonstrated that 80%–90% of patients with MDD present with comorbid insomnia symptoms.3 4 A key therapeutic challenge in current clinical practice is the lack of approved antidepressants with demonstrated efficacy in addressing both depressive and insomnia symptoms, often necessitating combination therapy with antidepressants and hypnotic agents for comprehensive symptom management. Although some pharmacological agents, such as agomelatine, have shown dual efficacy, their clinical application is limited by hepatotoxicity risks. Therefore, the development of antidepressant monotherapy with a favourable hepatic safety profile and proven efficacy in concurrently ameliorating both depressive symptoms and comorbid insomnia represents an unmet clinical need and a promising therapeutic opportunity.

GW117 is a multitarget agent that acts as an MT1/MT2 receptor agonist and a 5-HT2C receptor antagonist. It exerts therapeutic effects on both circadian rhythm disturbances and affective symptoms in patients with MDD through synergistic mechanisms.5,7 Preclinical studies have confirmed its significant antidepressant efficacy across various classical behavioural models.6 7 GW117 demonstrates favourable pharmacokinetic characteristics, including good oral bioavailability, rapid absorption, high blood–brain barrier permeability and preferential distribution in the central nervous system over peripheral tissues. Notably, comprehensive safety evaluations revealed no significant toxic effects on the central nervous, respiratory, cardiovascular or reproductive systems in experimental animals at all tested doses.8,10 Furthermore, haematological, biochemical and histopathological evaluations, particularly hepatic function parameters, showed no significant drug-related pathological alterations.

A phase 1 clinical study in healthy Chinese volunteers (ID: ChiCTR2100043858) demonstrated favourable pharmacokinetic properties for GW117, including rapid absorption with dose-dependent increases in plasma exposure following single-dose administration and no significant accumulation after multiple doses. The overall safety and tolerability profiles were satisfactory, particularly noting an excellent hepatic safety profile with no specific safety concerns.

This phase 2 study evaluated three dose levels (20, 40 and 60 mg/day) in patients with MDD. The primary objectives were to preliminarily evaluate the efficacy and safety of GW117 in patients with MDD and to explore the optimal dosing regimen for subsequent pivotal studies.

Methods

This multicentre, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase 2 dose-finding study was conducted across 17 clinical sites in China from June 2022 to November 2023. The trial protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to screening.

Patients

Eligible participants were outpatients aged 18–65 years diagnosed with MDD (single or recurrent episode, without psychotic features) according to Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders-5 criteria, with a Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression-17 item (HAMD-17) total score ≥22 and a Clinical Global Impression-Severity (CGI-S) score ≥4 at both screening and baseline assessments.

Key exclusion criteria included: (1) history of treatment-resistant depression; (2) suicide attempt within 1 year prior to screening; (3) active suicidal or self-harm behaviours during screening or baseline periods; (4) a score of ≥3 on Item 3 (Suicide) of the HAMD-17; (5) comorbid psychiatric disorders (eg, anxiety disorders); (6) prior non-response to agomelatine; (7) abnormal baseline alanine aminotransferase/aspartate aminotransferase (ALT/AST) levels or positive hepatitis B surface antigen or hepatitis C virus antibody test results; (8) a significant placebo response (defined as ≥25% reduction in HAMD-17 total score from screening to baseline).

Throughout the study period, participants were prohibited from concomitant use of any antipsychotics, antidepressants, anxiolytics, mood stabilisers or herbal/over-the-counter preparations with purported antidepressant or sedative effects (including dietary supplements). Additionally, strong inhibitors or inducers of CYP1A2, CYP2C9 or CYP2C19 enzymes (eg, fluvoxamine) were strictly excluded. Patients with severe insomnia were permitted short-term use (cumulative duration ≤2 weeks) of zaleplon, zopiclone, eszopiclone or zolpidem.

Study design

The study employed a stratified block randomisation method, with baseline HAMD-17 total score (≤26 vs >26) and gender as stratification factors. Eligible participants were randomly assigned (1:1:1:1) to receive GW117 (20, 40 or 60 mg) or placebo once daily before bedtime for 8 weeks.

This study employed a double-blind, single-dummy design. Randomisation lists for subjects and investigational products were generated by a statistician and uploaded into the Interactive Web Response System (IWRS). On enrolment, investigators at each site accessed the IWRS to obtain a participant’s randomisation number. At each visit for medication dispensing, the system provided the corresponding drug kit number to ensure blinding.

This study comprises three phases: (1) a screening period (3–7 days); (2) a double-blind treatment phase (8 weeks); (3) a drug withdrawal follow-up period (1 week). Following treatment completion, all medications were discontinued, and a safety follow-up was conducted via telephone interview on day 7 post-treatment. An Independent Data Monitoring Committee (IDMC) reviewed safety data after complete follow-up data had been collected from 30% of the enrolled participants. In the event of critical safety events, the sponsor or investigators may request an ad hoc IDMC meeting for dedicated safety evaluation.

This study was retrospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (ID: NCT06796868) on 23 April 2025. All procedures, including participant enrolment, intervention delivery and primary endpoint assessment, were conducted per the prespecified protocol prior to registration.

Endpoints

HAMD-17 and Montgomery-Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) have demonstrated good reliability and validity in global and Chinese populations and are widely used in antidepressant trials.11 The primary efficacy endpoint was the change in HAMD-1711,14 total score from baseline to the end of Week 8. Secondary endpoints included the change in the MADRS11 13 14 total score from baseline to the end of Week 8, the response rates (defined as a ≥50% reduction from baseline in the HAMD-17 or MADRS total scores) at Week 8, and the reduction in the CGI-S score from baseline.

Safety evaluations included adverse events (AEs), vital signs, body weight, physical examinations, electrocardiograms, laboratory tests (with a focus on liver function-related parameters) and suicidal ideation assessment via the Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale. AEs were recorded and coded with the use of the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) (V.26.1), and their relationship to the study drug was assessed by the investigators.

Statistical analysis

This phase 2 dose-finding study does not involve a rigorous sample size estimation based on statistical assumptions. The trial was designed with four groups (GW117 20 mg, 40 mg, 60 mg and placebo), with 70 participants per group (n=280 total), to enable a preliminary assessment of efficacy and safety.

Efficacy analysis was based on the full analysis set (FAS), defined as all randomised participants who received at least one dose of the study medication and had at least one post-baseline assessment for the primary endpoint. A mixed-effects model for repeated measures (MMRM) was applied to analyse changes from baseline in the HAMD-17, MADRS and CGI-S scores at Week 8. The model included the baseline HAMD-17 total score as a covariate, with gender, treatment group, time point and treatment-by-time point interaction as fixed effects; the dependent variable was the change from baseline in the HAMD-17 total score at each post-treatment time point. For the HAMD-17 and MADRS response rates, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using the Clopper-Pearson method. Rate differences (experimental groups vs placebo) and their 95% CIs were derived via the stratified Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel method, incorporating baseline stratification factors. Missing data were primarily handled using the MMRM approach, with stratification factors from randomisation incorporated into the model.

Subgroup analyses were performed based on baseline HAMD-17 total score (≤24 vs >24) and baseline HAMD-17 insomnia factor (sum of items 4–6; ≤4 vs >4). Key efficacy measures included reductions from baseline in the HAMD-17 and MADRS total scores at Week 8, along with corresponding response rates. These subgroup findings were further used to identify optimal response populations within the study cohort. For clinical interpretation, a baseline HAMD-17 total score of >24 points15 16 and a baseline HAMD-17 insomnia factor of >4 points17 18 were prospectively defined as indicative of severe depression and depression with severe insomnia, respectively.

Safety analyses were conducted in the safety population, which included all randomised participants who received at least one dose of the investigational product. AEs were coded using the MedDRA V.26.1. The following parameters were analysed: incidence counts and rates of all AEs, treatment-related AEs (TRAEs), serious AEs (SAEs), TRAEs leading to treatment discontinuation and grade ≥3 AEs. No imputation was performed for missing data in safety endpoints.

Results

Participants

Figure 1 illustrates the study flowchart and patient allocation scheme of this trial. A total of 379 participants were screened, of whom 280 were randomised to receive GW117 20 mg (n=70), 40 mg (n=71), 60 mg (n=69) and placebo (n=70). The safety analysis set included 278 participants (two randomised participants were excluded because one declined to participate due to family objections and the other withdrew consent, with neither receiving the study drug), the FAS included 276 and the per protocol set included 247. The patient recruitment period spanned from May 2022 to August 2023. During the double-blind treatment period, a total of 32 participants (11.43%) withdrew from the trial. The mean baseline HAMD-17 total score for patients was 24.0. With only 23 patients (8.21%) having a baseline HAMD-17 total score >26, the enrolled population predominantly presented with moderate disease severity. Efficacy

In the FAS (n=276), after 8 weeks of treatment, all GW117 dose groups demonstrated greater reductions in the HAMD-17 and MADRS total scores, as well as higher response rates compared with the placebo group (table 2). Specifically, the mean differences in the reduction of the HAMD-17 and MADRS total scores between the GW117 groups and the placebo group ranged from 0.29 to 1.16 and 1.01 to 1.62 points, respectively. The corresponding mean differences in response rates ranged from 10.51% to 17.15% for the HAMD-17 and 9.16% to 12.10% for the MADRS. However, the differences between groups were not statistically significant. Similar patterns were observed in other secondary efficacy indicators, such as the reduction in the CGI-S score. Notably, the placebo response rates were 57.1% on the HAMD-17 and 61.4% on the MADRS, which are substantial and exceed typical placebo responses reported in adult antidepressant trials.19,22

Analysis of stratification factors revealed that the distribution of patients by baseline HAMD-17 total score was skewed, with 253 patients having scores ≤26 and only 23 patients having scores >26, indicating that the analytical value of this stratification factor was relatively limited. Furthermore, no statistically significant differences were observed in the primary efficacy endpoint and key secondary endpoints between male and female participants.

Subgroup analyses were conducted in patients with severe baseline depression (HAMD-17 total score >24, n=89). After 8 weeks of treatment, all GW117 groups demonstrated greater reductions in HAMD-17 and MADRS total scores from baseline compared with placebo (table 2, online supplemental figure 1). The differences in the reduction of the HAM-D17 total score were −2.05 (−5.72 to 1.61), −0.53 (−4.18 to 3.13) and −1.06 (−5.09 to 2.96) for the 20 mg, 40 mg and 60 mg groups, respectively. The corresponding differences in the reduction of the MADRS total score were −4.86 (−9.91 to 0.18), −3.02 (−8.01 to 1.98) and −2.86 (−8.37 to 2.65). Response rates in the placebo group were markedly lower than in the FAS (HAMD-17: 50.00% vs 57.14%, MADRS: 50.00% vs 61.43%). This indicates that the baseline HAMD-17 total score is a sensitive factor affecting the efficacy outcomes. Specifically, higher baseline depression severity was associated with lower placebo effect, consistent with the findings from previous studies.23,26 Therefore, optimising baseline severity thresholds may effectively mitigate the placebo effect, thereby facilitating a more objective and comprehensive evaluation of investigational drug efficacy.

In patients with MDD presenting with severe insomnia symptoms at baseline (HAMD-17 insomnia factor >4; n=166), all GW117 groups demonstrated greater reductions from baseline in both HAMD-17 and MADRS total scores compared with the placebo group following 8 weeks of treatment (table 2, online supplemental figure 1). Specifically, the differences in the reduction of the HAMD-17 total score were −1.79 (−4.27 to 0.69), −1.48 (−3.90 to 0.94) and −1.36 (−3.86 to 1.14) for the 20 mg, 40 mg and 60 mg groups, respectively. The corresponding differences in the reduction of the MADRS total score were −3.07 (−6.56 to 0.42), −3.13 (−6.53 to 0.28) and −1.87 (−5.38 to 1.64). Compared with the FAS, response rates in the GW117 groups were markedly increased, while those in the placebo group remained essentially unchanged (HAMD-17: 56.52% vs 57.14%, MADRS: 60.87% vs 61.43%). These results indicate that the baseline HAMD-17 insomnia factor is a sensitive factor affecting the efficacy outcomes, with GW117 demonstrating enhanced antidepressant efficacy among patients with MDD presenting with severe insomnia symptoms.

In an exploratory post hoc analysis of the two sensitive factors, we examined patients with milder baseline symptoms. Among patients with an HAMD-17 total score ≤24 (n=187) and an HAMD-17 insomnia factor ≤4 (n=110), all GW117 groups demonstrated lower overall efficacy compared with the FAS after 8-week treatment (online supplemental table 1). We then defined a subgroup (n=84) with both mild depressive symptoms and non-severe insomnia (HAMD-17 total score ≤24 and insomnia factor ≤4). Compared with the FAS, this subgroup showed poorer efficacy outcomes, with none of the GW117 groups demonstrating greater reductions in the HAMD-17 or MADRS total scores or higher response rates compared with placebo (online supplemental table 2), suggesting that this subgroup is treatment-insensitive.

After excluding this treatment-insensitive population, we identified optimal beneficiaries for GW117 (HAMD-17 total score >24 or insomnia factor score >4, n=192). As anticipated, in this subgroup at Week 8, all GW117 dose groups demonstrated substantially greater efficacy compared with placebo (online supplemental table 3). Specifically, the differences in mean reductions (vs placebo) for the 20 mg, 40 mg and 60 mg GW117 groups were −2.29 (−4.64 to 0.06), −0.99 (−3.32 to 1.34) and −1.09 (−3.50 to 1.33) for the HAMD-17 total score, and −3.66 (−6.89 to −0.44), –2.22 (−5.41 to 0.98) and −1.81 (−5.11 to 1.49) for the MADRS total score. The corresponding differences in response rates for the HAMD-17 and MADRS reached 20.83%–22.49% and 16.22%–23.16%, respectively. These results confirm this subgroup as the optimal response subgroup (n=192) (figure 2A).

Further integrated analysis of efficacy endpoints in the optimal responder subgroup evaluated changes in total HAMD-17/MADRS scores, HAMD-17/MADRS sleep items and HAMD-17/MADRS total scores excluding sleep items and revealed substantial improvements for all GW117 dose groups versus placebo (figure 2B). Notably, the 20 mg group demonstrated statistically significant differences across all three MADRS-based measures, indicating that GW117 provides concomitant improvement of both depressive symptoms and comorbid insomnia, with antidepressant effects that are independent of sleep improvement. Based on these findings, a phase 2 clinical trial application for GW117 as a treatment of insomnia was submitted and approved by the China National Medical Products Administration.

Safety

GW117 was generally well-tolerated. Most treatment-emergent AEs (TEAEs) were mild (Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) grade 1). The incidence of TRAEs of grade 2 or higher was slightly lower in the GW117 groups than in the placebo group, with comparable rates across all doses. TRAEs with an incidence of ≥5% were primarily nervous system disorders, which occurred at a slightly higher frequency in GW117 groups than in the placebo group. Dizziness was the most common TRAE (placebo: 4.29%; 20 mg: 13.04%; 40 mg: 8.57%; 60 mg: 7.25%), followed by headache (placebo: 4.29%; 20 mg/60 mg: 7.25%; 40 mg: 5.71%). Somnolence was reported in the GW117 groups (20 mg: 5.80%; 40 mg: 2.86%; 60 mg: 1.45%) but not in the placebo group. The incidence of gastrointestinal TRAEs, such as dry mouth and nausea, was comparable between the GW117 groups and the placebo group. Discontinuation rates due to TEAEs were similar across all groups (table 3).

SAEs were reported in six patients. The incidence of SAEs in the GW117 groups was lower than that in the placebo group, with three in the placebo group (depression, bipolar disorder, lumbar fracture), one in the 20 mg group (depression) and two in the 40 mg group (ovarian cyst, angle-closure glaucoma). All SAEs resolved following hospitalisation. No cases of ALT/AST >3× upper limit of normal (ULN) were reported. The incidence of mild treatment-related liver enzyme elevations was comparable between the GW117 groups and the placebo group. No participants discontinued the study due to elevated liver enzymes, and no actual suicidal behaviours were reported.

After 30% of the participants had completed the collection of all follow-up data, the IDMC convened a safety data review meeting. No observable risk factors were identified, and the decision was made to continue the study as per the existing protocol.

Discussion

Main finding

Efficacy analysis

In this phase 2 clinical trial, GW117 demonstrated numerically greater improvements over placebo across all efficacy endpoints, although these differences did not reach statistical significance. This outcome is likely attributable to a substantial placebo response, a well-established challenge in psychiatric drug development. Our analysis identified several contributing factors. First, the enrolled population had lower baseline severity of depression than that typically observed in other MDD trials. Multiple meta-analyses have indicated an inverse correlation between the baseline severity of depression and the placebo effect, meaning patients with milder depression tend to exhibit a higher placebo effect. Consequently, contemporary antidepressant clinical trials predominantly enrol populations with greater baseline severity. For example, Auvility reported a baseline MADRS mean of 33.4 in its positive trials27 28; the four positive trials supporting vortioxetine’s New Drug Application (NDA) submission (11 492A/13 267A/315/316) had mean baseline MADRS scores of 34.0, 31.4, 32.1 and 32.2, respectively29; and agomelatine, a compound with a mechanism similar to GW117, had a mean baseline HAMD-17 score of approximately 27 in its positive trials.30 In addition to baseline severity, substantial intersite heterogeneity in the placebo effect was observed in our study, with placebo response rates reaching 100% at the treatment endpoint in multiple centres. Furthermore, this study was conducted during the coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic. Factors such as remote assessments and lockdowns may have interfered with rating procedures and potentially exacerbated the placebo effect.31,33

Both greater baseline depression severity and the presence of severe insomnia symptoms were identified as predictive factors for GW117 response. The established inverse correlation between depression severity and the placebo effect was confirmed in our subgroup analysis of patients with an HAMD-17 total score >24. Furthermore, the subgroup with an insomnia factor >4 provided compelling evidence that GW117’s efficacy markedly improved with increasing insomnia severity. This effect was not seen in the placebo group and was directly attributable to GW117’s mechanism of action. Currently, approved antidepressants specifically designed to simultaneously improve both depression and insomnia remain extremely scarce. Beyond the mechanistically similar compound agomelatine and the relatively weak antidepressant trazodone,34 the only other investigational agent is seltorexant, which is currently only in phase 1b trials as a monotherapy for MDD.35 A post hoc analysis defined an optimal response population by excluding non-responders (HAMD-17 total score ≤24 and insomnia factor ≤4). In this group, GW117 demonstrated significant, multidimensional improvement in depressive symptoms, including insomnia, with the 20 mg dose group exhibiting statistically significant and clinically meaningful efficacy.

The MADRS demonstrated greater sensitivity than the HAMD-17 in capturing the efficacy of GW117. Although the HAMD-17 was the primary endpoint in this study, the MADRS, as a secondary endpoint, proved more effective in detecting treatment effects. This is likely due to its focused assessment of core MDD symptoms and a wider per-item score range (0–6 points), which provides greater sensitivity in evaluating symptom changes. Based on these findings, the MADRS will be used as the primary efficacy endpoint assessment scale in subsequent clinical studies, with the HAMD-17 as the secondary efficacy assessment scale for further observation and validation.

Safety analysis

GW117 demonstrated a favourable safety profile and treatment compliance in this study, with no specific safety risks identified. No deaths occurred during the entire trial period. Most TEAEs were CTCAE Grade 1 and resolved without medical intervention. The incidence of CTCAE Grade 2 or higher TRAEs, SAEs and discontinuations due to TEAEs in the GW117 groups did not exceed the placebo group. No actual suicidal behaviour was reported; suicidal ideation markedly decreased after enrolment, with a greater reduction in the GW117 groups than in the placebo group.

Neurological AEs, such as headache, dizziness and somnolence, were more common with GW117. This observation is consistent with its dual-target mechanism of action on MT1/2 and 5-HT2C receptors, which involves coordinated modulation of both sleep–wake regulatory centres and the serotonergic system. Notably, the vast majority of these AEs were mild and transient, did not lead to serious clinical outcomes or trial discontinuation and were therefore considered expected and manageable.

GW117 demonstrated a favourable safety profile compared with traditional antidepressants, such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors and serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (eg, sertraline, escitalopram and vortioxetine) and other mechanism-based drugs such as agomelatine. Throughout the clinical study, no GW117-treated patients experienced common adverse reactions of second-generation antidepressants, such as sexual dysfunction, withdrawal reactions or weight gain.36,46 A particular advantage was observed in hepatic safety: no instances of ALT/AST >3× ULN were reported, and the incidence of mild hepatic enzyme elevations (ALT/AST >1× ULN) was comparable to placebo.

Drug-induced liver injury is a leading cause of drug withdrawal and label modification.47,49 Agomelatine, for instance, effectively alleviates depression and comorbid insomnia with a generally favourable tolerability profile.50 51 However, its notable dose-dependent hepatotoxicity has led to its rejection for marketing in the USA and prompted strict risk-management measures in Europe, mandating standardised liver enzyme monitoring before and during treatment.52,54 These requirements considerably limit its clinical utility and pose a significant burden on patients. More alarmingly, real-world data indicate that adherence to these monitoring protocols is highly inconsistent: only 7%–25% of patients undergo baseline liver function assessment, and 17%–60% of patients receive standardised liver enzyme monitoring during treatment.55,57 With agomelatine’s use increasing in markets like China, this safety concern exposes a growing population to risk. In this context, GW117 demonstrates a favourable overall safety profile with no observed hepatotoxicity, offering a potentially safer alternative that may improve adherence and therapeutic outcomes for patients with MDD.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as an exploratory study, the sample size was relatively small, and larger-scale studies are needed to further confirm these findings. Second, the high placebo response obscured the dose–response relationship, a phenomenon consistent with prior clinical studies and meta-analyses of other antidepressants (eg, agomelatine, paroxetine, fluoxetine and esketamine).58,62 The dose–response relationship of GW117 will be further validated in subsequent clinical studies. Third, baseline data showed that most patients had HAMD-17 total scores clustered in the 22–24 point range. This excessive concentration in the lower severity range resulted in an overall skewed distribution, potentially introducing trial bias. This issue will be addressed in subsequent clinical trials. Fourth, the identification of the optimal patient population, while mechanistically justified, was conducted post hoc. Future analysis requires prospective validation in a dedicated confirmatory trial designed and powered for this specific subgroup. Finally, this study is registered retrospectively. While all procedures were conducted strictly in accordance with the prespecified protocol, retrospective registration may introduce potential biases and complicate result interpretation. Future confirmatory trials will implement prospective registration to enhance scientific rigour and transparency.

Implications

While no statistically significant difference in efficacy was observed between GW117 and placebo in the overall population of this study, prespecified analyses identified two key predictors of treatment response: baseline depression severity and insomnia severity. Post hoc analysis further defined the optimal response population for GW117. These findings, consistent with its mechanism of action, corroborated common challenges in antidepressant development while providing novel insights to guide future research on GW117 and similar agents.

Conclusion

In this exploratory phase 2 trial, GW117 demonstrated a numerical advantage over placebo in the overall population, though statistical significance was not reached. Analysis identified greater baseline depression severity and severe insomnia symptoms as predictive factors for GW117 treatment response. A post hoc analysis suggested that patients with a HAMD-17 total score >24 or insomnia factor >4 may derive greater benefit from GW117 monotherapy, showing improvement across multiple depressive symptom domains, including insomnia. The 20 mg group demonstrated clinically and statistically significant improvement. Additionally, GW117 exhibited a favourable safety profile and good treatment compliance, notably without emergent liver safety signals, thus preliminarily achieving its design objectives and supporting further clinical development.
