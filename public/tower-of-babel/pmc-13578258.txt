Separating predictive accuracy from functional behaviour in machine-learning surrogate models for geoengineering applications
Authors: Mateusz Żeruń
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMachine-learning regression models are increasingly used as surrogate parameterisations in geoengineering workflows, where they function as continuous mappings across predictor domains rather than as pointwise predictors. However, model evaluation remains dominated by predictive accuracy metrics, which provide limited insight into surrogate behaviour between observations. This study demonstrates that predictive accuracy and functional behaviour represent distinct and complementary dimensions of surrogate adequacy. Using a controlled laboratory dataset describing thermal conductivity as a function of water content and bulk density, regression models with contrasting inductive biases are analysed as continuous response surfaces over the empirically supported predictor domain. A post hoc diagnostic framework is formalised to evaluate surrogate behaviour in terms of response-surface geometry, monotonicity, smoothness, local predictor effects, and residual structure across the predictor domain. Results demonstrate that models with comparable predictive accuracy can produce substantially different response-surface structures, revealing, within the analysed setting, a broad trade-off between numerical accuracy and functional regularity that is consistent with a strong influence of model inductive bias. To support comparative evaluation, a Functional Inconsistency Index (FII) is defined as a composite post hoc indicator of undesirable surrogate behaviour. The index does not measure physical validity or enforce governing equations but provides a reproducible heuristic measure based on monotonicity violations, normalised surface roughness, and output-range violations. Combined with conventional accuracy metrics, FII enables models to be positioned within an explicit accuracy–inconsistency decision space. The results highlight that predictive accuracy alone may be insufficient for evaluating machine-learning surrogates when they are reused as continuous parameterisations in modelling workflows. Introduction

Machine-learning models are increasingly embedded in geoengineering workflows as surrogate parameterisations approximating empirical relationships and material properties across continuous ranges of state variables. In practical applications, trained regression models are queried over such ranges to support spatial interpolation, mapping, optimisation, scenario analysis and coupling with numerical simulators1–3. In this role, a trained model functions as a component of a broader computational system, implicitly representing a continuous relationship between physical variables.

When used as surrogates, model adequacy cannot be assessed solely through predictive accuracy at calibration or test points. A regression model may achieve low test-set error while encoding response surfaces that are segmented, locally unstable or qualitatively inconsistent between observations. In geoengineering applications, such irregularities may propagate into interpolated parameter fields and introduce artificial gradients or discontinuities, with potential consequences for the robustness of downstream simulations. These potential effects arise from how the learned function behaves across the predictor domain—an aspect largely invisible to conventional error metrics4–6.

Despite the widespread use of machine-learning surrogates, evaluation practices remain dominated by pointwise predictive metrics such as root mean squared error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE) and the coefficient of determination. Model selection is typically driven by minimisation of prediction error on held-out datasets, implicitly assuming that numerical accuracy ensures functional adequacy. This reflects the prediction-oriented paradigm of statistical learning7,8, where accurate reproduction of observed targets is the primary objective. However, when models are interpreted as continuous parameterisations embedded within numerical workflows, predictive accuracy alone provides limited insight into behavioural suitability.

Recent studies illustrate this imbalance. For example, Zhao et al.9 report substantial improvements in the predictive performance of machine-learning approaches for estimating soil thermal conductivity relative to empirical formulations, yet their evaluation focuses almost exclusively on pointwise error statistics. Similar patterns occur across surrogate-modelling applications, where numerical gains are rarely accompanied by systematic assessment of response-surface geometry, monotonicity, smoothness or local stability1,10.

Existing approaches provide partial insight into model behaviour. Explainable machine-learning techniques, including partial dependence plots and individual conditional expectation curves, characterise marginal predictor effects11,12, while physics-informed and theory-guided approaches incorporate physical constraints during training3,13. However, these approaches do not address a common practical problem: evaluating the functional behaviour of already trained models when reused post hoc as surrogate parameterisations, particularly when retraining or reformulation is not feasible1,14.

From a methodological perspective, evaluation of surrogate behaviour constitutes a problem distinct from predictive performance. In geoengineering modelling practice, behavioural adequacy of model components has long been recognised as independent of calibration quality6,15,16. Nevertheless, operational frameworks for diagnosing the functional behaviour of machine-learning surrogates remain limited, allowing structurally inconsistent behaviour to remain undetected during downstream use.

An earlier application-oriented study using the same laboratory dataset compared machine-learning parameterisations with selected empirical formulations and reported differences between pointwise predictive accuracy and the functional regularity of reconstructed response surfaces17. The present study builds on that application by formalising the evaluation problem from a surrogate-modelling perspective and extending the assessment across controlled model configurations and repeated data partitions.

This study therefore addresses the identified methodological gap by formalising surrogate evaluation as a dual-objective problem that explicitly separates predictive accuracy from functional behaviour. The central premise is that a trained regression model used as a surrogate should be evaluated not only as a predictor, but also as a continuous functional representation. Rather than introducing new learning algorithms, a post hoc diagnostic framework is applied that treats trained models as response surfaces defined over the predictor domain. The framework evaluates global surface geometry, monotonicity, smoothness, local predictor effects and residual structure across the predictor domain using grid-based representations restricted to empirically supported regions. All diagnostics are computed after model training and do not influence calibration.

To support systematic comparison, a Functional Inconsistency Index (FII) is defined as a composite post hoc indicator of undesirable surrogate behaviour. FII combines domain-specific monotonicity violations, normalised surface roughness and output-range violations. It does not constitute a measure of physical validity and does not enforce governing equations; instead, it provides a transparent and reproducible heuristic for comparing selected aspects of functional behaviour alongside conventional accuracy metrics.

The framework is demonstrated using a controlled laboratory dataset describing thermal conductivity as a function of water content and bulk density. The deliberately low-dimensional setting is used to isolate model-dependent behaviour under controlled conditions and to reduce the influence of interaction complexity and data heterogeneity. Within this setting, regression models with contrasting inductive biases exhibit broad trade-offs between predictive accuracy and functional regularity across the evaluated hyperparameter configurations, while sensitivity to data partitioning is examined separately using an auxiliary analysis.

Although illustrated using thermal-conductivity data, the proposed framework may be transferable to other geoengineering problems in which machine-learning models are reused as continuous parameterisations within modelling workflows. It is not intended to replace conventional predictive-performance metrics, but to complement them through explicit evaluation of functional behaviour. Its diagnostic criteria remain context-dependent and require domain-specific definition and further validation before application to more complex, higher-dimensional modelling problems.

Materials and methods

Case study and data preparation

The case study was designed as a controlled testbed for diagnosing the functional behaviour of machine-learning surrogate models rather than for domain-specific analysis of thermal conductivity. The objective was to isolate model-dependent effects and examine how different regression approaches encode continuous functional relationships within a physically meaningful two-dimensional predictor space when reused as surrogate parameterisations in geoengineering workflows.

The analysis is based on a large, internally consistent laboratory dataset obtained from a single standardised testing programme and previously used in an application-oriented study of machine-learning parameterisation of thermal conductivity in moist, quartz-rich sands17. Reusing a unified data source reduces inconsistencies associated with merging heterogeneous datasets and limits confounding effects related to differences in sampling, laboratory procedures and data provenance.

Each observation is defined by two experimentally measured state variables, water content (w) and bulk density (), together with the corresponding thermal conductivity (λ). Prior to modelling, the dataset underwent basic quality control to remove incomplete records and values outside experimentally meaningful ranges. The dataset retained for moisture-domain assessment comprised 1716 observations. Exploratory analysis identified 261 samples with water content below 1%, characterised by limited moisture variability and representing a distinct air-dry laboratory regime rather than the continuous moist-sand domain targeted in this study. Including this subgroup could introduce an artificial low-moisture regime into the reconstructed response surfaces. A conservative threshold of was therefore adopted to define the modelling domain. This filtering step was used to delimit the intended domain of surrogate validity rather than to optimise predictive performance or favour a particular model family. A detailed justification is provided in Supplementary Section S1 and in the earlier application-oriented study17.

After filtering, the final dataset comprised N=1455 samples spanning the retained combinations of water content and bulk density. All model training and predictive evaluation were performed using this filtered dataset, while the domain definitions used for the individual response-surface diagnostics are specified in the corresponding methodological sections.

Data preprocessing was intentionally minimal. No imputation, feature engineering or nonlinear transformation of predictors was applied. Algorithm-specific scaling was used only where required and was fitted exclusively on the training subset to prevent information leakage. Surrogate modelling framework

In this study, machine-learning regression models are used as surrogate representations of a material parameter rather than as tools for isolated pointwise prediction. Trained models are interpreted as continuous mappings between measured state variables and thermal conductivity, consistent with their role as computational substitutes for empirical formulations or as components of broader geoengineering and environmental modelling workflows1,3,18.

A central premise is that surrogate modelling is defined by model usage rather than algorithm type. Although models are trained in a supervised setting using laboratory observations, their outputs are treated as reusable functional representations of the relationship between water content, bulk density and thermal conductivity across the empirically supported predictor domain. In this context, predictive accuracy and functional behaviour constitute distinct evaluation objectives. A model may reproduce observed values with low error while encoding response surfaces that exhibit segmentation, excessive local variability or trends that are inconsistent with the domain-specific expectations adopted for the analysed material. Such distinctions between calibration performance and internal model behaviour are well established in geoengineering and environmental modelling5,6,15.

To operationalise this distinction, trained models are analysed as two-dimensional response surfaces defined over the joint predictor space of water content and bulk density. After training, surrogate outputs are evaluated on regular grids spanning the observed predictor ranges. These grid-based representations support the analysis of response-surface geometry and local predictor effects. Convex-hull masking is applied to response-surface visualisation and selected domain-restricted interpretive diagnostics to limit the influence of unsupported extrapolation. Residual structure is evaluated separately using predictions for the held-out test observations, while the evaluation domain used for calculating the Functional Inconsistency Index is specified in “Functional inconsistency index and model comparison” section.

This distinction provides the methodological basis for the response-surface diagnostics introduced in “Evaluation of surrogate behaviour” section.

Machine-learning models and training

To examine how different modelling strategies behave when interpreted as surrogate parameterisations, regression algorithms with contrasting inductive biases were selected. Inductive bias refers to the structural assumptions embedded in a learning algorithm that constrain the class of admissible functions and influence how relationships are inferred from finite observations7,8,20. When models are reused as continuous surrogates, these assumptions can affect surface smoothness, locality, structural segmentation and behaviour across the predictor domain.

Five regression-model families were analysed: Support Vector Regression (SVR), Decision Tree regression (DT), Random Forest (RF), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) and k-Nearest Neighbours (kNN). These algorithms represent structurally distinct modelling strategies commonly used in geoengineering and environmental applications. The model set was selected to span contrasting regression structures rather than to provide an exhaustive benchmark of all available machine-learning architectures.

Support Vector Regression with a radial basis function kernel was adopted as a representative globally regularised model. SVR constructs surrogate functions through kernel-based regularisation that balances data fidelity and functional smoothness, promoting coherent response surfaces while limiting excessive local variation21.

Tree-based models were represented by single Decision Trees and two ensemble extensions, Random Forest and Extreme Gradient Boosting. These methods approximate nonlinear relationships through recursive partitioning of the predictor space and, in the ensemble models, aggregation of multiple tree structures11,22,23. Their inductive bias emphasises local adaptivity and may produce segmented or piecewise response surfaces when the fitted models are interpreted as continuous surrogates.

Instance-based learning was represented by the k-Nearest Neighbours algorithm, in which predictions are derived from local neighbourhoods in the predictor space without constructing an explicit global function24. This approach exhibits limited global regularisation and sensitivity to local sampling density, which may result in locally variable surrogate behaviour.

For each model family, five hyperparameter configurations were evaluated: a baseline configuration, an accuracy-tuned configuration selected using cross-validation, and three controlled variants representing different model configurations. These configurations were treated as variants within a fixed model family rather than as independent model types. The objective was not exhaustive optimisation or benchmarking of every possible model configuration, but controlled assessment of how tuning and regularisation influence surrogate behaviour within each structural class. The complete configurations and tuning ranges are reported in Supplementary Tables S2a–S2e.

All models were trained using the same general modelling workflow to support comparability across model families and hyperparameter variants. The dataset was partitioned into training and test subsets using a fixed 80/20 random split with a predefined random state. Standardisation was applied within modelling pipelines only for SVR and kNN, whereas tree-based models were trained using unscaled predictors. All preprocessing parameters were fitted and all hyperparameters were selected exclusively using the training subset. Hyperparameter tuning was performed by cross-validation within the training subset, ensuring that the independent test data did not influence model calibration8,25.

No functional diagnostics, including the Functional Inconsistency Index (FII), were used during model training, hyperparameter tuning or variant definition. Predictive and functional evaluations were performed separately after model fitting. Held-out test predictions were used for independent predictive checking and residual analysis, whereas the summary statistics presented for the representative models in Table 2 and Fig. 9 are reported as overall reconstruction metrics, as described in the corresponding results and figure captions.

Evaluation of surrogate behaviour

When machine-learning models are reused as surrogate parameterisations within geoengineering workflows, their suitability cannot be assessed solely through pointwise predictive accuracy. In such applications, trained models may be queried across continuous ranges of state variables and used as functional components within broader modelling systems. Evaluation should therefore consider how model predictions behave across the predictor domain, particularly when the resulting parameterisations are subsequently used for interpolation, mapping or numerical coupling1,4,15. These downstream applications provide the motivation for the present diagnostics but were not directly evaluated in this study.

Within the analysed domain of moist, quartz-rich sands, thermal conductivity is treated as a continuous, state-dependent material property that is expected to vary gradually and generally increase with water content and bulk density. Classical formulations therefore represent thermal conductivity as a continuous function of these variables26–28. These directional expectations are specific to the material class and predictor domain considered here and do not constitute universal requirements for other geomaterials or environmental conditions. In the present case, abrupt discontinuities, excessive local oscillations or strongly segmented regimes are treated as undesirable unless supported by the observed material response.

Surrogate behaviour is assessed using a set of complementary post hoc diagnostics targeting structural properties of model-predicted relationships rather than agreement with observations alone. Grid-based response-surface diagnostics are derived from model predictions after training, without modifying the fitted models. Partial dependence and individual conditional expectation analyses provide complementary information on predictor effects, whereas residual diagnostics are evaluated separately using held-out test observations. This approach follows established principles in environmental model evaluation, where behavioural adequacy is examined separately from calibration metrics2,6,29.

Response-surface evaluation: Each trained regression model is interpreted as a continuous mapping over the joint predictor space of water content and bulk density. Response surfaces are constructed by evaluating model outputs on regular grids spanning the observed predictor ranges, enabling analysis of global surface geometry, continuity and segmentation. For visualisation and selected interpretive diagnostics, grid nodes outside the convex hull of the training data are masked to limit the influence of unsupported extrapolation and boundary artefacts19. The computational domain used for the Functional Inconsistency Index is defined separately in “Functional inconsistency index and model comparison” section.

Monotonicity diagnostics: Within the analysed moist-sand domain, thermal conductivity is generally expected to increase with water content and bulk density. Local monotonicity is evaluated using first-order finite differences along each predictor direction. Negative directional differences are recorded as violations of the adopted monotonicity criterion19,30. This criterion is specific to the analysed material and predictor domain; in applications involving expected non-monotonic relationships, it would require modification, restriction to selected subdomains or omission.

Surface roughness: Local variation of the predicted response surfaces is assessed using first-order gradients with respect to water content and bulk density. Larger gradient-based roughness values indicate more rapid local changes in the reconstructed relationship. The exact definition and normalisation procedure used in the Functional Inconsistency Index are provided in “Functional inconsistency index and model comparison” section. Roughness is interpreted as a relative diagnostic of surface geometry rather than as a direct measure of physical validity or numerical stability.

Partial dependence and individual conditional expectation: Predictor effects are analysed using Partial Dependence Plots (PDP) and Individual Conditional Expectation (ICE) curves. PDPs summarise average marginal predictor effects11, whereas ICE curves reveal observation-specific response trajectories and heterogeneity12. Their comparison helps identify differences between average and local model behaviour that may not be visible in aggregated summaries14,31. PDP and ICE analyses are complementary diagnostics and are not components of the Functional Inconsistency Index.

Residual structure across the predictor domain: Prediction residuals for the held-out test observations are analysed as functions of water content and bulk density to identify regime-dependent biases or clustering of errors. Unlike aggregate accuracy metrics, this analysis shows whether prediction errors are distributed systematically across the observed predictor space16,32. Residual diagnostics are not calculated from the regular response-surface grid and are not components of the Functional Inconsistency Index.

Together, these diagnostics provide a structured, multi-perspective assessment of surrogate behaviour when regression models are interpreted as continuous parameterisations. A schematic overview of the diagnostic framework is shown in Fig. 3.

Functional inconsistency index and model comparison

The diagnostics introduced in “Evaluation of surrogate behaviour” section describe complementary aspects of surrogate behaviour, including directional coherence, surface roughness, local predictor effects, residual structure and the occurrence of predictions outside the adopted output range. Considered jointly, these diagnostics provide a multidimensional description of surrogate behaviour, which may complicate direct comparison between model configurations. Similar challenges arise in environmental and hydrological modelling, where multiple diagnostic criteria are considered alongside conventional error metrics to support transparent model comparison15,16.

To facilitate structured comparison, a Functional Inconsistency Index (FII) is defined as a composite post hoc indicator integrating three selected properties of surrogate response surfaces: monotonicity violations, normalised surface roughness and output-range violations. FII does not measure physical validity, verify compliance with governing equations or introduce constraints during model fitting. It provides a transparent heuristic for comparing selected aspects of functional behaviour under explicitly defined, case-specific criteria.

All FII components are derived exclusively from surrogate outputs and do not directly involve observed target values. FII is not used during model training, hyperparameter tuning or configuration definition. FII is interpreted strictly as a relative and context-dependent indicator. Its values are directly comparable only when the same dataset, predictor ranges, evaluation grid, component definitions, weighting scheme and roughness reference are retained. Lower FII values indicate fewer functional inconsistencies according to the adopted diagnostic criteria.

A low FII does not, by itself, demonstrate that a model is accurate, physically valid or suitable for surrogate use. For example, an excessively smooth, nearly flat or underfitted response surface may receive a low FII despite providing a poor representation of the observations. FII must therefore be interpreted together with conventional performance metrics and the individual behavioural diagnostics.

To compare numerical error and functional inconsistency, model configurations are positioned in a two-dimensional RMSE–FII space, in which lower values of both objectives are preferred. Model configurations that cannot be improved simultaneously in both dimensions are considered Pareto-efficient within this decision space2,15. Figure 4 presents the general decision concept. The framework does not identify a universally optimal model or prescribe a single balance between numerical accuracy and functional behaviour. Instead, it provides a transparent representation of the trade-offs among the evaluated configurations and supports model selection according to application-specific priorities. FII should therefore be treated as an organisational and comparative diagnostic that complements, rather than replaces, detailed inspection of the individual response-surface properties.

Implementation and reproducibility

The modelling and diagnostic analyses were implemented in Python as a modular workflow integrating data preparation, model training, response-surface evaluation, functional diagnostics and visualisation. The same general computational procedure was applied across all model families to support consistent comparison and transparent documentation of the analysis33–35.

Data filtering and train–test partitioning were applied uniformly across the evaluated models. Feature scaling was used only for algorithms that required it and was fitted exclusively on the training subset. Model fitting, hyperparameter tuning and post hoc diagnostic calculations were conducted using the procedures described in “Machine-learning models and training”, “Evaluation of surrogate behavior” and “Functional inconsistency index and model comparison” sections15,19.

Fixed random seeds were used for the principal train–test split, cross-validation procedures, stochastic model components and repeated-partition analysis. The relevant seeds, model configurations and software dependencies are documented in the accompanying repository. This supports repeatable execution of the computational workflow under the specified software environment but does not constitute predictive uncertainty quantification or guarantee bitwise-identical outputs across all platforms and library versions.

The source code, configuration files and implementation documentation are deposited in a https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21414810, with the permanent identifier provided in the Code Availability section. The archive includes the diagnostic workflow, model and hyperparameter configurations, scripts for the main and repeated-partition analyses, environment information and a synthetic demonstration dataset.

The original laboratory data cannot be distributed publicly because of institutional restrictions. Consequently, the demonstration dataset is provided to document the structure and execution of the workflow rather than to reproduce the exact numerical values, tables or figures reported in the manuscript. Exact numerical reproduction requires access to the original authorised dataset. Additional implementation details and guidance for executing the archived workflow are provided in Supplementary Section S8.

Results

Overview of predictive performance and surrogate behaviour

Comparative evaluation of numerical performance and surrogate behaviour reveals broad differences among the analysed regression approaches. Because all models were developed using the same dataset, preprocessing rules and modelling workflow, differences arising from data handling were minimised. The observed contrasts are consistent with the effects of model structure, regularisation and the evaluated hyperparameter configurations.

Within each model family, multiple hyperparameter configurations were evaluated to examine how tuning influences surrogate behaviour within a shared modelling approach. These configurations are treated as variants of the same model family rather than as independent modelling paradigms, allowing assessment of whether tuning changes characteristic response-surface behaviour or primarily modifies numerical performance.

Across model families, numerical performance and functional behaviour do not vary in parallel. Several configurations with low overall reconstruction error produce segmented, locally irregular or non-monotonic response surfaces when evaluated across the predictor domain. Conversely, configurations generating smoother and more directionally coherent surrogate representations generally exhibit higher reconstruction error, consistent with stronger regularisation. This pattern indicates that numerical accuracy and functional behaviour constitute complementary and potentially competing evaluation dimensions when regression models are reused as continuous parameterisations.

Distinct structural tendencies emerge across the evaluated model families. Support Vector Regression produces a smooth response surface and the lowest Functional Inconsistency Index (FII) among the representative configurations, but also exhibits higher reconstruction error. In contrast, the representative Random Forest, XGBoost and kNN configurations achieve lower reconstruction errors while showing stronger local adaptivity, surface segmentation and higher FII values. The representative Decision Tree occupies an intermediate position, combining lower FII than the ensemble and instance-based configurations with weaker numerical performance.

Hyperparameter variation modifies both numerical performance and surrogate geometry within each model family. Within the evaluated configurations, accuracy-oriented variants generally exhibit greater local adaptivity, whereas more strongly regularised variants tend to produce smoother response surfaces at the expense of numerical fit. The evaluated configurations therefore do not eliminate the broader differences observed among the model families, although the exact ordering of individual variants is not invariant.

Global response-surface characteristics

To examine the structural origins of the behavioural differences described in “Overview of predictive performance and surrogate behaviour” section, the representative surrogate models were analysed as response surfaces defined over the joint predictor domain of water content and bulk density. Model outputs were evaluated on identical regular grids spanning the observed predictor ranges. For visualisation, grid nodes outside the convex hull of the training data were masked to limit the influence of unsupported extrapolation.

Using a common grid definition and visualisation procedure reduces differences arising from grid resolution, evaluation geometry and colour scaling. The remaining contrasts illustrate how the evaluated model families and configurations represent the continuous relationship between the predictors and thermal conductivity. Within the analysed domain of moist, quartz-rich sands, thermal conductivity is expected to vary gradually and generally increase with water content and bulk density26–28.

In the analysed case, stronger global regularisation is associated with smoother response surfaces but weaker numerical fit, whereas more locally adaptive approaches achieve lower reconstruction errors while producing greater segmentation or short-scale variability. Monotonicity and surface-roughness diagnostics

To complement the global response-surface analysis presented in “Global response-surface characteristics” section, the surrogate models were evaluated using diagnostics targeting directional monotonicity and gradient-based surface roughness with respect to the individual predictors. These measures provide quantitative descriptors of the geometric differences observed when the fitted models are interpreted as continuous surrogate parameterisations.

Monotonicity was assessed independently along the water-content and bulk-density axes. For each response surface, first-order directional differences approximating and were calculated on the regular evaluation grid. Local violations were identified wherever a directional difference was negative, contrary to the generally increasing trends adopted for the analysed moist-sand domain. The diagnostics show broad differences among the representative model families. The globally regularised SVR configuration exhibits few monotonicity violations and relatively low surface roughness, consistent with its smooth response-surface geometry. In contrast, the representative tree-based ensemble configuration shows more frequent local directional violations and higher roughness, reflecting the segmented structure visible in Fig. 5. The kNN configuration preserves the broad directional trend but exhibits greater short-scale variability than SVR.

These results provide a quantitative counterpart to the geometric patterns described in “Global response-surface characteristics” section. Models with smoother response surfaces generally exhibit fewer directional violations and lower gradient-based roughness, whereas segmented or locally irregular surfaces are associated with higher values of one or both diagnostics. The agreement between the visual and quantitative assessments supports the interpretation that the observed geometric contrasts reflect differences in fitted surrogate behaviour rather than visualisation alone.

Across the evaluated hyperparameter variants, the broad family-level tendencies were generally retained, although the magnitude and spatial distribution of monotonicity violations and surface roughness varied among configurations. Hyperparameter tuning therefore modified the detailed behaviour of individual surfaces but did not remove the broader contrasts observed among the evaluated modelling approaches.

The monotonicity and surface-roughness measures contribute directly to the Functional Inconsistency Index defined in “Functional inconsistency index and model comparison” section. They describe selected directional and geometrical properties of the response surfaces and do not constitute tests of physical validity. Residuals reflect the combined effects of model approximation, data variability and measurement error. The present analysis does not separate these contributions and should not be interpreted as predictive uncertainty quantification. It is used only to identify whether held-out prediction errors exhibit systematic organisation across the observed predictor space.

Residual analysis is complementary to the Functional Inconsistency Index and is not included in its calculation. Together with the preceding diagnostics, it provides additional evidence that models with similar or favourable aggregate error metrics may differ in how their prediction errors are distributed across the analysed domain.

RMSE–FII trade-off and Pareto analysis

The behavioural differences identified in “Global response-surface characteristics”, “Monotonicity and surface-roughness diagnostics”, “Predictor effects and local response stability” and “Residual structure across the predictor domain” sections are summarised by positioning all evaluated model configurations in a joint RMSE–FII decision space (Fig. 9). RMSE represents the overall reconstruction error reported for each configuration, whereas the Functional Inconsistency Index (FII) summarises monotonicity violations, normalised surface roughness and output-range violations using the procedure defined in “Functional inconsistency index and model comparison” section.

Each point in Fig. 9 represents one hyperparameter configuration within a model family. Lower RMSE values indicate closer reconstruction of the retained observations, while lower FII values indicate fewer functional inconsistencies according to the adopted, case-specific diagnostic criteria. Lower values of both measures are therefore preferred.

The distribution of configurations shows a broad trade-off between numerical fit and functional regularity. Configurations with the lowest reconstruction errors generally exhibit higher FII values, whereas configurations with lower FII tend to show higher reconstruction errors. Across the evaluated configurations, RMSE and FII are moderately negatively associated (Spearman’s ρ = − 0.57\rho = 0.0.57ρ = − 0.57). This association describes the overall pattern within the analysed model set and does not imply a causal relationship between reconstruction error and functional inconsistency.

No single configuration simultaneously attains the lowest RMSE and the lowest FII. Several configurations can therefore be regarded as non-dominated within the two-objective comparison: improvement in one measure would require accepting deterioration in the other. Their relative positions illustrate alternative compromises rather than a single objectively optimal solution.

Configurations belonging to the same model family tend to occupy similar regions of the RMSE–FII space, although their positions also vary with hyperparameter settings. The evaluated tree-based and instance-based configurations are generally associated with lower reconstruction errors and moderate-to-high FII values. The more strongly regularised SVR configurations occupy regions characterised by lower FII and higher reconstruction error. Decision Tree configurations show intermediate combinations of the two measures.

The RMSE–FII representation provides a transparent means of comparing model configurations against two distinct objectives. It does not prescribe a universal balance between reconstruction performance and functional behaviour, and FII should not be used independently of the individual diagnostics or visual inspection of the response surfaces. Potential downstream uses in interpolation, mapping or numerical coupling motivate this comparison but were not directly tested in the present study.

The observed distribution supports the presence of an accuracy–regularity trade-off within the evaluated configurations and tuning ranges. It does not demonstrate that such a trade-off is an unavoidable property of all model architectures or that it could not be modified through broader hyperparameter exploration, alternative model families or different training strategies.

Synthesis of behavioural diagnostics and FII

The results presented in “Global response-surface characteristics”, “Monotonicity and surface-roughness diagnostics””, “Predictor effects and local response stability”, “Residual structure across the predictor domain” and “RMSE–FII trade-off and Pareto analysis” sections show that surrogate-model configurations with comparable numerical performance can exhibit substantially different functional behaviour when evaluated beyond aggregate error metrics. Differences in response-surface geometry, directional monotonicity and smoothness, local predictor-effect diagnostics and residual structure provide complementary information on the fitted surrogate relationships.

Across these diagnostics, broadly coherent but non-equivalent behavioural patterns emerge. More globally regularised configurations generally produce smoother and predominantly monotonic response surfaces and more coherent local predictor effects. In contrast, more locally adaptive configurations tend to generate greater surface segmentation, more frequent directional violations and stronger short-scale variability. These tendencies describe the configurations evaluated in the present study and should not be interpreted as universal properties of the corresponding model families.

The Functional Inconsistency Index (FII) provides a compact summary of three selected grid-based diagnostics: monotonicity violations, normalised surface roughness and output-range violations. The complementary predictor-effect and residual analyses are not components of FII but support interpretation of the fitted response surfaces and reveal behaviour that cannot be represented fully by a single composite indicator.

FII is calculated post hoc from model-predicted response surfaces and is not used during model training or hyperparameter tuning. It therefore characterises selected properties of the fitted surrogate functions under the adopted, case-specific diagnostic criteria. The index does not measure physical validity, verify compliance with governing equations or replace inspection of its individual components.

Within the RMSE–FII framework introduced in “RMSE–FII trade-off and Pareto analysis” section, configurations with similar reconstruction errors may occupy different positions in terms of functional inconsistency. Conversely, a low FII does not necessarily indicate an accurate or otherwise suitable model, because a strongly regularised or underfitted surface may satisfy the adopted regularity criteria while reproducing the observations poorly. Numerical performance and FII should therefore be interpreted jointly.

Taken together, the results support a multi-perspective evaluation workflow in which numerical metrics, response-surface diagnostics and complementary behavioural analyses are considered together during model comparison. The framework does not identify a universally optimal surrogate but provides a structured and transparent basis for making differences in fitted functional behaviour more explicit. The implications, limitations and potential transferability of this approach are discussed in the following section.

Discussion

Predictive accuracy and surrogate behaviour in geoengineering and environmental modelling

The results indicate that predictive accuracy alone may be insufficient for evaluating machine-learning models when they are used as continuous surrogate representations of material or environmental parameters. Conventional regression metrics such as RMSE and R2 summarise numerical agreement at evaluated observations but provide limited information on how a fitted model behaves between observations and across the predictor domain7,8. A related distinction between calibration performance and behavioural adequacy has long been recognised in environmental modelling6,16.

The present study extends this perspective to data-driven surrogate models. The combined analyses show that configurations with similar numerical performance can generate response surfaces with different geometries, directional behaviour, surface roughness, conditional predictor effects and residual distributions. The RMSE–FII representation further indicates a broad trade-off between reconstruction performance and functional behaviour within the evaluated model families, hyperparameter configurations and predictor domain. No single configuration simultaneously achieved the lowest reconstruction RMSE and the lowest FII. This finding supports the use of multi-criteria evaluation but should not be interpreted as evidence that the same trade-off must occur across other datasets, model architectures or tuning strategies10,15,36.

These findings are potentially relevant when machine-learning models are reused as parameterisations for interpolation, mapping or coupling with process-based simulations1,3. In such applications, local segmentation or rapid changes in a fitted response surface may introduce artificial variability into reconstructed parameter fields. The present study did not directly examine parameter-field interpolation, solver convergence or numerical coupling stability. These consequences therefore remain plausible downstream risks rather than demonstrated outcomes of the analysis.

Classical empirical and semi-empirical formulations provide a useful conceptual contrast because they impose a prescribed continuous functional structure, although their predictive performance and applicability may be limited outside the conditions for which they were developed26–28,37. An earlier application-oriented study using the same laboratory dataset compared machine-learning parameterisations with selected empirical formulations17. That study showed that improvements in numerical performance did not necessarily coincide with smoother or more regular reconstructed relationships. The present study does not repeat that benchmark but develops the methodological evaluation of machine-learning models when they are interpreted as continuous surrogates.

The observed differences do not imply that locally adaptive models are inherently unsuitable for surrogate applications. Rather, the evaluated configurations occupy different positions in the reconstruction-error–functional-behaviour space. More strongly regularised configurations generally produce smoother surfaces but may underfit local variation, whereas more locally adaptive configurations can improve numerical reconstruction while introducing segmentation or short-scale variability. The preferred balance therefore depends on the intended application and cannot be determined from FII or RMSE alone.

FII should be interpreted as a relative, context-dependent diagnostic rather than as a measure of physical validity. It aggregates monotonicity violations, normalised surface roughness and output-range violations under explicitly defined criteria for the analysed moist-sand domain. A low FII does not guarantee an accurate or physically adequate parameterisation, particularly when a model is excessively smooth or underfitted. Conversely, a higher FII does not by itself demonstrate that a model is unsuitable. Interpretation requires joint consideration of numerical performance, individual FII components, response-surface geometry and complementary predictor-effect and residual analyses.

Taken together, the results support evaluation workflows in which predictive metrics and functional diagnostics are treated as complementary sources of information. When fitted models are intended for reuse as continuous parameterisations, response-surface behaviour should be examined explicitly rather than inferred from numerical accuracy alone. The proposed workflow provides a transparent basis for such comparison within the analysed setting, while its application to other materials, predictor structures and higher-dimensional problems requires domain-specific criteria and further validation.

Inductive bias and surrogate behaviour

The differences in surrogate behaviour observed in this study are consistent with the influence of the inductive biases embedded in different regression model classes. Inductive bias defines the structural assumptions that constrain the hypothesis space of a learning algorithm and influence how functional relationships are inferred from finite observations7,8,20. However, the resulting response surfaces also depend on hyperparameter settings, training-data allocation and the distribution of observations within the predictor domain. The present results therefore support a strong contribution of model structure but do not isolate it as the sole determinant of surrogate behaviour.

Within the evaluated configurations, models belonging to the same family generally retained characteristic response-surface tendencies despite variation in hyperparameters. Hyperparameter changes shifted individual configurations within the reconstruction-error–functional-behaviour space but did not completely remove the broader differences observed between the evaluated model families. Accordingly, configurations from the same family tended to occupy related regions of the RMSE–FII space (Fig. 9), although within-family variability and overlap between families were also present. The repeated-partition analysis in “Sensitivity to data partitioning” section provides complementary evidence that the observed contrasts were not solely a consequence of one train–test allocation, but it does not separate the effects of architecture, hyperparameter specification and data allocation.

A relevant distinction in the present case is that between more globally regularised and more locally adaptive modelling strategies. The evaluated kernel-based SVR configuration produced a comparatively smooth and predominantly monotonic response surface across the analysed predictor domain. This behaviour is consistent with the global regularisation imposed by the selected kernel and hyperparameters, which limits short-scale variation but may also reduce the ability to reproduce local patterns in the observations10,17,36.

By contrast, the evaluated tree-based and instance-based configurations represented local variation more flexibly. Partitioning of the predictor space and neighbourhood-based prediction can improve local reconstruction but may also produce segmented surfaces, abrupt transitions or short-scale variability when the fitted models are interpreted as continuous parameterisations11,22,24. The patterns observed in “Global response-surface characteristics”, “Monotonicity and surface-roughness diagnostics”, “Predictor effects and local response stability” and “Residual structure across the predictor domain” sections are consistent with these representational mechanisms, but their magnitude remains dependent on the specific configurations and dataset examined here.

The analysis does not demonstrate that sampling structure and data coverage had no influence on the fitted surfaces. The models were evaluated over a common predictor domain, and convex-hull masking was used for selected visualisations and interpretative diagnostics, thereby limiting direct extrapolation in those analyses. Nevertheless, finite and unevenly distributed observations may still affect local model behaviour. The present results therefore support an association between model structure and response-surface characteristics rather than a complete causal separation of inductive bias from data-related effects. More broadly, the need to examine fitted model behaviour rather than rely on aggregate predictive accuracy alone is consistent with calls for more transparent and interpretable machine-learning evaluation in scientific applications38–41.

From a methodological perspective, surrogate evaluation should account for both model family and hyperparameter configuration. The hypothesis space associated with a learning algorithm constrains the types of relationships that can be represented, while hyperparameter optimisation determines how that capacity is expressed for a particular dataset. Model selection should therefore not be treated solely as the maximisation of predictive accuracy or solely as a choice between algorithmic families42.

From a practical perspective, the preferred balance depends on the intended use of the surrogate. When gradual variation and functional regularity are important, more strongly regularised configurations may be appropriate, provided that their potential for underfitting is assessed. When local predictive performance is prioritised, more adaptive configurations may be preferable, while their response-surface geometry should be examined for segmentation or short-scale irregularity.

Inductive bias consequently provides a useful framework for interpreting the accuracy–behaviour differences observed in this study, but it should be considered together with hyperparameter choice, data allocation and predictor-domain coverage. This interpretation supports a more transparent comparison of machine-learning surrogates without implying that model family alone determines their functional behaviour.

Operational implications of functionally irregular surrogates

The functional behaviour of surrogate models becomes particularly relevant when machine-learning regressors are embedded in geoengineering and environmental modelling workflows rather than used solely for pointwise prediction. In many geoscientific applications, surrogates are evaluated across continuous ranges of environmental states to support interpolation, parameter mapping or coupling with process-based simulators and modular modelling frameworks1,3,43,44. In such contexts, model outputs may be interpreted as continuous parameterisations, and the geometry of the learned functional relationship becomes relevant alongside numerical agreement at observed data points1,3,44.

Functionally irregular surrogate behaviour may introduce operational risks in these applications. Segmented, rapidly varying or locally non-monotonic response surfaces can generate artificial gradients, discontinuities or plateaus in reconstructed parameter fields. If these features are not supported by the underlying material behaviour, they may affect subsequent interpolation, aggregation, flux estimation or gradient-based calculations. Related problems have been reported in environmental modelling, where artefacts introduced during interpolation or parameterisation can be difficult to distinguish from genuine spatial or material variability4,38.

Such risks may become more important when surrogate outputs are supplied to dynamic or nonlinear process models. Local irregularities in an input parameterisation could potentially influence solver behaviour or propagate through model interactions and feedbacks18,36. However, the present study did not directly test parameter-field reconstruction, numerical coupling, solver convergence or the propagation of surrogate irregularities through downstream simulations. These consequences should therefore be regarded as plausible application-level risks requiring dedicated evaluation rather than as demonstrated outcomes of the present analysis.

A limitation of accuracy-only evaluation is that aggregate error metrics provide little information on fitted behaviour between evaluated observations. They summarise numerical deviations at discrete data points but do not directly describe surface continuity, directional behaviour or short-scale variation in the inferred functional relationship. As shown in “Global response-surface characteristics”, “Monotonicity and surface-roughness diagnostics”, “Predictor effects and local response stability”, “Residual structure across the predictor domain” and “RMSE–FII trade-off and Pareto analysis” sections, configurations with similar numerical performance may encode visibly different response-surface geometries.

Surrogate suitability should therefore be evaluated relative to its intended use. When predictions are reused as continuous parameterisations for interpolation, mapping or numerical modelling, response-surface diagnostics can help identify configurations that require additional scrutiny before deployment. The diagnostics proposed here do not demonstrate that a particular surrogate will cause numerical instability or operational failure, but they provide a structured means of identifying functional features that are not captured by pointwise predictive metrics alone1,38.

The role of FII: strengths, limitations and appropriate use

The Functional Inconsistency Index (FII) introduced in this study is intended as a diagnostic and comparative tool rather than as a measure of physical truth or validation against governing physical laws. FII does not impose constraints during model training and does not verify compliance with first-principles equations. Instead, it supports structured comparison of surrogate behaviour when machine-learning models are interpreted as continuous parameterisations within environmental modelling workflows. This perspective reflects the established distinction in geoengineering and environmental modelling between predictive performance and behavioural adequacy of model components38,44.

FII combines three explicitly defined grid-based diagnostics: monotonicity violations, normalised surface roughness and output-range violations. Response-surface visualisation, predictor-effect analyses and residual diagnostics are not components of the index but provide complementary evidence for interpreting the resulting scores. FII therefore does not replace detailed diagnostic analysis; rather, it provides a compact summary that remains traceable to its three constituent components.

A key limitation of FII is that it is not an absolute measure of physical realism. The index is defined relative to a specific dataset, predictor domain and modelling context, and its values are meaningful primarily for models evaluated under the same analytical conditions. Direct comparison of FII values across datasets, materials or environmental settings would therefore require harmonised diagnostic definitions, domains and normalisation procedures. FII should consequently be interpreted as a comparative diagnostic within a given modelling experiment rather than as a universal or transferable indicator of physical validity4.

The construction of FII involves explicit methodological choices, including the selection of diagnostic components, the definition of their reference scales and the aggregation weights. These choices introduce a degree of subjectivity. However, they are stated explicitly, allowing their implications and alternative formulations to be examined transparently. This approach is consistent with multi-criteria model evaluation, in which documented aggregation rules are preferable to implicit or undocumented judgement38,44.

FII is computed post hoc from surrogate response surfaces and is not used as a training objective or hyperparameter-selection criterion. This separation preserves the distinction between predictive optimisation and behavioural evaluation and enables the same diagnostic procedure to be applied to already fitted models without modifying their training process3,30. It may therefore also support retrospective examination of surrogate models developed within existing analytical workflows.

The principal strength of FII is its ability to organise three complementary aspects of response-surface behaviour into a single comparative indicator. As the number of candidate configurations increases, simultaneous interpretation of multiple diagnostic quantities becomes progressively more difficult. FII facilitates systematic comparison while preserving access to the underlying component values and the associated response surfaces14,39.

In the present study, lower FII values generally coincided with response surfaces that exhibited fewer directional violations, lower normalised roughness and fewer output-range violations. Nevertheless, the index should not be interpreted independently of its components. For example, the relatively low FII obtained by the representative Decision Tree configuration does not imply that its piecewise-constant geometry is necessarily preferable for every application. Rather, it shows that the composite score reflects the balance among the three included criteria and that additional surface inspection remains necessary. FII therefore complements numerical performance metrics by making selected trade-offs between reconstruction accuracy and functional behaviour more explicit during surrogate-model comparison1,3.

Generalisability beyond the present case study

Although this study is illustrated using laboratory measurements of thermal conductivity in moist sands, the underlying evaluation problem is not unique to this parameter or material class. Machine-learning regressors used as continuous surrogate functions may exhibit response-surface behaviour that is not fully described by pointwise predictive metrics. However, the specific trade-offs observed here depend on the analysed dataset, predictor domain, model configurations and diagnostic criteria. Their persistence in higher-dimensional environmental-modelling settings and across other model architectures requires further investigation. Related concerns arise in environmental and Earth-system applications in which data-driven models are used as components of larger computational workflows rather than solely as isolated predictors1,3,19.

The proposed workflow may be applicable to problems in which machine-learning regressors approximate environmental or material relationships that are subsequently evaluated across continuous predictor domains. Such uses occur in hydrology, hydrogeology and land-surface modelling, where fitted relationships may be reused for interpolation, mapping or integration into larger computational schemes10,36. In these contexts, examination of the fitted functional relationship may provide information that is complementary to conventional predictive-performance metrics.

Many geoengineering and environmental properties, including hydraulic conductivity, soil–water retention characteristics and effective porosity, are represented as spatially or state-dependent quantities. Their parameterisation can contribute to structural uncertainty and equifinality in environmental modelling4,38,44. When machine-learning surrogates are used to approximate such relationships, segmented or rapidly varying response surfaces may introduce artificial structure into subsequent analyses. The present study did not test these effects in downstream simulations, and their operational importance should therefore be assessed separately for each application.

The individual diagnostics used in the proposed workflow are not inherently restricted to thermal conductivity. Response-surface inspection, directional-behaviour assessment, roughness analysis, predictor-effect diagnostics and residual evaluation can be adapted to other modelling problems. Their interpretation nevertheless requires domain-specific expectations. In particular, monotonicity should be evaluated only where a directional relationship is scientifically justified; non-monotonic, threshold-type or multi-regime relationships may require modified criteria or omission of that component. Similar broader perspectives have been proposed for evaluating machine-learning reliability beyond aggregate predictive accuracy8,39,40.

FII is likewise adaptable rather than universally transferable. Its components, reference scales and weights may be modified to reflect the behaviour relevant to a particular material, process or application. Any such modification would require explicit justification and renewed sensitivity assessment. Numerical FII values should therefore be compared only among models evaluated using the same dataset, predictor domain and diagnostic formulation. The index supports relative post hoc comparison and should not be interpreted as a universal measure of physical adequacy44.

The present case study consequently provides a controlled, low-dimensional testbed for examining the distinction between predictive performance and fitted functional behaviour. The numerical results, model-family ordering and FII values should not be generalised directly to other datasets or applications. The broader methodological proposition is more limited: when machine-learning models are reused as continuous surrogate parameterisations, predictive accuracy and functional behaviour can represent complementary evaluation dimensions. Testing the framework in higher-dimensional settings, with additional material classes and with model architectures not considered here, remains necessary before broader conclusions can be drawn.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results.

First, the analysis is based on a single laboratory dataset representing a relatively coherent material domain of moist, quartz-rich sands. This controlled setting supported direct comparison of model configurations under consistent experimental and computational conditions, but it does not establish that the same model-family patterns or RMSE–FII relationships will occur in other geomaterials or environmental datasets. Differences in lithology, mineral composition, particle-size distribution, density state, measurement procedure and predictor coverage may alter both predictive performance and reconstructed surface behaviour. The results therefore provide evidence for the analysed case rather than external validation of the framework4,44.

Second, the predictor space is intentionally low-dimensional and includes only water content and bulk density. This design enables direct two-dimensional response-surface inspection and straightforward grid-based diagnostics, but it does not represent the higher-dimensional predictor spaces commonly encountered in geoengineering and environmental modelling1,10. Additional compositional, textural or material-state variables may introduce interactions, conditional effects and locally non-monotonic relationships that cannot be characterised adequately using the present formulation. The accuracy–behaviour patterns observed here may therefore become weaker, stronger or qualitatively different in higher-dimensional settings.

Third, FII depends on explicitly selected diagnostic components, reference definitions and aggregation weights. Alternative monotonicity criteria, roughness measures, output ranges or weighting schemes could produce different relative scores. These choices represent documented methodological judgement rather than a uniquely defined measure of functional adequacy. FII should therefore be interpreted as one transparent post hoc comparison strategy, and its formulation should be adapted and reassessed when applied to other materials or relationships4.

Fourth, the study does not provide formal predictive uncertainty quantification. The repeated-partition analysis examines sensitivity to train–test allocation but does not quantify uncertainty arising from measurement error, limited sampling, model parameters or model-form assumptions. Future extensions could combine the proposed behavioural diagnostics with probabilistic approaches based on quantile prediction, variational inference, Bayesian neural networks, Markov chain Monte Carlo or ensemble learning45–47. These approaches address an evaluation dimension that is distinct from FII and should be treated as complementary rather than interchangeable.

Fifth, the comparison is limited to five established regression families and a finite set of configurations. Neural-network architectures, including multilayer perceptrons and more recent attention-based approaches, were outside the scope of the controlled experiment. Their inclusion in future studies would help determine whether the observed accuracy–behaviour patterns persist across broader hypothesis spaces. Such comparisons should retain controlled tuning procedures and evaluate response-surface behaviour rather than predictive accuracy alone.

Future work should also examine other environmental parameters and higher-dimensional material relationships. The diagnostic criteria would need to reflect the expected behaviour of each application. In particular, monotonicity should not be imposed where non-monotonic, threshold-type, hysteretic or multi-regime responses are scientifically plausible. In those cases, the monotonicity component could be modified, restricted to justified subdomains or omitted.

Another direction involves combining post hoc behavioural diagnostics with theory-guided or physics-informed learning approaches. Although such methods can introduce constraints during model training3,30, post-training evaluation remains relevant for determining whether the fitted surrogate exhibits the intended functional behaviour, particularly when working with existing datasets and established modelling workflows.

The present study also did not directly evaluate interpolation of surrogate-derived parameter fields, coupling with process-based simulators, solver convergence or propagation of functional irregularities through scenario analyses. Embedding the evaluated surrogates in complete modelling workflows would allow these potential downstream consequences to be tested directly rather than inferred from response-surface geometry1,19. Such workflow-level evaluation represents an important step toward integrating surrogate assessment with practical environmental decision-making48.

Finally, the public repository accompanying the study is intended to document the computational methodology through code, configuration files and a synthetic demonstration dataset. It is not a complete user-oriented engineering application. Development of graphical or low-code interfaces could improve accessibility for practitioners, as demonstrated by recent geotechnical machine-learning applications and tool-development frameworks49,50. The original laboratory data are subject to institutional sharing restrictions; consequently, the public demonstration reproduces the analytical procedure rather than the exact manuscript-specific numerical results.

Conclusions

This study addresses a methodological gap in the evaluation of machine-learning models used as surrogate representations of geoengineering material parameters. When regression models are interpreted as continuous parameterisations rather than solely as pointwise predictors, conventional predictive metrics may not fully characterise their suitability. Functional behaviour across the predictor domain therefore provides a complementary dimension of surrogate-model evaluation.

Using a controlled laboratory dataset, the analysis showed that surrogate-model configurations with comparable reconstruction errors can exhibit substantially different response-surface geometries, directional behaviour, surface roughness, conditional predictor effects and residual structures. Within the evaluated setting, these differences were associated with model family and hyperparameter configuration, although they should not be interpreted as universal properties of the corresponding algorithms or as effects of model architecture alone.

To support systematic comparison, the Functional Inconsistency Index (FII) was introduced as a composite post hoc diagnostic based on monotonicity violations, normalised surface roughness and output-range violations. FII does not enforce physical laws, verify physical validity or replace conventional accuracy metrics and detailed surface inspection. Instead, it provides a transparent, relative and context-dependent summary of selected aspects of fitted surrogate behaviour.

The results indicate a broad trade-off between reconstruction performance and functional behaviour within the analysed dataset, model families and configurations. No single configuration simultaneously minimised reconstruction error and FII. Model comparison should therefore consider both numerical performance and the intended use of the surrogate. When predictions are subsequently used for interpolation, mapping or coupling with process-based models, response-surface behaviour may require additional scrutiny alongside predictive accuracy.

Although the framework was demonstrated using a low-dimensional thermal-conductivity dataset for moist sands, its individual diagnostics may be adapted to other surrogate-modelling problems. Such transfer requires domain-specific behavioural criteria, particularly where non-monotonic or multi-regime relationships are scientifically plausible. Further validation using other materials, higher-dimensional predictor spaces, additional model architectures, formal uncertainty-quantification methods and complete downstream modelling workflows is needed before broader conclusions can be drawn.
