Autopsy Confirmed Normal Pressure Hydrocephalus (NPH) Presenting With Corticobasal Syndrome
Authors: Manizhe Eslami Amirabadi, Shubir Dutt, Clair Yballa, Zachary A. Miller, Bruce L. Miller, Renaud La Joie, Salvatore Spina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDiagnostic large‐volume CSF drainage should be considered in patients with progressive cognitive/behavioral and motor symptoms localizing to frontal lobes in the presence of MRI findings consistent with NPH (DESH, dorsal sulcal crowding). An FDG‐PET‐Based Machine Learning framework has shown promising results in distinguishing NPH from other causes of frontotemporal syndromes.

Introduction

Normal pressure hydrocephalus (NPH) has been subjected to scientific debate since the introduction of the term in 1965. It is either idiopathic or secondary to congenital differences and changes in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) dynamics related to diseases of the subarachnoid space [1]. Vascular risk factors including pulse pressure and systolic blood pressure are associated with idiopathic NPH [2] and a prospective study showed an association between baseline systolic blood pressure and ventriculomegaly in 10 years [3] suggesting an effect of hypertension in this disorder. The prevalence of NPH is estimated to be around 1% in the older adult population [4] and it increases with age [4, 5].

Despite the challenges in making a diagnosis of NPH, it is important to consider NPH as a potentially modifiable contributing factor to cognitive and gait problems in the elderly population when ventriculomegaly is present. NPH clinical symptoms (gait impairment, cognitive changes, and urinary incontinence) and ventriculomegaly are relatively common in the elderly population. The symptom triad is often incomplete in patients with NPH, making the clinical diagnosis difficult [1]. The NPH diagnostic criteria [6], assigns a diagnosis of possible idiopathic NPH (iNPH) when the patient has more than one of the above symptoms (gait impairment described as “small stride, shuffle, instability during walking, and increase in instability on turning”), and those symptoms are not completely explained by other neurological or non‐neurological conditions and causes of secondary hydrocephalus (developmental or meningeal processes) are absent. A probable iNPH case is defined as a possible iNPH case with normal CSF analysis and pressure neuroimaging findings supportive of iNPH or improvement in symptoms with a tap test or CSF drainage test. Finally, definite iNPH cases are cases of probable iNPH who respond favorably to CSF diversion. The neuroimaging findings supportive of iNPH include dorsal sulcal crowding and disproportionately enlarged subarachnoid space hydrocephalus (DESH) in the presence of ventriculomegaly and Evans Index (EI) > 0.3 [7, 8]. A study comparing 26 iNPH patients with 30 “NPH mimics” found a higher probability of a normal DaT scan in the former group suggesting a role for dopaminergic imaging in the diagnosis of iNPH although 38% of patients with “NPH mimics” also had normal scans [9]. A prospective study showed that higher CSF pressure within the normal limits was associated with a better response to CSF diversion (improvement measured with the Rankin Scale (RS) of disability) [10]. A clinical trial of CSF diversion in iNPH patients showed improvement in the modified RS score in 63% of the cases when performed within 2 years from the symptom onset [11]. Here, we describe longitudinal follow‐up and eventual neuropathological diagnosis of an iNPH case with an unusual clinical presentation.

Case History/Examination

A 74‐year‐old right‐handed man presented 4 months after an accident while driving. He “veered off the road” unexpectedly without provocation or reason and his car tumbled over several times leading to loss of consciousness for 30 min, post‐traumatic amnesia, and left elbow fracture‐dislocation followed by two surgeries. At his initial assessment, he reported a 2‐year history of gait instability and frequent falls, progressive apathy and distractibility which worsened following the trauma. His family didn't notice abnormal posturing or movement in his left hand before the accident but wondered about “some change” that might have caused the accident. He didn't have psychosis, obsessive tendencies, changes in emotional expression, disinhibition, anosmia, or autonomic symptoms including urinary incontinence. He had difficulty with using his left hand attributed to weakness and pain following the trauma despite physical therapy. He lost 30 pounds of weight since the accident because of loss of appetite, apathy, and hypersomnia (12–16 h of sleep/24). There were no symptoms suggestive of dream enactment. He relied on his wife for most of his daily activities because of apathy but remained independent in basic ADLs.

Past medical history included a 200+ pack‐year history of smoking (quit after a myocardial infarction at age 58), diabetes Type II, dyslipidemia, hypertension, lumbar canal stenosis, and diabetic polyneuropathy.

On exam, there was hypomimia, no dysarthria or hypophonia, limited upward gaze, fragmented pursuits, axial > appendicular rigidity (right > left), and bilateral action and postural hand tremor without a resting component. There was diminished praxis and stereognosis of the left upper extremity. He had brisk reflexes throughout and a steppage gait because of left foot dorsiflexion weakness and a symmetric stocking pattern of sensory loss. Neuropsychological testing displayed executive dysfunction (bradyphrenia and error‐proneness), poor verbal learning with normal visual learning and recall, and normal performance in other cognitive domains.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

Brain MRI showed DESH, dorsal sulcal crowding, ventriculomegaly, and mild periventricular FLAIR white matter hyperintensities suggestive of trans ependymal edema (Figure 1). FDG‐PET scan showed relative hypometabolism of bilateral frontal lobes (Figure 2). Amyloid‐PET was performed with 11C Pittsburgh Compound B. Image was read as positive due to moderate binding in frontal and posterior cingulate/precuneus areas; PET quantification was consistent (43 Centiloids). Tau PET and DaT scans were not performed.

He was diagnosed with an amnestic multi‐domain cognitive impairment and possible corticobasal syndrome based on the Armstrong criteria (limb rigidity) which were felt as non‐completely explained by the consequences of his recent injury [12]. He also fulfilled the research criteria for possible corticobasal degeneration (CBD)—a distinct 4‐repeat (4R) tauopathy—(age, insidious onset, progression > 1 year and a possible CBS phenotype). Alzheimer's disease (AD), because of its overall prevalence and 4R tauopathies, was considered the most likely underlying pathology. Because of high suspicion for a neurodegenerative process explaining his clinical syndrome, although his brain MRI findings were observed to be consistent with iNPH, further investigation of this as a potential cause of his clinical syndrome was not pursued. He was started on donepezil and escitalopram. Working with a personal trainer, his appetite and mood slightly improved but he continued to have intrinsic hand muscle weakness and difficulty using his left hand. He was followed longitudinally at the Memory and Aging Center, University of California, San Francisco as a part of ongoing observational studies on frontotemporal dementia syndromes. Written informed consent for research participation and use of results in scientific publications was obtained from the patient and his guardian when enrolling in the research project.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

On neurological evaluation at age 75, his left hand was described as intermittently dystonic with limited praxis, but there was otherwise no clear disease progression, and diagnostic considerations remained the same. An EMG‐NCS showed neurogenic changes limited to the location of traumatic injury and no evidence of motor neuron disease. Exam remained stable for the next 2 years and due to lack of worsening of memory and executive function, AD was thought to be a less likely etiology compared to a 4R tauopathy (CBD or progressive supranuclear palsy) as the most likely underlying pathology. His last neuropsychological assessment at age 77 showed relatively stable verbal and visual memory, stable executive dysfunction, and relative sparing of other cognitive domains although he was significantly more dependent on his family. Apathy, irritability, and difficulty with problem‐solving limited his daily activities. He was followed through phone CDR assessment with his caregiver at ages 78 and 80 and his caregiver noted gradually worsening of memory, orientation, community affairs, and aggressive behavior. He returned to clinic at age 86 for agitation and confrontational behavior. He became incontinent in his early 80s but continued to walk with a walker. He died at age 87 (disease duration: 15 years).

At autopsy, he had a normal brain weight and no gross regional atrophy as shown in Figure 3. There were no neurodegenerative changes consistent with frontotemporal lobar degeneration. There were intermediate AD neuropathological changes (Thal 5, Braak 4 and CERAD neuritic plaque stage severe: Figure 4), with only rare neurofibrillary tangles in the frontal lobes. These findings were deemed insufficient to explain his early and persistent clinical symptoms localizing to the frontal lobes. There was evidence of periventricular white matter rarefaction as often observed in iNPH, and limited age‐related tau astrogliopathy, and limbic argyrophilic grain disease. Based on his imaging and final pathological findings, iNPH was the final diagnosis explaining his initial symptoms and slow disease progression. AD neuropathological changes explained his episodic memory deficit that emerged within the last years of his life.

Discussion

There is significant uncertainty in the diagnosis of patients with combined movement and cognitive deficits. Although our patient presented with cognitive symptoms and gait changes which, along with his MRI findings, might have suggested a diagnosis of iNPH, he received a diagnosis of corticobasal syndrome because of examination findings including asymmetric axial > appendicular and right > left limb rigidity. Therefore, clinicians presumed the presence of an underlying neurodegenerative process. Positive β‐amyloid PET imaging supported the clinical suspicion of neurodegenerative disease, although a tau PET study was not performed to confirm the presence of AD tauopathy with a distribution consistent with his clinical syndrome. Clinicians weighed the invasive nature of the evaluation strategy and treatment (diagnostic CSF drainage and CSF diversion) against the potential benefit in the setting of a neurodegenerative disease and did not recommend pursuing these interventions, despite MRI findings consistent with iNPH. In addition, the development of dystonia and worsening of motor dexterity increased the suspicion for a 4R tauopathy, further supporting the clinical suspicion of an underlying neurodegenerative disease. An FDG‐PET study with a limited number of patients (n = 7) did not find any cortical hypometabolism in iNPH patients compared to controls but found significant striatal hypometabolism in iNPH compared to control, suggesting a role of FDG‐PET for iNPH diagnosis. Our case did not show this striatal hypometabolism pattern [13]. Instead, his FDG‐PET scan showed bifrontal hypometabolism that added to the consideration of FTLD pathology. A newly developed FDG‐PET‐Based Machine Learning framework [14] using images (n = 3671) from well‐characterized patients with various dementia syndromes showed promising results in distinguishing iNPH cases from other neuropathological causes of frontotemporal syndromes. This tool might assist in correctly identifying iNPH as the potential cause of similar cases, guiding clinicians and patients when discussing management strategies. DAT scan imaging (for evaluation of basal ganglia dysfunction in the setting of alpha synucleinopathies or tauopathies as the cause of the motor symptoms vs. iNPH) [9], and future biomarkers of frontotemporal lobar degeneration may also improve the accuracy of diagnosis for patients with gradually worsening motor and cognitive/behavioral deficits pre‐mortem.

INPH is a heterogeneous condition with clinical manifestations that overlaps with various neurodegenerative dementias and ongoing controversy regarding its diagnosis and treatment. An underlying neurodegenerative process is found at autopsy in many patients with a clinical diagnosis of iNPH [15]. On the other hand, effective CSF diversion has been described in patients with comorbid iNPH and autopsy‐proven neurodegenerative disease [16]. A systematic review of different methods of CSF diversion, suggested the potential utility of less invasive methods such as lumbo‐peritoneal rather than ventriculoperitoneal shunts although the authors highlighted the need for further research standardizing clinical evaluation protocols for case selection for prospective clinical trials to confirm the efficacy [17]. A novel endovascular CSF shunt is also currently in clinical trials as another minimally invasive option [18]. Development of these less invasive modes of therapy will make identification of iNPH as a potentially modifiable factor contributing to gait and cognitive symptoms even more clinically important even in cases with less classic feature.

To add to the complexity, DESH is often misinterpreted as cortical atrophy [19] leading to the suspicion of a neurodegenerative process even in cases where the clinical symptoms can be solely explained by iNPH. Clinicopathological correlation studies on corticobasal syndromes report progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP) and AD as the most likely causes after Corticobasal degeneration [20, 21] but there are reported cases with no specific pathological findings to explain the CBS presentation (like our case), supporting the theory of a CSF dynamic disorder as the underlying cause for these rare cases [21]. Lack of disease‐specific biomarkers for many neurodegenerative processes, including an accessible tau biomarker for AD, limits timely and accurate diagnosis of neurodegenerative diseases. Additionally, an iNPH diagnosis confirmed at autopsy is limited because it is not possible to predict CSF diversion response during life solely based on neuropathological findings. In theory, performing a diagnostic CSF drainage could have altered this patient's care and quality of life at least temporarily if he showed improvement of his frontal lobe symptoms of gait disturbance and apathy after CSF drainage. It is difficult to accurately estimate the risks and benefits of CSF diversion in cases such as the one presented in this report because of the existing uncertainties in clinical diagnosis. As a result, counseling patients and caregivers about whether to pursue diagnostic and therapeutic procedures remains challenging.
