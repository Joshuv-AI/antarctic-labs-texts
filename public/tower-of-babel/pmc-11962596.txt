RiceSNP-ABST: a deep learning approach to identify abiotic stress-associated single nucleotide polymorphisms in rice
Authors: Quan Lu, Jiajun Xu, Renyi Zhang, Hangcheng Liu, Meng Wang, Xiaoshuang Liu, Zhenyu Yue, Yujia Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGiven the adverse effects faced by rice due to abiotic stresses, the precise and rapid identification of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with abiotic stress traits (ABST-SNPs) in rice is crucial for developing resistant rice varieties. The scarcity of high-quality data related to abiotic stress in rice has hindered the development of computational models and constrained research efforts aimed at rice improvement and breeding. Genome-wide association studies provide a better statistical power to consider ABST-SNPs in rice. Meanwhile, deep learning methods have shown their capability in predicting disease- or phenotype-associated loci, but have primarily focused on human species. Therefore, developing predictive models for identifying ABST-SNPs in rice is both urgent and valuable. In this paper, a model called RiceSNP-ABST is proposed for predicting ABST-SNPs in rice. Firstly, six training datasets were generated using a novel strategy for negative sample construction. Secondly, four feature encoding methods were proposed based on DNA sequence fragments, followed by feature selection. Finally, convolutional neural networks with residual connections were used to determine whether the sequences contained rice ABST-SNPs. RiceSNP-ABST outperformed traditional machine learning and state-of-the-art methods on the benchmark dataset and demonstrated consistent generalization on an independent dataset and cross-species datasets. Notably, multi-granularity causal structure learning was employed to elucidate the relationships among DNA structural features, aiming to identify key genetic variants more effectively. The web-based tool for the RiceSNP-ABST can be accessed at http://rice-snp-abst.aielab.cc.

Introduction

Abiotic stresses such as heat, flooding, drought, and nutrient deficiencies significantly reduce rice yields by disrupting essential growth and development stages [1]. Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) have emerged as a preferred DNA marker class in genomic studies [2]. SNPs are the most common type of genetic variation found in genomic DNA, representing the most abundant class of molecular markers [3]. In recent years, more and more studies have shown that SNPs in rice play an important role in rice breeding [4]. Although genome-wide association studies (GWAS) provides high-quality SNPs associated with abiotic stress traits (ABST-SNPs) in rice, challenges remain due to the data’s quality and limited availability [5].

Recently, several techniques for detecting SNPs have emerged, broadly categorized into electrophoresis and high-throughput assays [6]. Electrophoresis-based methods for SNP identification, including denatured gradient gel electrophoresis [7], allele-specific PCR [8], and cleaved amplified polymorphic sequence labeling [9], are known for being time-consuming and less accurate. The method can detect the presence of SNPs but does not provide detailed nucleotide information. In contrast, high-throughput techniques like DNA sequencing and high-resolution melting show superior accuracy. To overcome current assay limitations, novel methods employing customized DNA polymerases, duplex binding proteins, or CRISPR-associated (Cas) proteins have been developed [10]. However, the high cost of CRISPR-Cas systems limits their widespread application. To overcome current assay limitations, developing a robust model with high confidence is crucial for accurately and conveniently predicting ABST-SNPs in rice.

Computational methods have advanced significantly in crop genotype–phenotype association studies, particularly in the identification and analysis of biomolecular markers associated with key phenotypic traits [11, 12]. These approaches enable a deeper understanding of regulatory elements and genetic predictors critical for crop improvement. Sliding Window Association Test-Convolutional Neural Networks (SWAT-CNN) [13] is a three-step deep learning framework that involves segmenting the genome, CNN-based SNP selection, and classifying Alzheimer’s disease-associated SNPs. Similarly, TLVar [14] applies transfer learning to enhance predictions of disease-related non-coding variants. Both methods utilize one-hot encoding for feature extraction. Bidirectional encoder representations from transformers (BERT)-Enhancer [15] is a bioinformatics model that combines BERT embeddings with 2D CNNs to improve the accuracy of DNA sequence-based enhancer predictions, leveraging BERT for feature extraction. While significant progress has been made in human functional genomics [16, 17], directly applying these methods to crop research remains challenging. Deep learning genome-wide association study (DLGWAS) [18] proposes a dual-stream CNN deep learning model for phenotype prediction and SNP marker association analysis without the need for genotype imputation. DLGWAS has relatively low computational efficiency and relies on Graphics Processing Unit (GPU) for effective performance on large datasets. DNNGP [19] is a deep neural network-based method for genomic prediction that integrates multi-omics data from maize, wheat and tomato, to enhance the prediction of agronomic traits. It uses binary encoding for SNPs and principal component analysis (PCA) for dimensionality reduction. SoyDNGP [20] is a web-based deep learning framework for soybean breeding with advanced CNN architecture, 3D input, and coordinate attention, providing high predictive accuracy and broad crop applicability. These predictors exhibit limited interpretability, often requiring specialized expertise or interdisciplinary collaboration to clarify their biological significance. Overall, current methodologies still have scope for advancement in enhancing feature diversity [21–23], refining feature selection techniques [24], and improving model interpretability [25, 26].

In this study, a framework called RiceSNP-ABST based on the automatic design of CNNs was introduced for the accurate prediction of ABST-SNPs in rice. The novelty of the proposed method can be summarized in three key aspects: the generation of negative sample sets, optimization strategies for feature encoding, and an efficient CNN framework. Initially, six new and up-to-date datasets were constructed based on three negative sampling strategies across two DNA sequence lengths. During the feature construction phase, four feature encoding methods were used, including One-hot encoding, DNA2vec [27], DNABERT [28], and DNAshape [29]. Subsequently, the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method was employed for feature selection and optimization [30]. Moreover, we provide a comprehensive analysis of the significance of DNA shape structural information in predicting ABST-SNPs. Finally, the integrated features are input into the RiceSNP-ABST optimized based on AMBER [31]. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first application of a sequence and structural feature input approach utilizing feature fusion in a CNN neural network with residual connections for ABST-SNP identification. Furthermore, RiceSNP-ABST demonstrated superior performance compared to existing predictors in ABST-SNPs identification, highlighting the effectiveness of the proposed hybrid framework.

Materials and methods

Overview of RiceSNP-ABST

RiceSNP-ABST is a framework based on the automatic design of CNNs developed for the precise prediction of ABST-SNPs in rice. Figure 2 illustrates the overall framework of the proposed RiceSNP-ABST. First, based on three negative sampling strategies across two DNA sequence lengths, six datasets were created and subsequently an independent dataset was constructed. Second, four different features that cover sequence and structural information [32–34] were comprehensively extracted, and SHAP was used for effective feature selection. Third, the CNN-based prediction model is generated using an automated framework. Finally, the performance of RiceSNP-ABST was compared with traditional ML methods and other existing methods through cross-validation and independent assessment. Additionally, a web server for RiceSNP-ABST was developed for academic research purposes. The source code is available at https://github.com/luquan666/RiceSNP-ABST.

Data preprocessing

Training datasets

Given the current lack of benchmark datasets of SNPs-trait associations for crops, constructing a dependable, thorough, and stringent dataset is the initial crucial step in the development of computational predictors. According to three negative sample construction methods and two extraction sequence lengths, six training datasets were prepared.

For positive samples, a total of 4347 ABST-SNP data were collected from the GWAS Atlas database (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gwas/) [35]. Each data entry includes information such as Genomic Location, traits, P-value, R2, and Genes (Consequence Types). The dataset with the best performance in 5-fold cross-validation was selected as the benchmark dataset.

Independent dataset

To evaluate the predictive performance, 192 ABST-SNPs data were gathered from the Mbkbase database (www.mbkbase.org/rice) [40] as positive samples and 960 negative samples were randomly selected from the same database. The CD-HIT tool was configured with a 70% sequence identity threshold, resulting in a final dataset comprising 189 positive and 189 negative samples.

Feature representation

One-hot encoding

The four nucleotides comprising DNA are adenine (A), cytosine (C), guanine (G), and thymine (T). In this paper, a One-hot encoding method is utilized to represent DNA sequence segments, enabling each nucleotide to be encoded into a digital vector. The method of One-hot encoding involves assigning ‘A’ to [1,0,0,0], ‘T’ to [0,1,0,0], ‘C’ to [0,0,1,0], and ‘G’ to [0,0,0,1] in a sequence. DNA2vec

DNA2vec is a novel method based on the word embedding model [23, 27]. It utilizes a shallow two-layer neural network to calculate distributed representations of k-mers (a k-mer is a sequence fragment of length k) in DNA sequences. In DNA2vec, the concatenation of DNA vectors simulating nucleotide combinations was experimentally verified:

The feature encoding method demonstrates that applying word embedding to DNA sequences captures valuable information. In this section, DNA2vec was retrained using the whole-genome SNP-flanking DNA sequence of rice, with k-values ranging from 3 to 8, resulting in long vectors.

DNABERT

Deciphering the linguistic complexities embedded within genomes represents a pivotal challenge in biology. Foundational models like DNABERT and Nucleotide Transformer have notably advanced this domain [41, 42]. Existing works have largely hinged on k-mer, fixed-length permutations of A, T, C, and G, as the token of the genome language due to its simplicity. Nevertheless, the computational and sampling inefficiencies stemming from k-mer tokenization stand as formidable barriers in the development of expansive genome-based foundational models. To address the above limitations, the pre-trained model in DNABERT-2 [28] is used, resulting in 768-D vectors.

DNAshape features

Computational complexity, model interpretability, and data availability were considered, to achieve superior performance while preserving model simplicity. By using the Deep DNAshape method [29], DNA structural features can be predicted for any length and number of DNA sequences in a high-throughput manner. After investigating numerous structural properties of DNA, the following 13 typical shape features are chosen: minor groove width (MGW), Shear, Stretch, Stagger, Buckle, propeller twist (ProT), Opening, Shift, Slide, Rise, Tilt, Roll, and helix twist (HelT). These features represent various aspects of DNA shape, encompassing critical information about DNA structure and function.

Feature selection based on SHAP

Machine learning and the SHAP method [30] are effective for feature selection. We utilized the SHAP-RF method to rank the importance of input features and perform effective feature selection. The feature matrices encoded by One-hot, DNA2vec, DNABERT, and DNAshape methods are linearly concatenated to form a combined feature matrix. The matrix is then input into the RF model to rank the importance of all features. The top 1000 ranked features were divided into 10 feature subsets, with each subset including an increasing range of 100 features. We designed an experiment in which comparative studies were conducted on each feature subset across six training datasets. The feature combinations were incrementally reduced until a significant drop in AUC was observed on the benchmark dataset. In the final results, we will evaluate the performance of the four types of feature representations within the ultimate combination.

Model training and optimization

Designing model search space

The CNN was designed with six layers, each comprising eight distinct computational operations: 1D convolutions with filter sizes of 3, 4, 6, or 8 (conv3, conv4, conv6, conv8), and max pooling or average pooling with sizes of 3 or 4 (maxpool3, avgpool3, maxpool4, avgpool4). The hyperparameters for these computational operations were selected based on previous research [43]. Additionally, identity mapping was incorporated into each layer, facilitating a direct mapping of input to output without computational overhead (identity), potentially mitigating model complexity. The six convolutional layers were partitioned into three blocks, with each block doubling the number of filters from the preceding one, and each layer within a block possessing an equal number of filters. For the architectural search, the first block was initialized with eight filters. Furthermore, residual connections were introduced into the search space of the model architecture. Efficient neural architecture search

ENAS [45] was utilized as the optimization method for exploring child network architectures within the model space. ENAS utilizes an RNN as the controller model to sequentially predict the child model architecture from the model space. The controller RNN, parameterized by θ, generates the child model architectures with a log-likelihood π(a;θ) and is trained using REINFORCE [46]. An essential aspect facilitating the efficient sampling of child architectures is the parameter-sharing scheme implemented among these models [45]. The computational graph for a child model takes the form of a directed acyclic graph (DAG). Within the parameter-sharing framework, a comprehensive computational graph is constructed, referred to as the child DAG with parameters ω, encompassing all potential permutations of child model architectures. The main insight of ENAS is that every child model architecture corresponds to a subgraph of the child DAG, leading to shared training and expedited processing. The gradient for the child model parameters ω concerning its loss function L is derived via a Monte Carlo estimate based on the expectation across a collection of M architecture samples a:(M: N)

In this research, specific adjustments were made to θ and the child DAG parameters ω. The controller RNN was configured as a single-layer long short-term memory with 64 hidden units. Consistent with the original ENAS setup, N = 1 was chosen for ω updates, and regularization was applied to the residual connection ratio to maintain it at 0.4. The regularization parameter, indicative of the sparse nature of skip connections, has proven effective in producing high-performance CNNs as evidenced in prior research [45]. The configuration of the child DAG adhered to the model space delineated in the preceding section. The child DAG underwent initial training on the entire training dataset with a batch size of 1000 for a warm-up phase. Subsequently, the controller RNN randomly sampled 100 child architectures from the child DAG and assessed their rewards. These architectures and corresponding rewards were then utilized to optimize the controller RNN parameters θ. Following this, the child DAG was retrained using architectures sampled from the updated policy, π(a;θ).

Both the controller and child models were trained using the Adam optimizer. The training process alternated between the two models for 300 iterations, and the child architecture with the highest reward in the last controller step was selected. Sampled architectures were generated by uniformly sampling computational operations and residual connections at a proportion of 0.4, consistent with previous searched models. The child models obtained from the searched and sampled architectures were trained from scratch. Both architectures were scaled in width by a factor of 2, increasing the number of convolutional kernels, and dropout layers were added after each pooling layer. This approach is based on a heuristic previously reported in the literature.

Results and discussion

CNNs based on an automated framework

CNNs have been established as a standard tool for analyzing biological sequences [49, 50]. Optimizing network architectures is essential for maximizing the performance of CNNs; however, it requires extensive expertise in machine learning, as well as a substantial investment of time and effort. Consequently, this process poses a significant obstacle to the widespread and effective utilization of advanced DL techniques in genomics. In this study, Automated Modelling for AMBER [31] is introduced, a fully automated framework designed to streamline the development and deployment of CNNs tailored for genomic sequence analysis. Evaluating the effectiveness of three negative sampling strategies

Given the limited research on predicting SNP-phenotype associations related to abiotic stress resistance in rice, we constructed three negative sample sets—Random, Common, and Rearrange—using distinct methods tailored to various scenarios. Inspired by studies on human SNPs [51], the Random set was generated by randomly selecting loci across the genome. To enhance data authenticity [52], the Common set was formed using neutral loci from 160 000 common sites in the Rice SNP-Seek Database. From a data science perspective [53], the Rearrange set was created by computationally rearranging the sequences of positive samples. We evaluated the effectiveness of our proposed model using 5-fold cross-validation, which was repeated five times across six training datasets. Evaluating the effectiveness of the SHAP feature selection method

Table 4 and Fig. 3 illustrate DNABERT and DNAshape made significant contributions to identifying ABST-SNPs. It’s obvious that the improved performance after SHAP feature selection demonstrates the effectiveness of the selected features. Through SHAP values, we observe that among the 100 key features identified, 67 are associated with DNABERT and 30 with DNAshape. DNABERT, leveraging pre-training, substantially improves performance in identifying functional genetic variants and demonstrates strong potential for generalization to other species, including rice and wheat [54]. Demirci et al. found that specific DNAshape features can be used to predict crossover occurrence in various plant species, such as Arabidopsis thaliana, tomato, maize, and rice [55]. The DNA shape parameters specifically MGW and HelT modulate the binding of the bHLH transcription factor OsbHLH130 to its E-box motif in the promoter region, which induces the expression of OsWIH2 under drought stress [56]. OsWIH2, in turn, participates in the biosynthesis of cuticular wax, reducing water loss and reactive oxygen species accumulation, thereby significantly enhancing drought tolerance in rice [57]. To further demonstrate the effectiveness of SHAP, we compared its performance against other feature selection methods, including RFE [58], Boruta [59], and LightGBM [60]. RFE iteratively eliminates features with the lowest importance, refining the feature set until the target count is achieved. Boruta, using random forest comparisons with random ‘shadow’ features, identifies features of statistical significance. Although primarily designed for prediction, LightGBM also assesses feature importance by analyzing decision tree splits. Table 4 demonstrates that RiceSNP-ABST, with SHAP-based feature selection, consistently outperformed across all evaluation metrics.

In this study, we trained and optimized the model using the Rerrange_101nt dataset through 5-fold cross-validation. Our findings suggest that the prediction results for SNPs are improved by the synergistic effect with rice resistance genes and QTLs, enhancing rice’s ability to withstand abiotic stresses. In the independent dataset, a number of SNPs are linked to drought tolerance, nitrogen deficiency tolerance, and other stress resistances. For example, three SNPs are associated with the nitrogen deficiency resistance gene Gn1a [68], which encodes the cytokinin oxidase/dehydrogenase gene (OsCKX2). This gene reduces cytokinin degradation and improves nitrogen use efficiency in rice. Additionally, we collected 16 SNPs linked to salt tolerance [69], phosphorus deficiency tolerance [70], and submergence tolerance [71] (Table 11) from recent literature, ensuring they were independent of the benchmark dataset. Notably, The STG5 locus, associated with a QTL, enhances salt tolerance by regulating the OsHKT gene family to maintain Na+/K+ balance [69]. These results provide insights into the genetic mechanisms underlying rice’s resilience to abiotic stresses.

Conclusions

In this paper, a deep learning model aimed at predicting the ABST-SNPs in rice was proposed. To construct higher-quality datasets, a novel strategy of negative sample construction is proposed. Six benchmark datasets were created based on two sequence lengths (41 nt and 101 nt). For feature representation, methods including one-hot encoding, DNA2vec, DNABERT, and DNAshape were utilized. Due to the high dimensionality of features, SHAP-based methods were employed for feature optimization. The final predictive model is obtained by using a fully automated framework to search for optimal CNNs. Experimental results across various datasets demonstrate that RiceSNP-ASBT achieves superior overall performance compared to traditional machine learning methods and other existing methods. Moreover, through the ablation study, it is shown that residual connections significantly enhance the performance of RiceSNP-ABST. Furthermore, causal learning has revealed potential causal relationships among 13 DNA shape features. The results of various experiments have certificated that RiceSNP-ABST is a tool full of promise for distinguishing ABST-SNPs in rice.

Although RiceSNP-ABST offers significant advantages, further research is necessary. The current study focuses mainly on predicting ABST-SNPs in rice but faces challenges with poor data quality, limited encoding strategies, and low prediction accuracy. Additionally, the study focuses mainly on sequence-based and structural features, lacking integration of multi-omics features. Future work should address these limitations by focusing on the following aspects:

Enhance the model’s generalization ability by expanding the size and diversity of the training data through the integration of more comprehensive and varied rice genomic datasets.

The combination of current features with additional types, such as 3D structural data and multi-omics data features, will be explored.

Interpretability models will integrate biological knowledge to provide credible, meaningful explanations, clarifying genetic risks and mechanisms associated with abiotic stressors like temperature extremes, drought, flooding, and radiation.
