Upregulated astrocytic HDAC7 induces depression-like disorders via deacetylating PINK1 and inhibiting mitophagy
Authors: Rui-zhu Yue, Xing Guo, Cheng-jia Li, Li-juan Wu, Yu-tong Liu, Juan Hu, Bing-kang Yan, Hai-tong Lin, Ying-ling Tang, Wei-ming Yan, Shifeng Xiao, Yue Hao, Jinwang Ye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMajor depressive disorder (MDD) is a prevalent mental disorder and the leading cause of disability worldwide. Emerging evidence indicates that dysregulation of astrocyte mitochondria metabolism contributes to the pathophysiology of depression. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying the stress-induced astrocytic dysfunction remain poorly understood. Here, we show that impaired mitophagy, which occurring downstream of increased Class IIa histone deacetylases 7 (HDAC7) expression, play a critical role in astrocytic mitochondria dysfunction in a Lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced depression-like mouse model. Astrocyte-specific overexpression of HDAC7 in the hippocampus disrupted PTEN-induced putative kinase 1 (PINK1)-Parkinson protein 2 (Parkin)-dependent mitophagy, leading to reduced mitochondrial ATP release, neuronal damage and depressive-like behaviors. Mechanistically, HDAC7 impairs mitophagy by deacetylating PINK1, thereby suppressing phosphorylation of Parkin at Ser65 and inhibiting recruitment of downstream proteins translocase of outer mitochondrial membrane (TOMM) 20 and 40. Notably, Astrocyte-specific knockout or pharmacological inhibition of HDAC7 attenuates LPS-induced astrocytic mitophagy disruption, oxidative stress and mitochondrial adenosine triphosphate (ATP) release, alongside recovery of neuronal activity and reverse of depressive-like behavioral disorders in mice. Taken together, our data shed light on the intricate interplay between astrocytes, neuronal damage, and mitophagy in the etiology of depression, offering promising therapeutic targets for the treatment of MDD and other astrocyte-associated disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-025-03603-3.Keywords: HDAC7, PINK1, Mitophagy, Astrocyte, Depression

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a profound psychiatric condition predominantly characterized by persistent depressive mood and a marked reduction in interest or pleasure in activities, often accompanied by disturbances in sleep patterns, fluctuations in weight, impaired concentration, and reduced energy [1, 2]. Despite the availability of numerous pharmacological treatments for MDD, approximately 50% of patients fail to achieve remission following first-line pharmacological therapy [3], underscoring the urgent need for enhanced research into the molecular mechanisms underlying MDD. While the neuronal mechanisms underlying MDD have been largely identified, emerging evidence shows that dysregulation in glial cells, including microglia and astrocytes, may act as a critical factor in the onset and progression of depressive symptoms.

As the most prevalent glial cell type in the central nerves system (CNS), astrocytes play a critical role in modulating neuronal function in both physiological and pathophysiological conditions [4]. Through their interactions with neurons at tripartite synapses, astrocytes have been shown demonstrated to exert a tonic suppression of synaptic plasticity [5] and to facilitate heterosynaptic depression in the hippocampus through the release of ATP, which is metabolized to adenosine [6, 7]. Astrocytes, as well as microglia, serve as the innate immune cells in the CNS. Activated astrocytes and microglia in brain tissue samples from depressed suicidal patients [8, 9], combined with increased expression of pro-inflammatory factors in the blood and cerebrospinal fluid of patients with MDD [10, 11], suggest that neuroinflammation plays an important role in depression. Animal models indicate that astrocytes are activated by stress, triggering the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin (IL)−1, IL-6, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, and C-reactive protein (CRP), which contribute to depressive symptoms [12, 13]. Furthermore, multiple lines of evidence have demonstrated that reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated from damaged mitochondria leading the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines in astrocytes, and removal of impaired mitochondria via mitophagy is a potential therapeutic strategy for depression [14–16].

Mitophagy, a selective form of autophagy for the removal of damaged or superfluous mitochondria, plays a critical role in ensuring mitochondrial quality and maintaining normal mitochondrial function [17]. Accumulating evidence suggest that impaired mitophagy and mitochondrial dysfunction contribute to the pathogenesis of depression [18]. The canonical pathway for mitophagy is the PINK1-Parkin pathway. Upon mitochondrial stress, PINK1 accumulates on damaged mitochondria, where it recruits and phosphorylates the ubiquitin-like domain of Parkin at Ser65 epitopes, leading to Parkin activation. Activated Parkin ubiquitinates mitochondrial surface proteins, tagging damaged mitochondria for engulfment by autophagosomes and subsequent degradation via lysosomal fusion [19]. PINK1-deficient mice exhibited depression-like phenotypes, characterized by impaired adult hippocampal neurogenesis, reduced striatal plasticity, and decreased dopamine release [20, 21]. Pharmacological activation of PINK1/Parkin pathway was showed to restore neuronal mitophagy, suppress inflammation, and ameliorate depression-like behaviors in an LPS-induced model of depression. While the neuronal mechanisms underlying mitophagy and depression have been largely identified, the involvement of astrocytes in these processes remain largely unknown.

Histone deacetylates (HDACs) have been demonstrated to perform diverse functions in a wide range of biological processes, including cell survival, senescence, inflammatory responses, and metabolism. Distinguished from other HDACs, class IIa members (HDAC4, 5, 7, and 9) are characterized by their weak association with histone tails and their ability to shuttle between the cytoplasm and nucleus, enabling them to modulate diverse processes from signaling transduction to gene expression [22, 23]. A functional link between HDAC7 and autophagy has been established in neurodegenerative disease, where HDAC7 impairs the autophagosome-lysosome pathway via inhibiting transcription factor EB (TFEB) activity, thereby exacerbating neurodegeneration [24]. In the acute inflammation-challenged mice model, class IIa HDACs are shown to modulate phagocytic function of phagocytes and innate immune cell-mediated inflammation [25, 26]. Moreover, inhibition of class IIa HDAC7 promotes Nur77 translocation to mitochondria, where it converts the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 into a pro-apoptotic form, thereby inducing apoptosis in both cultured and patient-derived human CTCL cells [27]. Substantial evidence demonstrates that HDAC7 functions as a multifunctional regulator in neurodegenerative diseases, immune disorders, and cancers, where it dysregulates autophagy, modulates inflammatory responses, and orchestrates apoptotic processes through diverse molecular mechanisms. However, direct evidence implicating HDAC7 in astrocytic mitophagy within MDD remains lacking.

Hence, the present study aimed to ascertain the role of Class IIa HDACs in inflammation-induced depressive-like symptoms and astrocytic mitophagy dysfunction. Intriguingly, upregulated HDAC7 in astrocytes was indeed associated with depressive symptoms, neuronal damage, mitochondrial impairments and mitophagy disruption in astrocytes. We uncovered a novel mechanism through which upregulated HDAC7 disrupts PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy, leading to impaired mitochondrial dynamics, increased ROS production and decreased ATP generation in astrocytes. Maintaining mitophagic homeostasis in astrocytes through either the deletion of HDAC7 or its pharmacological inhibition provides neuroprotective effects that enhance neuronal survival and ameliorate depressive-like behaviors in mice. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting astrocytic HDAC7 for the intervention of MDD.

Materials and methods

Animals, stereotaxic surgery and drug treatment

Mice were housed in individually ventilated cages at a density of four to five mice per cage within a temperature-regulated facility maintained at 22 ± 2 °C, following a 12-h light/dark cycle with lights on at 08:00 am. Standard rodent chow and filtered water were available ad libitum. All experimental procedures adhered to the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and received approval from the Animal Care and Use Committee of Shenzhen University. Following anesthesia with isoflurane, the mice were secured in a stereotaxic frame. The surgical area was sterilized with iodophor, and an incision was made along the midline of the scalp. A burr hole was drilled in the skull at coordinates relative to bregma: −2.1 mm posterior, ± 2.1 mm lateral, and −1.7 mm ventral. AAV-GfaABC1D-HDAC7-HA or the control vector (1 μL; viral titer, 5.0 × 1012 vg/mL) was bilaterally injected into the hippocampus using a microinjection system (World Precision Instruments) at a rate of 0.125 μL/min. The needle was retained in position for 5 min before withdrawal. After the incision was closed with sutures, the mice were placed on a heating pad to facilitate recovery of consciousness. TMP195 was prepared at a concentration of 7.5 mg/mL in a sterile vehicle composed of 0.9% saline, 5% Tween-80, and 20% PEG-300 for intraperitoneal (i.p.) administration. Initially, the mice received an i.p. injection of either 50 mg/kg TMP195 or the vehicle. Two hours subsequently, they were administered an i.p. injection of either 3 mg/kg LPS or saline. Six hours following the initial TMP195/vehicle injection, a second i.p. injection of 50 mg/kg TMP195 was given. Behavioral assessments were conducted 24 h after the initial injection.

Generation of Hdac7flx/flx-Aldh1l1-CreERT2 mice

Hdac7flx/flx mice were purchased from GenPharmatech. Adult C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Changzhou Cavens Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd (Changzhou, China) [24]. Hdac7flx/flx mice were crossed with Aldh1l1-CreERT2 transgenic mice (JAX Stock No. 023748) to generate progeny homozygous for the Hdac7flx/flx allele and heterozygous for the Aldh1l1-CreERT2 transgene. These mice were utilized as experimental subjects following genotypic verification (SFig. 1 and SFig. 2). Genomic DNA was isolated from tail biopsies, hair follicles, or saliva using the Beyotime Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (D7283S, Beyotime). Genomic DNA was liberated by incubating the samples in an extraction buffer (96% DNA Extraction Solution, 4% Enzyme Mix) at 55 °C for 15 min, Then, the reaction was heat-inactivated at 95 °C for 5 min. Reactions were terminated with Stop Solution and stored at −20 °C until use. Genotyping of the HDAC7 allele was performed by PCR in a 20 μL reaction containing 10 μL Easy-Load™ Master Mix, 1 μL template DNA (2 ug/μL), 2 μL primer mix (forward: 5′-TCAGGAAGCCAGTACACCAGAACTG-3′; reverse: 5′-GGAAAGAGCTTGTGGGACGTCAC-3′), and 7 μL nuclease-free H₂O. The PCR protocol comprised an initial denaturation (95 °C, 5 min), followed by 10 touchdown cycles (20 s at 95 °C; 20 s annealing from 60 °C to 55 °C, decreasing by 0.5 °C per cycle; 50 s at 72 °C) and 26 standard cycles (20 s at 95 °C, 20 s at 55 °C, 50 s at 72 °C), with a final extension of 5 min at 72 °C. PCR products were electrophoresed on a 2% agarose gel, revealing amplicons of 247 bp (wild-type allele) and 352 bp (flxed allele). Detection of the Aldh1l1-CreERT2 transgene was performed using the following primers: wild-type allele (415 bp) with GAP1033-1 (5′-CTGTCCCTGTATGCCTCTGG-3′) and GAP1033-2 (5′-AGATGGAGAAAGGACTAGGCTACA-3′); mutant allele (198 bp) with GAP1033-3 (5′-GGCAAACGGACAGAAGCA-3′) and GAP1033-4 (5′-CTTCAACAGGTGCCTTCCA-3′). The thermocycling conditions were: 95 °C for 5 min; 20 cycles of 98 °C for 30 s, annealing from 65 °C to 55 °C (− 0.5 °C/cycle) for 30 s, and 72 °C for 45 s; then 20 cycles of 98 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 45 s; final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. To achieve astrocyte-specific knockout of HDAC7, two-month-old mice were administered intraperitoneal injections of tamoxifen (3 mg per mouse; Sigma, T5648), dissolved in corn oil (Sigma, C8267), once daily over a period of five consecutive days.

Cell culture

Primary mouse astrocytes were isolated from the cerebral cortices of postnatal C57BL/6 or Hdac7flx/flx mice, following the protocol previously established [28]. Briefly, the cortices were dissected and mechanically dissociated, after which the resulting cell suspension was subjected to centrifugation and resuspended in a complete culture medium consisting of DMEM/F-12 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. The cells were then cultured in T75 flasks, with the medium being replaced every three days. After a period of 7–10 days, microglial contamination was eliminated by orbital shaking of the cultures at 200–220 rpm for 14 h. Subsequently, the purified astrocytes were harvested through trypsinization and seeded at densities of 5 × 10^5 cells per well for Western blot/RT-qPCR analyses or 1 × 10^5 cells per well for immunofluorescence studies. HEK293T cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Transient transfection of HEK293T cells was conducted using LipoFactMax™ (ABP Biosciences, Beltsville, USA).

Behavioral testing

TST

The Tail Suspension Test (TST) is a well-established paradigm for evaluating despair-like behavior in rodents. The standardized protocol involves individually suspending mice using medical adhesive tape affixed 1.0 cm from the tip of the tail, with the animal positioned in an inverted posture approximately 40 cm above the ground. A blinded observer continuously monitors the behavior through a video recording system over a 5-min period. Cumulative immobility time, characterized by passive hanging with drooping limbs and an absence of body movement, is quantified using a stopwatch. This metric is significantly correlated with depressive-like despair behavior. Statistical analyses are conducted to record the total immobility time during the 5-min test interval.

FST

The Forced Swim Test (FST) was employed to assess despair-related behaviors in experimental animals. Each subject was introduced into a transparent vertical cylinder with dimensions of 50 cm in height and 20 cm in diameter, filled with water to a depth of 30 cm and maintained at a temperature of 23 ± 1 °C. Immobility was characterized by passive floating, marked by the absence of active movement or the presence of only minimal movements necessary to keep the head above water. The cumulative duration of immobility was recorded during a 5-min testing session.

SPT

The Sucrose Preference Test (SPT), an established measure of anhedonia, was conducted utilizing a two-bottle free-choice paradigm to assess drinking preferences [2]. After a three-day acclimation period to a 1% sucrose solution, the mice were randomly assigned to groups according to the specified methodology. On the second day of treatment, the subjects experienced a 24-h deprivation of both food and water. Subsequently, each mouse was granted unrestricted access to two bottles: one containing tap water and the other containing a 1% sucrose solution. To control for potential side preference effects on drinking behavior, the positions of the bottles were alternated among subjects. Sucrose preference was quantified using the following equation: Sucrose preference (%) = [sucrose intake (g)/total fluid consumption (g)] × 100%, where total fluid consumption is the sum of sucrose intake and water intake.

RT-qPCR

Mouse brain tissues were promptly harvested and preserved in liquid nitrogen. Cultured cells were swiftly collected following the removal of the medium and lysed using ice-cold TRIzol reagent (15596026, Invitrogen). Total RNA was extracted from both mouse brain tissues and cultured cells in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. The concentration and purity of the RNA were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). Reverse transcription of RNA to complementary DNA (cDNA) was executed utilizing the PrimeScript RT Kit (RR047A, Takara). Prior to reverse transcription, genomic DNA was removed by incubating the reaction mixture at room temperature for 30 min. Subsequently, this mixture was added to the RNA sample and incubated at 37 °C for 15 min, followed by a heat treatment at 85 °C for 5 s, and finally cooled to 4 °C. Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed using SYBR Master Mix on a Bio-Rad Connect Real-Time PCR platform (Bio-Rad). The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s; 39 cycles of amplification (95 °C for 5 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s); followed by a melt curve analysis (95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 95 °C for 5 s). Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method, normalized to the housekeeping gene GAPDH. The primer sequences used for RT-qPCR are listed in Table 1.

Western blotting and co-immunoprecipitation

Hippocampal tissues were meticulously dissected in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and subsequently homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer, which was supplemented with 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) and 0.1% protease inhibitor cocktail. Cells underwent washing with ice-cold PBS prior to lysis. The lysates were subjected to centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were carefully collected. Protein concentration was quantified using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (Beyotime, P0011). Equivalent amounts of protein were denatured in 2 × Laemmli buffer (comprising 50 mM Tris–HCl, 2% SDS, 10% glycerol, pH 7.6) at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by separation via SDS-PAGE and subsequent transfer onto nitrocellulose membranes (Whatman). The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in PBS for 1 h at room temperature, and then incubated overnight with primary antibodies at 4 °C. Following three washes with Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST), the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (P0018M, Beyotime) and quantified using a densitometry system (Kodak Image Station 4000 M). For co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP), cell lysates were pre-cleared with protein A/G agarose beads for 1 h at 4 °C. For the co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) procedure, cell lysates were initially pre-cleared using protein A/G agarose beads for 1 h at 4 °C. Subsequently, the pre-cleared lysates underwent immunoprecipitation overnight at 4 °C with the appropriate primary antibody. This was followed by a 2-h incubation with protein A/G beads at 4 °C. The resulting immune complexes were washed three times with ice-cold PBS, eluted in Laemmli buffer at 95 °C for 10 min, and then analyzed via immunoblotting. Immunofluorescence

Mice were anesthetized using isoflurane and subsequently subjected to transcardial perfusion with 0.9% saline for a duration of 10 to 15 min. This was followed by fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for an additional 10 to 15 min. The brains were then extracted and post-fixed in 4% PFA for 24 h at 4 °C. To achieve cryoprotection, the brains were sequentially immersed in 15%, 20%, and 30% sucrose solutions, each for approximately 48 h, with immersion duration determined by the sinking of the tissue as the endpoint criterion. Brain sections were then cut at a thickness of 30 μm using a cryostat microtome maintained at –20 °C. The tissue sections underwent a 5-min rinse in PBS at room temperature (RT), were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 (P0096, Beyotime) for 30 min, and subsequently blocked with QuickBlock™ blocking buffer (P0260, Beyotime) for 1 h at 37 °C. Following this, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies for 24 h at 4 °C, and then with appropriate fluorescent secondary antibodies for 2 h at 37 °C. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (5 μg/mL) for 10 min at RT under light-protected conditions. For immunofluorescence staining, cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 15 to 20 min, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 30 min, and subsequently blocked. For the immunofluorescence staining procedure, cells were initially fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for a duration of 15 to 20 min. Subsequently, they were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 30 min and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h at room temperature. Following this, the cells were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Post-incubation, the cells underwent three washes with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), each lasting 5 min. They were then incubated with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at 37 °C in a dark environment. After three additional PBS washes, the nuclei were stained with DAPI for 5 min. All samples were subsequently mounted and visualized using either a Zeiss LSM880 confocal microscope for tissue sections or an equivalent fluorescence microscope for cell samples. ATP assay

ATP production was measured using an ATP Assay Kit (S0026, Beyotime) following the manufacturer’s instructions. To measure ATP production, the ATP Assay Kit (S0026, Beyotime) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In the ATP assay following HDAC7 overexpression, primary astrocytes were cultured and transduced with either LV-GfaABC1D-HDAC7-P2A-HA or the corresponding control lentivirus for 7 days. The culture medium was aspirated, and cells were lysed by adding 200 μL of lysis buffer per well (equivalent to 1/10 of the original culture volume of 2 mL). To ensure complete lysis, the cells were gently pipetted or the plate was swirled to ensure full coverage with the lysis buffer. Typically, lysis occurred immediately upon contact with the buffer. The lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was collected for ATP measurement. In the ATP assays using HDAC7 knockout mice and TMP195 administration experiments, unilateral hippocampal tissues were collected from 6 mice per group. Approximately 100–200 μL of lysis buffer was added per 20 mg of tissue, and homogenization was performed using a glass homogenizer or similar device to ensure complete tissue disruption. Following centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, the supernatants were collected from the homogenates for ATP quantification. All reagents were thawed on ice. The ATP detection working solution was prepared as follows: an appropriate amount of ATP detection reagent was diluted at a 1:4 ratio with ATP detection diluent to obtain the working solution, which could be temporarily stored on ice. 100 μL of working solution was added to each sample and incubated at RT for 3–5 min to degrade background ATP. To save time, 10–20 wells or tubes could be processed simultaneously. Subsequently, 20 μL of sample or standard was added, followed by immediate mixing with a micropipette. After standing for at least 2 s, RLU values were measured using a luminometer.

TMRE assay

The mitochondrial membrane potential was evaluated utilizing the TMRE Assay Kit (C2001S, Beyotime) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Following experimental treatments, cells were enumerated and aliquoted. Subsequently, cells were subjected to centrifugation at 600 × g for 5 min at room temperature. The supernatant was removed, and the cell pellet was resuspended in an appropriate volume of TMRE staining working solution to achieve a concentration of 1 × 10^6 cells/mL. The cells were incubated at 37 °C for 20 min and then centrifuged again at 600 × g for 5 min. The supernatant was carefully aspirated without disturbing the cell pellet. The cells underwent two washes with pre-warmed (37 °C) culture medium: initially, they were resuspended in 1 mL of pre-warmed medium, centrifuged at 600 × g for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded; subsequently, they were resuspended in 1 mL of pre-warmed medium, centrifuged at 600 × g for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The samples were then analyzed via flow cytometry.

DHE assay

Primary astrocytes were cultured in T25 flasks for 7–10 days to allow adequate maturation and confluence. To purify the astrocytic population, the cultures were subjected to orbital shaking at 200–220 rpm for 14 h, effectively removing loosely attached non-astrocytic cells. The adherent astrocytes were then detached using trypsin digestion, collected by centrifugation, and replated into confocal imaging dishes at a density of 4 × 105 cells per well for subsequent experiments. To detect intracellular ROS, we performed dihydroethidium (DHE, S0063, Beyotime) staining. DHE was dissolved in anhydrous dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to make a 3 mM stock solution. Cells were incubated with 3 μM DHE in DMEM at 37 °C for 30 min for dye loading. After PBS washing (5 min), samples were immediately analyzed using an LSM880 confocal microscope (Zeiss, Germany).

Calcein AM/EthD-1

To preserve cellular morphology, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15–20 min. For antibody penetration, cells were then permeabilized with 0.1–0.5% Triton X-100 for 30 min, and finally blocked with 5% BSA at RT for 1 h to minimize nonspecific binding. Primary antibodies (diluted to optimal concentration) were then applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C for target-specific binding. The next day, samples were washed three times for 5 min each with PBS to remove unbound primary antibodies. Fluorescently conjugated secondary antibodies were incubated for 1 h at RT, protected from light. Following three 5 min PBS washes to remove excess secondary antibody, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min. Mounted specimens were imaged using fluorescence microscopy.

Electron microscopy (EM)

Tissue samples were initially fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde prepared in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) for 2 h at 4 °C to maintain ultrastructural integrity. Following primary fixation, the samples underwent three thorough rinses with 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.2) to eliminate residual fixative. Secondary fixation was then performed using 1% osmium tetroxide in the same buffer at 4 °C for 2 h to enhance membrane contrast. Post-fixation, the samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, and 100%), followed by infiltration and embedding in Epon-Araldite epoxy resin. Polymerization was conducted at 60 °C for 48 h, after which the blocks were trimmed and sectioned. Semi-thin Sects. (0.5–1 μm) were initially prepared and stained with toluidine blue for preliminary localization under light microscopy. Subsequently, ultrathin sections (60–80 nm) were cut using a diamond knife and collected onto copper grids. For contrast enhancement, the grids were stained with 3% uranyl acetate in 50% ethanol for 15 min, followed by rinsing and counterstaining with 2.7% lead citrate for 5 min. Finally, the specimens were examined using a JEM-1400 transmission electron microscope, and digital images were acquired for microstructural analysis.

MitoTracker assay

Primary astrocytes were cultured in T25 flasks for a duration of 7 to 10 days, followed by isolation through orbital shaking at a speed of 200 to 220 rpm for 14 h. Subsequently, the cultured cells underwent trypsinization, and the isolated astrocytes were replated into confocal dishes at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well. Upon reaching 50% confluence, the cells were subjected to transfection: the control group received the vector pcDNA3.1-PINK1-linker-EGFP-HA, whereas the experimental group was co-transfected with pcDNA3.1-HDAC7-HA and pcDNA3.1-PINK1-linker-EGFP-HA for a period of 48 h. For the purpose of mitochondrial staining, Mito Tracker (MTR) Deep Red FM (C1032, Beyotime) was utilized. A 50 μg aliquot of MTR Deep Red FM powder was dissolved in 460 μL of DMSO to prepare a 200 μM stock solution. The culture medium was initially removed, and the cells were incubated with the MTR working solution, diluted at a ratio of 1:5000, at 37 °C for 30 min. Following the removal of the working solution, fresh pre-warmed (37 °C) culture medium was added. Live-cell imaging was conducted using a live-cell imaging station.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10 (La Jolla, CA, USA). Differences between two groups were evaluated using an unpaired Student’s t-test. For comparisons involving three or more groups, either one-way ANOVA was utilized, followed by Tukey’s post hoc tests, as specified in the figure legends. Data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). A p-value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant, with significance levels indicated as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001).

Results

Impaired astrocytic mitophagy and mitochondrial function in the LPS-induced depression-like mouse model

As a widely established pro-inflammatory agent, LPS is routinely utilized to elicit depressive-like behaviors in various experimental models [26, 29]. In alignment with previous studies [30], our findings demonstrated that mice exhibited increased immobility in TST and FST behavioral tests (Fig. 1A and B), as well as a decreased preference for sucrose in SPT behavioral test (Fig. 1C) at 24 h post i.p. injection with LPS. Western blotting analysis of the mitophagy pathway demonstrated that the expression levels of PINK1, Parkin and the lysosomal marker LAMP1, was markedly decreased in the hippocampus of mice-injected with LPS, with the OMM protein TOMM20 and TOMM40 remain unchanged (Fig. 1D and E). Notably, the levels of autophagy, as indicated by LC3B (an autophagy formation marker) and LMAP1 (an autophagy degradation marker), was comparable between control and LPS-treated mice (Fig. 1D). In align with in vivo data, western blotting analysis showed that LPS treatment reduced the levels of PINK1, Parkin, and LAMP1 in cultured astrocytes, with TOMM20, TOMM40 and P62 remain unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 1 A and B).

To determine the mitophagy dynamics in astrocytes in vivo, we conducted fluorescence co-immunostaining analysis in mouse brain sections. The results indicated that the colocalization of LC3B and TOMM40 in GFAP positive astrocytes was markedly decreased in the ventral hippocampus of LPS-challenged mice relative to the control mice (Fig. 1F and G). Moreover, a similar reduced colocalization of LAMP1 and TOMM20 in GFAP positive astrocytes was also observed in the LPS-induced depression-like mouse model (Fig. 1H and I). Accordingly, reduced levels of multi-subunit complexes of the mitochondrial electron transport chain, including complex I (NADH: ubiquinone oxidoreductase subunit A10, NDUFA10), III (Ubiquinol-cytochrome C reductase, rieske iron-sulfur polypeptide 1, UQCRFS1) and complex IV (Cytochrome C oxidase subunit 5 A, COX5A) was detected in the LPS-treated mice brain (Fig. 1J and K).To further identify this phenomenon in vitro, we cultured primary mouse astrocytes and treated cells with LPS (250 ng/mL). In align with in vivo data, western blotting analysis showed that LPS treatment reduced the levels of mitochondrial complex I, III and IV in cultured astrocytes, without affecting complex II (Supplementary Fig. 1 C and D). Furthermore, EM analysis demonstrated increased fraction of damaged mitochondria in astrocytes from LPS-challenged mice, as characterized by mitochondria with round, swollen, and cristae loss morphology (Fig. 1L and M). Astrocytes in EM analysis is distinguished from other cell types by their oval nucleus, deep chromatin, and filament in cytoplasm [31]. The mitochondrial damage in astrocytes was further characterized by elevated level of ROS measured by DHE staining (Fig. 1N). Taken together, our results demonstrated disrupted mitophagy and impaired mitochondrial function in astrocytes in the LPS-induced depression-like mouse model.

Increased expression of HDAC7 correlates with autophagy impairments in astrocytes of the LPS-induced depression-like mouse model

Previous study suggests the critical role of class IIa HDACs in the functional regulation of autophagy lysosomal signaling pathways in astrocytes [24]. To explore involvement of class IIa HDACs in this LPS-induced depression-like mouse model, we analyzed their expression alternations and distribution in different cell tyles in mouse brains. Notably, a significant upregulation of HDAC7 expression was observed in the hippocampal tissue of LPS-injected mice compared to control mice treated with saline (Fig. 2A). Conversely, no significant changes were detected in the expression of other class IIa HDACs, namely HDAC4, HDAC5, and HDAC9 (Fig. 2A and B). Remarkably, co-immunostaining revealed that the elevated HDAC7 expression was predominantly localized in astrocytes within the brains of LPS-injected mice (Fig. 2C). HDAC7 expression in microglia was relatively low, and minimal differences were observed following LPS treatment (Fig. 2D). Whereas neuronal HDAC7 expression remained unchanged (Fig. 2E). Moreover, increased HDAC7 protein level was detected in primary cultured astrocytes following LPS stimulation, whereas no significant changes were observed in primary microglia or neurons (Fig. 2F-I). Taken together, these results suggest that HDAC7 is specifically upregulated in astrocytes in LPS-induced depression-like mouse model.

Through co-immunostaining of HDAC7 with LC3B, we observed that increased HDAC7 protein expression is correlated with reduced levels of autophagy formation marker LC3B in the mouse brains (Fig. 2J and K). Furthermore, a similar association was also identified between HDAC7 and lysosomal marker LAMP1 in astrocytes (Fig. 2L and M). Taken together, these findings suggest that upregulated HDAC7 may contribute to the disruption of astrocytic mitophagy dynamics in LPS-induced depression-like mouse model.

Overexpression of HDAC7 in astrocytes induces mitophagy disruption, neuronal loss and depression-like behavior in mice

To investigate the role of HDAC7 in the regulation of astrocytic mitophagy and mitochondrial functional dynamics in vivo, an adeno-associated virus (AAV) was engineered to express HDAC7 under the control of the astrocyte-specific promoter GfaABC1D (G1). Then we microinjected AAV-GfaABC1D-HDAC7-HA or AAV-GfaABC1D-HA into the hippocampus of 3-month-old WT mice (Fig. 3A). At 1 month post-injection, astrocyte-specific AAV expression in the ventral hippocampus was confirmed using confocal microscopy, and the expression of HDAC7 in the hippocampus was assessed by western blotting (Fig. 3B). Immunofluorescence co-staining analysis using the anti-LC3B, anti-TOMM40 and anti-GFAP antibodies revealed that HDAC7 overexpression resulted in prominently reduced colocalization of LC3B and TOMM40 in astrocytes (Fig. 3C and D). Similarly, the colocalization of LAMP1 and TOMM20 in astrocytes was also disrupted following HDAC7 overexpression (Fig. 3E and F). These results suggest that HDAC7 overexpression impairs mitophagy in astrocytes in vivo. In light of the above results, we assessed the alternations of astrocytic mitochondrial dynamics. EM analysis illustrated similar round, swollen and cristae loss mitochondrial morphology in astrocytes following HDAC7 overexpression (Fig. 3G and H). Moreover, reduced expression levels of the mitochondrial electron transport chain subunit complex I, III and IV was also observed in the hippocampal tissues of HDAC7-overexpressing mice (Fig. 3I and J). Consistent with the in vitro results, impairments of the mitochondrial complex subunit level were observed in HDAC7-overexpressing primary astrocytes (Supplementary Fig. 2 A and B). Taken together, these results indicate that overexpression of HDAC7 induces astrocytic mitophagy disruption and mitochondrial dysfunction in mice.

Then we assessed the impact of astrocytic HDAC7 on neuronal survival and behavioral performance. Immunostaining analysis of neuronal nuclear antigen (NeuN) and cleaved caspase-3 revealed that astrocytic HDAC7 overexpression resulted in a prominent increase of apoptotic neurons and decrease of neuron numbers in the granule layer of hippocampus (Fig. 3K-M). Behavioral measurements showed that astrocytic HDAC7 expression resulted in increased immobility in the TST and FST tests (Fig. 3N and O), as well as decreased sucrose preference in the SPT test (Fig. 3P). Collectively, these results indicate that astrocytic overexpression of HDAC7 induces neuronal loss and depression-like behaviors in mice.

HDAC7 binds to and deacetylates PINK1, resulting in mitophagy impairment and astrocyte neurotoxicity

PINK1 plays a critical role in the clearance of impaired mitochondria via mitophagy, and acetylation has been implicated in the regulation of PINK1 activity and the process of mitophagy [32]. To determine if HDAC7 interacts with PINK1, we performed co-immunoprecipitation in HEK293T cells expressing PINK1-HA along with either HDAC7 or a control vector. The results showed that HDAC7 directly interacted with PINK1 and decreased PINK1 acetylation (Fig. 4A and B). Furthermore, decreased PINK1 activity, as evidenced by phosphorylation levels of Parkin P65 at Ser65 was detected in an immunoprecipitation-based kinase activity assay (Fig. 4C and D). In algin with this, the interaction between PINK1 and TOMM20 and TOMM40 was also disrupted by HDAC7 overexpression (Fig. 4C and D). To further examine whether HDAC7 has an impact on the kinetics of PINK1 accumulation on depolarized mitochondria, we expressed GFP-fused PINK1 in primary cultured astrocytes and imaged cells live following depolarization with CCCP. Interestingly, the accumulation of PINK1 on nuclear translocation in astrocytes induced by CCCP was almost completely blocked by the overexpression of HDAC7 (Fig. 4E). Additionally, overexpression of HDAC7 inhibited the colocalization between PINK1 and p-Parkin (Ser65) (Fig. 4F and G), and the colocalization between PINK1 and TOMM20 (Fig. 4H and I). In summary, these findings indicate that HDAC7 is a key regulator of PINK1-Parkin pathway mediated mitophagy involving PINK1 acetylation. Then we assessed the impact of HDAC7 on mitochondrial function in cultured astrocytes. Our results showed that HDAC7 overexpression induces reduction in ATP levels (Fig. 4J), elevation in mitochondrial membrane potential as assessed by tetramethylrhodamine ethyl ester (TMRE) staining (Fig. 4K), and accumulation of ROS as assessed by DHE staining (Fig. 4L) in primary mouse astrocytes.

To further elucidate whether the HDAC7-expressing astrocytes have influence on neurons, we collected the conditioned medium (CM) from HDAC7 or control GFP overexpressing astrocytes. Purified astrocytes were transduced with viruses expressing either HDAC7 or a control GFP. Interestingly, we observed a prominently increased cell death in neurons treated with CM from HDAC7-expressing astrocytes (Fig. 4N). These data suggest that HDAC7 overexpression in astrocytes confers neurotoxicity on neurons.

Genetic deletion or pharmacological inhibition of HDAC7 ameliorates LPS-induced mitochondrial dysfunction in astrocytes in vitro

Given the established role of HDAC7 in the impairment of PINK1/Parkin-dependent mitophagy and the consequent loss of astrocyte function, we investigated the therapeutic potential of HDAC7 deletion in cultured astrocytes. Primary astrocytes purified from HDAC7flx/flx mice were transduced with either a Cre-expressing lentivirus or a control virus for a duration of seven days to achieve HDAC7 deletion. Subsequently, the cells were exposed to LPS for 24 h. Western blotting analysis revealed that HDAC7 deletion effectively restored the protein levels of mitochondrial electron transport chain subunit complexes I, III, and IV in LPS-treated cells (Fig. 5A and B). Moreover, the LPS-induced accumulation of ROS, as assessed by DHE staining (Fig. 5C and D), as well as the impairments of mitochondrial membrane potential assessed by TMRE staining (Fig. 5E), were also reversed by HDAC7 deletion in cultured astrocytes. Accordingly, the neurotoxicity of LPS-treated astrocytes, as measured by the astrocyte CM on primary neurons, was significantly blocked by HDAC7 deletion in astrocytes (Fig. 5F and G).

To further substantiate the protective effects of HDAC7 inhibition against LPS-induced mitochondrial dysfunction, we investigated mitochondrial function and astrocyte neurotoxicity using a selective class IIa HDAC inhibitor with high affinity for HDAC7. Specifically, we examined the impact of TMP195 on mouse primary astrocytes by pre-treating the cells with TMP195 for 1 h, followed by a 24-h treatment with a combination of LPS and TMP195. Consistent with findings from genetic deletion of HDAC7, TMP195 treatment effectively mitigated the LPS-induced reduction in levels of mitochondrial electron transport chain subunit complexes I, III, and IV (Fig. 5H and I), as well as the accumulation of ROS (Fig. 5J and K) and impairments in mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. 5L). Further analysis of neuronal viability using astrocyte-CM corroborated the neuroprotective effects of astrocytic HDAC7 inhibition (Fig. 5M and N). Collectively, these results identify HDAC7 as a critical mediator of functional impairment in LPS-challenged astrocytes, primarily through the disruption of mitochondrial dynamics, thereby impacting neuronal health.

HDAC7 knockout alleviates mitochondrial damage, reduces neuronal death, and ameliorates depression-like behaviors in mice

Given the neuroprotective effects of astrocytic HDAC7 deletion observed in vitro, we sought to explore the therapeutic potential of HDAC7 deletion in vivo. To this end, Hdac7flx/flx mice were crossed with the Aldh1L1-CreERT2 mouse line, resulting in the generation of Hdac7flx/flx/Aldh1L1-CreERT2 mice. For the targeted ablation of Hdac7 in astrocytes, a regimen of 20 mg tamoxifen was administered intragastrically to the mice at 3 months of age over a period of 7 consecutive days. This was followed by an i.p. injection of either LPS (3 mg/kg) or control saline at 4 months of age. 24 h post-LPS injection, mice were subjected to behavioral and pathological analyses. As expected, co-immunostaining of HDAC7 and GFAP verified the effective deletion of astrocytic HDAC7 in Hdac7−/− mice (Fig. 6A and B). Further co-immunostaining analyses of LC3B and TOMM40 demonstrated that HDAC7 deletion ameliorated mitophagy impairments in astrocytes within the hippocampus (Fig. 6C and D). Western blotting analysis corroborated these findings by showing that HDAC7 deletion reinstated the levels of mitochondrial electron transport chain subunit complexes (Fig. 6E and F). Additionally, EM analysis revealed a reduction in the proportion of impaired mitochondria in astrocytes in HDAC7−/− mice compared to HDAC7+/+ mice following LPS treatment (Fig. 6G and H). LPS-induced reduction in ATP level was also rescued by HDAC7 deletion (Fig. 6I). Collectively, these data suggest that astrocytic HDAC7 deletion attenuates LPS-induced mitophagy impairments and mitochondrial damages in vivo.

Transformation of astrocytes into a “reactive” state is pivotal in mediating neuronal damage associated with the progression of depression. A1 reactive astrocytes have been characterized as neurotoxic, whereas A2 reactive astrocytes are believed to confer neuroprotective effects [33]. Considering the documented neuroprotective benefits of HDAC7 deletion in mitigating LPS-induced neuronal damage in vivo, we investigated the expression of reactive astrocyte marker genes. RT-qPCR analysis revealed HDAC7 deletion significantly decreased the expression of pan-reactive (including Lcn2, Gfap, Serpina3n, and Vim) (Fig. 6J) and A1 neurotoxic reactive astrocyte markers (H2-D1, H2-T23, Gbp2, and Fkbp5) (Fig. 6K), while restoring the expression of A2 neuroprotective reactive astrocyte markers (S100a10, Dd109, Cd14) (Fig. 6L), and reducing levels of inflammatory cytokines (Ptgs2, Il1α, and Il1β) (Fig. 6M) in LPS-treated mice. Corresponding to the reversal of the reactive astrocyte phenotype, a reduction in cleaved caspase-3 signal in hippocampal neurons and a restoration of neuronal numbers were observed in LPS-treated mice with HDAC7 deletion (Fig. 6N-P). Furthermore, the depression-like behaviors exhibited by LPS-treated mice were ameliorated by HDAC7 deletion in astrocytes, as indicated by decreased immobility duration in the TST and FST tests, and restored sucrose preference in the SPT test (Fig. 6Q-S).

Taken together, these data offer compelling evidence for the neuroprotective effects of astrocytic HDAC7 deletion against LPS-induced astrocyte neurotoxicity, neuronal damage, and depression-like behavior.

Inhibiting HDAC7 by TMP195 alleviates LPS-induced astrocytic mitochondria damage, neuronal loss and depression-like behavior in mice

The effect of HDAC7 pharmacological inhibition in LPS-induced depressive mouse model was also explored using TMP195. 3-mon-old male C57BL/6 mice received an i.p. pre-injection of TMP195 three hours prior to the administration of LPS or control saline, followed by a second TMP195 injection 6 h post-LPS treatment. Consistent with the genetic data, co-immunostaining analyses showed that TMP195 treatment effectively ameliorated astrocytic mitophagy impairments in the brain, as evidenced by the restored colocalization of LC3B with TOMM40 and LAMP1 with TOMM20 in GFAP positive astrocytes (Fig. 7A-D). Again, TMP195 attenuated the levels of mitochondrial electron transport chain subunit complexes (Fig. 7E and F) and restored the morphology of astrocytic mitochondria in the hippocampus of LPS-injected mice (Fig. 7G and H). Accordingly, the reduction in ATP level observed in LPS-injected mice was reversed following TMP195 treatment (Fig. 7I). Additionally, immunostaining analysis showed that HDAC7 inhibition via TMP195 mitigated the LPS-induced activation of cleaved caspase-3 and the neuronal loss in the hippocampus (Fig. 7J-L). Treatment with TMP195 treatment also ameliorated the depression-like behaviors in LPS-injected mice, as evidenced by reduced immobility in TST and FST tests, along with increased sucrose preference in the SPT test (Fig. 7M-O). Overall, these findings indicate that pharmacological inhibition of HDAC7 with TMP195 can ameliorate astrocytic mitophagy dysfunction, mitochondria impairments and depression-like behavioral disorders in LPS-induced mice.

Discussion

In this investigation, we have discovered that HDAC7 regulates mitophagy in astrocytes and have elucidated the mechanism by which this process ameliorates MDD (Fig. 8). In the present study, utilizing the LPS-induced depression-like mouse model, we identified a marked upregulation of HDAC7 in astrocytes, which was associated with impaired astrocytic mitophagy and mitochondrial dysfunction. Our findings, for the first time, elucidate that HDAC7 impedes mitophagy by deacetylating PINK1, resulting in decreased phosphorylation of Parkin at Ser65. This process obstructs the recruitment of Parkin to the mitochondrial outer membrane proteins TOMM20 and TOMM40. We demonstrated that either genetic ablation or pharmacological inhibition of HDAC7 effectively reinstates mitophagy and mitigates mitochondrial functional impairments in astrocytes, both in vivo and in vitro. Moreover, HDAC7 deletion or inhibition prevents LPS-induced astrocyte neurotoxicity, facilitates neuronal survival, and reverses depressive-like behaviors in mice. Collectively, our data illuminate the complex crosstalk between astrocyte dysfunction and neuronal damage in the pathogenesis of depression, thereby supporting a potential therapeutic strategy of targeting HDAC7 for MDD and other astrocyte-related neurological diseases.

Proposed molecular mechanism by which HDAC7 impairs mitophagy in astrocytes leading to depression-like behaviors in mice. In astrocytes, LPS triggers HDAC7 upregulation, which binds to and deacetylates PINK1, hindering its mitochondrial translocation and reducing Parkin phosphorylation at p65. This diminishes Parkin’s binding to TOMM20 and TOMM40, disrupting mitophagy and causing mitochondrial dysfunction, characterized by increased oxidative stress, ROS release, decreased ATP production, and lower mitochondrial membrane potential. These defects impair astrocyte function, leading to a reactive phenotype that disrupts neuron communication, increases neuronal apoptosis, and induces depression-like behaviors in mice

HDAC7, a class IIa HDAC, is integral to the regulation of gene expression, cell proliferation, cell differentiation and cell survival, thereby influencing development, angiogenesis, immune function, inflammation and metabolism [34]. Studies has demonstrated a connection between HDAC7 and innate immune cells that regulate brain function [26, 35, 36], particularly in response to neuroinflammatory conditions, where it regulates the phagocytic ability and inflammatory responses of astrocytes [24, 26]. Moreover, genetic mutations in HDAC7 have been associated with neurological diseases, influencing the susceptibility to multiple sclerosis through the regulation of regulatory T cell-mediated neuroinflammation [37]. However, the direct association of HDAC7 with neuropsychiatric disorders remains inadequately understood, and its role in inflammatory responses related to mitophagy is poorly characterized. In the present study, we found that elevated levels of HDAC7 corelate with impaired mitophagy in astrocytes following LPS exposure in the murine brain, suggesting HDAC7’s involvement in mitophagy under inflammatory stress conditions. Study have indicated that HDAC7 can translocate between mitochondrial membrane and cytoplasmic compartments in response to apoptotic signaling [38]. Notably, mitochondrial dysfunction in astrocytes has been observed in the context of neuroinflammation and depressive symptoms [14, 39], implying that dysregulation of HDAC7 may influence mitochondrial dynamics. Consistent with our previous research linking astrocytes dysregulation to neuronal damage in Alzheimer’s disease and other psychiatric disorders [24, 26], the present study reveals that elevated levels of HDAC7 act as a pivotal mediator linking deficits in astrocytic mitophagy to neuronal damage within the pathophysiology of depression. Specifically, overexpression of HDAC7 in astrocytes leads to mitophagy disruption and mitochondria damage, which are associated with neuronal damage and depressive-like behaviors. Conversely, genetic deletion or pharmacological inhibition of HDAC7 ameliorated astrocytic mitophagy and mitochondrial impairments, reduced neuronal loss, and alleviated depression-like symptoms in LPS-induced mice. These findings further support the hypothesis that upregulated HDAC7 may play a significant role in determining astrocyte functionality for neurons in the context of inflammation-related mood disorders.

Neurons typically play a crucial role in the pathophysiology of depression, with the efficacy of antidepressant medications largely dependent on the regulation of neurotransmitters [40]. However, growing evidence shows that astrocyte-neuron interactions across various brain regions significantly contribute to the development of psychiatric disorders. Notably, postmortem analysis of the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus have revealed a reduction in the number of astrocytes in individuals diagnosed with major depression [41], and selective acute injury to astrocytes has been shown to be sufficient to induce depressive-like behaviors [42]. Importantly, studies have demonstrated that identified astrocyte-derived ATP as a critical modulator of depressive-like behavior [43]. Intriguingly, our observations indicate that HDAC7 overexpression leads to decreased ATP levels in astrocytes, while genetic deletion or pharmacological inhibition of HDAC7 restores ATP levels in LPS-induced mice. Our findings demonstrate that elevated HDAC7 levels induce mitochondrial impairments by disrupting PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy. Consequently, alterations in ATP levels may be attributed to HDAC7-mediated regulation of mitochondrial dynamics in astrocytes. Consistent with our observations in LPS-induced mice in vivo, an ATP-mediated mechanism linking astrocytes to depression has been proposed, demonstrating that enhancing ATP production in astrocytes effectively alleviates depressive-like disorders [44, 45]. Beyond increasing astrocytic ATP production, the antidepressant effects of targeting HDAC7 may also arise from mitigating astrocyte-derived neurotoxic factors and pro-inflammatory cytokines. This effect may be mediated by promoting TFEB-mediated autophagy lysosomal pathways [24] or by inhibiting nuclear factor κB activation-mediated pro-inflammatory responses [26] via HDAC7 blockage.

In the canonical PINK1-Parkin mitophagy pathway, loss of mitochondrial membrane potential prevents PINK1 import, leading to its accumulation on the OMM [46]. PINK1 subsequently phosphorylates ubiquitin (on specific OMM proteins) and the E3 ubiquitin ligase Parkin, thereby triggering Parkin’s recruitment to and activation on the OMM [47, 48]. Parkin in turn ubiquitinates numerous OMM proteins, allowing their recognition by bona fide autophagic adaptors enroute to degradation in the lysosomes [49]. The regulation of PINK1 accumulation on the mitochondrial surface and the subsequent recruitment of Parkin is influenced by various post-translational modifications, notably phosphorylation and acetylation [50–52]. While it has been established that PINK1 autophosphorylation is essential for Parkin recruitment to damaged mitochondria [50], the molecular mechanisms governing acetylation and deacetylation, such as those involving HDACs, remain to be fully elucidated. Previous research has indicated that SIRT3-mediated deacetylation of PINK1 activates mitophagy in the context of liver fibrosis [52]. In contrast to these findings, our study reveals that class IIa HDAC7, which is specifically upregulated in astrocytes following LPS stimulation, can directly bind to and deacetylate PINK1. This interaction inhibits PINK1 accumulation on depolarized mitochondria, resulting in impaired mitophagy and the accumulation of damaged mitochondria in astrocytes, as indicated by decreased Parkin phosphorylation and reduced colocalization of PINK1 with TOMM20. Furthermore, upon LPS treatment, we observed increased colocalization of LC3B with TOMM40 in both HDAC7-null and TMP195-treated astrocytes in the brain, suggesting increased PINK1 accumulation on the mitochondrial surface due to HDAC7 blockage. Anyway, the specific deacetylation site on PINK1 mediated by HDAC7 remains unidentified, further mass spectrometry analysis is needed in the future.

In conclusion, our results demonstrate that demonstrate that maintaining astrocytic mitophagic homeostasis via targeting HDAC7 restores impaired mitochondrial function, preserves normal membrane potential, balances ROS flux, and promotes ATP generation. Astrocytic mitophagic homeostasis serves as a critical functional nexus for cerebral energy-redox equilibrium, and its disruption constitutes a central node in the neuro-metabolic disturbances associated with depression. Restoration of this mechanism by targeting HDAC7, through the clearance of dysfunctional mitochondria, reestablishes cerebral homeostasis, thereby paving the way for developing glia-focused mitochondrial therapeutics for depression.
