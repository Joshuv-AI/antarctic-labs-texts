Normal weight, hidden risk: adiposity is a better determinant of liver cancer than body mass index
Authors: Eduardo H Gilglioni, Ao Li, Latifa Bakiri, Esteban N Gurzov
Published: 2026

Primary liver cancer, predominantly hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with a disproportionate burden in China (1). Although chronic viral hepatitis has historically driven hepatocarcinogenesis, the epidemiological trend is shifting toward metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) as a critical etiological factor (2). This transition exposes a major limitation in current risk stratification tools—namely, the reliance on body mass index (BMI) as a proxy for metabolic health.

In this context, the recent prospective cohort study by Wang and colleagues, published in the International Journal of Surgery (3), provides important evidence that normal-weight obesity (NWO)—defined by excess adiposity despite a normal BMI—is associated with an increased risk of liver cancer. Although the observed effect size was modest (hazard ratio 1.22), the large cohort, prolonged follow-up, and biological consistency with emerging mechanistic studies support the relevance of adiposity as a contributor to liver cancer risk, particularly at the population level.

Reframing hepatocarcinogenesis: beyond cirrhosis and BMI

The classical paradigm of HCC development in metabolic disease emphasizes progression from steatosis to steatohepatitis, fibrosis, and cirrhosis (4). However, this model is increasingly insufficient. A substantial and growing fraction of HCC arises in non-cirrhotic livers, particularly in metabolically dysregulated individuals (5). This observation suggests that hepatic fat accumulation and chronic inflammation are not merely upstream events but proximal oncogenic drivers capable of initiating tumorigenesis independently of fibrosis.

NWO represents a clinically underrecognized manifestation of this biology. Individuals with normal BMI but increased adiposity frequently exhibit visceral fat accumulation, insulin resistance, and systemic inflammation—features that converge on tumour-promoting pathways. Consequently, BMI-based classifications omit a population at meaningful oncologic risk.

Mechanistic convergence: metabolic reprogramming as a central driver

Adipose tissue depots are not merely inert energy storage compartments (Figure 1). Dysfunctional adipose tissue continuously releases free fatty acids, inflammatory cytokines, and lipotoxic metabolites into the circulation, chronically exposing the liver to metabolic stress. Excess adiposity-related hyperinsulinemia and activation of the insulin–insulin-like growth factor (IGF) axis promote hepatocyte proliferation, while inflammatory cytokines sustain a tumour-permissive microenvironment (6,7). Lipotoxic stress further induces oxidative damage, mitochondrial dysfunction, and endoplasmic reticulum stress, collectively fostering genomic instability (8).

Mechanistic insights from experimental models converge on metabolic reprogramming as a central link between adiposity and liver cancer. In mouse livers exposed to high-fat diets, increased fatty acid availability enhances hepatic glucose uptake, glycolytic flux, lactate production, serine biosynthesis, and pyruvate carboxylase activity, generating a metabolic profile resembling established HCC (9). Importantly, these alterations occur prior to major transcriptional rewiring or overt tumour formation, suggesting that nutrient availability itself can rapidly reshape hepatocyte metabolism.

This is exemplified by studies where transcription factors or protein tyrosine phosphatases were genetically manipulated (6,7,10-12). c-Fos, a member of the AP-1 transcription factor, acts as a nutrient- and insulin-responsive regulator linking obesity with metabolic adaptation in hepatocytes (10). Its hepatic activation in obesity promotes glycolysis, lipid accumulation, insulin resistance, and stress signalling, while supporting oncogenic cell survival under metabolic stress (10). In addition, obesity-driven signalling—particularly via protein tyrosine phosphatase non-receptor type 2 (PTPN2)-STAT3-dependent pathways—can directly reprogram hepatocytes and promote tumorigenesis independent of cirrhosis in mice (12). Extending this concept, protein tyrosine phosphatase receptor kappa (PTPRK) was identified as a critical phosphatase orchestrating hepatic metabolic reprogramming in obesity (11). Specifically, PTPRK upregulation in fatty livers enhances glycolysis and de novo lipogenesis through peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ)-associated pathways, thereby driving hepatocyte lipid accumulation and tumour-promoting metabolic states (11). Genetic ablation of PTPRK increases fructose-bisphosphatase 1 (FBP1) activity, reduces PPARγ-associated steatosis and suppresses liver tumour development in vivo, establishing a direct mechanistic link between obesity-induced metabolic rewiring and hepatocarcinogenesis.

Collectively, these studies support a unifying model in which lipid accumulation, metabolic flux reprogramming, and inflammation act in concert to drive malignant transformation, even in the absence of advanced fibrosis.

Epidemiological validation: the Wang et al. cohort

The study by Wang et al. (3) provides large-scale epidemiological support for these pre-clinical mechanistic insights. In a cohort of approximately 0.3 million adults with normal BMI and 12 years of longitudinal follow-up, increased body fat percentage was associated with a significantly elevated risk of liver cancer, with a clear dose-response relationship (Figure 1). Notably, the association was significantly stronger in hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg)-negative than in HBsAg-positive individuals, supported by a formal interaction test (Pinteraction =0.008), although this subgroup finding requires independent validation.

These findings extend the relevance of adiposity beyond traditional obesity and into populations previously considered low risk. Importantly, they align with emerging evidence that metabolic drivers of hepatocarcinogenesis are both underrecognized and increasing in prevalence.

Implications for clinical practice

These observations have immediate implications for clinical risk stratification. BMI alone is clearly insufficient to capture metabolic risk and fails to account for fat distribution, ectopic lipid deposition, and inflammatory status. Incorporation of body composition measurements—whether through imaging, bioimpedance, or circulating biomarkers—may provide a more accurate assessment of oncogenic risk.

Moreover, current surveillance strategies for liver cancer, largely restricted to patients with cirrhosis or viral hepatitis, may overlook individuals with adiposity-driven risk in the absence of advanced fibrosis. Expanding risk models to include metabolic parameters will be essential as the epidemiology of liver cancer continues to evolve.

Therapeutic perspectives: targeting biology rather than weight

The recognition of adiposity-driven hepatocarcinogenesis also reframes therapeutic priorities. Lifestyle intervention remains central, as diet, energy balance, and regular physical activity reduce visceral and ectopic fat accumulation, improve insulin sensitivity, and suppress hepatic de novo lipogenesis and fibrosis. While agents such as resmetirom, a thyroid hormone receptor-β agonist, or glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have demonstrated efficacy in reducing fibrosis, improving hepatic lipid metabolism and reducing lipotoxicity (13,14), they represent only one face of a broader therapeutic landscape.

Emerging approaches—including dual and triple incretin agonists, PPAR-targeted therapies, and fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21) analogues—aim to directly modulate lipid metabolism, insulin signalling, and inflammation (15). Importantly, novel mechanistic insights such as the PTPRK-driven metabolic axis suggest that targeting hepatic metabolic reprogramming itself may represent a rational strategy for cancer prevention and early treatment, particularly in metabolically defined patient subsets.

Conclusions

The study by Wang et al. (3) provides evidence that excess adiposity within a normal BMI range confers increased liver cancer risk, challenging entrenched assumptions regarding obesity and metabolic health. When integrated with emerging mechanistic data, these findings support a paradigm shift in which adiposity and metabolic dysfunction should be viewed as important drivers of hepatocarcinogenesis. Further validation across diverse populations and liver cancer subtypes is needed. As this study was conducted in a large Chinese cohort with a relatively high prevalence of NWO, future work should assess whether these findings can be replicated in other populations with differing adiposity patterns, lifestyles, and liver disease aetiologies.

A transition toward biologically informed definitions of obesity—centred on adiposity, metabolic function, and molecular pathways—will be essential for improving risk stratification, guiding therapeutic development, and ultimately reducing the burden of liver cancer.

In short, normal weight should no longer be considered synonymous with low risk.
