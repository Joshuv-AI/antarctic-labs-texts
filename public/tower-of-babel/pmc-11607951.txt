The emergency medical service dispatch recommendation system using simulation based on bed availability
Authors: Yeong-Yuh Xu, Shao-Jen Weng, Ping-Wen Huang, Lee-Min Wang, Chih-Hao Chen, Yao-Te Tsai, Ming-Che Tsai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe number of patients using emergency medical services (EMS) through ambulance dispatch has been increasing annually in Taiwan. Due to limited medical resource allocation, patients may not get on-time admission after they are sent to a hospital Emergency Department. This study aimed to construct a forecasting system to predict the availability of ED and ICU beds.Materials and methodsA simulation-based forecasting system integrated with Google Maps is proposed to provide ED recommendations for ambulance dispatch. The web crawler technique continuously collects open data from the emergency information systems. An arrival transfer mechanism was proposed to convert the raw data into a simulation input. The results were then integrated with hospital assessment and routing distance in Google Maps to provide the most appropriate hospital ED to which a patient should be sent.ResultsThe results provided forecast accuracy for bed availability for the next 20, 40, and 60 min in 10 selected hospitals in central Taiwan. In most hospitals, the forecasting accuracy is high. For example, the ED and ICU bed availability accuracies in the next 20, 40, and 60 min are [100%, 88.7%], [100%, 90.5%], and [100%, 92.1%]. The two scenarios also showed hospital recommendations based on the available beds, routing distance, and eight-dimensional assessments.ConclusionPrevious EMS research usually did not consider future bed availability as the research target. This study predicted future bed availability and recommended the most appropriate hospitals for EMS dispatchers.

Introduction

In 2015, a fire disaster occurred at the Formosa Fun Coast Water Park in Taiwan, where nearly 500 people were injured and 15 died [1, 2]. The accident almost crashed the hospital emergency system because many urgent patient arrivals required a limited number of beds within a short time. Uncertainty in patient arrival time is a critical problem in hospital emergency department (ED) operations [3]. The number of patients using emergency medical services (EMS) through ambulance dispatch has increased by 36% between 2009 and 2020, and the number has been increasing annually. This may be due to the fact that there is free and convenient access to ambulance services covered by national healthcare insurance in Taiwan. However, the problem that is usually ignored is that it is at full capacity when patients are transported to the nearest hospital. They often do not know bed availability until an ambulance arrives [4]. Once resources, such as the number of medical staff and beds, cannot be allocated efficiently to process continuous incoming patients, an overcrowding problem will arise. This will eventually reduce the quality of care and increase medical costs, such as a longer length of stay (LOS) and admission process, lower patient satisfaction, higher risk of mortality, and disruptions of patient treatment [5–8].

The arrival of ED patients was based on the ambulance transport process. In Taiwan, the ownership types of ambulance services are mainly categorized as fire departments, private non-hospitals, and hospital-based. According to Article 5 of the Regulations for Emergency Medical Services, rescue personnel should transport emergency patients to nearby and appropriate medical care institutions [9]. Bed availability, utilization, and waiting time are essential for any ED configuration. Before rescue personnel make the dispatch decision, they can ask if any nearby hospital is available to take care of patients. However, these rescue personnel would not be able to obtain detailed information on the present status of the ED operations in each hospital. They must manually assign patients to hospital EDs based on their experience. When overcrowding occurs, ED patients have to receive either postponed hospitalization, turn away, or be referred to another hospital. Regardless of the decision, these patients might be at life-threatening risk of delaying appropriate treatment. This problem stems from a fundamental lack of predictive clarity about available resources once an ambulance arrives at the EDs.

Consequently, numerous studies have focused on predicting the daily flow of patients to the ED. For example, Fernandes et al. applied and compared three machine learning techniques: logistic regression, random forests, and a random undersampling boosting algorithm to predict patients with a higher risk of ICU admission [10]. King et al. predicted ED admissions in a UK teaching hospital’s ED using XGBoost classifiers [11]. Sterling et al. used natural language processing techniques and neural networks to predict ED patient disposition [12]. Lucini et al. predicted ED admissions using text mining and machine learning approaches [13]. Other studies have also investigated how analytical techniques are applied in ED patient-related research, such as predicting the hospitalization of ED patients [14], patient disposition decisions [15], and patient length of stay [16]. The ultimate goal of these studies was to maximize patient flow and effectively allocate medical resources. However, the capacities of these hospitals are constrained. To maximize patient flow or optimize medical resources, we still cannot ensure that an available bed can be assigned to the patient in the shortest time.

In 2013, the Ministry of Health and Welfare in Taiwan decided that leading hospitals are required to provide information about the number of patients waiting in the ED on the first page of the hospital website. Although some hospitals in Taiwan have taken steps since 2013 to move in the direction of providing more information to potential ED patients, their systems are still lacking. For example, in a hospital, the network allows personnel to determine how many patients are waiting for doctors, how many are waiting for beds in the ED and regular rooms, and how many are waiting in the ICU. However, there is no information regarding the number of available beds. Even regional hospital networks have moved closer to integrating their information, but once again the information only shows how many patients are waiting; there is nothing about the number of beds available. Moreover, this information is insufficient, even if there is data on the number of available beds. For example, a regional hospital in central Taiwan has shown the number of available beds. However, it does not predict the number of patients that can be taken when a patient arrives or if transfers from other hospitals will occur.

When sufficient numbers of beds in a hospital are unavailable, patients tend to wait for beds in trolleys in the ED, other admissions are turned away, and bed blockages occur. Clinically, bed blocking occurs when patients wait in one ward or hospital until free beds are available in a more suitable ward or hospital [17]. Based on the discussion above, previous studies have tpically focused on predicting ED patient admissions. However, the accurate prediction of ED admissions does not guarantee effective medical resource allocation. In addition, the structure and operation of healthcare systems differ significantly between countries. To fill this research gap, this study proposes an available bed forecasting system (ABFS) in the Taiwan Emergency Healthcare Network, which can be implemented to show available beds based on time frames. The ABFS predicts the conditions in various EDs to understand why they reject some patients and admit others, and the information gained will be used to connect all ERs using a cloud-based simulation system. This research contributes to making networked resources available to the cloud so that medical personnel, including ambulance drivers, can avail themselves of these critical data. An EMT in the field can instantly look at his/her tablet and see where the most likely hospital has immediate resources, including staffing, equipment, and beds, which can inform and suggest the most appropriate hospital EDs that obviously affect patient care and outcomes.

Materials and methods

Data collection

In 2015, the Ministry of Health and Welfare (Taiwan) built an emergency information system that collaborated with 48 national advanced emergency responsibility and children’s hospitals. In the system, the number of waiting patients for hospitalization, admission, trolleys, and intensive care beds is reported and updated from these hospitals every 20 min. In addition, each hospital must report whether it has reached its full capacity. In this study, a web crawler technique collects the server log every 20 min and downloads it to the local server from July 1, 2023 to July 9, 2023. Each hospital generates 597 entries in the system.

The framework of available bed forecasting system

The framework comprises three parts: data transfer, analysis, and storage.

Data transfer

Although the emergency information system shows each hospital’s waiting patients, the number of available beds remains unknown. For example, a hospital may report that the number of patients waiting for hospitalization is 20. This means that there is no available bed for incoming patients. However, once a hospital reports that the number of hospitalized patients is zero, there are two possible outcomes. The number of available beds is greater than the number of incoming patients or there are no incoming patients. Therefore, we proposed a method for transferring the log into arrival rate and waiting time. The system updates every 20 min, and the number of waiting patients may remain the same, increase, or decrease. Data analysis

A discrete-event system (DES) simulation model was used to construct the hospital networks, and the model was combined with Google Maps. DES is a methodology for modeling the operation of a system as a sequence of discrete events, each occurring at a specific time point and changing the state of the system. When an emergency event occurs, EM heads to the reported location and picks up a wounded person. The transport time from the accident location to each nearby hospital was calculated based on the traffic flow and the locations of the nearest hospitals. In EMS, the system will forecast if there are available beds in a particular hospital in a certain time interval in the future (e.g., 20, 40, and 60 min) for reference. A hospital assessment, including eight service quality-related dimensions, was considered. These dimensions are medical facilities, quality of medical staff, specialties and services offered, patient satisfaction, medical safety and quality control, service efficiency, cost-effectiveness, and emergency medical services. These dimensions are extracted from the annual reports of each hospital using text mining algorithms. Subsequently, the content was transformed and calculated based on the appearance frequency. Each dimension is then assigned a score between 1 and 10 based on its annual evaluation. Detailed descriptions of the dimensions are provided in Table 1. Hospital assessment dimensions

The system then evaluates the bed availability, routing distance, and hospital assessment. According to the forecast data, it recommends three hospitals for the priority of which hospital to arrive to avoid the situation of no bed availability after arriving at the hospital, effectively utilizing hospital beds, and reducing unnecessary waste of delivery time.

Data storage

The ABFS contains the server and client ends. The server end includes an available bed forecast server, which is constructed on Microsoft Windows Server 2008 R2, and a web server, the InfluxDB database, on Linux (https://influxdata.com/). InfluxDB is an open-source time series database. It is optimized for fast, high-availability storage and retrieval of time-series data in fields such as operations monitoring, Internet of Things sensor data, and real-time analytics. Because the number of hospital beds changes with time-series data, InfluxDB is applied to store the information on beds from the database in Open Data and the forecast of available hospital beds in the future, which are analyzed using the available bed forecast simulation server. The Client end covers the web interface and Android interface. Users can browse the forecasted available beds in hospitals in the future and journey time through browsers or handheld mobile devices.

Results

Prediction results

According to the current system prototype, we generated a simulation forecast of hospital bed availability and compared it to real historical data on bed availability. Specifically, we input the arrival rate and waiting time up to a certain period into the simulation system to forecast the bed availability for the next 20, 40, and 60 min. The predicted bed availability was then matched with the actual real-time status obtained from the emergency information system for future periods. If the forecasted availability matched the actual real-time status, then the accuracy was recorded as 100%. Otherwise, the value is recorded as 0%. This process was conducted on a rolling basis to calculate overall average accuracy. For example, the forecasting accuracies of the ED beds for Hospital 5 for the next 20, 40, and 60 min were all 100%. The forecasting accuracies of the ICU beds for Hospital 5 for the next 20, 40, and 60 min are 96.9%, 97.2%, and 98.6%, respectively (Fig. 2). In Fig. 2, most hospitals in the network present high accuracy, with a few appearing to have low accuracy and a few at zero (possibly because of different emergency processes, medical resource allocation, ER examinations, and referral strategies among hospitals). In the future, this system will attempt to seek accurate data and processes of hospitals and strategies of hospitals currently in the network for accurate forecasting. Discussions

When operating an EMS system, certain uncertainty factors contribute to the high degree of complexity. This uncertainty may be related to the frequency and geographical location of calls within the entire coverage area, the available capacity and precise location of rescue teams when scheduling decisions must be made, the availability of beds in destination hospitals where patients can be allocated, or the travel and processing times (dispatch time, preparation time, on-scene time, and off-loading time), which can vary significantly due to factors such as traffic congestion or patient condition [18].

An important task involved in the daily operation of an EMS is hospital selection when patients need to be dispatched to hospitals after medical treatment. The most straightforward rule for hospital selection is to transport patients to the closest hospital [19]. This policy is widely implemented in simulation models, given that all hospitals have the same capabilities and unlimited capacity and are open all the time. However, these conditions are often not adequately practiced because hospitals differ in their specialty and size [20]. For example, the closest hospital may not have any beds available, or the specialized personnel may not be on duty; some hospitals specialize in treating children while others do not; or emergency departments at some hospitals are not open 24 h daily. Therefore, hospital selection considers more facts into account rather than simply selecting the hospital closest to a call site.

If a predictive system can be implemented using simulation, this information can be made available on the cloud for all emergency personnel to take advantage of in the treatment of patients. All of this is needed because it is virtually impossible for EMS personnel and dispatchers at the hospital to make judgment calls without adequate data. When emergency departments become crowded, incoming ambulances are sometimes diverted to other hospitals in an attempt to mitigate crowding. In 2003, 45% of the United States EDs reported being “on diversion” at some point within the year [21]. Such diversions have been linked to several negative consequences, such as prolonged transport times, delays in care, increased mortality, higher medical costs, and lower hospital revenue [21–23].

Regarding Abreu et al.’s review, the demand for EMS has been tackled through various methodologies, such as regression, time series, and neural network models, to create prediction models that elucidate the seasonal fluctuations in demand [24]. Certain researchers employed historical data to estimate the empirical distribution, aiding in selecting the vicinity for the destination hospital [25]. Henderson & Mason utilized authentic call-hospital assignment data within a trace-driven simulation [26]. Silva & Pinto proposed a prearranged schedule for directing calls to destination hospitals based on the nature of the medical emergency [27]. However, these studies did not consider both the future bed availability and routing distances. The research contributed to adding value to EMS research.

The proposed simulation-based forecasting system can improve the efficiency and effectiveness of EMS operations with higher accuracy in the forecasting of bed availability for both the ED and ICU, further suggesting optimal hospital destinations. This could help mitigate some of the negative impacts associated with hospital diversions, such as prolonged transport times, delays in care, and increased mortality rates, by making data-driven recommendations of available beds in real-time to EMS dispatchers and routing distances. However, it is important to note that while the system is designed to optimize hospital selection, it may ironically contribute to the phenomenon of ambulance diversion. Inasmuch as this system might help in referring ambulances to hospitals with free capacity, there is a risk of uneven distribution of patients that over time might cause overload in some facilities. Thus, this system should be implemented concurrently with strategies that ensure equitable distribution of patients and follow-up of hospital capacity. Because the system requires real-time data and integration of technology, it requires strong infrastructure to support and maintain it. Future studies are needed to examine how such systems could affect hospital operations and patient outcomes in the longer term, ensuring that the benefits derived from better decision-making do not have negative consequences in terms of existing EMS logistical challenges.

While the proposed system offers a promising approach for predicting bed availability and prioritizing ambulance dispatch, it is important to acknowledge the need for real-time testing to ensure the system’s accuracy and reliability in practical applications. Real-time testing would allow for validation of the system’s performance under dynamic and unpredictable conditions, such as sudden surges in patient arrivals or unexpected changes in hospital operations. However, given the constraints of this study, real-time testing was not conducted due to resource and time limitations. Instead, the system was developed and validated using historical and simulated data to ensure its robustness in modeling future bed availability and providing hospital recommendations. While this approach lays a strong foundation for the system’s feasibility, future work should focus on implementing pilot programs in collaboration with EMS providers and hospitals to test the system under real-world conditions. Such testing would provide valuable insights into the system’s operational effectiveness, identify areas for improvement, and address potential challenges related to data latency, system integration, and user adoption. Despite the current limitations, this study demonstrates the potential of a simulation-based approach and highlights the importance of integrating real-time testing as a critical next step in the system’s development.

Conclusion

In consideration of the national severe emergency response hospital’s real-time message system not taking the referral into account, the open data of hospitals in the network display real-time data with diagrams for users to read conveniently. Furthermore, the emergency operation processes of hospitals in the network are constructed using the network emergency operation simulation prototype of ABFS with the simulation software in order to solve the changing number of beds caused by the referral and to understand the problem of the emergency department refusing patients. Finally, the ABFS proposed by our study simulates the system linking with the emergency departments of several hospitals so as to understand the emergency information of hospitals in the network and to forecast and display the information of available beds in the future at 20, 40, and 30 min. This would enhance the accuracy of the information when hospitals take “time” into account so that there are available beds for patients after their arrival. The resources could be planned more efficiently so that patients whose injuries exceed the resources or abilities of a hospital could be referred to other hospitals or medical centers as early as possible. The dynamic process concept in the system can rapidly respond to the changing number of available beds in hospitals in the network due to referrals. The system is expected to promote the service quality of the domestic emergency medical network.
