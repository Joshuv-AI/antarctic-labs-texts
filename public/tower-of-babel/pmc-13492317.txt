Leadless atrial pacemakers at the Bachmann bundle region: Early experience and procedural challenges

Introduction

Bachmann bundle (BB) is the dominant interatrial conduction pathway and plays an important role in maintaining atrial electrical synchrony.1 Pacing at the BB region has been shown to reduce left atrial (LA) activation delay and promote interatrial synchrony.2,3 It is therefore considered a physiological atrial pacing strategy. Observational studies have suggested that BB area pacing (BBaP) may reduce atrial fibrillation (AF) burden and lower the risk of developing AF.

Leadless pacemakers (LPs) avoid complications related to transvenous leads and generator pocket, offering advantages to certain patients who require permanent pacing. Targeting the BB region with an atrial LP could combine the benefits of physiological atrial pacing with the safety advantages of a leadless system. However, data regarding the feasibility and procedural challenges of this approach remain limited.

Here, we report our early experience of implanting atrial LPs at the BB region in 3 patients, highlighting the challenges during the procedure and the anatomical and device-related considerations.

Case reports

Case 1

A 50-year-old woman presented with fatigue and recurrent presyncope and was diagnosed with sinus node dysfunction (SND). Echocardiography revealed persistent left superior vena cava, with a coronary sinus ostium measuring approximately 2.3 cm, and no significant right atrial enlargement. As left-sided transvenous lead implantation can be technically challenging in the presence of persistent left superior vena cava and right-sided implantation could affect right-hand activity, a leadless atrial pacemaker (Aveir AR, Abbott Medical, Sylmar, CA) was chosen after shared decision making.

The procedure was performed via femoral venous access. A pigtail catheter was used for contrast injection to delineate the anatomical characteristics of the superior vena cava (SVC)–right atrium (RA) junction (Figure 1A). The delivery system was then advanced to the high RA. With gentle clockwise rotation and slight catheter deflection, the device was positioned against the superior interatrial septum at the SVC-RA junction, achieving stable contact with the target region. Device position was confirmed in both left anterior oblique and right anterior oblique projections (Figure 1B). Satisfactory intracardiac electrograms were obtained (Figure 1C), with pacing P-wave morphology meeting BBaP criteria.3 Pacing at 130 beats/min confirmed intact atrioventricular conduction. Postoperative computerized tomography confirmed that the device was implanted in the BB region (Figure 1F). At 3-month follow-up, the pacing parameters were stable, with a pacing threshold of 1.0 V/0.4 ms, an impedance of 320 Ω, and sensing of 3.1 mV.

Case 2

A 70-year-old woman was admitted for dizziness and reduced exercise tolerance. Further evaluation confirmed SND. As she was a dancer with specific activity requirements, she ultimately opted for an atrial LP.

Intraoperative angiography at the SVC-RA junction revealed a relatively small high RA with a steep, vertically oriented SVC-RA junction (Figure 2A). Consequently, the BB region at this junction lay nearly parallel to the long axis of the delivery system, which was advanced from the inferior vena cava. Although stable contact could be temporarily achieved by rotating and deflecting the delivery catheter (Figure 2B), the device repeatedly dislodged from the septal surface during screwing, despite maintained catheter support. After 3 unsuccessful attempts, implantation at the BB region was abandoned. Case 3

A 67-year-old woman presenting with symptomatic sinus bradycardia and sinus pauses was considered for permanent pacing. After discussion of conventional transvenous and leadless pacing options, she chose atrial LP to minimize lifestyle limitations.

Intraoperative imaging revealed mild cardiac rotation with a relatively low-lying RA and a superiorly positioned LA (Figure 3A). The delivery catheter was advanced from the inferior vena cava toward the BB region. With slight rotation and catheter deflection, stable septal positioning was achieved. High-rate atrial pacing at 130 beats/min confirmed 1:1 atrioventricular conduction and the morphology of the paced P wave fulfilled the BBaP criteria.3 With adequate catheter support and maintained forward tension, the device was then deployed and successfully screwed into the septum on the first attempt (Figure 3B). Discussion

In this series of 3 patients with SND, implantation of an atrial LP targeting the BB region was attempted. 2 patients achieved successful implantation, while 1 patient required implantation at the base of RAA because of repeated device dislodgment from the BB region during active fixation.

In current clinical practice, atrial LPs are most commonly implanted at the RAA base or RA lateral wall to ensure stable fixation and reliable pacing parameters. However, pacing at these sites results in delayed LA conduction and interatrial desynchrony, which can potentially lead to adverse clinical outcomes, including the occurrence of AF.4,5 Meanwhile, patients requiring atrial pacing predominantly present with SND for whom preserving physiological atrial activation may be particularly important. BBaP offers superior interatrial electrical and mechanical synchrony compared with RAA pacing (RAAP) and could also improve interatrial conduction delay.6 In cases 1 and 3, BBaP was associated with a shortened P-wave duration and normal P-wave axis, which is consistent with physiological interatrial conduction. In contrast, case 2 demonstrated a prolonged P-wave duration and altered P-wave axis after RAAP, reflecting interatrial conduction desynchrony. Therefore, using an atrial LP to achieve BBaP could be an effective pacing strategy that combines the advantages of leadless technology with the benefits of more physiological atrial activation. Future studies could incorporate intracardiac recordings via a coronary sinus catheter to assess LA conduction time, along with echocardiographic assessment of atrial electromechanical synchrony and transmitral E/A waves, to further characterize the physiological effects of BB region pacing.

Although clinical experience remains limited, isolated case reports from other centers have also described successful atrial leadless pacing targeted to the BB area.7,8 Together with our cases, these observations suggest that BBaP using an atrial LP is technically feasible and safe in selected patients.

Our early experience also revealed that successful BB-targeted implantation appears to be influenced by anatomical characteristics. The BB region is located on the superior interatrial septum near the SVC-RA junction. Compared with the trabeculated RAA area, the endocardium of the BB region is relatively smooth, limiting passive anchoring during device positioning. Consequently, successful fixation at this site requires stable and sustained contact between the delivery system and the septum. Without adequate support, the device may dislodge from the endocardial surface during screwing. This challenge is particularly evident when the target area is oriented nearly parallel to the delivery system, as observed in case 2. In addition, the relatively small high RA further restricted the maneuverability of the delivery system and increased the technical difficulty.

Conversely, in case 1, the SVC-RA junction exhibited a slight leftward inclination, which enabled the delivery system to achieve stable septal contact and adequate axial support during helix deployment. In case 3, mild cardiac rotation positioned the RA relatively inferior to the LA, creating a more favorable catheter-septal angle and adequate support for active fixation. However, this represents only our early experience, and more cases are needed to observe the influence of anatomical characteristics on the implantation procedure of LPs targeting the BB region. Preprocedural cardiac computed tomography may provide additional value by enabling detailed assessment of septal orientation and related anatomical features, thereby facilitating procedural planning and patient selection for BB-targeted implantation.

Moreover, the outer helix of Aveir AR is primarily intended for mechanical anchoring. It is designed with a proximally beveled tip to minimize the risk of atrial perforation, given the relatively thin walls of the RAA and lateral atrium. However, the thicker superior interatrial septum allows for alternative fixation strategies. Further refinements in helix configuration and delivery system design could better compensate for the limited passive anchoring on the smooth surface of this site, thereby enhancing the success rate of BB-targeted implantation.

Conclusion

This case series reports our initial experience of atrial LPs implantation at the BB area. BB-targeted atrial leadless pacing appears to be feasible and safe, offering superior atrial synchrony compared with RAAP. Our preliminary observations suggest that successful implantation is highly dependent on anatomical characteristics and stable catheter-septal contact because of the smooth endocardium of the superior interatrial septum and the specific helix design of Aveir AR. Further device refinements and prospective studies with larger cohorts are warranted.
