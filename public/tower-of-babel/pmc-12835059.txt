Groundwater quality and health risk assessment in the Baddi-Barotiwala-Nalagarh industrial belt of the northwestern Himalayas
Authors: Ashima Awasthi, Sakshi Rana, Astha Thakur, Saneel K. Thakur, Deepshikha Jaswal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Groundwater is the primary source of drinking water in the Baddi–Barotiwala–Nalagarh (BBN) industrial belt of the northwestern Himalayas, where rapid pharmaceutical and manufacturing growth has intensified concerns over water quality and public health. This study highlighted the groundwater suitability for drinking purpose, with a strong focus on evaluating associated human health risks from fluoride and arsenic contamination. A total of 50 groundwater samples were analyzed for 13 physicochemical parameters with several (TH, Ca2+, Mg2+, Na+, Cl−, F−, K+, HCO3− and As) exceeding permissible limits. Drinking water quality index assessment revealed that most of the groundwater samples fell into “poor” category, indicating groundwater unsuitability for human consumption. Lifetime Cancer Risk (LTCR) from arsenic exceeded the acceptable benchmark in 92% samples for children, 88% for females and 86% for males, whereas fluoride revealed elevated Hazard Quotient (HQ) values in 12% of samples with males and children and 2% with females highlighting the greater vulnerability of younger population. Strict effluent control and sustainable groundwater monitoring are crucial to ensure safe water supply and protect human health in local communities of the northwestern Himalayas. Beyond local relevance, the outcomes contribute valuable insights for sustainable groundwater governance in rapidly industrializing regions of the Himalayas and support progress towards Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) 3 and 6.

Introduction

Groundwater is an indispensable freshwater resource that serves an important role in sustaining human life and supporting socioeconomic and ecological systems. Over 1.5 billion people across the globe depend on groundwater for their livelihood as well domestic needs, making it a vital resource for human sustenance1. As the largest accessible reservoir of freshwater, it meets the needs of approximately 66% of the global population for household and other purposes across both rural and urban areas2 and accounts for around 97% of the global freshwater supply2. In twenty-first century, the degradation of water quality has emerged as a critical global challenge3. Due to overexploitation of surface water the arid and semi-arid regions like China, India, Pakistan and Bangladesh primarily depend on the groundwater resources4.

In water stressed nations like India, groundwater is considered a lifeline, particularly in regions with limited surface water availability. Approximately 50% of urban residents and 80% of rural population in India depends on groundwater for household purpose5. India is recognized as the world leading consumer of groundwater with yearly extraction of approximately 251 cubic kilometers6. It serves as a critical resource across agricultural, municipal and industrial sectors, with agriculture alone accounting for approximately 89% of groundwater usage, while the domestic sector accounts for 9% followed by industrial sector which is 2%7. According to 2023 UNICEF report, over 892 million people still lack access to proper sanitation and unsafe water caused the death of more than 1,300 young children every day8.

However, this intense dependence on groundwater has raised a serious environmental and public health concerns, particularly due to increasing level of contamination. Both natural and anthropogenic activities contribute to the contamination of groundwater quality. Natural contamination occurs due to geological formations, weathering process however anthropogenic contamination arises due to urbanization, agricultural practices, untreated wastewater, open dumping and industrialization. This rapid urbanization, agricultural practices, industrialization and overpopulation have collectively increased the water demand significantly escalating the stress on groundwater resources resulting in the degradation of water quality.

In India intensified anthropogenic activities have led to rising environmental pollution significantly affecting the quality of groundwater. Major contributors to this deterioration include effluents from industrial facilities, discharge from sewage treatment plants and pollutants from different sources9. These sources release harmful pollutants such as heavy metals, nitrates and pathogens which pose risk to the human health and threaten the environmental sustainability10. Among various pollutants present in groundwater, nitrate and fluoride are common and are known to cause severe harm to human body as well as environment. Humans primarily ingest fluoride through drinking water. Fluoride contamination in Indian groundwater poses a serious health risk to humans, affecting around 66 million people with dental and skeletal fluorosis. Prolonged exposure to excessive fluoride may damage vital organs, including the kidneys, brain and heart. Arsenic contamination in the aquifers can result from both natural weathering of minerals and industrial activities which promotes its mobilization into the groundwater resources further increasing the risk of chronic health effects. Groundwater contamination has been recognised as a major driver of waterborne diseases, contributing to worldwide 3.4 million people deaths annually11. In numerous regions of Asian, African and India, millions of people die every year from water related diseases12. To estimate health risks from groundwater usage, especially exposure to fluoride, a health risk assessment is essential. As India has surpassed other nations to become the most populous country, unsustainable water consumption, poor water resources management and inadequate land use practices have led to the rapid depletion of aquifers and deterioration of shallow aquifer quality13. This scenario necessitates comprehensive assessment of groundwater quality, particularly in the regions experiencing urban expansion as well rapid industrialisation. To link the national context with site-specific challenges, it is important to examine areas where India’s broader groundwater stress converges with dense industrial activity.

Baddi-Barotiwala-Nalagarh (BBN) industrial corridor in Solan district of Himachal Pradesh is one such region of critical concern. In South Asia, the BBN region has emerged as a major pharmaceutical hub, producing approximately 35% of the area’s total pharmaceutical output14. Majorly 12 different types of industries, including pharmaceuticals, plastic, food, textile, chemicals and automobiles are present in BBN belt15. The rapid pace of industrialisation combined with urban expansion and agricultural runoff poses a serious threat to the region’s groundwater quality. While individual studies have analysed groundwater quality in Baddi and Nalagarh separately, there remains a noticeable lack of integrated scientific research addressing groundwater contamination across the entire area. This fragmented research limits the ability to fully understand and address the groundwater contamination challenges affecting the region. Therefore, a holistic assessment of groundwater status in BBN region is crucial to verify the availability of clean and potable water and to protect community health. Additionally, such an assessment is essential for maintaining ecological balance and formulate effective policies for sustainable industrial development. This study addresses critical research gaps by systematically evaluating groundwater quality in the BBN area through following key objectives: (a) determining the spatial variation of groundwater quality parameters using GIS based techniques (b) to assess the suitability of groundwater for drinking purposes (c) to apply a health risk assessment model to determine the non-carcinogenic health risk associated with fluoride exposure and carcinogenic risks from arsenic through drinking water.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive study in the BBN industrial belt that integrates groundwater quality assessment for drinking purpose with a detailed evaluation of human health risks.

Materials and methods

Study area

Baddi-Barotiwala-Nalagarh (BBN) is an industrial zone located in Solan district, spreads over 380 km2 in the northwestern Himalayan region14. The BBN area geographically lies between 30°40′53′′ and 31°22′01′′ northern latitudes and 76°36′ 10′′ and 77°15′14′′ eastern longitudes (Fig. 1). The altitude varies from 300 to 3000 m above mean sea level (msl) in the mountains of Shivalik ranges and outer Himalayas16. The study area is enclosed by Bilaspur district in north-west and Mandi district in the north, Sirmaur and Shimla districts in south-east and east. It also shares borders in the south and west with State of Haryana and Punjab. The region experiences an annual mean temperature ranging from 00Cto 45 °C and receives an average annual rainfall of around 1200 mm. The perennial Sirsa River flows in the north-westerly direction and serves as the major surface water source in the region. It eventually merges with the Satluj River at Ropar, Punjab.

Water sampling and analysis

The 50 groundwater samples were collected from different sampling locations which covers industrial regions of Baddi, Barotiwala and Nalagarh (BBN) in the Solan district. These samples were collected in pre-monsoon period (April, 2025). For water sample collection, clean high-density polyethylene (HDPE) bottles were used. These sampling bottles were prewashed with 10% nitric acid and subsequently rinsed thoroughly with distilled water. At each sampling station, bottles were again rinsed two to three time with sampling water to minimize the contamination risk. To ensure the accuracy of samples, water was allowed to flow for 10 min from borewell, tubewell, handpumps before collection. Most of these groundwater sources were located in agricultural fields and residential areas and were commonly utilized for irrigation and drinking purpose. The coordinates and elevation of all sampling sites were recorded with a GPS device. The sampling locations were subsequently georeferenced and mapped using ArcGIS software (10.4.1 version). All spatial distribution maps (Figs. 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 7) were generated using ArcGIS software (10.4.1 version), employing the Inverse Distance Weighted (IDW) interpolation technique. Field measurement of pH, TDS and EC were performed directly after sampling using Hanna (HI 98194) multiparameter portable water analysis device. The chosen anions such as F−, Cl−, SO42−, HCO3− and cations such as Ca+, K+, Na+, Mg2+ were measured using the standard procedure outlined by American Public Health Association17 (APHA). EDTA titration technique was employed to estimate the concentration of magnesium, calcium and total hardness (TH) whereas sodium and potassium concentrations were estimated through flame photometric technique. For detection of phosphate, sulphate and fluoride spectrophotometric method were used. Titration method was used to estimate the bicarbonate ions whereas chloride was measured by using the argentometric method. Arsenic was identified using microwave plasma-atomic emission spectroscopy (MP-AES). Statistical analysis was performed using Microsoft Excel 2010 and IBM SPSS Statistics software. To ensure the accuracy ionic concentration of data, charge balance error (CBE) was computed for each sampled groundwater. All samples showed CBE values within the acceptable limit of ± 5%, indicating good analytical accuracy. Drinking water quality index (DWQI) calculation

The suitability of water resources for human consumption is assessed by using the Drinking Water Quality Index (DWQI). DWQI is one of the active tools for the monitoring of groundwater pollution and its quality. This index assists in the evaluation of the groundwater quality by integrating the several parameters into one index. In DWQI the assessment of groundwater quality is done by considering multiple chemical parameters each allotted with different weightings, to provide a complete assessment of a specific water sample18. The hydrochemical characteristics of groundwater were assessed to determine its suitability for drinking purpose by analyzing key parameters and comparing them with existing water quality standards given by BIS19 and WHO20. Over the past decades, many researchers have highlighted the DWQI technique to describe and estimate the groundwater quality.

In the first step a total 10 groundwater quality parameter (pH, TDS, Cl−, HCO3−, Ca2+, Mg2+, TH, K+, Na+ and F−) were given weight (wi) ranging from 1 to 5 based on their importance in influencing the quality of groundwater. Correlation analysis

Pearson’s correlation is widely used statistical technique for determining the linear relationship between two continuous variables and for figuring out how one parameter changes or moves in relation to another. The link between two or more variables is represented by this bi-variate analytical metric. It is widely used approach to assess the association among different parameters without implying any causal link to pollution22. A correlation value above than 0.75 shows a strong relationship, while values between 0.5 and 0.75 show moderate and values below 0.5 represents weak correlation between variables.

Human health risk assessment (HHRA)

Non-carcinogenic health risk assessment (Fluoride)

In recent decade, the human health problems have been increasing at alarming rate due to various factors such as air pollution, consumption of contaminated water and increasing global temperature. Among these various challenges the quality of drinking water has been a rising concern particularly in developing countries like India where groundwater is contaminated due to elevated concentration of parameters such as fluoride. Fluoride concentration in groundwater has been identified in over hundred countries extremely affecting regions of Africa (Eygpt, Ethopia and Kenya), Asia (India, China, Pakistan) and Europe (Poland, Germany, Denmark) respectively23.This widespread contamination poses major health risks such as dental and skeleton fluorosis affecting the human population worldwide. The non-carcinogenic health risk was determined by evaluating the concentration of fluoride on health of local communities in the BBN area. In groundwater fluoride is mainly derived from geogenic sources particularly fluorite bearing minerals which progressively dissolve and release fluoride into aquifer systems.

According to US Environmental Protection Agency (USEPA)24 there are mainly three exposure routes including ingestion, inhalation and dermal absorption. Thus, the conduction of Health Risk Assessment (HRA) is essential for the improvement of environmental management25. Due to its comprehensive and systematic approach, the model plays important role to evaluate the long-term health effect of exposure26.

The risk estimation related to water having elevated level of fluoride was assessed, following the guidelines USEPA27. For the determination of non-carcinogenic risk assessment associated with fluoride chronic daily intake (CDI) and Hazard Quotient (HQ) was used. Carcinogenic health risk assessment (Arsenic)

Arsenic is highly toxic trace elements present in groundwater and well-known carcinogen posing severe risks to human health. Long term exposure to arsenic can lead to wide range of adverse health outcomes including dermatological effects and disorder of cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, endocrine as well as reproductive system29. In present study the determination of arsenic element was conducted to understand the potential carcinogenic health risk in BBN area. Results and discussions

Physicochemical characteristics of groundwater

The quantitative values of all examined physicochemical parameters along with WHO20 and BIS19 standards limits for groundwater samples are given in Tables 4 and 5. The observed pH value of BBN area varied from 6.06 to 7.65 with a mean of 6.74. The pH was found to be acidic in southern belt and eastern edge with few patches in the north-central part while the alkaline condition was present in northwest and central western regions of the study area (Fig. 2a). The measured EC values of the groundwater samples showed a variation between 221 µS/cm and 1700 µS/cm, with an average value of 986.18 µS/cm. A total 10% of groundwater samples surpassed the permissible limits of 1500 µS/cm as per WHO20 indicating the higher concentration of dissolved solids due to anthropogenic activities in the study area. Geospatial analysis revealed that EC showed higher concentration in southwest, northeast and southeast pockets in the BBN area (Fig. 2b).

The TDS level in BBN area ranged from 240 mg/L to 851 mg/L with an average of 494.64 mg/L. The analysis revealed every groundwater samples were found well within the permissible limit of 2000 mg/L as per BIS19 indicating their suitability for drinking purposes. In this study the TH values of the collected groundwater samples ranged from 270.57 mg/L to 1404.84 mg/L with an average value of 748.91 mg/L. Approximately 68% of the groundwater sample values surpassed the threshold limit of 600 mg/L as prescribed by BIS19 suggesting that majority of the groundwater samples evaluated in the BBN area were unfit for human consumption. According to spatial distribution map central and southern belt showed the higher concentration of TH in the study area (Fig. 2c). The findings are consistent with the results of previous research, which reported excessive hardness in the BBN groundwater33. In addition to this, previous study from southern India also documented higher TH in groundwater34.

The major cations concentration like Ca2+ in the study area varied between 51.30 mg/L to 238.07 mg/L with a mean value of 121.28 mg/L significantly higher than the permissible limit. A total of 2% of the groundwater sample values exceeded this permissible limit of 200 mg/L as prescribed by BIS19. The elevated calcium concentration observed in the BBN area might have been result of geochemical processes primarily due to weathering of minerals like calcite which released calcium into the aquifer system of BBN region. While Mg2+ concentration in groundwater samples ranged between 14.60 mg/L to 220.60 mg/L with a mean value of 108.31 mg/L. A total of 52% samples of groundwater were found to have values higher than the allowable threshold of 100 mg/L as recommended by BIS19. The elevated concentration of magnesium intake can cause cathartic effect severely affecting the human body. In accordance to geospatial map of Ca2+ higher concentration was found in the southwestern region (Fig. 2d) meanwhile southern and northern parts of the BBN region showed high magnesium levels (Fig. 2e) that might have been influenced by the industrial effluents released from pharmaceutical, fertilizers and textile industries present in study area. The measured Na+ concentration extended from 18 mg/L to 360 mg/L with an average of 128.68 mg/L. However, 22% samples value exceeded the permissible limit of 200 mg/L in the study area suggesting the presence of silicate minerals. The mapping of spatial variability of sodium highlighted the higher concentration in southwest, northwest and south east pockets of present study area (Fig. 2f). The K+ levels in the BBN area varied from 0.20 mg/L to 14.80 mg/L with an average value of 5.58 mg/L suggesting that 2% samples for K+ exceeded the standards limits of 12 mg/L as per WHO20 guidelines. The presence of these ions suggests active rock-water interaction contributing minerals dissolution and subsequent release of ion into aquifers.

The major anions such as chloride showed variation between 124.40 mg/L to 1628.80 mg/L having a mean concentration of 618.03 mg/L. A total of 22% collected samples value were found to be above the tolerable limit of 1000 mg/L as per BIS19. This higher chloride concentration may induce laxative effects deteriorating human health35. Earlier findings also reported high chloride level in region’s aquifer, aligning with our observations9. The HCO3− concentration in water samples of the BBN area ranged from 50 mg/L to 680 mg/L having a mean value of 261.80 mg/L. Total 2% of the groundwater samples in study area were found beyond the recommended value of 500 mg/L as per WHO20. The sulphate in groundwater samples in BBN area varied from 0.38 mg/L to 2.86 mg/L with an average value of 1.48 mg/L where all groundwater samples were found to be well within the permissible limits as per WHO20. Based on spatial distribution map of chloride the higher concentration was found in southern and some central parts (Fig. 3a) of the study area meanwhile elevated bicarbonates was shown in central and some northern parts of BBN area (Fig. 3b).

In addition to the analysis of physicochemical parameters, arsenic was analysed to assess its potential health effects. The statistical results revealed that arsenic in BBN area varied from 0 mg/L to 0.09 mg/L having mean value of 0.03 mg/L. The results showed that total of 30% samples in groundwater were found to be above the permissible limit of 0.05 mg/L set by BIS19. The observed elevated concentration of arsenic might be due to natural weathering of minerals along with anthropogenic inputs arising from improper waste dumping in the study area. Exposure to this high concentration of arsenic can cause several health effects including cancer, cardiovascular and liver damage, respiratory disorder, skin lesions and diabetes38. The spatial distribution map in Fig. 4 illustrated that southern and central region of BBN area was the most critical hotspot with higher concentration of arsenic suggesting immediate mitigation measures. Similarly, several studies from Uttar Pradesh39 and aquifers of Gangetic Basin40 reported higher concentration of arsenic in groundwater, which underscored the importance of regular monitoring of groundwater.

Human health risk assessment (HHRA)

Non-carcinogenic health risk assessment

The Hazard Quotient (HQ) values were determined to examine the non-carcinogenic health risk for males, females and children resulting from consumption of groundwater containing fluoride in BBN area. The calculated results revealed that the HQ value for males varied from 0.002 to 1.846 with an average of 0.352. In female the HQ ranged between 0.001 and 1.636 with a mean value of 0.312. And the HQ value for children varied from 0.002 to 2 with an average of 0.382 as shown in Table 7. It was found that HQ values exceeded in 12% of samples for males, 2% for females, and 12% for children indicating a potential risk of fluoride contamination in groundwater of the BBN area. This elevated concentration of fluoride causing health risk in the study area might be due to anthropogenic activities like industrialization as well as geogenic sources such as weathering of fluorite bearing minerals. In accordance to spatial distribution map it was observed that northern and north-eastern parts of the BBN area were the fluoride hotspot where HQ values exceeded 1 indicating health risk particularly for males and children. However, females showed relatively lower HQ and the southern part of the study area remained safe (Fig. 7). Previous studies also highlighted the presence of fluoride in groundwater in South west China due to the presence of different industries present in that area41. Elevated fluoride levels were similarly observed in Indo-Bangladesh groundwater system reflecting the severity of water contamination42.

Conclusion

This research showed that groundwater in the BBN industrial belt of north-western Himalaya is under serious stress. The Drinking Water Quality Index (DWQI) showed that 78% of the samples were unsuitable for drinking purpose, reflecting widespread deterioration in water quality caused by excessive salts, high hardness, fluoride and arsenic. Such poor DWQI results indicate a high likelihood of adverse health outcomes in the exposed population.Further, health risk assessment revealed significant non-carcinogenic risk from fluoride to children and men, raising concerns of dental and skeletal fluorosis, as well as severe carcinogenic risk from arsenic to all age groups, particularly in children. These findings highlight an urgent need for stricter compliance with pollution control board, artificial recharge and rainwater harvesting, adoption of salt tolerant crops, regulatory intervention, continuous monitoring of water quality and promotion of community level defluorination techniques.Appropriate mitigation measures such as water treatment technologies like electrocoagulation to remove industrial contaminants, source switching to safer aquifers and stricter regulation of industrial discharge should be adopted in the study area. Active steps to restore groundwater health and ensure sustainable groundwater usage are essential in this ecologically fragile Himalayan region.By ensuring all industrial units are connected to functioning effluent treatment plants can reduce the groundwater contamination in study area.The outcomes support policy frameworks aimed at achieving SDG 6 (clean water and sanitation) and SDG 3 (good health and well-being) through sustainable groundwater governance.The DWQI and health risk assessments involve assumptions about exposure rates, parameter weights, and reference values, which introduce uncertainty into the results. These methods also do not fully account for seasonal variations or local behavioural differences. Future studies should incorporate uncertainty analysis or probabilistic modelling to better capture these variations. Future research should also integrate isotope based hydrogeochemical tracing to precisely identify contamination sources.
