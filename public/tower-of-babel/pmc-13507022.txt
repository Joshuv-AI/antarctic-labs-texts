Green synthesis of Cu0.5Ni0.5Fe2O4 nanoferrites using honey for enhanced photocatalytic crystal violet dye degradation and antibacterial activity
Authors: S G Divakara, B Mahesh, B K Jayanna, Venkata Krishna Bayineni, G S Nanjundaswamy, Ketankumar A Ganure
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeveloping eco-friendly magnetic photocatalysts through sustainable synthesis routes is a key strategy for extending green technologies aimed at obtaining clean water. Their distinctive magnetic, surface, and catalytic properties have contributed to this interest. Herein, a renewable honey-mediated green synthesis strategy is developed to fabricate Cu0.5Ni0.5Fe2O4 mixed spinel nanoferrites (CNF) by following the key principles of green chemistry, which involve the use of bio-based reagents, aqueous media, and reduced chemical waste. Characterization by XRD, SEM/EDS, TEM, UV–Vis, and FTIR techniques validates the formation of Cu0.5Ni0.5Fe2O4 heterostructure with an average crystallite size of ~110 nm. As-prepared CNF photocatalyst exhibits superior UV-light-driven photocatalytic activity for the degradation of Crystal Violet (CV) and Alizarin Red S, showing 91.3% removal of CV in 120 min at low catalyst dosage. The enhanced activity is attributed to the synergistic interactions between Cu–Ni ferrites, facilitating effective charge separation and the generation of reactive oxygen species for dye mineralization. Moreover, CNF manifests significant antibacterial activity, especially against Gram-positive bacteria, which further supports the material’s multifunctionality in water remediation. Consequently, the current work presents a sustainable, inexpensive, and scalable method to produce multifunctional ferrite photocatalysts suitable for environmentally safe wastewater treatment and antimicrobial applications.Graphical abstract Keywords: Cu0.5Ni0.5Fe2O4, Honey-mediated synthesis, Heterostructure photocatalyst, Reactive oxygen species, Antibacterial applications

Introduction

Environmental pollution, with water pollution as a principal component, has become a widespread concern due to the rapid industrial and urban development [1]. Among various types of pollutants, synthetic dyes are of special concern because they constitute one of the largest classes of organic pollutants discharged into water bodies. These dyes are widely employed in the textile, cosmetic, pharmaceutical, and food-processing industries. During manufacturing and handling processes, about 12% of dyes such as methyl orange, methylene blue, and Congo red, are lost, while nearly 20% of global dye production is released directly into aquatic systems without adequate treatment [2]. Synthetic dyes are generally toxic, carcinogenic, and mutagenic, causing serious hazards to aquatic life and human health [2]. The unchecked discharge of these dyes further impacts photosynthesis in water bodies by preventing sunlight from penetrating the water, thus reducing biodiversity and deteriorating water quality [3].

To overcome these environmental issues, photocatalysis has gained prominence as a sustainable and efficient method for degrading harmful organic pollutants in water using light-driven reactions. Owing to its economic viability, high efficiency, and reduced secondary pollution, visible-light-driven photocatalysis has emerged as a promising strategy. However, the application of photocatalysis is limited due to challenges such as wide band gaps, inferior absorption of visible light, and high recombination rates of electron–hole pairs generated upon light irradiation [4]. The conventional water treatment processes, such as adsorption, biological treatment, membrane filtration, and chemical oxidation, have been widely accepted; however, each process has its own drawbacks. For instance, biological treatment results in the generation of secondary pollutants, while adsorption and filtration processes often lack long-term efficiency. Therefore, it is imperative to develop highly active, reusable, and eco-friendly photocatalysts for wastewater treatment [5].

The outstanding properties of newly designed materials synthesized by the unique combination of inorganic compounds are of vital significance. By integrating two or more simple constituents, such as metal oxides, sulfides, and ferrites, it is possible to design multi-dimensional materials that exhibit physicochemical properties that are absent in the individual components. These materials attract considerable attention due to their properties, including high surface area, reactivity, and tunable electronic behavior. Among them, ferrite nanoparticles (FNPs) have received remarkable attention over the last two decades and have emerged as promising materials for applications in photocatalysis, magnetic storage, biomedical imaging, drug delivery, wastewater treatment, and energy conversion.

Catalysis is one of the most prominent applications of such mixed systems, where precise control over the composition can result in the creation of highly efficient, stable, and selective photocatalysts, thus providing solutions to some of the most pressing global challenges. Mixed spinel ferrites have drawn significant attention for their versatile applications in pollutant degradation, hydrogen evolution, organic synthesis, and biomedical science [6]. However, the conventional method of synthesizing such mixed ferrite nanomaterials involves the use of toxic solvents, high temperatures, and expensive equipment, thus making the entire process environmentally and economically impracticable. Thus, there is a growing need to shift toward green, inexpensive, and sustainable synthesis methods. These approaches utilize natural and biodegradable resources, making them environmentally benign while maintaining high photocatalytic efficiency.

Spinel ferrites, such as NiFe2O4, CuFe2O4, and Ni–Cu various nanoferrites, are mostly recognized for their magnetic and electronic properties. Owing to their appropriate band gaps (~ 2 eV), these formulations exemplify significant photocatalytic performance under visible-light irradiation [7]. These materials have been engaged for the degradation of dyes, the removal of pharmaceuticals, and toxic metals from wastewater. One of the primary benefits of spinel ferrites is their magnetic separability, which facilitates effortless recovery and reuse through the application of an external magnetic field, enhancing their practicality for real-world applications [8]. Although these materials have several advantages, established synthesis strategies for spinel ferrites are often conjoined with impediments such as long processing times, non-uniform particle distribution, and the necessity for strict pH and temperature control. To address these limitations, green synthesis procedures have received notable attention. These methods focus on sustainability by employing natural, renewable materials and avoiding perilous reagents [9]. Recent research has advocated the thriving synthesis of ferrites using plant extracts, microorganisms, and waste biomass as templates or reducing agents [10].

Alizarin Red S (ARS), an anionic dye, and Crystal Violet (CV), a cationic dye, are widely utilized in biological, industrial, and medical applications. Crystal Violet, being non-biodegradable, persists in aquatic environments for extended periods and exhibits greater toxicity compared to anionic dyes [11]. Its complex aromatic structure and exceptional physicochemical, chemical, photochemical, and thermal stability underpin its widespread use as a coloring agent in textiles, paper products, cosmetics, detergents, and leather goods. In medical diagnostics, CV is a necessary histological dye, most notably used in Gram staining. The discharge of these dyes into aquatic systems introduces considerable perils to both environmental and human health. Despite their versatility, both CV and ARS are pertinent to toxicological and ecological concerns, with CV recognized as a carcinogen capable of inducing chromosomal modifications. The introduction of CV and ARS into water bodies endangers aquatic organisms, stressing the urgent need for effective and sustainable remediation strategies.

Recent studies have noticed the significance of ferrite and semiconductor nanocomposites in degrading pollutants [12]. For example, microemulsion-synthesized LaFeO₃/NiFe2O4 nanocomposites demonstrated 92% degradation efficiency attributable to improved charge transfer and diminished electron–hole recombination [13]. Green-fabricated Cu–Ni NPs exhibited substantial photocatalytic and antimicrobial effects [14]. In contrast, NiFe2O4/g-C₃N4 nanocomposites debased 65% of crystal violet in 180 min [15], whereas Cu2⁺-doped NiFe2O4–rGO nanocomposites showed enhanced activity due to higher surface area and conductivity. [16]. Other hybrids like NiFe2O4–ZnWO4 [17], CuNiFe2O4–MWCNTs [18], and Cu/Ni/rGO [19] have also displayed outstanding pollutant degradation efficiencies, highlighting the essence of developing ferrite-based nanohybrids for environmental remediation.

The presence of high sugar concentration, hydrogen peroxide production, low pH, proteins, phenolic compounds, or other active ingredients in honey drives a potential candidate for the synthesis of NPs [20]. Honey, being a natural and versatile compound, also functions as a reducing, stabilizing, and capping agent. Honey is primarily a carbohydrate-rich substance, with glucose and fructose contributing almost 80–85% of its composition. Additionally, sugars also contain a wide range of biologically active compounds, including organic acids and their salts, amino acids, proteins, minerals, vitamins, hormones, enzymes, essential oils, phenolic acids, and flavonoids. Honey, known for its antimicrobial, chemical, and medicinal effects, has been employed for the synthesis of gold, silver, platinum, palladium, ferrites, and metal oxide NPs [21]. Due to its inherent physicochemical characteristics, honey is utilized as a stabilizing agent for NP synthesis and inhibits agglomeration. Furthermore, the suggested synthesis route is environmentally sustainable, functions under ambient conditions, and prevents the formation of toxic byproducts.

This study aims to explore the eco-friendly synthesis of Cu0.5Ni0.5Fe2O4 mixed ferrites using honey as a green catalyst. The synergistic integration of the magnetic and photocatalytic functionalities of CuFe2O4 and NiFe2O4 is envisioned to significantly ameliorate charge carrier separation, broaden light absorption, and improve surface catalytic activities in the synthesized mixed ferrite. Herein, we explore the development and characterization of an effective mixed nanocatalyst capable of simultaneously addressing environmental and biological problems by facilitating the photocatalytic degradation of toxic organic pollutants and displaying antimicrobial properties. The photocatalytic activity was determined using crystal violet and alizarin red S as model reference dyes under UV light, and the maximum degradation efficiency was found for crystal violet (91.3%). The critical operational factors, such as (i) contact time, (ii) catalyst concentration, (iii) solution pH, and (iv) adsorption kinetics, were examined. The reusability of the material is also explored to determine its potential for sustainable water treatment applications. The prepared ferrites exhibited appreciable antibacterial activity, particularly against Gram-positive bacteria, highlighting their multifunctional potential for wastewater remediation. This research enables the advancement of green nanotechnology by equipping a scalable, cost-effective, and sustainable approach to the synthesis of high-performance photocatalysts. The use of honey as a bio template is environmentally benign and enables the controlled design of nanoparticles (NPs), in accord with the principles of sustainable development.

Materials and experimental procedure

Materials

All the chemicals used in this study were of analytical grade purity and were used without further purification. Copper (ii) nitrate hexahydrate (Cu(NO3)2·6H2O), iron(iii) nitrate nonahydrate (Fe(NO3)3·9H2O), nickel(ii) nitrate hexaahydrate (Ni(NO3)2·6H2O) and ethanol were all purchased from SRL Chemicals, India. The honey used in the study was procured directly from a beekeeper in the wild forests of Karnataka, India, and was used without further purification. The organic dyes Crystal violet (CV) & Alizarin Red S (ARS) (S. D. Fine-Chem. Ltd., India) were also used without further purification. Deionized water was used throughout the study.

Honey-mediated synthesis of CuFe2O4, NiFe2O4 and ferrite mixture (Cu0.5Ni0.5Fe2O4)

Cu0.5Ni0.5Fe2O4 composite (CNF) was synthesized by a simple sol–gel process followed by heat treatment. Briefly: Stoichiometric amounts of Fe(NO3)3.9H2O), Ni(NO3)2·6H2O and Cu(NO3)2·6H2O in the ratio of (2:1:1) were dissolved in 100 mL of distilled water and stirred for 30 min to produce a homogenous solution at 90 °C. To the hot precursor metal salt solution, 30 mL of honey solution was added dropwise and heated at 90 °C for 60 min under continuous stirring. The resultant solution was then heated to boiling, which led to the decomposition of monosaccharides in the honey. During this, the solution turned into a sol and then a gel. The, resultant suspension was then heated inside a muffle furnace maintained at 500 ℃ for 3 h. This combustion process produced a porous, voluminous powder by releasing N2, CO2, and H2O gases. The resulting nanomaterials were crushed using a mortar and pestle, and then washed with deionized water, and finally dried at 60 °C. The dried powder was then calcined in a muffle furnace for 2 h at 500 °C to improve crystallinity and cation homogeneity in the spinel structure. The calcined material was then ground in an agate mortar to obtain a fine powder. A similar procedure was followed to prepare nickel ferrite (NFO) and copper ferrite (CFO) with their respective metal salts and iron nitrate (Fig. 1).

Characterization

X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were conducted using a XRD Philips X’pert PAN PW3040 analytical diffractometer operating with Cu Kα radiation (λ=1.5406 A) in the 2θ range of 10–80°. The morphological characteristics of CNF were examined by field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, SU5000, Hitachi, Zeiss). Energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopy was conducted to analyze the elemental composition using FE-SEM (XL 30 S FEG, Philips, Netherlands) with a beam voltage of 10 kV. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis was performed using a JEOL JEM-2100 Plus operated at 80 and 200 kV. The photocatalytic experiments were carried out using a Shimadzu UV–1800 UV–Vis spectrophotometer equipped with quartz cuvettes, with irradiation and absorbance measurements performed over the wavelength range of 200–800 nm. The band gap energy (Eg) was measured using a diffuse reflectance UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV–Vis 2450). Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were recorded with FT-IR Thermo Nicolet iS5 using the KBr pellet technique within the wave number range of 400–4000 cm−1 to confirm the spinel structure of the samples.

Degradation experiments

A 20 mL dye solution of appropriate concentrations (CV (10 mg/L) & ARS (10 mg/L)) was taken in a 100 mL beaker to study photodegradation. The degradation investigation was performed by adjusting pH using NaOH/ H2SO4 solutions with the help of a pH meter (Elico-LI 120) and investigating the photodegradation of dyes. The effect of pH was determined in the range of 2.0–12.0. The catalyst effect was studied by varying amounts of catalyst from 0.5 to 2.0 mg. Absorbance was measured in 1 cm quartz cuvettes using a double beam UV-1800 ultraviolet–visible spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan).

Antimicrobial assay

The antimicrobial activity of NFO, CFO and CNF were tested using the agar well diffusion method, a common method used for preliminary screening of antibacterial potential [22]. Two Gram-positive bacterial strains (Staphylococcus aureus and Bacillus cereus) and two Gram-negative strains (Salmonella typhi and Escherichia coli) were selected as test organisms, representing common human pathogens [23]. Bacterial strains were obtained all through the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). The cultures were revived in Mueller–Hinton Broth (MHB; Microexpress, India) and incubated overnight at 37 °C in a rotary shaker. The bacterial suspensions were brought to a turbidity corresponding to 0.5 McFarland standard (approximately 1 × 108 CFU/mL) by confirming a spectrophotometric absorbance of 595 nm. The standardized inocula were uniformly lawn cultured on Mueller Hinton Agar (MHA) plates by swab streaking the inoculum. Wells of 6 mm diameter were aseptically bored using a sterile cork borer, and each well was filled with 30 µL of either the positive control gentamicin (10 µg/mL), the negative control 1% DMSO, or the test samples at a concentration of 20 mg/mL. After a 30-min period of incubation at room temperature, the plates were incubated at 37 °C overnight (18–24 h), after which the antimicrobial activity was determined by measuring the zones of inhibition (ZOI) in millimeters around the wells.

Results and discussion

Structural analysis

X-ray diffraction (XRD) is a powerful technique for evaluating phase composition, crystallinity, and purity of samples. The XRD patterns of the synthesized mixed and individual ferrites are presented in Fig. 2. The diffraction peaks appeared at 2θ values of 18.2°; 30.3°; 35.8°; 43.7°; 53.5°; 57.8°; and 62.6° correspond to the reflection planes (111), (220), (311), (400), (422), (511), and (440) respectively (Fig. 2a). The presence of a prominent peak corresponds to the (311) plane located at 2θ=35.8°, confirms the successful synthesis of CFO NPs and indicates a cubic structure. The narrow and well-defined diffraction peaks further demonstrate that the synthesized copper ferrite NPs are highly crystalline. Similarly, the XRD pattern of NFO shows distinct peaks at 2θ = 30.1°, 35.4°, 37.2°, 43.4°, 53.8°, 57.4°, 63.0°, and 74.6°, which corresponding to the (220), (311), (222), (400), (422), (511), (440), and (533) planes (Fig. 2b) [20]. According to JCPDS PDF card no. 10-86-2267, single-phase NFO exhibits a cubic spinel structure, with the intense (311) peak further verifies the formation of spinel phase. The XRD pattern of the CNF sample (2θ range: 10–80°), also shows the characteristic peaks at 2θ = 18.3°, 30.3°, 35.7°, 43.63°, 54.2°, 57.4°, 63.32°, and 74.2°, which can be indexed to the crystal planes of (111), (220), (311), (400), (422), (511), (440), and (533) in the pattern (Fig. 2c). The dominant diffraction peaks correspond to the lattice plane at (311), (222), (400), and (440), confirm the spinel structure. No additional peaks corresponding to impurities such as CuO and Fe2O3 were detected. The average crystallite size of CNF sample calculated using the Debye-Scherer equation, was found to vary between 8 and16 nm.

FTIR analysis

FTIR spectroscopy of honey-mediated biosynthesized FNPs was recorded in transmission mode over the range of 4000–400 cm−1 to identify the functional groups responsible for stabilizing mixed ferrite and related ferrite NPs (Fig. 3a). The broad absorption band at 3425 cm−1 corresponds to O–H stretching vibrations of water molecules, indicating their adsorption on the surface of the synthesized nanomaterials. Spinel ferrites usually exhibit distinct bands in the 400–1000 cm−1 region, associated with ionic vibrations. In the present case, two distinct bands were observed in the ranges of 390–525 cm−1 and 560–630 cm−1. The absorption peaks at 595 cm−1 and 465 cm−1 correspond to the metal–oxygen stretching vibrations: the band at 595 cm−1 refers to metal–oxygen stretching at the tetrahedral site, while the band at 465 cm−1 corresponds to metal–oxygen stretching at the octahedral sites. These two bands are prominent absorption bands in spinel ferrite structures. The FTIR spectrum of honey typically shows strong absorption bands related to water (around 3300 and 1640 cm⁻1) and carbohydrates (2933, 1640, 1110, and 930 cm⁻1). These bands mainly originate from C–O, C–C, and C–H stretching vibrations, along with C–H bending modes characteristic of carbohydrate structures. The fingerprint region (1500–650 cm⁻1) is largely associated with vibrational modes of functional groups belonging to sugars, lipids, and other carbohydrate-based components present in honey. The H–O–H groups of the adsorbed water molecules are responsible for the stretching vibration of the band at 1639 cm−1. The region between 1000 to 1700 cm−1 shows carbohydrate and protein-related modes (C–O and C–C groups) (Fig. 3b). The weak band at 2925 cm−1 is related to the C–H stretching, while the band at 1383 cm−1 likely due to C–H bending, C–O vibrations, or other organic groups. The biomolecular constituents of honey, including glucose, proteins, amino acids, enzymes, and vitamins, contributes to these absorptions. The band near 1400 cm–1 is likely due to carboxylate vibrations, or sugar-related modes arising from residues in the samples.

a FTIR spectra of CFO, NFO, and CNF nanopowders and b enlarged view of the fingerprint region (500–1500 cm⁻1)

Variations in peak positions and intensities reflect subtle changes in nanoparticle stabilization. In the CNF sample, the marked decrease in band intensities indicates thermal decomposition of the organic matrix, suggesting a transformation toward a more purified and crystalline phase. The FTIR results confirm that sugars, proteins, and other bioactive constituents in honey play a key role in the formation and stabilization of CNF NPs.

The force constants corresponding to the Fe–O and Cu–O stretching vibrations observed at 595 and 465 cm⁻1 were estimated using the relation F=μ(2πcν)2, yielding values of approximately 259 m⁻1 and 163 N m⁻1, respectively, which confirm the formation of a stable spinel lattice in the copper–nickel mixed ferrite.

Optical properties

The optical absorption spectrum is a reliable approach for determining the optical energy band gap of crystalline and amorphous materials. In this study, diffuse reflectance (DR) UV–visible spectroscopy was used to investigate the optical properties of the synthesized FNPs. The absorption spectra of all ferrite compositions are shown in Fig. 4, with absorbance recorded in the 200–800 nm range. A strong absorption edge was observed for all samples, indicating their semiconducting nature and suggesting potential for photocatalytic applications under UV–visible light.

Morphological studies

The SEM micrograph (Fig. 5 a–c) shows a uniform distribution of NPs with a nearly spherical morphology and the particle sizes ranging from 50 to 250 nm, with an average size of 110 ± 25 nm. The elemental compositions (Cu, Ni, Fe, and O) of prepared NPs have been observed by EDX analysis, along with their corresponding atomic percentages (Fig. 5d). Furthermore, the synthesized CNF nanomaterial revealed the presence of elements such as carbon (C), and potassium (K). The detected carbon and oxygen can be attributed to residual organic compounds derived from the natural fuel employed in the green synthesis, as honey is rich in sugars, pigments, minerals, organic acids, proteins, and other organic constituents [24]. The trace amount of K is likely derived from the precursor chemicals or mineral constituents of honey. The occurrence of such trace elements is commonly reported in green synthesis studies and aligns with the biogenic origin of the fuel used. Although the exact mechanism is still under investigation, the honey-mediated synthesis of ferrite NPs is thought to begin with the dissociation of metal salts into positively charged ions, which then interact with the hydroxyl groups of fructose and glucose present in honey [20]. The subsequent formation of metal hydroxide complexes stabilized by honey, leads to a gel-like precursor. Upon heating, this metal–honey gel undergoes thermal decomposition releasing N2 and CO2 gases and ultimately transforming into Cu0.5Ni0.5Fe2O4 mixed ferrite powder.

The HRTEM images clearly reveal the microstructural features and lattice fringes of the synthesized catalyst, confirming its crystalline nature. The measured interplanar spacing values are consistent with the respective crystal planes of the constituent phases, further supporting the successful formation of the catalyst [25]. Photocatalytic activity

Photodegradation studies

The effect of photocatalytic properties of CNF compound on the degradation of cationic and anionic dyes was examined by maintaining the quantity of photocatalyst, the concentration of dye, and the time of irradiation constant.

Figure 8 shows the photocatalytic degradation of 10 mg/L of CV under three experimental conditions: CV with UV and catalyst, CV with catalyst only, CV with light only. The results show that the remarkable light dependence of the photocatalytic degradation of CV. When examined in the absence of light, the catalysts showed no significant degradation of the dye. However, when a catalyst was combined with UV light, the degradation rate increased significantly. The CNF catalyst demonstrated the highest efficiency, achieving approximately 84% degradation in less than 60 min and a maximum of 91.3% within 120 min. In contrast, the NFO and CFO catalysts exhibited similar, yet lower, performance, with only 49% degradation in the first 60 min and reaching a maximum of 75% in 120 min. For comparison, CV alone with UV (without catalyst) resulted in only 62% degradation over the same 120-min period.

Effect of quantity of photocatalysts

The effect of different catalyst amounts on CV degradation was studied using different amounts ranging from 0.5 to 2.0 mg at a CV concentration of 10.0 mg/L. Figure 9a shows that the degradation rate increases with an increase in the amount of catalyst up to 1.0 mg. It could be attributed to an increase in the number of active sites and other defects on the surface, resulting in an expansion in the number of hydroxyls (•OH) and superoxide radicals (•O2−) with the increase in the amount of catalyst [18]. The turbidity of the suspension was absorbed when the catalyst exceeded 1.0 mg. The reduced efficiency at higher catalyst loadings was due to both particle aggregation and increased turbidity, which scatters UV light and reduces effective photon penetration. The suspension gets more turbid as the catalyst concentration increases resulting in increased scattering and absorption of UV radiation. Subsequently, light penetration into the reaction medium is reduced, and substantially a part of the catalyst particles remains ineffectively irradiated [26]. High catalyst concentrations may not be beneficial because aggregation could reduce the porosity, lessen the available surface area, and cause an insufficiency of active sites on the catalyst surface [18, 19]. Further, the recombination of electron–hole pairs increases at higher concentrations, decreasing the degradation efficiency [27]. The proposed system achieved a maximum of 91.3% degradation with 0.05 g/L of CNF (Fig. 9b), while with NFO, it reached 75.5% in the same period.

Effect of pH

The pH is the most significant factor that can affect the dye’s degradation process by changing the surface area of the CNF photocatalyst for adsorption and photon absorption. The effects of different pH values from 2.0 to 12.0 on dye degradation efficiency were studied and summarized in Fig. 10. The degradation efficiency of CV with CNF was found to be 88% at pH 2 due to a change in the color of the dye itself in the presence of a high concentration of protons. The degradation efficiency levels of CV were increased marginally from 82.77 at pH 4.0 to 84.45% at pH 6.0 with CNF. The degradation efficiency of CV was detected to be good in the alkaline medium. The degradation efficiency levels of CV with CNF advanced from 92.71 at pH 8.0 to 100.0% at pH 10.0. CNF photocatalyst degraded CV by more than 90% in the first 60 min and achieved 100% in 90 min at pH 10.0. In contrast, 100% degradation of CV was found in less than 30 min at pH 12.0. The enhanced decolorization observed in alkaline medium can be attributed to the combined effects of hydroxide-induced carbinol base formation, resulting in loss of color intensity and the generation of hydroxyl radicals (•OH), which promote the photocatalytic oxidation of dye molecules [28]. The higher degradation efficiency along with faster degradation rate under alkaline medium is mainly due to increased negative charge on the surface of photocatalysts. The electrostatic interaction improves the adsorption effects, thereby boosting the degradation performance. Furthermore, the hydroxyl ions available at higher pH form hydroxyl radicals, thereby raising the degradation efficiency [4]. The point of zero charge (pHzpc ≈ 6.8), which governs the surface charge of the catalyst and subsequently influences dye adsorption. At pH values above the pHzpc, the catalyst surface becomes negatively charged, which helps electrostatic attraction toward the cationic CV molecules, ensuing higher adsorption and enhanced photocatalytic degradation. Therefore, increase in degradation efficiency at higher pH can be attributed to the combined effects of higher adsorption and increased reactive oxygen species formation.

Recycling of the photocatalysts

The stability and reusability of the photocatalyst were assessed by using the catalysts for three photodegradation experiments by keeping the same concentration of CV dye. After each degradation process, the catalyst was washed with deionized water and alcohol to remove any adsorbed CV on the surface and dried in the oven for 1 h before reuse. As illustrated in Fig. 11. CNF photocatalyst showed significant stability of crystal structure even after the three cycles, reaching a maximum degradation efficiency of 88.8% CV and retained 97.5% of its original activity. The slight decrease in performance can be attributed to minor alterations in the morphological, optical, and textural characteristics of the photocatalyst. The decrease in the degradation efficiency could be ascribed to the loss of the photocatalyst due to washing in between the cycles. The observed reusability results were consistent with findings reported by other researchers indicating high durability, and sustainability of ferrite based photocatalysts. The stability and recyclability of the CNF photocatalyst specifies that it can be reused several times in wastewater treatment applications, which reduce the effluent treatment costs (Fig. 12 and 13).

Based on the experimental results and the foregoing discussion, it is clearly indicated that the recombination of electron–hole pairs in CNF is controlled well, which results in an increased lifetime of electron–hole pair separation and enhanced degradation efficiency. Furthermore, CNF acts as an effective photocatalyst for the degradation of cationic CV dye compared to anionic dyes (Fig. 14). The photocatalytic performance of CNF NPs is possibly elucidated through a proposed mechanism (Fig. 12). Under UV irradiation, electrons in the conduction band (CB) are excited to higher energy levels, leaving behind positively charged holes. At the catalyst surface, these accumulated electrons react with adsorbed oxygen molecules (O2) to generate superoxide radicals (•O2⁻). Simultaneously, the valence band (VB) holes interact with water molecules (H2O), resulting in the formation of hydroxyl radicals (•OH). These highly reactive oxygen-based species (•O2⁻ and •OH) act as secondary oxidants. Kinetic study

The dye degradation kinetics was performed using the fractional order kinetic model equation [20] Ln C/Co = − kt, where k and t are the rate constant of the dye degradation and time respectively. The kinetic behaviour of the CV dye at varied amounts of photocatalyst CNF is illustrated in Fig. 13, while the corresponding parameters such as rate constant and catalyst load are presented in Table 1. The rate constant values displayed by photocatalyst CNF for maximum degradation was 1.90 ⅹ 10–2 min−1, indicating the rapid degradation of dye with a distinctive half-life of 36.34 min. t1/2 = 0.693/k is the half-life (t1/2) used to estimate the time photocatalyst takes to degrade 50% of the initial dye concentration. The calculated low half-life (36.34 min) reflected a critical indication of the excellent efficiency of the CNF photocatalyst in carrying out the photocatalytic degradation of cationic dye CV.

Effect of photocatalytic properties of CNF on the degradation of anionic dye

Figure 14 illustrates the photocatalytic degradation of ARS using the CNF photocatalyst. The results indicate that CNF is not an effective photocatalysts for ARS dye, achieving a maximum degradation of only 32.3% after 120 min of irradiation. This lower activity can be attributed to the anionic nature of ARS and incongruent surface charge properties of the catalyst may have affected its absorptivity. Further, the presence of OH− ions on the catalyst surface leads to ionic repulsion between the negatively charged surface and the anionic ARS molecules, resulting in low degradation efficiency. These findings suggest that CNF exhibits a greater affinity and photocatalytic efficiency toward cationic dyes than anionic dyes.

The effect of enhancer

Figure 15 summarizes the effects of enhancers such as hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), silver nitrate (AgNO3), ethanol and p-benzoquinone on the degradation of CV in a photocatalytic process. The photocatalyst CNF was able to degrade CV by 91.3% in the absence of any enhancer. No significant improvement in degradation efficiency was observed when enhancers were added to the reaction mixture under UV light (Table 2). The degradation of CV decreased from 91.3 to 90.51%, 48.48%, and 25.44% with H2O2, ethanol and p-benzoquinone, respectively. However, when 0.005 g of AgNO3 was added, turbidity was observed in the solution, indicating the photoreduction of Ag⁺ ions to metallic silver NPs (Ag⁰) under photocatalytic conditions. These results demonstrate that the presence of enhancers is not required to achieve efficient photocatalytic degradation of CV with CNF photocatalyst (Fig. 16).

Antibacterial activity

The antibacterial activity of the ferrite-based nano formulations (NFO, CFO, and their composite CNF) was tested against two Gram-positive (S. aureus, B. cereus) and two Gram-negative (E. coli, S. typhi) bacterial strains at a concentration of 20 mg/mL (Table 3). The findings showed that the NPs exhibited moderate antibacterial activity, with greater efficacy against Gram-positive compared to Gram-negative strains (Fig. 17.). Among the Gram-positive bacteria, B. cereus showed the highest susceptibility, with inhibition zones of 2.3–2.7 mm (Table 3). The individual nanoforms (NFO and CFO) showed stronger inhibition against B. cereus compared to the combined CNF formulation, suggesting that the mixed ferrite system may have undergone partial aggregation or reduced surface reactivity, thereby diminishing its antibacterial potential. In contrast, S. aureus exhibited a different trend-CNF inhibited growth more effectively (2.0 mm) than NFO (1.8 mm) and CFO (1.5 mm). This indicates a synergistic interaction between the Ni- and Cu-based ferrite components, that could have enhanced ROS (reactive oxygen species) production and disrupted the bacterial membrane more efficiently. Similar synergistic effects have been reported in mixed metal oxide nanocomposites that enable electron transfer and ROS-induced bacterial destruction [33]. The Gram-negative strains, E. coli and S. typhi, were less inhibited, with inhibition zones ranging from 0.7 to 1.7 mm.

The minimal effect on S. typhi and slight inhibition of E. coli could be due to the lipopolysaccharide (LPS) outer membrane which can limit the penetration of the NPs. This is consistent with previous research studies showing Gram-negative bacteria are often more resistant to NPs, mainly because of their two-membrane structure [34]. The positive control (Gentamicin) produced the greatest inhibition zones demonstrating that the antimicrobial assay was valid, and the DMSO control exhibited no inhibition; thus, confirming these effects resulted solely from the NPs (Fig. 17).

These findings indicate that although NFO and CFO showed antibacterial activity on their own, in CNF they elicited strain-dependent synergistic effects that were only apparent in S. aureus, but inhibited activity in B. cereus. Additionally, weaker antibacterial performance against the Gram-negative strains emphasized the role of the bacteria cell wall structure in cellular interactions with NPs. Further modification of the particle surfaces or optimization of the NPs size may increase the antibacterial efficacy.

Conclusion

We have successfully prepared the mixed ferrites (Cu0.5Ni0.5Fe2O4) by a simple auto-combustion method followed by heat treatment. The material showed a reduced band gap of 1.72 eV, indicating enhanced light absorption and improved charge separation efficiency. The mixed ferrite NPs showed a higher photocatalytic degradation efficiency of 92% for the cationic crystal violet dye, mainly because of their unique morphological, structural, and textural properties in comparison with individual CuFe2O4 and NiFe2O4 NPs. The Cu0.5Ni0.5Fe2O4 hetero-structures with an average crystallite size of 110 nm proved to be efficient photocatalysts, displaying higher efficiency for the degradation of cationic dyes compared to anionic dyes. These determinations demonstrate their potential as promising photocatalysts. In addition, the catalyst retained 97.5% of its original activity after three reuse cycles, confirming its stability and reusability. Moreover, the antibacterial activity of NFO and CFO demonstrated strain-dependent synergistic impacts in CNF, which were evident in S. aureus but suppressed the activity in B. cereus. Among the Gram-positive bacterial isolates investigated, B. cereus demonstrated the greatest susceptibility, indicated by inhibition zone diameters of 2.3–2.7 mm. Further optimization of the particle surface properties, nanoparticle size, and the synthesis of NPs using plant extracts and other green materials may enhance the antibacterial properties and offer a green approach for water purification, as they combine magnetic separability, high catalytic activity, and eco-friendliness.
