Perinatal and long‐term implications of diagnosing gestational diabetes mellitus using only fasting and one‐hour post‐load glucose concentrations: Secondary analysis of the GEMS trial and its five‐year follow‐up
Authors: Qiliang Liu, Jane E. Harding, Greg D. Gamble, Carl Eagleton, Lisa Dawes, Caroline A. Crowther
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe proposed revised diagnostic criteria for gestational diabetes (GDM) in New Zealand include a fasting plasma glucose (FPG) concentration ≥5.3 mmol/L and 1‐h post‐load glucose (PLG) concentration ≥10.6 mmol/L on a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), but no 2‐h PLG. This study evaluates the impact of GDM diagnosis based on FPG and/or 1‐h PLG abnormalities on perinatal and long‐term outcomes.Material and MethodsThis secondary analysis of data from GEMS, a multicentre, randomized trial and its follow‐up, included women and their children who met the proposed revised GDM criteria and received treatment. Participants were classified into three groups: (1) both group: elevated FPG and 1‐h PLG, (2) fasting group: elevated FPG alone, and (3) one‐hour group: elevated 1‐h PLG alone. Between‐group pairwise comparisons were made using log‐binomial and normal‐identity regressions.ResultsOf 217 eligible women‐child pairs, 85 (39.2%) were in the fasting group, 61 (28.1%) in the both group, and 71 (32.7%) in the one‐hour group. Women in the fasting and both groups, compared with the one‐hour group, were more likely to be overweight or obese both at the first antenatal visit and five‐year follow‐up, more likely to have required pharmacotherapy for GDM (85.9% and 88.5% vs. 69.0%; p < 0.0001), and had higher rates of type 2 diabetes/prediabetes at 5 years (50.0% and 48.8% vs. 28.3%; p = 0.03). Their infants were born earlier (38.1 and 38.0 vs. 38.6 weeks; p = 0.02) with higher birthweight z‐scores (0.2 and 0.1 vs. −0.2; p = 0.03). Infants in the fasting group, compared with infants in the one‐hour group, had higher rates of being large‐for‐gestational age and neonatal intensive care unit admission. At 5 years, children in the fasting group, compared with those in the one‐hour group, had higher mean z‐scores in weight and height, and were more likely to have an abnormal developmental screening questionnaire score (24.4% vs. 5.1%; p = 0.04).ConclusionsWomen diagnosed with GDM based on an elevated FPG, compared with those with only an elevated 1‐h PLG, may have had increased diabetes risks at five‐year follow‐up, while their children may have greater infant health risks and greater growth at 5 years.

INTRODUCTION

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is an increasingly prevalent global health concern associated with significant maternal and child complications. Despite its widespread impact, there remains no international consensus on diagnostic thresholds for glucose concentrations during oral glucose tolerance testing (OGTT). 1 Emerging evidence suggests that women with GDM differ in their metabolic profiles and perinatal and long‐term risks of complications, and this is reflected in their specific OGTT abnormalities. 2 , 3 , 4 Women with an elevated fasting plasma glucose (FPG) concentrations predominantly exhibit defects of insulin sensitivity, 5 while those with elevated post‐load glucose (PLG) concentrations more often have defective insulin secretion. 5 , 6 , 7 Fasting hyperglycemia, compared with elevated PLG, has been more strongly associated with hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, 3 , 8 , 9 , 10 large‐for‐gestational‐age (LGA) infants, 2 , 3 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 prematurity, 3 neonatal hypoglycemia, 3 , 4 and higher maternal risks for impaired glucose tolerance 15 and type 2 diabetes 16 following childbirth. In contrast, women with elevated PLGs but normal FPGs have less need for antenatal insulin therapy, 2 lower infant birthweight, 10 and lower future risks for type 2 diabetes. 16

The proposed revision of the current New Zealand GDM diagnostic criteria (unpublished in February 2026) recommends screening all women whose first antenatal glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) is < 42 mmol/mol using a 75 g OGTT at 24 weeks' gestation. In the revised criteria, GDM will be diagnosed on a FPG ≥5.3 mmol/L or 1‐h PLG ≥10.6 mmol/L, while a 2‐h PLG is no longer required. However, no country has adopted diagnostic criteria for GDM using only the FPG and/or 1‐h PLG without a 2‐h PLG value, and no study has assessed the health outcomes of women and their children with different GDM subtypes defined by the abnormal FPG and 1‐h PLG in the revised New Zealand GDM criteria.

The objective of this study is to evaluate maternal and child health outcomes across GDM subtypes defined by different combinations of abnormalities in FPG and 1‐h PLG values using the revised New Zealand diagnostic criteria. Comparison of health outcomes between the proposed revised diagnostic criteria for GDM and the current diagnostic criteria that include a 2‐h PLG will be reported in another manuscript. For the current study, we hypothesized that women with both an elevated FPG and 1‐h PLG would have the highest risk of complications due to combined insulin resistance and secretory defects, 5 followed by those with an elevated FPG only, and then those with elevated 1‐h PLG alone. This study aimed to examine maternal and child outcomes in participants with these different patterns of OGTT abnormalities in a contemporary New Zealand cohort based on GDM diagnosis using the proposed revised criteria.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Study design and setting

This retrospective study is a secondary analysis of data from the GEMS (Gestational Diabetes Mellitus Diagnostic Detection Thresholds) randomized trial, 17 and its five‐year follow‐up study. 18 The GEMS trial assessed whether identifying and treating GDM using the lower International Association of Diabetes in Pregnancy Study Groups (IADPSG) diagnostic thresholds (FPG ≥5.1, 1‐h PLG ≥10.0 and/or 2‐h PLG ≥8.5 mmol/L), 19 compared with the higher current New Zealand criteria (FPG ≥5.5 and/or 2‐h PLG ≥9.0 mmol/L), 20 would improve infant outcomes without increasing maternal morbidity. 17

The GEMS trial was conducted in Auckland, New Zealand, from April 2015 to August 2020, and enrolled women with a singleton pregnancy without pre‐existing diabetes or previous GDM. 17 All participants had a 75 g, 2‐h OGTT between 24 and 32 weeks' gestation, with FPG, 1‐h PLG and 2‐h PLG measured, and were randomized to diagnosis of GDM using either the lower IADPSG criteria 19 or the higher New Zealand criteria. 20 Women diagnosed with GDM in either criteria group received lifestyle advice and pharmacotherapy as needed, while those not diagnosed with GDM received routine pregnancy care. The five‐year follow‐up included women and children who had been diagnosed and treated for GDM by either the lower or higher glycemic criteria, participants whose OGTT results fell between the two glycemic criteria and who had been untreated for GDM in the higher criteria group, and a randomly‐selected group of participants without GDM by the lower criteria, matched to treated participants by maternal age, body mass index (BMI), and ethnicity. 18 All participating women and caregivers of participating children provided written informed consent.

Data used for the current study were derived from the GEMS trial 17 and its five‐year follow‐up. 18 Maternal baseline characteristics, obstetric and infant outcomes were obtained from the trial data collection forms. Follow‐up data had been collected using standardized maternal and age‐appropriate child questionnaires, and through linkage with routinely collected health data from government agencies.

Definitions of study groups

We categorized women who were treated for GDM in the GEMS trial, and their children, into three groups based on the mother's OGTT results at trial entry: (1) fasting group: FPG ≥5.3 but 1‐h PLG < 10.6 mmol/L, (2) both group: FPG ≥5.3 and 1‐h PLG ≥10.6 mmol/L, and (3) one‐hour group: 1‐h PLG ≥10.6 but FPG < 5.3 mmol/L. Women and their children who did not fit into any of these groups were excluded from this study, as were those either not diagnosed with GDM or who remained untreated in the GEMS trial.

Study outcomes

Maternal pregnancy‐related outcomes included preeclampsia, induction of labor, Caesarean section, postpartum hemorrhage (≥500 mL), gestational weight gain, pharmacotherapy for GDM, breastfeeding at hospital discharge, and length of postnatal stay. Infant perinatal outcomes included gestational age at birth, infant sex, birthweight, length and head circumference and z‐scores, 21 large for gestational age (LGA; birthweight > 90th percentile), 21 small for gestational age (SGA; birthweight < 10th percentile), 21 preterm birth (< 37 weeks' gestation), composite serious infant outcome (any of: perinatal death, death of liveborn before discharge, birth trauma, shoulder dystocia), neonatal hypoglycemia (glucose < 2.6 mmol/L requiring treatment), neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission, and length of postnatal stay. Maternal outcomes at five‐year follow‐up included type 2 diabetes and/or prediabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, overweight/obesity, BMI, and renal impairment. Child outcomes included BMI, height and weight z‐scores, overweight/obesity (BMI z‐score > 2 standard deviations [SD] above the median for age less than five years, 22 > 1 SD for age ≥5 years 23 ), any neurodevelopmental impairment (any of visual or hearing impairment, developmental delay [any of caregiver‐reported: language, motor, cognitive or unspecified developmental delay], cerebral palsy, autism spectrum disorder, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder), abnormal Ages and Stages Questionnaire (ASQ) score, 24 emotional‐behavioral difficulties (total difficulties score ≥17 on the Strength and Difficulties Questionnaire [SDQ]), 25 doctor‐diagnosed diabetes, asthma, allergies, and caregiver‐reported general health. The ASQ‐3 24 is a parent‐completed, standardized developmental screening instrument that evaluates five areas, including communication, gross motor, fine motor, problem‐solving, and personal‐social functioning of children. Total scores for each area are calculated, with scores more than two standard deviations (SD) below the mean for each age and questionnaire‐specific cutoff defined as abnormal. The SDQ 25 is a brief, standardized behavioral screening tool that assesses emotional symptoms, conduct problems, hyperactivity and inattention, peer relationships, and prosocial behavior of children. The total difficulties score is the sum of the scores in all areas except the prosocial score.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics and clinical outcomes of women and their children in the fasting, both, and one‐hour groups were analyzed using an overall three‐group comparison, followed by prespecified pairwise comparisons, adjusted using the Tukey method to control the family‐wise error rate and maintain a 5% significance level. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD, and categorical variables as counts and proportions (%). Generalized linear models were used for all analyses. Binary outcomes are reported as risk difference (RD) using identity‐link functions or relative risk (RR) using log‐link functions (binomial distributions), and continuous outcomes as mean difference (MD) with identity‐link functions (normal distribution), all with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All analyses were performed using SAS® software version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, New Carolina, USA). For pairwise comparisons, statistical significance was adjusted for multiple testing using Bonferroni corrections by dividing the nominal alpha level of 0.05 by the three prespecified pairwise contrasts (p < 0.0167), whereas global three‐group comparisons were assessed at a two‐sided p < 0.05.

Exploratory multivariable sensitivity analyses were performed post hoc for the two main maternal and child outcomes at five‐year follow‐up (maternal type 2 diabetes or prediabetes, and child BMI and weight z‐score), adjusting for maternal primiparity at GEMS trial entry and BMI at first antenatal visit, as these showed the greatest between‐group differences in baseline characteristics.

RESULTS

Among the 217 eligible mother‐infant pairs, 85 (39.2%) were in the fasting group, 61 (28.1%) in the both group, and 71 (32.7%) in the one‐hour group (Figure 1, Table 1). Women in the fasting and both groups, compared with women in the one‐hour group, were less likely to be primiparous at trial entry (37.7% and 52.2% vs. 60.6%; p = 0.01), more likely to be overweight or obese (83.5% and 90.2% vs. 57.8%; p < 0.0001), and had higher mean BMI at first antenatal visit (32.6 and 32.7 vs. 27.3 kg/m2; p < 0.0001). Other demographic characteristics, including age, family history of diabetes, and ethnicity, were similar across groups.

Perinatal outcomes of women in the fasting group were similar to those in the both group (Table 2). However, women in the fasting and both groups were more likely to have received pharmacotherapy for GDM management compared with those in the one‐hour group (85.9% and 88.5% vs. 69.0%; p = 0.01), primarily due to increased insulin use (68.2% and 62.3% vs. 25.4%; p < 0.0001). Infants in the fasting and both groups were born earlier than those in the one‐hour group (38.1 and 38.0 vs. 38.6 weeks' gestation; p = 0.02), and those in the both group were more likely to have been born preterm (16.4% vs. 4.2%; RR 3.88, 95% CI 1.11 to 13.62; p = 0.03). Infants in the fasting and both groups had higher mean birthweight z‐scores compared with those in the one‐hour group (0.2 and 0.1 vs. −0.2; p = 0.03), while those in the fasting group were more likely to have been born LGA (16.5% vs. 4.2%; RR 3.90, 95% CI 1.16 to 13.15; p = 0.03). Infants in the fasting group were more likely to be admitted to the NICU than those in the one‐hour group (5.9% vs. 1.4%; RR 10.48, 95% CI 1.34, 81.91; p = 0.03). Other infant perinatal outcomes were similar among the three groups.

At five years after the index birth, health data were available for 152 (70.0%) women and 147 (67.7%) children of the 217 eligible participants (Figure). Baseline characteristics were similar between participants who were and were not followed up at five years (Table 3). Among participants who were followed up, fewer women in the fasting group were primiparous at trial entry compared with women in the both and one‐hour groups (37.9% vs. 51.2% and 67.9%; p < 0.005; Table 4). Women in the fasting and both groups, compared with those in the one‐hour group, had higher BMI at the first antenatal visit (33.04 and 32.01 vs. 27.53 kg/m2; p = 0.001), and were more likely to have been overweight or obese at trial entry (84.2% and 77.5% vs. 52.9%; p = 0.001). Ethnicity was similar among the three groups. However, compared with those in the fasting and both groups, women in the one‐hour group were less likely to be residing in the most deprived areas (decile 9–10) at five‐year follow‐up (31.0% and 34.2% vs. 15.1%; p = 0.02).

DISCUSSION

This study evaluated clinical outcomes in a contemporary New Zealand cohort of women with GDM and their children with subtypes defined by FPG and 1‐h PLG abnormalities on an OGTT. Women in the both and fasting groups, compared with women in the one‐hour group, were more often overweight or obese, more likely to have required pharmacotherapy for GDM management, and had higher rates of type 2 diabetes or prediabetes at five years. Their infants were born slightly earlier with higher birthweight z‐scores. Infants in the fasting group were more likely to have been born LGA and be admitted to NICU. At five years, children in the fasting group had higher weight and height z‐scores and were more likely to have abnormal ASQ scores, although overall neurodevelopmental outcomes were similar across groups. Adjustment for maternal BMI and primiparity attenuated the between‐group differences in maternal type 2 diabetes or prediabetes.

The distribution of prevalence of GDM subtypes in our study, with elevated FPG alone (39.2%) being the most common, followed by elevated 1‐h PLG alone (32.7%), and both abnormalities being the least common (28.1%), is similar to the distribution patterns reported in other studies conducted in multicultural populations. 13 , 26 , 27 Our findings, however, contrast with a Chinese retrospective cohort study, which reported most women had GDM diagnosed based on an elevated 1‐h PLG and/or 2‐h PLG value (77.8%), compared with those with both PLG and FPG abnormalities (14.3%) and those with abnormal FPGs alone (7.9%). 3 The differences in ethnic compositions, with the prior study including only Chinese women and our study involving a multiethnic cohort, likely contributed to this observed difference in GDM subtype prevalence. Of note, several large, multi‐ethnic cohort studies also highlighted that women of East Asian descent were more likely to be diagnosed with GDM based on elevated PLG values alone without fasting hyperglycemia, 28 , 29 while Indian, Pacific, Māori, and European women were more likely to be diagnosed based on elevated FPG. 30

We found women in the fasting and both groups required more pharmacotherapy for glycemic control compared with those in the one‐hour group, suggesting worse insulin sensitivity. Similar findings have been reported by others, 2 , 10 , 13 , 26 and may be related to the higher pre‐pregnancy BMI and greater parity in those affected by fasting hyperglycemia in these groups, with both factors being known to increase insulin resistance. 13 , 31 In contrast to larger retrospective studies 3 , 32 but similar to a systematic review of 8 observational studies that included 20 928 women with GDM, 2 we did not find increased rates of preterm birth or Caesarean section among women with an elevated FPGs alone.

At five years, women in the both and fasting groups had a higher prevalence of type 2 diabetes or prediabetes compared with women in the one‐hour group. This pattern is consistent with population‐based data showing fasting hyperglycemia is associated with greater long‐term diabetes risks than post‐load hyperglycemia alone, including in a Canadian population‐based study reporting FPG ≥5.3 mmol/L was linked to a 14‐fold increase in type 2 diabetes risk over a 4.4‐year follow‐up, compared with a 10‐fold increase in women with 1‐h PLG ≥10.6 mmol/L. 16 Fasting hyperglycemia, as present in both the fasting and both groups, is a strong independent predictor of future type 2 diabetes, 8 although elevations in 1‐h PLG have also been shown to independently predict postpartum impaired insulin sensitivity and later type 2 diabetes. 33 We speculated that baseline maternal characteristics, particularly maternal BMI and the associated insulin resistance, may have impacted the correlation between elevated FPG, either alone or combined with elevated 1‐h PLG, and later maternal diabetes following GDM. Higher antenatal BMI is consistently observed in women with fasting hyperglycemia or combined OGTT abnormalities in similar cohort studies. 3 , 11 , 27 Consistent with this, post hoc sensitivity analysis controlling for maternal BMI and primiparity attenuated the between‐group differences in five‐year type 2 diabetes or prediabetes rates, although the direction and size of the effect estimates remained consistent with an increased risk in the fasting and both groups.

In our cohort, the rate of post‐pregnancy type 2 diabetes was highest in the both group (17.1%), followed by the fasting (10.3%) and the one‐hour group (1.9%). This is consistent with a Mexican cohort study reporting type 2 diabetes was most frequently diagnosed in women with combined FPG and PLG abnormalities (10.0%) as early as six‐month postpartum, followed by those with elevated FPG alone (3.2%) and those with elevated PLG alone (1.5%). 27 This gradient of risk may be explained by the co‐existence of early‐phase insulin secretory and sensitivity defects in women with combined FPG and 1‐h PLG abnormalities, 5 which places them at the highest risks of long‐term diabetes compared with women with elevated FPG or 1‐h PLG alone. In contrast, an observational long‐term follow‐up study reported that PLGs at 1, 2, and 3 h, but not the FPG, were all significant predictors of maternal type 2 diabetes and prediabetes at 3‐month postpartum. 34

Elevated maternal FPG was associated with a slightly earlier gestational age at birth of infants in the both and fasting groups than in the one‐hour group, and a higher risk of preterm birth, a LGA infant, and a higher mean birthweight z‐score in the fasting group compared with the one‐hour group. These findings are in keeping with several studies reporting up to a 2.5‐fold increased risk of a LGA infant 3 , 9 , 13 , 14 and 59% higher risk of preterm birth 3 , 27 in infants exposed to elevated maternal FPG compared with exposure to elevated PLGs alone. Higher maternal pre‐pregnancy BMI in the fasting and both groups may have contributed to the higher birthweight z‐scores in their infants. Fetal exposure to prolonged maternal hyperglycemia associated with elevated FPG likely contributed to the higher rate of LGA births in the fasting than the one‐hour group due to fetal hyperinsulinemia and adipose tissue deposition. 35 In contrast to the Chinese retrospective cohort study, 3 where it remains uncertain whether routine neonatal hypoglycemia screening among infants of women with GDM was carried out, our study found similar neonatal hypoglycemia rates across all groups, likely reflecting the guideline recommendation for routine hypoglycemia screening in all GDM‐exposed infants in New Zealand. 20

At five years, children in the fasting group continued to have higher mean weight and height z‐scores than those in the one‐hour group. Although treated GDM is an established predictor of higher offspring BMI and obesity risk, 36 few studies have examined these outcomes by specific patterns of OGTT abnormalities. Prior studies suggest the association between GDM and higher offspring BMI often emerges in late childhood or adolescence, 37 , 38 particularly in children born LGA. 39 Thus, longer‐term follow‐up and early lifestyle interventions may be warranted for children exposed to women with an elevated FPG. The higher proportion of abnormal ASQ scores in the fasting than in the one‐hour group is concerning and warrants further investigation. This finding may reflect exposure to a more severe GDM subtype marked by fasting hyperglycemia, compounded by maternal obesity, with both being recognized contributors to adverse offspring psychomotor development. 40

Given the higher risks of adverse outcomes in women and their children in the both and fasting groups relative to the one‐hour group, some researchers have suggested subtyping women with fasting hyperglycemia as “High‐Risk GDM” and those with elevated PLGs alone as “Low‐Risk GDM.” 3 It has been proposed that this classification may enable more tailored management of GDM. 3 , 6 , 8 , 10 , 26 , 27 While we acknowledge the theoretical value of such risk stratification, no studies to date have evaluated the practical application of OGTT‐dependent GDM subtyping in guiding treatment. Larger prospective studies are warranted to determine whether differentiating the intensity of antenatal monitoring of fetal growth and maternal glucose, GDM treatment, and postpartum monitoring of women and their children based on the presence of fasting hyperglycemia will improve their future health.

Our study is strengthened by the use of high‐quality, prospectively collected clinical trial and follow‐up data of a contemporary cohort of New Zealand women and children. However, the relatively small sample size and follow‐up rates may have introduced potential selection and ascertainment bias, and limited our ability to perform meaningful subgroup analyses, such as for maternal ethnicity. However, baseline characteristics were similar between eligible participants who were and were not followed up, making differential loss to follow‐up less likely. In interpreting our findings, for each outcome we used both a global three‐group test to assess whether any differences existed across GDM subtypes and prespecified pairwise comparisons between each two GDM subtype groups adjusted for multiple testing using Bonferroni correction. Global p values were not adjusted because they represent a single hypothesis test per each outcome, while the large number of outcomes examined increased the risk of type I error. Conversely, the conservative nature of Bonferroni adjustment, together with the modest sample size, reduces statistical power in our pairwise comparisons, thus increasing the risk of type II errors and the possibility that true between‐group differences may have been missed. Therefore, results should be viewed as exploratory rather than definitive.

CONCLUSION

In this contemporary New Zealand cohort of women and their children diagnosed with GDM using the revised criteria based solely on fasting and 1‐h OGTT glucose values, women with elevated FPG, with or without an elevated 1‐h PLG, appeared to have higher risk of subsequent diabetes compared with those with elevated 1‐h PLG alone. Their children also tended to have more perinatal complications and higher growth measures at five years. Together, these findings suggest that GDM subtyping based on FPG and 1‐h PLG abnormalities may have potential values in risk stratification. However, these associations should be interpreted as exploratory in light of the modest sample size and follow‐up rate. Long‐term studies with larger sample sizes may help clarify the independent roles of different patterns of OGTT abnormalities in GDM and later maternal metabolic risks and child outcomes.
