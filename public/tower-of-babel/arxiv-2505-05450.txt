Screened Axio-dilaton Cosmology: Novel Forms of Early Dark Energy
and Anne-Christine Davis

Abstract
We study the cosmology of multi-field Dark Energy, using a well-motivated axio-dilaton model that contains the minimal number of fields to have the 2-derivative sigma-model interactions that power-counting arguments show naturally compete with General Relativity at low energies. Our analysis differs from earlier, related, studies by treating the case where the dilaton’s couplings to matter are large enough to require screening to avoid unacceptable dilaton-mediated forces in the solar system. We use a recently proposed screening mechanism that exploits the interplay between stronger-than-gravitational axion-matter couplings with the 2-derivative axion-dilaton interactions to suppress the couplings of the dilaton to bulk matter. The required axion-matter couplings also modify cosmology, with the axion’s background energy density turning out to resemble early dark energy. We compute the properties of the axion fluid describing the rapid oscillations of the axion field around the time-dependent minimum of its matter-dependent effective potential, extending the usual formalism to include nontrivial kinetic sigma-model interactions. We explore the implications of these models for the Cosmic Microwave Background and the growth of structure and find that for dilaton potentials of the Albrecht-Skordis form (itself well-motivated by UV physics), successful screening can be consistent with the early dark energy temporarily comprising as much as 10% of the total density in the past. We find that increasing the dilaton-matter coupling decreases the growth of structure due to enhanced Hubble friction, an effect that dominates the usual fifth-force effects that amplify structure growth.

Why not elsewhere?

The second important observation is that any scalar light enough to be cosmologically interesting mediates a long-range force that potentially competes with gravity. \displaystyle=

\displaystyle V^{\prime}_{\rm ax}({\mathfrak{a}})+\sum_{i}\left(\frac{{\cal U}_{i}^{\prime}}{1+{\cal U}_{i}}\right)\,\rho_{i}(\phi,{\mathfrak{a}})\simeq V^{\prime}_{\rm ax}({\mathfrak{a}})+\sum_{i}{\cal U}_{i}^{\prime}\,\rho_{i}(\phi,{\mathfrak{a}})\,,

where the final, approximate, equality drops subdominant powers of
{\cal U}_{i}
, for the reasons described above eq. (
2.11
). With the above choices the model is fully specified.

For future purposes two things are noteworthy about eqs. (
2.12
) through (
2.14
). First, notice that in the presence of matter the axion field is displaced from its vacuum value
{\mathfrak{a}}={0}
, moving towards the value
{{\mathfrak{a}}_{e}}
or
{{\mathfrak{a}}_{m}}
depending on whether the electron or Dark Matter density dominates. Second, notice that in the presence of an axion gradient the
W
-dependent terms of (
2.12
) themselves contribute as if the dilaton sees a gradient-dependent additional potential energy

\delta V_{{\scriptscriptstyle W}}=\tfrac{1}{2}W^{2}(\phi)(\partial{\mathfrak{a}})^{2}.

(2.16)

These are key features for the screening mechanism described below since (if large enough) the matter-dependence of the axion potential generates an axion gradient inside sufficiently large and dense macroscopic objects. But then the axion gradient makes
\delta V_{\scriptscriptstyle W}
nonzero, causing the dilaton to differ outside the source relative to the naive sum over the dilaton couplings of the source’s constituents. But once the parameters for these interactions are adjusted to ensure screening occurs the same terms also change the evolution of
{\mathfrak{a}}
and
\phi
in the early universe, due to the presence there of large densities of matter.

2.2
Criteria for screening and constraints

We next summarize the screening mechanism of
[
39
]
(those already familiar with this story can safely skip ahead to §
3
). To this end we assume the axion field is heavy enough not to mediate a long-range force, with a range much smaller than an AU ensured if
m_{\rm ax}\mathrel{\raise 1.29167pt\hbox{$>$\kern-8.50006pt\lower 4.30554pt\hbox{$\sim$}}}10^{-16}
eV. The dilation, on the other hand, is instead assumed light enough to evolve on cosmological times and so can mediate dangerous long-range forces. The idea is to choose axion-matter couplings so that macroscopic sources generate axion gradients and then to exploit the dilaton response to these gradients to reduce (
i.e.
‘screen’) the source’s effective ‘dilaton charge’. Screening occurs once two ingredients are in place: appropriate axion-matter couplings (to generate an axion gradient within or near the source) and derivative axion-dilaton couplings (so the axion gradient can modify the dilaton profile).

2.2.1
The Brans-Dicke problem

In regions where the vacuum dilaton potential and gradients of the axion field are both negligible the dilaton equation (
2.12
) becomes

\Box\phi\simeq\partial_{\phi}V_{\rm eff}(\phi)\simeq-\frac{\beta\rho}{M_{p}}\,,

(2.17)

where
\beta
is defined in (
2.3
) and the last equality uses (
2.15
). If this equation holds both inside and outside a nonrelativistic matter source (
e.g.
a star) then the field is unscreened and the static spherically symmetric solution for the field outside the source becomes

\phi(r)=\phi_{\infty}-\frac{\beta M}{4\pi M_{p}r}\,.

(2.18)

Such a solution describing the field outside the Sun can be ruled out within the solar system if
\beta\mathrel{\raise 1.29167pt\hbox{$>$\kern-8.50006pt\lower 4.30554pt\hbox{$\sim$}}}{\cal O}(10^{-3})

[
17
]
. The goal is to exploit the axion-dilaton and axion-matter couplings to reduce the size of the coefficient of
1/r
in (
2.18
) to acceptable levels without reducing
\beta
.

A remark about the quantity
\phi_{\infty}
proves useful later. In the naive telling of the story
\phi_{\infty}
is an integration constant obtained by demanding continuity with any spatially homogeneous asymptotic value taken at infinity. More generally,
\phi_{\infty}(t)
can be be a function of time chosen to satisfy the asymptotic field equation far from the source, such as a cosmological solution. But in truth the approximation of spherical symmetry usually breaks down at a finite distance from the source because the residual fields from other nearby sources start to contribute appreciably. In this case
\phi_{\infty}
would be chosen by matching to the value of the field at a radius close enough to be dominated by the spherically symmetric field of the nearest source, rather than further away.

The precise value of
\phi_{\infty}
often does not really matter, however, since any accidental shift ‘symmetry’ of the equations of the form
\phi\to\phi+c
(for constant
c
) can make
\phi_{\infty}
drop out of observable quantities. In the screening mechanism described here, however, the axion-dilaton interaction breaks the dilaton shift symmetry and so ensures the energy of the field depends on
\phi_{\infty}
and because of this its value in the ground state is instead determined by minimizing the energy. Agreement with ambient fields far from the source is then obtained at the cost of gradient energy, which can be much less expensive if the sources are more widely separated from one another compared to their size (see
[
39
]
for more details).

2.2.2
The screening mechanism

As described above, for axion-matter couplings we assume that it assigns an axion-dependence to particle masses in the Jordan frame, with
m_{i}=m_{i0}[1+{\cal U}_{i}({\mathfrak{a}})]
and the functions
{\cal U}_{i}
and the axion potential approximated by

{\cal U}_{i}({\mathfrak{a}})\simeq\frac{({\mathfrak{a}}-{\mathfrak{a}}_{i})^{2}}{2\Lambda_{i}^{2}}\,,

(2.19)

where
i=e,m
respectively denotes electrons and Dark Matter. The axion potential in vacuum,
V_{\rm ax}
, is as given in (
2.9
).

Inside a macroscopic object (like the Sun) whose electron density dominates the ambient Dark Matter density this type of interaction implies the axion ‘sees’ an effective matter-dependent scalar potential of the form

V_{\rm ax\,eff}({\mathfrak{a}})\simeq V_{\rm ax}({\mathfrak{a}})+m_{e0}\,{\cal U}_{e}({\mathfrak{a}})\,n_{e}\simeq V_{\rm ax}({\mathfrak{a}})+\frac{m_{e0}}{m_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}\;{\cal U}({\mathfrak{a}})\,\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle B}\,,

(2.20)

where electrical neutrality implies the electron and baryon number densities are equal and so we write
n_{e}=n_{\scriptscriptstyle B}=\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle B}/m_{\scriptscriptstyle N}
, where
n_{\scriptscriptstyle B}
and
\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle B}
are the local baryon-number and baryon-mass densities and
m_{\scriptscriptstyle N}
is the nucleon mass. We choose parameters such that the matter-dependent part of this potential dominates the vacuum contribution for the kinds of matter densities found in the Sun. Under these circumstances we expect to find
{\mathfrak{a}}\simeq{{\mathfrak{a}}_{e}}
deep inside matter, with fluctuations about this value having a density-dependent mass
m_{\rm ax}(\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle B},\phi)=m_{\mathfrak{a}}(\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle B})/W(\phi)
where

m_{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}(\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle B})\simeq\frac{m_{e0}\,n_{e}}{\Lambda_{e}^{2}}\simeq\frac{\rho_{e}}{\Lambda_{e}^{2}}\simeq\left(\frac{m_{e0}}{m_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}\right)\frac{\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle B}}{\Lambda_{e}^{2}}\,,

(2.21)

and
\rho_{e}
is the energy density in electrons.

If the axion Compton wavelength inside matter is much smaller than the distance over which
n_{e}
changes appreciably then the background axion profile,
{\mathfrak{a}}_{\rm ad}(x)
, can be approximated by the adiabatic result that satisfies
\left.\partial_{\mathfrak{a}}V_{\rm ax\,eff}\right|_{\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}}=0
. If it is also true that the axion mass in vacuum is much smaller than its matter-dependent one then it can be shown that the solution for
{\mathfrak{a}}_{\rm ad}(r)
robustly tends to jump from
{0}
to
{{\mathfrak{a}}_{e}}
within a narrow width
\ell\sim m_{\rm ax}(n_{e})^{-1}
near the source’s surface (see
[
39
]
for details). In these circumstances the background axion profile is well-approximated by a step function:

{\mathfrak{a}}=\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}(r)\simeq{{\mathfrak{a}}_{e}}\,\Theta(R-r)\,,

(2.22)

where
R
is the source radius and
\Theta
is the Heaviside step-function. Although these conditions are not strictly required for screening, they do make its analysis particularly simple.

With these choices, the step in the axion profile generates an axion gradient that is localized near
r=R
, and this in turn – by virtue of (
2.16
) – generates a delta-function contribution to the potential for the dilaton, localized near
r=R
. As usual, the presence of a delta-function potential induces a jump discontinuity in the derivative of the dilaton at
r=R
, and this is how the axion gradient modifies the dilaton profile. In particular, although the value of the radial derivative
\partial_{r}\phi
just inside the discontinuity is the usual one expected in the absence of screening, the jump discontinuity implies the derivative just outside is different, and if it is smaller the effective dilaton ‘charge’ of the source has been reduced.

To quantify this, if the dilaton profile for
r>R
is written as

\phi=\phi_{\rm loc}-\frac{LM_{p}}{r},

(2.23)

where
\phi_{\rm loc}
and
L
are integration constants, then (as discussed above) matching at the surface of the source would give
L=L_{0}:=2{\beta G_{\scriptscriptstyle N}M}
in the absence of screening. But keeping track of the jump discontinuity in
\partial_{r}\phi
when matching implies
L
for the exterior solution is instead given by

\frac{L}{R}=\frac{L_{0}}{R}+\Bigl(WW^{\prime}\Bigr)_{r=R}\left(\frac{R}{2\ell}\right)\frac{{{\mathfrak{a}}_{e}}^{2}}{M_{p}}\,,

(2.24)

where quantities like
WW^{\prime}(\phi)
are evaluated at
\phi(R)
. The effective dilaton charge of the source, including screening, can be defined by writing
L=2\beta_{\rm eff}G_{\scriptscriptstyle N}M
, and if so it is
\beta_{\rm eff}
and not
\beta
that must satisfy solar system constraints. The dilaton charge is screened when
L
is smaller than
L_{0}
, as is the case when
W^{\prime}
is negative.

2.2.3
Energetics

A complication to the above screening story is that
\beta_{\rm eff}
depends on the value taken by
\phi_{\rm loc}
, because this appears in the value
\phi(R)
of the field where the axion gradient occurs. As argued earlier, the way to determine the value for
\phi_{\rm loc}
is by minimizing the energy of the fields generated by the source.

There are two important
\phi_{\rm loc}
-dependent contributions to the energy of the fields surrounding the source. The first of these comes from the gradient energy of the dilaton field exterior to the source,

E_{\rm ext}=4\pi\int_{R}^{\infty}{\rm d}r\,r^{2}\;\frac{\phi^{\prime 2}}{2}=\frac{2\pi M_{p}^{2}L^{2}}{R}\,,

(2.25)

in which
\phi_{\rm loc}
enters through
L
due to the condition (
2.24
) and for weakly gravitating sources we can compute the energy in flat space. If this were the entire story then minimizing would set
L=0
, and thereby set the dilaton charge to zero (and so perfectly screen the source from the dilaton). This is not quite what happens because there is another
\phi_{\rm loc}
-dependent contribution to the energy, given by the axion and dilaton gradients interior to the source. For weakly gravitating sources these contribute

E_{\rm in}=2\pi\int_{0}^{R}{\rm d}r\,r^{2}\;\Bigl[\,W^{2}(\phi)({\mathfrak{a}}^{\prime})^{2}+(\phi^{\prime})^{2}\Bigr]\simeq\frac{\pi R^{2}{{\mathfrak{a}}_{e}}^{2}}{\ell}\Bigl(W^{2}\Bigr)_{r=R}\,,

(2.26)

where the approximate equality evaluates the integral using the narrow-width profile (
2.22
). Any other contributions to the energy that are independent of
\phi_{\rm loc}
do not participate in the minimization with respect to
\phi_{\rm loc}
and so can be dropped.

Further progress requires specifying the function
W
, so we consider two representative cases. In the case of an exponential dependence of (
2.4
) we have
W^{2}(\phi)=\exp[-\xi\phi/M_{p}]=\exp[-\phi/\Lambda_{\phi}]
and ref.
[
39
]
finds the effective dilaton charge evaluated at the field that minimizes the energy is

\beta_{\rm eff}\simeq\frac{R}{2\xi G_{\scriptscriptstyle N}M}\qquad\qquad\hbox{(exponential $W^{2}(\phi)$)}.

(2.27)

On the other hand, when
W^{2}
has a minimum, as in (
2.5
), we have
W^{2}=W^{2}_{\star}+\frac{1}{2}(\phi-\phi_{\star})^{2}/\Lambda_{\phi}^{2}

and the resulting effective dilaton coupling turns out in this case to be

\beta_{\rm eff}\simeq\frac{\beta}{1+(R/\ell)\frac{{{\mathfrak{a}}_{e}}^{2}}{4\Lambda_{\phi}^{2}}}\qquad\qquad\hbox{(quadratic $W^{2}(\phi)$)}\,.

(2.28)

This can also be much smaller than
\beta
if the denominator is large, as can happen if

{{\mathfrak{a}}_{e}}^{2}/\Lambda_{\phi}^{2}\gg 1
and/or the width
\ell
of the axion profile is sufficiently narrow relative to the source radius
R
.

3
Axion evolution and Early Dark Energy

We now turn to the cosmological implications of the screening mechanism discussed above. It is known that the axion-dilaton interaction plays a central role in the cosmological dynamics of axio-dilaton models
[
38
,
40
,
36
]
, and the axion-matter interactions required for screening can change things even more because of the matter-dependent axion potential they generate. In particular, we show here how these axion-matter interactions can provide a robust new production mechanism for a form of early dark energy.
11
11

11

We highlight this as a novel production and removal mechanism for early epochs of Dark Energy, with no claim that the result resolves the Hubble tension (as was Early Dark Energy’s initial motivation
[
46
]
).

We start with the study of background evolution for the coupled matter/axio-dilaton system, deferring the discussion of their cosmological perturbations to the next sections. For the axion the background evolution also splits up into two types of motion: slow evolution on timescales similar to the Hubble scale superimposed on rapid oscillations about the slower evolution. This section describes each of these in turn.

3.1
Adiabatic axion evolution

Much of the background axion dynamics can be simply understood using the time-dependent effective scalar potential (

\phi
and the particle densities
n_{i}
of the nonrelativistic matter to which the axion couples. When the evolution is adiabatic the axion’s central value simply traces out the time-dependent minimum of this potential.

3.1.1
Matter-dependent axion potential

With the choices outlined in the previous sections the effective potential experienced by the scalars is given by

The threshold value

\displaystyle\quad\left.+\tfrac{1}{2}\left(W^{2}g^{tt}+\frac{\overline{W}^{2}m^{2}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}}{\overline{m}^{2}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}}\right)\psi^{\star}\psi+\frac{W^{2}}{2m}g^{tt}\Bigl(i\psi^{\star}\dot{\psi}-i\dot{\psi}^{\star}\psi\Bigr)+\frac{W^{2}}{2m^{2}}g^{ij}\partial_{i}\psi^{\star}\,\partial_{j}\psi+\cdots\right].

The
\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}
-dependent terms of this action describe the corrections to the adiabatic approximation,
\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}={\mathfrak{a}}_{\rm ad}+\delta\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}
, such as (
3.21
) in the full theory before coarse-graining. The
\psi
-dependent terms describe the slow evolution of the oscillating part of the field.

The action
S_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}=\int{\rm d}^{4}x\langle{\cal L}_{\rm ax}\rangle
built from this lagrangian can be varied with respect to
g_{\mu\nu}
,
\phi
and
\psi
to give the contribution of axions to the metric, dilaton and background axion evolution once the fast axion oscillations are integrated out. For instance

T^{\mu\nu}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}=W^{2}(\phi)\Bigl\langle\partial^{\mu}{\mathfrak{a}}\,\partial^{\nu}{\mathfrak{a}}-\tfrac{1}{2}\,g^{\mu\nu}\,(\partial{\mathfrak{a}})^{2}\Bigr\rangle-g^{\mu\nu}\Bigl\langle V_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}({\mathfrak{a}})\Bigr\rangle=\frac{2}{\sqrt{-g}}\,\frac{\delta S_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}}{\delta g_{\mu\nu}}\,,

(4.9)

captures the axion-dependence of the Einstein equations, including the axion-dependence of the masses on the cosmological nonrelativistic fluids. Differentiating (
4.8
) with respect to
\phi
similarly reproduces the coarse-grained version of the right-hand side of the dilaton equation (
2.12
):

(WW^{\prime})\Bigl\langle(\partial{\mathfrak{a}})^{2}\Bigr\rangle+\Bigl\langle\partial_{\phi}V_{\rm eff}\Bigr\rangle=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\,\frac{\delta S_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}}{\delta\phi}\,.

(4.10)

Inhomogeneous fields are then described by expanding the resulting field equations about their homogeneous solutions, as for any other fluid, and (at leading order) dropping terms that do not contribute to the evolution of linear fluctuations. Before doing so, however, it is useful to re-express the axion evolution in terms of an approximate conservation law since this is convenient when deriving the evolution equations for the cosmological fluid. It also suggests recasting the variable
\psi
in a more directly physical way.

4.1.1
Axion dynamics and Madelung variables

Since averaging over fast oscillations removes all terms involving unequal powers of
\psi
and
\psi^{\star}
the lagrangian (
4.8
) enjoys an emergent approximate global symmetry,
\psi\to e^{i\alpha}\psi
. Part of the information in the field equation for
\psi
can be traded for the conservation of the corresponding Noether current, in much the same way as the field equation for
{\mathfrak{a}}
can be regarded as expressing Noether’s theorem for the current for the underlying axionic shift symmetry.

The conserved current density for this symmetry is
J^{\mu}=i\left[\psi^{\star}({\delta S}/{\delta\partial_{\mu}\psi^{\star}})-({\delta S}/{\delta\partial_{\mu}\psi})\psi\right]
and so the charge and spatial current densities

J^{t}=-\sqrt{-g}\,g^{tt}\frac{W^{2}}{m}\psi^{\star}\psi\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad J^{i}=\sqrt{-g}\,g^{ij}\frac{W^{2}}{2m^{2}}\Bigl[i(\partial_{j}\psi^{\star})\psi-i\psi^{\star}(\partial_{j}\psi)\Bigr]\,,

(4.11)

satisfy

\partial_{t}J^{t}+\partial_{i}J^{i}=0\,.

(4.12)

This suggests defining the fluid-like Madelung variables
[
61
]

\psi(x):=\sqrt{\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}(x)}\;e^{iS(x)}\,,

(4.13)

where
\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}
parameterizes the axion fluid density and spatial gradients of
S
will turn out to define the fluid velocity. With these definitions the current components become

J^{t}=-\sqrt{-g}\,g^{tt}\frac{W^{2}}{m}\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad J^{i}=\sqrt{-g}\,g^{ij}\frac{W^{2}\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}}{m^{2}}\,\partial_{j}S\,.

(4.14)

In terms of the new variables the lagrangian density (
4.8
) becomes

\displaystyle\Bigl\langle{\cal L}_{\rm ax}\Bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sqrt{-g}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}W^{2}g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}\,\partial_{\nu}\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}+\overline{V}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}(\phi,n_{i})+\tfrac{1}{2}\,m_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}^{2}(\phi,n_{i})\,(\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}-{\mathfrak{a}}_{\rm ad})^{2}-\frac{W^{2}\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}}{m}g^{tt}\dot{S}\phantom{\frac{\Phi}{2}}\right.

(4.15)

\displaystyle\quad\left.+\tfrac{1}{2}\left(W^{2}g^{tt}+\frac{\overline{W}^{2}m^{2}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}}{\overline{m}^{2}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}}\right)\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}+\frac{W^{2}}{2m^{2}}g^{ij}\left(\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}\partial_{i}S\,\partial_{j}S+\frac{\partial_{i}\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}\,\partial_{j}\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}}{4\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}}\right)+\cdots\right],

so the field equation obtained by varying
S
is just the conservation law (
4.12
) once (
4.14
) is used. The equation obtained by varying
\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}
on the other hand gives the ‘Hamilton-Jacobi’ equation

\frac{W^{2}}{m}g^{tt}\dot{S}=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(W^{2}g^{tt}+\frac{\overline{W}^{2}m^{2}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}}{\overline{m}^{2}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}}\right)+\frac{W^{2}}{2m^{2}}g^{ij}\partial_{i}S\,\partial_{j}S-\frac{W^{2}}{8m^{2}\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}}g^{ij}\partial_{i}\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}\,\partial_{j}\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}-D_{i}\left(\frac{W^{2}}{4m^{2}}g^{ij}\,\frac{\partial_{j}\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}}{\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}}\right)

(4.16)

from which the evolution of the axion fluid velocity
\vec{v}_{\mathfrak{a}}
is obtained below. Here
D_{i}
denotes the covariant derivative built using the Christoffel symbols of the spatial metric
g_{ij}
.

4.2
A useful class of metrics

For practical applications – such as to cosmology and to the solar system – we now restrict to a special class of metrics. In later sections we specialize further to small perturbations around cosmological spacetimes. Consider therefore a metric of the following form:

{\rm d}s^{2}=-{\rm d}t^{2}\Bigl[1+2\Phi(x)\Bigr]+a^{2}(t)\Bigl[1-2\Psi(x)\Bigr]\delta_{ij}\,{\rm d}x^{i}\,{\rm d}x^{j}\,,

(4.17)

In our later applications we take
\Phi,\,\Psi\ll 1
and explore perturbations about a spatially flat FRW metric, but we do not do so immediately. For solar-system applications we take
a=1
.

4.2.1
Current conservation

For these choices the current (
4.14
) becomes

J^{t}=\frac{a^{3}(1+\Phi-3\Psi)W^{2}\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}}{m(1+2\Phi)}\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad{\bf J}=\frac{a(1+\Phi-3\Psi)W^{2}\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}}{m^{2}(1-2\Psi)}\,\nabla S\,,

(4.18)

where spatial indices are now raised, lowered and contracted using the flat metric
\delta_{ij}
. Current conservation then implies the axion ‘number density’ and fluid velocity

n_{\mathfrak{a}}:=\frac{W^{2}\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}}{m}\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad{\bf v}_{\mathfrak{a}}:=\frac{\nabla S}{am}\,,

(4.19)

satisfy

\partial_{t}\left[\left(\frac{1+\Phi-3\Psi}{1+2\Phi}\right)n_{\mathfrak{a}}\right]+3Hn_{\mathfrak{a}}+\frac{1}{a}\nabla\cdot\left[\left(\frac{1+\Phi-3\Psi}{1-2\Psi}\right)n_{\mathfrak{a}}{\bf v}_{\mathfrak{a}}\right]=0,

(4.20)

where (as usual)
H=\dot{a}/a
and we use the expressions for
J^{\mu}
implied by eqs. (
4.14
) and (
4.19
). The definitions (
4.19
) are motivated by the observation that they make eq.(
4.20
) resemble the conservation equation for a fluid with a conserved number density
n_{\mathfrak{a}}
and velocity
{\bf v}_{a}
.

Specialized to a homogeneous configuration
\Phi=\Psi=0
and
n_{\mathfrak{a}}=\overline{m}_{\mathfrak{a}}(t)
the conservation law (
4.20
) implies the background density satisfies

\overline{n}_{\mathfrak{a}}=\frac{C}{a^{3}}\,,

(4.21)

for constant
C
. In the next section we compute the energy density for this axion fluid and show that its evolution agrees with the behaviour derived microscopically in (
3.22
) for the energy density in homogeneous oscillations, showing how these are captured in the fluid language by the background evolution.

For weak gravitational fields eqs. (
4.18
) can be linearized in
\Phi
and
\Psi
to become

J^{t}\simeq a^{3}(1-\Phi-3\Psi)\,n_{\mathfrak{a}}\quad\hbox{and}\quad{\bf J}\simeq a^{2}(1+\Phi-\Psi)\,n_{\mathfrak{a}}{\bf v}_{{\mathfrak{a}}}\qquad\hbox{(weak gravity)}\,

(4.22)

where the metric perturbations contribute to a small change in current and density.

4.2.2
Euler equation

Using the metric (
4.17
) in (
4.16
) (after multiplying through by
mg_{tt}/W^{2}
) gives the evolution equation

\displaystyle\dot{S}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-m\left[\Phi+\tfrac{1}{2}(1+2\Phi)\left(\frac{\overline{W}^{2}m^{2}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}}{W^{2}\overline{m}^{2}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}}-1\right)\right]

(4.23)

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{1}{2ma^{2}}\left(\frac{1+2\Phi}{1-2\Psi}\right)\left\{\nabla S\cdot\nabla S-\frac{\nabla\cdot\left[W^{2}\sqrt{(1+2\Phi)(1-2\Psi)}(\nabla\sqrt{\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}})\right]}{W^{2}\sqrt{\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}}\sqrt{(1+2\Phi)(1-2\Psi)}}\right\}\,,

where we recall the definition
m=\overline{m}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}/\overline{W}
made in eq. (
4.2
). Eq. (
4.23
) allows
\dot{S}
to be eliminated in future expressions. The explicit expression for
m^{2}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}/\overline{m}^{2}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}
appearing here is

\frac{m^{2}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}}{\overline{m}^{2}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}}=\frac{1+(\rho_{e0}/\rho_{e\,{\rm th}})+(\rho_{m0}/\rho_{m\,{\rm th}})}{1+(\overline{\rho}_{e0}/\rho_{e\,{\rm th}})+(\overline{\rho}_{m0}/\rho_{m\,{\rm th}})}\,,

(4.24)

with
\rho_{i0}:=A(\phi)\,m_{i0}n_{i}
as defined just below eq. (
3.2
) and we recall
\rho_{i\,{\rm th}}=m_{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}\Lambda_{i}^{2}
. In appendix
A
we use this to show the relationship between the phase of the axion field and the energy of axion particles recovers the standard expression expected for particles in a galactic halo.

Taking the gradient of (
4.23
) gives the Euler equation governing the evolution of the axion fluid velocity,
{\bf v}_{\mathfrak{a}}
. Written in terms of the fluid momentum

{\bf p}_{\mathfrak{a}}=m(t)\,{\bf v}_{\mathfrak{a}}\,,

(4.25)

this reads

\partial_{t}{\bf p}_{\mathfrak{a}}+H\,{\bf p}_{\mathfrak{a}}=-\frac{m}{a}\nabla\left\{\Phi+\tfrac{1}{2}(1+2\Phi)\left[\left(\frac{\overline{W}^{2}m^{2}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}}{W^{2}\overline{m}^{2}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}}\right)-1\right]+\tfrac{1}{2}v_{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}\left(\frac{1+2\Phi}{1-2\Psi}\right)+\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle Q}\right\}\,,

(4.26)

where
v_{\mathfrak{a}}
is the modulus of
{\bf v}_{\mathfrak{a}}
and we define the ‘quantum pressure’
\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle Q}
by

\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle Q}:=-\frac{1}{2m^{2}a^{2}}\left(\frac{1+2\Phi}{1-2\Psi}\right)\frac{\nabla\cdot\left[W^{2}\sqrt{(1+2\Phi)(1-2\Psi)}\;(\nabla\sqrt{\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}})\right]}{W^{2}\sqrt{\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}}\sqrt{(1+2\Phi)(1-2\Psi)}}\,.

(4.27)

4.2.3
Axion stress-energy

We next collect expressions for the axion fluid’s stress energy –
c.f.
(
4.9
) — in terms of the fluid variables. Writing (as above)
\langle{\cal L}_{\rm ax}\rangle=\sqrt{-g}\;{\cal P}
and specializing (
4.15
) to a homogeneous background
\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}(t)
and to the metric (
4.17
) gives

\displaystyle{\cal P}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{W^{2}\dot{\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}}^{2}}{2(1+2\Phi)}-\frac{W^{2}(\nabla{\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}})^{2}}{2(1-2\Psi)}-\overline{V}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}(\phi,n_{i})-\tfrac{1}{2}\,m_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}^{2}(\phi,n_{i})\,(\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}-{\mathfrak{a}}_{\rm ad})^{2}-\frac{W^{2}\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}}{m(1+2\Phi)}\dot{S}

(4.28)

\displaystyle\quad+\tfrac{1}{2}\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}\left(\frac{W^{2}}{1+2\Phi}-\frac{\overline{W}^{2}m^{2}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}}{\overline{m}^{2}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}}\right)-\frac{W^{2}}{2m^{2}a^{2}(1-2\Psi)}\left(\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}\nabla S\cdot\nabla S+\frac{\nabla\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}\cdot\nabla\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}}{4\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{W^{2}\dot{\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}}^{2}}{2(1+2\Phi)}-\frac{W^{2}(\nabla{\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}})^{2}}{2(1-2\Psi)}-\overline{V}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}(\phi,n_{i})-\tfrac{1}{2}\,m_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}^{2}(\phi,n_{i})\,(\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}-{\mathfrak{a}}_{\rm ad})^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\frac{\nabla\cdot\left[W^{2}\sqrt{(1+2\Phi)(1-2\Psi)}\;\nabla\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}\right]}{4m^{2}a^{2}\sqrt{(1+2\Phi)(1-2\Psi)^{3}}}\,,

where the second equality eliminates
\dot{S}
using (
4.23
) and
\overline{V}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}(\phi,n_{i})
is given by (
3.7
).

This expression is useful when evaluating the energy density,
\rho_{\rm ax}:=-{T_{t}^{t}}_{{\mathfrak{a}}(\rm eff)}=-g_{tt}T^{tt}_{{\mathfrak{a}}(\rm eff)}
, of the axion fluid. Using (
4.9
) this becomes

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm ax}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-g^{tt}\left\{W^{2}\dot{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}}^{2}+W^{2}\psi^{\star}\psi+\frac{W^{2}}{2m}\left[i\psi^{\star}\left(\dot{\psi}-\frac{\dot{m}}{m}\psi\right)+\hbox{h.c.}\right]\right\}-{\cal P}

(4.29)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+2\Phi}\left\{W^{2}\dot{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}}^{2}+W^{2}\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}-\frac{W^{2}\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}}{m}\dot{S}\right\}-{\cal P}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{W^{2}\dot{\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}}^{2}}{2(1+2\Phi)}+\frac{W^{2}(\nabla{\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}})^{2}}{2(1-2\Psi)}+\overline{V}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}(\phi,n_{i})+\tfrac{1}{2}\,m_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}^{2}(\phi,n_{i})\,(\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}-{\mathfrak{a}}_{\rm ad})^{2}+\frac{W^{2}\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}v_{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}}{2(1-2\Psi)}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}W^{2}(1+\Phi)}{(1+2\Phi)}+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}W^{2}\left[\left(\frac{\overline{W}^{2}m^{2}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}}{W^{2}\overline{m}^{2}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}}\right)-1\right]+\frac{W^{2}\nabla\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}\cdot\nabla\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}}{8m^{2}a^{2}\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}(1-2\Psi)}\,.

The first four terms of this expression describe the energy of the background evolution
\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}
while the rest capture the energy of the fluid describing fast axion oscillations. Notice in particular that for homogeneous backgrounds (for which gradients and
{\bf v}_{\mathfrak{a}}
vanish) in the absence of a gravitational field the fluid part of the energy becomes

\overline{\rho}_{f}=\overline{\varrho}_{\mathfrak{a}}\overline{W}^{2}=m(t)\,\overline{n}_{\mathfrak{a}}=\frac{Cm(t)}{a^{3}}\,,

(4.30)

where the second equality uses (
4.19
) for
n_{\mathfrak{a}}
and the last equality uses current conservation in the form given in (
4.21
). As advertised, this precisely captures the energy of the underlying homogeneous oscillation given in (
3.25
). Notice also that (
4.30
) implies the usual matter-like dependence
\overline{\rho}_{f}\propto a^{-3}
in the late universe when the axion mass is time-independent, but instead implies
\overline{\rho}_{f}\propto a^{-9/2}
in the earlier universe when
m\propto\sqrt{\rho_{m0}}\propto a^{-3/2}
.

The other components of the energy momentum-tensor are obtained in a similar way. Eq. (
4.9
) reveals the energy flux/momentum density is given by

U^{i}_{\rm ax}:=T^{ti}_{{\mathfrak{a}}(\rm eff)}=W^{2}g^{tt}g^{ij}\Bigl\langle\dot{\mathfrak{a}}\,\partial_{j}{\mathfrak{a}}\Bigr\rangle=W^{2}g^{tt}g^{ij}\left[\dot{\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}}\,\partial_{j}\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}+\frac{1}{2m}\Bigl(i\psi^{\star}\partial_{j}\psi+\hbox{h.c.}\Bigr)\right]\,.

(4.31)

The fluid part of this expression takes a familiar form once expressed in terms of the potentials
\Phi
and
\Psi
and the fluid variables:

{\bf U}_{\rm ax}=\frac{W^{2}}{a^{2}(1+2\Phi)(1-2\Psi)}\left(\frac{\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}\nabla S}{m}\right)=\frac{W^{2}\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}{\bf v}_{\mathfrak{a}}}{a(1+2\Phi)(1-2\Psi)}\,.

(4.32)

The shear tensor similarly becomes

\displaystyle T^{ij}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W^{2}g^{ik}g^{jl}\left[\partial_{k}\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}\,\partial_{l}\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}+\frac{1}{2m^{2}}\Bigl(\partial_{k}\psi^{\star}\,\partial_{l}\psi+\hbox{h.c.}\Bigr)\right]+g^{ij}{\cal P}

(4.33)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{W^{2}}{a^{4}(1-2\Psi)^{2}}\left[\partial^{i}\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}\,\partial^{j}\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}+\frac{1}{m^{2}}\left(\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}\,\partial^{i}S\,\partial^{j}S+\frac{\partial^{i}\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}\,\partial^{j}\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}}{4\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}}\right)\right]+\frac{{\cal P}\delta^{ij}}{a^{2}(1-2\Psi)}\,,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{W^{2}}{a^{4}(1-2\Psi)^{2}}\left[\partial^{i}\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}\,\partial^{j}\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}+a^{2}\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}v_{{\mathfrak{a}}}^{i}v_{{\mathfrak{a}}}^{j}+\frac{\partial^{i}\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}\,\partial^{j}\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}}{4m^{2}\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}}\right]+\frac{{\cal P}\delta^{ij}}{a^{2}(1-2\Psi)}\,,

with its characteristic dependence on
\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}v^{i}_{\mathfrak{a}}v^{j}_{\mathfrak{a}}
when the fluid is incompressible.

4.3
Linear perturbations

We next delve into the wonderous world of cosmological perturbations by linearizing the above expressions about a homogeneous cosmological background solution. To this end we regard both gravitational potentials
\Phi
and
\Psi
to be small and restrict to dilaton and matter configurations that are similarly linearized around homogeneous backgrounds

\phi=\overline{\phi}(t)+\delta\phi(x)\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad n_{i}=\overline{n}_{i}(t)+\delta n_{i}(x)\,.

(4.34)

We perform a similar split for the slowly evolving axion oscillations,
\psi=\overline{\psi}+\delta\psi
, by choosing

n_{\mathfrak{a}}=\overline{n}_{\mathfrak{a}}+\delta n_{\mathfrak{a}}\qquad\hbox{and so}\qquad\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}=\overline{\varrho}_{\mathfrak{a}}(t)\,\Bigl[1+\delta_{\mathfrak{a}}(x)\Bigr]

(4.35)

and assuming the axion fluid velocity
{\bf v}_{\mathfrak{a}}
vanishes in the background. If interested in post-Newtonian solar-system applications a natural choice for the size of the fluid speed is
v_{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}\sim\Phi
but in cosmology we instead drop
v_{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}
terms relative to linearized fluctuations. (In the remainder of this section we keep the
v_{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}
terms to keep our discussion general, but drop them in our later numerical evolution.)

We assume that the background evolution is well-described by adiabatic evolution – as is justified because subdominant terms in
H/\overline{m}_{\rm ax}
are negligible – and so
\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}={\mathfrak{a}}_{\rm ad}
everywhere. This implies that
\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}
inherits the fluctuation structure (
4.34
) of the fields
\phi
and
n_{i}
, with

\displaystyle\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}(x)={\mathfrak{a}}_{\rm ad}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}_{\rm ad}(t)+\frac{\frac{\delta\rho_{e0}}{\rho_{e\,{\rm th}}}\left[{{\mathfrak{a}}_{e}}+\frac{\overline{\rho}_{m0}}{\rho_{m\,{\rm th}}}({{\mathfrak{a}}_{e}}-{{\mathfrak{a}}_{m}})\right]+\frac{\delta\rho_{m0}}{\rho_{m\,{\rm th}}}\left[{{\mathfrak{a}}_{m}}+\frac{\overline{\rho}_{e0}}{\rho_{e\,{\rm th}}}({{\mathfrak{a}}_{m}}-{{\mathfrak{a}}_{e}})\right]}{\left(1+\frac{\overline{\rho}_{e0}}{\rho_{e\,{\rm th}}}+\frac{\overline{\rho}_{m0}}{\rho_{m\,{\rm th}}}\right)^{2}}

(4.36)

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}_{\rm ad}(t)+\frac{\left(\frac{\delta\rho_{m0}}{\rho_{m\,{\rm th}}}\right){{\mathfrak{a}}_{m}}}{\left(1+\frac{\overline{\rho}_{m0}}{\rho_{m\,{\rm th}}}\right)^{2}}\qquad\hbox{(when $\rho_{e0}/\rho_{e\,{\rm th}}$ is negligible)}\,,

where we recall
\rho_{i\,{\rm th}}=m_{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}\Lambda_{i}^{2}
and note that
\delta\rho_{i0}
is given in terms of
\delta\phi
and
\delta n_{i}
by

\delta\rho_{i0}=\left(-\beta\delta\phi+\frac{\delta n_{i}}{\overline{n}_{i}}\right)\overline{\rho}_{i0}\,.

(4.37)

The same is also true for the axion mass, which becomes

\displaystyle\frac{m^{2}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}}{\overline{m}^{2}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1+(\rho_{e0}/\rho_{e\,{\rm th}})+(\rho_{m0}/\rho_{m\,{\rm th}})}{1+(\overline{\rho}_{e0}/\rho_{e\,{\rm th}})+(\overline{\rho}_{m0}/\rho_{m\,{\rm th}})}\simeq 1+\frac{\delta\rho_{e0}}{\rho_{e\,{\rm th}}}+\frac{\delta\rho_{m0}}{\rho_{m\,{\rm th}}}+\cdots

(4.38)

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle 1+\frac{\delta\rho_{m0}}{\rho_{m\,{\rm th}}}\qquad\hbox{(when $\rho_{e0}/\rho_{e\,{\rm th}}$ is negligible)}\,,

The expansion of the current (
4.18
) then is
J^{\mu}=\overline{J}^{\mu}+\delta J^{\mu}
with

\overline{J}^{t}=a^{3}\overline{n}_{\mathfrak{a}}\,,\quad\delta J^{t}=a^{3}\Bigl[\delta n_{\mathfrak{a}}-\overline{n}_{\mathfrak{a}}(\Phi+3\Psi)\Bigr]\,,

(4.39)

and

\overline{J}^{i}=0\,,\quad\delta J^{i}=a^{2}\,\overline{n}_{\mathfrak{a}}v^{i}_{{\mathfrak{a}}}\,,

(4.40)

to linear order in the fluctuations. Current conservation therefore implies both
\overline{n}_{\mathfrak{a}}\propto a^{-3}
and

\partial_{t}\Bigl\{a^{3}\Bigl[\delta n_{\mathfrak{a}}-\overline{n}_{\mathfrak{a}}(\Phi+3\Psi)\Bigr]\Bigr\}+a^{2}\overline{n}_{\mathfrak{a}}\nabla\cdot{\bf v}_{\mathfrak{a}}=0\,.

(4.41)

Keeping lowest nontrivial order in the fluctuations in the Hamilton-Jacobi equation (
4.23
) gives

\dot{S}\simeq-m(t)\Bigl[\tfrac{1}{2}v_{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}+\Phi+\Phi_{\phi}+\Phi_{\rho}+\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle Q}\Bigr]\,,

(4.42)

where the time-dependent mass appearing here is

m(t)=\frac{\overline{m}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}}{\overline{W}}=\frac{m_{\mathfrak{a}}}{\overline{W}}\left(1+\frac{\overline{\rho}_{e0}}{\rho_{e\,{\rm th}}}+\frac{\overline{\rho}_{m0}}{\rho_{m\,{\rm th}}}\right)\,,

(4.43)

and we define the generalized potentials

\Phi_{\phi}:=-\frac{W^{\prime}}{W}\delta\phi\,,\quad\Phi_{\rho}:=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\delta\rho_{e0}}{\rho_{e\,{\rm th}}}+\frac{\delta\rho_{m0}}{\rho_{m\,{\rm th}}}\right)\,,

(4.44)

and the quantum pressure
\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle Q}
defined in (
4.27
) linearizes to:

\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle Q}\simeq-\frac{1}{2m^{2}a^{2}}\left[\frac{\nabla^{2}\sqrt{\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}}}{\sqrt{\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}}}\right]=\frac{1}{4m^{2}a^{2}}\left[-\frac{\nabla^{2}\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}}{\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}}+\frac{(\nabla\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}})^{2}}{2\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}}\right]\,.

(4.45)

In terms of these potentials the relationship between
\delta n_{\mathfrak{a}}
and
\delta_{\mathfrak{a}}
obtained from (
4.19
) is

\delta n_{\mathfrak{a}}=\overline{n}_{\mathfrak{a}}\left(\delta_{\mathfrak{a}}+\frac{\delta W^{2}}{\overline{W}^{2}}\right)=\overline{n}_{\mathfrak{a}}\left(\delta_{\mathfrak{a}}-2\Phi_{\phi}\right)

(4.46)

and the evolution of the axion fluid velocity is given by the linearized Euler equation (
4.26
), which states

\partial_{t}{\bf p}_{\mathfrak{a}}+H\,{\bf p}_{\mathfrak{a}}=-\frac{m}{a}\nabla\Bigl[\Phi+\tfrac{1}{2}v_{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}+\Phi_{\phi}+\Phi_{\rho}+\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle Q}\Bigr]\,,

(4.47)

for
{\bf p}_{\mathfrak{a}}=m(t)\,{\bf v}_{\mathfrak{a}}
.

The quantities relevant to the Einstein equations are the linearized energy density and stress energy, given explicitly in eqs. (
4.29
) and (
4.33
). For the homogeneous background these give the background pressure

\overline{{\cal P}}_{\rm ax}=\tfrac{1}{2}\overline{W}^{2}\dot{\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}}^{2}-\overline{V}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}(\overline{\phi},\overline{n}_{i})-\tfrac{1}{2}\,m_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}^{2}(\overline{\phi},\overline{n}_{i})\,(\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}-{\mathfrak{a}}_{\rm ad})^{2}\simeq-\overline{V}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}(\overline{\phi},\overline{n}_{i})\,,

(4.48)

which only receives contributions from
\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}
and not from the oscillatory fluid (as expected for a matter-type equation of state). The approximate equality here uses the adiabatic approximation that drops subdominant powers of
H/m
and for which
\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}\simeq{\mathfrak{a}}_{\rm ad}
.

The background energy density similarly is

\overline{\rho}_{\rm ax}=\tfrac{1}{2}\overline{W}^{2}\dot{\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}}^{2}+\overline{V}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}(\overline{\phi},\overline{n}_{i})+\tfrac{1}{2}\,m_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}^{2}(\overline{\phi},\overline{n}_{i})\,(\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}-{\mathfrak{a}}_{\rm ad})^{2}+\overline{\varrho}_{\mathfrak{a}}\overline{W}^{2}\simeq\overline{V}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}(\overline{\phi},\overline{n}_{i})+\overline{\varrho}_{\mathfrak{a}}\overline{W}^{2}\,,

(4.49)

with the approximate equality again assuming adiabatic evolution for
\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}
. The last term of this expression can be recognized as the background fluid energy density
\overline{\rho}_{f}=\overline{\varrho}_{\mathfrak{a}}\overline{W}^{2}
given in (
4.30
).

The fluctuation in energy density in
\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}
and in the oscillatory fluid is obtained by linearizing (
4.29
) about the background. In the adiabatic approximation the contribution from the fluctuations in
\overline{{\mathfrak{a}}}={\mathfrak{a}}_{\rm ad}
inherited from fluctuations in
\phi
and
n_{i}
are

\delta\rho_{\rm ad}\simeq\partial_{\phi}\overline{V}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}\,\delta\phi+\partial_{n_{i}}\overline{V}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}\,\delta n_{i}\,,

(4.50)

The fluctuation in the fluid energy density is similarly

\delta_{f}:=\frac{\delta\rho_{f}}{\overline{\rho}_{f}}=\tfrac{1}{2}v_{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}+\delta_{\mathfrak{a}}-\Phi-\Phi_{\phi}+\Phi_{\rho}\,.

(4.51)

We can now work at the level of linear perturbations and obtain the Euler equation in terms of the effective energy density of the axion. Using (
4.20
) the conservation equation is

\dot{\delta}_{f}-3\dot{\Psi}=\dot{\Phi}_{\phi}+\dot{\Phi}_{\rho}-\frac{1}{a}\Theta_{a},

(4.52)

where
\Theta_{a}:=\nabla\cdot{\bf v}_{\mathfrak{a}}
is the axion fluid’s velocity expansion and the two terms
\dot{\Phi}_{\phi}+\dot{\Phi}_{\rho}
on the right-hand side have their origins in the perturbations of the axion mass:
\partial_{t}\left(\frac{\delta m}{m}\right)
. This reproduces the usual result in the traditional case when the potentials
\Phi_{\phi}
and
\Phi_{\rho}
are absent.

We now turn to the Euler equation which reads

{{\dot{\bf v}}_{\mathfrak{a}}}+\left(H+\frac{\dot{m}}{m}\right){\bf v}_{\mathfrak{a}}=-\frac{1}{a}\nabla(\Phi+\Phi_{\phi}+\Phi_{Q}+\Phi_{\rho})\,,

(4.53)

the divergence of which then gives

\dot{\Theta}_{\mathfrak{a}}+\left({H}+\frac{\dot{m}}{m}\right)\Theta_{\mathfrak{a}}=-\frac{1}{a}\nabla^{2}(\Phi+\Phi_{\phi}+\Phi_{Q}).

(4.54)

This, together with the conservation equation, gives the growth equation for
\delta_{f}
in the quasi static approximation

\ddot{\delta}_{f}+\left({H}+\frac{\dot{m}}{m}\right)\dot{\delta}_{f}-\frac{1}{a}\nabla^{2}(\Phi+\Phi_{\phi}+\Phi_{Q}+\Phi_{\rho})=0,

(4.55)

which can be expanded once the Poisson equation and the Klein-Gordon equation have been used. Notice in particular that the Hubble friction is enhanced when the axion mass varies. Moreover gravity is modified, with the three new potentials
\Phi_{\rho}
,
\Phi_{\phi}
and
\Phi_{Q}
supplementing the gravitational potential
\Phi
.

5
Cosmological Perturbations

In the previous section the focus was mainly on the axion fluid, but we now collect all the components of the Universe and their interactions within the axio-dilaton models. The full description of the cosmology is in particular obtained by specifying the coupling between the different universal fluids. In particular, since dark matter and the baryons couple to the axion fluid we wish to investigate their mutual exchange of energy and momentum.

The exchange of energy-momentum between the components of the cosmological fluid means each component is not separately conserved on its own. But for the fluids we do not have an action formulation for the equations of motion and so cannot as easily read off how their energy density changes as a function of what the scalar fields are doing. This is most simply derived by computing the rate with which the scalar sector loses or emits energy as a function of the fluids and then using the overall conservation of stress energy, as guaranteed by the Bianchi identities as applied to the Einstein equation

G_{\mu\nu}=8\pi G(T^{\phi}_{\mu\nu}+T^{\mathfrak{a}}_{\mu\nu}+T^{{\rm tot}}_{\mu\nu}),

(5.1)

to infer how much the fluid sector absorbs or emits, as we now argue in detail.

The right-hand side of (
5.1
) separates out the parts of the slowly moving scalar energy-momentum tensors that are independent of the presence of matter, with

T^{\phi}_{\mu\nu}=\partial_{\mu}\phi\,\partial_{\nu}\phi-g_{\mu\nu}\Bigl[\tfrac{1}{2}\,(\partial\phi)^{2}+V_{\rm dil}(\phi)\Bigr],

(5.2)

and

T^{\mathfrak{a}}_{\mu\nu}=W^{2}(\phi)\partial_{\mu}{\mathfrak{a}}\,\partial_{\nu}{\mathfrak{a}}-g_{\mu\nu}\Bigl[\tfrac{1}{2}\,W^{2}(\phi)\,(\partial{\mathfrak{a}})^{2}+V_{\rm ax}({\mathfrak{a}})\Bigr]\,,

(5.3)

where
V_{\rm dil}
and
V_{\rm ax}
are as defined in and below eq. (
2.6
). The quantity
T^{\rm tot}_{\mu\nu}
denotes the rest of the total stress energy including both the axion fluid and the matter-dependent couplings with the axion and dilaton. Because the scalar stress energies exclude the scalar couplings to matter they are not covariantly conserved when evaluated at the solutions to the equations of motion: neither
D^{\mu}T^{\phi}_{\mu\nu}
or
D^{\mu}T^{\mathfrak{a}}_{\mu\nu}
vanish in general, though their nonzero values are easily computed using the scalar field equations. But the Bianchi identity
D^{\mu}G_{\mu\nu}=0
ensures the total stress energy is covariantly conserved and so
D^{\mu}T^{\rm tot}_{\mu\nu}=-D^{\mu}T^{\phi}_{\mu\nu}-D^{\mu}T^{\mathfrak{a}}_{\mu\nu}
, and this allows the fluid response to the scalar fields to be computed.

5.1
Einstein Equations

We start by writing down the background and perturbed Einstein equations in the presence of the coupled axion fluid, starting from (
5.1
).

The background Einstein equation is the Friedmann equation for the background metric,

3H^{2}M_{p}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\dot{\bar{\phi}}^{2}+W^{2}(\bar{\phi})\dot{\bar{{\mathfrak{a}}}}^{2}\right]+V_{\rm dil}(\bar{\phi})+V_{\rm ax}(\bar{{\mathfrak{a}}})+\tfrac{1}{2}\bar{\rho}_{f0}+\bar{\rho}_{{\textnormal{tot}}},

(5.4)

where
V_{\rm dil}
and
V_{\rm ax}
are respectively defined in (
2.7
) and (
2.9
), repeated here for convenience:

V_{\rm ax}(\bar{{\mathfrak{a}}})=\tfrac{1}{2}m_{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}\bar{{\mathfrak{a}}}^{2},\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad V_{\rm dil}(\bar{\phi})=U(\bar{\phi})\,e^{-\lambda\bar{\phi}/M_{p}}\,,

(5.5)

and
\bar{\rho}_{\textnormal{tot}}=\bar{\rho}_{m}+\bar{\rho}_{b}+\bar{\rho}_{r}
is the sum of the energy density of dark matter, baryonic matter, and additional relativistic species also present during the late time cosmology, respectively.

The quantity
\overline{\rho}_{f0}
appearing in (
5.4
) is not quite the same as the axion fluid density
\rho_{f}
given in (
4.30
), differing because here we do not include the matter coupling in the axion energy momentum tensor (lumping it instead into the energy density of matter). As a result it involves the mass
m_{\mathfrak{a}}
rather than the matter-dependent mass
\overline{m}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}(t)
:

\overline{\rho}_{f0}:=\Bigl\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\overline{W}^{2}\dot{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\,m_{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}\Bigr\rangle\,.

(5.6)

By contrast
\overline{\rho}_{f}
contains the energy density of the rapid axion oscillations, with

\Bigl\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\overline{W}^{2}\dot{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}\Bigr\rangle=\Bigl\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\,\overline{m}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}^{2}(t)\,{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}\Bigr\rangle=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\overline{\rho}_{f}=\tfrac{1}{2}\overline{W}^{2}\overline{\varrho}_{\mathfrak{a}}=\tfrac{1}{2}m(t)\,\overline{n}_{\mathfrak{a}}\,,

(5.7)

and so

\overline{\rho}_{f0}=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\overline{\rho}_{f}\left[1+\frac{m_{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}}{m_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}^{2}(t)}\right]=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\overline{\rho}_{f}\left(1+\frac{1}{1+{\cal C}_{e}+{\cal C}_{m}}\right),

(5.8)

where the last equality uses (
3.5
) to evaluate
m_{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}/m_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}^{2}(t)
.

At the level of linear perturbations the 00–component of the Einstein equations is

\displaystyle\left[\frac{k^{2}}{a^{2}}\Psi+3H\dot{\Psi}\right]M_{p}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\dot{\bar{\phi}}\,\delta\dot{\phi}+\tfrac{1}{2}\left\{2\Phi\left[V(\phi)+V(\bar{{\mathfrak{a}}})+\left(W^{2}+\frac{m_{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}}{m^{2}(t)}\right)\frac{\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}}{2}\right]+V,_{\phi}\delta\phi\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad+\tfrac{1}{2}WW,_{\phi}\delta\phi\,\dot{\bar{{\mathfrak{a}}}}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{4}a^{2}W^{2}\dot{\delta\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}}\left[1+\frac{k^{2}}{2m^{2}(t)a^{2}}+\frac{m_{\mathfrak{a}}^{2}}{m^{2}_{{\mathfrak{a}}\,{\rm eff}}(\rho_{m})}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad=-\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\delta\rho_{\textnormal{tot}}+2\Phi\bar{\rho}_{\textnormal{tot}}+\frac{W^{2}\delta\rho_{m}}{2m^{2}(t)\Lambda_{m}^{2}}\bar{\varrho}_{\mathfrak{a}}\right),

(5.9)

and the 0i–component reads

\displaystyle k^{2}(H\Phi+\dot{\Psi})M_{p}^{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}k^{2}\dot{\bar{\phi}}\,\delta\phi+\tfrac{1}{2}aW^{2}\bar{\varrho}_{\mathfrak{a}}\Theta_{\mathfrak{a}}+\tfrac{1}{2}a\bar{\rho}_{\textnormal{tot}}\Theta_{\textnormal{tot}}.

(5.10)

These agree with the result obtained by starting from the perturbed axio-dilaton equations in
[
38
,
40
,
36
]
and averaging terms. Here
\Theta_{\textnormal{tot}}=\partial_{i}v^{i}_{{\textnormal{tot}}}
is the divergence of the total velocity field,
{\bf v}_{tot}
, of all other fluids. These equations are used to solve for the evolution of cosmological perturbations.

5.2
Coupled axion-matter dynamics

As mentioned earlier, the Bianchi identity
D^{\mu}G_{\mu\nu}=0
allows us to derive the non-conservation equations for each fluid (with details of the calculations given in appendix
B
). For nonrelativistic matter we can neglect the pressure and so write

T^{b}_{\mu\nu}=\rho_{b}u_{\mu}^{b}u_{\nu}^{b}\quad\hbox{and}\quad T^{m}_{\mu\nu}=\rho_{m}u_{\mu}^{m}u_{\nu}^{m},

(5.11)

where
b
and
m
respectively denote ordinary matter (the combined baryon/electron fluid) and CDM. The fluid 4-velocities separately satisfy
u^{2}_{b}=u^{2}_{m}=-1
and so also
u^{\mu}_{b}D_{\nu}u_{\mu,b}=u^{\mu}_{m}D_{\nu}u_{\mu,m}=0
.

For Dark Matter the arguments of the appendix teach us that the Dark Matter fluid satisfies

\dot{\rho}_{m}u_{\nu}^{m}+3h_{m}\rho_{m}u_{\nu}^{m}+\rho_{m}\dot{u}_{\nu}^{m}=\left(-\frac{\beta}{M_{p}}\partial_{\nu}\phi+\left\langle\frac{\partial{\cal U}_{m}({\mathfrak{a}})}{\partial{\mathfrak{a}}}\partial_{\nu}{\mathfrak{a}}\right\rangle\right)T^{m}~,

(5.12)

where
T^{m}:=g^{\mu\nu}T^{m}_{\mu\nu}
and we drop the axion-electron coupling
{\cal U}_{e}
in comparison to the axion-CDM coupling
{\cal U}_{m}
. Here the overdot denotes
\dot{\rho_{a}}=u_{a}^{\mu}D_{\mu}\rho_{a}
and lower-case
h_{a}
denotes the local Hubble rate
3h_{a}=D_{\mu}u^{\mu}_{a}
for
a=m
or
b
, emphasizing that each fluid has an individualized time derivative and experiences its own Hubble flow. The brackets
\langle\,\cdots\rangle
denote the average over fast axion oscillations.

Because we work in an approximation where the baryon fluid moves in lock-step with the electron fluid, the baryons satisfy an equation identical to (
5.12
) but with
u^{m}_{\mu}\to u^{b}_{\mu}
,
\rho_{m}\to\rho_{b}
,
h_{m}\to h_{b}
and
T^{m}\to T^{b}
but
{\cal U}_{m}\to m_{e}\,{\cal U}_{e}/m_{\scriptscriptstyle N}
(see eq. (
2.20
) for why the electron/nucleon mass ratio appears).

Contracting (
5.12
) with
u^{\nu}_{m}
gives the rate of energy change due to the Dark Matter fluid’s interaction with the scalars. For Dark matter this becomes

\dot{\rho}_{m}+3h_{m}\rho_{m}=-\frac{\beta}{M_{p}}\rho_{m}\dot{\phi}+u^{\nu}_{m}\left\langle\frac{\partial{\cal U}_{m}({\mathfrak{a}})}{\partial{\mathfrak{a}}}\partial_{\nu}{\mathfrak{a}}\right\rangle a\rho_{m}\,.

(5.13)

Projecting (
5.12
) onto the directions orthogonal to the fluid evolution can be done with the tensor

h^{m}_{\mu\nu}=g_{\mu\nu}+u^{m}_{\mu}u^{m}_{\nu}

(5.14)

leading to the Euler equations

\dot{u}^{\mu}_{m}-\frac{\beta\dot{\phi}}{M_{p}}u^{\mu}+u^{\nu}_{m}\left\langle\frac{\partial{\cal U}_{m}({\mathfrak{a}})}{\partial{\mathfrak{a}}}\partial_{\nu}{\mathfrak{a}}\right\rangle u^{\mu}_{m}=\frac{\beta}{M_{p}}\partial^{\mu}\phi-\left\langle\frac{\partial{\cal U}_{m}({\mathfrak{a}})}{\partial{\mathfrak{a}}}\partial^{\mu}{\mathfrak{a}}\right\rangle.

(5.15)

The average over the rapid oscillations in these equations gives the following expression in terms of the slow axion fluid variables

\left\langle\frac{\partial{\cal U}_{m}({\mathfrak{a}})}{\partial{\mathfrak{a}}}\partial_{\mu}{\mathfrak{a}}\right\rangle=\frac{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}-{{\mathfrak{a}}_{m}}}{\Lambda_{m}^{2}}\partial_{\mu}{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}}+\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{m}^{2}}\partial_{\mu}{\left(\frac{\rho_{\mathfrak{a}}}{m^{2}(t)}\right)},

(5.16)

where the total density is involved through the axion adiabatic solution’s dependence on
\rho_{m}
and
\rho_{e}
.

Again an almost identical line of argument goes through for the baryon fluid, with the counterparts to (
5.13
) and (
5.15
) obtained by making the substitutions
u^{m}_{\mu}\to u^{b}_{\mu}
,
h_{m}\to h_{b}
,
\rho_{m}\to\rho_{b}
and
{\cal U}_{m}\to m_{e}\,{\cal U}_{e}/m_{\scriptscriptstyle N}
. Once this is done the analogue of (
5.16
) then is

\left\langle\frac{\partial{\cal U}_{e}({\mathfrak{a}})}{\partial{\mathfrak{a}}}\partial_{\mu}{\mathfrak{a}}\right\rangle=\frac{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}-{{\mathfrak{a}}_{m}}}{\Lambda_{e}^{2}}\partial_{\mu}{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}}+\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{e}^{2}}\partial_{\mu}{\left(\frac{\rho_{\mathfrak{a}}}{m^{2}(t)}\right)}\,.

(5.17)

The above equations – together with the Einstein and scalar-field equations – are the fully relativistic equations of the baryon-CDM-axion fluid system.

5.3
Linear perturbations

We next linearize these equations to compute the evolution of small inhomogeneous fluctuations around a homogeneous background. At the background level both
h_{b}
and
h_{m}
are simply given by the background Hubble scale
H
and time derivatives for the two fluids coincide with the cosmic time derivatives, leading to

\dot{\bar{\rho}}_{m}+3H\bar{\rho}_{m}=\left[-\frac{\beta}{M_{p}}\dot{\bar{\phi}}+\frac{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}-{\mathfrak{a}}_{m}}{\Lambda_{m}^{2}}\dot{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}}+\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{m}^{2}}\partial_{t}{\left(\frac{\bar{\varrho}_{\mathfrak{a}}}{m^{2}(t)}\right)}\right]\bar{\rho}_{m},

(5.18)

and

\dot{\bar{\rho}}_{b}+3H\bar{\rho}_{b}=\left\{-\frac{\beta}{M_{p}}\dot{\bar{\phi}}+\frac{m_{e}}{m_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}\left[\frac{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}-{\mathfrak{a}}_{m}}{\Lambda_{e}^{2}}\dot{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}}+\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{e}^{2}}\partial_{t}{\left(\frac{\bar{\varrho}_{\mathfrak{a}}}{m^{2}(t)}\right)}\right]\right\}\bar{\rho}_{b},

(5.19)

where we assume
m_{e}/m_{\scriptscriptstyle N}\ll 1
. We note in passing that these equations are unusual inasmuch as they depend on
\bar{{\mathfrak{a}}}(t)=\bar{{\mathfrak{a}}}(\bar{\rho}_{m},\bar{\rho}_{e})
, that depends only on the cold dark matter density and the electron density since the axion does not couple directly to protons and neutrons. In principle there are three conservation equations, one each for CDM, for the electrons and for the baryons, but in practice – and in the numerical work – we work in the limit where the baryons and electrons are strongly coupled to one another and so do not distinguish between the electrons and the nuclei. The Boltzmann code we use below also does not separate the different components of the baryons.

At the perturbative level, the increased number of terms makes for more work. But nothing can stop us now! So using

h_{b,m}=H-H\Phi-\dot{\Phi}+\frac{\Theta_{b,m}}{{\mathfrak{a}}}

where

\Theta_{b,m}=\partial_{i}v^{i}_{b,m}

and writing the 4-velocity
u^{\mu}_{b,m}
as
\{u^{0}_{b,m},u^{i}_{b,m}\}=\{1-\Phi,a^{-1}v^{i}_{b,m}\}
in Newtonian gauge, we find that the Dark Matter conservation equation reads

\displaystyle\dot{\delta\rho}_{m}+

\displaystyle 3H\delta\rho_{m}-3\dot{\Phi}\bar{\rho}_{m}+\frac{1}{a}\partial_{i}v^{i}_{m}\bar{\rho}_{m}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\beta}{M_{p}}\bar{\rho}_{m}\dot{\delta\phi}+\left[-\frac{\beta}{M_{p}}\dot{\bar{\phi}}+\frac{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}-{\mathfrak{a}}_{m}}{\Lambda_{m}^{2}}\dot{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}}+\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{m}^{2}}\partial_{t}{\left(\frac{\bar{\varrho}_{\mathfrak{a}}}{m^{2}(t)}\right)}\right]\delta\rho_{m}+F_{m},

while conservation of the baryon fluid is

\displaystyle\dot{\delta\rho}_{b}+

\displaystyle 3H\delta\rho_{b}-3\dot{\Phi}\bar{\rho}_{b}+\frac{1}{a}\partial_{i}v^{i}_{b}\bar{\rho}_{b}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\beta}{M_{p}}\bar{\rho}_{b}\dot{\delta\phi}+\left[-\frac{\beta}{M_{p}}\dot{\bar{\phi}}+\frac{m_{e}}{m_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}\left(\frac{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}-{\mathfrak{a}}_{m}}{\Lambda_{e}^{2}}\dot{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}}(\bar{\rho})+\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{e}^{2}}\partial_{t}{\left(\frac{\bar{\varrho}_{\mathfrak{a}}}{m^{2}(t)}\right)}\right)\right]\delta\rho_{b}+F_{b},

where the
F
terms involve the variation of the axion source term with respect to the total density

F_{m}=\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{m}^{2}}\partial_{t}\left(\frac{\delta\rho_{\mathfrak{a}}}{m^{2}(t)}\right)\bar{\rho}_{m}\quad\hbox{and}\quad F_{b}=\frac{m_{e}}{2m_{\scriptscriptstyle N}\Lambda_{e}^{2}}\partial_{t}\left(\frac{\delta\rho_{\mathfrak{a}}}{m^{2}(t)}\right)\bar{\rho}_{b}.

(5.22)

Now when we define
\delta_{b,m}={\delta\rho_{b,m}}/{\bar{\rho}_{b,m}}
, we find that the source terms almost cancel and we get

\dot{\delta}_{m}-3\dot{\Phi}+\frac{\Theta_{m}}{a}=-\frac{\beta}{M_{p}}\dot{\delta\phi}+\partial_{t}\left(\frac{\delta\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}}{2\Lambda_{m}^{2}m^{2}(t)}\right),\quad

(5.23)

and

\dot{\delta}_{b}-3\dot{\Phi}+\frac{\Theta_{b}}{a}=-\frac{\beta}{M_{p}}\dot{\delta\phi}+\partial_{t}\left(\frac{m_{e}\delta\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}}{2m_{\scriptscriptstyle N}\Lambda_{e}^{2}m^{2}(t)}\right).

(5.24)

Notice the new term from the exchange of energy between matter and the axion fluid. This term mimics a similar term in
-\partial_{0}\Phi_{\rho}
appearing in (
4.52
). This confirms that the matter and axion fluids exchange energy and interact via a term in
\rho_{m}\rho_{\mathfrak{a}}/2m^{2}(t)\Lambda_{m}^{2}
. Notice too that in the axion fluid equation an extra term in
\partial_{0}\Phi_{\phi}
implies that some of the energy flows into the dilaton perturbations too.

Finally using the time derivative along the fluid flow

\dot{u}_{b,m}^{i}=\dot{v}_{b,m}^{i}+\partial^{i}\Phi+H\frac{v_{b,m}^{i}}{a},

(5.25)

we find the Euler equations for the matter fluids

\displaystyle\dot{v}_{m}^{i}+\Biggl[H-\frac{\beta{\dot{\bar{\phi}}}}{M_{p}}+\frac{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}-{{\mathfrak{a}}_{m}}}{\Lambda_{m}^{2}}\dot{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}}(\bar{\rho})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{m}^{2}}\partial_{t}{\left(\frac{\bar{\varrho}_{\mathfrak{a}}}{m^{2}(t)}\right)}\Biggr]v_{m}^{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{a}\left[-\partial^{i}\Phi+\frac{\beta}{M_{p}}\partial^{i}\phi-\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{m}^{2}}\partial^{i}\left(\frac{\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}}{m^{2}(t)}\right)\right],

and

\displaystyle\dot{v}_{b}^{i}+\Biggl\{H-\frac{\beta{\dot{\bar{\phi}}}}{M_{p}}+\Biggl[\frac{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}-{{\mathfrak{a}}_{m}}}{\Lambda_{e}^{2}}\dot{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}}(\bar{\rho})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{e}^{2}}\partial_{t}{\left(\frac{\bar{\varrho}_{\mathfrak{a}}}{m^{2}(t)}\right)}\Biggr]\frac{m_{e}}{m_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}\Biggr\}v_{b}^{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{a}\left[-\partial^{i}\Phi+\frac{\beta}{M_{p}}\partial^{i}\phi-\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{e}^{2}}\partial^{i}\left(\frac{\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}}{m^{2}(t)}\right)\frac{m_{e}}{m_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}\right].

Taking the divergence of the Euler equations we have

\displaystyle\dot{\Theta}_{m}+\Biggl[H-\frac{\beta\dot{\phi}}{M_{p}}+\frac{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}-{{\mathfrak{a}}_{m}}}{\Lambda_{m}^{2}}\dot{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{m}^{2}}\partial_{t}{\left(\frac{\bar{\varrho}_{\mathfrak{a}}}{m^{2}(t)}\right)}\Biggr]\Theta_{m}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\Delta\Phi}{a}+\frac{\beta}{M_{p}}\frac{\Delta\phi}{a}-\frac{1}{2a\Lambda_{m}^{2}}\Delta\left(\frac{\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}}{m^{2}(t)}\right),

and

\displaystyle\dot{\Theta}_{b}+\Biggl[H-\frac{\beta\dot{\phi}}{M_{p}}+\Biggl[\frac{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}-{{\mathfrak{a}}_{m}}}{\Lambda_{e}^{2}}\dot{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{e}^{2}}\partial_{t}{\left(\frac{\bar{\varrho}_{\mathfrak{a}}}{m^{2}(t)}\right)}\Biggr]\frac{m_{e}}{m_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}\Biggr]\Theta_{b}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\Delta\Phi}{a}+\frac{\beta}{M_{p}}\frac{\Delta\phi}{a}-\frac{1}{2a\Lambda_{e}^{2}}\Delta\left(\frac{\varrho_{\mathfrak{a}}}{m^{2}(t)}\right)\frac{m_{e}}{m_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}.

The last term in both of these equations is a quantum pressure term from the axion fluid in the evolution of matter. This parallels the quantum pressure term in the axion fluid equations.

5.4
Dilaton evolution

The final ingredient is the dilaton equation in the presence of the coupled axion fluid, which reads

\ddot{\bar{\phi}}+3H\dot{\bar{\phi}}-WW,_{\phi}\left(\dot{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}}^{2}+\bar{\varrho}_{\mathfrak{a}}\right)=-\partial_{\phi}V_{\rm eff}(\bar{\phi}).

(5.30)

at the background level
whilst its perturbed counterpart is given by

\displaystyle\ddot{\delta\phi}+3H\dot{\delta{\phi}}+\left[\frac{k^{2}}{a^{2}}-\left(W,_{\phi}^{2}+W,_{\phi\phi}W\right)\dot{\bar{{\mathfrak{a}}}}^{2}+\left(W,_{\phi}^{2}-W,_{\phi\phi}W\right)\bar{\varrho}_{\mathfrak{a}}+V_{,\phi\phi}\right]\delta\phi

\displaystyle-\dot{\bar{\phi}}\left(\dot{\Phi}+3\dot{\Psi}\right)-WW,_{\phi}\left(1-\frac{k^{2}}{2m^{2}(t)}\right)\delta\rho_{\mathfrak{a}}+2\Phi\left(V,_{\phi}-WW,_{\phi}\bar{\rho}_{\mathfrak{a}}\right)

\displaystyle=\beta\bar{\rho}_{m}(\delta_{m}+2\Phi)+\beta\bar{\rho}_{b}(\delta_{b}+2\Phi)+WW,_{\phi}\frac{\delta\rho_{m}}{m^{2}\Lambda_{m}^{2}}\bar{\rho}_{\mathfrak{a}}.

(5.31)

We next turn to integrating these background and perturbation equations, specializing to the choices mentioned at the beginning of this paper for both
W(\phi)
and
V(\phi)
.

7
Conclusion

Dynamical dark energy requires the existence of very light fields since it is only scalars with masses smaller than the Hubble scale
H
that move over cosmological timescales, as would be required to be responsible for the time-dependence. On the other hand, it is also generic that a dimensionless scalar field that is gravitationally coupled to a scalar potential of size
V=v^{4}\mathfrak{u}(\phi)
– where
\mathfrak{u}(\phi)
is a generic order-unity function – acquires a mass of order
m\sim v^{2}/M_{p}\sim H
. So given the fact that the Dark Energy density is so small, it perhaps might not be a surprse to find very light scalars at play in the recent history of the universe.
16
16

16

Why
the Dark Energy density is so small in the first place is another question, though the hope is that Yoga models
[
38
]
are a step in the right direction for understanding this.

Of theories with light scalar fields those with only one scalar are special because only for these is it impossible to have two-derivative sigma-model interactions. Two-derivative interactions are special because they scale with energy in the same way as do the two-derivative interactions of GR and this allows them to compete with GR without also jeopardizing the low-energy expansion that justifies working within the semiclassical approximation. The axio-dilaton models we study here provide a minimal, well-motivated example of what the dynamics of such fields can look like.

If such scalars couple to matter with gravitational strength, they generically lead to large deviations from GR in the solar system and so would be ruled out by observations. This can be prevented either by having the scalars couple more weakly or by invoking screening mechanisms whereby the effective coupling of the light fields to matter is dynamically reduced for the macroscopic objects like the Sun or the Earth. A screening mechanism of this sort is known to exist for the kinds of axio-dilaton models we consider
[
39
]
, that uses the same kinetic sigma-model couplings between axion and dilaton that make the models interesting in the first place. Screening proceeds by coupling matter to the axion so that the axion field shifts from its vacuum value in the presence of matter. This implies that the axion field necessarily has gradients when going from outside to inside dense bodies, and it is the interaction of the dilaton with this axion gradient that drives the effective coupling of the dilaton to matter to small values.

In this paper we explore what the cosmology of these models looks like in a scenario where the dilaton of an axio-dilaton pair is both responsible for dark energy and is screened locally. Although the axion has a scalar potential and couples to ordinary matter it is not itself the Dark Matter, which we assume also couples to the axion. As might be expected. the presence of matter-axion and Dark Matter-axion couplings can change cosmology fairly dramatically because of axion interactions with the ambient matter and Dark Matter environments encountered in the early universe. We find in particular that an unexpected bonus of the axion-matter couplings is that the axion energy density behaves like a temporary cosmological constant at high redshift, but one that naturally evaporates once the local matter and Dark Matter density become sufficiently small.

The fact that axion cosmology with a matter-dependent potential can resemble Early Dark Energy (EDE) is interesting because early dark energy has been proposed as a possible alleviation mechanism for the Hubble tension
[
78
,
44
]
. Although it is not completely clear if this tension needs solving (
i.e.
whether it is physical or due to some uncontrolled astrophysical error), the possibility of linking screening of the light dark energy field – here the dilaton – to the existence of early dark energy generated by its partner the axion seems worthy of note. We find the amount of EDE produced in this way cannot be very large, being limited for some choices of model parameters by a tachyonic instability in the dilaton sector triggered by the kinetic coupling to the axion, or by possible indirect imprints on the CMB or the growth of structure through the specific matter couplings generating the EDE.

Of the options we explore it is Dark Energy potentials of the Albrecht-Skordis type that allow the largest contributions of EDE as a fraction of the total energy of the cosmic fluids, of order 5%. For these kinds of potentials we find that the growth of structure can be
reduced
when increasing the coupling to matter of the dilaton. This follows from the fact that the coupling to matter displaces the dilaton field from the vicinity of the minimum of its potential, in the process increasing the Hubble rate during structure formation and therefore the friction term in the growth equation. This is a background effect which counterbalances the natural tendency of scalar-tensor models to increase the growth by the presence of an attractive scalar interaction between CDM particles, as observed in e.g.
[
79
,
69
]
. This also could have interesting phenomenological consequences (though we here leave these for future work).

We do not here try to match the hints for a time-dependent equation of state
\omega_{0}

[

If such a model could be built, some of the features of the model presented here such as the existence of early dark energy would remain. On the other hand, features such as a decrease in structure growth would depend on the dilaton potential which could for instance be taken of the thawing type instead of the frozen kind. Appendix B
The Bianchi Identity

The Bianchi identity implies the non-conservation equation

D^{\mu}T^{{\textnormal{tot}}}_{\mu\nu}=-D^{\mu}(T^{\phi}_{\mu\nu}+T^{\mathfrak{a}}_{\mu\nu}).

(B.1)

Explicitly we have

D^{\mu}T^{\phi}_{\mu\nu}=\left(\frac{\beta}{M_{p}}T^{m}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial W^{2}(\phi)}{\partial\phi}(\partial{\mathfrak{a}})^{2}\right)\partial_{\nu}\phi,

(B.2)

and

D^{\mu}T^{\mathfrak{a}}_{\mu\nu}=-\frac{\partial U({\mathfrak{a}})}{\partial{\mathfrak{a}}}\partial_{\nu}{\mathfrak{a}}T^{m}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial W^{2}(\phi)}{\partial\phi}(\partial{\mathfrak{a}})^{2}\partial_{\nu}\phi,

(B.3)

from which we obtain

D^{\mu}T^{\textnormal{tot}}_{\mu\nu}=-\frac{\beta}{M_{p}}T^{\textnormal{tot}}\partial_{\nu}\phi+\frac{\partial{\cal U}_{m}({\mathfrak{a}})}{\partial{\mathfrak{a}}}\partial_{\nu}{\mathfrak{a}}T^{m}+\frac{\partial{\cal U}_{b}({\mathfrak{a}})}{\partial{\mathfrak{a}}}\partial_{\nu}{\mathfrak{a}}T^{b}.

(B.4)

Notice the absence of the mixing term
W^{2}(\phi)
. Indeed, the kinetic mixing leads to the exchange of energy between the axion and the dilaton. When considering the total energy momentun tensor the two scalars, this exchange cancels out.
We now average over the fast axion oscillations and get

\left\langle\partial_{\mu}{\mathfrak{a}}\frac{\partial{\cal U}_{i}({\mathfrak{a}})}{\partial{\mathfrak{a}}}\right\rangle=\frac{(\bar{\mathfrak{a}}(\rho)-{{\mathfrak{a}}_{m}})}{\Lambda_{i}^{2}}\partial_{\mu}\bar{\mathfrak{a}}+\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{i}^{2}}\partial_{\mu}|\psi|^{2}.

(B.5)

Using the fluid description for the axion fluctuations we have

\left\langle\partial_{\mu}{\mathfrak{a}}\frac{\partial{\cal U}_{i}({\mathfrak{a}})}{\partial{\mathfrak{a}}}\right\rangle=\frac{(\bar{\mathfrak{a}}(\bar{\rho})-{{\mathfrak{a}}_{m}})}{\Lambda_{i}^{2}}\partial_{\mu}\bar{\mathfrak{a}}+\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{i}^{2}}\partial_{\mu}\left(\frac{\rho_{\mathfrak{a}}}{m^{2}(t)}\right),

(B.6)

We can split the non-conservation equation into the CDM and the baryonic equations as
D^{\mu}T^{\textnormal{tot}}_{\mu\nu}=D^{\mu}T^{m}_{\mu\nu}+D^{\mu}T^{b}_{\mu\nu}
where

D^{\mu}T^{m}_{\mu\nu}=-\frac{\beta}{M_{p}}T^{m}\partial_{\nu}\phi+\frac{\partial{\cal U}_{m}({\mathfrak{a}})}{\partial{\mathfrak{a}}}\partial_{\nu}{\mathfrak{a}}T^{m},

(B.7)

and

D^{\mu}T^{b}_{\mu\nu}=-\frac{\beta}{M_{p}}T^{b}\partial_{\nu}\phi+\frac{\partial{\cal U}_{b}({\mathfrak{a}})}{\partial{\mathfrak{a}}}\partial_{\nu}{\mathfrak{a}}T^{b}.

(B.8)

These equations will give us the non-conservation of matter and the geodesic equations leading to the Euler equation for the baryon and CDM fluids as determined in the main text.
