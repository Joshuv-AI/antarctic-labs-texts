Pre-treatment of cabozantinib prior to hypofractionated radiotherapy increases immunomodulatory effects and tumor size reduction in a 4T1 breast cancer murine model
Authors: Fatemeh Keshavarz, Ameneh Kouchaki, Mohsen Soltanshahi, Mahmoud Reza Jaafari, Ahmad R. Mafi, Mahdi Shabani, Seyed Amir Jalali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Radiotherapy is one of the treatments used in breast cancer, which, in addition to immunomodulatory effects, causes immunosuppressive effects such as the increase in myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs). Cabozantinib is a multikinase inhibitor that targets multiple tyrosine kinases and has been shown to reduce MDSCs. This study aimed to combine radiotherapy with cabozantinib to improve treatment efficacy and promote a more favorable tumor microenvironment for the antitumor immune response. The 4T1 breast cancer cell line was injected into the flanks of BALB/c mice. Tumor-bearing mice were treated with a defined oral dose of cabozantinib and hypofractionated radiotherapy (2 × 10 Gy). Mice in different study groups were examined for the frequencies of TCD8+, TCD4+, Treg, MQ, and MDSC in tumor tissue, spleen, and tumor-draining lymph node by flow cytometry. Besides, the expression level of PD-L1 on tumor cells was checked by flow cytometry. Tumor size and survival of treated mice were also compared with those of the control group. Cabozantinib combined with radiotherapy, and especially cabozantinib in the early days of tumor induction, caused changes in the abundance of immune system cells, including an increase in the abundance of CD8 + T cells, an increase in the ratio of M1/M2 macrophages, and a decrease in Treg cells. Cabozantinib alone reduced the population of MDSC cells, and the population of these cells also decreased in the combination therapy group; however, this difference was not statistically significant. Combining cabozantinib with radiotherapy increased PD-L1 expression on the surface of tumor cells. Finally, combination therapy reduced tumor size and increased survival in mice. Our results showed that combining radiotherapy and cabozantinib reduced 4T1 tumor growth and enhanced anti-cancer immunomodulatory effects.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53530-3.

Introduction

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) poses several therapy obstacles due to the lack of expression of estrogen and progesterone receptors, as well as the HER2 protein. Due to the lack of these receptors, hormone-based therapies and HER2-targeted therapies are ineffective, largely limiting options to chemotherapy, surgery, and radiation therapy. Despite initial sensitivity to chemotherapy as the main treatment, TNBC often develops resistance, leading to recurrence and distant metastasis. TNBC is highly heterogeneous and aggressive, often showing rapid growth, high rates of metastasis, and a poorer prognosis compared to other breast cancers1,2.

Radiotherapy is a conventional cancer treatment that can destroy tumor cells directly and indirectly affect immune system cells3. Radiotherapy is classified based on the doses received (gray) and the number of doses (fraction). Conventional procedures require many fractions, resulting in a prolonged treatment process. In contrast, hypofractionated radiotherapy delivers higher doses in fewer radiation sessions. This approach can shorten treatment duration and has been shown to have immune-modulatory effects4,5. Radiotherapy induces immunogenic cell death (ICD) in tumor cells, which is followed by increased production of immunogenic markers, such as calreticulin and HMGB1, in the microenvironment. Finally, ICD improves tumor antigen uptake and presentation, as well as the immune responses6,7. Conversely, radiation has immunosuppressive effects by increasing myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), regulatory T cells (Tregs), and immunological checkpoint markers, such as programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1), at the tumor site. Targeting any of these immunosuppressants could be a feasible strategy to improve the therapeutic efficacy of radiotherapy8,9.

MDSCs have been found to be greatly expanded in peripheral blood circulation and are related to more severe symptoms and a poor prognosis in breast cancer patients10. They also contribute significantly to tumor angiogenesis by secreting matrix metalloproteinases such as MMP9. Targeting MDSCs in conjunction with other treatments predicts strong antitumor immunity in breast cancer11–13. Cabozantinib is a multi-targeted oral tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) that has been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration to treat a variety of malignancies14. This medicine targets several signaling pathways, including VEGF, MET, and AXL, which are used by tumor cells for angiogenesis, invasion, and metastasis15. This medication also inhibits MDSCs while increasing T and NK cell activation16,17. Given the importance of radiotherapy in the treatment of TNBC and its effects comparable to those of cancer vaccines, addressing the immunological limitations of this method can aid in the eradication of tumor cells. One of the problems of this treatment is the increase in MDSCs in breast cancer patients after radiation. Combining radiotherapy and cabozantinib may improve treatment efficacy and create a favorable tumor microenvironment for an anti-tumor immune response. Our previous studies on the efficacy of cabozantinib in reducing the number of MDSCs in murine breast cancer18 and the successful experience in treating the CT26 mouse colon cancer with hypofractionated radiotherapy19 In this study, we intend to evaluate cabozantinib in combination with radiotherapy in the 4T1 TNBC mouse model and investigate the antitumor effects of this combination.

Materials and methods

Materials and apparatus

Penicillin/streptomycin, RPMI-1640, and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were obtained from Gibco (Massachusetts, USA). Xylazine and ketamine were procured from Alfasan. Sofia, Bulgaria). Cell lines and animals were obtained from Pasteur Research Institute and Royan Institute (Tehran, Iran), respectively. Fluorescent-conjugated antibodies were purchased from BioLegend (San Diego, California, USA) and cabozantinib from Shanghai Alkynechem Co. (Shanghai, China). Finally, a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometry device (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, New Jersey, USA) and a Clinical Linear Accelerator with 6 MV X-rays (Zibo, Shandong Province, China) were used for radiotherapy.

Mice and cell lines

4T1 mouse breast cancer cells were purchased from the Pasteur Institute (Tehran, Iran) and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C, 5% CO2, and 95% humidity for optimal growth. Female BALB/c mice aged 6 to 8 weeks) Weight: 18–20 gr (were kept under conventional circumstances, including a 12 h light-dark cycle, stable temperature and humidity, and free access to food and water. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee and Research Advisory Committee at Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences (ethical code: IR.SBMU.AEC.1403.030).

Experimental cancer model establishment

4T1 was injected subcutaneously into the right flank of the mouse at a dose of 5 × 105 cells suspended in 65 µl of PBS. To minimize pain, BALB/c mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 12.5 mg/kg 2% xylazine and 100 mg/kg 10% ketamine before tumor inoculation. After the tumors reached a palpable size (day 7, average tumor volume = 58 ± 5.2 mm3 ), the mice were randomly divided into five study groups with ten mice in each group including cabozantinib (CBZ), radiotherapy (RT), treatment by cabozantinib followed by radiotherapy (CBZ + RT), radiotherapy and cabozantinib combination started simultaneously (RT + CBZ), and PBS vehicle injected control group (control) (Fig. 1). The day of tumor cell injection was considered day zero. At the end of the treatment period (day 25), four mice were euthanized to assess treatment efficacy, and six mice from each group were assigned to assess survival for 90 days. Mice were euthanized by gradual CO₂ inhalation followed by cervical dislocation to ensure death. CO₂ was delivered from a compressed gas cylinder at a flow rate of 4 L/min (~ 40% chamber volume per minute displacement) until death was confirmed. All methods are reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org).

Radiotherapy and treatment regimens

Radiation was given in two sessions on days 13 and 19 following tumor inoculation, when the tumor volume reached at least 200–300 cubic millimeters (2 × 10 Gy ). To alleviate discomfort or suffering prior to irradiation, the mice were sedated with xylazine and ketamine administered intraperitoneally. To maintain stability during radiotherapy and facilitate proper positioning within the irradiated field, the mice were placed in a plexiglass chamber; a 1.5 cm bolus was placed on the tumor, and a lead shield was used to protect the entire body except for the primary tumor area. This arrangement protects distant organs; the tumor-draining axillary lymph nodes (about 1.5–2 cm lateral) and spleen (about 3–4 cm contralateral) only get a small amount of scatter (< 5% dose). This makes sure that immune modulation reflects tumor-specific effects and cabozantinib pre-treatment. The Clinical Linear Accelerator, equipped with a 6 MV X-ray beam and a dose rate of 600 cGy/min, was used in accordance with the radiation treatment protocol. Radiotherapy was delivered using a 3 × 3 cm radiation field with a 5 mm margin, according to the treatment protocol shown in Fig. 1b. In the first group receiving cabozantinib alone, treatment with cabozantinib began one day after the tumors became palpable (on the eighth day) at a dose of 30 mg/kg orally and daily for a duration of 14 days18. The group receiving radiotherapy was irradiated on days 13 and 19. The 6-day interfraction interval is like a human schedule. It helps reoxygenate the body, stops repopulation, and boosts abscopal responses by maturing dendritic cells and expanding/replenishing CD8 + T cells between fractions. This is in line with what we saw with immune activation without TIL depletion4,20–22. In the groups that received combination therapy, the combination was initiated once, with cabozantinib as the priority, whereas in the other group, radiotherapy (day 13) was followed by cabozantinib (day 14).

Tumor volume and survival

Tumor size was measured twice weekly using calipers. The results were acquired by measuring the length and width of the tumor mass of each mouse and estimating the tumor volume using the formula: Tumor volume = (length × width2) × 0.5.

Six out of ten mice in each group were examined for 90 days to assess survival after being challenged with the tumor line and treated with medications. Any of the mice before the end of the research period were euthanized and withdrawn from the trial if they lost less than 15% of their starting weight, had difficulties obtaining water and food due to sickness and lethargy, or had a tumor volume larger than 2000 mm3.

Isolation of spleen cells and the tumor-draining lymph nodes

To assess immunological responses in 4T1-bearing mice following therapy on a specific schedule, four mice from each group were euthanized via spinal cord transection at the end of the treatment period (day 25). Using sterilized scissors, a one-centimeter transverse incision was created in the mouse abdomen, and then, with sterile tweezers, the mouse spleen and lymph node were removed and inserted in a petri plate containing RPMI-1640 medium. Single cells were prepared by passing the suspension through a 70-micrometer Falcon cell strainer (Biofil, Guangzhou, Guangdong, China). The spleen cells were treated with RBC lysis solution for 5 to 10 min and centrifuged at 1600 rpm for 5 min before removing the supernatant. The resultant pellet was mixed with RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% FBS before being processed for flow cytometry.

Isolation of infiltrating tumor cells

The tumor tissue was removed from the right flank of the mouse and cut into small pieces (day 25). Tumor digestion for single-cell isolation in RPMI-1640 medium containing collagenase type I (2 mg/ml) and DNase type I (10 units/ml) (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA) was performed at 37 °C for 1 h. The cells were then filtered through a 70 micrometer cell strainer and centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 10 min.

Staining of surface and intracellular markers for flow cytometry analysis

Fluorescence-conjugated antibodies were used to label cells from tumor tissue, spleen, and lymph node for the expression of cell surface markers CD45, CD4, CD25, CD11b, Ly6C, Ly6G, Gr-1, CD80, CD206, CD8, and PD-L1, and cytoplasmic marker FOXP3. Conjugated antibodies were APC anti-mouse CD45, FITC anti-mouse CD45, PerCP anti-mouse CD4, PE anti-mouse CD8, PE anti-mouse CD25, APC anti-mouse CD11b, PerCP anti-mouse CD11b, PE anti-mouse CD206, Alexa Fluor 488 anti-mouse CD80, PerCP anti-mouse Ly6C, BV421 anti-mouse Ly6G, PE anti-mouse Gr-1, and PE anti-mouse PD-L1. After staining the surface cell markers, the intracellular marker FOXP3 was labeled with the Alexa Fluor 488 anti-mouse FOXP3 antibody. To stain intracellularly, the surface markers from the previous step were fixed and permeabilized with the BioLegend True-Nuclear™ Transcription Factor Buffer Set. They were then stained with anti-mouse FOXP3 antibody for 30 min in the dark. It is worth noting that prior to all of these treatments, the cell solution was stained with the Zombie NIR fixable viability kit to identify live and dead cells. Live and CD45 + immune cells were selected to assess alterations in immune cells in the tumor, spleen, and lymph node. Then, the individual cells were gated, and the percentages of different immune cells in the control group were compared to those in the treatment groups using specific markers.

Statistical analysis

Following collection, data from FlowJo version 10.6 (FlowJo LLC, Ashland, Oregon, USA) were imported into SPSS version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA) and GraphPad Prism version 8.4.3 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA) for statistical analysis. First, the Wilk-Shapiro and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests were used to assess the normality of the data. Then, one-way ANOVA and the Kruskal–Wallis Test were used to compare the mean and median analyte values across groups. The Meier-Kaplan method was used to determine the mice’s survival rate. A P value of less than 0.05 was judged significant.

Results

A combination of hypofractionated radiotherapy and cabozantinib improves survival and reduces tumor size

Tumor growth was reduced in all treated study groups compared to the control group (Fig. 2a and b), but this reduction was statistically significant only in the groups receiving the combination therapy, and was most significant in the CBZ + RT group (mean ± SD: 310 ± 91.3 mm3) (p = 0.003). Radiotherapy and cabozantinib alone each produced a similar reduction in tumor growth (mean ± SD: 843.3 ± 119.4 mm3 and 960 ± 127.8 mm3, respectively).

Survival analysis showed that subjects receiving combination therapy had significantly improved survival compared with the control and monotherapy groups. Both the RT + CBZ and CBZ + RT groups showed delayed mortality onset and longer median survival times. Specifically, the 60-day survival rate in the combination therapy group) CBZ + RT and RT + CBZ ( was 33.3%, indicating that one-third of the treated subjects survived at this time point. In contrast, the survival rates in the other experimental groups were significantly lower (16.66% for radiotherapy and 0% for cabozantinib). At the end of the 60-day observation period, 2 mice remained in the CBZ + RT and RT + CBZ groups, indicating greater therapeutic efficacy for this regimen. Statistical analysis using the Meier-Kaplan method showed that the differences observed in the survival distribution among the combination therapy group )CBZ + RT and RT + CBZ( and the other groups were significant (P = 0.001). Additionally, body weight did not change significantly across groups during treatment, which may indicate low toxicity of the combination treatments (Fig. 2d).

Combination therapy increased CD8 + cells in the spleen, lymph node, and tumor site

In this study, assessments were performed to investigate changes in the population of immune effector cells, particularly CD8 + cytotoxic T lymphocytes, in the spleen, lymph node, and tumor microenvironment. These changes were assessed in several experimental groups, and comparisons were made to the control group. As shown in Fig. 3, in all study groups, the population of these cells in the spleen, lymph nodes, and tumor tissue increased relative to the control group. Statistical analysis showed that in the spleen, the CBZ + RT combination treatment produced the most statistically significant increase in the number of CD8 + T cells (p = 0.005, fold change (FC) = 2.6). While other treatment groups also showed an increase in CD8 + cell population compared with the control group, these increases did not reach statistical significance.

In lymph nodes and tumor tissues, both groups receiving combination therapies (CBZ + RT and RT + CBZ) showed a significant increase in CD8 + T cell infiltration compared with the control condition (p = 0.005, FC = 1.76; p = 0.04, FC = 1.56 for lymph nodes; and p = 0.0001, FC = 4.1; p = 0.004, FC = 3.17 for tumor, respectively). Notably, the CBZ + RT group again showed the highest mean increase in CD8 + cells and outperformed the CBZ monotherapy group, with statistical significance (p = 0.03, FC = 1.66; p = 0.006, FC = 2.77, respectively). These findings show that the synergistic treatment of cabozantinib (CBZ) and radiotherapy (RT) promotes the recruitment or expansion of cytotoxic T lymphocytes in peripheral and tumor-associated lymphoid tissues. Similarly, the frequency of these cells was increased compared to the average frequency of TCD8 + cells in normal BalbC mice examined in previous studies (Table 2 S).

We also examined the overall frequency of CD45 + leukocytes. No significant differences were observed in the frequency of CD45 + immune cells in spleen or tumor tissue across treatment groups (Fig. S2). Although combination therapies trended toward increased lymphocyte infiltration in spleen, this did not reach statistical significance.”

None of the interventions significantly changed the CD4 + cell population

Subsequently, we examined changes in the CD4 + T lymphocyte population across the three tissues: spleen, lymph nodes, and tumor. Flow cytometry data showed that the proportion of CD4 + T cells in the spleen did not differ significantly across study groups compared with the control group. Notably, the groups receiving the combination treatments (RT + CBZ) and (CBZ + RT) showed a moderate increase in the percentage of CD4 + T cells compared with the control group. These increases, although indicative of potential immune modulation, did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.05).

In parallel, analysis of the lymph node immune environment mirrored the findings in the spleen, with the frequency of CD4 + T cells remaining largely unchanged across all experimental paradigms, as shown in Fig. 4. No statistically significant changes were observed.

In contrast, examination of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes showed an upward trend in CD4 + T-cell infiltration across all treatment groups compared with the control group. In particular, the CBZ + RT group showed the greatest increase in the CD4 + cell population in the tumor microenvironment. Despite this observed trend, statistical analyses confirmed that these increases were not significant (p > 0.05).

Significant reduction in spleen and lymph node Treg cell populations in the CBZ + RT group

The number of regulatory T cells (Tregs) in the tumor tissue, spleen, and local lymph nodes was also assessed. The frequency of Treg cells in the spleen was statistically significantly lower in the CBZ + RT group than in the control and other treatment groups, according to flow cytometry analysis (p = 0.042, FC = 3 ). Compared with the RT-alone or CBZ-alone groups, this decline was even more pronounced. The frequency of Tregs in the RT-only group increased non-significantly compared with the control group. Compared with the CBZ + RT combination group directly, however, this increase was statistically significant (p = 0.009, FC = 3.9).

Evaluation of Treg frequency in the lymph nodes revealed a relatively consistent pattern. All experimental groups showed a decrease in the Treg proportion compared with the control group; however, statistical significance was achieved only in the CBZ + RT group (p = 0.03, FC = 2).

Analysis of tumor-infiltrating Treg cells, as shown in Fig. 5, revealed no statistically significant differences among the treatment groups compared with the control group (p > 0.05).

Cabozantinib alone and in combination with radiotherapy reduced the population of MDSCs in the spleen, lymph node, and tumor site

To investigate the immunomodulatory effects of cabozantinib, radiotherapy, and their combination (CBZ + RT) on MDSCs, we measured total MDSC abundance in the spleen, lymph nodes, and tumor tissue, and analyzed their major subsets, polymorphonuclear MDSCs (PMN-MDSCs) and monocytic MDSCs (M-MDSCs).

In the spleen, flow cytometry data showed that the CBZ monotherapy group had the greatest and statistically significant decrease in total MDSC numbers compared with the control group (p = 0.039). Conversely, the RT group showed increased MDSC abundance compared with the control group. However, this increase did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.05). When the RT group was directly compared to the CBZ and CBZ + RT groups, this increase was statistically significant (p = 0.008 and p = 0.04, respectively). Although the combined CBZ + RT group showed a trend toward lower MDSC abundance than the control group, this difference was not statistically significant.

Similar trends were observed in lymph nodes and tumor tissue, in which the CBZ group consistently showed the greatest reduction in MDSC numbers. RT treatment resulted in a nonsignificant increase compared with the control group, whereas MDSC abundance was significantly higher than in the CBZ or CBZ + RT groups. Combination treatment again resulted in a nonsignificant decrease in MDSC in these compartments.

In addition, the analysis was extended to characterize the PMN-MDSC and M-MDSC subpopulations across all tissues examined. As shown in Fig. 6, no statistically significant differences or correlations were observed for any of the subgroups in any treatment group or anatomical location (p > 0.05). This result suggests that although the total MDSC abundance may be modulated by treatment, the relative distribution of MDSC subsets remained largely unchanged under the experimental conditions.

Significant increase in the ratio of macrophages (M1/M2) in the CBZ + RT group compared to other groups

To characterize macrophage populations and their polarization states in systemic and tumor-associated compartments, we used flow cytometry with the pan-macrophage marker CD11b, along with markers for classical (M1) and alternative (M2) activation phenotypes—namely, CD80 and CD206, respectively.

In the spleen, analysis revealed an overall increase in the M1/M2 macrophage ratio in all treatment groups compared to the control group. However, statistical significance was achieved exclusively in the CBZ + RT combination group (p = 0.044). The RT-only and CBZ-only groups showed a trend toward an increase, but it did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.05).

Similar results were observed in the lymph nodes, where the M1/M2 ratio increased in all treatment groups. The CBZ + RT group showed the greatest increase in this ratio, further indicating a shift toward M1 macrophages under combination therapy. Despite these upward trends, none of the increases in the groups were statistically significant.

In the tumor microenvironment, the M1/M2 ratio increased across all experimental groups compared with the control group. Importantly, this increase was only statistically significant in the CBZ + RT group (p = 0.038). The RT-only and CBZ-only groups showed non-significant increases in M1/M2 ratios, suggesting that monotherapies have more limited effects on macrophage polarization in tumors (Fig. 7).

Finally, the expression level of programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) on the surface of tumor cells was quantitatively evaluated in the different treatment groups. As shown in Fig. 8, an increase in PD-L1 expression was observed in all experimental groups compared to the control group. Discussion

Radiotherapy is an important approach in cancer treatment that can affect immune cells, and its effects can be both stimulatory and inhibitory. Similarly, in cancer treatment, tyrosine kinase inhibitors are a class of drugs used to treat various cancers, and cabozantinib is one of them. Our goal in this study was to combine these two approaches to cover the shortcomings of each. In the combination therapy groups, we started treatment with cabozantinib in one group at the beginning of tumor appearance and performed radiotherapy after one week, and in the other group, we started treatment with cabozantinib after the first week of radiotherapy. Initially, we observed that tumor size decreased significantly in both groups relative to the control group; however, in the CBZ + RT group, this decrease was greater. Similarly, the survival rates in these two groups were higher than in the other groups; after 60 days, 2 mice remained in the CBZ + RT group and 1 in the RT + CBZ group. In the only similar study available23, it was reported that the combination of 14 Gy radiotherapy and cabozantinib (60 mg/kg) reduced tumor size compared with the control group but did not differ from cabozantinib alone. In our study, in both combination therapy groups, the size reduction was significant compared to the control group, a size reduction was observed compared to the CBZ and RT groups, but it was not significant. Similarly, the present study differed from the study mentioned in that it used a dose of cabozantinib (30 mg/kg) and radiotherapy (2 × 10 Gy). Nevertheless, the current study has some notable differences compared to the previous one regarding the dosage of cabozantinib, the duration of treatment, and the regimen for radiation therapy. Additionally, a closer look at the immune microenvironment has been taken in our study, which has not been emphasized before.

We also examined the immune cell population in the spleen, lymph node, and tumor in 4T1 tumor-bearing mice. Regarding CD8 + cells, our results showed that their population was significantly increased in the combination therapy groups, with the largest increase observed in the CBZ + RT group. Interestingly, in lymph node and tumor tissues, the number of these cells in the CBZ + RT group was significantly higher than in the CBZ group. This increase could be due to the positive effects of radiotherapy and optimizing the results of CBZ alone, as various studies have shown that hypofractionated radiotherapy increases the population of CD8 + cells24,25.

We also examined the frequency of CD4 cells. No significant relationship was observed in the number of these cells across the study groups. Regarding radiotherapy, the reported effects on CD4 cell counts are controversial. Some studies have reported a decrease in these cells, whereas others have reported no effect of radiotherapy on their frequency. A meta-analysis showed that no significant change in peripheral blood CD4 + T-lymphocyte counts was observed after radiotherapy in breast and prostate cancers26–29. On the other hand, there are few studies on the effects of cabozantinib on CD4 cells, and most have been conducted in combination with other treatments. These studies have reported both an increase in CD4 + and the stability of these cells during treatment. The results of the present study, both in terms of radiotherapy and cabozantinib, indicate that each of them, as well as their combination, is ineffective on this cell population30–32.

Next, we gated Treg cells from CD4 + cells. Here, the results changed significantly. The results showed that the number of Treg cells in the spleen and lymph nodes was lower than in the control group. Similarly, in the spleen, the number of these cells increased significantly in the radiotherapy group compared with the CBZ + RT group. This result could indicate that cabozantinib reduces the number of Treg cells, as studies have reported. A number of preclinical and clinical studies have shown that cabozantinib can significantly reduce the percentage of Treg cells in both mouse models and clinical samples33–35, and this effect is further enhanced when combined with another treatment. In the present study, although cabozantinib reduced the percentage of these cells in the spleen and lymph nodes, the reduction was not statistically significant. However, given that a significant reduction in the number of these cells was observed when comparing the radiotherapy and combination groups, these effects can be attributed, in part, to cabozantinib.

The frequency of myeloid populations was also examined here. In the spleen, lymph node, and tumor, the M1/M2 macrophage ratio was increased across all groups, but this increase was significant only in the CBZ + RT group. In a study by Gu et al. using a 4T1 mouse model, cabozantinib was reported to cause a very small, insignificant increase in the M1/M2 macrophage ratio compared with the control group, consistent with the present study15. Another study showed that cabozantinib can reduce polarization toward the M1 phenotype. Cabozantinib may suppress M1 macrophage polarization by inhibiting the MET and AXL signaling pathways, which are critical for the activation of proinflammatory macrophages35,36. On the other hand, radiotherapy can have different effects on the M1/M2 ratio depending on the dose and type of cancer, but it seems that after radiation, radiation-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) such as HMGB1 are released, which activate toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) signals to favor M1 polarization29,37,38. The rise in intratumoral M1/M2 ratios indicates a shift from an immunosuppressive to an inflammatory, cytotoxic environment that is favorable for T cells. M1 macrophages are known for their ability to stimulate Th1 responses and CD8 + T cells, thereby contributing to the eradication of tumor cells through the production of IL-12, TNF, and other proinflammatory cytokines. M2 macrophages promote immunosuppressive responses and tumor progression by producing IL-10 and TGF-β. An elevated M1/M2 ratio has been related to successful prognoses in patients with tumors like gastric cancer and NSCLC, reflecting improved survival rates in these patients. The high M1/M2 ratio in the present study could indicate a shift towards an antitumor environment that is favorable for tumor eradication39–41.

Another myeloid cell examined in this study was MDSCs. Vascular damage following radiotherapy induces hypoxia in the tumor microenvironment (TME), which upregulates hypoxia-inducible factor-1 (HIF-1) and increases the secretion of soluble factors, including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), CCL26, and CXCL12 (SDF-1). These factors recruit MDSCs from the bone marrow to the peripheral blood and tumor site. RT also increases the expression of colony-stimulating factor 1 (CSF1), which enhances the proliferation and differentiation of myeloid cells into MDSCs42. Original studies have also confirmed the increase in MDSCs following radiotherapy43,44. Here too, the population of these cells increased in the radiotherapy group; however, when combined with cabozantinib, the population of MDSCs decreased, with the greatest decrease observed in the CBZ + RT group. Although this decrease was not statistically significant, it may indicate optimization of radiotherapy when combined with cabozantinib. As in the spleen, lymph node, and tumor in the CBZ-treated group, a significant decrease in MDSCs was observed. This decrease in MDSCs was also reported in the study of Khaki et al.18. We analyzed PMN-MDSCs and M-MDSCs individually, highlighting their distinctions in phenotype, suppressive mechanisms, and clinical relevance. PMN-MDSCs mainly use reactive oxygen species to stop the immune system, while M-MDSCs use nitric oxide and cytokines, which affect both local and systemic immune inhibition. In the 4T1 breast cancer model, both subsets gather in the tumor and lymph nodes, and L-selectin is very important for PMN-MDSC migration. PMN-MDSCs are frequently more prevalent and functionally suppressive in solid tumors, which is associated with poorer outcomes in various cancers11,45–47. We compared these groups to see how cabozantinib affects immunosuppressive pathways, but we didn’t find any important results. There are a few studies on this subject. In the study of Zhang et al., no significant difference was observed in the PMN-MDSC and M-MDSC populations after radiotherapy, and radiotherapy increased both subtypes43.

Furthermore, we found that radiotherapy and cabozantinib induced an elevation in PD-L1 expression, which was more pronounced in combination therapy. These results are consistent with prior studies indicating that radiotherapy induces PD-L1 upregulation in various cancers48–51. whereas cabozantinib can also promote indirect PD-L1 upregulation via its effect on the tumor microenvironment34.From these observations, it can be inferred that, apart from the promotion of antitumor immune activation by combination therapy, it can also induce immune escape through PD-L1 upregulation.

Consequently, the next course of action should involve the combination of cabozantinib and hypofractionated radiotherapy with anti-PD-L1 therapy. Such a strategy may help sustain the antitumor immune response while counteracting the compensatory increase in immune checkpoint signaling. Moreover, because our study used only one radiotherapy schedule, additional work is needed to determine whether other fractionation regimens could produce similar or even greater immunomodulatory effects when combined with cabozantinib. Moreover, future investigations should consider mechanisms other than flow cytometry to clarify further the impact of this combination on the tumor immune microenvironment.

Finally, the present findings support the overall tolerability of this therapeutic approach in the 4T1 model. Although combination treatment has the potential to increase toxicity, we did not observe significant weight loss or obvious exacerbation of adverse events in the combination group. This suggests that the proposed regimen may achieve immune modulation without adding unacceptable toxicity, at least under the experimental conditions used here.

Overall, our results indicate that continuous cabozantinib administration during hypofractionated radiotherapy may be important for maintaining a favorable immune microenvironment by limiting immunosuppressive cells and promoting effector immune responses. This interpretation also helps explain why timing of cabozantinib relative to radiotherapy may influence treatment outcome and supports further investigation of concurrent scheduling.

Conclusion

The present study showed that combining cabozantinib with radiotherapy produced significant immunomodulatory and antitumor effects, reducing tumor size and increasing survival in a 4T1 mouse model. Cabozantinib should be initiated prior to radiotherapy to improve efficacy. This study observed an increase in the frequency of CD8 + cells, an increase in the M1/M2 macrophage ratio, and a decrease in Treg cells. Changes in CD4 + cells were not significant; however, the MDSC population decreased slightly. Finally, increased PD-L1 expression was also among the immunosuppressive effects observed in this study and in the combination of radiotherapy and cabozantinib.
