Calcium silicate-diorthocarbonate is a missing link in deep-mantle chemistry of silicates and carbonates
Authors: Georgios Aprilis, Apostolos Pantousas, Alena Aslandukova, Ilya Kupenko, Xiang Li, Susanne Müller, Michael Hanfland, Leonid Dubrovinsky, Anna Pakhomova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Interactions between carbonates and silicates play a central role in many industrial and planetary processes. Although current knowledge is largely limited to silicate–carbonate exchange mechanisms, high-pressure compounds incorporating both carbonate and silicate units have long been anticipated. Here we report the synthesis of the high-pressure silicate–diorthocarbonate CaSiC2O7 at 122(2) GPa and 2800(200) K. CaSiC2O7 is based on the framework of vertex-sharing SiO6 octahedra and CO4 tetrahedra. Ab initio calculations are consistent with the experimental results and indicate dynamical and mechanical stability of this phase over a wide pressure range. These findings reveal an additional pathway for carbonate–silicate interaction in carbonate-rich deep-mantle environments. CaSiC2O7 has a density lower than that of major mantle minerals and exhibits comparatively low acoustic velocities, suggesting that silicate-carbonates may contribute to seismic anomalies near the core–mantle boundary.

Introduction

Carbonates and silicates are fundamental to both chemistry and planetary science. They are important technological materials with a wide range of applications, including cement production1, catalysis2, water treatment3, among others. As geomaterials, silicates are the most abundant minerals in the Earth’s crust and mantle, thereby defining its structure and evolution. In turn, carbonates play a key role in regulating the Earth’s climate and habitability, as they are responsible for long-term hosting and recycling of the CO2 through subduction4. In addition, both silicates and carbonates are often discussed as contributors to the Earth’s seismic anomalies5–8.

Despite the fact that carbon and silicon are both group IV elements, their oxides reveal remarkably different bond characteristics. At ambient conditions, the crystal chemistry of carbonates is driven by sp2 hybridization of carbon atomic orbitals, which allows the formation of [CO3]2− groups with triangular planar geometry. In contrast, silicon adopts sp³ hybridization in most of the silicates, forming four directional bonds in a tetrahedral geometry [SiO4]4−. Interaction between carbonates and silicates draws long-lasting attention, driven by interest in CO2 sequestration in silicate-based cements9,10, silicate-carbonate global cycle11 and fate of subducting carbonates upon contact with silicate mantle12–14. In industrial and geological processes, silicate-carbonate exchange reactions (primarily involving (Ca, Mg)SiO3 and (Ca, Mg)CO3) are dominant and silicate-carbonate compounds containing both carbon and silicon are rare. Known to date silicate-carbonate minerals15,16 are based upon CO3 and SiO4 polyhedra and usually formed at elevated temperatures (up to ~1300 K) and moderate pressures (up to ~1 GPa).

High pressure enables the formation of phases with properties and stoichiometries that differ from those commonly encountered in conventional solid-state chemistry17. Pressure-induced densification typically increases atomic coordination (often given as Roman numbers). This trend is well-established for silicates where silicon transitions from four-fold (SiO4, or Si[IV]) towards six-fold (SiO6, or Si[VI]) coordination18–20, with the occasional formation of transient five-fold SiO5 (Si[V]) state21–24. Similarly, a change from sp2- to sp3-configuration of carbon25,26 drives high-pressure chemistry of carbonates27–29 and results in the formation of CO4 tetrahedra (C[IV]). This tendency reduces the chemical difference between carbonates and silicates as shown experimentally30 and theoretically31 for the SixC1-xO2 compound at 18–26 GPa and 600–980 K. Silicate-carbonate compounds containing both C[IV] and Si[VI] have long been predicted to form at high pressure25, although, to our knowledge, no experimental observations have been reported. The very recent observation of the calcium silicon double carbonate Ca2Si(CO3)4, containing both SiO6 and CO3 groups, provides further evidence for the possibility of such a pressure-induced pathway32.

Herein, we report on the synthesis of CaSi[VI]C[IV]2O7 in a laser-heated diamond anvil cell. In situ synchrotron-based single-crystal X-Ray diffraction of a polycrystalline sample was employed to solve and refine its structure at 122 GPa. Ab initio density functional theory has been employed to extend the experimental observation, particularly to derive its equation of state and elastic constants. The synthesis of CaSiC2O7 provides experimental evidence for silicate-carbonates built from CO4 tetrahedra and SiO6 octahedra, as well as for a sp³-dicarbonate containing [C2O7]6− units. Our results demonstrate that interactions between carbonates and silicates under lowermost-mantle conditions can produce silicate-carbonate phases and suggest that such compounds may contribute to seismic anomalies in this region.

Results

Experimentally determined crystal structure of CaSiC2O7

The silicate-carbonate CaSi[VI]C[IV]2O7 was synthesized from a starting assemblage consisting of CaSiO3, CaCO3 and Fe0.945O (used as the laser absorber; see “Methods” for details) at 2800(200) K in a laser-heated diamond anvil cell, together with other phases listed in Table S1. The synthesis pressure was measured to be 122(2) GPa after quenching, and it could be up to 134 GPa during the heating (see “Methods”).

The CaSiC2O7 compound crystalizes in the orthorhombic space group Cmcm (#63) with a = 4.4750(2) Å, b = 6.4691(8) Å, c = 9.013(3) Å, V = 260.9193(16) Å3 (Table S2). The asymmetric unit of CaSiC2O7 contains one calcium, one carbon, one silicon, and three oxygen atoms (Fig. 1). Calcium cations are 12-fold coordinated with formation of cuboctahedra, silicon atoms are octahedrally coordinated and carbon atoms are in tetrahedral coordination (Fig. 2). The Ca-O bond distances slightly vary in the range of 2.221(5) to 2.317(6) Å with an average of 2.26 Å (Fig. 2c). The SiO6 octahedra also possess a geometry close to a regular one. Two apical bonds Si-O2 and four equatorial bonds Si-O3 are of 1.627(4) Å and 1.635(4) Å, respectively, while O2-Si-O3 and O3-Si-O3 angles are of 88.53(18)° and 90.4(4)° (Fig. 2b). The CO4 tetrahedra reveal a higher level of distortion. C-O bond distances vary from 1.295(9) to 1.404(8) Å with an average of 1.329 Å (Fig. 2a) while the tetrahedral angles O–C–O are in the range of 106.4(5)–112.6(5)°. CO4 tetrahedra link through common O1 vertices with the formation of [C2O7]6− dimers. The SiO6 octahedra are isolated from each other and share common O2 and O3 vertices with CO4 tetrahedra, with the formation of the three-dimensional framework that hosts Ca atoms in its voids. The topology of CaSiC2O7 framework resembles the one of sodium trisilicate, Na2Si2[VI]Si4[IV]O14 (a = 8.922(2) Å, b = 4.8490(5) Å, c = 11.567(1) Å, β = 102.64(1)°, space group C2/c, Fig. S2)33. The sodium trisilicate belongs to the group of high-pressure framework silicates with mixed 4- and six-fold coordination of silicon34. Synthesized at pressures >4 GPa, these frameworks feature relatively dense arrangements of SiO4 tetrahedra and SiO6 octahedra and contain large sites suitable for alkali or alkaline-earth cations. Earlier, Oganov et al.25. proposed that these structures can be adapted by high-pressure silicate-carbonates33,34. Herein, we demonstrate that, at megabar pressures, sp3 carbon can substitute silicon in the tetrahedral site, forming a framework based on CO4 and SiO6 polyhedra.

Ab initio calculations for CaSiC2O7

To our knowledge, the phase synthesized in this work was not reported in previous theoretical studies. To assess whether the experimentally observed CaSiC2O7 phase can be reproduced theoretically, we have performed ab initio simulations (See “Methods”). Full structural relaxation at the experimental unit cell volume results in an excellent agreement between measured and calculated unit-cell parameters, atomic coordinates and interatomic distances (Table S3). Harmonic phonon dispersion calculations reveal that CaSiC2O7 is dynamically stable both at the synthesis pressure of 122 GPa and at 1 bar (Fig. S3). The pressure dependence of the theoretical values of the unit cell volume of CaSiC2O7 can be described by the third-order Birch-Murnaghan equation of state with the parameters V0 = 377.5(2) Å3, K0 = 151.2(9) GPa and K’ = 4.41(2) (Fig. 3).Fig. 3The pressure dependence of the CaSiC2O7 unit cell volume.Red diamonds represent experimental data upon decompression after synthesis at 122 GPa, while black solid circles are values from the calculations. The elastic constants (Cij) of CaSiC2O7, calculated at 0 K for both at 122 GPa (Table S4) and at ambient pressures (Table S5), indicate that the phase is mechanically stable under these conditions, according to the Born stability criteria for orthorhombic systems35 (Text S1). The bulk and shear moduli were derived from the elastic tensor by the Voigt and Reuss approach36 (Text S2). As expected, the bulk modulus K = 155 GPa calculated from the elastic constants at 1 bar is consistent with the value K0 = 151.2(9) GPa obtained from the equation-of-state fit, confirming the internal consistency of the calculations.

The calculated electronic density of states shows that CaSiC2O7 is a wide-bandgap semiconductor with a band gap (Eg) of 5.6 eV at 122 GPa (Fig. S4).

Discussion

Implications for the high-pressure chemistry of silicates-carbonates

The current study demonstrates the synthesis of the calcium silicate-diorthocarbonate, CaSi[VI]C[IV]2O7, based on CO4 and SiO6 units. Previously, about 50 silicate-carbonates were known at ambient conditions, with three being from the CaO–SiO2–CO2 system: spurrite, Ca5[SiO4]2(CO3)37, tilleyite, Ca5[Si2O7](CO3)238, and galuskinite, Ca7(SiO4)3(CO3)15. While being based on CO3 triangles and SiO4 tetrahedra at ambient conditions, these compounds were proposed as possible precursors for high-pressure silicate-carbonates hosting C[IV] and/or Si[VI]. However, spurrite, Ca5[SiO4]2(CO3), and tilleyite, Ca5[Si2O7](CO3)2, were found to decompose into their component silicates and carbonates at pressures below 4 GPa at ∼1300 K39. Recent studies at elevated temperatures demonstrated the phase stability of spurrite up to 27 GPa and 700 K, with no phase transitions being observed40. Tilleyite was also found to persist up to 7 GPa and 675 K, while at higher pressures it transformed to post-tilleyite phase containing again CO3 and SiO4 units41. Ab initio calculations have also suggested the stability of these calcium silicate-carbonates minerals up to 25–35 GPa42. The presence of rigid carbonate units was proposed to enhance the stability of SiO4 tetrahedra and to prevent their transition to SiO6 octahedra40. While this work was already under consideration, a calcium silicon double carbonate, Ca2Si(CO3)4, based on SiO6 octahedra and CO3 flat triangular groups, was obtained by reacting tilleyite with CO2 at 39.5 GPa and 2600 K32, demonstrating that these transitions can be induced by high temperatures. The synthesis of CaSi[VI]C[IV]2O7 at the megabar pressure regime completes the long-discussed crystal chemical trend. The phase adapts the structure and stoichiometry of sodium trisilicate, Na2Si2[VI]Si4[IV]O14, thus hinting that other high-pressure framework silicates with mixed 4- and six-fold coordination of silicon34 are plausible isomorphs for silicate-carbonates.

The CaSiC2O7 synthesized in this work is a sp3-dicarbonate hosting [C2O7] units (Fig. 2a). Its structure expands the known structural diversity of carbonates and illustrates the crystal-chemical relationship between high-pressure sp3-carbonates and low-pressure tetrahedral silicates. Typically, the structural diversity of C[IV] carbonates was considered more limited in contrast to Si[IV] silicates, due to the smaller flexibility of inter-tetrahedral C–O–C angles in comparison to Si–O–Si angles25,43. However, recent advancements in carbonates chemistry allowed to experimentally discover nesocarbonates with isolated CO4 tetrahedra (Me2CO4, Me = Ca, Sr)44,45, carbonates based on four-membered chains (Me4C4O13, Me = Mg/Fe)46–48 and four-membered pyramidal groups (MeC2O5, Me = Ca, Fe, Mn, Cd)48–51, inocarbonates with infinite chains of vertice-sharing CO4 tetrahedra (CaCO3)52,53, cyclocarbonates based on three-membered rings (Me3C3O9, Me = Fe, Mg, Ca)27,54–56, and a tectocarbonate with three-dimensional connectivity of tetrahedra (CaC2O5)57. Even greater structural diversity of carbonates is proposed on the basis of theoretical calculations7,58, highlighting the need for further experiments to explore the complex chemistry of high-pressure sp3-carbonates.

Implications for the deep carbon storage

The results obtained in the geologically relevant system CaCO3–CaSiO3–FeO have several implications for the mineralogy and elasticity of Earth’s lowermost mantle. Firstly, the formation of CaSiC2O7 indicates that high-pressure silicate-carbonates may serve as another class of carbon hosts in Earth’s deep interior. Carbonates, predominantly calcite (CaCO3) and dolomite (Ca, Mg(CO3)), carry the inorganic carbon into the deeps via subduction and interact with the minerals of the average mantle upon descent. Previous high-pressure-high-temperature studies within the (Ca, Mg, Fe)–Si–C–O system demonstrated that a plethora of phases can be formed depending on the phase assemblages and experimental conditions, including polymorphs of CaCO3, MgCO3, CaSiO3, MgSiO3, Fe3C, FeO, CaO, SiO2, Fe7C3, C, MgO, CO2 (Table S6). These observations allowed to propose melting4, decarbonation13,59,60, silicate-carbonate chemical exchange12,14,61, redox freezing with diamond or carbide formation13,62–65, as potential mechanisms that govern the fate of carbonates in the Earth’s deeps. Our results indicate a possible scenario for interactions between carbonates and silicates under the pressure and temperature conditions of the lowermost lower mantle, involving the formation of silicate-carbonate compounds hosting both carbon and silicon (Fig. 4). In the light of the ongoing discussions on the redox heterogeneity of the lower mantle66–68, presence of carbonates and silicate-carbonates can be envisioned in its locally oxidized domains, that are thought to arise from subducted crustal materials69, Fe3+ disproportionation reactions70,71, or core–mantle exchange processes72. Persistence of oxidized forms of carbon under these conditions is supported by the occurrence of carbonates as inclusions in deep diamonds73–75 as well as by experimental evidence of calcium carbonate formation from reactions between calcium silicate and iron carbides61. Importantly, calcium carbonates were also proposed to be less sensitive than (Mg, Fe)CO3 to redox breakdown throughout the lower mantle64. The silicate-diorthocarbonate CaSiC2O7 was obtained as a product of the subsolidus reaction between CaCO3 and CaSiO3 in the presence of FeO at conditions close to the core-mantle boundary. We note, however, that carbon contamination from the diamond anvils is a common issue in laser-heated diamond anvil cell experiments76–78, which prevents a quantitative carbon mass balance, leaving the precise formation pathway of CaSiC2O7 less tightly constrained than implied by the nominal CaCO3 + CaSiO3 + FeO starting assemblage alone. In addition, although iron is not incorporated into CaSiC2O7, the formation of Fe3+-bearing phases from FeO (Table S1) indicates electron-transfer reactions that may have influenced the local chemical environment during crystallization. Despite these uncertainties in the exact reaction pathway, the nominal starting assemblage remains geophysically relevant, as CaCO3, CaSiO3 and FeO have each been proposed as possible components of large low-velocity provinces (LLVPs)7,29,79–82 that extend thousands of kilometers horizontally and hundreds of kilometers vertically above the core-mantle boundary and are seismologically detected below Africa and Pacific Ocean83–85. As a representative of the high-pressure silicate-carbonate class, CaSiC2O7 is of interest for examining the elastic properties and possible contribution of this group of compounds to the seismic signatures of the lowermost mantle. For this, the elastic moduli and acoustic wave velocities at 122 GPa were derived from the calculated elastic parameters (Cij) as described in Texts S1 and S2. Their values are compared in Table 1 with the radial Reference Earth model (REM1D)86, with minerals of the pyrolitic lower mantle assemblage87 and with relevant high-pressure calcium carbonates. Interestingly, CaSiC2O7 is closer in elastic properties to carbonates rather than silicates, and together they belong to the class of low-density minerals with low compressional and shear velocities (Vp and Vs, respectively). This is in line with the observation on the bulk moduli of low-pressure silicate-carbonates, composed of CO3 and SiO4 units: there is negative correlation between CO3 content and compressibility42. Our results demonstrate that in the case of a reaction between silicates and carbonates within or at the boundaries of LLVPs, the possible formation of silicate-carbonates will reduce the bulk density (by consumption of dense CaSiO3 and production of low-density carbon-bearing phases) and decrease the velocities. In addition, the particularly low shear modulus (188 GPa) of CaSiC2O7 indicates a shear-soft phase whose presence would facilitate plume formation. The experimental confirmation of a silicate-carbonate phase suggests possible intricate chemical exchanges between carbonates and silicates in the heterogeneous lowermost mantle (Fig. 4). The co-existence of CaSiC2O7 with other phases (Table S1) indicates that the sample chamber likely remained in a non-equilibrium state, as is typical of laser-heated diamond anvil cell experiments. Consequently, the present experiments do not allow us to assess the thermodynamic stability of CaSiC2O7 under lowermost mantle conditions.

Independent theoretical support for the potential stability of this phase has recently been provided by Tsuchiya & Isobe (2026)90, whose study –motivated by the public preprint of the present work– predicts that CaSiC2O7 is thermodynamically stable over a broad range of lowermost mantle pressures. While these calculations offer an important framework for understanding the possible stability field of CaSiC2O7, experimental confirmation under equilibrium conditions is still required.

Further systematic in situ investigations of carbon- and silicon-rich systems across a range of pressures, temperatures and chemical compositions are therefore needed to establish the equilibrium stability of silicate-carbonate phases and to clarify their potential role in the mineralogy, geochemistry, and geophysics of Earth and Earth-like planets.

Methods

Sample preparation

A BX90 diamond anvil cell (DAC) equipped with synthetic diamond anvils with a culet size of 150 μm was prepared91. The rhenium gasket used was indented from an initial thickness of 220 μm down to 20 μm and laser-drilled to create a circular pressure chamber of 80 μm in diameter. A powdered natural sample of CaSiO3 wollastonite from Stanisław locality, Kopianec, Poland, containing approximately 5 vol% of CaCO3 (Fig. S5), was loaded into the pressure chamber. A crystal of 57Fe-enriched Fe0.945O of ~10 × 10 x 5 μm3 was also loaded into the pressure chamber92,93, sandwiched by layers of CaSiO3-CaCO3 mixture at the top and at the bottom. The DAC was then compressed to 122(2) GPa as determined by the pressure-induced shift of the Raman spectrum of the diamonds at the culets94. The sample was then heated for a few minutes from both sides at 2800(200) K using the double-sided Nd-YAG laser system at the Nuclear Resonance Beamline (ID18)95–97 of the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility (ESRF) using the gray-body spectroradiometry method to measure the surface temperature of the heated sample98. Due to thermal pressure expected to develop inside the pressure chamber during heating, the synthesis pressure may be as high as 134 GPa, as estimated using the approximation described in99 and the thermal expansion and bulk modulus values for CaSiO3 at 122 GPa100.

In situ single crystal X-Ray diffraction

In situ X-ray diffraction measurements were performed at the High-Pressure Diffraction Beamline ID15B (ESRF, Grenoble)101. An EIGER2 X CdTe 9 M detector was used, and a monochromatic X-ray beam (λ = 0.41505 Å) was focused on the sample with a spot area of approximately 1.5 × 1.5 μm2. An X-ray map of the laser-heated spot was obtained by scanning the sample chamber with a step of 2 μm at ω = 0o. The resulting map was analysed using the XDI software suite102. At each position of interest, single-crystal datasets were collected during ω rotations of ±35° of the DAC with a step of 0.5°. The resulting diffraction images were analysed using the CrysAlisPro software suite. The Domain Auto Finder (DAFi) program was used to search for reflection groups belonging to the individual single crystal domains103. The multigrain analysis allowed to identify several products of chemical reactions between CaSiO3 and CaCO3 in the presence of FeO, including the silicate-diorthocarbonate CaSiC2O7 described here (Fig. S6). The crystal structure solution and refinement were performed using the Jana2006 software104. Crystal data and structure refinement details are presented in Table S2. The Crystallographic Information File for CaSiC2O7 is deposited as Supporting Information as well as in the Cambridge Structural Database under deposition number CSD 2352267.

The pressure inside the DAC was then released in several steps as shown in Fig. 3. At each step, a new X-ray map of the laser-heated area was obtained, and the silicate-diorthocarbonate phase was located and probed as described before. The crystal structure of CaSiC2O7 was solved and refined down to the pressure of 58(2) GPa. The reciprocal space reconstructions at 122 and 58 GPa are shown in Fig. S7 (a) and Fig. S7 (b), respectively. Further decompression down to 34(2) GPa led to a drop of the diffraction intensity from the sample, not allowing to refine the crystal structure anymore.

Given that the decompression inside a non-hydrostatic pressure medium (in this case the CaSiO3-CaCO3 mixture) without the annealing of the sample chamber is known to induce residual stresses on the materials within, the decompression data are not considered for the calculation of the equation of state of CaSiC2O7, but are only used to check if the phase can survive on decompression and for the comparison of experimental and calculated unit cell volumes.

Computational Details

The first-principles calculations were done using the framework of density functional theory (DFT) as implemented in the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)105. To expand the electronic wave function in plane waves, we used the Projector-Augmented-Wave (PAW) method106,107. The Generalized Gradient Approximation (GGA) functional was used for calculating the exchange-correlation energies, as proposed by Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE)108. The PAW potentials with the following valence configurations of 3s23p64s2 for Ca (“Ca_sv”), 3s23p2 for Si (“Si”), 2s22p2 for C (“C”) and 2s22p4 for O (“O”) were used. Convergence tests with a threshold of 2 meV per atom in energy led to a Monkhorst-Pack109 k-point grid of 11 × 7 × 5 and an energy cut-off for the plane wave expansion of 750 eV. The phonon frequencies and phonon band structure calculations were performed in the harmonic approximation with non-analytical term correction with the help of PHONOPY software110 using the finite displacement method for a 3 × 3 × 2 supercell (made for the primitive cell (PPOSCAR)) with respectively adjusted k-points: 4 × 4 × 2. Equation of state (EoS) calculations were performed via variable-cell structural relaxations between 1 bar and 160 GPa. In the static EoS calculations, the effect of zero-point motion and temperature was neglected. The elastic constants were obtained using the stress–strain method111.
