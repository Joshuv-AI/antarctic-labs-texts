Machine learning approaches for resource management and forecasting in energy consumption systems
Authors: Hesam Mohammad Asghari, Ali Ghaffari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Renewable Energy Systems (RES) have become necessary with the growing need for sustainable energy globally; however, RES faces several problems such as power outages, instability and economic loss. These challenges are structured into three specific technical hurdles: (i) intermittency of wind and solar energy, (ii) forecasting uncertainty, and (iii) grid integration issues. In this context, recent technological trends in short-term wind power generation forecasting have increasingly moved towards hybridizing deep learning models with optimization algorithms to mitigate resource uncertainty. This study finds that Artificial Intelligence (AI) is one of the best solutions for overcoming these problems. To provide conceptual clarity, this research distinguishes AI as the overarching intelligent framework, while Machine Learning (ML) enables algorithmic learning of relationships from data, and Deep Learning (DL) utilizes hierarchical architectures for capturing complex non-linear temporal patterns. Methods such as Support Vector Machines (SVM) and Random Forest (RF) are applied alongside DL for predicting the amount of energy produced from RES and scheduling maintenance activities. To address existing research gaps, this research presents an innovative approach through combining a unique four-stage scientific data cleaning method with a two-phase AI stabilization system. The system uses the Markowitz Model as the first stage of determining the optimal hybrid ratio of wind and solar resources in addition to employing an energy-constrained battery smoothing process to reduce residual fluctuations. Results indicate that AI-based predictive maintenance can decrease the operational expenditure (OPEX) of RES up to 30.0%. Furthermore, the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) model demonstrated superior predictive power, achieving a Coefficient of Determination (R2) of up to 0.988, whereas a two-stage AI framework integrating Markowitz-based portfolio optimization and energy-constrained battery control achieves an average variance reduction of 95.4% in grid stability.

Introduction

The world is shifting toward the adoption of sustainable forms of energy; this causes nations to embrace the use of RES faster in their power distribution. However issues of stability, power interruptions, and losses still linger, and this persists even among developing nations like Iran1,2. The current study aims to show that the application of AI stands out as a particular solution that can address many of the issues being raised. This technology enhances the efficiency and savings that come with the application of RES due to the predictive analysis and advanced control systems that come with AI3,4. AI techniques like ML, deep DL, and RL are very helpful in estimating the production of energy, scheduling maintenance, as well as the management of the smart grid and thus result in the reduction of costs with the maximization of the efficiency of the system5–7. To clarify these paradigms, while machine learning focuses on using statistics in identifying data patterns, for instance, regression analysis via support vector machines, deep learning entails the use of multi-layered neural networks in discovering patterns from complex and unstructured sequences, such as time series via LSTM. On the other hand, reinforcement learning is based on the idea of using rewards to improve decision making in dynamic systems. Although reinforcement learning has shown great promise in achieving real-time grid balance, this paper focuses on machine learning and deep learning. Predictive maintenance of equipment with the help of AI technology can lead to a significant reduction in unplanned downtime and a decrease in maintenance costs, with industrial and research studies often reporting reductions on the order of a few tens of percent8–10. At the same time, AI predictive models have enhanced the predictability of solar power and wind power so that they can better match supply and production and facilitate more economically optimal dispatching. Further, AI is used for optimizing the energy storage system, load forecasting, and demand response (DR), or dynamic pricing3,11. Given the Iranian peak-hour grid overload and energy access disparities across different geographical points, AI will further aid the system’s decision-making and promote optimal energy dispatch2,12. Micro-grids with the aid of AI and hybrid systems are suitable for application at rural points that will promote the concept of energy independence2,13.

Even though the above-stated benefits have been obtained in several pilot and industrial applications, the computational power issues, integrity and availability of the data, and regulatory preparedness appear to hamper the widespread application of AI technology within many national power industries1,7,16. Further, the AI economics of cost savings, investment returns, and net profitability are still not being examined under rigorous, life-cycle–based scientific studies in contexts such as the Iranian power system8,17. The overarching economic justification for such transitions is provided by models like the Artificial Intelligence Econometric Model (AIEM)14, which has demonstrated significant improvements in resource allocation through intelligent forecasting.

While the primary focus remains on LSTM due to its efficacy in modeling temporal sequences, it should be situated within a broader computational context. Several studies demonstrate that interpretable ensemble learning and advanced data augmentation techniques can achieve high predictive accuracy when analyzing complex behaviors and failures in engineered systems18,19. Furthermore, integrating uncertainty quantification into interpretable machine learning architectures helps mitigate the black-box nature of deep learning, providing transparent decision pathways for evaluating structural integrity20,21.A concurrent shift is evident in computational modeling with the rise of Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs). These models move beyond traditional black-box approaches by embedding governing physical laws into the training process, proving highly accurate in renewable energy and thermal applications22. To address the high computational costs of high-fidelity simulations, surrogate models23–26 and stochastic integrated frameworks (e.g., ANN-PSO hybrids) have emerged as efficient alternatives for multiscale reliability assessments. Finally, specialized platforms now facilitate these advanced analytical tools through expert-driven interfaces; for instance, AI-DeMat27 provides a scalable, user-centric environment for complex materials design and engineering decision-making.

Research gaps and novelties

Despite vast research in terms of using AI methods in energy forecasting, there has been an identified knowledge gap. Most of the current literature focuses on analyzing accuracy without giving due consideration to the critical aspect of dispatchability and stability of the system. In addition to this, there has been a notable absence of comprehensive approaches, where physically-based data cleaning and portfolio optimization models have been combined.

There exists a necessity for conducting a well-designed study that investigates the economic efficiency of using AI technologies during the entire life cycle of renewable energy systems. The proposed work employs the concept of Cleaning-to-Stability that provides a unified solution to achieve data correctness and hybrid grid stability. Contrary to many past studies that utilize data without pre-processing or use simplistic methods of cleaning based on some threshold5,11,28, we design a scientific four-phase data cleaning approach coupled with a two-phase AI stabilization system.

The primary goals of the present research are to provide solutions to the previously identified knowledge gaps in current Energy Informatics, specifically within the Iranian energy sector. The first objective aims to determine the potential OPEX cost savings achievable through an AI-based predictive maintenance strategy. The second objective is to evaluate various ML and DL architectures to compare their performance and select the optimal approach for local energy forecasting. Lastly, the research develops a new, two-phase AI system designed to increase power grid reliability by integrating real-time energy predictions into an economically optimal management framework. The methodology adopted in this study represents a unique combination of both quantitative and qualitative approaches; it utilizes a mixed-methods design, involving the integration of traditional statistical economic models with advanced ML-based simulations. This approach ensures the scalability of the proposed method for hybrid renewable layouts in emerging economies. Literature review and theoretical framework

Paradigm shifts in AI-integrated energy informatics

The incorporation of artificial intelligence into engineering has progressed beyond the theoretical beginnings of AI and is now being applied as an integrated approach to manage nonlinear and multidimensional issues associated with power systems29. In contrast to developing AI systems that mimic humans, current AI implementations in renewable energy focus on autonomous decision making and predictive accuracy for effective management of resources30. The transition from single modality architectures toward more integrated multi modal systems allows for processing of volatile data sets that were not able to be processed by traditional frequentist models31,32.

Currently, there is a significant shift in the application of energy informatics toward informed-AI, where the computational models are becoming increasingly hybridized with physical constraints to provide stable operation of grids33,34. Therefore, this study takes advantage of this trend; it will develop a methodology using a combination of deep learning architectures as a decision support tool to help mitigate the intermittence of hybrid wind-solar structures in newly forming energy markets35.

Application and critical review of ML and DL in energy systems

The transition of ML from theory to practice has transformed it into an established engineering discipline capable of analyzing large, nonlinear energy datasets36,37. Consequently, data clustering and unsupervised learning methods38 are now considered essential techniques for discovering hidden patterns within unclassified environmental data, a prerequisite for optimal feature selection in contemporary renewable systems39.

As ML matured as a statistical tool, kernel-based models, including SVM40, proved successful in producing accurate predictions within high-dimensional spaces. To address the inherent noise and stochasticity of datasets common in regional wind corridors, ensemble-based techniques such as Boosting41 and Random Forests42 have emerged as the preferred methods for enhancing model predictability43,44. A key advantage of these ensemble models is their ability to maintain accuracy during periods of high weather intermittency in emerging markets.

The limitations of standard ML in processing massive volumes of unstructured information led to the emergence of DL as a distinct subset45. Although CNNs are currently utilized to interpret spatial data at an unprecedented scale46, they remain restrictive for interpreting temporal energy sequences. Therefore, recurrent structures, specifically LSTM networks47, have become the most effective architecture for such applications. Recent innovations have further refined these architectures, enhancing their performance under resource uncertainty and fluctuating power levels in hybrid systems15,48. Furthermore, by mitigating the vanishing gradient issue, LSTMs provide a robust link from raw environmental sequences to reliable, dispatchable power forecasting49.

Strategic optimization and decision-making paradigms in energy markets

Beyond predictive accuracy, operational excellence in hybrid energy systems requires decision-making paradigms of high complexity. RL has emerged as a critical framework for resolving challenges in sequential decision-making, where agents must optimize long term grid stability through rewards and penalties50. In contrast to static supervised models, which do not permit dynamic adjustments with respect to intermittent resource fluctuations, RL facilitates real-time adjustments within Markov Decision Process (MDP) structures51.

At the center of these industrial applications are mathematical optimization principles that have evolved from simple cost minimization toward multi objective reliability frameworks52. In modern energy informatics, optimization is no longer just about profit maximization; it is the primary mechanism for navigating both technical and economic constraints associated with power generation and storage53. Recent literature highlights the need to integrate these models with cost benefit analysis of photovoltaic-storage investments to guarantee long-term viability in emerging markets54.

This study builds upon these optimization principles by implementing a variance-minimization strategy. Specifically, we use Mean Variance Portfolio Theory55,56 to transition from an individual asset management approach to an integrated, low-risk hybrid model. This approach allows us to convert forecast outputs from the previous stages into stable, dispatchable, and economically efficient forms of energy delivery.

Cost-benefit analysis in energy systems

In order to make decisions about whether or not an investment is financially viable for integrated energy systems, including those with photovoltaic (PV) and battery energy storage system (BESS), the most appropriate tool to use is a cost-benefit analysis (CBA). The accelerated deployment of renewable resources worldwide has made the CBA a widely accepted method of evaluating the financial paybacks associated with investments as they compare to long-term expenses incurred through conducting business. In particular, a CBA is critical to determine the optimal configuration of PV and BESS systems to maximize energy supply and grid performance while also optimizing the paybacks associated with the investments in these systems54.

The characteristics of solar energy generation are primarily determined by atmospheric variables, leading to its classification as a highly intermittent resource in renewable energy systems. Therefore, when evaluating the reliability of grids and providing a consistent source of energy, solar energy poses significant integration challenges due to its inherent stochastic nature. However, the intermittency of solar energy may be mitigated using BESS; specifically, BESS allows for energy arbitrage, peak shaving, and load balancing. Each of the above-mentioned operations have corresponding monetary values, which form the basis of the cost-benefit evaluation57,58. An entire CBA considers both initial and operating costs. Initial costs consist of the costs associated with installing PV panels, the costs of the various components of BESS, the costs of inverters, and the costs of connecting to the grid. Operating costs consist of the costs associated with maintaining devices, purchasing additional electricity, and replacing devices. Income sources that generate revenue include income received from feed-in tariffs, income received from subsidies provided by government agencies, and income received from reducing reliance on the grid54. Proposed a model that included each of the above-mentioned factors and determined the Net Present Cost (NPC) and Net Present Value (NPV) over the course of a 25 year project. Discounted cash flows provide a time based means of comparing costs and benefits.

Synthesis of leading research and recent empirical findings

The Energy 4.0 paradigm represents a major shift in renewable energy management; indeed, recent studies show that deep learning has gone from being a theoretical concept to a practical necessity for analyzing the nonlinear dynamics of energy systems48. This is exemplified by60, who used the CNN-LSTM architecture to achieve a 12% decrease in energy consumption by combining the spatial anomaly detection capability of CNNs with the time-series forecasting capabilities of LSTMs. Furthermore44, demonstrated that recurrent structures are superior to others in terms of sustaining high levels of forecasting performance for a variety of climate-related variables. In a similar vein, recent advancements in machine learning have been successfully applied to predict complex user behaviors and infrastructure costs, further validating the robustness of data-driven architectures in energy-transport systems62. Moreover, the Energy 4.0 paradigm represents the development of advanced network integration strategies, as demonstrated by4, who developed a comprehensive roadmap to scale-up autonomous renewable energy infrastructure.

Furthermore, the social and economic framework of the transition to the Energy 4.0 paradigm can be identified by the development of methods to evaluate the financial feasibility of the transition. As demonstrated in the research within59,61, AI can be applied to optimize the commercial and residential infrastructure to achieve up to 36% in cost savings. This method was found to be scalable to large-scale energy infrastructure, especially relevant to developing countries where AI may provide a solution to address the unequal access to energy as demonstrated through39.

From a broader economic perspective14, demonstrated a comprehensive economic case for transitioning to the Energy 4.0 paradigm using the AIEM model, which demonstrates a 97.32% improvement in resource allocation when using AI for forecasting. The bibliometric study conducted in37 and the review written in43 demonstrate the need to develop a new regulatory environment to accommodate the high dimensionality of the data analyzed.

In contrast to these studies37,43, emphasized data variability and lack of localized frameworks as barriers to the widespread adoption of AI in renewable energy management, while this study will address the above-mentioned knowledge gap by evaluating the performance of AI models in highly variable wind corridors in Iran, such as the 120-day winds of Zabol49 using empirical data from those regions. In addition, this study will provide an operational model for optimizing economics and system sustainability consistent with the broader call to develop site-specific AI applications in emerging energy markets.

Figure 1 illustrates the research methodology, which is executed through three distinct stages:

Data Engineering: This stage focuses on ensuring signal integrity through the application of the Interquartile Range (IQR) technique for outlier detection, alongside Rate of Change (RoC) filters to maintain physical consistency and eliminate sensor anomalies.

Parallel Modeling: Three core tasks are executed in parallel: (1) establishing an economic baseline via OLS regression, (2) benchmarking classical machine learning algorithms (SVM, RF, and GB) against Deep Learning (LSTM) for predictive accuracy, and (3) minimizing generation volatility using Markowitz Theory.

System Synthesis: The stabilized forecasts and optimized weightings are integrated into a unified framework to analyze power network stability and ensure a reliable supply of electricity.

The Hybrid Data-Driven Approach in this research project represents an integrated methodology of forecasting and optimization at multiple levels. This framework is considered as hybrid in three ways: (1) Methodology, by employing deep learning methods (LSTM) to forecast energy production, and mean-variance theory from Markowitz Portfolio Optimization Theory to optimize portfolios; (2) Resources, by using both renewable resources (wind and solar), which can be volatile independently but stable when used together; (3) Systems, through the integration of system level outputs from data driven AI models with physical based BESS controls to maintain the reliability of the electrical distribution network.

Data collection and pre-processing

The primary data collection is focused on renewable energy installation sites throughout Iran. In this study, we are looking at wind energy installation locations located in areas of the country with higher than average winds as defined by SATBA. These include Zabol (located in Sistan and Baluchestan), Manjil (in Gilan), and Binalood (in Khorasan Razavi)49. Our data were collected through real-time monitoring systems provided by NASA weather APIs. To ensure empirical validity, this study adopts a hybrid data-driven approach; while the meteorological inputs ((v), (T), and temporal markers) are derived from high-resolution real-world historical records, the conversion into Estimated Power Output (kW) and the subsequent OPEX impacts are evaluated through validated computational simulations.

To distinguish between valid extreme meteorological events (e.g., storms) and sensor anomalies, a dual-verification logic was applied. While IQR identifies statistical deviations, the RoC filter in Stage 3 serves as a physical constraint. A point is only removed if it is both a statistical outlier and exhibits a physically impossible instantaneous change (e.g., > 15 m/s within 10 min), ensuring that legitimate high-velocity events are preserved.

Stage 3: Physical Consistency Filtering (RoC): An RoC filter was implemented to remove physically inconsistent spikes, effectively cleaning ‘stuck’ or malfunctioning anemometer records.

Stage 4: Feature Scaling: Given the diverse geographic scales, the RobustScaler was applied to ensure consistent input features, chosen for its resilience to the extreme outliers common in volatile wind environments.

This multi-stage cleaning protocol is essential to maintain high data fidelity, addressing common quality issues in AI-based forecasting such as sensor drift, communication gaps in SCADA systems, or noise from extreme climatic events63,64. Establishing such Clean-Data-In protocols is a prerequisite for preventing the Garbage-in, Garbage-out phenomenon in industrial-scale energy dispatching.

AI modeling and optimization framework

The AI algorithms utilized in this research are well-documented within the Power Systems literature7,48. Specifically, Multiple Linear Regression (MLR), implemented via Ordinary Least Squares (OLS), is used as a traditional baseline to provide ease of interpretation and a benchmark for evaluating economic impacts. LSTM networks are selected due to their superior capability to overcome the vanishing-gradient problem and predict complex long-term relationships in chaotic wind time-series data47. Lastly, Markowitz’s Mean-Variance Theory is employed during the Hybrid Optimization phase to provide a solid mathematical basis for determining the optimal balance between Risk (Variance) and Return (Power Output)55.

Machine learning algorithms

The following architectures are employed for power forecasting (Objective 2). The theoretical rationale for selecting each architecture is as follows.

SVM: SVMs map non-linear relationships between inputs and outputs through the use of kernel functions. This capability makes them highly effective for capturing complex, non-linear patterns within meteorological datasets40.

RF and GB: As ensemble-based methods, these architectures are well-suited to handle the uncertainty associated with the stochastic nature of (v). By aggregating multiple decision trees, they mitigate overfitting and provide superior robustness when dealing with sensor noise42.

LSTM: LSTMs are a specialized type of Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) designed to capture long-term sequential dependencies in time-series data. Through their gate mechanisms input, output, and forget these models preserve historical trends and mitigate the vanishing gradient problem, ensuring more reliable convergence compared to traditional RNNs47. During the training phase, the model’s internal trainable parameters are optimized to minimize the Mean Squared Error (MSE). Optimization is achieved through iterative adjustments to these parameters (i.e., weights and biases) utilizing the Adam optimizer. As such, the network learns complex temporal patterns within the data. Three key aspects of the enhanced LSTM framework are the basis for the term ‘enhanced.’ These include: (1) The addition of a four-stage data cleaning process in conjunction with IQR and RoC filtering methods to remove noise caused by sensors. (2) Utilizing a grid search algorithm to systematically determine the best hidden unit size and dropout rate (i.e., 0.20) to limit overfitting. (3) Implementing an input scheme that is informed by physical relationships; specifically, utilizing (T) and (P) as surrogate measures of air density (ρ) as a proxy for the cubic relationship between (v) and electricity generation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:P\propto\:{v}^{3}$$\end{document}).

The selection of the optimal hyperparameters for the LSTM model was conducted through a systematic Grid Search procedure. The second level of optimization focuses on high-level configurations rather than internal weights. Specifically, the search space aims to optimize the learning rate, number of hidden units, and dropout rate to maximize the model’s generalizability on unseen data. The search space included various combinations of learning rates (0.01, 0.001, 0.0001), hidden unit sizes (16, 32, 64, 128), and dropout rates (0.1 to 0.4). The configuration that yielded the lowest Validation Mean Squared Error (VMSE) over 50 iterations was selected as the final architecture, as detailed in Table 4. To capture the complex temporal dependencies and diurnal cycles of the wind corridors, the model utilizes a multivariate input vector reshaped into 3D tensors based on a 24-hour look-back window (Sequence Length).

To improve reliability (Objective 3), a two-stage Cleaning-to-Stability framework is proposed. The framework is structured as follows.

Stage 1: First, the Markowitz Model (Variance-Minimizing Portfolio Theory)55 determines the optimal hybrid weight of wind and solar assets to minimize combined variance. The objective function is defined to minimize the combined portfolio variance (risk) while maintaining a required power output level, thereby effectively balancing the stochastic contributions of both resources. This approach has been validated for enhancing grid reliability through resource diversification56.

Stage 2: System-Level Smoothing. Following the weights calculation, a battery smoothing algorithm using a proportional controller is employed. This second stage acts as an active filter to mitigate high-frequency spikes, transforming unstable hybrid outputs into a consistent, dispatchable power supply63,64.

Statistical evaluation and performance metrics

In order to provide a quantitative evaluation of the forecasting models and meet the high statistical standards required for peer review, several metrics were utilized, including Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE), Symmetric Mean Absolute Percentage Error (sMAPE), and the R215. The use of these metrics allows for a comprehensive assessment of reliability, accuracy, and generalizability. Model evaluation is comprehensive; it is explicitly noted that higher values (closer to 1.0) for the coefficient of determination and lower values for error metrics indicate superior model performance. Since the editor has recommended the R2 as a primary indicator, it serves as our initial method for assessing the goodness-of-fit for each model.

Coefficient of determination (R2)

This metric measures the proportion of variance in the power output that is predictable from the input meteorological variables. It provides a standardized measure of how well the model replicates observed outcomes.

9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}^{2}=1-\frac{\sum\:_{i=1}^{n}{\left({y}_{i}-{\widehat{y}}_{i}\right)}^{2}}{\sum\:_{i=1}^{n}{\left({y}_{i}-\bar{y}\right)}^{2}}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{y}_{i}$$\end{document} is the actual power value, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\widehat{y}}_{i}$$\end{document} is the predicted value, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{y}$$\end{document} is the mean of the actual values. Forecasting Performance: The R2 was employed to evaluate the proportion of variance in the wind power output that is predictable from the input variables, providing a robust measure of the model’s goodness-of-fit15.

Root mean square error (RMSE)

RMSE is utilized to quantify the magnitude of prediction error. By squaring the errors, this metric gives higher weight to larger deviations, which is critical for maintaining grid stability and safety.

10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:RMSE=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum\:_{i=1}^{N}{({y}_{i}-{\widehat{y}}_{i})}^{2}}$$\end{document}

Mean absolute error (MAE)

MAE represents the average absolute difference between predicted and actual values. It offers a direct and linear interpretation of the forecast error magnitude without penalizing outliers as heavily as RMSE.

11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:MAE=\frac{1}{N}\sum\:_{i=1}^{N}\left|{y}_{i}-{\widehat{y}}_{i}\right|$$\end{document}

Customized mean absolute percentage error (MAPE*)

To address the limitations of standard MAPE in renewable energy datasets where actual power \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{y}_{i}$$\end{document} can frequently be zero during periods of low wind a customized version is implemented. A floor value (set to 10.0 kW) is applied in the denominator to prevent division-by-zero and numerical instability48,64.

12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:MAP{E}^{*}=\frac{100\mathrm{\%}}{n}\:\sum\:_{i=1}^{n}\:\frac{|{y}_{i}-\hat {{y}}_{i}|}{max({y}_{i},10.0)}$$\end{document}

Symmetric mean absolute percentage error (sMAPE)

While standard MAPE is sensitive to the magnitude of actual values and inherently biased toward under-predictions, sMAPE provides a balanced comparison by using the average of actual and forecast values in the denominator. This bounds the error within a range of 0% to 200%, ensuring that neither under-predictions nor over-predictions are favored, thereby providing a more symmetric evaluation of model performance15.

13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:sMAPE=\frac{100\mathrm{\%}}{n}\sum\:_{i=1}^{n}\:\frac{|{y}_{i}-\hat {{y}}_{i}|}{\left(\right|{y}_{i}|+|\hat {{y}}_{i}\left|\right)/2}$$\end{document}

Uncertainty quantification (U95)

To provide a rigorous statistical validation, the expanded uncertainty at a 95% confidence level (U95) was calculated. This analysis was applied exclusively to the Testing Dataset to ensure an unbiased evaluation of the model’s performance on unseen data. The (U95) is calculated according to the ISO/GUM standard66 and established methodologies for short-term wind power forecasting67 as:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{U}_{95}=k\cdot\:\sqrt{{u}_{\mathrm{bias\:}}^{2}+{u}_{\mathrm{random\:}}^{2}}$$\end{document}

k = 1.96 is the coverage factor, ubias represents the mean forecast error (bias), and urandom is the standard deviation of the residuals.

Reducing operational costs via AI algorithms (Objective 1)

The primary mechanism by which AI reduces OPEX for wind energy is through predictive maintenance and optimized downtime planning. With AI-based real-time monitoring, we can quickly detect irregularities within the turbines to avoid unscheduled shutdowns. We employed a machine learning-based econometric proxy model, aligned with current cost forecasting frameworks62, to establish the relationship between unplanned downtimes and total OPEX. Our model, which incorporates both baseline maintenance requirements and AI-based efficiency measures, was found to be statistically valid.

Simulation results showed that our AI-driven methodology resulted in a 30.0% reduction in OPEX compared to traditional reactive maintenance methodologies. Such a decrease would be exceptionally beneficial for the high-maintenance regions of Iran, particularly considering the abrasive conditions caused by the 120-Day Winds and the arid desert environment of the Zabol area. By utilizing the high-precision LSTM forecast model, operators can transition from a reactive fix-failures cycle to a proactive predict-and-prevent strategy. Additionally, a 30.0% reduction in OPEX for a 50 MW power plant, similar to those located in Zabol, equates to substantial annual savings by allowing for scheduled technical interventions during low-wind periods. Consequently, the significant costs and safety hazards associated with emergency repairs during peak wind seasons are avoided. These findings were further validated using the AIEM14 and align with recent studies regarding data-driven optimization for improving grid reliability63.

Discussion

Discussion of findings

The findings provide strong evidence that AI can be effectively applied to RES, especially in the Iranian context, where wind energy variability is substantial. The integration of a two-stage AI-assisted model led to a total variance reduction of approximately 95.7% on average, confirming massive stability improvements1,63. Reducing Operational Costs (Objective 1): The simulation of a 30% reduction in downtime confirmed that AI-based predictive maintenance methods can result in tangible savings14. This economic feasibility is consistent with the results of the AIEM model14, which reported a 97.32% improvement in resource allocation efficiency through intelligent evaluation. Specifically a reduction of 30% of operating expenses at a 50 MW power plant like Zabol amounts to considerable savings annually because of the expensive nature of maintenance costs owing to the arid conditions of the Iranian region. Furthermore, comprehensive technical and economic assessments of wind potential in Eastern Iran have highlighted the viability of time-series modeling for long-term energy security and fossil fuel reduction49.

Effectiveness of Machine Learning (Objective 2): The results reveal that the LSTM neural network algorithm is highly successful at predicting the production of wind power. The LSTM model consistently outperformed all other models, demonstrating superior predictive power and achieving a (R2) of up to 0.98815,44,60. Notably, the results from using a second-order PR as a non-linear baseline model indicated that although fitting to non-linear interaction terms among features can provide a better fit than a MLR model, this was still inadequate to capture all the complexities involved with the sequential structure of the 120-day winds. These findings also show that while the superior performance of LSTMs can be attributed to their non-linearity, they are primarily effective because they have the capability to sequentially process information through gated memory. This robustness is further supported by recent studies applying machine learning to complex energy-transport behaviors, showcasing the versatility of data-driven architectures62. This superior performance aligns with the empirical evidence presented by60, who showcased the efficacy of LSTM-based architectures in capturing complex energy patterns, and the systematic review of44, which established the dominance of recurrent structures in volatile forecasting environments. This occurs because the structure of the LSTM network makes it ideally suited to the determination of the respective long-term patterns within highly variable data sets like the hour-by-hour electricity generated by the winds of Zabol, Manjil, and Binalood. AI’s Influence on System Reliability (Objective 3): AI-based optimization and control dramatically improve the reliability and stability of hybrid renewable energy systems. The most significant finding was the adaptive nature of AI optimization. In the highly volatile Zabol region, it minimized its reliance on wind, whereas in the more stable Manjil and Binalood regions, it confidently used a higher wind ratio. This demonstrates a robust and adaptive optimization strategy. The effectiveness of such data-driven optimization in enhancing smart grid reliability and cyber-resilient integration has been emphasized in contemporary energy management research63.

Our findings are also similar to what has recently come out in studies on hybrid renewable energy management68; however, the majority of those studies have utilized PSO, and RL, which are much more complicated than Markowitz and simple controllers that we utilized.

Given of this study’s context of Iran and the overall trend of renewable energy worldwide, the implications of this study need to be interpreted accordingly. As we demonstrated through the AI-enabled predictive proxy model, the cost savings of 30.0% demonstrate the practicality of the technology of RES. This is particularly important for the high-maintenance zone of Zabol. The high-performance of our proposed LSTM algorithm, further validates the capability of our proposed method to mitigate the impacts of intermittency issues, a key issue for Iran’s geography of the power grid. The substantial margin of stability (93–98%), enables the conversion of intermittent solar and wind resources into a single reliable and dispatchable hybrid resource59,61. Similarly, efficiencies and operational cost reductions (as much as 36%) have been seen in larger-scale smart infrastructures59,61, supporting the scalability of the proposed approach.

Practical implications, generalization, and physical constraints

The overall applicability of the developed conceptual framework is demonstrated through consistent model performance across three climatically diverse geographical regions: arid, mountainous, and semi-arid. These results confirm that the proposed model has not been overfitted to a single dataset, thereby exhibiting high potential for generalization to various wind corridors globally7,48.

Furthermore, the integration of (T) and (P) as input variables serves as an implicit physical constraint within the model’s architecture. Consequently, the forecasting solutions remain physically valid and aligned with the laws of aerodynamics regarding air density variations22,68. Adopting such a physics-aware modeling approach provides greater confidence for real-world engineering applications, where strict adherence to established physical laws is essential for reliable grid management. Adopting a physics-aware approach for data-driven modeling ensures that the generated solutions remain within feasible engineering bounds, a methodology proven effective in predicting complex parameters such as discharge coefficients and material strength69,70. This shift toward Physics-Informed architectures effectively mitigates the black-box nature of traditional deep learning, ensuring that the LSTM outputs do not violate the fundamental conservation laws of energy and mass22.

Assumptions and limitations

A number of underlying assumptions exist in the AI methods employed in the current study. Firstly, it is assumed that the wind pattern remains stationary over the entire time span of the dataset (i.e., no sudden and unpredictable climate shifts occurred). Secondly, it is assumed that high-quality sensor data is available to ensure the model’s correct operation. While the initial cleaning stage addressed identified systematic biases, we acknowledge that subsequent data processing might uncover further latent issues. Recognizing these assumptions and the following limitations is essential for ensuring transparency when deploying AI into national grid management systems. The study utilized existing datasets containing incomplete information; therefore, the anomalous Zabol 2025 file was excluded. The reliance on region-specific datasets and a limited three-year temporal window may restrict the direct applicability of these results to climatically distinct regions without localized recalibration. Additionally, while we incorporated physics-based representations, the (black-box) nature of deep learning techniques precludes achieving full physical interpretability a critical requirement for high-risk engineering applications. Furthermore, deep learning architectures like LSTM are susceptible to overfitting if the training data lacks inter-annual climatic variability; this risk was mitigated through dropout layers and early stopping. Although a simulated PV model was used to address missing solar irradiance data for Objective 3, future research should evaluate the proposed framework using higher-resolution real-world solar radiation datasets and assess the AI control system’s performance in real-time hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) simulations.

The evolution of AI-based energy forecasting from 2021 to 2025 offers significant opportunities to improve upon current state-of-the-art technologies. Recent advancements emphasize the potential of Multivariate Adaptive Regression Splines (MARS)69 and Variable Creator Machines (VCMs)71,72 for enhancing feature engineering. These white-box technologies facilitate the creation of superior features that represent complex meteorological relationships. Notably, recent evaluations of VCM components, specifically the High Correlated Variables Creator Machine (HCVCM) and the Stronger Variable Creator Machine (SVCM)70,71 have demonstrated their predictive superiority in complex engineering domains, such as predicting material strength. Furthermore, future research should investigate the integration of the Dynamic Selecting Machine73 to further refine model selection and enhance the robustness of energy forecasting frameworks. Beyond feature engineering, performing a quantitative sensitivity analysis is essential for understanding how input variables such as meteorological conditions and load demand affect system performance throughout the LSTM architecture. This approach enhances the study’s robustness by identifying the primary drivers of forecasting errors. Utilizing Archimedes Optimization-based frameworks or stochastic multiscale models allows for a rigorous evaluation of model reliability65,74. These methods can be integrated within Explainable AI (XAI) frameworks to provide a clearer picture of feature importance75, contributing to more resilient digital ecosystems that adapt dynamically to fluctuating energy regimes64.

Integrating data-driven approaches with PINNs represents a significant advancement for enhancing physical consistency in predictive models. From a solver perspective, the feasibility of PINNs lies in their ability to directly embed governing physical constitutive equations specifically partial differential equations (PDEs) into the loss function of the neural network22. Unlike traditional solvers (e.g., CFD/FEM), which are mesh-dependent and require substantial computational resources, PINNs operate as mesh-free solvers. Consequently, the calculation of governing equations is transformed into a constrained optimization problem, greatly reducing the simulation burden for hybrid energy systems while ensuring that AI-generated outputs remain strictly bounded by fundamental physical laws. Parallel to these technical improvements, future research should incorporate comprehensive financial modeling (CAPEX, LCOE, and NPV) to provide a complete lifecycle assessment62. This economic foresight, combined with the transition from Markowitz models to advanced Model Predictive Control (MPC) and RL specifically Deep Q-Learning (DQN) represents a significant opportunity for real-time, dynamic optimization of battery assets63.

Practical implications, interpretability, and real-time grid integration

Although the main objective of the present research focused on applying predictive modeling techniques to support forecast and maintenance activities, there exist many possibilities to incorporate the suggested framework in smart-grid control systems using real-time data. Demand-Side Management (DSM) utilizing LSTM-based predictions of future electrical load an essential component for efficient resource use can be applied to predict electric vehicle charging costs on an individual level based on historical consumer usage patterns62,63. Transitions from static simulation tools to real-time operation represent a significant improvement over previous applications. Using SCADA/EMS as the platform to implement the previously described optimization methods will provide grid managers with the ability to dynamically match both supply and demand of power at very high frequency rates. Additionally, web-based expert systems and multi-faceted digital platforms will provide the capability to execute such computationally intensive models in real-time to provide timely responses to changes within the grid27,64.

In order for industrial stakeholders to accept the concept of deploying real-time AI, there is a need for developing trustworthy and transparent XAI. Integrating XAI methodologies into AI frameworks particularly those utilizing deep-learning (black boxes) provides insight into how each weather-related variable influences the predictive model’s output18,75. Furthermore, expanding current architectures that quantify uncertainty will provide sufficient reliability in autonomous agents managing critical national infrastructure20,21. Finally, the information gained through AI may also be utilized directly in Energy Storage Optimization (ESO), specifically thermal energy storage technologies utilizing advanced phase-change materials. Such technologies serve as buffers to reduce variability resulting from the intermittent production of hybrid wind-solar systems22,65, thereby creating an integrated decision-support system that combines real-time accurate forecasts with optimal dispatch and storage.

Conclusion and recommendations

The concept of AI within renewable energy systems has transitioned from being a desirable ideal to becoming a necessary strategy for many countries, including Iran. This study illustrated how the Cleaning-to-Stability framework, utilizing data-driven optimization, significantly reduces the inherent uncertainties in predicting both wind and solar energy production63,64, providing a robust roadmap for a sustainable Energy 4.0 model43. The key findings and core takeaways are summarized as follows:

Predictive Maintenance Economic Feasibility: An OLS proxy model-based simulation confirmed that a 30% decrease in system downtime results in a 30.0% reduction in OPEX14. This demonstrates that AI is more than simply a technical tool; it is a critical instrument to support the financial viability of large-scale renewable energy generation in arid environments such as Zabol.

Enhanced High-Precision Forecasting: The Enhanced-LSTM model was found to perform best among all models tested, reaching a (R2) of up to 0.98815,47. The degree of success realized by this model is attributed to its gated memory structure, which enables the internal processing of complex, non-linear temporal relationships that traditional linear and polynomial regression models fail to capture.

Defining and Achieving System Stability: In this investigation, stability is formally defined as maintaining a consistent forecast error distribution where the expanded uncertainty (U95)66,67 remains strictly below the 3.5% threshold. Using a combination of a two-stage AI-assisted model with (Markowitz Optimization and Battery Control), power variance was reduced by 93% to 98%, ensuring that intermittent hybrid resources are converted into a stable and dispatchable energy resource.

Global Scalability and Generalizability: Although the primary empirical focus is on Iranian wind corridors, the framework is designed to be geographically neutral. The methodology is inherently scalable to diverse contexts, such as European offshore wind farms or equatorial solar regions, provided that high-fidelity meteorological data is available43,64.

Recommendations to stakeholders

Policy Makers: Fund the development of AI hardware and subsidize the implementation of hybrid renewable systems39. Support national AI mega-projects and establish partnerships to create data governance frameworks that protect digital sovereignty and address technology gaps39.

Energy Operators: Apply LSTM-based forecasting at the operational level (specifically in Zabol, Manjil, and Binalood) to increase revenues and improve grid reliability. Provide technical training opportunities for engineers working in AI-based energy management.

Researchers: Future studies should investigate CNN-LSTM architectures, utilize high-resolution solar irradiance measurements, and apply Digital Twin technologies9,16 to enhance prediction transparency. Furthermore, developing uncertainty-aware models integrating stochastic frameworks with XAI is essential20,26,75. Finally, bridging the gap between stochastic modeling and cybersecurity is vital for the widespread national adoption of these systems37,43.
