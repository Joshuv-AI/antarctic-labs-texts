Application Prospects of Nanotechnology in the Diagnosis and Treatment of Cardiovascular Diseases
Authors: Zailian Zhou, Jingjing Cui, Yecong Tang, Junlin Wang, Huizhi Mu, Guangzhe Li, Kun Shao, Xuejian Wang, Zhenzhu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCardiovascular diseases pose significant threats to human health due to their high incidence and mortality rates; therefore, early diagnosis, precise treatment, and improvement of long-term prognosis remain key research priorities in this field. Traditional diagnostic methods for cardiovascular diseases primarily rely on echocardiography, contrast-enhanced CT, or MRI, which exhibit low sensitivity and specificity, poor image clarity, and radiation exposure. Additionally, challenges associated with drug targeting efficacy and therapeutic effectiveness should be urgently addressed. By modulating the particle size, surface properties, and functional groups of various nanomaterials, nanotechnology offers superior advantages, including real-time fluorescence imaging, targeted drug delivery, and controlled release. Thanks to these advantages, nanotechnology demonstrates remarkable benefits in the diagnosis and treatment of diseases such as cancer. With the ongoing advancement of interdisciplinary medical-engineering research, nanotechnology has shown considerable potential in cardiovascular disease management. This article systematically reviews the current applications of nanomaterials, including both organic and inorganic ones, in cardiovascular imaging, targeted drug delivery, and integrated diagnosis-treatment approaches, while discussing the current status and challenges in the use of nanotechnology for the management of cardiovascular diseases. The article aims to promote the application of nanotechnology in the diagnosis and treatment of cardiovascular diseases and facilitate ultimate clinical translation.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases refer to a group of disorders involving the heart and the vasculature. Some of the most common cardiovascular diseases include coronary artery disease (CAD), acute myocardial infarction (AMI), heart failure (HF), hypertension, arrhythmias, pulmonary hypertension, and valvular heart disease. These conditions are characterized by extremely high morbidity and mortality rates.1 According to the World Health Organization, approximately 19.8 million people die from cardiovascular diseases annually, accounting for approximately 32% of all global deaths.2 With more in-depth research, diagnostic methods, and therapeutic approaches for cardiovascular diseases are continuously evolving. However, the existing diagnostic and treatment systems still suffer from significant limitations. Traditional imaging techniques have a limited capacity to detect early molecular and cellular-level alterations, making early diagnosis challenging. Furthermore, certain drugs suffer from issues such as low bioavailability, short circulation duration, inadequate targeted delivery efficiency, and systemic toxic side effects. On the other hand, although interventional and surgical treatments are widely employed in clinical practice, they may still induce complications, including vascular endothelial injury, thrombosis, restenosis, and infection.3,4 Therefore, how to achieve early and precise diagnosis, enhance the targeting accuracy and local accumulation efficiency of therapeutic agents, and reduce systemic adverse effects remains a critical issue in the field of cardiovascular disease research.

Nanotechnology refers to an interdisciplinary field involving the design, fabrication, and application of materials at the 1–100 nm scale. Fundamentally, nanotechnology regulates structural and interfacial properties at the nanoscale to provide specific physical, chemical, and biological functions.5 The concept can be traced back to Richard Feynman’s 1959 vision of precise atomic-scale manipulation and has since evolved alongside advances in materials science and bioengineering into a key platform for biomedical research.6 Nanotechnology can modulate the interactions between nanomaterials and biological systems by engineering particles with defined size, morphology, and surface properties. The in vivo behavior of nanomaterials is primarily governed by particle size, shape, and surface characteristics. Particle size determines circulation time and tissue penetration; particle morphology and mechanical properties influence cellular uptake and vascular interactions; while particle surface charge and hydrophilicity/hydrophobicity regulate protein adsorption, biodistribution, and immune clearance.7–9 Based on these properties, nanotechnology has been widely applied to improve drug delivery stability and targeting efficiency, enhance drug accumulation at diseased sites, and enable controlled release.10 Nanomaterials with magnetic, optical, or radioactive properties can be utilized for molecular imaging and diagnostic purposes.

With the interdisciplinary development of nanotechnology and biomedical engineering, nanomedicine has been widely investigated in various diseases, including cancer, infectious diseases, inflammatory disorders, neurological diseases, and tissue injury repair.11,12 Meanwhile, the limitations in the diagnosis and treatment of cardiovascular diseases have driven the gradual introduction of nanotechnology to this field, fostering the development of cardiovascular nanomedicine. Early research in this area primarily focused on drug delivery, utilizing nanocarriers to enhance drug solubility, prolong in vivo circulation duration, improve accumulation efficiency at lesion sites, and minimize systemic adverse effects. However, traditional single-drug delivery systems predominantly rely on passive accumulation or simple targeted delivery and do not offer precise response to lesion microenvironments, real-time imaging and monitoring, or therapeutic feedback mechanisms.13 Consequently, significant challenges remain in determining optimal drug release timing, achieving adequate lesion accumulation, and evaluating therapeutic efficacy. In recent years, cardiovascular nanomedicine has advanced beyond single-drug delivery toward the development of stimulus-responsive systems and integrated diagnostic-treatment platforms.12,14 Although notable progress has been made in preclinical studies, clinical translation still faces many challenges in terms of in vivo stability, targeting accuracy, biostability, biosafety, large-scale production, and quality control. Although a few cardiovascular nanocarriers have entered clinical trials, their long-term safety, metabolic clearance profiles, and clinical effectiveness need further validation due to factors such as material properties, disease-specific microenvironments, and variations in physiological characteristics.

Previous reviews have predominantly focused on specific cardiovascular diseases, individual nanomaterials, or therapeutic efficacy assessment in preclinical settings, neglecting comprehensive comparisons of different nanomaterials and integrated discussions on clinical research, nanotoxicity, and barriers to clinical translation.15,16 To address this gap, this article systematically discussed the applications of various nanomaterials, including liposomes, polymer nanoparticles, micelles, nanofibers, hydrogels, iron oxide nanoparticles, gold nanoparticles, and carbon-based materials, in cardiovascular imaging, targeted drug delivery, myocardial repair, and integrated diagnosis-treatment approaches. By integrating preclinical studies with existing clinical evidence, this review objectively assesses the current state of nanomedicine research in cardiovascular diseases and highlights prevailing translational challenges and key issues requiring resolution before future clinical applications. Applications of Nanotechnology in Cardiovascular Diseases.

Nanomaterials

Nanotechnology has given rise to nanomaterials, which have demonstrated good prospects in disease diagnosis, treatment, and prevention due to their unique physical, chemical, and biological properties. These features establish a robust foundation for the advancement and implementation of nanotechnology in medical fields. Nanomaterials can be divided into two major categories based on their chemical composition: organic and inorganic. Organic nanomaterials are typically composed of organic molecules or polymers and exhibit good biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, and biodegradability. Examples of these nanomaterials include liposomes, polymer nanoparticles, micelles, nanofibers, and hydrogels. The typical representatives of inorganic nanomaterials comprise metals (silver, gold, iron, copper, aluminum, cadmium, cobalt, and zinc), metal oxides (titanium oxide, iron oxide, magnetite, cerium oxide, and zinc oxide), and carbon-based materials. Silica dioxide, lanthanide nanoparticles, quantum dots, and other rare nanoparticles are also classified as inorganic nanomaterials.17,18 Various nanotechnology-based materials used in the medical field are summarized in Table 1. Organic Nanomaterials

Liposomes

Liposomes are spherical lipid vesicles formed by the self-assembly of natural or synthetic phospholipids in an aqueous phase. They are single-layer or multilayer structures whose diameters typically range from 50 to 500 nm.19 Their membrane structure is primarily composed of a phospholipid bilayer, often supplemented with cholesterol to modulate membrane fluidity, permeability, and stability. The liposome structure was first described by Bangham et al in the 1960s and has since evolved into one of the most established nanocarrier systems in the biomedical field.34 Due to their dual composition of a hydrophilic aqueous core and a hydrophobic lipid bilayer, liposomes can encapsulate both hydrophilic and hydrophobic drugs. Factors such as particle size, surface charge, lipid composition, and surface modification predominantly affect the in vivo behavior of liposomes. These factors determine their circulation duration, biodistribution, cellular uptake, and drug release characteristics.35 Conventional liposomes lack specific targeting modifications and mostly bind to plasma proteins upon systemic administration, leading to their recognition and clearance by mononuclear phagocytes. To overcome these limitations, functionalized systems, such as polyethylene glycol-modified liposomes, ligand-modified liposomes, and stimulus-responsive liposomes, have been developed, which can extend circulation time, enhance stability, improve tissue or cellular targeting, and optimize drug release profiles.20

For diagnostic purposes, liposomes, characterized by their hydrophilic interior cavity and hydrophobic lipid bilayer structure, can carry various diagnostic or imaging-related components. Consistently, they have been utilized for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), nuclear medicine imaging, fluorescence imaging, ultrasound imaging, and multimodal imaging.36–38 For instance, by encapsulating components such as gadolinium, manganese, radionuclides, fluorescent dyes, or gases, liposomes can improve the stability and intracellular distribution of imaging probes while enhancing contrast between pathological and normal tissues.39,40 Furthermore, surface modification or conjugation with targeting ligands can enhance liposome accumulation at specific lesion sites, enabling their application in diagnosis, drug delivery monitoring, and therapeutic efficacy evaluation.41

For therapeutic purposes, liposomes improve treatment indices and reduce toxicity in non-targeted tissues by optimizing drug solubility, stability, pharmacokinetic properties, and tissue distribution. Since the approval of polyethylene glycolized liposomal doxorubicin (Doxil®) in 1995,42 numerous liposome formulations have been deployed clinically, primarily for cancer treatment, antifungal treatment, photodynamic therapy (PDT), pain management, and vaccine delivery.43–47 Additionally, liposome formulations can protect biomacromolecules, such as DNA, RNA, and proteins, from degradation and facilitate their cellular delivery, making them highly valuable for vaccine administration and nucleic acid therapy.48 In recent years, liposomes have also been employed to deliver anti-inflammatory drugs, antioxidants, nucleic acid-based therapeutic molecules, or repair-promoting factors, thereby enhancing their delivery efficiency in atherosclerosis, myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury, and myocardial repair.49 However, compared to fields like oncology and infectious diseases, clinical evidence regarding the use of liposomes in the treatment of cardiovascular diseases remains limited. Most of the existing studies are still at the preclinical stage, and the long-term safety, targeting efficacy, large-scale production capabilities, and clinical translation potential of liposomes require further validation.

Polymer Nanoparticles

Polymer nanoparticles are nanoscale carriers composed of natural or synthetic polymers, whose diameters typically range from 10 to 1000 nanometers.50 Based on their degradation characteristics, polymer nanoparticles can be classified into biodegradable and non-biodegradable types. Non-degradable polymers may persist in the body for extended periods, which increases the risk of tissue accumulation and chronic inflammation. Thus, biodegradable polymers have garnered greater attention in drug delivery research. Natural polymers, such as chitosan, alginates, and gelatin, generally exhibit excellent biocompatibility and degradability; however, factors such as source, purity, and preparation methods often affect their performance. Common synthetic biodegradable polymers include poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), polylactic acid (PLA), polyglycolic acid (PGA), and polycaprolactone (PCL). Among them, PLGA has been extensively studied, and several PLGA-based pharmaceutical products have been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA).21,22

In the field of diagnostics, polymer nanoparticles primarily serve as delivery and stabilization platforms for imaging probes. Traditional contrast agents often suffer from issues such as short circulation duration in vivo, inadequate targeting capability, and potential toxicities. In contrast, polymer matrices can enhance colloidal stability, biocompatibility, and lesion accumulation capacity by encapsulating or immobilizing imaging components.51 For instance, PVP-coated zinc ferrite nanoparticles used for MRI imaging not only improved the stability of magnetic particles in biological environments but also mitigated potential risks associated with direct contact between exposed inorganic particles and tissues, thereby enhancing imaging contrast.52 Similarly, polymer-coated iron oxide nanoparticles employed in dual-mode MRI/fluorescence imaging not only provided simultaneous dual imaging signals but also allowed surface modification with targeting molecules. These features enabled probe accumulation at specific lesions or tissues with high receptor expression, thereby improving imaging specificity.53

Concerning therapeutic purposes, polymer nanoparticles were shown to enhance treatment efficacy by altering drug delivery pathways and release profiles in vivo. Drugs can be encapsulated within polymer matrices to prevent premature degradation or rapid clearance. Additionally, polymer degradation or structural modifications contribute to sustained or controlled drug release, maintaining relatively stable local concentrations at target sites. Surface modifications, such as the conjugation of antibodies, peptides, aptamers, and small-molecule ligands, enhance nanoparticle-target cell interaction, improve cellular uptake, and prevent off-target effects. The stimulus-response system further enhances delivery precision. For instance, pH-responsive polymer nanoparticles can be more effective in the acidic environment of tumors, inflammatory tissues, or intracellular endosomes/lysosomes. The acidic microenvironment can trigger drug release from these pH-responsive polymer nanoparticles, enabling targeted action in the pathological microenvironment and preventing premature release in systemic circulation.54 Additionally, polymer nanoparticles can integrate therapeutic drugs with imaging probes onto a single platform, forming an integrated diagnostic and therapeutic system for simultaneous monitoring of nanoparticle distribution, drug release, and treatment response.55,56 However, the clinical translation of complex polymer nanosystems is limited by factors such as preparation complexity, batch reproducibility, long-term safety concerns, and regulatory evaluation requirements.

Micelles

Micelles are nanoscale carriers formed through the self-assembly of amphiphilic molecules or polymers. They are typically characterized by a hydrophilic shell and a hydrophobic core.23 When the concentration of amphiphilic molecules reaches the critical micelle concentration (CMC), the hydrophobic segments aggregate inward, while the hydrophilic segments orient toward the aqueous phase. This results in the formation of a core–shell structure. Since micelles mainly rely on their hydrophobic core for drug loading, they possess limited capacity to encapsulate hydrophilic drugs or macromolecular biopharmaceuticals. Therefore, recent studies have increasingly attempted to improve drug-loading capacity, stability, and targeted delivery through block copolymer design, composite carrier construction, and surface functionalization.57 Amphiphilic diblock copolymers, such as polyethylene glycol–polycaprolactone (PEG-PCL) and polyethylene glycol–polylactic acid (PEG-PLA), as well as triblock copolymers, such as poloxamers, are commonly used copolymers. Graft copolymers and cationic copolymers are also commonly employed for micelle preparation.58

For diagnostic purposes, polymeric micelles are mainly employed to optimize the dispersibility, stability, and lesion-targeted delivery of imaging components in vivo. Polymeric micelles can encapsulate complex contrast agents, fluorescent molecules, and other imaging components, while their surface modification contributes to their accumulation at lesion sites. For instance, Maghsoudinia et al developed ultrasound-responsive nanodroplets loaded with Gd-DOTA and doxorubicin (DOX). This system enabled MRI/ultrasound imaging and antitumor drug delivery. Under ultrasound stimulation, acoustic droplet vaporization (ADV) promoted local drug release and allowed imaging-guided drug administration.59

Regarding therapeutic applications, polymeric micelles are mainly applied to improve the delivery of poorly water-soluble drugs and promote drug release at lesion sites.24 Poor water solubility, undesirable biodistribution, and considerable systemic toxicity can limit the application of drugs such as paclitaxel (PTX), docetaxel (DTX), and DOX. Polymeric micelles can improve the aqueous dispersibility and circulation stability of such drugs. Genexol-PM is a PTX-loaded monomethoxy polyethylene glycol-poly(D, L-lactide) (mPEG-PDLLA) polymeric micelle formulation that has been approved for the treatment of breast cancer and non-small cell lung cancer in South Korea. This formulation delivers PTX in a cremophor EL-free micellar form and reduces the adverse effects of conventional solubilizing agents.60 Stimuli-responsive micelles can improve drug release control. For instance, pH-responsive polymeric micelles designed by Bolu et al promoted the release of DTX in acidic conditions.61 Luteinizing hormone-releasing hormone (LHRH)-targeted redox-responsive crosslinked micelles can leverage elevated levels of glutathione (GSH) in tumor cells to trigger drug release and enhance the efficacy of chemotherapeutic agents in preclinical models of triple-negative breast cancer.62 Compared with cancer, there is limited evidence regarding the application of polymeric micelles for cardiovascular diseases. Most studies in this regard mainly focused on the preclinical models of atherosclerosis, vascular inflammation, and ischemic injury.

Nanometer Fibers

Nanofibers are fine fibers with a diameter of 1–1000 nanometers. Their structural characteristics include a highly specific surface area, high porosity, surface functionalization, and three-dimensional network structures resembling extracellular matrices. Nanofibers can be prepared via electrospinning, self-assembly, and phase separation. Due to its relatively mature process, broad material applicability, and ability to control fiber diameter, orientation, and pore structure, electrospinning is widely adopted in biomedical research.63

Regarding diagnostic purposes, nanofibers are mainly applied as sensing platforms. Electrospun nanofibers possess a large specific surface area, porous structures, and easily functionalized surfaces. These features allow the immobilization of recognition molecules, such as enzymes, antibodies, and nucleic acid probes, thereby improving target capture efficiency. When combined with conductive polymers, metals, or metal oxides, nanofibers can enhance electrochemical or optical signal responses; therefore, they have been widely investigated in electrochemical sensing, optical sensing, and biosensing.25,26

For therapeutic applications, nanofibers are mainly used for tissue engineering, wound repair, local drug delivery, and implantable scaffolds. Since electrospun nanofibers can mimic the fibrous structure of the native extracellular matrix (ECM), they can support cell adhesion, proliferation, and tissue regeneration. For example, a polycaprolactone/gelatin–bioactive glass (PCL/gelatin–BG) bilayer electrospun nanofibrous scaffold was used in bone tissue engineering, where PCL provided mechanical support, gelatin enhanced cell compatibility, and BG contributed to osteogenic processes. Collectively, these features enhanced the ability of the scaffold to support bone repair.64 Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) electrospun nanofibers loaded with epidermal growth factor (EGF) and fibroblast growth factor (FGF) have been developed as biological dressing scaffolds for wound repair. EGF and FGF can promote the function of epithelial cells and fibroblasts, thereby supporting wound healing.65,66 Core–shell electrospun nanofibers can encapsulate drugs such as curcumin, tetracycline, and anticancer agents in the core layer and regulate drug diffusion through the outer polymer layer, enabling relatively sustained local release.67 As implantable scaffolds, electrospun micro/nanofibrous small-diameter vascular grafts can provide tubular support structures, while thermoplastic polyurethane/silk fibroin (TPU/SF) nanofiber–hydrogel composite vascular grafts can improve scaffold flexibility and cellular compatibility.68 Overall, substantial experimental evidence supports the value of nanofibers in tissue repair, local therapy, and tissue replacement materials; however, issues such as degradation control, cell infiltration, long-term mechanical stability, and scalable manufacturing consistency still restrict their clinical application.

Hydrogels

Hydrogels are hydrophilic polymer networks formed through physical or chemical crosslinking of polymer chains, resulting in a three-dimensional structure.69 Owing to their high water content, tunable mechanical properties, and favorable biocompatibility, hydrogels have been widely investigated as biomaterial platforms in the field of regenerative medicine and drug delivery. Based on their origin, hydrogels can be classified into natural (eg, collagen, hyaluronic acid, and cellulose), synthetic (eg, polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) and polyethylene glycol (PEG)), and semi-synthetic systems. The latter integrates the bioactivity of natural polymers with the structural tunability of synthetic materials.

The application of hydrogels in biomedical research primarily focuses on localized drug delivery, tissue repair, wound management, and injectable therapeutic platforms.27 Their highly hydrated three-dimensional networks can encapsulate drugs, growth factors, cells, and extracellular vesicles. This enables the controlled local release of therapeutic agents through diffusion, material degradation, or stimulus-responsive mechanisms. Certain hydrogels can fill irregular tissue defects via injection or in situ gel formation, thereby prolonging the local retention time of therapeutic agents.28 For instance, in myocardial infarction, injectable hydrogels and hydrogel patches are employed to deliver cells, angiogenic factors, or extracellular vesicles, modulate the local microenvironment of the damaged myocardium, and support myocardial repair.70,71 In wound management, hydrogel dressings can maintain a moist environment, absorb exudates, and carry antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, or pro-healing components to improve burn, chronic wounds, and infectious wounds.72 Additionally, conductive hydrogels and stimulus-responsive hydrogels can be employed in flexible sensing and wearable monitoring systems to detect glucose, lactate, pH changes, or inflammatory molecules in wound exudates.73,74 Overall, hydrogels offer advantages such as excellent hydration properties, localized retention capacity, and controllable release characteristics; however, factors such as mechanical strength, degradation rate, batch consistency, sterilization methods, scalability, and long-term biocompatibility confine their clinical application.

Inorganic Nanomaterials

Superparamagnetic Iron Oxide Nanoparticles (SPIONs)

SPIONs typically consist of a magnetite (Fe3O4) or maghemite (γ-Fe2O3) core with a surface coating, and exhibit superparamagnetism and strong magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) relaxation effects. Surface modification with dextran, carboxydextran, polyethylene glycol (PEG), silica, or other polymers can modulate the colloidal stability, circulation time, protein adsorption, and cellular uptake of SPIONs. Based on these properties, SPIONs and related iron oxide nanocarriers have been widely utilized to advance contrast-enhanced MRI, cell tracking, inflammation imaging, magnetic targeting, and magnetic hyperthermia.

Concerning diagnostic purposes, SPIONs can alter local signal intensity on T2/T2*-weighted MRI, thereby enhancing the contrast between lesion sites and adjacent normal tissues. Adjustment of particle size, surface coatings, and targeting ligands can further improve the in vivo stability of SPIONs and increase their accumulation in specific tissues, inflammatory sites, or pathological microenvironments.29 Therefore, they have been used not only for contrast enhancement in MRI but also for cell tracking, inflammation imaging, and molecular imaging. For example, ferumoxides and ferucarbotran were previously used for liver MRI. They mainly enhance focal liver lesions through uptake by the mononuclear phagocytes.29 In addition, SPIONs targeting vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1), P-selectin, or macrophage-associated signals have been explored for molecular imaging of inflammation and vascular lesions, reflecting inflammatory cell infiltration, endothelial activation, and changes in the lesion microenvironment in MRI.30,75

Regarding therapeutic applications, SPIONs are mainly used for magnetic field-assisted delivery and magnetic hyperthermia. Owing to their magnetic responsiveness, SPIONs contribute to the local accumulation of drug-loaded particles at lesion sites under an external magnetic field, thereby improving the delivery efficiency of drugs, genes, or bioactive molecules while minimizing exposure in non-target tissues.76 The surfaces of SPIONs can also be modified with targeting peptides, antibodies, or polymer coatings to improve their stability and lesion-targeting ability. Under an alternating magnetic field, SPIONs generate localized heat; therefore, they have been widely investigated for magnetic hyperthermia, particularly in cancer treatment.77 Under magnetic guidance, drug-loaded SPIONs can increase local drug concentrations in cancer or injured tissues. Cardiac homing peptide-modified, melatonin-loaded SPIONs were used to enhance cardiac accumulation and prevent myocardial fibrosis in animal models of cardiac injury.78 These studies indicate that the therapeutic value of SPIONs lies in combining magnetic responsiveness, targeted delivery, local accumulation, and externally triggered treatment. However, in vivo iron metabolism, long-term safety, immune responses, and precise magnetic-field control in deep tissues should be considered for further application.

Gold Nanoparticles (AuNPs)

AuNPs are noble metal nanomaterials with tunable size, morphology, and surface chemistry. Owing to the high atomic number and unique electronic properties of gold, AuNPs exhibit localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR), surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS), and efficient photothermal conversion. These features support the application of AuNPs in imaging, sensing, and photothermal therapy (PTT). Modulation of the morphology of AuNPs and preparing them in different forms, such as gold nanospheres, nanorods, nanoshells, and nanocages, can help further adjust their optical absorption wavelength, signal-enhancing ability, and photothermal conversion efficiency.

Regarding diagnostic applications, AuNPs are mainly used as probes for computed tomography (CT), photoacoustic imaging (PAI), SERS, optical imaging, and multimodal imaging. Their high atomic number enhances X-ray attenuation, making them ideal contrast agents for CT scan, whereas LSPR and SERS effects contribute to signal amplification, molecular detection, and lesion tracking. Gold nanorods, nanoshells, and nanocages exhibit strong absorption in the near-infrared (NIR) region; therefore, they are frequently used for PAI and image-guided interventions. Recent reviews have shown that AuNP-based diagnostic systems are mainly applied in tumor imaging and molecular detection. They are also being explored for infection, inflammation, and atherosclerotic conditions. For example, targeted AuNPs can enhance CT or photoacoustic signals in tumor sites, while AuNP-based probes can help identify macrophages, lipid components, or inflammatory signals in atherosclerotic plaques.79–81

Regarding therapeutic applications, AuNPs are mainly investigated for PTT, radiosensitization, drug delivery, and treating bacterial infections. Their photothermal effect enables local heating under NIR irradiation, which can be used for tumor ablation or photothermal treatment of bacterial infections.31,32 In addition, the surfaces of AuNPs can be modified with drugs, nucleic acids, antibodies, or targeting ligands to construct delivery systems or theranostic platforms. Unlike cancer, the therapeutic application of AuNPs in cardiovascular diseases remains largely in the preclinical phase and mainly involves plaque targeting, macrophage enrichment, and local photothermal modulation.82,83 Before further biomedical application of AuNPs, future studies should address the long-term tissue retention, biological clearance, nonspecific distribution, limited light penetration depth, potential thermal injury, and manufacturing consistency of AuNPs.

Carbon Nanomaterials (CNMs)

CNMs are carbon-based materials composed of carbon atoms as the basic structural units, with at least one dimension smaller than 100 nm. Common types of CNMs include carbon nanotubes (CNTs), graphene and its derivatives, fullerenes, and carbon dots. CNMs have been widely investigated for biomedical applications thanks to their high specific surface area, favorable electrical and thermal properties, tunable surface chemistry, and certain mechanical strength.33

In diagnostic research, CNMs are mainly used for biosensing, molecular detection, and imaging probe development. Graphene and reduced graphene oxide (rGO) exhibit favorable electrical conductivity and modifiable surfaces; thus, they are commonly utilized in electrochemical biosensors and field-effect transistor (FET)-based sensing platforms.84 For example, a platinum nanoparticle-modified rGO-FET sensor has been used to detect B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP), a biomarker of HF, in whole blood samples, showing a low detection limit and sufficient specificity.85 Because of their fluorescence properties, water dispersibility, and surface modifiability, carbon dots are used in fluorescence imaging and molecular detection. Gadolinium-modified fullerenes and other carbon-based materials have also been explored as contrast agents for MRI.86

For therapeutic and regenerative medicine purposes, CNMs are mainly used for drug delivery, PTT, and production of tissue engineering scaffolds and antibacterial materials. The electrical conductivity of CNTs and graphene-based materials makes them ideal candidates for electroactive tissue engineering, particularly cardiac tissue engineering.87 CNT composite scaffolds can improve cardiomyocyte electrical coupling, synchronous contraction, and functional maturation. Graphene oxide (GO) or rGO can also be combined with hydrogels, silk fibroin, and other materials to improve conductivity and mechanical properties. For instance, injectable oligo(polyethylene glycol fumarate)/graphene oxide (OPF/GO) hydrogels have been administered in animal models of myocardial infarction to provide mechanical support and enhance electrical signal conduction in the infarct region and surrounding myocardium.88 In addition, CNMs have been investigated for drug delivery, PTT, and synthesis of antibacterial materials.89 However, long-term in vivo retention, degradation/clearance behavior, dose-related toxicity, and batch-to-batch consistency remain key issues for the translation of CNMs in the biomedical field.

Application of Nanotechnology in CVDs

The demands for early diagnosis, targeted therapy, and efficacy monitoring in cardiovascular diseases underscore the need for nanotechnology. In recent years, cardiovascular nanomedicine research has evolved from drug delivery to disease diagnosis, therapeutic delivery, and integrated diagnostic-therapeutic platforms. Nanoparticle-based systems can enhance drug circulation in the body, promote localized accumulation at the lesion site, and advance molecular imaging capabilities by modulating particle size, surface properties, and functional modifications. However, most existing studies remain at the preclinical stage, and challenges such as insufficient targeting efficiency under complex hemodynamic conditions, limited penetration of biological barriers, uncertain in vivo distribution, and long-term biosafety concerns hinder clinical translation. Therefore, this section investigates the diagnostic, therapeutic, and integrated diagnostic-therapeutic applications of nanotechnology across various cardiovascular diseases and analyzes their scientific foundations, translational potential, and limitations.

CAD

CAD is one of the major cardiovascular diseases with high incidence and mortality rates worldwide. The pathogenesis of CAD involves the gradual accumulation of lipid and inflammatory plaques in the coronary arteries, leading to luminal narrowing and vascular wall stiffness. Subsequently, these alterations result in insufficient myocardial blood supply and myocardial hypoxia. In severe cases, CAD can progress to myocardial infarction or even myocardial necrosis. Thus, atherosclerosis is the key risk factor for CAD, while myocardial infarction represents its most severe clinical manifestation.

Early diagnosis is critical to reduce the risk of acute cardiovascular events. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), computed tomography (CT), positron emission tomography (PET), and fluorescence imaging techniques are the conventional imaging modalities used to assess atherosclerosis. These methods can only detect advanced atherosclerotic lesions, hemodynamic abnormalities, or significant morphological changes of the vasculature, yet fail to accurately evaluate plaque vulnerability.90 Nanomaterials have been leveraged to overcome these limitations by enabling biological visualization of plaque components and the inflammatory status, thereby precisely evaluating disease progression. For instance, targeted imaging of specific cells, such as macrophages, vascular endothelial cells, and vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), can contribute to early and accurate diagnosis and monitoring of CAD.

Macrophages are pivotal inflammatory cells in atherosclerotic plaques. These cells are involved in processes such as lipid uptake, foam cell formation, inflammatory cytokine release, and matrix degradation. The abundance and activation status of macrophages are closely associated with plaque progression and instability. Therefore, nanoscale imaging strategies targeting macrophages have become a critical focus in molecular diagnostic research for atherosclerosis.91 Scavenger receptor A (SR-A) is highly expressed in activated macrophages and serves as a potential target for imaging inflammatory plaques. Hou et al developed SR-A-targeted PLGA-PEG-PLGA nanoparticles loaded with the near-infrared fluorescent dye DiR and the magnetic imaging component Fe3O4. In an atherosclerosis model, this dual-modal nanoplatform accumulated in the macrophage-rich regions of the aortic arch, enabling combined near-infrared fluorescence/MRI plaque imaging.92 Osteopontin (OPN) is a secretory protein associated with inflammatory cell recruitment and foam cell activation, whose expression was shown to correlate with plaque instability. Using persistent luminescence nanoparticles (PLNPs), Zhang et al developed an OPN-targeted probe for imaging studies of early-stage vulnerable plaques.93 CD40, as an inflammation-related co-stimulatory molecule, was also utilized for molecular imaging of atherosclerotic plaques. Wu et al developed a dual-modal probe targeting CD40. The probe was composed of SPIONs and the fluorescent dye Cy5.5 (CD40-Cy5.5-SPIONs), highlighting the capabilities of MRI-optical imaging in an experimental model of atherosclerosis. In addition to targeting receptors or inflammatory molecules, the phagocytic function of macrophages is frequently employed to promote the accumulation of iron oxide nanoparticles, lipid nanoparticles, or polymer nanoparticles in inflammatory plaques.94,95

Vascular endothelial injury triggers the release of multiple adhesion molecules, contributing to inflammatory cell infiltration and plaque rupture. Hence, adhesion molecules overexpressed in atherosclerotic lesions represent promising diagnostic targets. Vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1) is a typical endothelial biomarker for imaging atherosclerotic lesions. VHPKQHR (VHP) peptide-modified magnetic liposomes were constructed to target VCAM-1. These liposomes enabled targeted imaging of atherosclerotic plaques and prolonged the circulation time of loaded therapeutic drugs.96 Moreover, integrin αvβ3 is overexpressed in the hypoxic endothelial cells of newly formed blood vessels in atherosclerotic plaques. Toshiro et al designed RGD-modified ferritin nanoparticles targeting αvβ3. The nanoparticles highly accumulated in plaque neovascular endothelial cells and enhanced MRI signals for accurate visualization of intra-plaque angiogenesis.97

VSMCs are a critical cellular component of atherosclerotic plaques, with their phenotypic transformation, proliferation, migration, and extracellular matrix remodeling playing pivotal roles in plaque formation and stability regulation. Consequently, VSMC-related molecules have also been employed in molecular imaging of atherosclerotic plaques. Profilin-1 is a specific biomarker of VSMCs. Zhang et al fabricated a multifunctional theranostic nanoparticle targeting profilin-1. This system combines high-resolution MRI anatomical imaging with sensitive NIRF molecular detection. Such dual-modal imaging supports dynamic monitoring of plaque morphology and molecular alterations, helping evaluate lesion progression and guide therapeutic decisions.98 In addition to cell-targeted imaging strategies, synergistic optimization of nanobiosensors and molecular imaging technology can support the early diagnosis of CAD. Shand et al reviewed cardiovascular diagnostic nanosystems. Gold nanoparticle and quantum dot biosensors achieved fg/mL-level ultrasensitive detection of circulating cardiac biomarkers, such as cTnI and BNP. Nanoparticle contrast agents also advance MRI and PET imaging of plaque structure and inflammation.99

Beyond plaque identification and assessment of vulnerability features, local intervention targeting coronary atherosclerotic lesions represents another important direction in the treatment of CAD using nanomedicine. Compared with systemic administration, nanotherapeutic strategies emphasize the local delivery and retention of therapeutic molecules in atherosclerotic plaques or vascular injury sites to reduce exposure in non-target tissues.16 Recent studies have focused on the nanodelivery of conventional anti-atherosclerotic agents. For instance, they loaded statins, anti-inflammatory drugs, rapamycin, or antioxidants into liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, or reconstituted high-density lipoprotein nanoparticles to enhance plaque accumulation and optimize drug release behavior.30 Duivenvoorden et al developed simvastatin-loaded reconstituted high-density lipoprotein nanoparticles, which accumulated in the atherosclerotic plaques of ApoE-deficient mice and suppressed the inflammatory responses of plaque macrophages.100 With a deeper insight into the plaque immune microenvironment, CAD nanotherapy has gradually progressed from the delivery of lipid-lowering or anti-proliferative drugs to the regulation of macrophage function and inflammation. Flores et al reported a pro-efferocytic single-walled carbon nanotube system that was taken up by lesional macrophages. The carbon nanotube system promoted the clearance of apoptotic cells by modulating CD47/SIRPα signaling, thereby reducing atherosclerotic burden in ApoE-deficient mice.101 Macrophage membrane-coated ROS-responsive nanoparticles have also been used to deliver atorvastatin and sequester pro-inflammatory cytokines. These nanoparticles exhibited anti-inflammatory and plaque-modulating properties in the mouse models of atherosclerosis.102 Some nanoplatforms also integrate imaging and therapeutic functions for plaque localization, drug delivery, and monitoring of therapeutic response. For example, profilin-1-targeted magnetic nanoparticles were loaded with rapamycin and used for MRI/NIRF imaging and treatment of atherosclerotic plaques, enabling plaque identification and local drug delivery using the same platform.103 Beyond plaque-directed therapy, nanotechnology has also been applied to post-PCI restenosis and stent-related local treatment. Drug-eluting stents, nanocoated stents, and locally controlled-release systems can deliver rapamycin, paclitaxel, or other anti-proliferative drugs to inhibit excessive VSMC proliferation and neointimal formation while promoting endothelial repair.104

Overall, nanotherapy for CAD has mainly focused on plaque targeting and local delivery strategies in preclinical settings. Most data are still from in vitro experiments and animal models, while clinical translation remains limited to a small number of early-stage human studies. Future research endeavors should address lesion-specific delivery in coronary arteries, long-term safety, immune responses, and integration with existing interventional treatment systems.

AMI

Among various types of cardiovascular diseases, AMI is associated with a sudden onset, high mortality, and a high recurrence rate. It severely impairs cardiac pumping function, electrophysiological stability, quality of life, and systemic metabolic balance. Cardiomyocyte apoptosis, inflammatory infiltration, and cardiac fibrosis contribute to the progression of AMI. Targeted interventions against these processes can alleviate myocardial injury and preserve cardiac function. Therefore, studies have explored the application of nanotechnology in the early diagnosis of acute MI, rapid assessment of target lesions, and precise treatment.

Nanotechnology is mainly employed for highly sensitive detection of myocardial biomarkers and molecular imaging of myocardial injury after AMI. In vitro, nanomaterials can improve the detection of low-concentration cardiac troponin I (cTnI) by increasing the sensing interface, enhancing electron transfer, and amplifying detection signals. For example, Mansuriya et al developed a graphene quantum dot/gold nanoparticle (GQD/AuNP)-modified screen-printed gold electrode immunosensor, which provided an interface for anti-cTnI antibody immobilization and enhanced cTnI detection sensitivity.105 Similarly, graphene oxide/AuNP amplification systems have been employed for surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS)-based immunodetection of cTnI and enabled the analysis of low-abundance biomarkers.106 For in vivo imaging, nanoprobe systems can help visualize cardiomyocyte apoptosis and inflammatory cell infiltration after AMI. Annexin V-labeled cross-linked iron oxide nanoparticles can recognize phosphatidylserine exposed on the surface of apoptotic cells. Herein, AnxCLIO-Cy5.5 was applied to MRI/near-infrared fluorescence imaging of early cardiomyocyte apoptosis in the mouse models of ischemia/reperfusion injury.107 In addition, magnetofluorescent iron oxide nanoparticles, such as CLIO-Cy5.5, can be taken up by macrophages infiltrating the infarct foci. With the help of these nanoparticles, MRI and fluorescence imaging can be used to more effectively monitor inflammatory responses after AMI.108 Overall, the value of nanotechnology in the diagnosis of AMI lies mainly in improving the sensitivity of biomarker detection and visualizing early pathological changes, although most molecular imaging probes remain at the preclinical stage.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) play a pivotal role in regulating cardiomyocyte apoptosis, inflammatory responses, angiogenesis, and myocardial repair, making them important targets in the treatment of AMI. However, miRNAs exhibit poor stability in vivo, are prone to degradation by nucleases, and may lead to off-target effects; thus, their application relies on appropriate delivery systems. Hong et al fabricated a poly-L-lysine/low-molecular-weight heparin core-shell nanocomposite loaded with a miR-1 inhibitor to relieve microvascular obstruction in the infarct foci. The heparin shell suppressed fibrin deposition and factor Xa activity to improve microcirculation, while targeted delivery of the miR-1 inhibitor restrained cardiomyocyte apoptosis.109 Yu et al synthesized PEI-modified silica nanoparticles (F-silica-miR-24) to deliver cytoplasmic miR-24. The nanosystem downregulated the pro-apoptotic molecule Bim and inhibited cardiomyocyte apoptosis and fibrosis, thereby mitigating pathological remodeling after AMI.110 Chen et al designed a ZIF-8 nanoparticle-conductive microneedle patch for local sustained delivery of miR-30d. ZIF-8 prevented the lysosomal degradation of miR-30d, and the patch co-delivered AuNPs to reconstruct electrical conduction in the infarct zone. This platform maintained stable miR-30d expression in the myocardium and prevented cardiomyocyte apoptosis and fibrosis.111

In AMI, macrophages regulate the immune microenvironment through M1/M2 phenotype switching. M1-type macrophages primarily mediate inflammatory responses and remove necrotic tissues, whereas M2-type macrophages resolve inflammation and promote angiogenesis and tissue repair. Accordingly, various nanodelivery systems have been employed to modulate macrophage phenotypic switch and modify the inflammatory environment following myocardial injury. For instance, Liu et al developed an inflammation-responsive injectable carrier for sustained release of curcumin and recombinant human type III collagen. GelB-Cur nanoparticles reduced the proportion of M1 macrophages and promoted M2 polarization, thereby improving cardiac function and preventing adverse ventricular remodeling.112 Likewise, the polydopamine-modified black phosphorus nanosheet composite hydrogel generated by Zhang et al exhibited sustained release in the infarcted region, regulated macrophage polarization, eliminated reactive oxygen species (ROS), and suppressed oxidative stress and inflammation.113 Zhu et al designed platelet membrane-coated PLGA nanoparticles loaded with berberine (BBR@PLGA@PLT). The biomimetic nanoparticles induced M2 polarization of macrophages in the infarct zone, potentiated cardiac function, downregulated collagen deposition in the myocardium, and stimulated angiogenesis.114

In the repair phase following AMI, elevated levels of growth factors and cytokines can transform cardiac fibroblasts into myofibroblasts, leading to excessive deposition of extracellular matrix and pathological myocardial fibrosis. Ultimately, these alterations impair cardiac function and increase the risk of HF. Currently, there are limited effective intervention strategies targeting this process. Fu et al prepared acellular peritoneal matrix nanodroplets loaded with pirfenidone to extend drug release and mitigate cardiac fibrosis.115 Ji et al constructed GSH-responsive nanoparticles that deliver anti-fibrotic agents selectively to activated cardiac fibroblasts in the infarct zone. This targeted system enhances the efficacy of anti-fibrotic therapy and minimizes systemic side effects.116 Gu et al reported a tannic acid-modified MnO2 nanoenzyme (MnO2@TA) with superoxide dismutase-and catalase-like properties. The nanoparticle scavenged ROS and inhibited fibroblast activation, thereby preventing myocardial fibrosis.117

In summary, nanomedicine research related to AMI has encompassed multiple areas, including early biomarker detection, molecular imaging, and targeted therapy. Previous animal studies have demonstrated significant application potential in terms of miRNA delivery, immune regulation, and anti-fibrotic interventions. Nevertheless, relevant clinical trials are scarce, and most multifunctional nanomaterials have not been translated into clinical trials.

HF

HF is the terminal stage of various cardiovascular diseases and is accompanied by prominent clinical dilemmas, including low drug delivery efficiency and insufficient myocardial regeneration capacity. Heart transplantation remains the only radical treatment for end-stage HF, yet its widespread application is restricted by donor shortage, surgical complexity, high medical costs, and postoperative complications, such as immune rejection. These limitations highlight the urgent need for innovative therapeutic strategies. In this context, the integration of nanotechnology with stem cell therapy and cardiac tissue engineering has gradually emerged as an important direction for myocardial regeneration therapy.

Stem cell therapy offers a promising regenerative strategy for the treatment of HF. It can promote the repair of damaged cardiomyocytes and partly improve cardiac function. However, the transplanted stem cells exhibit significant early loss in the myocardium, with approximately 95–99% of stem cells undergoing apoptosis or migrating from the lesion site within 72–80 hours after transplantation, resulting in low retention rates and implantation efficiency. This issue is primarily attributed to inadequate myocardial homing capacity, the local inflammatory microenvironment, and cellular damage due to oxidative stress.118–120 To address these challenges, nanotechnology has been employed to optimize stem cell delivery and the local microenvironment, thereby enhancing cell retention and survival in the myocardium. Tang et al leveraged the inherent targeting properties of platelets to combine them with stem cells. In rat and pig models of AMI, this system enhanced the enrichment and colonization efficiency of stem cells in the ischemic regions of the myocardium.121 Tian et al developed an injectable adhesive hydrogel embedded with pigment nanoparticles to maintain the viability of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells and modulate the inflammatory microenvironment. In an HF model, this combination promoted angiogenesis, inhibited myocardial fibrosis, and prevented adverse ventricular remodeling.122 Furthermore, melatonin has been used as a powerful endogenous antioxidant to protect cells against oxidative damage. However, melatonin cannot exert long-lasting effects in harsh microenvironments. Therefore, Ma et al developed a melatonin (Mel)-loaded poly(lactide-co-glycolide)-monomethoxy-poly-(polyethylene glycol) (PLGA-mPEG) sustained-release delivery system. These Mel-NPs effectively protected adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (ADSCs) against hypoxia and reoxygenation. In addition, compared with free melatonin, the Mel-NPs elevated the survival rates of ADSCs in rats with AMI.123

Cardiac tissue engineering offers a structure-based regenerative strategy for treating HF. It combines biomimetic scaffolds with cell therapy to simulate the ECM in the myocardium, thereby promoting cell adhesion, growth, and functional recovery. Nanofiber scaffolds are typically fabricated via electrospinning, phase separation, or self-assembly. They enable the structural replication of the nanoscale fiber network of the ECM and provide mechanical support and a growth interface for cardiomyocytes, thereby facilitating cell alignment and functional maturation.124,125 For instance, electrospun PLGA nanofiber scaffolds developed by Nguyen et al supported the adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation of cardiac progenitor cells into functional cardiomyocytes. Benefiting from their large specific surface area, porous 3D architecture, and ECM-mimicking features, PLGA nanofibers optimize cardiomyocyte morphology and electrophysiology, enabling the fabrication of contractile myocardial grafts.126 Thus, conductive nanomaterials, such as carbon nanomaterials and gold nanoparticles, can be utilized to enhance the electrical conductivity of scaffolds and improve the synchronized contraction of cardiomyocytes. Bioprinting technology can also be employed to construct vascularized structures and improve tissue integration. Furthermore, nanofiber materials can be processed into cardiac patches or injectable hydrogels, which limit ventricular dilation and improve cardiac function through mechanical support and local remodeling, thereby decelerating the progression of HF.127,128

In summary, nanotechnology-assisted stem cell therapy and cardiac tissue engineering have indicated significant potential for myocardial repair; however, most findings remain confined to animal experiments with limited evidence supporting clinical translation. Currently, only a small number of nanotherapies have entered early-stage clinical trials, and their outcomes are still preliminary. Overall, this field remains far from developing reproducible and clinically validated treatment systems.

Hypertension

Hypertension is among the most common cardiovascular diseases worldwide.129 Oral antihypertensive medications remain the first-line treatment option; however, they often suffer from short plasma half-life and low bioavailability due to the hepatic first-pass effect. In addition, they may lead to systemic adverse effects, including angioedema, reflex tachycardia, and orthostatic hypotension. Therefore, it is of significant importance to develop drug delivery systems with controlled release, prolonged circulation duration, and stable antihypertensive effects. In this context, nanodrug delivery systems are increasingly employed to enhance the pharmacokinetic profile and therapeutic efficacy of antihypertensive medications.

Dugad et al fabricated azinidine (AZN)-loaded solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs) via hot-melt emulsification. Optimized nanostructures can greatly improve drug solubility and oral bioavailability, while a degradable lipid matrix can enable sustained drug release for the management of hypertension.130 Yao et al encapsulated hydrophobic isoliensinine into PEG-PLGA nanocarriers (PEG-PLGA@isoliensinine), overcoming its extremely low aqueous solubility and poor bioavailability (2.44%). Benefiting from the EPR effect, these nanoparticles accumulate at hypertensive lesion sites and achieve controlled release.131 Dudhipala et al prepared irbesartan (IR)-loaded SLNs modified with hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin (HP-β-CD/SLNs-IR). The cyclodextrin inclusion complex markedly improved the solubility of this angiotensin II receptor antagonist, enhancing oral bioavailability and prolonging pharmacodynamic properties.132 Trani et al engineered PEG-PCL polymeric micelles for sustained delivery of amlodipine. In vitro tests revealed a 2.5-fold higher cumulative drug release with these polymeric micelles compared with free amlodipine, forming a controllable nanoplatform for personalized treatment of hypertension in the long term.133 Zhao et al developed lipid–polymer hybrid lipid nanoparticles (YF4-LNPs) loaded with the tetrapeptide YF4 (Tyr-Gly-Leu-Phe). By combining the characteristics of solid lipids and polymers, the formulation effectively protected polypeptide drugs against enzymatic degradation in the gastrointestinal tract and improved transmembrane permeability.134 Similarly, Li et al synthesized folate-targeted lipid nanoparticles (FA-VP5-LNPs) loaded with the ACE inhibitory peptide VP5. The nanosystem revealed favorable stability and sustained-release capacity, and its potent antihypertensive effects lasted up to 6 days in rat models, resolving the need for frequent administration and improving patient compliance.135

Overall, nanotechnology has the potential to enhance the delivery characteristics of antihypertensive drugs by modulating their solubility, bioavailability, and sustained-release behavior. However, the clinical translation of previous findings remains constrained by factors such as pharmacokinetic complexity, in vivo targeting efficiency, and scalability.

Arrhythmia

Arrhythmia describes disturbances in cardiac rhythm due to aberrant cardiac electrophysiology.136 Current mainstay treatments for arrhythmia include antiarrhythmic medications, catheter-based radiofrequency ablation, and cardiovascular implantable electronic devices (CIEDs), such as pacemakers, implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs), and cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) systems. Each of these conventional approaches has its own inherent drawbacks. Pharmacological intervention is associated with systemic side effects, while ablation brings complications associated with invasive procedures.137,138 Meanwhile, implantation of CIEDs raises concerns regarding device infection, lead dislodgement, and electromagnetic interference risks.139 Against this backdrop, nanotechnology and nanomaterials have increasingly been explored to optimize drug delivery in the treatment of arrhythmia and resolve complications associated with medical devices. Nanoscale drug carriers fabricated via nanotechnology can help address the inherent drawbacks of conventional oral antiarrhythmic formulations. Shah et al used a Caco-2 monolayer cell model to investigate the intestinal absorption of carvedilol-loaded solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs). The nanosystem facilitated transmembrane drug transport through clathrin-mediated endocytosis and improved the intestinal bioavailability of carvedilol.140 Likewise, Jang et al incorporated mesoporous silica nanoparticles into solid self-emulsifying drug delivery systems (S-SNEDDS) to build a carvedilol delivery platform. The combined formulation improved drug solubility, physical stability, and tissue targeting, supporting better absorption of agents prone to strong hepatic first-pass metabolism and optimizing the in vivo effects of oral beta-blockers.141 In terms of alternative delivery routes, Jaiswal et al developed an SLN-based transdermal patch to deliver diltiazem hydrochloride, enhance the skin permeability of drugs, and promote the efficiency of drug loading, thereby overcoming the limitations of conventional transdermal systems.142 Maryam et al developed liposomes for praloxol delivery, which achieved an encapsulation rate of approximately 70% and improved drug stability and bioavailability.143 Furthermore, Motawea et al developed PLGA nanoparticles for amiodarone delivery. These PLGA nanoparticles maintained antiarrhythmic activity in animal models and reduced drug-related toxicity; however, the long-term safety and clinical translation of these nanoparticles still require further validation.144

Additionally, nanotechnology can lower the risk of complications associated with catheter radiofrequency ablation and cardiovascular implantable electronic devices. Liu et al fabricated budesonide-loaded PLGA nanoparticles for local drug delivery. During radiofrequency catheter ablation (RFCA), the nanoparticles responded to local thermal stimulation produced by ablation electrodes, thereby enabling on-demand controlled drug release. This smart delivery system relieved acute myocardial inflammation triggered by radiofrequency and subsequent thermal injury, potentially preventing the recurrence of arrhythmia after ablation surgery.145 In another study, Zhuge et al designed ROS-responsive dual-locked liposomes combined with PDT. The nanocarriers accumulated at ablation lesions and inhibited the recovery of conduction in damaged cardiomyocytes. This combination therapy suppressed angiogenesis and fibroblast activation, lowered the recovery rate of postoperative atrioventricular conduction, and optimized long-term prognosis after RFCA.146 Moreover, conventional CIEDs, including pacemakers and ICDs, rely on lithium batteries with a service life of 4–10 years, which requires secondary replacement surgeries. To resolve this issue, Ruhparwar et al constructed a multi-walled carbon nanotube-based twist-type cardiac bioenergy harvester (TECH). The device transformed mechanical energy generated by myocardial contraction into electric energy, providing stable and long-term power for implantable cardiac devices and avoiding repeated surgical operations.147

Taken together, nanotechnology-based strategies may improve the management of arrhythmia in preclinical studies. However, most evidence has come from experimental studies, and none of the nanocarrier-based therapies have entered clinical trials. Currently, clinical translation remains limited to early nano-enabled ablation technologies.

Other CVDs

Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a chronic progressive cardiovascular disease whose current clinical treatments primarily alleviate symptoms and delay disease progression, with no curative approach available. By enhancing pulmonary targeting and controlled drug release, nanomedicine delivery systems offer a potential strategy for the treatment of PAH. Sildenafil, as a vasodilator, can inhibit the remodeling of pulmonary arterioles; however, insufficient targeting specificity and systemic side effects limit its clinical application. Li et al prepared glucuronic acid-modified liposomes loaded with sildenafil. The targeted modification optimized the in vivo distribution of encapsulated drugs. In mouse models, these liposomes suppressed pulmonary arterial remodeling more effectively than free sildenafil. Specifically, they reduced pulmonary artery pressure by 32%, vascular media thickness by 40%, and right ventricular hypertrophy degree by 45%.148 Besides, inhalable sildenafil citrate-loaded PLGA microbeads achieved local sustained drug release in the lung, serving as a safer and more effective alternative to conventional oral and intravenous regimens for the treatment of PAH.149 Ambrisentan is a classic endothelin receptor antagonist, whose low water solubility undermines its therapeutic benefits in PAH. Shewale et al fabricated ambrisentan-loaded solid lipid nanoparticles, which enhanced the solubility and dissolution rate of ambrisentan. Moreover, oral administration of this nanoformulation improved patient comfort, medication compliance, and overall therapeutic outcomes.150 In another study, Li et al constructed sialic acid-modified liposomal nanocarriers (SA-NP@Am-CS) loaded with ambrisentan. This pH/redox dual-responsive nanosystem cross-linked with chitosan successfully targeted pulmonary VSMCs and confined hypoxia-induced PAH lesions. This nanocarrier extended the blood circulation time of ambrisentan, inhibited pathological vascular remodeling, and reversed the abnormal contraction of VSMCs. Furthermore, inhalation administration enhanced the accumulation of the nanosystem in the lungs, minimized systemic side effects, and improved therapeutic efficacy.151–153

Valvular heart disease is a common progressive cardiovascular disorder. Although cardiac valve replacement has become the primary treatment modality for valvular heart disease, it may still result in various complications, such as valve calcification, thrombosis, anticoagulation-related bleeding, and infective endocarditis. Therefore, developing cardiac valve materials with long-term blood compatibility and anti-calcification properties represents a critical direction in biomaterial research. Bionic nanomaterials have demonstrated significant potential for enhancing the performance of artificial valves. Cheng et al proposed a bioengineering modification strategy based on erythrocyte membrane-mimetic nanocarriers. They covalently immobilized drug-loaded nanoparticles on bioprosthetic heart valves crosslinked by glutaraldehyde. This modification method did not damage the original structure, stability, and mechanical performance of bioprosthetic heart valves, but prevented thrombosis and valvular calcification, suppressed the inflammatory response, and facilitated endothelialization. Collectively, this modification method improved the long-term blood compatibility and tissue integration ability of artificial heart valves.154 Ma et al generated a photo-crosslinked composite hydrogel mNCC-MeGel (mNG) by conjugating TEMPO-oxidized modified nanocrystalline cellulose with methyl acrylate-modified gelatin. The nanofiber reinforcement effect of modified nanocrystalline cellulose strengthened the mechanical performance of hydrogels without impairing the inherent biological functions of gelatin. This multi-scale hydrogel material can serve as a versatile biomimetic platform for cardiac valve tissue engineering.155 Yu et al fabricated a mesoporous silica nanoparticle-based dual drug delivery system (MSN@VEGF-PEI-siRNA) loaded with vascular endothelial growth factor and RunX2 siRNA. Relying on electrostatic adsorption, the system achieved sustained release of vascular endothelial growth factor and effectively silenced the expression of RunX2 gene. This dual-functional nanoplatform inhibited valvular calcification and promoted valve tissue repair.156

Taken together, existing preclinical findings suggest that nanosystems can optimize the treatment of PAH and valvular heart disease. Despite promising preclinical results, numerous challenges still restrict the clinical transformation of relevant nanotherapies. So far, only a small number of nanotherapeutic formulations have entered clinical research.

Cardiovascular Nanotoxicity and Translational Safety

After entering systemic circulation, nanomaterials will be exposed to complex hemodynamic forces and biological environments that cannot be fully reproduced by conventional in vitro cytotoxicity assays. Endothelial injury, immune activation, off-target organ accumulation, and insufficient long-term safety data remain major barriers to the clinical translation of cardiovascular nanomedicines.157

Nanomaterial-Induced Cardiovascular Injury

Hemodynamic forces and protein-corona formation are jointly involved in the interaction of nanoparticles with the vascular endothelium. Based on material composition, surface chemistry, and dose, certain nanomaterials may induce oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, barrier dysfunction, and adhesion molecule expression in the vascular endothelium, thereby aggravating vascular inflammation and atherosclerosis.158 Some inorganic nanoparticles may release metal ions or catalyze ROS generation, whereas the toxicity of polymeric carriers is closely associated with their chemical composition, degradation kinetics, and local exposure. Under specific experimental conditions, these processes may contribute to cardiomyocyte injury and trigger profibrotic signaling. Studies on perovskite-based inorganic nanosystems have shown potential oxidative toxic effects on the lungs and heart, although their relevance to clinically used cardiovascular nanomedicines remains uncertain.159,160

Nanoparticle-Mediated Immune Dysregulation

Nanoparticle-immune interaction is another important safety concern. Certain nanocarriers may promote macrophage polarization toward a pro-inflammatory M1-like phenotype, thereby upregulating the release of tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β). In susceptible vascular lesions, sustained inflammatory signaling may contribute to plaque destabilization. Repeated exposure has also been linked to persistent low-grade systemic inflammation in experimental models, which may worsen ventricular remodeling in hypertension or HF.161,162

Biodistribution and Organ Accumulation

Particle size, surface charge, and surface chemistry strongly affect nanoparticle biodistribution. Ultra-small particles may show enhanced tissue penetration or rapid clearance, whereas larger nanostructures are more likely to be removed by the mononuclear phagocyte system, particularly in the liver and spleen. Biodegradable polymeric carriers generally undergo faster degradation and clearance, while some inorganic nanomaterials may show prolonged tissue retention. Therefore, repeated administration may lead to cumulative exposure, raising concerns about delayed effects on pulmonary vasculature or the myocardium under chronic exposure.163

Long-Term Biosafety and Translational Restrictions

Most current toxicological evaluations rely on short-term exposure in animal studies and do not adequately address delayed cardiovascular effects after repeated or prolonged exposure. In addition, testing frameworks have not yet been fully harmonized for the assessment of cardiovascular-specific nanotoxicity, and there are limited validated nanomedicine-specific biomarkers for myocardial injury, endothelial dysfunction, immune activation, and plaque stability.164

Endothelial injury, persistent inflammation, undesirable organ accumulation, and insufficient long-term safety data remain key obstacles concerning the advancement of nanotechnology for cardiovascular diseases. Surface engineering strategies, including PEGylation, cell membrane biomimetic coating, and lesion-responsive degradable nanostructures, may prevent nonspecific retention and inflammatory stimulation. Standardized cardiovascular nanotoxicology platforms and long-term safety assessments in large animal models are still needed to support clinical translation.165

Challenges in the Application of Nanotechnology for Cardiovascular Diseases

Insufficient Targeted Delivery

Efficient targeted delivery remains a major challenge in the use of nanomedicines for cardiovascular diseases. The cardiovascular system is characterized by rapid blood flow, high hemodynamic shear stress, and heterogeneous endothelial barriers, all of which may interfere with nanoparticle adhesion, vascular retention, and penetration into the lesion site.166 After systemic administration, nanoparticles can adsorb plasma proteins and form a protein corona, which may mask targeting ligands, alter biological identity, and promote nonspecific uptake by the reticuloendothelial system. Moreover, many of the available nanocarriers cannot effectively adapt to dynamically changing pathological conditions, such as fluctuating inflammation, endothelial activation, oxidative stress, and vascular permeability.167 Biomimetic and stimuli-responsive nanocarriers, combined with molecular imaging and multi-omics profiling, should be explored to improve lesion-specific delivery and enable personalized therapeutic strategies.

Barriers to Standardized Production and Clinical Translation

Scalable and reproducible manufacturing remains another key obstacle. Minor variations in synthesis conditions can affect particle size, surface charge, drug-loading efficiency, colloidal stability, and release behavior. Alterations in these features can change pharmacokinetic characteristics, vascular targeting efficiency, and cardiovascular safety. In addition, the high cost of raw materials, precision manufacturing, and comprehensive characterization limit the industrial-scale production of cardiovascular nanomedicines.3

A substantial translational gap also exists between preclinical models and human cardiovascular diseases in clinical practice. Rodent models cannot fully reproduce human hemodynamics, lipid metabolism, plaque complexity, or chronic inflammatory responses. These models often neglect clinically relevant comorbidities, such as hypertension, diabetes, renal dysfunction, and aging. As a result, therapeutic efficacy and safety observed in small animals may not accurately reflect clinical outcomes. Most previous studies have relied on short-term evaluations, whereas long-term GLP-compliant toxicity studies and validation in large animals remain limited.168,169

At the clinical and regulatory levels, cardiovascular nanomedicines that have entered clinical evaluation largely remain in Phase I/II trials, with few studies progressing to large-scale Phase III randomized controlled trials.170 Regulatory consensus has yet to be established regarding nanomedicine-specific endpoints, including molecular imaging biomarkers and quantitative biodistribution measures. Moreover, global standards for nanotoxicological and cardiovascular safety assessments remain considerably inconsistent. Collectively, manufacturing variability, limited predictability of preclinical findings, and regulatory uncertainty undermine the clinical translation of nanotechnology for cardiovascular diseases.171

Ethical Dilemmas and Long-Term Risks

Ethical concerns are closely linked to uncertainties regarding long-term safety and clinical accessibility. Since some nanomaterials may persist in vivo or accumulate in specific organs, incomplete knowledge about chronic toxicity does not align with patient-informed consent and risk-benefit assessment. High research, development, and manufacturing costs may also widen disparities in access to advanced nanomedicine-based therapies, particularly in low- and middle-income regions. Addressing these issues requires transparent assessment of potential benefits and risks, long-term post-treatment monitoring, and closer collaboration among clinicians, material scientists, toxicologists, ethicists, regulators, and industry partners.172

Future Perspectives and Limitations

Future studies should focus on nanoplatforms that combine precise targeting, controlled degradation, and lesion-responsive drug release. Biomimetic coatings, multi-stimuli-responsive structures, and imaging-guided delivery systems may help overcome barriers to vascular delivery and improve the spatial control of treatment. Furthermore, scalable manufacturing protocols, standardized characterization methods, and batch-to-batch quality control are essential for clinical translation. More predictive safety evaluation systems are also required. Large-animal models, human organoid platforms, microphysiological systems, and long-term GLP-compliant toxicity studies may provide more reliable data on biodistribution, immune responses, cardiovascular toxicity, and delayed organ injury. In parallel, global regulatory harmonization is required to define nanomedicine-specific endpoints, imaging biomarkers, pharmacokinetic parameters, and cardiovascular safety criteria. This review faced several limitations. It focused mainly on mainstream nanocarriers and only briefly discussed less commonly used nanomaterials. In addition, since different materials were used across studies, disease models, dosing regimens, and outcome measures, direct quantitative comparison among delivery systems remains challenging. Conclusion

In summary, by modulating particle size and surface properties and thanks to drug-loading capacity and functional modification, nanotechnology provides new technical approaches for the early diagnosis, precision therapy, and therapeutic monitoring of cardiovascular diseases. This review systematically summarized representative organic and inorganic nanomaterials, including liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, hydrogels, SPIONs, and gold nanoparticles, and discussed their applications in the management of cardiovascular diseases, such as CAD, AMI, HF, hypertension, arrhythmias, PAH, and valvular heart disease. In terms of diagnosis, nanomaterials can serve as contrast agents, imaging probes, or multimodal imaging platforms. They can enhance signal intensity and lesion resolution in RMI, fluorescence imaging, photoacoustic imaging, ultrasound imaging, and nuclear medicine imaging. These properties may improve the early detection of atherosclerotic plaque inflammation, myocardial ischemic injury, vascular endothelial dysfunction, and cardiac structural remodeling. Concerning therapeutic purposes, nanocarriers can achieve local drug enrichment and controlled release by targeting inflammatory cells, injured endothelium, ischemic myocardium, or remodeled myocardial tissue, thereby improving the efficiency of drug delivery and reducing systemic adverse effects. Some nanoplatforms can also integrate imaging, targeted delivery, and therapeutic functions, providing an important research direction for theranostic applications in cardiovascular diseases.

Overall, nanotechnology not only expands the technical strategies available for the diagnosis and treatment of cardiovascular diseases but also offers new possibilities for precision drug delivery, therapeutic monitoring, and individualized intervention. By contributing to the early identification of pathological changes, enhancing targeted therapeutic effects, and enabling dynamic monitoring of the treatment process, nanotechnology may prevent disease progression, lower the risk of adverse cardiovascular events, and improve the long-term outcomes of patients. However, most of the previous studies have adopted in vitro experiments and animal models. Challenges, such as the complex vascular microenvironment, protein corona formation, unstable targeting efficiency, difficulties in large-scale manufacturing, insufficient long-term safety data, and the lack of unified regulatory standards, should be addressed in future studies to facilitate clinical translation. Moreover, future studies should prioritize standardized fabrication, systematic evaluation of toxicities, and high-quality clinical trials to promote the translation of nanotechnology to clinical diagnosis, precision therapy, and theranostic applications in cardiovascular diseases.
