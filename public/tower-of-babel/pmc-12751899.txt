Differences in pericoronitis knowledge among Chinese undergraduates: a cross-disciplinary survey in Northeastern Universities
Authors: Zhinuo Zhuang, Jing Fu, Yutong Zhang, Xiaoxu Feng, Ziqi Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPericoronitis, an inflammation of the gum around a partially erupted third molar (wisdom tooth), frequently affects young adults. Despite its prevalence, the level of awareness and understanding of pericoronitis among undergraduates in various academic fields remains poorly characterized, especially among students outside health-related majors. Improving this knowledge is important to encourage timely care and prevent complications.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted in April 2025 among 664 first- and second-year undergraduates from three universities in Northeastern China (majoring in dentistry, general medicine [non-dental], and non-medical fields). A validated 17-item questionnaire (Cronbach’s α = 0.82) assessed knowledge of pericoronitis (etiology, symptoms, diagnosis, and treatment) and general oral health awareness. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and non-parametric tests. Knowledge scores (0–12) were computed, with a threshold (≥ 7) for “good” knowledge.ResultsDental students demonstrated significantly higher pericoronitis knowledge than their medical and non-medical peers, with second-year dental students scoring the highest. For example, over 90% of dental sophomores answered key questions correctly, versus roughly half of medical and one-third of non-medical students (p < 0.001). Nonetheless, even dental students showed some misconceptions, and chronic-case management was poorly understood by all groups. Students with a family dental background or personal pericoronitis experience were significantly more likely to attain a “good” knowledge score.ConclusionsA pronounced disparity in pericoronitis-related knowledge exists among undergraduates, favoring dental students and revealing substantial deficits in non-dental fields. These gaps indicate clear targets for cross-disciplinary education to improve awareness, preventive behaviors, and earlier recognition and management of common conditions such as pericoronitis. In summary, dental undergraduates markedly outperformed medical and non-medical peers. Embedding concise, evidence-based oral-health content within non-dental programs is a practical step to narrow this gap and support timely care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07349-5.

Introduction

Pericoronitis is an inflammation of the gum tissue (operculum) around a partially erupted third molar (wisdom tooth) [1].It is a common oral infection in young adults, often presenting with pain, swelling, and difficulty in jaw movement [2, 3]​.If left untreated, an impacted wisdom tooth can lead to pericoronitis and other complications such as pain, trismus, spread of infection, or cyst formation​.Early recognition and management are therefore important to prevent severe outcomes.

Additionally, pericoronitis is relatively common among young adults, globally, it accounts for an estimated 6–9% of dental emergency visits, and cross-sectional studies of college populations report that approximately 5–10% have pericoronitis at a given time [2, 4].Although pericoronitis is not as life-threatening as some oral diseases, its high prevalence and potential to cause severe pain or even dangerous cellulitis (e.g., Ludwig’s angina) justify attention as a preventable oral health issue [5].

Despite the frequency of third molar impaction and pericoronitis, public awareness of this condition remains limited. A recent study in India reported that 63% of college students had no awareness of the potential problems associated with third molars, and only 27.2% were aware of their possible impact on general health​. In the same cohort, 10.5% of the students examined were found to have pericoronitis, underscoring the relevance of this issue in the college-age population​ [6, 7]. However, cultural and healthcare differences between India and China may impact such awareness; for instance, recent Chinese public health initiatives increasingly integrate oral health into general healthcare [8].This necessitates investigating pericoronitis awareness specifically among Chinese students. Such findings highlight a concerning gap in oral health knowledge among young adults. University students from non-health-related majors, in particular, may receive little to no education on oral health in their curriculum. By contrast, students in dental or medical fields are expected to have higher awareness of conditions like pericoronitis due to their training. Indeed, prior research has observed that health-related students tend to score significantly better on oral health knowledge than their non-health peers​ [9]. This disparity suggests that large segments of the student population could be at risk of unrecognized oral health issues, simply because they lack exposure to relevant information.

In China, public awareness of oral health is increasingly recognized as a national priority [8]. However, existing educational initiatives predominantly focus on young children or individuals already seeking dental care. There remains a significant gap in targeting broader populations, particularly university students outside health-related fields. To date, limited research has explored the oral health knowledge of this demographic, and, to our knowledge, no studies have specifically addressed their understanding of pericoronitis. Considering that university students—typically aged 20 to 25—are at the peak age for third molar eruption and the onset of related conditions, it is both relevant and necessary to investigate their awareness of pericoronitis at this stage [4]. Additionally, comparing students across diverse academic disciplines can reveal whether certain groups have particular misconceptions or knowledge deficits that need to be addressed.

This study aims to assess undergraduate students’ understanding of pericoronitis in Northeastern China and to compare knowledge levels across health-related and non-health-related academic disciplines. It further seeks to identify specific misconceptions or deficiencies—such as misunderstanding of the causes, symptoms, prevention, or management of pericoronitis—that could inform the design of targeted educational strategies. Ultimately, the findings are intended to support the integration of fundamental oral health education into general university curricula, promoting greater awareness and preventive behaviors regarding conditions like pericoronitis among the broader student population.

Methods

Study design and participants

A cross-sectional survey was conducted in April 2025. The questionnaire was distributed to first- and second-year undergraduate students majoring in dentistry, non-dental medical disciplines, or non-medical fields at universities in Northeastern China. Inclusion criteria required participants to be first- or second-year undergraduates enrolled at one of these three universities and within one of the above major categories. We focused on freshmen and sophomores to capture baseline knowledge before extensive professional training. Students beyond second year or outside the specified majors were not recruited. All participants provided informed consent. To enhance representativeness, students from three different universities were surveyed. These universities (one health-focused institution and two comprehensive universities) were selected based on accessibility and to include a diverse mix of student disciplines, although they were chosen by convenience rather than random sampling.To further minimize potential bias and enhance the comparability of the student population across institutions, we intentionally selected universities with similar National College Entrance Examination admission scores. This criterion was applied to ensure that the baseline academic background and cognitive ability of undergraduates across the three universities were roughly equivalent, reducing confounding variables that could otherwise skew knowledge-based comparisons. A convenience sampling approach was used, disseminating the survey link via university student networks to maximize participation. Because participation was voluntary, this recruitment strategy could introduce self-selection bias (for instance, students with greater interest in health topics might be more inclined to respond); we attempted to mitigate this by widely distributing the survey invitation across various faculties and classes. A priori, we planned to detect a 20-percentage-point between-group difference in the proportion achieving a “good” knowledge score with 80% power at a two-sided α of 0.05, implying ~ 97 participants per group (≈ 108 allowing ~ 10% non-response); accordingly, we targeted ~ 200 per major group to ensure adequate power across subgroups. We focused on first- and second-year students to capture baseline knowledge before they receive extensive professional training.

Questionnaire

A structured 17-item questionnaire assessed participants’ demographics, general oral health attitudes, and specific knowledge of pericoronitis. The questionnaire had three sections: (1) Demographics (5 items): age, gender, major, academic year, and whether the student or family members had a background in dentistry or personal experience with pericoronitis. (2) General oral health awareness (4 items): items included familiarity with the term “pericoronitis” routine dental visit habits, toothbrushing frequency, and perception of oral health importance. (3) Specific knowledge of pericoronitis (8 items): these addressed common age of onset, typical location (upper vs. lower jaw), clinical signs (e.g., trismus, fever), diagnostic methods (e.g., use of a periodontal probe), and treatment of acute and chronic cases.

All knowledge items had one correct answer. Content validity was confirmed by two dental faculty members, and a pilot test (n = 30) guided minor revisions. Cronbach’s α was 0.82, indicating good internal reliability. We did not perform a separate factor analysis to assess structural validity, as the knowledge items spanned diverse aspects of pericoronitis rather than a single construct. Similarly, test–retest reliability was not examined due to the one-time cross-sectional administration of the survey. Based on pilot timing, a 70-second minimum response time was set to exclude inattentive submissions. Each correct answer earned one point, yielding a total knowledge score from 0 to 12. Scores ≥ 7 were categorized as “good” knowledge, reflecting ≥ 60% accuracy a basic competency level (≥ 60% correct). This threshold was selected to indicate a majority of correct answers, consistent with common practice in knowledge surveys.

Data collection procedure

The questionnaire was administered online via the Wenjuanxing platform. The survey link was distributed through university WeChat groups and academic forums to reach a broad student sample, and responses were collected over approximately two weeks. Participation was anonymous and voluntary; response times were recorded to detect rapid submissions.

Statistical analysis

Data were exported to IBM SPSS 26.0. Descriptive statistics summarized demographics. Because knowledge scores were non-normally distributed, group comparisons were made using non-parametric tests (Kruskal–Wallis for multiple groups, Mann–Whitney for two-group comparisons). Categorical response differences were assessed with chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests as appropriate. Two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical clearance

This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the School and Hospital of Stomatology, China Medical University (Ethics Approval No. K2021059). The research was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Before participants proceeded to the questionnaire, they were presented with an electronic informed consent form explaining the study’s purpose, procedures, and confidentiality. Only those who consented (by clicking “Agree and Continue”) were allowed to fill in the questionnaire. Participants were informed that their involvement was voluntary and that they could withdraw at any time by not submitting the survey. All responses were anonymous; no personal identifiers were collected, and data were stored securely with access limited to the research team.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 675 questionnaires were collected in this study. After excluding 11 invalid responses (completion time < 70 s), 664 valid questionnaires remained for analysis. Of the 664 respondents, 117 (17.6%) were dental students, 267 (40.2%) were medical students (non-dental fields, e.g., general medicine, nursing), and 280 (42.2%) were non-medical students (e.g., engineering, humanities, economics). The sample included 308 first-year (46.4%) and 356 second-year (53.6%) students, with a roughly even year distribution within each major group. Gender was balanced: 301 males (45.3%) and 363 females (54.7%). Only 111 students (16.7%) reported having a family member in the dental profession. Regarding pericoronitis exposure, 305 respondents (45.9%) indicated personal or close second-hand experience of pericoronitis (self or friends/family), while 359 (54.1%) had no such experience. These demographics suggest a broadly representative sample of undergraduates, facilitating comparisons across different student groups. General oral health awareness

Table 2 presents student responses to general oral health questions (Q1–Q4), categorized by academic discipline and year of study. Overall, dental students exhibited notably higher levels of oral health awareness than their peers in other majors. Roughly half of them were familiar with the term “pericoronitis,” compared to just over 20% of medical students and about 24% of non-medical students, a statistically significant difference (p < 0.001). Although sophomores reported slightly higher familiarity than freshmen, the difference between year levels was not substantial.

Regarding routine dental visits, about half of the dental students—51.7% of freshmen and 50.9% of sophomores—reported having regular check-ups. This contrasts with approximately one-third of medical students and an even smaller proportion of non-medical students. The difference across academic disciplines was statistically significant (p < 0.001), but no notable increase was observed between academic years within any group, suggesting that the habit of seeking regular dental care did not improve simply with time at university.

Most students across all groups reported brushing their teeth at least twice a day. Brushing frequency was slightly higher among dental and medical students (approximately 77–85%) than among non-medical students (around 62–70%), and the difference was statistically significant (p < 0.001). However, brushing habits appeared consistent between freshmen and sophomores, regardless of major.

When asked about the importance of oral health to general health, all dental students (100%) recognized it as essential. High levels of agreement were also seen among medical students (96.5% of freshmen and 92.0% of sophomores). In contrast, only about 85% of non-medical students shared this view, indicating that roughly 15% did not perceive oral health as an integral part of overall well-being (p < 0.001).

In summary, dental students demonstrated stronger awareness and more positive oral health behaviors than students in other disciplines. Medical students also performed better than non-medical students, particularly in recognizing the relationship between oral and systemic health. Across all groups, however, differences between first- and second-year students were minimal, indicating that without targeted instruction, general oral health knowledge and habits tend to remain stable during the early years of university. (These results focus primarily on differences by academic discipline, as year-on-year changes were generally small.)

Pericoronitis-specific knowledge

Dental students significantly outperformed their peers in nearly all items assessing knowledge of pericoronitis. One of the most marked differences was observed in identifying the typical age group affected: 71.9% of second-year and 43.3% of first-year dental students correctly selected the 18–25 age range, compared to only 35–47% of medical students and 22% of non-medical students. This suggests that by the second year, most dental students are aware that pericoronitis commonly affects young adults, while awareness among students in other disciplines remains limited.

When asked about the most common anatomical site for pericoronitis, the majority of students across all majors correctly identified the lower jaw. Interestingly, non-medical students performed slightly better than dental students on this item, although the difference was not statistically significant. This result was considered an anomaly and may reflect chance or non-curricular influences.

For clinical symptom recognition, particularly trismus and systemic signs like fever or chills, dental students showed much higher accuracy. Nearly 95% of second-year and over 78% of first-year dental students correctly recognized trismus as a symptom, compared to 70–78% of medical and only 56–57% of non-medical students. Similarly, 90–95% of dental students correctly identified systemic symptoms, while the rates among medical and non-medical students were substantially lower, at 60–72% and 50–57%, respectively. These findings reflect the benefit of early dental training in developing both clinical and diagnostic awareness.

A similar pattern emerged when students were asked whether pericoronitis could spread to surrounding tissues. While most dental students answered correctly (75–84%), only about 54–62% of medical students and fewer than 53% of non-medical students were aware of this potential complication. The contrast was even more striking in questions about diagnostic procedures. The use of a periodontal probe as a diagnostic tool was correctly identified by 94.7% of second-year and 73.3% of first-year dental students. However, only around 61–62% of medical students and just 31–36% of non-medical students recognized this basic clinical method, indicating a general lack of familiarity with even simple diagnostic practices outside the dental field.

Regarding the initial management of acute pericoronitis, which includes infection control, irrigation, and pain relief rather than immediate extraction, 66.7% of first-year and 75.4% of second-year dental students selected the correct response. In contrast, the correct answer was chosen by only 56–57% of medical students and 43–53% of non-medical students. Even among dental sophomores, about one in four did not identify the recommended treatment, highlighting gaps even within dental training.

Knowledge of how to manage chronic or recurrent pericoronitis when the third molar has sufficient space to erupt was particularly limited. The correct procedure—operculectomy—was identified by only 38.6% of second-year and 6.7% of first-year dental students, with rates dropping to 5–8% among both medical and non-medical students. This finding suggests that detailed management strategies, especially for chronic cases, are not yet widely taught in the early years of dental education and are virtually absent in non-dental curricula.

Taken together, these results underscore the strong influence of specialized dental education in shaping knowledge of pericoronitis. By the second academic year, dental students display superior understanding of symptoms, diagnosis, and to a lesser extent, treatment. Nonetheless, gaps persist even within this group, particularly regarding correct therapeutic strategies. Medical students demonstrated a moderate level of understanding, likely reflecting exposure to general health science courses, while non-medical students showed minimal awareness, highlighting the lack of oral health content in their education. Influence of personal exposure and family background

Personal and familial experience with dentistry influenced knowledge levels. Students with a family member in the dental profession had higher knowledge: 65/111 (58.5%) achieved a good total score (≥ 7) versus 270/553 (48.8%) of those without (χ², p = 0.029). Likewise, 181/305 (59.3%) of students with personal/close experience of pericoronitis attained good scores, compared to 170/359 (47.4%) of those without such experience (p = 0.001). These associations indicate that exposure to dentistry, whether through family or personal experience, correlated with better overall knowledge. However, self-reported “experience” with pericoronitis may be unreliable – without a professional diagnosis, some students could be misattributing other dental issues (e.g., a severe caries-related infection or periapical pathology) to “pericoronitis” due to similar symptoms (pain, fever, trismus). This underscores the importance of proper dental evaluation for such conditions.

In contrast to the complex pattern observed with personal exposure, having a familial dental background was associated with a uniformly positive effect on knowledge. Students with a family member in dentistry answered a greater proportion of general oral health questions correctly than those without such a background (Fig. 2A). They also demonstrated superior accuracy on pericoronitis-specific knowledge questions (Fig. 2B). This consistent advantage suggests that early exposure to dental information through family members enhances both general oral health awareness and specific knowledge of pericoronitis.

Interestingly, subgroup analysis of question responses (Fig. 2C) showed that students with pericoronitis experience scored lower on some general oral health items (Q3 on brushing habits and Q4 on oral health importance) than those without. Conversely, those with experience scored higher on professional knowledge items (Fig. 2D). These patterns suggest a complex effect of personal exposure. One possible explanation is reverse causation: students with poorer oral habits may be more likely to suffer pericoronitis, thus comprising the experienced group and explaining their lower preventive behaviors. On the other hand, having experienced the condition (or knowing someone who did) may have prompted students to learn more about its symptoms and management, leading to their higher scores on specific questions.

Discussion

This study reveals substantial disparities in knowledge of pericoronitis among undergraduate students from different academic disciplines. Nearly half of the participants reported either personal experience or indirect exposure to pericoronitis, underscoring the condition’s relative prevalence in this age group. Given that young adults are at increased risk for third molar-related complications, it is important that students across all fields—not just those studying dentistry—can recognize and understand the condition to seek or recommend timely care [1].

The most prominent finding was the superior performance of dental students in almost all knowledge domains. Sophomore dental students scored highest overall, reflecting the influence of formal dental education. In contrast, students in general medical and especially non-medical programs often struggled to identify even basic signs, symptoms, or appropriate treatments. This is perhaps unsurprising, as oral health is a core focus in dental curricula from the outset, while general medical and non-medical programs devote little to no formal training to dental topics [9–11]. However, the scale of the knowledge gap is notable and suggests a systemic shortfall in interdisciplinary health education. Similar patterns have been reported internationally. For example, a study in Saudi Arabia found dental students performed significantly better than medical or nursing students in oral health knowledge, with accuracy rates of 77% vs. 44% respectively [9, 12].

Encouragingly, dental students demonstrated significant improvement between their first and second years, likely due to curricular exposure to foundational subjects such as oral anatomy and pathology. However, this improvement was not observed among students in other fields, indicating that without structured education, oral health knowledge remains static. Even so, knowledge gaps persisted among second-year dental students, especially regarding appropriate management strategies. For example, about 25% of them selected incorrect treatments for acute pericoronitis, such as recommending immediate extraction. This raises concerns about the adequacy of early clinical training in practical management decision [13, 14]. This misconception could stem from limited early clinical exposure: students might assume extraction is a definitive solution for any wisdom tooth problem, not fully realising that conservative measures are the recommended first-line approach for acute pericoronitis.

These deficiencies were even more pronounced among non-dental students. While medical students demonstrated some understanding of symptoms—perhaps due to general biological or clinical exposure—many lacked awareness of key treatment principles. In clinical settings, general practitioners or emergency physicians may be the first to encounter patients with pericoronitis. If they are unfamiliar with basic management steps (e.g., recommending irrigation or analgesics while awaiting dental care), suboptimal treatment or delays in care may occur [2, 13]. Chronic pericoronitis management was poorly understood across all non-dental disciplines, with correct responses virtually absent. Even among dental sophomores, less than 40% correctly identified operculectomy as the indicated procedure, suggesting that this topic is likely addressed later in the curriculum or only during clinical rotation [14]. This universally poor performance indicates a curricular blind spot: key concepts of chronic pericoronitis management are not covered at all in non-dental programs and are introduced only in the later stages of dental training. Addressing this gap by incorporating such content earlier in both dental and general medical curricula could help eliminate these misconceptions.

Diagnostic knowledge also revealed stark contrasts, and—critically—diagnosis is primarily clinical and hinges on careful periodontal probing of the pericoronal pocket, which remains the cornerstone for identifying local inflammation and debris retention in suspected pericoronitis [15, 16]. Nearly all dental students correctly identified periodontal probing as a key diagnostic method for pericoronitis.( However, only 30–65% of medical students and a minority of non-medical students recognized this tool. This suggests that students outside of dentistry are unfamiliar with even basic oral examination techniques. In real-world practice, this gap may result in misdiagnoses or failure to refer. Basic training in oral examination skills—such as recognizing gingival swelling or using simple probing methods—could greatly enhance interdisciplinary care and early intervention, particularly if integrated into general medical education [17–19].

Interestingly, better academic knowledge did not always translate into improved personal behavior. Although dental students were more likely to attend regular check-ups and maintain good brushing habits, only about half reported routine dental visits, and brushing frequency was only slightly higher than among medical students. Moreover, no significant improvements in oral health behavior were observed from first to second year, suggesting that increased knowledge did not lead to proportional changes in self-care. This reflects a well-documented phenomenon in health education, where knowledge alone is often insufficient to modify personal behavior. In other words, improving oral health literacy – the ability to not only understand but also effectively use oral health information – is necessary to translate knowledge into healthier behavior. These findings highlight the need for curricula to emphasize not only clinical content but also preventive philosophy and personal responsibility from the outset [7, 13].

On the other hand, students in non-medical fields exhibited lower levels of both knowledge and behavior. Around 15% of these students did not recognize oral health as integral to overall health, and their rates of routine dental visits and twice-daily brushing were substantially lower. This attitude gap likely contributes to their weaker oral hygiene behaviors. Although over 90% of medical students acknowledged the oral-systemic connection, this recognition was markedly lower among non-medical students. Broader public health efforts or curricular reforms introducing basic oral health concepts into general education could help bridge this divide and reduce preventable oral conditions in the long term [2, 7, 17].

Overall, the findings reflect the fragmented nature of health education. The wide knowledge gap between dental and non-dental students, even by the second academic year, indicates that most academic programs outside of dentistry provide minimal instruction in oral health. This has been documented in other settings as well. Surveys in the United States, for instance, have shown that medical schools allocate very limited time to oral health topics, resulting in low dental literacy among general practitioners [17, 19]. To address this, health education should move toward a more integrated model. Introducing core dental topics—such as the recognition and basic management of common infections like pericoronitis—into medical, nursing, and allied health programs could promote better patient care and interdisciplinary collaboration. Even brief instruction or clinical demonstrations could enhance the ability of non-dental professionals to triage dental complaints and provide appropriate interim advice [20].

For example, a pilot program at a U.S. medical school demonstrated that even a single interactive oral health session significantly improved medical students’ comfort with oral examinations and their ability to recognize periodontal disease risks [17]. This suggests that brief, targeted interventions can yield substantial gains in oral health competency among non-dental trainees. Drawing on such evidence, our findings can be translated into educational practice by incorporating fundamental oral health content early in the curricula of non-dental majors and through interprofessional training. Establishing short modules or workshops led by dental faculty for medical and other students would familiarize future practitioners with common dental conditions. Likewise, dental curricula may benefit from introducing topics like chronic pericoronitis management earlier in training, ensuring students acquire these competencies before clinical practice. By charting such a reform path, educational stakeholders can directly address the knowledge gaps identified in this study and ultimately improve patient care outcomes.

Furthermore, the inclusion of in-depth clinical questions (diagnosis and management) likely challenged non-dental students, potentially underestimating their knowledge. However, we included these items to illuminate gaps in exposure that could be addressed through education.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample was limited to first- and second-year undergraduates from three universities in a single region (Northeastern China) selected by convenience. As a result, the findings may not be fully generalizable to all Chinese undergraduates or to students in other regions. The three universities, while diverse in their academic profiles, do not encompass the entire range of educational contexts. Moreover, participation was voluntary via an online survey link, introducing potential self-selection bias; students with higher interest in health or oral health topics may have been more likely to participate, potentially inflating overall knowledge levels in our sample. Second, the study relied on a self-reported questionnaire to gauge knowledge. Although the questionnaire underwent expert content validation and showed good internal consistency, it may not capture the full depth or nuance of students’ understanding. We also did not assess test–retest reliability of the questionnaire, so we cannot determine the stability of responses over time. Third, the cross-sectional design provides a snapshot of associations but precludes causal inference regarding the impact of education on knowledge. Fourth, by surveying only early-year students, we assessed baseline knowledge prior to advanced training; more senior students or graduates with greater clinical experience might perform better, particularly on detailed diagnostic or management questions. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable baseline data on pericoronitis awareness among a diverse student population and highlights clear targets for educational reform [21–23].

Conclusion

Pronounced disparities were observed in pericoronitis knowledge among undergraduate students, with dental students far outperforming their medical and non-medical peers. Widespread misconceptions were noted even among health-related majors, especially in diagnosis and management. These findings underscore the need for interdisciplinary educational initiatives. Integrating basic oral health content into general curricula and fostering collaboration between dental and other medical disciplines are critical steps forward. Such efforts could improve oral health literacy and ensure timely recognition and proper management of pericoronitis and other common dental conditions among young adults. To truly bridge these gaps, implementing targeted curriculum reforms—embedding oral health principles in general education and reinforcing dental training on less familiar topics (such as chronic pericoronitis management)—will be essential to translate our results into improved educational and health outcomes.
