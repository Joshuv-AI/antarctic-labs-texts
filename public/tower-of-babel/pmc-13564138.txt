Complex enzyme hydrolysis of okara to enrich soluble dietary fiber and peptides and insights into the dietary fiber–protein interaction mechanism
Authors: Jingmin Sun, Shikai Zhao, Xiaoyang Sun, Yu Chen, Tingwei Zhu, Deli Wei, Lifen Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe interaction between dietary fiber and protein inhibits the conversion of insoluble dietary fiber (IDF) into soluble dietary fiber (SDF) and the hydrolysis of okara protein. Viscozyme® L and pepsin were used for sequential enrichment SDF and peptides, and the interaction mechanisms between the fiber and protein were investigated by analyzing the structures of IDF, SDF, peptides, and their mixed systems. After complex enzyme hydrolysis, the SDF/IDF ratio and peptide content of okara reached 0.48 and 54.38%, respectively. Compared with hydrolysis using Viscozyme® L or pepsin alone, complex enzyme hydrolysis effectively degraded IDF and protein, disrupted hydrogen bonds in SDF–peptide, and hydrolyzed the amorphous regions of IDF. The hydrolysis of the amorphous regions of IDF and the disruption of hydrogen bonds between SDF and peptide enhanced the enrichment of SDF and peptides. Overall, this study provides a practical framework for the development and utilization of okara.Keywords: Okara, Enzyme, Dietary fiber, Peptide, Protein

Introduction

Okara is the byproduct generated during the production of soybean milk and tofu. Approximately 7% of the world's soybeans are used for the production of tofu, soy milk, fermented soybeans, and natto, and every 1 kg of soybean yields 1.1–1.2 kg of wet okara during tofu or soy milk processing (O'Toole, 1999). Dry okara serves as a rich source of dietary fiber (DF, 50%–60%), protein (20%–30%), fat (10%–12%), and other bioactive compounds such as isoflavones, saponins, and phytosterols (Wu et al., 2025). The DF in okara can absorb water and expand in the body to promote satiety and suppress food intake, which can regulate body weight and prevent obesity (Akhlaghi, 2024; Tan et al., 2016). Furthermore, the proportion of essential amino acids in okara protein is almost similar to that in commercial soybean protein isolates (Eze, 2019). Nevertheless, okara is generally utilized as animal feed or soil fertilizer or is discarded as waste due to its high moisture content, rapid spoilage, and associated environmental pollution risks (Liang et al., 2023; Spiess et al., 2025). Therefore, the accumulation of okara represents a major challenge for the soybean processing industry.

Okara DF is categorized into insoluble dietary fiber (IDF) and soluble dietary fiber (SDF), both of which are indigestible in the small intestine. IDF primarily consists of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, accounting for ≥90% of total DF. High IDF content can exert adverse effects when incorporated into foods (Lin et al., 2025). Conversely, adequate intake of SDF is essential for human health, conferring benefits such as probiotic effects, blood glucose regulation, serum lipid reduction, and prevention of obesity and cancer (Hua et al., 2021; Feng et al., 2025). Therefore, it is necessary to modulate the proportion of DF, particularly by increasing the SDF content to improve its hydration properties and physiological functionality. In this regard, studies have explored strategies to alter the SDF/IDF ratio, including enzymatic hydrolysis, mechanical degradation, extrusion cooking, thermal processing and various integrated methods (Ul Ain et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2022; Yoshida, da Silva and Prudencio, 2023; Zhao et al., 2024). Enzymatic treatment can reduce the cell wall rigidity of okara and improve the protein extraction yield and is considered a promising method. In fact, researchers have reported that Viscozyme® L treatment reduced the IDF content by 33% and increased the SDF content by 110% in soybean residue (Ricarte et al., 2024). Moreover, Zhang et al. (2023) reported that Viscozyme® L hydrolysis of soybean residue cell wall polysaccharides increased the SDF content from 1.17% to 2.15% compared with the raw material.

DF can interact with proteins via hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and electrostatic forces (Zhou et al., 2021). Such interactions between DF and protein interfere with the conversion of IDF into SDF, while simultaneously hindering the hydrolysis of okara protein and the subsequent generation of small-molecule peptides (de Figueiredo et al., 2018; Tao et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2023). Zhang et al. (2023) reported that the removal of major constituents such as protein, fat, and starch can expose more enzyme-binding sites, which enhances enzyme permeability, thereby facilitating its entry into IDF and loosening the IDF structures and consequently increasing the yield of SDF. Fayaz et al. (2019) also reported that rupturing the protein–fiber complexes significantly improved the extraction yield of protein. The substrate specificity of a single enzyme restricts its hydrolysis efficiency when applied to complex matrices. Phuong et al. (2022) reported that the combined use of cellulase and protease more efficiently disrupts the structure of algal cell walls. Song et al. (2022) also reported that complex enzyme systems can augment cell wall disruption and facilitate the hydrolysis of glycoprotein complexes, thereby releasing greater amounts of polysaccharides. To date, no studies have been reported on the sequential enrichment of SDF and peptides from okara using a combination of Viscozyme® L and proteases. Furthermore, elucidating the interactions between DF and protein under the action of these combined enzymes is of critical importance for enhancing the co-enrichment efficiency of both SDF and peptides.

In this study, Viscozyme® L and pepsin were used for sequential enrichment of SDF and peptides from okara. The complex enzyme hydrolysis process was optimized. Moreover, the effects of complex enzyme hydrolysis on the structures of IDF, SDF, and peptides, as well as their mixed systems, were also investigated to clarify the interaction mechanisms between the fiber and protein of okara. This study aims to systematically elucidate the mechanism of fiber–protein interaction and provide a theoretical rationale for the enrichment of SDF and peptides.

Materials and methods

Materials

Wet okara was obtained from a local market (Anyang, Henan Province, China), and dried in an oven at 55°C for 12 h. The proximate chemical composition of the dried okara was as follows: 67.77% total DF, 13.94% protein, 5.67% lipids, 8.74% moisture, and 3.03% ash. Pepsin (≥250 U/mg) was obtained from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Viscozyme® L (≥100 FBGU/g), consisting of cellulase, hemicellulase, arabanase, β-glucanase, and xylanase derived from Aspergillus aculeatus, was procured from Novozymes (Bagsvaerd, Denmark). Monosaccharide standards (rhamnose, arabinose, galactose, glucose, mannose, glucuronic acid, galacturonic acid, xylose, and fucose) and 1-phenyl-3-methyl-5-pyrazolone (PMP) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai, China). All other chemicals used in this study were of analytical or chromatographic grade.

Complex enzyme hydrolysis of okara

The complex enzyme hydrolysis process of okara is depicted in Fig. 1. Okara flour (20 g) was dispersed in deionized water at a 1:10 (w/v) ratio and sheared for 270 s with a high-speed wall-breaking conditioning machine (Joyoung, Foshan, China) to obtain a suspension. The pH (4.0, 4.5, 5.0, 5.5, and 6.0) of the suspension was adjusted using a 0.1 M HCl solution, followed by the addition of Viscozyme® L at different concentrations (0.00%, 0.25%, 0.50%, 0.75%, 1.00%, 1.25%, and 1.50%, v/w). Different enzyme hydrolysis temperatures (45°C, 50°C, 55°C, 60°C, and 65°C) and times (2 h, 3 h, 4 h, 5 h, and 6 h) were also investigated. The pH was adjusted every 30 min. Subsequently, the enzyme was inactivated for 5 min in a boiling water bath. After cooling to 25°C, the pH (1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, and 3.0) of the suspension was adjusted using a 6 M HCl solution, followed by the addition of pepsin (0.00%, 0.25%, 0.50%, 0.75%, 1.00%, 1.25%, and 1.50%, v/w). Pepsin hydrolysis was conducted in a water bath at different temperatures (27°C, 32°C, 37°C, 42°C, and 47°C) for varying times (0.5 h, 1.0 h, 1.5 h, 2.0 h, 2.5 h, and 3.0 h). Then, the pH was adjusted, and the enzyme was inactivated as described earlier. The suspension was centrifuged at 5000 × g for 10 min using a TDL-5-A centrifuge (Shanghai Anting Scientific Instrument Factory, Shanghai, China) to obtain the aqueous and residue phases. The aqueous phase was freeze-dried for subsequent structural analysis and preparation of SDF and peptides, and the residue phase was freeze-dried for structural analysis and IDF preparation. The aqueous phase, residue phase, SDF, IDF, and peptides obtained from untreated okara served as the control. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Structural analysis of aqueous phase, SDF, and peptide

Separation of SDF and peptide

SDF: the freeze-dried aqueous phase (1.00 g, m1) was dissolved in 20 mL of deionized water and mixed with 95% ethanol at a 1:4 (v/v) ratio. This mixture was allowed to precipitate for 12 h at 25°C, and the precipitate was collected by centrifugation at 5000g for 20 min and then freeze-dried to yield SDF (m4). The supernatant was subjected to vacuum distillation (40°C, −0.1 MPa) to remove the ethanol and then freeze-dried to obtain the peptide for the structure analysis.

Fourier transform infrared analyses of aqueous phase and peptide

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) analyses were performed as described by Chen et al. (2024) with some modifications. The freeze-dried aqueous phase and peptide samples (1 mg) were accurately weighed and mixed with 100 mg of potassium bromide (KBr) to form homogeneous pellets. The FTIR spectra were acquired using a Bruker Alpha II spectrometer (Bruker Optik GmbH, Ettlingen, Germany) at room temperature (25°C). Each sample was scanned 32 times over a wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm−1, with a spectral resolution of 4 cm−1. KBr pellets served as the background reference for baseline correction. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Fluorescence spectroscopy analyses of aqueous phase and peptide

Fluorescence spectroscopy analyses were performed as described by Sun et al. (2025) with some modifications. The freeze-dried aqueous phase and peptide samples were dissolved in a phosphate-buffered solution (PBS: 10 mM, pH 7.0) to a final concentration of 0.1 mg/mL. Intrinsic fluorescence spectra were recorded using a Hitachi F-7000 fluorescence spectrophotometer (Hitachi High-Technologies Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) with an excitation wavelength of 290 nm, excitation and emission slit widths of 5 nm, and a sensitivity of 2 nm. Emission spectra were recorded from 300 to 500 nm. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Surface hydrophobicity measurement of aqueous phase and peptide

Surface hydrophobicity measurement was performed as reported by Sun et al. (2023). The freeze-dried aqueous phase and peptide samples were dissolved in PBS (10 mM, pH 7.0) to prepare solutions with final protein concentrations of 0.0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, and 1.0 mg/mL. Each solution (4 mL) was mixed thoroughly with 20 μL of 8 mM 8-Anilino-1-Naphthalenesulfonic acid (ANS). Fluorescence intensity was measured using a Hitachi F-7000 fluorescence spectrophotometer (Hitachi High-Technologies Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) with an excitation wavelength of 370 nm and an emission wavelength of 490 nm (excitation and emission slit widths of 5 nm). Surface hydrophobicity (H0) was determined as the initial slope of the linear regression between the fluorescence intensity and protein concentration (mg/mL). All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Ultraviolet–visible spectroscopy measurement of aqueous phase and peptide

The freeze-dried aqueous phase and peptide samples were dissolved in PBS (10 mM, pH 7.0) to a final protein concentration of 1 mg/mL, as described by Shi et al. (2022) with slight modifications. Ultraviolet–visible (UV) absorption spectra were recorded from 200 to 400 nm at 25°C using a TU-1900 spectrophotometer (Beijing Purkinje General Instrument Co., Ltd.‌‌‌, Beijing, China), with PBS as the blank reference. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy analyses of aqueous phase

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) analysis was performed as described by Matignon et al. (2014) with some modifications. The freeze-dried aqueous phase was suspended in deionized water to a concentration of 0.1 g/mL. The samples were sequentially stained with 0.1% (w/v) Nile Red for lipids, 0.01% (w/v) fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) for proteins, and 0.1% (w/v) Calcofluor White for DF, with incubation at room temperature for 30 min. The samples were then imaged using a FV3000 confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Images were acquired at excitation wavelengths of 405 nm, 488 nm, and 518 nm.

FTIR analyses of SDF

SDF (1 mg) was accurately weighed and mixed with 100 mg of KBr to form homogeneous pellets. FTIR spectra were also acquired using a Bruker Alpha II spectrometer (Bruker Optik GmbH, Ettlingen, Germany) at room temperature (25°C). The operating parameters of infrared were consistent with those used for the peptide analysis. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Monosaccharide composition and molecular weight analyses of SDF

The monosaccharide composition analysis of SDF was conducted as described by Wu et al. (2023). The SDF (1 mg/mL) was hydrolyzed with 0.5 mL of 4 M trifluoroacetic acid at 110°C for 6 h and 90 min. The resulting hydrolysate was mixed with 200 μL of methanol and dried under nitrogen. The procedure was repeated three times to remove residual trifluoroacetic acid, and then the sample was reconstituted in 1 mL of distilled water for derivatization. Next, 20 μL of the hydrolysate was combined with 100 μL of 28.0%–30.0% (v/v) ammonia solution and 100 μL of 0.5 M PMP methanol solution, incubated for 30 min at 70°C and then dried under nitrogen. The residue was dissolved in 200 μL of 10% (v/v) acetonitrile aqueous solution and extracted four times with equal volumes of chloroform (discarding the lower organic phase). The upper aqueous phase was filtered through a 0.22 μm organic membrane for analysis.

Monosaccharide compositions were examined using an ACQUITY UPLC I-Class with a binary pump (Waters, Eschborn, Germany) and a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer Xevo TQ-S (Waters) with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.7 μm). Mobile phase A was 5% (v/v) acetonitrile with 10 mM ammonium acetate (pH 9.0, adjusted using ammonium hydroxide), and mobile phase B was 95% (v/v) acetonitrile aqueous solution. The gradient was as follows: 0.0–7.0 min, 88%–85% A; 7.0–8.5 min, 85%–0% A; 8.5–11.0 min, 0%–100% A, and 11.0–13.0 min, 88%–12% A. The flow rate was 0.3 mL/min, the column temperature was 35°C and the injection volume was 1 μL.

The molecular weight (Mw) of SDF was determined using high-performance liquid chromatography, according to the method described by Li et al. (2015) with minor modifications. Briefly, 50 mg of SDF was dissolved in the mobile phase and diluted to 10 mL in a volumetric flask. Standard samples included dextrose standards (T-2000, T-300, T-150, T-10, and T-5). The chromatographic conditions were as follows: column, Ultahydrogel™ Linear (300 mm × 7.8 mm); mobile phase, 0.1 N sodium nitrate; flow rate, 0.5 mL/min; and column temperature, 40°C. The standard curve was y = −0.538 + 13.913, R2 = 0.9932.

Results and discussion

Effects of complex enzyme hydrolysis on the SDF/IDF ratio and peptide content of okara

Viscozyme® L hydrolysis

The effects of Viscozyme® L hydrolysis on the SDF/IDF ratio and peptide content are illustrated in Fig. 2. The SDF/IDF ratio and peptide content increased initially and then remained stable with the increasing enzyme concentration. The SDF/IDF ratio and peptide content reached the highest values of 0.26 and 54.16%, respectively, at an enzyme concentration of 0.50% (Fig. 2A and B). Under this condition, the SDF content was 15.03%, representing a 13.67% increase compared with the raw material. The SDF/IDF ratio significantly increased with the increase in time from 2 h to 4 h and reached its peak at 4 h (Fig. 2C). With a further increase in time, the SDF/IDF ratio decreased significantly. This decline might be due to the excessive hydrolysis of IDF and SDF into glucose, thereby reducing the yield of SDF (Zhang et al., 2024).

The effects of Viscozyme® L hydrolysis on the SDF/IDF ratio and peptide content of okara.

The maximum values of the SDF/IDF ratio and peptide content were detected at pH 5.0 (Fig. 2E and F). Similarly, reported that the optimal pH for Viscozyme® L activity was 5.0. Xiang et al. (2024) also reported that SDF and protein were less likely to agglomerate at pH 5.0 because of electrostatic repulsion. Hence, the SDF content was improved at this pH. As depicted in Fig. 2G and H, the SDF/IDF ratio and peptide content reaching their peak values at 50°C. The SDF content reached its maximum value (Fig. S1G). Hwang et al. (2013) also reported that the optimal temperature of Viscozyme® L was 49.19°C. High temperatures increase the kinetic energy of the molecules in the system, resulting in a faster reaction. Nevertheless, an excessively high temperature causes denaturation and inactivation of enzymes due to the breakage of weak bonds that determine the enzyme structure (de Figueiredo et al., 2018).

Pepsin hydrolysis

Fig. 3 illustrates the effects of pepsin hydrolysis on the SDF/IDF ratio and peptide content. The maximum values of the SDF/IDF ratio and peptide content were detected at pepsin concentrations of 0.50% and 0.75%, respectively (Fig. 3A and B), which might be due to the increasing content of SDF by inhibiting the unfolding of native proteins and their hydrolysis (Ahmad et al., 2020; Ullah et al., 2023). The SDF/IDF ratio and peptide content increased rapidly within the first 2 h (Fig. 3C and D). However, this increasing trend was not obvious within 2–3 h.

The effects of pepsin hydrolysis on the SDF/IDF ratio and peptide content of okara.

The pepsin hydrolysis pH significantly influenced the SDF/IDF ratio and peptide content, with the maximum values reaching 0.48 and 54.19% at pH 1.5, respectively (Fig. 3E and F). Strongly acidic conditions promote the cleavage of hydrogen bonds and β-1,4-glycosidic bonds through protonation, thereby generating low-molecular-weight oligosaccharides and increasing the SDF content (Zheng & Li, 2018). As shown in Fig. 3G and H, the SDF/IDF ratio increased but the peptide content had no significant differences from 27°C (52.11%) to 37°C (54.38%). The increase in the SDF/IDF ratio might be because higher temperature enhances the solubility of SDF (Caprita et al., 2011). Pepsin demonstrates relatively high enzymatic activity between 20°C and 50°C (Han & Sari, 2024). Hence, there were no significant changes in the peptide content with increasing temperatures.

Above all, the SDF/IDF ratio and peptide content were reached to 0.48 and 54.38%, respectively, which were higher than those reported in existing studies (Cui et al., 2026; Vong et al., 2017). However, interactions between DF and protein remain a central focus of ongoing research, as they inhibit the conversion of IDF into SDF and the hydrolysis of protein. Therefore, to improve the conversion of IDF to SDF and the hydrolysis of protein, the structures of SDF, IDF, peptides, and their mixed systems of untreated okara (Control, C), Viscozyme® L–treated okara (V), pepsin–treated okara (P), and Viscozyme® L complex pepsin–treated okara (VP) were examined to clarify the mechanism of fiber–protein interactions.

Effects of complex enzyme hydrolysis on the SDF–peptide interaction

Microstructure of the aqueous phase

CLSM was used to determine the impact of enzyme hydrolysis on the microstructure of the aqueous phase (Fig. 4) and to visualize the spatial distribution of SDF and peptides. The aqueous phase of untreated okara primarily contained SDF, peptides, and oil. SDF was present as large aggregates (Fig. 4C1), and the aggregation of peptide and SDF could be observed (Fig. 4C3). After Viscozyme® L hydrolysis, the aggregates of SDF structure degraded, resulting in a looser morphology (Fig. 4V1). SDF exhibited larger aggregates with small hollow regions on their surfaces after pepsin hydrolysis (Fig. 4P1). Compared with Viscozyme® L or pepsin hydrolysis, the complex enzyme hydrolysis resulted in a more extensive degradation of SDF, producing finer and more uniformly distributed fractions in the aqueous phase (Fig. 4VP1). The observation of peptide and SDF regions in the enzyme-treated samples revealed a high colocalization of peptides with SDF areas. The electrostatic interaction between protein and polysaccharide becomes increasingly attractive, resulting in their colocalization (Brüls-Gill et al., 2024). Moreover, the hydrolysis of okara protein could improve its electrostatic interaction with SDF, thereby inducing their colocalization. Díaz-Piñero et al. (2026) also showed that electrostatic attractions between the positively charged amino groups of whey protein isolate and the negatively charged sulfate groups of agar promote protein–agar colocalization. These findings demonstrated the binding of okara protein or peptides to SDF.

FTIR analysis of aqueous phase, SDF, and peptides

FTIR analysis provides insights into the primary functional groups present in the samples. The FTIR spectra for the aqueous phase, peptides, and SDF are displayed in Fig. 5. The absorption band at 3100–3700 cm−1 corresponds to the stretching vibrations of hydroxyl groups (–OH) (Dai et al., 2023). Characteristic absorption peaks near 2900 cm−1 are attributed to C–H stretching vibrations (Lin et al., 2025). The amide A band (3300–3400 cm−1), amide I band (1600–1700 cm−1), and amide II band (1500–1600 cm−1) are the key FTIR regions used to characterize protein and peptide conformation and structural features (Yang et al., 2023). The region spanning 1200–950 cm−1 represents the fingerprint area for carbohydrates (Meng et al., 2025). Compared with the control, the –OH absorption peaks of the aqueous phase shifted to higher wavenumbers (blue shift) after hydrolysis by pepsin and the complex enzyme (Fig. 5A). This finding indicated a weakening of intramolecular interactions within polysaccharide chains, as well as those between polysaccharides and proteins, accompanied by a reduction in hydrogen bonding (Jin et al., 2020; Lin et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2023). Moreover, Viscozyme® L hydrolysis induced a slight red shift in the hydroxyl absorption peak of the aqueous phase, which is potentially attributable to the disruption of the SDF structure and the increased exposure of –OH groups (Zhao et al., 2024).

Fig. 5B displays the FTIR spectra of the peptides. Compared with the control, the amide A band (3300–3400 cm−1) exhibited a blue shift of peptide after hydrolysis by Viscozyme® L, pepsin, and complex enzyme, suggesting the disruption of hydrogen bonds within the proteins (Yang et al., 2023). No significant alterations were detected in the remaining spectral peaks. Fig. 5C shows the FTIR spectra of the SDF. All hydroxyl absorption peaks also exhibited a blue shift compared with those in the control, probably due to the disruption of intermolecular and intramolecular hydrogen bonds resulting from the enzyme hydrolysis of SDF. Compared with the Viscozyme® L and pepsin treatments, the complex enzyme treatment resulted in a more pronounced shift, indicating a greater extent of disruption of hydrogen bonds. This stronger disruption of SDF–peptide hydrogen bonds contributed to higher SDF/IDF ratio and peptide content. The absorption peaks at 1730 and 1640 cm−1 were assigned to the carboxyl ester band and free carboxyl groups, respectively (Chylińska et al., 2016). Compared with those of peptides and SDF, the peaks of –OH of the aqueous phase displayed a significant red shift, except for the complex enzyme hydrolysis. This phenomenon suggested the formation of hydrogen bonds between the hydroxyl groups of polysaccharides and the amino groups of proteins in the aqueous phase, thereby inhibiting their stretching and vibration (Dong et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2024). Therefore, the complex enzyme hydrolysis exerted a stronger disruptive effect on the interactions between SDF and peptides, while also increasing the SDF/IDF ratio and peptide content.

Intrinsic fluorescence, surface hydrophobicity and UV spectroscopy of the aqueous phase and peptides

Fig. 5D and G depicts the effects of enzyme hydrolysis on the intrinsic fluorescence of the peptides and aqueous phase. Tryptophan (Trp) is highly sensitive to its local microenvironment, and conformational changes in proteins can cause corresponding alterations in the fluorescence emission spectra of Trp. The maximum emission of all samples was ≥330 nm, indicating that some Trp residues reside in relatively polar microenvironments (Vivian & Callis, 2001).

Compared with the control, the maximum emission of the peptide obtained from pepsin and complex enzyme hydrolysis exhibited a red shift, suggesting that Trp residues, previously buried within the hydrophobic core, became exposed to a more polar environment (del Rio et al., 2021; Tao et al., 2019). Hamuro et al. (2008) also reported that pepsin preferentially hydrolyzes peptide bonds adjacent to aromatic residues, including phenylalanine, Trp, and tyrosine. In contrast, the maximum emission of the peptide obtained from Viscozyme® L hydrolysis showed a slight blue shift, which is probably due to the reaggregation of SDF and peptides. Tang et al. (2024) reported the okara proteins could aggregate through hydrophobic interactions.

The surface hydrophobicity (H0) of the peptides obtained from pepsin and complex enzyme hydrolysis was higher than that of the peptides obtained from the control and Viscozyme® L hydrolysis. A greater number of Trp residues in the protein became exposed, thereby increasing the presence of hydrophobic amino acids on the molecular surface (Cui et al., 2021). In the Viscozyme® L hydrolysis, the interactions between SDF and peptides increased as evidenced by the FTIR results, leading to a decreasing trend in H0. The trends in λmax and H0 values (Fig. 5G and H) of the aqueous phase were similar to those of the peptides (Fig. 5D and E). Nevertheless, the λmax value of the aqueous phase generally exhibited a shift toward shorter wavelengths than that of the peptides. This phenomenon might be attributed to collisions among protein–polysaccharide molecules, wherein the presence of polysaccharides improves intermolecular interactions, resulting in the encapsulation of certain Trp residues within other macromolecular side chains (Gao et al., 2024). The increased exposure of hydrophobic regions and the red-shifted fluorescence observed within the pepsin and complex enzyme groups further facilitated protein hydrolysis, consistent with the significantly elevated peptide content.

The effects of enzyme hydrolysis on the UV spectroscopy analysis of the peptides and aqueous phase are depicted in Fig. 5F and I. The typical range for UV spectra in this study is 190–350 nm. Compared with the control treatment, both pepsin and complex enzyme treatments induced a slight red shift (Fig. 5F), which might be due to the enzymatic hydrolysis of okara protein that facilitated its degradation and consequently increased the exposure of tyrosine and Trp residues (Liu et al., 2024). The change trends in the UV absorption of the aqueous phase and the peptide were consistent with the fluorescence results and indicated the changes in the tertiary structure of the protein.

Monosaccharide composition, Mw, and crystallinity of SDF

Table 1 summarizes the monosaccharide composition of SDF. SDF is composed primarily of galactose, arabinose, and xylose, a composition that is consistent with previous reports on okara SDF (Liang et al., 2023). The contents of galactose in the SDF after Viscozyme® L and complex enzyme hydrolysis increased compared with those in the control, suggesting the partial depolymerization of hemicellulose and its conversion from IDF to SDF (Lin et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2024). This result was consistent with the SDF/IDF ratio (Fig. 2A).

Structural analysis of residue phase and IDF

Microstructure of residue phase

The impact of enzyme hydrolysis on the microstructure of the residue phase was determined by CLSM (Fig. 6). Fig. 6C1 depicts the relatively intact skeletal structure of DF, within which protein components were encapsulated. Compared with the control, the Viscozyme® L hydrolysis effectively disrupted the skeletal structure of DF, yielding large sheet-like fragments (Fig. 6V1). With complex enzyme hydrolysis, the IDF structure underwent severe damage and progressive dissociation (Fig. 6VP1, 6v VP2 and 6 VP3). This more extensive degradation of IDF by the complex enzyme was consistent with the significantly increased SDF/IDF ratio, demonstrating a synergistic effect between the two enzymes. Similar to the trends observed for SDF and peptides, the observations of IDF and protein revealed that IDF and okara protein colocalized within the same regions. Wang et al. (2018) discovered a similar phenomenon, where protein and fiber residues could form complex aggregates.

FTIR analysis of IDF and residue phase

The FTIR spectra of IDF and the residue phase are shown in Fig. 7. The spectral patterns across all IDF samples were similar, indicating that the enzyme hydrolysis did not alter the functional groups or chemical bonds of the IDF (Fig. 7A). Compared with the control, the –OH absorption peaks of IDF after complex enzyme hydrolysis exhibited a red shift, indicating enhanced exposure of hydroxyl groups and strengthened hydrogen bonding interactions both between polysaccharide chains and within individual molecules (Li et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2024).

Compared with the control, the –OH absorption peak in the residue phase showed a blue shift after enzyme hydrolysis (Fig. 7B). Similarly, Ma et al. (2024) also discovered the same phenomenon when investigating the deproteinization of grape polysaccharides. This finding suggests that protein hydrolysis disrupts the hydrogen bonding interactions between IDF and proteins. Compared with the residue phase, the complex enzyme and Viscozyme® L hydrolysis induced a significant red shift at the –OH/N–H stretching band of IDF, indicating increased hydrogen bonding within and between polysaccharide chains (Li et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2024). In contrast, the IDF samples treated with pepsin exhibited a marked blue shift, suggesting that the removal of protein disrupted the hydrogen bonding interactions in IDF–protein complexes (Ma et al., 2024).

Monosaccharide composition, Mw, and crystallinity of IDF

Table 1 shows the monosaccharide composition of IDF. The monosaccharide composition of IDF was similar to that of SDF. Compared with the control treatment, the Viscozyme® L and complex enzyme hydrolysis decreased the levels of galactose and arabinose in the IDF. These changes suggest the partial depolymerization of hemicellulose and its conversion from IDF to SDF (Lin et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2024). All the Mw values of IDF were ∼1000 kDa, consistent with previous reports (724–2081 kDa) (Lu et al., 2013). Compared with the control treatment, the enzyme hydrolysis decreased the Mw value of IDF. The crystallinity of IDF increased significantly with enzyme hydrolysis. Compared with the control (8.92%), the crystallinity of IDF increased 12.89%, 45.85%, and 88.34% after hydrolysis with the Viscozyme® L, pepsin, and complex enzymes, respectively. The preferential hydrolysis of the amorphous regions of IDF resulted in a higher proportion of crystalline structures (Zhao et al., 2006). The highest crystallinity achieved by the complex enzyme treatment further demonstrated the preferential hydrolysis of amorphous regions, which facilitated the conversion of IDF to SDF and supported the elevation of SDF yield.

Mechanism of fiber–protein interaction of okara

Fig. 8 depicts the mechanism of fiber–protein interactions in okara. Viscozyme® L primarily acted on IDF and converted it into SDF. Moreover, the SDF in okara also could be degraded by Viscozyme® L, and the degraded SDF underwent reaggregation. Viscozyme® L hydrolysis improved the hydrogen bonding between SDF and peptide but weakened their hydrophobic interactions. Simultaneously, it increased the hydrogen bonding interactions between IDF and protein. Pepsin hydrolysis of proteins promoted the release of SDF. Under strongly acidic conditions, IDF was partially degraded, which was accompanied by increased hydrophobic interactions and weakened hydrogen bonds between SDF and peptides. The complex enzyme first hydrolyzed the IDF to promote the release of embedded proteins. It also disrupted the hydrogen bonds between SDF and peptides, thus promoting the SDF/IDF ratio and the generation of peptides. The complex enzyme exhibited a stronger capacity to disrupt hydrogen bonds than Viscozyme® L or pepsin according to aqueous phase structure analysis. These changes corresponded to the highest SDF/IDF ratio and peptide content achieved under the complex enzyme treatment, demonstrating a correlation between the microstructural, fiber–protein interactions and the enhanced macroscopic outcomes. Hydrogen bonds might be the major interaction force between fiber and protein. Meanwhile, the complex enzyme hydrolysis of the amorphous regions of IDF exposed a more ordered and regular structure. Furthermore, colocalization phenomena were detected between SDF and peptide, as well as between IDF and protein, and this colocalization might be related to their gel properties (Brüls-Gill et al., 2024).

The fiber–protein interaction mechanism diagram of okara during enzyme hydrolysis.

This study systematically investigated dietary fiber-protein interactions across the aqueous phase (containing SDF and peptide) and the residue phase (containing IDF and protein). This dual-phase approach provided novel insights into how dietary fibers associate with peptides in the aqueous phase and with protein in the residue phase during enzymatic hydrolysis. Both SDF–peptide and IDF–protein interactions were primarily driven by hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions, yet the forces differed between the two phases with enzymatic hydrolysis.

Conclusions

Complex enzyme hydrolysis combining Viscozyme® L and pepsin markedly improved the SDF/IDF ratio (0.48) and peptide content (54.38%) of okara. CLSM findings revealed that complex enzyme hydrolysis degraded both SDF and IDF, while inducing colocalization between SDF and peptide, as well as between IDF and proteins. Compared with Viscozyme® L hydrolysis, complex enzyme hydrolysis further weakened the hydrogen bonding within SDF–peptide and increased H0 of peptides according to FTIR, fluorescence, surface hydrophobicity, and UV analyses. In contrast to pepsin hydrolysis, complex enzyme hydrolysis enhanced DF degradation and the hydrogen bonding within the IDF–protein. Furthermore, the results from monosaccharide composition, Mw, and crystallinity analyses indicated that the complex enzyme preferentially hydrolyzed the amorphous regions of IDF, thereby increasing its structural order.

Okara has great potential for incorporation into a variety of food products, owing to its nutritional and biological value. Complex enzyme hydrolysis of okara not only minimizes waste generation but also expand its economic and nutritional accessibility in the food industries. To fully realize the nutritional and functional potential of okara, future investigations are required not only to characterize its bioactive properties but also to evaluate its digestibility and interactions with other food components–factors that are critical for its successful application in real food systems.
