Balancing carbon reduction and treatment adequacy in sustainable continuous renal replacement therapy
Authors: Cyveen Weeraratna, Benjamin W. Sansom, Forbes McGain, Andrew A. Udy, Emily J. See
Published: 2026

We thank Huang et al. for their considered analysis of our research article.1

This retrospective observational study was based at two tertiary intensive care units (ICUs) over an 8-year period, yielding 70,000 h of therapeutic data for analysis. Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) was administered to a broad range of critically ill patients with differing pathologies, necessitating a spectrum of prescriptions and doses. The CRRT guidelines used at both centres are informed by the current evidence base for timing of initiation,[2], [3], [4], [5], [6] dialysis dose,[7], [8], [9] citrate anticoagulation,10,11 and blood flow rates.12,13 Machine-recorded data facilitated quantification of consumables associated with therapies, which was then translated into carbon emissions and costs.

How are environmental gains balanced against treatment adequacy?

Optimising patient outcomes with targeted prescriptions remains our primary objective, while the sustainable delivery of therapy represents an important secondary benefit. We reiterate that high-value care (i.e. care that avoids unnecessary diagnostics and therapies) is both patient-centred and sustainable.2.Effluent dose is both a clinical as well as an environmental variable.

2.Effluent dose is both a clinical as well as an environmental variable.

Effluent dose is both a clinical as well as an environmental variable.

The current international recommendation for effluent dose is 20–25 ml/kg/hr.14 We note that high effluent rates (e.g. 30–45 ml/kg/hr) have not been shown to improve patient outcomes.15,16 Emerging data further suggest that lower effluent rates (e.g. 10–12 ml/kg/hr) may be preferable and are currently under investigation in the WISDOM, LIMIT, and KETZEREI trials.17,18 Notably, these guidelines do not preclude individualisation based on specific clinical scenarios (e.g. severe electrolyte or acid-base disturbances). However, such disturbances tend to be short-lived following initiation of CRRT, permitting reversion to the baseline prescription.

Given the availability of high-quality clinical trials, the impact of effluent dose on clinical outcomes was not specifically examined in this work. We have, however, included predicted carbon emissions and financial cost data for effluent doses ranging from 10 ml/kg/hr to 50 ml/kg/hr to inform the environmental, social and governance framework arm of practice guidelines.3.Reporting prescribed versus delivered dose, downtime, potassium, and acid-base correction, fluid-balance target achievement, and escalation events alongside carbon dioxide equivalent emissions.

Our results are presented as “patient-dialysis-day carbon dioxide equivalent”, a service unit derived to represent 24 h of actual CRRT delivery. We specifically sought to quantify consumables resulting from therapies delivered.

Real-time prescribing by clinicians, adjusted for circuit inefficiencies, acid-base and electrolyte corrections, volume removal/fluid balance management, and escalation events comprises all required therapeutic manoeuvres, which secondarily translate into consumables. We defined the delivered therapeutic dose ranges (<30 ml/kg/hr, 30–40 ml/kg/hr and >40 ml/kg/hr) and presented the associated medians and interquartile ranges of waste totals, CO2.ekg/day and AUD$ costs. We then performed multivariate analyses specifically looking at how variables associated with improved circuit life, influenced by circuit anticoagulation and blood flow rates, and the use of auto-effluent impacted the quantity of consumables required together with their influence on CO2.ekg/day and AUD$ costs.4.Filter life

4.Filter life

Filter life

Filter sets are the costliest in terms of carbon and currency, thereby providing the motivation to maximise their duration of use in line with manufacture recommendations. Measurable factors influencing circuit life in our study included circuit anticoagulation and blood flow rates. We agree that catheter dysfunction, inflammatory burden, and anticoagulation, impact circuit lifespan. While catheter site selection may be dictated by clinical circumstance, and the ability to modify inflammatory burden is limited, choice of anticoagulation provides a common opportunity to enhance circuit lifespan.5.Citrate anticoagulation

5.Citrate anticoagulation

Citrate anticoagulation

Regional citrate anticoagulation, in widespread use in Australasia, has been shown to be superior to systemic heparin due to prolongation of circuit life,19 but citrate-containing fluids are more expensive than conventional fluids, and higher effluent doses are often required to match the prescribed blood flow rate. On balance, our findings were favoured towards citrate: “Mediation analysis confirmed that citrate reduced CO2 emissions, and financial cost indirectly via filter life extension, but these gains were partly offset by direct increases in CO2 and cost attributable to citrate.”1 We did not account for the consumables associated with monitoring of both citrate and noncitrate circuits, as the information was not available. However, broadly we would not expect a significant impact on CO2 emissions or to differ widely across circuits.6.Auto-effluent

6.Auto-effluent

Auto-effluent

Finally, auto-effluent functionality is specific to PrisMax® (Vantive, IL, USA) devices and can be used with either citrate or noncitrate circuits, as reflected in our patient cohort. It confers significant carbon advantages with extended-duration therapy. While we observed that circuit lifespan is longer in citrate compared with noncitrate circuits with auto-effluent connection, determining whether this represented a true causal association was beyond the scope of our analysis. Importantly, neither the use of auto-effluent nor extended filter life (within the manufacturer’s recommendation) has foreseeable negative impacts on patient outcomes, but both have important impacts on carbon emission reduction.

In summary, sustainable CRRT should be examined through the dual lenses of patient benefit and environmental impact. This principle extends beyond CRRT to healthcare interventions more broadly. Engagement of critical care researchers with clinical, economic, and environmental outcomes is likely to become increasingly central to future practice and research.20
