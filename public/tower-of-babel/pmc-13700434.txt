Feasibility of multidomain cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic biomarker assessment for in-hospital mortality in older heart failure inpatients: an exploratory retrospective cohort study
Authors: Musa Bin Bashir, Chen Chen, Ho Yong Il, Rzgar Mustafa Ahmed, Navneet Navneet, Zhiquan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) dysfunction integrates cardiac, renal, metabolic, and inflammatory risk and is particularly relevant for older patients hospitalized with heart failure. Whether standardized multidomain CKM-related biomarker assessment is feasible using real-world hospital data in older heart-failure inpatients and what limitations arise from such data remains poorly characterized.MethodsRetrospective cohort study using hospital records from Zhongnan Hospital (Jan 2022–Dec 2024). Patients with missing in-hospital outcome status were excluded. Primary outcome was in-hospital mortality. Associations between age, sex, ECG major abnormality, BNP, BUN, IL-6, and mortality were evaluated using Firth penalized logistic regression in complete-case patients. Nested ROC models described apparent discrimination (DeLong tests), bootstrap resampling estimated optimism-corrected discrimination, and an exploratory CKM biomarker burden analysis was performed.ResultsAmong 1,645 records, 1,131 patients had interpretable in-hospital outcomes (64 deaths, 1,067 survivors). A complete CKM biomarker panel (BNP, BUN, and IL-6) was available in only 186 patients (16.4%; 17 deaths, 169 survivors; median non-survivor age 82 years), who were older and had higher observed mortality than patients excluded for incomplete data. In this selected complete-case cohort, only older age (adjusted OR per year, 1.10; 95% CI, 1.04–1.16; P < 0.001) and BNP per doubling (OR, 1.40; 95% CI, 1.05–1.86; P = 0.021) were independently associated with mortality; sex, ECG major abnormality, BUN, and IL-6 were not. Apparent discrimination was numerically higher for the biomarker-enriched than the clinical-ECG model (AUC 0.841 vs. 0.762), but nested DeLong comparisons were not significant (P ≥ 0.060) and the optimism-corrected C-index was 0.79. A complete CKM biomarker panel was available in only a minority of patients, indicating that standardized multidomain CKM-related assessment was not systematically implemented in routine care. Introduction

Older patients hospitalized with heart failure frequently present with overlapping cardiac dysfunction, renal impairment, metabolic abnormalities, systemic inflammation, and age-related physiological decline. These interrelated processes are increasingly understood within the cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) framework, which posits that cardiovascular outcomes arise from interactions across multiple domains rather than isolated organ-specific abnormalities [1, 2]. The American Heart Association PREVENT equations, published in 2024, estimate longer-term cardiovascular risk in general, largely outpatient populations by integrating demographic, cardiovascular, renal, and metabolic information [3]; they are cited here only as conceptual support for multidomain risk integration, not as a tool intended for acute in-hospital mortality.

In acute-care settings, risk stratification must rely on variables that are rapidly available and routinely collected. B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) is a well-established biomarker of myocardial wall stress and hemodynamic burden in heart failure [4–6]. Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) reflects renal impairment, neurohormonal activation, and congestion, and has been incorporated into mortality risk scores for decompensated heart failure [7]. Interleukin-6 (IL-6) is a central inflammatory cytokine linked to atherosclerosis, myocardial injury, and residual cardiovascular risk [8, 9]. Electrocardiography (ECG) provides additional, widely available information, with major abnormalities indicating ischemic, conduction, or structural disturbances.

Despite the clinical plausibility of combining these domains, the incremental value of age, sex, ECG major abnormality, BNP, BUN, and IL-6 for short-term mortality assessment remains insufficiently defined. Existing risk models often focus on selected syndromes or curated trial populations, whereas real-world acute datasets face incomplete biomarker testing, heterogeneous presentations, and few outcome events. Retrospective complete-case analyses are vulnerable to selection bias, overfitting, and optimistic performance estimates [10–12]. Moreover, whether a complete multidomain CKM-related biomarker panel is even systematically captured in routine acute care — a prerequisite for any such assessment — is itself uncertain.

In this retrospective acute-care cohort study, we explored whether standardized multidomain CKM-related biomarker assessment (BNP, BUN, and IL-6), alongside routinely available demographic and ECG information, is feasible using real-world hospital data in older heart-failure inpatients, and what limitations arise from such data. Within this feasibility-oriented framing we described associations with in-hospital mortality, compared nested models to characterize apparent discrimination, and performed an exploratory CKM biomarker domain-burden analysis. Given the retrospective design, substantial missing data, and few mortality events, the study is intended as a feasibility-oriented, hypothesis-generating analysis rather than the development or validation of a prognostic model.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective, single-center cohort study used electronic health records from older patients admitted to the Department of Cardiology, Zhongnan Hospital, Wuhan University; Institute of Myocardial Injury and Repair, Wuhan University, with a primary heart-failure-related diagnosis identified from International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) discharge codes, between January 2022 and December 2024. The term older is used in the conventional geriatric sense of age 65 years or older; no age threshold was applied at selection, and the observed age distribution is reported in the Results. The study evaluated whether demographic variables, admission electrocardiographic findings, and cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic biomarkers were associated with in-hospital mortality. The prespecified variables of interest were age, sex, ECG major abnormality, B-type natriuretic peptide, blood urea nitrogen, and interleukin-6.

The study used anonymized retrospective clinical data and did not involve direct patient contact, intervention, or alteration of clinical care. The study was reviewed by the relevant institutional authority at Zhongnan Hospital of Wuhan University, and the requirement for individual informed consent was waived because of the retrospective use of anonymized routinely collected hospital data. All data were handled confidentially and analyzed in accordance with institutional requirements and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study population and cohort assembly

The complete cohort included 1,645 hospital records from the study period. Records with missing, unclear, or non-informative in-hospital outcome status were excluded before mortality analysis. After excluding 514 records for this reason, 1,131 patients were included in the mortality analytic cohort, including 64 patients who died during hospitalization and 1,067 survivors.

The primary multivariable analysis was restricted to patients with complete data for all final-model variables: age, sex, ECG major abnormality, BNP, BUN, and IL-6. Patients missing one or more of these final-model variables were excluded from the complete-case primary-model analysis. After excluding 945 patients with incomplete final-model data, the final complete-case primary-model cohort included 186 patients, including 17 in-hospital deaths and 169 survivors.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients were eligible if they were admitted to the Department of Cardiology, Zhongnan Hospital of Wuhan University, during the study period with a primary heart-failure-related diagnosis (ICD-10 discharge codes) and had electronic records suitable for assessment of in-hospital outcome status. No age restriction was applied. Records were excluded from the mortality analytic cohort if in-hospital outcome status was missing, unclear, or non-informative. For the primary complete-case model, patients were further excluded if data were missing for age, sex, ECG major abnormality, BNP, BUN, or IL-6.

Outcome

The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality, defined as death occurring during the index hospitalization. Patients discharged alive were classified as survivors. Records without clear in-hospital outcome status were excluded before mortality analysis.

Clinical and diagnostic variables

Age and sex were extracted from the clinical dataset. Age was analyzed as a continuous variable in years. Sex was coded as male = 1 and female = 2 in the original dataset; therefore, regression estimates for sex compare female patients with male patients.

Primary diagnosis group was based on the primary discharge diagnosis assigned by a senior cardiologist according to ICD-10 and extracted from the electronic medical record, then categorized as refractory heart failure, chronic kidney failure with heart failure, acute heart failure, or other heart-failure diagnosis. Diagnosis group was used for descriptive characterization of the cohort but was not included in the final multivariable model.

Electrocardiographic assessment

Electrocardiographic data were obtained from the admission ECG or, when the admission ECG was unavailable, from the ECG recorded closest to admission during the index hospitalization; no maximum interval from admission was prespecified, and the resulting variation in measurement timing is acknowledged as a limitation. ECG findings were linked with clinical and biomarker data.

ECG recordings were interpreted by a senior cardiologist using standard clinical 12-lead electrocardiographic interpretation; findings were not separately re-coded using a formal research classification system such as the Minnesota Code, and inter-reader reliability was not formally assessed. For the main analysis, ECG findings were summarized using the binary variable ECG major abnormality. A recording was classified as showing a major abnormality if one or more of the following findings was reported, consistent with categories conventionally regarded as major abnormalities (cf. the Minnesota Code classification [25]): atrial fibrillation or flutter; second- or third-degree atrioventricular block; left or right bundle-branch block; intraventricular conduction delay; low voltage; left ventricular hypertrophy; pathological Q waves (anterior, inferior, or lateral); ST-segment elevation or depression; T-wave inversion; or composite ischemic ST/T change. Isolated premature atrial or ventricular complexes and first-degree atrioventricular block were treated as minor findings and did not by themselves define a major abnormality. Patients without any recorded major abnormality were classified as having no major ECG abnormality. This classification scheme was applied consistently throughout the present analysis; the wording in the current revision clarifies the pre-existing coding rules specifically that isolated premature atrial or ventricular complexes and first-degree atrioventricular block were never counted as major abnormalities and does not represent a re-derivation of the ECG variable. Accordingly, the prevalence of ECG major abnormality reported in Table 1 and the associated regression estimates are unchanged from the previous version.

This binary ECG variable was included in the primary Firth penalized logistic regression model and in the baseline clinical-ECG model used for nested AUC analyses. Because major abnormalities were near-universal in this heart-failure cohort, the binary variable had limited capacity to discriminate; individual ECG subtypes were therefore described separately by survival status (Table S5) but were not modeled in multivariable analyses because of the limited number of in-hospital deaths. Descriptive ECG results were reported using available-case denominators.

Biomarker variables

Biomarker data were obtained from routine hospital blood tests performed during the index hospitalization and linked with the clinical and ECG data. Three prespecified biomarkers were selected to represent key cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic domains: BNP for the cardiac/hemodynamic domain, BUN for the kidney-related domain, and IL-6 for the inflammatory domain. IL-6 was chosen for the inflammatory domain because it is the upstream cytokine that drives hepatic C-reactive protein synthesis (the IL-6 to C-reactive protein acute-phase axis) and is mechanistically central to the inflammatory pathway emphasized in the CKM framework and to emerging anti-inflammatory therapy in cardiovascular disease [8, 9]. In this cohort, IL-6 was also the most frequently measured inflammatory marker (available in 446 patients), more often than high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (282 patients) or C-reactive protein (147 patients); substituting these markers would therefore not have increased, and would likely have reduced, the complete-case sample.

When more than one biomarker measurement was available, the value recorded closest to admission was used. BNP and IL-6 were reported in pg/mL, and BUN was reported in mmol/L. BNP and IL-6 were transformed using base-2 logarithms because of right-skewed distributions; base-2 logarithms were used so that each unit corresponds to a doubling of concentration, giving directly interpretable per-doubling odds ratios (a monotone rescaling of the natural logarithm that does not affect model fit). Regression estimates for BNP and IL-6 are therefore reported per doubling of concentration. BUN was modeled as a continuous variable per 1 mmol/L increase. BNP, BUN, and IL-6 were measured as part of routine hospital laboratory testing according to institutional clinical laboratory protocols.

Missing data and complete-case analysis

Missing data was handled using complete-case analysis for the primary multivariable model. The mortality analytic cohort included 1,131 patients with known in-hospital outcome status. The final complete-case primary-model cohort included only patients with available data for age, sex, ECG major abnormality, BNP, BUN, and IL-6.

Patients missing one or more final-model variables were excluded from the primary complete-case analysis. Predictor missingness was assessed within the mortality analytic cohort, and patients included in the complete-case model were compared with those excluded because of incomplete final-model data. Because biomarker availability was incomplete in routine clinical practice, the complete-case cohort may not fully represent the broader mortality analytic cohort. Therefore, model estimates and discrimination metrics were interpreted as exploratory and potentially affected by complete-case selection.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges, and categorical variables as counts with percentages. Baseline characteristics were compared using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous variables and chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables, as appropriate.

The primary analysis used Firth penalized logistic regression to evaluate associations between prespecified predictors (age, sex, ECG major abnormality, BNP, BUN, IL-6) and in-hospital mortality. Firth penalization was employed to reduce small-sample bias and address separation concerns, given the limited number of mortality events. The final complete-case cohort contained 17 deaths and 6 candidate predictors, yielding an events-per-variable ratio of approximately 2.8, which falls below the recommended minimum of 10–20 for reliable prediction model development [13]. Therefore, all estimates should be interpreted as exploratory.

Apparent discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals. Nested models were compared using DeLong’s method. Calibration was summarized using the Brier score, calibration intercept, and calibration slope.

An exploratory CKM biomarker domain-burden analysis was performed using adverse complete-case quartile thresholds (BNP > 3402.3 pg/mL, BUN ≥ 13.9 mmol/L, IL-6 > 50.5 pg/mL); these thresholds were specific to this dataset and were not based on externally validated cut-points. Patients were grouped by number of abnormal domains (0–1, 2, or 3), and mortality was assessed using Firth penalized logistic regression adjusted for age, sex, and ECG major abnormality.

All analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.1.

Results

Cohort assembly and complete-case derivation

Between January 2022 and December 2024, the complete cohort comprised 1,645 records. After exclusion of 514 records with missing or unclear in-hospital outcome status, 1,131 patients were included in the mortality analytic cohort, including 64 in-hospital deaths and 1,067 survivors. The final complete-case cohort used for multivariable modeling included 186 patients with complete data for age, sex, ECG major abnormality, BNP, BUN, and IL-6; this cohort included 17 deaths and 169 survivors (Fig. 1).

A complete CKM biomarker panel (BNP, BUN, and IL-6) was available in only 186 of 1,131 patients (16.4%), so the primary complete-case analysis was necessarily restricted to a selected minority of the cohort. Missingness was substantial for individual model variables: within the mortality analytic cohort, BNP was available in 701 patients, BUN in 517, and IL-6 in 446. Overall, 945 of 1,131 patients were excluded from the final complete-case model because of incomplete final-model data (Table S1; Supplementary Fig. S1). Compared with excluded patients, patients included in the complete-case model were older and had a higher observed in-hospital mortality rate, whereas BNP, BUN, and IL-6 distributions were broadly similar among patients with available measurements (Table S2). Baseline characteristics according to in-hospital mortality

Baseline characteristics according to in-hospital mortality are shown in Table 1. Patients who died in hospital were older than survivors, with a median age of 82.0 years compared with 71.0 years among survivors. The distribution of primary diagnosis group differed by mortality status. Acute heart failure was more frequent among patients who died than among survivors, whereas chronic kidney failure with heart failure was less frequent among patients who died.

ECG major abnormality was common in both groups and did not differ significantly between survivors and patients who died. BNP concentrations were numerically higher among patients who died, although this difference was not statistically significant. BUN and IL-6 concentrations were significantly higher among patients who died in hospital.

Final firth penalized logistic regression model

In the complete-case cohort, age and BNP were independently associated with in-hospital mortality in the final Firth penalized logistic regression model (Fig. 2;Table S3). Each 1-year increase in age was associated with higher odds of in-hospital death (adjusted OR, 1.10; 95% CI, 1.04–1.16; P < 0.001). BNP, modeled per doubling after log2 transformation, was also associated with higher mortality odds (adjusted OR, 1.40; 95% CI, 1.05–1.86; P = 0.021).

Female sex, ECG major abnormality, and BUN were not significantly associated with in-hospital mortality after adjustment. IL-6 showed a positive but statistically non-significant association with mortality when modeled per doubling after log2 transformation (adjusted OR, 1.26; 95% CI, 0.97–1.64; P = 0.090). The final model had an apparent AUC of 0.841 in the complete-case cohort. In an exploratory description of individual ECG findings by survival status (available-case; Table S5), no major subtype was significantly associated with mortality after accounting for multiple comparisons; T-wave inversion was more frequent among non-survivors (41.2% vs. 15.7%; unadjusted P = 0.016) but was not significant after correction and is hypothesis-generating. Thus, in the complete-case model, age and BNP were the only variables consistently and independently associated with in-hospital mortality, whereas sex, ECG major abnormality, BUN, and IL-6 were not; these latter associations should be regarded as exploratory.

Incremental discrimination of nested mortality models

Because all nested models were fitted and evaluated in the same 186-patient complete-case cohort, the following discrimination estimates are apparent and exploratory rather than measures of validated incremental value. Apparent discrimination was evaluated across nested models fitted in the same complete-case cohort of 186 patients, including 17 deaths and 169 survivors (Fig. 3; Table S4). The clinical-ECG model, including age, sex, and ECG major abnormality, had an AUC of 0.762 (95% CI, 0.656–0.869). After addition of BNP, the AUC increased numerically to 0.803 (95% CI, 0.716–0.890; ΔAUC, + 0.041; P = 0.148 vs. clinical-ECG model). Addition of BUN resulted in an AUC of 0.799 (95% CI, 0.709–0.890; ΔAUC, + 0.037; P = 0.189), whereas the final model including IL-6 had the highest apparent AUC of 0.841 (95% CI, 0.760–0.923; ΔAUC, + 0.079; P = 0.060). Thus, although apparent discrimination was numerically higher in the biomarker-enriched models, paired DeLong comparisons did not show statistically significant improvement compared with the clinical-ECG model. Accordingly, the numerically higher AUC of the biomarker-enriched model should not be interpreted as evidence that adding CKM-related biomarkers improves discrimination.

An exploratory CKM biomarker domain-burden analysis was performed in the complete-case cohort using adverse complete-case quartile thresholds for BNP, BUN, and IL-6 (Fig. 4; Table S4). After adjustment for age, sex, and ECG major abnormality using Firth penalized logistic regression, the adjusted odds ratios were 1.53 for patients with 2 abnormal domains and 4.14 for patients with 3 abnormal domains, compared with patients with 0–1 abnormal domains. Confidence intervals were wide and crossed 1.0 for both comparisons. The 3-domain group included only 5 patients and 1 death; therefore, these estimates should be interpreted cautiously. Because the highest-burden group comprised only 5 patients with a single in-hospital death, this graded pattern is a descriptive, hypothesis-generating observation and should not be interpreted as evidence of a domain-combination effect or of validated CKM-based risk stratification.

Discussion

In this retrospective single-center cohort of older patients hospitalized with heart failure, age and BNP were the most consistent independent markers of in-hospital mortality. Among 1,131 patients with interpretable outcome status, 64 died during hospitalization. In the complete-case cohort (186 patients, 17 deaths, median age of non-survivors 82 years), each additional year of age was associated with 10% higher odds of in-hospital death, and each doubling of BNP with 40% higher odds. Sex, ECG major abnormality, BUN, and IL-6 were not statistically significant after adjustment.

Although the apparent AUC was numerically higher for the biomarker-enriched model than for the clinical-ECG model (0.841 vs. 0.762), the nested comparisons were not statistically significant, and both models were fitted and evaluated in the same small complete-case cohort. This numerical difference therefore reflects apparent, internal discrimination only and should not be interpreted as evidence that CKM-related biomarkers improve risk stratification, nor as external validation or clinical readiness [10, 11, 13, 19, 24].

The exploratory CKM burden analysis showed a graded increase in observed mortality across abnormal domains (8.3%, 12.0%, and 20.0%), but adjusted estimates were imprecise, and the highest burden group contained only 5 patients and 1 death. This analysis remains hypothesis-generating.

Comparison with existing evidence

Our findings align with the contemporary CKM framework, which emphasizes that cardiovascular outcomes arise from interacting cardiac, renal, metabolic, and inflammatory domains rather than isolated abnormalities [1, 2]. The AHA PREVENT equations similarly support integrating multidomain information for risk estimation [3].

The strong association between age and mortality is clinically plausible in geriatric populations. Advanced age often reflects cumulative disease burden, frailty, reduced physiological reserve, and heightened vulnerability during acute hospitalization – all central concerns in geriatric cardiology.

BNP was the strongest biomarker signal, consistent with heart failure guidelines establishing natriuretic peptides as central markers of myocardial wall stress and congestion [4–6, 20]. However, given our retrospective, event-limited design, this finding should be viewed as an exploratory risk signal rather than a validated clinical threshold. Because biomarkers were measured during acute decompensation, their association with mortality may partly reflect severity of illness at presentation rather than information incremental to bedside clinical assessment, and the data do not establish that the CKM framework adds prognostic value beyond established markers such as BNP; the framework is used here as an organizing concept for routinely available variables.

BUN and IL-6 showed coherent unadjusted associations but were attenuated in adjusted models. This may reflect limited statistical power, biomarker missingness, overlap between domains, or the dominant risk signal captured by age and BNP. Nevertheless, the biological relevance of renal dysfunction and inflammation in acute cardiovascular deterioration remains supported by contemporary literature [1, 2, 8, 9, 17, 18, 23].

The lack of an independent association with broad binary ECG abnormality should be interpreted cautiously. ECG abnormalities are prognostically important in acute cardiovascular assessment [21, 22], but our composite, although based on cardiologist interpretation, was near-universally present (approximately 91–95% in both groups), so the binary variable had little capacity to discriminate. This reflects the variable’s distribution and construction in this cohort rather than evidence that ECG lacks prognostic importance. In an exploratory description of individual findings, no subtype was robustly associated with mortality, although T-wave inversion showed a nominal association that warrants evaluation in adequately powered studies.

Clinical and scientific implications for geriatric cardiology

These findings provide a clinical rationale for evaluating rather than adopting clinically interpretable, multidomain risk assessment in older hospitalized patients. Age, ECG, BNP, BUN, and IL-6 are familiar to clinicians and, where measured, are routinely available, which would make such an approach comparatively transparent; however, the present data neither demonstrate that this multidomain assessment improves risk stratification nor that it is currently implemented consistently enough to support routine use. This orientation toward transparent, routinely available information is relevant given evidence that real-world management decisions in older hospitalized cardiac patients, such as anticoagulation after in-hospital falls in atrial fibrillation, are frequently shaped by clinical, social, and access-related considerations rather than by formal risk scores alone [26].

However, this model should not be used for clinical decision-making. The most defensible interpretation is that age and BNP are robust risk markers in this dataset, while BUN, IL-6, ECG abnormality, and CKM burden require further evaluation. The CKM burden analysis is conceptually useful, as it examines whether simultaneous abnormalities across domains identify older patients at higher short-term risk – a question highly relevant to geriatric multimorbidity. But because thresholds were data-derived and the highest burden group was very small, this analysis remains hypothesis-generating.

Methodological considerations

Several methodological issues are central to interpretation. First, biomarker missingness was substantial: only 186 of 1,131 patients (16.4%) had complete data. Complete-case analysis is transparent but risks selection bias. Patients included in the complete-case model were older and had higher observed mortality than excluded patients, suggesting the complete-case cohort may not fully represent the broader population. Beyond its statistical implications, the limited availability of a complete CKM biomarker panel indicates that standardized multidomain CKM-related biomarker assessment was not systematically implemented in routine care in this retrospective dataset; IL-6 in particular was measured in fewer than half of patients. Importantly, BNP, BUN, and IL-6 are blood-based measures that are not inherently impractical to obtain, and their incomplete capture reflects prevailing testing patterns rather than any intrinsic barrier to measurement. These observations should therefore not be read as evidence that multidomain CKM-related assessment is already established for routine risk stratification. Instead, they provide a rationale for prospective studies in which currently underused markers such as IL-6 are measured according to a standardized protocol and their incremental prognostic value is rigorously evaluated.

Second, the final model was based on only 17 deaths. Firth penalized logistic regression reduces small-sample bias but cannot overcome limited outcome events. Non-significant associations for sex, ECG abnormality, BUN, and IL-6 should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of no association [13].

Third, discrimination and calibration estimates are internally apparent only. AUCs and calibration metrics were estimated in the same dataset used to fit the model and may be optimistic. Future prediction research requires prespecified modeling, robust missing-data methods, internal and external validation, and calibration assessment before implementation [10, 11, 13, 19, 24].

Strengths and limitations

Strengths include the use of real-world acute-care data, integration of routinely available clinical and biomarker variables in an interpretable CKM framework, transparent cohort derivation, appropriate use of Firth penalized regression, and cautious framing as exploratory risk stratification rather than a deployable prediction tool.

Limitations include single-center retrospective design, exclusion of records with unclear outcome status, substantial biomarker missingness, complete-case selection bias, only 17 deaths in the final model (limiting precision and increasing instability risk), residual confounding and absence of comorbidity indices such as the Charlson Comorbidity Index, vital signs, echocardiography, medications, or other severity markers, ECG findings based on standard clinical interpretation rather than formal research coding, and modeled as a broad binary variable, no prespecified maximum interval between admission and ECG or biomarker measurement, and reliance on internal bootstrap validation without external validation; apparent discrimination was optimistic on internal validation (optimism-corrected C-index 0.79).

Future research

Larger multicenter prospective cohorts with standardized data collection and more complete biomarker measurement are needed. Future studies should prespecify predictors, ECG classification rules, biomarker timing, missing-data handling, and outcome ascertainment. Multiple imputation should be considered, with complete-case sensitivity analyses. Prediction model research should include internal validation, external validation, calibration assessment, and clinical utility analysis before implementation [10, 11, 13, 19, 24]. Further work should evaluate whether granular ECG phenotypes, serial biomarker trajectories, and prespecified CKM domain thresholds improve risk stratification in older acute cardiovascular populations.

Conclusion

In this selected complete-case cohort of older patients hospitalized with heart failure, age and BNP were the most consistent markers of in-hospital mortality, whereas sex, broad ECG major abnormality, BUN, and IL-6 were not significant after adjustment. A complete CKM biomarker panel was available in only a minority of patients, indicating that standardized multidomain CKM-related biomarker assessment was not systematically implemented in routine care in this dataset. The numerically higher apparent discrimination of biomarker-enriched models, the graded mortality pattern across exploratory CKM-burden groups, and the associations of BUN, IL-6, and individual ECG findings were not statistically robust and remain hypothesis-generating, reflecting substantial biomarker missingness, complete-case selection, few mortality events, and the absence of external validation. The feasibility, incremental prognostic value, and clinical utility of broader CKM-related multidomain biomarker assessment therefore remain unproven and require standardized, prospectively assembled, and externally validated studies. These results should not be interpreted as a deployable clinical prediction tool.
