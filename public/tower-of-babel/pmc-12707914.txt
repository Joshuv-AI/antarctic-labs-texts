Cost-Effectiveness Analysis of Fecal Microbiota Transplantation for Patients with Irritable Bowel Syndrome
Authors: Qiran Wei, Mingjun Rui, Yingcheng Wang, Siew C. Ng, Joyce H.S. You
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionStudies have demonstrated clinical benefits of fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) for treatment of irritable bowel syndrome (IBS). This study aimed to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of FMT versus standard drug treatment for patients with moderate-to-severe IBS who failed to respond to first-line therapy from the US payer’s perspective.MethodsTwo 1-year Markov models were developed to examine the outcomes of FMT versus standard drug treatment in patients with constipation-predominant IBS (IBS-C) and diarrhea-predominant IBS (IBS-D). The primary model outcomes included direct medical cost and quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) gained. Model inputs were obtained from literature and public data. Sensitivity analyses were performed to examine the robustness of model results.ResultsIn the base-case analysis, FMT gained higher QALYs (by 0.0159 QALYs for IBS-C and 0.0166 QALYs for IBS-D) with cost savings (USD 7,835 for IBS-C and USD 9,015 for IBS-D) when compared to standard drug treatment. Key influential parameters identified by one-way sensitivity analysis were response probabilities of FMT and first-line treatment, the utility values of therapeutic response and nonresponse, and FMT cost. The probabilities of FMT to be accepted as the preferred strategy at a willingness-to-pay threshold of USD 50,000/QALY were 99.86% and 99.89% for IBS-C and IBS-D, respectively.ConclusionFMT appears to be cost-effective for patients with moderate-to-severe IBS who failed to respond to first-line therapy from the US payer’s perspective.

Introduction

Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is a chronic functional bowel disorder characterized by changes in bowel habits and recurrent abdominal pain. IBS is categorized into four subtypes based upon the predominant type of symptoms: IBS with predominant constipation (IBS-C), IBS with predominant diarrhea (IBS-D), IBS with mixed bowel habits, and unsubtyped IBS [1, 2]. The estimated prevalence of IBS in the US ranges between 4.7% and 6.1% [3–5]. IBS-C accounts for approximately 33.6%, IBS-D for 28.1%, IBS with mixed bowel habits for 33.9%, and unsubtyped IBS for 4.4% [5]. IBS is not a life-threatening disease, yet it contributes to substantial healthcare cost and work productivity loss. The median (interquartile range) total cost for IBS patients was USD 13,288 (5,307–37,071) per patient per year in the USA [6]. Patients with IBS were reported to have significantly higher overall work impairment compared to those without IBS (35.54% versus 25.29% for IBS-C and 20.7% versus 13.2% for IBS-D, respectively) [7, 8]. Patients often receive oral polyethylene glycol for IBS-C and oral loperamide for IBS-D as the first-line treatment [9]. Conventional first-line therapy may be inadequate for treatment of moderate-to-severe IBS symptoms [10, 11]. Among patients with moderate-to-severe IBS who regularly received first-line therapy, less than 30% patients achieved significant clinical improvement in 1 month [12]. Linaclotide and tenapanor for IBS-C, and rifaximin and eluxadoline for IBS-D are recommended as subsequent treatment options for patients who failed first-line treatment [13, 14].

Alterations in the composition or balance of the intestinal microbiota, known as dysbiosis, are associated with various gastrointestinal disorders including IBS [15]. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) is a therapeutic intervention that involves transferring fecal microbiota from a healthy donor to a recipient, aiming to restore microbial diversity [16, 17]. A systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated that FMT delivered via colonoscopy, gastroscopy, nasojejunal tube, or rectal enema significantly improved clinical response (RR = 1.91, 95% CI: 1.26–2.91) without a corresponding increase of adverse events in patients with IBS [18]. Furthermore, clinical findings showed the high response rate with FMT (62%–69%) for different IBS subtypes to sustain in a 3-year follow-up period [19, 20].

Despite the increasing evidence on clinical benefits and safety of FMT for IBS treatment, the high cost per FMT course poses a barrier to healthcare payer to adopt it in routine practice. In addition, the cost-effectiveness of FMT for moderate-to-severe IBS is yet to be examined. To assist the healthcare payer in the decision-making process on FMT for IBS treatment, this study aimed to assess the clinical and economic outcomes of FMT versus standard drug treatment for patients with moderate-to-severe IBS who failed to respond to first-line therapy from the perspective of US payer.

Methods

Model Design

Two Markov models (Fig. 1a, b) were developed to simulate the clinical and health economic outcomes in two hypothetical cohorts of adult patients with moderate-to-severe IBS-C and IBS-D who failed to respond to first-line therapy. Two treatment strategies were evaluated: (1) FMT and (2) standard (second-line) drug treatment. The model time horizon was 1 year with monthly cycle. The primary model outcomes included direct medical cost and quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs). Moderate-to-severe IBS was defined as an IBS Severity Scoring System (IBS-SSS) score >175, and without symptoms associated with gastrointestinal or other infections [12].

Simplified Markov models of FMT and standard drug treatment for IBS-C (a) and IBS-D (b). AEs included FMT-related diarrhea in IBS-C, FMT-related constipation in IBS-D, linaclotide-related diarrhea, tenapanor-related diarrhea, rifaximin-related upper respiratory tract infection, and eluxadoline-related pancreatitis. IBS-C, constipation-predominant irritable bowel syndrome; IBS-D, diarrhea-predominant irritable bowel syndrome; FMT, fecal microbiota transplantation; AE, adverse event.

In the FMT group, all patients received FMT 60 g via gastroscopy [12]. In the standard drug treatment group, patients received predominant symptom-specific second-line treatment: Linaclotide (290 µg orally once daily for 4 weeks) for IBS-C, and rifaximin (550 mg orally three times daily for 14 days) for IBS-D [9, 21].

In both treatment groups, patients were assessed for treatment response to FMT or standard therapy. Treatment response adopted treatment responder definition: Reported adequate relief of abdominal pain and discomfort, or reported satisfactory relief of IBS symptoms [22]. In the FMT group, patients responded to FMT were further monitored for relapse. Second-line treatment (4-week linaclotide for constipation and 2-week rifaximin for diarrhea) were started for patients who were non-responsive to FMT or when relapse occurred. Patients who achieved remission with second-line treatment were monitored for potential relapse, and the second-line drug treatment was resumed when relapse occurred. Non-responders to second-line drug treatment were advanced to third-line treatment (oral tenapanor 50 mg twice daily for constipation and oral eluxadoline 100 mg twice daily for diarrhea) for the remaining time of model.

In the standard drug treatment group, the patients who achieved remission with second-line treatment were monitored for potential relapse, and the same second-line drug treatment was resumed if the IBS relapsed. Non-responders to second-line drug treatment were advanced to third-line treatment, as described in the FMT group. In the FMT group and standard treatment group, patients might experience treatment-related adverse events and receive management for the adverse events.

A European randomized clinical trial on patients with moderate-to-severe IBS (n = 165) reported both short-term response rate [12] and long-term recurrence rate [19] of FMT. The monthly probabilities of response to FMT (0.528 and 0.555 for IBS-C and IBS-D, respectively) and FMT-related adverse events (FMT-related diarrhea in IBS-C; FMT-related constipation in IBS-D) were derived from the 1-month FMT response rates [12]. The monthly probabilities of relapse post-FMT treatment (0.013 for IBS-C, 0.010 for IBS-D) were calculated from the 1-year follow-up data [19].

The response probability of linaclotide (0.264) was calculated from response rate of linaclotide (290 μg daily) showed in a clinical trial (n = 800) for moderate-to-severe IBS-C patients in the USA [24]. The probabilities of relapse post-linaclotide treatment (0.260) and linaclotide-related diarrhea were estimated from the findings of linaclotide effectiveness in IBS-C patients (n = 800) in a US study [24]. The response and adverse event (diarrhea) probabilities of tenapanor (0.281) were calculated from the response rate and adverse event rate of tenapanor (50 mg twice daily) in IBS-C patients reported by a US clinical trial (n = 606) [26]. The response and adverse event (upper respiratory tract infection) probabilities for rifaximin (0.334) was calculated from the combined response rate and adverse event rate of two clinical trials (n = 1,260) of rifaximin (550 mg three times daily for 2 weeks) in IBS-D patients [25]. The probability of relapse post-rifaximin therapy (0.376) was calculated from the symptom relapse rate following rifaximin treatment reported by a multi-country study in IBS-D patients (n = 1,074) [28]. The response probability for eluxadoline (0.309) was calculated from the response rate of eluxadoline (100 mg twice daily) in IBS-D patients in a multi-country clinical trial (n = 1,146) [27]. The eluxadoline-related adverse event (pancreatitis) probability was estimated from the adverse event rate of eluxadoline (100 mg twice daily) in IBS-D patients reported by three clinical trials (n = 2,814) [29].

Utility Inputs

The expected QALYs gained in each treatment group were estimated from the health state-specific utility value and cumulative time spent in each health state. Health utility values for treatment response and nonresponse/relapse states were retrieved from data reported by the PROOF observational cohort study [30]. In the PROOF cohort, the health utility value was 0.78 in IBS patients with relief of symptoms and 0.73 for those without relief of symptoms. There were no significant differences in utilities among IBS-C and IBS-D subgroups (p = 0.55), and the utility values of symptom relief and no symptom relief were, therefore, adopted for response and nonresponse/relapse of IBS-C and IBS-D. AE-specific disutilities were applied to account for the impact of treatment-related AE on expected QALYs, and the disutility estimates were derived from published quality-of-life studies [30–32]. The model time horizon was 1 year, and discounting was, therefore, not performed on the QALY gained.

Cost Inputs

The cost analysis considered the direct medical costs of FMT, medications, outpatient care, and AE management. The cost of FMT included the costs of FMT preparation and instillation, and donor and recipient testing, and were listed by the 2025 Hospital Outpatient Prospective Payment (Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services [CMS]) [33] and 2025 CMS Clinical Laboratory Fee Schedule [34]. The medication costs were approximated from National Average Drug Acquisition Cost Database [36]. The cost of outpatient clinic visit was obtained from 2025 CMS Physician Fee Schedule [35]. The frequency of outpatient consultations per year for responders and non-responders were retrieved from the data reported by a cost-effectiveness analysis on IBS management in the USA [41]. The management costs of AEs (per episode) were derived from published cost analyses [37–40]. All costs were adjusted to year 2025 USD using the Medical Care Consumer Price Index provided by the US Bureau of Labor Statistics [42]. The model time horizon was 1 year, and discounting was, therefore, not performed in the cost analysis.

Cost-Effectiveness Analysis and Sensitivity Analysis

All analyses were conducted using TreeAge Pro Healthcare 2025 (TreeAge Software, USA). If a treatment strategy gained higher QALYs when compared to an alternative, the incremental net monetary benefit (INMB) was calculated using the formula:INMB=(QALYgain×WTP)−incrementalcostwhere willingness-to-pay (WTP) adopted a threshold of USD 50,000 per QALY.

A strategy was accepted as cost-effective if the INMB was above zero, indicating that the monetary benefits of the strategy outweighed its incremental cost [43]. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the model outputs. One-way sensitivity analysis was performed by varying each model parameter across the range for sensitivity analysis (Table 1) to identify influential variables and threshold values, if any. The ranges for sensitivity analysis were high/low values, 95% confidence interval, or (when both were not available) variation of base-case value by ±20%. The variation of INMB against five most influential model inputs was presented in a tornado diagram. Probabilistic sensitivity analysis was conducted with Monte Carlo simulation by spontaneously drawing all model inputs from the assigned distribution. Gamma distributions were applied for cost inputs and beta distributions for utilities and probabilities. The total cost and QALYs were recalculated for each treatment group over 10,000 iterations, and results were presented in the scatterplots and cost-effectiveness acceptability curves.

Probabilistic sensitivity analysis was conducted by 10,000 Monte Carlo simulations of the cost and QALYs gained in each treatment group for IBS-C and IBS-D. Discussion

This is the first study to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of FMT for patients with moderate-to-severe IBS. In the base-case analysis, FMT was a cost-effective strategy when compared to standard treatment, with higher QALYs gained and cost saving for both IBS-C and IBS-D. The robustness of base-case findings was supported by the one-way and probabilistic sensitivity analyses. The one-way sensitivity analysis identified that the response probabilities of FMT and second-line therapy, the utility values of therapeutic response and nonresponse, and FMT cost were the most influential factors. Among these influential parameters, the response probability of FMT demonstrated the highest impact on the variation of INMBs of FMT for both IBS-C and IBS-D. A higher response probability of FMT reduced usage of second-line/third-line IBS treatments, thus lowered the total treatment cost and increased the QALYs gained, thereby increasing the INMBs of FMT. The probabilistic sensitivity analysis also indicated the probability of >99% for FMT to be the cost-effective strategy at WTP USD 50,000/QALY.

The major cost-saving drivers of FMT in IBS management were the lower FMT cost (compared to those of most second/third-line drugs), higher response and lower relapse probabilities. The cost of FMT was lower than 2-week cost of rifaximin and monthly cost of tenapanor, and comparable to monthly cost of eluxadoline. Microbiota dysbiosis is considered a significant factor in the pathogenesis of IBS [44]. By restoring microbial diversity and balance, FMT alleviate symptoms more effectively with sustain therapeutic effects [12, 19].

Tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) are also considered alternative pharmacological options of IBS. A systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the efficacy of antidepressants for IBS treatment and found that the data were characterized by substantial methodological limitations, including uncertainty regarding allocation concealment and blinding, as well as heterogeneity in study populations and dosing regimens [45]. The clinical evidence of SSRIs for IBS was also reviewed, and the findings indicated that SSRIs had no significant benefits compared with placebo in symptom relief (RR = 0.74; 95% CI: 0.52–1.06), and adequate abdominal pain response (RR = 0.63; 95% CI: 0.35–1.12) [46]. The certainty of evidence on efficacy of TCAs and SSRI is rated as low in the current guideline recommendation [13, 14], and TCAs and SSRIs were, therefore, not considered in the present models for adult patients with moderate-to-severe IBS-C and IBS-D.

Besides FMT and drug treatment, the potential benefits of dietary, psychological therapy, and other microbial therapy are also demonstrated in managing IBS symptoms [9]. A systematic review and meta-analysis (n = 5,710 IBS patients) showed that, comparing to a sham diet, a low-fermentable oligosaccharide, disaccharide, monosaccharide, and polyols (low-FODMAP) diet (RR = 3.22; 95% CI: 1.70–6.26) and low-FODMAP diet combined with probiotics (RR = 17.79; 95% CI: 3.27–112.54) significantly alleviated IBS symptoms [47]. Another systematic review and meta-analysis (with 48 trials) reported that 51.9% of 1,232 patients in the psychological therapy group did not achieve IBS symptom relief, versus 76.1% of 1,102 patients in the placebo group (RR = 0.68; 95% CI: 0.61–0.76). The evidence for psychotherapy in IBS yet remains limited that clinical trials did not control for drug use, and direct comparisons with drug treatment are lacking. The application of psychotherapy in clinical trials was determined by patient preference, cost, and accessibility, with no evidence favoring one type over another [48]. Previous cost-effectiveness analyses have reported that dietary therapy and psychological therapy gained higher QALYs and saved cost when compared to linaclotide for IBS-C [49], and rifaximin and eluxadoline for IBS-D [50]. Similar to our study, those evaluations identified the intervention cost, the utility of response, and response probability of intervention as key drivers of cost-effectiveness results. These results implied the importance of reducing intervention cost or enhancing response probability to achieve acceptable cost-effectiveness of a treatment intervention. Future research on comparing FMT with other non-drug strategies such as dietary therapy, behavioral interventions, and probiotics are warranted to provide comprehensive insights to the health economic evidence of IBS management.

Outcomes of several delivery routes for FMT have been evaluated in IBS patients, including colonoscopy, gastroscopy, nasojejunal tube, enema, and oral capsules. A systematic review and meta-analysis (n = 615 IBS patients) revealed that endoscopy, nasojejunal tube, and rectal enema were associated with significant improvement in clinical response, IBS-SSS and quality-of-life when compared to placebo. FMT delivered via oral capsules did not show statistically significant improvement in the IBS outcomes [18]. The dose of FMT is also a factor affecting treatment outcomes. A clinical trial of FMT 50 g delivered via upper endoscopy did not result in significant reduction in IBS-SSS scores [51]. Another clinical trial demonstrated that a higher dose of FMT enhanced response rate in IBS treatment [52]. Also, patient acceptability remains a critical factor influencing the adoption of FMT treatment. Surveys indicated that, while patients were willing to consider FMT, concerns about the safety, efficacy, potential cost, and the discomfort of stool transplantation might act as barriers [53, 54]. Future research and cost-effectiveness analyses focusing on optimization of FMT strategies for IBS management, including refining delivery methods, determining appropriate dose, and addressing patient concerns to enhance acceptability, are highly warranted.

In addition to IBS, FMT has been investigated for its cost-effectiveness in several other conditions. A systematic review reported that FMT was cost-effective compared with antibiotic therapy for recurrent Clostridium difficile infection (CDI), regardless of delivery method, from societal, healthcare system, or payer perspectives [55]. A model-based study of FMT in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) found that FMT administered 3 times per course was cost saving, reducing costs (by USD 3,868) and leading to higher QALY (by 0.068 QALYs) [56]. A cohort trial-based study for patients with urinary tract infection caused by multidrug-resistant organisms indicated FMT was cost saving compared with standard pre-FMT management, with significantly reductions in recurrent infections (from 4 to 0), hospitalization days (from 15 to 4), health contacts (from 28 to 11), and monthly costs following FMT (from USD 6,687 to USD 1,574) [57]. Previous clinical trials have reported that FMT is a promising therapeutic option across gastrointestinal and non-gastrointestinal disorders, such as CDI [58, 59], IBD [60, 61], IBS [12], obesity and metabolic syndrome [62, 63], and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease [64]. Future research is warranted to evaluate the cost-effectiveness FMT in other potential indications.

This study has several limitations. The model did not incorporate mortality, given that IBS is a non-fatal condition. The model simplified the management of IBS and might not fully capture the clinical complexity of IBS treatment. The model did not consider societal cost associated with IBS. The inclusion of indirect cost, such as productivity loss due to IBS symptoms, may amplify the cost savings associated with FMT. The pathophysiology of IBS is multifactorial, and management typically requires diverse and ongoing long-term treatment [9]. The present models with 1-year timeframe, therefore, did not capture the long-term impact of FMT in IBS. Drug treatment strategies and medical costs vary substantially across countries, which may influence the generalizability of our findings to other regions. Researchers could adapt the present models using region-specific model inputs to generate local cost-effectiveness evidence of FMT for IBS in other regions.

Conclusion

FMT appears to be cost-effective for patients with moderate-to-severe IBS-C and IBS-D from the US payer’s perspective. The effectiveness of FMT and second-line IBS drug treatments, utility values of therapeutic response and nonresponse, and the FMT cost were the key influential factors on the cost-effectiveness of FMT.
