Fortifying healthcare: exploring organizational resilience in Israeli healthcare organizations - a quasi-longitudinal study
Authors: Tamir Velner, Zohar Rubinstein, Bruria Adini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealth Organizations (HOs) worldwide are vital to any nation’s capacity to withstand crises. The heightened focus on organizational resilience (OR) can be largely attributed to the global COVID-19 pandemic. The terror attack on Israel on October 7, 2023, underscored the essential nature of resilience in healthcare settings. By emphasizing OR’s components, HOs can enhance their ability to anticipate, respond to, and recover from disruptions. This research hypothesized that the terror attack would serve as a catalyst for enhancing organizational resilience within healthcare organizations. This study aims to compare levels of organizational resilience among healthcare organizations before and after a mega-mass casualty incident and to identify the key predictors of resilience across various healthcare settings.MethodsA quasi-longitudinal study. Three HOs were assessed pre-7.10.23 and six HOs were assessed following this incident, in the midst of an ongoing conflict (two hospitals, two district health bureaus, and two Emergency medical services regions). An organizational resilience assessment tool, developed through literature review, expert validation, pilot testing, and a Delphi process to rank the importance of each category, included 29 items across seven categories. Data were collected anonymously via an online survey and results were analyzed both separately and in aggregate to assess resilience levels.ResultsA total of 1,035 participants completed the survey across three healthcare organization types: general hospitals (N = 345), district health bureaus (N = 300), and emergency medical service regions (N = 390). Organizational resilience (OR) was measured separately for each organization type at two timepoints: before and after the October 7, 2023 events. No statistically significant changes in OR were observed post-event. Multiple linear regression identified six predictors that explained 81.4% of the variance in OR (Adjusted R² = 0.814), with transformational leadership emerging as the strongest predictor.ConclusionsThe study stresses the importance of improving organizational resilience in healthcare organizations facing extreme events. Managers in healthcare organizations should prioritize investment in transformational leadership, technological advancement, and employee well-being to enhance organizational resilience. Due to the differing levels of organizational resilience across disciplines within healthcare organizations, it is recommended to concentrate efforts and resources on sectors with lower resilience levels. This approach will enhance targeted interventions, save resources, and establish an effective timeline for strengthening organizational resilience. Background

Organizational resilience (OR), particularly in healthcare settings, has been recognized in recent years, especially in the wake of the COVID-19 pandemic, as an important component of the survivability of services during crises [1]. Healthcare systems worldwide have realized the critical need for proactive crisis preparedness and the development of robust organizational resilience strategies [2]. OR is viewed as a crucial factor in understanding and enhancing patient safety and quality of care [3]. By ensuring OR, organizations can enhance their ability to anticipate, respond to, and recover from disruptions in challenging contexts.

The International Organization for Standardization (2017) characterizes organizational resilience as an organization’s capacity to absorb and adapt to a changing environment, allowing it to achieve its goals, survive, and prosper. Most researchers define organizational resilience as the ability to address internal and external changes, risks, or disturbances [4–6]. Key characteristics and components of a resilient organization encompass developing situational awareness, managing vulnerabilities, allocating resources, improvisation, anticipating events, continuous learning, collaboration, individual resilience, flexibility, and redundancy [7, 8].

The scientific literature emphasizes several key factors that influence organizational resilience. The organizational ethos that is reflected through managers’ perceptions of risks, is described as “concern or attention to crisis preparations” [9]. Organizational culture can either mitigate or exacerbate organizational disasters, potentially even initiating crisis events [10]. Cultivating an organizational culture of resilience establishes the groundwork for effective crisis recovery, future success, and sustainability [11]. Leadership and human capital are identified as crucial pillars in developing organizational resilience.

Strong leadership that fosters cohesive, interdependent teams has been identified as a crucial element in enhancing organizational resilience. Resilient leadership and organizational culture significantly contribute to building organizational resilience [12]. Situational awareness, which involves understanding what is happening within the organization, is a hallmark of strong leadership. A lack of situational awareness can lead to an increased potential for human error and malfunctions [13]. Learning capacity is also crucial; health organizations, for example, can enhance their resilience against future disruptions by learning from disaster response efforts and adjusting their work practices accordingly [14].

Neiman (2019) argued that learning serves as a “prerequisite for resilience” [15]. Researchers have pointed out the complex and multidimensional nature of organizational resilience, emphasizing its connection to an organization’s capability to learn and adapt [16], where learning stands as an essential and critical element [17]. Moreover, the ability to learn is positively linked to the development and maintenance of organizational resilience [18]. Adaptability plays a crucial role; studies on stress and resilience have consistently found that higher levels of control in stressful situations enhance individuals’ ability to cope [19]. The performance of an organization, i.e. its potential reactions to stress arising from unexpected and threatening situations, may include changes in concentration, irritability, anxiety, insomnia, decreased productivity, and conflicts among personnel.

The performance of an organization is influenced by various factors, including team support, monitoring stressful situations, self-care, and taking regular breaks to connect with other team members [20]. When evaluating the factors contributing to the success of any organization, it is crucial to remember that an organization is made up of employees whose productivity is essential for the organization’s achievement and survival [21].

Many organizations are frequently confronted with pressures that threaten their survival [22, 23]. This challenging environment compels organizations to be flexible, adaptive, and innovative to not only survive but also thrive [24]. Evaluating and enhancing organizational resilience enables organizations to better understand the shifting landscape and improve their capacity to respond to emerging threats. Additionally, under uncertain and perilous conditions, resilience is crucial for organizations to endure, grow, and remain competitive [25].

Organizational resilience in healthcare systems is shaped by multiple interrelated factors, among which transformational leadership, technological advancement, and employee well-being play a central role in fostering adaptive capacity and sustaining performance during crises. Transformational leadership emphasizes fostering growth, development, and success among individuals within the organization by cultivating a positive and empowering work environment. Leaders who engage proactively with team members to encourage well-being and resilience are better positioned to enhance the adaptive capacity of their organizations. Previous studies have shown that proactive leadership, particularly when promoting employee well-being in contexts of sustained stress, significantly contributes to strengthening organizational resilience [26].

Technological advancement refers to the continuous development, improvement, and integration of technologies and their applications within organizations. This process involves the creation and implementation of tools, methods, products, and systems that enhance efficiency, productivity, and outcomes across multiple domains. Empirical research has demonstrated that organizations that continuously adopt innovative technologies are better equipped to cope with crises and uncertainties [27]. Although resilience and innovation are distinct concepts, they can be mutually reinforcing. In turbulent environments, organizations often increase their innovation efforts to maximize returns on investment, thereby strengthening their resilience [28, 29]. The role of technology in organizational resilience is multifaceted: while technological advancement can improve efficiency, adaptability, and crisis response [30, 31], it may also impose additional administrative burden and stress on healthcare staff, potentially undermining resilience [32–34].

Employee well-being encompasses organizational initiatives designed to ensure the health, satisfaction, and overall welfare of the workforce. This includes fostering a supportive work environment that prioritizes employees’ physical, psychological, and emotional needs. Evidence highlights the strong relationship between employee well-being and organizational resilience, underscoring the role of well-being in improving performance, adaptability, and organizational stability in the face of crises [35–38]. Thus, prioritizing well-being contributes not only to individual employee outcomes but also to the broader organizational capacity to endure and adapt during periods of disruption.

The Israeli healthcare system and organizational challenges during COVID-19 and the iron swords war

The Israeli healthcare system operates under a centralized structure led by the Ministry of Health, which supervises a diverse range of organizations. General hospitals provide acute and specialized care and faced unprecedented patient loads and operational stressors during the COVID-19 pandemic [39]. Emergency Medical Services (EMS) play a pivotal role in pre-hospital care and crisis response, rapidly expanding their capacity during the pandemic through measures such as drive-through testing and emergency transport [40, 42]. District Health Bureaus are responsible for managing public health, disease surveillance, and community-based prevention, and were critical in implementing vaccination programs and coordinating regional responses during crises [41]. Both during COVID-19 and the Iron Swords war, these organizations encountered overlapping challenges that tested their resilience: hospitals struggled with resource shortages and surges in patient demand [39], EMS organizations faced intense operational pressures and infection risks [40, 42], and District Health Bureaus managed large-scale vaccination rollouts and containment strategies amid staff shortages and heightened public anxiety [41]. These experiences exposed structural vulnerabilities but also highlighted adaptive capacities across organizational types.

On October 7, 2023, Hamas launched a surprise attack against Israel, leading to over 1,200 fatalities and more than 9,000 injuries. The attack on Israel highlighted the critical importance of enhancing healthcare resilience during large-scale emergencies. The response to this mega mass casualty event (MMCE) demonstrated several key aspects of organizational resilience in emergency medical services: Centralized management, streamlined communication, swift resource mobilization and adaptive strategies [42].

Primarily, resilience empowers organizations to swiftly act and devise alternatives to manage risks while identifying potential advantages that may emerge from uncertain conditions [43, 44]. Enhancing organizational resilience boosts the ability to handle crises, increases productivity, and aids in maintaining the continuous operation of a healthcare organization [45]. Organizational resilience is crucial not only for the survival and functioning of the organization itself but also for the broader community, especially during times of distress, given that communities depend on the services provided by these organizations [46].

The study aims to compare organizational resilience in healthcare organizations before and after a mega-mass casualty event and to identify predictors of organizational resilience across diverse healthcare entities. The study’s hypothesis was that the MMCE of October 7, 2023, would enhance the level of organizational resilience within healthcare organizations.

Methods

Study design

A quasi-longitudinal study was conducted. One hospital, one district health bureau, and one region of the national EMS, were assessed both pre- (during 2022) and post- the October 7th 2023 MMCE, while three similar healthcare organizations (an additional hospital, district health bureau, and region of the national EMS) from the same geographical area were assessed solely in the post-MMCE measurement. The second measurement was not conducted exclusively in the same three institutions as the first round because the October 7th MMCE created extraordinary operational and ethical constraints. The original three organizations were re-assessed to enable quasi-longitudinal comparisons; however, to broaden the scope of the study and enhance the robustness of post-event analyses, we also included three additional, comparable organizations from the same geographical area. This approach allowed us to balance feasibility in the wake of the crisis with the need for meaningful data collection. While this design does not provide a fully longitudinal dataset, it offers valuable quasi-longitudinal insights while also expanding the generalizability of post-event findings.

As a result, comparisons of findings before and after the MMCE were made only for the initial three organizations, while the post- MMCE analysis to identify predictive factors was conducted across all six organizations. All six healthcare organizations included in the study are geographically located in southern Israel, in close proximity to the Gaza Strip. Data collection was implemented by sending the medical personnel in the six participating health organizations a URL link that they could access through their computer or smartphone. Access to potential participants was coordinated through the administrations of the respective healthcare organizations, which disseminated the questionnaire links internally via institutional communication platforms, such as email systems and secure organizational WhatsApp groups. The survey was distributed digitally and completed anonymously by participants after providing informed consent. No identifying information was collected at any stage of the process, and all data were stored securely in accordance with ethical approval protocols.

Study tools

A series of structured stages were implemented to develop and validate the assessment tool for evaluating organizational resilience in healthcare settings. A comprehensive review of scientific and gray literature led to the definition of organizational resilience, tailored to both general and healthcare-specific contexts. Based on the findings of the literature review, 29 items were defined, representing key aspects of organizational resilience, which were grouped into seven categories (e.g., organizational culture, adaptability). Examples of items include: “Our organization has embraced a culture that values learning from mistakes and deriving lessons for ongoing improvement” (organizational culture) and “The internal communication within the organization is strong, efficient, and effective” (adaptability). Content validity was established by 12 academic and professional experts. The tool was refined through pilot testing in a healthcare facility that was not later included in the study itself. Using a modified Delphi process with 19 expert participants and a 75% consensus threshold, the categories were ranked by their relative importance to organizational resilience. The finalized tool was subsequently implemented across the three types of healthcare organizations. The Organizational Resilience assessment tool is presented in Table A5.

Three validated instruments were employed to assess the independent variables—transformational leadership, technological advancement, and employee well-being. All scales used a five-point Likert-type format ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”). Transformational leadership was measured using the Leadership Quotient (LQ) Model, developed by Pasternack et al. [47]. This nine-item instrument captures leadership dimensions, including vision, empowerment, and alignment with organizational values. Technological advancement was assessed using a validated scale developed by Chen and Huang [48], which consists of seven items reflecting managerial and technical innovation. The instrument has demonstrated strong validity and reliability in prior organizational studies [49]. Employee well-being was measured using the Workplace Well-Being Scale, developed by Pradhan and Hati [50], which comprises nine items evaluating perceptions of job security, organizational support, professional growth, and workplace climate. All instruments were originally developed in English and underwent forward–backward translation into Hebrew in accordance with Brislin’s standardized cross-cultural adaptation procedures (51) to ensure conceptual and linguistic equivalence. Internal consistency coefficients (Cronbach’s α) for all scales exceeded 0.80, indicating satisfactory reliability. A detailed summary of item distribution and psychometric properties is provided in Table A3.

Study population and sampling

The study population consisted of both medical and administrative teams employed in the three types of healthcare facilities that were included in the study – hospitals, EMS, and district health bureaus. In the hospitals, the population consisted of physicians, nurses, and administrators; in the EMS, it included paramedics, medics, and ambulance drivers; while in the district health bureaus, the population consisted of nurses, environmental health, and food control personnel. We employed a convenience sampling approach to disseminate the organizational resilience assessment tool among personnel from the three types of healthcare facilities. Although the data collection was not stratified by profession, efforts were made to ensure a diverse representation across departments and professional roles. For that purpose, participants were asked to indicate their role within the organization, enabling descriptive and inferential analyses of inter-professional differences.

The assessment tool was distributed according to the calculated sample size requirements, except in the relatively small health bureau, where the tool was disseminated to all employees. G*Power software was used to calculate the minimum sample size for effective statistical power, employing a multivariate linear regression test with 10 independent variables. The parameters used for the calculation were an alpha size of 0.05 (type 1 error), a power of 0.95 (type 2 error), and an effect size of 0.15. The minimum sample size determined for each organization was 166 participants.

Data analysis

The findings were analyzed using SPSS software (version 28) to identify correlations and differences within and between the participating organizations. The findings were compared between the health organizations as well as analyzed in an aggregated mode to identify elements that may impact the organizational resilience in the overall healthcare system. The levels of organizational resilience in the three initial organizations pre- and post-MMCE were compared using a two-way ANOVA test. The identification of the factors that explain organizational resilience was done by using a multivariate linear regression test. The results were considered significant if p < 0.05.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

By securing written consent from the participants and obtaining approvals from TEL AVIV University Ethics Committee (# 0004464-7 from 26/03/2024), the researchers demonstrated adherence to ethical principles and established the necessary authorizations to proceed with the study in a responsible and transparent manner. We confirm that our study adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, and all procedures were conducted in accordance with its ethical guidelines.

Results

A total of 1,035 participants completed the Organizational Resilience Questionnaire across six healthcare organizations: general hospitals (N = 345), district health bureaus (N = 300), and emergency medical services (EMS) regions (N = 390). The demographic characteristics of the study population are presented in Table A1.

Reliability of the study tools

The reliability of the Organizational Resilience Questionnaire was assessed across all organizations using Cronbach’s alpha, demonstrating excellent internal consistency (α = 0.97). The two categories with the highest reliability were situational awareness (α = 0.90) and adaptation capability (α = 0.91), together comprising 14 of the 29 items. In addition, the three independent variables—transformational leadership (α = 0.93), technological advancement (α = 0.94), and employee well-being (α = 0.87)—also demonstrated very high reliability (Table A3).

Comparison of organizational resilience levels across three healthcare organizations before and after the MMCE (October 7th)

A comparison of mean organizational resilience levels before (May 2022) and after (January 2024) the MMCE, across the three types of healthcare organizations, revealed consistent patterns (Table A2). EMS reported the highest average levels in both time points (T0: M = 3.91, SD = 0.79; T1: M = 3.86, SD = 0.73), while the district health bureau reported the lowest (T0: M = 3.73, SD = 0.60; T1: M = 3.66, SD = 0.57). The general hospital showed relatively stable scores across both measurements (T0: M = 3.79, SD = 0.67; T1: M = 3.81, SD = 0.63). At the overall system level, resilience scores were also stable (T0: M = 3.82, SD = 0.70; T1: M = 3.80, SD = 0.66), as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Mean levels of organizational resilience across six healthcare organizations following the October 7th MMCE

Following the events of October 7, 2023, the second measurement was conducted across all six organizations. Results showed that the two general hospitals had nearly identical levels of resilience (3.82 and 3.81; p > 0.05). In contrast, significant differences (p < 0.05) were observed between the two EMS regions: one region demonstrated relatively high resilience (3.86), while the other recorded the lowest level among all organizations (3.55). Similarly, resilience levels differed significantly between the two district health bureaus (3.82 vs. 3.66; p < 0.05). These findings are illustrated in Fig. 2.

Professional roles

Analysis by professional role in the second measurement revealed consistent differences across organizations. Among EMS personnel, paramedics reported the lowest levels of resilience (3.51 and 2.95), whereas management and volunteers reported the highest (4.12 and 4.37, respectively). Within the hospitals, physicians demonstrated the lowest resilience scores (3.5 and 3.6), while management and administrative staff reported the highest level (4.3 and 3.9, respectively). In district health bureaus, management personnel consistently reported the highest resilience (3.9 and 4.2). All differences across professional roles were statistically significant (p < 0.05).

Predictors of organizational resilience

The multivariable linear regression model (Table A4) revealed that transformational leadership, employee well-being, technological advancement, age, religiosity (traditional), and organization type (hospital) significantly predicted organizational resilience. Among these, transformational leadership (β = 0.473, p < 0.001) was the strongest predictor, followed by employee well-being (β = 0.245, p < 0.001) and technological advancement (β = 0.173, p < 0.001). The regression model was highly significant, F(19, 1008) = 237.689, p < 0.001, explaining approximately 82% of the variance in organizational resilience (R² = 0.818). Each factor was positively associated with resilience, indicating that higher levels of these variables were linked to greater organizational resilience. When examined separately by organization type, the predictors accounted for 80% of the variance in hospitals, 84% in EMS, and 81% in district health bureaus.

Discussion

Building resilience offers dual benefits: it helps individuals, communities, and organizations to effectively manage disruptions and enhance their existing systems. Additionally, it promotes the formation of new relationships, inspires new projects, and opens unforeseen opportunities. This set of benefits is referred to as the resilience dividend [52]. Strengthening the organizational resilience of health systems can reduce their vulnerability to crises. This is achieved by ensuring they are better prepared, respond effectively, and maintain or minimally disrupt the delivery of essential healthcare services [53].

The research hypothesis proposed that the events of October 7, 2023, would enhance the level of organizational resilience within healthcare organizations. A key factor supporting this notion is their role as catalysts for change; disasters can act as crucibles for organizations, often resulting in a transformed or redefined sense of identity. Such profound experiences can trigger substantial shifts in how organizations conceptualize and implement resilience strategies. The intense pressure and challenges posed by these catastrophes may compel organizations to reevaluate their existing approaches, leading to innovative and more robust resilience frameworks [54].

An argument explaining why the events of October 7, 2023 did not affect the level of organizational resilience of the three health organizations in southern Israel, could be that these organizations consistently operate in a state of emergency preparedness and have learned to swiftly transition from routine to a crisis mode. This ongoing readiness and adaptability likely contribute to their high level of organizational resilience [55]. Health systems that regularly face emergencies develop what is known as “routine resilience,” which enables them to effectively respond to and recover from shocks. Such organizations likely have well-established emergency protocols, trained staff, and flexible systems that allow them to quickly adapt to changing circumstances. Their experience in dealing with frequent emergencies may have helped them build robust governance structures, clear leadership roles, and effective coordination mechanisms, all of which are crucial for maintaining resilience during crises [56].

While the overall resilience mean scores across organizations were relatively high (ranging between 3.7 and 3.9 on a 5-point scale), none exceeded the threshold of 4.0. We interpret scores above 4.0 as indicative of very high resilience, whereas the observed values represent a high but not extreme level. This distinction is important, as it indicates that sufficient variance remains to avoid a ceiling effect and ensures that meaningful differentiation among respondents is still possible.

The absence of clustering near the maximum value further suggests that the measurement tool maintained adequate sensitivity at the upper end of the scale, thereby avoiding the limitations typically associated with ceiling effects. A ceiling effect occurs when a large proportion of respondents achieve the highest possible score, thereby constraining variability and limiting interpretability. Previous psychometric evaluations of resilience instruments, such as the CD-RISC, have documented ceiling effects when responses cluster toward the maximum, reducing the tool’s ability to distinguish between high levels of resilience [57, 58]. In contrast, the present findings demonstrate sufficient dispersion below the maximum, supporting the responsiveness and discriminatory capacity of the current tool across the observed range.

The multivariate linear regression analysis identified three factors—transformational leadership, technological advancement, and employee well-being—that together explain over 81% of organizational resilience. Authentic leadership style emerged as a focal point in discussions of organizational resilience, being identified as one of the primary contributors to an organization’s ability to withstand and adapt to challenges [59, 60]. Youssef and Luthans (2005) posited that resilient managers, acting as authentic leaders, serve as exemplary role models for their employees [61]. These leaders’ optimistic outlook and self-efficacy enable them to guide their staff in bouncing back from initial setbacks. Over time, this resilient attitude permeates throughout the organization, with employees gradually developing their own resilience. The resilience exhibited by leaders acts as a contagious strength within the organization, gradually spreading across all levels. When team members are consistently exposed to a resilient leader over a significant period, they begin to internalize and adopt a similar resilient mindset and behavioral framework. Leadership plays a pivotal role in achieving organizational resilience. It involves the ability to guide, motivate, and unite individuals toward a shared goal [62].

In the current era, technological progress is transforming various aspects of society, with healthcare being at the epicenter of this revolution. Modern healthcare institutions face a multitude of challenges, ranging from addressing public health crises to incorporating cutting-edge digital solutions. Amidst this complex environment, organizational resilience emerges as the cornerstone of success for healthcare entities. This resilience manifests as their remarkable capacity to not only endure but also flourish in the face of adversity [30]. The study highlights technological advancement as both a predictor of organizational resilience and a potential stressor. Although technology supports performance and crisis response [32], it may also increase.

workload and reduce staff well-being [63]. This dual impact underscores the need to manage technological integration carefully to ensure it strengthens rather than undermines resilience.

The human element is crucial when evaluating organizational success factors. As Schaufeli, Ouweneel, and Le Blanc (2013) pointed out, organizations are fundamentally composed of individuals, and their productivity is essential for the organization’s success and long-term viability [21]. Given that work and family are widely recognized as the two most significant roles in an individual’s life, it logically follows that difficulties in balancing these responsibilities and meeting their frequent conflicting demands could potentially result in overall life dissatisfaction. The challenge of juggling professional obligations with familial commitments, when not managed effectively, may lead to decreased satisfaction across various aspects of one’s life [64]. Research indicates a positive correlation between employee resilience and perceptions of team support and organizational backing [65]. Notably, organizational support emerges as the most critical factor in fostering employee resilience. This finding underscores the pivotal role of a supportive organizational environment in enabling and encouraging resilient behaviors among employees [66].

The paramedics in the EMS (across both geographical regions) reported the lowest levels of organizational resilience compared to other groups within their organizations. This may suggest that these key personnel, who are crucial to the functioning of health organizations, have a heightened awareness of existing threats and risks and have accumulated significant negative experiences from dealing with numerous emergency and crisis situations [67]. Risk awareness is considered a crucial component of organizational resilience, particularly in healthcare settings. It refers to how an organization understands and reflects on risks that may affect patients, potential adverse events, and the organization’s overall functioning [68].

Paramedics provide round-the-clock prehospital emergency care and play a pivotal role during major incidents or disasters, both locally and internationally [69]. Their relatively lower levels of perceived organizational resilience may be linked to insufficient institutional support, shaped by several structural and organizational factors. While physicians, paramedics, and other healthcare professionals often share the same work environment, their distinct roles and responsibilities expose them to different pressures that may affect well-being and resilience [70]. Both groups frequently operate under a “do more with less” mentality, facing heavy clinical and administrative demands with limited resources, which may leave them feeling undervalued and overburdened [71]. In addition, hospital management systems tend to prioritize clinical outcomes and documentation requirements, sometimes overlooking the support needs of frontline staff such as physicians and paramedics [72]. Evidence from frontline healthcare providers during COVID-19 demonstrated that the absence of institutional support was a significant source of distress, underscoring how inadequate organizational backing during crises can negatively affect perceptions of resilience [73].

It is important to note that the study design does not allow for causal inferences. The findings should therefore be interpreted as associations between organizational resilience and the examined variables, rather than as evidence of direct causal relationships. This distinction ensures a more accurate understanding of the results and aligns with the methodological limitations of the study.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. The relatively small number of participants in some professional subgroups, particularly paramedics and physicians, limits the ability to generalize the findings, which should therefore be interpreted with caution and not assumed to represent the entire profession. Additionally, only half of the healthcare organizations (3 out of 6) participated in both measurement points, restricting comparability over time. As in most studies based on structured questionnaires, reliance on self-report measures introduces the risk of social desirability and common method bias. Future research should employ multi-method approaches and larger, more diverse samples to enhance validity and generalizability.

Conclusions

Nurturing organizational resilience in healthcare settings is vital to ensure operational continuity during emergencies. By enhancing their resilience, organizations can more effectively manage unforeseen emergencies, mitigate their impact on the overall health system, and contribute to saving lives. It will also enable healthcare providers to create more robust, adaptable, and effective healthcare organizations capable of thriving in an increasingly complex and unpredictable environment [74].

Due to the differing levels of organizational resilience across disciplines within healthcare organizations, it is recommended to concentrate efforts and resources on sectors with lower resilience levels. This approach will enhance targeted interventions, save resources, and establish an effective timeline for strengthening organizational resilience. Transformational leadership, technological advancement, and employee well-being play a critical role in fostering a resilient healthcare organization. Healthcare management should leverage this knowledge to design and implement targeted educational intervention programs. By focusing on enhancing leadership practices, embracing technological innovations, and prioritizing employee well-being, organizations can effectively strengthen their resilience. This proactive approach not only prepares healthcare institutions to better navigate challenges but also promotes a sustainable and adaptive organizational culture. Integrating these key factors into strategic planning is essential for healthcare organizations aiming to thrive in an increasingly complex and dynamic environment.

Implications of the research for healthcare organizations

The findings of this study carry important implications for healthcare organizations seeking to enhance organizational resilience. Notably, certain professional subgroups—particularly paramedics—reported relatively lower perceptions of resilience, suggesting the need for targeted, tailor-made interventions that directly address their unique challenges. At the organizational level, the identification of transformational leadership, employee well-being, and technological advancement as key predictors of resilience underscores the importance of fostering supportive leadership practices, investing in staff welfare, and strategically integrating technology. Future research should build on the current findings by translating them into clearly articulated and evidence-based research directions. Longitudinal and intervention-based designs are particularly needed to elucidate causal pathways linking transformational leadership, technological advancement, and employee well-being to organizational resilience, as well as to evaluate the effectiveness of targeted resilience-building programs tailored to specific professional groups. Comparative analyses across diverse healthcare settings and sectors may further reveal how contextual and structural variations shape resilience outcomes. Pursuing these directions will enable future studies to move beyond descriptive insights toward the development of actionable, empirically grounded strategies that strengthen adaptive capacity and workforce sustainability within complex and uncertain healthcare environments.
