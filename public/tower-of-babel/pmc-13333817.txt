Aerobic Methane Oxidation in the Water Column of the Shallow Eutrophic Temperate Lake Kasumigaura
Authors: Thi Khanh Huyen Nguyen, Yukina Amano, Shoya Yano, Thi Hoang Yen Tran, Thanh Luu Pham, Kazuya Shimizu, Yuma Fujioka, Yoichi Katakura, Motoo Utsumi, Qintong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lake Kasumigaura, the second-largest lake in Japan, is shallow, well-oxygenated, and eutrophic. Since shallow lakes are hotspots for methane（CH4）emissions, understanding CH4 oxidation - the process that reduces CH4 emissions - is essential. This study examines aerobic CH4 oxidation in Lake Kasumigaura to clarify seasonal fluctuations over six years and the factors that influence it. There was a seasonal dynamic in aerobic CH4 oxidation in Lake Kasumigaura, with the highest rates in autumn and the lowest in spring. The peak values for both the specific CH4 oxidation rate (0.0764 ± 0.04 h-1) and the CH4 consumption rate (14.9 ± 13.3 nM CH4 h-1) occurred in autumn. Moderate activity was recorded in both summer and winter. Using quantitative PCR and Next-Generation Sequencing, it was found that, unlike in other freshwater lakes, Lake Kasumigaura’s methanotrophic community is mainly composed of Methylocystis, a Type II methanotroph. Redundancy analysis revealed niche differentiation: Methylocystis (Type II) correlates strongly with TN and NO3–, while Type I methanotrophs are less abundant and more sensitive to dissolved CH4 and water temperature (W.T.). Variations in the dominant methanotrophs (revealed by pmoA gene analyses) in lake water, along with the complex relationships between environmental factors and these communities, highlight the unique biogeochemical properties of Lake Kasumigaura.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47321-z.

Introduction

Freshwater ecosystems contribute significantly to atmospheric CH4 emissions, accounting for approximately 16–27% of global natural CH4 sources, with lakes alone contributing about 70% of this amount1–11. In freshwater lakes, CH4 is primarily produced through methanogenesis, a process driven by the microbial degradation of organic matter under anoxic conditions in lake sediments11–14. In these ecosystems, a substantial proportion of the generated CH4 is oxidized by methanotrophic bacteria before it is released into the atmosphere, predominantly through aerobic CH4 oxidation15. This microbial CH4 sink plays a crucial role in regulating CH4 emissions from lakes and significantly influences the global CH4 budget16–21.

Shallow lakes constitute a substantial proportion of the global lake population, both in terms of numerical abundance and surface area22. Within this category, shallow eutrophic lakes - characterized by elevated nutrient levels - are significant contributors to CH4 emissions4,5,11,23. The inherent shallowness of these lakes often leads to frequent vertical mixing, disrupting thermal stratification and potentially enhancing CH4 release to the atmosphere6,9,24–27. Consequently, the water columns of shallow lakes are infrequently stratified and exhibit considerable spatial variability in oxygen (O2) and CH4 concentrations. These conditions facilitate the coexistence of CH4 and O2, thereby creating optimal conditions for aerobic CH4 oxidation in the water column28,29. The water column itself serves as a dynamic, and relatively unexplored habitat where substantial CH4 oxidation may occur28,30–32.

Aerobic methanotrophs primarily perform aerobic CH4 oxidation. They are widely distributed across natural habitats, typically observed at oxic-anoxic interfaces12. These methanotrophs are classified into three groups: Type I and Type X, both belonging to the Gammaproteobacteria, and Type II, belonging to the Alphaproteobacteria12,15,33. These lineages demonstrate ecologically adaptive preferences: Type I methanotrophs tend to dominate in environments with relatively high CH4 availability and moderate O2 levels, whereas Type II methanotrophs are better suited to conditions with lower CH4 concentrations and more strongly oxic or disturbance-prone environments12,34–36. Recent studies employing stable isotope probing, metagenomics, and CH4 flux measurements have demonstrated that methanotrophs in the water column can effectively oxidize a considerable fraction of the upward CH4 flux, thereby mitigating net emissions from lakes37–39. These findings underscore the ecological significance of pelagic methanotrophs, which are functionally distinct from their benthic counterparts and exhibit a capacity for rapid adaptation to variations in water-column structure and O2 availability.

Despite these advances, current understanding of aerobic CH4 oxidation in lakes water column remains biased toward permanently37,39,40 or seasonally stratified lakes13,17–19,32,41–45. In contrast, shallow, non-stratified lakes – characterized by frequent sediment-water exchange and persistent water-column oxygenation that may fundamentally alter CH4 oxidation dynamics1,46–48– remain poorly studied. Moreover, investigations that integrate seasonal variability, methanotrophic community structure, and direct CH4 oxidation measurements, particularly over long timescales, remain rare.

Lake Kasumigaura, the second-largest lake in Japan, represents an ideal natural system to address these knowledge gaps. As a shallow, eutrophic lake49, characterized by continuous wind-driven mixing and the absence of a persistent anaerobic hypolimnion, Lake Kasumigaura supports predominantly aerobic CH4 oxidation within the water column47,48. Previous studies have shown that bacterial CH4 oxidation constitutes a major CH4 sink in this lake, substantially reducing atmospheric emissions47,48. However, the mechanisms driving observed seasonal variability – whether governed by physical mixing, chemical conditions, or methanotrophic community dynamics – remain poorly constrained. Given the distinct ecological preferences of methanotroph lineages, we hypothesize that the unique environmental conditions of Lake Kasumigaura promote lineage-specific, seasonally varying patterns of aerobic CH4 oxidation. Accordingly, this study investigates seasonal changes in dissolved CH4 concentrations, aerobic CH4 oxidation, and methanotroph populations in the water column, aiming to understand (1) the seasonal dynamics of aerobic CH4 oxidation and (2) the microbial mechanisms underpinning these dynamics in a shallow eutrophic lake.

Results

Dynamics of aerobic methane oxidation in Lake Kasumigaura

Dissolved CH4 concentrations varied across stations and depths, with higher concentrations at the bottom than at the surface (Table S1). Surface CH4 averaged 142.3 ± 95.4 nM, while bottom CH4 averaged 171.5 ± 129.6 nM nM. Dissolved CH4 in Lake Kasumigaura varied over seasons (Fig. 1a), peaking in summer (247.9 ± 125.8 nM) and autumn (211.2 ± 147.4 nM), then declining in winter (88.3 ± 54.3 nM), dropping to the lowest concentration in spring (81.9 ± 45.2 nM).

In Lake Kasumigaura, the specific CH4 oxidation rate ranged from 0.0013 ± 0.0008 to 0.207 ± 0.04 h-1. Regarding the seasonal pattern, the highest oxidation activity in Lake Kasumigaura occurred in autumn, with the specific CH4 oxidation rates of 0.0764 ± 0.04 h-1 and consumption rates of 14.9 ± 13.4 nM CH4 h-1. The lowest was recorded in spring, with specific CH4 oxidation rates of 0.016 ± 0.01 h-1 and consumption rates of 1.43 ± 1.2 nM CH4 h-1 (Fig. 1b and d).

Abundance of methanotrophs in Lake Kasumigaura

The qPCR results targeting the pmoA gene showed that the abundance of methanotrophs ranged from 9.24 × 105 ± 1.00 × 105 to 7.53 × 107 ± 4.65 × 106 copies L-1 (Fig. 1c). Median pmoA copy numbers were 3.68 × 106 in spring, 7.01 × 106 in summer, 1.26 × 107 in autumn, and 4.87 × 106 in winter. Summer and Autumn show higher median pmoA gene copy numbers, indicating enhanced methanotroph proliferation during the warm seasons. Still, there is no significant seasonal difference in Lake Kasumigaura (p > 0.05).

A total of 1,488 Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs) were identified from 32 bottom water samples collected from Lake between March 2022 and March 2025. In the Lake Kasumigaura bottom water column, both Type I and Type II methanotrophs were detected (Fig. 2). The detected Type II methanotrophs include Methylocystis, Methylosinus, and Uncultured Type II. The Type I methanotrophs were classified as Methylosarcina, Methyloparacoccus, Methylomonas, Methylomicrobium, Methylolobus, Methyloglobulus, Methylogaea, Methylococcus, Methylocaldum, Methylobacter, and Uncultured Type I. Within the methanotrophic community, Methylocystis was identified as the dominant genus across stations and seasons (Fig. 2). Other genera, including Methylococcus and Uncultured Type I, occurred at substantially lower relative abundances. Overall, the community composition remained largely consistent among bottom-layer samples. This spatially uniform pattern indicates that the methanotrophic community in Lake Kasumigaura is compositionally homogeneous across stations. The Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarities of Hellinger-transformed ASVs data (Fig. S1) indicated a subtle seasonal pattern. Although summer samples appeared slightly more dispersed, an analysis of multivariate dispersion using ANOVA (betadisper) revealed no statistically significant differences in dispersion across seasons (F (3, 28) = 1.44, p = 0.25). Additionally, pairwise Tukey’s tests were all non-significant (p > 0.05).

Relationships among environmental variables and methanotrophic community structure

Spearman correlation analysis (Fig. 3) revealed significant associations among CH4-related variables and environmental factors. Dissolved CH4 concentration was positively correlated with W.T. and negatively correlated with DO. The CH4 consumption rate showed positive correlations with dissolved CH4 concentration and the specific CH4 oxidation rate, and negative correlations with DO. The specific CH4 oxidation rate was positively correlated with dissolved CH4 concentration and pmoA gene abundance. Additionally, NH4+ showed positive correlations, whereas NO2−, pH, and ORP were negatively correlated with CH4-related parameters.

Redundancy analysis (RDA) was performed to explore the relationships between methanotrophic community composition and environmental variables in the water column. The overall model was statistically significant (P = 0.018), with the first two canonical axes accounting for 74.7% and 10.9% of the constrained variance, respectively (Fig. 4). RDA1 represented a gradient contrasting warmer water temperatures and higher dissolved CH4 concentrations with increased NO3− concentrations. Type I methanotrophs, including Uncultured Type I and Methylococcus, were associated with warmer and CH4-enriched conditions, whereas the dominant lineage, Methylocystis (Type II), was associated with higher NO3− concentrations. RDA2 reflected variation related to DO and ORP.

Discussion

The dissolved CH4 concentrations in Lake Kasumigaura ranged from 16.7 ± 0.9 nM to 674.2 ± 5.1 nM, consistently above the atmospheric equilibrium of 2.6 nM (at 25 °C, 1 atm). These levels are generally lower than those in other shallow temperate lakes, such as Lake Heideweiher (3900 ± 1100 nM), and Lake Windsborn (1500 ± 1600 nM)9, the small shallow lakes in southwest Germany (60–20000 nM)50, as well as eutrophic lakes like Lake Taihu (2110 nM)51, Lake Chaohu (1010 nM)51, Lake Dianshan (2440 nM)51, Lake George (200–800 nM)52 and Lake Mai Ndombe (500–490.749 nM)52. Seasonally, dissolved CH4 concentration peaked in summer and remained elevated into autumn (Fig. 1a), consistent with enhanced benthic supply and wind-driven mixing in polymictic systems53–57. In autumn, bottom waters were well oxygenated (DO at 9.1 ± 1.2 mg L-1; Table S2), and temperatures were moderate (20.1 ± 5.9 °C), a combination that may favor rapid oxidation of CH4. Overall, the accumulation of dissolved CH4 during summer, followed by cooler W.T. and higher O2 levels in autumn, suggests a seasonal pattern that could increase CH4 oxidation in autumn.

In autumn, the specific CH4 oxidation rate (h-1), the CH4 consumption rate (nM CH4 h-1), and pmoA gene abundance were highest (Fig. 1b, c and d). The positive correlations among specific CH4 oxidation rate, CH4 consumption rate, and pmoA gene abundance (Fig. 3) suggest that methanotroph abundance contributes to aerobic CH4 oxidation potential, whereas the overall CH4 consumption rate is jointly regulated by biomass and substrate availability. The positive relationship between dissolved CH4 and specific CH4 oxidation rate indicates substrate-driven physiological responses, whereas the lack of a significant correlation between CH4 concentration and pmoA abundance implies that biomass accrual may occur on a slower temporal scale. CH4 oxidation decreased in winter, likely due to low temperature57–59. However, the oxidation activity remained measurable (specific rate at 0.0257 ± 0.02 h-1; consumption rate at 1.94 ± 1.9 nM CH4 h-1), suggesting that the water column contributes to year-round CH4 reduction in Lake Kasumigaura. In winter, the continuous CH4 oxidation activity may be due to the year’s highest DO (12 ± 0.8 mg L-1) and well-mixing conditions (Table S2)18,31. These seasonal variations in CH4 oxidation and pmoA abundance are further reflected in the community-environment relationships revealed by the RDA (Fig. 4).

The dominant genera of Type I methanotrophs (Uncultured Type I and Methylococcus) are strongly associated with warmer and elevated CH4 availability (Fig. 4). This aligns with the known “copiotrophic” strategy of Type I methanotrophs, which use the RuMP pathway and are favored in high-substrate (high dissolved CH4) environments due to their higher maximum growth rates15,60. Such conditions typically occur during warm periods when enhanced sediment methanogenesis and reduced mixing increase CH4 supply to the water column2. Conversely, the dominance of Methylocystis (Type II methanotrophs) under lower CH4 and higher NO3− conditions suggests adaptation to more oxidized, nutrient-rich environments. This pattern suggests Methylocystis dominance during periods of more vigorous mixing, where O2 penetrates deeper, CH4 is more efficiently oxidized, and dissolved CH4 concentrations are lower. This is consistent with their “oligotrophic” strategy: Type II methanotrophs, using the Serine pathway15, possess a higher affinity for CH4 at low concentrations12, and the genus Methylocystis often has the metabolic flexibility to perform nitrogen fixation, providing a crucial competitive advantage in the low concentration dissolved CH4, potentially nitrogen-stressed niche that results from high nitrification rates in well-oxygenated bottom water61. The predominance of Methylocystis across diverse environmental conditions may be explained by its superior metabolic flexibility relative to other methanotrophic genera61. For instance, several strains can utilize acetate and ethanol in addition to methane62,63, or some strains have two forms of methane monooxygenase (pMMO1 and pMMO2), allowing them to be active under a wide range of CH4 concentrations and thus adapt more effectively to environmental fluctuations61. Consequently, the seasonal shift between high CH4 and warm conditions (favoring Type I) and low CH4 and high NO3− conditions (favoring Methylocystis -Type II) acts as the primary driver of methanotroph community dynamics in the lake Kasumigaura’s bottom layer. This ecological complementarity ensures that CH4 oxidation remains active across seasonal transitions, sustaining the CH4 sink from summer enrichment to autumn intensification and winter persistence.

Over the past three decades, Lake Kasumigaura has experienced noticeable increases in W.T. across all seasons, with the most pronounced rise in winter (+ 2.7 °C), followed by summer (+ 1.4 °C), spring (+ 0.3 °C), and autumn (+ 0.2 °C) (Table S3). This warming, particularly during cold seasons, has likely enhanced vertical mixing and increased the temporal overlap between CH4 and O2 in the water column. Consequently, the specific CH4 oxidation rate in Lake Kasumigaura has undergone a marked seasonal redistribution: it has risen during cold seasons (spring/winter) but declined during warm seasons (summer/autumn), compared with the findings of Utsumi et al. 47 conducted three decades ago. Interestingly, the CH4 consumption rate shows an opposite trend to the specific CH4 oxidation rate. This inversion points out a transition in the primary control mechanism – from kinetic control under cold, O2-rich conditions to substrate control under warm, CH4-rich conditions. During spring and winter, enhanced vertical mixing and high O2 availability favor efficient enzyme kinetics and maintain oxic-methane overlap in the water column17,61. Under such conditions, methanotrophs exhibit high specific activity but are constrained by a limited CH4 substrate pool, resulting in modest total consumption. In contrast, during summer and autumn, higher W.T. and intensified sediment-water exchange increase CH4 supply, while localized O2 depletion restricts oxidation efficiency37,43,64. Consequently, CH4 consumption rises despite lower specific rates, a pattern characteristic of CH4-rich but moderately oxygenated regimes. This shift aligns with the ecological partitioning observed in our RDA (Fig. 4). Methylocystis (Type II) remains the dominant lineage year-round, whereas Type I taxa become more active during the warm seasons, when increased substrate availability favors rapid physiological responses. Together, these complementary strategies, Methylocystis (Type II) providing a persistent oxidative background and Type I responding to episodic CH4 enrichment, stabilize aerobic CH4 oxidation across seasons in Lake Kasumigaura.

To further evaluate the impact of warming, we compared the W.T.-CH4 oxidation relationships between the historical period [1991–1996]47 and the recent period [2019–2025] (Fig. S2). The recent dataset exhibited shallower slopes than the historical dataset for both specific CH4 oxidation rate and CH4 consumption rate, indicating that CH4 oxidation has become less sensitive to temperature than it was three decades ago. This reduced slope in the recent period suggests that CH4 oxidation in Lake Kasumigaura is currently influenced by additional regulating factors (such as substrate availability, microbial community structure, etc.) beyond temperature alone. On multi-year timescales, warming-enhanced vertical mixing may extend the effective oxidation window, thereby driving subtle yet persistent changes in the interannual pattern of CH4 oxidation in Lake Kasumigaura.

Conclusions

This study reveals distinct seasonal patterns in the dynamics of aerobic CH4 oxidation (dissolved CH4 concentrations, specific CH4 oxidation rates, and CH4 consumption rate) in Lake Kasumigaura, driven by the interplay of physical, chemical, and biological factors. Elevated aerobic CH4 oxidation in autumn arises from enhanced microbial activity under conditions combining high CH4 availability, moderate W.T., and sufficient oxygenation. Both Type I and Type II methanotrophs contribute to CH4 mitigation but occupy contrasting ecological niches. Type I methanotrophs respond sensitively to dissolved CH4 and W.T., while Methylocystis (Type II), the dominant lineage in Lake Kasumigaura, strongly associates with TN and NO3−. Together, these complementary strategies sustain an efficient, year-round CH4 sink in this shallow, well-mixed lake.

This study assessed methanotroph abundance but did not directly measure their activity. Future work should therefore combine activity-based approaches, such as stable-isotope probing (SIP) and meta-transcriptomics, to identify active CH4-oxidizers. In addition, it should be noted that the water-column CH4 oxidation reported in this study should be interpreted as the fraction of CH4 that escapes from sediment, not the total ecosystem-scale CH4 oxidation. Given that substantial CH4 oxidation may occur aerobically or anaerobically within sediments, integrated investigations across the sediment–water interface are needed to quantify the relative contributions of benthic and pelagic oxidation. Such efforts are essential for a comprehensive understanding of CH4 mitigation in shallow, well-mixed lakes.

Materials and methods

Sampling

Lake Kasumigaura is located in Ibaraki Prefecture (Fig. 5a). It spans 220 km2 and is situated at an elevation of 0.16 m above sea level. The lake is shallow, with an average depth of 3.8 m and a maximum depth of 7.3 meters65.

Sampling was conducted at three stations, including Takahama-iri (Avg. Depth 4.3 m), Lake Center (Avg. Depth 6 m), Tsuchiura-iri (Avg. Depth 3.9 m) (Fig. 5b), in Lake Kasumigaura on a quarterly basis from November 2019 to July 2025, except for the spring sampling period, which was suspended due to the COVID-19 pandemic. The sampling activities were conducted in collaboration with the Ibaraki Kasumigaura Environmental Science Center (Tsuchiura City, Ibaraki Prefecture, Japan) and were carried out with appropriate authorization. No additional field permits were required beyond this formal approval. Regarding the seasonal period, samples taken from March to May were considered spring samples, from June to August, summer samples, from September to November, autumn samples, and from December to February, winter samples. W.T., pH, conductivity, oxidation-reduction potential (ORP), and dissolved oxygen (DO) were measured in situ vertically by 0.5 m increments using a Multi Water Quality Checker U-51 (HORIBA, Kyoto, Japan). Water samples for water chemistry measurements were collected at a depth of 0.5 m below the surface (referred to as surface samples) and 0.5 m above the sediment (referred to as bottom samples) by a peristaltic pump and then stored in co-washed 1 L polyethylene bottles on ice on board and kept at 4 °C until further analysis in the laboratory. Over 6 years, a total of 67 samples were collected (Spring − 12 samples, Summer − 20 samples, Autumn − 20 samples, and Winter − 15 samples). Water samples for CH4 measurement were collected using a Niskin-X Water Sampler (A.G.O. Environmental Ltd., Victoria, Canada) and were promptly treated on board.

Water chemistry measurement

Water samples were first filtered through a Whatman™ GF/C filter (Cytiva, USA). The filtrate was then analyzed for the concentrations of ammonium (NH4+), nitrate (NO3−), nitrite (NO2−), and phosphate (PO43−) in water samples using a TRAACS 800 Autoanalyzer (Bran-Luebbe, SPXFLOW, North Carolina, US). Total Nitrogen (TN) and Total Phosphorus (TP) were analyzed using PACKTEST (KYORITSU CHEMICAL-CHECK Lab., Corp., Kanagawa, Japan) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Dissolved CH4 concentration and CH4 oxidation rate measurement

For the CH4 measurement, lake water samples were added to 100 mL sterile glass vials, overflowing to minimize gas exchange with the atmosphere, in independent field triplicates. The filled glass vials were immediately sealed with butyl rubber stoppers and aluminum caps, without introducing bubbles. NaOH solution (final concentration = 0.1 M) was injected into each sealed vial using a syringe to halt microbial activity. At the same time, an equivalent volume of water was simultaneously withdrawn through a second needle to maintain constant internal pressure and total volume. The treated samples were subsequently stored on ice during transportation and refrigerated at 4 °C in the laboratory before gas chromatography was performed within 1 month of the sampling date.

Bottom water samples were used in the incubation experiment to measure CH4 oxidation rates. The water samples were processed as described above without the addition of NaOH solution. The samples were kept at in situ W.T. in a cooler box during transportation and then incubated for 9 h in the dark in the laboratory at in situ W.T.. Every 2 to 3 h, three vials were removed from the incubator, treated with NaOH solution (final conc. 0.1 M) to halt microbial activity, and stored at 4 °C for further use.

To create a headspace, about 70 mL of N2 (99.99% N2) was injected into each sealed vial using a syringe connected to silicone tubing through a miniature check valve (MFTD-W-3-V, PTFE; GL Sciences, Tokyo, Japan) and a three-way stopcock for gas injection and headspace preparation. At the same time, an equivalent volume of water was simultaneously withdrawn through a second needle to maintain constant internal pressure and total volume. The final amount of water retained in each vial was approximately equivalent to the volume of 60 g of Milli-Q water, ensuring a consistent headspace-to-water ratio among all replicates. The vials were then vigorously shaken to achieve gas-liquid equilibrium1,31,47,48,66,67. Two milliliters of air sample extracted from the gas phase were injected into a gas chromatograph equipped with a flame ionization detector (GC-14B, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). Detailed operating parameters are provided in Table S4. The dissolved CH4 concentration in the liquid phase was then calculated using the Henry’s Law constant, and the dissolved CH4 concentration in water samples was determined based on the volume ratio of gas to liquid phases in the vial47,68–70.

The specific CH4 oxidation rate (h-1) was determined from the slope of the linear regression of the natural logarithm of dissolved CH4 concentration versus incubation time. Because no dilution or nutrient addition was performed, the bacterial populations were assumed to maintain their natural activity, and the initial oxidation rates were likely representative of in situ conditions. In vials, dissolved CH4 concentrations declined exponentially over time, and the specific oxidation rate provided a standardized measure of methanotrophic activity independent of CH4 concentration. According to Utsumi et al.47, Gas chromatography with flame ionization detection (GC–FID) enabled highly reproducible CH4 analyses. Consequently, short incubation periods (less than 10 h) were sufficient, limiting CH4 consumption to 20–30% of the initial concentration and minimizing bottle effects and shifts in CH4 and O2 during measurement. The temporal decline in dissolved CH4 followed an exponential decay, with a linear relationship between the natural logarithm of the dissolved CH4 concentration and incubation time. Dissolved CH4 concentrations never increased during incubations, confirming the absence of methanogenesis under oxic conditions, as CH4 formation is strictly anaerobic. The actual CH4 oxidation rate (CH4 consumption rate) (nM CH4 h-1) was obtained by multiplying the specific oxidation rate by the measured ambient CH4 concentration, enabling comparisons among samples with different CH4 levels47,68.

DNA extraction

Water samples (70 to 150 mL) were filtered through a 0.2 μm pore size polycarbonate filter (diameter 47 mm, Nucleopore Track-Etch Membrane Filters, Whatman, Global Life Sciences Solutions Operations UK Ltd, Amersham Place, Little Chalfont, Buckinghamshire, UK), which were then stored at –30 °C before DNA extraction. DNA was extracted from the frozen filters using the ISOL for Bead Beating DNA extraction Kit (Nippon Gene CO., LTD., Toyama, Japan) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The DNA concentration and purity were determined using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, US) with Nanodrop 1000 Software (v3.8.1).

Real-time PCR assay

Real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed using the SYBR Green method to estimate methanotroph abundance. For the quantification of methanotrophs, the pmoA gene, which encodes the pMMO, was used as the biomarker with the primer sets A189f (5′-GGNGACTGGGACTTCTGG-3′) and mb661r (5′-CCGGMGCAACGTCYTTACC-3′)71. All qPCR assays were performed on an Applied Biosystems 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Dreieich, Germany) equipped with ABI 7500 Software (v2.0.1). The copy number of the methanotrophic pmoA gene in all samples was determined in triplicate on non-diluted samples. Detailed reaction mixture composition and thermal cycling conditions are summarized in Tables S5 and S6. Standards for real-time PCR were generated by using serial dilutions of known copies of PCR fragments. To ensure each real-time assay was conducted under high efficiency and specificity, assay validation was performed to optimize primer concentration and annealing temperature. The final amplification efficiency of qPCR ranged from 111% to 115%, but the R2 of the standard curve was 0.99 or higher, indicating high reproducibility of quantification.

Sequencing and phylogenetic analysis of the pmoA gene

To investigate the seasonal diversity of methanotrophs, we used a MiSeq next-generation sequencer (Illumina, California, USA) to sequence the pmoA gene in bottom-layer samples. A total of 32 bottom water samples collected between March 2022 and March 2025 were included in the phylogenetic and community composition analyses after quality filtering.

For PCR amplification, we used the adapter-attached primer set A189f/mb661r71 and Takara Ex Premier DNA Polymerase (Takara Bio., Otsu, Japan). The detailed PCR reaction mixture and thermal cycling are summarised in Tables S7 and S8. Amplicons were separated by electrophoresis on a 1.0% agarose gel and purified using the NucleoSpin® gel and PCR clean-up kit (MACHEREY-NAGEL GmbH & Co. KG, Duren, Germany). Purified PCR products were stored in 1.5 mL tubes at –30 °C until sequencing. The partial pmoA sequences generated in this study have been registered in the DNA Data Bank of Japan (DDBJ) under BioProject PRJDB39735 (PSUB046684), with accession numbers SAMD01782465-SAMD01782496.

Taxonomic annotation of pmoA ASVs was performed using VSEARCH72(v2.30.0) via global alignment against the curated pmoA reference database pmoa780973 with an identity threshold ≥ 0.85. A relatively permissive identity threshold was selected to accommodate the high evolutionary divergence of functional pmoA genes and to retain lineage-level ecological diversity. Similarity thresholds of 80–90% have been reported in previous studies as an appropriate balance between diversity retention and conservative classification for CH4 oxidation and denitrification genes74–76. Taxonomic labels were assigned by linking best-hit reference sequences to the corresponding taxonomy mapping file. In addition, BLAST searches against the DDBJ nucleotide database were manually cross-checked to confirm phylogenetic affiliation and prevent misclassification of representative and dominant ASVs. ASVs that could not be confidently assigned to a cultured genus were conservatively classified as “Uncultured Type I” or “Uncultured Type II” lineages based on their phylogenetic affiliation.

To ensure reproducibility, all samples were processed using an identical PCR protocol and bioinformatic pipeline. Consistent detection of dominant taxa across stations and sampling seasons further supports the robustness of the observed phylogenetic patterns.

Statistical analysis

Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to examine relationships among environmental variables, dissolved CH4, specific CH4 oxidation rates，CH4 consumption rates, and pmoA gene copy number. Seasonal differences among groups shown in Fig. 1 were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Data normality was verified prior to analysis. To identify linkages between the methanotroph community (genus-level) and ecological drivers, RDA was performed, with environmental predictors as explanatory variables, conditioning on Station and Season to remove spatial–temporal autocorrelation77,78. This RDA used environmental variables (multiple z-score normalized) as explanatory variables, while the pmoA ASVs (Hellinger-transformed) served as the response variable.
