When plasmacytoma defies expectations: An unusual maxillary presentation with mixed imaging features in a young patient
Authors: Mohamed Bouallou, Achraf Amine Sbai, Drissia Benfadil, Azeddine Lachkar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Plasmacytoma of the maxilla in adolescents is exceedingly rare and represents a diagnostic challenge, particularly when associated with atypical imaging features that may mimic aggressive maxillofacial tumors. We report the case of a 17-year-old female presenting with a progressively enlarging painless right lateronasal swelling. Computed tomography revealed a large maxillary lesion with an unusual mixed osteolytic–osteosclerotic pattern, including intralesional mineralization and multiple osteolytic lacunae. Histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses confirmed a monoclonal plasma cell neoplasm consistent with solitary extramedullary plasmacytoma, while systemic evaluation excluded multiple myeloma. The patient underwent radiotherapy (45 Gy), achieving complete remission with no recurrence at 21 months. This case highlights an uncommon imaging phenotype and a potential diagnostic pitfall, emphasizing the importance of imaging–pathology correlation for accurate diagnosis and optimal management.

Introduction

Solitary plasmacytoma is an uncommon plasma cell neoplasm defined by a localized monoclonal proliferation of plasma cells in the absence of systemic features of multiple myeloma. It represents a rare subset of plasma cell dyscrasias and is classically divided into solitary plasmacytoma of bone and extramedullary plasmacytoma, the latter most frequently arising in the upper aerodigestive tract [1].

Although head and neck involvement is common in extramedullary forms, maxillary localization remains rare, particularly in young patients. Plasmacytoma typically affects individuals in the fifth to sixth decades of life, making its occurrence in adolescence highly unusual and diagnostically challenging [2]. Clinical presentation is often nonspecific, commonly characterized by painless swelling or dental symptoms, which may delay diagnosis.

Radiologically, plasmacytomas are usually described as well-defined osteolytic lesions [3]. However, atypical imaging patterns, including mixed osteolytic and osteosclerotic features, have been increasingly reported, potentially mimicking a broad spectrum of benign and malignant maxillofacial lesions and complicating the diagnostic approach.

We report an unusual case of maxillary plasmacytoma in a 17-year-old patient with a mixed osteolytic–osteocondensing radiological pattern. This report emphasizes the diagnostic challenges posed by atypical imaging features and highlights the importance of imaging–pathology correlation for accurate diagnosis.

Case presentation

A 17-year-old female with no significant past medical history was referred to our Department of Otorhinolaryngology for evaluation of a progressively enlarging right lateronasal swelling evolving over a 3-month period. The patient denied any history of systemic disease, prior radiotherapy, facial trauma, or previous surgical procedures. She also reported no history of persistent fever, constitutional symptoms, allergies, or unintended weight loss.

According to the patient and her family, the symptoms initially began 3 months prior to admission with the gradual onset of a small, painless swelling located in the right lateronasal region. Because the lesion was initially asymptomatic, it was neglected during the early phase. However, a marked increase in size during the month preceding consultation prompted medical evaluation.

On admission, the patient was in good general condition and hemodynamically stable. General physical examination revealed no abnormalities. Otorhinolaryngological examination identified a firm, well-circumscribed, rounded mass located in the right lateronasal region. The lesion was nontender and measured approximately 1.3 cm in its palpable component, with no associated inflammatory signs. This clinical measurement reflected only the superficial and clinically accessible portion of the lesion.

Flexible nasofibroscopy demonstrated a normal-appearing nasal mucosa. A mild bulging of the right lateral nasal wall was observed without obstruction of the nasal meatuses. The nasopharynx appeared normal, and examination of the laryngeal structures revealed no pathological findings.

Intraoral examination revealed gingival erythema associated with poor oral hygiene and mobility of the left maxillary second molar (tooth 27). No mucosal ulceration or intraoral mass was identified. Cervical examination did not reveal any palpable lymphadenopathy. Otoscopic examination was unremarkable, and the remainder of the clinical examination did not demonstrate any additional abnormalities.

A noncontrast CT scan of the head and neck revealed a large right maxillary tumor of osseous origin. The lesion appeared irregular in shape but well delineated, showing continuity with adjacent osseous structures. It exhibited predominantly osteocondensing characteristics with heterogeneous density, containing areas of intralesional mineralization as well as multiple osteolytic lacunae. Quantitatively, approximately 60%-65% of the lesion demonstrated osteosclerotic/osteocondensing components, while 35%-40% consisted of osteolytic lacunae of variable size distributed throughout the lesion.

Multiple small osteolytic lacunae were present, ranging from a few millimeters up to approximately 8-10 mm, without formation of a dominant cavitary defect.

The cortical bone of the maxilla was markedly thinned and remodeled, with focal areas of cortical interruption along the anterior maxillary wall and alveolar process, consistent with limited cortical breach rather than extensive destruction.

No periosteal reaction (such as spiculated “sunburst” appearance or Codman triangle) was identified.

No calcified periosteal new bone formation or aggressive periosteal response was observed.

A minimal soft-tissue component was present, extending slightly into the adjacent gingival and subperiosteal region, without evidence of frank invasion of the nasal cavity, or orbital floor.

Magnetic resonance imaging was not performed due to a contraindication related to the patient’s implanted cardiac pacemaker, which precluded MRI acquisition for safety reasons.

Under local anesthesia, 3 incisional biopsies were obtained using an osseous curette. Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated strong expression of CD38 and CD138 with kappa light-chain restriction, while CD19, CD20, HHV-8, and cyclin D1 were negative, confirming the monoclonal nature of the proliferation.

A comprehensive systemic evaluation was subsequently performed to exclude an underlying systemic plasma cell disorder, particularly Multiple Myeloma. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the head, neck, thorax, abdomen, and pelvis revealed no evidence of lymph node involvement, additional osseous lesions, or distant metastases.

Laboratory investigations showed normal complete blood count, serum calcium levels, hepatic and renal function, albumin levels, and total protein levels. Uric acid levels were within normal limits. The erythrocyte sedimentation rate and lactate dehydrogenase levels were also normal. Serum protein electrophoresis with immunofixation did not demonstrate any monoclonal (M) protein, and urine analysis was negative for Bence–Jones proteinuria. Bone marrow examination demonstrated normal cellularity with minimal plasma cell infiltration (1%).

Following multidisciplinary discussion, the diagnosis of solitary bone plasmacytoma was established in accordance with the International Myeloma Working Group criteria.

The patient subsequently underwent definitive external beam radiotherapy, delivering a total dose of 45 Gy in 25 fractions over a period of 7 weeks. The treatment was well tolerated, with no significant adverse effects such as mucositis or xerostomia.

Radiotherapy was delivered using intensity-modulated radiotherapy, with a planning target volume (PTV) encompassing the gross tumor volume (GTV) defined on CT, with a 5-10 mm margin adapted to anatomical constraints of the maxillofacial region. The clinical target volume (CTV) included the involved right maxillary bone, adjacent alveolar process, and areas of cortical remodeling, while respecting uninvolved critical structures.

Organs at risk (OARs), including the contralateral maxilla, oral cavity, ipsilateral orbit, optic apparatus, and parotid gland, were carefully contoured and dose constraints were respected according to institutional head-and-neck radiotherapy protocols to minimize late toxicity.

Given the patient’s age (17 years), particular attention was paid to maxillofacial growth structures and dental development. Beam arrangement and dose distribution were optimized to limit irradiation of uninvolved growth centers, reduce the risk of facial growth disturbance, trismus, and long-term dental complications, while maintaining adequate tumoricidal dose coverage.

The patient underwent monthly follow-up with otorhinolaryngological examination and nasofibroscopy.

Post-treatment imaging and surveillance were systematically performed to monitor local response and exclude systemic progression. A first post-therapeutic CT scan was obtained at 10 months, demonstrating complete radiological regression of the maxillary lesion with restoration of normal bony architecture and no evidence of residual or recurrent disease. A subsequent CT performed at 21 months confirmed sustained complete remission without local recurrence.

Biological follow-up was conducted every 3-6 months and included complete blood count, serum calcium, renal and hepatic function tests, serum protein electrophoresis with immunofixation, and urine analysis for Bence–Jones proteinuria, all of which remained within normal limits throughout the follow-up period.

Systemic surveillance was maintained in accordance with solitary plasmacytoma follow-up recommendations, including clinical evaluation for signs of progression, periodic laboratory monitoring, and reassessment for any myeloma-defining events. No clinical, biological, or radiological evidence of progression to multiple myeloma was observed during follow-up.

Bone marrow assessment was not repeated during follow-up given the absence of clinical or biological suspicion of systemic evolution and the stable normal baseline marrow evaluation (1% plasma cell infiltration at diagnosis).

Clinically, the patient remained asymptomatic with no recurrence of facial swelling, nasal obstruction, or dental symptoms.

From a functional standpoint, dental stability was preserved, with no progression of tooth mobility and no evidence of treatment-related osteoradionecrosis or maxillary structural collapse. Masticatory function and facial symmetry remained satisfactory throughout follow-up.

Discussion

Solitary plasmacytoma is a rare plasma cell neoplasm characterized by a localized proliferation of monoclonal plasma cells in the absence of systemic involvement. It accounts for approximately 3%-6% of all plasma cell dyscrasias, with an estimated annual incidence ranging from 0.15 to 0.45 cases per 100,000 individuals [4].

Two main clinicopathological entities are recognized: solitary plasmacytoma of bone (SPB) and extramedullary plasmacytoma (EMP). SPB represents the predominant subtype, accounting for nearly 70% of cases, whereas EMP comprises approximately 30% [5].

SPB preferentially involves the axial skeleton, particularly the vertebrae, pelvis, and ribs, reflecting the distribution of hematopoietically active marrow, whereas EMP arises most commonly within the upper aerodigestive tract, which accounts for nearly 80% of cases, including the nasal cavity, paranasal sinuses, and nasopharynx [6].

From an epidemiological perspective, plasmacytoma predominantly affects middle-aged to elderly individuals, with a median age of 55-60 years and a marked male predominance [7]. Therefore, the occurrence of a maxillary plasmacytoma in a 17-year-old female represents an exceptionally rare presentation.

Clinically, maxillary plasmacytomas present with nonspecific symptoms related to progressive tumor expansion and local bone destruction, including facial swelling, dental mobility, gingival inflammation, nasal obstruction, and epistaxis [2].

In our patient, the initial presentation as a painless lateronasal swelling associated with gingival erythema and dental mobility is consistent with previously reported cases, in which early manifestations are often insidious and may lead to delayed diagnosis.

A focused review of the literature (Table 1) confirms the exceptional nature of such presentations in young patients, with only a limited number of cases reported to date. Notably, the clinical presentation across reported cases remains relatively homogeneous, dominated by painless swelling and dental symptoms, whereas imaging findings demonstrate considerable variability. Imaging plays a central role in the diagnostic approach to maxillary tumors, not only for lesion detection but also for precise assessment of locoregional extension and bone involvement. CT remains the modality of choice for assessing osseous involvement, owing to its superior spatial resolution and ability to delineate cortical integrity, trabecular architecture, and the relationship of the lesion with adjacent anatomical structures, including the maxillary sinus, nasal cavity, and alveolar process.

Classically, plasmacytomas are described as well-defined osteolytic lesions with cortical thinning or destruction, reflecting their locally aggressive yet relatively indolent behavior [11].

In this context, the present case is particularly distinctive, demonstrating a predominantly osteocondensing lesion with superimposed osteolytic components, an exceedingly rare imaging phenotype, especially in a young patient.

From a diagnostic standpoint, such heterogeneous radiologic features necessitate a rigorous and comprehensive differential diagnosis, particularly in a young patient presenting with a maxillary lesion. The combination of osteocondensing and osteolytic components observed in the present case considerably broadened the spectrum of potential diagnoses.

High-grade primary bone malignancies, particularly osteosarcoma, represented an important diagnostic consideration. Osteosarcomas of the maxillofacial skeleton typically demonstrate aggressive radiologic features including poorly defined margins, cortical destruction, spiculated periosteal reactions (sunburst appearance), Codman triangle formation, and a substantial soft-tissue component resulting from rapid extracortical extension [17]. Although the presence of intralesional mineralization initially raised concern for an osteogenic neoplasm, the absence of aggressive periosteal reaction, extensive soft-tissue invasion, and diffuse cortical destruction argued against this diagnosis.

Fibro-osseous lesions, including ossifying fibroma and fibrous dysplasia, were also considered. Ossifying fibroma generally presents as a well-circumscribed expansile lesion with varying degrees of internal calcification and cortical expansion, whereas fibrous dysplasia classically exhibits a homogeneous “ground-glass” matrix with preservation of bony contours and gradual osseous remodeling [18]. In contrast, our lesion demonstrated a heterogeneous architecture characterized by multiple discrete osteolytic lacunae intermixed with osteosclerotic areas, a pattern that is atypical for both entities.

Odontogenic tumors, particularly calcifying epithelial odontogenic tumor, calcifying odontogenic cyst, cemento-ossifying lesions, and complex odontoma, may occasionally exhibit mixed radiolucent–radiopaque appearances. However, these lesions are usually associated with tooth displacement, impacted teeth, or characteristic odontogenic calcification patterns and rarely demonstrate the extensive combination of osteolysis, cortical remodeling, and diffuse intralesional mineralization observed in the present case.

Primary bone lymphoma also entered the differential diagnosis because of the patient's age and the possibility of presenting as a destructive maxillofacial lesion. Nevertheless, lymphoma typically manifests as a permeative osteolytic process associated with a disproportionate soft-tissue mass and relatively limited reactive bone formation. The predominantly osteocondensing appearance and absence of a significant soft-tissue component made this diagnosis less likely.

Chronic osteomyelitis was another consideration, particularly given the coexistence of sclerosis and bone remodeling. However, chronic osteomyelitis is usually associated with clinical signs of infection, including pain, fever, inflammatory symptoms, elevated inflammatory markers, sequestrum formation, or periosteal new bone formation. None of these findings were present in our patient, rendering an infectious etiology unlikely.

Given the patient's young age, Langerhans cell histiocytosis also warranted consideration. Maxillofacial involvement may produce osteolytic lesions associated with dental mobility and the characteristic appearance of “floating teeth.” However, Langerhans cell histiocytosis generally presents as purely lytic bone destruction without significant osteosclerotic reaction or intralesional mineralization, features that differed substantially from the imaging appearance observed in our case.

Taken together, the coexistence of osteolytic lacunae and predominant osteocondensing components, associated with limited cortical breach, absence of aggressive periosteal reaction, minimal soft-tissue extension, and lack of systemic manifestations, favored an unusual plasma cell neoplasm rather than the aforementioned malignant, infectious, fibro-osseous, or odontogenic conditions. Nevertheless, given the substantial overlap in imaging appearances, definitive diagnosis relied on histopathological and immunohistochemical confirmation.

Histopathological examination remains the cornerstone of diagnosis. Microscopically, plasmacytomas are characterized by a diffuse proliferation of plasma cells with eccentric nuclei, coarse chromatin, and abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm. Immunohistochemistry plays a critical role in confirming the monoclonal nature of the proliferation.

Typical findings include strong expression of plasma cell markers such as CD38 and CD138, associated with immunoglobulin light-chain restriction [1]. In the present case, tumor cells demonstrated strong positivity for CD38 and CD138 with kappa light-chain restriction, whereas B-cell markers such as CD19 and CD20 were negative. These immunophenotypic features are consistent with those described in previously reported cases of solitary plasmacytoma.

The exclusion of multiple myeloma is a critical prerequisite for the diagnosis of solitary plasmacytoma. Current International Myeloma Working Group recommendations advocate comprehensive staging including bone marrow examination, serum and urine monoclonal protein assessment, serum free light-chain analysis, and whole-body imaging using PET-CT or whole-body MRI whenever available [19]. In the present case, systemic evaluation included extensive cross-sectional imaging, bone marrow assessment, serum protein electrophoresis with immunofixation, and urine studies, all of which were negative for systemic involvement. No clinical, biological, or radiological evidence of multiple myeloma was identified during the initial workup or throughout follow-up.

Radiotherapy remains the standard of care for solitary plasmacytoma, given the high radiosensitivity of plasma cell tumors. In a multicenter international analysis including 84 patients, local control rates reached 96%, with improved outcomes observed for radiation doses exceeding 40 Gy [20]. Similarly, a large retrospective series reported 5-year local control rates of approximately 80%-90%, particularly for primary SBP [21]. Earlier studies have also shown that local control may approach 100% when doses ≥45 Gy are delivered, emphasizing the radiosensitivity of plasma cell tumors [22].

Based on these data, most contemporary guidelines recommend total radiation doses ranging between 40 and 50 Gy delivered in conventional fractions, which provide an optimal balance between tumor control and treatment-related toxicity [21]. Furthermore, dose–response analyses suggest that doses above 40 Gy are associated with higher complete remission rates and reduced local relapse, particularly in bulky or head and neck lesions [20].

Despite favorable local control, long-term follow-up is mandatory due to the risk of progression to multiple myeloma. Previous studies indicate that approximately 30%-50% of SBP may evolve into systemic disease, most commonly within the first decade following diagnosis [23]. Consequently, careful surveillance including periodic clinical evaluation, laboratory testing, and imaging when indicated is recommended. The absence of recurrence or systemic progression at 21 months in our patient is encouraging. However, prolonged surveillance remains essential.

This case broadens the radiologic spectrum of plasmacytoma and highlights that, although typically affecting older individuals, it may exceptionally occur in young patients and should be considered in the differential diagnosis of atypical maxillary lesions.

Furthermore, the presence of an unusual mixed osteolytic–osteosclerotic pattern underscores the potential for diagnostic confusion with other maxillofacial tumors, reinforcing the indispensable role of histopathological confirmation.

Finally, this observation supports the effectiveness of radiotherapy in achieving durable local control and emphasizes the importance of a multidisciplinary approach in optimizing diagnostic accuracy and therapeutic outcomes.

Conclusion

This case illustrates a rare maxillary plasmacytoma in an adolescent with atypical mixed osteolytic–osteosclerotic features, posing a significant diagnostic challenge, and underscores the pivotal role of histopathological confirmation and the effectiveness of radiotherapy in achieving durable local control, while highlighting the necessity of long-term surveillance.
