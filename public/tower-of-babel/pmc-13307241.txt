Synergistic Pyroelectric‐Bolometric Coupling in Tellurene Nanowire for Energy‐Efficient Artificial Vision
Authors: Thi Uyen Tran, Ngoc Thanh Duong, Ngoc Linh Nguyen, Nayeong Lee, Minh Chien Nguyen, Koyal Suman Samantaray, Yoonmyung Lee, Seong Chu Lim, Sungjoo Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThermal–energy harvesting, which converts heat into electrical signals, offers a promising route toward sustainable energy utilization. However, achieving efficient and stable thermal–electrical conversion in low‐dimensional materials remains challenging. Although few‐layer Tellurene (Te) exhibits pyroelectricity owing to intrinsic broken symmetry, this effect is confined to the trilayer limit, ‐restricting its practical applications. Here, we demonstrate that Te nanowires (Te NWs) overcome this limitation through edge‐induced symmetry breaking, enabling robust and persistent pyroelectric polarization. Under photothermal excitation, Te NWs exhibit a synergistic pyroelectric–bolometric coupling, wherein photothermal modulation of polarization and resistance cooperatively enhances the thermally driven photocurrent. Without external bias, the device operates in a fully self‐driven mode, exhibiting excitatory synaptic responses governed solely by thermally driven charge dynamics. With biases, the response becomes bidirectional, emulating both excitatory and inhibitory synaptic characteristics. Leveraging these dual mechanisms, a Te NW‐based artificial vision system achieves 97% digit recognition accuracy through light‐driven learning without external electrical bias, demonstrating energy‐efficient, self‐stimulated neuromorphic functionality. This work establishes Te NWs as a promising material platform that bridges thermal–energy harvesting, bioinspired sensing, and self‐stimulated neuromorphic computing.

Introduction

The accelerating demand for sustainable energy has intensified interest in technologies capable of harvesting the abundant yet largely wasted thermal energy. Among various strategies, thermal energy harvesting, which converts otherwise wasted heat into usable electrical energy, has emerged as a compelling approach [1, 2, 3]. One of the most promising mechanisms for such conversion is the pyroelectric effect, in which temporal temperature variations modulate spontaneous polarization and generate an electric current. Pyroelectricity has been widely explored for thermal sensing, energy harvesting, and infrared imaging [4, 5]. In parallel, the bolometric effect–where temperature‐dependent electrical conductance modulates the output current–offers an additional and highly sensitive thermally driven transduction pathway [6, 7].

Despite substantial progress, the development of materials capable of robust, stable, and scalable thermal–electrical conversion is still unresolved. Most existing two‐dimensional (2D) and polymer‐based pyroelectric materials face intrinsic limitations that hinder their practical deployment. For instance, 2D semiconductors such as MoS2 and In2Se3 typically exhibit a low pyroelectric coefficient, resulting in weak electrical output [8, 9]. Ferroelectric polymers such as P(VDF‐TrFE) require strict thickness control and poling processes, while their performance often degrades under repeated thermal cycling or high‐temperature operation [10, 11]. These challenges underscore the need for alternative materials capable of sustaining strong and stable pyroelectric responses under realistic conditions. In this context, 2D Te has emerged as a particularly intriguing candidate owing to its unique helical chain and anisotropic structure, which give rise to distinctive electronic and optical properties [12, 13]. Although pyroelectricity has been observed in 2D Te, it is restricted to the trilayer limit, where inversion symmetry is naturally broken. Beyond this thickness, restored centrosymmetry suppresses the pyroelectric polarization and restricts its utility in practical applications [14].

To address the limitations of low‐dimensional pyroelectrics, we investigate Te NWs, whose boundary‐induced symmetry breaking enables stable spontaneous polarization extending beyond the trilayer limit. These edge‐driven atomic displacements establish Te NWs as a robust pyroelectric platform without stringent thickness constraints. Moreover, the coexisting bolometric response–arising from temperature‐induced resistive modulation–further amplifies the overall photoresponse. Their synergistic coupling produces a markedly enhanced thermally driven photocurrent, presenting a substantial advance for photothermal optoelectronics. Figure 1 illustrates the concept and operating principle of the Te NW–based artificial vision system. Inspired by retinomorphic visual processing in the human eye (Figure 1a), the Te NW system fundamentally operates through thermoelectric transduction, enabling compact and energy‐efficient visual sensing (Figure 1b). In this architecture, sensory information progresses from low‐level optical acquisition to higher‐level processing and recognition (Figure 1c), with the intrinsic synaptic behavior of the Te NW governing temporal stimulus integration and data compression for subsequent processing. This sensory functionality is realized within a single Te NW that exploits its intrinsic pyroelectric–bolometric coupling (Figure 1e). Under optical excitation, the nanowire is predominantly heated by photothermal absorption, producing temperature oscillation, whereas photocarrier generation remains a secondary effect. These temperature variations modulate the spontaneous polarization, producing transient pyroelectric currents (Figure 1d), while the heating‐induced increase in channel resistance (ΔR > 0) contributes an additional bolometric current component (Figure 1f). Through the synergistic action of these two thermally driven mechanisms, the Te NW directly converts light into electrical synaptic signals, effectively unifying sensing, memory, and preprocessing within a single nanoscale element. This integrated capability highlights Te NWs as a compelling materials platform for highly energy‐efficient artificial visual perception.

Results and Discussion

Edge‐Induced Origin of Pyroelectricity in Te NWs

Te NWs were synthesized via a hydrothermal route (see Methods and Figure S1), and their crystalline phase was confirmed by Raman spectroscopy (Figure S2). As shown in Figure S3, Te crystallizes in a chiral trigonal structure composed of helical chains of covalently bonded atoms spiralling along the c‐axis. These chains are arranged in a hexagonal lattice and stabilized by van der Waals interactions, resulting in pronounced electrical and thermal anisotropy [15, 16, 17]. The morphology and cross‐section of the nanowire, characterized by atomic force microscopy (AFM) and high‐resolution scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM), reveal smooth, uniform surfaces and a distinctive chiral chain arrangement of Te atoms (Figure 2a,b). At the nanowire edge, multiple Te atomic layers stack along the e⃗ direction (perpendicular to a–c plane), forming an inclined plane. Notably, within the edge chains, atoms display pronounced off‐center displacements, giving rise to a locally non‐centrosymmetric lattice. These edge‐specific distortions arise from lone‐pair‐driven interchain repulsion, which enlarges the interchain spacing and stabilizes spontaneous polarization [18]. This spontaneous polarization is the prerequisite for pyroelectricity, as thermal fluctuations modulate the polarization and produce a distinct electrical response [1, 2].

To shed light on the origin of the spontaneous polarization at the edge of Te NWs, we conducted density functional theory (DFT) calculations [19, 20] focusing on the off‐center displacement of layer‐center atoms (LCAs) along a‐axis within the inclined edge. As shown in Figure 2d, the four‐layer Te slab exhibits three distinct structural phases–α, β, and α′–corresponding to LCA located to the left, center, and right relative to neighboring atoms along the Te chains. The β‐state adopts the centrosymmetric space group P21/m, whereas the α‐ and α′‐states reduce to the non‐centrosymmetric space group P21, featuring opposite LCA displacements of ± 0.4 Å. Differential charge‐density maps reveal that the α and α′ configurations exhibit markedly asymmetric charge redistribution around the LCAs–where red and blue denote negative and positive charges, respectively–giving rise to a net polarization. In contrast, the β‐state displays a symmetric charge distribution in which positive and negative charge centers cancel, yielding zero polarization. These results indicate that spontaneous polarization originates from edge‐localized off‐center displacements, driven by lone‐pair repulsion and increased interchain spacing [18]. The polarization of the α and α′ states reverses symmetrically about the β phase, arising from symmetry changes in the charge‐density distribution induced by LCA displacement.

Climbing‐image nudged elastic band (NEB) calculations [21] reveal a transition pathway between α and α′ states via the intermediate β‐state, yielding an energy barrier of ∼35 meV per layer and confirming a well‐defined and stable polarization‐switching (Figure 2c). To further assess thermal effects on the polarization landscape, we computed total energies at different LCA displacements for progressively expanded a‐axis lattice constants, assuming that temperature influences the structure primarily through thermal expansion while phonon‐induced vibrational effects remain negligible. As the lattice constant increases, the energy barrier separating the α and α′ polarization states rises, suppressing thermally driven fluctuations and stabilizing the spontaneous polarization (Figure 2e). This barrier enhancement originates from the relative stabilization of the centrosymmetric β‐like configuration under lattice expansion, which raises the relative energy of the polar α and α′ structures. Consequently, while thermal expansion broadens the lattice spacing, the associated increase in the switching barrier reinforces the polarized state, enabling a more robust and stable pyroelectric response under photothermal excitation.

Although our DFT model concentrates on polarization along the a‐axis, the structural symmetry of Te NWs along both the a‐ and b‐axes suggests that polarization components may also exist along the b‐axis. Because the helical chains form a trigonal lattice, the chain stacking near the surface viewed along the b‐axis is symmetry‐equivalent to that along the a‐axis, supporting the possibility of polarization components in both directions. This is supported by piezoresponse force microscopy (PFM) measurements, which reveal surface polarization components in both orientations: an in‐plane response along the a‐axis (Figure 2f,g) and an out‐of‐plane response along the b‐axis (Figure 2h and Figure S4). The in‐plane PFM phase image acquired without external poling (Figure 2f) shows a clear contrast along the nanowire, indicating a built‐in polarization aligned with the a‐axis. Corresponding amplitude and phase hysteresis loops in Figure 2g exhibit the characteristic butterfly‐shaped amplitude profile and 180° phase reversal, confirming the presence of switchable polarization consistent with our theoretical predictions. Complementary out‐of‐plane PFM measurements (Figure 2h) reveal clear amplitude–phase hysteresis loops, providing direct evidence of polarization along the b‐axis and thus confirming the presence of dual‐axis polarization in Te nanowires. Experimental Observation of Pyroelectric Response in Te NWs

To probe the pyroelectric response of Te NW, the device was stimulated by 532 nm laser modulated at controlled frequencies. The resulting transient photocurrent was subsequently amplified and filtered using a preamplifier and recorded by an oscilloscope. The detailed experimental setup is illustrated in Method and Figure S6. As shown in Figure 3a, Te NW exhibits a distinct polarity reversal: a negative current emerges during laser irradiation (heating), followed by a positive current immediately after the laser is switched off (cooling). This signature is characteristic of pyroelectricity, where temperature variations perturb the spontaneous polarization, causing compensating surface charges to redistribute and thereby generate a transient current (Figure 3b). Under isothermal conditions, free charges (electrons or ions) fully screen the bound polarization charges, preventing any current flow. Upon heating, the increase in temperature reduces the net dipole moment and therefore lowers the spontaneous polarization. As a result, compensating surface charges redistribute, generating a transient pyroelectric current. If the system is subsequently cooled, the spontaneous polarization increases, driving an opposite redistribution of compensating charges and producing a current of reversed polarity [1, 2]. To rule out the possible contribution of surface oxygen adsorption/desorption to the transient signals, control measurements were conducted in ambient air and under high vacuum at 300 K. As shown in Figure S7, the transient photocurrent responses exhibit nearly identical polarity reversal, waveform, and pulse‐width dependence in both environments. If oxygen adsorption/desorption dominated the response, significant changes in amplitude or relaxation dynamics would be expected under vacuum due to the absence of surface oxygen. Instead, the persistence of the characteristic bipolar response indicates that the signals originate primarily from intrinsic thermal processes within the Te nanowire, including pyroelectric polarization change driven by dT/dt and temperature‐dependent resistance variation associated with the bolometric effect, rather than surface adsorption effects. Notably, the asymmetric magnitudes of the heating‐ and cooling‐induced currents indicate the presence of an additional thermally activated contribution–most plausibly a bolometric component–during the heating phase. This effect is discussed in detail in Figure 4.

The laser pulse width is a crucial parameter determining the pyroelectric response, as illustrated in Figure 3c. Long pulses (PW = 800 ms) allow the thermal wave to fully diffuse through the nanowire, producing a larger temperature variation and consequently higher current amplitudes. In contrast, short pulses limit thermal diffusion due to rapid heating–cooling cycles, resulting in smaller temperature variations and weaker pyroelectric currents. This behavior reflects the interplay between the pulse duration and the thermal diffusion: longer pulses promote a more complete thermal response, whereas shorter pulses generate sharper but more spatially confined temperature variations [22, 23].

To further elucidate the neuromorphic characteristics of Te NWs, paired‐pulse facilitation (PPF) measurements were performed (Figure 3d,e). PPF is a fundamental form of short‐term synaptic plasticity that enables the temporal processing of sequential stimuli–an essential mechanism for decoding auditory and visual information in biological systems [24]. Two consecutive optical spikes (PW = 200 ms) were applied to the Te NW device with varying inter‐spike intervals (Δt). The PPF ratio, defined as the ratio of the second cooling‐induced pyroelectric current (A2) to the first (A1), reaches a maximum of approximately 4 at Δt = 20 ms and decays monotonically with increasing Δt, exhibiting the typical exponential relaxation in biological synapses. The dependence of PPF on Δt is well fitted by a double‐exponential model [24, 25]: PPF = 1 + C1exp(–x/τ1) + C2exp(–x/τ2), where C1 = 0.7, C2 = 10 represent the initial facilitation magnitudes, and τ1 = 14 ms and τ2 = 1.5 s correspond to the fast and slow relaxation time constants, respectively. These time constants closely match those of biological synapses, confirming that Te NW device effectively mimics the temporal dynamics of neural processing. Under repeated optical stimulation, the device exhibits a progressive conductance enhancement, indicative of short‐term plasticity (Figure 3f). The extracted potentiation current after each stimulus is plotted in Figure 3g, exhibiting a high linearity factor of 0.89. This near‐uniform synaptic weight update is essential for stable, predictable neuromorphic learning, highlighting the robustness of the thermally driven synaptic behavior in Te NWs.

Synergetic Pyroelectric‐Bolometric Coupling in Te NWs

The photoresponse of the Te NW device under applied drain–source bias (Vds) is presented in Figure 4a,b. Introducing an external bias markedly amplifies the response magnitude compared to the unbiased condition. Moreover, the bias polarity governs the direction of the thermally induced current: under positive Vds, negative currents dominate during the heating phase (laser on) and positive currents appear during cooling (laser off), whereas the sequence reverses when a negative Vds is applied. This bias‐dependent inversion primarily arises from the reversal of spontaneous polarization under opposite electric fields, consistent with the polarization switching discussed in Figure 2, with additional contributions from field‐driven carrier drift. These effects together enable directional control of thermally generated charge flow, allowing the device to emulate excitatory and inhibitory synaptic responses– an essential capability for adaptive optoelectronic and neuromorphic systems [26].

The influence of Vds on the response speed is shown in Figure 4c. At zero bias, the photoresponse exhibits rise and decay times of approximately 48 and 22 ms, respectively. Applying an external field accelerates both processes: the rise time decreases to ∼30 ms, and the decay time improves dramatically to ∼130 µs. This enhancement originates from two cooperative mechanisms: (i) the applied electric field lowers potential barrier at the Te NW/metal junction, facilitating faster charge redistribution during heating and cooling, and (ii) the electric‐field‐driven carrier drift accelerates charge transport within the channel, expediting charge extraction and relaxation throughout the thermal cycle [27, 28].

Beyond the pyroelectric contribution, a pronounced asymmetry between the heating and cooling currents reveals that an additional thermally driven process contributes to the overall signal. This asymmetry is attributed to bolometric effect, wherein laser‐induced heating increases the resistivity of the Te NWs. During illumination, the photothermal heating elevates the lattice temperature of the Te NWs, leading to an increase in resistivity because the device operates in the metallic regime at Vgs = 0 V, below the metal‐insulator transition (MIT) occurring near Vgs ≈ 3 V (Figure 4d,e). The temperature–dependent output characteristics (Figure S8) reveal a monotonic decrease in current with increasing temperature, consistent with metallic conduction. The linear I–V behavior further confirms an Ohmic contact, excluding photovoltaic contributions to the photoresponse. This metallic behavior originates from the relatively high intrinsic hole concentration of Te NWs (∼ 1018 cm−3, detailed in Note S1), which mitigates Coulomb scattering, and promotes delocalized carrier transport [29, 30]. The resulting rise in resistance suppresses the intrinsic current, producing a negative bolometric contribution that superimposes with the pyroelectric signal during the heating phase. When cooling begins, the resistance relaxes toward its initial value, diminishing the bolometric contribution and allowing the change in pyroelectric polarization to dominate the transient response. Thus, the pyroelectric and bolometric contributions produce the characteristic current asymmetry, highlighting that polarization dynamics and temperature‐dependent resistance jointly govern the transient photoresponse.

Building on the synergistic coupling between the pyroelectric and bolometric effects discussed above, the synaptic functionality of the Te nanowire (NW) device was examined under repetitive optical stimulation with opposite drain biases (Figure 4f–h). The pyroelectric response generates fast transient currents governed by the temperature change rate (dT/dt), enabling spike‐like sensing under optical excitation, while the bolometric effect introduces temperature‐dependent resistance variation that allows gradual conductance evolution under applied bias. Their cooperative action produces spike‐like responses together with controllable baseline modulation, enabling multilevel conductance states. It is noted that the symmetry of the bipolar photocurrent depends strongly on the optical stimulation conditions, particularly the pulse width and pulse repetition. For a single optical pulse with relatively long duration (Figure 4c), the device undergoes a complete heating–cooling cycle, allowing the temperature to rise and subsequently relax. This results in nearly symmetric bipolar transients. Under short and repetitive optical stimulation used in the neuromorphic measurements (Figure 4f,g), the device temperature does not fully return to equilibrium between pulses, leading to gradual thermal accumulation that enhances the bolometric resistance modulation. Consequently, the transient spikes become superimposed on a slowly drifting baseline current, resulting in asymmetric or apparently unipolar responses during repetitive stimulation. Under a positive bias (Vds = 0.5 V), successive optical pulses gradually induce a gradual increase in current, demonstrating a stepwise potentiation (Figure 4f). Conversely, negative bias (Vds = −0.5 V) yields a progressive current decrease, corresponding to synaptic depression (Figure 4g). The cumulative response over 256 pulses (Figure 4h) exhibits a smooth and symmetric conductance change, with linearity factors of 0.89 for potentiation and 0.87 for depression, indicating nearly uniform weight update. Such high linearity and reproducibility are desirable for accurate weight modulation in neuromorphic networks. These results establish Te NWs as a robust photoactive artificial synapse capable of bias‐programmable potentiation and depression, enabling neuromorphic computing driven by photothermal stimuli.

Te Nanowire Hardware for Light‐Driven Artificial Vision

Building on the demonstrated pyroelectric and bolometric functionalities, we implemented a Te NW‐based retinomorphic vision system that integrates optical/thermal sensing, memory, and processing within a single hardware platform (Figure 5a). The system architecture is inspired by the biological retina, where photoreceptors capture light, bipolar cells perform preliminary signal processing, and ganglion cells convert these signals into spiking outputs transmitted to the brain [31]. Analogously, Te NW unit functions as a photosensitive synapse that directly converts photothermal stimuli into electrical signals with inherent memory, thereby merging sensing and processing functions at the device level. Figure 5b schematically illustrates the image‐to‐optical spike encoding and stimulus configuration, clarifying how optical information is captured, converted, and preprocessed within the device framework. A downsampled 20 × 20 pixel handwritten digit image from the MNIST dataset was segmented into optical spike sequences. Each group of five consecutive pixels, read sequentially from left to right, was transformed into 5‐bit optical pulse trains, where binary “0” and “1” correspond to white and black pixels, respectively, yielding 80 sequential input spikes per image. These optical spike trains were delivered to Te NW synaptic device, which produced distinct transient currents that served as weighted inputs for the neural network. Representative synaptic responses to five encoded spike sequences are presented in Figure 5c, while real‐time current responses are provided in Figure S9. The temporal conductance evolution exhibits linearity and strong history dependence, yielding multiple stable and reproducible conductance states dictated by the input sequence. Since each 5‐bit sequence (25 = 32 possible combinations) generates a unique conductance state, Te NW device realizes 32 distinct final current levels, as shown in Figure 5e. This behavior confirms the multilevel characteristics essential for analog signal encoding and computation. These experimentally obtained current matrices were then supplied to a fully connected neural network (Figure 5d). The network architecture consists of two hidden layers with 100 neurons activated by a sigmoid function and 10 neurons with a linear activation function. The SoftMax classifier computes the probability distribution across ten categories (digits 0–9). The model was trained using 48 000 images for 100 epochs with a batch size of 32. After optimization, the trained network accurately mapped optical inputs to the corresponding digit classes. During the testing phase using 12 000 images, the Te NW retinomorphic system achieved a classification accuracy of 97% (Figure 5f,g). The confusion matrix (Figure 5f) confirms uniformly high recognition accuracy across all digit classes, and the accuracy curve (Figure 5g) demonstrates stable convergence under both unbiased and biased conditions. The full set of 32 output currents and the corresponding biased‐condition confusion matrix are provided in Figure S10. The synergistic pyroelectric–bolometric coupling in our device originates intrinsically from photothermal heating and therefore operates under both unbiased and biased conditions. Evaluating the device under different drain‐bias conditions distinguishes the self‐driven regime (Vds = 0 V) from the bias‐assisted regime (Vds ≠ 0 V), providing insight into how the applied electric field influences signal amplitude, polarity control, and conductance modulation. This comparison clarifies the respective roles of intrinsic photothermal processes and field‐assisted carrier dynamics in determining the retinomorphic computing performance. These results demonstrate that Te NW synaptic device enable reliable light‐driven neural computation, effectively bridging photothermal perception and machine learning within a compact, energy‐efficient hardware platform.

To examine the scalability of the proposed device concept beyond a single‐device demonstration, a one‐dimensional 1 × 5 array of Te NW pyro–bolometric sensors was fabricated (Figure S11). To evaluate array‐level artificial vision using this multi‐device platform, we constructed a customized handwritten‐digit dataset (0–9), where each image is represented by a [5 × 5] pixel matrix (Figure S12a). Each pixel contains two intensity levels, black and gray, which are converted into binary optical pulses (black = 1, laser ON; gray = 0, laser OFF). The pixels are sequentially read column‐wise to generate 5‐bit optical pulse sequences that are applied to a [1 × 5] array, enabling spatiotemporal encoding of visual information (Figure S12b). The photothermal responses from Devices #1–#5 map the spatial pixel information into electrical signals, producing distinct current distributions for different digit images (Figure S13). These encoded current signals were then used as input features for a supervised learning framework based on logistic regression. After 100 training iterations, the classifier achieved an overall recognition accuracy of 80% on random 25 test images, as shown in the confusion matrix (Figure S14). These results demonstrate that the photothermal responses generated by the 1 × 5 Te NW sensor array preserve sufficient spatial information to enable reliable digit recognition, highlighting the feasibility of using pyro–bolometric devices for neuromorphic visual processing.

Conclusions

Our study establishes Te NWs as a robust platform for thermal–electrical energy conversion enabled by the synergistic coupling of pyroelectric and bolometric effects. Under photothermal excitation, spontaneous polarization and electrical conductance are modulated concurrently: edge‐induced symmetry breaking preserves pyroelectric polarization, while temperature‐dependent resistivity dictates the bolometric response. The cooperative interaction of these mechanisms amplifies thermally driven photocurrents and facilitates bidirectional, bias‐controlled excitatory and inhibitory synaptic modulation. Harnessing these functionalities, we demonstrate a retinomorphic vision system that integrates photothermal sensing, memory, and neuromorphic computation, achieving 97% digit recognition accuracy through light‐driven learning without external power. These results not only deepen the understanding of pyroelectric–bolometric coupling in low‐dimensional Te but also highlight its promise as a multifunctional material platform for intelligent, energy‐efficient optoelectronic and sensory technologies.

Methods

Tellurene Synthesis

Te NWs was synthesized via hydrothermal method [32, 33]. 104 mg of sodium tellurite (Na2TeO3) and 0.548 g of polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) were first dissolved in 33 mL of deionized water under vigorous magnetic stirring at room temperature. Subsequently, 3.648 mL of ammonium hydroxide and 1.824 mL of hydrazine monohydrate were added into the solution. The resulting homogeneous mixture was transferred to a teflon‐lined stainless‐steel autoclave and heated at 180°C for 20 h. The silver‐gray precipitate was collected by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 5 min and washed three times with deionized water to eliminate residual impurities. The purified Te NWs were stored in distilled water for subsequent device fabrication.

Device Fabrication

Hexagonal boron nitride (hBN; HQ Graphene) was mechanically exfoliated onto a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) stamp, while Te nanowires (NWs) dispersed in distilled water were drop‐cast onto separate PDMS films. The hBN flakes were first transferred onto SiO2/Si substrates, providing an atomically flat, chemically clean interface. Te NWs were subsequently transferred onto the hBN layer to form the active channel. Metal electrodes were patterned by electron‐beam lithography (EBL), followed by electron‐beam evaporation of Pd/Au (30/80 nm) and lift‐off process to complete the device structure.

Characterization

The morphology of the synthesized Te samples and fabricated devices was examined using optical microscopy (OM, Olympus BX51M). Sample thickness and surface topography were characterized by non‐contact atomic force microscopy (AFM, Park Systems NX‐10). Piezoelectric force microscopy (PFM) measurements were conducted using Cr/Pt‐coated conductive tips to probe polarization behavior. Raman spectra were collected on a WITec Alpha 300 system under 532 nm laser excitation to confirm material quality and vibrational characteristics. Electrical transport measurements were performed at room temperature using a Keithley 4200 parameter analyzer and from 200–300 K using a dual‐channel sourcemeter (Keithley 2636A) integrated within a closed‐cycle refrigerator (CCR). Optical stimulus from a 532 nm laser was modulated by a function generator (33600A), and the transient photoresponse was captured by an oscilloscope (DSOX1102G) after signal amplification and filtering through a preamplifier (SR560). The detailed transient photocurrent setup is provided in Figure S6.

Neural Network Computation

The handwritten digit recognition task was performed using the MNIST dataset, consisting of 48 000 training and 12 000 testing images, each down‐sampled to 20 × 20 pixels to match the optical‐spike encoding scheme. A fully connected feedforward neural network was implemented in Python using the Keras library with a TensorFlow backend. The network consisted of an input layer followed by two hidden layers containing 100 and 10 neurons activated by sigmoid and linear functions, respectively. The output layer consisted of 10 neurons with a SoftMax activation, providing a normalized probability distribution across digit classes (0–9). The SoftMax classifier enabled supervised learning and quantitative evaluation of classification accuracy during training and inference.

DFT Analysis

First‐principles density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed to probe the electronic structure and polarization behavior of a Te slab representative of the surface layers in Te nanowires. The Te (100) surface was modeled using a four‐layer slab constructed in a 1 × 4 supercell, with an 18 Å vacuum region introduced along the out‐of‐plane direction to prevent spurious interactions between periodic images. All calculations were carried out using the Quantum ESPRESSO package [34, 35]. A plane‐wave basis set was employed for the Kohn–Sham wavefunctions and electron density, and the interactions between valence electrons and ionic cores were described using optimized norm‐conserving Vanderbilt (ONCV) pseudopotentials sourced from the Materials Cloud Project [36]. The kinetic‐energy cutoffs were set to 30 Ry for the wavefunctions and 240 Ry for the charge density. Exchange–correlation effects were treated within the PBE functional. All atomic coordinates were relaxed until the Hellmann–Feynman forces on every atom were below 0.001 eV Å−1. Monkhorst–Pack k‐point grids of 5 × 2 × 1 and 10 × 2 × 1 were used for total‐energy and polarization calculations, respectively. The electric polarization of the layered Te slab was computed using the Berry‐phase formalism [37] as implemented in Quantum ESPRESSO. To examine the transition between the two symmetry‐related polarized states, we performed the climbing‐image nudged elastic band (NEB) calculation using linear interpolation approximation for the layer‐center atom (LCA) between the α and α′ configurations. For each interpolated structure, only self‐consistent field (SCF) calculations (without atomic relaxation) were performed to determine the energy barrier and the evolution of the polarization as a function of the LCA displacement.
