Abstract
. The paper investigates quantitative weak mixing of Salem substitutions flows. We prove that for a substitution whose substitution matrix is irreducible over the rationals and the dominant eigenvalue is a Salem number, for almost every suspension flow with a piecewise constant roof function, quantitative weak mixing holds with a rate that is slightly worse than a power of log ⁡ log \log\log . We do not know if this is sharp, but we do show that for any suspension flow of this kind, quantitative weak mixing with a polynomial rate is impossible. Results for specific systems are often much weaker than for “typical” or “generic” ones. In the Appendix we explain how a minor modification of an argument from Bufetov and Solomyak (2014) yields very weak, but nevertheless quantitative weak mixing estimates of log ∗ \log^{*} type for the self-similar suspension flow over a Salem substitution. Simultaneously this provides first quantitative decay rates for the Fourier transform of Salem Bernoulli convolutions.

Introduction

In this paper we study ergodic-theoretic and spectral properties of substitution dynamical systems, more specifically, suspension flows over Salem type
substitutions. Substitutions and associated dynamical systems appear in many different contexts, in particular, in the study of interval exchange
transformations with a periodic Rauzy induction, translation flows along stable/unstable foliations of pseudo-Anosov diffeormorphisms, see
[
14
]
.
as well as in
mathematics of quasicrystals and “aperiodic order,” see
[
4
]
.

The basic definitions on substitutions may be found in the standard references
[
30
,
31
]
. Consider an alphabet with
d\geq 2
symbols, denoted by
{\mathcal{A}}=\{1,\dots,d\}
. Let
{\mathcal{A}}^{+}
represent the set of all nonempty words composed of letters from
{\mathcal{A}}
.
A
substitution
is a mapping
\zeta:{\mathcal{A}}\to{\mathcal{A}}^{+}
, which extends naturally to
{\mathcal{A}}^{+}
and
{\mathcal{A}}^{{\mathbb{Z}}}
by concatenation. The
substitution space
consists of all bi-infinite sequences
x\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathbb{Z}}
such that every word appearing in
x
is a subword of
\zeta^{n}(a)
, for some
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
and some
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. The
substitution dynamical system
is given by the left shift on
{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathbb{Z}}
, restricted to
X_{\zeta}
, and is denoted by
T
.

The
substitution matrix

{\sf S}_{\zeta}=({\sf S}(i,j))
is the
d\times d
matrix where
{\sf S}(i,j)
represents the number of times the symbol
i
appears in
\zeta(j)
. The substitution is called
primitive
if there exists some
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that all entries of
{\sf S}_{\zeta}^{n}
are strictly positive.
It is well known that the
{\mathbb{Z}}
-action given by the left-shift on a primitive substitution subshift is minimal and uniquely ergodic; see
[
31
]
. We assume that the substitution is both primitive and
non-periodic
, which, in the primitive case, is equivalent to the space
X_{\zeta}
being infinite.

The continuous counterpart comes from considering suspensions over
X_{\zeta}
.
Namely, we consider
{\mathbb{R}}
-actions obtained as
suspension (or special) flows over
(X_{\zeta},T)
, with a piecewise-constant roof function depending only on the 1st symbol. Such a function
may be identified with a positive
suspension vector

\vec{s}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+}
, see the next section for details.
Substitution
{\mathbb{R}}
-actions of this kind appear in the study of translation flows and as tiling dynamical systems on the line.
Special choices for the suspension vector are
\vec{s}={\bf 1}
(the vector of
1
’s), and
\vec{s}=
the right PF (Perron–Frobenius) eigenvector of
{\sf S}_{\zeta}^{\sf T}
. With the first choice, we essentially recover the substitution subshift. The second choice is referred to as the
self-similar
suspension flow.

By a result of Dekking–Keane
[
15
]
and Clark–Sadun
[
13
]
, substitution systems and their suspensions are never strongly
mixing, however, they may be weakly mixing, see
[
19
]
for an algebraic characterization. Here our focus is on the property of

quantitative weak mixing
(QWM), which has been intensively studied recently.

Weak mixing is a fundamental property of measure-preserving systems; it has several equivalent characterizations, see, e.g.,
[
16
]
. One of them is absence of non-trivial discrete spectrum; equivalently, the spectral measure
\sigma_{f}
corresponding to any
L^{2}
-function of mean zero has no point masses.
Given a weakly mixing system, it is natural to ask what is the rate of decay of the spectral measure of a small neighborhood of a point, as the radius tends to zero, for a
sufficiently “nice” test function
f
. Another
characterization of weak mixing is in terms of absolute Cesàro averages: a probability preserving flow
(X,\phi_{t},\mu)
is weakly mixing if and only if

\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{R}\int_{0}^{R}\left|\langle f\circ\phi_{t},g\rangle\right|^{2}dt\to 0,\ \ \mbox{for all}\ f\in L^{2}_{0}(X),\ g\in L^{2}(X).

The flow is said to have the property of
QWM with a rate
h
for some increasing function
h:[0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)
,
h(0)=0
, if for all
f\in L^{2}_{0}(X)
sufficiently “nice” and

g\in L^{2}(X)
,

(1.1)

\frac{1}{R}\int_{0}^{R}\left|\langle f\circ\phi_{t},g\rangle\right|^{2}dt\leq C_{f,g}\cdot h(R^{-1}),

see
[
25
]
.
If this holds with
h(r)=r^{\gamma}
, we say that QWM holds with a Hölder rate
\gamma
, for
h(r)=(\log(1/r))^{-\gamma}
we say it is
\log
-Hölder, etc.

Remark 1.1
.

It is known that spectral bounds of the form

(1.2)

\sigma_{f}(B_{r}(\omega))\leq C_{f}h(r),\ \ \omega\in{\mathbb{R}},\ r\in(0,r_{0}),\ \ \int f\,d\mu=0,

imply QWM with the same rate, for the same
f
. (It is essential that (
1.2
) be
uniform in the spectral parameter

\omega
.)
This was shown by Strichartz
[
34
]
for
h(r)=r^{\alpha}
, and extended by Last
[
26
]
; see
Knill
[
25
]
. In the reverse direction, it was verified in
[
26
,
25
]
that QWM with the rate
h^{2}
implies (
1.2
).

Let us return to the primitive substitution
\zeta
. The Perron-Frobenius (PF) eigenvalue of
{\sf S}_{\zeta}
,
which we denote by
\alpha
, plays an important role. Assume, for simplicity, that
{\sf S}_{\zeta}
is irreducible over
{\mathbb{Q}}
. If
\alpha
is a
Pisot number
,
which means that all the other eigenvalues of
{\sf S}_{\zeta}
are inside the open unit disk, then the substitution
{\mathbb{Z}}
-action and suspension flows over it have a
large discrete component, hence not weakly mixing, see
[
13
]
(conjecturally, they are
pure discrete
, see
[
2
]
).
The case when the second-largest eigenvalue satisfies
|\alpha_{2}|>1
was studied in
[
5
,
7
]
:
under this assumption, QWM with a Hölder rate was obtained for Lebesgue almost every suspension vector
\vec{s}
.
(There it was assumed that the test function is a “cylindrical function of level zero,” see the next section for definitions, which is, in a sense, the “nicest” function possible. In fact, this can be upgraded to the case of “weakly Lipschitz” test functions, see
[
9
]
.) In
[
6
]
and
[
8
]
analogous results were
obtained for almost every translation flow on a flat surface of genus 2. Forni
[
20
]
,
using a different method, obtained Hölder QWM for translation flows for an arbitrary genus
\geq 2
. After seeing his preprint, Bufetov and the second-named
author obtained in
[
9
]
similar results, using a “symbolic approach”. More recently, Avila, Forni, and Safaee
[
3
]
established Hölder QWM
for almost all interval exchange transformations (IET’s) of non-rotation type. Significantly,
all of these results on QWM rely on the second Lyapunov exponent being positive, either
for the Kontsevich-Zorich, or for the Rauzy-Zorich cocycle — the property which corresponds to
|\alpha_{2}|>1
in the substitution case.

There are some isolated results which do not fall into the above scheme: Moll
[
28
]
obtained power-log QWM for a substitution conjugate to the
Chacon map, for which
{\sf S}_{\zeta}
has an eigenvalue 1; in the IET setting this is related to another result in
[
3
]
, namely, power-log QWM for
almost all rotation class IET’s that are not rotations.

In this paper we obtain first quantitative estimates for suspension flows over
Salem type
substitutions, that is, when

\alpha
is a Salem number. Recall
that this means
\alpha
is an algebraic integer, whose conjugates satisfy
|\alpha_{j}|\leq 1
, with at least one of them on the unit circle.
This is a “borderline” case, interesting from several points of view and generally more difficult to analyze. Our main result, which we state
precisely in the next section, asserts that for Lebesgue a.e. suspension flow over a Salem type substitution, holds QWM with a rate that is a
power of
\log\log
, up to a
\log\log\log
correction, namely, with a function

(1.3)

h_{\beta}(r):=\exp\left(-\frac{\beta\log\log\log_{\alpha}(1/2r)}{\log\log\log\log_{\alpha}(1/2r)}\right).

Here “
\log
” denotes the logarithm to base 2. On the other hand, we show that for
any
suspension vector
\vec{s}
, the resulting
{\mathbb{R}}
-action cannot
have Hölder QWM.

As a rule, it is much harder to obtain results for a particular system than for a “typical” or “generic” one. In the case of a specific substitution with

|\alpha_{2}|>1
it was shown in
[
5
]
that QWM holds with a log-Hölder rate for the self-similar suspension flow. Recently, analogous results were
obtained for the substitution
{\mathbb{Z}}
-action, see
[
10
]
. For Salem type substitutions, we are not aware of any QWM results in the literature.
Considering the self-similar suspension flow over a Salem substitution, the first-named author obtained in
[
27
]
Hölder spectral bounds of the form (
1.2
), with
h(r)=r^{\gamma(\omega)}
, but only for algebraic spectral parameters
\omega\in{\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)
and with
r_{0}=r_{0}(\omega)
. This was extended
to the case of
{\mathbb{Z}}
-actions in
[
10
]
as well.
These bounds are, unfortunately, not strong enough to yield any QWM rate.
It turns out that a very weak,
\log^{*}
QWM rate for the self-similar suspension flow may be deduced essentially by the same method as in
[
5
]
.
Although we do not expect it to be sharp by any means, arguably, any quantitative rate is better than no rate. We sketch the derivation of this
result in the
Appendix.

A connection between spectral bounds for substitution flows and the decay rate of the Fourier transform for Bernoulli convolution measures (a much-studied
class of self-similar “fractal” measures on the line, see
[
29
,
35
]
), was pointed out
in
[
5
]
. In the Appendix we recall this connection and note that the proof of QWM with a
\log^{*}
rate also yields

\log^{*}
Fourier decay for Salem Bernoulli convolutions.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In the next section we present the background in more detail and state our main result. In Section 3 we prove the
main theorem, and in Section 4 we show the absence of uniform Hölder bounds. We conclude in the Appendix as mentioned above.

2.
Background and statement of results

2.1.
Suspension flows.

Let
\zeta
be a primitive non-periodic substitution on
{\mathcal{A}}=\{1,\dots,d\}
, with a substitution matrix
{\sf S}_{\zeta}
.
We obtain the substitution dynamical system
(X_{\zeta},T)
, which is minimal and uniquely ergodic, with a invariant Borel probability measure

\mu
. Recall the definition of suspension flows
Let
\vec{s}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{d})\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}
and
F:X_{\zeta}\times{\mathbb{R}}\longrightarrow X_{\zeta}\times{\mathbb{R}}
defined by
F(x,t)=(T(x),t-s_{x_{0}})
. The
suspension flow with the roof vector

\vec{s}
, is defined as

X^{\vec{s}}_{\zeta}=(X_{\zeta}\times{\mathbb{R}})/\sim

where
\sim
is the equivalence relation defined by
(x,t)\sim(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})
if and only if
F^{n}(x,t)=(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})
, for some
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. There is a natural
{\mathbb{R}}
-action on
X^{\vec{s}}_{\zeta}
given by the “vertical” flow
(\phi_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
:
\phi_{t}(x,t^{\prime})=(x,t^{\prime}+t)\,(\text{mod}\sim)
. From now on we identify
X^{\vec{s}}_{\zeta}
with the
fundamental domain
of points
(x,t)\in X_{\zeta}\times{\mathbb{R}}
such that
0\leq t<s_{x_{0}}
.
The
{\mathbb{R}}
-action
(X^{\vec{s}}_{\zeta},\phi_{t})
is uniquely ergodic as well, with the unique invariant Borel probability measure
\widetilde{\mu}_{\vec{s}}

defined,
up to normalization, as

\mu|_{[a]}\times{\mathcal{L}}^{1}|_{[0,s_{a}]}
, when restricted to
[a]\times[0,s_{a}]
,
where
{\mathcal{L}}^{1}
is the Lebesgue measure on the line,
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
, and
[a]
denotes the cylinder set.

We say that a function
f\in L^{2}(X_{\zeta},\mu)
is
cylindrical of level 0
if it depends only on
x_{0}
, the 0-th term of the sequence
x\in X_{\zeta}
. Cylindrical functions of level 0 form a
d
-dimensional vector space, with a basis

\{{1\!\!1}_{[a]}:\,a\in{\mathcal{A}}\}
. Denote by
{\rm Cyl}(X_{\zeta})
the space of cylindrical functions of level zero.

Lip-cylindrical functions
for a suspension flow are defined by

(2.1)

f(x,t)=\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{A}}}{1\!\!1}_{[j]}\cdot\psi_{j}(t),\ \ \ \mbox{where}\ \ \psi_{j}\ \mbox{is Lipschitz on}\ [0,s_{j}].

Denote by
\text{Lip}(X^{\vec{s}}_{\zeta})
the space of Lip-cylindrical functions on
X^{\vec{s}}_{\zeta}
.

2.2.
Spectral theory: general flows

Let
(X,\phi_{t},\nu)
be any probability-preserving
{\mathbb{R}}
-action.
Recall that for
f,g\in L^{2}(X,\nu)
the (complex) spectral measure
\nu_{f,g}
on
{\mathbb{R}}
is determined by the Fourier transform:

\displaystyle\widehat{\nu}_{f,g}(-t)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}e^{2\pi it\omega}\,d\nu_{f,g}(\omega)=\langle f\circ\phi_{t}\,,g\rangle,\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}},

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the scalar product in
L^{2}(X,\nu)
. We write
\nu_{f}=\nu_{f,f}
.

Closely related to spectral measures are
twisted Birkhoff integrals
. For
R\geq 1
,
f\in L^{2}(X,\nu)
,

\omega\in{\mathbb{R}}
, and
y\in X
, it is defined as

S^{f}_{R}(y,\omega)=\int_{0}^{R}f(\phi_{t}y)e^{2\pi it\omega}dt.

Set
G_{R}(f,\omega)=\dfrac{1}{R}\|{S^{f}_{R}(\cdot,\omega)}\|^{2}_{{}_{L^{2}(\nu)}}
.
The next lemma is essentially from
[
22
]
, see
[
5
, Lemma 4.3]
for a proof.

Lemma 2.1
.

For all
\omega\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
r\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
we have

(2.2)

\sigma_{f}(B_{r}(\omega))\leq\frac{\pi^{2}}{4R}G_{R}(f,\omega),\ \ \ \mbox{with}\ \ R=(2r)^{-1}.

2.3.
Spectral theory: substitution flows

A useful general bound for twisted Birkhoff integrals in the context of substitution
{\mathbb{R}}
-actions was obtained in
[
5
, Prop. 4.4]
and

[
7
, Prop. 3.2(ii)]
. In order to state it, we need to introduce another definition.

A word
v\in{\mathcal{A}}^{+}
is called a
return word
for
\zeta
if
v
starts with a letter
c
for some
c\in{\mathcal{A}}
and
vc
is admissible for the language of
X_{\zeta}
. From the uniform recurrence of
(X_{\zeta},T)
it follows that the set of
irreducible
return words (i.e., those which cannot be
split into a concatenation of shorter return word) is finite and non-empty.
A word
v
is called a
good return word
for the substitution
\zeta
if
v
starts with some letter
c\in{\mathcal{A}}
and

vc
occurs as a subword in
\zeta(b)
for every
b\in{\mathcal{A}}
. Denote the set of good return words by
GR(\zeta)
.
Denote by

\ell(v)=[\ell(v)_{j}]_{j\leq d}
the
population vector
of
v\in{\mathcal{A}}^{+}
, where
\ell(v)_{j}
is the number of letters
j
in
v
.

Proposition 2.2

(
[
7
, Prop. 3.2(ii)]
)
.

There exist
\lambda\in(0,1)
,
C_{1}>0
and depending only on the substitution
\zeta
, such that for all
f\in{\rm Lip}({X^{\vec{s}}_{\zeta}})
,

\left|S^{f}_{R}((x,t),\omega)\right|\leq C_{1}\|f\|_{\infty}\cdot\min\{1,|\omega|^{-1}\}\cdot R\prod_{n=0}^{\lfloor\log_{\alpha}R\rfloor}\left(1-\lambda\max_{v\in GR(\zeta)}{\big\|\bigl\langle{({\sf S}_{\zeta}^{\sf T})}^{n}\omega\vec{s},\ell(v)\bigr\rangle\bigr\|}^{2}_{{}_{{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}}}\right),

for all
R>1
,
(x,t)\in{X^{\vec{s}}_{\zeta}}
, and
\omega\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

(In
[
7
, Prop. 3.2(ii)]
the product is up to
\lfloor\log_{\alpha}R\rfloor-C_{2}
, for some
C_{2}>0
depending on
\zeta
, but this can be
“absorbed” into
C_{1}
since
\lambda<1
and
\|z\|\leq\frac{1}{2}
for all
z\in{\mathbb{R}}
.)

Consider the Abelian group
\Gamma
(a subgroup of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d})
generated by the population vectors of all return words for
X_{\zeta}
.
We have
{\sf S}_{\zeta}\Gamma\subset\Gamma
,
hence the real span of
\Gamma
is a rational
{\sf S}_{\zeta}
-invariant subspace. By assumption,
{\sf S}_{\zeta}
is irreducible, hence
\Gamma
has full rank, i.e.,
it is a lattice. Passing to a sufficiently high power of the substitution, which does not change the substitution space,
we can assume that every “elementary” return word (i.e., not a concatenation
of shorter return words) is a good return
word, and we obtain from Proposition
2.2
, combined with
[
10
, Lemma 4.2]
:

(2.3)

\left|S^{f}_{R}((x,t),\omega)\right|\leq C_{1}\|f\|_{\infty}\cdot\min\{1,|\omega|^{-1}\}\cdot R\prod_{n=0}^{\lfloor\log_{\alpha}R\rfloor}\left(1-\lambda\cdot\bigl\|({\sf S}_{\zeta}^{\sf T})^{n}\omega\vec{s}\bigr\|^{2}_{{}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/\Gamma^{*}}}\right),\ R>1.

2.4.
Statement of results

Let
\zeta
be a substitution of Salem type, i.e., the PF eigenvalue of the substitution matrix
{\sf S}_{\zeta}

is a Salem number and
{\sf S}_{\zeta}
is irreducible over
{\mathbb{Q}}
. Our main result is as follows.

Theorem 2.3
.

There exists
\beta=\beta(\zeta)>0
such that
for Lebesgue-a.e.
\vec{s}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+}
, for every Lip-cylindrical function
f
of mean zero, the spectral measure

\sigma_{f}
of the suspension flow
({X^{\vec{s}}_{\zeta}},\phi_{t},\widetilde{\mu}_{\vec{s}})
satisfies

(2.4)

\sigma_{f}(B_{r}(\omega))\leq\widetilde{C}_{\zeta,\vec{s}}\|f\|^{2}_{\infty}\cdot h_{\beta}(r),\ \ r>0,\ \ \ \mbox{where}\ \ h_{\beta}(r)=\exp\left(-\frac{\beta\log\log\log_{\alpha}(1/2r)}{\log\log\log\log_{\alpha}(1/2r)}\right),

for all
\omega\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Thus the system has QWM with the rate
h_{\beta}(r)
for such functions
f
.

We do not know if this is sharp; QWM with a log-Hölder rate seems more plausible. In any case, we know that Hölder QWM rate is impossible
for any
\vec{s}
, see the next theorem.
The idea of the proof of Theorem
2.3
is based on a variant of the Erdős-Kahane technique, in the framework of
“vector expansions in base
{\sf S}_{\zeta}
”, developed in

[
10
]
. As in many other recent papers on QWM, the proof proceeds via (
2.3
), followed by Lemma
2.1
. Thus we need to consider
the distance from
({\sf S}_{\zeta}^{\sf T})^{n}\omega\vec{s}
to the nearest point in the lattice
\Gamma^{*}
. We “win”, so to say, if this distance is not too small
some of the time, in a quantitative way, which has to be uniform in the spectral parameter
\omega
.
However, we do have some freedom in the choice of the roof vector
\vec{s}
, since we are
allowed to throw away a set of measure zero.

Theorem 2.4
.

For any substitution
\zeta
of Salem type on
d
symbols, for any
\vec{s}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and any
\alpha>0
, for almost every Lip-cylindrical function
f

of mean zero, for any
C_{f}
, the inequality (
1.2
) is violated for
h(r)=r^{\alpha}
, for a sequence of
R=R_{n}\to\infty
.

The proof of Theorem
2.4
is analogous to the proof of
[
10
, Theorem 2.7]
, which deals with the cases when

\vec{s}=\vec{1}
and when
\vec{s}
is the PF eigenvector.

3.
Proof of the Theorem
2.3

3.1.
The set-up

By assumption, we have a substitution
\zeta
on
d
symbols with an irreducible over
{\mathbb{Q}}
substitution matrix
{\sf S_{\zeta}}
having a Salem number

\alpha
as the PF eigenvalue. The other eigenvalues are the Galois conjugates of
\alpha=\alpha_{1}
: they are

\alpha_{2}=\alpha^{-1}
, and the rest are
\alpha_{3},\ldots,\alpha_{d}
, complex conjugate pairs
of modulus one.
Fix a height vector
\vec{s}
and thus the corresponding suspension flow
({X^{\vec{s}}_{\zeta}},\phi_{t},\widetilde{\mu}_{\vec{s}})
.
Let
A={\sf S_{\zeta}^{T}}
and we consider its action on
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
.
The matrix
A
is diagonalizable over
{\mathbb{C}}
; we are going to use the decomposition of (real) vectors in terms of the
(complex) basis of eigenvectors of
A
. Let
\{{\bf e}^{*}_{j}\}_{j\leq d}
be a basis consisting of eigenvectors for the transpose matrix
A^{\sf T}
, and let

\{{\bf e}_{j}\}_{j\leq d}
be the dual basis, i.e.,
\langle{\bf e}_{k},{\bf e}_{j}^{*}\rangle=\delta_{kj}
. If
{\mathbf{x}}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}c_{k}{\bf e}_{k}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
, then

c_{k}=\langle x,{\bf e}_{k}^{*}\rangle
and

(3.1)

\langle A^{n}{\mathbf{x}},{\bf e}_{k}^{*}\rangle=\alpha_{k}^{n}c_{k},\ \ k=1,\ldots,d.

Fix
B>1
and then
\omega\neq 0
, so that
|\omega|\in[B^{-1},B]
.
Consider the lattice
L:=\Gamma^{*}<{\mathbb{R}}^{d}<{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
, where
\Gamma
is generated by the population vectors of return words,
see Section 2.3.
Note that
L
depends only on
\zeta
, and

AL\subset L
. Below we will use
\ell^{\infty}
metric and matrix norm, unless stated otherwise.

We will run the scheme of
[
10
, Section 3]
, replacing the vector
\vec{1}
by
\vec{s}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+}
, namely,
we write

A^{n}\omega\vec{s}={\bf p}_{n}+\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}_{n},\ \ n\geq 0,

where
{\bf p}_{n}
is the nearest lattice point, so that

\|\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}_{n}\|=\bigl\|A^{n}\omega\vec{s}\bigr\|_{{}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/\Gamma^{*}}}=\bigl\|({\sf S}_{\zeta}^{\sf T})^{n}\omega\vec{s}\bigr\|_{{}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/\Gamma^{*}}}.

Note that
{\bf p}_{n}
and
\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}_{n}
depend on
\omega
.

In order to keep the paper self-contained, we repeat the first steps of
[
10
, Section 3]
.
Define

{\mathbf{z}}_{0}:=-{\bf p}_{0},\ \ {\mathbf{z}}_{n}:=A{\bf p}_{n-1}-{\bf p}_{n},\ \ n\geq 1.

We have
A({\bf p}_{n-1}+\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}_{n-1})={\bf p}_{n}+\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}_{n}
, hence

(3.2)

{\mathbf{z}}_{n}=A{\bf p}_{n-1}-{\bf p}_{n}=\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}_{n}-A\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}_{n-1}\in L,\ \ n\geq 1.

The following is now immediate:

Lemma 3.1
.

Let

{\|\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}_{n}\|}\leq b_{L}\ \ \mbox{for all}\ \ n\geq 0

(ii)

(iii)

In view of (

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}_{n}={\mathbf{z}}_{n}+A\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}_{n-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbf{z}}_{n}+A({\mathbf{z}}_{n-1}+A\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}_{n-2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbf{z}}_{n}+A{\mathbf{z}}_{n-1}+A^{2}\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}_{n-2}=\ldots

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbf{z}}_{n}+A{\mathbf{z}}_{n-1}+\cdots+A^{n-1}{\mathbf{z}}_{1}+A^{n}\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}_{0},

hence

\omega\vec{s}=-{\mathbf{z}}_{0}-A^{-1}{\mathbf{z}}_{1}-A^{-2}{\mathbf{z}}_{2}-\cdots-A^{-n}{\mathbf{z}}_{n}+A^{-n}\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}_{n},\ \ n\geq 1,

since

We view (

Denote

\omega\langle\vec{s},{\bf e}_{j}^{*}\rangle=\Phi^{(j)}_{n}(1/\alpha_{j})+\frac{\langle\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}_{n},{\bf e}_{j}^{*}\rangle}{\alpha_{j}^{n}},\ \ j=1,\ldots,d,

(3.5)

|\langle\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}_{n},{\bf e}_{j}^{*}\rangle|\leq C_{0}\|\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}_{n}\|\leq C_{1}=C_{1}(A,L)\ \ \mbox{for all}\ \ n,j.

Thus

\zeta

We can assume that

is a positive PF eigenvector, hence

\omega=\Phi^{(1)}_{n}(1/\alpha)+\frac{\langle\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}_{n},{\bf e}_{1}^{*}\rangle}{\alpha^{n}}.

We obtain

(3.7)

\langle\vec{s},{\bf e}_{3}^{*}\rangle=\frac{\Phi^{(3)}_{n}(1/\alpha_{3})+\frac{\langle\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}_{n},{\bf e}_{3}^{*}\rangle}{\alpha_{3}^{n}}}{\omega}=\frac{\Phi^{(3)}_{n}(1/\alpha_{3})+\frac{\langle\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}_{n},{\bf e}_{3}^{*}\rangle}{\alpha_{3}^{n}}}{\Phi^{(1)}_{n}(1/\alpha)+\frac{\langle\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}_{n},{\bf e}_{1}^{*}\rangle}{\alpha^{n}}}\,

\displaystyle\left|\langle\vec{s},{\bf e}_{3}^{*}\rangle-\frac{\Phi^{(3)}_{n}(1/\alpha_{3})}{\Phi^{(1)}_{n}(1/\alpha)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{|\langle\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}_{n},{\bf e}_{3}^{*}\rangle|}{\omega}+\frac{|\Phi^{(3)}_{n}(1/\alpha_{3})|\cdot|\langle\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}_{n},{\bf e}_{1}^{*}\rangle|}{\omega\alpha^{n}|\Phi^{(1)}_{n}(1/\alpha)|}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle B|\langle\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}_{n},{\bf e}_{3}^{*}\rangle|+\frac{C_{1}C_{2}B(n+1)}{\alpha^{n}(B^{-1}-C_{1}\alpha^{-n})},

where

.

Note that
{\bf e}_{3}^{*}
is a complex vector; the inequality (

Identical inequalities are obtained if we use the eigenvalue
\overline{\alpha}_{3}

.
Let
|{\mathbf{x}}|_{3}:=|\langle{\mathbf{x}},{\bf e}_{3}^{*}\rangle|

|A{\mathbf{x}}|_{3}=|{\mathbf{x}}|_{3}

Recall that we have

(3.9)

{\mathbf{z}}_{n}={\bf 0}\ \implies\ \boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}_{n}=A\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}_{n-1}\ \implies{|\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}_{n}|}_{3}={|\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}_{n-1}|}_{3}.

Further, let

be the projection, commuting with
A
, onto
the
real

A

acts a rotation, with eigenvalues
\alpha_{3}
and

.

3.2.
The main proposition

The estimate (

Proposition 3.3
.

There exists
\beta=\beta(\zeta)>0
such that for Lebesgue-a.e.
\vec{s}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+}

for every Lip-cylindrical function

\sigma_{f}(B_{r}(\omega))\leq C_{\zeta,\vec{s}}\|f\|^{2}_{\infty}\cdot\min\{1,|\omega|^{-2}\}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{\beta\log\log\log_{\alpha}(1/2r)}{\log\log\log\log_{\alpha}(1/2r)}\right),\ \ r\leq(2R_{0})^{-1},

for all
\omega\neq 0
, where

(3.11)

R_{0}=\alpha^{2^{\scriptstyle{\lfloor\gamma\log(B)/(\log\log B)\rfloor}!}},\ \ \mbox{with}\ \ \gamma=\gamma(\zeta,\vec{s})\ \ \mbox{and}\ \ B=\max\{16,\lceil|\omega|\rceil,\lceil|\omega|^{-1}\rceil\}.

Denote

(3.12)

\Psi(\tau):=\frac{\log\tau}{\log\log\tau},

where
\tau\geq 4
,
so that the formula for

in (

(3.13)

R_{0}=\alpha^{2^{\scriptstyle{\lfloor\gamma\cdot\Psi(B)\rfloor!}}}.

Proof.

By “scale

” we mean the range

.
It is convenient to consider the sequence of scales

so that
n_{1}=2,n_{2}=2^{2},n_{3}=2^{6},\ldots

Now we define the exceptional set. Recall that the sequences
{\mathbf{z}}_{n}
and
\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}_{n}
depend on
\omega
and
\vec{s}
. First we define the “bad set at scale
n_{k}
” as follows:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{k}={\mathcal{E}}_{k}(B)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\vec{s}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+},\ \langle\vec{s},{\bf e}_{1}^{*}\rangle=1:\ \exists\,\omega\neq 0,\ |\omega|\in[B^{-1},B],\ \ \mbox{satisfying:}\right.

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \mbox{(E1)}\ \ \#\{\ell\leq n_{k},\ {\mathbf{z}}_{\ell}\neq 0\}<k/2;

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \mbox{(E2)}\ \ {\mathbf{z}}_{\ell}=0\ \mbox{for all}\ \ell\in(n_{k-1},n_{k});

\displaystyle\left.\ \ \ \ \ \ \mbox{(E3)}\ \ |\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}_{n_{k-1}}|_{3}<(2k/n_{k})^{1/2}\right\}.

And then let

(3.14)

{\mathcal{E}}={\mathcal{E}}(\Upsilon)=\bigcap_{k_{0}=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{B=16}^{\infty}\ \bigcup_{k=k_{0}+\lfloor\Upsilon\cdot\Psi(B)\rfloor}^{\infty}{\mathcal{E}}_{k}(B).

The parameter
\Upsilon\geq 1
will be fixed later.
Because of our normalization, admissible roof vectors
\vec{s}
lie in
a
(d-1)
-dimensional simplex in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+}
. Denote by

the
r
-dimensional Lebesgue measure.

Lemma 3.4
.

There exists
\Upsilon\geq 1
such that
{\mathcal{L}}^{d-1}({\mathcal{E}}(\Upsilon))=0
.

Proof.

We write
{\mathcal{E}}={\mathcal{E}}(\Upsilon)
, but keep the dependence on
\Upsilon
in mind.
In is enough to show that
{\mathcal{L}}^{2}({\rm Proj}_{3}({\mathcal{E}}))=0
. In view of Remark
3.2
, this is equivalent to
{\mathcal{L}}^{2}({\rm Pr}_{3}({\mathcal{E}}))=0
, where
{\rm Pr}_{3}({\mathbf{x}})=\langle{\mathbf{x}},{\bf e}_{3}^{*}\rangle\in{\mathbb{C}}
. Clearly,

(3.15)

{\rm Pr}_{3}({\mathcal{E}})\subseteq\bigcap_{k_{0}=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{B=16}^{\infty}\ \bigcup_{k=k_{0}+\lfloor\Upsilon\cdot\Psi(B)\rfloor}^{\infty}{\rm Pr}_{3}({\mathcal{E}}_{k}(B)).

Thus it suffices to get an efficient cover of the set of projections
{\rm Pr}_{3}({\mathcal{E}}_{k}(B))
for
k
sufficiently large.
Let
\vec{s}\in{\mathcal{E}}_{k}(B)
. This means that there exists
\omega
with
|\omega|\in[B^{-1},B]
, such that the properties (E1)-(E3) hold.
We fix such an
\omega
and use (
3.8
) for
n=n_{k-1}
:

(3.16)

\displaystyle\left|\langle\vec{s},{\bf e}_{3}^{*}\rangle-\frac{\Phi^{(3)}_{n_{k-1}}(1/\alpha_{3})}{\Phi^{(1)}_{n_{k-1}}(1/\alpha)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle B|\langle\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}_{n_{k-1}},{\bf e}_{3}^{*}\rangle|+\frac{C_{1}C_{2}B(n_{k-1}+1)}{\alpha^{n_{k-1}}(B^{-1}-C_{1}\alpha^{-n_{k-1}})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle B^{2}\left(\frac{2k}{n_{k}}\right)^{1/2}+\frac{C_{1}C_{2}B(n_{k-1}+1)}{\alpha^{n_{k-1}}(B^{-1}-C_{1}\alpha^{-n_{k-1}})},

where the second inequality follows from
B\geq 2
and from (E3).

We want to make sure that the second term in (
3.16
) is dominated by the first one.
Note that if
\alpha^{n_{k-1}}\geq 2BC_{1}
, then

(3.17)

\frac{C_{1}C_{2}B(n_{k-1}+1)}{\alpha^{n_{k-1}}(B^{-1}-C_{1}\alpha^{-n_{k-1}})}\leq\frac{C_{1}C_{2}B^{2}(n_{k-1}+1)}{\alpha^{n_{k-1}}}.

We have
n_{k-1}=2^{(k-1)!}\geq k^{2}
for
k\geq 4
, hence
\alpha^{k^{2}}\geq 2BC_{1}
will guarantee (
3.17
) for
k\geq 4
.
In the definition (
3.14
) we have

k\geq\Upsilon\cdot\frac{\log B}{\log\log B}\geq\Upsilon\cdot(\log B)^{1/2}
for
B\geq 16
, hence

\alpha^{k^{2}}\geq\alpha^{\Upsilon^{2}\log B}=B^{\Upsilon^{2}\log\alpha}\geq 2BC_{1},

for
\Upsilon\geq\Upsilon_{0}(\zeta)
.
It remains to check that

\frac{C_{1}C_{2}B^{2}(n_{k-1}+1)}{\alpha^{n_{k-1}}}\leq B^{2}\left(\frac{2k}{n_{k}}\right)^{1/2},

for
k\geq k_{0}(\zeta)
(most importantly, independent of
B
), which is easy to see from the fact that

n_{k}=2^{k!}\ll\alpha^{2^{(k-1)!}}=\alpha^{n_{k-1}}
for large
k
.
Finally, all of the above
imply that

\left|\langle\vec{s},{\bf e}_{3}^{*}\rangle-\frac{\Phi^{(3)}_{n_{k-1}}(1/\alpha_{3})}{\Phi^{(1)}_{n_{k-1}}(1/\alpha)}\right|\leq 2B^{2}\left(\frac{2k}{n_{k}}\right)^{1/2}

for
k\geq k_{0}+\lfloor\Upsilon\cdot\Psi(B)\rfloor
, with
k_{0}=k_{0}(\zeta)
and
\Upsilon\geq\Upsilon_{0}(\zeta)
.
In view of Remark
3.2
,
{\rm Pr}_{3}(\vec{s})
belongs to the disk of radius
2C_{3}B^{2}\cdot(2k/n_{k})^{1/2}
, centered at the point given by the real and
imaginary parts of the complex number
\displaystyle{\frac{\Phi^{(3)}_{n_{k-1}}(1/\alpha_{3})}{\Phi^{(1)}_{n_{k-1}}(1/\alpha)}}
, in
the invariant plane corresponding to
\alpha_{3}
, for some
C_{3}=C_{3}(\zeta)
.
In view of (E1), the number of possible such expressions is bounded by the number of subsets of
[1,n_{k-1}]
of cardinality
<k/2
, times

(\#F)^{\frac{k}{2}}
.Thus,

{\mathcal{L}}^{2}({\rm Proj}_{3}({\mathcal{E}}_{k}))\leq 4C^{2}_{3}B^{4}\sum_{1\leq i<k/2}{n_{k-1}\choose i}\cdot(\#F)^{k/2}\cdot\frac{2k}{n_{k}}\,.

Using a crude upper bound

\sum_{1\leq i<\ell/2}{m\choose i}<{m\choose\ell}<\frac{m^{\ell}}{\ell!}<\Bigl(\frac{m}{\ell}\Bigr)^{\ell}e^{\ell}\ \ \mbox{ for}\ \ m\gg\ell>1,

we obtain for
k\geq k_{0}+\lfloor\Upsilon\cdot\Psi(B)\rfloor
:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}^{2}({\rm Proj}_{3}({\mathcal{E}}_{k}))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4C^{2}_{3}B^{4}\left(\frac{2^{(k-1)!}}{k}\right)^{k}\cdot(\#F\cdot e)^{k}\cdot\frac{2k}{2^{k!}}=8C^{2}_{3}B^{4}\frac{(\#F\cdot e)^{k}}{k^{k-1}}\,.

Next, assuming that
k_{0}=k_{0}(\zeta)
is sufficiently large, we have

\frac{(\#F\cdot e)^{k}}{k^{k-1}}<2^{-k}k^{-k/2}<2^{-k_{0}}k^{-k/2},\ \ \ \mbox{for}\ \ \ k\geq k_{0},

and hence

(3.18)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}^{2}\left(\bigcup_{k=k_{0}+\lfloor\Upsilon\cdot\Psi(B)\rfloor}^{\infty}{\rm Pr}_{3}({\mathcal{E}}_{k}(B))\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 8C^{2}_{3}B^{4}\cdot 2^{-k_{0}}\cdot\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{k=\lfloor\Upsilon\cdot\Psi(B)\rfloor}^{\infty}k^{-k/2}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle C_{4}B^{4}\cdot 2^{-k_{0}}(\Upsilon\cdot\Psi(B))^{-\Upsilon\cdot\Psi(B)/2}.

for some
C_{4}=C_{4}(\zeta)
,
since the series decays super-exponentially.

Claim 1
.

We have

(3.19)

\Psi(B)^{\Psi(B)}\geq B^{1/2},\ \ \ \mbox{for all}\ \ B\geq 16.

Proof of the claim.

Note that
\Psi(B)
is positive for
B\geq 16
.
We have

\log\Bigl(\Psi(B)^{\Psi(B)}\Bigr)=\frac{\log B}{\log\log B}\cdot\log\Bigl(\frac{\log B}{\log\log B}\Bigr)=\log B\Bigl(1-\frac{\log\log\log B}{\log\log B}\Bigr)\geq\frac{1}{2}\log B,\ \ B\geq 16,

and the claim follows.
∎

We will have
\Upsilon\geq 2
, so that

(\Upsilon\cdot\Psi(B))^{-\Upsilon\cdot\Psi(B)/2}\leq\Psi(B)^{-\Upsilon\cdot\Psi(B)/2}\leq B^{-\Upsilon/4},

in view of the claim.
Together with (
3.18
), this yields

{\mathcal{L}}^{2}\left(\bigcup_{k=k_{0}+\lfloor\Upsilon\cdot\Psi(B)\rfloor}^{\infty}{\rm Pr}_{3}({\mathcal{E}}_{k}(B))\right)\leq C_{4}B^{4}\cdot 2^{-k_{0}}B^{-\Upsilon/4}.

Assuming that
\Upsilon\geq 24
, we obtain

{\mathcal{L}}^{2}\left(\bigcup_{B=16}^{\infty}\ \bigcup_{k=k_{0}+\lfloor\Upsilon\cdot\Psi(B)\rfloor}^{\infty}{\rm Pr}_{3}({\mathcal{E}}_{k}(B))\right)<C_{4}\cdot 2^{-k_{0}}\sum_{B=16}^{\infty}B^{4-\Upsilon/4}<C_{4}(\pi^{2}/6)\cdot 2^{-k_{0}},

using the formula for the sum of the series
\sum n^{-2}
. The latter tends to
0
as

, and hence
{\mathcal{L}}^{2}({\rm Pr}_{3}({\mathcal{E}}))=0
by (
3.15
),
as desired. The proof of the lemma
is complete.
∎

3.3.
Conclusion of the proof: “glueing” near zero and at infinity

Here we finally prove (

).
In view of
\sigma_{f}({\mathbb{R}})=\|f\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|f\|_{\infty}^{2}
, it is enough to obtain this inequality for

, with a uniform constant

By
[

(we are in case (iii) of Adamczewski’s theorem), together with

, whose proof is valid for

Next we are going to “glue” this bound with (

Thus, assume that

\sigma_{f}(B_{r}(\omega))\leq\sigma_{f}(B_{r+|\omega|}(0))\leq C_{5}\|f\|_{\infty}^{2}(r+|\omega|).

If

, then

We write

and prove the contrapositive. We have, denoting

∎

4.1.
Some algebraic number theory (see e.g.
[
11
]
)

. A
number field
is a finite field extension
K
of
{\mathbb{Q}}
, and the
degree
of
K
is the dimension of
K
as a vector space over
{\mathbb{Q}}
. An element
\alpha\in K
is an
algebraic integer
if it is a root of a monic polynomial with coefficients in
{\mathbb{Z}}
. Algebraic integers in
K
form a ring denoted by
\mathcal{O}_{K}
.

Let
K_{1},K_{2}
be two number fields. A
{\mathbb{Q}}
-
homomorphism
between
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
is a field homomorphism
\sigma:K_{1}\longrightarrow K_{2}
such that
\sigma(r)=r
, for every
r\in{\mathbb{Q}}
. It is well known that there exist exactly
d
distinct
{\mathbb{Q}}
-homomorphisms between a number field
K
of degree
d
and
{\mathbb{C}}
. We will call these
{\mathbb{Q}}
-homomorphisms between
K
and
{\mathbb{C}}
the
embeddings
of
K
into
{\mathbb{C}}
. If the image
\sigma(K)
is contained in
{\mathbb{R}}
, we will say
\sigma
is a
real
embedding. Note that if
\sigma
is not real, then
\overline{\sigma}
is another embedding, and we will call
\{\sigma,\overline{\sigma}\}
a
conjugate pair
.

Let
\alpha_{1}
be an algebraic number and
\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{d}
the rest of the roots of its minimal polynomial. In the particular case that
K={\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha_{1})
, the
d
distinct embeddings are completely determined by
\sigma_{j}(\alpha_{1})=\alpha_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,d
. For
\eta\in K
, we call
\sigma_{1}(\eta),\dots,\sigma_{d}(\eta)
the
conjugates
of
\eta
. We define the

of
\eta\in K
by
\textup{Tr}(\eta)=\sum_{\sigma}\sigma(\eta)
, where the sum runs over all embedding of

in
{\mathbb{C}}
. In particular, if
\eta\in\mathcal{O}_{K}
then
\textup{Tr}(\eta)\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

4.2.
Proof of Theorem
2.4

This is a minor variation of the proof of
[

, for which we need a version of
[
10
, Lemma 6.2]
.
Thus we introduce notation consistent with
[
10
]
rather than with the earlier sections here.

Let

be an integer matrix, irreducible over
{\mathbb{Q}}
, whose characteristic polynomial is the minimal polynomial of
a Salem number
\alpha
of degree

. Denote by
\alpha_{0}=\alpha^{-1}
,

\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\dots\alpha_{m}

Let

with entries in

Proposition 4.1
.

For every
{\varepsilon}>0

(4.1)

\lvert\lvert\eta A^{n}\vec{s}\rvert\rvert_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}<{\varepsilon},\quad\forall n\geq 0.

Proof.

The argument is very similar to that of
[
10
, Lemma 6.2]
. Denote
\omega^{v}=\omega\langle e_{1},v\rangle
, for

. Then,

\displaystyle\langle\omega A^{n}\vec{s},v\rangle

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}(\omega^{v}\alpha^{n})-\sigma_{0}(\omega^{v})\alpha^{-n}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bigl(\sigma_{j}(\omega^{v})\alpha^{n}_{j}+\overline{\sigma_{j}(\omega^{v})}\alpha^{-n}_{j}\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad+C_{2}\omega^{v}\alpha^{-n}+\omega^{v}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bigl(C_{2j+1}\alpha^{n}_{j}+\overline{C_{2j+1}}\alpha^{-n}_{j}\bigr)

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}(\omega^{v}\alpha^{n})+(C_{2}\omega^{v}-\sigma_{0}(\omega^{v}))\alpha^{-n}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bigl(H_{j}\alpha_{j}^{n}+\overline{H_{j}}\alpha_{j}^{-n}\bigr),

. Consider the following embedding of

into
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\oplus{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\simeq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
(we identify
{\mathbb{C}}
with

\displaystyle\tau:\omega

\displaystyle\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}\omega,&C_{2}\omega-\sigma_{0}(\omega),&C_{3}\omega-\sigma_{1}(\omega),&C_{5}\omega-\sigma_{2}(\omega),&\dots,&C_{2m+1}\omega-\sigma_{m}(\omega)\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\simeq\begin{pmatrix}\omega,&C_{2}\omega-\sigma_{0}(\omega),&{\rm Re}(C_{3}\omega-\sigma_{1}(\omega)),&{\rm Im}(\omega-\sigma_{1}(\omega)),&\dots,&{\rm Im}(C_{2m+1}\omega-\sigma_{m}(\omega))\end{pmatrix}

{\mathbb{Z}}[\alpha]

{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

is different from zero:

\footnotesize{M=\begin{pmatrix}1&C_{2}-1&{\rm Re}(C_{3}-1)&{\rm Im}(C_{3}-1)&\cdots&{\rm Im}(C_{2m+1}-1)\\
\alpha&C_{2}\alpha-\alpha^{-1}&{\rm Re}(C_{3}\alpha-\alpha_{1})&{\rm Im}(C_{3}\alpha-\alpha_{1})&\cdots&{\rm Im}(C_{2m+1}\alpha-\alpha_{m})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\

\widetilde{M}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1&1&\cdots&1\\
\alpha&\alpha^{-1}&\alpha_{3}&\alpha_{4}&\cdots&\alpha_{d}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\

whose determinant is not equal to zero, since it is the Vandermonde matrix of different numbers.

For any
{\varepsilon}>0
, let

, to be specified later. Consider the system of inequalities in the variables

, where

\tau_{j}(\omega)=C_{2j+1}\omega-\sigma_{j}(\omega)

By “Minkowski’s first theorem,” (see

It satisfies (

\lvert\lvert w\rvert\rvert_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\asymp\max_{j}|\langle w,{\mathbf{e}_{j}}\rangle|,\ \ w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

where
\{{\mathbf{e}_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{d}
is the standard basis in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
∎

Appendix B
On the Fourier decay for Salem Bernoulli convolutions

B.1.
Upper bound

For
\lambda\in(0,1)
and
p\in(0,1)
let

\nu_{\lambda}^{p}
be the distribution of the random series
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\pm\lambda^{n}
, where the signs are chosen i.i.d., with probabilities
p,1-p
.
The “classical” Bernoulli convolutions are obtained for
p=\frac{1}{2}
; the case when
p\neq\frac{1}{2}
is called “biased”. This is a remarkable family of
“fractal” measures, which have been extensively studied for almost 100 years, but many open questions remain.
It is not our intention to give a general survey of the topic; the reader
is referred to
[
29
,
35
]
and references therein. However, we do recall the principal results on Fourier decay. It is easily seen that

(B.1)

\widehat{\nu}_{\lambda}^{p}(\xi)=\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}\Bigl(pe^{-2\pi i\lambda^{n}\xi}+(1-p)e^{2\pi i\lambda^{n}\xi}\Bigr),\ \ \ \ \ \widehat{\nu}_{\lambda}^{1/2}(\xi)=\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}\cos(2\pi\lambda^{n}\xi).

Erdős
[
17
]
discovered that if
\lambda^{-1}\neq\frac{1}{2}
is a Pisot number, then

\widehat{\nu}_{\lambda}^{p}(\xi)
does not vanish at infinity. Later, Salem
[
33
]
proved
that in all other cases
\lim_{|\xi|\to\infty}\widehat{\nu}_{\lambda}^{p}(\xi)=0
. However, the question of decay rate is much more subtle and is still far from being
resolved. Erdős
[
18
]
demonstrated that a power Fourier decay holds for Lebesgue-a.e.
\lambda
, and Kahane
[
23
]
showed that
Erdős’ argument actually yields power Fourier decay for all
\lambda
outside of a set of Hausdorff dimension zero. Their argument forms a basis for what
became known as the “Erdős-Kahane technique”. For specific
\lambda
our knowledge is rather poor. Kershner
[
24
]
obtained a power of the
logarithm upper bound for Bernoulli convolutions corresponding to rational
\lambda\neq 1/q
. In
[
5
]
such bounds were shown for
non-Pisot and non-Salem algebraic integers. For other results on Fourier decay of self-similar measures, see
[
36
,
32
]
and references therein. To our knowledge, in the literature there are no quantitative upper bounds for the Fourier decay of Salem Bernoulli convolutions, i.e., when
\lambda=\alpha^{-1}
is a reciprocal of a Salem number. We show that (admittedly, “super-weak”)
\log^{*}
upper bound follows from Proposition
A.3
.

Corollary B.1
.

Let
\alpha
be a Salem number and
\lambda=\alpha^{-1}
. For
p\in(0,1)
consider the biased Bernoulli convolution
\nu_{\lambda}^{p}
. Then
there exist
A=A_{\alpha,p}
and
C=C_{\alpha,p}
, such that

(B.2)

|\widehat{\nu}_{\lambda}^{p}(\xi)|\leq A\exp\bigl(-C\log^{*}|\xi|\bigr),\ \ |\xi|\geq 1.

Sketch of the proof.

We recall the (standard) proof of
[
5
, Corollary A.3]
.
Let
\xi\in[\alpha^{N-1},\alpha^{N}]
, and set
t=\alpha^{-N}\xi
. Then

\displaystyle|\widehat{\nu}_{\lambda}^{p}(\xi)|=\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}\Bigl|pe^{-2\pi i\lambda^{n}\xi}+(1-p)e^{2\pi i\lambda^{n}\xi}\Bigr|\leq\prod_{n=0}^{N-1}\Bigl|p+(1-p)e^{4\pi i\alpha^{n}t}\Bigr|\leq\prod_{n=0}^{N-1}\Bigl(1-{\frac{1-p}{2}}\|2\alpha^{n}t\|_{{}_{{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}}}^{2}\Bigr),

using an elementary inequality
|1+e^{2\pi i\tau}|\leq 2-\frac{1}{2}\|\tau\|_{{}_{{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}}}^{2}
for
\tau\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Now
Proposition
A.3
easily implies the desired claim.
∎

B.2.
Lower bounds

For completeness, we briefly discuss lower bounds for
|\widehat{\nu}_{\lambda}^{p}(\xi)|
. They were suggested to us (in general terms, without
details) by Peter Varjú [personal communication]. These bounds are based on an observation of Kahane
[
23
]

(see
[
29
, Section 5]
for a detailed exposition)
that Salem
Bernoulli convolutions can never have a power Fourier decay.
They follow from the well-known result that for a Salem number
\alpha
and
{\varepsilon}>0

there exists a nonzero
\eta\in{\mathbb{Z}}[\alpha]
, such that
\|\eta\alpha^{n}\|_{{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}}<{\varepsilon}
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
(of which Proposition
4.1
is
a generalization), with a quantitative bound similar to (
4.3
). We omit the details.

Proposition B.2
.

Let
\alpha
be a Salem number of degree
d
,
p\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, and suppose that
\gamma>0
and
C\geq 1
are such that for all
\xi\geq\alpha
holds

(B.3)

\left|\widehat{\nu}_{\lambda}^{p}(\xi)\right|\leq C[\log_{\alpha}(\xi)]^{-\gamma},\ \ \ \lambda=\alpha^{-1}.

Then

(B.4)

\gamma\leq{\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}(d-1)\log_{\alpha}[(1-2p)^{-1}].

For the unbiased Bernoulli convolution
\nu_{\lambda}=\nu_{\lambda}^{1/2}
we got a weaker lower bound. The difference between these cases comes from the fact
that the terms of the product in (
B.1
) are bounded in absolute value from below by
1-2p>0
, when
p\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, whereas those for

\widehat{\nu}_{\lambda}^{1/2}
vanish at
\alpha^{n}/4
and
3\alpha^{n}/4
.

Proposition B.3
.

Let
\alpha
be a Salem number of degree
d
, and suppose that
\beta,\gamma>0
and
C\geq 1
are such that for all
\xi
sufficiently large holds

(B.5)

\left|\widehat{\nu}_{\lambda}^{1/2}(\xi)\right|\leq C[\log_{\alpha}(\xi)]^{-\gamma(\log_{\alpha}\log_{\alpha}(\xi))^{\beta}},\ \ \ \lambda=\alpha^{-1}.

Then

\beta\leq 1.
