Lecture Notes in Loop Quantum Gravity. LN4: Hamiltonian framework
L.Fatibene, M.Ferraris, A. Orizzonte

Abstract
We discuss a covariant setting for Hamiltonian formalism in a relativistic field theory and we use this to obtain again the properties of Hamilton principal functional in Newtonian mechanics, relativistic mechanics, Klein-Gordon, electromagnetism, and Ashtekar-Barbero-Immirzi gravitational theory.



1
Introduction

Let us take a break from the specific problem of LQG and let us look back at it from a more general viewpoint.
In LN3, we discussed Hamilton-Jacobi setting for Holst formulation of GR.
The main aim there was to point out that some of the field equations are in fact constraint equations, they simply restrict
initial conditions allowed so that, after we solved the Cauchy problem in a compact bubble
D
, we are able to build out of it
a solution of the initial covariant equations; see
[
1
]
,
[
2
]
.

As a matter of fact, Hamilton-Jacobi equations are properties of the Hamilton principal functional, namely the action functional
S[\sigma]
computed along solutions.
In LN3, we started from a generic action functional and we showed that it is
in fact a boundary functional, it depends on fields at the boundary of the bubble, namely what we called the
pre-quantum configuration
.

Of course, usually this analysis is carried out in the Hamiltonian framework, where we have further structures which help the analysis,
although there is no general agreement on how to get a Hamiltonian framework for a relativistic theory.
In field theory, we have a number of, slightly different, Hamiltonian formalisms; see
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
,

[
8
]
,
[
9
]
.

The different Hamiltonian formalisms available highlight different aspects: some preserve general covariance,
some reproduce Hamiltonian formalism for mechanics and its geometric structure.
Generally speaking, in field theories one has two different sources of problems with Hamiltonian formalism.

First of all, with more that one single independent coordinate, the physical state is parameterized by boundary fields rather than by
numbers. That is the issue one refers when saying field theories resemble infinite dimensional mechanical systems.
Because of this issue, Hamilton principal functional in field theory is a
functional
.

On the other hand, dynamics in field theories are almost always degenerate, maybe only with the exception Klein-Gordon field.
Usually, one cannot solve for all derivatives of fundamental fields. The Lagrangian in GR (or in Maxwell electromagnetism) depends
on the curvature, which in turns depends only on the skew part of derivatives of the connection.
That directly shows that one has no much hope to invert Legendre transform, as it often happens in mechanics.
Moreover, field theories typically have huge symmetry groups.
Via hole argument, one directly sees that Cauchy problem is ill-posed, typically one has to restrict Cauchy fields to a subset which is usually dependent of the observers. As a consequence some of the field equations do not participate to the Cauchy problem and provide further constraints on initial conditions.

The analysis of constraints which comes from Legendre transform being degenerate and the ones which arise from dynamics is quite complicated in general, especially if one wants to keep the original covariance unbroken or at least under control.

As a matter of fact, it is useful to review the Hamiltonian formalism from scratch.
Eventually, it has to be an equivalent way of formulating the dynamics. Since constraint equations are from field equations, they can be discussed in either formulation.
Although we tend to understand the difference between Lagrangian and Hamiltonian formulations of potentially the same theory in terms of the different structures they provide us (Poisson brackets, symplectic structure, …), it might be more effective to start from a more fundamental perspective.

As Hamilton himself claimed
[
10
]
:

Lagrange’s function states,
Mr Hamilton’s function would solve the problem.
The one serves to form the differential equations of motion, the other would give their integrals

Euler-Lagrange framework is a way of obtaining the equations, while Hamiltonian formalism is a way of characterizing the solution space
of a theory.
In this view, the language used becomes less compelling.
The Hamiltonian point of view focus on solution space, on physical states, it defines the phase space of a theory, regardless the mathematical framework we use.

Of course, what can be said about the solutions is also encoded in the field equations.
What we did in LN2 and LN3 is an analysis of solutions spaces or phase space, namely a Hamiltonian discussion although we used a Lagrangian setting for it.

In fact, in the simple case of a regular Lagrangian dynamics in mechanics, the two viewpoints are equivalent.
Any point in
\mathbb{R}\times TQ
can be taken as an initial condition for the system and, in view of the Cauchy theorem which holds true in that case,
it identifies a unique solution. The Legendre transform is one-to-one thus what can be said on
\mathbb{R}\times TQ
can be equivalently moved onto
\mathbb{R}\times T^{\ast}Q
.
Either
\mathbb{R}\times TQ
or
\mathbb{R}\times T^{\ast}Q
can be regarded as the phase space of the theory, we have one solution for each point there.

On cotangent bundle, we have a natural symplectic structure, which in fact can be dragged back on the tangent bundle
(see
[
11
]
). The only difference, is that the symplectic structure which is canonical on the cotangent bundle, is not canonical on the Lagrangian setting since the Legendre map (thence the induced symplectic structure on
TQ
) depends on the Lagrangian.

When we allow non-regular dynamics, the Legendre transform becomes degenerate, for example, it defines a constraint in
S\subset T^{\ast}Q
.
If we use velocities in this case, different velocities are projected onto the same initial conditions in
T^{\ast}Q
and ultimately correspond to a single solution.
The symmetry between tangent and cotangent is broken: physical states are points in
S\subset T^{\ast}Q
.

In field theory, we witness a
further
phenomenon: in view of the symmetries of the system, some of the equations are not evolutionary, they become constraint of allowed initial conditions. The state of the system is not in
J^{1}{\hbox{\cal C}}
(which in field theory takes the role of
\mathbb{R}\times TQ
in mechanics).
Degenerate dynamics account for different initial conditions to produce the same solution (under-determination),
symmetries account for some initial conditions to be excluded (over-determination).
No surprise that when the two issues are present together, the situation becomes cryptic.
Over-determination restricts both
J^{1}{\hbox{\cal C}}
in the Lagrangian formalism and
S
in the Hamiltonian formalism.

Here we want to discuss how to define a covariant Hamiltonian framework in a, possibly degenerate, field theory
in order to have a least a framework to compare different Hamiltonian frameworks, to compare their structures
and maintain covariance under control.
We do not expect this analysis to add anything substantial to LN3: we still have the same constraint equations,
the same over and under-determinations. These are properties of the field equations, not of the framework we use to obtain them.

Of course, not adding anything new is not necessarily implying one cannot be easier than the other, or highlight relations with a more familiar situations, as e.g. in mechanics.

2
Legendre map in field theory

Let us consider a field theory described by a Lagrangian dynamics on a configuration bundle
[C\rightarrow M]
.
A
configuration
is a global section
\sigma:M\rightarrow C
of such bundle.
This whole geometric structure is encoded into the global properties of solutions, in particular in their transformation rules from one observer to another, observers being identified with trivializations of the bundle.

There is no reason or real advantage in considering local sections if we want to maintain information about transformation rules as we have to do in relativistic theories or gauge natural theories.

As the (possibly higher order) tangent bundles
[T^{k}Q\rightarrow\dots\rightarrow TQ\rightarrow Q]
are defined to account for total derivatives of curves and consequently they provide a definitive framework to deal with ODE on a general manifold
Q
, the same occurs in field theory: we define jet prolongations
[J^{k}C\rightarrow\dots\rightarrow J^{1}C\rightarrow C\rightarrow M]
which account for partial derivatives of sections, i.e. of fields, and consequently they provide the definitive framework to deal with PDE on a general manifold and configuration bundle.

For any fibered coordinate system
(x^{\mu},y^{i})
on
C
, configurations are locally expressed as
y^{i}(x)
, so that
x^{\mu}
are called
independent coordinates
, and
y^{i}
are
field coordinates
.
A configuration precisely gives the field values at any point in the base spacetime
M
.
Accordingly, we define fibered coordinates
j^{k}y:=(x^{\mu},y^{i},y^{i}_{\mu},\dots,y^{i}_{\mu_{1},\dots\mu_{k}})
on the jet prolongations.
The coordinates
y^{i}_{\mu}
are meant to represent first partial derivatives of fields,
y^{i}_{\mu\nu}
are understood to be symmetric and to represent second partial derivatives, and so on.
In general, we can change fibered coordinates on
C
as

x^{\prime\mu}=x^{\prime\mu}(x)\hskip 17.00024pty^{i}=y^{i}(x,y)

(3)

and we understand that change of fibered coordinates on
C
induces a change of coordinates on the jet prolongations, for example

y^{i}_{\mu}=\bar{J}_{\mu}^{\nu}\left(J^{i}_{\nu}+J^{i}_{k}y^{k}_{\nu}\right)

(4)

where
\bar{J}_{\mu}^{\nu}
is the anti Jacobian of the transformation
x^{\prime\mu}=x^{\prime\mu}(x)
, while
(J^{i}_{\nu},J^{i}_{k})
are the Jacobians of
y^{i}(x,y)
.

If we fix a vector bundle
[E\rightarrow C\rightarrow M]
, we define a
k
-order differential operator
to be a vertical map
\mathbb{D}:J^{k}C\rightarrow E
.
The pre-image of the zero section
0:M\rightarrow E
with respect to the differential operator
\mathbb{D}:J^{k}C\rightarrow E

defines a subset
S\subset J^{k}C
which represents a PDE, just in an intrinsic and geometric way, invariant with respect to change of coordinates, on
C
.

On
E
we can fix coordinates
(x^{\mu},y^{i},e_{A})
so that the differential operator is locally expressed as

\mathbb{D}:J^{k}C\rightarrow E:j^{k}y\mapsto(x^{\mu},y^{i},e_{A}(j^{k}y))

(7)

while the associated PDE, for example for
k=1
, are

e_{A}(x^{\mu},y^{i}(x),\partial_{\mu}y^{i}(x))=0

(8)

In a variational setting, dynamics is defined out of a
Lagrangian

L
.
A
k
-order Lagrangian can be considered both as a horizontal form on
J^{k}C
, or as a bundle map
L:J^{k}C\rightarrow A_{m}(M)
, where
A_{m}(M)

is the bundle of
m
-forms on
M
(pulled-back on
C
along the projection map
\pi:C\rightarrow M
).
In both cases, the Lagrangian is in the form

L=L(x^{\mu},y^{i},y^{i}_{\mu},\dots,y^{i}_{\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{k}})d\sigma

(9)

where
d\sigma=dx^{0}\land\dots dx^{m-1}
is the local basis of
m
-forms defined by local coordinates
x^{\mu}
on
M
.

Variational calculus can be formulated in terms of maps between bundles, which are finite dimensional manifolds, with not much, if any, reference to any functional spaces. In this view, variational calculus is a way to define bundle maps,
which eventually can be evaluated on a configuration. Evaluation along a configuration ideally has to be the last things ones do.
That is why, in mechanics, one does not really fixes a curve to find equations of motions and uses the coordinate on the tangent bundle instead.
That is why we can write the equation of a pendulum as a dynamical system on a cylinder
TS_{1}
, that is a submanifold of
T^{2}S_{1}\subset TTS_{1}
, namely as

a+\omega^{2}\sin(x)=0

(10)

Given a compact region
D\subset M
and a configuration
\sigma:M\rightarrow C
we can lift that to a section
j^{k}\sigma:M\rightarrow J^{k}C
of any jet prolongation,
in particular the one with the
k
equal to the order of the Lagrangian we used to specify the dynamics.
We can define the
action
of the configuration
\sigma
in the domain
D
as

A_{D}[\sigma]=\int_{D}(j^{k}\sigma)^{\ast}L

(11)

which is well defined (
D
is compact and
(j^{k}\sigma)^{\ast}L
is a regular form on
D
, thus the integral exists and it is finite).

We define a
deformation
on
D
is a vertical vector field
X=X^{i}(x,y)\partial_{i}
on
C
,
which has a vertical flow
\Phi_{s}:C\rightarrow C
which in fact drags a section
\sigma
to define a 1-parameter family of sections
\sigma_{s}:=\Phi_{s}\circ\sigma
.

We have a bundle
[V(C)\rightarrow C]
for vertical vectors on
C
, with coordinates
(x^{\mu},y^{i},X^{i})
.

It is pretty easy to show that the deformation of the action can be expressed as a bundle map

\delta L:J^{k}C\rightarrow V^{\ast}(J^{k}C)\otimes_{C}A_{m}(M)

(12)

The deformation
X
also can be lifted to the jet prolongations, in particular
j^{k}X
is a section of
[V(j^{k}C)\rightarrow J^{k}C\rightarrow C]
.
Then we can pair

\langle\delta L|j^{k}X\rangle:J^{k}C\rightarrow A_{m}(M)

(16)

which encodes for the deformation of the Lagrangian.
Let us set

\pi_{i}={\partial L\over\partial y^{i}}\hskip 17.00024pt\pi_{i}^{\mu}={\partial L\over\partial y^{i}_{\mu}}\hskip 17.00024pt\pi_{i}^{\mu\nu}={\partial L\over\partial y^{i}_{\mu\nu}}\hskip 17.00024pt\dots

(17)

For a first order Lagrangian, we have

\langle\delta L|j^{1}X\rangle:J^{1}C\rightarrow A_{m}(M):(j^{1}y)\mapsto\left(\pi_{i}X^{i}+\pi_{i}^{\mu}d_{\mu}X^{i}\right)d\sigma

(18)

Variational calculus can be encoded in this way by global maps between bundles, namely

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}:J^{2k}C\rightarrow V^{\ast}(C)\otimes_{C}A_{m}(M)

(19)

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}:J^{2k-1}C\rightarrow V^{\ast}(J^{k-1}C)\otimes_{C}A_{m-1}(M)

so that one has the so-called
first variation formula

\langle\delta L|j^{k}X\rangle=\langle\mathbb{E}|X\rangle+d\langle\mathbb{F}|j^{k-1}X\rangle

(20)

which is satisfied by any deformation as equality of maps
J^{k}C\rightarrow A_{m}(M)
.

That can be easily shown by choosing coordinates and locally going through the usual integrations by parts one does in variational calculus.
For globality, one can fix the order
k
and check transformation laws.
One can see the proof in
[
12
]
, for order
k=1,2
in
[
13
]
.
For a first order Lagrangian, we have

\mathbb{E}=(\pi_{i}-d_{\mu}\pi_{i}^{\mu})\>\bar{d}y^{i}\otimes d\sigma\hskip 17.00024pt\mathbb{F}=\pi_{i}^{\mu}\>\bar{d}y^{i}\otimes d\sigma_{\mu}

(22)

where
\bar{d}y^{i}
is a the dual basis of
\partial_{i}
in
V(C)
.
The whole first variation formula can be evaluated along a configuration
\sigma
, namely setting
y^{i}=y^{i}(x)
, to become the usual local computation one does for variational calculus.

Notice that
V^{\ast}(C)\otimes_{C}A_{m}(M)
is a vector bundle therefore, by definition,
\mathbb{E}
is a differential operators of order (at most)
2k
,
the corresponding PDE,
(\mathbb{E}_{i}\circ j^{2k}\sigma)=0
, are the
Euler-Lagrange equations
, the
field equations
of our system.

2.1
The Legendre map and phase bundle

Let us restrict, for the sake of simplicity, to first order Lagrangians.
We define the
Legendre map
as

\mathbb{F}:J^{1}C\rightarrow V^{\ast}(C)\otimes A_{m-1}(M):j^{1}y\mapsto\pi_{i}^{\mu}(j^{1}y)\>\bar{d}y^{i}\otimes d\sigma_{\mu}

(23)

This defines the
(Hamiltonian) phase bundle

P(C)=V^{\ast}(C)\otimes A_{m-1}(M)
, namely
the bundle in which momenta
\pi_{i}^{\mu}
live.
It has coordinates
(x^{\mu},y^{i},p_{i}^{\mu})
and as usual that transformations on
[C\rightarrow M]
induce transformations on
P(C)

\begin{cases}x^{\prime\mu}=x^{\prime\mu}(x)\cr y^{\prime i}=y^{\prime i}(x,y)\cr\end{cases}\qquad\qquad\Rightarrow\quad p^{\prime\mu}_{i}=\bar{J}J^{\mu}_{\nu}p^{\nu}_{k}\bar{J}^{k}_{i}

(24)

In other words, we have defined a functor
P(\cdot)

which associates to a configuration bundle
C
its Hamiltonian phase bundle
P(C)
.

Of course, in field theory, we do not require
\mathbb{F}
to be invertible since this does not happen in most models.
However, let us require as regularity that the map
\mathbb{F}
is constant rank, i.e. the image
\Lambda=\mathbb{F}(J^{1}C)\subset P(C)
is a sub-manifold
which is called the
primary constraint
.

2.2
The multisymplectic bundle and the canonical Liouville form

Let us define
Poincaré-Cartan forms
to be of
m
-forms
\omega
on
C
such that for any 2 vertical vectors
u,v\in V(C)
one has

v\hbox to4.0pt{\leaders{\hrule height=0.3pt}{\hfill}}\vbox to8.0pt{\leaders{\vrule width=0.3pt}{\vfill}}\kern 2.0ptu\hbox to4.0pt{\leaders{\hrule height=0.3pt}{\hfill}}\vbox to8.0pt{\leaders{\vrule width=0.3pt}{\vfill}}\kern 2.0pt\omega=0
.

That means that locally a Poincaré-Cartan form
\omega
is written as

\omega=p\>d\sigma+p_{i}^{\mu}\>dy^{i}\land d\sigma_{\mu}

(25)

If we change coordinates in
C
that induced transformations of Poincaré-Cartan forms

\displaystyle\omega=

\displaystyle p^{\prime}\>d\sigma^{\prime}+p^{\prime\mu}_{i}\>dy^{\prime i}\land d\sigma^{\prime}_{\mu}=

(28)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Jp^{\prime}\>d\sigma+J\bar{J}_{\mu}^{\nu}p^{\prime\mu}_{i}\left(J^{i}_{\alpha}dx^{\alpha}+J^{i}_{k}\>dy^{k}\right)\land d\sigma_{\nu}=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle J\left(p^{\prime}+\bar{J}_{\mu}^{\nu}J^{i}_{\nu}p^{\prime\mu}_{i}\right)d\sigma+J\bar{J}_{\mu}^{\nu}p^{\prime\mu}_{i}J^{i}_{k}\>dy^{k}\land d\sigma_{\nu}

i.e. transformation laws for coefficients

p=J\left(p^{\prime}+\bar{J}_{\mu}^{\nu}J^{i}_{\nu}p^{\prime\mu}_{i}\right)\hskip 17.00024pt\hskip 17.00024ptp_{k}^{\nu}=J\bar{J}_{\mu}^{\nu}p^{\prime\mu}_{i}J^{i}_{k}

(29)

We can define the
multisymplectic bundle

Z(C)\subset A_{m}(C)
as the subbundle with coordinates
(x^{\mu},y^{i},p,p_{i}^{\mu})
and transition functions above.

As it happens on cotangent bundles, on
Z(C)
one can define a canonical
Liouville
m
-form

\Theta=p\>d\sigma+p_{i}^{\mu}\>dy^{i}\land d\sigma_{\mu}

(30)

The canonical Liouville
m
-form is a global form exactly in view of transformation laws

\displaystyle\Theta=

\displaystyle p^{\prime}\>d\sigma^{\prime}+p^{\prime\mu}_{i}\>dy^{\prime i}\land d\sigma^{\prime}_{\mu}=

(33)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Jp^{\prime}\>d\sigma+J\bar{J}_{\mu}^{\nu}p^{\prime\mu}_{i}\>\left(J^{i}_{\alpha}dx^{\alpha}+J^{i}_{k}dy^{k}\right)\land d\sigma_{\nu}=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle J\left(p^{\prime}+\bar{J}_{\mu}^{\nu}p^{\prime\mu}_{i}J^{i}_{\nu}\right)d\sigma+J\bar{J}_{\mu}^{\nu}p^{\prime\mu}_{i}J^{i}_{k}\>dy^{k}\land d\sigma_{\nu}=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p\>d\sigma+p_{k}^{\nu}\>dy^{k}\land d\sigma_{\nu}=\Theta

A manifold
(M,\omega)
with a closed, non-degenerare,
k
-form is called a
multisymplectic manifold
of degree
k
.
On the bundle
Z(C)
we have the
(m+1)
-form
\omega=d\Theta
which is clearly closed (in fact exact) and non-degenerate since

\displaystyle\vec{v}\hbox to4.0pt{\leaders{\hrule height=0.3pt}{\hfill}}\vbox to8.0pt{\leaders{\vrule width=0.3pt}{\vfill}}\kern 2.0ptd\Theta=

\displaystyle\vec{v}\hbox to4.0pt{\leaders{\hrule height=0.3pt}{\hfill}}\vbox to8.0pt{\leaders{\vrule width=0.3pt}{\vfill}}\kern 2.0pt\left(dp\land d\sigma+dp_{i}^{\mu}\land dy^{i}\land d\sigma_{\mu}\right)=

(34)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle vd\sigma-v^{\alpha}dp\land d\sigma_{\alpha}+v_{i}^{\mu}dy^{i}\land d\sigma_{\mu}+

\displaystyle-v^{i}dp_{i}^{\mu}\land d\sigma_{\mu}+v^{\alpha}dp_{i}^{\mu}\land dy^{i}\land d\sigma_{\mu\alpha}=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle v=0,\>v^{\alpha}=0,\>v^{i}=0,\>v_{i}^{\mu}=0\hskip 8.50012pt\Rightarrow\hskip 8.50012pt\vec{v}=0

Therefore
(Z(C),\omega)
is a multisymplectic bundle of degree
k=m+1
, which reduces to the usual symplectic structure in mechanics when
m=1
.

Also
Z(\cdot)
is a functor which defines the multisymplectic bundle
Z(C)
out of the configuration bundle
C
.
Of course, we have a projection map
p:Z(C)\rightarrow P(C)

which is well defined exactly because
p_{i}^{\mu}
transform in the same way on
P(C)
and
Z(C)
.

2.3
Poincaré-Cartan form in the Lagrangian setting

The dynamics of the theory is also described by the
Poincaré-Cartan form
on
J^{1}C

\Theta_{L}=L(j^{1}y)d\sigma+\pi_{i}^{\mu}(j^{1}y)\left(dy^{i}-y^{i}_{\alpha}dx^{\alpha}\right)\land d\sigma_{\mu}=-{{\cal H}}d\sigma+\pi_{i}^{\mu}dy^{i}\land d\sigma_{\mu}

(35)

where we set
{{\cal H}}(j^{1}y):=\pi_{i}^{\mu}y^{i}_{\mu}-L
for the
total energy
.
The Poincaré-Cartan form
\Theta_{L}
is a global form of the type we used to define
Z(C)
, which means that the Lagrangian in fact defines a global map

\Phi_{L}:J^{1}C\rightarrow Z(C):j^{1}y\mapsto\left(x,y,p=-{{\cal H}},p_{i}^{\mu}=\pi_{i}^{\mu}\right)

(36)

The map is well defined since the total energy
{{\cal H}}
transforms as
-p
in fact

\displaystyle-{{\cal H}}=

\displaystyle-\pi_{i}^{\mu}y^{i}_{\mu}+L=-J\left(\bar{J}^{\mu}_{\beta}\pi^{\prime\beta}_{k}J^{k}_{i}y^{i}_{\mu}-L^{\prime}\right)=-J\left(\pi^{\prime\beta}_{k}(y^{\prime k}_{\beta}-\bar{J}_{\beta}^{\mu}J^{k}_{\mu})-L^{\prime}\right)=

(45)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle J\left(-{{\cal H}}^{\prime}+\bar{J}_{\beta}^{\mu}J^{k}_{\mu}\pi^{\prime\beta}_{k}\right)

One can easily get that
\Theta_{L}
is an alternative way of describing the dynamics, on equal footing of the Lagrangian.
In fact, the action can be written as

A_{D}[\sigma]=\int_{D}(j^{1}\sigma)^{\ast}L=\int_{D}(j^{1}\sigma)^{\ast}\Theta_{L}

(46)

since we have that
\omega^{i}:=dy^{i}-y^{i}_{\alpha}dx^{\alpha}
is a
contact 1-form
, i.e.

(j^{1}\sigma)^{\ast}\omega^{i}=\left(d_{\alpha}y^{i}-y^{i}_{\alpha}\right)dx^{\alpha}=0\hskip 17.00024pt\omega^{\prime i}=J^{i}_{k}\left(dy^{k}-y^{k}_{\beta}dx^{\beta}\right)=J^{i}_{k}\omega^{k}

(47)

In view of these transformation laws, we have

\Theta^{\prime}_{L^{\prime}}=L^{\prime}d\sigma^{\prime}+\pi^{\prime\mu}_{i}\omega^{\prime i}\land d\sigma^{\prime}_{\mu}=JL^{\prime}d\sigma+J\bar{J}_{\mu}^{\nu}\pi^{\prime\mu}_{i}J^{i}_{k}\>\omega^{k}\land d\sigma_{\nu}=\Theta_{L}

(48)

which implies the transformation laws for the Lagrangian
JL^{\prime}=L
(which is equivalent to the Lagrangian globality) and it, in turns, implies that of momenta

J\bar{J}_{\alpha}^{\mu}\pi^{\prime\alpha}_{k}J^{k}_{i}=\pi^{\mu}_{i}

(49)

The variation of the action is then

\displaystyle\delta A_{D}[\sigma]=

\displaystyle\int_{D}{d\over ds}(j^{1}\sigma)^{\ast}\Phi_{s}^{\ast}\Theta_{L}=\int_{D}(j^{1}\sigma)^{\ast}\pounds_{\hat{X}}\Theta_{L}=

(50)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{D}(j^{1}\sigma)^{\ast}\left(\hat{X}\hbox to4.0pt{\leaders{\hrule height=0.3pt}{\hfill}}\vbox to8.0pt{\leaders{\vrule width=0.3pt}{\vfill}}\kern 2.0ptd\Theta_{L}+d(\hat{X}\hbox to4.0pt{\leaders{\hrule height=0.3pt}{\hfill}}\vbox to8.0pt{\leaders{\vrule width=0.3pt}{\vfill}}\kern 2.0pt\Theta_{L})\right)

where
\hat{X}
is the prolongation of the deformation to the Lagrangian phase space.
Using Stokes theorem we have

\delta A_{D}[\sigma]=\int_{D}(j^{1}\sigma)^{\ast}\hat{X}\hbox to4.0pt{\leaders{\hrule height=0.3pt}{\hfill}}\vbox to8.0pt{\leaders{\vrule width=0.3pt}{\vfill}}\kern 2.0ptd\Theta_{L}+\int_{\partial D}(\hat{X}\hbox to4.0pt{\leaders{\hrule height=0.3pt}{\hfill}}\vbox to8.0pt{\leaders{\vrule width=0.3pt}{\vfill}}\kern 2.0pt\Theta_{L})

(51)

The boundary term vanishes for deformations fixed on the boundary and field equations reads as

(j^{1}\sigma)^{\ast}\hat{X}\hbox to4.0pt{\leaders{\hrule height=0.3pt}{\hfill}}\vbox to8.0pt{\leaders{\vrule width=0.3pt}{\vfill}}\kern 2.0ptd\Theta_{L}=0

(52)

Of course, these are equivalent to the Euler-Lagrange equations.

Let us remark that the Poincaré-Cartan form
\Theta_{L}=\Phi_{L}^{\ast}\Theta
is pull-back of the canonical Liouville form on
Z(C)
.
Also, the map
\Phi_{L}
factorizes through
\mathbb{F}:J^{1}C\rightarrow\Lambda
, i.e. there exists a map
\Phi_{H}:\Lambda\rightarrow Z(B)
such that
\Phi_{H}\circ\mathbb{F}=\Phi_{L}
.

Proof
: The map
\mathbb{F}:J^{1}B\rightarrow\Lambda
is surjective and of constant (maximal) rank. It is then a projection and it defines a fibration
\mathbb{F}:J^{1}B\rightarrow\Lambda
.
We need to prove that
\Phi_{L}
is constant along the fibers of such a fibration.
Let us consider a curve
\gamma:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow J^{1}B:s\mapsto(x^{\mu},y^{i},y^{i}_{\mu}(s))
in the fiber, then

{d\over ds}\left(\mathbb{F}\circ\gamma\right)={\partial L\over\partial y^{i}_{\mu}\partial y^{k}_{\alpha}}v^{k}_{\alpha}=0

(55)

i.e.
\mathbb{F}\circ\gamma

The first is the inverse Legendre transform, the second is equivalent to Euler-Lagrange equation.
Hamilton equations define a dynamical system on
\mathbb{R}\times T^{\ast}Q
given by the Hamiltonian field

X_{H}=\partial_{t}+{\partial H\over\partial p_{i}}{\partial\over\partial q^{i}}-{\partial H\over\partial q^{i}}{\partial\over\partial p_{i}}

(71)

which can be mapped back into
\mathbb{R}\times TQ
equivalently into

\displaystyle X_{L}=\partial_{t}+u^{i}{\partial\over\partial q^{i}}+(X_{L})^{i}{\partial\over\partial u^{i}}

(72)

\displaystyle(X_{L})^{i}=A^{ij}\left({\partial L\over\partial q^{j}}-{\partial L\over\partial t\partial u^{j}}-{\partial L\over\partial q^{k}\partial u^{j}}u^{k}\right)

where
A^{ij}
is the inverse Hessian of the Lagrangian with respect to velocities.

Let us stress, neither the Lagrangian or the Hamiltonian are in fact
scalar functions
, they both are components of forms which transform accordingly. Only by restricting to special transformations, e.g.
(t^{\prime}=t+t_{0},q^{\prime}=q(t,q))
, they behave as functions.
Also in both frameworks, dynamics is expressed as a dynamical system, as for any normal ODE.
Moreover, the dynamical systems are mapped one into the other by Legendre transform, so that also the solutions are mapped one into the others.

This is in a nutshell the ordinary situation in classical mechanics.
However, we can also consider a
relativistic point
, which is described somehow in a similar way on
C=\mathbb{R}\times M
with coordinates
(s,x^{\mu})

where
s
is a parameter (not necessarily time) and
M
is spacetime (including in some sense time).
On spacetime, we are interested in
trajectories
(somehow
unparameterized
curves), thus when we specify dynamics we want it to be invariant with respect to re-parameterizations. This can easily be done by fixing the Lagrangian to be

L=\sqrt{-g_{\mu\nu}(x)u^{\mu}u^{\nu}}+A_{\mu}(x)u^{\mu}

(73)

for some covector
A=A_{\mu}dx^{\mu}
. Let us fix
(M,g)
to be Minkowski and choose
x^{\mu}
to be orthonormal coordinates so that
g_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}
.

One can check that this is invariant with respect to parameterizations as we required.
We define the momenta

\pi_{\mu}:={-\eta_{\mu\nu}u^{\nu}\over\sqrt{-\eta_{\alpha\beta}u^{\alpha}u^{\beta}}}+A_{\mu}\quad\Rightarrow(\pi-A)\cdot(\pi-A)=-1

(74)

The Legendre map
\mathbb{F}:\mathbb{R}\times TM\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\times T^{\ast}M
is clearly not surjective since
p-A
needs to be on surface, which is our primary constraint
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}\times T^{\ast}M
.
We can use
p_{i}
to parameterize
\Lambda
and set the embedding giving
p_{0}
as a function of
p_{i}
.

In this case we have

{{\cal H}}=\pi_{\mu}u^{\mu}-L={-u\cdot u\over\sqrt{-u\cdot u}}+A\cdot u-\sqrt{-u\cdot u}-A\cdot u\equiv 0

(75)

thus we have

\Theta_{L}=\pi_{\mu}dx^{\mu}={-u\cdot dx\over\sqrt{-u\cdot u}}+A\cdot dx\qquad\qquad\Theta_{H}=p_{\mu}dx^{\mu}

(76)

and the equations turn out to be

\dot{x}=u\qquad\qquad\dot{u}=u\hbox{$d\over ds$}\left(\ln\left(\sqrt{-u\cdot u}\right)\right)+(\sqrt{-u\cdot u})u\hbox to4.0pt{\leaders{\hrule height=0.3pt}{\hfill}}\vbox to8.0pt{\leaders{\vrule width=0.3pt}{\vfill}}\kern 2.0ptF

(77)

where we set
F=dA
for the 2-form
F
.
Here reparameterizations work exactly as for the hole argument (they are symmetries and they can be compact supported in
\mathbb{R}
) and they spoil uniqueness of solutions, Cauchy theorem cannot hold since when we have a solution any of its reparameterization is again a solution.

We have to discuss whether we have constraint equations as well.
In order to do that, we can change coordinates
\omega^{2}=-u\cdot u
and
v^{i}={u^{i}\over u^{0}}
, which can be inverted as

u^{0}={\omega\over\sqrt{1-|\vec{v}|^{2}}}\qquad\qquad u^{i}={\omega v^{i}\over\sqrt{1-|\vec{v}|^{2}}}

(78)

By obvious reasoning, in dimension 4, we can set
F_{0i}=E_{i}
and
F_{ij}=\epsilon_{ij}{}^{k}B_{k}
, so that

(u\hbox to4.0pt{\leaders{\hrule height=0.3pt}{\hfill}}\vbox to8.0pt{\leaders{\vrule width=0.3pt}{\vfill}}\kern 2.0ptF){}^{i}_{\>\cdot}=u^{0}(E-v\times B)
and
(u\hbox to4.0pt{\leaders{\hrule height=0.3pt}{\hfill}}\vbox to8.0pt{\leaders{\vrule width=0.3pt}{\vfill}}\kern 2.0ptF)^{0}=u^{0}v\cdot E
, and split the second equation as

\hbox{$\dot{u}^{0}\over u^{0}$}=\hbox{$d\over ds$}\left(\ln(\omega)\right)+\omega v\cdot E\qquad\hbox{$\dot{u}^{i}\over u^{0}$}=v^{i}\hbox{$d\over ds$}\left(\ln(\omega)\right)+\omega(E-v\times B)

(79)

These can be recast as

\dot{\omega}=\dot{\omega}\qquad\dot{v}=\omega\left(E-v\times B\right)-\omega(v\cdot E)v\quad\left(\Rightarrow\>v\cdot\dot{v}=\omega(1-|v|^{2})v\cdot E\right)

(80)

The first equation of these is identically satisfied, so that
\omega
is uncostrained.
For the second equation we can solve
v\cdot E={v\cdot\dot{v}\over\omega(1-|v|^{2})}
and substitute it back

\dot{v}+{v\cdot\dot{v}\over(1-|v|^{2})}v=\omega\left(E-v\times B\right)

(81)

We can further simplify it. Let us fix
\gamma=\hbox{$1\over\sqrt{1-|v|^{2}}$}
and consider its derivative
\dot{\gamma}=\gamma^{3}v\cdot\dot{v}
and then compute directly

\hbox{$1\over\gamma$}\hbox{$d\over ds$}\left(\gamma v\right)=\dot{v}+\hbox{$(v\cdot\dot{v})v\over 1-|v|^{2}$}=\omega\left(E-v\times B\right)

(83)

Then the
m
equations of motions are in fact equivalent to
m-1

\hbox{$d\over ds$}\left(\gamma v\right)=\omega\gamma\left(E-v\times B\right)

(84)

which determines the physical velocity
v
and leave
\omega
completely undetermined.
There is no constraint on initial conditions, in fact there should be
{{\cal H}}=0
which, however, is identically satisfied since
{{\cal H}}\equiv 0
.

4
Klein-Gordon field

The first example in field theory is (real) Klein-Gordon field, for the sake of simplicity on Minkowski spacetime.
The configuration bundle is
[C\rightarrow M]
with
C=M\times\mathbb{R}
which is trivial so that the sections are
scalar
fields.
We pick coordinates
(x^{\mu},\varphi)
on
C
, with
x^{\mu}
orthonormal with respect to the Minkowski metric
\eta={\hbox{diag}}(-1,1,1,1)
.

The dynamics is induced by the Lagrangian

L=-\hbox{$1\over 2$}(\varphi_{\mu}\eta^{\mu\nu}\varphi_{\nu}+\mu^{2}\varphi^{2})d\sigma

(85)

We define momenta as
\pi^{\mu}=-\varphi^{\mu}
. The Legendre transform is trivially invertible, hence no primary constraint.
The total energy reads as

{{\cal H}}=-\varphi^{\mu}\varphi_{\mu}+\hbox{$1\over 2$}(\varphi_{\mu}\eta^{\mu\nu}\varphi_{\nu}+\mu^{2}\varphi^{2})=-\hbox{$1\over 2$}(\varphi_{\mu}\eta^{\mu\nu}\varphi_{\nu}-\mu^{2}\varphi^{2})

(86)

hence we have the Hamiltonian in the form

H(x,\varphi,p)=-\hbox{$1\over 2$}(p^{\mu}\eta_{\mu\nu}p^{\nu}-\mu^{2}\varphi^{2})

(87)

We can define the maps

\displaystyle\Phi_{L}(j^{1}y)=

\displaystyle(x,\varphi,p^{\mu}=-\varphi^{\mu},p=-{{\cal H}})

(88)

\displaystyle\Phi_{H}(x^{\mu},\varphi,p^{\mu})=

\displaystyle(x^{\mu},\varphi,p^{\mu},p=-H(x,\varphi,p^{\mu}))

so that we get the Poincaré-Cartan forms

\Theta_{L}=Ld\sigma-\varphi^{\mu}\omega\land d\sigma_{\mu}\qquad\qquad\Theta_{H}=-Hd\sigma+p^{\mu}d\varphi\land d\sigma_{\mu}

(89)

This is pretty much the same as regular mechanics, no constraints.
The field equations

\eta^{\mu\nu}\varphi_{\mu\nu}-\mu^{2}\varphi=0

(90)

defines a well-posed Cauchy problem.

The Hamilton principal functional is

\displaystyle\delta S=

\displaystyle{\displaystyle d\over ds}\int_{D_{s}}(j^{1}\varphi_{s})^{\ast}\Theta_{L}=\int_{D}(j^{1}\varphi)^{\ast}\pounds_{j^{1}X}\Theta_{L}=

(91)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{D}(j^{1}\varphi)^{\ast}\left(j^{1}X\hbox to4.0pt{\leaders{\hrule height=0.3pt}{\hfill}}\vbox to8.0pt{\leaders{\vrule width=0.3pt}{\vfill}}\kern 2.0ptd\Theta_{L}+d(X\hbox to4.0pt{\leaders{\hrule height=0.3pt}{\hfill}}\vbox to8.0pt{\leaders{\vrule width=0.3pt}{\vfill}}\kern 2.0pt\Theta_{L})\right)=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{D}(j^{1}\varphi)^{\ast}(j^{1}X)\hbox to4.0pt{\leaders{\hrule height=0.3pt}{\hfill}}\vbox to8.0pt{\leaders{\vrule width=0.3pt}{\vfill}}\kern 2.0ptd\Theta_{L}+\int_{\partial D}(j^{1}\varphi)^{\ast}(X\hbox to4.0pt{\leaders{\hrule height=0.3pt}{\hfill}}\vbox to8.0pt{\leaders{\vrule width=0.3pt}{\vfill}}\kern 2.0pt\Theta_{L})

On solutions, the first integral vanishes, thus

\displaystyle\delta S=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial D}(j^{1}\varphi)^{\ast}(X\hbox to4.0pt{\leaders{\hrule height=0.3pt}{\hfill}}\vbox to8.0pt{\leaders{\vrule width=0.3pt}{\vfill}}\kern 2.0pt\Theta_{L})=

(92)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial D}\left(u_{\alpha}(\varphi^{\alpha}\varphi_{\beta}-\hbox{$1\over 2$}(\varphi_{\mu}\varphi^{\mu}+\mu^{2}\varphi^{2})\delta^{\alpha}_{\beta})\delta x^{\beta}+u_{\alpha}p^{\alpha}\delta\varphi\right)d\zeta

where
d\zeta
is the standard
(m-1)
-form induced by coordinates on the boundary
\partial D
.
Then, we have

\hbox{$\delta S\over\delta x^{\beta}$}=u_{\alpha}(\varphi^{\alpha}\varphi_{\beta}-\hbox{$1\over 2$}(\varphi_{\mu}\varphi^{\mu}+\mu^{2}\varphi^{2})\delta^{\alpha}_{\beta})=u_{\alpha}\hat{T}^{\alpha}_{\beta}\qquad\hbox{$\delta S\over\delta\varphi$}=u_{\alpha}p^{\alpha}

(93)

which, in fact, coincide with what we found previously.

As a matter of fact, Poincaré-Cartan forms, either
\Theta_{L}
or
\Theta_{H}
, provide us with a way of computing the Hamilton principal functional
directly.
The relation with Poincaré-Cartan form also suggests a direct link with conservation laws.
On the other hand, the relation with multisymplectic structure also opens to a definition of Poisson or symplectic structures.
We do not discuss neither here.

5
Electromagnetic field

We can consider electromagnetism on Minkowski spacetime
(M,\eta)
. We fix a structure
U(1)
-principal bundle
[P\rightarrow M]
(which, in this case, is necessarily trivial, since the base
M
is contractible; see
[
14
]
).
The electromagnetic field is described in terms of a connection on
P
which is locally described in terms of a covariant potential
A=A_{\mu}dx^{\mu}
.

Since
P
is trivial, connections allow global potential, thus the space
{\hbox{Con}}(P)
of connections
A
on the structure bundle
P
is isomorphic to the cotangent bundle
T^{\ast}M
.
However, if it were a covector
A_{\mu}
would transform as
A^{\prime}_{\mu}=\kern 3.0pt\bar{\kern-3.0ptJ}_{\mu}^{\nu}A_{\nu}
while, in view of gauge transformations, in fact it transforms as
A^{\prime}_{\mu}=\kern 3.0pt\bar{\kern-3.0ptJ}_{\mu}^{\nu}(A_{\nu}+\partial_{\nu}\alpha)
namely, as a connection on
P
.
The isomorphism
{\hbox{Con}}(P)\rightarrow T^{\ast}M
is not canonical, we have one isomorphism for each (global) trivialization of
P
.
Again, bundles are there to take care of transformation laws.

For the sake of simplicity, we restrict to orthonormal coordinates
x^{\mu}
, which exist on Minkowski spacetime.
Let us set
F=dA
for the
field strength
and the Lagrangian can be written as

L=-\hbox{$1\over 4$}F^{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}

(94)

We define momenta as
\pi^{\mu\nu}=-F^{\nu\mu}=F^{\mu\nu}
.
The Legendre transform is not invertible, since of course we cannot solve for it
d_{\alpha}A_{\beta}
as the momenta depends only on the skew part namely, on
F_{\alpha\beta}
.
The total energy reads as

{{\cal H}}=F^{\mu\nu}d_{\nu}A_{\mu}-L=\hbox{$1\over 2$}F^{\mu\nu}(d_{\nu}A_{\mu}-d_{\mu}A_{\nu})+\hbox{$1\over 4$}F^{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}=-\hbox{$1\over 4$}F^{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}

(95)

hence we have the Hamiltonian in the form

H(x,A,p)=-\hbox{$1\over 4$}p^{\mu\nu}p_{\mu\nu}

(96)

We can define the maps

\Phi_{L}(j^{1}y)=(p^{\mu\nu}=F^{\mu\nu},p=-{{\cal H}})\qquad\Phi_{H}(p^{\mu\nu})=(p^{\mu\nu},p=-H(x,A,p^{\mu}))

(97)

so that we get the Poincaré-Cartan forms

\Theta_{L}=\hbox{$1\over 4$}F^{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}d\sigma-F^{\mu\nu}dA_{\nu}\land d\sigma_{\mu}\qquad\qquad\Theta_{H}=-Hd\sigma+p^{\nu\mu}dA_{\nu}\land d\sigma_{\mu}

(98)

The Hamilton functional reads as

\displaystyle\delta S=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial D}(j^{1}\sigma)^{\ast}(X\hbox to4.0pt{\leaders{\hrule height=0.3pt}{\hfill}}\vbox to8.0pt{\leaders{\vrule width=0.3pt}{\vfill}}\kern 2.0pt\Theta_{L})=

(99)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial D}-u_{\alpha}(\hbox{$1\over 4$}F^{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}\delta_{\beta}^{\alpha}\delta x^{\beta}-F^{\alpha\nu}(\delta x^{\beta}d_{\beta}A_{\nu}-\delta A_{\nu}))d\zeta

Let us remark that not all fields
X=\delta x^{\beta}\hbox{$\partial\over\partial x^{\beta}$}+\delta A_{\beta}\hbox{$\partial\over\partial A_{\beta}$}
are symmetries, just the one generated by gauge transformations on
P
, for which we have

\pounds_{X}A_{\nu}=\delta x^{\beta}d_{\beta}A_{\nu}-\delta A_{\nu}=\delta x^{\beta}F_{\beta\nu}+\nabla_{\nu}\xi_{V}

(100)

The components
(\delta x^{\beta},\xi_{V})
are in fact independent. We can further expand

\displaystyle\delta S=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial D}-u_{\alpha}(\hbox{$1\over 4$}F^{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}\delta_{\beta}^{\alpha}\delta x^{\beta}-F^{\alpha\nu}(\delta x^{\beta}F_{\beta\nu}+\nabla_{\nu}\xi_{V}))d\zeta=

(101)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial D}u_{\alpha}\left(\left(F^{\alpha\nu}F_{\beta\nu}-\hbox{$1\over 4$}F^{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}\delta_{\beta}^{\alpha}\right)\delta x^{\beta}-F^{\alpha\nu}\nabla_{\nu}\xi_{V}\right)d\zeta

Then, we have

\displaystyle\hbox{$\delta S\over\delta x^{\beta}$}=u_{\alpha}(F^{\alpha\nu}F_{\beta\nu}-\hbox{$1\over 4$}F^{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}\delta_{\beta}^{\alpha})=u_{\alpha}\hat{T}^{\alpha}_{\beta}

(102)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\nu}\left(\hbox{$\delta S\over\delta A_{\nu}$}\right)=-\nabla_{\nu}\left(u_{\alpha}F^{\alpha\nu}\right)=D_{a}E^{a}

As we already noticed, the Hamilton functional does not depend on
A_{0}
, which is then a gauge field and accounts for an extra constraint
which is associated to over-determination.

When we choose initial conditions, we need to meet the constraint
D_{a}E^{a}=0
on
\partial D
.
That corresponds to conservation of the electric charge, it is just a bit trivial since we are considering the vacuum theory.
On the contrary, the part associated to
\delta x^{\beta}
corresponds to conservation of energy and momentum.

6
ABI gravity

We already discussed the Lagrangian for ABI model, which is

L=F^{i}\land L_{i}+\nabla k^{i}\land\left(K_{i}-\beta L_{i}\right)-\hbox{$1\over 2$}\epsilon_{ijk}k^{i}\land k^{j}\land\left(\left(\beta^{2}-1\right)L^{k}-2\beta K^{k}\right)

(103)

where, for the sake of simplicity, we neglect the cosmological constant and
here we also set
\beta=\gamma
, as it is usually done.

The theory is first order in
A^{i}_{\mu}
and
k^{i}_{\mu}
only, so that we have 2 momenta

p_{i}^{\mu\alpha}={\delta L\over\partial d_{\alpha}A^{i}_{\mu}}=\epsilon^{\alpha\mu\rho\sigma}L_{i\rho\sigma}\qquad\pi_{i}^{\mu\alpha}={\delta L\over\partial d_{\alpha}k^{i}_{\mu}}=-\hbox{$1+\gamma^{2}\over\gamma$}\epsilon^{\alpha\mu\rho\sigma}u_{\rho}e_{k\sigma}

(104)

from which it follows that

u_{\alpha}p_{k}^{\mu\alpha}=\hbox{$1\over 2\gamma$}\epsilon_{kij}u_{\alpha}\epsilon^{\alpha\mu\rho\sigma}\epsilon^{i}_{\rho}\epsilon^{j}_{\sigma}\qquad\qquad u_{\alpha}\pi_{k}^{\mu\alpha}=0

(105)

The Poincaré-Cartan form reads as

\Theta_{L}=-\left(p_{i}^{\mu\alpha}d_{\alpha}A^{i}_{\mu}+\pi_{i}^{\mu\alpha}d_{\alpha}k^{i}_{\mu}-L\right)d\sigma+p_{i}^{\mu\alpha}dA^{i}_{\mu}\land d\sigma_{\alpha}+\pi_{i}^{\mu\alpha}dk^{i}_{\mu}\land d\sigma_{\alpha}

(106)

Its contraction along
X=\delta x^{\beta}\hbox{$\partial\over\partial x^{\beta}$}+\delta A^{i}_{\mu}\hbox{$\partial\over\partial A^{i}_{\mu}$}+\delta k^{i}_{\mu}\hbox{$\partial\over\partial k^{i}_{\mu}$}
evaluated on the boundary is

\displaystyle X\hbox to4.0pt{\leaders{\hrule height=0.3pt}{\hfill}}\vbox to8.0pt{\leaders{\vrule width=0.3pt}{\vfill}}\kern 2.0pt\Theta_{L}=

\displaystyle\left(Lu_{\alpha}\delta x^{\alpha}+u_{\alpha}p_{i}^{\alpha\mu}\pounds_{X}A^{i}_{\mu}+u_{\alpha}\pi_{i}^{\alpha\mu}\pounds_{X}k^{i}_{\mu}\right)d\zeta=

(107)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(Lu_{\alpha}\delta x^{\alpha}+u_{\alpha}p_{i}^{\alpha\mu}\pounds_{X}A^{i}_{\mu}\right)d\zeta

from which we infer

{\delta S\over\delta k^{k}_{\mu}}=u_{\alpha}\pi_{k}^{\alpha\mu}=0\qquad{\delta S\over\delta A^{k}_{\mu}}u_{\mu}=u_{\mu}u_{\alpha}p_{k}^{\alpha\mu}=0

(108)

The Hamilton functional depends on the tangent part of the connection,
A^{i}_{a}
.
Moreover, we have

\displaystyle{\delta S\over\delta A^{k}_{a}}=

\displaystyle\hbox{$1\over 2\gamma$}\epsilon_{kij}\epsilon^{abc}\epsilon^{i}_{b}\epsilon^{j}_{c}=\hbox{$1\over\gamma$}E^{a}_{k}

(109)

The Lie derivative of the connection is

\pounds_{X}A^{i}_{\nu}=\delta x^{\beta}F^{i}_{\beta\nu}+\nabla_{\nu}\xi^{i}_{V}

(110)

hence we have

\displaystyle X\hbox to4.0pt{\leaders{\hrule height=0.3pt}{\hfill}}\vbox to8.0pt{\leaders{\vrule width=0.3pt}{\vfill}}\kern 2.0pt\Theta_{L}=

\displaystyle\left((u_{\alpha}p_{i}^{\alpha a}F^{i}_{\beta a}+Lu_{\beta})\delta x^{\beta}+u_{\alpha}p_{i}^{\alpha a}D_{a}\xi^{i}_{V}\right)d\zeta=

(111)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\left(\hbox{$1\over\gamma$}E^{a}_{i}F^{i}_{\beta a}+Lu_{\beta}\right)\delta x^{\beta}-\hbox{$1\over\gamma$}D_{a}E^{a}_{k}\xi^{i}_{V}\right)d\zeta

That gives us the
Gauss constraint
and, moreover, we have

{\delta S\over\delta x^{\beta}}=\hbox{$1\over\gamma$}E^{a}_{i}F^{i}_{\beta a}+Lu_{\beta}

(112)

from which we get

{\delta S\over\delta x^{\beta}}u^{\beta}=\hbox{$1\over\gamma$}E^{a}_{i}F^{i}_{\beta a}u^{\beta}-L\qquad{\delta S\over\delta x^{\beta}}\partial_{b}x^{\beta}=\hbox{$1\over\gamma$}E^{a}_{i}F^{i}_{ba}

(113)

That gives us the second boundary equation (the momenta constraint) as well as

{\delta S\over\delta x^{\beta}}u^{\beta}=-\hbox{$1\over 2\epsilon$}\left(\epsilon^{ij}{}_{k}F^{k}_{ab}+2(1+\gamma^{2})\hat{k}^{i}_{[a}\hat{k}^{j}_{b]}\right)E^{a}_{i}E^{b}_{j}

(114)

Accordingly, we have the constraint equations

D_{a}E^{a}_{k}=0\qquad E^{a}_{i}F^{i}_{ba}=0\qquad\left(\epsilon^{ij}{}_{k}F^{k}_{ab}+2(1+\gamma^{2})\hat{k}^{i}_{[a}\hat{k}^{j}_{b]}\right)E^{a}_{i}E^{b}_{j}=0

(115)

directly from the Poincaré-Cartan form
\Theta_{L}
(or, equivalently,
\Theta_{H}
).

7
Conclusions and Perspectives

Here we sketched one particular framework for covariant Hamiltonian formulation.
There are different ones which slightly differ from this.

Our framework is based on Poincaré-Cartan forms, which are defined in both a Lagrangian and Hamiltonian context.
Here we introduced them for first order field theories, although they are canonical at any order in mechanics, while in higher order field theories there is one Poincaré-Cartan form for each connection on the base manifold.
For second order field theory, one can still choose one canonical representative, at orders higher than 2 the dependence on the connection is unavoidable.

Although non-unicity is important in a general analysis from a mathematical physics viewpoint, here we aim to an application to a particular first order theory so that it does not play a relevant role.
In particular, Poincaré-Cartan form provides us with a geometric relation with the Hamilton principal functional and, consequently, with constrain equations,
once again without relaying on functional space, but rather algebraically on equations.

One can summarize all we did until now by saying that we went after a way of describing the
physical states
of a covariant system.
All in all, a
state
is what uniquely determines a solution (which in a covariant setting is a
history
of the system,
determining a solution means to uniquely determine the evolution of the system).
That is relevant both for a classical and quantum viewpoint.
As a result of our analysis, at least in this context, a physical state is represented by the value of fields at the boundary of a compact bubble, which
moreover
satisfy boundary equations, if any.
We already discussed that: from a classical viewpoint, it guarantees one can find a solution to the Cauchy problem and then to the original covariant equations.

From a quantum viewpoint, it is natural to quantize the physical state, i.e. pre-quantum configurations obeying boundary equations, which is the quantization scheme we described.

Further investigation have to be devoted to highlight a direct link between constraint equations and conservation laws.
The relation is very apparent in mechanics where conservation laws are related to first integrals, namely to functions on the phase space the conservation of which is, in fact, consequence of equations of motion. It is well known that first integrals can be used to replace some of the equations of motion and, being functions of the initial conditions, namely on the phase space, they behave as constraint equations.

In field theories, conservation laws are not functions, they are
currents
, namely
(m-1)
-forms. The relations between conservation laws and boundary equations are well-known at the level of functionals.
Further investigation is due to consider whether a more direct link at variational level can make possible to get a covariant framework for boundary equations as it happens for conservation laws.
In particular, we have quite a well-developed framework for covariant conservation laws in a wide class of field theories (namely, gauge natural theories)
that provides covariant quantities from which each observer can eventually get its own relative quantities.
It would not be much of a surprise the same thing happened for boundary equations. Moreover, the covariant framework for conservation laws generalizes some structures (e.g. Bianchi identities) which also play a role with boundary equations and it may allow a general and easier approach which avoid the
a priori
breaking of general covariance,
as one does in ADM framework.

Although this might be important in general, it would not change much in the specific case of LQG we are here considering.

As far as LQG is concerned, before going on to quantization, we need to better discuss connections space (and connection holonomies) and some group structures to be used later on to discuss spin networks.
