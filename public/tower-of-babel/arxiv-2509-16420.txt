The discrete Laplace asymptotic method and its application to the 3XOR satisfiability problem
J. William Helton, Jared A. Hughes, Peter Schlosser

Abstract
. A standard way to calculate the asymptotic behavior of integrals of the form ∫ 𝒲 g ⁡ ( x ) ​ e − n ​ h ​ ( x ) ​ 𝑑 x \int_{\mathcal{W}}g(x)e^{-nh(x)}dx is the (continuous) Laplace asymptotic method. However, also discrete sums like ∑ x ∈ 𝒲 ∩ Λ n g n ​ ( x ) ​ e − n ​ h n ​ ( x ) \sum_{x\in\mathcal{W}\cap\Lambda_{n}}g_{n}(x)e^{-nh_{n}(x)} have similar behavior, when Λ n \Lambda_{n} is a discrete grid which becomes infinitely fine, and the functions g n g_{n} and h n h_{n} converge to g g and h h respectively. We go even further, and also derive the asymptotic formula for sums of the form ∑ x ∈ 𝒲 ∩ Λ n 𝒮 n ​ ( x ) \sum_{x\in\mathcal{W}\cap\Lambda_{n}}\mathcal{S}_{n}(x) , where the summand 𝒮 n ​ ( x ) \mathcal{S}_{n}(x) asymptotically behaves as g n ​ ( x ) ​ e − n ​ h n ​ ( x ) g_{n}(x)e^{-nh_{n}(x)} . The motivation, and also an immediate application, will be filling in all details in the classical breakthrough paper [ 5 ] , which gives the solvability (phase transition) threshold of the 3XOR-SAT problem using the second moment method. Various analytical arguments there were lightly described, but the appendix to this paper combines recent results to fill all of them in. We would expect our theorems on asymptotics to apply to other (especially combinatorial) problems as well. For example, they seem effective on 3XOR-GAME problems.

1.
Introduction

The basic problem the discrete Laplace asymptotic method
addresses goes as follows.
The setting is in
a bounded region
\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for a fixed
integer
d\geq 1
.
Moreover, we consider a sequence of lattices

(1.1)

\Lambda_{k}\coloneqq\frac{1}{n_{k}}A_{k}\mathbb{Z}^{d}+v_{k},

where
(n_{k})_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
is a sequence of integers with
n_{k}\geq k
,
(v_{k})_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
a sequence of vectors with
\lim_{k\to\infty}v_{k}=v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
(A_{k})_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a sequence of matrices which converge as
\lim_{k\to\infty}A_{k}=A
to an invertible matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Given

\mathcal{S}_{k}:\mathcal{W}\cap\Lambda_{k}\to\mathbb{C}
a sequence of functions with asymptotic approximation
\mathcal{S}_{k}(x)\sim g_{k}(x)e^{-n_{k}h_{k}(x)}
, we want to see how

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathcal{W}\cap\Lambda_{k}}\mathcal{S}_{k}(x)

behaves asymptotically as
k\to\infty
. If we are in a case where
g_{k}\to g
and
h_{k}\to h
, the sum may behave like a Riemann sum, and we optimistically obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathcal{W}\cap\Lambda_{k}}\mathcal{S}_{k}(x)\sim\sum_{x\in\mathcal{W}\cap\Lambda_{k}}g_{k}(x)e^{-n_{k}h_{k}(x)}\sim\frac{n_{k}^{d}}{|\det(A_k)|}\int_{\mathcal{W}}g_{k}e^{-n_{k}h_{k}}dx\sim\Omega_{k},

where the classical Laplace method for integrals applies to give

\displaystyle\Omega_{k}\coloneqq\frac{(2\pi n_{k})^{\frac{d}{2}}g(x_{0})e^{-n_{k}h(x_{0})}}{|\det(A)|\sqrt{\det(\mathcal{H}\{h\}(x_0))}}.

Here,
x_{0}\in\mathcal{W}
is the point where the global minimum of the function
h
is obtained, and
\mathcal{H}\{h\}(x_{0})
is the Hessian matrix evaluated at this point.
The goal of this paper is to give reasonably
general conditions under which these results do hold. More precisely, our first main result,
Theorem

2.7
, implies that

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathcal{W}\cap\Lambda_{k}}g_{k}(x)e^{-n_{k}h_{k}(x)}\sim\Omega_{k}

holds under precise conditions. Our second main result,
Theorem

2.8
, then even proves

\sum_{x\in\mathcal{W}\cap\Lambda_{k}}\mathcal{S}_{k}(x)\sim\Omega_{k},

in the same setting, plus a few assumptions connecting
\mathcal{S}_{k}
to
g_{k}e^{-n_{k}h_{k}}
.

The papers’ final section,
Section

3
, describes two applications to special systems of linear equations over the binaries; these correspond to classical 3XOR satisfiability problems (3XOR-SAT) and to 3XOR games (3XOR-GAME). The issue is the ratio of number of equations
m
to number of unknowns
n
for which the system of equations is likely to be solvable: Does there exist a “solvability threshold”
c^{*}
, so that asymptotically
m/n<c^{*}
implies solvable, and
m/n>c^{*}
implies not solvable, with high probability?

The classic beautiful tour de force
of Dubois–Mandler
[
5
]
says yes for 3XOR-SAT and computes
c^{*}
.
One of the many steps in their proof is
[
5
, Lemma 3.6]
, which asserts a discrete Laplace asymptotic formula,
and gives the intuitive justification that it is analogous to the classical integral Laplace asymptotic formula.

Section

3
rigorously proves their conclusion as an application of our theorems.
In support of this, and also using recent results of
[
2
,
3
]
, we produced an appendix, which fills in all of the analytical details which were alluded to, but not included in
[
5
]
.

After this, in
Section

3.4
, we point out that analysis of the 3XOR-GAME thresholds
produce a big messy sum whose asymptotics our theory addresses. In unpublished work we have filled in most of the details.

2.
The discrete Laplace asymptotic method

This section gives and proves a discrete variation of the Laplace asymptotic method. As a motivation, in
Theorem

2.1
it starts with the (already known) continuous analog of what we are proving. Discretizing the domain of integration then turns the continuous Laplace method into the discrete Laplace method, see
Theorem

2.7
. Finally, in
Theorem

2.8
, we further generalize our results by replacing the function
g_{k}e^{-n_{k}h_{k}}
by arbitrary discrete summands
S_{k}
, which only asymptotically behave as
g_{k}e^{-n_{k}h_{k}}
.

2.1.
The continuous Laplace method

To give context for the situation, we start with the classical continuous Laplace method, which our discrete Laplace method in
Section

2.2
will imitate, and which the asymptotics for discrete summands in
Section

2.3
extends. The following
Theorem

2.1
is a slight variation of the statement in
[
8
, Theorem 15.2.2]
. For further terms in the respective asymptotic expansion, see
[
8
, Theorem 15.2.5]
, which relies on
g,h
being infinitely often differentiable.

Theorem 2.1

(Continuous Laplace method)
.

Let
\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be open and bounded, and
h\colon\mathcal{W}\to\mathbb{R}
. Let us assume that there exists some
x_{0}\in\mathcal{W}
, such that
h\in C^{2}(\mathcal{N}_{0})
in an open neighborhood
\mathcal{N}_{0}
of
x_{0}
, and there holds the following two assumptions:

i)

The Hessian
\mathcal{H}\{h\}(x_{0})
is strictly positive definite.

ii)

For every open neighborhood
\mathcal{N}
of
x_{0}
, there is
\inf_{x\in\mathcal{W}\setminus\mathcal{N}}h(x)>h(x_{0})
.

Then for every Riemann integrable function
g\colon\mathcal{W}\to\mathbb{C}
, which is continuous near
x_{0}
, there converges

(2.1)

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}n^{\frac{d}{2}}e^{nh(x_{0})}\int_{\mathcal{W}}g(x)e^{-nh(x)}dx=\frac{(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}g(x_{0})}{\sqrt{\det(\cH\{h\}(x_0))}}.

Remark 2.2
.

Before we start the Discrete Laplace Method, we emphasize that integrability over all of
\mathcal{W}
is not significant, since the Discrete Laplace Method concerns sums over a lattice intersected with
\mathcal{W}
. Instead, we only need integrability over a small neighborhood
\mathcal{N}_{0}
where
h
is minimized, and relevant bounds outside the neighborhood.

2.2.
The Discrete Laplace method

The idea of the discrete Laplace method is to approximate the integral on the left hand side of (
2.1
) by its Riemann sum. In other words, we will partition the integration domain
\mathcal{W}
into a lattice
\Lambda_{k}
of the form (
1.1
), and approximate

(2.2)

\int_{\mathcal{W}}g(x)e^{-n_{k}h(x)}dx\sim\frac{\absolutevalue{\det(A_k)}}{n_{k}^{d}}\sum\limits_{x\in\mathcal{W}\cap\Lambda_{k}}g_{k}(x)e^{-n_{k}h_{k}(x)},

where the prefactor
\frac{|\det(A_k)|}{n_{k}^{d}}
is the cell volume of the lattice. In
Theorem

2.7
we then show that under the following
2.3
, the right hand side of this approximation still converges to the same limit as the continuous Laplace method in Eq.(
2.1
) suggests.

Assumption 2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be bounded, and for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
consider a lattice
\Lambda_{k}
as in Eq.(
1.1
), as well as functions
g_{k}\colon\mathcal{W}\cap\Lambda_{k}\to\mathbb{C}
and
h_{k}\colon\mathcal{W}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Then we say that these objects satisfy the
discrete Laplace assumptions
, if there exists some
x_{0}\in\Int(\mathcal{W})
, such that
h_{k}\in C^{2}(\mathcal{N}_{0})
in an open neighborhood
\mathcal{N}_{0}
of
x_{0}
, and there holds the following four assumptions:

i)

There exist functions
g\in C(\mathcal{N}_{0})
and
h\in C^{2}(\mathcal{N}_{0})
, such that there converges

\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\|g_{k}-g\|_{\infty,\,\mathcal{N}_{0}\cap\Lambda_{k}}=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\|h_{k}-h\|_{C^{2}(\mathcal{N}_{0})}=0;

ii)

In the point
x_{0}
there is

h_{k}(x_{0})=h(x_{0}),\quad\nabla h_{k}(x_{0})=0,\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{H}\{h\}(x_{0})\text{ is strictly positive definite};

iii)

For every open neighborhood
\mathcal{N}
of
x_{0}
, there is

(2.3)

\liminf_{k\to\infty}\inf_{x\in\mathcal{W}\setminus\mathcal{N}}h_{k}(x)>h(x_{0});

iv)

There exists some
C_{g}\geq 0
and
l\in\mathbb{N}
, such that

(2.4)

\|g_{k}\|_{\infty,\mathcal{W}\cap\Lambda_{k}}\leq C_{g}k^{\ell},\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}.

The following three Lemmas
2.4
,
2.5
and
2.6
will now be preparatory for the main theorem of this subsection, Theorem
2.7
. In
Lemma

2.4
we will prove that the sum in Eq.(
2.2
) tends to zero on every region
\mathcal{W}\setminus\mathcal{N}
away from
x_{0}
, where
h
is strictly larger than
h(x_{0})
, see Eq.(
2.3
).

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be bounded, and for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
consider a lattice
\Lambda_{k}
as in Eq.(
1.1
), as well as functions
g_{k}\colon\mathcal{W}\cap\Lambda_{k}\to\mathbb{C}
and
h_{k}\colon\mathcal{W}\to\mathbb{R}
, which satisfy
2.3
. Then for every open neighborhood
\mathcal{N}\subseteq\mathcal{W}
of
x_{0}
, there converges

\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}n_{k}^{-\frac{d}{2}}e^{n_{k}h(x_{0})}\sum\limits_{x\in(\mathcal{W}\setminus\mathcal{N})\cap\Lambda_{k}}g_{k}(x)e^{-n_{k}h_{k}(x)}=0.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{W}
is bounded, and the cell volume of the lattice in Eq.(
1.1
) is
\absolutevalue{\det(A_k)}n_{k}^{-d}
, with
\absolutevalue{\det(A_k)}\to\absolutevalue{\det(A)}
,
there exists a constant
C_{A}\geq 0
, such that the number of lattice points inside
\mathcal{W}
is bounded by

\absolutevalue{\cW\cap\Lambda_k}\leq C_{A}n_{k}^{d},\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}.

Since the limit inferior in Eq.(
2.3
) is strictly larger than
h(x_{0})
, there exists some
N\in\mathbb{N}
, such that

h_{\min}\coloneqq\inf_{k\geq N}\inf_{x\in\mathcal{W}\setminus\mathcal{N}}h_{k}(x)>h(x_{0}).

Using also the upper bound (
2.4
) of the norm
\|g_{k}\|_{\infty,\mathcal{W}\cap\Lambda_{k}}
, as well as
n_{k}\geq k
, there converges

\displaystyle n_{k}^{-\frac{d}{2}}e^{n_{k}h(x_{0})}\sum\limits_{x\in(\mathcal{W}\setminus\mathcal{N})\cap\Lambda_{k}}\!\absolutevalue{g_k(x)}\,e^{-n_{k}h_{k}(x)}

\displaystyle\leq n_{k}^{-\frac{d}{2}}\big|(\mathcal{W}\setminus\mathcal{N})\cap\Lambda_{k})\big|C_{g}k^{l}e^{-n_{k}(h_{\min}-h(x_{0}))}

\displaystyle\leq C_{A}\,C_{g}\,n_{k}^{\frac{d}{2}}n_{k}^{\ell}\,e^{-n_{k}(h_{\min}-h(x_{0}))}\overset{k\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}0.\qed

The next lemma now handles the sum (
2.2
) in neighborhoods
\mathcal{N}
of
x_{0}
, and with a function
h_{k}
in the exponent which is of the form
\frac{1}{2}(x-x_{0})^{\top}H(x-x_{0})
.
Theorem

2.7
will apply this
lemma to a small ball around
x_{0}
, where
h
is locally quadratic.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
H\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be strictly positive definite,
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
(\Lambda_{k})_{k}
a sequence of lattices of the form (
1.1
). Then for every open neighborhood
\mathcal{N}
of
x_{0}
, there converges

(2.5)

\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}n_{k}^{-\frac{d}{2}}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{N}\cap\Lambda_{k}}e^{-\frac{n_{k}}{2}(x-x_{0})^{\top}H(x-x_{0})}=\frac{(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}{|\det(A)|\sqrt{\det(H)}}.

Proof.

Applying the change of variables
y:=\sqrt{n_{k}}\,(x-x_{0})
, transforms the lattice
\Lambda_{k}
and the neighborhood
\mathcal{N}
into

(2.6)

\widetilde{\Lambda}_{k}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{k}}}A_{k}\mathbb{Z}^{d}+\sqrt{n_{k}}\,(v_{k}-x_{0}),\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{N}_{k}:=\sqrt{n_{k}}\,(\mathcal{N}-x_{0}).

Under this change of variables, we can rewrite the sum on the left hand side of Eq.(
2.5
) as

(2.7)

\sum_{x\in\mathcal{N}\cap\Lambda_{k}}e^{-\frac{n_{k}}{2}(x-x_{0})^{\top}H(x-x_{0})}=\sum\limits_{y\in\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{k}\cap\widetilde{\Lambda}_{k}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}y^{\top}Hy}.

Next we note that the lattice
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{k}
in Eq.(
2.6
) has a unit cell volume of
|\det(A_k)|n_{k}^{-\frac{d}{2}}
, and the neighborhoods
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{k}
cover the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
when
k\to\infty
. This means, if we multiply the equation (
2.7
) with
|\det(A_k)|n_{k}^{-d/2}
, it becomes an approximating sum of the Gaussian integral

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}|\det(A_k)|n_{k}^{-\frac{d}{2}}\sum\limits_{x\in\mathcal{N}\cap\Lambda_{k}}e^{-\frac{n_{k}}{2}(x-x_{0})^{\top}H(x-x_{0})}

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}|\det(A_k)|n_{k}^{-\frac{d}{2}}\sum\limits_{y\in\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{k}\cap\widetilde{\Lambda}_{k}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}y^{\top}Hy^{\top}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}y^{\top}Hy}dy=\frac{(2\pi)^{d/2}}{\sqrt{\det(H)}}.

Using also
\lim_{k\to\infty}\absolutevalue{\det(A_k)}=\absolutevalue{\det(A)}\neq 0
, gives the stated limit (
2.5
).
∎

The final preparatory
Lemma

2.6
, gives a quadratic Taylor estimate from above and from below of the functions
h_{k}
in a neighborhood of its minimum
x_{0}
.

Lemma 2.6
.

In the setting of Assumption
2.3
, for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists an open neighborhood
\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}
of
x_{0}
and some
K_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
, such that for every
x\in\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}
,
k\geq K_{\varepsilon}

(2.8)

\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2}(x-x_{0})^{\top}\mathcal{H}\{h\}(x_{0})(x-x_{0})\leq h_{k}(x)-h(x_{0})\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon}{2}(x-x_{0})^{\top}\mathcal{H}\{h\}(x_{0})(x-x_{0}).

Proof.

Without loss of generality we will only consider
x_{0}=0
,
h(x_{0})=0
, and assume that the neighborhood
\mathcal{N}_{0}
in
2.3
is convex. Since
h_{k}\in C^{2}(\mathcal{N}_{0})
is twice continuously differentiable, with
h_{k}(0)=0
and
\nabla h_{k}(0)=0
by Assumption
2.3
ii), the Taylor formula provides for every
x\in\mathcal{N}_{0}
some
\xi_{k,x}\in[0,x]
, such that

(2.9)

h_{k}(x)=\frac{1}{2}x^{\top}\mathcal{H}\{h_{k}\}(\xi_{k,x})x,\qquad x\in\mathcal{N}_{0}.

Since there is also
\mathcal{H}\{h\}(0)
strictly positive definite, again by Assumption
2.3
ii), there exists some
h_{0}>0
, such that

x^{\top}\mathcal{H}\{h\}(0)x\geq h_{0}|x|^{2},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}.

Consequently, we can estimate

\frac{\big|x^{\top}\big(\mathcal{H}\{h_{k}\}(\xi_{k,x})-\mathcal{H}\{h\}(0)\big)x\big|}{x^{\top}\mathcal{H}\{h\}(0)x}\leq\frac{\big\|\mathcal{H}\{h_{k}\}(\xi_{k,x})-\mathcal{H}\{h\}(0)\big\|}{h_{0}},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}.

This inequality now turns the Taylor expansion (
2.9
) for every
x\in\mathcal{N}_{0}
into

\displaystyle\Big|h_{k}(x)-\frac{1}{2}x^{\top}\mathcal{H}\{h\}(0)x\Big|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\big|x^{\top}\big(\mathcal{H}\{h_{k}\}(\xi_{k,x})-\mathcal{H}\{h\}(0)\big)x\big|

(2.10)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\big\|\mathcal{H}\{h_{k}\}(\xi_{k,x})-\mathcal{H}\{h\}(0)\big\|}{2h_{0}}\,x^{\top}\mathcal{H}\{h\}(0)x.

Let us now choose
\varepsilon>0
arbitrary. Since
h\in C^{2}(\mathcal{N}_{0})
, there exists an open neighborhood
\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}
of
x_{0}
, such that

\|\mathcal{H}\{h\}(\xi)-\mathcal{H}\{h\}(0)\|<\varepsilon h_{0},\qquad\xi\in\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}.

Moreover, since there converges
\lim_{k\to\infty}\|h_{k}-h\|_{C^{2}(\mathcal{N}_{0})}
by
2.3
i), there exists some
K_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
, such that

\sup\limits_{\xi\in\mathcal{N}_{0}}\|\mathcal{H}\{h_{k}\}(\xi)-\mathcal{H}\{h\}(\xi)\|<\varepsilon h_{0},\qquad k\geq K_{\varepsilon}.

Combining these two estimates, we then get

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{H}\{h_{k}\}(\xi_{k,x})-\mathcal{H}\{h\}(0)\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{H}\{h_{k}\}(\xi_{k,x})-\mathcal{H}\{h\}(\xi_{k,x})\|

\displaystyle\quad+\|\mathcal{H}\{h\}(\xi_{k,x})-\mathcal{H}\{h\}(0)\|\leq 2\varepsilon h_{0},\qquad x\in\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon},\,k\geq K_{\varepsilon}.

Using this in the estimate (
2.10
), then gives

\Big|h_{k}(x)-\frac{1}{2}x^{\top}\mathcal{H}\{h\}(0)x\Big|\leq\varepsilon\,x^{\top}\mathcal{H}\{h\}(0)x,\qquad x\in\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon},\,k\geq K_{\varepsilon}.\qed

Now we are ready prove the convergence of the discrete Laplace method
Theorem

2.7
. It is a generalization of
[
5
, Lemma 3.6]
, which they loosely justified as being provable by an
approach similar to that for
the continuous analog, e.g.
Theorem

2.1
.

Theorem 2.7

(Discrete Laplace method)
.

Let
\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be bounded, and for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
consider a lattice
\Lambda_{k}
as in Eq.(
1.1
), as well as functions
g_{k}:\mathcal{W}\cap\Lambda_{k}\to\mathbb{C}
and
h_{k}:\mathcal{W}\to\mathbb{R}
, which satisfy Assumption
2.3
. Then there converges

(2.11)

\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}n_{k}^{-\frac{d}{2}}e^{n_{k}h(x_{0})}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{W}\cap\Lambda_{k}}g_{k}(x)e^{-n_{k}h_{k}(x)}=\frac{(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}g(x_{0})}{|\det(A)|\sqrt{\det(\cH\{h\}(x_0))}}.

Proof.

First we note that for the (uniform) convergence of a sequence of complex functions, it is equivalent to ask for the (uniform) convergence of the respective positive and negative part of the real and complex part. Using this fact in the limit (
2.11
) but also in the Assumption
2.3
i), we can without loss of generality assume that
g,g_{k}\geq 0
. For an easier notation let us also set
x_{0}=0
,
h(x_{0})=0
and use the abbreviation
H:=\mathcal{H}\{h\}(x_{0})
.

Let us fix
\varepsilon>0
arbitrary. Then by Lemma
2.6
, there exists some neighborhood
\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}
of
x_{0}
and some
K_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
, such that

(2.12)

\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2}x^{\top}Hx\leq h_{k}(x)\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon}{2}x^{\top}Hx,\qquad x\in\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon},\,k\geq K_{\varepsilon}.

Since by
2.3
i), there converges
\lim_{k\to\infty}\|g_{k}-g\|_{\infty,\,\mathcal{N}_{0}\cap\Lambda_{k}}=0
uniformly to a continuous function
g
, we can choose
\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{N}_{0}
small enough and
K_{\varepsilon}
large enough, such that also

|g_{k}(x)-g(0)|\leq\|g_{k}-g\|_{\infty,\mathcal{N}_{0}\cap\Lambda_{k}}+|g(x)-g(0)|\leq\varepsilon,\qquad x\in\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}\cap\Lambda_{k},\,k\geq K_{\varepsilon}.

This is equivalent to

(2.13)

g(0)-\varepsilon\leq g_{k}(x)\leq g(0)+\varepsilon,\qquad x\in\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}\cap\Lambda_{k},\,k\geq K_{\varepsilon}.

With this preparation, we turn to estimating the sum. To do so, we split it up into the two terms

(2.14)

\sum\limits_{x\in\mathcal{W}\cap\Lambda_{k}}g_{k}(x)e^{-n_{k}h_{k}(x)}=\sum\limits_{x\in(\mathcal{W}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon})\cap\Lambda_{k}}g_{k}(x)e^{-n_{k}h_{k}(x)}+\sum\limits_{x\in\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}\cap\Lambda_{k}}g_{k}(x)e^{-n_{k}h_{k}(x)}.

For the first sum, it is already shown in Lemma
2.4
, that there converges

(2.15)

\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}n_{k}^{-\frac{d}{2}}\sum_{x\in(\mathcal{W}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon})\cap\Lambda_{k}}g_{k}(x)e^{-n_{k}h_{k}(x)}=0.

For the second sum in Eq.(
2.14
), we use the first inequality in Eq.(
2.12
) and the second inequality in Eq.(
2.13
), to estimate

\sum_{x\in\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}\cap\Lambda_{k}}g_{k}(x)e^{-n_{k}h_{k}(x)}\leq(g(0)+\varepsilon)\sum_{x\in\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}\cap\Lambda_{k}}e^{-n_{k}\frac{(1-\varepsilon)}{2}x^{\top}Hx},\qquad k\geq K_{\varepsilon}.

If multiplying
n_{k}^{-\frac{d}{2}}
and sending
k\to\infty
,
Lemma

2.5
tells us that the right hand side converges to

(2.16)

\limsup\limits_{k\to\infty}n_{k}^{-\frac{d}{2}}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}\cap\Lambda_{k}}g_{k}(x)e^{-n_{k}h_{k}(x)}\leq\frac{(g(0)+\varepsilon)(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}{|\det(A)|\sqrt{(1-\varepsilon)^{d}\det(H)}}.

Adding now the two limits (
2.15
) and (
2.16
), gives the upper bound of the limit superior

(2.17)

Since the upper bound of the limit superior (

∎

3.
Application: 3XOR-SAT

This section is devoted to giving an idea of how our theory, in particular
Theorem

2.8
, can be applied. We describe two examples called 3XOR-SAT and 3XOR-GAME. Since the formulas of 3XOR-GAME are of similar structure but much more complicated, we will only consider the 3XOR-SAT problem in detail.

3.1.
3XOR solvability problems

The 3XOR problems we shall discuss are equivalent to solving (binary) linear equations of the form

\Gamma z=b\pmod{2},

with
\Gamma
a
m\times n
matrix whose entries are all nonnegative integers, and
b
is vector whose entries are all 0 or 1. In addition, the matrix
\Gamma
has very special structure.

3XOR-SAT equations
are those where
\Gamma
is any XOR matrix, which means each row adds exactly to 3. Note that this is only possible for matrices
\Gamma
with entries 0, 1, 2, or 3.

3XOR-GAME equations
are those where the columns of
\Gamma
partition into 3 parts

\Gamma=\begin{pmatrix}A&B&C\end{pmatrix},

with
A,B,C
are
m\times\frac{n}{3}
matrices whose rows each have exactly one 1, the rest are 0s. Clearly, every 3XOR-GAME equation is a 3XOR-SAT equation.

A matrix
\Gamma
is called a
2-core
, provided each column has a sum which is at least 2. If
\Gamma
is not a 2-core, we can remove the columns with all 0s or only one 1 as well as remove appropriate rows, to obtain a linear system whose solvability is the same as the original system. With this motivation, we will focus efforts now on matrices
\Gamma
which are 2-cores.

The interpretation of these systems as games will not be described in this article, since our only interest is in the solvability of the respective linear equations. However, without definitions we say that 3XOR games are 3 player (Alice, Bob, Charlie) cooperative games. Binary solutions to such a game’s equation produces a perfect strategy for that game. Let us also mention that kXOR equations are defined similarly, but only
k=3
is used here.

A major issue is the so called
solvability threshold
, i.e. fix the size
m\times n
of the system, and understand the solvability of randomly (uniformly distributed) generated 3XOR matrices
\Gamma
and vectors
b
. In particular, we study the probability

(3.1)

P_{m,n}:=P(\Gamma z=b\;\text{has a binary vector solution }z).

A basic phenomena is the existence of a “solvability threshold”
c^{*}
, which is the unique non negative number, such that as
m,n\to\infty
and
\frac{m}{n}\to c
, the probability (
3.1
) of randomly generated

m\times n
3XOR problems converges to

(3.2)

\lim\limits_{m/n\rightarrow c}P_{m,n}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }c<c^{*},\\
0,&\text{if }c>c^{*}.\end{cases}

Our interest in games came from a recent advance
[
1
,
9
]
on 3XOR-GAMEs showing real (as opposed to binary) solutions give a “perfect quantum strategy” for a 3XOR-GAME. Unfortunately, the classical game threshold had not been analyzed and doing so led to needing the discrete Laplace method.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
(n_{k})_{k}
,
(m_{k})_{k}

There there exists an open neighborhood

Proof.

The approximation (

∎

A.1.
Standard asymptotic notation

Here we will give a short review of standard notation for asymptotics.

Definition A.2
.

i)  Let
(a_{k})_{k},(b_{k})_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
be sequences of complex numbers. We say that

a_{k}\sim b_{k},\qquad\text{as }k\to\infty,

if and only if

\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\Big|\frac{b_{k}}{a_{k}}-1\Big|=0.

ii)  Let
a_{k},b_{k}:\mathcal{W}_{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be two sequences of functions, defined on some set
\mathcal{W}_{k}
. We say that

(A.5)

a_{k}(x)\sim b_{k}(x),\qquad\text{as }k\to\infty,\text{ uniformly for }x\in\mathcal{W}_{k},

if and only if

\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\sup\limits_{x\in\mathcal{W}_{k}}\Big|\frac{b_{k}(x)}{a_{k}(x)}-1\Big|=0.

The notion of asymptotics in Definition
A.2
now has the following useful properties.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
(a_{k})_{k},(b_{k})_{k},(c_{k})_{k},(d_{k})_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
are sequences. Then the notion
\sim
of asymptotics in Definition
A.2
has the following properties:

i)

If
a_{k}\sim b_{k}
and
b_{k}\sim c_{k}
, as
k\to\infty
, then also
a_{k}\sim c_{k}
, as
k\to\infty
.

ii)

If
a_{k}\sim b_{k}
and
c_{k}\sim d_{k}
, as
k\to\infty
, then also
a_{k}c_{k}\sim b_{k}d_{k}
, as
k\to\infty
.

iii)

If
a_{k}\sim b_{k}
, as
k\to\infty
, then also
\frac{1}{a_{k}}\sim\frac{1}{b_{k}}
, as
k\to\infty
.

If
a_{k},b_{k},c_{k},d_{k}:\mathcal{W}_{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
are sequences of functions, the above results i) and ii) are still valid if one considers
\sim
as in Eq.(
A.5
) uniformly for
x\in\mathcal{W}_{k}
. The property iii) needs the additional assumption
\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{W}_{k}}\frac{|a_{k}(x)|}{|b_{k}(x)|}<\infty
.

A.2.
Ingredients of the summands
\mathcal{S}_{m,n}(r,\alpha)
and estimates on them

A central role in this subsection will play the function

(A.6)

Q(x):=\frac{x(e^{x}-1)}{e^{x}-1-x},\qquad x>0.

Since
Q
is strictly increasing, with limits
\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}Q(x)=2
and
\lim_{x\to\infty}Q(x)=\infty
, there exists the (again strictly increasing) inverse function

Q^{-1}\colon(2,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty).

With these functions
Q
and
Q^{-1}
, and also their combinations

(A.7)

\displaystyle P(\xi):=

\displaystyle\sqrt{Q^{-1}(\xi)Q^{\prime}(Q^{-1}(\xi))},\qquad\xi\in(2,\infty),

(A.8)

\displaystyle A(\xi):=

\displaystyle e^{Q^{-1}(\xi)}-1-Q^{-1}(\xi),\qquad\xi\in(2,\infty),

let us define the functions

(A.9)

\displaystyle g^{S}(p,q):=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi q}\,P(\frac{p}{q})},&1\leq q<\frac{p}{2},\\
1,&q=\frac{p}{2},\\
0,&q=0\text{ or }q>\frac{p}{2},\end{cases}\qquad p\in\mathbb{N},\,q\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

(A.10)

\displaystyle h^{S}(\xi):=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\xi\ln(Q^{-1}(\xi))-\ln(A(\xi)),&\xi\in(2,\infty),\\
\ln(2),&\xi\in(0,2]\cup\{\infty\}.\end{cases}

Note that one can simply show that the function
h^{S}
is continuous in the point
\xi=2
, but not at
\infty
, since there is
\lim_{\xi\rightarrow\infty}h^{S}(\xi)=\infty\neq\ln(2)=h^{S}(\infty)
.

Proposition A.4
.

i)  There exist constants
0<c_{S}\leq C_{S}
, such that the Stirling numbers satisfy the estimate

(A.11)

c_{S}\frac{p!}{q!}g^{S}(p,q)e^{-qh^{S}(\frac{p}{q})}\leq S_{2}(p,q)\leq C_{S}\frac{p!}{q!}g^{S}(p,q)e^{-qh^{S}(\frac{p}{q})},\qquad p\in\mathbb{N},\,q\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

ii)  Let
(m_{k})_{k},(n_{k})_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, with
\lim_{k\to\infty}m_{k}=\lim_{k\to\infty}n_{k}=\infty
and
\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{m_{k}}{n_{k}}>\frac{2}{3}
. Then there exists a neighborhood
\mathcal{N}_{0}
of
(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
, such that

(A.12)

S_{2}(3rm_{k},\alpha n_{k})\sim\frac{(3rm_{k})!}{(\alpha n_{k})!}g^{S}(3rm_{k},\alpha n_{k})e^{-\alpha n_{k}h^{S}(\frac{3rm_{k}}{\alpha n_{k}})},\quad\text{as }k\to\infty,

uniformly for
(r,\alpha)\in\mathcal{N}_{0}\cap\Lambda_{m_{k},n_{k}}
. Here the asymptotic
\sim
is understood in the sense of Eq.(
A.5
).

Proof.

i)  For
q=\frac{p}{2}
, one can easily verify inductively from Eq.(
A.1
) the specific Stirling numbers
S_{2}(2q,q)=\frac{(2q)!}{q!2^{q}}
. With the explicit values
g^{S}(2q,q)=1
and
h^{S}(\frac{2q}{q})=\ln(2)
, we then conclude the equality

S_{2}(p,q)=\frac{(2q)!}{q!2^{q}}=\frac{p!}{q!}g^{S}(p,q)e^{-qh^{S}(\frac{p}{q})}.

For
q>\frac{p}{2}
, the Stirling numbers
S_{2}(p,q)=0
vanish, and likewise for
q=0
(since the case
p=0
is not covered by the statement (
A.11
)). In both cases also
g^{S}(p,q)=0
, and hence (
A.11
) is satisfied with equality. For the crucial region
1\leq q<\frac{p}{2}
, we know from
[
3
, Theorem 1.2.1]
that there exists a function
E\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
, with
\lim_{p\to\infty}E(p)=0
, which is an upper bound of

(A.13)

\Big|\frac{F(p,q)}{S_{2}(p,q)}-1\Big|\leq E(p),\qquad 1\leq q<\frac{p}{2},

where the function
F
is given by

F(p,q):=\frac{p!\sqrt{2\pi(p-2q)}}{q!(p-2q)!}\Big(\frac{p-2q}{e}\Big)^{p-2q}g^{S}(p,q)e^{-qh^{S}(\frac{p}{q})}.

This estimate on the one hand shows that

(A.14)

\frac{F(p,q)}{S_{2}(p,q)}\leq\bigg|\frac{F(p,q)}{S_{2}(p,q)}-1\bigg|+1\leq E(p)+1\leq\sup\limits_{p\in\mathbb{N}}E(p)+1=:\frac{1}{c_{F}}.

On the other hand, we can choose
N\in\mathbb{N}
large enough, such that
E(p)\leq\frac{1}{2}
for every
p\geq N
. For those values of
p
we then get

\frac{F(p,q)}{S_{2}(p,q)}\geq\frac{1}{2},\qquad p\geq N,\,1\leq q<\frac{p}{2}.

If we now choose
\frac{1}{C_{F}}:=\min\Big\{\frac{1}{2}\,,\,\min_{1\leq q<\frac{p}{2}<\frac{N}{2}}\frac{F(p,q)}{S_{2}(p,q)}\Big\}>0
, we get the lower bound

(A.15)

\frac{F(p,q)}{S_{2}(p,q)}\geq\frac{1}{C_{F}},\qquad 1\leq q<\frac{p}{2}.

Combining now Eq.(
A.14
) and (
A.15
) gives the upper and lower bound of the Stirling number

(A.16)

c_{F}F(p,q)\leq S_{2}(p,q)\leq C_{F}F(p,q).

Next, from the Stirling approximation

(A.17)

\Big|\frac{n!}{\sqrt{2\pi n}}\Big(\frac{e}{n}\Big)^{n}-1\Big|\leq e^{\frac{1}{12n}}-1,\qquad n\in\mathbb{N},

there follows the estimate

(A.18)

2-e^{\frac{1}{12}}\leq\frac{n!}{\sqrt{2\pi n}}\Big(\frac{e}{n}\Big)^{n}\leq e^{\frac{1}{12}},\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Using this inequality with
n=p-2q
, then gives

(A.19)

2-e^{\frac{1}{12}}\leq\frac{\frac{p!}{q!}g^{S}(p,q)e^{-qh^{S}(\frac{p}{q})}}{F(p,q)}=\frac{(p-2q)!}{\sqrt{2\pi(p-2q)}}\Big(\frac{e}{p-2q}\Big)^{p-2q}\leq e^{\frac{1}{12}}.

Combining now Eq.(
A.16
) and (
A.19
) then finally gives the upper and lower bound (
A.11
) of the Stirling numbers, namely

\frac{c_{F}}{e^{\frac{1}{12}}}\frac{p!}{q!}g^{S}(p,q)e^{-qh^{S}(\frac{p}{q})}\leq S_{2}(p,q)\leq\frac{C_{F}}{2-e^{\frac{1}{12}}}\frac{p!}{q!}g^{S}(p,q)e^{-qh^{S}(\frac{p}{q})}.

ii)  Since there is
\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{m_{k}}{n_{k}}=:c>\frac{2}{3}
, we are able to choose
\widetilde{c}\in(\frac{2}{3},c)
arbitrary but fixed. Corresponding to this
\widetilde{c}
, there exists some
\varepsilon>0
, and some
N\in\mathbb{N}
, such that

\frac{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon}{\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon}<\frac{3\widetilde{c}}{2},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{m_{k}}{n_{k}}\geq\widetilde{c},\quad\text{for every }k\geq N.

Choose now
\mathcal{N}_{0}
as the open ball of radius
\varepsilon
, centered at
(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
. Then for every
(r,\alpha)\in\mathcal{N}_{0}
and every
k\geq N
, there is

\frac{\alpha}{r}\leq\frac{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon}{\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon}<\frac{3\widetilde{c}}{2}\leq\frac{3m_{k}}{2n_{k}}.

Rearranging this inequality means that

(A.20)

\alpha n_{k}<\frac{3rm_{k}}{2},\qquad(r,\alpha)\in\mathcal{N}_{0},\,k\geq N.

Then the inequality (
A.13
) with the values
p=3rm_{k}
and
q=\alpha n_{k}
, gives

\Big|\frac{F(3rm_{k},\alpha n_{k})}{S_{2}(3rm_{k},\alpha n_{k})}-1\Big|\leq E(3rm_{k})\leq E\Big(3\Big(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon\Big)m_{k}\Big),\qquad(r,\alpha)\in\mathcal{N}_{0}\cap\Lambda_{m_{k},n_{k}},\,k\geq N,

where in the second inequality we used
r\geq\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon
as well as the monotonicity of
E
. Note that in
[
3
, Theorem 1.2.1]
, the function
E
is not assumed to be monotone decreasing, and only defined for integers
p\geq 1
. However, it is obvious that one can choose a function
E
which satisfies the inequality (
A.13
), still converges as
\lim_{x\to\infty}E(x)=0
, is monotone decreasing as well as defined on the whole half line
(0,\infty)
, for example
\widetilde{E}(x):=\sup_{p\in\mathbb{N},p\geq x}E(p)
. Since the right hand side is independent of
(r,\alpha)
this inequality also holds for the supremum

\sup\limits_{(r,\alpha)\in\mathcal{N}_{0}\cap\Lambda_{m_{k},n_{k}}}\Big|\frac{F(3rm_{k},\alpha n_{k})}{S_{2}(3rm_{k},\alpha n_{k})}-1\Big|\leq E\Big(3\Big(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon\Big)m_{k}\Big),\qquad k\geq N.

Finally, we can perform the limit
k\to\infty
of this inequality. Using
\lim_{p\to\infty}E(p)=0
, one sees that the right hand side of this inequality vanishes, and we obtain the asymptotics

(A.21)

S_{2}(3rm_{k},\alpha n_{k})\sim F(3rm_{k},\alpha n_{k}),\qquad\text{as }k\to\infty,\text{ uniformly in }(r,\alpha)\in\mathcal{N}_{0}\cap\Lambda_{m_{k},n_{k}}.

Since the Stirling approximation
Equation

A.17
shows that

(A.22)

n!\sim\sqrt{2\pi n}\,\Big(\frac{n}{e}\Big)^{n},\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty,

we can further simplify the asymptotics (
A.21
) to

\displaystyle S_{2}(3rm_{k},\alpha n_{k})

\displaystyle\sim F(3rm_{k},\alpha n_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{(3rm_{k})!\sqrt{2\pi(3rm_{k}-2\alpha n_{k})}}{(\alpha n_{k})!(3rm_{k}-2\alpha n_{k})!}\Big(\frac{3rm_{k}-2\alpha n_{k}}{e}\Big)^{3rm_{k}-2\alpha n_{k}}

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt\times g^{S}(3rm_{k},\alpha n_{k})e^{-\alpha n_{k}h^{S}(\frac{3rm_{k}}{\alpha n_{k}})}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{(3rm_{k})!}{(\alpha n_{k})!}g^{S}(3rm_{k},\alpha n_{k})e^{-\alpha n_{k}h^{S}(\frac{3rm_{k}}{\alpha n_{k}})}.\qed

Let us now consider some crucial bound on the function
g^{S}
. The upcoming inequality (
A.24
) is by no means obvious since
g^{S}
has a singularity for
q\to(\frac{p}{2})^{-}
. However, since we only consider
(r,\alpha)\in\mathcal{W}\cap\Lambda_{m,n}
on the grid, it happens that there exists a uniform upper bound.

Lemma A.5
.

For every
p\in\mathbb{N}
,
q\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
with
1\leq q<\frac{p}{2}
, there holds the estimate

(A.23)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi p}}\leq g^{S}(p,q)\leq\frac{\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{2\pi(p-2q)}}

Furthermore, for every
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
there holds the estimate

(A.24)

g^{S}(3rm,\alpha n)\leq 1,\qquad(r,\alpha)\in\widehat{\mathcal{W}}\cap\Lambda_{m,n}.

Proof.

It can be checked easily from the definition of
Q
in (
A.6
), that
Q^{-1}
and
Q^{\prime}
admit the upper and lower bounds

\xi-2\leq Q^{-1}(\xi)\leq\xi,\quad\xi\in(2,\infty),\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{1}{3}\leq Q^{\prime}(x)\leq 1,\quad x\in(0,\infty).

From these two inequalities it then follows that

\frac{\xi-2}{3}\leq Q^{-1}(\xi)Q^{\prime}(Q^{-1}(\xi))\leq\xi,\qquad\xi\in(2,\infty),

and we immediately conclude that the function
g^{S}
is for every
1\leq q<\frac{p}{2}
bounded by

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi p}}\leq g^{S}(p,q)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi qQ^{-1}(\frac{p}{q})Q^{\prime}(Q^{-1}(\frac{p}{q}))}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{2\pi(p-2q)}}.

For the second inequality (
A.24
), let
p=3rm
and
q=\alpha n
. We only have to consider
1\leq q<\frac{p}{2}
, since all other cases are trivial by the definition of
g^{S}
in (

). Since
p=3rm
and

are integers due to

,

holds with

. So we conclude the uniform upper bound

g^{S}(3rm,\alpha n)\leq\frac{\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{2\pi(3rm-2\alpha n)}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\leq 1.\qed

Next, we want to derive an estimate as well as an asymptotic formula for the binomial coefficient.
We will do this using the functions

(A.25)

\displaystyle g^{B}(p,q):=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{q}}{\sqrt{2\pi\max\{p,1\}\max\{q-p,1\}}},\qquad p\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\,q\in\mathbb{N},\,p\leq q,

(A.26)

\displaystyle h^{B}(\xi):=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\xi\ln(\xi)+(1-\xi)\ln(1-\xi),&\xi\in(0,1),\\
0,&\xi=0\text{ or }\xi=1.\end{cases}

Note that the function
h^{B}
is continuous in
\xi=0
and in
\xi=1
.

Lemma A.6
.

i)  There exist constants
0<c_{B}\leq C_{B}
such that for every
p\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
q\in\mathbb{N}
with
p\leq q
, the binomial coefficient is bounded from below and from above by

(A.27)

c_{B}\,g^{B}(p,q)e^{-qh^{B}(\frac{p}{q})}\leq\binom{q}{p}\leq C_{B}\,g^{B}(p,q)e^{-qh^{B}(\frac{p}{q})}.

ii)  Let
(p_{k})_{k},(q_{k})_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, with
\lim_{k\to\infty}p_{k}=\lim_{k\to\infty}q_{k}=\infty
and
\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}\in(0,1)
. Then the binomial coefficient admits the asymptotics

(A.28)

\binom{q_{k}}{p_{k}}\sim g^{B}(p_{k},q_{k})e^{-q_{k}h^{B}(\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}})},\qquad\text{as }k\to\infty.

i)  For
p=0
or
p=q

\binom{q}{p}=1=\frac{\sqrt{q}}{\sqrt{\max\{p,1\}\max\{q-p,1\}}}=\sqrt{2\pi}\,g^{B}(p,q)e^{-qh^{B}(\frac{p}{q})}.

ii)  Using the Stirling asymptotics (

h_{y}({\frac{1}{3}},\alpha_{y}({\frac{1}{3}}))>0.

Lemma

\lim_{r\to 1}h_{y}(r,\alpha_{y}(r))>0.

So the infimum cannot be located on either of the two endpoints, thus it must be a local minimum attained for some

. However, Dubois-Mandler in

5

has only one local minimum on the interior of

; located at
(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
and with

Their proof has considerable detail so we do not reproduce it. (Note the notational difference; they instead prove

, where

and

). Since
\alpha_{y}(\frac{1}{2})=\frac{1}{2}
by (

), this implies the infimum of
h_{y}(r,\alpha_{y}(r))
is attained only at

.
∎

A.4.3.

Now that all the preparations are done, we are in the position to prove the initial theorem of this subsection.

Proof of

A.8
.

The goal is to show

for all
(r,\alpha)\in\mathcal{W}\setminus\{(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})\}

Thus, the problem is reduced to finding the minimum of

in the trapezoid

A.10

, such that the infimum of

,

for
