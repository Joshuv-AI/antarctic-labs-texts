The multifaceted roles of tumor-infiltrating plasma cells in cancer immunology
Authors: Yuting Jiang, Qian Zhang, Zhengnan Yang, Kailin Yang, Shengtao Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractActivated B cells differentiate into plasma cells that can produce a vast repertoire of antibodies. The discovery of tumor-infiltrating plasma cells (TIPCs) has highlighted the importance of their interactions with the host immune system in cancer hallmarks. Notably, tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs) serve as crucial sites for TIPC generation, supporting activation, maturation, and class-switch recombination (CSR). These structures strongly influence the functional orientation of TIPCs and are often associated with favorable clinical outcomes in various cancers. CSR further diversifies antibody isotypes, enabling distinct effector functions. Nevertheless, there are still large controversies over the exact roles of TIPCs, with evidence pointing to both pro- and antitumor activities depending on the tumor context. In this review, we revisit the basics of plasma cell biology, the impact of TLSs and CSR on TIPC function, and the contrasting effects of TIPCs on tumor progression and immune responses. We aim to provide further insights into the potential utility of targeting plasma cell biology as therapeutic approaches for cancer patients in clinical settings.Keywords: Plasma cell, Antibody isotypes, Antibody-independent function, Tumor microenvironment, Tertiary lymphoid structure, Class-switch recombination, Dual effects

Introduction

Tapping into the power of the immune system to combat cancer has been both inspirational and challenging. Immunotherapy has revolutionized cancer therapy, mainly based on the well-established pivotal roles of T cells in tumor immunosurveillance [1]. However, the relative contributions of the tumor-infiltrating B cells (TIBs) and their differentiated forms, such as plasma cells (PCs) or antibody secreting cells (ASCs), remain enigmatic [2], despite evidence indicating that TIL-Bs constitute a substantial part of the immune cell population, comprising as much as 40% of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) in some tumors [[3], [4], [5]].

TIBs exhibit greater phenotypic and functional heterogeneity compared to their counterparts in peripheral blood or non-tumor tissues [6]. Characterized subpopulations include PCs, regulatory B cells (Bregs), double-negative (DN) B cells, exhausted-like B cells (e.g., CD69⁺CD27⁻CD21⁻), germinal center (GC)-like B cells, and memory B cells [7]. Bregs actively sculpt an immunosuppressive niche through interleukin-10 (IL-10), transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), and granzyme B (GZMB), inhibiting effector T cells and promoting regulatory T cells (Tregs) [8]. Conversely, memory B cells and GC-like B cells display potent antigen-presenting capabilities and enhance the activation of CD4⁺ T cells. Notably, the enrichment of GC-like B cells and TLSs has been associated with improved clinical outcomes in several tumor types, including HPV+ head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) [9].

In several tumor types, including ovarian cancer, colorectal cancer, and nasopharyngeal carcinoma, TIPCs constitute a substantial proportion of TIBs. Notably, in glioblastoma, approximately 47.47% of intratumoral B-lineage cells were predicted to be TIPCs, a stark contrast to the 0.5%−1.4% typically observed in the peripheral blood of healthy individuals [10]. These cells undergo extensive metabolic rewiring and transcriptional reprogramming to sustain massive antibody production, laying the cornerstone of the humoral response during infection, chronic inflammation, and cancer immunity [11].

TIPCs can generate antibodies through two distinct mechanisms: one acquires tumor reactivity through somatic hypermutations (SHMs), while the other exhibits germline-encoded autoreactivity independent of SHMs [7,12]. TIPCs in ovarian cancer are both somatically mutated and clonally expanded, producing tumor-reactive antibodies against matrix metallopeptidase 14 (MMP14), a molecule abundantly expressed on cancer cells [12]. This dual mechanism of antibody autoreactivity closely resembles that observed in autoimmune diseases [13]. However, while autoimmune PCs typically emerge in the context of sustained inflammatory activation, TIPCs exist within a dynamically evolving tumor microenvironment that shapes their differentiation and function. As a result, TIPCs may adopt either antitumor or pro-tumor roles, depending on cues from the evolving tumor milieu.

TIPCs are increasingly recognized for mediating antitumor immunity through tumor-specific antibody secretion [[14], [15], [16], [17]], whereas the presence of certain subsets has been shown to be a negative indicator of prognosis [[18], [19], [20]]. Protective roles are exemplified by polyclonal IgA from TIPCs in ovarian cancer, which inhibits metastasis through IgA transcytosis and myeloid cell redirection [21]. Nevertheless, tumor-educated PCs in lung adenocarcinoma secrete HSPA4-specific IgG to foster pre-metastatic niche formation [22]. Intriguingly, single-cell RNA sequencing of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) reveals that plasma-like B cells initially suppress tumor growth but later drive progression in advanced stages [23]. This context-dependent functional switch may arise from immunoglobulin isotype specificity (e.g., IgA vs. IgG) and dynamic crosstalk with evolving tumor microenvironments [2].

Emerging evidence extends PC functionality beyond antibody secretion. Particularly, plasma cell-derived cytokines and extracellular vesicles are gaining attention as key mediators of tumor-immune crosstalk [24,25]. Collectively, this review focuses on the evolving functions of plasma cells across the major aspects of tumor immunity (Table 1), and promising roles as targets or effector agents of cancer immunotherapies.

Basics of plasma cell biology

B-lineage cell development in the bone marrow

In the bone marrow, hematopoietic stem cells differentiate into multipotent lymphoid progenitors, some of which become committed to the B-cell lineage. The onset of commitment to the B-lineage is the rearrangement of diversity and joining gene segments at the Ig heavy (H) chain locus occurring in CD10+CD22+CD34+progenitor (pro-B) cells. After additional variable rearrangement, the precursor B cell receptor (pre-BCR) is constituted by correctly rearranged µ heavy chains and surrogate light (L) chains in the large pre-B cells. Further, through several rounds of cell division, IgL recombination at the λ or κ locus is completed in the small pre-B cell stage. Subsequently, a newly formed immature B cell displays a BCR composed of functionally rearranged IgH and IgL. Typically, immature B cells express only surface IgM. Prior to entering peripheral organs, B cells need to establish central tolerance to autoantigens through mechanisms such as clonal deletion and receptor editing, which result in the death of approximately 85% of newly produced B cells [26]. Immature B cells egress from the bone marrow through coordinated CXCL12/CXCR4 axis downregulation and sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) gradient sensing [27,28], and then migrate to the secondary lymphoid organs (SLOs) guided by the CCL21-CCR7, CCL19-CCR7 or CXCL13-CXCR5 axis [29,30].

Plasma cell maturation and activation in the periphery

In the circulation, immature B cells express weakly self-reactive BCRs and are subject to regulation by several peripheral tolerance mechanisms involving anergy, ignorance and inhibition by T cells. Circulating to the SLOs, newly migrated immature B cells undergo further development and selection in the splenic marginal zone and lymphoid follicles. Both IgD and IgM BCRs with identical specificity are displayed in the resulting mature naïve B cells, which comprise three major well-defined subsets, namely B1, mostly located at mucosal sites and in pleural and peritoneal cavities, follicular B2 (FOB) and marginal zone B (MZB) cells [31]. FOB cells are the principal long-lived population and present in the lymphoid follicles of the lymph nodes and spleen [32]. Conversely, MZB cells about the splenic marginal sinus, which facilitates rapid defense against blood-borne pathogens [32]. Despite that MZB and B1 cells are able to respond to T cell-independent antigens (TI antigens), FOB cells, the best studied subset, are particularly adept at responding to antigens that simultaneously elicit follicular helper T (Tfh) cell activation [33,34].

Plasma cells originate primarily from mature B cell compartments, including memory B cells, GC centroblasts and MZB cells. There is a two-phase process by which an antigen stimulates the production of long-lived PCs [35]. In the first phase of the primary response, following BCR activation, co-receptor engagement, CD4+ T cell help and cytokine signals, there are three trajectories of activated naïve B cells, including differentiation into GC B cells, short-lived plasma cells or memory B cells [36]. In response to TI antigens, MZB cells rapidly proliferate into plasmablasts and short-lived plasma cells.

In the second phase of the primary response, the newly differentiated GC B cells participate in a newly formed GC reaction, accompanied by three major fates: generation of long-lived PCs, differentiation into memory cells, or re-entry into the GC reaction [35]. GC is a specialized microenvironment where B cells undergo SHM and affinity-based selection for affinity maturation. SHM, an antigen-selected evolution, occurs primarily in the dark zone (DZ) [37]. The non-functional BCRs generated from SHM events account for almost 75% programmed cell death [38]. Recent findings uncover that CSR is triggered during the initial T cell: B cell interaction prior to GC formation, and largely ceases upon the GC B cell differentiation and the onset of SHM [39]. In the light zone (LZ), B cells with the most stable Tfh interplays mediated by signaling lymphocytic activation molecule (SLAM) and intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1) have a higher tendency to express interferon regulatory factor 4 (IRF4) above the threshold necessary to support expression of the canonical PC transcription factor B-lymphocyte-induced maturation protein 1 (BLIMP1) but to quench B-cell lymphoma 6 (BCL6) and additional GC-related programs, and to differentiate into long-lived PCs [40].

Recent work has revealed that the GC LZ is a hypoxic niche, where oxygen tension shapes B cell metabolism and fate [41]. Under hypoxia, stabilization of hypoxia-inducible factors (HIF-1α/HIF-2α) and concomitant inhibition of mTORC1 reduce B cell proliferation and survival, while sharply limiting AID expression and CSR to the pro‐inflammatory IgG2c isotype [41]. B cell-specific deletion of the von Hippel-Landau tumor suppressor protein, mimicking chronic HIF activation, recapitulates these effects in vivo, resulting in loss of antigen‐specific GC B cells, impaired generation of high‐affinity IgG2c antibodies and memory B cells, and weakened recall responses. By analogy, the chronically hypoxic TME may impose a similar metabolic brake on TIPCs, altering their antibody secretion, isotype distribution, and overall anti‑tumor efficacy.

The differentiation of B cells into PCs is believed to be strongly influenced by the affinity of their BCRs [42]. Intriguingly, recent studies demonstrate that PC precursors (pre-PCs)—including even those bearing low-affinity BCRs—can undergo permissive selection from the GC LZ into the PC differentiation pathway [43,44]. However, once exiting the GC microenvironment, these pre-PCs exhibit affinity-dependent clonal expansion modulated by the strength of prior Tfh-derived signals, particularly through Myc expression dynamics [44]. This results in preferential proliferation of high-affinity pre-PCs, ultimately leading to their overrepresentation in the circulating antibody repertoire. This mechanism reconciles the apparent paradox of affinity-permissive GC egress with the observed serological affinity maturation.

Tumor antigens, tertiary lymphoid structures, and antibody isotypes

There is a rising acknowledgement that tumor rejection antigens can be roughly divided into two categories [45]. One class consists of non-mutated proteins, to which T cells display incomplete tolerance. On the other hand, in the course of the multistep transformation of normal cells to clinical malignancies, tumor neoantigens arising as a consequence of tumor-specific mutations can elicit innate and adaptive antitumor immune responses [46]. The majority of neoantigens are not encoded by the normal human genome. Correspondingly, it is postulated that neoantigens are of paramount significance in cancer containment relative to non-mutated self-antigens, in light of the central tolerance mechanism during T cell development [45,47]. Notwithstanding, neoantigens commonly originate from passenger mutations, giving rise to heterogeneous distribution and variable immunogenicity among distinct clones of cancer cells [48]. Hence, the less immunogenic the mutation-derived neoantigens are, the faster the progression of tumor cell clones becomes [48,49].

Subsequently, B cells encounter tumor-associated antigens and produce a diversity of antibodies. Bulk RNA sequencing has uncovered the occurrence of somatically mutated Ig genes in tumor tissues [50,51]. Indeed, the presence of intratumoral GC B cells has been reported in several investigations and is frequently detected in the context of tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs) [[52], [53], [54]]. TLSs are well-organized, transient ectopic lymphoid aggregates that resemble SLOs in structure, composed mainly of B cell follicles, T cell zones, dendritic cell (DC) networks, stromal cells, macrophages, endothelial cells, and high endothelial venules [55]. In the tumor mass, TLSs are typically located in stromal regions, mainly at the invasive margin [56]. Although SLOs are the major sites where an efficient adaptive immune response occurs, recent studies about the tumor microenvironment (TME) have documented that intratumoral TLSs can directly regulate antitumor defenses [54,55]. Since TLSs facilitate inflows of different immune cell populations, such as T cells, DCs and macrophages, into the tumor site, the formation of TLSs may boost antigen presentation, trigger the production of tumor-specific antibodies and strengthen cytokine-mediated signaling [57], and is correlated with favorable clinical outcomes and superior responses to immunotherapies in a variety of neoplasms [58]. By leveraging their proximity to cancer nests, B cells located within mature TLSs have quicker access to tumor-specific antigens compared to those located in distant periphery organs [59]. In GCs within mature TLSs, selected B cell clones differentiate into plasma cells producing antibodies, particularly IgG and IgA isotypes, targeting cancer cells, whereas B cells may differentiate into Bregs with immunosuppressive activities in the absence of mature TLSs.

The oncological outcomes resulting from tumor-specific antibodies are, in part, determined by their isotype, as each antibody isotype may stimulate a distinct downstream effector mechanism [60]. Following B cell activation, the first isotype produced is often pentameric IgM, which is highly effective at trapping low-concentration antigens and activating complement. The isotype switching emerges at CSR, which is initiated by activation-induced cytidine deaminase (AID). CSR is a DNA-rearrangement reaction occurring in the locus encoding the constant (C) domain of IgH, which allows IgM-IgD B cells to produce antibodies of IgG, IgA, or IgE classes [61]. Hence, CSR further diversifies Ig repertoires and imparts antibodies with distinct effector means to eradicate antigens without altering the antigen-binding specificity. The downstream effector functions of each antibody isotype can be further regulated by factors such as antibody allotypes, which refer to single-nucleotide polymorphisms in the C domain, and post-translational modifications, notably glycosylation [62]. An IgG1 variant specific to the Asian population (hIgG1-G396R) has been shown to confer prolonged survival of colorectal cancer patients by augmenting the differentiation of IgG1+ PCs, antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP) activity, and antigen processing and presentation to T cells by DCs [63]. However, there remain substantial obstacles to understanding the dynamic nature of CSR in the polyclonal milieu of TLSs or SLOs [64]. Fortunately, high-resolution insights could be afforded into isotype switching with the advent of high-dimensional spectral flow cytometry, scRNA-seq, single-cell BCR sequencing and so on [5,6,[65], [66], [67]].

The origins of TIPCs remain unresolved, with controversies regarding their developmental trajectories across tumor microenvironments. Recent scRNA-seq-based investigations have systematically classified TIPCs into 4–10 functionally distinct subsets across human cancers [5,6,67]. Ma et al. proposed that TIPCs arose from two independent developmental pathways: i) the canonical GC pathway typically occurring within organized lymphoid structures, and ii) an alternative extrafollicular (EF) pathway mediating rapid, GC-independent responses (Fig. 1) [67]. A tumor type–specific preference in TIPC differentiation trajectories has been documented, with colon adenocarcinomas primarily relying on the GC pathway, whereas liver hepatocellular carcinomas engage the EF differentiation route. Clinically, EF pathway dominance correlates strongly with compromised antitumor immunity and adverse prognosis.

The equilibrium between EF and GC differentiation is orchestrated through integrated metabolic-epigenetic circuitry [67]. Of particular interest, atypical memory (AtM) B cells—characterized by an exhausted and bystander-like phenotype—have been identified as primary precursors of EF-derived TIPCs. These GC-independent precursors undergo spatiotemporal repositioning during TLS maturation, transitioning from central positions in immature TLSs to peripheral compartments. Mechanistically, the glutamine-derived metabolite α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) potentiates AtM B cell differentiation through dual mechanisms: transcriptional upregulation of fate-determining factors (T-box transcription factor (T-bet) and basic leucine zipper transcription factor, ATF-like (BATF)) and concurrent activation of mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) signaling. This metabolic rewiring endows AtM B cells with immunomodulatory capabilities that paradoxically attenuate antitumor T-cell responses and foster an immunosuppressive TME. In contrast to the proposition by Ma et al., which posited that TIPCs primarily originated through the EF pathway, evidenced by low levels of SHM [67], Yang et al. argued that the possibility of GC-dependent differentiation should not be entirely ruled out [6]. Their analysis identified two distinct subsets of atypical B cells, featured by FCRL4 and FGR expression, respectively. These subsets exhibited significantly different rates of CSR, reflecting varying degrees of GC engagement during TIPC development. Current evidence suggests the dichotomy between GC-derived and EF-generated TIPCs may be overly simplistic, with emerging data supporting a context-dependent model of TIPC differentiation influenced by multiple regulatory mechanisms.

Notably, emerging evidence suggests that TIPCs may originate from peripheral lymphoid tissues rather than being locally generated within the TME. In glioblastoma, the absence of mature TLSs supports the notion that TIPCs are produced and undergo maturation in peripheral lymphoid organs [10]. These cells are subsequently recruited into the GSC niche via the CCL2–CCR2 chemokine axis, where they contribute to tumor progression.

Antitumoral actions of plasma cells

Multiple lines of evidence show that the infiltration of melanoma [68,69], prostate [16] and lung tumors [70,71] by plasma cells, and high levels of intratumoral antibodies in breast [72], HNSCC [51], melanoma [51,73,74], lung [75], endometrial [76] and ovarian cancers [14,77] correlate with superior clinical outcomes, underlying the vital roles of intratumorally resident plasma cells. The oncolytic effects of TIPCs are mainly accomplished by antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) and ADCP executed by activated myeloid cells, improving antigen presentation efficiency and driving complement activation (Fig. 1) [62].

IgG

RNA-seq analysis of isotypes from 9025 TCGA samples has proven IgG as the most predominant class, followed by IgA and IgM, spanning 32 diverse tumor types [78,79]. In a human papillomavirus (HPV) positive HNSCC cohort, 80%–90% of antibody-secreting cells produce IgG in the tumor stroma, reflecting its predominant role in the humoral immune defense [80]. Higher levels of HPV-specific antibodies correlate with better outcomes and lower recurrence risk in HPV+ HNSCC patients [80,81].

Beyond recognizing viral antigens, endogenous IgG antibodies can directly target tumor cell surfaces. Large-scale analyses have demonstrated that tumor cells from multiple human cancers are frequently coated with IgG antibodies [12]. IgG coating was also observed in renal cell carcinoma and urothelial carcinoma. Among the IgG subclasses (IgG1, IgG2, IgG3 and IgG4), from the standpoint of tumor-suppressing responses, the IgG1 subclass is of paramount importance since its ability to initiate ADCC and ADCP, facilitate complement-dependent cytotoxicity, and broaden T-cell responses through the highly efficient delivery of IgG1-complexed cancer antigens via the activatory receptors for the fragment crystallizable region of IgG (FcγRs) on DCs (Fig. 1) [82,83]. Polyclonal IgG antibodies, predominantly IgG1, isolated from ascites fluids and sera of ovarian cancer patients effectively promote ADCP and ADCC against tumor targets in vitro [12].

Although IgG3 antibodies are also able to induce the aforementioned antitumor mechanisms, IgG3 is less effective in melanoma prognosis [74]. Compared to IgG1, IgG3 has inferior affinity and a lower level of accumulated hypermutations, while possessing high affinity for FcγRI and FcγRIIIA [84]. Nonspecific IgG3 competes more efficiently than IgG1 for vacant FcγRs [2]. The switch from IgG3 to IgG1 (IgG3-1) is enriched in various cancers and confers a survival benefit for patients with high SHM rates [78]. Moreover, there are positive relationships between the IgG3-1 switch level and the expression level of FCGR3A (encoding CD16a) on NK cells, indicating a synergistic tumor-killing effect by NK cells and TIPCs [78].

Equally important is the newly uncovered mechanism of pathological IgG internalization by cancer cells via endocytosis [23]. In the malignant cells of NSCLC, IgGs bind to the assembly polypeptide 2 (AP-2) complexes that are pivotal for clathrin-mediated endocytosis [23,85]. Inside tumor cells, IgGs then interact with the cytosolic Fc receptor tripartite motif containing 21 (TRIM21) [86]. Acting as an E3 ubiquitin protein ligase, TRIM21 transfers Fab-recognizing targets to the ubiquitin proteasome system, a fundamental mechanism of immune surveillance coupled to antigen degradation [86]. Indeed, Ras homologue C (RhoC), an oncogene, is upregulated in NSCLC cells and serves as a representative target for IgGs [87]. The recognition of RhoC by the imported IgGs stimulates its degradation via the ubiquitin-mediated pathway, implying the potential antitumor effects of these IgGs [23].

Reportedly, TIPC is the principal source of immunoglobulin kappa C (IGKC), further in support of the protective immune response. In accordance, PC-expressed IGKC has been recognized as a positive indicator of patient survival with solid cancers [15,88,89], and as a compatible immune predictor of response to chemotherapy as well as immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) (Table 1) [90]. High IGKC expression exhibited a negative correlation with total tumor mutational burden (TMB), a key genomic marker of immunotherapy response [5,91]. A similar inverse correlation with clonal TMB was observed, supporting the predictive value for ICB efficacy [5]. These findings raise the possibility that while IGKC-high PCs contribute to favorable immune responses in certain contexts, their abundance may be reduced in tumors with high genomic instability, implying immune escape mechanisms. Despite this, the precise biological contributions of the IGKC signature have not been fully elucidated [92].

IgA

Additionally, robust immune protection is mediated by IgA in many gynecologic tumors, in particular, high-grade serous ovarian cancers (HGSOCs) [21], high-grade endometrioid type and serous endometrial cancers (Fig. 2) [76]. Biswas and coworkers confirmed that the production of class-switched IgA was the predominant response in ovarian cancer, followed by IgG, in line with TCGA data [21]. Remarkably, the coating of malignant cells with IgA, instead of IgG, is linked with improved survival rates and heightened infiltration of intra-epithelial T cells. The intratumoral IgA antibodies, recognizing a wide range of extracellular tumor antigens, direct Fcα/µR+ myeloid cells to antagonize cancer progression. Apart from its antigen-reliant function, IgA is competent to elicit MHC-independent, polymeric Ig receptor (pIgR)-mediated transcytosis [21]. The pIgR is quasi-universally expressed on ovarian and endometrial tumor cells. The IgA-pIgR interactions incite non-specific transcytosis through pIgR+ malignant cells to thwart ovarian cancer evolution, possibly by downregulating tumor-promoting ephrins and upregulating IFNγ receptors [21,93]. These remodeled tumor cells become sensitive to CTL-mediated killing [21]. Moreover, the occupancy of pIgR by IgA drives an array of genome-wide transcriptional alterations in inflammatory pathways related to TNF and IFN signaling in endometrial tumor cells, along with endoplasmic reticulum stress and proapoptotic pathways [76].

The double-edged sword of IgA+ plasma cells in cancer biology. (a) IgA⁺ PCs or plasmablasts contribute to the formation of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment by promoting M2-like tumor-associated macrophages, recruiting and activating myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and Tregs, and directly suppressing cytotoxic CD8⁺ T lymphocytes via PD-1/PD-L1 signaling. They also present antigens to CD4⁺ T cells via MHC-II, further reinforcing immune tolerance. (b) Conversely, IgA can exert antitumor effects. Through transcytosis mediated by the pIgR, IgA binds to cancer cells and modulates tumor signaling, including suppression of Ephrin-MEK/ERK pathways and upregulation of IFNγ-related responses. IgA also enhances ADCP by engaging Fc receptors on myeloid cells, thereby boosting immune-mediated tumor clearance. Created by BioRender.

IgE

With potent immunological activity, IgE is an ancient and evolutionarily conserved isotype of immunoglobulins [94]. IgE responses are most commonly observed in epithelial tissues, including the skin, gut and lungs, where IgE-binding cells typically reside. The consistent presence of external insults is a significant challenge faced by these barrier surfaces. Accordingly, isotype class switching to IgE occurs when stress surveillance by intraepithelial γδ+ T cells is initiated upon topical exposure to the common carcinogen and environmental xenobiotic DMBA (7,12-dimethylbenz[a]anthracene) [95]. The endogenous IgE response confers protection against epithelial neoplastic transformation, and the FcεRI gene expression in human squamous cell carcinoma predicts improved outcomes [95]. Besides, IgE specific for tumor antigens can engage FcεRI on tumor-infiltrating macrophages and monocytes, eliciting cytotoxic effects against tumor cells. Notably, IgE has a remarkably potent binding affinity for the FcεRI expressed on tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) [96], of which increased infiltrations may contribute to tumor development, invasion, neoangiogenesis, and immune escape in many malignancies [97,98]. IgE has the potential to prime macrophages to induce anti-tumor responses [96]. Emerging data suggest that IgE may exert a more significant impact than IgG on reprogramming M2 macrophages towards pro-inflammatory anti-tumoral states, featured by increased expression of M1 macrophage markers including CD80 and TNF-α [99,100]. These data imply that delivery of a tumor-specific IgE could modify the immunological interactions in the TME, possibly leading to tumor elimination [96,101]. In fact, a pioneering first-in-human trial was underway to assess the efficacy of MOv18 IgE, a chimeric antibody recognizing folate receptor alpha (FRα), in the management of solid cancers such as ovarian cancer (NCT02546921) [99]. This Phase I dose-escalation trial demonstrated that MOv18 IgE possessed a tolerable safety profile [102]. Notably, anti-tumor activity was observed in a platinum-resistant ovarian cancer patient with a fall in CA125 and tumor shrinkage, and the maximum tolerated dose was not reached, underscoring the therapeutic potential of IgE antibodies in oncology [102].

TLS

A common denominator among work investigating TIPC-mediated activity is the importance of TLSs. The presence and density of TLSs have been regarded as favorable prognostic biomarkers in multiple types of cancer [3,4,57]. The fate of intratumoral B cells largely depends on the presence and characteristics of TLSs [103]. Updated findings support that mature TLSs serve as crucial locations for in situ maturation, expansion, and isotype switching of B cells, while immature TLSs may promote the generation of Breg cells secreting immunosuppressive factors or T-bet+ double-negative memory B cells [104]. Upon tumor antigen-induced B-cell activation, ongoing humoral responses take place within local cancer-associated TLSs or less organized clusters of infiltrating lymphocytes in most malignancies, leading to the emergence of PCs producing high titers of tumor-specific Igs [14,17,52]. Dense PC infiltrations surrounding TLS are strongly related to multiple cytotoxicity-associated gene products and high levels of CD4+ and CD8+ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes [14]. For ovarian cancer patients, the prognostic benefit undertaken by CD8+ T cells is contingent on the presence of PCs [14]. The most compelling evidence for the essential role of B cells in tumor residency comes from studies of TLS–plasma cell circuits [17]. A spatial transcriptomic analysis of renal cell carcinoma revealed a full continuum of B cell differentiation stages, from activation to PC formation, all enriched at TLSs [17]. Moreover, TLS-driven PCs are able to disseminate into the tumor core along CXCL12+ fibroblastic tracks, locally producing IgG and IgA, and initiating apoptosis of IgG-coated tumor cells [17]. Based on the available data, the TLS is hypothesized to facilitate optimal antitumor immunity via close coordination of antibody- and cytolytic-mediated effector actions. A plasma-cell signature associated with TLSs has demonstrated a positive prognostic value and therapeutic response to immunotherapy for cancer patients [14,17,105].

Comparative perspectives on immunoglobulin isotypes in antitumor immunity

While immunoglobulin isotypes share canonical effector mechanisms such as ADCC, ADCP, and complement activation, their functional divergence arises from distinct receptor engagement, cellular targets, and spatiotemporal regulation within the TME.

IgG1, IgA, and IgE converge on effector cell activation but through disparate pathways: IgG1 primarily signals via FcγR on DCs and NK cells [12,82,83], IgA engages pIgR on tumor cells and Fcα/µR on myeloid subsets [21,93], whereas IgE reprograms macrophages through FcεRI [95,96]. Notably, IgA and IgE exhibit tissue-specific dominance, with IgA in mucosal malignancies and IgE in epithelial barrier cancers, reflecting evolutionary adaptation to anatomical niches.

The interplay between isotypes can be synergistic or antagonistic. For instance, IgG1 and IgA may cooperatively enhance antigen presentation: IgG1-opsonized tumor cells promote DC cross-priming of CD8+ T cells, while IgA-mediated pIgR transcytosis downregulates tumor-intrinsic immunosuppressive signals [21,93]. Conversely, IgG4 and IgG3 may counteract protective responses. IgG4, via Fab-arm exchange, blocks IgG1-mediated ADCC and immune complex formation [[106], [107], [108]], while IgG3 competes with IgG1 for FcγR binding due to its higher receptor affinity, potentially dampening cytotoxic efficacy in melanoma [74,78,84].

The functional output of TIPCs is shaped by the immunological landscape of the TME. In TLS-rich tumors, coordinated IgG/IgA production drives antitumor immunity through B cell-CD8+ T cell crosstalk [14,17]. However, in immunosuppressive niches dominated by TGF-β or IL-10, IgA+ plasma cells may adopt regulatory phenotypes (e.g., PD-L1+IL-10+), directly inhibiting CTLs [20,109]. These findings emphasize how the TME orchestrates the dual immunological roles of TIPCs, paving the way for understanding how these cells can also adopt pro-tumorigenic functions within immunosuppressive tumor ecosystems.

Pro-tumorigenic TIPCs and their interplays with the surrounding milieu

As a hallmark feature, plasma cells are responsible for producing IgGs, which encompass anti-cancer antibodies as a key component [2]. Notwithstanding, increased infiltration of TIPCs and elevated serum Ig concentrations are linked to cancer progression and poor prognosis [19,22,25,74,[110], [111], [112]]. For instance, in glioblastoma, TIPCs promote tumor progression by secreting IgG that binds to FcγRIIA on glioblastoma stem cells (GSCs) [10]. This interaction activates the AKT-mTOR axis, driving GSC proliferation and self-renewal. TIPCs are recruited to GSC niches via CCL2-CCR2 signaling, reinforcing an immunosuppressive TME. These findings suggest that TIPCs in glioblastoma not only support tumor growth but also maintain the hierarchical structure of the tumor through direct interactions with GSCs [10]. Therefore, it is inferred that various solid tumors select robust interactions with host immune responses to establish an immunosuppressive TME, frequently characterized by the accumulation of myeloid cells, Treg cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts and regulatory lymphocyte responses [113,114].

Extracellular vesicles

An important mode of the tumor-generated communication signals is tumor-derived extracellular vesicles (tEVs), also known as microparticles, exosomes or ectosomes [115,116]. The ability of tEVs to traverse long distances in the body without dilution or loss of their contents, like intracellular material or surface receptors, renders them a class of candidate information carriers between tumor and immune cells [116]. The interactions between PCs and tEVs represent a crucial tumor-promoting mechanism. Such tEV-PC interactions may be undermined by subcapsular sinus (SCS) macrophages in draining lymph nodes [117]. A compromised macrophage barrier permits full-fledged tEVs to enter the lymph node cortex and then activate PC proliferation along with increased IgG concentration [117]. Significantly, transferring circulating IgGs from SCS macrophage-depleted mice accelerates melanoma progression [117]. Thus, in contrast to tumor-promoting TAMs [98], the anti-tumor effects of SCS macrophages are partly due to their ability to impede pro-tumor IgG responses, indicating an attractive target for cancer immunotherapy [117]. Notably, plasma cells are able to produce exosomes as well. Taking HGSOC as an example, patients with the mesenchymal subtype, characterized by an abundance of TIPCs, suffer from the worst clinical outcomes [118]. The miR-330-3p contained in TIPC-secreted exosomes hijacks the phenotypic switch into mesenchymal subtype via increasing expression of junctional adhesion molecule B in an unconventional manner [25]. Consistently, the elimination of plasma cells can result in an attenuation of malignant development.

Immune complexes with IgG

The pro-tumoral effects of TIPCs are attributed, in part, to their ability to generate autoantibodies that may form immune complexes with complement proteins. In a K14-HPV16 transgenic mouse model of squamous epithelial carcinoma, B cells, including plasma cells, are found in premalignant skin, accompanied by IgG deposition [119,120]. Deposited immune complexes activate the receptors for Fcγ on myeloid cells, mainly mast cells and macrophages, which stimulates myeloid suppressor-cell activities such as the production of IL-6 [110], and subsequently triggers de novo carcinogenesis [119]. As expected, administration of B cell-depleting antibodies improves the response to standard-of-care chemotherapy in mice bearing pre-existing squamous cell carcinomas by reprogramming macrophages to recruit CTLs [120].

For complement-enriched tumors, IgG1 antibodies may foster tumor progression [121]. C1q is hijacked by renal cell cancer cells, which manufacture C1r, C1s, C3 and C4 as well. IgG1 binding to C1q triggers the classical complement pathway, creating a chronic inflammatory tumor-promoting environment via the C5a/C5aR and C3a/C3aR axes [121]. Noteworthy is the fact that these cancer cells are efficiently protected from complement-induced cellular damage by high expression levels of complement inhibitors like CD59, CD55 and CD46 [122]. Importantly, C1q can also act as a cancer-promoting factor by promoting T-cell exhaustion [121], and favoring angiogenesis independent of complement activation [123]. Interestingly, pathogenic IgG, mainly IgG1 generated from tumor-educated B cells, targets heat shock protein family A member 4 (HSPA4) to promote premetastatic niche formation, and the CXCR4/SDF1α-axis-mediated lymph node metastasis in breast cancer [22].

Immunoregulatory molecules

Immune modulatory chemokines are related to the natural course of cancer as well as to response to treatment, and PC is reported as a crucial type of cytokine-producing B cells. As an illustration, an innate response activator (IRA) B cells in mice, similar to CD138+ PCs, can secrete proinflammatory cytokine granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) [124]. Both human and mouse PCs can also produce IL-17, which is primarily secreted by T helper 17 cells [124,125]. Furthermore, TIPC may be one of the major producers of the immunosuppressive cytokine IL-35 in lung adenocarcinomas [112]. IL-35 is composed of the p35 and Epstein-Barr virus-induced gene 3 (EBI3) subunits, with EBI3 expression being a limiting factor predominantly upregulated in CD138⁺ mature PCs.

Furthermore, as negative regulators of immunity, plasmocytes/plasma cells serve as an important source of IL-10 in vivo [126]. In fact, IRF4, a transcription factor unique to plasma cells, has been demonstrated to promote the expression of IL-10 [107]. Thereby, B cell subsets with the strongest proclivity for IL-10 synthesis are also the most prone to differentiating into ASCs [126]. The IL-10-driven regulatory activities of PCs comprise at least two distinct yet interwoven layers. The initial layer is represented by the differentiation of B cells into natural regulatory plasma cells, expressing lymphocyte-activated gene 3 (LAG-3) [127]. In naïve mice, the development of natural regulatory PCs occurs through a BCR-dependent mechanism, probably in reaction to endogenous TI type II immunogens. LAG-3, an inhibitory receptor with MHC-II as a ligand, induces T-cell dysfunction and exhaustion. Analogous to programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), LAG-3 is a putative marker of T cell exhaustion in cancers [128]. LAG-3 is uniformly present on CD4+ type 1 T regulatory (Tr1) cells and is essential for their immune suppression [129,130]. Of particular importance, the LAG-3 is also constitutively expressed on IL-10+CD138hi cells, emphasizing LAG-3 as a specific biomarker for IL-10-expressing natural regulatory PCs [127]. Additionally, IL-10+CD138hiLAG-3+ PCs express immune checkpoint receptors, such as CD200, programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) and PD-L2, contributing to immune down-regulation [127].

Also of significance are the contributions of IgA+ plasmocytes, expressing PD-L1 as well as IL-10, to the establishment and sustainment of an immunosuppressive TME through directly restraining cytotoxic T lymphocyte activation (Fig. 2) [20,109]. The emergence of immunosuppressive IgA+ cells in humans and mice with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease is a consequence of chronic liver inflammation, leading to the accumulation of Tfh cells and increased secretion of TGF-β, IL-21, and IL-33 [109]. These immunosuppressive IgA+ cells are distinct from intestinal IgA+ B cells, which are responsible for maintaining microbial homeostasis, suppressing inflammation, and preventing tumorigenesis [109]. Moreover, exposed to high concentrations of TGF-β possibly from myofibroblasts, the lymphotoxin-producing CD20+ B cells may transform into IgA+ plasmocytes [20]. Remarkably, the IgA+IL-10+ subset is the most prevalent in patients with metastatic and therapy-resistant prostate cancers [20], while circulating IgA is a well-established adverse prognostic indicator in this setting [131]. Ablation of IgA+PD-L1+IL-10+ cells promotes the reinvigoration and expansion of CD8+ T cells, as demonstrated by the CTL-based regression of hepatocellular carcinoma [109] and CTL-mediated elimination of oxaliplatin-treated castrate-resistant prostate cancer, which are typically refractory to most treatments [20]. Besides, PD-L1 inhibition substantially curtails the abundance of IgA+ cells, by disrupting PD-1:PD-L1 Tfh-B-cell interplays, required for plasma cell maturation [132]. Also, the engagement of FcαRI by IgA may lead to the generation of immunosuppressive IL-10 by myeloid cells [133].

Interaction with M2 macrophages

In addition, the immunoregulatory IL-10 drives Th2-skewed immune responses via inducing differentiation of IgG4+ B cells; meanwhile, human IgG4 antibodies are competent to antagonize IgG1-directed anti-cancer immunity in melanoma (Fig. 1) [106]. The unusual characteristics of IgG4, including the ability to undergo Fab-arm exchange with other IgG4 molecules and low affinity for C1q and Fc receptors, enable it to form bispecific IgG4 molecules rather than large immune complexes [107,108]. Despite Fujimoto and colleagues' finding that IgG4-enriched stroma was correlated with positive outcomes of patients with early-stage lung cancers [134], a growing body of literature supports that high IgG4 levels correlate with carcinoma development and poor prognosis [106,135,136]. There is a positive linear correlation between IgG4+ PCs and tumor-associated macrophages within pancreatic cancer tissues [135]. IgG4+ TIPCs may contribute to polarization of macrophages into M2-like phenotypes. M2-polarized macrophages synergize with high IgG4 in impairing antitumor immunity [135,136].

Targeting plasma cells for cancer therapy in clinical practice

On the whole, the activity of the immune system against malignant diseases is subject to inhibitory and stimulatory forces, and the field of immuno-oncology is rapidly gaining momentum and experiencing significant growth. Thus, immunotherapy is a promising complement to standard cancer treatments. However, the up-to-date cancer therapies primarily are concentrated on other targets like T cells, with few strategies dedicated to TIPCs in solid cancers [137].

Promoting antibody-mediated immunity: potential and pitfalls

Encouragingly, evolving insights into the multifaceted cellular and antibody-driven mechanisms engaged by TIPCs open avenues for developing potential TIPC-based treatment approaches. These strategies could focus on augmenting cancer-preventive potency and persistence, including promoting the generation of tumor-specific antibodies, facilitating intratumoral TLS maturation, enhancing tumor-killing activities of effector cells, and blocking immunosuppressive factors.

To optimize TIPC-based approaches, patient stratification should consider antibody subclass predominance, hypermutation intensity, and the emergence of ectopic lymphoid tissues, along with the abundance of TIPCs producing tumor-specific antibodies. A meaningful correlation has been established between abundant IgG-labeled tumor cells and elevated response rates as well as improved survival in patients receiving immune checkpoint inhibitors [17,72]. Notably, cancer type should be taken into consideration when evaluating the predictive utility of the dominant Ig class. A case in point is that IgA-targeted immunotherapies may outperform IgG-targeted approaches in mucosal tumors expressing pIgR [21].

Therapeutic recapitulation of TIPC-mediated antitumor effects can be achieved through administration of recombinant antibodies and immune adjuvants that amplify downstream pathways, including ADCC and antigen dissemination [14]. TLS-associated PCs in colorectal cancer liver metastases (CRLM) produce tumor-reactive antibodies in situ, such as monoclonal antibody 6 (mAb6), which specifically binds to cancer cells and enhances macrophage-mediated phagocytosis [30]. Reformatted as fully human IgG1 antibodies, mAb6 showed significant antitumor effects in vitro and in humanized mouse models, highlighting the therapeutic potential of TIPC-derived antibodies [30]. In addition, administration of allogeneic IgG with DC stimuli can elicit powerful T cell-orchestrated tumor rejection in preclinical models of melanoma, breast, lung, and pancreatic cancers [138]. Considering ADCC as a key antitumor mechanism conferred by tumor antigen-specific PCs, manipulating effector cells, such as NK cells, seems to be a promising therapeutic modality. Inhibiting negative NK cell regulators like NK group 2 member A receptor (NKG2A) may restore NK cell cytotoxicity [139].

Moreover, given that current immunotherapeutic regimens prioritize the revitalization of exhausted CD8+ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes [140], combining TIPC-targeted therapies with checkpoint inhibitors may elicit synergistic anti-tumor responses. This hypothesis is supported by the observation that successful tumor regression in NSCLC patients receiving neoadjuvant anti-PD-1 therapy is associated with the presence of PC infiltrates [141]. Furthermore, elevated baseline infiltration of TIPCs has been identified as a strong predictor of improved overall survival in NSCLC patients receiving atezolizumab treatment [142]. Importantly, this predictive association appears to be independent of cytotoxic CD8⁺ T cell signatures, suggesting a distinct and complementary role for the humoral immune response in mediating the efficacy of PD-L1 blockade.

It is essential to acknowledge that antibody-based therapies are not universally beneficial and may exert unanticipated pro-tumoral effects depending on the tumor context and isotype. Specifically, IgG1-based immune checkpoint inhibitors, while designed to restore immune surveillance, might inadvertently contribute to tumor progression by directly interacting with FcγRIIA-expressing tumor cells. In glioblastoma, for example, IgG1 immune checkpoint antibodies bind to FcγRIIAhi GSCs and thereby enhance GSC proliferation and tumor growth at clinically relevant doses [10].

Critically, this effect can be mitigated through FcγRIIA blockade. Preclinical studies demonstrate that monoclonal antibody-mediated blockade of FcγRIIA (IV.3) effectively suppresses IgG-induced PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling in GSCs, reversing the pro-tumorigenic effects of both TIPC-derived IgG and therapeutic IgG1 checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., atezolizumab, ipilimumab) [10]. These findings underscore the therapeutic potential of disrupting FcγRIIA signaling in tumors with aberrant IgG1 engagement. Accordingly, incorporating FcγR profiling into therapeutic decision-making may help guide the development of more precise immunotherapies [143].

Ablation of pro-tumorigenic PCs: opportunities and caution

As stated above, TIPCs may either exert potent tumor-suppressing effects or foster tumor progression and development, depending on the tumor immune contexture as well as the specific isotype. While enhancing antibody-mediated immunity holds promise, the dual roles of TIPCs in tumor progression necessitate a complementary strategy: selectively eliminating pro-tumorigenic TIPCs based on patient-specific immune profiles. Therefore, it is imperative to rationally risk-stratify patients with cancers for successful TIPC-related treatments.

Pertaining to TIPC-depleting approaches, stratification strategies could be formulated based on the serum or intratumoral abundance of tumor-associated Igs or pro-tumor TIPCs, and the spectrum of costimulatory molecules and cytokine profiles produced by TIPCs, all of which are tied to an increased risk of cancer progression. For instance, in prostate cancer, a critical aspect of achieving successful immunotherapy outcomes may involve suppressing or removing IgA+ TIPCs, as these TIPCs express PD-L1 and IL-10, which dampen CD8⁺ T cell activity and contribute to chemoresistance [20]. This strategy could be further augmented by concurrent treatment with immunogenic chemotherapy agents like oxaliplatin.

One major challenge is the lack of specific markers that can clearly distinguish pro-tumorigenic TIPCs from protective ones. Many conventional markers, such as CD38 and CD138, are shared across functionally diverse TIPC subsets [5,6,67,144], making it difficult to selectively deplete the harmful populations without affecting beneficial ones. Furthermore, CD38 is widely expressed not only on TIPCs but also on a range of immune cell types, including myeloid cells (macrophages, monocytes, neutrophils, and DCs), T cells, and NK cells [145]. This widespread expression complicates both the mechanistic interpretation and therapeutic precision of CD38-targeted interventions, as on-target effects in non-PC compartments may obscure anti-tumor efficacy. For instance, Daratumumab, an anti-CD38 monoclonal antibody, has demonstrated substantial survival benefits in patients with multiple myeloma, representing a major advancement in PC-directed therapy [146]. However, in the context of prostate cancer, Daratumumab treatment effectively depleted CD38⁺ T cells, NK cells, and myeloid cells within tumor tissues, bone marrow, and peripheral blood mononuclear cells, yet failed to induce meaningful antitumor responses [147]. Notably, this study did not assess the impact of Daratumumab on the abundance or function of CD38⁺ TIPCs, leaving a critical gap in our understanding of its immunomodulatory effects within the TIPC compartment.

Depleting TIPCs may impair long-term humoral immunity, increasing susceptibility to infections and reducing vaccine efficacy. This is critical in immunocompromised patients due to cancer or prior treatments. The tumor microenvironment is highly dynamic, and the functional state of TIPCs may shift depending on treatment, disease stage, or immune contexture. This notion aligns with the “self-tuning model” proposed by Simons et al., in which PC longevity is not intrinsically fixed but rather emerges as a system-level property governed by the availability of survival factors [148]. Unlike the direct competition model [149,150], where new PCs displace old ones, the self-tuning model posits that competition occurs through survival factor modulation [148]. Perturbations such as therapeutic intervention or immune clearance can transiently shift the system from a steady state to a bistable regime, temporarily extending the lifespan of newly generated PC. A frequently discussed example is the use of anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies, such as rituximab or ofatumumab, for B cell depletion. While preclinical models supported their use in solid tumors, clinical trials have shown that neoadjuvant rituximab did not improve outcomes in metastatic melanoma or renal cell carcinoma following IL-2 therapy [151]. Although PCs often lose expression of CD20 or CD19 [152], anti-CD20 treatment is nonetheless associated with decreased antibody levels, implying an indirect effect on PC persistence [153]. This conceptual framework implies that TIPCs may similarly adapt to fluctuating survival signals or niche dynamics, highlighting a key limitation of current depletion-based strategies, which often assume a static, uniformly targetable population [148].

Given the functional heterogeneity of TIPCs, clinical responses to their depletion vary markedly across tumor types and individuals, highlighting the need for precise timing, patient selection, and personalized immune profiling to avoid unintended consequences. In conclusion, the dual nature of TIPCs demands a two-pronged approach: selectively amplifying their antitumor functions while eliminating pro-tumorigenic subsets. Future efforts should focus on integrating PC-targeted activation and depletion strategies with comprehensive immune profiling (e.g., FcγR status, Ig isotypes), thereby maximizing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing immune disruption.

Conclusion and future direction

The functional role of TIPCs in cancer is inherently dualistic and shaped by tumor context, exhibiting both immunostimulatory and immunosuppressive properties. This paradox has stimulated growing interest in delineating the regulatory frameworks underlying TIPC behavior across distinct tumor ecosystems.

Unlike tumor-infiltrating T cells or myeloid cells, TIBs rarely exist on their own; instead, they are typically recruited in response to existing cellular and molecular cues within the tumor milieu [3,6,154]. This suggests that their function is highly modulated by surrounding immunological cues. In TLS⁺ colorectal cancer liver metastases, CCL19-expressing cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are enriched and facilitate the recruitment of CCR7⁺ naïve T/B cells and dendritic cells, thereby sustaining local T cell viability [30]. Conversely, TLS⁻ tumors show a marked absence of both CCL19⁺ CAFs and CCR7⁺ immune cells. IgG⁺ plasma cells predominate in TLS⁺ tumors, whereas IgA⁺ plasma cells are enriched in TLS⁻ tumors. Strikingly, the IgA⁺/IgG⁺ TIPC ratio is negatively correlated with the frequency of CCL19⁺ CAFs, highlighting a stromal-immune axis shaping antibody responses [30]. Molecular subtypes further dictate TIPC behavior. For instance, mesenchymal-subtype HGSOC displays the highest TIPC abundance and poor prognosis [155]. Another remarkable example is that a high intratumoral IgG1/IgA ratio is associated with better survival for KRASmut but not KRASwt lung adenocarcinoma, while IgG1 clonality alone remains neutral in KRASmut cases [156]. This implies a distinct mechanism, possibly involving antigen presentation rather than Fc-mediated functions. Besides, high intratumoral IgG4 levels predict favorable outcomes in squamous cell carcinoma and STK11mut adenocarcinoma [156].

Intratumoral expression patterns, including isotype distribution (e.g., IgG, IgA, IgE), clonal expansion, and SHM, collectively reflect the immune pressure and antigenic landscape within the tumor. For example, in an immune-permissive microenvironment with sufficient tumor antigenicity, focused B cell responses can give rise to large IgG1+ plasma cell clones producing high-affinity, tumor-specific antibodies. In contrast, immunosuppressive environments tend to skew B cell class-switching toward IgA and other isotypes, which are relatively less antigen-specific but more involved in cytokine-mediated immunoregulation. Importantly, the prognostic values of Ig isotype abundance, somatic hypermutation frequency, and clonal expansion are not always concordant. For instance, high Ig expression with low clonality, indicative of a non-specific or bystander B cell response, was associated with poor prognosis, comparable to that observed in patients with low immunoglobulin expression [157]. Indeed, a high IgA proportion is often associated with low clonality, reflecting a passive response to the intratumoral cytokine milieu rather than active, antigen-driven selection [20,109].

Despite accumulating evidence linking TIPCs and TIPC-derived Igs to clinical outcomes, most studies have focused on CD20⁺ B cells rather than TIPCs specifically, assessed only a limited range of antibody isotypes, or confined their analysis to single tumor subtype instead of conducting pan-cancer investigations. The precise roles of PC-mediated immunity in tumorigenesis and immunosurveillance are still matters of ongoing debate. So, further in-depth studies are warranted on the phenotype, spatial organization, and antigen specificity of TIPCs. In this regard, single-cell technologies hold great promise for providing an unbiased and comprehensive view of their antigenic landscape. Another critical yet underexplored area is how conventional cancer therapies influence the functional properties and prognostic significance of TIPCs, as the predominance of primary, untreated tumor tissues in most research highlights the requirement for further investigation involving treated or recurrent tumors. In parallel with the advancement of plasma cell-targeted therapies, it is of great importance to devise novel tools to precisely identify individuals likely to benefit from synergistic antitumor responses.
