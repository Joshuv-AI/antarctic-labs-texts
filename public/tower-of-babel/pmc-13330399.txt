Modular assembly of nanocomposite hydrogel with NIR-responsive black phosphorus release and mild photothermal synergy for enhanced regenerative microenvironment and accelerated infectious bone repair
Authors: Xiangru Chen, Zhiwei Sun, Zhi Zheng, Yinghao Cao, Zhou Wan, Heyan Huang, Qianlong Huang, Changmao Zhang, Jiajia Chang, Hui Chen, Changyan Jiang, Qijun Xu, Yanqing Yang, Chuchao Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Infectious bone defects remain a clinical challenge due to the lack of spatiotemporal control over bacterial infection, immune microenvironment modulation, vascular regeneration, and osteogenic differentiation. Herein, we engineered a modularly assembled 808 nm near-infrared (NIR)-responsive therapeutic system with three functional modules, including a 3D-printed honeycomb-mimetic poly(lactic acid) (HP) framework as a mechanically supportive module, collagen-graphene oxide-black phosphorus (CGB) nanocomposite hydrogel as an ECM-mimetic filling module, and NIR-responsive black phosphorus (BP)-release module. Under NIR-induced mild photothermal effects, CGB@HP hydrogel exhibited NIR-responsive BP-release, BP’s degradation into PO43−, and upregulation of heat shock protein 47, thus promoting antibacterial activity, osteogenesis and angiogenesis capacities, and induction of macrophage polarization toward the pro-reparative M2 phenotype. In vivo validation in a rat model of infectious critical-sized bone defects further demonstrated that the CGB@HP system accelerated bone healing by attenuating local inflammation, upregulating pro-healing factor secretion, recruiting endogenous stem cells, and stimulating vascular regeneration. Collectively, this work introduces a modular NIR-responsive platform that leverages synergistic physical (mild photothermal therapy) and chemical (BP release/degradation) cues to remodel the damaged tissue microenvironment. By integrating structural support, bioactive molecule delivery, and NIR-responsive immunomodulation, this modular assembly offers a promising strategy and broad implications for infectious bone regeneration through the combination of multifunctional modules into a construct.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04434-x.

Introduction

With improved living standards and increased longevity, bone diseases arising from trauma, tumors, chronic inflammation, and infections have become prevalent, representing a leading cause of global disability [1]. Clinical management of infectious bone defects is hindered by implant site infections, where persistent inflammation and bacterial biofilm formation not only impede regeneration but also threaten implant stability, compromising patient safety [2]. While antibiotic-loaded implants offer antibacterial efficacy, their limitations pose major barriers, including antibiotic resistance risks, difficulties in sustaining localized drug release, and misalignment with the protracted osteogenic timeline [3, 4]. Thus, developing smart bone repair materials that combine environment-responsive antibacterial properties, anti-inflammatory functions, and angiogenic-osteogenic synergy is a priority for advancement.

Photothermal therapy (PTT) has emerged as a promising modality due to its spatiotemporally controlled antibacterial effects and heat-stimulated osteogenesis [5]. Mild PTT (40–50 °C) provides an optimal balance by eradicating pathogens through protein denaturation while promoting bone regeneration and avoiding collateral damage to healthy tissues [6]. Black phosphorus (BP), possessing a layer-dependent bandgap, exhibits exceptional near-infrared (NIR)-responsive photothermal conversion and degrades into non-toxic phosphate ions (PO43−) under physiological conditions [7]. These phosphates serve as essential building blocks for cellular metabolism [8]. Furthermore, phosphates chelate free Ca²⁺ to form calcium phosphate, initiating a biomineralization cascade that involves nucleation, crystal growth, and maturation into hydroxyapatite, which is the key mineral phase in bone tissue [9]. This process is essential for promoting the mineralization critical for in situ bone repair, as it enhances the deposition of bone-like apatite and supports structural integrity [10].

Bone repair requires a coordinated cascade of immune activation, vascular ingrowth, and osteogenic differentiation [11]. Macrophages play a pivotal role in this process, where their polarization from proinflammatory (M1) to anti-inflammatory (M2) phenotypes is crucial for transitioning from inflammation to the resolution phase [12, 13]. Specifically, M1 macrophages impair healing via proinflammatory cytokines, whereas M2 macrophages promote angiogenesis and osteogenesis through anti-inflammatory and pro-reparative cytokines [14]. Timely M2 activation couples vascular reconstruction with osteogenesis, driving bone formation [15]. Recent advances highlight angiogenesis and osteogenesis as pivotal events in fracture healing, with neovascularization being essential for tissue engineering success [16, 17].

Among bone tissue engineering hydrogels, 3D-printed systems have garnered significant attention for personalized customization and high precision [18, 19]. Native bone features a hierarchical architecture with a shell-like cortical bone exterior and spongy bone core at the macroscale, while containing abundant extracellular matrix (ECM) at the microscale [20]. ECM-mimetic hydrogels facilitate adequate oxygen/nutrient transport, tissue infiltration, and neovascularization, making them ideal for bone regeneration [21].

Despite these advances, current biomaterials face challenges in achieving spatiotemporal coordination of antibacterial activity, immunomodulation, angiogenesis, and osteogenesis. To address this, we designed a modularly assembled ECM-mimetic hydrogel​ that integrates three functional modules (Fig. 1): (1) a 3D-printed honeycomb-like poly(lactic acid) (HP) framework for mechanical support, (2) a type I collagen-graphene oxide-black phosphorus (CGB) nanocomposite as an ECM-mimetic filling module, and (3) an NIR-responsive BP-release system for controlled activation. We hypothesize that this design will synergistically enhance bone regeneration through NIR-responsive BP release and activation of M2 macrophage polarization, angiogenesis, and osteogenic differentiation, ultimately providing a comprehensive solution for infectious bone defect repair.

Results & discussion

Fabrication and characterization of the assembled hydrogels

Native bone exhibits a hierarchical architecture with a dense outer compact bone and porous cancellous interior [22]. To mimic this structure and enable spatiotemporal regulation of immune microenvironment, angiogenesis, and osteogenesis, we designed a modular assembled ECM-mimetic hydrogel via fused deposition modeling (FDM) 3D printing of a PLA framework, infilled with an NIR-responsive CGB composite. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the system combines a stable PLA framework with a functional CGB@HP hydrogel, enabling synergistic physical (photothermal) and chemical (BP release/degradation) cues that promote M2 macrophage polarization, vascularization, MSC recruitment, and bone regeneration.

BP nanosheets were prepared using NMP exfoliation to address aqueous instability [7]. Material characterization confirmed the assembly and stability of graphene oxide-black phosphorus nanocomposite (GO@BP). SEM and TEM revealed that pristine BP tended to aggregate, while GO@BP showed improved dispersion with BP nanosheets anchored uniformly on GO surfaces (Fig. S1A, B). EDS mapping further verified the homogeneous distribution of P elements across the GO substrate (Fig. S2). DLS indicated nanoscale sizes for both BP (50.7–68.1 nm) and GO (459–615 nm), consistent with the dimensions observed in TEM observations (Fig. S3). AFM topography illustrated that the thickness of BP nanosheets adsorbed on GO was measured around 6.4 nm (Fig. S4A), while the lateral dimensions were quantified as 74.72 ± 10.64 nm for BP and 569.6 ± 118.6 nm for GO (Fig. S4B) [23]. These AFM-derived values align closely with the DLS data, confirming the nanoscale characteristics and uniformity of the nanocomposite. The enhanced stability arises from π–π stacking and hydrogen bonding between GO and BP [24]. These interactions are suitably weak to allow NIR-triggered BP release, facilitating spatiotemporal control in bone repair.

To improve interfacial compatibility, the PLA framework was alkalized to expose active sites [25, 26]. Optical images showed the original HP framework with a honeycomb-like porous structure (Fig. 2A). After CGB incorporation, the resulting CGB@HP hydrogel exhibited a uniform brown hue. Slight shrinkage under NIR irradiation suggested photo-responsive behavior, useful for NIR-responsive BP-degradation and controlled BP-release [27]. SEM revealed a well-interconnected porous morphology with smooth connections between the PLA framework and CGB hydrogel (Fig. 2B). Post-NIR irradiation, the hydrogel remained structurally intact, with surface crystal formation likely due to BP oxidation. EDS mapping confirmed homogeneous dispersion of BP within the CGB matrix (Fig. 2C).

To confirm successful hydrogel assembly, we characterized the material using FTIR, Raman, and XRD spectroscopy (Fig. 2D-F). The FTIR spectra of HP framework exhibited a distinct C-O stretching peak at 1750 cm⁻¹, typical of PLA [28]. The CGB@HP composite showed characteristic peaks corresponding to BP nanosheets (at 1491, 1010, and 815 cm⁻¹) [29] and collagen amide linkages (1632, 1539, and 1447 cm⁻¹) [30], confirming the incorporation of each component. After NIR irradiation, all major peaks remained unchanged, indicating chemical stability under photothermal stimulation [31]. Raman spectroscopy further verified the presence of carbon-based materials in CGB@HP, showing clear D (1338 cm⁻¹) and G (1597 cm⁻¹) bands originating from GO [32]. XRD patterns confirmed the semi-crystalline structure of PLA in HP (peaks at 16.7° and 19.0°), while new peaks in CGB@HP at 29°, 39°, and 48° were assigned to the (020), (111), and (211) crystal planes of BP nanosheets. A weak GO (001) peak was also observed near 12° [33]. Minimal intensity changes after NIR suggested slight structural rearrangements without phase alteration [34].

Mechanical performance is essential for bone repair, influencing cell behavior and structural support [35]. The HP framework showed an elastic modulus of 212.1 ± 16.79 MPa, matching the range of cranial cancellous bone (Fig. 2G, H) [36]. After incorporating the CGB hydrogel, the modulus increased to 259.9 ± 11.2 MPa, attributed to the reinforcement from GO and BP and strong interfacial interactions with PLA [37]. Post-NIR irradiation, a slight decrease to 246.2 ± 12.36 MPa was observed, likely due to photothermally induced hydrogel shrinkage, though the value remains suitable for bone regeneration applications.

Photothermal performance in vitro

The photothermal performance of the CGB@HP hydrogel underpins its NIR-responsive regulation of the bone microenvironment, enabling bacterial elimination, controlled BP degradation, and subsequent osteogenic modulation [38]. Here, the photothermal performance of CGB@HP hydrogel was systematically evaluated under 808 nm NIR irradiation (Fig. 3A). The hydrogel exhibited rapid temperature increase from a baseline temperature of 28.4–29.6 °C, reaching 50.2 °C at 1.0 W cm⁻² (Fig. 3B, C), which falls within the mild PTT range that is widely recognized for balancing effective antibacterial activity with minimal tissue damage [39]. This temperature profile is consistent with reports on mild PTT systems, such as those based on polydopamine-coated microspheres or nanocomposite hydrogels, where temperatures around 50 °C achieve synergistic antibacterial and osteogenic effects without causing significant harm to healthy tissues [40], optimal for antibacterial activity while avoiding tissue damage. The pristine HP framework, from a baseline temperature of 26.1–26.2 °C, showed minimal temperature increases not exceeding 3.0 °C under various NIR irradiation powers (0.5–1.5 W cm− 2), indicating its limited photothermal conversion capability (Fig. S5-7). Cyclic irradiation tests, conducted from a baseline temperature of 29.6 °C, demonstrated favorable photostability with < 5% attenuation after four cycles (Fig. 3D). As shown in Fig. 3E, the hydrogel exhibited broad absorption across the measured spectrum (300–1000 nm), with strong NIR absorption due to the π-π transitions of GO and the interband transitions of BP, providing a spectral basis for efficient light-to-heat conversion. The photothermal conversion efficiency reached 42.4%, significantly higher than individual GO (11.8%) or BP (11.2%) components (Fig. 3F, Fig. S8, fitting equation: y = 471.2x − 140.6, R² > 0.99), attributed to the synergistic GO@BP microstructure that prevents aggregation and oxidation. Notably, the CGB@HP hydrogel retained superior photothermal performance and photothermal conversion efficiency, achieving a temperature of 50.2 °C that not only exerts antibacterial effects but also stimulates BP degradation, releasing phosphate ions that enhance endothelial function and angiogenic factor secretion, which was key for promoting early-stage vascularization in bone repair [41].

Phosphate ion release and mineralization capacity were assessed in simulated body fluid (SBF) [42]. The release mechanism involves the degradation of BP components within the CGB@HP hydrogel, where BP undergoes degradation in aqueous environment to generate PO43−. As depicted in the biphasic release profiles (Fig. 3G, H), the CGB@HP + NIR group showed accelerated phosphate ion release, achieving 55.2 ± 2.7% cumulative release within 48 h, nearly double the non-irradiated control (25.4 ± 2.6%). After 35 days, the NIR group reached 84.7 ± 2.2% release versus 44.6 ± 1.9% for controls. Mineralization ability, a critical indicator of osteogenic potential [43], was directly visualized in Fig. 3I. SEM images confirmed enhanced hydroxyapatite crystallization on BP surfaces in the NIR-treated group, with dense and plate-like aggregates formed via accelerating BP degradation to release PO43− (correlating with the 1.9-fold higher P release) and solution color lightening further indicating enhanced mineralization [8]. This NIR-responsive system enables spatiotemporally controlled PO43− release: initial burst supports early-stage immunomodulation and angiogenesis, while sustained release maintains a pro-mineralizing microenvironment for bone repair.

Antibacterial property in vitro

The antibacterial efficacy of CGB@HP hydrogel against methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and Escherichia coli (E. coli) was evaluated to address infectious bone defects (Fig. 4A). MRSA, characterized by heightened virulence, robust biofilm formation, and extensive antibiotic resistance driven by antibiotic overuse, is more recalcitrant to eradication than wild-type S. aureus [44, 45]. While the HP framework showed obvious bacterial colonies of MRSA and E. coli on agar plates and exhibited no intrinsic antibacterial activity, the CGB@HP hydrogel reduced MRSA and E. coli viability to 37.00 ± 4.86% and 67.37 ± 7.73%, respectively, attributed to bacterial adsorption and physical disruption by GO@BP nanomaterials. NIR irradiation (1.0 W cm⁻², 3 min) further enhanced bacterial eradication, achieving 98.15 ± 0.77% and 94.03 ± 2.26% inhibition against MRSA and E. coli, demonstrating potent photothermal antibacterial synergy (Fig. 4B, C, E). SYTO9/PI live/dead staining corroborated these findings, showing strong red fluorescence (dead bacteria) in the CGB@HP + NIR group (981.80 ± 64.44% for MRSA and 967.00 ± 53.19% for E. coli relative to control) (Fig. 4B, D, F). Membrane integrity assays using o-nitrophenyl β-D-galactopyranoside (ONPG), which is hydrolyzed by β-galactosidase released from the bacterial cytoplasm upon inner membrane damage to produce o-nitrophenol detectable at 420 nm [46], revealed elevated absorbance at 420 nm under NIR, indicating severe membrane damage (Fig. 4G, I). Consistent with this, cytoplasmic protein leakage increased sharply after irradiation, reaching 194.70 ± 10.23 µg mL⁻¹ (MRSA) and 183.90 ± 11.09 µg mL⁻¹ (E. coli) in the CGB@HP + NIR group (Fig. 4H, J). Collectively, these results demonstrate that CGB@HP hydrogels exert robust antibacterial effects through the synergistic action of bacterial adsorption and PTT, effectively addressing the challenge of MRSA and E. coli in infectious bone defects.

Biocompatibility evaluation in vitro

To evaluate the biocompatibility of the CGB@HP hydrogel through direct cell-material interactions, bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were seeded directly into the hydrogel composite. This approach allows for simulating in vivo conditions where cells intimately contact the material, testing adhesion, proliferation, and cytotoxicity [47]. The effects of CGB@HP hydrogels with mild PTT on BMSCs and HUVECs were evaluated (Fig. 5A). Live/dead staining at days 1 and 5 showed sparse cell survival in the HP group, while CGB@HP enhanced proliferation, further augmented by NIR irradiation (CGB@HP + NIR). BMSCs infiltrated hydrogel pores, whereas HUVECs adhered preferentially to PLA framework surfaces (Fig. 5B). Quantitative fluorescence (Fig. 5D, F) and MTT assays (Fig. 5E, G) confirmed superior viability and proliferation in the CGB@HP + NIR group, with the highest relative fluorescence and OD values. Mechanistically, three synergistic factors contribute: (1) Phosphate ions from BP degradation promote cell metabolism and proliferation. Prior work shows phosphate supplementation activates PI3K/Akt signaling to boost BMSC osteogenesis and HUVEC angiogenesis [7]. What’s more, mild PTT accelerates this release. (2) The hierarchical porous structure guides cell behavior [48], attracting BMSCs to infiltrate pores, while HUVECs align on lumens. This differential proliferation may be explained by the structural preferences observed earlier (Fig. 5A). (3) Mild PTT upregulates heat shock proteins, enhancing cell survival and signaling [49, 50].

Hemocompatibility was assessed via hemolysis assay (Fig. 5H). Supernatants of HP, CGB@HP, and CGB@HP + NIR groups remained colorless, similar to the negative control (0.9% NaCl), whereas Triton X-100 (positive control) showed vivid red. Hemolysis rates of all hydrogels were below 5% (Fig. 5I), indicating their suitability for medical applications [51]. This favorable biocompatibility stems from the stable hydrogel structure, minimizing component leakage and BP degradation into non-toxic phosphates, avoiding RBC membrane disruption [8]. Collectively, CGB@HP with mild PTT enhances cell proliferation and hemocompatibility, underpinned by phosphate release, structural guidance, and thermal signaling.

Immunomodulatory property in vitro

Macrophage polarization plays a pivotal role in bridging inflammatory responses and bone regeneration [11]. While M1 macrophages dominate initial inflammatory stages, their persistence impedes osteogenesis by promoting osteoprogenitor apoptosis and inflammatory cytokine release [52, 53]. In contrast, M2 macrophages support tissue repair through anti-inflammatory factor secretion (e.g., IL-4, IL-10, VEGF) [54, 55]. Thus, redirecting macrophage polarization from pro-inflammatory M1 to pro-regenerative M2 is a promising strategy in bone defects [56]. To specifically evaluate the paracrine effects of NIR-triggered phosphate release from the hydrogel while excluding direct cell-material contact interference, we employed a Transwell co-culture system. This setup allows soluble factors released from the hydrogel (upper chamber) to diffuse and act on macrophages (lower chamber), better mimicking the in vivo microenvironment where bioactive ions regulate immune responses through spatial signaling [57]. Using LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 macrophages to mimic infectious bone lesions, we evaluated polarization patterns via flow cytometry, ELISA, qRT-PCR, and immunofluorescence (Fig. 6A).

Immunofluorescence revealed distinct polarization shifts (Fig. 6B). LPS stimulation strongly induced CD86 (M1 marker) expression, while CGB@HP attenuated M1 signals and elevated CD206 (M2 marker). The CGB@HP + NIR group showed near-complete M1 suppression and robust M2 induction. Quantification confirmed these trends. LPS increased CD86 by 4.10-fold, whereas CGB@HP + NIR reduced it to 1.36-fold and elevated CD206 to 3.17-fold versus unstimulated controls (Fig. 6C, D). Flow cytometry further demonstrated the highest M2 polarization efficiency (CD206⁺/CD86⁺ ratio of 1.45) under CGB@HP + NIR treatment (Fig. 6G, H). At the molecular level, qRT-PCR showed CGB@HP + NIR significantly downregulated M1 markers (CD86, IL-6, TNF-α, iNOS) and upregulated M2 markers (CD206, IL-4, IL-10, Arg-1) (Fig. 6E, F). ELISA confirmed elevated anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-4, IL-10) and reduced pro-inflammatory factors (IL-6, TNF-α) in supernatants (Fig. S9-12), indicating a comprehensive immunomodulatory shift.

Mechanistically, macrophage reprogramming involves: (1) Phosphate ions from NIR-accelerated BP degradation, which may support polarization through metabolic and signaling pathways [9]; and (2) mild photothermal activation of HSPs, potentially regulating JAK-STAT and NF-κB pathways to favor M2 polarization [58, 59]. These processes align with previous findings on PI3K/Akt1 signaling in macrophage polarization [60], suggesting CGB@HP + NIR creates an anti-inflammatory microenvironment conducive to immune-mediated bone regeneration. In summary, CGB@HP with NIR irradiation effectively promotes M1-to-M2 macrophage transition via phosphate- and photothermal-mediated mechanisms, highlighting its potential for immunomodulatory bone tissue engineering.

Angiogenesis capacity in vitro

Vascularization is essential for bone repair, enabling nutrient delivery and supporting osteogenesis [61]. To evaluate the paracrine-mediated angiogenic effects of NIR-triggered phosphate release from the hydrogel, we employed a Transwell co-culture system that separates the material from HUVECs while allowing soluble factor diffusion. We evaluated the angiogenic capacity of CGB@HP hydrogel with NIR irradiation using HUVECs migration and tube formation assays (Fig. 7A). The scratch wound assay showed that CGB@HP + NIR achieved significantly faster wound closure (67.29 ± 4.53%) compared to CGB@HP (36.68 ± 5.59%), HP (20.89 ± 3.67%), and Control (16.11 ± 4.03%) groups at 12 h (Fig. 7B, D), indicating enhanced cell migration. In tube formation assays, CGB@HP + NIR promoted more mature and interconnected vascular networks with increased junctions (78.92 ± 3.19) and vessel area (22.22 ± 1.05%) versus other groups (Fig. 7C, E, F). This improvement results from the sustained release of BP and phosphate ions from CGB@HP hydrogels, which are potent angiogenic inducers, and the auxiliary role of NIR irradiation in magnifying these effects.

Molecular analysis revealed that CGB@HP + NIR upregulated key angiogenic regulators. Western blot and qRT-PCR showed increased expression of HIF-1α, CD31, and VEGF at both protein and mRNA levels (Fig. 7G-I). Mechanistically, BP stabilizes HIF-1α, activating VEGF transcription and promoting CD31-mediated vessel stabilization [62, 63]. The photothermal effect accelerates BP degradation, reinforcing this pro-angiogenic signaling axis. In summary, these findings demonstrate that CGB@HP with NIR promotes angiogenesis through coordinated cellular responses and HIF-1α/VEGF pathway activation, creating a favorable microenvironment for vascularized bone regeneration.

Osteogenic activity in vitro

The recruitment of endogenous osteoprogenitor cells is crucial for initiating bone regeneration [22, 64]. Transwell assays were used to evaluate how the CGB@HP hydrogel with NIR irradiation influences BMSC recruitment and osteogenic differentiation. The ALP activity (early-stage osteogenic indicator), mineralization, and molecular expression assays were introduced to evaluate the osteogenic efficacy (Fig. 8A). The CGB@HP + NIR group showed the most intense ALP staining and highest activity (7.21 ± 0.64 mmol/min/mg at day 7 and 13.17 ± 0.79 mmol/min/mg at day 14), significantly surpassing other groups (Fig. 8B, F). Late-stage mineralization, evaluated by Alizarin Red S staining, revealed abundant mineralized nodules in the CGB@HP + NIR group, confirming enhanced osteogenic maturation (Fig. 8C, G). This synergistic effect likely stems from the combination of BP release/degradation and NIR-induced upregulation of HSPs. As previously demonstrated by Xue et al., NIR irradiation accelerates the degradation of BP-containing hydrogels into PO43−, which enhances the combination of Ca2+ for in situ biomineralization, thereby promoting osteogenesis [65].

At the molecular level, western blot and qRT-PCR analyses demonstrated that CGB@HP + NIR robustly upregulated key osteogenic markers of Runx2, ALP, BMP-2, and OPN at both protein and mRNA levels (Fig. 8H-J). Notably, HSP47, a collagen chaperone that activates PI3K/Akt signaling to promote Runx2 transcriptional activity and matrix maturation [66], was significantly elevated in the CGB@HP + NIR group (Fig. 8H, I). This combined strategy effectively accelerates BMSC differentiation and bone matrix formation, highlighting its potential in bone tissue engineering.

Bone regeneration therapeutic efficacy in vivo

In bone tissue engineering, developing hydrogels that synergistically regulate antibacterial capacity, angiogenesis, osteogenesis, and immune microenvironments is critical for repairing critical-sized infectious bone defects [67]. The fabricated CGB@HP hydrogels with NIR irradiation have exhibited excellent antibacterial capacity, inherent immunomodulatory, osteoinductive, and angiogenic capacities in vitro. However, their in vivo therapeutic efficacy under mild photothermal therapy remained uncharacterized. To validate the in vivo antibacterial effect, we conducted additional experiments using an MRSA-infected bone defect model (Fig. S15). After treating with the hydrogel plus NIR irradiation (1.0 W cm⁻², 3 min, once a day) for 3 days, we assessed the antibacterial efficacy via agar plate colony counting. The results showed that the CGB@HP + NIR group achieved an antibacterial rate of 91.95 ± 3.71%, further confirming that temperature increase effectively reduces bacterial infection expansion in vivo. Building on this, we established a rat model of an infectious critical-sized cranial defect (diameter = 5 mm) to evaluate how CGB@HP hydrogels modulate the regenerative niche with or without NIR irradiation (3 min every 7 days). Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT), histological staining, immunohistochemical staining, immunofluorescent staining, and transcriptomic analysis were applied to evaluate the bone regeneration capacities (Fig. 9A). Three-dimensional micro-CT reconstructions revealed minimal new bone formation in the control group at both 4 and 8 weeks, validating the recalcitrance of critical-sized defects​ to spontaneous healing (Fig. 9B). In stark contrast, the CGB@HP and CGB@HP + NIR groups exhibited progressive bone ingrowth at 4 weeks, with the CGB@HP + NIR group forming a more complete osseous architecture by 8 weeks. Notably, only the CGB@HP + NIR group developed a continuous bone bridge across the defect at 8 weeks, while the HP group displayed only sporadic new bone tissue. Quantitative analysis of bone parameters, including bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp), and bone mineral density (BMD), further confirmed these trends (Fig. 9C-F). At 8 weeks, the CGB@HP + NIR group achieved a BV/TV of 26.18 ± 1.42%, significantly surpassing the CGB@HP (17.55 ± 0.70%), HP (9.40 ± 0.61%), and control (4.42 ± 0.90%) groups. Similarly, BMD (0.21 ± 0.01 g cm− 3), Tb.Th (349.78 ± 7.20 μm), and reduced Tb.Sp (204.21 ± 23.38 μm) were most pronounced in the CGB@HP + NIR group, underscoring its superior capacity to enhance bone quantity and quality. To underscore the importance of the PLA-based framework’s toughness, we added a quantitative comparison between the Control group and the HP group. At 8 weeks post-implantation, the HP group exhibited significantly higher bone regeneration outcomes, including BV/TV, BMD, Tb.Th, and Tb.Sp, compared to the Control group (p < 0.01). This result directly demonstrates the framework’s ability to provide mechanical support and prevent defect site collapse. Furthermore, histological analysis (Fig. 9G, H) revealed that the Control group displayed defect areas filled primarily with fibrous connective tissue, indicating poor structural guidance, whereas the HP group maintained a well-defined scaffold architecture that offered stable spatial support. This toughness facilitates a favorable microenvironment for osteoblast migration, proliferation, and new bone ingrowth, thereby enhancing bone tissue regeneration. To address the biodegradability of the PLA-based framework, we incorporated observations from H&E staining (Fig. 9G) and Masson’s trichrome staining (Fig. 9H) across 4 and 8 weeks. Key findings include: (1) At 4 weeks, limited but observable tissue ingrowth into the framework pores was noted, indicating initial partial degradation of the PLA-based composite matrix. (2) By 8 weeks, the framework edges became irregular (loss of sharp boundaries), and the depth of tissue infiltration into the scaffold interior increased significantly, confirming progressive degradation. Notably, the CGB@HP + NIR group showed more pronounced edge irregularity and deeper tissue ingrowth compared to non-irradiated groups, suggesting that the mild photothermal effect of NIR accelerates scaffold degradation via localized heating.

To verify the ability of the NIR-responsive BP release/degradation and mild photothermal platform to promote osteogenesis and biomineralization during bone repair, immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence analyses were performed at both 4 and 8 weeks (Fig. 10). For angiogenesis, dual staining of CD31 (markers of endothelial cells) and α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA, markers of smooth muscle cells) revealed that the CGB@HP + NIR group exhibited the highest fluorescence intensity and colocalization of these markers, signifying mature vessel formation (Fig. 10B, F, G). This was corroborated by elevated VEGF expression in the CGB@HP + NIR group (Fig. 10A, E). For osteogenesis, BMP-2 immunofluorescence, a key marker expressed by MSCs and osteoblasts during bone formation, showed the strongest signal in the CGB@HP + NIR group, with the largest positive area. This finding confirms the active participation of both osteoprogenitor cells and mature osteoblasts in the regeneration process, and is aligned with micro-CT and histological evidence of increased bone volume fraction (BV/TV) and trabecular thickness (Fig. 10C, H). Mechanistically, this synergy arises from NIR-responsive BP release/degradation. NIR-induced mild PTT accelerates BP degradation, releasing phosphate ions (PO43−) for in situ biomineralization, while BP itself stabilizes HIF-1α to upregulate VEGF and BMP-2 expression. Additionally, PTT-induced heat shock proteins, such as HSP47, explain the ordered collagen deposition in Masson staining.

During tissue injury, immune cells initially migrate into the wound sites and participate in the process of tissue remodeling [68]. Macrophages transition from pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype and can be motivated by the stimulation of immunomodulatory factors, thus managing the progress of bone tissue regeneration [69]. To evaluate the immune modulation, CD68 and CD163 were stained (Fig. 10D, I). The CGB@HP + NIR group showed the highest percentage of CD163⁺/CD68⁺ M2 macrophages (39.37 ± 2.64%), significantly exceeding CGB@HP (24.73 ± 2.53%) and HP groups (11.88 ± 2.30%). This aligns with prior studies showing BP-containing hydrogels promote M1-to-M2 polarization via phosphate-mediated STAT6 activation, while NIR-induced PTT effect further suppresses pro-inflammatory NF-κB signaling. Finally, systemic biocompatibility was confirmed by histopathological examination of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney), which showed no pathological abnormalities across all groups, underscoring the safety of the CGB@HP + NIR platform (Fig. S16).

Collectively, the superior performance of the CGB@HP + NIR platform stems from the synergistic interplay of multiple biological processes. (1) Modular design strategy. The PLA-constructed framework provides robust mechanical support and a bioactive surface, serving as the foundation for modular assembly of the CGB nanocomposite hydrogel and recruitment of host-derived cells. Within the CGB hydrogel, GO maintains the dispersion of BP via π-π stacking and hydrogen bonding, while Col offers an organic template and active sites for in situ mineralization. These synergistic properties establish the basis for BP-mediated microenvironment regulation. (2) Sustained pro-regenerative microenvironment via the synergistic effects of NIR-responsive BP release/degradation. Under NIR irradiation, BP undergoes controlled degradation, generating phosphate ions that achieve a near-doubling in concentration (55.2% cumulative release vs. 25.4% in controls), as demonstrated in our data. These phosphates play a pivotal role in bone repair by enhancing immunomodulation (promoting M2 macrophage polarization), osteogenesis (stimulating osteoblast differentiation and mineralization), and angiogenesis (upregulating VEGF expression). This process, combined with mild photothermal therapy, enables spatiotemporal control of bioactive molecule delivery, synergizing to ensure favorable immunomodulatory, osteogenic, and angiogenic activities critical for bone defect repair. (3) Long-term physical-chemical intervention for orchestrated regeneration. Through the long-term combined effects of physical (mild hyperthermia) and chemical (BP release/degradation) interventions, this NIR-responsive multifunctional therapeutic system achieves an improved regenerative microenvironment and efficient bone regeneration.

Building on BP-based materials [7, 70–72], which have demonstrated favorable effects in tissue repair, our modular assembled NIR-responsive hydrogel exhibits advantages over other studies in key aspects such as material design, antibacterial efficiency, osteogenic performance, in vivo bone regeneration, and underlying mechanisms, with comprehensive comparisons summarized in Table S2. Bone regeneration mechanisms in vivo

To elucidate the potential mechanism of the CGB@HP hydrogel in infectious bone defect repair at the molecular level, we employed transcriptomic and bioinformatic analyses to systematically identify key signaling pathways and biological processes involved in the repair process. These approaches allow for an unbiased exploration of gene expression patterns that may not be fully captured by conventional cellular assays [73]. RNA sequencing was performed on calvarial tissues collected from rats at 8 weeks post-treatment. Transcriptomic profiling identified 30,493 co‐expressed genes across experimental groups. Among these, 20,111 genes exhibited differential expression between the PBS control group and the CGB@HP + NIR group, with 164 genes showing |log₂FC| ≥ 5.5 and p ≤ 0.05, which were defined as significantly dysregulated genes. By intersecting these significantly dysregulated genes with 5659 genes functionally associated with the healing process of infectious bone defects (retrieved from GeneCard and OMIM‐Gene‐Map‐Retrieval, as shown in Fig. 11A), 164 candidate genes were identified. Volcano plots (Fig. 11B) and heatmaps (Fig. 11C) visually characterized the differential expression patterns of these 164 genes, where upregulated and downregulated genes were distinctly distinguished based on -log10(p-value) and log2(FoldChange) axes. Protein‐protein interaction (PPI) network analysis (Fig. 11D) was further conducted to explore the functional relationships among these candidates, wherein the node size represented the strength of interactions with other proteins. This analysis pinpointed pivotal signaling molecules involved in infectious bone repair, including SRC, CCND1, ANXA2, ITGA5, and NOTCH1, suggesting their crucial roles in the infectious bone repair process.

Pathway enrichment analysis using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG, Fig. 11E) demonstrated that these 164 intersected genes were significantly enriched in pathways essential for the healing of infectious bone defects, including the IgSF CAM signaling, ECM-receptor interaction, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, and regulation of actin cytoskeleton. These pathways have been well-established in the literature to play pivotal roles in regulating inflammation resolution, immune modulation, and angiogenesis, all of which are key processes for infectious bone regeneration. For example, the IgSF CAM signaling mediates immune cell adhesion and migration during bone infection. The PI3K-Akt pathway is indispensable for osteoblast survival and differentiation. The ECM-receptor interaction regulates ECM remodeling for infectious bone repair. Regulation of the actin cytoskeleton controls cell motility during infection and repair, where NIR alters actin dynamics, facilitating immune cell chemotaxis and osteoblast migration to bone defect sites.

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis (Fig. 11F) revealed significantly enriched biological processes, including MAPK cascade, cell adhesion, cell migration, osteoblast differentiation, and angiogenesis. The MAPK cascade modulates inflammation, osteogenic differentiation, and immune responses. NIR stimulation activates this pathway, promoting M2 macrophage polarization, osteoblast proliferation, and accelerating infectious bone defect repair by balancing anti-infection and osteogenesis. Cell adhesion ensures osteogenic cell retention and immune cell-pathogen interaction. Cell migration enables immune cell recruitment and osteoprogenitor homing to infected bone sites. In addition, angiogenesis delivers oxygen, nutrients, and immune effectors for bone repair.

Conclusion

In this study, we engineered a modularly assembled NIR-responsive platform that synergistically integrates three key modules, including a structural supporting module based on PLA frameworks, a bioactive filling module enabling NIR-triggered sustained release of BP, and a photothermal synergy module mediated by NIR irradiation. A distinctive aspect of this study is the modular assembly strategy, which allows for independent optimization of each functional component (support, release, and photothermal response) while facilitating their synergistic integration. This approach enables precise spatiotemporal control over therapeutic actions, a feature that enhances the platform’s ability to address multiple aspects of bone regeneration simultaneously, including efficient bacterial eradication, macrophage polarization, vascular development, and osteogenic differentiation. Leveraging the photothermal conversion and sustained-release properties of GO@BP alongside the mineralization sites provided by Col, the fabricated NIR-responsive platform exhibits spatial distribution of factors including BP, PO43−, and HSPs under NIR irradiation. Such a design orchestrates synergistic therapeutic effects encompassing immunomodulation, angiogenesis, and osteogenesis. In vitro evaluations demonstrated that when combined with mild photothermal therapy under appropriate NIR exposure, the CGB@HP hydrogel therapeutic platform exhibited notable antibacterial performance, biocompatibility, potent osteogenic and angiogenic capacities, as well as favorable immunomodulatory functions. In vivo studies further corroborated that NIR-assisted CGB@HP hydrogel accelerated multiple regenerative processes during the healing of critical-sized infectious bone defects in rat models. A series of pathological and molecular changes were observed, including macrophage M2 polarization, neovascularization, and osteogenesis. Mechanistically, by integrating physical (mild photothermal therapy) and chemical (BP delivery/degradation) interventions, this NIR-responsive platform rapidly switched macrophages from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype in the early inflammatory stage. Simultaneously, it effectively stimulated vascular tissue growth and the recruitment and proliferation of endogenous stem cells, ultimately expediting the bone repair process. The modular assembly approach provides scalability, as individual modules can be adapted or replaced for different therapeutic needs, and offers mechanistic clarity by decoupling the contributions of each component to the overall regenerative outcome. Collectively, this study offers a promising strategy and broad implications for infectious bone regeneration through the combination of multifunctional modules into an entity.
