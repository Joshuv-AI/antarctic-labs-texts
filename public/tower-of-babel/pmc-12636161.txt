Investigating inequalities in NO2 air pollution concentrations on novel indicators relating to small spatial areas
Authors: Aislinn Hoy, Gretta Mohan, Anne Nolan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Nitrogen dioxide (NO2), a significant air pollutant from transport-related fossil fuel combustion, presents a critical environmental health challenge. This study analyses NO2 trends in Ireland from 2010 to 2020, leveraging the country’s low-pollution baseline as a unique case study to analyse inequalities in NO2 concentrations across the country. Data at a small area level from Census 2022 were integrated with measurements of NO2 concentrations to assess environmental inequalities across novel indicators such as housing tenancy and self-rated health alongside conventional socioeconomic measures. The research uncovers clear evidence of environmental inequalities in NO2 concentrations, with higher levels found in neighbourhoods with larger proportions of non-white populations and non-owner-occupied housing. A stratified urban-rural analysis shows these disparities were most pronounced in urban areas. A U-shaped relationship between NO2 levels and deprivation, educational attainment, and skills, suggests that while the most and least deprived may experience similar NO2 concentrations, the health burden on disadvantaged groups may be amplified by co-occurring vulnerabilities. These findings underscore an urgent need for targeted interventions to mitigate the disproportionate burden of NO2 air pollution on vulnerable communities in Ireland.

Introduction

Air pollution is a global health risk, with the World Health Organization (WHO) identifying it as the biggest environmental health risk in Europe [1]. While air pollution emissions have declined in the last two decades, resulting in better air quality, nitrogen dioxide (NO2), exposure remains a key concern. NO2 is a gaseous pollutant from burning petrol or diesel in vehicles [2]. In 2021, the WHO significantly lowered its annual average NO2 guideline from 40 µg/m3 to 10 µg/m3 [3, 4]. This aligns with the EUs 2024 Ambient Air Quality Directive, which mandates Member States to achieve a maximum annual average of 20 µg/m3 by 2030 [5].

Fairburn et al. [6] comments that there are relatively few national ecological studies using small area level data to examine the social inequalities of air quality, citing challenges in obtaining small area statistics with good socio-economic, socio-demographic or index data. This paper addresses this gap by conducting the first study of NO2 at a small geographical area in Ireland. Ireland serves as a valuable case study for international research on NO2 air pollution due to a unique juxtaposition of its generally clean atmospheric conditions and the presence of concentrated, localised urban pollution. This favourable baseline air quality, attributed to a lack of heavy industry and its geographically exposed island location with high wind levels [7], creates a distinct environment for studying the dynamics of pollution in “hotspots” that are not overwhelmed by regional or industrial emissions. This setting allows researchers to isolate and investigate the specific impacts of NO2 sources, predominantly traffic, in a way that is often challenging in more heavily industrialised nations. Furthermore, the country’s adherence to European legislation and its implementation of significant policy measures, such as the phased smoky coal ban [8]1, provide a robust framework for assessing environmental policies on a national scale.

In the context of the extant international literature in this field, the research presented in this paper also overcomes temporal mismatches by using high-resolution NO2 data (2010–2020) with recent 2022 census information. It innovatively examines NO2 concentrations alongside indicators of socioeconomic position and health, such as housing tenancy and smoking status, providing new insights into air quality disparities [9].

Health consequences of NO2

Breathing NO2 can irritate the airways, aggravating respiratory diseases leading to coughing, wheezing, and difficulty breathing [10–12]. A meta-study concluded that the magnitude of the effect of long-term exposure to NO2 on mortality is at least as important as that of PM2.52 [13]. The mortality burden of disease attributable to NO2 includes diabetes mellitus, stroke and asthma [14], and a meta-analysis found a positive relationship between NO2 and all-cause mortality, mortality from respiratory conditions, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [15]. In 2021, exposure to NO2 levels exceeding WHO recommendations was estimated to cause 52,000 premature deaths in the EU-27 [4, 14]. Furthermore, NO2 can also react with sunlight to produce Ozone, a secondary pollutant with significant health effects [16].

Additionally, traffic-related air pollution has been found to be associated with poorer birth outcomes, such as, low birth weight and premature birth [17–19], as well as neurological disorders such as autism [20]. Economically, air pollution more broadly, reduces life expectancy, increases healthcare costs, and lowers productivity through absenteeism [21].

Social gradients in health are well established [22–24], where socially and economically marginalised groups may experience increased susceptibility to negative air pollution related health effects because of higher baseline rates of disease [25]. The potential for unequal exposure to NO2 is a major policy concern, as socioeconomically disadvantaged or vulnerable populations (e.g., lower-income households) may live in areas with higher NO2 concentrations. For instance, lower property values near busy transport routes make these areas more affordable [26, 27], and studies have shown that schools in disadvantaged areas are exposed to higher NO2 levels [28, 29].

Moreover, research has suggested that spatial inequalities in environmental exposures often place disproportionate health risks on people who are already the most vulnerable or susceptible to those risks, for example, those with underlying prior health impairments or poorer access to health care [25, 30].

Extant international and Irish-based studies on NO2 and health outcomes

International research, particularly from the US, typically finds that more vulnerable communities, particularly those comprised of racial minorities and lower income groups, are more likely to be exposed to higher NO2 pollution levels [31–35]. In the Massachusetts area, Rosofsky et al. [35] found that inequalities in NO2 exposure were more significant in urban compared to rural areas, and more pronounced along racial/ethnic lines than by income or education. However, the study also noted that poorer rural areas had higher NO2 exposure than wealthier ones.

Crouse et al. [36], identified a significant association between elevated outdoor NO2 concentrations and neighbourhood deprivation in Montreal, Canada. This deprivation encompassed both material factors (e.g., lower income) and social factors (e.g., a higher prevalence of individuals living alone), highlighting areas with a dual burden of high NO2 exposure and considerable socio-economic disadvantage. However, the study also revealed that this pattern was not universally consistent. Some deprived areas exhibited low pollution, while certain affluent city-centre districts recorded high NO2 levels.

A majority of UK-based studies that investigate air pollution associated with NO2 find evidence of inequalities on racial and income lines [37–42]. However, the correlation between air pollution from NO2 and socio-economic inequalities across studies arising from the European context is more inconsistent. For instance, Temam et al. [43] examined associations between indicators of socio-economic position and NO2 for sixteen European cities, finding high levels of heterogeneity across the cities studied. In general, they found that lower neighbourhood socio-economic position (as measured by unemployment levels) was generally associated with higher NO2 exposure. However, the opposite pattern, i.e. higher NO2 air pollution concentrations in areas of higher social economic status, were revealed in studies originating from Denmark [44], Paris [45], and the Czech Republic [46]. In commenting on a cross-country analysis between the Netherlands and England, Fecht et al. [39] remark that relationships between the geographical distribution of vulnerable communities and air pollution (including NO2) levels are more complex and less universal than assumed.

In Australia, research shows an inverse relationship between NO2 concentrations and socioeconomic disadvantage [47]. Lower NO2 concentrations were observed for areas with a higher proportion of non-Indigenous individuals. A separate study by Cooper et al. [48], found that in Australia, higher exposure to NO2 was experienced by ethnic minority groups, socioeconomically disadvantaged residents in urban areas, and the elderly in rural areas.

Turning to the study setting for which the present research is conducted, Domhnaill et al. [49] uncovered no association between NO2 levels and respiratory medicine prescriptions in Ireland. However, a separate study by Carthy et al. [11], found that among people over 50 in Ireland, NO2 concentrations were associated with a higher likelihood of asthma and the use of respiratory medications.

Research by Quintyne et al. [50] connected Dublin’s hospital admissions to the Air Quality Index for Health (AQIH), finding an increased likelihood of hospitalisation for asthma, chronic obstructive airways disease (COAD), and heart failure when air quality worsened. A survey revealed that while most Irish people know NO2 is a major pollutant, they’re unaware it increases the risk of medical problems like asthma and heart disease [51]. Hoy et al. [52] estimated the annual cost of treating stroke and asthma cases attributable to NO2 pollution in Ireland to be nearly €2.7 million and €0.4 million, respectively.

Finally, Hoy et al. [53] found that nearly all small areas in Ireland exceeded the WHOs annual guideline for PM2.5 in 2016. While there was no strong evidence of widespread socioeconomic inequality in PM2.5 concentrations, more disadvantaged rural areas did exhibit higher levels than their more affluent counterparts. The present study distinguishes itself from the work of Hoy et al., (ibid) primarily through its focus on NO2 concentrations. This difference is crucial as NO2 is a more localised pollutant, with concentrations peaking near emission sources like roadways [54], necessitating a significantly finer analytical resolution. Consequently, the current work employs contemporary NO2 concentration data with an enhanced spatial resolution of approximately 0.0025 km2, contrasting sharply with the 1 km × 1 km PM2.5 data used in Hoy et al., (ibid). Furthermore, the socioeconomic context is updated through the utilisation of 2022 Census data, offering a more contemporary perspective than Hoy et al., (ibid)‘s reliance on 2016 Census information.

Contribution of the present research

This study investigates the distribution of NO2 concentrations in Ireland, a topic central to understanding environmental inequalities. We note that the concept of environmental equity posits that all individuals and communities should experience a proportionate share of environmental benefits and burdens, including air pollution and its associated health risks, alongside equitable access to environmental amenities [55]. In contrast, environmental justice seeks to achieve this equity by creating policies and processes that ensure fair distribution and recognise the diverse experiences of affected communities [56]. According to Walker and Bulkeley [57], an unequal distribution of environmental burdens is not necessarily an injustice, but rather the fairness of the processes leading to that distribution is what matters. This is especially relevant for air pollution, as socioeconomic factors can increase vulnerability. Walker [58. 215] describes this as a “triple jeopardy,” where those least able to cope with poor health are simultaneously exposed to higher levels of harmful pollutants.

In response to these considerations, this study leverages the most contemporary available data on NO2 concentrations in the Republic of Ireland, measured at the smallest administrative spatial unit for the decade spanning 2010 to 2020. For this research, NO2 recordings have been methodically linked to detailed demographic, socio-economic, and health profiles of small areas, as well as a composite index of deprivation derived from the most recent 2022 census. This enables a high-resolution, national-level analysis of NO2 exposure across all small areas, alongside the ability to stratify by urban and rural regions. Given the strong correlation between NO2 and local traffic, this stratification is vital and promises new insights into NO2-related inequalities. This research also offers novel contributions by analysing NO2 concentrations against housing tenancy, self-rated health, and smoking rates, offering contemporary quantitative data on environmental inequality3. This information can help environmental and governmental bodies create more effective policies to reduce air pollution.

Where patterns of NO2 inequalities are uncovered in this work, these call into question the fairness of current environmental distributions, and the societal mechanisms that contribute to such inequalities. However, this research cannot make direct claims about environmental justice since it is limited to a descriptive analysis of exposure patterns and does not examine procedural fairness, the socio-historical development of these disparities, or the involvement of affected communities in decision-making [58]. As such, the scope of the study is confined to the documentation of environmental inequality [29].

Materials and methods

This paper examines potential social and economic inequalities in air quality across Ireland as measured by NO2 concentration levels and using data which describes the characteristics of the smallest defined geographic units in Ireland. The smallest administrative unit in the Republic of Ireland are Small Areas (SAs) (N = 18,919), defined by population rather than permanent geographic boundaries, with a mean population of 272 individuals or 97 households in 20224. These SAs represent socially homogenous neighbourhoods and are the most nationally comparable Irish spatial unit. Each SA is also assigned a rural or urban classification by the Irish Central Statistics Office for 2022, with 11,799 of the SAs classified as urban (62%). A more disaggregated six-way urban/rural classification can also be applied to SA levels [59], using a census respondent’s stated place of work as a measure of distance to services and amenities, combined with population density information5.

NO2 as the measure of air pollution

Measuring NO2 is complex due to varied emission sources, primarily traffic, and strong influences from meteorological conditions like wind and atmospheric stability, which affect pollutant dispersion [60]. Urban morphology, such as tall buildings and street canyons, further traps pollutants [61, 62]. Additionally, sensor accuracy, especially for low-cost devices, is affected by environmental factors like temperature and humidity, necessitating careful calibration to obtain reliable measurements [63].

This investigation leverages Environmental Exposome Data sourced from the Gateway to Global Aging project [64]. Specifically, it incorporates high resolution spatial estimates of NO2 concentrations, globally predicted at a resolution of 0.00456° (equivalent to 0.0025 km2) and an hourly temporal granularity spanning 2005 to 2020 [65]. Estimates of NO2 concentrations were generated via land use regression models, informed by data from 8,250 NO2 air quality monitors distributed across Europe, Asia, North America, and Africa. The input datasets for these predictions encompassed remote sensing data from the Ozone Monitoring Instrument, detailed road networks, built environment characteristics, and pertinent meteorological variables. The conversion of nitrogen dioxide concentrations from parts per billion (ppb) to micrograms per cubic metre (μg/m3) is performed using a conversion factor of 1.91 (i.e., 1 ppb NO2 corresponds to 1.91 μg/m3).

This research systematically examines the annual average concentrations of NO2 within the Republic of Ireland for the years 2010 to 2020. As aforementioned, the dataset provides a highly granular spatial resolution of approximately 0.0025 km2, which is particularly advantageous for studying NO2 due to its inherently localised dispersion characteristics [54]. Initially, this investigation explores the temporal evolution of average NO2 concentrations over the 2010–2020 period, thereby establishing a critical context for the subsequent analyses. For the principal examination of demographic and socioeconomic inequalities concerning NO2 concentrations, the study primarily utilises the most recently available 2020 data to optimise temporal synchronisation with Ireland’s 2022 census. Nevertheless, recognising the disruptive impact of 2020s COVID-19 lockdowns on traffic volumes and, consequently, NO2 exposures [40], a thorough analysis of the 2010–2019 data is also conducted. This supplementary analysis, incorporating an annual mean trend from 2010 to 2020, enables the identification of potential variations linked to pandemic mitigation strategies and the comprehensive assessment of NO2 concentration changes over time.

The NO2 concentration data is aggregated to the SA geospatial level to align with the census demographic and socio-economic variables. To account for the spatial distribution of the population within SAs, estimates of NO2 concentrations for residential buildings are weighted by the number of addresses within each building. This approach corrects for the assumption that a population is spread uniformly across each SA, which is especially not the case in rural areas. Deprivation, demographic and socioeconomic measures

Pobal Haase-Pratschke deprivation index

The Pobal HP index is an area-based index constructed using census information to assign all SAs in the Republic of Ireland a social deprivation score [66]. The index is modelled based on three main dimensions: the demographic profile of an area, social class composition, and labour market conditions (see Appendix Figure A-2 for further information on the 10 indicators included across the three dimensions). For the 2022 Irish census, the Pobal HP index assigned each of the 18,919 SAs a relative deprivation score, which ranges between −30 and below (extremely disadvantaged) to + 30 and over (extremely affluent). As illustrated in Figure 3, each SA in the country is assigned a relative score, and then categorised into one of eight categories that reflect the level of deprivation/affluence of an area. It is evident that more affluent areas concentrate around the capital city of Dublin, and its commuter zones in the East of the country, as well as the areas around other cities such as Cork, Limerick, and Galway, while the West and the Northwest including border areas with Northern Ireland are relatively more deprived.

For the purposes of analysis, and to align with the direction of the other indicators employed in this study, the ranking of the HP index scores must be changed so that the ordering of the HP scores for SAs are ranked from most advantaged SAs to the least advantaged. Then from this re-ordering, quintiles of the HP index are created to examine how NO2 concentration levels vary across SA geographies. Quintile one consists of the 20% of SAs who fall into the most advantaged HP index ranking, and quintile five consists of the 20% of SAs who are the most disadvantaged. We note that the quintile classification that is conducted for the purposes of this research is different from the eight categories presented in Figure 3 to allow for consistency in the presentation of the HP index and the 2022 Irish Census of Population Small Area Population Statistics which this work also employs to provide a more comprehensive analysis of the socio-economic landscape of the small geographic areas under investigation.Fig. 3Pobal HP index for Ireland 2022

Analytical approach

This paper first examines time trends for national, urban, and rural average NO2 concentrations for the decade 2010–2020. Graphical representations of NO2 concentrations in 2020 of the SA characteristics are presented, including the HP index, and the seven SAPS demographic, socioeconomic and health-related indicators.6

The research then presents an empirical analysis of NO2 concentrations in 2020 by indicators of the demographic and socio-economic make up of SAs using concentration indices. The concentration index is a summary index calculated as follows:

Average NO2 concentrations by HP quintiles and the seven SAPS indicators are presented in Fig. 4, with associated box-plots. Taking first the HP deprivation index, the most advantaged quintile, quintile 1 was exposed to on average 7.6 µg/m3 of NO2, while the next relatively more deprived quintiles (2, 3 and 4) had lower average NO2 concentrations; however, the most deprived quintile (5) had a higher average NO2 concentration of 6.4 µg/m3. The line graph therefore shows a u-shaped trend in NO2 concentrations by deprivation levels, where the least and the most deprived SAs experienced relatively higher NO2 concentrations than other SAs. This pattern was replicated for the box-plot presentation of the HP index quintiles, with a larger interquartile range for the most affluent quintile (1). An ANOVA test of differences revealed a statistically significant association between the HP index quintiles and NO2 concentrations at the national-level, F(4,18828) = 413.62, p < 0.001. Since this ANOVAs F-score is statistically significant, post-hoc pairwise tests with Bonferroni correction were performed to determine exactly which pairs of quintile groups differ significantly from each other in terms of NO2 concentrations. Table 4 shows that differences in NO2 concentrations were significant for all comparisons of the HP index quintiles, except for HP index quintile 3 versus quintile 4.

Differences in inequality patterns of NO2 concentrations were more evident across the seven SAPS indicators depicted in Fig. 4. A u-shaped association of NO2 concentrations by educational attainment was also observed for SAs characterised by shares of the least- and most- non-degree educated quintiles. An ANOVA test demonstrated a statistically significant association between educational attainment quintiles and NO2 concentrations at the national-level (F(4,18826) = 894.5, p < 0.001). The post-hoc pairwise tests for the quintiles groups in Table 4 show that differences in NO2 concentrations were significant for all comparisons, with the exception of education quintiles 2 and 5, and 4 and 3.

There was also a very slight u-shaped trend evident for average NO2 concentrations by quintiles of non-professional socioeconomic group, where the quintile representing the SAs with greater shares of the least non-professional socio-economic group (i.e. more advantaged socio-economic groups) had relatively higher average NO2 concentrations (F(4,18832) = 127.2, p < 0.001).

A decreasing trend of NO2 concentrations by quintiles of non-working age populations was observed where SAs made up of greater shares of children (0–14 years) and older people (65+ years) were less exposed to air pollution (F(4, 18,828) = 1028.9 p < 0.001). This is supported by the negative concentration index, reported in Appendix Table A-1, indicating that areas made up of higher shares of non-working age populations had lower levels of NO2.

On the other hand, SAs characterised by greater shares of non-owner occupier households and non-white populations experienced relatively higher NO2 concentrations as depicted in the quintile charts and box-plots (ANOVA for owner-occupation quintiles F(4,18825) = 879.2, p < 0.001); for ethnicity quintiles (F(4,18828) = 2317.5, p < 0.001)). This is further exemplified in the positive concentration indices documented in Appendix Table A-1, of 0.11 and 0.16 respectively, which confirm inequalities towards areas with higher levels of non-white populations, and areas with higher levels of renters (i.e. non-owner- occupied housing).

In terms of the health-related SAPS indicators, SAs with the greatest shares of smokers were more exposed to NO2 concentrations (F(4, 18,828) = 97.2, p < 0.001), complemented by the positive concentration index of 0.08 in Appendix Table A-1. For the quintiles of increasing proportion of the population with poorer self-rated health there was a slight u-shape where the quintiles of the SAs with the least and most shares of populations with poorer health had slightly higher average NO2 concentrations (F(4, 18,828 = 451.7, p < 0.001), though the concentration index value was very small at 0.003 for this indicator. Table 4 also shows that pairwise quintile differences in NO2 concentrations, were significant for all comparison groups, apart from poorer self-rated health quintiles 5 and 1, as well as 3 and 2, and 4 and 2.

The patterns of NO2 concentrations by the HP index and the seven SAPS indicators for SAs which fall into areas which are defined as urban are presented in Appendix Figure A-4. The average NO2 concentrations for the urban area SAs group was higher than that for the national sample, and broadly, many of the patterns observed for the national sample in terms of concentrations of NO2 by deprivation and socio-economic indicators was similarly observed – though the u-shaped pattern on the HP index was slightly less marked, and the trends for air pollution and housing tenancy in urban SAs (u-shaped) were different from the national pattern (upward sloping). For the urban group, SAs made up of the least shares of non-owner-occupied housing (i.e. the most owner-occupiers, quintile 1) experienced higher average NO2 concentrations (7.5 µg/m3) than SAs with progressively higher shares of non-owner-occupied housing (quintiles 2,3,4), though quintile 5, which comprised of urban SAs with the most non-owner-occupied housing had the highest NO2 concentrations overall (8.4 µg/m3).

Discussion

The established link between NO2 and adverse health outcomes, including respiratory illness and mortality, combined with the potential for inequalities in exposure to air pollution among more disadvantaged groups, which are more susceptible to poorer health, merits thorough research scrutiny to inform policy development in this important area. This study provides the first high resolution analysis of NO2 concentrations over time in Ireland, and for the most recent year of data available (2020), these pollutant concentrations are linked to 2022 small area census information on deprivation, demographic, socio-economic and health characteristics. Importantly, in the case of NO2, an environmental pollutant which is strongly linked to traffic activity, the analysis is carried out at the national level, as well as stratified by urban and rural areas.

The data reveals that Ireland has experienced relatively low levels of NO2 concentrations – where the average annual concentration was lower than even the WHOs revised guideline limit of 10 µg/m3 back in 2010. In the decade 2010–2020 a small average decline in national NO2 concentrations was observed, while urban areas had approximately twice the average annual level of NO2, reflecting the higher volume of vehicular traffic in urban areas and urban design features such as street canyons and high rise-buildings that trap pollutants [68]. Nonetheless, the rate of decline in NO2 was not observed to be different for urban and rural areas. In addition, some SAs had recorded the maximum average of 16 µg/m3, throughout the decade.

From an environmental inequalities’ perspective, this study on NO2 concentrations in Ireland highlights significant disparities in exposure across different population segments. Where the air pollution data was mapped on to the census 2022 Pobal HP index, and the seven SAPS indicators measuring the demographic, socio-economic and health characteristics of small areas, the analysis of potential inequalities in pollutant concentrations uncovered important distinctive patterns by indicators. For the national level, a u-shaped pattern of NO2 concentrations was illustrated, where the most and least disadvantaged quintiles experienced the highest NO2 according to the overall deprivation measure, the HP index, and educational attainment, as accords with a number of other European-based studies [6, 37, 69, 70]. Importantly, the urban-rural stratification reveals that such u-shaped patterns were more apparent for urban areas - with rural areas displaying more flat trends across quintile groups. This pattern is likely linked to the spatial distribution of densely populated and highly polluted urban areas (which drives the national result), where individuals from the more disadvantaged groups may reside in homes located near pollutant sources such as main roads. Disadvantaged populations are often compelled by affordability to reside in areas proximate to major transport arteries or industrial zones, which are typically associated with lower property values but higher pollution burdens [71]. This is further underscored by the correlation between higher NO2 and areas with a greater proportion of non-owner-occupied housing for the national level, suggesting that renters may have less agency in selecting residences away from pollution sources due to market limitations [72].

At the other end, the strong association between NO2 and more advantaged areas may be due to more advantaged groups residing in more attractive and expensive central parts of city centres or gentrified inner city areas where they enjoy the benefits associated with city living despite greater pollution levels [39, 44, 70, 73]. Within this urban context, housing market dynamics and socio-economic stratification likely play a crucial role in shaping exposure disparities.

Related to this discussion, rural SAs with a greater proportion of non-professional socio-economic groups had lower average NO2 concentrations. This may reflect the tendency for more individuals in more professional socio-economic groups in rural areas to live in closer proximity to roadways for greater accessibility reasons – and close proximity to roads in rural areas may increase property values [35].

There was a clear indication from the data presented that SAs comprised of higher shares of non-white ethnic populations were exposed to higher levels of NO2 air pollution – a trend seen in the national, urban and rural analyses. Disparities based on ethnicity were more pronounced in urban areas than rural areas, as has been observed in other studies [30, 35].

Similarly, for an unexplored indicator in the context of this research field, which relates to the housing tenancy composition of areas and air pollution, the analysis uncovered that for the national level, where there were greater shares of non-owner occupying housing arrangements, higher concentrations of NO2 pollution were observed.

On the other hand, this investigation discovered that SAs characterised by greater proportions of children and older people (the non-working age population), tend to live in areas of lower NO2 pollution, consistent with evidence for England and the Netherlands [39]. This is a positive finding, since children and older people who may be more vulnerable to the effects of air pollution or have compromised health were relatively less exposed. We note the overall results from this investigation found more evidence of air pollution inequalities in Ireland in relation to NO2 concentrations compared to another study which found relatively little evidence of air pollution inequalities using PM2.5 data [53]. This is likely due to differences in spatial resolution of the pollutant data available between the two studies, where the NO2 concentration data employed in this research is at a much higher, finer grained-resolution than the PM2.5 data.

In terms of the health profiles of areas, the SAs with the highest share of those with poorer health were most exposed to NO2 pollution, lending credence to concerns of a double jeopardy where unhealthier people are exposed to unhealthier environments. Furthermore, there was a positive relationship between the proportion of smokers in small areas and concentrations of NO2, also found by Samoli et al. [74] in a study of European metropolitan areas. This intersection of pre-existing health conditions and environmental risk highlights a critical concern for health-related environmental inequalities, as it implies that the burden of pollution is unfairly distributed, exacerbating health disparities for those already most vulnerable.

The identified patterns of NO2 inequality in this study raise critical questions regarding the fairness of current environmental distributions and the underlying societal mechanisms. While our findings evidence disparities in health-related air pollution across socio-economic groups, a direct commentary on environmental justice is outside the scope of this work. This is because the research design prioritises a descriptive analysis of exposure patterns, rather than explicitly examining the social and historical development of these disparities [58].

Policy implications

In all, this study finds evidence of some important inequalities in Ireland in terms of concentrations of NO2 air pollution – where more deprived areas, areas made up of larger shares of non-white ethnic populations and non-owner occupier households had greater risks of NO2 exposure. Although, on an international level Ireland has a relatively low level of NO2, it remains crucial from a health inequalities perspective that more vulnerable groups are not unjustly at a greater risk of the health-damaging effects of breathing NO2. As such, this study has valuable implications for policy makers. Since NO2 is a marker for traffic-related pollution [2, 31], the findings of this analysis suggests that measures to reduce environmental inequality should include a focus on traffic-related measures – particularly in urban areas. Traffic controls could include the implementation of congestion charging zones [40, 42], the banning of older, higher-emission vehicles from densely populated areas and city centres, and the enforcement of speed limits. In addition, policies for healthier and more sustainable transport systems are required, including encouraging more active travel and the use of public transportation [75]. Better active and public transport connections between city centres and their greater suburban and commuter areas, as well as interconnections between cities, to reduce car dependence, are also vital. As a priority, the socio-economic context should be considered in intervention planning and identifying potential hotspots of health impacts in targeting interventions in order to reduce inequalities. Studies have demonstrated that interventions which control road traffic emissions, and greater enforce air quality regulations can have benefits for socioeconomically disadvantaged groups [41], and reduce health inequalities [76]. Insights on this topic are particularly important in the context of European ambitions to reduce emissions from the transport sector and achieve climate targets. Evidence such as that presented in this research which lend support to the targeting of policies to mitigate effects for vulnerable populations should prove helpful in preparing more coherent public policies to reduce air pollution that are acceptable to the wider population. This is especially significant as EU Member States work to implement the new Ambient Air Quality Directive of annual average NO2 concentrations of 20 µg/m3 by 2030 [5], and Ireland has also committed to achieving the WHOs revised guidelines of lower annual average NO2 concentrations [4, 77].

Limitations

While this study advances understanding of socioeconomic and demographic-related concentrations of NO2 pollution in Ireland, several research limitations must be acknowledged. While the analysis is conducted at a very small spatial level, it may be noted that even at this level of aggregation, the findings could be subject to ecological fallacy, where the group-level associations between NO2 exposure and the various indicators of deprivation, demographic, and socio-economic make-up of areas cannot assume to translate into individual experiences [78]. An individual and household’s actual exposure to air pollution is also influenced by indoor air pollution, as well as exposure risks during occupation, mobility patterns and own behaviours in response to air pollution risks e.g., wearing of face coverings etc. In particular, those from more disadvantaged socioeconomic groups may disproportionately encounter indoor air pollution where those in more disadvantaged areas may live in comparably poorer quality, older housing stock for which outdoor air pollution can leak into [79]. Furthermore, the associations uncovered between sociodemographic characteristics of SAs in Ireland and NO2 exposure are likely to be location specific, particularly given the relatively lower level of NO2 in Ireland than other countries, thus any generalisation of findings to other countries may be approached with caution.

Future research directions

To more comprehensively understand the health implications of the NO2-related air pollution inequalities documented in this study, further investigation into associated health outcomes is warranted. This could entail examining the direct relationship between NO2 exposure and indicators such as life expectancy, mortality, disease incidence (including respiratory illnesses), and birth outcomes. While census Small Area Population Statistics currently do not contain these specific health records, the successful integration of relevant health data would significantly enhance our understanding of how differential NO2 exposure patterns influence population health. In addition, alternative modelling approaches that move beyond descriptive analysis, e.g., using Generalised Additive Models [80, 81] or spatial clustering techniques [82], could help to further our understanding of the nature of the correlations between NO2 concentrations and socio-demographic characteristics at local levels.

Future studies should investigate whether interventions introduced to address air pollution and traffic issues for instance the roll-out of public electric buses, re-routing of traffic and stricter speed limits, have social and health inequalities effects. To complement these findings, future studies could employ longitudinal and individual-level data to build a more accurate picture of factors that could affect disparities in exposure to air pollution over time, including population mobility patterns, individual, and household factors (e.g. age and quality of housing stock).

Conclusion

To conclude, the results of this research provide the first Irish-based evidence of environmental inequalities in concentrations of NO2 air pollution, particularly in areas composed of greater shares of non-white populations and non-owner occupier housing. The stratified analysis demonstrates that these issues pertain in particular for urban jurisdictions, where there are denser road networks and NO2 emitting traffic. Where a u-shaped pattern between NO2 air pollution and levels of deprivation and educational attainment was observed, this may be cause for concern since while more disadvantaged populations also encounter relatively similar levels of pollution as the most advantaged population, the more disadvantaged populations may be at a greater risk of health damaging effects from NO2 since they may have poorer quality housing, poorer access to healthcare services, or existing underlying conditions. One positive finding was also uncovered, where there was a progressively lower concentration of NO2 for small areas comprised of greater shares of older people or children (i.e. non-working age populations), who are more vulnerable to adverse health effects from NO2 air pollution (e.g. stroke, asthma etc.).

Since the impact of environmental hazards can be unequal across different categories of people, future more comprehensive research is needed. More acutely, where the evidence presented in this research suggests an overburdening of especially vulnerable groups to NO2 related air pollution in Ireland, this work can act as a stimulus for greater, more effective, policy-making. Using the information provided, national and local governments, and environmental bodies, can seek opportunities for more targeted air pollution and traffic interventions to reduce disparities, for example by decreasing existing emissions and preventing the arrival of new sources, policies could also consider redressing harms in overburdened communities.
