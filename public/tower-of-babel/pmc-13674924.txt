Biosimilars in diabetic macular edema: Promise, progress, and pitfalls
Authors: Vanaja Jain, Vivek P Dave, Manoj P Shettigar, De-Kuang Hwang, Sibel Demirel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diabetic macular edema (DME) is a common microvascular complication of diabetes and a major cause of vision impairment. Intravitreal vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) inhibitors remain the gold standard for its management. However, the high cost and limited accessibility of reference anti-VEGF agents have driven growing interest in biosimilars—biologic therapies designed to match the efficacy, safety, and immunogenicity of their reference products. Following the expiration of patents for agents such as aflibercept and ranibizumab, several biosimilars have been developed, approved, and introduced into clinical practice. Evidence from real-world studies—including RE-ENACT2 (REal-life assessmENt of safety And effeCTiveness of Razumab) and REAR-RD2 (REal-world observational study to evaluate the risk factors in the management of REtinal Diseases-2)—as well as randomized clinical trials such as INSIGHT and CT-P42 has demonstrated clinical equivalence between these biosimilars and their reference anti-VEGF counterparts in the treatment of DME. This article provides an updated overview of the current evidence base, recent advances in regulatory pathways, and ongoing challenges in integrating biosimilars into routine DME management.

Diabetic macular edema (DME) is the leading cause of visual impairment in patients who have diabetes. About 22.27% of individuals with diabetes develop diabetic retinopathy, and 4.07% develop DME.[1] The introduction of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) agents in the early 2000s revolutionized the treatment of DME.[2] Anti-VEGFs inhibit VEGF, the main factor responsible for increased vascular permeability and leakage in diabetic retinopathy. Thus, it addresses the underlying mechanism of DME, resulting in reduced central macular thickness and improved visual acuity.

Method of Literature Review

A detailed literature search was done on PubMed and Google Scholar databases by using the following keywords: Biosimilars, Diabetic Macular Edema, Anti-VEGF, Razumab, CDSCO, and so on. Non-English articles were excluded.

Challenges in Reference Biologics

Robust evidence from landmark clinical trials—including RISE and RIDE, VIVID-DME (VEGF Intervention for Vision Impairment in DME), VISTA-DME (Visual Impairment Study of Treatments for Aflibercept in DME), and protocols from the DRCR.net (Diabetic Retinopathy Clinical Research Network)—has established anti-VEGF therapy as the current standard of care for DME.[3] Ranibizumab, aflibercept, brolucizumab, and faricimab have been approved for DME, while bevacizumab and Pegaptanib are used as off-label drugs.[4] However, the development of these reference anti-VEGFs is limited by the demanding research and development process and a low conversion rate from development to commercialization.[5] As a result, these therapies are relatively limited in availability, expensive, and often inaccessible to many patients. This has driven the development of more affordable alternatives, namely, biosimilars.

Biosimilar drugs are biological molecules that closely match the reference biologic with no clinically meaningful differences in terms of pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, immunogenicity, safety, and efficacy.[67] Various regulatory and health agencies have established definitions and guidelines for biosimilars to ensure consistency in their development and approval. The World Health Organization (WHO) defined biosimilars as products that are highly similar in terms of their quality, safety, and efficacy to an already licensed reference product.[8] The European Medicines Agency (EMA) and the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) highlighted that while slight variations between a biosimilar and its reference product are expected, these differences must not have a clinically meaningful impact on the biosimilar’s quality, efficacy, or safety profile.[910]

For approval, a biosimilar is not required to independently establish clinical efficacy and safety. It just needs to show that it is similar to the reference product, ensuring that treatment outcomes would not differ from those of the reference product. Hence, the overall research and development costs for these products are reduced, making them more affordable than reference molecules.

Among biosimilars, interchangeable biosimilars are those that meet ‘additional requirements’ beyond demonstrating biosimilarity, allowing them to be substituted for the reference biologic at the pharmacy level without the direct intervention of the prescribing healthcare provider. The FDA designation of interchangeability requires specific switching studies that alternate between the reference biologic and the biosimilar without increasing safety risks, reduced efficacy, or immunogenicity.[11] EMA and WHO do not have specific regulations for interchangeability. The FDA approved Cimerli (ranibizumab-eqrn) as an interchangeable biosimilar to Ranibizumab in August 2022.[12] In May 2024, Yesafili (aflibercept-jbvf) and Opuviz (aflibercept-yszy) were approved by the FDA as the first interchangeable biosimilars to Eylea (Aflibercept).[13]

Regulatory overview

Global biosimilar regulation is built on stringent, science-based frameworks that ensure safety, efficacy, and quality. These frameworks adopt a stepwise development pathway, beginning with comprehensive analytical comparability, followed by focused nonclinical studies and tailored clinical trials aimed at confirming similarity rather than re-establishing independent efficacy. The EMA was the first to establish biosimilar guidelines in 2005 based on analytical, preclinical, and clinical comparability.[14] The FDA, under the 2009 Biologics Price Competition and Innovation Act, created an abbreviated approval route for biosimilars.[615] WHO provided science-based guidance, aiding nations that do not have any established systems and promoting global harmony.[6] In India, the Central Drugs Standard Control Organization (CDSCO) and Department of Biotechnology (DBT) regulate biosimilars through the “Guidelines on Similar Biologics,” given in 2012 and revised in 2016 and 2025.[1617]

The CDSCO guidelines outline a stepwise approach for demonstrating similarity between a biosimilar and its reference biological product. This includes a preclinical evaluation involving animal studies, a clinical evaluation including Phase I studies and Phase III trials, postmarketing surveillance, and periodic safety updates.[6]

Establishing Reference Biological ProductThe guidelines state that the Reference Biological Product (RBP) is central for comparability across studies. The reference product should have a well-established safety and efficacy as all subsequent comparisons are made against it. Because biologics are highly sensitive to even minor changes in cell banks, culture conditions, or purification processes, manufacturing consistency is critical, and regulators consider the manufacturing process itself a fundamental determinant of product identity. Similarity to RBP must be proven through analytical, preclinical, and clinical comparisons.

The 2025 revision proposes additional scientific principles to align the evaluation process with global standards. It includes an updated approval pathway, advanced analytical methods, and detailed guidance on reference standards and expanded in vitro studies. The revision also formalized the statistical framework for clinical similarity margins.[16]

Biosimilars to ranibizumab, aflibercept, and bevacizumab approved for DME

Yesafili (MYL-1701P), an aflibercept biosimilar, received the FDA regulatory approval in May 2024 as an interchangeable biosimilar, supported by Phase 3 data from the INSIGHT trial.[2122] Other aflibercept biosimilars include Pavblu (ABP 938, aflibercept-ayyh), FYB203/AHZANTIVE (aflibercept-mrbb), and Opuviz (aflibercept-yszy), approved by the FDA in 2024.[232425] Eydenzelt (CT-P42), a biosimilar to aflibercept, was approved on October 10, 2025.[26]

Bevacizumab biosimilars, including MVASI, ONS-5010, and BevaciRel, are not FDA-approved for intravitreal use but have been used off-label, with emerging real-world data in DME.[2127] Meanwhile, biosimilars like Byooviz are approved for age-related macular degeneration (AMD), though they currently lack substantial published data for DME.[28]

Clinical evidence and real-world data

It is essential to acknowledge that biosimilars are designed to be highly similar—not identical—to the reference molecule; however, these differences are clinically irrelevant when supported by robust comparability data. Since the introduction of biosimilars in the retina, both prospective and real-world studies have compared the biosimilar anti-VEGF to the reference anti-VEGF in DME. Clinical Studies Evaluating Biosimilars in Diabetic Macular Edema

Among the prospective studies evaluating aflibercept biosimilars in DME, the INSIGHT and CT-P42 studies are two double-masked, randomized controlled trials that have played a landmark role [Table 3]. In the INSIGHT trial, MYL-1701P showed equivalent efficacy to Aflibercept in DME, with a mean best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) gain of 6.60 vs 6.56 letters and a central subfield thickness (CST) reduction of 112 vs 124 µm at week 8. Ocular treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) occurred in 30.9% vs. 29.5% in the two groups, and antidrug antibodies (ADAs) were low in both groups, confirming MYL-1701P as a safe, effective, and affordable biosimilar.[22]

INSIGHT and CT-P42 Study Outcomes

In the CT-P42 trial, CT-P42 demonstrated equivalent efficacy to reference Aflibercept in DME, with a mean BCVA improvement of 9.43 vs. 8.85 letters at week 8 and a treatment difference of 0.58 letters. Secondary endpoints, including changes in CST and Diabetic Retinopathy Severity Scale (DRSS) scores, were similar through week 24. Safety profiles were comparable, with treatment-emergent adverse events in 50.3% (CT-P42) vs. 53.7% (Aflibercept).[32]

Most clinicians, however, prefer real-world data over data generated from registration trials, particularly when evaluating newer biosimilars.[33] Two real-world studies, RE-ENACT 2, published in 2019, and REAR-RD 2, published in 2022, demonstrated the efficacy of Razumab in patients with retinal diseases in real-world clinical practice in India.[2930]

The REAR RD-2 study, a large multicenter, retrospective, observational analysis, included 436 eyes with DME and followed patients over 48 weeks. The study demonstrated sustained improvements in BCVA from baseline through Month 12. In addition to functional gains, there was a marked reduction in intraretinal fluid (IRF) and subretinal fluid (SRF), indicating significant anatomical recovery. The incidence of intraocular inflammation remained low, reinforcing the favorable safety profile of Razumab in routine clinical practice.[30]

Similarly, the RE-ENACT 2 study (2019), a multicenter retrospective data collection analysis involving 103 eyes with DME over 48 weeks, reported significant visual and anatomical improvements. Mean BCVA improved from 0.78 logMAR at baseline to 0.55 logMAR at Week 48 (P < 0.0001). Anatomically, CST decreased from 455.5 µm to 332.2 µm, demonstrating substantial macular drying. No ocular or systemic safety concerns were identified.[29]

Apart from Razumab, Ranieyes and Ranizurel, two newer Ranibizumab biosimilars, have been approved in India. The BRESER (2025) and RaSER (2022) studies provide supportive real-world evidence for the use of these ranibizumab biosimilars in the management of DME, particularly in routine clinical settings. The BRESER study, a multicenter, retrospective, uncontrolled observational analysis involving 112 eyes, evaluated the efficacy and safety of Ranieyes over a mean follow-up of 7.7 ± 5.4 weeks. It demonstrated significant early functional and anatomical benefits, with improvement in BCVA and a statistically significant reduction in CST from baseline (P < 0.05). No drug-related ocular or systemic adverse events were reported, underscoring its favorable safety profile.[20]

Similarly, the RaSER study (2022), a smaller retrospective, consecutive, interventional multicenter study involving 11 eyes, assessed Ranizurel in treatment-naïve DME patients over a mean duration of 8.3 ± 7.9 weeks. The study reported encouraging visual outcomes and a rapid anatomical response, particularly in macular edema resolution. Despite its limited sample size, the findings suggest that Ranizurel is both safe and effective, with potential utility as a first-line biosimilar option in DME.[19]

A large, multicenter, retrospective study by Chakraborty et al.[34] compared the efficacy of innovator ranibizumab and biosimilar ranibizumab in treating DME. It concluded that biosimilar ranibizumab was as effective as innovator ranibizumab in visual outcomes and macular thickness reduction at 1-year follow-up.

Switching between different anti-VEGF agents is safe for managing retinal diseases, in both clinical trials and real-world practice.[35] The TRANSFORM-DME evaluated outcomes in patients with DME who transitioned from intravitreal aflibercept to biosimilar ranibizumab. Patients initially received intravitreal Aflibercept 2.0 mg in 0.05 mL as three monthly loading doses. They were switched to intravitreal injections of the biosimilar ranibizumab (Razumab) 0.5 mg in 0.05 mL on a pro re nata (PRN) basis after a treatment-free period of 8–12 weeks. The study demonstrated that biosimilar ranibizumab is noninferior to aflibercept, offering a cost-effective option for the long-term management of DME.[31] Thus, biosimilar ranibizumab is a viable alternative in settings where cost is a significant consideration.

Randomized clinical trials (RCTs), including INSIGHT (MYL-1701P) and CT-P42, have demonstrated that biosimilars are equivalent to reference products in terms of visual acuity gains (approximately 6.6 to 9.4 letters) and reductions in central subfield thickness (CST). Large-scale studies, such as RE-ENACT 2 and REAR-RD2 evaluating Razumab, have shown sustained visual and anatomical improvements over 48 weeks in routine clinical practice. Biosimilars are increasingly adopted as first-line treatments in cost-sensitive healthcare settings and are now recommended to address high-cost drug expenditures. Adverse Events

Concerns regarding the safety of biosimilars may stem from their abbreviated approval pathways. Protein structure, glycosylation patterns, excipients, storage conditions, and shear stress during handling can all alter immunogenic potential, underscoring the need for stringent pharmacovigilance for biosimilars.

The CRsIBS Study compared innovator and biosimilar ranibizumab and showed comparable safety profiles, with most adverse events being mild to moderate. Ocular effects, such as pain, blurring, and hemorrhage, were comparable, whereas anterior chamber inflammation was higher in the biosimilar group (56 vs 32 eyes). Systemic events were rare; however, headaches were more frequent with the biosimilar (231 vs 169 cases).[34]

The INSIGHT trial evaluated the safety and immunogenicity of MYL-1701P compared to reference aflibercept, finding similar rates of both ocular and nonocular treatment-emergent adverse events (AEs) across groups. Most AEs were mild to moderate in severity.[22] Additionally, the incidence of treatment-induced or treatment-boosted antidrug antibodies (ADAs) was low and comparable between groups. Daneshtalab et al.[36] reported no serious adverse effects with intravitreal Stivant, either alone or in combination with triamcinolone. Balasubramanium noted transient intraocular pressure (IOP) elevation and suprachoroidal hemorrhage with Razumab that resolved by day 7.[37]

AEs associated with biosimilars are typically mild to moderate, such as transient IOP elevation and subconjunctival hemorrhage, and occur at rates comparable to those observed with reference biologics. A significant ongoing challenge is the nocebo effect, in which negative perceptions of biosimilars contribute to increased reporting of minor subjective AEs.

Economic considerations

Patients with DME have low baseline quality of life indices that keep on worsening as the disease progresses. This is a result not only of vision loss but also of the high cost of treatment. Biosimilars offer significant cost savings compared to the reference anti-VEGF molecule without compromising visual outcomes. By lowering treatment costs, biosimilars can substantially reduce the financial burden on patients and health systems, improve treatment adherence, and expand access to treatment in low- and moderate-income communities.[3839]

Ranibizumab biosimilars are more cost-effective than both ranibizumab and aflibercept.[40] The introduction of biosimilar ranibizumab in 2015 was a key turning point in India. The marked reduction in the cost of ranibizumab biosimilars in India compared to the United States underscores the impact of India’s market competition and healthcare policies.[41] Virani et al.[27] showed that bevacizumab biosimilars are significantly more cost-effective than reference bevacizumab with a 10.8 ICER (incremental cost-effectiveness ratio).

A study by Zhang et al.[42] showed that a bevacizumab biosimilar (bevacizumab-vikg) may paradoxically increase costs in the United States (US). This is because the Drug Quality and Security Act (DQSA) could prohibit the use of repackaged, off-label bevacizumab currently used for intravitreal injections. However, it remains uncertain whether on-label bevacizumab will receive the FDA approval.[43]

Challenges

Despite the tremendous role of reference anti-VEGFs in DME, biosimilars approved and utilized for the same are considerably less. Many physicians need to expand their understanding of biosimilars. The bio-USER (Biosimilars for retinal diseases: United States-Europe awareness survey) survey assessed awareness of biosimilar anti-VEGF agents among retina specialists in the US and Europe. It revealed that despite being aware of the biosimilars, many physicians needed additional information (18.75%) and real-world evidence (25%) before considering a switch to a biosimilar.[44]

Safety concerns among clinicians and patients regarding biosimilars contribute to a nocebo, or negative-perception effect.[45] This negative perception often leads to increased reporting of minor AEs, such as low-grade inflammation. This contributed to the initial perception of a heightened immunogenic response associated with Razumab. However, subsequent evidence from real-world cohorts has not demonstrated any unexpected safety signals with Razumab.[46]

Reducing the nocebo effect requires a comprehensive, multifaceted approach. Essential strategies involve promoting open and empathetic communication, presenting information in a positive and balanced way, and educating both patients and healthcare professionals about biosimilars and the nocebo effect. These measures foster trust, shape realistic expectations, and empower patients, ultimately minimizing negative perceptions and enhancing treatment outcomes.[47]

Further, different geographical regions differ in patient profiles and finances; thus, an approach effective in one region may not be effective in another.[48] Another limitation is the paucity of pivotal and long-term ophthalmic studies for the use of biosimilars in DME. Many biosimilars, especially bevacizumab biosimilars, are primarily evaluated for systemic diseases and extrapolated to ophthalmic conditions.[49] Ocular drug behavior differs from systemic disposition, underscoring the need for appropriately powered, DME-specific randomized trials with long-term follow-up to address this evidence gap.

Future Directions

The future of biosimilars in DME is promising and can further evolve through streamlined regulations, expanded clinical evidence, and technological advances. Advancements in research and personalized treatment approaches shall enhance affordability and optimize patient outcomes.[40] Combination therapies and alternative drug regimens can be explored to broaden therapeutic potential and improve patients’ quality of life.

Conclusion

Biosimilars represent a clinically equivalent, more accessible, and cost-effective alternative to reference anti-VEGF therapies in the management of DME. Accumulating clinical evidence, including well-designed trials conducted exclusively in DME populations, has consistently demonstrated therapeutic equivalence to reference agents, with comparable improvements in visual acuity and similar safety and immunogenicity profiles.

With the growing body of research, the conduct of dedicated DME-specific studies, and increasing awareness and confidence among clinicians and patients, biosimilars are poised to play an expanding role in retinal practice. Their wider adoption has the potential to reduce treatment burden, improve access to timely care, and ultimately enhance visual outcomes and quality of life for individuals with diabetes worldwide.
