Variability in the TLR3 type I interferon pathway is predictive of RNA vaccine responses

ABSTRACT
Biological predictors of variable vaccine responses are lacking. We hypothesized that variability in prevaccine innate immune responses, specifically for type I interferons (IFN-I), is predictive of postvaccine antigen-specific responses. To test this, we assessed prevaccine immune responses at protein and transcriptomic levels following whole blood stimulation with Toll-like receptor (TLR) viral agonists in healthy adolescents and adults. Four weeks after the second vaccine dose, with either the BNT162b2 mRNA or CoronaVac inactivated virus vaccine, we assessed antigen-specific T cell cytokine responses and plasma antibody levels. BNT162b2 vaccinees had increased production of the antigen-specific T cell cytokines interleukin-2 (IL-2), interferon-γ, and IL-21 after severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 spike stimulation, as well as increased antibody levels and serum pseudo-neutralization compared with CoronaVac recipients. In direct support of our hypothesis, we find that prevaccine poly(I:C) (polyinosine-polycytidylic acid; TLR3 viral agonist) IFN-I responses were significantly associated with the postvaccine T cell cytokine responses. In an independent cohort of 990 healthy donors, we confirmed the significant association between poly(I:C)-induced IFN-α and spike-induced cytokines in mRNA vaccine recipients. We further confirmed this specific association in a cohort of healthy Europeans and identified a common genetic polymorphism in TLR3 that affects IFN-I induction and subsequent vaccine-specific T cell responses. This study shows that preexisting innate immune variability can predict the effectiveness of vaccine responses and identifies pathways relevant to mRNA vaccination. Targeting the specific innate immune pathway relevant for a vaccine may provide a new approach for tailoring vaccines to different populations.

INTRODUCTION

Vaccination is one of the most cost-effective public health strategies worldwide and is key to preventing severe disease from several infections (1). Despite these successes, vaccine-induced protection varies between and within populations (2). This variability can result from either the vaccine formulation used or may be due to inherent immune variability between individuals (2). While certain factors, such as old age or male sex (3, 4), are known to result in poorer vaccine responses, a good understanding of the mechanisms behind such effects limits their use as predictors.

Systems vaccinology studies have previously been performed to identify predictors of strong or weak vaccine responses (5, 6). These have focused mostly on baseline gene expression, plasma cytokines, or early postvaccine time points (7, 8). On the basis of indirect correlates of protection, most of these studies have post hoc divided vaccine recipients into binary responders and nonresponders for analysis. This limits statistical power and neglects more nuanced interpretation of more variable vaccine responses. Despite these limitations, studies have identified type I interferon (IFN-I) gene signatures to be predictive of live attenuated vaccine responses, as is the case for influenza or yellow fever vaccines (4, 8). Confirming the importance of IFN-I responses in other vaccine contexts, systems vaccinology studies of the BNT162b2 (Pfizer) mRNA COVID-19 vaccine in humans found an enrichment in interferon-stimulated gene (ISG) signatures in myeloid cells after secondary immunization (9).

We hypothesized that variability in prevaccine innate immune responses, critical for vaccine priming, is associated with, and predictive of, vaccine responsiveness. To test this, we took advantage of a clinical study comparing two different licensed COVID-19 vaccines, the BNT162b2 mRNA vaccine and the CoronaVac inactivated virus vaccine, in a population of healthy adolescents and adults in Hong Kong. While BNT162b2 induced significantly higher titers of neutralizing antibodies, it was previously shown that T cell immunogenicity was not different between the two vaccines in this cohort (10). Furthermore, observational studies have demonstrated similar effectiveness of the two vaccines for preventing symptomatic or severe disease after severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection (11, 12), highlighting the need to better understand the mechanisms of vaccine-induced immune protection. Differences in immune responses induced by these two vaccines provide additional biological variability to test our hypothesis of whether variability in preexisting induced innate immune responses are predictive of vaccine-specific responses.

In contrast with previous systems vaccinology studies, we assessed variability in innate immune responses after whole blood stimulation to multiple agonists. To minimize technical variability, which can preclude detection of biological signatures, we used TruCulture whole blood stimulation assays. We have previously shown these assays to be more reproducible than conventional peripheral blood mononuclear cell stimulation (13). In this study, we also used TruCulture to study postvaccine immunity through stimulation with SARS-CoV-2 spike protein (14). We used ultrasensitive cytokine (Simoa) and gene expression profiling (NanoString) to have a continuous numerical measure of antigen-specific T cell responses after vaccination to capture the full breadth of vaccine-induced immunity for enhanced predictive power.

RESULTS

Comparison of vaccine-induced cytokine responses and antibody levels between BNT162b2 and CoronaVac recipients

Four weeks after the second vaccine dose, we assessed the whole blood immune response to SARS-CoV-2 spike stimulation by measuring IFN-γ, interleukin-2 (IL-2), and IL-21 by Simoa digital enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) after 22 hours of stimulation. IFN-γ was selected to reflect type 1 T helper cell (TH1 cell) responses, IL-2 as a surrogate marker of effector and memory T cell responses, and IL-21 T follicular helper cell (TFH cell) responses. All vaccine recipients showed a significant induction of all three cytokines compared to unstimulated controls. However, BNT162b2 (BB) recipients had significantly (P < 0.001) higher levels of all three analytes compared to CoronaVac (CC) recipients (Fig. 1A). No significant differences were observed between children and adults (fig. S1A), or between males and females (fig. S1B), for either vaccine. All three cytokines showed significant correlations with each other for both vaccines (Fig. 1B), with higher Spearmans correlation coefficient (Rs) scores for BB (Rs = 0.813 to 0.902) compared to CC (Rs = 0.619 to 0.864), reflecting a stronger and more robust spike-specific response after mRNA vaccination.

We also assessed serological responses through four different assays; ELISA-based spike-specific immunoglobulin G (SIgG), a surrogate virus neutralization assay (SVNT), S-Flow IgG binding, and S-Flow receptor binding domain (RBD) neutralization as detailed in Materials and Methods. As previously described (10), we saw significantly stronger serological responses in BB recipients compared to CC recipients with all antibody measures (fig. S2). In contrast to the T cell cytokine responses, the antibody data showed more significant and stronger correlations in the CC recipients (except between the two neutralization assays). However, this may reflect the distribution of the antibody responses in BB recipients, which were all at the maximum for SVNT and showed greater variability for S-Flow IgG binding (fig. S2). Cytokine-antibody correlations were only significant when the total population was assessed, and not per vaccine group, perhaps reflecting a less tightly coordinated immune network at this 4-week postvaccine time point and the value of studying two different vaccine formulations in parallel (Fig. 1B). To minimize overcorrection for potential false discoveries, we kept anti-spike IgG as the sole antibody phenotype in downstream analyses.

Comparison of whole blood SARS-CoV-2 antigen-specific transcriptomic responses between BNT162b2 and CoronaVac vaccine recipients

To have a more global comparison of immune responses induced by the different vaccines, we assessed transcriptomic responses in the Null and spike TruCulture tubes 4 weeks after the second vaccine dose using NanoString gene expression arrays. Principal components analysis (PCA) of the 800 immune genes measured showed a clear separation between the spike responses of BB recipients, with less distinct responses for CC-vaccinated group (Fig. 2, A and B). To assess more directly the vaccine-induced responses, we compared the spike stimulation with the Null unstimulated control separately for each vaccine. This showed a stronger gene induction as assessed by log2 fold change (>1.5) and false discovery rate (FDR) adjusted P values for BB recipients (Fig. 2C) compared to CC (Fig. 2D). Most differentially expressed genes were up-regulated in both groups, with few genes down-regulated (Fig. 2, C and D). Pathway analysis comparing the spike conditions of both vaccines identified natural killer cell (NK cell) activity and lymphocyte trafficking enriched in CC recipients; and TH2 cell responses, nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) signaling, IL-2 signaling, and IFN-II responses as enriched in BB recipients (Fig. 2E). Other pathways appeared to be differentially induced between the two vaccines although were not statistically significant after FDR adjustment. Given the importance of T cell pathway priming, we looked specifically at TH1 (fig. S3A) and TH2 (fig. S3B) genes for both vaccines. Expression levels of these individual genes were consistent with results from the pathway and cytokine analysis, with most TH1/TH2-specific genes elevated in CC recipients compared to BB recipients after spike stimulation. We next directly compared the spike responses between both vaccines and identified 86 differentially expressed genes (P < 0.05, log2 fold change > 1.5) (Fig. 2F). As expected from the previous analysis, more genes were up-regulated in BB recipients including IFN-γ and IL-21. In contrast, more chemokine receptors were up-regulated in CC recipients consistent with the pathway analysis.

Prevaccine-induced innate immune responses are associated with postvaccine responses

After establishing vaccine-specific phenotypes covering T and B cell immunity, we next tested our hypothesis that they were associated with prevaccine innate immune variability. We selected agonists that target specific innate immune pathways important in the IFN-I response, namely, Toll-like receptor 3 (TLR3)/melanoma differentiation-associated protein 5 (MDA5) activated by poly(I:C) (polyinosine-polycytidylic acid), TLR7/8 activated by R848, and TLR9 activated by oligodeoxynucleotide (ODN). We stimulated blood from all vaccine recipients immediately before vaccination with these three agonists in addition to a control unstimulated Null condition. We measured the levels of 24 cytokines in the supernatants of stimulated whole blood by Luminex multianalyte profiling and IFN-α and IFN-β by digital ELISA. PCA showed distinct clustering based on the stimulus, with no obvious clustering based on the type of vaccine received, as expected at this prevaccine time point (Fig. 3, A and B).

To test whether prevaccine innate immune responses were associated with postvaccine-specific responses, we ran multiple linear regression models that included age, gender, vaccine type, and secreted cytokines to independently explain spike-induced IFN-γ (Fig. 3C), IL-2 (Fig. 3D), IL-21 (Fig. 3E), and SIgG (Fig. 3F) after vaccination. For each vaccine response variable, there were four independent models [Null, ODN, poly(I:C), and R848] from which we obtained FDR-corrected P values for each association (Fig. 3, C to F). Postvaccine spike-induced IFN-γ was significantly (FDR P < 0.05) associated with prevaccine poly(I:C)-induced IFN-α, IFN-γ, and CXCL9 and ODN-induced CCL2 and CCL3 (Fig. 3C). IL-2 showed a similar pattern for poly(I:C) (in addition to CCL4, CCL8, and IL-1RA) and ODN, as well as CXCL9 and CXCL8 associations in the Null (Fig. 3D). For IL-21 and IgG, the only significant (FDR P < 0.01) associations were under the Null condition for CXCL9 (Fig. 3E) and CXCL11 (Fig. 3F), respectively.

We next performed the analysis for each vaccine separately to test for vaccine-specific immune pathway associations. This showed that the significant associations (FDR P < 0.05) with prevaccine poly(I:C)-induced IFN-α responses were only present in mRNA vaccine recipients consistently for both spike-induced IFN-γ and IL-2 (Fig. 4, A and B). In contrast, for CC recipients, only CXCL9 and CXCL11 levels under the Null condition were consistently associated with IL-2 and IgG (Fig. 4, B and D). More conventional visualization of these values demonstrated linear correlations between prevaccine poly(I:C)-induced IFN-α and postvaccine spike-induced IFN-γ (Fig. 4E) and IL-2 (Fig. 4F) in mRNA recipients and between prevaccine Null and spike-induced IL-2 (Fig. 4G) and vaccine-specific IgG (Fig. 4H).

Prevaccine gene signatures are associated with postvaccine responses

To expand our analysis of vaccine response prediction, we performed NanoString gene expression analysis on the prevaccine Null, poly(I:C), and ODN whole blood stimulated samples. PCA showed all stimulated samples segregated from the Null control along PC1 (Fig. 5A). R848-stimulated samples segregated from poly(I:C) and ODN stimulations, along PC2, and poly(I:C) and ODN clustered separately along PC3. On the basis of the cytokine results, we calculated separate gene scores (z scores) for IFN-I and -II [previously described (15)] under each prevaccine stimulation condition. Integrating these gene scores into the linear models showed significant associations between poly(I:C) IFN-I gene scores with spike-induced IFN-γ, IL-2, and IL-21 only in the mRNA vaccine recipients, confirming the cytokine protein associations (Fig. 5B). We also calculated gene z scores for IFN-I and -II on the postvaccination spike-induced gene expression as an additional vaccine endpoint. As expected from the cytokine data, these were significantly higher in BB recipients compared to CC (fig. S4, A and B). Integration of the spike-specific IFN-γ gene score in linear regression models confirmed a strong association for poly(I:C)-induced immunity with mRNA vaccines (Fig. 5C). The spike IFN-I gene score was also associated with poly(I:C)-induced type I and type II gene scores (fig. S4C). For CC, the prevaccine ODN-induced IFN-I and IFN-II gene scores were significantly associated with spike type II responses (Fig. 5C), an association that was not previously observed at the protein level. To explore further associations, we also assessed individual genes that may be associated with prevaccine poly(I:C) responses and spike-induced cytokines in BB recipients. This showed significant associations between multiple prevaccine-induced ISGs (e.g., IFI44, MX1, and OAS) consistent with previous results, as well as some individual chemokines (CXCL1/CXCL8), and all three cytokine postvaccine responses (Fig. 5, D to F), suggesting an importance for TLR3/MDA5-induced cellular migration in mRNA vaccine immunogenicity.

Validation cohort confirms association between poly(I:C)-induced IFN-α and SARS-CoV-2 spike–induced cytokines in BNT162b2 vaccine recipients

We wanted to validate the association between poly(I:C)-induced immune responses and the mRNA vaccine–specific responses in an independent cohort. For this, we first took advantage of the Healthy Human Global Project–Hong Kong (HHGP-HK) cohort that we recently established in Hong Kong (16). The advantage of this cohort is that it contains donors who had received either the mRNA BNT162b2 or the CoronaVac vaccine as part of local public COVID-19 vaccination campaigns. Whole blood TruCulture stimulations for both poly(I:C) and SARS-CoV-2 spike proteins were also collected, from which we measured cytokines with the same Simoa digital ELISA used in the COVID-19 Vaccination in Adolescents and Children (COVAC) cohort. The main difference in the experimental design of this validation cohort was that poly(I:C) and SARS-CoV-2 spike whole blood stimulations were performed on the same day and at variable times after vaccination for each donor. Analysis of the age distribution for the vaccines showed a strong age bias, with older donors more likely to have received the CC vaccine and younger donors the BB vaccine (fig. S5A). In contrast, the average number of weeks since last antigen exposure (either vaccination or confirmed infection) was highly similar between the two vaccine groups with an average time of 38 weeks (fig. S5B). We then ran linear regression models to predict SARS-CoV-2 spike–induced IFN-γ, IL-2, and IL-21 integrating donor age and gender, time since last antigen exposure, vaccine type (CC or BB), and poly(I:C)-induced IFN-α (n = 990). Notably, analysis of the relationship between spike-induced cytokines and poly(I:C)-induced IFN-α showed highly significant associations (P < 0.01) in only BB vaccine recipients (Fig. 6A). These results confirm the importance of this innate immune pathway for mRNA vaccine–induced T cell responses, as well as the ability to retroactively test such associations up to a year postvaccination. We then took advantage of a recent longitudinal analysis of the Milieu Intérieur (MI) cohort, a French study where recruitment also occurred in the year following widespread COVID-19 vaccines (17). We focused this analysis on donors who received three doses of the mRNA BNT162b2 (n = 112). Using a linear model to predict SARS-CoV-2 spike–induced IFN-γ, IL-2, and IL-21 while integrating donor age and gender, time since last antigen exposure, and poly(I:C)-induced IFN-α, we observed significant associations for IFN-γ (P < 0.01) and IL-2 (P < 0.05) (Fig. 6A). In this cohort, we previously described a trans protein quantitative trait loci in the TLR3 gene after stimulation with poly(I:C) (18). We therefore hypothesized that this polymorphism (rs3775291) may also affect vaccine antigen–specific responses. Analysis of spike-induced cytokines as a function of the different genotypes revealed significant effect sizes (Fig. 6B) and associations (P < 0.05) (Fig. 6C) for all three cytokines confirming our hypothesis. Consistent with our previous results, individuals with a CC genotype had increased poly(I:C)-induced IFN-α and subsequently higher T cell responses to mRNA vaccination (Fig. 6, D and E). This result further confirms the association with the TLR3 pathway and its importance for responses to RNA vaccination.

DISCUSSION

Innate immune responses are critical for antigen presentation and successful primary vaccination. Therefore, the level and quality of innate immune responses are likely to have a subsequent impact on the magnitude of the vaccine-induced adaptive immune response. Hence, we hypothesized that variability in specific innate immune responses before vaccination would be associated with, and predictive of, the quality of vaccine-specific responses. The challenge in testing such a hypothesis lies in knowing which innate immune pathways are the most relevant and quantifying the variability in such pathways before vaccination. The initial COVID-19 vaccination campaign in Hong Kong offered a unique opportunity to address this question by comparing two markedly different vaccine formulations that would prime different arms of innate immunity. We stimulated whole blood prevaccine using highly standardized assays, with agonists targeting TLR3, MDA5, TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9 innate pathways relevant for RNA or inactivated viral vaccines. This revealed associations between the prevaccine innate pathways specific for the different vaccine types, namely, poly(I:C)-induced responses for mRNA vaccines and ODN-induced responses for inactivated viral vaccines. IFN-I and -II pathways were consistently associated at both cytokine and gene expression levels, with the T cell response to each vaccine, with specific upstream pathways associated with each vaccine type. These results of TLR-induced immune signatures correlating with vaccine outcomes are consistent with a previous study that characterized baseline innate immune endotypes as predictive of vaccine-induced antibodies for diverse vaccines (19).

The specific role of IFN-I responses in vaccine priming, in particular for mRNA vaccines, has been somewhat unclear until now. Initial seminal studies for the development of mRNA vaccines by Karikó et al. (20) highlighted the need to reduce IFN-I induction, as high levels had a detrimental impact on RNA translation. In contrast with these observations were the first studies of mRNA COVID-19 vaccines in both humans and mice, which consistently reported an early IFN-I gene signature and later IFN-II response (9). In addition, studies of patients with impaired IFN-I responses, either due to neutralizing autoantibodies or rare inborn errors of immunity, showed no defect in mRNA vaccine–induced antibody responses. Crucially, these studies did not examine T cell immunity (21). Work performed in knockout mice showed that BNT162b2 is recognized by MDA5, which leads to induction of a strong IFN-I response required for a vaccine-specific CD8 T cell response (22). Consistent with the human studies (21), the vaccine-induced antibody levels in MDA5 knockout mice were not affected (22).

We propose that the initial IFN-I response is important for antigen-presenting cell activation, but the level is critical, with excessive or prolonged IFN-I signaling likely becoming detrimental for downstream adaptive immunity and for the translation of vaccine nucleoside-modified mRNA as originally demonstrated by Karikó et al. (20). Our results also confirm the importance of TLR3/MDA5 for mRNA vaccine responses and not TLR7/8 an important question which has so far only been addressed in mouse models (22). It also provides additional knowledge for understanding the impaired vaccine responses in autoimmune patients with elevated IFN such as systemic lupus erythematosus (23). More widely, we provide a proof-of-concept approach for using systems immunological studies in humans to better understand immune pathways and mechanisms required for robust vaccine responses, as well as an approach to validate such findings in established human cohort studies. This included integration of genetic analysis that demonstrated a potential mechanism for how common polymorphisms in innate receptors may influence vaccine responses as previously reported for these specific genetic variants (24). Whether such an approach can be applied to other vaccine platforms will require additional studies.

Despite the widespread use and successful impact of vaccination, an important open question remains on how to best induce long-term immunity. This was highlighted by initial studies reporting lower immunogenicity from CoronaVac vaccination compared to mRNA vaccines based on pseudo-neutralization assays (25), while in contrast, retrospective epidemiological studies reported high levels of population-level protective immunity (26, 27). Our postvaccine transcriptomic analysis comparing the responses induced by these two different approaches may provide clues to understanding mechanisms of inactivated viral vaccine-induced protection, in addition to the previously reported effect on T cell immunity (28). Specifically, we uncovered up-regulation of NK cell activity and lymphocyte trafficking that was not observed in mRNA recipients, a finding consistent with a previous study, which reported IFN-γ–producing NK cells in CC recipients (29). Further studies comparing different means of protection induced by different vaccine platforms may help to identify the optimum combinations for long-lasting immune protection against viral infection or even allow population-specific strategies for at risk or vulnerable populations, such as the elderly or immunocompromised.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

COVAC clinical study

Donors included in this study were participants in the COVAC, a registered clinical study [Department of Health of Hong Kong (HK), Clinical Trial Certificate 101894; clinicaltrials.gov, NCT04800133] for administration of two doses of the two COVID-19 vaccines, BB (Pfizer) or CC (SinoVac), as previously described (10). The original immunobridging study aimed to assess the reactogenicity and immunogenicity of the two vaccines in children compared to adults. The research protocol and procedures were approved by the University of Hong Kong (HKU)/Hospital Authority HK West Cluster Institutional Review Board (HAHKW IRB; UW21-157) and were in compliance with the October 2013 Declaration of Helsinki principles. These were performed in Community Vaccination Centres supported by HKU under the HK Government’s COVID-19 Vaccination Programme.

COVAC study participants

This study included 11- to 17-year-old children (n = 219) and ≥18-year-old adults (n = 33). Recruitment targeted schoolchildren across HK. Potential participants were required to be healthy and with no known history of COVID-19 by self-reporting, at any of the three study visits. Absence of natural SARS-CoV-2 infection was independently confirmed by lack of IgG antibody to spike-RBD at baseline and absence of open reading frame 8 IgG antibody at any visit. Additional exclusion criteria included history of severe allergy, significant neuropsychiatric conditions, immunocompromised states, transfusion of blood products within 60 days, hemophilia, pregnancy, or breastfeeding. Study physicians provided information to participants and their parents or legally acceptable representatives (LARs) and obtained informed consent from participants aged 18 years or above or from parents or LARs of underage participants. Assent was also obtained from underage participants (10).

COVAC vaccine administration

Peripheral blood was taken before vaccination and 4 weeks after the second dose. Recipients received either two injections of BB (109 children/15 adults) or CC (110 children/19 adults), which were 0.3 ml containing 30 μg of COVID-19 mRNA vaccine embedded in lipid nanoparticles or 0.5 ml containing 600 SU (subunits), equivalent to 3 μg of inactivated SARS-CoV-2 virus as antigen, respectively. The two doses of BNT162b2 or CoronaVac were given 21 to 28 and 28 to 35 days apart, respectively. Two doses were administered during the course of this clinical study following the pharmaceutical manufacturer’s instructions at the time, which was before booster recommendations due to emergence of novel variants and waning immunity that were observed subsequently.

HHGP-HK cohort

The HHGP-HK cohort was previously described elsewhere (16). The HHGP-HK clinical study protocol was first approved by the Institutional Review Board of The HKU/HAHKW IRB on 16 August 2021 with reference number UW21-549. HHGP-HK is registered with the HKU Clinical Trial Registry (HKUCTR) and is available for public access on www.HKUCTR.com with study identifier HKUCTR-2959. The study is sponsored by the Centre for Immunology & Infection and was conducted as a single center study at the HKU without any investigational product. The protocol is registered under ClinicalTrials.gov (study number NCT05174624). Briefly, 1025 healthy donors of Han Chinese ancestry were recruited on the basis of defined inclusion/exclusion criteria and stratified by age (20 to 79 years) and gender (half women/half men). Vaccine and recent infectious history with SARS-CoV-2 were recorded in a clinical case report form, and whole blood stimulations was performed as described above. Among the 1025 recruited donors, 731 reported receiving BNT162b2 and 259 received CoronaVac as their final vaccine dose. Thirty-five donors were not included in this study due to unvaccinated or unreliable vaccination status.

Milieu Intérieur cohort

The longitudinal Milieu Intérieur was previously described elsewhere (17). Briefly, 415 donors of Western European ancestry who were originally recruited in 2011 (30) were resampled 10 years after the initial study. The 10-year follow-up “V3” study was approved by the Comité de Protection des Personnes–Nord Ouest III (Committee for the Protection of Persons) on 27 January 2022. The study was sponsored by the Institut Pasteur and was conducted as a single center study without any investigational product. The protocol is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (study# NCT05381857). Vaccine and recent infectious history with SARS-CoV-2 were recorded in a clinical case report form, and whole blood stimulations was performed as described above. Among the 415 donors recruited in this particular study, 112 reported receiving three doses of BNT162b2 who were included in the analysis. Genetic datasets were previously described (31).

Functional immune assays

TruCulture (Rules Based Medicine) whole blood functional assays were performed as previously described (32). Batch preprepared tubes including media and stimulator including media and stimulator were thawed at room temperature, and 1 ml of fresh blood was distributed into each tube within 15 min of collection. Tubes were mixed by inverting them and incubated at 37°C for 22 hours in a dry block incubator. After the incubation time, a valve was manually inserted into the tube to separate the supernatant from the blood cells. The supernatant was collected, aliquoted, and immediately stored at −80°C for protein secretion analysis. Cell pellets were resuspended in 2 ml of TRIzol LS (Sigma-Aldrich), and tubes were vortexed for 2 min at 2000 rpm and stored at −80°C for gene expression analysis. At the first time point before vaccination (V1), 1 ml of whole blood was stimulated with poly(I:C) (20 μg/ml), R848 (Resiquimod) (1 μM), and ODN (CpG-A; ODN2216) (25 μg/ml) (all from Invivogen) and a Null control. Four weeks after the second vaccine dose, 1 ml of whole blood was incubated with SARS-CoV-2 spike protein (1 μg/ml) (Rules Based Medicine) and a Null control as detailed above. For the validation cohorts, HHGP-HK and Milieu Intérieur, stimulations with Null, poly(I:C), and SARS-CoV-2 spike protein were performed on the same day of study inclusion using the same batch of TruCulture tubes as used in COVAC study.

Cytokine immunoassays

Cytokines in the supernatant were measured by Luminex multianalyte assays (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 23 proteins and by digital ELISA (Simoa, Quanterix) for IFN-α and IFN-β (prevaccine innate stimulations) or IFN-γ, IL-2, and IL-21 (postvaccine spike stimulations) using homebrew assays as previously described (33). The IL-21 assay was developed with monoclonal antibodies produced by Evitra using a previously described epitope (34).

NanoString gene expression arrays

Total RNA was extracted from TRIzol-stabilized cell pellets using NucleoSpin 96 miRNA kit (Macherey-Nagel). RNA concentrations were measured using QuantiFluor RNA system kit (Promega), and RNA integrity numbers were determined using the Agilent RNA 6000 Nano kit (Agilent Technologies). Total RNA samples were analyzed using the Human Host Response panel (NanoString) profiling 800 immunology and host response–related human genes at Cold Spring Biotech Crop, Taiwan. Gene expression data were normalized, as previously described (35), using the nSolver software (NanoString).

Gene set enrichment analysis

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed on NanoString data to compare the whole blood transcriptomic responses to spike protein between those vaccinated with the BNT162b2 and with the CoronaVac vaccines. GSEA (version 4.2.2) was run using a pathway dataset built from the NanoString Human Host Response panel. Parameters were set as follows: method = expression dataset, permutation type = gene_set, number of permutations = 1000, enrichment statistic = weighted, min set size = 5, max set size = 200. An FDR adjusted P value of <0.05 was taken as significant.

Serological assays

Anti-Spike IgG binding and pseudo-neutralization assays were performed on serum collected 4 weeks after the second dose as previously described (36). S-Flow assays for IgG binding and RBD-ACE (angiotensin converting enzyme) neutralization were also performed on serum collected 4 weeks after the second dose (36).

Cytokine gene scores

The IFN-I and -II gene scores were calculated on the basis of previously defined gene signatures for whole blood stimulated with recombinant IFN-β and IFN-γ, respectively (15). The genes for the IFN-I score included the following: BST2, CCL8, CCR1, CXCL10, IFI35, IFIH1, IFITM1, IRF7, MX1, STAT2, TNFSF10, and TNFSF13B; and those for the IFN-II score included the following: CXCL9, HLA-DMB, HLA-DPA1, HLA-DPB1, IDO1, JAK2, SOCS1, and HLA-DRA. Gene scores were calculated as the average gene level z scores per sample.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was carried out in RStudio (version 4.2.2). Paired t tests (Wilcoxon) were used to compare the postvaccine Null and spike conditions within the same vaccine. Unpaired t tests were used to compare the spike response between vaccine types. Linear models including age and gender were used to assess associations between prevaccine cytokine concentrations and prevaccine gene signature scores with various postvaccine phenotypes (the spike transcriptomic response, spike-induced protein secretion, pseudo-neutralization, etc.) from which P values and percentage of variance explained from the models are reported. For analysis of vaccine associations in the HHGP-HK and MI cohorts, an additional covariate in the model included time since last spike antigen exposure (self-reported vaccination or infection). Associated P values were presented as −log10 values using Graphpad Prism. PCAs were generated using the prcomp package, volcano plots were generated using the EnhancedVolcano plot package, box plots were generated using ggplot2, and correlation analysis was performed using the ggplot2 extension ggally. Multiple testing was corrected by FDR adjustment; FDR P values < 0.05 and < 0.01 were considered as significant, and both thresholds are visualized on graphs as indicated.
