MiPEP133 enhances anti-tumor immunity in nasopharyngeal carcinoma by activating CD8 + T cells
Authors: Mingjing Zhu, Huisi Zhang, Ding Liang, Fan Huang, Lanhua Wu, Zuoting Cao, Sixia Chen, Min Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundActivation of the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) is crucial for controlling nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) progression. This study aims to identify miPEP133 as novel therapeutic targets for NPC treatment.MethodsRNA-Seq was used to assess miPEP133’s effect on NPC cells and T-cell-related signaling pathways. IHC measured miPEP133 protein expression in human NPC tissues. Functional assays evaluated miPEP133’s role in anti-tumor immunity. NPC cells were co-cultured with T cells to examine miPEP133’s impact on CD8 + T-cell activation, cytotoxic factor secretion, and DC maturation via flow cytometry, ELISA, and a subcutaneous xenograft model. Immunofluorescence (IF), ELISA, and Western blot assessed miPEP133’s effect on NPC cell immunogenicity. Finally, electron microscopy, IF, Western blot, and RT-qPCR systematically verified miPEP133’s molecular mechanism in NPC cells and its immunomodulatory effects on T cells.ResultsThis study confirmed that miPEP133 expression in NPC was positively correlated with CD8 + T-cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity and may serve as an independent prognostic factor for survival. High miPEP133 increased CD8 + T-cell infiltration, enhanced secretion of Granzyme B, IFN-γ, and IL-2 from CD8 + T cells, and promoted DC maturation. Moreover, miPEP133 boosted tumor cell antigen presentation via endoplasmic reticulum stress. In a nude mouse xenograft model, miPEP133 significantly inhibited tumor growth, further validating its role in enhancing anti-tumor immunity. Mechanistically, miPEP133 activated type I interferon responses by regulating the STING pathway, subsequently reactivating T-cell immunity via the JAK1/STAT1/STAT4 axis.ConclusionsMiPEP133 overexpression in NPC cells significantly enhances CD8 + T-cell-mediated immunity, revealing a novel anti-tumor mechanism. These findings demonstrate miPEP133’s critical role in shaping the NPC immune microenvironment, highlighting its therapeutic potential.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-026-08779-7.

Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is a malignant tumor originating from the epithelial cells of the nasopharyngeal mucosa [1]. Its incidence exhibits significant geographical clustering, being higher in southern China, Southeast Asia, northern and northeastern Africa, Alaska, and western Canada [2]. Currently, radiotherapy combined with chemotherapy constitutes the primary treatment for this disease [3]. Although these therapies effectively control tumor growth, over 30% of patients experience recurrence or distant metastasis following treatment [4]. Notably, NPC represents the most aggressive subtype among head and neck malignancies, displaying the highest metastasis rate. Moreover, more than 70% of patients present with locally advanced or advanced disease at initial diagnosis [5]. As patients with advanced NPC often exhibit reduced sensitivity to radiotherapy and chemotherapy, clinical management faces considerable challenges. Thus, investigating the biological characteristics and etiology of NPC holds significant clinical importance.

The tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) is a complex system composed of various immune cells and immunoregulatory molecules. These components interact with tumor cells, regulating tumor growth, progression, and the intensity and orientation of anti-tumor immune responses [6, 7]. Studies indicate that the occurrence and progression of NPC are closely associated with TIME [8]. CD8 + T cells, as crucial defensive immune cells, play a significant role in tumor initiation, progression, and immune escape [9]. Immunogenic cell death (ICD), a distinct form of cell death, can release damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) to induce dendritic cell (DC) maturation, thereby stimulating anti-tumor immunity. This phenomenon has attracted increasing attention in recent years [10–12]. Therefore, a detailed investigation into immune components within the tumor microenvironment is vital for understanding tumor pathogenesis, predicting disease progression, and developing novel therapeutic strategies.

MiPEP133 is a small peptide encoded by miR-34a precursor RNA (MIR-34AHG), first discovered by our research group. Previous studies have confirmed that this peptide localizes to mitochondria and exhibits tumor-inhibitory functions by inducing cancer cell apoptosis and inhibiting cell migration and invasion [13]. Recently, the interaction between mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) and the immune microenvironment has garnered considerable attention [14]. Under certain conditions, mitochondria release mtDNA, which activates multiple innate immune signaling pathways, including the cGAS-STING signaling pathway, inflammasomes, and Toll-like receptors [15, 16]. Our earlier research demonstrated that miPEP133 expression in tumor cells could induce mitochondrial damage and subsequent mtDNA release; however, its impact on the anti-tumor immune response in NPC remains unclear. In this study, RNA-seq analyses revealed that the expression of miPEP133 is positively correlated with anti-tumor immune responses in NPC. miPEP133 promotes the release of mtDNA, thereby activating the IFN-I response in NPC cells. Concurrently, miPEP133 induces ICD, which facilitates the maturation of DC and ultimately enhances the anti-tumor immune function of CD8 + T cells. Together, these findings demonstrate that miPEP133 potentiates anti-tumor immunity in NPC through the activation of CD8 + T cells.

Methods

Cell culture and stable cell line construction

The human NPC cell lines, C666-1 and TWO3, used in this study were provided by Professor Zeng Musheng (Cancer Center, Sun Yat-sen University). Cells were cultured routinely in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum at 37 °C with 5% CO2. To establish stable cell lines overexpressing miPEP133, cells were infected with a lentiviral vector containing the miPEP133 coding sequence. Stable transformants were selected using puromycin screening. Subsequently, knockdown and re-expression of STING were achieved in cell lines using a lentiviral vector and a plasmid, respectively, the gene expression level and protein knock-down efficiency were verified by RT-qPCR and western blot respectively.

RNA-seq analysis

Gene expression levels were quantified using RSEM and normalized as TPM values. Differential expression analysis was performed based on raw read counts using DESeq2. P values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) procedure, and genes with an adjusted P value (padj, equivalent to FDR) < 0.05 and fold change (FC) ≥ 1.5 or ≤ 0.67 were considered differentially expressed genes (DEGs). GO enrichment analysis was conducted using GOATOOLS, and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed using KOBAS. Enrichment significance was evaluated using adjusted P values/FDR. In addition, the complete DEG list and pathway enrichment results, including enrichment statistics and FDR values, have been provided as Supplementary Tables 1–4.

Clinical samples

This study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University. NPC tissue samples were obtained from the Department of Pathology, and a retrospective analysis was conducted on 27 patients diagnosed with NPC at our institution between June 2017 and December 2018. The overall survival (OS) follow-up period was defined as the date of initiation of treatment until death or the last contact date. The median follow-up period was 45.5 months (range, 13–78 months).

Animal model and experimental design

This animal study was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Guangxi Medical University Five-week-old male nude mice were maintained under standard conditions in the Experimental Animal Center of Guangxi Medical University. After random grouping, each mouse received a subcutaneous injection of 5 × 106 cells (miPEP133-overexpressing or control vector). Two groups additionally received intravenous injection of human PBMCs after tumor establishment. Tumor length (L) and width (W) were measured daily. After three weeks, animals were euthanized, and tumors were harvested for subsequent analyses.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

After dewaxing and rehydration, tissue sections were stained with antibodies against miPEP133, CD8 + T cells, and Ki67. Staining was visualized using the avidin-biotin horseradish peroxidase complex and 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB; BD Biosciences). Representative images were captured using an Olympus CX31 microscope at magnifications of 20× and 40×. Three fields of view per slide were randomly selected to calculate the mean number of positive cells and standard error (SE).

ELISA

Cell culture supernatants (48-hour NPC cell culture, 72-hour co-culture with effector cells) were collected. Concentrations of IFN-β, IL-2, IFN-γ, and Granzyme B were measured using ELISA kits (Neobioscience) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Absorbance values were measured using a BioTek 800 TS microplate reader. Data analysis included only samples exhibiting good linearity (R2 ≥ 0.95) within the quantifiable range; concentrations below detection limits were recorded as 0 pg/mL.

Western blot analysis

Total proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Solarbio, R0020) containing 1 mM PMSF (Solarbio, P0100) and protease inhibitors (Roche, 04693116001). Membrane proteins were isolated using a membrane protein extraction kit (Beyotime, P0027). Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore), and blocked with 5% skim milk. Membranes were incubated sequentially with primary antibodies overnight (4 °C) and fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies (Invitrogen). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), and cDNA was synthesized using the PrimerScript™ RT Kit (Takara). Real-time PCR reactions using SYBR Green fluorescent dye were performed on an ABI StepOne Real-Time PCR System (Life Technologies). GAPDH served as the internal control. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells (2 × 104) were seeded onto confocal slides, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, and washed with PBS. Cells were blocked for 60 min at room temperature in PBS containing 5% normal serum and 0.3% Triton X-100. Slides were incubated overnight (4°C) in a humid chamber with primary antibodies (e.g., mtDNA, CRT). Fluorescent secondary antibodies were applied (60 min, dark conditions), followed by nuclear staining with DAPI. F-actin, ROS, ER Tracker, MitoSOX and mitochondrial permeability transition pore (MPTP) was performed according to manufacturers’ instructions. Slides were mounted using anti-fluorescence-quenching mounting medium containing DAPI (Cell Signaling). Images were obtained using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope.

Flow cytometry analysis

Apoptosis was assessed using an Annexin V-APC/7-AAD apoptosis detection kit (Lianke Bio). Tissues were mechanically minced and digested (37 °C, 30 min) with HBSS solution containing collagenase D and DNase I (Sigma-Aldrich). Single-cell suspensions were prepared using 70-µm strainers (Thermo Fisher Scientific). T cells from healthy volunteer-derived PBMCs were activated (anti-CD3/CD28 antibodies, ultra-leaf, Biolegend) for 3 days or co-cultured with NPC cells for 48 h. After Fc receptor blockade, cells were stained for surface markers to assess CD8 + T cells among CD3 + T cells populations and expression of CD40, CD80, and CD86 among CD11c+ cells. Analyses were performed using a BD LSRFortessa flow cytometer (BD Biosciences), and data were analyzed with FlowJo software.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Samples were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde and 1% osmium tetroxide, dehydrated in an acetone gradient (30%–100%), and infiltrated using gradient ratios (3:1, 1:1, 1:3) of Epon-812 resin before embedding. Ultrathin Sects (60–90 nm) were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and ultrastructural analyses were performed using a JEOL JEM-1400FLASH transmission electron microscope.

Cell migration and invasion assays

In the co-culture system, 1 × 106 PBMCs were seeded in the lower chamber of a 24-well plate containing 1 mL complete RPMI-1640 medium. 5 × 104 NPC cells were seeded in the upper Transwell chamber (8 μm pore size). The uncoated chambers were used for migration assays, whereas chambers precoated with Matrigel were used for invasion assays. 200 µL serum-free RPMI-1640 medium was added to the upper chamber. After incubation at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 48 h, cells were fixed, stained, washed, and air-dried at room temperature. Migrated or invaded cells from nine randomly selected microscopic fields (200× magnification) were quantified using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

Experimental data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) from at least three independent experiments. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software) or SPSS Statistics 25 (IBM). Two-tailed, unpaired Student’s t-tests were used to compare two groups, and one-way or two-way ANOVA was used for comparisons among multiple groups. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Intrinsic expression of miPEP133 in NPC enhances anti-tumor immunity

Previous studies reported that miPEP133 has tumor-suppressive functions [13]. To investigate the role of miPEP133 in regulating anti-tumor immunity in NPC, RNA-seq first revealed that miPEP133 expression in NPC cells significantly correlated with immune-related pathways such as “positive regulation of innate immune response” and “regulation of type I interferon production” (Fig. 1a). Analysis of differentially expressed genes showed that HMGB1 was significantly upregulated in the miPEP133-overexpressing group (Fig. 1b and c). Immunohistochemical analysis of 27 NPC tissue samples indicated a positive correlation between miPEP133 expression and infiltration of CD8 + T cells in tumor tissues (r = 0.6392, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 1d, e). Furthermore, univariate Cox regression analysis revealed that miPEP133, alcohol consumption, and CD8 + T-cell were significantly associated with OS (all P < 0.05). Multivariate Cox regression analysis, adjusting for these variables, confirmed that miPEP133 remained an independent prognostic factor for OS in NPC patients (P < 0.05) (Table 1). Functional verification experiments involved overexpressing miPEP133 in C666-1 and TWO3 cells via viral infection, followed by a 48-hour co-culture with PBMCs. Flow cytometry demonstrated that tumor cells in the high miPEP133 expression NPC cells and PBMCs co-culture group exhibited a significantly increased apoptosis rate compared with the other groups, including the control, miPEP133 alone, and control co-cultured with PBMCs groups (Fig. 1f). Transwell assays further indicated that miPEP133 overexpression markedly inhibited migration and invasion of NPC cells in the presence of PBMCs (Fig. 1g, h). MiPEP133 inhibits NPC tumor growth in nude mice by enhancing anti-tumor immunity

To verify the anti-tumor immunomodulatory effects of miPEP133 in vivo, an NPC xenograft model was established in nude mice, followed by injection of human PBMCs through the tail vein, and the survival rate of human CD3 + T cells in nude mouse blood reached 19.3%(Supplementary Fig. 1a). The group co-injected with miPEP133-overexpressing TWO3 cells and human PBMCs exhibited significantly reduced tumor volumes and weights compared with the other groups (Fig. 2a–c). Further flow cytometry analysis revealed that this combined treatment group showed a markedly higher proportion of CD8 + T cells infiltrating peripheral blood, tumor tissues, and tumor-draining lymph nodes (Fig. 2d-i). Additionally, immunohistochemical staining revealed significantly lower proportions of Ki67-positive cells in this group compared with others (Fig. 2j), indicating notable inhibition of tumor cell proliferation.

These findings suggest that miPEP133 enhances anti-tumor immune responses by promoting infiltration and activation of CD8 + T cells in the tumor microenvironment of NPC nude mouse models, effectively suppressing tumor growth.

MiPEP133 in NPC cells enhances CD8 + T cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity and promotes DC maturation

T cells play a critical role in anti-tumor immune responses. To clarify the mechanism by which miPEP133 influences T cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity, a co-culture system comprising T cells derived from PBMCs and NPC cells was established. Flow cytometry showed significantly increased proportions of CD8 + T cells in the co-culture system under high miPEP133 expression conditions (Fig. 3a, b). Further analysis indicated that the co-culture group of miPEP133-overexpressing NPC cells and T cells exhibited significantly elevated expression levels of Granzyme B, interferon-γ (IFN-γ), and interleukin-2 (IL-2) in CD8 + T cells (Fig. 3c-h). ELISA assays of the co-culture supernatants confirmed significantly increased secretion of these three cytokines in the co-culture group with high miPEP133 expression compared with the control groups (Fig. 3i, j). Additionally, DC maturation status was evaluated, and flow cytometry revealed that miPEP133 significantly increased the expression of co-stimulatory molecules (CD40, CD80, and CD86) on DC surfaces (Fig. 3k-p).

MiPEP133 enhances NPC cell immunogenicity by activating ER stress

To investigate how miPEP133 influences the TIME, NPC cells were co-cultured with PBMCs. NPC cells with high miPEP133 expression recruited more PBMCs to aggregate. (Fig. 4a) and increased tumor cell apoptosis (Fig. 1f). Based on this, we hypothesized that miPEP133 induces ICD in NPC cells. To validate this hypothesis, changes in typical ICD markers under different miPEP133 expression levels were examined by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM), Western blotting, and ELISA. Results showed miPEP133 significantly increased the specific exposure of calreticulin (CRT) on tumor cell surfaces (Fig. 4b-d). Concurrently, the release of high mobility group protein B1 (HMGB1) into cell culture supernatants from the miPEP133-overexpressing group was almost twice that of controls (Fig. 4e). These findings confirm that miPEP133 effectively induces ICD in NPC cells.

Considering that excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation-induced ER stress triggers ICD [17], ROS levels were further examined using DCF-DA staining and localized using ER-Tracker. Immunofluorescence co-localization analysis demonstrated that miPEP133 overexpression markedly increased ROS production, predominantly localized within the ER region (Fig. 4f). Western blot analysis further indicated significantly elevated expression of ER stress-related proteins (ATF4, CHOP and p-EIF2α) in miPEP133-overexpressing cells (Fig. 4g, h).

These data suggest that miPEP133 effectively activates the immunogenic death pathway in NPC cells by inducing ROS production and triggering ER stress.

MiPEP133 activates the STING pathway by promoting mtDNA release, activating T cells and enhancing anti-tumor immunity

Previous studies have shown that miPEP133 localizes to mitochondria and leads to a decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential [13]. This study further examined its regulatory role in the release of mtDNA and downstream signaling pathways in NPC cells. Recent reports indicated that mtDNA significantly contributes to immune activation through the STING/TBK1/IRF3 signaling axis, promoting type I interferon responses [18]. Our experimental results demonstrated that elevated miPEP133 expression significantly increased the secretion of IFN-β in cell culture supernatants (Fig. 5a). Transmission electron microscopy revealed mitochondrial morphological alterations, characterized by obvious swelling, cristae disruption, and fragmentation, following miPEP133 overexpression (Fig. 5b). Meanwhile, MitoSOX staining showed that miPEP133 overexpression markedly elevated mtROS levels (Fig. 5c), while assessment of mPTP opening detected a pronounced decrease in fluorescence intensity relative to the control, indicating increased mitochondrial membrane permeability (Fig. 5d). Collectively, these findings indicate that miPEP133 induces mitochondrial structural damage and functional impairment.

To further evaluate the release of mtDNA into the cytoplasm. RT-qPCR analysis revealed significantly increased cytoplasmic expression levels of mitochondrial genes (D-loop1, D-loop2, and D-loop3) in miPEP133-overexpressing C666-1 cells (Fig. 5e). Meanwhile, immunofluorescence co-staining with the mitochondrial outer membrane marker TOMM20 and TFAM antibody revealed markedly increased TFAM‑positive signals in the cytoplasm of the miPEP133‑overexpressing group, suggesting mtDNA release and accumulation in the cytoplasm (Fig. 5f, g). These findings suggest that miPEP133 expression induces mitochondrial damage and facilitates mtDNA release into the cytoplasm. More importantly, Western blot demonstrated that miPEP133 overexpression markedly increased the phosphorylation of STING, TBK1, and IRF3 in C666-1 and TWO3 NPC cells (Fig. 5h–k), confirming activation of the STING pathway during the miPEP133-mediated immune response.

To determine whether the STING pathway is essential for miPEP133-mediated immune activation, a STING gene knockdown and rescue experiment was performed. Among the shRNAs tested, sh-STING3 exhibited the highest knockdown efficiency and was therefore selected for subsequent studies (Supplementary Fig. S1b-c). Western blotting confirmed significantly decreased phosphorylation of STING’s downstream effectors TBK1 and IRF3 following STING knockdown, indicating effective inhibition of STING signaling. Re-expression of STING restored the activation of the STING-TBK1-IRF3 pathway(6a). Functional assays indicated that STING knockdown effectively reversed miPEP133-induced immune activation, reflected by inhibition of CD8 + T cell activation and decreased secretion of Granzyme B, IFN-γ, and IL-2. Restoration of STING expression rescued CD8 + T-cell activation and substantially recovered IFN-γ and IL-2 production, while granzyme B secretion was partially restored(Fig. 6b, c). Additionally, a similar trend was observed for IFN-β secretion, which was reduced to a level comparable to that of the NC group following STING knockdown and increased again upon STING re-expression (Fig. 6d). In a nude mouse xenograft model, STING knockdown also diminished miPEP133-induced immune activation. Compared to the group treated with miPEP133-overexpressing NPC cells and PBMCs, tumors derived from STING-knockdown NPC cells expressing miPEP133 grew more rapidly, showing significantly increased tumor volume and weight (Fig. 6e-g). Immunohistochemical staining further indicated a higher proportion of Ki-67–positive cells in the STING-knockdown miPEP133-overexpressing NPC group (Fig. 6h), suggesting enhanced tumor proliferation.

MiPEP133 promotes IFN-β secretion by NPC cells and activates the JAK-STAT pathway in T cells, driving anti-tumor immunity

To clarify the molecular mechanisms underlying T cell activation, RNA-seq transcriptomic analysis was performed on T cells in a co-culture system. Compared with the control group, 3291 genes were differentially expressed in the miPEP133-overexpressing co-culture group, including 2074 upregulated genes and 1217 downregulated genes. Notably, several key immune-related genes, such as IFNB1, IFNG, IL-2, and STAT4, were significantly upregulated (Fig. 7a, b). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that these differentially expressed genes were significantly associated with the JAK-STAT signaling pathway (Fig. 7c). Western blot assays confirmed effective activation of the JAK-STAT pathway under miPEP133-overexpression co-culture conditions, as demonstrated by significantly increased phosphorylation of JAK1, STAT1, and STAT4 (Fig. 7d-g). Importantly, co-culture with STING-knockdown NPC cells expressing miPEP133 significantly inhibited this enhanced phosphorylation effect (Fig. 7h, i).

Discussion

Microproteins are endogenous polypeptides encoded by small open reading frames (smORFs), typically fewer than 100 amino acids in length. Although commonly found in plant systems [19–21], microproteins are relatively rarely identified in animals. Notably, the human long non-coding RNA MIR155HG (pri-miRNA-155), a critical regulator of hematopoiesis, inflammation, immunity, and tumorigenesis, encodes a 17-amino-acid micropeptide, miPEP155. This micropeptide inhibits autoimmune inflammatory reactions by regulating antigen transport and presentation processes in antigen-presenting cells [22]. MiPEP133, the microprotein studied here, is encoded by an open reading frame in the precursor of miR-34a and has been identified as a tumor suppressor in various cancers [13]. However, its role in NPC, particularly in the interaction between tumor cells and the TIME, remains unclear.

This study demonstrates, for the first time, the mechanism by which miPEP133 enhances anti-tumor immunity in NPC through the activation of CD8 + T cells. The results show that miPEP133 expression in NPC cells induces the release of mtDNA into the cytoplasm, thereby activating the STING signaling pathway and promoting a type I interferon (IFN-I) response. Concurrently, miPEP133 expression triggers the production of ROS and induces ER stress, as evidenced by the upregulation of ER stress-associated proteins, including ATF4, CHOP, and p-EIF2α. These events lead to ICD in NPC cells, which in turn facilitates DC maturation. Collectively, these processes activate the JAK1/STAT1/STAT4 signaling pathway in T cells, resulting in significantly increased CD8 + T cell infiltration and elevated expression of cytotoxic factors within the tumor microenvironment (Fig. 8). In summary, this study elucidates a critical role for miPEP133 in mediating anti-tumor immunity in NPC and highlights this microprotein as a promising therapeutic target for NPC treatment.

The molecular mechanisms by which miPEP133 enhances anti-tumor immunity via activating CD8 + T Cells in NPC

Tumor immunosuppression refers to the dysfunctional state of immune cells within the tumor microenvironment, characterized by impaired or exhausted effector immune cells and the recruitment and activation of immunosuppressive cells. This phenomenon is closely associated with NPC progression [23]. Previous studies confirmed the prognostic significance of tumor-infiltrating effector T cells in NPC [24, 25]. Through clinical sample analysis, our study demonstrated for the first time that miPEP133 expression levels positively correlate with CD8 + T-cell infiltration. Univariate Cox regression analysis indicated that alcohol consumption history, miPEP133 expression, and CD8 + T-cell levels were significantly associated with OS; further multivariate Cox regression analysis confirmed that miPEP133 serves as an independent prognostic factor. Although disease stage did not emerge as an independent prognostic factor for survival in this study, this observation may be attributed to the high concentration of enrolled cases at stages III/IV. Nevertheless, prior studies have fully established the associations between miPEP133 downregulation and advanced stage, recurrence, metastasis, and worse survival [13]. Building upon these existing findings, the current study further reveals that among patients with advanced disease, those with high miPEP133 expression exhibit higher CD8 + T cell infiltration and better survival, thereby further underscoring the immunological prognostic value of miPEP133 in the advanced patients. Importantly, in vitro and in vivo experiments showed that miPEP133 promotes CD8 + T-cell proliferation and significantly increases the secretion of key cytokines, including Granzyme B, IFN-γ, and IL-2, thereby enhancing the anti-tumor immune response.

MiPEP133, the first microprotein identified from a human tumor-suppressive miRNA, is primarily localized in mitochondria. Our findings indicate that miPEP133 facilitates mtDNA release, likely through a mechanism involving enhanced mitochondrial ROS production. This oxidative stress promotes mitochondrial membrane permeabilization and loss of membrane potential, culminating in mitochondrial damage and the consequent cytosolic release of mtDNA. Recent studies have highlighted the critical role of mtDNA in anti-tumor immunity. In breast cancer and colon adenocarcinoma models, radiation-induced cytoplasmic mtDNA stimulates IFN-I production by activating the STING signaling pathway, thus triggering effective anti-tumor immune responses [26–28]. Similarly, this research confirms that cytoplasmic mtDNA in NPC activates the endogenous STING signaling pathway and IFN-I response, further STING knockdown and rescue experiments confirmed the critical role of this pathway in miPEP133-mediated immune activation. STING depletion markedly attenuated the immune-stimulatory effects induced by miPEP133, whereas restoration of STING expression reactivated the pathway and partially rescued CD8 + T-cell activation as well as the production of granzyme B, IFN-γ, and IL-2. Collectively, this process elicits a robust antitumor immune response in NPC.

ICD, a unique form of cell death, activates anti-tumor immunity through the release of tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) and damage-associated DAMPs [29]. ROS production and ER stress play essential roles in this process [30, 31], promoting CRT translocation and DAMPs release (e.g., HMGB1). These signals induce DC maturation, ultimately enhancing anti-tumor immunity [32–34]. This study demonstrated that miPEP133 promotes ROS accumulation within the ER, induces ER stress, increases CRT exposure on the cell membrane, and facilitates HMGB1 release. These events culminate in ICD of NPC cells and inhibit tumor progression.

Previous studies have indicated that activation of the JAK-STAT signaling pathway initiates transcription of immune-related genes and mediates cytokine-driven T-cell differentiation through specific receptor kinase modules [35]. For instance, IL-12 and IL-23 activate JAK2/TYK2 through receptor binding, leading to STAT4 phosphorylation, IFN-γ production, and Th1 cell differentiation [36]. Conversely, Zhang et al. reported that Yuzhi Zhixue effectively reduced Th1 and Th2 cell proportions by decreasing phosphorylation of JAK1/JAK2/STAT3/STAT6, thereby restoring regulatory T-cell populations [37]. Notably, interferon is also crucial in activating the JAK-STAT pathway, inducing expression of numerous interferon-stimulated genes [38]. This study found that miPEP133 overexpression in NPC cells triggered a IFN-I response, leading to the release of IFN-β, which subsequently activated JAK-STAT signaling in T cells within the co-culture system. Specifically, IFN-β activates JAK1, promoting phosphorylation of STAT1 and STAT4, as validated by RNA-seq data.

Based on these findings, we propose miPEP133 as a potential therapeutic target for patients experiencing immune suppression or immune escape. MiPEP133 may exert therapeutic effects by activating immune responses in patients with NPC. Currently, the clinical translation research of miPEP133 is focusing on developing peptide-based therapeutic strategies for NPC. However, the in vivo application of exogenous peptides remains challenged by limited stability, tissue-targeting efficiency, and potential off-target toxicity. Therefore, future studies are needed to systematically evaluate the dose-response effects, pharmacokinetic properties, including peptide half-life, and toxicological profile of exogenous miPEP133 to establish a safe and effective dosing strategy. The present study provides mechanistic proof-of-concept for the biological role of miPEP133, whereas further pharmacodynamic and safety evaluations in preclinical models will be required before its potential therapeutic application can be fully assessed.

Conclusions

This study systematically clarified a novel mechanism of miPEP133 in NPC. MiPEP133 promotes T-cell-mediated anti-tumor immune responses and enhances tumor cell immunogenicity, both synergistically contributing to tumor suppression. These findings confirm the inhibitory role of miPEP133 in NPC progression and suggest its potential as a clinical therapeutic target.
