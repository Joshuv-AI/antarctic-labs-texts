Clinical cut scores for the Farsi version of the Level of Personality Functioning Scale—Self-Report (LPFS-SR)
Authors: Azad Hemmati, Amin Nazari, Saeid Komasi, Leslie C Morey, Carla Sharp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Level of Personality Functioning Scale—Self-Report (LPFS-SR) requires culturally-validated cut-off scores to ensure its utility in diverse populations. This study aimed to establish clinical thresholds and reference data for the Farsi LPFS-SR to effectively distinguish clinical personality dysfunction from nonclinical functioning in Iran.MethodsWe analyzed data from distinct clinical (n = 374 interview-diagnosed inpatients, 22% female, mean age 28.3 ± 6.9 years) and nonclinical (n = 1,119 community participants, 60% female, mean age 28.3 ± 8.1 years) samples across western Iran. Optimal cut-off scores were determined using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis.ResultsThe Global LPFS-SR demonstrated excellent capacity to discriminate the clinical from the nonclinical group. The optimal clinical cut-off score for the Global LPFS-SR was determined to be 283. Gender-specific analyses revealed high diagnostic accuracy in both groups (female AUCs = 0.88–0.91; male AUCs = 0.85–0.87).ConclusionThis study provides the preliminary empirically-derived clinical cut-off scores and comprehensive reference data for the Farsi LPFS-SR. This threshold enables reliable and efficient screening for the severity of personality pathology in Iranian clinical and research settings, supporting the localized application of the Alternative Model for Personality Disorders (AMPD).Clinical trial number Not applicable.Keywords: AMPD, Levels of personality functioning scale, LPFS-SR, Cut-off score

Introduction

The Alternative Model for Personality Disorders (AMPD), introduced in DSM-5 Section III, represents a paradigm shift from categorical symptom counting toward a dimensional conceptualization of personality pathology, with Criterion A capturing impairments in self and interpersonal functioning as the core indicator of personality disorder (PD) severity [1]. To operationalize this construct, the DSM-5 introduced the Levels of Personality Functioning Scale (LPFS), which defines severity along a continuum ranging from adaptive functioning to extreme impairment [1]. The conceptual foundations of the LPFS were informed by earlier theoretical and empirical work emphasizing self and interpersonal dysfunction as central features of PDs [2], and subsequent empirical studies demonstrated that LPFS ratings load strongly on a general factor of personality pathology severity [3]. This dimensional severity-based approach was later incorporated into the ICD-11, where personality pathology is explicitly framed in terms of levels of personality dysfunction severity rather than discrete diagnostic categories [4–6].

A growing body of research supports the validity, reliability, and clinical utility of Criterion A and LPFS-based assessments across diagnostic systems and populations [7–9]. More recent integrative reviews and empirical investigations continue to affirm Criterion A as a theoretically coherent and clinically meaningful construct, reinforcing its relevance in contemporary psychopathology research rather than positioning it as a historically limited or outdated framework [7–11].

Many earlier instruments assessing personality pathology were primarily developed for research purposes and did not fully align with the LPFS conceptualization of severity in personality functioning [7]. Following the formal introduction of the AMPD in 2013, several new assessment tools—including structured and semi-structured interviews as well as self-report instruments—were developed to specifically operationalize Criterion A in both DSM-5 and ICD-11 frameworks [7–13]. This expansion of assessment tools reflects a broader shift toward dimensional severity-based models that aim to capture core personality dysfunction more precisely than categorical symptom-based approaches.

Several self-report instruments have been developed to assess personality functioning, including the LPFS-Brief Form (LPFS-BF) [14], LPFS–Self-Report (LPFS-SR) [15], Levels of Personality Functioning Questionnaire for Adolescents [16], DSM-5 Levels of Personality Functioning Questionnaire (DLOPFQ) [17], Self and Interpersonal Functioning Scale (SIFS) [18], and the LPFS-BF 2.0 [19]. Comparative evaluations of these measures have shown that most demonstrate acceptable psychometric properties [8]. In a detailed content analysis, Waugh et al. [20] found that the DLOPFQ, LPFS-SR, and LPFS-BF 2.0 most closely align with the theoretical definitions of Criterion A.

Despite this progress, no single self-report instrument has been identified as universally optimal across all clinical and cultural contexts [20]. Zimmermann et al. [12] suggested that the LPFS-SR provides the most comprehensive self-report assessment of PD severity among currently available instruments. Supporting this conclusion, multiple studies have demonstrated the reliability and validity of the original LPFS-SR [21] and its translated versions across diverse cultural settings, including Farsi-speaking populations [22–27]. Recent validation studies published between 2023 and 2025 continue to replicate these findings, reporting stable factor structures and strong internal consistency across clinical and community samples [24–30].

At the same time, the literature is not without debate. McCabe et al. [31] raised concerns regarding discriminant validity among Criterion A measures, and Sleep et al. [32] questioned aspects of the original factor structure proposed for the LPFS-SR. In contrast, Morey et al. [7] argued that Criterion A—and the LPFS-SR in particular—can reliably distinguish PDs from other forms of psychopathology when evaluated within appropriate clinical frameworks.

Taken together, these contrasting findings highlight that Criterion A research remains an active and evolving field rather than a settled or obsolete literature. Importantly, the persistence of debate underscores the need for methodologically rigorous studies that evaluate the LPFS-SR under real-world clinical conditions, using interview-confirmed diagnoses and empirically derived decision thresholds.

Recent cross-cultural psychometric investigations have further strengthened the empirical foundation of LPFS-based instruments while simultaneously emphasizing the importance of local norms and clinical benchmarks. For example, updated normative data and psychometric support have been reported for the LPFS-SR in Poland [25] and for the LPFS-BF 2.0 in large Norwegian clinical samples [28], as well as severity benchmarks in Polish adults [29] and structural validation in Iranian samples [30]. Crucially, even the most recent multi-country and longitudinal studies caution against relying on generalized or non-clinical norms, instead advocating for empirically derived, sample-specific cut-off scores to support clinical decision-making [27–29, 33].

In the Iranian context, prior research has demonstrated the reliability and construct validity of the Farsi LPFS-SR [22, 34]; however, its clinical application has remained constrained by methodological limitations. Specifically, small clinical sample sizes, reliance on convenience sampling, and insufficient statistical power have limited the ability of prior studies to derive robust, interview-anchored cut-off scores suitable for routine clinical screening.

The present study

Dimensional approaches to diagnosis assess clinical status using empirically derived norms and cut-off scores rather than symptom counts [35], an approach that is particularly valuable when standard diagnostic interviews are unavailable, costly, or time-consuming to implement [36]. In its original development, the LPFS-SR proposed cut-off points (1 SD, 1.5 SD, and 2 SD above the mean) based on a non-clinical Western sample [15]. Subsequent studies have attempted to refine LPFS-SR norms and thresholds in Western populations [27, 33]; however, it is well established that cut-off scores derived in one cultural or clinical context may not generalize to others.

Grounded in the AMPD’s dimensional framework, the present study is based on the theoretical expectation that individuals with interview-confirmed PD diagnoses will exhibit significantly greater impairment across the global LPFS-SR score and all subdomains (Identity, Self-Direction, Empathy, and Intimacy) compared to non-clinical individuals. This expectation provides a clear conceptual link between existing theory, prior empirical findings, and the study’s aims.

A narrative review of AMPD research in Iran emphasized the clinical importance of establishing cut-off scores for both Criterion A and Criterion B measures while noting the lack of such data in the existing literature [37]. Although empirically derived cut-off scores for the Personality Inventory for DSM-5 (PID-5) have recently been established in Iranian samples [38, 39], no comparable evidence-based thresholds have been published for the Farsi LPFS-SR. While Hemmati et al. [22] examined the diagnostic utility of the LPFS-SR in Iran, the limited size of their clinical and non-clinical samples restricted the generalizability and clinical applicability of their findings.

The present study addresses this critical gap by employing a substantially larger, rigorously screened clinical sample—diagnosed via structured clinical interview—and a large non-clinical comparison group. This design enables robust statistical analyses, including Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analyses and optimal cut-point estimation using the Youden index [40, 41], while appropriately addressing missing data considerations [41].

By establishing culturally specific, empirically derived cut-off scores for the Farsi LPFS-SR, this study advances both scientific and clinical objectives. Scientifically, it contributes contemporary cross-cultural evidence testing the generalizability of Criterion A as a core severity construct beyond Western populations. Clinically, it provides practitioners with a validated, cost-effective screening tool that enhances early identification of personality pathology and supports evidence-based referral decisions in Iranian mental health settings.

Methods

Design and samples

This study investigated a clinical sample of inpatients diagnosed with personality disorders (PD) and a non-clinical sample. Participants in both groups were adults (aged 18 years and older) from western Iran who were recruited for a cross-sectional study conducted between 2021 and 2023. All participants completed the Farsi version of the LPFS-SR [34]. Data were collected in person by three graduate student researchers. Prior to analysis, preliminary data screening led to the exclusion of protocols deemed invalid (e.g., exhibiting uniform or complete non-response) or containing more than 10% missing responses, a procedure implemented to mitigate potential bias in subsequent statistical analyses [30]. The final analytical sample comprised 1,119 non-clinical participants and 374 individuals with PD who provided valid protocols. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and all participants provided voluntary informed consent. Ethical approval for this research was obtained from the ethics committee of the Kurdistan University of Medical Sciences (IR.MUK.REC.1397.037).

Non-clinical sample

Non-clinical participants were recruited through convenience sampling method from three western provinces of Iran, with a significant portion (n = 778) being students at the University of Kurdistan. All individuals who provided consent to participate reported being free from substance use disorders and had not used any psychotropic medication within the four weeks preceding data collection. Individuals with any self-reported history of personality disorder (PD) were also excluded from this sample. Of the 1,226 completed assessments, 107 were excluded due to invalid protocols identified during data screening. The final non-clinical sample comprised 1,119 individuals, ranging in age from 18 to 63 years (mean age = 28.27 years, SD = 8.14). The majority of this sample identified as female (n = 675, 60.3%) and reported being single (n = 791, 70.7%).

Clinical sample

The clinical sample comprised 411 hospitalized inpatients recruited from Farabi Psychiatric Hospital in Kermanshah (n = 167) and Quds Psychiatric Hospital in Sanandaj (n = 244). These patients had received prior personality disorder (PD) diagnoses from experienced psychiatrists based on structured diagnostic interviews adhering to the DSM-5 Section II PD criteria. Information regarding potential comorbid disorders was not available to the researchers. Following data screening, 37 protocols were identified as invalid and subsequently excluded. The final clinical inpatient sample with PD consisted of 374 individuals aged 18–47 years (mean age = 28.34 years, SD = 6.87). The majority of this sample were male (n = 292, 78.1%) and single (n = 252, 67.4%). As detailed in Table 1, the clinical inpatient sample included the following PD diagnoses: borderline (n = 311, 83.2%), antisocial (n = 25, 6.7%), paranoid (n = 12, 3.2%), obsessive-compulsive (n = 6, 1.6%), schizoid (n = 4, 1.1%), schizotypal (n = 4, 1.1%), narcissistic (n = 4, 1.1%), avoidant (n = 4, 1.1%), histrionic (n = 3, 0.8%), and dependent (n = 1, 0.3%). The high number of male and borderline cases in this sample is likely attributable to a substantial proportion of the inpatients being male offenders referred from the prison system. Measure

The level of personality functioning scale-self report (LPFS-SR)

The LPFS-SR [15] is an 80-item self-report measure that assesses dimensional aspects of personality functioning. It evaluates disturbances in self and interpersonal functioning along a global severity continuum, aligning with Criterion A of the Alternative Model for Personality Disorders (AMPD) in DSM-5 Section III. The LPFS-SR assesses personality functioning through four key areas: Identity (21 questions) and Self-Direction (16 questions) which together represent self-functioning, and Empathy (23 questions) and Intimacy (20 questions), which together represent interpersonal functioning. Respondents indicate their level of agreement with each of the 80 statements using a four-point scale ranging from “Totally False” to “Very True.” The initial research validating the LPFS-SR [15] showed strong internal consistency for both the overall score (α = 0.96) and the component scores (Identity: α = 0.89, Self-Direction: α = 0.88, Empathy: α = 0.82, Intimacy: α = 0.88). Subsequent research has supported the reliability and validity of the original LPFS-SR [21] as well as its translated versions [24–27].

Hemmati et al. [22] validated the Farsi translation of the LPFS-SR in both Iranian clinical and non-clinical samples. Their findings indicated acceptable validity of the LPFS-SR within a non-Western cultural context such as Iran. While that study, utilizing Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, demonstrated significant discrimination by the LPFS-SR between college student and personality disorder (PD)-diagnosed patient samples, it focused solely on the total score and employed a limited sample size. Nazari et al. [23] also reported in another Iranian study that all components of the LPFS-SR and the Structured Interview of Personality Organization-Revised (STIPO-R) – an instrument of the object-relations model – were positively and significantly intercorrelated. The current study used the algorithm provided by Morey [15] for weighting item scores (weights ranging from − 0.5 for Level 0 “little or no impairment” items to + 3.5 for Level 4 “extreme impairment” items). None of the items are reverse-coded, although some are weighted negatively and thus deduct from score totals (see instructions above). The weights (-0.5, 0.5, 1.5, 2.5, or 3.5) are applied item by item. Our study showed strong internal consistency for the overall scale (α = 0.97) and the subscales (Identity: α = 0.92, Self-Direction: α = 0.90, Empathy: α = 0.86, Intimacy: α = 0.91).

Data analysis

Prior to analyzing the research objectives, we assessed the normality of the data. The skewness and kurtosis values for both global personality functioning and its dimensions indicated that nearly all measures exhibit a normal distribution (i.e., falling between − 1 and + 1). As a result, we employed parametric statistics for all subsequent analyses. To distinguish patients diagnosed with PD from non-clinical samples, we established cut points for the LPFS-SR using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses. We reported the Area under the ROC Curve (AUC) alongside a 95% confidence interval (CI), as well as sensitivity and specificity values. Youden’s Index was utilized to determine the cut points that provide the highest clinical utility. This index ranges from − 1.0 to + 1.0, with higher values indicating greater diagnostic effectiveness [40]. The Youden Index serves as a useful summary metric for the ROC curve, assessing the performance of a diagnostic marker and facilitating the selection of an optimal threshold for that marker [41]. Ultimately, we evaluated the effectiveness of the cutoff scores for each measure in terms of the true positive rate (TPR) and true negative rate (TNR). The TPR represents the likelihood that a genuinely positive case will yield a positive test result, whereas the TNR denotes the likelihood that a genuinely negative case will produce a negative test result [38].

In the next step, we computed the mean and standard deviation (SD) of the LPFS-SR within the non-clinical sample, as well as the values at 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 SD above the mean. This approach was based on practical guidelines for determining PD cutoff points in both U.S [40]. and Iranian populations [36]. We calculated independent t and Cohen’s d values to evaluate effect sizes, with comparisons made through p-values. Due to the unequal gender distribution among patients with PD and in the non-clinical sample, as well as the differing mean scores between male and female participants, all analyses were conducted on both the overall sample and by sex groups. Finally, we calculated an agreement coefficient for the cut-off scores related to global personality functioning and the dimensions of the LPFS-SR across the sex groups, utilizing the intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC). A significance level of ≤ 0.05 was established for the two-tailed tests, and all analyses were executed using SPSS-27 software.

Results

Table 2 presents the cutoff scores for the global personality functioning and its dimensions as measured by the LPFS-SR in the overall sample. The data indicate that both global personality functioning and the corresponding dimensions effectively differentiate patients with PD from those in a non-clinical group. Specifically, the AUC value for global functioning was recorded at 0.90 (CI = 0.88 to 0.92), with a significant cutoff point established at 283 (p < 0.001). Analysis of the self and interpersonal functioning dimensions yielded AUC values between 0.89 and 0.90 (all p < 0.001), with cutoff scores of 151 and 137, respectively. The AUC values for the four specific components of the LPFS-SR ranged from 0.88 to 0.90, and the associated cutoff points varied from 45 to 86 (all p < 0.001). The Youden index for all measures, with values ranging from 0.61 to 0.67, indicated that these cutoff scores effectively differentiate patients with PD from the non-patient population. Notably, the Youden index highlighted global personality functioning as the most effective metric for identifying individuals with PD. In the full sample, the TPR for diagnosing patients ranged from 79% to 90%, while the TNR varied from 73% to 85%. For the female subgroup, the TPR for diagnosing patients with PD was between 80% and 86%, and the TNR was between 76% and 87%. In the male subgroup, the TPR ranged from 79% to 96%, while the TNR fell between 52% and 79%.

In the female group, the mean scores for all LPFS-SR scales were found to be lower in the non-clinical women compared to the female patients with PD, with Cohen’s d values ranging from − 1.61 to -1.81 (all p < 0.001). Discussion

This study aimed to establish clinical cutoff scores and reference data for the Farsi version of the Level of Personality Functioning Scale—Self-Report (LPFS-SR) in an Iranian population, encompassing both clinical and nonclinical samples. The results indicate that the LPFS-SR effectively distinguishes between clinical and nonclinical groups based on global dysfunction and its subscales of self- and interpersonal functioning. Specifically, ROC analyses revealed high discriminative accuracy across all scales, with global dysfunction yielding an Area Under the Curve (AUC) of 0.90 in the full sample, and similarly robust results across gender-specific analyses (AUCs ranging from 0.86 to 0.91). These findings suggest that the LPFS-SR can serve as a reliable screening tool to identify individuals at risk for personality pathology in Iranian clinical settings.

The identified preliminary cutoff score of approximately 283 for global dysfunction provides clinicians with an evidence-based threshold for screening significant personality pathology in Iranian populations. These results closely align with prior international findings [7, 12], further supporting the global validity of LPFS-SR as a measure for assessing personality disorder severity across diverse cultural contexts. The inclusion of diverse subgroups such as community residents, college students, and clinical inpatients enhances the robustness and external validity of our results, making them more applicable for general clinical practice.

Moreover, the subscale analyses identified critical dimensions relevant for clinical practice. Among these, interpersonal functioning (with an AUC of 0.90) and particularly, intimacy (AUC 0.90–0.91), emerged as strong discriminators of clinical status, especially in female patients. This finding aligns with existing literature emphasizing interpersonal dysfunction as central to the manifestation and progression of personality pathology, particularly in disorders such as borderline and antisocial personality disorders [3, 18, 21]. Given that approximately 83% of our clinical sample was diagnosed with borderline personality disorder, the prominence of interpersonal functioning dimensions, particularly intimacy, is clinically meaningful and consistent with international research findings [11]. Clinicians should, therefore, pay particular attention to intimacy-related issues when assessing female patients for personality disorders, considering their high sensitivity to clinical dysfunction.

Gender-specific analyses revealed noteworthy differences, particularly concerning sensitivity and specificity values. While the female subgroup displayed consistently high diagnostic accuracy, reflected by higher Youden’s indices (ranging from 0.62 to 0.68), the male subgroup showed somewhat lower indices (ranging from 0.55 to 0.61). Similar observations have been noted in previous studies investigating personality assessment instruments, which suggest that males and females may express personality pathology differently [36, 38]. Thus, future research and clinical practice should emphasize nuanced, gender-informed interpretations of LPFS-SR scores to maximize diagnostic accuracy and tailored intervention strategies.

Furthermore, the descriptive analyses highlighted significant severity differences between clinical and nonclinical groups. Patients diagnosed with personality disorders exhibited mean LPFS-SR scores consistently exceeding 1.5 standard deviations above those of nonclinical groups, underscoring the instrument’s ability to differentiate pathological from reference personality traits effectively. These severity distinctions further reinforce the clinical utility of dimensional models such as the LPFS-SR, highlighting their advantage in capturing subtle but clinically meaningful variations in personality functioning. Such dimensional measures offer clinicians a more precise and nuanced framework for assessing personality pathology compared to traditional categorical diagnostic methods [6, 35], facilitating targeted treatment planning and monitoring of therapeutic outcomes.

Overall, these findings provide robust support for using LPFS-SR as a practical, reliable, and culturally valid measure for identifying and assessing personality pathology severity within Iranian clinical and research contexts. These robust findings from the current study align strongly with the emerging body of contemporary international research supporting the unidimensional structure and clinical utility of the Level of Personality Functioning construct. Our results, which demonstrate excellent discriminative capacity in the Iranian sample, are congruent with the high diagnostic accuracies reported in recent validation studies, such as the Polish LPFS-SR adaptation [25] and the establishment of severity benchmarks in the Polish population [29]. Furthermore, the recent cross-cultural study that included Iranian data found good measurement invariance for the LPFS-BF 2.0 [30], lending significant external validation to the premise that the underlying construct of personality functioning is captured similarly in the Iranian context as it is globally. Our work directly builds upon this foundation by providing the missing piece: empirically optimized clinical cut-offs for the full LPFS-SR to effectively leverage this validated construct in Iranian clinical practice.

While mean differences in LPFS-SR scores between men and women in both clinical and nonclinical groups appeared minimal, and overall diagnostic accuracy remained high, a detailed examination of the sex-specific analyses revealed a subtle but critical reduction in diagnostic accuracy among the male subsample (e.g., AUC = 0.87 vs. AUC = 0.90). This finding precludes any absolute conclusion about the absence of gender differences in the clinical utility of the LPFS-SR.

This observed variance in diagnostic performance warrants deeper interpretation. It may reflect gender-based variations in the symptomatic manifestation of Criterion A impairments, a phenomenon consistently debated in the literature. For instance, personality pathology in men is sometimes theorized to be more commonly expressed through externalizing behaviors (e.g., antagonism, disinhibition), which may not be fully or accurately captured by a self-report instrument primarily sensitive to internalizing deficits (e.g., identity and intimacy struggles) as assessed by the LPFS-SR. Alternatively, it could be influenced by response bias, where men and women differ in their propensity to accurately self-report levels of severe personality dysfunction. The clinical implication is that while the general cut-off is effective, clinicians should exercise increased caution and supplement the LPFS-SR score with comprehensive diagnostic interview data when screening male patients whose scores fall near the established threshold, due to the slightly higher potential for misclassification in this group.

While, former studies on the AMPD have found that the self-functioning domains (especially identity) tend to be the strongest single predictors of overall PD severity, in our finding—that intimacy emerged as the strongest predictor for the limited sample of women—is unique and demands critical interpretation. This finding is restricted to the small subsample of women in our clinical group (n = 82), who are predominantly drawn from a forensic/inpatient setting. Women who end up in this highly restrictive and severe clinical-forensic setting likely represent the extreme end of the PD spectrum. For these individuals, the most immediate and severe manifestations of their pathology may be the breakdown of relationships, profound lack of trust, relational exploitation, and difficulties with affiliation—all key components of the Intimacy domain. While identity pathology may be the underlying mechanism, the interpersonal failure (intimacy and empathy) is often the most behaviorally salient feature that leads to severe social impairment or institutionalization. The finding is contextualized within the Iranian culture, where family and interpersonal cohesion play a dominant role in defining social roles, particularly for women. It is plausible that a woman’s sense of self and social standing is so intricately tied to her Intimacy functioning (i.e., her success in forming and maintaining relationships) that profound failures in this domain are disproportionately linked to severe overall dysfunction, more so than in less collectivistic or relational-focused cultures.

The derived cut-off scores should be interpreted with caution due to the specific characteristics of our samples, which impact the generalizability of the findings. The nonclinical group was largely comprised of university students, a demographic known to exhibit higher levels of education and potentially lower overall psychological distress compared to the general adult population. This specificity suggests the nonclinical norms and, consequently, the derived cut-off thresholds may be more representative of a younger, educated segment of the Iranian population. Furthermore, the clinical sample was neither balanced by sex (being predominantly male) nor diagnostic category, as Borderline Personality Disorder (BPD) patients constituted the largest single diagnostic subgroup. Given that Criterion A impairments (LPFS-SR scores) are hypothesized to be most severe in BPD compared to other disorders, the ROC analysis optimally separates this specific, high-severity clinical group from non-clinical functioning. Therefore, while the cut-offs are robust for BPD screening, their application to the full range of other personality disorder diagnoses (e.g., those with lower expected severity of Criterion A impairment) or to sex-matched general population samples requires further investigation.

Despite the aforementioned, grounding the gender-specific differences within the cultural context of the Iranian population is a crucial element. This deduction is vital for explaining our unique finding and for maximizing the generalizability and comparative value of our study. In the Iranian context, familial and social relationships are central to a woman’s identity and perceived competence. Social roles often place a heavy emphasis on maintaining a cohesive, successful family unit and harmonious social network. Failures in the Intimacy domain (difficulty maintaining close, mutually satisfying relationships, lack of empathy for others) may therefore be more overtly dysfunctional and distressing for women, leading to higher reported severity on these items and explaining why this domain was a strong predictor of clinical status for our female sample. In contrast, traditional cultural expectations often link a man’s identity to Self-direction, particularly in terms of agency, responsibility, and achievement (e.g., employment, providing for the family, exhibiting self-control). Dysfunction in Self-direction (e.g., rigid goal setting, difficulty achieving goals, impulsive behavior) is arguably more salient for the clinical referral and institutionalization of men, especially within the forensic setting that dominated our male clinical sample. The cultural stigma surrounding certain forms of psychiatric illness and personality dysfunction can influence how symptoms are reported. Self-stigma may lead to the internalizing or denial of Identity issues, while the more public failures of Interpersonal (Intimacy/Empathy) or Behavioral (Self-direction) domains might be the visible reasons for clinical presentation.

Strengths, limitations, and future directions

This study presents several notable strengths. Firstly, the robust sample size, comprising distinct clinical and non-clinical subgroups, facilitated the generation of detailed reference data and highly reliable preliminary cutoff scores. Secondly, the application of rigorous statistical analyses, including Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves and Youden’s Index, enhances the accuracy and clinical applicability of the derived preliminary cutoff points. Even though, our separate analyses conducted by gender significantly enhance the understanding of personality functioning scale performance across demographic groups within the Iranian cultural context, this study does not support the application of gender-specific cut-off scores. This is primarily due to the similarity of cut-off scores across genders and the predominantly male composition of the clinical forensic sample diagnosed with BPD. This sample composition is atypical with respect to gender, given that most studies report a higher prevalence of BPD in women. Furthermore, certain traditional personality disorders, such as antisocial PD, exhibit marked gender differences in prevalence, and attempts to equate cut-off scores to yield similar prevalence rates may obscure rather than clarify the natural distribution of these features.

In addition to the above, several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, “A significant limitation of this study is the compositional heterogeneity and imbalance between the clinical and nonclinical groups. The reliance on a nonclinical sample largely composed of students limits the generalizability of the derived reference data and cut-off scores to the broader, non-student adult Iranian population, potentially introducing a selection bias. More critically, the clinical sample’s disproportionate representation of male patients and individuals diagnosed with Borderline Personality Disorder (BPD) restricts the scope of our conclusions. As the cut-off scores were optimized using a sample where BPD severity was dominant, they are optimally tuned for screening BPD and may not accurately reflect the severity threshold required for reliably identifying other personality disorder categories, such as those characterized by less severe LPFS impairment (e.g., Cluster A disorders). Future studies are therefore necessary to validate these cut-offs across a more balanced range of personality disorder diagnoses and a demographically representative non-clinical sample. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences; longitudinal studies are necessary to ascertain the temporal stability and predictive validity of the identified cut-off scores. Third, the clinical sample, predominantly composed of individuals diagnosed with BPD, potentially limits the generalizability of the findings to other personality disorder categories. Fourth, while the study included multiple psychiatric hospitals in Western Iran, the findings may not be generalizable to other regions or cultural contexts within the country. Fifth, the non-clinical sample is heavily skewed toward university students, and the clinical sample is heavily skewed toward forensic inpatients. Therefore, the preliminary reference data should be interpreted cautiously and may be most applicable to young, educated adults. Moreover, a true general population sample would contain undiagnosed, subclinical, or mild PD cases. By excluding individuals with any self-reported history of PD, the non-clinical reference group is indeed “healthier” than the population average. Also, because the clinical group is predominantly from a forensic setting, the derived clinical cut-off scores may be specifically optimized for detecting severe, treatment-resistant, or forensic-relevant personality dysfunction and may be less generalizable to less severe outpatient clinical settings. Sixth, the AMPD and the LPFS are fundamentally dimensional constructs. Consequently, while the application of cut-off scores may serve pragmatic functions in screening or to inform treatment recommendations, these thresholds should not be interpreted as definitive indicators of the presence or absence of a personality disorder. Rather, personality functioning exists on a continuum and is a matter of degree. Seventh, the unknown status of psychiatric comorbidities is a limitation that may restrict the generalizability of the cut-off scores, particularly if the severity observed in our sample is partly a function of high comorbidity rates. Eighth, there is a critical need to contextualize our findings geographically, as our data were collected primarily from the Western provinces of Iran. Our sample primarily reflects the socio-cultural and potentially clinical presentation characteristics of populations in Western Iran (e.g., provinces of Kurdistan, Kermanshah, etc.). The Western provinces are often distinct from the more centrally-located in terms of ethnic composition, socio-economic factors, and potentially access to specialized mental healthcare. This cultural and structural variability means that the preliminary reference data may not directly map onto the general population of all of Iran.

Considering the mentioned limitations, future research should prioritize the investigation of longitudinal predictive validity and include a broader spectrum of personality disorder diagnostic categories. Evaluating the performance of the LPFS-SR across diverse cultural and geographical contexts is also crucial. Future research should incorporate more diverse clinical populations to enhance the representativeness of the cut-off scores. Additionally, the integration of self-report assessments with structured clinical interviews could further bolster the reliability and clinical utility of the scale. Establishing cut-offs using a criterion measure, particularly an interview-based assessment of personality functioning, is also suggested. Besides, the geographical limitation strongly motivate the need for future multi-site studies across Iran to validate these preliminary cut-offs and establish truly national reference data. In addition, future validation studies employing diverse clinical samples and longitudinal designs will be crucial to solidify and extend the clinical relevance of these findings.

Conclusions

This study yields clinically valuable reference data and valid preliminary cutoff scores for the Farsi adaptation of the Level of Personality Functioning Scale – Self-Report (LPFS-SR), representing a significant contribution to personality disorder assessment practices within Iran. A global dysfunction cutoff score of approximately 283 effectively discriminates individuals with clinically significant personality pathology. Furthermore, specific subscale cutoff scores offer enhanced granularity for assessment and diagnostic purposes. While the findings demonstrate high diagnostic accuracy across genders, nuanced interpretation, particularly for male populations, is advisable. Consequently, clinicians and researchers can confidently utilize these benchmarks in screening, clinical assessment, and therapeutic planning. The established cut-off scores primarily confirm the LPFS-SR’s ability to distinguish gross personality psychopathology (i.e., severe, diagnosed, and often treatment-resistant PD) from a healthy non-clinical population. The 1.5 SD threshold should be seen as a preliminary, high-threshold indicator of clinically significant severity, appropriate for screening high-risk populations or confirming the need for a comprehensive diagnostic assessment.
