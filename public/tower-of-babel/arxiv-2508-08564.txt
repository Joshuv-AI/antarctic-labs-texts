Abstract
We propose a novel kernel-based two-sample test that leverages the spectral decomposition of the maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) statistic to identify and utilize well-estimated directional components in reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS). Our approach is motivated by the observation that the estimation quality of these components varies significantly, with leading eigen-directions being more reliably estimated in finite samples. By focusing on these directions and aggregating information across multiple kernels, the proposed test achieves higher power and improved robustness, especially in high-dimensional and unbalanced sample settings. We further develop a computationally efficient multiplier bootstrap procedure for approximating critical values, which is theoretically justified and significantly faster than permutation-based alternatives. Extensive simulations and empirical studies on microarray datasets demonstrate that our method maintains the nominal Type I error rate and delivers superior power compared to other existing MMD-based tests.



Keywords:
Kernel Two-Sample Test, Multiplier Bootstrap, Spectral Decomposition

1
Introduction

We introduce a new kernel-based two-sample test that leverages the spectral decomposition of the mean embedding difference between two distributions. Our approach focuses on identifying and utilizing well-estimated directional components, which leads to more powerful and robust inference compared to existing methods. By emphasizing components with desirable statistical properties, our test achieves improved finite-sample performance and computational efficiency, particularly when combined with a multiplier bootstrap procedure for approximating critical values.

Given two probability distributions
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
defined on a separable metric space
\mathcal{X}
, the two-sample testing problem seeks to determine whether these distributions are identical:

H_{0}:\mathbb{P}=\mathbb{Q}\quad\text{vs.}\quad H_{1}:\mathbb{P}\neq\mathbb{Q},

based on independent random samples
\mathcal{X}_{m}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
and
\mathcal{Y}_{n}=\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
drawn from
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
, respectively. Throughout this paper, we assume that the sample proportion
\hat{p}=m/N
converges to
p\in(0,1)
as the total sample size
N=m+n
tends to infinity.

For univariate data, several classical nonparametric tests are widely used, such as the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test
[
Smirnov 1948
]
, the Wald–Wolfowitz runs test
[
Wald and Wolfowitz 1940
]
, the rank-sum test
[
Mann and Whitney 1947
]
, and the Cramér–von Mises test
[
Anderson 1962
]
. Extensions of these methods to the multivariate setting date back to the 1960s, see, e.g.,
Weiss 1960
and
Bickel 1969
. Recently, kernel-based approaches have become increasingly popular for two-sample testing, owing to their flexibility and strong power in detecting a wide range of distributional differences across various domains, see, e.g.,
Gretton et al. 2009
,
Gretton et al. 2012a
,
Gretton et al. 2012b
,
Sejdinovic et al. 2013
,
Ramdas et al. 2015
,
Shekhar et al. 2022
,
Song and Chen 2024
, and
Chatterjee and Bhattacharya 2025
.

Kernel-based methods fundamentally rely on embedding probability distributions into a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)
\mathcal{H}_{k}
using a characteristic kernel
k
. These approaches enable the comparison of distributions by examining their mean embeddings in the RKHS. Formally, each distribution is represented by its mean element:
\mathbb{P}\mapsto\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbb{P}}=\mathbb{E}_{X}(k(X,\cdot))
and
\mathbb{Q}\mapsto\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbb{Q}}=\mathbb{E}_{Y}(k(Y,\cdot))
,
where
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is a characteristic reproducing kernel. Two distributions are identical if and only if their mean embeddings coincide in the RKHS. Thus, the two-sample testing problem is equivalent to
H_{0}:\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbb{P}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbb{Q}}=\boldsymbol{0}
vs.
H_{1}:\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbb{P}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbb{Q}}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
,
where
\boldsymbol{0}\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
denotes the zero element in the RKHS. The well-known maximum mean discrepancy (MMD)
[
Gretton et al. 2012a
]
is defined as the squared norm of the difference between the mean embeddings

\text{MMD}^{2}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})=\|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbb{P}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbb{Q}}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}^{2}.

The key innovation of this paper is to exploit the spectral decomposition of the mean embedding difference
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbb{P}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbb{Q}}
to identify and utilize components with desirable statistical properties. To begin with, let us consider the mixture distribution
\rho=p\mathbb{P}+(1-p)\mathbb{Q}
, and let
Z
be a random variable distributed according to
\rho
. We define the integral operator
L_{k}
on
L^{2}(\rho)
as follows:

(L_{k}f)(z)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}k(z,z^{\prime})f(z^{\prime})\,d\rho(z^{\prime}),\quad\forall f\in L^{2}(\rho),

where
L^{2}(\rho)
is the space of square-integrable functions with respect to
\rho
. The operator
L_{k}
is self-adjoint and compact, and admits the spectral decomposition
L_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}\,\phi_{j}\otimes\phi_{j}
,
where
\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
are the eigenvalues and
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
are the corresponding orthonormal eigenfunctions in
L^{2}(\rho)
, that is,
L_{k}\phi_{j}=\lambda_{j}\phi_{j}
and
\langle\phi_{i},\phi_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)}=\delta_{ij}
, with
\delta_{ij}
the Kronecker delta. The tensor product
\boldsymbol{a}\otimes\boldsymbol{b}
between Hilbert spaces
H
and
F
is defined as the linear operator
H\to F
given by
(\boldsymbol{a}\otimes\boldsymbol{b})\boldsymbol{h}=\langle\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{h}\rangle_{H}\boldsymbol{a}
.

By Mercer’s theorem, the kernel
k
admits the expansion
k(z,z^{\prime})=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}\,\phi_{j}(z)\,\phi_{j}(z^{\prime})
. Using this, the difference of mean embeddings can be written as

\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbb{P}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbb{Q}}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}\,d_{j}\,\phi_{j},

where
d_{j}:=\mathbb{E}(\phi_{j}(X))-\mathbb{E}(\phi_{j}(Y))=\langle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbb{P}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbb{Q}},\phi_{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
for
j\geq 1
are referred to as the directional components. Consequently, the MMD can be expressed as

\text{MMD}^{2}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})=\sum_{i,j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}d_{i}d_{j}\langle\phi_{i},\phi_{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}d_{j}^{2},

where we have used the fact that
\langle\phi_{i},\phi_{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}=\lambda_{i}^{-1/2}\lambda_{j}^{-1/2}\delta_{ij}
. This shows that the null hypothesis
H_{0}
and the alternative hypothesis
H_{1}
can be equivalently reformulated as

H_{0}:d_{j}=0\quad\text{for all }j\quad\text{vs.}\quad H_{1}:d_{j}\neq 0\text{ for some }j.

Furthermore, as discussed in Remark
4
, the biased estimator of the MMD introduced by
Gretton et al. 2012a
admits a spectral decomposition of the form:

\widehat{\text{MMD}}^{2}_{b}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sigma_{j}^{2}\hat{d}_{j}^{2},

where
\sigma_{j}^{2}
is the
j
-th eigenvalue of the Gram matrix
\boldsymbol{K}
[see Equation (
1
)], and
\hat{d}_{j}
denotes the empirical directional component as defined in Equation (
2
).

Building on the concept of directional components, we introduce a new two-sample test statistic. The biased version of this statistic is defined as

\hat{T}_{b}=\sum_{c=1}^{C}\hat{T}_{b}^{(c)},\text{ where }\hat{T}_{b}^{(c)}=\sum_{j=1}^{J(N)}(\sigma_{j}^{(c)})^{2}\left(\hat{d}_{j}^{(c)}\right)^{2},

with the superscript
(c)
denoting quantities associated with the
c
-th kernel
k^{(c)}
. Here,
J(N)
specifies the number of directional components included, and will go to infinity as
N
goes to infinity. For clarity of exposition, we have discussed the biased version of the test statistic here. In the following sections, however, we will primarily focus on the unbiased version, as defined in Equation (
8
).

A central motivation for focusing on the directional components is that their estimation quality varies substantially across directions, directly impacting the finite-sample performance of the test. Poorly estimated components can introduce considerable noise, reducing both the power and reliability of the test statistic. To rigorously assess the estimation quality of each directional component, we leverage perturbation theory from functional analysis, which yields non-asymptotic error bounds between the population and empirical covariance operators. These results highlight the factors influencing the accuracy in each direction and reveal that components associated with smaller eigenvalues typically have larger estimation errors. As a result, constructing test statistics that emphasize well-estimated components can lead to more powerful and robust inference. However, a single kernel often yields only a limited number of accurately estimated directions, motivating the use of multiple kernels to capture a broader set of well-estimated components and further improve overall performance.

In addition, analyzing directional components provides a transparent characterization of the asymptotic null distribution of the test statistic, which is crucial for developing an efficient multiplier bootstrap procedure for approximating critical values. Specifically, we show that under the null hypothesis, the empirical directional components — when scaled by
\sqrt{N}
— converge to a scaled standard normal distribution. This transparent asymptotic distribution enables the use of a multiplier bootstrap to approximate the null distribution of the test statistic, offering substantial computational efficiency over permutation-based methods while maintaining statistical validity.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the estimation of directional components and establishes their asymptotic properties under the null hypothesis, the fixed alternative hypothesis, and a sequence of local alternatives. Section
3
derives non-asymptotic error bounds for estimating directional components, emphasizing how estimation quality varies across directions. Section
4
details the construction of the proposed test statistic and presents its asymptotic properties. In Section
5
, we develop a multiplier bootstrap procedure leveraging the asymptotic distribution of the directional components. Section
6
reports simulation studies that assess the finite-sample performance of the proposed test and the multiplier bootstrap procedure under both balanced and unbalanced settings. We also provide an empirical application to demonstrate the usefulness of our method. Finally, Section
7
concludes.

Throughout the paper, we make the following assumption.

Assumption 1
.

The kernel
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies: (i)
\mathbb{E}_{X}(k(X,X)^{1/2})<\infty
and
\mathbb{E}_{Y}(k(Y,Y)^{1/2})<\infty
, (ii)
k
is characteristic, i.e., the mean embeddings are one-to-one functions.

These conditions, see, e.g., Lemma 3 of
Gretton et al. 2012a
, ensure that
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbb{P}},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbb{Q}}\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
and the MMD defines a metric on the space of probability distributions.

.

2
Directional Components: Estimation and Asymptotic Properties

2.1
Estimation of Directional Components

Following
Rosasco et al. 2010
, we define the restriction operator
\boldsymbol{G}_{N}:\mathcal{H}_{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
by

\boldsymbol{G}_{N}f=\left(f(z_{1}),\ldots,f(z_{N})\right)^{\top}=\left(f(x_{1}),\ldots,f(x_{m}),f(y_{1}),\ldots,f(y_{n})\right)^{\top},\,\,\,\forall f\in\mathcal{H}_{k},

where
z_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,N
denote the pooled sample points from both
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
, i.e., realizations from the mixture distribution
\rho
. Its adjoint operator
\boldsymbol{G}_{N}^{\top}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathcal{H}_{k}
is given by

\boldsymbol{G}_{N}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}k(z_{i},\cdot)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}k(x_{i},\cdot)+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{m+i}k(y_{i},\cdot),

where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m},\alpha_{m+1},\ldots,\alpha_{N})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a vector of coefficients. The adjoint property ensures
\langle\boldsymbol{G}_{N}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha},f\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}=\langle\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{G}_{N}f\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}=N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}\langle k(z_{i},\cdot),f\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
, where
\mathbb{R}^{N}
is equipped with the inner product scaled by
1/N
.

The empirical covariance operator can be written as

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{G}_{N}^{\top}\boldsymbol{G}_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}k(z_{i},\cdot)\otimes k(z_{i},\cdot)=\frac{\hat{p}}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}k(x_{i},\cdot)\otimes k(x_{i},\cdot)+\frac{1-\hat{p}}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}k(y_{j},\cdot)\otimes k(y_{j},\cdot),

where
\hat{p}=m/N
. The associated Gram matrix is

\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{G}_{N}\boldsymbol{G}_{N}^{\top}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{K_{XX}}&\boldsymbol{K_{XY}}\\
\boldsymbol{K_{YX}}&\boldsymbol{K_{YY}}\end{pmatrix},

(1)

with the associated blocks defined by

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{K_{XX}})_{ij}=\frac{1}{N}k(x_{i},x_{j}),\quad(\boldsymbol{K_{YY}})_{ij}=\frac{1}{N}k(y_{i},y_{j}),

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{K_{XY}})_{ij}=\frac{1}{N}k(x_{i},y_{j}),\quad\boldsymbol{K_{YX}}=\boldsymbol{K_{XY}}^{\top}.

The singular value decomposition (SVD) of
\boldsymbol{G}_{N}
[see Proposition 9 in
Rosasco et al. 2010
] yields

\boldsymbol{G}_{N}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sigma_{j}\sqrt{N}\boldsymbol{U}_{j}\otimes\hat{e}_{j},

where
\{\boldsymbol{U}_{j},\sigma_{j}^{2}\}_{j=1}^{N}
are the eigenpairs of
\boldsymbol{K}
,

\displaystyle\hat{e}_{j}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{j}}\sqrt{N}\boldsymbol{G}_{N}^{\top}\boldsymbol{U}_{j},\text{ and }\sqrt{N}\boldsymbol{U}_{j}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{j}}\boldsymbol{G}_{N}\hat{e}_{j}.

Let
\hat{\phi}_{j}=\sigma_{j}^{-1}\hat{e}_{j}=\sigma_{j}^{-2}\sqrt{N}\boldsymbol{G}_{N}^{\top}\boldsymbol{U}_{j}
be the Nyström approximation of the eigenfunction
\phi_{i}

[
Williams and Seeger 2000
]
, and note that
\left(\hat{\phi}_{j}(x_{1}),\ldots,\hat{\phi}_{j}(x_{m}),\hat{\phi}_{j}(y_{1}),\ldots,\hat{\phi}_{j}(y_{n})\right)^{\top}=\boldsymbol{G}_{N}\hat{\phi}_{j}=\sqrt{N}\boldsymbol{U}_{j}
. A natural estimator for the
j
-th directional component is

\hat{d}_{j}=\langle\hat{\mu}_{\mathbb{P}}-\hat{\mu}_{\mathbb{Q}},\hat{\phi}_{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\hat{\phi}_{j}(x_{\alpha})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\beta=1}^{n}\hat{\phi}_{j}(y_{\beta})=\langle\boldsymbol{l},\sqrt{N}\boldsymbol{U}_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{N}},

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{l}=(N/m,\ldots,N/m,-N/n,\ldots,-N/n)^{\top}
, with the first
m
entries equal to
N/m
and the last
n
entries equal to
-N/n
.

This empirical estimator admits the following decomposition:

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{l},\sqrt{N}\boldsymbol{U}_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}

\displaystyle=\langle\boldsymbol{l},\boldsymbol{G}_{N}\hat{\phi}_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}=\langle\boldsymbol{l},\boldsymbol{G}_{N}\phi_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}+\langle\boldsymbol{l},\boldsymbol{G}_{N}(\hat{\phi}_{j}-\phi_{j})\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}

\displaystyle=\langle\hat{\mu}_{\mathbb{P}}-\hat{\mu}_{\mathbb{Q}},\phi_{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}+(R_{X,j,m}-R_{Y,j,n}),

where

\displaystyle R_{X,j,m}=\langle\boldsymbol{l}_{m},\boldsymbol{G}_{m}(\hat{\phi}_{j}-\phi_{j})\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\hat{\phi}_{j}-\phi_{j})(x_{i}),

\displaystyle R_{Y,j,n}=\langle\boldsymbol{l}_{n},\boldsymbol{G}_{n}(\hat{\phi}_{j}-\phi_{j})\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat{\phi}_{j}-\phi_{j})(y_{i})

capture the Nyström approximation error for the samples from
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
, respectively. Here,
\boldsymbol{G}_{m}:\mathcal{H}_{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\boldsymbol{G}_{n}:\mathcal{H}_{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
denote the restriction operators for the respective samples, and
\boldsymbol{l}_{m}=(N/m,\ldots,N/m)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\boldsymbol{l}_{n}=(N/n,\ldots,N/n)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are the corresponding weight vectors. The difference
(R_{X,j,m}-R_{Y,j,n})
quantifies the total approximation error introduced by the Nyström method in estimating the eigenfunctions.

The following two lemmas formalize the properties of this approximation error. The first lemma provides a first-order perturbation expansion for
\hat{\phi}_{j}-\phi_{j}
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
L_{N}
be the empirical counterpart of the integral operator
L_{k}
, i.e.,

(L_{N}f)(z)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}k(z,z_{i})f(z_{i}).

Suppose the eigenvalues of
L_{k}
are simple, i.e.,
\lambda_{j}>\lambda_{j+1}>0
for all
j
, and the kernel is bounded, i.e.,
\sup_{z\in\mathcal{X}}k(z,z)<\infty
. Then, the first-order approximation is

\hat{\phi}_{j}(z)-\phi_{j}(z)=\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\phi_{j}(z_{\alpha})\phi_{i}(z_{\alpha})\right)\phi_{i}(z)+O_{p}(N^{-1}).

Proof.

The proof follows by applying the time-independent, non-degenerate perturbation theory (Rayleigh–Schrödinger) to the empirical integral operator
L_{N}
. This technique is standard in quantum mechanics; see, for example, Chapter 7 of
Griffiths and Schroeter 2018
. A detailed derivation is provided in Appendix
B.1
.
∎

The following lemma characterizes the asymptotic distributions of the approximation error terms
R_{X,j,m}
and
R_{Y,j,n}
. These results are essential for understanding the limiting behavior of the empirical directional components
\hat{d}_{j}
, which are a direct application of the central limit theorem and the perturbation expansion results in Lemma
1
. Detailed derivations can be found in Appendix
B.2
.

Lemma 2
.

Under the assumptions of Lemma
1
, we have

\sqrt{N}R_{X,j,m}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}}\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{\mu}_{X,i}^{2}\mathrm{Var}(\psi_{i,j}(Z)))=R_{X,j},

where
\psi_{i,j}(z)=\phi_{i}(z)\phi_{j}(z)
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{X,i}=\mathbb{E}(\phi_{i}(X))
.

Similarly, for
R_{Y,j,n}
,

\sqrt{N}R_{Y,j,n}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}}\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{\mu}_{Y,i}^{2}\mathrm{Var}(\psi_{i,j}(Z)))=R_{Y,j},

with
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{Y,i}=\mathbb{E}(\phi_{i}(Y))
.
Furthermore, under the null hypothesis,

\sqrt{N}R_{X,j,m}-\sqrt{N}R_{Y,j,n}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

Under the fixed alternative hypothesis,

\sqrt{N}R_{X,j,m}-\sqrt{N}R_{Y,j,n}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}R_{X,j}-R_{Y,j}=O_{p}(1).

Lemma
2
implies that
\hat{d}_{j}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}d_{j}
.

Remark 1
.

The result
\sqrt{N}R_{X,j,m}-\sqrt{N}R_{Y,j,n}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0
under the null hypothesis demonstrates that the Nyström approximation error vanishes asymptotically, even after scaling by
\sqrt{N}
. This property is crucial for establishing the limiting null distribution of the proposed test statistic in subsequent sections. In contrast, under the fixed alternative hypothesis, the scaled approximation error remains bounded and contributes to the variability of the test statistic, as discussed in Theorem
2
and Remark
7
. In addition, the boundedness in probability of the approximation error under the fixed alternative follows from

\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}}\leq\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{|\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}|}\leq\frac{1}{\gamma_{j}}\sum_{i\neq j}\lambda_{i}<\infty,

where
\gamma_{j}=\min_{i\neq j}|\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}|>0
is the minimum eigengap for the
j
-th eigenvalue.

2.2
Asymptotic Properties of Empirical Directional Components

We first establish the asymptotic distribution of the directional components under the null hypothesis, i.e.,
H_{0}:\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbb{P}}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbb{Q}}=\boldsymbol{\mu}
.

Define the centered restriction operator
\overset{\circ}{\boldsymbol{G}}_{N}:\mathcal{H}_{k}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{N}
as

\overset{\circ}{\boldsymbol{G}}_{N}f=\left((f(z_{1})-\mathbb{E}(f(Z))),\ldots,(f(z_{N})-\mathbb{E}(f(Z)))\right)^{\top}.

It follows that

\begin{split}\hat{d}_{j}&=\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\phi_{j}(x_{i})-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}\right)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\phi_{j}(y_{i})-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}\right)\right)+\left(R_{X,j,m}-R_{Y,j,n}\right)\\
&=\langle\boldsymbol{l},\overset{\circ}{\boldsymbol{G}}_{N}\phi_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}+\left(R_{X,j,m}-R_{Y,j,n}\right),\end{split}

(3)

where
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}=\langle\boldsymbol{\mu},\phi_{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}=\mathbb{E}(\phi_{j}(X))=\mathbb{E}(\phi_{j}(Y))
is the
j
-th coordinate of the mean element in the RKHS. By the law of large numbers and the continuous mapping theorem,

\hat{d}_{j}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{E}\left(\phi_{j}(X)-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}\right)-\mathbb{E}\left(\phi_{j}(Y)-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}\right)=0.

Furthermore, by the central limit theorem and Lemma
2
, when
H_{0}
holds, we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{N}\hat{d}_{j}=

\displaystyle\left(\sqrt{\frac{N}{m}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\phi_{j}(x_{i})-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}\right)+\sqrt{\frac{N}{n}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\phi_{j}(y_{i})-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}\right)\right)+\sqrt{N}\left(R_{X,j,N}-R_{Y,j,N}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}a_{j}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-p}}b_{j},

where the terms
a_{j}
and
b_{j}
are independent normal random variables with mean zero and variance
S_{j}^{2}=\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{j}(X))=\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{j}(Y))
.

Equivalently, the asymptotic distribution can be written as

\sqrt{N}\hat{d}_{j}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\frac{S_{j}W_{j}}{\sqrt{p(1-p)}},

(4)

where
W_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is the standard normal random variable.

Now consider the fixed alternative hypothesis:

H_{1}:\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbb{P}}=\boldsymbol{\mu}+\boldsymbol{g},\,\,\,\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbb{Q}}=\boldsymbol{\mu},

where
\lVert\boldsymbol{g}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\neq 0
measures the deviation of
H_{1}
from
H_{0}
. In this case,

\begin{split}\sqrt{N}\hat{d}_{j}&=\left(\sqrt{\frac{N}{m}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\phi_{j}(x_{i})-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}-\boldsymbol{g}_{j})+\sqrt{N}\boldsymbol{g}_{j}\right.\\
&\quad\left.-\sqrt{\frac{N}{n}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\phi_{j}(y_{i})-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j})\right)+\sqrt{N}(R_{X,j,m}-R_{Y,j,n})\\
&\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\begin{cases}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}a_{X,j}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-p}}b_{Y,j}+(R_{X,j}-R_{Y,j})\right)+\lim_{N\to\infty}\sqrt{N}\boldsymbol{g}_{j},\quad\text{if }\boldsymbol{g}_{j}\neq 0,\\[8.61108pt]
\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}a_{X,j}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-p}}b_{Y,j}+(R_{X,j}-R_{Y,j})\right),\quad\text{if }\boldsymbol{g}_{j}=0,\end{cases}\end{split}

(5)

where
\boldsymbol{g}_{j}=\langle\boldsymbol{g},\phi_{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}=\langle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbb{P}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbb{Q}},\phi_{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}=d_{j}
,
a_{X,j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,S_{X,j}^{2})
,
b_{Y,j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,S_{Y,j}^{2})
, and
S_{X,j}^{2}=\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{j}(X))
and
S_{Y,j}^{2}=\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{j}(Y))
.

If
\boldsymbol{g}_{j}=0
, the limiting random variable is bounded in probability. Otherwise, the distribution is shifted by
\lim_{N\to\infty}\sqrt{N}\boldsymbol{g}_{j}
, which diverges as
N\to\infty
. This demonstrates that each directional component
\hat{d}_{j}
is sensitive only to shifts in the
i
-th direction.

Finally, we study the power property of the directional components under the Pitman-type local alternative hypothesis:

H_{1,N}:\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbb{P}}=\boldsymbol{\mu}+\frac{\boldsymbol{g}}{\sqrt{N}},\,\,\,\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbb{Q}}=\boldsymbol{\mu}.

In this case, we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{N}\hat{d}_{j}

\displaystyle=\left(\sqrt{\frac{N}{m}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\phi_{j}(x_{i})-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}-\frac{\boldsymbol{g}_{j}}{\sqrt{N}})+\boldsymbol{g}_{j}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.-\sqrt{\frac{N}{n}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\phi_{j}(y_{i})-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j})\right)+\sqrt{N}(R_{X,j,m}-R_{Y,j,n})

\displaystyle\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\begin{cases}\frac{S_{j}W_{j}}{\sqrt{p(1-p)}}+\boldsymbol{g}_{j}&\text{if }\boldsymbol{g}_{j}\neq 0,\\[8.61108pt]
\frac{S_{j}W_{j}}{\sqrt{p(1-p)}},&\text{if }\boldsymbol{g}_{j}=0.\end{cases}

(6)

3
Estimation Quality of Directional Components

Although directional components are consistently estimable, their estimation accuracy varies significantly across different directions. As established by the first-order perturbation expansion in Lemma
1
, the Nyström approximation error is inversely proportional to the eigengap
\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}
. Consequently, components associated with smaller eigenvalues typically exhibit larger estimation errors. In this section, we leverage a key result from perturbation theory to provide a more transparent characterization of the estimation quality for directional components.

To begin with, analogue to
\boldsymbol{G}_{N}
, for the population case, let
\boldsymbol{G}:\mathcal{H}_{k}\to L^{2}(\rho)
be the inclusion operator, then

\Sigma=\boldsymbol{G}^{\top}\boldsymbol{G}=\mathbb{E}\left[k(Z,\cdot)\otimes k(Z,\cdot)\right],

where
Z
is a random variable with law
\rho=p\mathbb{P}+(1-p)\mathbb{Q}
. Hence,
\Sigma=p\Sigma_{\mathbb{P}}+(1-p)\Sigma_{\mathbb{Q}}
,
with
\Sigma_{\mathbb{P}}=\mathbb{E}_{X}(k(X,\cdot)\otimes k(X,\cdot))
and
\Sigma_{\mathbb{Q}}=\mathbb{E}_{Y}(k(Y,\cdot)\otimes k(Y,\cdot))
.

Recall its empirical counterpart:

\hat{\Sigma}=\boldsymbol{G}_{N}^{\top}\boldsymbol{G}_{N}=\frac{\hat{p}}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}k(x_{i},\cdot)\otimes k(x_{i},\cdot)+\frac{1-\hat{p}}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}k(y_{j},\cdot)\otimes k(y_{j},\cdot).

The following lemma [see
Koltchinskii and Giné 2000
and
Zwald and Blanchard 2005
for details] is needed to formalize the concept of the estimation quality of directional components.

Lemma 3
.

Let
A
be a symmetric positive Hilbert–Schmidt operator on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with simple positive eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq 0
. Let
\tilde{\delta}_{j}=\min\{\delta_{j},\delta_{j-1}\}
, where
\delta_{j}=0.5(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{j+1})
. Let
B
be another symmetric positive operator such that
\|B\|_{HS}<\tilde{\delta}_{j}/2
, and suppose
(A+B)
remains positive with simple nonzero eigenvalues.

Let
P_{j}(A)
(resp.
P_{j}(A+B)
) denote the orthogonal projector onto the eigenspace of
A
(resp.
A+B
) corresponding to the
j
-th eigenvector. Then,

\|P_{j}(A)-P_{j}(A+B)\|_{HS}\leq\frac{2\|B\|_{HS}}{\tilde{\delta}_{j}}.

In this lemma, we use the notation
\|\cdot\|_{HS}
to denote the Hilbert–Schmidt norm of an operator.

Remark 2
.

In our context, the operator
A
corresponds to the population uncentered covariance operator, i.e.,
A=\Sigma
and
A+B=\hat{\Sigma}
. Since
\Sigma\phi_{j}=\mathbb{E}_{Z}[\phi_{j}(Z)k(Z,\cdot)]=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}\mathbb{E}_{Z}[\phi_{j}(Z)\phi_{i}(Z)]\phi_{i}(\cdot)=\lambda_{j}\phi_{j}(\cdot)
, and
\langle\phi_{j},\phi_{i}\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)}=\delta_{ij}
, the projection operator onto the
j
-th eigenfunction of
\Sigma
is
P_{j}(\Sigma)=\phi_{j}\otimes\phi_{j}
.

For the empirical case,

\hat{\Sigma}\hat{\phi}_{j}=\sqrt{N}\frac{\boldsymbol{G}_{N}^{\top}\boldsymbol{G}_{N}\boldsymbol{G}_{N}^{\top}\boldsymbol{U}_{j}}{\sigma_{j}^{2}}=\sqrt{N}\frac{\boldsymbol{G}_{N}^{\top}\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{U}_{j}}{\sigma_{j}^{2}}=\sqrt{N}\boldsymbol{G}_{N}^{\top}\boldsymbol{U}_{j}=\sigma_{j}^{2}\hat{\phi}_{j}.

Moreover, recall
(\hat{\phi}_{j}(x_{1}),\ldots,\hat{\phi}_{j}(x_{m}),\hat{\phi}_{j}(y_{1}),\ldots,\hat{\phi}_{j}(y_{n}))^{\top}=\boldsymbol{G}_{N}\hat{\phi}_{j}=\sqrt{N}\boldsymbol{U}_{j}
, we have

\langle\sqrt{N}\boldsymbol{U}_{i},\sqrt{N}\boldsymbol{U}_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}=\frac{1}{N}\left(\sqrt{N}\boldsymbol{U}_{i}\right)^{\top}\left(\sqrt{N}\boldsymbol{U}_{j}\right)=\boldsymbol{U}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{U}_{j}=\delta_{ij},

which shows that the Nyström approximations
\{\sqrt{N}\boldsymbol{U}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
form an orthonormal basis in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Therefore, the projection operator onto the
j
-th empirical eigenfunction of
\hat{\Sigma}
is
P_{j}(\hat{\Sigma})=\hat{\phi}_{j}\otimes\hat{\phi}_{j}
.

Remark 3
.

The bound on the Hilbert–Schmidt norm of the difference between the projection operators provides a quantitative measure of the approximation error between the eigenfunctions of the population covariance operator
\Sigma
and its empirical counterpart
\hat{\Sigma}
. In particular,

\|P_{j}(\Sigma)-P_{j}(\hat{\Sigma})\|_{HS}^{2}=2\left(1-\langle\phi_{j},\hat{\phi}_{j}\rangle^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho)}\right)\geq 2\left(1-\langle\phi_{j},\hat{\phi}_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)}\right)=\|\phi_{j}-\hat{\phi}_{j}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}.

This inequality shows that the Hilbert–Schmidt norm controls the
L^{2}
-distance between the true and empirical eigenfunctions.

Typically, the eigen-gap
\tilde{\delta}_{j}
decreases as
j
increases, implying that the leading eigenfunctions associated with larger eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}
are estimated more accurately. This observation motivates the construction of test statistics based primarily on the leading eigenfunctions, i.e., the first few directional components, to enhance statistical power and estimation reliability, as the empirical directional components are directly related to the Nyström approximations of the eigenfunctions.

4
Test Statistic and Its Asymptotic Properties

4.1
Construction of the Test Statistic

A single reproducing kernel typically yields only a limited number of directional components with high estimation quality. To improve estimation accuracy across more directions, it is beneficial to consider multiple kernels. Let
k^{(c)}
denote the
c
-th kernel, and use the superscript
(c)
for all associated quantities. We propose the following aggregated test statistic:

\hat{T}_{N}=\sum_{c=1}^{C}\hat{T}_{N}^{(c)}

(7)

where
C
is a fixed, pre-specified number of kernels, and
\hat{T}_{N}^{(c)}
denotes the contribution from the
c
-th kernel. Each component
\hat{T}_{N}^{(c)}
is constructed as follows.

First, construct the Gram matrix
\boldsymbol{K}^{(c)}
based on the
c
-th kernel:

\boldsymbol{K}^{(c)}=(\boldsymbol{G}_{N}^{(c)})(\boldsymbol{G}_{N}^{(c)})^{\top}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{K_{XX}}^{(c)}&\boldsymbol{K_{XY}}^{(c)}\\
\boldsymbol{K_{YX}}^{(c)}&\boldsymbol{K_{YY}}^{(c)}\end{pmatrix},

and perform the eigen-decomposition, sorting the eigenvalues such that
(\sigma_{1}^{(c)})^{2}>(\sigma_{2}^{(c)})^{2}>\cdots>0
.

Next, select the first
J(N)
eigenvalues and their corresponding eigenvectors, and construct a low-ranking Gram matrix:

\tilde{\boldsymbol{K}}^{(c)}=\sum_{j=1}^{J(N)}(\sigma_{j}^{(c)})^{2}\boldsymbol{U}_{j}^{(c)}(\boldsymbol{U}_{j}^{(c)})^{\top}=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\boldsymbol{K}}_{XX}^{(c)}&\tilde{\boldsymbol{K}}_{XY}^{(c)}\\
\tilde{\boldsymbol{K}}_{YX}^{(c)}&\tilde{\boldsymbol{K}}_{YY}^{(c)}\end{pmatrix}.

Finally,
\hat{T}_{N}^{(c)}
is defined as

\begin{split}\hat{T}_{N}^{(c)}=\frac{1}{m(m-1)}\left(\boldsymbol{1}_{m}^{\top}N\tilde{\boldsymbol{K}}_{XX}^{(c)}\boldsymbol{1}_{m}-\mathrm{Tr}(N\tilde{\boldsymbol{K}}_{XX}^{(c)})\right)&+\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\left(\boldsymbol{1}_{n}^{\top}N\tilde{\boldsymbol{K}}_{YY}^{(c)}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}-\mathrm{Tr}(N\tilde{\boldsymbol{K}}_{YY}^{(c)})\right)\\
&\,-\frac{2}{mn}\boldsymbol{1}_{m}^{\top}N\tilde{\boldsymbol{K}}_{XY}^{(c)}\boldsymbol{1}_{n},\end{split}

(8)

where
\boldsymbol{1}_{m}
and
\boldsymbol{1}_{n}
are vectors of ones with lengths
m
and
n
, respectively, and
\mathrm{Tr}(\cdot)
denotes the trace operator.

Remark 4
.

Note that
\hat{T}_{N}^{(c)}
is an unbiased estimator of
\sum_{j=1}^{J(N)}\lambda_{j}^{(c)}(d_{j}^{(c)})^{2}
. For asymptotic analysis, it is often convenient to consider the following biased version:

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{N,b}^{(c)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{J(N)}(\sigma_{j}^{(c)})^{2}(\hat{d}_{j}^{(c)})^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{J(N)}(\sigma_{j}^{(c)})^{2}\langle\boldsymbol{l},\sqrt{N}\boldsymbol{U}_{j}^{(c)}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{J(N)}(\sigma_{j}^{(c)})^{2}\left(\langle\boldsymbol{l},\boldsymbol{G}_{N}\phi_{j}^{(c)}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}+(R_{X,j,m}^{(c)}-R_{Y,j,n}^{(c)})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\boldsymbol{1}_{m}^{\top}N\tilde{\boldsymbol{K}}_{XX}^{(c)}\boldsymbol{1}_{m}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}^{\top}N\tilde{\boldsymbol{K}}_{YY}^{(c)}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}-\frac{2}{mn}\boldsymbol{1}_{m}^{\top}N\tilde{\boldsymbol{K}}_{XY}^{(c)}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}.

The only difference between
\hat{T}_{N}^{(c)}
and
\hat{T}_{N,b}^{(c)}
is the use of
1/(m(m-1))
versus
1/m^{2}
(and
1/(n(n-1))
versus
1/n^{2}
), as well as the trace correction terms. Specifically,

\hat{T}_{N}^{(c)}\approx\hat{T}_{N,b}^{(c)}-\frac{1}{m^{2}}\mathrm{Tr}(N\tilde{\boldsymbol{K}}_{XX}^{(c)})-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\mathrm{Tr}(N\tilde{\boldsymbol{K}}_{YY}^{(c)}).

In large samples, the approximation error becomes negligible.

Remark 5
.

A brief note on computational complexity: computing the full eigen-decomposition of the Gram matrix
\boldsymbol{K}
via SVD requires
O(N^{3})
operations, which can be prohibitive for large
N
. However, since only the top
J(N)
eigenpairs are needed, more efficient algorithms such as the Lanczos method can be employed, reducing the cost to
O(N^{2}J(N))
. In practice, when
J(N)
is small (e.g.,
J(N)=1
in our simulation studies), the computational overhead is minimal.

4.2
Asymptotic Properties of the Test Statistic

In this section, we focus on the asymptotic properties of
\hat{T}_{N}^{(c)}
as the final test statistic
\hat{T}_{N}
in (
7
) is simply the sum of the individual components.

Theorem 1
.

Under the null hypothesis
H_{0}:\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbb{P}}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbb{Q}}=\boldsymbol{\mu}
, we have

N\hat{T}_{N}^{(c)}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\frac{1}{p(1-p)}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}^{(c)}\left(S_{j}^{(c)}\right)^{2}\left(\left(W_{j}^{(c)}\right)^{2}-1\right),

where
W_{j}^{(c)}
are independent standard normal random variables, and
(S_{j}^{(c)})^{2}=\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{j}^{(c)}(X))=\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{j}^{(c)}(Y))
are the variances of the
j
-th eigenfunction under the null hypothesis.

Remark 6
.

It is worth noting that when
J(N)=N
,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{K}}^{(c)}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\sigma_{j}^{(c)})^{2}\boldsymbol{U}_{j}^{(c)}(\boldsymbol{U}_{j}^{(c)})^{\top}=\boldsymbol{K}^{(c)}
, and thus the statistic
T_{N}^{(c)}
coincides numerically with the unbiased MMD test statistic

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{N}^{(c)}=\frac{1}{m(m-1)}\left(\boldsymbol{1}_{m}^{\top}N\boldsymbol{K}_{XX}^{(c)}\boldsymbol{1}_{m}-\mathrm{Tr}(N\boldsymbol{K}_{XX}^{(c)})\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\left(\boldsymbol{1}_{n}^{\top}N\boldsymbol{K}_{YY}^{(c)}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}-\mathrm{Tr}(N\boldsymbol{K}_{YY}^{(c)})\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{mn}\boldsymbol{1}_{m}^{\top}N\boldsymbol{K}_{XY}^{(c)}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}.

Under the null hypothesis, after scaling by
N
, its asymptotic distribution is [see Theorem 12 in
Gretton et al. 2012a
]

\frac{1}{p(1-p)}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\bar{\lambda}_{j}^{(c)}\left(\left(W_{j}^{(c)}\right)^{2}-1\right),

where
\bar{\lambda}_{j}^{(c)}
are the eigenvalues of the integral operator associated with the centered kernel
\bar{k}^{(c)}(x,x^{\prime})=k^{(c)}(x,x^{\prime})-\mathbb{E}(k^{(c)}(X,x^{\prime}))-\mathbb{E}(k^{(c)}(x,X^{\prime}))+\mathbb{E}(k^{(c)}(X,X^{\prime}))
:

\left(L_{\bar{k}^{(c)}}\bar{\phi}_{j}^{(c)}\right)(x)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\bar{k}^{(c)}(x,x^{\prime})\bar{\phi}_{j}^{(c)}(x^{\prime})\,d\mathbb{P}(x^{\prime})=\bar{\lambda}_{j}^{(c)}\bar{\phi}_{j}^{(c)}(x).

By the uniqueness of weak convergence, it follows that

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}^{(c)}\left(S_{j}^{(c)}\right)^{2}\left(\left(W_{j}^{(c)}\right)^{2}-1\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\bar{\lambda}_{j}^{(c)}\left(\left(W_{j}^{(c)}\right)^{2}-1\right)\text{ in distribution}.

The following corollary states the asymptotic null distribution of the test statistic
\hat{T}_{N}
, which follows directly from Theorem
1
.

Corollary 1
.

Under the null hypothesis
H_{0}
, we have

N\hat{T}_{N}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\frac{1}{p(1-p)}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{1}_{C}^{\top}\left(\mathcal{N}\left(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\varsigma}_{(j)}\right)\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{p(1-p)}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\lambda_{j}^{(c)}\left(S_{j}^{(c)}\right)^{2},

where
\boldsymbol{\varsigma}_{(j)}
is the
C\times C
covariance matrix with entries

\boldsymbol{\varsigma}_{(j);s,t}=\mathrm{Cov}\left(\sqrt{\lambda_{j}^{(s)}}\phi_{j}^{(s)}(Z),\sqrt{\lambda_{j}^{(t)}}\phi_{j}^{(t)}(Z)\right).

Next, we consider the asymptotic distribution of the test statistic
\hat{T}_{N}^{(c)}
under the fixed alternative hypothesis.

Theorem 2
.

Under the fixed alternative hypothesis
H_{1}:\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbb{P}}=\boldsymbol{\mu}+\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbb{Q}}=\boldsymbol{\mu}
, with
\|\boldsymbol{g}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{(c)}}>0
, we have

\sqrt{N}\left(\hat{T}_{N}^{(c)}-\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}^{(c)}\left(\boldsymbol{g}_{j}^{(c)}\right)^{2}\right)\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}^{(c)}\boldsymbol{g}_{j}^{(c)}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}a_{X,j}^{(c)}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-p}}b_{Y,j}^{(c)}+\left(R_{X,j}^{(c)}-R_{Y,j}^{(c)}\right)\right),

where
a_{X,j}^{(c)}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\left(S_{X,j}^{(c)}\right)^{2}\right)
,
b_{Y,j}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\left(S_{Y,j}^{(c)}\right)^{2}\right)
,
\left(S_{X,j}^{(c)}\right)^{2}=\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{j}^{(c)}(X))
,
\left(S_{Y,j}^{(c)}\right)^{2}=\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{j}^{(c)}(Y))
, and
\boldsymbol{g}_{j}^{(c)}=\langle\boldsymbol{g},\phi_{j}^{(c)}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{(c)}}=\langle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbb{P}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbb{Q}},\phi_{j}^{(c)}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{(c)}}=d_{j}^{(c)}
.

Remark 7
.

The term
\boldsymbol{g}_{j}^{(c)}
represents the deviation signal in the
j
-th direction, and its magnitude directly affects the power of the test. The power is also influenced by the noise level, which is determined by the variance of the random variable

\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}a_{X,j}^{(c)}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-p}}b_{Y,j}^{(c)}+\left(R_{X,j}^{(c)}-R_{Y,j}^{(c)}\right).

In particular, the variance of the Nyström approximation term
R_{X,j}^{(c)}-R_{Y,j}^{(c)}
is governed by the sum
\sum_{i\neq j}\lambda_{i}^{(c)}/(\lambda_{j}^{(c)}-\lambda_{i}^{(c)})
. When
\lambda_{j}^{(c)}
is larger, this sum is smaller, resulting in reduced variance for the approximation error and consequently greater test power.

The following consistency corollary is a direct consequence of Theorem
2
.

Corollary 2
.

Under the fixed alternative hypothesis
H_{1}
, for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\mathrm{P}_{H_{1}}\left(N\hat{T}_{N}\geq t\right)\to 1.

We also establish the asymptotic local power properties of
\hat{T}_{N}^{(c)}
and
\hat{T}_{N}
under the Pitman-type local alternative hypothesis, which are a direct consequence of Theorem
1
and Equation (
2.2
).

Theorem 3
.

Under the local alternative hypothesis
H_{1,N}:\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbb{P}}=\boldsymbol{\mu}+\frac{\boldsymbol{g}}{\sqrt{N}},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbb{Q}}=\boldsymbol{\mu}
, we have

N\hat{T}_{N}^{(c)}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}^{(c)}\left(\frac{S_{j}^{(c)}W_{j}^{(c)}}{\sqrt{p(1-p)}}+\boldsymbol{g}_{j}^{(c)}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{p(1-p)}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}^{(c)}\left(S_{j}^{(c)}\right)^{2},

where
\boldsymbol{g}_{j}^{(c)}
,
W_{j}^{(c)}
, and
S_{j}^{(c)}
are as defined in Theorems
1
and
2
.

Corollary 3
.

Under the local alternative hypothesis
H_{1,N}:\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbb{P}}=\boldsymbol{\mu}+\frac{\boldsymbol{g}}{\sqrt{N}},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbb{Q}}=\boldsymbol{\mu}
, we have

N\hat{T}_{N}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{1}_{C}^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{p(1-p)}}\mathcal{N}\left(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\varsigma}_{(j)}\right)+\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{j}}\odot\boldsymbol{g}_{j}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{p(1-p)}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\lambda_{j}^{(c)}\left(S_{j}^{(c)}\right)^{2},

where
\boldsymbol{\varsigma}_{(j)}
is as defined in Corollary 1,
\odot
is the Hadamard product,
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{j}=(\lambda_{j}^{(1)},\ldots,\lambda_{j}^{(C)})^{\top}
, and
\boldsymbol{g}_{j}=(\boldsymbol{g}_{j}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{g}_{j}^{(C)})^{\top}
.

5
Multiplier Bootstrap for Critical Values

In this section, we present a simple multiplier bootstrap procedure for approximating the critical values of the test statistic
\hat{T}_{N}
. Without loss of generality, we assume
m\geq n
, i.e., the sample size from distribution
\mathbb{P}
exceeds that from
\mathbb{Q}
. The procedure is as follows.

1.

For each kernel
k^{(c)}
, construct the centering matrix

H=\boldsymbol{I}_{m}-\boldsymbol{1}_{m}\boldsymbol{1}_{m}^{\top}/m
,
where
\boldsymbol{I}_{m}
is the
m\times m
identity matrix. Compute the centered low-rank Gram matrix for
\mathcal{X}_{m}
:

N\bar{\boldsymbol{K}}_{XX}^{(c)}=HN\tilde{\boldsymbol{K}}_{XX}^{(c)}H^{\top}.

Remark 8
.

Note that
N\bar{\boldsymbol{K}}_{XX}^{(c)}=\sum_{j=1}^{J(N)}(\sigma_{j}^{(c)})^{2}H(\sqrt{N}\boldsymbol{U}_{j}^{(c)})_{m}(\sqrt{N}\boldsymbol{U}_{j}^{(c)})_{m}^{\top}H^{\top}
,
where
(\boldsymbol{U}_{j}^{(c)})_{m}
denotes the first
m
entries of
\boldsymbol{U}_{j}^{(c)}
, corresponding to the
\mathbb{P}
sample:

\left(\sqrt{N}\boldsymbol{U}_{j}^{(c)}\right)_{m}=\left(\hat{\phi}_{j}^{(c)}(x_{1}),\ldots,\hat{\phi}_{j}^{(c)}(x_{m})\right)^{\top}.

2.

Generate i.i.d. standard normal random variables
v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
that are independent with
X
, and compute the bootstrap test statistic:

\hat{T}_{N,\mathrm{bootstrap}}^{(c)}=\frac{1}{m}\left(\frac{1}{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}N\bar{\boldsymbol{K}}_{XX}^{(c)}\boldsymbol{v}-\frac{1}{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}\mathrm{Tr}(N\bar{\boldsymbol{K}}_{XX}^{(c)})\right),

where
\boldsymbol{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{m})^{\top}
. The aggregated bootstrap statistic is
\hat{T}_{N,\mathrm{bootstrap}}=\sum_{c=1}^{C}\hat{T}_{N,\mathrm{bootstrap}}^{(c)}
.

3.

Repeat Step 2 for
B
bootstrap replicates to obtain
\{\hat{T}_{N,\mathrm{bootstrap}}^{(b)}\}_{b=1}^{B}
. The critical value at significance level
\alpha
is approximated by the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of the bootstrap distribution.

Let
\mathrm{Pr}^{\ast}(\cdot)
be the conditional probability related to
\boldsymbol{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{m})^{\top}
given the samples
\mathcal{X}_{m}
and
\mathcal{Y}_{n}
. The following theorem provides a theoretical justification for the proposed multiplier bootstrap procedure.

Theorem 4
.

Under
H_{0}
,
H_{1}
, or
H_{1,N}
, for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\mathrm{Pr}^{\ast}\left(\frac{\sigma_{j}^{(c)}}{\sqrt{m}}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}H\left(\sqrt{N}\boldsymbol{U}_{j}^{(c)}\right)_{m}\leq t\right)-\mathrm{Pr}\left(\sqrt{\lambda_{j}^{(c)}}S_{X,j}^{(c)}W_{j}^{(c)}\leq t\right)\longrightarrow 0\text{ in probability},

\mathrm{Pr}^{\ast}\left(\sum_{c=1}^{C}\frac{\sigma_{j}^{(c)}}{\sqrt{m}}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}H\left(\sqrt{N}\boldsymbol{U}_{j}^{(c)}\right)_{m}\leq t\right)-\mathrm{Pr}\left(\boldsymbol{1}_{C}^{\top}\mathcal{N}\left(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\varsigma}_{(j)}^{X}\right)\leq t\right)\longrightarrow 0\text{ in probability},

where
\boldsymbol{\varsigma}_{(j)}^{X}
is the
C\times C
covariance matrix with entries

\boldsymbol{\varsigma}_{(j);s,t}^{X}=\mathrm{Cov}\left(\sqrt{\lambda_{j}^{(s)}}\phi_{j}^{(s)}(X),\sqrt{\lambda_{j}^{(t)}}\phi_{j}^{(t)}(X)\right).

As a direct consequence of Theorem
4
, conditional on the samples
\mathcal{X}_{m}
and
\mathcal{Y}_{n}
, the bootstrap component test statistic
\hat{T}_{N,\mathrm{bootstrap}}^{(c)}
converges in distribution to the same limiting law as the original component test statistic
\hat{T}_{N}^{(c)}
under the null hypothesis:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}^{\ast}\left(\hat{T}_{N,\mathrm{bootstrap}}^{(c)}\leq t\right)-\mathrm{Pr}\left(\frac{1}{p(1-p)}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}^{(c)}\left(S_{j}^{(c)}\right)^{2}\left(\left(W_{j}^{(c)}\right)^{2}-1\right)\leq t\right)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow 0\text{ in probability for any }t\in\mathbb{R}.

Also, the aggregated bootstrap test statistic
\hat{T}_{N,\mathrm{bootstrap}}
converges in distribution to the same limiting law as the original aggregated test statistic
\hat{T}_{N}
under the null hypothesis:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}^{\ast}\left(\hat{T}_{N,\mathrm{bootstrap}}\leq t\right)-\mathrm{Pr}\left(\frac{1}{p(1-p)}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{1}_{C}^{\top}\left(\mathcal{N}\left(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\varsigma}_{(j)}\right)\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{p(1-p)}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\lambda_{j}^{(c)}\left(S_{j}^{(c)}\right)^{2}\leq t\right)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow 0\text{ in probability for any }t\in\mathbb{R},

since using the first-order perturbation expansion results in Lemma
1
, we have

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(N\bar{\boldsymbol{K}}_{XX}^{(c)})}{m}=\sum_{j=1}^{J(N)}\lambda_{j}^{(c)}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\hat{\phi}_{j}^{(c)}(x_{i})-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\hat{\phi}_{j}^{(c)}(x_{\alpha})\right)^{2}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}^{(c)}\left(S_{X,j}^{(c)}\right)^{2}.\end{split}

(9)

This result establishes the theoretical validity of the proposed multiplier bootstrap procedure. Under the null hypothesis, the bootstrap statistic converges to the same limiting distribution as the original test statistic, ensuring accurate control of Type I errors. Under the fixed alternative, since the bootstrap statistic is bounded in probability while the original statistic tends to infinity in probability, the resulting test is consistent.

In addition to the bootstrap procedure described above, we also propose a second multiplier bootstrap procedure, which utilizes all samples from both distributions
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
to construct the bootstrap test statistic. The details can be found in Appendix
C
.

6
Simulation and Empirical Studies

6.1
Simulation Studies

In this section, we present a comprehensive set of simulation studies to assess the finite-sample performance of the test statistic
\hat{T}_{N}
, considering both multiple-kernel (
\hat{T}_{N,multi}
) and single-kernel (
\hat{T}_{N,single}
) settings. We primarily compare our method to the standard MMD test statistic using a Gaussian kernel with the median heuristic, denoted as
\hat{T}_{N,\mathrm{MMD}}
. For all proposed test statistics, we set the truncation level at
J(N)=1
and use Gaussian kernels
k(s,t)=\exp(-\|s-t\|_{2}^{2}/\gamma)
.
For
\hat{T}_{N,single}
, we set
\gamma_{med}=\mathrm{median}\left\{\|z_{i}-z_{j}\|_{2}^{2}:1\leq i<j\leq N\right\}
,
where
\mathcal{X}_{m}\cup\mathcal{Y}_{n}=\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{N}\}
is the pooled sample, while for
\hat{T}_{N,multi}
, we use different scaled median heuristics for each kernel:

\{\gamma^{(c)}\}=\left\{\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},1.0,2.0\right\}\gamma_{med}.

Conclusion

In this paper, we addressed the problem of testing the equality of two distributions using the kernel embedding framework. Our main contribution is the development of new test statistics based on directional components, which are particularly effective at detecting orthogonal deviations from the null hypothesis. We established that these components can be consistently estimated from data, and, crucially, that their estimation quality varies across directions: components associated with larger eigen-gaps (and typically larger eigenvalues) are estimated more accurately. This insight motivates the construction of test statistics that truncate the standard MMD statistic by excluding components with smaller eigenvalues. Furthermore, by leveraging multiple kernels and aggregating their respective truncated MMD statistics, we can further enhance the power of the test, essentially combining well-estimated directions from different kernels.

A further advantage of focusing on directional components is the resulting transparent characterization of the asymptotic distributions under various hypotheses. This structure enables the development of computationally efficient multiplier bootstrap procedures for inference. Extensive simulation studies demonstrate that the proposed test statistics maintain their nominal Type I error rate, achieve high power against a range of alternatives, and consistently outperform the standard MMD test with permutation-based inference. Finally, applications to three publicly available microarray datasets illustrate both the practical utility and computational efficiency of our approach.

Appendix A
Useful Lemmas

The following lemma provides a non-asymptotic upper bound for the difference between
\hat{\Sigma}
and
\Sigma
. The proof can be found in
Shawe-Taylor and Cristianini 2003
or
Zwald and Blanchard 2005
, and is omitted here for brevity.

Lemma 4
.

Suppose that
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}k(x,x)\leq M
. Then, with probability at least
1-e^{-\xi}
,

\|\hat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{HS}\leq\frac{2M}{\sqrt{n}}\left(1+\sqrt{\frac{\xi}{2}}\right),

where
\|\cdot\|_{HS}
denotes the Hilbert-Schmidt norm.

As the sample size
n
increases, the empirical covariance operator
\hat{\Sigma}
converges to
\Sigma
in Hilbert–Schmidt norm at the rate
O_{p}(n^{-1/2})
.

The following lemma comes from
Kato 1987
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a separable Hilbert space with
A
,
B
self-adjoint compact operators. Let
(\gamma_{j})_{j\geq 1}
be an enumeration of discrete eigenvalues of
B-A
, then there exist extended enumerations
(\beta_{j})_{j\geq 1}
and
(\alpha_{j})_{j\geq 1}
of discrete eigenvalues of
B
and
A
respectively such that,

\sum_{j\geq 1}\phi(\beta_{j}-\alpha_{j})\leq\sum_{j\geq 1}\phi(\gamma_{j}),

where
\phi
is any nonnegative convex function with
\phi(0)=0
.

By choosing
\phi(t)=|t|^{p}
,
p\geq 1
, the above inequality becomes

\sum_{j\geq 1}|\beta_{j}-\alpha_{j}|^{p}\leq\sum_{j\geq 1}|\gamma_{j}|^{p}.

Letting
p=2
and recalling that
\|B-A\|_{HS}^{2}=\sum_{j\geq 1}|\gamma_{j}|^{2}
, it follows that

\sum_{j\geq 1}|\beta_{j}-\alpha_{j}|^{2}\leq\|B-A\|_{HS}^{2}.

Moreover, since
\lim_{p\to\infty}(\sum_{j\geq 1}|\gamma_{j}|^{p})^{1/p}=\sup_{j\geq 1}|\gamma_{j}|=\|B-A\|
, we have that

\sup_{j\geq 1}|\beta_{j}-\alpha_{j}|\leq\|B-A\|.

where
\|\cdot\|
is the operator norm.

Remark 9
.

Let
A=\Sigma
and
B-A=\hat{\Sigma}-\Sigma
, these two lemmas imply that

|\sigma_{j}^{2}-\lambda_{j}|=o_{p}(1),

and

\sum_{j\geq 1}\sigma_{j}^{2}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}\sum_{j\geq 1}\lambda_{j}.

Appendix B
Proofs for Main Results

B.1
Proof of Lemma
1

The first-order ansatz for the perturbation is

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{j}=\phi_{j}+\boldsymbol{\delta}\phi_{j},

\displaystyle\sigma_{j}^{2}=\lambda_{j}+\boldsymbol{\delta}\lambda_{j}.

Since
L_{N}\hat{\phi}_{j}=\sigma_{j}^{2}\hat{\phi}_{j}
, we have after ignoring the second order terms:

\displaystyle(L_{k}+\Delta L)(\phi_{j}+\boldsymbol{\delta}\phi_{j})

\displaystyle=(\lambda_{j}+\boldsymbol{\delta}\lambda_{j})(\phi_{j}+\boldsymbol{\delta}\phi_{j})

\displaystyle\implies L_{k}\phi_{j}+\Delta L\phi_{j}+L_{k}\boldsymbol{\delta}\phi_{j}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{j}\phi_{j}+\lambda_{j}\boldsymbol{\delta}\phi_{j}+\boldsymbol{\delta}\lambda_{j}\phi_{j}

\displaystyle\implies\Delta L\phi_{j}+L_{k}\boldsymbol{\delta}\phi_{j}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{j}\boldsymbol{\delta}\phi_{j}+\boldsymbol{\delta}\lambda_{j}\phi_{j}.

Take the inner product with
\phi_{j}
with respect to the
L^{2}(\rho)
:

\displaystyle\langle\Delta L\phi_{j},\phi_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)}+\langle L_{k}\boldsymbol{\delta}\phi_{j},\phi_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)}

\displaystyle=\langle\lambda_{j}\boldsymbol{\delta}\phi_{j},\phi_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)}+\langle\boldsymbol{\delta}\lambda_{j}\phi_{j},\phi_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{self-adjoint of }L_{k}}{\implies}\langle\Delta L\phi_{j},\phi_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)}+\lambda_{j}\langle\boldsymbol{\delta}\phi_{j},\phi_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{j}\langle\boldsymbol{\delta}\phi_{j},\phi_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)}+\boldsymbol{\delta}\lambda_{j}.

Thus,

\boldsymbol{\delta}\lambda_{j}=\langle\Delta L\phi_{j},\phi_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)}.

Next, take the inner product with
\phi_{i}
for
i\neq j
:

\displaystyle\langle\Delta L\phi_{j},\phi_{i}\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)}+\langle L_{k}\boldsymbol{\delta}\phi_{j},\phi_{i}\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{j}\langle\boldsymbol{\delta}\phi_{j},\phi_{i}\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)}+\boldsymbol{\delta}\lambda_{j}\langle\phi_{j},\phi_{i}\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)}

\displaystyle\implies\langle\Delta L\phi_{j},\phi_{i}\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)}+\lambda_{i}\langle\boldsymbol{\delta}\phi_{j},\phi_{i}\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{j}\langle\boldsymbol{\delta}\phi_{j},\phi_{i}\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)},

thus,

\langle\boldsymbol{\delta}\phi_{j},\phi_{i}\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)}=\frac{\langle\Delta L\phi_{j},\phi_{i}\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)}}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}}.

We now express
\boldsymbol{\delta}\phi_{j}
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}\phi_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\neq j}\langle\boldsymbol{\delta}\phi_{j},\phi_{i}\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)}\phi_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{\langle\Delta L\phi_{j},\phi_{i}\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)}}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}}\phi_{i}.

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\langle\Delta L\phi_{j},\phi_{i}\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)}

\displaystyle=\langle L_{N}\phi_{j},\phi_{i}\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)}-\langle L_{k}\phi_{j},\phi_{i}\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)}

\displaystyle=\langle L_{N}\phi_{j},\phi_{i}\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\phi_{j}(z_{\alpha})\int_{\mathcal{X}}k(z_{\alpha},z)\phi_{i}(z)d\rho(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{i}}{N}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\phi_{j}(z_{\alpha})\phi_{i}(z_{\alpha}).

Note that all the second-order terms are ignored in the above derivation. We now investigate their convergence rate.

\displaystyle\Delta L\phi_{j}=L_{N}\phi_{j}-L_{k}\phi_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\phi_{j}(z_{\alpha})k(z_{\alpha},\cdot)-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\phi_{j}(z)k(z,\cdot)d\rho(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\phi_{j}(z_{\alpha})k(z_{\alpha},\cdot)-\lambda_{j}\phi_{j}(\cdot)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\phi_{j}(z_{\alpha})\phi_{i}(z_{\alpha})\phi_{i}(\cdot)+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\lambda_{j}\phi_{j}(z_{\alpha})^{2}\phi_{j}(\cdot)-\lambda_{j}\phi_{j}(\cdot)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\phi_{j}(z_{\alpha})\phi_{i}(z_{\alpha})\phi_{i}(\cdot)+\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\phi_{j}(z_{\alpha})^{2}-1\right)\lambda_{j}\phi_{j}(\cdot).

Since all the coefficients,
(1/N\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\phi_{j}(z_{\alpha})\phi_{i}(z_{\alpha}))
and
(1/N\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\phi_{j}(z_{\alpha})^{2}-1)
, are of order
O_{p}(N^{-1/2})
and
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}<\infty
, we have

\|\Delta L\phi_{j}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho)}=O_{p}(N^{-1}).

Thus,

\displaystyle\|\Delta L\boldsymbol{\delta}\phi_{j}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}=O_{p}(N^{-1})

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\lambda_{j}\boldsymbol{\delta}\phi_{j}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}=O_{p}(N^{-1}).

Putting everything together, we have

\hat{\phi}_{j}(z)-\phi_{j}(z)=\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\phi_{j}(z_{\alpha})\phi_{i}(z_{\alpha})\right)\phi_{i}(z)+O_{p}(N^{-1}).

Proof of Lemma
2

We focus on the first term,
\sqrt{N}R_{X,j,m}
, since the analysis for
\sqrt{N}R_{Y,j,n}
is analogous.

\displaystyle\sqrt{N}R_{X,j,m}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{N}}{m}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\left(\hat{\phi}_{j}(x_{\alpha})-\phi_{j}(x_{\alpha})\right)+O_{p}(N^{-1/2})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{N}}{m}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\left(\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\beta=1}^{N}\phi_{i}(z_{\beta})\phi_{j}(z_{\beta})\right)\phi_{i}(x_{\alpha})\right)+O_{p}(N^{-1/2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\beta=1}^{N}\phi_{i}(z_{\beta})\phi_{j}(z_{\beta})\right)\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\phi_{i}(x_{\alpha})+O_{p}(N^{-1/2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\beta=1}^{N}\psi_{i,j}(z_{\beta})\right)\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\phi_{i}(x_{\alpha})+O_{p}(N^{-1/2})

\displaystyle\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}}\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{\mu}_{X,i}^{2}\mathrm{Var}(\psi_{i,j}(Z)))=R_{X,j},

where
\psi_{i,j}(z)=\phi_{i}(z)\phi_{j}(z)
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{X,i}=\mathbb{E}(\phi_{i}(X))
.

Similarly, we have

\sqrt{N}R_{Y,j,n}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}}\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{\mu}_{Y,i}^{2}\mathrm{Var}(\psi_{i,j}(Z)))=R_{Y,j},

with
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{Y,i}=\mathbb{E}(\phi_{i}(Y))
.

When the null hypothesis holds, we have

Appendix C
A Second Multiplier Bootstrap Procedure

In addition to the bootstrap procedure described in the main text, we propose a second multiplier bootstrap procedure. This alternative approach utilizes all samples from both distributions
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
to construct the bootstrap test statistic. The procedure is as follows:

1.

For each kernel
k^{(c)}
, construct two centering matrices:

H_{m}=\boldsymbol{I}_{m}-\frac{1}{m}\boldsymbol{1}_{m}\boldsymbol{1}_{m}^{\top},\quad H_{n}=\boldsymbol{I}_{n}-\frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}^{\top}.

2.

Compute the following centered low-rank Gram matrices:

\displaystyle N\bar{\boldsymbol{K}}_{XX}^{(c)}=H_{m}N\tilde{\boldsymbol{K}}_{XX}^{(c)}H_{m},

\displaystyle N\bar{\boldsymbol{K}}_{YY}^{(c)}=H_{n}N\tilde{\boldsymbol{K}}_{YY}^{(c)}H_{n},

\displaystyle N\bar{\boldsymbol{K}}_{XY}^{(c)}=H_{m}N\tilde{\boldsymbol{K}}_{XY}^{(c)}H_{n}.

3.

Generate independent standard normal random vectors

\boldsymbol{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{m})^{\top},\quad\boldsymbol{r}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{n})^{\top},

independent of the samples
\mathcal{X}_{m}
and
\mathcal{Y}_{n}
. Compute the bootstrap test statistic:

\hat{T}_{N,\mathrm{bootstrap}}^{(c)}=N(A+B-2C),

(11)

where

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m(m-1)}\left(\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}N\bar{\boldsymbol{K}}_{XX}^{(c)}\boldsymbol{v}-\mathrm{Tr}(N\bar{\boldsymbol{K}}_{XX}^{(c)})\right),

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\top}N\bar{\boldsymbol{K}}_{YY}^{(c)}\boldsymbol{r}-\mathrm{Tr}(N\bar{\boldsymbol{K}}_{YY}^{(c)})\right),

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{mn}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}N\bar{\boldsymbol{K}}_{XY}^{(c)}\boldsymbol{r}.

The aggregated bootstrap test statistic is then given by

\hat{T}_{N,\mathrm{bootstrap}}=\sum_{c=1}^{C}\hat{T}_{N,\mathrm{bootstrap}}^{(c)}.

(12)

4.

Repeat Step 3
B
times to obtain
B
bootstrap replicates
\hat{T}_{N,\mathrm{bootstrap}}^{(c),b}
for
b=1,\ldots,B
. The critical value at significance level
\alpha
is given by the
(1-\alpha)
quantile of the bootstrap distribution.

Let
\mathrm{Pr}^{\ast}(\cdot)
be the conditional probability related to
\boldsymbol{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{m})^{\top}
and
\boldsymbol{r}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{n})^{\top}
given the samples
\mathcal{X}_{m}
and
\mathcal{Y}_{n}
. The following theorem establishes the validity of this second multiplier bootstrap procedure.

Corollary 4
.

Under
H_{0}
,
H_{1}
, or
H_{1,N}
, for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\mathrm{Pr}^{\ast}\left(\hat{T}_{N,\mathrm{bootstrap}}^{(c)}\leq t\right)-\mathrm{Pr}\left(\Gamma\leq t\right)\longrightarrow 0\quad\text{in probability,}

where
\Gamma
is a random variable with distribution

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}^{(c)}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}a_{X,j}^{(c)}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-p}}b_{Y,j}^{(c)}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}^{(c)}\left(S_{X,j}^{(c)}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{1-p}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}^{(c)}\left(S_{Y,j}^{(c)}\right)^{2}\right).

where
a_{X,j}^{(c)}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\left(S_{X,j}^{(c)}\right)^{2}\right)

are independent for each

.

Proof.

The proof follows from Theorem
4
.
Note:

\displaystyle\frac{N}{m(m-1)}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}N\bar{\boldsymbol{K}}_{XX}^{(c)}\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{N}{m-1}\sum_{j=1}^{J(N)}(\sigma_{j}^{(c)})^{2}\left(\sqrt{m}\langle\boldsymbol{l}_{m}\odot\boldsymbol{v},H\left(\sqrt{N}\boldsymbol{U}_{j}^{(c)}\right)_{m}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\right)^{2},

\displaystyle\frac{N}{n(n-1)}\boldsymbol{r}^{\top}N\bar{\boldsymbol{K}}_{YY}^{(c)}\boldsymbol{r}

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{N}{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^{J(N)}(\sigma_{j}^{(c)})^{2}\left(\sqrt{n}\langle\boldsymbol{l}_{n}\odot\boldsymbol{r},H\left(\sqrt{N}\boldsymbol{U}_{j}^{(c)}\right)_{n}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\right)^{2},

\displaystyle\frac{N}{mn}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}N\bar{\boldsymbol{K}}_{XY}^{(c)}\boldsymbol{r}

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{N}{\sqrt{mn}}\sum_{j=1}^{J(N)}(\sigma_{j}^{(c)})^{2}\left(\sqrt{m}\langle\boldsymbol{l}_{m}\odot\boldsymbol{v},H\left(\sqrt{N}\boldsymbol{U}_{j}^{(c)}\right)_{m}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left(\sqrt{n}\langle\boldsymbol{l}_{n}\odot\boldsymbol{r},H\left(\sqrt{N}\boldsymbol{U}_{j}^{(c)}\right)_{n}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\right).

In addition, similar to Equation (
9

\displaystyle\frac{N}{m}\frac{\mathrm{Tr}\left(N\bar{\boldsymbol{K}}_{XX}^{(c)}\right)}{m-1}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}^{(c)}\left(S_{X,j}^{(c)}\right)^{2},

\displaystyle\frac{N}{n}\frac{\mathrm{Tr}\left(N\bar{\boldsymbol{K}}_{YY}^{(c)}\right)}{n-1}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}\frac{1}{1-p}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}^{(c)}\left(S_{Y,j}^{(c)}\right)^{2}.

It is straightforward to verify that under the null hypothesis,

D.4
Comparison with the Mahalanobis aggregated MMD

Chatterjee and Bhattacharya 2025
introduced a Mahalanobis aggregated MMD test statistic that leverages the Mahalanobis distance among MMD statistics computed with different kernels. Their approach shares two key features with ours: (1) the use of multiple kernels to enhance test power, and (2) the application of a multiplier bootstrap procedure to determine critical values. Given these similarities, it is natural to compare the performance of our proposed method with that of
Chatterjee and Bhattacharya 2025
.

We adopt the DGPs corresponding to settings
S1
–
S3
in their paper:

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}=(0.5^{|i-j|})_{1\leq i,j\leq d}

•
