Age‐related long‐term effects of vaginal delivery, pregnancy, and sphincter injury on anal continence: A matched cohort study
Authors: Ida E. K. Nilsson, Jwan Al‐Mukhtar Othman, Jennie Larsudd‐Kåverud, Sigvard Åkervall, Mattias Molin, Ian Milsom, Maria Gyhagen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIt is still poorly understood to what extent various obstetric and non‐obstetric factors contribute to anal incontinence in women from a lifetime perspective. This study aimed to assess the contribution of pregnancy, delivery mode, obstetric anal sphincter injury (OASI), and age on anal incontinence in women aged 40–64 years.Material and MethodsData from the Swedish Medical Birth Register and the Total Population Register were linked to responses to an anal incontinence questionnaire collected in 2014 and 2015. The study included 13 480 women, 20 years from first birth, with BMI data: 4192 nulliparas, 2411 with two cesarean deliveries, and 6877 with two vaginal deliveries. Women were matched by exact age and BMI (±3 units), yielding three cohorts of 1961 women each. A subgroup analysis compared vaginally delivered women without OASI to those with one or two OASIs.ResultsThe prevalence of fecal incontinence was highest in women with 2 vaginal deliveries (14.2%) and lowest in those with 2 cesarean deliveries (10.2%, p < 0.001). Fecal incontinence was significantly higher in nulliparous women (12.9%) compared with the cesarean group (p < 0.01). Both bothersome fecal (p = 0.001) and anal (p < 0.001) incontinence were more common following vaginal delivery compared to cesarean delivery. Among women with 2 vaginal deliveries, the prevalence of fecal incontinence nearly doubled in those with OASI compared to those without (23.3% vs. 13.7%, p < 0.01). Women with OASI reported a greater symptom burden, demonstrated by a higher Jorge‐Wexner score (2.38 vs. 1.42, p < 0.001), increased use of protective products (7.8% vs. 3.0%, p = 0.025), and a higher reported impact on daily lifestyle (20.7% vs. 10.1%, p = 0.002). Only women with two vaginal deliveries showed a significant age‐related increase in fecal incontinence.ConclusionsLong‐term fecal incontinence was more common and severe after vaginal delivery, particularly with OASI. An age‐related effect on fecal incontinence was observed only among vaginal deliveries. Even with complete prevention of OASI through perineal protection, a significant age‐related increase in fecal incontinence between 40 and 62 years would remain, arising solely from the effect of vaginal delivery. Preventing OASI remains an important obstetric target to reduce fecal incontinence later in life.

INTRODUCTION

Fecal incontinence (FI) is a particularly distressing form of pelvic floor disorder (PFD) with far‐reaching consequences affecting women's physical, psychological, and social well‐being. Involuntary bowel leakage is associated with stigmas such as social withdrawal, reduced self‐esteem, impaired sexual function, and a diminished quality of life. 1 , 2 , 3 Despite its profound impact, FI is underdiagnosed and undertreated, largely due to feelings of shame, the normalization of symptoms, and a lack of awareness, particularly among older women. With rising life expectancy and more women remaining professionally active later in life, the societal impact is becoming increasingly evident. 4 , 5 Vaginal delivery (VD) is the most significant modifiable risk factor of FI, although age and conditions associated with loose or liquid stools also contribute. 1 FI is common in the general population and studies on FI in community dwelling women have shown that most of them had other significant risk factors, which are old age, obesity, irritable bowel syndrome, rectal urgency, post cholecystectomy diarrhea, diabetes, etc. However, most of these studies did not find any association between FI and an obstetrical history or sphincter injury. 1 , 4 Obstetric anal sphincter injury (OASI) is the primary risk factor for FI in parous women, 6 , 7 affecting approximately 5% of first‐time mothers, 8 highlighting the need for prevention, accurate diagnostics, and effective management in obstetric care. 9 Fear of permanent anal incontinence (AI) following VD is a significant factor contributing to women's preference for cesarean delivery (CD). 10 , 11 Two Cochrane Reviews concluded that CD is not protective against AI. 12 , 13 We aimed to assess the relative contributions of pregnancy and mode of delivery to age‐related long‐term FI in women aged 40–64 years, and to evaluate the effect of OASI on bowel continence. Nulliparous women were included to clarify the influence of non‐obstetric factors on FI.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

The present study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. 14 This matched cohort study involved 35 000 women. Those eligible for matching were drawn from two large, nationwide, randomly selected source cohorts as described previously. 7 , 15

Women with two deliveries were identified through the Swedish Medical Birth Register (MBR), from a source cohort of 69 224 women who had two singleton births between 1992 and 1998 and no subsequent births up to the time of the questionnaire survey. A simple random sample of 15 000 women was selected: 11 000 with 2 VDs and 4000 with 2 CDs. Nulliparous women were identified by Statistics Sweden from the Total Population Register (TPR), based on a cohort of 625 810 women. From this cohort, a simple random sample of 20 000 women was drawn. All women were surveyed for PFDs in 2014 and 2015. Parous women were assessed approximately 20 years after their first birth. Women with multifetal delivery or an ongoing pregnancy were excluded. The MBR, established in 1973 and maintained by the National Board of Health and Welfare, collects data about more than 98% of all births in Sweden, including antenatal and obstetric information (such as information on OASI) from all clinics and units. 16 It includes all women with deliveries in Sweden. During the study period, most immigrants were of Nordic or other European descent. The TPR, operational since 1968, provides nearly complete demographic data, including information on births, deaths, and migration, with high reporting coverage (95% of immigrations reported within four weeks). The high quality and validity of both registers for population‐based research have been previously reported. 16 , 17 , 18 A detailed description of the sampling procedures, flow charts, cohort characteristics, response rates, and non‐responder analyses has been published. 7 , 15 Women outside the 40–64‐year age range and those with missing data on AI and/or body mass index (BMI), calculated using self‐reported height and weight in the questionnaire and expressed as weight (kg) divided by height squared, (m2), were excluded. Matching (1:1:1) was performed based on exact age and ±3 BMI units, both of which are considered to be confounders and risk factors for bowel incontinence (Figure 1, Figure S1). The matching procedure yielded three cohorts of 1961 women each, resulting in a well‐balanced distribution of age and BMI between groups, with standardized mean differences (SMDs) below 0.10, the accepted threshold for imbalance (Table 1). To assess the effect of 2 pregnancies, nulliparous women were compared with the 2 CDs cohort. The additive effect of the trauma associated with vaginal birth was evaluated as the difference between the 2 CD and 2 VD groups. Birth register data were used prospectively and combined with information from a PFD‐specific postal and web‐based questionnaire survey in 2014 and 2015. The 40‐item questionnaire included questions on demographics, PFDs, and a control question regarding ongoing pregnancy, among other items. The English version of the questionnaire is available as an Appendix in Supporting Information. FI and AI were defined according to the International Urogynecological Association (IUGA) and the International Continence Society (ICS) as any involuntary leakage of solid and/or liquid stool with and without concomitant gas leakage. AI was defined as involuntary leakage of gas; isolated gas incontinence (IGI) and/or FI. 19 AI was evaluated in terms of type, frequency, and impact using the validated Jorge‐Wexner incontinence score (0 = perfect continence; 20 = complete incontinence). Frequency of leakage was graded on a five‐point scale: 0 = absent, 1 = ≤1/month, 2 = >1/month but <1/week, 3 = ≥1/week but <1/day, and 4 = ≥1/day. The total score included both symptom frequency and coping measures, i.e., use of protective products due to involuntary bowel leakage and the impact of bowel incontinence on daily lifestyle, providing a composite measure of severity. 20 Scores were categorized as: Mild 1–3, Moderate 4–8, and Severe 9–20. The degree of symptom‐specific bother of FI and IGI was dichotomized into “Not bothersome” (no problem or a minor nuisance) and “Bothersome” (some bother, much bother, and a major problem).

Statistics

Descriptive data for continuous variables were presented with mean and standard deviation (SD), median, and interquartile range (IQR, Q1–Q3), while categorical data were presented with numbers and percentages. SMD was calculated as the difference between the group means divided by the pooled SD. For pairwise group comparisons, the Mantel–Haenszel chi‐square test was used for ordered categorical variables, Fisher's Exact test for dichotomous variables, and the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables. When comparing proportions between groups with or without OASI, the difference in proportions with 95% confidence interval (CI) was presented. The estimated age‐related probability and OR with 95% CI for a 10‐year increase in FI outcomes were obtained by logistic regression. In each analysis, missing data were excluded and presented. No adjustment for multiple testing was made. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Inc., Cary, NC).

RESULTS

Hysterectomy was least common after 2 VDs (4.1%) compared to 7.6% and 7.3% in nulliparous and 2 CD women (Table S1). Among the 1961 women with 2 VDs, 108 (5.5%) had one, and 8 (0.4%) had two OASIs (not in table). The prevalence of FI was highest after 2 VDs (14.2%) and lowest after 2 CDs (10.2%, p < 0.001); nulliparas had 12.9% (vs 2 CDs, p < 0.01) (Table 2). FI was nearly twice as common in women with OASI compared with those without OASI (23.3% vs. 13.7%, p < 0.01) (Table 3). The prevalence of AI and IGI was also higher after 2 VDs compared to 2 CDs. Bothersome symptoms of FI, AI, and IGI were consistently most prevalent after 2 VDs. Bothersome FI was reported by 4.2% after 2 VDs, compared with 3.6% after 2 CDs (p = 0.001) and 3.1% among nulliparous women. Similarly, bothersome AI occurred in 7.0% after 2 VDs, 5.6% in the 2 CD group (p < 0.001), and 5.1% in nulliparas, while bothersome IGI was reported by 2.6% after 2 VDs vs. 1.9% in both comparison groups (Table 2).

Women with 2 VDs reported a greater symptom burden than those with 2 CDs, reflected by higher Jorge‐Wexner scores (1.47 vs. 1.11, p < 0.001), more frequent use of protective products (3.3% vs. 1.9%, p = 0.009), and a greater negative impact on daily life (10.7% vs. 7.0%, p < 0.001) (Table 4). Among women with 2 VDs, symptom severity was further increased by OASI, with higher Jorge‐Wexner scores (2.38 vs. 1.42; p < 0.001), greater use of protective products (7.8% vs. 3.0%; p = 0.025), and a stronger impact on daily lifestyle (20.7% vs. 10.1%, p = 0.002) (Table 5).

At age 40, the predicted risk of FI was similar among nulliparous women and those with 2 CDs or 2 VDs, suggesting a latent obstetric effect (Figure 2A,B). Between ages 40 and 62, the risk of FI increased significantly after 2 VDs, particularly in women with OASI (Table S2A,B). In contrast, women with 2 CDs and nulliparas showed nearly identical risk trajectories, indicating the absence of an additional effect of pregnancy without VD. Across ages, the probability of FI was higher in women with OASI than in those without (Figure 2A,B). In women with 2 VDs, including those with OASI (n = 1961), the predicted probability of FI increased from 0.099 at age 42 to 0.214 at age 61 (p < 0.001) (Table S2A). Among those without OASI (n = 1845), representing the effect of 2 VDs only, the increase from 0.099 to 0.199 remained statistically significant (p = 0.004), with the predicted probability at age 61 lowered by 0.015 compared with those with OASI (Table S2A and Figure 2A). The age‐related increase in FI was threefold in the subgroup of women with OASI (n = 116), but this trend did not reach statistical significance (OR per 10 years, 2.10; 95% CI, 0.81–5.44; p = 0.13) (Figure 2B and Table S2B).

DISCUSSION

Long‐term fecal incontinence was more common and severe after vaginal delivery, particularly when complicated by obstetric anal sphincter injury, which doubled the risk and affected nearly one in four women with OASI. An age‐related increase in fecal incontinence was observed only among women with two vaginal deliveries, suggesting that vaginal birth may have lasting effects on bowel continence that become evident after 40 years of age, also indicating a potential latency period. In contrast, women with two cesarean deliveries and nulliparous women showed similar, but not significant, age‐related trends, implying that pregnancy itself does not contribute to an increased risk of fecal incontinence in the age range 40 to 62 years.

The widely held view that CD does not protect against FI is largely based on systematic reviews with short‐term follow‐up. 6 , 12 , 13 A Cochrane review by Nelson et al. could not demonstrate any significant association between mode of delivery and FI or AI and concluded that there was insufficient evidence to support CD to preserve anal continence. 12 It was noted that the reviewed studies were fraught with significant shortcomings, low statistical power, varying assessment tools and definitions, and short follow‐up periods. Mixing short and long‐term data tends to underestimate long‐term symptoms such as bowel incontinence, which often manifest years after childbirth. Long‐term data on FI remain limited. In a large prospective study, Hagen et al. reported FI rates of 16.5% after CD and 20.2% for VD 20–26 years after childbirth. 21 Although higher than in our study (10.2% and 14.2%, respectively), the difference between these delivery modes was similar. Volløyhaug et al. presented similar results for FI (6.1% after spontaneous VD, 8.9% after CD, and 12.1% after instrumental delivery) assessed 15–23 years after index birth. 22 Further, a population‐based study of 3 755 110 individuals (1.6 million women) found specialist care for AI to be more common among women with VDs only (0.37%) compared to those with CDs only (0.22%), OR 1.65 (95% CI 1.49, 1.82). 23 In a population‐based Swedish cohort study including 5236 primiparous women, the prevalence of FI after 20 years was 14.5% in VD and 10.6% in CD (adjusted OR 1.43, 95% CI 1.16–1.77), with no difference between elective and emergency cesarean deliveries. 24

In a Norwegian study from 2016, OASI was linked to a more than two‐fold risk of bowel incontinence 15–23 years after VD, consistent with our findings. 25 In this study, nulliparous women had a higher prevalence of FI than those with 2 CDs, although symptom severity and daily life impact were generally lower in the nulliparous cohort. This may reflect morbidities such as inflammatory bowel disease or functional bowel disorders. 26 , 27 , 28

The findings of this study emphasize the importance of preventing OASI during vaginal delivery to reduce the risk of bowel incontinence later in life. Given the relatively small number of OASIs, the age‐related effect on FI was not significant. However, the prevalence of FI nearly doubled, from 13.7% in women with VD without OASI to 23.3% in those with OASI, consistent with our previous findings based on a larger amount of data. 7 The use of prediction tools to identify individuals at high risk of OASI antenatally, 29 applying and upholding perineal protection techniques, 30 and timely management of anal injuries postpartum are essential components of preventive obstetric care. Incorporating long‐term risk estimates into counseling may further support informed decision‐making regarding delivery mode, particularly for women at increased risk. Because FI also occurs in women without childbirth‐related injury, pelvic floor evaluation and management should be comprehensive and individualized, addressing both obstetric and non‐obstetric contributing factors. 31

The main strengths of this study lie in its use of high‐quality national registers and a robust study design using matched cohorts. The MBR and TPR are considered highly reliable due to their completeness and validated data. 16 , 17 The study cohorts were large and surveyed within specified time frames, which improves internal consistency. One‐to‐one matching enhances control over confounding variables, thus improving the precision, accuracy, and efficiency of statistical analyses. 32 The use of a symptom‐specific questionnaire is considered the most suitable method to capture sensitive conditions such as FI. 33 , 34 Previous research has also shown that self‐reported data are generally consistent and valid when symptoms are present at the time of reporting. 34 , 35 , 36

Additionally, the validity of outcome measures based on self‐report depends on participants' ability to perceive, recall, and accurately report symptoms, factors that may be influenced by aging. The prevalence of AI and FI may be overestimated, as women experiencing symptoms are more likely to participate in questionnaire studies. 37 , 38 Moreover, data on symptom onset in relation to pregnancy and delivery are lacking.

CONCLUSION

Even in the absence of OASI, vaginal delivery was associated with an age‐related risk of bowel incontinence. There is a wealth of evidence that has demonstrated the occurrence of neuromuscular and connective tissue injury, ischemia, pudendal neuropathy, and unrecognized sphincter defects as a consequence of a vaginal delivery that may contribute to anal incontinence following delivery. Potential risk factors, including instrumental delivery, fetal size, and maternal anthropometric characteristics, were not specifically examined in this study and warrant further investigation to clarify their role in long‐term fecal incontinence.
