Mortality and morbidity consequences of particulate matter in South Korea: evidence from transboundary air pollution
Authors: Osung Kwon, Tae-Hyun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExposure to fine particulate matter (PM2.5) is harmful to people, leading to respiratory disease and even death. The association between PM2.5 pollution and health outcomes has become a key foundation for public health policy development. This study aims to quantify the effects of PM2.5 on health outcomes (mortality, morbidity) and estimate the benefits of reducing PM2.5 levels in South Korea.MethodsThis study uses an instrumental variable (IV) analysis to estimate the effects of PM2.5 on health outcomes in South Korea, using mortality data for 2015–2022 and morbidity data for 2016–2019. Local air pollution in South Korea is instrumented using a wind-direction indicator with air pollution levels measured at upwind Chinese monitors to address potential endogeneity concerns. Estimates of PM2.5-related death and the life-year loss are used to calculate the economic benefits of preventing PM2.5-related deaths.ResultsThe results indicate that a 1 µg/m³ increase in local PM2.5 levels is associated with a 0.17% rise in older (65+) adult mortality rate and a 5.1% increase in respiratory hospitalizations. Significant rises in mortality and morbidity were observed for disease categories directly associated with PM2.5 pollution. Considering only the avoided life years lost, the 5.58 µg/m³ reduction in PM2.5 levels achieved in South Korea from 2015 to 2022 corresponds to an estimated economic benefit of USD 14.14 billion. The study also found that a 1 µg/m³ increase in PM2.5 raised hospital visits by 1.2% for acute upper respiratory infections and 2.6% for lower respiratory infections.ConclusionThese findings highlight the magnitude and cost of the health risks posed by air pollution, underscoring the importance of effective policies to manage air quality. This study provides clear evidence of the harmful effects of air pollution on human health, particularly on vulnerable groups and related diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-29307-8.

Introduction

Air pollution has well-documented adverse effects on individual and public health, prompting governments worldwide to strengthen air quality standards. For example, the United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) lowered the annual standard from 12.0 µg/m³ to 9.0 µg/m³ in February 2024 [1]. The EPA estimates that this revised standard will prevent up to 4,500 premature deaths, avoid 290,000 lost workdays, and generate approximately USD 46 billion in public health benefits by 2032 [2]. Quantifying the health impacts of marginal increases in pollution is essential for understanding the determinants of population health and designing effective environmental policies. Although extensive literature has documented the health consequences of air pollution, reliably estimating the effects of ambient PM2.5 remains challenging due to endogeneity concerns and measurement error [3, 4]. Local pollution levels correlate with urbanization, industrialization, traffic intensity, and meteorological conditions, all of which independently influence health [5]. These relationships introduce endogeneity and measurement error that can bias conventional estimates. Transboundary pollution, however, provides plausibly exogenous variation that is independent of local confounders [3, 4, 6]. This presents an opportunity to isolate the short-term health impacts of PM2.5 exposure.

PM2.5 denotes particulate matter with a diameter of 2.5 micrometers or less [2]. This fraction typically consists of a mixture of various substances, such as sulfates and nitrates, which are formed largely through the combustion of fossil fuels and vehicle emissions [7]. Particulate matter measured in a specific region includes primary particles directly emitted from vehicles, factories, and power plants, as well as secondary particles formed in the atmosphere from precursor gases such as sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides, which can travel long distances and often originate from transboundary sources [8]. Local air quality is also influenced by airborne pollutants transported from neighboring countries via cross-border movement [9, 10]. Pui et al. [8] observed that PM2.5 particles can remain airborne for several days to weeks and travel thousands of kilometers, influenced by pollutant characteristics, geographical conditions, and meteorological factors, resulting in increased concentrations in distant areas.

The long-range transport of PM2.5 in East Asia is governed by well-documented atmospheric mechanisms. During the cold season, the East Asian winter monsoon produces strong northwesterly winds along the eastern flank of the Siberian High, generating cold surges that can carry particulate matter over long distances; observational analyses indicate that PM2.5 driven by such surges can travel up to roughly 2,000 km within two days, with the transport distance depending on wind speed and direction as well as on particle lifetime and deposition velocity [11]. These prevailing wind patterns help explain why episodes of elevated PM2.5 in downwind regions frequently coincide with the winter and early-spring seasons [12, 13]. In the South Korean context, high-PM2.5 episodes have been linked to the formation of high pressure over upwind Chinese regions and low pressure over the Korean Peninsula, accompanied by persistent westerly flow that transports pollutants across the Yellow Sea; back-trajectory and satellite analyses show that ambient PM2.5 concentrations in Korean cities rise as air masses pass over major industrial centers in northeastern China—such as Beijing, Tianjin, and Shenyang—located within roughly 1,000 km of the peninsula [6].

Air pollution in South Korea is influenced by domestic factors, including urbanization, industrialization, and vehicle usage, as well as external sources [14, 15]. Domestic factors can directly affect public health, making it difficult to isolate the effects of air pollution on health outcomes [7, 9, 16]. For example, industrialization and increased vehicle use can cause health deterioration, thereby introducing endogeneity when estimating the impact of air pollution on health. Air pollutants originating from external sources play a crucial role in addressing endogeneity issues while estimating the health impacts of domestic air quality.

Figure 1 illustrates the dominant wind directions in South Korea. As described above, the seasonal predominance of northwesterly winds during the winter monsoon transports externally generated pollutants into specific regions of the peninsula [17, 18]. South Korea therefore serves as an important case study for understanding the impact of transboundary PM2.5, because its downwind geographic position relative to major Chinese source regions makes it particularly sensitive to pollutants carried by these prevailing winds.

The Beijing and Tianjin regions, the major economic centers of China, experience severe air pollution due to rapid urbanization, population growth, and industrialization [21, 22]. These figures provide a visual illustration of the potential pathways by which pollutants from major industrial cities (e.g., Beijing and Tianjin) and ADS source regions (e.g., Bayannur and Ulaanbaatar) may be transported toward South Korea by the dominant wind patterns [23, 24]. We treat these spatial patterns as descriptive and suggestive rather than as direct evidence of the magnitude of transboundary contributions, which is estimated formally through the instrumental-variable analysis. By incorporating exogenous variation in PM2.5 levels, such as PM2.5 transported from China, it is possible to isolate the influence of domestic PM2.5 on health outcomes.

A substantial body of research has examined the health effects of air pollution, and recent work has increasingly relied on quasi-experimental designs to address the endogeneity of pollution exposure. Studies exploiting policy changes, traffic variation, and shifts in wind direction as sources of exogenous variation have documented the effects of particulate matter on mortality and healthcare utilization [25, 26]. In particular, Deryugina et al. [3] used changes in wind direction to identify the mortality and medical-cost effects of acute PM2.5 exposure among the elderly in the United States, while related work has extended such designs to infant and child health outcomes [16, 27]. In the South Korean context, prior studies have documented the contribution of transboundary pollution to domestic air quality and its associated health consequences [9, 28], and cross-national analyses have quantified the international spillovers of air pollution [17, 29].

This study develops a quasi-experimental framework to estimate the short-term effects of ambient PM2.5 on mortality, healthcare utilization, and medical expenditures in South Korea. The analysis draws on microdata from administrative cities, combining detailed daily health records with high-frequency air quality measurements. To address endogeneity, the study employs an instrumental-variables strategy that uses local wind conditions and PM2.5 concentrations measured at upwind monitoring sites outside South Korea to capture exogenous variation in pollution inflows. The resulting estimates are then used to calculate the economic damages associated with PM2.5-induced life years lost and to quantify heterogeneity in these impacts across age groups.

This study makes four contributions to existing literature. First, it provides an age-stratified analysis of the effects of PM2.5 across the entire population, identified through a quasi-experimental IV strategy, whereas prior work has focused primarily on single vulnerable groups, such as infants or the elderly. Second, it utilizes city-level National Health Insurance Service data to investigate acute morbidity effects of PM2.5, specifically upper and lower respiratory infections, thereby improving upon studies that examine broad disease categories or focus only on mortality. Third, it offers evidence on the role of transboundary pollution in overcoming endogeneity challenges in estimating the domestic health impacts of air quality. Finally, it translates reductions in PM2.5 into economic benefits, providing valuable reference points for environmental policymaking in South Korea. Unlike earlier studies that rely on short time periods or small samples to assess domestic pollution [9–12, 18, 20, 28], this analysis leverages comprehensive data to quantify both the health effects and the monetary benefits associated with improved air quality. Specifically, the health effects are translated into economic terms using a conventional value of a statistical life-year [30], and the resulting benefits are compared with public expenditure on climate and air-quality programs [31].

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: the Methods section outlines the data and empirical model; the Results and Policy Implications sections present the findings and their policy relevance; and the final sections discuss the study’s limitations and conclude.

Methods

Air pollution and meteorological data

Air pollution in South Korea

Air pollution data were obtained from the Korea Environment Corporation’s Air Korea database, which provides hourly measurements at the pollution-monitor level. Since 2004, the National Air Quality Information Management System has collected and managed nationwide air quality data, including sulfur dioxide (SO2), carbon monoxide (CO), nitrogen dioxide (NO2), ozone (O3), and particulate matter (PM10), based on measurements from 642 real-time monitoring stations installed across 162 districts and cities. Real-time PM2.5 measurements have been available since 2015, with hourly data reported at each monitoring station. South Korea’s extensive network of air quality monitoring sites, shown in Fig. 3, enables accurate measurement of PM2.5 concentrations across diverse geographic areas, thereby improving the precision of estimates on the effects of air pollution. This study focused on monitors with daily PM2.5 concentration readings from 2015 to 2022. Hourly measurements from each monitor were first aggregated to the daily level by averaging the hourly pollution readings. A daily average was used to provide a more representative measure of overall exposure to PM2.5 throughout the day, reducing the potential for outlier hours to skew results. City-level daily pollution measures were then constructed by averaging all monitor-specific daily concentrations within each city in South Korea. Specifically, the city-level measure was calculated as the simple arithmetic daily mean of pollution concentrations across all monitoring stations within each city, without applying additional spatial weighting or interpolation. All cities included in the analysis had at least one monitoring station throughout the study period.

Air pollution in China

Daily PM2.5 pollution measures for China were constructed using data from the World Air Quality Index (WAQI) monitoring network. This database provides hourly and daily pollution levels from air quality monitors operated by environmental protection agencies worldwide and includes information on the precise location of each monitor. Chinese monitoring stations were selected based on the South Korea–China Joint Asian Dust Storm Monitoring Network, which operates dust monitoring stations in China’s source regions and along its transit routes—prioritizing stations in the northeastern and eastern provinces of China that are major contributors to transboundary pollution—to ensure relevance for explaining variations in South Korea’s PM2.5 pollution. The suitability of the selected external monitoring stations was further supported by an atmospheric transport and dispersion model (see Figure A3 in the Appendix). Fig. 4 presents the locations of the Chinese PM2.5 monitors used in this analysis, along with distance buffers centered on South Korea.

PM2.5 monitoring sites in China with Distance buffers centered on South Korea. Notes: This figure illustrates the locations of PM2.5 monitoring sites established under the South Korea-China Joint Asian Dust Storm Monitoring Network and the distance buffers centered on South Korea.

Daily external PM2.5 pollution measures were constructed by weighting pollution levels at each Chinese monitoring site using the inverse of its distance to South Korea’s centroid. This approach assigned greater weight to monitors located closer to South Korea, thereby more accurately capturing the relationship between pollution concentration and geographic proximity [4, 5]. For example, PM2.5 levels in Dandong exerted a stronger influence on South Korea’s ambient air quality than levels in Xilingolmeng due to Dandong’s closer proximity. It should be emphasized that these external measures are used to construct the instrument rather than to predict local exposure in Korea; domestic PM2.5 exposure is measured directly from Korea’s monitoring network. Accordingly, the role of external data is to provide exogenous variation independent of local confounders, and the instrument’s validity depends on this exogeneity rather than on the absolute accuracy of the external concentrations.

Weather conditions

Weather data from 2015 to 2022 were obtained from the MET Data Portal managed by the Korea Meteorological Administration (KMA). The analysis used weather data from land-based stations, incorporating measurements from the Automated Synoptic Observing System (ASOS) and Automatic Weather Stations (AWS). These systems provided hourly meteorological observations, including temperature, precipitation, and wind speed and direction. Hourly weather measurements from each monitoring station were averaged to create daily station-level observations. Daily city-level weather variables were constructed by averaging the daily measurements across all monitoring stations within each city. The wind direction variable is defined as the direction from which the wind blows, and its circular nature is accounted for when computing averages. Mortality and morbidity data

This study focuses on the short-term (acute) health effects of PM2.5 for two reasons. First, the identification strategy exploits high-frequency, day-to-day variation in transboundary pollution driven by wind direction; this plausibly exogenous variation is well suited to identifying acute responses to short-term exposure, but not the effects of chronic exposure, which unfold over years and require a distinct identification approach [32]. Second, acute responses to short-term pollution episodes—such as episode-driven increases in mortality and hospital visits—carry direct policy relevance for air-quality warning systems and short-term interventions. Estimating the long-term effects of chronic exposure lies beyond the scope of this study and is left for future research.

Mortality

Mortality data were constructed using the Cause-of-Death Statistics and Resident Registration Status data, derived from death certificates collected between 2015 and 2022. The cause-of-death records included individual-level information such as the date and time of death, age, sex, city of residence, and primary cause of death classified according to the Korean Standard Classification of Disease (KCD), the Korean adaptation of the WHO’s International Classification of Diseases (ICD) used to classify diseases and causes of death in health statistics.

Daily deaths in each city were identified for all-cause mortality and disaggregated by cause of death using Statistics Korea’s 104-item classification of causes of death, which is based on the KCD. Specifically, respiratory diseases (codes 5, 6, 74–78), cardiovascular diseases (codes 66–72), other internal diseases (codes 20, 53, 61, 80, 81, 89–92), comprising transport accidents (code 97) and other external causes (codes 98–104), were identified [3]. The corresponding ICD-10 codes are provided in Appendix Table A1. The individual hourly death records were aggregated to the city level to obtain daily death counts.

Mortality rates were calculated by dividing the number of deaths by cause and age group in each city by the population of the corresponding demographic subgroup. The resulting ratio was multiplied by 1 million to obtain the number of deaths per million people, which served as the mortality variable for this analysis. The unit of observation in this analysis is the city-day. For each city-day, mortality rates were calculated separately by age group and cause of death, so that multiple age-specific mortality variables (e.g., 65+, 65–69, infant, child, adult) are defined within the same city-day observation rather than corresponding to separate subsamples.

Morbidity

Morbidity data were obtained from the National Health Insurance Sharing Service (NHISS) for the period 2016–2019 [33]; this window reflects data availability, as the NHISS releases finalized microdata only after a substantial processing lag, and 2016–2019 was the most recent finalized dataset available at the time of analysis. The NHISS provides daily individual-level information on healthcare utilization, including hospital visits and medical costs, for a representative sample of 1 million NHI beneficiaries. The NHI is a mandatory system covering all South Korean residents, with about 97% of the population—approximately 48 million people—enrolled [34]. The dataset contains detailed information for each beneficiary, including identification numbers, sex, age, city of residence, dates of medical visits, primary and secondary diagnoses, and medical expenditures, all recorded according to the KCD. Medical costs were measured as the total medical expenditures recorded in the NHISS claims data for each visit, aggregated to the city-day level based on beneficiaries’ city of residence and date of service.

Hospital visit records and medical costs were aggregated to the city-day level based on beneficiaries’ city of residence and date of service. The numerator of the morbidity measure is the number of claims (i.e., hospital visits/cases) rather than the number of unique patients; because the insurance data record each instance of care, repeated visits by the same beneficiary are counted as separate cases. Morbidity rates were calculated by converting hospitalization counts to rates using resident registration status data, disaggregated by city and month-year. Due to data access and extraction constraints, the analysis focused on cases where respiratory diseases, known to be particularly sensitive to short-term PM2.5 exposure, were the primary reason for hospital visits [35]. Respiratory-related hospital visits were defined as those with a primary diagnosis under KCD codes J00–J99, corresponding to diseases of the respiratory system under the WHO’s International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) (see Appendix Table A2). Respiratory diseases were selected as the primary outcome of interest because they are particularly sensitive to short-term PM2.5 exposure [3].

To address these concerns, the study employed an instrumental variable (IV) strategy, instrumenting local PM2.5 using transported PM2.5 from Chinese sources. External PM2.5 was calculated using inverse-distance weighting, assigning greater weight to external monitoring stations closer to South Korea, as shown in Fig. 4, with distances measured to the centroid of South Korea. The northwesterly wind variable equaled 1 if the daily average wind direction in a city fell within [260–360°, 0–20°], and 0 otherwise. This range corresponds to the arc of directions from which winds blow from northeastern China—the principal source region for transboundary PM2.5—and from the Gobi and Mongolian Plateau source regions of ADS, across the Yellow Sea toward the Korean Peninsula. It is consistent with the main transport pathways documented in atmospheric studies, which identify northwesterly flow as a dominant synoptic condition associated with high-PM2.5 episodes transported from China to Korea [6, 11, 13], and with the coverage of the Korea–China Joint Asian Dust Storm Monitoring Network (Figs. 1 and 2, and 4). Sensitivity tests with narrower wind-direction ranges yielded results similar to those of the main specification, supporting the robustness of this parameterization. The interaction captured the combined effect of transported PM2.5 and prevailing northwesterly winds, while also enabling each component to contribute individually to the regression.

The rationale for this interaction rests on the physical mechanism of transboundary transport: elevated pollution at upwind external sources raises local levels in a Korean city only when the prevailing winds carry those pollutants toward the peninsula. The interaction between external PM2.5 and the northwesterly-wind indicator therefore isolates the component of local PM2.5 attributable to transboundary inflow, consistent with atmospheric evidence on northwesterly-driven transport to Korea [6, 36] and with quasi-experimental studies that use wind direction as exogenous variation in pollution exposure [3, 4]. Because these winds are not plausibly driven by a city’s own emissions, the interaction captures variation in local PM2.5 that is largely orthogonal to domestic sources, supporting the exclusion restriction. This approach also helps mitigate measurement error in local pollution data: because classical measurement error in station readings is, by definition, independent of true exposure, it is unlikely to be correlated with pollution at external sources used to construct the instrument [38]. This restriction minimized the influence of local pollution variations, ensuring that the observed variation in Eq. Instrument strength was confirmed by large first-stage F-statistics reported in all regression tables, indicating that the IV effectively explains variation in endogenous PM2.5 and minimizes weak instrument bias, supporting the reliability of the two-stage least squares (2SLS) estimates.

All data processing and statistical analyses were conducted in R version 4.4.1. The two-stage least squares estimation with high-dimensional fixed effects and city-clustered standard errors was implemented using the feols function of the fixest package and the felm function of the lfe package, and the first-stage F-statistics were computed using the fixest package.

Results and discussion

Baseline characteristics of study variables

Table 1 presents descriptive statistics for the primary variables analyzed over the study period, across 479,572 city-day observations. This final analytic sample covers 247 administrative cities from 2015 to 2022. It is smaller than a fully balanced panel (247 cities × 2,922 days = 721,734 city-days) because city-days with missing PM2.5 or meteorological data were excluded, as PM2.5 monitoring was introduced on a rolling basis across cities and contains gaps. Age- and cause-specific mortality and morbidity rates are separate variables defined within the same set of city-day observations, rather than corresponding to distinct subsamples. The average daily PM2.5 concentration was 20.3 µg/m³, with a standard deviation of 13.5 µg/m³. Among the entire elderly population (65 and older), the mean three-day total mortality rate was 215 deaths per million, with a standard deviation of 105. Mortality rates increased with age, from 61.7 deaths per million in the 65–67 age group to 822 deaths per million in the 85 and above group. In addition to age-specific analysis, the study examined the differential effects of PM2.5 exposure on respiratory-related hospital utilization classified by KCD codes. Analyzing PM2.5 impact on elderly mortality: different time windows

The baseline specification employed a three-day window to capture the short-term effect of PM2.5 on elderly mortality. This analysis was extended by examining different time windows to explore whether the impact persists beyond three days. Two potential outcomes were considered. First, estimates may increase if deaths are delayed beyond the initial three days, capturing additional mortality and reducing underestimation. Second, estimates may decrease due to short-term mortality displacement, where individuals close to death succumb earlier due to PM2.5 exposure, limiting additional deaths in extended windows [47].

To evaluate these effects, the impact of a one-day increase in PM2.5 on elderly mortality over the subsequent one to seventeen days was estimated, controlling for relevant weather variables (see Fig. 6). Fig. 6 shows that estimates of elderly mortality initially increased as the observation window was extended, suggesting that short-term mortality displacement does not substantially affect the results. Beyond a certain duration, the rate of increase slowed, indicating that the acute effects of PM2.5 exposure stabilize, with no further mortality observed beyond a specific point.

Methodologically, the IV estimates exceed their OLS counterparts—consistent with attenuation bias from measurement error that the transboundary instrument helps correct—and the absence of effects beyond a three-day lag indicates an acute rather than accumulated response. Epidemiologically, the concentration of mortality effects among the elderly aligns with evidence that older adults are especially susceptible to the cardiovascular and respiratory consequences of PM2.5, owing to a higher prevalence of pre-existing cardiopulmonary conditions and reduced physiological reserve [48]. Policy Implications

The results demonstrate the significant impact of short-term PM2.5 exposure on health outcomes, highlighting the critical role of environmental regulations in improving air quality and protecting public health. Estimating the short-term effects of PM2.5 exposure is essential for assessing the economic efficiency of air quality policies. In particular, quantifying the direct and indirect economic damages of air pollution enables policymakers to incorporate the projected benefits of improved air quality into the design and evaluation of environmental regulations [51–53].

This study quantifies the economic benefits of reducing PM2.5 levels by estimating age-specific life-year losses. These losses are derived by combining the IV estimates from Table 5 with life expectancy data from South Korea’s Complete Life Table. The results indicate that a 1 µg/m³ increase in PM2.5 concentration leads to a loss of 3.45 life years per million elderly individuals over a three-day period, whereas the same increase results in a loss of 1.06 life years per million adults over three days.

A back-of-the-envelope calculation illustrates the policy relevance of these findings. Between 2015 and 2022, the national annual average PM2.5 concentration decreased by 5.58 µg/m³ (Panel B, Fig. 2). This reduction is estimated to yield an additional 79,208 life years annually among the adult population and 62,213 life years among the elderly. The economic benefit for each age group was calculated as: Economic Benefit = Life-Year Loss per 1 µg/m³ (per million persons) × 365 days × Population (in millions) × PM2.5 Reduction (5.58 µg/m³) × Value per Statistical Life-Year (VSLY). Applying a VSLY of USD 100,000 [30], this calculation yields an economic benefit of approximately USD 14.14 billion—USD 7.92 billion for the adult population and USD 6.22 billion for the elderly (Appendix Table A5). This estimate reflects the short-term benefits of reduced PM2.5 exposure, implying that the total benefit, including long-term effects, would likely be larger.

During the same period, the South Korean government allocated a total of USD 11.5 billion to the Ministry of Environment for climate, air quality, and environmental safety initiatives [31]. Therefore, the estimated economic benefit of USD 14.14 billion is comparable to the government’s total year investment, underscoring the cost-effectiveness of air-quality improvement policies.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the analysis relies on city-day-level data rather than individual-level exposure and outcome data. This aggregate structure means that the estimates capture population-level responses to ambient PM2.5 and may not directly translate into individual-level risks, a concern commonly known as the ecological fallacy. In addition, station-based city averages are an imperfect proxy for personal exposure, potentially introducing measurement error. Differences in instrumentation and measurement protocols across countries may likewise introduce error into the external data used to construct the instrument. However, because such classical measurement error is plausibly independent of the instrument, it is unlikely to bias the two-stage least squares estimates and, if anything, reduces the likelihood of detecting significant effects.

The instrumental-variable strategy employed in this study addresses the endogeneity of local pollution and mitigates attenuation bias arising from classical measurement error; accordingly, the estimates are best interpreted as capturing population-level effects identified under the assumptions of instrument relevance and the exclusion restriction, rather than as establishing individual-level relationships. Second, data access constraints necessitated a temporal mismatch between the mortality (2015–2022) and morbidity (2016–2019) analyses, and the focus on respiratory hospital visits likely underestimates the total morbidity burden. Third, the limited statistical power in detecting mortality impacts among infants and children reflects South Korea’s demographic profile rather than a definitive absence of risk. Despite these constraints, the transboundary instrumental-variable strategy provides a quasi-experimental framework for evaluating the health consequences of short-term PM2.5 exposure and the cost-effectiveness of regional air quality interventions.

Conclusions

Air pollution, particularly fine particulate matter (PM2.5), is a well-established public health concern, prompting the implementation of stringent air quality policies worldwide. Accurately estimating the health impacts of PM2.5 exposure is crucial for developing effective environmental policies. However, identifying the relationships between PM2.5 exposure and health outcomes remains challenging due to endogeneity issues and measurement errors.

This study leverages transboundary air pollution as a source of exogenous variation to isolate the impact of PM2.5 on health outcomes in South Korea. The results show that short-term PM2.5 exposure markedly increases mortality, hospital visits, and medical costs, with the largest effects observed among older populations. By integrating age-specific life expectancies with estimated PM2.5 effects, the study quantifies life-year losses and demonstrates the economic benefits of reducing PM2.5 pollution. The nationwide reduction in PM2.5 levels between 2015 and 2022 is estimated to have contributed to mortality reductions valued at USD 14.14 billion.

This study also emphasizes the generalizability and applicability of the methodological framework for estimating mortality costs and identifying vulnerable populations. The analysis of life-year losses and treatment effect heterogeneity highlights the need for policies tailored to the most at-risk populations. These findings provide a robust foundation for evidence-based environmental policy, underscoring the importance of sustained efforts to reduce PM2.5 exposure and protect public health, particularly among the most vulnerable groups.

These findings underscore the critical necessity of transboundary cooperation in air quality management, as local health outcomes are demonstrably linked to regional pollutant transport. By offering quasi-experimental evidence on the relationship between short-term PM2.5 exposure and multi-dimensional health costs, this study provides a framework for evaluating the cost-effectiveness of environmental regulations and targeted interventions for vulnerable populations. Future research should build upon this model by investigating the long-term cumulative effects of chronic exposure on life expectancy and expanding the morbidity analysis to include non-respiratory disease categories as data accessibility improves. Additionally, applying this transboundary instrumental variable approach to other regions could further validate the global scalability of this methodology for assessing the true societal burden of air pollution.
