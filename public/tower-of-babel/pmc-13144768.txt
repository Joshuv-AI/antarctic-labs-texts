Perceived Discrimination Among Patients With Head and Neck Cancer
Authors: Thomas P Hoag, Gabriella Boone, Matthew Simpson, Lisa Vélez‐Vélez, Angela Mazul, Sean T Massa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveHead and neck cancer (HNC) accounts for 5% of U.S. cancers, causing ~14,400 deaths annually. Racial minorities and socioeconomically disadvantaged HNC patients experience worse outcomes, presenting at advanced stages and receiving less effective treatment. Even after adjusting for socioeconomic status, insurance, and disease severity, racial and ethnic disparities persist, suggesting additional contributing factors. Perceived discrimination may drive these disparities. This study examines associations between perceived discrimination and demographic, racial, and oncologic factors in HNC patients, hypothesizing associations with race and later‐stage diagnoses.MethodsSeventy two HNC patients diagnosed and treated in the past 5 years were recruited. Discrimination was measured using the Discrimination in Medical Settings Scale (DMS), scoring from 7 (no discrimination) to 35 (high discrimination). Patient characteristics and DMS scores were summarized, and associations were analyzed using general linear models. Qualitative insights were collected to guide future research.ResultsMost participants were Non‐Hispanic White (86.1%), male (68.1%), with an average DMS score of 9.1. Black patients reported lower (nonsignificant) discrimination scores than White patients. Lower‐income patients reported significantly higher discrimination scores than high‐income patients. No association was found between DMS scores and cancer stage. Qualitative data revealed higher discrimination perceptions among patients with stigmatized histories (e.g., substance use) and lower perceptions among those with limited healthcare exposure.ConclusionsPerceived discrimination in HNC patients is most strongly associated with income, with lower‐income patients reporting higher levels of discrimination. Addressing income‐related barriers and health‐related social needs may help to mitigate perceived discrimination in this population.Level of Evidence3.Keywords: head and neck, healthcare disparities, healthcare quality assessments

Introduction

Head and neck Cancer (HNC) accounts for an estimated 5% of all malignancies in the United States, causing approximately 14,400 deaths annually [1]. Significant disparities in HNC outcomes exist, with racial minorities and patients of lower socioeconomic status presenting at more advanced stages, receiving inferior treatments, and experiencing worse survival rates [2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. Although HNC affects more White individuals, Black patients tend to be younger, have lower incomes, live farther from treatment centers, and have less education and insurance coverage. These factors may contribute to delays in diagnosis and poorer outcomes [3, 7]. Disparities in head and neck cancer (HNC) are often linked to historically dominant risk factors such as tobacco and alcohol use. While cigarette smoking has declined overall, higher rates persist among Black individuals with lower education levels [8, 9]. Lower socioeconomic status is also an independent risk factor for alcohol use disorder [10]. Furthermore, the rise of Human Papillomavirus (HPV), particularly type 16, has introduced further disparity. HPV‐positive oropharyngeal cancer, more common in white patients, responds better to treatment, and offers a more favorable prognosis [2, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. However, the American Cancer Society (ACS) Cancer Facts and Figures of 2024 reports the persistence of racial and ethnic disparities in cancer outcomes, even when SES, health insurance status, age, severity of disease, and health status are comparable [16]. This suggests additional factors are at play [16] Despite the well‐documented disparities in HNC outcomes, the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. Research often focuses on factors like HPV status, insurance coverage, and stage at diagnosis, which are available in large registry datasets. As a result, unmeasured causal variables and confounders are often overlooked. The American Association for Cancer Research (AACR) 2024 Cancer Disparities Progress Report cites perceived discrimination as one driver of disparities in cancer related outcomes including delays in diagnosis and treatment initiation [17]. Studies show that Black patients report higher perceived discrimination in healthcare, which correlates with reduced healthcare utilization and worse outcomes [18, 19]. This study aims to characterize perceived discrimination among HNC patients to understand the racial, oncologic, and demographic factors associated with this discrimination. We hypothesize that perceived discrimination varies by self‐reported race and that higher perceived discrimination is associated with delays, specifically, more advanced stage at diagnosis. These results will inform strategies to reduce these disparities and improve patient outcomes.

Materials and Methods

Data Source

Patients with a diagnosis of head and neck cancer of any histology treated at least 3 months but no more than 5 years prior to the survey date were recruited from the Saint Louis University HNC clinic. REDCap (https://projectredcap.org/software/) was used to record, store, and anonymize patient data. Patients were recruited and administered the survey in clinic, and informed consent was obtained from all study participants prior to participation. Additional patient data, including demographic information and oncologic variables, was gathered through their electronic health record (EHR). Patients were excluded for known or suspected recurrence, or inability to complete the questionnaire. The research protocol, including all proposed human procedures, was reviewed and approved by the Saint Louis University Institutional Review Board (IRB), ensuring compliance with all applicable regulations governing human subject research, and allowing the commencement of study activities.

Measures

Discrimination was measured using the Discrimination in Medical Settings Scale (DMS), adapted from the Everyday Discrimination Scale (EDS). The DMS is a validated measure of patients' perceived discrimination in the healthcare setting [20]. The DMS survey (Table 1) has 7 questions, each of which can be answered on a scale of 1 (“never”) to 5 (“always”). The minimum total score possible is 7, corresponding to “Never” selected for all 7 questions and the maximum score is 35 corresponding to “Always” selected for all 7 questions.

Other self‐reported patient characteristics of interest included race/ethnicity (Non‐Hispanic Black, Non‐Hispanic White), sex (female, male), and income ($14,999 or less, $15,000 to $49,999, $50,000 to $99,999, $100,000 or more, did not respond). Demographic and oncologic characteristics collected from the EHR included age at diagnosis (years), insurance (Medicaid/other public, Medicare, Private, Uninsured), whether the patient lives in a metropolitan area of 1 million or more residents, cancer site (non‐HPV‐related mucosal, HPV‐positive oropharynx, other), stage at presentation (unstaged, I, II, III, IV), histologic type (basal cell carcinoma, salivary/mucoepidermoid carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, thyroid), surgical treatment (yes, no), radiation therapy (yes, no), chemotherapy (yes, no), and days from biopsy to surgery among those who received surgery.

Statistical Analysis

Overall patient characteristics were summarized using counts and percentages or means and standard deviations (SD) as appropriate. The distribution of response values to each DMS question was summarized as counts and percentages. Mean DMS scores for different patient groups were summarized using means and SDs. General linear models estimated associations of patient characteristics with mean DMS score. Bivariable results are reported as crude mean differences (CMD). To minimize overfitting concerns, variables that were either theoretically associated with DMS score or were associated with DMS score in the crude analysis were included in a multivariable general linear model, with results reported as adjusted mean differences (AMD). Because the skewed nature of DMS scores may have increased the probability of false positive results, the standard α of 0.05 was lowered to 0.025, and 97.5% confidence intervals (CIs) are reported for CMDs/AMDs. Patient characteristics stratified by stage at presentation were summarized using counts and percentages or means and SDs as appropriate; cumulative logit regression estimated associations of relevant patient characteristics with having a later stage at presentation among patients who received a stage, with bivariable results reported as crude odds ratios (CORs) and multivariable results reported as adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% CIs. All CIs were created using robust standard errors to mitigate heteroscedasticity or overdispersion as appropriate.

Statistical analysis was performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA). All results were two‐tailed. Results were considered statistically significant if the CI did not overlap with 0.

Qualitative Data

In addition to the planned quantitative analysis, many participants volunteered qualitative information on their experiences with discrimination that was not captured by the study instrument. Considering the dearth of literature on this topic, data from this exploratory qualitative analysis are reported in the results to guide future study.

Results

Patient Characteristics

There were 72 HNC patients included in the study. Most patients were Non‐Hispanic White (86.1%), male (68.1%), from metropolitan areas with 1 million or more residents (87.5%), had non‐HPV‐related mucosal cancer (61.1%), had squamous cell carcinoma (87.5%), received surgery (86.1%), received radiation (61.1%), and did not receive chemotherapy (62.5%), with an average age of 62.9 years (SD 12.9). Half of the patients had Medicare insurance (50.0%). The most common income group was $50,000 to $99,999 (26.4%), and the most common stage at presentation was I (36.1%) (Table 1).

DMS Score

The average DMS score was 9.1 (SD 3.2). DMS scores were very right skewed, with over half (52.8%) of patients responding “Never” to all questions (DMS score = 7). The third quartile of DMS scores was 10, and the highest score was 21 out of a possible highest score of 35 (Figure 1).

Qualitative Data

The highest DMS score, 21, was reported by a White, middle class, female—a demographic considered advantaged. However, she reported discrimination within the healthcare setting due to her history as a former intravenous drug user with multiple hospitalizations for overdoses and suicidality. This history was not captured in the variables we collected and despite many years of sobriety and good health, her past experiences led to significant perceived discrimination.

For many patients, their cancer diagnosis and treatment marked their first consistent interaction with the healthcare system. These patients often focused on their positive experiences with their HNC providers and staff, and reported lower levels of perceived discrimination, although they admitted they had few healthcare interactions to base their responses on. In contrast, some patients described experiences of perceived discrimination in other medical settings such as primary care or dental visits, shaping their expectations for future care. While not statistically measured, these narratives suggest that perceptions of discrimination may accumulate and influence future patient‐provider relationships across multiple healthcare settings.

Some participants described conversations surrounding insurance coverage, treatment costs, and scheduling conflicts during healthcare interactions created feelings of being perceived as difficult or burdensome. However, these experiences were often not interpreted as overt discrimination. While these dynamics may reduce healthcare engagement and influence health outcomes, they were not reflected in the DMS score.

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the association between self‐reported race and perceived healthcare discrimination among HNC patients, as well as its relationship with stage of presentation as a proxy for delayed diagnosis.

Our results showed no significant difference in perceived discrimination between HNC patients of different races; in fact, Black patients reported less discrimination, although this difference was not statistically significant after controlling for age at diagnosis, sex, and income. This differs from prior research outside the HNC population demonstrating higher discrimination among racial minorities [18, 19, 21, 22]. These results may be explained by an over‐representation of White patients within our study population, which is typical for a HNC cohort. Black patients have fewer interactions with the healthcare system over their lifetime compared to White patients [23, 24]. This may reduce their opportunities to encounter discrimination. However, patients with less frequent healthcare interactions are objectively more vulnerable and have been shown in other studies to be less likely to seek immediate treatment, to present at later stages, and experience worse treatment outcomes [25]. While some of these patients may not encounter overt discrimination in healthcare settings, their reduced healthcare exposure may increase their risk of poor outcomes. Patients who do report increased discrimination have been shown to utilize the healthcare system less and are more likely to miss follow‐up appointments, lab checks, and repeat scans [4, 26]. Our cohort did not capture patients who do not attend follow‐up appointments; it is possible these patients may have offered a different perspective on discrimination.

While most of the literature has demonstrated racial differences in discrimination within the healthcare system, few studies have reported mixed findings, with some no longer identifying significant disparities by race [27, 28]. This evolving pattern may reflect ongoing changes in healthcare diversity and broader social awareness of equity and inclusion. It is possible that perceived discrimination is not experienced uniformly within racial groups across the contemporary medical setting. Evidence also demonstrates that the relationship between race and discrimination is confounded by socioeconomic factors impacting patient perceptions [29].

DMS score in this study was not associated with stage at presentation. This suggests discrimination may not be directly translatable to later stage diagnosis as hypothesized in our patient population. Using stage as a proxy for delayed care and therefore later stage diagnosis may not capture the consequences of discrimination on adverse health outcomes. Rather, discrimination may have a more significant impact on downstream healthcare engagement, including initiation of treatment, continuity of care, or reduced trust in providers [26]. It is also likely that stage at presentation is influenced by a multitude of biologic and clinical factors and does not always represent a preventable delay in diagnosis. These complexities may obscure any measurable effect of psychosocial factors (discrimination) on this variable if such effects exist.

Our data suggests income may be more salient to perceived discrimination than race, with lower‐income HNC patients reporting more perceived discrimination within healthcare settings. Other studies have shown similar results within the general patient population [30, 31, 32]. Lower‐income individuals utilize health resources less and experience worse health outcomes [5, 8, 33, 34, 35, 36]. Commonly cited barriers include difficulties obtaining childcare, accessing public transportation, inadequate insurance coverage, and high costs and copays [37, 38]. The AACR Cancer Disparities Progress Report found patients who experience financial toxicity from healthcare are at greater risk of mortality, regardless of insurance status [39]. Financial toxicity is defined by the financial burden and distress experienced by patients as a result of medical care costs, particularly in serious or chronic illnesses such as cancer [40]. The three components of financial toxicity include material hardship (including medical debt), psychological distress, and maladaptive coping behaviors such as foregoing necessary care, medications, or basic needs due to cost concerns. Patients who face financial toxicity are more likely to delay diagnoses or forgo recommended treatments, creating disparities in care quality based on socioeconomic status rather than medical need [41]. Perceived discrimination is more prevalent among lower‐income populations and may be associated with decreased healthcare utilization and poorer health outcomes. Financial toxicity may intersect with perceived discrimination by shaping healthcare interactions in ways that reflect structural socioeconomic inequities. For example, these patients may have to engage in frequent discussions about costs, insurance, or delayed care, altering patient‐provider communication, reinforcing power imbalances, or exposure to stigma related to socioeconomic status. This overlap suggests that financial toxicity may contribute to disparities beyond cost barriers but also be reinforcing experiences of discrimination that hinder engagement within the healthcare system.

Notably, the association between income and discrimination was not strictly linear. The lowest and second‐highest income groups reported higher levels of discrimination, whereas the second‐lowest income group did not differ significantly from the highest income group. This pattern may reflect differences in expectations, healthcare exposure, or access to support across income levels. Individuals in moderate‐income ranges may experience financial strain without qualifying for assistance programs, potentially heightening sensitivity to financial toxicity. Prior research has demonstrated a significant decline in marketplace insurance enrollment among individuals earning approximately 120%–150% of the federal poverty level (FPL), with the greatest impact observed near 138% FPL [42]. This coverage gap aligns closely with our second‐highest income group, while the second‐lowest income group would likely fall within Medicaid expansion eligibility thresholds. Although individuals in the lowest income group may qualify for Medicaid, out‐of‐pocket costs such as copays and other basic healthcare expenses may still contribute to financial burden. Differences in healthcare utilization and perceptions of negative experiences across socioeconomic groups may also influence how discrimination is reported. These findings warrant further investigation in a larger cohort.

It is crucial to recognize the complex and varied circumstances that shape our patients' healthcare experiences and perceptions of discrimination. Our qualitative data suggests the experience of discrimination in healthcare is nuanced and cannot be entirely characterized by patient demographics. While DMS score provides important insights, it does not fully capture the influence of individual experiences on perceived discrimination in the health care system. This highlights the opportunity to expand the line of questioning in future studies to encompass unique social determinants and health‐related social needs associated with discrimination.

Understanding the causative factors that contribute to disparities among HNC patients is necessary to achieve equity in treatment and is vital to the improvement in health outcomes for all patients. It is highly possible that perceived discrimination is one causative factor contributing to these disparities. The AACR 2024 Report highlights interventions designed to eliminate discrimination and disparities and improve outcomes for patients. These include broadening the racial and ethnic backgrounds of providers and increasing reach to underrepresented populations for participation in research trials [17]. Eliminating incidences of discrimination within the healthcare setting is imperative in the mission to achieve better outcomes for our vulnerable patients.

Limitations

This study was limited by both sample size and distribution. The high variance in DMS scores among White patients, compared to Black patients, complicates accurate analysis and interpretation. Future studies should include a larger sample size of both Black and White patients to accommodate the large potential skew in DMS scores. Additionally, all patients surveyed were head and neck cancer survivors, which introduces a selection bias, as those who have successfully completed their treatment may be less inclined to reflect negatively on their healthcare experiences. Future research should aim to survey patients at the time of biopsy or the start of treatment and follow them as a prospective cohort throughout their treatment course. This approach will also enable tracking of missed appointments from diagnosis through treatment, providing insight into social determinants that may create barriers to patient care.

Because this dataset did not include detailed care timeline variables, stage at presentation was used as a proxy for delays in care. The addition of more precise assessments of care timelines, such as time from referral to consultation, time to surgery or adjuvant therapy, missed appointments, or treatment interruptions, may better capture the ways in which perceived discrimination influences healthcare engagement and outcomes.

Finally, discrimination is a complex and deeply personal experience that cannot be fully captured by a single survey. The social determinants of health that influence patient outcomes are vast and varied, and our data set only partially represents these factors.

Future Direction

Our analysis points to income as an important factor contributing to perceived discrimination. An ongoing follow‐up investigation will examine specific income‐related barriers that may contribute to negative healthcare experiences. These may include the number of missed appointments related to lack of transportation or inability to miss work and healthcare avoidance due to high co‐pays. While income may represent one social determinant, there are many others which may have a profound impact on our patients' perception of their healthcare relationships and ultimately their outcomes. The study mentioned above will investigate associations between DMS score and objective measures of healthcare utilization and clinical outcomes including treatment delays (time from biopsy to surgery and time from surgery to adjuvant therapy), total treatment duration, missed appointments, prolonged hospitalizations, and treatment‐related complications. Finally, future studies should assess whether perceived discrimination results in long‐term outcomes disparities related to morbidity, recurrence rates, and survival [43, 44, 45, 46]. Eventually, interventions such as sliding scale payment systems, increased flexibility in scheduling, and improved communication regarding diagnosis and treatment timelines may lead to better outcomes, especially for vulnerable patient groups.

Conclusion

This study sought to examine the social factors contributing to disparities in head and neck cancer outcomes, with a focus on the role of perceived healthcare discrimination. We hypothesized that discrimination is one factor driving these disparities and explored its relationship to patient demographics among head and neck cancer survivors. This study found perceived discrimination among head and neck cancer patients is influenced by a complex interplay of factors beyond race, including socioeconomic status and increased health‐related social needs. Our results suggest that income plays a significant role in shaping patients' perceptions of discrimination, underscoring the need to address systemic barriers such as financial toxicity which can lead to treatment gaps and inadequate healthcare utilization. Qualitative analysis indicates that patients with stigmatized conditions and greater health‐related social needs experience more discrimination. Addressing both income‐related barriers and the stigmatization of vulnerable patients is crucial to eliminating discrimination and achieving equitable outcomes for all head and neck cancer patients.
