The effect of 12 weeks of High Intensity Functional Training (HIFT) on serum mitokines and metabolic risk factors in patients with metabolic syndrome: a randomized control trial
Authors: Rasoul Eslami, Mohammad Moayedi, Bakhtyar Tartibian, Ali Aeen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMetabolic syndrome is a condition associated with mitochondrial dysfunction. Mitokines such as FGF-21 and GDF-15, recognized as biomarkers associated with metabolic stress, play crucial roles in regulating energy and inflammation. This study aimed to investigate the effects of High-Intensity Functional Training (HIFT) on the serum levels of FGF-21 and GDF-15, metabolic risk factors and body composition in middle-aged men with metabolic syndrome.MethodsIn a randomized controlled trial, 24 men (age = 42.40 ± 6.90 years) with metabolic syndrome were randomly assigned to either the HIFT group (n = 12) or the control group (n = 12). The HIFT group performed exercise sessions twice a week for 12 weeks, with each session lasting 50 min. Anthropometric and biochemical assessments were conducted before and after the intervention.ResultsAfter the intervention, both primary outcomes—serum FGF-21 and GDF-15 levels—significantly decreased in the HIFT group (P = 0.001). Furthermore, body fat percentage, fasting blood glucose, HbA1c, blood lipids, and LDL-C decreased, whereas skeletal muscle mass and HDL increased (P < 0.001).ConclusionExercise training as HIFT was associated with improvements in select metabolic biomarkers by modulating the serum levels of mitokines, metabolic risk factors and body composition. These findings are associative and do not confirm direct mechanistic effects. Therefore, this type of training may be considered as a potential adjunctive non-pharmacological approach for the management of metabolic syndrome in middle-aged men, but further research with larger samples and active control groups is required.Trial registrationIranian Registry of Clinical Trials identifier: IRCT20241130063899N1, prospectively registered 01-11- 2025, https://irct.behdasht.gov.ir/trial/80642.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13102-026-01905-4.

Introduction

Metabolic syndrome (MetS), formerly known as insulin resistance syndrome or syndrome X, is a complex metabolic disorder comprising a cluster of risk factors, such as central obesity, dyslipidemia, elevated blood pressure, and insulin resistance, that significantly increase the risk of type 2 diabetes (T2DM) and cardiovascular disease (CVD) [1–4]. The prevalence of this syndrome is increasing worldwide, including in Iran, where factors such as genetics, unhealthy diet, sedentary lifestyle, and rising obesity play significant roles in its onset [5–6]. Approximately one in three Iranian adults (30.4%) is affected by MetS, with disproportionately higher burden among women and specific high-risk populations [7].

In recent years, the role of mitochondrial dysfunction in the pathophysiology of metabolic diseases has gained increasing attention. Mitochondria, which serve as cellular energy production centers, play a vital role in regulating glucose and lipid metabolism and inflammatory responses [8]. Given the emerging role of mitochondrial dysfunction in the pathophysiology of MetS, recent research has focused on circulating proteins known as mitokines. Mitokines are a set of circulating proteins and peptides that secreted under conditions of metabolic stress. These compounds function similarly to hormones and effectively regulate energy, inflammation, and cellular function [9, 10]. Among the most important mitokines are Fibroblast Growth Factor 21 (FGF-21) and Growth Differentiation Factor 15 (GDF-15). FGF-21 is primarily secreted by the liver and activates signaling pathways such as the AMPK and PI3K/AKT pathways through FGFR receptors and the β-Klotho cofactor, leading to increased insulin sensitivity and the regulation of energy metabolism [11, 12]. On the other hand, GDF-15, which belongs to the TGF-β family, plays a role in regulating inflammatory responses, cell proliferation, and metabolic stress via the SMAD pathway [13]. Elevated levels of these mitokines in disease conditions are considered a compensatory response of the body to metabolic disorders. Interestingly, previous studies have shown that regular physical activity can modulate the levels of these markers and improve metabolic indices [14, 15]. However, it is important to note that these pathway descriptions are based on prior literature and are speculative.

High Intensity Functional Training (HIFT) is a modern training method that combines aerobic, resistance, and functional movements in an interval format. Some studies have shown that high-intensity combined training can reduce FGF-21 and GDF-15 levels, improve body composition, increase muscle mass, and reduce insulin resistance [14, 16]. For example, a study by Małkowska et al. revealed that trained individuals had lower levels of FGF-21 and GDF-15 than untrained individuals, suggesting likely reduced metabolic stress and improved mitochondrial function [17]. Additionally, recent studies have indicated that HIFT can effectively improve body composition. In a study by Cavedon et al., participants with more than 10 h per week of HIFT experience had a significant decrease in body fat percentage and an increase in lean muscle mass [18]. However, the precise impact of HIFT on these markers in populations with metabolic syndrome has not been fully investigated. Therefore, the aim of the present study was to investigate the effects of 12 weeks of HIFT on mitokines (FGF-21 and GDF-15), metabolic risk factors and body composition in middle-aged men with metabolic syndrome. Accordingly, the research hypothesis was that these exercise training models modulate the levels of mitochondrial dysfunction markers (FGF-21, GDF-15) involved in metabolic syndrome in middle-aged men with metabolic syndrome. We also hypothesize that this improvement will coincide with better metabolic health and body composition.

Methods

Study design

This was a randomized controlled trial (two-group design with pretest and posttest) conducted among patients with metabolic syndrome in Tehran, Iran. The experimental procedures and study protocols were approved by the ethics committee (code: IR.ATU.REC.1402.029), and all stages were conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration. To ensure comprehensive and transparent reporting of the study design and results, CONSORT reporting guidelines were used (Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials identifier: IRCT20241130063899N1, https://irct.behdasht.gov.ir/trial/80642). Physical activity levels were assessed at baseline and post-intervention using the short form of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) to ensure participants met the sedentary criterion and to monitor for any changes during the study [19]. Additionally, participants maintained a daily physical activity logbook, which was reviewed weekly by the research team to ensure compliance with the instruction to maintain their habitual lifestyle (i.e., no new exercise outside of the study protocol).

To objectively evaluate and monitor dietary intake, all participants completed three 24-hour dietary recalls (24 h) at baseline and again at post-intervention. The 24 h method is widely recognized as a valid and reliable tool for assessing dietary intake in clinical research and is considered the method of choice for assessing average dietary intakes in populations. Recalls were conducted on two non-consecutive weekdays and one weekend day to capture intra-week variability. Participants were instructed to maintain their habitual dietary patterns throughout the study period. Any participant reporting a change in total daily caloric intake exceeding ± 10% from baseline to post-intervention was to be excluded; however, no participant met this criterion for exclusion.

To determine the number of participants, G*Power analysis software (version 3.1) was used. Participants were identified through public announcements in medical centers and specialized clinics. Patient referrals were made by treating physicians, and diagnosis confirmation was based on the study’s inclusion and exclusion criteria. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) middle-aged men aged between 35 and 55 years (To control for the effects of sex hormone fluctuations (particularly estrogen) on mitokines expression, only men were included); (2) no regular physical activity (less than 30 min per day, 3 times a week, for 3 months) and (3) the presence of at least 3 out of 5 criteria for diagnosing metabolic syndrome according to the American Heart Association/National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute scientific statement: 1- central obesity (waist circumference ≥ 102 cm); 2- Dyslipidemia (TG ≥ 150 mg/dL); 3- HDL-C < 40 mg/dL; 4- Blood pressure ≥ 130/85 mmHg; and 5- Fasting plasma glucose ≥ 110 mg/dL. The exclusion criteria included individuals with chronic heart diseases (e.g., heart failure or ischemic or coronary artery diseases), pulmonary diseases (e.g., COPD or severe asthma), active cognitive or psychiatric disorders, musculoskeletal or joint injuries limiting physical activity, and a history of advanced renal or hepatic diseases. Additionally, individuals who experienced new clinical symptoms or exercise-related complications during the intervention period were withdrawn from the study [20].

After initial screening, eligible individuals were selected as research samples. The purpose and procedures of the research were subsequently fully explained to the volunteers. They were then asked to sign an informed consent form if they agreed to participate. Only those who signed the consent form, met the entry criteria, and had none of the exclusion criteria were accepted as final participants in the study. Eligible participants were randomly assigned to either a control group or a HIFT group through a simple randomization procedure, using a computer-generated random sequence. The sample size calculated by G*Power was 12 per group. However, to account for an anticipated dropout rate of approximately 30%, a target of 15 participants per group was initially set and registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT20241130063899N1). 168 individuals were screened for eligibility. Based on the study’s inclusion criteria, 50 non-athlete men with metabolic syndrome were recruited. Of the 50 volunteers, 30 were randomized (15 per group). After randomization, 6 participants (3 from each group) were excluded for the reasons listed in Fig. 1, leaving a final analytic sample of 24 participants (12 per group) (Fig. 1). Participants in the control group were advised to maintain their normal lifestyle and keep a logbook to record physical activity during the study period. The experimental group performed HIFT exercises for 12 weeks. Anthropometric measurements, body composition, and blood sampling (after overnight fasting) were performed 24 h before the first training session and 48 h after the completion of the training program. The participants were asked not to change their lifestyle or diet during the study. A schematic of the study design is presented in Fig. 1.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes of this study were the serum concentrations of Fibroblast Growth Factor- 21 (FGF-21) and Growth Differentiation Factor-15 (GDF-15). Secondary outcomes included anthropometric and body composition indices (weight, BMI, body fat percentage, and skeletal muscle mass) and metabolic risk factors (fasting blood glucose, HbA1c, total cholesterol, triglycerides, HDL-C, and LDL-C).

Anthropometric indices and body composition

Standing height, waist circumference, and hip circumference were measured using a tape meter. An Inbody770 machine (Model: Zuse 9.9, South Korea) was used to measure weight, body mass index (BMI), body fat percentage (BFP), and skeletal muscle mass (SMM).

Blood sampling and analysis

One day before starting the training protocols and 48 h after the last training session, blood samples (5 ml) were drawn from the antecubital vein in a fasting state (12 h fasting) at a specific time of day. Vacutainer tubes containing a clot activator and a serum separator gel were used for sample collection. The samples were left at room temperature for 5 min to complete clotting. The samples were subsequently centrifuged at 3000×g for 10 min and stored in cryovials at -20 °C for short-term storage; however, samples were stored at -80 °C for long-term storage.

The enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) method was used to measure the serum levels of FGF-21 and GDF-15. All factors were assessed by ELISA using a ZellBio Germany kit with the following catalog numbers: FGF-21: ZB-11,983 C-H9648; GDF-15: ZB-10,037 C-H9648.

Measurement of metabolic indices

Serum glucose concentration was measured using the enzymatic-colorimetric GOD-PAP method with commercial Pars Azmun kits (Tehran, Iran) on an autoanalyzer. The intra-assay coefficient of variation for this method was reported to be less than 2.2%.

Triglyceride (TG) and total cholesterol levels were measured using enzymatic-colorimetric methods (GPO-PAP and CHOD-PAP, respectively), and HDL and LDL values were determined using direct enzymatic methods via commercial Pars Azmun kits (Tehran, Iran) on an autoanalyzer. The intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation for these tests were reported to be less than 3%.

HIFT protocol

In this study, participants in the training group performed HIFT training twice a week for 12 weeks (Tables 1, 2 and 3). Before the intervention began, all participants in the HIFT group attended two familiarization sessions during which each exercise was demonstrated, practiced, and corrected to ensure proper form.

Intensity prescription and monitoring

Exercise intensity was prescribed and monitored using multiple methods to ensure standardization and reproducibility. First, the Borg CR10 Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE) scale was used as the primary intensity control method. Participants were instructed to maintain an RPE between 7 and 10 (on a 0–10 scale) for all working sets, corresponding to ‘very hard’ to ‘maximal’ exertion. Previous HIFT validation studies have demonstrated that this RPE range correlates with 85–95% of heart rate reserve (HRR) and 80–90% of maximal oxygen uptake (VO₂max) [21].

Second, heart rate (HR) was continuously monitored during the first and last training session of each week using Polar H10 chest straps (Polar Electro, Kempele, Finland). Peak heart rate (HRpeak) and average heart rate (HRavg) were recorded for each session. Participants were instructed to achieve HR values ≥ 85% of age-predicted maximum heart rate (HRmax = 208 − 0.7 × age) during working intervals.

Third, the session Rating of Perceived Exertion (sRPE) was recorded 30 min after each session using a modified Borg CR10 scale. The training load was calculated as sRPE × session duration (in minutes) and monitored weekly to ensure progressive overload.

Progression Model (Periodization): The 12-week training program consisted of three 4-week phases. Each phase followed an undulating periodization model: Weeks 1–3 (accumulation phase), Week 4 (deload week, 60–70% volume reduction), Weeks 5–7 (intensification phase), Week 8 (deload week), Weeks 9–11 (peak phase), and Week 12 (taper week). This periodization strategy is consistent with established principles of exercise prescription [22, 23] and was designed to optimize physiological adaptations while minimizing the risk of overtraining and injury (Table 2).

Movement standardization

All exercise sessions were supervised by a certified exercise physiologist (author M.M.) with specific training in HIFT methodologies. A standardized movement checklist was used for each exercise, with instructors correcting deviations from proper technique in real-time. The tempo of movements was controlled using a metronome, with a prescribed 1-second concentric phase and 2-second eccentric phase for all resistance-based exercises (e.g., squats, push-ups, rows). For aerobic exercises (e.g., jumping jacks, high knees), participants were instructed to maintain a cadence of 120–140 steps per minute, verified using a digital metronome (Table 3).

Each training session included a 10-minute warm-up (5 min of jogging or brisk walking and 5 min of low-intensity active movements and stretching), followed by four sets of HIFT exercises. Each set included four movements from different categories in the following order: aerobic exercise, lower body strength, upper body strength, and core strength exercises [24, 25]. Exercises were designed based on the AMRAP (As Many Rounds As Possible) system. The duration of each exercise bout was initially 6 min, with 3 min of rest between them (work: rest ratio of 1:2). In subsequent weeks, exercise time increased, but the work: rest ratio remained constant. The total time of each training session, including warm-up and cool-down, approximately was 50 min (Table 1) [24, 25]. All training protocols were executed by an exercise physiologist.

Statistical methods

The sample size was estimated a priori using G*Power software (version 3.1). The calculation was based on detecting a significant difference in the primary outcome, serum FGF-21 levels. Based on data from a previous study on exercise in individuals with metabolic syndrome, with anticipated mean and standard deviation (SD) of FGF-21 levels in the baseline of 215.0 ± 120.6 pg/mL [14], an anticipated percent difference in outcomes of exercise group of 30%, an alpha level of 0.05 and a desired power (1-β) of 0.80, the analysis indicated a minimum requirement of 12 participants per group. To examine between‑group differences following the 12‑week training program, the ANCOVA test was used, where the pretest was used as a covariate factor. Prior to conducting the ANCOVA, the following assumptions were tested and met: (1) Normality of residuals was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, which revealed no significant deviation from normality (P > 0.05 for all dependent variables). (2) Homogeneity of variances was confirmed using Levene’s test, which showed no significant differences between groups (P > 0.05 for all variables). (3) Homogeneity of regression slopes was tested by examining the interaction between the covariate (pre-test scores) and the group factor. The interaction was not statistically significant for any outcome (P > 0.05 for all), indicating that the assumption of equal regression slopes was met. Therefore, ANCOVA was an appropriate analytical approach for this dataset. For Pearson correlation analyses, normality of the variables was also confirmed by Shapiro–Wilk tests (p > 0.05 for all). As a sensitivity check, Spearman’s rank correlations were computed and yielded similar results; we report Pearson for consistency. All analyses were performed using SPSS (version 21). Statistical significance was accepted if p ≤ 0.05.

Results

Of the 50 volunteers, 30 were randomized (15 per group). After randomization, 6 participants (3 from each group) were excluded for the reasons listed in Fig. 1, leaving a final analytic sample of 24 participants (12 per group). An independent t-test showed no significant differences between the two groups at baseline for any of the variables (P > 0.05). Baseline body weight appeared somewhat imbalanced between groups (99.19±13.29 kg in HIFT vs. 86.90±17.01 kg in control). This difference was not statistically significant (P=0.089) and occurred by chance in randomization. In addition, ANCOVA with pre test as covariate was used to adjust for any baseline differences.

Training intensity adherence

Mean session RPE (0–10 scale) was 8.4 ± 0.6 (range 7.8–9.1). Mean heart rate (HR) during working intervals was 162 ± 8 bpm, corresponding to 88 ± 3% of age-predicted HRmax (HRmax = 208–0.7×age). Mean HR during full sessions (including rest) was 145 ± 7 bpm.

Body composition indices

ANCOVA was performed to examine differences in post-test scores between the HIFT and control groups, adjusting for pre-test scores. For weight, there was a significant effect of group on post-test scores after controlling for pre-test performance [F(1, 21) = 79.135, P < 0.001, partial η² =0.82]. HIFT training also had a significant effect on BMI [F(1, 21) = 97.10, P < 0.001, partial η²=0.85], fat percentage [F(1, 21) = 81.80, P < 0.001, partial η² =0.82), and muscle mass [F(1, 21) = 45.68, P < 0.001, partial η² =0.72] (Table 5). Therefore, after 12 weeks of HIFT, weight, BMI, and fat percentage were decreased while muscle mass was increased in men with metabolic syndrome (Table 5).

Mitokine factors

For mitokine outcomes, an ANCOVA was conducted to assess the effect of HIFT training on FGF-21 and GDF-15 serum levels, with baseline as the covariate. A significant difference was found between the two groups for FGF-21 [F(1, 21) = 97.54, P < 0.001, η2 = 0.852] and GDF-15 [F(1, 21) = 93.09, P < 0.001, η2 = 0.846] (Fig. 2).

Metabolic indices

Compared with the results of the control group, our results revealed that HIFT can decrease fasting blood glucose [F(1, 21) = 6.33, p = 0.022, η2 = 0.272], HbA1c [F(1, 21) = 26.852, P < 0.001, η2 = 0.599], cholesterol [F(1, 21) = 50.77, P < 0.001, η2 = 0.749], triglycerides [F(1, 21) = 58.18, P < 0.001, η2 = 0.774], and LDL-c [F(1, 21) = 60.35, P < 0.001, η2 = 0.780], whereas HDL-c was increased [F(1, 21) = 44.57, P < 0.001, η2 = 0.724] (Fig. 3).

Correlation between changes in mitokines and body composition parameters

To explore the relationship between changes in mitokines and improvements in body composition, a Pearson correlation analysis was performed on the change scores (Δ, post-test minus pre-test) within the HIFT group. Given the very small sample size (n = 12), these analyses are strictly exploratory and hypothesis-generating; they should not be interpreted as evidence of dose–response relationships. All reported correlations are presented with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to indicate estimate precision. The analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between ΔFGF-21 and ΔBody Fat Percentage (r = 0.623, P = 0.049) and a significant negative correlation between ΔFGF-21 and ΔSkeletal Muscle Mass (r=-0.595, P = 0.043). Similarly, ΔGDF-15 showed a significant negative correlation with ΔSkeletal Muscle Mass (r=-0.598, P = 0.048). These findings suggest that greater reductions in mitokine levels may be associated with greater improvements in body composition. However, these correlation findings should be interpreted with caution due to the limited sample size and the exploratory nature of these analyses. The observed correlations, while statistically significant, require confirmation in larger, adequately powered studies. Furthermore, correlation does not imply causation, and unmeasured confounding variables may influence these relationships.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the effects of high-intensity functional training (HIFT) on mitokines and related metabolic risk factors in patients with metabolic syndrome. Our results demonstrated that a 12-week HIFT intervention was associated with a significant reduction in the serum levels of the mitokines FGF-21 and GDF-15, alongside an improvements in metabolic risk factors (blood glucose, cholesterol, TG, LDL, and HDL) in patients with metabolic syndrome.

Mitokines are recognized as emerging biomarkers for assessing inflammatory status and mitochondrial function and play crucial roles in regulating energy, stress responses, and metabolic health [26]. From a biological perspective, GDF-15, a cytokine responsive to metabolic and inflammatory stress, increases under conditions such as insulin resistance, tissue damage, and mitochondrial disorders [13]. Its reduction following exercise may indicate decreased systemic inflammation, improved pancreatic beta-cell function, and better-regulated energy homeostasis. The observed decrease in GDF-15 in our study may be interpreted in light of previous research suggesting its association with improved metabolic status. It has been proposed that exercise-induced adaptations may influences pathways such as the GDF15–GFRAL–RET axis, a pathway involved in regulating appetite, lipid, and glucose metabolism [12]. However, it is critical to emphasize that our study did not directly measure any components of the GDF15–GFRAL–RET axis or any other molecular pathway. The following discussion of potential pathways is purely speculative and hypothesis-generating, not based on direct evidence from our study. Therefore, we cannot draw any conclusions regarding the involvement of this or any other molecular pathway. Our findings are limited to observing a statistically significant reduction in circulating GDF-15 levels following the 12-week HIFT intervention. In this context, numerous studies have been conducted on the improvement of mitokines through exercise training, most of which have yielded results that are consistent with the present findings. Furthermore, a study by Chang and Namkung revealed that a 12-week combined exercise program led to a reduction in circulating GDF-15 levels and improved physical fitness in patients with metabolic syndrome [14]. These changes were associated with decreased insulin resistance and increased skeletal muscle mass, suggesting the regulatory role of combined exercise in inflammatory and metabolic pathways.

Furthermore, GDF-15 activates the SMAD signaling pathway by binding to type I (ALK4/7) and type II (ActRII/ActRIIB) serine/threonine kinase receptors. In this pathway, SMAD2 and SMAD3 are phosphorylated, then form a complex with SMAD4 and translocate to the cell nucleus, regulating the expression of target genes involved in processes such as the stress response, inflammation, metabolism, and cell proliferation [27]. From this perspective, the reduction in serum GDF-15 levels following the exercise intervention in the present study may indicate reduced stimulation of inflammatory pathways or an improved metabolic status in individuals with metabolic syndrome [13]; however, we did not evaluate inflammatory markers in this study. We also observed a negative correlation between ΔGDF-15 and ΔSkeletal Muscle Mass in the HIFT group. In this regard, previous studies have shown that high-intensity combined training can reduce GDF-15 levels and simultaneously increase muscle mass [14, 16].

The present study also revealed that 12 weeks of HIFT significantly reduced serum FGF-21 levels compared with those in the control group. FGF-21 is primarily secreted by liver cells and plays a key role in regulating energy, glucose, and lipid metabolism [28]. By binding to FGFR1c receptors and the β-Klotho cofactor, it activates intracellular signaling pathways such as AMPK and PI3K/AKT pathways, leading to increased insulin sensitivity, reduced lipogenesis, and increased lipolysis [29]. In metabolic syndrome, FGF-21 levels are typically elevated, which is considered a compensatory response to insulin resistance and chronic inflammation [30]. While the observed improvements in body composition and metabolic parameters are consistent with the known physiological roles of pathways such as AMPK/PGC-1α (involved in energy regulation) and AKT/mTOR (involved in protein synthesis), however we did not directly measure any molecular markers of these pathways. Therefore, future research should incorporate direct measurements of these pathway components (e.g., via muscle biopsy or phosphoprotein analysis) to establish causal mechanisms.

In chronic metabolic disease states such as metabolic syndrome, elevated levels of FGF-21 and GDF-15 are generally considered compensatory responses to mitochondrial stress, insulin resistance, and chronic inflammation. The finding that both mitokines decreased following 12 weeks of HIFT should therefore be interpreted as a potential indicator of reduced metabolic stress and improved mitochondrial function. This is distinct from the acute exercise response, in which these biomarkers may transiently increase following a single session. Our study assessed chronic adaptations after a sustained intervention, and the observed reductions are consistent with improved metabolic status rather than an acute stress response. Nevertheless, alternative interpretations exist- for example, the reductions might reflect a normalization of the compensatory response. Therefore, without direct measurements of mitochondrial function or inflammatory cytokines, we cannot distinguish between these possibilities and interpretations remain indirect and preliminary.

Interestingly, other studies have also demonstrated a reduction in FGF-21 expression, as a marker of mitochondrial stress, following exercise training. For example, Chang and Namkung reported that a 12-week combined aerobic and resistance exercise program significantly reduced serum FGF-21 levels in women with metabolic syndrome [14]. These changes were associated with improved insulin resistance, increased skeletal muscle mass, and reduced leptin levels, indicating the efficacy of combined training in modulating pathways related to inflammation, mitochondrial function, and energy metabolism. Additionally, a systematic review and meta-analysis by Liu et al. revealed that concurrent training (combining aerobic and resistance exercise), particularly programs lasting more than 10 weeks, led to a significant reduction in FGF-21 levels in adults [16]. This reduction was specifically observed in individuals with obesity, type 2 diabetes, and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease and was associated with improved cardiovascular health indicators; increased muscle strength; and reductions in body weight, BMI, and body fat percentage.

From a metabolic standpoint, the reduction in FGF-21 following regular moderate- to high-intensity exercise was associated with improvements in biological profiles, including glucose, insulin, HOMA-IR, HbA1c, cholesterol, triglycerides, HDL, LDL, and FFAs. These findings suggest that FGF-21 expression which increases in disease states and its reduction after exercise training may be indicators of improved liver function, energy regulation, and reduced systemic inflammation. Dun et al. [30] demonstrated that high-intensity interval training (HIIT) was more effective than moderate-intensity continuous training (MICT) in improving metabolic syndrome indicators and body composition in cardiac patients [31]. In that study, HIIT led to significant reductions in waist circumference, fasting blood glucose, triglycerides, and diastolic blood pressure, whereas MICT did not produce significant changes. In the present study, the reduction in FGF-21 levels following high-intensity HIFT was also associated with improved body composition, blood glucose, and lipid profiles. Our training design, which incorporated aerobic and resistance stimuli within a high-intensity functional framework, likely activated energy regulation pathways (AMPK/PGC-1α) and anabolic pathways (AKT/mTOR) and reduced inflammatory signaling [32]. This alignment with the findings of Liu et al. strengthens the validity of our results and highlights FGF-21 as a reliable biomarker for assessing the efficacy of exercise interventions.

Our findings revealed that greater reductions in mitokine levels were associated with greater improvements in body composition. Most studies examining the effects of exercise training on mitokines have concurrently reported improvements in body composition (reduced fat, increased muscle mass, improved BMI) and lipid profiles [14, 16, 31, 33]. The findings of the present study are consistent with these results; participants experienced significant improvements in blood glucose, body composition (reduced fat, increased muscle mass), and lipid profile (reduced cholesterol, TG, and LDL; increased HDL) following the HIFT protocol. Unlike traditional HIIT performed on a cycle ergometer (which primarily involves single-planar, lower-body dominant movements), HIFT incorporates multi-planar (sagittal, frontal, transverse), full-body, compound movements (e.g., squats, lunges, push-ups, rows, rotational core exercises). This greater movement complexity and muscle mass recruitment may contribute to enhanced metabolic stress, greater energy expenditure, and potentially superior improvements in body composition and mitokine profiles. Therefore, the multifaceted structure of HIFT, which includes functional movements, resistance, and interval training, appears to simultaneously stimulate aerobic and anaerobic pathways, thereby facilitating the concurrent activation of anabolic (AKT/mTOR) and energy regulation (AMPK/PGC-1α) pathways [24]. By applying intermittent and multifaceted stress to energy systems, HIFT was able to concurrently reduce body fat, increase lean muscle mass, and improve lipid profile and blood glucose. These characteristics highlight the importance of multimodal exercise design in improving metabolic syndrome and suggest HIFT as an option for its management. However, because we lacked an active exercise comparator, we cannot conclude that the observed benefits are specific to HIFT; they may reflect general adaptations to exercise training. Moreover, our sample consisted exclusively of middle-aged men, so our findings may not apply to women or other age groups.

Conclusion

In summary, this study found that 12 weeks of HIFT in middle-aged men with metabolic syndrome was associated with a significant reduction in serum mitokine levels (FGF-21 and GDF-15) and concurrent improvements in blood glucose levels, lipid profiles, and body composition. These findings are associative and do not confirm direct mechanistic pathways. The reductions in mitokines, which are typically elevated as compensatory responses in chronic metabolic disease, may reflect improved metabolic status, but this remains speculative without direct mechanistic measurements. Ultimately, these findings suggest that HIFT may be considered a suggested adjunctive nonpharmacological approach for the management of metabolic syndrome, but further research with larger, more diverse samples and active control groups is needed.

Limitations and suggestions

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, while the sample size was sufficient to detect significant changes in our primary outcomes, the final sample size (n = 12 per group) is relatively small, which limits statistical power and increases susceptibility to both type I and type II errors. We did not adjust for multiple comparisons in the primary analyses; this should be considered when interpreting the borderline p-values, particularly for the correlation analyses. The absence of adjustment for multiple comparisons is a limitation that may inflate the risk of false-positive findings. Therefore, all results should be interpreted cautiously and are considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Second, the absence of an active exercise comparator group (e.g., moderate-intensity continuous training or resistance training alone) limits our ability to directly compare the efficacy of HIFT with traditional exercise modalities. Therefore, we cannot conclude that HIFT is superior to other forms of exercise, nor can we determine whether the observed effects are specific to HIFT or reflect general exercise-related adaptations. Third, despite asking participants to maintain their usual diet and lifestyle and using logbooks and dietary recalls to monitor this, we cannot rule out the potential confounding effects of subtle changes in dietary intake or spontaneous physical activity on the observed outcomes. Fourth, the study population consisted exclusively of middle-aged men (35–55 years), which restricts the generalizability of the findings. While this inclusion criterion was reasonable for controlling sex-hormone variability, the results may not apply to women, older adults, younger individuals, or patients with varying severity of metabolic syndrome. We avoid broad clinical generalizations. Fifth, our study did not measure key mechanistic indicators such as inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6), or direct markers of mitochondrial function (e.g., mitochondrial membrane potential, ATP production). This limits our ability to draw definitive conclusions about the exact pathways through which HIFT exerts its effects. Sixth, the intervention duration was only 12 weeks with no long-term follow-up; therefore, the sustainability of the observed changes remains unknown. Seventh, outcome assessors were not blinded to group allocation, which may introduce measurement bias, although standardized protocols were followed. Eighth, mitokines are indirect markers of mitochondrial function; we did not directly measure mitochondrial parameters (e.g., mitochondrial membrane potential, ATP production, or mitochondrial density.

Therefore, future studies should address these limitations by including larger and more diverse samples, incorporating active control groups, implementing stricter controls over dietary and lifestyle factors, and measuring a broader range of mechanistic biomarkers (including inflammatory cytokines and direct markers of mitochondrial function).
