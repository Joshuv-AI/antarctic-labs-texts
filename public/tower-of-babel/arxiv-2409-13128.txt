Fast and Numerically Stable Implementation of Rate Constant Matrix Contraction Method
Shinichi Hemmi, Satoru Iwata, Taihei Oki

Abstract
The rate constant matrix contraction (RCMC) method, proposed by Sumiya et al. (2015, 2017), enables fast and numerically stable simulations of chemical kinetics on large-scale reaction path networks. Later, Iwata et al. (2023) mathematically reformulated the RCMC method as a numerical algorithm to solve master equations whose coefficient matrices, known as rate constant matrices, possess the detailed balance property. This paper aims to accelerate the RCMC method. The bottleneck in the RCMC method lies in the greedy selection of steady states, which is actually equivalent to the greedy algorithm for maximum a posteriori (MAP) inference in determinantal point processes (DPPs) under cardinality constraints. Hemmi et al. (2022) introduced a fast implementation of the greedy DPP MAP inference, called LazyFastGreedy, by combining the greedy algorithm of Chen et al. (2018) with the lazy greedy algorithm by Minoux (1978), a practically efficient greedy algorithm that exploits the submodularity of the objective function. However, for instances arising from chemical kinetics, the straightforward application of LazyFastGreedy suffers from catastrophic cancellations due to the wide range of reaction time scales. To address this numerical instability, we propose a modification to LazyFastGreedy that avoids the subtraction of like-sign numbers by leveraging the properties of rate constant matrices and the connection of the DPP MAP inference to Cholesky decomposition. For faster implementation, we utilize a segment tree, a data structure that manages one-dimensional arrays of elements in a semigroup. We also analyze the increase in relative errors caused by like-sign subtractions and permit such subtractions when they do not lead to catastrophic cancellations, aiming to further accelerate the process. Using real instances from chemical reactions, we confirm that the proposed algorithm is both numerically stable and significantly faster than the original RCMC method.

Introduction

1.1
Background

Simulation of chemical kinetics is a pivotal tool for predicting chemical reactions on reaction path networks.
A reaction path network consists of
n
equilibrium states (EQs) and paths connecting two EQs through transient states (TSs).
The state of a chemical reaction at time
t\geq 0
is represented as the
yield vector

x(t)
, which is an
n
-dimensional vector whose
v
th
(v\in V\coloneqq\{1,\dotsc,n\})
component
x_{v}(t)
corresponds to the yield (amount) of the
v
th EQ.
The dynamics of the yield vector follow the
master equation

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)=Kx(t).

(2)

In (
2
),
K={(K_{uv})}_{u,v\in V}
is an
n\times n

rate constant matrix
, which is determined from the potential energies of EQs and TSs if the system is described by the microcanonical ensemble
[
22
,
23
]
.
The rate constant matrix
K
satisfies the following conditions
[
11
]
:

(RCM1)

K_{uv}\geq 0
for
u\neq v
,

(RCM2)

K_{vv}=-\sum_{u\neq v}K_{uv}
for

v\in V
,

(RCM3)

there exists a positive vector
\pi={(\pi_{v})}_{v\in V}
such that
K_{uv}\pi_{v}=K_{vu}\pi_{u}
for
u,v\in V
.

The
(u,v)
off-diagonal entry
K_{uv}
of
K
represents the reaction rate constant from the
v
th EQ to the
u
th EQ, and is non-negative by
(RCM1)
.
The second condition
(RCM2)
comes from the conservation law of the sum of yields, i.e.,
\sum_{v\in V}x_{v}(t)
is constant for all
t
.
The last condition
(RCM3)
and the vector
\pi
are referred to as the
detailed balance condition
and a
stationary
(or
equilibrium
)
distribution
, respectively.
The yield vector
x(t)
converges to
\pi
as
t\to\infty
regardless of the initial yield
x(0)
if the network is connected
[
1
]
.
The conditions
(RCM1)
–
(RCM3)
can be rephrased to state that
L=-K\Pi
is a (weighted)
graph Laplacian matrix
, where
\Pi
is the diagonal matrix with diagonal entries
\pi_{1},\dotsc,\pi_{n}
.
In probability theory, this system of
x(t)
is known as the
continuous-time Markov chain
, and the master equation (
2
) is called the
Kolmogorov forward equation
.

Rate constant matrices are typically ill-conditioned due to the wide range of reaction time scales.
Furthermore, recent advancements in automatic chemical reaction pathway search techniques, such as the anharmonic downward
distortion following (ADDF) method
[
21
]
and the single-component artificial force induced reaction (SC-AFIR) method
[
16
]
, have resulted in large-scale (
n\gtrsim$10true000$
), sparse (
\nnz(K)\lesssim$30true000$
), and extremely ill-conditioned rate constant matrices; the ratio of the largest to smallest non-zero off-diagonal entries of
K
can exceed
10^{200}
(see
Table

3
).
Numerically solving such a stiff differential equation has been recognized as a challenging task.
While one can obtain
x(t)
for every
t
from the eigendecomposition of
K
via the explicit formulation of the analytic solution
x(t)=\mathrm{e}^{tK}x(0)
, computing the eigendecomposition of ill-conditioned matrices requires time-consuming multi-precision arithmetic as the double-precision arithmetic suffers from heavy catastrophic cancellations.

To address these issues, Sumiya et al.
[
23
,
24
]
presented the
rate constant matrix contraction
(RCMC) method for approximately solving master equations described by the canonical or microcanonical ensemble.
The RCMC method relies on neither finite difference, multi-precision arithmetic, nor subtraction of like-sign numbers.
The original description
[
23
,
24
]
of the RCMC method was based on chemical intuitions.
Subsequently,
[
11
]
provided a mathematical reformulation of the RCMC method, showing that it works for the master equation (
2
) with any coefficient matrix
K
satisfying
(RCM1)
–
(RCM3)
and performing a theoretical error analysis.
Recent work
[
8
]
applies the reformulated RCMC method to the sensitivity analysis of complicated reaction path networks.

The reformulated procedure of the RCMC method is outlined as follows
[
11
, Section 3]
.
The input of the RCMC method is a rate constant matrix
K
, an initial yield vector
p
, and the end time
t_{\mathrm{max}}
of the simulation.
The RCMC method runs in two steps: Steps 1 and 2.
Step 1 constructs a sequence of growing sets
\emptyset=S^{(0)}\subsetneq S^{(1)}\subsetneq\dotsb\subsetneq S^{(k)}\subsetneq V
of EQs with
0\leq k\leq n
in a greedy fashion.
This procedure is essentially the pivoted Gaussian elimination for Cholesky decomposition on
L=-K\Pi
with a minor modification to avoid subtraction of like-sign numbers.
Intuitively, every EQ
v\in S^{(j)}
is regarded as “steady” after some time
t^{(j)}
, i.e.,
\dot{x}_{v}(t)\approx 0
for
t\geq t^{(j)}
.
On the other hand, EQs in
T^{(j)}\coloneqq V\setminus S^{(j)}
are said to be “transient”.
The value
k
is the number of EQs that are regarded as steady at
t_{\mathrm{max}}
.
In typical use such as
t_{\mathrm{max}}=$86true400\text{\,}\mathrm{s}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{c}$
(
=\text{one day}
),
k
roughly satisfies
0.4n\lesssim k\lesssim 0.7n
.
The idea of bipartitioning EQs by steadiness comes from the classical
quasi-steady state approximation
(QSSA).

Step 2 of the RCMC method computes a vector
q^{(j)}
and the time
t^{(j)}
for
j=1,\dotsc,k
by explicit matrix calculations.
It is guaranteed that
0<t^{(1)}<\dotsb<t^{(k)}\leq t_{\mathrm{max}}
and each
q^{(j)}
is a non-negative vector with
\sum_{v\in V}q^{(j)}_{v}=\sum_{v\in V}p_{v}
.
The value
t^{(j)}
is a reference time when the EQs in
S^{(j)}
and
T^{(j)}\coloneqq V\setminus S^{(j)}
are regarded as steady and transient, respectively, and the vector
q^{(j)}
serves as an approximation to
x(t^{(j)})
.
This is a “full” scheme to obtain a rough sketch of the entire trajectory of the yield vector
x(t)
through
t\in[0,t_{\mathrm{max}}]
.
If only the yield vector
x(t_{\mathrm{max}})
at the last moment
t_{\mathrm{max}}
is needed, it suffices to compute only the last vector
q^{(k)}
.
We call the former and the latter way the
full
and
last
options, respectively.
See
Section

2.1
for more on the RCMC method.

The advantages of the RCMC method lie in its numerical stability and running time.
Regarding numerical stability, the RCMC method is free from subtractions of like-sign numbers, and thus, catastrophic cancellation never occurs.
In fact, it is reported in
[
11
, Section 6]
that using double-precision floating-point numbers is enough for obtaining a good approximation to
x(t)
.
The paper
[
11
]
also observed that the RCMC method with
full
option runs within seconds for rate constant matrices with
n\approx$1700$
.

1.2
Aim of This Paper

The aim of this paper is to speed up the (reformulated) RCMC method.
This is motivated by the fact that large-scale rate constant matrices with
n\gtrsim 10,000
are recently obtained by GRRM and AFIR.
For such large-scale matrices, the RCMC method took more than 5 minutes, even with the faster
last
option (see
Section

6
).
Since the RCMC method is repeatedly applied in the kinetics-based navigation in AFIR
[
22
]
, its speeding up is crucial for accelerating the entire procedure of AFIR.

Step 1 of the RCMC method runs in
\Theta(kn^{2})
time.
Step 2 takes
\Theta(k^{3})
and
\Theta(k^{2})
times for
full
and
last
options, respectively, assuming that
k=\tilde{\Omega}(\sqrt{n})
and
K
is a sparse matrix, i.e., the number of non-zero entries in
K
is
\tilde{\Theta}(n)
(see
Section

2.2
).
Here,
\tilde{\Omega}
and
\tilde{\Theta}
hides logarithmic factors.
This means that Step 1 is the bottleneck of the RCMC method.
We have also confirmed in numerical experiments that Step 1 actually takes a large portion of the running time for small
k
; see
Section

6
.

Our starting point for accelerating Step 1 lies in its connection to the greedy algorithm for
maximum a posteriori
(MAP)
inference
for
determinantal point processes
(DPPs) under cardinality constraints.
A DPP is a probability distribution on the power set
2^{V}
of a finite set
V=\{1,\dotsc,n\}
determined from an
n\times n
positive semi-definite matrix
L

[
15
]
.
The probability of observing
X\subseteq V
is proportional to
\det L_{XX}
, where
L_{XX}
denotes the principal submatrix of
L
with rows and columns indexed by
X
.
The MAP inference for DPPs under the cardinality constraint, which is to compute
X
with maximum
\det L_{XX}
subject to
|X|=k
, has many applications in machine learning, such as recommendation systems
[
3
]
, document summarization
[
13
]
, and diverse molecular selection
[
18
]
.
While exact solving the MAP inference is NP-hard
[
12
]
, a greedy algorithm provides a good approximation due to the submodularity of the function
X\mapsto\log\det L_{XX}
; see
Section

2.3
for detail.

We then observe in
Section

2.4
that Step 1 of the RCMC method is essentially equivalent to a slight generalization of the greedy MAP inference for DPPs.
Therefore, any implementation of the greedy MAP inference for DPPs can be used as that of Step 1.
We refer to the original implementation
[
11
]
of Step 1 as
Greedy
.

[
3
]
presented an algorithm, called
FastGreedy
in
[
9
]
, for faster implementation of greedy MAP inference, improving the running time from
\Theta(kn^{2})
to
\Theta(k^{2}n)
.

FastGreedy
is based on Doolittle’s algorithm
[
4
]
for Cholesky decomposition, which computes entries of the Cholesky factor directly.
Combining
FastGreedy
with the lazy greedy algorithm
[
17
]
for cardinality-constrained submodular function maximization,
[
9
]
proposed a practically faster implementation, called
LazyFastGreedy
.
Intuitively,
LazyFastGreedy
reduces the running time by delaying the computation of entries in the Cholesky factor that are not immediately needed.
The running time of
LazyFastGreedy
depends on how well this lazy heuristic works.
In the best case,
LazyFastGreedy
runs in
\Theta(k^{3})
time and is not slower than
FastGreedy
even in the worst case (up to logarithmic terms).
In our numerical experiment,
FastGreedy
ran faster than
Greedy
, and
LazyFastGreedy
outperformed
FastGreedy
in real instances.
See
Section

3
for details on
FastGreedy
and
LazyFastGreedy
.

Unfortunately, however, both
FastGreedy
and
LazyFastGreedy
suffer from numerical instability.
In fact, our numerical experiment reported in
Section

6
indicates that the set
S^{(j)}
computed by these two algorithms with double-precision floating-point numbers was incorrect at some
j
because of the accumulated cancellation errors.

1.3
Our Contributions

To achieve fast and numerically stable computation, we present a modification of
LazyFastGreedy
, termed
StableLazyFastGreedy
.
As stated above, Step 1 of the RCMC method is essentially the Cholesky decomposition of the graph Laplacian matrix
L=-K\Pi
.
The only point where
LazyFastGreedy
may subtract like-sign numbers is in the computation of diagonal entries of the Cholesky factor
C
of
L
.
Due to the conditions
(RCM1)
–
(RCM3)
on
K
, the Cholesky factor
C
has the property that its off-diagonal entries are non-positive, and every diagonal entry is the minus of the sum of the off-diagonal entries with the same column.
Therefore, we can retrieve each
v
th diagonal entry of
C
by summing all the non-zero off-diagonal entries in the same column, i.e.,
C_{vv}=-\sum_{v\in T^{\prime}}C_{uv}
with
T^{\prime}\coloneqq T^{(j)}\setminus\{v\}
.
As
C_{uv}\leq 0
, this way of computing
C_{vv}
does not involve additions of opposite-sign numbers.
This is, however, incompatible with the approach of
LazyFastGreedy
, which benefits from delaying the computation of off-diagonal entries of
C
.

Our idea for overcoming this issue is to consider the
compression

L^{[j,v]}
of
L
by
T^{\prime}
, a matrix obtained by aggregating the rows (columns) of
L
indexed by
T^{\prime}
into a single row (column).
The compression
L^{[j,v]}
has the properties that (i) the Cholesky factor
C^{[j,v]}
of
L^{[j,v]}
is the compression of
C
by
T^{\prime}
and (ii)
L^{[j,v]}
is again a graph Laplacian matrix.
The first property implies that we can obtain
C_{vv}
as the negative of an off-diagonal entry
C^{[j,v]}_{\star v}
, where
\star
denotes the index of the compressed row.
Furthermore, the second property enables us to regard the compression
L^{[j,v]}
as an input matrix instead of
L
and obtain
C^{[j,v]}_{\star v}
in the same way as other off-diagonal entries of the Cholesky factor.
If the lazy heuristics work well, this reduces the computational cost compared to computing
C_{uv}
for all
u\in T^{\prime}
.

In implementing
StableLazyFastGreedy
, the values of compressed entries
L^{[j,v]}_{\star w}=\sum_{u\in T^{\prime}}L_{uw}
for all
w\in S^{(j)}\cup\{v\}
are required for computing
C^{[j,v]}_{\star v}
.
To calculate the compressed entries only with additions of non-negative numbers, we employ a data structure called a
segment tree
(or a
range tree
)
[
2
]
.
A segment tree manages a one-dimensional array of
n
entries of a semigroup and allows
\mathrm{O}(\log n)
-time queries for executing the semigroup operation on a consecutive subarray and updating an array element.
Using segment trees regarding the set of non-negative reals with the addition as a semigroup, we can compute the compressed entries for successive
j=0,1,2,\dotsc
efficiently.

If the lazy heuristics works the best,
StableLazyFastGreedy
runs in
\Theta(k^{3})
time (ignoring logarithmic factors), which is as asymptotically fast as
LazyFastGreedy
.
However, if the lazy heuristics works poorly, the worst-case time complexity swells up to
\Theta(k^{3}n)
.
To reduce this complexity in practice, we introduce
RelaxedStableLazyFastGreedy
, which implements additional heuristics called the
relaxing heuristics
.
Relaxing heuristics aims to accelerate the algorithm by allowing the subtraction of like-sign numbers if their ratio is smaller than a prescribed threshold.
To bound errors that may occur by subtractions, for two positive numbers
\hat{a}
and
\hat{b}
containing errors from their true values
a
and
b
, respectively, we give an upper bound on the relative error of
\hat{a}-\hat{b}
to the true difference
a-b
.
Through our error analysis, we can determine the threshold depending on the error tolerance.
When the relaxing heuristics works well, i.e., much computation has been done with subtractions,
RelaxedStableLazyFastGreedy
runs in
\Theta(k^{2}n)
time even if the lazy heuristics work the worst.

2.3
Greedy MAP Inference for DPPs

Let
L\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times V}
be a positive semi-definite (PSD) matrix.
A
determinantal point process
(DPP)
[
15
]
with a kernel matrix
L
is a probability distribution on
2^{V}
such that the probability of obtaining every
X\subseteq V
is proportional to
\det L_{XX}
.
The
maximum a posteriori
(MAP)
inference
for DPPs (under the cardinality constraint) is the problem of finding
X\subseteq V
with
|X|=k
that maximizes
\det L_{XX}
, where
k\in[0,n]
is a prescribed parameter.
This problem is equivalent to maximizing a function
f_{L}\colon 2^{V}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
defined by

\displaystyle f_{L}(X)\coloneqq\log\det L_{XX}\quad(X\subseteq V),

(6)

where
\log 0
is set to
-\infty
, under
|X|\leq k
.
The function
f_{L}
is known to be
submodular

[
5
]
.
Here, a function
f\colon 2^{V}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
is called
submodular
if
f(v\mathrel{|}X)\geq f(v\mathrel{|}Y)
for all
X,Y\subseteq V
with
X\subseteq Y
and
v\in V\setminus Y
, where
f(v\mathrel{|}X)\coloneqq f(X\cup\{v\})-f(X)
denotes the
marginal gain
of
v\in V\setminus X
under
X\subseteq V
.

Maximizing a submodular function
f\colon 2^{V}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
under the cardinality constraint
|X|\leq k
requires exponentially many queries of function values in general
[
19
]
, and even the MAP inference for DPPs is NP-hard
[
12
]
.
Thus, polynomial-time approximation algorithms are commonly used for this problem.
The standard
greedy method
works as follows: starting from
S^{(0)}\coloneqq\varnothing
, in each
j
th (
j\in[k]
) iteration, take
s^{(j)}\coloneqq\argmax\big\{\,{f(v\mathrel{|}S^{(j-1)})}\;\big|\;{v\in T^{(j-1)}}\,\big\}
with
T^{(j-1)}\coloneqq V\setminus S^{(j-1)}
and let
S^{(j)}\coloneqq S^{(j-1)}\cup big\{s^{(j)}big\}
.
The greedy method evaluates
f
a total of
\Theta(kn)
times, and enjoys a
(1-1/\mathrm{e})
-approximation guarantee if
f
is
monotone
, i.e.,
f(X)\leq f(Y)
for
X\subseteq Y

[
20
]
.
Even if not, the greedy method is commonly adopted as a practical heuristic.

3.2

The

[
17
]
is a practically faster implementation of the greedy method for maximizing a general submodular function

under a cardinality constraint.
In addition to the current solution

on the marginal gain

for each

At each

without updating the others, hence we add

to

to construct

.
Otherwise, this procedure is repeated until an element with the largest marginal gain is found.
The upper bounds

If the lazy update works the best, the method evaluates the objective function only
\Theta(n)
times.
While the worst-case time complexity of the lazy greedy remains unchanged up to slight additional costs of heap operations, it empirically outperforms the naïve greedy method in many real instances
[

In addition to

and

in

.
Recall from

\displaystyle M_{\mathrm{diag}}=\sum_{j=1}^{k+1}c_{j}\left(\sum_{l=1}^{j-1}(l-1)+j-1\right)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{k+1}j(j-1)c_{j}.

(25)

By

\displaystyle\frac{k(k+1)(k+2)}{6}\leq M_{\mathrm{diag}}\leq\frac{k(k+1)(k+2)(4n-3k-1)}{24}

and the lower and upper bounds are attained if the lazy heuristics work the best and worst, respectively.

.

\Theta(M_{\mathrm{offdiag}}+M_{\mathrm{diag}}+\nnz(K))+\mathrm{O}\big(\sum_{j=1}^{k+1}jc_{j}\log n\big)

\Theta(k^{3}+\nnz(K))+\mathrm{O}(k^{2}\log n)
and
\Theta(k^{3}n+\nnz(K))+\mathrm{O}(k^{2}n\log n)

Proof.

The most costly parts of

and

(and

) in

5
Relaxing StableLazyFastGreedy

5.1
Acceleration by Relaxing Stabilization

If the lazy heuristic is effective,
StableLazyFastGreedy
runs in
\Theta(k^{3})
time by
Theorem

4.3
, assuming
k=\Omega(\log n)
.
This performance is comparable to that of
LazyFastGreedy
.
However, if the lazy heuristic performs poorly, the running time increases significantly to
\Theta(k^{3}n)
.
The bottleneck is the inner product calculation at
5
in
Algorithm

5
.
To speed up the computation, we introduce a further heuristic based on the following error analysis on catastrophic cancellations.

Suppose that a computer stores an approximation
\hat{a}\in\mathbb{R}
of a non-zero real number
a\in\mathbb{R}
, where the error arises from previous computations using finite-precision arithmetic.
The
relative error
of
\hat{a}
with respect to
a
is defined as
\err_{a}(\hat{a})\coloneqq\absolutevalue{a - \hat{a}}/\absolutevalue{a}
.
It is easily checked that for two positive reals
a
and
b
with
a>b
and their approximations
\hat{a}
and
\hat{b}
, the relative error of
\hat{x}\coloneqq\hat{a}-\hat{b}
with respect to
x\coloneqq a-b
is bounded as

\displaystyle\err_{x}(\hat{x})\leq\frac{a+b}{a-b}\max\{\err_{a}(\hat{a}),\err_{b}(\hat{b})\}=\frac{1+r}{1-r}\max\{\err_{a}(\hat{a}),\err_{b}(\hat{b})\},

(27)

where
r\coloneqq b/a

[
10
, Section 1.7]
.
Therefore, if
r\leq\varepsilon/(2+\varepsilon)
for some error tolerance parameter
\varepsilon\geq 0
, then
\err_{x}(\hat{x})
is bounded by
(1+\varepsilon)\max\{\err_{a}(\hat{a}),\err_{b}(\hat{b})\}
.
This implies that if an increase in relative error by a factor of at most
1+10^{-6}
is acceptable, for example, we can subtract two numbers whose ratio is at most
10^{-6}/(2+10^{-6})\approx 5\times 10^{-7}
.
Although this requirement may seem hardly attainable, it is often met in rate constant matrices of chemical reaction networks.
However, since algorithms only have the approximations
\hat{a}
and
\hat{b}
, but their true values, the ratio
b/a
is not directly computable.
The following theorem shows that the same error bound holds even if
\hat{b}/\hat{a}
is used instead of
b/a
under the mild assumption that
1+\varepsilon\leq{\max\{\err_{a}(\hat{a}),\err_{b}(\hat{b})\}}^{-1}
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
a,\hat{a},b,\hat{b}\in\mathbb{R}
be non-zero reals of the same sign with
|a|>|b|
,
|\hat{a}|>|\hat{b}|
,
x\coloneqq a-b
,
\hat{x}\coloneqq\hat{a}-\hat{b}
, and
e\coloneqq\max\{\err_{a}(\hat{a}),\err_{b}(\hat{b})\}
.
For
\varepsilon\in[0,e^{-1}-1]
, if
\hat{b}/\hat{a}\leq\varepsilon/(2+\varepsilon)
, then
\err_{x}(\hat{x})\leq(1+\varepsilon)e
holds.

Proof.

We can assume
e\leq 1
because the claim is vacuously true for
e>1
as
[0,e^{-1}-1]
is empty.
We further assume that
a,b,\hat{a}
, and
\hat{b}
are positive without loss of generality.
Let
\delta_{a}
and
\delta_{b}
be the values such that
\hat{a}=(1+\delta_{a})a
and
\hat{b}=(1+\delta_{b})b
, respectively, and
r\coloneqq\hat{b}/\hat{a}
.
Note that
e=\max\{\absolutevalue{\delta_a},\absolutevalue{\delta_b}\}
holds by
\hat{a},\hat{b}>0
and
r<1
follows from
\hat{a}>\hat{b}
.
The relative error of
\hat{x}
with respect to
x
is written as

\displaystyle\err_{x}(\hat{x})=\frac{\absolutevalue{x - \hat{x}}}{x}=\frac{\absolutevalue{a\delta_a + b\delta_b}}{a-b}=\frac{\absolutevalue{\frac{\hat a \delta_a}{1+\delta_a} + \frac{\hat b \delta_b}{1+\delta_b}}}{\frac{\hat{a}}{1+\delta_{a}}-\frac{\hat{b}}{1+\delta_{b}}}=\frac{\absolutevalue{\frac{\delta_a}{1+\delta_a} + \frac{\delta_b}{1+\delta_b}r}}{\frac{1}{1+\delta_{a}}-\frac{1}{1+\delta_{b}}r}=\absolutevalue{f_r(\delta_a, \delta_b)},

(28)

where
f_{r}(p,q)
is a function defined by

\displaystyle f_{r}(p,q)\coloneqq\frac{\frac{p}{1+p}+\frac{q}{1+q}r}{\frac{1}{1+p}-\frac{1}{1+q}r}\quad\left(-e\leq p,q\leq+e,\,\frac{1}{1+p}>\frac{1}{1+q}r\right).

(29)

Note that the domain of
f_{r}
can be restricted to
(p,q)
with
1/(1+p)>r/(1+q)
because
1/(1+\delta_{a})>r/(1+\delta_{b})
is implied by
a>b
.

In the following, we show that
f_{r}(p,q)
is monotone non-decreasing with respect to
p
and
q
for fixed
q
and
p
, respectively.
In fact, since
(p,q)=(+e,+e)
and
(-e,-e)
are in the domain of
f_{r}(p,q)
, the monotonicity gives lower and upper bounds on
f_{r}(\delta_{a},\delta_{b})
as follows:

\displaystyle-(1+\varepsilon)e\leq-\frac{1+r}{1-r}e=f_{r}(-e,-e)\leq f_{r}(\delta_{a},\delta_{b})\leq f_{r}(+e,+e)=+\frac{1+r}{1-r}e\leq+(1+\varepsilon)e,

(30)

where the first inequality is obtained from
r\leq\varepsilon/(2+\varepsilon)
, showing the claim of the theorem.

Reformulating the expression, we have

\displaystyle f_{r}(p,q)=\frac{1-\frac{1-q}{1+q}r}{\frac{1}{1+p}-\frac{1}{1+q}r}-1=\frac{r-\frac{1-p}{1+p}}{\frac{1}{1+p}-\frac{1}{1+q}r}+1.

(31)

Therefore,
f_{r}(p,q)
is monotone non-decreasing with respect to
p
and
q
if and only if
1-\frac{1-q}{1+q}r\geq 0
and
r-\frac{1-p}{1+p}\leq 0
, respectively.
These inequalities follow from

\displaystyle r\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2+\varepsilon}\leq\frac{1-e}{1+e}\leq\min\left\{\frac{1-p}{1+p},\frac{1+q}{1-q}\right\},

(32)

where the second and third inequalities are from the facts that a function
u\mapsto(1-u)/(1+u)
is monotone decreasing and
{(1+\varepsilon)}^{-1}\geq e\geq+p,-q
.
Hence, the monotonicity of
f_{r}(p,q)
has been proved.
∎

We incorporate
Theorem

5.1
into
5
in
Algorithm

5
using the following relationship:

\displaystyle C_{\star l}^{[j,u]}=C_{\star l}^{[j-1,s^{(j-1)}]}-C_{ul},

(33)

which comes from
C_{\star l}^{[j,u]}=\sum_{v\in T^{(j-1)}\setminus\{u\}}C_{vl}
and
C_{\star l}^{[j-1,s^{(j-1)}]}=\sum_{v\in T^{(j-1)}}C_{vl}
.
Note that
C_{\star l}^{[j-1,s^{(j-1)}]}
is readily available since it is computed in
StablyComputeDiagonal
(j-1,s^{(j-1)})
, which is previously invoked.
Thus, according to
Theorem

5.1
, we can obtain
C_{\star l}^{[j,u]}
using (
33
) when
C_{ul}/C_{\star l}^{[j-1,s^{(j-1)}]}\leq\varepsilon/(2+\varepsilon)
with a prescribed error tolerance
\varepsilon
, avoiding the computation of the inner product.
We term this heuristic the
relaxing heuristic
and the improved algorithm as
RelaxedStableLazyFastGreedy
.

Complexity Analysis for RelaxedStableLazyFastGreedy

We finally analyze the running time of
RelaxedStableLazyFastGreedy
.
Recall that
M_{\mathrm{offdiag}}
and
M_{\mathrm{diag}}
denote the total dimensions of vectors for which the inner products are computed at

in
Algorithm

and
5
,

in

Since the difference between

and

is only
5

, the running time estimated in
Theorem

4.3
is still valid for

; that is,
RelaxedStableLazyFastGreedy
runs in
\Theta(M_{\mathrm{offdiag}}+M_{\mathrm{diag}}+\nnz(K))+\mathrm{O}\big(\sum_{j=1}^{k+1}jc_{j}\log n\big)
time.
Here, the equality (

) for

holds in both

Lemma 5.2
.

In
RelaxedStableLazyFastGreedy
,

satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k+1}(j-1)c_{j}\leq M_{\mathrm{diag}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{k+1}j(j-1)c_{j}

(34)

and the lower and upper bounds are attained if the relaxing heuristics work the best and worst, respectively.
In particular,
M_{\mathrm{diag}}
is equal to

•

k(k+1)/2=\Theta(k^{2})
if both the lazy and relaxing heuristics work the best,

•

k(k+1)(3n-2k-1)/6=\Theta(k^{2}n)
if the lazy and relaxing heuristics work the worst and best, respectively,

•

k(k+1)(k+2)/6=\Theta(k^{3})
if the lazy and relaxing heuristics work the best and worst, respectively, and

•

k(k+1)(k+2)(4n-3k-1)/24=\Theta(k^{3}n)
if both the lazy and relaxing heuristics work the worst.

Proof.

If the relaxing heuristic works perfectly,
5
in
Algorithm

5
does not compute an inner product and
6
contributes to
M_{\mathrm{diag}}
by
j-1
, resulting in

.
If the relaxing heuristic does not work at all,

is the same as
StableLazyFastGreedy
and (
25
) holds.
The latter part of the claim is obtained by substituting the bounds on
c_{j}
given in
Lemma

3.7
.
∎

Lemma

5.2
leads us to the following time complexity on
RelaxedStableLazyFastGreedy
.

Theorem 5.3
.

The running time of
RelaxedStableLazyFastGreedy
is
\Theta(M_{\mathrm{offdiag}}+M_{\mathrm{diag}}+\nnz(K))+\mathrm{O}\big(\sum_{j=1}^{k+1}jc_{j}\log n\big)
, which boils down to

•

\Theta(k^{3}+\nnz(K))+\mathrm{O}(k^{2}\log n)
if the lazy heuristics works the best,

•

\Theta(k^{2}n+\nnz(K))+\mathrm{O}(k^{2}n\log n)
if the lazy and relaxing heuristics work the worst and best, respectively, and

•

\Theta(k^{3}n+\nnz(K))+\mathrm{O}(k^{2}n\log n)
if both the lazy and relaxing heuristics work the worst.

Even if the lazy heuristics works poorly, the relaxing heuristics can improve the dominating term in the complexity from
\Theta(k^{3}n)
up to
\Theta(k^{2}n)

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we have proposed a fast and numerically stable implementation of the greedy part (
Item

1
) of the RCMC method for chemical kinetics simulation.
Our technique is to modify the lazy greedy for the DPP MAP inference to avoid subtractions of like-sign numbers, leveraging properties of rate constant matrices.
For faster computation, we employ segment trees and partially allow like-sign subtractions if they do not cause catastrophic cancellations.
Using real instances from chemical reactions, we have confirmed that the presented algorithm runs numerically stably and much faster than the original method.
Accelerating
Item

2
, a new bottleneck, is left for future investigation.
