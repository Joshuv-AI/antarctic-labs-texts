Lost at the Beginning of Reasoning
Baohao Liao, Xinyi Chen, Sara Rajaee, Yuhui Xu, Christian Herold, Anders Søgaard, Maarten de Rijke, Christof Monz

Abstract
Recent advancements in large language models (LLMs) have significantly advanced complex reasoning capabilities, particularly through extended chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning that incorporates mechanisms such as backtracking, self-reflection, and self-correction. Despite these developments, the self-correction abilities of LLMs during long CoT reasoning remain underexplored. And recent findings on overthinking suggest that such models often engage in unnecessarily redundant reasoning. In this work, we empirically show that the first reasoning step exerts a disproportionately large influence on the final prediction—errors introduced at this stage can substantially degrade subsequent reasoning quality. This phenomenon is consistently observed across two state-of-the-art open-source reasoning model families: DeepSeek-R1 and Qwen3. To address this, we propose an efficient sampling strategy that leverages a reward model to identify and retain high-quality first reasoning steps while discarding suboptimal ones, achieving up to a 70% reduction in inference cost without sacrificing accuracy. Finally, we introduce a new benchmark specifically constructed with deliberately flawed first reasoning steps to systematically evaluate models’ self-correction capabilities, offering a foundation for future research on robust reasoning in LLMs.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable performance across a variety of reasoning tasks, ranging from mathematical problem solving to multi-hop question answering

. More recently, the advent of reasoning-oriented LLMs capable of performing long chain-of-thought (long-CoT) reasoning at test time has led to substantial advancements in these domains

. A widely held hypothesis attributes this progress to the models’ ability to backtrack, self-reflect, and self-correct, effectively leveraging contextual feedback to iteratively refine their responses.

Recent studies have suggested that LLMs engaged in long-CoT reasoning may exhibit a tendency to overthink—that is, to produce unnecessarily extended reasoning processes to solve problems
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
]
. This observation raises questions about the model’s capacity for backtracking, self-reflection, and self-correction. It suggests that LLMs may lack awareness of the information they have already processed, leading to redundant or inefficient reasoning. Moreover,
Liu et al. 2024a
demonstrates that LLMs are prone to the "lost-in-the-middle" phenomenon, wherein information located in the middle of a long context is often overlooked. While their analysis is conducted in the context of information retrieval, it remains an open question whether similar positional biases affect long CoT as well.

In this work, we introduce a novel and previously underexplored perspective on long-CoT reasoning: many reasoning failures in long-CoT LLMs stem not from errors made mid-chain, but rather from flaws at the beginning of reasoning. Our experiments demonstrate that the first reasoning step has the most significant influence on the final prediction. When this first step is incorrect, the model is considerably more likely to arrive at an incorrect final answer (40% accuracy drop), highlighting the limited self-correction capabilities of current long-CoT LLMs.

Motivated by this finding, we propose an efficient early pruning algorithm that exploits the predictive power of the first reasoning step. Specifically, by evaluating the quality of the first step, we identify and discard less promising reasoning paths early, continuing generation only for the more promising ones. This approach significantly reduces inference cost. Across four long-CoT LLMs and four challenging mathematical and scientific reasoning benchmarks, our method maintains prediction accuracy while reducing inference budget by up to 70%.

Finally, we introduce
LaBoR
, a self-correction benchmark constructed by an automatic pipeline, aiming at quantifying LLMs’ ability to recover from initial reasoning errors. Each LaBoR instance contains a question paired with a faulty first reasoning step, requiring the model to detect and correct the initial error to produce the correct final answer. To our knowledge, LaBoR is the first benchmark specifically designed to assess self-correction in long-CoT settings. Despite its simplicity, LaBoR effectively captures the extent to which LLMs struggle to recover from early mistakes. Through extensive evaluation of eight state-of-the-art open-source long-CoT LLMs, we observe a substantial vulnerability: performance drops by up to 11.4% when the initial step contains an error.

Contributions.
Our main contributions are as follows:
(1) We empirically establish a strong positive correlation between the first reasoning step and the final prediction (§

);
(2) We propose an efficient early pruning algorithm that halts generation for less promising initial steps, thereby improving inference efficiency (§

(3) We introduce

3
Lost at the beginning of reasoning

Motivated by the finding of
Liu et al. 2024a
, which demonstrates that query-relevant information is more impactful when positioned at either the beginning or end of an LLM’s context window, we first investigate whether a similar positional effect exists in long-CoT reasoning (§
3.1
). Our analysis reveals that the first reasoning step has the greatest impact to the final conclusion. To validate this observation, we further perform two ablation studies, confirming the critical role of the first step in determining the model’s final prediction (§
3.2
and §
3.3
).

Notation.
Let
p
represent the input prompt, consisting of both a system instruction and a user query. A reasoning model
\mathcal{M}
produces a sequence of CoT reasoning steps
t=[t_{1},t_{2},...,t_{T}]
, followed by a final conclusion
c_{T}
, such that the complete model output is given by
t\oplus c_{T}=\mathcal{M}(p)
, where
\oplus
means concatenation. In models such as DeepSeek-R1
[
8
]
and Qwen3
[
9
]
, the input-output format adheres to the following:

p\ \mathrm{{<}think{>}}\ t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{T}\ \mathrm{{<}/think{>}}\ c_{T}

The final prediction
q_{T}
is then derived by applying an extraction function
g
to the conclusion, i.e.,
q_{T}=g(c_{T})
, where
g
may, for example, extract values enclosed within
\boxed{}
.

The conclusion
c_{T}
can be interpreted as a summary of the essential reasoning steps leading to the final prediction. This raises an interesting question:
Is there a positional bias in how reasoning steps contribute to the conclusion?
In other words, do certain steps have a disproportionately greater influence on
c_{T}
than others?

3.2
Correlation between the first reasoning step and the final prediction

Given that the first reasoning step closely resembles the final conclusion, we investigate whether the essential reasoning required for the final prediction is already encapsulated in the first step. To this end, we analyze the prediction when conditioned solely on the first reasoning step. Specifically, we compute
c_{1}=\mathcal{M}(p\mathrm{{<}think{>}}t_{1}\mathrm{{<}/think{>}})
, and derive the corresponding prediction
q_{1}=g(c_{1})
, which we compare against the ground truth
a
. Based on this comparison, we categorize each first reasoning step as either
first correct
(if
q_{1}=a
) or
first incorrect
(otherwise).

Experimental setup.
From the questions and their CoT traces obtained in §
3.1

For each remaining question and its corresponding first reasoning step
t_{1}
, we obtain the initial prediction
q_{1}
as previously described. To better visualize the final outcomes, we categorize the questions into three groups based on the pass@1 accuracy of the final prediction

Result.
As shown in Figure

When the first reasoning step is incorrect, the model’s final prediction is also likely to be incorrect. On average, final prediction accuracy drops by 40% when the first reasoning step is incorrect, with the most substantial decrease (43%) occurring for difficult questions (0–33% range) and a notable decline (33%) even for easier questions (67–100% range). In this section, we observe that the reasoning model is particularly vulnerable at the initial stage of the reasoning process; an error in the first step can propagate and substantially degrade the final prediction.
Can we develop a method to identify and retain more promising first reasoning steps while discarding suboptimal ones to enhance the overall generation efficiency?

4
Early pruning with hint from first step

In this section, we propose an efficient and straightforward sampling method to identify a promising first reasoning step. By doing so, we can terminate the generation process early when a suboptimal first step is detected, thereby reducing unnecessary computational overhead.

4.1
Problem definition

In contrast to the notation introduced in Section §
3
, we incorporate a random seed
\epsilon
to introduce stochasticity into the sampling process. Specifically, a sampled trace is computed as
t\oplus c_{T}=\mathcal{M}(p,\epsilon)
. By varying the random seed
\epsilon^{n}
, we obtain diverse generations, denoted as
t^{n}\oplus c^{n}_{T}=\mathcal{M}(p,\epsilon^{n})
, where
t^{n}=[t^{n}_{1},t^{n}_{2},\ldots,t^{n}_{T}]
.
5
5

5

In prior experiments, we sampled 64 CoT traces per question using 64 distinct random seeds
\{\epsilon^{n}\}_{n=1}^{64}
.

A widely adopted technique for reasoning tasks is majority voting or self-consistency generation
[
42
]
. To promote exploration of the reasoning space, models are typically sampled at high temperatures, resulting in diverse outputs. Majority voting then serves to aggregate these outputs by selecting the most frequent final prediction. Formally, majority voting over
K
samples is defined as:

q_{\text{maj@}K}=\text{mode}(\{q^{n}_{T}\}_{n=1}^{K})\quad\text{where}\quad q^{n}_{T}=g(c^{n}_{T})

However, for models generating long CoT traces, majority voting becomes computationally expensive, as it requires sampling
N
complete reasoning paths independently. In this section, we propose a more efficient approach that samples only
M
full traces, where
M<N
, while maintaining comparable majority voting performance to the one with
N
samplings.

4.2
Methodology

In Section §
3
, we demonstrated a strong positive correlation between the first reasoning step and the final prediction. This observation motivates a method that identifies the top
M
most promising first reasoning steps out of a total of
N
, and continues generation only for these selected
M
candidates, while discarding the remaining
(N-M)
.

Let a reasoning model generate
N
candidate first reasoning step
\{t_{1}^{1},t_{1}^{2},\dots,t_{1}^{N}\}
from a prompt
p
with different random seeds
\{\epsilon^{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
. Each
t_{1}^{n}
is the first reasoning step of a full reasoning trajectory. We define a scoring function
r:t_{1}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that estimates the promise of a first step, e.g., rating from a reward model. We then select the top
M
first steps based on their scores:

\mathcal{R}_{\text{top}}=\text{TopM}(\{r(t_{1}^{n})\}_{n=1}^{N})

Only the selected
M
first steps
\{t_{1}^{n}\mid n\in\mathcal{R}_{\text{top}}\}
are used for further multi-step generation. The remaining
(N-M)
are discarded. Since the first step typically requires only a small number of tokens to generate, this selection approach efficiently reduces computation by avoiding full sampling for the less promising
(N-M)
candidates.

Discussion.
With the emergence of long CoT models such as DeepSeek-R1, PRMs have received less attention for two main reasons: (1) PRM training is less scalable due to the high cost of acquiring step-wise annotated data, limiting their applicability to specific domains; and (2) most open-source PRMs are constrained by short context windows (e.g., 4K tokens), which are incompatible with the extended reasoning capabilities of long CoT models (e.g., 32K tokens). In this work, we demonstrate a promising approach that leverages short-context PRMs (or RMs) to guide long CoT models toward more efficient generation.

In Section

, we observe that reasoning models exhibit a pronounced vulnerability to initial errors, highlighting their limited capacity for self-correction. Despite the importance of this limitation, there currently exists no benchmark specifically designed to quantitatively assess the self-correction ability of long-CoT LLMs. To address this gap, we propose
LaBoR
(
L

a

5.1
Benchmark construction

We construct our self-correction benchmark, LaBoR, with the following automatic pipeline:

I. Question collection.
We compile a total of 100 questions sourced from AIME24, AIME25, and HMMT, along with 10 reference questions from AIMO2
[
48
]
. We exclude GPQA due to its multiple-choice format, which allows models to potentially guess correct answers. Additionally, we prioritize recent questions to reduce the risk of data leakage from training corpora.

II. First step generation.
For each question, we use DS-R1-Qwen-7B to generate up to 8 candidate first reasoning steps
\{t^{n}_{1}\}_{n=1}^{8}
, terminating generation when the trigger phrase “Alternatively” is produced. This phrase indicates a shift in reasoning, which we use to identify plausible initial errors. We discard reasoning steps that do not contain this phrase, as they likely represent coherent and correct thought processes. This results in a pool of 667 candidate first steps.

III. First step filtering.
We then prompt DS-R1-Qwen-7B to predict an answer
q_{1}^{n}
conditioned on the question and each individual first step, as
\mathcal{M}(p<\text{think}>t_{1}^{n}</\text{think}>)
. If
q_{1}^{n}
is correct, we infer that the reasoning step likely contains necessary reasoning for a correct prediction, and exclude it. After this filtering step, 495 first reasoning steps remain.

IV. Cross-model verification.
To further refine the dataset, we feed each remaining first step into both DS-R1-Qwen-7B and DS-R1-Qwen-14B using
\mathcal{M}(p<\text{think}>t_{1}^{n}\text{ Alternatively})
and collect the final prediction
q_{T}
. If either model produces a correct answer, we remove the corresponding first step, as this suggests the incorrect reasoning is too easily recoverable. This step yields 301 surviving reasoning steps.

V. Per-question sampling.
To reduce the cost of model evaluation, we randomly retain one first reasoning step per question. This results in a final set of 53 questions, each paired with a corresponding incorrect first reasoning step.

Descriptive statistics.
Among the 53 questions in LaBoR, 8 are sourced from AIME24, 17 from AIME25, 4 from AIMO2, and 24 from HMMT. The number of tokens in the first reasoning steps ranges from 344 to 3928, with an average of 1355 tokens. Two data points are illustrated in Appendix

5.2
Evaluation

LaBoR is designed as a self-correction benchmark, which requires the reasoning model to interpret the first incorrect reasoning step as if it were generated by the model itself. To evaluate this behavior, we prepend the first erroneous step to the model’s reasoning process using the format
\mathcal{M}(p<\text{think}>t_{1})
, prompting the model to continue reasoning from that point. Importantly, if this first step is instead included alongside the question in the user prompt
p
before the thinking tag, the model may misinterpret it as a distractor or part of the input query—an unintended behavior. Unfortunately, the desired format is only supported by self-deployed models. The APIs of closed-source models generally require both the question and the first step to be provided together in the user message, making it impossible to put the first reasoning step inside the thinking tags. 6
Conclusion

In this paper, we empirically demonstrate that the first reasoning step plays a critical role in determining the final outcome of a model’s reasoning process. Errors introduced early can significantly degrade overall performance. Motivated by this observation, we propose an efficient sampling strategy that identifies and retains high-quality first reasoning steps, reducing the inference computation up to 70% across three model families. To better understand models’ resilience to early errors, we introduce a benchmark featuring intentionally flawed first steps, enabling systematic assessment of self-correction capabilities of reasoning models. We hope this benchmark supports future research toward more robust and reliable reasoning in LLMs.

Appendix A
Limitations

Our work, while illuminating the critical role of the first reasoning step in the chain-of-thought processes of LLMs, possesses several limitations. First, our empirical evaluations primarily focus on mathematical and scientific reasoning tasks. Thus, the findings regarding the significance of first reasoning steps and the effectiveness of our pruning strategy may not directly generalize to broader domains such as code writing, commonsense reasoning, or creative writing tasks. Despite this limitation, our method possesses inherent flexibility and can potentially generalize or adapt to new scenarios with suitable modifications or training adjustments. Second, our proposed early pruning method relies on a process reward model (PRM) trained specifically on mathematical reasoning datasets, limiting its applicability to contexts where high-quality reward models are unavailable or challenging to develop. Lastly, the LaBoR benchmark specifically targets mathematical reasoning tasks and consists of a relatively small number of samples, potentially limiting the generalizability of our findings. However, the automated pipeline we propose makes it feasible to extend the benchmark to new domains or larger datasets.

Appendix B
Boarder impacts

This work identifies a critical vulnerability in LLMs: the disproportionate influence of the first reasoning step on the final output during long CoT reasoning. Our findings reveal that even minor early mistakes can drastically degrade model performance, and that current models have limited capacity for self-correction. To address this, we introduce a dual contribution: (1) a lightweight early pruning method that discards unpromising initial reasoning paths, reducing inference cost while preserving accuracy; and (2) LaBoR, a benchmark specifically designed to evaluate models’ self-correction abilities in the presence of flawed first steps.

Our method directly improves the efficiency and robustness of LLMs, two crucial goals for scaling their deployment. The proposed early pruning mechanism can reduce inference cost by up to 70% without performance degradation, contributing to the sustainability of LLM use.

Additionally, the LaBoR benchmark enables more rigorous evaluation of a model’s reasoning capabilities, especially its ability to self-correct—a key desideratum for safe deployment in high-stakes domains such as education, law, and scientific discovery. By spotlighting the limitations of current models, this work encourages the development of reasoning architectures that are more trustworthy and interpretable.

There are also risks. Efficiency improvements may lower the barrier to misuse—enabling broader deployment of LLMs in contexts where safety mechanisms are underdeveloped (e.g., disinformation generation or rapid, low-cost model abuse). The benchmark, while designed for rigorous evaluation, could potentially be reverse-engineered as an adversarial testbed to construct prompts that reliably induce reasoning failures.

More broadly, this work underscores a weakness in LLM reasoning—namely, overreliance on initial cues—which may deepen existing user trust in model outputs without fully addressing reliability. If misinterpreted, this may create a false sense of security in self-correction capabilities and lead to blind adoption in sensitive workflows.
