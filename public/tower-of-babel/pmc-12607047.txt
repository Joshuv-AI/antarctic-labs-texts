Effects of clinical learning environment on the professional identity of Chinese nursing students: multiple mediating effects of learning engagement and stress-related growth
Authors: Xueping Peng, Lu Yang, Simin Yang, Xiaojiao Wang, Weizheng Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe shortage of nurses is the global nursing industry’s problem; low professional identity is the main reason. As a reserve group of nurses, the professional identity of practicum nursing students influences the expansion and stability of the nursing workforce. Analysis of the factors and mechanisms influencing professional identity may provide essential guidance for managing and intervening in the nursing workforce. This study aimed to identify the positive predictors and pathways of professional identity of nursing students during their practicum.MethodsA quantitative cross-sectional study was conducted using the demographics, the clinical learning environment scale, the Utrecht work engagement scale-student scale, the Chinese version of the stress-related growth scale: short form, and the professional identity scale. Data were obtained from 952 Clinical nursing students from three medical schools in Sichuan Province. Data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and PROCESS v3.3 (IBM, v26.0) in SPSS.ResultsThe results showed that professional identity was positively related to the clinical learning environment (r = 0.407, P < 0.01), learning involvement(r = 0.506, P < 0.01), and stress-related growth(r = 0.455, P < 0.01). Similarly, the clinical learning environment was positively correlated with learning involvement (r = 0.484, P < 0.01) and stress-related growth (r = 0.546, P < 0.01). Meanwhile, the Process version 3.3 plug-in SPSS 26 tested and confirmed the multiple mediating effects of learning involvement and stress-related growth on the clinical learning environment and professional identity; the total effect of the clinical learning environment on professional identity was 0.284[ Bootstrap 95% CI: 0.260,0.308], of which the direct effect value was 0.155[ Bootstrap 95% CI: 0.124,0.185], accounting for 54.58% of the total effect and the total indirect effect was 0.129[ Bootstrap 95% CI: 0.124,0.185], accounting for 45.42% of the total effect.ConclusionsThis study found that improving the nursing students’ clinical learning environment, including interpersonal relationships, working atmosphere, team culture, student participation, task orientation, innovation, and personalization, as well as increasing their learning engagement, leads them to achieve stress-related growth, which both conducive to enhancing their professional identity as nurses. More importantly, improving the clinical learning environment can not only increase the learning input of nursing students but also help them better obtain stress-related growth, thus enhancing their professional identity. Teaching institutions need to strengthen the investigation and guidance of teaching hospitals, especially the construction of the working atmosphere, task orientation, and humanistic construction of teaching hospitals et al., create a good practice environment; on the other hand, it is necessary to pay attention to and guide nursing students’ practice dynamically and consciously plan their learning and career growth, which may be more conducive to the promotion of nursing students’ professional identity, it is also conducive to the expansion of the nursing team and improve the quality of nursing care.Keywords: Nursing students, Practicum, Professional identity, Clinical learning environment, Learning engagement, Stress-related growth

Introduction

The shortage of nurses has been a significant problem for the global nursing industry, reaching a crisis level [1]. If no action is taken, there will be a global shortfall of 5.7 million Nurses by 2030, according to the State of the World’s Care 2020 report by the World Health Organization and the International Council of Nurses [2]. Low professional identity (PI) is a significant factor contributing to nursing talent turnover [3]. As clinical reserve nurses, the PI of nursing students directly impacts the nursing field’s human resources and talent supply [4, 5]. However, research indicates that the level of professional identity among nursing students is not high, and their willingness to engage in clinical nursing work is not optimistic [6]. A lower PI can lead nursing students to change their career path midway through their studies, leave their jobs after graduation, or exhibit passive and negative behaviours in clinical work, severely affecting the stability of the nursing workforce and the quality of care.

In contrast, the higher the professional identity of nursing students, the more willing to continue working in the nursing field after graduation and maintain a positive attitude toward their profession [4, 7, 8]; therefore, enhancing nurse students’ PI may serve as a potential strategy for expanding and stabilising the nursing workforce, enhancing the quality and safety of healthcare services and worthing that the practice period is a transition stage from nursing students to nurses, and also a key period for the formation of PI [9–11]. Some studies have confirmed the significant impact of the internship process on the formation of PI [12, 13].

Theoretical background

Multiple factors, including gender, education level, enrollment intentions, and family economic status, influence nursing students’ PI levels. Arreciado et al. point out that exposure to clinical practice in nursing students produces PI [14]. Holland believes that nursing students’ PI results from an individual’s self-knowledge and professional environment [15]. Similarly, Goodson et al. argue that situational characteristics and intrinsic motivational factors influence PI [16]. The relevant theory is the Organismic integration theory.

Organic integration theory is a theory of human motivation that studies the interaction between external forces and intrinsic motivation. After decades of development, the theory has been studied in many fields and is also used to explain various research on work motivation. The theory posits that whether the external environment positively impacts individuals depends on whether the environment can stimulate the individual’s intrinsic motivation [17]. Empirical research supports this theory [18, 19]. According to this, we focused on the external factor—the learning environment (CLE) —and the internal factors—learning engagement (LE) and stress-related Growth (SRG), which may have a positive impact on the PI of nursing students. To our knowledge, no empirical study has directly examined the relationship between the fore. Exploring the possible positive relationship and action path among the four may not only enrich the theoretical research of organic integration theory and nursing students’ PI but also may provide theoretical guidance for managers and decision-makers to take intervention measures. Based on the organic theory and literature review, the following research hypotheses were proposed.

H1:CLE significantly predicts the PI of nursing students

The CLE provides the context and relationship conditions for forming nursing students’ PI. Nursing students will directly experience the future professional reality in a Clinical Environment [20]. A satisfactory CLE is conducive to the development of students’ clinical competence and has a significant impact on students’ experience outcomes [21, 22]. It also helps increase students’ motivation to pursue a career in nursing [23, 24]. Previous studies have shown that the higher the CLE, the stronger the PI of nursing students and the more positive their professional values [10, 25–27].

H2: LE mediates the relationship between CLE and PI

LE is a significant variable related to academic performance, achievement, perseverance, and retention [28, 29], proves that learners have sufficient physical and mental strength to study diligently and maintain high levels of vitality; it can also be interpreted as having a positive, fulfilling, and work-related mindset [30]. CLE is an independent factor affecting medical students’ LE [31–33]. A positive CLE can motivate nursing students to continue learning, and the better the students’ perception of the educational environment, the higher the degree of study investment [34, 35]. LE is also an important variable related to PI, as it positively correlates with academic performance and predicts PI [36]. Students who engage in learning are more focused on facing challenges and appear more interested in their majors [37]. García et al. reviewed empirical research on work engagement in nursing, showing that LE is positively related to nurses’ PI [38].

H3: SRG mediates the relationship between CLE and PI

Clinical nursing is a challenging profession. Previous research reports indicate that nursing students face various types of stress during their clinical learning process, which is significantly higher than that experienced by students in fields such as medicine, sociology, and pharmacy [39–41]. Several studies have found that stress threatens the optimal development of a PI [42, 43]. A stressful work pace and a stifling internship CLE can also diminish PI among nursing students [27]. Some individuals experience a series of positive growth experiences after undergoing stress, known as SRG. This positive growth can alleviate the adverse effects of stress and positively regulate and influence PI [44]. Noteworthy, to become a competent nursing staff, they need the ability to self-growth [45]. Individuals with higher levels of SRG may have better ability and confidence to perform nursing tasks and thus have a higher sense of PI [46].

Methodology

Participants

This is a cross-sectional study. Students from three Nursing specialities at medical schools in Sichuan Province participated in our online survey on the Questionnaire Star website from September to October 2024. Those students began their Clinical Practice in June or July 2024 at the same third-grade general hospital in Chengdu. In China, the Course study of nursing includes two parts: Theory and practice. The students need to complete the study of theory, experiment, and model in school and then participate in continuous learning at a teaching hospital for more than 8 months, where they will study in different departments to master the theory and skills required for various system diseases. To ensure the homogeneous teaching quality of nursing students with the same educational background across different schools, the education department has formulated a unified teaching syllabus and assessment mechanism for school education and the practice of teaching hospitals. Therefore, the curriculum, content, and quality of the equivalent education nursing students in the three schools are generally consistent. The hospital where the students participated in the internship was a tertiary hospital and also a teaching hospital that had undergone examination and review. The arrangement of practical teaching followed the unified syllabus of the Education Department, so this investigation and study are representative to a certain extent.

The Questionnaire Star website provides a safe and convenient way to collect anonymous data. The questionnaire link was sent to the student by the grade practice management teacher, who had been trained on the key points of the questionnaire’s anonymous collection and neutral interpretation of the question entries. The inclusion criteria for nursing students were: ①≥18 years old; ② interning in a hospital; ③ aware of the survey and volunteered to participate. The exclusion criteria were as follows: ① clinical practice ≤ 3 months (May not have adapted to clinical practice and have a basic understanding of clinical work); ② did not contact patients directly; ③ were not on duty as vacation or disease during the investigation period; ④ had a history of mental illness or depression. Each student completing the questionnaire anonymously can review and change their answers if necessary, but they have only one opportunity to submit the online questionnaire. They can stop the survey at any time if they do not want to participate. Submitted responses are considered voluntary participation.

Demographic variables

The self-designed demographic questionnaire was utilized in this study to collect the characteristics of participants, including gender (male, female), age, educational background (college degree, bachelor’s degree, or graduate degree), Student origin (town, rural), whether the voluntary choice of nursing professional, whether the immediate family engaged in medical undertakings were collected.

The clinical learning environment scale(CLES)

Dunn & Hansford [47] developed the scale, which was translated and validated by Zhu [48].The scale includes 6 dimensions: interpersonal relationship, working atmosphere and team culture, student participation, task orientation, innovation, and personalization, and 7 items in each dimension, items employs a 5-point Likert scoring method, with a minimum score of 1 and a maximum score of 5, corresponding to the options from “strongly disagree” to “agree.” A higher score indicates a higher level of evaluation of the learning environment. In previous studies, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of this scale was 0.931 [48], while in this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.988.

Utrecht Work Engagement Scale-Student ( UWES-S )

The scale was developed by Schaufeli & Salanova, et al. [29] and was translated and validated by Li & Huang [49]. The scale consists of 17 items and employs a 7-point Likert scale, with a minimum score of 1 and a maximum score of 7, corresponding to the options ranging from “never” to “always.” A higher score indicates a higher level of LE. The scale demonstrates good reliability and validity; in previous studies, Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.965 [49], while in this study, Cronbach’s α score for this scale was 0.919.

Chinese version of the stress-related growth scale: Short Form(SRG-SF)

Li et al. [50] translated and revised this scale based on the short version of the scale developed by Park et al. [51]. It consists of 15 items and uses a Likert-3 scoring system, where “not at all” is scored as 0 points, “a little” as 1 point, and “a lot” as 2 points. A higher score indicates a higher level of SRG. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of this scale was 0.945.

Professional Identity Scale for Nursing Students (PISNS)

This scale was developed by Hao et al. [52], consisting of a total of 17 items, utilizing a 5-point Likert scoring method, ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree,” with scores assigned from 1 to 5. The total score ranges from 17 to 85, with higher scores indicating a higher level of PI. This scale is widely used in China and is reliable and valid. In previous studies, the Cronbach’s α of this scale was 0.827 [52], while in this study, the Cronbach’s α of this scale was 0.963.

All the Chinese version scales in this study have been widely used in previous studies and have demonstrated good reliability and validity, making them suitable tools for research.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using Excel 2019 and SPSS 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) for data entry and analysis; continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical data were expressed as frequency and percentage. Differences in characteristics of each variable about PI were compared using independent sample t-tests or chi-square tests, and multiple group data were compared pairwise using the Least Significant Difference method. The correlation between continuous data and PI was analyzed using Pearson correlation analysis, and inter-group comparisons were represented using t-tests. Pearson correlation analysis also examined the relationship between learning environment, LE, SRG, and PI. The PROCESS v3.3 (IBM, v26.0) in SPSS was used to analyze the mediating effect of LE and SRG on the relationship between the CLE and nursing students’ PI. A bootstrap method was used to estimate 5000 resamples, with a significance level of α = 0.05 (two-tailed).

Discussion

In our study, the level of PI among nursing interns is slightly higher than that reported as moderate in most research [53–56] and lower than the findings during the COVID-19 period [57, 58]. The difference may be related to a specific social period. The positive public evaluation of the nursing profession during the COVID-19 period may have led to a higher PI. Meanwhile, global economic growth has slowed in recent years, making nursing an increasingly attractive employment option and enhancing the PI within the field. On the other hand, the Survey respondents were in the early stages of their clinical internships; they had just entered the clinical environment and may still have retained a sense of novelty about the profession. Furthermore, the subjects were predominantly chosen voluntarily for nursing [15]. There may not be a specific psychological gap in professional expectations that leads them to low PI. In summary, educators should monitor the PI levels of nursing students, providing timely intervention and support to those in the lower range to reduce the loss of nursing talent.

This research findings indicate that having a bachelor’s degree or higher is a barrier to PI. The results are consistent with those of previous studies [59, 60]. Students with higher educational levels may have higher expectations for their careers, and the tedious nature of clinical work, as well as irregular night shifts, may differ significantly from these expectations, leading to a lower sense of purpose in their work. On the other hand, students with higher educational levels may experience less cross-disciplinary employment pressure and have more employment opportunities or a greater likelihood of changing majors for further studies, thus having a lower sense of PI. In light of this, educational institutions may need to consider practical strategies to enhance the PI and commitment levels of nursing students with a bachelor’s degree or higher. Additionally, clinical settings should consider recruiting a certain proportion of Junior College degrees nurses, as they may possess a higher level of PI and commitment, while also encouraging and facilitating continued education to improve their qualifications, ensuring a high-quality professional standard, which may be one of the effective measures to address the current shortage of nurses.

Notably, our study extends the research scope of the Organismic Integration Theory and demonstrates its scientific validity in explaining the interaction between external forces and intrinsic motivation at the PI level among nursing students. External factors-CLE has a positive effect on PI, additionally, it can positively influence the PI by affecting the internal factors-LE and SRG. Clinical teaching hospitals may need to create a harmonious and warm learning environment [61]; besides, educators should encourage intern nurses to plan their career growth consciously, increasing their LE while paying attention to their stress levels, actively guide to cope with stress and facilitate growth under pressure to enhance their professional confidence and PI [6, 43, 62]. It is noteworthy that among the various factors influencing nursing students’ PI, the educational environment is a significant and highly operational aspect. For instance, improvements can be made through practical and effective methods in areas such as the hospital environment, teacher selection, teaching attitudes, teaching objectives and models, and establishing social relationships. Therefore, this study not only provides a direction for interventions to improve nursing students’ PI but also offers a feasible intervention pathway.

Limitations

This study is cross-sectional, and conclusions should be made cautiously regarding causal relationships among variables. This research only investigated the situation of nursing students at the early stage of clinical internships; future studies could conduct longitudinal research to explore the relationships among variables.The nursing students in this survey were in the early stages of their internship. Due to the influence of factors such as cognition, results at different internship stages may vary. Future research may consider conducting longitudinal studies to compare cognitive changes at different stages of the internship. The subjects included in this study were only nursing students from clinical internships in the western provinces of China and from the same clinical teaching hospital; due to cultural, economic, and regional factors and characteristics of the environment, the generalizability of the conclusions may be limited; future research should expand to different regions and ethnic groups, or take multi-regional sampling to validate and extend our findings. This study focused on the impact of the CLE, LE, and SRG on nursing students’ PI. Other factors may influence or moderate nursing students’ PI, and future research could consider including these potential influencing factors.

Conclusion

Internships are crucial in integrating theory and practice for nursing students, with learning and growth as the central themes. The extent of LE and the ability to continuously grow under various pressures during the internship may significantly impact nursing students’ PI. Our research confirms the positive predictive role and pathways of the CLE, LE, and SRG on the PI of nursing interns. The findings provide a theoretical basis for managing and intervening in the PI of nursing interns and offer important references for the long-term and stable development of the nursing workforce.
