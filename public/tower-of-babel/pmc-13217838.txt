A case of infective endocarditis caused by Streptococcus gordonii complicated with bacterial meningitis and cerebral infarction —— Application of metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS)
Authors: Jinchun Ye, Lu Ye, Wei Sun, Shuhua Xie, Zhaohui Lai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study reports a case of infective endocarditis (IE) caused by Streptococcus gordonii. The patient presented with cerebral infarction as the initial manifestation, complicated by bacterial meningitis and mitral regurgitation. The diagnosis of Streptococcus gordonii-induced infective endocarditis was facilitated by metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). Streptococcus gordonii was detected by CSF mNGS within 40 h after admission, which was 30 h earlier than the positive result of blood culture. During anti-infective therapy, the patient experienced recurrent thromboembolic events and underwent emergency mechanical thrombectomy due to occlusion of the left vertebral artery. Despite aggressive treatment, the patient eventually died of heart failure. This case indicates that Streptococcus gordonii is a rare pathogen of infective endocarditis, and its clinical presentation complicated by cerebral infarction and bacterial meningitis is distinctive; particularly, complex cases requiring mechanical thrombectomy are extremely rare in clinical practice. As an important complement to conventional bacterial culture, mNGS can shorten diagnostic delay, especially in patients with negative blood or CSF cultures. For patients with concurrent cerebral infarction and meningitis, the possibility of infective endocarditis should be highly suspected, and indications for valve replacement surgery should be evaluated as early as possible in high-risk cases.

Introduction

Streptococcus gordonii is a Gram-positive bacterium belonging to the viridans streptococci group, which commonly colonizes the oral cavity and participates in the formation of oral biofilms. As a rare causative pathogen of infective endocarditis (IE), although it is a commensal organism of the oral cavity, cases of IE caused by this bacterium complicated with concurrent cerebral infarction and bacterial meningitis are far less common than those caused by other pathogens, with only sporadic reports available to da [1], [2]. Patients with subacute IE usually present with atypical and insidious symptoms, which predispose to delayed diagnosis and misdiagnosis. The occurrence of central nervous system complications significantly increases the mortality of patients with IE; therefore, early identification and timely intervention are critical. In view of the diagnostic challenges and poor prognosis of such complex cases, this case report aims to highlight the clinical value of mNGS in etiological diagnosis and the key therapeutic points of IE complicated with central nervous system complications. Herein, we report a case of IE with cerebral infarction as the initial manifestation and concurrent bacterial meningitis, focusing on its diagnostic approach and therapeutic insights.

Case description

A 45-year-old male patient presented with caries and pain in the left maxillary premolar 6 months before admission, and self-administered analgesics. Two weeks prior to admission, he developed recurrent fever with a peak temperature of 38.5 °C. Three days before admission, dizziness, unsteady gait, headache, and mild dysarthria occurred, without facial asymmetry, limb weakness, chest pain, chest distress, or expectoration. His past medical history was unremarkable. He denied hypertension, diabetes mellitus, smoking, alcohol consumption, intravenous drug use, hereditary diseases, substance abuse, or recent dental procedures. Neurological examination showed somnolence and mild dysarthria, with no other abnormalities in cranial nerve function. Muscle strength of all four limbs was normal. Babinski sign, Chaddock sign, and Oppenheim sign were negative bilaterally. Nuchal rigidity, positive Kernig sign, and Brudzinski sign were present. A grade 4/6 systolic murmur was audible over the mitral valve area. No spinal percussion tenderness, Janeway lesions, or Osler nodes were observed. Empirical anti-infective therapy was initiated on day 1 of admission, with intravenous vancomycin (1 g every 12 h, dose adjusted according to renal function) combined with meropenem (2 g every 8 h). Before antibiotic administration, three sets of blood cultures, CSF culture, and CSF mNGS specimens were collected. CSF culture was negative, whereas CSF mNGS identified S. gordonii as the dominant pathogen with a read count ratio of 94.1%; the proportion of other detected microorganisms was extremely low and considered contamination or background signal (Fig. 4 A). Accordingly, the antibiotic regimen was promptly adjusted to vancomycin (1 g every 12 h) plus ceftriaxone (2 g every 12 h).At 72 h after admission, blood culture confirmed S. gordonii infection. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing showed that the strain was sensitive to vancomycin, linezolid, cefotaxime, and penicillin. After 3 days of anti-infective treatment, the patient’s body temperature returned to normal. Repeat blood cultures on day 7 and day 10 of admission were both negative. On day 14 of admission, the patient’s cerebral infarction acutely worsened, with aggravated dysarthria, dysphagia, and decreased consciousness to stupor. Follow-up brain MRI revealed new multiple acute infarctions in the pons and corpus callosum, as well as occlusion of the V4 segment of the left vertebral artery (Figs. 1B, 1 C). Repeated TTE showed anterior mitral valve malposition, posterior mitral valve prolapse, and severe mitral regurgitation, with no vegetations observed.Although the neurological deficits associated with the new infarction and vertebral artery occlusion were relatively mild, interventional radiologists decided to perform mechanical thrombectomy considering the risk of secondary aneurysmal hemorrhage due to vascular destruction caused by infected thrombi. A 10-mm yellow thrombus was retrieved intraoperatively (Fig. 2A); however, angiography demonstrated persistent occlusion of the left vertebral artery with contrast extravasation, indicating an extremely high bleeding risk if the procedure continued. The intervention was therefore terminated. mNGS of the thrombus sample showed only a very low background signal of S. gordonii (Fig. 4B), which was attributed to effective anti-infective therapy and a marked reduction in pathogen load in the circulation.Postoperatively, the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for further management. During ICU stay, his condition deteriorated further and acute left heart failure occurred. During preparation for valve replacement surgery, the patient suffered sudden cardiac arrest and died despite resuscitation efforts.

Discussion

Streptococcus gordonii is a Gram-positive bacterium belonging to the viridans streptococci group, which commonly colonizes the oral cavity and participates in the formation of oral biofilms. Under conditions such as immunosuppression, trauma, or invasive medical procedures, this bacterium can bind to platelets via various adhesion proteins and form vegetations on cardiac valves, thereby triggering infection [3−4]. In the present case, the patient had a history of toothache 6 months before admission, and poor oral hygiene with multiple caries in the left upper molars was noted on admission, which may have predisposed him to IE. Studies have demonstrated that viridans streptococci are more likely to induce mitral valve prolapse compared with other bacterial infections, which may be related to the crucial role of unique surface adhesion proteins in the pathogenesis of IE [5−6]. Approximately 20% of patients with IE develop central nervous system complications, among which stroke accounts for up to 60% [3]. The patient in this case presented with cerebral infarction at admission, and early imaging findings provided a basis for the diagnosis and treatment of cerebral infarction. Given the absence of common risk factors for cerebral infarction (e.g., dyslipidemia, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, arterial dissection, and sickle cell disease), embolic stroke was considered the underlying etiology.Meningitis is a rare complication of IE and is easily overlooked in clinical practice. A retrospective analysis of 1345 patients with IE reported that the incidence of meningitis was only 6%, and Staphylococcus aureus was the most common pathogen in patients with neurological complications [4]. Compared with IE caused by other viridans streptococci, S. gordonii-related IE is more prone to multivalvular involvement, and the main clinical manifestations include fever and cardiac murmurs. Approximately 20% of patients develop cerebral embolism, while central nervous system infection is extremely rare [5]. The concurrent meningitis and cerebral infarction secondary to S. gordonii infection reported herein is clinically distinctive; particularly, complex cases requiring mechanical thrombectomy are extremely rare in clinical practice. Patients with IE complicated by central nervous system involvement usually have a poor prognosis, especially those with moderate to severe cerebral infarction or intracranial hemorrhage [10]. The patient in this case experienced recurrent embolic events with progressive deterioration and eventually died of heart failure.

According to the modified Duke criteria [6], the diagnosis of IE requires evidence of endocardial infection, such as echocardiographic findings (vegetations, abscess formation, new dehiscence of a prosthetic valve, or new valvular regurgitation) combined with positive blood culture results. In this patient, TTE did not reveal obvious vegetations; however, mitral valve prolapse with moderate regurgitation was present, blood culture confirmed S. gordonii infection, and CSF mNGS provided microbiological evidence of the same pathogen. Thus, the diagnosis of IE was firmly established.mNGS played a vital role in the diagnosis and management of this case. Although blood culture finally confirmed S. gordonii infection, CSF mNGS detected the pathogen earlier than blood culture, supporting the timely adjustment of empirical antimicrobial therapy and facilitating early clinical suspicion of IE to accelerate the diagnostic process. Slow-growing bacteria such as viridans streptococci and Brucella species usually require 3 days or longer for detection and may even yield negative culture results. Rapid and accurate mNGS enables earlier targeted therapy, which is crucial for improving the prognosis of patients with severe infections.

Currently, mNGS has been widely used for pathogen identification, which can improve diagnostic accuracy, shorten the time to confirmation, and reduce missed diagnoses, especially for culture-negative specimens or infections caused by rare pathogens [7]. However, both false-negative and false-positive results should be considered when using mNGS for the diagnosis of central nervous system infections. A false-negative result may occur because mNGS is a direct detection method that requires a sufficient amount of microbial nucleic acid in the sample. A false-positive result may arise from environmental microbes and their nucleic acids, even when best practices are implemented to minimize contamination [8]. In the present case, multiple background microorganisms were detected in CSF by mNGS, which requires careful discrimination in clinical interpretation.Studies have reported that the positive rate and sensitivity of plasma mNGS are significantly higher than those of conventional culture, and the combination of traditional culture and plasma mNGS can markedly improve the detection rate of pathogens [9]. Early application of mNGS in bloodstream infections may better guide antimicrobial therapy. In addition to pathogen detection in routine specimens, pathological analysis of retrieved thrombi to clarify the etiology of cerebral infarction and guide anti-infective treatment has gained increasing attention. A histopathological study of thrombi from 65 patients undergoing thrombectomy found bacteria in 4 samples, and microbiological analysis provided guidance for early anti-infective therapy [10]. In this patient, mNGS of the retrieved thrombus during anti-infective treatment showed an extremely low level of S. gordonii, suggesting that embolic events may still occur even if most bacteria in the circulation have been eliminated by adequate antibiotic therapy.

Early administration of antibiotics is crucial for patients with IE. According to the 2015 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines, vancomycin, penicillin G, and ceftriaxone are commonly used for the treatment of streptococcal endocarditis, with a treatment course of 4–6 weeks, and aminoglycosides may be added for the first 2 weeks [11]. The 2021 ESC/EACTS guidelines recommend that urgent or emergency surgery for left-sided valvular IE should be evaluated based on three key aspects: severity of heart failure, uncontrolled infection, and prevention of embolic events. For patients with neurological complications who meet the indications for emergency surgery, the operation should be performed promptly unless intracranial hemorrhage is present; intervention may be delayed for 3–4 weeks if the patient’s condition is stable [12], [13], [14]. Considering that the patient’s blood cultures became negative after anti-infective therapy, no acute heart failure was observed, and the patient was in the acute phase of cerebral infarction, a multidisciplinary team consisting of neurologists and cardiac surgeons suggested performing valve surgery 2 weeks after the initiation of anti-infective treatment.The role of emergency mechanical thrombectomy in patients with IE complicated by intracranial large-vessel embolism remains controversial. Global literature and multicenter retrospective analyses, including a Spanish cohort study that compared 50 patients with large-vessel occlusion caused by bacterial embolism secondary to IE who underwent neurointerventional thrombectomy with patients with non-infectious etiologies, demonstrated comparable efficacy and safety between the two groups. This indicates that mechanical thrombectomy is increasingly widely used and has achieved certain success in cases of large-vessel occlusion secondary to IE [15]. Nevertheless, the operation in this patient was terminated prematurely due to intraoperative hemorrhage, and no obvious aneurysm was found at the surgical site. As a common complication of thrombectomy, intracranial hemorrhage requires careful evaluation before thrombectomy in patients with cerebral embolism secondary to IE, with comprehensive consideration of the impact of bleeding on the subsequent management of IE.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that Streptococcus gordonii can cause severe central nervous system complications, including cerebral embolism and meningitis. mNGS remains an important complement to conventional culture, especially for culture-negative specimens, and can reduce diagnostic delay. For patients with cerebral infarction complicated by meningitis, the possibility of IE should be highly suspected, and a comprehensive etiological workup should be performed in a timely manner. Mechanical thrombectomy may be a feasible therapeutic option for IE-related stroke complicated by large-vessel occlusion. However, vascular wall integrity must be carefully evaluated to avoid hemorrhagic complications that could compromise subsequent surgical management of IE.
