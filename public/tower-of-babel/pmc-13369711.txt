Decoding shared pathogenic networks of oxidative stress in neuropsychiatric disorders to prioritize multi-target therapeutics from natural products
Authors: Xuan Xu, Zhu Ni, Yan-Bin Wang, Sha-Sha Fan, Yanan Du, Xu Wang, Xiang-Yu Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeurodegenerative and psychiatric disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease (PD), Huntington’s disease (HD), and schizophrenia (SZ), are characterized by progressive neuronal loss and synaptic dysfunction. Despite their severity, effective disease-modifying treatments remain unavailable, largely due to the elusive nature of their underlying molecular mechanisms.MethodsTo elucidate these mechanisms, we conducted an integrative systems biology analysis incorporating transcriptomic datasets, in silico proteomic networks, and inferred metabolomic profiles. Machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) models were employed to identify regulatory networks associated with oxidative stress, immune response, and synaptic signaling. Furthermore, network pharmacology approaches were applied to explore multi-target intervention strategies using bioactive compounds from traditional Chinese medicine (TCM).ResultsOur integrative analysis revealed extensive overlap in dysregulated biological processes across all four disorders, particularly involving oxidative stress and immune activation. We identified TP53, NFE2L2, and PPP3CA as central regulatory hubs driving these pathologies. Notably, computational predictions highlighted that TCM-derived compounds, specifically stigmasterol and dodecanoic acid, exhibit promising multi-target effects for modulating these oxidative and inflammatory responses. Subsequent in vivo experimental validation was performed exclusively to corroborate the disease-associated pathways and core gene dysregulation in an Aβ42-induced AD model. These findings demonstrated molecular and behavioral phenotypes consistent with our multi-dimensional computational predictions, establishing a robust mechanistic rationale that merits future in vivo pharmacological validation for the predicted bioactive compounds.ConclusionThis study highlights the utility of a multi-disease, multi-dimensional framework in uncovering shared pathogenic signatures. By integrating computational analytics with pharmacological modeling and experimental validation, we identified key regulatory genes and natural compounds with therapeutic potential. These findings provide a theoretical foundation for the development of multi-target, personalized treatment strategies against neurodegeneration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10565-026-10208-w.Keywords: Brain Disorders, Systems Biology, Network Pharmacology, Machine Learning, Oxidative Stress, Traditional Chinese medicine

Introduction

Neurological and psychiatric disorders, including Alzheimer's disease (AD), Parkinson's disease (PD), Huntington's disease (HD), and schizophrenia (SZ), represent a significant and escalating global health burden, affecting millions worldwide (GBD 2021 Nervous System Disorders Collaborators 2024). These disorders are characterized by complex pathophysiological changes, including progressive neuronal loss (AD, PD, HD), synaptic dysfunction, and neurotransmitter dysregulation (SZ), ultimately leading to severe cognitive, motor, and behavioral impairments (Poewe et al. 2017; Long and Holtzman 2019). Despite decades of intensive research, the molecular mechanisms underlying these conditions remain incompletely understood, and current therapeutic strategies primarily focus on symptomatic relief rather than addressing the fundamental causes of neurodegeneration. This underscores an urgent need for deeper insights into the complex biological processes driving these disorders (Cummings et al. 2021; Ross and Tabrizi 2011).

A major challenge in studying neurological and psychiatric disorders is their clinical and molecular heterogeneity. While each disease exhibits distinct clinical phenotypes and primary pathological hallmarks, emerging evidence suggests they converge on several fundamental, shared biological mechanisms. These widely recognized shared pathways predominantly include chronic neuroinflammation driven by aberrant glial activation, mitochondrial dysfunction leading to severe oxidative stress, impaired protein homeostasis (proteostasis), and progressive synaptic deterioration (Zhang et al. 2023b). However, the majority of existing studies have focused on individual diseases in isolation, overlooking the potential for cross-disease analyses that could reveal common therapeutic targets (Gadhave et al. 2024). This fragmented approach has hindered the identification of broadly applicable treatments, highlighting the importance of integrative research spanning multiple neurological and psychiatric conditions (Khan and Iturria-Medina 2024).

Furthermore, the rationale for integrating schizophrenia (SZ) into a comparative framework alongside classical neurodegenerative diseases (AD, PD, and HD) is grounded in the emerging paradigm of "neuroprogression" (Steullet et al. 2016). Traditionally viewed strictly as a neurodevelopmental psychiatric disorder, SZ is increasingly recognized to exhibit progressive, neurodegenerative-like structural brain reductions and cognitive decline over time. Recent high-impact literature proposes a "superimposed oxidative stress damage" model, suggesting that early neurodevelopmental vulnerabilities in SZ are subsequently compounded by chronic, progressive redox imbalances and neuroinflammation during adulthood (Nagendra et al. 2026). This continuous cycle of oxidative stress drives premature cellular senescence, parvalbumin interneuron loss, and synaptic deterioration, bridging the pathophysiological gap between psychiatric neurodevelopment and classical neurodegeneration (Cuenod et al. 2022). Therefore, co-analyzing SZ with AD, PD, and HD provides a highly justified biological continuum to explore shared vulnerabilities in neuroimmune crosstalk and redox homeostasis (Perluigi et al. 2024; Morén et al. 2025). Advances in high-throughput omics technologies offer significant opportunities to elucidate the molecular landscape of these disorders (Hasin et al. 2017). However, the substantial scale and inherent complexity of data generated by these technologies pose significant challenges for comprehensive integration and interpretation. Traditional statistical approaches demonstrate limited capacity to fully capture the intricate interactions among genes, proteins, and pathways that drive disease progression (Emmert-Streib et al. 2020). To overcome these limitations, machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) methodologies have emerged as powerful analytical tools, offering robust capabilities for modeling high-dimensional data and detecting subtle patterns that conventional methods may fail to identify (Ching et al. 2018). Nevertheless, the application of ML and DL approaches in comparative analyses across multiple neurodegenerative disorders remains inadequately explored (Gawehn et al. 2016).

Network pharmacology represents an advanced analytical paradigm for elucidating complex interactions between drugs, molecular targets, and disease mechanisms (Hopkins 2008). This systematic approach employs network representations to identify critical molecular nodes and regulatory pathways that influence pathological processes, subsequently revealing potential therapeutic interventions (Barabási et al. 2011). However, current network pharmacology investigations have largely focused on single diseases or isolated drug-target interactions, limiting opportunities for cross-disease therapeutic insights (Zhao and Iyengar 2012). Furthermore, the successful integration of multi-omics data with network pharmacology analyses presents substantial methodological challenges, particularly concerning the temporal dynamics of biological systems and the computational complexity inherent in large-scale dataset processing (Liu et al. 2018).

Building upon these foundational insights, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of four major neurological disorders (AD, PD, HD, and SZ) through the implementation of advanced computational approaches, incorporating systems-level integration with ML and DL models. We systematically examined publicly available transcriptomic datasets alongside curated genomic risk profiles and proteomic interaction databases to elucidate shared and disease-specific molecular signatures, emphasizing pathways associated with oxidative stress, immune response, and synaptic function (Zhang et al. 2016). Our investigation further extended to network pharmacology analyses exploring the therapeutic efficacy of traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) compounds, which exhibit established multi-target capabilities in modulating essential biological processes (Li and Zhang 2013).

While oxidative stress and neuroinflammation are well-established contributors to brain disorders (Dash et al. 2025), the conventional paradigm often investigates these mechanisms in a reductionist, single-disease manner (Dharshini et al. 2025). Therefore, a key contribution of our study lies not merely in reconfirming the existence of these established mechanisms, but rather in deploying a multi-dimensional, cross-disease Artificial Intelligence (AI) integration framework to decode the highly complex, non-linear transcriptomic networks that orchestrate this shared stress response, rather than solely reconfirming the presence of oxidative stress. This cross-disorder perspective facilitates the discovery of common molecular hubs that conventional statistical methods fail to capture. Furthermore, by systematically coupling these AI-derived genomic hubs with network pharmacology, we transformed these shared molecular signatures into actionable, multi-target therapeutic networks. This approach explicitly identifies how specific bioactive compounds from traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) can simultaneously modulate these convergent pathological networks across multiple conditions.

To validate the robustness of our computational predictions regarding disease mechanisms, we designed in vivo experiments using well-established animal models of neurodegenerative diseases. These in vivo experiments were specifically utilized to corroborate the pathological pathways and core target dysregulation associated with an Aβ42-induced AD model, employing behavioral assays, histological analyses, and transcriptomic profiling Importantly, the predicted TCM compounds were not administered in these current animal experiments. Instead, by experimentally validating the upstream genetic hubs, we establish a compelling translational rationale that warrants empirical evaluation of these multi-target compounds in subsequent pharmacological studies. The experimental findings not only corroborate the computational insights but also emphasize the translational potential of this integrative approach.

To navigate this multi-layered framework, our study was logically structured into five interconnected phases: (1) Discovery: utilizing WGCNA on bulk transcriptomics to identify shared pathogenic modules; (2) Prioritization: deploying ML and DL architectures to distill these massive modules into core predictive hub genes; (3) Cellular Resolution: integrating single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to map the specific cell-type expression of these hubs; (4) Translational Prediction: applying network pharmacology and molecular docking to identify TCM-derived compounds that structurally target these hubs; and finally, (5) Biological Validation: utilizing an in vivo Aβ42 model to physically corroborate the dysregulation of these upstream genetic hubs, thereby anchoring our computational predictions in biological reality.

In summary, this study integrates large-scale transcriptomic data, network pharmacology, and advanced computational techniques in the context of neurological and psychiatric disorders research. By focusing on shared molecular mechanisms and leveraging ML and DL approaches, we provide further insights into the pathogenesis of these disorders and highlight potential therapeutic strategies with multi-target applications. Our research aims to enhance the understanding of these disorders and contribute to the development of more effective treatments that benefit patients across multiple disease categories (Liu et al. 2024). Experimental procedures

Bulk RNA-seq data collection

Bulk RNA-seq datasets for Alzheimer's disease (AD), Parkinson's disease (PD), schizophrenia (SZ), and Huntington's disease (HD) were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. The analyzed datasets included GSE33000, GSE63063, GSE48350, and GSE11882 for AD; GSE99039, GSE5747, and GSE28894 for PD; GSE38485, GSE73129, and GSE12649 for SZ; and GSE33000, GSE135589, and GSE51799 for HD. Detailed sample information is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

The GSE33000 dataset comprises gene expression profiles from 624 dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) samples, encompassing AD and HD patients and control subjects, analyzed using a custom Agilent 44 K microarray platform (GPL4372). The dataset represents predominantly Caucasian populations, with stringent quality control protocols implemented to ensure data integrity. The GSE48350 dataset includes microarray expression profiles obtained from four distinct brain regions (hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, superior frontal cortex, and post-central gyrus) in AD cases and normal controls, with subjects ranging from 20 to 99 years of age. For comprehensive analysis of age-related and AD-specific transcriptional changes, the AD samples in GSE48350 were analyzed in conjunction with normal control samples from GSE11882, a dataset comprising exclusively non-diseased brain tissue specimens.

For PD analysis, we integrated single-cell transcriptional profiles from GSE157783, which characterized idiopathic Parkinson's disease (IPD), with bulk RNA sequencing datasets (GSE99039, GSE5747, GSE28894) representing various PD-affected brain regions. These datasets collectively revealed molecular signatures associated with PD pathogenesis, including dopaminergic neuron degeneration and neuroinflammation processes.

To ensure data compatibility across different experimental platforms and protocols, we applied batch effect correction using the ComBat algorithm from the sva package (version 3.44.0) (Leek et al. 2012). This preprocessing step facilitated the integration of diverse datasets, ensuring consistency and reproducibility in downstream analyses.

Collection and integration of drug targets, bioactive compounds, and corresponding herbal medicine information

We compiled comprehensive drug-target interaction data from multiple specialized databases, including the Integrated Traditional Chinese Medicine Database (ITCM) (Tian et al. 2023) and the Bioinformatics Analysis Tool for Molecular Mechanism of Traditional Chinese Medicine (BATMAN-TCM) (Liu et al. 2016). These were further supplemented by other widely recognized and authoritative resources in the field, such as the Drug-Gene Interaction Database (DGIDB) (Cotto et al. 2018), Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD) (Davis et al. 2021), PubChem (Wang et al. 2009), and DrugBank (Knox et al. 2024). These resources provided extensive information on TCM herbs, chemical constituents, and their molecular interactions.

The ITCM database provided annotations on TCM herbs and bioactive compounds based on pharmacological and clinical studies, while BATMAN-TCM offered curated data on herbal ingredients and their molecular targets through network pharmacology analyses. We further incorporated data from specialized TCM databases, including the Herbal Ingredients’ Targets Database (HIT) (Yan et al. 2022), Encyclopedia of Traditional Chinese Medicine (ETCM) (Zhang et al. 2023c), TCM-ID (Huang et al. 2018), TCMID (Huang et al. 2018), Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology (TCMSP) (Ru et al. 2014), and TCM Database@Taiwan (Chen 2011).

We supplemented database-derived information with text-mined data from recent literature to capture newly reported bioactive compounds. However, to ensure the high fidelity and biological relevance of our network, rigorous screening criteria were subsequently applied. Specifically, we strictly excluded compound-target interactions derived solely from pure text-mining without further empirical verification. We exclusively retained interactions supported by direct physical binding evidence (e.g., surface plasmon resonance, X-ray crystallography) or robust functional validation (e.g., in vitro enzymatic assays, in vivo pharmacological models). Data validation involved cross-referencing across multiple sources and removing redundant entries. We prioritized compound-target interactions based on experimental or computational evidence reliability. The final curated dataset contained 374,091 TCM-ingredient-target interactions, establishing a comprehensive resource for investigating TCM molecular mechanisms in neurological disorders.

Single-cell RNA sequencing data collection

To investigate cellular heterogeneity and disease-specific molecular changes, we obtained single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets from the Brain Cell Atlas and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). For AD, single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) data were derived from the prefrontal cortex (Brodmann area 10) of 48 brain specimens from the Religious Orders Study and Memory and Aging Project (ROSMAP) (Mathys et al. 2019). The cohort comprised 24 individuals with elevated β-amyloid levels and pathological hallmarks of AD, and 24 individuals with minimal β-amyloid burden or pathological features. The dataset generated 80,660 single-nucleus transcriptomic profiles using droplet-based snRNA-seq technology.

For PD, we analyzed the GSE157783 dataset containing snRNA-seq data from midbrain tissue of six control and five idiopathic Parkinson's disease (IPD) cases. This dataset provided a high-resolution atlas for exploring cell-state changes associated with IPD, particularly in dopaminergic neurons and glial cells (Smajić et al. 2022).

In HD, we analyzed the GSE180928 dataset containing single-cell transcriptomic profiles from the cingulate cortex, caudate nucleus, and nucleus accumbens. The dataset, derived from postmortem brain samples of seven HD patients and six controls, revealed disrupted oligodendrocyte maturation, altered cell-to-cell signaling, and dysregulation of the Prkce-SWI/SNF axis (Lim et al. 2022).

For SZ, we analyzed the GSE184878 dataset containing scRNA-seq data from cerebral organoids generated from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs). The organoids were derived from fibroblast-reprogrammed iPSCs from SZ patients (n = 3) and matched controls (n = 4), with single-cell suspensions prepared using the 10X Genomics Chromium platform. This dataset illuminated neurodevelopmental processes affected by SZ-associated genomic risk loci (Notaras et al. 2022).

Animal model and experimental procedures

Male Sprague–Dawley rats (8 weeks old, 200 ± 20 g) were procured from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Benxi, China). A total of 20 rats were randomly divided into two groups: an Alzheimer’s disease (AD) model group and a control sham-operated group. The sample size of 10 animals per group was not derived from a formal a priori effect-size power calculation, but was rigorously justified utilizing Mead's Resource Equation, a standardized statistical method for animal research (Festing 2006). According to this equation (E=N-B-T, where N is total degrees of freedom, B is block degrees of freedom, and T is treatment degrees of freedom), our experimental design yields an Error degree of freedom (E) of 18 (i.e., 19-0-1). An E value between 10 and 20 indicates an adequately powered experiment that effectively detects significant inter-group differences while strictly adhering to the 3Rs (Reduction) principles of animal ethics. Furthermore, this sample size aligns with established empirical precedents in neuropharmacological studies utilizing the acute intracerebroventricular Aβ42 model, which exhibits highly robust phenotypic penetrance and lower intra-group variance compared to aging models. Prior to the experiments, all animals were acclimated for 1 week under controlled environmental conditions (temperature: 22–24 °C, humidity: 50%–60%, 12-h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to food and water. The animal experiments were ethically approved by the Institutional Review Board of Hubei Minzu University (No.2024092).

To establish the AD model, 5 µL of Aβ42 solution (Yaoqiang Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) was bilaterally injected into the lateral ventricles via intracerebroventricular administration. Cognitive impairments induced by Aβ42 were assessed using the Morris water maze (MWM) system, constructed by the Institute of Materia Medica, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (Shanghai, China). Prior to Aβ42 injection or sham surgery, rats underwent a 5-day training period to learn to locate a hidden platform in the third quadrant of the maze. Two days after surgery, cognitive function was evaluated by conducting probe trials, during which the platform was removed. Each rat’s performance was quantified by recording the time spent crossing the platform region within 1 min. Testing sessions were initiated from the first, second, and fourth quadrants for each trial, and swimming trajectories were recorded and analyzed using a video capture system.

Following behavioral assessments, animals were euthanized, and bilateral hippocampi were dissected for further analyses. Nissl staining was performed on hippocampal sections (n = 6 per group) to assess neuronal density and morphological changes. Additionally, bilateral hippocampal tissues (n = 3 per group) were subjected to RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol, and RNA quality was assessed with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Libraries were prepared for sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq platform, generating 150 bp paired-end reads. Preprocessed RNA-seq data were aligned and quantified to produce gene expression matrices for subsequent bioinformatic analyses. These data provided insights into the molecular mechanisms of neurodegeneration and potential therapeutic targets.

Differentially expressed genes analysis for bulk RNA-seq

To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between control and disease samples, we utilized the R packages “Limma” (Ritchie et al. 2015) and “DESeq2” (Love et al. 2014), which are widely recognized for their robustness in RNA-seq and microarray data analysis. Limma was applied for microarray data analysis, while DESeq2 was employed for RNA-seq count data analysis. To prevent technical artifacts from confounding the downstream analysis, known technical batch effects within individual datasets (such as specific array slide IDs or sequencing runs) were corrected using the removeBatchEffect function from the “Limma” package prior to data visualization. It is important to emphasize that the differential expression analysis was performed independently for each individual dataset, rather than on a globally merged cohort, to rigorously preserve dataset-specific biological signals and circumvent cross-study batch effects. Crucially, to rigorously prevent non-disease-related variance from skewing our results, the differential expression calculations were executed utilizing multivariable linear models. Demographic confounders (such as Sex and Age) and biological covariates (such as Brain Region or Dataset Origin) were explicitly incorporated into the design matrix (e.g., ~ Group + Sex + Age + Region) during the statistical testing phase. This rigorous approach ensures that the resulting P-values and fold changes are precisely adjusted for baseline heterogeneity at the exact point of statistical inference. Given the profound cellular heterogeneity in bulk brain tissue, transcriptomic signals from highly vulnerable, dying neuronal subpopulations are heavily diluted by the robust expression profiles of surrounding surviving glia. To effectively counteract this 'dilution effect' and minimize Type II errors (false negatives) for genuine but masked pathogenic signals, we purposefully applied a mathematically sensitive differential expression threshold of P < 0.05 and |log2FC|≥ 0.1. Detailed information regarding dataset-specific batch corrections and covariates is provided in Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1. Functional interpretation of identified DEGs was conducted through Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis and Gene Ontology (GO) analysis using the “clusterProfiler” R package (Wu et al. 2021). These analyses revealed biological pathways, molecular functions, and cellular processes associated with neurological disorder pathogenesis.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis

We performed Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) on batch-corrected, normalized bulk RNA-seq data to identify gene co-expression networks across the four neurological disorders. WGCNA constructed co-expression modules representing functionally related gene clusters that often correspond to biological pathways or regulatory networks (Langfelder and Horvath 2008). Each disorder cohort underwent independent analysis. To prevent technical artifacts from confounding network topology, dataset-specific technical batch effects (e.g., physical array slide IDs or multi-center origins) were mathematically eliminated utilizing a linear regression framework prior to network construction, strictly when explicit technical metadata was available. Specifically, technical batch variables were explicitly modeled as covariates, and their estimated effects were subtracted from the normalized expression matrix. Notably, to avoid over-manipulation of the expression matrices, genuine biological and demographic variance (such as Sex, Age, and Brain Region) was deliberately preserved during this step. Network construction was executed by calculating an unsigned Topological Overlap Matrix (TOM) to measure gene interconnectedness, followed by average linkage hierarchical clustering. A dynamic tree-cutting algorithm was then applied to delineate distinct modules, setting a minimum module size of 30 genes and a dissimilarity threshold of 0.25 for merging highly similar gene clusters. To ensure an optimal scale-free topology, the soft-thresholding power (β) for each dataset was dynamically estimated utilizing a candidate vector (ranging from 1 to 20) to achieve a model fit index threshold of R2>0.90.

Module construction utilized the blockwiseModules function (parameters: TOMType = “unsigned”, minModuleSize = 30, mergeCutHeight = 0.25). Hierarchical clustering identified distinct modules, each assigned a unique color identifier. A comprehensive list of all WGCNA hyperparameters is systematically provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Module characterization involved calculating module eigengenes (MEs), representing the first principal components of module expression profiles. Two quantitative metrics were computed for module assessment:

Module Membership (MM): The correlation between individual gene expression profiles and ME, quantifying gene centrality within modules.

Module Membership (MM): The correlation between individual gene expression profiles and ME, quantifying gene centrality within modules.

Gene Significance (GS): The correlation between gene expression profiles and clinical traits.

Gene Significance (GS): The correlation between gene expression profiles and clinical traits.

Hub genes were identified based on above-average MM and GS values within their respective modules, supplemented by differential expression analysis (P ≤ 0.05) to ensure biological relevance to disease states.

AutoML predictive model construction

We constructed predictive models for disease classification using hub genes from modules exhibiting strong disease-state associations (top 50% by module-trait correlation). Model development utilized H2O’s AutoML framework (LeDell and Poirier 2020), which enables automated optimization of multiple machine learning algorithms. The analysis protocol involved stratified data partitioning (70% training, 30% testing) to ensure evaluation reliability and maintain class proportions. A globally fixed random seed (seed = 123) was applied throughout the data splitting and model initialization phases to guarantee strict computational reproducibility. The framework generated up to 50 models encompassing gradient boosting machines (GBM), random forests (RF), and neural networks (NN) and was optimized utilizing a tenfold internal cross-validation strategy during the training phase. Model assessment incorporated multiple performance metrics: Area Under the Curve (AUC), Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUCPR), log-loss, Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), precision, recall, and F1-score. All detailed algorithmic configurations and random seeds for the AutoML pipeline are summarized in Supplementary Table 3.

Model selection prioritized the top five performers based on AUC and AUCPR metrics, which effectively address imbalanced dataset challenges. Gene importance quantification employed a weighted scoring system (weights: 1.0, 0.8, 0.6, 0.4, 0.2) based on model ranking. The resultant weighted importance scores enabled systematic prioritization of hub genes for potential diagnostic and therapeutic applications.

Deep learning model construction

We selected the top 50 genes (or all available genes for smaller modules) from each module based on feature importance scores. Expression matrices were constructed with samples as rows and gene expression values as columns. Samples were labeled as 0 (healthy controls) and 1 (disease groups). To address inherent class imbalance, the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) was applied exclusively to the training partitions. To capture synergistic inter-module interactions, we systematically evaluated all non-empty combinatorial subsets of the identified key modules. It is important to clarify that this integration did not employ a mathematical cross-product of gene expression values (which would exponentially inflate dimensionality and induce severe overfitting). Instead, for any given combination of modules, the data were integrated via strict horizontal feature concatenation.

Mathematically, let XM1∈RS×n1 and XM2∈RS×n2 represent the expression matrices for two selected modules across S samples, where n1 and n2 are the number of prioritized top genes (up to 50). The fused input tensor was defined as Xfused=[XM1‖XM2]∈RS×(n1+n2), where ‖ denotes column-wise concatenation after systematically filtering out any overlapping genes to ensure unique feature representation. By utilizing this additive dimensionality strategy rather than multiplicative feature engineering, we maintained computational tractability. This structural arrangement yielded a unified, high-dimensional sequential input tensor for each sample. Rather than manually engineering interaction terms, we relied explicitly on the multi-head self-attention mechanisms of the Transformer architecture to dynamically compute and capture the complex, non-linear inter-module dependencies natively during the deep learning training process.

The strategic implementation of deep learning was designed to unravel highly non-linear and complex synergistic interactions among these hub genes, which traditional machine learning algorithms may fail to fully capture. Although transcriptomic profiles are not inherently sequential time-series data, we organized the input genes into a fixed topological sequence arranged in descending order of their AutoML-derived feature importance scores. This spatial ordering allowed sequence-dependent architectures, such as RNN/LSTM, to effectively process the expression profiles as ordered sequences, thereby capturing cumulative regulatory dependencies and hierarchical interactions from the most to the least critical genes. Furthermore, to rigorously mitigate the risk of overfitting frequently associated with training complex networks on a restricted feature space (50 genes) and moderate sample sizes, we implemented a multifaceted regularization strategy. This included prior dimensionality reduction (selecting only the top hub genes to avoid the curse of dimensionality), the integration of Dropout layers (rates of 0.2–0.3) and Batch Normalization across all networks, and stringent model evaluation through stratified tenfold cross-validation.

The integrated dataset was used to develop and evaluate four deep learning architectures implemented in PyTorch:

Transformer-based Model: Implemented with 8 attention heads and 4 layers of multi-head self-attention mechanisms to capture long-range dependencies in gene expression patterns.

Recurrent Neural Network (RNN): Employed a bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network with three layers, a hidden dimension of 256, and a dropout rate of 0.3 for sequential pattern analysis.

All models were trained for 20 epochs using binary cross-entropy loss and optimized via the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.0001. A StepLR scheduler (step size = 5, γ = 0.5) was utilized for adaptive learning rate decay. Furthermore, dynamic thresholding based on the F1-score was implemented during the evaluation phase. Performance evaluation utilized accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and ROC-AUC metrics. Comprehensive hyperparameter settings are detailed in Supplementary Table 3.

Selection of optimal models and features

Top-performing module combinations were selected based on predictive performance metrics. Hub genes from these module combinations were validated using Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) with Random Forest classifiers (configured as an ensemble of 100 constituent decision trees), which provided additional confirmation of their predictive significance. Outputs from deep learning models were further processed to identify key genes and robust modules associated with disease states.

Gene importance scores were aggregated across models, and genes were ranked based on their total importance and frequency of occurrence across modules. Genes present in at least two distinct modules and consistently prioritized across models were considered robust and were selected for further analysis. Genes with the highest importance scores were investigated for their potential as therapeutic targets by mapping them to existing drug databases, ensuring relevance to neurological disorders.

This integrated pipeline, combining WGCNA, AutoML, and deep learning, systematically identified key regulatory modules and their constituent genes. This approach enabled robust predictive modeling and provided critical insights into the molecular mechanisms of neurological disorders.

Drug target enrichment analysis

Key genes identified through deep learning models were subjected to drug-target enrichment analysis to evaluate their therapeutic potential. For each gene module, genes were mapped to drug targets from our curated datasets. Overlaps between module genes and known drug target genes were evaluated using a hypergeometric test. P-values were calculated to assess the significance of enrichment, with a threshold of P ≤ 0.05 used to identify significant associations.

Enrichment results included overlapping genes, associated drug components, related herbs, and PubChem Compound Identifiers (CIDs). To rank drug components and their associated genes, a scoring system was applied. This system integrated the following factors with equal weights: Enrichment p-values, Module importance, and Gene importance scores.

Drug components and genes with the highest scores were prioritized for further investigation. Significant drug components were annotated with related herbs and overlapping genes, and results were summarized at both the gene and component levels.

This analysis provided valuable insights into key drug components and herbs with potential therapeutic relevance for the identified high-priority genes, laying the groundwork for further experimental validation and drug development efforts.

Single-cell transcriptomic and metabolic analysis

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from four diseases were analyzed using “Seurat” (Hao et al. 2021), “SCP (Single Cell Pathway)”, and “scMetabolism” (Wu et al. 2022) R packages to investigate cellular heterogeneity, pathway activity, and metabolic states. First, raw scRNA-seq data were processed in Seurat for quality control, including the removal of cells with excessive mitochondrial gene expression (> 20%) or low feature counts (< 200 genes). Data were then normalized and scaled. Dimensionality reduction was performed using principal component analysis (PCA), followed by clustering and visualization through Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). Cell clusters were annotated based on differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and canonical markers to identify cell types. Next, SCP was used to infer pathway activities across clusters by mapping gene expression data to curated pathway databases. This analysis enabled the identification of pathway dysregulation specific to disease-associated cell types. Finally, scMetabolism was applied to quantify metabolic pathway activity at the single-cell level. Pathway scores were used to identify cell type-specific metabolic states and potential metabolic reprogramming in disease-associated cell types. By integrating transcriptomic and metabolic data, we characterized cell type-specific alterations across the four diseases, providing mechanistic insights and identifying potential therapeutic targets.

Molecular docking of key target genes and corresponding compounds

Molecular docking of key target genes and their corresponding compounds was performed using the Molecular Operating Environment (MOE) software. The 3D structures of the target proteins, sourced from UniProt and the Protein Data Bank (PDB) with the following specific identifiers: TRPC5 (PDB ID: 7wdb), PPP3CA (PDB ID: 6nuf), KCNMA1 (PDB ID: 6v5a), GABRG2 (PDB ID: 7qne), and FZD2 (PDB ID: 7x8p). Protein structures were prepared by removing water molecules and co-crystallized ligands, followed by hydrogen atom addition and protonation state assignment at physiological pH using the QuickPrep tool. The binding sites were rigorously defined using the MOE Site Finder module based on alpha-sphere geometry. For each target, the active site was defined by the pocket enveloping the native co-crystallized ligand; for targets without a native ligand, the cavity with the highest Propensity Ligand Binding (PLB) score was selected. Ligands were prepared by converting 2D structures from PubChem into 3D conformations and subjected to energy minimization using the AMBER10:EHT force field. Docking was executed via the Triangle Matcher algorithm with London dG initial scoring. For each run, 10 poses were generated and refined using the GBVI/WSA dG function. The top-ranked pose with the lowest binding energy was retained for final interaction analysis, including hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic contact evaluation.

Molecular docking was executed using the MOE docking module, employing the Triangle Matcher algorithm for initial ligand positioning and the London dG scoring function for scoring and ranking docking poses. The docking procedure was repeated multiple times to ensure reproducibility and identify the most energetically favorable ligand–protein complex. The final docked complexes were further analyzed to assess the interactions between the ligands and target proteins, including hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and electrostatic interactions. The results were visualized and analyzed using the MOE visualization tools, providing insights into the binding affinity and evaluating the structural compatibility of the compounds with the target proteins, thereby establishing a theoretical structural basis for their anticipated pharmacological activities. It is important to note that these simulations establish a theoretical structural basis for potential target engagement, rather than confirming direct functional pharmacological efficacy.

Statistical analysis

All bioinformatics and statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.0). For comparisons between two groups, the Student’s t-test was used for normally distributed data, while the Wilcoxon test was applied to non-normally distributed data. Results were considered statistically significant at *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001. This study utilized publicly available datasets that had received prior ethical approval, with informed consent obtained from all participants. All research procedures adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, ensuring the highest ethical standards for human subject research.

Convergent molecular mechanisms in brain disorders

Functional enrichment analysis of the refined 120 shared DEGs across AD, PD, HD, and SZ highlighted their roles in key cellular components, including secretory granule membrane (ID: GO:0030667, P_adj = 1.98 × 10⁻7) and ficolin-1-rich granule membrane (ID: GO:0101003, P_adj = 3.72 × 10⁻3). These genes were also significantly involved in biological processes related to cellular response to type II interferon (ID: GO:0071346, P_adj = 2.81 × 10⁻2) and regulated exocytosis (ID: GO:0045055, P_adj = 2.81 × 10⁻2). Furthermore, exploratory KEGG pathway analysis revealed nominal associations with osteoclast differentiation (ID: hsa04380, P = 3.60 × 10⁻3), toxoplasmosis (ID: hsa05145, P = 8.74 × 10⁻3), and inositol phosphate metabolism (ID: hsa00562, P = 1.89 × 10⁻2); although these did not reach significance after multiple testing correction, they collectively underscore a convergent cascade of cellular stress and signaling dysregulation among these neuro-psychiatric conditions (Supplementary Fig. 2C-2D, Supplementary Table 4).

Taken together, these convergent functional profiles reveal a highly structured pattern of molecular pathology shared across AD, PD, HD, and SZ. Specifically, the consistent enrichment in secretory granule dynamics and regulated exocytosis points towards a ubiquitous disruption in synaptic vesicle trafficking and neurotransmission. Concurrently, the robust implication of type II interferon responses highlights a shared neuroinflammatory landscape, likely driven by aberrant glial activation. These overarching trends suggest that despite their diverse clinical phenotypes and distinct genetic etiologies, these major neurological and psychiatric disorders ultimately converge on fundamental vulnerabilities in neuroimmune crosstalk and synaptic homeostasis, providing a unified framework for identifying broad-spectrum therapeutic targets.

Disorder-specific functional dynamics and pathway analysis

The GO and KEGG annotation analysis of shared DEGs (appearing in at least two datasets for each disease) across AD, PD, HD, and SZ revealed several key biological functions and processes implicated in the pathology of these neurodegenerative conditions (Supplementary Table 6–7).

In AD, the clustering of GO terms revealed distinct functional modules primarily driven by vesicle-mediated transport in synapse (ID: GO:0099003, P_adj = 8.83 × 10⁻13) and regulated exocytosis (ID: GO:0045055, P_adj = 1.76 × 10⁻16) (Supplementary Fig. 2E), emphasizing a profound and specific disruption of neuronal connectivity and protein homeostasis. For PD, the enriched biological processes tightly clustered into overarching themes centered around neurotransmitter transport (ID: GO:0006836, P_adj = 2.64 × 10⁻2) and platelet morphogenesis (ID: GO:0036344, P_adj = 3.01 × 10⁻2), underscoring localized neuroinflammation and compromised cell survival mechanisms unique to its pathogenesis (Supplementary Fig. 2F).

Similarly, in SZ, the clustering analysis aggregated the GO terms into dominant functional networks related to nucleotide-sugar transmembrane transport (ID: GO:0015780, P = 8.31 × 10⁻5) and negative regulation of neuron differentiation (ID: GO:0045665, P = 1.67 × 10⁻4). Although these associations did not remain significant after strict multiple testing correction, their exploratory trends highlight aberrant neurodevelopmental and stress-response processes as potential disease mediators (Supplementary Fig. 2G). In HD, disease-specific DEGs formed cohesive functional modules predominantly associated with leukocyte migration (ID: GO:0050900, P_adj = 5.02 × 10⁻3) and integrin-mediated signaling pathway (ID: GO:0007229, P_adj = 5.02 × 10⁻3), suggesting that disruptions in precise metabolic and oxidative homeostatic functions play critical roles in its pathophysiology (Supplementary Fig. 2H).

Discovery of critical gene networks in neurological and psychiatric diseases

To logically advance our multi-dimensional framework and move beyond isolated single-gene analyses, we first sought to identify the broad, shared transcriptomic signatures across AD, PD, HD, and SZ. To achieve this, we deployed WGCNA on the covariate-adjusted datasets to construct unbiased co-expression networks and capture system-level pathogenic modules. Among the datasets, GSE5747 yielded the largest number of modules (25), while GSE12649 produced the fewest (6). The blue module from the GSE33000-HDHC dataset exhibited the highest correlation, with a correlation coefficient (Cor) of 0.703 and a P-value of 3.54 × 10⁻48, indicating a significant positive association. Conversely, the turquoise module from the GSE33000-HDHC dataset showed a correlation coefficient of −0.633 and a P-value of 1.13 × 10⁻53, reflecting a robust negative association. For SZ, the greenyellow module from GSE38485 emerged as the most significantly associated, with a correlation coefficient of −0.402 and a P-value of 1.06 × 10⁻10. Similarly, the green module from GSE99039 was most strongly correlated with PD, with a correlation coefficient of 0.234 and a P-value of 7.23 × 10⁻7 (Supplementary Table 8).

Further validation using the Mantel test demonstrated strong correlations between modules across datasets. The GSE33000 blue module in AD showed the highest correlation with the yellow module (Mantel R = 0.68, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3A). For the GSE99039 green module in PD, the brown module demonstrated the strongest correlation (Mantel R = 0.65, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3B). The greenyellow module from GSE38485 in SZ correlated most strongly with the brown module (Mantel R = 0.61, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3C), while the turquoise module from GSE33000 in HD was highly associated with the blue module (Mantel R = −0.77, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3D).

Evaluation of predictive gene modules across multiple disorders

While WGCNA successfully identified extensive shared pathogenic networks, these massive co-expression modules encompass hundreds of genes, rendering them too broad for precise therapeutic targeting. Therefore, to logically distill these large networks into a concise set of core predictive drivers, we subsequently subjected the high-priority modules to rigorous machine learning and deep learning feature prioritization. The top 50% of modules with the highest disease association in each dataset were analyzed using 50 machine learning models (Supplementary Table 9). GSE33000 (AD and HD), GSE28894 (PD), and GSE38485 (SZ) demonstrated the best model performance metrics across the four diseases (Supplementary Fig. 3A-3B).

In the GSE33000-ADHC dataset, the turquoise module was a highly predictive module for AD, achieving an AUC of 0.984 and an AUCPR of 0.971 using a GLM model. It exhibited low Logloss (0.154) and RMSE (0.209), with a high F1-Score of 0.938. The black module, analyzed with a deep learning model, also performed strongly (AUC = 0.972, AUCPR = 0.958, F1-Score = 0.916). Rather than relying on pre-defined curated gene sets, our unbiased, data-driven functional characterization revealed that the computationally derived black module was intrinsically enriched in oxidative stress and detoxification pathways, driven by core co-expressed hub genes such as PRDX5 and NFE2L2. KEGG analysis identified shared mechanisms with other neurodegenerative diseases, including chemical carcinogenesis and Parkinson’s disease (Supplementary Fig. 4, Fig. 4A, Supplementary Table 10).

Key transcription factors driving functional and regulatory networks

Having computationally isolated a core set of multi-disease hub genes via our AI pipeline, we next sought to understand the upstream regulatory mechanisms governing their expression. The transcription factor (TF) enrichment analysis identified RELA, HIF1A, and TP53 as pivotal regulators across the full set of genes and the top 100 important genes. For the full gene set, RELA was linked to immune and extracellular matrix-related targets, such as CD74 and CCL5, reflecting its role in inflammation and tissue remodeling. HIF1A regulated hypoxia-related genes, including HBEGF and VEGFD, emphasizing its critical involvement in oxygen homeostasis and angiogenesis. Similarly, TP53 was associated with cell cycle and DNA repair genes, such as E2F1 and CDKN1A, underlining its essential function in maintaining genomic stability.

Within the top 100 genes, the regulatory roles of RELA, HIF1A, and TP53 were more refined. For instance, RELA regulated migration- and chemoresistance-associated genes like MMP14, ABCB1, and CXCL1, highlighting its role in cancer invasion and drug resistance. HIF1A targeted angiogenesis and proliferation-related genes, including VEGFA and CCND1, while TP53 was linked to critical cell cycle checkpoints and tumor suppressor genes, such as CHEK1 and RASSF1. These findings demonstrate distinct but overlapping regulatory mechanisms among these TFs, reflecting their roles in stress adaptation, hypoxia response, and genomic stability (Fig. 5C).

GO and KEGG analysis highlights pathway-level insights across genes

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed key functional patterns across the full gene set and the top 100 genes. For the full gene set, GO analysis emphasized processes such as oxygen homeostasis, steroid hormone response, and ribonucleotide metabolism, involving genes like HIF1A, NR3C1, COX2, and ATP5PF. These genes are central to metabolic adaptation and responses to environmental stress. KEGG analysis further corroborated these findings, identifying key pathways such as oxidative phosphorylation and central carbon metabolism in cancer, underscoring the importance of metabolic and hypoxic responses.

For the top 100 genes, GO analysis highlighted processes such as response to hypoxia, stress-induced transcriptional regulation, and immune signaling pathways, driven by genes like RELA, HIF1A, and TP53 (Fig. 5D). KEGG pathway analysis revealed disease-specific pathways, such as VEGF signaling, immune checkpoint regulation, and the AGE-RAGE signaling pathway in diabetic complications, showcasing the relevance of these genes in stress adaptation, immune modulation, and disease progression (Fig. 5E, Supplementary Table 14).

The systemic approach highlights the potential of Danshen, Dongchongxiacao, and Sanqi in addressing complex diseases through multi-target and multi-pathway interactions. These findings provide a comprehensive molecular framework for understanding the efficacy of TCM in modulating oxidative stress, immune dysregulation, and metabolic dysfunction, laying the foundation for future experimental validation and therapeutic exploration.

In Vivo validation of core pathogenic hub genes in an AD rat model

Given the significant enrichment of target genes in glial cells associated with AD (Supplementary Fig. 6B-6C, Supplementary Table 15), we conducted in vivo validation using utilizing a rat model of Aβ42-induced cognitive impairment. Histological analysis using Nissl staining further corroborated these findings, revealing a significant reduction in neuronal cell density within the hippocampal CA1 and CA3 regions of AD rats relative to controls (Fig. 8A, Supplementary Table 17) Behavioral experiments demonstrated that the AD group exhibited markedly poorer performance in spatial detection tasks compared to normal controls (Fig. 8B, Supplementary Table 17). This behavioral impairment was consistent with the neurodegenerative phenotype observed in the AD model.

Shared and divergent gene expression patterns across brain disorders

Through enrichment analysis of drug targets and their associated importance scores, we identified 235 target genes linked to bioactive compounds (Supplementary Table 20). Among these, eight genes (TP53, NFE2L2, TRPC5, PPP3CA, GABRG2, KCNMA1, IDH1, and FZD2) demonstrated significant differential expression in the AD model compared to controls, as confirmed by RNA-seq data (Fig. 8F–G).

To further rigorously validate the clinical relevance of these eight prioritized core hub genes in human populations, we systematically interrogated their expression profiles across the large-scale bulk transcriptomic cohorts of the four brain disorders. Our cross-disease validation revealed highly convergent dysregulation patterns: TP53, PPP3CA, and GABRG2 were significantly dysregulated across AD (P = 3.87 × 10⁻2, P = 3.20 × 10⁻11, and P = 2.40 × 10⁻16, respectively), PD (P = 4.78 × 10⁻3, P = 3.67 × 10⁻2, and P = 2.01 × 10⁻2, respectively), and HD (P = 1.09 × 10⁻38, P = 4.01 × 10⁻32, and P = 3.00 × 10⁻26, respectively) cohorts. Notably, NFE2L2 demonstrated significant differential expression across AD (P = 2.04 × 10⁻25), HD (P = 7.91 × 10⁻18), and SZ (P = 2.63 × 10⁻2) datasets, reinforcing its ubiquitous role in oxidative stress responses across varied neurological and psychiatric landscapes. Furthermore, FZD2 exhibited robust upregulation in both AD (P = 1.76 × 10⁻7) and HD (P = 7.01 × 10⁻10), while KCNMA1 was significantly and uniquely upregulated in the PD cohort (logFC = 0.569, P = 3.01 × 10⁻5). Although TRPC5 and IDH1 were identified as critical targets in network pharmacology and single-cell analyses, their global bulk-level alterations did not reach statistical significance. This phenomenon likely reflects the dilution of highly cell-type-specific transcriptomic signals within bulk tissue homogenates, underscoring the necessity of multi-resolution single-cell validation.

Single-cell data provided additional insights into the cell-type-specific expression patterns of these genes across brain disorders. Shared expression patterns were observed for PPP3CA and KCNMA1, which were significantly expressed in neurons across AD, PD, and HD, underscoring their central roles in neuronal signaling and function. NFE2L2, a critical regulator of oxidative stress responses, exhibited elevated expression in astrocytes and microglia in both PD and HD, reflecting its involvement in glial-mediated neuroprotection.

Distinct patterns emerged in AD, where PPP3CA and GABRG2 were predominantly expressed in excitatory neurons, while KCNMA1 showed a broader expression profile, encompassing inhibitory neurons, oligodendrocytes, and astrocytes, suggesting its multifaceted role in synaptic and glial interactions. In PD, NFE2L2 was enriched in microglia and oligodendrocytes, reflecting its dual role in immune response and myelination, while TRPC5 was specifically expressed in GABAergic and dopaminergic neurons, indicating its involvement in neurotransmitter signaling. Notably, PPP3CA exhibited the highest expression in CADPS2 + neurons in PD, suggesting its disease-specific cellular function.

In SZ, overall gene expression was lower, with PPP3CA showing enrichment in radial glia (RG), indicating a potential role in neurodevelopment, and IDH1 being more prominently expressed in neurons. In HD, NFE2L2 displayed glial-specific expression in astrocytes and microglia, while PPP3CA and KCNMA1 remained neuronally enriched, consistent with their involvement in neuronal differentiation and axonal maintenance (Supplementary Fig. 6D, Supplementary Fig. 9).

Metabolic pathway analysis of bioactive compounds

To further explore the functional relevance of the identified bioactive compounds, their roles in key metabolic pathways were analyzed.

In the glutathione metabolism pathway (BIO-ko00480, R00644), L-ascorbate peroxidase (EC 1.11.1.11) catalyzes the conversion of ascorbic acid (C00072) to dehydroascorbate (C05422). Prominent in Ascomycota (e.g., Pyricularia, Glarea) and Basidiomycota (e.g., Moniliophthora), this pathway is critical for managing oxidative stress and maintaining redox homeostasis (Fig. 9F, left).

In the ascorbate and aldarate metabolism pathway (BIO-ko00053, R00068), L-ascorbate oxidase (EC 1.10.3.3) catalyzes the oxidation of ascorbic acid (C00072) to dehydroascorbate (C05422). This pathway, conserved in Gammaproteobacteria (e.g., Pseudomonas, Methylovulum) and Ascomycota (e.g., Paracoccidioides), underscores its importance in oxidative stress regulation across bacterial and fungal taxa (Fig. 9F, right).

In the glycerophospholipid metabolism pathway (BIO-ko00564, R01316), phospholipase A1 (EC 3.1.1.32) and phospholipase A2 (EC 3.1.1.4) catalyze the hydrolysis of phosphatidylcholine (C00157) into lysophosphatidylcholine (C04233). This pathway, conserved across taxa such as Gammaproteobacteria, Bacteroidota, and Chordata, plays a pivotal role in lipid remodeling and cellular signaling (Fig. 9G, left).

The fatty acid biosynthesis pathway (BIO-ko00061, R08159) involves the fatty-acid synthase system (EC 2.3.1.85), processing intermediates such as tetradecanoyl-ACP (C06761) and tetradecanoic acid (C06424). Active in taxa such as Malassezia and Gloeophyllum, this pathway supports lipid metabolism and membrane biosynthesis (Fig. 9G, right).

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive exploration of the molecular mechanisms underlying neurodegenerative diseases, including AD, PD, HD, and SZ. By integrating multi-dimensional systems biology data, in vivo experiments, network pharmacology, and advanced computational techniques, we have identified key targets, pathways, and bioactive compounds, offering novel insights into the pathogenesis of these disorders.

Our study highlights TP53, HIF1A, and NFE2L2 as central regulators across multiple neurodegenerative diseases. From a rigorous neurotoxicological perspective, the emergence of these genetic hubs profoundly reflects the brain’s multifaceted response to chronic oxidative stress and toxic insults. Existing toxicological literature firmly establishes NFE2L2 (Nrf2) as the master transcriptional regulator of the endogenous antioxidant defense system in the central nervous system (CNS) (Brandes and Gray 2020) [R1]. Under severe oxidative or xenobiotic stress, the NFE2L2 pathway serves as the primary toxicological defense mechanism, mitigating lipid peroxidation and preventing progressive neurotoxicity (Saha et al. 2020). Consequently, modulating the NFE2L2 axis is recognized as a highly potent neuroprotective strategy. Conversely, chronic unmitigated oxidative stress directly induces persistent DNA damage, triggering the stabilization and hyperactivation of TP53. In post-mitotic neurons, TP53 shifts from maintaining genomic stability to orchestrating pro-apoptotic toxic cascades and mitochondrial dysfunction, particularly in AD and PD (Chang et al. 2012; Culmsee and Mattson 2005). While their individual roles in oxidative stress, hypoxia adaptation, and cell cycle regulation have been historically documented, our integrative computational framework systematically identifies them as highly conserved topological hubs within a cross-disease pathogenic network.. Previous studies have demonstrated the critical involvement of TP53 in neuronal apoptosis and mitochondrial dysfunction, particularly in AD and PD (Culmsee and Mattson 2005; Talebi et al. 2021). Similarly, HIF1A and NFE2L2 are well-known regulators of oxidative stress responses, key pathological features of neurodegeneration (Gaisina et al. 2018; Boas et al. 2021). These findings reinforce the importance of targeting these TFs for developing broad-spectrum therapeutic interventions. By mapping these shared, computationally derived hubs to specific TCM compounds, our study transitions from the broad concept of "antioxidant therapy" toward a more delineated, multi-target pharmacological network. This network provides a theoretical basis for interventions that may simultaneously mitigate the superimposed oxidative damage observed in both neurodevelopmental (SZ) and neurodegenerative (AD, PD, HD) trajectories.

Beyond confirming these established roles, our study offers novel insights into the interconnectedness of these TFs across multiple diseases. For example, while TP53 is traditionally recognized for its role in apoptosis, our network analysis reveals its interaction with HIF1A and NFE2L2 in modulating mitochondrial function and oxidative stress. This aligns with recent evidence indicating that TP53 regulates mitochondrial dynamics and autophagy, processes crucial for neuronal surviva (Almeida et al. 2021; Dinh et al. 2021). Furthermore, the identification of RELA (NF-κB) as a key regulator of inflammation in AD and PD emphasizes the role of neuroinflammation as a central driver of neurodegeneration (Lanzillotta et al 2015). Our findings suggest that RELA interacts with TP53 and NFE2L2, underscoring the interplay between inflammation, oxidative stress, and cell cycle regulation in these diseases.

In line with our previous studies, we also identified NFE2L2 and KCNMA1 as mitochondrial epistasis-related genes in AD (Xu et al. 2024; Xu et al. 2022). The identification of TRPC5, PPP3CA, GABRG2, and KCNMA1 as key players in calcium signaling and neurotransmitter release highlights synaptic dysfunction as a hallmark of neurodegeneration. Dysregulated calcium homeostasis has long been recognized as a critical factor in neurodegenerative diseases (Pchitskaya et al. 2018). However, our study provides new insights into the specific roles of these genes in disease progression. For instance, TRPC5, a calcium-permeable ion channel, plays an important role in neuronal excitability and synaptic plasticity (Bröker‐Lai et al. 2017), While previous studies have focused on its role in normal neuronal function, our findings suggest that TRPC5 may also contribute to disease processes, particularly in PD and HD, where calcium dysregulation is prominent. This novel insight positions TRPC5 as a promising therapeutic target for mitigating synaptic dysfunction. Similarly, PPP3CA (calcineurin), a key mediator of calcium signaling, translates oxidative stress-induced calcium dyshomeostasis into structural synaptic toxicity. As a calcium-dependent phosphatase, hyperactive PPP3CA acts as a critical executioner of neurotoxicity, driving dendritic spine loss and profound cytoskeletal breakdown, thereby linking synaptic dysfunction to broader cellular processes in neurodegenerative diseases (Wu et al. 2010; Norris 2018). GABRG2 and KCNMA1, which regulate GABAergic neurotransmission and potassium ion channel activity, respectively, were also implicated in our study. These genes underline the importance of excitatory-inhibitory balance and neuronal excitability in the progression of diseases such as AD and SZ.

Our analysis highlights key shared pathways, such as HIF-1 signaling, NF-κB signaling, and the synaptic vesicle cycle, as central to the pathophysiology of multiple neurodegenerative diseases. The HIF-1 signaling pathway, which regulates cellular responses to hypoxia, has long been recognized for its role in mitigating oxidative stress and preserving mitochondrial function. In both AD and PD, where mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative damage are prominent, dysregulation of HIF-1 signaling exacerbates neuronal vulnerability (Mitroshina et al. 2021). Similarly, the NF-κB pathway, a master regulator of inflammation, drives neuroinflammation—a hallmark of neurodegeneration—by activating microglia and astrocytes, leading to the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (Guo et al. 2024; Shih et al. 2015). The consistent enrichment of these pathways across diseases underscores their role as fundamental mediators of neurodegenerative processes, making them attractive targets for interventions aimed at reducing oxidative and inflammatory damage.

Beyond these shared mechanisms, our study identifies disease-specific pathway features that provide novel insights into disease progression and heterogeneity. For instance, autophagy and mitophagy pathways are prominently dysregulated in HD, consistent with the established role of impaired protein clearance in this disease (Martinez-Vicente 2015). However, our analysis suggests that these pathways are also dysregulated in AD and PD, albeit to a lesser extent. This finding aligns with recent studies showing that autophagy dysfunction contributes to the accumulation of toxic protein aggregates in AD and PD (Nixon and Rubinsztein 2024; Nixon 2013). These findings suggest that enhancing autophagic flux may represent a unifying therapeutic strategy, while targeting specific regulators of autophagy could address disease-specific vulnerabilities. Another key finding is the enrichment of synaptic vesicle cycle and neurotransmitter secretion pathways in PD and AD, emphasizing the role of synaptic dysfunction in these diseases (Ovsepian et al. 2018; Esposito et al. 2012; Dunn et al. 2017). While previous studies have broadly implicated synaptic dysfunction in neurodegeneration, our study reveals specific molecular mechanisms, such as the involvement of PPP3CA in calcium signaling and GABRG2 in inhibitory neurotransmission. These findings highlight the critical interplay between synaptic activity and calcium homeostasis, providing potential targets for restoring synaptic function in diseases characterized by excitatory-inhibitory imbalance.

The identification of bioactive compounds from TCM, such as stigmasterol, dodecanoic acid, and honokiol, as potential multi-target therapies highlight the promise of natural products in modulating oxidative stress, inflammation, and metabolic dysfunction. For example, stigmasterol, a compound derived from Dongchongxiacao, has been shown to regulate cholesterol metabolism via HMGCR, a key enzyme in lipid homeostasis (Bakrim et al. 2022). Similarly, dodecanoic acid, a compound found in Sanqi, targets TLR4 to reduce neuroinflammation, an increasingly recognized driver of disease progression in PD and AD (Beamer and Shepherd 2012). These findings suggest that natural compounds with pleiotropic effects could address the multifactorial nature of neurodegeneration.

Molecular docking analyses further validated these compounds by demonstrating stable interactions with disease-relevant targets. For instance, stigmasterol and tetradecanoic acid interact with PPP3CA, implicating their potential to restore calcium signaling and synaptic function. Similarly, honokiol, derived from Houpo, is known for its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects (Talarek et al. 2017). Our findings extend this understanding by showing that honokiol also targets KRAS, a regulator of lipid metabolism and signaling pathways involved in cell survival and apoptosis. This dual activity suggests that honokiol may have therapeutic applications beyond neurodegeneration, such as in diseases characterized by metabolic dysfunction and chronic inflammation. The multi-target potential of these compounds aligns with emerging paradigms in drug discovery that prioritize the modulation of interconnected pathways rather than single-target interventions. By simultaneously addressing oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and synaptic dysfunction, these natural products offer a comprehensive therapeutic approach to combat the complexity of neurodegenerative diseases. Additionally, their potential for fewer off-target effects and better safety profiles compared to synthetic drugs enhances their clinical appeal.

The cell-type-specific analyses presented in this study provide critical insights into the distinct and shared cellular contributions to neurodegenerative disease pathology, offering a foundation for the development of cell-type-specific therapeutic strategies. Microglia and astrocytes emerge as central players in both AD and HD, highlighting their pivotal roles in neuroinflammation and neuronal homeostasis. The involvement of microglia in AD aligns with the well-established role of microglial activation in driving neuroinflammation, a hallmark of AD pathology (Liddelow et al. 2017). However, astrocytes appear to play a more prominent role in HD, where they are essential for maintaining neuronal integrity amidst mutant huntingtin protein aggregation. These findings align with prior research demonstrating that astrocyte dysfunction contributes to synaptic and metabolic deficits in HD (Brandebura et al. 2023). The dual roles of these glial cell types in inflammation and neuronal support underscore their potential as therapeutic targets, particularly for modulating immune responses and restoring synaptic and metabolic homeostasis.

In PD, oligodendrocytes and oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs) show significant enrichment, emphasizing the importance of myelination and synaptic signaling in this disease. Oligodendrocyte dysfunction has been implicated in synaptic transmission deficits in PD, further supported by evidence linking demyelination to alpha-synuclein pathology and neuronal communication impairments (Zou et al. 2023). These findings suggest that targeting oligodendrocyte health and myelination processes may hold promise for restoring synaptic function in PD.

For SZ, the enrichment of RG highlights the neurodevelopmental origins of the disease. RGs are essential for brain development, acting as scaffolds for neuronal migration and differentiation. Their dysregulation in SZ supports the hypothesis that the disorder arises from disruptions in early brain development (Stachowiak et al. 2013). This observation emphasizes the need for early interventions aimed at restoring neurodevelopmental processes and mitigating long-term neurocognitive deficits. While neuroinflammation emerges as a shared theme across neurodegenerative diseases, the specific cell types involved and their functional roles vary significantly. This heterogeneity underscores the need for precision therapies tailored to the unique contributions of microglia, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, or radial glia in each disease. By targeting these cell-specific mechanisms, it may be possible to mitigate disease progression more effectively and improve patient outcomes.

Metabolic dysregulation is a common feature across neurodegenerative diseases, but the specific pathways involved differ, reflecting the unique pathophysiology of each disorder. In AD, pyruvate metabolism and TCA cycle dysregulation in microglia and astrocytes point to the central role of mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress in disease progression. These metabolic disturbances align with the known accumulation of amyloid-beta and tau, which exacerbate energy deficits and oxidative damage (Butterfield and Halliwell 2019). Targeting mitochondrial metabolism and oxidative stress management may, therefore, represent a promising therapeutic strategy for AD.

In PD, the enrichment of inositol phosphate metabolism in oligodendrocytes warrants a critical examination beyond the generalized concept of lipid dysfunction. Oligodendrocytes are tasked with the massive energetic and biosynthetic burden of producing and maintaining myelin sheaths, a process that requires continuous and intensive phosphoinositide (PI) turnover. While classical PD research has predominantly focused on α-synuclein aggregation within dopaminergic neurons and its role in disrupting vesicular trafficking and membrane integrity (Lehri-Boufala et al. 2015; Venda et al. 2010), recent literature increasingly highlights early oligodendroglial dysfunction and myelin lipid droplet accumulation as primary events that precede overt neuronal death (Smajić et al. 2022; Agarwal et al. 2020). Inositol phosphate signaling is not only the structural backbone for myelin membrane biosynthesis but also serves as an indispensable secondary messenger system for calcium-dependent myelination processes. By specifically pinpointing inositol phosphate metabolism within oligodendrocytes, our study bridges a critical knowledge gap. It reveals that PD-associated lipid dysregulation is highly compartmentalized; the failure of inositol signaling in these specific glial cells likely compromises the trophic and physical support normally provided to the highly unmyelinated and vulnerable axonal arbors of dopaminergic neurons. Restoring lipid metabolism pathways may help mitigate synaptic dysfunction and neuronal degeneration in PD, particularly through interventions targeting oligodendrocyte health.

In SZ, oxidative phosphorylation and sulfur metabolism emerge as critical pathways in radial glia, offering a profound mechanistic insight into the neurodevelopmental origins of the disorder. Radial glia serve as the primary neural progenitor cells and migratory scaffolds during embryonic corticogenesis. Their mitotic and differentiation trajectories are extraordinarily sensitive to intracellular redox imbalances (Morén et al. 2025; Rawani et al. 2024). Crucially, sulfur metabolism is the indispensable foundational pathway for the transsulfuration cascade, which synthesizes glutathione (GSH) and hydrogen sulfide (H2S)—the brain's primary antioxidant buffering systems. While decades of psychiatric literature have documented mitochondrial impairments linked to synaptic and cognitive deficits (Prabakaran et al. 2004), alongside bulk-tissue and peripheral GSH deficits in schizophrenia patients (Steullet et al. 2016), our single-cell metabolic enrichment specifically localizes this core vulnerability to radial glia. This adds a critical spatial and developmental layer to the existing knowledge gap: it suggests that the "superimposed oxidative stress" model of SZ does not merely begin in adulthood, but rather originates from an innate developmental failure of radial glia to buffer reactive oxygen species (ROS) via sulfur metabolism. This early metabolic defect could drive premature glial depletion, disrupted neurogenesis, and the subsequent cortical thinning and interneuron deficits universally observed in SZ. Therapies aimed at enhancing mitochondrial function and managing oxidative stress in radial glia could provide novel approaches to address the underlying metabolic vulnerabilities in SZ.

In HD, the enrichment of fatty acid biosynthesis and ascorbate metabolism highlights the importance of lipid metabolism and oxidative stress management in the context of mutant huntingtin-induced toxicity. The accumulation of mutant huntingtin disrupts cellular redox balance and lipid homeostasis, leading to neuronal degeneration. Targeting these pathways with interventions designed to stabilize lipid metabolism and enhance antioxidant defenses may offer therapeutic benefits for HD.

The disease-specific mechanisms identified in this study highlight the need for precision medicine approaches in neurodegenerative diseases. In HD, the prominence of autophagy and mitophagy pathways aligns with the well-documented role of impaired protein clearance in HD pathology (Martinez-Vicente et al. 2010). These pathways are critical for the degradation of mutant huntingtin protein aggregates, which contribute to neurodegeneration. Notably, our analysis reveals that autophagy impairment also contributes to AD and PD, albeit with distinct pathological consequences. In AD, defective autophagy accelerates Aβ accumulation, while in PD, it promotes the aggregation of α-synuclein (Nixon 2013; Wong and Cuervo 2010). These findings position autophagy modulation as a shared yet context-dependent therapeutic target across neurodegenerative diseases, offering opportunities for both broad-spectrum and disease-tailored interventions.

In PD, the enrichment of synaptic vesicle cycle and neurotransmitter secretion pathways highlights synaptic dysfunction as a central driver of neurodegeneration. The pathological aggregation of α-synuclein disrupts vesicular trafficking, leading to impaired synaptic transmission and neuronal connectivity (Venda et al. 2010). This mechanistic insight suggests that therapies targeting synaptic vesicle dynamics—such as enhancing vesicle recycling or inhibiting α-synuclein oligomerization—could restore synaptic integrity and delay disease progression. For instance, small molecules or gene therapies aimed at stabilizing synaptic proteins may hold promise for PD-specific interventions.

For schizophrenia (SZ), the prominence of oxidative phosphorylation and sulfur metabolism pathways implicates mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress as key contributors to its neurodevelopmental and synaptic deficits. These findings align with emerging evidence linking SZ to impaired energy metabolism and redox imbalance, which disrupt neuronal maturation and synaptic plasticity. Targeting mitochondrial resilience—via antioxidants or metabolic enhancers—may mitigate these deficits, offering a novel therapeutic avenue for SZ.

The identification of multi-target therapies and cell-type-specific therapeutic strategies offers new opportunities for the development of effective treatments for neurodegenerative diseases. For example, targeting microglia and astrocytes in AD and HD could mitigate neuroinflammation and promote neuronal survival, while targeting oligodendrocytes and OPCs in PD could improve myelination and synaptic function (Anwar and Rivest 2020; Kwon and Koh 2020). In addition, the exploration of key metabolic pathways, particularly glycerophospholipid metabolism and glutathione metabolism, indicates that modulating these pathways may yield therapeutic benefits in diseases marked by oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction. The integration of multi-omics data with sophisticated computational models and network pharmacology has significantly advanced our ability to identify critical therapeutic targets, pathways, and bioactive compounds. For instance, the identification of stigmasterol, dodecanoic acid, and honokiol as potential multi-target therapies emphasizes the potential of natural products in modulating oxidative stress, inflammation, and metabolic dysregulation (Talarek et al. 2017; Zhang et al. 2023a; Jayakumari et al. 2021). Molecular docking analyses confirmed the stable interactions of these bioactive compounds with key molecular targets, such as PPP3CA and KRAS. These results underscore their potential to regulate critical pathways, including calcium signaling, lipid metabolism, and inflammatory responses. For instance, the interaction between tetradecanoic acid and PPP3CA suggests its role in modulating calcineurin activity, which is integral to calcium-dependent signal transduction and immune regulation.

While this study provides valuable insights into the multi-dimensional mechanisms underlying neurodegenerative diseases, several limitations must be acknowledged. Firstly, regarding data modalities, the reliance on transcriptomic data as the primary sequencing input—without direct de novo mass spectrometry or global metabolomic profiling—constrains the absolute depth of molecular insights into disease pathology. Additionally, the identification of ubiquitous central hubs, such as TP53, NFE2L2, and PPP3CA, must be interpreted with caution. While these genes are biologically critical for general cellular stress signaling and neuronal survival, their emergence as central hubs may be partially confounded by network topological bias. Because such highly pleiotropic genes are historically heavily researched, they inherently exhibit denser connectivity within curated biological databases, disproportionately elevating their algorithmic importance. Consequently, rather than representing unique, disease-specific mechanistic drivers, these hubs more likely reflect a generalized, convergent 'neuro-stress' and inflammatory response shared across these debilitating conditions. Moreover, while our deep learning architectures were heavily regularized to mitigate the risks of modeling moderate sample sizes, the use of publicly available datasets introduces potential biases related to sample heterogeneity. Future large-scale, multi-center longitudinal validations are crucial to confirm these non-linear predictive signatures.

Secondly, to fully contextualize our analytical framework, it is essential to cross-compare our computational approach with existing methodologies used to model pharmacological networks. Previous pioneering studies have successfully utilized entropy-based random walk algorithms to infer ingredient-target associations (Wu et al. 2023), proximity-based modeling to decode herb combinations (Wang et al. 2021), advanced approaches to predict multi-enzyme inhibition networks (Salekeen et al. 2022), and tripartite drug-gene-microbe interactomes (Salekeen et al., 2024). While these database-inferred models provide extensive topological coverage, they can sometimes be constrained by existing literature biases. In contrast, our current framework utilizes WGCNA and deep learning architectures directly driven by dynamic, patient-derived cross-disease transcriptomic data. By validating our in silico predictions with primary gene expression alterations, our methodology serves as a critical complementary layer—transitioning from static, database-inferred topologies to dynamic, data-driven pathophenotypic networks.

Finally, from an experimental and translational perspective, it is critical to recognize that the intracerebroventricular Aβ42 injection utilized in this study represents an acute toxicity model. While effective for rapidly validating specific downstream pathways, it cannot perfectly recapitulate the chronic, progressively degenerative oxidative stress environment characteristic of human conditions. Furthermore, it is paramount to acknowledge that the connection between the identified hub genes (such as NFE2L2 and TP53) and the actual therapeutic efficacy of the predicted TCM-derived compounds remains strictly correlative at this stage. Although our network pharmacology and molecular docking analyses strongly predict binding plausibility and structural target engagement, these findings do not equate to functional validation. These in silico simulations cannot differentiate between agonizing, antagonizing, or neutral binding effects. Future research must utilize target-specific knockout animal models, in vitro enzymatic assays, pharmacological inhibitors, and comprehensive behavioral assays to definitively prove whether these compounds directly modulate the NFE2L2 or TP53 axes and define their true biological efficacy. Subsequent preclinical studies must also rigorously evaluate the blood–brain barrier (BBB) permeability and pharmacokinetic stability of these compounds. Addressing these collective limitations through chronic animal models and extensive mechanistic profiling will ultimately facilitate the development of personalized, multi-target neuroprotective therapies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study presents an integrative framework for exploring the shared and disease-specific molecular mechanisms underlying neurodegenerative and psychiatric disorders. By integrating multi-dimensional omics-derived data, advanced computational modeling, and network pharmacology, we identified critical therapeutic targets, pathways, and bioactive compounds with therapeutic potential. These findings contribute to a broader understanding of the complex pathophysiology of these conditions and provide a theoretical foundation for the development of multi-target therapeutic strategies. Such approaches have the potential to address both the convergent and unique features of these disorders, ultimately facilitating future preclinical and clinical explorations aimed at improving patient outcomes.
