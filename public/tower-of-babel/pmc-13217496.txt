Emerging mechanistic pathways linking heat, sleep and mental health in low-resource urban settings: Insights from Kampala and Beyond
Authors: Monica H Swahn, Hakimu Sseviiri, Kelly E Murray, Lara Dugas, Godfrey S Bbosa, Gideon Matovu, Jane Palmier, Revocatus Twinomuhangi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRising ambient temperatures linked to climate change increasingly threatens mental health, particularly in low-resource urban environments across Africa, where extreme heat can disrupt sleep, elevating risks of anxiety and depression. This short communication outlines the mechanistic pathways through which acute and chronic heat exposure influence mental health via sleep disruption, drawing on evidence from Kampala in Uganda, and comparable urban centers across Africa. The heat–sleep–mental health pathway is bidirectional: heat-related biological processes such as neuroinflammation and HPA-axis dysregulation disrupt sleep, and sleep loss in turn exacerbates these same biological stress responses. These biological processes interact with heat-amplifying environmental factors, including heat-retaining housing, poor ventilation, and limited vegetation cover, as well as social stressors related to poverty, gendered labor roles, and overcrowding. Together, these factors amplify physiological strain and psychological distress that can trigger mental illness. A forward research agenda emphasizes longitudinal and mixed-methods designs, biomarker integration, and gender-responsive interventions to better characterize and mitigate these risks. Advances in low-cost measurement technologies, such as digital hygrometers and mobile health (mHealth) tools, now allow high-resolution monitoring of temperature, humidity, and sleep quality in community settings. Recognizing sleep as a modifiable mediator between heat exposure and mental health offers an actionable framework for improving resilience to climate-related stressors in rapidly urbanizing regions.Keywords: Climate change, Climate stress, Heat stress, Mental health, Psychiatric epidemiology, Anxiety, Depression, Urban, Kampala, Uganda, Africa, Women, Youth

Introduction: climate change and urban mental health concerns

Rising ambient temperatures due to climate change are increasing mental health risks worldwide, including in African cities where rapid urbanization, poverty, and inadequate housing intensify the effects of extreme heat [1,2]. Prolonged heat exposure contributes to stress, poor sleep quality, anxiety, and depressive symptoms, with growing evidence linking extreme temperatures to elevated rates of suicide, aggression, and mood disorders [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. Evidence indicates that heatwaves and higher nighttime temperatures impair sleep quality, a key regulator of emotional and physiological health [2,11]. Sleep disruption influences neurobiological processes such as hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis dysregulation, inflammation, and neurotransmitter imbalances that underlie mood regulation, forming a cycle of sleep disruption [[3], [4], [5]]. Urban slum conditions, including poor-quality housing, overcrowding, and inadequate ventilation, intensify nighttime heat and humidity [9,10,[12], [13], [14], [15]]. Yet, the mechanisms connecting heat, sleep, and mental health remain underexplored, particularly in low-resource urban contexts like Kampala, where climatic, ecological, and socioeconomic stressors converge [[16], [17], [18]]. This Short Communication presents a conceptual framework positioning sleep disruption as a central, modifiable pathway linking heat exposure to mental health, particularly in low-resource urban settings.

Across Africa, extreme temperatures have expanded sharply, and heatwaves have become increasingly intense and prolonged, with both East and West African regions experiencing disproportionate impacts [15,[19], [20], [21], [22]]. In cities, the urban heat island (UHI) effect is exacerbated by rapid urbanization, heat-retaining materials, poor waste management, and loss of vegetation and natural water channels [12,14,23]. Among these factors, waste management practices can play an important but often overlooked role in shaping localized heat conditions. Poor waste management can contribute to localized heat accumulation. In many informal settlements, waste is frequently burned or allowed to accumulate in open spaces, introducing heat-absorbing materials, reducing vegetation cover, and releasing additional heat and pollutants into the local environment, all of which can intensify urban heat island effects.

The 2016 Kampala Climate Action Plan reported average temperatures of 20–22 °C, with highs of 27–28 °C and lows of 17–18 °C [24]. The report warned that over the previous five decades, average temperatures in Kampala had risen by about 1.5 °C and were projected to rise by another 1.5 to 3 °C by 2050. About 60% of Kampala’s residents live in slums and informal settlements, which are especially susceptible to heat stress due to heat-absorbing building materials, overcrowding, and limited ventilation [[12], [13], [14]]. These areas also often lack trees and open spaces that provide shade and cooling [12,[24], [25], [26], [27]]. Combined with Kampala’s humid tropical climate, even a small rise in temperature can exacerbate thermal stress and impair thermoregulation, compounding health risks [25,26].

Conceptual framework: mechanistic pathways linking heat, sleep, and mental health

Heat exposure affects mental health through biological, environmental, and social pathways that converge on sleep disruption, a critical mechanism influencing emotional and physiological regulation. Residents of slums and informal urban settlements face disproportionate heat stress, particularly indoors, due to iron sheet roofing that accumulates heat, overcrowding, and poor ventilation [28]. Optimal sleep occurs at 60–67°F (16–20 °C) [29], yet indoor temperatures in informal dwellings often exceed 77°F (25 °C) at night [[30], [31], [32], [33], [34]]. During heatwaves, prolonged nighttime heat exposure and disrupted sleep become key pathways linking heat stress to adverse mental health outcomes.

Biological mechanisms

Acute and chronic heat exposure disrupt thermoregulation through the central and peripheral nervous systems, including the hypothalamus, autonomic nervous system, and transient receptor potential vanilloid channels (TRPV3, TRPV4) [[35], [36], [37], [38], [39], [40], [41], [42]]. Resulting neuroinflammation and oxidative stress trigger pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, TNF-α, IL-17, and IL-33, which cross the blood–brain barrier and affect neurotransmitters and transcription factors in mood-regulating regions such as the hippocampus and amygdala [43,44]. Heat stress also activates the HPA axis, elevating cortisol levels, impairing glucocorticoid receptor sensitivity, and disrupting serotonin pathways, all of which increase vulnerability to anxiety and depression [43,44]. Nighttime heat suppresses melatonin secretion, disturbing circadian rhythms and sleep quality [2,45]. Heat-induced alterations in serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine pathways, along with disruptions in intracellular cascades such as BDNF/ERK1/2/CREB and p38-MAPK, have been linked to cognitive decline, anxiety, and depression [[35], [36], [37],[46], [47], [48], [49], [50], [51], [52], [53]]. These heat-induced changes also disrupt the gut–liver–brain axis and contribute to mood dysregulation [[53], [54], [55], [56], [57]]. Effects may be particularly pronounced in women, as hormonal fluctuations during the luteal phase can increase core temperature and reduce thermoregulatory capacity [58,59].

Environmental mechanisms

The UHI effect intensifies heat exposure in slums and informal settlements. Poorly ventilated housing with iron-sheet roofing traps heat, while high humidity and limited green cover hinder cooling, posing challenges to mental health [28]. In Kampala, as mentioned, average temperatures have risen by ∼1.5 °C, and nighttime heat events are increasingly frequent [12,[24], [25], [26], [27],60,61]. These conditions impair thermoregulation, elevate physiological strain, and fragment sleep, leading to mood deterioration.

Social mechanisms

Poverty, overcrowding, and gendered labor roles heighten heat exposure and psychological stress, particularly for women and youth, who spend long hours in poorly ventilated homes or informal workplaces, compounding thermal discomfort [16,28]. Persistent heat exposure contributes to irritability, cognitive fatigue, and reduced emotional regulation [62]. In low-resource settings, stigma and limited access to mental health services perpetuate untreated distress, reinforcing vulnerability to heat-related anxiety and depression [[63], [64], [65], [66], [67]]. Chronic thermal discomfort leads to emotional exhaustion and reduced resilience, particularly among women facing restrictive gender norms and limited decision-making power [32,68,69].

Innovative measurement opportunities for heat exposure in low-resource settings

Low-cost sensors, such as the Arduino kits, Raspberry Pi systems, HOBO data loggers, and smartphone-integrated devices, are increasingly used for environmental monitoring [27]. Digital hygrometers (e.g., Hygrochron iButton, DS1923) are well suited for informal urban environments because they are compact, battery- or solar-powered, and capable of long-term, high-frequency temperature and humidity logging. In Kampala, these sensors have effectively captured indoor and outdoor data, enabling analysis of real-world environmental exposures [70]. As an example, Van de Walle and colleagues (2022) monitored temperatures across 45 Kampala sites and found differences across measurement sites of up to 14.5 °C and Humidex values exceeding 40 °C on 68% of days, with frequent reports of heat-related symptoms such as headaches, dehydration, dizziness, and higher health costs [70]. Similar findings from others demonstrate that communities are aware of these risks and rely on communication networks and informal strategies to adapt to rising temperatures [71]. While technology such as sleep wearables, temperature and humidity sensors, and GIS-based spatial tools offers strong potential for understanding heat–sleep interactions, research in informal urban contexts underscores the importance of considering user acceptability, cultural appropriateness, and participant engagement when introducing monitoring technologies [[72], [73], [74]]. Together, these research priorities emphasize an integrated, multi-level approach to heat-related mental health that centers lived experience, participatory approaches, and practical, scalable interventions [[75], [76], [77]].

Conclusions and implications for policy and practice

Mitigating heat-related mental health risks in urban Africa requires interventions that reduce exposure to extreme heat and address its downstream impacts on sleep and mental health. Although several of these interventions address physical heat exposure or thermal comfort, they are included because heat exposure and sleep disruption are upstream drivers of mental health outcomes. Strategies that reduce nighttime heat exposure or improve sleep environments may therefore indirectly mitigate anxiety and depressive symptoms. Recognizing that informal settlements are typically unplanned and poorly integrated into urban infrastructure is essential, requiring interventions across multiple levels of the heat–sleep–mental health pathway, including climate-resilient housing, equitable access to cooling technologies, and gender-responsive mental health services in city planning. In Kampala and similar urban centers, shaded communal spaces and women-led cooling initiatives, and leveraging women’s central roles in caregiving and community mobilization, could substantially reduce heat exposure.

Interventions should also target tin-roofed and poorly ventilated structures. Reflective white paint or “cool roofs” offer a cost-effective adaptation strategy. As an example, Nutkiewicz and colleagues demonstrated that cool roofs in informal settlements could reduce annual heat stress exposure by up to 91 percent, while the HABVIA study in Ghana and South Africa is exploring their impact on sleep behavior and mood outcomes [78,79]. Establishing communal cooling shelters and well-ventilated spaces offering safer nighttime environments can substantially reduce thermal exposure and support well-being, but stronger evidence linking heat stress directly to mental health outcomes is needed to inform governmental investment.

Climate and environmental policies rarely address mental health explicitly, underscoring the need for cross-sector collaboration among urban planners, health authorities, and community organizations. Local governments can help ensure equitable access to culturally relevant mental health services embedded within broader heat adaptation frameworks. This integration will require interdisciplinary research, evidence-informed policy innovation, and participatory implementation to strengthen resilience in climate-vulnerable communities. Clinics and local health workers should incorporate heat-safety guidance and sleep health promotion into routine care, recommending practical approaches to maintaining cooler environments. Community-based strategies such as relaxation sessions, mindfulness training, and peer support groups can enhance emotional resilience during heatwaves [62].

By identifying sleep as a modifiable mediator linking heat exposure to anxiety and depression, this framework advances understanding of climate-related mental health risks. Drawing from neuroscience, public health, and environmental science, this interdisciplinary framing synthesizes mechanistic pathways that impair affective regulation under heat stress. While prior research has documented elevated psychosocial distress, suicidal ideation, and substance use among youth in Kampala’s slums, this synthesis supports context-specific interventions and informs equitable policy and adaptation strategies [16,63,65]. Ultimately, these efforts can enhance mental health resilience in climate-vulnerable populations with broader implications for sustainability and urban well-being.
