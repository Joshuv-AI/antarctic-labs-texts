Stoichiometry-driven electronic heterogeneity in covalent organic frameworks for coupled H2O2 photoproduction and chemoselective fragrance upgrading
Authors: Jie-Yu Yue, Zi-Shuo Xu, Yan Guo, Jing-Xian Luo, Peng Yang, Bo Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoupling H2O2 photoproduction with value-added organic transformations avoids the sluggish kinetics of water oxidation, yet integrating both processes within one photocatalyst remains challenging. Here, we report stoichiometry-driven electronic heterogeneity in covalent organic frameworks (COFs) as a strategy for simultaneous H2O2 photoproduction and chemoselective fragrance upgrading. Adjusting monomer stoichiometry with distinct geometric symmetries perturbs local structural symmetry, generating electronically differentiated microenvironments within the COF skeleton. The optimized PTPD-COF1 achieves a H2O2 photosynthetic rate of 10.4 mmol g−1 h−1 in pure water, surpassing its binary analogues, and delivers coupled rates of 36.1, 53.2, and 21.6 mmol g−1 h−1 with near-quantitative, chemoselective conversion of cinnamyl, anisyl, and o-anisyl alcohols into fragrances. Theoretical analysis shows the modulated electronic environment enhances local dipole moments and lowers carrier effective masses, altering energy barriers for *OOH/*OH formation and C-H cleavage. In this work, stoichiometry-driven electronic heterogeneity is established as a design principle for multifunctional organic photocatalysts.Subject terms: Photocatalysis, Porous materials, Photocatalysis, Photocatalysis

Introduction

H2O2 is a versatile, environmentally benign oxidant with widespread applications in medical disinfection, industrial bleaching, environmental remediation, and chemical synthesis1,2. While anthraquinone autoxidation remains the industrial mainstay, its high energy intensity has spurred the pursuit of photocatalytic oxygen reduction and water oxidation reactions (ORR/WOR) as sustainable and low-carbon alternatives3,4. However, the significant kinetic mismatch between the ORR and WOR presents a formidable barrier5. Specifically, the sluggish kinetics of WOR impede timely hole scavenging, thereby exacerbating charge recombination and severely restricting the efficiency of photocatalytic H2O2 production6,7.

Given the intrinsic difficulty of directly accelerating WOR kinetics, coupling H2O2 photosynthesis with value-added organic transformations has emerged as a compelling strategy to circumvent this bottleneck while generating valorized products8–13. Among the diverse organic transformations, fragrance compounds, particularly those obtained from lignin-derived precursors, command substantial market value in the pharmaceutical, cosmetic, and personal care industries14. Specifically, the selective oxidative upgrading of lignin-based aromatic alcohols, such as cinnamyl alcohol (CA), anisyl alcohol (AA), and o-anisyl alcohol (OA), into their corresponding aldehydes represents a pivotal axis in modern biorefineries15–18. Nevertheless, achieving efficient integration of H2O2 photosynthesis and selective organic oxidation within a single photocatalytic platform remains a significant challenge, calling for bifunctional photocatalysts with precisely engineered electronic structures to simultaneously optimize electron and hole utilization.

Covalent organic frameworks (COFs) constitute a privileged platform for photocatalytic H2O2 production, distinguished by their crystalline architectures, tunable optoelectronic properties, and well-defined porous channels that expedite mass transport19–23. The inherent modularity of these frameworks facilitates the precise manipulation of light harvesting, charge carrier dynamics, and active site distribution24–28. Guided by reticular chemistry, the rational engineering of framework topology allows for the fine modulation of the underlying electronic structures29,30. Previous studies, including ours, have demonstrated that topological engineering, utilizing cpt, hxl, hit, and kgd networks, can significantly regulate photocatalytic performance31–34. While these topological variations establish a structural basis for improving photocatalytic performance, achieving superior efficiency demands more precise engineering of the local electronic environment beyond the reach of topological control alone. Recently, inducing local asymmetric electron distributions through heteroatom incorporation or spatial modulation of electronegative substituents at the monomer level has been shown to enhance photocatalytic activity in COFs35–37. However, these monomer-intrinsic approaches largely fix the electronic characteristics at the stage of monomer selection, offering limited tunability over the local electronic environment once the framework composition is determined.

The strategic frontier therefore lies in moving beyond the inherent electronic periodicity of conventional COF architectures to engineer electronically differentiated microenvironments in a unified framework scaffold. Such electronic heterogeneity within COF lattices can, in principle, facilitate the spatial decoupling of oxidative and reductive catalytic functionalities. Against this backdrop, multicomponent COF platforms38,39, by integrating building blocks with divergent geometric symmetries and electronic characteristics, offer a viable route to generate electronically diverse landscapes, holding great potential for the synergistic coupling of both half-reactions. Although mixed-linker COFs typically form statistical solid solutions40, combining monomers with mismatched connectivities, such as tritopic and ditopic nodes, introduces intrinsic geometric incompatibility that is expected to introduce local symmetry breaking at each substitution site. Importantly, the compositional ratio of monomers with distinct geometric symmetries serves as a particularly useful and continuously tunable parameter to regulate symmetry breaking, thereby enabling stoichiometry-driven electronic modulation as a promising yet nascent design dimension for coupled H2O2 production and value-added organic transformations. While stoichiometric variation inevitably co-modulates multiple physicochemical properties, the key question is whether the electronic environment can be identified as the primary driver through convergent evidence, including physical-mixture controls, composition-dependent activity trends, kinetic analysis, and atomic-level calculations.

Here, we show a stoichiometry-driven electronic modulation strategy in multicomponent COFs with kgd topology toward constructing electronically heterogeneous microenvironments for concurrent H2O2 photogeneration and fragrance upgrading. Through systematic variation of the stoichiometric ratio among hexatopic, tritopic, and ditopic monomers, a series of COFs (PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF) with progressively tuned local electronic landscapes are prepared. The stoichiometrically optimized PTPD-COF1 demonstrates superior H2O2 evolution activity of 10.4 mmol g−1 h−1 in pure water, exceeding its binary counterparts, and further delivers markedly boosted H2O2 evolution rates of 36.1, 53.2, and 21.6 mmol g−1 h−1 with near-quantitative selective conversion of cinnamyl, anisyl, and o-anisyl alcohols to their aldehyde counterparts in the coupled system. Mechanistic investigations disclose that the stoichiometry-induced electronic heterogeneity amplifies local dipole moments and lowers charge carrier effective masses, consequently reshaping the energy barriers for key reaction intermediates and C-H activation. This work establishes the stoichiometric ratio of geometrically asymmetric monomers as a readily accessible synthetic handle for engineering charge carrier utilization, offering a generalizable strategy for multifunctional photocatalytic platforms.

Results

Synthesis and structural characterizations

To construct electronically distinct microenvironments through stoichiometric control, a series of imine-linked multicomponent COFs with kgd topology were designed (Fig. 1). A binary PTT-COF with high structural symmetry was first synthesized via the [3 + 6] condensation between the tritopic monomer 4,4’,4”-(pyrimidine-2,4,6-triyl)trianiline (PDTA) and the hexatopic linker 4,4’,4”,4”‘,4”“,4”“‘-(triphenylene-2,3,6,7,10,11-hexayl)hexabenzaldehyde (TPA). Subsequently, incorporation of the ditopic monomer, 4,4’-(pyrimidine-4,6-diyl)dianiline (PDDA), together with systematic variation of the PDTA:PDDA molar ratios (0:1, 3:1), yielded PDT-COF and PTPD-COF1, respectively. The coexistence of linkers with distinct connectivities (tritopic vs. ditopic) at the same condensation nodes generated non-equivalent local coordination environments in PTPD-COF1 that were absent in the binary COFs. This structural feature led to spatially distributed variations in local electronic environments within PTPD-COF1.

Design strategy for electronically heterogeneous COFs.

Schematic illustration of stoichiometry-driven electronic modulation, evolving from binary frameworks (PTT-COF and PDT-COF) to the multicomponent PTPD-COF1 with electronically differentiated microenvironments arising from the geometric mismatch of tritopic and ditopic linkers.

To validate the long-range crystallinity of the synthesized COFs, powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) analysis was conducted. Figure 2a illustrated that the PXRD pattern of PTT-COF displayed a significant peak at 5.91°, accompanied by minor peaks at 9.67° and 12.10°, corresponding to the (110), (120), and (220) planes, respectively. Structural simulations performed via Materials Studio revealed that the experimental PXRD pattern of PTT-COF was best matched by the ABC stacking mode (Fig. 2a, purple line), rather than AA or AB configurations. Unit cell parameters for PTT-COF were determined via Pawley refinement, yielding a = b = 29.50 Å, c = 11.15 Å, α = β = 90°, and γ = 120° (Rwp = 1.95% and Rp = 1.15%). Figure 2b demonstrated that the experimental PXRD curve of PTPD-COF1 had a prominent peak at 5.92°, followed by weak peaks at 10.46° and 12.02°, assigned to the (310), (330), and (040) facets, respectively. The best level of agreement between the simulated and experimental PXRD patterns for PTPD-COF1 was shown by the ABC stacking arrangement (Fig. 2b, purple line). Pawley refinement produced lattice parameters of a = 51.10 Å, b = 29.50 Å, c = 11.15 Å, and α = β = γ = 90° (Rwp = 1.75% and Rp = 1.14%) for PTPD-COF1. In Fig. 2c, the PXRD profile of PDT-COF displayed obvious diffraction peaks at 3.51° and 6.30°, responding to (110) and (300) planes. The experimental PXRD pattern of PDT-COF exhibited better agreement with the simulated results for the 2D ABC stacking mode (Fig. 2c, purple line) than with other possible arrangements, such as the 2D AA stacking mode, the 2D AB stacking mode, and the 1D AA stacking mode. The lattice parameters of PDT-COF were a = b = 50.08 Å, c = 11.32 Å, α = β = 90°, and γ = 120° (Rwp = 1.01% and Rp = 0.59%). The corresponding structural models and atomic coordinates of PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF were displayed in Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Tables 1-3.

The permanent porosity of PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF was investigated by N2 adsorption-desorption measurements at 77 K. As illustrated in Fig. 2d, e, PTT-COF and PTPD-COF1 exhibited typical type I adsorption isotherms. The specific surface areas were calculated using the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) model, affording values of 511.51, 558.61, and 348.47 m2 g−1 for PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF, respectively. Pore size distributions obtained using a nonlocal density functional theory (NLDFT) model revealed primary pore diameters of 0.86, 0.87, and 1.40 nm for PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF, respectively (Fig. 2d–f). Crucially, the experimentally determined major pore sizes for all three COFs resided exclusively within the microporous regime. This observation was highly consistent with the simulated ABC stacking models, thereby further effectively ruling out the formation of other stacking modes, which would have otherwise exhibited significantly larger pore dimensions.

The morphological features of the as-synthesized series (PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF) were elucidated via scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Supplementary Fig. 2) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Supplementary Fig. 3). PTT-COF displayed a flower-like aggregated morphology assembled from stacked lamellar sheets. Upon introducing the ditopic linker PDDA, the morphology underwent a notable transformation: PTPD-COF1 and PDT-COF exhibited a finer granular stacked morphology with reduced particle sizes compared to PTT-COF. Further structural insights were obtained through high-resolution TEM (HR-TEM) in Fig. 2g–i, which resolved lattice fringes with d-spacings of around 1.4 ± 0.1, 1.4 ± 0.1, and 2.3 ± 0.1 nm, corresponding to the (110), (310), and (110) planes of PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF, respectively. These values were in good agreement with the theoretical d-spacings of 1.49, 1.49, and 2.52 nm calculated from the PXRD data using Bragg’s law (d = λ/2sinθ). The quantitative consistency between PXRD (reciprocal space) and HR-TEM (real space) supported the reliability of the crystallographic indexing. These results provided direct visual evidence for the high crystallinity of PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF, indicating that their structural integrity was maintained following stoichiometric modulation.

To further elucidate the chemical structures of PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF, Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) and solid-state 13C-NMR spectroscopy were employed. As depicted in Supplementary Fig. 4, the Schiff-base reaction was confirmed by the disappearance of the -NH2 peaks at 3400–3200 cm−1, the attenuation of the -CHO peaks at 1698 cm−1, and the appearance of imine bonds at 1627, 1626, and 1626 cm−1 for PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF, respectively. Additionally, the solid-state 13C NMR spectra (Supplementary Fig. 5) exhibited peaks at 158.98, 159.28, and 161.08 ppm for PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF, respectively, originating from the carbon atoms in the imine bonds. Besides, the chemical composition of PTPD-COF1 was further verified by the 1H NMR spectrum following acid digestion (Supplementary Fig. 6)41. The integration of characteristic signals yielded a PDTA:PDDA molar ratio of approximately 3:1, which aligned perfectly with the theoretically predicted stoichiometry and confirmed the high fidelity of our synthetic strategy.

Additionally, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was conducted. The N 1 s spectra of PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF (Supplementary Fig. 7) were resolved into two distinct peaks, corresponding to the nitrogen species in the imine bonds and pyrimidine rings42. Moreover, deconvolution of the C 1 s spectra (Supplementary Fig. 8) revealed three characteristic peaks assigned to carbon atoms in benzene rings, pyrimidine rings/imine linkages, and residual aldehyde groups, respectively, further corroborating the chemical compositions of the as-synthesized COFs43,44. The thermal stability of the three prepared COFs was assessed using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA). The TGA curves in Supplementary Fig. 9 indicated that PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF retained nearly 96% of their initial weight at 400 °C, thereby demonstrating thermal stability. Moreover, PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF maintained their crystalline structures after exposure to various solvents and H2O2 solutions, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 10, highlighting their robust structural stability. Intriguingly, the ternary PTPD-COF1 exhibited the smallest water contact angle among the as-synthesized COFs, reflecting the enhanced surface affinity toward water molecules (Supplementary Fig. 11). Such augmented hydrophilicity of PTPD-COF1 relative to PTT-COF and PDT-COF was conducive to the proton-coupled electron transfer process, thereby facilitating interfacial charge transfer for H2O2 production45.

Optical property investigation

To elucidate the impact of stoichiometry-driven electronic modulation on the optoelectronic behavior, the photophysical characteristics of PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF were systematically investigated. As shown in the UV-vis diffuse reflectance spectra (UV-vis DRS, Fig. 3a), all three COFs featured robust light absorption extending into the visible region, indicating their suitability for solar-driven catalysis. Tauc plot analysis (Fig. 3a, inset) yielded optical bandgaps of 2.68, 2.71, and 2.69 eV for PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF, respectively, demonstrating that the stoichiometry-driven symmetry breaking subtly tuned the electronic structure of the frameworks. Mott-Schottky analysis (Supplementary Fig. 12) confirmed the n-type semiconducting nature of PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF, with flat-band potentials of −0.89, −1.02, and −1.08 V (vs. Ag/AgCl), respectively. Consequently, the conduction band (CB) positions of PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF were ascertained at −0.69, −0.82, and −0.88 V (vs. NHE), with corresponding valence band (VB) potentials of 1.99, 1.89, and 1.81 V. The electronic band structures, depicted in Fig. 3b, revealed that PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF were thermodynamically capable of driving the ORR, WOR, and the oxidation of fragrance alcohols2,46–50.

Subsequently, the separation and transfer characteristics of photoinduced charge carriers in PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF were studied. As shown in Fig. 3c, electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurements showed that under both dark and light irradiation conditions, PTPD-COF1 had more unpaired electrons than PTT-COF and PDT-COF. Steady-state photoluminescence (PL) spectra (Fig. 3d) revealed divergent emission profiles for the three COFs. Notably, PTPD-COF1 exhibited the lowest PL intensity among the three as-synthesized COFs. In semiconductor physics, a diminished PL intensity typically signified the effective suppression of the radiative recombination of photogenerated charge carriers51. Time-resolved fluorescence decay measurements (Fig. 3e) displayed average lifetimes of 1.96, 2.28, and 1.79 ns for PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF, respectively. This suppressed recombination was corroborated by transient photocurrent responses (Fig. 3f), where PTPD-COF1 exhibited higher photocurrent densities than PTT-COF and PDT-COF, signifying enhanced charge separation and migration kinetics under illumination. Furthermore, the electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS, Supplementary Fig. 13) showed the smallest Nyquist semicircular radius for PTPD-COF1, confirming minimized charge-transfer resistance across the catalyst interface. These enhanced dynamics were primarily attributed to the intensified relative internal electric field (IEF) within PTPD-COF1, as corroborated by the integrated analysis of open-circuit potential and zeta potential results (Supplementary Figs. 14-16)52.

Femtosecond time-resolved transient absorption (fs-TA) spectroscopy with 420 nm excitation was employed to elucidate the excited-state and charge-transfer dynamics. As shown in Fig. 3g, h and Supplementary Fig. 17, all three COFs exhibited broad excited-state absorption (ESA) bands spanning 550-750 nm along with ground-state bleaching (GSB) features around 450 nm. Notably, PTPD-COF1 displayed stronger ESA and GSB signals than PTT-COF and PDT-COF, suggesting a larger population of photoinduced excited-state species45. While all three COFs exhibited progressive ESA decay over the measured temporal window, a careful examination of the initial dynamics revealed a distinction: PTPD-COF1 displayed a distinctive signal buildup within the first ~1 ps, with the ESA intensity at 1 ps exceeding that at 0.5 ps in the 550–750 nm region (Fig. 3h). This rise feature was absent in PTT-COF and PDT-COF, for which the ESA signals decayed monotonically from 0.5 ps onward, suggesting a distinct early-time excited-state evolution in PTPD-COF1 that may be associated with more efficient formation of long-lived photoinduced species53. Furthermore, the decay kinetics monitored at 650 nm were fitted with a biexponential function (Fig. 3i and Supplementary Fig. 17). The prolonged average lifetime of PTPD-COF1 at 650 nm (Figs. 3i, 847.1 ps), compared with those of PTT-COF (700.1 ps) and PDT-COF (757.5 ps), indicated longer-lived photoinduced charge-related species and suppressed recombination, which was favorable for driving subsequent photocatalytic redox reactions51. Taken together, these spectroscopic and electrochemical signatures suggested that stoichiometry-driven electronic modulation promoted charge carrier separation and transfer, with PTPD-COF1 exhibiting a favorable electronic environment for photocatalysis.

H2O2 photosynthetic performance

First of all, the photocatalytic H2O2 production performance of PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF was systematically assessed under visible light irradiation (λ > 420 nm, 300 W Xe lamp), wherein all H2O2 concentrations were rigorously quantified via the titanium sulfate colorimetric protocol (Supplementary Fig. 18). Figure 4a demonstrated that in ultrapure water with O2, PTPD-COF1 exhibited a remarkable H2O2 photosynthetic rate of 10406 μmol g−1 h−1 recorded as the initial rate within the first 1 h, substantially surpassing those of PTT-COF and PDT-COF (5784 and 4782 μmol g−1 h−1, respectively). Over extended irradiation for 4 h, the average H2O2 production rates for PTPD-COF1, PTT-COF, and PDT-COF were 6445, 3960, and 3522 μmol g−1 h−1, respectively, with the activity trend fully preserved.

To elucidate the effect of monomer stoichiometry on photocatalytic performance, PTPD-COF2 with a PDTA:PDDA molar ratio of 1:1 was synthesized (Supplementary Fig. 19, Supplementary Table 4) and evaluated under identical conditions. PTPD-COF2 delivered an H2O2 production rate of 8216 μmol g−1 h−1 (Supplementary Fig. 20), surpassing both binary PTT-COF and PDT-COF, yet remaining substantially lower than that of PTPD-COF1. The resulting composition-dependent activity trend across the series (PDT-COF < PTT-COF < PTPD-COF2 < PTPD-COF1) indicated that the 3:1 ratio provided a favorable balance between stoichiometry-induced electronic heterogeneity and long-range structural order. Notably, a physical mixture of PTT-COF and PDT-COF at a 3:1 molar ratio yielded an H2O2 production rate of merely 5641 μmol g−1 h−1 (Supplementary Fig. 20), which was comparable to those of the individual binary COFs and markedly inferior to that of PTPD-COF1. This control experiment further supported that the superior catalytic activity of PTPD-COF1 arose from the integrated electronic modulation within the multicomponent framework rather than from a simple additive contribution of the constituent binary phases.

The net accumulation of H2O2 by photocatalysts represents the dynamic balance between its formation and decomposition processes54. The photocatalytic formation of H2O2 follows zero-order kinetics with a rate constant kf (unit: mM h−1), while its decomposition follows first-order kinetics with a rate constant kd (unit: h−1). For PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF, the kf values were 0.67, 1.44, and 0.52 mM h−1, and the kd values were 0.17, 0.28, and 0.11 h−1, respectively (Fig. 4b). Although PTPD-COF1 exhibited a slightly higher kd than its counterparts, the markedly enhanced kf value (1.44 mM h−1, 2.1 and 2.8 times those of PTT-COF and PDT-COF) substantially outweighed the increase in decomposition kinetics, ultimately resulting in its superior net H2O2 accumulation.

Subsequently, the long-term H2O2 photoproduction durability of PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF was evaluated. As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 21, PTPD-COF1 with its tailored electronically differentiated microenvironment exhibited sustained H2O2 accumulation for 10 h. Conversely, for the binary counterparts PTT-COF and PDT-COF, the H2O2 yield started to decrease after 6 h of irradiation. Following 10 h of sustained photocatalytic operation, PTPD-COF1, PTT-COF, and PDT-COF all retained their structural integrity as corroborated by PXRD analysis (Supplementary Fig. 22), FT-IR spectra (Supplementary Fig. 23), and solid-state 13C NMR spectra (Supplementary Fig. 24). These results ruled out catalyst degradation as the main factor responsible for the decreased H2O2 output, which was instead attributed to the competitive decomposition of accumulated H2O2 by photogenerated charge carriers and reactive oxygen species54–56. This process more readily overtook the relatively lower formation rates of PTT-COF and PDT-COF but remained outpaced by the substantially higher generation rate of PTPD-COF1.

To substantiate the kinetic model and experimentally evaluate H2O2 decomposition pathways in the present system, additional H2O2 stability and decomposition experiments were performed with an initial H2O2 concentration of 2.00 mM under four controlled conditions (Supplementary Fig. 25a). In the absence of both PTPD-COF1 and light under an O2 atmosphere, the H2O2 concentration remained nearly constant over 4 h, indicating that spontaneous decomposition was negligible. Under light irradiation without PTPD-COF1, a minor H2O2 concentration decrease of approximately 4.5% was observed, which was attributed to background photolysis. In the presence of PTPD-COF1 under dark conditions, only marginal H2O2 decomposition of approximately 2.5% was detected, demonstrating that PTPD-COF1 itself did not substantially promote H2O2 decomposition without photoexcitation. By contrast, under light irradiation with PTPD-COF1 in an O2 atmosphere, the H2O2 concentration increased from 2.00 to 2.45 mM over 4 h, confirming that photocatalytic H2O2 formation outweighed concurrent H2O2 consumption under operando conditions. The fitted decomposition constant (kd = 0.28 h−1) should be interpreted as an apparent operando H2O2 loss term associated with photoinduced consumption pathways, rather than spontaneous decomposition or catalyst induced dark decomposition. This interpretation was further supported by the H2O2 decomposition experiment performed under an Ar atmosphere (Supplementary Fig. 25b), in which H2O2 loss was substantially suppressed compared with that under an O2 atmosphere, supporting the contribution of O2-derived reactive species to the photoinduced H2O2 consumption pathway.

In addition, the H2O2 generation capabilities of PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF were evaluated under air and Ar without sacrificial agents (Fig. 4c). In air and pure water, the initial H2O2 production rates within the first hour for PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF were 4840, 8050, and 3853 μmol g−1 h−1, respectively. Notably, even under Ar, H2O2 production rates of 131, 486, and 104 μmol g−1 h−1 were detected for PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF, respectively. These observations collectively verified that PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF drove H2O2 photosynthesis through pathways encompassing both ORR and WOR, with ORR playing the predominant role. The solar-to-chemical conversion (SCC) efficiencies for PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF were quantified as 0.61%, 1.33%, and 0.48%, respectively. The apparent quantum efficiency (AQE) measurements conducted at discrete monochromatic wavelengths (420, 450, and 520 nm) revealed maximum values at 420 nm: 6.06%, 10.69%, and 5.55% for PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 26). Based on comprehensive evaluations of H2O2 evolution rates, SCC, and AQE metrics, PTPD-COF1 ranked among the most efficient COF-based photocatalysts for H2O2 synthesis reported in the last two years, as substantiated by comparative benchmarking in Supplementary Table 5. Additionally, during 5 photocatalytic cycles in air and pure water, PTPD-COF1 illustrated better H2O2 production capability (Supplementary Fig. 27) and structural stability (Supplementary Figs. 28-29) compared with PTT-COF and PDT-COF. The above investigations suggested that stoichiometry-driven electronic modulation of COFs enhanced sustainable H2O2 photogeneration, providing a promising strategy for decentralized H2O2 synthesis.

The mechanistic pathways governing photocatalytic H2O2 generation over PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF were elucidated through systematic investigation. Reactive intermediate species were identified via quenching experiments employing KBrO3, TBA, TEMPOL, β-carotene, and CH3OH as specific scavengers for electrons, •OH, O2•-, 1O2, and holes, respectively (Fig. 4d). Pronounced suppression of H2O2 production upon introduction of KBrO3, TEMPOL, and β-carotene confirmed the essential participation of electrons, O2•-, and 1O2 in the photocatalytic processes mediated by PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF. Addition of CH3OH (10 vol%) enhanced H2O2 yields by mitigating electron-hole recombination, whereas tert-butanol (10 vol%) exerted negligible influence, thereby suggesting that •OH radicals did not function as key intermediates in these photocatalytic systems.

The generation of O2•- and 1O2 species was further verified through EPR spectra. Upon capture with 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO), PTPD-COF1 exhibited elevated O2•- signals relative to PTT-COF and PDT-COF (Fig. 4e). Analogously, 1O2 signals detected through 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine (TEMP) trapping experiments demonstrated greater intensity for PTPD-COF1 in comparison with PTT-COF and PDT-COF (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 30). Introduction of benzoquinone (BQ, O2•- scavenger) resulted in substantial attenuation of the 1O2-associated EPR signals, supporting that 1O2 generation occurred via charge-transfer-mediated interaction between O2•- and photoinduced holes57. Additionally, O2•- formation was verified through a colorimetric assay employing nitrotetrazolium blue chloride (NBT). As depicted in Supplementary Fig. 31, prolonged photoirradiation led to enhanced O2•- generation by PTPD-COF1 compared with PTT-COF and PDT-COF, thereby substantiating the EPR observations presented in Fig. 4e. These collective findings validated the operative ORR pathways: O2-O2•--H2O2 alongside O2-O2•--1O2-H2O257. Moreover, quantitative determination of H2O2 selectivity and electron transfer number during ORR was accomplished via rotating ring-disk electrode (RRDE) measurements for PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF (Supplementary Fig. 32). PTPD-COF1 demonstrated H2O2 selectivity exceeding 85% throughout the potential range of −1.0 to −0.4 V (vs. Ag/AgCl), with electron transfer numbers approximating 2.3. In contrast, PTT-COF and PDT-COF exhibited moderate H2O2 selectivity of 75% and 64%, respectively, with electron transfer numbers around 2.6, indicating less favorable selectivity toward the 2e- ORR pathway. The superior ORR selectivity of PTPD-COF1 corroborated that stoichiometry-driven electronic heterogeneity optimized the local electronic environment, thereby enhancing H2O2 formation.

Systematic control tests using KBrO3 and CH3OH as electron and hole scavengers, respectively, were carried out under the Ar environment to clarify the WOR mechanism (Fig. 4g). The H2O2 production rates of the prepared COFs were significantly reduced by the addition of CH3OH (10 vol%), demonstrating hole involvement in H2O2 formation under oxygen-depleted circumstances. Conversely, KBrO3 addition (1 mM) enhanced H2O2 yields compared to additive-free conditions, and no detectable O2 was observed (Supplementary Fig. 33), thereby validating the 2e- WOR pathway. Notably, EPR spectra revealed an absence of •OH signals for PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF (Supplementary Fig. 34). The convergent evidence from scavenger experiments, EPR spectra, and VB positions supported that PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF facilitated direct 2e- WOR for H2O2 photosynthesis.

Additionally, isotope-labeling experiments with 18O2 and H218O supported that both ORR and WOR proceeded via 2e- pathways over PTPD-COF1 (Supplementary Fig. 35). To investigate the ORR pathway, PTPD-COF1 was irradiated in H216O with 18O2 for 4 h. After Ar purging to remove residual gases, MnO2 was introduced to convert H2O2 into O2, and the evolved species were analyzed by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS). Detection of both 16O2 and 18O2 (Supplementary Fig. 35a) suggested the coexistence of ORR and WOR processes. Further insight was obtained from experiments using Ar-saturated H218O. The absence of 18O2 prior to H2O2 decomposition ruled out direct O2 generation via a 4e- WOR pathway (Supplementary Fig. 35b). Conversely, the appearance of 18O2 after MnO2-induced decomposition (Supplementary Fig. 35c) confirmed that H2O2 formation proceeded through a 2e- WOR pathway58,59.

After that, the practical utility of PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF was further evaluated through H2O2 photosynthetic experiments in various natural water sources, including seawater, spring water, and tap water, all of which enabled efficient H2O2 production (Supplementary Fig. 36). Additionally, PTPD-COF1 was integrated into a cross-flow device for continuous H2O2 production. Under visible light illumination, the system yielded a consistent H2O2 concentration (approximately 0.36 mM) over a 20 h period (Fig. 4h). After 20 h of photocatalysis under cross-flow conditions, PTPD-COF1 maintained its crystallinity and chemical structure, as confirmed by PXRD patterns (Supplementary Fig. 37) and FT-IR spectra (Supplementary Fig. 38). These results underscored the durability of PTPD-COF1 under dynamic flow conditions.

To elucidate the reaction intermediates generated during H2O2 photosynthesis over PTPD-COF1, in situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectra (DRIFTS) were collected in pure water under continuous visible light irradiation for 60 min. As depicted in Fig. 4i, no discernible signals were detected under dark conditions. Upon illumination, four characteristic bands emerged at 876, 1016, 1161, and 1228 cm−1, attributed to adsorbed O2, *OH, O2•-, and *OOH species, respectively, providing spectroscopic evidence consistent with concurrent 2e- ORR and 2e- WOR paths in this photocatalytic system60,61. The concurrent appearance of ORR-associated intermediates (adsorbed O2, O2•-, and *OOH) and WOR-associated *OH species indicated that both reductive and oxidative pathways were rapidly activated upon photoexcitation, consistent with the simultaneous generation of electrons and holes under illumination. All four bands increased monotonically with irradiation time without reaching saturation, indicating sustained catalytic activity. Notably, the ORR-associated bands (*OOH at 1228 cm−1 and O2•- at 1161 cm−1) exhibited markedly faster intensity growth than the WOR-associated *OH band at 1016 cm−1, suggesting that the ORR pathway contributed more substantially to H2O2 production than the WOR pathway19,62. This observation was fully consistent with the atmosphere-dependent experiments (Fig. 4c), where the H2O2 production rate under O2 was significantly higher than that under Ar.

H2O2 photogeneration coupled with fragrance upgrading

Given that the H2O2 photosynthesis over PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF was driven by a synergistic ORR-dominated and WOR-assisted pathway, we proposed an integrated dual-functional photocatalytic system to couple H2O2 production with the selective oxidation of AA, OA, and CA for fragrance upgrading. As shown in Fig. 5a, upon coupling the photocatalytic reaction with fragrance upgrading, the H2O2 amounts over PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF all displayed substantial augmentation compared to those in pure water conditions. Among the three as-synthesized catalysts, PTPD-COF1 exhibited the most significant enhancement in H2O2 production rates, reaching 53.2, 21.6, and 36.1 mmol g−1 h−1 in the presence of AA, OA, and CA, respectively.

To quantitatively disentangle the contributions of hole scavenging and catalyst-intrinsic electronic modulation to the enhanced H2O2 production, we performed kinetic analysis of the coupled systems analogous to that presented in Fig. 4b for pure water. The kf and kd values were extracted by fitting the time-dependent H2O2 concentration profiles54, while the alcohol oxidation rate constants (k) were obtained from pseudo-first-order kinetic fitting (Supplementary Fig. 39)63. In the AA-coupled system, the kf values were 3.01, 6.97, and 2.83 mM h−1 for PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF, respectively, representing 4.5, 4.8, and 5.4-fold enhancements relative to the corresponding pure-water values (0.67, 1.44, and 0.52 mM h−1). A similar trend was observed in the CA-coupled system, where the kf values increased to 1.94, 4.68, and 1.78 mM h−1 for PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF, corresponding to 2.9, 3.3, and 3.4-fold enhancements, respectively. The broadly comparable relative enhancements across the three COFs confirmed that hole scavenging by the alcohol substrate was the primary driver of the increased H2O2 yield in the coupled system. However, PTPD-COF1 consistently maintained the highest absolute kf under all conditions, and this advantage was already established in pure water where no hole scavenger was present, indicating that the superior performance originated from the stoichiometry-modulated electronic environment rather than from the hole scavenging effect. More critically, the alcohol oxidation rate constants exhibited a pronounced catalyst dependence: for AA oxidation, k varied from 0.08 h−1 (PDT-COF) to 0.66 h−1 (PTPD-COF1), representing an 8.3-fold difference under identical substrate concentration and reaction conditions. This substantial variation, which could not be accounted for by the hole scavenging mechanism alone, evidenced the role of the intrinsic electronic structure of the COFs in governing the oxidative half-reactions.

Meanwhile, the conversions of AA, OA, and CA over PTPD-COF1 were 99%, 94%, and 99% (61%, 50%, and 88% over PTT-COF; 24%, 27%, and 85% over PDT-COF, Fig. 5b). PTPD-COF1 showed good selectivity toward AAD ( ~ 100%), OAD ( ~ 100%), and CAD (94%). However, the selectivity for CAD over PTT-COF and PDT-COF was 28% and 14%, respectively. If the enhanced H2O2 production were attributable solely to the thermodynamic effect of hole consumption, the oxidation selectivity would be expected to show minimal catalyst dependence. The pronounced selectivity differences provided further evidence that the electronic structure of the COFs not only governed the reaction rate but also directed the oxidation pathway toward the desired aldehyde products. The relevant GC spectra and reaction selectivity curves were presented in Supplementary Figs. 40-45. The 1H NMR spectra of the obtained AAD, OAD, and CAD over PTPD-COF1 were displayed in Supplementary Figs. 46-48. To further verify the stoichiometry-dependent trend in the coupled system, PTPD-COF2 (PDTA:PDDA = 1:1) was also evaluated under identical conditions (Supplementary Figs. 49-50). PTPD-COF2 afforded H2O2 production rates of 42.2, 12.2, and 20.9 mmol g−1 h−1 coupled with AA, OA, and CA conversions of 95%, 77%, and 96%, respectively. These values were intermediate between those of PTPD-COF1 and the binary COFs, further confirming the composition-dependent performance trend in the coupled photocatalytic system.

The mechanism of alcohol oxidation in the H2O2 production coupling system was investigated via quenching experiments using CA + PTPD-COF1 and AA + PTPD-COF1 as examples. As displayed in Fig. 5c, the inhibition of CA conversion in air upon the addition of TEMPOL, β-carotene, and KI underscored the roles of O2•-, O21, and holes in the PTPD-COF1 photocatalytic system. Moreover, under Ar, the addition of KI led to the attenuation of CA transformation, and the introduction of KBrO3 served to promote CAD yields, confirming the role of holes for CAD formation. Similar phenomena were observed in the AAD formation process photocatalyzed by PTPD-COF1 in Supplementary Fig. 51. In addition, DMPO-trapped carbon-centered radicals were observed in CA + PTPD-COF1 and AA + PTPD-COF1 systems in Supplementary Fig. 52. The above evidence demonstrated that O2•-, O21, and holes all contributed to the oxidation of alcohols. Importantly, after 5 photocatalytic cycles (each cycle for 4 h), the H2O2 amount and CAD selectivity did not show striking changes (Supplementary Fig. 53). And PDPT-COF1 kept the crystallinity and chemical structures (Supplementary Fig. 54).

Theoretical insight into H2O2 photoproduction and fragrance upgrading

To elucidate the ground-state electronic origin of the stoichiometry-driven modulation, periodic DFT calculations of electron density, electron localization function (ELF), and electrostatic potential (ESP) were performed for PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF (Supplementary Fig. 55). The electron density and ELF maps revealed continuous π-conjugation along the imine-linked backbones, while indicating that the spatial arrangement of electron-dense and electron-localized regions followed symmetry-dependent patterns dictated by the linker connectivity. Specifically, a C3-symmetric distribution was observed around each TPA node in PTT-COF, whereas PDT-COF exhibited a C2-symmetric arrangement. In contrast, PTPD-COF1 displayed a mixed and lower-symmetry distribution pattern, associated with the coexistence of tritopic and ditopic linkers and the resulting disruption of local electronic periodicity. The ESP maps further provided insight into the electrostatic landscape of these frameworks. The binary COFs showed regular alternation of positive and negative potential regions in accordance with their uniform coordination environments, whereas PTPD-COF1 exhibited a more irregular electrostatic potential distribution throughout the framework, reflecting the geometric and electronic mismatch between PDTA and PDDA linkers. These results collectively supported the presence of electronically differentiated microenvironments in the mixed-linker framework. While these calculations represented averaged electronic properties, they were consistent with the proposed structural origin of electronic heterogeneity and provided a plausible ground-state basis for the enhanced excited-state charge separation discussed below.

The calculated unit cell dipole moments of PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF were 5.97, 7.61, and 3.44 D, respectively. Generally, a larger dipole moment provides a stronger driving force for charge separation62,64. Through stoichiometric modulation, PTPD-COF1 exhibited the largest dipole moment among the three COFs; this theoretical trend aligned well with the experimentally determined relative IEF intensities (Supplementary Fig. 16). Furthermore, the effective masses of charge carriers for the three COFs were evaluated (Fig. 5d-f), as diminished effective masses were typically instrumental in expediting charge carrier transport kinetics32,65,66. The calculation results demonstrated that PTPD-COF1 possessed significantly reduced effective masses for both electrons (me = 0.42) and holes (mh = 1.07) compared with PTT-COF (me = 0.51, mh = 1.29) and PDT-COF (me = 2.40, mh = 3.46). Additionally, the dipole moment and effective masses of PTPD-COF2 (PDTA:PDDA = 1:1) were calculated to further validate the stoichiometry-dependent trend (Supplementary Fig. 56). PTPD-COF2 exhibited a dipole moment of 6.28 D with mₑ = 0.46 and mₕ = 1.24, which were intermediate between those of PTPD-COF1 and the binary COFs. This result corroborated that the 3:1 stoichiometric ratio afforded the most favorable electronic environment among all the compositions investigated. Thus, PTPD-COF1 was theoretically predicted to facilitate superior charge carrier separation and migration, thereby underpinning its enhanced photocatalytic performance.

Subsequently, the excited-state electron and hole distributions of PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF were scrutinized utilizing the hybrid PBE0 functional, which afforded superior computational precision. As illustrated in Fig. 5d–f, the yellow and blue isosurfaces denoted the spatial regions of electron accumulation and hole accumulation in the excited states of PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF, respectively (the corresponding data for PTPD-COF2 were presented in Supplementary Fig. 56). The photoexcited electrons were primarily localized on the benzene rings, imine linkages, and pyrimidine rings (numerical labels), whereas the photogenerated holes were predominantly sequestered within the benzene and pyrimidine moieties (alphabetical labels) of the three prepared COFs. The excited-state charge distribution of PTPD-COF2 was also calculated for comparison (Supplementary Fig. 56). Although PTPD-COF2 exhibited a similar electron-hole separation pattern to PTPD-COF1, the photogenerated charges were distributed over a greater number of sites with reduced local concentration, indicating a more diffuse charge landscape. Intriguingly, the residual aldehyde groups in PTPD-COF1 did not serve as primary accumulation centers for photogenerated electrons or holes. We hypothesized that these moieties contributed to the enhanced performance by synergistically augmenting the local dipole moment and surface hydrophilicity of PTPD-COF1. The active catalytic centers for the ORR pathways in PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF were identified by evaluating O2 adsorption energies at the electron-enriched areas, and the optimized adsorption configurations were illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 57. Consequently, the benzene ring adjacent to the imine bonds from monomer TPA was identified as the most probable catalytic center for the 2e- ORR path of PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF (site 1 in Fig. 5d–f), with the O2 adsorption energies of −0.11, −0.20, and −0.10 eV, respectively, indicating the strongest O2 binding on PTPD-COF1. The optimized adsorption configurations of H2O within the hole-accumulation regions of the three COFs were depicted in Supplementary Fig. 58. The benzene ring close to the pyrimidine was most likely to be the catalytic center for the 2e- WOR path in PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF. PTPD-COF1 exhibited the strongest H2O adsorption among the three COFs, as evidenced by its most negative adsorption energy of −0.22 eV, compared to −0.18 and −0.16 eV for PTT-COF and PDT-COF, respectively.

To quantify the interfacial charge redistribution, Bader charge analysis was performed (Fig. 5g-i and Supplementary Fig. 59). Upon O2 adsorption, 0.06|e| was transferred to the adsorbed O2 on PTPD-COF1, exceeding those on PTT-COF (0.03|e | ) and PDT-COF (0.01|e | ). Conversely, H2O adsorption induced a charge transfer of 0.10|e| from H2O to PTPD-COF1, again surpassing PTT-COF (0.07|e | ) and PDT-COF (0.05|e | ). Similarly, CA adsorption at the hole-accumulation sites (Supplementary Fig. 60) resulted in a charge transfer of 0.29|e| from CA to PTPD-COF1, larger than those on PTT-COF (0.11|e | ) and PDT-COF (0.08|e | ), indicating stronger electronic coupling between CA and the active sites of PTPD-COF1, which was expected to facilitate C-H bond activation and likely contributed to the higher CAD selectivity (94% vs. 28% and 14%) observed experimentally. These results demonstrated that the stoichiometry-engineered electronic heterogeneity of PTPD-COF1 simultaneously enhanced electron donation to O2, electron withdrawal from H2O, and interfacial charge transfer from CA, collectively facilitating the ORR, WOR, and selective alcohol oxidation pathways.

Following this, the Gibbs free energies for photocatalytic H2O2 generation via the 2e- ORR route were evaluated for PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF (Fig. 5j). For PTT-COF and PTPD-COF1, the desorption of *HOOH was the rate-determining step (RDS) of ORR, with energy barriers of 0.28 and 0.14 eV, respectively. By contrast, *OOH intermediate generation became the RDS in PDT-COF, with a corresponding energy barrier of 0.32 eV. Regarding the 2e- WOR pathway, *OH generation was identified as the RDS for PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF, with energy barriers of 2.64, 2.01, and 2.69 eV, respectively (Fig. 5k). As for the conversion of CA, C-H bond cleavage served as the pivotal kinetic bottleneck, with energy barriers calculated at 1.26, 1.13, and 1.29 eV (Fig. 5l) for PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, and PDT-COF, respectively. The related reaction coordinates were displayed in Supplementary Figs. 61-63. The lowest C-H cleavage barrier of PTPD-COF1 (1.13 eV) was thus directly linked to the strongest interfacial charge transfer upon CA adsorption identified by Bader charge analysis, providing a unified atomic-level rationale for its highest CAD selectivity (94%) among the three COFs. These computational insights corroborated that stoichiometry-driven electronic modulation effectively lowered the kinetic barriers of the RDS, consistent with our experimental findings. The proposed mechanisms for H2O2 photoproduction coupled with CAD formation were schematically illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 64. Upon light irradiation, PTPD-COF1 generated electron-hole pairs that were spatially separated to drive two concurrent half-reactions: (1) on the reduction side, photogenerated electrons reduced O2 to O2•-, which was subsequently converted to H2O2; (2) on the oxidation side, photogenerated holes, O2•-, and 1O2 synergistically oxidized CA to CAD. This coupling demonstrated the synergistic interaction between the two half-reactions, where the consumption of holes by CA oxidation suppressed charge recombination and thereby promoted H2O2 production on the reductive side.

Discussion

In summary, we have developed a stoichiometry-driven electronic modulation strategy to engineer local electronic heterogeneity in multicomponent COFs for coupled H2O2 production and fragrance upgrading. By precisely tuning the ratios of monomers with distinct connectivity, electronically differentiated microenvironments were generated within the framework, leading to improved charge separation and optimized reaction energetics. The resulting PTPD-COF1 exhibited superior photocatalytic performance, achieving high H2O2 production rates in both pure water and alcohol-containing systems, along with excellent selectivity toward aldehyde products. Mechanistic investigations indicated that the enhanced activity originated primarily from the catalyst’s electronic structure, which governed both charge carrier separation and oxidation reactivity. Importantly, this work demonstrates that stoichiometric control offers an effective handle to modulate the electronic landscape of COFs beyond conventional structural design. Such an approach provides opportunities for constructing multifunctional photocatalytic systems by integrating reduction and oxidation processes within a single platform. We anticipate that this strategy will be applicable to a broad range of photocatalytic transformations requiring efficient charge carrier management and coordinated reaction pathways.

Methods

Synthesis of PTT-COF

PTT-COF was synthesized via the condensation of TPA (17.06 mg, 0.02 mmol) and PDTA (14.14 mg, 0.04 mmol). The monomers were charged into a 10 mL ampoule tube containing a mixture of o-DCB (0.5 mL) and DMAc (0.5 mL), followed by sonication to ensure homogeneity. Subsequently, 0.1 mL of aqueous acetic acid (HAc, 6 M) was added to the system. The tube was degassed through three freeze-pump-thaw cycles and then flame-sealed. After heating at 120 °C for 5 days, the resulting precipitate was collected by filtration and washed thoroughly with THF and water. The crude product was further purified by Soxhlet extraction with THF for 24 h and dried under vacuum to yield a light yellow powder (88.6%).

Synthesis of PDT-COF

PDT-COF was prepared using a similar procedure with TPA (17.06 mg, 0.02 mmol) and PDDA (15.74 mg, 0.06 mmol). The monomers were dispersed in a mixture of o-DCB (0.5 mL) and DMAc (0.5 mL). Then, 0.1 mL of trifluoroacetic acid was added as the catalyst. The mixture was sealed after three freeze-pump-thaw cycles and heated at 120 °C for 7 days. The resulting mixture was filtered and washed with THF and water. After Soxhlet extraction with THF for 24 h and vacuum drying, a light yellow powder was obtained with a yield of 80.2%.

Synthesis of PTPD-COF1

For the synthesis of the multicomponent PTPD-COF1, TPA (17.06 mg, 0.02 mmol), PDTA (10.61 mg, 0.03 mmol), and PDDA (2.62 mg, 0.01 mmol) were charged into a 10 mL ampoule tube with o-DCB (0.5 mL) and DMAc (0.5 mL). After sonication, 0.2 mL of HAc (6 M) was added to the mixture. The tube was sealed following three freeze-pump-thaw cycles and maintained at 150 °C for 5 days. The obtained precipitate was filtered, washed with THF and water, and subjected to Soxhlet extraction with THF for 24 h. Drying under vacuum afforded a light yellow powder (90.7% yield).

Photocatalytic H2O2 production

In a typical experiment, the photocatalyst (PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, PTPD-COF2, or PDT-COF, 5 mg) was dispersed in deionized water (40 mL) in a quartz reactor by sonication for 5 min. The suspension was saturated with O2 and then irradiated under an O2 atmosphere using a Xe lamp equipped with a 420 nm cutoff filter (CHL-HXF300-T3), while the reactor temperature was maintained by a circulating water bath. At specific time intervals, 500 μL aliquots of the reaction mixture were withdrawn and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter to remove residual photocatalyst particles. Each filtrate was mixed with 30 μL of a pre-prepared titanium sulfate solution and diluted to a total volume of 2 mL. The H2O2 concentration was then determined from the absorbance at 408 nm using a UV-vis spectrophotometer.

Photocatalytic H2O2 generation coupled with fragrance upgrading

The photocatalyst (PTT-COF, PTPD-COF1, or PDT-COF, 5 mg), the corresponding substrate (CA, AA, or OA, 0.1 mmol), and deionized water (40 mL) were combined in a quartz reactor and dispersed by sonication for 5 min. The suspension was irradiated with a Xe lamp equipped with a 420 nm cutoff filter (CHL-HXF300-T3), while O2 was continuously bubbled through the reaction mixture and the reactor temperature was maintained by a circulating water bath. At given irradiation times (1, 2, 3, and 4 h), aliquots of the reaction mixture were withdrawn and filtered to remove residual photocatalyst; the H2O2 concentration in the filtrate was then determined by UV-vis spectrophotometry. Meanwhile, 2 mL of the reaction supernatant was extracted with CH2Cl2 (2 mL), and the yield and selectivity of CAD, AAD, and OAD were determined by GC analysis using n-dodecane (4 μL) as an internal standard. The chemical structures of the oxidation products were further confirmed by 1H NMR spectroscopy.
