Introduction

Circadian rhythms are endogenous oscillations with an approximately 24-hour cycle.1 It is organised by the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), known as the ‘central master clock’, and modulated by endogenous, behavioural and environmental factors.1 2 Circadian disruption is associated with an increased risk of the development of neurologic, psychiatric, metabolic, cardiovascular, allergic and immune disorders.1 2

Biomarkers of circadian rhythm mainly include melatonin and cortisol secretions, as well as core body temperature.1 Among all biomarkers, plasma melatonin is the most reliable and commonly used biomarker of endogenous circadian rhythms. Melatonin acts through multiple pathways to regulate the sleep-wake cycle and promote sleep.3 Cortisol, in contrast to melatonin, acts as an antagonistic hormone in the regulation of circadian rhythms. It is secreted in large quantities during the first hour after waking, rapidly reaching its peak before declining sharply. To regulate the sleep-wake cycle, melatonin concentrations rise as light fades prior to darkness, peak in the middle of the night (between 02:00 and 04:00), and fall when exposed to light.4 Postoperative plasma melatonin and cortisol levels are dynamically measured every 6 hours to calculate the circadian rhythm parameters (mesor, amplitude and acrophase), while their secretory rhythm may be modified by anaesthesia and surgery.5 To minimise disruption of participant sleep by multiple blood drawings, plasma melatonin and cortisol levels are dynamically measured every 6 hours during the postoperative follow-up period at seven time points.5 The measurement of the dim light melatonin onset (DLMO) in clinical practice poses challenges because multiple blood samples are required.

Melatonin concentrations in saliva constitute 24–33% of those found in plasma.6 Interestingly, saliva is an alternative to blood sampling, and it can be easily obtained from the patients under home conditions, and saliva collection is less invasive than blood collection. 7 8Therefore, saliva can be used as an alternative to plasma to determine the concentrations and circadian rhythm of melatonin.

Multiple studies validate salivary melatonin as a reliable proxy for serum measurements. Gooneratne et al first demonstrated a strong correlation between salivary and serum melatonin levels in older adults with comorbidities (r=0.659),9 while subsequent studies confirmed this relationship in healthy volunteers (r=0.764, p<0.001)7 and bipolar disorder patients (intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC)=0.937),10 collectively supporting its utility across clinical populations. However, the relationship between salivary melatonin levels and plasma melatonin measurements in surgical patients is unknown.

Previous studies have revealed that the circadian rhythm of melatonin is significantly altered by anaesthesia drugs, surgery, inflammation and pain.5 11 12 Furthermore, our studies found a potential linkage of abnormal plasma melatonin secretion patterns to postoperative sleep disturbances and postoperative delirium (POD), which is associated with an increased risk of mortality.5 12 Therefore, the circadian rhythm of melatonin plays a critical role in perioperative patient prognosis.13 However, to our knowledge, few studies have compared both the peak melatonin levels (measured at 04:00) and circadian rhythms using both plasma and saliva matrices in surgical patients. In this report, we present the design of a prospective study to analyse the correlation between peak salivary and plasma melatonin concentrations in patients undergoing surgery.

Methods and analysis

Trial design

This multicentre prospective study was conducted in October 2023 at Peking University Third Hospital in Beijing and the Affiliated Tai'an City Central Hospital of Qingdao University in China. We aim to recruit 102 patients who will undergo elective neurosurgical and thoracic procedures under general anaesthesia. This study is being conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013) and is registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2300070470). Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants prior to study initiation. Figure 1 presents the flow chart of this study. Patient and public involvement

No patient involved.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome is to assess the correlation between the peak concentrations of salivary and blood melatonin (measured at 04:00 on the first postoperative day) in patients.

Secondary outcomes

The secondary outcome includes analysing the correlation between the salivary and blood melatonin rhythm parameters (mesor, amplitude and acrophase) during the perioperative period in surgical patients, as well as the correlation between salivary and blood cortisol concentrations and rhythm parameters. Additional exploratory analyses examine whether the peak levels and rhythm parameters of melatonin and cortisol in saliva and blood correlate with patients’ perioperative sleep quality, anxiety and depression.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

Participants are included if they meet the following criteria: age of ≥18 years, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification of I to III, and postoperative admission to a general ward.

Exclusion criteria

Exclusion criteria encompass the following conditions and circumstances: diagnosed neurodegenerative disorders (Parkinson’s disease, dementia),14 clinically significant neuropsychiatric conditions, severe deafness or vision problems, impaired communication capacity, substance use disorders (including alcohol dependence and illicit drug addiction), active oral pathologies, occupational engagements requiring overnight shifts, voluntary withdrawal of consent, unanticipated hospital discharge.

Preoperative baseline data collection and evaluation

Baseline data are recorded by trained investigators, including demographic data, smoking and alcohol consumption status, preoperative diagnosis, the ASA physical status classification and the results of physical examinations, laboratory tests and instrumental assessments. We also assess patients’ preoperative sleep quality, as well as anxiety and depression. Subjective sleep assessment is evaluated using the Pittsburgh sleep quality index (PSQI), the Athens insomnia scale (AIS), numerical rating scale (NRS) and sleep diaries for preoperative. Drowsiness is assessed with the Stanford sleepiness scale (SSS), while anxiety and depression levels are evaluated using the Hamilton anxiety rating scale (HAMA) and the Hamilton depression rating scale (HAMD).15 16

Perioperative management

All participants are placed in a standardised room with room temperature and a consistent light-dark cycle. The participants are instructed to avoid caffeine, alcohol and chocolate. During the study period, individual activities are restricted from 21:00 to 06:00 daily, during which they will be protected from factors, including television, noise and mobile phones.12 The lights are turned off between 21:00 and 06:00. Additionally, samples are collected using flashlight illumination to minimise light exposure (controlled to <10 lux).17

Anaesthesia and analgesia

All patients are conducted between 8:00 AM and 6:00 PM. During the perioperative phase, sedatives (benzodiazepine and dexmedetomidine) or anticholinergics are avoided. Anaesthesia is intravenously induced with 0.2 mg/kg etomidate, 0.5–1.5 mg/kg propofol, 0.3 µg/kg sufentanil and 0.2 mg/kg cisatracurium. Anaesthesia is maintained with sevoflurane, remifentanil and cisatracurium. For patients undergoing video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery for lung resection, ultrasound-guided T4 and T6 paravertebral nerve block analgesia is performed before skin incision, with 10 mL of 0.33% ropivacaine injected at each point.

For postoperative analgesia management, if a patient’s NRS pain score exceeds 4, tramadol is administered for rescue analgesia.

Intraoperative data collection

On the day of surgery, intraoperative parameters are recorded by anaesthesiologists, including the time of surgery (AM/PM), duration of anaesthesia and surgery, intraoperative input volumes (crystalloids, colloids and blood transfusions), output volumes (blood loss and urine output), the mean arterial pressure, heart rate, Bispectral Index (BIS) value, complications and adverse events.18 19

Postoperative management and follow-up

Trained researchers perform three follow-up assessments on all patients postoperatively: on the night of surgery (8:00 PM), the first postoperative day and the second postoperative day (8:00 AM). These assessments include the recording of patients’ sleep patterns, the intensity of postoperative pain and postoperative complications.

Sleep assessmentParticipants’ objective sleep quality is assessed using a Fitbit Charge 3 (Fitbit Inc., San Francisco, California, USA) wrist band that integrates triaxial accelerometry and photoplethysmography to classify sleep stages (wakefulness, light sleep, deep sleep and REM sleep).20 21 Subjective sleep quality is concurrently evaluated using the PSQI, the SSS, the Athens Insomnia Scale (AIS),22 the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) (0=worst sleep and 10=best sleep)23 and a sleep diary.

Sample collection

Based on the principles of circadian rhythm parameter fitting using a circadian rhythm software and to minimise the impact of repeated postoperative blood draws on patients’ sleep, blood samples are collected at 6-hour intervals over a 24-hour period. Considering the rhythm of melatonin secretion, which begins to rise between 8:00 PM and 10:00 PM and peaks between 2:00 AM and 4:00 AM, the blood collection time points are selected to encompass the peak melatonin secretion as much as possible. Therefore, the final sampling time: pre-anaesthesia, 22:00 on the day of surgery, 04:00, 10:00, 16:00 and 22:00 on postoperative day 1. During the perioperative period, 1 mL of peripheral blood is drawn at each time point. Blood samples are stored in EDTA anticoagulation tubes, centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min to separate the plasma, and then stored in sterile polypropylene tubes at −80°C. Plasma melatonin concentrations are measured using ELISA with a detection limit of 1.6 pg/mL.

For saliva collection, participants abstain from oral intake for 30 min. A Salivette swab (Sarstedt, Germany) is held sublingually for 1 min until saturated, then transferred to a sterile tube.27 The tube is centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 3 min, and the samples are stored at −80°C until analysis. Paired blood and saliva specimens from all participants will undergo stratified subgroup analyses.

Blinding

Preoperative and postoperative data collection are performed by physicians who are not involved in sample collection or testing. The analysis of salivary and plasma melatonin levels will be conducted by the Laboratory Department of Peking University Third Hospital, with laboratory personnel remaining blinded to the sample collection process. Statistical analysis will be carried out by epidemiological experts from the Epidemiology Research Center of Peking University Third Hospital, who will also be blinded to the group allocation.

Sample size estimation

The sample size was calculated based on the confidence interval method for Spearman's rank correlation coefficient, using PASS software. According to previous studies, the correlation coefficient between plasma and salivary melatonin concentrations is 0.764.7 To obtain a two-sided 95% confidence interval with a target width of 0.2, a minimum of 92 participants was required. Accounting for an estimated dropout rate of approximately 10%, the final target sample size was set at 102 participants.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses will be conducted using SPSS version 25.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA). The R package MetaCycle will be used to detect the circadian rhythm parameters including mesor (mean value of 24-hour rhythm), amplitude (the difference between the maximum and average value), and peak time (time of peak). Rhythmic P less than 0.05 is considered to have a circadian rhythm.9 One-way ANOVA analysis will be performed on Graphpad Prism 8.0.2 (GraphPad Software) to compare the differences between saliva and plasma melatonin. P Values less than 0.05 are considered indicative of statistical significance.9 To assess the statistical differences between the circadian rhythm parameters (mesor, amplitude and acrophase) of saliva and plasma melatonin, paired sample t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests will be performed. The correlation between the two will be determined by Pearson correlation or Spearman rank correlation. Data processing will be performed using the mice package in R with the method of chained equations.28 Simple linear regression models will be used to analyze the association between plasma and salivary concentrations and the rhythm parameters of melatonin and cortisol. All cases with protocol deviations, including those lost to follow-up or with incomplete sample collection, will be retained in the final analysis. Missing data will be addressed through multiple imputation approaches, including mean substitution and regression-based imputation techniques. All statistical tests will be two-tailed, with a significance level set at p<0.05.

Data management and storage

All research data is systematically collected and documented in the case report forms. Patient identifiers will be strictly protected and not disclosed beyond the hospital premises. For research findings intended for publication, all personally identifiable information will be replaced with unique coded identifiers. The coding key will be securely maintained at Peking University Third Hospital with restricted access. Statistical analysis will be conducted exclusively by the principal investigator (PI) and authorised research personnel under strict confidentiality protocols.

Ethics and declarations

This study was conducted in strict compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and China’s Good Clinical Practice guidelines. The study protocol (M2023009) has been reviewed and approved by the Medical Science Research Ethics Committees of Peking University Third Hospital, with annual progress reports submitted for continuing ethics oversight. Prior to enrolment, all participants are provided with written informed consent through standardised documentation that has undergone linguistic validation. Registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (www.chictr.org.cn, ChiCTR2300070470; registration date: 2023-05-19), this trial adheres to the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform standards. An independent data monitoring committee will conduct interim safety reviews using predefined stopping rules. All protocol amendments will undergo formal ethics review prior to implementation. Study findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications.

Data safety monitoring

The data monitoring committee (DMC), comprising two specialists, will be monitoring the safety and progress of the trial. The DMC can give suggestions on safety issues and even pause the study.

Discussion

This multicentre clinical study is designed to analyse the correlation between peak salivary and plasma melatonin concentrations in surgical patients. We aim to determine if saliva specimens provide a reliable, non-invasive and practical method for assessing melatonin and cortisol circadian rhythms for studying CRD in surgical patients.

Studies have demonstrated that saliva and plasma melatonin levels show strong correlations in non-surgical populations. Gooneratne et al conducted the first study to compare saliva and serum melatonin levels in older adults with moderate levels of co-existent disease and reported that there was a very good correlation between saliva and serum melatonin levels (r=0.659).9 Besides, a study indicated that there was a significant correlation between the plasma and salivary melatonin concentrations (r=0.764, p<0.001) in 21 healthy volunteers.7 In addition, Leibenluft et al measured plasma and salivary melatonin simultaneously in a sample of 12 bipolar disorder patients and therefore concluded that the intraclass correlation coefficient between plasma and salivary measures of melatonin was 0.93.7 Many studies showed that saliva melatonin assessment offers a great deal of promise as a potential surrogate marker of the serum melatonin level in older adults with co-existent disease, healthy volunteers and bipolar disorder.7 9 10

However, the perioperative period presents a unique physiological state characterised by inflammation, surgery, anaesthesia and pain.2931 These factors can alter hormone secretion, potentially modifying the relationship between salivary and plasma melatonin. Our previous work has shown that circadian rhythms are highly susceptible to disruption by anaesthesia and surgery.11

If our study confirms a strong correlation, salivary melatonin assay could improve postoperative circadian monitoring. The non-invasive nature of saliva collection will facilitate frequent, patient-friendly sampling on surgical wards.32 This opens the door for integration with emerging wearable biosensing platforms.33 34 Future systems could combine physiological data from consumer-grade wearables (Fitbit) with periodic salivary biomarker measurements, creating a holistic, real-time picture of a patient’s circadian health. Such integrated monitoring could enable personalised chronotherapeutic interventions, such as timed administration of melatonin or targeted light therapy, to actively resynchronise disrupted circadian rhythms and mitigate associated complications.3537

Postoperative delirium remains a common and serious complication, strongly linked to circadian disruption.38 A validated salivary melatonin assay could facilitate the routine identification of patients at high risk for circadian disruption on admission or in the early postoperative period. This would allow for the early implementation of non-pharmacological, chronobiological interventions aligned with recent clinical guidelines on delirium prevention.39 40 For instance, patients with a significantly delayed or blunted melatonin rhythm could receive intensified environmental controls (strict light-dark cycles, noise reduction) or be prioritised for trials of supplemental melatonin or melatonin receptor agonists, potentially reducing the incidence and severity of delirium.4145

A recognised challenge involves the risk of insufficient saliva volume due to perioperative factors such as fluid restriction, opioid-induced xerostomia or prolonged fasting.46 Our countermeasures integrate evidence-based optimisation: preoperatively, patients would receive controlled intravenous hydration (1–2 mL/kg/h crystalloids) to maintain euvolemia.47 Postoperatively, early oral rehydration is encouraged as soon as clinically feasible, as even minimal water intake could stimulate salivary flow. To address residual volumetric variability, we would standardise minimum collection thresholds (0.5 mL) and employ mixed-effects statistical models to account for missing data, ensuring robust rhythm parameter analysis even with incomplete samples.48

However, this study protocol has several limitations. First, the 6-hour intervals between saliva and blood sample collections may have missed the peak secretion levels of melatonin and cortisol.49 Second, we only assess the circadian rhythms of melatonin and cortisol within the first 24 hours postoperatively, and the melatonin disturbance might have lasted for a longer period.50 51 Thirdly, the small sample size may limit the statistical power and reduce the level of evidence.52 Finally, the generalisability of our findings is constrained by the homogeneity of the cohort (eg, exclusion of ASA IV-V patients and night-shift workers53 and the standardised perioperative environment (light <10 lux, activity restrictions), which may not reflect real-world clinical heterogeneity. Additionally, the focus on thoracic and neurosurgical procedures limits extrapolation to other surgeries with distinct inflammatory or analgesic profiles (eg, abdominal or cardiovascular surgeries).
