EPI-Sense: an integrated hemodynamic sensing and analysis system for early pressure injury warning
Authors: Ziyang Bao, Yan Jiang, Mingseng Guo, Hua Cao, Wei Cun, Ke Xu, Kesheng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPressure injury poses a critical challenge in geriatric care. Current computer-aided diagnosis is limited by an exclusive reliance on surface texture, neglecting subcutaneous hemodynamics essential for distinguishing Reactive Hyperemia from Stage 1 PI and detecting Deep Tissue Pressure Injuries. To address this, we constructed a customized cross-polarized acquisition system and a dedicated clinical dataset.MethodsWe present the Physio-Guided Network (PGNet), a multi-modal framework leveraging remote Photoplethysmography (rPPG) to provide complementary non-contact hemodynamic cues related to local microcirculatory dynamics. Specifically, we engineered a Frequency-Domain SNR-Weighted strategy to retrieve attenuated physiological signals from necrotic tissue, and a Physio-Visual Gating Unit for dynamic modality arbitration.ResultsPatient-level leave-one-out validation supports the feasibility of the proposed framework for differentiating morphologically similar early pressure injury states. The results indicate that rPPG-driven hemodynamic guidance provides complementary information beyond visual-only analysis.ConclusionsBy bridging superficial morphology with non-contact hemodynamic sensing, this study demonstrates the clinical relevance and technical feasibility of rPPG-guided multimodal analysis for early pressure injury warning, providing patient-level evidence that supports its potential value for differentiating clinically ambiguous RH, S1, and DTPI cases and laying the groundwork for future multicenter, device-independent validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08796-6.Keywords: Feasibility analysis, Geriatric care monitoring, Multi-modal fusion, Pressure injury, Remote photoplethysmography (rPPG)

Introduction

Pressure Injury (PI), also known as pressure ulcer, is a prevalent and debilitating complication in the geriatric population worldwide, imposing a substantial burden on healthcare systems [1]. According to recent global epidemiological analyzes, the incidence of PI in long-term care facilities ranges from 3.4% to 32.4% [1, 2]. The mortality rates for elderly patients with severe injuries increasing by two to four times [3]. Early detection (e.g., at Stage 1) allows for timely intervention, potentially increasing healing rates to more than 80% and preventing progression to osteomyelitis or sepsis [4].

In actual clinical diagnosis, nurses are required to evaluate skin lesions according to the NPIAP/EPUAP guidelines [1]. To achieve this, medical personnel must rely on manual inspection and palpation to assess skin integrity, temperature, and the reaction to blanch [5]. However, these manual diagnostic methods are time-consuming, labor-intensive, and inherently subjective. Factors such as lighting conditions and observer experience can significantly affect diagnostic consistency, easily leading to misdiagnosis—particularly when distinguishing early-stage Pressure injury from benign reactive hyperemia [5, 6].

Although High-Frequency Ultrasound (HFUS) can detect deep tissue damage below the epidermis and accurately assess the extent of infiltration [7], its deployment is severely restricted by high costs and the need for specialized sonographers, making it unsuitable for daily high-frequency monitoring in home-care or nursing homes.

In contrast, Computer-Aided Diagnosis technology has emerged as a promising alternative to alleviate these limitations. With the rapid development of Deep Learning, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) [8] and Vision Transformers (ViTs) [9] have achieved dermatologist-level performance in various medical imaging tasks. In the field of automatic PI diagnosis, researchers have made numerous contributions by classifying and segmenting wound images to assist nursing staff [10, 11].

However, a critical limitation persists in current literature: most state-of-the-art methods only consider a single modality—namely, RGB clinical images. These approaches treat Pressure injury classification as a purely visual texture recognition problem, relying heavily on surface features such as discoloration, eschar, or skin breakage [11]. This texture-dependent paradigm reaches an intrinsic limitation when addressing the fine-grained differentiation required in the 3-class task.

A primary challenge lies in distinguishing Stage 1 (S1) Pressure injury from Reactive Hyperemia (RH). To the best of our knowledge, this study provides an exploratory task-specific investigation of non-contact rPPG-derived hemodynamic features for differentiating RH, S1, and DTPI in early pressure injury assessment. While advanced stages (e.g., Stages 2–4, Unstageable) exhibit conspicuous morphological features readily identifiable by standard vision, the precise discrimination and containment of early-stage precursors remain a largely unexplored frontier. Crucially, the clinical value of preemptive stratification at these nascent stages far outweighs that of classifying established lesions, as it provides an important actionable window to reverse tissue damage before irreversible ulceration occurs. While both conditions (S1, RH) manifest visually as superficial erythema with overlapping color and texture distributions, their pathological divergence is hemodynamic rather than morphological.

Clinically, this distinction relies on the blanching test—a dynamic assessment of microcirculatory refill. Static RGB imaging, by definition, lacks the temporal dimension required to capture this vascular responsiveness. Consequently, relying solely on surface visual features creates an intrinsic observational deficit, rendering the model blind to the subcutaneous blood flow dynamics essential for accurate staging [12].

Second, for DTPI, the skin surface may remain intact while the underlying soft tissue undergoes ischemic necrosis [13]. As noted in recent studies on skin lesion analysis [14], utilizing only lateral surface information may result in the model overfitting to superficial artifacts while ignoring the depth information related to tissue viability. Consequently, purely visual models are limited in their ability to reflect functional changes associated with inside-out pathological progression, potentially leading to decision ambiguity in clinically ambiguous cases [15].

To overcome this morpho-functional dissociation, some studies in dermatology have attempted to fuse clinical images with dermoscopy or ultrasound [16]. However, for Pressure injury monitoring in elderly care, we require a solution that is non-contact, low-cost, and ubiquitous. To this end, we propose a novel perspective: leveraging remote Photoplethysmography (rPPG) as an indirect functional cue related to local microcirculatory dynamics. rPPG technology can extract the Blood Volume Pulse (BVP) from standard video streams, providing a window into local microcirculatory dynamics without physical contact.

Accordingly, this study addresses a specific and clinically important gap in early pressure injury warning: the use of non-contact local hemodynamic sensing to assist the fine-grained differentiation among RH, S1, and DTPI. Existing pressure injury algorithms mainly rely on surface morphology, whereas prior iPPG/rPPG studies have largely focused on systemic vital signs or local perfusion assessment in other clinical contexts. Therefore, the novelty of this work lies in the task-specific integration of cross-polarized video acquisition, weak-signal rPPG enhancement, lesion/reference physiological normalization, and physio-guided multimodal learning for early pressure injury warning.

Related work

Computer vision for pressure injury diagnosis

In recent years, deep learning has emerged as a compelling technical approach for automated Pressure injury assessment, significantly advancing feature representation capabilities beyond the limitations of traditional assessment [10, 17]. To address the need for rapid screening in clinical settings, one-stage object detection models have gained significant attention due to their inference efficiency. Aldughayfiq et al. [18] utilized the YOLOv5 architecture to achieve four-class PI detection, enhancing model robustness across diverse scenarios through extensive data augmentation. Building upon this, Liu et al. [19] recently introduced an advanced intelligent machine vision system based on YOLOv8, demonstrating that lightweight detection models can achieve rapid determination of PI stages, offering a viable path for real-time mobile monitoring.

Beyond qualitative classification, precise measurement of the wound area is critical for tracking the healing process. Liu et al. [20] developed a deep learning model combining U-Net and Mask R-CNN for PI surface assessment, achieving high accuracy in delineating wound boundaries. However, relying solely on 2D segmentation fails to capture the volumetric characteristics crucial for severity assessment. To address the inherent lack of depth perception in planar imaging, Liu et al. [21] innovatively integrated a LiDAR camera with U-Net, significantly reducing errors in geometric area measurement by incorporating depth information.

Distinct from studies focusing solely on morphological structure, recent works have dedicated efforts to resolving clinically ambiguous fine-grained differentiation tasks. Brehmer et al. [22] proposed a multimodal deep learning framework specifically designed to distinguish Pressure injury from Incontinence-Associated Dermatitis (IAD), effectively reducing the misdiagnosis rate between these visually similar conditions. Simultaneously, to overcome the inherent limitations of visible light imaging, Pandey et al. [23] explored the application of thermal imaging. However, conventional modalities such as RGB photography and thermography are inherently restricted to superficial assessments. They fail to capture the deep microcirculatory hemodynamics essential for differentiating morphologically similar conditions, specifically distinguishing reactive hyperemia from stage 1 PI. This limitation highlights the critical need for a new diagnostic modality capable of probing invisible subcutaneous viability.

Remote photoplethysmography in healthcare

Remote Photoplethysmography has evolved from a novel computer vision task to a viable tool for clinical physiological monitoring. Initially, research efforts were predominantly concentrated on extracting systemic vital signs, such as heart rate (HR) and oxygen saturation (SpO2), from facial videos. Bondarenko et al. [24] conducted a comprehensive analysis of facial regions of interest, demonstrating that selective processing of specific facial areas significantly enhances signal quality compared to full-face averaging. To further improve estimation accuracy in unconstrained environments, Agarwal et al. [25] recently introduced the SHINE framework, utilizing the synergy of Transformers and contrastive learning to robustly estimate SpO2.

Expanding beyond systemic vitals, recent studies have confirmed the versatility of Imaging Photoplethysmography (iPPG) in assessing local microcirculatory dynamics. Kamshilin et al. [26] validated the feasibility of iPPG for assessing endothelial function by monitoring the skin’s perfusion response to local heating. In the intraoperative domain, Kamshilin et al. [27] successfully applied this technology to visualize real-time tissue perfusion changes during abdominal surgeries. Extending this to neurosurgery, Shcherbinin et al. [28] enabled the detection of subtle cortical blood flow fluctuations during aneurysm operations. Finally, Qiu et al. [29] provided a comprehensive review of these surgical applications, highlighting iPPG as a promising tool for improving patient safety.

However, a critical gap remains in applying rPPG to pressure injury-prone sites, where intrinsic signal amplitudes are significantly weaker than in vascular-rich facial regions. This anatomically induced attenuation renders standard algorithms ineffective against environmental noise, necessitating a specialized strategy tailored to salvaging faint pulsatile markers from these low-signal zones.

Multimodal fusion learning in medical imaging

Multimodal learning aims to synthesize complementary information from different sensors to breakthrough the diagnostic bottlenecks of single modalities. In the field of skin lesion analysis, fusing visual information at different scales (e.g., clinical and dermoscopic images) or integrating heterogeneous data has become a mainstream strategy to improve accuracy. To capture second-order correlations between modalities, Wei et al. [30] proposed a multi-modal bilinear fusion network based on a hybrid attention mechanism for the joint classification of clinical and dermoscopic images, effectively exploiting the complementarity of two views. Addressing the need for feature enhancement, Ji et al. [31] developed EFAM-Net, which successfully combines deep semantic features with hand-crafted texture and shape features through a designed Enhanced Feature Fusion Module. Similarly, Lin et al. [32] introduced a multi-task learning framework based on label-level fusion, utilizing metadata to assist in the training of the primary visual task.

More notably, to integrate surface and depth information, Xiao et al. [33] proposed an attention-guided learning network that fuses clinical images with ultrasound images, demonstrating that depth information is crucial for differentiating complex skin lesions. Existing fusion strategies remain suboptimal for Pressure injury diagnosis as they implicitly assume symmetric reliability across modalities, neglecting the severe spatiotemporal heterogeneity inherent in non-contact monitoring. This modal conflict renders simple concatenation or static weighting ineffective against scenarios where a single modality is dominated by noise. Consequently, there is an urgent need for a gating mechanism capable of dynamically recalibrating feature weights based on signal-to-noise ratios, ensuring robust decision-making even when one modality fails.

Custom high-fidelity data acquisition system

Conventional rPPG research predominantly targets highly perfused facial regions. In contrast, pressure injury sites present a physiological challenge. Due to sparse capillary density and a thicker stratum corneum [34], the intrinsic microcirculatory signals in these areas are significantly attenuated [35]. Consumer-grade devices often compromise signal integrity through Automatic Gain Control (AGC) and lossy compression. To circumvent these technical bottlenecks, we engineered a bespoke acquisition platform. This system delivers the high spatio-temporal fidelity essential for resolving minute hemodynamic fluctuations within compressed tissues.

The imaging unit consists of an industrial-grade color camera (Hikrobot MV-CH050-60UC) equipped with a large-aperture fixed-focus lens (M1406-12MP, 6 mm, F1.4). During the acquisition process, the camera is fixed at a distance of to ensure that the field of view (FOV) clearly covers the entire Pressure injury area and the surrounding reference skin [36]. The system is configured with a high spatial resolution of 2448 × 2048 to preserve intricate texture details, while the sampling rate is set to . This temporal resolution was selected to ensure sufficient oversampling of the Blood Volume Pulse (BVP) signal, thereby preserving the morphological integrity of systolic and diastolic peaks against high-frequency noise.

Tissue exudates and the stratum corneum often cause severe specular artifacts [37, 38]. To suppress them, we implemented a cross-polarized illumination scheme (Fig. 2). The system utilizes a DC-driven 8 W white LED for stable broadband illumination [39]. Crucially, a linear polarizer and an analyzer are aligned in an orthogonal () configuration [37, 40]. This design physically decouples surface glare from the target signal. Since surface reflections retain incident polarization, they are effectively blocked. This optical gating strategy serves as a critical prerequisite for our algorithm, ensuring that the captured photons carry genuine, albeit weak, hemodynamic information rather than superficial noise [41].

ROI selection and robust tracking

To decouple specific lesion responses from inherent physiological and environmental baselines, we implemented an automated dual-ROI initialization protocol. By applying adaptive threshold segmentation to the initial frame, the system algorithmically delineates the erythema region as the target Lesion ROI while simultaneously identifying adjacent non-lesion tissue as the reference Normal Skin ROI [42]. To handle variations in baseline skin color across patients, we compute an erythema index () for each valid pixel and set the lesion segmentation threshold dynamically as:

The 95th percentile captures the top 5% of the most erythematous pixels, adapting to each patient’s lesion intensity, while the lower bound prevents false positives in healthy skin. This ensures that only regions with physiologically meaningful hyperemia are selected as the lesion ROI. By utilizing the normal skin as a dynamic control, we perform a ratio-based calibration to reduce inter-individual baseline intensity differences and illumination-related variation, enabling the capture of relative pathological deviations.

In view of the inevitable minor patient movements during acquisition, we employ the Kanade-Lucas-Tomasi (KLT) algorithm for robust tracking [43]. First, Shi-Tomasi corner features are detected within the ROIs. Subsequently, Pyramidal Optical Flow is utilized to frame-by-frame estimate the displacement vectors of these feature points, thereby dynamically updating the ROI boundary coordinates. This step effectively corrects planar motion bias, ensuring that the subsequently extracted spatial mean RGB series consistently originates from the exact same anatomical location, thus establishing a solid foundation of spatial consistency for high-fidelity physiological signal analysis.

Data-driven feature engineering and selection

To address signal attenuation in necrotic tissue and prevent overfitting in limited-sample regimes [46], we prioritized explicit feature engineering over end-to-end learning. We constructed a high-dimensional feature pool spanning time-domain (e.g., RPR, RMS), frequency-domain (e.g., Spectral Entropy), and nonlinear dynamics (e.g., Hjorth parameters), normalized by a lesion-to-reference ratio. Physio-guided multi-modal dual-stream network

To address the limitation that existing CNNs often fail to detect early-stage pressure injuries by neglecting subcutaneous hemodynamics [48], we propose an asymmetric dual-stream architecture comprising a deep spatial encoder and a hierarchical hemodynamic embedding network (Fig. 6). This framework reconciles the spatial complexity of visual data with the semantic density of physiological priors by using rPPG-derived perfusion and complexity metrics as functional references. Through a Physio-Visual Gating mechanism, the network dynamically recalibrates visual representations to prioritize microcirculatory dysfunction over superficial artifacts, thereby supporting classification in clinically ambiguous cases [49].

Dataset construction and protocols

Data acquisition and rigorous annotation

To establish a functional benchmark, we constructed the Clinical Video Pressure Injury (CVPI) dataset under strict IRB approval. This cohort captures the dynamic hemodynamic fluctuations essential for differentiating RH, S1, and DTPI. All cases were labeled according to the NPIAP/EPUAP pressure injury staging criteria. RH and S1 were differentiated using bedside blanching assessment, with transient blanchable erythema assigned to RH and persistent non-blanchable erythema assigned to S1. DTPI was identified based on expert clinical assessment, including characteristic deep red, maroon, or purple discoloration, possible epidermal separation or blood-filled blister, palpation findings, clinical records, and available follow-up information. Two senior wound-care specialists independently reviewed all cases while blinded to model predictions and rPPG-derived features. Inter-rater reliability reached a Cohen’s κ of 0.77, indicating substantial agreement, and disagreements were resolved by a third senior wound-care consultant through consensus discussion.

Performance comparison with unimodal visual baselines

To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed multi-modal framework relative to standard morphological analysis, we benchmarked PGNet against a comprehensive suite of visual backbones—ranging from representative CNNs to Vision Transformers—under the identical Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV) protocol. As detailed in Table 2, the integration of hemodynamic features in PGNet resulted in an overall accuracy of 79.5% and a weighted F1-score of 80%. Compared with visual-only CNN and Transformer baselines, PGNet achieved the highest macro-F1 score of 79%, suggesting a more balanced performance across RH, S1, and DTPI. The model showed particularly strong DTPI performance, with a precision of 93.3%, recall of 82.4%, and F1-score of 87.5%. These findings provide patient-level evidence that rPPG-derived hemodynamic cues can complement surface morphology when differentiating clinically ambiguous early pressure injury states, while future multicenter validation will be needed to further evaluate generalizability.

Patient-level uncertainty and diagnostic metrics

We performed additional patient-level uncertainty analysis to avoid overinterpreting point estimates. All uncertainty analyses were conducted over patient-level leave-one-out predictions rather than over augmented windows, thereby preserving patient independence. Nonparametric bootstrap resampling was repeated 5,000 times. In each iteration, 44 patients were sampled with replacement, and accuracy, macro-F1, and weighted-F1 were recalculated. We also report raw-count confusion matrices and one-vs-rest diagnostic metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC).

As shown in Tables 3, 4 and 5, PGNet correctly classified 35 of 44 patients, corresponding to an observed accuracy of 79.55%. The observed macro-F1 and weighted-F1 scores were 78.95% and 79.79%, respectively. The bootstrap 95% confidence interval for accuracy was 68.13%–90.91%, quantifying the statistical uncertainty associated with the current patient-level cohort. In the one-vs-rest analysis, DTPI showed high specificity and PPV, reaching 96.30% and 93.33%, respectively, with an AUC of 0.993. These results suggest that the hemodynamic branch provide useful complementary information for identifying occult deep-tissue injury. The bootstrap confidence intervals further make the uncertainty of the current patient-level validation transparent. Together with the raw-count confusion matrix and one-vs-rest diagnostic metrics, these results provide clinically interpretable patient-level evidence for the feasibility and diagnostic potential of PGNet.

Failure-case analysis

To better characterize the limitations of the proposed framework, we further analyzed all nine misclassified patient-level LOOCV cases. For each failure case, we examined the true class, predicted class, predicted probabilities, and confidence margin. The confidence margin was defined as the difference between the highest and second-highest predicted probabilities. A smaller confidence margin indicates that the model considered two classes similarly probable, suggesting boundary ambiguity rather than a highly confident error.

As summarized in Table 6, the remaining misclassifications were mainly concentrated between clinically adjacent or morphologically ambiguous categories. Specifically, RH-to-S1 errors and DTPI-to-S1 errors each accounted for three of the nine failure cases, corresponding to 33.33% respectively. S1-to-RH errors occurred in two cases, accounting for 22.22%, whereas S1-to-DTPI error occurred in one case, accounting for 11.11%. The RH-to-S1 and S1-to-RH errors together accounted for five of the nine errors, reflecting the intrinsic difficulty of distinguishing transient reactive hyperemia from early non-blanchable erythema using short non-contact videos. In addition, the three DTPI-to-S1 errors suggest that deep-tissue injury may still be underestimated when the surface appearance is subtle or when the extracted hemodynamic signal does not show a sufficiently distinct damping pattern.

In terms of modality effectiveness, the experimental results demonstrate a distinct empirical advantage of multi-modal fusion over uni-modal baselines. Specifically, while the Visual Only model achieved an Accuracy of 61.4% and a Macro-F1 of 0.62 outperforming the Physio Only model (54.5% Accuracy) both uni-modal approaches failed to break through a distinct performance bottleneck. As observed in the Visual-Only matrix, the single-modality model exhibits significant ambiguity in distinguishing adjacent severity stages, specifically misclassifying 29% of DTPI samples as S1. The correct classification rate for the critical S1 class rose from 71% to 82%, while the recognition of occult DTPI similarly improved from 71% to 82%. Further quantitative analysis of the fusion mechanisms and training strategies indicates that the proposed attention-based fusion module and weighted loss strategy are pivotal factors in sustaining high model performance. Regarding the fusion method, compared to the naive feature concatenation baseline (w/o SE-Block) which achieved 70.5% Accuracy, the incorporation of the SE-Block attention module further improved accuracy from 70.5% to 79.5% and macro-F1 from 68.0% to 79.0%. This confirms the effectiveness of the attention mechanism in feature recalibration. Regarding training strategies, removing the physiological-guided weighted loss constraint (w/o Weighted) resulted in the most severe degradation, with Accuracy plummeting to 63.6%—only marginally surpassing the pure visual baseline. Similarly, removing the deep supervision mechanism (w/o DeepSup) led to a drop in Accuracy to 68.2%. Judging by the performance hierarchy of the variants, the weighted constraint on the physiological branch contributes most significantly to the overall performance, followed by deep supervision and the attention module.

Robustness evaluation under simulated visual and illumination perturbations

To further evaluate the sensitivity of the proposed framework under simulated image-space visual perturbations, we extended the original Gaussian-noise stress test by adding simulation-based illumination perturbations. Specifically, brightness decrease, brightness increase, contrast decrease, contrast increase, and gamma correction were applied to the test images. The rPPG-derived physiological features were kept unchanged, allowing us to isolate the influence of visual illumination variation on the image branch and the final multimodal decision.

As shown in Table 8, PGNet remained more stable than the visual branch under all simulated illumination perturbations. Under clean input, PGNet achieved an accuracy of 79.55% and a macro-F1 score of 78.95%, consistent with the main patient-level LOOCV result. Under brightness decrease, the PGNet performance remained unchanged, while brightness increase caused only a mild decrease. More challenging perturbations, such as contrast decrease and gamma correction, led to larger performance reductions, with the lowest PGNet accuracy observed under γ=0.7 correction. Nevertheless, even under this strongest perturbation, PGNet maintained an accuracy of 72.73%, whereas the visual branch decreased to 52.27%. These results suggest that the rPPG-derived physiological branch provides complementary information that partially compensates for illumination-related visual degradation.

Discussion

This study introduces a multimodal framework that augments surface texture analysis with non-contact rPPG-derived hemodynamic information for early pressure injury warning. By targeting the clinically challenging differentiation among RH, S1, and DTPI, the proposed framework demonstrates that local hemodynamic cues can provide complementary physiological information beyond texture-only assessment and help reduce ambiguity in morphologically similar cases. The patient-level leave-one-out validation results provide clinically meaningful feasibility evidence in a carefully annotated single-center cohort. Future multicenter, device-independent, and prospective studies will be important to further evaluate the generalizability and translational potential of this approach.

Physiological interpretability and hemodynamic basis

In Table 7, the impact of multi-modal fusion is most evident in the S1 Recall metric. Compared to the texture-centric Swin Transformer [60], the proposed framework exhibits an 11.0% increase in sensitivity for this early-stage category. This performance gap corroborates a core physiological premise: purely vision-based models face inherent difficulties in differentiating non-blanchable erythema from transient skin variations due to their restriction to surface-level features. Conversely, the integration of hemodynamic signatures enables the system to resolve this morphological ambiguity, effectively utilizing tissue perfusion data to clarify diagnostic boundaries that remain obscure in standard RGB imaging.

As detailed in Fig. 11, the RH and S1 groups exhibit a distinct surge in pulsatile amplitude and spectral energy, aligning with the active vasodilation characteristic of the early inflammatory cascade. This hyperdynamic perfusion signature, which is difficult to capture using static RGB cameras alone, empowers PGNet to accurately resolve the morphologically ambiguous early-stage lesions noted in Table 7. Conversely, for the occult DTPI, Table 7 shows that PGNet maintains robust classification performance despite the lack of surface breakage. The physiological basis for this capability is revealed in the time-domain analysis of Fig. 11: DTPI signals manifest a characteristic signal damping compared to the RH group. Crucially, while visible light penetration is physically confined to the superficial dermis, the hemodynamic consequences of DTPI are not. Pressure-induced tissue damage is often characterized by a deep-to-superficial progression, in which impaired perfusion, edema, and microvascular compression in deeper tissue layers can alter the vascular response observable at the skin surface. Although rPPG is limited by optical penetration depth and cannot directly measure subcutaneous viability, attenuation or blunting of the surface pulsatile waveform provides an indirect functional cue associated with local microcirculatory compromise. In this context, PGNet does not rely on rPPG as a direct depth-imaging signal; instead, it learns perfusion-related damping patterns that complement surface morphology and help distinguish visually similar RH, S1, and DTPI cases with different hemodynamic characteristics.

Hemodynamic signal analysis across pathological stages

Furthermore, the spectral analysis in Fig. 11 confirms the local specificity of these features. The Power Spectral Density of the lesion ROI is distinctly separated from that of adjacent normal skin, evolving dynamically with pathological severity. This differential signal effectively rules out systemic circulation confounders, proving that the Precision reported in Table 7 stems from locking onto local microcirculatory dysfunction rather than global heart rate artifacts.

Critical analysis of ablation and training strategies

To elucidate the mechanistic origins of the performance superiority achieved by PGNet, we synthesized the quantitative ablation results with qualitative Grad-CAM visualizations. As detailed in Table 7, removing the Physio-Visual Gating Unit caused the accuracy to decrease from 79.5% to 70.5%, while eliminating deep supervision further reduced the accuracy to 68.2%. These results indicate that both physiological feature-guided recalibration and branch-level supervision are important for maintaining diagnostic robustness.

The role of the Physio-Visual Gating Unit is further supported by the Grad-CAM visualization in Fig. 12. Compared with the visual baseline, which mainly localized the general lesion area and showed relatively diffuse activation, PGNet produced more concentrated responses around lesion-relevant regions. This suggests that the rPPG-derived physiological features helped recalibrate the visual representation toward local hemodynamic abnormalities rather than relying only on surface appearance. This effect is clinically meaningful because RH, S1, and DTPI can present with similar or subtle visual signs, while their local perfusion and pulsatile responses may differ. By incorporating relative perfusion and waveform-complexity features, the Physio-Visual Gating Unit provides a lightweight mechanism for guiding visual attention, which helps explain the more focused Grad-CAM activation and the improved classification performance of PGNet over the visual-only baseline.

Grad-CAM visualization comparison

Furthermore, visual features are typically easier to learn than weak physiological signals. Without auxiliary supervision, gradients may be dominated by the visual branch, leaving the physiological branch under-trained. Deep supervision enforces classification constraints on intermediate branches, encouraging the physiological features to acquire independent discriminative power and provide more reliable information to the gating unit. Therefore, the ablation results and Grad-CAM visualizations jointly support the clinical plausibility of PGNet’s design.

Clinical implementation challenges and mitigation strategies

Building on the patient-level feasibility evidence provided by EPI-Sense, several practical factors should be considered to support future clinical translation. First, motion artifacts remain a major concern because elderly, critically ill, or bedridden patients may have spontaneous movement, discomfort, or difficulty maintaining a stable posture during video acquisition. In addition, pressure-prone anatomical sites often require patient repositioning or turning, which increases the difficulty of stable video collection and reliable rPPG extraction. Future systems should therefore incorporate automated motion-quality assessment, real-time acquisition feedback, and re-acquisition prompts when signal quality is insufficient.

Second, skin-tone variation, device differences, workflow latency, and nursing usability should be evaluated prospectively. Because Fitzpatrick skin type was not prospectively recorded in the current pilot cohort, formal subgroup fairness validation was not possible. Future multicenter studies should include skin-tone-aware calibration, multi-device testing, and stratified subgroup analysis, especially for Fitzpatrick types IV–VI. Although model inference is lightweight, the complete bedside workflow also includes patient positioning, video acquisition, ROI checking, signal-quality assessment, and feature extraction. Therefore, future deployment should focus on standardized acquisition protocols, simplified user interfaces, automated quality-control alerts, and concise nurse training materials to reduce clinical workload.

Conclusion

This study presents a non-contact framework for early pressure injury warning by integrating customized cross-polarized video acquisition, weak rPPG signal enhancement, and physio-guided multimodal classification. By augmenting surface texture analysis with local hemodynamic sensing, the proposed framework provides preliminary evidence for reducing the observational ambiguity among RH, S1, and DTPI. These results support the feasibility of the proposed multimodal system and provide a foundation for future non-invasive geriatric care monitoring. It is worth emphasizing that constructing a patient-level clinical video dataset for early pressure injury assessment is operationally challenging, because patients are often elderly, bedridden, and require careful repositioning under ethical and nursing workflow constraints. Future multicenter and multi-device validation will further strengthen the evidence for clinical generalizability and support broader translation of the proposed framework.

Limitations and future scope

As a pilot feasibility study, this work has several limitations. First, the dataset was collected from a single center and included 44 independent patients. Although strict patient-level LOOCV and bootstrap confidence intervals were used to preserve patient independence and quantify uncertainty, no independent external validation cohort was available. Therefore, the reported performance should be interpreted as internal patient-level feasibility evidence rather than proof of clinical generalizability. Second, pathology or MRI confirmation was not routinely available for all RH/S1/DTPI cases, because these examinations are not standard bedside procedures for early pressure injury screening. The reference labels were therefore based on NPIAP/EPUAP criteria, bedside blanching assessment, expert consensus, clinical records, and available follow-up information. Third, Fitzpatrick skin type, anatomical site, and body mass index were not prospectively stratified, limiting formal subgroup and fairness evaluation. Fourth, all videos were acquired using the same customized cross-polarized imaging system, and robustness across different cameras, clinical centers, anatomical sites, and acquisition environments remains to be validated. Finally, the current ablation study mainly focused on modality-level and network-level components; more fine-grained evaluation of preprocessing modules, including weighted POS extraction, signal-quality weighting, lesion/reference normalization, and skin-tone-aware calibration, should be conducted in larger cohorts. Future work will focus on prospective multicenter recruitment, standardized acquisition protocols, device-independent validation, longitudinal outcome confirmation, subgroup-aware evaluation, and automated quality-control modules for motion and signal reliability.
