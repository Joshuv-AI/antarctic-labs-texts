Full-History Graphs with Edge-Type Decoupled Networks for Temporal Reasoning
Osama Mohammed, Jiaxin Pan, Mojtaba Nayyeri, Daniel Hernández, Steffen Staab

Abstract
Modeling evolving interactions among entities is critical in many real-world tasks. For example, predicting driver maneuvers in traffic requires tracking how neighbouring vehicles accelerate, brake, and change lanes relative to one another over consecutive frames. Similarly, detecting financial fraud hinges on following the flow of funds through successive transactions as they propagate across the network. Unlike classic time-series forecasting, these settings demand reasoning over who interacts with whom and when , calling for a temporal-graph representation that makes both the relations and their evolution explicit. Existing temporal-graph methods use snapshot graphs to represent temporal evolution. In this paper, we introduce a full-history graph that instantiates one node for every entity at every timestep and separates two edge sets: (i) intra-timestep edges that capture relations within a single frame, and (ii) inter-timestep edges that connect an entity to itself at consecutive steps. To learn on this graph we design an Edge-Type Decoupled Network (ETDNet) with parallel modules: a graph-attention module aggregates information along intra-timestep edges, a multi-head temporal-attention module attends over an entity’s inter-timestep history, and a fusion module combines the two messages after every layer. When evaluated on driver-intention prediction (Waymo) and Bitcoin fraud detection (Elliptic++), ETDNet consistently surpasses strong baselines, lifting Waymo joint accuracy to 75.6 % (vs. 74.1 %) and raising Elliptic++ illicit-class F1 to 88.1 % (vs. 60.4 %). These gains demonstrate the benefit of representing structural and temporal relations as distinct edges in a single graph.

Full history graphs may be applied directly to any domain where events unfold in ordered steps.
It preserves both fine-grained temporal ordering and structural context,
and it serves as the backbone for our novel dual-branch GNN introduced in
Section
3
.

Our model adopts a parallel message passing strategy. At each GNN layer, one round of message passing occurs over instantaneous relationships first, while at the same time, another round of message passing over history edges occurs. The first branch leverages a graph attention block to model intra-timestep relationships among nodes. While effective at capturing intra-timestep relationships, GNNs are prone to over-smoothing, especially in deeper layers. Therefore, the second branch integrates a self-attention-based block that processes inter-timestep edges and preserves feature diversity across time. By combining these branches, our model captures both immediate context and long-range dependencies.

We demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach on two challenging real-world applications: driver-intention forecasting and dynamic fraud detection in transaction networks. Our experimental results show that our model outperforms existing baselines by leveraging the full temporal history of the data.

Our main contributions are:

1.

A full-history graph representation with two edge sets: (i) intra-timestep edges connecting entities that interact within the same frame, and (ii) inter-timestep edges linking temporally successive events through self-links or cross-entity hand-offs.

2.

A dual-branch network consisting of (i) graph attention for intra-timestep edges and (ii) temporal self-attention for inter-timestep edges, thereby avoiding the depth limits and over-smoothing issues of GNNs.

3.

Extensive experiments across traffic and finance demonstrating superior accuracy, ablations confirming each design choice, and analyses of runtime & memory.

Temporal graph neural networks (TGNNs) adapt static GNNs to evolving relational data, enabling applications in recommendation, traffic forecasting, and blockchain analytics
[
Longa et al.(2023b)Longa, Scardapane, and Bacciu
]
. Existing methods can be grouped into three lines of research:
(i)
memory–based
models that combine a GNN with recurrent or external-memory updates,
(ii)
time-aware attention
models that extend Transformer or GAT layers with temporal encodings, and
(iii)
decoupled
architectures that process spatial and temporal edges in separate branches before fusing the messages.
The proposed ETDNet falls into the third group but differs by operating on a full-history graph and by assigning a dedicated attention mechanism to each edge family, yielding clearer inductive bias and lower memory than prior decoupled designs.

Snapshot GNN baselines.

Early works treat a dynamic graph as a sequence of static “snapshots”
and run a conventional GNN at every step, optionally sharing weights.
Representative baselines include GCN
[
Kipf and Welling(2017)
]
,
GraphSAGE
[
Hamilton et al.(2017)Hamilton, Ying, and Leskovec
]
, and the spatio-temporal
extension ST-GCN
[
Yu et al.(2018)Yu, Yin, and Zhu
]
.
While effective on short horizons, snapshot models conflate spatial and
temporal signals inside the same message‐passing layer and must stack
many layers to reach distant timesteps, which amplifies
over-smoothing.
Our experiments (Section
4
) confirm that performance drops
once trajectories exceed two–three hops, motivating an explicit
temporal branch.

Memory–bank temporal GNNs.

To avoid recomputing snapshots, memory-based TGNNs maintain a per-node
state that is updated whenever an event arrives. TGN stores incoming
messages in a mailbox and applies a GRU update before each
prediction
[
Rossi et al.(2020)Rossi, Chamberlain, Frasca, Eynard, Monti, and Bronstein
]
; JoDIE augments this idea with coupled
recurrent units for users and items
[
Kumar et al.(2019)Kumar, Zhang, and Leskovec
]
.
Memory banks capture long histories but require
O(|V|H)
storage
(nodes
\times
window) and mix spatial and temporal information in the
same recurrent cell. ETDNet discards the global mailbox and instead
attends only over the
local
window of
\mathcal{H}
predecessors,
cutting memory while still preserving long temporal context.

Continuous-time attention kernels.

A second line of work replaces discrete updates with continuous-time
encodings. TGAT introduces sinusoidal time
features
[
Xu and et al.(2020)
]
; DyRep uses Hawkes processes to model event
intensity
[
Trivedi et al.(2019)Trivedi, Farajtabar, Biswal, and Zha
]
; DyGFormer couples these ideas with a
global transformer and relative-time biases
[
Huang et al.(2023)Huang, Poursafaei, Danovitch, Fey, Hu, Rossi, Leskovec, Bronstein, Rabusseau, and Rabbany
]
.
Such kernels improve temporal fidelity but still merge spatial and
temporal neighbours inside a single attention matrix, leaving
edge-type–specific inductive bias unexplored. ETDNet keeps the
continuous-time benefits (windowed self-attention) yet processes
domain
D
and temporal
\mathcal{H}
edges in
distinct

modules, allowing each branch to specialise.

Spatial–temporal separation.

Recent work has begun to decouple the two channels.
STGNN applies separate convolutions to static and dynamic
adjacency
[
Shao et al.(2022)Shao, Zhang, Wei, Wang, Xu, Cao, and Jensen
]
; TG-NeXt factorises message passing by
edge timestamp and direction
[
Park et al.(2024)Park, Lee, and Yun
]
;
SemanticFormer stacks two semantic “heads” for actor and map graphs in
trajectory forecasting
[
Sun et al.(2024)Sun, Feng, Guo, Li, and Theodorou
]
.
These models validate the benefit of separation but either rely on
hand-crafted channels (STGNN, SemanticFormer) or keep a snapshot
backbone (TG-NeXt).
Our contribution complements them with (i) a
full-history
graph
representation that makes temporal causality explicit and
(ii) a dual-attention layer (SA + HA) that assigns dedicated parameters
to each edge family while remaining lightweight.

In summary, prior TGNNs leave three gaps.
(i)
Snapshot
stacks conflate space–time signals and over-smooth after a few hops
[
Yu et al.(2018)Yu, Yin, and Zhu
,
Longa et al.(2023a)Longa, Lachi, Santin, Bianchini, Lepri, Lio, Scarselli, and Passerini
,
Chen and et al.(2023)
]
.
(ii)
Memory–bank
or continuous-time models retain history but still mix edge types and incur
O(|V|B)
storage
[
Rossi et al.(2020)Rossi, Chamberlain, Frasca, Eynard, Monti, and Bronstein
,
Xu and et al.(2020)
,
Trivedi et al.(2019)Trivedi, Farajtabar, Biswal, and Zha
,
Huang et al.(2023)Huang, Poursafaei, Danovitch, Fey, Hu, Rossi, Leskovec, Bronstein, Rabusseau, and Rabbany
]
.
(iii) Recent “decoupled” networks use handcrafted channels or snapshot backbones
[
Shao et al.(2022)Shao, Zhang, Wei, Wang, Xu, Cao, and Jensen
,
Park et al.(2024)Park, Lee, and Yun
,
Sun et al.(2024)Sun, Feng, Guo, Li, and Theodorou
]
.
Our ETDNet fills these gaps with a
full-history
graph that keeps causal order explicit and a lightweight
dual-attention
layer that treats intra- and inter-timestep edges separately, yielding lower memory and stronger long-range reasoning.

3
Methodology

We propose a full-history graph learning framework that encapsulates time-evolving entities and their interactions in a unified structure, followed by learning from such structure. Section
3.1
formally defines the graph representation. Section
3.2
describes our dual-branch neural architecture, which processes intra-timestep and inter-timestep information in parallel and fuses them for downstream classification. Section
3.3
details the training algorithm.

3.1
Full-History Graph Representation

Let
T=\{0,1,\dots,\tau_{\max}\}
denote a discrete set of timesteps.
Entities are separated into

dynamic
ones (e.g., vehicles), denoted by
U
, and
static
ones (e.g., stop signs), denoted by
S
.
For each dynamic entity
u\in U
and each timestep
t\in T_{u}\subseteq T
at which
u
exists,
we create a node
u^{t}
with a feature vector
\mathbf{x}_{u,t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Every static entity
s\in S
is represented by a single node
s
whose feature
\mathbf{x}_{s}
is shared across all timesteps.
The complete node set is

\widetilde{V}\;=\;\underbrace{\{\,u^{t}\mid u\in U,\;t\in T_{u}\,\}}_{\text{dynamic nodes}}\;\cup\;\underbrace{S}_{\text{static nodes}}.

Edge types.

There are two types of time-related interactions between nodes in the graph, which are formalized as follows:

(i)

Intra-timestep edges

D\subseteq\{(v^{t},w^{t})\mid t\in T\}

link entities that interact
within
the same timestep. Several relation labels may co-exist in a heterogeneous graph.

(ii)

Inter-timestep edges

\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{(v^{t},w^{t+1})\mid t<\tau_{\max}\}

connect
temporally successive events
.
The most common case is the self-edge
(u^{t},u^{t+1})
for the same entity, but
\mathcal{H}
can also include cross-entity links
(p^{t},\,q^{\,t+1})
, such as when a transaction at
t
immediately initiates a related one at
t{+}1
.

Additionally, each dynamic (i.e., the per-timestep replica of an entity) node can interact with static nodes in (i), and there is also interaction between the static nodes themselves.
The full-history graph is

G=(\widetilde{V},\,E),\qquad E=D\cup\mathcal{H}.

This construction
(i) retains the complete temporal record without sliding windows;
(ii) lets the model process
D
and
\mathcal{H}
with separate, edge-type-specific modules (Section
3.2
); and
(iii) accommodates both static entities and cross-entity temporal flows, making the template transferable from, e.g., traffic scenes to transaction networks or any domain where events can be unfolded in discrete order. Because every
\mathcal{H}
edge points from
t
to
t{+}1
, any path that follows only these edges is acyclic, whereas
D
edges remain inside a single timestep—including static–dynamic contacts—and may form cycles. Retaining two distinct time-related edge families, therefore, keeps temporal ordering explicit while allowing the subsequent architecture to assign different update rules to simultaneous versus cross-time interactions.

Figure
1
illustrates a three-timestep traffic scenario:
coloured solid edges show intra-timestep interactions, while dashed red
edges mark the inter-timestep
next
links.
The same full-history construction applies to other dynamic domains, such as financial-transaction graphs.

3.2
Edge-Type Decoupled Architecture

After we construct the full-history graph, we use our proposed network, Edge-Type Decoupled Network (ETDNet), to learn on the graph. ETDNet takes as input the full-history graph and stacks
L
identical layers. Within each layer, we (i) gather information from nodes that share the current timestep (via self-attention module),
(ii) integrate the recent history of each dynamic entity (via history attention module), and
(iii) combine those two messages with the node’s previous embedding (via fusion layer).
After the final layer, we feed the resulting embeddings to a task-specific prediction head.

ETDNet executes these steps through a stack of modular blocks, each responsible for one edge family or for merging their outputs. Figure
3
shows the overall proposed architecture for ETDNet.

Block overview.

Each layer contains three blocks:

1.

Step Attention (SA)
— A stack of
K_{s}
sublayers, each containing
H_{s}
attention heads, applied over
D

2.

History Attention (HA)
— A self-attention head with
H_{t}
attention heads over the past
B
steps of
\mathcal{H}
;

3.

Fusion Layer (FL)
— a residual MLP that merges the current embedding with the two messages.

K_{s}
is the number of stacked SA sublayers,

H_{s}
and
H_{t}
are the numbers of attention heads in SA and HA, respectively,
and
B
is the fixed history-window length.

Unlike many snapshot GNNs, HA therefore sees a
set
of time-ordered predecessors, not just the node’s own past, and degenerates to a simple self-chain when
\mathcal{H}
contains only

(u^{t-1},u^{t})
edges.

Below we denote the embedding of node
x
at layer
l
by

h^{(l)}_{x}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
h^{(0)}_{x}=\mathbf{x}_{x}
).
For dynamic entities, we write
u^{t}
; static entities carry no time
superscript but participate in the same computation.

1. Step Attention (SA)

SA aggregates information over
intra-timestep
edges
D
.
For each node
u^{t}
we set

\mathcal{N}_{D}(u^{t})=\{\,v^{t}\mid(v^{t},u^{t})\!\in\!D\}
.
Inside SA we stack
K_{s}
identical sublayers; every sublayer
uses
H_{s}
attention heads.

With head index
r
and sublayer index
k
we first compute attention scores

e_{uv}^{(r,k)}=\mathrm{LeakyReLU}\!\Bigl(\mathbf{a}_{r,k}^{\!\top}\bigl[\,\mathbf{W}_{Q,r,k}\,h^{(l,k-1)}_{u^{t}}\;\|\;\mathbf{W}_{K,r,k}\,h^{(l,k-1)}_{v^{t}}\,\bigr]\Bigr),

(1)

where

\mathbf{W}_{Q,r,k},\mathbf{W}_{K,r,k}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}\times d}

project queries and keys to the per-head width

d^{\prime}=d/H_{s}
with
d
as dimension size, and

\mathbf{a}_{r,k}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{2d^{\prime}}
is the learnable
scoring vector.
We then normalize the attention score, as

\alpha_{uv}^{(r,k)}=\frac{\exp(e_{uv}^{(r,k)})}{\sum_{w^{t}\in\mathcal{N}_{D}(u^{t})}\exp(e_{uw}^{(r,k)})},

(2)

thus,
\alpha_{uv}^{(r,k)}
is the attention weight of neighbour

v^{t}
with respect to
u^{t}
for head
r
.
We then perform neighborhood aggregation by

m_{u^{t}}^{(r,k)}=\sum_{v^{t}\in\mathcal{N}_{D}(u^{t})}\alpha_{uv}^{(r,k)}\;\mathbf{W}_{V,r,k}\,h^{(l,k-1)}_{v^{t}},

(3)

where

\mathbf{W}_{V,r,k}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}\times d}
maps values to the head space.

After that, we compute the residual update.
All
H_{s}
head messages are concatenated, projected back to
d
,
added to the residual, and normalised:

h^{(l,k)}_{u^{t}}=\mathrm{LayerNorm}\!\Bigl(\mathbf{O}_{k}\,[m_{u^{t}}^{(1,k)}\|\dots\|m_{u^{t}}^{(H_{s},k)}]+h^{(l,k-1)}_{u^{t}}\Bigr),

(4)

with shared output projection

\mathbf{O}_{k}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.

Stacking the
K_{s}
SA sublayers gives a
K_{s}
-hop receptive field restricted
to the current timestep while keeping the network shallow. The final intra-timestep message is

m^{D}_{u^{t}}:=h^{(l,K_{s})}_{u^{t}}
, which is passed to the Fusion
Layer.

2. History Attention (HA)

HA aggregates information that arrives
through the inter-timestep
edge set

\mathcal{H}
. For the current node
u^{t}
we collect up to

B
predecessors reachable along
\mathcal{H}
:

\mathcal{P}_{B}(u^{t})=\Bigl\{\,w^{\tau}\;\Big|\;w^{\tau}\!\in\!\operatorname{Hop}_{\leq B}(\mathcal{H},u^{t}),\;\tau<t\Bigr\},

where
\operatorname{Hop}_{\leq B}(\mathcal{H},u^{t})
is the set of nodes
that can reach
u^{t}
via a directed path of length
\leq B
in

\mathcal{H}
. In a driver-intention graph this set contains exactly

u^{t-1}
(self-edge); in a transaction graph it may contain several
transfers that “fan in” to the current one. We order

\mathcal{P}_{B}(u^{t})
by time, obtain
m=\lvert\mathcal{P}_{B}(u^{t})\rvert\leq B
,
and zero-pad to length
B
:

Z_{u,t}=\bigl[h^{(l)}_{w^{\tau_{1}}}\;\|\;\dots\;\|\;h^{(l)}_{w^{\tau_{m}}}\bigr]\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times d}.

Using head index
r\in\{1,\dots,H_{t}\}
we compute the query–key–value
projections

Q_{r}=Z_{u,t}\mathbf{W}_{Q,r}^{\top},\quad K_{r}=Z_{u,t}\mathbf{W}_{K,r}^{\top},\quad V_{r}=Z_{u,t}\mathbf{W}_{V,r}^{\top},

(5)

where

\mathbf{W}_{Q,r},\mathbf{W}_{K,r},\mathbf{W}_{V,r}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}\times d}

are shared, learnable matrices and
d^{\prime\prime}=d/H_{t}
.

We then compute the scaled dot-product attention

A_{r}=\operatorname{Softmax}\!\bigl(Q_{r}K_{r}^{\!\top}/\sqrt{d^{\prime\prime}}\bigr),\qquad O_{r}=A_{r}V_{r},

(6)

where
A_{r}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{B\times B}
and

O_{r}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{B\times d^{\prime\prime}}
.

Finally, we take the most-recent row
O_{r}[m-1,:]
from each attention
head, concatenate them, project back to dimension
d
, and apply layer
normalisation:

m^{\mathcal{H}}_{u^{t}}=\operatorname{LayerNorm}\!\Bigl(\Bigl[\!\bigl\|_{\,r=1}^{H_{t}}\!O_{r}[m-1,:]\Bigr]\,\mathbf{O}_{\tau}\Bigr),

(7)

where each
O_{r}[m-1,:]\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}
is the current-time
context from head
r
and

\mathbf{O}_{\tau}\in\mathbb{R}^{H_{t}d^{\prime\prime}\times d}
is a shared,
learnable output projection.

Attention over the full window lets each entity reference distant past
states in a single step while avoiding the oversmoothing that can arise
when many GNN layers are stacked.

3. Fusion Layer (FL)

Finally, we update the embedding:

h^{(l+1)}_{u^{t}}=\mathrm{LayerNorm}\!\bigl(h^{(l)}_{u^{t}}+\mathrm{ReLU}\!\bigl(\mathbf{F}\,[\,h^{(l)}_{u^{t}}\|m^{D}_{u^{t}}\|m^{\mathcal{H}}_{u^{t}}]\bigr)\bigr),

(8)

q
where
h^{(l)}_{u^{t}}
is the input from the previous ETD layer,

m^{D}_{u^{t}}
and
m^{\mathcal{H}}_{u^{t}}
are the domain- and
history-messages produced by SA and HA, respectively, and

\mathbf{F}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{d\times 3d}
is the shared, learnable
fusion projection.

By decoupling
D
and
\mathcal{H}
we keep the two message types

Snapshot GNNs such as ST-GCN
[
Yu et al.(2018)Yu, Yin, and Zhu
]
or GTEA

[
Xie et al.(2023a)Xie, Li, Tam, Liu, Ying, Lau, Chiu, and Chen

]
) merge all interactions in a single module,
which can lead to oversmoothing once we stack several layers.
Our design limits the depth to
L{=}3
,
retains a
K_{s}
-hop intra-timestep and a

-step inter-timestep receptive

(see Section

).

Unless stated otherwise, we use

,

,

,

, and

.

Design rationale.

Separating intra- and inter-timestep edges into two dedicated modules addresses the fact that spatial interactions change differently from cross-time continuity.
A single aggregator would have to compromise between these two update rates, whereas the dual design lets the Step-Attention branch specialise on dense, frame-local context and the History-Attention branch focus on long-range temporal cues.
The residual fusion gate then allows each node to weigh the two messages adaptively, adding only 0.2% to the parameter count while preserving the representational benefits of both views.

Discussion.

ETDNet performs consistently across two very different time grids: 10 Hz traffic frames and monthly blockchain snapshots, showing that the architecture is largely agnostic to temporal resolution and depends only on the temporal order. Accuracy rises until the history window covers about 4 s of motion (Waymo) or 4 months of transactions (Elliptic
++
) and then falls as older, low-weight nodes dilute attention (Tables
6
–
7
); choosing
B
by real-time span rather than raw frame count therefore keeps the window compact even at finer sampling rates.

The results highlight two takeaways: (i) explicit edge-type decoupling is essential as mixing all edges in a single kernel drops performance on both tasks; (ii) History Attention is the decisive component whenever long-range evidence is needed, while the domain branch suffices for purely local cues.

Step Attention currently computes dense, multi-head attention over

5
Conclusion

We introduced
ETDNet
, a dual–branch temporal GNN that keeps

domain
and
temporal
interactions disentangled within a
full-history graph. Across two markedly different benchmarks,
ETDNet delivered consistent gains over recent temporal baselines while
using fewer parameters and shorter training times.

Key findings.

(1) On
Waymo
, explicit separation of same-frame interactions
from cross-frame motion yielded the highest joint accuracy to date on
simultaneous speed–direction prediction, with the ablation study
confirming the distinct value of each branch.
(2) On
Elliptic++
, the same architecture raised illicit–F1 to
88 %—a double-digit improvement over the best memory-bank and
evolving-weight models—demonstrating strong transfer to a
structurally dissimilar, highly imbalanced setting.
(3) Sensitivity analyses showed that performance is stable w.r.t. the
number of layers, attention heads, and history-window length, making the
model easy to tune.

Outlook.

ETDNet already scales linearly with the number of domain and temporal
edges, but further speed-ups are possible by adopting sparse attention
kernels inside the intra-timestep branch and by learning adaptive
history windows. Extending the framework to irregular or
continuous-time events—by replacing the fixed discrete grid with
time-aware positional encodings—forms another promising path. We
believe that the principle of
edge-type decoupling
will remain
useful as temporal graphs grow in size and heterogeneity, and we hope
ETDNet provides a solid stepping-stone for future work.
