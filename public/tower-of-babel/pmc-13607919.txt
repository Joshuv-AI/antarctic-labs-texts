Clinical characteristics of postoperative anterior arch fracture of the atlas following C1 laminectomy: 14-year experience of a single neurosurgical center
Authors: Yushi Nagano, Hitoshi Yamahata, Masanori Yonenaga, Jun Sugata, Ryutaro Makino, Nayuta Higa, Shingo Fujio, Tomoko Hanada, Ryosuke Hanaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective:Postoperative anterior arch fracture of the atlas (PAFA) is a rare but increasingly recognized complication of C1 laminectomy. However, the pathophysiology remains poorly understood. We retrospectively investigated the incidence of PAFA and associated risk factors.Methods:We retrospectively reviewed the records of patients who underwent C1 laminectomy at our institution between January 2010 and December 2023. The clinical records and radiological findings were analyzed. Atlantodental interval (ADI) and retro-odontoid distance (ROD) were measured using preoperative sagittal reconstructed computed tomography or T1-weighted magnetic resonance imaging.Results:In total, 51 patients were included in this study. The indications for surgery included posterior fossa lesions, Chiari malformation type I, cervical canal stenosis at C1, and spinal tumors. During follow-up (mean, 69 ± 34 months), PAFA developed in three (5.9%) patients. All patients underwent decompression surgery for cervical canal stenosis at C1. The mean interval between surgery and fracture detection was 11 ± 5 months. Two patients presented with neck pain, which improved with conservative treatment. One patient developed a vertebral artery occlusion and cerebral infarction associated with lateral mass displacement. Patients with PAFA were significantly older (P = 0.04) and had a significantly greater ROD (8.4 ± 3.1 vs. 3.2 ± 2.4 mm; P = 0.01) than those without; ADI did not differ significantly between groups.Conclusions:PAFA is an uncommon but important complication of C1 laminectomy, particularly in elderly patients with cervical canal stenosis at the craniocervical junction. An increased ROD may radiologically predict fracture risk. PAFA can occasionally lead to serious vascular complications; thus, careful postoperative surveillance is recommended, particularly during the first postoperative year.

INTRODUCTION

The atlas (C1) is a ring-shaped vertebra located immediately inferior to the occipital condyles and functions as a structural support for the craniocervical junction (CCJ).[12] Similar to the Titan Atlas in Greek mythology, it supports weight transmission from the skull to the cervical spine.[3] The atlas consists of bilateral lateral masses connected by the anterior and posterior arches. These lateral masses transmit axial loading from the cranial cavity to the superior articular facets of the axis (C2).[3] Along with the axis and surrounding ligamentous structures, the atlas plays a crucial role in craniocervical stability and cervical motion, including flexion, extension, and axial rotation.[12]

C1 laminectomy is commonly performed for several neurosurgical purposes, including enlargement of the operative field during posterior fossa or spinal surgery, decompression of cervical spinal canal stenosis at the CCJ, and foramen magnum decompression in patients with Chiari malformation. In most cases, posterior arch reconstruction is not performed after laminectomy. C1 laminectomy is widely accepted as a safe and effective procedure, and its usefulness has been well documented.[4567] However, several authors have reported postoperative anterior arch fracture of the atlas (PAFA) following C1 laminectomy.[8910111213141516]

Unlike traumatic atlas fractures caused by motor vehicle accidents or falls, PAFA may occur during ordinary daily activities and has been described in various clinical settings, including cervical canal stenosis, Chiari malformation, spinal tumors, and posterior fossa lesions.[8910111213141516] Despite increasing recognition of this complication, its incidence and associated risk factors remain poorly understood.

In the present study, we investigated the incidence of PAFA after C1 laminectomy at our institution over 14 years. We also evaluated the clinical and radiological characteristics associated with PAFA to identify the factors predictive of postoperative C1 fracture.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This retrospective study was approved by our Institutional Ethics Committee and the requirement for written informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective and noninvasive nature of the study. Between January 2010 and December 2023, 5652 patients underwent surgery at our hospital; of these, 83 underwent C1 laminectomy. Patients with congenital bone anomalies, tumors involving the CCJ structures, concomitant screw fixation, or follow-up periods of <2 years were excluded.

Operative records, inpatient charts, and outpatient follow-up records were retrospectively reviewed. The following data were collected: preoperative diagnosis, presence of PAFA, surgical procedures, and patient demographics. PAFA was diagnosed using postoperative computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). In patients who developed PAFA, the interval between the surgery and radiological diagnosis was recorded.

To evaluate the radiological risk factors for PAFA, we measured the atlantodental interval (ADI) and retro-odontoid distance (ROD) using preoperative sagittal reconstructed CT or T1-weighted MRI. The ADI was defined as the distance between the posterior aspect of the anterior arch of the atlas and the anterior surface of the odontoid process [Figure 1a]. The ROD was defined as the distance between the posterior surface of the odontoid process and the corresponding anterior margin of the dura mater and was measured as previously described [Figure 1b].[1718] All measurements were obtained using the same PACS (Synapse PACS 4.1.3; Fujifilm Medical Systems, Tokyo, Japan).

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were performed using the Chi-square test and Mann–Whitney U-test, as appropriate. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 24.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

RESULTS

The final study cohort comprised 51 patients (21 men and 30 women), with a mean age of 38.6 ± 28 (range, 1–87) years. Indications for C1 laminectomy included enlargement of the operative field for posterior fossa tumors (n = 22), cerebrovascular diseases (n = 2), foramen magnum decompression for Chiari malformation type I (n = 17), decompression for cervical canal stenosis at the C1 level (n = 7), and spinal tumor removal (n = 3). DISCUSSION

This study investigated the incidence and clinical characteristics of PAFA following C1 laminectomy over a 14-year period at a single neurosurgical center. Among the 51 patients, three cases (5.9%) of PAFA were identified. All fractures occurred in elderly patients who underwent decompression for cervical canal stenosis at the C1 level. An increased ROD was significantly associated with the occurrence of PAFA. To date, this is the longest follow-up study to examine the pathophysiology of PAFA after C1 laminectomy at a single neurosurgical center, with a variety of operative indications.

Incidence and clinical features of PAFA

Although C1 laminectomy is widely performed, PAFA remains an underrecognized postoperative complication.[8910111213141516] In the present study, three (5.9%, 3/51) cases of PAFA were encountered after C1 laminectomy over a mean follow-up of 64 ± 34 months, while Shimizu et al. reported a higher incidence in patients with spinal diseases (14.2%).[9] The lower incidence observed in the present study may reflect differences in the patient populations, as our cohort included many younger patients with intracranial pathologies. Although the incidence differed between the two studies, fractures were reported after C1 laminectomy.

Table 5 summarizes previous reports of PAFA, including 18 cases. Most previously reported cases of PAFA developed within the first postoperative year, and neck pain was the most commonly reported symptom. The causative factor was not remarkable in 13 cases; however, minor trauma or usual behavior was associated with onset in four cases. Similarly, fractures in our series were detected at a mean of 11 months after surgery. Two patients experienced neck pain and did not recall any excessive movement at symptom onset. Although the pathophysiology of PAFA is not fully understood, close attention should be paid to the patient’s symptoms, including new-onset neck pain, especially at 1–2 years postoperatively.

Mechanism of PAFA

Traumatic atlas fractures are typically caused by axial loading during high-energy trauma.[20] By contrast, PAFA appears to result from chronic biomechanical stress after removal of the posterior arch. Finite element analyses have demonstrated that C1 laminectomy substantially increases stress concentration in the anterior arch.[10] As the posterior arch removal width increases, the mechanical load on the anterior arch also increases;[21] additionally, the fracture site corresponded to the load concentration point. These findings suggest that disruption of the ring structure of the atlas alters load distribution, rendering the anterior arch vulnerable, even during ordinary daily activities.

Risk factors associated with PAFA

While PAFA occurred due to the load concentrated on the anterior arch, all patients did not suffer from PAFA after C1 laminectomy, indicating that the presence of other factors increased fracture occurrence. All patients with PAFA in our study had cervical canal stenosis at the C1 level. Previous reports similarly identified spinal degenerative diseases including cervical spondylotic myelopathy, ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL), atlantoaxial instability, and retro-odontoid pseudotumors as common underlying conditions [Table 5].

The significantly larger ROD observed in patients with PAFA may reflect the chronic degeneration or instability of the CCJ. Enlargement of the retro-odontoid space is associated with conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis, transverse ligament degeneration, and atlantoaxial instability.[18] The pathophysiology of each anatomical structure indicates their potential vulnerability compared with the normal anatomy. Therefore, an increased ROD may serve as a useful radiological marker for identifying patients at a risk of developing PAFA.

Cervical spondylotic myelopathy and OPLL are associated with PAFA owing to the decreased range of cervical motion. Previous studies demonstrated that reduced subaxial motion increases stress concentration in the atlas, thereby promoting fracture development.[9] We did not evaluate cervical range of motion or degenerative changes in all participants because patients with intracranial diseases usually do not undergo cervical evaluation. In cases of C1 laminectomy, cervical dynamics evaluation should be considered preoperatively.

Anatomical characteristics are also associated with PAFA onset. Gebauer et al. described the effect of axial force applied to the superior facet of the atlas from the top and observed that the facet joint expanded laterally.[22] Shimizu et al. focused on this lateral expansion and measured the C1 inferior facet angle – the angle between the tangent line of the inferior articular process and the parallel line of the inferior endplate of C2 – on CT coronal images, assuming that the steep angle may accelerate the lateral mass slippage.[9] They compared the parameters with and without PAFA and found that a large inferior facet angle of the atlas was a risk factor for PAFA onset. Evaluation of the facet angle of the atlas is considered important for the preoperative prevention of PAFA.

According to previous studies, the average age of patients with PAFA is 54 ± 22 (4–86) years, and most are male (14/18 cases). The average age and sex distributions in the present study were similar to those in previous studies. Old age and male sex were associated with cervical degenerative changes.[23] Degenerative changes in the spine can cause cervical spondylosis, which leads to cervical spondylotic myelopathy. The prevalence of OPLL is higher in males than in females.[24] These studies indicate that male sex and aging are associated with PAFA through subaxial ankylosis due to decreased cervical motion.

Evaluation of the bone fragility of the atlas is also an important factor.[914] Ishihama et al. described a case of atlantoaxial subluxation with osteopetrosis and reported that PAFA occurred only 1 week after C1 laminectomy.[14] Osteopetrosis is a rare disorder characterized by osteoclast dysfunction and impaired bone resorption, leading to bone fragility and an increased risk of fractures.[25] Patient 3 was on dialysis and suffered PAFA 6 months after operation. Dialysis is associated with a risk of fracture compared with the general population.[26] Patient-related factors may also contribute to bone fragility and susceptibility to fractures; therefore, careful preoperative assessment of bone quality is important when planning C1 decompression surgery.

Management of PAFA

An optimal treatment for PAFA has not yet been established; nevertheless, conservative treatment was successful in most reported cases, including the two patients in our series. Surgical stabilization may be necessary in patients with persistent pain, progressive instability, or neurological deterioration.

One patient in our study developed a vertebral artery occlusion associated with lateral mass displacement, emphasizing that PAFA may cause mechanical symptoms, as well as alter vertebral artery hemodynamics, potentially resulting in ischemic stroke.

Potential role of C1 reconstruction

Since disruption of the atlas ring appears to be central to the pathogenesis of PAFA, reconstruction of the posterior arch may help prevent fractures. Several laminoplasty techniques have been proposed using plates, screws, rods, hydroxyapatite spacers, or autologous bone grafts.[272829303132] Although short-term outcomes have been favorable, evidence regarding the long-term prevention of PAFA remains insufficient. Further biomechanical and clinical studies are required to determine whether reconstruction should be routinely considered after C1 laminectomies.

Limitations

This study has several limitations; first, its retrospective design may have resulted in an underestimation of the PAFA incidence because follow-up imaging protocols were not standardized. Second, this was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size. Larger multicenter studies are therefore necessary to further clarify the pathophysiology and risk factors of PAFA after C1 laminectomy.

CONCLUSIONS

PAFA is an uncommon but clinically important complication of C1 laminectomies. In the present series, PAFA occurred predominantly in elderly patients undergoing decompression for cervical canal stenosis and was associated with an increased ROD, with most fractures developing within the first postoperative year.

Although many cases can be managed conservatively, PAFA may occasionally result in serious vascular complications, including vertebral artery occlusion and cerebral infarction. Therefore, careful postoperative surveillance is warranted, particularly in patients with degenerative craniocervical pathology and radiological evidence of retro-odontoid enlargement. Preoperative assessment of craniocervical biomechanics and consideration of reconstruction techniques may help reduce the risk of PAFA after C1 laminectomy.
