Characteristics and prognostic factors of cognitive impairment in autoimmune glial fibrillary acidic protein astrocytopathy
Authors: Yasuko Mori, Akira Takekoshi, Yoya Ono, Nobuaki Yoshikura, Takayoshi Shimohata, Akio Kimura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAutoimmune glial fibrillary acidic protein astrocytopathy (GFAP-A) is a condition that can occasionally cause cognitive impairment (CI). This study aimed to clarify the characteristics of CI in patients with GFAP-A expression and its associated prognostic factors.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included patients diagnosed with GFAP-A at Gifu University, Japan. The prevalence and types of CI and the course of their development was investigated. Furthermore, the odds ratio (OR) of the associated factors of concomitant CI and prognostic factors of persistent CI were investigated using binary logistic regression.ResultsCI was observed in 120 of the 291 patients (41.2%). The types of CI varied, and the initial symptoms were rarely CI. Factors associated with the development of CI were age (OR 1.03, 95% CI 1.01–1.05, p=0.02) and lymphocyte count in cerebrospinal fluid (OR 0.99, 95% CI 0.98–0.999, p<0.01). 37 of 198 patients (18.6%) had cognitive impairment at the last follow-up (≥ 6 months). Factors associated with CI at the final follow-up were age (OR 1.07, 95% CI 1.03–1.11, p<0.01) and the interval from onset to treatment initiation (OR 1.01, 95% CI 1.002–1.01, p=0.013). The modified Rankin Scale score at 6 months after treatment (OR 2.102, 95% CI 1.404–3.380, p<0.01) was associated with the transition of CI to sequelae.Conclusion41.2% of patients with GFAP-A developed CI, with 18.6% having persistent deficits at the last follow-up. Older age and delayed treatment were risk factors, and the affected patients showed less acute inflammation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00415-026-14083-7.Keywords: Autoimmune GFAP astrocytopathy, Cognitive impairment, Sequelae of cognitive impairment, Age, The duration between the onset and the treatment

Introduction

Autoimmune glial fibrillary acidic protein astrocytopathy (GFAP-A) is an autoimmune meningoencephalitis/meningoencephalomyelitis characterized by GFAPα antibodies in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) [1]. While the underlying mechanism of GFAP-A remains unclear, histopathology of the brain from patients with GFAP-A showed that MHC class I-positive astrocytes were infiltrated by CD8-positive cytotoxic T cells containing granzymes and perforin [2]. Clinically, GFAP-A develops at any age and presents with a variety of clinical features [3], and it was reported that 30–41% of patients with GFAP-A complain of cognitive impairment [3, 4]. GFAP-A is a potentially curative disease requiring appropriate immunosuppressive therapy [1]. Therefore, it is important to recognize GFAP-A as a potential cause of cognitive impairment and a treatable form of dementia. Regarding the types of cognitive impairment, one study reported a decline in language, attention, and memory function [5], and another study reported a case series with apathy [6]. The mode of onset of cognitive impairment varies widely, ranging from acute development [7–9] to chronic progression, which takes several months to diagnose [5, 10–14]. Focusing on the prognosis, cognitive impairment was reported to be in 28.1% of patients with sequelae of GFAP-A [15]. However, many reports indicate that cognitive impairment could completely resolve, although it took a long time for some to resolve [8, 11, 14, 16–19].

To date, many reports have described patients with GFAP-A complaining of cognitive impairment; however, few reports have systematically investigated the characteristics and associated/prognostic factors of cognitive impairment in a large GFAP-A cohort. We investigated the characteristics of patients with concomitant cognitive impairment at disease onset, the factors associated with concomitant cognitive impairment, and the factors predictive of persistent or residual cognitive impairment during follow-up.

Methods

Patients and inclusion criteria

This research studied a total of 1576 patients with suspected inflammatory central nervous system (CNS) diseases who were referred to Gifu University for GFAPα antibody assay from January 1, 2019, to January 31, 2025. A flowchart of the patient selection process is shown in Fig. 1. Among 418 patients who presented clinically with meningoencephalitis or meningoencephalomyelitis and were diagnosed with GFAP-A based on the detection of GFAPα antibodies in CSF using both cell-based assay (CBA) and tissue-based assay (TBA), 291 patients for whom detailed clinical information was obtained from the attending physicians were included in the analysis. Furthermore, among these, 198 patients who had a follow-up period of ≥ 6 months and for whom clinical data at the last follow-up were available were included in the sub-analysis.

Flow chart of the included patients. Of the 418 patients diagnosed with GFAP-A, 291 patients with sufficient information on cognitive impairment were included. In the analysis of the sequelae of cognitive impairment, we included 191 patients who were followed up for more than 6 months after treatment and had sufficient information on sequelae

Clinical data and term definition

Patients’ clinical data, including cognitive impairment, were extracted from the medical history sheet at the time of requesting antibody testing and questionnaires after the diagnosis of GFAP-A. Both forms were completed by attending physicians. “Development of cognitive impairment” (hereafter, they are referred to as "patients with cognitive impairment.") was defined as documentation of “cognitive impairment present” in the clinical findings section of the survey form. Symptoms considered indicative of cognitive impairment by GFAP-A include cognitive dysfunction, disturbances of consciousness, and psychiatric symptoms. No standardized or consistent criteria or assessment scales have been provided for assessing cognitive impairment. Although the training levels of the assessors (attending physicians) varied, most were neurologists or pediatric neurologists. The attending physicians made the determination based on an overall assessment of the pre-admission history and clinical course, selecting the most representative symptom or combination of symptoms. “With sequelae of cognitive impairment” is defined as patients who were observed for more than 6 months after the start of immunotherapy, and “cognitive impairment” or suspected related conditions were documented in the sequelae section of the clinical findings on the questionnaire.

Classification and mode of onset of cognitive impairment

We classified patients with cognitive impairment using a medical history sheet. The classification of cognitive impairment for extraction was based on symptoms that met the criteria for neurocognitive domains (complex attention, executive function, learning and memory, language, perceptual-motor, and social cognition) as defined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders. 5th ed, text rev. (DSM-5-TR) [20].

In addition to the symptoms described above, as reference findings, items that may correspond to “Neurological disorder” as defined by the DSM-5-TR but are more difficult to distinguish from disturbances of consciousness and are challenging to classify in detail, were recorded as “episodes suggestive of cognitive impairment.” Furthermore, symptoms that do not fall under neurocognitive domains but are generally considered part of behavioral and psychological symptoms (BPSD) were recorded as “BPSD.”

In addition, we investigated the duration between the onset of GFAP-A and the first recognition of cognitive impairment, as well as the initial symptoms of patients with GFAP-A with or without cognitive impairment.

Associated and prognostic factors of cognitive impairment

In the investigation of the factors associated with concomitant cognitive impairment, the primary outcome was the prevalence (presence or absence) of cognitive impairment during the acute phase. To select explanatory variables, we referred to the clinical data of 25 reported GFAP-A cases who had documented cognitive impairment as of January 2025 and for whom detailed patient information was available [5, 7–14, 16–19, 21–24]. In those cases, the frequently documented clinical features were as follows: over 50 years old (16/25 cases), over 1 month of period between onset and medical attention (12/17 cases), brain MRI white matter lesions (WMLs) and/or perivascular linear contrast enhancement (PLCE) (20/22 cases) (supplementary Figure 1), and elevated intrathecal antibody production (14/14 cases). On the other hand, the infrequently documented clinical features were as follows: over 100/μL of cell count in CSF (3/21 cases), presence of fever (8/25 cases), presence of headache and/or meningeal signs (7/25 cases). Taking these findings into account, we selected items from our accumulated data that provided more accurate data, represented findings more specific to the pathophysiology, and had fewer missing values as explanatory variables, which are as follows: patient background (age, sex), days between GFAP-A onset and the start of immunotherapy, clinical symptoms (fever, meningeal signs), CSF findings (lymphocyte counts, presence of oligoclonal bands (OCBs)), and brain MRI findings (PLCE). The presence of consciousness disturbance was included as an adjustment variable considering its potential impact on the evaluation of cognitive impairment in the clinical practice of GFAP-A. A binary logistic regression analysis was performed to determine the odds ratio (OR) for each explanatory variable.

Furthermore, we targeted two subgroups to investigate the prognostic factors of cognitive impairment. The outcome of this validation was the frequency (presence or absence) of cognitive impairment sequelae at the final follow-up in both groups. The first subgroup (subgroup 1) included 198 patients who were followed up for at least 6 months after starting treatment. In the subgroup 1 analysis, when selecting explanatory variables, priority was objective and available at the initiation of treatment, considering that comorbid cognitive impairment might not have been fully diagnosed at that point. Finally, the explanatory variables were age, duration from onset to immunotherapy, and CSF cell count at admission. The latter was chosen as a more objective indicator among the clinical and laboratory findings that reflect the severity of acute inflammation (headache, meningeal signs, fever, and CSF cell count). We excluded potential objective-associated factors, such as OCBs and PLCE, from our explanatory variables. This decision was based on sample size limitations and the fact that the positivity of OCBs and PLCE in the questionnaire data was not limited to a specific time point. Indeed, some cases may show positive results only within a certain period after hospital admission. To ensure the clinical applicability of our results, we prioritized prognostic prediction using data available early in the admission period, which necessitated the exclusion of these late-developing factors. Binary logistic regression analysis was used to estimate the ORs.

The second subgroup (subgroup 2) included 79 patients who developed cognitive impairment in the acute phase and were followed up for at least 6 months after starting treatment. Subgroup 2 was derived from subgroup 1. In this subgroup analysis, we aimed to identify factors predicting outcomes after treatment initiation in patients with previously confirmed cognitive impairment. To identify the factors associated with the development of cognitive impairment observed during the acute phase into sequelae, binary logistic regression analysis was performed to estimate the OR for each explanatory variable. Based on the clinical relevance to the prognosis and minimal missing data, the following three items were chosen as explanatory variables: age, time to treatment, and modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score 6 months after the start of therapy (hereafter abbreviated as 6Ms mRS). Owing to the limited sample size of the sequelae group, we did not evaluate the precise impact of the treatment details in the multivariate analysis.

Statistical analysis

In the univariate analysis between the two groups, Fisher’s exact test (two-tailed) was used for categorical variables, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used for continuous variables. To investigate the factors associated with the presence or absence of cognitive impairment in the acute phase or at the last follow-up, binary logistic regression analysis was performed for each variable. Patients with missing data were excluded from analysis. R software [version 4.5.1] was used to conduct binary logistic regression. Statistical significance was set at p <0.05 significant.

Results

Analysis of cognitive impairment

Classification of cognitive impairment

A detailed classification of cognitive impairment in 108/120 patients with cognitive impairment could be extracted (Table 1). Although the types of cognitive impairment were diverse, disorientation (n=73), memory impairment (n=50), and frontal lobe dysfunction (apathy, executive dysfunction, Attentional impairment, and related conditions; n=26) were particularly prominent. Parietal lobe dysfunction (acalculia, agnosia, and apraxia) was also observed. Most patients exhibited multiple concurrent cognitive impairments. Many patients also had episodes suggestive of cognitive impairment, including incoherence (n=24), slow response (n=35), and delirium (n=34). The documented BPSD were hallucinations and delusions (n=11) and talking to oneself (n=3).

Time from onset to recognition of cognitive impairment and initial symptoms

Regarding 112 of the 120 patients with cognitive impairment, we identified the period between the onset of GFAP-A expression and the timing of recognizing cognitive impairment; the median (range) was 17 (1–1005) days (Fig. 2). Cognitive impairment within 2 weeks of onset was recognized in 49 patients (44%), and 46 patients (41%) were recognized as having cognitive impairment more than 30 days after onset.

The initial symptoms of patients with GFAP-A are shown in Table 2. In patients with GFAP-A and cognitive impairment, the initial symptoms were rarely cognitive impairment: fever (n=41), headache (n=21), anorexia (n=18), fatigue (n=18), myalgia and arthralgia (n=8), dizziness and lightheadedness (n=7), lower limb weakness (n=5), and finger tremor (n=5). Furthermore, compared to the group without cognitive impairment, the patient group with cognitive impairment showed a lower frequency of fever (36.5% vs. 55.6%, p=0.002) and headache (18.3% vs. 33.3%, p=0.007), but a higher frequency of anorexia (16.5% vs. 5.8%, p=0.005).

The associated factors of comorbid cognitive impairment

A total of 291 patients with GFAP-A and available clinical data were compared between groups with and without the development of cognitive impairment using univariate analysis (Table 3). Patients with cognitive impairment were older (median [range]; 62 [5–89] years vs. 50 [2–81] years, p<0.01), had fewer females (21.7% vs. 35.7%, p=0.013), and had longer days between onset and treatment (51 [4–1084] days vs. 16 [3–335] days, p<0.01). Regarding the clinical symptom, the patients with cognitive impairment showed lower prevalence of fever (59.3% vs 80.0%, p<0.01), headache (39.8% vs 63.8%, p<0.01), meningeal signs (42.5% vs 60.5%, p<0.01), nausea (11.9% vs 30.2, p<0.01), and higher prevalence of psychosis (45.4% vs 20.5%, p<0.01), consciousness disturbance (79.2% vs 64.9%, p<0.01), hyperreflexia (61.0% vs 47.4%, p=0.023), and myoclonus (38.7% vs 25.1%, p=0.020). No significant differences were observed regarding the following symptoms: general fatigue, anorexia, ataxia, tremor, convulsion, paralysis, sensory disturbance, urinary dysfunction, respiratory failure, orthostatic hypotension, hiccups, and oculomotor dysfunction. The laboratory findings showed fewer cell counts (49.5 [1–324] vs 100.5 [4–761], p<0.01), and lymphocyte counts (48 [1–310]/μL vs 98.5 [4–470]/μL, p<0.01), and higher positivity rate of OCBs (79.4% vs 54.4%, p<0.01) and higher IgG index value (0.7 [0.08–2.19] vs 0.62 [0.033–3.94], p=0.015). Brain MRI showed a higher prevalence of high-intensity WMLs (66.1% vs. 50.9%, p=0.014) and PLCE (56.5% vs. 38.1%, p<0.01) in patients with cognitive impairment than in those without.

Sequelae of cognitive impairment

Clinical characteristics of patients with sequelae of cognitive impairment in all the patients with GFAP-A

In a cohort of 198 patients with GFAP-A who were followed up for more than 6 months after therapy, clinical features were compared between 37 patients with cognitive impairment sequelae and 161 patients without such sequelae (Table 4). No significant difference was seen in the follow-up period between patients with and those without sequelae (median [range], 12 [6–72] months vs. 9 [6–125] months, p=0.63). The univariate analysis indicated that patient with sequelae of cognitive impairment were older (68 [24–89] year-old vs 52 [2–82] year old, p<0.01), had more days between the onset and the therapy (87.5 [7–1084] days vs 16 [3–540] days, p<0.01), lower prevalence of fever (37.8% vs 83.8%, p<0.01), headache (43.2% vs 61.9%, p=0.043), meningeal signs (27.0% vs 60.1, p<0.01), and nausea (10.8% vs 30.8%, p=0.013), than those without sequelae. Brain MRI revealed a higher prevalence of high-intensity WMLs (69.4% vs. 48.3%, p=0.035) and PLCE (58.3% vs. 37.9%, p=0.050). CSF findings showed a higher positive rate of OCBs (87.5% vs 58.1%, p<0.01), a higher value of IgG index (0.8 [0.4–2.18] vs 0.62 [0.033–3.94], p<0.01), and fewer cell counts (42 [1–324]/μL vs 97 [7–761]/μL, p<0.01). The relapse frequency in patients with sequelae was higher than that in patients without sequelae (27.0% vs. 7.1%, p<0.01).

Clinical characteristics of patients with residual cognitive impairment as a sequela of the acute phase

In a cohort of 79 patients with GFAP-A who were observed to have cognitive impairment in the acute phase and were followed up for more than 6 months after therapy, the clinical features were compared between 37 patients with residual cognitive impairment (residual group) and 42 patients with improved cognitive impairment (improved group) (Table 5). No significant difference was seen between the two groups during the follow-up period (median [range]: 12 [6–72] months vs. 9 [6–58] months, p=0.625).

The univariate analysis indicated that the patients in residual group were, older (68 [24–89] years old vs 56 [23–82] years old, p<0.01), had more days from the onset to the immunotherapy (87.5 [7–1084] days vs 20.5 [4–540] days, p=0.016), had lower prevalence of fever (37.8% vs 81.0%, p<0.01) and meningeal signs (27.0% vs 57.1%, p=0.012), higher value of IgG index (0.8 [0.4–2.182] vs 0.64 [0.08–2.19], p=0.016), than those in the improved group. In the binary logistic regression analysis used to estimate the prognostic factors for belonging to the residual group; after excluding four patients with missing data, the final analysis included 35 patients in the residual group and 40 patients in the improved group. The only significant prognostic factor was 6Ms mRS (OR 2.102, 95% CI 1.404–3.380; p<0.01), with no significant differences in age or interval between onset and initiation of therapy (Fig. 4b).

Discussion

This cohort study investigated the clinical characteristics of patients with GFAP-A, focusing on the development and sequelae of cognitive impairment. This study revealed the prevalence of newly developed cognitive impairment and its sequelae in patients with GFAP-A, types of cognitive impairment, initial symptoms, interval between disease onset and recognition of cognitive impairment, factors associated with the development of cognitive impairment, and prognostic factors related to the persistence of cognitive impairment sequelae.

In this cohort, 41.2% of patients with GFAP-A developed cognitive impairment, and 18.6% of all patients had left cognitive impairment at the last follow-up. This prevalence is consistent with previous reports [3, 15]. The rate of cognitive impairment as a sequela was lower than 28.1%, based on the previous literature [15].

The types of cognitive impairment were inconsistent and variable, although disorientation, memory impairment, and frontal lobe dysfunction were prominent. A large number of frontal lobe dysfunctions, including apathy, have been reported in a case series of apathy [6]. However, there was no specific clinical course and cognitive symptoms, such as the period of mutism and akinetic state following excitement in the acute phase of N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor encephalitis or the prominent anterograde amnesia because of limbic lesions in Leucine-rich glioma-inactivated 1 encephalitis [25, 26]. Thus, it is difficult to suspect GFAP-A based solely on features of cognitive impairment.

Another aspect of the types of cognitive impairment was that many cases were difficult to distinguish and were combined with consciousness disturbances and psychosis. Many patients had episodes that were difficult to distinguish from consciousness disturbances: incoherence, slow response, and deliriousness. Furthermore, many patients with cognitive impairment have been recognized as having combined consciousness disturbance and psychosis. This has also been reported in previous reports [27]. Most patients with GFAP-A present with diffuse meningoencephalitis rather than localized lesions. Meningoencephalitis varies in terms of the extent of lesions and the aggressiveness of inflammation, and these features change with disease progression. These pathological backgrounds may account for variations in clinical presentation—whether the condition is clinically recognized as cognitive impairment, identified as consciousness disturbance, or deemed difficult to differentiate—and may contribute to the aforementioned diversity of cognitive symptoms.

Based on these findings, we emphasize that there is no typical clinical course of GFAP-A accompanied by cognitive impairment, as both the type of cognitive impairment and the timing of its onset vary considerably. For example, in our cohort, one patient presented with fever and headache, followed by the acute development of aphasia and disorientation. Another patient experienced headache followed by apathy and anorexia, was initially diagnosed with depression, and developed gait disturbance several months later, at which point brain MRI abnormalities were first identified. In contrast, a third patient experienced general fatigue for several months, followed by memory impairment and subsequent ataxia. Due to such inconsistent presentations, patients are frequently misdiagnosed with dementia or depression, particularly in the absence of fever or headache, which may lead to delayed diagnosis and treatment. In addition, limited awareness of GFAP-A may further contribute to delayed diagnosis.

While it is difficult to diagnose based solely on the type of cognitive impairment to suspect GFAP-A earlier, it is important to note that in many cases with GFAP-A accompanied by cognitive impairment, it may not be the initial symptom. Of all the cases of cognitive impairment, only one patient presented with an initial symptom meeting the neurocognitive domain of the DSM-5TR, and four patients developed BPSD. Among other cases that developed acute initial symptoms, 95.7% were uncommon for neurodegenerative dementia, such as fever, headache, urinary dysfunction, and arthralgia. The combination of these symptoms may be useful for distinguishing GFAP-A deficiency from neurodegenerative dementia at an earlier stage. However, it should be noted that among patients with GFAP-A, those with cognitive impairment are more likely to lack episodes of fever or headache compared to those without cognitive impairment.

The median time between the onset and recognition of cognitive impairment was 17 days. Further, 44% were recognized as having cognitive impairment within 2 weeks, while approximately the same proportion (41%) needed more than 1 month for recognition. In the group where cognitive impairment developed at an earlier stage, it is conceivable that age-related vulnerability and concomitant pathologies of the CNS lowered the threshold for the onset of cognitive impairment [28, 29]. Conversely, in the group that developed cognitive impairment after a prolonged illness, the absence of acute inflammatory findings may have delayed the diagnosis, potentially leading to persistent inflammation and the subsequent development of cognitive impairment [30, 31].

This study found that the factors associated with the development of cognitive impairment were older age and lower CSF lymphocyte count. This study also indicated a possible association between disease onset and treatment, although no significant differences were observed (p=0.051). Lower CSF lymphocyte counts may be partially related to the lower prevalence of clinical signs of acute inflammation, such as fever, headache, and meningeal signs, which were significant in the univariate analysis. Furthermore, these cases were characterized by a higher prevalence among older adults, a population with a high frequency of neurodegenerative dementia. These factors may lead to a potential delay in the initiation of GFAP-A immunotherapy. Some reported GFAP-A cases presented with slowly progressive cognitive impairment that took more than 1 month to go to the hospital and were diagnosed with GFAP-A [12, 18]. Although some of these cases were treated as atypical GFAP-A cases, this cohort revealed that slow progression and the absence of acute inflammation are typical characteristics of a subset of GFAP-A with cognitive impairment. However, it cannot be ruled out that the lymphocyte counts had already peaked in some patients because of delayed diagnosis.

For all patients with GFAP-A, the prognostic factors for the sequelae of cognitive impairment were older age and days from onset to treatment. For patients who developed cognitive impairment, the prognostic factors were only 6Ms mRS, and were not associated with older age or duration from onset to treatment. These results suggest that older age and number of days from onset to therapy, which are prognostic factors for all patients, are more strongly associated with the development of cognitive impairment. Finally, careful interpretation of the mRS is required, particularly given the small sample size of subgroup 2, as the residual cognitive impairment itself may inflate the mRS score, especially in cases where the final follow-up approached 6 months. For example, an mRS score of 2 is defined as slight disability, with the patient being unable to carry out all previous activities, but still able to look after their own affairs without assistance.

Although the ORs were statistically significant, their per-unit effects were small, suggesting limited utility for predicting outcomes in individual patients. However, because these variables were analyzed as continuous measures, their impact across clinically meaningful ranges may be greater than that implied by the unit-based ORs (Supplementary 1). For example, age was associated with the development of cognitive (OR per year: 1.03) and residual cognitive impairments (OR per year: 1.07). While the effect of a 1-year increase in age appears modest, a 10-year increase corresponds to ORs of 1.34 and 1.75, respectively, and a 20-year increase corresponds to ORs of 1.79 and 3.06, respectively. Similarly, the interval from disease onset to treatment was associated with residual cognitive impairment (OR per day: 1.01). Although the effect of a 1-day delay is minimal, delays of 1 week and 1 month correspond to ORs of 1.04 and 1.20, respectively. Given the wide range of onset ages and the substantial variation in the interval between disease onset and treatment (3–1058 days) observed for GFAP-A, these cumulative effects may result in clinically meaningful differences in the risk of cognitive impairment. Nevertheless, the predictive utility of these factors at the individual patient level remains limited, and the findings should be interpreted with caution.

Despite these analyses of prognostic factors, the only factor that clinicians can improve is the duration between disease onset and treatment. These findings suggest that early diagnosis and treatment are important to avoid the sequelae of cognitive impairment. Conversely, cognitive impairment without irreversible pathological changes may be improved by proper treatment, even if patients are of an advanced age or have a longer duration between onset and therapy. Therefore, biomarkers that reflect the underlying pathology should be investigated.

This study has some limitations. First, the clinical evaluations of cognitive impairment have been inconsistent. This was a retrospective study conducted at a single tertiary care center based on information from the medical history sheet at the time of requesting antibody testing and a questionnaire after diagnosis. Therefore, mild cognitive impairment may have been overlooked in some cases. In some cases, it is difficult to distinguish between cognitive impairment and impaired consciousness. Different attending physicians may have diagnosed the same case as either impaired consciousness or cognitive impairment. Second, the assessments of cognitive impairment were not consistent in all cases. Comprehensive neuropsychological assessments had not yet been conducted in many cases. Therefore, we could not assess the proportion and severity of affected cognitive domains. Third, variations in the granularity of clinical information and timing of the last follow-up may have led to information bias. Some patients may not have had sufficient cognitive function before disease onset, although each patient’s medical history was checked. The follow-up period might have been shorter in more severe cases that might have been discharged to another hospital or nursing home more frequently. The prognostic factors identified in this study may not correspond to those associated with permanent cognitive impairment. Even in patients with sequelae of cognitive impairment, cognitive function may have improved after the last follow-up. Finally, treatments were not evaluated in the present study. In examining the factors associated with cognitive impairment, the multivariate analysis could not evaluate the effects of steroid dosage, frequency, maintenance therapy dose, and non-steroid therapies. Although GFAP-A patients are frequently treated with corticosteroids, a standardized treatment procedure is urgently required to improve patient prognosis. In the future, to investigate the exact prevalence and characteristics of cognitive impairment in patients with GFAP-A, a long-term prospective cohort study needs to be conducted.

Conclusion

For GFAP-A, older age and less acute inflammation were associated with combined cognitive impairment, while older age and delayed treatment were risk factors for sequelae of cognitive impairment. Therefore, it is important to initiate immunotherapy before cognitive impairment becomes irreversible and to consider GFAP-A as a treatable cause of dementia. Future prospective cohort studies using standardized cognitive and neuropsychological assessment protocols are warranted.
