From passive fillers to active implants: A 3D-printed trilayer piezoelectric scaffold recapitulating native bone hierarchy
Authors: Yanbo Shan, Suyun Li, Yixuan Wang, Ye Lei, Gang Wang, Qing Zhou, Yuzheng Lu, Yanbin Wu, Hongyu Jiang, Zhengrui Zhou, Cheng Huang, Shaoyang Xu, Ning Wen, Yu Wei, Rujie He, Lisheng Zhao
Published: 2027

ABSTRACT
Critical-sized alveolar bone defects affect over 10 million patients annually, yet current synthetic bone substitutes act as passive osteoconductive fillers with unpredictable outcomes. Native bone, by contrast, achieves robust regeneration through a trilayered hierarchy of cancellous, cortical, and metabolically active periosteal tissues, with intrinsic piezoelectricity converting mechanical loading into endogenous electrical cues. While piezoelectric biomaterials have emerged as promising active implants, existing homogeneous scaffolds fail to recapitulate native bone's structural complexity and electromechanical coupling; to date, no work has combined this trilayer architecture with piezoelectric functionality in a single construct. Here we report a trilayer bioinspired piezoelectric scaffold fabricated via a two-step conformal 3D printing strategy, composed of a strontium-doped β-tricalcium phosphate (Sr-β-TCP) triply periodic minimal surface (TPMS) cancellous layer, a dense cortical layer, and a barium titanate/silk fibroin (BTO/SF) piezoelectric periosteal layer. Under non-invasive low-intensity pulsed ultrasound (LIPUS) stimulation, the scaffold generates bioelectrical signals that support robust osteogenic activity and pro-angiogenic potential in vitro. Transcriptomic profiling reveals that these electrical cues are associated with intracellular calcium influx and upregulation of phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/Akt) and Wnt/β-catenin pathways, consistent with enhanced osteogenic differentiation. In a critical-sized bone defect model, the trilayer scaffold with LIPUS achieves substantially superior bone regeneration relative to controls. This work pioneers the integration of native bone hierarchy with piezoelectric functionality into a single active construct, shifting bone tissue engineering from passive filling to bioelectric-guided repair. Coupling structural biomimicry with electroactive stimulation, this strategy holds potential for future bone regenerative applications, including alveolar bone reconstruction.

Introduction

Alveolar bone defects caused by trauma, periodontitis, tumor resection, or congenital malformations affect more than 10 million patients worldwide each year, posing a major clinical challenge. Compared with long bone defects, repair in the oral and maxillofacial region is further hindered by a harsh microbial environment, complex and dynamic biomechanical forces, and demanding aesthetic requirements [1]. For decades, clinical treatment has depended on autogenous bone grafts, allografts, and guided bone regeneration (GBR) [2,3]. However, autografts suffer from insufficient donor supply and unavoidable donor-site morbidity, whereas allografts carry substantial risks of immune rejection and disease transmission [4,5]. Most commercially available synthetic bone substitutes serve only as passive osteoconductive fillers and lack the ability to actively promote bone formation [6]. This intrinsic passivity often results in unpredictable outcomes and prolonged healing, particularly in critical-sized defects that cannot heal spontaneously. Accordingly, clinicians urgently need next-generation tissue-engineered scaffolds that can actively coordinate the entire bone regeneration process through rational biomimetic design.

Native bone is a hierarchically organized composite tissue whose remarkable mechanical and biological functions arise from its distinctive trilayer architecture [7]. From the innermost to the outermost layer, it comprises highly porous cancellous bone, dense cortical bone, and a thin yet metabolically active periosteum. The cancellous bone forms a three-dimensional interconnected network that supports cell colonization, nutrient diffusion, and vascular ingrowth [8]. The cortical bone provides the substantial mechanical strength necessary for load-bearing [9,10]. Most notably, the periosteum, a rich source of blood vessels, sensory nerves, and osteoprogenitor cells, contributes more than 70% of early osteogenesis during fracture healing by delivering essential nutrients, bioactive factors, and progenitor cells [11,12]. In addition to these cellular and paracrine functions, intrinsic piezoelectricity is a universal property of native bone tissue across both the mineralized matrix and the periosteal compartment, with the periosteum serving as a key electromechanical coupling interface that converts physiological mechanical loads into endogenous electrical cues guiding osteogenic activity [[13], [14], [15]]. Under physiological loading, collagen fibrils generate these endogenous electrical cues, which modulate membrane potential, enhance calcium influx, and activate core osteogenic signaling pathways [16,17]. This electromechanical coupling helps explain why mechanical stimulation effectively promotes bone repair. Accordingly, mimicking the native electrophysiological microenvironment with electroactive biomaterials offers a promising strategy to strengthen osteogenic differentiation and extracellular matrix mineralization [18,19].

In recent years, remarkable advances have emerged in piezoelectric bone scaffolds [20]. For instance, a collagen and demineralized bone matrix composite scaffold integrated with barium titanate (BTO) promoted stem cell migration, proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation under ultrasound stimulation, thereby enhancing vascularization and repairing critical-sized mandibular defects [21]. Likewise, a polydopamine-mediated BTO-decorated sulfonated polyetheretherketone (PBSP) scaffold generated stable bioelectrical signals that activated Piezo1-mediated calcium influx and the downstream Akt/GSK3β/β-catenin signaling pathway, accelerating bone regeneration in rat calvarial defects [22]. These landmark studies clearly demonstrate that piezoelectric scaffolds can actively convert mechanical or ultrasonic energy into instructive bioelectrical signals, moving far beyond the role of passive fillers [23,24].

Nevertheless, a critical gap persists. Most existing piezoelectric scaffolds remain homogeneous and monolithic, failing to reproduce the hierarchical, compositional, and functional heterogeneity of native bone [25]. Moreover, although extensive efforts have successfully replicated the porous architecture of cancellous bone, the periosteum's essential osteogenic functions are often overlooked. Most importantly, no study to date has successfully integrated the full trilayer structural hierarchy of native bone, spanning cancellous, cortical, and periosteal compartments, with its intrinsic piezoelectric functionality within a single construct. Bone regeneration is a spatially organized and dynamically coordinated process that requires a porous framework for cell ingrowth and vascular invasion, a rigid matrix for mechanical support, an active cellular reservoir for rapid osteogenesis, and a dynamic biophysical microenvironment that couples osteogenic lineage specification with pro-angiogenic signaling [26]. Conventional fabrication techniques inherently limit the integration of multiple materials, distinct mechanical gradients, and complex biofunctionalities within a single scaffold. In contrast, high-resolution digital light processing (DLP) three-dimensional (3D) printing delivers exceptional fabrication precision and enables the construction of intricate multiscale hierarchical architectures [27]. For its part, direct ink writing (DIW) conformal printing features trace-along in-situ deposition, which provides outstanding design flexibility and addresses the challenge of multi-material integration [28]. By combining the strengths of DLP in high-precision structural control with the advantages of DIW in conformal multi-material deposition, this two-step manufacturing strategy therefore offers a unique opportunity to overcome the aforementioned limitations.

Herein, we report the design and fabrication of a trilayer bioinspired piezoelectric scaffold that, for the first time, recapitulates the hierarchical structural framework of native bone and functionally mimics the piezoelectric electromechanical function of the periosteum within a single active construct. Via a two-step DLP-DIW conformal printing process, we fabricated an inner strontium-doped β-tricalcium phosphate (Sr-β-TCP) triply periodic minimal surface (TPMS) cancellous layer, a middle dense Sr-β-TCP cortical layer, and an outer flexible piezoelectric periosteal layer composed of barium titanate nanoparticles and silk fibroin (BTO/SF). Upon stimulation with FDA-approved low-intensity pulsed ultrasound (LIPUS), the scaffold generates controllable and sustained bioelectrical signals that are associated with intracellular calcium influx and concurrent activation of the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/Akt) and canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways. We systematically characterized the scaffold's physicochemical properties, evaluated its in vitro cytocompatibility, osteogenic potential and pro-angiogenic capacity, investigated its in vivo bone regeneration efficacy in a rat femoral condyle defect model, and performed transcriptomic analysis to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms. This work represents a paradigm shift from passive osteoconductive fillers to active, bioelectric-guided bone implants by integrating hierarchical structural biomimicry with dynamic piezoelectric stimulation, enabling coordinated osteogenesis and angiogenesis, and offering potential for the clinical treatment of oral alveolar bone defects following further preclinical validation. Materials and methods

Materials

We used reagents of analytical grade or higher purity throughout this study unless otherwise noted. β-Tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP; ≥99% purity), strontium carbonate (SrCO3; ≥99.9% purity), 1,6-hexanediol diacrylate (HDDA), trimethylolpropane triacrylate (TMPTA), and common organic solvents came from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (China). KOS163 polymeric dispersant, diphenyl (2,4,6-trimethylbenzoyl) phosphine oxide (TPO) ultraviolet photoinitiator, and carbon black were obtained from Lubrizol Management (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., RYOJI (Germany), and Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., respectively. Barium titanate (BaTiO3, BTO) powder (<2 μm, 99.5% metals basis) and degummed regenerated silk fibroin (SF) powder (≥95% purity) were sourced from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. and Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd., respectively. Deionized water (18.2 MΩ cm) was purified using a Milli-Q system (Millipore, USA).

Fabrication of the trilayer biomimetic piezoelectric scaffold

We fabricated the scaffolds through a combined DLP 3D printing and DIW conformal printing strategy. First, an integrated digital model of the bilayer ceramic backbone was designed in Rhino software. This model comprised an inner cancellous layer with a gyroid-type TPMS architecture (80% porosity, 300 to 700 μm pore size) and an outer dense cortical layer, forming a single monolithic structure for one-step printing. We then prepared a 10 mol% strontium-doped β-TCP (10Sr-TCP) ceramic slurry by dispersing pre-reacted 10Sr-TCP powders in an HDDA/TMPTA resin matrix at a solid loading of 30 vol%. After adding TPO photoinitiator, KOS163 dispersant, and carbon black, the mixture was ball-milled in a planetary mill (QM-3SP2, Nanjing University Instrument Plant, China) to ensure homogeneity. The scaffolds were printed using a DLP printer (AutoCera, Beijing 10dim Tech. Co., Ltd., China) under parameters adapted from our previously reported method [29]. The green bodies were subsequently sintered at 1250 °C for 100 min in a muffle furnace (Hefei Facerom Furnace Co., Ltd., China) to obtain bilayer ceramic scaffolds (BS).

Prior to slurry preparation, BTO powders were first pressed into thin sheets and sintered at 900 °C for 8 h to promote solid-state reaction and improve crystallinity. After controlled cooling to induce the cubic-to-tetragonal phase transition at approximately 120 °C, the sintered BTO sheets were subjected to high-voltage poling treatment to align electric domains and impart stable piezoelectric activity. The polarized BTO was then ground into fine particles for subsequent slurry formulation. For the piezoelectric periosteal layer, BTO/SF composite slurries with BTO loadings of 0, 20, and 40 wt% were prepared and cured into standalone films (SF, SF-BTO20, and SF-BTO40, respectively) for preliminary characterization. We then uniformly deposited the SF-BTO slurry via a DIW printer (3D-Bioplotter, EnvisionTEC GmbH, Germany) onto the outer surface of the sintered BS backbone in a conformal manner. The final integrated trilayer biomimetic piezoelectric scaffold was designated as TS. Cylindrical specimens were fabricated at 10 mm diameter × 2 mm height for in vitro studies and 2.5 mm diameter × 3 mm height for in vivo studies. A concentric ring layout was used for in vitro specimens to ensure uniform cell seeding and stimulation, while an axially stacked configuration was adopted for in vivo implants to match the native bone hierarchy at the defect site.

Physicochemical characterization

Phase composition and microstructure analysis

A stereomicroscope (SMZ25, Nikon, Japan) examined the macroscopic morphology and overall structural integrity of the scaffolds. Surface and cross-sectional microstructures were observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JSM-7500F, JEOL, Japan). An energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDS, Quantax EDS, Bruker, Germany) integrated with the SEM system analyzed elemental distribution. Crystalline phase composition was characterized by X-ray powder diffraction (XRD, D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany). XRD patterns were collected over a 2θ range of 10 to 80° at a scanning rate of 2°/min and a step size of 0.033°. Functional group analysis was conducted by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Tensor II, Bruker, Germany) over the wavenumber range of 4000 to 400 cm−1 using the KBr pellet method.

Mechanical property tests

Mechanical properties of the scaffolds and SF-based periosteal films at room temperature were evaluated using a universal testing machine (Instron Legend 2367, USA). Tensile modulus and ultimate tensile strength were measured at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/min. The compressive strength of cylindrical BS and TS scaffolds (6 mm diameter × 6 mm height) was determined by axial compression at a crosshead speed of 0.05 mm/min. The interfacial shear strength between the ceramic backbone and the piezoelectric periosteal layer was assessed using a tensile shear test. Sr-TCP ceramic sheets bonded with SF-BTO20 films were fixed on polyvinyl chloride (PVC) plates and tested at a crosshead speed of 2.5 mm/min, with strength calculated as the maximum load divided by the contact area.

Piezoelectric performance evaluation

The piezoelectric properties of SF, SF-BTO20, and SF-BTO40 films (10 mm × 10 mm × 1 mm) were evaluated. The piezoelectric coefficient (d33) was measured using a quasi-static d33 m (ZJ-3, Institute of Acoustics, Chinese Academy of Sciences, China). Ferroelectric hysteresis loops were obtained using a ferroelectric tester (Precision Premier II, Radiant Technologies, USA). For dynamic piezoelectric response, cyclic mechanical stimulation at an acceleration of 5 m/s2 was applied by a linear motor, and the resulting current-time curves were recorded with a programmable electrometer (KEITHLEY 6514, Keithley, USA). The piezoelectric effect was further demonstrated using a custom-bulit circuit in which the film was connected in series with a small LED bulb that illuminated under external mechanical compression.

In vitro degradation and ion release performance

The in vitro degradation of SF-based films (SF, SF-BTO20, SF-BTO40) and ceramic scaffolds (TPMS, BS, TS) was evaluated using a proteinase K assay. Lyophilized proteinase K (Merck KGaA, Germany) was dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Gibco, USA) to a final concentration of 0.4 mg/mL at an enzyme-to-material mass ratio of 1:50. All samples were incubated at 37 °C, and the immersion medium was refreshed every 3 days. Parallel samples incubated under identical conditions were used for ion-release measurement. At predetermined time points, equal volumes of supernatants were harvested, and Ba2+ concentrations from film extracts as well as Ca2+, Sr2+ and Ba2+ concentrations from scaffold extracts were quantified by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES, 5800, Agilent Technologies, USA). For degradation testing, residual mass of films was recorded at days 0, 3, 6, 9, 12 and 15, while scaffolds were monitored weekly for 5 weeks. At each time point, specimens were rinsed with deionized water and lyophilized to constant weight. The residual mass percentage was calculated as (Wt/W) × 100%, where W is the initial dry weight and Wt is the dry weight at time t.

Cell culture

Mouse preosteoblast cell line MC3T3-E1 was obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Cells were cultured in α-minimum essential medium (α-MEM, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) and 1% (v/v) penicillin-streptomycin solution (100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin, Gibco, USA). Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were purchased from the same source and cultured in complete Endothelial Cell Medium kit (ECM, ScienCell Research Laboratories, Carlsbad, USA), following the manufacturer's instructions. All cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. When cells reached 80 to 90% confluence, they were detached using 0.25% trypsin-ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) solution (Gibco, USA) and passaged at a ratio of 1:3.

In vitro biological evaluations

Cell viability and morphological observation

Prior to cell seeding, all scaffolds were sterilized by ultraviolet irradiation for 30 min on each side and preincubated in complete α-MEM for 24 h at 37 °C. MC3T3-E1 cells were seeded onto scaffold surfaces at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well in 24-well plates and cultured under standard conditions. Cells were assigned to four groups: BS and TS groups received no treatment, while BS + L and TS + L groups were subjected to LIPUS stimulation using a UT-1001 ultrasound device (Bohe Medical Technology Co., Ltd., China). The LIPUS parameters were 2.5 W/cm2 intensity, 1 MHz frequency, 15 s per treatment, twice daily throughout the culture period. For conditioned medium (CM) generation, MC3T3-E1 cells were cultured on respective scaffolds under corresponding treatments. Supernatants were collected, sterile-filtered through 0.22 μm membranes, and mixed at a volume ratio of 1:1 with fresh ECM for subsequent HUVEC incubation.

Cell viability of scaffold-seeded MC3T3-E1 and CM-treated HUVECs was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) on days 1, 3, and 5 post-seeding. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured with a microplate spectrophotometer (Epoch 2, BioTek UK Ltd., UK), with three parallel samples per group. For live/dead staining, cells were stained on day 5 using a Calcein-AM/propidium iodide (PI) Live/Dead Cell Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Fluorescent images were captured with a stereomicroscope (SMZ25, Nikon, Japan). Cell adhesion and morphology of MC3T3-E1 cells were observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JSM-7500F, JEOL, Japan). Briefly, samples were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, critical-point dried, and sputter-coated with gold. For cytoskeleton visualization, MC3T3-E1 cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Biorigin, China), permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and stained with Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated phalloidin (Servicebio, China) and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Servicebio, China). Fluorescent images were acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscope (LSM 880, Zeiss, Germany).

Osteogenic differentiation assessment

After MC3T3-E1 cells were seeded onto BS and TS scaffolds in 24-well plates and cultured for 24 h to allow adhesion, the medium was replaced with osteogenic induction medium (OM). The OM consisted of complete α-MEM supplemented with 10 mM β-glycerophosphate, 100 nM dexamethasone, and 50 μg/mL ascorbate-2-phosphate, and was refreshed every 3 days throughout the induction period.

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining and activity assay: On day 7 of osteogenic induction, ALP staining was performed using a BCIP/NBT staining kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The stained samples were then visualized and imaged with a stereomicroscope (SMZ25, Nikon, Japan). For ALP activity quantification, cells were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, China), and ALP activity was measured using an Alkaline Phosphatase Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Total protein content in each sample was determined with an enhanced bicinchoninic acid (BCA) Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) for normalization.

Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining and mineralization quantification: On day 21 of osteogenic induction, calcium nodules formed by differentiated osteoblasts were stained with Alizarin Red S Solution (Solarbio, China) and imaged using the same stereomicroscope. For quantitative analysis, the stained calcium deposits were dissolved in 10% cetylpyridinium chloride (CPC, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 30 min at room temperature. The absorbance at 562 nm was measured using a microplate spectrophotometer (Epoch 2, BioTek UK Ltd., UK).

Angiogenic functional evaluation

Transwell migration assay: The migratory ability of HUVECs was evaluated using an 8 μm-pore Transwell chamber system (Corning, USA). Briefly, HUVECs suspended in serum-free ECM were seeded in the upper chamber at 5 × 104 cells per well, and the CM from each group was added to the lower chamber as the chemoattractant. Following 24 h incubation, non-migrated cells on the upper membrane surface were gently removed with cotton swabs. Migrated cells on the lower membrane surface were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Biorigin, China) for 30 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Solarbio, China). Migrated cells were imaged under a laser scanning confocal microscope (LSM 780, Zeiss, Germany), and cell counts were quantified for statistical analysis.

Matrigel tube formation assay: In vitro angiogenic tube formation was assessed using Matrigel matrix (Corning, USA). Briefly, 10 μL of pre-thawed Matrigel was evenly coated onto a μ-Slide 15 Well 3D plate (Ibidi, Germany) and polymerized at 37 °C for 30 min. HUVECs were seeded onto the solidified Matrigel at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and incubated with the corresponding CM for 4 h in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. After incubation, capillary-like tubular structures were imaged using an optical microscope (Olympus, Japan), and quantitative indicators were analyzed using Image Pro Plus 6.0 software.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis

On days 3 and 7 of osteogenic induction, total RNA was extracted from cells cultured on the scaffolds using the FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme, China) and reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) with 5×RT Master Mix (TOYOBO, Japan). qPCR was then performed on a StepOne Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA) using 2×RealStar Fast SYBR qPCR Mix (Genstar, China). The expression of osteogenesis-related genes, including alkaline phosphatase (Alp), runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2), collagen type I alpha 1 chain (Col1a1), and secreted phosphoprotein 1 (Spp1), was analyzed. For HUVECs cultured with CM for 5 days, angiogenesis-associated genes including vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA), hypoxia inducible factor 1 subunit alpha (HIF1A), platelet and endothelial cell adhesion molecule 1 (PECAM1), and von Willebrand factor (VWF) were assayed following the identical experimental workflow. All primer sequences were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) and provided in Tables S1 and S2. Gene expression levels were normalized to the species-matched housekeeping gene Gapdh/GAPDH and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Western blot (WB) analysis

On day 7 of osteogenic induction, total proteins were extracted from MC3T3-E1 cells on scaffolds using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Servicebio, China). In parallel, total proteins were collected from HUVECs cultured with CM for 5 days using the identical lysis procedure. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA assay (Servicebio, China). Equal amounts of protein (30 μg per lane) were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto 0.45 μm polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Servicebio, China). After blocking with protein-free rapid blocking solution (Servicebio, China) at room temperature for 10 min, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against collagen type I alpha 1 (COL1A1, 1:1000), runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2, 1:1000), osteopontin (OPN, 1:1000), cluster of differentiation 31 (CD31, 1:1000), vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA, 1:1000, Proteintech, China), and species-matched GAPDH (1:2000, Servicebio, China). The membranes were then incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000, Servicebio, China) for 30 min at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Servicebio, China) with the SCG-W3000 PLUS imaging system, and band intensities were quantified using AIWBwell™ software (Servicebio, China).

Immunofluorescence staining

On day 7 of osteogenic induction, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Biorigin, China) for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 10 min, and blocked with 5% goat serum (Solarbio, China) for 1 h at room temperature. The samples were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against RUNX2 (1:100) and osteocalcin (OCN, 1:100, Proteintech, China). For HUVECs cultured with CM for 5 days, parallel immunofluorescence staining was performed following the same fixation, permeabilization, blocking and imaging procedures, with primary antibodies targeting CD31 (1:100) and VEGFA (1:100, Proteintech, China). After washing, the samples were incubated with Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated secondary antibody (1:200, Abcam, UK) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by nuclear staining with DAPI (Servicebio, China) for 5 min. Fluorescent images were acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscope (LSM 880, Zeiss, Germany).

Transcriptome sequencing and validation

RNA sequencing analysis

To explore the molecular mechanisms underlying the enhanced osteogenic differentiation induced by composite scaffolds combined with LIPUS, three groups were analyzed: the BS group, the TS group, and the TS + L group. Briefly, MC3T3-E1 cells were cultured in 6-well plates with BS and TS scaffolds for 7 days, and the TS + L group received daily LIPUS stimulation. On day 7, the cells were rinsed with PBS (Gibco, USA) and lysed in TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). Total RNA was stored at −80 °C and shipped on dry ice to Shanghai Majorbio Bio-pharm Technology Co., Ltd. for purification, library construction, and high-throughput sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA). Raw reads were quality-controlled, and clean reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome using HISAT2. Gene expression was quantified as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 v1.22.1, with thresholds of Benjamini-Hochberg corrected p < 0.05 and |log2(fold change)| ≥ 1. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed using the hypergeometric test, with corrected p < 0.05 considered significant. All bioinformatic analyses were conducted on the Majorbio Cloud Platform (https://www.majorbio.com).

Transcriptome validation

After 7 days of culture, qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses were conducted as described above to validate the key osteogenic signaling pathways identified by transcriptome sequencing. In addition, intracellular Ca2+ fluorescence staining was performed. The culture medium was removed, and the cells were washed with PBS (Gibco, USA). Fluo-4 acetoxymethyl ester (Fluo-4 AM, 1.0 μM, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) working solution was added to fully cover the cells, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 30 min to load the fluorescence probe. The fluorescence intensity of Fluo-4 AM was then observed using a laser scanning confocal microscope (LSM 880, Zeiss, Germany).

For pharmacological loss-of-function validation, LY294002 (Selleck Chemicals, USA), a specific PI3K inhibitor, was used to verify the target signaling pathway. LY294002 was supplemented in osteogenic induction medium at a final concentration of 10 μM throughout the osteogenic induction period. After 7 days of induction, qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses were performed according to the aforementioned protocols to detect the expression of core pathway molecules and osteogenesis-related genes and proteins. Primer sequences used for qRT-PCR in this section were provided in Table S3.

In vivo bone regeneration study

Animal surgery

All animal experiments were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of the Chinese PLA General Hospital (Approval No. 2022-X18-133). Male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats, aged 8 weeks and weighing 250 to 280 g, were used in this study. The rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 3% sodium pentobarbital (30 mg/kg). A longitudinal incision was made over the right knee joint to expose the femoral condyle, and a critical-sized bone defect measuring 2.5 mm in diameter and 3 mm in depth was drilled using a hand drill. Rats were randomly assigned to five groups (n = 3 per time point) using a random number table after one week of acclimation: the Control group (no scaffold implantation), the BS group (BS scaffold implantation), the BS + L group (BS scaffold implantation combined with LIPUS stimulation), the TS group (TS scaffold implantation), and the TS + L group (TS scaffold implantation combined with LIPUS stimulation). For groups receiving LIPUS treatment, stimulation was administered daily at 2.5 W/cm2 intensity and 1 MHz frequency, with 30 s per session and twice per day throughout the observation period. At 4 and 8 weeks post-implantation, the rats were euthanized, and the femurs were collected for subsequent analysis. All in vivo outcome assessments were performed on anonymized coded samples to minimize observer bias.

Micro-computed tomography (Micro-CT) analysis

The harvested femurs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Biorigin, China) for 24 h and then scanned using a Micro-CT system (NMC-200, Pingsheng Medical Technology, China). Scanning was performed under the following parameters: tube voltage, 80 kV; tube current, 0.06 mA; and pixel size, 0.051 × 0.051 × 0.051 mm. Three-dimensional reconstruction was conducted using Recon software, and the bone defect region of interest (ROI) was analyzed with Avatar software. A radiodensity-based threshold segmentation approach, calibrated against pure scaffold and native rat bone standards, was used to distinguish newly formed bone from residual β-TCP scaffold. Key bone regeneration parameters, including bone volume/total volume (BV/TV), trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp), and bone mineral density (BMD), were quantified.

Histological staining

After micro-CT scanning, the femurs were decalcified in 10% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) solution (Solarbio, China) for six weeks, followed by dehydration, paraffin embedding, and sectioning at a thickness of 4 μm. Serial sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), Masson's trichrome (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and toluidine blue (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) to assess new bone formation and collagen deposition. Major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) were harvested at 8 weeks post-euthanasia and processed for HE staining following the same histological workflow. Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was performed using a DAB chromogenic kit (Servicebio, China) to detect osteogenesis-related proteins (RUNX2, OPN) and angiogenesis-related proteins (CD31, VEGFA) in the bone defect area. All histological images were captured using a bright-field microscope (SWE-CX63, Servicebio, China).

Statistical analysis

All physicochemical characterization assays were performed with 3 independent samples per group, all in vitro cellular experiments included three independent biological replicates, and in vivo experiments employed 3 animals per group at each time point. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 software. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using the independent samples t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc multiple comparison tests. All data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Results and discussion

Optimization and characterization of BTO/silk fibroin membranes mimicking the periosteum

To mimic the electromechanical function of the periosteum, BTO nanoparticles were incorporated into an SF matrix at loadings of 0, 20, and 40 wt% to fabricate piezoelectric membranes. FTIR spectra (Fig. 1A) confirmed the characteristic amide I and amide II bands of SF at 1650 cm−1 and 1540 cm−1, respectively, and the presence of BTO-related features indicated successful incorporation. XRD (Fig. 1B) verified the crystalline BTO phase, with diffraction peaks at 2θ = 31.5°, 38.8°, and 45.3° that intensified with increasing BTO loading. SEM (Fig. 1C) showed that BTO addition preserved the intrinsic porous morphology of SF, while EDS mapping (Fig. 1D) demonstrated homogeneous distribution of Ti and Ba, indicating uniform nanoparticle dispersion. Consistent with the introduction of stiff ceramic domains, mechanical testing (Fig. 1E and F) revealed a dose-dependent decrease in tensile modulus and tensile stress with increasing BTO content relative to pure SF, likely reflecting disruption of the SF network and reduced stress-transfer efficiency. Meanwhile, in vitro degradation (Fig. 1G) revealed lower degradation rates at higher BTO fractions, possibly attributed to reduced enzyme-accessible hydrolysis sites resulting from the dispersed inorganic nanoparticles. Functionally, the piezoelectric coefficient increased with BTO loading, with d33 values rising from 0 pC/N for pure SF to 0.9 pC/N for SF-BTO20 and 1.9 pC/N for SF-BTO40 (Table S4). Correspondingly, ferroelectric activity intensified with higher BTO content, as evidenced by a larger polarization–electric field hysteresis loop in SF-BTO40 than in SF-BTO20 (Fig. 1I), and current–time signals under periodic mechanical stimulation increased with BTO content (Fig. 1J). In addition, mechanical stimulation of SF-BTO20 generated sufficient electrical output to illuminate an LED in a custom-built circuit assay (Fig. 1K and L), confirming practical electromechanical signal transduction.

However, stronger piezoelectricity does not necessarily translate into superior biological outcomes, and biosafety represents a critical constraint in material selection, with the Ba2+ release profile being an important consideration in this trade-off. Cytocompatibility assays (Fig. S1) indicated that SF-BTO40 markedly inhibited MC3T3-E1 proliferation, with abundant dead cells observed after 5 days, whereas SF-BTO20 maintained cytocompatibility comparable to that of pure SF. Consistent with the release profiles (Fig. 1H), the pronounced decrease in cell viability in the SF-BTO40 group is attributed to the combined effects of nanoparticle-induced membrane damage and excessive Ba2+ release, which disrupts intracellular calcium homeostasis. In contrast, SF-BTO20 exhibits sustained, low-level Ba2+ release without burst effects over the degradation period, which aligns with its favorable cytocompatibility. Therefore, SF-BTO20 was selected as the optimal periosteum-mimicking piezoelectric layer for subsequent trilayer scaffold fabrication, based on a holistic balance of piezoelectric performance, mechanical properties, degradation kinetics, barium ion release profile, and cytocompatibility, rather than solely maximizing piezoelectric output.

Building on this selection, a nuanced and balanced view of its biosafety profile is necessary. On one hand, several lines of evidence support a manageable safety profile for SF-BTO20. First, the sustained, low-level Ba2+ release described above avoids burst exposure, and the resulting ion concentrations show no detectable cytotoxicity in vitro, as verified by the aforementioned cellular assays. Second, as documented in a prior comprehensive toxicological review, low-level barium ions can be effectively cleared from the body, primarily via fecal excretion, with 77–91% of absorbed barium eliminated within 14 days after exposure [30]. On the other hand, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of the current safety evaluation. The present assessment is restricted to short-term in vitro cellular responses, and cannot fully capture the potential risks of barium tissue accumulation during long-term scaffold degradation. Systematic long-term in vivo toxicological studies, focusing on barium biodistribution, tissue accumulation and potential adverse effects, remain an essential prerequisite for further clinical translation.

Overall, these results demonstrate that the BTO fraction systematically modulates physicochemical properties and electromechanical bioactivity, enabling a design trade-off between electrical output and biosafety. Barium titanate was selected as the piezoelectric component because it is a widely studied lead-free piezoceramic capable of converting mechanical cues into localized electrical signals, a mechanism increasingly recognized as important in osteogenic mechanotransduction and bone remodeling [31,32]. Previous studies have further shown that ultrasound-activated piezoelectric constructs containing BTO can generate bone-relevant electrical microcurrents and promote osteogenesis, partly via calcium-dependent and Piezo1-mediated pathways [33,34].

Silk fibroin was selected as the matrix because it is a biofunctional and processable platform that accommodate dispersed nanoparticles while preserving favorable biocompatibility and tunable degradation [35,36]. SF-based bioinspired periosteum substitutes have demonstrated improved osteogenic outcomes in vivo, and the ability of SF to form flexible, porous structures supports fluid exchange, nutrient transport, and cell infiltration, which are key requirements for effective periosteum-mimicking applications [37]. Moreover, SF-based piezoelectric materials have been widely explored for electroactive implants, in which nanoparticle incorporation provides electrical responsiveness while maintaining structural robustness [38].

In this material system, SF-BTO20 represents an optimal biologically functional window. Its d33 value of 0.9 pC/N is sufficient to generate measurable electrical output under stimulation and falls squarely within the reported range of human periosteum (0.1–1.4 pC/N) [39,40], while cytocompatibility remains comparable to that of pure SF. Importantly, although mechanical properties gradually declined with increasing BTO content in the present study, SF-BTO20 remains fully compatible with periosteum function. Its moderate mechanical properties provide sufficient deformability to convert mechanical stimuli into electrical output, as supported by the current response and LED activation. In addition, its moderated degradation kinetics help preserve structural integrity during the critical early phase of bone formation, with gradual resorption as new bone matures. This combined effect of temporally matched degradation and electrical stimulation creates a dynamic niche that supports progressive osteogenesis. Taken as a whole, the selection of SF-BTO20 follows the design principle that biomimetic piezoelectric periosteal systems require holistic optimization across multiple functional dimensions, rather than single-parameter maximization.

Fabrication and characterization of the trilayer scaffold integrating cancellous, cortical, and periosteal compartments

The trilayer scaffolds were fabricated via a hierarchically precise two-step manufacturing strategy combining high-resolution DLP 3D printing and DIW conformal printing (Fig. 2A). First, Sr-doped β-TCP BS scaffolds with a cancellous-cortical hierarchical architecture were produced by DLP printing followed by high-temperature sintering. Subsequently, the SF-based bioink was conformally deposited onto the outer surface of BS scaffolds via DIW, which precisely traces the pre-designed 3D contour to achieve homogeneous surface coverage, ultimately generating TS scaffolds. SEM observation revealed that the cancellous layer of the scaffold featured a biomimetic TPMS porous architecture with fully interconnected pores (Fig. 2C). Archimedes drainage measurements and dimensional calculations confirmed that the sintered scaffolds matched well with the designed porosity and pore size range, with well-controlled sintering shrinkage (Fig. S3). Importantly, although recent pioneering studies have developed functional bilayer scaffolds combining periosteum and bone compartments, their bone components typically rely on conventional stochastic or straight-pore architectures that cannot reproduce the intricate trabecular topology of native alveolar bone [13,41]. In contrast, the TPMS design offers clear structural advantages, characterized by continuous smooth curvature that minimizes stress concentration under dynamic occlusal loading. Its high surface-to-volume ratio and uninterrupted pore connectivity facilitate early cell infiltration, nutrient diffusion, and vascular ingrowth, which are key prerequisites for robust regeneration throughout the scaffold interior [42,43]. High-magnification cross-sectional SEM further verified tight interfacial integration between the SF-BTO piezoelectric membrane and the underlying β-TCP scaffold, with no observable gaps, cracks, or delamination at the bonding interface (Fig. 2D).

Subsequent phase and compositional characterization further validated the structural design rationale. XRD results confirmed that the crystalline phase remained pure β-TCP, with no detectable impurity phases, indicating that Sr doping did not disrupt the intrinsic crystal structure (Fig. 2E). Elemental mapping by EDS revealed homogeneous distributions of O, P, Ca, and Sr throughout the β-TCP matrix, confirming the successful incorporation of 10 mol% Sr (Fig. 2F). This concentration was previously identified in our systematic investigation of Sr-doped β-TCP TPMS scaffolds as optimal for balancing mechanical strength with dual osteogenic and angiogenic bioactivity [29]. Notably, such uniform Sr dispersion ensures consistent ion release across the cancellous region, enabling spatially synchronized bioactivity to regulate osteogenic signaling and bone remodeling without localized overexposure. In addition, Sr incorporation is widely recognized as an effective strategy to improve the biological performance of calcium phosphate-based scaffolds while preserving crystallographic integrity [44,45].

Consistent with this hierarchical design, compressive mechanical testing showed that the introduction of a dense cortical layer substantially enhanced scaffold compressive strength (Fig. 2G). Compared with the pure TPMS cancellous scaffold, BS and TS exhibited an approximately four-fold increase in compressive strength, forming a mechanically graded construct better suited to the functional demands of load-bearing oral and maxillofacial bone sites. This cortex-like reinforcement effect is consistent with findings from previous TPMS-based bioceramic studies, in which a dense outer shell significantly improves effective stiffness and fracture resistance for load-bearing applications [46]. To quantitatively evaluate the interfacial bonding reliability between the piezoelectric layer and the ceramic substrate, shear testing was performed with the loading configuration schematically illustrated in Fig. 2H. The shear stress-displacement curve demonstrated that the integrated constructs achieved a maximum shear strength of approximately 16.7 kPa (Fig. 2I). Such adhesion is sufficient to prevent delamination during surgical manipulation and in vivo physiological loading, ensuring reliable mechanical load transfer from the rigid scaffold body to the flexible piezoelectric membrane.

In terms of long-term structural reliability, in vitro degradation assays compared the degradation kinetics of the three scaffold formulations (TPMS, BS and TS) (Fig. 2J). TPMS and BS exhibited generally comparable degradation behaviors. The TS group showed a slightly faster degradation rate relative to BS, primarily resulting from the gradual degradation of the superficial SF-BTO piezoelectric layer. This degradation behavior aligns well with the early phase of bone regeneration, as it preserves sufficient mechanical support and piezoelectric stimulation during the critical first 4 weeks of healing while progressively resorbing to create space for new bone ingrowth [47]. Premature degradation would compromise matrix organization and vascular invasion, whereas excessively slow degradation could hinder tissue integration and provoke chronic foreign body reactions [48]. Concurrent with scaffold degradation, the release profiles of bioactive ions were monitored over 15 days. Ca2+ and Sr2+ showed sustained steady release for all three groups, consistent with their shared β-TCP matrix composition (Fig. 2K and L). In contrast, time-dependent Ba2+ release was exclusively observed in the TS group, with no obvious burst-release effects (Fig. 2M). This multi-ion co-release provides persistent biochemical cues to modulate osteogenic and angiogenic cellular responses during scaffold resorption.

Most importantly, the electromechanical functionality of the integrated trilayer scaffold was validated under clinically relevant oral loading conditions. When connected to a custom-built circuit and subjected to periodic simulated occlusal loading in a biomimetic oral model, the TS scaffold generated sufficient electrical output to illuminate an LED bulb (Video 1). This result provides direct functional evidence that the complete hierarchical assembly, rather than merely isolated piezoelectric components, can convert physiological mechanical input into detectable electrical cues, successfully recapitulating the electromechanical coupling mechanism of native bone and periosteum.

Collectively, the results presented in Fig. 2 demonstrate that the trilayer scaffold successfully integrates multiple essential biomimetic features: a TPMS-based cancellous architecture with optimized Sr doping to enhance bioactivity, a mechanically robust dense cortical layer to provide load-bearing support, degradation kinetics matched to the pace of tissue regeneration, sustained multi-ion release for continuous biochemical stimulation, and scaffold-level piezoelectric biomimicry. Together, these features address key limitations of existing piezoelectric bone repair materials and highlight the promise of the scaffold for repairing load-bearing alveolar bone defects.

In line with the proliferation trends, live/dead staining further confirmed the excellent cytocompatibility of all fabricated scaffolds, as abundant green fluorescent live cells and only a few red fluorescent dead cells were observed in all four groups (Fig. S4). SEM observations supported these findings by revealing firm cell adhesion to the scaffold surfaces, with extensive cellular colonization and spreading on both the Sr-β-TCP scaffold side and the SF-BTO piezoelectric membrane side in the TS and TS + L groups (Fig. S5). Phalloidin-DAPI staining further showed that MC3T3-E1 cells exhibited well-spread morphologies, organized actin cytoskeletons, and extensive intercellular pseudopodial connections (Fig. 3B). These results demonstrate that all scaffold formulations possess favorable in vitro cytocompatibility. Notably, the uniform cell spreading, intact cytoskeletal organization, and extensive intercellular connections observed across all groups indicate that the scaffolds provide a permissive three-dimensional microenvironment for cell-matrix interactions. During osteogenic commitment, cell adhesion and cytoskeletal tension serve as critical mechanobiological prerequisites, as they mediate the transduction of biophysical cues into intracellular signaling pathways that sustain osteogenic gene expression and mineral-associated matrix production [53,54].

For osteogenic differentiation evaluation, a full-spectrum characterization was performed covering the entire osteogenic cascade from early enzymatic activation to late extracellular matrix mineralization. ALP, the most widely accepted early marker of osteogenic differentiation, was examined on day 7. Both qualitative staining and quantitative enzymatic activity assays showed that the TS + L group exhibited the highest ALP expression among all experimental groups (Fig. 3C and D). ARS staining on day 21 was applied to assess late-stage mineralized matrix deposition, a defining feature of terminal osteogenic differentiation. Consistent with the ALP results, the TS + L group displayed the most extensive and dense calcium nodule formation, indicating markedly enhanced terminal mineralization capacity (Fig. 3E and F). Corroborating these biochemical findings, immunofluorescence staining was performed on day 7 to visualize the spatial distribution and semiquantitatively assess the expression of key osteogenic proteins. OCN, a secreted protein closely associated with extracellular matrix mineralization, exhibited the highest expression in the TS + L group and was broadly distributed throughout the cell layer (Fig. 3G and H). RUNX2, the master regulator of osteogenic differentiation, was predominantly localized in the nucleus in all groups, with markedly stronger fluorescence intensity in the TS + L group (Fig. 3I and J). Quantitative real-time PCR further confirmed the enhanced osteogenic transcriptional program in the TS + L group. The expression of Runx2 and its downstream target genes, including Alp, Col1a1, and Spp1, was robustly upregulated in the TS + L group (Fig. S6; Fig. 3K). Western blot analysis further validated these transcriptional and immunofluorescence findings at the total protein level. Significantly elevated levels of COL1A1, RUNX2, and OPN were detected in the TS + L group compared with all other groups (Fig. 3L and M).

The TS + L group exhibited the strongest osteogenic efficacy among all treatment groups. This enhanced biological performance stems from three complementary design advantages. First, the biomimetic trilayer structure provides an optimal physical microenvironment for osteogenesis. The TPMS-based porous architecture regulates cell orientation, promotes cellular organization, and improves nutrient and oxygen permeability, thereby creating favorable conditions for osteogenic commitment. Previous studies have confirmed that the curvature and three-dimensional topological features of TPMS structures can accelerate osteogenesis by enhancing mass transport and guiding cellular alignment, outperforming traditional orthogonal pore structures that often suffer from poor cell infiltration and inefficient nutrient exchange [43,55]. Second, the scaffold offers intrinsic bioactive advantages. Sustained release of strontium and calcium ions from the Sr-β-TCP matrix provides continuous ionic cues that directly support preosteoblast function and extracellular matrix mineralization, consistent with well-established evidence that an appropriate ionic microenvironment promotes osteoblast differentiation [56]. Most notably, the SF-BTO composite membrane is capable of converting ultrasound-induced mechanical input into localized, physiologically relevant electrical signals. This property may contribute to recreating the native electromechanical microenvironment of bone tissue and upregulating key osteogenic signaling pathways linked to intracellular calcium dynamics [49], as supported by the comparison between the TS and TS + L groups with identical material composition. In contrast to conventional piezoelectric scaffolds that rely on erratic endogenous mechanical forces to generate weak, inconsistent electrical cues, this ultrasound-triggered on-demand electrical stimulation delivers more robust and controllable osteogenic inputs. For clinical translation, this combined strategy offers distinct advantages over traditional piezoelectric bone regeneration approaches. The present system exploits LIPUS as a non-invasive external trigger to deliver precisely regulated stimulation to the defect site [57]. LIPUS has already been extensively investigated in clinical bone-healing applications and has demonstrated an excellent safety profile, eliminating the need for implanted electrodes or power sources [58]. Additionally, silk fibroin-based piezoelectric composites exhibit superior biodegradability and biocompatibility compared with non-degradable piezoelectric polymers such as PVDF, thereby avoiding the risks of chronic inflammation and secondary surgery for device removal [59].

In contrast, the BS + L and TS groups achieved only modest osteogenic improvements relative to the BS group, with comparable effects observed in these two intermediate groups. In the BS + L group, LIPUS provided only mild mechanical stimulation to cells and lacked the piezoelectric component needed to convert this mechanical energy into the electrical signals that primarily drive enhanced osteogenesis. In the TS group, incorporation of the SF-BTO membrane improved the intrinsic bioactivity of the scaffold surface through the ability of silk fibroin to promote calcium phosphate mineralization [60,61], as well as through weak endogenous piezoelectric signals generated by cellular contractions. However, without external ultrasound activation, these signals remained weak and inconsistent, resulting in a beneficial but still suboptimal osteogenic response. Taken together, these in vitro results demonstrate that all fabricated scaffolds are cytocompatible and support osteogenic differentiation. Critically, given that the TS and TS + L groups share identical material composition, scaffold architecture, and polarization state of the piezoelectric layer, the enhanced osteogenic activity observed in the TS + L group can be specifically attributed to LIPUS-activated piezoelectric effects rather than material-related confounding factors. When paired with the inherent structural and bioactive advantages of the trilayer design, this piezoelectric stimulation delivers the strongest pro-osteogenic outcome across all treatment groups, promoting preosteoblast proliferation, adhesion, and the full osteogenic differentiation cascade from early gene activation to terminal matrix mineralization.

Building on the identified signaling network, intracellular calcium levels were further assessed by fluorescence staining, given the central role of calcium signaling as an upstream regulator of both the PI3K/Akt and Wnt signaling pathways. The results showed that the TS group had a significantly higher intracellular calcium concentration than the BS group, whereas the TS + L group exhibited the strongest calcium fluorescence intensity among all groups (Fig. 5C and D). These findings align with the growing consensus that piezoelectric materials and LIPUS converge on mechanosensitive calcium influx, creating an intracellular environment conducive to osteogenic cascades [49,65]. This process is primarily mediated by Piezo1 channels, which convert mechanical and electrical stimuli into downstream signaling events, including activation of the Akt/GSK3β/β-catenin pathway [22].

Given the concurrent elevation of intracellular calcium and activation of both PI3K/Akt and Wnt/β-catenin signaling, together with the indicated close crosstalk between the two pathways from transcriptomic analysis, we further performed pharmacological inhibition with LY294002, a specific PI3K inhibitor. This experiment was designed solely to verify the requirement of PI3K/Akt signaling in the downstream osteogenic program, and the concurrent changes in Wnt pathway molecules were assessed to evaluate network-level crosstalk. qRT-PCR results showed that LY294002 treatment significantly downregulated the mRNA levels of Igf1, Axin2, Alp, and Runx2 in the TS + L group (Fig. 5E). Consistently, Western blot analysis revealed that LY294002 markedly reduced the phosphorylation of AKT and LRP6, as well as the expression of active β-catenin and osteogenic marker proteins (Fig. 5F; Fig. S8B). These loss-of-function findings indicate that PI3K/Akt acts as an important upstream regulatory node in this signaling network; blockade of PI3K/Akt not only attenuates its own pathway activation but also reduces Wnt/β-catenin signal transduction and the downstream osteogenic program, consistent with interdependent crosstalk between the two cascades.

The present mechanistic study offers several important insights into ultrasound-activated piezoelectric bone regeneration. First, transcriptome analysis identified calcium signaling as the enriched upstream pathway, and demonstrated that the activation levels of the downstream PI3K/Akt and Wnt/β-catenin pathways, as well as the expression levels of all osteogenic markers, were the highest in the TS + L group, which is consistent with the strongest osteogenic phenotype in this group. Second, the highest calcium fluorescence intensity observed in TS + L was quantitatively associated with stronger activation of the PI3K/Akt and Wnt/β-catenin pathways, consistent with a functional link between enhanced calcium influx and osteogenic signaling [66]. Third, KEGG enrichment analysis shows concurrent activation of both pathways, consistent with a bidirectional cross-regulatory network rather than a strictly unidirectional cascade and supportive of an integrated model of osteogenic regulation [67]. Overall, these findings support a candidate mechanistic model underlying the enhanced pro-osteogenic effect of the combined trilayer scaffold and LIPUS treatment, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 5G. In this model, piezoelectric cues derived from SF-BTO and amplified by LIPUS stimulation are proposed to be associated with a pronounced intracellular calcium influx and concurrent activation of the PI3K/Akt and Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways, which in turn correspond with robust upregulation of osteogenic genes and proteins. It should be noted that the present transcriptomic analysis does not adopt a full 2×2 factorial design, and the inclusion of the BS + L group in follow-up studies will enable more precise dissection of the independent transcriptomic contribution of each variable.

In vitro angiogenic capacity mediated by the trilayer-scaffold-derived conditioned medium

Angiogenesis is a well-recognized rate-limiting step in bone defect repair. To investigate the overall pro-angiogenic bioactivity of the trilayer piezoelectric scaffold combined with LIPUS stimulation, we adopted an indirect conditioned medium paradigm. Conditioned media were harvested from MC3T3-E1 cells cultured on scaffolds under four conditions (BS, BS + L, TS, and TS + L) and then applied to HUVEC cultures (Fig. 6A). This indirect paradigm specifically isolates the effects of soluble factors, enabling assessment of the integrated pro-angiogenic contribution from both scaffold-released bioactive ions and osteoblast-derived paracrine mediators. All conditioned media supported HUVEC viability without detectable cytotoxicity, as verified by CCK-8 and live/dead staining. The TS + L group induced the most pronounced time-dependent proliferative response, with significantly higher optical density values at days 3 and 5 than those in the other three groups (Fig. 6B; Fig. S9). Transwell migration assays further showed that HUVECs treated with TS + L conditioned medium exhibited the strongest migratory capacity (Fig. 6C and D). Endothelial migration is an essential early step that enables vascular sprouting into the extracellular matrix and is associated with corresponding differences in subsequent vascular morphogenesis. Matrigel tube formation assays further confirmed this functional progression at the phenotypic level. The TS + L group formed the most extensive and interconnected capillary-like networks, with significantly higher junction counts and total tube length than the control groups, whereas the BS group formed only sparse, discontinuous tubular structures (Fig. 6E and F; Fig. S10). This result indicates that the conditioned medium from the TS + L group, containing both scaffold-released bioactive ions and osteoblast-derived paracrine factors, is sufficient to drive full endothelial differentiation and the assembly of functional vascular networks, reflecting the overall angiogenic bioactivity of the scaffold-cell co-culture system. At the molecular level, qRT-PCR revealed significant upregulation of key angiogenic genes (VEGFA, HIF1A, PECAM1, VWF) in the TS + L group (Fig. 6G). Western blotting and immunofluorescence staining corroborated these findings at the protein level, with the highest CD31 and VEGFA expression detected in the TS + L group (Fig. 6H–M). These molecular alterations are consistent with the functional phenotypes observed across all assays.

Mechanistically, the overall angiogenic effect observed in the conditioned medium assay arises from two complementary contributors converging on the HIF-1α–VEGFA axis. First, sustained Sr2+ release from the β-TCP matrix accumulates in the conditioned medium and exerts a pro-angiogenic effect on HUVECs. As a well-characterized bioactive trace element in bone repair, Sr2+ stabilizes HIF-1α protein by inhibiting its ubiquitin-dependent degradation, thereby transcriptionally activating downstream targets that regulate endothelial activation and vascular marker expression. This ion-driven regulatory mechanism has been widely confirmed in strontium-functionalized biomaterials, where controlled, sustained Sr2+ delivery reliably elicits endothelial angiogenic responses without cytotoxicity associated with excessive ion exposure [68,69]. Consistent with this, TS + L treatment triggered coordinated transcriptional activation of HIF1A and VEGFA, accompanied by elevated angiogenic protein signatures in HUVECs, reflecting a Sr2+-primed transcriptional state that favors angiogenesis. Second, piezoelectric electrical cues from the SF-BTO layer under LIPUS stimulation are proposed to further amplify angiogenesis by reshaping the osteoblast secretory profile, consistent with the enhanced angiogenic phenotype observed in the TS + L group relative to the TS group. LIPUS transduces mechanical stimuli into intracellular biochemical cascades to upregulate angiogenic growth factors, while piezoelectric BTO further converts mechanical energy into localized endogenous microcurrents, boosting the osteoblast secretory response. This mechanistic model is further corroborated by independent evidence. Wu et al. [70] demonstrated that mechanically activated BaTiO3 piezoelectric biomaterials reshape the secretory profile of osteogenic cells and promote osteogenesis–angiogenesis coupling via paracrine signaling, with efficacy significantly exceeding that of mechanical stimulation alone. Similarly, LIPUS stimulation upregulates VEGF and other angiogenic mediators in osteoblastic cells, and the conditioned medium subsequently induces angiogenic differentiation in endothelial cells [71]. Taken together, Sr2+ primes endothelial angiogenic transcription via the HIF-1α axis, while the LIPUS-activated piezoelectric layer supplies amplified osteoblast-derived paracrine mediators that collectively promote endothelial sprouting and network assembly.

From a physiological perspective, coupled osteogenesis–angiogenesis is essential for effective bone regeneration, as vascular ingrowth precedes and supports subsequent ossification [72]. During bone repair, angiogenic remodeling peaks in the early healing window and aligns tightly with the temporal progression of bone formation. Ren et al. [73] demonstrated that microvascular architectural and functional changes closely track early bone growth, confirming that vascular remodeling provides both structural and functional support for osteogenesis. Mechanistically, newly formed vessels deliver oxygen and nutrients, mediate osteoprogenitor trafficking and inflammatory-to-regenerative signal transitions to shape osteoblast differentiation, and in turn receive angiogenic cues from osteoblast lineage cells to stabilize nascent vasculature and sustain endothelial maturation, establishing a bidirectional feedback loop that is particularly critical in defects where native vascularization is compromised [74].

In line with this bidirectional coupling, our in vitro results show that the TS + L system drives both endothelial angiogenic activation and osteogenic signaling through the combined effects of direct ion stimulation and intercellular paracrine crosstalk. Rather than relying on a single pathway, the LIPUS-activated trilayer scaffold establishes a coordinated regenerative microenvironment that more closely recapitulates the native repair cascade. Collectively, the pro-angiogenic effects observed in this indirect conditioned medium assay, including enhanced proliferation, migration, and capillary-like network formation, paired with upregulation of the HIF-1α/VEGFA axis, together with the scaffold's intrinsic osteogenic activity, provide a dual biological rationale for load-bearing bone defect repair via the coordinated action of ion-driven stimulation and paracrine-mediated osteogenesis-angiogenesis coupling.

In vivo bone regeneration in a rat femoral condyle critical-sized defect model

To validate the enhanced bone regenerative effect of the trilayer piezoelectric scaffold combined with LIPUS stimulation observed in vitro, a rat femoral condyle critical-sized defect model was established, and bone regeneration was assessed at 4 and 8 weeks after implantation (Fig. 7A; Fig. S11). At 4 weeks post-implantation, micro-CT analysis revealed initial new bone formation at the defect margins in all groups. Compared with the Control group, the TS + L group already displayed markedly improved bone regeneration, as reflected by significantly higher BV/TV, Tb.N, and BMD together with a lower Tb.Sp (Fig. S12A and B). Consistently, histological observations showed that the scaffold-implanted groups largely maintained the overall contour of the constructs, with the remaining scaffold material appearing as acellular regions. In the TS + L group, osteoid tissue was more abundant along the pore edges at the defect periphery, whereas the Control group was predominantly filled with loose fibrous connective tissue (Fig. S12C; Fig. S13A and B). At this early-stage time point, immunohistochemical staining revealed that the TS + L group exhibited the highest expression of osteogenic markers RUNX2 and OPN, alongside the strongest angiogenic potential reflected by CD31 and VEGFA signals (Fig. S12D–G; Fig. S13C–F).

At 8 weeks post-implantation, the TS + L group achieved the most robust bone regeneration among all groups. Micro-CT images demonstrated that the defect was filled with substantially more newly formed bone than the other groups, showing strong bone continuity and tight integration with the surrounding native tissue (Fig. 7C). Quantitative microarchitectural analysis confirmed that the TS + L group exhibited the optimal parameters, including the highest BV/TV, Tb.N, and BMD values and the lowest Tb.Sp (Fig. 7D). In contrast, the Control group showed minimal spontaneous healing and the defect remained largely occupied by fibrous tissue. These micro-CT findings were further supported by histological evaluation. HE staining showed that the TS + L group formed abundant interconnected mature bone trabeculae extending from the defect periphery toward the center (Fig. 7E). Supporting these observations, Masson's trichrome and toluidine blue staining further confirmed enhanced bone matrix deposition and mineralization within defects of the TS + L group (Fig. S14A and B). Consistently, immunohistochemical staining demonstrated the strongest expression of osteogenic markers RUNX2 and OPN in the TS + L group at 8 weeks, with semi-quantitative H-score analysis verifying significant differences relative to other groups (Fig. 7F–H; Fig. S14C and D). Meanwhile, the TS + L group also exhibited the highest neovascularization level, as evidenced by elevated CD31 and VEGFA expression and maximal vessel density within the defect region (Fig. 7G–I; Fig. S14E and F). Notably, histological assessment of major organs at 8 weeks showed no evident systemic pathological lesions in any group (Fig. S15), supporting the overall systemic biocompatibility of the scaffolds. Collectively, these in vivo results demonstrate that the trilayer SF-BTO piezoelectric TPMS-structured scaffold, when combined with LIPUS stimulation, exerts the most pronounced pro-osteogenic effect among all groups, accompanied by enhanced neovascularization, thereby achieving the most robust regeneration of critical-sized bone defects.

The in vivo trends are consistent with our in vitro cellular and mechanistic studies, supporting the notion that the trilayer scaffold combined with LIPUS stimulation enhances osteogenesis in association with activation of the PI3K/Akt and Wnt/β-catenin pathways. The superior in vivo performance of the trilayer scaffold stems from its anatomically matched biomimetic design, with each layer aligned to the corresponding native bone compartment of the rat femoral condyle defect upon implantation. The outer piezoelectric bone-mimicking layer was made slightly thicker to strengthen local mechano-electrical conversion, the middle dense layer was intended to provide mechanical support against physiological loading during early healing, and the inner TPMS-structured layer offered interconnected porous channels to facilitate cell infiltration and bone ingrowth. Meanwhile, this porous framework also creates a favorable microenvironment for neovascularization, where piezoelectric stimulation and bioactive ion release concurrently facilitate vascular ingrowth to deliver sufficient oxygen, nutrients, and osteoprogenitor cells for osteoblastic activity and bone-matrix deposition, further reinforcing bone-repair efficiency [75]. Such functionally graded, multilayer designs are increasingly recognized as beneficial because they better recapitulate the spatial and temporal requirements of bone repair than homogeneous single-layer constructs [76]. This concept is particularly relevant to the sequential nature of bone regeneration, where initial osteoprogenitor recruitment, maintenance of early stability, and subsequent formation of cancellous-like trabecular structure all require distinct microenvironments [77].

Histologically, new bone tissue originated from the defect margins, progressively infiltrated the interconnected pore channels, and grew along the continuous curved surfaces of the TPMS architecture. This spatial progression is consistent with the staged mechanism of intramembranous ossification, in which collagenous matrix deposition precedes osteoid formation and later mineralization into mature bone tissue [78]. The TPMS geometry is well suited for such processes because its nearly zero mean curvature and large surface area-to-volume ratio can provide favorable conditions for osteoblast attachment and proliferation [79]. As the scaffold gradually degrades, it can further support progressive replacement by mineralized tissue and contribute to the time-dependent increase in regenerated bone volume observed between 4 and 8 weeks. Overall, these results highlight that matching scaffold degradation kinetics to the dynamics of bone formation is critical for optimizing regeneration outcomes [80,81]. Although the TS + L group exhibited the most effective healing, the defect was not fully closed at 8 weeks, suggesting that further optimization of LIPUS parameters, such as treatment duration, stimulation intensity, and frequency, may better synchronize mechanotransductive signaling with in vivo remodeling. Alternatively, modest enhancement of the scaffold's piezoelectric output may also improve the consistency and extent of osteogenic stimulation for large critical-sized defects.

The significant regeneration efficacy observed in this rat femoral condyle model suggests general potential for bone repair applications. Nevertheless, important limitations should be acknowledged. The rat femoral condyle defect model cannot directly reproduce the specific anatomical microenvironment, occlusal loading patterns, and tissue healing characteristics of human oral alveolar bone repair. Accordingly, we have exercised caution in extrapolating the present findings to dental clinical applications. Further validation in clinically relevant large-animal oral defect models is an essential prerequisite for clinical translation in the dental field, and minipig models are particularly suitable for this purpose given their high similarity to humans in craniofacial anatomy, bone remodeling kinetics, and occlusal biomechanics [82]. From a longer-term technical perspective, a promising direction is the development of a self-powered piezoelectric system that harnesses endogenous oral mechanical stimuli such as mastication, muscle contraction, and speech to generate osteogenic electrical signals. This would reduce reliance on external LIPUS devices and further improve patient convenience and treatment compliance.

Conclusion

This work presents the first trilayer biomimetic piezoelectric scaffold that unites the full cancellous, cortical, and periosteal hierarchy of native bone with intrinsic piezoelectric functionality in a single construct fabricated via a two-step DLP-DIW conformal printing strategy. This active implant achieves coordinated optimization of load-bearing mechanical support, an osteoconductive porous microenvironment, pro-angiogenic potential, and electromechanical conversion capacity. Upon activation by clinically translatable non-invasive LIPUS stimulation, the structurally biomimetic system elicits a pronounced enhanced therapeutic effect that couples robust osteogenesis with functional angiogenesis, substantially accelerating the regeneration of critical-sized bone defects. Mechanistically, this strategy is associated with calcium influx and concurrent activation of the PI3K/Akt and Wnt/β-catenin signaling cascades. These findings support a candidate model in which calcium signaling may act as a central hub linking piezoelectric cues to downstream osteogenic transcription, marking a fundamental shift from passive osteoconductive fillers to active bioelectric-guided repair. This approach holds potential for future clinical translation toward bone regenerative therapies, including applications in oral alveolar bone reconstruction. Prior to any clinical adoption in dental applications, validation in large-animal oral defect models and precise tuning of layer-specific degradation kinetics are essential prerequisites. Looking further ahead, this piezoelectric biomaterial platform paves the way for endogenous force-driven self-powered vascularized bone repair systems and for new avenues for next-generation bioactive bone graft substitutes.
