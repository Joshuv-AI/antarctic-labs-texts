A CEL simulation approach for penetration-leveling of the multi-bucket foundation incorporating the temperature analogy method
Authors: Kunpeng Gao, Zhiyuan Cheng, Kun Yu, Yu Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The multi-bucket foundation is widely used in offshore engineering, and the foundation inclination during installation necessitates leveling to ensure its bearing capacity. Although the numerical simulation offers an efficient and rapid approach of validating leveling strategies, the significant large deformation during foundation leveling makes conventional approaches unable to directly simulate, let alone reliably capture the associated seepage distribution characteristics. This study presents a CEL simulation approach incorporating the temperature analogy method to address this gap. Through comparative analysis with the results of a physical model experiment of the four-bucket foundation leveling, it is found that this method can accurately reflect the displacement of the foundation. Moreover, based on the temperature analogy method, seepage distribution patterns during leveling, which are difficult to observe in experiments, were analyzed using CEL. These findings can effectively guide the evaluation of leveling strategies during the penetration-leveling process of the multi-bucket foundation and provide an analysis tool for seepage characteristics during the foundation leveling process.

Introduction

The multi-bucket foundation is widely employed as the foundation for offshore structures such as wind turbines and drilling platforms. Particularly in the deep-sea environment, the multi-bucket foundation offers advantages over traditional pile foundations, including rapid construction, potential reusability, and minimal impact on the marine environment, making it a preferred foundation type for structures in deep-sea areas1–4.

During the installation of a multi-bucket foundation, uneven seabed conditions or spatial variations in soil properties can induce undesirable inclination. An excessively large inclination angle not only causes additional penetration resistance, but also significantly reduces the bearing capacity and anti-overturning ability of the multi-bucket foundation after its installation. The Norwegian DNV-ST-0126 standard stipulates a maximum allowable tilt tolerance of ± 5° for offshore foundation installations5, underscoring the criticality of effective leveling techniques. However, current research primarily focuses on aspects such as penetration resistance and bearing capacity of multi-bucket foundations. Studies on the penetration-leveling issue of multi-bucket foundations are scarce and predominantly based on 1 g physical model experiments6–11. These leveling experiments have yielded a series of significant insights, including the determination of the leveling ultimate negative pressure thresholds (defined as the maximum negative pressure applied to buckets prior to soil seepage failure) under specific engineering conditions, identification of factors influencing the ultimate negative pressure threshold (typically related to the bucket foundation penetration depth and soil properties), the efficacy of different leveling strategies, the characterization of bucket foundation displacement behavior during leveling, and elucidation of bucket-soil interaction mechanisms.

While physical model experiments have provided significant results, their considerable time and economic costs limit their applicability for simulating a wide range of engineering scenarios. Furthermore, monitoring limitations inherent in the physical model experiment hinder the in-depth and comprehensive observation of the soil-bucket interaction during the multi-bucket foundation leveling. Numerical simulation, a widely adopted technique in ocean engineering, offers advantages such as lower economic cost and enhanced repeatability12–16. Numerical simulation can capture data that is difficult to obtain experimentally, enabling a deeper understanding of underlying mechanisms17–19. Once the simulation method is validated, it can directly guide similar engineering practice in other engineering scenarios. Regarding the leveling problem of the multi- bucket foundation, a reliable numerical simulation method can rapidly and cost-effectively evaluate various leveling strategies when the bucket foundation tilts under any working conditions, effectively guiding engineering practice.

Despite the benefits of the numerical simulation, studies applying this technique to the leveling process of multi-bucket foundations appear to be limited. This apparent scarcity is likely due to several technical challenges inherent in simulating this process20, including: (1) dealing with large deformations that can lead to mesh distortion; (2) accurately modeling the seepage effects during leveling, which requires fluid–solid coupling under significant deformation; (3) maintaining computational efficiency.

Common strategies for addressing large deformation problems generally fall into two categories: Discontinuum mechanics-based methods and Continuum mechanics-based methods. Discontinuum mechanics-based methods, such as the discrete element method (DEM), inherently avoid mesh distortion by representing soils or rocks as discrete particles. However, challenges associated with computational cost and material parameter calibration restrict their practical applicability in geotechnical engineering. Among continuum approaches, the finite element method (FEM) is widely employed due to its computational efficiency and the availability of widely validated constitutive models. The ALE (Arbitrary Lagrangian–Eulerian) and CEL (Coupled Eulerian–Lagrangian) methods are two commonly used approaches in the finite element method for solving large-deformation problems. The ALE method addresses the challenges of simulating large deformation problems by combining the strengths of both Lagrangian and Eulerian descriptions. Its core principle involves mapping the solution variables from an old mesh to a new mesh at a specified frequency by sweeping iteratively over the adaptive mesh domain and moving nodes to smooth the mesh. In Abaqus, each such mapping process is specifically referred to as an advection sweep. At least one advection sweep is performed in every adaptive mesh increment. The numerical stability of the advection sweep is maintained only if the difference between the old mesh and the new mesh is small. In Abaqus/Explicit, the advection sweep is triggered whenever the accumulated nodal displacement during a mesh sweep exceeds 50% of the characteristic length of any adjacent element (typically taken as the element edge length). However, when dealing with extreme large deformation problems, the recurrent advection sweeps may introduce boundary inaccuracy: the newly adjusted mesh can no longer represent the original material interface with sufficient precision. Although this error can be reduced by further mesh refinement, the associated increase in computational cost is considerable. Moreover, an inappropriate ALE frequency setting can become critical: too low a frequency delays the update, leading to excessive distortion and possible solution failure; too high a frequency triggers excessive remaps, drastically degrading efficiency without significantly improving accuracy. In summary, since the ALE method still relies on a Lagrangian mesh framework, it faces challenges under extreme deformation conditions, including misrepresentation of the material boundary, cumulative inaccuracies, low computational efficiency, and even premature termination of calculations21,22. The Abaqus manual explicitly states23: "ALE adaptive meshing does not alter the topology (elements and connectivity) of the mesh, which implies some limitations on the ability of this method to maintain a high-quality mesh upon extreme deformation."

The CEL method employs a fixed Eulerian mesh through which the material can flow, while Eulerian and Lagrangian bodies interact via Eulerian–Lagrangian contact algorithm. Because the mesh in the Eulerian part never deforms, mesh distortion within the Eulerian domain is inherently avoided. Consequently, CEL is more robust and computationally efficient than ALE for problems involving extremely large deformations. For further details on the CEL method, refer to Section "CEL (coupled Eulerian–Lagrangian) method". The CEL method has seen widespread application in geotechnical engineering in recent years, including studies of bearing capacity and failure mechanism of strip footing24, soil state under engineering disturbance25, the risk of sliding for submersible platforms26, the tunnel excavation27–29, the blast response30,31, pile penetration and bearing capacity32–35, the slope stability36,37, and the bucket/anchor foundation installation and extraction38–42. In this paper, ABAQUS/CEL was selected to simulate the leveling process of the multi-bucket foundation, which is a typical large deformation problem.

Although ABAQUS/CEL effectively handles large-deformation problems, it cannot directly perform seepage simulation because it lacks pore-pressure elements23. ABAQUS/CEL offers standard stress elements (E3D8R) and temperature-coupled elements (EC3D8RT), where the latter can model heat conduction processes. Through theoretical analysis (detailed in Section "Feasibility of seepage simulation using temperature field analogy in CEL"), a strong analogy between the governing equations of seepage and heat transfer has been demonstrated, enabling the use of temperature fields to simulate seepage behavior. This analogy-based approach can overcome the limitations of ABAQUS/CEL for seepage analysis.

This paper introduces a numerical simulation approach for the leveling of multi-bucket foundations, leveraging ABAQUS/CEL and a temperature analogy method to model the leveling process and seepage induced by negative pressure. While the temperature analogy itself is a known mathematical methodology, its novelty in this study lies in its integration into the CEL framework to establish a practical tool capable of simultaneously handling large deformations and visualizing seepage distributions—tasks that are currently difficult to achieve with standard FEM or physical experiments alone. The proposed method enables the validation of diverse leveling strategies when the foundation tilts during penetration, offering efficient and practical guidance. Section "Method" outlines the fundamental principles of the CEL method, theoretically demonstrates the similarity between temperature and seepage fields through governing equation analysis, verifies the reliability of seepage simulation in ABAQUS/CEL using classic Darcy experiments and impermeable wall seepage cases, and defines the parameter correspondence between the two fields. Section "Numerical simulation of multi-bucket foundation leveling process" applies this method to simulate a four-bucket leveling experiment, presenting a comparative analysis between numerical and experimental results. Section "Seepage analysis" analyzes the distribution characteristics of excess pore water pressure and seepage velocity around the multi-bucket foundation based on simulation outcomes, deriving key conclusions to enhance the understanding of seepage behavior during the leveling process.

It should be noted that the proposed method adopts a decoupled approach, where deformation and seepage are simulated sequentially rather than simultaneously. While fully coupled soil–water models exist in the literature and offer higher theoretical accuracy, they often come with significant computational costs and convergence difficulties, especially for large-deformation problems involving complex multi-bucket foundation geometries. The primary contribution of this study lies not in achieving full coupling, but in providing a practical, computationally efficient engineering tool that can capture the key deformation characteristics and seepage patterns during leveling, as validated by physical experiments. This pragmatic approach fills a gap in current practice, where rapid assessment of leveling strategies under various working conditions is urgently needed.

Method

CEL (coupled Eulerian–Lagrangian) method

In a traditional analysis, usually based on the Lagrangian analysis, nodes of the analysis domain move with the material. Hence, the large deformation of the analysis material usually induces severe element distortion and leads to computation failure. By contrast, in an Eulerian analysis, nodes are fixed in space and therefore do not move with the material. The material boundary is approximately tracked using the Eulerian volume fraction (EVF) for each Eulerian element, where EVF = 1 denotes an element fully occupied by material and EVF = 0 denotes an empty element. Therefore, regions analyzed using the Eulerian method typically require very dense meshes to enable relatively accurate estimation of the deformed material boundary. The schematic principles of Lagrangian and Eulerian descriptions.

ABAQUS/CEL incorporates the Eulerian algorithm and permits the designation of different parts entirely as Eulerian analysis domains, or partially as Eulerian domains and partially as Lagrangian domains. The contact relationship between Eulerian parts is described as a sticky behavior. This description allows tensile stress transfer across interfaces but prohibits sliding. Although reasonable for scenarios like the simulation of a lead bullet penetrating a steel plate, this approach is unsuited for pile-soil contact characterized by potential relative sliding and separation. In ABAQUS/CEL, a more advanced contact algorithm can be employed between Lagrangian and Eulerian parts, which supports sliding and separation between Eulerian parts and Lagrangian parts. This contact algorithm is an enhanced immersed boundary method, where Lagrangian structures can occupy void regions within the Eulerian mesh. The algorithm automatically computes and tracks the interface between Lagrangian and Eulerian domains, eliminating the need to generate a conforming mesh for the Eulerian domain23. Therefore, when using the ABAQUS/CEL, the pile is typically designated as a Lagrangian part, while the soil is designated as an Eulerian part. Moreover, to improve computational efficiency, structures like piles are often considered as rigid bodies.

ABAQUS/CEL can only employ the explicit dynamic analysis method and cannot consider pore-pressure. This means the computational efficiency is extremely low, and it is unable to directly solve geotechnical problems involving seepage. Abaqus/Explicit provides two time increment setting modes: automatic time increment and user-defined time increment. Regarding the other issue of seepage simulation, although ABAQUS/CEL does not support pore-pressure elements, it supports heat transfer simulation. There is a mathematical similarity between the basic equations of heat transfer problems and seepage problems. Therefore, the temperature field can be used to analogize the pore-water pressure distribution formed by seepage. The demonstration of this idea is detailed in Section "Feasibility of seepage simulation using temperature field analogy in CEL".

Numerical simulation of multi-bucket foundation leveling process

Overview of the benchmark experiment

Research on multi-bucket foundations leveling is limited. Zhang43 presented a valuable experimental study on the leveling of a four-bucket foundation, providing a benchmark dataset for numerical simulation validation in this paper. In the experiment, a four-bucket foundation was embedded 100 mm into the saturated sand and initially tilted by 3°, followed by a series of leveling measures. The foundation, rigidly connected by steel beams, comprised buckets with a diameter D of 300 mm, height H of 250 mm, and wall thickness t of 2 mm. The center-to-center distance between adjacent buckets was 300 mm. The steel material properties included: Elastic Modulus E = 200 GPa, Poisson’s ratio u = 0.3, and density \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho$$\end{document}=7850 kg/m3. The total weight of the foundation was approximately 550N. The soil used in the experiment was characterized by the following parameters: the internal friction angle φ = 34.4°, the cohesion c = 3 kPa, the permeability coefficient k = 4.12 × 10⁻⁶ m/s, the relative compaction \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${D}_{r}$$\end{document}=0.69, the saturated unit weight \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\gamma }_{w}$$\end{document}=22.41 kN/m3, and the void ratio e = 0.57.

In the experiment, two leveling strategies were implemented, both involving a gradual increase in suction pressure: (1) the single bucket suction method, where suction pressures were gradually increased up to 5 kPa and applied exclusively to the elevated bucket (the high-position bucket), resulting in a tilt reduction from 3.08° to 0.089°; (2) the combined suction method, where suction pressures were gradually increased up to 3 kPa and simultaneously applied to both the elevated bucket and adjacent buckets, achieving a tilt correction from 3.15° to 0.135°. This paper focuses on the experimental process of the first leveling strategy and employs the CEL method for numerical simulation, aiming to validate the applicability of the ABAQUS/CEL method for simulating the leveling process of the multi-bucket foundation.

The main basis for the simulation validation in this paper is the test numbered TP3-h1 in the experiment. Since the penetration data reported in this test before leveling is not detailed enough (only the self-weight penetration depth is available), reference was also made to the penetration test numbered PS-1 under the same conditions in the experiment (which includes both the self-weight penetration depth and the reaction force value at a penetration depth of 92 mm).

Numerical simulation process

This numerical simulation aims to replicate the experiment to verify the effectiveness of our proposed method for simulating the leveling process of a multi-bucket foundation. To balance computational efficiency and accuracy while minimizing boundary effects, a cuboid soil domain of 1800 mm × 1800 mm × 850 mm was chosen. This size allowed for sufficient stress wave dissipation and reduced boundary reflections, ensuring reliable results without excessive computational cost. The foundation model was meticulously constructed based on the geometric specifications provided by the experiment, with a diameter of 300 mm, a height of 250 mm and a center-to-center distance between two adjacent buckets of 300 mm. The four-bucket foundation was modeled as a rigid body, considering the large difference in stiffness between soil and steel and enhancing computational efficiency.

The soil was modeled as a homogeneous and isotropic material, with its constitutive behavior described by the Mohr–Coulomb model. Although advanced constitutive models (e.g., hypoplastic or NorSand) could better capture dilation, strain softening, or cyclic behavior in saturated soils under suction—critical for seepage-induced failures—we retained the Mohr–Coulomb model for two key reasons: (1) Data constraints: The benchmark experiment only provided basic strength parameters derived from standard geotechnical tests, lacking the complex calibration parameters required for advanced models; (2) Computational robustness: The Mohr–Coulomb model exhibits superior convergence in large-deformation CEL analysis, avoiding numerical instability caused by over-parameterization. While the soil’s parameters were obtained through laboratory geotechnical tests in the experiment, the absence of in-situ tests, such as cone penetration tests (CPT), within the test tank introduces uncertainties regarding the soil’s homogeneity and stress state. Consequently, adjustments to soil mechanical parameters became necessary for numerical modeling. Based on the observed 60 mm self-weight penetration depth in the experiment, the Elastic Modulus employed in our numerical simulation was back-analyzed and calibrated to 30 kPa, while keeping other soil parameters consistent with the experimentally reported values. The back-analysis was performed by simulating self-weight penetration with varying Elastic Modulus. Details are presented in Table 3, which shows that 30 kPa yields a penetration depth (64.03 mm) closest to the experimental 60 mm (Test NO. PS-1) and 65 mm (Test NO. The boundary conditions applied to the numerical model included horizontal displacement constraints on the lateral sides of the soil domain, and vertical displacement constraints on the bottom surface. This ensures sufficient stress wave dissipation and avoids boundary reflections interfering with the soil-bucket interaction.

To ensure numerical accuracy while balancing computational efficiency, special attention was paid to mesh design. Given the unique geometric constraints of the thin-walled bucket (2 mm thickness) and the requirement of the CEL method for fine meshing in critical interaction zones (soil-bucket contact areas) to capture local deformation, the width of the soil element beneath the bucket wall was set to 2 mm. In the initial stage of the study, we attempted to discretize the soil around the bucket with element sizes of 1 mm or smaller to improve contact stress accuracy; however, this led to severe computational challenges: the stable time increment fell below 10⁻1⁰ seconds after bucket penetration, and the simulation failed to proceed effectively. To balance computational feasibility and result reliability, we conducted a mesh sensitivity analysis using penetration depth as the key validation indicator (detailed in the subsequent mesh independence verification section). When the global mesh seed was set to 25 mm (consistent with Case 2 in Fig. 4 and the soil around the bucket was locally refined to 2 mm, the calculated penetration depth (64.03 mm) achieved convergence, and it also aligned well with the experimental penetration depth (60 mm in Test NO. As uniformly fine meshes would lead to excessive element counts (a 2 mm uniform mesh for the 1800 mm × 1800 mm × 850 mm soil domain would require 344 million elements, which would render the analysis infeasible), a radial meshing strategy with local refinement was employed to reduce element counts.

To verify mesh independence, five configurations were tested: two with vertical refinement (global sizes 12.5 mm and 25 mm, with 10 mm length refinement in the bucket-influenced zone) and three without refinement (global sizes 25 mm, 50 mm, and 100 mm), as shown in Fig. 4. The self-weight penetration depth, experimentally reported as 60 mm (Test NO. PS-1) and 65 mm (Test NO.TP3-h1), served as key validation indicators. Results showed that Case 1 (12.5 mm global size with vertical refinement) yielded a penetration depth of 67.37 mm, while Case 2 (25 mm global size with vertical refinement) yielded 64.03 mm, which were both close to the experimental value. Considering computational efficiency, Case 2 reduced the element count by approximately 65% compared to Case 1, making it the optimal choice for final simulations.

Seepage analysis

Based on the high consistency between the simulation and the experiment, the temperature analogy method, implemented within the ABAQUS/CEL numerical simulation framework, was employed to investigate the seepage field distribution during the leveling process. Owing to the inherent disparity between the stabilization time of bucket-soil deformation and the steady-state time of seepage, simultaneously simulating the movement of the bucket foundation and the seepage process is challenging. This paper proposes the following approach to simulate seepage: when the bucket foundation moves to a key position, the calculation is paused and the foundation is fixed; then, the seepage when the foundation is at this position is simulated using the temperature analogy method mentioned in Section "Feasibility of seepage simulation using temperature field analogy in CEL".

Following this approach, seepage calculations were attempted in the model presented in Section "Numerical simulation of multi-bucket foundation leveling process". Although the program ran without errors, computation was extremely slow due to limitations of the available computational resources. To improve efficiency, four representative time points (0 s, 0.06 s, 0.12 s, 0.18 s) were selected based on the bucket foundation displacements shown in Fig. 7. Using buckets’ displacements at these moments, thin three-dimensional models (10 mm thickness) were constructed to approximate two-dimensional behavior based on the A-C and A-B bucket cross-sections. The reason for selecting two cross-sections lies primarily in the consideration that the 2D simplified model may not accurately capture the seepage patterns of the 3D model, and the use of two cross-sections enables a more comprehensive understanding of the spatial characteristics of the seepage field. This simplification significantly improved computational efficiency while maintaining acceptable accuracy, making the analysis feasible even with limited computational resources.

In the simulation, a -0.005 °C temperature boundary was applied to the soil surface inside the high-position bucket (the bucket which, in the experiment, was subjected to a 0.005Mpa negative pressure), while a 0 °C temperature boundary was set for the soil surface outside the high-position bucket. The initial temperature of the entire soil model was 0 °C, which means there was no excess pore-water pressure in the soil at the initial simulation time. Specifically, the magnitude of temperature changes numerically equals that of excess pore-water pressure. The calculated seepage characteristics of the A-C and A-B bucket cross-sections exhibited high consistency. Therefore, only the seepage results of the A-B bucket cross-section were analyzed. Figure 8 presents the contour plots of pore-water pressure distribution, seepage velocity, and seepage vector on the A-B bucket cross-section.Fig. 8Seepage analysis on the A-B bucket cross-section at representative step times.

Seepage analysis on the A-B bucket cross-section at representative step times.

From Fig. 8, the following seepage regularities are observed during the leveling process of the bucket foundation. (1) The influence range of excess pore-water pressure is concentrated around the high-position bucket where negative pressure is applied, and the distribution range of excess pore-water pressure under the bucket (vertical direction) is significantly greater than that on both sides of the bucket (horizontal direction). (2) With increasing depth, the excess pore-water pressure decreases rapidly within the penetration depth range of the bucket foundation, while after exceeding this depth, the reduction rate of excess pore-water pressure gradually slows down. (3) As analysis time progresses from 0 to 0.18 s, the maximum seepage velocity consistently occurs at the bucket tip and decreases with increasing penetration depth. For example, at the analysis time of 0 s, the penetration depth on the right side of the high-position bucket was about 70 mm, with a seepage velocity of approximately 3.6 × 10⁻2 mm/s at the tip. By the analysis time of 0.18 s (when the bucket foundation had been leveled), as the penetration depth increased to approximately 100 mm, the seepage velocity at the bucket tip decreased to 3.1 × 10⁻2 mm/s. This indicates that a larger penetration depth can effectively reduce the seepage velocity, thereby lowering the possibility of seepage failure. It also implies that when the bucket foundation has a greater penetration depth, a larger negative pressure can be applied for leveling. (4) Seepage with a relatively high velocity occurs near the inner side of the bucket wall, while the seepage velocity in the middle area inside the bucket is significantly reduced. This suggests that seepage failure is more likely to occur near the inner wall of the bucket.

Notably, excess pore-water pressure demonstrates a significant linear decrease within the penetration range, and seepage velocity follows a similar trend, though the descending trend deviates from the linear relationship near the soil surface.

Excluding the bucket tip, excess pore-water pressure increases linearly from the outside to the inside of the bucket, with a slightly steeper increase rate (slope of the curve) inside the bucket than outside. This lateral variation diminished as the distance from the soil surface decreased.

It should be explicitly stated that these seepage characteristics currently lack direct experimental or field verification. This limitation stems from the technical and practical challenges in seepage monitoring during multi-bucket foundation leveling, including the difficulty of installing miniature seepage sensors in small-scale model tests without interfering with soil-bucket interactions, and the scarcity of public field data from practical engineering projects involving this relatively new foundation type. Future studies should therefore prioritize designing dedicated experimental setups to validate the temperature analogy method for seepage simulation—for example, adopting larger-scale multi-bucket models to facilitate the embedding of seepage monitoring sensors (e.g., miniature pore pressure transducers and flow meters) that can accurately capture localized seepage signals. Such experimental validation will help further confirm the reliability of the proposed simulation approach and enhance its engineering applicability.

Discussion and conclusion

Limitations

The present simulation method rests on the ABAQUS/CEL algorithm and the mathematical identity between the steady-state heat conduction equation and the saturated laminar seepage equation outlined in Section "Feasibility of seepage simulation using temperature field analogy in CEL". Consequently, its applicability is strictly limited to specific flow regimes where this mathematical identity holds, and several critical limitations must be explicitly elaborated.Application scope of the method: The method is tailored to Darcy flow in saturated porous media with Reynolds numbers typically in the range 1–10. Beyond this range, the seepage law shifts to the Darcy-Forchheimer form, and the linear mapping to Fourier’s law of heat conduction breaks down. For unsaturated soils, seepage behavior is dominated by matric suction and the soil–water characteristic curve, introducing nonlinearity and hysteresis that have no counterpart in the heat conduction equations outlined in Section "Feasibility of seepage simulation using temperature field analogy in CEL", rendering the temperature analogy unsuitable for such conditions. Furthermore, even within the laminar regime, heat conduction (diffusion of molecular kinetic energy) and seepage (viscous flow shaped by pore geometry, viscosity, and gravity) are fundamentally distinct physical processes, leading to additional limitations. For instance, regarding transient problems, scenarios like rapid excess pore-water pressure generation and dissipation, hydraulic pressure propagation is often idealized as instantaneous, while heat diffusion is governed by thermal diffusivity. Simulating transient pore-water pressure changes via the analogy requires artificial time scaling with tenuous physical justification. Besides that, seepage directly alters soil effective stress, while thermal flux primarily induces volumetric changes. This difference makes calibrating thermal expansion coefficients to replicate seepage-induced effective stress effects extremely difficult, reflecting the analogy’s inherently decoupled nature. For multi-bucket foundation leveling specifically, these limitations do not affect its practical utility. Since the leveling process is typically slow and seabed soils are saturated, which matches the method’s applicable conditions, and simulation results align well with model tests, this method could effectively capture the bucket displacement and seepage distribution during leveling. And this method has certain guiding significance in engineering practice. If a more accurate and more universally applicable method is required, a potential indirect approach for future studies could involve inverting simulated temperature distributions to excess pore-water pressure fields, then incorporating these as nodal force corrections to modify the soil skeleton’s effective stress.Decoupling of deformation and seepage: The sequential approach (simulating deformation first, then steady-state seepage on fixed geometries) ignores real-time interactions where seepage alters effective stresses and vice versa. For fast-loading scenarios (e.g., sudden suction jumps), this may underestimate transient pore-pressure spikes that could trigger localized seepage failure. Fully coupled schemes (e.g., Abaqus/CFD or user-defined VUMAT subroutines) would theoretically resolve this limitation, but they require extensive secondary development and are computationally prohibitive for large-deformation CEL analysis on standard workstations. Additionally, as noted in Item a, water pressure is generally considered to transmit instantaneously, whereas heat transfer does not—these are inherently distinct physical processes. This fundamental difference makes it difficult for the temperature analogy method to align the time scales of heat transfer and soil deformation: specifically, when bucket movement and soil deformation stabilize, heat transfer (analogous to seepage/ excess pore water pressure) has not completed, and computational challenges arise when the heat transfer time scale is excessively short. Soil constitutive model limitations: The Mohr–Coulomb model, while simple and robust, cannot capture dilation, strain softening, or void ratio evolution during shear—behaviors relevant to seepage-induced failures. The model also overlooks void ratio evolution during deformation, which may slightly overestimate soil stiffness in long-term leveling. Advanced state-dependent models (e.g., hypoplastic or NorSand) would better represent sand’s response, but they require complex parameters that were not provided by the benchmark experiment. Using such models with assumed parameters would introduce greater uncertainty than the Mohr–Coulomb model with measured parameters (friction angle φ, cohesion c). However, exploring the application of advanced constitutive models can serve as a key direction for future research.Explicit dynamics artifacts quasi-static assumption applicability: CEL’s explicit solver would introduce high-frequency numerical oscillations. These numerical oscillations usually do not affect the analysis of result trends and inherent regularities, but they pose challenges to more accurate quantitative analysis. Measures such as artificial viscosity optimization or post-processing filtering could mitigate these oscillations. Furthermore, from the perspective of practical engineering, adjustments are controlled extremely slowly to avoid soil failure and structural overturning. This slow adjustment ensures relatively stable seepage, and there is sufficient time for the dissipation of excess pore water pressure, making the quasi-static approximation reasonably justified to a certain extent, which is also confirmed by the comparison with model test results. Nevertheless, while the quasi-static assumption is applicable for such gradual leveling processes, it may fail to fully capture transient dynamics during the initial stage of multi-bucket foundation leveling, particularly when negative pressure changes abruptly. It is important to recognize that the leveling process is inherently a transient dynamic problem; therefore, the quasi-static assumption cannot be employed if more precise simulations are desired.Limitation of lacking full-scale model comparison: The numerical simulation was validated using a 300 mm-scale model instead of full-scale data, which is a notable limitation. This choice is primarily due to scarce publicly available full-scale engineering data (as multi-bucket foundations are newly widely applied in offshore engineering, with no usable validation data found in literature) and alignment with the study’s core objective—exploring a feasible numerical workflow for leveling rather than directly predicting full-scale performance. Small-scale model tests, as the mainstream research form for this topic, provide reliable displacement and soil parameter data sufficient to verify the method’s ability to capture key leveling behaviors, making the benchmark selection appropriate for method exploration. Future studies will supplement full-scale validation once relevant data or test results become accessible to enhance engineering applicability.

Conclusion

Current research on multi-bucket foundation leveling primarily relies on physical model experiments, with limited numerical simulation studies, especially for the large deformation and seepage during the penetration-leveling of the foundation. This study addresses this gap by combining the ABAQUS/CEL method with a temperature analogy approach to simulate the leveling process. Along the bucket wall, excess pore-water pressure increases approximately linearly from outside to inside, with slightly higher pressure in the central area inside the bucket compared to near the walls (this difference diminishes with depth).

Seepage velocity characteristics The maximum seepage velocity occurred at the bucket tip, with a symmetrical distribution around the tip (velocities inside the bucket are slightly higher than outside). Seepage velocity near the inner bucket wall is significantly higher than in the central area, indicating a higher risk of seepage failure at the wall. Notably, seepage velocity decreases with increasing penetration depth, enabling the application of greater negative pressure for leveling when the bucket is more deeply embedded.

While the proposed method adopts a decoupled approach and does not achieve the theoretical rigor of fully coupled soil–water models, its practical value lies in providing an efficient, experimentally validated engineering tool for rapid assessment of leveling strategies under various working conditions. This fills a current gap in offshore geotechnical practice, where quick and reliable guidance is often needed before more sophisticated analyses can be performed.
