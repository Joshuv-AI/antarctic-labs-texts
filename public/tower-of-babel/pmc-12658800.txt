Evaluating the Performance of Different Substrates for Preparing Enzyme-Immobilized Bioreactors
Authors: Mahbuba Jannat, Swarnila Chakma, Md. Junayed, Akib Atique Khan Dhrubo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A suitable substrate is needed for enzymatic bioreactors that can provide better stability and reusability for enzyme immobilization. In this work, glass slides and silicone tubing were used as substrates for horseradish peroxidase (HRP) enzyme immobilization. First, the substrates were treated with (3-amino­propyl)­triethoxy­silane (APTES), followed by either dextran polyaldehyde (DPA) or glutar­aldehyde (GA) treatment. Finally, the enzyme was immobilized on the surface of each substrate and used for phenol degradation. It is found that HRP immobilized on glass slides can degrade 21% phenol (1 mg/mL); meanwhile, on silicone bioreactors it can degrade 10% phenol. Moreover, the maximum HRP loading was found to be 6% with 2.52 U total activity on a DPA-treated glass slide, whereas the minimum loading was 3% with 1.26 U total activity on GA-treated silicone tubing. Afterward, the reusability of bioreactors prepared on DPA- and GA-terminated glass slides and silicone tubing was checked for phenol degradation, and it was found that glass slide substrates maintain 100% of the original activity regardless of the chemical treatment; however, silicone tubing loses activity and retains 91.5% of the original activity after 8 cycles of use. Finally, the storage stability of chemically treated (DPA- or GA-terminated surface) glass slides for preparing HRP-immobilized bioreactors was checked over a period of 4 months by using phenol degradation phenomena to find which one(s) can be used as a ready-to-use substrate for enzyme immobilization. It is found that DPA-terminated surfaces can preserve 100% of the original activity, whereas GA-terminated surfaces can hold about 90% of the original activity.

Introduction

Phenols and their derivatives are priority pollutants that have toxic, carcinogenic, and mutagenic effects on humans, animals, and aquatic organisms. These compounds are generated from different industries, especially the textile industry, where dyes containing phenolic compounds are frequently applied. , Moreover, advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) such as ozonation, electro-Fenton, and hydroxyl or sulfate radical treatment, which are frequently used for textile dyes removal, are known to produce phenolic components as byproducts as well. , To remove these phenolic compounds, physiochemical and advanced methods have been attempted and achieved moderate success. Among the conventional methods, activated carbon adsorption was successful to selectively eliminate toxic components like phenol by entrapping it over the activated surface. Though satisfactory adsorption capacity at 2.01 mg/g was achieved, such processes are bound with operational limitationslarge pressure drop, difficult regeneration, and high chemical cost. UV/H2O2, electro-Fenton, and other AOPs are quite potent in removal of organic components even in trace amount; however, the byproducts and residues formed after oxidation may induce more contamination than the original pollutants. Recently, biological methods such as the acclimatized activated sludge method or the use of membrane bioreactors have become popular means of phenol removal from wastewater. Jalayeri et al. achieved more than 99% phenol removal from an aqueous solution of concentration below 1000 ppm by using activated sludge. Nonetheless, loss of efficiency due to fouling and inoperativeness in the high concentration range are major drawbacks of conventional biological systems. Oxido­reductase enzymes like laccase, tyrosinase, peroxidases, etc. can be used to accelerate the phenol degradation process. Horseradish peroxidase (HRP) has shown promising results in the removal of a wide spectrum of aromatic compounds, including phenols, by forming phenoxy radicals in the presence of H2O2. Phenol removal using HRP is nontoxic and environmentally friendly in nature, as the formed byproducts are innocuous and easily separable from the mixture.

A number of studies have reported efficient phenol removal upon homogeneous application of commercially obtained or renewably extracted HRP enzyme. , Yet, concerns regarding the storability, recyclability, and enzyme denaturation in the presence of impurities complicate homogeneous usage. Immobilization strategies, either physical or chemical, have been developed to improve stability, reusability, and retention of enzymatic activity over long periods. Physical immobilization includes adsorption over the surface or entrapment within polymeric materials, , but these techniques are susceptible to issues like weak attachment of enzyme with the surface which further result in enzyme loss in flowing conditions. On the other hand, chemical methods, e.g., covalent bonding or cross-linking, usually resolve these issues by providing better stability. However, factors that commonly affect the performance of immobilized enzymes (in terms of their activity, selectivity, reusability, storability, etc.) are the immobilization support materials, techniques, and operating conditions such as temperature and pH.

Heterogeneous bioreactors have been developed in various ways with immobilized enzymes where the support material must be mechanically and thermally strong, nontoxic, economical, easily available, and biocompatible with the enzyme. Researchers have successfully entrapped enzymes onto glass capillaries, silica and polymer films, biochar support, and flexible tubing surfaces like polytetra­fluoro­ethylene (PTFE) to degrade phenolic compounds. Major challenges faced in these processes are the enzyme detachment due to leaching and nonselective reaction. To address these issues, nonreactive cross-linkers can be used that provide strong attachment to the surface, thermal and mechanical stability in harsher conditions, and also a large surface-to-volume ratio for the enzymes. Paraformaldehyde and glutaraldehyde (GA) have been employed on surfaces such as PTFE and inert tubing for HRP immobilization. Honda et al. attempted to prepare a layer of cross-linkers using a mixture of GA and formaldehyde to immobilize HRP where the enzyme membrane could latch on. Recently, polymeric aldehydes have gained popularity as they can provide more stability, reusability, and a larger extent of enzyme loading through possessing a higher density of functional groups to capture the enzyme. , Darain et al. improved the HRP immobilization technique by introducing reactive functional groups on the surface using a combination of (3-amino­propyl)­triethoxy­silane (APTES) and GA and immobilized HRP on a poly­(methyl methacrylate) channel for the detection of goat immunoglobulin G (IgG). However, this system was still not completely free from clogging issues. Later, Jannat and Yang tried to modify flexible tubing in a similar manner, comparing two different cross-linkers, where organo­silanes were used to create a two-dimensional amine network, followed by the use of different aldehydes as cross-linkers to provide functional groups to effectively capture the enzymes on the surface. Although the developed immobilization strategy was very robust for enzyme immobilization, introducing functional groups on an inert surface was challenging. Therefore, there is a need for a detailed study to find a suitable substrate for enzyme immobilization.

In this work, a comparative study was developed to find a ready-to-use substrate for HRP enzyme immobilization. For this purpose, HRP-immobilized bioreactors were prepared on different substrates. Then, their performance for phenol degradation was checked to find suitable substrate(s). First, the specific surfaces, i.e., the inner surface of silicone tubing and glass slides, were modified using 5% APTES to prepare an amine-terminated surface, followed by treatment with two different aldehydes, dextran polyaldehyde (DPA) and glutaraldehyde (GA), for cross-linking. Next, HRP was immobilized onto the modified surfaces. Afterward, to find suitable substrate(s) for enzyme immobilization, the activity retention, reusability, and stability of the immobilized enzyme were checked by using phenol degradation. Finally, the performance of the chemically treated substrates as ready-to-use products for enzyme immobilization was compared by storing them for a prolonged time before the introduction of HRP.

Experimental Section

Materials

(3-Aminopropyl)­triethoxysilane (APTES) (99%), glutaraldehyde (GA) (50% aqueous solution), horseradish peroxidase (HRP) type VI-A, essentially salt-free, lyophilized powder, 950–2000 units/mg solid (using ABTS), 2,2′-azino-bis­(3-ethylbenzo­thiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS), phosphate-buffered silane (PBS buffer), phenol, dextran from Leuconostoc spp., M r 450,000–650,000 (DPA), and 30% H2O2 were purchased from Merck (Germany) and used as received. Pharmaceutical grade silicone tubing (1/8 in. (3.2 mm) i.d. × 1/4 in. (6.4 mm) o.d.) was purchased from Vanguard Product Corporation (USA), and glass slides (plain, size 25 mm × 75 mm) were purchased from Merck (Germany) and used as received.

Surface Modification for Enzyme Immobilization

The substrates were prepared following a well-established method reported in the literature. Briefly, silicone tubing and glass slides were first cleaned with a soap solution. For cleaning, the substrates were incubated in soap overnight and left on a shaker at 70 rpm. Next, the substrates were cleaned rigorously with deionized water. Subsequently, the substrates were submerged in an aqueous solution containing 5% APTES at 50 °C in a water bath for 1 h. After incubation, the substrates were rinsed with deionized water. Then, they were heated in a drying oven at 100 °C for 3 h to cross-link APTES and prepare the amine-terminated surface. Afterward, some of the substrates were incubated in 5% dextran polyaldehyde (DPA) solution overnight to introduce aldehyde groups. Similarly, 5% glutaraldehyde (GA) solution was also used to prepare aldehyde-terminated surfaces. These substrates were then rinsed with deionized water and heated in a drying oven at 100 °C for 3 h. These aldehyde-terminated surfaces can be used for enzyme immobilization. Figure shows the step-by-step preparation of an enzyme-immobilized surface.

Step-by-step enzyme immobilization technique on glass slide or silicone tubing.

Activity of Immobilized HRP Enzyme

For HRP immobilization, 1 mg/mL of HRP solution was prepared in PBS buffer (0.1×, pH 5.0). Equal areas of DPA-modified and GA-modified substrates were incubated with 50 μL of HRP solution to immobilize the enzyme on the surface. The samples were left in a humidified chamber overnight, followed by washing of the samples with deionized water. To check the activity of HRP in immobilized form, 9.1 mM ABTS substrate with 0.3% H2O2 was used, following the standard protocol provided by Sigma-Aldrich. In this method, 290 mL of ABTS and 0.1 mL of H2O2 were mixed and introduced on the substrates (silicone tubing and glass slide) with immobilized HRP, and 2 min was allowed for the reaction to occur. Afterward, the absorbance value measured by a spectro­photometer at 405 nm was used to calculate the total activity (U) of HRP. One unit (1 U) of HRP will oxidize 1 μmol of ABTS per minute. Free enzyme activity was measured in a similar manner. The following equation was used to calculate activity retention of the immobilized enzyme: Activityretention=ActivityofimmobilizedHRPActivityoffreeHRP×100%

As a control, ABTS–H2O2 solution was introduced on clean substrate and only chemically treated substrate, as well. The results obtained from this experiment are shown in the Supporting Information (Figure S1).

Measurement of Enzyme Loading in the Immobilized Format

To immobilize the enzyme, DPA- and GA-functionalized substrates (silicone tubing and glass slide) were filled with 50 μL of HRP enzyme solution (1 mg/mL) and incubated overnight. After immobilization, how much enzyme immobilized on the surface was checked by the method reported in the literature. Briefly, 50 μL of the HRP enzyme solution (1 mg/mL) that was used for immobilization was mixed with PBS buffer to make a 3 mL solution. Then, the concentration of enzyme in the solution was measured by using the absorbance value at 405 nm. A similar test was done for free enzyme before using for incubation. Enzyme loading was determined by the following formula: Enzymeloading=C1−C2C1×100% where C 1 is the initial concentration of HRP enzyme before incubation and C 2 is the concentration of HRP enzyme after incubation in the solution.

Characterization of Enzyme-Immobilized Substrates

Image of immobilized enzymes on GA- and DPA-treated glass slides and tubing were taken by using a scanning electron microscope (ZEISS EVO 18). All SEM samples were kept in a drybox before images were taken. The samples were coated with gold by using a sputter-coater (Quorum). Moreover, FTIR spectra of immobilized enzyme on different substrates were taken within the wavenumber range 400–4000 cm–1 (ThermoScientific Nicolet IS5) after the samples were freeze-dried.

Efficiency of HRP-Immobilized Substrates for Phenol Degradation

To evaluate the performance of HRP-immobilized substrates for phenol degradation, 1 mg/mL phenol and 0.3% H2O2 (reaction mixture) were mixed in a 1:1 volume ratio and introduced on the substrates. The phenol solution was left on the surface for 10 min. Afterward, a UV–vis spectrophotometer was used to record the absorbance at 270 nm for measuring phenol degradation. Since phenol shows an absorption peak at 270 nm, that peak will start to decrease with the formation of other products in the presence of HRP enzyme. , To compare free HRP’s phenol degradation efficiency with that of immobilized HRP, free HRP solution (3 U) was introduced in the phenol solution. Finally, the efficiency of the immobilized enzyme was determined by using the following equation: Degradationefficiency=(1−ConcentrationofphenolafterdegradationInitialconcentrationofphenol)×100%

Reusability of the Immobilized Enzymes

For testing the reusability of the immobilized HRP, several HRP-functionalized bioreactors (both glass slides and tubing) were prepared and stored at 4 °C until used. Samples were taken out and introduced into the phenol–H2O2 mixture for 10 min. Afterward, how much phenol was degraded was measured by measuring the absorbance of the reaction product. This value was noted, and the bioreactor was washed after use. Then, the experiment was carried out multiple times with the same bioreactor. The amount of phenol degradation represents the activity of the immobilized enzyme. Reusability was reported as the normalized activity of the immobilized enzyme, calculated by the following formula: Normalizedactivity=PhenoldegradationbyusedbioreactorPhenoldegradationbyfreshlypreparedbioreactor×100%

Effect of Substrate on Storage Stability of Immobilized Enzyme

Since glass slides and silicone tubing were used as immobilization substrates, tests were performed to determine which substrate provides better storage stability of the immobilized enzyme. To check this criterion, both glass slides and silicone tubing were treated with DPA and GA. The HRP enzyme was then immobilized and stored at 4 °C for about 1 month, after which the substrates were introduced in the phenol–H2O2 mixture for 10 min. Phenol degradation by the samples was calculated by using the following formula and data were compared. Normalizedactivity=PhenoldegradationbystoredbioreactorPhenoldegradationbyfreshlypreparedbioreactor×100%

Stability of the Chemically Treated Substrate for Enzyme Immobilization

To check whether our chemical treatment of the substrates is robust enough for enzyme immobilization, a few substrates with DPA- and GA-terminated surfaces were prepared and stored at room temperature for further use. After 1 month of preparation, 50 μL of HRP enzyme solution (1 mg/mL) was introduced on the substrates and the mixture incubated overnight. Then, these substrates were exposed to the phenol–H2O2 mixture for 10 min. Similar experiments were continued over 4 months with samples prepared every month to check the stability of the chemical treatment. The following formula was used to report the stability of chemically treated substrates in terms of phenol degradation: Normalizedactivity=PhenoldegradationbybioreactorpreparedonstoredchemicallytreatedsubstratePhenoldegradationbyfreshlypreparedbioreactor×100%

Results and Discussion

Enzyme Immobilization on Solid Substrate

The success of enzyme immobilization highly depends on the enzyme loading on the substrate and the activity retention of the immobilized enzyme. Table shows the enzyme loading and total activity of the immobilized enzyme on the substrates. It is found that enzyme loading on a glass slide substrate prepared by GA treatment is 5%, whereas enzyme loading on the silicone tubing surface is 3%. On the other hand, enzyme loading on a DPA-treated glass slide is 6%, and that on silicone tubing is 4%. The values for glass slides are slightly higher than those for silicone tubing regardless of the chemical treatment (GA and DPA functionalization). It is found in the literature that silicone surfaces which contain siloxane groups remain in the polymeric form and this surface needs to be activated by oxygen plasma or ozone treatment to get the silanol group (Si-OH) for enzyme immobilization. Since functional groups produced by oxygen plasma or ozone treatment are short-lived, we followed the absorption techniques reported in the literature. ,, Briefly, silicone tubing was exposed to an aqueous solution of APTES for absorption and silanization, followed by cross-linking of APTES at a high temperature on the surface. However, pharmaceutical grade silicone tubing is slightly hydrophobic, which makes absorption of APTES from aqueous solutions less effective. On the other hand, the glass slide surface contains silanol groups which are readily available for enzyme immobilization. Moreover, silicone tubing suffers from physical property changes after being treated with APTES, and this might affect the immobilization efficiency of the silicone tubing compared to glass slides. Among the chemical treatments, it was found that the DPA-functionalized surfaces showed slightly higher enzyme loading than the GA-terminated surfaces. This is also well discussed in the literature: DPA-functionalized surfaces provide a high-density aldehyde layer compared to GA-terminated surfaces for enzyme immobilization. Therefore, enzyme loading is lowest for the GA-terminated silicone tubing and highest for the DPA-terminated glass slide. The total activity of immobilized HRP on glass slides and silicone tubing was measured following the Sigma-Aldrich enzyme activity assay. It was found that the total activity on DPA-terminated glass slide was 2.52 U, whereas on GA-terminated silicone tubing it was 1.26 U. Since the loading of enzyme on the GA-terminated silicone tubing surface was the least and that on the DPA-terminated glass slide was highest, activity was found accordingly. Because we used pharmaceutical grade silicone tubing which is biocompatible, enzyme activity was not affected by it. Moreover, glass slides are well adapted for handling biomolecule immobilization, which results in better enzyme loading along with activity retention. Therefore, it can be said that enzyme activity was only dependent on enzyme loading regardless of the substrate used.

Next, SEM images of enzyme-immobilized substrates were taken (shown in Figure ). Figures a and b show the images of immobilized HRP on silicone tubing surfaces. It is visible from the images that some cracks were developed on the surface regardless of the chemical treatment (DPA or GA). Although higher enzyme loading is obtained on DPA-treated surfaces (Table ), cracks that develop may cause leakage from the surface during exposure to the substrate and result in reduced activity. This hypothesis is proved in our experiments (Table ), where we found the total enzyme activity for DPA-treated tubing to be 1.68 U, which is lower than the activity for DPA-treated glass slides (2.52 U). Figures c and d show the images of immobilized HRP on glass slide surfaces. It is seen that no visible cracks developed on the glass slides, and HRP can attach on the surface regardless of the treatment (DPA or GA). In this case, we also found that enzyme loading was higher on DPA-treated surfaces (Table . This might be because DPA has more aldehyde groups than GA to anchor the enzymes on the surface.

Finally, Figure shows the FTIR analysis of the enzyme-immobilized substrates (HRP-immobilized bioreactors). This analysis was carried out to verify the presence of HRP on the substrates and the enzyme immobilization mechanism by checking the presence of functional groups. It is found that all the samples showed peaks for amide I (1650–1600 cm–1) and amide II (1470–1400 cm–1) stretching vibrations, which confirm the presence of characteristic protein secondary structures. Amide I produces stretching vibrations due to the presence of a carbonyl (CO) bond within the amide group, while amide II involves a combination of N–H bending and C–N stretching vibrations. Moreover, the absorption peaks around 1100–1000 and 800 cm–1 confirm the presence of the Si–O–Si bonds’ stretching and bending vibrations, respectively. This Si–O–Si was formed due to the cross-linking of APTES molecules which forms Si–O–Si bonds on both the glass slide and silicone tubing surfaces. , Finally, the presence of a peak around 1690–1650 cm–1 confirms the presence of a Schiff base (imine bond CN), which is formed due to the reaction between APTES and the aldehyde groups of DPA or GA. This absorption band is due to the stretching vibration of the CN double bond, which sometimes produces a weak peak in the spectra, evident in those for the DPA glass slide and GA silicone tubing substrate in Figure . This might also be attributable to other bond formation by the nitrogen atom present in the imine group. Moreover, the imine group produces C–H stretching vibrations which appear around 3100 cm–1. Therefore, FTIR spectral analysis also confirms the covalent immobilization of the HRP enzyme on the glass slide and silicone tubing substrates.

Phenol Degradation by HRP-Immobilized Bioreactors

To check the performance of prepared bioreactors, a phenol–H2O2 mixture was introduced on them, and the efficiency of the substrates for phenol degradation was determined. Table shows the efficiency of the immobilized substrates in terms of phenol degradation. It was found that 10% phenol was degraded when GA-treated silicone tubing was used, and 13% phenol was degraded when DPA-treated tubing was used. On the other hand, the GA-treated glass slide provided 17% phenol degradation, and the DPA-treated glass slide provided 21% phenol degradation. It is found in the literature that immobilized HRP (0.67 U/mL) can degrade about 96% phenol (2 mM) in 6 h. Since the reaction time was only 10 min here, a maximum of 21% phenol (1 mg/mL) was degraded by the DPA-functionalized glass slide substrate, which is promising compared to existing studies. It was found that free enzymes can degrade only 15% phenol even though they had higher activity (3 U) than any immobilized enzyme. This is because phenol produces more phenoxy radicals and polymeric compounds, which usually distort the active site of the enzyme. This active site distortion is more prominent in the free enzyme than in the immobilized ones since active sites are freely floating in the free enzyme-containing reaction mixture.

Reusability of Bioreactors Prepared on Glass Slide and Silicone Tubing Substrate

One of the most important criteria for enzyme immobilization is to ensure reusability. Since enzymes are expensive, using homogeneous mixtures of enzymes is not cost-effective; therefore, immobilizing enzymes can promote their reusability. In this experiment, glass slide substrates with a DPA-terminated surface and GA-terminated surface were taken and HRP enzyme was immobilized. Figure shows the reusability of the HRP enzyme-immobilized bioreactors prepared on DPA- and GA-terminated surfaces over 8 cycles. This experiment was repeated three times with three different HRP enzyme-immobilized bioreactors prepared on DPA- and GA-terminated surfaces. It was found that the activity of the immobilized enzyme was 100% over the period of 8 cycles in the case of the DPA-terminated substrate. The slight fluctuation during the fourth and sixth cycles might be because of some experimental errors. It was also found that the activity of the immobilized enzyme on the GA-terminated surface was almost 100% over the period of 8 cycles. Therefore, it can be said that glass slides acts as suitable substrates for HRP enzyme immobilization regardless of the cross-linker used for substrate preparation.

Reusability of the enzymatic bioreactor when HRP enzyme was immobilized on DPA- and GA-functionalized glass slides. Reusability was measured in terms of normalized activity of enzyme, where phenol degradation by the used sample was compared with that obtained with the freshly prepared sample. Each experiment was repeated 3 times.

Similar to glass slide bioreactors, DPA- and GA-terminated silicone tubing substrates were taken for HRP immobilization and tested for the reusability of the reactor. Figure shows the reusability of the tubing bioreactor over the period of 8 cycles of use. It was found that the HRP enzyme-immobilized bioreactor prepared on DPA-functionalized tubing retained 100% activity over the first 5 cycles of use. However, the activity decreased gradually from the sixth cycle and reached 91.5% after 8 cycles of use. Similarly, the HRP-immobilized bioreactor on GA-functionalized tubing showed decreasing activity with cycles of use, as well. It was found that this sample retained 97% activity over 4 cycles of use, which decreased to 93% during the fifth cycle. This activity reduction continued and reached 91.5% after 8 cycles of use. Therefore, it is evident that enzymatic bioreactors prepared on tubing substrate show reduced activity with the increasing number of uses regardless of the cross-linker used for substrate preparation. This might be because of leaching of the immobilized enzyme or activity loss with time. Moreover, silicone tubing has a tendency to lose chemical stability, which might cause enzyme activity loss. Therefore, glass slides provide the most suitable substrate if reusability of the bioreactor is required.

Effect of Solid Substrate on Immobilized Enzyme Activity during Storage

It is found that both DPA- and GA-functionalized tubing substrates lose activity with time (Figure ), whereas glass slide substrates retain almost 100% activity (Figure ) over the whole duration of use. Figure shows the effect of the substrate, which can keep the immobilized enzyme more active even after storage for a long time. To check the enzyme-immobilized substrates’ long-term stability, normalized activity of the enzyme-functionalized substrates was measured, where phenol degradation by the stored substrate was compared with that that obtained with the freshly prepared substrate. It was found that immobilized HRP on both GA- and DPA-terminated glass slides retained 100% activity even after storage for 1 month. On the other hand, immobilized enzyme on DPA- and GA-terminated tubing lost activity over the period of storage and retained 91% activity after 1 month of storage. It was already mentioned that silicone tubing developed cracks on the surface after APTES treatment, which further result in HRP being loosely bound upon immobilization (Figure ). Therefore, some enzymes might leak from the silicone tubing surface during storage for prolonged periods of time.

Stability of Substrate as a Ready-to-Use Platform for Bioreactor Preparation

The main purpose of this research is to find a suitable substrate for enzyme immobilization. To find a suitable substrate, a comparison between glass slides and silicone tubing was made in terms of their phenol degradation efficiency during reuse and storage. Different surface preparation techniques (GA- or DPA-terminated surface) were also applied. From the studies discussed in the previous sections, it is proved that glass slides act as better substrates for enzyme immobilization than silicone tubing in terms of enzyme loading, activity retention, reusability, etc. Therefore, we wanted to check this substrate’s performance as a ready-to-use platform for bioreactor preparation. Since most of the enzymes have an amine group, they can form an imine bond (-CN-) with aldehyde if exposed to an aldehyde-terminated surface and be immobilize in a stable manner. Therefore, it would be great if an aldehyde-terminated surface could be provided as a ready-to-use substrate for enzyme immobilization. It would minimize the work involved in surface modification before enzyme immobilization. To test this hypothesis, we prepared some chemically treated glass slides (DPA- and GA-terminated) and stored them for a few months before the HRP immobilization. Figure shows the stability of chemically treated surfaces for enzyme immobilization in terms of phenol degradation. It was found that the DPA-terminated surface provided 100% activity retention of immobilized HRP, whereas the GA-terminated surface lost activity continuously from 100% to about 90% over the storage period of 4 months. It is evident that DPA-terminated surfaces provide better stability than those with GA and can be ready-to-use substrates for enzyme immobilization over a period of 4 months. This is because DPA has a complex structure with multiple aldehyde groups, and it forms a polymeric network on the surface that cannot be removed from the surface. On the other hand, GA contains only two aldehyde groups, of which one is used to form an imine bond with the surface amine and the other remains to form a covalent bond with the enzyme. Therefore, GA cannot form polymerized complex structures and can easily be removed from the surface. The disappearance of aldehyde will result in less enzyme immobilization since there are not enough aldehyde groups to facilitate covalent bond formation with enzyme molecules.

Conclusions

This work sought to develop a ready-to-use substrate for enzyme immobilization. First, chemical treatment was done with either GA or DPA on the inner surface of APTES-terminated flexible silicone tubing and glass slides to get aldehyde-terminated surfaces, and then HRP enzyme was immobilized on these substrates. The performance of these enzyme-immobilized bioreactors was checked by measuring phenol degradation. It is found that glass slides work as a better substrates than silicone tubing since glass slide bioreactors can retain 100% activity of immobilized HRP after usage of 8 cycles and storage of 1 month. To identify suitable ready-to-use substrates for enzyme immobilization, the storage stability of chemically modified glass slides (both DPA- and GA-terminated surfaces) before enzyme immobilization was studied. It is reported that a DPA-functionalized glass surface could immobilize HRP with 100% of activity throughout storage for 4 months, which is evident from its phenol degradation performance. Nowadays, enzymatic bioreactors are popular because of their specific nature, and they are frequently used in many industries, including pharmaceutical industries, wastewater treatment plants, and so on. However, multiple steps are necessary for their preparation, which makes this technology tedious for real-life applications. Therefore, a commercially available, ready-to-use substrate would be helpful for preparation of the enzymatic bioreactor in a single step right before the use. In conclusion, this study will be helpful for preparing suitable ready-to-use substrates to commercialize that can be stored for prolonged periods, after which enzyme can be immobilized in one step as needed.
