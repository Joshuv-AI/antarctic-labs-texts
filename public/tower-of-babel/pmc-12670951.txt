Implementation of a stepped anti-inflammatory reliever therapy intervention with budesonide-formoterol 160/4·5 mcg by Turbuhaler versus usual care for adults presenting during exacerbations of obstructive lung disease suggestive of asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease in a resource-limited setting: an open-label, cluster randomised trial
Authors: Greg J Fox, Ngo Quy Chau, Pham Ngoc Yen, Vu Van Giap, Qingbin Li, Stephen Jan, Erick Huang, Joel Negin, Nguyen Thu Anh, Helen Reddel, Guy B Marks
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundIn resource-limited countries, chronic respiratory disease is common, and access to diagnosis and to the multiple medications in asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease guidelines is limited. Building on a previous pilot study, we hypothesised that implementation of a simple stepped anti-inflammatory reliever (AIR) approach with budesonide-formoterol in patients presenting with obstructive airways disease and recurrent exacerbations in resource-limited settings would reduce exacerbations compared to usual care.MethodsWe conducted a 52-week open-label, cluster randomised controlled trial among adults presenting with recurrent acute respiratory symptoms in Vietnam. 40 district health facilities were assigned to usual care or intervention, which comprised budesonide-formoterol 160/4·5 mcg Turbuhaler: Step 1, one inhalation as-needed for symptoms; Step 2, one inhalation twice-daily plus one inhalation as-needed; Step 3, referral for higher-level care. This was accompanied by quarterly clinical review. Primary outcome was the proportion of participants with at least one moderate or severe exacerbation over 12-months. Secondary outcomes included respiratory hospitalisations, grade 3-4 adverse events, and all-cause mortality. The trial was registered on ANZCTR.org.au (identifier ACTRN12620000649910).FindingsAnalysis included 3095 participants recruited between 21 July 2020 and 11 January 2022 (control:1421 vs. intervention: 1674; 75·4% [2334/3095] males; median age 63 years [IQR 55–70]). For the primary outcome, 36·0% (511/1421) control participants had at least one exacerbation during follow-up compared to 28·6% (478/1674) intervention participants (relative risk (RR) 0·794; 95% CI 0·649–0·971; p = 0·03); there was no significant interaction with baseline blood eosinophil count (β = −0·133; 95% CI −0·343 to 0·076; p = 0·21). For the secondary outcomes, fewer participants were hospitalised in the intervention group (control: 24·1% [342/1421] vs. intervention: 17·4% [292/1674]; RR 0·737, 95% CI 0·544–0·998; p = 0·05), and all-cause mortality was similar between groups (control: 3·7% [53/1421] vs. intervention: 3·4% [57/1674]; RR 0·887, 95% CI 0·548–1·435; p = 0·62). There was no significant difference in grade 3-4 adverse events (control: 6·3% [90/1421] vs. intervention: 6·4% [107/1674]; RR 1·004, 95% CI 0·747–1·349). Pneumonia risk was similar (control: 0·8% [11/1421] vs. intervention: 0·4% [7/1674]; RR 0·417, 95% CI 0·135–1·286).InterpretationAmong participants presenting with recurrent exacerbations of undifferentiated chronic respiratory disease, a stepped anti-inflammatory reliever approach was associated with fewer exacerbations and hospitalisations than usual care. This approach may represent a feasible population-level strategy to reduce the global burden of chronic respiratory disease.FundingThis trial was funded by the National Health and Medical Research Council of Australia. The study sponsor was the Woolcock Institute of Medical Research. Budesonide-formoterol was provided by AstraZeneca.Keywords: Asthma, Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, Exacerbations, Anti-inflammatory reliever, Maintenance and reliever therapy, Budesonide-formoterol

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed using the terms “randomised clinical trial”, “exacerbations”, “COPD”, “asthma” and “inhaled corticosteroids” on 15 February 2025 for articles published in English only. Clinical trials and meta-analyses demonstrated the effectiveness in asthma of a combination of inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) with formoterol (a rapidly-acting long-acting beta-agonist [LABA] with a wide dose response for safety and efficacy) reliever alone or as maintenance-and-reliever therapy (MART) for reducing severe asthma exacerbations and urgent health care utilisation. A randomised trial of MART for COPD demonstrated similar frequency of exacerbations compared to conventional ICS-LABA plus as-needed SABA, with a lower cumulative dose of ICS. The effectiveness of using an ICS-formoterol anti-inflammatory reliever approach for managing undifferentiated obstructive airways disease has not been evaluated in resource-limited settings, nor has it been initiated during exacerbations.

Added value of this study

On the background of qualitative and quantitative research about diagnostic and treatment pathways in Vietnam, and following a pilot study, we conducted a cluster randomised controlled implementation trial (n = 3095). We found that patients presenting in a resource-limited setting with acute respiratory symptoms consistent with an exacerbation of obstructive airways disease, and with a history of previous episodes, were less likely to experience another exacerbation if they received a package of care including stepped anti-inflammatory reliever treatment with low-dose budesonide-formoterol, accompanied by three-monthly clinic reviews, compared with those treated with usual care. The frequency of hospitalisations was reduced at 12 months with the intervention, with no difference in mortality. Interaction analyses demonstrated no evidence of an interaction by baseline blood eosinophil count, or for other prespecified subgroups.

Implications of all the available evidence

In community-level health facilities in resource-limited settings, a package of care comprising a stepped treatment approach with ICS-formoterol dry powder inhaler, and quarterly clinic reviews, can be feasibly and safely implemented among patients presenting during recurrent exacerbations of undifferentiated obstructive airways disease. This anti-inflammatory reliever approach reduces the risk of further exacerbations, with no increase in adverse effects. The findings support the use of this simple approach in primary care facilities to reduce the burden of chronic respiratory disease in resource-limited settings.

Introduction

Asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) affect almost half a billion people worldwide,1 most of whom live in low-and middle-income countries. Globally, 96% of asthma deaths and 84% of COPD deaths occur in these countries.1 Both conditions are heterogeneous, and although they can share some pathological and clinical features, some of their causes and some pathological and physiological features are distinct.2 Furthermore, treatment guidelines for asthma and COPD are different, and each involves several medications.3,4 However, distinguishing between asthma and COPD is often challenging for clinicians and this can be a barrier to timely diagnosis and treatment, particularly in community-level health facilities in resource-limited settings5,6 where patients frequently present during an acute exacerbation.7,8 In these settings, use of clinical algorithms that integrate treatment for asthma and COPD may simplify clinical decision-making and increase access to effective therapies.

In people with asthma, inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) markedly reduce severe exacerbations, even in patients with infrequent symptoms. In people with COPD, treatment starts with long-acting beta-agonists (LABA) and long-acting muscarinic antagonists (LAMA), with ICS added for patients with a history of exacerbations, particularly if blood eosinophil count is ≥0·3 × 109/L.3 However, important factors limiting the use of these medicines in both asthma and COPD include the reluctance of many clinicians to prescribe, and patients to take, regular ICS-containing therapy,7 the complexity of conventional diagnostic and treatment approaches, the multiplicity of inhaler devices,9 their lack of availability in low- and middle-income countries,9,10 and patients’ reliance instead on short-acting beta2-agonist (SABA) inhalers or oral therapies. In both asthma and COPD, SABA over-use is associated with increased risk of exacerbations and death.11,12 Furthermore, the cumulative risks of oral corticosteroids, even when used only occasionally,13 prompt treatment strategies to reduce their use. From an environmental perspective, SABA delivered by pressurised metered dose inhaler (pMDI) contributes most of the carbon footprint of respiratory care.14

Major progress has been achieved in addressing these issues in asthma, with strong evidence that use of a combination of ICS with formoterol (a rapid-acting, full agonist LABA with efficacy and safety over a wide dose range) as an anti-inflammatory reliever (‘AIR’), alone or as maintenance-and-reliever therapy (MART), substantially reduces the risk of severe exacerbations compared with SABA-based regimens, with lower mean ICS doses.15,16 This approach is simple, requiring only a single medication across treatment steps. A randomised controlled trial of MART in COPD showed a similar exacerbation rate and lower total ICS dose compared to conventional ICS-LABA with as-needed SABA.17

The substantial overlap between the inhaled therapies recommended for asthma and COPD,3,4 the simplicity of the AIR approach, and its effectiveness in phenotypes with either low or high Type-2 inflammation,18, 19, 20 suggest that common treatment algorithms could simplify care in resource-limited settings, while reducing exacerbation frequency. However, the effectiveness of this approach among patients with undifferentiated airways disease in resource-limited settings has not been evaluated, in comparison to existing approaches to clinical care, nor its initiation in the setting of an exacerbation.

Based on our quantitative and qualitative investigation of diagnostic and prescribing practices in Vietnam, and a pilot study,7,21,22 we designed the Vietnam COPD, Asthma and Prevention of Smoking (VCAPS4) trial. The aim of this cluster randomised implementation study was to evaluate the effectiveness of a stepped algorithm using AIR therapy for the treatment of undifferentiated airways disease among patients presenting to healthcare facilities in Vietnam.

Methods

Study design and participants

After conducting a single-arm pilot study (n = 313),22 we conducted an open-label, 52-week cluster randomised controlled implementation trial to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of AIR therapy with budesonide-formoterol (160/4·5mcg budesonide-formoterol, Symbicort Turbuhaler, AstraZeneca) given by a stepped algorithm (Figure S1), compared with usual care, in patients presenting with acute respiratory symptoms. The study was conducted in 40 district health centres in Vietnam, a middle-income country with limited access to effective therapies for chronic respiratory disease.7,21,22 Included health facilities were government-run District Health Centres, which deliver primary care for respiratory illness, located in one of the four participating provinces and less than 2 h by road from the Provincial Centre.

Ethics

The study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the University of Sydney (2019/334) and the Institutional Review Board at the Ministry of Health Vietnam (7752/QD-BYT). An Independent Data Monitoring Committee reviewed all serious adverse events and adverse events of special interest. The protocol was prospectively registered (ANZCTR ACTRN12620000649910). Consent for a site to participate in the study was provided by the Director of the Health Centre. All participants provided written informed consent.

Randomisation and masking

The unit of randomisation was the District Health Centre. Facilities (clusters) were randomised 1:1 to intervention or control groups using simple randomisation, stratified by province. Randomisation was performed by a person who was not engaged in study implementation and was blinded to the identity of the districts. Staff at each facility were trained only in the methods relevant to their group allocation, to avoid contamination. Participants and study staff were not blinded to group allocation due to the nature of the intervention.

Procedures

Patients aged ≥12 years attending study sites with acute respiratory symptoms (cough, dyspnea, wheeze and/or chest tightness) were eligible for screening if they also had ≥1 prior episode of respiratory symptoms requiring assessment by a healthcare facility in the prior 24 months. Eligibility for enrolment included a symptom score ≥3/9 on a screening questionnaire (Table S1, Supplementary Methods), 23 or airflow limitation (FEV1/FVC ratio <0·7 or, if spirometry could not be performed, a peak expiratory flow of <80% predicted). Exclusion criteria were hypoxaemia requiring supplemental oxygen, tuberculosis, chronic sputum production, known pregnancy, allergy to study drugs, or a probable alternative diagnosis (Table S2). In the control group, participants received usual care from their health facility, with collection of baseline data at enrolment, and scheduled follow-up by telephone for outcomes data. In the intervention group, treatment comprised a pragmatic stepped algorithm using budesonide-formoterol 160/4·5mcg Turbuhaler (Figure S1). At enrolment, all participants were instructed to take budesonide-formoterol one inhalation whenever needed for symptom relief (Step 1), up to a maximum of 12 inhalations in any 24-h period. They were trained in use of the inhaler and given a printed action plan. At 28 days, and at each subsequent visit, symptom control (Respiratory Symptom Questionnaire, RSQ, Table S3), exacerbations, and inhaler technique were assessed. Participants having ≥1 exacerbation since the previous visit or with poor symptom control (RSQ score ≥8/16), were prescribed Step 2 (MART), one inhalation twice-daily plus 1 inhalation as needed. Participants with an exacerbation or poor symptom control while on Step 2 were referred to specialist care at a provincial hospital (Step 3). The treating physician could prescribe additional medications based on clinical need. Clinical assessments were 3-monthly, or with dispensing of every second inhaler, whichever was sooner. Participants failing to attend a scheduled visit were telephoned to assess symptom control and exacerbations.

Average inhaler usage was calculated from dose counters of returned inhalers; if it was >6 inhalations/day, the patient was counselled by a doctor. If excessive use continued for another month, budesonide-formoterol was ceased.

Blood was drawn at the baseline visit for eosinophil count. A clinical assessment at the clinic was scheduled 28 days post-enrolment. Additional follow-up assessments for study outcomes were performed by telephone by study staff at 1, 3, 6, 9 and 12 months (Figure S2).

Outcomes

The primary study endpoint, evaluated at the individual participant level, was the proportion of participants with ≥1 moderate-severe exacerbations, defined as acute worsening of respiratory symptoms for which the participant had an unscheduled clinic visit, took oral corticosteroids, or attended an emergency department, over 12 months from enrolment. Data for calculating the primary outcome was collected by telephone at 1, 3, 6, 9 and 12 months in both groups.

Secondary outcomes included all-cause mortality, the proportion of participants hospitalised with exacerbations, and in the intervention group only, the proportion of participants assessed by a provincial/central health facility to determine the ‘step’ of treatment at 3, 6, 9 and 12 months after enrolment. One of the protocol-specified secondary outcomes was the cumulative 12-month oral prednisone dose (mg). Due to the pragmatic design, this information could not be reliably collected by telephone and was therefore not reported. The main safety outcome was the proportion of participants who had Grade 3-4 adverse events in both groups.

Statistics

The calculated sample size required was 3393 participants. We assumed that, at baseline, 80% of participants would satisfy the airflow limitation criteria, with 20% qualifying by the screening questionnaire. For control group participants, we assumed that 37·8% of those with airflow limitation24 and 17% with possible asthma25 would have ≥1 moderate-severe exacerbation during 12 months’ follow-up, giving an estimated overall mean event rate of 30% in the control group. This would enable detection of a relative risk (RR) of 0·6 or less for the primary outcome, based on an expected RR of 0·3 for people with asthma25 and 0·8 for people with COPD.26 Other assumptions were alpha of 0·05, power of 80%, 10% loss to follow-up and intraclass correlation for clusters of 0·04.27

Statistical analyses adhered to a pre-specified statistical analysis plan. The primary and secondary analyses were undertaken in the intention-to-treat (ITT) population. Relative risks and 95% CIs were derived from a mixed-effects log-binomial regression model with a robust variance estimator, accounting for clustering at site level as a random intercept. The intraclass correlation coefficient measured the proportion of variance explained by the clustering structure. Formal statistical adjustments for multiplicity were not made. The model fit was evaluated using likelihood ratio test, the Akaike information criterion, and Pearson's residual plots. A Kaplan–Meier plot was generated to display the cumulative proportion of participants free from exacerbation over time by treatment group. The proportion of participants assessed by a provincial/central health facility to determine treatment ‘step’ was analysed descriptively in the intervention group.

The primary results were unadjusted, given the randomised controlled trial design. As a sensitivity analysis, an adjusted multivariable model was used to re-estimate the primary outcome. To guide covariate selection and avoid collider bias, a Directed Acyclic Graph was constructed (Figure S3) based on biological and clinical knowledge. The final model included treatment arm and seven covariates: age, sex, smoking status at enrolment, recent use of inhaled corticosteroids (past 30 days), blood eosinophil levels at enrolment, number of health facility visits for respiratory issues in the past 24 months, and province.

Medication use was summarized using counts and percentages descriptively at prespecified timepoints, by treatment group. In the control group, medication data were collected at baseline and 1 month. In the intervention group, data were collected at baseline, 1 month, and 12 months. Study-supplied budesonide–formoterol was recorded separately for the intervention group, while all other respiratory medications were categorized by therapeutic class for both groups. Average daily budesonide-formoterol use over the 12-month period in the intervention group was analysed descriptively.

As part of the 2 × 2 factorial cluster randomised trial, a smoking cessation intervention was allocated to District Health Centres independently of allocation to the respiratory disease intervention. The smoking cessation component of the trial will be reported separately. The potential effect of randomisation to the smoking intervention was evaluated by inclusion of an interaction term between the respiratory disease intervention and a 3-level factor: non-smoker; smoker with smoking cessation intervention; smoker without smoking cessation intervention.

For safety analyses, outcomes were summarised by counts and proportions within each group. Relative risks with 95% CIs were estimated for the main safety outcome (grade 3–4 adverse events) and selected others (any adverse events (grade 1–5) as sensitivity and adverse events of special interest), while all remaining safety outcomes were reported descriptively.

In accordance with the statistical analysis plan, interaction analyses were conducted on pre-specified subgroup variables selected for their clinical relevance. Where the p-value for the interaction term was less than 0·1, results for the intervention effect would be presented separately by each category of the effect modifier. Post hoc exploratory analyses included changes in the Modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) Dyspnea grade and the Respiratory Symptoms Questionnaire (RSQ) score (mixed-effects linear models), exacerbation rates (negative binomial regression), and time to first exacerbation (Cox regression). Self-reported SARS-CoV-2 incidence was compared descriptively between groups.

Given the cluster-randomised design, we evaluated the potential for informative cluster size by summarising the cluster-level participant baseline characteristics and applied bias-corrected generalized estimating equation (GEE) methods with robust standard errors for sensitivity. Further details are provided in the Supplementary Appendix. All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata 18 software (StataCorp, 2023).

Role of the funding source

This trial was funded by the Australian National Health and Medical Research Council. In-kind support was provided by AstraZeneca, in the form of budesonide-formoterol used in the trial. Neither organisation had any role in the design, data collection, analysis, interpretation of data, writing of the report or decision to submit the paper for publication.

Results

Forty District Health Centres were randomised to the intervention (n = 20) and control (n = 20) groups. Overall, 3095 participants were recruited between 21 July 2020 and 11 January 2022 (control: N = 1421; intervention: N = 1674). Fig. 1 shows the consort diagram. There were no clinically important differences in demographic and baseline clinical characteristics between randomisation groups (Table 1, Table S6). Overall, 75% (2334/3095) of participants were male, and median age was 63 years (IQR 55–70). Current smoking was reported by 29·6% (915/3095) participants, and 41·4% (1281/3095) reported a prior diagnosis of COPD. At enrolment, 64·5% (1997/3095) of participants had FEV1/FVC <0·7, 92·9% (2875/3095) had screening questionnaire score ≥3 (median 6, IQR 4–7), 22·2% (688/3095) had used any ICS in the previous 30 days, and 46·2% (1429/3095) had a blood eosinophil count of 0·3 × 109/L or more; the median number of episodes of respiratory symptoms requiring assessment by a healthcare facility in the preceding 24 months was 4 (IQR 2–10; Table 1).

For the secondary outcomes, fewer participants were hospitalised in the intervention group than in the control group (control: 24·1% [342/1421] vs. intervention: 17·4% [292/1674]; RR 0·737, 95% CI 0·544–0·998; p = 0·05; Table S2). There was no significant difference in all-cause mortality (control: 3·7% [53/1421] vs. intervention: 3·4% [57/1674]; RR 0·887, 95% CI 0·548–1·435; p = 0·62) between the two groups. The proportion of intervention participants on each step of treatment at scheduled study visits is shown in Figure S4 and Table S7. At 12 months, 67·3% (1127/1674) of participants were on Step 1 (as-needed-only budesonide-formoterol). Very few participants (<1% [3/1674]) were referred for higher level care (Step 3).

Medication prescription in the control group was collected at in-person visits, which occurred at baseline and 1 month in the control group; and at baseline, 1 month and 12 months in the intervention group. At baseline, 33·4% (474/1421) of control participants were prescribed inhaled SABA and 17·7% (251/1421) were prescribed any ICS, decreasing to 24·3% (346/1421) and 11·5% (163/1421) respectively at 1 month. At 1 month and at 12 months, almost 89·0% (1490/1674) of Intervention participants were still using the study budesonide-formoterol (Table 3). The effect of the intervention was consistent in sensitivity analyses, including the adjusted analysis and the robustness test for informative cluster size (Supplementary Results, Table S12, Table S16). Interaction analyses indicated no evidence that the effect of the intervention differed in any of the prespecified subgroups, including baseline blood eosinophil count ≥0·3 × 109/L vs. < 0·3 × 109/L (Figure S7, Table S13). Post-hoc exploratory analyses included changes in mMRC grade, which showed fewer individuals in the intervention group experienced breathlessness at 12 months (Figure S5) and RSQ, which showed no difference between groups over time (Figure S6). Analyses of exacerbation rates and time to first exacerbation, by treatment group are shown in Table S14. Self-reported SARS-CoV-2 incidence was similar between groups (Table S11).

Discussion

This cluster randomised controlled implementation trial found that, among patients presenting in a resource-limited setting with acute symptoms consistent with an exacerbation of obstructive airways disease, and with a history of previous such episodes, those who were treated with a stepped anti-inflammatory reliever algorithm with low-dose budesonide-formoterol, and three-monthly clinic reviews, were less likely to experience another exacerbation or be hospitalised for acute respiratory symptoms, than those treated with the local standard of care. No difference was observed in mortality or serious adverse events over the twelve-month follow-up period.

This study further extends the anti-inflammatory reliever (AIR) approach using combination low-dose ICS-formoterol, initially developed as maintenance and reliever therapy (i.e. MART),15 and later expanded to as-needed-only ICS-formoterol for mild asthma.4,16 Since 2019, this simple stepped approach has been recommended by the Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) as the preferred evidence-based therapy across treatment steps, because of the significant reduction in severe exacerbations compared with conventional SABA-based therapy and the simplicity of the single-inhaler approach across treatment steps4,14; it also has a low carbon footprint.14,30,31 MART has also been evaluated in one study in COPD.17 In the present study, we evaluated a simple three-step anti-inflammatory reliever algorithm in patients with frequent exacerbations of undifferentiated respiratory disease. Participants receiving the intervention were followed up at clinics every three months. Critically, this was conducted under real-world conditions in Vietnam, a resource-limited setting, where presentations for respiratory symptoms mostly occur only during acute episodes, and the capacity to distinguish between different forms of respiratory disease is limited.7 The findings of this study are most applicable to healthcare facilities in LMICs where diagnostic capacity is limited. Ongoing efforts to strengthen diagnostic capability remains an important priority in these settings.

Previous AIR studies have shown that the magnitude of benefit varies with the population and comparator treatment. In mild asthma, AIR-only therapy reduces severe exacerbations and emergency visits/hospitalisations by 65% compared with SABA alone.16 In moderate-severe asthma, the reduction in severe exacerbations and in hospitalisations with MART is more modest but achieved with lower ICS doses than with SABA-based regimens.15 In a COPD study, exacerbation risk was similar, and ICS doses lower, with MART versus conventional high-dose ICS-LABA therapy with SABA reliever.17 In the present study, we found an overall 21% reduction in moderate-to-severe exacerbations compared with usual care. Median age was 63 years, and prevalence of current smoking was high, suggesting that a minority of participants had typical asthma, which is more likely to be steroid responsive than COPD. In asthma, AIR-only and MART reduce severe exacerbations in patients with T2-high or T2-low biomarkers.18, 19, 20 Current COPD guidelines recommend addition of ICS for patients with frequent exacerbations, particularly if blood eosinophil count is ≥ 0·3 × 109/L,3 but we found no significant interaction in treatment effect for this eosinophil threshold. Importantly, the benefit of MART in asthma is not just an anti-inflammatory effect, as formoterol reliever reduces the risk of severe exacerbations compared with SABA reliever.32 The safety and effectiveness of as-needed formoterol (alone or in combination with budesonide) up to a total of 12 inhalations (54mcg delivered dose) in any 24-h period has been established from multiple studies33,34; no other LABA has these properties. The anti-inflammatory reliever strategy and dry powder inhaler used in this study also provide a very large reduction in the carbon footprint of respiratory care.14,30,31

Over-use of SABA is widespread among people with asthma and COPD and is associated with increased risk of exacerbations and death.11,12 Reassuringly, just 0·4% (8/1870) of participants in the intervention group demonstrated excessive use of budesonide-formoterol (Table S9). Very few intervention participants (2·8% (53/1870) used more than an average total of 4 inhalations/day (>640 mcg budesonide, >24 mcg formoterol). Furthermore, no difference in reported serious adverse events (Grade 3 or 4) nor all-cause mortality was observed during 12-months’ follow-up. Importantly, the risk of pneumonia or tuberculosis was low, and similar between the two groups. Our findings provide reassurance that an undifferentiated approach to treatment of patients with recurrent exacerbations of respiratory disease is feasible, and likely to be safe, in resource-limited settings, even when commenced with as-needed-only therapy in the context of an acute exacerbation, and delivered by a dry powder inhaler. The cost-effectiveness of the trial will also be evaluated.

Intervention studies in patients presenting during an acute respiratory exacerbation are usually limited to asthma or COPD, require an existing confirmed diagnosis, and focus on short-term outcomes, e.g. re-presentation. However, in resource-limited settings, patients often present only during exacerbations, when confirming the diagnosis can be particularly difficult.4,7 Our study demonstrates the opportunity for initiating interventions in patients during exacerbations of obstructive airways disease, in high- and low-resource settings.

Limitations of this trial include the predominance of older adults, consistent with our recent observational study,7 and the high smoking rate, suggesting that asthma is less common than COPD in acute presentations at these healthcare facilities. Nevertheless, we found a 21% reduction in the primary endpoint. Second, exacerbations were self-reported, with data collected at follow-up phone calls. Nevertheless, the same ascertainment method was applied in both groups, minimising potential bias. Third, it is possible that the effect of the intervention could partly be explained by a regression to the mean, as individuals in the intervention group had a higher number of respiratory presentations to health facilities than the control group in the preceding 24 months, However, the difference between groups persisted after adjustment for prior exacerbations. In addition, we were also unable to collect accurate data regarding oral corticosteroid use on account of unlabelled medication dispensing to participants by health facilities. Furthermore, this trial was conducted in just one country, and its findings may not be applicable to all settings. Nevertheless, the inclusion of both rural and urban communities, in the north and south of Vietnam, increase its generalisability. Finally, the absence of blinding due to the open label cluster-randomised design may have influenced management by clinicians.

Strengths of the study included its pragmatic design, the prior quantitative and qualitative research about diagnostic and treatment practices,21,22 a pilot study (n = 313)22 to assess feasibility, identify barriers and optimise study resources, and implementation of the approach in a real-world setting among 40 district healthcare facilities in a resource-limited country. Care in the control group accorded with current practice within the Vietnamese health system, with infrequent ICS use and common oral bronchodilator use7; the study is generalisable to other resource-limited settings.10 Secondly, the primary outcome was highly relevant to this setting, comprising moderate-severe exacerbations that resulted in health care utilisation or oral corticosteroid use. Thirdly, we evaluated interactions by pre-specified baseline variables, but none was significant, including baseline blood eosinophils ≥0·3 × 109/L.

In conclusion, this cluster-randomised implementation trial in a resource-limited setting demonstrated that anti-inflammatory reliever therapy for patients presenting with an exacerbation of undifferentiated obstructive airways disease was associated with fewer subsequent exacerbations and hospitalisations. This may represent a feasible population-level strategy to reduce the global burden of chronic respiratory disease, and may encourage greater access to these essential asthma medicines in low- and middle-income countries.
