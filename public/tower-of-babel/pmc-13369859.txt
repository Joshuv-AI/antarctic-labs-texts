Hot isostatic pressing effects on multiscale mechanical properties and chromium-dependent strength retention in LPBF Fe-based shape memory alloys
Authors: Md Muhiul Islam Muhit, Gabriel Awuku Dzukey, Umesh Gandhi, Ala Qattawi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This work investigates the effect of Hot Isostatic Pressing (HIP) on FeMnAlNi and FeMnAlNiCr shape memory alloys (SMAs) fabricated by Laser Powder Bed Fusion (LPBF). Specimens with and without in-situ laser remelting were HIP-treated at 1150 °C and 150 MPa for 4 h, thereby eliminating most internal porosity and increasing the relative density from ~ 97% to 99.98%. This densification substantially improved ductility, with the Cr-free alloy reaching elongations up to 24%, while the strength and hardness decreased. The addition of ~ 4 at% Cr mitigated these softening effects, enabling higher retention of strength and hardness after HIP. Notably, a remelted Cr-containing sample achieved an excellent property balance with post-HIP indentation-derived equivalent ultimate tensile strength of ~ 1085 MPa, hardness nearly 295 HV, and a modest modulus increase to 161.8 GPa. In contrast, the Cr-free alloy exhibited a larger drop in strength but greater gains in ductility, underscoring a composition-dependent trade-off between strength and plasticity. Nanoindentation testing confirmed these trends, showing reduced nano-hardness and elastic recovery after HIP in both alloys. While Cr additions preserved more of the reversible transformation capacity, pop-out events observed during unloading suggest localized transformation-related deformation during indentation, and that HIP homogenization likely does not suppress pseudoelasticity. Overall, this study bridges a research gap in combining Cr alloying with post-HIP treatment. The results highlight the synergistic role of LPBF parameter control, Cr alloying, and HIP treatment in tailoring Fe-SMAs with near-full density, high strength retention, and reliable ductility.

Introduction

Shape Memory Alloys (SMAs) are unique functional materials that can recover plastic strain via the shape memory effect (SME) and exhibit superelastic behavior due to reversible martensitic phase transformations1–5. These unique behaviors enable SMAs to serve in aerospace actuators, biomedical devices, and civil infrastructure systems1,6. While NiTi based SMAs are the most widely used, Fe-based SMAs (Fe-SMAs) have attracted growing interest as a low-cost alternative with higher transformation temperatures and good workability7–9. Ni-Ti alloys dominate due to their excellent cyclic stability4,10, but their high cost and processing complexity have driven interest in lower-cost alternatives11. Cu-based SMAs were introduced as economical options, yet their brittleness and poor fatigue resistance limited widespread use12–14. Fe-SMAs have therefore gained attention as promising candidates due to their lower cost15–18, good workability, and higher transformation temperatures. Fe-SMAs utilization has been expanded to different alloy systems, including FeMnAlNi, which exhibit large pseudoelastic strains via reversible bcc↔fcc transformations19–21.

However, challenges remain in optimizing the properties of Fe-SMAs for practical use, and additional alloying elements are being investigated to improve performance. In particular, the addition of chromium (Cr) has been reported to influence phase stability and mechanical properties in Fe–Mn-based SMAs15,22,23, potentially enhancing corrosion resistance and strength. Alloying additions such as Cr have been reported to influence the mechanical and thermal stability of Fe-based alloys through solid-solution strengthening and improved resistance to high-temperature degradation, making them of interest for enhancing the performance of FeMnAlNi-based systems. The addition of Cr stabilizes the ferrite phase24, influences martensitic transformations, and enhances corrosion resistance22,25. Felice et al.26 demonstrated that an LPBF-fabricated Fe-17Mn-5Si-10Cr-4Ni alloy achieved ~ 821 MPa fracture stress with 26% fracture strain. Despite these advances, systematic studies on LPBF-fabricated FeMnAlNiCr alloys are scarce, and the combined effect of Cr and advanced post-processing has not been comprehensively addressed.

Widely used additive manufacturing (AM) techniques, such as LPBF, enable the production of complex, near-net-shape SMA components directly from powder feedstock, with minimal waste and high design flexibility27–29. While LPBF typically promotes rapid solidification resulting in refined substructures at the cellular level30,31. It also tends to form coarse columnar grains along the building direction. LPBF parts can exhibit increasing hardness and strength compared to conventionally processed alloys32,33. However, this comes at the cost of ductility, as well as introducing challenges such as porosity, microcracks, and residual stresses34–36. Characterization studies reveal defects including lack-of-fusion voids (~ 100 μm)37, keyhole pores (10–50 μm)38,39, and dense dislocation networks associated with high thermal gradients40. In Fe-SMA parts, these defects and the high thermal stresses from rapid solidification can reduce ductility and functional strain recovery, necessitating post-processing to achieve reliable properties.

Conventional thermal treatments (solution annealing, aging, etc.) have been applied to LPBF or wrought Fe-SMAs with some success in improving ductility and the SME41–43. Niendorf et al.30 showed that annealing/aging enhanced martensitic transformation and strength in FeMnAlNi SMA. Cyclic thermal treatments have refined grain structures and improved superelasticity, though sometimes with hardness loss from abnormal grain growth43. For LPBF Fe–17Mn–5Si–10Cr–4Ni, Kim et al.41 found that annealing at 800 °C for 0.5 h maximized the SME by refining the hcp-ε phase and stacking fault density. While such treatments improve functional behavior and ductility, they are less effective at eliminating porosity or uniformly enhancing strength across LPBF-fabricated materials.

Hot isostatic pressing (HIP), which involves elevated temperature and isostatic pressure, is a particularly promising post-process for AM metals as it can eliminate porosity and relieve residual stress while concurrently heat-treating the material. HIP has been shown to enhance the mechanical properties of LPBF components in steels and superalloys by healing internal voids and promoting microstructural homogenization44–46. For example, Akilan et al.47 reported that HIP reduced porosity in LPBF Fe–Cr–Ni steels from ~ 5.4% to < 0.1%, with improved mechanical properties. Limited studies on SMAs suggest comparable results; Singh et al.48 reported that HIP enhanced density and strength in Cu-Al-Ni-Mn SMAs, while Cullaz et al.49 reported improved ductility in NiTiHf alloys, though with some grain coarsening. Despite these advantages, prior studies on Fe-SMAs have not comprehensively examined the combined effect of Cr alloying and HIP on LPBF Fe-SMAs. The present work seeks to bridge this gap by investigating how HIP treatment affects the multiscale mechanical behavior of LPBF FeMnAlNi SMAs, with and without Cr additions.

In this study, two alloy compositions were focused on FeMnAlNi and a variant alloy containing ~ 4 at% Cr (FeMnAlNiCr). The effects of HIP on tensile properties (derived via indentation plastometry), hardness, densification, and nanoindentation response are evaluated for both alloys. Special attention is given to the role of Cr in stabilizing mechanical properties during HIP. By integrating in situ laser remelting with post-HIP processing, this study aimed to establish the relationships among LPBF process parameters, HIP conditions, and alloy composition in determining the mechanical and nanomechanical performance of Fe–Mn–Al–Ni-based SMAs. Beyond optimizing the strength–ductility balance, the work reveals how Cr addition influences microstructural evolution and transformation behavior under HIP, offering new insights into designing high-performance, additively manufactured Fe-SMAs for structural and smart engineering applications.

The primary objective of this work is therefore to evaluate the influence of alloy composition (Cr addition) and HIP post-processing on the mechanical response and densification behavior of LPBF-produced FeMnAlNi-based alloys.

Materials

Gas-atomized pre-alloyed powders of two Fe-based SMA compositions were used. Both powders were produced by argon-protected gas atomization. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging confirmed smooth, spherical morphologies in both powders, with uniform elemental distributions. LPBF fabrication

Specimens were fabricated using an open-architecture 3D LPBF system equipped with a 1070 nm fiber laser and 500 W. The building chamber was backfilled with high-purity N2 (O2 < 0.2%) to minimize oxidation. The selection of LPBF process parameters was guided by prior studies on SMAs, which identified suitable ranges for achieving high-density parts with minimal porosity. Alhamdi et al.64 reported that a laser power of 175–200 W combined with a scan speed of 800 mm/s yielded high-quality FeMnAlNi builds, with laser remelting further improving density and surface quality. Similarly, Anwar et al.15 found that volumetric energy densities of 62–93 J/mm³ produced defect-free Fe-based SMA samples. As shown in Table 2, the FeMnAlNi samples were printed with a laser power of 175 W, a scan speed of 800 mm/s, a hatch spacing of 0.10 mm, and a layer thickness of 0.03 mm. A subset of these samples received an in-situ laser remelting pass, followed by a second laser-scanning pass at lower laser power to reduce porosity further. A total of 6 Cr-containing FeMnAlNiCr alloy samples were fabricated using similar parameter sets, as listed in Table 3. The first three as-built samples, without an in-situ laser remelting pass, were fabricated at laser powers of 150, 175, and 200 W, and the remaining three samples included a remelting pass at a laser power similar to that of the first three. All other process parameters, such as laser scanning speed, layer thickness, scanning strategy, and hatch spacing, are similar to those of the Cr-free alloy. The fabricated samples from both sets are presented in Fig. 2.

In this study, each sample name consists of two parts: the alloy type and the processing condition. Samples labeled with “R” refer to the Cr-free FeMnAlNi alloy, while “NS” refers to the Cr-containing FeMnAlNiCr alloy. The bracketed terms show how the sample was made. So, “(as-built)” means it was fabricated using only the primary process parameters, and “(Re.)” means a remelting pass was applied afterward. For example, 10R (as-built) is a Cr-free sample made without remelting, while 4R (Re.) includes a remelting step. Likewise, 2NS (as-built) and 5NS (Re.) represent Cr-containing samples without and with remelting. This naming approach keeps references clear and consistent throughout the discussion.

Hot isostatic pressing (HIP)

The LPBF specimens were HIP-treated in a Molybdenum furnace with Uniform Rapid Cooling (URC), a maximum temperature of 1500 °C, a pressure capability of 207 MPa, and argon as the inert gas medium. For Fe-based alloy systems and SMAs, previous studies48–55 have demonstrated that HIP parameters in the range of 875–1200 °C, 140–206.84 MPa, and 2–6 h result in reliable pore closure, densification, and microstructural refinement, corresponding to 70–80% of the alloy melting temperature. Based on a broad review of HIP applications in those studies, the selected profile for HIP treatment comprised a temperature of 1150 °C, a pressure of 150 MPa, and a holding time of 4 h. This specific profile was therefore selected to balance densification and microstructural control without risking grain coarsening or secondary phase instability. The temperature of 1150 °C lies above the recrystallization threshold of Fe-Mn-Al-Ni-based SMAs, ensuring stress relief and elimination of sub-grain defects, while the 150 MPa pressure is sufficient to collapse residual porosity in AM microstructures. A holding time of 4 h was adopted to allow complete pore closure and diffusion processes to stabilize, consistent with profiles frequently reported for Fe-based alloys and SMAs. All samples of both compositions underwent the same HIP cycle, and no encapsulation was required due to their high as-built density (> 97%) and small pore sizes.

Mechanical testing (macro-scale)

Quasi-static tensile properties in terms of yield strength- Ys, ultimate tensile strength- UTS, and elongation-% were obtained through a profilometry-based indentation plastometry method (PIP)56 following ASTM52965-25. This technique enables the extraction of a stress–strain curve by indenting the material with a hardened spherical indenter and inverse modeling of the material’s plastic response.

Because Fe-based SMAs can accommodate deformation through a combination of plastic slip and stress-induced martensitic transformation, the indentation stress state (triaxial/compressive) does not reproduce the uniaxial loading path of a standard tensile test. Accordingly, the PIP-derived stress–strain response is reported here as an indentation-derived equivalent (effective) tensile response, primarily intended for comparative assessment across processing conditions (as-built vs. HIP, remelted vs. non-remelted, and Cr-free vs. Cr-containing), rather than as a direct substitute for standardized uniaxial tensile properties. Trends from PIP are interpreted in conjunction with Vickers hardness, nanoindentation modulus/hardness, and densification measurements.

In this study, a 1 mm radius spherical indenter was used on specimens ~ 4 mm thick. The residual indent profiles were measured with a stylus profilometer and analyzed using the inverse finite element algorithm in reference57, yielding equivalent tensile properties for each sample. Multiple indentations were performed per sample to ensure repeatability. Additionally, conventional Vickers hardness (HV) was measured on polished cross-sections under a 1 kgf load (HV1), with at least 5 indents per condition. Archimedes’ density measurements were used to calculate the relative density (tensile properties (derived via indentation plastometry) before and after HIP for each sample. All tests were conducted before and after the HIP was applied to the samples, and the test results were recorded.

To ensure repeatability and statistical reliability, multiple measurements were performed for each condition. For the PIP measurements, several indentations were conducted on each specimen, and the reported yield strength and ultimate tensile strength values correspond to the average obtained from the inverse finite element analysis of the individual indent profiles. Vickers hardness measurements were performed with at least five independent indents per sample, and the reported hardness values represent the average of these measurements. Similarly, nanoindentation tests were carried out at multiple locations on each specimen, and the reported nano-hardness and elastic modulus values correspond to the averaged results from these measurements.

Nanoindentation testing

To probe nanomechanical behavior, an instrumented nanoindentation experiment was conducted on selected samples (discussed later in Sect. 3.3) using a Berkovich diamond indenter with a modulus of 1.141 × 10⁶ MPa and a Poisson’s ratio of 0.07. Tests employed a continuous stiffness measurement mode in accordance with ASTM E2546-1558, enabling measurement of nano-hardness and elastic modulus of the pre- and post-HIP samples as a function of penetration depth. A maximum load of 400 mN was applied, yielding an indentation depth of nearly 2200 nm. Additionally, to prevent interference between adjacent indentations, each indentation array consisted of 16 indents spaced 50 μm apart. For each sample, a total of 48 indentations were conducted with 3 arrays. From the unloading segments of the nanoindentation curves, the elastic modulus (E) was determined by the Oliver–Pharr method59. An elastic recovery (ER) ratio was calculated as the percentage of maximum indentation depth recovered upon complete unloading. Any discrete displacement bursts (pop-in or pop-out events) in the load-displacement curves were also recorded for analysis of phase transformations during indentation. Optical microscopy was employed to capture images of the spatially distributed nanoindentation arrays, ensuring proper alignment, verifying spacing, and providing clear visualization of the indents for subsequent analysis, as shown in Fig. 3.

Results and discussion

Microstructure analysis

The XRD patterns of the basic alloy and the Cr-free and Cr-modified alloys (as listed in Table 1), for both before and after HIP, are illustrated in Fig. 4. In pre-HIP condition, the base alloy displays a dual-phase α (BCC) and γ (FCC) structure. The addition of ~ 4% Cr serves as an effective ferrite stabilizer, entirely inhibiting premature γ precipitation during rapid solidification, resulting in a highly textured, single-phase α matrix60. Following HIP treatment, these metastable structures are driven toward equilibrium, resulting in precipitation of the ductile γ phase, as indicated by peaks at 2θ angles of approximately 43.5°, 50.5°, 74.5°, and 90.0° in the supersaturated matrix. Significantly, the post-HIP Cr-alloy maintains a robustly stable α parent phase (noted by the pronounced reflection near 82.0°), which provides the crystallographic foundation necessary for the reversible martensitic transformation. The essential B2 (β) nano-precipitates, which are crucial for limiting plastic slip and facilitating superelasticity, possess nearly identical lattice parameters to the parent BCC phase, resulting in significant overlap with the primary α peaks in conventional XRD. Their requisite precipitation during high-temperature thermal treatments is a well-documented factor contributing to superelasticity in this alloy system61. The microstructural observations (Fig. 5) are consistent with the XRD results. The pre-HIP samples exhibit heterogeneous structures with visible porosity and microcracks. The melt pool lines associated with the LPBF manufacturing process were highly visible and obscured the visibility of grains and phase distribution, whereas the post-HIP samples show a more homogeneous, denser microstructure with reduced defects and no melt pool lines or microcracks.

Densification after HIP

The application of HIP proved to be highly effective in enhancing the relative density (RD) of all the samples from both alloy systems. The effects of HIP on RD of LPBF Fe-SMA samples are shown in Fig. 6a and b for Cr-containing samples and Cr-free samples, respectively. Bars show RD after HIP for each sample, and the line connecting the dots represents the RD before HIP.

All samples achieved near-complete densification after HIP treatment at 1150 °C, 150 MPa, and 4 h. Pre-HIP RD ranged from ~ 97.86% to ~ 99.56% in these specimens, reflecting some process-dependent porosity. After HIP, most samples reached > 99.7% RD. The largest densification gains were observed in samples with the highest initial porosity. One Cr-containing as-built sample (1NS) improved from 97.86% to 99.73%. Samples that were already nearly fully dense (e.g., sample 7R with ~ 99.26% RD pre-HIP) showed minimal change (after HIP: 99.27%). The application of pressure at elevated temperature effectively healed lack-of-fusion voids and microcracks, as expected from the closure mechanisms of HIP44. This densification improved ductility in all cases since internal defects function as crack-initiation sites that limit the strain at failure. These findings are consistent with prior studies48,52,53,62,63 on other alloys and SMAs that reported near-full densification of LPBF-processed metallic systems post-HIP.

Effect of HIP on mechanical properties

To ensure robust interpretation for Fe-SMAs, the PIP-derived Ys and UTS are treated as effective strength metrics under an indentation-informed inverse analysis. Importantly, the PIP trends are internally consistent with independent indicators measured in this study: HIP produces near-full densification and systematically increases elongation while reducing HV and nano-hardness, consistent with annealing/recovery and defect closure. Therefore, PIP results are used to quantify the relative magnitude of softening/retention across compositions and processing routes rather than to claim a unique, transformation-resolved uniaxial tensile constitutive law. This is supported by independent indicators of mechanical properties in this study, such as relative density, HV, nanoindentations, softening/recovery, and pop-out tests.

The effect of HIP treatment on the indentation-derived equivalent tensile properties of both alloys has been evaluated in terms of YS, UTS, HV, and Elongation. The analysis is based on before-and-after HIP test results and correlates them with underlying potential causes.

First, the mechanical properties of the FeMnAlNiCr alloy before and after HIP were analyzed in this work and are illustrated in Fig. 7. The results showed that all Cr-containing samples exhibited a drop in both Ys and UTS due to HIP-induced annealing, as depicted in Fig. 7a and b, respectively. Most Cr-containing post-HIP samples showed a ~ 20–50% decrease in Ys, except for one sample, and the UTS dropped by ~ 30% on average compared to their pre-HIP conditions. The high tensile strength observed in the as-built LPBF samples (pre-HIP) may be attributed to microstructural features such as high dislocation density and cellular/dendritic substructures that typically form due to the rapid cooling rates inherent to LPBF. However, the microstructure is often coarse, columnar, and may also contain significant residual stresses and porosity. Despite the HIP-induced softening, the post-HIP strengths remained relatively high, with UTS values ranging from ~ 880 to 1085 MPa across the Cr-containing samples.

HV showed a corresponding reduction: pre-HIP HV ranged from 344.62 to 410.18 HV, which fell to ~ 247–295 HV after HIP (a 20–33% decrease) as shown in Fig. 7c. A similar decreasing trend in strength after HIP is observed in published work47,64,65. The loss of strength and HV is primarily attributed to the reduction in dislocation density and possible microstructural changes induced during the high-temperature HIP cycle. Under certain conditions, such as elevated pressure and sufficient temperature, HIP may promote recrystallization, potentially refining grain structure rather than causing coarsening.

Interestingly, one Cr-containing sample (4NS) exhibited an anomalous response. This sample’s Ys increased from 386 to 597 MPa (+ 54%) after HIP, as shown in Fig. 7a. This, coupled with a relatively stable HV, suggests a possible secondary strengthening mechanism. This increase is likely attributable to the combined effects of densification and the specific LPBF processing strategy employed for this sample. The 4NS condition employed a remelting strategy to improve melt pool homogeneity and reduce lack-of-fusion defects during fabrication. Subsequent HIP processing further eliminates residual internal porosity and improves metallurgical bonding, thereby increasing the material’s effective load-bearing cross-section. In this case, the strengthening effect associated with defect elimination appears to outweigh the softening typically associated with high-temperature recovery. This behavior suggests that defect closure played a dominant role in determining the yield strength of this condition, whereas recovery-driven softening appears to dominate in the other samples66. Apart from the outlier sample results, all other Cr-containing samples followed the expected softening trend.

On the other hand, ductility was universally improved in the Cr-containing samples after HIP treatment, as shown in Fig. 7d. The elimination of residual porosity and stress led to higher elongation in all cases. For example, a 2NS (as-built) sample fabricated with 175 W laser power with no remelting increased from 12% to 15%, while a 4NS (Re.) fabricated with 150 W laser power with in-situ remelting exhibited the most significant improvement in elongation, resulting in 11.3% for pre-HIP to 19% post-HIP. Although these gains were modest compared to the Cr-free alloy (discussed later), they confirm that HIP effectively enhances toughness without completely sacrificing strength.

The Cr-free FeMnAlNi alloy exhibited pronounced strength reduction after HIP, which is shown in Fig. 8. On average, the Ys decreased by 55–56% from pre-HIP to post-HIP measurements, as shown in Fig. 8a, while the UTS decreased by ~ 47% (Fig. 8b). For instance, the as-built sample fabricated with 175 W laser power without remelting (10R) dropped from 461 to 224 MPa in Ys, as shown in Fig. 8a, and from 1443 to 736 MPa in UTS, as illustrated in Fig. 8b, while the remelted sample 5R (175 W/140 W) decreased from 281 to 142 MPa in Ys and from 1431 to 812 MPa in UTS. This more uniform and severe softening, compared with the Cr-containing alloy, is attributed to recrystallization and a reduction in dislocation density during HIP. The microstructure formed during LPBF, often characterized by columnar grains and residual defects, may undergo significant evolution during HIP due to elevated temperatures. Depending on the thermal exposure, HIP can trigger recrystallization or grain coarsening, as observed in similar alloys through Ostwald ripening and recovery mechanisms54.

HV measurements showed a similar convergence. Pre-HIP values ranged from 373 to 428 HV, whereas all samples fell within 192–205 HV after HIP, corresponding to a ~ 50% reduction. Figure 8c shows the clear drop of HV across all the Cr-free samples. The homogenization of HV indicates that HIP largely erased microstructural distinctions arising from different LPBF scan strategies. The relief of residual stress and the migration of geometrically necessary dislocations contribute to the observed softening. Similar effects have been reported in other alloys, such as SS316L, where post-heat treatments, including HIP, lead to a reduction in dislocation density and overall HV53.

In contrast, ductility was consistently improved for all samples post-HIP for the Cr-free FeMnAlNi alloy, as shown in Fig. 8d, similar to the Cr-containing samples. The as-built 10R increased in elongation from 13.9% to 17.0%, while 5R (Re.) showed the largest gain, from 16.9% to 24.0% (~ 40% improvement). Only one remelted sample (3R) fabricated using 175 W primary laser power and 87.5 W remelting laser power deviated, decreasing from 24.3% to 18%, possibly due to grain boundary embrittlement associated with excessive grain growth. Overall, HIP induced substantial annealing softening while enhancing ductility, underscoring the trade-off between strength and toughness in the Cr-free FeMnAlNi SMA system.

The observed influence of Cr addition on the mechanical response can be interpreted based on established metallurgical effects of Cr in Fe-based alloys. Chromium acts as a solid-solution strengthening element due to atomic size and modulus mismatch with the Fe matrix, which increases lattice resistance to dislocation motion and contributes to improved strength retention. In addition, Cr may influence microstructural stability during high-temperature exposure by reducing diffusion-driven recovery processes. From a broader perspective, Cr additions are also known to enhance oxidation resistance and high-temperature stability in Fe-based alloys, suggesting that Cr incorporation could provide additional benefits for applications requiring improved thermal durability.

Nanomechanical behavior and elastic recovery

A series of nano-mechanical evaluations was conducted on FeMnAlNi and FeMnAlNiCr SMAs to assess their behavior in both as-fabricated and HIP-processed conditions. Four representative samples were selected: 10R (as-built) and 4R (remelted) from the FeMnAlNi system, and 2NS (as-built) and 5NS (remelted) from the FeMnAlNiCr system. All specimens were produced via LPBF, with as-built samples using a laser power of 175 W, and remelted variants were additionally processed with a remelting scan at a primary laser power of 175 W (Tables 2 and 3). Following fabrication, each sample underwent HIP treatment (1150℃, 150 MPa, and 4 h). Importantly, the pairs 10R/2NS and 4R/5NS were fabricated under identical process parameters (as listed in Tables 2 and 3). Nanoindentation testing was then conducted on both pre- and post-HIP specimens, providing average hardness and modulus values along with associated standard deviations. This sample selection was also used later to evaluate the effect of Cr addition.

HIP effect on elastic modulus and nano-hardness

From the nano-indentation test, the elastic modulus values exhibited more subtle, sample-dependent changes, as shown in Fig. 9a. In the FeMnAlNi alloy, the modulus decreased moderately; 10R dropped from 165.0 to 157.9 GPa (~ 4.3% reduction), while 4R decreased more substantially from 152.2 to 136.1 GPa (~ 10.5%). The difference between the two conditions highlights the role of thermal history. The as-built 10R retained more stiffness because its microstructure still contained a high dislocation density imparted by LPBF. In contrast, 4R, which underwent an additional remelting cycle before HIP, likely experienced enhanced recovery and recrystallization during HIP, resulting in a greater loss of stiffness. Similar reductions in modulus following dislocation recovery and grain coarsening have been reported for HIP-treated LPBF alloys such as 316 L stainless steel67 and NiTi SMAs68.

By comparison, the FeMnAlNiCr alloy was remarkably stable, as shown in Fig. 9a. Sample 2NS exhibited almost no change in modulus, while 5NS increased from 153.5 to 161.8 GPa (+ 5.4%). This suggests that HIP not only preserved but also slightly enhanced stiffness in the remelted Cr-containing condition, likely due to densification and microstructural stabilization. Cr is a known solid-solution strengthener in Fe alloys, and it also influences phase stability23,69. Its presence likely suppressed abnormal grain growth during HIP, maintaining a fine-enough grain size to preserve stiffness while reducing porosity. This explains why the Cr-containing alloys exhibited less hardness loss and better modulus retention after HIP than their Cr-free counterparts.

Fig. 9Nano-mechanical behavior analysis of FeMnAlNi and FeMnAlNiCr alloys in pre- and post-HIP conditions (a) Elastic modulus results, and (b) Nano-Hardness results.

Nano-mechanical behavior analysis of FeMnAlNi and FeMnAlNiCr alloys in pre- and post-HIP conditions (a) Elastic modulus results, and (b) Nano-Hardness results.

In contrast, nanoindentation results revealed that HIP produces a pronounced reduction in nano-hardness in both alloy systems, although the extent of softening differs noticeably, as shown in Fig. 9b. In the FeMnAlNi alloy, hardness declined by nearly half after HIP, as clearly visible on the left side of Fig. 9b. The as-built 10R sample corresponded to a 52.5% reduction, while the remelted 4R sample dropped from 5.70 GPa to 3.12 GPa. In contrast, the FeMnAlNiCr alloy retained more of its hardness. As shown on the right of Fig. 9b, the as-built 2NS decreased modestly with a 27% reduction, and 5NS from 5.26 GPa to 4.16 GPa (~ 21% reduction). Thus, while HIP softens both alloys, FeMnAlNi loses well over half of its hardness, whereas FeMnAlNiCr maintains 75–80% of its pre-HIP values. Similar reductions in nano-hardness and ER following high-temperature post-processing have been reported in LPBF FeMnAlNi SMAs treated by in situ/ex situ heat treatments70.

During the HIP at 1150 °C, recovery and recrystallization occur, annihilating dislocations, merging sub-grain boundaries, and promoting grain growth, thereby reducing strength and hardness despite improved densification. Similar softening has been reported in LPBF steels treated above 1000 °C, where the dislocation density decreased by factors of 2.5–4, accompanied by corresponding grain coarsening and strength loss71,72. Phase transformations may also contribute to the mechanical softening observed in the FeMnAlNi alloy during HIP. At elevated temperatures, previously stress-induced martensite can revert to austenite, consistent with diffusionless austenite reformation mechanisms reported in Fe–Mn–Ni systems71. Comparable austenite reversion and hardness reduction upon high-temperature tempering have also been observed in maraging steels72.

Elastic recovery and pop-out phenomenon

Elastic recovery (ER) obtained from the unloading portion of nanoindentation curves provides indirect insight into the transformation-related deformation behavior of Fe-SMAs before and after HIP. Figure 10a,b shows the correlation of ER with nano-hardness and modulus for an overall insight into both alloys. All samples exhibited measurable ER in the as-built state, which consistently decreased following HIP treatment. For Fe-Mn-Al-Ni, the ER dropped from 23.8% to 14.1% in 10R (as-built) sample and from 19.1% to 15.4% in 4R sample after HIP (Fig. 10a). The Cr-containing alloys followed a similar trend, with 2NS sample declining from pre-HIP 19.4% to post-HIP 15.6% and 5NS sample from 20.4% to 15.5% (Fig. 10b). These reductions correlate directly with the softening observed in nano-hardness and modulus shown by dots connected with straight lines in Fig. 10a and b, confirming that HIP reduces the recoverable fraction of strain by eliminating dislocations, promoting grain coarsening, and stabilizing the austenite matrix.

Fig. 10Correlation of ER with nano-hardness and modulus (a) FeMnAlNi samples (b) FeMnAlNiCr samples.

Correlation of ER with nano-hardness and modulus (a) FeMnAlNi samples (b) FeMnAlNiCr samples.

Representative load-displacement curves (Fig. 11a,b) further confirm the effect of HIP on transformation-related behavior. The pre-HIP 10R and 4R samples reached maximum loads of 420 mN and 411 mN, respectively, whereas their post-HIP counterparts carried lower loads (214 mN and 217.5 mN, respectively). In all cases, the loading segments were smooth and continuous, with no evidence of pop-in events. Distinct pop-out events were observed only in the post-HIP unloading curves of 10R and 4R, which are clearly highlighted in Fig. 11a and b near the end of unloading. This abrupt displacement burst indicates a stress-induced reversion from martensite to austenite at the microscale. Similar pop-out behavior during unloading has been reported in Cu-Al-Be SMAs, where it was linked to reversible transformations rather than plasticity73. Algamal et al.70 observed comparable pop-out features in LPBF-fabricated FeMnAlNi SMAs. By contrast, NiTi thin films showed smooth recovery without bursts, attributed to thin-film constraints suppressing transformation localization74.

Multiple nanoindentation tests were performed at various locations on each sample to ensure repeatability. Pop-out events during unloading were observed in several indentation curves, indicating that the behavior was reproducible and not limited to isolated events. The persistence of pop-outs in bulk FeMnAlNi after HIP suggests that transformation-related deformation mechanisms may still occur locally during indentation. However, nanoindentation alone cannot confirm macroscopic reversible phase transformation.

Fig. 11Loading-unloading behavior of FeMnAlNi pre- and post-HIP samples (a) 10R and (b) 4R.

Loading-unloading behavior of FeMnAlNi pre- and post-HIP samples (a) 10R and (b) 4R.

However, the absence of a visible pop-out during unloading in the pre-HIP Cr-free samples (10R and 4R) as presented in Fig. 11a and b, alone does not indicate the absence of shape memory behavior. Supporting evidence from XRD and DSC in the literature confirms that these alloys undergo a reversible γ′-to-α martensitic transformation, characteristic of SMAs. The shape memory behavior of both FeMnAlNi and FeMnAlNiCr alloys investigated in this study is governed by a reversible martensitic transformation between the γ′ (fcc) austenite and α (bcc) martensite phases. For the Cr-free FeMnAlNi alloy, this transformation has been extensively validated through X-ray diffraction (XRD), differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), and cyclic mechanical testing. Anwar et al.70 demonstrated phase coexistence using XRD and identified transformation temperature windows via DSC, thereby confirming the γ′-α phase change characteristic of thermoelastic shape memory alloys. The DSC curves adapted from their work show clear transformation peaks, confirming the reversible martensitic transformation behavior of the FeMnAlNi alloy. Prior studies further support these findings. For instance, Omori et al.75 and Zhang et al.25 reported superelastic recovery and thermal peaks, while Mino et al.76 and Peng et al.77 identified transformation signatures for this Cr-free alloy type through structural and magnetic measurements.

For the Cr-containing alloy, a 13Al composition (Fe-34Mn-13Al-7.5Ni -4Cr) studied by Xia et al.78, which closely resembles the alloy (Fe-33.92Mn-11.82Al-7.55Ni-4.11Cr) used in this work, exhibited full superelastic recovery up to 2% strain under compressive loading at room temperature, supporting its relevance as a transformation-capable reference system. Their XRD analysis confirmed the presence of both γ and α phases across different quenching conditions. Additionally, DSC analysis revealed a solvus temperature of 1153 °C for the 13Al alloy, indicating the thermal range over which phase transformation activity is retained. Together, these findings confirm the reversible martensitic transformation behavior characteristic of SMAs. In this study, the observed elastic recovery and pop-out behavior during nanoindentation further support the presence of stress-induced transformation in both alloy systems. The combination of literature data and results provides clear structural and functional evidence that both alloy systems are SMAs governed by a reversible γ′↔α transformation mechanism.

The representative load–load-displacement curves for the Cr-containing 2NS and 5NS samples presented in Fig. 12a and b visually confirm their reduced ER. Interestingly, a subtle but distinct pop-out phenomenon is observed in both the pre- and post-HIP unloading curves for the 2NS and 5NS samples, clearly highlighted in Fig. 12a and b, respectively, indicating stress-induced reverse phase transformations. The persistence of pop-out after HIP treatment, though less pronounced, further implies that despite substantial homogenization, HIP does not fully eliminate these transformation-capable microstructural regions.

Fig. 12Loading-unloading behavior of FeMnAlNiCr for pre- and post-HIP samples (a) 2NS and (b) 5NS.

It should be noted that the nanoindentation pop-out events observed in this study are interpreted only as localized indicators of transformation-related deformation behavior rather than as definitive evidence of macroscopic reversible phase transformation. While prior literature provides transformation temperature ranges for similar Fe–Mn–Si-based alloys, the prolonged high-temperature exposure during HIP processing could potentially influence phase stability and transformation characteristics. Direct thermodynamic characterization techniques, such as differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) and X-ray diffraction (XRD), are required to determine transformation temperatures and phase fractions in post-HIP materials quantitatively. Such characterization was beyond the scope of the present work but will be considered in future studies.

Effect of chromium addition

Cr alloying strongly influenced the mechanical response of Fe-SMAs under HIP treatment, as demonstrated through direct comparisons between Cr-free and Cr-containing samples fabricated under identical LPBF parameters. Representative pairs-10R vs. 2NS (as-built) and 4R vs. 5NS (remelted)-were selected for this analysis as mentioned in Sect. 3.3. The only difference between the samples of each pair is that one has Cr and the other does not have Cr in its composition. This allowed a fair comparison of the effect of having Cr in the alloy. The results of pre- and post-HIP treatment are summarized in Fig. 13. The Cr-containing alloys exhibited markedly greater resistance to HIP-induced softening, retaining higher YS, UTS, and HV than the Cr-free counterparts. This improvement is consistent with the solid-solution strengthening effect of Cr and its potential influence on grain boundary stability during high-temperature exposure. In contrast, the greater softening observed in the Cr-free alloys may be associated with more extensive microstructural recovery during HIP.

Bottom images: Comparison of indentation-derived equivalent mechanical and nanomechanical properties of remelted FeMnAlNi and Fe–Mn–Al–Ni–Cr alloys in (c) Pre-HIP and (d) Post-HIP conditions.

The Cr significantly improved strength retention after HIP in the Cr-containing alloy. In the as-built state shown in Fig. 13a, the Cr-containing sample 2NS exhibited a higher Ys than its Cr-free counterpart 10R and a slightly higher UTS. After HIP, as shown in Fig. 13b, the differences widened: the Cr-containing 2NS sample retained Ys of 386 MPa and a UTS of 880 MPa, whereas 10R dropped to 224 MPa and 736 MPa, respectively. A similar trend was observed under the remelted condition, as shown in Fig. 13c and d. Pre-HIP, 5NS, and 4R were comparable (Fig. 13c), but post-HIP 5NS retained substantially higher strength (336 MPa Ys, 1085 MPa UTS) compared to 4R (168 MPa Ys, 767 MPa UTS), as shown in Fig. 13d. These findings highlight Cr’s role in stabilizing strength under high-temperature HIP exposure, which may be associated with solid solution strengthening and improved microstructural stability during the HIP hold period. These differences suggest that Cr promotes solid solution strengthening by introducing atomic-scale lattice distortion79, which increases resistance to dislocation motion.

The hardness trends reinforced the tensile results. Pre-HIP, Cr-free as-built samples exhibited slightly higher HV (for instance, 10R: 373.55 HV) than Cr-containing ones (2NS:369.76 HV) as seen in Fig. 13a, consistent with their higher dislocation density and residual stress. However, after HIP, the Cr-alloys clearly retained greater hardness (Fig. 13b). Similarly, for the remelted pair, the results in Fig. 13c and d exhibit that 5NS decreased only to 294.68 HV, while 4R dropped sharply to 201.44 HV after HIP treatment. This behavior suggests that the addition of Cr improves microstructural stability during HIP processing. In contrast, ductility gains were somewhat greater in Cr-free alloys. For instance, 10R elongation improved from 13.9% to 17%, while 2NS increased from 12 to 15%, as shown in Fig. 13a and b, respectively. Similarly, 4R rose from pre-HIP 15.1% to post-HIP 17%, compared to 14.2% to 15% for 5NS, as shown in Fig. 13c and d, respectively. These results suggest that Cr enhances strength and hardness retention but slightly limits improvements in ductility after HIP in both the as-built and remelted LPBF Fe-SMA samples.

The pre- and post-HIP nanoindentation results of the as-built sample pair (10R and 2NS), as shown in Fig. 13a and b, and the remelted sample pair (4R and 5NS), as shown in Fig. 13c and d, indicate a clear influence of Cr on nano-hardness and modulus retention after HIP. In the pre-HIP as-built condition (Fig. 13a), the Cr-free alloy (10R) exhibited the highest nano hardness (7.08 GPa), whereas the Cr-containing counterpart (2NS) showed a lower value (5.23 GPa). Similarly, for the pre-HIP remelted samples (Fig. 13a), the Cr-free 4R sample had a slightly higher hardness (5.71 GPa) than the Cr-added 5NS (5.26 GPa). After HIP, both alloy systems softened, but the extent differed markedly. The Cr-free alloys showed a large hardness reduction of ~ 45–50%, while the Cr-containing alloys exhibited only a ~ 20–27% decrease, as depicted in Fig. 13b and d. A similar trend was observed in the elastic modulus: the Cr-free 4R sample decreased from pre-HIP 152.2 GPa to post-HIP 136.1 GPa, whereas the Cr-containing 5NS increased slightly from pre-HIP 153.5 GPa to post-HIP 161.8 GPa. These results indicate that Cr enhances thermal stability and mechanical retention during HIP, likely due to its solid-solution strengthening effect and its influence on microstructural stability. Consequently, Cr-containing alloys exhibit higher post-HIP nano-hardness and modulus than their Cr-free counterparts. Overall, the strategic combination of LPBF parameters, Cr alloying, and HIP is a compelling approach to obtain a balanced, high-strength, high-density Fe-SMA via AM.

The mechanistic interpretations presented here are based on the observed mechanical trends and the known metallurgical effects of high-temperature HIP processing in Fe-based alloys. In particular, the reductions in hardness and strength after HIP are consistent with thermally activated recovery processes such as dislocation annihilation, partial recrystallization, and microstructural relaxation that commonly occur during high-temperature exposure.

Conclusions

A HIP treatment at 1150 °C and 150 MPa for 4 h was highly effective in densifying additively manufactured Fe-SMAs, achieving relative densities above 99.7% and substantially relieving internal stresses. This process significantly improved ductility, increasing elongation from 16.9% to 24% in Cr-free Fe-SMA. Still, it also reduced indentation-based Ys and hardness by approximately 50%, consistent with thermally activated recovery processes that may occur during high-temperature HIP exposure.

The addition of ~ 4 at% Cr mitigated this softening effect, with Cr-containing samples retaining over 70% of their pre-HIP strength (UTS ≈ 1.1 GPa, hardness ≈ 295 HV). In one instance, a Cr-containing sample processed with 150 W primary laser power using in-situ laser remelting exhibited increased post-HIP yield strength, better hardness retention, higher density, and greater elongation.

Nanoindentation results supported this trend: Cr-free alloys exhibited a ~ 45–50% reduction in nano-hardness after HIP, while Cr-containing alloys showed only ~ 20–25% reduction. The elastic modulus also decreased for the Cr-free alloy, whereas the Cr-containing alloy showed a stable response, even increasing the modulus in one case. Pop-out events observed during unloading confirmed that stress-induced martensitic reversion and thus pseudoelastic behavior were preserved.

The mechanical response obtained from PIP was interpreted alongside hardness, nanoindentation, and relative density measurements to provide a consistent assessment of the mechanical behavior of the Fe-SMA samples. Because indentation induces a multiaxial stress state and Fe-based shape memory alloys can accommodate deformation through both plastic slip and stress-induced martensitic transformation, the PIP-derived stress–strain curves are considered here as indentation-derived equivalent strength metrics rather than direct substitutes for standardized uniaxial tensile properties. Nevertheless, the trends observed from PIP are consistent with the independent mechanical indicators measured in this study. In particular, the reduction in PIP-derived equivalent Ys and UTS after HIP processing correlates with decreases in Vickers hardness and nanoindentation hardness, indicating recovery and microstructural relaxation associated with high-temperature processing. At the same time, the significant improvement in relative density after HIP suggests effective closure of internal defects and pores, which explains the improved ductility observed in the PIP-derived stress–strain response despite the moderate reduction in strength. Therefore, when interpreted alongside hardness, nanoindentation, and densification data, the PIP results provide a consistent, comparative measure of strength retention and mechanical response across the different processing conditions and alloy compositions investigated in this work.

Microstructural characterization combined with XRD analysis reveals that the mechanical response of the LPBF-produced FeMnAlNi-based alloys is governed by phase constitution, microstructural homogeneity, and defect state. HIP processing enhances densification and reduces internal defects, while Cr addition promotes phase stability and microstructural refinement, collectively influencing the observed mechanical performance.

Overall, the combination of LPBF process parameters, HIP, and Cr alloying produced near-defect-free Fe-SMAs with a favorable balance of strength and ductility. These results demonstrate their potential as cost-effective alternatives to NiTi for high-temperature structural and functional applications, including seismic dampers, actuators, and energy-absorbing devices.
