Injury risk classification from feature grouped tabular athlete data using deep neural networks: a cross sectional benchmark study
Authors: Han Yan, Xiaoqiao Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The classification of sports injuries is one of the most important tasks in athlete monitoring, and commonly available benchmark datasets are often small, with limited injury classes, missing data, and inadequate documentation of injury occurrence, injury labels, and data provenance. This study evaluates a feature-grouped deep neural network for binary injury-risk classification on the University Football Injury Prediction dataset from Kaggle. The 18 input variables are grouped into four feature groups in tabular format: demographics and anthropometrics, training factors, physical fitness indicators, and lifestyle habits. A separate branch of the neural network encodes each feature group, and concatenation or attention-based gates fuse them. Under a leakage-aware preprocessing and evaluation protocol, the proposed model is compared against logistic regression, random forest, gradient boosting, and a single-stream multilayer perceptron. The performance metrics are: AUROC, AUPRC, F1 score, balanced accuracy, Brier score, expected calibration error, bootstrap confidence intervals, and seed-based stability analysis. Results indicate minimal gains from feature-grouped neural fusion over classical and single-stream baselines, particularly in precision-recall and probability calibration. These results, however, must be treated with caution, as the benchmark is internally assessed, small, and lacks a complete, independently verified longitudinal follow-up or a complete, documented labeling procedure. Explanability analysis is thus discussed in terms of the descriptive interpretation of models, rather than the causal identification of injury risk factors. In summary, this work offers a reproducible reference standard for feature-grouped tabular injury classification and underscores the necessity of externally validated, prospectively collected, longitudinal athlete datasets before such classification models can be considered for real-world training and/or clinical decision-making.

Introduction

Injuries are relatively rare but can have significant consequences for athlete training, performance, and welfare, making sports injury risk classification an important problem in athlete monitoring. In the real world of sport, the nature of the injury could be related to a number of dimensions of an athlete’s information, such as demographic data, training loads, previous injury history, physical fitness, recovery, and/or lifestyle behaviors [1]. The current study, however, does not provide a model for longitudinal wearable monitoring, real-time decision support, or prospective injury forecasting. Rather, it focuses on a more specific and reproducible task in machine learning: classifying injury risk as a binary outcome from a cross-sectional, tabular athlete profile. This is also significant, as this study relies on the University Football Injury Prediction dataset, which provides 1 record per athlete, instead of repeated time-stamped observations [2]. The model, therefore, can assess statistical relationships among the grouped tabular features of the benchmark set, but it cannot define causal injury mechanisms or validate future injury predictions. This study aims to evaluate and compare cross-sectionally the feature-grouped neural fusion approach with classical and flat-feature baselines in leakage-aware preprocessing, class-imbalance-sensitive evaluation, calibration analysis, and cautious model interpretation.

In this framework, our research has two interdependent aims, which are operationally relevant in the field of sports training: (i) the ability to classify injury risk from cross-sectional athlete profiles, and (ii) the evaluation of whether modality-grouped deep learning representations improve discrimination and calibration relative to classical baselines on a static tabular benchmark. The most significant methodology problem is to establish strong, practical relationships among multimodal cues and to prevent the typical pitfalls of sports prediction (e.g., label noise, missingness, time leakage, and small samples). The rest of this section explores the exposure-outcome relationship, summarizes the applicable monitoring modalities, and places deep neural networks in risk modeling with limited-to-medium data capacity. One of the main postulates of load management is that the risk of injury is not only determined by how hard athletes train, but also by the training-induced stress, which is influenced by individual tolerance, fitness, and recovery. The theoretical foundation of training load research is focused on the need to be explicit about the variables being considered as training stimulus, athlete response, and the temporal relationship between them and injury outcomes [3]. In the absence of that foundation, models can be able to learn associations that are statistically powerful but operationally deceptive (especially where the exposure measure is a proxy, when the exposure measure is aggregated with arbitrary windows, when the exposure measure does not capture the structure of sports, e.g., match congestion, match tactics). Training injury studies have also highlighted methodological pitfalls in recurrent analyses: ratio-based constructs, discretization decisions, and adjustable time windows can inflate researchers’ degrees of freedom, making apparent predictability and interpretation more challenging [4]. This is particularly concerning in machine learning systems conditioned on observational logs, such as interventional cases, where an intervention (e.g., reduced load after early symptoms) may create a feedback loop between cause and effect. The acute-chronic workload ratio (ACWR) is a widely used metric. Recent evidence syntheses indicate that the predictive value of ACWR depends on implementation details (e.g., definitions of workloads and windowing) and is non-linear, supporting the use of richer exposure models and individualized baselines instead of simply applying a single heuristic feature [5]. This motivates the investigation of representation-learning approaches that can capture nonlinear interactions among grouped feature domains, even when the available data are cross-sectional rather than longitudinal.

A recent scoping review of sports medicine has described the present context of utilization of wearable systems within the context of athletic training and rehabilitation, and outlines that such systems have the capacity to deliver real-time data on workload and movement quality otherwise inaccessible outside of the laboratory [6]. Complementary to this, the latest research in the field of digital health points to a new role for so-called precision monitoring techniques that integrate wearable sensing with analytics pipelines to personalize interventions based on individual profiles rather than population averages [7]. Such changes encourage a multimodal learning environment in which signals are considered complementary perspectives: external load refers to what was done, internal load refers to physiological cost, and biomechanical proxies refer to how the athlete manifested the load. It is important to model these together, since the nature of injury mechanisms and states of readiness can manifest as very subtle cross-modal discrepancies (e.g., high internal load at a given external load level, or worsening movement correlates despite constant volume).

Deep learning has two properties that are especially useful in training and injury modeling, namely: (i) training nonlinear, history-conditional representations on sequential data, and (ii) learning to combine heterogeneous modalities without having to perform a lot of manual feature engineering. Nonetheless, sports injury prediction has remained limited by small cohort sizes, low event rates, inconsistent labels, and low external validity. According to a systematic review of machine learning approaches to sport injury prediction and prevention, most studies have limitations in their datasets and evaluation methods; therefore, reported performance cannot be relied on or transferred to practice [8]. It is at this point that close attention to experimental design and to model regularization is as crucial as model choice. The evidence from prospective cohorts also explains why claims of high AUC should be approached with caution. For example, applying machine learning to large screening batteries to predict ACL injury demonstrates both the capabilities of multivariable modeling and the challenges of making reliable, consistent predictions when rare events are involved, despite substantial measurement effort [9]. These facts drive deep models toward strategies that directly address scarcity: rigorous temporal cross-validation protocols, austere architectures, concentrated representations, and contrived handling of missingness. A potentially fruitful avenue is transfer and representation learning across sports or contexts, particularly when the target setting has few labeled injury events. Recent literature suggests using temporal graph encodings with graph neural networks and transfer learning across sports to enhance generalization in the face of sparse data, aligning with the constraints of applied sports teams [10]. To ensure that these approaches are reported transparently and can be evaluated critically, recent guidance (TRIPOD + AI) sets refined reporting expectations for prediction models constructed by regression or machine learning [11].

The rest of the paper is structured as follows: Section "Related work" reviews the related literature on injury risk prediction and multimodal learning for athlete monitoring. Section "Materials and methods" section outlines the data and feature modalities, the problem of risk stratification under static conditions, the proposed modality-conscious fusion model, the training process, and implementation details. Section "Results" presents a report on baseline comparisons, calibration, robustness analysis (including missing-modality tests), and ablation studies. Section "Discussion" discusses key findings, limitations, and practical implications, and Section "Conclusion and future work" provides a conclusion and outlines directions for future work.

Related work

Injury-risk prediction from training, fitness, and lifestyle factors

One of the key aspects of modern sports-injury analytics is that risk is dynamic: it changes with recent exposures and recovery, as well as the athlete’s underlying capacity. Training-load monitoring is thus typically used as a viable substitute for the concept of exposure, which encompasses both internal (e.g., heart-rate-based indices, perceived exertion) and external (e.g., distance, accelerations, mechanical intensity) workload. But the interpretability and usefulness of action-taking are complicated by the fact that load measures are relative and constantly changing over the season and grossly contaminated by the effects of coaching intent and selection (i.e., decreased load may be an effect rather than a cause of new symptoms arising) [12, 13]. Syntheses of systematic evidence have consistently found that a significant proportion of published injury prediction models are associated with avoidable methodological flaws, ineffectiveness in addressing class imbalance, overly optimistic performance, and insufficient validation, which undermines confidence in their practical implementation [14]. In the literature on football injuries, the reviews of ML-based injury prediction focus both on the variety of candidate features (past injury history, cumulative workload summaries, wellness proxies, and performance tests) and the absence of consensus regarding robust feature definitions and reporting criteria that would allow the reproducibility of studies between teams and leagues [15]. Recent prospective studies in athletics (track and field) demonstrate the potential and complexity of the operation for estimating risk daily. The IPredict-AI protocol formalizes daily monitoring of training exposure, psychological condition, sleep, and symptoms to relate model-based risk feedback to injury outcomes across an athletic season [16]. Follow-on cohort evidence indicates that merely providing ML-based daily risk feedback is not necessarily linked to lower injury burden, and emphasizes that prediction should be integrated into the correct behavioral, clinical, and training decision-making process to generate downstream value [17]. Professional soccer Studies have also been modeled to represent non-contact injury risk in terms of short-term and longer-term combinations of internal and external load measures. These designs reflect the implicit assumption that within-athlete departures from baseline would be more informative than absolute thresholds, but sample sizes and the paucity of outcomes remain major limiting factors [18, 19]. Beyond training exposure and lifestyle, recovery determinants, particularly sleep, are increasingly being identified as quantifiable contributors to injury susceptibility. Future evidence in recreational runners suggests that low sleep quality is associated with a higher risk of running-related injury, which, in turn, encourages the incorporation of sleep-deprivation cues (where possible) [20]. To augment this, wearable-sensing reviews talk about how the combination of physiological and biomechanical monitoring can provide earlier warning about maladaptation patterns, though they also warn of the implementation level that implementation must be done with special care to the quality of the data, compliance on the part of the athlete, and a careful interpretation of the data [21]. Ma et al. [22] used a multidimensional tabular feature setting, in conjunction with SHAP based interpretable machine learning, to predict the risk of injury among university footballers. Their work is relevant because it emphasizes the same general application area and highlights the importance of interpretability in analyzing an athlete’s risk of injury. The present study focuses on methodological aspects; however, it differs from previous studies in which only conventional machine-learning models are compared and subsequently explained by post hoc SHAP interpretation, as we compare a compact feature-grouped neural architecture that employs feature-group-specific encoders for predefined feature groups and a learned fusion.

Multimodal fusion approaches in health and sports analytics

The goal of multimodal learning is to integrate heterogeneous signals (e.g., workload summaries, HR/HRV, sleep, questionnaire responses, and contextual metadata) in a homogeneous predictive representation. Overall, common types of fusion strategies include early fusion (concatenating features before modeling), late fusion (ensembling modality-selective predictors), and intermediate fusion (learning joint representations with attention, gating, or cross-modal interaction layers). Extensive literature has suggested that intermediate fusion can be more expressive for describing conditional dependencies (e.g., workload is more informative when recovery is poor), whereas late fusion can be more resilient to missing or unreliable modalities [23, 24]. In medical and precision-health applications, where multimodal structure can be highly similar to that of athlete monitoring, scoping reviews highlight that multimodal integration is effective for risk stratification when modalities capture complementary data, but it also risks missingness, temporal misalignment, and site/device domain shift [25]. The different paradigms include holistic multimodal AI designs that encourage end-to-end pipelines that view multimodal ingestion, temporal modeling, and decision support as jointly designed components rather than feature engineering on demand [26]. Another recurrent practical problem is the lack of modalities (e.g., athletes do not complete questionnaires, sensor failures, or HRV is noisy). Strong multimodal learning literature, consequently, emphasizes the use of strategies such as modality dropout, uncertainty-aware gating, and objectives trained to remain unchanged when parts of these modalities are unavailable [27]. In clinical environments with extensive use of imaging, scoping reviews of multimodal AI also note advantages of combining high-frequency sensor streams with lower-frequency contextual or self-report variables, and are similar to sports scenarios in which training load is continuous, but injury labels are discrete and lagged [28]. In more general terms, the reviews of multimodal integration and tutorials emphasize standardization of preprocessing, strong attention to temporal alignment, and active consideration of partially observed inputs as a precondition for valid cross-study comparisons [29]. Lastly, methods-focused interpretability has observed that, as multimodal fusion models become more complex, the authors are required not only to report their performance but also to describe which modalities contribute to the decision and under what circumstances, in order to justify meaningful stakeholder trust [30]. In the light of recent work on structured and robust representation learning, it is also beneficial to encode features and fuse them together adaptively in small and partially observed prediction tasks. Examples include the use of ontology-enhanced subgraph reasoning to enhance inductive prediction in the absence of direct supervision [31] and wearable sensor-based transfer learning to boost the robustness of abnormal running posture detection across scenarios [32]. Likewise, the multimodal (mixed signal) approach in dual-branch networks with spatial-temporal fusion has shown the benefits of fusing complementary signal branches for fatigue assessment [33]. Moreover, recent graph text contrastive alignment and hierarchical fusion reasoning approaches demonstrate that contrastive learning and hierarchical fusion can enhance the consistency of representation from diverse information sources [34]. These papers do not directly pertain to the classification of sports injuries from a cross-sectional point of view. Still, they serve as a good methodological motivation for the approach used in the present feature-grouped encoding and gated fusion.

Model interpretability and uncertainty for decision support

Models of injury risk, even when predictive performance is satisfactory, are held to unusually high standards for adoption, since they are instead used to determine training prescription and availability decisions. As a result, interpretability and uncertainty will be treated as primary requirements in the literature rather than add-ons. In multimodal and longitudinal contexts, reviews facing the clinical community focus on the fact that explainability should be used to answer both (i) within-modality drivers (e.g., how high-intensity running suddenly increases) and (ii) cross-modality interactions (e.g., how high-intensity running and poor sleep interact), which stay constant despite temporal drift and incomplete data [35]. More comprehensive surveys of healthcare categorize explainability strategies into intrinsic interpretability (transparent models, monotonic constraints, sparse structures) and post hoc explanations (feature attributions, counterfactuals, example-based reasoning), with the latter potentially weak when models are poorly calibrated or input distributions change [36]. Parallel to that, uncertainty-related views hold that predictive systems should be shaped to express their confidence, using calibrated probabilities, prediction sets, or abstention strategies, when downstream choices are costly, and labels are noisy or slow [37]. In more recent reviews of the WIREs, the practicality of adoption is also highlighted: the usefulness of an explanation should be based on the audience (coach vs. clinician), the timing (next session vs. season), and the space to act (reducing load, modifying intensity, enhancing recovery) [38]. To deploy the uncertainty reliably, there is also an increment in the uncertainty being framed to encompass larger verification/validation processes. The verification, validation, and uncertainty quantification (VVUQ) efforts of digital-twin-like decision systems include explicit claims, stress testing, and monitoring to ensure that model outputs are reliable across changing populations of athletes, devices, and training regimens [39]. Supplementary theme-issue views in uncertainty quantification support the view that biological and healthcare decision systems generally do not apply UQ as much as other engineering sectors. However, such decisions have similar safety consequences, and this position can be translated instinctively to the context of elite sport performance [40]. Lastly, uncertainty quantification in healthcare AI commonly involves calibration, conformal prediction, ensemble learning, Bayesian or dropout-based uncertainty estimation, and selective prediction mechanisms, which may be adapted in sports injury risk modeling to support more cautious and responsible interpretation of model outputs [41]. Recent work in the field of representation learning, in other contexts outside of sports analytics, also offers some interesting methodology for how to solve small, incomplete, and structured prediction tasks. It has been found that external structure and prior knowledge can enhance the representation quality with limited supervision through semantic-enhanced reasoning methods. Other methods have been based on the principles of contrastive alignment to enhance inter-modality fidelity and hierarchical fusion to minimize inter-modality inconsistencies. These studies are not direct injury prediction models and do not involve athlete monitoring data, but are used to draw inspiration for the general design principle followed in this work: design feature groups to be encoded separately and then fused into a common predictive representation. For this reason, the present study relies on these works exclusively as methodological background, and does not rely directly on them as clinical and/or sports science evidence.

Materials and methods

Dataset and cohort description

We use the University Football Injury Prediction dataset hosted on [42], which is a tabular benchmark dataset of 800 university football players. The rows are related to an individual athlete and include 18 input variables: demographics/body composition, training exposure and training history, physical fitness indices, and lifestyle habits, along with a binary injury label. The dataset is treated in this study as a public machine-learning benchmark rather than as a verified prospective athlete surveillance cohort. There is not enough information in the available dataset documentation to confirm the original sampling frame, the procedure used to diagnose the injuries, the timing of the injuries, the time period in which the injuries were followed, or the extent to which any injuries were simulated or synthetically generated on the basis of the available data.

Outcome definition (injury label) and prediction setting

The binary injury variable is the target label, with the positive class representing an athlete with an injury record, and the negative class representing an athlete with no injury record. As the data is cross-sectional, the temporal ordering between the predictors and the injury label cannot be assessed independently. Other factors, such as the number of previous injuries, are clinically reasonable predictors if they are measured before the target injury – but it is not specified when in this dataset. This means that the task is not a prospective forecast of injuries, but rather a cross-sectional study of injury status or injury risk classification. The important distinction is that the model can detect statistical correlations within the benchmark dataset; however, it cannot identify causal risk factors or ensure predictive validity in future datasets. The dataset was split into training, validation, and test sets in a 64:16:20 ratio by stratified sampling. The binary injury label was used to maintain the class prevalence across all subsets for stratification. The test set was not used during training, only during evaluation. To minimize reliance on any particular random seed, all experiments were conducted with five different random seeds: 42, 123, 456, 789, and 1024. All results are presented as the mean over the five runs, with standard deviation summarizing split and initialization variability unless otherwise specified. Finally, held-out test predictions were used to compute bootstrap confidence intervals to quantify sampling uncertainty for the final model comparison.

Feature groups used for grouped representation learning

The input variables are broken into four clinically interpretable tabular feature groups: demographics and anthropometrics, training factors, physical fitness indicators, and lifestyle habits to support grouped representation learning. These groups are not modalities in the strict sense of multimodal learning, as they are not distinct data types, such as imaging, video, wearable time series, or text. Rather, they are subsets of tabular variables. The summary of modality mapping and variable types is presented in Table 1.

Imbalance handling

We evaluate three complementary strategies, i.e., weighted binary cross-entropy (WBCE), focal loss, and resampling to mitigate class imbalance during training.

Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{w}_{1}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{w}_{0}$$\end{document} be class weights, typically \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{w}_{1}=\frac{N}{2{N}_{1}}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{w}_{0}=\frac{N}{2{N}_{0}}$$\end{document}. The loss is:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{l}_{\mathrm{WBCE}}(y,\widehat{p})=-\left({w}_{1}ylog\left(\widehat{p}\right)+{w}_{0}(1-y)log(1-\widehat{p})\right)$$\end{document}

To focus learning on hard examples, we use focal loss with a focusing parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\gamma\:\ge\:0$$\end{document}and optional balancing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\alpha\:\in\:\left[\mathrm{0,1}\right]$$\end{document}:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{l}_{\mathrm{focal}}(y,\widehat{p})=-\alpha\:y\:(1-\widehat{p}{)}^{\gamma\:}log(\widehat{p})-(1-\alpha\:\left)\right(1-y){\widehat{p}}^{\gamma\:}log(1-\widehat{p})$$\end{document}

When \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\gamma\:>0$$\end{document} correctly classified examples contribute less, reducing the dominance of easy negatives.

Alternatively, we modify the effective training distribution by oversampling positives or undersampling negatives to obtain \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}^{{\prime\:}}$$\end{document} with improved balance:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}^{{\prime\:}}\sim\:\mathrm{Resample}({D}_{\mathrm{tr}};\rho\:)$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\uprho\:}$$\end{document} denotes a target positive prevalence (e.g., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\rho\:=0.5$$\end{document} for balanced minibatches). Resampling is applied only within \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$$\end{document} and never to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{\mathrm{val}}$$\end{document} or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{\mathrm{te}}$$\end{document}, preserving unbiased evaluation. It is stratified using the injury label to divide the dataset into training (64%), validation (16%), and test (20) subsets (random seed = 42). Each experiment is run 5 times (seeds 42, 123, 456, 789, 1024), and the average and standard deviation are reported. Hyperparameter search via grid search: latent dimension r in {32, 64, 128}, dropout rate in {0.1, 0.3, 0.5}, learning rate in {1 × 10− 4, 5 × 10− 4, 1 × 10− 3}, weight decay in {1 × 10− 4, 1 × 10− 3}, focal loss gamma in {0, 1, 2}, attention entropy beta in {0, 0.01, 0.1}. Across all strategies, we select the approach that yields the best validation AUPRC and calibration (e.g., Brier score) while maintaining stable performance across random seeds.

Proposed feature-grouped deep learning

The feature-grouped neural structure is shown in Fig. 1. The individual athlete record is split into four tabular feature groups: demographics and anthropometrics, training factors, physical fitness indicators, and lifestyle habits. Each group is passed through its own multilayer perceptron (MLP) encoder, yielding a 64-dimensional representation. Simple concatenation or attention-based gating is used to fuse the resulting feature-group embeddings. In the gated version, the model learns a non-negative weight for each feature group, which is used to form a weighted representation that is passed to the prediction head. The output head gives a binary injury probability. After training, the performance of the model is evaluated using probability calibration and predictive uncertainty by applying temperature scaling and Monte Carlo dropout, respectively.

Training procedure, regularization, and hyperparameters

All neural models were trained using AdamW optimization with a learning rate of 5 × 10− 4, β1 = 0.90, β2 = 0.999, ε = 1 × 10− 8, and weight decay of 1 × 10− 3. The batch size was 64, and training was performed for up to 200 epochs. Early stopping was used when the loss did not decrease for 20 epochs during validation; the validation loss was also used as a secondary metric to select the final checkpoint, with AUPRC as the primary metric. A dropout rate of 0.30 was applied after the hidden layers in both the encoders and the prediction head.

We used shallow multilayer perceptrons for each feature-group encoder. The categorical position (in the model) variable was embedded as a learnable representation prior to concatenation with the corresponding continuous demographic characteristics, and continuous variables were standardized prior to entry into the model. Two fully connected layers with GELU activation, layer normalization, and dropout were employed in each encoder. All feature-group representations ended in a 64-dimensional latent space. In the case of concatenation fusion, the four 64-dimensional feature-group embeddings were concatenated and then fed into the prediction head. In the case of gated fusion, the weights of the feature groups were first normalized by a gating network, and the resulting weighted feature representation was then used as input to the same prediction head. The prediction head comprised two fully connected layers with GELU activation and dropout, followed by a final sigmoid layer for binary injury probability.

The hyperparameter search considered latent dimension r ∈ {32, 64, 128}, dropout rate ∈ {0.10, 0.30, 0.50}, learning rate ∈ {1 × 10− 4, 5 × 10− 4, 1 × 10− 3}, weight decay ∈ {1 × 10− 4, 1 × 10− 3}, focal-loss γ ∈ {0, 1, 2}, and attention entropy β ∈ {0, 0.01, 0.10}. The final selected configuration was r = 64, dropout = 0.30, learning rate = 5 × 10− 4, weight decay = 1 × 10− 3, focal-loss γ = 2, attention entropy β = 0.01, batch size = 64, and maximum epochs = 200. The experiments were conducted with Python 3.10, PyTorch 2.1, scikit-learn 1.3, XGBoost 2.0, and SHAP 0.43.

Model complexity and stability control

The dataset used is relatively small (800 athletes and 18 input variables); thus, the proposed neural architecture was made deliberately small. While the goal is not necessarily to demonstrate the superior performance of deep learning on small tabular datasets, it aims to validate whether there’s a consistent benefit over flat-feature baselines from separate feature-group encoding and learned fusion. We used shared latent dimension capacity control, shallow group-based encoders, dropout and weight decay, early stopping, and validation-based hyperparameter selection. There were five random seeds used during training, and the results were summarized as the mean and standard deviation. This design decreases the chances of the reported improvement being due to a single favorable split or due to the model being over-capacity, as shown in Table 2.

Discrimination metrics

Model discrimination was assessed using the conventional binary classification metrics: AUROC, AUPRC, F1 score, and balanced accuracy. The AUROC summarizes the performance of ranking across decision thresholds, and AUPRC is highlighted because the injury class is imbalanced and there is greater value in positive class retrieval than in accuracy.

For a selected threshold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\tau\:$$\end{document}, precision, recall, F1 score, and balanced accuracy were computed as follows:23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{Precision}\left(\tau\:\right)=\frac{TP\left(\tau\:\right)}{TP\left(\tau\:\right)+FP\left(\tau\:\right)}$$\end{document}24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{Recall}\left(\tau\:\right)=\frac{TP\left(\tau\:\right)}{TP\left(\tau\:\right)+FN\left(\tau\:\right)}$$\end{document}25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:F1\left(\tau\:\right)=\frac{2\times\:\mathrm{Precision}\left(\tau\:\right)\times\:\mathrm{Recall}\left(\tau\:\right)}{\mathrm{Precision}\left(\tau\:\right)+\mathrm{Recall}\left(\tau\:\right)}$$\end{document}26\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:BA\left(\tau\:\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{TP\left(\tau\:\right)}{TP\left(\tau\:\right)+FN\left(\tau\:\right)}+\frac{TN\left(\tau\:\right)}{TN\left(\tau\:\right)+FP\left(\tau\:\right)}\right]$$\end{document}

AUROC and AUPRC were computed from the predicted probabilities on the held-out test set.

Calibration metrics and threshold selection

Calibration was assessed using the Brier score and expected calibration error, which are widely used to evaluate the reliability of predicted probabilities. The Brier score was calculated as:27\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:BS=\frac{1}{\mid\:{D}_{\mathrm{te}}\mid\:}{\sum\limits_{i\in\:{D}_{\mathrm{te}}}}({\widehat{p}}_{i}-{y}_{i}{)}^{2}$$\end{document}

For expected calibration error, test predictions were divided into Kprobability bins. For each bin \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{B}_{k}$$\end{document}, empirical accuracy and mean confidence were computed, and ECE was defined as:28\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:ECE={\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}}\frac{\mid\:{B}_{k}\mid\:}{\mid\:{D}_{\mathrm{te}}\mid\:}\mid\:\mathrm{acc}\left({B}_{k}\right)-\mathrm{conf}\left({B}_{k}\right)\mid\:$$\end{document}

The classification threshold was selected only on the validation set and then applied unchanged to the test set. The primary threshold was selected by maximizing validation F1 score:29\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\tau\:}^{*}=\mathrm{arg}\:{\mathrm{max}}\underset{\tau\:}\:F\:{1}_{\mathrm{val}}\left(\tau\:\right)$$\end{document}

As a sensitivity analysis, Youden’s index was also considered:30\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:J\left(\tau\:\right)=TPR\left(\tau\:\right)-FPR\left(\tau\:\right)$$\end{document}

When recall was prioritized, the threshold was selected according to:31\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\tau\:}^{*}=\mathrm{m}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{x}\left\{\tau\::{\mathrm{Recall}}_{\mathrm{val}}\left(\tau\:\right)\ge\:{r}_{0}\right\}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{r}_{0}$$\end{document}denotes the required minimum recall.

Confidence intervals and significance testing

Uncertainty for the reported metrics was estimated by nonparametric bootstrapping of held out test set. A number of B=2000 bootstrap iterations were used for each metric over test subjects that were sampled with replacement. The 95% confidence interval was calculated from the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution:32\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:C{I}_{95\mathrm{\%}}\left(M\right)=\left[{Q}_{0.025}\left({\{M}^{\left(b\right)}{\}}_{b=1}^{B}\right),{Q}_{0.975}\left({\{M}^{\left(b\right)}{\}}_{b=1}^{B}\right)\right]$$\end{document}

For model comparison, paired bootstrap differences were computed between the proposed model and each baseline using the same test instances:33\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{\Delta\:}}^{\left(b\right)}={M}_{\mathrm{proposed}}^{\left(b\right)}-{M}_{\mathrm{baseline}}^{\left(b\right)}$$\end{document}

The mean difference, the 95% confidence interval, and the two-sided bootstrap p value were shown. These comparisons were interpreted as supportive rather than definitive due to the small size of the data sets and the small test set (20% of 800 athletes). Where there was no absolute difference or confidence intervals overlapped, the improvement was indicated as ‘modest’ or ‘no conclusive difference’.

Results

Cohort overview and data characteristics

We first summarize the cohort and the statistical structure of the input space to provide context for subsequent model performance. Figure 2 summarizes the dataset’s composition, the distribution of missing data, and the relationships among features. As Fig. 2(a), the dataset contains two outcome groups (injury and non-injury). Such an imbalance between these sets motivates the use of evaluation metrics sensitive to prevalence (e.g., AUPRC) and training methods that are sensitive to imbalance, since the majority class can sway naive accuracy. The missingness rate for each feature is reported in Fig. 2(b). All in all, missingness is modest and concentrated in a small number of variables, which points toward using median/mode imputation as a fixed default. However, more feature-specific missingness also warrants subsequent robustness tests that add missing values to recreate incomplete monitoring. To explain the multimodal structure in which the proposed architecture operates, Fig. 2(c) presents the feature inventory by modality and variable type as a single overview. The distribution shows that the benchmark is mostly continuous, with a small categorical component (e.g., position), which dictates the neural model’s encoding scheme (standardizing continuous variables and one-hot encoding or embeddings for categorical variables). Lastly, Fig. 2(d) plots pairwise correlations between continuous features. A number of correlations are anticipated (e.g., body-size variables are correlated, and some fitness tests share common variance). In contrast, numerous cross-modality correlations are moderate, suggesting that different modalities provide somewhat complementary information. This within-modality organization and cross-modality complementation encourage multimodal fusion rather than single-stream modeling.

Table 3 found that among the tested models, the feature-grouped attention model achieved the maximum mean AUROC, AUPRC, F1 score, balanced accuracy, Brier score, and ECE. The best practical improvement was achieved for the AUPRC metric, which is particularly useful in the context of class imbalance. Some of the models have overlapping confidence intervals, such as the single-stream MLP, concatenation fusion, and attention-based fusion. The results must therefore be taken as an indication of a low level but persistent advantage for clustered neural fusion and should not be interpreted as proof of superiority. There was no change in AUROC or AUPRC when using temperature scaling, but there was an improvement in Brier score and ECE, which is as expected since the probabilities are being rescaled, but does not alter the ranking of examples.

Figure 3 is used to complement the results presented in Table 3, these curves include both the ROC (Fig. 3(a)) and the precision-recall curves (Fig. 3(b)). The ROC curves show generally comparable ranking behavior for the better models, and the precision-recall curves offer a more informative picture of the models’ behavior when there is class imbalance. It is seen that the proposed attention-based model shows a higher mean precision-recall curve over multiple recall values, but the gap with the single-stream MLP and concatenation fusion does not seem to be significant at all recall values. This allows for a conservative conclusion: grouped fusion seems to be beneficial on this benchmark, but benefits must be confirmed with larger, independently gathered athlete datasets.

Calibration and decision-threshold analysis

Since we are dealing with the problem of deciding when a risk exists under uncertainty using injury-risk prediction, we would not only assess discrimination but also the accuracy of predicted probabilities. Calibration and thresholding behavior are reported in Fig. 5. The reliability diagram in Fig. 5(a) is prior to calibration, and we see deviations outside the diagonal, indicating systematic over- or under-confidence. Figure 5(b) shows the reliability diagram after temperature scaling, indicating greater consistency between predictions and the observed outcome frequency, especially in the middle and high-risk bins where decision sensitivity is of utmost importance. We also assess the impact of operational decisions on classification performance. The data in Fig. 5(c) were plotted as F1 score against decision threshold and indicate the threshold range where the balance between precision and recall is optimal. Figure 5(d) shows precision and recall as the threshold is varied: lower thresholds increase sensitivity at the cost of more false alarms, while higher thresholds reduce the number of alerts but may miss real injuries. This discussion justifies setting a threshold that is consistent with the desired use scenario (screening versus conservative alerting), while preserving calibrated probabilities to be used consistently.

Quantitative uncertainty analysis

In order to illustrate the calibration analysis, epistemic uncertainty was measured via Monte Carlo dropout during the course of inference. For each test instance, the trained model ran several random forward passes with dropout. The final injury risk score was obtained using the predictive mean, and the predictive variance of the stochastic passes was calculated as an uncertainty estimate. Predictions from the test were then assigned to the low-, medium-, and high-uncertainty groups by tertiles of prediction variance. The low uncertainty group had the best classification and calibration performance with an AUROC of 0.912, AUPRC of 0.664, F1 score of 0.650, and Brier score of 0.091 as reported in Table 6. The high uncertainty group, on the other hand, exhibited weaker discrimination (AUROC = 0.751) and probability reliability (AUPRC = 0.335, F1 = 0.389, Brier = 0.210). The results show that the greater the variability in MC dropout rates, the less predictable the results. Thus, uncertainty bounds provide a useful warning indicator and can be flagged as a high-uncertainty case to be interpreted with caution rather than as a completely confident automatic prediction.

Discussion

This paper has shown that predicting injury risk using routinely monitored athlete variables is aided by explicitly modeling multimodal variables and learning cross-domain interactions. In test-set experiments, the proposed multimodal deep model achieves better discrimination than classical baselines and a single-stream MLP, with the most evident benefits observed in precision-recall performance under class imbalance. As reported in the ablation results, the predictive signal is spread evenly across training exposure, fitness indicators, and lifestyle habits, and not due to one feature group dominating another. Attention/gated fusion offers an additional advantage over simple concatenation, with modalities per athlete weighted adaptively and more robust even when modalities are partially missing. The conclusion indicated that post-hoc scaling can enhance the reliability of the probabilities without affecting ranking effectiveness, enabling more defensible decisions with a threshold. Explainability analyses are consistent with the model-design aim in that they assign risk to interpretable variables and emphasize misclassifications arising from ambiguous or incomplete signals.

As applied, the findings justify a practical message for the sports training setting: the injury-risk classification on this benchmark benefits from modality-grouped encoding of exposure, capacity, recovery, and lifestyle context rather than reliance on workload proxies alone. A multimodal model can integrate diverse monitoring streams into a usable risk score for triage, flagging, or decision support, especially when combined with estimates of calibration and uncertainty to distinguish between high-confidence and ambiguous situations. The research’s sensitivity to training and fitness modalities indicates that staff can focus on regular exposure and readiness measures while still leveraging the benefits of lifestyle variables that often signal early signs of diminished recovery capacity. The robustness to missing-modality stresses also aligns with real-world operational conditions, where questionnaires are incomplete or data are unavailable. A fusion algorithm that degrades gracefully under simulated partial observability shows promise for operational settings, although field validation under authentic missingness patterns is needed before any deployment recommendation can be made.

Before, injury-prediction studies often relied on engineered summaries of training load and a small number of covariates, and performance constraints were often determined by the relative scarcity of labels and nonhomogeneous measurement practices. Our results support the broader phenomenon that integrating risk modeling is more effective in cases with multiple complementary sources, and they also explain the relevance of integration. Early fusion assumes that all inputs are represented as a flat vector, which can waste structure compared to modality-conscious encoders that induce representations that capture the statistical characteristics of each domain (e.g., varying scales, noise structure, and semantic meaning). Attention/gated fusion is also useful, since a modality’s usefulness is context-dependent: the same work rate may pose varying risks depending on recovery signals, fitness capacity, or injury history. Adaptive weighting is a tool that enables the articulation of context-sensitive relevance that would otherwise be hard to achieve with fixed-weight additive models. The explainability findings align with this description, with the model often relying on interactions between feature sets rather than on individual-variable stimuli. Another restriction is that all experiments were performed on internal splits of one public benchmark dataset. Internal reliability was improved by stratification, repeated random seeds, and bootstrap confidence intervals, but these are not a substitute for external validation. Thus, the outcomes of this study cannot be extrapolated to other teams, seasons, age categories, competition levels, injury definitions, or measurements. This is particularly relevant in sports injury prediction, where changes in the datasets can come from various coaching methods, athlete populations, sensors used, medical reporting, and the definition of exposure. Thus, the present results can only be used as internal benchmark evidence.

Conclusion and future work

This study investigated a reproducible benchmark for classifying injury risk in feature-grouped tabular athlete data. The proposed neural model first employed separate feature group encoders to encode demographics and anthropometrics, training factors, physical fitness indicators, and lifestyle habits, using the University Football Injury Prediction dataset, and then fused the encoded data with learned fusion. The proposed model outperformed logistic regression, random forest, gradient boosting, and one-stream MLP slightly in terms of mean discrimination and calibration metrics. These results should be taken with a grain of salt, however, due to the small sample size, class imbalance, cross-sectional nature of the data, and lack of complete documentation of original sampling, ordering, and injury labeling.

The framework should be tested in prospective cohorts of athletes with validated injury definitions and longitudinally collected exposure histories in future studies. Additionally, integrating wearable time series, training session documentation, recovery strategies, and clinical evaluations into future studies is needed to shift from cross-sectional classification to real-world injury forecasting. Such models should be considered for use as decision support for sport training or medical applications, but require larger external validation studies.

Several extensions could strengthen the evidence base for eventual decision-support applications, though none of the current findings are deployment-ready. Quantifying generalizability and establishing which modalities are consistent across settings are priorities for external validation in independent cohorts. A second way is to shift beyond snapshot features to longitudinal multimodal monitoring, incorporating high-frequency wearable streams, session context, and athlete-reported outcomes; this would allow sequence models to reflect load accumulation, recovery patterns, and acute deviations. Third, learning multiple tasks might leverage a common structure across related outcomes, e.g., predicting the presence and severity of injury, time loss, or performance readiness, thereby enhancing sample and clinical utility. Further effort is also required to formalize uncertainty-aware deployment policies (e.g., not acting when uncertain), to assess fairness across a variety of subgroup definitions, and to examine the use of human-in-the-loop, where the predictions are used to inform but never to decide on coaching and medical judgment.
