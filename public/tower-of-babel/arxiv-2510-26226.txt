Estimating heritability of survival traits using censored multiple variance component model
Do Hyun Kim, Hua Zhou, Brendon Chau, Aubrey Jensen, Judong Shen, Devan Mehrotra, Gang Li, Jin J. Zhou

Abstract
Characterizing the genetic basis of survival traits, such as age at disease onset, is critical for risk stratification, early intervention, and elucidating biological mechanisms that can inform therapeutic development. However, time‐to‐event outcomes in human cohorts are frequently right‐censored, complicating both the estimation and partitioning of total heritability. Modern biobanks linked to electronic health records offer the unprecedented power to dissect the genetic basis of age-at-diagnosis traits at large scale. Yet, few methods exist for estimating and partitioning the total heritability of censored survival traits. Existing methods impose restrictive distributional assumptions on genetic and environmental effects and are not scalable to large biobanks with a million subjects. We introduce a censored multiple variance component model to robustly estimate the total heritability of survival traits under right-censoring. We demonstrate through extensive simulations that the method provides accurate total heritability estimates of right-censored traits at censoring rates up to 80 % 80\% given sufficient sample size. The method is computationally efficient in estimating one hundred genetic variance components of a survival trait using large-scale biobank genotype data consisting of a million subjects and a million SNPs in under nine hours, including uncertainty quantification. We apply our method to estimate the total heritability of four age-at-diagnosis traits from the UK Biobank study. Our results establish a scalable and robust framework for heritability analysis of right-censored survival traits in large-scale genetic studies. Keywords: accelerated failure time (AFT), censored linear regression, linear mixed model (LMM), randomized numerical linear algebra (RandNLA), variance components model

Introduction

Modern biobanks, such as the UK Biobank, the Million Veteran Program, and the National Institutes of Health (NIH) All of Us Research Program
(
sudlow_uk_2015
;
gaziano_million_2016
;
sankar_precision_2017
)
, provide extensive longitudinal and genomic data. These resources enable large-scale studies of the genetic underpinnings of time-to-event outcomes, including chronic diseases, cancer, and neurodegenerative diseases.

These data facilitate genome-wide association studies (GWASs) that identify single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) or genes associated with survival traits via single-variant
(
dey_efficient_2022
;
bi_fast_2020
)
or multi-marker analyses
(
choi_variancecomponents_2024
;
sun_inference_2023
;
wu_multimarker_2021
;
chen_sequence_2014
)
. Indeed, recent GWASs have implicated genetic variants in survival traits such as type 2 diabetes, Alzheimer’s disease, heart disease, and fractures
(
dey_efficient_2022
;
bi_fast_2020
;
choi_variancecomponents_2024
;
sun_inference_2023
)
. An implicit but essential assumption in GWAS is that the disease has a genetic basis, i.e., heritability. Heritability is defined as the proportion of phenotypic variation in a population attributable to genetic variation
(
falconer_introduction_2009
;
visscher_heritability_2008
)
, and accurate estimation provides valuable insight into the polygenicity of complex traits.

In animal breeding, heritability estimation for survival traits has been used to select and breed cows for extended milk production
(
ducrocq1996bayesian
;
korsgaard1998bayesian
;
yazdi2002heritability
)
. These studies used Bayesian parametric hazard models with frailty terms whose variance components were estimated via Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods. While effective in small-scale settings, these approaches are unsuitable for modern human biobank data due to the simplicity of the models with a single genetic component and computational bottlenecks arising from MCMC-based estimation.

In human genetics, methods for investigating the role of genetic and environmental effects on censored survival traits have been developed for pedigree data. One class of methods adopted Bayesian parametric survival models with normally distributed frailty terms
(
pitkaniemi_genetic_2007
;
locatelli_correlated_2007
)
. These methods not only estimated genetic and family-related environmental variance components but also estimated the cure fraction of the cohort “immune” to developing the disease. These methods are limited by restrictive distributional assumptions and resource intensive MCMC computation for obtaining posterior parameter estimates. Another class relied on martingale residuals from Cox proportional hazards (Cox PH) models
(
yoo_practical_2001
;
wintrebert_assessing_2006
)
, but required normality assumptions on residuals or frailty terms. Additional approaches included random effects Cox PH model
(
pankratz_randomeffects_2005
)
and Tobit variance component model
(
epstein_tobit_2003
)
, both involving computationally intensive numerical integration. Overall, these methods are ill-suited for biobank-scale human data: they are designed for family data, computationally expensive, and unable to model multiple genetic variance components that capture different genomic characteristics.

More recently, efforts have been made to estimate the heritability of survival traits using population-level human genetic data
(
gorfine_heritability_2017
;
ojavee_genomic_2021
)
. Heritability Estimation using a Regularized Regression Approach (HERRA)
(
gorfine_heritability_2017
)
used an accelerated failure time (AFT) model as the base model and selected a subset of SNPs through multiple rounds of variable selection. An inverse probability censoring weighting approach was used to account for right-censoring. However, because HERRA does not estimate multiple genetic variance components, it is less robust to different genetic architectures, potentially introducing biases in heritability estimates
(
haplotype_reference_consortium_comparison_2018
)
.

Another method, BayesW
(
ojavee_genomic_2021
)
, also employed an AFT model but assumed a Weibull distribution for survival traits. It modeled genetic effects using a latent mixture of Normal distributions, allowing for multiple genetic variance components and improved robustness across different underlying genetic architectures. A scalable MCMC technique, leveraging message-passing interfaces (MPIs), was developed to obtain posterior distributions of heritability estimates. While the latent mixture assumption on genetic effects enhanced robustness, the Weibull distributional assumption for survival traits restricts the model’s flexibility. Additionally, despite its scalability, BayesW remains computationally intensive, requiring several days to analyze biobank-scale data.

In this paper, we introduce Censored Variance Component (CVC) model for estimating the narrow-sense heritability
h^{2}

(
visscher_heritability_2008
)
. Our method extends the scalable method-of-moments approach leveraging randomized trace estimator for analyzing and estimating heritability with biobank-scale genetic data
(
pazokitoroudi_efficient_2020
)
. The CVC model accommodates right-censored survival traits, is able to estimate partitioned heritability and robust to model misspecification, and scales efficiently to biobank-scale data. A key feature of our approach is the use of synthetic variables
(
KoulSusarlaVvanRyzin81RightCensor
;
Leurgans87RandomCensoring
;
Zheng87PseudoVariableCensor
)
, whose moments match the corresponding moments of the uncensored survival trait, enabling unbiased variance component estimation via least squares.

Through simulations on both synthetic and real UK Biobank genotypes, we demonstrate that our method provides accurate heritability estimates even under model misspecification induced by diverse genetic architectures. We also compare the CVC model’s performance to BayesW and liability-threshold (LT) models
(
lee_estimating_2011
)
and show superior performance of the CVC model in estimation accuracy and uncertainty quantification of total and partitioned heritability. We further apply our method to analyze age-at-diagnosis survival traits in UKB participants, and provide total heritability as well as partitioned heritability estimates of those traits.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. In Section 2, we introduce the CVC model, first- and second-moment synthetic variables, and discuss randomized trace estimator and jackknife standard error estimator. In Section 3, we present the CVC model’s performance when the model is correctly specified and when it is misspecified through simulation studies. We also compare the statistical performance of the CVC model to the ones of BayesW and LT models, and benchmark the running times of the CVC model against those of the BayesW model. In Section 4, we present the total and partitioned heritability estimates of four age-at-diagnosis traits from the UKB study. In Section 5, we provide concluding remarks, limitations and future direction. Additional technical details and simulation results are included in Supplementary Material.

2
Censored variance component model

We present the CVC model, which leverages synthetic variables to analyze right-censored survival traits. First, we introduce a multi-component linear random-effects model augmented by synthetic variables, enabling estimation of multiple variance components using a least-squares optimization. Next, we extend this model to a multi-component linear mixed-effects framework, incorporating fixed effects arising from confounders. We also briefly discuss a randomized trace estimator, which significantly improves computational efficiency when fitting the model to biobank-scale genotype data involving up to one million subjects and one million SNPs. Lastly, we describe the column-wise block jackknife method for estimating the standard errors of the heritability estimates.

2.1
Censored multi-component linear random-effects model with synthetic variables and total heritability

Suppose
{\bm{y}}
is an
N
-vector of phenotypes and
{\bm{X}}
is an
N\times M
standardized genotype matrix. We relate
{\bm{y}}
and
{\bm{X}}
through the following linear random-effects model:

\displaystyle{\bm{y}}\mid\bm{\beta}_{1},...,\bm{\beta}_{K},\bm{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}{\bm{X}}_{k}\bm{\beta}_{k}+\bm{\varepsilon},

(1)

\displaystyle\bm{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{D}_{e}({\mathbf{0}},\sigma_{e}^{2}{\bm{I}}_{N}),

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}_{k}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{D}_{k}\left({\mathbf{0}},\frac{\sigma_{k}^{2}}{M_{k}}{\bm{I}}_{M_{k}}\right),\ k\in\{1,...,K\},

where
{\bm{X}}_{k}
is an
N\times M_{k}
standardized genotype matrix for the partition
k
with
\sum_{k}M_{k}=M.

\bm{\beta}_{k}
is a random genetic effect associated with the
k
th genomic partition and
\mathcal{D}
refers to an arbitrary distribution.
\sigma^{2}_{k}
is the genetic variance component for the
k
th genomic partition and
\sigma^{2}_{e}
is the environmental variance component. We assume
\bm{\beta}_{1},...,\bm{\beta}_{K},\bm{\varepsilon}
are independent.

The total heritability is defined as

h^{2}_{\mathrm{SNP}}=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sigma_{k}^{2}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sigma_{k}^{2}+\sigma^{2}_{e}}

(2)

and the partitioned heritability for the partition
k
is defined as

h^{2}_{k}=\frac{\sigma_{k}^{2}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sigma_{k}^{2}+\sigma^{2}_{e}},\quad k\in\{1,...,K\}.

(3)

In Section
S.1
of Supplementary Material, we show that the heritability estimates based on the log-scale survival traits
y_{i}
are first-order approximations of the heritability of the survival traits
t_{i}
on the original scale.

Let
{\bm{K}}_{k}={\bm{X}}_{k}{\bm{X}}_{k}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}/M_{k}
be the genetic relationship matrix (GRM) associated with the
k
th genomic partition. Then,

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}{\bm{y}}{\bm{y}}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sigma^{2}_{k}{\bm{K}}_{k}+\sigma^{2}_{e}{\bm{I}}_{N}.

(4)

The method-of-moments estimates of variance components is obtained by minimizing the least squares criterion:

(\hat{\bm{\sigma}}^{2}_{g}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}},\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{e})=\arg\min_{\bm{\sigma}^{2}_{g},\sigma^{2}_{e}}||{\bm{y}}{\bm{y}}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}-(\sum_{k}\sigma^{2}_{k}{\bm{K}}_{k}+\sigma^{2}_{e}{\bm{I}})||_{F}^{2},

(5)

where
\bm{\sigma}_{g}^{2}=(\sigma^{2}_{1},...,\sigma^{2}_{K}){{}^{\mathrm{T}}}
. This is equivalent to solving the following normal equations

\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{T}}&{\bm{b}}\\
{\bm{b}}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}&N\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\sigma}^{2}_{g}\\
\sigma^{2}_{e}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{c}}\\
{\bm{y}}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}{\bm{y}}\end{bmatrix},

(6)

where
T_{k\ell}=\operatorname{tr}{\bm{K}}_{k}{\bm{K}}_{\ell}
,
b_{k}=\operatorname{tr}{\bm{K}}_{k}=N
, and
c_{k}={\bm{y}}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}{\bm{K}}_{k}{\bm{y}}
for
k,\ell=1,2,...,K.

Now, in the context of survival traits, we assume
y_{i}=\log t_{i}
where
t_{i}
are time-to-event outcomes we observe. Since
y_{i}
are subject to right censoring, what we observe are right-censored traits
u_{i}=\min(y_{i},r_{i})
with their censoring indicators
\delta_{i}=\mathbbm{1}_{\{y_{i}\leq r_{i}\}}
, where
r_{i}
are censoring variables on a log scale, which is assumed to be unconditionally independent of one another and
y_{i}
. This motivates us to introduce synthetic variables by
Zheng87PseudoVariableCensor
who proposed a transformation of the form

y^{*}_{i}=\delta_{i}\varphi_{1}(u_{i})+(1-\delta_{i})\varphi_{2}(u_{i})

such that
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}(y^{*}_{i})=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}(y_{i}).
Here we employ the following first moment synthetic variable:

y^{*}_{i1}=y^{*}_{i1}(G)=u_{i}+\int_{-\infty}^{u_{i}}\frac{G(t)}{1-G(t)}dt,

(7)

where
G(t)
is the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of
r_{i}
. This form of the synthetic variable was first introduced by
Leurgans87RandomCensoring
. We further generalize it to the second moment synthetic variable

y^{*}_{i2}=y^{*}_{i2}(G)=u_{i}^{2}+\int_{-\infty}^{u_{i}}\frac{2tG(t)}{1-G(t)}dt,

(8)

which satisfies
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}(y^{*}_{i2})=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}(y_{i}^{2})
. Define the synthetic variable matrix
\bm{Y}^{*}
with entries

\bm{Y}^{*}_{ij}=\bm{Y}^{*}_{ij}(G)=\begin{cases}y^{*}_{i2}&i=j,\\
y^{*}_{i1}y^{*}_{j1}&i\neq j.\end{cases}

(9)

Then,

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}(\bm{Y}^{*})=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}{\bm{y}}{\bm{y}}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}

(10)

since
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}(y^{*}_{i2})=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}(y_{i}^{2})
for all
i
, and
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}(y^{*}_{i1}y^{*}_{j1})=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}(y_{i}y_{j})
for
i\neq j.
In Section
S.2
of Supplementary Material, we show derivations of first-, second- and mixed-moment synthetic variables.

Returning to our original least-squares criterion (
5
), since
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}{\bm{y}}{\bm{y}}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\bm{Y}^{*}
, we can write

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\bm{Y}^{*}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sigma^{2}_{k}{\bm{K}}_{k}+\sigma^{2}_{e}{\bm{I}}_{N},

(11)

leading to the least-squares criterion:

(\hat{\bm{\sigma}}^{2}_{g}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}},\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{e})=\arg\min_{\bm{\sigma}_{g}^{2},\sigma_{e}^{2}}||\bm{Y}^{*}(\hat{G})-(\sum_{k}\sigma^{2}_{k}{\bm{K}}_{k}+\sigma^{2}_{e}{\bm{I}})||_{F}^{2},

where
\hat{G}
is the Kaplan-Meier estimator for
G
. The normal equation becomes

\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{T}}&{\bm{b}}\\
{\bm{b}}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}&N\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\sigma}^{2}_{g}\\
\sigma^{2}_{e}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{c}}\\
\operatorname{tr}\bm{Y}^{*}(\hat{G})\end{bmatrix},

(12)

which is same as (
6
) except for
\operatorname{tr}\bm{Y}^{*}
on the right-hand side of the equation. We opt not to impose nonnegative constraints on the variance components, which in the ideal case where the nuisance function
G
is known would yield an unbiased estimator; with
G
replaced by
\hat{G}
, unbiasedness is not guaranteed, but simulation results show minimal bias under low to moderate censoring and diminishing bias with increasing sample size. The package
CVC.jl
provides both constrained and unconstrained estimates, and all analyses in this paper use the unconstrained version.

2.2
Censored multi-component linear mixed-effects model

Assume the following linear mixed-effects model

{\bm{y}}\mid\bm{\varepsilon},\bm{\beta}_{1},...,\bm{\beta}_{K}={\bm{W}}\bm{\alpha}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\bm{X}}_{k}\bm{\beta}_{k}+\bm{\varepsilon},

(13)

where
{\bm{W}}
is an
N\times C
covariate matrix and
\bm{\alpha}
is a
C
-vector of fixed effects. Note that (
13
) now incorporates the fixed effect
\bm{\alpha}
, hence the name
mixed
-effects model, as opposed to the
random
-effects model in (
1
). Let
{\bm{V}}={\bm{I}}-{\bm{P}}_{W}
be the orthogonal projector onto the null space of
{\bm{W}}
. Then

{\bm{V}}{\bm{y}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\bm{V}}{\bm{X}}_{k}\bm{\beta}_{k}+{\bm{V}}\bm{\varepsilon}

and

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}{\bm{V}}{\bm{y}}{\bm{y}}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}{\bm{V}}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sigma^{2}_{k}{\bm{V}}{\bm{K}}_{k}{\bm{V}}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}+\sigma^{2}_{e}{\bm{V}}{\bm{V}}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}.

(14)

Since
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}({\bm{V}}\bm{Y}^{*}{\bm{V}}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}})=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}({\bm{V}}{\bm{y}}{\bm{y}}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}{\bm{V}}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}})
, the least squares criterion becomes

(\hat{\bm{\sigma}}^{2}_{g}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}},\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{e})=\arg\min_{\bm{\sigma}_{g}^{2},\sigma_{e}^{2}}\left\|{\bm{V}}\bm{Y}^{*}(\hat{G}){\bm{V}}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}-\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sigma^{2}_{k}{\bm{V}}{\bm{K}}_{k}{\bm{V}}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}+\sigma^{2}_{e}{\bm{V}}{\bm{V}}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}\right)\right\|^{2}_{F},

which is equivalent to the following normal equation

\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{T}}&{\bm{b}}\\
{\bm{b}}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}&N-r\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\sigma}^{2}_{g}\\
\sigma_{e}^{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{c}}\\
\operatorname{tr}\bm{Y}^{*}(\hat{G}){\bm{V}}\end{bmatrix}

(15)

with
T_{k\ell}=\operatorname{tr}{\bm{K}}_{k}{\bm{V}}{\bm{K}}_{\ell}{\bm{V}}
,
b_{k}=\operatorname{tr}{\bm{V}}{\bm{K}}_{k}
, and
c_{k}=\operatorname{tr}\bm{Y}^{*}(\hat{G}){\bm{V}}{\bm{K}}_{k}{\bm{V}}
for
k,\ell=1,2,...,K.

2.3
Randomized trace estimator

The computational bottleneck in evaluating the terms in the normal equation is computing the term
T_{k\ell}
. In the random-effects model, this term is
\operatorname{tr}{\bm{K}}_{k}{\bm{K}}_{\ell}
and its computational complexity is
O(N^{2}M_{k})
, which is quadratic in
N
and becomes intractable for biobank-scale
N
. To improve the computational efficiency, we employ a randomized trace estimator

\widehat{\operatorname{tr}{\bm{K}}_{s}{\bm{K}}_{t}}=\frac{1}{B}\frac{1}{M_{k}}\frac{1}{M_{\ell}}\sum_{b=1}^{B}{\bm{z}}_{b}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}{\bm{K}}_{k}{\bm{K}}_{\ell}{\bm{z}}_{b},\quad z_{1},...,z_{B}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}({\mathbf{0}},{\bm{I}}_{N}),

where
B
is the number of independent random Gaussian vectors
z_{b}
. Taking the expectation of the estimator, one can readily see that it is an unbiased estimator of the trace. The computational complexity reduces to
O(NM_{k}B)
, which is now linear in
N
. It has been shown that
B=10
is sufficient to obtain a sufficiently accurate estimate of the trace while only minimally impacting the accuracy of heritability estimates
(
wu_scalable_2018
)
.

2.4
Jackknife standard error estimation of heritability estimates

To obtain the standard errors of the heritability estimates, we employ column-wise block jackknife. We partition an
N\times M_{k}
standardized genotype matrix
{\bm{X}}_{k}
associated with the variance component
\sigma_{k}^{2}
into
J
non-overlapping blocks

{\bm{X}}_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{X}}_{k}^{(1)}&{\bm{X}}_{k}^{(2)}&\cdots&{\bm{X}}_{k}^{(J)}\end{bmatrix}.

Let
{\bm{X}}_{k}^{(-j)}
be the
k
th genotype matrix
{\bm{X}}_{k}
with
{\bm{X}}_{k}^{(j)}
block removed, and
{\bm{K}}_{k}^{(-j)}
be the GRM constructed from
{\bm{X}}_{k}^{(-j)}.

The normal equation for the
j
th block jackknife estimate is

\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{T}}^{(j)}&{\bm{b}}^{(j)}\\
{\bm{b}}^{(j)}{{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}}&N-C\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\sigma}_{g}{{}^{2}}^{(j)}\\
\sigma_{e}{{}^{2}}^{(j)}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{c}}^{(j)}\\
\operatorname{tr}\bm{Y}^{*}(\hat{G}){\bm{V}}\end{bmatrix}

where
T_{k\ell}^{(j)}=\operatorname{tr}{\bm{K}}_{k}^{(-j)}{\bm{V}}{\bm{K}}_{\ell}^{(-j)}{\bm{V}}
,
b_{k}^{(j)}=\operatorname{tr}{\bm{V}}{\bm{K}}_{k}^{(-j)}
and
c_{k}^{(j)}=\operatorname{tr}\bm{Y}^{*}(\hat{G}){\bm{V}}{\bm{K}}_{k}^{(-j)}{\bm{V}}
for
k,\ell\in\{1,...,K\}
and
j\in\{1,...,J\}.
If we define the
j
th block jackknife estimate of
h^{2}
as
\hat{h}^{2(j)}=\frac{{\mathbf{1}}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}\hat{\bm{\sigma}}_{g}{{}^{2}}^{(j)}}{{\mathbf{1}}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}\hat{\bm{\sigma}}_{g}{{}^{2}}^{(j)}+\hat{\sigma}_{e}{{}^{2}}^{(j)}}
, then the block jackknife standard error estimate of
\hat{h}^{2}
is given by

\hat{\sigma}_{\text{jack}}=\sqrt{\frac{J-1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}(\hat{h}^{2}{{}^{(j)}}-\hat{\mu}_{\text{jack}})^{2}},\quad\hat{\mu}_{\text{jack}}=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\hat{h}^{2}{{}^{(j)}}.

(16)

For all our data analyses, we set
J=100
, as this has been shown to be sufficient for obtaining unbiased standard error estimates
(
pazokitoroudi_efficient_2020
)
. The derivation of working arrays for one-pass scanning of genotype data is provided in Section
S.3
of Supplementary Material, and our
CVC.jl
implementation enables efficient fitting of the proposed model.

3
Simulation studies

We perform simulation studies to assess the performance of the CVC model. We consider two scenarios—one where the model is correctly specified and one where it is misspecified. In the correctly specified scenario, we evaluate the model’s performance under varying sample sizes, numbers of SNPs, and numbers of genomic partitions. In the misspecified scenario, we do not assume that the genetic variance components of SNPs are identical within associated genomic partitions. This scenario is designed to assess the robustness of the CVC model in estimating heritability under different underlying genetic architectures, determined by the percentage of causal SNPs (i.e., SNPs with non-zero genetic variance components) and by the degree to which the magnitude of the genetic effect variance is linked to minor allele frequency (MAF) and linkage disequilibrium (LD) score (referred to as MAF-LD coupling). The LD score of a SNP is defined as the sum of squared correlations between that SNP and all other SNPs, often restricted to those within a specified megabase window. Specifically, we use LDAK (linkage-disequilibrium adjusted kinships) scores
(
speed_improved_2012
)
, which are modified LD scores that account for local LD structure by assigning higher weights to SNPs in regions of low LD.

We also compare the performance of the CVC model to other methods in estimating heritability, evaluating relative bias, mean squared error (MSE), mean absolute error (MAE) and coverage. For total heritability estimates, we compare the CVC model to BayesW and LT models. For partitioned heritability estimates, we compare the CVC and BayesW models. We do not include HERRA in the comparisons because it is not designed to accommodate multiple variance components, and no publicly available software implementation exists.

3.1
Correctly specified model

We assess the performance of the CVC model in estimating the total heritability
h_{\text{SNP}}^{2}
and the partitioned heritability
h_{k}^{2}
for the
k
th partition when the model is correctly specified. We used the following generative model to simulate uncensored survival traits on the log scale:

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}_{k}

\displaystyle\sim N\left({\mathbf{0}},\frac{\sigma_{k}^{2}}{M_{k}}{\bm{I}}_{M_{k}}\right),

\displaystyle{\bm{y}}\mid\bm{\beta}

\displaystyle\sim N\left({\bm{W}}\bm{\alpha}+\sum_{k}{\bm{X}}_{k}\bm{\beta}_{k},\sigma_{e}^{2}{\bm{I}}\right),

where
{\bm{W}}
is a randomly generated covariate matrix of size
N\times C
(including an intercept column of ones),
{\bm{X}}_{k}
is the
k
th partitioned genotype matrix of size
N\times M_{k}
, and
\sigma_{e}^{2}
is the environmental variance component. The genomic variance components
\sigma_{k}^{2}
were sampled uniformly from the interval
(0,1).
The environmental variance component
\sigma_{e}^{2}
was set to
\left(\frac{1}{h^{2}}-1\right)\sum_{k}\sigma_{k}^{2}
for a given total heritability
h_{\text{SNP}}^{2}\in\{0.2,0.5,0.8\}
. The fixed effects
\alpha_{1}
,…,
\alpha_{C}
were uniformly sampled from the interval
(0,1)
.

We obtained
{\bm{X}}_{k}
by partitioning standardized genotype matrix
{\bm{X}}
into
K
partitions. Each SNP in the
k
th partition shared the same associated variance component
\sigma_{k}^{2}/M_{k}
. The standardized genotype matrix
{\bm{X}}
is the standardized form of the genotype matrix
{\bm{G}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{g}}_{1}&\cdots&{\bm{g}}_{M}\end{bmatrix}
. The
(i,j)
-th entry of
{\bm{X}}
is defined as

x_{ij}=\frac{1}{N}(g_{ij}-\bar{g}_{j})^{2},\quad\bar{g}_{j}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}g_{ij}.

The genotype matrix
{\bm{G}}=(g_{ij})
was generated such that

g_{1j},...,g_{Nj}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\text{Bin}(2,f_{j})

with a first-order autoregressive correlation structure among the SNPs, parametrized by
\rho=0.1

(
jiang_set_2021
)
. The MAF
f_{j}
was generated as
f_{j}=0.01+0.49\times\text{Beta}(0.2,0.8).

To generate right-censored outcomes
u_{i}=\min(y_{i},r_{i})
for
i=1,...,N
with a desired censoring rate
\Delta\in\{0.2,0.5,0.8\}
, we simulated censoring variables
r_{i}=r_{i}(\mu_{c})
from

c_{1},...,c_{N}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mu_{c}+\sigma_{e}N(0,1),

where
\mu_{c}
is the solution to the equation

\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbbm{1}_{\{y_{i}>r_{i}(\mu_{c})\}}}{N}-\Delta=0.

We set the number of covariates to
C=10
, the number of jackknife blocks to
J=100
, and the number of random Gaussian vectors to
B=10
.

3.1.1
Impact of sample size

We evaluate the impact of sample size on the CVC model’s performance on estimating total heritability
\hat{h}^{2}_{\text{SNP}}
(Figures
1
a,
S.7
). Simulations were conducted with
N\in\{10,000,25,000,50,000\}
,

3.2
Model misspecfication induced by underlying genetic architectures

We evaluate the accuracy of the CVC model in estimating and partitioning total heritability
h^{2}_{\text{SNP}}
under different genetic architectures. In Section
3.1
, we assumed that all SNPs within a partition shared the same variance component; here, we allow each SNP to have a unique variance component determined by its MAF and LD. We simulated the phenotypes using the covariate matrix
{\bm{W}}
and standardized genotype matrix
{\bm{X}}
consisting of
N=276,169
unrelated participants of self-reported British white ancestry from the UKB study. We excluded subjects who were outliers for the genotype heterozygosity or missingness, showed mismatch between genetic and self-reported sex, and had putative sex chromosome aneuploidy. The covariate matrix
{\bm{W}}
included an intercept and 23 covariates, including sex (self-reported), birth year, assessment center and top 20 genetic principal components (PCs). The genotype data included
M=592,454
SNPs meeting the following criteria: the UKB Axiom array SNPs, autosomal SNPs with
\leq 1\%
missingness, passing Hardy–Weinberg test at the significance level of
10^{-7}
, and residing outside MHC region (Chr6:28-33 Mb). Section
S.4
of Supplementary Material contains the UKB Field IDs used to apply the filtering criteria. The simulated data were created using
CVCData.jl
, a package written in
Julia
programming language for fidelity and reproducibility.

We simulated uncensored survival traits with heritability
h^{2}\in\{0.2,0.5,0.8\}
under different genetic architectures:

\displaystyle\sigma_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle=s_{j}w_{j}^{b}[f_{j}(1-f_{j})]^{a},

(17)

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}=(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},...,\beta_{M}){{}^{\mathrm{T}}}

\displaystyle\sim N({\mathbf{0}},\,\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_{1}^{2},\sigma_{2}^{2},...,\sigma^{2}_{M})),

\displaystyle{\bm{y}}\mid\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}

\displaystyle\sim N({\bm{W}}\bm{\alpha}+{\bm{X}}\bm{\beta},\sigma_{e}^{2}{\bm{I}}_{N}),

where
a\in\{0,0.75\},b\in\{0,1\}
and
\sigma_{e}^{2}=\left(\frac{1}{h^{2}}-1\right)\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sigma_{j}^{2}
. Here,
s_{j}\in\{0,1\}
denotes the causal variant status,
w_{j}
is the LDAK score, and
f_{j}
is the MAF of SNP
j
. The parameters
a
and
b
control the extent to which the variances of genetic effects depend on LD and MAF, respectively. For example, when both
a
and
b
are zero, the variances of all genetic effects are identical across SNPs, reducing to the correctly specified model.

For each model, we varied the causal variant rates (CVRs) which were set to either 1% or 100%. Causal SNPs were selected based on MAFs from one of three intervals:
[0,0.5]
,
[0.01,0.05]
and
[0.05,0.5]
. Specifically, for CVR = 1%, we randomly selected 1% of all SNPs to be causal, restricting selection to SNPs whose MAFs were within either
[0,0.5]
,
[0.01,0.05]
or
[0.05,0.5]
. For CVR = 100%, all SNPs with MAF in
[0,0.5]
were set as causal. The fixed effects
\alpha_{c}
were sampled uniformly from the interval
(0,1).

We partitioned the genotype data into 24 LD-MAF bins, where four LD bins were based on LDAK score quartiles, and six MAF bins were based on the intervals created by the MAF knots:
0.01,0.02,0.03,0.04
and
0.05
. This partitioning assumed each partition shared the same variance component magnitude, which differed from the generative model in (
17
), thereby inducing model misspecification.

3.3
Comparisons to other methods

We compare the performance of the CVC, BayesW and LT models by evaluating bias, MSE, MAE and coverage of true heritability under two different error distributions: normal and Gumbel (Tables
4
,
S.18
, Figures
4
,
S.14
,
S.15
,
S.16
). Coverage is defined as the proportion of confidence intervals (for CVC or LT) or credible intervals (for BayesW) containing the true heritability across data replicates. For total heritability, we compare the CVC model with both the BayesW and LT models; for partitioned heritability, we compare the CVC model with the BayesW model only.

Simulation data were generated under the correctly specified model described in Section
3.1
, except in the Gumbel error distribution setting. In that scenario, log-scale survival traits were generated as

{\bm{y}}\mid\bm{\beta}\sim\text{Gumbel}\left({\bm{W}}\bm{\alpha}+\sum_{k}{\bm{X}}_{k}\bm{\beta}_{k},\sigma_{e}^{2}{\bm{I}}\right)

such that the BayesW model is correctly specified, given its assumption that the conditional distribution of survival traits follows a Weibull distribution.

We also considered simulation data generated under the misspecified model described in Section
3.2
; however, the BayesW model produced errors for all data replicates and chains, preventing comparison in this scenario.

Simulation settings included
h^{2}_{\text{SNP}}\in\{0.2,0.5,0.8\}
,
\Delta\in\{0.2,0.5,0.8\}
,
N=20000
,
M=20000
,
C=10
and
K=5
with 100 replicates per setting. Across all error distributions, replicates and chains, the BayesW model failed to fit datasets with
\Delta=0.5
and
0.8
; thus, we report comparisons only for
\Delta=0.2
.

For the BayesW model, we generated MCMC samples from five independent chains, each of length
3,000
with a burn-in of
1,000
and thinning of
5
, for each data replicate. The number of quadrature points was set to
25
. The mean of the posterior samples was used as the point estimate for heritability, and credible intervals were computed from the corresponding posterior quantiles. In the normal error scenario, all chains failed in the
93
rd replicate when
h^{2}_{\text{SNP}}=0.8
. In Gumbel error scenario, for
h^{2}_{\text{SNP}}=0.2
,
33
data replicates failed in at least one chain; thus, results for this setting are based on the remaining
67
replicates. See Section
S.6
of Supplementary Material for BayesW software configurations and errors messages.

For the LT model, we fit the CVC model without the synthetic-variable transformation, treating the censoring indicator
\delta_{i}
as a continuous quantitative trait (case:
\delta_{i}=1
, control:
\delta_{i}=0
). The observed-scale heritability estimate,
\hat{h}^{2}_{\text{binary}}
, was then converted to the liability scale
\hat{h}^{2}_{\ell}
, following
(
hou_accurate_2019
)
:

\hat{h}^{2}_{\ell}=\frac{d^{2}_{\text{pop}}(1-d_{\text{pop}})^{2}}{[f(L)]^{2}d_{\text{GWAS}}(1-d_{\text{GWAS}})}\hat{h}^{2}_{\text{binary}},

where
f(z)
is the standard normal density and
L=\Phi{{}^{-1}}(1-d_{\text{pop}})
is the liability threshold, with
\Phi(z)
denoting the standard normal CDF. We assumed the population prevalence
d_{\text{pop}}=1-\Delta
, and because case-control status was derived from time-to-event data, we set the sample prevalence
d_{\text{GWAS}}
equal to
d_{\text{pop}}
. Under these assumptions,

\hat{h}^{2}_{\ell}=\frac{\Delta(1-\Delta)}{[f\left(\Phi{{}^{-1}}(\Delta)\right)]^{2}}\hat{h}^{2}_{\text{binary}}.

Table
4
presents the relative bias, MSE, and MAE of total heritability estimates for all models. Across all simulation settings, the CVC model consistently achieves superior accuracy, exhibiting smaller relative biases than both of the BayesW and LT models. In both error scenarios, the LT model performs poorly, with relative bias ranging from
-95.39\%
to
81.61\%
, whereas the CVC model’s relative bias ranges from
-22.49\%
to
4.81\%
. For the normal error scenario with
\Delta=0.2
, the CVC model’s relative bias is minimal (
-0.15\%
to
0.20\%
) compared with the BayesW model (
-1.47\%
to
59.41\%
). In the Gumbel error scenario, the CVC model’s relative bias ranges from
-0.39\%
to
1.66\%
, while the BayesW model’s relative bias ranges from
1.02\%
to
116.52\%
. The CVC model also yields consistently lower MSE and MAE than the BayesW model across all settings. Taken together, these results indicate that the CVC model is robust to different underlying survival trait distributions, whereas the BayesW and LT models are not, leading to substantial inaccuracies in total heritability estimates.

For uncertainty quantification of total heritability, Figures
4
and
S.14
show that the CVC model is well calibrated across all nominal levels (0.10-0.99) and simulation settings, with no statistically significant differences between nominal and actual coverage at the 95% level in most cases. This suggests that the CVC model’s block jackknife standard errors provide accurate confidence intervals when the model is correctly specified. By contrast, the BayesW and LT models are severely overconfident and show substantially miscalibrated. Thus, when the distributional assumptions of the BayesW model do not hold, it cannot yield accurate inference for total heritability.

Table
S.18
presents the partitioned heritability results, comparing the CVC and BayesW models. Across all
h^{2}_{\text{SNP}}\in\{0.2,0.5,0.8\}

-1.52\%
to
32.64\%

-14.85\%

937.80\%

to
48.87\%
, whereas the BayesW model’s relative bias ranges from
-29.13\%
to
1880.41\%
. The CVC model also attains lower or comparable MSE and MAE when total heritability is
0.2
or
0.5
, and only slightly higher values when total heritability is
0.8
; these differences are marginal. Overall, the CVC model provides more accurate partitioned heritability estimates than the BayesW model.

For uncertainty quantification of partitioned heritability, Figures
S.15
and
S.16
show that, across all settings and bins—except for a few cases—the CVC model’s actual coverage does not significantly differ from nominal coverage at the 95% level, whereas the BayesW model is overconfident in most scenarios. These findings further underscore the CVC model’s superior uncertainty quantification for partitioned heritability compared to the BayesW model.

5
Discussion

We have proposed a novel method to robustly quantify both total and partitioned heritability of right-censored survival traits. The method is computationally efficient and highly scalable, as demonstrated by the analysis of genotype data with one million subjects and one million SNPs in under nine hours. Simulation studies show that the CVC model can accurately estimate the total heritability for survival traits generated under diverse genetic architectures, and that it outperforms the BayesW model in terms of accuracy, uncertainty quantification and computational efficiency.

When the underlying data-generating process follows an accelerated failure time model with a given censoring rate, the LT model fails to provide accurate heritability and standard error estimates. This is expected, as the LT model uses only disease status and ignores the timing of the event. Consequently, direct comparison between the heritability of disease status and that of a survival trait such as age at onset may be inappropriate: the former measures the proportion of the variance in lifetime risk attributable to genetic factors, whereas the latter measures the proportion of variance in a censored continuous trait attributable to genetic factors. Indeed,
pitkaniemi_genetic_2007
reported that, while substantial familial variation exists in susceptibility to diabetic nephropathy (DN), variation in time to DN is less influenced by shared familial factors. These findings suggest that the two trait types are related phenotypes but not identical, with both shared and distinct genetic architectures
(
feng_findings_2020
)
.

In our real-data analysis of age at diagnosis of smoking initiation, our heritability estimates using the entire cohort deviated somewhat from previous estimates obtained after excluding censored subjects. This is not surprising, as removing subjects still at risk changes the definition of heritability to apply only to the affected subset. Similarly, ignoring censoring and treating truncated values of censored traits as uncensored can lead to substantial bias, with the magnitude of bias increasing with the censoring rate
(
taraszka_abstract_2024
;
epstein_tobit_2003
)
. In contrast, the CVC model analyzes the full cohort, accounting for right censoring in a principled manner and avoiding ad hoc preprocessing.

Several limitations of the proposed method suggest directions for future research. First, the accuracy and precision of estimates decline as censoring rates increase, with substantial deterioration for traits with very high censoring (
\Delta>0.8
). Nevertheless, many traits of scientific interest, such as age at menopause and time to menarche
(
ojavee_genomic_2021
)
, have relatively low censoring rates, for which accurate and precise estimates can be obtained. The method’s computational efficiency also enables analysis of biobank-scale datasets (e.g., 500,000 subjects or more), which can improve precision even for highly censored traits. Since estimation is based on least squares, future work could explore weighted least squares using working variances, which may further improve precision.

Second, in many settings, a subset of the cohort may be nonsusceptible and will never experience the event of interest. Our current framework does not account for this “cured” subgroup. Previous work has estimated both the cure fraction and the variance components for age at onset using family data
(
pitkaniemi_genetic_2007
;
locatelli_correlated_2007
)
. Extending the CVC framework to jointly model cure fraction and heritability could allow estimation of both lifetime risk and age at onset heritability, providing insight into differences in their genetic architectures. Moreover, because censoring is relevant only among the susceptible subgroup in such models, the effective censoring rate may decrease, potentially improving accuracy and precision.

Third, in all analyses we set the number of independent random Gaussian vectors to
B=10
and the number of jackknife blocks to
J=100
, following previous work
(
wu_scalable_2018
;
pazokitoroudi_efficient_2020
)
. Our simulations suggest these settings are sufficient to achieve accurate estimates and standard errors across a range of scenarios. However, the minimal values of
B
and
J
required to maintain accuracy, and the point at which further increases yield only marginal gains, remain unclear. A systematic sensitivity analysis would help clarify these thresholds.

Finally, the CVC model requires access to individual-level genotype and phenotype data. As more biobanks with linked genomic data become available, developing methods for summary-level data will be crucial for both scalability and privacy. Several approaches have been proposed for estimating heritability of continuous traits from summary statistics
(
schizophrenia_working_group_of_the_psychiatric_genomics_consortium_ld_2015
;
speed_sumher_2019
;
jeong_scalable_2024
)
. Building on these ideas to accommodate survival traits would enable large-scale heritability analyses and enrichment studies in datasets with tens of millions of SNPs and multiple functional annotations
(
finucane_heritability_2018
)
, thereby providing a more detailed understanding of their genetic architecture.

In the biological and health sciences, the heritability of time-to-event traits provides critical insight into the genetic architecture of complex outcomes where event timing is key. Our proposed method offers a scalable framework for inference on the heritability of survival traits from high-dimensional genetic data. Continued methodological development in this area will be essential for dissecting complex genetic architectures, including those influenced by rare variants, epistasis, and gene–environment interactions.

Appendix S.1
Heritability in observed time scale

We show that the heritability in the observed scale,
h^{2}_{o}
, is approximately equal to the heritability in the log-time scale,
h^{2}
. For the
i
th subject the LMM looks like

y_{i}=\log T_{i}={\bm{w}}_{i}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}\bm{\alpha}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\bm{x}}_{ik}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}\bm{\beta}_{k}+\epsilon_{i}

with
\bm{\beta}_{k}\sim\mathcal{D}({\mathbf{0}},\frac{\sigma_{k}^{2}}{M_{k}}{\bm{I}}_{M_{k}})
,
\bm{\varepsilon}\sim(0,\sigma_{e}^{2}{\bm{I}})
and
\bm{\beta}_{1},...,\bm{\beta}_{K},\bm{\varepsilon}
are independent. Let
\mu_{i}={\bm{w}}_{i}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}\bm{\alpha}
and
g_{i}=\sum_{k}{\bm{x}}_{ik}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}\bm{\beta}_{k}
. Then,

\operatorname{\mathbb{V}ar}g_{i}=\sum_{k}\sigma_{k}^{2}({\bm{K}}_{k})_{ii}\approx\sum_{k}\sigma_{k}^{2}=:\sigma_{g}^{2}.

assuming the estimated genetic similarity of a subject to himself,
({\bm{K}}_{k})_{ii}
, is close to one. We have defined the total heritability in log-time scale to be

h^{2}=\frac{\sigma_{g}^{2}}{\sigma_{g}^{2}+\sigma_{e}^{2}}.

The total variance of time-to-event phenotype of the
i
th subject is given by

\operatorname{\mathbb{V}ar}T_{i}=\operatorname{\mathbb{V}ar}\exp(\mu_{i}+g_{i}+\epsilon_{i})=\{\exp(\mu_{i})\}^{2}\operatorname{\mathbb{V}ar}\exp(g_{i}+\epsilon_{i})=:\gamma_{i}^{2}\operatorname{\mathbb{V}ar}\exp(g_{i}+\epsilon_{i}).

Similar to the
h^{2}_{o}
defined in
ducrocq_two_1999
, we define

h^{2}_{o}=\frac{\operatorname{\mathbb{V}ar}(\gamma_{i}\exp g_{i})}{\operatorname{\mathbb{V}ar}\{\gamma_{i}\exp(g_{i}+\epsilon_{i})\}}=\frac{\operatorname{\mathbb{V}ar}\exp g_{i}}{\operatorname{\mathbb{V}ar}\exp(g_{i}+\epsilon_{i})}.

For a random variable
X
with
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}X=0
, the first-order Taylor approximation of
\exp X
about
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}X=0
is

\exp X\approx 1+X\implies\operatorname{\mathbb{V}ar}\exp X\approx\operatorname{\mathbb{V}ar}X.

Thus,

h_{o}^{2}\approx\frac{\operatorname{\mathbb{V}ar}g_{i}}{\operatorname{\mathbb{V}ar}(g_{i}+\epsilon_{i})}=\frac{\sigma_{g}^{2}}{\sigma_{g}^{2}+\sigma_{e}^{2}}=h^{2}.

Similarly, if we define
g_{ik}={\bm{x}}_{ik}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}\bm{\beta}_{k}
, then the heritability associated with the
k
th genotype partition in the observed scale can be defined as

h_{ok}^{2}=\frac{\operatorname{\mathbb{V}ar}\exp g_{ik}}{\operatorname{\mathbb{V}ar}\exp(g_{i}+\epsilon_{i})}\approx\frac{\sigma_{k}^{2}}{\sigma_{g}^{2}+\sigma_{e}^{2}}=h^{2}_{k}.

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{BM_{k}^{(-j)}M_{\ell}^{(-j)}}\langle{\bm{U}}_{(k,j)}-\tilde{{\bm{U}}}_{(k,j)},{\bm{U}}_{(\ell,j)}-{\bm{H}}{\bm{H}}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}{\bm{U}}_{(\ell,j)}\rangle

4.

c_{i}=\operatorname{tr}\bm{Y}^{*}{\bm{V}}{\bm{K}}_{i}{\bm{V}}=\operatorname{tr}{\bm{K}}_{k}\bm{Y}^{*}-2\operatorname{tr}{\bm{K}}_{k}{\bm{P}}\bm{Y}^{*}+\operatorname{tr}{\bm{P}}{\bm{K}}_{k}{\bm{P}}\bm{Y}^{*}
:

(a)

\operatorname{tr}{\bm{K}}_{k}{\bm{P}}\bm{Y}^{*}=\frac{1}{M_{k}}[\tilde{{\bm{R}}}_{(k,0)}+\tilde{{\bm{Q}}}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}_{(k,0)}({\bm{H}}{\bm{H}}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}\bm{y}^{*}_{1})]

where

\displaystyle{\bm{R}}_{(k,j)}

\displaystyle=\langle{\bm{A}}_{(k,j)},\tilde{{\bm{A}}}_{(k,j)}\rangle,\quad\tilde{{\bm{A}}}_{(k,j)}={\bm{X}}_{k}^{(j)}{{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}}\bar{{\bm{H}}},\quad\bar{{\bm{H}}}={\bm{D}}{\bm{H}},

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{R}}}_{(k,0)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{J}{\bm{R}}_{(k,j)}

\displaystyle{\bm{Q}}_{(k,j)}

\displaystyle={\bm{X}}_{k}^{(j)}{\bm{X}}_{k}^{(j)}{{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}}\bm{y}^{*}_{1}

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{Q}}}_{(k,0)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{J}{\bm{Q}}_{(k,j)}.

Let

\tilde{{\bm{R}}}_{(k,j)}=\tilde{{\bm{R}}}_{(k,0)}-{\bm{R}}_{(k,j)},\quad\tilde{{\bm{Q}}}_{(k,j)}=\tilde{{\bm{Q}}}_{(k,0)}-{\bm{Q}}_{(k,j)}.

Then,

\operatorname{tr}{\bm{K}}_{k}^{(-j)}{\bm{P}}\bm{Y}^{*}=\frac{1}{M_{k}^{(-j)}}[\tilde{{\bm{R}}}_{(k,j)}+\tilde{{\bm{Q}}}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}_{(k,j)}({\bm{H}}{\bm{H}}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}\bm{y}^{*}_{1})].

(b)

\operatorname{tr}{\bm{K}}_{k}\bm{Y}^{*}=\frac{1}{M_{k}}[\bm{y}^{*}_{1}{{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}}\tilde{{\bm{Q}}}_{(k,0)}+\tilde{{\bm{F}}}_{(k,0)}]
where

\tilde{{\bm{F}}}_{(k,0)}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}{\bm{F}}_{(k,j)},\quad{\bm{F}}_{(k,j)}=\langle{\bm{X}}_{k}^{(j)},{\bm{D}}{\bm{X}}_{k}^{(j)}\rangle.

Let
\tilde{{\bm{F}}}_{k,j}=\tilde{{\bm{F}}}_{(k,0)}-{\bm{F}}_{(k,j)}
. Then,

\operatorname{tr}{\bm{K}}_{k}^{(-j)}\bm{Y}^{*}=\frac{1}{M_{k}}[\bm{y}^{*}_{1}{{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}}\tilde{{\bm{Q}}}_{(k,j)}+\tilde{{\bm{F}}}_{(k,j)}].

(c)

\operatorname{tr}{\bm{P}}{\bm{K}}_{k}{\bm{P}}\bm{Y}^{*}=\frac{1}{M_{k}}\operatorname{tr}{\bm{P}}{\bm{X}}_{k}{\bm{X}}_{k}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}{\bm{P}}{\bm{D}}+\frac{1}{M_{k}}\bm{y}^{*}_{1}{{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}}{\bm{P}}{\bm{X}}_{k}{\bm{X}}_{k}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}{\bm{P}}\bm{y}^{*}_{1}
:

i.

\operatorname{tr}{\bm{P}}{\bm{X}}_{k}{\bm{X}}_{k}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}{\bm{P}}{\bm{D}}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b}\tilde{{\bm{U}}}_{(k,0,b)}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}\tilde{\tilde{\bm{z}}}_{b},\quad\tilde{\tilde{\bm{z}}}_{b}={\bm{H}}\bar{{\bm{H}}}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}{\bm{z}}_{b}
, and

\hat{\operatorname{tr}}{\bm{P}}{\bm{X}}_{k}^{(-j)}{\bm{X}}_{k}^{(-j)}{{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}}{\bm{P}}{\bm{D}}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b}\tilde{{\bm{U}}}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}_{(k,j,b)}\tilde{\tilde{\bm{z}}}_{b}.

ii.

\bm{y}^{*}_{1}{{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}}{\bm{P}}{\bm{X}}_{k}{\bm{X}}_{k}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}{\bm{P}}\bm{y}^{*}_{1}=\tilde{{\bm{V}}}_{(k,0)}
where

{\bm{V}}_{(k,j)}=||{\bm{X}}_{k}^{(j)}{{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}}({\bm{H}}{\bm{H}}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}\bm{y}^{*}_{1})||_{2}^{2},\quad\tilde{{\bm{V}}}_{(k,0)}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}{\bm{V}}_{(k,j)}.

Let
\tilde{{\bm{V}}}_{(k,j)}=\tilde{{\bm{V}}}_{(k,0)}-{\bm{V}}_{(k,j)}
. Then,

\bm{y}^{*}_{1}{{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}}{\bm{P}}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}{\bm{X}}_{k}^{(-j)}{\bm{X}}_{k}^{(-j)}{{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}}{\bm{P}}\bm{y}^{*}_{1}=\tilde{{\bm{V}}}_{(k,j)}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}{\bm{P}}{\bm{K}}_{k}{\bm{P}}\bm{Y}^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M_{k}}\left[\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b}\tilde{{\bm{U}}}_{(0,b,k)}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}\tilde{\tilde{\bm{z}}}_{b}+\tilde{{\bm{V}}}_{(k,0)}\right],

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}{\bm{P}}{\bm{K}}_{k}^{(-j)}{\bm{P}}\bm{Y}^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M_{k}^{(-j)}}\left[\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b}\tilde{{\bm{U}}}_{(j,b,k)}{{}^{\mathrm{T}}}\tilde{\tilde{\bm{z}}}_{b}+\tilde{{\bm{V}}}_{(k,j)}\right].

Appendix S.5
Survival trait phenotyping definition

PheCode-based survival traits were used following the PheCode and ICD9/1CD10 mapping file provided in
dey_efficient_2022
. We also followed their phenotyping algorithm to create each survival trait:

•

An event is censored (
\delta=0
) if a subject does not have a PheCode.

•

An event is uncensored (
\delta=1
) if a subject has a PheCode.

•

Censoring time is defined as
t-\text{date of birth}
where
t
is the earliest date among the last non-imaging visit to any of hte UKB assessment centers, the last time any ICD code associated with the PheCode was recorded, the time of death (if death was recorded).

•

Uncensored time is defined as
s-\text{date of birth}
where
s
is the earliest recorded date among any of PheCode-specific ICD codes.

•

The date of birth was rounded to the nearest full month.

In addition to PheCode-based survival traits, we curate UKB Field ID based survival traits as shown in Table
S.8
. These traits were selected for their lower censoring rates compared to PheCode-based traits. The phenotyping algorithm for each trait looks like

•

An event is censored if a subject does not have any record among all visits (also known as instances in UKB terminology) corresponding to the Field ID.

•

An event is uncensored if a subject has a record of visit corresponding to the Field ID.

•

Censoring time is defined as
\text{the earliest assessment center visit}-\text{date of birth}

•

Uncensored time is defined as the earliest assessment center visit corresponding to the Field ID.

Appendix S.6
BayesW software configuration and error messsage summary

We compiled the software using the Intel compiler (version 2020.4), following the instructions provided in
this
GitHub repository. The compilation utilized the
boost
module (version 1.73.0) and the
eigen
module (version 3.3.9). Below, we provide the configuration script used to set the environmental variables for MPI:

#!/bin/bash
MPI_RANKS=$1
export OMP_NUM_THREADS=$(( NSLOTS / MPI_RANKS ))
export OMP_PLACES=cores
export OMP_PROC_BIND=close
export OMP_WAIT_POLICY=active
export I_MPI_FABRICS=shm:ofi
export UCX_TLS=tcp
export I_MPI_HYDRA_BOOTSTRAP=ssh
export I_MPI_DEBUG=5
export I_MPI_PIN=1
export I_MPI_PIN_DOMAIN=omp
export KMP_MALLOC_POOL_INCR=16M
export KMP_INIT_AT_FORK=FALSE

For all analyses, we opted to use BED files directly rather than the sparse files that can be generated by the BayesW software. For each group, we set the slab variances to 0.0001, 0.001, 0.01 and 0.1. The additional options used are listed below:

--shuf-mark 1
--sync-rate 10
--bed-sync

For the correctly specified model scenario with errors following a normal distribution, we set
MPI_RANKS = 4
with
NSLOTS = 8
. An error code 1003 was produced in all chains across all data replicates with censoring rates 0.5 and 0.8. The same error occurred in all chains in the 93rd data replicate with censoring rate of 0.2. Inspection of the source code indicated that the error code 1003 was generated within the
arms
function in the
BayesW_arms.cpp
file as shown in the following code snippet:

if((xinit[0] <= xl) || (xinit[ninit-1] >= xr)){
/* initial points do not satisfy bounds */
return 1003;
}

For scenarios in which errors followed a Gumbel distribution, we set
MPI_RANKS = 1
with
NSLOTS = 4
. In the setting with heritability of 0.2 and a censoring rate of 0.2, error code 1003 was produced in all chains for 14 data replicates, while error code 2000 occured in at least one chain for 19 data replicates. Altogether, 33 data replicates produced either error code 1003 or 2000 with no overlap between the two error types. For this scenario, we included only the 67 data replicates without any errors in any chain in our analyeses. For censoring rates of 0.5 and 0.8, error code 1003 was produced in all chains across all data replicates. We were unable to determine the nature or cause of error code 2000.

For misspecified model scenario, we set
MPI_RANKS = 1
with
NSLOTS = 2
. Error code 1003 was produced for all datasets across all censoring rates and genetic architectures.

For the analysis of survival traits from the UKB, we set
MPI_RANKS = 1
with
NSLOTS = 8
, allocating a total of 188GB of RAM distributed evenly across eight slots. The BayesW software produced errors for all four age-at-diagnosis traits. In particular, the following error message was generated when analyzing the “first live birth” trait:

hydra: /u/local/apps/boost/1_73_0/gcc-4.8.5/include/boost/random/gamma_distribution.hpp:118:

boost::random::gamma_distribution<RealType>::gamma_distribution(const RealType &, const RealType &) [with RealType = double]:

Assertion `_beta > result_type(0)’ failed.

All other traits produced the error message

#FATAL#: malloc failed on line 730 of data.cpp

which may be due to exceeding the allocated 188GB of RAM. We also attempted to run the software using the sparse file format with the same amount of memory, but errors were produced for all four traits.
