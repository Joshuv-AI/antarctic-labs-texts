Paternal age, de novo mutations, and offspring health? New directions for an ageing problem
Authors: Robert John Aitken
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis Directions article examines the mechanisms by which a father’s age impacts the health and wellbeing of his children. Such impacts are significant and include adverse birth outcomes, dominant genetic conditions, neuropsychiatric disorders, and a variety of congenital developmental defects. As well as age, a wide variety of environmental and lifestyle factors are also known to impact offspring health via changes mediated by the male germ line. This picture of a dynamic germ line responsive to a wide range of intrinsic and extrinsic factors contrasts with the results of trio studies indicating that the incidence of mutations in the male germ line is low and exhibits a linear, monotonic increase with paternal age (∼two new mutations per year). While the traditional explanation for this pattern of mutation has been the metronomic plod of replication errors, an alternative model pivots around the ‘faulty male’ hypothesis. According to this concept, the genetic integrity of the male germ line can be dynamically impacted by age and a variety of other factors, and it is the aberrant repair of such damage that drives mutagenesis. Fortunately, DNA proofreading during spermatogenesis is extremely effective and these mutant cells are either repaired or deleted by apoptosis/ferroptosis. There appear to be only two mechanisms by which mutant germ cells can escape this apoptotic fate: (i) if the germ cells acquire a mutation that by enhancing proliferation or suppressing apoptosis, permits their clonal expansion (selfish selection hypothesis) or (ii) if a genetically damaged spermatozoon manages to fertilize an oocyte, which then fixes the damage as a mutation (or epimutation) as a result of defective DNA repair (oocyte collusion hypothesis). Exploration of these proposed mechanisms should not only help us better understand the aetiology of paternal age effects but also inform potential avenues of remediation.Keywords: male ageing, oxidative stress, sperm DNA damage, genetic mutation, epigenetic mutation, antioxidant therapy

Introduction

Since the mid-1960s, all modern advanced economies have been characterized by an increase in longevity and a decrease in total fertility rate associated with a prosperity-led demographic transition (Aitken, 2022b,c, 2024a,b). As a result of these changes, advanced economies are now entering a new phase of social evolution (The Second Transition) characterized by high levels of personal autonomy, education, diverse forms of union, and small family sizes that reflect the progressive postponement of marriage and parenthood (Lesthaeghe, 2014; Aitken, 2024a,b). While the reproductive consequences of delayed childbearing are well-recognized in women (Gindoff and Jewelewicz, 1986), much less is known about such impacts in men (Aitken, 2023). Our current understanding is that advanced paternal age sees a progressive decline in fertility (Harris et al., 2011; Belloc et al., 2014) accompanied by a reduction in bioavailable testosterone levels and the appearance of multiple age-dependent pathologies including osteoporosis, depression, benign prostatic hypertrophy, prostate cancer, and erectile dysfunction (Drake and Khosla, 2012; Zitzmann, 2020; Aitken 2023; Ajayi et al., 2023). Paternal ageing is also associated with a profound increase in oxidative DNA damage in spermatozoa (Evenson et al., 2020; Vaughan et al., 2020; Aitken and Lewis, 2023; Behdarvandian et al., 2023), the appearance of multiple epigenetic changes in the germ line (Ashapkin et al., 2023) and an elevated incidence of de novo genetic mutations in the offspring (Kaltsas et al., 2023). These changes are, in turn, associated with a range of paternal-age-effect disorders in the progeny, including adverse birth outcomes, congenital birth defects such as heart malformations and cleft palate, dominant genetic conditions and a wide range of neuropsychiatric disorders (Urhoj et al., 2017; Aitken et al., 2020; Khachadourian et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2022). The overall impact of paternal ageing on reproductive health has previously been discussed in Human Reproduction (Aitken 2023) and will not be reiterated here. The purpose of this article is to build on these foundations and to speculate on the directions future research in this area might take.

Undoubtedly, the future will see important advances in our ability to counteract many pathologies that characterize the ageing male, driven, in part, by the increased longevity associated with modern society and the desire of pharmaceutical companies to medicalize the ageing process. However, for the purpose of this ‘Directions’ article I am going to focus on the fundamental manner in which paternal age at the time of conception influences the health and wellbeing of future generations. What sorts of genetic or epigenetic change appear in the offspring as a consequence of advanced paternal age? How are such changes induced? and How can such changes be diagnosed and prevented in clinical practice? In modern society, where the boundaries of age-dependent infertility are constantly being challenged, resolution of these issues is becoming ever more critical.

Paternal age effects

Central to our understanding of how male ageing affects offspring health are a group of genetic conditions, known collectively as the ‘paternal-age-effect’ (PAE) disorders. These diseases are characterized by an extremely strong correlation between paternal age and disease prevalence and include spontaneous conditions such as achondroplasia and Apert syndrome (Wood and Goriely, 2022). Conventionally, such mutations are thought to arise as a consequence of intrinsic replication error, the incidence of which increases as a linear function of paternal age in lockstep with the number of germ cell divisions, with limited interference from extrinsic (lifestyle, environmental) factors. In keeping with this hypothesis, the number of germ line mutations in the male germ line has been found to be low but to increase in a metronomic fashion throughout life, ultimately accounting for 70–80% of all de novo mutations in our species (Kong et al., 2012l; Girard et al., 2016). This replication error hypothesis is supported by trio studies (in which the incidence of de novo mutations is inferred by comparing a given subject’s genome with that of the parents), which indicate that only around two new mutations are added for each additional year of paternal life (Gao et al., 2019; Goldmann et al., 2019; Wood and Goriely, 2022). Occasionally, hypermutagenicity is known to occur in response to powerful drivers such as such as chemotherapy (Kaplanis et al., 2022) and severe male infertility (Kunisaki et al., 2024) but overall mutations rates in the male germ line are low, and carefully controlled.

Intriguingly, the slow monotonic increase in de novo mutations with paternal age seems at odds with the profound range of pathological conditions exhibited by the offspring of ageing fathers (Janeczko et al., 2020). Furthermore, low levels of genetic variation in the male germ line do not resonate with the known dynamic impact of environmental (e.g. heat, radiofrequency electromagnetic radiation, pollutants) and lifestyle (diet, smoking, alcohol) factors on the quality of spermatogenesis and fertility (Houston et al., 2018, 2019; Naidu et al., 2021; Dehghanbanadaki et al., 2024). The sum of these observations suggests that the traditional view of a gradual increase in replication error-generated mutations with paternal age conflicts with the concept of a dynamic germ line that is sensitive to a range of extrinsic factors and can affect offspring health in multiple ways. Some additional elements must be involved.

The importance of cell death

To fulfil their dual role as guardians of the human genome and engines of male-driven evolution, male germ cells must permit some genetic variation—but not too much. The male germ line’s response to this challenge has been to rely extensively on programmed cell death to delete genetically damaged cells, rather than depend too heavily on error-prone DNA repair pathways (Bloom et al., 2019). Thus, the germinal epithelium of the testes is equipped to exhibit apoptosis and ferroptosis in response to germ cell injury, rapidly deleting damaged cells from the germ cell pool (Bromfield et al., 2019; Sharma et al., 2023). These quality control processes are so efficient that they remove 75–80% of all damaged germ cells from the testes and, thereby, help maintain the low mutation rate characteristic of the male germ line (Rodriguez et al., 1997; Sakkas and El-Fakahany, 2018).

All stages of germ cell development are vulnerable to programmed cell death, from primordial germ cells to fully differentiated spermatozoa. The process is particularly fast and efficient, orchestrating a dramatic cull of germ cells during fetal life and the first spermatogenic wave around puberty, followed by the continuous removal of defective spermatogonia/spermatocytes in adult life (Rodriguez et al., 1997; Nguyen et al., 2020). However, as germ cells differentiate into spermatozoa and become progressively more transcriptionally and translationally silent, their capacity to undergo apoptosis is progressively diminished. Nevertheless, even a mature spermatozoon can undergo a truncated form of intrinsic apoptosis in response to the oxidative stress associated with sperm senescence (Koppers et al., 2011). Thus, the results of trio studies do not necessarily indicate an inherently low level of genetic turmoil in the male germ line. Rather, I would argue, they represent the culmination of an extremely efficient selection process that has eliminated any germ cell exhibiting evidence of genetic damage and then singled out the one-in-a-hundred-million ejaculated spermatozoa to create an embryo.

I propose that there are only two ways in which this selection process can be overridden to generate genetic disease in the offspring—and they operate at opposite ends of the spermatogenic process: (i) during the early stages of spermatogenesis, a germ cell may avoid programmed cell death if the mutation it acquires provides its lineage with a selective advantage over other germ cells by either increasing the rate of germ cell proliferation or, potentially, decreasing the efficiency of apoptosis (the selfish selection hypothesis) or (ii) at the other end of the spermatogenic process, spermatozoa exhibiting high levels of genetic or epigenetic damage are destined for an apoptotic death unless they achieve fertilization and can take advantage of the repair capacity of the oocyte. In this case, mutations may arise as a result of defective DNA damage repair as the embryonic genome prepares for S-phase of the first mitotic division or, in the case of mosaicism, during the early stages of embryonic cleavage (the oocyte/embryo collusion hypothesis) (Acuna-Hidalgo et al., 2015; Rahbari et al., 2016; Lindsay et al., 2019; Aitken 2022a).

The selfish selection model

According to the elegant ‘selfish selection’ model proposed by Goriely and Wilkie (2012) spontaneous activating mutations in key signalling pathways, particularly the Receptor Tyrosine Kinase (RTK)-RAS-MAPK pathway, confer a selective advantage on affected germ cells permitting their clonal expansion and generating nests of mutated germ cells in the testes of ageing males (Wood and Goriely, 2022). The ‘selfish selection’ of germ cells possessing mutations in such paternal-age-effect genes is responsible for a variety of rare dominant genetic conditions (e.g. Apert, Crouzon and Pfeiffer syndromes, achondroplasia, thanatophoric dysplasia and hypochondroplasia) that are known to be highly dependent on the age of the father at the moment of conception (Crow, 2000; Wood and Goriely, 2022).

Overall, there can be no doubt that the selfish selection hypothesis can go a large way towards explaining the link between these dominant genetic disorders and paternal age. However, these clinical conditions are extremely rare: one in every 200 000 births in the case of Apert syndrome and approximately one in 30 000 births for achondroplasia. Where does this leave all of the other developmental consequences of advanced paternal age from neuropsychiatric disorders such as spontaneous schizophrenia or autism to congenital malformations, infertility, and miscarriage? Are they also due to mutations in the RTK-RAS-MAPK signalling pathway?

In theory, any mutation that allowed male germ cells to achieve a rapid clonal expansion could lead to an over-representation in differentiated spermatozoa and enhance the probability of becoming an inheritable defect. Such a suggestion has been made (Wood and Goriely, 2022) for mild mutations in RTK-RAS-MAPK pathway which could potentially be responsible for a broad range of neuropsychiatric conditions in the offspring of ageing males (Fig. 1). Weak mutations in this pathway might be expected to favour the clonal expansion of mutated cells but to generate a less severe phenotype in the progeny that does not impact their reproductive fitness and, as a result, would be more likely to exhibit intergenerational transfer.

A proposed framework for understanding the impact of paternal age on offspring health. Within the ageing germ cell population (i) the progressive buildup of intrinsic replication errors and (ii) accumulated DNA damage inflicted by a lifetime’s exposure to extrinsic environmental and lifestyle stressors, combined with (iii) age-dependent declines in the efficacy of DNA repair and antioxidant availability lead to an increase in genetic damage. Such damage is usually addressed by effective DNA repair (particularly the BER pathway) and, if the damage is too great, the extremely rapid and efficient induction of apoptosis. Programmed cell death operates at all stages of germ cell development including spermatogonia, spermatocytes, spermatids and even spermatozoa and effectively removes around 75–80% of these damaged cells from the germinal epithelium. The combination of DNA repair and apoptosis ensures that the mutational load carried by the male germ line is low and only increases slowly with paternal age. There are only two ways in which germ cells can avoid this apoptotic fate. (A) The occurrence of germ line mutations in signal transduction pathways that result in either an increase in cell proliferation and/or a decrease in apoptosis (selfish selection). These mutations lead to a clonal expansion of the mutated cells that become enriched in the seminiferous tubules of ageing males. For example, lesions in the RTK-RAS-MAPK pathway are known to be involved in a wide range of genetic diseases (achondroplasia, Apert syndrome etc.) but other pathways are theoretically possible including the PTEN-PI3K-AKT signal transduction cascade. (B) Spermatozoa possessing high levels of DNA damage may escape apoptosis and retain sufficient functionality to be rescued by fertilization. At this point, the DNA damage is either repaired by the oocyte or, if this process is compromised, becomes fixed as a genetic or epigenetic mutation in the embryo (oocyte collusion). These pathways finally converge to generate a wide range of paternally-mediated pathologies in the offspring including congenital birth defects, neuropsychiatric disorders, cancers and miscarriage. BER, base excision repair; SSC, spermatogonial stem cell.

The possibility that such mild mutations might be involved in mediating the impact of paternal age on neuropsychiatric disorders is supported by the finding that mutations within the RAS-MAPK pathway that culminate in the activation of RAS (the so-called RASopathies) are associated with a range of developmental disorders that feature cognitive impairments, symptoms of autism spectrum disorders (ASDs) and intellectual disability (Adviento et al., 2014; Borrie et al., 2017). Many of these RASopathies are known to involve de novo mutations in the germ line (Zampino et al., 2007; Rauen, 2013) and for several of these conditions (e.g. neurofibromatosis and Costello syndrome), a paternal age effect has been suggested (Dubov et al., 2016). These data are consistent with selfish selection operating for a broad range of RASopathies and it will be fascinating to see if this possibility is affirmed in future studies.

Of course, genetic mutations that result in constitutive activation of the RTK-RAS-MAPK pathway do not just enhance the survival and clonal expansion of affected germ cells. Such pathways are also central to the initiation and propagation of cancers, thereby explaining why patients possessing germ line mutations in this pathway, also exhibit an increased susceptibility to cancer in both their testes and the tissues of their progeny (Goriely et al., 2009; Liu et al., 2023). Such associations emphasize the link between mutations that drive the selfish selection of germ cells and the process of oncogenesis. Indeed, cancer biology has taught us that there are many proto-oncogenes that when mutated stimulate the proliferation and survival of affected cells (Nussinov et al. 2022). We might therefore speculate that germ line mutations in proto-oncogenic receptors and downstream signalling cascades might be involved in, not just the susceptibility of the offspring to cancer, but also the selfish-selection of mutant germ cells that favoured their vertical transmission to future generations. If this is the case, the mutations of interest would not only be in the RTK-RAS-MAPK pathway but, theoretically, in any growth factor, receptor, signal transduction kinase, or nuclear transcription factor encoded by proto-oncogenes. As an example, let us briefly consider the PTEN (Phosphatase and TENsin homolog)—PI3K (phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase)—AKT (Ak strain transforming) signal transduction pathway.

Potential involvement of PI3K signal transduction in paternally mediated mutagenesis

This pathway is centrally involved in the regulation of cell proliferation and survival and is negatively regulated by PTEN, a phosphatidylinositol-3,4,5-trisphosphate 3-phosphatase that regulates the availability of PI3. Mutations that result in constitutive activation of this pathway in the germ line, including inactivating mutations in PTEN, result in a variety of inheritable pathologies in the offspring associated with disordered brain development, autism and cancer (Parsons et al., 2005; Carnero et al., 2008; Orrico et al., 2009; Rivière et al., 2012; Lucas et al., 2014; Mester and Charis, 2015; Yehia et al., 2020; Liang et al., 2022). Given the importance of the PTEN/PI3K/AKT pathway in the aetiology of familial disease states, is it possible that the prevalence of such mutations reflects ‘selfish selection’ of mutant spermatogonia? If so, how would such a selection operate?

As indicated above, one of the reasons why male germ cells exhibit lower mutational rates than somatic cells (Moore et al., 2021) is that the apoptotic process is extremely efficient in deleting mutant cells. Hence, any mutation that suppressed the high rate of apoptosis characteristic of the male germ line, would naturally lead to a clonal expansion of mutated cells and potentially the appearance of pathology in the offspring. Since the PTEN/PI3K/AKT pathway is responsible for suppressing intrinsic apoptosis in the germ line (Feng et al., 2000; Koppers et al., 2011; Chen et al., 2022) any mutation that promoted this signal transduction cascade would result in the inhibition of apoptosis and enhanced propagation of the mutant germ cells. The observation that paternal age is involved in the origin of pathogenic mutations in the PTEN/PI3K/AKT pathway (Choi et al., 2012) certainly supports this model. Inheritable developmental disorders and cancers may therefore owe their origin to mutations in this signal transduction cascade that repress apoptosis in the male germ line, thereby facilitating their over-representation within the germinal epithelium (Fig. 1). Future research into the importance of pathways outside of the traditional RTK-RAS-MAPK signalling cascade in the selfish selection of mutant germ cells will be critical in determining the general applicability of the selfish selection model in the aetiology of paternal-age-effect pathologies.

The selfish selection model potentially explains how rare spontaneous mutations can become enriched within the germ line with increasing age to the point that they can impact the health and wellbeing of the offspring. However, this model does not explain how the mutations arose in the first place. Is it just intrinsic replication error or are extrinsic factors also involved?

Oxidative DNA damage and the oocyte collusion model

Whether the mutations that drive the selfish selection model are simply a function of replication number and the intrinsic error rate of DNA polymerase in the germ line, or are the product of extrinsic lifestyle and environmental factors, is an area of intense debate. The traditional replication error hypothesis certainly resonates with the metronomic march of de novo mutations with age but does not accord with either the pattern of germ line replication over the life course (Gao et al., 2019) or the exponential pattern of disease in relation to paternal age (Crow, 2000). In order to account for discrepancies in this model the replication error concept has been supplemented (not replaced) with the ‘faulty male’ hypothesis which essentially posits that ageing males are less able to repair and/or protect their DNA from damage than females and that de novo mutations appearing as a consequence of paternal age are the result of aberrant DNA repair or, in principle, abortive apoptosis (Gao et al., 2019; Hahn et al., 2023).

The factors responsible for DNA damage in the male germ line are not fully resolved but it is already clear that oxidative stress is a key player in this context (Nguyen-Powanda and Robaire, 2020). Haploid germ cells, especially elongating spermatids and spermatozoa, are particularly vulnerable to oxidative attack because their highly compartmentalized, stripped-down architecture restricts the availability of intracellular antioxidants as well as their capacity for DNA repair. Moreover, this increased vulnerability seems to increase with advancing age, such that the age-related increase in sperm DNA fragmentation (Evenson et al., 2020) is correlated with the formation of highly mutagenic oxidative base adducts such as 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8OHdG) (Zubkova and Robaire, 2006; Weir and Robaire, 2007; Smith et al., 2013a,b; Nguyen-Powanda and Robaire, 2020; Vaughan et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2021; Aitken and Lewis, 2023). These changes reflect the progressive downregulation of antioxidant capacity as well as the impairment of base excision repair (the major enzyme system responsible for correcting DNA damage due to oxidation, deamination and alkylation) in the male germ line with age (Intano et al., 2002; Paul et al., 2011; Krokan and Bjørås, 2013; Zhao et al., 2021). Some of the de novo mutation signatures occurring in the male germ line are certainly consistent with an aetiology involving oxidative stress. Particularly important in this context may be the high incidence of cytosine deamination and the associated increase in CpG>TpG mutations seen in germ cells with advancing paternal age. This mutational signature could be driven by an oxidative mechanism involving the formation of 5-methylcytosine glycol following attack by reactive oxygen species (Cao et al., 2009; Gao et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2024).

In addition to the high incidence of oxidative DNA damage in the spermatozoa of ageing males, the capacity of oocytes to repair such damage decreases significantly with age (Horta et al., 2020). The ageing couple therefore represent a perfect storm characterized by increases in oxidative DNA damage in the spermatozoa and parallel decreases in the DNA repair capacity of the oocyte (Fig. 1). Fertilization of the ageing oocyte with DNA-damaged spermatozoa may therefore result in de novo mutations following fertilization as a result of deficiencies in the oocyte’s inability to accurately complete DNA repair in the lead up to the first mitotic division or, in the case of mosaicism, the early stages of cleavage (Aitken 2022a).

A more dynamic model of paternal impacts on offspring health

The concept that sperm DNA damage is of central importance in the genesis of de novo genetic mutations presents us with a much more dynamic view of mutagenesis in the germ line than the traditional replication error hypothesis. According to this model, it is not so much the gradual increase in replication errors that determines the mutational load subsequently carried by the embryo, but a more interactive process in the which the opposing forces of DNA damage and apoptosis/DNA repair are pitched against one another.

This ‘faulty male’ hypothesis, with defective DNA repair at its core, accords well with our understanding of the vulnerability of the male germ line to damage by lifestyle and environmental factors that negatively impact offspring health. In addition to age, paternal exposure to such factors as obesity, alcohol consumption, smoking, environmental toxicants, and chemotherapy are all known to impact the progeny, generating a range of adverse outcomes including cancer and poor mental health (Ji et al., 1997; Easey and Sharp, 2021; Carter et al., 2023; Dehghanbanadaki et al. 2024) (Fig. 1). The lack of correspondence between this more dynamic view of mutagenesis in the germ line and the genetically conservative results of trio studies may simply reflect the high level of gamete selection operating at all stages of the reproductive process from spermatogenesis to fertilization (Ganeva et al., 2021; Yaghoobi et al., 2022). If oxidative stress is instrumental in the causation of age-dependent increases in sperm DNA damage, then collateral peroxidative damage to the sperm plasma membrane, would provide an explanation as to why DNA-damaged spermatozoa generally cannot achieve fertilization (Aitken et al., 1989). However, such selection mechanisms are not infallible because experimental studies have demonstrated that levels of oxidative stress capable of damaging sperm DNA may not be sufficient to prevent fertilization (Aitken et al., 1998). Moreover, this must be the case in vivo; otherwise, how would factors such as paternal smoking or testicular hyperthermia generate inheritable disease in the progeny (Gavriliouk and Aitken, 2015; Wan et al., 2020)?

Are de novo mutations the whole story?

The disparity between low de novo mutation rates in the male germ line and the widespread impacts of paternal age on offspring health may also reflect the importance of factors other than point mutations in the mediation of paternal age effects. For example, we should acknowledge that other types of genetic change associated with paternal age, including structural mutations and copy number variations, may profoundly impact embryonic viability and development (Belyeu et al., 2021). In addition, we may be underestimating the ability of age-dependent epigenetic defects in the spermatozoa (including changes to the DNA methylation and histone profile as well as oxidative damage to the centriole and non-coding RNA species) to influence embryonic development (Guillaumet-Adkins et al., 2017; Lu et al., 2017; Ashapkin et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2023). The fact that oxidative attacks on methylated CpG islands can lead to both epigenetic (Valinluck et al., 2004) and genetic (Lee et al., 2002) modifications within the germ line suggests that both types of change could be involved in mediating the impact of paternal age (Fig. 1). We should also not forget that the oxidative stress associated with ageing results in long telomeres in the male germ line, that are then inherited by the offspring (Moazamian et al., 2022). Although long telomeres are traditionally regarded as a good thing, this may not necessarily be the case, given that long telomeres have been associated with an increased risk of certain cancers including chronic lymphocytic leukaemia and sarcoma (Savage, 2024).

Clearly, future studies in this area should focus on the mechanisms by which male ageing, in combination with lifestyle and environmental stressors, might influence the epigenetic as well as the genetic status of the male germ line and thereby impact the viability and developmental normality of the offspring (Holtgrewe et al., 2018; Ton et al., 2018; Du Fossé et al., 2020; Dehghanbanadaki et al., 2024). In this context, it would be very helpful if trio studies could be designed that related the levels of genetic and epigenetic damage in spermatozoa to the mutational load subsequently carried by progeny. It is only through such studies that we shall be able to fully understand the clinical significance of sperm DNA damage and determine whether the clinical assessment of this parameter is a diagnostic necessity or an unnecessary luxury (Aitken and Lewis, 2023).

In Fig. 1, a possible model is advanced suggesting that the complex array of pathologies observed in the offspring of ageing fathers has an intricate aetiology involving a range of non-exclusive, inter-related, intrinsic and extrinsic factors that culminate in mutations as a result of replication error, defective DNA repair and abortive apoptosis. These mechanisms, coupled with selfish selection and the ability of damaged spermatozoa to achieve conception, subvert the biological imperative to keep mutagenesis in the ageing germ line under strict control.

Future treatment of the ageing male

Now that we have gained some limited understanding of the mechanisms underpinning the impacts of male ageing on reproductive health, what are the therapeutic implications of this knowledge? Given the central significance of oxidative stress in the age-dependent demise of both Leydig and germ cells (Aitken, 2023), the whole question of whether the consequences of ageing can be ameliorated by antioxidant administration needs to be addressed, once and for all. Such an approach seems logical; however exploration of this therapeutic concept requires urgent research on the nature, doses, and timing of antioxidant therapy, as well as the identification of subjects for whom such treatment might be appropriate, or contraindicated. The essential component of any redox situation is that the pro- and anti-oxidant sides of the oxidoreduction equation should be balanced. If patients are antioxidant deficient then there is every reason to believe that antioxidant therapy might be helpful. However, if the patient is already in a state of redox balance, then administering high doses of powerful antioxidants might create a state of reductive stress that will be just as harmful to the patient’s reproductive health as its oxidative counterpart (Sengupta et al., 2022). Developing a simple point-of-care assay for detecting redox imbalance in the ageing male population is therefore critical. Once ageing patients have been identified that are in a state of oxidative stress then they should be treated with antioxidant mixtures that are chemically justifiable in terms of the potency, half-life, and concentration of the constituents (Gharagozloo et al., 2016). In the future, we might even refine this approach by using nanoparticles functionalized with peptides that bind the surface of target cells and deliver a site-specific correction of oxidative stress (Fraser et al., 2024).

Conclusions

One of the most serious consequences of paternal ageing is the impact this process has on the health and wellbeing of the offspring and, potentially, future generations. Research in this area will aim to clarify the mechanisms by which paternal ageing impacts offspring health and how such impacts might be avoided or countered. The inspirational geneticist James Crow once remarked that the ‘greatest mutational health hazard to the human genome is fertile older males’ (Crow, 1997). Given the modern tendency to reproduce at later ages, we should heed his concerns. If the impact of paternal ageing on offspring health is to be countered, it will require a much deeper understanding of the (epi)genetic changes involved and the causative mechanisms that underpin them. Naturally, we shall never eradicate the ageing process, however an understanding of the mechanisms by which paternal age influences the male germ line will be extremely helpful in fashioning strategies to reduce its impact on future generations.
