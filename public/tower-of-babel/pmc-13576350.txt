Secreted protein circuits in the gastrointestinal tumour microenvironment: determinants of immunotherapy response and resistance
Authors: Kexun Li, Zilong Qian, Jie Mao, Yongtao Han, Xuefeng Leng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmune checkpoint blockade has transformed treatment in selected gastrointestinal (GI) cancers, yet primary resistance, incomplete responses and acquired resistance remain common. This heterogeneity is not explained by tumour-cell genomics alone; extracellular signalling programmes within the tumour microenvironment can determine immune recruitment, access and adaptation to therapy. The tumour secretome—including cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, complement components, matricellular proteins, soluble checkpoint molecules and extracellular-vesicle-associated cargo—regulates immune-cell recruitment, exclusion, suppression, tertiary lymphoid structure formation and exhaustion across anatomical and molecular contexts. Across gastric and esophageal cancers, colorectal cancer, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, hepatocellular carcinoma and biliary tract cancers, recurrent suppressive circuits include TGF-β, VEGF, CXCL12-CXCR4, CXCL8/IL-8-CXCR1/2, CCL2-CCR2, CSF1-CSF1R, IL-6-family cytokines, SPP1/osteopontin, periostin, galectins, DKK1, MIF, complement and soluble or vesicular PD-L1. Conversely, CXCL9/10/11-CXCR3 signalling and CXCL13-associated tertiary lymphoid structures characterise immune-permissive states that can support checkpoint responsiveness. We organise these circuits into four overlapping functional modules—myeloid-enriched, fibroblast-driven exclusion, angiogenic-immunosuppressive and immune-permissive—and apply a four-level evidence hierarchy that separates clinical validation from mechanistic inference. Clinically useful secretome biomarkers will therefore need to integrate cellular source, spatial localisation, receptor context, temporal dynamics and linkage to actionable immune-state transitions.Keywords: Gastrointestinal cancer, Tumour microenvironment, Secretome, Immune checkpoint blockade

Introduction

The therapeutic narrative of GI immunotherapy is often framed around tumour-intrinsic markers such as mismatch-repair deficiency, microsatellite instability, tumour mutational burden, viral antigens, PD-L1 expression, WNT-β-catenin activation, and oncogenic drivers. These variables remain indispensable, but they do not fully explain clinical behaviour. T cells must be recruited, enter tissue, receive antigen-presenting and costimulatory signals, avoid suppressive myeloid and fibroblast programmes, and remain metabolically competent in a hostile niche. Secreted proteins provide much of this instruction set and therefore help explain why histologically similar tumours can have divergent responses to the same checkpoint regimen [1–5].

GI cancers are especially dependent on extracellular circuits because the organs from which they arise are physiologically built to integrate luminal antigens, microbial products, epithelial repair, stromal remodelling and immune tolerance. The stomach and esophagus are exposed to acid injury, infection, reflux and metaplasia; the intestine balances immunity against commensal organisms; the pancreas is embedded in a fibrotic wound-healing programme; and the liver receives portal venous blood enriched with microbial and dietary antigens. Tumours co-opt these tissue programmes, producing site-specific secretomes rather than a generic inflammatory milieu [6–9].

The term ‘secreted proteins’ is used broadly here to encompass soluble cytokines and chemokines, growth factors, shed receptors and ligands, complement fragments, extracellular-matrix and matricellular proteins, secreted enzymes, and proteins carried in extracellular vesicles. Their biological effects depend on distinct modes of distribution and activation: IL-8 and CXCL12 form chemotactic gradients; TGF-β is stored in latent matrix-bound complexes and activated locally; VEGF couples angiogenesis to immune regulation; SPP1 and periostin link matrix remodelling to immune-cell state; and soluble or exosomal PD-L1 can extend checkpoint signalling beyond direct cell-cell contact [10, 11].

Main text

Scope and evidence-interpretation framework

This article is a narrative review focused on secreted-protein circuits that influence immune checkpoint blockade and checkpoint-containing combinations in GI malignancies. We use the term “secretome” broadly to include soluble cytokines and chemokines, growth factors, shed receptors and ligands, complement fragments, matricellular proteins, secreted enzymes and extracellular-vesicle-associated proteins. We emphasise mediators with mechanistic links to immune recruitment, exclusion, suppression, tertiary lymphoid structure (TLS) formation, exhaustion, vascular access or adaptive resistance.

This narrative mechanistic review was informed by structured searches of PubMed and Web of Science Core Collection from 1 January 2010 to 30 June 2026. Search terms combined gastrointestinal cancer types (including gastric/gastro-esophageal cancer, esophageal cancer, colorectal cancer, pancreatic cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, cholangiocarcinoma and gallbladder cancer) with secreted mediators, immunotherapy and spatial-omics concepts. Reference lists and forward citations of key publications were also screened. Priority was given, where available, to human gastrointestinal cancer studies that resolved cellular source, receptor-bearing populations, spatial localisation, treatment-associated dynamics or functional validation; mechanistic and pan-cancer studies were used when GI-specific evidence was limited. Purely computational associations without independent biological support and studies without relevant immune or therapeutic endpoints were not prioritised. Because this is a narrative mechanistic synthesis rather than a systematic review or meta-analysis, the evidence levels represent the strongest direct support identified for each tumour context rather than a formal risk-of-bias assessment.

To reduce overinterpretation, each mediator is interpreted through five questions: which cell type produces the protein; where the protein is localised; which receptor-expressing cells are positioned to respond; whether the circuit is dynamically altered by therapy; and whether perturbation of the circuit changes the relevant immune state. We distinguish four practical levels of evidence. Level 1 indicates clinical validation of a therapeutic target/axis or regimen in a GI cancer context, usually through a randomised or practice-changing therapeutic signal; it does not, by itself, prove the proposed secretome mechanism. Level 2 indicates human clinical correlative or early interventional evidence. Level 3 indicates strong preclinical, single-cell/spatial or mechanistic evidence. Level 4 indicates emerging or hypothesis-generating evidence (Table 1).

This framework intentionally separates observation from interpretation. For example, high serum CXCL8/IL-8 is an observation; neutrophil-rich resistance is an interpretation that requires tissue or pharmacodynamic support. Similarly, high TGF-β abundance is not equivalent to active stromal exclusion unless ligand activation, SMAD output, fibroblast state and CD8 + T-cell topology are documented. The translational objective is not to catalogue every secreted molecule, but to identify extracellular programmes that can be measured, perturbed and monitored (Table 1).

Where secretome biology becomes clinically visible

Current GI immunotherapy successes expose the need for a microenvironmental layer of interpretation. Nivolumab plus chemotherapy established a first-line standard in advanced gastric, gastro-esophageal junction and esophageal adenocarcinoma, with longer follow-up confirming durable benefit in selected patients [12, 13]. Pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy improved outcomes in HER2-negative advanced gastric or gastro-esophageal junction cancer [14]. In esophageal squamous-cell carcinoma, PD-1-based combinations improve survival, although benefit remains heterogeneous [15, 16].

In colorectal cancer, pembrolizumab and nivolumab-ipilimumab have produced major benefit in MSI-H/dMMR disease, yet not all antigen-rich tumours achieve durable control [17–23]. In hepatocellular carcinoma, atezolizumab-bevacizumab established the clinical value of combining PD-L1 and VEGF blockade; however, IMbrave150 does not by itself establish VEGF-mediated immune suppression as the causal mechanism of benefit [24, 25]. Durvalumab-tremelimumab provides a distinct dual-checkpoint strategy [26]. In biliary tract cancer, adding durvalumab or pembrolizumab to gemcitabine-cisplatin improves outcomes, but predictive biomarkers remain inadequate [27, 28].

The secretome as an extracellular immune operating system

Secreted proteins regulate tumour immunity through recurring mechanisms. First, they control recruitment: CXCL9, CXCL10 and CXCL11 attract CXCR3-expressing effector T cells, whereas CCL2, CXCL1, CXCL2, CXCL5 and CXCL8/IL-8 favour monocytes, neutrophils and granulocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cells according to receptor context. Second, they regulate differentiation and survival: CSF1 supports macrophages, IL-6-family cytokines reinforce STAT3-dependent inflammatory states, and TGF-β can drive fibroblast activation, Treg differentiation and cytotoxic T-cell suppression. Third, they modify physical access through vessels and matrix. Fourth, they alter antigen presentation by dendritic cells and macrophages. Fifth, they transmit information systemically through blood, lymph and extracellular vesicles. Sixth, they contribute to adaptive resistance after therapy by re-establishing suppressive inflammation or stromal barriers [29–33] (Table 2).

Major classes of secreted proteins that regulate GI tumour immunity

The biological effect of a secreted protein is rarely dictated by mean abundance alone. Source, spatial distribution, receptor localisation and timing determine meaning. A CXCL12 gradient generated by fibroblasts at the invasive margin can retain or mislocalize effector T cells, whereas CXCL12 in vascular or lymphoid niches may have different implications. Circulating IL-8 can mark neutrophil-rich tumour inflammation, but it can also arise from infection, biliary obstruction, liver dysfunction, cachexia or mucosal injury. Active TGF-β at the tumour margin is not biologically equivalent to latent ligand stored in fibrotic or cirrhotic tissue.

Secreted proteins can be organised into overlapping operational modules for translational interpretation. A myeloid-enriched module may include CCL2, CXCR2 ligands, CSF1, IL-6, complement fragments and SPP1. A fibroblast-driven exclusion module may comprise TGF-β, CXCL12, periostin, collagen, tenascin and matrix metalloproteinases. An angiogenic-immunosuppressive module links VEGF-A and ANGPT2 with hypoxia, endothelial dysfunction and myeloid recruitment. By contrast, an immune-permissive module is characterised by IFN-γ, CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11, CXCL13, lymphotoxin-related signals, mature dendritic cells and TLS. A modular interpretation reduces the risk of overreading single analytes and helps explain why empiric combinations may fail when patients are not selected for the dominant resistance state (Fig. 1).

The gastrointestinal tumour secretome as an immune operating system

These modules are intended to describe overlapping functional states rather than discrete molecular taxonomies. A lesion may occupy more than one state simultaneously, and therapy can shift the balance among angiogenic, stromal, myeloid and immune-organised programmes. Accordingly, the modules should be treated as translational hypotheses to be tested through ligand-receptor activity, spatial proximity, protein localisation and treatment-linked dynamics—not as settled clinical classifiers of malignancy.

Immune-permissive circuits

CXCL9/10/11-CXCR3: recruitment requires spatial connection to function

The CXCL9-CXCL10-CXCL11-CXCR3 axis is one of the most clearly defined immune-permissive chemokine systems. Interferon-induced CXCR3 ligands recruit activated T cells and natural killer cells. In responsive tumours, antigen recognition can induce IFN-γ, which stimulates tumour, endothelial and myeloid cells to produce CXCR3 ligands, establishing a positive-feedback loop that amplifies effector-cell recruitment. This biology helps explain the sensitivity of many MSI-H/dMMR colorectal cancers and provides a rationale for radiotherapy, vaccines, innate immune agonists and vascular normalisation as strategies to improve effector-chemokine access [29, 30].

CXCR3 ligands should not be treated as unconditional markers of therapeutic benefit. Chronic interferon signalling can coexist with T-cell exhaustion, antigen-presentation defects and adaptive checkpoint upregulation. A lesion can express CXCL10 yet still exclude T cells if TGF-β, VEGF, CXCL12 or dense extracellular matrix imposes a stronger physical or functional barrier. The key biomarker question is therefore not simply whether CXCR3 ligands are present, but whether the chemokine field is spatially connected to antigen-presenting cells and tumour-cell killing (Fig. 2).

Chemokine topologies that determine immune access in GI tumours

CXCL13 and tertiary lymphoid structures

CXCL13 contributes to B-cell recruitment, follicular organisation and TLS formation. Across cancer types, mature TLS have been associated with improved clinical outcome and immunotherapy response, although maturity, organisation and location are decisive [31–33]. In GI tissues, this axis is particularly relevant because mucosa-associated lymphoid architecture and antigen sampling are physiological features of the organ system.

CXCL13 may be produced by follicular-helper-like T cells, exhausted tumour-reactive T cells, dendritic-cell states or stromal cells; it is therefore most informative when interpreted together with B-cell follicles, follicular dendritic-cell networks, high endothelial venules, T-cell clonality and proximity to tumour nests. A mature TLS-rich tumour may be well suited to checkpoint blockade, antigen-release approaches or vaccines. Conversely, a TLS-poor tumour may require vascular, stromal or innate-immune priming before checkpoint blockade can be productive. Broad anti-inflammatory therapy could also disrupt productive CXCL9/10/11 and CXCL13 programmes; immune-permissive circuits should therefore be interpreted together with suppressive modules rather than in isolation (Fig. 3).

Suppressive secreted-protein modules create distinct modes of immunotherapy resistance

TGF-β: a spatial barrier rather than a generic soluble biomarker

TGF-β is secreted as a latent complex, stored in extracellular matrix, activated by integrins and proteases, and sensed by epithelial, endothelial, fibroblast, myeloid and lymphoid cells. In established cancers, tumour cells often disable the tumour-suppressive arms of TGF-β signalling while retaining its stromal and immunosuppressive effects. The most relevant immunotherapy phenotype is spatial exclusion: cytotoxic T cells accumulate at the tumour margin but fail to penetrate malignant nests. In colorectal cancer models and metastases, TGF-β-rich stromal programmes attenuate response to PD-L1 blockade, whereas pathway inhibition can restore T-cell infiltration and therapeutic sensitivity [34–36].

TGF-β should therefore be assessed by active ligand localisation, phosphorylated SMAD output, fibroblast state, matrix architecture and CD8 + T-cell distance from tumour glands rather than by total ligand abundance alone. The pathway is most actionable when active signalling coincides with T-cell exclusion and when tumour-cell dependence on tumour-suppressive TGF-β signalling has been lost. Because systemic blockade can impair wound healing and immune tolerance, lesion- and state-selected strategies are more defensible than universal addition to checkpoint blockade.

VEGF and angiogenic immune suppression

VEGF is not merely an angiogenic growth factor. It promotes abnormal and hypoxic vasculature, impairs dendritic-cell maturation, facilitates the accumulation of regulatory T cells and myeloid-derived suppressor cells, alters endothelial adhesion molecules and can contribute to T-cell exhaustion [37–41]. Clinical validation is strongest in hepatocellular carcinoma, where atezolizumab-bevacizumab improved outcomes compared with sorafenib [24, 25]. These results establish the therapeutic relevance of VEGF blockade in a checkpoint-containing regimen, while the proposed immune-access mechanism derives from preclinical and translational evidence rather than being directly demonstrated by the randomised trial [37–41]. Mechanistically, the treatment objective is not simple vessel starvation but vascular normalisation sufficient to improve perfusion, antigen presentation and immune-cell entry.

Anti-VEGF therapy should therefore be viewed as a platform for immune access. Excessive or poorly timed angiogenesis inhibition may worsen hypoxia and induce CXCL8, CXCL12, CCL28 and alternative myeloid programmes. In GI cancers complicated by cirrhosis, portal hypertension, bleeding risk, bowel-wall vulnerability or ascites, the benefit-risk balance depends on both the tumour secretome state and host vascular resilience.

CXCL12-CXCR4: fibroblast-mediated immune sequestration

CXCL12-CXCR4 is a canonical stromal chemokine barrier. In pancreatic cancer models, CXCL12 produced by FAP-expressing cancer-associated fibroblasts can coat tumour cells and exclude T cells, whereas CXCR4 inhibition synergizes with checkpoint blockade. Human studies in pancreatic and colorectal cancers indicate that CXCR4 blockade can induce integrated immune remodelling, although clinical benefit depends on the presence or generation of tumour-reactive effector cells [42–44].

The relevant translational endpoint is not plasma drug exposure alone, but redistribution of T cells from stroma into tumour epithelium, attenuation of peritumoural CXCL12 gradients and improved antigen-presenting-cell function. Because fibroblasts are heterogeneous and some subsets may restrain tumour growth, the therapeutic goal should be to reprogramme or block harmful ligand-receptor interactions rather than deplete stroma indiscriminately [42–44].

CXCL8/IL-8-CXCR1/2 and neutrophil-rich resistance

CXCL8/IL-8 and related CXCR1/2 ligands such as CXCL1, CXCL2 and CXCL5 recruit neutrophils and granulocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cells, support angiogenesis and maintain wound-healing inflammation. In pancreatic cancer, CXCR2 inhibition suppresses metastasis and augments immunotherapy in preclinical models. Clinically, high baseline IL-8 and failure of IL-8 to decline early during therapy have been associated with reduced checkpoint-blockade benefit, suggesting that IL-8 can function both as a driver and as a dynamic marker of non-productive inflammation [45–47].

In GI oncology, serum IL-8 is heavily confounded by infection, biliary obstruction, liver dysfunction, cachexia, mucosal injury and treatment toxicity. It is most informative when combined with tissue evidence of neutrophil or polymorphonuclear myeloid-derived suppressor cell (PMN-MDSC) infiltration, tumour CXCR2-ligand expression and limited effector T-cell entry. Short-course or sequential CXCR1/2 blockade may be more plausible than chronic complete inhibition, particularly in patients at risk of bacterial translocation or biliary infection [45–47].

CCL2-CCR2 and CSF1-CSF1R: macrophage recruitment, survival and state

CCL2 recruits CCR2 + inflammatory monocytes, whereas CSF1 supports macrophage survival and differentiation. In pancreatic cancer, where these axes are especially well studied, recruited and resident macrophages occupy stromal, perivascular and invasive-margin niches. CCR2 inhibition combined with chemotherapy and CSF1R blockade can remodel macrophage states, but clinical translation remains difficult because macrophages are plastic, alternative recruitment pathways can compensate, and indiscriminate depletion may remove antigen-presenting or tissue-repair functions [48–50].

Macrophage-directed secretome therapy should therefore be state-specific. The desired outcome is not macrophage elimination, but a shift from suppressive macrophage-fibroblast loops towards antigen presentation, interferon-induced chemokine production and effector-cell support. Pharmacodynamic readouts should include macrophage-state conversion, CXCL9/10 induction, T-cell redistribution and compensatory neutrophil recruitment rather than macrophage density alone [48–50].

IL-6-family cytokines, IL-17 and IL-23: inflammation is not immunity

GI cancers often arise in chronically inflamed tissues, but chronic inflammation can promote tumour growth, angiogenesis, epithelial plasticity, myeloid recruitment and T-cell dysfunction. IL-6-family cytokines activate JAK-STAT3 signalling in tumour cells, fibroblasts and immune cells; IL-23 and IL-17 link barrier inflammation to neutrophil recruitment and tumour-promoting cytokine loops [51–54]. These pathways illustrate why anti-inflammatory therapy must be mechanistically selected: blocking suppressive inflammation may enhance antitumour immunity, whereas blocking interferon-dependent chemokines may impair effector recruitment.

Serum IL-6 or C-reactive protein is easy to measure but nonspecific. Clinical usefulness depends on integration with tissue STAT3 activation, myeloid composition, treatment toxicity, infection status and liver function. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma and hepatocellular carcinoma, inflammatory fibroblasts, macrophages, tumour cells and background organ disease can all contribute to these signals, making source-aware interpretation essential.

Matrix-coupled secreted proteins: SPP1, periostin, galectins and proteases

SPP1, also known as osteopontin, links macrophages, fibroblasts, endothelial cells and tumour cells. It binds integrins and CD44-family receptors, supports adhesion and migration, and can polarise immune cells towards suppressive states. Single-cell and spatial studies in colorectal cancer have identified interactions between FAP+ fibroblasts and SPP1 + macrophages as components of suppressive niches, with related SPP1-associated patterns reported in gastric cancer and other solid tumours [55–59].

Periostin, galectin-1, galectin-3 and matrix metalloproteinases demonstrate that extracellular matrix is an active immune-signalling reservoir rather than a passive scaffold. These proteins regulate adhesion, collagen organisation, glycan-dependent T-cell and myeloid interactions, angiogenesis, latent cytokine activation and receptor or ligand shedding [60–64]. Therapeutic development should emphasise niche-specific and receptor-specific intervention, because broad matrix depletion can damage protective stromal functions.

Developmental and stress-linked escape: DKK1, MIF, complement and vesicular checkpoints

DKK1, a secreted WNT-pathway modulator, can mark developmental immune escape and has been linked to impaired dendritic-cell recruitment, CD8 + T-cell dysfunction, suppressive myeloid programmes and checkpoint resistance. It is most informative when interpreted together with WNT-β-catenin activity, dendritic-cell abundance and lesion site [65–67]. Macrophage migration inhibitory factor links stress responses, hypoxia, macrophage states and tumour-cell survival, and should be interpreted with CD74 or chemokine-receptor expression, SPP1 + macrophages and T-cell dysfunction [68, 69]. Complement fragments such as C5a can recruit suppressive myeloid cells, but complement may also reflect antibody-mediated opsonization or tissue injury, making localisation and functional validation necessary [70].

Checkpoint biology extends beyond membrane-bound ligands. Soluble PD-L1 and exosomal PD-L1 can circulate, suppress T-cell activation at a distance and report tumour-immune communication [71–73]. Extracellular vesicles can additionally carry TGF-β, MHC molecules, death ligands, enzymes and other proteins, together with non-protein cargo.

Disease-specific secretome landscapes

Gastric and gastro-esophageal cancers integrate mucosal inflammation, Epstein-Barr virus-positive immune infiltration, MSI status, PD-L1 expression, HER2-directed antibody biology, diffuse-type stromal remodelling, peritoneal dissemination and angiogenesis. Immune-organised tumours with effector chemokines and TLS may respond to checkpoint-rich strategies, whereas diffuse or scirrhous tumours may be dominated by TGF-β, CXCL12, periostin, SPP1 + macrophages, CXCL8/IL-8-CXCR1/2 signalling or DKK1. Anti-angiogenic therapy can remodel the gastric tumour microenvironment, but benefit depends on whether VEGF-driven immune suppression is truly dominant [74, 75] (Fig. 4; Table 3).

Site-specific secreted-protein states and immunotherapy implications

Esophageal cancers add treatment-induced complexity. PD-1-based systemic combinations improve survival in advanced disease [15, 16], while toripalimab combined with definitive chemoradiotherapy has shown activity in locally advanced esophageal squamous-cell carcinoma [76]. Because radiotherapy, cytotoxic therapy and checkpoint blockade can remodel the tumour microenvironment on different timescales, secretome studies should prioritise longitudinal sampling rather than relying on pretreatment tissue alone.

Recent single-cell analyses of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma treated with neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy identified a CXCL13 + exhausted CD8 + T-cell state enriched in pretreatment responders, whereas post-treatment non-responders showed more deeply exhausted CXCL13 + CD8+ T cells together with LRRC15 + fibroblasts and SPP1 + macrophages [77]. These findings support a model in which the meaning of CXCL13 and SPP1 depends on cellular state and tissue context rather than bulk abundance alone; they do not establish either marker as a validated stand-alone predictor.

Colorectal cancer demonstrates that antigenicity and microenvironmental permission must align. MSI-H/dMMR tumours often have high neoantigen burden, immune infiltration and interferon-driven chemokines, explaining their sensitivity to PD-1 blockade. Resistance can arise through antigen-presentation defects, JAK-STAT alterations, TGF-β-rich stroma, suppressive myeloid infiltration or metastatic-site tolerance [17–22, 34–36, 78–81]. MSS colorectal cancer is more heterogeneous: a liver metastasis may combine TGF-β-mediated exclusion, VEGF and hypoxia, SPP1 + macrophage-fibroblast niches, CXCR2-driven neutrophil inflammation and DKK1/WNT-linked dendritic-cell exclusion [78–81].

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma is an archetype of secretome-driven immune resistance. CAF-derived CXCL12 can exclude T cells; CXCR2 ligands recruit neutrophils; CCL2 and CSF1 sustain macrophages; IL-6 and TGF-β reinforce inflammatory and fibrotic suppression; VEGF and hypoxia distort vascular access; and matrix proteins restrict cellular movement and drug delivery. Checkpoint blockade has minimal activity except in rare dMMR tumours because immune interactions are organised against productive T-cell immunity [82, 83].

Hepatocellular carcinoma arises in an organ designed for immune tolerance. VEGF, TGF-β, IL-6, WNT-β-catenin activation, SPP1 + macrophages, MIF, DKK1 and suppressive myeloid programmes can coexist with cirrhosis, viral hepatitis, metabolic inflammation and portal hypertension [65–69, 84, 85]. Biliary tract cancers share desmoplasia, cholestatic inflammation, hepatic tolerance and chemotherapy-induced immune remodelling. TLS-associated CXCL13 programmes may mark immune-permissive tumours, whereas dense CAF and macrophage programmes may suppress effector-cell access [86].

Single-cell profiling has defined S100P+SPP1- and S100P-SPP1 + intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma states with distinct immune composition and prognosis; the S100P-SPP1 + state showed greater SPP1 + macrophage infiltration, less aggressive behaviour and better survival [87]. More recent single-cell and spatial work implicated ASPH in an invasive programme and showed preclinical inhibition by cepharanthine, while lymph-node tumour cells expressing tumour-specific MHC-II without adequate CD80/CD86 costimulation were associated with CD4 + T-cell anergy [88]. Spatial proteomics further identified CD163hi M2-like resident macrophages in close interaction with CD8 + T cells as a poor-prognosis niche [89].

Secreted proteins as biomarkers

Most single-analyte cytokine studies remain insufficient for clinical decision-making. A clinically useful biomarker must address four mechanistic questions: which cell type produces the protein, where the protein is localised, which receptor-expressing cells are positioned to respond, and whether perturbing the protein alters the relevant therapeutic mechanism. Without this information, a statistically significant association can remain biologically ambiguous. TGF-β may represent latent matrix stores or active stromal exclusion; IL-8 may reflect infection, tissue injury or neutrophil-rich resistance; and soluble PD-L1 may originate from tumour cells, immune cells, alternative splicing or vesicular release [34–36, 45–47, 71–73].

Tissue-based assays should therefore move beyond bulk abundance towards spatially informed interpretation. Multiplex immunohistochemistry, imaging mass cytometry, spatial transcriptomics and spatial proteomics can define whether T cells are excluded from tumour nests, whether CXCL13-rich TLS are mature, whether SPP1 + macrophages contact fibroblasts, whether VEGF-high regions are hypoxic and whether CXCL12 gradients surround tumour glands. Single-cell RNA sequencing can assign cellular sources and receptor distribution, but RNA is an imperfect surrogate for protein abundance, activation state and extracellular localisation.

Circulating assays remain valuable because they can be sampled longitudinally and capture systemic inflammation, target engagement and early therapeutic response. On-treatment change may be more informative than baseline abundance in many settings. GI cancers, however, present frequent confounders, including biliary obstruction, bacterial translocation, bowel inflammation, cachexia, cirrhosis, infection, steroid exposure, surgery and chemotherapy-related toxicity. Circulating secretome markers should therefore be paired with clinical context and, when feasible, tissue or imaging evidence of the corresponding state.

Pre-analytical handling is itself part of the biomarker. VEGF and TGF-β1 are especially sensitive to sample matrix: clotting and platelet activation can substantially increase serum concentrations, and residual platelet contamination can also inflate plasma measurements [90, 91]. Prospective studies should therefore prespecify anticoagulant, processing interval, centrifugation and platelet-depletion steps, freeze-thaw cycles, and the use of serum versus plasma; values obtained from different matrices should not be pooled without analytical bridging.

Tissue availability and turnaround time impose additional constraints. Small endoscopic or needle biopsies must first support routine diagnosis and mandatory molecular testing, whereas multiplex or spatial assays may require additional sections, controlled fixation, specialized imaging or sequencing, and batch bioinformatics [92]. Until abbreviated workflows are analytically validated, a tiered approach—routine immunohistochemistry/in situ assays or focused panels first, with high-plex spatial profiling reserved for selected cases or trials—is more realistic than assuming rapid clinical turnaround for every biopsy.

Single-cell and spatial omics reveal secretome circuitry

Bulk transcriptomic or proteomic data do not by themselves establish the cellular origin, recipient population or spatial distribution of secreted mediators. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) can identify ligand-producing and receptor-bearing cell states, but dissociation removes anatomical context; conversely, ligand-receptor inference is statistical and does not prove protein secretion, receptor engagement or downstream signalling. Secretome circuitry therefore requires triangulation across cellular source, spatial proximity, protein-level localisation and functional perturbation.

Across gastrointestinal cancers, these methods now resolve recurrent but context-dependent microenvironmental states. In colorectal cancer, spatial analyses have identified multicellular immune hubs and FAP+ fibroblast-SPP1 + macrophage niches linked to extracellular-matrix remodelling and immune exclusion [55, 56]. Gastric cancer studies similarly implicate SPP1 + macrophage states [58], while recent ESCC and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma datasets define treatment-associated T-cell, fibroblast and macrophage configurations that cannot be inferred from bulk abundance alone [77, 87–89]. Together, these observations support the four modules as overlapping functional states rather than fixed molecular subtypes.

Future studies should combine spatial transcriptomics, spatial proteomics and causal perturbation to distinguish biologically active secretome circuits from transcriptomic co-occurrence. Spatial-CITE-seq enables high-plex protein and whole-transcriptome co-mapping at cellular resolution [93]. Spatial tri-omic approaches can add chromatin or histone-state information to RNA and protein measurements [94], while Perturb-DBiT links pooled CRISPR perturbations to spatially resolved transcriptional and tumour-microenvironmental consequences in intact tissue [95]. These platforms are methodologically powerful, but their translational value will depend on reproducibility, tissue compatibility, cost, turnaround and prospective linkage to treatment decisions.

Therapeutic strategy and trial design

Secreted proteins provide strategies for rational immunotherapy combinations. One strategy is barrier removal: TGF-β modulation for stromal exclusion, CXCR4 blockade for CXCL12-mediated sequestration, VEGF inhibition for vascular and myeloid suppression, and selective matrix or galectin modulation for extracellular barriers. A second strategy is myeloid reprogramming through CCR2, CXCR2, CSF1R, IL-6-JAK-STAT3, MIF, SPP1 or complement-directed approaches. A third is immune priming with vaccines, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, oncolytic viruses, STING agonists or TLR agonists. A fourth is immune organisation, preserving or inducing CXCL9/10/11 and CXCL13-associated immunity and TLS organisation. A fifth is control of systemic communication through soluble checkpoint and extracellular-vesicle-associated pathways [71–73] (Table 4).

Translational matching of secreted-protein state to combination strategy

Anti-VEGF therapy may normalise vessels at one dose and worsen hypoxia at another. CXCR2 blockade may reduce suppressive neutrophils but impair antimicrobial defence during mucosal injury. TGF-β blockade may open stroma but increase toxicity around surgery or radiation. CSF1R blockade may reprogramme macrophages but induce compensatory neutrophil recruitment. Combinations should therefore be designed as temporal programmes rather than as simultaneous drug accumulation.

In advanced biliary tract cancer, first-line bintrafusp alfa plus gemcitabine-cisplatin did not provide a clinically meaningful survival benefit over chemotherapy alone, despite the rationale for simultaneous TGF-β trapping and PD-L1 blockade [96]. In hyaluronan-high metastatic pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, PEGPH20 added to nab-paclitaxel/gemcitabine increased objective response rate but failed to improve overall or progression-free survival in HALO-301 [97]. Myeloid-axis translation has also been difficult: the CCR2 inhibitor PF-04136309 with nab-paclitaxel/gemcitabine raised concern for pulmonary toxicity and showed no efficacy signal above chemotherapy [98], while a randomised phase 2 CSF1R programme using cabiralizumab with nivolumab-based regimens failed to improve progression-free survival in advanced pancreatic cancer [99]. These trials do not establish a single cause of failure. Plausible contributors include inadequate selection for a target-dependent secretome state, redundant or compensatory myeloid/stromal biology, dose and schedule effects, and toxicity that limits sustained pathway suppression. The broader lesson is that target abundance is not equivalent to target dependence.

Trial design should evolve from all-comer combinations towards adaptive enrichment by secretome state. A platform trial could assign patients to immune-angiogenic, TGF-β/CXCL12-excluded, macrophage-fibroblast, CXCR2-neutrophil, immune-desert or TLS/effector-chemokine arms. Mandatory biopsies are challenging, but feasible designs can combine archival tissue, targeted fresh biopsies, baseline and early on-treatment plasma, and imaging markers of perfusion and inflammation. The central pharmacodynamic question is whether the targeted secretome state converts in the expected direction (Table 5).

Clinical anchor points for secretome-guided immunotherapy in gastrointestinal cancer

Secreted proteins are normal tissue regulators. TGF-β maintains epithelial repair and immune tolerance; IL-6 and IL-8 participate in infection responses; VEGF maintains vascular homeostasis; CSF1 supports tissue macrophages; CXCR4 participates in haematopoiesis; complement mediates host defence; and matrix proteins contribute to tissue repair. Patients with GI cancer often have mucosal injury, cirrhosis, biliary obstruction, malnutrition, ascites or prior surgery. Precision is therefore not only about efficacy; it is also about avoiding unnecessary blockade when the target programme is not dominant.

Proposed secretome-guided clinical workflow

A practical workflow can be implemented in stages. First, completed trials should be retrospectively annotated using available tissue and plasma to test whether TGF-β exclusion, VEGF-driven angiogenesis, CXCL8/IL-8-CXCR1/2 inflammation, CXCL13-associated immunity/TLS organisation or SPP1-associated macrophage/stromal states explain exceptional benefit and resistance. Second, standard-of-care cohorts should prospectively collect baseline plasma, archival tissue, optional fresh biopsies and early on-treatment plasma. Third, interventional enrichment should restrict secreted-axis drugs to patients whose tumours show the corresponding state. Fourth, adaptive therapy should use on-treatment conversion to continue, intensify or redirect treatment (Fig. 5).

Clinical implementation of secretome-guided immunotherapy

A future pathology report may not catalogue every cytokine. Instead, it may assign a functional extracellular phenotype: immune-organised with mature TLS; TGF-β-active stromal exclusion; angiogenic-myeloid suppression; CXCR2-neutrophil inflammation; SPP1-associated macrophage/stromal state; or developmental dendritic-cell exclusion. Such terminology will be useful only if supported by validated assays and treatment evidence, but it aligns pathology, immunology, oncology and trial design around a shared therapeutic question: which extracellular programme is preventing immunity in this patient, in this lesion, at this time?

Limitations and priorities for validation

Several limitations should be recognised. First, many secretome signals are context-dependent and pleiotropic; a mediator that supports immune suppression in one spatial niche can reflect immune activation or tissue repair in another. Second, circulating assays are vulnerable to pre-analytical and host-physiology confounders, especially in patients with liver dysfunction, biliary obstruction, infection or recent invasive procedures; platelet-dependent pre-analytical effects are particularly relevant to VEGF and TGF-β1 measurements [90, 91]. Third, single-cell transcriptomic data provide valuable source mapping but cannot substitute for spatial protein localisation or activation-state measurement. Fourth, many proposed combinations are supported by preclinical or early-phase evidence, and several mechanistically attractive stromal or myeloid programmes have failed in unselected or incompletely selected clinical populations [96–99] (Fig. 6).

Context-specific evidence map of secretome circuits across gastrointestinal cancers. L1, clinical validation of a therapeutic target/axis or regimen in a GI cancer context (not direct proof of the proposed secretome mechanism); L2, human clinical correlative or early interventional evidence; L3, strong preclinical, single-cell/spatial or mechanistic evidence; L4, emerging or hypothesis-generating evidence

The next phase should prioritise assay reproducibility, sample timing, tissue-source annotation, pharmacodynamic endpoints and prospective state-selection. Secretome-guided therapy will be convincing only when it improves a clinically relevant decision: selecting checkpoint blockade alone, adding a specific barrier-removal strategy, sequencing priming before checkpoint maintenance, or avoiding a toxic combination in patients without the target state.

Conclusions

The GI tumour microenvironment is an extracellular communication network as much as a cellular ecosystem. Secreted proteins shape whether immunity is recruited, excluded, suppressed, organised or exhausted, thereby helping explain divergent responses to checkpoint blockade, anti-angiogenic therapy and TLS-associated inflammation. The translational opportunity is not simply to measure more cytokines, but to define secretome states that are spatially resolved, mechanistically interpretable, targetable and dynamically monitorable. A secretome-guided approach could improve GI immunotherapy by matching extracellular barriers to rational combinations and using early pharmacodynamic conversion as a decision point.
