Early Versus Delayed Treatment with Ocrelizumab: A Multicenter, Real-World Observational Study in People with Multiple Sclerosis
Authors: Enrique Alvarez, Robert T Naismith, Le H Hua, Elmor D Pineda, Hela Romdhani, Annie Guérin, Mohira Lévesque-Leroux, Gayatri Marathe, Kunio Nakamura, Daniel Ontaneda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGrowing evidence suggests that first-line (1L) treatment with high-efficacy disease-modifying therapies (HE-DMTs) may have greater clinical benefit in multiple sclerosis (MS) than delaying HE-DMTs to second- or subsequent-line (2L+) therapy. However, MS treatment is often initiated using an escalation approach, involving initial treatment with low- to moderate-efficacy DMTs followed by escalation to HE-DMTs in the presence of new disease activity or disability worsening. Ocrelizumab is a HE-DMT approved for the treatment of relapsing and primary progressive MS, but real-world outcomes of ocrelizumab as a 1L vs 2L+ therapy are not well characterized.MethodsReal-world data from people with MS (pwMS) receiving treatment at four centers in the USA were analyzed. The effectiveness of ocrelizumab when administered as 1L or 2L+ treatment was compared for up to 60 months in follow-up. The primary outcome was time to confirmed disability worsening (based on Patient-Determined Disease Steps [PDDS]). Secondary outcomes were time to requiring a walking aid (PDDS ≥ 4), annualized patient-reported relapse rate, and magnetic resonance imaging measures (e.g., brain volume and number and volume of new T2 lesions).ResultsA total of 403 pwMS (1L n = 309, 2L+ n = 94) were identified. Overall, pwMS who received 1L ocrelizumab experienced less confirmed disease worsening (25.8% vs 42.6%; P = 0.03) and requirement for a walking aid (6.5% vs 27.4%; P < 0.01) compared with the 2L+ cohort. The 1L group also had a significantly lower change in T2 lesion volume (mean difference − 0.797 mL; P < 0.05) and a lower risk of developing new T2 lesions than the 2L+ group (incidence rate ratio 0.88 [95% CI 0.78–0.99]; P = 0.04). Relapse rate and brain volume outcomes were not significantly different between groups.ConclusionThis study contributes to the growing pool of evidence that 1L treatment with HE-DMTs such as ocrelizumab can improve clinical outcomes for pwMS compared with delaying HE-DMT to 2L+ .Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40120-026-00951-x.Keywords: Multiple sclerosis, Observational study, Ocrelizumab, Treatment delay

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Ocrelizumab is a high-efficacy disease-modifying therapy (HE-DMT) indicated for the treatment of relapsing forms of multiple sclerosis (MS) and primary progressive MS.This study compared real-world clinical outcomes in people with MS (pwMS) who were treated with ocrelizumab as either a first-line (1L) or a second- or subsequent-line (2L+) therapy, based on emerging evidence that 1L treatment with HE-DMTs can result in better clinical outcomes in pwMS than the standard escalation approach. What was learned from the study? Compared with pwMS who were treated with ocrelizumab in the 2L+ setting, the 1L cohort experienced significantly reduced risks of disease worsening, requiring a walking aid, and developing new T2 lesions; the 1L cohort also had a reduced rate of change in volume of T2 lesions.This study showed that early treatment with HE-DMTs has several important clinical benefits for pwMS and the results are in alignment with prior work favoring initial treatment with HE-DMT over escalation approaches.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a complex neurodegenerative and inflammatory disease of the central nervous system (CNS) that affects an estimated 2.9 million people worldwide [1, 2] and causes demyelination and axonal loss, resulting in physical and cognitive disability [3, 4]. Disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) are a cornerstone of MS therapy, and many options with varying levels of efficacy are approved. High-efficacy DMTs (HE-DMTs) have demonstrated superior ability to reduce relapse rates, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-detected lesion activity, and disability progression compared with low- or moderate-efficacy DMTs (LE- or ME-DMTs), but limited clinical guidance on early HE-DMT treatment and concerns about long-term safety limit HE-DMT use [5–7]. Although LE-DMTs or ME-DMTs are less effective at preventing relapses and progression than HE-DMTs, their well-established and longer-term safety follow-up data may make them more appealing to clinicians and their patients [5–7]. As such, European and American treatment guidelines for MS treatment emphasize individualized treatment decisions based on disease activity, patient preferences, and risk–benefit profiles [1, 8, 9].

Historically, MS has been treated using an escalation approach that initiates first-line (1L) treatment with a LE- or ME-DMT and escalates to a HE-DMT if new disease activity occurs [6, 8–10]. However, recent data suggest that postponing HE-DMT therapy to second- or subsequent-line (2L+) treatment may result in poorer outcomes for people with MS (pwMS). Several studies have demonstrated that an alternative early high-efficacy approach where a HE-DMT is initiated as 1L treatment may delay disease worsening and improve clinical outcomes for pwMS [11–13]. Based on these studies, the MS treatment paradigm is shifting to embrace more 1L treatment with HE-DMTs for pwMS [5, 6].

Ocrelizumab is a CD20-directed cytolytic antibody HE-DMT approved for the treatment of people with relapsing MS (pwRMS) and people with primary progressive MS (pwPPMS) [13–15], and its utility as a 1L HE-DMT is supported by extensive safety and efficacy data. Long-term data from the phase III OPERA I/II (NCT01247324 and NCT01412333) and ORATORIO (NCT01194570) studies demonstrated that after 11 years of continuous ocrelizumab treatment, 75% of pwRMS were progression-free and 90% did not require a walking aid, while more than 33% of pwPPMS were progression-free and 80% did not require a wheelchair, respectively [16]. In clinical trials, disability accumulation was significantly lower in pwMS who initiated ocrelizumab 2 years earlier than in those with delayed ocrelizumab treatment [16], consistent with previous reports indicating the benefits of 1L ocrelizumab [17–19]. Additionally, over an 11-year follow-up period across 13 clinical trials, ocrelizumab exhibited a stable and favorable safety profile in pwRMS and pwPPMS, with strong adherence and infrequent serious infections [20]. Although these clinical studies are promising for the use of ocrelizumab as a 1L therapy in relapsing MS, real-world studies with longer follow-up periods are needed to determine whether long-term benefits in relapse reduction and disease progression reduction are sustained. The goal of this retrospective, noninterventional, observational study was to observe real-world outcomes for pwMS treated with either 1L or 2L+ ocrelizumab in the USA.

Methods

Ethical Approval

Anonymized patient data were collected from participating clinical sites following institutional review board approval at each site and in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1964 and its later amendments. The study was approved by the Cleveland Clinic Institutional Review Board (23-1329), the Colorado Multiple Institutional Review Board (23-0339), and the Washington University in St. Louis Institutional Review Board (202308182). The study was determined to be minimal risk as a result of using secondary data/data collected for routine clinical practice; therefore, informed consent and HIPAA authorization were waived.

Study Overview

This study was a retrospective, noninterventional, observational study of pwMS treated with ocrelizumab across four US centers in a real-world setting, namely the Cleveland Clinic in Cleveland, Ohio; Cleveland Clinic in Las Vegas, Nevada; University of Colorado in Aurora, Colorado; and Washington University in St. Louis, Missouri.

Study Objectives

The primary objective was to compare confirmed disease worsening based on Patient-Determined Disease Steps (PDDS) in pwMS treated with either 1L or 2L+ ocrelizumab. Secondary objectives were to compare the time to sustained use of a walking aid, annualized patient-reported relapse rate, and MRI outcomes (including annualized number of new T2 lesions and the rate of change [ROC] in T2 lesion volume and brain volume) between the 1L and 2L+ ocrelizumab cohorts. The exploratory objective was to assess the correlation between the number of new T2 lesions and confirmed disability worsening based on PDDS.

Data Collection

Deidentified clinical data were abstracted from electronic medical records from Cleveland Clinic, University of Colorado, and Washington University centers in the USA between August 2023 and August 2024 for pwMS who met the selection criteria. Centers were asked to shift dates of clinical events by a random number prior to sharing the data to maintain protection of health information. The applied offset was different for each pwMS but the same for a specific pwMS, such that the relative number of days between dates for events was preserved.

Quantitative MRI data were obtained from the Cleveland Clinic sites using fully automated segmentation techniques, as previously published [21, 22]. The University of Colorado and Washington University provided anonymized MRI data that were quantitatively analyzed by icobrain ms software (Icometrix, Leuven, Belgium) [23, 24].

Study Population

Two cohorts were included in the study. The 1L cohort included pwMS who initiated treatment with ocrelizumab as a 1L DMT. The 2L+ cohort comprised pwMS who initiated subsequent treatment with ocrelizumab in 2L+ after initial therapy with other MS DMTs observed in the real-world; criteria for treatment escalation were not assessed or defined for the 2L+ cohort in this observational study. For both cohorts, the index date was defined as the date of 1L DMT initiation, and pwMS were required to have a diagnosis of MS and be ≥ 18 years of age at 1L DMT initiation. PwMS who had previously been treated with rituximab were excluded from analysis. Full inclusion and exclusion criteria are listed in the electronic Supplementary Material (Supplemental Methods).

PwMS were included in the study if the participating center had complete data on the participant’s MS medical history, including age at MS diagnosis, MS disease course, and MS care information (i.e., DMTs received and first and last dates of administration). Inclusion required a 1L DMT initiation on or after January 1, 2016, and received ≥ 2 courses of ocrelizumab (either 1L or 2L+), defined as one or two initial 300-mg dose infusions plus one subsequent 600-mg dose. The first dose of ocrelizumab was received on or after its approval date (March 28, 2017) and ≥ 18 months prior to the data collection date, to allow for a potential follow-up of ≥ 18 months after the first ocrelizumab dose. Each pwMS was required to have available data on ≥ 2 patient-reported outcome (PRO) results for ≥ 1 of the collected PRO measures, with one collected before or ≤ 60 days after 1L initiation and one anytime thereafter (before the date of the last visit at the MS department in the medical center at the time of data collection).

Study Duration

The study design is shown in Fig. 1. Data for pwMS were analyzed in two different time periods: full follow-up and other 1L DMT follow-up. The full follow-up was defined as the period from the 1L DMT initiation (whether ocrelizumab or a different DMT) to the date of the last visit to the MS department. For the 2L+ cohort, the other 1L DMT follow-up was defined as the period from 1L DMT initiation to the day before 2L+ ocrelizumab initiation. To ensure fair and accurate comparisons between groups, an algorithm was applied to obtain an “other 1L DMT” follow-up duration for the 1L cohort that had a similar distribution of duration as the 2L+ cohort. Specifically, the other 1L DMT follow-up duration in the 2L+ cohort was assumed to follow a normal distribution, and this estimated normal distribution was used to generate the other 1L DMT follow-up duration for each pwMS in the 1L cohort, with estimated parameters based on the 2L+ cohort. The end of the other 1L DMT follow-up for each pwMS in the 1L cohort was defined as the earliest of either (1) the index date plus the generated duration or (2) the end of the full follow-up.

Real-World PROs

MS Worsening

Disease worsening was assessed using the PDDS, a validated PRO which correlates well with MS disability as determined by the Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) [25–27]. EDSS was not assessed because it was not used routinely in clinical practice across all centers and was therefore not available for all patients. PDDS scores range from 0 to 8, reflecting normal abilities to being bedridden [25]. For the full follow-up, pwMS were assessed for worsening if they had one baseline and ≥ 2 follow-up PDDS measures separated by ≥ 6 months during the full follow-up. Assessed MS worsening outcomes were (1) confirmed disease worsening, defined as worsening (PDDS increase of ≥ 1 point) confirmed 6 months or later after the first event, with no interval improvement; and (2) time to requiring a walking aid, defined as an increase in the PDDS score to ≥ 4 confirmed 6 months or later after the first time it was observed, with no interval improvement among pwMS with a baseline measure of PDDS score < 4. The PDDS score of 4 indicates early use of a cane, with higher scores reflecting more severe disability [25, 28]. In the other 1L DMT follow-up, pwMS were further required to have ≥ 1 PDDS measure, and only the first worsening event was required to occur within the other 1L DMT follow-up, with the confirmation event occurring any time during the full follow-up.

MS Relapses

The annualized patient-reported relapse rate was assessed among pwRMS who had ≥ 1 visit with data on patient-reported relapses. For the full follow-up, the outcome was defined as the total number of patient-reported relapses divided by the total patient-years. In the other 1L DMT follow-up, the annualized patient-reported relapse rate was calculated as the total number of relapses divided by the total patient-years between the index date and the last visit in the other 1L DMT follow-up. Annualized patient-reported relapses were assessed rather than the annualized relapse rate (ARR) to highlight the patient’s perception of their disease and because ARR data were incomplete.

Quantitative MRI Lesions and Brain Volume

Annualized ROC in T2 lesions and brain volume were assessed in pwMS from Cleveland Clinic sites with ≥ 1 re-baseline MRI scan (within first 12 months post index), ≥ 2 years follow-up post re-baseline MRI, and available brain volume data for ≥ 2 quarters during the first 2 years post re-baseline MRI. MRI lesion and brain volume data from other centers were not combined because of differences in imaging technologies, and small sample sizes at other centers did not support separate analyses. As a result of varying time between MRI scans, brain volume data were transformed into quarterly data. If pwMS had > 1 MRI in a quarter, the average volume was used. The last observation carried forward (LOCF) approach was used for quarters with no available data. Low-quality MRI scans (e.g., scans with noise and artifacts) were excluded from analysis.

Data for the number of new T2 lesions were aggregated across pwMS from all centers. PwMS were included if they had ≥ 1 MRI scan with data on number of new T2 lesions and ≥ 12 months of observation between the index date and date of last MRI scan. The correlation between the number of new T2 lesions (0, 1, ≥ 2 lesions) and PDDS was assessed at the patient-MRI level, among scans with PDDS data measured within a 3-month window ± the MRI scan date.

Statistical Analyses

Descriptive Analyses

Demographic data were tabulated separately for pwMS in the 1L and the 2L+ cohorts. Continuous variables were presented as means (standard deviations; SD), medians (interquartile ranges; IQR) and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages.

Entropy Balancing

Entropy balancing was used to ensure that baseline characteristics of both cohorts (1L and 2L+) were balanced across covariates, including race, sex, education, employment status, living situation, age at diagnosis, MS subtype, comorbidities, disability scores, and number of relapses at baseline as well as healthcare payer type. Specifically, pwMS in the 2L+ ocrelizumab cohort were reweighted so that the distribution of the included covariates in the reweighted cohort had the exact same moments as in the 1L ocrelizumab cohort. PwMS included in each measured outcome differed between the cohorts, and therefore each outcome analyzed was reweighted separately for each assessed outcome. Covariates included in each analysis are indicated in the results. After entropy balancing, characteristics were considered balanced if standardized mean differences (SMDs) were < 0.1. Entropy balancing was chosen over alternative approaches such as inverse probability weighting (IPTW) because it achieves exact covariate balance on specified moments without relying on correct specification of a propensity score model [29]. Unlike IPTW, entropy balancing directly enforces balance through constrained reweighting while keeping the resulting weights as close as possible to the original. In addition, compared with PS matching, entropy balancing maximized the statistical power of this study by preserving the full analytic sample rather than discarding unmatched observations, which was a particularly important consideration given the small sample sizes available for some outcomes.

Comparative Analyses of Efficacy Outcomes

For all analyses, 95% confidence intervals (CI) were estimated using a nonparametric bootstrapping procedure with 499 replications. Models were adjusted for covariates as described in the Results.

MS Worsening

The same statistical methods were used for PDDS-based time to confirmed worsening and time to walking aid requirement. Time to MS worsening was assessed using Kaplan–Meier (KM) curves. To compare worsening rates between the two study cohorts, log-rank tests were performed at each of the timepoints. Analyses were censored at the date of the last PDDS measure (≥ 6 months prior to the end of the follow-up) to allow sufficient time to observe the confirmation of worsening. PwMS who did not experience confirmed worsening were censored at the date of the last PDDS measure (≥ 6 months prior to the end of the follow-up). For the other 1L DMT follow-up analysis, pwMS who did not experience confirmed worsening were censored at the earliest of either (1) the date at the end of other 1L DMT follow-up or (2) the date of the last PDDS measure, which was ≥ 6 months prior to the end of the full follow-up. Cox proportional hazard models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HR) of confirmed disease worsening based on PDDS scores between the 1L and 2L+ ocrelizumab cohorts. The models were adjusted for the following selected covariates given their potential impact on the disease progression: age at MS diagnosis, MS disease course (RMS/PPMS), and PDDS score at baseline.

Patient-Reported MS Relapses

Incidence rate ratios (IRR) for between-group (1L and 2L+) differences for annualized patient-reported relapse rates were assessed using negative binomial regression models. The models were further adjusted for age at MS diagnosis and number of patient-reported relapses at baseline.

Quantitative MRI Lesions and Brain Volume

The mean difference in ROC of T2 lesion volume and brain volumes between the 1L and 2L+ ocrelizumab cohorts over the first 2 years following a re-baselined MRI were estimated using mixed models for repeated measures. Annualized percentage ROC was also assessed using the log-transformed volume. The IRR for new T2 lesions between the 1L and 2L+ ocrelizumab cohorts was estimated using Poisson regression models. The models were further adjusted for age at MS diagnosis, MS disease course (RMS/PPMS), PDDS score, and MRI outcome of interest measured at re-baseline. Spearman correlation coefficient was used to explore the correlation between the number of new T2 lesions (0, 1, or ≥ 2) and the presence of worsening based on PDDS assessed at the patient level.

Results

PwMS Characteristics

A total of 403 pwMS were included across four centers: 309 in the 1L cohort and 94 in the 2L+ cohort (Table 1). The majority of pwMS from both cohorts were from the Cleveland Clinic Ohio site (1L, 69.6%; 2L+, 70.2%). The mean (SD) age at diagnosis in the 1L and 2L+ cohorts was 39.5 (11.8) and 36.4 (9.8) years, respectively. In the 1L and 2L+ cohorts, most pwMS were female (59.9% and 79.8%), white (77.3% and 84.0%), and not Hispanic (94.8% and 92.6%). At index date for the 1L/2L+ groups (i.e., 1L ocrelizumab initiation for 1L cohort and 1L DMT initiation for 2L+ cohort), 79.9%/88.3% had RMS, 58.9%/48.9% were employed full time, and 81.9%/79.8% were living at home without assistance. Among 1L (81.6%) and 2L+ (76.6%) pwMS with PDDS assessed, mean (SD) baseline PDDS scores were 1.8 (1.8) and 1.1 (1.4), respectively. A subset of each cohort was used for each analysis depending on data availability, and patient populations for individual analyses are described in the relevant results section (Fig. 1b).

Treatment Characteristics

First-line treatment characteristics for the 2L+ ocrelizumab cohort are listed in Table 2. The most common treatments before ocrelizumab initiation were fingolimod (36.2%) and dimethyl fumarate (28.7%). The majority of pwMS (75.5%) were receiving ME-DMTs prior to ocrelizumab initiation. The top reasons for DMT discontinuation prior to ocrelizumab initiation were physician’s decision (17.0%), other (16.0%), disease activity (10.6%), and disease progression (8.5%). The mean (SD) time between 1L DMT initiation and initiation of ocrelizumab in 2L+ was 1.7 (1.1) years. During that time, pwMS were prescribed an average (SD) of 1.2 (0.4) prior DMTs, ranging from 1 to 3 DMTs; most pwMS (85.1%) were prescribed only one DMT prior to initiating 2L+ therapy with ocrelizumab. The mean (SD) time between the last dose of DMT prior to ocrelizumab initiation and ocrelizumab initiation was 12.4 (11.7) months.

Patient-Reported Outcomes

MS Worsening: Full Follow-up

The pwMS included in the analysis of confirmed disease worsening based on PDDS are described in Table 3. Most characteristics were balanced across the 1L and 2L+ cohorts following entropy balancing, except for ethnicity, likely owing to the reduced number of ethnic minorities. In the 1L ocrelizumab cohort, 49 (25.8%) pwMS reported disease worsening during the full follow-up compared with 29 (42.6%) in the 2L+ ocrelizumab cohort (Fig. 2). During the full follow-up, pwMS who initiated 1L ocrelizumab had a 58% lower risk of experiencing disease worsening than those in the 2L+ ocrelizumab cohort (HR 0.42 [0.19–0.94], P = 0.03) (Fig. 2).

MS Worsening: Other 1L DMT Follow-up

Characteristics of pwMS were generally similar after entropy balancing in those assessed for confirmed disease worsening based on PDDS (Table S3) and time to walking aid requirement (Table S4) in the other 1L DMT follow-up. During the other 1L DMT follow-up, pwMS (characteristics in Table S3) who initiated 1L ocrelizumab had a 75% lower risk of disease worsening than the 2L+ cohort (HR 0.25 [0.08–0.73], P = 0.01; Fig. S1). PwMS with a baseline PDDS score of < 4 (Table S4) in the 1L cohort had a 93% lower risk of worsening to requiring a walking aid than the 2L+ cohort (HR 0.07 [0.02–0.29], P < 0.01; Fig. S2). These trends were consistent with the full-follow up.

Following entropy balancing, pwRMS in the 1L cohort had a shorter duration of follow-up than pwRMS in the 2L+ cohort (3.4 vs 4.9 years, respectively [SMD 0.83]). Quantitative MRI Lesions and Brain Volume: Full Follow-up Analysis

PwMS who initiated ocrelizumab in 1L had no significant change in T2 lesion volume during the first 2 years post baseline. Alternatively, pwMS who initiated ocrelizumab in 2L+ vs 1L experienced a significant increase in T2 lesion volume during the same time frame, leading to a significant difference of − 0.797 mL (95% CI − 1.52 to − 0.116; P < 0.05; Table 4) between groups. Changes in brain volume were not statistically different between the 2L+ and 1L groups; the difference in annualized ROC in whole brain fraction was 0.0004 (95% CI − 0.0024 to 0.0029; P = 0.75) favoring 1L, corresponding to an annualized percentage ROC of 0.04% (95% CI − 0.29 to 0.34%; P = 0.79; Tables 4 and 5).

Discussion

Early initiation of ocrelizumab was associated with favorable clinical outcomes. PwMS who initiated 1L ocrelizumab had lower risks of disease worsening (− 58%; based on PDDS) and requirement for a walking aid (− 84%) than those who started ocrelizumab as 2L+ therapy. The risks for disease worsening and requirement for a walking aid were further reduced by − 75% and − 93%, respectively, for 1L compared with 2L+ when compared with the period prior to 2L+ ocrelizumab initiation (i.e., other 1L DMT follow-up). The annualized patient-reported relapse rate was significantly lower in the 1L cohort than the 2L+ cohort at the other 1L DMT follow-up (IRR 0.54 [0.31–0.94], P = 0.03); however, relapse rates at the full follow-up were similar for the 1L and 2L+ ocrelizumab groups, suggesting relapse control may occur irrespective of when ocrelizumab is administered. Conversely, worsening of disability appears sensitive to the point in the disease course when the medication is initiated. Notably, the 1L group also experienced a significantly lower increase in T2 lesion volume and a lower risk of developing new T2 lesions than the 2L+ group. Changes in brain volume were statistically similar across groups, possibly as a result of a lack of power. These data support the use of ocrelizumab for early (1L) MS treatment to improve clinical outcomes and disease control rather than delaying treatment to later lines of therapy.

The results presented here are aligned with those of other studies showing the benefit of early HE-DMT treatment for MS. A retrospective cohort study of 748 pwMS demonstrated that, compared with patients who received 2L+ ocrelizumab, those treated with 1L ocrelizumab had a significantly reduced annual rate of events associated with a relapse and a lower risk of hospitalization within 1 year [11]. Additionally, in the prospective single-arm phase IIIb ENSEMBLE study (NCT03085810), people with early-stage relapsing–remitting MS who were treated with 1L ocrelizumab maintained low disease activity based on clinical and MRI measures over 4 years of follow-up [30]. Several retrospective cohort studies in Europe have indicated that, compared with an escalation approach, initial therapy with HE-DMTs is associated with significantly lower EDSS score after 5-year follow-up [31], lower probability of 6-month confirmed EDSS score worsening [32], lower probability of a first relapse [32], a significantly higher chance of achieving no evidence of disease activity within 2 years [12], and significant reductions in the rate of confirmed disability worsening and relapse outcomes [33, 34]. Furthermore, observational studies from MSBase, an international real-world registry, have demonstrated that early treatment with HE-DMTs lowered the risk of conversion to secondary progressive MS [35] and resulted in less disability accrual over 10 years [36] compared with delayed HE-DMT treatment. Two ongoing prospective randomized trials (TREAT-MS [NCT03500328] and DELIVER-MS [NCT03535298]) are directly comparing early HE-DMT vs escalation therapy in terms of clinical outcomes, quality of life, and safety; these trials will further elucidate the potential advantages of early HE-DMT treatment.

Some guidelines have recommended the escalation therapy approach for pwMS; however, numerous studies demonstrating the benefits of early HE-DMT therapy have contributed to a shift in care that now prioritizes the prevention of irreversible disability through the early targeting of inflammatory mechanisms using HE-DMTs [5, 6]. An Italian expert opinion group has agreed that a higher benefit and a better risk–benefit ratio may be achieved with early HE-DMT treatment, regardless of prognostic factors, vs an escalation approach [6]. Barriers to incorporating early treatment with HE-DMTs include restricted access/prohibitive costs of HE-DMTs, lack of clinical guidelines to inform insurance policies and guide efficient clinical decision-making, insufficient patient knowledge of treatment options, and concerns about the long-term risks of HE-DMT treatment. Available data suggest that the safety profile of HE-DMTs may not be too different than those of ME-DMTs, particularly early in the disease course [5]. Continued research may overcome these barriers and aid in the further clarification of clinical guidelines.

These results should be interpreted with caution due to several limitations. The number of participants included in the study was limited by data availability for each visit (varying routine assessments at varying time intervals) across the participating sites. The majority of pwMS in the study were from Cleveland Clinic sites, and data may not be generalizable to global cohorts. Additionally, while all eligible charts for pwMS in these centers were extracted, the eligibility criteria requiring the availability of ≥ 2 results for ≥ 1 of the PROs of interest may have introduced selection bias and demonstrate the challenges of obtaining consistent data in the real world. Moreover, data availability varied across the participating sites. Some variables were not collected routinely or at all in a subset of the eligible pwMS. Thus, the analyses were conducted among the subset of pwMS with available data for each of the study outcomes, who may have characteristics different from those of the general population; nonetheless, this heterogeneity reflects the realities of clinical practice and enhances the generalizability of the findings to real-world settings. There also may be some inherent bias in comparing patients who changed treatment with those who had remained on just one medication. We sought to reduce this bias through entropy analysis, but some residual confounding may still exist as a result of unobserved factors. Outcomes assessed by pwMS (i.e., PROs and patient-reported relapses) may further introduce response bias and inaccurate reflections of disease worsening. However, previous findings validated the use of the PDDS and showed strong correlations with physician-reported outcomes and the EDSS. Additionally, the relatively high annualized patient-reported relapse rate for both cohorts (approx. 0.8) may be because relapses were self-reported, rather than confirmed by EDSS or a clinician. Though self-reported relapses may not be able to distinguish pseudo-relapses from true relapses and PROs are distinct from clinical data, they still provide useful information regarding patient perspectives of disease and treatment outcomes, which is informative both for clinical trials and real-world studies.

The design of the study required that pwMS in the 2L+ ocrelizumab cohort had discontinued their 1L DMT; this may have selected for a 2L+ ocrelizumab cohort that included pwMS who failed their first DMT and, therefore, may have had more severe disease. However, recent research has shown that the most common reason for DMT switching is due to insurance formulary changes [37]. Furthermore, the findings of the current study show that the 1L ocrelizumab cohort had a higher proportion of pwMS with indicators of disease severity than the 2L+ ocrelizumab cohort, such as having PPMS (20.1% vs 11.7%), having higher baseline PDDS score (mean of 1.8 vs 1.4), and requiring the use of walking aids (17.5% vs 5.6%). Therefore, the 2L+ ocrelizumab cohort did not appear to be biased toward more severe or progressive MS pathology. Additionally, while important in DMT decision making, safety outcomes were out of scope for this study.

Conclusion

Compared with the group who received ocrelizumab as 2L+ therapy, pwMS who received ocrelizumab as 1L therapy experienced better outcomes as measured by several real-world outcomes, including clinical PROs (disease worsening and time to requiring a walking aid) and MRI measures (T2 lesion volume and number of new T2 lesions). This study contributes to the growing evidence supporting the clinical benefits of early treatment with HE-DMTs vs escalation therapy for MS.
