Functional uniqueness and stability of Gaussian priors in optimal L 1 L^{1} estimation
Leighton Barnes, Alex Dytso

Abstract
This paper studies the functional uniqueness and stability of Gaussian priors in optimal L 1 L^{1} estimation. While it is well known that the Gaussian prior uniquely induces linear conditional means under Gaussian noise, the analogous question for the conditional median (i.e., the optimal estimator under absolute-error loss) has only recently been settled. Building on the prior work establishing this uniqueness, we develop a quantitative stability theory that characterizes how approximate linearity of the optimal estimator constrains the prior distribution. For L 2 L^{2} loss, we derive explicit rates showing that near-linearity of the conditional mean implies proximity of the prior to the Gaussian in the Lévy metric. For L 1 L^{1} loss, we introduce a Hermite expansion framework and analyze the adjoint of the linearity-defining operator to show that the Gaussian remains the unique stable solution. Together, these results provide a more complete functional-analytic understanding of linearity and stability in Bayesian estimation under Gaussian noise.

Introduction

Bayesian estimation sits at the heart of statistics, signal processing, and information theory. Although nonlinear estimators inevitably arise in practice, linear estimators continue to play a central role — they are easy to compute, easier to analyze, and often serve as the baseline against more sophisticated methods. Consequently, characterizing when and why optimal estimators are linear has been a long standing line of inquiry.

In this paper, we consider the classical Gaussian observation model

Y=X+Z,\qquad Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),

(1)

where the noise
Z
is taken independently of the signal
X
, and we seek to estimate
X
from the noisy observation
Y
. The optimal estimator depends on the loss function. Under
L^{2}
loss, the optimal estimator is the conditional mean; under
L^{1}
loss, it is the conditional median. In the former case, a plethora of methods exist showing that Gaussian is the unique prior distribution on
X
that induces linearity; the interested reader is referred to
[
1
]
for the review of some of these methods.

In contrast, the corresponding question for
L^{1}
loss is more subtle. The conditional median does not enjoy orthogonality properties, lacks an explicit integral representation, and is not invariant under the same algebraic structures that render the conditional mean tractable. Until recently, it was not known whether the Gaussian prior is the unique distribution that yields a linear conditional median. In our prior work
[
1
]
, we resolved this question affirmatively, developing a Fourier–analytic and operator–theoretic approach to show that Gaussian priors are indeed the only ones inducing linear conditional medians under Gaussian noise. The proof required some results from the theory of tempered distributions, and critically used the non-negativity of the prior probability measure for
X
. Indeed, without non-negativity, uniqueness fails to hold, and there is an infinite-dimensional family of signed measures that induce linearity.

This paper takes the next step in this line of work by studying a stronger property:
stability
. Uniqueness tells us that only Gaussian priors lead to exactly linear estimators. Stability asks a more practical question: if the optimal estimator is
almost
linear, does the prior have to be
almost
Gaussian? Answering this tells us how robust the linearity phenomenon really is.

We study stability for both
L^{2}
and
L^{1}
losses. For the
L^{2}
case, we provide an explicit quantitative rate in terms of the Lévy metric, refining and strengthening classical results. For the
L^{1}
case, we introduce a Hermite expansion framework and analyze the adjoint of the linearity-defining operator to show that the Gaussian remains the unique stable solution.

1.1
Past Work

There is a long history of studying when Bayesian estimators are linear. In the Gaussian noise model with squared-error loss: the conditional mean is affine if and only if the prior is Gaussian. This fact appears in several forms in the literature and can be understood through conjugate exponential-family structure
[
2
]
, orthogonality arguments
[
3
]
, and analytic characterizations of Gaussians; see

In contrast, much less was known about linearity under absolute-error loss. The conditional median does not satisfy an orthogonality principle or other similar analytic properties. Only recently has it been established that, in the Gaussian setting, the Gaussian prior is also the only prior that yields a linear conditional median
[
1
]

Outside the Gaussian setting, the picture changes. For the Poisson noise model, gamma priors are known to induce affine conditional means under

3.1

We show that if the conditional expectation is close to a linear function in mean-squared error, then the input distribution must be close to a Gaussian in the Lévy metric, which is defined next.

An important property of the Lévy metric is that convergence of distributions in the Lévy metric is equivalent to weak convergence of distributions

Hermite expansion and the properties of the adjoint operator
T^{*}_{a}
instead of any results from the theory of tempered distributions.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\psi(y)=\mathsf{med}(X|Y=y)
and assume that the density of
X
satisfies
f(x)\leq M
for all
x
. For any
\varepsilon\geq 0
, if

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left(\frac{1-a}{2}y^{2}-\min_{x\in\left[ay-\sqrt{2a\varepsilon},ay+\sqrt{2a\varepsilon}\right]}\frac{(x-y)^{2}}{2}\right)\big|ay-\psi(y)\big|\mathrm{d}y\leq B<\infty

(62)

and

\displaystyle\left|\psi^{-1}(x)-\frac{x}{a}\right|\leq\Delta(x)\;,\quad\sum_{i}b_{i}\leq\varepsilon\;,

(63)

where
\Delta(x)=b_{i}
when
x\in[i,i+1)
,
then

|\langle f,H_{n}\rangle|\leq L_{n}\varepsilon,

(64)

for
n\geq 1
and
L_{n}=\mathcal{O}(n^{\frac{1}{4}})
.

Before getting to the proof, let us consider the assumptions of the theorem. The condition (
62
) assumes that
\psi(y)
and
ay
differ only by a function that decays like a Gaussian. In particular, if
\varepsilon
is small, the difference needs to decay like
\exp\left(-\frac{a(1-a)}{2}y^{2}\right)
. Fortunately, it can be checked that this assumption is indeed satisfied by any density
f(x)
that is given by
\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{a}{1-a}\right)+\xi(x)
where
\xi
is a compactly supported perturbation, and the set of such densities is dense in the ambient space of all square-integrable
f(x)
. The condition (
63
) is a slightly stronger and more uniform version of
\psi(y)
and
ay
being close in
L^{1}
distance. Note that

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\psi(y)-ay|\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\big|\psi^{-1}(x)-\frac{x}{a}\big|\mathrm{d}x

(65)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Delta(x)\mathrm{d}x=\sum_{i}b_{i}\leq\varepsilon\;.

(66)

The punchline of the theorem, that
|\langle f,H_{n}\rangle|
is small for
n\geq 1
, means that
f
must be approximately orthogonal to every Hermite function in the complete orthonormal sequence other than the Gaussian
H_{0}
, and in this sense
f
must be close to a Gaussian. In the corollary that follows, we will show how this can imply
f
is close to the density
\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{a}{1-a}\right)
in
L^{2}
distance provided that the Hermite coefficients of
f
decay fast enough.

The assumptions in Theorem
2
are much stronger than those in Theorem
1
. In particular, we assume the optimal estimator is pointwise very close to a linear function, instead of just close on average. However, the resulting stability is also stronger (even though it is in the style of “weak” convergence) in the sense that it is functional stability of the density
f
, instead of a statement about CDFs. Note that the techniques used in Theorem
1
have no hope of working unmodified in the
L^{1}
case, since they do not, in a fundamental way, use the non-negativity of
f
, and stability fails when
f
can be negative. This necessitated the new assumptions and techniques in the following proof.

Proof.

Consider the orthonormal Hermite function expansion

f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{i}H_{i}(x)\;.

(67)

Our goal is to show that under the assumptions of the theorem, all
c_{i}
coefficients must be small except for
c_{0}
which corresponds to the Gaussian
H_{0}(x)
.

Defining
\widetilde{f}(x)=e^{\frac{x^{2}(1-a)}{2a}}f(x)
, we will first show that

\displaystyle\int_{z-0.5}^{z+0.5}\widetilde{f}(ax)dx\leq C_{0}

(68)

for any
z
and some constant
C_{0}
. To this end, note that according to Proposition
1
the conditional median
\psi(y)
satisfies

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\mathsf{sign}(x-\psi(y))\exp\left(-\frac{(x-y)^{2}}{2}\right)\mathrm{d}x=0

(69)

by definition. Then using the above display, (
21
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle T_{a}[f](y)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\mathsf{sign}(x-ay)\exp\left(-\frac{(x-y)^{2}}{2}\right)\mathrm{d}x

(70)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\big[\mathsf{sign}(x-ay)-\mathsf{sign}(x-\psi(y))\big]\exp\left(-\frac{(x-y)^{2}}{2}\right)\mathrm{d}x\;

(71)

Under the assumptions of the theorem,

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|ay-\psi(y)|\mathrm{d}y\leq\varepsilon\;,

(72)

and since
\psi
is nondecreasing, as shown in Lemma
1
, this implies

\sup_{y}|ay-\psi(y)|\leq\sqrt{2a\varepsilon}\;.

(73)

Using (
71
), (
73
), and
f(x)\leq M
,

\displaystyle\big|T_{a}[f](y)\big|

\displaystyle\leq 2M\big|ay-\psi(y)\big|\exp\left(-\min_{x\in\left[ay-\sqrt{2a\varepsilon},ay+\sqrt{2a\varepsilon}\right]}\frac{(x-y)^{2}}{2}\right)\;.

(74)

The integral equation in (
21
) can be massaged into a convolution operator as was done in
[
1
, Prop. 5]
,

\displaystyle e^{\frac{(1-a)y^{2}}{2}}T_{a}[f](y)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widetilde{f}(ax)\mathsf{sign}(x-y)\exp\left(-\frac{a(x-y)^{2}}{2}\right)\mathrm{d}x

(75)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widetilde{f}(ax)g^{\prime}(y-x)\mathrm{d}x

(76)

for a kernel
g^{\prime}
with an antiderivative
g
. Following (
76
) and using dominated convergence to pull the derivative outside of the integral (and then integrating),

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widetilde{f}(ax)g(y-x)dx\leq C_{1}+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{\frac{(1-a)y^{2}}{2}}\big|T_{a}[f](y)\big|\mathrm{d}y\;.

(77)

Then using (
74
) along with the assumption (
62
),

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widetilde{f}(ax)g(y-x)dx\leq C_{2}\;.

(78)

Using the trick from
[
1
]
that

g(x)\geq c1_{[-0.5,0.5]}(x)

(79)

where
c>0
and
1_{[-0.5,0.5]}
is the indicator function for the interval
[-0.5,0.5]
, we have

\int_{y-0.5}^{y+0.5}\widetilde{f}(ax)\mathrm{d}x\leq C_{0}

(80)

for all
y
as desired.

Next, we use the growth estimate from (
80
) to show that

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\big|e^{\frac{(1-a)y^{2}}{2}}T_{a}f(y)\big|\mathrm{d}y\leq C_{3}\varepsilon\;.

(81)

For this, following (
75
) and using (
69
),

\displaystyle e^{\frac{(1-a)y^{2}}{2}}T_{a}[f](y)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widetilde{f}(ax)\left[\mathsf{sign}(ax-ay)-\mathsf{sign}(ax-\psi(y))\right]\exp\left(-\frac{a(x-y)^{2}}{2}\right)\mathrm{d}x

(82)

so that

\displaystyle\big|e^{\frac{(1-a)y^{2}}{2}}T_{a}[f](y)\big|\leq 2\left|\int_{ay}^{\psi(y)}\widetilde{f}(ax)\mathrm{d}x\right|

(83)

and

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\big|e^{\frac{(1-a)y^{2}}{2}}T_{a}[f](y)\big|\mathrm{d}y\leq 2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left|\int_{ay}^{\psi(y)}\widetilde{f}(ax)\mathrm{d}x\right|\mathrm{d}y\;.

(84)

We can rewrite (
84
) as

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\big|e^{\frac{(1-a)y^{2}}{2}}T_{a}[f](y)\big|\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left|\int_{\frac{x}{a}}^{\psi^{-1}(x)}\mathrm{d}y\right|\widetilde{f}(ax)\mathrm{d}x

(85)

\displaystyle=2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left|\psi^{-1}(x)-\frac{x}{a}\right|\widetilde{f}(ax)\mathrm{d}x

(86)

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Delta(x)\widetilde{f}(ax)\mathrm{d}x

(87)

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{0}\sum_{i}b_{i}\;.

(88)

The inequality (
87
) uses the assumption (
63
), and the final inequality (
88
) uses the growth estimate (
80
). We have therefore shown that the assumptions

\left|\psi^{-1}(x)-\frac{x}{a}\right|\leq\Delta(x)\;,\quad\sum_{i}b_{i}\leq\varepsilon\;,

(89)

where
\Delta(x)=b_{i}
when
x\in[i,i+1)
, imply

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\big|e^{\frac{(1-a)y^{2}}{2}}T_{a}[f](y)\big|\mathrm{d}y\leq C_{3}\varepsilon\;.

(90)

In Proposition
5
in Appendix
A
, we show how to construct functions
\varphi_{n}
for
n\geq 1
such that
\varphi_{n}
is mapped by the adjoint of
T_{a}
to
H_{n}
. Letting
\phi_{0}(y)=e^{-\frac{(1-a)y^{2}}{2}}
, these
\varphi_{n}
have the nice property that
\|\phi_{0}\varphi_{n}\|_{2}
are finite. We have:

\displaystyle\big|\langle T_{a}f,\varphi_{n}\rangle\big|=\big|\langle\phi_{0}^{-1}T_{a}f,\phi_{0}\varphi_{n}\rangle\big|\leq C_{3}\varepsilon\|\phi_{0}\varphi_{n}\|_{\infty}\leq C_{3}\varepsilon\|\mathcal{F}[\phi_{0}\varphi_{n}]\|_{1}

(91)

for
n\geq 1
. Furthermore, Proposition
5
also shows that

\|\mathcal{F}[\phi_{0}\varphi_{n}]\|_{1}=\mathcal{O}(n^{\frac{1}{4}})

(92)

for some
d<1
. Using Proposition
6
below to justify the interchange of limits,

\langle T_{a}f,\varphi_{n}\rangle=\langle f,T_{a}^{*}\varphi_{n}\rangle=\langle f,H_{n}\rangle=c_{n}\;.

(93)

Putting this all together,

|c_{n}|=|\langle f,H_{n}\rangle|\leq L_{n}\varepsilon

(94)

for
n\geq 1
where
L_{n}=\mathcal{O}(n^{\frac{1}{4}})
.

∎

Corollary 1
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2
and
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, suppose that the Hermite coefficients
c_{n}
additionally satisfy

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|c_{n}|n^{\frac{1}{4}}<\infty\;.

(95)

Then,

\left\|f-c_{0}H_{0}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq C\varepsilon\;.

(96)

Proof.

We have

\left\|f(x)-c_{0}H_{0}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n}^{2}\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|c_{n}|L_{n}\varepsilon\leq C\varepsilon\;.

(97)

∎

Fast decay of Hermite coefficients occurs for many functions of interest, and in particular it is known that the coefficients
c_{n}
will decay exponentially if and only if both
f
and its Fourier transform have Gaussian decay (with any variance)
[
12
]
.

4
Conclusion

This work establishes a functional framework that links the approximate linearity of Bayesian estimators to the near-Gaussianity of the underlying prior. For the
L^{2}
loss, we obtained an explicit quantitative rate in the Lévy metric, while for the
L^{1}
loss we introduced a Hermite–analytic method establishing that the Gaussian the unique stable solution. Several refinements remain open, particularly for the
L^{1}
case such as relaxing the technical decay assumptions. For the
L^{2}
case it would also be interesting to find if the current order in terms of
\epsilon
is tight.
