Predictive factors for a successful external cephalic version: an observational study of four years’ experience in China
Authors: Jiao Yi, Zhiguo Tang, Xianxia Chen, Xiaobao Zhang, Bin Zhang, Dehong Liu, Xiumei Wu, Chenmin Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo identify predictive factors affecting the outcome of external cephalic version (ECV).Materials and methodsThe retrospective study included 222 women with singleton gestations who underwent ECV for breech presentation and transverse lie between February 2019 and July 2024. Based on the outcome of ECV, the participants were divided into two groups (successful ECV group and unsuccessful ECV group). Data included patient characteristics and pregnancy parameters were analyzed. The primary outcome was the success of ECV. In order to determine the predictive factors of ECV success, we conducted a multivariable logistic regression analysis. The adjusted odds ratio and corresponding 95% confidence interval were calculated, and a receiver operating characteristic curve was used to evaluate the highest discriminatory capacity of variables for the ECV success.ResultsThe overall ECV success rate was 62.6%. No differences between adverse neonatal outcomes were found between the two groups. After adjusting for variables including maternal age, pre-pregnancy BMI, gravidity, parity, gestational age at ECV, fetal weight of previous pregnancy, breech type, transverse, nuchal cord, placental location, anesthesia, amniotic fluid index, duration of procedure, interval of ECV-to-delivery, and fetal weight, multivariate logistic regression analysis found that amniotic fluid index and gestational age at ECV had a positive relation with the success of ECV; Based on the receiver operating characteristic curve, the optimal cutoff value of amniotic fluid index was 144 mm, the sensitivity was 83.5%, and the specificity was 85.5%. A cutoff value of gestational age at ECV of 37 + 5 weeks yielded a sensitivity and specificity of 83.5% and 85.5%, respectively, for successful ECV.ConclusionsECV seems to be a successful, safe procedure with a high success rate. Amniotic fluid index and gestational age at ECV are the most important discriminatory indicators predictive of ECV success or failure.

Introduction

Breech presentation complicates approximately 3–4% of pregnancies at term [1], is responsible for antenatal stillbirth and a number of individual obstetric risk factors for adverse perinatal outcomes, such as oligohydramnios, fetal growth restriction, and congenital anomalies [2]. Meanwhile, breech presentation is one among the commonest indications of cesarean section worldwide [3]. This is cause for concern because cesarean section is the most important contributing factors to short and long-term consequences for maternal and neonatal health [4], and is also responsible for the increase in maternal mortality related to childbirth [5]. As such, efforts are being directed toward the reduction of cesarean section, which is within the top priorities in clinical practice.

Currently, the available managements for breech presentation include breech vaginal delivery, cesarean section and external cephalic version (ECV). Breech vaginal delivery is not considered by majority of pregnant women due to increased risk of neonatal morbidity and mortality. In addition, only women with a lower maternal BMI at the time of birth as well as neonatal birth weight ≤3800 g was associated with a higher rate of successful vaginal birth [6]. Furthermore, there was good evidence confirmed that planned caesarean section was better than planned vaginal birth for the term fetus in the breech presentation [7]. Therefore, ECV appeared to be a valuable management technique, and if successful, ECV would avoid the rate of cesarean sections by approximately a half [8], and in turn, obviating maternal and fetal morbidity associated with cesarean delivery. For these reasons, the main obstetrics societies such as the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) [9], the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (RCOG) [10], as well as the French national college of obstetricians and gynecologists (CNGOF) [11] recommend ECV as the first-choice for breech presentation pregnancy to reduce the rising cesarean rates.

Unfortunately, ECV is frequently not routinely performed, and women who declined the procedure were more likely to be nulliparous [12]. It has been claimed that the underlying reasons for low acceptance rate might be attributed to concerns about the uncertainty of safety and efficacy of ECV [13]. Meanwhile, ECV can have adverse emotional and economic consequences when unsuccessful [14]. As described by authors, the ability to predict an estimated outcome of ECV attempt may improve the rates of patient consent and prevent unpleasant procedures performed on women with lower likelihood of success. Therefore, the aim of this study was to determine the factors associated with successful ECV attempts based on objective patient characteristics and ultrasound parameters, which could be obtained quickly and easily through clinic interview or with routine clinical practice. The secondary aim was to assess outcomes of women who have successful ECV compared with those who do not. We believe these factors will be useful tool for providing a personalized probability of success for pregnant women who initially reject ECV and facilitating decision-making.

Materials and methods

Study design

This retrospective cohort study was performed at seven obstetrics departments affiliated to the Hefei Maternal and Child health care Hospital, Hefei, China between February 2019 and July 2024, it is the largest tertiary obstetrics and gynecology hospital in Anhui province, with 20,000 deliveries on average per year. Because of the retrospective design of the study, the need to obtain informed consent from eligible women was waived by the institutional ethics committee of Hefei Maternal and Child health Care Hospital. This retrospective research was approved by the institutional ethics committee of Hefei Maternal and Child health Care Hospital (Approval number: YYLL20240424-AYDXJXM-LL-03-1.0) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

The inclusion criteria were women with singleton pregnancies with pregnancy ≥36 weeks gestation and intact membranes in a breech presentation without contraindications to ECV procedure (such as onset of active labor, rupture of membranes, vaginal bleeding, severe preeclampsia or eclampsia, multiple gestation, previous cesarean section, abnormal cardiotocography and growth restriction associated with an abnormal umbilical artery Doppler index) [15]. In addition, women with a fetus in the transverse presentation were not excluded from the study.

The exclusion criteria were multiple pregnancies, a previous cesarean delivery (cesarean section delivery during the previous pregnancy), uterine anomalies (uterus subseptus, uterus septus, uterus bicornis, uterus unicornis) [16], fetal malformation (such as congenital malformations and deformations of the musculoskeletal, cardiovascular, and urogenital system) [17], or intrauterine fetal death, coagulation disorders, severe pre-eclampsia or eclampsia, fetal growth restriction associated with an abnormal umbilical artery Doppler index, non-reassuring fetal status before ECV, as well as pregnancies complicated by placenta previa and antepartum hemorrhage. Women presenting with premature rupture of membranes were likewise excluded.

Pregnancy age was determined by fetal crown-rump measured by the first trimester ultrasound.

Demographic information and obstetrical characteristics were extracted from the medical files, including: maternal age, pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI), gravidity, parity, gestational weeks at ECV, fetal weight of previous pregnancy, amniotic fluid index, placental location (anterior, posterior or other types), type of breech presentation (frank, complete, incomplete or transverse lie), presence of cord neck (1 loop, 2 loops or no nuchal cord), the use of anesthesia, and the duration of procedure.

The maternal and neonatal outcomes were collected from the labor electronic records, including delivery gestational age, time interval from ECV to delivery, cases of spontaneous reversion after successful attempts, cases of spontaneous version to cephalic position after failed attempts, switching to cesarean section, and indications for cesarean section. Neonatal outcomes, including neonatal asphyxia (5 min Apgar score of < 7), neonatal weight, and neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission were also recorded.

All the above-mentioned data were collected from the electronic medical records.

Clinical procedures

ECV was performed by experienced consultants (two associate chief physicians who performed the procedure with at least 7 years’ experience by each physician) in accordance with the institution’s standardized protocol (beginning at 36 weeks of gestation). All eligible women were given informed consent regarding the advantages, disadvantages, and expected neonatal and maternal complications of an ECV. Fetal non-stress test for assessment of fetal status and ultrasound examination of the type of breech, fetal biometry, amniotic fluid, as well as placental position were routinely performed just prior to ECV attempt. Thirty minutes before starting an ECV procedure, a tocolytic agent (terbutaline or ritodrine) was used intravenously till procedure completion. Regional anesthesia was not used routinely unless maternal request (spinal or epidural anesthesia). Thereafter, under ultrasound guidance, the operator moved the baby in the forward direction first, if this attempt was failed, a backward roll can be tried. An ECV was considered successful if fetal vertex presentation was achieved and confirmed by ultrasound immediately following the ECV procedure. ECV failure was defined as a maximum of three unsuccessful attempts. For unsuccessful cases, based on patient choice women were either scheduled for cesarean delivery or discharged for routine obstetric care till the 39th week for selective cesarean section (women who preferred for carrying out primary cesarean section and declined to vaginal birth) if a breech presentation or transverse lies.

The type, dosage, and duration of prophylactic tocolytic therapy were at the discretion of the clinical judgement of the physician. In case of cardiovascular disease or renal function damage, terbutaline was considered to be contraindicated. For women who complicated with heart disease, hyperthyroidism, tachycardia or uncontrolled diabetes, ritodrine was prohibited. We chose intravenous terbutaline 0.25 mg diluted by 100 ml saline solution for its rapid onset. The initial dosage was 2–5 ug/min, and increased 2.5 ug/min every 20 min (maximum 17.5 ug/min) until uterine contraction stopped, and then continued for no more than 48 h. Regarding ritodrine, diluting 100 mg of ritodrine in 500 ml of 5% glucose solution, starting at 50 ug/min and increasing by 50 ug/min every 10 min until contractions stop.

For successful ECV procedure, if maternal and fetal status were reassuring, the women were discharged after 24 h for routine antenatal care until delivery.

Indications for emergency cesarean section: non-reassuring fetal status (prolonged deceleration does not recover within five minutes), umbilical cord prolapse, significant vaginal bleeding, and placental abruption.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were performed by SPSS 13.0 statistical packages (SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA). Categorical variables were expressed by percentage. Continuous variables were expressed by mean and standard deviation (SD). Comparisons of two groups were analyzed with T-test for continuous variables with normally distributed and the Chi-square test for categorical variables as appropriate. For continuous variables with non-normal distribution, the Wilcoxon-Rank-Sum-Test was selected. A multivariable analysis was performed using a logistic regression model to identify the association between ECV outcome and various parameters. Based on literature and clinical reasoning, the variables selected in multivariate analysis including: maternal age, pre-pregnancy BMI, gravidity, parity, gestational age at ECV, fetal weight of previous pregnancy, type of breech presentation (frank, complete, incomplete or transverse lie), presence of nuchal cord, placental location, the use of anesthesia, amniotic fluid index, the duration of procedure, time interval from ECV to delivery, and neonatal weight [8,18–21]. The adjusted odds ratio (AOR) as well as its 95% confidence interval (CI) were utilized to quantify this association. A receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was constructed to evaluate the predictive ability of amniotic fluid index and gestational age at ECV for the ECV success. A p < 0.05 was used only for statistical significance.

Results

During the study period, 222 cases met the inclusion criteria, and 139 women underwent successful ECV. Hence, the overall ECV procedure success rate in this study was 62.6% (139/222). Furthermore, among the successful ECV procedures, nearly 86.3% (120/139) of women delivered vaginally.

Of the cases of successful ECV, 55.8% (67/120) had spontaneous vaginal delivery, and 44.2% (53/120) underwent induction of labor. The rate for both elective and emergency cesarean after a successful ECV was 13.7% (19/139). The most frequent indication for cesarean birth was fetal distress (7/19, 5 cases due to intrapartum fetal distress and 2 cases due to non-reassuring fetal status after ECV attempts), other indications included dystocia (2 cases), failed labor induction (4 cases), significant vaginal bleeding after ECV (2 cases), maternal request (2 cases), and breech presentation (2 cases, revision to breech presentation before labor).

There was no spontaneous version to cephalic presentation after failed ECV procedures.

One newborn in the successful ECV group was admitted to the NICU, primarily owing to mild asphyxia. One neonatal NICU admission has been documented in the unsuccessful ECV group mainly due to neonatal grunting with normal Apgar scores.

Two groups were categorized according to the outcome of ECV, group 1 (successful ECV group, 139 cases), and group 2 (unsuccessful ECV group, 83 cases).

No statistically significant differences were found in terms of maternal age, pre-pregnancy BMI between groups (p = 0.727 and 0.753, respectively). Discussion

The major finding was that the positive association between amniotic fluid index, gestational age at ECV and the possibility of successful ECV. Another important finding in our study was that the sensitivity of 83.5% and the specificity of 85.5% with an AUC of 0.922 were found for amniotic fluid index, with the optimum cut-off value was 144 mm. Additionally, when the cut-off value was set at 37 + 5 weeks, the gestational age at ECV was found to have 83.5% sensitivity and 85.5% specificity for predicting a successful ECV (AUC 0.922).

In recent years, concerns regarding factors of success of ECV have continually increased. Indeed, the uncertainty of success rates in addition to several sequalae of ECV have led many women to refuse an ECV attempt. Instead of offering an ECV to the general population, it might be preferable to conduct it to a selected population based on objective clinical characteristics which is a critical component to prevent the unfavorable performance with minimal chance for success. Nuchal cord, a relatively common finding at delivery, was associated with an increased risk of hypercarbia and acidemia [22]. As an obstetric phenomenon that is of clinical relevance, it will remain an open question as to whether nuchal cord is a unfavorable variables leading to ECV failure. Of note, data available on this intervention for pregnant women with nuchal cord were scarce. Only one study has addressed the question, showing that nuchal cords were associated with a decreased rate of successful ECV and increased incidence of fetal heart rate decelerations during the procedure [23]. In the current study, we did not observe any difference in the nuchal cords rate, nor provide evidence for an association between nuchal cords and estimated chance of a successful ECV. It is likely that several factors may contribute to the differences in conclusion between studies, including patient characteristics, procedural techniques and smaller populations. It is, therefore, important to be aware that future studies attempting to establish the determinants associated with ECV success should consider nuchal cords.

Of note, another surprising finding in our study was the absence of association between parity and successful ECVs. Currently, parity is part of these important prognostic parameters under discussion. Numerous studies have investigated a positive association between parity and the success of ECV [24–26]. Whereas other recent two reports indicated contradicting results. In a retrospective study including 204 patients, authors reported that ECV success rate did not improve via multiparity [27]. On a prospective cohort study with retrospective data acquisition, authors also ruled out parity as an independent factor influencing the success of ECV [28]. Our findings are similar to the results of the two above-mentioned studies. Although there was statistically significant difference in the rate of multiparous between the two groups, with higher odds of multiparous were noted in successful ECV group. Regrettably, using multiple logistic regression analysis, the correlation between parity and successful ECV was negligible which means that this dependency was not so strong as to affect the ECV success rate. A possible explanation for this unique finding may be attributable to the differences in patient characteristics and low sample size. Our study highlights additional data are needed to further identify the impact of parity for the chance of successful ECV.

In our study, the major finding was the positive association between amniotic fluid index and the possibility of successful ECV, and the sensitivity of 83.5% as well as the specificity of 85.5% with an optimal cutoff value of 144 mm were found for amniotic fluid index. Theoretically, increased amount of amniotic fluid could be significantly associated with higher rates of successful ECV. A possible explanation can be due to bigger spaces in the uterus of these women with adequate amniotic fluid index, which is important to make the procedure simpler and easier. Numerous studies have investigated the role of amniotic fluid index in successful ECV attempts. Three retrospective cohort studies found that adequate amniotic fluid played an important prognostic role in successful ECV [24,26,29]. However, it is important to note that for all of these mentioned studies, no definite threshold for amniotic fluid index was identified, through which allowed the prediction of individual likelihood of success with sufficient accuracy. To our knowledge, only limited data addressed the issue of amniotic fluid index cutoff value for successful ECV. A study reported significantly lower success rates with a maximum vertical pocket of < 4 centimeters [30]. Additionally, other study indicated that women with amniotic fluid index below 10 centimeters have less chances of success in ECV [31]. The findings of this research were in agreement with previous reports, demonstrating that amniotic fluid index was an independent determinant of successful ECV, and our data has shown good discrimination values with an AUC of 0.922 to classify an individual with an ECV success or failure.

Another important finding in our study was a strong positive association between gestational age at ECV and successful ECV procedures, and the optimal cutoff value of gestational age at ECV was 37 + 5 weeks, with a sensitivity of 83.5%, a specificity of 85.5%, respectively. The ideal week of gestation for ECV has been evaluated in numerous studies, with important scientific committees recommended that initial ECV should be performed after the 37th week of pregnancy [9,10,32]. We believe that possible reasons for the recommendation may be attributable to high probability of spontaneous version to cephalic position, low likelihood of reversion to breech presentation after ECV, and avoiding the risk of premature birth [33]. Another possible explanation may be due to the fact that initiating ECV before term had no favorable impact on maternal and neonatal outcomes [33,34]. Notably, little information is available regarding the appropriate time-point to carry out ECV, as recognizing this factor is useful to counsel women and improve the rates of successful ECV. In the current cohort, we added further weight to the accumulating body of existing literature that reported a significant correlation between gestational age at ECV and successful ECV attempts, with promising AUC results of 0.922 to show better outcomes in women attempting ECV at 37 + 5 weeks.

Recent years, debates focusing on the safety of ECV persist. Complications that result from the procedure are of clinical relevance, which is an important consideration in patient-centered counseling and shared decision making about whether to proceed with ECV. Reports have pointed out that ECV was a safe procedure [18,35], didn’t increase the risk of unfavorable obstetric outcomes [36]. In this study, adverse events after ECV were consisted of umbilical cord prolapse (0.4%, 1/222), rupture of membranes (0.4%, 1/222) and placental abruption (0.4%, 1/222). Furthermore, fetal distress and vaginal bleeding were seen in seven cases (3.2%, 7/222) and four cases (1.8, 4/222), respectively, and no fetal deaths were found. Moreover, all of these cases had good birth outcomes. This was consistent with a recently published study, confirming that the complications of ECV included non-reassuring fetal status (4.8%, n = 55), placental abruption (1.6%, n = 18), spontaneous rupture of membranes (0.6%, n = 7), and cord prolapse (0.1%, n = 1) [37]. In contrast, a study demonstrated that the associated complications rate of ECV was <1% [38]. However, in that study, the complications were not specifically stated, it was therefore difficult to make comparisons with us. Meanwhile, there was some evidence assessed neonatal outcomes based on NICU admission, Apgar scores, the need for respiratory support, and so on, suggesting that ECV procedure itself had no effect on neonatal status [27,39], which was in agreement with that of our study. Additionally, in comparison to those with failed ECV attempts, women following a successful ECV had increased time interval of ECV-to-delivery, larger gestational week at delivery and higher birth weight. These findings were consistent with previous studies that were attempted at analyzing the outcomes of referrals for ECV [19,40]. Therefore, we believe our results provide supportive evidence that ECV is an reliable, valuable strategy in this unique patient cohort.

The main limitation of our study was the retrospective nature, the generalizability of our findings to all women with breech presentation or transverse lies may be insufficient. Second, during the study period, only 222 women underwent ECV. However, our center is the largest maternity department in local, with approximately 20,000 births per year, so the small sample size thereby leading to potential selection bias. Finally, the BMI was restricted to pre-pregnancy, the weight gain of women who wish to receive ECV could not be determined. However, this study also included a number of strengths. A main strength was that being conducted in a single hospital in a population with similar characteristics, as it lowers the risk of differences in hospital practices, such as obstetricians’ experience and protocol regarding ECV, which may be potentially affected clinical outcomes in different centers. Furthermore, we followed up these participants till deliveries, enabling us to gather robust and complete data regarding certain factors which was related to ECV success, giving us with the opportunity to better explore the clinically relevant predictors for successful ECV.

In conclusion, it is reassuring that ECV is a safe and effective procedure for correcting the fetal malpresentation. Our data highlight the importance of evaluating amounts of amniotic fluid and the gestational age at which ECV is performed, which are strongly correlated with the chances of success. In addition, we demonstrate that optimum amniotic fluid index and gestational age at ECV for a successful ECV are 144 mm and 37 + 5 weeks, respectively. This is a particularly critical component to predicting probability of procedure success and assisting in decision making about whether to undergo the ECV procedure.
