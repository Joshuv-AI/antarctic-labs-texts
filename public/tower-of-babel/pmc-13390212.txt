Psychometric properties of the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) in the Dominican Republic
Authors: Zoilo Emilio García-Batista, Kiero Guerra-Peña, Adriana Alvarez-Hernandez, Antonio Cano-Vindel, Roger Muñoz-Navarro, Luciana Sofía Moretti, Jairo Espinal-Martínez, Leonardo A Medrano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) is one of the most widely used instruments worldwide for screening and quantifying the severity of depressive symptoms. Its brevity, public-domain availability, and strong empirical support have facilitated its broad adoption across clinical and research settings. However, despite its extensive international use, evidence regarding its psychometric performance in the Dominican Republic remains limited. The absence of locally validated instruments entails critical challenges, including reduced diagnostic accuracy, suboptimal assessment of symptom severity, and constraints on clinical decision-making and context-sensitive mental health research. Establishing the validity and reliability of the PHQ-9 in this context is therefore essential to support both applied screening efforts and epidemiological investigations. The present study examined the internal structure, reliability, and convergent validity evidence of the PHQ-9 in a Dominican adult sample.MethodsA total of 914 adults participated in the study. Dimensionality was evaluated using parallel analysis based on polychoric correlations and exploratory factor analysis with principal axis factoring. Construct validity evidence was further examined through confirmatory factor analysis using the diagonally weighted least squares estimator. Internal consistency was assessed using McDonald’s omega and Cronbach’s alpha coefficients. Convergent validity evidence was estimated through Spearman correlations between PHQ-9 and Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) scores.ResultsFindings supported a robust unidimensional structure. Although parallel analysis based on polychoric correlations suggested more than one factor, inspection of the eigenvalue structure, principal axis factor loadings, and confirmatory factor analysis supported retention of a one-factor solution. Reliability indices indicated high internal consistency, with both omega and alpha coefficients exceeding .80. PHQ-9 scores showed strong positive associations with GAD-7 scores, providing convergent validity evidence consistent with the well-documented overlap between depressive and anxiety symptomatology.ConclusionsThe PHQ-9 demonstrates solid psychometric performance in Dominican adults, supporting its use as a reliable screening measure of depressive symptom severity in both research and applied settings. These findings extend cross-cultural validation evidence to a previously underexamined Caribbean context. Future studies incorporating structured clinical interviews and depression-specific gold-standard measures are warranted to further examine diagnostic validity, measurement invariance, and optimal cut-off scores.Keywords: PHQ-9, Depression, Psychometric validation, Dominican Republic, Mental health

Introduction

Depression is a debilitating mental disorder characterized by persistent sadness, hopelessness, loss of energy, and diminished interest or pleasure in daily activities [2]. It is among the most prevalent mental health conditions worldwide and constitutes a leading contributor to years lived with disability [44]. Recent global estimates indicate that more than 280 million people are affected by depressive disorders, reflecting a substantial and growing public health burden [45]. Beyond its high prevalence, depression exerts a profound impact on quality of life, psychosocial functioning, and occupational performance, and is frequently associated with physical and mental comorbidities, including cardiovascular disease, metabolic disorders, anxiety conditions, and increased mortality risk [18, 38].

In Latin America and the Caribbean, depression represents a major component of the regional mental health burden and ranks among the leading causes of disability [32]. Epidemiological surveillance and health system assessments have consistently documented substantial treatment gaps, with a large proportion of individuals affected by depressive disorders receiving no formal mental health care. Within the Dominican Republic, available epidemiological estimates derived from the Global Burden of Disease Study indicate that depressive disorders are among the principal contributors to years lived with disability, particularly among women and working-age adults [24]. National mental health system profiles further highlight structural constraints, including limited availability of specialized services, concentration of psychiatric care in urban centers, and restricted community-based mental health coverage [46].

In this context, psychometric research has emerged as an essential component in the study and treatment of depression. Accurate assessment of this disorder is not only essential for proper clinical diagnosis but also provides a solid basis for scientific research and the implementation of effective therapeutic interventions. Psychometric measurement of depression has become a central pillar in clinical research and clinical practice, allowing for a deeper understanding and more accurate assessment of the nature and severity of this disorder [14, 15].

Screening instruments for the assessment of depressive symptomatology have become highly relevant tools in public mental health, as they facilitate early detection and help reduce underdiagnosis. Delays in treatment initiation are consistently associated with poorer clinical course and functional outcomes. Despite the recognized importance of early identification, epidemiological evidence suggests that a substantial proportion of depression cases remain undetected in routine care settings. For instance, a systematic review and meta-analysis conducted across low- and middle-income countries reported that more than 50% of depression cases go unrecognized in primary care contexts [11]. In this regard, the availability of psychometrically sound screening instruments constitutes a critical prerequisite for accurate detection and clinical decision-making [7, 40].

One of the most widely used screening tests worldwide is the 9-item version of the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9). Each of the items corresponds to the diagnostic criteria of the fourth edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM). They are assessed on a scale of 0 to 3 (0 = not at all; 1 = several days; 2 = more than half the days; 3 = nearly every day). The PHQ-9 has the advantage of being a short, self-administered, easy-to-fill questionnaire that serves as a reliable screening and severity-assessment tool for depressive symptomatology [29, 40].

According to Kroenke [27], the PHQ-9 is the most widely used depression screening scale worldwide. Its international adoption is attributable to several factors. First, its nine items map directly onto the DSM diagnostic criteria for depressive disorders, allowing the instrument to function both as a screening tool and as a measure of symptom severity. In screening contexts, the diagnostic performance of the PHQ-9 has been evaluated through sensitivity and specificity indices (respectively reflecting the instrument’s ability to correctly identify true cases and true non-cases of depression). Finally, unlike several proprietary depression scales, the PHQ-9 is in the public domain and freely available for both clinical and research use.

The PHQ-9 has been translated, adapted, and psychometrically validated across a wide range of cultural, clinical, and community contexts. Validation studies have been conducted in primary care settings, where the instrument has demonstrated strong screening accuracy for detecting probable depressive disorders [27, 42]. Similarly, its psychometric properties have been examined in psychiatric outpatient populations and hospital-based samples, confirming its utility for both diagnostic screening and severity assessment [40].

Beyond clinical settings, the PHQ-9 has also been validated in general population samples and community-based cohorts, supporting its use in epidemiological and public health research [28]. Additional studies have evaluated the scale in specific subpopulations, including university students, individuals with chronic medical conditions, and post-stroke patients, demonstrating adequate reliability and structural validity across diverse health conditions and developmental stages [9, 29].

Cross-cultural research further supports the robustness of the PHQ-9, with validated versions available across multiple languages and regions, including Latin America, Europe, Asia, and Africa. These studies consistently report satisfactory internal consistency, predominantly unidimensional or bifactorial structures, and theoretically coherent associations with related constructs such as anxiety, functional impairment, and quality of life [3, 7, 19].

Within this body of evidence, particular attention should be given to the large-scale meta-analytic investigation conducted by Bianchi et al. [5], which represents the most comprehensive integrative psychometric analysis of the PHQ-9 to date. This study synthesized data from 29 independent samples across seven countries and five languages, yielding a pooled sample of 58,272 participants (63% female,M age = 43, SD age = 13). Results supported the unidimensional structure of the PHQ-9 in both pooled and subgroup analyses, with strong factor loadings (0.72–0.89) and high internal consistency (ω > 0.90). Importantly, no meaningful structural differences were observed across sex, age, or language groups. Taken together, these findings provide strong evidence of the scale’s structural stability and cross-national applicability.

Nevertheless, psychometric properties are inherently population-dependent and require empirical verification within each specific cultural and epidemiological context. Even widely used instruments may exhibit variations in factor structure, item functioning, or score distributions depending on linguistic adaptation, clinical composition, or sociocultural influences [3, 7, 19, 40]. Moreover, contemporary research in depression measurement suggests that construct representation and symptom configuration may vary across contexts due to differences in distress expression, somatic emphasis, and response styles [14]. Accordingly, local validation studies remain methodologically necessary to confirm dimensionality, reliability, and score functioning within specific populations.

Despite the extensive empirical support and global dissemination of the PHQ-9, no studies to date have examined its psychometric performance in the Dominican Republic. Consequently, it remains unclear whether its structural and reliability properties replicate in this context or whether contextual factors may influence its measurement functioning [7]. The present study therefore aimed to: (a) examine the internal structure of the PHQ-9 in a Dominican adult sample, (b) estimate internal consistency reliability; and (c) provide validity evidence based on relations to external variables by examining the association between PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores. By establishing these foundational psychometric properties, this study seeks to extend the cross-cultural validation literature and provide empirical support for the interpretability and use of PHQ-9 scores in Dominican research and applied settings.

Methodology

Participants and procedures

Participants were recruited using a non-probability convenience sampling strategy based on voluntary response. Recruitment was conducted through online dissemination, including social media platforms and institutional and community networks, with the objective of reaching adult residents across different regions of the Dominican Republic. This strategy was intended to maximize sociodemographic heterogeneity; however, the resulting sample should not be interpreted as statistically representative of the national population. Data were collected through a self-administered online survey. Eligibility criteria required participants to: (a) be 18 years of age or older, (b) hold Dominican nationality and currently reside in the Dominican Republic, and (c) provide informed consent prior to participation. The survey link was publicly accessible, participation was anonymous, and no financial incentives were offered.

Instruments

Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) evaluates depressive symptomatology during the last two weeks by means of 9 items, using a four-alternative Likert-type response scale ranging “(0 = not at all, 1 = several days, 2 = more than half the days, 3 = nearly every day)". The PHQ-9 scale scores assess 5 severity levels: none (0–4 points), mild (5–9 points), moderate (10–14 points), moderately severe (15–19 points) and severe (20–27 points). For this study, the Spanish version of the PHQ-9 [10] was used, which has been shown to possess satisfactory sensitivity (0.84) and excellent specificity (0.92) compared to the Structured Clinical Interview, considered the gold standard at the time of assessment [23, 30].

Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) is an instrument that has been designed to identify probable cases of generalized anxiety [37]. It is composed of 7 items with a Likert-type response mode, which includes options from 0 (no day) to 3 (almost every day). For its correction, a direct score is obtained from the sum of the scores of all the items, which can fluctuate between 0 and 21. Prior research supports the GAD-7’s reliability and validity evidence across different contexts (e.g., internal structure and relations with external variables). Regarding internal consistency reliability, the Spanish version has shown excellent Cronbach’s alpha estimates (e.g., α = 0.93), and significant associations with conceptually related measures [16].

Statistical analysis

Item-level descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, and skewness coefficients) were first computed to examine the distributional properties of PHQ-9 responses and assess potential deviations from normality. Inspection of skewness informed subsequent analytic decisions, including the use of polychoric correlations for factor analyses and non-parametric correlation coefficients for external validity analyses.

The dimensional structure of the PHQ-9 was examined through a sequence of complementary psychometric procedures. Given the ordinal response format, factor-analytic procedures were conducted on a polychoric correlation matrix, which provides more accurate estimates of inter-item associations under ordinal measurement conditions [8, 17]. Parallel analysis was conducted to inform the number of latent factors. This procedure compares eigenvalues derived from the observed data with those obtained from randomly generated datasets matched to the empirical data in sample size and number of variables, thereby providing a reference distribution of eigenvalues expected by chance alone. In the present study, parallel analysis was recomputed in R using polychoric correlations and 1,000 randomly generated datasets. Factor retention decisions were informed by the parallel analysis results, inspection of the eigenvalue structure, exploratory factor loadings, and theoretical interpretability.

As a complementary approach, exploratory factor analysis was conducted using principal axis factoring on the polychoric correlation matrix. This extraction method was selected because it aims to identify latent constructs based on shared variance and does not require multivariate normality. Because only one factor was ultimately retained, rotation was not required and did not affect the final solution; the reported loadings correspond to the unrotated one-factor extraction.

Construct validity evidence based on internal structure was further evaluated using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Considering the ordinal indicators and observed departures from multivariate normality, models were estimated using the diagonally weighted least squares (DWLS) estimator, which provides robust parameter estimates and standard errors under such conditions [12, 31]. Model fit was assessed using multiple complementary indices, including the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR). Model adequacy was interpreted according to widely accepted criteria [22, 26, 36]: CFI and TLI values ≥ 0.90 indicate acceptable fit and ≥ 0.95 excellent fit,RMSEA values < 0.08 indicate adequate fit and < 0.05 excellent fit,and SRMR values ≤ 0.08 indicate acceptable fit.

Internal consistency reliability was estimated using both McDonald’s omega (ω) and Cronbach’s alpha (α). Omega was included as a model-based reliability coefficient that does not assume tau-equivalence, whereas alpha was reported to facilitate comparison with prior PHQ-9 validation studies. Coefficients ≥ 0.70 were interpreted as indicative of acceptable reliability.

Evidence of validity based on relations to other variables was examined through the association between PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores. Given the well-documented overlap between depressive and anxiety symptomatology within the internalizing spectrum, a positive association was expected. Similar approaches have been employed in previous PHQ-9 validation studies, where correlations with anxiety measures such as the GAD-7 were examined as part of validity evidence based on relations to related constructs (e.g., [33, 35, 39]). Because both instruments use ordinal response formats and the item distributions showed marked positive skewness, Spearman’s rank-order correlation coefficient (ρ) was used to estimate the magnitude of the association. This decision was based primarily on measurement level and distributional characteristics rather than solely on significance tests of normality, which are highly sensitive in large samples.

Missing data were minimal. Across 8,226 possible PHQ-9 item responses (914 participants × 9 items), only 13 values were missing (0.16%). Given this negligible proportion (well below commonly cited thresholds), missing item responses were replaced using item-level median imputation, a conservative and appropriate strategy for ordinal data [34]. Participants with more than 20% missing PHQ-9 items were to be excluded in accordance with common scoring practices,however, no cases exceeded this threshold, and therefore no participants were removed due to missing data.

Data management was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 25). Parallel analysis and exploratory factor analysis were conducted in R using polychoric correlations. Confirmatory factor analysis was estimated in Mplus (Version 7.4) using the DWLS estimator.

Results

The mean PHQ-9 total score in the full sample was M = 7.16, SD = 5.90, with a median of 6 (IQR = 7). The mean values of the PHQ-9 items showed variation, with item 9 presenting the lowest mean (M = 0.18) and item 3 the highest (M = 1.16), reflecting trends toward responses in the moderate to high range within the scale. Standard deviations indicated consistent variability across items, with the smallest dispersion in item 9 (SD = 0.568) and the largest in item 5 (SD = 1.10), suggesting a homogeneous distribution of responses across items. Skewness ranged from a slight skewness in item 3 (Skewness = 0.53) to a noticeable skewness in item 9 (Skewness = 3.65), pointing to a skew in responses toward one of the extremes of the scale, particularly in item 9 (Table 2).

Sex differences in depression: results of the PHQ-9

Given the non-normal distribution of the data by group, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied to determine the differences in PHQ-9 scores between the male (n = 246) and female (n = 668) groups. The findings indicated a significant difference in median PHQ-9 scores for males (median = 4, interquartile range = 7) and females (median = 7, interquartile range = 8), with a U value = 101,397.5, p < 0.001. This suggests that the levels of depressive symptomatology reported by women were significantly higher compared to those of men. The rank-biserial correlation (r_rb) was 0.24, indicating a small effect.

Scale dimensionality

To investigate the underlying structure of the PHQ-9, parallel analysis based on polychoric correlations and 1,000 random datasets was conducted. This procedure suggested a three-factor solution. However, this result was interpreted with caution, as parallel analysis based on polychoric correlations may overextract factors under conditions of ordinal measurement and skewed item distributions. Inspection of the eigenvalue structure revealed a clearly dominant first factor (λ = 5.67), followed by a sharp decline to the second eigenvalue (λ = 0.74), indicating a strong general factor. Exploratory factor analysis using principal axis factoring further supported a unidimensional interpretation, with all items loading strongly on a single factor (0.72–0.87) and 58.5% of the variance explained. Therefore, consistent with theoretical expectations and prior literature, a one-factor solution was retained (Fig. 2).

Confirmatory factor analysis

Following the theoretical model and the analysis of the dimensional structure performed, an evaluation of the model fit was carried out through a confirmatory factor analysis. The evaluated model demonstrated an excellent fit, as observed by the chi-square indices χ2 (14) = 38.56 (p < 0.001), Comparative Fit Index (CFI) = 0.99 and Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) = 0.99, and acceptable fit with a Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) = 0.064 (90% CI = 0.054, 0.076) and SRMR = 0.051. The factor loadings for each scale item were found to be significant and exhibited high values (≥ 0.72), indicating a strong and significant association with the proposed factor (Fig. 3).

Reliability

This section analyzes the consistency of the participants' responses through McDonald's Omega and Cronbach's Alpha coefficients. The importance of this consistency analysis lies in its ability to verify whether the items of the instrument reliably measure the construct they intend to assess, as highlighted by Förster and Rojas [13] and Ventura and Caycho [41]. The results obtained for McDonald's Omega (Ꞷ = 0.891) and for Cronbach's alpha (α = 0.886) demonstrate a high internal consistency of the scale. Validity evidence based on relations to other variables

Because PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores are measured on ordinal response scales and both exhibited marked positive skewness (PHQ-9 item skewness range: 0.53–3.65), Spearman’s rank-order correlation was used. This choice is grounded in the ordinal measurement level and observed distributional asymmetry; it does not rely solely on the Shapiro–Wilk test, which is expected to be statistically significant at the present sample size (N = 914) even under minor deviations from normality. Results showed a strong, positive association between depressive symptom severity (PHQ-9) and anxiety symptoms (GAD-7), ρ = 0.73, p < 0.001. This finding provides convergent validity evidence, indicating that higher depressive symptomatology is associated with higher anxiety symptomatology, consistent with prior research [25, 33, 35, 39].

Discussion

Although the PHQ-9 is the most widely used depression screening instrument worldwide [27], no prior studies had examined its psychometric functioning in the Dominican Republic. The absence of locally validated instruments for assessing depressive symptoms limits diagnostic precision, may contribute to incomplete detection of symptomatology, and constrains the development of culturally grounded mental health research and practice [6, 29]. Addressing this gap is particularly relevant in light of the documented burden of depressive and anxiety disorders in the country [43].

The present study examined the psychometric performance of the PHQ-9 in a Dominican adult sample. Overall, the findings supported a unidimensional structure, high internal consistency, and theoretically coherent associations with anxiety symptomatology. These results provide meaningful, although circumscribed, evidence regarding the construct validity of the PHQ-9 within the Dominican population. The instrument demonstrated a coherent internal structure consistent with prior factorial research, alongside strong reliability and theoretically expected associations with related constructs. Moreover, the findings align with a substantial body of cross-cultural psychometric research documenting the structural robustness and reliability of the PHQ-9 across diverse linguistic, clinical, and community settings [3, 7, 19, 40]. In this sense, the present study extends existing validation evidence to a previously underexamined Caribbean context and provides regionally specific support for the structural and relational functioning of the scale.

Regarding dimensionality, the overall pattern of evidence supported a single latent factor underlying PHQ-9 responses. Although parallel analysis based on polychoric correlations suggested more than one factor, inspection of the eigenvalue structure revealed a clearly dominant first factor, and both exploratory and confirmatory analyses converged on a unidimensional interpretation. This pattern is consistent with prior large-scale and cross-cultural research supporting the PHQ-9 as a largely unidimensional measure of depressive symptom severity across diverse populations and settings [3, 5, 7, 19, 40].

Reliability analyses indicated high internal consistency, with both McDonald’s omega and Cronbach’s alpha exceeding recommended thresholds [13, 41]. The agreement between these reliability indices supports the precision of PHQ-9 total scores in capturing depressive symptom burden in the Dominican sample and is consistent with findings reported in previous validation research [5, 30].

Evidence based on relations to other variables indicated a significant positive association between PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores. This pattern is theoretically coherent given the well-documented comorbidity and shared internalizing liability between depressive and anxiety disorders [33, 35]. Nevertheless, the strength of construct validity inferences should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Because the present study relied on a single external correlate, the observed association provides partial evidence of convergent validity rather than a comprehensive demonstration of construct validity. Contemporary psychometric frameworks emphasize that construct validity requires the accumulation of evidence across a broader nomological network, including additional depression-specific measures, discriminant constructs, and ideally clinical diagnostic indicators. The absence of such complementary external validators in the present study therefore limits the scope of construct validity conclusions that can be drawn from these findings.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the sample was obtained through a non-probabilistic convenience strategy and consisted primarily of individuals from the general population. As a result, findings cannot be interpreted as population-representative. Notably, the sample displays marked sociodemographic skewness: approximately 47% of participants held a master’s degree or doctorate, and 46% reported monthly income exceeding 60,000 DOP. These figures are substantially higher than population-level estimates for the Dominican Republic, suggesting the sample over-represents highly educated and higher-income segments of the population. This skewness may limit the generalizability of the psychometric findings to lower-income and less formally educated segments of Dominican society, where prevalence and expression of depressive symptoms may differ. In addition, because the study employed a cross-sectional design with a single measurement occasion, test–retest reliability could not be evaluated. Future research should include clinical samples, probabilistic designs, and longitudinal assessments to strengthen generalizability and examine the temporal stability of PHQ-9 scores.

A further limitation concerns the absence of a structured clinical interview or other gold-standard diagnostic measure. Although such procedures are required to evaluate sensitivity, specificity, and optimal cut-off scores, the present study was designed to examine foundational psychometric properties, including dimensionality and internal consistency. Establishing structural coherence is methodologically prior to diagnostic calibration, as the interpretation and justification of global cut-off scores presuppose a well-defined latent structure. Future research should incorporate structured clinical interviews and depression-specific reference standards to examine diagnostic accuracy and determine locally appropriate decision thresholds.

A methodological limitation of the parallel analysis concerns the number of simulated datasets used as a reference distribution. The present study employed 20 randomly generated datasets, which is substantially lower than the 1,000 iterations conventionally recommended for stable percentile-based parallel analysis [17, 21]. This constraint was imposed by the default settings of JASP Version 0.17.1, which did not offer a user-adjustable replication parameter in the parallel analysis module at the time of the analysis. Nonetheless, the magnitude of the eigenvalue difference between the observed first factor (5.69) and its simulated counterpart (1.15) is sufficiently large that the single-factor retention decision is unlikely to be reversed with a greater number of simulations. Future studies are encouraged to employ software environments that support 1,000 or more iterations, such as the psych or EFAtools packages in R, to ensure greater stability of the parallel analysis solution.

The gender composition of the sample should be considered when interpreting the present findings. Women were overrepresented (73.1% of participants) and reported significantly higher depressive symptom scores than men. Nonparametric comparisons (Mann–Whitney U) indicated higher median PHQ-9 scores among females (median = 7) relative to males (median = 4), although the associated effect size was small (r_rb = 0.24). This pattern is consistent with extensive epidemiological evidence indicating that depressive symptoms and depressive disorders are more frequently reported among women across diverse populations and cultural contexts [4, 45].

Beyond epidemiological differences, methodological research on health surveys has shown that women tend to participate more frequently in health-related and psychological studies and are generally more willing to report emotional distress and seek health information compared with men. Differences in help-seeking behavior, emotional expression, and reporting styles may therefore contribute both to higher observed symptom scores and to gender imbalances in voluntary survey participation [1].

Nevertheless, the combined effect of gender imbalance and higher symptom reporting among women may influence overall score distributions within the sample. Consequently, caution is warranted when generalizing the present findings to populations with more balanced gender compositions. Future research should aim to recruit more demographically balanced samples and formally test sex-based measurement invariance of the PHQ-9 to ensure that the scale operates equivalently across gender groups within the Dominican population.

Another limitation concerns the absence of ethnicity and race reporting within the study sample. Ethnocultural variables are increasingly recognized as relevant in psychometric evaluations, as they may influence symptom expression, cultural interpretations of psychological distress, response styles, and measurement invariance. This consideration is particularly salient in the Dominican Republic, a country characterized by substantial ethnoracial diversity shaped by Afro-Caribbean, European, and Indigenous heritage, alongside regional sociocultural variability. The lack of ethnicity/race data precluded the examination of subgroup psychometric functioning or differential item performance. Consequently, caution is warranted when generalizing findings across the full ethnocultural spectrum of the Dominican population. Future validation research should incorporate detailed ethnicity and race indicators to more comprehensively evaluate cross-group stability of the PHQ-9.

Despite the limitations noted above, the findings of the present study provide evidence supporting the structural coherence and reliability of the PHQ-9 within this Dominican adult sample. These results extend the growing body of cross-cultural research on the PHQ-9 and contribute new empirical evidence from a previously understudied Caribbean context. In doing so, the study strengthens the psychometric foundation for the use of the instrument in Spanish-speaking populations of the Caribbean region and provides a basis for future validation work. The availability of locally generated evidence regarding the functioning of the PHQ-9 may also facilitate its use in research, primary care screening initiatives, and public health studies focused on the early identification of depressive symptoms in the Dominican population.

Conclusions

The findings of this study support the use of the PHQ-9 as a reliable and psychometrically sound instrument for assessing depressive symptoms in Dominican adults. Overall, the scale showed a clear unidimensional structure, strong internal consistency, and a meaningful association with anxiety symptoms, all of which are consistent with previous international research on the PHQ-9.

This study contributes new evidence from the Dominican Republic, a context in which the psychometric properties of the PHQ-9 had not previously been examined. In this sense, the results help extend the cross-cultural validation literature on depression screening tools in Spanish-speaking Caribbean populations. From an applied perspective, having locally validated instruments is important for both research and mental health practice. The availability of a brief and reliable screening tool such as the PHQ-9 may support early detection efforts, epidemiological studies, and mental health initiatives in community and healthcare settings throughout the country.
