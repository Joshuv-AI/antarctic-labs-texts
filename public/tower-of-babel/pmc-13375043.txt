Is uroflow reproducible in healthy boys? A community-based observational study from Southern India
Authors: T. Anil Kumar, Selvin Theodore Jayanth Ezhilarasu, Partho Mukherjee, Sudhindra Jayasimha, Chandrasingh Jeyachandra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:This study evaluates the reproducibility of uroflowmetry in children aged 5-15 years to establish the age at which uroflowmetry is representative.Methods:Of 291 boys screened, uroflowmetry was done in 247 eligible boys. The flow was repeated at two weeks. A total of 227 children were included for analysis. Twenty were excluded due to a voided volume of less than 50ml and an interrupted flow pattern. The maximum flow rate (Qmax), average flow rate (Qavg), voided volume (VV), time to maximum flow, flow time and voiding time were recorded. The reproducibility of all parameters was assessed using the Inter-correlation coefficient (ICC) and correlation using Pearson’s coefficient.Result:Qmax, Qavg showed excellent concordance in boys of 5-15 years [ICC >0.6], implying the flows were reproducible. VV showed excellent concordance in boys aged 8-15 years [ICC >0.6], but only moderate correlation in boys aged 5-7 years [ICC 0.4-0.6], probably due to the wide variation in voided volumes. Qmax and Qavg showed significant correlation in all age groups, with voided volume in first and second flow [Pearson’s coefficient: 0.49 & 0.53 for Qmax1 and Qmax2; 0.48 & 0.51 Qavg1 and Qavg2 respectively], implying reproducibility of flow in this age group.Conclusion:Uroflowmetry is reproducible in children aged 5-15 years. Qmax and Qavg showed excellent reproducibility, and voided volume showed good reproducibility. Qmax shows excellent reproducibility even in the smallest of the age groups studied [5-7 years].

INTRODUCTION

Uroflowmetry is the most utilised form of urodynamic testing and the most helpful investigation for evaluating lower urinary tract symptoms. It aids in selecting patients for more complex urodynamic testing[1] and provides an objective way to determine the response to the treatment in certain diseases.[2] Measured flow rates in children were first published by Kaufman in 1957.[3] In the evaluation of children, the biggest concern is the reproducibility. If a test is to be used reliably to study a pathology, it must be reproducible. Unlike in adults, the evidence to support the reproducibility of uroflowmetry in children is poor. Furthermore, the age at which uroflowmetry is reproducible in children has not yet been studied. Our primary aim was to evaluate whether uroflowmetry is reproducible in school-aged boys. The secondary objective was to evaluate the age at which uroflowmetry would be most reproducible.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A prospective study was conducted in a school in South India. The Institutional Review Board approved the study (IRB no. 10847). The school administrators and teachers were informed about the study. With their permission, all children were educated about the study during their school assembly, and a consent form, along with an information pamphlet about the test, was sent individually to the parents. Healthy school boys of 5–15 years whose parents consented were included in the study [Figure 1]. After obtaining their assent, they were evaluated with relevant urological history and a brief clinical examination. Those without any urinary symptoms, urological intervention, possible urinary tract infection and apparent anatomical abnormalities were recruited to the study. Females were excluded from this study because we aimed to have a homogeneous group with similar voiding patterns, and because this was conducted in a school setting, there were logistical and privacy concerns if it involved female children. Children were advised to drink water (as they preferred) on the morning of the test. They were subsequently made to void on a uroflowmeter when they had a strong urge to void, ensuring adequate privacy thus mimicking their normal physiological void. It was repeated at 2 weeks with similar precautions and those children who could not void were given adequate hydration and were asked wait until they voided normally, thereby eliminating undue pressure on the boys from external factors such as teachers. Boys whose parents refused to consent, had history of any previous urological intervention, were suspected to have urinary tract infection, showed a staccato/interrupted pattern or tower/box-shaped curves on the uroflowmetry or had a voided volume (VV) <50 mL were excluded from the study. The uroflowmeter machine (MMS, The Netherlands, 2015 make) was installed in a specific toilet in their environment, while the recording equipment was kept outside at a separate place to ensure complete privacy. All the eligible boys were made to urinate into the uroflowmeter, and the resulting uroflow pattern was saved. After 2 weeks, the same boys were made to urinate again in the uroflow machine, and the data was saved. All uroflow parameters, namely maximum flow rate (Qmax), average flow rate (Qavg), VV, and time to maximum flow, were noted. The VV in different age groups was calculated as a percentage of the expected bladder capacity. Expected bladder capacity was calculated based on the Koff’s formula.[4]

Statistical analysis

Assuming an intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.65 (for the Qmax) based on a prior adult study,[5] a sample size of 230 boys was calculated with a precision of 0.15. In the above study[5] on 147 adult patients, the researchers evaluated the cohort’s average scores for both the parameters (Qmax and Qavg). They then used ICC to measure how much results varied within the same person and compared each visit’s peak urinary flow. As the primary objective was to study the reproducibility, the sample size was based on the precision of the ICC formula.[6] The Z-scores were calculated to check the sample reference ranges, and ICC was calculated to check the repeatability of the uroflow parameters (whether the flow done twice at 2 weeks apart follow within the calculated reference value) and was presented with 95% confidence interval (CI) and subgroups were analyzed to determine at what age they were representative. An ICC: >0.75 was considered excellent correlation; 0.60–0.74 was regarded as good correlation; 0.40–0.60 was regarded as moderate correlation; <0.40 was regarded as poor correlation.[7]

Pearson’s correlations were used to assess the relationships between Qmax, Qavg, and VV. Regression analyses were used to check whether reproducibility is affected by age or body mass index (BMI) and whether age affects uroflow variables. A P ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. STATA 13.1 (StataCorp LLC, Texas, USA) software was used for analysis.

RESULTS

A total of 291 healthy boys aged 5–15 years were considered for the study, of whom 247 were recruited after obtaining informed consent (44 declined consent) [Supplementary Figure 1].

A total of 20 were excluded from the study, including boys with meatal stenosis (n = 3), those who voided <50 mL, or had a staccato or interrupted flow pattern. All children who had staccato flow also had VV <50 mL. Three children could not follow instructions well. A total of 227 children were included in the analysis.

The mean age was 10.88 ± 2.57. The mean VVs were 153.2 ± 103.97 mL in the first flow and 156.2 ± 100.25 mL in the second flow. 62% of the children had VV more than 50% of the expected bladder capacity. The mean Qmax was 17.42 ± 5.64 (mL/s) in the first and 16.93 ± 5.67 (mL/s) in the second flows, respectively. The mean average urine flow rates were 13.777 ± 5.49 (mL/s) for the first and 14.21 ± 5.46 (mL/s) for the second flows, respectively [Supplementary Table 1].

The Bland–Altman reproducibility plot for Qmax in Figure 1 showed >95% of values lying within the limits of agreement (−9.11, 8.13) with minimal bias (dotted line near the 0 value).

The plots for Qavg in Figure 2 and VV in Figure 3 showed similar findings: >95% of values lie within the limits of agreement ([− 8.56, 9.42] and [−196.73, 202.73], respectively), with minimal bias.

The findings from the above Bland–Altman plots indicate good agreement and high reproducibility between the two sets of measurements for Qmax, Qavg, and VV. These results indicate that repeated measurements using the same method are likely to yield very similar results, demonstrating high reliability.

In a variance analysis, we documented significant group differences in volume, average flow, and maximum flow. These findings justify stratifying the data by age, as each group demonstrated unique and measurable differences in voiding performance. Qmax and Qavg showed significant correlations with VV in the first and second flow (Pearson’s correlation coefficients of 0.49 and 0.48 for Qmax1 and Qavg1; 0.53 and 0.51 for Qmax2 and Qavg2, respectively; P < 0.0001).

On analysis, differences were observed in uroflow parameters across ages from 5 to 15 years, with significant differences emerging after 7 years. However, no significant difference in uroflow rates was noted among children 12–15. Hence, we subdivided the children into four groups: 5–7 years, 8–9 years, 10–11 years and 12–15 years.

Subgroup reproducibility analysis

ICC showed excellent reproducibility for Qmax even in the youngest age groups. Qavg showed good reproducibility across all age groups, with excellent reproducibility after 12 years. VV showed increased reproducibility with age, with moderate reproducibility at 5–7 years and excellent thereafter [Table 1]. Due to the substantial variability in VVs within the 5–7 year cohort – indicated by a broad CI – these results may lack the precision required for definitive conclusion.

Linear regression analysis demonstrated that age is a significant predictor of uroflow variability, accounting for 20.20% of the variance in Qmax, 17.92% in Qavg and 3.93% in VV. In boys aged 5–15 years, each 1-year increase in age corresponded to a mean increase of 0.99 ml/s for Qmax (95% CI: 0.75–1.22), 0.90 ml/s for Qavg (95% CI: 0.67–1.13), and 7.9 ml for VV (95% CI: 3.9–11.9).

DISCUSSION

All the variables of uroflowmetry, namely, flow curve pattern, Qmax, Qavg, voiding time, flow time, VV and time to maximum flow, need to be measured as standardized by the International Continence Society.[8] However, the most clinically useful variables are flow curve pattern, Qmax and VV.[9] Measured flow rates in children were first reported by Kaufman[3] in 1957, who showed normal flow rates ranging from 13 to 26 mL/s, at a minimal VV of 100 mL. Gutierrez Segura’s report[10] confirmed that as the VV and age increase, the Qmax and Qavg increase. In India, to date, two studies have developed nomograms, only one specifically for children.[11] The other one included healthy people over a wide age range, including 5–15 years in one group, and developed separate nomogram for each group.[12] The limitations of studies conducted to date in children for developing a nomogram are that some consider only one uroflow record, while others use either the best of two or the average of two.[131415161718] The evidence to support the reproducibility of uroflowmetry in children is poor. Even the minimum VV required to reach any conclusion is not well established in children.[1517] Some studies have considered a VV of 50 mL, while others have even considered 20 mL for evaluation.[101215] Franco et al. showed that flow indices (corrected for bladder volume) and VVs of 50 mL showed good reproducibility and are excellent tools to estimate voiding efficiency in normal voiders.[1920] Van Batavia et al. found poor inter-rater agreement in uroflow curve pattern analysis based on a study of 119 curves evaluated by 7 raters. They suggested replacing subjective pattern classification with objective, age-specific flow indices.[21]

Only a few studies have considered the normal bell-shaped pattern of uroflow for evaluation,[1016] while most of them have taken pooled data from the entire pediatric population.[12151617] All these limitations have been minimized to the extent possible in our study by maintaining a strict inclusion criterion with a devised algorithm. All the apprehension among children was addressed by maintaining privacy during examination and voiding and by counselling them. Even the children who could not void were given adequate hydration and waited until a normal void occurred, eliminating the stress.

The reproducibility of uroflowmetry has been demonstrated only in adults, with no studies in children.[6] This study was therefore required to fill this gap in our knowledge on this subject. Gierup[13] did not find a significant difference between the first and second uroflow records and suggested that only one uroflow record is sufficient; however, the study involved only 70 children, which we believe is not a sufficient sample size. Gupta et al.[11] asked children after one flow whether the survey was representative of their regular voiding habits, a subjective measure of representativeness that may not be reliable. To overcome this, we have measured uroflow parameters in the same individual 2 weeks apart and analyzed them for reproducibility, which shows good to excellent reproducibility between Qmax and Qavg as shown on the Bland–Altman reproducibility plot. Our study showed that uroflow parameters are repeatable even in children at the earliest toilet-trained age and can be repeated at age 5.

Di Scipio et al.[22] found only low correlation between age, height, and weight and Qmax and Qavg. Neither BMI nor age affected the reproducibility of uroflow parameters in our study. Kajbafzadeh et al.[16] reported statistically significant increases in urine flow rates with age in girls, but not in boys. The Qmax increased by 0.92 (mL/s/year), and Qavg increased by 0.51 (mL/s/year) in girls between the ages of 7 and 14. On the contrary, our study showed statistically significant increases in urine flow rates with age for boys and that as age increases by 1 year, Qmax increases by 0.99 (mL/s/year), Qavg increases by 0.90 (mL/s/year), and VV increases by 7.9 (mL/year) in healthy boys of 5–15 years.

Thus, uroflowmetry can be advocated as a reliable and reproducible test with reasonable accuracy across all age groups, especially in children above 8 years, and VV plays an important role in interpretation.

We had a few limitations. This was a single-center study that included only boys, and numbers were not equally distributed among all age groups. Postvoid residue was not calculated, and bladder volume corrected flow indices would enhance the results and interpretation, as shown by Agarwal et al.[23]

CONCLUSION

Uroflowmetry is reproducible in boys aged 5–15 years. Qmax and Qavg are reproducible across all age groups studied with good-to-excellent correlation. Qmax shows excellent reproducibility even in the youngest age groups studied (5–7 years]. However, the findings of this study cannot be generalized to all children, as it did not include girls and involved a selected community of boys.
