Nonlinear Dynamical Friction from the Doppler-Shifted Equilibrium Memory Kernel
N. R. Sree Harsha, Zhenyuan Yu, Chuang Ren, Virginia Billings, Michael Huang

Abstract
We present a statistical mechanics framework for modeling non-equilibrium transport coefficients using the Generalized Langevin Equation (GLE). We show that the kernel, obtained via the Fluctuation-Dissipation Theorem (FDT) from the stochastic force autocorrelation measured in a thermal equilibrium state, is sufficient to model the dynamics of the system in a Non-Equilibrium Steady State (NESS). This approach provides a computationally efficient path to modeling complex transport problems. We apply this framework to the canonical problem of test particle drag in a uniform plasma. The GLE formalism is shown to naturally capture non-Markovian phenomena through the moments of the kernel, including an effective mass renormalization and oscillatory relaxation. We demonstrate that the standard Chandrasekhar stopping power formula arises naturally as the Markovian limit of this equilibrium memory kernel. These theoretical predictions are quantitatively validated by direct Particle-in-Cell simulations, which confirm the predicted oscillatory structure of the memory kernel. This work thus establishes a practical method for predicting non-equilibrium transport properties from first-principles equilibrium simulations.



I
Introduction

The calculation of transport coefficients for systems driven far from equilibrium remains one of the most enduring challenges in statistical physics. Since the pioneering work of Onsager
Onsager1931
and Green
Green1954
, it has been understood that the dissipative properties of a system are intimately linked to its spontaneous internal fluctuations. While the Fluctuation-Dissipation Theorem (FDT) provides a rigorous map between these fluctuations and the linear response of a system near thermal equilibrium

;
Kubo1966
, extending this relation to Non-Equilibrium Steady States (NESS) typically requires perturbative techniques or computationally expensive non-equilibrium simulations
Evans2008
;
Zwanzig1961
. For a driven subsystem (such as a particle dragged through a fluid, a colloid in a bath, or a defect migrating in a solid) characterizing the nonlinear response usually mandates a brute force approach. This involves conducting a series of separate simulations at various driving forces to map out the response curve point-by-point

;
Zwicknagel1999
.

In this paper, we present a general statistical mechanics framework that circumvents this computational bottleneck for a broad class of subsystems. We demonstrate that the nonlinear friction of a driven system can be reconstructed
ab initio
from the fluctuations of the
undriven
equilibrium bath, provided the subsystem samples the bath modes along a specific Doppler-shifted resonance manifold. We utilize the Generalized Langevin Equation (GLE) to describe the stochastic dynamics of a macroscopic variable
A(t)
coupled to a high-dimensional thermal environment

;
Grabert1982
. As established in the theory of stochastic processes

;

, the projection of deterministic Hamiltonian dynamics onto a reduced subset of variables naturally gives rise to a non-Markovian memory kernel (

), which dictates the system’s irreversible behavior

\nu(v)
is mathematically contained within the equilibrium force autocorrelation function (FACF) of the static system.

To validate this general formalism, we apply it to the canonical problem of dynamical friction (stopping power) in a collisionless plasma. This system serves as an ideal testbed due to its known non-trivial features, specifically the transition from Stokes-like drag at low velocities to the

decay characteristic of the Chandrasekhar limit at high velocities

;

This bridges the gap between abstract statistical mechanics and practical transport modeling, offering a method akin to modern liquid theory techniques

II
Doppler-Shifted Fluctuation-Dissipation Theorem

We begin by establishing the general statistical mechanical framework that links non-equilibrium transport to equilibrium fluctuations. Our focus in this section is general and applies to any macroscopic variable. We will demonstrate that the dissipative forces in this driven state are not new dynamical entities, but rather kinematic transformations of the bath’s inherent equilibrium noise. This derivation rests on two theoretical pillars: first, that the linear response function of the medium is invariant under Galilean transformations (Sec.
II.1
), and second, that this invariance permits a kinematic (Doppler-shifted) extension of the FDT (Sec.
II.2
).

II.1
Galilean Invariance of the Memory Kernel

Consider the linear response of the bath variable
\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)
to a moving perturbation source
\rho_{ext}(\mathbf{r},t)=Q\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{v}t)
. The dynamics of the bath fields are governed by a linear wave equation (e.g., Vlasov-Poisson or Maxwell equations) of the form

\hat{L}\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)=S_{ext}(\mathbf{r},t),

(1)

where
\hat{L}
is a linear differential operator describing the medium
IchimaruBasic
. For a homogeneous plasma,
\hat{L}
is invariant under spatial translation
LandauKinetics
. The formal solution is given by the Green’s function
G(\mathbf{r},t)

\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)=\int d\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\int dt^{\prime}G(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t-t^{\prime})\rho_{ext}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime}).

(2)

Substituting the source trajectory
\rho_{ext}=Q\delta(\mathbf{r}^{\prime}-\mathbf{v}t^{\prime})
, the wake field becomes

\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)=Q\int dt^{\prime}G(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{v}t^{\prime},t-t^{\prime}).

(3)

The induced force on the particle is the gradient of this wake evaluated at the particle’s instantaneous position
\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{v}t

F_{ind}(t)=-Q\nabla\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)\Big|_{\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{v}t}.

(4)

Shifting the integration variable to
\tau=t-t^{\prime}
(the time lag), the argument of the Green’s function becomes

\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{v}t^{\prime}=\mathbf{v}t-\mathbf{v}(t-\tau)=\mathbf{v}\tau.

(5)

Thus, the force depends only on the time lag
\tau
, not the absolute time
t

F_{ind}=-Q^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau\left[\nabla G(\mathbf{x},\tau)\right]_{\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{v}\tau}.

(6)

This confirms that the response function (and thus the memory kernel) is fundamentally a function of the relative separation
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{v}\tau
. In the Fourier domain, this spatial shift
\mathbf{v}\tau
corresponds exactly to the phase factor
e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{v}\tau}
which shifts the frequency argument of the susceptibility

\chi_{moving}(\omega)=\chi_{static}(\omega-\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{v}).

(7)

This proves that the non-equilibrium friction is strictly a Doppler-shifted sampling of the static equilibrium response function.

II.2
Kinematic Extension of the FDT

With the Galilean invariance established, we now derive the generalized FDT for a driven system. Consider a macroscopic subsystem described by the generalized coordinate
\mathbf{A}(t)
interacting with a fluctuating field
\Psi(\mathbf{q},t)
of a high-dimensional environmental bath, initially in thermodynamic equilibrium at temperature
T

Kubo1966
. The interaction is defined locally in the configuration space
\mathbf{q}
conjugate to
\mathbf{A}
, such that the generalized force exerted on the subsystem is the gradient of the field sampled at the subsystem’s instantaneous state:
\mathbf{F}(t)=-\nabla\Psi(\mathbf{q},t)|_{\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{A}(t)}
.

We define the non-equilibrium state of the subsystem by the condition that it evolves with a constant non-zero mean flux
\langle\dot{\mathbf{A}}(t)\rangle=\mathbf{J}
. To determine the dissipation in this driven state, we adopt the reference frame co-moving with the flux
\mathbf{J}
, where the subsystem is instantaneously stationary. According to the GLE derived via the Mori-Zwanzig projection operator formalism
Mori1965
;
Zwanzig1961
, the steady-state friction coefficient
\nu(\mathbf{J})
governing the energy dissipation
\langle P_{diss}\rangle=-M\nu(\mathbf{J})J^{2}
is given by the time-integral of the force autocorrelation function experienced in the co-moving frame

\nu(\mathbf{J})=\frac{1}{Mk_{B}T}\text{Re}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau\langle\mathbf{F}(\tau)\cdot\mathbf{F}(0)\rangle_{\mathbf{J}},

(8)

where
\langle\dots\rangle_{\mathbf{J}}
denotes the ensemble average over the bath degrees of freedom in the presence of the driven flux. We expand the bath field
\Psi(\mathbf{q},t)
into its spectral modes
(\mathbf{k},\omega)
defined in the laboratory rest frame, where
\mathbf{k}
represents the wavenumber in the configuration space. Along the driven trajectory
\mathbf{A}(t)=\mathbf{J}t
, the force experienced by the subsystem is a sampling of these modes

\mathbf{F}(t)\propto\int d\mathbf{k}\int d\omega\,i\mathbf{k}\,\tilde{\Psi}(\mathbf{k},\omega)e^{i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{A}(t)-\omega t)}.

(9)

Substituting the linear trajectory
\mathbf{A}(t)=\mathbf{J}t
, the phase factor becomes
e^{i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{J}-\omega)t}
. This term reveals that the subsystem effectively samples the bath fluctuations at a Doppler-shifted frequency
\Omega^{\prime}=\omega-\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{J}
.

Assuming the linear response limit—where the driven subsystem does not globally distort the statistical properties of the bath—the mode correlations are described by the equilibrium structure factor
S_{eq}(\mathbf{k},\omega)
of the undisturbed bath
HansenBook
. The force autocorrelation function thus simplifies to

\langle\mathbf{F}(\tau)\cdot\mathbf{F}(0)\rangle_{\mathbf{J}}\propto\int d\mathbf{k}\int d\omega\,k^{2}S_{eq}(\mathbf{k},\omega)e^{i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{J}-\omega)\tau}.

(10)

Substituting this correlation into the friction integral, the time integration over
\tau
yields a Dirac delta function,
\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{J}-\omega)\tau}d\tau=\pi\delta(\omega-\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{J})
, which enforces a generalized Cherenkov resonance condition. The non-equilibrium friction coefficient is therefore

\nu(\mathbf{J})=\frac{\pi C}{Mk_{B}T}\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,g(\mathbf{k})\,S_{eq}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{J}),

(11)

where
C
is a coupling constant and
g(\mathbf{k})
is a geometric factor (e.g.,
k^{2}
for conservative potentials). This result demonstrates that non-equilibrium transport coefficients for a driven variable can be recovered purely from the equilibrium fluctuations of the bath, provided they are sampled along the resonant manifold
\omega=\mathbf{k}\cdot\dot{\mathbf{A}}
.

This formalism constitutes a kinematic extension of the standard FDT, generalizing its applicability from static equilibrium to driven steady states. By identifying the relevant noise source as the equilibrium spectrum sampled along the Doppler-shifted resonance manifold
\omega=\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{J}
, we unify the linear and non-linear transport regimes into a single geometric framework: low-flux motion probes the static, Ohmic response (recovering Stokes’ Law
Callen1951
), while high-flux motion probes the decaying high-frequency tail of the spectrum (recovering the
1/v^{2}
Chandrasekhar limit
Chandrasekhar1943
). This extended FDT can be used to reduce the computational cost of characterizing non-linear transport from
\mathcal{O}(N)
non-equilibrium simulations to a single
\mathcal{O}(1)
equilibrium simulation
Peter1991
.

III
Drag in Collisionless Plasma

To validate the general formalism derived in Sec.
II
, we apply it to the canonical problem of a heavy ion traversing a collisionless electron plasma. We proceed by first defining the exact stochastic dynamics via the GLE, deriving the explicit non-Markovian memory kernel, and finally demonstrating that the standard Chandrasekhar stopping power formula arises strictly as the Markovian limit of this more general theory.

III.1
The Non-Markovian Memory Kernel

We consider a heavy test particle of charge
Q
and mass
M
interacting with a plasma bath of density
n
, charge
q
, and mass
m
. The motion of the test particle is governed by the GLE, which explicitly accounts for the finite response time (memory) of the surrounding medium
Mori1965

M\dot{v}(t)=-\int_{0}^{t}\gamma(t-\tau)v(\tau)d\tau+R(t),

(12)

where
v(t)
is the particle velocity,
R(t)
is the fluctuating random force arising from thermal collisions, and
\gamma(t)
is the memory kernel describing the retarded friction.

For a system in thermodynamic equilibrium at temperature
T
, the random force satisfies the second Fluctuation-Dissipation Theorem (FDT), linking the memory kernel to the force autocorrelation function (FACF) of a stationary particle
Kubo1966

\gamma(t)=\frac{1}{Mk_{B}T}\langle R(t)\cdot R(0)\rangle.

(13)

This relation establishes that the dissipative properties of the medium are fully determined by the equilibrium fluctuations of the electric field at the particle’s position. In the frequency domain, the friction kernel
\gamma(\omega)
is related to the longitudinal dielectric function
\epsilon(k,\omega)
of the plasma via the spectral density of field fluctuations
IchimaruBasic

\gamma(\omega)=\frac{2Q^{2}}{3M\epsilon_{0}}\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{1}{k^{2}\omega}\mathrm{Im}\left[\frac{-1}{\epsilon(k,\omega)}\right].

(14)

For a Maxwellian plasma in the weak-coupling limit (
\ln\Lambda\gg 1
), the dielectric response is governed by the Vlasov equation. The imaginary part of the dielectric function, which dictates energy dissipation via Landau damping, follows a Gaussian profile determined by the thermal velocity distribution
IchimaruBasic
. Evaluating the integral over wavenumbers yields the explicit Gaussian memory kernel

\gamma(\omega)=\frac{nQ^{2}q^{2}\ln\Lambda}{3\pi^{3/2}\epsilon_{0}^{2}Mv_{\mathrm{th}}^{3}}\left[\frac{1}{\zeta}e^{-\zeta^{2}}\right],

(15)

where
\zeta=\omega/(\sqrt{2}k_{\mathrm{max}}v_{\mathrm{th}})
is the dimensionless frequency. Before taking any limiting cases, we can express the exact mean acceleration of the particle by substituting the memory kernel back into the averaged GLE. Taking the ensemble average
\langle R(t)\rangle=0
, the exact deceleration is given by the convolution of the particle’s entire velocity history with the inverse transform of Eq. (
15
)

\langle\dot{v}(t)\rangle=-\frac{1}{M}\int_{0}^{t}\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\gamma(\omega)e^{-i\omega(t-\tau)}\right]\langle v(\tau)\rangle d\tau.

(16)

This integro-differential equation represents the exact non-Markovian stopping power. Unlike standard drag formulas, this equation accounts for transient effects, such as the time required for the dielectric wake to build up around an accelerating particle, or the memory drag experienced by a particle undergoing rapid oscillations.

III.2
The Markovian Limit: Recovering Chandrasekhar

The standard theory of dynamical friction, as derived by Chandrasekhar
Chandrasekhar1943
or Spitzer
Spitzer1962
, assumes the projectile moves at a constant mean velocity
\mathbf{v}_{0}
and that the plasma response is instantaneous (Markovian). In our framework, this corresponds to the ballistic limit of the exact Eq. (
16
).

When the particle velocity evolves slowly compared to the plasma relaxation time
\tau_{p}\sim\omega_{p}^{-1}
, we can approximate the trajectory as
\mathbf{v}(\tau)\approx\mathbf{v}_{0}
. The convolution integral then simplifies to a Fourier transform evaluated at the Doppler shift frequencies. The mean drag force becomes the real part of the memory kernel evaluated at the Doppler-shifted resonance
\omega=\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{0}

\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{v}_{0})=-M\mathbf{v}_{0}\int\frac{d\Omega_{k}}{4\pi}\mathrm{Re}[\gamma(\omega=\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{0})].

(17)

Substituting the Gaussian kernel from Eq. (
15
) and performing the angular integration over the Doppler shift yields

\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{v}_{0})=-\frac{nQ^{2}q^{2}\ln\Lambda}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}^{2}mv_{\mathrm{th}}^{2}}G\left(\frac{v_{0}}{v_{\mathrm{th}}}\right)\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{0}.

(18)

Here,
G(x)
is the standard Chandrasekhar function

G(x)=\frac{\mathrm{erf}(x)-x\mathrm{erf}^{\prime}(x)}{2x^{2}}.

(19)

This derivation demonstrates that the nonlinear Chandrasekhar drag curve is strictly a kinematic sampling of the equilibrium memory kernel, validated by the recovery of the standard formula.

III.3
Asymptotic Limits

The physical content of the Doppler-shifted FDT is best illustrated by examining the asymptotic limits of Eq. (
18
), which correspond to distinct regimes of the equilibrium fluctuation spectrum.

1. The Low-Velocity (Stokes) Limit (
v_{0}\ll v_{\mathrm{th}}
):

When the projectile velocity is much smaller than the electron thermal speed, the Doppler shift is small, and the particle probes the low-frequency, Ohmic part of the fluctuation spectrum. Expanding the error function for small arguments,
G(x)\approx\frac{2}{3\sqrt{\pi}}x
, we obtain a linear friction law

\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{v}_{0})\approx-\left(\frac{nQ^{2}q^{2}\ln\Lambda}{6\pi^{3/2}\epsilon_{0}^{2}mv_{\mathrm{th}}^{3}}\right)\mathbf{v}_{0}.

(20)

This recovers the Stokes-like viscous drag predicted by the theory of Brownian motion
Callen1951
.

2. The High-Velocity Limit (
v_{0}\gg v_{\mathrm{th}}
):

For projectiles exceeding the thermal speed, the particle interacts with the high-frequency tail of the spectrum (Landau damping). In this limit, the Chandrasekhar function decays as
G(x)\approx 1/(2x^{2})
, leading to the characteristic inertial drag

\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{v}_{0})\approx-\frac{nQ^{2}q^{2}\ln\Lambda}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}^{2}mv_{0}^{2}}\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{0}.

(21)

This recovers the standard
1/v^{2}
stopping power formula
Peter1991
. The ability of the single equilibrium kernel Eq. (
15
) to reproduce both the diffusive (Stokes) and inertial (Chandrasekhar) regimes confirms that the Doppler-shifted FDT provides a unified description of transport across the entire velocity phase space.

IV
PIC Simulation and Validation

To quantitatively validate the Doppler-shifted FDT and the GLE framework, we performed high-fidelity Particle-in-Cell (PIC) simulations using the EPOCH code
Arber2015
. Our numerical strategy involved two distinct sets of simulations: (A)
Equilibrium runs
to extract the memory kernel from the thermal fluctuations of a static bath, and (B)
Non-equilibrium drag runs
to measure the actual slowing down of a test projectile. The goal is to demonstrate that the kernel derived purely from Set A can accurately predict the trajectory observed in Set B.
We simulated a fully ionized, two-species plasma consisting of electrons and protons (
m_{i}/m_{e}=1836
) in a 2D periodic domain. To suppress numerical noise and resolve the fine-scale structure of the dielectric wake, we employed a high-resolution grid with
N_{x}=1024
cells and a particle density of 2000 particles per cell (PPC).

The physical parameters were chosen to represent a canonical thermal plasma: a number density of
n_{0}=10^{19}\,\mathrm{m}^{-3}
and an electron temperature of
T_{e}=1\,\mathrm{eV}
. The simulation timestep was set to
\Delta t=0.025\,\mathrm{fs}
to satisfy the Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) condition given the fine spatial resolution. The total simulation duration was
500\,\mathrm{fs}
, sufficient to capture multiple plasma periods (
\omega_{pe}^{-1}
) and the relevant collisional relaxation timescales.
In the first set of 20 ensemble simulations, the plasma was initialized in a thermal Maxwellian equilibrium. To sample the force fluctuations without perturbing the bath, we introduced "ghost" particles (species
tplus
and
tminus
in the input deck) with zero current contribution (
zero_current = T
). These passive tracers moved along ballistic trajectories at fixed velocities but did not generate their own electromagnetic fields.

We recorded the electric field fluctuations
E(t)
experienced by these tracers. The memory kernel
\gamma(t)
was then extracted via the inversion of the second FDT (Eq.
13
) using the Force Autocorrelation Function (FACF) averaged over the ensemble:

\gamma(t)=\frac{Q^{2}}{Mk_{B}T}\langle E(t)\cdot E(0)\rangle_{\mathrm{ensemble}}.

(22)

This procedure yielded the non-Markovian friction kernel solely from equilibrium noise.
In the second set of 20 ensemble simulations, we introduced active test particles with an initial directed momentum corresponding to a supersonic velocity
v_{0}=3.0v_{th}
. Unlike the passive tracers in Set A, these particles were fully coupled to the Maxwell solver, allowing them to excite wakes and experience self-consistent drag forces. We tracked the velocity decay
v(t)
of these projectiles to serve as the ground truth for validation.

The validation results are summarized in Fig.

. We compared the velocity decay observed in the brute-force drag simulations (Set B) against the theoretical trajectory predicted by solving the GLE (Eq.
16
) using the equilibrium-extracted kernel from Set A. The GLE prediction (dashed red line) shows remarkable agreement with the full non-equilibrium PIC results (solid black line) for the
3.0v_{th}
projectile. Crucially, the GLE captures not only the mean decay rate (stopping power) but also the transient oscillatory features at early times (
t<\omega_{p}^{-1}
). These oscillations correspond to the ringing of the plasma wake formation, a non-Markovian memory effect that is completely absent in standard Chandrasekhar or Balescu-Lenard slowing-down models.

This agreement quantitatively verifies that the equilibrium memory kernel, when properly Doppler-shifted, contains the complete nonlinear transport physics required to describe high-speed drag.

V
conclusion

In this work, we have presented a rigorous statistical mechanical framework for calculating non-linear transport coefficients in driven systems without relying on direct non-equilibrium simulations. By reformulating the drag problem within the context of the GLE, we demonstrated that the dissipative forces acting on a high-speed projectile are not distinct dynamical entities, but are mathematically equivalent to a kinematic sampling of the bath’s equilibrium fluctuations.

The central result of this study is the Doppler-Shifted FDT. We showed that for systems governed by linear response, the non-Markovian memory kernel is invariant under Galilean transformations. Consequently, the full velocity-dependent stopping power curve can be reconstructed
ab initio
from the force autocorrelation function of a single stationary particle. This insight unifies the diffusive, Ohmic friction at low velocities (Stokes’s Law) and the inertial, wake-dominated drag at high velocities (Chandrasekhar limit) into a single geometric operation on the equilibrium spectrum.

We validated this formalism by applying it to the canonical problem of a heavy ion traversing a collisionless Maxwellian plasma. We derived an explicit Gaussian memory kernel from the Vlasov dielectric response and showed that its Markovian (ballistic) limit exactly reproduces the Chandrasekhar dynamical friction formula
Chandrasekhar1943
. Furthermore, we recovered the exact non-Markovian equation of motion, which captures transient history forces that are often neglected in standard collisional models
Spitzer1962
;
Balescu1960
. While the standard characterization of stopping power requires a brute force approach involving
\mathcal{O}(N)
non-equilibrium simulations to map the drag curve point-by-point
Zwicknagel1999
, ours reduces this cost to a single
\mathcal{O}(1)
equilibrium simulation. Moreover, by extracting transport properties from a thermal bath rather than a violently driven wake, this method avoids common simulation artifacts such as finite-size wake wrapping and numerical heating
Starrett2015
.

This framework also offers a promising path for exploring transport in regimes where analytical kinetic theories break down, such as Warm Dense Matter (WDM) and strongly coupled plasmas
Murillo2000
;
Daligault2019
. In these systems, where the assumption of binary collisions fails, the equilibrium memory kernel remains a well-defined and accessible quantity. Extending the Doppler-shifted FDT to these complex fluids could provide a first-principles method for predicting viscosities, stopping powers, and thermal relaxation rates that are critical for modern inertial confinement fusion efforts
Hu2024
.
