Assessment of the impact of Z-Spring appliances and clear aligners on salivary parameters and periodontal health in children: a randomized clinical trial
Authors: İrem Kapici, Ahmet Yalcin Gungor, Ozge Erken Gungor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRemovable appliances greatly affect oral hygiene and increase the amount of plaque retention. The aim of this prospective study was to evaluate the effects of anterior crossbite treatment with Z-spring appliance and clear aligners on periodontal health and salivary parameters.MethodsThirty children aged 7–12 years were divided into two equal groups for anterior crossbite treatment: Clear aligners (Aligner group) and Z-spring appliance (Z-spring group). Periodontal health was assessed using plaque index (PI), gingival index (GI), and bleeding on probing (BOP). Salivary flow rate, pH, and buffering capacity were also measured. Assessments were performed at baseline (T0) and post-treatment (T1).ResultsAligner group exhibited significant increases in PI (p = 0.003) and GI (p = 0.002) scores, alongside a reduction in salivary buffering capacity (p = 0.039). Z-spring group demonstrated significant increases in unstimulated (p = 0.008) and stimulated salivary pH (p = 0.008). Intergroup comparisons revealed higher PI (p = 0.002) and GI (p = 0.003) scores in Aligner group, while Z-spring group showed increased stimulated salivary flow rate (p = 0.039).ConclusionsClear aligner treatment negatively affected oral hygiene in children within a short treatment period. This study is the first to compare Z-spring appliances and clear aligners for anterior crossbite correction, providing novel insights into their effects on oral health.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov NCT06858033. Introduction

Anterior crossbite refers to an occlusal discrepancy characterized by the palatal positioning of the maxillary anterior teeth relative to the mandibular anterior teeth, which may arise from skeletal or dental etiologies. Skeletal anterior crossbites are predominantly associated with skeletal anomalies, such as mandibular prognathism and deficient midfacial height. In contrast, dental anterior crossbites result from abnormal axial inclination of the maxillary anterior teeth [1].

Dental anterior crossbites, caused by the restriction of maxillary incisor movement by the mandibular incisors, generally do not self-correct and thus require intervention. The optimal treatment period is reported to be between 8 and 11 years of age, when root development and tooth eruption are still ongoing². Various treatment modalities are employed, including Hawley appliances with springs, composite inclined planes, fixed or removable acrylic planes, and stainless steel or composite crowns applied to the anterior teeth [2–4].

The Z-spring appliance is a conventional and effective removable appliance used in the treatment of anterior crossbite. It consists of a precisely contoured Z-shaped spring fabricated from stainless steel wire, positioned palatal to the affected tooth. Upon activation, the spring delivers a gentle and consistent force against the lingual surface of the tooth, guiding it labially into the correct position within the arch. The appliance is anchored by a rigid acrylic palatal plate, which also functions as a posterior bite plane to disclude the posterior teeth and eliminate occlusal interference, thereby facilitating unimpeded tooth movement. Retention is typically enhanced through Adams clasps on the first molars and a supplemental labial bow [5, 6] (Fig. 1).

In recent years, treatment approaches have expanded with the introduction of clear aligners⁷⁸. Owing to the aesthetic limitations of alternative methods, clear aligners offer a comfortable and well-tolerated option for treating anterior crossbite during mixed dentition [7, 8].

However, the placement of fixed or removable appliances in the oral cavity can significantly hinder oral hygiene and promote dental plaque accumulation¹¹. The material composition, surface roughness, and surface energy of the appliance influence dental plaque adherence. These factors can also alter salivary flow rate, pH, and buffering capacity [9, 10].

This study aims to evaluate and compare the effectiveness of Z-spring appliances and clear aligners in the management of anterior dental crossbite in children, along with their effects on periodontal health and salivary parameters.

Methodology

Study design

This study was designed as a two-group, parallel, randomized clinical trial with a 1:1 allocation ratio. The manuscript has been prepared in accordance with the CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) guidelines to ensure transparent and standardized reporting of clinical trial methodology.

Ethical aspects and registration

The study was reviewed and approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Akdeniz University Faculty of Medicine and the Turkey Medicines and Medical Devices Agency of the Ministry of Health, Turkey. This study was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06858033) on 20 January 2025, following the completion of data collection. Prior to treatment, the medical histories of all participants were recorded in case report forms, and informed consent was obtained from their parents. All patients were diagnosed with dental anterior crossbite by an experienced orthodontist based on a comprehensive orthodontic examination, including cephalometric radiographs (lateral cephalometric analysis [ANB angle within ± 2° normal range]), panoramic radiographs, intraoral and extraoral photographs. Clear aligner therapy was performed using the Crystal Aligner system (Orthoclear Health Technologies Co., Antalya, Turkey), while Z-spring appliances were custom-fabricated through the collaboration of a pediatric dentist and an orthodontist.

Sample description

The sample size was determined using G*Power 3.1 (Franz Faul, Universität Kiel, Germany) based on the primary outcome variable, the Plaque Index (PI). A priori power analysis assuming a large effect size (d = 1.25), a significance level of α = 0.05, and a statistical power of 90% indicated that a total of 30 participants were required, with 15 participants allocated to each group.

Randomization

A total of 59 patients presenting with anterior crowding were initially screened for participation in the study. Of these, 21 patients were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria, and 8 patients voluntarily withdrew. Consequently, 30 patients were enrolled in the study. The CONSORT flow chart is shown in Fig. 2.

Fig. 2CONSORT flow chart

CONSORT flow chart

To ensure unbiased allocation, the 30 participants were randomly assigned to one of two groups using a computer-generated randomization method implemented in Python. Aligner group (n = 15) was designated for clear aligner treatment, while Z-spring group (n = 15) was assigned to Z-spring appliance therapy. Randomization was performed using Python’s random module, which facilitates random assignment (https://docs.python.org/3/library/random.html).

Clinical measurements and monitoring

Prior to the initiation of orthodontic therapy, all patients received comprehensive oral hygiene instruction and motivation during their first appointment. Furthermore, strict instructions were given for both appliances, mandating a minimum wear time of 22 h per day, with removal permitted only for meals and the consumption of beverages other than water. To ensure adherence to these protocols and to maintain motivation, all patients were scheduled for regular follow-up visits at approximately 10-day intervals for routine appliance management (activation of the Z-spring or progression to the next clear aligner tray). During each visit, oral hygiene was reinforced, and clinical monitoring was performed.

The formal clinical examinations for this study were then performed to assess periodontal health. The Plaque Index (PI) was used to evaluate the thickness of plaque at the gingival margin [13]. The Gingival Index (GI) was employed to assess the severity of gingival inflammation based on criteria of color, consistency, and bleeding tendency [14]. Bleeding on probing (BOP) was recorded as a binary parameter (present/absent) within 20 s after probing [15]. Salivary parameters, including unstimulated flow rate, pH, and buffering capacity, were also evaluated. All measurements were conducted by a single examiner (a pediatric dentist) at two points: before treatment (T0) and upon completion of treatment (T1).

Saliva collection and analysis

Salivary parameters were assessed under standardized conditions in a quiet clinical setting between 9:00 AM and 12:00 PM. Participants were instructed to refrain from eating, drinking, or performing oral hygiene procedures for at least two hours prior to testing. All measurements were conducted using the GC Saliva-Check Buffer Kit (GC America, Inc., Alsip, IL, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocols.

Unstimulated saliva analysis

For unstimulated salivary flow rate assessment, a visual method was used, in which participants turned their lower lip inward and the examiner recorded the time (in seconds) for saliva droplets to appear on the lip mucosa under controlled lighting conditions. A prolonged salivary droplet formation time exceeding 60 s was classified as indicative of low unstimulated flow rate, while times below this threshold were considered normal [16].

Unstimulated salivary pH was measured immediately after the flow rate assessment. Participants were asked to gently spit the collected saliva into a sterile collection container. A pH indicator strip was then dipped into the sample for exactly 10 s. The resulting color change was compared to a standard color chart under neutral white light conditions. Based on the kit’s classification, the pH values were categorized as follows: highly acidic (pH 5.0–5.8, red zone), moderately acidic (pH 6.0–6.6, yellow zone), and healthy/normal (pH 6.8–7.8, green zone) [10, 16, 17].

Stimulated saliva analysis

After a 30-minute rest period to eliminate residual effects, stimulated saliva parameters were assessed. Participants chewed 1 g of paraffin gum for 30 s, then spit all produced saliva into a graduated collection tube and continued chewing for a full 5 min. The total volume was recorded to determine the stimulated saliva flow rate, and the results were classified as very low (< 3.5 mL), low (3.5–5 mL), or normal (>5 mL) according to clinical criteria [18].

Stimulated saliva pH was measured using the same colorimetric method as for unstimulated samples. The pH strip was dipped into freshly collected, stimulated saliva for 10 s, and the results were interpreted using a standardized color scheme under consistent lighting conditions.

Buffering capacity was determined by applying a drop of stimulated saliva to each of the three sections of the specialized test strip from the kit. After exactly 2 min of application, the color changes were scored according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Buffering capacity was categorized as: very low (scores 0–5), low (6–9), and normal/high (10–12) based on established clinical thresholds [19].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (v23.0, IBM Corp., Chicago, IL, USA). Numerical data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (for normally distributed data) or median [interquartile range] (for non-normally distributed data), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. The Shapiro–Wilk test was applied to assess normality. Between-group comparisons were performed using the independent samples t-test (normal data) or Mann–Whitney U test (non-normal data), while within-group comparisons (baseline vs. post-treatment) were analyzed using the paired t-test (normal data) or Wilcoxon signed-rank test (non-normal data). Categorical variables were analyzed with the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. All tests were two-tailed, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 30 participants aged 7–12 years were included in the study and completed the protocol with no dropouts. The sample comprised 10 females and 20 males. The mean age of the entire cohort was 8.8 (± 1.75) years. The groups were similar in size but showed a difference in age distribution. The mean age in the clear aligner group (6 females, 9 males) was 8.1 (± 1.37) years, while the mean age in the Z-spring group (4 females, 11 males) was 9.6 (± 1.77) years.

Critically, the two groups were well-matched at baseline in terms of the initial malocclusion severity. The mean initial negative overjet was − 2.2 mm (range: −2.7 to −1.7) in the clear aligner group and − 2.1 mm (range: −2.7 to −1.9) in the Z-spring group, with no statistically significant difference between them (p = 0.53).

A significant difference in the average treatment duration was observed between the two modalities. The mean treatment time for the clear aligner group was 3.21 (± 0.75) months, which was notably longer than the 1.59 (± 0.89) months recorded for the Z-spring group.

All participants attended their scheduled follow-up appointments regularly, confirming excellent adherence to the clinical protocol.

Discussion

The timely correction of anterior dental crossbite in the mixed dentition is crucial to prevent functional imbalances, aesthetic concerns, and potential complications in the developing dentition. This study specifically compared the effects of two treatment modalities (clear aligners and the Z-spring appliance on oral health during this essential corrective process. The observed changes in periodontal and salivary parameters are directly attributable to the anterior crossbite correction and the distinct biomechanical properties of each appliance. In this study, the findings conclusively demonstrate the specific oral biomarker responses elicited by treating this malocclusion with two fundamentally different approaches.

The treatment of anterior crossbite in children using clear aligners is a relatively new and increasingly popular approach [20]. This method minimizes aesthetic concerns and provides a comfortable and highly tolerable treatment option compared to traditional removable appliances. Clear aligners help maintain periodontal health, prevent demineralization of teeth, and avoid speech impairments caused by the bulk of traditional removable appliances [21–23].

Previous studies on clear aligners have reported mixed results regarding periodontal health. While some studies on adult populations have shown aligners to be favorable over fixed appliances [24–26], the findings in this pediatric cohort demonstrated the opposite. This discrepancy may be primarily due to the critical factor of patient age and compliance. Adult patients likely possess more matured oral hygiene habits and a higher degree of motivation, allowing them to maintain hygiene around aligners. In contrast, children, as in this study, may underestimate the need for meticulous cleaning due to the removable and seemingly “invisible” nature of aligners, leading to increased plaque accumulation. Furthermore, the longer treatment duration in the aligner group (3.21 months) compared to the Z-spring group (1.59 months) provided a prolonged period for potential neglect, exacerbating the effect.

This interpretation is supported by the integrative review of Partouche et al. (2021), which concluded that factors like patient compliance and oral hygiene instruction might be more decisive than the appliance type itself [9]. While their review found lower plaque accumulation with aligners in mixed-age studies, it also highlighted significant methodological heterogeneity, including variations in patient age a key differentiator of this study. In line with the present findings, a significant worsening in both PI and GI scores was observed in the Aligner group, while BOP remained unchanged. The lack of significant change in BOP suggests that the inflammatory response may lag behind the initial plaque accumulation and gingival irritation in this short-term context. The comparison between groups further revealed that this deterioration in periodontal health (PI and GI) was significantly greater in the Aligner group than in the Z-spring group.

It can be proposed that beyond patient-dependent factors, the appliance design itself is a major contributor to this outcome. Unlike the localized Z-spring appliance, the full-arch coverage of the clear aligner creates a vast, sheltered ecosystem that is ideal for plaque biofilm development, especially if not cleaned meticulously. This design fundamentally alters the oral environment, limiting salivary flow and natural cleansing mechanisms over the entire dentition, which appears to be particularly detrimental in a less compliant pediatric population.

Clear aligners are designed with smooth surfaces, and each aligner is replaced every 10–14 days. Thus, biofilm retention is expected to be lower compared to fixed or traditional removable appliances [27–29]. However, in this study, children and parents may have underestimated the importance of oral care due to the frequent aligner changes, leading to increased PI and GI scores in the Aligner group. This suggests that the theoretical advantage of aligner smoothness and exchangeability can be negated by user behavior, particularly in a pediatric population where parental supervision and patient motivation are variable. The act of replacing the appliance may have been misperceived as a substitute for active cleaning, reducing the vigilance required for maintaining oral hygiene. Consequently, the stagnant oral environment under the aligner, combined with potentially inadequate brushing, likely facilitated greater plaque accumulation and gingival inflammation than anticipated, overriding the biomaterial benefits proposed in previous adult studies.

In the Z-spring group, significant increases were observed in both unstimulated and stimulated salivary pH values. This favourable shift in the oral environment, along with the maintenance of periodontal health (as evidenced by non-significant changes in PI and GI), can be mechanistically linked to the appliance’s pronounced effect on salivary function. The pivotal evidence comes from the direct comparison between the study groups: the Z-spring group exhibited a significantly greater increase in stimulated salivary flow rate compared to the Aligner group (Table 4).

A compelling mechanism can be proposed to explain these combined findings: The bulkier, rigid design of the Z-spring appliance acts as a potent mechanical stimulant to the salivary glands. This resultant increase in salivary flow likely conferred a dual protective effect. First, the enhanced mechanical flushing action (“cleansing effect”) would have facilitated the removal of food debris and nascent plaque, thereby limiting their accumulation and contributing to the stability of the PI and GI scores. Second, the increased volume and availability of saliva effectively neutralized acids, explaining the significant rise in pH.

In contrast, the Aligner group, which lacked this stimulatory effect on flow rate, experienced a significant buildup of plaque and gingival inflammation, likely due to the combination of a retentive appliance design and the absence of this natural cleansing mechanism.

Limitations

The limitations of this study should be considered. First, the treatment duration was not standardized but was determined by achieving the therapeutic goal (correction of the crossbite). This clinical reality resulted in a significantly longer treatment time for the Aligner group. While this reflects actual clinical practice, the extended period of aligner use may have contributed to the observed differences in oral hygiene outcomes.

Second, despite the robust computer-generated randomization process, baseline imbalances in some parameters (PI, GI) were observed a known possibility in randomized trials. Our primary analysis strategy, which focused on within-patient changes from baseline, was specifically chosen to mitigate the potential impact of these initial differences.

Finally, patient compliance with appliance wear and oral hygiene protocols was based on patient and parent reporting. An attempt was made to standardize this factor through strict, uniform oral hygiene instructions provided to all participants.

Conclusion

This study revealed that PI and GI scores were significantly higher with clear aligners compared to Z-spring appliances. This age-specific result is critical because younger patients may have less developed manual dexterity and oral hygiene habits, potentially increasing the impact of appliance design on plaque accumulation. Therefore, optimal appliance selection may depend not only on the type of malocclusion but also on the patient’s age and maturity. This is the first study to compare conventional removable appliances with clear aligners in the treatment of anterior crossbites, providing valuable evidence for clinical practice and contributing to the existing literature.
