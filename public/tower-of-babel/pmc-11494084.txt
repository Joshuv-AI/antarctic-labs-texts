Investigating compatibilization of polyoxymethylene/styrene-butadiene-styrene immiscible blend through addition of hydroxylated graphene
Authors: Seyed Alireza Sadri, Mahdi Arefkhani, Parsa Dadashi, Amir Babaei, Mohammad Abbasi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe main objective of this work was to use hydroxyl-functionalized graphene as a compatibilizer for an immiscible blend. A neat polyoxymethylene/styrene-butadiene-styrene binary blend was prepared at a constant ratio (80/20) and then was compounded with different loading amounts of hydroxyl-functionalized graphene (0.25, 0.5, 0.75, and 1 wt%). The formation of droplet-matrix morphology during blending was observed in microscopy images. A deep understanding of the compatibility was inspected through studying the mechanical, rheological, and microstructural properties. By inspecting the localization of nanoparticles, triple functions of hydroxyl-functionalized graphene as a compatibilizing/reinforcing/lubricating agent were elucidated. The mechanical properties showed that the best compatibility with the ultimate performance was related to the nanocomposite containing 0.25 wt%. Furthermore, the electrical conductivity of the prepared nanocomposites was investigated. Thermodynamic/kinetic studies showed the tendency of hydroxyl-functionalized graphene to disperse droplets, however as the loading value increases, the probability of its presence in the matrix also increases, creating conductive pathways for conductive purposes. The lowest resistance and highest volume of electrical conductivity (8.4 × 10−6 S.cm−1) were shown by 1 wt% FG.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-76507-6.Keywords: Polyoxymethylene/Styrene-butadiene-styrene blend, Graphene, Compatibility, Mechanical/Electrical propertiesSubject terms: Mechanical and structural properties and devices, Synthesis of graphene, Mechanical properties, Nanocomposites, Mechanical properties, Polymer characterization, Synthesis of graphene, Mechanical and structural properties and devices, Nanoparticles, Nanoscale materials, Synthesis and processing

Introduction

It is widely accepted that almost no single polymer can provide the required performance in modern engineering applications, which often require high strength and toughness simultaneously. Polymer blend structures, i.e., materials consisting of at least two matrix phases, are developed as a basic strategy to meet such a need1,2. The properties of the polymer blend can be tuned by changing the composition ratio and the type of polymer. Additionally, the use of various fillers to create new and desirable properties as an innovative approach to adapt the capabilities of the polymer matrix in different applications is highly favored by the academic and industry communities3–6.

Polyoxymethylene (POM) as an interesting class of engineering thermoplastic exhibits some key factors and desirable properties such as high mechanical strength and rigidity, good corrosion/fatigue resistance, low friction, excellent self-lubricating properties, moldability, and dimensional stability. It is well-established that POM can be a good substitute for metals in a wide range of applications due to its high stiffness and strength7,8. However, it also possesses some limitations such as limited UV resistance, chemical sensitivity, thermal-oxidative degradation, flammability, and sensitivity to notch and impact. It can be relatively categorized as a polymer with brittle behavior compared to some other engineering plastics9,10. Needless to say, the brittle nature of POM limits its application in some industries11. Accordingly, looking for an economical and facile approach is required and should be considered to accomplish tougher and high-impact resistance properties. It is well-established that the addition of an elastomeric phase to a rigid matrix can increase the toughness behavior and elongation at break12. In this scenario, plentiful studies have documented the modification of toughened POM with various elastomers8,11,13–16.

Styrene-butadiene-styrene (SBS) is a type of synthetic copolymer rubber with a linear structure that is commonly used in various applications. SBS has excellent elasticity and flexibility, making it a popular choice for applications that require impact resistance17–19. Herein, we chose this kind of copolymer to make POM tougher and more flexible. It should be mentioned that these two polymers are thermodynamically immiscible and blending them make a system with phase separate morphology that exhibits relatively poor mechanical performance as a consequence of weak interfacial interaction between them10. To reduce the immiscibility between the polymer phase, compatibilization is required, which means the introduction of a third component that reduces the interfacial tension between phases20. Therefore, searching for a third component as a low-cost compatibilizing agent is highly required to extend the performance of immiscible polymer blends to a high degree. To the best of our knowledge, so far, there is only one paper on the thermal stability of the POM binary blends containing SBS (1, 3, and 5 wt%) in which the tensile testing properties are also mentioned10. Surveying the literature represents the existence of several compatibilization strategies that have been extensively investigated8. Beyond the conventional method (i.e., surface modification of dispersed elastomeric phase (e.g., malleated SBS/SEBS, etc.), reactive coupling agents, etc.), one approach that has been intensively investigated recently is the role of nanoparticles as potential compatibilizers for immiscible blends12,21.

As an interesting 2D material, graphene has received significant attention due to its extraordinary properties, such as super specific surface area, high thermal/electrical conductivity, superb mechanical strength, self-lubricating, etc. It is characterized by a single-layer hexagonal structure arranged by sp2 C-atoms22–24. Various researches have shown that graphene and its derivatives can be used as a compatibilizing agent in polymer blends with poor interfacial interaction and phase-separated morphology12,20,25–31. This occurs as a consequence of the fact that graphene can minimize the interfacial tension between the two polymer phases32.

Accordingly, the main focus of the current research was to investigate the compatibility of the POM/SBS binary blend with the incorporation of hydroxyl-functionalized graphene (FG) to improve the performance of mechanical properties and impact toughness. We previously suggested the potential of this type of nanoparticle as a great low-cost nanofiller for the polymer industry22.

To the best of our knowledge, the effect of FG addition on the properties of the POM/SBS binary blend has not been investigated so far. Therefore, following our previous work12, we aimed to investigate whether the introduction of FG as a third component in the POM/SBS blend without any reactive agent can lead to a decrease in interfacial tension between the two phases and an increase in mechanical properties or not. Such a valuable effort has been made to introduce SBS as a suitable alternative elastomer and to use graphene derivatives, here FG, as an innovative idea to adapt the POM blend to make tougher ones.

Therefore, in the first step, FG were synthesized and analyzed through XRD, Raman, FTIR, and TGA analyses. Secondly, to find a deep understanding regarding the correlation between the microstructure and the performance of the POM/SBS binary blend, the effect of adding FG at different loading values (0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1 wt%) on the final performance of the system was investigated in terms of thermal, mechanical, rheological and electrical properties.

Materials and methods

Materials

Commercial grades of polyoxymethylene (POM, FORMOCON® FM090, Taipei, Taiwan) with MFI = 9.0 gr/10 min (190 °C/2.16 kg), melting temperature of 165 °C and styrene-butylene-styrene (SBS-3566, shore A = 70, LCY. Taiwan) with styrene and butadiene content 30% and 70% by weight, respectively, were purchased. Natural graphite flake was supplied from Beijing Invention Biology Engineering & New Material Co., China. Sulfuric acid (H2SO4 98%) and sodium persulfate (Na2S2O4 99%) were obtained from Merk, Germany.

Synthesis of FG

The FG was synthesized according to our prior research work using the improved bubble-exfoliation method22. Briefly, 30 gr of natural flake graphite was dried at 80 °C in a vacuum oven for 24 h. Next, 5 gr of it was added to the concentrated H2SO4 (150 mL) into the 1000 mL glass beaker, and the mixture was stirred for 5 min. Then, Na2S2O8 (50 gr) was slightly added to the mixture under stirring. In this stage, the color of the mixture was changed from black to navy blue (Fig. S1a) due to intercalation of the gallery of staked graphite layers. After the complete addition of the oxidizer, the mixture was heated to 80 °C under vigorous stirring to initiate functionalization. In this stage, the mixture started to expand at 40 °C and ended at 80 °C. This mixture turned into a yellow paste after the functionalization process (Fig. S1b), and then the vessel was placed at the ambient conditions to cool completely. Next, the paste was slowly added to a beaker containing cold distilled water. It is suggested that this step be done under the hood and the paste is added to the water in several steps since it releases gas as a byproduct. After that, the FG was filtered using filter paper and was washed several times with deionized water to reach natural pH. Next, the synthesized FG was completely dried in an oven at 55 °C for 120 h. These processes were repeated for the remaining flake graphite. It should be noted that the density of synthesized FG was remarkably diminished compared to the natural graphite (Fig. S1c). Processing of binary POM-SBS blend and its nanocomposites

The POM is susceptible to hydrolysis under melt-processing conditions in the presence of small amounts of water, hence before melt-processing, all polymer pellets and synthesized FG were kept in an oven at 80 °C for 4 h. Firstly, the neat binary blend with constant mixing ratio (POM/SBS 80/20) were melt-mixed using a single-screw extruder machine (Brabender® GmbH & Co. KG) and used for further steps. The process profile temperature and screw speed were set at 150-150-165-185-200-210 °C and 50 rpm, respectively. The prepared neat binary blend was reinforced with various loading values of FG (0.25, 0.5, 0.75, and 1 wt%) via a two-screw extruder counter-rotating machine (COLLIN Lab & Pilot Solutions GmbH). In this step, the process profile temperature and screw speed were set at 170-175-180-180-185-190-190-210 °C and 110 rpm, respectively. The prepared compounds were kept in an air-blowing cabin (80 °C, 24 h) and then were hot-molded in certain shapes (Fig. S2) using an injection molding machine (FTN90, ZHEJIANG SOUND MACHINERY MANUFACTURE Co., Ltd.) with a thermal profile of 180-190-200-200 ºC and mold temperature of 40 °C.

Characterization

The X-ray diffraction patterns of the raw graphite, prepared FG, and nanocomposites were inspected through a wide-angle X-ray diffractometer (WAXS, Philips PW1730) with CuKa radiation of wavelength (λ) = 0.15406 nm. Raman spectroscopy analysis (TAKRAM P50C0R10-532 nm diode laser, TEKSAN Instruments, Iran) was used to investigate the functionalization of graphite. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was conducted using a STA PT 1600, Linseis, Germany instrument (heating rate at 20 °C/min under air atmosphere). Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrometer (Perkin Elmer-Spectrum RXIFTIR) was used to confirm the functionalization. To study the cryo-fracture surface of neat blends and prepared nanocomposites scanning electron microscopy (SEM, NEON 40, ZEISS) was used. The samples were fractured in liquid nitrogen and before SEM visualization, the fractured surface of the samples was coated with gold. 120 visible droplets were considered to measure the numerical mean radius (Rn) and volumetric mean radius (Rv) of the droplets. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was carried out via a JEOL (JEM-2100). Optical microscopy (OM) was used to visualize the morphology of the prepared nanocomposites. For this purpose, to prepare a thin layer of each sample, a very small amount of each injected sample was cut and placed between two commercial microscopic glasses and placed in an oven at 200 ºC for 30 min. Tensile and flexural properties of the prepared samples were performed based on ISO 527-2 and ASTM D790-17, respectively, using a tensile testing machine (GOTECH testing machine, Taiwan) with speed of 50 mm.min−1. Notched impact resistance of the prepared neat blends and nanocomposites was measured based on ASTM D256 through Impact tester (SIT-20E, Iran). Five samples were tested and the average mean was reported for the mechanical properties. Thermal behavior of the samples was investigated using a differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) (Perkin Elmer Inc., USA) under a nitrogen atmosphere at a linear heating rate of 15 °C/min. To remove the thermal history of the material, the samples (14 mg) were heated from 25 to 200 °C. Then, the sample was cooled to room temperature to observe the crystallization temperature. Afterward, the second heating was carried out to 200 °C to observe the melting temperature. Rheological measurements were fulfilled using a rheometric mechanical spectrometer (RMS, Anton Paar, MCR302) with parallel-plate geometry (gap 1 mm, diameter 25 mm) at 190 °C under a nitrogen environment. The experiments were performed in the frequency range of 0.01–1000 rad.s−1 and strain of 1%. In the simulation section, the molecular structure of POM and SBS along with FG was manually constructed aiding the BIOVIA Material Studio software (Fig. S3). A POM molecular chain with six repeat units (C10H18O7) and an SBS copolymer consisting of three styrene and seven butadiene units (C54H86) were developed. A triclinic graphene sheet (C70H40O9, lattice parameters: 44.3 Ǻ × 44.3 Ǻ × 12.1 Ǻ; α = β = 90 ◦, γ = 120 ◦) with random hydroxyl functional groups was constructed on one side of its surface. Geometry optimization was carried out to minimize the energy of initially constructed structures of FG, SBS, POM, POM/FG, and SBS/FG. Energy calculations were conducted using the semi-empirical parameterized model 6 (PM6) implemented in Gaussian 16 software. The configuration of the polymers was chosen in such a way as to consider the effect of side branches and functional groups with the FG. The initial interaction distance between the polymers segment and FG was set to 4 Ǻ34. The DC electrical conductivity was measured using a Keithley 610 C solid-state electrometer with the two-point probe method. Impedance spectroscopy was performed using a Sharif solar potentiostat, EIS mode in the frequency range of 100–100,000 Hz at room temperature.

Results and discussion

Characterization of synthesized FG

Figure 1(a-b) shows the XRD pattern of graphite and synthesized FG. Graphite showed two peaks at around 26.5º (002) and 54.7º (004), implying the stacked galleries of graphite layers and crystalline order alongside the c-axis. By comparing the XRD patterns of FG and graphite, a significant change in the intensity of the peak at 26.5º is observed in a completely reduced form. In addition, the peak at 54.7º completely disappeared in the pattern of FG. This is high likely due to the disruption of the ordered structure of graphite. Noteworthy, the peak position of FG showed no displacement which notifies that the functionalization and partial exfoliation occurred along the c-axis12,22,31. This significant decrease in peak intensity indicates a decrease in the number of sheets and reaching graphene with a few-layer structure. In some cases, the synthesized FG showed a structure similar to the XRD pattern of thermally reduced graphene oxide (rGO)35. But rGO is the result of additional processes such as chemical or thermal on GO. Our graphene is synthesized rapidly without any reduction process. Therefore, between low-oxide GO or functionalized graphene, we preferred to name it as the second one which was finally selected as functionalized graphene (FG).

XRD patterns of FG and graphite (a-b). Raman spectra of FG and graphite (c-d). Synthesized FG powder (e). FTIR spectra of FG and graphite.

To verify the functionalization, Raman spectra of the graphite and synthesized FG were inspected (Fig. 1(c-d)). Graphite is characterized by three main bonds; D, G, and 2D bonds, which appear in the range of 1350, 1588, and 2719 cm−1, respectively3,24,35. In the case of the FG spectrum, the D bond was intensified which is suggestively due to the changes in the sp2 to sp3 hybrid state arising from the functionalization process. Also, the peak position of the D and G bond of FG was changed compared with the graphite counterpart which could be ascribed to the functionalization of graphite platelets3. Moreover, the 2D bond of FG exhibited an upshift and considerable decrease in the intensity which can be related to the reducing the number of stacked layers as a result of functionalization36. In addition, the intensity ratio of D bond / G bond (ID/IG) and 2D bond / G bond (I2D/IG) is generally assumed as a functionalization parameter. In this view, a higher value of ID/IG and a lower value of I2D/I is considered as functionalization degree and the presence of defects in the graphene structure, as showed in the case of synthesized FG compared to the graphite which could be related to the successful functionalization of graphite33,37. Figure 1e represents the prepared FG particles in powder form.

FTIR analysis was conducted to inspect the functional groups of the FG to verify the successfulness functionalization (Fig. 1f). The graphite can be characterized by some main characteristic peaks consisting of -OH (3448 cm−1), CH2 (2924 –2856 cm−1), C = C (1639 cm−1), C = O (1740 cm−1), and C-H (aromatic ring out of plane vibration at 650–900 cm−1)38–40. After the functionalization of graphite and turning to the FG, some noticeable changes were observed in the FG spectrum. The C = O, CH2, and C-H groups disappeared, while new characteristic peaks such as C-O (1100 cm−1) and C-OH (1400 cm−1) emerged in the FG spectrum, suggesting the functionalization of graphite platelets22,38.

Further confirmation of the functionality was received by inspecting TGA/DTG thermographs (Fig. S4(a-b)). The major characteristic mass loss step related to carbon combustion was observed on their decomposition temperature: > 500 °C. The graphite and FG showed one DTG peak with the Tmax values of ~ 697 °C and ~ 871 °C, respectively. The distinguished Tmax can be attributed to the maximum external heat energy required to overcome the strong bonding within the carbon lattice structure. The observed lower Tmaxin FG elucidated that less energy is needed to overcome weaker sp3 hybridized carbon with a high-density defect after functionalization41,42.

The obtained outcomes from XRD, Raman, FTIR, and TGA-DTG elucidate the successful functionalization of hydroxylated graphene (noted as FG), which is consistent with our previously published research work22.

It can be observed that by addition of FG to the blend, firstly radius of dispersed droplets increased and decreased in the following when the FG loading was increased. The obtained higher values of Rn and Rv for the nanocomposite having 0.25 wt% of the FG compared with the others, even the neat counterparts, can be associated to the dominant localization of the FG in the SBS droplet phase (as discussed in Sect. 3.2), consequently higher viscosity and elasticity leading to the suppressing droplet breakup as well as higher tendency to droplets coalescence12,26. On the other hand, in the case of the other nanocomposites, a downtrend was observed in the values of Rn and Rv (up to 1 wt%). This means, although the FG have tendency to be localized in the SBS phase, when it was used in higher loading amounts, all of them have insufficient time to fully migrate to the SBS droplets (preferred phase), or even the droplets do not have enough space to accept more FG. Thus, portion of the FG migrates toward the SBS droplets and most of them are located at interface and some of them remains within POM matrix through kinetic mechanism. Accordingly, FG located in the matrix and specially in the interface hinder the coalescence of the droplets (confine the movement of SBS) through kinetic and thermodynamic mechanisms, resulting in lower size of the dispersed droplets12,25,26,45. TEM images of the nanocomposites filled with 0.5 and 0.75 wt% (Fig. S5(a-b)) illustrate how FG are localized in the matrix and droplets. It can be seen that the FG well dispersed within the system. The bright areas and dark areas correspond to the matrix and dispersed droplets in which FG is localized, respectively. Furthermore, better visibility can be seen from the OM images shown in Fig. 4a. Some important facts can be pointed from the OM images out: (1) most of the FG are localized in droplets, (2) the size of the droplets has become smaller with the increase in the loading percentage of FG, and (3) overfilling has led to the formation of agglomerates in the system. Rheological characterization

Rheological characterization is crucial for researchers to evaluate the dispersion and localization of nanoparticles in polymer blends. Additionally, it aids in characterizing the morphology of these materials and understanding the correlation between microstructure and properties12,46,47.

Figure 5(a-c) shows complex viscosity (η*), storage modulus (G′), and loss modulus (G′′) versus frequency (ω) for POM/SBS samples and their nanocomposites containing different amounts of FG. According to Fig. 5(a-b), the η* and G′ of the neat binary blend at low frequencies were higher than those filled with FG. It can be due to the elasticity arisen from the interface of two polymers which is reflected in the none-Newtonian behavior in η* and higher G′ at low frequencies. In the low frequency region, both η* and G′ were significantly decreased at 0.25 wt% and slightly increased for 0.5 and 0.75 wt%. Surprisingly, for the case of POM/SBS-1, the η*was significantly decreased. As it is known, sources which can contribute in the elasticity of such multi-phase system are related to the polymers-FG and FG-FG interactions, interfacial elasticity, elasticity induced by the deformed dispersed droplets, and also sliding effects of FG particles on each other26. The lowest η* and G′ values at low frequencies stands for the case of POM/SBS-0.25 among the studied samples. Such observation can be suggestively related to the reduction of elastic sources arising from interfacial relaxation of entangled chains as a consequence of FG-platelets localization at the interface25. However, by increasing the loading amounts of FG up to 0.75 wt%, other sources of elasticity such as FG-polymer are formed, which means the tethered chains increased and the chains mobility confines. Such an elastic source is clearly evident from the change in the Newtonian behavior of η* and the increase in the upward slope at low frequencies related to the filled nanocomposites with 0.75 and 1 wt%. In the case of POM/SBS-1, the notable reduction at whole frequency region in η*is suggestively due to the dominant sliding effect gallery of FG25.

Plots of complex viscosity (a), storage modulus (b), loss modulus (c), damping factor (d) versus frequency, Han plots (log G′-log G″) (e), and weighted relaxation spectrum versus relaxation times at two scales (f-g) for neat POM/SBS blend and its nanocomposites with different content of FG nanoparticles.

Moreover, a slightly decrease in the G′′ for the nanocomposites containing 0.25, 0.5, and 0.75 wt% at low frequency and a notable reduction in whole frequency regions for 1 wt% was observed (Fig. 5c), manifesting the reduction of elasticity sources arising from interfacial elasticity of two polymeric phases in below 1 wt% FG and lubricating effect of FG at 1 wt%. It would be more discussed in the relaxation sections in the following.

Figure 5d shows tan δ versus angular frequency of POM/SBS and its nanocomposites containing different amounts of FG. Introducing 0.25 wt% of FG leads to an increment in tan δ compared to neat POM/SBS. This means that a liquid-like (viscous) behavior was amplified, showing that the sources of elasticity at the interface are notably reduced. Increasing the loading amounts of FG up to 0.75 wt% exhibited a down trend across low frequencies in the tan δ value compared to 0.25 wt%, meaning a solid-like (elastic) behavior is somewhat intensified resulting from formation of more tethered chains within the system46. In the case of POM/SBS-1, tan δ was decreased significantly compared to its counterparts, even than the neat blend, which indicates the highest elastic behavior arising from the overfilling of the blend with FG, and formation of FG-FG network structure.

Figure 5e represents Han plots (log G′-log G″) of the neat blend and its nanocomposites. At low frequencies, a slope of 2 is related for homogeneous system, while any deviation from this slope suggests sample heterogeneity12,46,48. The neat binary blend showed a nonhomogeneous system with a slope of 0.99 and was turned into a more homogeneous system by introducing only 0.25 wt% FG. This homogeneity somewhat decreased (as the slope decreased) by increasing the loading amount of FG. The long relaxation of interconnected network of polymer chains between the matrix and dispersed droplets is the main reason elasticity source in compatible blends47. The increase in homogeneity of the system (lower slop deviation) shows that FG was able to reduce this source of elasticity through localizing at the interface and favoring the relaxation of interconnected chains by suggestively sliding effect of aggregated platelets up to 0.75 wt%. As loading amounts increased up to 1 wt%, the elasticity sources originating from polymers-FG and FG-FG interactions amplifies and an elevated elasticity is observed. The outcomes show a similar trend with the abovementioned results.

To achieve a deep understanding of the elastic contribution of each component, the weighted relaxation time curve of all prepared samples was inspected which is shown in Fig. 5(f-g). The obtained results show two shoulders with one sequence at long times for all samples (Fig. 5f). The polymer chains-relaxation and shape-relaxation of dispersed SBS droplets are associated by the first and second shoulder, respectively. Moreover, the tail at long times is related to the relaxation of interconnected chains resided in the interface zone12,48. It can be seen that the elastic contribution of matrix-related parts is almost comparable for the case of neat binary blend and those reinforced with 0.25, 0.5, and 0.75 wt%, while a significant decrease is observed in the case of POM/SBS-1. It could be suggested that the formation of nanoparticle-polymer interaction and subsequent difficulty in the mobility of the polymer chains occurred is compensated by the sliding effect of added FG up to 0.75 wt%. However, in the case of POM/SBS-1 nanocomposite, the number of nanoparticles residing in the matrix is as much as enough to form some stacks and aggregate. Accordingly, a higher sliding effect would result in faster relaxation of polymer chains and consequently lower intensity in the chain-related relaxation peak. On the other hand, the elastic contribution of dispersed SBS droplets was promoted by incorporating the FG (higher intensity and longer relaxation) up to 0.75 wt%. This elucidates the fact that there is a strong tendency for FG to migrate into SBS dispersed droplets during processing time (as discussed from thermodynamic/kinetic point of view, Sect. 3.2). On the other hand, the amount of relaxation of the droplets did not change significantly with the increase of FG, which indicates that the droplets can accept a certain amount of FG and not more. A noticeable decrease in the intensity of peaks along with movement towards lower time can be observed for the case of POM-SBS-1, which implies the lowest contribution of matrix-related elastic and dispersed droplet parts (viscose dominant behavior). It can be ascribed to the overfilling and formation of lots of FG-aggregates and consequently lubricating effect of stacked particles, easy mobility of the chains and faster relaxation.

The interesting point is that the presence of FG in this binary system caused a faster relaxation at the interface of two polymers (Fig. 5g) when the added FG is lower than 1 wt% as a consequence of the lubricating effect of FG stacks localized at the interface. However, in the case of POM/SBS-1, superior tethered chains at the FG-polymers interface overcome the lubricating effect of FG, and solid-like behavior is enhanced. In other words, the most elastic response observed in G′ (Fig. 5b) of this sample is related to the elastic contribution of the interface, not to the chains or relaxation of deformed droplets.

A transition from a liquid-like (viscous) to solid-like (elastic) behavior can be detected by a crossover point (angular frequency wherein G′ = G′′)26,49. From Fig. S6, the liquid-like behavior is more prominent for all studied samples, since G′′ > G′. The neat binary blend exhibited a crossover point at a frequency of 266 1/s, resulting from the formation of a percolated network structure through interaction between the matrix and dispersed droplets which confines the segmental motions of the chains at the interphase, indicating larger relaxation time. As the loading amount of FG increases, the crossover occurs at higher frequencies, as follows:

POM/SBS-0.25: 280 1/s < POM/SBS-0.5: 288 1/s < POM/SBS-0.75: 294 1/s < POM/SBS-1: 343 1/s.

This means a shorter relaxation time due to the presence of stacked FG platelets especially at the interphase which made the movement of the chains easier and the elastic behavior occurs later. Another fact that can be obtained from crossover point is the rheological percolation. It is described by a sudden change in rheological properties due to the onset of solid-like behavior in the melt state (a transition from liquid-like to solid-like behavior), which is triggered by network formation in polymer/FG composites50. This transition can be detected from crossover point, where the G′ lies upper that G′′51. As the filler content increased (Fig. S6), the slope of G′ decreased and a second crosspoint formed at low frequency. This indicates that FG cannot form a percolating network at bellow of 1 wt% and only reaches close to percolation threshold formation. Another confirmation that can cover the formation of rheological percolation threshold is the tan δ where it is less than 1 (tan δ < 1)50. As shown in Fig. 5d, no rheological percolation threshold has been formed in the nanocomposites.

Electrical properties

AC electrical conduction and impedance spectroscopy can be used as an informative method for evaluating the microstructure of polymer nanocomposites55. Figure 6a shows impedance or resistivity (R) for POM/SBS and its nanocomposites containing different concentrations of FG. According to Fig. 6a, the R decreased with frequency, indicating the capacitive-like behavior of the samples at high frequencies. This means that by increasing the frequency, the capacitance behavior of samples dominates resistive-like behavior56. The nanocomposites containing FG nanoparticles up to 0.75 wt% showed similar R across a range of frequencies. However, with an increase in FG concentration to 1 wt%, the R decreased significantly, which is in good harmony with the rheological and morphological results. This behavior was observed because, for the sample containing 0.25 wt%, the nanoparticles were mostly localized in the SBS droplet phase, and the number of FG particles in the POM phase was low, preventing the formation of conductive path networks. As the nanoparticle concentration was increased up to 1 wt%, the tendency of nanoparticles localizing in the POM phase increased, consequently forming more conductive path networks and resulting in a sudden decrease in impedance56,57.

As can be seen, the electrical conductivity of POM/SBS was equal to 1.9 × 10–15S.cm-1, which is in the range of insulating materials58. The did not change significantly with the incorporation of 0.25 wt% FG, indicating that a conductive path had not yet formed at this concentration55,59. However, for the POM/SBS-0.5 sample, the electrical conductivity increased by 2 orders of magnitude, it remained within the range of insulating materials though58. This increase in electrical conductivity is a result of the initial transition from insulating to conductive behavior. Further incorporation of FG particles increased the electrical conductivity by 4 orders of magnitude for the sample containing 0.75 wt% FG. This demonstrates the formation of bridging between particles and tunneling of electrons between conductive particles55. POM/SBS-0.75 can be applicable for antistatic packaging and coating applications59. Further addition of FG leads to the formation of more conductive paths and the domination of direct contact mechanisms over tunneling55, increasing the to 8.4 × 10−6S.cm-1, which is applicable in sensors and EMI shielding applications. A monotonic increase in electrical conductivity and its significant upsurge for the POM/SBS-1 sample are consistent with the microscopic results and rheological evaluations. This is another agreement for the localization of the FG within the interface and matrix when the loading values increase as discussed comprehensively above. Furthermore, as previously mentioned, more FG-FG interactions at 1 wt% of nanoparticles resulted in the domination of the direct contact mechanism over electrical tunneling, thereby enhancing electrical conductivity55.

Mechanical properties

The Young’s modulus (YM), tensile strength (TS), elongation at breaks (EB), and flexural modulus (FM) of the neat POM, prepared POM/SBS blend as well as those reinforced with different loading of the FG (0.25, 0.5, 0.75, and 1 wt%) are shown in Fig. S9 and Table 2. It is clear that by the addition of SBS into the POM matrix the YM (52.2%), TS (46.2%), and FM (34.4%) significantly decreased and EB (15.2%) moderately increased. Probably, the presence of both SBS (as an elastomeric phase) and improper interaction between two polymers caused this performance. Therefore, adding this elastomer to the POM matrix reduced the brittleness of this polymer and led to strengthening the toughness. As can be seen, the reinforcement of this binary blend with FG led to a slight increase in TS and a notable increase in EB. The possible reason behind such performance is that FG located at the interface facilitated both the interfacial interaction between two phases and stress concentration on the droplets, which makes more efficient strain-induced deformation on the SBS droplets through bridging between two phases12. Overall, it can be seen from Table 2 that the nanocomposite reinforced with 0.25 wt% of FG demonstrates the highest amount of EB (103.1% increase) along with acceptable TS (6.5% increase), corroborating the compatibilizing effect of FG when located at the interface. Higher amounts of FG not only decrease the size of dispersed droplets through thermodynamical and kinetical mechanisms as a result of interfacial localization but also increase the chance of formation of aggregate and stacks of FG platelets can provide premature fracture and lower EB. The interesting point is the lower stiffness of POM/SBS-1 nanocomposite compared to the other samples which can be related to the lubricating effect of stacked FG which facilitates the movement of entangled polymer chains. Furthermore, the onset of a non-linear increase in YM as a function of the filler content indicates the percolation61. From Fig. S10, no sudden change in the YM of the prepared nanocomposites is observed, which means that no mechanical percolation threshold occurred in the range of fillers used.

The Izod notched impact test was carried out to determine the impact resistance (IR) of the studied samples and the obtained results are shown in Table 2. Thermal properties

DSC analysis was conducted to investigate the addition effect of FG on the POM/SBS binary blend from melting and crystallization point of view. The melting and crystallization thermographs of the studied samples are shown in Fig. S11(a-b), and the corresponding data are listed in the Table S4. Interestingly, it can be seen that, the melting and crystallization temperature of the immiscible POM/SBS blend decreased slightly with the addition of FG. Against what we were expecting from the addition of nanofiller of which their addition promotes the nucleating and consequently higher melt and crystallization, FG addition decreased Tc and Tm. This often occurs due to restricted polymer chains mobility, which implies changes in the crystal structure with the addition of the filler.

Conclusion

The compatibility of a neat POM/SBS binary blend at the 80/20 weight ratio was promoted by using a synthesized unique structural hydroxyl-functionalized graphene (FG). Although, the tendency of the FG was predicted towards the SBS droplets aiding two concepts (theoretical thermodynamic prediction and kinetic simulation). As the loading value increases, more FG is located at the interface and within the matrix which was elucidated with the microstructural and rheological studies. Additionally, the triple functions of FG (compatibilizing/reinforcing/lubricating role) were investigated in this system through comprehensive rheological and mechanical properties. In this view, the elastic contribution of interface-located nanoparticles was deeply inspected. Moreover, the toughness behavior of the POM was improved by using SBS and FG as a compatibilizer. Interestingly, the obtained range of volume electrical conductivity for POM/SBS-FG polymer blend nanocomposites presents opportunities for their use in electrostatic coating, packaging, sensors, and EMI shielding.
