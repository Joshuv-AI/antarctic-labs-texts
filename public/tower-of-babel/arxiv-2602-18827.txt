Steady states and dynamics of a higher dimensional thin film equation
Shen Bian

Abstract
We study a higher-dimensional thin film equation that incorporates competitive effects between aggregation and repulsion, where repulsion is modeled by fourth-order diffusion and aggregation by backward second-order degenerate diffusion, with a degenerate diffusion exponent m > 0 m>0 . We first conduct a systematic analysis of the existence and geometric properties of steady-state solutions for all m > 0 m>0 , revealing a critical threshold m ∗ = ( d + 2 ) / ( d − 2 ) m^{*}=(d+2)/(d-2) for variational compactness and solution structure. For 0 < m < m ∗ 0<m<m^{*} , we then prove that, under natural regularity constraints, radially decreasing steady states coincide with both the extremals of the Gagliardo-Nirenberg-Sobolev inequality and the global minimizers of the free energy. Moreover, we establish the uniqueness of such steady states for m ≠ 1 + 2 / d m\neq 1+2/d . Furthermore, in the supercritical regime 1 + 2 / d < m < m ∗ 1+2/d<m<m^{*} , we identify a sharp threshold given by the L m + 1 L^{m+1} norm of the unique radial steady-state solution, which distinguishes between global existence for initial data below the threshold and finite-time blow-up for initial data above the threshold. The main contribution of this work is to use steady-state solutions as a theoretical pivot to construct a unified analytical framework that connects parameter classification, variational structure and dynamic behavior. This framework elucidates how the regularity barrier prevents infinite energy descent and selects stable equilibrium states, and thus predicts the global evolution of the system, thereby providing a unified variational principle for understanding steady-state selection and dynamic bifurcations in such higher-order degenerate diffusion equations.

Introduction

This paper investigates the fourth-order nonlinear degenerate parabolic equation

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u_{t}=-\nabla\cdot(u\nabla\Delta u)-\nabla\cdot(u\nabla u^{m}),&x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},~t>0,\\
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)\geq 0,&x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\end{array}\right.

where the diffusion exponent
m>0
. This model is governed by a fundamental competition between two nonlinear effects: the fourth-order term enforces stabilization and repulsion, whereas the backward second-order diffusion term drives destabilizing aggregation.

In one spatial dimension, equation (
1
) is a special case of the long-wave unstable generalized thin film equation

\displaystyle u_{t}+(u^{n}u_{xxx})_{x}+(u(u^{m})_{x})_{x}=0,

(3)

where
u(x,t)
represents the height of the evolving free surface. The exponents
m
and
n
correspond to the powers in the destabilizing second-order diffusive term and the stabilizing fourth-order diffusive term respectively. This class of models appears in many physical systems involving fluid interfaces
[
50
,
53
]
. When
n=1
and
m=1
, the equation describes a thin jet in a Hele–Shaw cell
[
1
,
28
,
36
]
. When
n=m=3
, the equation describes fluid droplets hanging from a ceiling
[

]
. Over the past fifteen years, these models have also been the subject of rigorous and extensive mathematical analysis
[

,

,

,

In this paper, we consider equation (

\displaystyle u_{0}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\cap L^{m+1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}),\quad\nabla u_{0}\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}),\quad\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|x|^{2}u_{0}(x)dx<\infty.

(4)

Equation (
1
) possesses several a priori estimates. The total mass is conserved

\displaystyle M:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}u_{0}(x)dx=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}u(x,t)dx.

(5)

It also admits a dissipation principle for the free energy
F(u)
, defined by

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}F(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|\nabla u|^{2}dx-\frac{1}{m+1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}u^{m+1}dx,\\[8.53581pt]
\frac{dF(u)}{dt}=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}u\left|\nabla(\Delta u+u^{m})\right|^{2}dx\leq 0,\end{array}\right.

where the second identity is obtained by multiplying equation (
1
) by
\mu=-\Delta u-u^{m}
. The main property of this energy is that it consists of two terms: the gradient energy
\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|\nabla u|^{2}dx
and the potential energy
-\frac{1}{m+1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}u^{m+1}dx
. The difference in signs allows for a competition between aggregation and repulsion.

Note that equation (
1

is a solution to equation (
1
), then the scaled function

\displaystyle u_{\lambda}(x)=\lambda^{\frac{2}{m-1}}u(\lambda x,\lambda^{\frac{4m-2}{m-1}}t)

(8)

\displaystyle\|u_{\lambda}\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}=\|u\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})},\qquad p:=\frac{d(m-1)}{2}.

(9)

For the critical case
m=1+2/d
, the scaling reduces to the mass-invariant scaling
u_{\lambda}=\lambda^{d}u(\lambda x,\lambda^{d+4}t)
, under which the second-order aggregative term
\lambda^{dm+d+2}\nabla\cdot(u_{\lambda}\nabla u_{\lambda}^{m})
and the fourth-order repulsive term
\lambda^{2d+4}\nabla\cdot(u_{\lambda}\nabla\Delta u_{\lambda})
balance. For the subcritical case
0<m<1+2/d
, aggregation dominates repulsion for small
\lambda
, which prevents spreading. While for large
\lambda
, repulsion dominates aggregation, thus precluding blow-up. On the contrary, for the supercritical case
m>1+2/d
, aggregation dominates repulsion for large
\lambda
, and finite-time blow-up may occur. For small
\lambda
, repulsion dominates aggregation, allowing infinite-time spreading. This type of competition between aggregation and repulsion is a common feature in many nonlinear models, including Hele-Shaw flow, stellar collapse, chemotaxis models, and the nonlinear Schrödinger equation (see
[
11
,
13
,
15
,
17
,

,

,

For the subcritical case
0<m<1+2/d
, this is the repulsion-dominated regime. In one dimension,
[
9
]
proved that blow-up is impossible for
m<3
. For higher-dimensional cases with
m<1+2/d
,
[
24
]
established the global existence of gradient-flow solutions for general initial data.

For the critical case
m=1+2/d
, this is the fair competition regime where repulsion and aggregation balance. It has been the subject of extensive research in the existing literature, yielding a wealth of significant results in both one dimension and higher dimensions. In one dimension,
[
61
]
showed that there exists a critical mass
M_{*}
defined by the best constant of the Sz. Nagy inequality
[
51
]
, such that solutions exist globally in time if the initial mass is less than
M_{*}
, whereas there are blowing-up solutions if the initial mass is larger than
M_{*}
. Moreover,

is the mass of the compactly supported steady states, which have also been studied in detail. Furthermore, in higher dimensions,
[
47
]
determined a critical mass
M_{c}
via the optimisers of a generalised Nagy inequality and proved finite-time blow-up of solutions for
M>M_{c}
. We point out that establishing the global existence of solutions in higher-dimensional cases is far more challenging than in one-dimensional cases due to stricter regularity estimates, including discussions on compactness and estimates of higher-order regularity.
[
24
]
employed the classical variational minimising movement scheme and the flow interchange technique to derive suitable regularity for weak solutions and proved the global existence of solutions for
M<M_{c}
.

For the supercritical case
m>1+2/d
, this is the aggregation-dominated regime. In one dimension,
[
8
]
established the existence of finite-time blow-up solutions for
m>3
, and properties of steady states were also investigated in
[

42
,
43
]
. For higher-dimensional cases, to the best of our knowledge, there is little information regarding global existence or finite-time blow-up. This is seemingly due to the fact that mass conservation no longer plays a primary role, and the geometric structure of possible singularities is far more complex than that in lower-dimensional cases. The main goal of this paper is to investigate the global existence and finite-time blow-up of solutions in the supercritical case in higher dimensions, as well as the existence of steady states for all
m>0
.

We emphasize that, in contrast to this well-documented mass-critical exponent, there exists a distinct and fundamental energy-critical exponent
m=(d+2)/(d-2)>1+2/d
within the supercritical regime, which has received little attention to date. In fact, substituting the scaling (
8
) into the free energy
F(u)
yields

\displaystyle F(u_{\lambda})=\lambda^{\frac{2(m+1)}{m-1}-d}F(u).

(10)

The free energy is invariant when
m=(d+2)/(d-2)
. For this reason, we refer to
(d+2)/(d-2)
as the energy-critical exponent. More importantly, it also serves as the critical exponent for the steady-state solutions to (
1
) (see Section
2.1
below).

In this work, we first investigate the existence and fundamental properties of steady-state solutions for all
m>0
, and prove that no radial steady-state solutions exist when
m>(d+2)/(d-2)
. For the range
0<m<(d+2)/(d-2)
, we further show that the extremal functions of the Gagliardo-Nirenberg-Sobolev (GNS) inequality (see (
13
) below) under the corresponding constraints are exactly radially decreasing, compactly supported steady-state solutions, which also attain the global minimum of the free energy. Notably, this steady-state solution is unique when
m\neq 1+2/d
. In particular, for
1+2/d<m<(d+2)/(d-2)
, we establish a sharp threshold based on the
L^{m+1}
-norm of the unique steady-state solution: solutions exist globally if the initial data lies below this threshold, while finite-time blow-up occurs if the initial data exceeds it.

The contribution of this work is threefold: to characterize the variational structure of the free energy, to identify ground-state solutions as extremals of the GNS inequality, and to establish a sharp threshold that separates global existence from finite-time blow-up. Our results also clarify the role of the regularity barrier in stabilizing equilibrium states. These results are summarized in Table
1
below.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section

4

5

Dynamical solutions for

We first state the definition of the weak solution that will be used in Section

Definition 2.1

(Weak solution)
.

Let

be the initial data satisfying (

) and

\displaystyle u\in L^{\infty}\bigl(0,T;H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\bigr)\cap L^{\infty}\bigl(0,T;L^{1}\cap L^{m+1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\bigr),

\displaystyle u^{1/2}\nabla\Delta u\in L^{2}\bigl(0,T;L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\bigr)

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\psi u(\cdot,t)\,dx-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\psi u_{0}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle-\frac{m}{m+1}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\Delta\psi\,u^{m+1}\,dx\,ds.

(21)

Our third result concerning dynamical solutions in the supercritical regime is presented as follows:

1.

For
1+2/d<m<(d+2)/(d-2)
, we assume
F(u_{0})<F(U_{\ast})
.

(i)

If
\|u_{0}\|_{m+1}>\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}
, then there exist solutions that blow up in finite time. See Theorem
5.4
.

(ii)

If
\|u_{0}\|_{L^{m+1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}<\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}
, then the solution exists globally in time, and the second moment satisfies
\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|x|^{2}u(\cdot,t)dx=+\infty
. See Theorem

.

We remark that for
F(u_{0})<F(U_{\ast})
, the case
\|u_{0}\|_{m+1}=\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}
cannot occur. See Remark
5.2
. For
F(u_{0})>F(U_{\ast})
, we cannot exclude two possibilities: either the second moment of solutions diverges, or the

-norm of solutions is unbounded. If both are bounded, the solution may converge to
U_{\ast}
. We will comment further on these issues in Section

.

Our result provides a sharp criterion for the dichotomy between global existence and finite-time blow-up of dynamical solutions for
1+2/d<m<(d+2)/(d-2)
. The principal difficulty we address stems from the fact that, unlike in the critical case, mass conservation alone fails to give adequate scaling control. Our approach overcomes this by systematically exploiting the variational structure of steady-state solutions and their deep connection with the free energy functional. However, for
m\geq(d+2)/(d-2)
, the lack of embedding compactness prevents the steady-state solutions from serving as a tool for determining the behavior of dynamical solutions, leaving an open problem in that case. While previous studies have mainly focused on the critical exponent
m=1+2/d
, the present work extends the analysis to a broader supercritical range, thereby connecting the well-understood mass-critical theory with the less explored energy-critical regime.

Remark 2.2

(Physical interpretation: potential well and threshold dynamics)
.

The above result reveals a sharp threshold behavior dictated by the potential-well structure in gradient-flow dynamics:

1.

Initial energy below the steady-state energy

F(u_{0})<F(U_{\ast})
guarantees that the system has already “crossed the ridge”. Its evolution can only proceed
downhill (energy decreasing)
and cannot climb back over the energy barrier.

2.

The initial
L^{m+1}
norm

\|u_{0}\|_{m+1}
determines
which downhill path
the system follows.

(a)

If
\|u_{0}\|_{m+1}<\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}
, the system enters a
gentle valley (inside the potential well)
where
\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{m+1}
and
\|\nabla u(\cdot,t)\|_{2}
remain uniformly bounded. Hence, the free energy is uniformly bounded from below and decreases to a limit
F_{\infty}<F(U_{\ast})
. The second moment necessarily becomes unbounded, implying that the mass escapes to spatial infinity and the solution vanishes locally on every compact set. Whether the profile, after a suitable translation, converges to a steady state with energy lower than
F(U_{\ast})
remains an open question. It may instead approach a singular measure at infinity.

(b)

If
\|u_{0}\|_{m+1}>\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}
, the system slides into a
steep ravine (outside the potential well)
, where attraction dominates, the gradient grows rapidly, and finite-time blow-up (rupture) occurs.

This threshold mechanism shows that blow-up depends not only on how low the initial energy is, but more crucially on whether the
initial concentration
, measured by the
L^{m+1}
norm, exceeds a critical level. Physically, the norm
\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}
marks the
branching point of the downhill path
. Below it, dissipation (surface tension) dominates. Above it, attraction (self-focusing) takes over. The condition
F(u_{0})<F(U_{\ast})
locks the system into a “downhill mode”, while the concentration threshold sharply decides between convergence to a regular equilibrium and finite-time singularity.

Qualitative properties of the steady profiles

This section is devoted to the analysis of steady-state solutions to (
1

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot(U_{s}\nabla\Delta U_{s})+\nabla\cdot(U_{s}\nabla U_{s}^{m})=0,\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\left(U_{s}\nabla\mu_{s}\right)=0,\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

in the sense of distributions.

\displaystyle\mu_{s}=\overline{C}\quad\text{a.e. in }\Omega,

0,&|x|\geq 2,\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}U_{s}\nabla\mu_{s}\cdot\nabla\left(\mu_{s}\psi_{R}^{2}\right)dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}U_{s}\left|\nabla\mu_{s}\right|^{2}\psi_{R}^{2}dx-2\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}U_{s}\mu_{s}\psi_{R}\nabla\mu_{s}\cdot\nabla\psi_{R}dx

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}U_{s}\mu_{s}^{2}\left|\nabla\psi_{R}\right|^{2}dx\to 0.

Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(\mu_{s}-\frac{1}{M}\int_{\Omega}\mu_{s}U_{s}dx\right)\psi(x)dx=0,\quad\text{for any }\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega).

\displaystyle-\Delta U_{s}-U_{s}^{m}=\frac{1}{M}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left|\nabla U_{s}\right|^{2}dx-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}U_{s}^{m+1}dx\right)=\overline{C}\quad\text{a.e. in }\Omega.

Proposition 3.3

Let

Step 2

Step 3
(Proof of (iii))  For

Remark 3.5

When

For non-star-shaped domains, the situation differs. When

4
GNS extremals and global minimizers of the free energy for
0<m<\frac{d+2}{d-2}

Based on the above analysis, Theorem

(iii) implies that for

, there are no radially symmetric steady-state solutions to (
1
). In this section, we focus on the case
0<m<(d+2)/(d-2)
. Section
4.1
establishes the equivalence between radially symmetric steady-state solutions under mass constraints and the extremal functions of the GNS inequality (see (
52

1
).

4.1
Extremal functions of the Gagliardo-Nirenberg-Sobolev inequality

For
0<m<(d+2)/(d-2)
, we first introduce the functional

\displaystyle J(u):=\frac{\|u\|_{m+1}^{\alpha+2}}{\|u\|_{1}^{\alpha}\|\nabla u\|_{2}^{2}},\quad\alpha=\frac{d+2-(d-2)m}{dm}.

(49)

Then we have the following lemma:

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
0<m<(d+2)/(d-2)
. For
u\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\cap H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, then

\displaystyle C_{*}:=\displaystyle\sup_{u\neq 0}J(u)<\infty.

(50)

Proof.

Applying the Sobolev inequality
[
46
, pp.202]
and Hölder’s inequality for
0<m<(d+2)/(d-2)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|u\|_{m+1}^{\alpha+2}\leq\|u\|_{1}^{\alpha}\|u\|_{2d/(d-2)}^{2}\leq S_{d}^{-1}\|u\|_{1}^{\alpha}\|\nabla u\|_{2}^{2},

(51)

where
S_{d}
is the best Sobolev constant. Consequently,
C_{*}
is finite and bounded from above by
S_{d}^{-1}
.
∎

We next turn to the existence of optimizers for the GNS inequality

\displaystyle\|u\|_{m+1}^{\alpha+2}\leq C_{*}\|u\|_{1}^{\alpha}\|\nabla u\|_{2}^{2},\quad\alpha=\frac{d+2-(d-2)m}{dm},

(52)

which can be proved by similar arguments as in
[
13
, Lemma 3.3]
.

Proposition 4.2
.

(Extremals of the GNS inequality)  Let
0<m<\frac{d+2}{d-2}
and
\alpha=\frac{d+2-(d-2)m}{dm}
. Assume
u\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\cap H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. Then the equality in (
52
) holds if and only if
u=\lambda V(\mu|x-x_{0}|)
for any real numbers
\lambda>0,\mu>0
and some
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, where the nonnegative function
V
is a radially symmetric, non-increasing function that solves the Euler-Lagrange equation

\displaystyle-\Delta V=\frac{\alpha+2}{2}\frac{\|V\|_{m+1}^{\alpha+1-m}}{\|V\|_{1}^{\alpha}C_{*}}V^{m}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\frac{\|V\|_{m+1}^{\alpha+2}}{\|V\|_{1}^{\alpha+1}C_{*}}\quad\text{a.e. in supp}(V).

(53)

Proof.

We aim at proving that the equality in (
52
) can be achieved. For this purpose, we consider a maximizing sequence
\{u_{j}\}\in L^{1}\cap H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{j\to\infty}J(u_{j})=C_{*}.

(54)

In Step 1, we argue that the maximising sequence
\{u_{j}\}
can be assumed to be non-negative, radially symmetric and non-increasing for any
j\geq 0
. Subsequently, in Step 2, we establish that the sequence has a limit
V
such that the equality holds in (
52
). That is,

\displaystyle\lim_{j\to\infty}u_{j}=V,\quad J(V)=C_{*}.

(55)

Finally, the Euler-Lagrange equation solved by
V
is obtained in Step 3.

Step 1
(Radially symmetric, non-increasing assumption)  First, we may assume that
u_{j}
is a non-negative, radially symmetric, and non-increasing function for any
j\geq 0
. Indeed, the fundamental identity
\|\nabla u_{j}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\nabla|u_{j}|\|_{2}^{2}
from
[
35
, Lemma 7.6]
implies
J(|u_{j}|)=J(u_{j})
, so that
|u_{j}|
is also a maximizing sequence. Next, let
u_{j}^{*}
denote the symmetric decreasing rearrangement of
|u_{j}|
. Then Riesz’s rearrangement inequality
[
46
, pp.87]
ensures that

\|u_{j}^{*}\|_{1}=\|u_{j}\|_{1}
and
\|u_{j}^{*}\|_{m+1}=\|u_{j}\|_{m+1}
. Moreover, the classical Pólya-Szegő inequality
[
56
]
yields

\displaystyle\|\nabla u_{j}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|\nabla u_{j}\|_{2}^{2}.

(56)

Consequently, we have

\displaystyle J(u_{j})=J(|u_{j}|)=\frac{\|u_{j}\|_{m+1}^{\alpha+2}}{\|u_{j}\|_{1}^{\alpha}\|\nabla u_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}\leq\frac{\|u_{j}^{*}\|_{m+1}^{\alpha+2}}{\|u_{j}^{*}\|_{1}^{\alpha}\|\nabla u_{j}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}}=J(u_{j}^{*}).

This entails that
(u_{j}^{*})_{j}
is also a maximizing sequence.

Step 2
(Existence of the supremum)  We next turn to the convergence of the maximizing sequence. Indeed, since
u_{j}
is radially symmetric and non-increasing, we find that

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}u_{j}(x)dx=d\alpha_{d}\int_{0}^{\infty}u_{j}(r)r^{d-1}dr\geq d\alpha_{d}\int_{0}^{R}u_{j}(r)r^{d-1}dr\geq\alpha_{d}u_{j}(R)R^{d}

which gives
u_{j}(R)\leq CR^{-d}
. Moreover,
u_{j}\in H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, and the Sobolev inequality yields

\displaystyle C\|\nabla u_{j}\|_{2}\geq\|u_{j}\|_{2d/(d-2)}\geq\left(\alpha_{d}R^{d}\right)^{\frac{d-2}{2d}}u_{j}(R).

(57)

Hence, we have

\displaystyle u_{j}(R)\leq G(R):=C_{0}\inf\left\{R^{-d},~R^{-(d-2)/2}\right\},\quad\text{for any }R>0.

(58)

Since
u_{j}
is bounded on
(R,\infty)
for any
R>0
, we deduce from Helly’s selection principle
[
46
, pp.89]
that
there exist a subsequence of
{u_{j}}
(not relabeled for
convenience) and a non-negative, non-increasing function
V
such that
u_{j}\to V
point-wisely. Moreover, the condition
0<m<(d+2)/(d-2)
implies

\displaystyle\left\|G(|x|)\right\|_{m+1}^{m+1}=d\alpha_{d}\int_{0}^{\infty}G(r)^{m+1}r^{d-1}dr=C_{1}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{r^{(m+1)\frac{d-2}{2}}}r^{d-1}dr+\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r^{d(m+1)}}r^{d-1}dr\right]<\infty.

Then the conditions

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u_{j}\leq G(|x|),\\
\|G\|_{m+1}<\infty,\\
u_{j}\to V\quad\text{point-wisely}\end{array}\right.

together with the dominated convergence theorem ensure

\displaystyle\lim_{j\to\infty}\|u_{j}\|_{m+1}=\|V\|_{m+1}.

(59)

On the other hand, the point-wise convergence of
u_{j}
and Fatou’s lemma yield
\|V\|_{1}\leq\|u_{j}\|_{1}
and
\|\nabla V\|_{2}\leq\|\nabla u_{j}\|_{2}
. Hence, we arrive at

\displaystyle C_{*}=\lim_{j\to\infty}J(u_{j})=\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{\|u_{j}\|_{m+1}^{\alpha+2}}{\|u_{j}\|_{1}^{\alpha}\|\nabla u_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}\leq\frac{\|V\|_{m+1}^{\alpha+2}}{\|V\|_{1}^{\alpha}\|\nabla V\|_{2}^{2}}=J(V)\leq C_{*}.

(60)

Therefore, we conclude that
J(V)=C_{*}
.

Step 3
(Euler-Lagrange equation for
V
)  We have already shown that
V
is a radially symmetric and non-increasing function. Let us now derive the Euler-Lagrange equation for
V
. We introduce

\Omega=\left\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}~|~V(x)>0\right\}.

For any
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that
V+\varepsilon\varphi\geq 0
for
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
. Then a direct computation of

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=0}J(V+\varepsilon\varphi)=0

(61)

leads to

\displaystyle 0=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left(\Delta V+\frac{\alpha+2}{2}\frac{\|\nabla V\|_{2}^{2}}{\|V\|_{m+1}^{m+1}}V^{m}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\frac{\|\nabla V\|_{2}^{2}}{\|V\|_{1}}\right)\varphi(x)dx,\quad\text{for any }\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega).

(62)

This gives that
V
satisfies the following Euler-Lagrange equation

\displaystyle-\Delta V=\frac{\alpha+2}{2}\frac{\|\nabla V\|_{2}^{2}}{\|V\|_{m+1}^{m+1}}V^{m}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\frac{\|\nabla V\|_{2}^{2}}{\|V\|_{1}}\quad\text{a.e. in supp}(V),

(63)

Moreover, using

\displaystyle\|V\|_{m+1}^{\alpha+2}=C_{*}\|V\|_{1}^{\alpha}\|\nabla V\|_{2}^{2},

(64)

we can rewrite (
63
) as

\displaystyle-\Delta V=\frac{\alpha+2}{2}\frac{\|V\|_{m+1}^{\alpha+1-m}}{\|V\|_{1}^{\alpha}C_{*}}V^{m}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\frac{\|V\|_{m+1}^{\alpha+2}}{\|V\|_{1}^{\alpha+1}C_{*}}\quad\text{a.e. in supp}(V).

(65)

Finally, for any solution
V
to (
65
), the re-scaled function

\displaystyle W(x)=\lambda V(\mu x),\text{ for arbitrary }\lambda,\mu>0

(66)

also solves (
65
) and satisfies

\displaystyle J(V)=J(W).

(67)

This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

Particularly, taking

\displaystyle\lambda=\left(\frac{\alpha\|V\|_{m+1}^{m+1}}{(\alpha+2)\|V\|_{1}}\right)^{1/m},\quad\mu=\left(\frac{(\alpha+2)\|\nabla V\|_{2}^{2}}{2\|V\|_{m+1}^{m+1}}\lambda^{m-1}\right)^{1/2}

(68)

in (

), we obtain

\displaystyle-\Delta W=W^{m}-1,\quad x\in B(0,R).

(69)

For
m=1
, from Theorem
3.4
(i), we find that the extremal function satisfies

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}W^{\prime\prime}+\frac{d-1}{r}W^{\prime}+W=1,\quad 0<r<R,\\
W(0)<\infty,\quad W^{\prime}(0)=0,\\
W(R)=0,\quad W^{\prime}(R)=0.\end{array}\right.

A direct computation gives

\displaystyle W(r)=1-\frac{J_{\frac{d-2}{2}}(r)}{J_{\frac{d-2}{2}}(R)},

(73)

where

is the spherical Bessel function of the first kind of order

.

In particular, for

,

\displaystyle W(r)=1-\frac{R}{r}\frac{\sin r}{\sin R}.

(74)

Using

, we get

\displaystyle R\cos R-\sin R=0,\text{ i.e., }\tan R=R.

(75)

Consequently, recalling definitions (
15
) and (
17
), we are now ready to deduce the following result concerning the radial steady states and the extremal functions of the GNS inequality (

).

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
0<m<\frac{d+2}{d-2}
and
\alpha=\frac{d+2-(d-2)m}{dm}
. Then the following statements hold:

(i)

If
m\neq 1+2/d
, then in
\mathcal{Y}_{M,P_{\ast}}
, the GNS inequality (
52
) admits a unique optimizer
U_{\ast}
, which is the unique radially decreasing, compactly supported steady-state solution to (
1
).

(ii)

If
m=1+2/d
, then in
\mathcal{Y}_{M_{c}}
, the GNS inequality (
52
) admits infinitely many optimizers. These optimizers are radially decreasing, compactly supported steady-state solutions to (
1
), and form a one-parameter family under mass-invariant scaling.

Proof.

Step 1
(Proof of (i))  For
m\neq 1+2/d
, we restate the definition of
P_{\ast}
from (
18
) as

\displaystyle P_{\ast}=\left(\frac{\alpha+2}{2C_{*}M^{\alpha}}\right)^{\frac{1}{m-\alpha-1}}=\left(\frac{(d+2)(m+1)}{2dmC_{*}M^{\alpha}}\right)^{\frac{dm}{(dm-d-2)(m+1)}}.

(76)

Then, for a given solution
V_{0}(x)
to (
53
), we set

\displaystyle U_{\ast}(x)=\frac{1}{\lambda_{\ast}}V_{0}\left(\frac{x}{\mu_{\ast}}\right),

(77)

where

\displaystyle\lambda_{\ast}=\left(\frac{M}{\|V_{0}\|_{1}}\frac{\|V_{0}\|_{m+1}^{m+1}}{P_{\ast}^{m+1}}\right)^{1/m},\quad\mu_{\ast}=\left(\frac{\lambda M}{\|V_{0}\|_{1}}\right)^{1/d}.

(78)

It follows that
U_{\ast}
is also a solution to (
53
) satisfying

\displaystyle-\Delta U_{\ast}=U_{\ast}^{m}+\frac{1}{M}\frac{(d-2)m-(d+2)}{(d+2)(m+1)}\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}^{m+1}\quad\text{a.e. in supp}(U_{\ast})

with

\displaystyle\|U_{\ast}\|_{1}=M,\quad\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}=P_{\ast}.

(80)

This indicates that
U_{\ast}
is a radially symmetric, non-increasing steady-state solution to (
1
).

Next, applying Theorem
3.4
(i), we conclude that for
0<m<(d+2)/(d-2)
,
U_{\ast}
solves the free boundary problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-u^{\prime\prime}-\frac{d-1}{r}u^{\prime}=u^{m}+\frac{1}{M}\frac{(d-2)m-(d+2)}{(d+2)(m+1)}P_{\ast}^{m+1},\quad 0<r<R,\\[5.69054pt]
u^{\prime}(0)=0,\quad u(R)=u^{\prime}(R)=0\end{array}\right.

with
0<R<\infty
. It follows from
[
57
, Theorem 3]
that there is a unique solution to (
4.1
). This completes the proof of (i).

Step 2
(Proof of (ii))  For
m=1+2/d
, one has
\alpha+2=m+1
. Then, (
53
) reduces to

\displaystyle-\Delta V=\frac{\alpha+2}{2}\frac{1}{\|V\|_{1}^{\alpha}C_{*}}V^{m}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\frac{\|V\|_{m+1}^{m+1}}{\|V\|_{1}^{\alpha+1}C_{*}}\quad\text{a.e. in supp}(V).

(83)

Hence, a solution
V_{c}
to (

) is also a steady-state solution to (

) if and only if

\displaystyle\|V_{c}\|_{1}=M_{c}=\left(\frac{m+1}{2C_{*}}\right)^{1/\alpha}.

(84)

Thus,
V_{c}
satisfies

\displaystyle-\Delta V_{c}=V_{c}^{m}-\frac{m-1}{m+1}\frac{\|V_{c}\|_{m+1}^{m+1}}{M_{c}}\quad\text{a.e. in supp}(V_{c}).

(85)

Observe that equation (
85
) is scaling invariant: if
V_{c}
is a solution, then
\mu^{d}V_{c}(\mu x)
is also a solution for any
\mu>0
, with the same mass

. Therefore, the solutions of (

) form a one-parameter family of radial steady states. This completes the proof of (ii).
∎

4.2
Energy landscape and minimization properties of
U_{\ast}

In this subsection, using the optimizers of the GNS inequality (
52
), we proceed to investigate the global minimizers of the free energy
F(u)
. We first recall the following well-known result concerning the infimum of
F
(see
[
24
]
), and a brief proof is included for completeness.

Lemma 4.5

(Infimum of the free energy)
.

For
m>1+2/d
, we have

\inf_{u\in\mathcal{Y}_{M}}F(u)=-\infty.

(86)

Proof.

Consider the scaling
u_{\lambda}(x)=\lambda^{d}u(\lambda x)
for
\lambda>0
. A direct computation yields

\displaystyle F(u_{\lambda})=\frac{\lambda^{d+2}}{2}\|\nabla u\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{\lambda^{dm}}{m+1}\|u\|_{m+1}^{m+1}.

Since
m>1+2/d
implies
dm>d+2
, the negative term dominates as
\lambda\to\infty
, so
F(u_{\lambda})\to-\infty
. Thus, the infimum of
F
is
-\infty
.
∎

By Lemma
4.5
, the free energy
F(u)
is unbounded from below in the full space
\mathcal{Y}_{M}
. In addition, Proposition
3.1
indicates that any steady-state solution to (
1
) is a critical point of
F(u)
. Thus, steady-state solutions cannot be global minimizers of
F(u)
in this space. However, under suitable additional constraints, such steady states become global minimizers of the free energy. To state this precisely, we first define the set of steady-state solutions with mass
M
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{M}:=\{u\in\mathcal{Y}_{M}:u\text{ is any steady-state solution to }\eqref{tfsystem}\}.

(87)

Proposition 4.6

(Aggregation-dominated regime)
.

Let
1+\frac{2}{d}<m<\frac{d+2}{d-2},\alpha=\frac{d+2-(d-2)m}{dm}
, and
P_{\ast}
be defined by (
18
). Let
U_{\ast}
be the unique optimizer from Theorem
4.4
(i). Then the following statements hold:

(i)

U_{\ast}
is the unique global minimizer of
F(u)
in
\mathcal{Y}_{M,P_{\ast}}
.

(ii)

U_{\ast}
is the unique global minimizer of
F(u)
in
\mathcal{S}_{M}
.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\inf_{u\in\mathcal{Y}_{M,P_{\ast}}}F(u)=\displaystyle\inf_{u\in\mathcal{S}_{M}}F(u)=F(U_{\ast})=\frac{dm-(d+2)}{(d+2)(m+1)}P_{\ast}^{m+1}.

(88)

Proof.

We first state two results directly derived from Theorem
4.4
and Proposition
3.1
(iii):

\displaystyle u\text{ is an optimizer of the GNS inequality \eqref{GNS1} }

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\|u\|_{m+1}^{\alpha+2}=C_{*}\|u\|_{1}^{\alpha}\|\nabla u\|_{2}^{2},

(89)

\displaystyle u\text{ is a steady-state solution to \eqref{tfsystem} }

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\frac{dm}{m+1}\|u\|_{m+1}^{m+1}=\frac{d+2}{2}\|\nabla u\|_{2}^{2}.

(90)

Step 1
(Proof of (i))  For a given mass
M>0
and any
v\in\mathcal{Y}_{M,P_{\ast}}
, applying the GNS inequality (
52
), we get

\displaystyle F(v)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla v\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{m+1}\|v\|_{m+1}^{m+1}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2C_{\ast}M^{\alpha}}\|v\|_{m+1}^{\alpha+2}-\frac{1}{m+1}\|v\|_{m+1}^{m+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2C_{\ast}M^{\alpha}}P_{\ast}^{\alpha+2}-\frac{1}{m+1}P_{\ast}^{m+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{dm-(d+2)}{(d+2)(m+1)}\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}^{m+1}=F(U_{\ast}),

(91)

where we have used Corollary
3.2
. Thus, for any
v\in\mathcal{Y}_{M,P_{\ast}}
, it holds that
F(v)\geq F(U_{\ast})
. Equality in (
91
) is attained if and only if
v=U_{\ast}
, by virtue of (
89
). This completes the proof of (i).

Step 2
(Proof of (ii))  For any
w\in\mathcal{S}_{M}
, by the GNS inequality (
52
) and identity (
90
), we obtain

\displaystyle\|w\|_{m+1}^{\alpha+2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{*}M^{\alpha}\|\nabla w\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=C_{*}M^{\alpha}\frac{2dm}{(d+2)(m+1)}\|w\|_{m+1}^{m+1}

\displaystyle=\|w\|_{m+1}^{\alpha+2}\frac{\|w\|_{m+1}^{m-\alpha-1}}{P_{\ast}^{m-\alpha-1}}=\|w\|_{m+1}^{\alpha+2}\frac{\|w\|_{m+1}^{m-\alpha-1}}{\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}^{m-\alpha-1}}.

(92)

Since
m-\alpha-1>0
for
m>1+2/d
, we conclude that
\|w\|_{m+1}\geq\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}
for all
w\in\mathcal{S}_{M}
. Then Corollary
3.2
assures that

\displaystyle F(w)=\frac{dm-(d+2)}{(d+2)(m+1)}\|w\|_{m+1}^{m+1}\geq\frac{dm-(d+2)}{(d+2)(m+1)}\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}^{m+1}=F(U_{\ast}).

(93)

Equality in (
92
) holds if and only if
w=U_{\ast}
, thanks to (
89
). This completes the proof of (ii).
∎

Remark 4.7
.

Although
F(u)
is unbounded from below on
\mathcal{Y}_{M}
(Lemma
4.5
),
U_{\ast}
is radially decreasing and the unique global minimizer of
F(u)
in both
\mathcal{S}_{M}
and
\mathcal{Y}_{M,P_{\ast}}
that satisfies
F(U_{\ast})>0
. This makes
U_{\ast}
a saddle point in the full energy landscape: it attracts radial perturbations but is unstable under certain non-radial concentrations.
The boundedness of the gradient and
L^{m+1}
-norm acts as a regularity barrier that prevents the system from following energy-collapse paths,
thereby selecting the steady state
U_{\ast}
as an accessible and stable metastable configuration.

Proposition 4.8

(Fair competition regime)
.

Let
m=1+\frac{2}{d},\alpha=\frac{d+2-(d-2)m}{dm}
, and
M_{c}
be defined by (
16
). Then there exist infinitely many global minimizers of

, and these minimizers are radially decreasing, compactly supported steady-state solutions

to (

).

\displaystyle\displaystyle\inf_{u\in\mathcal{Y}_{M_{c}}}F(u)=F(u_{c})=0.

(94)

Proof.

For any

, the GNS equality (
52
) gives

\displaystyle F(u_{c})

since

. Moreover, Equality in (

) holds if and only if

∎

The precise value of the critical mass

Proposition 4.10

(Diffusion-dominated regime)

5
Global existence and finite-time blow-up

In this section, we use the characterization of
U_{\ast}
to investigate dynamical solutions to (
1
) in the supercritical regime
1+2/d<m<(d+2)/(d-2)
. We establish a sharp threshold determined by the
L^{m+1}
norm of
U_{\ast}
, such that
solutions blow up in finite time if the initial data lies above this threshold (Section
5.2
), whereas solutions exist globally if the initial data lies below the threshold (Section
5.3
).

5.1
A priori estimates for
F(u_{0})<F(U_{\ast})

We first derive a priori estimates under the energy condition
F(u_{0})<F(U_{\ast})
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
1+\frac{2}{d}<m<\frac{d+2}{d-2},\alpha=\frac{(d+2)-(d-2)m}{dm}
, and
U_{\ast}
be the unique optimizer from Theorem
4.4
(i). Assume that
u(x,t)
is a weak solution to (
1
) with an initial condition
u_{0}
satisfying (
4
), (
5
) and

\displaystyle F(u_{0})<F(U_{\ast}).

(100)

(i)

If

\displaystyle\|u_{0}\|_{m+1}<\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1},

(101)

then there exists a constant
\mu_{1}<1
such that the corresponding weak solution
u
satisfies

\displaystyle\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{m+1}<\mu_{1}\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}

(102)

for all
t>0
.

(ii)

If

\displaystyle\|u_{0}\|_{m+1}>\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1},

(103)

then there exists a constant
\mu_{2}>1
such that the corresponding weak solution
u
satisfies

\displaystyle\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{m+1}>\mu_{2}\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}

(104)

for all
t>0
.

Proof.

First, applying the GNS inequality (
52
), we infer from the expression of
F(u)
that

\displaystyle F(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla u\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{m+1}\|u\|_{m+1}^{m+1}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2C_{*}M^{\alpha}}\|u\|_{m+1}^{\alpha+2}-\frac{1}{m+1}\|u\|_{m+1}^{m+1}.

(105)

Moreover, substituting the optimizer
U_{\ast}
into
F(u)
yields

\displaystyle F(U_{\ast})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla U_{\ast}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{m+1}\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}^{m+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2C_{*}M^{\alpha}}\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}^{\alpha+2}-\frac{1}{m+1}\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}^{m+1}.

(106)

We define the auxiliary function

\displaystyle g(x)=\frac{1}{2C_{*}M^{\alpha}}x^{\alpha+2}-\frac{1}{m+1}x^{m+1}.

(107)

By combining (
5.1
) and (
5.1
), we get

\displaystyle g\left(\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}\right)=F(U_{\ast})\geq g\left(\|u\|_{m+1}\right)

(108)

for all

. Now, under the condition

\displaystyle F\left(u_{0}\right)<F\left(U_{\ast}\right),

(109)

there exists a constant
0<\delta<1
such that

\displaystyle F\left(u_{0}\right)<\delta F\left(U_{\ast}\right).

(110)

Since

is non-increasing in time, it follows that

\displaystyle g\left(\|u\|_{m+1}\right)\leq F(u)\leq F(u_{0})<\delta F\left(U_{\ast}\right)=\delta g\left(\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}\right).

(111)

On the other hand, since
\alpha+2<m+1
for
m>1+2/d
, a direct computation shows that the maximum of
g(x)
is attained at

\displaystyle x_{*}=\left(\frac{\alpha+2}{2C_{*}M^{\alpha}}\right)^{\frac{1}{m-\alpha-1}}=\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}.

This implies that
g(x)
is strictly increasing for
x<\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}
. Therefore, if

\displaystyle\|u_{0}\|_{m+1}<\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1},

(112)

then (
111
) ensures that there exists
\mu_{1}<1
(depending on
\delta
) such that, for all
t>0
,

\displaystyle\|u\|_{m+1}<\mu_{1}\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}.

(113)

Conversely,
g(x)
is strictly decreasing for
x>\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}
. Thus, if

\displaystyle\|u_{0}\|_{m+1}>\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1},

(114)

using (
111
) again, we conclude that exists
\mu_{2}>1
(depending on
\delta
) such that

\displaystyle\|u\|_{m+1}>\mu_{2}\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}.

(115)

This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

We note that if
F(u_{0})<F(U_{\ast})
, the case
\|u_{0}\|_{m+1}=\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}
cannot occur. Indeed, it follows directly from Proposition
4.6
(i) that
F(u_{0})\geq F(U_{\ast})
whenever
\|u_{0}\|_{m+1}=\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}=P_{\ast}
.

5.2
Finite-time blow-up

In this subsection, we prove a finite-time blow-up result for (
1
) with large initial data. We employ the standard argument relying on the evolution of the second moment of solutions, following the approach developed in
[
37
]
.

Let
u(x,t)
be a weak solution to (
1
) with the maximal existence time
T_{max}
. We define the blow-up criterion

\displaystyle\|\nabla u(\cdot,t)\|_{2}+\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{m+1}<\infty,\quad\text{for }0<t<T_{max},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to T_{max}}\left(\|\nabla u(\cdot,t)\|_{2}+\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{m+1}\right)=\infty.

(116)

We first introduce the second moment by

\displaystyle m_{2}(t):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|x|^{2}u(x,t)dx,

(117)

and obtain the following identity.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
u
be a weak solution to (
1
) on
[0,T)
for some
T\in(0,\infty]
. Then the second moment
m_{2}(t)
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}m_{2}(t)=2(d+2)F(u)-2\frac{dm-(d+2)}{m+1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}u^{m+1}dx,\quad t\in[0,T].

(118)

Proof.

Integrating by parts in (
1
), we compute

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}m_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=2\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}x\cdot(u\nabla(\Delta u))dx+\frac{2m}{m+1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}x\cdot\nabla u^{m+1}dx

\displaystyle=-2d\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}u\Delta udx-2\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(x\cdot\nabla u)\Delta udx-\frac{2dm}{m+1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}u^{m+1}dx

\displaystyle=2d\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|\nabla u|^{2}dx-(d-2)\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|\nabla u|^{2}dx-\frac{2dm}{m+1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}u^{m+1}dx

\displaystyle=(d+2)\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|\nabla u|^{2}dx-\frac{2dm}{m+1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}u^{m+1}dx

\displaystyle=2(d+2)F(u)-2\frac{dm-(d+2)}{m+1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}u^{m+1}dx,

(119)

where we have used

\displaystyle 2\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(x\cdot\nabla u)\Delta udx

\displaystyle=-2\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\nabla(x\cdot\nabla u)\cdot\nabla udx

\displaystyle=-2\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|\nabla u|^{2}dx-2\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}x\cdot D^{2}u\nabla udx

\displaystyle=-2\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|\nabla u|^{2}dx-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}x\cdot\nabla(|\nabla u|^{2})dx

\displaystyle=(d-2)\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|\nabla u|^{2}dx.

This completes the proof.
∎

We now state our finite-time blow-up result.

Theorem 5.4
.

(Finite-time blow-up)
Let
1+2/d<m<(d+2)/(d-2)
. Let
u(x,t)
be a weak solution defined in Definition
2.1
on
[0,T_{w})
. Under assumptions (
4
), (
100
) and (
103
), then
T_{w}<\infty
and
u(x,t)
blows up in finite time, in the sense that
\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to T_{w}}\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{m+1}=\infty
.

Proof.

The key tool is the time evolution of the second moment. Recalling that
m>1+2/d
, we use (
118
) together with (
100
) and estimate (
104
) from Proposition
5.1
(with some
\mu_{2}>1
) to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}m_{2}(t)

\displaystyle<2(d+2)F(u_{0})-2\mu_{2}\frac{dm-(d+2)}{m+1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}U_{\ast}^{m+1}dx

\displaystyle<2(d+2)F(U_{\ast})-2\mu_{2}\frac{dm-(d+2)}{m+1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}U_{\ast}^{m+1}dx

\displaystyle=2(1-\mu_{2})\frac{dm-(d+2)}{m+1}\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}^{m+1}<0.

(120)

Here we have used the fact that
F(u)
is non-increasing in time and Corollary
3.2
. It then follows from (
5.2
) that there exists
T>0
such that
\displaystyle\lim_{t\to T}m_{2}(t)=0
.

Next, by Hölder’s inequality we have

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}u(x)dx=

\displaystyle\int_{|x|\leq R}u(x)dx+\int_{|x|>R}u(x)dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle CR^{\frac{dm}{m+1}}\|u\|_{m+1}+\frac{1}{R^{2}}m_{2}(t).

(121)

Choosing
R=\left(\frac{Cm_{2}(t)}{\|u\|_{m+1}}\right)^{\frac{m+1}{dm+2(m+1)}}
results in

\displaystyle M\leq c\|u\|_{m+1}^{\frac{2(m+1)}{dm+2(m+1)}}m_{2}(t)^{\frac{dm}{dm+2(m+1)}},

(122)

which implies

\displaystyle\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to T}\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{m+1}\geq\displaystyle\lim_{t\to T}M^{\frac{dm+2(m+1)}{2(m+1)}}m_{2}(t)^{-\frac{dm}{2(m+1)}}=\infty.

(123)

Since
1<m+1<\frac{2d}{d-2}
, we further conclude that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to T}\|\nabla u\|_{2}=\infty

(124)

by virtue of the GNS inequality (
52
). Thus the proof is completed.
∎

5.3
Global existence

In this subsection, we present a global existence result for (
1
) with small initial data.

Theorem 5.5
.

(Global existence)
Let
1+2/d<m<(d+2)/(d-2)
. Assume that (
4
), (
100
) and (
101
) hold. Then there exists a weak solution to (
1
) such that for all
0<t<\infty
,

\displaystyle\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{m+1}+\|\nabla u(\cdot,t)\|_{2}\leq C\left(\|u_{0}\|_{1},\|\nabla u_{0}\|_{2}\right)

(125)

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}u\left|\nabla(\Delta u+u^{m})\right|^{2}dxds\leq C\left(\|u_{0}\|_{1},\|\nabla u_{0}\|_{2}\right).

(126)

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}m_{2}(t)=\infty.

(127)

Proof.

The proof can be divided into two parts. Step 1 establishes the global existence of weak solutions. Step 2 shows the blow-up of the second moment and thereby proves (
127
).

Step 1
(Global existence)  Under assumption (
101
), it follows from (
102
) in Proposition
5.1
that for any
t>0
, there exists
\mu_{1}<1
such that

\displaystyle\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{m+1}<\mu_{1}\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}.

(128)

Furthermore, for any
t>0
, the non-increasing of the free energy
F(u)
in time implies that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla u(\cdot,t)\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{m+1}\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{m+1}^{m+1}+F(u_{0})\leq C\left(\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1},F(u_{0})\right),

(129)

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}u\left|\nabla(\Delta u+u^{m})\right|^{2}dxds\leq F(u_{0})+\frac{1}{m+1}\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{m+1}^{m+1}\leq C\left(\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1},F(u_{0})\right).

(130)

By combining the Sobolev embedding theorem with Hölder’s inequality for
1<r<\frac{2d}{d-2}
, we infer from (
129
) that

\displaystyle\|u\|_{r}\leq C,\text{ for any }1\leq r\leq\frac{2d}{d-2}.

(131)

Therefore, for any
T>0
, the following uniform regularity estimates hold:

\displaystyle u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}\cap H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})),

(132)

\displaystyle u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{r}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})),\text{ for any }1\leq r\leq 2d/(d-2),

(133)

\displaystyle u^{1/2}\nabla\Delta u\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})),

\displaystyle u^{1/2}\nabla u^{m}\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})).

On the other hand, the second moment satisfies

\displaystyle m_{2}(t)\leq m_{2}(0)+2(d+2)F(u_{0})t,\quad\text{for any }0<t<T.

(134)

Thus far, uniform bounds on the competing terms in the free energy have been established, which serve as the key ingredient to exploit the gradient-flow structure of (
1
). Following the argument framework in Section 4 of
[
24
]
, for
1+2/d<m<(d+2)/(d-2)
, we can establish the existence of a weak solution to (
1
) on
[0,T]
for any
0<T<\infty
. The proof relies on the JKO scheme developed therein. Since our estimates yield exactly the required assumptions for that approach, the remaining steps including discrete approximation energy estimates and passage to the limit follow verbatim. We thus omit the repetitive details.

Step 2
(Proof of (
127
))  Suppose, for contradiction, that there exists an increasing sequence of times
\{t_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\to\infty
such that

\displaystyle m_{2}(t_{k})=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|x|^{2}u(x,t_{k})dx

(135)

is bounded. Since
u\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\cap H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, we first claim that there exist a subsequence (still denoted
t_{k}
) and a function
u_{\infty}
such that as
k\to\infty
,

\displaystyle u(\cdot,t_{k})\rightharpoonup u_{\infty}\text{ in }H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}),

\displaystyle u(\cdot,t_{k})\rightarrow u_{\infty}\text{ in }L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}),

(136)

\displaystyle u(\cdot,t_{k})\rightarrow u_{\infty}\text{ in }L^{m+1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})

(137)

and
\|u_{\infty}\|_{1}=M
. Moreover, the second moment of the limit satisfies

\displaystyle 0<\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|x|^{2}u_{\infty}dx\leq\displaystyle\liminf_{k\to\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|x|^{2}u(\cdot,t_{k})dx<\infty,

(138)

since concentration toward a Dirac delta at the origin is ruled out by the uniform bound (
132
).

On the other hand, a direct consequence of (
128
) is that the free energy
F(u)
is bounded from below:

\displaystyle F(u_{0})-\displaystyle\liminf_{k\to\infty}F(u)(t_{k})=\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{0}^{t_{k}}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}u(x,s)\left|\nabla\left(\Delta u+u^{m}\right)\right|^{2}dx\right)ds.

(139)

As a consequence, the Fisher information is integrable and

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{t_{k}}^{\infty}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}u(x,s)\left|\nabla\left(\Delta u+u^{m}\right)\right|^{2}dx\right)ds=0,

(140)

which implies that, up to the extraction of a subsequence, the limit
u_{\infty}
as
k\to\infty
satisfies

\displaystyle\nabla(\Delta u_{\infty}+u_{\infty}^{m})=0\quad\text{a.e. in supp}(u_{\infty}).

(141)

This follows by arguments similar to those in the existence proof in Section 2 of
[
14
]
. Therefore,
u_{\infty}
is a radially symmetric steady-state solution to (
37
) with mass
M
. By Proposition
4.6
(ii), we have

\displaystyle F(U_{\ast})\leq F(u_{\infty}).

(142)

However, (
137
) yields

\displaystyle F(u_{\infty})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla u_{\infty}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{m+1}\|u_{\infty}\|_{m+1}^{m+1}

\displaystyle\leq\displaystyle\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla u(\cdot,t_{k})\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{m+1}\|u(\cdot,t_{k})\|_{m+1}^{m+1}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\liminf_{k\to\infty}F(u(\cdot,t_{k}))\leq F(u_{0})<F(U_{\ast}),

(143)

which contradicts (
142
). Hence, (
127
) holds.

To complete the proof of (
127
), it remains to justify the convergences (
136
) and (
137
) used above. We first establish (
136
). Let
0\leq\chi_{R}\leq 1
be a function in
C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
such that
\chi_{R}=1
on
B_{R}(0)
and
\chi_{R}=0
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus B_{2R}(0)
. We decompose

\displaystyle M=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}u(\cdot,t_{k})dx=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}u(\cdot,t_{k})\chi_{R}dx+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}u(\cdot,t_{k})(1-\chi_{R})dx.

(144)

Then for sufficiently large
R
,

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}u(\cdot,t_{k})(1-\chi_{R})dx\leq\int_{|x|\geq R}u(\cdot,t_{k})dx\leq\frac{1}{R^{2}}\int_{|x|\geq R}|x|^{2}u(\cdot,t_{k})dx<\varepsilon,

(145)

while

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}u(\cdot,t_{k})\chi_{R}dx=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}u_{\infty}\chi_{R}dx.

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\left|M-\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}u(\cdot,t_{k})\chi_{R}dx\right|<\varepsilon,\quad\text{as }R\to\infty,

(146)

which implies

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}u_{\infty}dx=M>0.

(147)

This establishes (
136
).

We next prove (
137
). Using the condition
1<m+1<2d/(d-2)
and the compact Sobolev embedding
W^{1,2}(B_{R}(0))\hookrightarrow L^{q}(B_{R}(0))
for
1\leq q<2d/(d-2)
, we obtain local strong convergence in
L^{m+1}(B_{R}(0))
for any fixed
0<R<\infty
. Moreover, it follows from the GNS inequality (
52
) that

\displaystyle\int_{|x|\geq R}u^{m+1}dx

\displaystyle\leq C_{*}\left(\int_{|x|\geq R}udx\right)^{\frac{d+2-(d-2)m}{d+2}}\|\nabla u\|_{2}^{\frac{2dm}{d+2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(M,C_{*},F(u_{0})\right)~\left(\frac{m_{2}(t)}{R^{2}}\right)^{\frac{d+2-(d-2)m}{d+2}}

(148)

upon using (
145
). Therefore, as
R\to\infty
and
k\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left|u(\cdot,t_{k})-u_{\infty}\right|^{m+1}dx=\int_{|x|<R}\left|u(\cdot,t_{k})-u_{\infty}\right|^{m+1}dx+\int_{|x|\geq R}\left|u(\cdot,t_{k})-u_{\infty}\right|^{m+1}dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{|x|<R}\left|u(\cdot,t_{k})-u_{\infty}\right|^{m+1}dx+C\int_{|x|\geq R}\left(\left|u(\cdot,t_{k})\right|^{m+1}+\left|u_{\infty}\right|^{m+1}\right)dx\to 0.

This completes the proof of Theorem
5.5
.
∎

5.4
Possible dynamic behaviors for
F(u_{0})>F(U_{\ast})

For initial data satisfying
F(u_{0})>F(U_{\ast})
, energy dissipation no longer prevents the solution from approaching the steady state
U_{\ast}
. The long-time behavior of global solutions is conjectured to depend on the boundedness of two key quantities:

\displaystyle\textbf{(P1) }\sup_{t\geq 0}\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{m+1}<\infty,\quad\text{(concentration control)}

\displaystyle\textbf{(P2) }\sup_{t\geq 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{2}u(x,t)\,dx<\infty.\quad\text{(mass confinement)}

Based on the gradient-flow structure and analogy with related models, we expect the following scenarios:

1.

(P1) and (P2):
The solution is expected to converge (possibly up to translation) to
U_{\ast}
.

2.

(P1) and not (P2):
Mass is expected to escape to infinity while the profile remains bounded in
L^{m+1}
. After a suitable time-dependent translation that follows the escaping core, the profile may converge locally to a steady state.

3.

not (P1) and (P2):
Unbounded concentration growth within a confined region suggests the possibility of infinite-time singularity formation as a Dirac mass, and the free energy would tend to
-\infty
.

4.

not (P1) and not (P2):
The solution may exhibit complex dynamics involving simultaneous concentration growth and mass dispersion, possibly leading to intricate asymptotic patterns.

Remark 5.6
.

With the exception of the first case, the precise asymptotic behavior in the other scenarios depends on further details of the model and the initial data. A rigorous analysis of these cases remains an open challenge.
