Bounds for Distributionally Robust Optimization Problems
Brandon Tam, Silvana M. Pesenti

Abstract
We study distributionally robust optimization (DRO) problems with uncertainty sets consisting of high-dimensional random vectors that are close in the multivariate Wasserstein distance to a reference random vector. We give conditions when the images of these sets under scalar-valued aggregation functions are contained in and contain uncertainty sets of univariate random variables defined via a univariate Wasserstein distance. This provides lower and upper bounds for the solution to general multivariate DRO problems that are computationally tractable. Furthermore, we generalize the results to uncertainty sets characterized by Bregman-Wasserstein divergences, which allows for asymmetric deviations from the reference random vector. Moreover, for DRO problems with risk measure criterion in the class of signed Choquet integrals, we derive semi-analytic formulae for the upper and lower bounds and the distribution that attains these bounds.

Introduction

Optimization problems with stochastic components (stochastic programs) occur frequently in the finance and risk management literature. The classical approach to handle stochasticity, proposed independently by
Beale (1955)
and
Dantzig (1955)
, is to optimize the expected value of the stochastic objective. This results in problems of the form

\inf_{\bm{a}\in\mathcal{A}}{\mathbb{E}}[\ell(\bm{a},{\bm{X}})],

(1)

where
\mathcal{A}
is a set of feasible actions,
\ell
is a real-valued loss function,
{\bm{X}}
is a random vector, and the expectation is taken with respect to (wrt)
{\bm{X}}
. Although
\ell
is assumed to be affine in the original problem, subsequent developments in optimization theory; see, e.g.,
Rockafellar (1970)
,
Barbu and Precupanu (2012)
, have allowed for the study of more general real-valued loss functions. We refer to
Shapiro et al. (2021)
for a comprehensive discussion of stochastic programs.

To evaluate the expectation in (
1
), the distribution of
{\bm{X}}
is assumed to be known. However, in many practical applications, the distribution is only partially known or needs to be estimated. Difficulties arising from estimation or partial knowledge of the underlying distribution are well studied in finance (
Michaud (1989)
), decision theory (
Smith and Winkler (2006)
), and risk management (
Cont et al. (2010)
,
Embrechts et al. (2015)
,
Pesenti et al. (2016)
). To address this restriction,
Scarf (1958)
proposed a distributionally robust version of
Equation

1
, termed distributionally robust optimization (DRO). DRO problems take the form

\inf_{\bm{a}\in\mathcal{A}}\,\sup_{{\bm{Z}}\in\mathcal{U}}\,{\mathbb{E}}[\ell(\bm{a},{\bm{Z}})]\,,

(2)

where
\mathcal{U}
is a set of plausible alternative random vectors centered around the reference random vector
{\bm{X}}
— so–called uncertainty sets. Over the past two decades, DRO problems have become popular in operations research and economics; indicatively see
Rahimian and Mehrotra (2022)
and
Hansen and Sargent (2008)
.

Early research in DRO focuses on linear programs with ellipsoidal uncertainty sets (
Ben-Tal and Nemirovski (1998)
,
Ben-Tal and Nemirovski (1999)
). Here we work, among others, with uncertainty sets defined by a Wasserstein distance constraint. The Wasserstein distance is a well-studied metric originating from the field of optimal transport (
Villani (1973)
). This metric is now widely used outside of the theory of optimal transport, with important applications in mathematics, probability, and statistical theory (
Panaretos and Zemel (2019)
,
Dobrushin (1970)
,
Munk and Czado (1998)
). In particular, worst-case expectation problems (i.e., the inner optimization problem in (
2
)) with Wasserstein uncertainty are well studied in the operations research literature. For instance,
Zhao and Guan (2018)
provide a characterization for the worst-case distribution (i.e., the distribution of the random vector attaining the inner supremum in (
2
)) in data-driven risk-averse two-stage stochastic optimization problems when the reference distribution is the empirical distribution of observed data. In
Esfahani and Kuhn (2018)
, the authors consider loss functions of the form
\ell({\bm{x}})=\max_{k\in\{1,\ldots K\}}\ell_{k}({\bm{x}})
, reformulate the worst-case expectation problem as a finite convex program, and obtain an explicit upper bound for the worst-case expectation when
\ell
is proper, convex, and lower semi-continuous. For arbitrary loss functionals
\ell
,
Blanchet and Murphy (2019)
and
Gao and Kleywegt (2023)
derive a strong duality reformulation for the worst-case expectation problem. Another related Wasserstein DRO problem is the distributionally robust chance-constrained (DRCC) problem (
Xie (2021)
,
Chen et al. (2024)
). DRCC problems take the form

\min_{\bm{a}\in\mathcal{A}}\left\{\bm{c}^{T}\bm{a}\right\}\ \text{subject to}\ \inf_{{\bm{Z}}\in\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{P}\big({\bm{Z}}\in\mathcal{C}({\bm{a}})\big)\geq 1-{\varepsilon},

where
\bm{a},\bm{c}\in{\mathds{R}}^{m}
and
\mathcal{C}(\bm{a})
is a constraint set that depends on the decision variable
\bm{a}
. In
Chen et al. (2024)
, the authors study the chance constraint and derive an exact reformulation for data-driven chance constraints. In contrast,
Xie (2021)
derives inner and outer approximations for the set of actions satisfying the chance constraint. Both
Xie (2021)
and
Chen et al. (2024)
assume that there exists
N
historical samples from the true underlying distribution and the distribution of the reference random vector is the empirical distribution of these samples. In this paper, the reference distribution is not necessarily the empirical one.

Additionally, in our work, we consider both the Wasserstein distance and an asymmetric generalization of the Wasserstein distance known as the Bregman-Wasserstein (BW) divergence. The BW divergence was first introduced by
Carlier and Jimenez (2007)
, its geometry is discussed in
Kainth et al. (2025)
, and BW uncertainty sets are studied in
Guo et al. (2017)
,
Pesenti and Vanduffel (2024)
,
Pesenti et al. (2024b)
, and
Pesenti and Nguyen (2026)
. Here, we focus on the inner optimization problem in (
2
) and assume that the risk factor
{\bm{X}}
is multivariate. Specifically, we consider uncertainty sets defined using multivariate Wasserstein distance and multivariate BW divergence constraints. DRO problems involving the multivariate Wasserstein distance are challenging because explicit formulas for the multivariate Wasserstein distance between distributions are only known for a few distributions such as the Gaussian (
Dowson and Landau (1982)
,
Gelbrich (1990)
). Numerical algorithms, e.g., the Sinkhorn algorithm, can be used in multivariate settings, but it is well known that the computational cost significantly increases with the dimension of
{\bm{X}}
(
Cuturi (2013)
). We contribute to the literature by proposing upper and lower bounds to the inner optimization problem in (
2
) that depend only on a univariate Wasserstein distance, regardless of the dimension of
{\bm{X}}
. We further derive expressions for the bounds that allow for efficient numerical evaluation, and characterize special cases under which a closed-form expression for the bounds exists. We also identify cases when the upper and lower bounds coincide and derive bounds for uncertainty sets characterized via the BW divergence. For some choices of BW uncertainty sets, we establish an intimate connection between the uncertainty set and the aggregation function via composable Bregman divergences, see
Section

4.4
.

We illustrate our lower and upper bounds on risk-aware DRO problems, where the expected value in (
2
) is replaced by a law-invariant risk functional
\rho
(also called a risk measure). Risk functionals are well studied in the literature. One of the earliest and most significant classes of risk functionals is the class of coherent risk measures, which was first introduced in
Artzner et al. (1999)
. This class includes the expected value and the popular Expected Shortfall (ES). Over the past two decades, various generalizations have been proposed, including convex risk measures (
Föllmer and Schied (2002)
), generalized deviation measures (
Rockafellar et al. (2006)
), and the characterization of law-invariant coherent risk measures (
Kusuoka (2001)
). More recently,
Wang et al. (2020)
provided an extensive study of signed Choquet integrals, which are a large class of risk functionals subsuming the well-known class of distortion risk measures. DRO problems with distortion risk measures are for example studied in
Cai et al. (2023)
,
Pesenti and Jaimungal (2023)
,
Bernard et al. (2024)
,
Coache and Jaimungal (2026)
, and
Moresco et al. (2024)
.
Cai et al. (2023)
consider moment based constraints for multivariate risks,
Pesenti and Jaimungal (2023)
study portfolio optimization,
Bernard et al. (2024)
focus on Wasserstein distance constraints for univariate risks, and
Coache and Jaimungal (2026)
and
Moresco et al. (2024)
work with robust dynamic risk measures. Another related work is
Bernard and Pesenti (2026)
, who consider BW uncertainty around the choice of distortion risk measure rather than the underlying distribution. In our work, we consider multivariate risk factors, any law-invariant risk functional, and provide an application of our results to the class of signed Choquet integrals.

The paper is structured as follows.
Section

2
introduces the notation, while
Section

3
is devoted to the inner optimization problem in (
2
) for law-invariant risk functionals when
\mathcal{U}
is a multivariate Wasserstein uncertainty set and
\ell
is a Lipschitz continuous function. In
Section

4
, we generalize our results to the case when
\mathcal{U}
is a BW uncertainty set.
Section

5
contains explicit bounds when the risk functional is a signed Choquet integral, and
Section

6
illustrates numerical examples.

2
Multivariate Wasserstein Uncertainty

We work throughout with a non-atomic probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{B},\mathbb{P})
, where
\mathcal{B}:=\mathcal{B}({\mathds{R}}^{n})
is the Borel sigma algebra on
{\mathds{R}}^{n}
for some
n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
. We further denote vector-valued random variables (rvs) on
(\Omega,\mathcal{B},\mathbb{P})
with capital boldface letters and real-valued vectors with lowercase boldface letters. For a random vector
{\bm{X}}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
, we write
F_{{\bm{X}}}({\bm{x}}):=\mathbb{P}({\bm{X}}\leq{\bm{x}})
,
{\bm{x}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in{\mathds{R}}^{n}
for the cumulative distribution function (cdf) of
{\bm{X}}
, where vector inequalities are understood component-wise. For
a\geq 1
, we let
\left\lVert{\bm{x}}\right\rVert_{a}:=(\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\left\lvert x_{i}\right\rvert^{a})^{\frac{1}{a}}
denote the
\mathcal{L}^{a}
norm on
{\mathds{R}}^{n}
, where
{\mathcal{N}}:=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Furthermore, for
p\geq 1
we denote by
\mathcal{L}^{p}_{n}
the space of all
n
-dimensional random vectors
{\bm{X}}
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{B},\mathbb{P})
such that
{\mathbb{E}}[\left\lVert{\bm{X}}\right\rVert_{a}^{p}]<\infty
, and by
\mathcal{M}_{p}({\mathds{R}}^{n})
the corresponding set of cdfs. As
a
is fixed throughout, we write
\mathcal{L}^{p}_{n}
and omit its dependence on the norm
a
. Moreover, if
n=1
, we omit the dimension subscript and simply write
\mathcal{L}^{p}
. For any pair of random vectors
{\bm{X}},{\bm{Y}}
, we denote by
{\bm{X}}{\overset{d}{=}}{\bm{Y}}
equality in distribution and by
{\bm{X}}={\bm{Y}}

\mathbb{P}
-almost sure equality. Additionally, we denote by
{\mathds{R}}^{n}_{\geq}
the set of vectors in
{\mathds{R}}^{n}
with non-negative components.

The (
p
)-Wasserstein metric with norm
a
on
{\mathds{R}}^{n}
(which we just call the Wasserstein distance) between
F\in\mathcal{M}_{p}({\mathds{R}}^{n})
and
G\in\mathcal{M}_{p}({\mathds{R}}^{n})
is defined as

W^{n}(F,G):=\inf_{F_{{\bm{X}}}=F,F_{{\bm{Y}}}=G}({\mathbb{E}}[\left\lVert{\bm{X}}-{\bm{Y}}\right\rVert_{a}^{p}])^{\frac{1}{p}},

where the infimum is taken over all cdfs on
{\mathds{R}}^{n}\times{\mathds{R}}^{n}
with
n
-dimensional marginal distributions
F
and
G
. As we fix
a
and
p
throughout the exposition, we simply write
W^{n}
, where
n
indicates the dimension of the marginals, and omit the dependence on the power
p
and the norm
a
.
For
n=1
, we omit the dimension superscript, i.e., we write
W(\cdot,\cdot):=W^{1}(\cdot,\cdot)
, and note that the norm
a
becomes irrelevant. For
n=1
, by the well-known fact that the infimum is attained by the comonotonic coupling (
Dall’Aglio (1956)
), the 1-dimensional Wasserstein distance has representation

W(F,G):=\Big(\int_{0}^{1}\left\lvert F^{-1}(s)-G^{-1}(s)\right\rvert^{p}\,ds\Big)^{\frac{1}{p}}\,,

(3)

where
F^{-1}(\alpha):=\inf\{x\in{\mathds{R}}~|~F(x)\geq\alpha\}
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
, is the (left-continuous) quantile function of
F
.

Next, we introduce the notation for uncertainty sets characterized by the Wasserstein distance.

Definition 2.1

(Wasserstein Uncertainty Sets)

For
{\varepsilon}\geq 0
, the Wasserstein uncertainty set with radius
{\varepsilon}
around the reference random vector
{\bm{X}}\in\mathcal{L}^{p}_{n}
is

{\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{{\varepsilon}}^{n}({\bm{X}}):=\{{\bm{Z}}\in\mathcal{L}^{p}_{n}~|~W^{n}(F_{{\bm{Z}}},F_{{\bm{X}}})\leq{\varepsilon}\}.

(4)

Similar to the Wasserstein distance, for
n=1
we omit the dimension superscript and simply write
{\mathcal{U}}_{{\varepsilon}}(X):={\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{{\varepsilon}}^{1}(X)
. The parameter
{\varepsilon}
(also called tolerance distance) represents the magnitude of uncertainty, that is, the larger
{\varepsilon}
, the larger the uncertainty.

Throughout the paper, we focus on the inner problem of (
2
) and use
g\colon{\mathds{R}}^{n}\to{\mathds{R}}
to denote an aggregation (or prediction) function that maps input risk factors
{\bm{X}}
to a univariate output decision random variable
g({\bm{X}})
. The function
g
should be interpreted as
\ell(a,\cdot)
for a fixed decision
a
, where
\ell
is the loss function in (
2
). Here, we assume that the aggregation function is given, e.g., estimated via statistical methods, and that the uncertainty stems from the risk factors
{\bm{X}}
. In an investment setting, for example,
g({\bm{X}})
might represent the payoff of a portfolio, where
{\bm{X}}
is the vector of asset prices subject to uncertainty and
g
depends on the type of investments. The objective of our DRO problem is a law-invariant risk functional
\rho:\mathcal{L}^{p}\to{\mathds{R}}
, subsuming the expected value, where
\rho
is law invariant if
X{\overset{d}{=}}Y
implies
\rho(X)=\rho(Y)
. Specifically, for
n>1
, we consider

\sup_{{\bm{Y}}\in{\bm{\mathcal{U}}}^{n}_{{\varepsilon}}({\bm{X}})}\rho\big(g({\bm{Y}})\big)\,.

(5)

For optimization problem (
5
) to be non-trivial, we assume throughout that the aggregation function
g(\cdot)
is non-constant.

{assumption}

The aggregation function
g\colon{\mathds{R}}^{n}\to{\mathds{R}}
is non-constant.

There are different streams of literature that aim at simplifying robust DRO problems.
Pesenti et al. (2024a)
for example derives necessary and sufficient conditions on the uncertainty set and the risk measure to recast a non-convex risk-aware DRO problem as a convex one. Here, the authors mostly mostly work with univariate uncertainty sets. In contrast, the uncertainty set in our problem is defined via the multivariate Wasserstein distance, making it difficult to solve without resorting to numerical algorithms such as the Sinkhorn algorithm. Thus, instead of explicitly calculating
(
5
)
, we derive upper and lower bounds that are characterized by tractable univariate DRO problems of the form

\sup_{Z\in{\mathcal{U}}_{{\varepsilon}^{\prime}}(g({\bm{X}}))}\rho(Z)\,.

(6)

This DRO problem is typically more tractable as the uncertainty is around the univariate rv
g({\bm{X}})
, see for instance
Liu et al. (2022)
for a closed-form expression when
\rho
is a distortion risk measure.

Comparing DRO problems (
5
) and (
6
), we observe that the former accounts for uncertainty in the risk factors
{\bm{X}}
(which then propagates to uncertainty in
g({\bm{X}})
), while the latter models uncertainty directly on the aggregate
g({\bm{X}})
. While optimization problem (
6
) is over the univariate uncertainty set
{\mathcal{U}}_{{\varepsilon}^{\prime}}(g({\bm{X}}))
, the corresponding aggregate univariate uncertainty set of DRO problem (
5
) is
g\big({\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{{\varepsilon}}^{n}({\bm{X}})\big):=\{g({\bm{Z}})~|~{\bm{Z}}\in{\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{{\varepsilon}}^{n}({\bm{X}})\}
, that is, each random vector in the uncertainty set
{\bm{Z}}\in{\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{{\varepsilon}}^{n}({\bm{X}})
is mapped to a potential aggregate output
g({\bm{Z}})
. Therefore, (
5
) is equivalent to

\sup_{{\bm{Y}}\in{\bm{\mathcal{U}}}^{n}_{{\varepsilon}}({\bm{X}})}\rho\big(g({\bm{Y}})\big)=\sup_{Z\in g({\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{{\varepsilon}}^{n}({\bm{X}}))}\rho(Z)\,.

In general the two univariate uncertainty sets are not the same, i.e.,
g\big({\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{{\varepsilon}}^{n}({\bm{X}})\big)\neq{\mathcal{U}}_{{\varepsilon}}(g({\bm{X}}))
. In

3
DRO with Wasserstein Uncertainty

In this section, we prove conditions on the aggregation function
g
such that the image (under
g
) of the multivariate uncertainty set around
{\bm{X}}
is contained in a univariate uncertainty set around
g({\bm{X}})
, i.e.
g\big({\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{{\varepsilon}}^{n}({\bm{X}})\big)\subseteq{\mathcal{U}}_{{\varepsilon}^{\prime}}(g({\bm{X}}))
. This provides upper bounds for the DRO problem (
5
) that are univariate DRO problems of type (
6
). We furthermore characterize special cases where the set inclusion becomes a set equality, i.e.,
g\big({\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{{\varepsilon}}^{n}({\bm{X}})\big)={\mathcal{U}}_{{\varepsilon}^{\prime}}(g({\bm{X}}))
, and thus the DRO problems (
5
) and (
6
) coincide. Note that the tolerance distances for the two uncertainty sets are in general different.

3.1
Lipschitz Aggregation Functions

We first consider the class of
K
-Lipschitz continuous functions. Recall that a function
f:{\mathds{R}}^{n}\to{\mathds{R}}
is
K
-Lipschitz (wrt the
\mathcal{L}^{a}
norm) if
\left\lvert f({\bm{x}})-f({\bm{y}})\right\rvert\leq K\left\lVert{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\right\rVert_{a}
for all
{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in{\mathds{R}}^{n}
. Our first main result states that for a
K
-Lipschitz aggregation function
g
, the uncertainty set stemming from the risk factors is contained in the univariate uncertainty set around the aggregate
g({\bm{X}})
with a tolerance distance of
K{\varepsilon}
– the product of the Lipschitz constant and the tolerance distance
{\varepsilon}
of the multivariate uncertainty set. Lipschitz continuous functions are prevalent in portfolio optimization, important for fairness assessment (
Dwork et al. (2012)
), as well as ubiquitous in machine learning. Indeed, any fully connected network (FCN) and any convolutional neural network (CNN) with Lipschitz activation functions (e.g., ReLU, SoftPlus, sigmoid) is itself Lipschitz continuous. We refer to
Virmaux and Scaman (2018)
,
Jordan and Dimakis (2020)
, and
Kim et al. (2021)
for details and discussions on Lipschitz constants of neural networks.

Theorem 3.1

(Lipschitz aggregation)

Suppose that
g:{\mathds{R}}^{n}\to{\mathds{R}}
is
K
-Lipschitz. If
{\bm{X}}\in\mathcal{L}^{p}_{n}
with
g({\bm{X}})\in\mathcal{L}^{p}
, then for any
{\varepsilon}\geq 0
,

g\big({\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{{\varepsilon}}^{n}({\bm{X}})\big)\subseteq{\mathcal{U}}_{K{\varepsilon}}(g({\bm{X}})).

Proof 3.2

Let
Z\in g\big({\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{{\varepsilon}}^{n}({\bm{X}})\big)
. Then, by definition of
g\big({\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{{\varepsilon}}^{n}({\bm{X}})\big)
, there exists a random vector
{\bm{Z}}
such that
W^{n}(F_{{\bm{Z}}},F_{{\bm{X}}})\leq{\varepsilon}
and
Z=g({\bm{Z}})
. Therefore,

\displaystyle W(F_{g({\bm{Z}})},F_{g({\bm{X}})})

\displaystyle=\inf_{X^{\prime}{\overset{d}{=}}g({\bm{X}}),\ Z^{\prime}{\overset{d}{=}}g({\bm{Z}})}{\mathbb{E}}[\left\lvert X^{\prime}-Z^{\prime}\right\rvert^{p}]^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle=\inf_{g({\bm{X}}^{\prime}){\overset{d}{=}}g({\bm{X}}),\ g({\bm{Z}}^{\prime}){\overset{d}{=}}g({\bm{Z}})}{\mathbb{E}}\big[\left\lvert g({\bm{X}}^{\prime})-g({\bm{Z}}^{\prime})\right\rvert^{p}\big]^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{g({\bm{X}}^{\prime}){\overset{d}{=}}g({\bm{X}}),\ g({\bm{Z}}^{\prime}){\overset{d}{=}}g({\bm{Z}})}{\mathbb{E}}\big[K^{p}\left\lVert{\bm{X}}^{\prime}-{\bm{Z}}^{\prime}\right\rVert_{a}^{p}\big]^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{{\bm{X}}^{\prime}{\overset{d}{=}}{\bm{X}},\ {\bm{Z}}^{\prime}{\overset{d}{=}}{\bm{Z}}}{\mathbb{E}}\big[K^{p}\left\lVert{\bm{X}}^{\prime}-{\bm{Z}}^{\prime}\right\rVert_{a}^{p}\big]^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle=K\,W^{n}(F_{{\bm{Z}}},F_{{\bm{X}}})

\displaystyle\leq K\,{\varepsilon}.

Hence, we conclude that

.

The above theorem implies that if the aggregation function
g
is Lipschitz, then the DRO problem (
5
) can be upper bounded by a univariate DRO problem of type (
6
) whose tolerance distance is scaled by the Lipschitz constant of
g
.

Next, we discuss cases when the set inclusion in
Theorem

3.1
becomes a set equality, and thus the DRO problems (
5
) and (
6
) coincide. We first show that Lipschitz continuity alone is in general not sufficient. To see this, let
g({\bm{x}})=\sin(x_{1})
(which is Lipschitz with
K=1
),
{\varepsilon}=3
,
{\bm{X}}\in\mathcal{L}^{p}_{n}
, and
Z=g({\bm{X}})+{\varepsilon}
. Since
{\mathbb{E}}[\left\lvert Z-g({\bm{X}})\right\rvert^{p}])^{\frac{1}{p}}={\varepsilon}
, it follows that
Z\in{\mathcal{U}}_{K{\varepsilon}}(g({\bm{X}}))
. Furthermore, for any random vector
{\bm{Y}}
, the support of
g({\bm{Y}})=\sin(Y_{1})
is a subset of
[-1,1]
. Since
Z=\sin(X_{1})+3
is supported on a subset of
[2,4]
, it follows that there does not exist a random vector
{\bm{Z}}
such that
g({\bm{Z}})=Z
and therefore
Z\notin g\big({\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{{\varepsilon}}^{n}({\bm{X}})\big)
.

To show set equality, we observe that any
K
-Lipschitz function
f\colon{\mathds{R}}^{n}\to{\mathds{R}}
has decomposition

f({\bm{x}})=\widetilde{f}({\bm{x}}^{(1)})+{\bm{\beta}}^{T}{\bm{x}}^{(2)},

(7)

where
{\bm{x}}=({\bm{x}}^{(1)},{\bm{x}}^{(2)})
with
{\bm{x}}^{(1)}\in{\mathds{R}}^{m}
,
{\bm{x}}^{(2)}\in{\mathds{R}}^{n-m}
for some
m\leq n
,
{\bm{\beta}}\in{\mathds{R}}^{n-m}
, and
\widetilde{f}:{\mathds{R}}^{m}\to{\mathds{R}}
is Lipschitz continuous. When
f
has no linear components, then
f=\widetilde{f}
and
m=n
. In contrast, when
f
has at least one linear component, then
\widetilde{f}
and
m
are no longer unique as linear functions are also Lipschitz continuous. Throughout, we always choose the smallest possible
m
to obtain the smallest Lipschitz constant for
\widetilde{f}
.

In an investment setting, when
{\bm{x}}
is a vector of risky asset prices and
f
is the portfolio payoff,
{\bm{\beta}}^{T}{\bm{x}}^{(2)}
represents the payoff from investing in
\beta_{i}
units of asset
x^{(2)}_{i}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n-m\}
. Moreover,
\widetilde{f}({\bm{x}}^{(1)})
can be used to model non-linear payoffs that arise from investments in financial derivatives such as options.

Our next result characterizes a univariate uncertainty set around
g({\bm{X}})
that is contained in the image of the multivariate uncertainty set
g\big({\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{{\varepsilon}}^{n}({\bm{X}})\big)
, yielding a lower bound for optimization problem (
5
). The result states that the radius of the smaller set is proportional to the norm of the coefficient vector
{\bm{\beta}}
in the linear part of the decomposition (
7
). Combining this result with
Theorem

allows us to identify when set equality holds. We refer to
Mao et al. (2025)
for a similar result in the case when

is linear.

Proposition 3.3

Let
g:{\mathds{R}}^{n}\to{\mathds{R}}
be a Lipschitz continuous function (wrt the
\mathcal{L}^{a}
norm) with decomposition (
7
). If
{\bm{X}}\in\mathcal{L}^{p}_{n}
with
g({\bm{X}})\in\mathcal{L}^{p}
, then for any
{\varepsilon}\geq 0
,

{\mathcal{U}}_{\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b}{\varepsilon}}(g({\bm{X}}))\subseteq g\big({\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{{\varepsilon}}^{n}({\bm{X}})\big),

where
\frac{1}{a}+\frac{1}{b}=1
.

Proof 3.4

First, we note that if
\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b}=0
, then
{\mathcal{U}}_{\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b}{\varepsilon}}(g({\bm{X}}))=\{V~|~V{\overset{d}{=}}g({\bm{X}})\}
, so the set containment is trivial. Thus, we assume wlog that
\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b}>0
.

Let
Z\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b}{\varepsilon}}(g({\bm{X}}))
. We consider the cases of
a>1
and
a=1
separately. First, assume that
a>1
. Define the
(n-m)
-dimensional vector

{\bm{\beta}}^{\frac{b}{a}}:=(\sgn(\beta_{1})\left\lvert\beta_{1}\right\rvert^{\frac{b}{a}},\ldots,\sgn(\beta_{n-m})\left\lvert\beta_{n-m}\right\rvert^{\frac{b}{a}}),

where
\sgn(\cdot)
is the sign function. Let
{\bm{Z}}=({\bm{Z}}^{(1)},{\bm{Z}}^{(2)})\in{\mathds{R}}^{m}\times{\mathds{R}}^{n-m}
, where
{\bm{Z}}^{(1)}:=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{m})
and

{\bm{Z}}^{(2)}:=\Big(X_{m+1}+\tfrac{(\beta^{\frac{b}{a}})_{1}(Z-g({\bm{X}}))}{{\bm{\beta}}^{T}{\bm{\beta}}^{\frac{b}{a}}},\ldots,X_{n}+\tfrac{(\beta^{\frac{b}{a}})_{n-m}(Z-g({\bm{X}}))}{{\bm{\beta}}^{T}{\bm{\beta}}^{\frac{b}{a}}}\Big),

and
(\beta^{\frac{b}{a}})_{i}
is the
i
-th component of
{\bm{\beta}}^{\frac{b}{a}}
. It suffices to show that
g({\bm{Z}})=Z
and
W^{n}(F_{{\bm{Z}}},F_{{\bm{X}}})\leq{\varepsilon}
. By definition of
{\bm{Z}}
, we have

\displaystyle g({\bm{Z}})

\displaystyle=\widetilde{g}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{m})+{\bm{\beta}}^{T}{\bm{X}}^{(2)}+{\bm{\beta}}^{T}\Big(\tfrac{{\bm{\beta}}^{\frac{b}{a}}(Z-g({\bm{X}}))}{{\bm{\beta}}^{T}{\bm{\beta}}^{\frac{b}{a}}}\Big)

\displaystyle=g({\bm{X}})+Z-g({\bm{X}})

\displaystyle=Z.

Furthermore,

\displaystyle W^{n}(F_{{\bm{Z}}},F_{{\bm{X}}})

\displaystyle=\inf_{{\bm{X}}^{\prime}{\overset{d}{=}}{\bm{X}},{\bm{Z}}^{\prime}{\overset{d}{=}}{\bm{Z}}}\big({\mathbb{E}}\big[\left\lVert{\bm{Z}}^{\prime}-{\bm{X}}^{\prime}\right\rVert_{a}^{p}\big]\big)^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\big({\mathbb{E}}[\left\lVert{\bm{Z}}-{\bm{X}}\right\rVert_{a}^{p}]\big)^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle=\Big({\mathbb{E}}\big[\lVert{\bm{\beta}}^{\frac{b}{a}}\rVert_{a}^{p}\tfrac{\left\lvert Z-g({\bm{X}})\right\rvert^{p}}{\lvert{\bm{\beta}}^{T}{\bm{\beta}}^{\frac{b}{a}}\rvert^{p}}\big]\Big)^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle=\Big({\mathbb{E}}\big[\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert^{\frac{p\,b}{a}}_{b}\tfrac{\left\lvert Z-g({\bm{X}})\right\rvert^{p}}{\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert^{p\,b}_{b}}\big]\Big)^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\big({\mathbb{E}}[\left\lvert Z-g({\bm{X}})\right\rvert^{p}]\big)^{\frac{1}{p}}}{\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b}}{\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b}}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle={\varepsilon},

where the third equality follows from the fact that
\lVert{\bm{\beta}}^{\frac{b}{a}}\rVert_{a}^{p}=(\sum_{i=1}^{n-m}\left\lvert\beta_{i}\right\rvert^{b})^{\frac{p}{a}}=\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert^{\frac{p\,b}{a}}_{b}
and
\lvert{\bm{\beta}}^{T}{\bm{\beta}}^{\frac{b}{a}}\rvert^{p}=\left\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{n-m}\left\lvert\beta_{i}\right\rvert^{1+\frac{b}{a}}\right\rvert^{p}=\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b}^{p\,b}
, completing the proof for the case of
a>1
.

If
a=1
, then let
j^{*}\in\{1,\ldots,n-m\}
such that
\left\lvert\beta_{j^{*}}\right\rvert=\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{\infty}
, where
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\infty}
denotes the
\mathcal{L}^{\infty}
norm, i.e.,
\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{\infty}:=\max\{|\beta_{1}|,\ldots|\beta_{n-m}|\}
. Let
{\bm{Z}}
be the random vector
{\bm{X}}
with the
(m+j^{*})
-th coordinate replaced by
X_{m+j^{*}}+\tfrac{\sgn(\beta_{j^{*}})(Z-g({\bm{X}}))}{\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{\infty}}
. Then, by similar arguments to the case of
a>1
, we have
g({\bm{Z}})=Z
and
W^{n}(F_{{\bm{Z}}},F_{{\bm{X}}})\leq{\varepsilon}
.

Thus, for a
K
-Lipschitz function
g
with decomposition (
7
),
Theorem

3.1
and
Proposition

3.3
yield the following sequence of set inclusions of univariate uncertainty sets

{\mathcal{U}}_{\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b}{\varepsilon}}(g({\bm{X}}))\subseteq g\big({\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{{\varepsilon}}^{n}({\bm{X}})\big)\subseteq{\mathcal{U}}_{K{\varepsilon}}(g({\bm{X}}))\,.

(8)

The smallest uncertainty set
{\mathcal{U}}_{\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b}{\varepsilon}}
has a tolerance distance proportional only to the linear term in the decomposition (
7
), while the largest set
{\mathcal{U}}_{K{\varepsilon}}(g({\bm{X}}))
has a tolerance distance scaled by the Lipschitz constant of

. Thus, if
K=\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b}
, then
g\big({\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{{\varepsilon}}^{n}({\bm{X}})\big)={\mathcal{U}}_{K{\varepsilon}}(g({\bm{X}}))
, and we obtain an equivalent reformulation for DRO problem (
5
) in terms of a DRO problem of type (
6
). For
K>\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b}
, we obtain better bounds when the Lipschitz constant

is close to the norm of
{\bm{\beta}}
(i.e.,
g
is close to linear). We refer the reader to
Section

6

.

Note that the largest set is not affected by the choice of decomposition (

{\mathcal{U}}_{\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b}{\varepsilon}}(g({\bm{X}}))
is the largest when we choose the largest possible value for
\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b}
(or equivalently, the smallest possible value for

).

Next, we state two cases of set equality (which implies equality of DRO problems (

) and (
6
)) in

. For this, we first connect the Lipschitz constants of

and
\widetilde{f}
in the decomposition (

Lemma 3.5

Let
g:{\mathds{R}}^{n}\to{\mathds{R}}
be a
K
-Lipschitz function (wrt the
\mathcal{L}^{a}
norm) with decomposition (
7
). Then,

K\leq\min\left\{L+\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b},n^{\frac{1}{b}}\max\{L,\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b}\}\right\},

where
L
is the Lipschitz constant of
\widetilde{g}
(i.e., the non-linear part of
g
) and
\frac{1}{a}+\frac{1}{b}=1
.

Proof 3.6

Let
g({\bm{x}})=\widetilde{g}({\bm{x}}^{(1)})+{\bm{\beta}}^{T}{\bm{x}}^{(2)}
. Then,

\displaystyle\left\lvert g({\bm{x}})-g({\bm{y}})\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\widetilde{g}({\bm{x}}^{(1)})+{\bm{\beta}}^{T}{\bm{x}}^{(2)}-\widetilde{g}({\bm{y}}^{(1)})-{\bm{\beta}}^{T}{\bm{y}}^{(2)}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert\widetilde{g}({\bm{x}}^{(1)})-\widetilde{g}({\bm{y}}^{(1)})\right\rvert+\left\lvert{\bm{\beta}}^{T}({\bm{x}}^{(2)}-{\bm{y}}^{(2)})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq L\left\lVert{\bm{x}}^{(1)}-{\bm{y}}^{(1)}\right\rVert_{a}+\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b}\left\lVert{\bm{x}}^{(2)}-{\bm{y}}^{(2)}\right\rVert_{a},

where the second inequality follows from Hölder’s inequality. To show that
K\leq L+\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b}
, observe that

\displaystyle L\left\lVert{\bm{x}}^{(1)}-{\bm{y}}^{(1)}\right\rVert_{a}+\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b}\left\lVert{\bm{x}}^{(2)}-{\bm{y}}^{(2)}\right\rVert_{a}

\displaystyle\leq L\left\lVert{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\right\rVert_{a}+\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b}\left\lVert{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\right\rVert_{a}

\displaystyle=(L+\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b})\left\lVert{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\right\rVert_{a}.

Next, we show that
K\leq n^{\frac{1}{b}}\max\{L,\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b}\}
. From above we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert g({\bm{x}})-g({\bm{y}})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq L\left\lVert{\bm{x}}^{(1)}-{\bm{y}}^{(1)}\right\rVert_{a}+\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b}\left\lVert{\bm{x}}^{(2)}-{\bm{y}}^{(2)}\right\rVert_{a}

\displaystyle\leq L\left\lVert{\bm{x}}^{(1)}-{\bm{y}}^{(1)}\right\rVert_{1}+\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b}\left\lVert{\bm{x}}^{(2)}-{\bm{y}}^{(2)}\right\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\max\{L,\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b}\}(\left\lVert{\bm{x}}^{(1)}-{\bm{y}}^{(1)}\right\rVert_{1}+\left\lVert{\bm{x}}^{(2)}-{\bm{y}}^{(2)}\right\rVert_{1})

\displaystyle=\max\{L,\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b}\}\left\lVert{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\right\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\leq n^{\frac{1}{b}}\max\{L,\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b}\}\left\lVert{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\right\rVert_{a},

where the first inequality follows from the fact that
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{a}\leq\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{1}
for
a\geq 1
and the last inequality follows from the fact that
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{1}\leq n^{\frac{1}{b}}\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{a}
.

Corollary 3.7

Suppose
g:{\mathds{R}}^{n}\to{\mathds{R}}
satisfies the conditions of
Lemma

3.5
. Let
{\bm{X}}\in\mathcal{L}^{p}_{n}
with
g({\bm{X}})\in\mathcal{L}^{p}
and
{\varepsilon}\geq 0
. Assume any one of the following holds:

i)

a=1
and
\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\geq L

ii)

m=0

Then,
g\big({\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{{\varepsilon}}^{n}({\bm{X}})\big)={\mathcal{U}}_{K{\varepsilon}}(g({\bm{X}}))
.

Proof 3.8

By (
8
) it suffices to show that
K\leq\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b}
for both cases.

For
i)
, by

3.5
,
K\leq\min\left\{L+\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b},n^{\frac{1}{b}}\max\{L,\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b}\}\right\}=\min\{L+\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{\infty},\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\}=\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{\infty}
.

For
ii)
, by
Lemma

3.5
,
K\leq\min\left\{L+\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b},n^{\frac{1}{b}}\max\{L,\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b}\}\right\}=\min\{\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b},n^{\frac{1}{b}}\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b}\}=\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b}

Note that case

corresponds to the case when

is linear, which was first proven in

. Our result shows that equality also holds for a larger class of aggregation functions when the norm of the multivariate Wasserstein distance used to define the uncertainty set is 1. With the set inclusions (

Proposition 3.9

(Worst-case risks under Lipschitz aggregation)

Let
g:{\mathds{R}}^{n}\to{\mathds{R}}
be a
K
-Lipschitz function with decomposition (
7
). Further, let
{\bm{X}}\in\mathcal{L}^{p}_{n}
with
g({\bm{X}})\in\mathcal{L}^{p}
,
{\varepsilon}\geq 0
, and
\rho
be a law-invariant risk functional. Then,

\sup_{Y\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{b}{\varepsilon}}(g({\bm{X}}))}\rho(Y)\leq\sup_{{\bm{Y}}\in{\bm{\mathcal{U}}}^{n}_{{\varepsilon}}({\bm{X}})}\rho\big(g({\bm{Y}})\big)\leq\sup_{Y\in{\mathcal{U}}_{K{\varepsilon}}(g({\bm{X}}))}\rho(Y)\,.

(9)

Moreover, if
a=1
and
\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\geq L
, or if
a\geq 1
and

, then the inequalities become equalities.

Proof 3.10

The inequalities follow from
Theorem

3.1
and
Proposition

3.3
and the equality cases follow from
Corollary

3.7
.

One might wonder if scaling the aggregation function
g
to obtain a smaller Lipschitz constant has any impact on the bounds. By positive homogeneity of the Wasserstein distance, it holds that
W(F_{c\,Y},F_{c\,Z})=c\,W(F_{Y},F_{Z})
for any

and
Y,Z\in\mathcal{L}^{p}
. This implies that
{\mathcal{U}}_{c\,{\varepsilon}}(g({\bm{X}}))=\{Y~|~\tfrac{1}{c}\,Y\in{\mathcal{U}}_{{\varepsilon}}(\tfrac{g({\bm{X}})}{c})\}
. Therefore, if
\rho
is positive homogeneous (i.e.,
\rho(\theta\,Y)=\theta\,\rho(Y)
for all
\theta\geq 0
), then the upper bound in (
9
) is

\sup_{Y\in{\mathcal{U}}_{K{\varepsilon}}(g({\bm{X}}))}\rho(Y)\,=\sup_{\left\{Y~\big|~\tfrac{1}{K}\,Y\in{\mathcal{U}}_{{\varepsilon}}\big(\tfrac{g({\bm{X}})}{K}\big)\right\}}\rho(K\,\tfrac{1}{K}Y)=K\sup_{Z\in{\mathcal{U}}_{{\varepsilon}}\big(\tfrac{g({\bm{X}})}{K}\big)}\rho(Z).

That is, the upper bound in (

) is equal to
K
times the worst-case risk when the uncertainty set has radius
{\varepsilon}
around the reference rv
\widehat{g}({\bm{X}})
, where
\widehat{g}(\cdot):=\frac{g(\cdot)}{K}
is a
1
-Lipschitz function.

3.2
Locally Lipschitz Aggregation Functions

We extend the results of the preceding section to
g
being locally Lipschitz. Specifically we allow the Lipschitz condition on
\widetilde{g}
to hold locally instead of globally. That is, we work with aggregation functions
g:{\mathds{R}}^{n}\to{\mathds{R}}
satisfying

g({\bm{x}})=\widetilde{g}({\bm{x}}^{(1)})+{\bm{\beta}}^{T}{\bm{x}}^{(2)}\,,

(10)

where
{\bm{x}}=({\bm{x}}^{(1)},{\bm{x}}^{(2)})
with
{\bm{x}}^{(1)}\in{\mathds{R}}^{m}
,
{\bm{x}}^{(2)}\in{\mathds{R}}^{n-m}
for some
m\leq n
,
{\bm{\beta}}\in{\mathds{R}}^{n-m}
, and
\widetilde{g}:{\mathds{R}}^{m}\to{\mathds{R}}
is locally Lipschitz. Recall that a function
f\colon{\mathds{R}}^{n}\to{\mathds{R}}
is locally Lipschitz if for any
{\bm{z}}\in{\mathds{R}}^{n}
, there exists a neighbourhood
S
of
{\bm{z}}
such that

restricted to

is Lipschitz.

In the locally Lipschitz case, we require some additional boundedness assumptions on the risk factors
{\bm{X}}:=({\bm{X}}^{(1)},{\bm{X}}^{(2)})
with
{\bm{X}}^{(1)}\in\mathcal{L}^{p}_{m}
and
{\bm{X}}^{(2)}\in\mathcal{L}^{p}_{n-m}

C\subseteq{\mathds{R}}^{m}
, such that

\text{supp}({\bm{X}}^{(1)})\subseteq C,

(11)

where
\text{supp}({\bm{Y}})
denotes the support of a random vector
{\bm{Y}}
. Note that the risk factors associated with the linear part of
g
can be unbounded as long as
{\bm{X}}\in\mathcal{L}^{p}_{n}
and
g({\bm{X}})\in\mathcal{L}^{p}
. Due to this boundedness assumption, we slightly modify the notation of the uncertainty sets used in
Section

3.1

Definition 3.11

(Wasserstein Uncertainty)

For
{\varepsilon}\geq 0
,
m\leq n
, and
C\subseteq{\mathds{R}}^{m}
, the Wasserstein uncertainty set with radius
{\varepsilon}
for random vectors satisfying (
11
) is

{\bm{\mathcal{U}}}^{n,m}_{{\varepsilon},C}({\bm{X}}):=\{{\bm{Z}}\in\mathcal{L}^{p}_{n}~|~W^{n}(F_{{\bm{Z}}},F_{{\bm{X}}})\leq{\varepsilon}\ \text{and}\ \text{supp}\big((Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{m})\big)\subseteq C\},

(12)

where
{\bm{X}}=({\bm{X}}^{(1)},{\bm{X}}^{(2)})\in\mathcal{L}_{p}^{n}
and
\text{supp}({\bm{X}}^{(1)})\subseteq C
.

We note that the set
{\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{{\varepsilon}}^{n}({\bm{X}})
introduced in
Section

2
is a special case of (
12
) with

.

Before stating the next result, we recall a useful property of locally Lipschitz functions from
Scanlon (1970)
. This property guarantees the existence of the Lipschitz constant

in the statement of

3.13

Lemma 3.12

(Theorem 2.1 from
Scanlon (1970)
) Let
(M,d)
be a metric space. A function
f:M\to{\mathds{R}}

.

The following result is the analogue to
Theorem

3.1

(Locally Lipschitz aggregation)

4
DRO with Bregman-Wasserstein Uncertainty

Here, we consider uncertainty sets defined via a BW divergence, which is a generalization of the Wasserstein distance. Differently to the Wasserstein distance, the Bregman divergence is asymmetric and thus suitable when uncertainties arise in an asymmetric manner around the reference distribution. Specifically, we consider uncertainty sets defined as all random vectors that have a BW divergence of at most
{\varepsilon}
around a reference random vector. We refer to
Pesenti et al. (2024b)
for a portfolio application.

Key results are in
Section

4.2
, where we generalize
Theorem

3.1
to BW divergences with separable Bregman generators. In
Section

4.3
, we study a special case of the Mahalanobis distance, which is an example of a BW divergence with a separable generator. For the Mahalanobis distance, we derive bounds for the worst-case risk under weaker assumptions on the risk functional. Finally, in
Section

4.4
, we consider Bregman generators that are compositions of a univariate generator and a real-valued aggregation function.

4.1
Multivariate Bregman-Wasserstein Uncertainty

Before defining the BW divergence, we recall the definitions of a Bregman generator and the Bregman divergence. The Bregman generator associated with a BW divergence determines its key properties. In particular, unlike the Wasserstein distance, the BW divergence is not symmetric unless its Bregman generator is of the form
\Phi({\bm{x}})={\bm{x}}^{T}A{\bm{x}}
for some positive definite matrix
A
. This asymmetry is useful, for example, if
g({\bm{X}})
is the portfolio payoff of an investment. In this case, deviations in the negative direction (i.e., losses) should have larger weight than deviations in the positive direction (i.e., gains).

Definition 4.1

(Bregman Divergence)

Let
\mathcal{X}^{n}\subseteq{\mathds{R}}^{n}
be a convex domain. A Bregman generator is a function
\Phi:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to{\mathds{R}},\ n\geq 1
, that is convex and differentiable. The Bregman divergence associated with Bregman generator
\Phi
is defined as

B_{\Phi}\big({\bm{z}}_{1},{\bm{z}}_{2}\big):=\Phi({\bm{z}}_{1})-\Phi({\bm{z}}_{2})-\nabla\Phi({\bm{z}}_{2})\cdot({\bm{z}}_{1}-{\bm{z}}_{2})\,,\quad{\bm{z}}_{1},{\bm{z}}_{2}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}\,,

where
\nabla\Phi({\bm{z}})
denotes the gradient of
\Phi
and
\cdot
denotes the dot product. For
n=1
, we use
\phi
for univariate generators.

Note that the convexity of
\Phi
guarantees that the Bregman divergence is non-negative. Moreover, if
\Phi
is strictly convex, then the Bregman divergence
B_{\Phi}\big({\bm{z}}_{1},{\bm{z}}_{2}\big)
is zero if and only if
{\bm{z}}_{1}={\bm{z}}_{2}
(and therefore is a mathematical divergence). Here, we use convexity in the non-strict sense unless otherwise stated, and we refer to
Pesenti et al. (2024b)
for examples of non-strictly convex generators to model distributional uncertainty.

Definition 4.2

(BW Divergence)

Let
\Phi:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to{\mathds{R}}
be a Bregman generator. Then, the BW divergence associated with
\Phi
, from cdf
F
to cdf
G
, is defined as

{\mathcal{B}}^{n}_{\Phi}[F,G]:=\inf_{F_{{\bm{X}}}=F,F_{{\bm{Y}}}=G}{\mathbb{E}}\big[B_{\Phi}\big({\bm{X}},{\bm{Y}}\big)\big].

For random vectors
{\bm{X}},{\bm{Y}}
, with cdfs
F,G
, respectively, we use the notation
{\mathcal{B}}^{n}_{\Phi}({\bm{X}},{\bm{Y}}):={\mathcal{B}}^{n}_{\Phi}[F_{{\bm{X}}},F_{{\bm{Y}}}]
, that is, round brackets for random vectors and square brackets for cdfs. Moreover, we omit the dimension superscripts for
n=1
and simply write
{\mathcal{B}}_{\phi}[F,G]:={\mathcal{B}}^{1}_{\Phi}[F,G]
and
{\mathcal{B}}_{\phi}({\bm{X}},{\bm{Y}}):={\mathcal{B}}^{1}_{\Phi}({\bm{X}},{\bm{Y}})
.

When the Bregman generator is the squared 2-norm, i.e.
\Phi({\bm{x}})=\left\lVert{\bm{x}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
, then the BW divergence reduces to the squared
2
-Wasserstein distance with norm
\mathcal{L}^{2}
. Furthermore, for
n=1
, the infimum is achieved by the comonotonic coupling (
Pesenti and Vanduffel (2024)
). In other words, we can rewrite the 1-dimensional BW divergence as

{\mathcal{B}}_{\phi}[F,G]=\int_{0}^{1}B_{\phi}\big(F^{-1}(t),G^{-1}(t)\big)\,dt.

(13)

For
n=1
and using (
13
), it is straightforward to prove that the BW divergence is convex in its first component on the space of quantile functions (
Pesenti et al. (2024b)
). This is however, not necessarily the case for arbitrary dimensions. Indeed, even though the Bregman divergence is convex in its first argument, the BW divergence is generally not convex in its first argument on the space of random vectors. We refer to
Pesenti and Vanduffel (2024)
,
Kainth et al. (2025)
, and the references therein, for detailed discussions of the BW divergence.

With the definition of the BW divergence at hand, we now introduce the BW uncertainty set.

Definition 4.3

(BW Uncertainty Sets)

For
{\varepsilon}\geq 0
, we define the BW uncertainty set around the reference random vector
{\bm{X}}
associated with the Bregman generator
\Phi:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to{\mathds{R}}
by

\bm{\mathfrak{B}}^{n}_{\Phi,{\varepsilon}}({\bm{X}}):=\{{\bm{Z}}~|~{\mathcal{B}}^{n}_{\Phi}[F_{{\bm{Z}}},F_{{\bm{X}}}]\leq{\varepsilon}\}.

Again, we omit the dimension superscript for
n=1
and write
\mathfrak{B}_{\phi,{\varepsilon}}(X):=\bm{\mathfrak{B}}_{\Phi,{\varepsilon}}^{1}(X)
. Moreover, whenever we write
\mathfrak{B}_{\phi,{\varepsilon}}(X)
or
\bm{\mathfrak{B}}^{n}_{\Phi,{\varepsilon}}({\bm{X}})
, we tacitly assume that the uncertainty sets contain at least two, and thus infinitely many elements.

4.2
Separable Bregman Generators

In this section, we consider separable Bregman generators, which are functions
\Phi:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to{\mathds{R}}
of the form
\Phi({\bm{x}})=\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{N}}}\phi_{k}(x_{k})
, where
\phi_{k}:\mathcal{X}\to{\mathds{R}}
,
k\in{\mathcal{N}}
, are themselves Bregman generators. Note that the popular Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence and the Mahalanobis distance are Bregman divergences with separable generators. We refer to
Kainth et al. (2025)
for a detailed discussion of the KL divergence and
Section

4.3
for a discussion of the Mahalanobis distance.

Example 4.4

(KL Divergence)

Define

{\mathcal{S}}:=\left\{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathds{R}}^{n}~|~{\bm{y}}=\Big(\frac{e^{x_{1}}}{1+\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}e^{x_{i}}},\ldots,\frac{e^{x_{n}}}{1+\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}e^{x_{i}}}\Big),\,{\bm{x}}\in{\mathds{R}}^{n}\right\}.

Then,
\Delta^{n}:=\{{\bm{z}}=(z_{0},{\bm{y}})\in{\mathds{R}}^{n+1}~|~{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{S}},\ z_{0}=1-\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}y_{i}\}
is the open unit simplex in
{\mathds{R}}^{n+1}
. Consider the Bregman generator
\Phi({\bm{z}})=\sum_{k=0}^{n}z_{k}\log(z_{k}),\ {\bm{z}}\in\Delta^{n}
(i.e., the negative Shannon entropy). Then,
\nabla\Phi({\bm{z}})=(\log(z_{0})+1,\ldots,\log(z_{n})+1)
and thus

B_{\Phi}[{\bm{z}}_{1},{\bm{z}}_{2}]=\sum_{i=0}^{n}z_{1i}\log\big(\frac{z_{1i}}{z_{2i}}\big),

which is the KL divergence on the simplex. If we remove the restriction onto the simplex, then we recover the generalized KL divergence (see e.g.,
Miller et al. (2023)
).

In the next theorem, we establish a connection between the BW divergences for rvs and random vectors when the multivariate generator
\Phi
is separable. In particular, we construct a new uncertainty set defined via univariate BW divergences that contains the multivariate uncertainty set
\bm{\mathfrak{B}}_{\Phi,{\varepsilon}}^{n}({\bm{X}})
. This connection allows us to derive an analogue to
Proposition

3.9
for separable Bregman generators.

Theorem 4.5

(Separable Bregman generators)

Let
\Phi({\bm{x}})=\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{N}}}\phi_{k}(x_{k})
be a separable Bregman generator. Then, for any
{\varepsilon}\geq 0
and random vector
{\bm{X}}
,

i)

\bm{\mathfrak{B}}_{\Phi,{\varepsilon}}^{n}({\bm{X}})\subseteq\{{\bm{Z}}~|~\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}{\mathcal{B}}_{\phi_{i}}(Z_{i},X_{i})\leq{\varepsilon}\}

ii)

\bm{\mathfrak{B}}_{\Phi,{\varepsilon}}^{n}({\bm{X}})\cap\aleph\,=\{{\bm{Z}}~|~\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}{\mathcal{B}}_{\phi_{i}}(Z_{i},X_{i})\leq{\varepsilon}\}\cap\aleph
,
where

\aleph:=\{{\bm{Z}}~|~Z_{i},X_{i}\ \text{are comonotonic{} for all}\ i\in{\mathcal{N}}\}.

Proof 4.6

We first prove
i)
. For any random vectors
{\bm{X}}
and
{\bm{Z}}
, define the sets
{\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}}_{i}:=\{(Z_{i}^{\prime},X_{i}^{\prime})~|~X_{i}^{\prime}{\overset{d}{=}}X_{i},\;Z_{i}^{\prime}{\overset{d}{=}}Z_{i}\}
,
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
, and

{\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}}:=\{({\bm{Z}}^{\prime},{\bm{X}}^{\prime})~|~(X_{i}^{\prime},Z_{i}^{\prime})\in{\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}}_{i}\ \text{for all}\ i\in{\mathcal{N}}\}.\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathcal{S}}:=\{({\bm{Z}}^{\prime},{\bm{X}}^{\prime})~|~{\bm{X}}^{\prime}{\overset{d}{=}}{\bm{X}},{\bm{Z}}^{\prime}{\overset{d}{=}}{\bm{Z}}\}

Since
\Phi
is separable,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{B}}_{\Phi}^{n}({\bm{Z}},{\bm{X}})

\displaystyle=\inf_{({\bm{Z}}^{\prime},{\bm{X}}^{\prime})\in{\mathcal{S}}}{\mathbb{E}}\big[\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\phi_{i}(Z_{i}^{\prime})-\phi_{i}(X_{i}^{\prime})-\phi_{i}^{\prime}(X_{i}^{\prime})(Z_{i}^{\prime}-X_{i}^{\prime})\big]

\displaystyle=\inf_{({\bm{Z}}^{\prime},{\bm{X}}^{\prime})\in{\mathcal{S}}}\,\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}{\mathbb{E}}\big[\phi_{i}(Z_{i}^{\prime})-\phi_{i}(X_{i}^{\prime})-\phi_{i}^{\prime}(X_{i}^{\prime})(Z_{i}^{\prime}-X_{i}^{\prime})\big]

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{({\bm{Z}}^{\prime},{\bm{X}}^{\prime})\in{\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}}}\,\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}{\mathbb{E}}\big[\phi_{i}(Z_{i}^{\prime})-\phi_{i}(X_{i}^{\prime})-\phi_{i}^{\prime}(X_{i}^{\prime})(Z_{i}^{\prime}-X_{i}^{\prime})\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\inf_{(Z_{i}^{\prime},X_{i}^{\prime})\in{\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}}_{i}}{\mathbb{E}}\big[\phi_{i}(Z_{i}^{\prime})-\phi_{i}(X_{i}^{\prime})-\phi_{i}^{\prime}(X_{i}^{\prime})(Z_{i}^{\prime}-X_{i}^{\prime})\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}{\mathcal{B}}_{\phi_{i}}(Z_{i},X_{i}),

where the inequality holds since
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}}
. Moreover, the second last equality holds since
({\bm{Z}}^{\prime},{\bm{X}}^{\prime})\in{\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}}
if and only if
(Z_{i}^{\prime},X_{i}^{\prime})\in{\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}}_{i}
for all
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
and each summand in the optimization objective depends only on one

.

Since
\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}{\mathcal{B}}_{\phi_{i}}(Z_{i},X_{i})\leq{\mathcal{B}}_{\Phi}^{n}({\bm{Z}},{\bm{X}})
, it follows that for any
{\varepsilon}\geq 0
, the inequality
{\mathcal{B}}_{\Phi}^{n}({\bm{Z}},{\bm{X}})\leq{\varepsilon}
implies that
\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}{\mathcal{B}}_{\phi_{i}}(Z_{i},X_{i})\leq{\varepsilon}
. Therefore, we conclude that
i)
holds.

Second, we prove
ii)
. From
i)
, it follows that

\bm{\mathfrak{B}}_{\Phi,{\varepsilon}}^{n}({\bm{X}})\cap\aleph\subseteq\{{\bm{Z}}~|~\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}{\mathcal{B}}_{\phi_{i}}(Z_{i},X_{i})\leq{\varepsilon}\}\cap\aleph.

To prove the reverse inclusion, it suffices to show that
\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}{\mathcal{B}}_{\phi_{i}}(Y_{i},X_{i})={\mathcal{B}}_{\Phi}^{n}({\bm{Y}},{\bm{X}})
for any

{\bm{Y}}\in\{{\bm{Z}}~|~\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}{\mathcal{B}}_{\phi_{i}}(Z_{i},X_{i})\leq{\varepsilon}\}\cap\aleph\,.

From the proof of
i)
, we have
\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}{\mathcal{B}}_{\phi_{i}}(Y_{i},X_{i})\leq{\mathcal{B}}_{\Phi}^{n}({\bm{Y}},{\bm{X}})
for any random vectors
{\bm{X}}
and
{\bm{Y}}
. Thus, it remains to show that
\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}{\mathcal{B}}_{\phi_{i}}(Y_{i},X_{i})\geq{\mathcal{B}}_{\Phi}^{n}({\bm{Y}},{\bm{X}})
for any
{\bm{Y}}\in\aleph
. As the infimum in the univariate BW divergence is attained by the comonotonic coupling, it follows that

\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}{\mathcal{B}}_{\phi_{i}}(Y_{i},X_{i})=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}{\mathbb{E}}[B_{\phi_{i}}(Y_{i},X_{i})]={\mathbb{E}}[B_{\Phi}({\bm{Y}},{\bm{X}})]\geq{\mathcal{B}}_{\Phi}^{n}({\bm{Y}},{\bm{X}}),

where the inequality follows from the definition of the BW divergence.

We are now ready to derive lower and upper bounds for DRO problem (
5
) with uncertainty quantified with a separable Bregman generator when the risk functional
\rho
is law invariant, subadditive, and comonotonic additive. Recall that a risk functional
\rho
is subadditive if
\rho(X+Y)\leq\rho(X)+\rho(Y)
for all rvs
X,Y
for which the risk functional is well defined. Moreover, a risk functional is comonotonic additive if
\rho(X+Y)=\rho(X)+\rho(Y)
for all comonotonic rvs
X,Y
for which the risk functional is well defined.

Proposition 4.7

(Worst-case risks for separable Bregman generators)

Suppose

ii)

If
{\bm{X}}
is comonotonic

4.3
The Mahalanobis Distance

In
Section

4.2
, we work with an arbitrary separable Bregman generator
\Phi
. Under stronger assumptions on the structure of
\Phi
, we can obtain tighter bounds for the worst-case risk under fewer assumptions on the risk functional
\rho
. One example is the Wasserstein distance discussed in
Section

3.1
. Here, we present an analogue of
Theorem

3.1
when the Bregman generator is the Mahalanobis distance. The Mahalanobis distance is a popular distance used in classification problems (
McLachlan (1999)
) as well as in financial applications, see e.g.,
Jaimungal and Pesenti (2026)
.

Definition 4.9

(Mahalanobis Distance)

Let
Q
be a symmetric, positive-semi-definite
n\times n
matrix. Then, the squared Mahalanobis distance between
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathds{R}}^{n}
and
{\bm{y}}\in{\mathds{R}}^{n}
is given by
({\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}})^{T}Q({\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}})
.

The main result of this subsection is for a special case of the Mahalanobis distance when
Q
is a diagonal matrix. Note that the
\mathcal{L}^{2}
norm is a special case of the Mahalanobis distance with
Q
equal to the identity matrix, i.e.,
\bm{\mathfrak{B}}^{n}_{{\bm{x}}^{T}{\bm{x}},{\varepsilon}}({\bm{X}})={\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{\sqrt{{\varepsilon}}}^{n}({\bm{X}})
and
\mathfrak{B}_{x^{2},K^{2}{\varepsilon}}\big(g(X)\big)={\mathcal{U}}_{K\sqrt{{\varepsilon}}}(g({\bm{X}}))
. Hence,
Theorem

3.1
(with
a=2
and
\lambda_{\min}=\lambda_{\max}=1
) is a special case of this result.

Theorem 4.10

(Mahalanobis distance)

Let
g:{\mathds{R}}^{n}\to{\mathds{R}}
be a
K
-Lipschitz function (wrt the
\mathcal{L}^{2}
norm) with decomposition (
7
) and
{\bm{X}}\in\mathcal{L}^{p}_{n}
with

Proof 4.11

We first prove that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{B}}_{\lambda_{\min}x^{2}}[F_{g({\bm{Z}})},F_{g({\bm{X}})}]

\displaystyle=\inf_{X{\overset{d}{=}}g({\bm{X}}),\ Z{\overset{d}{=}}g({\bm{Z}})}{\mathbb{E}}[\lambda_{\min}(Z-X)^{2}]

\displaystyle=\inf_{g({\bm{X}}^{\prime}){\overset{d}{=}}g({\bm{X}}),\ g({\bm{Z}}^{\prime}){\overset{d}{=}}g({\bm{Z}})}\lambda_{\min}\,{\mathbb{E}}\big[\left\lvert g({\bm{Z}}^{\prime})-g({\bm{X}}^{\prime})\right\rvert^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq K^{2}{\varepsilon}\,,

where the first inequality follows by Lipschitz continuity of

Next, we prove the first set inclusion. Note that since

\mathfrak{B}_{\lambda_{\max}x^{2},\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}{\varepsilon}}\big(g({\bm{X}})\big)=\{V~|~V{\overset{d}{=}}g({\bm{X}})\}

\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{2}>0
. Let
Z\in\mathfrak{B}_{\lambda_{\max}x^{2},\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}{\varepsilon}}(g({\bm{X}}))
. Define the
(n-m)
-dimensional vector

\widetilde{{\bm{\beta}}}:=(\sgn(\beta_{1})\left\lvert\beta_{1}\right\rvert,\ldots,\sgn(\beta_{n-m})\left\lvert\beta_{n-m}\right\rvert),

where
\sgn(\cdot)
is the sign function. Let
{\bm{Z}}=({\bm{Z}}^{(1)},{\bm{Z}}^{(2)})\in{\mathds{R}}^{m}\times{\mathds{R}}^{n-m}
, where
{\bm{Z}}^{(1)}:=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{m})
and

{\bm{Z}}^{(2)}:=\Big(X_{m+1}+\tfrac{\widetilde{\beta}_{1}(Z-g({\bm{X}}))}{{\bm{\beta}}^{T}\widetilde{{\bm{\beta}}}},\ldots,X_{n}+\tfrac{\widetilde{\beta}_{n-m}(Z-g({\bm{X}}))}{{\bm{\beta}}^{T}\widetilde{{\bm{\beta}}}}\Big).

It suffices to show that
g({\bm{Z}})=Z
and
{\mathcal{B}}_{{\bm{x}}^{T}Q{\bm{x}}}^{n}(F_{{\bm{Z}}},F_{{\bm{X}}})\leq{\varepsilon}
. The first fact follows from similar calculations as in the proof of
Proposition

3.3
. For the second claim,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{B}}_{{\bm{x}}^{T}Q{\bm{x}}}^{n}(F_{{\bm{Z}}},F_{{\bm{X}}})

\displaystyle=\inf_{{\bm{X}}^{\prime}{\overset{d}{=}}{\bm{X}},{\bm{Z}}^{\prime}{\overset{d}{=}}{\bm{Z}}}{\mathbb{E}}\big[({\bm{Z}}^{\prime}-{\bm{X}}^{\prime})^{T}Q({\bm{Z}}^{\prime}-{\bm{X}}^{\prime})\big]

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}\big[({\bm{Z}}-{\bm{X}})^{T}Q({\bm{Z}}-{\bm{X}})\big]

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}\big[\lambda_{\max}\sum_{i=1}^{n-m}\frac{\widetilde{\beta}_{i}^{2}\big(Z-g({\bm{X}})\big)^{2}}{({\bm{\beta}}^{T}\widetilde{{\bm{\beta}}})^{2}}\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\big[\lambda_{\max}\big(Z-g({\bm{X}})\big)^{2}\big]}{\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}{\varepsilon}}{\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle={\varepsilon},

where the second equality follows from similar calculations as in the proof of
Proposition

3.3
.

The following corollary follows from
Theorem

4.10
.

Corollary 4.12

Suppose the assumptions of
Theorem

4.10
are satisfied. Then, for any
{\varepsilon}\geq 0
and law-invariant risk functional
\rho
,

\sup_{Y\in\mathfrak{B}_{\lambda_{\max}x^{2},\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}{\varepsilon}}(g({\bm{X}}))}\rho(Y)\leq\sup_{{\bm{Y}}\in\bm{\mathfrak{B}}^{n}_{{\bm{x}}^{T}Q{\bm{x}},{\varepsilon}}({\bm{X}})}\rho\big(g({\bm{Y}})\big)\leq\sup_{Y\in\mathfrak{B}_{\lambda_{\min}x^{2},K^{2}{\varepsilon}}(g({\bm{X}}))}\rho(Y).

Since the (univariate) Bregman divergence satisfies
B_{c\phi}\big({\bm{z}}_{1},{\bm{z}}_{2}\big)=c\,B_{\phi}\big({\bm{z}}_{1},{\bm{z}}_{2}\big)
for
c\geq 0
, the lower bound can be rewritten as a DRO problem over a Wasserstein uncertainty set as follows

\sup_{Y\in\mathfrak{B}_{\lambda_{\max}x^{2},\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}{\varepsilon}}(g({\bm{X}}))}\rho(Y)=\sup_{Y\in\mathfrak{B}_{x^{2},\frac{\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}{\varepsilon}}{\lambda_{\max}}}(g({\bm{X}}))}\rho(Y)=\sup_{Y\in{\mathcal{U}}_{{\varepsilon}^{\prime}}(g({\bm{X}}))}\rho(Y)\,,

where
{\varepsilon}^{\prime}:=\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{2}\sqrt{\frac{{\varepsilon}}{\lambda_{\max}}}
.
Similarly the upper bound can be recast as a Wasserstein DRO problem over the uncertainty set
{\mathcal{U}}_{{\varepsilon}^{\dagger}}(g({\bm{X}}))
with
{\varepsilon}^{\dagger}:=K\sqrt{\frac{{\varepsilon}}{\lambda_{\min}}}
.

We further see that the tolerance distance for the lower bound is proportional to the norm of
{\bm{\beta}}
(i.e., the linear term of the aggregation function), while for the upper bound it is proportional to the Lipschitz constant of
g
. Thus, the bounds are tighter, if the linear term in
g
dominates (i.e.,
K^{2}-\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
is small) and the eigenvalues of
Q
are close to each other. Furthermore, when
g
is linear and
\lambda_{\min}=\lambda_{\max}
, the inequalities become equalities. This is not surprising since the case when
\lambda_{\min}=\lambda_{\max}
corresponds to a scaled version of the squared Wasserstein distance.

4.4
Composable Bregman Generators

Here, we consider composable Bregman generators
\Phi:\mathcal{X}\to{\mathds{R}}
of the form
\Phi({\bm{x}})=\phi(g({\bm{x}}))
, where
g:\mathcal{X}\to{\mathds{R}}
and
\phi:{\mathds{R}}\to{\mathds{R}}
are Bregman generators and
\phi
is non-decreasing. Non-decreasingness of
\phi
(combined with convexity of
g
), ensures that
\Phi
is convex.

Recall that for a DRO problem
\sup_{{\bm{X}}\in{\mathcal{U}}}\rho\big(g({\bm{X}})\big)
with some uncertainty set
{\mathcal{U}}
to be well defined, the aggregation function
g\colon{\mathds{R}}^{n}\to{\mathds{R}}
, mapping input to output, is often convex and
{\mathcal{U}}
is a convex set. Thus, composable Bregman generators allow us to tailor the uncertainty set to the aggregation function at hand. Indeed, for a univariate and non-decreasing Bregman generator
\phi
, the uncertainty set induced by the composable BW divergence, i.e.,
\bm{\mathfrak{B}}^{n}_{\phi\circ g,{\varepsilon}}({\bm{X}})
, is quantified via the Bregman divergence as a composition of the aggregation function and
\phi
. Thus, the aggregation function plays a pivotal role in defining the uncertainty set.

For composable Bregman generators, we again bound the risk-aware DRO problem with multivariate BW uncertainty with a risk-aware DRO problem with univariate BW uncertainty. Depending on the choice of law-invariant risk functional, the univariate DRO problem can be solved analytically. We refer to
Section

5.1
where we solve the univariate BW DRO problem when
\rho
is a signed Choquet integral.

Theorem 4.13

(Composable Bregman generators)

Let
\Phi:\mathcal{X}\to{\mathds{R}}
be a Bregman generator of the form
\Phi({\bm{x}})=\phi(g({\bm{x}}))
, where
g:\mathcal{X}\to{\mathds{R}}
,
\phi:{\mathds{R}}\to{\mathds{R}}
are Bregman generators and
\phi
is non-decreasing. Then, for any law-invariant risk functional and
{\varepsilon}\geq 0
,

\sup_{{\bm{Z}}\in\bm{\mathfrak{B}}^{n}_{\Phi,{\varepsilon}}({\bm{X}})}\rho(g({\bm{Z}}))\,\leq\,\sup_{Z\in\mathfrak{B}_{\phi,{\varepsilon}}(g({\bm{X}}))}\rho(Z).

Proof 4.14

It suffices to show that
{\bm{Z}}\in\bm{\mathfrak{B}}^{n}_{\Phi,{\varepsilon}}({\bm{X}})
implies that
g({\bm{Z}})\in\mathfrak{B}_{\phi,{\varepsilon}}(g({\bm{X}}))
. For any random vector
{\bm{Z}}
,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{B}}^{n}_{\Phi}[F_{{\bm{Z}}},F_{{\bm{X}}}]

\displaystyle=\inf_{{\bm{Z}}^{\prime}{\overset{d}{=}}{\bm{Z}},{\bm{X}}^{\prime}{\overset{d}{=}}{\bm{X}}}{\mathbb{E}}[\Phi({\bm{Z}}^{\prime})-\Phi({\bm{X}}^{\prime})-\nabla\Phi({\bm{X}}^{\prime})\cdot({\bm{Z}}^{\prime}-{\bm{X}}^{\prime})]

\displaystyle=\inf_{{\bm{Z}}^{\prime}{\overset{d}{=}}{\bm{Z}},{\bm{X}}^{\prime}{\overset{d}{=}}{\bm{X}}}{\mathbb{E}}[\phi(g({\bm{Z}}^{\prime}))-\phi(g({\bm{X}}^{\prime}))-\phi^{\prime}(g({\bm{X}}^{\prime}))\nabla g({\bm{X}}^{\prime})\cdot({\bm{Z}}^{\prime}-{\bm{X}}^{\prime})]

\displaystyle=\inf_{{\bm{Z}}^{\prime}{\overset{d}{=}}{\bm{Z}},{\bm{X}}^{\prime}{\overset{d}{=}}{\bm{X}}}{\mathbb{E}}\big[\phi(g({\bm{Z}}^{\prime}))-\phi(g({\bm{X}}^{\prime}))-\phi^{\prime}(g({\bm{X}}^{\prime}))\nabla g({\bm{X}}^{\prime})\cdot({\bm{Z}}^{\prime}-{\bm{X}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad-\phi^{\prime}(g({\bm{X}}^{\prime}))(g({\bm{Z}}^{\prime})-g({\bm{X}}^{\prime}))+\phi^{\prime}(g({\bm{X}}^{\prime}))(g({\bm{Z}}^{\prime})-g({\bm{X}}^{\prime}))\big]

\displaystyle=\inf_{{\bm{Z}}^{\prime}{\overset{d}{=}}{\bm{Z}},{\bm{X}}^{\prime}{\overset{d}{=}}{\bm{X}}}{\mathbb{E}}[B_{\phi}(g({\bm{Z}}^{\prime}),g({\bm{X}}^{\prime}))+\phi^{\prime}(g({\bm{X}}^{\prime}))B_{g}({\bm{Z}}^{\prime},{\bm{X}}^{\prime})]

\displaystyle\geq{\mathcal{B}}_{\phi}[F_{g({\bm{Z}})},F_{g({\bm{X}})}]+\inf_{{\bm{Z}}^{\prime}{\overset{d}{=}}{\bm{Z}},{\bm{X}}^{\prime}{\overset{d}{=}}{\bm{X}}}{\mathbb{E}}[\phi^{\prime}(g({\bm{X}}^{\prime}))B_{g}({\bm{Z}}^{\prime},{\bm{X}}^{\prime})]

\displaystyle\geq{\mathcal{B}}_{\phi}[F_{g({\bm{Z}})},F_{g({\bm{X}})}],

where the last inequality follows from the fact that
\phi^{\prime}(g({\bm{X}}^{\prime}))
is non-negative (since
\phi
is non-decreasing) and the Bregman divergence is non-negative. Thus, for any
{\bm{Z}}\in\bm{\mathfrak{B}}^{n}_{\Phi,{\varepsilon}}({\bm{X}})

{\mathcal{B}}_{\phi}[F_{g({\bm{Z}})},F_{g({\bm{X}})}]\leq{\mathcal{B}}^{n}_{\Phi}[F_{{\bm{Z}}},F_{{\bm{X}}}]\leq{\varepsilon}
.

From the proof, we observe that the quality of the bound depends on the random variable
\phi^{\prime}(g({\bm{X}}^{\prime}))B_{g}({\bm{Z}}^{\prime},{\bm{X}}^{\prime})
. In particular, the bound is better when the expected value of
\phi^{\prime}(g({\bm{X}}^{\prime}))B_{g}({\bm{Z}}^{\prime},{\bm{X}}^{\prime})
is small. Moreover, if

, then equality in
Theorem

4.13
holds; however this is a strong requirement.

5
Explicit Bounds for Signed Choquet Integrals

Next, we derive explicit bounds for the worst-case risk when the risk functional belongs to the class of signed Choquet integrals. In
Section

5.1
, we characterize the distribution that attains the largest risk over a univariate BW uncertainty set. Then, in
Section

5.2
, we apply these results to derive formulae for the bounds established in Sections
3
and
4
. We first recall the definition of a signed Choquet integral, which was introduced by
Choquet (1954)
.

Definition 5.1

(Signed Choquet Integrals)

A signed Choquet integral, denoted
I_{h}
, is a mapping from
\mathcal{L}^{p}
to
\mathbb{R}
given by

I_{h}(X)=\int_{-\infty}^{0}\big[h(\mathbb{P}(X\geq x))-h(1)\big]\,dx+\int_{0}^{\infty}h(\mathbb{P}(X\geq x))\,dx,

(15)

where
h:[0,1]\to{\mathds{R}}
is non-constant, has bounded variation, and satisfies
h(0)=0
. The function
h
is called the distortion function of
I_{h}
.

Note that the integrals in
Equation

15
may be infinite, in which case
I_{h}(X)
may not be well defined. In this paper, we assume that
X
is such that
I_{h}(X)
is finite and thus well defined, and refer to
Wang et al. (2020)
for an extensive discussion on signed Choquet integrals. When the distortion function
h
is non-decreasing and satisfies
h(1)=1
, then
I_{h}(X)
is a distortion risk measure. The class of distortion risk measures includes many commonly used risk measures, including the Value-at-Risk (VaR) and the ES. However, several important risk measures such as the Interquartile Range, Mean-Median Difference, and Gini Deviation belong to the class of signed Choquet integrals but are not distortion risk measures. Furthermore, inverse S-shaped distortions, which are popular in economics (
Tversky and Kahneman (1992)
), belong to the class of signed Choquet integrals.

If the distortion function
h

is absolutely continuous, then by lemma 3 in
Wang et al. (2020)
, the signed Choquet integral has representation

I_{h}(X)=\int_{0}^{1}\gamma(u)F_{X}^{-1}(u)\,du\,,

(16)

where
\gamma:[0,1]\to{\mathds{R}}
is called a distortion weight function and defined by
\gamma(u):=\frac{d^{-}}{dx}h(x)|_{x=1-u}
, where
\frac{d^{-}}{dx}
denotes the left derivative. Throughout the exposition, we only work with signed Choquet integrals with representation (
16
). Moreover, as signed Choquet integrals are law invariant, we use the notation
\widetilde{I}_{h}(F_{X}^{-1}):=I_{h}(X)
whenever
X
has quantile function
F_{X}^{-1}
. Additionally, for the class of distortion risk measures, the distortion weight function
\gamma
is non-negative (since
h
is non-decreasing) and
\int_{0}^{1}\gamma(u)\,du=h(1)-h(0)=1
, thus
\gamma
is a density on
[0,1]
.

5.1
Worst-case Quantile Function for Univariate Risks

The largest signed Choquet integral under univariate BW uncertainty is given by

\max_{\{G^{-1}~|~G\in\mathcal{M}_{p}({\mathds{R}})\}}\widetilde{I}_{h}(G^{-1}),\quad\text{subject to}\quad{\mathcal{B}}_{\phi}(G^{-1},F^{-1})\leq{\varepsilon}\,,

(17)

where
{\mathcal{B}}_{\phi}(G^{-1},F^{-1}):={\mathcal{B}}_{\phi}[G,F]
.
We call the quantile function attaining the maximum the worst-case quantile function. The power
p
of the feasible set
\mathcal{M}_{p}({\mathds{R}})
must be chosen such that the divergence constraint is well defined. For example, for the squared
2
-Wasserstein distance,
p=2
is suitable.

For
\phi(x)=x^{2}
, optimization problem (
17
) coincides with the upper and lower bounds given by
Proposition

3.9
(when
a=p=2
) for an appropriately chosen tolerance distance. Moreover, for a separable Bregman generator
\Phi({\bm{x}})=\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{N}}}\phi_{k}(x_{k})
, we can compute the upper and lower bounds in Proposition
4.7
by solving the optimization problem (
17
) for each
\phi_{k}
separately.

While optimization problem (
17
) with the Wasserstein distance and distortion risk measures has been studied in
Liu et al. (2022)
, little has been done with BW uncertainty. An exception is
Pesenti and Vanduffel (2024)
, which studies optimization problem (
17
) for non-decreasing and strictly concave distortion functions
h
(i.e.,
\gamma
non-negative and strictly decreasing). We generalize their result in two directions. First, we consider concave distortion functions
h
that are not necessarily non-decreasing — corresponding to the class of subadditive signed Choquet integrals. Second, we consider non-decreasing
h
that are not necessarily concave — corresponding to monotone signed Choquet integrals.

To solve optimization problem (
17
) for non-concave
h
, we recall the isotonic projection, which is the continuous analogue to the isotonic regression; see, e.g.,
Brunk (1965)
,
Barlow and Brunk (1972)
,
Barlow et al. (1972)
. The use of isotonic projections to solve problems related to distortion risk measures can for example be found in
Pesenti (2022)
and
Bernard et al. (2024)
. For this we write
\mathcal{L}^{2}((0,1)):=\{\ell\colon(0,1)\to{\mathds{R}}~|~\int_{0}^{1}\ell^{2}(u)\,du<\infty\}
to denote the set of all square-integrable functions defined on
(0,1)
. Moreover, for any
\ell\in\mathcal{L}^{2}((0,1))
, we denote by
\left\lVert\ell\right\rVert_{2}:=\sqrt{\int_{0}^{1}\ell^{2}(u)\,du}
its
\mathcal{L}^{2}
norm.

Definition 5.2

(Isotonic Projection)

The isotonic projection of a function
\ell\in\mathcal{L}^{2}((0,1))
, denoted
\ell^{\uparrow}
, is

\ell^{\uparrow}:=\argmin_{j\in\mathcal{K}}\int_{0}^{1}\big(j(u)-\ell(u)\big)^{2}du,

where
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathcal{L}^{2}((0,1))
is the set of all left-continuous and non-decreasing square-integrable functions.

Note that
\mathcal{K}
can be identified as the set of quantile functions. Furthermore, the isotonic projection preserves the ordering of functions in
\mathcal{L}^{2}((0,1))
, discussed next.

Proposition 5.3

(Ordering property)

Let
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}((0,1))
. If
\ell_{2}(s)\leq\ell_{1}(s)
for all
s\in(0,1)
, then
\ell_{2}^{\uparrow}(s)\leq\ell_{1}^{\uparrow}(s)
. This result also holds when all inequalities are replaced with strict inequalities.

Proof 5.4

See Appendix
A
.

Theorem 5.5

(Worst-case quantile function)

Let
I_{h}
be a signed Choquet integral with representation (
16

. Suppose

\lim_{x\to-\infty}\phi^{\prime}(x)=-\infty

Proposition 5.7

I_{h}(g({\bm{X}}))+\left\lVert{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{2}{\varepsilon}\leq\sup_{{\bm{Y}}\in{\bm{\mathcal{U}}}^{n}_{{\varepsilon}}({\bm{X}})}I_{h}\big(g({\bm{Y}})\big)\leq I_{h}(g({\bm{X}}))+K\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{2}{\varepsilon}.

7
Conclusion

We prove that the image of multivariate Wasserstein uncertainty sets under Lipschitz aggregation functions are contained in and contain Wasserstein uncertainty sets of univariate rvs. This provides lower and upper bounds to risk-aware DRO problems with law-invariant risk functionals. We further show that for special cases of the aggregation function, the upper and lower bounds are equal, and therefore we can solve the multivariate DRO problem without directly computing the multivariate Wasserstein distance. Finally, we generalize our results to uncertainty sets characterized by asymmetric BW divergences and illustrate the bounds on the class of signed Choquet integrals.

Appendix A
Isotonic Projections

Here, we prove some properties of the isotonic projection and show the max-min formulation of isotonic projections, which is essential to prove
Proposition

5.3
. The max-min formulation and its proof for the isotonic regression case can be found in
Barlow et al. (1972)
.

Proposition A.1

(Properties)

Let
\ell\in\mathcal{L}^{2}((0,1)),\ k\geq 0
and
c\in{\mathds{R}}
. Then, all of the following hold:

i)

(kl)^{\uparrow}=k(\ell)^{\uparrow}
,

ii)

(\ell+c)^{\uparrow}=\ell^{\uparrow}+c
,

iii)

\ell^{\uparrow}(s)=\ell(s)+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}(\theta_{i}-\ell(s)){\mathds{1}}_{s\in I_{i}}
,
where
\mathcal{I}
is a countable index set,
I_{i}
are mutually disjoint sub-intervals of
(0,1)
with endpoints
a_{i},b_{i}\in{\mathds{R}}
and
\theta_{i}\in{\mathds{R}}
. Moreover, for any
i\in\mathcal{I}
,

\theta_{i}=\frac{1}{\left\lvert I_{i}\right\rvert}\int_{a_{i}}^{b_{i}}\ell(s)ds,

(30)

where
\left\lvert I_{i}\right\rvert:=b_{i}-a_{i}
is the length of the interval
I_{i}
.

Proof A.2

Case
i)
follows by Theorem 2.6 in
Brunk (1965)
since
\mathcal{K}
, the set of left-continuous and non-decreasing functions, is a closed convex cone.

Case
ii)
follows by noting that

\displaystyle(\ell+c)^{\uparrow}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{j\in\mathcal{K}}\int_{0}^{1}\big(j(s)-(\ell(s)+c)\big)^{2}ds=\argmin_{j\in\mathcal{K}}\int_{0}^{1}\big((j(s)-c)-\ell(s)\big)^{2}ds\,.

By definition of
\ell^{\uparrow}
, the argmin of the right hand side
j^{*}
satisfies
j^{*}-c=\ell^{\uparrow}
, thus
(\ell+c)^{\uparrow}=\ell^{\uparrow}+c
.

Case
iii)
, for a function
f
we denote by
f^{*}
its concave envelope. Then, as
\ell^{\uparrow}(s)=\frac{d}{ds}\big(\int_{0}^{s}\ell(u)du\big)^{*}
, it holds that
\ell^{\uparrow}(s)=\ell(s)+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}(\theta_{i}-\ell(s)){\mathds{1}}_{s\in I_{i}}
by Lemma 5.1 in
Brighi and Chipot (1994)
. To prove
Equation

30
, recall that the intervals
I_{i}
are disjoint, thus we have
\theta_{i}=\argmin_{\theta\in{\mathds{R}}}\int_{I_{i}}(\theta-\ell(s))^{2}ds
. Calculating the argmin yields (
30
).

Before stating the max-min formulation for isotonic projections, we require additional definitions.

Definition A.3

(Average Value of a Function)

Let
S
be a non-empty sub-interval of
(0,1)
and
\ell\colon(0,1)\to{\mathds{R}}
be an integrable function. Then the average value of
\ell
on
S
, is given by
\text{Av}_{l}(S):=\frac{1}{\left\lvert S\right\rvert}\int_{S}l(s)ds
.

For a set with only one element, i.e.
S=\{x\}
, it holds that
\text{Av}_{\ell}(S)=\ell(x)
. Additionally, we drop the subscript
\ell
of
\text{Av}_{\ell}(\cdot)
, whenever it is clear from the context.

Definition A.4

(Upper and Lower Sets -
Barlow et al. (1972)
, Def. 1.4.1)

We define the following two notions of sets.

i)

A set
L\subseteq(0,1)
is a lower set wrt the quasi-order
\leq
, if for any
y\in L
and any
x\in(0,1)
, the inequality
x\leq y
implies that
x\in L
.

ii)

A set
U\subseteq(0,1)
is an upper set wrt the quasi-order
\leq
, if for any
y\in U
and any
x\in(0,1)
, the inequality
x\geq y
implies that
x\in U
.

We denote the class of all lower sets by
S_{\mathcal{L}}
and the class of all upper sets by
S_{{\mathcal{U}}}
.

From the definition of a lower set,
S_{\mathcal{L}}
is the set of all sub-intervals of
(0,1)
with left endpoint
0
. Similarly,
S_{{\mathcal{U}}}
is the set of all sub-intervals on
(0,1)
with right endpoint
1
. Moreover, for any
\ell\in\mathcal{K}
and
a\in{\mathds{R}}
, the set
\{\ell\geq a\}:=\{s\in(0,1):\ell(s)\geq a\}
is an upper set and
\{\ell\leq a\}:=\{s\in(0,1):\ell(s)\leq a\}
is a lower set. This also holds when the inequalities in the sets are replaced with strict inequalities.

We use the following lemma to prove the max-min formulation for isotonic projections. The citation for each part of the lemma references the corresponding result in
Barlow et al. (1972)
for isotonic regression. Note that cases
ii)
and
iii)
are proven in proposition A.3. of
Bernard et al. (2024)
, but we include a short proof for completeness.

Lemma A.5

Let
\ell\in\mathcal{L}^{2}((0,1))
. Then, all of the following hold:

i)

(Theorem 1.3.6) For any function
\psi:{\mathds{R}}\to{\mathds{R}}
,
\int_{0}^{1}(\ell(s)-\ell^{\uparrow}(s))\psi(\ell^{\uparrow}(s))\,ds=0
.

ii)

(Equation 1.3.3) For any
f\in\mathcal{K}
, we have
\int_{0}^{1}(\ell(s)-\ell^{\uparrow}(s))(\ell^{\uparrow}(s)-f(s))\,ds\geq 0
.

iii)

(Equation 1.3.8) For any
f\in\mathcal{K}
, we have
\int_{0}^{1}(\ell(s)-\ell^{\uparrow}(s))f(s)\,ds\leq 0
.

iv)

(Theorem 1.4.3) Let
a\in{\mathds{R}}
,
L\in S_{\mathcal{L}}
and
U\in S_{{\mathcal{U}}}
. Then,

a)

\text{Av}(L\cap\{\ell^{\uparrow}\geq a\})\geq a
,

b)

\text{Av}(L\cap\{\ell^{\uparrow}>a\})>a
,

c)

\text{Av}(U\cap\{\ell^{\uparrow}\leq a\})\leq a
,

d)

\text{Av}(U\cap\{\ell^{\uparrow}<a\})<a
,

whenever the sets are non-empty.

Proof A.6

Barlow et al. (1972)
shows the above results for the isotonic regression, here we provide a proof for the isotonic projection.

For case
i)
, let
\ell\in\mathcal{L}^{2}((0,1))
. Then, it holds that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\big(\ell(s)-\ell^{\uparrow}(s)\big)\psi(\ell^{\uparrow}(s))\,ds

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\int_{I_{i}}(\ell(s)-\theta_{i})\psi(\theta_{i})\,ds=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\psi(\theta_{i})\left(\int_{I_{i}}\ell(s)\,ds-|I_{i}|\theta_{i}\right)=0,

where the first and last equalities follow from
Proposition

A.1
,
iii)
.

For case
ii)
, let
f\in\mathcal{K}
and
\alpha\in[0,1]
, then it holds that
(1-\alpha)\ell^{\uparrow}+\alpha f\in\mathcal{K}
.
By definition of the isotonic projection, the function
g(\alpha)=\int_{0}^{1}\big(\ell(s)-[(1-\alpha)\ell^{\uparrow}(s)+\alpha f(s)]\big)^{2}\,ds
attains its minimum on
[0,1]
at
\alpha=0
. Moreover,
g^{\prime}(0)=2\int_{0}^{1}\big(\ell(s)-\ell^{\uparrow}(s)\big)\big(\ell^{\uparrow}(s)-f(s)\big)\,ds
. Finally, since
g
is quadratic in
\alpha
and attains its minimum at
\alpha=0
, it follows that
g^{\prime}(0)\geq 0
and the desired result holds.

For case
iii)
, we apply
Lemma

A.5
,
i)
to the identity function and obtain
\int_{0}^{1}\big(\ell(s)-\ell^{\uparrow}(s)\big)\ell^{\uparrow}(s)\,ds=0
. Combining this fact with part
ii)
of this lemma yields the inequality.

For case
iv)
, we only prove
b)
as the remaining cases follow by similar arguments.
Let
a,a_{1},a_{2}\in{\mathds{R}}
with
a_{2}>a_{1}
, and
\psi:{\mathds{R}}\to\{0,1\}
such that
\psi(x)={\mathds{1}}_{a_{1}<x<a_{2}}
. By
Lemma

A.5
,
i)
, we have

\int_{0}^{1}\big(\ell(s)-\ell^{\uparrow}(s)\big)\psi(\ell^{\uparrow}(s))\,ds=\int_{0}^{1}\big(\ell(s)-\ell^{\uparrow}(s)\big){\mathds{1}}_{a_{1}<\ell^{\uparrow}(s)<a_{2}}\,ds=0\,.

(31)

Since for any
U\in S_{{\mathcal{U}}}
, the function
{\mathds{1}}_{x\in U}
is non-decreasing, we have
Lemma

A.5
,
iii)
, that

\int_{0}^{1}\big(\ell(s)-\ell^{\uparrow}(s)\big){\mathds{1}}_{s\in U}\,ds\leq 0.

Therefore, for any

,

\displaystyle\int_{L\cap\{\ell^{\uparrow}>a\}}(\ell(s)-a)\,ds

\displaystyle>\int_{L\cap\{\ell^{\uparrow}>a\}}\big(\ell(s)-\ell^{\uparrow}(s)\big)\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{\{\ell^{\uparrow}>a\}}\big(\ell(s)-\ell^{\uparrow}(s)\big)\,ds-\int_{L^{\complement}\cap\{\ell^{\uparrow}>a\}}\big(\ell(s)-\ell^{\uparrow}(s)\big)\,ds\,,

A

A\cap B=B\setminus(A^{\complement}\cap B)

Equation

Equation

\int_{L\cap\{\ell^{\uparrow}>a\}}(\ell(s)-a)\,ds>0

a\,<\,\frac{1}{\left\lvert L\cap\{\ell^{\uparrow}>a\}\right\rvert}\int_{L\cap\{\ell^{\uparrow}>a\}}\ell(s)\,ds=\text{Av}(L\cap\{\ell^{\uparrow}>a\})\,,

provided
