Point-of-Care Ultrasound Foundations in Physics and Image Acquisition: An Innovative Hands-on Active Learning Workshop
Authors: Cherie Tator, Saul Flores, Alan Riley, Kiyetta Alade
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionWe developed an innovative, active learning workshop to introduce foundations of point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) physics and instrumentation. The workshop design aimed to reduce cognitive load and encourage learner-centered engagement through hands-on exploration that reinforces key principles and connects theory to practice.MethodsFirst-year medical students participated in small-group, student-driven exercises to explore POCUS machine controls, transducers, and image acquisition skills. Each group was supported by a faculty member to guide activities and ensure accuracy. Students scanned pickles, hard-boiled eggs, and olives with pits; these items were selected for their low cost, sonographic characteristics, and recognizability. Later in the semester, students demonstrated image optimization skills as part of a formal assessment.ResultsSurvey results suggest that the workshop enhanced understanding of ultrasound physics and its relationship to imaging acquisition. Of 231 students assessed, 97% (n = 223) were able to select the correct probe and 71% (n = 164) successfully optimized their images independently during the subsequent assessment.DiscussionThis interactive workshop provided learners with hands-on experience using familiar household objects to demonstrate the relationship between ultrasound physics, instrumentation, and image generation. Instruction emphasized principles of image formation rather than image interpretation, distinguishing it from patient-based workshops. This approach aims to promote more deliberate image acquisition throughout the curriculum and into clinical practice.

Introduction

Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) is a noninvasive, cost-effective diagnostic tool that provides real-time information at the bedside. When used by a skilled operator, POCUS can improve diagnostic accuracy, expedite clinical decision making, and enhance procedural safety.1,2 Its value in modern clinical practice is well-established, with growing recognition of its importance across medical specialties. As such, integrating POCUS into undergraduate medical education (UME) is increasingly seen as essential for preparing future physicians.3–6 Despite its growing importance, formal education in POCUS remains inconsistent in UME.6 Although many resources are available for learning ultrasound, selecting appropriate materials that are easily attainable, highly recognizable, and affordable can be difficult.

Employing engaging, exploratory methods to teach ultrasound physics and instrumentation may reduce cognitive load by facilitating visualization of complex 3-dimensional structures.7,8 Using familiar household items provides an affordable, reproducible approach for novice UME learners to explore physics and knobology, regardless of prior educational background. Rather than focusing on unfamiliar human anatomy, recognizable objects such as pickles, eggs, or olives allow students to concentrate on image formation.7–9 While water or gel-based phantoms and task trainers illustrate spatial relationships, they often require additional time, materials, and maintenance.9

The workshop aims were to provide hands-on experience where learners could directly correlate ultrasound physics and instrumentation with image acquisition through interactive activities, and to decrease learners’ cognitive load by using items they are already familiar with. These foundational skills were then measured in subsequent assessments as part of a longitudinal curriculum design.10 The purpose of this report is to describe the development and implementation of an active learning workshop providing foundational knowledge on ultrasound physics and instrumentation.

Methods

The workshop review process was conducted under a protocol approved by the Baylor College of Medicine Institutional Review Board (no. H-52771; April 24, 2023). Our institutional POCUS curriculum is longitudinal, with activities integrated within multiple courses. First-year medical students (MS1) were assigned an asynchronous module and given 1 hour of protected time during the first week of their first academic year of UME (Appendix A). The module provided foundational information on ultrasound physics and instrumentation. In the second and third week of the academic year, MS1 learners attended the Introduction to POCUS workshop. This workshop was designed as a 1-hour 45-minute session, accommodating 16 students per session to provide a low student-to-instructor ratio (4:1).11 We offered the number of sessions needed to allow each student to participate once.

At the completion of the workshop, a survey was offered (Appendix B). All MS1 students completed additional activities throughout the semester, including modules and hands-on scan sessions, which reinforced ultrasound physics and instrumentation. At the conclusion of the semester, all MS1 students underwent a formal, formative hands-on assessment involving tasks from the POCUS workshop, with evaluators assessing their performance and providing feedback on what went well and how they could improve in real time (Appendix C).

We selected POCUS faculty through a college-wide application search with personal statements, curriculum vitae review, letters of recommendation, and an interview process. This process allowed us to select a multidisciplinary team of qualified faculty with proven experience in the clinical utilization and teaching of POCUS. Members from this multidisciplinary team served as our POCUS experts. Prior to the workshop, the experts attended a live, virtual train-the-trainer session to become familiar with the workshop objectives and design (Appendix D). Those unable to attend the live session were asked to view the recorded session and attest to their completion of the video review.

To perform each workshop activity, each station had 2 ultrasound machines from different manufacturers and suppliers (Appendix E). Machines were either cart-based or laptops. Each machine had 3 probes available for use, linear array, phased array, and curvilinear array. The room was set up with 4 pairs of 3 × 5–ft tables and 5 chairs per station, with nearby electrical outlets at each station. Instructor cards (Appendix F) and student worksheets (Appendix G) were placed on the tables. One POCUS faculty was designated as the overall facilitator for the sessions, keeping track of time and providing directions for each activity (Appendix H) as well as offering additional assistance to faculty.

Instructor cards and student worksheets were created for this workshop as an outline of expectations for faculty and a guided experience for our learners. Student worksheets were created as an active learning strategy to guide their activities. Each student was provided with a copy and encouraged to fill out the answers. Students worked as a group to complete the items on the worksheets. Each activity introduced key concepts while building upon the previous section(s) (Figure 1). The activities were introduced separately to remove distractions and to foster a purposeful and intentional approach to ultrasound as these learners continue in their career. Instructor cards described each activity with an outline of suggested student interactions, a student worksheet summary, and an answer key for questions posed on the worksheet. These cards were laminated to allow them to be sanitized and reused.

Summary of the key features of 5 activities in each session within the point-of-care ultrasound physics and basic imaging acquisition workshop.

We created a postworkshop survey aimed to ascertain students’ perception of the workshop and their perceived preparedness from asynchronous didactics, with students rating their agreement with items using 3- or 5-point Likert scales and given an opportunity to provide free-response comments (Appendix B). Students were informed about the survey through announcements and accessed it via posted QR codes, which linked to our institutionally approved survey platform, Microsoft Forms (Office 365).

To gauge the effectiveness of our workshop, we incorporated foundational POCUS skills within the low-stakes, formative, hands-on assessment at the end of the semester (Appendix C). During this assessment, students were required to select the appropriate transducer, demonstrate correct transducer handling technique, identify image orientation, request adjustments to depth and gain, and follow recommendations using sonographic terminology to successfully acquire an image.

Results

The survey (Figure 2) was accessible to a subset of participants, specifically, 42% (n = 96) of the 231 workshop attendees. Among these 96 students who had the option to complete the survey, 25 responded, representing an overall participation rate of 11% and a 26% response rate within the subgroup offered the survey. Responding students gave positive feedback, including “It didn't feel like a physics class which made it a great session because I was still learning,” “this was awesome,” and “I really liked the teacher to student ratio!” Most of the respondents indicated feeling they had adequate time to scan, but when asked whether the sessions kept a good pace and allowed the students to complete the objectives, 88% (n = 22) agreed, while 12% (n = 3) felt there was too much time for each activity.

First-year medical student perceptions of the point-of-care ultrasound physics and basic imaging acquisition workshop with regard to comfort, time, and knowledge gained (N = 25 survey respondents).

A total of 231 learners completed the end of semester skills assessment, with oversight and feedback from 5 assessors (Figure 3). Of these learners, 71% (n = 164) were able to correctly identify whether their image needed to be optimized using depth and/or gain. When choosing the correct probe to obtain the view, 97% of learners (n = 223) correctly identified, without help, which probe they needed.

Results from the end of semester skills assessment measuring students’ ability to adjust depth and gain and to select the appropriate transducer after participating in the point-of-care ultrasound physics and basic imaging acquisition workshop (N = 231 learners). Discussion

POCUS has become an integral part of many subspecialties, driving the demand for expansion of its teaching into UME. Ultrasound physics is a necessary but sometimes difficult topic to teach and learn. This innovative, hands-on ultrasound physics workshop allowed first-year medical students to become more familiar with basic operations of ultrasound machines and develop applied knowledge of how images are generated while also decreasing the students’ cognitive load. Scanning familiar household objects allows students to have a clear mental model of their subject, reducing the mental demand associated with unfamiliar human anatomy. This activity highlights how ultrasound physics can distort reality and produce misleading representations. In a recently published workshop, plastic containers were used as phantoms; although fruits were visible through it, image detail was somewhat compromised due to attenuation from the container structure, and there was no assessment included.9 Using everyday materials fosters curiosity, minimizes uncertainty, and ensures affordability across resource settings.8

Hands-on workshops translate theoretical knowledge into tangible and functional experiences, but it is crucial for students to have a safe and supportive learning environment.12–14 Faculty engagement further enhances the experience by encouraging creativity and critical thinking.8 The objectives guided the design of this workshop, ensuring sufficient time for students to safely explore ultrasound interactions with inanimate objects. Facilitator training was implemented to ensure consistency and preparedness and minimize variability in the learning experience. The active learning sessions encouraged student participation to lessen the feeling of hierarchy, with a facilitator guiding and enhancing the discussions. These intentional strategies created a psychologically safe environment for reinforcing asynchronous didactic content through kinesthetic engagement while scanning inanimate objects. Overall, this approach offered a cost-effective yet impactful learning experience.14 This workshop also provided a unique opportunity to process information on ultrasound physics and instrumentation, as well as to identify image orientation, which could prove beneficial for multiple types of adult learners. The inclusion of student worksheets and inanimate objects provided students with a team-based learning approach, with faculty available to answer questions and elaborate as needed. This design provided an atmosphere of curiosity and creative thinking.

Our workshop was geared toward new MS1 learners; however, it can be used or adapted to the needs of any institution. Using an assortment of equipment is recommended to highlight differences in machine operation between vendors, makes, and models, even though the underlying physics remains consistent. Additional household objects can be incorporated depending on their sonographic characteristics, and worksheets can be adapted for graduate-level learners, who generally have a strong grasp on anatomy and do not need the simplicity that household objects offer. Incorporating pickles, eggs, and olives as learning tools is cost effective when compared with use of standardized patients.

There were several overarching areas for clarification and improvement of the workshop to allow for continued success at our institution and for effective reproducibility at other institutions, including the areas of workshop design, implementation, and instructor preparedness (Figure 4). After reviewing learner feedback, assessment performance, and spontaneous voluntary feedback from our POCUS faculty, we were able to define revisions for future workshop iterations.

Limitations

Prior to the POCUS assessment, all MS1 students participated in a second hands-on workshop dedicated to cardiac and pulmonary imaging. Consequently, student performance cannot be attributed solely to the Introduction to POCUS workshop.10 Repetition and a multifaceted educational approach likely contributed to their success.15 Curricular constraints permit only 1 POCUS assessment per semester for MS1 learners, which limited our ability to perform a postworkshop assessment prior to additional POCUS training. Including such an assessment would have strengthened the validity of our findings. To evaluate image optimization, we deliberately selected a single focus for these early learners, which may have contributed to their strong performance. In clinical assessments further along in their UME POCUS education, combinations of these items and additional domains will be necessary to evaluate continued growth. Our students’ near-perfect probe selection during the workshop activities is likely attributable to multiple factors. We believe the students were knowledgeable with regard to the correct probe for the intended cardiac exam; however, additional factors may have influenced this outcome.

At our institution, survey fatigue among UME students is a concern, and the school of medicine is intentional in minimizing ad hoc student evaluations and surveys. Approval to disseminate our survey was delayed, resulting in fewer than half of students having the opportunity to complete it. Additionally, we did not collect retrospective evaluations from students who completed the workshop before the survey became available. Although survey feedback was highly positive, the overall response rate was low, and the data reflected only a subset of participating students, introducing potential response bias.

Several factors may have influenced the generalizability of this workshop and the reproducibility of the results. Despite standardized train-the-trainer sessions, variability in teaching styles may have introduced inconsistencies, as some faculty emphasized clinical context while others adhered strictly to the educational objectives. Verbal cues followed standard ultrasound terminology but may not be universally accepted as terminology, and probe manipulation differs across specialties.16 Finally, the high-fidelity simulator differs from conventional ultrasound machines; students did not manually select probes but chose between 2 options on a cart, and image optimization was performed by evaluators rather than students, reducing task complexity. Although not a barrier at our institution, other potential challenges warrant consideration. Successful implementation of this workshop requires adequate facilities and sufficient resources to overcome budgetary constraints for equipment and faculty support.

Conclusion

Our hands-on workshop created an engaging environment for students to apply theoretical knowledge to practical ultrasound use and image acquisition. Students demonstrated the ability to operate basic ultrasound functions, optimize images, and use ultrasound terminology in subsequent assessments. Although this workshop is part of a longitudinal curriculum, it can also serve as a broadly applicable, stand-alone introduction to POCUS or ultrasound and be tailored to specific complementary session needs. This approach offers a practical framework to enhance student engagement and reinforce foundational physics through experiential learning. Future directions may include condensing workshop time by 15 minutes, reordering activities, and revising train-the-trainer materials to emphasize consistency in sonographic terminology and session objectives. To improve faculty participation, training will be offered asynchronously rather than through scheduled meetings. Additionally, surveys will be distributed immediately after each workshop to ensure all students have the opportunity to provide feedback on their experience.
