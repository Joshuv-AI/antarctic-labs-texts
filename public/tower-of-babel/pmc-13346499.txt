Micro-computed tomography indicates Eurotamandua, putative anteater from the Eocene of Messel, Germany, is related to pangolins, not xenarthrans
Authors: Kenneth D Rose, Renate Rabenstein, Jörg Habersetzer, Timothy J Gaudin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEurotamandua joresi, based on an articulated skeleton unearthed half a century ago at the middle Eocene Messel Pit near Darmstadt, Germany, was initially described as the oldest known anteater and the only member of mammalian clade Xenarthra from outside the New World. The implied biogeographical link to South America raised suspicions about the relationships of Eurotamandua and its similarity to true anteaters (Vermilingua). Because the skeleton was held in a private collection for many years, only a cast was available for study after the initial report. Even when the original specimen became accessible, it was clear that important anatomical features were not visible because many bones were in articulation, and the skeleton was compressed in matrix. Moreover, it had been encased in fiberglass-reinforced resin, further obscuring skeletal details. Here we report new evidence revealed through micro-computed tomography of the entire skeleton and digital segmentation of individual elements. For the first time, phylogenetically critical anatomy can be seen in three dimensions. Many unique features, especially of the forelimb, indicate that Eurotamandua was a highly specialized digger most closely related to extinct Holarctic palaeanodonts (Pholidotamorpha: pangolins and their extinct relatives) and not to Xenarthra. Introduction

The Messel Pit Fossil Site, declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1995, has produced some of the most extraordinary Eocene fossils in the world1,2. Particularly noteworthy is the unique skeleton of the enigmatic Eocene mammal Eurotamandua joresi (Hessisches Landesmuseum Darmstadt HLMD-Me 17000), which was discovered in the oil shale of the Messel Pit in 1974 by amateur paleontologist Gerhard Jores. He and fellow collector Ulrich Kessler embedded the fossil in a plaque of polyester resin reinforced with fiberglass, with the right side exposed, a method of preservation that has long been used for Messel fossils. While this procedure results in spectacular display specimens1,2, it has the disadvantage of obscuring critical anatomical details, making scientific study difficult and interpretation problematic. Even so, initial preparation of the specimen revealed an unusual skeleton unlike any other that had been found at Messel. This prompted Jores to invite Gerhard Storch, curator of mammals at the Senckenberg Museum and Research Institute in Frankfurt, to study and publish a description of the skeleton3. No other remains of Eurotamandua have been found at Messel, but two isolated forelimb bones (a humerus and an ulna) from the Unterkohle of the Geiseltal lignite mine were subsequently found in the collection of the Geiseltalmuseum in Halle and were assigned to Eurotamandua4. Both Messel and the Geiseltal Unterkohle are of middle Eocene age (earliest Lutetian, European biostratigraphic zone MP 11), c. 48.0 Ma5.

With a total head and body length of about 86 cm (122 cm including the tail), Eurotamandua joresi is one of the largest mammals in the Messel local fauna. In his initial study of the skeleton, Storch3 emphasized its overall resemblance in size and body form to the extant anteater Tamandua (Xenarthra: Myrmecophagidae), including a tubular, edentulous snout, and a robust, fossorially-adapted skeleton. He enumerated nearly 20 unusual or unique anatomical features he considered to be present in Eurotamandua that were otherwise diagnostic of Xenarthra or Myrmecophagidae (the latter now divided into two families constituting Vermilingua, or New World anteaters). Of paramount importance he cited the presence of xenarthrous intervertebral joints, ischial–pseudosacral synostosis, and specialized anatomy of the manual third digit. Based on his comprehensive study, Storch3 concluded that Eurotamandua belonged to the same family as living anteaters, which were then all assigned to Myrmecophagidae (Xenarthra: Vermilingua). Adding to its significance, it was by far the oldest vermilinguan—about 30 million years older than the oldest known New World anteater6.7—and the only one known from outside Central and South America. Additional characters supporting vermilinguan affinities were added over the next two decades8,9.

Meanwhile, other researchers began to doubt the validity of many of the characters thought to unite Eurotamandua with Vermilingua, and ultimately to question the Messel fossil’s relationship to Xenarthra10–16. Unfortunately, the disputed characters often proved difficult to resolve, even using radiography, because of the way the skeleton is preserved—articulated, flattened and somewhat deformed, with only one side exposed in the resin plaque. It was generally believed that only additional specimens might clarify these features.

The advent of micro-computed tomography capable of scanning large specimens like Eurotamandua changed this outlook, by providing access to detailed anatomical data previously thought to be impossible to recover. In 2011 we arranged for the holotype skeleton of Eurotamandua joresi to be scanned at high resolution. Six micro-CT-scans were made, two of which were considered most suitable for segmentation. These two scans have different resolutions (see Methods). Processing the scans, including painstaking segmentation of individual articulated elements, took several years to accomplish. The result was a 3D model of the skeleton, virtually extracted from surrounding matrix and resin (Fig. 1), in which many bones were subsequently isolated (disarticulated) from each other for detailed study17–19. For the first time individual elements could be examined three-dimensionally, and previously obscured joint surfaces and other details studied at high magnification, in order to reassess phylogenetically informative characters of the skeleton, including those that led to Storch’s conclusion.

The anatomical data that emerged from our micro-CT study were incorporated into a phylogenetic analysis based on the character-taxon matrix of Gaudin et al.16. The new analysis includes nearly 100 new or modified characters combined with additional taxa, in order to reevaluate the phylogenetic position of Eurotamandua, particularly with respect to Xenarthra and Pholidota. In the discussion below, character numbers refer to those given in Supplementary File S3 or in reference 16.

Results

Reassessment of supposed vermilinguan features in Eurotamandua

Storch identified apparent accessory intervertebral joints (= xenarthrous joints)3 on three posterior thoracic and/or lumbar vertebrae in Eurotamandua. As in vermilinguans, and xenarthrans generally, the joints appeared to form between the anapophyses of the anterior vertebra and the base of the metapophysis and transverse process of the succeeding vertebra20. Although similar accessory joints have been reported in some Mesozoic nontherian mammals21,22, among eutherians they are present only in post-diaphragmatic vertebrae of Xenarthra and are typically best developed in the lumbar series. Consequently, if this trait could be verified in Eurotamandua, it would be compelling evidence of xenarthran affinities. After restudy of the vertebral column, however, Szalay and Schrenk13 convincingly argued that the vertebrae in question are lumbars, which lack any evidence of the progressive caudal enlargement of the anapophyses or of articular facets around the processes, such as occur in vermilinguans. Instead, they could detect only damage and matrix in the critical region. Other researchers reached the same assessment10,20. Upon later re-examination, Storch acknowledged that anatomy he previously interpreted as xenarthrous joints could no longer be confirmed, owing perhaps to damage during preparation or casting9. Our micro-CT data of Eurotamandua also did not reveal any evidence of xenarthrous articulations in the thoracic or lumbar vertebrae (Fig. 2). Indeed, the morphology of the anapophyses in Eurotamandua closely approximates that in the palaeanodonts Metacheiromys23(AMNH 131777) and Tubulodon24.

Likewise, synostosis between the ischium and the transverse processes of proximal caudal vertebrae incorporated into the sacrum (pseudosacrals) was rejected by Szalay and Schrenk13. The pelvic region of the skeleton is crushed and fragmented, making interpretation ambiguous, especially on the right side where Storch had identified synostosis3(Fig. 1). However, unequivocal evidence of synostosis or of a resulting sacroischial foramen on the right side is lacking in Eurotamandua. By rotating the 3D model of the skeleton in order to view the pertinent region at different angles, we confirmed that the pseudosacral transverse processes are superimposed and compressed onto the ischium, creating the appearance of sacroischial fusion where no synostosis is present (Supplementary Fig. S1 online). This is confirmed on the left side, where the transverse processes also appear to be intact, not broken, and show no evidence of fusion to the ischium.

The tubular, edentulous snout of Eurotamandua is one of its most obvious resemblances to vermilinguans, although it is longer than that of Cyclopes (Vermilingua: Cyclopedidae), and slightly or much shorter than those of Tamandua and Myrmecophaga, respectively. In this feature Eurotamandua also resembles pangolins. The edentulous jaws (character 393) and tubular shape of the snout are specializations for ant- and termite-feeding that occur only in these groups among placental mammals, and they evolved independently in Xenarthra and Pholidota, which are only distantly related25. Therefore, this morphology does not imply a unique relationship between Eurotamandua and vermilinguans.

Storch considered the specialized anatomy of the manual third digit, especially its metacarpal (Mc III), to be one of the most important characters linking Eurotamandua with Vermilingua3, epitomizing its fossorially-adapted forelimb. He cited specifically the robustness of the bones of the manus, the conspicuous dorsal tubercle on Mc III (character 439, for insertion of the extensor carpi radialis muscle), and the prominent distal keel that is dorsoventrally extensive on the metacarpal. The first two features are readily verified in the skeleton; but a prominent distal keel—which is particularly characteristic of myrmecophagids (but also exists in some pangolins)—in fact was not visible in the embedded skeleton. It was inferred from a bone fragment near the distal end of the metacarpal3, which is not the dislodged distal keel but appears to be an extraneous bone chip (see Fig. 19 in ref. 13). The absence of a prominent keel on Mc III and the other metacarpals was only confirmed with certainty following CT-scanning and segmentation of the individual metacarpals (further described in the next section). In addition to the third metacarpal, Storch mentioned the short and stout proximal phalanx of this digit3. Relatively short, robust elements of the manus are widespread among digging mammals, including other xenarthrans besides vermilinguans, as well as palaeanodonts, pangolins, chrysochlorids, burrowing rodents, moles, the marsupial mole Notoryctes, and echidna26. In this context, it is notable that only the third digit is robust and hypertrophied in vermilinguans, whereas the other metacarpals and phalanges are slender and relatively elongate compared to their girth27. In the manus of Eurotamandua, however, all of the digits are robust (although the first and fifth are reduced), and all the metacarpals and phalanges (except the unguals) are short and very wide (characters 445, 447).

Another character Storch considered shared by Eurotamandua and vermilinguans is a secondary scapular spine3, situated caudal to the main scapular spine and more or less parallel to it. In vermilinguans and other xenarthrans, the secondary spine is a ridge independent from the main spine and from the caudal border of the scapula. The secondary spine demarcates the postscapular or teres fossa from the infraspinous fossa, whereas the main scapular spine separates supra- and infraspinous fossae. Presence of a secondary scapular spine is considered a diagnostic trait of Xenarthra10,15,28,29, not just Vermilingua. Nevertheless, it is not limited to xenarthrans, and is present in members, often diggers, of several other orders10, including derived palaeanodonts30. The secondary spine in Eurotamandua (character 202; Supplementary Fig. S2a online) is confluent with the thickened caudal border of the scapula near the glenoid end. It diverges posteriorly toward the vertebral border (but fades out before reaching it), creating a small postscapular fossa. But unlike the vermilinguan condition, it is much weaker and converges distally toward the scapular spine well before reaching the glenoid. This is quite different from the vermilinguan condition and compares more closely to that of the palaeanodont Metacheiromys in shape and proportions10,13.

Storch also noted a large coracoid process as a feature shared with Vermilingua9. The scapulae are not well preserved in the Eurotamandua skeleton: the CT data confirm that the right and left scapulae are compressed together so the perimeters of the two blades cannot be easily differentiated, the cranial margin is unclear, and the glenoid region is missing. Consequently, the coracoid process cannot be identified and is probably not preserved. What Storch indicated as the coracoid process9 is more likely an artifact generated by compression of the two scapular blades together. Nevertheless, it is notable that the scapular spine in Eurotamandua is strong and elevated, tilts caudally, and widens toward the acromion–all of which are features of Metacheiromys, but not of vermilinguans.

Most of the other characters suggesting vermilinguan affinities3,8,9 are either ambiguous in the fossil even after micro-CT scanning (e.g., extent of various cranial elements in and near the orbit, absence of a ventral tubercle on the atlas vertebra) or are not restricted to Vermilingua or even to Xenarthra (see Supplementary File S1 online). Notably, although the skull presents the outward appearance of being complete and well preserved, it shows evidence of damage and deformation, especially posteriorly13. Consequently, many sutures, especially in and near the orbit and in the basicranium, are difficult to discern; and the micro-CT scan shows that the braincase is filled with matrix containing a hash of bone fragments, rendering its internal anatomy largely indecipherable. Several of the characters that are not restricted to vermilinguans are closer anatomically to conditions in Palaeanodonta, as indicated above and in Supplementary File S1 (e.g., morphology of the premaxilla, mandibular symphysis [see next section], jugal, humeral entepicondyle, ulnar anconeal process, and astragalus).

New data accessed through micro-CT scanning

Previous accounts of the anatomy of Eurotamandua were necessarily limited to exposed elements in the embedded skeleton and what could be determined from two-dimensional radiographs. In this section we highlight additional important anatomical traits, newly revealed by micro-computed tomography, that bear on the phylogenetic position of Eurotamandua. A detailed description of the skeleton based on the high-resolution micro-CT scans is presented elsewhere31.

Despite deformation and damage to the skull, computed tomography has elucidated several details of cranial anatomy (see Supplementary File S1 online), including one in the basicranium that is of particular importance. Previous radiographic evidence suggested the presence of a large hypotympanic sinus (anterior accessory bulla) anterior to the auditory bulla in Eurotamandua, an important synapomorphy of myrmecophagidsexcept for Cyclopes8. However, this structure has been reinterpreted as more likely the inflated squamosal at the posterior end of the zygomatic arch13. Rotation of the 3D model generated from our micro-CT scans clarifies that it is an epitympanic sinus extending into the inflated squamosal (characters 386, 391; Supplementary Fig. S3 online). Thus it is situated anterolateral and dorsal to the tympanic bulla, rather than anteromedial and ventral to the tympanic bulla like the hypotympanic sinus (accessory bulla) in myrmecophagids8,32. An inflated squamosal is characteristic of palaeanodonts, in which it is continuous with the epitympanic sinus that houses the enlarged malleus30,32,33. Reinterpretation of the putative hypotympanic sinus as the inflated squamosal/epitympanic sinus invalidates another vermilinguan feature and adds to characters shared with palaeanodonts.

Another notable cranial trait, which was not evident in the embedded skeleton, concerns the hard palate. Radiographs of the cranium appeared to show the palate of Eurotamandua extending posteriorly8, a hallmark of vermilinguans. However, based on our micro-CT data and the 3D model generated from the scans, the choanae (which mark the boundary between the nasal cavity and the nasopharynx) appear to be situated medial to the zygomatic process of the maxilla, i.e., well forward of the condition in vermilinguans. This would indicate that the hard palate in Eurotamandua was not elongated as in vermilinguans, in which it is distinctive in extending far posteriorly, medial to the ear region27,32.

The mandible of Eurotamandua consists of very shallow, elongate, toothless dentaries (characters 393, 394) with a distinct but reduced coronoid process (Fig. 1, Supplementary Fig. S4 online). Among extant mammals, only vermilinguans, pangolins, and echidnas approximate this morphology. Storch described the symphyseal region of the mandible of Eurotamandua as ventrally downturned3,9, as in vermilinguans34–36; however, the 3D model generated from our micro-CT scans shows that although the dentaries may be incomplete at the anterior end, they do not bend ventrally at the tip, but instead slightly laterally. Moreover, the probable symphyseal scar (on the previously hidden anteromedial surface) is in line with the long axis of the ramus rather than located at the base of a downturned dentary as in vermilinguans. In addition, visible in the 3D model is a weak ridge-like prominence on the posteromedial surface of the dentary, which thickens and inclines posteroinferiorly, closely resembling the distinctive medial buttress of palaeanodonts and Eomanis15,37,38(character 395, Supplementary Fig. S4 online).

As demonstrated by the anatomy of the third digit (see previous section), the forelimb of Eurotamandua is highly adapted for fossorial behavior. Many new details of specific forelimb modifications are revealed in the micro-CT data. In addition to the scapular characters noted above, a significant new feature is revealed in the 3D model generated by the micro-CT scans: the acromion process, visible on the left scapula, is prominent and extends over the proximal humerus, bifurcating into a larger anterior branch and a shorter posterior metacromion (characters 198, 199). This bifid morphology closely resembles that of Metacheiromys (Supplementary Fig. S2 online) and differs from the unbranched, hook-shaped acromion of vermilinguans, which projects anterior to the glenohumeral joint.

Now that the humerus of the holotype can be viewed three-dimensionally, it is clear that the humeral head (missing in the Geiseltal specimen) is distinctly ovoid, its long axis running proximomedial to distolateral (characters 205, 402) and facing mainly posteriorly, compared with the rounder and more proximally-facing humeral head of Tamandua. The greater tuberosity is prominent and extends proximally slightly above the humeral head (character 223), unlike the lower, more reduced greater tuberosity of vermilinguans. The Geiseltal humerus was previously shown to bear detailed similarity to that of palaeanodonts (and to contrast with myrmecophagids) in the morphology of the deltopectoral and supinator crests, deltoid tubercle, capitulum, and lateral articular shelf14. The micro-CT data confirm all of these features in the holotype, apart from the lateral articular shelf which is broken off. It also enables a better assessment of the shape of the medial epicondyle, which is detached from the humerus. It is medially elongate but less proximodistally expanded than in myrmecophagids. In all these features, Eurotamandua is quite similar to palaeanodonts (Fig. 3). Moreover, Eurotamandua lacks the distinctive features of the humerus of vermilinguans, including the separate deltoid and pectoral crests, the deltoid crest carrying a large distolateral prong39.

The forearm long bones are short and robust. The ulnae of the holotype, viewed three-dimensionally, have a very prominent, expanded, and medially inflected olecranon process (characters 227, 228, 404, 405), which is relatively longer than in Tamandua and other vermilinguans and flares out proximally, closely similar to that of palaeanodonts. As in the Geiseltal ulna14, the semilunar notch is wide (character 230) and tightly curved (as in palaeanodonts but unlike the more open notch in vermilinguans), with a prominent coronoid process and a wide and sharp, moderately projecting anconeal process (character 231), in contrast to the narrow, reduced anconeal process of vermilinguans. The ulnar shaft distal to the notch is laterally compressed, and relatively deep. Its posterior margin is sigmoidal, convex proximally and concave distally (character 406). It thus resembles the ulna of palaeanodonts more than the relatively straight, shallow ulnar shaft of myrmecophagids (Fig. 4). The radius is also laterally compressed and deepens dramatically in its distal half. It is strongly arched, concave on its ulnar border and convex anterolaterally. The head (proximal articulation) is elliptical, shallowly concave, and has a proximally projecting lateral margin (character 412), whereas the head of myrmecophagids is nearly round. In all these details the ulna and radius closely resemble those of palaeanodonts.

All the elements of the manus, save for the intermediate phalanx of digit V, are preserved in the holotype, although most were largely or completely obscured in the skeletal plaque. Those of the left manus (including all eight carpal bones and all five metacarpals) have been digitally isolated from their articulated state in the plaque, revealing their anatomy in three dimensions31(Fig. 5). While there are also detailed resemblances to palaeanodonts in carpal anatomy, here we will focus on the metacarpals and phalanges, whose morphology is distinctive and strikingly similar to that of palaeanodonts, but differs markedly from that of vermilinguans. While the robust proportions of the elements of digit III in Eurotamandua and Vermilingua are indeed similar, all the metacarpals of Eurotamandua are extremely robust and short (the length barely twice the width; characters 445, 446) and longitudinally arched (character 438), so that the palmar surface is distinctly concave and the distal articulation faces distopalmarly. Although the third metacarpal (Mc III) is most prominent, Mcs II and IV are also sizable and similar in proportions to Mc III, whereas Mcs I and V are substantially reduced. The extensor tubercle on the dorsomedial surface of Mc III of Eurotamandua (Fig. 6) is conspicuous and situated at midshaft (character 439)—almost identical in expression to that of Metacheiromys but slightly more distal. Mc II bears a smaller extensor tubercle dorsolaterally (character 439), as in palaeanodonts23,24. Notwithstanding their unusual shapes, the articular facets at the proximal ends of Mcs II, III, and IV for the carpal bones and for adjacent metacarpals are also closely similar to those in Metacheiromys (Figs. 5 and 6). Mc IV has a clearly beveled distal articulation in dorsal perspective. Perhaps most extraordinary of all are the distal articular surfaces of Mcs II–IV, best preserved on Mc III, consisting of a concavoconvex cylinder dorsally with a wide extensor fossa above it and a pair of flexor fossae ventrally (palmarly) divided by a vestigial remnant of the keel (characters 441, 442). The fossae accommodate a prominent extensor process and a pair of flexor processes separated by a shallow notch (for the vestigial keel) on the proximal phalanges, which served to restrict flexion and extension at the metacarpophalangeal joints.

Like the metacarpals, the phalanges are very robust (Fig. 7; character 447), the proximal ones as wide as long (digits II and IV), or noticeably wider than long (digit III). In addition to the modifications at the proximal end, the distal articulations of the proximal phalanges lack the typical dorsoventrally rounded condyles present in most mammals3; instead the distal condyles are somewhat truncated and limited to an arc of less than 90° that faces only distally and plantarly. A deep notch between the condyles (digit III) accommodates the extensor process of the intermediate phalanx, prohibiting hyperextension at this joint. The intermediate phalanges are also robust, those of digits III and IV of similar proportions (roughly equal in width and length), whereas that of digit II is slightly longer than wide. Prominent flexor processes on the intermediate phalanges promoted powerful but restricted flexion at the interphalangeal joint. Unlike the proximal phalanges, the intermediates retain fully rounded (180°) distal condyles, which allowed a fairly wide range of flexion and extension of the terminal phalanges. The claw-bearing terminal (ungual) phalanges of the central three digits are enlarged, while those of the first and fifth digits are much reduced. The unguals of the central digits are long and shallow (character 448), the dorsopalmar depth of ungual III only one-third its length. Ungual III is almost as long as the rest of the digit (Mc III + the first two phalanges combined), and with its keratin sheath would have been much longer. Ungual II is about three-fourths the length of ungual III, and ungual IV is just over half the length of ungual III. In coronal section these unguals are more or less triangular (with a ventral base and dorsal apex), the dorsal border relatively sharp and the ventral border wider and almost flat. In lateral perspective the dorsal margin is somewhat curved and the ventral edge very weakly concave. The central unguals are moderately wide proximally (also about one-third the length), tapering distally, with prominent extensor and flexor processes, the latter extending half the length of the ungual. In virtually every detail, the metacarpals and phalanges closely correspond to those of Eocene palaeanodonts (Figs. 5, 6 and 7). On the other hand, while Tamandua superficially resembles Eurotamandua in having an enlarged digit III with robust elements, the anatomy and proportions of its Mc III and phalanges differ substantially. Mc III in Tamandua is relatively more slender, with greatly expanded articular ends, and a salient dorsoventral keel distally. The proximal phalanx, although similar in being wider than long, differs in shape of its articular ends; and the intermediate phalanx is relatively longer and more slender than in Eurotamandua. Both of these phalanges further differ from those of Eurotamandua in lacking a median extensor process dorsally. The ungual phalanx of Tamandua differs from that of Eurotamandua in being noticeably more curved, proximally deeper, and hooded, and it bears a shallow dorsal fissure for half its length.

The hind limb elements are generally conservative and moderately robust, but not as specialized as the forelimb. Only two of the four hind limb features Storch considered to be shared with myrmecophagids3 have limited distribution; they concern the femur and the astragalus. Storch observed that the femoral head of vermilinguans and Eurotamandua is nearly aligned with the femoral shaft (rather than angled medially). Like vermilinguans, Eurotamandua appears to have a very high-angle femoral neck, with the femoral head facing almost superiorly. Similar femoral morphology is also seen in the pangolins Phataginus and to a lesser extent Patriomanis, whereas palaeanodonts tend to have a more medially divergent femoral head. The orientation of the femoral head in Eurotamandua probably reflects posture or locomotor behavior, but whether it has phylogenetic significance is unknown.

The astragalus is largely obscured in the plaque. Based on what was then visible, Storch described the astragalus of Eurotamandua as sharing a broad trochlea, short neck, and slightly concave navicular facet with vermilinguans3. The micro-CT scan confirms the first two features but indicates that the navicular facet was misinterpreted. It is not concave (a derived state characteristic of vermilinguans and, independently, extant pholidotans), but in fact is ellipsoid and convex (character 59; Supplementary Fig. S5 online). A relatively broad trochlea, short neck, and rounded head are traits shared with palaeanodonts23,24,37 and basal pholidotans (Euromanis, Patriomanis)13,40; however, they are relatively primitive features. By contrast, the astragalus of Tamandua has a more asymmetrical trochlea and a nearly circular, shallowly concave head.

Phylogenetic analyses

Gaudin et al.16 performed a comprehensive phylogenetic analysis of living and fossil pangolins and their potential relatives, including palaeanodonts and Eurotamandua, using 395 morphological characters. Their analysis firmly placed Eurotamandua near the base of Pholidota, and Palaeanodonta as the sister taxon of Pholidota, the two constituting the Pholidotamorpha. However, it did not test the relationship to Xenarthra. Here we present results of new phylogenetic analyses of Eurotamandua using the modified matrix of Gaudin et al.16. In order to test the possibility of xenarthran affinities, our analyses include the extant xenarthrans Tamandua (Myrmecophagidae) and Dasypus (Dasypodidae), and a stem eutherian outgroup (see Methods). We have also added 53 new characters from the forelimb skeleton and modified 44 of the original characters, based on the new CT data and newly added taxa. Forelimb characters dominate the new character set because this is the most intensively studied part of the skeleton to date.

Our unconstrained PAUP analysis resulted in a single most parsimonious tree (2023 steps, CI = 0.519; RI = 0.5928; HI = 0.481) that placed Tamandua well within Pholidota, as sister taxon of extant manids, and Dasypus as sister taxon of a clade consisting of Eurotamandua and palaeanodonts. Thus neither Xenarthra nor Pholidota were monophyletic in this analysis, conflicting with the current consensus of morphological and molecular evidence25,41,42. It also failed to recover several well-supported pangolin clades16. This result appears to be strongly influenced by myrmecophagy and convergent associated anatomy and behavior in Tamandua and pholidotes, and fossorial behavior in Dasypus, Eurotamandua, and palaeanodonts. For these reasons we consider this result unlikely to reflect true relationships.

Consequently, we performed a second analysis constraining the monophyly of Xenarthra a priori. Except for this constraint, the second analysis was conducted in an identical manner to the first. It, too, produced a single most parsimonious tree (2057 steps, CI = 0.511; RI = 0.5785; HI = 0.4892; see Fig. 8), in which Dasypus and Tamandua are united in a clade (Xenarthra) separate from all other taxa in the analysis, most of which compose Pholidotamorpha. It also corroborates the position of Eurotamandua as sister taxon of Palaeanodonta, as previously suggested13,14. Although slightly longer than the tree from the unconstrained analysis, this tree is preferred because it is consistent with results from Gaudin et al.16 as well as the current understanding of mammalian phylogeny. A list of apomorphies for the preferred (constrained) tree is provided in Supplemental File S3 online.

Characters are polarized via comparison to composite stem eutherian outgroup comprised of the closely related Late Cretaceous taxa Ukhaatherium and Asioryctes. Discussion and conclusions

Computed tomography has greatly enhanced our knowledge of Eurotamandua, by enabling us to see the skeleton in three dimensions and providing access to bones that were previously hidden in the matrix or in the resin plaque. In addition, it affords the ability to digitally isolate individual skeletal elements in order to view all surfaces, including articulations. As a result, we have a clearer, more confident understanding of the skeletal anatomy.

Anatomy

As discussed above, Eurotamandua lacks two of the most distinctive diagnostic characters of Xenarthra—xenarthrous intervertebral articulations, and ossifications between pseudosacral vertebrae and the ischium. These two features surely influenced the interpretation of Eurotamandua as a xenarthran. Although it shares a tubular, edentulous snout with vermilinguans, this condition evolved convergently in Pholidota, which are also myrmecophagous. No known palaeanodont was completely toothless, but reduction and loss of teeth was a trend in the clade and several taxa approach this condition, retaining only a canine, one or two rudimentary peg-like cheek teeth, and variably a tiny incisor23,38. Palaeanodonts are generally accepted as stem pholidotans16,25,43,44. Several other resemblances to vermilinguans have been shown to be spurious, or to occur in non-xenarthrans as well. While the central digit of the manus is predominant and the third metacarpal is robust and specialized in both Eurotamandua and vermilinguans, detailed comparison reveals many differences from the forelimb anatomy of vermilinguans. At the same time, the extent of precise similarity in almost every forelimb element of Eurotamandua and Eocene palaeanodonts such as Metacheiromys, Palaeanodon, and Tubulodon, is striking and unparalleled in any other mammal, living or fossil. Proportions and unique articular surfaces of the metacarpals and phalanges are almost identical. The similarity extends to the hind limb as well. Although the latter is considerably less specialized in vermilinguans, it is robust and superficially resembles that of Eurotamandua in some aspects. However, as with the forelimb, careful comparisons using the new CT data reveal that hind limb elements differ in detail, and in some respects contrast markedly in Eurotamandua and vermilinguans.

Implications for behavior, habits

The tubular, edentulous snout of Eurotamandua is strong evidence that it was an obligate myrmecophage. Modifications of the forelimb of Eurotamandua, especially in the manus, indicate highly specialized digging ability26. They include very prominent deltopectoral and supinator crests and a strongly developed medial epicondyle on the humerus, an enlarged and medially-inflected olecranon process on the ulna, and modifications to stabilize the elbow and radioulnar joints and restrict motion more to flexion and extension with limited supination. The radius and ulnar shafts distal to the elbow joint are short and robust, and the radius expands distally. The metacarpals and phalanges display a series of unusual anatomical specializations for powerful digging. The size and shape of the ungual phalanges closely conform with those of extant fossorial and semi-fossorial mammals45. Eurotamandua may have used its forelimbs to tear open termite or ant nests. Based on preserved gut contents in the holotype of E. joresi, which included insect chitin and wood particles, it has been speculated that this individual may have foraged in a tree-termite nest shortly before its death46.

Implications for phylogenetic position

The phylogenetic analysis provides strong support that Eurotamandua is the sister taxon of Palaeanodonta, and that both are basal members of Pholidotamorpha. Despite superficial resemblances to vermilinguans, similarities to palaeanodonts are far more numerous and compelling. Eurotamandua is neither a vermilinguan nor a xenarthran. Nor is there any evidence to support assigning Eurotamandua to its own higher clade Afredentata, or to indicate that Afredentata, Xenarthra, and Palaeanodonta form a clade that originated in Africa in the early Cenozoic13. On the contrary, computed tomography of the holotype of Eurotamandua joresi provides convincing new evidence of a close relationship between Eurotamandua and palaeanodonts.

Implications for paleobiogeography

As Eurotamandua has been shown to be a pholidotamorphan, there is no evidence at present that Xenarthra ever reached the Old World. The fossil record currently supports the origin and early evolution of palaeanodonts in North America, probably in the Paleocene47. Palaeanodonts have been found in Eocene deposits at Ellesmere Island48, near the North Atlantic dispersal route, and in France49, suggesting that Eurotamandua likely evolved from or shared a common ancestor with North American palaeanodonts.

Methods

Micro-CT

—Scanning of the holotype was conducted at the AUDI laboratory in Neckarsulm, Germany, with 180 kV and 20 µA. The plaque containing the fossil, which measures roughly a meter long, was fixed with a specially designed mount19. A vertical sensor shift enabled resolutions of 113.84 μm and 156.68 μm, respectively. Two datasets of 2,800 projections each were segmented with VGStudio MAX 3.1 and 3.4.5 (Volume Graphics, Heidelberg, Germany). Digital extraction of the skeleton from the matrix and resin, and segmentation of isolated skeletal elements, were particularly complicated and time-consuming because fiberglass had been used to reinforce the resin plaque. Embedding Messel fossils in fiberglass-reinforced resin is now forbidden.

Comparative taxa

Comparisons were made primarily to extant and extinct specialized diggers, especially myrmecophagous forms. These include extant Xenarthra (Myrmecophagidae: Tamandua and Myrmecophaga; Dasypodidae: Dasypus and Euphractus), Pholidota (extant Manidae: Manis, Phataginus, and Smutsia; extinct pholidotans Eomanis, Euromanis, and Patriomanis), and the Eocene palaeanodonts Palaeanodon, Metacheiromys, and Tubulodon (= Alocodontulum38). For species and specimen numbers compared, see Supplementary File S2 online.

Phylogenetic analysis

The data matrix analyzed here consisted of 20 taxa and 448 discrete morphological skeletal characters. The former included the 17 taxa examined in the phylogenetic analysis of Gaudin et al.16, i.e., Eurotamandua joresi, seven of the nine extant pangolin species50,51, the extinct pangolins Patriomanis americana, Cryptomanis gobiensis, Necromanis spp., Eomanis waldi, Euromanis krebsi, the metacheiromyid palaeanodonts Palaeanodon ignavus and Metacheiromys spp., and two laurasiatherian extant outgroup taxa, Erinaceus sp. and Nandinia binotata. In order to test the putative relationship between Eurotamandua and xenarthran anteaters, the extant anteater Tamandua tetradactyla was added to the matrix, along with a second xenarthran, the basal extant cingulate Dasypus novemcinctus. Because Xenarthra lies outside Laurasiatheria25,41,42,52, we added a stem eutherian OTU to polarize the characters in the matrix, composed of the two closely related Cretaceous asioryctitheriid genera Asioryctes and Ukhaatherium53,54. A combined OTU was employed in order to maximize the number of characters that could be coded, as both taxa are incompletely known. The 448 characters in the data matrix included the 395 discrete skeletal characters from the Gaudin et al. analysis16, drawn from the entire skeleton, along with 53 new skeletal forelimb characters, some highlighted in Results, above, and others described elsewhere31(see Supplementary File S3 online). Because new taxa were added to the analysis, 44 of the 395 characters from the Gaudin et al. matrix16 required modification to accommodate new character states (see Supplementary File S3).

The characters in the data matrix were scored based on the specimens and published descriptions utilized in the Gaudin et al. analysis16. Scorings based on specimens or data not available in that study, or added for the present study, together with their sources, along with any new characters scored for the taxa in the Gaudin et al. study, are provided elsewhere31 and in Morphobank Project 5821 online.

The data matrix was analyzed using the computer program PAUP (Version 4.0a build 169)55. Analyses of the entire matrix were conducted using PAUP’s branch and bound algorithm in order to ensure that a globally parsimonious solution would be obtained. Characters were optimized using PAUP’s DELTRAN option in all analyses (see ref. 56 for full justification—essentially this option is believed to be more conservative in that it only assigns synapomorphies to a clade if there is unambiguous evidence that the character evolved at the base of the clade), and all character state changes were weighted equally. In those instances in which intraspecific variation was noted for a given character in a given taxon (or there was more than one possible coding due to uncertainty), the taxon was coded for all relevant states and treated as polymorphic in the PAUP analyses. Of the 448 characters, 214 were multistate, and 104 of these were ordered along numerical, positional, or structural morphoclines16(see Supplementary File S3 online). Several characters proved to be parsimony-uninformative in the final analyses, but all values reported for consistency index exclude uninformative characters. As noted above, characters were polarized via comparison to a composite outgroup of stem eutherian taxa comprised of the genera Asioryctes and Ukhaatherium.

To assess the robusticity of the preferred (constrained) tree, a bootstrap analysis was conducted. The bootstrap analysis employed PAUP’s heuristic search algorithm, with 500 bootstrap replicates and starting trees obtained by random stepwise addition. Other PAUP settings were identical to those employed in the original analysis. Results of the bootstrap analysis are shown in Fig. 8.
