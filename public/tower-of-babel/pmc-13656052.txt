Preserving genome integrity to combat cardiac aging: beyond LINE-1 and cGAS-STING
Authors: Ahmed Abdel-Latif, Nageswara R. Madamanchi
Published: 2026

Population aging has transformed cardiovascular disease from an isolated pathological process into one of the major manifestations of biological aging (1-3). Even in the absence of overt cardiovascular risk factors, advancing age is accompanied by progressive myocardial remodeling characterized by cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, interstitial fibrosis, impaired diastolic relaxation, chronic low-grade inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and diminished regenerative capacity. These changes substantially increase susceptibility to heart failure, atrial fibrillation, ischemic heart disease, and other age-associated cardiovascular disorders. Although current therapies effectively treat many clinical manifestations of cardiovascular disease, few directly target the fundamental mechanisms that drive cardiac aging itself.

Over the past decade, several interconnected hallmarks of cardiac aging have emerged, including mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, genomic instability, epigenetic alterations, cellular senescence, and chronic sterile inflammation (“inflammaging”) (4-6). Among these, activation of the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase-stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING) pathway has gained considerable attention as a central mediator linking genomic damage to innate immune activation (7). However, the upstream events responsible for persistent activation of this pathway during physiological aging remain incompletely understood.

An emerging concept in aging biology is that transposable elements, particularly long interspersed nuclear element-1 (LINE-1), are not merely passive genomic remnants but active participants in age-related tissue dysfunction. LINE-1 retrotransposons comprise approximately 17% of the human genome and are normally maintained in a transcriptionally repressed state through multiple layers of epigenetic regulation, heterochromatin organization, and post-transcriptional RNA surveillance (8-10). During aging, however, progressive deterioration of these regulatory mechanisms permits LINE-1 derepression, resulting in reverse transcription of LINE-1 RNA, accumulation of cytoplasmic DNA intermediates, activation of innate immune signaling, and amplification of cellular senescence.

In this issue, Yang et al. provide compelling evidence that age-dependent LINE-1 activation is not merely a biomarker of cardiac aging but a mechanistic contributor to myocardial dysfunction through activation of the cGAS-STING pathway (11). Using complementary genetic and pharmacological approaches, the authors demonstrate that suppression of LINE-1 reverse transcription or inhibition of STING attenuates inflammation, cellular senescence, myocardial remodeling, and age-associated cardiac dysfunction in mice (11). Their study positions LINE-1 as a potentially actionable therapeutic target and broadens our understanding of how age-associated genome instability interfaces with innate immunity.

Beyond its immediate findings, this work raises broader questions regarding the mechanisms responsible for LINE-1 derepression during aging, the interplay between oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and transposable element activation, and the translational feasibility of targeting this pathway in age-related cardiovascular disease.

LINE-1 activation links genomic instability to cardiac inflammaging

Yang and colleagues address a fundamental question—what molecular events convert age-related genomic instability into chronic sterile inflammation and progressive myocardial dysfunction (11).

The authors first demonstrate that LINE-1 expression progressively increases in the aging murine heart, with elevated expression of both ORF1p and ORF2p in cardiomyocytes. To determine whether LINE-1 activation merely accompanies aging or actively contributes to cardiac dysfunction, they generated cardiomyocyte-specific deletion of Mov10, an RNA helicase that suppresses LINE-1 retrotransposition by promoting degradation of LINE-1 RNA and disrupting retrotransposon ribonucleoprotein assembly (12,13). Loss of MOV10 resulted in robust LINE-1 derepression, accompanied by impaired systolic and diastolic function, cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, interstitial fibrosis, and premature molecular features of cardiac aging, including activation of senescence-associated pathways and increased expression of p16, p21, and multiple components of the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP).

A major strength of the study is the integration of complementary genetic and pharmacological approaches to establish mechanistic relationships. LINE-1 activation was associated with marked stimulation of the cGAS-STING signaling cascade, leading to activation of TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1), interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3), and nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), increased type I interferon signaling, inflammatory cytokine production, and macrophage infiltration (6,7,11). Importantly, genetic suppression of LINE-1 or pharmacological inhibition using the reverse transcriptase inhibitor lamivudine (3TC) attenuated these inflammatory and senescence phenotypes. Similarly, inhibition of STING with H-151 reduced downstream inflammatory signaling and substantially improved cardiac function in aged mice.

These findings support a mechanistic model in which age-dependent LINE-1 derepression generates immunostimulatory nucleic acid intermediates that activate innate immune sensing pathways, thereby establishing a self-perpetuating cycle of inflammation and cellular senescence (10). Rather than representing an isolated molecular abnormality, LINE-1 activation emerges as a potential nexus connecting genomic instability, innate immunity, and myocardial remodeling.

Equally important is the translational significance of these observations. The demonstration that pharmacological inhibition of either LINE-1 reverse transcription or STING signaling ameliorates age-associated cardiac dysfunction suggests that this pathway may be therapeutically modifiable. Although further validation in human tissues and clinical studies will be required, the possibility of repurposing clinically available reverse transcriptase inhibitors provides an attractive starting point for developing interventions that target fundamental mechanisms of cardiovascular aging rather than its downstream consequences.

What initiates LINE-1 derepression during cardiac aging?

While Yang et al. convincingly demonstrate that LINE-1 activation drives cGAS-STING-dependent inflammation and accelerates cardiac aging (11), an equally important question remains unanswered: what initiates LINE-1 derepression in the aging heart? Addressing this question is essential because the earliest molecular events that permit LINE-1 activation may represent more attractive therapeutic targets than the downstream inflammatory pathways they trigger.

In healthy cells, LINE-1 elements are maintained in a transcriptionally silent state through multiple complementary mechanisms, including epigenetic repression, DNA methylation, repressive histone modifications, and post-transcriptional RNA surveillance. Aging is accompanied by progressive erosion of these genome-protective mechanisms, creating a permissive environment for transposable element activation. Increasing evidence from aging biology suggests that loss of heterochromatin integrity is a central event in this process. Among the key regulators is the longevity-associated deacetylase sirtuin 6 (SIRT6), which suppresses LINE-1 transcription by recruiting transcriptional repressors to LINE-1 loci. During aging, persistent DNA damage redistributes SIRT6 from repetitive genomic regions to sites requiring DNA repair, thereby relieving repression of LINE-1 transcription. Concurrent age-associated DNA hypomethylation and loss of repressive histone marks, particularly trimethylated histone H3 lysine 9 (H3K9me3), further destabilize heterochromatin, creating a permissive chromatin state that promotes transcriptional activation of previously silenced retrotransposons (14).

Beyond epigenetic regulation, post-transcriptional surveillance also deteriorates with age. MOV10, an RNA helicase that recognizes and promotes degradation of LINE-1 transcripts, constitutes a second line of defense against retrotransposition. The elegant cardiomyocyte-specific Mov10 knockout model developed by Yang et al. demonstrates the importance of this surveillance pathway (11). Whether physiological aging similarly impairs MOV10 abundance or activity in human cardiomyocytes remains an important question deserving further investigation.

These observations suggest that LINE-1 activation is unlikely to represent an isolated molecular event. Rather, it is probably one manifestation of a broader age-associated decline in genome maintenance. Progressive heterochromatin erosion, impaired DNA repair, defective RNA surveillance, and persistent genomic stress may converge to create a permissive environment for retrotransposon activation. In this context, LINE-1 serves not only as a source of immunostimulatory nucleic acids but also as an indicator that fundamental mechanisms preserving genomic stability have begun to fail.

Genome maintenance rather than immune suppression: redefining therapeutic strategies

The therapeutic implications of the study by Yang et al. extend well beyond the demonstration that pharmacological inhibition of LINE-1 or STING attenuates cardiac aging in mice (11). More broadly, the study invites reconsideration of where therapeutic intervention should be directed within the LINE-1-cGAS-STING signaling axis. Should treatment focus on suppressing downstream innate immune signaling, or should it aim to preserve the upstream mechanisms that maintain genomic integrity?

The efficacy of STING inhibition observed in this study provides compelling proof-of-concept that the cGAS-STING pathway is a major mediator of age-associated cardiac inflammation (6,7,15). However, STING is also a central component of host defense against viral and bacterial pathogens and contributes to antitumor immune surveillance. Consequently, prolonged systemic inhibition of innate immune signaling may carry unintended consequences, particularly in older individuals whose immune competence is already compromised. These considerations do not diminish the therapeutic importance of STING but suggest that chronic immune suppression may be better viewed as one component of a broader therapeutic strategy rather than the ultimate solution for cardiovascular aging.

An alternative paradigm is to intervene further upstream by preserving genomic stability and preventing the generation of immunostimulatory nucleic acids. Aging is characterized by the progressive accumulation of oxidative DNA damage, telomere dysfunction, mitochondrial DNA instability, and epigenetic drift (2,16,17). Together, these alterations disrupt heterochromatin organization, impair DNA repair pathways, and promote derepression of transposable elements such as LINE-1. Simultaneously, damaged mitochondria release mitochondrial DNA into the cytosol, while genomic instability may generate micronuclei and fragmented nuclear DNA. All these DNA species have the potential to activate cGAS, suggesting that LINE-1-derived DNA represents one component of a broader network of cytosolic DNA signals that converge on innate immune activation.

This perspective raises an important conceptual question: is chronic activation of cGAS-STING the primary pathogenic event in cardiac aging, or is it the downstream consequence of a progressive failure in genome maintenance? If the latter is correct, therapeutic strategies directed at reducing oxidative DNA damage, preserving heterochromatin integrity, enhancing DNA repair, maintaining RNA surveillance, or facilitating clearance of cytosolic DNA may ultimately prove more effective and physiologically compatible than sustained inhibition of innate immune signaling (2,10,18).

Among these upstream mechanisms, preservation of mitochondrial integrity deserves particular attention. Mitochondria are both a major source and target of reactive oxygen species during aging. Persistent mitochondrial oxidative stress promotes nuclear and mitochondrial DNA damage, activates DNA damage response pathways, impairs mitochondrial quality control, and contributes to chronic inflammation and age-associated cardiac dysfunction (17,19,20). Although whether mitochondrial dysfunction directly facilitates LINE-1 derepression remains incompletely understood, accumulating evidence suggests that oxidative stress, genomic instability, and transposable element activation participate in a self-reinforcing cycle that progressively amplifies inflammaging (2,10,14,18). Future studies should therefore define how mitochondrial dysfunction, epigenetic remodeling, LINE-1 activation, and innate immune signaling interact throughout the course of cardiac aging rather than considering these processes as isolated pathogenic pathways. An important unanswered question is whether preservation of mitochondrial homeostasis can indirectly suppress LINE-1 activation by limiting oxidative DNA damage, maintaining chromatin integrity, and ultimately preserving genome stability.

Viewed from this broader perspective, the study by Yang et al. may represent more than the identification of a novel therapeutic target (11). It shifts attention toward preservation of genome integrity as a fundamental determinant of cardiac aging (Figure 1). Therapeutic strategies that stabilize chromatin architecture, improve DNA repair capacity, preserve mitochondrial homeostasis, or prevent inappropriate activation of endogenous retrotransposons may ultimately interrupt the initiating events that drive chronic sterile inflammation.

Future perspectives

The study by Yang et al. (11) opens several important avenues for future investigation. First, although the authors convincingly demonstrate LINE-1 activation in murine hearts, validation in human myocardium is essential. Whether LINE-1 expression correlates with biological age, frailty, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), atrial fibrillation, ischemic cardiomyopathy, or other forms of cardiovascular disease remains unknown. Integration of comprehensive human cardiac cell atlases with single-cell transcriptomic, epigenomic, and spatial transcriptomic technologies will be essential for identifying the specific cardiac cell populations in which retrotransposon activation occurs and for determining whether LINE-1 activation represents a conserved feature of human cardiovascular aging and HFpEF (21,22). Application of these high-resolution technologies to human myocardial specimens will be essential for determining whether LINE-1 activation represents a universal feature of cardiovascular aging or is restricted to specific pathological phenotypes and cellular subpopulations. Such approaches could not only clarify the cellular basis of cardiac aging but also identify novel therapeutic targets while helping preserve the physiological functions of innate immunity that remain essential for host defense and tissue homeostasis.

Second, the molecular events responsible for LINE-1 derepression deserve greater attention (14,23). Aging is accompanied by progressive heterochromatin erosion, DNA hypomethylation, altered histone modifications, persistent DNA damage responses, declining RNA surveillance, and mitochondrial dysfunction. These interconnected processes are unlikely to function independently; rather, they probably converge to create a permissive chromatin environment that enables LINE-1 derepression and retrotransposon activation. Whether interventions that preserve mitochondrial homeostasis can suppress LINE-1 activation by limiting oxidative DNA damage and preserving chromatin and genome stability remains an important question for future investigation.

Third, future studies should define the relative contribution of multiple endogenous sources of cytosolic DNA during cardiac aging. In addition to LINE-1-derived complementary DNA (cDNA), mitochondrial DNA released during mitochondrial dysfunction, chromosomal fragments contained within micronuclei, and damaged nuclear DNA are all capable of activating cGAS (24).Understanding how these danger-associated nucleic acids cooperate to drive sterile inflammation may reveal opportunities to intervene before chronic activation of innate immunity becomes established. Recent evidence further suggests that the relationship between LINE-1 and innate immune signaling is bidirectional, with STING participating not only in sensing LINE-1-derived nucleic acids but also in regulating retrotransposon activity. Defining these reciprocal regulatory mechanisms may reveal additional opportunities to modulate chronic inflammation while preserving physiological immune function (25).

Finally, therapeutic development should extend beyond inhibition of downstream inflammatory signaling. Although reverse transcriptase inhibitors and STING antagonists provide compelling proof-of-concept, preservation of genome integrity may represent a more fundamental therapeutic objective. Strategies that maintain heterochromatin stability, enhance DNA repair, preserve mitochondrial homeostasis, strengthen RNA surveillance, facilitate clearance of cytosolic DNA, or prevent inappropriate retrotransposon activation may interrupt the earliest pathogenic events while preserving the essential physiological functions of innate immunity.

Conclusions

Yang et al. identify LINE-1 derepression as an important driver of cardiac aging and establish a mechanistic link between endogenous retrotransposon activation, cGAS-STING signaling, cellular senescence, and myocardial dysfunction (11). Their work expands the growing recognition that genomic instability and innate immunity are intimately intertwined during cardiovascular aging and identifies LINE-1 as a potentially actionable therapeutic target.

Equally important, however, the study shifts attention toward a more fundamental biological question: why does the aging heart lose its ability to maintain genomic integrity? LINE-1 activation is unlikely to represent an isolated molecular abnormality; rather, it may be one manifestation of a broader collapse in genome surveillance characterized by epigenetic drift, heterochromatin erosion, impaired RNA quality control, persistent DNA damage, mitochondrial dysfunction, and accumulation of immunostimulatory nucleic acids. Viewed in this context, chronic activation of cGAS-STING may represent the downstream consequence of progressive failure of genome maintenance rather than the primary initiating event.

This broader perspective suggests that future therapies for cardiac aging should not simply suppress inflammation but instead preserve the mechanisms that prevent inappropriate immune activation in the first place. Enhancing genome stability, maintaining mitochondrial integrity, improving DNA repair, strengthening endogenous RNA surveillance, and facilitating efficient clearance of cytosolic DNA may ultimately prove more effective and physiologically compatible than chronic inhibition of innate immune signaling alone.

The work by Yang et al. (11) therefore represents more than the identification of a novel signaling pathway. It provides a framework for investigating the genome instability-innate immunity axis as a unifying mechanism of cardiac aging and encourages a shift from treating the consequences of inflammaging toward preventing its origins.
