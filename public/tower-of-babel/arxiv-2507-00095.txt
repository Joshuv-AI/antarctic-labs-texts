Authentication of Continuous-Variable Quantum Messages
Mehmet Hüseyin Temel, Boris Škorić

Abstract
We introduce the first quantum authentication scheme for continuous-variable states. Our scheme is based on trap states, and is an adaptation of a discrete-variable scheme by Broadbent et al. Broadbent et al. 2013 , but with more freedom in choosing the number of traps. We provide a security proof, mostly following the approach of Broadbent and Wainewright Broadbent and Wainewright 2016 . As a necessary ingredient for the proof we derive the continuous-variable analogue of the Pauli Twirl.



1
Introduction

With the rapid advancement of quantum technologies and the increasing deployment of quantum communication systems, new protocols for the secure transmission of quantum information have been proposed. While Quantum Key Distribution (QKD)
Bennett and Brassard 2014
;
Alléaume et al. 2010
;
Diamanti et al. 2016
;
Cao et al. 2022
is the most widely known application—using quantum systems to establish shared classical keys for classical encryption—quantum cryptography provides a broader set of protocols designed to protect quantum data itself. These include quantum encryption
Ambainis et al. 2000a
;
Ambainis et al. 2000b
;
Boykin and Roychowdhury 2003
;
Brádler 2005
;
Jeong et al. 2015
, quantum secret sharing
Liu et al. 2023
;
Cleve et al. 1999
, and quantum message authentication
Barnum et al. 2002
;
Broadbent and Wainewright 2016
;
Aharonov et al. 2017
.

1.1
Quantum Authentication

Message authentication is a fundamental task in cryptography that enables a receiver to verify whether a message has been tampered with during transmission, and whether it originates from the claimed sender.
By enabling tamper detection and origin verification,
quantum authentication serves as a critical building block for advanced cryptographic protocols such as quantum one-time programs
Broadbent et al. 2013
and secure multiparty quantum computation
Ben-Or et al. 2006
;
Dupuis et al. 2012
;
Dulek et al. 2020
.

A quantum authentication scheme works with a classical symmetric key and
consists of two keyed procedures: encoding (or encryption) and decoding (or decryption).
The sender encodes the quantum message using the key.
The recipient gets a quantum state and decodes it using the same key;
attempts to forge or manipulate the quantum message are detected with high probability.

The first Quantum Authentication Scheme (QAS) was introduced by Barnum et al.
Barnum et al. 2002
,
where they also provided security definitions.
One of the key results of their work was that any QAS must encrypt the quantum message. Their construction relied on purity-testing codes derived from quantum error-correcting codes (QECCs). It was later shown that such purity-testing codes can also satisfy universal composability
Hayden et al. 2016
.

The original security definition has since been strengthened by more robust proposals
Oppenheim and Horodecki 2005
;
Hayden et al. 2016
;
Garg et al. 2017
.
It was shown that partial or even complete key reuse is possible, depending on the amount of key leakage
Oppenheim and Horodecki 2005
;
Portmann 2017
;
Dulek and Speelman 2018
.
A variety of QAS constructions have been proposed, based on polynomial codes
Ben-Or et al. 2006
;
Aharonov et al. 2017
, Clifford codes
Aharonov et al. 2017
, threshold codes
Dulek et al. 2025
, and trap codes
Broadbent et al. 2013
;
Broadbent and Wainewright 2016
.

1.2
Trap Code-Based Quantum Authentication

In this paper, we focus on trap code-based quantum authentication, first introduced in
Broadbent et al. 2013
and later refined with a more efficient security proof in
Broadbent and Wainewright 2016
.
The main idea behind trap codes is to insert dummy states—referred to as traps—into the quantum message (which has already been encoded using a QECC), and then apply a secret permutation and One-Time Pad encryption.
The traps are used to detect any tampering by an adversary.

1.3
Contribution

All of the quantum authentication schemes referred above are designed for discrete-variable (DV) quantum states.
In contrast, continuous-variable (CV) quantum message authentication remains relatively unexplored.
CV systems are particularly attractive for practical implementations due to their compatibility with existing optical communication infrastructure.

In this work, we introduce the first quantum authentication scheme for CV quantum states.
Our construction is an adaptation of the DV trap code-based QAS proposed by Broadbent, Gutoski, and Stebila
Broadbent et al. 2013
, and it follows the proof technique of Broadbent and Wainewright
Broadbent and Wainewright 2016
.
While our construction and security proof take the same global steps as
Broadbent and Wainewright 2016
,
the differences between DV and CV
lead to several nontrivial features, e.g.
the necessity to allow a small probability of error in the verification step;
fine tuning scheme parameters in order to obtain properly matched step-like functions;
a CV analogue of the Pauli twirl.

Our contributions are:

•

We propose the first quantum authentication scheme for CV states.
In contrast to existing DV schemes,
our construction allows for a variable number of trap states.

•

We provide a security proof for the proposed scheme, adapting and extending techniques from the DV setting to the CV setting.

•

We introduce the notion of a
CV Twirl
, an analogue to the Pauli Twirl.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Notation

We use standard notation from quantum information theory.
Quantum states are represented by density operators (positive semi-definite, trace-one operators) acting on Hilbert spaces, and we write them as
\rho,\sigma
, etc., with subscripts indicating associated registers.
The identity operator is denoted by
\mathbb{1}
, and the partial trace over a subsystem
A
is
{\rm Tr}\,_{A}
.
We write
\|A\|_{1}
for the 1-norm, and

\|\rho-\sigma\|_{\rm tr}=

\frac{1}{2}\|\rho-\sigma\|_{1}
is the trace distance between two quantum states.
The quadrature operators
\hat{x}
and
\hat{p}
correspond to the position and momentum observables, respectively.
A coherent state
|z\rangle
is defined as the eigenstate of the annihilation operator

\hat{a}=\frac{\hat{x}+i\hat{p}}{\sqrt{2}}

with complex eigenvalue
z\in\mathbb{C}
.

We will use the notation
|X\rangle
for a single-mode squeezed state that is narrow in the direction of the x-quadrature, and centered on zero.
The Wigner function of such a squeezed state is Gaussian, with variance
e^{-r}
in the
x
-direction and

e^{r}
in the
p
-direction; the
r
is called the squeezing parameter.
Similarly we define the single-mode squeezed state
|P\rangle
.

The displacement operator
D(\beta)=e^{\beta\hat{a}^{{\dagger}}-\beta^{*}\hat{a}}
shifts the phase space of a mode.
The action on a coherent state is given by
D(\beta)|z\rangle=|z+\beta\rangle
.
Quantum One-Time Pad (QOTP) encryption for CV is achieved by applying a secret displacement
chosen from a wide complex Gaussian distribution, for each mode independently.

A CV Quantum Error Correcting Code (CV-QECC) is called an
[[n,1,d]]
code if it
encodes one mode to
n
modes and
is capable of correcting arbitrarily large displacements in up to
t=\lfloor(d-1)/2\rfloor
out of
n
modes.

2.2
Security definitions and useful lemmas

Lemma 2.1
.

(See e.g.
Walls and Milburn 1994
)
The displacement operation
D
satisfies the property

D(\beta)D(\gamma)=e^{i\,{\rm Im}\,(\beta\bar{\gamma})}D(\beta+\gamma).

(1)

We use the definition of a quantum authentication scheme given by Broadbent et al.
Broadbent and Wainewright 2016
,
but with a small modification: we allow for a small probability that a decoding error occurs.

Definition 2.2

(Quantum message authentication scheme)
.

A quantum authentication scheme (QAS) is a polynomial-time set of encryption and decryption channels
\{\mathcal{E}_{k}^{M\rightarrow C},\mathcal{D}_{k}^{C\rightarrow MF})\mid k\in\mathcal{K}\}
where
\mathcal{K}
is the set of possible keys,
M
is the input system,
C
is the encrypted system, and
F
is a flag system indicating either acceptance
|acc\rangle
or rejection
|rej\rangle
such that

\forall_{\rho_{M}}\quad\Big\|(\mathcal{D}_{k}\circ\mathcal{E}_{k})(\rho_{M})-\rho_{M}\otimes|{\rm acc}\rangle\langle{\rm acc}|_{F}\Big\|_{\rm tr}\leq\varepsilon_{\rm dec},

(2)

where
\varepsilon_{\rm dec}
is a small decoding error probability.

We allow the message register
M
to be entangled with a reference system
R
that belongs to the adversary.
The input to the scheme is expressed as a joint quantum state
\rho_{MR}
.

The adversary applies a joint unitary
U_{CR}
on the encoded message and the reference system.
For a fixed key
k
, the corresponding real-world quantum channel is defined as

\mathcal{C}_{k}^{MR\to MRF}:\quad\rho_{MR}\mapsto(\mathcal{D}_{k}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{R})\left(U_{CR}\;(\mathcal{E}_{k}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{R})(\rho_{MR})\;U_{CR}^{\dagger}\right).

(3)

The security definition relies on comparing this real-world channel with an idealized simulator which has access only to the ideal functionality. The ideal functionality either accepts the message by outputting message register M, or rejects it by outputting a fixed dummy state
\Omega_{M}
. The simulator may also modify the reference system
R
. The idealized process can be expressed as ideal channel
\mathcal{F}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{MR\to MRF}:\quad

\displaystyle\rho_{MR}\mapsto

\displaystyle(\mathbb{1}_{M}\otimes\mathcal{U}_{R}^{{\rm acc}})(\rho_{MR})\otimes|{\rm acc}\rangle\langle{\rm acc}|

(4)

\displaystyle+\Omega_{M}\otimes{\rm tr}\,_{M}\big[(\mathbb{1}_{M}\otimes\mathcal{U}_{R}^{{\rm rej}})(\rho_{MR})\big]\otimes|{\rm rej}\rangle\langle{\rm rej}|,

where for each attack
U_{CR}
there exists two CP maps
\mathcal{U}_{R}^{{\rm acc}}
and
\mathcal{U}_{R}^{{\rm rej}}
acting only on the reference system
R
, satisfying
\mathcal{U}_{R}^{{\rm acc}}+\mathcal{U}_{R}^{{\rm rej}}=\mathbb{1}_{R}
.

Definition 2.3

(Security of quantum message authentication
Broadbent and Wainewright 2016
)
.

Let
\{(\mathcal{E}_{k}^{M\to C},\mathcal{D}_{k}^{C\to MF})\mid k\in\mathcal{K}\}
be a quantum message authentication scheme.
The scheme is
\eta
-secure if for all attacks there exists a simulator
\mathcal{F}
such that

\forall\rho_{MR}\quad\quad\Big\|\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{K}}|}\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{K}}}\mathcal{C}_{k}(\rho_{MR})-\mathcal{F}(\rho_{MR})\Big\|_{\rm tr}\leq\eta,

(5)

where the simulator has access only to the ideal functionality of the scheme.

3
Trap Code CV Quantum Authentication Scheme

We construct our CV quantum authentication scheme by adapting the trap code-based DV construction of Broadbent et al.
Broadbent et al. 2013
to CV quantum states.
The encryption process begins with encoding the message modes using a quantum error-correcting code (QECC). Subsequently, two sets of trap modes are inserted.
The entire set of modes is permuted and then encrypted using a CV quantum one-time pad.
The decoding process reverses the encoding steps:
the received state is first decrypted and de-permuted, after which the integrity of the trap modes is verified.
If the trap modes are intact, the message modes are decoded using the QECC, and ‘accept’ is flagged.
If the trap modes are not intact, a ‘reject’ is flagged, the message state is discarded (traced out), and a dummy message is output instead.

Encoding

The encoding process, denoted by
\mathcal{E}^{M\to C}_{k}
, takes as input the single-mode message state
\rho_{M}
.
A CV QECC with parameters
[[n,1,d]]
is applied to
\rho_{M}
, encoding it
to
{\rm Enc}(\rho_{M})
which consists of
n
modes.
The QECC is able to correct displacements in
\leq t
modes, where
d=2t+1
.

After encoding,
z
states squeezed in the
x
-quadrature and
z
states squeezed in the
p
-quadrature are appended to the encoded message, forming a system of
n+2z
modes.
For proof-technical reasons we set
2z>n
.
These squeezed states are denoted as
|X\rangle
and
|P\rangle
, respectively, and act as traps.
They are centered on zero and have squeezing parameter
r
.
The entire set of modes is then permuted according to a secret key
k_{1}
.
Finally, a QOTP is applied according to a secret key
k_{2}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n+2z}
drawn
from a Gaussian distribution with variance
\Delta^{2}\gg 1
.
We write
k=(k_{1},k_{2})
.
The output Hilbert space has
n+2z
modes.

The QAS encoding is expressed as:

\mathcal{E}^{M\to C}_{k}:\rho_{M}\mapsto\rho_{C}\quad\quad\rho_{C}=D_{k_{2}}\pi_{k_{1}}\Big(\text{Enc}(\rho_{M})\otimes|X\rangle\langle X|^{\otimes z}\otimes|P\rangle\langle P|^{\otimes z}\Big)\pi_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}D_{k_{2}}^{\dagger},

(6)

where
\pi_{k_{1}}
is the permutation and
D_{k_{2}}
is the QOTP displacement operator.

Decoding

The decoding
\mathcal{D}^{C\to MF}_{k}
begins by applying the inverse displacement
D_{k_{2}}^{\dagger}
and inverse permutation
\pi_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}
to the received cipherstate.
The last
2z
modes, corresponding to appended squeezed trap states, are then measured using homodyne detection.
The measurement outcomes are denoted as
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{z}
and
(p_{i})_{i=1}^{z}
.
We define the following condition for acceptance:

\forall_{i\in\{1,\dots,z\}}\quad|x_{i}|\leq\epsilon\;\wedge\;|p_{i}|\leq\epsilon.

(7)

In order to prevent the decoding error probability
\varepsilon_{\rm dec}
from becoming large,
the values of
r
and
\epsilon
are chosen such that
\epsilon\gg e^{-r/2}
.

In case of Accept, QECC-decoding Dec
:\rho_{C}\to\rho_{M}
is applied to the first
n
modes, and a flag
|\text{acc}\rangle\langle\text{acc}|
is appended.
If any trap state fails the condition, the message system
M
is traced out, and a fixed dummy state
\Omega_{M}
is output instead. In this case, the flag
|\text{rej}\rangle\langle\text{rej}|
is appended.

We define a POVM
V
that acts on the trap space and has outcomes
\{\text{acc},\text{rej}\}
.
The POVM elements are given by

V^{\text{acc}}_{\epsilon}=\mathbb{1}^{\otimes n}\otimes\Bigg[\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}\!\!{\rm d}x\,|x\rangle\langle x|\Bigg]^{\otimes z}\otimes\Bigg[\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}\!\!{\rm d}p\,|p\rangle\langle p|\Bigg]^{\otimes z},\quad\quad V^{\rm rej}_{\epsilon}=\mathbb{1}-V^{\rm acc}_{\epsilon},

(8)

where
|x\rangle
is an
x
-quadrature eigenstate and
|p\rangle
is a
p
-quadrature eigenstate.
The decoding process is expressed as follows,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{C\to MF}_{k}:\rho_{C}\mapsto\text{Dec}\,\left({\rm Tr}\,_{\rm trap}\sqrt{V^{{\rm acc}}_{\epsilon}}\pi_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}D_{k_{2}}^{{\dagger}}\rho_{C}D_{k_{2}}\pi_{k_{1}}\sqrt{V^{{\rm acc}\dagger}_{\epsilon}}\right)\otimes|\text{acc}\rangle\langle\text{acc}|

\displaystyle+\Omega_{M}{\rm Tr}\,_{M,\rm trap}\left(\sqrt{V^{{\rm rej}}_{\epsilon}}\pi_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}D_{k_{2}}^{{\dagger}}\rho_{C}D_{k_{2}}\pi_{k_{1}}\sqrt{V^{{\rm rej}\dagger}_{\epsilon}}\right)\otimes|\text{rej}\rangle\langle\text{rej}|.

(9)

We briefly show that our scheme satisfies Def.
2.2
.
If there is no noise, each trap state has the following probability of passing the verification:

\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}\!{\rm d}x\;(2\pi e^{-r})^{-1/2}\exp(-\frac{x^{2}}{2e^{-r}})

={\rm Erf}\frac{\epsilon}{e^{-r/2}\sqrt{2}}
. Then

1-\varepsilon_{\rm dec}=\Big({\rm Erf}\frac{\epsilon}{e^{-r/2}\sqrt{2}}\Big)^{2z}.

(10)

Given our parameter tuning
e^{-r/2}\ll\epsilon
, the above expression is close to 1.

4
Security of our Scheme

4.1
Approach

We prove the security of our scheme using a simulator-based approach similar to the one employed by Broadbent and Wainewright
Broadbent and Wainewright 2016
.
The main idea is to model a simulator that mimics the ideal functionality of the scheme and then compare it with the real-world execution.
This comparison establishes the security of our construction by showing that any adversary interacting with the real protocol cannot distinguish it from the ideal case except with negligible probability.

The security proof of
Broadbent and Wainewright 2016
relies on a simulator that replaces the entire ciphertext
C
with EPR pairs and permutes them.
The simulator retains one half of each EPR pair and then it runs the adversary on these EPR states and the reference system
R
.
After the attack, the simulator unpermutes the states and performs Bell measurements on the EPR pairs to determine whether they have been tampered with.
If the EPR pairs remain intact, the simulator outputs “accept”; otherwise, it outputs “reject,” as prescribed by the ideal functionality.

We adapt the proof method for our CV construction

•

Qubits are replaced by modes.

•

Each two-qubit EPR pair is replaced by the two-mode squeezed vacuum.

•

The DV “accept” and “reject” projectors become the POVM (
8
).

•

The Pauli Twirl is replaced by a CV Twirl.

4.2
CV Twirl

Lemma 4.1

(CV Displacement Twirl)
.

Let
D(\cdot)
be the single-mode displacement operator. For any
\rho
it holds that

\int_{\mathbb{C}}d^{2}\gamma\frac{1}{2\pi\Delta^{2}}e^{-\frac{|\gamma|^{2}}{2\Delta^{2}}}\,D^{\dagger}(\gamma)D(\beta)D(\gamma)\,\rho\,D^{\dagger}(\gamma)D^{\dagger}(\beta^{\prime})D(\gamma)=e^{-2\Delta^{2}|\beta-\beta^{\prime}|^{2}}D(\beta)\rho D^{\dagger}(\beta^{\prime}).

(11)

Proof:
From (
1
) we have
D(\beta)D(\gamma)=e^{\frac{\beta\bar{\gamma}-\bar{\beta}\gamma}{2}}D(\beta+\gamma)
.
Multiplying from the left with
D(-\gamma)
and applying (
1
) again yields

D^{{\dagger}}(\gamma)D(\beta)D(\gamma)=e^{\frac{\beta\bar{\gamma}-\bar{\beta}\gamma}{2}}D(-\gamma)D(\beta+\gamma)

=e^{\frac{\beta\bar{\gamma}-\bar{\beta}\gamma}{2}}e^{\frac{-\gamma(\bar{\beta}+\bar{\gamma})+\bar{\gamma}(\beta+\gamma)}{2}}D(\beta)

=e^{\beta\bar{\gamma}-\bar{\beta}\gamma}D(\beta)
.
By the same reasoning it holds that

D^{{\dagger}}(\gamma)D^{{\dagger}}(\beta^{\prime})D(\gamma)=e^{-\beta^{\prime}\bar{\gamma}+\bar{\beta}^{\prime}\gamma}D^{{\dagger}}(\beta^{\prime})
.
We get

D^{{\dagger}}(\gamma)D(\beta)D(\gamma)\;\;\rho\;\;D^{{\dagger}}(\gamma)D^{{\dagger}}(\beta^{\prime})D(\gamma)=e^{\gamma(\bar{\beta}^{\prime}-\bar{\beta})-\bar{\gamma}(\beta^{\prime}-\beta)}\;D(\beta)\rho D^{{\dagger}}(\beta^{\prime}).

(12)

We write
\gamma=x+iy
, which gives

\gamma(\bar{\beta}^{\prime}-\bar{\beta})-\bar{\gamma}(\beta^{\prime}-\beta)=-2ix{\rm Im}\,(\beta^{\prime}-\beta)+2iy{\rm Re}\,(\beta^{\prime}-\beta)

and
|\gamma|^{2}=x^{2}+y^{2}
.
The integral over the complex plane becomes two separated Gaussian integrals over
x
and
y
.
Performing the integrals yields (
11
).

\square

Remark.

In the DV case the Pauli twirl result is

{\mathbb{E}}_{Q}Q^{{\dagger}}PQ\rho Q^{{\dagger}}{P^{\prime}}^{{\dagger}}Q=\delta_{PP^{\prime}}P\rho P^{{\dagger}}
, where
P,P^{\prime},Q
are
n
-qubit Paulis.
Instead of the Kronecker delta, our result has a Gaussian factor.
Note that the Gaussian factor
e^{-2\Delta^{2}|\beta-\beta^{\prime}|^{2}}
for
\Delta\gg 1

essentially acts as a Dirac delta function which enforces
\beta^{\prime}=\beta
.

4.3
Real World Channel

We introduce shorthand notation

.
It holds that

|\langle x|D(\beta)|X\rangle|^{2}=(2\pi e^{-r})^{-1/2}\exp(-\frac{(x-\sqrt{2}{\rm Re}\,\beta)^{2}}{2e^{-r}})

and

|\langle p|D(\beta)|P\rangle|^{2}=(2\pi e^{-r})^{-1/2}\exp(-\frac{(p-\sqrt{2}{\rm Im}\,\beta)^{2}}{2e^{-r}})
.
We get

\displaystyle g_{1}(\beta)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}\!{\rm d}x\,|\langle x|D(\beta)|X\rangle|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}{\rm Erf}\frac{e^{r/2}(\epsilon+\sqrt{2}{\rm Re}\,\beta)}{\sqrt{2}}+\frac{1}{2}{\rm Erf}\frac{e^{r/2}(\epsilon-\sqrt{2}{\rm Re}\,\beta)}{\sqrt{2}}

(16)

\displaystyle g_{2}(\beta)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}\!{\rm d}p\,|\langle p|D(\beta)|P\rangle|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}{\rm Erf}\frac{e^{r/2}(\epsilon+\sqrt{2}{\rm Im}\,\beta)}{\sqrt{2}}+\frac{1}{2}{\rm Erf}\frac{e^{r/2}(\epsilon-\sqrt{2}{\rm Im}\,\beta)}{\sqrt{2}}

(17)

For
r\gg 1
and properly tuned
\epsilon
(
\epsilon>e^{-r/2}
),
this combination of error functions acts as a selection function that
equals (almost) 1 if
|{\rm displacement}|\leq\epsilon
and (almost) 0 otherwise.
The product of all the contributions from the trap states yields an overall selection function
G
,

G(\pi,\vec{\alpha})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\prod_{j=1}^{z}g_{1}\left([\pi^{-1}\vec{\alpha}]_{X_{j}}\right)\;g_{2}\left([\pi^{-1}\vec{\alpha}]_{P_{j}}\right).

(18)

Finally, we can write the real-world channel as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{MR\to MRF}(\rho_{MR})={\mathbb{E}}_{k_{1}}\int{\rm d}^{2}\vec{\alpha}|\chi(\vec{\alpha})|^{2}\Bigg\{G(\pi_{k_{1}},\vec{\alpha})

\displaystyle\text{Dec}\Bigg(({[D_{C}(\pi_{k_{1}}^{-1}\vec{\alpha})]}_{\rm msg}\otimes U_{R}^{\vec{\alpha}})\text{Enc}_{M}(\rho_{MR})({[D_{C}(\pi_{k_{1}}^{-1}\vec{\alpha})]}_{\rm msg}\otimes U_{R}^{\vec{\alpha}})^{\dagger}\Bigg)\otimes|\text{acc}\rangle\langle\text{acc}|

\displaystyle+[1-G(\pi_{k_{1}},\vec{\alpha})]\Omega_{M}{\rm Tr}\,_{M}{\rm Dec}\Bigg(({[D_{C}(\pi_{k_{1}}^{-1}\vec{\alpha})]}_{\rm msg}\otimes U_{R}^{\vec{\alpha}})\text{Enc}_{M}(\rho_{MR})({[D_{C}(\pi_{k_{1}}^{-1}\vec{\alpha})]}_{\rm msg}\otimes U_{R}^{\vec{\alpha}})^{\dagger}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\otimes|\text{rej}\rangle\langle\text{rej}|\Bigg\}

(19)

4.4
The ideal channel

We now specify the ideal channel (
4
) for our scheme, again closely following
Broadbent and Wainewright 2016
.
The register
C
contains one side of
n+2z
EPR pairs.
(The other side is denoted as
C^{\prime}
.)
The attack is applied to the
k_{1}
-permuted modes;
then the modes are unpermuted and finally it is verified if the EPR pairs are
unmodified.
Specifically, the simulator checks if more than
t
modes out of the first
n
have been noticeably displaced,
and if any of the trap modes have been displaced by more than
\delta
.

Note that in the CV setting a ‘standard’ EPR pair is given by the two-mode squeezed vacuum

|\rm EPR\rangle=\frac{1}{\pi\sinh s}\int\!{\rm d}^{2}\alpha\;e^{-|\alpha|^{2}(\frac{1}{\tanh s}-1)}|\alpha,\alpha^{*}\rangle

(20)

where
|\alpha,\alpha^{*}\rangle
stands for the tensor product
|\alpha\rangle\otimes|\alpha^{*}\rangle
of two coherent states.
(see e.g.
Jeong et al. 2000
).
The ‘quality’ parameter
s
determines the amount of entanglement between the two modes.
At
s\to\infty
there is perfect correlation between their
x
-quadratures and
perfect anticorrelation between their
p
-quadratures.
We will work in the limit
s\to\infty
.
The above state
|\rm EPR\rangle
can be represented as a 50/50 beamsplitter mixture of two individual squeezed vacuums,
one squeezed in the
x
-direction and one in the
p
-direction.
At
s\to\infty
these become the
|x=0\rangle
and
|p=0\rangle
eigenstate respectively.
A displaced EPR state
|{\rm EPR}(\beta)\rangle
, with
\beta\in{\mathbb{C}}
, is created by mixing

|x={\rm Re}\,\beta\rangle
with
|p={\rm Im}\,\beta\rangle
.
Since
\{|x\rangle\}_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}}
and
\{|p\rangle\}_{p\in{\mathbb{R}}}
are single-mode orthogonal bases,
the states
\{|{\rm EPR}(\beta)\rangle\}_{\beta\in{\mathbb{C}}}
form an orthogonal basis of the two-mode Hilbert space.
This is the equivalent of the four Bell states in DV.
As in the DV case, we can map one ‘Bell’ basis state into another by applying a QOTP encryption
(displacement) to one side of the EPR pair.
Thus it holds that

2\int_{\mathbb{C}}\!{\rm d}^{2}\beta\,D_{C}(\beta)|{\rm EPR}\rangle\langle{\rm EPR}|_{CC^{\prime}}D_{C}(\beta)^{{\dagger}}=\mathbb{1}_{C}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{C^{\prime}},

(21)

where the subscripts
C,C^{\prime}
label the two modes.
Next we look at the verification step.
For displacement
u\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}

we define a ‘Hamming weight’
w_{\delta}(\vec{u})=\#\{j|\;\;|u_{j}|>\delta\}
which counts how many of the
n
modes
have a noticeable displacement.
The set of displacements that get accepted by the simulator is given by

{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathcal{F}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\{(\vec{u},\vec{\gamma},\vec{\psi})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n+z+z}|\;\;w_{\delta}(\vec{u})\leq t\;\wedge\;\forall_{i}|{\rm Re}\,\gamma_{i}|\leq\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{2}}\;\wedge\;\forall_{i}|{\rm Im}\,\psi_{i}|\leq\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{2}}\}.

(22)

The simulator’s POVM for the verification is written as

(V^{\rm acc}_{\mathcal{F}},V^{\rm rej}_{\mathcal{F}})
, with
V^{\rm rej}_{\mathcal{F}}=\mathbb{1}-V^{\rm acc}_{\mathcal{F}}
.
We have

V^{\rm acc}_{\mathcal{F}}=2^{n+2z}\int_{{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathcal{F}}}\!\!\!\!\!{\rm d}^{2}\vec{\beta}\;D_{C}(\vec{\beta})|{\rm EPR}\rangle\langle{\rm EPR}|_{CC^{\prime}}^{\otimes(n+2z)}D_{C}^{{\dagger}}(\vec{\beta}).

(23)

The mapping that represents the ideal channel is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{MR\to MRF}:\rho_{MR}\mapsto{\rm Tr}\,_{CC^{\prime}}{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi\in{\mathcal{S}}_{n+2z}}\Big\{

(24)

\displaystyle\Big(\sqrt{V^{\rm acc}_{{\mathcal{F}}}}\pi_{C}^{\dagger}U_{CR}\pi_{C}(\rho_{MR}\otimes|{\rm EPR}\rangle\langle{\rm EPR}|^{\otimes(n+2z)}_{CC^{\prime}})\pi_{C}^{\dagger}U_{CR}^{\dagger}\pi_{C}\sqrt{{V^{{\rm acc}\dagger}_{{\mathcal{F}}}}}\Big)\otimes|\text{acc}\rangle\langle\text{acc}|

\displaystyle+\Omega_{M}{\rm Tr}\,_{M}\Big(\sqrt{V^{\rm rej}_{{\mathcal{F}}}}\pi_{C}^{{\dagger}}U_{CR}\pi_{C}(\rho_{MR}\otimes|{\rm EPR}\rangle\langle{\rm EPR}|^{\otimes(n+2z)}_{CC^{\prime}})\pi_{C}^{\dagger}U_{CR}^{\dagger}\pi_{C}\sqrt{{V^{{\rm rej}\dagger}_{{\mathcal{F}}}}}\Big)\otimes|\text{rej}\rangle\langle\text{rej}|\Big\}.

Here
{\mathcal{S}}_{n+2z}
stands for the set of permutations of
n+2z
modes.
Again we write

U_{CR}=\int{\rm d}^{2}\alpha_{1}...{\rm d}^{2}\alpha_{n+2z}\,\chi(\vec{\alpha})\,D_{C}(\vec{\alpha})\otimes U_{R}^{\vec{\alpha}}
with normalisation

\int{\rm d}^{2}\alpha_{1}...{\rm d}^{2}\alpha_{n+2z}\,|\chi(\vec{\alpha})|^{2}=1
.
Again we use
\pi^{{\dagger}}D(\vec{\alpha})\pi=D(\pi^{-1}\vec{\alpha})
.
Furthermore we rotate
\sqrt{V_{\mathcal{F}}}
under the
CC^{\prime}
-trace so that the square roots combine into
V_{\mathcal{F}}
;
then we substitute the POVM (
23
) into (
24
).
This gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{MR\to MRF}(\rho_{MR})\propto{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi\in{\mathcal{S}}_{n+2z}}\int{\rm d}^{2}\vec{\alpha}{\rm d}^{2}\vec{\alpha}^{\prime}\chi(\vec{\alpha})\overline{\chi(\vec{\alpha}^{\prime})}{\rm Tr}\,_{CC^{\prime}}\int{\rm d}^{2}\vec{\beta}\Big\{

\displaystyle I(\beta\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathcal{F}})U^{\alpha}_{R}\rho_{MR}U_{R}^{\alpha^{\prime}\dagger}\otimes D_{\pi^{-1}\vec{\alpha}^{\prime}}^{{\dagger}}D_{\beta}|{\rm EPR}\rangle\langle{\rm EPR}|^{\otimes(n+2z)}_{CC^{\prime}}D_{\beta}^{{\dagger}}D_{\pi^{-1}\vec{\alpha}}|{\rm EPR}\rangle\langle{\rm EPR}|^{\otimes(n+2z)}_{CC^{\prime}}\otimes|{\rm acc}\rangle\langle{\rm acc}|

\displaystyle+I(\beta\notin{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathcal{F}})\Omega_{M}\otimes{\rm Tr}_{M}[\cdots{\rm same}\cdots]\otimes|{\rm rej}\rangle\langle{\rm rej}|\Big\}

(25)

Here all the displacements act on the
C
space;
the
I(\beta\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathcal{F}})
is an indicator function that equals 1 when the condition is met;
the abbreviation ‘same’ stands for the same state in
MRCC^{\prime}
space as in the line above.
Since the EPR states count as beamsplitter-mixtures of perfect
x
- or
p
-eigenstates,
the trace
{\rm Tr}_{CC^{\prime}}
acting on the displaced EPR states yields a product of Dirac delta functions,

\delta(\vec{\beta}-\pi^{-1}\vec{\alpha})\delta(\vec{\beta}-\pi^{-1}\vec{\alpha}^{\prime})
,
which can be rewritten as

\delta(\vec{\alpha}^{\prime}-\vec{\alpha})\delta(\vec{\beta}-\pi^{-1}\vec{\alpha})
.
Carrying out the integrals over
\vec{\alpha}^{\prime}
and
\vec{\beta}
yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{MR\to MRF}(\rho_{MR})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi\in{\mathcal{S}}_{n+2z}}\int{\rm d}^{2}\vec{\alpha}|\chi(\vec{\alpha})|^{2}\Big\{I(\pi^{-1}\vec{\alpha}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathcal{F}})U_{R}^{\alpha}\rho_{MR}U_{R}^{\alpha\dagger}\otimes|{\rm acc}\rangle\langle{\rm acc}|

(26)

\displaystyle+I(\pi^{-1}\vec{\alpha}\notin{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathcal{F}})\Omega_{M}\otimes{\rm Tr}_{M}U_{R}^{\alpha}\rho_{MR}U_{R}^{\alpha\dagger}\otimes|{\rm rej}\rangle\langle{\rm rej}|\Big\}.

4.5
Finishing the proof

Note that we can write

I(\pi^{-1}\vec{\alpha}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathcal{F}})U_{R}^{\alpha}\rho_{MR}U_{R}^{\alpha\dagger}

in the more complicated form

I(\pi^{-1}\vec{\alpha}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathcal{F}}){\rm Dec}\Big([D_{\pi^{-1}\vec{\alpha}}]_{\rm msg}\otimes U_{R}^{\alpha}\;\;{\rm Enc}\rho_{MR}\;\;[D_{\pi^{-1}\vec{\alpha}}]_{\rm msg}^{{\dagger}}\otimes U_{R}^{\alpha\dagger}\Big)
.
This equality holds because, under the condition on
\vec{\alpha}
, the decoding is guaranteed to recover
\rho_{MR}
.
We use the more complicated form to express the difference
{\mathcal{C}}-{\mathcal{F}}
in a compact form,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}(\rho_{MR})-{\mathcal{F}}(\rho_{MR})={\mathbb{E}}_{\pi\in{\mathcal{S}}_{n+2z}}\int{\rm d}^{2}\vec{\alpha}\;|\chi(\vec{\alpha})|^{2}\Big[\Big\{G(\pi,\vec{\alpha})-I(\pi^{-1}\vec{\alpha}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathcal{F}})\Big\}

\displaystyle{\rm Dec}\Big([D_{\pi^{-1}\vec{\alpha}}]_{\rm msg}\otimes U_{R}^{\alpha}\;\;{\rm Enc}\rho_{MR}\;\;[D_{\pi^{-1}\vec{\alpha}}]_{\rm msg}^{{\dagger}}\otimes U_{R}^{\alpha\dagger}\Big)\otimes|{\rm acc}\rangle\langle{\rm acc}|

\displaystyle+\Big\{1-G(\pi,\vec{\alpha})-I(\pi^{-1}\vec{\alpha}\notin{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathcal{F}})\Big\}\Omega_{M}\otimes{\rm Tr}_{M}{\rm Dec}(\cdots{\rm same}\cdots)\otimes|{\rm rej}\rangle\langle{\rm rej}|\Big]

(27)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{\pi\in{\mathcal{S}}_{n+2z}}\int{\rm d}^{2}\vec{\alpha}\;|\chi(\vec{\alpha})|^{2}\Big\{G(\pi,\vec{\alpha})-I(\pi^{-1}\vec{\alpha}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathcal{F}})\Big\}

\displaystyle\Big\{{\rm Dec}\Big([D_{\pi^{-1}\vec{\alpha}}]_{\rm msg}\otimes U_{R}^{\alpha}\;\;{\rm Enc}\rho_{MR}\;\;[D_{\pi^{-1}\vec{\alpha}}]_{\rm msg}^{{\dagger}}\otimes U_{R}^{\alpha\dagger}\Big)\otimes|{\rm acc}\rangle\langle{\rm acc}|

\displaystyle-\Omega_{M}\otimes{\rm Tr}_{M}{\rm Dec}(\cdots{\rm same}\cdots)\otimes|{\rm rej}\rangle\langle{\rm rej}|\Big\}.

(28)

Next we use the triangle inequality to obtain the following bound

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{C}}(\rho_{MR})-{\mathcal{F}}(\rho_{MR})\|_{\rm tr}\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi\in{\mathcal{S}}_{n+2z}}\int{\rm d}^{2}\vec{\alpha}\;|\chi(\vec{\alpha})|^{2}\Big\{G(\pi,\vec{\alpha})-I(\pi^{-1}\vec{\alpha}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathcal{F}})\Big\}

\displaystyle\Big\|{\rm Dec}\Big([D_{\pi^{-1}\vec{\alpha}}]_{\rm msg}\otimes U_{R}^{\alpha}\;\;{\rm Enc}\rho_{MR}\;\;[D_{\pi^{-1}\vec{\alpha}}]_{\rm msg}^{{\dagger}}\otimes U_{R}^{\alpha\dagger}\Big)\otimes|{\rm acc}\rangle\langle{\rm acc}|

\displaystyle-\Omega_{M}\otimes{\rm Tr}_{M}{\rm Dec}(\cdots{\rm same}\cdots)\otimes|{\rm rej}\rangle\langle{\rm rej}|\Big\|_{\rm tr}

(29)

\displaystyle\leq\int{\rm d}^{2}\vec{\alpha}\;|\chi(\vec{\alpha})|^{2}{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi\in{\mathcal{S}}_{n+2z}}\Big\{G(\pi,\vec{\alpha})-I(\pi^{-1}\vec{\alpha}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathcal{F}})\Big\}.

(30)

In the last step we used that the trace distance between two normalised states cannot exceed 1.

Note that the functions
G
and
I
are very similar.
The indicator
I
exactly selects displacements
\vec{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n+2z}

such that in the traps part of
\pi^{-1}\vec{\alpha}
the measured component is
\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{2}}
-close to zero,
and in the message part of
\pi^{-1}\vec{\alpha}
the Hamming weight
w_{\delta}
is low.

The function
G
is not an exact indicator function, having continuous behaviour.
However, for
e^{-r/2}\ll\epsilon
it is extremely close to a step function;
we will assume that we are in this regime.
The
G
enforces the same conditions as
I
on the traps part of
\pi^{-1}\vec{\alpha}
,
but ignores the message part.

The expression
G(\pi,\vec{\alpha})-I(\pi^{-1}\vec{\alpha}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathcal{F}})
evaluates either to 0 or 1;
it cannot become negative since
I
imposes more conditions than
G
.
The value 1 occurs only if the traps are intact but the message has uncorrectable noise.
(See Table
1
).

For the final step in the proof we have to tune the parameter
\delta
to
\delta=\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{2}}

in order to obtain symmetry between all the modes.
Let
u
be the number of modes in
\vec{\alpha}
that contain a large displacement.
We consider only vectors
\vec{\alpha}
that can yield
G-I=1
for some permutation
\pi
.
Such a vector must have
u\in\{t+1,\ldots,n\}
.
The expression
{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi\in{\mathcal{S}}_{n+2z}}(G-I)
is the probability,
given a random permutation of
n+2z
modes, of placing the
u
noisy ones precisely
in the first
n
positions. This probability is given by

P(u)=\frac{{n\choose u}u!}{{n+2z\choose u}u!}=\frac{n!}{(n+2z)!}(n-u+1)\cdots(n-u+2z).

(31)

As
P(u)
is a decreasing function of
u
we can write
P(u)\leq P(t+1)
.
Next we write

P(t+1)=\frac{{n\choose t+1}}{{n+2z\choose t+1}}=\prod_{j=0}^{t}\frac{n-j}{n+2z-j}<\prod_{j=0}^{t}\frac{n}{n+2z}=(\frac{n}{n+2z})^{t+1}.

(32)

Here we have used the inequality

\frac{n-j}{n+2z-j}\leq\frac{n}{n+2z}
, which holds for
2z>n
.

Finally we use the normalisation of
\chi
and obtain the end result

\forall_{\rho_{MR}}\quad\|{\mathcal{C}}(\rho_{MR})-{\mathcal{F}}(\rho_{MR})\|_{\rm tr}<(\frac{n}{n+2z})^{t+1}.

(33)

Hence we satisfy the security definition (
2.3
) with
\eta=(\frac{n}{n+2z})^{t+1}
.
This is very similar to the DV result
(\frac{1}{3})^{t+1}
in
Broadbent and Wainewright 2016
,
but with flexibility in the number of traps.

5
Discussion

Our continuous-variable construction and its security proof bring no real surprises to those familiar with
the discrete-variable quantum authentication schemes.
However, some technical hurdles had to be overcome, e.g. dealing with the non-perfect CV QOTP, introducing the CV twirl
and handling the approximate step functions.
A more rigorous treatment of the approximate step functions (in which the difference between the
I
and
G
indicators
ends up as a small addition to
\eta
) is left for future work.

Note that the scheme authenticates a single-mode state.
This is readily generalized to multiple modes either by authenticating each mode individually or
by applying a quantum error-correcting code to a multi-mode message.
We note that
our counting argument (
31
) is presented in a bit more direct way
than the derivation in the Appendix of
Broadbent and Wainewright 2016
.
