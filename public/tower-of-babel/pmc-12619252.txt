Identifying indicators of “success” in managing the COVID-19 pandemic in Europe: insights from an interdisciplinary expert consultation
Authors: Zhuxin Mao, Zita Zsabokorszky, Joke Bilcke, Philippe Beutels
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReflecting on what we have been through and learned during the COVID-19 pandemic can help prepare for similar challenges in the future. A quintessential question is what may indicate “success” in pandemic management. We aimed to establish a workable set of indicators for success over different distinct pandemic stages, as well as cumulatively from pandemic emergence to epidemic control.MethodsFirst, using an iterative brainstorm and a pilot survey process, we developed a set of indicators of success for the entire pandemic (from late 2019 to date) and for six distinct COVID-19 pandemic stages: (1) pre-pandemic, (2) rising virus prevalence outside Europe, (3) rising virus prevalence in other countries or regions within Europe, (4) within-country first wave of virus, (5) subsequent virus waves pre-vaccination, (6) post-vaccination. Secondly, these indicators were validated by COVID-19 experts from different fields through an online survey. Experts were asked to rank the proposed indicators according to importance, along with answering two open-ended questions and five background questions.ResultsThe developed indicator lists differed according to the pandemic stages. A total number of 45 experts completed the survey (29% response rate). All 50 indicators were ranked by at least 70% of the respondents as important. Notably, experts from all disciplines agreed that mortality was the most important indicator over the entire pandemic, during the first wave and subsequent waves pre-vaccination, while vaccination coverage was considered the most important indicator post-vaccination. Experts had different ranking preferences for the remaining indicators.ConclusionReflecting on the COVID-19 pandemic, this list could serve as a valuable resource to point towards which indicators are most crucial to monitor, informing future pandemic response strategies. Meanwhile, as this study currently only relied on input from European experts, it could serve as a validation set for experts outside Europe as well as the general public.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-025-01751-9.Keywords: Preparedness, Policy-making, Public health, Vaccination coverage, Mortality, ICU, Hospital admissions, QALYs

Contributions to the literature• Existing evaluation of pandemic management mostly relies on selected indicators based on authors’ interpretations, but lacks a unified understanding of what may be indicative of successful pandemic management.• In addressing the abstract and multi-dimensional nature of the concept of success, this study adopted an iterative method to gather insights from experts in developing and validating a comprehensive list of indicators and/or actions that help determine success in managing the COVID-19 pandemic.• Reflecting on the COVID-19 pandemic, this list could serve as a valuable resource to inform and refine future pandemic response efforts, enhancing preparedness for and management of emerging health challenges.

Introduction

As we are leaving the crisis phase of the COVID-19 pandemic, following the World Health Organization (WHO’s) declaration that it is no longer a public health emergency of international concern (PHEIC) [1], it is essential to reflect on lessons learnt to better prepare for similar challenges in the future. The development of the COVID-19 pandemic revealed that many countries were ill-prepared to manage such a crisis [2, 3], with shortages of essential resources, inadequate supplies, and less effective policies, despite having experience with previous pandemics, epidemics or other crises. Accumulating studies have focused on investigating the far-reaching and multi-faceted impacts of pandemics, including impacts on morbidity, mortality and economy [4]. In addition to evaluating the impacts, the literature has also offered recommendations for mitigating the impacts of future outbreaks, as we also gained valuable insights into how different response strategies can influence the spread of the diseases and, ultimately, the outcomes of the pandemic [5, 6].

Most European (used in this article to mean countries within the European Economic Area (EEA) plus Switzerland and the United Kingdom (UK)) countries experienced the initial introduction and early transmission of SARS-CoV-2 around the same time, with the possible exception of Italy, which was the first heavily affected country in the region. While there were many similarities in their responses, there were important differences in how the various countries in Europe have responded to the challenges posed by the pandemic at different stages with important associated differences in outcomes [7]. Clearly, countries differed in public health and social measures (PHSMs) [8, 9] and pharmaceutical interventions (PIs, most importantly vaccines) [10], adherence to these interventions [11], as well as in COVID-19 disease burden and economic impact [12, 13]. These differences in pandemic management and outcomes offer an opportunity to analyse and learn from them.

Previous studies have made efforts to assess pandemic preparedness, and most of these studies primarily focused on reviewing epidemic parameters for modelling purposes [14] or evaluating preparedness using indicators chosen based on the authors’ interpretations of what constitutes preparation [3, 15]. Some studies compared the effectiveness of PHSMs across countries. These investigations often focused on the reproduction number [16], the case growth rate [17], the number of confirmed cases [18] or mortality [19] as the outcome of interest. However, beyond morbidity and mortality, the COVID-19 pandemic also had broader, indirect effects, as discussed by the WHO, such as fear of infection among the general population and impaired access to healthcare for non-COVID-19 conditions [20]. In addition, since countries that performed well in one aspect may not necessarily perform well in other aspects, comparisons in specific outcomes cannot provide a comprehensive picture of a country’s overall performance during the pandemic. Similarly, while there have been meta-analyses about the epidemiological impact of certain PHSMs [21, 22], these tend to focus on PHSMs in a certain time frame, and do not tend to address country performances at different stages, or cumulatively over time.

Clearly, to evaluate and compare the success of countries in managing a pandemic, it is important to understand what indicates “success”, accounting for different perspectives and different pandemic stages. The aim of this study was to gather insights from experts across various disciplines and to reach a consensus in identifying indicators of success in pandemic management, based on the recent COVID-19 experience. This could inform the development of effective response strategies for future pandemics with similar characteristics.

Methods

In this study, we developed and validated a list of indicators of success in managing the COVID-19 pandemic that would be supported by a large group of experts from different fields. We first used an iterative and rotating process among members of the European project ESCAPE (Efficient and rapidly SCAlable EU-wide evidence-driven Pandemic response plans through dynamic Epidemic data assimilation) [23] to develop a list of indicators of success. In a second phase, we validated these lists of indicators by collecting responses through an online questionnaire from an expert audience outside ESCAPE. The process of developing and validating the lists of indicators, and the analysis of responses from the validation questionnaire is described in detail in the following sections.

Developing lists of success indicators

Lists of success indicators were developed through brainstorm sessions and a pilot survey among ESCAPE members. ESCAPE includes members from seven different countries with a variety of scientific backgrounds, including infectious disease modelling, biostatistics, epidemiology, and health economics. This study forms part of work package 5 (WP5) of the ESCAPE project, which consists of 20 members from Belgium, France, Italy, the Netherlands, Portugal, Switzerland, and the UK.

Initial brainstorm session

We started with a brainstorm session on January 13th 2023 to identify potential indicators of success and to identify different stages of the pandemic at the ESCAPE project kick-off meeting with researchers (n = 8) interested in this topic. The brainstorm session led to an agreement on the general direction subsequent surveys would take. For example, we agreed to consider and frame both outcome-based indicators and process-based actions (the ability to take actions) across multiple dimensions, including economic, health and healthcare domains. Consensus was also reached that indicators would be considered separately at six different stages of a pandemic, i.e. (1) pre-pandemic, (2) rising virus prevalence outside Europe, (3) rising virus prevalence in other countries or regions within Europe, (4) within-country first wave of virus, (5) subsequent virus waves pre-vaccination, (6) post-vaccination (waves occurring after vaccination programs were initiated, which for COVID-19, in Europe, was typically from December 2020 or January 2021 onwards).

Pilot survey

Building on the initial brainstorm session, we (the authors) developed seven lists of indicators (one for the entire pandemic, and one for each of the six pandemic stages) and designed a pilot survey to obtain feedback on the proposed indicator lists from the whole project team. With the pilot survey we asked specifically which indicators ESCAPE members thought were most important (1) over the entire pandemic, (2) during each of the three earliest stages, (3) during each of the three last stages, as well as if there were any missing indicators. The pilot survey was discussed and validated during an online meeting. Following this, minor textual improvements were made, and it was clarified in the survey that respondents could use all their knowledge to decide which indicators would have been important during a given pandemic stage (see appendix). The subsequent version was further subjected to several iterations of formatting, testing and implementation in Qualtrics for flexible use on several devices. Then the link to the pilot survey was emailed about two weeks before an online workshop (June 2nd 2023) with all ESCAPE members. A reminder email with a link to the online pilot survey was sent on June 12th 2023).

An online workshop

The results of the pilot survey were then used as input for a more in-depth discussion about potential indicators of success during an online workshop with the ESCAPE members on June 15th 2023. The aim of the workshop was two-fold: first, to elicit feedback on which indicators to use for further validation; second, to collect feedback on the format of a survey for a wider audience.

The online workshop took three hours and consisted of an introduction, three small-group brainstorm sessions and a conclusion. The workshop participants were randomly divided into five break-out groups of four or five persons for each brainstorm session, including one of the organizers to facilitate the session. The three brainstorm sessions focused on the same three questions from the pilot survey. Participants were asked to select indicators that should be prioritized and to add missing indicators.

Validating lists of success indicators

Developing the validation survey

The pilot survey, including its aim, the question formats, as well as the included indicators, was revised and updated based on the results and feedback of the online workshop. We consolidated the results and finalized a survey for a wider audience outside ESCAPE. The finalized survey started with two open-ended questions: (1) Which indicators and/or actions determine if a country or region in Europe can be considered more successful at managing the COVID-19 pandemic? (2) Which country or region in Europe was the most successful during the COVID-19 pandemic since the beginning of the outbreak? Respondents were then provided with a list of indicators and were asked to rank the indicators according to how important they were to determine whether a country or region was more successful over the entire pandemic. Tied ranking was allowed and respondents could also add indicators that were not included in the list presented to them. Subsequently, they were asked to rank the list of indicators for each of the six phases of the pandemic. At the end of the survey, five brief background questions were posed, inquiring about respondents’ age, gender, involvement in managing the COVID-19 pandemic, field of expertise, and years of experience.

Distributing the validation survey

The updated survey was distributed electronically among experts from a wider range of disciplines to validate the indicators of success by non-participants of the ESCAPE project. We aimed to query a diverse group of respondents from different countries and different fields of speciality, including sociologists, psychologists, medical doctors, political scientists, economists, ethicists or philosophers, and policymakers, with at least five years of experience within their discipline. We first asked ESCAPE WP members to provide contact details from their country of at least one person for each of the above-mentioned seven fields of expertise. Given the low response rate (see Results), we actively identified and invited 123 persons serving on key advisory committees in Belgium (Superior Health Council, Pandemic management expert group, Risk Assessment Group) and/or having a proven track record based on their discipline-specific research on COVID-19 (as identified through Web of Science full articles). Additionally, we extended our invitation to the international participants of an online ESCAPE outreach event and asked those who met our inclusion criteria (at least five years’ experience within their discipline), to respond to our survey.

Data analysis

For the open-ended questions from the validation survey that collected non-numerical data, we conducted a content analysis to summarize the indicators mentioned by the respondents and calculated the frequency of occurrence of the missing indicators that were not covered in our original list of indicators.

For the numerical ranking data, we conducted descriptive analysis. We calculated the frequency of occurrence for each indicator ranked in the first, second, and third positions. For each individual indicator, we also obtained the frequency of respondents who did not assign any rank to that specific indicator. We calculated the mean, median, minimum and maximum ranking positions for each indicator. The mean ranking position for each indicator was calculated by summing the total ranks and dividing by the number of respondents who assigned ranks to that indicator.

We then performed a comparative subgroup analysis based on area and years of expertise, gender and age group. Subgroup analysis by country was not performed due to the small sample size.

The analysis was conducted in Microsoft Excel and R.

Results of the validation survey

Respondents

In total, we collected 45 responses from 10 non-Belgian experts (19.6% response rate), 30 Belgian experts (24.4% response rate) and another five eligible expert participants at the outreach event. Given the study design, we were unable to systematically collect reasons for non-response, but when these were made clear, they included mostly “no time”, “no interest”, and exceptionally “do not feel qualified”. More than half of the respondents (58%) indicated that they had more than 20 years of experience in their field of expertise. 69% of the respondents were actively involved in managing COVID-19 responses, of whom more than 65% were involved in managing COVID-19 response in Belgium. 22% of our respondents were identified as economists, 24% as medical doctors (clinical), while at least one expert from areas such as sociology, psychology and political science responded.

Selected and missing indicators of success

As answers to the first open question on indicators of success, a few indicators which were not covered in the originally proposed indicator lists were identified, including the supply of basic substances and food, speed of policy reaction and vaccination rate in specific groups, which were mentioned by four, eight and four respondents, respectively (Appendix A.3.1). Additionally, 18 respondents emphasized unsolicited that in terms of mortality, the focus should be on “excess” mortality. Vaccination coverage was mentioned by five respondents as a missing success indicator for the entire pandemic, while surveillance and collaboration between regions and the government was indicated as missing for the pre-pandemic phase by four respondents (Appendix A.3.2). All other indicators not included in the ranking lists were proposed by less than four respondents.

All 51 success indicators included in the lists were ranked by at least 70% of the respondents, with all respondents assigning a rank to at least one indicator over the entire pandemic and in the pre-pandemic stage, and for the other five phases, two, three, four, five, and four participants, respectively, not providing any rank (Appendix A.3.3).

Ranking of indicators of success in managing COVID-19 pandemic

When looking at indicators of success in managing COVID-19 over the entire pandemic timeline, 53% of respondents ranked mortality as the most important indicator, while over 85% ranked this indicator within the most important three positions (ranks one, two and three). Health and health care indicators including life years lost, ICU/hospital admission and occupancy were also ranked among the three most important indicators (ranks one, two and three, indicating the highest importance) by over 60% of the respondents. GDP per capita received the least number of votes for the most important three positions, but still 11 respondents placed this indicator within their top three most important rankings (Fig. 1). The rankings over the entire pandemic were comparable to the ranking results during the first wave and the subsequent waves pre-vaccination, where health and health care indicators were most likely to be ranked as the most important three and GDP per capita was least likely (Fig. 1). At the pre-pandemic stage, as the prevalence of the virus rose outside of Europe and the prevalence of the virus in other countries or regions within Europe but outside the country of interest rose, the frequency of ranks one, two or three showed relatively minor variation among the included indicators. In the post-vaccination phase, vaccination coverage stood out compared to other indicators, consistently ranking within the three most important positions. These results are confirmed by the summary statistics (Table 3). For instance, mortality was ranked as the most important indicator over the entire pandemic also based on having the mean (median) [range] rank value which was closest to one (1.84). On the other hand, the mean ranks of trust in institutions and government (4.86) and GDP per capita (4.84) were consistently considered as less important relative to others, and the two indicators also tended to have a higher percentage of respondents not ranking them (Appendix A.3.3).

The range of rankings for each indicator varied greatly, partially explained by allowing for tied ranking. Subgroup analysis

Experts seemed to agree about the most important success indicator for the entire pandemic and the pandemic phases within the country or region, regardless of their disciplines, gender, age, and years of experience (Appendix A.4.1 and A.4.2). Over the entire pandemic, mortality had consistently the mean rank closest to one (i.e. the rank indicating most important), whereas during the first and subsequent waves pre-vaccination, either mortality or ICU admissions & occupancy had the mean rank closet to one (i.e. indicating most important) for any of the subgroups. For the phase after vaccination was initiated, experts from different subgroups agreed vaccination coverage to be the most important indicator of success. This is also true for experts invited by WP members and among the Belgian experts (Appendix A.4.3). On the other hand, experts recruited from the outreach event tended to assign better ranks (indicating higher importance) to each indicator. For example, they treated each indicator over the entire pandemic to be important, assigning most important three ranks (ranks one, two and three) to every indicator, thus resulting in smaller differences in average ranking positions for each indicator compared to other groups.

Respondents from different disciplines tended to have different ranking preferences for the remaining indicators (Table 4). However, the sample size per expertise field is small so results should be considered with caution. Public health experts and sociologists were more likely to prioritize equity and inequality impact compared to other experts. The majority of the (bio)medical scientists and sociologists in our sample treated on-site school days lost as relatively important, while none of the epidemiologist respondents assigned a most important ranking to it. The average rank given by medical doctors tended to be larger compared to other expert groups, for most of the indicators, while sociologists tended to consider most indicators as most important by consistently assigning many of them to positions one to three. Epidemiologists, public health experts and medical doctors tended to agree that GDP per capital was relatively less important, as none of them assigned the top three ranks to it. Subgroup differences based on age, gender, and years of experience were found to be less pronounced and are presented in Appendix A.4.2.

Countries considered most successful in managing COVID-19

The frequency of occurrence of country or region in Europe considered the most successful is shown in Appendix A.5. Five respondents left the question blank, while 13 respondents considered this question to be challenging and/or unanswerable. For instance, one expert explicitly stated that “Impossible to answer this question, as a country may have performed well on a certain indicator while ranking poorly on another”, and similarly, an expert illustrated that “I don’t have enough knowledge on different aspects of ‘success’ … I think different countries/regions were more successful than others”. Nordic countries including Denmark (n = 6), Norway (n = 5), Sweden (n = 3) and Finland (n = 2) were most likely to be mentioned as the most successful countries in Europe by respondents.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and validated a list of indicators that could be used to determine if a country or region in Europe was more successful in managing COVID-19, over the entire pandemic as well as during different distinct pandemic stages. Overall, the proposed list of success indicators was validated by a diverse group of experts. In particular, experts agreed on severe disease burden (mortality and/or ICU admissions & occupancy) being the most important indicator of success over the entire pandemic and during the first and subsequent waves pre-vaccination, while vaccination coverage was considered the most important success indicator during stages after vaccination was initiated. GDP per capita was considered a less important indicator compared to others, often occupying a worse mean ranking position. GDP per capita also had a higher proportion of non-responses, suggesting that some respondents chose not to assign a rank to this indicator, likely because they considered it less important. This observation may also be due to the complexity of economic summary indicators. Despite providing definitions for each indicator in our survey, experts unfamiliar with economic terms may have found GDP per capita less understandable. For instance, while all economist respondents provided rankings for GDP per capita, five medical doctors abstained from assigning ranks to this indicator.

Interestingly, Nordic countries were frequently mentioned as the most successful countries in managing COVID-19 by the respondents in the survey. Concurrently, these countries were found to have a lower level of excess mortality compared to other European countries between 2020 and 2023 [24, 25]. The recognition of Nordic countries’ success, along with their favorable excess mortality outcomes, is in line with our result which shows that mortality plays an important role in determining whether a country was considered successful in managing the COVID-19 pandemic. This further validates mortality as a key indicator for successful management of pandemics.

Previous systematic reviews have identified that epidemiological outcomes, mainly deaths (mortality) and confirmed cases (incidence rate), were the primary outcomes frequently utilized to evaluate the effectiveness of the PHSMs during COVID-19 [26, 27]. Hospitalizations and ICU admissions were also found to be used, although less frequently [27], maybe due to the lack of data. Vaccination coverage has also been used as an indicator for comparing countries’ responses and outcomes during the COVID-19 pandemic [28, 29], with these investigations specifically highlighting its association with case fatality rate. Vaccination coverage has also been found to be influenced by various country-level factors, including stringency of implementing restrictive measures, population age, gender and urbanization, corruption, government ideology and cultural context [30]. In addition, other health and health care indicators, such as mental health [31] and health-related quality of life (HRQoL) [32, 33], have been employed for assessing the performance of countries during the pandemic. Some studies have also incorporated societal indicators or actions, such as inequality [34] and government trust [35] to conduct cross-country comparisons. However, it is worth noting that the listed indicators are not stand-alone and can be closely interrelated. Indicators can also serve as determinants for one another, for example, vaccination has been found to prevent infections, hospitalizations, ICU admissions and deaths [25, 36–38], even as social contact rates restored to pre-COVID-19 levels [39, 40], thus also enabling a return to pre-COVID-19 economic activity.

Measuring success is inherently challenging, as it encompasses various aspects, such as economic, health, healthcare and societal indicators, which often interact with one another [25, 30]. Even when focusing on a single indicator, such as mortality in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic, measurement remains challenging due to various metrics. Excess mortality, which is the difference between the number of deaths during the pandemic and the number of deaths that would be expected based on historical data, has been considered a more accurate measure of the total mortality impact of the pandemic than reported COVID-19 deaths [12, 25]. The proportion of deaths per average case, or case-fatality ratio, has also been regarded as a key parameter to understand the impact of the pandemic [41]. However, because it is typically based on confirmed cases, which can be influenced by factors such as testing capacity, it may make between-country comparisons misleading. In addition, specific attention may be required for mortality in nursing homes or among elderly people [42]. Similarly, vaccination coverage in the general population or specific high-risk groups such as the elderly and healthcare professionals, as well as primary and booster dose administrations, can be considered to contribute differently to the overall success of pandemic management [30, 43]. Additionally, some indicators are subjective (e.g., mental health, trust in government) and require simultaneous efforts, such as population surveys, to quantify, while some are standard measures but may only become available after a delay (e.g., excess mortality, GDP growth).

Moreover, European countries differ substantially in demographic, economic, social and health system characteristics. These differences influenced the context policy makers faced and the tools they had available to manage the pandemic. For instance, countries with a relatively younger population composition, greater hospital bed capacity or a smaller average household size could afford less stringency in social contact reductions without incurring higher excess mortality and/or hospital bed capacity problems versus other countries, ceteris paribus. The role of such contextual factors needs to be considered in research that would aim to understand country differences in achieving certain indicators of success. An example is provided by Varbanova et al. [30] for vaccination coverage, one of the indicators of successful management that we identified. In related research, we will operationalize more of these indicators to measure the degree of successful management in cross-country comparison.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to gather expert opinions to identify indicators of success in managing the COVID-19 pandemic. Previous studies evaluating countries’ performance typically selected indicators based on authors’ interpretations of success criteria and most of these studies focused on COVID-19-related cases and deaths [44, 45]. In addressing the abstract and multi-dimensional nature of the concept of success, we adopted an iterative method to gather insights from experts across various disciplines and to reach a consensus. However, our study may disproportionately reflect expert perspectives in a limited number of countries in Europe, and within that had overrepresentation of experts in Belgium. We note, though, that many of these experts had also served as advisors in international contexts. Although we aimed to obtain opinions from a diverse group of experts, the response rate was low, which may lead to selection biases. As a result, the views captured may not fully represent the broader expert community. The low response rate may be due to potential participants’ busy workload and may also reflect the challenges in addressing the inherently difficult question: what indicates “success” in pandemic management.

In addition, we did not collect opinions from the general public or experts outside Europe, potentially introducing biases from contextual uniformity. Despite this, our indicators were validated across the diverse group of expert respondents and remained robust. For example, irrespective of fields of expertise, gender and age, experts consistently regarded mortality and ICU admissions as the most important indicators during the first and subsequent waves pre-vaccination and over the entire pandemic. In addition, we obtained insights from a diverse and relatively large sample of expert respondents using different channels and sources, especially considering the busy schedule of individuals in the years following the COVID-19 pandemic. Another strength of our approach is that we incorporated open-ended questions to provide experts with the opportunity to identify any potential missing indicators. Their suggestions helped us revise and improve the list. For instance, the list could be expanded by the speed of acting through appropriate PHSMs, and the supply of basic substances, as these were mentioned frequently.

Expectedly, our results revealed subgroup differences, as experts from varying disciplines indicated distinct preferences in their ranking of most of the indicators. This highlights the importance of incorporating opinions from diverse fields to ensure the comprehensiveness of the indicator list. For example, public health experts in our sample were more likely to place equity and inequality as more important indicators while other experts were not; sociologist respondents tended to consider all aspects when thinking of success by consistently assigning many of the indicators with the most important rankings. These discrepancies therefore underscore the challenges in precisely specifying the importance levels for each indicator. Nonetheless, our findings provide compelling evidence indicating a consensus among experts, with health and health care indicators, especially mortality, being universally recognized as the most important factors across the expert groups.

Conclusion

In sum, our study gathered expert opinions to develop and validate a comprehensive list of indicators of success in managing the COVID-19 pandemic. Reflecting on the COVID-19 pandemic, this list could serve as a valuable resource to inform and refine future pandemic response efforts, enhancing preparedness against emerging health challenges.
