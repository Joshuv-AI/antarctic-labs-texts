What intensivist need to know about hantavirus cardiopulmonary syndrome
Authors: René López, Gregory J Mertz, Pablo A Vial
Published: 2026

Hantavirus infection is a zoonosis caused by Orthohantaviruses, typically acquired in rural settings through inhalation of aerosolized rodent excreta. Two major clinical syndromes are recognized. Hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome (HFRS) is the predominant Old-World presentation and is mainly caused by Hantaan, Puumala, Dobrava, and Seoul viruses in Europe and Asia. Hantavirus cardiopulmonary syndrome (HCPS), by contrast, is the New World presentation and is caused by Sin Nombre virus, Andes virus, Araraquara virus, Juquitiba virus, Choclo virus, and Laguna Negra virus in the Americas. Andes virus remains distinctive because person-to-person transmission has been documented [1, 2]. HCPS carries a case fatality rate of approximately 30–45%, whereas mortality in HFRS is lower, generally 1–12% [1]. In a globalized world, imported cases may present to ICUs far from endemic regions, making HCPS an important travel-associated critical illness [3]. For intensivists, HCPS is defined by speed. Patients may deteriorate from a nonspecific febrile illness to severe hypoxemic respiratory failure and shock over a matter of hours to a few days [4]. Synoptic overview of seven key concepts for intensivists to retain: risk of transmission; pathophysiology of permeability pulmonary edema; temporal profile of Andes virus infection; personal protective equipment; admission laboratory findings for risk stratification; hemodynamic monitoring to guide a physiology-based supportive approach; fluid restriction and early initiation of vasoactive agents; and, finally, the Crowley criteria [5] for initiation of VA-ECMO

The biological hallmark of HCPS is endothelial dysfunction with increased vascular permeability. Thrombocytopenia is an early and characteristic feature. The downstream consequences are capillary leak, non-hydrostatic pulmonary edema, hemoconcentration, relative intravascular hypovolemia, and progressive circulatory failure [1, 5]. The process does not appear to be primarily due to direct lytic endothelial injury, but rather to functional disruption of the endothelial barrier. Experimental data show interaction with β3-integrin-related pathways, dysregulation of vascular endothelial growth factor, and altered VE-cadherin expression in this process. Protocadherin-1 (PCDH1), expressed in human airway epithelial and endothelial cells has been shown to be necessary as a cellular receptor for New World viruses that produce HCPS. Additional mechanisms, including bradykinin and RhoA pathway activation, have been proposed, although supporting evidence remains limited [1, 4].

This pathophysiology explains the distinctive hemodynamic profile of severe HCPS. In the sickest patients, profound capillary leak leads to reduced effective preload, low stroke volume, rapidly worsening pulmonary edema, and progressive circulatory dysfunction [5]. Cardiogenic shock appears central in fatal HCPS, but it develops in a background of intense capillary leak and cannot be interpreted in isolation. Recent bedside studies using transpulmonary thermodilution have refined this physiological picture, showing low stroke volume, reduced global end-diastolic volume, impaired cardiac performance, high extravascular lung water, and increased pulmonary vascular permeability [6]. These findings explain why liberal fluid administration may transiently improve filling while accelerating pulmonary edema and worsening oxygenation, yet excessive fluid restriction may aggravate profound hypovolemia and tissue hypoperfusion. HCPS therefore requires repeated bedside reassessment and physiology-guided support rather than protocolized resuscitation extrapolated from septic shock [4].

Clinical suspicion should arise in any patient who presents with fever, thrombocytopenia, and rapidly progressive respiratory symptoms, especially when accompanied by a compatible exposure history and by hemoconcentration, leukocytosis with immunoblasts, and/or bilateral infiltrates. In reports where incubation periods can be assessed, median incubation periods range from 18 to 21 days whereas documented incubation periods have ranged from 7 to 42 days [7]. The prodromal phase usually lasts 2–7 days and includes fever, headache, myalgia, abdominal pain, vomiting, and diarrhea [1]. This is followed by the cardiopulmonary phase, characterized by abrupt onset of cough, dyspnea, tachycardia, and hypotension, progressing within hours to non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema, respiratory failure, and often shock. Most deaths occur within the first 24 h after hospital admission. In most cases, patients deteriorate before serology or PCR results are available, meaning that critical decisions must often be made on presumptive clinical grounds [1, 4].

Risk stratification is particularly important because it may support early transfer and escalation of care. Platelet count appears to be clinically useful: admission platelet counts above 115,000/mm3 have been associated with lower risk of severe progression, whereas counts below 40,000/mm3 have been associated with a markedly higher risk of deterioration, including the need for veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) [8]. Proteinuria may also help identify patients at higher risk of poor outcome. In the ICU, the diagnostic approach should therefore prioritize recognition over delay [9]. Serology remains the most widely used diagnostic method, but RT-PCR can detect infection before antibodies appear and, in Andes virus infection, may identify viral RNA in peripheral blood buffy coat even before symptom onset [10] during recognized outbreaks or after a high risk exposure such as a needle stick, wild rodent bites, or inadvertent administration of hantavirus-infected blood products.

Supportive care remains the cornerstone of treatment. ICU staff should use personal protective equipment according to current recommendations, including a gown, gloves, eye protection, and an N95 respirator or higher. Mechanical ventilation should follow a lung-protective strategy [4]. Positive end-expiratory pressure should be titrated cautiously, since tolerance of its hemodynamic effects is often poor [4]. Fluid therapy should be cautious and generally restrictive, aiming to preserve tissue perfusion while avoiding further worsening of pulmonary edema [4]. Early norepinephrine is a practical first-line vasoactive strategy, with the addition of inotropes when myocardial dysfunction becomes evident in patients with persistent hypoperfusion [4]. Invasive hemodynamic monitoring can be particularly helpful when deciding whether conventional support remains sufficient or whether advanced rescue strategies should be mobilized [4, 6].

For the most critically ill patients, VA-ECMO has emerged as a major rescue therapy [5]. In severe HCPS, VA-ECMO can bridge patients through a short but catastrophic period of cardiopulmonary failure [5]. Early cannulation, particularly with contemporary percutaneous strategies, appears to be associated with better outcomes than delayed rescue after progression to refractory multisystem failure [11]. Targeted high-volume hemofiltration has also been reported as an adjunctive strategy in selected patients, in some cases potentially avoiding VA-ECMO, although evidence remains limited [12].

No specific therapy has shown clear benefit once HCPS is established. Ribavirin did not improve outcomes when given after the onset of the cardiopulmonary phase [13], and high-dose methylprednisolone was ineffective in a controlled Chilean trial [14]. Convalescent plasma [15], monoclonal antibodies, and other immune-based interventions remain investigational [1]. The most effective treatment is early, high-quality intensive care delivered in the right place. HCPS should therefore be regarded as a New World endemic disease with global relevance: a rapidly progressive travel-associated critical illness in which diagnosis is often presumptive, management must be decisive, and early transfer to an experienced ECMO center may determine survival [1, 4, 5, 11].
