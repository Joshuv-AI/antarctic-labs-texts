Structural basis for inhibition of TcrXyn10A xylanase by nonspecific xylose binding at subsite − 2
Authors: Ki Hyun Nam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractXylanases play a critical role in the degradation of lignocellulose-based biomass for the production of biofuels, value-added chemicals, and various industrial applications. Xylanase activity is often inhibited by reaction products such as xylose, which reduces the efficiency of industrial applications involving xylan degradation. Xylanase TcrXyn10A is an industrially promising enzyme that efficiently hydrolyzes agro-food byproducts, but its catalytic activity decreases in the presence of xylose, and the mechanism underlying this inhibition remains unclear. To understand the product inhibition mechanism, the crystal structure and molecular dynamics simulations of xylose-bound TcrXyn10A were analyzed. The xylose molecule binds to the − 2 subsite within the substrate-binding cleft of one of the two molecules in the asymmetric unit, indicating that xylose acts as a competitive inhibitor with low binding affinity. The bound xylose is stabilized by hydrogen bonds and a π–π interaction with five conserved residues located at the subsite − 2. Xylose binding induces subtle movements of interacting residues, resulting in a narrowing of the substrate-binding cleft around subsites − 2 and − 1. Structural comparison with other xylobiose-bound GH10 xylanases revealed that the binding mode of xylose at the − 2 subsite differed depending on the number of xylose units occupying the binding cleft. These findings provide insights into the molecular basis of xylose-induced inhibition in TcrXyn10A and contribute to a broader understanding of product inhibition mechanisms across the GH10 family.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-57315-6.Keywords: Xylanase, Xylose inhibition, Substrate binding cleft, Biomass degradation, Crystal structureSubject terms: Biochemistry, Structural biology

Introduction

Lignocellulosic biomass has emerged as an abundant and sustainable resource for the production of renewable fuels, chemicals, and biomaterials, offering a promising alternative to fossil-based resources1–4. Lignocellulose, the major structural component of plant biomass, is primarily composed of lignin (15–20%), hemicellulose (25–30%) and cellulose (40–50%)5–7. Hemicellulose plays a crucial role in the complete utilization of biomass and requires enzymatic hydrolysis for efficient degradation8. The breakdown of xylan, the predominant hemicellulosic polysaccharide, into smaller sugars such as xylose or xylooligosaccharides (XOS) is a key step for industrial applications7,9.

Xylanases are hydrolytic enzymes that cleave the β−1,4 backbone of the plant cell-wall polysaccharide xylan, facilitating its degradation and subsequent conversion into bioethanol and other bio-based products7,10. In addition, xylanases are widely used in diverse industrial applications, including paper bleaching, animal-feed improvement, and various biotechnological processes11.

The Carbohydrate-Active enZymes (CAZy) database classifies xylanases into several families based on amino acid sequence similarities and catalytic mechanisms12. To date, xylanase activity has been reported in several glycoside hydrolase (GH) families, including GH5, 7, 8, 10, 11, 12, 16, 26, 30, 43, 44, 51, and 6213. These enzymes can be broadly categorized into single catalytic domain xylanases (GH5, 7, 8, 10, 11, and 43), bifunctional enzymes containing two catalytic domains (GH16, 51, and 62), and enzymes exhibiting secondary or auxiliary xylanolytic activity11,13. Each of these xylanases exhibits distinct substrate specificities, biochemical functions, and metabolic and cellular functions7,14,15. Among these, GH10 xylanases are well characterized for their high catalytic efficiency and broad substrate specificity, hydrolyzing both xylan and cellulose16,17. GH10 enzymes have a larger substrate-binding cleft and broader specificity, enabling them to accommodate substituted xylan chains and cleave near branched residues7,18. This catalytic versatility allows GH10 xylanases to efficiently degrade diverse plant xylans, releasing fermentable sugars and prebiotic XOS10,19.

During the enzymatic degradation of biomass, various compounds are released as byproducts, including phenolic compounds from lignin decomposition, organic acids generated from carbohydrate hydrolysis, and sugar derivatives20,21. While some of these compounds serve as valuable intermediates for biochemical synthesis, others can significantly inhibit enzymatic activity, thereby reducing bioconversion efficiency22. Understanding the inhibitory effects of biomass-derived compounds is therefore critical for optimizing biomass conversion strategies and enhancing enzyme performance in large-scale industrial applications23,24.

The endo-xylanase Xyn10A (TcrXyn10A) from the filamentous fungus Thermoascus crustaceus, expressed in Pichia pastoris, exhibits high enzymatic activity toward both xylan and cellulose25; however, the production of the recombinant enzyme is costly due to the characteristics of the expression host. To reduce production costs, TcrXyn10A was recently overexpressed in Escherichia coli, which also exhibited both xylanase and cellulase activities26. TcrXyn10A exhibits optimal enzymatic activity at 50–60 °C and pH 5.0, with specific activity of 525.8 U/mg toward beechwood xylan26. In addition, TcrXyn10A effectively degrades xylans derived from corn stalks and perilla, highlighting its potential for industrial applications. Meanwhile, the inhibitory effect analysis showed that the enzyme activity of TcrXyn10A was reduced by lignocellulose-derived compounds such as acetic acid, vanillin, and xylose, whereas 5-hydroxymethylfurfural and glucose increased the enzyme activity26. Among these inhibitory molecules, xylose, the reaction product of TcrXyn10A acting on the xylan substrate, reduced the enzyme activity by approximately 10% compared with its maximum activity26; however, the mechanism by which xylose inhibits TcrXyn10A has not been elucidated. In addition, other GH10 xylanases have also been reported to exhibit reduced activity in the presence of xylose14,20,27,28. However, the structural basis for the xylose-induced inhibitory mechanism of GH10 xylanases has not yet been fully elucidated.

To understand the xylose-induced inhibitory effect on TcrXyn10A, here the crystal structure of xylose-bound TcrXyn10A was determined. The xylose-binding site and xylose-induced conformational changes in the substrate-binding cleft of TcrXyn10A were analyzed. The binding mode of the xylose molecule on TcrXyn10A was compared with those of other xylooligosaccharide-bound GH10 family enzymes. Based on structural analysis and molecular dynamics (MD) simulation results, a xylose-induced inhibition mechanism was proposed. These findings provide insights into the xylose-induced inhibitory effect in TcrXyn10A and other GH10 family enzymes.

Materials and methods

Protein expression and purification

The cloning and protein preparation of TcrXyn10A have been reported in a previous study26. Briefly, the DNA vector containing the synthesized TcrXyn10A gene (UniProt code: A0A3G2WJY4, Asn19-Arg327) was transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) cells. Protein expression was induced by Isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) at 18 °C overnight. After cell disruption and removal of cell debris, the supernatant was purified using affinity chromatography with Ni-NTA resin (Qiagen, Venlo, Netherlands), followed by size-exclusion chromatography using a HiLoad Sephacryl S-100 column (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA), and eluted with buffer containing 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) and 200 mM NaCl. Protein purity was confirmed by 12% SDS–PAGE. The protein concentration was determined using a Bradford assay. The purified protein was then concentrated to 20 mg/mL for crystallization.

Crystallization

Crystallization of TcrXyn10A was performed using the hanging-drop vapor diffusion method at 22 °C. Protein solution (2 µL) was mixed with crystallization solution (2 µL) consisting of 0.1 M HEPES (pH 7.5), 0.2 M NaCl, and 25% (w/v) polyethylene glycol 3350, and equilibrated against 0.5 mL of the crystallization solution in 24-well VDX plate (Hampton Research, Aliso Viejo, CA, USA). Suitable crystals for X-ray diffraction experiments were obtained within two months.

X-ray diffraction data collection

X-ray diffraction data were collected at beamline 11 C of the Pohang Accelerator Laboratory (PAL, Republic of Korea)29. For xylose-bound TcrXyn10A, native crystals were soaked in the crystallization solution supplemented with 10 mM xylose for 30 min and then soaked in the cryoprotectant solution for 5 s. TcrXyn10A crystals were mounted on the goniometer using a nylon loop under a liquid-nitrogen stream at 100 K. Diffraction images were recorded on a PILATUS 6 M detector (DECTRIS, Baden, Switzerland). Diffraction data were indexed, integrated, and scaled using the HKL2000 program30.

Structure determination

The phase problem was solved by molecular replacement using the crystal structure of native TcrXyn10A (PDB code: 9VK4) as a search model with MOLREP31 implemented in CCP432 at Global Science Data hub Center (GSDC) at Korea Institute of Science and Technology Information (KISTI)33. Iterative model building was performed using COOT34. The model structure was refined using phenix.refinement implemented in PHENIX35. Water molecules were added during refinement with default parameters. The previously reported native TcrXyn10A dataset (PDB code: 9VK4)26 was reprocessed and re-refined to improve the structural model in this study. The final model structure was validated with MolProbity36 and the PDB validation server. Structure figures were generated using PyMOL (Schrödinger, LLC.).

Bioinformatics

The binding affinity between xylose and TcrXyn10A was calculated with PRODIGY-LIGAND37. Homologous amino acid sequences of TcrXyn10A were searched using BLAST38. Sequence alignment was performed using Clustal Omega39 and visualized using ESPript 3.040. The substrate-binding cleft was analyzed using KVFinder-web41. The protein–ligand interactions were visualized using PoseEdit42.

Molecular dynamics simulation

All-atom MD simulations were performed for the xylose-bound TcrXyn10A and the apo form of TcrXyn10A, generated by removing xylose from the bound structure, using GROMACS (version 2024.4)43 with the AMBER99SB-ILDN force field44. The systems were solvated with the TIP3P water model45 in a triclinic simulation box with a minimum distance of 10 Å between the protein and the box edges. Counterions were added to neutralize the total charge of the systems. After energy minimization using the steepest descent algorithm until the maximum force was below 1000 kJ mol− 1 nm− 1, the systems were equilibrated under constant volume and temperature (NVT) conditions, followed by equilibration under constant pressure and temperature (NPT) conditions. Production MD simulations were subsequently performed for 200 ns at 328 K, corresponding to the optimal temperature for enzyme activity. Trajectory analyses were carried out using built-in GROMACS tools.

Results

Structure determination of xylose-bound TcrXyn10A

To understand the xylose-induced inhibitory effect on TcrXyn10A, the crystal structures of xylose-soaked TcrXyn10A (TcrXyn10AXYL) were determined. In the initial xylose soaking experiments, TcrXyn10A crystals were incubated in a solution containing 5 mM xylose for 1–10 min; however, no reliable electron density map for xylose was observed. Subsequently, crystals soaked in a solution containing 10 mM xylose for 30 min yielded the xylose-bound state of TcrXyn10A. In addition, in this study, the previously determined native structure of TcrXyn10A (TcrXyn10ANAT)26 was reprocessed and re-refined, which provided improved R-values (Table 1). Both TcrXyn10ANAT and TcrXyn10AXYL crystals belonged to the same orthorhombic space group P212121 with similar unit cell dimensions (Table 1), indicating that xylose soaking did not significantly affect protein packing. Two TcrXyn10A molecules were occupied in the asymmetric unit. The electron density map was clearly observed for residues Ala29–Arg327. The final models of TcrXyn10ANAT and TcrXyn10AXYL were refined to 2.1 and 2.5 Å resolution, with Rwork/Rfree values of 16.88%/21.95% and 21.35%/27.02%, respectively. TcrXyn10A exhibited the typical (β/α)8 TIM-barrel fold containing six subsites (− 3 to + 3) (Fig. 1A and B). Two conserved catalytic glutamate residues, Glu156 and Glu262, were located between subsites − 1 and + 1 on the substrate-binding cleft, acting as the general acid and nucleophile to mediate double-displacement hydrolysis. The substrate-binding cleft of TcrXyn10A was exposed to the solvent region in the crystal lattice (Supplementary Figure S1). Superimposition of the TcrXyn10ANAT and TcrXyn10AXYL structures showed high similarity, with a root mean square deviation (RMSD) of 0.180–0.241 Å. In the TcrXyn10AXYL, electron density map corresponding to the xylose molecule was clearly observed at subsite − 2 of the substrate-binding site in only one TcrXyn10A molecule (Fig. 1C), whereas no electron density map for the xylose molecule was observed in the other TcrXyn10A molecule in the asymmetric unit (Supplementary Figure S2). Superimposition of molecules A and B in the xylose-bound TcrXyn10A structure showed a high similarity, with an RMSD of 0.236 Å, indicating that the overall structures of the apo and xylose-bound forms of TcrXyn10A were similar. However, the xylose-binding site differed significantly between the apo and xylose-bound structures.

Xylose binding site in TcrXyn10A

In the TcrXyn10AXYL, the xylose molecule was located at partial negative charged surface of subsite − 2 in the substrate-binding cleft (Fig. 2A). The O1 and O5 atoms of xylose were oriented toward the substrate-binding cleft, whereas the O2–O4 atoms were oriented toward the solvent region (Fig. 2A). The xylose molecule was located approximately 5.12 Å away from the catalytic site between Glu156 and Glu262. The closest distances between the O1 atom of xylose and the side chains of Glu156 and Glu262 were 7.12 and 4.63 Å, respectively (Fig. 2A). The xylose molecule was surrounded by five amino acid residues (Asn72, Lys75, Gln115, Trp292, and Trp300) of TcrXyn10A and was stabilized through hydrogen-bond and π–π interactions (Fig. 2B). The O1 atom of xylose interacted with the NZ atom of Lys75, the NE1 atom of Trp292, and a water molecule (W1) at distances of 3.62, 3.36, and 2.77 Å, respectively (Fig. 2B). This xylose-interacting water (W1) molecule formed a water bridge with the OE1 atom of Glu262 and other water molecule (W2) at a distance of 3.49 Å and 2.97 Å, respectively. The W2 molecule was interacted with OE2 atom of Glu156 and Glu262 at distance of 3.59 and 3.06 A, respectively (Fig. 2B and C). The O2 and O4 atoms of xylose interacted with the NE2 atom of Gln115 and the ND2 atom of Asn72, respectively, at distances of 2.89 Å and 3.34 Å. The O5 atom of xylose interacted with the NE1 atom of Trp292 at distances of 2.98 Å. In addition, the xylose molecule interacted with Trp292 and Trp300 through edge-to-face and displaced π–π interactions, respectively, at distances of < 4.5 Å (Fig. 2B and C). The calculated binding affinity between TcrXyn10A and xylose was − 6.13 kcal/mol. Meanwhile, the B-factor of the xylose molecule was 48.85 Å2, which is approximately two times higher than that of the whole protein (25.43 Å2) in the xylose-bound TcrXyn10A structure, indicating that the xylose molecule was loosely bound to subsite − 2 of the substrate-binding cleft.

To identify whether the xylose-binding site of TcrXyn10A is conserved, the amino-acid sequences from homologous GH10 xylanases were analyzed. The results showed that the five xylose-binding residues in TcrXyn10A were conserved among other xylanase GH10 enzymes from Aspergillus cristatus (AcrXyn, sequence identity: 71.7%), Aspergillus chevalieri (AchXyn, 71.7%), Aspergillus ruber (AruXyn, 70.5%), Aspergillus saccharolyticus (AsaXyn, 71.1%), Penicillium daleae (PdaXyn, 72.3%), Penicillium cataractarum (PcaXyn, 72.6%), Penicillium canescens (PcaXylA, 69.7%), Aspergillus niger (AniXlnC, 73.3%), and Talaromyces purpureogenus (TpuXynA, 69.1%) (Fig. 2D). These xylose-binding residues are commonly located in highly conserved regions of the GH10 family, suggesting that other xylanase homologs may also interact with xylose at a similar subsite through conserved xylose-binding residues.

Xylose-binding induced conformational change of the substrate-binding cleft

To understand how xylose binding affects the structural change of substrate binding cleft, the crystal structures of the TcrXyn10AXYL was compared with xylose-unbound TcrXyn10A (TcrXyn10AApo) from native and xylose-soaked TcrXyn10A. Superimposition of the TcrXyn10AXYL and TcrXyn10AApo from the xylose-soaked structure exhibited high similarity, with an RMSD of 0.236 Å, indicating that xylose binding does not induce significant overall conformational changes in the protein. This superimposed structure showed rotation of the indole group of Trp300 in the TcrXyn10AXYL toward the xylose molecule by approximately 30° when compared with the TcrXyn10AApo (Fig. 3A). The indole group of Trp300 in the TcrXyn10AXYL shifted toward the xylose molecule by approximately 0.8 Å compared with the TcrXyn10AApo. Subtle movements of the side chains of Glu156, Glu262, Asn72, Lys75, and Trp292 between the TcrXyn10AXYL and TcrXyn10AApo structures were observed within 1 Å, whereas the side-chain conformation of Gln115 was almost identical (Fig. 3A).

Superimposition of the TcrXyn10AXYL with two TcrXyn10AApo from TcrXyn10ANAT structures showed high similarity, with RMSDs of 0.180 and 0.214 Å. These structures also revealed a significant conformational change in Trp300 (Fig. 3B). The indole group of Trp300 in the TcrXyn10AXYL was rotated toward the xylose molecule by 20° and 30°, and the position of the CH2 atom of the Trp300 indole group in the TcrXyn10AXYL was shifted by 0.50 and 1.05 Å compared with TcrXyn10AApo. Subtle movements of the side chains of the two catalytic residues, Glu156 and Glu262, as well as Asn72, Lys75, and Gln115, were observed within 1 Å, whereas the side-chain position of Trp292 was almost identical (Fig. 3B).

These structural analyses showed that xylose binding induces subtle rearrangements of the side chains of xylose-binding residues. In particular, the side-chain shift of Trp300 toward the xylose molecule leads to a narrower substrate-binding cleft at subsite − 1 compared with other TcrXyn10A molecules. The distance between the OE1 (or NE2) atom of Gln115 and the CH2 atom of Trp300 in TcrXyn10AXYL was 8.63 Å at subsite − 1, whereas the corresponding distances in the native and xylose-soaked TcrXyn10AApo structures ranged from 9.01 to 9.64 Å (Fig. 3C). Similarly, the distance between the CH2 atoms of Trp112 and Trp300 was 7.89 Å at subsite − 1 in the TcrXyn10AXYL, compared with 8.55–8.97 Å in the TcrXyn10AApo structures (Fig. 3C). Meanwhile, the distance between the ND2 atom of Asn72 and the CB atom of Trp300 in the TcrXyn10AXYL was 7.41 Å at subsite − 2, whereas in other TcrXyn10AApo molecules it ranged from 7.29 to 7.39 Å, indicating that xylose binding slightly increased the width of the substrate-binding cleft at subsite − 2 (Fig. 3C). The calculated substrate-binding cleft volumes for the xylose-binding region, comprising Asn72, Lys75, Gln115, Trp292, and Trp300, were 73.87 Å3 for the TcrXyn10AXYL structure and 79.92–89.42 Å3 for the TcrXyn10AApo structures. These results indicate that xylose binding induces conformational changes and positional shifts of Trp300, which affect the width of the substrate-binding cleft at subsites − 1 and − 2 of TcrXyn10A.

Structural comparison of TcrXyn10A with homolog xylanases

To identify whether the binding mode of xylose is similar to the substrate-binding mode, the xylose-binding mode of TcrXyn10A was compared with that of XynA from Thermoascus aurantiacus (TauXynA), which shows high sequence and structural similarity, with a Z-score of 52.9, an RMSD of 0.4 Å, and a sequence identity of 82%. Crystal structures of TauXynA in complex with xylobiose (XYL2: TauXynAXYL2, PDB ID: 1GOR and 1GOQ)46, and feruloyl-arabinose linked to O3 of the non-reducing xylose (FAX2; TauXynAFAX2, 2BNJ)47 were compared with that of TcrXyn10A.

The sequence alignment showed that the xylose-binding and catalytic residues of TcrXyn10A were conserved in TauXynA (Fig. 4A). The TauXynAXYL2 structures 1GOR and 1GOQ were soaked with xylobiose, and diffraction data were collected at 100 K (cryo) and 291 K (room temperature, RT), respectively (Fig. 4B). In both TauXynAXYL2, the two xylose units of the xylobiose molecule were located at subsites − 2 and − 1 of the substrate-binding cleft (Fig. 4C). Interestingly, in TauXynAXYL2 at RT, two single xylose unit from xylobiose was observed at subsites + 1 and − 4 (Fig. 4D), likely due to the longer incubation time (45 min) with xylobiose, indicating that incubation time can affect the binding occupancy of the xylose unit in xylobiose. In TauXynAFAX2, two xylose units of the feruloyl-arabinoxylobiose molecule, linked to the O3 atom of the non-reducing xylose, were bound at subsites − 2 and − 1 (Fig. 4E). Superimposition of these XOS-bound TauXynA structures showed that the binding modes of the two xylose units from xylobiose and FAX2 at subsites − 2 and − 1 were nearly identical (Supplementary Figure S4). Superimposition of TcrXyn10A with TauXynA showed that the positions and conformations of the side-chain residues involved in catalysis and xylose binding were slightly different due to differences in xylose molecule binding (Supplementary Figure S4). The position of the xylose molecule at subsite − 2 in TcrXyn10A was almost similar with xylose unit at subsite − 2 from XOS in TauXynA; however, the binding mode of the xylose molecule differed. In TcrXyn10A, the O1 and O5 atoms of xylose were directed toward the substrate-binding cleft, whereas in TauXynA, the O1, O2, and O3 atoms of the xylose unit in xylobiose were directed toward the substrate binding cleft, indicating that the xylose unit in xylobiose is rotated by approximately 180° around the O1 atom compared with the xylose in TcrXyn10A. These results indicate that the orientation of xylose bound at subsite − 2 varies depending on the number of xylose units. Meanwhile, in TauXynAXYL at RT, in addition to xylobiose bound at subsites − 2 and − 1, a single xylose unit from xylobiose was partially observed near subsites + 1 and − 4. In contrast, in TcrXyn10A, xylose was not observed at any subsites other than subsite − 2.

In TauXynA, the xylose unit of xylobiose at subsite − 2 interacts through hydrogen bonds with Asn47, Lys50, Gln90, and Trp267, as well as a π–π interaction with Trp267 and Trp275 (Table S1). The calculated binding affinities of the xylose molecule at subsite − 2 in TauXynXYL2 at Cryo, TauXynXYL2 at RT, and TauXynFAX2 were − 6.22, − 6.14, and − 6.14 kcal/mol, respectively, indicating comparable binding affinity to that calculated for TcrXyn10A. These results indicate that although the xylose binding modes at subsite − 2 differ between TcrXyn10A and TauXynA, their binding affinities are similar in the crystal structures.

Molecular dynamics simulation analysis

The crystal structure of xylose-bound TcrXyn10A showed that the xylose molecule stably interacted with residues located near subsite − 2 and with surrounding water molecules; however, xylose was observed in only one of the two TcrXyn10A molecules in the asymmetric unit. In addition, the xylose did not completely inhibit the enzymatic activity of TcrXyn10A26. These results indicated that the binding affinity of xylose for TcrXyn10A may not be high. To further evaluate the stability of xylose binding to TcrXyn10A (TcrXyn10Axylose), MD simulations were performed using the TcrXyn10AXYL structure and an apo-state TcrXyn10A model (TcrXyn10Aapo), which was generated by removing the bound xylose from the same structure to minimize initial model bias.

Analysis of backbone RMSD profiles revealed that TcrXyn10AXYL exhibited the higher RMSD value than TcrXyn10AApo after 120 ns (Fig. 5A). Average RMSD values of TcrXyn10AXYL and TcrXyn10Apo were approximately 0.223 and 0.157 nm, indicating that xylose binding induces additional conformational change. The average Rg and SASA of TcrXyn10AApo were approximately 1.88 and 120 nm2, which were slightly decreased when compared with TcrXyn10AXYL with Rg and SASA value of approximately 1.91 nm and 131 nm2, respectively (Fig. 5B and C), indicating that the xylose-bound complex has a more expanded global conformation and promotes enhanced surface exposure. RMSF profiles of the TcrXyn10AXYL and TcrXyn10AApo were highly similar, indicating that xylose binding does not broadly alter the intrinsic flexibility pattern of TcrXyn10A. However, a significant flexibility was observed in the Gly80-Gly84 in the TcrXyn10AXYL (Fig. 5D). These flexible regions are located on the side of the TIM-barrel domain toward the subsite − 3 direction and are separated from the xylose molecule and subsite − 3 by approximately 23 Å and 28 Å, respectively. Accordingly, xylose binding affects the local flexibility of the loop region within the TIM-barrel fold of TcrXyn10A.

Molecular dynamics simulations analysis of apo and xylose-bound TcrXyn10A. Plot of RMSD (A), Rg (B), SASA (C), and RMSF (D) for apo TcrXyn10A (black) and the xylose-bound TcrXyn10A complex (red). (E) Plot of the distance between xylose and the xylose-binding site. (F) Plot of the distance between the centers of mass of Asn72 and Trp300 involved in xylose binding.

To investigate the positional stability of xylose within the binding cleft of TcrXyn10A, the RMSD between xylose and xylose binding site was calculated from the MD simulation trajectory of the TcrXyn10AXYL. During the early phase of the simulation, the xylose RMSD remained relatively low, in the range of 0.25–0.28 nm, indicating stable binding of xylose to TcrXyn10A. However, after approximately 3.8 ns, the RMSD exhibited a sharp increase and significant fluctuations ranging from ~ 1 to 7 nm, indicating a loss of positional stability that preceded complete dissociation of xylose from the binding site. Consistently, analysis of the center-of-mass (COM) distance between Asn72 and Trp300, between which xylose binds, remained stable fluctuations in the range of 2.7–2.9 nm during the early phase of the simulation. However, the COM distance fluctuated significantly after approximately 3.8 ns, indicating that xylose dissociation induces increased conformational fluctuations in the subsite − 2 region. Taken together, MD simulations indicated that xylose binds weakly to TcrXyn10A, and its release enhances the molecular flexibility of TcrXyn10A.

Discussion

Xylanases are widely utilized in various industries including biomass degradation, biofuel production, pulp bleaching, and feed processing48,49. The activity of industrially valuable xylanases is often inhibited by reaction products generated during xylan hydrolysis, such as xylose and other lignocellulose-derived compounds20,50. Understanding the molecular mechanism underlying catalytic inhibition by biomass product is essential for improving enzyme performance and optimizing industrial applications.

In this study, the inhibitory mechanism of xylose against the potentially industrially relevant xylanase TcrXyn10A was investigated. The xylose-soaking experiment revealed that the xylose molecule binds at the substrate-binding cleft of TcrXyn10A, specifically occupying subsite − 2. This structural result indicates that xylose acts as a competitive inhibitor, competing directly with the xylan substrate for the same binding site. The occupation of subsite − 2 by xylose prevents proper positioning of the xylan substrate along the catalytic groove, thereby reducing turnover efficiency. These structural analyses suggest a possible competitive inhibition mechanism. However, allosteric effects of xylose cannot be completely ruled out based on the current data.

TcrXyn10A contains six subsites within the substrate-binding cleft, and thus, in principle, xylose molecules could bind to all subsites when considering a xylan substrate. However, in the present structure, the xylose was observed to bind only to subsite − 2. This observation indicates that subsite − 2 is the most preferred binding site for xylose compared with the other subsites, whereas occupancy of other subsites likely requires multiple xylose molecules rather than a single xylose. Similarly, in xylanase GH11, xylose was found to bind only to subsite − 2 among the six subsites51. Their study suggested that the xylose-binding site corresponds to the initial substrate recognition site during xylan binding51.

The xylose-binding residues in TcrXyn10A are highly conserved among GH10 xylanases, suggesting that similar inhibitory binding may occur in other members of the GH10 family. Structural comparison of xylose-bound TcrXyn10A with the XOS-bound TauXynA demonstrated that both xylose and the xylose units of xylobiose commonly occupy subsite − 2 and interact with conserved residues such as Glu71, Asn72, Lys75, Trp292, and Trp300 (numbered in TcrXyn10A). However, their binding orientations of the xylose molecule differ, indicating that the binding mode of the xylose unit at subsite − 2 depends on the number of xylose units in the ligand.

Interestingly, although two TcrXyn10A molecules are present in the asymmetric unit, the xylose binding was observed in only one TcrXyn10A molecule. Similar asymmetric binding has been reported in other crystal structures, in which ligands bind to only some of the multiple protein molecules within an asymmetric unit52–54. For example, in the crystal structure of the ligand-binding domain (LBD) of the chemoreceptor PctA (PDB code: 5LTV), seven PctA-LBD molecules are present in the asymmetric unit, but only two molecules contain GABA within the binding pocket of the membrane-distal module, likely due to crystal packing effects52. Consequently, GABA-induced conformational changes of the loop connecting β4 and β5 were observed in only two PctA-LBD molecules52. In the crystal structure of the TetR/AcrR-like regulator SCO3201 (PDB code: 7PT0), the protein forms a homodimer, and a potential polyamine inducer is bound to only one protomer, inducing conformational and positional changes in the α3 helix region53. These observations suggest that asymmetric substrate binding can be attributed to allosteric conformational changes induced by ligand binding. Xylose isomerase (XI) forms a homotetramer, and two metal ions bind to the active site of each protomer and are involved in the catalytic reaction54. In the crystal structure of XI from Streptomyces avermitilis (PDB code: 8YUD), the metal ion occupancy of the active sites in the four protomers within the asymmetric unit differs among molecules, exhibiting distinct metal ion coordination states due to variations in metal ion binding55. These results indicated that the ligand or cofactor binding behavior and occupancy can be variable in the proteins in sample crystallization condition due to crystal packing, allosteric effect or unknow reasons.

In MD simulation, the xylose molecule was not stably bound to TcrXyn10A. These results suggest that xylose exhibits a low binding affinity for TcrXyn10A and that the partial occupancy observed in the crystal structure likely reflects weak binding under crystallization conditions. These results are consistent with previous biochemical results showing that xylose-preincubated TcrXyn10A reduces enzyme activity but does not abolish it26.

Based on structural analysis results, a xylose inhibition mechanism for TcrXyn10A is proposed (Fig. 6). (i) The xylose molecule preferentially binds to subsite − 2 in the substrate-binding cleft. (ii) The xylose molecule is stabilized by hydrogen bonds and π–π interactions with conserved residues located near subsite − 2; however, this binding affinity is weak. (iii) This xylose binding potentially exerts a competitive inhibitory effect by occupying the substrate-binding site, but the xylose molecule can be easily released upon substrate access due to its low binding affinity. To clearly elucidate the xylose-induced inhibition mechanism, further in-depth biochemical studies, including kinetic analyses of xylose inhibition, will be necessary.

Proposed mechanism of xylose inhibition in TcrXyn10A. (A) Xylose preferentially binds to subsite − 2 within the substrate-binding cleft, where it is stabilized by hydrogen bonds and π–π interactions with conserved residues. (B) Occupancy of xylose in the substrate-binding cleft exerts a competitive inhibitory effect by interfering with proper substrate positioning. (C) Upon substrate access, xylose can be displaced from subsite − 2, allowing catalytic turnover to proceed.

Although the xylose binding affinity on TcrXyn10A inferred was considered low, this low binding affinity of xylose to subsite − 2 still affects the TcrXyn10A activity during the biomass degradation. In particular, where xylose accumulates to high concentrations during industrial biomass degradation, transient product binding can still reduce catalytic efficiency.

In principle, site-directed mutagenesis of residues involved in xylose binding could decrease xylose product binding affinity and thus alleviate inhibition. However, since these residues are conserved and directly participate in substrate recognition, such mutations may also reduce substrate affinity and catalytic activity. Consequently, engineering approaches to weaken xylose binding must be carefully balanced to preserve catalytic function. Alternatively, optimizing reaction conditions rather than modifying the enzyme itself may be a more practical approach. Adjusting factors such as pH, temperature, or the presence of co-solvents could mitigate xylose inhibition by altering the equilibrium between bound and unbound forms.

Conclusion

The crystal structure of TcrXyn10A revealed that the xylose molecule preferentially binds to subsite − 2 within the substrate-binding cleft of TcrXyn10A with low binding affinity, suggesting competitive inhibition by interfering with xylan substrate access. This xylose molecule is stabilized by hydrogen bonds and π–π interactions with conserved residues in the substrate-binding cleft; however, its binding affinity is weak due to a binding mode different from that of the substrate. These results explain how xylose reduces the enzymatic activity of TcrXyn10A but does not abolish it. These findings provide a structural basis for understanding xylose-induced inhibition in TcrXyn10A and offer insights into the inhibitory mechanism in other GH10 xylanase family members.
