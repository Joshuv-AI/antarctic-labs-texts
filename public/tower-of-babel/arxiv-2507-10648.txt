Abstract
We present 1 1 , 800 800 multiwavelength Type Ib/c supernovae light curve models obtained by running the radiation transport code Sedona and varying the mass distribution, velocity profile, and abundance ejecta profiles of helium star progenitors. To create a flexible but physically-informed grid, we use autoencoders to construct a representation of ejecta profiles derived from stellar evolution models. We present simulated nearest-neighbor multiband light curves matches to SN 1994I, SN 2007gr, and iPTF13bvn to demonstrate that realistic light curves can be generated in our grid. We show that the ejecta velocity distribution, in particular, strongly influences the light curve, while variation in Ni 56 \rm{}^{56}Ni mixing alone has a limited impact on the bolometric light curve, even in extreme and unphysical mixing schemes; however, mixing can modestly impact color evolution. Finally, we show that the Ni 56 \rm{}^{56}Ni mass, ejecta mass, and both the magnitude and structure of ejecta velocity distribution can be inferred from the multiband light curves, enabling improved inference over widely used semi-analytical models.



I
Introduction

Stripped-envelope supernovae (SESNe) arise from massive stars whose outer envelope has been at least partially stripped prior to explosion (see
Smartt 2009
for a review). Hydrogen-free SESNe can be classified observationally as SNe Ib (with spectroscopic helium signatures) or as SNe Ic (without helium signatures), and are primarily powered by the decay of
\rm{}^{56}Ni
. Some single-star progenitor systems have been proposed for SESNe
(
Wheeler & Levreault 1985
;
Gaskell et al. 1986
;
Begelman & Sarazin 1986
;
Woosley et al. 1993
)
, but interacting binary progenitors are likely more prevalent and persuasive
(
Nomoto et al. 1995
;
Podsiadlowski et al. 1992
;
Woosley et al. 1995
;
Kobulnicky & Fryer 2007
)

SESNe, observationally, span a relatively large range of explosion energy, peak brightness, and light curve shape, including subclasses such as broad-lined SNe Ic (SNe Ic-BL;
Iwamoto et al. 1998
;
Maeda et al. 2002
;
Modjaz et al. 2016
), SNe associated with long gamma-ray bursts (GRB-SNe;
Dessart et al. 2012
;
Cano 2013
;
Modjaz et al. 2016
), superluminous SNe (SLSNe;
Dessart et al. 2012
;
Wang et al. 2015
;
Nicholl et al. 2017
;
Nicholl 2021
), narrow-lined SNe Ibn and SNe Icn
(
Maeda & Moriya 2022
;
Pellegrino et al. 2022
;
Davis et al. 2023
)
, ultrastripped SNe
(
Tauris et al. 2015
;
De et al. 2018
;
Sawada et al. 2022
)
, and luminous SNe
(
Gomez et al. 2022
)
. Crucially, how much of the SESN light curve diversity can be explained by
\rm{}^{56}Ni
radioactive decay (as opposed to other engines) is not currently known.

A frequently used technique for modeling the light curve of SNe Ibc is the semianalytical “Arnett Model”
(
Arnett 1982
)
which approximates the full radiative diffusion process through a one-zone, gray opacity model. This model explicitly assumes that the SN ejecta is in thermal equilibrium with energy inputted by
\rm{}^{56}Ni
decay or, equivalently, that the spatial dependence of the energy profile is fixed, and only the normalization changes with time (i.e., “self-similarity”; see assumption four and Equation 13 of
Arnett 1982
).

Because
\rm{}^{56}Ni
mixing is caused by explosion asymmetry and by Rayleigh-Taylor instabilities
(
Dessart et al. 2012
;
Dessart et al. 2015
;
Dessart et al. 2016
;
Woosley 2019
;
Ertl et al. 2020
;
Woosley et al. 2021
)
, there is no reason to expect the
\rm{}^{56}Ni
to be distributed such that the ejecta is continuously in thermal equilibrium (see
Khatami & Kasen 2019
for the drawbacks of the self-similarity assumption). This simplifying assumption causes the Arnett model to generally overestimate
m_{\rm Ni}
for SESNe
(
Dessart et al. 2015
;
Dessart et al. 2016
;
Khatami & Kasen 2019
)
.

One-dimensional Monte Carlo radiative transfer (MCRT) codes offer better accuracy than semianalytical models while remaining computationally feasible for simulating populations of SN light curves. MCRT codes track photon packets through ejecta described with a layer-by-layer prescription of velocity, density, and chemical abundances using up to
10^{6}
atomic line transitions. As such, these codes provide more flexibility in describing ejecta profiles and allow for studies of influences of
\rm{}^{56}Ni
mixing in the ejecta.

How the distribution of
\rm{}^{56}Ni
in the ejecta influences the light curve has been studied extensively in literature using MCRT methods
(
Dessart et al. 2012
;
Dessart et al. 2015
;
Dessart et al. 2016
;
Khatami & Kasen 2019
;
Woosley 2019
;
Ertl et al. 2020
;
Moriya et al. 2020
;
Woosley et al. 2021
)
. In particular, models with
\rm{}^{56}Ni
mixed farther into shallower ejecta layers are bluer prior to peak, have a more gradual rise, have an earlier peak, have a higher photospheric velocity during peak, and more rapidly transition to the nebular phase
(
Dessart et al. 2012
;
Moriya et al. 2020
;
Woosley et al. 2021
)
. However, to date many MCRT studies have been limited to relatively small (
\lesssim 100
) model grids for a set of derived parameters. In coming years, as surveys like the Vera C. Rubin Observatory’s Legacy Survey of Space and Time reveal large samples of SESN light curves, broader grids are essential to constrain two important uncertainties: 1) the progenitor system(s) of SESNe and 2) the power source(s) of SESNe. Furthermore, such grids need to be embedded within inference routines to statistically infer SESN properties.

Here, we use
SEDONA
to systematically explore the physical parameters of a larger set of SESNe. Our models build on the works of
Woosley 2019
;
Ertl et al. 2020
;
Woosley et al. 2021
, who calculated a grid of 81 SESN light curves resulting from helium stars that were evolved from helium ignition to core collapse in
kepler
using the progenitor-agnostic mass-loss prescription from
Yoon 2017
.
Woosley et al. 2021
(hereafter, W21) evolved the stellar models that successfully exploded using the MCRT code
SEDONA
and presented relationships between generated light curves and the physical parameters of SESN ejecta.

In this work, we generate ejecta profiles derived from the W21 grid, and examine how each SN parameter independently influences the observed light curve. We generate models by sampling from the distributions of physically relevant dimensions of the W21 models (e.g.
\rm{}^{56}Ni
mass, ejecta mass, and velocity gradient). We also motivate the next paper in this series, which will present an inference framework for SESN ejecta physical parameters using the results from MCRT. Because this work is observationally motivated, we present parameters and measurements that are easily translatable to observed light curves, and we align all light curves so time zero is set to the time of
r
-filter peak.

In this work, we explore the space of SESN ejecta profiles by parameterizing and interpolating between the W21 model set. Following W21, we simulate all SESNe in the MCRT code
SEDONA

(
Kasen et al. 2006
)
.
SEDONA
calculates the time-varying spectral energy distribution (SED) of expanding SN ejecta.
SEDONA
constructs an SN ejecta profile from a layer-by-layer prescription of velocity, density, temperature, and chemical abundance.
SEDONA
then evolves this system assuming homologous expansion (i.e. ejecta velocity is proportional to layer radius). The temperature structure is iteratively solved assuming thermal and radiative equilibrium from the given initial temperature profile estimate.

All models presented here are simulated in one dimension with a standard, limited set of chemical elements:

\rm{}^{1}H,^{4}He,^{12}C,^{14}N,^{16}O,^{20}Ne,^{24}Mg,^{28}Si,^{32}S,^{36}Ar,^{40}Ca

\rm{}^{1}H

Rodrigo et al. 2024

II.2
Parameterizing Ejecta Profiles

Here we describe our parameterization of the distributions of SN ejecta mass, velocity, and abundances for input into
SEDONA
. To meaningfully explore the range of these distributions, the dimensionality of each needs to be reduced to only a few physically relevant parameters. Importantly, the light curve produced by a lower-dimensionality encoding of an ejecta profile should be equivalent to the light curve resulting from the original ejecta profile parameterization.

II.2.1
Elements Tracked

We first explore which of the
17
chemical element distributions need to be independently parameterized, i.e., identify which elements have nearly identical impacts on the SN light curves. Elements can influence the light curve in two ways: 1) providing input energy via radioactive decay and 2) increasing the opacity of the ejecta. The radioactive decay of
\rm{}^{56}Ni
is the only power source considered here. All chemical elements contribute to opacity through the three sources of opacity we consider in this paper: 1) free-free interactions, 2) Thomson scattering, and 3) line-expansion opacity. Free-free interactions and Thomson scattering opacities are particularly enhanced at early times, when the ionization fraction tends to be higher. We isolate the individual contribution of each element’s distribution to the light curve by running multiple simulations where the mass distribution of each element is exchanged with another element (without changing the total ejecta mass) for several models in the W21 grid. We find all elements other than
\rm{}^{56}Ni
have a largely identical influence on the light curve. In the end, we independently parameterize the mass distributions of
\rm{}^{4}He
and
\rm{}^{56}Ni
, and we combine the mass distributions of all other elements into one: the “bulk” ejecta. We generate the bulk mass distribution by adding the average mass of each element that is not
\rm{}^{4}He
or
\rm{}^{56}Ni
in each layer throughout the W21 grid. Though
\rm{}^{4}He
does not influence the resulting light curve, we independently parameterize it as a point of comparison for a future study in which
\rm{}^{4}He
interactions are accurately modeled in an NLTE implementation of
SEDONA
.

We confirm that parameterizing only the
\rm{}^{4}He
mass distribution,
\rm{}^{56}Ni
mass distribution, and bulk mass distribution reproduces the light curves of models in the W21 grid (where the mass distributions of
all
elements are parameterized). We find an absolute fractional difference of
\sim 1\%
in the bolometric light curve between original model and the bulk-averaged model. We also find that the mean fractional difference in spectra from the original model and bulk-averaged model is
\sim 5\%
over the relevant wavelength range. However, we emphasize that the goal is to reproduce light curves, rather than the detailed features in the spectrum; therefore, we do not inspect individual spectroscopic features. Finally, each mass and velocity distribution in this study is re-interpolated to have 256 layers, so that every model is parameterized with 256 cells in
SEDONA
.

II.2.2
Mass and Velocity Distributions

We next find a method to parameterize the shape of each mass and velocity distribution to further reduce the dimensionality of the ejecta space. We find simple parametric models (e.g. Chebyshev polynomial expansion or similar) cannot reproduce the W21 model distributions without requiring an unacceptably large number of parameters.

Instead, we encode mass and velocity distributions with a small set of parameters using autoencoders (AEs), a class of neural networks. A neural network is a machine learning algorithm that approximates a arbitrary function by using layers of linear combinations followed by simple, nonlinear activation functions. An AE is a kind of neural network that learns a compressed representation of the input set
(
Kramer 1991
)
, where the space of compressed representation is known as the “latent space”. An AE has an “encoder” portion where the input set is mapped into the latent space and a “decoder” portion where the latent representation is mapped to the original data space.

We use four separate AEs to learn latent-space representations of the
\rm{}^{4}He
,
\rm{}^{56}Ni
, bulk, and velocity distributions. All four AEs are constructed to have a latent dimensionality of one (i.e. the latent representation of each distribution is a scalar). The latent variable for all four distributions is constrained to the range
[0,1]
. Each AE has fully connected layers, with sigmoid activation functions throughout, and are trained for
20
,
000

To characterize the precision of models generated by our AEs, we calculate the difference between light curves generated from encoded/decoded models and light curves from the W21 grid. We pass the velocity and mass profiles from models in the W21 grid through the corresponding AEs and then simulate light curves from the AE-reconstructed mass and velocity profiles. We then calculate the average of the fractional difference of the bolometric light curve from the decoded ejecta profile and the light curve from the W21 grid at all times. This fractional difference is less than
1\%
for every model in the W21 grid, implying that the AE method of encoding ejecta profiles is sufficiently accurate for our purposes.

The top three panels of Figures
2
and
3
show how variations in the ejecta velocity profile influences the light curve. Increasing the difference between outer velocity and inner velocity (
\Delta
vel; top left panel), increasing the inner velocity (top center panel), and increasing
vel_{50}
(top right panel) while holding everything else constant all result in a brighter peak luminosity and a shorter
t_{rise}
. Higher velocities cause the light curve to evolve (and therefore redden/cool) more quickly with a shorter overall diffusion timescale, following intuition from the Arnett model. Of the three velocity parameters, the outer velocity and
vel_{50}
have the strongest influence on the light curve over our explored parameter ranges, with the inner velocity having a more modest effect.

However, each velocity parameter influences the timing (and therefore color) of the light curve differently. Changes to
\Delta
vel do not change
t_{rise}
but decrease
t_{decline}
. Consequently, increasing the outer velocity results in redder colors at later times, while leaving early colors largely unaffected (top left panel of Figure
3
). Increasing the inner velocity results in both a shorter
t_{rise}
and shorter
t_{decline}
, although the impact on both the bolometric light curve and colors is minimal. Increasing
vel_{50}
has an intermediary effect, where both
t_{rise}
and
t_{decline}
are decreased, but
t_{decline}
is more strongly influenced than
t_{rise}
.

Because the physical size of the ejecta layers is set by the velocity profile, changing the velocity profile can be compared to mixing
all
of the elements simultaneously, rather than just the
\rm{}^{4}He
, bulk, or
\rm{}^{56}Ni
distribution. Therefore, the influence of changing the velocity profile can be compared to the “boxcar mixing method” of
Dessart et al. 2012
;
Dessart et al. 2015
;
Teffs et al. 2020
;
Woosley et al. 2021
, in which the ejecta elemental abundance profiles are convolved with a boxcar function to emulate mixing. There is a key difference between mixing by changing the velocity profile and boxcar mixing: our method of changing the velocity profile necessarily changes the density profile, while conserving the elemental relative abundance profiles; whereas boxcar mixing conserves both the density profile (see e.g.
Dessart et al. 2012
;
Teffs et al. 2020
) and the velocity profile, while changing the relative abundance profiles. However, this effect is captured in the bottom three panels of Figures
2
and
3
, and are explored later in this Section.

Nevertheless, analogies between the two methods of mixing can be drawn. When
\Delta
vel is increased, the inner velocity is unchanged and only the outer velocity is increased, causing the elements in the ejecta to be spread (i.e.
mixed
) over a larger range of velocities. With little mixing (corresponding to low
\Delta
vel),
t_{decline}
is much larger than
t_{rise}
. As the ejecta becomes more mixed (corresponding to increasing
\Delta
vel),
t_{decline}
decreases, creating a more symmetric light curve about the peak. As such, the influence of increasing
\Delta
vel on the light curve can be understood as the result of two effects: 1) the overall velocity scaling of the ejecta is increased, causing the light curve to be brighter at peak and to evolve more quickly, and 2) the ejecta is more mixed, creating a more symmetric light curve about its peak. This trend of increasing light curve symmetry via mixing is also found by
Dessart et al. 2012
;
Moriya et al. 2020
;
Teffs et al. 2020
. Changes to the inner velocity do not alter the extent of ejecta mixing, since the overall velocity range and distribution remain fixed. Therefore, the influence of the inner velocity on the light curve reflects only the effect of rescaling the overall ejecta velocity. Although changing
vel_{50}
does not impact the inner or outer velocity, it does alter the distribution of velocities in the ejecta. Increasing
vel_{50}
shifts more of the ejecta to higher velocities, analogous to enhanced “mixing” of the ejecta. In this sense, the influence of
vel_{50}
on ejecta mixing is similar to that of the
\Delta
vel.

The middle panels of Figures
2
and
3
show how the total masses of
\rm{}^{4}He
, the bulk, and
\rm{}^{56}Ni
influence the light curve. As detailed in Section
II
, within the range of
\rm{}^{4}He
masses in the W21 grid, variations in
\rm{}^{4}He
mass (middle left panel) do not affect the light curve appreciably. Increasing the bulk mass (middle center panel) results in a dimmer peak and a longer-lived light curve, consistent with expectations from the Arnett model. Both
t_{rise}
and
t_{decline}
increase with larger bulk mass. In this case, photons from
\rm{}^{56}Ni
decay must diffuse through more mass, leading to delayed and cooler/redder emission at and beyond peak. Increasing the mass of
\rm{}^{56}Ni
(middle right panel) increases the luminosity of the light curve at all phases, as expected. Similar to bulk mass, increasing the
\rm{}^{56}Ni
mass also results in a longer-lived transient due to an increased total ejecta mass.

W21 find comparable relationships to the ones we present in Figures
2
and
3
. In contrast with our work, W21 map observed light curve parameters to the physical parameters of the
star
(e.g. pre-SN mass) prior to explosion, whereas we focus on the ejecta (e.g.
\rm{}^{56}Ni
mass and bulk mass). Nevertheless, comparisons can be made if the sum of
m_{\rm He}
,
m_{\rm bulk}
, and
m_{\rm Ni}
is taken to be a proxy for pre-SN mass. W21 find that both
t_{rise}
and
t_{decline}
both increase with larger pre-SN mass, consistent with our results. However, W21 find that peak luminosity increases with pre-SN mass at low pre-SN masses and decreases with pre-SN mass at larger pre-SN masses. This is because both
\rm{}^{56}Ni
mass and ejecta mass increase with increasing pre-SN mass, analogous to the trends shown in the middle row of Figure
2
. W21 also find that models with higher pre-SN mass progenitors tend to be redder throughout their evolution, in agreement with our findings.

The bottom panels of Figures
2
and
3
illustrate how mixing of
\rm{}^{4}He
(left), the bulk (center), and
\rm{}^{56}Ni
(right) impacts the light curves. As before,
\rm{}^{4}He
has a minimal influence on the light curve. In contrast, a model in which the bulk is mixed farther out will exhibit brighter and later peaks. These models are significantly redder before light curve peak, colors that gradually converge over time. As
\rm{}^{56}Ni
is mixed farther out,
t_{rise}
is slightly decreased, while
t_{decline}
and peak luminosities are largely unchanged. Compared to less-mixed models, strong
\rm{}^{56}Ni
mixing results in somewhat bluer colors before peak and redder colors after peak. Overall, mixing of
\rm{}^{56}Ni
has only modest effects on the light curve over the range of
\rm{}^{56}Ni
mass profiles explored in the W21 grid.

That the bolometric light curve is largely insensitive to
\rm{}^{56}Ni
mixing is well established and supported by several radiative transfer studies of SESNe
(
Dessart et al. 2012
;
Dessart et al. 2015
;
Moriya et al. 2020
;
Woosley et al. 2021
)
.
Dessart et al. 2012
model mixing by a convolving the mass profile with a boxcar, coupling the mixing of both the bulk mass and
\rm{}^{56}Ni
mass. They find that more highly mixed models tend to have a longer
t_{rise}
, but the peak luminosity and
t_{decline}
are unchanged. Our analysis is consistent with this behavior (see bottom center and bottom right panels of Figure
2
): when both the bulk and
\rm{}^{56}Ni
are mixed farther out,
t_{rise}
increases and the light curve is otherwise unchanged.
Moriya et al. 2020
, in constrast, simulate models with varying
\rm{}^{56}Ni
mixing without mixing the bulk. They also find that the peak luminosity is largely unchanged (see e.g. their Figure 3).
Moriya et al. 2020
also show that although the light curves of more mixed models peak earlier, differences in the bolometric light curve are minimal when aligned by peak time. Like us (see bottom right panel of Figure
3
), they find that the most mixed models are typically bluer before peak and become redder than the the less mixed models after peak.

Taken altogether, we show that the observed light curve properties
t_{rise}
,
t_{decline}
, peak luminosity, and color evolution are influenced by the nine physical parameters explored here in complex and often degenerate ways. Nevertheless, we show in Section
V
that it is possible to constrain many of these physical parameters using simple regression on the observed light curve features.

V
Demonstrating Simple Physical Inference from the Grid

In this Section, we explore which physical parameters can be constrained from the observed parameters of the light curve. This analysis motivates the second paper in this series, where we will present a heavily expanded inference methodology to be tested on a large sample of SESNe.

-enriched layers which also more directly influence the bluer portion of the SED. Therefore, the
t_{rise}
of bluer filters are more strongly influenced by changes to the inner velocity profile.

V.2
New Fitting Functions for Peak Bolometric Luminosity and
\rm{}^{56}Ni
Mass

Next, we construct an equation using least-squares regression to predict the peak bolometric luminosity from the physical parameters in Equation
1
.

L_{\rm peak,bol}=10^{40.1}\left(\frac{m_{\rm Ni^{56}}}{\rm M_{\odot}}\right)^{0.81}\times\left(\frac{v_{25}}{\rm 3,500~km~s^{-1}}\right)^{0.29}\times\\
\left(\frac{v_{50}}{\rm 5,000~km~s^{-1}}\right)^{0.04}\times\left(\frac{v_{75}}{\rm 10,000~km~s^{-1}}\right)^{0.12}\rm erg/s

(1)

The bolometric peak luminosity scales as
\propto m_{\rm Ni^{56}}^{0.8}
, which is comparable to the Arnett rule, where this exponent is equal to
1
. We find this equation reproduces the bolometric peak luminosity with
R^{2}=0.92
.

We also construct equations to infer
m_{\rm Ni}
from the bolometric light curve properties:

m_{\rm Ni}=0.04\left(\frac{L_{\rm peak,~bol}}{\rm 10^{42}~erg~s^{-1}}\right)^{1.04}\times\left(\frac{t_{\rm rise,bol}}{\rm 15~d}\right)^{0.30}\\
\times\left(\frac{t_{\rm decline,bol}}{20~\rm d}\right)^{0.41}~\rm{M_{\odot}}

(2)

Using Equation
2
, we find a mean fractional difference between predicted and true
m_{\rm Ni}
of
\sim 6\%
(and
R^{2}=0.96
). In contrast, the Arnett Rule has a larger mean fractional difference of
\sim 25\%
. As a proof of concept, we apply this equation to the bolometric light curve properties of SN1994I, SN2007gr and iPTF13bvn, finding
m_{\rm Ni}
\sim 0.4-0.6
for the three events, at the lower end of our matched-model inference technique.

In this Section, we have shown that it is possible to constrain many physical parameters using simple, observable features from the
SEDONA
models. These inferences can potentially be improved if 1) a more detailed inference algorithm were used, 2) more attention were given to interpolating between models in the nine-dimensional physical space, and 3) inference were done on the entire observed light curve or even the entire observed SED. All three of these improvements will be implemented in the next study presented by the authors of this work.

VI
Expanding Range of
\rm{}^{56}Ni
Mass Distributions

In this Section, we probe how varying the
\rm{}^{56}Ni
mass distribution beyond the range of
\rm{}^{56}Ni
mass distributions from the W21 grid impacts the light curves. Prior work has suggested that
\rm{}^{56}Ni
mixing can drive the observed diversity in SESNe, and furthermore that
\rm{}^{56}Ni
mixing can change the shape and color of the resulting light curve
(
Khatami & Kasen 2019
;
Afsariardchi et al. 2021
;
Rodríguez et al. 2024
)
. To test this, we explore whether changing the distribution of
\rm{}^{56}Ni
alone can significantly alter an SESN light curve. Because the range of
\rm{}^{56}Ni
mass profiles presented in W21 is constrained to a tight range of
\rm{}^{56}Ni
mass distributions (where
r_{\rm 95,Ni}
only varies by
\sim
0.3), we introduce a larger range of generated
\rm{}^{56}Ni
mass profiles and present their light curves. We randomly choose 200 models from the original grid of
1
,
000
samples, and for each model simulate four additional
\rm{}^{56}Ni
distributions (thereby generating a second grid of 800 samples). These new distributions are generated using zero-centered Gaussian distributions of varying widths in velocity space. This setup allows us to isolate and better understand the qualitative impact of
\rm{}^{56}Ni
mixing on the resulting light curves.

In exploring this grid, we find two dominant light curve behaviors resulting from mixing the
\rm{}^{56}Ni
farther out into shallower layers of the ejecta. First, in most models where
\rm{}^{56}Ni
is confined to within the bulk of the ejecta, we observe that increased mixing leads to a faster rise and decline, but no substantial change in the peak luminosity. Second, in a smaller subset of models in which
\rm{}^{56}Ni
is mixed far enough out such that a significant fraction is ahead of the bulk mass, we observe a more substantial change in luminosity with little change to the time of the peak. Our results suggest that
\rm{}^{56}Ni
mixing, has minimal impact on the bolometric luminosity of the light curve unless it is mixed well into the outer layers, consistent with the findings of e.g.,
Dessart et al. 2012
;
Dessart et al. 2015
;
Moriya et al. 2020
;
Woosley et al. 2021
.

In Figure
10
, we present two representative models from our extended grid demonstrating each of these scenarios. The top row highlights a model in which the
r_{\rm 95,Ni}
is larger than
r_{\rm 95,bulk}
for all of the
\rm{}^{56}Ni
distributions generated. In this model, as
r_{\rm 95,Ni}
is increased, the peak luminosity increases by almost 55%, while the peak time is largely unchanged. The row of Figure
10
show a model where
r_{\rm 95,Ni}
is smaller than
r_{\rm 95,bulk}
for all but the most extreme mixing. In this model, mixing
\rm{}^{56}Ni
into outer regions of the ejecta produces little change in the peak luminosity, but it does reduce the time from explosion to peak.

Highly mixed ejecta profiles, where much of the
\rm{}^{56}Ni
is beyond the bulk mass, are not usually produced in studies where ejecta profiles are constructed from grids of evolved stars, such as in
Dessart et al. 2012
;
Dessart et al. 2015
;
Dessart et al. 2016
;
Woosley 2019
;
Ertl et al. 2020
;
Woosley et al. 2021
. In those works, mixing within the ejecta profile is emulated by convolving the ejecta profile with a boxcar function, where all of the elements are mixed simultaneously. Because of this, the
\rm{}^{56}Ni
, which is concentrated in the core of the pre-mixed ejecta, is unlikely to be mixed to be beyond the rest of the ejecta material. However, ejecta profiles with such highly mixed
\rm{}^{56}Ni
can be generated if mixed
\rm{}^{56}Ni
distributions are analytically generated from an equation (e.g. a Gaussian distribution), as in
Khatami & Kasen 2019
and in our extended grid.

In
Khatami & Kasen 2019
, different
\rm{}^{56}Ni
distributions are generated by mixing
\rm{}^{56}Ni
outwards to an arbitrary radius.
Khatami & Kasen 2019
explore a broad range of
\rm{}^{56}Ni
mixing from strongly centrally located
\rm{}^{56}Ni
to
\rm{}^{56}Ni
that has been mixed all the way to the outer ejecta profile–well beyond typical SESN models. Just as in the grid we present in this section, the
\rm{}^{56}Ni
distribution is allowed to vary independently of the other elements in the ejecta profile. Because of this,
Khatami & Kasen 2019
is able to get ejecta profiles where
\rm{}^{56}Ni
can be beyond much of the other elements in the ejecta. As such,
Khatami & Kasen 2019
find a strong dependence of the peak bolometric luminosity on the extent of
\rm{}^{56}Ni
mixing, as in the top row of Figure
10
. Here, we have shown that this strong dependency is not found if
\rm{}^{56}Ni
is never mixed to be substantially beyond the other ejecta elements.

Figure 10:
Two SESN ejecta models in which (top left) the bulk distribution is sufficiently narrow such that
\rm{}^{56}Ni
can be mixed out beyond the bulk distribution vs (bottom left) a bulk distribution sufficiently wide that even extreme mixing
\rm{}^{56}Ni

\rm{}^{56}Ni

VII
Conclusions

In this paper we have explored how the physical ejecta properties of hydrogen-free SNe, powered only by the radioactive decay of
\rm{}^{56}Ni
, impact their resulting light curves. We use
SEDONA
, a radiative transfer code, to evolve one-dimensional SN ejecta to generate time-dependent spectral energy distributions and multiband light curves. Our study builds on the set of 81 SESN models from W21 that resulted from evolving helium stars from the zero-age main sequence until core collapse. From the W21 SESN ejecta profiles, we construct a grid of
1
,
000
ejecta profiles. We explicitly break the correlations that physical parameters of ejecta to isolate how each physical parameter independently influences the light curve. We list our key findings and contributions here:

•

We parameterize the velocity and mass distributions of
\rm{}^{4}He
,
\rm{}^{56}Ni
, and remaining elements using 256 discrete layers. We find specifying only these four distributions is sufficient to reproduce SESN ejecta profiles and multiband light curves presented originally in W21. To reduce dimensionality of our problem, we use autoencoders (a class of neural networks) to compress these distributions into a nine-dimensional parameter space consisting of the inner velocity, outer velocity,
m_{\rm He}
,
m_{\rm Ni}
,
m_{\rm bulk}
, and four latent variables that describe the shapes of the velocity,
\rm{}^{4}He
,
\rm{}^{56}Ni
, and bulk mass distributions. Light curves generated using this reduced representation match the those from the full parameterization (which uses
\sim 10^{4}
numbers) to within
\sim 1
% accuracy.

•

We systematically explore how each physical parameter influences the resulting SESN light curves by breaking physical correlations in the W21 grid. This allows us to isolate how ejecta mass,
m_{\rm Ni}
, and the velocity structure shape the bolometric and multiband light curve evolution. Broadly, we recover trends predicted by Arnett-like models. However, we also find that rise and decline times in redder filters correlate most strongly with outer ejecta velocities, while bluer filters are more sensitive to inner layers. We find that many parameters exhibit degenerate effects and interact in complex ways, highlighting the need for robust inference frameworks with radiative transfer models.

•

We generate a grid of
1
,
000
light curves by uniformly sampling the nine physical parameters. Within this grid, we identify light curves that closely match multiband observations of SN1994I, SN2007gr, and iPTF13bvn, suggesting that the grid is able to generate realistic light curves. The corresponding models also have physical parameters consistent with those inferred by detailed studies of these SESNe. While some SESNe certainly require additional power sources, our results demonstrate that a broader range of SESN diversity than previously appreciated may be explainable through 56Ni-powered explosions.

•

To further investigate the role of mixing, we present an additional grid of
800
simulations in which the
\rm{}^{56}Ni
distribution is varied while holding all other ejecta properties fixed. We find that
\rm{}^{56}Ni
mixing has minimal impact on the peak luminosity if the
\rm{}^{56}Ni
remains largely interior to the bulk of the ejecta mass. However, when
\rm{}^{56}Ni
is mixed beyond the bulk mass, it can significantly boost peak luminosity. Multiband lightcurves are more sensitive to modest
\rm{}^{56}Ni
mixing than the bolometric light curve. Our findings underscore that it is the
relative
distribution of
\rm{}^{56}Ni
with respect to the total mass (not mixing alone) that drives changes to the light curve. Our results further highlight the importance of flexible, independent control over individual element distribution when interpreting SN light curves.

•

We demonstrate that simple properties of SESN light curves, such as peak luminosity and rise/decline timescales, can be used to infer key physical parameters of the ejecta. Using our large grid of models, we perform simple regression of the ejecta properties from the light curve. We find these properties can be used to constrain
m_{\rm Ni}
,
m_{\rm bulk}
, extent to which
\rm{}^{56}Ni
is mixed, and the velocity profile of SESN ejecta profiles.

We have shown that detailed physical inference from multiband SESN light curves is possible, even when using simple observable parameters. With the rapidly increasing discovery rate of Type Ibc SNe thanks to surveys like the Vera C Rubin Observatory’s Legacy Survey of Space and Time, fast methodologies for extracting physical inference from broadband light curves are essential. The grid of radiative transfer-based models presented here offers a foundation for such efforts. In the next paper of this series, we will present a more sophisticated inference framework that utilizes the complete multiband light curves. By applying it to a statistical sample of SESNe, we aim to constrain the distribution of
\rm{}^{56}Ni
masses across the SESN population, explore systematic differences between Type Ib and Ic explosions, characterize the diversity of their ejecta profiles, and assess how often additional power sources are necessary to explain SESN light curves.
