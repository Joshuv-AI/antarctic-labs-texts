Longitudinal Changes of Visual Sensitivity With Geographic Atrophy Progression Assessed by Defect-Mapping Microperimetry
Authors: Abera Saeed, Robyn H. Guymer, Xavier Hadoux, Maxime Jannaud, Darvy Dang, Lauren A. B. Hodgson, Emily K. Glover, Erin E. Gee, Peter van Wijngaarden, Zhichao Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo determine the relationship between structural and functional changes over time in the progression of geographic atrophy (GA) as assessed by defect-mapping microperimetry, an approach optimized to characterize the spatial extent of deep visual sensitivity losses.MethodsA total of 57 eyes from 50 participants underwent defect-mapping microperimetry testing of the central 8° radius (with a 10-dB stimuli presented once each at 208 locations) over a median of five visits, scheduled at 3-monthly intervals. GA lesion(s) on fundus autofluorescence in the corresponding region tested on microperimetry at each visit were manually annotated.ResultsAt a global level, change of GA extent in the central 8° radius explained a large proportion of the variance in the change in the proportion of locations missed (nonresponse) on defect-mapping microperimetry (R2 = 0.52). Locally, test locations that were entirely outside or within GA lesion(s) had a 0.3% (P = 0.305) and 2.3% (P < 0.001) probability of worsening between the two visits, respectively. In contrast, test locations with a 0% to 25%, 25% to 50%, 50% to 75%, or 75% to 100% change in the extent of GA overlapping between visits had a 4.1%, 12.0%, 17.5%, and 37.9% probability of worsening, respectively (all P < 0.001).ConclusionsThis study confirms that longitudinal changes in GA extent are associated with functional changes on defect-mapping microperimetry, both on a global and local level. These findings highlight the expected functional relevance of GA progression, and demonstrates the potential effectiveness of this microperimetry testing strategy for capturing visual function decline associated with GA progression.

Geographic atrophy (GA) is a late stage of AMD characterized by the progressive loss of the RPE and subsequent degeneration of the overlying photoreceptors.1–5 There have been no treatments to slow the progression of GA until recently, where two treatments have been shown to slow the growth of GA as measured on fundus autofluorescence (FAF) imaging.6,7 Howevever, trials of these treatments have not demonstrated a significant effect on any of their pre-specified secondary visual function endpoints, including best-corrected visual acuity, reading speed, and visual sensitivity as assessed by microperimetry (or fundus-controlled perimetry).6,7

The impact of GA on best-corrected visual acuity is highly dependent on its location within the macula and its encroachment on the fovea. These factors limit the ability of this measure to capture the functional impact of GA fully,8–10 and thus its usefulness as an outcome measure in trials evaluating new treatments for GA. Microperimetry has been used in numerous treatment trials aimed at slowing GA progression and associated visual function loss.11–15 These trials have typically used a conventional thresholding strategy on microperimetry to measure visual sensitivities across test locations throughout the macular region. When using this approach, a recent analysis of data from the large Chroma and Spectri Phase III GA trials observed that there were only weak correlations between the change in the GA area over time and change in either mean sensitivity or the number of absolute scotomatous points (Spearman correlation coefficients of approximately 0.01 and 0.26, respectively).14 These findings highlight the limitations of standard threshold-based microperimetry for detecting progressive functional loss associated with GA growth.

In response to these challenges, we have recently developed a microperimetry testing strategy optimized to quantify the spatial extent of deep visual sensitivity losses, which has shown promise for providing a more effective means for capturing progressive functional loss over time.16 This testing strategy—which we have termed a “defect-mapping” approach—involves presenting suprathreshold stimuli only once at each location. This approach allows functional testing to be performed with a much higher spatial density than possible with the conventional threshold-based strategy over a similar test duration. This is because the conventional threshold-based strategy requires multiple stimuli to be presented at each location to obtain estimates of visual sensitivities. However, with the defect-mapping testing strategy, these stimulus presentations are instead spent assessing different locations of the retina for the presence of deep visual sensitivity losses.17 In our recent cross-sectional study, we observed a strong correlation between the proportion of locations missed on defect-mapping microperimetry and the GA extent in the corresponding region tested (Spearman correlation coefficient = 0.90),16 being substantially higher than what was observed at baseline in the Chroma and Spectri Phase 3 trials at (Spearman correlation coefficient of −0.42 for mean sensitivity and 0.59 for the number of absolute scotomatous points).14 However, it remains to be determined whether defect-mapping microperimetry can also effectively capture progressive visual function decline that is closely correlated with GA growth. Addressing this question was examined in a prospective longitudinal study, as described in this report.

Methods

This study evaluated individuals who were enrolled in a prospective study of atrophic AMD at the Centre for Eye Research Australia. This prospective study was conducted in accordance with the International Conference on Harmonization Guidelines for Good Clinical Practice and with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki, and institutional review board approval was obtained for this study. All enrolled participants provided written informed consent.

Participants

This study included individuals who were 50 years or older with GA secondary to AMD, where the GA was 175 µm or greater in diameter on FAF imaging (defined as a region of definite decrease autofluorescence18) and best-corrected visual acuity of 20/100 or better in at least one eye. Any eye with neovascular AMD or that had undergone any intervention for AMD previously was excluded. Individuals with any ocular, systemic, or neurological condition(s) that prohibit the reliable assessment of visual function or the retina were also excluded from this study.

This study sought to perform longitudinal microperimetry testing of participants at 3-monthly intervals, up to a total of nine visits. If both eyes met the eligibility criteria at baseline, participants either performed testing of only one eye over time, both eyes at all follow-up visits, or both eyes at different follow-up visits (e.g., 3-monthly testing of one eye over 24 months, then subsequent 3-monthly testing of the other eye over 24 months), based on their preference.

Retinal Imaging

FAF images covering a 30° × 30° region centered on the macula were acquired using the Spectralis HRA+OCT device (Heidelberg Engineering GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany). On this device, FAF images were obtained using an excitation light source of 488 nm and emitted fluorescence signals between 500 and 700 nm were detected. At least 100 frames were averaged for the FAF images, and each image had a minimum dimension of 768 × 768 pixels. OCT volume scans of the central 20° × 20° region were also acquired using this same device, consisting of 97 B-scans and 1024 A-scans and 16 frames averaged per B-scan. Macular-centered color fundus photographs with a 45° diameter were also obtained with the Canon CR6-45NM device (Canon, Saitama, Japan).

Microperimetry Testing

The Macular Integrity Assessment device (CenterVue, Padova, Italy) was used for microperimetry testing in this study. All tests were performed after pupillary dilation and before performing any assessments that could bleach the retina or compromise the ocular surface. A line-scanning laser ophthalmoscope with a central wavelength of 850 nm acquired near-infrared fundus images covering 36.5° × 36.5° at 25 frames per second to enable fundus tracking during perimetry testing. This device presents Goldman Size III (0.43° diameter) stimuli against a mesopic background with a luminance of 1.27 cd/m2. The luminance of the stimuli ranged between 1.35 and 318 cd/m2, thereby providing a dynamic range of 36 dB of differential contrast.

Microperimetry testing in this study was performed using a defect-mapping strategy,16,17 which involved assessing the spatial extent of deep visual sensitivity abnormalities. This method involved presenting only stimuli with a luminance of 32.9 cd/m2 (or 10 dB, based on the dynamic range of the device) once, at each of 208 locations, within the central 8° radius region (or approximately 4.6-mm diameter region). An isotropic stimulus pattern with an interstimulus interval of 1° was used in this study. The stimulus luminance used corresponds to the floor of the effective dynamic range of the device, which is defined as the stimulus luminance, where 5% of the retest values include 0 dB (and are thus considered statistically indistinguishable from the physical floor of the dynamic range of the device).19 During testing, a red central fixation target that was 1° in diameter was used, and the stimulus grid was centered on the anatomical fovea manually when the fixation was nonfoveal (such as if GA involves the fovea).

At each study visit, participants were required to complete two reliable defect-mapping microperimetry tests. Any test with more than 25% false-positive errors—determined through catch trials of 10-dB stimulus presentations to the optic nerve head—was considered unreliable,16,17,20,21 and it was discarded and repeated where possible. After the first test, all subsequent tests during the same visit and at subsequent visits were performed using the follow-up function so that the same retinal locations are assessed across all the tests.

Image Annotations and Processing

FAF images from all follow-up visits were first manually coregistered with the baseline FAF image. This was achieved by placing fiducial points at retinal vessel bifurcations, and image coregistration was performed through radial transformations (to account for radial distortions) and affine transformation (including translation, scaling, and rotation), as previously described.22 For each visit, all areas of GA on the FAF images that were 175 µm or greater in diameter were annotated by a grader (either D.D. or E.E.G.), and reviewed together with a senior grader (L.A.B.H.). Any disagreements were resolved through open adjudication, and revisions were made as necessary. Small regions of hypoautofluorescence of less than 175 µm were not annotated in this study, consistent with the protocol from another recent study.23 All annotations of GA were performed using custom-written software (Cross-Modality Annotation Software [XMAS], Ophthalmic Neuroscience Unit, Centre for Eye Research Australia, East Melbourne, Australia).24,25

Because all the microperimetry tests were performed using the follow-up function and were thus all spatially coregistered, the near-infrared fundus image captured by the microperimeter with the best image quality at baseline was then manually coregistered with the baseline FAF image. Coregistration between these two modalities was performed using the same approach described above. These steps are all illustrated in a study schematic in Figure 1.

Schematic of study design, illustrating the coregistration process. For each follow-up visit, fundus autofluorescence (FAF) images for each eye were first manually coregistered with the baseline FAF image. The baseline FAF image was then coregistered with the near-infrared reflectance image from one of the two microperimetry tests performed at baseline with the best image quality. All the microperimetery tests over time were spatially coregistered with the baseline test through fundus-tracking. As a result, all the FAF images and microperimetry tests at each visit were coregistered, thereby enabling an evaluation of structural and functional changes over time in topographically corresponding regions and locations.

To evaluate the relationship between the longitudinal structural and functional changes thoroughly, both global and local analyses were performed. For the global analysis, the percentage of the corresponding 8° radius region assessed by microperimetry that had GA on FAF imaging, and the percentage of locations missed (PLM; or the proportion of locations with a nonseeing response to the single 10-dB stimuli presented) at all visits was derived. These two parameters enabled the evaluation of the relationship between the longitudinal structural and functional changes at a global level.

For the local analysis, the percentage of the corresponding 0.43° diameter region at each location sampled on microperimetry testing that had GA on FAF imaging, or the distance from the closest edge of the stimuli to the nearest GA margin, was also derived. These measures allowed for a detailed evaluation of the association between local structural and functional changes over time.

Statistical Analyses

An extended description of the statistical analyses is provided in the Supplementary Materials. Briefly, this study focused on evaluating the association between structural and functional changes between two visits, performed by evaluating all unique pairwise comparisons of defect-mapping microperimetry tests over time. The two visits in each unique pairwise comparison were termed the baseline and follow-up visits. Both microperimetry tests performed at each visit were used in the analyses; we have previously shown that no systematic intrasession changes with the PLM occur between these two tests.16

The correlation between global structural and functional changes longitudinally was evaluated by Spearman rank correlation coefficient and the proportion of variance (R2) in the PLM on defect-mapping microperimetry, explained by the percentage of GA within the corresponding 8° radius region on FAF imaging.

For local analyses, associations between structural and functional changes over time were evaluated by calculating the probability of worsening of the response at individual test locations. These locations were categorized as either entirely outside or within a GA region across both visits, or partially overlapping with GA at either visit. For the latter, locations were grouped by the change in the percentage of GA overlapping in 25% bins. For locations that were entirely outside or within a region of GA at both visits, further analyses were performed to examine the difference in the probability of worsening based on their distance from the GA margin at baseline or at the follow-up visit, and the change in this distance at the second or first visit respectively (both grouped in 125-µm bins). All analyses were conducted using Stata software version 18 (StataCorp, College Station, TX).

Results

A total of 57 eyes from 50 participants were included in this study, and these participants were on average 76 ± 7 years old (range, 52–87 years old) and 31 participants (62%) were female. The median baseline GA area in the central 30° × 30° region of these eyes were 3.66 mm2 (interquartile range [IQR], 2.24–6.20 mm2). These eyes underwent two defect-mapping microperimetry tests at each visit, over a median of five visits (IQR, 4–6 visits) over 14 months (IQR, 9–18 months), and a total of 286 study visits were included in the analyses. The median percentage of the central 8° radius region tested on microperimetry that had GA was 17% (IQR, 12%–32%; or a median GA area of 2.80 mm2 [IQR, 1.92–5.34 mm2] within the region).

Longitudinal Changes Visual Sensitivity and GA

At a global level overall, there was a moderate and significant correlation between the change in the PLM on microperimetry and the change in the percentage of the corresponding central 8° radius region annotated as having GA on FAF (Spearman correlation coefficient = 0.52, 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.26–0.71; P < 0.001) (Fig. 2). The change in GA extent explained a large proportion of variance in the PLM (R2 = 0.52; 95% CI, 0.10–0.83). At the local pointwise level overall, test locations falling entirely within GA lesion(s) at both the baseline and follow-up visits exhibited a small but statistically significant increase in the probability of worsening between the visits (i.e., an increased likelihood of nonresponse to stimuli; 2.3%; 95% CI, 1.8%–2.8%; P < 0.001) on defect-mapping microperimetry. In contrast, test locations falling entirely outside GA lesion(s) at both the baseline and follow-up visits did not show a significant worsening between the visits (0.3%; 95% CI, −0.2% to 0.8%; P = 0.305). Test locations that overlapped with the GA margin at either the baseline or a follow-up visit showed a significant association between the probability of worsening and the change in extent of GA overlap at the test location (P < 0.001). Specifically, locations that showed a 0% to 25%, 25% to 50%, 50% to 75%, and 75% to 100% change in extent of GA overlap had a probability of worsening of 4.1% (95% CI, 3.6%–4.7%), 12.0% (95% CI, 11.3%–12.8%), 17.5% (95% CI, 16.6%–18.3%), and 37.9% (95% CI, 37.2%–38.6%), respectively, on defect-mapping microperimetry (all P < 0.001); these findings are illustrated in Figure 4A.

Further analyses revealed that, for test locations entirely outside the GA lesions at both the baseline and follow-up visits, there was a significant interaction between its baseline distance from the GA margin and change in this distance at the follow-up visit, impacting the probability of worsening on defect-mapping microperimetry (interaction P < 0.001). These findings are illustrated in Figure 4B. For example, test locations falling between 125 and 250 µm outside the GA margin at baseline that showed a 0- to 125-µm and 125- to 250-µm increase in proximity toward GA margin at follow-up had a 1.2% (95% CI, 0.5%–1.8%) and 7.2% (95% CI, 6.1%–8.2%) probability of worsening respectively. In contrast, test locations falling between 250 and 375 µm outside the GA margin at baseline that showed a 0- to 125-µm and 125- to 250-µm increase in proximity toward GA margin had a 0.7% (95% CI, 0.0%–1.4%) and 3.9% (95% CI, 3.0%%–4.9%), respectively.

Further analyses revealed that, for test locations entirely within the GA lesions at both the baseline and follow-up visits, there was also a significant interaction between their distance from the GA margin at baseline, and the change in this proximity to the GA margin at the follow-up visit for the probability of worsening on defect-mapping microperimetry (P < 0.001). These findings are also illustrated in Figure 4C, showing for example how test locations falling between 125 and 250 µm within the GA margin at the follow-up visit that showed a 0- to 125-µm and 125- to 250-µm increase in distance inside the GA margin from baseline had a 2.6% (95% CI, 1.9%–3.3%) and 18.1% (95% CI, 16.3%–19.8%) probability of worsening, respectively. In contrast, test locations falling between 250 and 375 µm inside the GA margin at the follow-up visit that showed a 0- to 125-µm and 125- to 250-µm increase in proximity toward the GA margin had a 1.1% (95% CI, 0.4%–1.9%) and 10.8% (95% CI, 9.3%%–12.4%) probability of worsening, respectively.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that longitudinal changes in GA extent explained a large proportion of the variance (52%) in the global functional changes on defect-mapping microperimetry testing. This finding was supported by analyses of functional changes at the local level, where there was a significant association between the probability of functional worsening with the change in the GA extent overlapping individual test locations over time. These findings collectively demonstrate the effectiveness of defect-mapping microperimetry testing for capturing progressive functional loss associated with GA growth, and also confirm the expected functional significance of GA growth.

The findings of this study are broadly consistent with the findings from the Chroma and Spectri Phase III trials, showing that there was a correlation between the change in the number of absolute scotomatous points on conventional threshold-based microperimetry testing and GA growth over 48 weeks.14 However, the level of correlation observed in these previous trials was weaker (Spearman correlation coefficient = 0.26, approximately)14 than compared with those observed in this study (Spearman correlation coefficient = 0.52). Note that the level of correlation in this study was observed when comparisons of changes between 3-monthly visits were included. The level of correlation would be even higher if only comparisons over a larger time interval was included, as larger longitudinal changes relative to measurement variability would be expected to occur (Spearman correlation coefficient = 0.68 and 0.87 when including only time intervals of ≥12 and ≥24 months, respectively). Nonetheless, the above difference in the strength of the correlation in longitudinal structural and functional changes observed could be explained partly by the evaluation of the GA extent in the entire FAF image (covering the central 30° × 30° region) in the Chroma and Spectri trials,14 rather than the corresponding region tested on microperimetry performed in this study. Indeed, we saw a decrease in the level of correlation between structural and functional changes when evaluating GA extent over the entire FAF image (Spearman correlation coefficient = 0.40), but this level of correlation was still higher than observed in the Chroma and Spectri trials.14 This difference is most likely explained by the defect-mapping microperimetry testing strategy used, which may be more efficient at quantifying the spatial extent of deep visual sensitivity defects associated with GA. This strategy optimizes testing by assessing each location a single time, thereby enabling a higher spatial density in testing. In contrast, threshold-based microperimetry may be hindered in its capacity to capture functional decline in GA effectively, primarily owing to the substantial measurement variability commonly encountered in regions with decreased visual sensitivity.19,21 Recent computer simulations further corroborate these findings, showing that a high-density suprathreshold adaptive testing procedure achieved better accuracy than a 4-2 staircase strategy for assessing functional loss in advanced glaucoma when the two strategies were matched for test duration.26 These findings lend broader support to the usefulness of the defect-mapping microperimetry testing strategy used in this study for eyes with GA. This defect-mapping microperimetry testing strategy is also likely to be more effective at characterizing progressive visual sensitivity decline than a previous novel approach using patient-tailored grids with standard threshold-based testing,27 which would likely also be subject to the large measurement variability expected when evaluating the junctional zone of GA (which is characterized by having reduced visual sensitivities). Note also that such an approach is suboptimal for sampling eyes with multifocal GA lesions, and it would also likely miss sampling regions that develop GA over time.

In our recent cross-sectional study using defect-mapping microperimetry to evaluate local structure–function associations in eyes with GA, we observed that the probability of missing the stimuli at test locations at the GA margin increased significantly with an increasing level of its overlap with the GA lesion(s).16 This current longitudinal study corroborated these findings by demonstrating that the probability of worsening (i.e., of transitioning from a seeing to nonseeing response) was also significantly associated with the change in the percentage of overlap with GA lesion(s) over time. For example, test locations that showed a 0% to 25% and 75% to 100% increase in percentage of overlap with GA between study visits showed a 4.1% and 37.9% mean probability of functional worsening, respectively.

We also observed in our previous cross-sectional study that test locations that fell entirely between 0 and 500 µm outside the GA margin showed a small, but significantly higher probability of being missed compared with more distal nonlesional regions (increasing with greater proximity to the GA margin).16 In this current longitudinal study, we also observed that for locations that fell entirely outside the GA lesion on two visits, the probability of worsening between these two visits was significantly associated with an interaction between the distance of the test location at baseline and its change over time. For example, test locations that were between 125 and 250 µm outside the GA margin at baseline showed a 1.2% and 7.2% mean probability of worsening if the change in their distance from the GA margin at the follow-up visit was 0 and 125 µm and 125 and 250 µm, respectively, whereas test locations that were between 250 and 375 µm outside the GA margin showed a 0.7% and 3.9% mean probability of worsening, respectively. These findings are unsurprising and also biologically plausible given previous histopathological findings that photoreceptor loss and RPE dysmorphia extend beyond the atrophic lesion into the perilesional region.1,4,28 Such pathological changes likely account for greater functional loss present in this perilesional region compared with more distal nonlesional regions. An increase in the proximity of test locations toward this perilesional region would thus be expected to be associated with an increase in the likelihood of functional loss over time, as observed in this longitudinal study.

Our previous cross-sectional study also revealed that test locations entirely within 0 to 250 µm of the GA margin showed a significantly lower probability of being missed when compared with locations further inside the GA margin (decreasing with greater proximity to the GA margin).16 In this longitudinal study, we similarly found that the probability of worsening between study visits was significantly associated with an interaction between the baseline distance inside the GA margin and the change in this distance over time. For example, test locations that were between 125 and 250 µm inside the GA margin at the follow-up visit showed a 2.6% and 18.1% mean probability of worsening if these locations had showed a 0- to 125-µm and 125- to 250-µm increase in distance from the GA margin between visits respectively. Meanwhile, test locations that were between 250 and 375 µm inside the GA margin showed a 1.1% and 10.8% mean probability of worsening, respectively. These findings suggest that there is a greater likelihood of surviving photoreceptors1,2,4 being present closer to the GA margin when compared with regions further inside the GA lesion. This finding likely explains the observed association in this study that an increase in distance from the GA margin for a test location within the atrophic lesion (owing to GA growth) is linked to a higher probability of functional decline.

These findings collectively demonstrate that the global correlations observed between GA extent and the defect-mapping microperimetry results are supported by corroborating evidence of associations between the local structural and functional changes. Taken together, these results show how defect-mapping microperimetry could be an effective approach for capturing visual function loss over time associated with GA growth, and also confirm the expected functional relevance of GA growth. We, therefore, continue to recommend this microperimetry testing strategy for use in clinical trials of new treatments seeking to slow GA growth.

Limitations of this study include errors that are expected to be present with the manual coregistration of the FAF images over time, and the manual coregistration of the FAF image with the near-infrared image obtained on microperimetry testing. Errors are also expected with the limits of the speed of fundus tracking (25 frames per second) performed on microperimetry, but all these errors are expected to be random (and thus introduce noise), rather than be systematic in nature. Despite such inherent errors, statistically significant and biologically plausible associations were still observed between longitudinal changes in structure and function in this study. Strengths of this study include the fact that two microperimetry tests were performed at each visit, and that the study visits were scheduled at 3-monthly intervals, which together provided a large amount of data for performing a detailed evaluation of the association between local structural and functional changes (such as an evaluation of even a 0% to 25% change in GA area overlapping a 0.43° diameter [or approximately 124 µm] test location).

In conclusion, this study showed that longitudinal changes in GA extent explained a large proportion of the variance (50%) in the functional changes on defect-mapping microperimetry testing at a global level. Significant associations were also observed between local structural and functional changes at a pointwise level. These findings demonstrate the potential effectiveness of defect-mapping microperimetry testing for capturing functional decline associated with GA growth, and they also confirm the expected functional relevance of GA progression.
