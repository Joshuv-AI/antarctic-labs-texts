Sedentary time during pregnancy and fetal malpresentation in the Japan Environment and Children’s Study
Authors: Kimiyo Kikuchi, Takehiro Michikawa, Seiichi Morokuma, Norio Hamada, Kiyoko Kato, Masayuki Ochiai, Mayumi Tsuji, Kiyoshi Yoshino, Reiko Suga, Toshihiro Kawamoto, Shouichi Ohga, Michihiro Kamijima, Michihiro Kamijima, Shin Yamazaki, Maki Fukami, Reiko Kishi, Chiharu Ota, Koichi Hashimoto, Chisato Mori, Shuichi Ito, Ryoji Shinohara, Hidekuni Inadera, Takeo Nakayama, Ryo Kawasaki, Yasuhiro Takeshima, Seiji Kageyama, Narufumi Suganuma, Shouichi Ohga, Takahiko Katoh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFetal malpresentation, such as breech, transverse, and oblique presentations, is a frequent diagnosis in obstetrical practice. While several maternal factors have been studied, the potential relationship between maternal sedentary behavior and fetal malpresentation remains underexplored. This study aimed to examine the association between sedentary behavior during pregnancy and fetal malpresentation using data from a large longitudinal cohort study conducted in Japan.MethodsThis prospective longitudinal cohort study recruited approximately 100,000 pregnant women from 15 Regional Centers in Japan. Participants completed self-administered questionnaires in the first and either the second or third trimester of pregnancy. Sedentary behavior was defined as the number of hours spent sitting or lying down per day. The fetal presentation at delivery was recorded from medical records. Logistic regression analyses were conducted to estimate the odds ratio of the association between sedentary time and the likelihood of fetal malpresentation.ResultsHere we show that among 73,813 eligible participants, the mean maternal age is 31.3 years, 27.4% deliver at ≥35, 3.0% experience fetal malpresentation, and 48.7% have female infant. The median sedentary time is 5 hours (interquartile range: 3–8). Women who report ≥12 hours sedentary time per day have higher odds for fetal malpresentation compared to those reporting <6 hours (adjusted odds ratio: 1.17; 95% confidence interval: 1.02–1.35).ConclusionsThe findings emphasize the need to address sedentary behavior as a modifiable risk factor during pregnancy, which may implicate future interventions aimed at reducing the incidence of fetal malpresentation.

Introduction

Fetal malpresentation, commonly referred to as failure to turn, is a frequent diagnosis in obstetrical practice. This condition encompasses various types, including breech, transverse, and oblique presentations1. Epidemiological data indicate that breech presentation is the most common among these, occurring in 3–5% of cases. Oblique and transverse lies are observed in fewer than 0.05% of pregnancies2. Approximately one-fourth of fetuses present with malpresentation at or before 28 weeks of gestation3. Due to the increased risk of perinatal and neonatal mortality or severe morbidity associated with these conditions, planned cesarean delivery is often recommended for fetal malpresentation4. Several factors have been identified as predictors of malpresentation, including nulliparity5–7, advanced maternal age6, placenta previa8, congenital anomalies9, tobacco use during pregnancy5, previous cesarean delivery10,11, and assisted reproductive technology12.

In recent years, sedentary behavior during pregnancy has emerged as a lifestyle factor that may impact child health. Sedentary behavior is defined as any waking activity characterized by sitting, reclining, or lying down13, with low energy expenditure (approximately ≤1.5 metabolic equivalents)14–16. Prolonged sedentary behavior in adults is generally associated with increased mortality from various diseases, such as cardiovascular disease17, cancer18, and type 2 diabetes19,20. Although evidence is mixed, prolonged sedentary behavior during pregnancy has been linked to adverse outcomes, including excessive fetal growth, macrosomia21, and a large neonatal abdominal circumference22.

Additionally, maternal standing, walking, and position changes may stimulate the fetus’s vestibular system through changes in acceleration and gravity. The fetus’s vestibular organs and motor control circuit mature after the second trimester of pregnancy, and the fetus perceives the mother’s movement23,24. This mechanism was also suggested in a mouse experiment25. Based on these findings, although currently unproven, a mother’s movements may potentially stimulate the fetus’s rotation. Accordingly, prolonged sedentary behavior may potentially reduce this stimulation and contribute to persistent malpresentation.

Given these potential effects, a recent expert consensus suggested limiting sedentary time during pregnancy to no more than 7 h per day26. In addition, sedentary behavior has been inversely associated with low birth weight27 and correlates with unfavorable maternal biological markers, including elevated levels of C-reactive protein and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol28.

The potential relationship between sedentary behavior during pregnancy and fetal malpresentation has not yet been directly evaluated in population-based studies. Understanding this relationship could facilitate the development of preventive strategies to reduce the incidence of fetal malpresentation. Therefore, this study aims to examine the association between sedentary behavior during pregnancy and fetal malpresentation using data from a large longitudinal cohort study in Japan. Here we show that longer pregnancy sedentary time is associated with fetal malpresentation.

Methods

Study design and participants

This prospective, longitudinal cohort study analyzed data from the Japan Environment and Children’s Study (JECS), which is registered in the University Hospital Medical Information Network Clinical Trials Registry (UMIN000030786; registration date: 12 January 2018). The protocol and its detailed profile have been previously detailed29,30. Approximately 100,000 pregnant women and their children were recruited from 15 Regional Centers across Japan between January 2011 and March 2014. These participants will be followed longitudinally from the mothers’ first trimester of pregnancy until the children reach 18 years of age.

To assess the association between sedentary behavior during pregnancy and fetal malpresentation, we included data from all women who had completely delivered. Exclusion criteria were applied to remove cases of multiple enrollments, multiple births, miscarriages, stillbirths, congenital anomalies, delivery before 34 weeks of gestation as fetal presentation is often unstable31, and incomplete data regarding maternal age at delivery, fetal presentation, maternal sedentary behavior during pregnancy, or maternal and child covariates (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Flow diagram illustrating the selection process of study participants.

Flow diagram illustrating the selection process of study participants.

The JECS protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ministry of the Environment’s Institutional Review Board on Epidemiological Studies (no. 100910001) and the Ethics Committees of all participating institutions. All procedures adhere to relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study.

Procedure

Mothers were asked to complete self-administered questionnaires during their first and either second (14~27 weeks) or third trimesters (28 weeks and after) of pregnancy. Additionally, medical records were transcribed at the time of birth to supplement the questionnaire data.

Assessment of maternal sedentary behavior during pregnancy and fetal presentation

Maternal sedentary behavior during pregnancy was assessed using a question regarding the hours spent sitting or lying down, excluding sleep. We used data from the second or third trimesters because the fetal position begins to stabilize during this period. Participants were asked about their activity during the previous week: “In an ordinary day, how much time do you spend sitting or lying down on a week day? This includes time sitting at a desk, talking with friends, reading, or sitting or lying down to watch television. Do not include sleeping time.” This question was based on the Japanese short version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire32,33. Responses were categorized into five groups: <6 h, 6 to <8 h, 8 to <10 h, 10 to <12 h, and ≥12 h, following a previous study34. Since our study sample size was large, we further subdivided the ≥10-hour group. Fetal presentation was transcribed from the medical records at the time of delivery categorized as vertex, breech, transverse, or other positions. Presentations categorized as breech or transverse, as well as those noted as “other” (e.g., oblique), were collectively classified as “malpresentation.”

Physical activity during pregnancy was assessed using questionnaires that asked about the time spent doing vigorous physical activities (such as heavy lifting, digging, aerobics, or fast bicycling), moderate physical activities (such as carrying light loads, bicycling at a regular pace, or playing doubles tennis), and walking for at least 10 min at a time. The total physical activity was calculated in metabolic equivalent (MET) minutes per week.

Statistical analysis

A descriptive analysis of the data was initially performed, categorizing participants based on the duration of sedentary time during pregnancy. Following this, logistic regression analyses were conducted to estimate the odds ratio (OR) as an approximation of the risk ratio to assess the association between sedentary time and the likelihood of fetal malpresentation. The regression models were adjusted for maternal and child covariates, selected based on prior studies35–38. The model A was adjusted for maternal covariates that are directly associated with pregnancy sedentary behavior. The model B, which was additionally adjusted for child covariates that may also influence fetal presentation, was used to assess the robustness of the association. Maternal covariates included maternal age at delivery, marital status, educational attainment, employment status, household income, smoking habits, alcohol consumption, parity, pre-pregnancy body mass index, infertility treatment, previous cesarean section, history of uterine related disease (myoma and anomalies), current pregnancy complications (e.g., placenta previa, oligohydramnios, polyhydramnios), and physical activity during pregnancy32,39. Child covariates included gestational age at birth, sex, birth weight, and chest-to-head circumference ratio. Participants with missing values in covariates were excluded from the analyses. Because maternal covariates in our main model (model A) were collected during pregnancy, missingness for these covariates was unlikely to depend on outcome condition at birth. Considering that the results were unlikely to be distorted by missing data, we performed a complete-case analysis for covariates (listwise deletion). Basic sociodemographic characteristics of the participants were incorporated into the models to minimize potential confounding bias. Initially, the analysis was adjusted for maternal covariates alone, and subsequently adjusted by additionally including child covariates. All analyses were performed using STATA version 16.1 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA), utilizing the jecs-ta-20190930 dataset released in October 2019.

Ethics approval

The JECS protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ministry of the Environment’s Institutional Review Board on Epidemiological Studies (no. 100910001) and the Ethics Committees of all participating institutions. All procedures adhere to relevant guidelines, regulations, and the Declaration of Helsinki.

After adjusting for maternal factors, the pattern of association remained statistically significant for sedentary time ≥12 h (adjusted odds ratio [AOR]: 1.19; 95% CI: 1.03–1.36). This trend persisted even after further adjustment for child factors, with mothers having sedentary times of ≥12 h continuing to show the highest risk of fetal malpresentation (AOR: 1.17; 95% CI: 1.02–1.35) relative to those with sedentary times of <6 h. Although the odds ratios of each category did not show smooth linear dose-response pattern, the highest sedentary time group showed the greatest increase in risk. In parity stratified analyses, this trend persisted among primiparous women as presented in the Supplementary Table 1 (AOR: 1.18; 95% CI: 1.00–1.39). However, we found no evidence of effect modification (P for interaction = 0.889). Similarly in physical activity stratified analyses, longer sedentary time tended to be an increased risk of fetal malpresentation in both low and high physical activity groups as presented in the Supplementary Table 2 and we found no evidence of effect modification (P for interaction = 0.409).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is among the first to identify an association between prolonged sedentary time during pregnancy and an increased risk of fetal malpresentation. In particular, pregnant women who spent ≥12 h per day in a sedentary position had the highest risk of malpresentation compared to those with shorter sedentary durations. This trend persisted even after adjusting for maternal and infant covariates identified from previous studies, supporting the robustness of these findings.

A key strength of this study is the large, prospective cohort design, which allowed for the adjustment of a wide range of maternal and infant factors known to influence fetal presentation. Additionally, the use of data from the JECS, a well-established longitudinal cohort, adds credibility to the results. However, there are limitations that must be acknowledged. First, the sedentary time of pregnant women was assessed only once during the second and third trimester and was self-reported based on a typical weekday. This might have attenuated the association due to non-differential measurement errors. Also, it may have introduced recall bias or variability in how participants interpreted the question. More objective and repeated measures, such as wearable activity trackers, would improve the accuracy of sedentary time estimates in future research. Second, fetal presentation was assessed only at the time of delivery, making it unclear whether the malpresentation persisted throughout pregnancy or developed later. Therefore, causality between sedentary time and fetal malpresentation cannot be definitely established. Longitudinal assessments of fetal position, along with more precise measurements of sedentary behavior, are necessary to address this limitation. Third, the IPAQ used to assess sedentary time and physical activity may not capture heterogeneous patterns such as prolonged weekday sedentary time combined with high weekend physical activity. Fourth, because of the large sample size, even small differences in outcomes may be statistically significant. However, as highest sedentary time group showed the greatest increase in risk, the prolonged sedentary time may have an association with fetal malpresentation.

Although the precise mechanisms between maternal sedentary behavior and fetal malpresentation remain unclear, several plausible explanations have been proposed. One potential factor is the increase in systemic inflammation associated with prolonged sedentary behavior, which may affect fetal vestibular function and thus fetal positioning40. Previous studies have shown that prolonged inactivity is associated with elevated inflammation as indicated by plasma C-reactive protein41. Impaired vestibular function could, in turn, lead to abnormal fetal positioning. Supporting this hypothesis, a study found that compared to fetuses in cephalic presentation, fetuses with malpresentation exhibit distinct eye movement patterns, specifically a significant decrease in horizontal eye movements and an increase in vertical and oblique eye movements, indicating potential vestibular development issues42.

In addition, maternal physical activity during pregnancy stimulates the fetal nervous system, potentially promoting proper fetal movement and positioning. For example, low-intensity exercise in early pregnancy has been shown to improve umbilical blood flow and placental circulation, which can aid in the fetal heart’s adaptation to its environment43. Increased fetal activity provides the fetus with opportunities to adjust and adopt a normal vertex position, potentially reducing the likelihood of malpresentation44.

The findings in this study contribute to the prevention of fetal malpresentation. Incorporating the knowledge into clinical practice may help improve pregnant women’s awareness on the sedentary time and encourage more self-management. The findings may also be generalizable to broader populations. However, anthropometric differences and pelvic shape may vary across racial and ethnic groups, and those differences may affect the fetal malpresentation. Further studies are needed to clarify the potential influences and to confirm the generalizability of our findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlights the association between extended sedentary periods during pregnancy and an increased risk of fetal malpresentation. The findings suggest that maternal sedentary behavior may be a modifiable factor that could be addressed through targeted interventions aimed at reducing sedentary time during pregnancy. Encouraging pregnant women to engage in regular physical activity may have significant implications for reducing the incidence of fetal malpresentation and improving birth outcomes, although further research is needed to confirm these findings and explore causal mechanisms.
