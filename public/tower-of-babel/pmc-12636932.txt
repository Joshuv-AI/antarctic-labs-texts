Knowledge, attitude and practice of patients with functional gastrointestinal disorders toward their diseases: a web-based cross-sectional study in a Chinese outpatient setting
Authors: Chenjuan Wang, Zhekai Ying, Rong Zhou, Yiling Luo, Tianchen Lin, Mengyuan Shen, Jiannong Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to investigate the knowledge, attitude and practice (KAP) of patients living with functional gastrointestinal disorders (FGIDs) toward their diseases.DesignA web-based cross-sectional study was conducted.SettingThe gastroenterology outpatient department of Zhejiang Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine, Zhejiang, China.ParticipantsThe study enrolled 503 patients with FGIDs from the Gastroenterology Outpatient Department of our hospital between September and October 2023.InterventionsNot applicable for cross-sectional study.Primary and secondary outcome measuresParticipants completed a self-designed questionnaire that collected sociodemographic information and assessed KAP scores. The primary outcome measures were KAP scores.ResultsThe mean KAP scores were 6.57±2.76 (possible range: 0–10) for knowledge, 30.00±4.08 (possible range: 7–35) for attitude and 30.16±4.92 (possible range: 8–40) for practice. Pearson’s correlation analysis indicated a positive and moderate correlation between knowledge and attitude (r=0.330, p<0.001), a weak positive correlation between knowledge and practice (r=0.152, p=0.001) and a weak positive correlation between attitude and practice (r=0.132, p=0.003). Structural equation model analysis revealed that drinking (estimate=−0.979, p=0.016) and household income (estimate=0.388, p<0.001) directly affected knowledge. Furthermore, knowledge (estimate=0.486, p<0.001) directly influenced attitude, while knowledge (estimate=0.206, p=0.015) and attitude (estimate=0.112, p=0.045) directly affected practice.ConclusionsPatients with FGIDs demonstrated moderate knowledge, positive attitudes and moderate practices regarding their disease. Drinking habits and household income reportedly influenced their KAP outcomes. Targeted educational interventions are warranted to enhance practice behaviours among patients with FGIDs.Keywords: Knowledge, Attitude, Functional bowel disorders, Cross-Sectional Studies

Introduction

Functional gastrointestinal disorders (FGIDs) are common medical conditions that affect the functioning of the digestive system without any identifiable structural or biochemical abnormalities.1 The most common FGIDs include irritable bowel syndrome, functional dyspepsia and functional constipation affecting 5–26%, 11.5–14.7% and 14–15.3% population globally and 6.8%, 10.7% and 9.3% in China, respectively.2,5 These disorders can cause chronic or recurrent gastrointestinal symptoms and have a significant impact on quality of life.6 Its background dates back to the mid-20th century when medical professionals began recognising gastrointestinal symptoms without clear organic causes.7 Initially labelled as ‘psychosomatic’ or ‘functional’ disorders, FGIDs posed a challenge for diagnosis and treatment due to the absence of clear pathological changes.8 Extensive research has revealed that FGIDs are multifactorial, involving physiological, psychological, environmental and genetic factors.1 9 10 The aetiology of FGIDs involves interactions between gut physiology, gut–brain axis, psychological factors, immune function, gut microbiota and genetic predisposition, in addition to stress, diet, lifestyle and cultural factors.11 12 Diagnostic criteria for FGIDs have evolved over time, with standardised criteria developed by organisations like the Rome Foundation.13 The current research aims to understand the underlying mechanisms and explore potential therapeutic targets.14 Collaborative efforts between clinicians and researchers are essential to improving the management and quality of life for those affected by FGIDs.

The knowledge, attitude and practice (KAP) of patients with FGIDs are crucial for their overall well-being. When patients have knowledge about their condition, they can make informed decisions and actively participate in their care. This knowledge also helps them recognise signs of exacerbation, seek timely medical advice and adhere to treatment plans.15 Increased awareness of FGIDs can reduce anxiety and fear related to unexplained symptoms, and thus could improve mental well-being. The attitude of patients towards their condition impacts their coping strategies and treatment adherence. A positive attitude fosters self-efficacy and resilience, leading to lifestyle changes, dietary modifications and stress reduction that improve symptom management and quality of life.16 In contrast, a negative attitude may hinder treatment compliance and exacerbate symptoms.17 Patient practice reflects the application of their knowledge and attitudes in managing their condition. Good practices, including diet, exercise, stress management and medication adherence, alleviate symptoms, reduce healthcare visits and improve patient outcomes.18 Healthcare professionals have a vital role in promoting positive KAP by providing accurate information, addressing concerns and fostering a supportive environment.19 Promoting self-management skills and open communication enhances practice among patients.20 In essence, a well-informed, positive and proactive approach to managing FGIDs improves patient outcomes and quality of life, and healthcare professionals can play a pivotal role in empowering patients to effectively manage their condition.

Although FGIDs have been extensively studied, most research has focused on clinical management and pathophysiology, with limited attention to patients’ self-management knowledge and practices. While healthcare providers’ knowledge and attitudes toward FGIDs treatment have been investigated,21 comprehensive assessments of patients’ own KAP remain scarce. A study from China found that patients with FGIDs’ illness perceptions significantly influenced their coping strategies and healthcare-seeking behaviours, with patients who had better understanding of their condition showing improved treatment adherence and quality of life.22 However, that study focused primarily on illness perception rather than systematically evaluating the inter-relationships among KAPs. Furthermore, existing studies rarely examine the complex interrelationships among KAP dimensions, particularly in Asian populations where FGID prevalence is high but patient-centred research is limited.

This study is particularly valuable as it uses structural equation modelling (SEM) to analyse the complex inter-relationships among sociodemographic factors and KAP, providing a deeper, more nuanced understanding of how these factors influence patient behaviours in a Chinese population.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted in the gastroenterology outpatient department of our hospital, between September and October 2023. The inclusion criteria for participants were: (1) outpatients experiencing gastrointestinal discomfort, including nausea, belching, constipation, abdominal pain, diarrhoea; (2) aged ≥18 years; and (3) diagnosed with FGIDs according to Rome IV criteria.23 The exclusion criteria for patients were: (1) gastrointestinal discomfort attributed to other clinical diagnoses; (2) presence of other digestive tract diseases such as cholecystitis, abnormal liver function, digestive tract tumours, etc; and (3) pregnant women. The inclusion and exclusion of patients were based on medical records review, and no additional clinical investigations were performed specifically for this study.

Questionnaire

The questionnaire was designed according to references relevant to FGIDs.23 24 The first draft of the questionnaire was revised with input from two gastroenterologists and one psychotherapist (see online supplemental appendix-questionnaire). The self-designed questionnaire was initially pretested on 50 patients, and the reliability tests yielded a Cronbach’s α value of 0.649. Based on the pretest results, a question in the practice section, ‘you recorded the food that lead to discomfort and avoid them’, was identified as a factor that reduced reliability and was difficult for patients to answer. After excluding this question, a new pretest was performed on another 50 patients, resulting in a Cronbach’s α value of 0.779, which is an acceptable level of internal consistency. The final questionnaire consisted of four dimensions with a total of 38 questions: sociodemographic characteristics (13 questions), knowledge (10 questions), attitude (7 questions) and practice (8 questions). The knowledge dimension comprised 10 items, with 1 point awarded for correct answers and 0 points for incorrect or unclear answers. The score range for this dimension was 0–10 points. Based on Bloom’s cut-off criteria,25 the knowledge scores were categorised as follows: ‘poor’ (scores <6, <60%), ‘moderate’ (scores 6 to <8, 60–79%) and ‘good’ (scores from 8 to 10, ≥80%). The attitude dimension contained seven items, with scores ranging from 1 (very negative) to 5 (very positive). The total score for this dimension ranged from 7 to 35 points. Based on Bloom’s cut-off criteria: scores from 7 to 20 (<60%) indicated a negative attitude, scores from 21 to 27 (60–79%) indicated a moderate attitude and scores from 28 to 35 (≥80%) indicated a positive attitude. The practice dimension had eight items, with scores ranging from 1 (very negative) to 5 (very positive). The total score for this dimension ranged from 8 to 40 points. Based on Bloom’s cut-off criteria: scores from 8 to 23 (<60%) indicated an inactive practice, scores from 24 to 31 (60–79%) indicated a moderate practice and scores from 32 to 40 (≥80%) indicated a proactive practice.

Data collection

Participants who met the inclusion criteria were sent the questionnaire electronically through ‘Sojump (https://www.wjx.cn/vm/rXBoT59.aspx#)’.1,6 Remuneration to encourage participation, monitoring the answering duration, providing preliminary training to researchers and clarifying questions for patients were performed. Participants who completed the questionnaire received a small digital payment of ¥5.00 (approximately US$0.7) as a token of appreciation for their time.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated using Cochran’s formula: n=Z²pq/e², where Z=1.96 (95% confidence level), p=estimated proportion of patients with good KAP levels. Since no previous studies have used our questionnaire to assess KAP levels, we set p=0.50 to yield the maximum sample size estimate, q=1, p=0.50 and e=margin of error (0.05). This yielded a minimum sample size of 384 participants with valid responses.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS V.26.0 (IBM, Armonk, New York, USA). The demographic characteristics of the respondents and their KAP scores were analysed descriptively. Continuous variables were expressed as mean±SD and compared by Student’s t-test or analysis of variance, while categorical variables were expressed as n (%). Pearson’s correlation analysis was used to evaluate the correlation among KAP dimensions. SEM was performed to identify the path relations among sociodemographic characteristics and KAP based on the following hypothesis: the hypotheses were formulated from two sources. First, the interactions among KAP were based on the established KAP theoretical model. Second, the impact of demographic characteristics on KAP was derived from an analysis of baseline comparisons and clinical experience. H1: knowledge has a positive effect on attitude; H2: attitude has a positive effect on practice; H3: smoking has a negative effect on knowledge and attitude; H4: drinking has a negative effect on knowledge and practice; H5: education has a positive effect on knowledge and attitude; H6: household income has a positive effect on knowledge and practice. Comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), incremental fit index (IFI), χ² ratio of df (CMIN/DF) and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) were used to evaluate the model fit of SEM. All tests were two-sided, with p<0.05 indicating statistical significance.

Patient and public involvement

Patients were involved as study participants who completed the questionnaires. No patients were involved in the design, conduct or reporting of this research.

Results

A total of 503 valid questionnaires were collected from patients with FGIDs, including 219 males (43.54%), aged 36.83±14.90 years. The final sample of 503 valid questionnaires exceeded our calculated minimum requirement, ensuring adequate statistical power to detect meaningful relationships between variables. Their mean scores of KAP were 6.57±2.76, 30.00±4.08 and 30.16±4.92, respectively. In addition, patients’ knowledge score significantly varied in different residence (p=0.003), education (p=0.004), household income (p<0.001), marital status (p=0.025), smoking (p=0.008), drinking (p=0.004), medical insurance (p=0.013) and family history (p<0.001). Attitude scores significantly varied in different gender (p=0.005), education (p=0.036) and smoking (p=0.015). Practice scores were significantly varied in household income (p=0.006) and drinking (p=0.019) (table 1).

The distribution of correct responses to knowledge-based questions related to FGIDs showed varying levels of knowledge on FGIDs. The highest percentage of correct responses was for question K3 regarding the knowledge about the general symptoms of FGIDs. The lowest percentage of correct responses was for question K4 regarding the inheritance of the disease (figure 1A). For the attitude dimension of FGIDs, in response to question A1, the majority of patients (59.64%) showed strong agreement that FGIDs are related to diet. For question A2, the majority (45.33%) of patients showed disagreement that the FGIDs symptoms are mild. Most of the patients (54.27%) showed disagreement that FGIDs have some psychological impact (A3). The majority of patients (58.85%) disagreed that FGIDs are easy to treat (A4), while others (48.31%) believed that aggressive treatment leads to good prognosis (A6). Most of the patients (58.65%) disagreed that FGIDs have little impact on quality of life (A5). The majority of patients (34.19%) showed agreement that they would still care for the disease even in the case of mild symptoms or none at all (A7). Most of the patients (44.33%) showed willingness to learn more about the disease (figure 1B). The distribution of responses as for each question in the practice dimension showed that the majority of patients ‘always’ actively treat FGIDs (P1, 47.51%), follow the doctor’s instructions for regular follow-up (P2, 54.08%), quit smoking and alcohol (P3, 61.83%) and decrease coffee intake and high-fat diet (P8, 36.78%). On the other hand, the majority of patients responded that they ‘sometimes’ avoid overeating (P4, 29.82%). The majority of patients responded that they ‘seldom’ have drugs for promoting gastrointestinal motility (P5, 27.24%). Mostly, patients ‘usually’ remained calm at the onset of symptoms of FGIDs (P6, 35.59%). Finally, the majority of patients responded that they ‘sometimes’ participate in sports (P7, 30.02%) (figure 1C).

Pearson’s correlation analysis showed a positive correlation between knowledge and attitude (r=0.330, p<0.001), between knowledge and practice (r=0.152, p=0.001) and between attitude and practice (r=0.132, p=0.003) (table 2). Moreover, SEM showed that drinking (estimate=−0.979, p=0.016) and household income (estimate=0.388, p<0.001) showed direct effect on knowledge. Knowledge has a direct effect on attitude (estimate=0.486, p<0.001) and practice (estimate=0.206, p=0.015). Attitude showed a direct effect on practice (estimate=0.112, p=0.045) (table 3 and figure 2). The results of various fitting indicators showed that the CMIN/DF value of 1.651, which is lower than the reference standard, indicates a good fit of the SEM model. Other indicators like RMSEA, IFI, TLI and CFI had values of 0.036, 0.966, 0.925 and 0.964, respectively, also indicate a good fit of the SEM model (table 4).

Discussion

This study suggested that patients with FGIDs showed moderate knowledge, positive attitude and moderate practice toward their disease. Drinking and household income might have an effect on their KAP. The findings of this study provide valuable insights, both for patients and healthcare providers, to improve the clinical outcomes, compliance and disease management in patients, as well as reduce the disease occurrence.

The analysis of demographic characteristics of FGIDs revealed that residence, education, household income, marital status, smoking, drinking, medical insurance and family history were significantly associated with knowledge scores. This is in line with previous studies that have shown that sociodemographic factors such as education and income level greatly affect the knowledge of individuals about their health conditions.15 Additionally, family history has been shown to play a vital role in creating awareness and knowledge of certain health conditions among family members.26 The study also found that gender, education and smoking were significantly associated with attitude scores. These findings are consistent with previous research that has shown that gender and education greatly influence the attitude of patients towards their health condition.27 Likewise, smoking has been linked to attitudes towards health, with people who smoke often having different perspectives and attitudes towards their own health compared with people who have never smoked.28 The study showed that education, household income, drinking and family history were significantly associated with the practice scores. These results are consistent with previous studies that have shown that sociodemographic factors, such as education and income, can influence individuals’ health-related behaviours and practices.29 Family history has also been found to influence individuals’ health practices, as individuals with a family history of certain conditions may engage in preventive behaviours or lifestyle modifications.30 Overall, these findings support previous literature on the influence of demographic characteristics on KAP scores in patients with FGIDs. These results highlight the importance of considering sociodemographic factors when assessing and addressing patients’ KAPs related to their health conditions. Furthermore, it is important for healthcare providers to tailor educational interventions and support based on these factors to improve patient outcomes and management of FGIDs.

The analysis of KAP scores of patients with FGIDs showed that the majority of patients had varying levels of knowledge on FGIDs. Specifically, patients demonstrated variable performance across knowledge-based questions, achieving a moderate overall knowledge level, with the highest percentage of correct responses being for general symptoms of FGIDs and the lowest for the inheritance of the disease. These findings are consistent with previous literature that has shown patients to have a general understanding of the symptoms of FGIDs but less knowledge about the genetic factors contributing to these disorders.31 32 In terms of attitudes towards FGIDs, the majority of patients agreed that FGIDs are related to diet, disagreed that the symptoms are mild and disagreed that there is a psychological impact. Furthermore, the majority disagreed that FGIDs are easy to treat but believed that aggressive treatment leads to a good prognosis. These attitudes align with previous research that has shown patients to be aware of the impact of diet on FGIDs but have varying perceptions of the severity and treatment options.33 The majority of patients reported actively treating FGIDs, following the guideline of their doctor for regular follow-up, quitting smoking and alcohol and decreasing coffee intake and high-fat diet. However, they reported only sometimes avoiding overeating, rarely using drugs for promoting gastrointestinal motility and usually remaining calm at the onset of symptoms. These mixed findings, with some positive behaviours but inconsistent adherence in other areas, explain the overall moderate practice score. These findings are consistent with the literature that has found patients to engage in self-care practices such as dietary modifications and adherence to medical advice but may struggle with lifestyle changes and symptom management.16 Xiong et al investigated illness perception among patients with FGIDs in China and found that patients’ understanding of their condition significantly influenced their coping strategies and healthcare utilisation.22 While their study focused on illness perception rather than KAP assessment, their findings support our observation that better disease understanding (knowledge) is associated with more appropriate health behaviours (practice). This relationship between cognitive factors and behavioural outcomes aligns with the positive correlations between knowledge and practice observed in our study. Overall, these findings highlight the need for patient education on the genetic factors contributing to FGIDs and the importance of a multidimensional approach to treatment that includes both medical and lifestyle interventions.

Pearson’s correlation analysis showed a positive and moderate correlation between knowledge and attitude, a weak positive correlation between knowledge and practice and a significant weak positive correlation between attitude and practice. These findings suggest that individuals with better knowledge about FGIDs tend to have more positive attitudes towards FGIDs and are more likely to engage in practices related to FGIDs. SEM analysis examined the relationships between four variables (KAP and predictor variables) and found several significant associations. Drinking was negatively associated with knowledge, indicating that individuals who consume alcohol are likely to have lower knowledge about FGIDs, which is in accordance with previous studies.34 On the other hand, household income was positively associated with knowledge, indicating that individuals with higher income tend to have better knowledge about FGIDs, as previously reported.31 Additionally, SEM analysis revealed a strong and significant relationship between knowledge and attitude, suggesting that individuals with improved knowledge about FGIDs tend to have more positive attitudes towards them. There was also a weak but significant relationship between practice and attitude, indicating that individuals who engage in FGID-related practices are more likely to have positive attitudes towards them. Furthermore, knowledge was found to have a positive and significant relationship with practice, suggesting that individuals with better knowledge about FGIDs are more likely to engage in practices related to FGIDs. The various fitting indicators, such as the CMIN/DF value, RMSEA, IFI, TLI and CFI, all indicated a good fit of the SEM model. These findings provide support for the relationships between KAP and predictor variables in understanding FGIDs.

This study has a few limitations. First, this study was conducted in a specific outpatient department, which could lead to selection bias, and the selected patients do not represent the entire population of patients diagnosed with FGIDs. Second, the cross-section design of the study limits the ability to establish causal relationships between variables. Third, the web-based questionnaire may exclude individuals who do not have access to or are not comfortable with online questionnaires, leading to a biased sample. Fourth, the self-reported data from patients may introduce the possibility of recall bias, social desirability bias or misinterpretation of questions. Fifth, the study was conducted within a relatively short period of time, which may not capture seasonal or temporal variations in the prevalence or characteristics of FGIDs. Sixth, this study did not collect information on the specific treatments patients had tried before the survey or how these prior treatments may have influenced their understanding of the disease, which is a potential area for future research. Seventh, the diagnosis of FGIDs was based solely on existing medical records review according to Rome IV criteria, without performing additional clinical investigations specifically for this study. This approach may have introduced diagnostic uncertainty, as some patients with mild organic diseases might have been misclassified as having FGIDs. Future studies should consider incorporating standardised diagnostic procedures to ensure diagnostic accuracy. Eighth, the remuneration offered to participants, while modest, may have introduced a form of selection bias by potentially attracting participants motivated by the payment rather than genuine interest, which may affect the reliability of the study. Finally, this study does not explicitly mention control or adjustment for potential confounding factors that could influence the relationship between KAP dimensions and the studied variables, which may impact the validity of the conclusions drawn.

In conclusion, patients with FGIDs showed moderate knowledge, positive attitude and moderate practice. Drinking and household income might have an effect on their KAP. The findings of this study reveal that while patients have some understanding of FGIDs, there are areas where knowledge gaps exist, particularly regarding the genetic aspects of the disease and consistent implementation of self-management practices, indicating the need for educational interventions and targeted communication to improve public awareness and understanding of the disease.
