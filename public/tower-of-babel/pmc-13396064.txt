Real-World Treatment Switch Rates, and Healthcare Costs of Botulinum Toxin Type A Among Patients with Cervical Dystonia in the USA: A Retrospective Claims Analysis
Authors: Ning Cheng, Annaliza Dominguez, Shivaji R. Manthena, Darshini Shah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionReal-world evidence on comparative treatment patterns and cost is limited for botulinum toxin type A (BoNTA) treatments for cervical dystonia (CD). This study aims to evaluate treatment switch rates, healthcare resource utilization (HCRU), and healthcare costs among patients with cervical dystonia initiating BoNTAs in the USA.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed real-world data from Optum’s de-identified Clinformatics® Data Mart Database between January 1, 2016 and December 31, 2024. Eligible adults with CD with ≥ 12-month continuous enrollment before and after the first BoNTA treatment were included. The first date of BoNTA administration after the first CD identification in the study period was assigned as the index date. Treatment switch rates were assessed during the first 12 months following BoNTA initiation and at anytime before the study period end. Annual healthcare resource use and healthcare costs were also assessed in the 12 months following treatment initiation.ResultsOverall, 1756 adults with CD initiated therapy on BoNTAs. OnabotulinumtoxinA (onabotA) (88.6%; 1555/1756) was utilized most, followed by incobotulinumtoxinA (incobotA) (9.3%; 163/1756), and abobotulinumtoxinA (2.2%; 38/1756). As a result of the small sample size of abobotulinumtoxinA, comparative analysis was focused on onabotA and incobotA. Treatment switch was comparable between onabotA and incobotA during 12-month follow-up. Treatment switch at anytime before study period end was lower for onabotA versus incobotA initiators (2.6% vs. 6.8%; P = 0.007). Annual HCRU and costs were comparable between onabotA and incobotA, except CD-related costs and toxin-related costs were lower for onabotA.ConclusionPatients with CD who initiated therapy on onabotA had comparable switch rates at 12 months but were less likely to switch to another BoNTA at anytime and had lower or comparable costs to incobotA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40120-026-00967-3.

Introduction

Cervical dystonia (CD), also known as spasmodic torticollis, is a rare neurological disorder characterized by involuntary contractions of the neck and upper shoulder muscles, resulting in abnormal postures and/or movements of the neck, shoulder, and head [1–3]. The prevalence of CD in the USA is 390 cases per million [4]. It is more common in women and typically manifests between the ages of 40 and 60 years [4]. CD is often associated with disability, pain, anxiety, and depression, which substantially impact the daily lives and productivity of people with CD, leading to decreased quality of life and increased direct and indirect healthcare costs [5–7].

Botulinum toxin injections are a first-line treatment for CD [8]. The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved onabotulinumtoxinA (onabotA), incobotulinumtoxinA (incobotA), abobotulinumtoxinA (abobotA), daxibotulinumtoxinA-lanm (daxibotA), and rimabotulinumtoxinB (rimabotB) for the treatment of CD in adults [9–12].

Previous studies have demonstrated the efficacy and safety of botulinum toxins for the treatment of CD [13–18]. However, botulinum toxins differ in potency, duration of action, clinical profile, and annual treatment costs [19, 20]. Some patients with CD may switch to a different toxin treatment from the initial treatment due to poor response, change in physician preference, patient preference, or treatment costs [19]. Studies have demonstrated onabotA has enhanced quality of life, achieved high levels of clinician and patient satisfaction, and is associated with significant reductions in healthcare resource utilization [21, 22]. Although decision makers within hospital systems, payors, or clinics may force a BoNT switch, usually for cost-saving purposes, studies on movement disorder treatment indicate that this negatively affects and creates additional burden [23, 24]. Some studies have demonstrated that patients who switch CD treatments incur increased healthcare costs and a lower level of improvement in the disease symptoms [25, 26]. Thus, it is important to understand the treatment patterns, switch rates, and healthcare costs in patients with CD.

Current real-world evidence comparing botulinum toxin type A (BoNTA) treatments in terms of switching, healthcare resource use (HCRU), and costs is limited, highlighting the need for updated comparative data. This evidence may further provide information for physicians and healthcare decision makers in making treatment decisions for CD.

This real-world study used data from Optum’s de-identified Clinformatics® Data Mart Database (Optum® CDM) to evaluate treatment switching, HCRU, and healthcare costs among adults diagnosed with CD in the USA who initiated treatment with a BoNTA.

Methods

Data Source and Study Design

This study was an observational, retrospective, real-world cohort study of administrative claims data from the Optum® CDM database, from January 1, 2016 to December 31, 2024. CDM is a large administrative health claims database that captures patient records annually for commercially insured and Medicare Advantage members. It utilizes medical and pharmacy claims to derive patient-level enrollment information, healthcare costs, and resource utilization information. The population is geographically diverse, spanning all 50 states, and is statistically de-identified under the Expert Determination method, consistent with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), and managed according to the Optum® customer data use agreement.

Patients with a CD diagnosis were identified using the International Classification of Diseases 10th Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM), G24.3, for spasmodic torticollis. BoNTA administration was identified after CD diagnosis through Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System (HCPCS) claim codes. HCPCS codes are outlined in the Supplementary material.

The first date of toxin administration after the first CD identification in the study period was assigned as the index date. The BoNTA administered to patients was defined as the index toxin, including onabotA, abobotA, or incobotA. Each patient had a 12-month (360-day) baseline pre-index period before the index date and a 12-month (360-day) follow-up post-index period included after the index date.

Study Population

The study sample included adult patients (≥ 18 years of age) with at least one claim for onabotA, abobotA, or incobotA after the first CD diagnosis between January 1, 2017 and December 31, 2023. Eligible patients had continuous enrollment of medical coverage for at least 12 months pre- and at least 12 months post-index date.

Patients with any medical claim for any BoNTA during the 12-month pre-index period, and with more than one medical claim on the index date, were excluded. Patients with a diagnosis claim for other toxin-related non-CD indications during the 12-month pre-index period or anytime during the study period were also excluded.

Baseline demographics were collected for each patient on their respective index date. Baseline clinical characteristics and comorbidities were assessed for each patient and based on ICD-10-CM codes during the pre-index period. Since an insufficient sample size (n = 38) was captured for abobotA, this group was excluded from comparative analyses of outcome measures. The article does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Outcome Measures

This study evaluated the real-world treatment switch rates, all-cause, CD-related, and toxin-related HCRU and healthcare costs in adult patients with CD for onabotA and incobotA.

Treatment switching was defined as the presence of a non-index BoNT claim. Treatment switch rate was assessed as the percentage of patients who switched from their CD-related index BoNTA to a CD-related non-index BoNT (onabotA, incobotA, abobotA, and rimabotB) after index date during the 12-month post-index follow-up period and at any time after index through the end of the study period.

Annual all-cause, CD-related, and toxin-related HCRU and costs were evaluated during the 12-month follow-up period after initiation of index toxins (onabotA or incobotA). All the outcome measures were evaluated by the index toxin cohort.

All-cause HCRU and costs included total inpatient admissions, ED visits, and outpatient visits, and their respective costs. CD-related HCRU and costs evaluated medical costs (inpatient admissions, ED visits, outpatient visits) for any visit with a CD diagnosis. Toxin-related costs were determined from visit claims with costs attributed to respective index toxin HCPCS codes indicating administration at inpatient, outpatient, long-term care, and ED visits. Annual toxin administration was described as the mean number of toxin-related visits within a 12-month post-index follow-up period. All costs presented are inflated to 2024 USD using the US Bureau of Labor Statistics consumer price index (CPI) data from the Bureau of Labor Statistics [27].

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive analyses were used to evaluate the study measures, including baseline patient demographics and clinical characteristics, switch rates, costs, and injection frequency. Categorical variables were reported in frequency (n) and percentage (%), whereas continuous variables were reported with mean ± SD. Differences in annual costs were evaluated across index toxins. HCRU is reported as the mean number of visits. Healthcare costs are reported as the mean US dollars (USD). Costs were determined by summing costs associated with each service category for each patient during the post-index period. Costs are reported as total costs, which include all medical costs.

HCRU and corresponding costs were adjusted with the upper and lower 1% of botulinum toxin claims removed to reduce skewing within the wide range of HCRU and costs reported in claims across all therapies. Statistical significance was assessed using a t test (analysis of variance, ANOVA) and chi-square (Fisher’s exact) tests for the continuous and categorical variables, respectively. P values are reported, with statistically significant differences being defined as P < 0.01 or P < 0.001. A multivariable logistic regression model adjusting for the effects of confounders (age, gender, race, region, payer, and CCI) was conducted for patients’ switch status. All analyses were performed using the Instant Health Data (IHD) software (Panalgo, Boston, MA, USA) and R, version 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Study Population and Baseline Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

A total of 1756 patients with CD met the prespecified eligibility criteria (Table 1). The majority of patients initiated BoNTA monotherapy on onabotA (88.6%; 1555/1756), followed by incobotA (9.3%; 163/1756), or abobotA (2.2%; 38/1756). No claims for daxibotA (approved in 2023) were captured in this study due to its brief on-market time at the time this study was conducted. Since abobotA has a small sample size, comparative analysis of treatment switch rates, HCRU, and treatment costs was focused on onabotA and incobotA treatment groups only. In both treatment groups, most patients were white and female (Table 2). The mean (standard deviation (SD) age was 60.7 (14.9) and 62.1 (14.1) years for onabotA and incobotA, respectively. The majority of patients in both groups had Medicare coverage (onabotA 55.1%; incobotA 57.1%). No significant differences in baseline demographic characteristics were observed between the two treatment groups. Treatment Switching

During the 12-month post-index follow-up period, onabotA initiators and incobotA initiators had a similar treatment switch rate (onabotA 1.2%, incobotA 1.2%; P = 1.00) (Fig. 1). Adjusted analyses using the logistic regression model could not be conducted because of low 12-month switch rates in both treatment groups.Fig. 1Proportion of patients who switched† treatment during 12-month and anytime‡ post-index follow-up period for onabotulinumtoxinA (onabotA) and incobotulinumtoxinA (incobotA)

Proportion of patients who switched† treatment during 12-month and anytime‡ post-index follow-up period for onabotulinumtoxinA (onabotA) and incobotulinumtoxinA (incobotA)

Treatment switch rate at anytime during the post-index follow-up period was considerably lower in patients who initiated therapy on onabotA compared to those who initiated therapy on incobotA (onabotA 2.6%, incobotA 6.8%; P = 0.007) (Fig. 1). Among onabotA initiators, 97.4% did not switch to another BoNTA treatment at anytime during the post-index follow-up period. After adjustment for baseline characteristics, patients with CD who initiated incobotA had three times higher odds of switching versus onabotA initiators at anytime during post-index [odds ratio (OR) 3.0, 95% CI 1.2–7.4, P = 0.01]. Discussion

This retrospective analysis compared treatment switching, HCRU, and costs of the two most commonly utilized BoNTAs for CD treatment—onabotA and incobotA. This study demonstrated that most patients with CD who initiated therapy on BoNTA were indexed on onabotA. Patients who were indexed on abobotA were excluded from the focused comparative analysis because of limited sample size; the lower utilization of abobotA is consistent with previous real-world findings for CD treatment [28]. Real-world utilization of BoNTAs among patients with CD demonstrates an imbalance, likely attributable to earlier regulatory approval and longer clinical history of onabotA relative to comparator BoNTAs. The difference in market share is reflected by the unequal sample sizes observed in this present real-world study. Patients who started treatment with onabotA and those who started with incobotA had similar rates of switching to other BoNT treatments during the 12 months of follow-up. However, at any time after the index date before the study period end, onabotA initiators demonstrated lower switch rates compared to incobotA initiators. These results indicate that patients who initiated therapy on onabotA were less likely to switch to other BoNTAs relative to incobotA initiators during their treatment period beyond the initial 12 months. Additionally, during the 12-month post-index follow-up period, onabotA and incobotA initiators had similar total mean annual all-cause, CD-related, and toxin-related visits. While annual all-cause healthcare costs were comparable between onabotA and incobotA initiators, CD-related total medical, inpatient, outpatient, and toxin-related costs were significantly lower for onabotA compared to incobotA during the 12-month post-index period. Real-world treatment switch rates may be helpful to inform healthcare providers when determining which BoNTA to initiate in patients with CD. This study found that while switch rates were comparable between cohorts in the 12 months following initiation, 97.4% of those who initiated therapy on onabotA did not switch at any time after initiation, which expands on findings from previous studies. Similar to the current study, a previous retrospective claims study based on data from Truven MarketScan database and Optum® CDM (2016–2019) demonstrated that patients with CD on onabotA were less likely to switch to another toxin treatment compared to patients who started on incobotA [29]. In patients with CD, dosing of BoNTAs is adjusted based on various clinical factors such as symptom severity and the patient’s response [9–12]. Examining the switch over a longer follow-up period may be appropriate in this indication, where patients with CD may require time to reach optimal treatment.

The economic burden of CD in the USA is substantial, and comparative analyses of treatment patterns and healthcare costs of common treatments are important to healthcare providers, payors, and patients [30, 31]. In this study, the HCRU analyses showed similar all-cause, CD-related, and toxin-related HCRU, suggesting similar monitoring and administration visits among these patients with CD receiving BoNTA treatment. These findings are consistent with and add to the body of evidence from the earlier real-world CD treatment patterns study, which demonstrated comparable injection frequencies across BoNTA toxin cohorts [29]. The cost analyses of this study found lower CD-related and toxin-related costs for onabotA versus incobotA. The higher costs associated with incobotA initiators may be due to several factors. First, several studies indicate incobotA administration doses tend to be higher compared to onabotA for CD [32, 33]. Since BoNTs are reimbursed per unit injected, this may contribute to the higher toxin-related costs for incobotA initiators despite having similar toxin-related visits to onabotA initiators. It is worth noting that baseline differences across treatment cohorts were not adjusted for in these HCRU and cost analyses, which may be further explored in future studies.

Overall, this real-world retrospective study focuses on treatment switching between the two most commonly utilized BoNTAs and may be used in decision-making when determining which BoNT could be the potential treatment option to initiate in patients with CD. These updated real-world data show that more patients with CD initiate therapy on onabotA, and had lower or comparable rates of treatment switching, HCRU, and healthcare costs compared to incobotA initiators. This suggests that starting CD treatment with onabotA may reduce treatment switching without increasing healthcare costs.

Limitations

As a result of the limitation of the claims data, the data may contain misclassified data due to coding errors, which may introduce variability in study outcomes. Insights into the reasons for choice of toxin treatments or disease-related progression, or cause for switching between toxin treatments cannot be inferred in this study, since the administrative claims do not reflect any cause or administered dose. Integrated claims and electronic records data are needed for future analysis. Further, the higher proportion of patients identified in the onabotA group versus other BoNTAs may contribute to selection bias in the study. However, adjusted analysis for switch rates was conducted to accommodate this.

Conclusions

A higher proportion of adult patients with CD initiated therapy on onabotA compared to other BoNTA treatments. Switch rates during the 12-month follow-up period were comparable between the two most commonly utilized BoNTAs, onabotA and incobotA; however, patients were less likely to switch from onabotA compared to incobotA when the follow-up period was extended beyond 12 months. Overall, HCRU and healthcare costs were similar between the two BoNTAs; however, CD-related and toxin-related costs were significantly lower for onabotA initiators versus incobotA initiators. These results suggest that initiating treatment with onabotA may decrease the treatment switching and be associated with lower healthcare costs for patients with CD.
