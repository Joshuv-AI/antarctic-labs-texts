Highly Sensitive Ce-Doped ZnO Nanocomposite Sensor for Detecting Lithium Battery Leakage
Authors: Yutong Guo, Guozheng Zhu, Ru Wang, Xinran Wei, Zhijie Xu, Hongmin Zhu, Fanli Meng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study clarifies the detection process of the Ce–ZnO sensor for ethyl methyl carbonate (EMC) gas. This study presents new opportunities for enhancing zinc oxide (ZnO) sensors and identifying lithium battery leaks. This study utilized a hydrothermal technique to synthesize Ce-doped ZnO nanosheets and examined the gas-sensing efficacy of ZnO nanosheets with varying amounts of Ce doping. In comparison to pure ZnO sensors, the sensor with 0.5 atom % Ce doping enhanced the response value to EMC gas from 1.49 to 9.86 and reduced the ideal working temperature by 30 °C. The detection limit was decreased to 50 ppb, and the response/recovery time also was shortened compared to pure ZnO. This sensor has exceptional stability and selectivity for EMC gas. This research elucidates the detection mechanism of the Ce–ZnO sensor for EMC gas and introduces novel opportunities for enhancing ZnO sensors and identifying lithium battery leaks.

Introduction

The optimal choice for electric power systems is lithium-ion batteries (LIBs), which have benefits including enhanced amounts of energy, little self-discharge, extended lifespan of cycles, and lack of memory effects. Nonetheless, the leakage of electrode materials and electrolyte solutions from batteries can easily initiate spontaneous combustion, resulting in the emission of substantial amounts of hazardous vapors and liquids that may lead to extensive fires. Moreover, the leaking electrolyte interacts with multiple atmospheric components, resulting in the formation of hazardous chemicals such as hydrofluoric acid and lithium hydroxide. The chemical and electrochemical corrosion of battery components due to electrolyte interactions results in diminished performance of lithium batteries. − Consequently, to construct extremely secure lithium-ion battery systems, we employ proactive diagnostics via the real-time monitoring of electrolyte leaks. This method seeks to detect early indicators of failure, offering a crucial opportunity for prompt intervention to prevent thermal runaway chain reactions and therefore preserving battery performance and safety during its full lifespan. Battery Management Systems (BMS) that identify leakage signals, including electrolyte vapors such as ethyl methyl carbonate (EMC), dimethyl carbonate (DMC), and diethyl carbonate (DEC), , have emerged as the preferred option for monitoring due to their early warning capabilities. Research indicates that gas sensors employing metallic oxide semiconductor architectures are among the finest feasible choices due to their swift reaction time, high sensitivity, cost efficiency, and ease of integration with building management systems. In recent decades, numerous semiconductor metal oxides, including Fe3O4@uio66, SnO2–HOC, NiO/Si–NiWO4/WO3, Al2O3/CuO, Co/Pb–SnO, WO3 , and so on, have been identified as appropriate materials for the development of gas sensors to detect LIB leakage. Nonetheless, these sensors have limitations, including excessively elevated working temperatures, diminished reaction sensitivity, and elevated detection thresholds, hindering the early detection of trace electrolyte losses.

As one of the earliest identified semiconductor metal oxides, zinc oxide (ZnO) continues to be the most prevalent oxide gas-sensitive material, ascribed to its elevated electron mobility during operational conditions and exceptional chemical and thermal stability. Metal doping (Ca, Mg, Pd, Al) further augments its bandgap, thermal tolerance, sensitivity, and stability. Electrolyte constituents (lithium salts, solvents, compounds, etc.) exhibit thermal compatibility, leading to the widespread utilization of EMC, which exhibits appropriate viscosity and an expanded liquid range in comparison to DMC and DEC in various LIBs. Ce-doped ZnO materials were synthesized using the hydrothermal technique in our investigation. The elemental distribution, morphological features, and phase composition of the synthesized materials were meticulously analyzed through XRD, SEM, and XPS investigations. The 0.5 atom % Ce-doped ZnO sensor exhibited exceptional reaction characteristics toward EMC, capable of detecting EMC at the parts per billion level, as demonstrated by the comparison and assessment of the gas detection reactions of the synthesized samples to EMC. This sensor possesses a rapid reaction time and elevated sensitivity, facilitating real-time surveillance of EMC gas produced by LIB leakage and improving lithium battery safety.

Experimental Section

Preparation

The chemical reagents used in this experiment are pure enough for analytical purposes and do not need to be cleaned up anymore.

To make pure ZnO, 30 mL of water was put in a beaker, and then 20 mmol of CO­(NH2)2 (urea, purity ≥99%, Shandong Xiya Chemical Co., Ltd., Shandong Province, China) and 10 mmol of Zn­(CH3COO)2·2H2O (zinc acetate dihydrate, purity ≥99%, Shandong Xiya Chemical Co., Ltd., Shandong Province, China) were added. For 10 min, the solution was agitated hard until it was clear and translucent. The mixture was placed into a 50 mL pressurized PTFE bioreactor and incubated in an oven at 120 °C for 5 h. This gathered material was then purified using anhydrous ethanol and deionized water sequentially, centrifuged several times, and subsequently dried in a heated oven at 80 °C for 24 h. Finally, it was heated in a muffle furnace at a rate of 2 °C per minute to 400 °C, with a calcination time of 2 h to yield ZnO, which is henceforth referred to as pure ZnO.

To make Ce-doped ZnO, 30 mL of water was added to a beaker; then 20 mmol of CO­(NH2)2 and 10 mmol of Zn­(CH3COO)2·2H2O were added to make Ce-doped ZnO. At the same time, 0.2, 0.1, 0.05, or 0.02 mmol of Ce­(NO3)3·6H2O (cerium nitrate hexahydrate, purity ≥99%, Shandong Xiya Chemical Co., Ltd., Shandong Province, China) was added. To get a clear, translucent consistency, the solution was stirred hard for 10 min. The solution was placed in a 50 mL pressurized PTFE bioreactor and left in a heated oven at 120 °C for 5 h. Then, the sample was centrifuged several times and cleaned with anhydrous ethanol and deionized water. After that, the extracted material was placed in an oven at 80 °C for 24 h. Finally, it was heated in a muffle furnace at a rate of 2 °C/min to 400 °C, with a calcination time of 2 h. This technique made ZnO that was doped with 0.2, 0.1, 0.05, or 0.02 mmol of Ce. For the sake of this research, they are called 2 at% Ce–ZnO, 1 at% Ce–ZnO, 0.5 at% Ce–ZnO, and 0.2 at% Ce–ZnO. The temperature of the test environment was 25 °C, with a humidity level of 25% RH.

Characterization

The crystalline structure of the sample was examined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.1542 nm). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was employed to investigate the sample microstructure and surface characteristics, operating at an acceleration voltage of 2 kV and achieving a resolution of 3.5 nm. An EDS spectrometer with a Bruker XFlash 6|60 detector was utilized for scanning analysis to evaluate the elemental composition and distribution patterns of the material. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) utilizing Al Kα radiation (Thermo Fisher, UK) was concurrently applied to examine the valence states and surface elements. The binding energy of the C 1s peak was standardized at 284.8 eV.

Sensor Manufacturing and Gas Sensing Measurement

The gas sensor preparation method has two stages: (1) Dispersing the sample in ethanol via ultrasonic dispersion to create a homogeneous slurry; (2) employing a 1–10 μL pipet to uniformly apply the ethanol slurry onto the exterior of the ceramic tube. The principal sensing component comprises a ceramic tube and a nickel–chromium alloy heating wire. Two parallel gold electrodes and four platinum conductors are present in the ceramic tube. The operating temperature is supplied by a nickel–chromium alloy coil that passes through the ceramic tube. For the purpose of detecting resistance variations, the platinum wires are connected to a precision measurement and source unit (SMU, BC02A), while the alloy coil is connected to a direct current heating power source during the testing phase. Gas Sensing Characteristics

For MOS gas sensors, the optimal operating temperature is typically determined by the material’s chemical and electrical properties. An increase in the gas’s activation energy from a low to an optimal value speeds up the interaction with oxygen adsorbed on the surface of the substance. When the temperatures surpass the optimal range, the activation energy of the gas becomes exceedingly high. The internal dynamics of charge carriers are disrupted, the adsorption of gas molecules is impeded, and the diffusion between the gas and adsorbed oxygen is reduced. As a result, the sensor’s overall reaction is reduced. ,, In order to ascertain the most effective operating temperature for EMC detection, we selected the range of 150–350 °C and evaluated the sensor’s reaction to 100 ppm EMC at various temperatures. The pure ZnO sensor’s reaction value increases rapidly as the temperature increases, reaching a maximum of 1.49 at 280 °C before progressively decreasing, as illustrated in Figure a. For ZnO sensors doped with varying concentrations of Ce, the sensor reaction values increased with rising temperature, peaking at 250 °C before gradually decreasing. Sensors doped with varying Ce concentrations exhibited higher EMC reactions than those of the pure ZnO sensor at every temperature. The pure ZnO sensor was considerably outperformed by the 0.5 atom % Ce–ZnO sensor, which exhibited the highest reaction values at all temperatures, reaching 9.86 at 250 °C. Sensors doped with varying Ce concentrations exhibited higher EMC reactions than the pure ZnO sensor at every temperature. This suggests that Ce doping improves the performance of ZnO EMC sensors, with the 0.5 at% doping concentration exhibiting the most significant enhancement and providing superior advantages for the detection of lithium battery leaks. The optimal operating temperature for every sensor was used for subsequent gas-sensing tests.

Gas Sensor Mechanism

Primarily, the operational principle behind metal oxide semiconductor gas sensors is derived from chemical reactions between the target gases and surface molecules. Charge transfer occurs on the material surface, inducing changes in material resistance and thereby generating a reaction. ZnO is a quintessential n-type semiconductor material. As illustrated in Figure a,b, stable oxygen anions (O–) are generated as the sensor is functional in air at a predetermined working temperature; oxygen molecules chemically attach onto the surface of the ZnO and pull electrons out of its conduction band. The sensor shows high resistance as a result of the simultaneous formation of a low-electron-density space charge layer close to the ZnO surface. − The following equations explain the reaction process 4 O2(g)→O2(abs) 5 O2−(abs)+e−→2O−(abs)(100°C<T<300°C) 6 O2−(abs)+e−→2O−(abs)(100°C<T<300°C) 7 O−(abs)+e−→O2−(abs)(T>300°C)

The target gas experiences redox reactions with adsorbed oxygen anions upon exposure to the reducing gas EMC, generating and reintegrating captured electrons into the conduction band. Additionally, the 0.5 at. % Ce–ZnO gas sensor exhibits a superior gas-sensing reaction to EMC gas. This enhanced performance is primarily attributed to the 0.5 at% Ce doping, which improves the electronic structure and defect characteristics of pure ZnO. By increasing the quantity of sites of activity on the material’s surface, this alteration efficiently encourages the chemical sorption of oxygen species. XPS analysis indicates that 0.5 atom % Ce–ZnO exhibits a higher oxygen vacancy concentration than pure ZnO, strongly supporting the aforementioned conclusion.

Conclusions

In this paper, the hydrothermal approach was used to synthesize Ce-doped ZnO sensors. The ZnO sensor doped with 0.5 at% Ce at 250 °C demonstrated exceptional EMC sensing capabilities. When exposed to EMC as low as 50 ppb, the sensor showed a response of 1.03. High stability and EMC selectivity were demonstrated by the sensor response, which reached 9.86 at 100 ppm EMC with a reaction time of roughly 43 s and a recovery time of roughly 26 s. Additionally, we clarified the gas-sensing process that underlies the Ce-doped sensor’s improved responsiveness. In conclusion, the findings show that our sensor is a potential Ce-doped ZnO EMC gas sensor that is portable, low-cost, and high-performance. It provides a new method for identifying LIB electrolyte leakage.
