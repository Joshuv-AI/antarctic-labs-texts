Ergodic averages for commutative transformations along return times Thanks: The first author was partially funded by ANID/Fondecyt/1241346 and Centro de Modelamiento Matemático (CMM) FB210005, BASAL funds for centers of excellence from ANID-Chile.
Sebastián Donoso, Sovanlal Mondal, Vicente Saavedra-Araya

Abstract
. In this paper, we extend recent results on the convergence of ergodic averages along sequences generated by return times to shrinking targets in rapidly mixing systems, partially answering questions posed by the first author, Maass and the third author in [ 6 ] . In particular, for a fixed parameter a ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) a\in(0,1) and for generic y ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] y\in[0,1] , we establish both L 2 L^{2} and pointwise convergence for single averages and multiple averages for commuting transformations along the sequences ( a n ​ ( y ) ) n ∈ ℕ (a_{n}(y))_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}} , obtained by arranging the set { n ∈ ℕ : 0 < 2 n ​ y mod 1 < n − a } \Big\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:0<2^{n}y\mod{1}<n^{-a}\Big\} in an increasing order. We also obtain new results for semi-random ergodic averages along sequences of similar type.

1.
Introduction

A
measure preserving system
(m.p.s. for short) is a tuple
(X,\mathcal{X},\mu,T)
where
(X,\mathcal{X},\mu)
is a probability space and
T\colon X\to X
is a
measure preserving transformation
. That is,
T
is measurable with respect to
\mathcal{X}
and
\mu(T^{-1}A)=\mu(A)
for all
A\in\mathcal{X}
. The m.p.s.
(X,\mathcal{X},\mu,T)
is ergodic if every set in the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{I}(T):=\{A\in\mathcal{X}:\ \mu(A\Delta T^{-1}A)=0\}
has either zero or full measure.

Two fundamental theorems in ergodic theory are von Neumann’s mean ergodic theorem and Birkhoff’s pointwise ergodic theorem. The mean ergodic theorem asserts that for any measure-preserving system
(X,\mathcal{X},\mu,T)
and for any function
f\in L^{2}
, the
time averages

(1/N)\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(T^{n}x)
converge in
L^{2}
to
\mathbb{E}(f|\mathcal{I}(T))
.
If the system is ergodic, then the above limit becomes the corresponding
space average

\int_{X}fd\mu
. The pointwise ergodic theorem upgrades the mode of convergence to the pointwise (
\mu
-almost everywhere) setting.
Following Furstenberg’s ergodic-theoretic proof of Szemerédi’s theorem
[
10
]
in the late 1970s, a major line of research in ergodic theory has focused on the study of
multiple ergodic averages
. These expressions generalize the classical von Neumann and Birkhoff ergodic theorems to averages of the form:

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\prod_{j=1}^{k}f_{j}(T_{i}^{a_{j}(n)}x)

where
(X,\mathcal{X},\mu,T_{i})
is a m.p.s., the transformations
T_{i}
commute, and
f_{1},\dots,f_{k}
are bounded measurable functions. Regarding the
L^{2}
-convergence of these averages, we refer the reader to the book by Host and Kra
[
11
]
for a comprehensive overview of the area, as well as Frantzikinakis’ open problems in the field
[
7
]
(and their updates on Frantzikinakis’ website). Regarding pointwise convergence, recent substantial progress was made by several authors using harmonic analysis
[
13
,
15
]
, particularly regarding polynomial sequences of different degrees. For further details about pointwise convergence of ergodic averages, we refer the reader to the book by Krause
[
14
]
.

Our main results in this paper are Theorem
A
,
B
,
C
and
D
, where we establish convergence of multiple averages along random sequences in different settings.
The study of ergodic averages along random sequences was first considered by Bourgain in
[
2
]
. Before we state the result, let us introduce some terminology.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space and let
(X_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a sequence of random variables defined in this space taking values in
\{0,1\}
such that
{\mathbb{P}}(X_{n}=1)=n^{-a}
for some
a>0
. For
\omega\in\Omega
such that
X_{m}(\omega)=1
for infinitely many
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the
random sequence of integers generated by
(X_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
as

(1.1)

a_{n}(\omega):=\inf\Big\{k\in{\mathbb{N}}:\ X_{1}(\omega)+\cdots+X_{k}(\omega)=n\Big\}.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
p\geq 1
. A sequence of integers
(a_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is said to be
pointwise
L^{p}
-good
if for every m.p.s.
(X,\mathcal{X},\mu,T)
and every function
f\in L^{p}
,
the ergodic averages

\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(T^{a_{n}}x)

converges for
\mu
-almost every
x\in X
.

Under this terminology, Bourgain in
[
2
]
proved the following result.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space, and let
(X_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a sequence of independent random variables, taking values in
\{0,1\}
, such that
\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=1)=n^{-a}
for some
a\in(0,1)
. Then, if
p>1
, for
\mathbb{P}
-almost every
\omega\in\Omega
, the random sequence of integers
(a_{n}(\omega))_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is pointwise
L^{p}
-good.

It is known from
[
12
, Theorem C]
that the above theorem does not hold when
a\geq 1
. See also
[
20
]
for a finer result in this direction. From now onwards, we will confine our discussion to
a\in(0,1)
.

In
[
17
]
, LaVictoire extended the above theorem to
p=1
if
a\in(0,1/2).
Due to the Law of Large Numbers, for
\mathbb{P}
-almost every
\omega\in\Omega
,
a_{n}(\omega)/n^{1/(1-a)}
converges to a non-zero constant. Hence, the random sequence of integers
(a_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
can be understood as a random version of
\lfloor n^{c}\rfloor
for
c:=\frac{1}{1-a}.
It is worth mentioning that the deterministic case has been studied recently by Krause and Sun
[
16
]
, showing that
\lfloor n^{c}\rfloor
is pointwise
L^{1}
-good whenever

, extending previous work of Urban and Zienkiewicz
[
23
]
and Mirek
[
19
]

Regarding multiple averages, the random sequences framework has proven to be a powerful approach, providing pointwise results in cases where the corresponding deterministic setting remains out of reach. An interesting result in this direction was provided by Frantzikinakis, Lesigne and Wierdl in
[
8
]
.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space, let
(X_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a sequence of independent random variables, taking values in
\{0,1\}
, such that
\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=1)=n^{-a}
for some
a\in(0,1/2)
, and let
(a_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be the random sequence of integers generated by

Then, for
\mathbb{P}
-almost every
\omega\in\Omega
, the following holds: For every probability space
(X,\mathcal{X},\mu)
, every pair of commuting measure-preserving transformations
T_{1},T_{2}:X\to X

, the averages

(1.4)

\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f_{1}\circ T_{1}^{a_{n}(\omega)}\cdot f_{2}\circ T_{2}^{a_{n}(\omega)}

, and their limit coincides with the

-limit of

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f_{1}\circ T_{1}^{n}\cdot f_{2}\circ T_{2}^{n}.

If
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are powers of the same transformation, the convergence can be improved to be pointwise.

This result was later extended to several transformations by Frantzikinakis, Lesigne and Wierdl in
[

Other type of multiple averages studied by Frantzikinakis, Lesigne and Wierdl are the so-called
semi-random ergodic averages

Theorem 1.7

(
[
8

)

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space, let
(X_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}

\{0,1\}
, such that
\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=1)=n^{-a}

\mathbb{P}

, every pair of commuting measure-preserving transformations

\lim_{N\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f_{1}(T_{1}^{n}x)f_{2}(T_{2}^{a_{n}(\omega)}x)=\mathbb{E}(f_{1}|\mathcal{I}(T_{1}))(x)\mathbb{E}(f_{2}|\mathcal{I}(T_{2}))(x)

A natural question is whether a similar result can be obtained when the random variables
(X_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
satisfy a weaker notion of independence. In particular, we focus in the case where the random variables arise as hitting times in rapidly mixing systems, which was the context studied in
[
6
]
for single averages. Before stating our results, we introduce some terminology.

For a function
f\colon[0,1]\subseteq\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}

\|f\|_{\operatorname{BV}}:=\displaystyle\|f\|_{\infty}+\sup_{0=x_{0}<x_{1}<\cdots<x_{m}=1}\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}|f(x_{i+1})-f(x_{i})|,

where the supremum is taken over all the finite sequences
0=x_{0}<x_{1}<\cdots<x_{m}=1
of

f
has bounded variation if
\|f\|_{BV}<\infty

Definition 1.8
.

Let
Y:=([0,1],\mathcal{B}([0,1]),\nu,S)
be a m.p.s. We say that the system

has a
decay of correlation for functions of bounded variation against

with rate

if there exists

such that

\left|\operatorname{Cov}(f,g\circ S^{m})\right|=\left|\int_{[0,1]}f\cdot g\circ S^{m}d\nu-\int_{[0,1]}fd\nu\int_{[0,1]}gd\nu\right|\leq C\rho(m)\|f\|_{\operatorname{BV}}\|g\|_{L^{1}}

for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
f
with bounded variation and
g\in L^{1}(\nu)
.

If the system
([0,1],\mathcal{B}([0,1]),\nu,S)
has a fast decay of correlation, it was shown in
[
6

that, for
\nu
-almost every

\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:\ S^{n}y\in(0,n^{-a})\}

is pointwise
L^{2}
-good. In this direction, we obtain the following semi-random ergodic theorem.

Let
([0,1],\mathcal{B}([0,1]),\nu,S)
be a measure-preserving system with decay of correlation for functions with bounded variation against
L^{1}

\nu

(X,\mathcal{X},\mu)

2.
Preliminaries

The proof of our results follows the strategy of Frantzikinakis, Wierdl, and Lesigne
[
8
,
9
]
for random sequences constructed from independent random variables, although in our setting we must adapt the approach to account for the lack of independence.

A key ingredient in the proof is the so-called
lacunary trick
. This widely used strategy relies on the fact that convergence of averages along suitably chosen lacunary subsequences is sufficient to establish convergence of the corresponding ergodic averages. This is formalized in the following result, whose proof can be found in

[
8
, Corollary A.2]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space. For every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, consider a non-negative measurable function
f_{n}\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, and let
(W_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be an increasing sequence of positive numbers such that

(2.1)

\lim_{\gamma\to 1^{+}}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{W_{\lfloor\gamma^{n+1}\rfloor}}{W_{\lfloor\gamma^{n}\rfloor}}=1.

For
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\omega\in\Omega
, define

A_{N}(\omega):=\dfrac{1}{W_{N}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f_{n}(\omega).

Assume that there exist a function
f\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
and a real-valued sequence
(\gamma_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
1<\gamma_{k}<+\infty
,
\gamma_{k}\to 1
as
k\to\infty
, and for all
k\in\mathbb{N}

\lim_{N\to\infty}A_{\lfloor\gamma_{k}^{N}\rfloor}(\omega)=f(\omega)

for
\mathbb{P}
-almost every
\omega\in\Omega
.
Then,

\lim_{N\to\infty}A_{N}(\omega)=f(\omega)

for
\mathbb{P}
-almost every
\omega\in\Omega
.

If we consider a random sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
generated by
\{0,1\}
-valued random
variables
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
(see Definition
1.1
), then the averages

(2.2)

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f\bigl(T^{a_{n}(\omega)}x\bigr)\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{\sum_{n=1}^{N}X_{n}(\omega)}\sum_{n=1}^{N}X_{n}(\omega)\,f(T^{n}x)

have the same limiting behavior. A similar equivalence holds for the other types of averages considered in this paper.

We use the following lemma, proved in
[
6
, Lemma 2.3]
,
to understand the behavior of
\sum_{n=1}^{N}X_{n}(\omega)
in our non-independent setting and, in particular, to show that the
sequence
(a_{n}(\omega))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is well defined almost surely.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space and
(X_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a sequence of uniformly bounded and non-negative random variables defined on it. Suppose that there exist
a\in(0,1)
and
c>0
such that
\mathbb{E}(X_{n})=cn^{-a}
and
\varepsilon>0
such that

\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{m=n+1}^{N}\mathrm{Cov}(X_{n},X_{m})\ll N^{2-2a-\varepsilon}.

Then,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{W_{N}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}X_{n}(\omega)=1

for almost every
\omega\in\Omega
, where
W_{N}:=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}(X_{n})
.

Hence, the study of the limiting behavior of (
2.2
) can be reduced to understanding

\frac{1}{W_{N}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}X_{n}(\omega)\,f(T^{n}x),

where
W_{N}:=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}(X_{n})
. Throughout this paper,
W_{N}
behaves like
N^{1-a}
for
some
a\in(0,1)
. Therefore, we may take advantage of Lemma
2.1
to reduce the
problem to the study of lacunary subsequences.

Another classical tool we use is the following version of the
van der Corput trick
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
V
be an inner product space, let
N\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
v_{1},\dots,v_{N}\in V
.
Then, for every integer
M
with
1\leq M\leq N
, we have

\left\|\sum_{n=1}^{N}v_{n}\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{2N}{M}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\|v_{n}\|^{2}+\frac{4N}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N-m}\langle v_{n+m},v_{n}\rangle\right|.

We now introduce some notation which will be used throughout the paper.

Notation

•

Let
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be sequences of positive real numbers.
We write
a_{n}\ll b_{n}
if and only if there exists a constant
C>0
, independent of
n
, such that

a_{n}\leq Cb_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
We also write
a_{n}\sim b_{n}
if
a_{n}\ll b_{n}
and
b_{n}\ll a_{n}
.

•

For a positive integer
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

[n]:=\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
.

•

\bigsqcup
denotes
disjoint
union.

Finally, we will use the following lemma which is a minor modification of
[
8
, Lemma 3.3]
. For completeness, we provide a proof here.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\left(s(n)\right)
be a sequence of positive integers be such that
s(n)\leq n^{2}
and
(Z_{m,n})_{m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a family of random variables, uniformly bounded by
1
, with mean zero. Assume that
\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{N}}
is such that for every fixed
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
the random variables
\{Z_{m,n}:n\in\Lambda\}
are independent. Further, let
(\rho_{n})
be a sequence of positive numbers and
N_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that

(2.3)

\sup_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}(\operatorname{Var}(Z_{m,n}))\leq\rho_{n}\text{ for all $n\in{\mathbb{N}}$ and }\frac{\log N}{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\rho_{n}}\leq\dfrac{1}{7}\text{ for all $N\geq N_{0}$}.

Then, there exists a universal constant
A
such that for every
N\geq N_{0}
and
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have

(2.4)

\mathbb{P}\left(M_{m,N}\geq A\sqrt{R_{N}\log N}\right)\leq\frac{2}{N^{4}},

where

(2.5)

M_{m,N}=\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|\sum_{n\in[N]\cap\Lambda}Z_{m,n}\cdot e(s(n)t)\right|

and
R_{N}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\rho_{n}
.

Proof.

Fix
m\geq 1
. It will be sufficient to prove the desired estimate for

(2.6)

M_{m,N}=\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|P_{m,N}(t)\right|,

where

(2.7)

P_{m,N}(t)=\sum_{[N]\cap\Lambda}Z_{m,n}\cdot\cos(2\pi s(n)t)

In a similar way, one gets an estimate with
\sin(2\pi s(n)t)
in place of
\cos(2\pi s(n)t)
.

Since
|Z_{m,n}|\leq 1
and
\mathbb{E}_{\omega}(Z_{m,n})=0,
by
[
22
, Lemma 1.7]
, we have
\mathbb{E}_{\omega}(e^{\lambda Z_{m,n}})\leq e^{\lambda^{2}\text{Var}(Z_{m,n})}
for all
\lambda\in[-1,1]
. Hence, for every
m\in[N],\lambda\in[-1,1],
and
t\in[0,1]
, we get that

(2.8)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\omega}(e^{\lambda P_{m,N}(t)})

\displaystyle=\prod_{n\in[N]\cap\Lambda}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}(Z_{m,n}\cdot\cos\left(2\pi s(n)t)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{n\in[N]\cap\Lambda}e^{(\lambda\cos 2\pi s(n)t)^{2}\text{Var}(Z_{m,n})}

\displaystyle\leq e^{\lambda^{2}R_{N}},

Since
s(n)\leq n^{2}
, by using the mean value theorem, one can see that there exist random intervals
I_{m,N}
of length
|I_{m,N}|\geq\frac{1}{N^{3}}
such that
|P_{m,N}(t)|\geq\frac{M_{m,N}}{2}
for every
t\in I_{m,N}
. Using this we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\omega}(e^{\lambda M_{m,N}(t)}/2)

\displaystyle\leq N^{3}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left(\int_{I_{m,N}}(e^{\lambda P_{m,N}(t)}+e^{-\lambda P_{m,N}(t)})dt\right)

\displaystyle\leq N^{3}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left(\int_{[0,1]}(e^{\lambda P_{m,N}(t)}+e^{-\lambda P_{m,N}(t)})dt\right)

\displaystyle=N^{3}\cdot\int_{[0,1]}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left(e^{\lambda P_{m,N}(t)}+e^{-\lambda P_{m,N}(t)}\right)dt

\displaystyle\leq 2N^{3}e^{\lambda^{2}R_{N}}.

The last inequality follows from (
2.8
).

Therefore, we have

(2.9)

\mathbb{E}_{\omega}(e^{\lambda M_{m,N}/2})\leq 2N^{3}\cdot e^{\lambda^{2}R_{N}}.

Rewriting the above expression we get

(2.10)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left(e^{\lambda/2\left(M_{m,N}-2R_{N}\lambda-2\log(N^{7})\lambda^{-1}\right)}\right)\leq\frac{2}{N^{4}}.

This implies that

(2.11)

\mathbb{P}\left(M_{m,N}\geq 2R_{N}\lambda+14\log N\cdot\lambda^{-1}\right)\leq\frac{2}{N^{4}}.

The above inequality is true for all
\lambda\in[-1,1]
. Note that the function
f(\lambda)=2R_{N}\lambda+14\log N\cdot\lambda^{-1}
attains a minimum
\sqrt{28R_{N}\log N}
at
\lambda=\sqrt{\frac{7\log N}{R_{N}}}
. By the given condition,
\sqrt{\frac{7\log N}{R_{N}}}<1
for all
N\geq N_{0}
. Plugging in
\lambda=\sqrt{\frac{7\log N}{R_{N}}}
in (
2.11
), we get the desired conclusion.
∎

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
Y_{n}
be a sequence of
\{0,1\}
-valued independent random variables with
\mathbb{P}(Y_{n}=1)=\tau_{n}
and
\mathbb{P}(Y_{n}=0)=1-\tau_{n}
. Suppose

(2.12)

Y^{\prime}_{n}=Y_{n}-\tau_{n},\quad T(N)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\tau(n)\text{ and }P_{N}(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}Y^{\prime}_{n}e(nt).

Suppose that
\frac{\log N}{T(N)}\leq\frac{1}{7}
for
N\geq N_{0}
. Then there is a universal constant
C>0
such that for every
N\geq N_{0}
, we have

(2.13)

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|P_{N}(t)|\geq C\sqrt{T(N)\log N}\right)\leq\frac{2}{N^{4}}.

Proof.

Observe that
Y_{n}^{\prime}
satisfies the hypothesis of the above theorem with
\rho_{n}=\tau_{n}
.
∎

3.
Proof of Theorem
A
and
B

3.1.
Heuristic idea of the proofs

Firstly, note that the averages

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f_{1}(T_{1}^{a_{n}(y)}x)f_{2}(T_{2}^{a_{n}(y)}x)\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sigma_{n}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}X_{n}(y)f_{1}(T_{1}^{n}x)f_{2}(T_{2}^{n}x)

have the same limiting behavior. On the other hand, from
[
5
]
, we know that

\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f_{1}(T_{1}^{n}x)f_{2}(T_{2}^{n}x)

converges in mean, and the convergence is pointwise if
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are powers of the same transformation
[
3
]
. Therefore, it suffices to show that for
\lambda
-almost every
y\in[0,1]
(independent of the measure-preserving system),

(3.1)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sigma_{n}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(X_{n}(y)-\sigma_{n})f_{1}(T_{1}^{n}x)f_{2}(T_{2}^{n}x)=0

for
\mu
-almost every
x\in X
, where
\sigma_{n}:=\mathbb{E}(X_{n})=n^{-a}
. To show (
3.1
), we will approximate
X_{n}
by
X_{n}^{\prime}
in such a way so that

(i)

X_{n_{k}}^{\prime}
are mutually independent random variables whenever
n_{k}
’s are far away from each other, and

(ii)

for
\lambda
-almost every
y
,
(X_{n}(y))_{n}
and
(X_{n}^{\prime}(y))_{n}
generate essentially the same random sequence.

Based on these properties, we will partition the given random ergodic average into a small number of pieces so that each piece becomes a random ergodic average generated by
independent
random variables. For the independent case, some techniques are already known for showing convergence (for instance, see Theorem
1.5
). We will use them to get our desired results.

3.2.
Approximation of the given intervals with suitable dyadic intervals

Let
I_{n}=(0,n^{-a})
.
For each
n^{-a}
, let
k
be the positive integer such that
r^{-(k+1)}<n^{-a}\leq r^{-{k}}
. Use this to define a
r
-adic rational
\gamma_{n}
with denominator at most
r^{{{\frac{2k}{a}}}}
and such that

(3.2)

0<(\gamma_{n}-n^{-a})\leq\frac{1}{r^{{\frac{2k}{a}}}}.

Also, we make sure that the chosen sequence
(\gamma_{n})
is non-increasing.

Define

(3.3)

J_{n}=\Big\{y\in[0,1]:r^{n}y\mod 1\in(0,\gamma_{n})\Big\}

In the following lemmas, we will show that
J_{n}
have some nice properties.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
N\geq 4
be an integer. For any positive integer
k\geq 2
and every finite sequence of positive integers
n_{1}<n_{2}<n_{3}<\cdots<n_{k}\leq N
satisfying
n_{i}-n_{i-1}>2\log N
for
i=2,3,\cdots,k
, we have

(3.4)

\lambda\Big(\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}J_{n_{i}}\Big)=\lambda(J_{n_{1}})\lambda(J_{n_{2}})\cdots\lambda(J_{n_{k}}).

Proof.

Note that for every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

(3.5)

J_{n}=\bigsqcup_{l=0}^{r^{n}-1}\left(\dfrac{l}{r^{n}}+\dfrac{1}{r^{n}}(0,\gamma_{n})\right):=\bigsqcup_{l=0}^{r^{n}-1}J_{l,n}.

Thus,
J_{n}

is a periodic set with period
\frac{1}{r^{n}}
, and each of its subinterval
J_{l,n}
has length
\frac{\gamma_{n}}{r^{n}}
. Also, for each
l\in\left\{0,1,\cdots,r^{n}-1\right\}
, we define
I_{l,n}
to be one full period cycle of
J_{n}
, that is,
I_{l,n}:=\left(\dfrac{l}{r^{n}},\dfrac{l+1}{r^{n}}\right)
. It will be sufficient to show that

(3.6)

\lambda\left(I_{l,n_{1}}\cap\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}J_{n_{i}}\right)\right)=\lambda(J_{l,n_{1}})\cdot\prod_{i=2}^{k}\lambda(J_{n_{i}}).

Indeed, summing over
l
in its range, we will get

(3.7)

\lambda\left([0,1]\cap\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}J_{n_{i}}\right)\right)=\sum_{l=0}^{r^{n}-1}\lambda(J_{l,n_{1}})\cdot\prod_{i=2}^{k}\lambda(J_{n_{i}})=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\lambda(J_{n_{i}}).

We will establish (
3.6
) by induction on the number of indices. Before that, let us make the following observation. By definition,
\gamma_{n}
is a rational number of the form
\frac{s}{p^{t}}
for some
s,t\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
t\leq 2\log n
. Since for
1\leq i<k-1
,
n_{i+1}>n_{i}+2\log N\geq n_{i}+2\log n_{i}\geq n_{i}+t
, the length of each subinterval of
J_{n_{i}}
is an integer multiple of the period of
J_{n_{i+1}}
. Moreover, the initial point of
J_{l,n_{i}}
is the same as the initial point of
I_{s,n_{i+1}}
for some
s
. Hence,

(3.8)

J_{l,n_{i}}=\bigsqcup_{s\in A_{i+1}}I_{s,n_{i+1}},

for some indexing set
A_{i+1}
.

Let
n_{1},n_{2}\in{\mathbb{N}}
be such that
1\leq n_{1}<n_{2}\leq N
and
n_{2}-n_{1}\geq 2\log N
. By (
3.8
), for any
l\in\left\{0,1,\cdots,r^{n_{1}}-1\right\}
we have

(3.9)

I_{l,n_{1}}\cap J_{n_{1}}\cap J_{n_{2}}=J_{l,n_{1}}\cap J_{n_{2}}=\bigsqcup_{s\in A_{2}}I_{s,n_{2}}\cap J_{n_{2}}.

Hence

(3.10)

\begin{split}\lambda\left(I_{l,n_{1}}\cap J_{n_{1}}\cap J_{n_{2}}\right)=\lambda\left(\bigsqcup_{s\in A_{2}}I_{s,n_{2}}\cap J_{n_{2}}\right)=\sum_{s\in A_{2}}\lambda\left(I_{s,n_{2}}\cap J_{n_{2}}\right)\\
=\sum_{s\in A_{2}}\lambda(I_{s,n_{2}})\cdot\lambda(J_{n_{2}})=\lambda\left(\bigsqcup_{s\in A_{2}}I_{s,n_{2}}\right)\cdot\lambda(J_{n_{2}})=\lambda(J_{l,n_{1}})\cdot\lambda(J_{n_{2}}).\end{split}

Let
n_{1},n_{2},\cdots,n_{k}
be as in the lemma. Suppose that (
3.6
) is true for the last
(k-1)
indices. That is,

(3.11)

\lambda\left(I_{s,n_{2}}\cap\left(\bigcap_{i=2}^{k}J_{n_{i}}\right)\right)=\lambda(J_{s,n_{2}})\cdot\prod_{i=3}^{k}\lambda(J_{n_{i}}).

By (
3.8
), for any
l\in\left\{0,1,\cdots r^{n_{1}}-1\right\}
, we have

(3.12)

I_{l,n_{1}}\cap\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}J_{n_{i}}\right)=J_{l,n_{1}}\cap\left(\bigcap_{i=2}^{k}J_{n_{i}}\right)=\bigsqcup_{s\in A_{2}}I_{s,n_{2}}\cap\left(\bigcap_{i=2}^{k}J_{n_{i}}\right).

Hence

\begin{split}\lambda\left(I_{l,n_{1}}\cap\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}J_{n_{i}}\right)\right)=\lambda\left(\bigsqcup_{s\in A_{2}}I_{s,n_{2}}\cap\left(\bigcap_{i=2}^{k}J_{n_{i}}\right)\right)=\sum_{s\in A_{2}}\lambda\left(I_{s,n_{2}}\cap\left(\bigcap_{i=2}^{k}J_{n_{i}}\right)\right)\\
=\sum_{s\in A_{2}}\lambda(J_{s,n_{2}})\cdot\prod_{i=3}^{k}\lambda(J_{n_{i}})=\lambda\left(\bigsqcup_{s\in A_{2}}I_{s,n_{2}}\right)\cdot\prod_{i=2}^{k}\lambda(J_{n_{i}})=\lambda(J_{l,n_{1}})\cdot\prod_{i=2}^{k}\lambda(J_{n_{i}}),\end{split}

where we used our induction hypothesis (
3.11
) and the fact that
\lambda(J_{s,n_{2}})=\lambda(I_{s,n_{2}})\cdot\lambda(J_{n_{2}})
.
This proves (
3.6
) and thereby finishes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let

(3.13)

S(y):=\Big\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:r^{n}y\mod 1\in I_{n}\Big\}\text{ and }T(y):=\Big\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:r^{n}y\mod 1\in J_{n}\Big\}

Then for
\lambda
-almost every
y\in[0,1]
,
S(y)\triangle T(y)
is a finite set, where
\triangle
means symmetric difference.

Proof.

Let

(3.14)

F_{n}=\Big\{y\in[0,1]:r^{n}y\mod 1\in(n^{-a},\gamma_{n})\Big\}.

Clearly,
\lambda(F_{n})=(\gamma_{n}-n^{-a})
.
By the Borel-Cantelli Lemma, it suffices to show that
\lambda(F_{n})
is summable. Indeed,

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\lambda(F_{n})=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\sum_{n=r^{\frac{k}{a}}}^{r^{\frac{k+1}{a}}}(n^{-a}-\gamma_{n})\leq\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\sum_{n=r^{\frac{k}{a}}}^{r^{\frac{(k+1)}{a}}}\frac{1}{r^{\frac{2k+1}{a}}}<\infty,

as desired. ∎

3.3.
Proof of Theorem
A

Let
X_{n}^{\prime}
be a sequence of
\{0,1\}
-valued random variables defined by

(3.15)

X_{n}^{\prime}(y)=1\text{ iff }r^{n}y\mod 1\in J_{n}.

We have
\lambda(X_{n}^{\prime}=1)=\lambda(J_{n})=\gamma_{n}
, and let
Z_{n}=X_{n}^{\prime}-\gamma_{n}
and
W_{N}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\gamma_{n}
. By Lemma
3.2
, it will be sufficient to show that for Lebesgue a.e.
y
and for every
f\in L^{2}(X)

(3.16)

\frac{1}{W_{N}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}Z_{n}(y)f(T^{n}x)\to 0\text{ for a.e. $x$}.

By Herglotz’s spectral theorem and the lacunary trick (i.e., Lemma
2.1
), it will be sufficient to show that there exists a sequence
(b_{n})
, and for Lebesgue almost every
y
there exists a constant
C(y)
such that

(3.17)

\left|\sup_{t}\sum_{n=1}^{N}Z_{n}(y)e(nt)\right|\leq C(y)b_{N},

where
e(x)=e^{2\pi ix}
, and for every
\rho>1
,

(3.18)

\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\left|\dfrac{b_{\lfloor\rho^{N}\rfloor}}{W_{\lfloor\rho^{N}\rfloor}}\right|^{2}<\infty.

To prove this, we want to apply Corollary
2.5
. So, we write

\displaystyle\{1,\cdots,N\}=\bigsqcup_{k\in R}S_{k},\text{ where }R=\lceil{2\log N\rceil}\text{ and }S_{k}=\{n\in[N]:n\equiv k\mod R\}.

Without loss of any generality, we can assume that
R
divides
N
. Indeed, if
R
does not divide
N
, then we write
R=N^{\prime}+j
for some
j
,
0\leq j\leq R
. Then we work with
N^{\prime}
. The contribution of
N^{\prime}+1,N^{\prime}+2,\cdots,N
will be insignificant.
Let
C
be the constant of Corollary
2.5
. By union bound, we get

(3.19)

\begin{split}&\lambda\left(\sup_{t}\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N}Z_{n}(y)e(nt)\right|\geq CR\sqrt{W_{N}\log N}\right)\\
&\leq\lambda\left(\bigcup_{k=1}^{R}\left\{\sup_{t}\left|\sum_{n\in S_{k}}Z_{n}(y)e(nt)\right|\geq C\sqrt{W_{N}\log N}\right\}\right)\\
&\leq\sum_{k=1}^{R}\lambda\left(\sup_{t}\left|\sum_{n\in S_{k}}Z_{n}(y)e(nt)\right|\geq C\sqrt{W_{N}\log N}\right).\end{split}

For a fixed
k\in[R]
,

(3.20)

\begin{split}\sup_{t}\left|\sum_{n\in S_{k}}Z_{n}(y)e(nt)\right|&=\sup_{t}\left|\sum_{q=1}^{N/R}Z_{k+qR}(y)e((k+qR)t)\right|\\
&=\sup_{t}\left|\sum_{q=1}^{N/R}Z^{\prime}_{q}(y)e(qRt)\right|,\end{split}

where
Z_{q}^{\prime}(y)=Z_{k+qR}
when
k+qR\leq N
, otherwise
Z_{q}^{\prime}(y)
. By Lemma
3.1
,
Z_{q}^{\prime}
are independent random variables. Hence, applying Corollary
2.5
, we get

\lambda\left(\left\{\sup_{t}\left|\sum_{q=1}^{N/R}Z^{\prime}_{q}(y)e(qt^{\prime})\right|\geq C\sqrt{\left(\sum_{q=1}^{N/R}{\gamma_{k+qR}}\right)\log(N/R)}\right\}\right)\leq\frac{1}{(N/R)^{4}}\leq\dfrac{R^{4}}{N^{4}}.

Then,

(3.21)

\lambda\left(\left\{\sup_{t}\left|\sum_{q=1}^{N/R}Z^{\prime}_{q}(y)e(qt^{\prime})\right|\geq C\sqrt{W_{N}\log N}\right\}\right)\leq\frac{R^{4}}{N^{4}}.

Combining (
3.19
),(
3.20
) and (
3.21
), we get

(3.22)

\lambda\left(\left\{\sup_{t}\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N}Z_{n}(y)e(nt)\right|\geq CR\sqrt{W_{N}\log N}\right\}\right)\leq\frac{R^{5}}{N^{4}}

Hence, by Borel-Cantelli lemma, for Lebesgue a.e.
y
there is a constant
C(y)
such that

(3.23)

\displaystyle\sup_{t}\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N}Z_{n}(y)e(nt)\right|\leq C(y)(2\log N+1)\sqrt{N^{1-a}\log N}.

Thus, taking
b_{n}=(2\log N+1)\sqrt{N^{1-a}\log N}
both (
3.17
) and (
3.18
) are satisfied. This proves (
3.16
) and thereby finishes the proof of Theorem
A
.

3.4.
Proof of Theorem
B

Proof.

We use the notation from the last proof. As before it is sufficient to show that for Lebesgue a.e.
y
and for every
f\in L^{2}(X)

(3.24)

\frac{1}{W_{N}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}Z_{n}(y)f_{1}(T_{1}^{n}x)f_{2}(T_{2}^{n}x)\to 0\text{ for a.e. $x$}.

By the lacunary trick (i.e., Lemma
2.1
) and the Borel-Cantelli lemma, it is enough to show that

(3.25)

\sum_{N\in\left\{\lfloor\rho^{k}\rfloor:k\in{\mathbb{N}}\right\}}\left\|\frac{1}{N^{1-a}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}Z_{n}(y)T_{1}^{n}f_{1}\cdot T_{2}^{n}f_{2}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu)}<\infty.

We have that

\displaystyle A_{N}(y)

\displaystyle:=N^{2-2a}\cdot\left\|\sum_{n=1}^{N}Z_{n}(y)T_{1}^{n}f_{1}\cdot T_{2}^{n}f_{2}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu)}

\displaystyle=N^{2-2a}\cdot\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{R}\sum_{n\in[N]\cap S_{k}}Z_{n}(y)T_{1}^{n}f_{1}\cdot T_{2}^{n}f_{2}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu)}

\displaystyle\leq N^{2-2a}\cdot\left(\sum_{k=1}^{R}\left\|\sum_{n\in[N]\cap S_{k}}Z_{n}(y)T_{1}^{n}f_{1}\cdot T_{2}^{n}f_{2}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(2\log N+1)\cdot\sum_{k\in[R]}A_{N}^{(k)}(y),

where

\displaystyle A_{N}^{(k)}(y):

\displaystyle=N^{2-2a}\cdot\left\|\sum_{n\in[N]\cap S_{k}}Z_{n}(y)T_{1}^{n}f_{1}\cdot T_{2}^{n}f_{2}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu)},

and in the last step we used the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality
and the fact that
R=\lceil 2\log N\rceil
.

\displaystyle A_{N}^{(k)}(y):

\displaystyle=N^{2-2a}\cdot\left\|\sum_{n\in[N]\cap S_{k}}Z_{n}(y)T_{1}^{n}f_{1}\cdot T_{2}^{n}f_{2}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu)}

\displaystyle=N^{2-2a}\cdot\left\|\sum_{q\in[N/R]}Z_{k+qR}(y)T_{1}^{k+qR}f_{1}\cdot T_{2}^{k+qR}f_{2}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu)}

Applying van der Corput’s lemma with

, we get

,
where

\displaystyle A_{2,N}^{(k)}(y)

\displaystyle\leq N^{2-2a}\cdot\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left|\left|\sum_{n=1}^{M-m}Z^{\prime}_{n+m}(y)\cdot Z^{\prime}_{n}(y)S^{n}g_{m}\right|\right|_{L^{2}(\mu)}

where

2.4
with taking

and
\Lambda=\Lambda_{l}:=[N-m]\cap\Lambda_{l,m}

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1,m}

\displaystyle\Lambda_{2,m}

\displaystyle=\{n:(2k+1)m<n\leq(2k+2)m\text{ for some non-negative integer }k\}.

\displaystyle\lambda\left(y:M_{m,N}(y)>\eta\right)

4.
Proof of Theorem

Throughout this section, let

\rho

Let

\rho(\Delta_{n})\ll\dfrac{1}{n},\text{ where }\Delta_{n}:=\varphi(n+1)-\varphi(n).

For each

\varphi

as

\{n\in\mathbb{N}:S^{\varphi(n)}y\in I_{n}\}=\{a_{1}(y)<a_{2}(y)<\cdots\}.

By defining

(4.1)

X_{n}(y):=\mathbbm{1}_{I_{n}}(S^{\varphi(n)}y),

we can write

\{a_{1}(y)<a_{2}(y)<\cdots\}=\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:\ X_{n}(y)=1\}.

Despite the lack of independence between the random variables
X_{n}
, we still can establish a good control on the dependence by taking advantage of the decay of correlation for the system and the growth rate of
\varphi
. In fact, if
m\geq n
, notice that

(4.2)

\begin{split}\operatorname{Cov}(X_{n},X_{m})&=\int_{[0,1]}\Big(\mathbbm{1}_{I_{n}}\circ S^{\varphi(n)}\Big)\Big(\mathbbm{1}_{I_{m}}\circ S^{\varphi(m)}\Big)d\nu-\int_{[0,1]}\mathbbm{1}_{I_{n}}d\nu\int_{[0,1]}\mathbbm{1}_{I_{m}}d\nu\\
&=\int_{[0,1]}\mathbbm{1}_{I_{n}}\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{I_{m}}\circ S^{\varphi(m)-\varphi(n)}d\nu-\int_{[0,1]}\mathbbm{1}_{I_{n}}d\nu\int_{[0,1]}\mathbbm{1}_{I_{m}}d\nu.\end{split}

Since each interval
I_{n}
is union of at most
\ell

\mathbbm{1}_{I_{n}}
has bounded variation, and moreover, the bound is uniform for all
n
. Thus, recalling that
\rho
denotes the rate of decay of correlation, for any

(4.3)

\begin{split}|\operatorname{Cov}(X_{n},X_{m})|&\ll\rho\Big(\varphi(m)-\varphi(n)\Big)\|\mathbbm{1}_{I_{n}}\|_{BV}\cdot\|\mathbbm{1}_{I_{m}}\circ S^{\varphi(m)}\|_{L^{1}(\nu)}\\
&\ll\rho(\Delta_{n})\mathbb{E}(X_{m}).\end{split}

In particular, notice that

\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{m=n+1}^{N}|\operatorname{Cov}(X_{n},X_{n+m})|\ll\log(N)N^{1-a}.

By a straightforward application of Lemma 2.3 in
[
6
]
, we obtain the following.

Proposition 4.1
.

Assume the hypotheses of Theorem
C
, and let
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be as defined in
(

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N^{1-a}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}X_{n}(y)=1.

The main strategy in the proof of Theorem

is motivated by the semi-random case for independent random variables studied by Frantzikinakis, Wierdl and Lesigne

8

. Specifically, we make use of the following reduction.

Proposition 4.2

(Cf. Proposition 2.4 in
[
8
]
)
.

Assume the hypotheses of Theorem

, and let
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be as defined in

Suppose that for Lebesgue-almost every

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\|\frac{1}{W_{[\gamma^{k}]}}\sum_{n=1}^{[\gamma^{k}]}Z_{n}(y)\cdot T_{1}^{X_{1}(y)+\cdots+X_{n}(y)}f_{1}\cdot T_{2}^{n}f_{2}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}<+\infty,

Considering this reduction, we devote the rest of the section in showing (

Proposition 4.3
.

Assume the hypotheses of Theorem
C
, let

be as defined in

), and let

\sum_{m=1}^{\lfloor N^{b}\rfloor}\sum_{n=1}^{N}X_{n+m}(y)X_{n}(y)\ll N^{b+1-2a}.

Proof.

Let us consider

S_{N}:=\sum_{m=1}^{\lfloor N^{b}\rfloor}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(X_{n+m}X_{n}-\mathbb{E}(X_{n+m})\mathbb{E}(X_{n}))

A_{N}:=N^{-c}S_{N}\ \text{ where }\ c:=b+1-2a.

Since
\mathbb{E}(X_{n+m})\ll\mathbb{E}(X_{n})

N^{-c}\sum_{m=1}^{\lfloor N^{b}\rfloor}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}(X_{n+m})\mathbb{E}(X_{n})\ll N^{-c}\sum_{m=1}^{\lfloor N^{b}\rfloor}\sum_{n=1}^{N}n^{-2a}\ll 1.

So it suffices to show that almost every

Expanding

\displaystyle S_{N}^{2}=\sum_{m=1}^{\lfloor N^{b}\rfloor}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{\lfloor N^{b}\rfloor}\sum_{n^{\prime}=1}^{N}

\displaystyle\Big(X_{n+m}X_{n}X_{n^{\prime}+m^{\prime}}X_{n^{\prime}}+\sigma_{n+m}\sigma_{n}\sigma_{n^{\prime}+m^{\prime}}\sigma_{n^{\prime}}

\displaystyle-X_{n+m}X_{n}\sigma_{n^{\prime}+m^{\prime}}\sigma_{n^{\prime}}-X_{n^{\prime}+m^{\prime}}X_{n^{\prime}}\sigma_{n+m}\sigma_{n}\Big).

Taking expectation and rearranging terms, we can write

(4.6)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}(S_{N}^{2})&\ll\sum_{m=1}^{\lfloor N^{b}\rfloor}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{\lfloor N^{b}\rfloor}\sum_{n^{\prime}=1}^{N}\Bigg(|\operatorname{Cov}(X_{n},X_{n+m}X_{n^{\prime}}X_{n^{\prime}+m^{\prime}})|\\

&\ll\frac{1}{n}.\end{split}

With a similar argument, we obtain

|\mathrm{Cov}(X_{n},X_{n+m})|\ll\frac{1}{n},

and for

,

|\mathrm{Cov}(X_{n+m},X_{n^{\prime}}X_{n^{\prime}+m^{\prime}})|\ll\dfrac{1}{n+m}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(S_{N}^{2})

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{m=1}^{\lfloor N^{b}\rfloor}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{\lfloor N^{b}\rfloor}\sum_{n^{\prime}=n+m+1}^{N}n^{-1}+\sum_{m=1}^{\lfloor N^{b}\rfloor}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{\lfloor N^{b}\rfloor}\sum_{n^{\prime}=n}^{n+m}1

\displaystyle\ll N^{1+2b}\log(N)+N^{1+3b}.

Note that

Since

also converges to

h\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
,

T^{X_{n+1}(y)+\cdots+X_{k}(y)}h=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\mathbbm{1}_{\sum_{i=1}^{m+r}X_{n+i}(y)=j}T^{j}h.

After applying this idea and the triangular inequality we obtain

\displaystyle A_{4,N}\ll

\displaystyle N^{-1+4a-b-c}\cdot\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{m+r}\sum_{k_{2}=0}^{r}\sum_{k_{3}=0}^{m}\biggl\|\sum_{n=1}^{N-r}Z_{n+m+r}\cdot Z_{n+r}\cdot Z_{n+m}\cdot Z_{n}\cdot

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{\sum_{i=1}^{m+r}X_{n+i}=k_{1}}(n)S^{-r}T^{k_{1}}f\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\sum_{i=1}^{r}X_{n+i}=k_{2}}(n)S^{-r}T^{k_{2}}f\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{n+i}=k_{3}}(n)T^{k_{3}}f\biggr\|_{L^{2}}.

Note that the random variables are viewed as constant inside the
L^{2}(X,\mu)
norm, then

\displaystyle A_{4,N}\ll A_{5,N}:=N^{-1+4a-b-c}\cdot

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{m+r}\sum_{k_{2}=0}^{r}\sum_{k_{3}=0}^{m}\biggl|\sum_{n=1}^{N-r}Z_{n+m+r}\cdot Z_{n+r}\cdot Z_{n+m}\cdot Z_{n}\cdot

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{\sum_{i=1}^{m+r}X_{n+i}=k_{1}}(n)\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\sum_{i=1}^{r}X_{n+i}=k_{2}}(n)\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{n+i}=k_{3}}(n)\biggr|.

Using equations (
4.8
),(
4.10
), (
4.11
) and the previous estimate, for almost every
y\in[0,1]
,

(4.12)

A_{N}^{2}(y)\ll_{y}N^{2(a-b)}+N^{-2d_{1}}+N^{-\varepsilon}+A_{5,N}(y).

Note that at this point, the upper bound for
A_{N}
no longer depends on the measure-preserving system; it depends only on the random variables. This is essential to ensure we can choose a full-measure set that works for every m.p.s.

Let us note that

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}(A_{5,N})\ll N^{-1+4a+c}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\mathbb{E}\Bigg(\dfrac{1}{m(m+r)r}\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{m+r}\sum_{k_{2}=0}^{r}\sum_{k_{3}=0}^{m}\biggl|\sum_{n=1}^{N-r}Z_{n}\cdot U_{n,m,r,k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}\biggr|\Bigg)\end{split}

where

U_{n,m,r,k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}:=Z_{n+m+r}\cdot Z_{n+r}\cdot Z_{n+m}\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\sum_{i=1}^{m+r}X_{n+i}=k_{1}}\mathbbm{1}_{\sum_{i=1}^{r}X_{n+i}=k_{2}}\mathbbm{1}_{\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{n+i}=k_{3}}.

Using Jensen’s inequality,

\begin{split}&\Bigg(\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\Bigg(\dfrac{1}{m(m+r)r}\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{m+r}\sum_{k_{2}=0}^{r}\sum_{k_{3}=0}^{m}\biggl|\sum_{n=1}^{N-r}Z_{n}\cdot U_{n,m,r,k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}\biggr|\Bigg)\Bigg)^{2}\\
&\ll\dfrac{1}{m(m+r)r}\mathbb{E}\Bigg(\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{m+r}\sum_{k_{2}=0}^{r}\sum_{k_{3}=0}^{m}\biggl|\sum_{n=1}^{N-r}Z_{n}\cdot U_{n,m,r,k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}\biggr|^{2}\Bigg)\\
&\ll\dfrac{1}{m(m+r)r}\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{m+r}\sum_{k_{2}=0}^{r}\sum_{k_{3}=0}^{m}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\big(Z_{n}^{2}\cdot U_{n,m,r,k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}^{2}\big)\\
&\quad+\dfrac{1}{m(m+r)r}\sum_{n_{1}\neq n_{2}}^{N-r}\mathbb{E}\Bigg(\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{m+r}\sum_{k_{2}=0}^{r}\sum_{k_{3}=0}^{m}Z_{n_{1}}U_{n_{1},m,r,k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}\cdot Z_{n_{2}}U_{n_{2},m,r,k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}\Bigg)\\
&\ll\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\Big(Z_{n}^{2}Z_{n+m}^{2}Z_{n+r}^{2}Z_{n+m+r}^{2}\Big)+\dfrac{1}{m(m+r)r}\sum_{n_{1}=1}^{N-r}\sum_{n_{2}=n_{1}+1}^{N-r}\mathbb{E}\Big(Z_{n_{1}}\widetilde{Z}_{n_{1},n_{2},m,r}\widetilde{U}_{n_{1},n_{2},m,r}\Big),\end{split}

where

\displaystyle\widetilde{Z}_{n_{1},n_{2},m,r}

\displaystyle:=Z_{n_{1}+m}Z_{n_{1}+r}Z_{n_{1}+m+r}Z_{n_{2}}Z_{n_{2}+m}Z_{n_{2}+r}Z_{n_{2}+m+r},

\displaystyle\widetilde{U}_{n_{1},n_{2},m,r}

\displaystyle:=\mathbbm{1}_{\sum_{i=1}^{m+r}X_{n_{1}+i}=\sum_{i=1}^{m+r}X_{n_{2}+i}}\mathbbm{1}_{\sum_{i=1}^{r}X_{n_{1}+i}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}X_{n_{2}+i}}\mathbbm{1}_{\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{n_{1}+i}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{n_{2}+i}}.

Therefore,

(4.13)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}(A_{5,N})&\ll N^{-1+4a+c}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\Big(Z_{n}^{2}Z_{n+m}^{2}Z_{n+r}^{2}Z_{n+m+r}^{2}\Big)}\\
&+N^{-1+4a+c}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\dfrac{1}{r}\sqrt{\sum_{n_{1}=1}^{N-r}\sum_{n_{2}=n_{1}+1}^{N-r}\Big|\mathbb{E}\Big(Z_{n_{1}}\widetilde{Z}_{n_{1},n_{2},m,r}\widetilde{U}_{n_{1},n_{2},m,r}\Big)\Big|}.\end{split}

By expanding the squares and rearranging terms, we can write

(4.14)

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}(Z_{n}^{2}Z_{n+m}^{2}Z_{n+r}^{2}Z_{n+m+r}^{2})\\
=&\operatorname{Cov}(Z_{n}^{2},Z_{n+m}^{2}Z_{n+r}^{2}Z_{n+m+r}^{2})+\mathbb{E}(Z_{n}^{2})\operatorname{Cov}(Z_{n+m}^{2},Z_{n+r}^{2}Z_{n+m+r}^{2})\\
&+\mathbb{E}(Z_{n}^{2})\mathbb{E}(Z_{n+m}^{2})\Big(\operatorname{Cov}(Z_{n+r}^{2},Z_{n+m+r}^{2})+\mathbb{E}(Z_{n+r}^{2})\mathbb{E}(Z_{n+m+r}^{2})\Big).\end{split}

If we write
\displaystyle h_{k}:=\mathbbm{1}_{I_{k}}-\sigma_{k},
note that

Z_{n}^{2}=h_{n}^{2}\circ S^{\varphi(n)}

and

Z_{n+m}^{2}Z_{n+r}^{2}Z_{n+m+r}^{2}=h_{n+m}^{2}\circ S^{\varphi(n+m)}\cdot h_{n+r}^{2}\circ S^{\varphi(n+r)}\cdot h_{n+m+r}^{2}\circ S^{\varphi(n+m+r)}.

Note that
\|h_{k}\|_{\operatorname{BV}}\ll 1
. Using that the system
([0,1],\mathcal{B}([0,1]),\nu,S)
has a decay of correlation with exponential rate
\rho
, we obtain that

\Big|\operatorname{Cov}(Z_{n}^{2},Z_{n+m}^{2}Z_{n+r}^{2}Z_{n+m+r}^{2})\Big|\ll\rho\big(\varphi(n+\min\{m,n\})-\varphi(n)\big)\ll\rho\big(\varphi(n+1)-\varphi(n)\big).

If
r>m
, we can proceed analogously for the other terms of (
4.14
) and obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Z_{n}^{2}Z_{n+m}^{2}Z_{n+r}^{2}Z_{n+m+r}^{2})\ll\rho(\varphi(n+1)-\varphi(n))+\sigma_{n}^{4}.

In case
m\geq r
, we use the trivial estimation
\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}(Z_{n}^{2})\operatorname{Cov}(Z_{n+m}^{2},Z_{n+r}^{2}Z_{n+m+r}^{2})|\ll\sigma_{n}^{2}.

Therefore,

(4.15)

\begin{split}&\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\Big(Z_{n}^{2}Z_{n+m}^{2}Z_{n+r}^{2}Z_{n+m+r}^{2}\Big)}\\
&\ll\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\rho(\varphi(n+1)-\varphi(n))+\sigma_{n}^{4}}+\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{r=1}^{m}\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sigma_{n}^{2}}\\
&\ll N^{b+c}\log^{1/2}(N)+N^{b+c+1/2-2a}+N^{2b+1/2-a}\\
&\ll N^{1/2+b+c-2a}+N^{1/2+2b-a}.\end{split}

On the other hand, using the decay of correlation, we have that

\begin{split}\Big|\mathbb{E}\Big(Z_{n_{1}}\widetilde{Z}_{n_{1},n_{2},m,r}\widetilde{U}_{n_{1},n_{2},m,r}\Big)\Big|&=\Big|\operatorname{Cov}(X_{n_{1}},\widetilde{Z}_{n_{1},n_{2},m,r}\widetilde{U}_{n_{1},n_{2},m,r})\Big|\\
&\ll\rho\Big(\varphi(n_{1}+1)-\varphi(n_{1})\Big).\end{split}

Therefore,

(4.16)

\begin{split}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\dfrac{1}{r}\sqrt{\sum_{n_{1}=1}^{N-r}\sum_{n_{2}=n_{1}+1}^{N-r}\Big|\mathbb{E}\Big(Z_{n_{1}}\widetilde{Z}_{n_{1},n_{2},m,r}\widetilde{U}_{n_{1},n_{2},m,r}\Big)\Big|}\ll N^{b}\log(N)^{3/2}N^{1/2}.\end{split}

Using (
4.15
) and (
4.16
) in (
4.13
),

(4.17)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}(A_{5,N})&\ll N^{-1+4a+c}\Big(N^{1/2+b+c-2a}+N^{1/2+2b-a}+N^{b}\log(N)^{3/2}N^{1/2}\Big)\\
&\ll N^{-1/2+4a+c+b}\Big(N^{c-2a}+N^{b-a}+\log(N)^{3/2}\Big).\end{split}

Therefore, recalling
c=2a+\varepsilon
and
b=a+\varepsilon
,

\mathbb{E}(A_{5,N})\ll N^{-d_{2}},

where
d_{2}:=1/2-7a-3\varepsilon>0.
In particular, for any
\gamma>1
,

\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}(A_{5,\lfloor\gamma^{N}\rfloor})<\infty.

Hence, for almost every
y\in[0,1]
,

\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}A_{5,\lfloor\gamma^{N}\rfloor}(y)<\infty.

It follows from (
4.12
) that, for almost every
y\in[0,1]
(independent of the measure-preserving system),

\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}A_{\lfloor\gamma^{N}\rfloor}^{2}(y)<\infty,

finishing the proof.
∎

5.
Proof of Theorem
D

Proof.

Let us define
Z_{n}:=X_{n}-n^{-a}
. Since the averages

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}T^{n}f\cdot T^{\lfloor n^{1+b}\rfloor}g

converge in
L^{2}
(see
[
1
]
), it follows, as in the previous cases, that a standard lacunary
argument reduces the problem to showing that for any
\rho>1
, almost surely, the following holds:

For any measure preserving system
(X,\mathcal{X},\mu,T)
and for any
f,g\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
,

(5.1)

\sum_{N\in\left\{\lfloor\rho^{k}\rfloor:k\in{\mathbb{N}}\right\}}\left\|\frac{1}{N^{1-a}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}Z_{n}(\omega)T^{n}f\cdot T^{\lfloor n^{1+b}\rfloor}g\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu)}<\infty.

By applying van der Corput’s lemma with
M=\lfloor N^{c}\rfloor
,
c<1
, we get

(5.2)

A_{N}^{2}:=\left\|\frac{1}{N^{1-a}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}Z_{n}T^{n}f\cdot T^{\lfloor n^{1+b}\rfloor}g\right\|^{2}\ll A_{1,N}+A_{2,N},

where

\displaystyle A_{1,N}

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{1-c}}{N^{2-2a}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left\|Z_{n}\cdot T^{n}f\cdot T^{\lfloor n^{1+b}\rfloor}g\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}\text{ and }

\displaystyle A_{2,N}

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{1-c}}{N^{2-2a}}\sum_{m=1}^{\lfloor N^{c}\rfloor}\left|\int_{X}\sum_{n=1}^{N-m}Z_{n}Z_{n+m}T^{n}f\cdot T^{\lfloor n^{1+b}\rfloor}g\cdot T^{n+m}\overline{f}\cdot T^{\lfloor{(n+m)}^{1+b}\rfloor}\overline{g}d\mu\right|.

Recalling that the random variables are independent, by the strong law of large numbers and using the fact that
\mathbb{E}(X_{n}^{2})=n^{-a}
, almost surely we have

\displaystyle A_{1,N}\leq\frac{N^{1-c}}{N^{2-2a}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}|Z_{n}|^{2}\ll\frac{N^{1-c}}{N^{2-2a}}\cdot N^{1-a}=N^{a-c}.

Composing with
T^{-n}
and using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we get

\displaystyle A_{2,N}

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{1-c}}{N^{2-2a}}\sum_{m=1}^{\lfloor N^{c}\rfloor}\left|\int_{X}\sum_{n=1}^{N-m}Z_{n}Z_{n+m}f\cdot T^{\lfloor n^{1+b}-n\rfloor}g\cdot T^{m}\overline{f}\cdot T^{\lfloor{(n+m)}^{1+b}-n\rfloor}\overline{g}d\mu\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{N^{1-c}}{N^{2-2a}}\sum_{m=1}^{\lfloor N^{c}\rfloor}\left\|\sum_{n=1}^{N-m}Z_{n}Z_{n+m}T^{\lfloor n^{1+b}-n\rfloor}g\cdot T^{\lfloor{(n+m)}^{1+b}-n\rfloor}\overline{g}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}

Observe that if
0<b<1
,
1\leq m\leq N^{c}
and
1\leq n\leq N
then we have

(5.3)

0\leq(n+m)^{1+b}-n^{1+b}\leq N^{(b+c)}.

Hence, for every
m\in[\lfloor N^{c}\rfloor]
, we can partition the set
[N]
as

[N]=\bigsqcup_{r\in[N^{(b+c)}]}S_{m,r},

where

S_{m,r}=\left\{n\in[N]:\lfloor{(n+m)}^{1+b}-n\rfloor-\lfloor n^{1+b}-n\rfloor=r\right\}.

Thus, we get

\displaystyle A_{2,N}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{N^{1-c}}{N^{2-2a}}\sum_{m=1}^{\lfloor N^{c}\rfloor}\sum_{r=1}^{\lfloor N^{(b+c)}\rfloor}\left\|\sum_{n\in[N-m]\cap S_{m,r}}Z_{n}Z_{n+m}T^{\lfloor n^{1+b}-n\rfloor}g\cdot T^{(\lfloor{n}^{1+b}-n\rfloor+r)}\overline{g}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}

Letting
g_{r}=g\cdot T^{r}\overline{g}
, we get

(5.4)

\displaystyle A_{2,N}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{N^{1-c}}{N^{2-2a}}\sum_{m=1}^{\lfloor N^{c}\rfloor}\sum_{r=1}^{\lfloor N^{(b+c)}\rfloor}\left\|\sum_{n\in[N-m]\cap S_{m,r}}Z_{n}Z_{n+m}T^{\lfloor n^{1+b}-n\rfloor}g_{r}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}.

Using Herglotz’s spectral theorem and the fact that
g_{r}
is bounded by
1
, we get

(5.5)

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{n\in[N-m]\cap S_{m,r}}Z_{n}Z_{n+m}T^{\lfloor n^{1+b}-n\rfloor}g_{r}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{m\in\lfloor N^{c}\rfloor}\max_{r\in[N^{b+c}]}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|\sum_{n\in[N-m]\cap S_{m,r}}Y_{n}Y_{n+m}e\left({\lfloor n^{1+b}-n\rfloor}t\right)\right|

For fixed
r\in[N^{b+c}]
and
m\in[N^{c}]
, we want to apply Lemma
2.4
for
Z_{m,n}=Z_{n}\cdot Z_{n+m}
,
s(n)={\lfloor n^{1+b}-n\rfloor}
and
\Lambda=S_{m,r}\cap[N-m]\cap\Lambda_{k,m}
,
k=1,2
where
\Lambda_{k,m}
are defined as follows:

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1,m}

\displaystyle=\{n:2km<n\leq(2k+1)m\text{ for some non-negative integer }k\},\text{ and }

\displaystyle\Lambda_{2,m}

\displaystyle=\{n:(2k+1)m<n\leq(2k+2)m\text{ for some non-negative integer }k\}.

Observe that
\Lambda_{1,m}\cup\Lambda_{2,m}={\mathbb{N}}
,
\{Z_{m,n}:n\in\Lambda_{k,m}\}
is independent, and

\displaystyle\text{Var}(Z_{m,n})

\displaystyle=\sigma_{n}\sigma_{m+n}-\sigma_{n}\sigma_{n+m}^{2}-\sigma_{n}^{2}\sigma_{n+m}+\sigma_{n}^{2}\sigma_{n+m}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{n}\sigma_{n+m}\leq\sigma_{n}^{2}\sim n^{-2a}.

Thus, condition (
2.3
) is satisfied with
\rho_{n}=n^{-2a}
. By Lemma
2.4
we get

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|\sum_{n\in[N-m]\cap S_{m,r}}Z_{n}Z_{n+m}e\left({\lfloor n^{1+b}-n\rfloor}t\right)\right|\geq 2A\sqrt{R_{N}\log N}\right)\leq\frac{2}{N^{4}}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{m\in\lfloor N^{c}\rfloor}\max_{r\in[N^{b+c}]}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|\sum_{n\in[N-m]\cap S_{m,r}}Z_{n}Z_{n+m}e\left({\lfloor n^{1+b}-n\rfloor}t\right)\right|\geq 2A\sqrt{R_{N}\log N}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{m\in\lfloor N^{c}\rfloor}\bigcup_{r\in[N^{b+c}]}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|\sum_{n\in[N-m]\cap S_{m,r}}Z_{n}Z_{n+m}e\left({\lfloor n^{1+b}-n\rfloor}t\right)\right|\geq 2A\sqrt{R_{N}\log N}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m\in[N^{c}]}\sum_{r\in[N^{b+c}]}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|\sum_{n\in[N-m]\cap S_{m,r}}Z_{n}Z_{n+m}e\left({\lfloor n^{1+b}-n\rfloor}t\right)\right|\geq 2A\sqrt{R_{N}\log N}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2N^{b+2c}}{N^{4}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{N^{2}}.

By the Borel-Cantelli lemma, almost surely we have

\max_{m\in[N^{c}]}\max_{r\in[N^{b+c}]}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|\sum_{[N]\cap J_{m,r}}Z_{m,n}\cdot e(s(n)t)\right|\ll_{\omega}\left(\sqrt{\log N\cdot\sum_{n=1}^{N}\rho_{n}}\right).

Combining (
5.4
) and (
5.5
) we get,

\displaystyle A_{2,N}

\displaystyle\ll_{\omega}\frac{N^{1-c}}{N^{2-2a}}\sum_{m=1}^{\lfloor N^{c}\rfloor}\sum_{r=1}^{\lfloor N^{(b+c)}\rfloor}\sqrt{\log N}\cdot N^{\frac{1}{2}-a}

\displaystyle\leq N^{1-c-2+2a+c+b+c+\frac{1}{2}-a}=N^{a+c+b-\frac{1}{2}}.

In conclusion, almost surely
(A_{N}^{2})
is summable along any lacunary sequence as long as we have

(5.6)

b<1,a<c<1\text{ and }b+c+a<\frac{1}{2}.

Since we are given
(b+2a)<\frac{1}{2}
, it is possible to choose
c
satisfying (
5.6
). This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎
