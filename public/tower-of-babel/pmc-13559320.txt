International variability in pain management for the minimally invasive repair of pectus excavatum: a survey by the Chest Wall International Group
Authors: Elise J van Polen, Jean H T Daemen, Djura Knol, Lori M van Roozendaal, Aimée J P M Franssen, Nicky Janssen, Dawn Jaroszewski, Lisa E McMahon, Marcelo Martinez-Ferro, José R M de Campos, Manuel López, Yvonne L J Vissers, Erik R de Loos, CWIG collaborator group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Nuss procedure for pectus excavatum correction is often accompanied by severe postoperative pain. Several analgesic techniques have been researched; however, a consensus on the optimal strategy has not been reached. This survey aims to quantify the international variability in perioperative pain management following the Nuss procedure.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was designed using Qualtrics XM and distributed via the Chest Wall International Group (CWIG), the world’s largest medical association for chest wall deformities. Medical specialists directly involved in the treatment of pectus excavatum were included. Demographics and views on the use of various analgesic techniques were collected. Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS (v27.0). The index of qualitative variation (IQV) was calculated to quantify variability in responses.ResultsThe analysis included 71 participants from across Europe (47%), North America (28%), South America (13%), Asia (9%), Australia (3%), and Africa (1%). Of these participants, 70% had >10 years of experience with the Nuss procedure. Participants using standardized pain management (n=64) showed a high global variance in primary analgesic technique (IQV 0.86). A continental sub-analysis solely showed a low variance in North America (IQV 0.14) with a preference towards intercostal nerve cryoablation (94%). Most participants used additional analgesic techniques (89%) in their standardized protocol.ConclusionsThese findings revealed high international variability in pain management following the Nuss procedure. Nevertheless, results did suggest a global agreement on a multimodal approach. Future research should focus on providing high-quality evidence on the optimal technique to reduce variability in perioperative pain management.Keywords: Pectus excavatum, pain management, cross-sectional survey, international variability

Highlight box

Key findings

• This cross-sectional cohort survey demonstrates substantial international variability in pain management methods for the Nuss procedure, reflected by an index of qualitative variation of 0.86, while also revealing broad consensus on the use of a multimodal approach.

What is known and what is new?

• Several analgesic techniques are available for managing postoperative pain following the Nuss procedure for pectus excavatum; however, no consensus has yet been reached regarding the optimal approach.

• This manuscript quantifies the current variability in pain management approaches and highlights qualitative evidence that supports the underlying rationales for their use.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Significant variability exists among international pain management protocols, highlighting gaps in knowledge regarding the most effective approaches.

• Future research should aim to generate high-quality evidence on the optimal surgical techniques for pectus repair, with a comprehensive focus on all aspects of postoperative recovery, ultimately guiding the development of a standardized Early Recovery After Surgery protocol for this procedure.

Introduction

Background/rationale

The minimally invasive repair of pectus excavatum (MIRPE), also known as the Nuss procedure, is recognized as an effective surgical approach characterized by reduced blood loss, preservation of costal cartilage, and smaller incisions relative to traditional open techniques. However, this procedure can lead to severe postoperative pain, primarily due to stretching of the chest wall and intercostal nerves (1). Lack of adequate pain management may result in chronic pain and prolonged opioid usage (2-4). Several methods of pain management, including thoracic epidural analgesia (TEA), locoregional blocks, patient-controlled intravenous analgesia, intercostal nerve cryoablation and management with oral or intravenous analgesics have been reported in the perioperative care of pectus excavatum, and thorough research has been performed proving their individual effectiveness (5). The recent pectus care guidelines state that intercostal nerve cryoablation is a promising alternative to TEA and should be considered in the perioperative care of pectus excavatum patients (6). The spread of current literature on the subject, however, indicates that no uniform pain management protocol is being used internationally, probably as each respective form of pain management has its own set of advantages and disadvantages. In addition, the strength of the recommendation is only a Class IIa recommendation and was based on Level B evidence (6). To develop a concise perioperative pain protocol containing the most effective analgesic strategy, it is imperative to first delineate the global distribution of techniques currently employed.

Objectives

This study aims to quantify the international practice variation in perioperative pain management protocols and assess the underlying rationale for the use of various protocols by conducting a global survey. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1265/rc) (7).

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional single-round survey design was employed to assess the international practice variation in perioperative pain management for the Nuss procedure. Informed consent was obtained from every participant prior to inclusion. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Research Committee Zuyd—Zuyd University of Applied Sciences (METCZ; approval No. Z2024138; approval date: 31-01-2025). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Setting & participants bias & study size

The survey was distributed to members of the Chest Wall International Group (CWIG), the world’s largest medical association dedicated to chest wall deformities. CWIG currently comprises 157 medical professionals with a specialized interest in chest wall pathology (8). Its membership spans across six continents. Only CWIG members were eligible to participate, ensuring a dedicated interest in the perioperative care of pectus excavatum. Furthermore, they were required to be medical specialists who have completed training (e.g., attendings, heads of department, professors) and were directly involved in the care of pectus excavatum. Other medical personnel were excluded. The survey was distributed in January 2025 using the survey platform Qualtrics (Provo, UT, USA), and participants were given eight weeks to complete the survey, with reminder emails sent out at 2-, 5-, and 7-weeks post distribution to maximize the response rate.

Survey

A structured survey was developed based on a literature review performed on perioperative pain management strategies for the Nuss procedure followed by an expert meeting. The survey included multiple-choice and open-ended questions. The first part of the survey covered eligibility, after which demographic and professional characteristics were assessed. The second part of the survey focused on pain management practices and attitudes towards specific analgesic modalities. Participants were asked to distinguish between their primary pain management modality and additional analgesic treatments. A complete overview of the survey questions can be found in Table S1. A pilot survey was distributed among five CWIG-board members and authors to ensure clarity of the survey.

Variables

Participant characteristics included type of medical specialist and years of experience and professional characteristics such as the type of hospital, their annual case load, country of practice, the minimum and maximum age they adhere to for surgical treatment and whether standard pain management protocols are implemented. The primary outcome measure was international variability in primary analgesic technique. Secondary outcome measures included details on the primary analgesic techniques and qualitative data on why the participant uses their primary analgesic technique and what would motivate them to change to a different technique. Additional analgesic techniques and their details were also recorded. Participants who did not utilize intercostal nerve cryoablation were asked to provide their reasons for not adopting the technique and to indicate whether they intend to use it in the future.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data were presented as mean and standard deviation (SD) or as median and interquartile range (IQR) in the presence of skewness. Nominal and ordinal data were depicted as frequencies and percentages. International variability was quantified using the index of qualitative variation (IQV). The IQV is a measure of variability for categorical variables, which ranges from 0 (no variation) to 1 (maximum variation). It is based on the ratio of actual differences in the distribution divided by the number of possible differences in the distribution. The corresponding calculated value represents the extent of variation or diversity within the data (9,10). An IQV of higher than 0.8 is often chosen to indicate a high measure of variance (11). Data were analyzed IBM SPSS Statistics for macOS, Version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Qualitative data were analyzed using an inductive coding technique. Responses were reviewed, categorized and quantified, and discrepancies were resolved where appropriate by discussion.

Results

Participants

The survey was distributed amongst 157 current CWIG members, of which 150 met the predefined eligibility criteria. A total of 112 responses were collected, of which 41 were excluded due to incomplete surveys (n=22), duplicate entries (n=15), or non-medical specialist respondents (n=4). This resulted in 71 valid responses included in the final analysis, corresponding to a response rate of 45%.

Participant data

Amongst the 71 participants, the majority were pediatric surgeons (n=42, 59%). Most participants had at least 10 years of experience (n=50, 70%), with the largest subgroup reporting 16–20 years of experience (n=16, 23%). Participants represented a diverse international cohort spanning 23 different countries (Table 1). The largest proportion practiced in the USA (n=19, 27%), followed by The Netherlands (n=9, 13%).

Professional characteristics

Most participants worked in academic hospitals (n=50, 70%). Regarding hospital funding, the majority were affiliated with public hospitals (n=52, 73%), the rest practiced in private institutions. The median annual case load of Nuss procedures was 90 cases (IQR 35-180) (Table 1).

Procedural characteristics

The minimum age for undergoing the Nuss procedure showed an IQR of 10–13 years, with a median of 12 years. The maximum age range showed a median of 35 years (IQR 22–55 years). Four participants disclosed that they employ a different pain management protocol for younger and older patients, with a transition age of 18 years. Sixty-four participants (90%) used a standardized pain management protocol in their hospital; however, a variation was seen in who is responsible for overseeing postoperative pain management. A single healthcare provider was responsible for pain management in 43 cases (67%), most commonly the surgeon (n=18, 28%), a multidisciplinary approach was used in 21 cases (33%). The most frequently reported combination involved an anesthesiologist, surgeon, and pain team (n=9, 14%) (Table 1).

Variability in pain management protocols

Globally, intercostal nerve cryoablation was the most frequently used primary analgesic technique amongst respondents (n=30, 47%), followed by TEA (n=14, 22%). Nevertheless, a high degree of variance was seen in global preference in primary analgesic technique (IQV =0.86) (Table 2). A continental sub-analysis shows a clear preference for intercostal nerve cryoablation amongst North American specialists with a low degree of variance (IQV =0.14), particularly amongst surgeons practicing in the USA (100%), as shown in Table S2. European and South American respondents showed no clear preference for a single technique, as a high degree in variance was observed with an IQV of 0.95 and 0.87, respectively (Table 2). A slightly lower degree of variance (IQV 0.76) was seen amongst Asian participants; however, considering the small sample size, this may not be representative. Oceania was represented by only one participant; thus, variance could not be assessed.

Most participants (n=54, 84%) indicated using at least one additional technique (Table S3). Specifically, locoregional blocks were the most reported additional techniques (as a single technique or a combination) in 61% of the participants who indicated using an additional technique, followed by “other” analgesia (57%).

Intercostal nerve cryoablation

Intercostal nerve cryoablation was indicated as the primary analgesic technique by 47% of the respondents (n=30). Of these respondents, most use probes manufactured by AtriCure, Inc. (Mason, OH, USA) (n=21, 70%), with the remaining identifying Metrum CryoFlex (Warsaw, Poland) as their supplier (n=9, 30%) (Table S4). A thoracoscopic approach was the most common technique to apply cryo nerve block (n=25, 83%), while a sole percutaneous approach was indicated by only one participant, and the remaining four participants made use of both techniques. According to the responses, “previous thoracic surgery” was the main reason for these respondents to use a percutaneous approach (Table S4). The majority of respondents (n=25, 83%) indicated T3 as their cranial limit for intercostal nerve freezing, with 53% of participants indicating T8 as the caudal limit (Table S4). Two respondents indicated that they do not have strict limits but rather freeze two levels cranial and two levels caudal to the bar. The leading proportion of participants (n=18, 60%) indicated the region lateral to the transition line between the internal intercostal membrane and internal intercostal muscle, around 5 cm lateral to the spine (region 6 in Figure S1). Respondents showed a mean of 4.4 (SD 1.9) years of experience using intercostal nerve cryoablation, and the most frequently indicated previous technique was TEA (n=19, 63%) (Table S4).

When prompted why they decided to introduce intercostal nerve cryoablation in their hospital, 70% of respondents indicated “postoperative pain reduction” as one of their reasons. Other reasons included “reduced opioid consumption” (30%) and “reduced hospital stay” (16%) (Figure 1A). Respondents were subsequently asked to indicate if they felt any improvements could be made to the technique. To this, 50% (n=15) responded that they would prefer a reduced “freezing time”, 20% (n=6) felt that improvements such as improved insulation or flexibility could be made to the probe, and 20% (n=6) would like to have the option of a percutaneous approach (Figure 1B).

This was mostly indicated by North American (n=1, 100%), South American (n=4, 80%), and European (n=9, 75%) respondents. Lack of availability was most important for Asian participants (n=4, 80%) (Table 4).

TEA, locoregional blocks, and PCA

TEA, locoregional blocks and PCA were used by 22% (n=14), 8% (n=5), and 9% (n=6) of respondents respectively as the primary analgesic technique. Details on the variability in protocol can be found in Table S4.

The most important rationale for using these techniques was their effectiveness as indicated by 71% (n=10) of respondents using TEA, 60% (n=3) of respondents using locoregional blocks and 67% (n=4) of respondents using PCA (Figure 2A). The most frequent recorded reason to switch to a different technique in was length of hospital stay reduction for TEA (n=10, 71%), and postoperative pain reduction for locoregional blocks (n=5, 100%) and PCA (n=4, 67%) (Figure 2B).

Other analgesic medications

Other analgesic medications, including oral analgesics and non-patient-controlled intravenous analgesics, were indicated by 14% (n=9) of the respondents as their primary technique and by 56% (n=31) of the respondents as an additional technique. Fifty percent of the respondents indicated that preoperative medication was not necessary for the Nuss procedure. For those who did provide preoperative medication, acetaminophen (n=8, 20%), non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) (n=7, 18%), oral neuropathic analgesics (n=9, 23%), and opioids (n=6, 15%) were most frequently used. Postoperatively during hospital stay, acetaminophen (n=30, 75%), NSAIDs (n=32, 80%) and opioids (n=34, 85%) were used by most respondents, muscle relaxers were added by respondents using intercostal nerve cryoablation (n=7, 47%) and locoregional blocks (n=1, 33%) as their primary technique but not by TEA and PCA. After discharge, the use of acetaminophen and NSAIDs remained consistent at 73% (n=29) and 83% (n=33), respectively. The use of opioids, however, decreases after discharge to 55% (n=22). Further use of other analgesic medications per the primary technique can be seen in Figure S2.

Discussion

This cross-sectional survey presents data on the international use of perioperative pain management protocols concerning the Nuss procedure for pectus excavatum correction. A global representation was achieved by recruiting CWIG respondents. While a few respondents indicated relying on a patient-tailored approach, the vast majority reported adherence to a standardized in-hospital protocol; however, high global variation was seen in the primary pain management technique of these protocols. Furthermore, most respondents (84%) indicated that at least one additional pain management technique should be incorporated as part of a multimodal pain management protocol. Yet, there is considerable variation as to which additional technique is used. Thus, this data represents controversy amongst medical specialists in the expert opinions on the optimal perioperative pain management protocol.

Currently, five different pain management methods are reported in the literature for perioperative pain management after the Nuss procedure: TEA, locoregional pain blocks, patient-controlled intravenous analgesia, intercostal nerve cryoablation, and other additional methods such as oral analgesics (5,12). A recent narrative review reflects on the specific advantages and disadvantages of each technique, describing TEA as the historic gold standard and thus the control comparator in multiple cohort studies and randomized controlled trials (12). This is also reflected in a previous survey study performed by Muhly et al. (1). This study, published in 2014, found that out of 55 institutions in Europe (9%), North America (88%), and Australia (3%), 91% of institutions utilized TEA as their primary analgesic technique. In comparison to our results eleven years later, TEA was used by a mere 22% of the respondents, with no reported use in the USA. This indicates a clear shift to other pain management techniques, such as intercostal nerve cryoablation, as seen with the results of this survey. When prompted what the rationale for using a thoracic epidural, respondents indicated the effectiveness of the technique as the main reason. However, respondents also indicated that pain reduction, improved cost-effectivity and reducing length of hospital stay would be reasons to switch to a different analgesic technique, which is in line with the significant failure rates of TEA and the prolonged hospital stay needed to transition to oral analgesia, which in turn leads to higher hospital costs (13). Interestingly, respondents utilizing TEA also indicated an improved patient experience/comfort as a reason for switching to another analgesic technique, inferring that patient discomfort with, especially in the younger pectus population, is something to be considered in one’s decision-making process, as shown by only 14% of participants using TEA solely treating patients under 24 years of age.

As standard practice, only a small proportion of respondents reported using PCA or locoregional blocks as the primary analgesic technique, though those that do claim effectiveness as their reasoning. However, as an additional analgesic technique, it’s popularity rises as half of the respondents indicated using a locoregional block and a quarter using PCA. This is consistent with recent literature describing that intercostal nerve cryoablation or TEA combined with an adjunct analgesic technique is seen as superior to the primary technique alone (5,14).

Intercostal nerve cryoablation is a relatively novel pain management technique for the Nuss procedure. Its usage was first described in 2016 by Keller et al. in the USA following approval by the Food and Drug Administration (15). It makes use of reversible nerve damage to inhibit pain signals and thus provides pain management over a longer period. The current results show a clear international shift towards intercostal nerve cryoablation, as 47% of respondents have indicated using it as their primary analgesic technique. This, however, may be a skewed view, as most respondents practice in North America, where the technique was first introduced, and in Europe, which followed shortly thereafter. No clear professional characteristics could be identified for intercostal nerve cryoablation users, except the tendency to work in an academic hospital (83%). Reasoning for transitioning to intercostal nerve cryoablation included improved pain management, reduced opioid usage, and shorter length of hospital stay. This is reflected in literature where a recent meta-analysis describes a median reduction of opioid consumption of 72% for inpatient care and 61% for outpatient care and a reduction of hospital stay by 1.77 days when compared to “non-cryo” alternatives (16). Whilst the technique is gaining popularity, there are still numerous uncertainties pertaining to the optimal method. This is portrayed in our results where a variability is seen in approach, range, probe application time and location, temperature, and the necessity for and type of additional modality. This was also portrayed in the analysis of the qualitative data, where 10% of respondents indicated that intercostal nerve cryoablation could be further improved by introducing a standardized protocol.

Future perspectives

Recording the global variability in perioperative pain management not only allows us to gain insight into the current trends but also points us towards knowledge gaps in the existing literature. At present, no definitive Early Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) guidelines exist for pectus excavatum surgery, as they do for other thoracic procedures, and recent guidelines solely advise ERAS protocols to be used but do not elaborate on what they should entail (17). Recent articles have described their own ERAS protocols, including intercostal nerve cryoablation as their primary pain management technique; however, even these studies, which were both conducted in the USA contain differences in protocol, using different locoregional blocks (18,19). Furthermore, both protocols describe the standard use of neuropathic medications (gabapentin) postoperatively, whilst 70% of respondents in this survey do not make use of these medications, and a study by Rettig et al. [2021] found no evidence to support the use, illustrating the variations in current protocols and the need for standardization (20). Additionally, it should be considered that perioperative practices as described in this article may be based on expert opinion and the surgeon’s experience instead of scientific evidence. Closing the identified knowledge gaps through high-level evidence studies may be the first step in providing a recommendation for a standard pain management protocol and eventually working towards an ERAS protocol for pectus surgery. However, these studies should also pay attention to risk-benefit profiles of each analgesic technique.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered for this cross-sectional survey study. Firstly, its cross-sectional design captures practices at a single moment in time, even though pain management protocols are constantly evolving. Several respondents, for example, indicated that intercostal nerve cryoablation was about to be implemented in their practice; thus, repeating this same survey, one year or even a few months later, may provide different results. Furthermore, though CWIG is the largest medical association dedicated to chest wall deformities, it may still lead to a bias in the cohort as not it may not represent all chest wall surgeons. CWIG-members are generally employed in specialized high-volume chest wall centers which ensured expertise, however, may not be a reflection of global routine practice. This bias is enhanced by a response rate of 45% and a small number of respondents from Asia, Oceania, and Africa were represented, which may give a skewed view of the current practices especially in these low- and middle-income regions as these regions may only have access to certain resources. Participant fatigue should also be considered. The data showed that 22 surveys were not completed, thus, the response rate may be increased with a concise survey. Lastly, interpretation of survey questions may have varied among respondents due to cultural and linguistic differences, potentially influencing how certain terms or clinical scenarios were understood. Given the international and multidisciplinary nature of the sample, differences in training, terminology, and institutional norms may have affected the consistency of responses. Although efforts were made to design clear and neutral questions, subtle variations in interpretation may have introduced response bias and should be considered.

Conclusions

This cross-sectional survey found considerable global variability in perioperative pain management protocols. Although an apparent shift from TEA to intercostal nerve cryoablation is seen, especially in Europe and North America, there are still significant knowledge gaps preventing a standard perioperative pain management protocol. Future research should focus on providing high-level evidence to close these gaps, paving the way for a possible ERAS protocol for pectus surgery.
