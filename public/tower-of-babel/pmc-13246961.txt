Characteristics of functional bacteria involved in carbon, nitrogen and sulfur cycles in PM2.5 under different meteorological conditions
Authors: Yi Wang, Xu Yan, Wenbo Zhang, Shuai Zhu, Fengxiao Hai, Xinzhi Xie, Kaili Ma, Xiaopin Li, Yunping Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe meteorological conditions during dust events and rainfall have a significant impact on the composition of atmospheric microbial communities. This study investigated the microbial community structure and the characteristics of functional bacteria involved in carbon, nitrogen and sulfur cycles of bioaerosols in PM2.5. Samples were collected in central China from March to April (2023) under normal, rainfall, and dust weather conditions. Differences in the composition of bacterial communities under various meteorological conditions and the effects of the resultant changes on carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycling were also observed. The results showed that the dominant phyla in the bacterial aerosols were Pseudomonadota, Bacillota and Actinobacteriota, accounting for 33.7%, 27.0%, and 17.8%, respectively. Species richness and diversity varied significantly under the different weather conditions. A large proportion of airborne bacteria that are functionally associated with carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycles were identified, with Bacillus, Sphingomonas, Pseudomonas, Paracoccus and Acinetobacter being the dominant genera. Most bacteria are related to these material cycles. The carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycling pathways are potentially affected by weather conditions. The effects of environmental factors such as meteorological parameters and soluble ions on the functional bacterial community were analyzed. Relative humidity (RH), PM2.5 and ammonium (NH4+) were significantly correlated with the functional bacteria. Overall, this study discusses the effects of meteorological conditions on the distribution of bacterial communities and functional bacteria involved in carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycling in the atmosphere. The relationships between the metabolic activity of carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycling and the corresponding concentrations of atmospheric elements forms were also revealed. Introduction

As a key component of aerobiology, bioaerosols have attracted increasing attention due to their role in linking terrestrial and marine ecosystems via dust-mediated long-distance transport1. Bioaerosols typically refer to the active biological substances suspended in the atmosphere, including viruses, bacteria, fungi, pollen and some of their by-products, such as endotoxins and polysaccharides, and their aerodynamic diameters are within 100 μm2,3. Bioaerosols and PM2.5 (also known as fine particulate matter, is defined as airborne particles with an aerodynamic diameter ≤ 2.5 μm.), are primarily derived from near-surface sources such as soil, water, plants, and human activity. Microorganisms in the atmosphere can participate in processes such as ice-nucleation activity and cloud formation processes4. The ubiquity of microorganisms on Earth makes them pivotal constituents of the material cycle, actively participating in the conversion of carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur across diverse environmental settings5. Microbial activity indirectly affects atmospheric chemical processes and climate change, and bioaerosols have a potential role in altering atmospheric chemical composition through their metabolic activity6–8. Therefore, it is important to study effects of bioaerosols on carbon, nitrogen, sulfur and other major elements cycling in the atmosphere.

Through their metabolic activities, microorganisms mediate biogeochemical cycling of carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur, while also possessing the capacity to metabolize atmospheric pollutants9. The results of Liu et al. (2022) and Liu et al. (2020) revealed that bacterial metabolism may contribute to the accumulation of ammonium (NH4+) and sulfate (SO42−). Bacteria consume or accumulate substances such as NH4+, SO42−, and organic carbon (OC) by participating in carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycles10. The impact of atmospheric microorganisms on cycle pathways in the atmosphere has always been a focus of research. NH4+ and SO42− are dominant secondary aerosols and are important substances that cause air pollution, directly or indirectly affecting the ecological environment and human health11,12.

Atmospheric processing of mineral dust encompasses chemical transformations, interactions with atmospheric components, and aging during transport, with deposition mainly mediated by gravitational settling, turbulence, and precipitation to connect atmospheric dust dynamics to surface systems13. Dust and rainfall events are frequent in northern China during spring. Previous studies have shown and that dust and rainfall events affect bacterial community structure to a certain extent14. As an important component of aerosols in the atmosphere, dust particles not only reduce the visibility of the atmosphere but also endanger human health15,16. Dust events also bring large numbers of foreign bacteria into local areas, causing major changes in the bacterial community structure17,18. Rainfall washes particulate matter out of the atmosphere, whereas high relative humidity (RH) deposits particulate matter, which affects the bacterial community structure19–21. found that there was no statistically significant correlation between meteorological factors such as temperature (T), RH, wind speed (WS), and microbial activity. This contradicts the conclusions of Zhen et al. (2017)19. Their research showed that meteorological factors may change the level of microbial activity by accelerating the spread of bacteria. It was also found that meteorological factors had a greater impact on the bacterial community in the atmosphere than that of atmospheric pollutants, and this relationship was more significant22. However, under different weather conditions, the variations in the community structure of atmospheric bacteria involved in carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycling, the specific pathways of these biogeochemical cycles, and the associations between functional bacteria and ammonium (NH₄⁺), sulfate (SO₄²⁻), and nitrate remain inadequately investigated.

This study aimed to elucidate the effects of different meteorological conditions on the community structure of atmospheric bacteria, as well as the subsequent alterations to atmospheric carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycling processes mediated by these microbes. To this end, twenty samples of fine particulate matter (PM2.5) were collected from central China during spring in 2023. Microbial activity was characterized by 16 S rRNA gene sequencing, and the concentration and community structure of total bacteria and functional bacteria for carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur metabolisms in the atmosphere were analyzed. Correlations between bioaerosols and carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycles were also explored. Furthermore, differences in bacterial community structure and environmental factors during normal, rainfall, and dust weather conditions were investigated. This study provides valuable scientific insight into the atmospheric composition changes caused by airborne bacteria under different meteorological conditions.

Materials and methods

Aerosol sampling

As shown in Fig. S1, the sampling site was located on the roof of a six-story experimental building in Henan Normal University, Xinxiang, China (35°19 N, 113°54 E; approximately 30 m above the ground), and there were no major sources of pollution nearby. Samples were collected in March and April, 2023. The dust source regions in northern China are frequently affected by sandstorms, and the study area is expected to be impacted by dust intrusions originating from these regions. In this study, sampling was conducted separately under rainy weather, normal weather, and dust weather conditions. In this study, a quartz fiber filter membrane (90 mm, Whatman QMA, UK) was coated with tin foil paper and calcined at 450 °C for 8 h in a muffle furnace to eliminate the influence of organic impurities. A medium-flow sampler (LY-2030 A, Laoshan Application, Qingdao, China) was used to capture atmospheric PM2.5. The average flow rate was 100 L/min, and the sampling time was 23.5 h every alternate day. The sampler was disinfected with 75% alcohol before sampling. The collected samples were placed into self-sealing bags and stored at − 20 °C for subsequent sample analysis.

On an ultra-clean bench, the collected quartz filter membrane was cut into two halves with ceramic scissors and transferred to a sterile centrifuge tube containing 50 ml ultra-pure water; centrifuged at 4 °C with 200 g centrifugal force for 3 h in a refrigerated centrifuge (CF16RN, Hitachi, Japan); followed by an ultrasonic water bath shock for 10 min, using a 0.22 μm polyether sulfone (PES) filter membrane (Membrana, Germany) for suction filtration, the filtrate was refrigerated and stored for subsequent non-biological component analysis; the filter membrane was used for subsequent biological component analysis after cryopreservation23.

Data collection

The meteorological factors observed during the sampling period, including PM2.5, SO2, CO, NO2, T, RH, WS, and O3, were obtained from the Henan Province Air Quality Forecasting and Publishing System (http://1.192.88.18:18111/page/PublishingSystem/index.html). These data are summarized in Table S1. The water-soluble anions (F-, Cl-, NO3- and SO42-) and cations (Na+, NH4+, K+, Mg2+ and Ca2+) in the pre-treated filtrate were quantified using ion chromatography (Dionex Aquion IC, Thermo Scientific, USA). Cu, Ni, Pb, Cd, Ba, Mg, Mn, Al, As, and Fe in the samples were all analyzed using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES; Agilent Technologies, Inc., Malaysia), with all metals undetected except As and Fe. The mass concentrations of organic carbon (OC) and elemental carbon (EC) in the samples were detected using a thermal/optical carbon analyzer (DRI Model 2001, Atomslytic Inc., USA). The measured data are presented in Table S2.

16 S rRNA gene sequencing and data processing

First, DNA was extracted from particles enriched on a PES filter membrane. PES membranes containing a large number of microorganisms were cut up, ground into powder, and loaded into a PowerSoil DNA separation kit (MoBio Laboratories, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Other DNA extraction details can be found in previous studies24. The extracted genomic DNA was detected using microspectrophotometry (NanoDrop 2000, Thermo Scientific, USA) and 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. 338 F-806R (5-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3; 5-GGTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3) was used as the primer to analyze the V3–V4 hypervariable region of the 16 S rRNA gene by bacterial sequencing. After amplification, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) products were gelled using a gel recovery kit (AxyPrepDNA, AXYGEN, USA), and then quantified using a blue fluorescence quantification system (QuantiFluor™-ST, Promega, USA). Finally, the bacterial PCR products were sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform. The sequencing results were spliced for quality control based on overlap. The ribosomal database project (RDP) classifier Bayes algorithm was used to conduct a taxonomic analysis of 97% of operational taxonomic units (OTU) representative sequences at similar levels, and the community species composition of each sample was counted at each taxonomic level. In this study, 20 samples were identified using high-throughput sequencing technology, and the average length of the optimized sequences was 417 bp. The samples were grouped according to rainy days, dust weather, and normal weather conditions. All raw sequencing data were submitted to NCBI with an accession number of PRJNA1364731.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 26 (IBM Corp., 2019) and Origin 2021 (OriginLab Corp., 2020) software were used for routine data analysis and plotting, respectively25. SPSS 26 is a branch of statistics used to summarize, organize, and present basic characteristics of a dataset. It includes measures of central tendency, dispersion, and distribution shape, without making inferences about a larger population. The impact of environmental factors on ecological community structure was analyzed using redundancy analysis (RDA). RDA is a combined regression and principal component analysis26.

The figures were drawn using Canoco 5. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was performed using the “vegan” and “ggplot2” packages in R 4.3.0 to study changes in bacterial community structure under different weather conditions. Functional Annotation of Prokaryotic Taxa (FAPROTAX) divides microbial species into functional groups by analyzing the presence and abundance of microbial functional genes27. To evaluate the correlation between functional bacteria involved in carbon, nitrogen and sulfur cycles and chemical composition, the Spearman correlation coefficient was used28.

Results

Bacterial community composition

According to the taxonomic statistics, 44 phyla and 1499 bacterial species were identified (Fig. S2). The similarity and specificity of the OTU components in the environmental samples were analyzed using a Venn diagram29. As shown in Fig. 1a, 196 and 1314 species were unique to rainy days and dust events, respectively. Of these, 45 species were present on both rainy days and during dust events. Although there were some differences in the levels of OTU among the three types of weather, there were 1314 species that were common to all three. The predominant species were Achromobacter sp., Bacillus sp., Methylobacterium_brachiatum and Thermoactinomyces_vulgaris. Fig. S2a shows that the dominant phyla of bacterial aerosols at the taxonomic level were Pseudomonadota (19.0–64.0%), Bacillota (2.0–8.0%), and Actinomyces (2.0–8.0%).

(a) Venn map of bacterial communities at OTU level, (b) intergroup comparative analysis of Beta diversity of bioaerosols under different meteorological conditions (PCoA), and Ace index values (c) and Shannon index values (d) under different weather conditions.

Differences in bacterial communities under different meteorological conditions

To study the differences in bacterial communities in the atmosphere under different weather conditions, alpha diversity analysis was performed on the environmental samples (Fig. 1. (c and d)). The species richness and diversity indices of the bacterial communities were calculated under different weather conditions. The coverage index was close to or equal to one (Table S3). This indicates that the sequencing depth of this study covered all species in the sample, representing the actual situation30. The Chao and Ace indices of dust events were generally high, and the Shannon and Simpson indices were also higher on average than those on rainy days (Table S3). These data indicate that the richness and diversity of the bacterial communities were higher during dust events, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies31,32.

Effects of meteorological conditions on the community structure of airborne functional bacteria

Through literature analysis, a total of 65 potential functional bacteria participating in carbon, nitrogen and sulfur cycles were screened in this study. The types and relative abundances of functional bacteria under different meteorological conditions were analyzed, as shown in Fig. 2, which shows the most representative bacterial phyla.

Community structure of typical functional bacteria at phylum (a) and genus (b) levels under different meteorological conditions and principal component analysis at the genus level of functional bacteria (c).

At the phylum level, Pseudomonadota had the highest relative abundance of functional bacteria for carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur metabolisms under both rainy and normal weather conditions. Actinobacteriota had the highest relative abundance during dust events (Fig. 2a). At the genus level, the highest proportions of Bacillus and Sphingomonas in the samples on rainy days were 39.4%, and 32.5%, respectively. During dust weather, the highest proportions of Bacillus and Sphingomonas were 68.5%, and 21.1%, respectively (Fig. 2b). The first principal axis, PCoA1, explained 42.9% of the variance in the data, indicating the largest difference between samples. The second coordinate axis, PCoA2, explained 17.2% of the variance in the data (Fig. 2c).

Differences in carbon, nitrogen and sulfur cycle pathways under meteorological conditions

Figure 3 shows the differences in the specific pathways of the carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycles under various weather conditions through the FAPROTAX function prediction box plot.

Differences in specific pathways of carbon, nitrogen and sulfur cycling in the air under different meteorological conditions. (a) carbon cycle, (b) sulfur cycle, (c) nitrogen cycle. The color of the line corresponds to the name of each box line diagram. In the box line diagram, red represents normal weather, blue is rainy day, and green is dust weather.

For the carbon cycle, the microbial groups of carbon fixation for OC generation under dust events conditions (0.19%) were distinctly higher than those under normal weather conditions (0.08%) and rainy days (0.01%), although the overall proportion was relatively low (Fig. 3a). However, several processes lead to simultaneous OC consumption, including aerobic respiration, fermentation, and methanogenesis. The overall effect was likely determined by the proportion of carbon cycle bacteria under different meteorological conditions. The highest average proportion of OC consumption was observed during dust events conditions (5.24%), with proportions of 3.88% and 3.07% during normal weather conditions and on rainy days, respectively. Under the overall effect of the carbon cycle functional bacteria, the lowest concentration of OC occurred during dust events (4.51 µg/m3) (Table S2). This may be due to the fact that Bacillus survives easily during dust weather33.

For the sulfur cycle (Fig. 3b), bacteria with thiosulfate and sulfite oxidation functions lead to SO42− accumulation. In this study, the highest proportion of thiosulfate oxidation and sulfate reduction functional microbial groups were observed during rainy days (Fig. 3b), corresponding to the lowest concentration of SO42− (3.07 µg/m3) (Table S2).

For the nitrogen cycle (Fig. 3c), NH4+ accumulated from three microbial metabolism pathways: nitrate reduction, ammoniation, and nitrogen fixation. The total proportions of bacteria in NH4+ accumulation were 10.39%, 12.31%, and 10.93% under normal, rainy, and dust weather conditions, respectively. The average concentrations of NH4+ were 0.48 µg/m3, 0.54 µg/m3 and 0.44 µg/m3 under normal, rainy and dust weather conditions, respectively. For the nitrate consumption, nitrate reduction and denitrification pathway participated, with the former being predominant. The highest relative abundance and lowest NO3− concentrations were found during rainy weather, with values of 12.00% and 5.66 µg/m3, respectively.

Potential environmental factors affecting the carbon, nitrogen and sulfur cycles

The presence and characteristics of microbial aerosols in the atmosphere are unstable and affected by several factors34. In this study, RDA was used to reveal the relationship between environmental factors and the dominant functional genera in the carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycles. The results showed that functional bacteria in the atmosphere were closely related to water-soluble ions, metal elements, carbon substances, and meteorological parameters (Fig. 4). The data on which the meteorological parameters, ions, and metal elements under different meteorological conditions were obtained, are listed in Table S1 and Table S2.

The relationship between functional bacteria and meteorological factors (a), ions and metal elements (b). (c) Correlation heat map of functional bacteria and carbon, nitrogen and sulfur elements. RH, relative humidity; T, temperature; WS, wind speed; OC, organic carbon; EC, elemental carbon.

As shown in Fig. 4a, the effects of meteorological parameters on functional bacteria for carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur metabolisms in the atmosphere are complex. At the genus level, the CO and O3 levels were negatively correlated with Streptomyces, Paenibacillus and Hymenobacter. CO and O3 were positively correlated with the Paracoccus involved in thiosulfate oxidation. It is evident that the same environmental factor exerts varying effects on different functional groups, leading to distinct impacts on the metabolism pathways of carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycling. RH and PM2.5 were significantly correlated with bacteria for carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur metabolisms. The RH was positively correlated with Acinetobacter involved in nitrification, and Sphingomonas was involved in fermentation and nitrogen fixation35. There was a significantly positive correlation between PM2.5 and Cellulomonas involved in fermentation and aerobic respiration.

As shown in Fig. 4b, Na+ and Cl− were negatively correlated with functional bacteria in most PM2.5 samples. High Na+ concentrations may disrupt the function and structure of bacteria, thereby reducing their survival rates36. In contrast, Ca2+ was positively correlated with most bacteria involved in carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur recycles. Ca2+ promotes microbial activity by regulating microbial processes such as cell division and differentiation37. As and Fe were positively correlated with multifunctional bacteria, however, this correlation was not significant.

The correlation between SO42−, NO3−, NH4+ and OC and the activity of functional bacteria was further demonstrated using Spearman correlation coefficient analysis (Fig. 4c). The NH4+ and NO3− levels were significantly correlated with Bacillus. NO3− was positively correlated with Bacillus, whereas NH4+ was negatively correlated. The primary reason for this phenomenon is that Bacillus is involved in the nitrification process in the nitrogen cycle. Simultaneously, NH4+ was significantly and negatively correlated with Nitrosomonas, which is involved in nitrification, and significantly and positively correlated with Sphingomonas, which is involved in nitrogen fixation. SO42− was significantly positively correlated with Pseudomonas involved in sulfite oxidation, and positively correlated with Paracoccus involved in thiosulfate oxidation. OC and EC were negatively correlated with most functional bacteria. These phenomena confirm that microorganisms play an important role in the atmospheric carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycles.

Discussion

Relations of meteorological factors to microbial abundances

As shown in the figure, the proportion of each dominant species varied in the different samples. Compared with rainy days, Bacillota and Actinobacteriota had a higher proportion during dust events, whereas Pseudomonadota had a lower proportion. Actinobacteriota exhibit enhanced persistence in hostile environments, possibly because of their inherent resistance to arid conditions38. At the genera level of genus taxonomy (Fig. S2b), the dominant genera in the bacterial aerosols were Achromobacter (0–18.0%), Bacillus (2.0–12.0%), norank_f__norank_o__Chloroplast (1.0–18.0%), and Sphingomonas (2.0–13.0%). In addition, compared with rainy days, Achromobacter were less abundant during dust events, whereas Bacillus exhibited greater abundance. This may be due to the high resistance of Bacillus, a Gram-positive bacterium capable of forming oval spores, to strong radiation and dry environments, enabling its survival under harsh conditions33,39. In contrast, Achromobacter is a Gram-negative bacterium, and Fang et al.40 and Goffau et al.41 demonstrated that Gram-positive bacteria exhibit not only faster growth under low atmospheric relative humidity (RH) but also superior resistance and viability compared to Gram-negative counterparts.

Impacts of rainy and dust event weathers on airborne bacterial communities

Based on the Bray–Curtis distance algorithm, beta diversity intergroup comparative analysis (PCoA) was performed on the samples in this work8. There were significant differences in species abundance between rainy and dust weather conditions (Fig. 1b). A statistical t-test was employed to determine the significance of the differences in Ace and Shannon index values between normal, rainy, and dust weather conditions (Fig. 1c, d). It was obvious that there were significant differences in species richness and community diversity between rainy days and dust events, with P values < 0.01 and 0.05, respectively. The highest species richness and community diversity were observed during dust events. One possible reason for this phenomenon is that a large amount of foreign dust and microorganisms attach to the surface soil, and many exogenous bacteria are lifted into the atmosphere37,42. This directly affects the activity of microorganisms in the atmosphere and significantly changes the community structure. Factors such as pollution source distribution and particulate matter composition may also affect bacterial community structure, resulting in significant differences in atmospheric bacterial community structure in different weather conditions. Vehicle exhaust and fuel combustion can produce secondary aerosols such as SO42− and NO3−, which affect the PM2.5 bacterial community43. Various compounds and toxins contained in particulate matter may promote or inhibit microbial growth, further affecting bacterial community institutions39,44.

Potential relationship between atmospheric biological aerosols and the carbon, nitrogen and sulfur cycles

Bacteria in atmospheric bioaerosols participate in carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycle pathways, which in turn change the forms of elements in the atmosphere5,45. In this study, Bacillus, Sphingomonas, Pseudomonas, Paracoccus, Acinetobacter and Streptomyces were the primary functional bacteria present in the atmosphere (Fig. 2b). Bacillus is often present as the dominant bacterium in PM2.5, with the ability to participate in the carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycles46,47. Sphingomonas is primarily involved in fermentation and nitrogen fixation35. Pseudomonas may be involved in atmospheric processes such as ammoniation, denitrification, and sulfite oxidation36. Paracoccus and Acinetobacter are involved in nitrification and denitrification48, and Paracoccus is associated with thiosulfate oxidation49. Streptomyces affects the fermentation and aerobic respiration cycle pathways50.

Figure 2 showed that the types of functional bacteria in PM2.5 samples collected during normal, rainy, and dust weather conditions were similar, however, their relative abundances differed greatly. This indicates that meteorological conditions have varying effects on the relative abundance of functional bacteria for carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur metabolisms. A similar phenomenon has been reported by María et al.51. The relative amounts of functional bacteria were also found varied under same meteorological condition. A possible reason for this phenomenon was that the characteristics of functional bacteria were different, the manner in which they existed in the atmosphere, and the probability of survival under different conditions also differed39. In addition, the microbial similarity between the PM2.5 samples were analyzed using the PCoA method based on the distance matrix52. Distinct community structures of functional bacteria were observed between rainy days and sandstorm periods. On rainy days, the elevated atmospheric relative humidity (RH) may modify the surface properties and hygroscopicity of functional bacteria-associated particles, thereby facilitating the wet deposition of these particulates19. Dust events carry a large number of particles in the atmosphere, and a variety of bacteria and viruses are adsorbed onto the surface of the particulate matter53. Compared with dust events, the community structure of functional bacteria on rainy days was similar to that during normal weather. These data show that the abundance and viability of functional bacteria involved in carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycles in the atmosphere differ under various weather conditions.

The results of this study indicated that the average concentrations of OC, SO42−, NH4+ and NO3− in the atmosphere also differed on normal, rainy, and dust events days (Table S2). At the genus level, Bacillus, Sphingomonas and Acinetobacter were the dominant functional bacteria in normal weather conditions, accounting for 10.9%, 3.1%, and 1.9% of the total bacteria, respectively (Fig. 5a). The average proportion of Sphingomonas (5.1%) among the total bacteria on rainy days was slightly higher than that of Bacillus (5.0%) (Fig. 5b). During dust events, the proportion of Bacillus (16.2%) was significantly higher than that of Sphingomonas (4.3%) (Fig. 5c). Bacillus consumes OC and NH4+ by participating in metabolic pathways such as fermentation, aerobic respiration, and nitrification8(Ma et al., 2024). The lowest OC and NH4+ concentrations were observed on dust events days. Sphingomonas participates in metabolic pathways, such as fermentation and nitrogen fixation, to reduce OC and accumulate NH4+. The highest relative abundance of the sum of Bacillus and Sphingomonas occurred during dust events, which is a possible reason for the lowest average concentrations of OC and NH4+ under these conditions (Table S2). Paracoccus participated in thiosulfate oxidation, accumulated SO42−, and the lowest proportion of Paracoccus was found under dust weather conditions (0.5%), corresponding to the lowest average concentration of SO42− (Fig. 5b).

Average proportion of functional bacteria in (a) normal, (b) rainy and (c) dust weather at the genus level.

In this study, the concentrations of various forms of carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur generally corresponded to the relative abundances of the functional microbial groups in PM2.5. These results indicate that atmospheric elemental cycles are closely associated with bioaerosols. Bioaerosol-driven formation and transformation processes may constitute a crucial component of carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycling in natural environments. However, bioaerosols and PM2.5, are primarily derived from the long-range transport and near-surface sources such as soil, water, plants, and human activity54. Based on the results of this study, regardless of the source of functional microorganisms, their effects on the carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycles exist and may further influence atmospheric chemistry.

This study revealed the potential impact of functional microorganisms on carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycles in the atmosphere. These microorganisms can use SO2, NO2, other pollutants, and water-soluble ions to perform metabolic activity under different meteorological conditions, thereby affecting their content and atmospheric chemical reactions54,55. The influence of meteorological conditions on microbial community structure cannot be ignored. Weather, such as rainy days and dust events, shapes different communities of bacteria in the atmosphere and further influences the atmospheric environment.

Conclusion

This study investigated the effects of meteorological conditions on the community structure of functional microorganisms in PM2.5 involved in the carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycles in the atmosphere, in central China. Significant disparities existed in both the bacterial concentrations and relative abundances of functional bacteria among normal, rainy, and dust events days, with a close association observed between these variations and atmospheric carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycles. Owing to variations in the dominant functional bacteria under meteorological conditions, the metabolic activity of the carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycle pathways also varied, resulting in changes in the forms of atmospheric elements. The conclusions of this study provide a new perspective and data reference for future research on atmospheric environmental changes.
