Outcomes of Surgical Strategies for Prosthetic Joint Infection Following Unicompartmental Knee Arthroplasty: A 2-Year Follow-up
Authors: Baijian Wu, Zhishuo Zhang, Ruan Chen, Zhenggui Yu, Ye Yang, Wenbo Li, Wenming Zhang, Xinyu Fang, Zida Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThere are limited guidelines on the standard surgical management of unicompartmental prosthetic joint infection (uPJI). The purpose of this study was to review the infection control and functional recovery of a standardized institutional treatment protocol, which included debridement, antibiotics, and implant retention (DAIR) and revision, to provide insights into the proper management of uPJI.MethodsTwenty-seven patients (28 knees) diagnosed with uPJI according to the Musculoskeletal Infection Society (MSIS) criteria from 2017 to 2023 were analyzed. Patients with acute or hematogenous uPJI underwent DAIR, whereas chronic infections were managed with revision strategies according to an institutional protocol; these cohorts represent different infection chronicity categories and were not intended for direct comparative effectiveness analysis. The follow-up ranged from 24 to 102 months. Demographic information, infection types, clinical outcomes, functional assessments, and culture results were recorded.ResultsInfection control, defined according to MSIS outcome reporting criteria within 1 year after the index surgery, was achieved in 89.3% of cases (25 / 28): 80% after DAIR (12 / 15) and 100% after revision (13 / 13). Chronic uPJI cases received 2-stage revision with a unicompartmental spacer (n = 10), 2-stage revision with a bicompartmental spacer (n = 1), or 1-stage total knee arthroplasty (TKA) (n = 2). Three failures required reoperation (2 DAIR and 1 two-stage revision with a unicompartmental spacer). Unicompartmental spacers were retained in 4 two-stage cases. At 2-year follow-up, range of motion, Hospital for Special Surgery scores, visual analog scale, and 12-item Short Form Health Survey all improved significantly in both the DAIR and the revision patients.ConclusionsDAIR is suitable for acute or hematogenous uPJI, whereas revision is suitable for chronic uPJI. One-stage revision to TKA and 2-stage revision with unicompartmental spacers may be appropriate in carefully selected patients.

The number of unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA) procedures is increasing because of its advantages in preserving more bone, cartilage, and ligaments; accelerating recovery; and improving function.12) As in total knee arthroplasty (TKA), prosthetic joint infection (PJI) is a catastrophic complication after UKA.34) Previous studies have reported wide variation in the incidence of unicompartmental PJI (uPJI). Labruyere et al.5) reported a 0.2% incidence of uPJI; another study involving 1,576 UKA procedures also reported a relatively low incidence of uPJI.6) In contrast, a multicenter study reported incidences of uPJI ranging from 1.9% to 4.6% in different regions.7) As the number of UKA procedures increases,8) uPJI has become a problem that cannot be ignored.910)

In traditional TKA, 2-stage revision is considered the preferred treatment for chronic PJI,11) whereas for acute PJI, debridement, antibiotics, and implant retention (DAIR) are preferred.12) The efficacy of traditional treatment strategies in the treatment of PJI after UKA has not been fully investigated, and it is unclear whether the treatment of PJI after UKA should mirror that of PJI after TKA.13) The International Consensus Meeting (ICM) guidelines recommend complete removal of additional knee compartments during 2-stage revision for uPJI.513) This approach is supported by Goodfellow et al.,14) who advocated for the removal of compartments. In contrast, Yun et al.15) used a modified spacer to successfully control the uPJI, which preserved the existing anatomy of the patellofemoral joint and lateral compartment. A notable feature of uPJI is that it may involve both the prosthesis and residual cartilage. Cartilage destruction and subsequent progressive arthritis are potential causes of PJI treatment failure.13) Therefore, precise infection delineation is critical in the treatment of uPJI, which directly impacts existing anatomy preservation.

At present, there is no consensus among surgeons on the standard surgical management of uPJI due to the limited number of reports and the lack of relevant studies of uPJI. We investigated whether the other compartments of the joint are anatomically intact and the pathogen does not invade the cartilage, subchondral bone or cancellous bone of the other compartments. The retained natural cartilage resulting from a unicompartmental spacer may be more resistant to persistent biofilms. Theoretically, concurrent preservation of viable tissue and improvement of the blood supply may increase antibiotic delivery and aid in infection control.15) In this study, we reviewed the clinical outcomes of uPJI patients at a single PJI center, aiming to assess the infection control and functional recovery of a standardized treatment protocol for uPJI, particularly focusing on the use of unicompartmental spacers in localized chronic infection.

METHODS

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University prior to commencement (IRB No. MTCA,ECFAH Of FMU [2015]084-2). All the authors have reviewed and confirmed the accuracy of the whole manuscript. All the experimental contents and methods have been obtained with the informed consent of the patients.

Demographic Data

This was a continuous single-center retrospective study of patients who were surgically treated for uPJI at the Bone and Joint Infection Centre of our institution between June 2017 and January 2023. The diagnosis of uPJI was based on the Musculoskeletal Infection Society (MSIS) criteria,16) which include 2 major criteria and 6 minor criteria (Supplementary Table 1). The type of uPJI was classified as acute postoperative infection (symptom duration < 4 weeks and < 4 weeks after surgery), hematogenous infection (symptom duration < 4 weeks and > 4 weeks after surgery), or chronic infection (symptom duration > 4 weeks) according to the Tsukayama classification.17) Exclusion criteria were patients with inflammatory joint disease and patients with incomplete medical history or follow-up data. All demographic data, inflammation markers, perioperative management, and clinical outcomes were recorded in detail.

Surgical Methods

All procedures were performed by the same team of orthopedic surgeons. Surgical decisions were made based on the ICM. In general, patients with longer symptom durations (> 4 weeks) were treated with revision, whereas patients with shorter symptom durations were treated with DAIR (< 4 weeks).17) The operating range and the decision to use a unicompartmental spacer versus 1-stage conversion to TKA were routinely planned by preoperative metal artifact reduction sequence magnetic resonance imaging (MARS-MRI) and confirmed by intraoperative direct vision at our institution.1819)

Our study included 15 knees treated with DAIR and 13 patients treated with revision. Of the acute or hematogenous infection cases managed with DAIR, 4 patients were primary cases treated at our institution, whereas 11 were referred from other hospitals following symptom onset. Referred patients were evaluated using the same institutional diagnostic and treatment protocol after admission. DAIR involves complete debridement, synovectomy, and replacement of the polyethylene insert, which was similar to our previous experience.20) The difference is that debridement of the posterior space of the contralateral compartment in uPJI is difficult. A curved curette was used to clean the posterior aspect of the infected chamber to thoroughly remove the bone cement debris, followed by a deep rinse with at least 6 L of sterile 0.9% saline, 3% hydrogen peroxide, and liquid iodine using a pulsed douche to create a gap between the cruciate ligament and the femoral condyle. Finally, a completely new unicompartmental insert was reimplanted. To protect the ligament and meniscus, we entered the joint through a diagonal incision of the quadriceps muscle to minimize damage to the patellar ligament. During debridement, a combination of blunt dissection and sharp dissection was employed to prevent excessive traction or tearing of the ligament and meniscus. During the debridement and handling of the prosthesis, we ensured the prosthesis position was correct and avoided excessive compression or friction on the surrounding ligament and menisci. If necessary, we adjusted the prosthesis position or replaced its components.

Revision techniques mainly included removal of all infected prostheses, thorough debridement, and subsequent implantation of a spacer or revision components. During revision surgery, the removed prosthesis was sonicated, and the sonication fluid was collected for culture.21) At our institution, revision surgery mainly consisted of 1-stage revision to TKA and 2-stage unicompartmental revision with preservation of other compartment structures. The 2-stage unicompartmental revision involves complete removal of all the primary surgical components at the time of the first-stage surgery, followed by implantation of the unicompartmental spacer. When the infection was under control, patients could choose 2-stage replantation according to their own economic and physical conditions. This treatment is rarely reported.15) Therefore, we only evaluated (1) chronic uPJI; (2) localized infection on MARS-MRI, defined as infection signals confined to the prosthetic compartment, including synovitis, localized bone marrow edema adjacent to the prosthesis, or periprosthetic fluid collections, without evidence of cartilage destruction, subchondral bone edema, or abscess formation in the contralateral compartment (Fig. 1 compares the MARS-MRI results of localized and non-localized infections); and (3) localized infection, confirmed visually by the surgeon during surgery. Intraoperative frozen-section histopathology was performed when necessary to support infection diagnosis. A threshold of ≥ 5 neutrophils per high-power field in at least 5 high-power fields was considered suggestive of active infection. This technique was performed when uPJI met these conditions and the patient gave informed consent.

Preparation of a Unicompartmental Spacer

First, we confirmed the absence of infection or degenerative changes in the remaining intra-articular compartments of the patients, along with satisfactory condition of the local soft tissues and ligaments. Using a test model, we assessed the position of the tibial prosthesis, joint line reconstruction, and the extent of distal and posterior condylar defects to restore balance in the flexion-extension gap and the lower limb mechanical axis. Next, antibiotic-loaded bone cement was applied to secure polyethylene spacers or to manually fashion tibial spacers. After the cement had hardened, the femoral component was removed, and the augmentation was fixed with fresh bone cement. In cases where polyethylene spacers (Zimmer Biomet) were used, their posterior surfaces were textured with serrations to enhance contact area on the tibial side. Spacers were fabricated using antibiotic-impregnated bone cement and secured in place via the model. Finally, the femoral prosthesis was implanted above the tibial component in a highly flexed position.

Perioperative Management

Antibiotics were withheld for 2–4 weeks prior to aspiration in all patients with chronic infection. Arthrocentesis was performed before surgery. Joint fluid was collected for cell count analysis, culture, and metagenomic next-generation sequencing testing. Etiology and drug-resistance results were interpreted according to previously reported standard protocols.212223) For patients who underwent revision, cement (Heraeus) mixed with antibiotics was used to prepare spacers or fix the prosthesis (Zimmer Biomet). Sensitive antibiotics were used in patients with positive culture results before surgery. If the patient’s preoperative culture was negative, 3 g of vancomycin and 1 g of meropenem were added to 40 g of cement. All patients were treated with oral antibiotics after 2–4 weeks of intravenous antibiotics. The overall course depended on the type of pathogen and infection control. Patients with positive culture results were treated with sensitive antibiotics according to the drug sensitivity results. Patients with negative culture results were treated with vancomycin + meropenem for 2–4 weeks, followed by treatment with oral quinolones and rifampicin.

Partial or complete weight-bearing was allowed postoperatively for all infected joints, and patients were required to undergo active and passive rehabilitation. All patients were re-examined regularly at 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and every year after the operation. Functional outcomes were evaluated at the latest follow-up with a minimum duration of 2 years by range of motion (ROM), the modified Hospital for Special Surgery (HSS) knee score, the 12-item Short Form Health Survey (SF-12), and the visual analog scale (VAS) at the latest follow-up (minimum 2 years).24) For patients undergoing 2-stage revision with a unicompartmental spacer, functional scores reported in this study were assessed after the second-stage reimplantation. Whereas infection control was assessed within 1 year after the index procedure according to the MSIS criteria,25) it was defined as the absence of local inflammation, infection/aseptic revision, or uPJI-related death within 1 year of the initial surgery, with or without long-term antibiotic suppression and with normal inflammatory marker levels. Patients undergoing DAIR and those undergoing revision surgery represented different infection chronicity categories. Therefore, no direct efficacy comparison analysis was conducted between these 2 groups.

Statistical Analysis

The information is presented as the frequency of each category and the percentage of the total number of variables that can be grouped into categories. For variables that have a range of values, the average value and how much these values vary (standard deviation) are provided. To analyze the categorical data, either Fisher’s exact test or Pearson’s chi-square test was used. Continuous variables were confirmed to follow a normal distribution by the Shapiro-Wilk test, and an independent-sample t-test was utilized. The statistical software IBM SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp.) was used for all these analyses. A p-value of < 0.05 was used as the threshold to determine if the results were statistically significant.

RESULTS

Demographics of Cases

All patients completed at least 2 years of follow-up and the follow-up period ranged from 24 to 102 months. A total of 27 patients (28 knees), including 15 males and 13 females, were included in the study. One patient had bilateral involvement, resulting in 28 knees from 27 patients. Each knee was analyzed as an independent surgical case. The age range was 54-87 years. The mean follow-up duration was 45 ± 25.6 months. The mean body mass index was 24.8 ± 3.5 kg/m2 and the mean Charlson Comorbidity Index was 3.58 ± 1.63. The mean antibiotic use duration was 10.5 ± 2.4 weeks. Primary surgery was performed on 27 knees treated with medial UKA and 1 treated with lateral UKA. The infections included 11 cases of acute postoperative infection, 4 cases of hematogenous infection, and 13 cases of chronic infection. Among the included patients with acute or hematogenous uPJI, 4 cases were initially treated at our institution and 11 cases were referred from outside hospitals after the onset of infection symptoms. The detailed demographic data are shown in Table 1.

Treatment and Clinical Outcomes

The treatment and clinical outcomes are shown in Table 2. The overall infection control rate within 1 year was 89.3% (25 / 28). The infection control rates were 80% and 100% in patients treated with DAIR and revision, respectively. Infection recurred in 3 of the 15 patients who underwent DAIR. Two of these patients underwent repeated DAIR, and the infection was eventually controlled; in the remaining patient, all unicompartmental components were removed, and a unicompartmental spacer was implanted. Reimplantation to TKA was performed in the second stage. The infection was controlled in all 13 patients with chronic uPJI. Among the 10 patients in the study with unicompartmental spacers, 6 patients underwent reimplantation. One unicompartmental spacer fracture occurred 3 years after the first-stage revision, and the patient subsequently underwent reimplantation according to the established procedure (Fig. 2). Kaplan-Meier curves for non-infective survival for initial treatment and prosthetic survival for all-cause unplanned reoperation are shown in Fig. 3. For Kaplan-Meier analysis, events for non-infective survival were defined as recurrence of infection requiring surgical intervention, while events for prosthetic survival included any unplanned reoperation regardless of cause. Patients without events were censored at the time of last follow-up. The outcomes of the different treatments are shown in a tree diagram (Fig. 4).

The detailed preoperative and 2-year follow-up functional status are shown in Table 3. For patients undergoing 2-stage revision, functional scores in Table 3 were evaluated after second-stage reimplantation. Four patients retained their unicompartmental spacers without reimplantation. Therefore, these cases were not included in Table 3 analyses. At the latest follow-up, these patients demonstrated a mean ROM of approximately 85°–100° and were able to perform basic daily activities with acceptable pain control. Both the DAIR and the revision significantly improved pain and function at the 2-year follow-up. In terms of joint function, the 2-year follow-up knee joint function of the patients significantly improved. The mean modified HSS score improved from 49.4 to 82.8 (mean difference 2.96, 95% CI 1.69–4.23, p < 0.001) and from 53.0 to 75.8 (mean difference 1.60, 95% CI 0.62–2.58, p = 0.005), respectively. Similarly, there was a significant improvement in the ROM of infected joints (96.9° ± 10.3° vs. 72.9° ± 17.7°, mean difference 1.66, 95% CI 0.73–2.59, p < 0.001; 92.8° ± 12.4° vs. 67.7° ± 14.7°, mean difference 1.85, 95% CI 0.81–2.89, p < 0.001). Clinical outcomes revealed a significant reduction in the VAS score at follow-up (4.3 to 1.4, mean difference 3.60, 95% CI 2.13–5.07, p < 0.001; 4.7 to 1.6, mean difference 2.96, 95% CI 1.59–4.33, p < 0.001). Moreover, the overall quality of life (SF-12) improved (47.2± 8.9 vs. 34.4 ± 8.5, mean difference 1.47, 95% CI 0.59–2.35, p < 0.001; 46.0 ± 8.4 vs. 36.8 ± 9.4, mean difference 1.03, 95% CI 0.17–1.89, p = 0.014). There were no statistically significant differences in preoperative and follow-up pain and function including VAS, HSS, SF-12, and ROM between the DAIR and revision groups.

Culture Results

The positive rate of culture was 92.86% (26 / 28) (Table 2). Microbiological analysis revealed that the most common pathogens were coagulase-negative staphylococci (CoNS; n = 17), Mycoplasma hominis (n = 3) and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA; n = 2), with methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus, Paracoccus yeei, Candida albicans, and Ralstonia pickettii occurring in 1 patient each. There was no significant difference between the types of microorganisms and infection control (p = 0.845).

DISCUSSION

UKA is a reliable method for the treatment of single-compartment osteoarthritis (OA) with satisfactory clinical outcomes. As the number of UKA procedures increases, the number of uPJI cases is expected to increase accordingly.26) However, there is little relevant literature and no consensus on the management of uPJI. Guidelines for the treatment of uPJI include DAIR, 1-stage revision to TKA, and 2-stage revision to TKA, as described by the ICM.513) The present study demonstrated a relatively conservative surgical strategy for uPJI. Our results indicate that a symptom-duration–stratified approach conceptually similar to the traditional PJI algorithm is feasible and yields acceptable short- to mid-term infection control in our center for uPJI management.

In this study, the rate of septic reoperation after DAIR was 20% (3 / 15), which was similar to the rate reported by Hernandez et al.13) It has also been shown that chondrocytes and subchondral osteocytes can impede infection control. However, a recent multicenter study confirmed the efficacy of DAIR in the treatment of acute uPJI.27) McCormick et al.27) analyzed the clinical outcomes of 52 patients with acute uPJI, the largest number to date, and reported that DAIR is a safe and moderately effective procedure. Previous studies have demonstrated that staphylococcal infection is an independent risk factor for failure after TKA DAIR.28) Although the number of patients in this study did not allow for an association between the organism and infection control, all 3 failures were treated with DAIR for staphylococcal infection (CoNS and MRSA). Given this similarity of results,27) the management of acute staphylococcal uPJI should be further optimized. Notably, cartilage may be damaged in the early stage of infection, and the intra-articular pressure gradually increases with the destruction of cartilage and soft tissue.29) The destruction of cartilage and ligaments (caused by infection or debridement) could theoretically lead to the progression of OA in other compartments, further contributing to UKA failure after DAIR. Hernandez et al.13) and Chalmers et al.30) reported 2 cases and 1 case, respectively, of further conversion to TKA after DAIR due to disease progression. Fortunately, there were no similar cases in this study; however, this study represents only the results of short- to medium-term follow-up.

Hernandez et al.13) suggested that patients with a longer duration of uPJI or more severe host and/or limb conditions need to undergo revision. In the present study, most of the patients with chronic infection underwent revision surgery. During revision management, we chose to use an articulating unicompartmental spacer to control infection and maintain joint function, in contrast to the approach used in previous studies.1330) We confirmed localized uPJI by both MARS-MRI and direct vision. The results confirm that this technique is viable. It is necessary to clarify that to the best of our knowledge, there is currently no direct evidence that residual cartilage causes the failure of uPJI control. Previous studies have also emphasized that pathogens retained on components lacking biological activity are significant factors contributing to the persistence of PJI.31) In contrast, biologically active anatomical structures might be potential antibacterial barriers. At the time of the initial operation, direct conversion from UKA to TKA can be difficult because of the presence of bone defects, the need for augmentation, and the need for restoration of the joint line and rotation.32) Preservation of bone stock and intact ligaments in the unaffected compartments with unicompartmental spacers may preserve as much bone as possible and provide a reference for potential subsequent surgery. Maintaining near-physiological joint mechanics and kinematics with the articulating spacer may contribute to the observed functional improvement even before final reimplantation and simplify rehabilitation. While the hypothesis that retained viable cartilage enhances resistance to biofilm persistence requires further investigation,28) our 100% infection control rate in this subgroup suggests that thorough debridement of the infected compartment combined with effective local/systemic antibiotics can achieve eradication without removing uninvolved tissues. However, these encouraging infection control results are only the early to mid-term outcomes and their long-term results are still unknown. Due to factors such as cartilage involvement and compartmental progression, UKA still has the potential for long-term failure.32) Therefore, these results warrant further follow-up.

Another application of this research is that the MARS-MRI offers a novel approach in uPJI management. T2-weighted fat-suppressed sequences detect subchondral bone marrow edema patterns and cartilage fissures—features predictive of pathogen invasion into adjacent compartments.3334) Along with employing high-bandwidth sequences and multi-acquisition techniques, this modality effectively suppresses metallic artifacts from unicompartmental implants, enabling visualization of critical interfaces that conventional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) obscures.35) As a result, it is often difficult to distinguish the metal-bone interface and the soft tissue around the prosthesis on conventional MRI. In our previous study, MARS-MRI was able to identify the hip PJI at an early stage and define its extent.18) To the best of our knowledge, this is the first time that this technique has been applied to uPJI to define the extent of infection. In our cohort, MARS-MRI findings were concordant with intraoperative observations in 11 of 13 revision cases (84.6%), while 2 cases demonstrated minor discrepancies regarding suspected contralateral synovial involvement, highlighting its capability for precise location of infectious lesions (Figs. 5 and 6). This capability allows surgeons to preoperatively anticipate the involvement of other compartments, significantly informing the surgical strategy. This potentially spares patients from unnecessary removal of healthy cartilage and bone in uninvolved compartments.

The long-term retention of a unicompartmental spacer represents an unconventional yet clinically valuable option for selected patients who are not suitable for reimplantation. Among the 4 retained-spacer patients in our series, knee ROM ranged from 85° to 100° and all patients maintained independent ambulation without signs of recurrent infection during follow-up. The preserved bone stock and collateral ligament integrity provided sufficient stability for limited daily activity. However, potential risks such as progressive bone resorption, spacer wear, or secondary loosening remain concerns. Close clinical and radiographic monitoring is essential, and reimplantation should be reconsidered if mechanical or infectious complications develop.3637)

In addition to controlling infection, the purpose of uPJI treatment is to maintain joint function. In the treatment of PJI after TKA, articulating spacers improve joint function better than do static spacers.3839) Therefore, all patients in our study who underwent 2-stage revision received articulating spacers to facilitate postoperative functional exercise.39) Notably, all patients in the study had significant improvements in postoperative ROM and HSS scores at the 2-year follow-up. This finding is consistent with previous results showing that both DAIR and revision were effective in improving joint function.2740) Similarly, pain perception and quality of life are significantly improved after systemic anti-infection management.40) However, it is important to note that functional improvements observed within each group should therefore be interpreted as within-group recovery rather than comparative treatment effectiveness. The reason is that the DAIR group consisted primarily of acute infections and the revision group consisted of chronic infections. The confounding factors resulting from different infection levels are not intended for effectiveness comparison.

Apart from the main goal of infection control, the surgical strategy selection must also take into account its related medical and economic impacts, as well as surgical morbidity. DAIR remains the preferred treatment option for acute infection, but its economic feasibility highly depends on the success rate of its initial treatment. Although it is costly and associated with many complications, 2-stage revision is an indispensable solution for the most complex cases with severe bone loss or resistant bacteria. While 1-stage revision becomes an attractive option, it can balance excellent infection control effects, better patient-centered treatment outcomes, and economic efficiency in patients with appropriate selection. Ultimately, this decision should vary from person to person, taking into account the specific circumstances of the patient, microbiological data, as well as the available institutional expertise and resources.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, this was a small sample size, retrospective study without a control group, which reflects the rarity of uPJI and introduces potential statistical bias. Owing to the limited follow-up time, it is difficult to compare the long-term clinical efficacy of the management of chronic uPJI. In a previous report,30) patients who underwent DAIR or revision still underwent aseptic revision for other reasons, such as OA progression or prosthetic loosening. Secondly, the small sample size also precluded stratified analysis by infection severity or microbiological characteristics. In addition, it should also be acknowledged that symptom duration, although commonly used in clinical algorithms, represents an imperfect surrogate for biofilm maturity and does not fully account for host factors, pathogen virulence, or implant stability. This is important for other medical staff applying the protocol. Furthermore, the use of MARS-MRI to evaluate the extent of infection was gradually incorporated into our clinical workflow after 2021, which may have influenced the selection of surgical strategies, particularly the use of unicompartmental spacers in highly selected cases. Therefore, treatment decisions in later cases may have benefited from improved imaging assessment compared with earlier cases. This temporal evolution in diagnostic strategy may introduce a degree of selection bias.

A symptom-duration–stratified approach similar to traditional PJI algorithms was feasible for uPJI management in our center, for which DAIR is effective for acute or hematogenous uPJI, while 1-stage TKA is applicable to chronic uPJI. For chronic uPJI highly localized to the implanted compartment, 2-stage revision with a unicompartmental spacer is a viable and effective treatment option, achieving high rates of infection eradication and functional recovery while preserving native anatomy in unaffected compartments. This strategy offers a bone and tissue-preserving alternative to conventional complete compartment removal. Future larger, prospective, multicenter validation studies with longer follow-up are warranted to confirm these findings and refine patient selection criteria.
