Physician Management of Non-Cavity Distorting Intramural Fibroids and Infertility: A Survey of REI Providers
Authors: Megan K Wasson, Daniela Diego, John C Petrozza, Jennifer F Kawwass, Michael F Neblett 2nd
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeUterine fibroids commonly impact women of reproductive age, with the submucosal subtype known to adversely affect fertility. Recently, non-cavity distorting intramural fibroids have been found to impair the uterine environment leading many reproductive endocrinologists to surgically remove these fibroids prior to infertility treatment. However, the frequency with which this intervention occurs in actual practice remains in question.Patients and MethodsA database of 407 US-based reproductive endocrinology and infertility (REI) clinics was synthesized from the Centers for Disease Control & Prevention National ART Surveillance System and the Society for Assisted Reproductive Technology’s Clinic List. Medical directors at each site were invited to complete a REDCap-administered cross-sectional survey on management of non-cavity distorting intramural fibroids in patients pursuing infertility treatment.ResultsA total of 112 (28%) REI physicians completed the survey, representing a variety of clinic settings, locations, and volumes. The majority of respondents (72%) consider surgical management of non-cavity distorting intramural fibroids based on factors such as size, with myomas 4.1–6 cm or larger more likely to be considered for removal. Approximately 42% of REI physician respondents surgically remove non-cavity distorting intramural fibroids themselves, while 58% refer to other gynecologic surgeons.ConclusionMany physicians consider the removal of non-cavity distorting intramural fibroids prior to infertility treatment. Additional research is needed to better understand the indications for surgical management of various fibroid subtypes and the true scope of removal in current practice.Keywords: infertility, fibroids, in vitro fertilization, myomectomy, survey

Introduction

Uterine fibroids (leiomyomas or myomas) are noncancerous solid neoplasms that originate from uterine smooth muscle cells and fibroblasts.1 They are highly prevalent, affecting up to 70–80% of women during their reproductive years.2 Common symptoms associated with fibroids include abnormal uterine bleeding, anemia, pain, pelvic pressure, urinary frequency, constipation, and obstetric complications.3 In addition to these symptoms, fibroids can impair fertility, reducing both natural fertility and the likelihood of live birth through assisted reproductive technology (ART).4 Fibroids are the sole cause of infertility in 2–3% of cases and are also linked to pregnancy loss. Women with fibroids experience a 60% increased risk of miscarriage.5,6

The impact of uterine fibroids on fertility as well as quality of life, lost time at work, and total healthcare expenditures underscores the need for intervention.2,7 However, the exact approach, operative or nonoperative, and indication for treatment remain controversial. In 2017, the American Society for Reproductive Medicine (ASRM) published guidelines regarding the role of fibroid removal in asymptomatic patients for optimization of fertility outcomes and reduction of miscarriage rates.8,9 They found fair evidence that hysteroscopic myomectomy for submucosal myomas improves clinical pregnancy rates, drawing from many studies that reported a significant decrease in pregnancy rates following in vitro fertilization (IVF) in patients with uterine cavity distortion (9%) compared to those without distortion (29.5%).4,10–16 Insufficient evidence was available for the formulation of recommendations regarding the removal of intramural fibroids with no cavitary distorting component.8

Since then, new research has emerged in support of the idea that non-cavity distorting fibroids impact fertility as well. To date, nine meta-analyses have examined this specific subtype, with most linking non-cavity distorting fibroids to poorer IVF outcomes.17–25 All analyses published after the release of the 2017 guidelines demonstrate a negative impact. Recent studies suggest possible mechanisms through which non-cavity distorting fibroids impair fertility, including reduced blood supply, delayed embryo transport, altered expression of factors necessary for implantation, and disruption of an array of critical processes involved in placentation. Additionally, these fibroids may also contribute to fallopian tube obstruction and increased uterine contractility, thereby reducing access to and hospitability of the uterine environment.4,26

ASRM guidelines have not yet been updated to incorporate these more recent findings. In order to maximize fertility outcomes in women with non-cavity distorting intramural fibroids, many Reproductive Endocrinology and Infertility (REI) specialists have begun to look towards surgical intervention prior to IVF or frozen embryo transfer. This is especially true for fibroids ≥ 4–6 cm, though some advocate for removal of fibroids ≥ 2.0 cm depending on location.4 This approach remains controversial, and further investigation is needed to better understand the frequency and type of operative management that occurs in actual practice as well as the counseling involved in this decision making. Thus, the purpose of this study was to explore the current landscape with regards to operative management of non-cavity distorting intramural fibroids to inform current treatment standards and identify specific avenues of future research.

Materials and Methods

REI physicians currently practicing within the United States (US) were invited to complete an anonymous cross-sectional survey regarding their approach to management of non-cavity distorting intramural fibroids in female patients pursuing IVF. An initial recruitment email was sent to a total of 407 individuals. Two rounds of follow up emails encouraging participation were sent to non-responders.

Completion of the survey required approximately 5–10 minutes of time, and no personally identifiable information was collected. The study did not meet criteria for human subjects research or clinical investigation per policies and regulations put forth by the Emory University Institutional Review Board and was accordingly deemed exempt from review.

Study Participants

Recruitment occurred via email drawing from a database of REI providers employed at ART clinics across the US. This database was generated by cross referencing clinics registered in the Centers for Disease Control & Prevention National ART Surveillance System (2019) with the Society for Assisted Reproductive Technology’s Clinic List (2021).27–29 Clinics no longer in operation, located in US territories, or without public contact information were excluded from the final database.

Eligibility was determined by asking respondents about their current practice as an REI attending followed by informed consent. If participants answered no or did not provide consent to participate, the survey was immediately terminated. Participants were given the opportunity to enter their email at the conclusion of the survey for a chance to win a $100 Amazon gift card. Importantly, participants maintained the right to withdraw from the survey at any point by choosing to abort partially completed forms.

Survey Development & Distribution

The survey consisted of 23 questions formatted as free text, Likert scale, select all that apply, drop down, and multiple choice. Content of the survey involved questions about diagnosis and management of non-cavity distorting intramural fibroids in patients pursuing infertility treatment. Queries pertaining to fibroid location followed the categorization system put forth by the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) which classifies fibroid subtypes based on position within the uterine wall and proximity to the endometrial cavity.9 Per this classification, subserosal fibroids (FIGO types 5–7) are located on the outer surface, intramural fibroids (FIGO types 3 and 4) reside within the uterine wall, and submucosal fibroids (FIGO types 0–2) protrude into the uterine cavity. Additional questions regarding preferred operative approach were included for providers who affirmed their decision to pursue surgical intervention under certain circumstances as well as questions to assess whether REI providers surgically manage fibroids themselves or refer patients to other gynecologic surgeons. The survey was piloted by a small group of REI physicians from three different clinics to ensure clarity and refine content before broader distribution.

The final survey was distributed through the REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) electronic data capture tool hosted at Emory University over the course of six weeks. REDCap is a secure, web-based software platform designed to support data capture for research studies, providing 1) an intuitive interface for validated data capture; 2) audit trails for tracking data manipulation and export procedures; 3) automated export procedures for seamless data downloads to common statistical packages; and 4) procedures for data integration and interoperability with external sources.30,31

Statistical Analysis

Analysis of the data took place over the course of four weeks after the conclusion of the recruitment period. Survey responses were analyzed using SAS 9.4 software with graphics produced via Microsoft Excel (Version 2023). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize respondent demographics, clinical practice patterns, and factors influencing surgical decision-making. A sub-analysis was conducted to compare respondents and nonrespondents based on clinic setting and clinic location. Chi-square tests of independence were used to evaluate statistically significant differences in the distribution of these variables between the two groups.

Results

Of the 407 REI providers invited to participate in this study, a total of 112 completed the survey for a response rate of 28%. Respondents were geographically diverse, with representation from the Northeast (22%), Midwest (20%), Southeast (26%), Southwest (10%), and West (22%). Clinic setting was similarly stratified with 80% of submissions from providers working in private practice followed by academic (14%) and hybrid (5%) settings. In terms of size, approximately 77% of clinics employed more than two REI physicians. Roughly half the clinics had an REI provider specializing in reproductive surgery on staff. Nearly all (97%) clinics performed more than 100 oocyte retrievals per year with the most frequently occurring volume greater than 700. Additional demographic information is presented in Table 1.

Characteristics of Clinic Respondents

Regarding the type of patients seen at REI clinics across the US, the majority (63%) of physicians estimated caring for 10–25% of patients diagnosed with uterine fibroids in an average month (Table 1). Clinics varied widely in terms of how many of these patients were diagnosed with the non-cavity distorting intramural subtype (Table 1). Additional imaging for fibroid characterization was ordered by 40% of providers in the form of saline infusion sonogram (60%) or MRI (37%). Most respondents (72%) would consider surgical management of non-cavity distorting intramural fibroids based on other factors (Figure 1A).

The most cited factors impacting patient counseling for or against surgical intervention included fibroid size, FIGO classification, intra/post-op complication concerns, patient symptoms, history of failed fertility treatments, risk of miscarriage or poor obstetric outcome, abnormal imaging characteristics, and rapid growth of fibroids (Figure 1B). Insurance coverage exerted little to no influence over the decision to recommend operative intervention to enhance fertility for most respondents (Figure 1C).

For providers who identified fibroid size as a factor, the majority recommended surgical management for non-cavity distorting intramural fibroids measuring 4.1–6 cm or larger (Figure 2A). Similarly, surgical management was commonly advised for fibroids classified as FIGO type 3 and above (Figure 2B). Most participants (74%) reported counseling individuals with FIGO type 3 fibroids differently compared to individuals with fibroids of another FIGO type. For respondents who reported a concern for intra- or post-operative complications, excessive blood loss, post-operative adhesive disease, uterine rupture, compromised ovarian function, and delay in starting fertility treatments were the most common driving factors. Of greatest concern was pre- or post-operative adhesive disease followed by delivery complications and delay in starting fertility treatments (Figure 3).

Regarding the surgical management of fibroids, most REI physicians in the study do not remove fibroids themselves (58%) but rather refer to fellowship trained MIGS (minimally invasive gynecologic surgeon) physicians (70%), general gynecologic surgeon (19%), or another REI provider in the same practice (12%) (Figure 4A and B). Discussion

In the US, 1 in 8 women ages 15 to 49 years utilize infertility services, many of whom struggle with female factor infertility of which uterine fibroids is a known contributor.32 Approximately 40–68.6% of women are diagnosed with uterine fibroids during this period, an underestimate of the true leiomyoma burden given the number of individuals with asymptomatic fibroids who are likely not captured by this metric.6,7,23 Fibroids, particularly non-cavity distorting intramural myomas measuring 4 cm and above, are associated with lower pregnancy rates and poor reproductive outcomes.4–6 REI providers who participated in this study reported 10–25% of their patients were diagnosed with uterine fibroids per month, with a notable prevalence of the non-cavity distorting subtype. Once fibroids were identified, approximately 40% of providers pursued additional imaging, with most favoring saline infusion sonography, though MRI was preferred in complex cases exceeding ultrasonography’s limitations. These findings align with prior research showing that 12.6% of women undergoing IVF have fibroids and emphasize the need for continued investigation into the diagnosis and management of this condition.21

Of note, approximately 72% of respondents in this study would consider surgical removal of non-cavitary distorting fibroids with the decision hinging on a few key factors, most important of which was fibroid size. Several metanalyses identified a deleterious effect of non-cavity distorting intramural fibroids ranging from 3 to 6 cm in diameter.4,20,33–38 Fibroids secrete TGF-β3, which impairs implantation by reducing HOXa-10, a key regulator of endometrial receptivity. Larger fibroids produce more TGF-β3, further suppressing HOXa-10, with variable effects based on fibroid proximity to the uterine cavity.4,23,39 Many other implantation factor abnormalities have been associated with fibroids, especially intramural non-cavitary distorting ones. Beyond these molecular disruptions, non-cavity distorting fibroids contribute to mechanical and physiological impairments that further hinder fertility. Uterine cavity distortion, impaired endometrial and myometrial blood supply, and increased uterine contractility amount to a suboptimal environment for implantation. Additionally, these fibroids can diminish sperm and embryo transport, contribute to sexual dysfunction, and limit ovarian access for ART.4

Some experts suggest that both fibroid size and proximity to the endometrial lining should be considered when evaluating impact on reproductive outcomes.4 In our study, FIGO classification was a common factor influencing recommendations for surgical intervention, with 74% of respondents counseling patients differently when FIGO type 3 fibroids were present. Proximity to the endometrial lining and the junctional zone is hypothesized to affect implantation and placentation, leading to a greater impact of type 3 fibroids on reproductive outcomes compared to types 4 and 5.4,34,40 Research demonstrates significantly reduced live birth rates, implantation rates, and clinical pregnancy rates in women with type 3 fibroids measuring ≤ 6 cm compared to controls without fibroids, with this effect extending to type 3 fibroids as small as 2 cm.23,35 Based on this evidence, a type 3 myoma of 2 cm or larger may warrant surgical removal. Despite these findings, most respondents considered 4 cm the threshold at which surgery should be considered (Figure 2A). However, more prospective and randomized trials are needed to establish definitive criteria to guide evidence-based practice.

Other major factors involved in the decision to pursue surgery or not is concern for intraoperative and postoperative complications, as noted by many respondents in our study (Figure 1B). Laparoscopic myomectomy carries nearly a 10% complication rate, influenced by factors such as fibroid number, size, position, and surgeon experience.4,23 Excessive blood loss was another issue frequently cited by respondents, which can lead to blood transfusion or conversion to laparotomy or hysterectomy (Figure 3).4 Despite surgical route, intrauterine and intra-abdominal adhesion formation occurs in 23–88% of cases which increases the risk for subsequent complications including infertility, bowel obstruction, and chronic pelvic pain.4 Given the potential for long-term sequelae and the relatively high rates of fibroid recurrence (approaching 60% within 4–5 years), surgical decisions must weigh risks against benefits and emphasize the importance of shared decision-making.4

Consideration of reproductive goals and fertility outcomes were also notable. Uterine healing following fibroid removal may delay pregnancy and influence counseling for or against surgery. Recovery time depends on the surgical approach, with hysteroscopic surgery typically requiring 6–8 weeks of uterine healing and laparoscopic/laparotomy procedures necessitating 3–6 months, though some recommend up to 12 months to minimize the risk of uterine rupture in future pregnancies.4 This delay can be particularly challenging for women facing age-related fertility decline, a group predisposed to greater myoma formation.4,7,21,23 In our study, 42% of respondents cited delays in starting fertility treatment as a key factor in their decision-making. While the risk of uterine rupture or dehiscence during delivery is relatively low, it remains an influential concern, noted by 30% of respondents. This risk is largely linked to myometrial tissue injury or poor tissue approximation during myoma removal and thus cesarean delivery if often recommended over vaginal birth.4,23 Patients must be educated about these potential risks, as well as other obstetric complications such as first-trimester bleeding, preeclampsia, intrauterine growth restriction, and preterm birth, for which there is no evidence of reduction following myomectomy.4

In light of these potential complications, it is important to consider the evidence and rationale for operative intervention from all angles. In the US, many women have insurance coverage for surgical procedures, like myomectomies, but not for infertility care, giving rise to a bias towards surgical intervention for management of fibroids.2 The state of insurance coverage for reproductive services remains in flux. As of September 2023, twenty-one states plus Washington D.C. have passed fertility insurance coverage laws, fifteen of which include IVF coverage and seventeen of which cover fertility preservation for iatrogenic infertility.41 It stands to reason that forward momentum in the arena of fertility aid could have an impact on myoma removal in the future. Interestingly, insurance coverage did not seem to play a major role in the decision-making process for respondents in our study, the majority of whom reported that insurance had little to no bearing on their recommendations (Figure 1C).

The role of REI physicians in surgical interventions to optimize fertility has evolved, with many transitioning out of the operating room to focus on ART practices and instead referring patients to fellowship-trained MIGS providers for surgical management before IVF. Our study found that 58% of REI specialists refer patients to other providers for non-cavity distorting intramural fibroid removal. Of the minority of REI physicians who perform their own surgeries, most favored abdominal myomectomy, though approaches varied. Minimally invasive techniques, which are associated with less blood loss, less postoperative pain, fewer adhesions, shorter hospital stays, and reduced overall morbidity are increasingly emphasized in fertility care, especially in fibroid cases amenable to a minimally invasive approach.4 To address the need for surgical expertise within fertility clinics and meet the growing demand for comprehensive fertility care, the Society of Reproductive Surgeons has established the Surgical Scholars Track within high-surgical-volume REI fellowships, focusing on training future REIs in these advanced, minimally invasive techniques.42

An important limitation of this study is the 28% survey completion rate despite multiple follow-up attempts to encourage participation. This raises the potential for response bias, as those who completed the survey may differ in meaningful ways from those who did not. However, efforts were made to enhance the generalizability of the findings by contacting a diverse range of ART operations, including clinics of varying types, sizes, locations, and volumes. Sub-analysis of survey data with regards to clinic setting revealed no significant difference between respondents (Academic= 14%, Private= 80%, Hybrid= 5%) and nonrespondents (Academic= 15%, Private= 82%, Hybrid= 3%; p=0.42; see Supplementary Table 1). Clinic location was similarly distributed between respondents (Northeast= 22%, Midwest= 20%, Southeast= 26%, Southwest= 10%, West= 22%) and nonrespondents (Northeast= 22%, Midwest= 17%, Southeast= 20%, Southwest= 14%, West= 27%; p=0.50). Additionally, the survey was emailed to all US ART clinics with available contact information along with follow up of initial nonrespondents to maximize representation. That said, the survey was primarily disseminated to clinic leads and, thus, the results of this study may not fully capture all physician perspectives at each site. Furthermore, survey questions that allowed free-text responses were analyzed thematically, introducing a qualitative element that carries an inherent potential for subjectivity which should be considered when interpreting the above results. Any conclusions based on these findings are ultimately a commentary on the day-to-day practices of acting reproductive endocrinologists and are not meant to serve as formalized recommendations at this time. Validation of the above data and additional information gathering is needed prior to the revision of existing evidence-based guidelines.

Going forward, there are many potential avenues of research with regards to non-cavity distorting intramural myomas, including better understanding the role of neoadjuvant medical therapies, the impact of fibroid biological heterogeneity on fertility, and developing a consensus on recommended recovery time after myomectomy.4 An important piece of this conversation that should not be overlooked is the patient perspective, with the recommendation for future data collection to incorporate this important viewpoint. As it stands, our study provides valuable insight into current practice patterns, forming a basis for revisions of societal guidelines aimed at addressing the fertility challenges posed by this fibroid subtype. With over 186 million people affected by infertility globally and the prevalence of uterine fibroids on the rise, standardized management strategies are critical to improving fertility outcomes for patients impacted by this condition.21,23,43

Conclusion

Non-cavity distorting intramural fibroids may impair the intrauterine environment, leading many reproductive specialists to consider surgical removal prior to infertility treatment. Results of this cross-sectional survey provide insight into current practice patterns and factors influencing the decision for or against operative management of this fibroid subtype.
