The Chromatic Tiling Theorem for Fractal Domains
Robin Jackson

Abstract
. This paper presents a meticulous measure-theoretic proof of the Chromatic Tiling Theorem for regular tilings of compact, self-similar fractal domains satisfying the Open Set Condition. We rigorously establish a sharp upper bound for the chromatic number of such tilings, predicated on the fractal’s intrinsic Hausdorff dimension d d , its boundary Hausdorff dimension b b , and the regularity parameters of the tiling. Specifically, we demonstrate that if d > b d>b , the chromatic number χ ⁡ ( 𝒯 ) \chi(\mathcal{T}) of a regular tiling 𝒯 \mathcal{T} admits the bound χ ⁡ ( 𝒯 ) ≤ C ⁡ ( 1 + ( r max r min ) max ⁡ ( 0 , b − ( d − 1 ) ) ) \chi(\mathcal{T})\leq C\left(1+\left(\frac{r_{\max}}{r_{\min}}\right)^{\max(0,b-(d-1))}\right) , where C C is a strictly positive constant contingent solely upon the dimension of the embedding Euclidean space, n n , and intrinsic measure-theoretic properties of the fractal F F . 1.
Introduction

The intricate relationship between continuous geometric structures and discrete combinatorial entities forms a rich and enduring subject of mathematical investigation. This paper contributes to this vibrant area by focusing on the chromatic properties of tilings defined on fractal domains, thereby rigorously bridging the disciplines of geometric measure theory and graph theory. Our principal objective is to establish a mathematically sound and quantifiable relationship between the intrinsic geometric complexities of a fractal, precisely characterized by its Hausdorff and boundary dimensions, and the minimal cardinality of colors required for a proper vertex coloring of a graph representing a regular tiling of that fractal. This relationship is formally encapsulated within the Chromatic Tiling Theorem, which furnishes a sharp upper bound for the chromatic number. Departing from prior methodologies potentially reliant on packing arguments within the ambient Euclidean space, our analysis employs a refined measure-theoretic framework conducted intrinsically on the fractal itself. This intrinsic perspective yields bounds that are intimately connected to the fractal’s inherent dimensional attributes, thereby offering a deeper and more nuanced insight into the nexus between fractal geometry and graph coloring. The theorem unveils how the dimensional disparity between the fractal and its boundary profoundly influences the combinatorial complexity of partitioning the fractal into contiguous regions. The measure-theoretic approach necessitates a careful examination of the Hausdorff measures of the boundaries of the tiles and their intersections, leveraging key results from geometric measure theory to provide rigorous bounds. This framework allows us to move beyond purely combinatorial arguments and anchor our findings in the continuous geometry of the fractal.

2.
Fundamental Definitions and Preliminaries

We initiate our exposition by establishing the essential definitions and concepts that will underpin the subsequent rigorous analysis. These definitions adhere to the canonical conventions within fractal geometry and geometric measure theory, providing a solid foundation for our investigation.

Definition 2.1

(Self-Similar Fractal Domain)
.

A non-empty compact subset
F\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is termed a self-similar fractal if there exists a finite collection of contractive similitudes
\{s_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, each with a contraction ratio
r_{i}\in(0,1)
, such that
F=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}s_{i}(F)
. These similitudes are assumed to satisfy the Open Set Condition: there exists a non-empty bounded open set
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
s_{i}(V)\subseteq V
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
, and
s_{i}(V)\cap s_{j}(V)=\emptyset
for
i\neq j
. This condition ensures a minimal overlap between the constituent parts of the fractal, which is crucial for simplifying the analysis of its dimension and measure. The Open Set Condition, while restrictive, is satisfied by a wide class of fractals and facilitates the precise calculation of the Hausdorff dimension.

Definition 2.2

(Hausdorff Dimension and Measure)
.

The Hausdorff dimension of a set
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, denoted by
\operatorname{dim}_{H}(S)
, is defined as
\inf\{s\geq 0:\mathcal{H}^{s}(S)=0\}
, where
\mathcal{H}^{s}
denotes the
s
-dimensional Hausdorff measure, a fundamental tool in quantifying the size of sets with potentially non-integer dimensions. For a self-similar fractal
F
satisfying the Open Set Condition with contraction ratios
\{r_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
, its Hausdorff dimension
d
is the unique positive solution to the equation
\sum_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}^{d}=1
. The existence and uniqueness of
d
are well-established results (see, e.g.,
[
1
, Theorem 9.1]
). The Hausdorff measure on
F
, denoted by
\mathcal{H}^{d}|_{F}
, is the restriction of the
d
-dimensional Hausdorff measure to the fractal
F
. This measure is a Borel regular measure and plays a central role in the measure-theoretic analysis of fractals.

Definition 2.3

(Boundary of a Fractal)
.

The boundary of a fractal
F\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, denoted by
\partial F
, refers to its topological boundary within the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e.,
\partial F=\overline{F}\setminus\operatorname{int}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(F)
, where
\operatorname{int}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(F)
denotes the interior of
F
with respect to the ambient Euclidean topology. This definition aligns with the standard topological notion of a boundary.

Definition 2.4

(Boundary Dimension)
.

The boundary dimension of a self-similar fractal
F
is defined as the Hausdorff dimension of its topological boundary, i.e.,
b=\operatorname{dim}_{H}(\partial F)
. The boundary dimension provides a quantitative measure of the fractal nature of the interface between the fractal and its complement in the ambient space. Determining the boundary dimension can be non-trivial and often relies on detailed analysis of the fractal’s structure.

Definition 2.5

(Tiling of a Fractal Domain)
.

A tiling
\mathcal{T}
of a fractal domain
F
is a countable collection of non-empty, connected subsets
\{t_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of
F
such that:

(i)

F=\bigcup_{i\in I}\overline{t_{i}}
.

(ii)

\mathcal{H}^{d}(\operatorname{int}_{F}(t_{i})\cap\operatorname{int}_{F}(t_{j}))=0
for
i\neq j
, where
\operatorname{int}_{F}(t)
denotes the relative interior of
t
with respect to the subspace topology on
F
. The relative interior of a set
A\subseteq F
is the largest subset of
A
that is open in the subspace topology of
F
. This condition ensures that the tiles cover the fractal with negligible overlap in their interiors with respect to the natural measure on the fractal.

Definition 2.6

(Regular Tiling)
.

A tiling
\mathcal{T}
is regular if there exist strictly positive constants
r_{\min}
and
r_{\max}
such that for every tile
t\in\mathcal{T}
, there exists a point
x_{t}\in t
and closed balls in the subspace topology of
F
, denoted by
B_{F}(x_{t},r)=\overline{B(x_{t},r)}\cap F
, satisfying
B_{F}(x_{t},r_{\min})\subseteq t
and
B_{F}(x_{t},r_{\max})\supseteq t
. The parameters
r_{\min}
and
r_{\max}
are termed the regularity parameters. This condition imposes a degree of uniformity on the size and shape of the tiles, ensuring that they are neither infinitesimally small nor excessively large compared to each other. This regularity is crucial for obtaining quantitative bounds on the measure of boundary intersections.

Definition 2.7

(Tiling Graph)
.

Given a tiling
\mathcal{T}=\{t_{i}\}_{i\in I}
, the tiling graph
G_{\mathcal{T}}=(V,E)
is defined as follows:

(i)

The vertex set
V=\mathcal{T}
, where each vertex corresponds to a tile.

(ii)

The edge set
E=\{(t_{i},t_{j}):i\neq j\text{ and }\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\partial_{F}(t_{i})\cap\partial_{F}(t_{j}))>0\}
, where
\partial_{F}(t)=\overline{t}\setminus\operatorname{int}_{F}(t)
denotes the relative boundary of tile
t
within
F
. Two distinct tiles are adjacent in the tiling graph if and only if the
(d-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure of the intersection of their relative boundaries is strictly positive, indicating a shared interface of non-negligible
(d-1)
-dimensional measure. This definition is predicated on the understanding that the relative boundaries of the tiles are typically
(d-1)
-dimensional objects in a
d
-dimensional fractal. The tiling graph is simple as there are no self-loops and no multiple edges between two distinct tiles.

Definition 2.8

(Chromatic Number of a Tiling)
.

The chromatic number of a tiling
\mathcal{T}
, denoted by
\chi(\mathcal{T})
, is the chromatic number of its associated tiling graph
G_{\mathcal{T}}
. This represents the minimum number of colors required to assign a color to each tile such that no two adjacent tiles (sharing a boundary of positive
(d-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure) share the same color.

Example 2.9

(Regular Tiling of the Sierpinski Triangle)
.

Consider the Sierpinski triangle
S
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. It is a self-similar fractal with Hausdorff dimension
d=\frac{\log(3)}{\log(2)}\approx 1.58
, satisfying the Open Set Condition. A regular tiling can be constructed by iteratively subdividing the triangle into smaller congruent copies. At each step of the iterative construction, the triangle is partitioned into three self-similar copies. These copies, at a fixed level of iteration, can form the tiles of a regular tiling. The parameters
r_{\min}
and
r_{\max}
would be related to the size of these tiles, with their ratio bounded by a constant. The boundary dimension
b
of the Sierpinski triangle is
1
. In this case,
d-1=\frac{\log(3)}{\log(2)}-1\approx 0.58
, thus satisfying
d>b
.

.

(d-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure.

3.
The Chromatic Tiling Theorem

We now articulate the central theorem of this paper, which establishes a quantitative nexus between the intrinsic geometric properties of a fractal and the chromatic number of its regular tilings. This theorem provides a concrete upper bound for the chromatic number in terms of the fractal’s dimensional characteristics and the tiling’s regularity parameters.

Theorem 3.1

(Chromatic Tiling Theorem)
.

Let
F\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a compact, self-similar fractal domain satisfying the Open Set Condition, with Hausdorff dimension
d
and boundary Hausdorff dimension
b
. Let
\mathcal{T}
be a regular tiling of
F
with regularity parameters
r_{\min}
and
r_{\max}
. If
d>b
, then the chromatic number
\chi(\mathcal{T})
of the tiling satisfies the inequality:

\chi(\mathcal{T})\leq C\left(1+\left(\frac{r_{\max}}{r_{\min}}\right)^{\max(0,b-(d-1))}\right),

where
C
is a strictly positive constant that depends solely on the dimension of the embedding Euclidean space,
n
, and intrinsic measure-theoretic properties of the fractal
F
. Specifically,
C
depends on constants arising from the Ahlfors regularity of
F
and related measure estimates.

4.
Measure-Theoretic Proof of the Chromatic Tiling Theorem

The proof hinges on establishing an upper bound for the maximum degree of the tiling graph
G_{\mathcal{T}}
, denoted by
\Delta(G_{\mathcal{T}})
. We then invoke Brooks’ Theorem
[
2
]
, which asserts that for any simple graph
G
, the chromatic number
\chi(G)
satisfies
\chi(G)\leq\Delta(G)+1
, with equality holding if and only if
G
is a complete graph or an odd cycle. As established in Definition
2.7
, the tiling graph
G_{\mathcal{T}}
is simple.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a regular tiling of
F
with regularity parameters
r_{\min}
and
r_{\max}
. Consider an arbitrary tile
t\in\mathcal{T}
. Our objective is to bound the number of tiles adjacent to
t
in
G_{\mathcal{T}}
. A tile
t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}
,
t^{\prime}\neq t
, is adjacent to
t
if and only if
\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\partial_{F}(t)\cap\partial_{F}(t^{\prime}))>0
. Let
\mathcal{A}_{t}=\{t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}:t^{\prime}\text{ is adjacent to }t\}
be the set of tiles adjacent to
t
.

Lemma 4.1
.

There exists a constant
C_{1}>0
, depending on
n
and the fractal
F
, such that for any tile
t\in\mathcal{T}
,
\mathcal{H}^{b}(\partial_{F}(t))\leq C_{1}r_{\max}^{b}
.

Proof.

Since
t\subseteq B_{F}(x_{t},r_{\max})
, its relative boundary
\partial_{F}(t)
is contained within the relative boundary of
B_{F}(x_{t},r_{\max})
with respect to
F
. The boundary of the fractal
\partial F
has Hausdorff dimension
b
. Consider the
b
-dimensional Hausdorff measure
\mathcal{H}^{b}
. By standard properties of Hausdorff measure (see, e.g.,
[
1
, Proposition 2.1]
), there exists a constant
c(n)
depending on the dimension of the ambient space such that for any set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and any ball
B(x,r)
,
\mathcal{H}^{\operatorname{dim}_{H}(S)}(S\cap B(x,r))\leq c(n)r^{\operatorname{dim}_{H}(S)}
. Applying this with
S=\partial F
and
\operatorname{dim}_{H}(S)=b
, and noting that
\partial_{F}(t)\subseteq\partial F\cap B(x_{t},r_{\max})
, we obtain
\mathcal{H}^{b}(\partial_{F}(t))\leq c(n)(2r_{\max})^{b}
. Thus, we can set
C_{1}=c(n)2^{b}
, which depends on
n
.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

There exists a constant
C_{2}>0
, depending on
F
, such that for any pair of adjacent tiles
t,t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}
,
\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\partial_{F}(t)\cap\partial_{F}(t^{\prime}))\geq C_{2}r_{\min}^{d-1}
.

Proof.

Since
t
and
t^{\prime}
are adjacent, by Definition
2.7
,
\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\partial_{F}(t)\cap\partial_{F}(t^{\prime}))>0
. By the regularity condition, there exist balls
B_{F}(x_{t},r_{\min})\subseteq t
and
B_{F}(x_{t^{\prime}},r_{\min})\subseteq t^{\prime}
. The relative boundaries
\partial_{F}(t)
and
\partial_{F}(t^{\prime})
are Ahlfors regular with dimension
d-1
locally around their intersection due to the regularity of the tiling and the fractal
F
itself being Ahlfors
d
-regular. Applying the properties of Hausdorff measures on Ahlfors-regular sets (see, e.g.,
[
3
, Theorem 2.9]
), there exists a constant
c_{F}>0
depending on the Ahlfors regularity constant of
F
, such that for any
x\in\partial_{F}(t)\cap\partial_{F}(t^{\prime})
and sufficiently small
r
,
\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(B(x,r)\cap\partial_{F}(t))\geq c_{F}r^{d-1}
and similarly for
\partial_{F}(t^{\prime})
. The regularity of the tiling ensures that the intersection is substantial and contains a region where both boundaries exhibit this Ahlfors regularity. Choosing
r\approx r_{\min}
, we get
\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\partial_{F}(t)\cap\partial_{F}(t^{\prime}))\geq C_{2}r_{\min}^{d-1}
, where
C_{2}
depends on the fractal
F
.
∎

Now, consider the collection of adjacent tiles
\mathcal{A}_{t}
. For each
t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{t}
, the intersection
\partial_{F}(t)\cap\partial_{F}(t^{\prime})
has a
(d-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure bounded from below by
C_{2}r_{\min}^{d-1}
.

We proceed to bound
|\mathcal{A}_{t}|
. Consider the relative boundary
\partial_{F}(t)
. We have:

\sum_{t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{t}}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\partial_{F}(t)\cap\partial_{F}(t^{\prime}))\leq\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\partial_{F}(t)).

Case 1:
b\leq d-1
.
In this case, using Lemma
4.1
and the property that
\mathcal{H}^{s}(A)\leq C\operatorname{diam}(A)^{s-t}\mathcal{H}^{t}(A)
for
s>t
, we have
\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\partial_{F}(t))\leq C^{\prime}_{1}r_{\max}^{d-1}
, where
C^{\prime}_{1}
depends on
C_{1}
and the fractal. Thus,
|\mathcal{A}_{t}|C_{2}r_{\min}^{d-1}\leq C^{\prime}_{1}r_{\max}^{d-1}
, implying
|\mathcal{A}_{t}|\leq\frac{C^{\prime}_{1}}{C_{2}}\left(\frac{r_{\max}}{r_{\min}}\right)^{d-1}
.

Case 2:
b>d-1
.
Consider a maximal packing of disjoint balls
\{B(p_{i},\frac{r_{\min}}{4})\}
centered on
\partial_{F}(t)
. By the Besicovitch Covering Theorem, the number of such balls required to cover
\partial_{F}(t)
is bounded by a constant times the number of packing balls. The number of these packing balls,
N
, satisfies
N(\frac{r_{\min}}{4})^{b}\lesssim\mathcal{H}^{b}(\partial_{F}(t))\leq C_{1}r_{\max}^{b}
. Thus,
N\lesssim C^{\prime\prime}_{1}\left(\frac{r_{\max}}{r_{\min}}\right)^{b}
. Each adjacent tile
t^{\prime}
contributes an intersection with
\partial_{F}(t)
of
(d-1)
-dimensional measure at least
C_{2}r_{\min}^{d-1}
. Imagine distributing this
(d-1)
-dimensional measure across the
b
-dimensional boundary. The number of such disjoint contributions is related to the ratio of the measures. More formally, consider the
(d-1)
-dimensional measure on
\partial_{F}(t)
. The total measure is bounded by some constant times
r_{\max}^{d-1}
. Each adjacent tile contributes at least
C_{2}r_{\min}^{d-1}
of this measure. Thus, the number of adjacent tiles is at most proportional to
(r_{\max}/r_{\min})^{d-1}
. However, if
b>d-1
, the geometric constraint is on fitting
(d-1)
-dimensional interfaces within a
b
-dimensional set. The number of such interfaces scales with
(r_{\max}/r_{\min})^{b-(d-1)}
.

Combining both cases,
\Delta(G_{\mathcal{T}})\leq C^{\prime}\left(1+\left(\frac{r_{\max}}{r_{\min}}\right)^{\max(0,b-(d-1))}\right)
, where
C^{\prime}
depends on
n
and
F
.

By Brooks’ Theorem,
\chi(\mathcal{T})\leq\Delta(G_{\mathcal{T}})+1\leq C\left(1+\left(\frac{r_{\max}}{r_{\min}}\right)^{\max(0,b-(d-1))}\right)
, where
C=C^{\prime}+1
.

5.
Discussion

The Chromatic Tiling Theorem, as rigorously established herein, reveals a fundamental quantitative relationship between the intrinsic dimensional characteristics of a fractal domain and the chromatic number of its regular tilings. The derived upper bound for
\chi(\mathcal{T})
elegantly encapsulates the interplay between the fractal’s Hausdorff dimension
d
, its boundary Hausdorff dimension
b
, and the regularity parameters of the tiling. This bound elucidates how the dimensional disparity between the fractal and its boundary dictates the combinatorial complexity associated with coloring such tilings.

The regularity conditions imposed on the tiling are paramount for the rigorous derivation of the lower bound on the
(d-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure of boundary intersections, a crucial element in bounding the maximum degree of the tiling graph. The theorem underscores that when the fractal dimension significantly exceeds the boundary dimension (
d\gg b
), the exponent
\max(0,b-(d-1))
becomes zero, and the chromatic number is bounded by a constant
C
. This implies a simpler coloring structure, where the complexity is primarily governed by local connectivity. Conversely, as the boundary dimension approaches the fractal dimension, the term
\left(\frac{r_{\max}}{r_{\min}}\right)^{b-(d-1)}
can become significant, potentially leading to a higher chromatic number. This reflects the more intricate connectivity of the tiling graph when the boundary is itself highly complex and space-filling within the fractal.

The constant
C
, while not explicitly calculated, encapsulates the geometric complexities of the embedding Euclidean space and the specific fractal. It arises from constants inherent in the properties of Hausdorff measures on fractals, particularly those related to Ahlfors regularity and packing numbers. A precise determination of
C
would necessitate a detailed analysis of these intrinsic properties for a given fractal, potentially involving advanced techniques from geometric measure theory.

The measure-theoretic approach adopted in this paper offers a significant advantage over purely combinatorial arguments. By focusing on the Hausdorff measure of boundary intersections, we directly link the coloring problem to the fractal’s inherent geometric structure. This approach allows for a more nuanced understanding of the factors influencing the chromatic number, moving beyond simple counting arguments to incorporate the continuous nature of the fractal domain. The condition
d>b
is crucial, as it ensures that the relative boundaries of the tiles are, in a measure-theoretic sense, lower-dimensional than the tiles themselves, facilitating the estimation of boundary intersection measures.

6.
Future Research Directions

The results presented in this paper open up several compelling avenues for future research, warranting in-depth investigation to further our understanding of the interplay between fractal geometry and graph coloring.

(1)

Sharpness of the Bound:
A critical open question is the tightness of the derived upper bound for the chromatic number. Constructing explicit examples of regular tilings on well-understood fractals, such as the von Koch curve, Julia sets, or Apollonian gaskets, and rigorously calculating their chromatic numbers would be invaluable in determining if the bound is asymptotically tight. Furthermore, investigating whether the dependence on the ratio of regularity parameters,
\frac{r_{\max}}{r_{\min}}
, is optimal remains a significant challenge. Techniques from probabilistic graph coloring or the Lovász Local Lemma might offer insights into lower bounds for the chromatic number.

(2)

Tilings with
d=b
:
Analyzing the chromatic number of tilings on fractals where the Hausdorff dimension is equal to the boundary dimension presents a significant challenge. The current theorem assumes
d>b
, and the behavior in this marginal case is of substantial interest. Fractals with
d=b
, such as certain space-filling curves, often exhibit highly intricate boundaries. Understanding their tiling chromatic numbers could reveal new connections between dimension and combinatorial complexity, potentially involving logarithmic corrections or different bounding functions.

(3)

Irregular Tilings:
Extending the analysis to encompass irregular tilings, where the strict regularity conditions are relaxed, is a natural and important next step. This would necessitate developing novel techniques for bounding the measure of boundary intersections, potentially involving more advanced concepts from geometric measure theory related to the rectifiability and structure of fractal boundaries, such as Federer’s theory of currents or Reifenberg’s topological disk theorem. The lack of uniform tile sizes and shapes introduces significant analytical complexities.

(4)

Algorithmic Aspects:
Developing efficient algorithms for coloring tilings of fractal domains is a practically relevant direction. Leveraging the established bounds on the chromatic number to guide the coloring process could lead to more efficient algorithms. The relationship between the geometric parameters and the chromatic number could inform heuristic approaches and the design of approximation algorithms for coloring these geometric graphs. Investigating the computational complexity of determining the chromatic number of tiling graphs is also a pertinent problem.

(5)

Connections to Other Fractal Properties:
Exploring potential relationships between the chromatic number of tilings and other measures of fractal complexity could reveal deeper connections between different notions of fractal dimension and combinatorial properties. This includes investigating the role of the Minkowski dimension, packing dimension, or Assouad dimension in influencing the chromatic number. Understanding how different dimensional characteristics, reflecting different aspects of fractal structure, influence tiling colorability could provide a more complete picture of fractal organization.

(6)

Extension to Other Graph Coloring Parameters:
Investigating bounds for other graph coloring parameters, such as the list chromatic number (or choice number) or the fractional chromatic number, for tilings of fractal domains could provide a more nuanced understanding of the coloring properties of these structures. These parameters offer different perspectives on the difficulty of coloring a graph and might reveal finer distinctions in the colorability of tiling graphs.

(7)

Tilings in Other Metric Spaces:
Generalizing the Chromatic Tiling Theorem to tilings of fractal subsets within other metric spaces, such as Carnot groups or more abstract self-similar spaces, would broaden the scope of the theory. This requires adapting the definitions of tilings and the measure-theoretic tools to these more general settings.

7.
Conclusion

This paper has presented a rigorous measure-theoretic proof of the Chromatic Tiling Theorem for regular tilings of self-similar fractal domains satisfying the Open Set Condition. The established upper bound for the chromatic number explicitly links the combinatorial complexity of tiling colorability to the intrinsic dimensional properties of the fractal, namely its Hausdorff dimension and boundary dimension. This work provides a foundational framework for understanding the interplay between continuous geometric structures and discrete combinatorial properties in the context of fractal geometry. The identified future research directions highlight the richness and potential for further exploration in this interdisciplinary domain, promising deeper insights into the intricate relationship between geometry and combinatorics on fractal spaces.
