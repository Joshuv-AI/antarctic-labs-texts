A method of compensating for the excitability of spinal motoneurones when estimating the magnitude of potentials evoked in skeletal muscles
Authors: Richard G. Carson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn recognition of the mediating influence of spinal motoneurone excitability on the amplitude of motor evoked potentials (MEPs) generated by transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), it is customary to reject trials/study participants if the level of electromyographic (EMG) activity recorded before the delivery of TMS exceeds some threshold. The thresholds employed in practice vary considerably (2.5–100 µV) and appear arbitrary. It is now known that a change in root mean squared (r.m.s.) EMG from 1 to 5 µV is associated with a 20% increase in the probability that the MEP amplitude will exceed the 50 µV criterion for resting motor threshold. Furthermore, trial‐to‐trial fluctuations in r.m.s. EMG over a range not extending beyond 2 µV are associated with systematic variations in the amplitude of MEPs. These outcomes suggest that most EMG rejection thresholds fail in their principal purpose – which is to obviate the influence of spinal motoneurone excitability on the amplitude of evoked potentials. A new method (implemented using both quantile and least squares linear regression techniques) is presented, that can be used to compensate for this influence. Using a very large sample it is demonstrated that when the compensation is adequate, it can alter the ostensible impact of an experimental factor on the magnitude of MEPs. An additional benefit of the method is that it permits the use of most if not all datapoints, and thus preserves the full power of the experimental design. Key points Customarily, experimental trials are rejected if electromyographic (EMG) activity recorded before transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) exceeds some threshold.In practice, adopted thresholds vary considerably (2.5–100 µV) and appear arbitrary.Most thresholds fail to obviate the influence of spinal motoneurone excitability on the amplitude of motor evoked potentials (MEPs).A new method based on linear regression is described that compensates for the relationship between (‘background’) EMG and MEP amplitude.The compensation is shown to alter the ostensible impact of experimental factors on the magnitude of MEPs.

Introduction

In the period since transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) was first introduced (Barker et al., 1985), the technique has predominantly been used to make inferences concerning the state of neural circuits within (motor) cortex. The scientists who first pioneered the application of TMS and compared it to other forms of cortical stimulation (e.g. Thompson et al., 1991) were, however, clear in their recognition that the magnitude of the motor evoked potential (MEP) is also contingent on the (momentary) state of the spinal motoneurone pool (see also Kiers et al., 1993). The instrumental significance of this state is highlighted by similarities in the extent to which responses to cervicomedullary (Maertens de Noordhout et al., 1992) transcranial electrical (Hess et al., 1987) and cortical magnetic stimulation are potentiated by weak (≤ 5% maximum) voluntary contractions. Studies in which the excitability of spinal motoneurones (registered via surface electromyographic (EMG) recordings) innervating an otherwise ‘relaxed’ muscle has been manipulated (e.g. Mathis et al., 1996), suggest that there is a linear association with MEP amplitude, even for levels of (in that case, enveloped) EMG activity lower than 20 µV.

In recognition of the mediating influence of spinal motoneurone excitability on MEP amplitude, it has become customary to reject trials if the level of EMG activity recorded in a period (typically 50 or 100 ms) preceding the delivery of TMS exceeds a pre‐defined threshold. The thresholds employed in practice (generally defined in terms of root mean squared (r.m.s.) signal amplitude) vary considerably. While values of 10 µV (e.g. Mooney et al., 2025) and 20 µV (e.g. Vallence et al., 2023) are used frequently, much higher thresholds of 50 µV (e.g. Castiglione & Aron, 2021; Duque & Ivry, 2009) or 100 µV (e.g. Duque et al., 2010, 2012) are not unknown. The assumption implicit in the designation of these thresholds is that fluctuations of the signals recorded in retained trials reflect digitisation or instrumentation noise (e.g. Li et al., 2022), rather than changes in the state of the spinal motoneurone pool.

The wide range of thresholds adopted by different laboratories suggests that the values are somewhat arbitrary. The lowest rejection threshold of which I am aware is that of 2.5 µV used by Calvert and Carson (2023). At first glance, this value may appear sufficiently stringent. Recent results indicate otherwise. O'Sullivan et al. (2025) reported that a change in (‘background’) r.m.s. EMG from 1 µV to 5 µV is associated with a 20% increase in the probability that the corresponding MEP amplitude will exceed the standard 50 µV criterion commonly used to identify a resting motor threshold (RMT). Carson (2026) demonstrated that trial‐to‐trial fluctuations in r.m.s. EMG over a range not extending beyond 2µV are associated with systematic variations in the amplitude of MEPs. These outcomes suggest that most background EMG rejection thresholds fail in their principal purpose – which is to obviate the influence of spinal motoneurone excitability on the amplitude of evoked potentials. A further consideration is that removal of trials/participants characterised by levels of EMG exceeding a designated threshold will reduce the number of datapoints available for analysis and thus the statistical power of the experimental design.

The purpose of the present paper is to demonstrate a method that can be used to compensate for the association that exists between the level of EMG activity present immediately prior to the delivery of TMS and the magnitude of the resulting MEP. Using a very large sample it is demonstrated that when the compensation is adequate, it has the capacity to alter the ostensible impact of an experimental factor on the magnitude of evoked potentials. An additional benefit of the method described is that it permits the use of most if not all datapoints, and thus preserves the full power of the experimental design.

Methods

Participants

One hundred and eighty‐two neurologically healthy volunteers (aged 18–30 years, mean age = 22.0 years, standard deviation = 2.6 years; 102 females) were engaged. None reported previous neurological or psychiatric disease, nor the use of medications deemed to be a contraindication for TMS (Rossi et al., 2021). Based on responses to the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory (Oldfield, 1971), all were designated right‐handed. The procedures, to which the participants provided written informed consent, were approved by the Trinity College Dublin School of Psychology Research Ethics Committee (SPREC102011‐01). Other than in respect of pre‐registration, the study was undertaken in accordance with the most recent Declaration of Helsinki. The experimental protocols described in the current report were implemented in the context of a motor learning study. The primary testing session was of approximately 3 h duration. Participants returned 1 week later for a ‘retention’ session, which lasted approximately 30 min. Each participant was given a 20‐euro shopping voucher as a token of appreciation for their commitment of time.

Recording procedures

The participants sat in a chair with both upper limbs supported. Their forearms were placed in mid‐pronation and stabilised by vacuum cushions (Vac‐Pac Size 11, Olympic Medical, Seattle, WA, USA). Their elbows were semi‐flexed (100–120°). Their hands were secured at mid palm in custom built manipulanda. As these did not permit extension of the wrist, and flexion of the wrist was opposed by a stiffness load (∼0.67 N m/θ (rad)), the hands of the participants were effectively immobile. An orthopaedic neck brace (RC0007M, Cervical Collar, Aspen Medical Products, Irvine, CA, USA) was worn to curtail the range of head motion. The electromyographic (EMG) activity of left and right flexor carpi radialis (FCR) and extensor carpi radialis (ECR) was recorded using pairs of Ag–AgCl electrodes (Cleartrace 1700, ConMed, Haverhill, MA, USA). A reference electrode was located on the lateral epicondyle of the right humerus. For the present purpose, only data recorded from the right FCR muscle are reported. Prior to affixing the electrodes, a topical abrasive (Nuprep, Weaver & Co., Aurora, CO, USA) was applied to the skin which was then sanitised with an antiseptic (70% isopropanol). The EMG signals were amplified (gain = 1000) by BioPac EMG100C amplifiers (Biopac Systems UK, Pershore, UK), band‐pass (100–1000 Hz) filtered (NL125/6, Digitimer Ltd, Welwyn Garden City, UK), digitised (16 bit precision; ±5 V input range) at a sampling rate of 5 kHz (Micro1401, CED, Cambridge, UK), and recorded with Signal software (Signal 7.01, CED). The resolution with which the signals were digitised was approximately 0.15 µV. The lower limit of the band pass filter was adopted to eliminate movement artefacts associated with ballistic movements (e.g. Ruddy et al., 2016) performed by the participants later in the same session. It has been shown that when using comparable filter settings, electrical activity of muscles can be resolved via surface EMG with fidelity sufficient to allow multi motor unit action potentials to be characterised (Sebik et al., 2013). It is therefore considered unlikely that the band pass filter used in the present study adversely affected resolution of the features to be extracted from the EMG signals (Karacan et al., 2023).

Stimulation procedures

For every participant included in this report at least one ‘paired‐pulse’ TMS protocol was used. These protocols comprised trials in which a conditioning stimulus (CS) was delivered in advance of a test stimulus (TS) and other trials during which only a TS was delivered. The analyses presented herein are based exclusively on trials that comprised only a TS. These data have not been published previously.

The ‘test’ magnetic stimulus was delivered by a Magstim 200 (monophasic waveform) stimulator (Magstim Company, Whitland, Dyfed, UK) using a figure of eight coil (internal wing diameter 70 mm). This was placed over the left hemisphere with the axis of intersection between the two loops oriented at approximately 45 degrees to the sagittal plane, to induce posterior to anterior (P–A) current flow across the motor strip (Kammer et al. 2001).

To determine the optimal site for stimulation, the output of the stimulator was increased incrementally from 30% of maximum stimulator output (% MSO) with the centre of the coil first positioned over a craniometrically defined location 2 cm anterior and 6 cm lateral to the vertex, until a MEP was visible on an oscilloscope displaying the EMG signal at high gain. Subsequently, the coil was moved in small (∼0.5 cm) increments from this position, along the anterior‐to‐posterior and mediolateral axes, and the stimulation intensity increased or reduced. The search culminated at the coil location which elicited a MEP at the lowest stimulation intensity. This location was registered by marking directly on the scalp the outline of the coil and a vector indicating the long axis of its handle. These markings were used subsequently to reproduce the position and orientation of the coil. The coil was then removed, replaced at the marked location, and stimulation applied again to verify that MEPs could still be evoked at the same intensity. If MEPs were not reliably elicited, the procedure for determining the optimal site was repeated.

During the test procedures, the stimulating coil and cable was supported either by an overhead suspension system or by articulated mechanical arms. The experimenter standing (on a pedestal) above and behind the participant held the coil over the left hemisphere. The experimenter monitored the position and orientation of the coil continuously and, if necessary, made minor adjustments to ensure that it remained in alignment with the scalp markings.

Resting motor thresholds (rMT) are typically defined as the minimum stimulation intensity required to elicit a MEP with a peak‐to‐peak amplitude of 50 µV in the target muscle (Rossi et al., 2009). For most of the participants in the present study (n = 157), these were obtained using a maximum likelihood protocol – referred to as the parameter estimation by sequential testing (PEST) method, described by Awiszus (2003). This protocol utilises a sigmoid‐shaped logistic function to determine the stimulation intensity at which there exists a 0.5 probability of eliciting a MEP with the defined peak‐to‐peak amplitude (i.e. 50 µV). The method was implemented using the MTAT 2.0 programme (Awiszus & Borckardt, 2011). For 25 participants, the rMT was obtained as the lowest stimulation intensity at which MEPs with peak‐to‐peak amplitude of approximately 50 µV were evoked in at least 5 of 10 consecutive trials (i.e. following Rossi et al., 2009).

Test protocols

All participants completed (median = 20, mean = 19, minimum = 8; maximum = 20) trials during which the TS was delivered over the left hemisphere at an intensity of 120% rMT. The recording of each trial commenced 0.5 s before the TS and concluded 0.5 s following delivery of the TS.

In each testing block there were also ‘paired‐pulse’ trials. For some participants, on these trials, the CS was applied to the opposite (right) hemisphere. In such cases, the procedure was designed to elicit long interval ‘inter‐hemispheric inhibition’ (lIHI) or ‘inter‐hemispheric facilitation’ (IHF) (Table 1). In all such cases, trials on which only a CS was delivered (n ≥ 10 trials) were also included. For other participants, the CS was delivered to the left hemisphere. In such cases, the procedure was designed to elicit ‘long‐interval intracortical inhibition’ (LICI), ‘short‐interval intracortical inhibition’ (SICI), ‘short short’‐interval intracortical inhibition’ (sSICI), ‘intracortical facilitation’ (ICF), or ‘short‐interval intracortical facilitation’ (sICF) (Table 1). In every case, the time that elapsed between successive trials was approximately 8 s – ‘jittered’ by 50%, such that the range was between 4 and 12 s. The total duration of each block of (maximum = 68) trials was always less than 10 min.

Data reduction

To quantify the short‐latency component of the response to the TS (e.g. Rothwell et al., 1991), the amplitude of each MEP elicited in rFCR was obtained as the absolute difference between the maximum and minimum values of the EMG time series in an interval appropriate to the duration of potentials evoked in FCR (Grospretre & Martin, 2014), commencing 12.5 ms and concluding 45 ms following delivery of the magnetic stimulus. The root mean square (r.m.s.) of the background EMG recorded in the rFCR was calculated for a period (100 ms duration) that commenced 103 ms and ended 3 ms prior to the TS. The selection of a 100 ms interval was based on the outcome of a recent study (Carson, 2026). In this it was shown that (in an IHI paradigm), estimates of r.m.s. EMG had greater adequacy when used as covariates (in analyses of MEP amplitudes), when derived from a pre‐stimulus interval of 100 ms duration, than when intervals of 10 ms or 50 ms duration were used instead. These data processing steps were undertaken using custom MATLAB (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) scripts.

All subsequent statistical analyses were implemented in R (R Core Team, 2023).

Results and analyses

The phenomenon to be addressed

It is well established that distributions of MEP amplitude values tend to exhibit substantial deviations from normality (Nielsen, 1996). Unfortunately, due to the small number of observations typically obtained (i.e. for each stimulus configuration) in many studies that employ TMS, this feature is frequently overlooked (Goetz et al., 2014). Samples composed of r.m.s. EMG records derived from separate trials exhibit similar characteristics. Deviations from normality may be particularly pronounced in circumstances (e.g. when ‘at rest’) in which a relatively small number of motor units (i.e. drawing upon a limited portion of the motoneuron pool) are represented in the EMG recording (Rupasov, Lebedev, Erlichman et al., 2012a). Theoretical analysis suggest that skewed distributions are to be expected in such cases. This is because the sampled values are small (relative to the potential range), variances are large (reflecting the causal influence of multiple independent random processes with arbitrary statistical properties) and the values cannot be negative (Limbert et al., 2001). Given these considerations, the use of non‐parametric measures of association, such as Spearman's rho would usually be indicated. As a first step in corroborating the presence of a positive association (Carson, 2026) between the amplitudes of potentials evoked by cortical magnetic stimulation, and the level of EMG activity recorded during the 100 ms duration period prior to the TS, the Spearman correlation method was therefore applied. The median number of paired observations (i.e. of MEP amplitude and r.m.s. EMG) available in calculating a correlation coefficient (rho) for each participant was 20. Subsequently, the mean value of rho across the 182 participants was calculated. Associated bias corrected adjusted confidence intervals (CI) based on 10,000 bootstrap samples were also generated. The magnitude of the association was rho = 0.09 (95% CI = 0.05–0.13). Although this mean correlation coefficient was somewhat lower than the corresponding value of 0.15 reported by Carson (2026), one can nonetheless have a high degree of confidence that the effect (i.e. the positive association of MEP amplitude and r.m.s. EMG) is present in the population.

A key point that bears particular emphasis is that the association is present for levels of ‘background’ EMG that are well below the screening thresholds that are applied conventionally to exclude trials on which ‘muscle activity’ is deemed to be present. The mean (across 182 participants) of the median r.m.s. values obtained for each participant across all trials (typically n = 20) was 0.75 µV (95% CI = 0.70–0.84). The mean (across 182 participants) of the maximum r.m.s. values obtained for each participant across all trials was 1.43 µV (95% CI = 1.26–1.66).

Compensation for the association

Evidently, covariation of MEPs amplitudes and EMG recorded (‘at rest’) prior to stimulation can be detected when the range over which the (r.m.s.) EMG signal amplitudes extend is less than 2 µV. It is clearly desirable to remove from estimates of MEP amplitude the influence of variations in the post‐synaptic state of the spinal motoneurons that the r.m.s. EMG reflects. In principle, this may be achieved through covariate adjustment. Generally, this comprises two steps: (1) regress the variable of primary interest (here MEP amplitude) on the relevant covariate (i.e. r.m.s. EMG) using a linear regression model, and (2) employ the residuals generated by the linear regression model in place of the original variable. Until relatively recently, the challenge faced by an investigator seeking to use non‐parametric (e.g. rank‐based) methods to implement these steps was to determine an appropriate means of estimating the residuals. As described by Carson (2026), this determination can now be achieved using various methods, including those (e.g. Liu et al., 2018) which generate ‘probability‐scale residuals’. Carson (2026) also showed that – in seeking to compensate for an association between r.m.s. EMG and MEP amplitude, largely equivalent outcomes (i.e. in terms of the properties of the residuals that are generated) can be achieved using parametric methods if the variables included in a (linear) regression model are first transformed such that the sample distributions exhibit minimal deviations from normality. The close correspondence of outcomes obtained using the parametric and non‐parametric methods also suggested that (least squares based) linear models can provide a robust approximation of the relationship between MEP amplitude and r.m.s. EMG – if the sample distributions of these variables are first ‘Gaussianised’.

The approach taken here (i.e. in applying parametric methods) is to employ the so‐called ‘Lambert Way’ (W) transformation – which is a means of modelling skewed, heavy‐tailed data, to Gaussianise a distribution (Goerg, 2011, 2015). In implementation via the R package LambertW (Goerg, 2015), an instance of ‘non‐normality’ may be modelled as either symmetric heavy‐tailed (‘h’), skewed heavy‐tailed (‘hh’), or skewed (‘s’). As it is not possible to characterise a priori the deviations from normality exhibited by individual MEP amplitude and r.m.s. EMG sample distributions, an empirical approach was therefore adopted.

Transformations were applied for each participant to the respective samples of MEP amplitude and r.m.s. EMG values, in each case modelling the distributions (separately) as ‘h’, ‘hh’ and ‘s’. Following each transformation, the degree of deviation from normality (i.e. of the Gaussianised data) was expressed quantitatively using the extension of the Shapiro–Wilk (SW) normality test (Shapiro & Wilk, 1965) described by Hernandez (2021). In accordance with this method, the estimated normality value (N‐value) is positive only when the distribution is likely normal. For each participant and each variable (i.e. MEP amplitude and r.m.s. EMG) the variant of the Gaussianised data retained for further analysis was that which yielded the largest N‐value – corresponding to the smallest deviation from normality. With respect to MEP amplitude, the frequency with which each model provided the basis for the optimal transformation was: 4 for ‘h’, 18 for ‘hh’, and 160 for ‘s’. The corresponding values for the r.m.s. EMG were: 8 for ‘h’, 70 for ‘hh’, and 104 for ‘s’. Before being Gaussianised the mean (n = 182 participants) value of N for the samples of r.m.s. EMG was −3.97 (95% CI = −4.82 to −3.19). For the samples of MEP amplitudes, the mean value of N was −1.34 (95% CI = −1.87 to −0.91). After being Gaussianised the mean value of N for the samples of r.m.s. EMG was 1.03 (95% CI = 0.72–1.25). For the samples of MEP amplitudes, the corresponding mean value of N was 1.59 (95% CI = 1.48–1.67). It is evident therefore that the transformations were largely effective in increasing the normality of the sample distributions.

A standard (least squares based) linear regression (LSR) was then applied (separately for each participant) to regress the Gaussianised MEP amplitude values obtained using the optimal model (‘h’ or ‘hh’ or ‘s’) on the corresponding Gaussianised r.m.s. EMG values. The resulting predicted values represented the optimal fit to the relationship between r.m.s. EMG and MEP amplitude, at the values of r.m.s. EMG present in the data available for each participant. The residuals correspond to variations in MEP amplitude that are not predicted by r.m.s. EMG. The mean value of N for all samples of residuals (n = 182 participants) was 1.46 (95% CI = 1.33–1.56). As would be anticipated, using the Spearman method, the correlation coefficients (n = 182 participants) representing the (non‐parametric) association between the residuals and the r.m.s. EMG calculated for the 100 ms preceding the TS could not be confidently differentiated from zero (mean rho = 0.01; 95% CI = 0.0–0.03). When calculated using the Pearson method (for which there is no contraindication, given normality of the residuals and transformed r.m.s. EMG sample distributions), the mean value of r was −5.8 × 10−17 (95% CI = −1.5 × 10−16–2.7 × 10−17).

Generation of adjusted MEP amplitudes

A positive value of a residual indicates that an estimate of MEP amplitude is larger than that which would be predicted by r.m.s. EMG alone – to an extent corresponding to its magnitude. A negative residual value indicates an estimate of MEP amplitude that is smaller than would be predicted for the r.m.s. EMG recorded in the 100 ms preceding the stimulation. The use of the unadjusted residuals as the basis for further analysis is, however, limited by the fact that for a given sample the sum of the residuals is necessarily equal to zero. It is therefore desirable that the residuals are expressed relative to an appropriate reference value (Carson & Leemans, 2024). The parameters of the linear regression model used to generate each set of residuals affords a means of addressing this requirement.

In an idealised scenario (Fig. 1) in which a linear relationship between r.m.s. EMG and MEP amplitude accounts for 100% of the variance present in the data, the appropriate reference is obtained as the value of the intercept (i.e. with the ordinate (y) axis). This corresponds to the predicted value of the MEP amplitude when the r.m.s. EMG is equal to zero. In this idealised case, the adjusted MEP amplitudes for the sample are simply obtained by adding the value of the intercept to each of the residuals. If there is no relationship between r.m.s. EMG and MEP amplitude, the best estimate for all values of r.m.s. EMG is the mean of the original MEP amplitude values. That is, if the slope of the regression line is equal to zero, the reference value is provided by the mean of the actual values (which necessarily equates to the mean of the predicted values and, in this specific case only, to the value of the intercept). Thus, the extent to which the appropriate reference value differs from the mean of the actual values is contingent on the slope of the regression line.

With respect to real data, however, a linear model will not typically account for all variation inherent to the sample (Fig. 2). The use of a linear regression model dictates that there is no uncertainty about the estimated slope at the mean of the ‘predictor’ variable (the r.m.s. EMG in the present instance). Even though prediction will also be most precise at the mean, there will nonetheless be some uncertainty concerning the true vertical position of the regression line at this point. The degree of uncertainty increases with separation from the centre of the distribution of the predictor variable. That is, unless the value of the intercept coincides with the value of the mean, the standard error of the ordinate at the intercept will always be larger than the standard error of the ordinate at the mean of the ‘predictor’ variable (Fig. 2B and C ).

Since the standard error of the estimate is a measure of the accuracy of prediction, we wish to accord to the intercept (in its contribution to the generation of a reference value) a weighting that varies inversely with its standard error. Specifically, as the standard error of the estimate at the intercept tends to infinity, the contribution of the intercept to the generation of a reference value should tend towards zero. As noted above, as the slope of the regression line approaches zero, the reference value derived from the intercept will converge upon that of the mean of the original values. Accordingly, therefore, the relative weighting of the intercept should also be such that, with increases in its standard error, the reference value converges on the mean. In summary, the extent to which the reference value used to adjust the residuals differs from the mean of the predicted/actual values will depend on the slope of the regression line and the uncertainty with which its intercept can be estimated.

The weighting scheme defined

For the present purposes, the total standard error (SET) is defined as the sum of the standard error of the prediction for the intercept (SEi), and the standard error of prediction for the mean (SEm). In circumstances in which the data are clustered tightly around the line of best fit (low overall variance) the SEi will be lower than in circumstances in which the data are widely dispersed. In the former case, the SEi will be in closer approximation to the SEm than in the latter case. Since the SEi can never be lower than the SEm (both will approach zero as the dispersion of the points tends to zero), the proportion of the SET constituted by the SEm can never exceed 0.5. It is constrained to the range 0 to 0.5. In contrast, the SEi/SET may range between 0.5 and 1.

In summary, SEi/SET ranges between 0.5 and 1, SEm/SET ranges between 0 and 0.5. The weighting for the intercept (W i) is therefore: 1−SEiSET+SEmSET

The weighting for the mean (W m) is therefore: SEiSET−SEmSET

In the (extreme) case in which SEi/SET = 1 and SEm/SET = 0, it follows that the weighting for the intercept = 0; the weighting for the mean = 1. In the (other extreme) case in which SEi/SET = 0.5 and SEm/SET = 0.5, it follows that the weighting for the intercept = 1; the weighting for the mean = 0.

The weighting scheme applied

The weighting scheme described above was used to derive a reference value for each of the participant‐specific (n = 182) samples. The relevant reference value was then added to the residuals (typically n = 20) generated for each participant by application of the linear regression model. Each resulting set of values constituted the adjusted MEP amplitudes for that participant. Examples of the fits obtained for individual participants are shown in Figs 3 and 4.

The relative influence of the slope and the standard error of prediction

The scheme applied to generate the reference value derived for each participant entails that it is dictated by the slope of the regression line and by the magnitude of the standard error of prediction for the intercept – expressed relative to the standard error of prediction at the mean. In principle, the weighting of the intercept can range between 0 and 1. In practice, the empirical values tend to be less than 0.5 (Fig. 5A ). Consistent with the positive sign of the correlation coefficients representing the associations between r.m.s. EMG and MEP amplitude, positive estimates of the slope predominate over those which are negative. Furthermore, extreme (very large or very small) values of the slope tend to arise only when the standard error of the prediction for the intercept (SEi) is relatively high. Since the weighting of the intercept is correspondingly low (Fig. 5A ), in such instances the reference value will be closer to the mean of the original values than to the intercept generated by the regression, and the magnitude of the adjustment made to the MEP amplitude will be relatively modest.

Implementation using quantile regression

In assessing the adequacy with which a standard LSR model can be applied to sample data, one of the key diagnostic criteria is that the residuals are normally distributed. Of the 182 samples to which the standard model was applied (i.e. using Gaussianised data), 13 did not satisfy this criterion. That is, the N‐value calculated for the residuals was negative in these cases. An entirely analogous procedure based on a quantile regression (QR) model was therefore also applied (to all samples). Whereas LSR estimates the conditional mean as a linear combination of the predictors, QR estimates a conditional quantile function (such as the 0.5 quantile, i.e. the median) as a linear combination of the predictors. Unlike least squares regression, QR does not entail an assumption that the outcome variable has constant variance or that samples conform to a particular parametric distribution. It is also robust to the presence of outliers. It was implemented here by means of the rq() function from the quantreg package (Koenker, 2022). For the most part, the model specification was equivalent to that used for least squares regression. The ‘tau’ parameter was specified as 0.5, indicating that the conditional quantile for the median was to be used. As confidence intervals (i.e. 95%) for prediction are reported rather than standard errors, the values were first converted to the equivalent standard errors before applying the algorithms described above.

There was an extremely close correspondence between the outcomes yielded by the parametric (least squares) and non‐parametric (quantile) regression models (Fig. 6). This was quantified by calculating (for each of the 169 samples for which the residuals satisfied the normality assumption of the standard model) the difference between the sample median of the adjusted MEP amplitudes derived using LSR and the sample median obtained through QR. Across the 169 participants, the mean difference (i.e. of the individual medians) between the estimates generated by the parametric (102.9 µV) and non‐parametric (102.8 µV) regression methods was 0.1 µV (bootstrapped 95% CI = −0.004 – 0.003 µV). The magnitude of the correlation (Spearman's rho) between the medians of the adjusted MEP amplitudes generated by the two regression methods was 0.92 (95% CI = 0.89–0.94).

Magnitude of the relationship between ‘background’ r.m.s. EMG and MEP amplitude

The slope derived from each regression model provides an indication of the relationship that exists between ‘background’ r.m.s. EMG and MEP amplitude. As has been emphasised, real data are characterised by variability, which is made apparent through the dispersion of individual data points relative to the regression line. The estimated magnitude of the slope (i.e. the extent to which it differs from zero) must be interpreted in this context. In standard least squares regression (LSR) the r‐squared value (also known as the coefficient of determination), quantifies the proportion of the variation in the dependent variable that is predictable from the independent variable. With respect to quantile regression, the pseudo r‐squared (Koenker &Machado, 1999) fulfils a similar role. In both cases, the corresponding square root (i.e. r) has a ready interpretation in terms of standardised slope (Trafimow, 2015). Since r provides a standardised measure of the strength and direction of the linear relationship, ranging from −1 for a perfect negative relationship to 1 for a perfect positive relationship, it can be used to weight the magnitude of the slope derived for each regression model. This approach was adopted here to estimate a (weighted) slope (derived using QR) for each of the 182 participants. The mean slope (n = 182) was 26.2 (95% CI = 10.0–50.8). In other words, an elevation of 1 µV in r.m.s. EMG was associated with an increase in MEP amplitude of approximately 26 µV. The corresponding value obtained when the weighted slope was derived using LSR was 30.3 (95% CI = 7.6–68.0).

Practical impact of adjustment for variations in ‘background’ r.m.s. EMG

It is not difficult to conceive of specific research questions for which the outcomes may depend on whether the data have first been adjusted to compensate for the association between ‘background EMG’ and MEP amplitude. For example, an investigator may wish to determine whether the magnitude of the descending (corticospinal) volley generated by TMS applied over the motor cortex is altered by a particular intervention. It is evidently critical to be assured that any observed variation in MEP amplitude (on which inferences concerning the descending volley are usually based) occurs independently of an intervention‐induced alteration in the state of the spinal motoneuron pool.

To illustrate the practical impact of the method of adjustment described above, I therefore compare data already used for its illustration with those obtained from the same participants following a series of movements performed subsequently by the opposite limb. In brief, following the TMS protocols already set out, each participant used their left arm to execute 150 ‘ballistic’ wrist flexion movements (see Ruddy et al., 2016 for details of an equivalent task). Five minutes after the cessation of these movements, the same TMS protocol was used again. The analyses described below were undertaken to determine whether inferences that can made by contrasting MEP amplitudes elicited prior to and following this intervention are contingent on whether the MEP amplitudes have been adjusted for variations in ‘background’ r.m.s. EMG.

In addressing the issue as to whether compensation for background EMG has a material effect on variations in MEP amplitudes expressed across experimental conditions, robust statistical methods (Algina et al., 2005; Wilcox, 2017) were used. The standard approach of first expressing the change from pre‐ to post‐intervention as a difference score (of the two medians) for each participant was followed. A standardised robust effect size (ES) measure (and corresponding bootstrap confidence interval) was then calculated on the difference scores following the methods described by Kirby and Gerlanc (2013).

When the analysis was performed using the unadjusted MEP amplitudes, the ES (corresponding to Cohen's d) was 0.31 (95% CI 0.17–0.45) (Fig. 7A ). If the adjusted (by means of the QR method) MEP amplitudes were used instead, the corresponding ES was 0.09 (95% CI −0.07 to 0.24) (Fig. 7B ). Following the use of LSR, the ES obtained for the adjusted MEP amplitudes was 0.11 (95% CI −0.05 to 0.26). Thus, if the analysis were based on the unadjusted MEP amplitudes, it would be inferred that the values obtained were larger following the 150 movements performed by the opposite limb than was the case prior to this training, whereas if the analysis were based on MEP amplitudes adjusted in respect of r.m.s. EMG recorded prior to stimulation, it would be inferred that the MEP amplitudes observed prior to and following the intervention did not differ (i.e. for both methods of adjustment, the ES confidence interval included zero).

Impact of the period in which the background EMG was estimated

When quantifying background EMG, the objective is typically to assay the post‐synaptic state of the spinal motoneurons in the closest practicable temporal proximity to the delivery of the stimulation. Since the data are digitised at a fixed frequency, there is, however, a trade‐off between this proximity and the number of samples that are available to estimate central tendency (i.e. of the r.m.s. EMG). While the most (statistically) reliable estimates will be obtained for longer pre‐stimulus periods, necessarily in such cases, the initial samples will have been obtained further in advance of the stimulus than if a shorter period was used instead. With the view to following customary practice, a pre‐stimulus interval of 100 ms duration was adopted in the present investigation. Supplementary analyses were, however, also undertaken to assess any potential impact of the period in which the background EMG was estimated. To this end, intervals of 10 ms and 50 ms duration (following Carson, 2026) were also employed. Furthermore, a second interval of 50 ms duration, which commenced 103 ms prior to the stimulus and concluded 53 ms prior to the stimulus was used, with the aim of dissociating any effect of interval duration from that arising from the proximity of the interval in which the r.m.s. EMG was estimated, to the onset of stimulation.

The first outcome measure examined in this context was the Spearman correlation coefficient representing the magnitude of the association between the MEP amplitudes, and the level of EMG activity recorded during the respective intervals prior to the TS. The values of rho were as follows: 10 ms interval, 0.06 (95% CI = 0.02–0.09); 50 ms interval, 0.07 (95% CI = 0.03–0.11); 103 ms to 53 ms prior to the TS, 0.09 (95% CI = 0.05–0.12). By way of comparison, the value of rho obtained for the 100 ms interval was 0.09 (95% CI = 0.05–0.13). Since the confidence intervals overlap, ostensible differences in the magnitudes of these measures of association should be interpreted cautiously. It was clearly not the case, however, that the magnitude of the association between the MEP amplitude and background EMG was greater for the interval (10 ms) which comprised a higher proportion of samples in closest proximity to the TS.

As a second means of assessing the impact of the interval used to estimate r.m.s. EMG, separate effect size estimates for the difference between pre‐ and post‐intervention adjusted MEP amplitudes were generated. Recall that when the analysis was performed using the unadjusted MEP amplitudes, the ES was 0.31 (95% CI 0.17–0.45) (Fig. 7A ). If the adjustment (by means of the QR method) of MEP amplitudes was based on the 100 ms pre‐stimulus interval, the corresponding ES was 0.09 (95% CI −0.07 to 0.24). For the 50 ms interval, the ES was 0.06 (95% CI −0.09 to 0.21); for the 10 ms interval, 0.13 (95% CI −0.03 to 0.27); and for the 103 ms to 53 ms pre‐stimulus interval, 0.11 (95% CI −0.05 to 0.28). Thus, regardless of the interval used for its estimation, compensation for background EMG markedly attenuated (i.e. to the point of elimination) differences in MEP amplitude otherwise observed to exist between pre‐ and post‐intervention conditions.

At least based on analyses of the present data, it can be concluded that the duration of the period preceding stimulation, over which estimates of r.m.s. EMG are obtained, exerts rather subtle effects on the magnitude of associations with MEP amplitude. In the absence of clear evidence to support an alternative, it therefore appears that the 100 ms pre‐stimulation interval (that is used customarily) provides an adequate basis upon which to compensate for the excitability of spinal motoneurones when estimating the magnitude of evoked potentials.

The impact of exogenous (non‐physiological) sources of noise present in EMG recordings

Surface EMG recordings, including those from which estimates of MEP magnitude, are derived, may be subject to ‘contamination’ by various potential sources of ‘noise’. Due to the alternating current (AC) present in mains‐powered stimulation and recording equipment, along with the ubiquitous electromagnetic fields generated by nearby appliances (including lighting) and wiring, noise with a frequency matching that of the electrical supply (i.e. 50/60 Hz) and/or its higher harmonics, is often present. So called digitisation noise may also be introduced through the process of analog to digital conversion. To these exogenous potential sources of noise, we can add movement artefacts, electrocardiographic (ECG) artefacts, and fluctuations arising from changes in skin conductance or the impedance of the electrode–skin interface.

Prior to digitisation of the data that have been described, a high‐pass (analog) filter of 100 Hz was applied. It is improbable that changes in the impedance of the electrodes, skin conductance (as might arise from variations in level of arousal) or heart rate would have occurred at frequencies higher than the stopband of this filter. For a similar reason, line‐frequency (in this case 50 Hz) interference, was not typically present in the EMG records. It is, however, pertinent to consider other potential sources of noise, including those with a frequency content that matches harmonics of the AC supply, which may vary on the time scale of fluctuations in interference EMG signals (up to 500 Hz, e.g. Muceli & Merletti, 2024). Since these sources are extrinsic to the body (i.e. they are non‐physiological), there is little reason to suppose (a priori) that transitory changes in EMG signal amplitude to which they give rise reflect causal processes that affect corticospinal excitability (and thus MEP amplitude). Rather, it is more likely to be the case that the representation of any such sources of noise in EMG records, will serve to diminish the magnitude of the statistical association between r.m.s. EMG and MEP amplitude. Further analyses were undertaken to examine this conjecture.

Power spectrum analyses of the EMG records were conducted using the 100 ms duration intervals preceding the delivery of TMS. To enhance the resolution of the estimates obtained, the entire set of 100 ms duration pre‐stimulus epochs (typically n = 20) obtained for each participant were catenated to produce a data record of (usually) 2 s duration. A high pass digital filter of 20 Hz was then applied to remove the influence of artefacts arising from the process of catenation. Following linear detrending and the removal of DC bias, a Hanning window was applied to each catenated time series. The power spectral density (PSD) function was calculated using Welch's method, with an approximate frequency resolution of 1.22 Hz (Welch, 1967).

Visual inspection of the power spectra (n = 182) revealed, for some participants, narrow peaks at specific higher harmonics (150, 250, 350 and 450 Hz) of the line (AC supply) frequency. The percentage of the total power of the signal in the range 20–500 Hz, accounted for by these harmonics was then obtained. This was achieved by first calculating the power in the immediate vicinity of each harmonic using a bracket that spanned approximately 2.44 Hz. For example, power at 150 Hz was calculated as the sum of the power present at 149.00, 150.22 and 151.44 Hz. The values generated for the four respective brackets (i.e. around 150, 250, 350 and 450 Hz) were then summed, and expressed relative to the total power present between 20 and 500 Hz. The power constituted by the four harmonics was 27% (95% CI = 24–29%) of the total power.

Importantly, since this method yielded a separate estimate for each participant, it was also possible to determine whether there was an association between the percentage of the total power of the EMG signal accounted for by these non‐physiological sources and the individual specific correlations between r.m.s. EMG and MEP amplitude. The magnitude of the Spearman correlation coefficient (rho) that quantified this association (n = 182) was −0.20 (95% CI = −0.34 to −0.05). This indicates that the participants for whom a relatively low proportion of the total EMG power spectrum was accounted for by harmonics of the line frequency tended to exhibit the larger correlations between r.m.s. EMG and MEP amplitude. Conversely, those participants for whom a relatively high proportion of the total power was accounted for by harmonics of the line frequency tended to exhibit lesser (including negative) correlations between r.m.s. EMG and MEP amplitude. This finding implies that, to the extent that extraneous (i.e. non‐physiological) sources of variation are represented in the EMG recording, correlation coefficients that quantify the association between pre‐stimulus r.m.s. EMG and the amplitude of the TMS evoked potential will underestimate the true magnitude of the underlying physiological relationship. By extension, if non‐physiological sources of variation (e.g. line frequency related) constitute a sufficiently large portion of the EMG signal content, the relationship that exists between the excitability of spinal motoneurones and the magnitude of MEPs will be masked.

How much of the variance in MEP amplitudes can be accounted for by fluctuations in ‘background’ r.m.s. EMG?

The non‐parametric (Spearman) correlation coefficient corresponding to the association between the amplitudes of potentials evoked by cortical magnetic stimulation and the level of EMG activity recorded in a notionally quiescent muscle during the 100 ms duration period prior to the TS was relatively modest (rho = 0.09 (95% CI = 0.05–0.13)). It is therefore reasonable to enquire whether associations of at least this magnitude might be obtained in other similar experimental contexts. To address this issue, I consider here data obtained from a further 33 participants included in experiments that used apparatus, TMS procedures and instrumentation (i.e. for EMG recordings) that were identical to those described above. In this instance, analog to digital conversion was by means of a National Instruments NI PCI‐6251 m Series DAQ board.

In these experiments, as per the protocol described by Carson et al. (2008) (see also Carroll et al., 2001), an ‘active recruitment curve’ for right FCR was generated by delivering TMS over the left hemisphere (at 120% RMT) during isometric contractions (applying right wrist flexion torque) of varying intensities (10%, 20%, 30%, 40% and 50% of ‘maximum voluntary contraction’ (MVC)). Magnetic stimulation was delivered only when the applied torque was maintained within 2% of the designated target for a period of 600 ms. There were 10 trials for each target torque. In addition, there were 10 (‘at rest’) trials in which the participant was not required to apply torque. The contraction and ‘at rest’ trials were presented together in a randomised sequence. Quantile regression methods were used to describe the relationship between MEP peak‐to‐peak amplitude and the r.m.s. amplitude of the EMG signal in the 100 ms period preceding the delivery of stimulation.

The mean (across 33 participants) pseudo r‐squared value (Koenker &Machado, 1999) derived for the trials (typically n = 50 per participant) on which there were voluntary contractions was 0.38 (95% CI = 0.33–0.42). To put this value in context, an r‐squared value of 0.46 was reported by Carroll et al. (2001) for the first dorsal interosseous muscle. In respect of the current data, the corresponding figure calculated for trials in which the participant was not required to apply torque was 0.12 (95% CI = 0.08–0.17). It is striking that, in conditions in which ‘at rest’ trials were within a sequence that also included voluntary contractions, the pseudo r‐squared value was approximately one third of that obtained for the active recruitment curve.

During the ‘at rest’ trials in which torque was not to be applied, the mean (across 33 participants) of the median r.m.s. values obtained for each participant across all trials was 1.26 µV (95% CI = 1.06–1.58). By way of comparison, the mean of the median r.m.s. values for the 10% MVC contraction condition was approximately 20 times greater (24 µV (95% CI = 20–29)). The mean of the maximum r.m.s. values obtained for each participant across all ‘at rest’ trials was 3.19 µV (95% CI = 2.53–4.48). Thus, it was not simply the case that the participants erroneously performed voluntary contractions during the no torque trials. Indeed, the experimental control software precluded stimulation in such circumstances. Notably, when ‘at rest’ trials were interspersed with trials on which wrist flexion torques in the range 10–50% were applied, the ‘background’ EMG r.m.s. amplitude was almost double that recorded in circumstances in which only ‘at rest’ trials were included (i.e. the value of 0.75 µV reported above for the 182 participants engaged in otherwise identical experimental conditions). The maximum r.m.s. values (i.e. mean = 3.19 µV) obtained were also approximately double those which characterised trials (i.e. for the 182 participants) in only ‘at rest’ conditions (1.43 µV). All these r.m.s. values remain well below the lower limits used conventionally for ‘background EMG’ screening. The mean (across 33 participants) of the median MEP amplitude values obtained for each participant across all (interspersed ‘at rest’) trials was 211 µV (95% CI = 171–266). In contrast, the corresponding mean value registered for the 182 participants described previously, in ‘at rest’ only trials, was 106 µV (95% CI = 97–117). Even though they would not be liable for exclusion by the application of typical screening procedures, the elevated pre‐stimulus r.m.s. EMG values recorded when ‘at rest’ trials were interspersed with contraction trials, were accompanied by markedly and discriminably larger MEP amplitudes (note that for both cohorts, the TMS procedures and intensity (120% RMT) and the analog signal conditioning, were the same). When the MEP amplitudes recorded in the interspersed ‘at rest’ trials were adjusted for the r.m.s. amplitude of the EMG signal recorded in the 100 ms period preceding the delivery of stimulation (by means of the QR method), the mean (across 33 participants) was 175 µV (95% CI = 137–223). This corresponds to a reduction (35 µV) of approximately 17% of the unadjusted value. The corresponding diminution obtained for the 182 participants engaged in the primary analyses was 7%.

In short, these additional analyses reveal that, given an ostensibly minor change in experimental context (in this example ‘at rest’ trials that were interspersed with contraction trials, rather than conducted in isolation), associated variations in ‘background EMG’ extending over a typical range of less than 3.5 µV can account for approximately 12% of observed fluctuations in MEP amplitude. By way of reference, variations in voluntary EMG activity observed in the generation of ‘active’ (10%–50% MVC) recruitment curves accounted for 38% of fluctuations in MEP amplitude. Furthermore, following compensation for such (notionally small, i.e. falling below conventional screening thresholds) variations in ‘background EMG’, the resulting estimations of MEP amplitude were altered by 17%, on average.

Discussion

The data reported herein corroborate the previous finding (Carson, 2026) that fluctuations in the excitability of spinal motoneurones, manifested as variations in ‘background’ EMG signals extending over a range that does not exceed 2 µV, are reliably associated with the amplitudes of motor potentials evoked contemporaneously in a skeletal muscle (see also O'Sullivan et al., 2025). As per the Techniques for Physiology guidelines, the aim of the current presentation is to outline a means of adjusting for this association, with the intent of equipping investigators with a method that can be used to address more adequately, deeper questions about physiology. Nonetheless, it may be apposite to remark upon the assumed mechanistic basis for the association that has been observed.

Theoretical considerations

Lloyd (1945) proposed that the ‘excited zone’ of the motoneurone pool innervating a skeletal muscle comprises a ‘discharge zone’ and a ‘subliminal fringe’. The former refers to motoneurones in receipt of excitation sufficient to exceed firing thresholds. The latter corresponds to cells depolarised by excitatory input, but not brought to firing threshold (Denny‐Brown & Sherrington, 1928). Descending input to the pool generated by cortical TMS will cause the recruitment of motoneurones (contributing to MEPs) in proportion to the size of the subliminal fringe (Carroll et al., 2002; Turton & Lemon, 1999; see also Theodosiadou et al., 2023). This has been referred to as ‘spatial recruitment’ (e.g. Slot & Sinkjær, 1994). The descending volley may also cause some motoneurones in the discharge zone (those late in the after‐hyperpolarisation period) to fire – to which the term ‘temporal recruitment’ (e.g. Slot & Sinkjær, 1994) has been applied.

Since the discharge zone is composed of motoneurones already in receipt of excitation sufficient to exceed firing thresholds, the combined agency of its size and the compound firing rate of its constituent elements (i.e. prior to stimulation) can be inferred from the (surface) EMG interference pattern, in so much as the (r.m.s.) amplitude of this signal reflects the summation of the electrical potentials originating in only those individual muscle fibres innervated by discharging motoneurones. It has long been established (e.g. Bedingham & Tatton, 1984), primarily through stimulation of peripheral afferents, that the magnitude of evoked excitatory reflexes varies in accordance with the pre‐existing level of ‘motor discharge’ (i.e. as reflected in EMG). Referred to as ‘automatic gain compensation’, this phenomenon (considered to be a functional attribute of skeletal muscle reflexes) has been attributed to changes in both spatial recruitment and temporal recruitment of motoneurones that follow variations in the net excitation of the pool (Matthews, 1986). The positive relationship that was observed to exist (for most participants in the present study) between fluctuations in the size of MEPs and the contemporaneous EMG signal amplitude is consistent with the manifestation, if not the functional utility, of automatic gain compensation (e.g. Turton & Lemon, 1999).

If an investigator wishes to employ MEPs as a basis upon which to make an inference concerning the magnitude of the descending (‘corticospinal’) volley brought about by TMS (when the participant is nominally ‘at rest’), it is thus necessary to adjust for the moderating influence (on MEP amplitude) of variations in the net excitation of the spinal motoneurone pool. In respect of this aim, the first objective addressed by the present technique was to estimate the size of the MEP that would have been obtained in each trial under (hypothetical) conditions in which the pre‐existing level of ‘motor discharge’ had remained constant across trials. The approach taken in achieving this objective was to express the amplitude of each MEP relative to the value predicted by the contemporaneous EMG signal amplitude. This was done by regressing MEP amplitude on the r.m.s. EMG recorded during the 100 ms prior to stimulation. The residuals generated by this method may be interpreted as the difference between the observed values and those which would have been obtained had the net excitation of the spinal motoneurone pool remained invariant (i.e. across trials).

The intercept (with the y‐axis) of the linear regression model (i.e. for which the extrapolated EMG signal amplitude has zero magnitude) corresponds to a state in which all motoneurones comprising the ‘excited zone’ are in the subliminal fringe and none are in the discharge zone. Notionally therefore, in this state, the generation of a MEP in response to TMS occurs entirely through spatial recruitment of motoneurones (i.e. of those in the subliminal fringe). As there are no motoneurones in the discharge zone, there is no temporal recruitment of motoneurones. Importantly, however, even though the intercept is taken to reflect a (usually) theoretical condition in which there are no motoneurones in the discharge zone, it is nonetheless derived from a series of observations (i.e. constituting the sample) in which EMG activity was manifest.

What further inferences may be made? In relation to the Ia afferent mediated stretch reflex (in which context the principle of automatic gain compensation was elaborated), it is a simplifying proposition that the background excitatory drive to the pool (i.e. independent of the excitatory input which elicits the evoked response) depolarises all motoneurones equally (Matthews, 1986). Correspondingly, it is assumed that (for the Ia mediated stretch reflex) spatial recruitment is in accordance with the size principle (Henneman & Mendell, 1981) and contributes proportionately (i.e. by following the net excitation of the pool) to automatic gain compensation (Matthews, 1986). It has been shown that the descending volleys generated by TMS also produce orderly recruitment of spinal motoneurones in accordance with the size principle (Bawa & Lemon, 1993). If, in respect of the elicitation of MEPs, the proportionality assumed for the Ia mediated stretch reflex response also applies, the intercept of the regression model can also be accorded a further specific physiological interpretation. All other factors being equal (a point to be considered in greater detail below), its value quantifies the spatial recruitment of motoneurones by the descending volley (estimated over all trials in the sample), independently of the EMG signal amplitude that pertained as each MEP was generated. Accordingly, through the addition of this (sample specific) value to the residuals (which quantify within sample, trial‐to‐trial, variations in extent of recruitment brought about by the descending volley), comprehensive adjustment for the moderating influence (on MEP amplitude) of variations in the net excitation of the spinal motoneurone pool can (in principle) be achieved.

It might be remarked that the interpretation offered in relation to the intercept of the regression model shares with the ‘triple stimulation technique’ (TST) (Magistris et al., 1998, 1999) the assumption that the measure (i.e. the value of the intercept and the TST response) relates only to the spatial recruitment of motoneurones. In the case of the TST, an influence of multiple discharges on the response magnitude (i.e. temporal recruitment) is precluded by the (electrophysiological) process through which the response is elicited (Rösler et al., 1999).

Practical considerations

Necessarily, the application of a linear regression model to data obtained experimentally will not account for all variation inherent to the sample (potential extraneous sources of variation are discussed again below). Consequentially, there will be a degree of uncertainty associated with the (ordinate) position of the intercept generated by the model. In recognition of this fact, a weighting scheme was implemented to compute a mitigated reference value, with respect to which the residuals could subsequently be expressed. In this scheme, the weighting accorded to the intercept is inversely proportional to the standard error of its estimate – as this is rendered relative to the standard error of the ordinate at the mean of the predictor variable (which is the r.m.s. EMG). The application of this weighting scheme entails that, as the uncertainty associated with estimation of the intercept tends towards zero, the mitigated reference value approaches that of the intercept. Conversely, as the uncertainty associated with estimation of the intercept rises, the mitigated reference value tends towards the mean of the original MEP amplitude values (which is necessarily the same as the ordinate value estimated via the model at the mean of the predictor variable). Likewise, as the slope of the regression line tends towards zero and the intercept converges upon the mean of the original values, the same mitigated reference value will ultimately be obtained. Although derived through a statistical procedure, in this latter case the mitigated reference value (equal to the mean of the original values) has an obvious physiological interpretation. It relates to a circumstance (i.e. no relationship between the amplitudes of MEPs and the contemporaneous EMG signal amplitudes) in which the principle of automatic gain compensation was not expressed. In this instance and when the value of the intercept yielded by the regression model is sufficiently uncertain, the best available estimates of the magnitude of the descending volley brought about by TMS are obtained as the sums of the residuals with this mitigated reference value. Equivalently, these are the original MEP amplitude values.

The adjustments to be applied in practice lie on a continuum, with the extent of the adjustment in each case determined by the slope of the (linear) relationship between MEP amplitude and r.m.s. EMG (i.e. the degree to which automatic gain compensation was manifested), and the uncertainty associated with estimation of the intercept.

Notable features of implementation

It is well documented that distributions of samples which comprise MEP amplitude values, whether obtained sequentially from a single individual (Goetz et al., 2014; Nielsen, 1996) or recorded from different individuals (Nielsen, 1996; Ravnborg & Dahl, 1991), tend to display marked deviations from normality. This characteristic extends also to samples composed of r.m.s. EMG records derived from separate trials (e.g. Rupasov, Lebedev, Erlichman et al., 2012a, Rupasov, Lebedev, Erlichman, Lee et al., 2012b). The data included in the present report conformed, in so much as most of the samples (of both MEP amplitudes and r.m.s. EMG) were not normally distributed. A transition from a log‐normal to a normal distribution has been reported for sequences of MEP amplitudes that extend beyond 20 trials (Schmidt et al., 2009). Since in most TMS studies, however, fewer than 20 responses are gathered in each sequence, there is typically a need for data transformation prior to (parametric) statistical analyses, such as those based on the ordinary least squares technique and methods that involve calculation of the mean as measure of central tendency.

Logarithmic transformations have been used frequently with a view to addressing the skewness inherent to samples composed of MEP amplitudes (e.g. Calvert, & Carson, 2023; Nielsen, 1996). This is not, however, a panacea. The blanket derivation of logarithms with a fixed base (such as a natural logarithm) may for some samples increase, rather than decrease, the degree of skewness (Feng et al., 2013). Even if the Box–Cox method (Box & Cox, 1964) is used to select one from a family of potential transformations, there remains a requirement to determine whether its application is appropriate, through statistical modelling of the actual and transformed data (Curran‐Everett, 2018). It is also necessary to consider that in most cases, log‐transformed data have no straightforward physiological interpretation. Model coefficients obtained from (e.g. ordinary least squares) modelling must be transformed into to the original scale through exponentiation. Even then the outcome is not necessarily translatable. For example, the geometric mean (i.e. the antilog of the lognormal mean) is a different quantity from the arithmetic mean of the original values (Feng et al., 2013).

One approach taken here was to employ an implementation of the Lambert W transformation (Goerg, 2011), a method motivated by systems theory that uses moments to Gaussianise, i.e. ‘unskew’, a random variable. As implemented via the LambertW package (e.g. Goerg, 2015), it can be applied to transform data that exhibit skewness and heavy tails (Tukey's g‐ and h‐distribution parameters, e.g. Yan & Genton, 2019). As the degree of tail‐heaviness or skewness of each sample was not known a priori, an empirical approach was adopted, whereby the normality of the transformed sample distribution was assessed under the assumption that the data were (1) symmetric heavy‐tailed, (2) skewed heavy‐tailed, or (3) skewed. The option that yielded the highest degree of normality was adopted to transform each respective sample of MEP amplitude and r.m.s. EMG values prior to the application of ordinary least squares modelling. The estimates (e.g. the residuals and the intercept) derived from the model through the application of this method were on the same scale as the original values.

While, for the purposes of statistical inference and modelling, it is advisable to work with the Gaussianised data, it is possible to transform the results back to the ‘skewed world’ (Goerg, 2011) of the MEP amplitudes. In the present report, this step was not taken. That is, after regressing the Gaussianised MEP amplitudes on the Gaussianised r.m.s. EMG values, the adjusted residuals were not back‐transformed to approximate the degree of skewness present in the original MEP amplitude data. Should a researcher consider it necessary or desirable to back‐transform the data prior to the next phase of the analysis pipeline, this step can be readily implemented. Omission of a back‐transformation might well be motivated by theoretical considerations. For example, Goetz et al. (2019) argue that the absolute difference (maximum minus minimum) measure of amplitude conventionally derived from the EMG record is especially sensitive to the largest spikes occurring within the short intervals that contain the nadir and zenith of the MEP, and that the generative physiological processes are most likely Gaussian.

Despite the appeal of a transform that permits the use of standard (least squares) models, there may occur instances in which the characteristics of the sample distribution are such that effective normalisation (i.e. of the residuals) is not achieved. This was the case for approximately 7% of the samples considered here. A second approach based on the use of QR was therefore also pursued. This robust method does not require that the samples or the residuals conform to a specific parametric distribution. It is also resistant to the presence of outliers. It was shown that the parametric (least squares) and non‐parametric (quantile) regression methods generated adjusted MEP amplitude values that were virtually indistinguishable. The mean difference in the participant‐specific medians (n = 169) produced by the two methods was 0.1 µV. The corresponding magnitude of their (Spearman's rho) correlation was 0.92. Given the broader application of the non‐parametric method, for most implementations, the use of quantile regression models is recommended.

Implications

The technique that has been described provides a means of compensating for the relationship that exists between the post‐synaptic state of the spinal motoneuron pool (as reflected by the contemporaneous EMG signal) and the magnitude of a response evoked in the innervated muscle by (e.g. cortical) stimulation. The proposed method of adjusting MEP amplitudes is presented as an alternative to the practice of rejecting experimental trials if the level of EMG activity recorded in a period preceding the delivery of stimulation exceeds a pre‐defined threshold. It has been noted that such thresholds can be arbitrary. The results presented here demonstrate that the practice itself is inadequate and fails to serve its intended purpose. A key finding in this regard is that there was no association between the magnitude of the difference between the original and adjusted MEP amplitude values and the level of the r.m.s. EMG signal recorded immediately prior to stimulation. That is, participants exhibiting higher levels of ‘background’ EMG were not systematically those for whom the greatest degree of adjustment (in MEP amplitude) was indicated. The rationale that underpins the technique presented here is that the key characteristic of an individual's data for which compensation is indicated is the magnitude of the association between the excitability of the motoneurone pool and the size of the stimulation evoked response – as this is manifested in the slope of the (linear) relationship between r.m.s. EMG and MEP amplitude. The necessary compensation cannot simply be achieved by setting more stringent exclusion criteria.

There have been previous hints that the custom of using thresholds as a screening tool is deficient. Calvert and Carson (2023) employed a procedure whereby individual trials were rejected if the r.m.s. EMG in recorded in FCR (or in the ECR) (100 ms) prior to stimulation exceeded 2.5 µV. Thereafter, they calculated for each participant, the quartiles of the remaining r.m.s. values (separately for each muscle). In circumstances in which an individual r.m.s. value was greater than the third quartile plus 1.5 times the interquartile range, the corresponding MEP was disregarded. These two steps led to the rejection of 15.6% of trials. The trimmed means of the remaining r.m.s. values were 1.34 µV for FCR and 1.36 µV for ECR. Nonetheless, four of the 30 participants exhibited (for the retained trials) non‐trivial (Kendall's tau > 0.12) associations between the r.m.s. EMG and MEP amplitudes. The correlations reported by Calvert and Carson were based on data pooled over three consecutive blocks of trials. It is reasonable to assume that an even greater degree of association would have been present had the corelations been based only on trials within a single block.

Thus, a key determination arising from the present study is that background EMG rejection thresholds fail in their intended purpose – which is to counter the influence of spinal motoneurone excitability on the amplitude of evoked potentials. This appears to be the case, even when thresholds as low as 2.5 µV (e.g. Calvert & Carson, 2023) are employed. It is worth reflecting on the likely repercussions when higher rejection thresholds are used. The present analyses indicate (at least for the FCR preparation considered here) that each 1 µV elevation of r.m.s. EMG is associated (based on quantile regression) with an increase in MEP amplitude of approximately 26 µV. Should it be the case that a rejection threshold of 10 µV is used in a hypothetical study, in which the r.m.s. EMG recorded in two experimental conditions varies by as little as 5 µV (e.g. 3 µV versus 8 µV), a corresponding difference in MEP amplitude of 130 µV would be anticipated – in the absence of any change in the efferent volley generated by TMS. Readers may wish to draw their own conclusions as to the potential implications when rejection thresholds of 50 µV or 100 µV are employed.

It is reasonable to enquire as to the consequences that might follow adoption of the technique that has been described. Illustrations (including a replication) were presented here. Consideration was firstly given to a study in which the participants used their left arm to perform 150 ‘ballistic’ wrist flexion movements, having been instructed to maximise on each attempt the acceleration of joint rotation. In this task, the FCR was a principal agonist. Prior to and following these movements, MEPs were obtained in the (opposite) right FCR using standard TMS protocols (e.g. Ruddy et al., 2016). In the absence of adjustment for contemporaneous r.m.s. EMG, effect size estimates (based on 182 participants) indicated that MEPs recorded following the movements of the opposite limb were of greater magnitude than those observed beforehand. Following application of the technique described herein, no such differences were obtained. The same pattern of outcomes was noted for a replication study that included 203 participants. Demonstrably therefore, adjusting for ‘background’ muscle activity can have a material influence on that which is deemed to be the impact on MEP amplitudes of an intervention.

In a further illustration, it was shown that in a context in which ‘active’ recruitment curves are generated, levels of ‘background’ EMG recorded during ‘at rest’ trials that are interspersed with voluntary contractions trials, are markedly greater than is the case when all trials are undertaken with the participant ‘at rest’. Correspondingly, MEPs recorded under the former condition are larger than would otherwise be the case. Indeed, in regression models, fluctuations in the magnitude of the EMG signal prior to stimulation accounted for approximately 12% of the variation in MEP size. Using the procedures described herein to compensate for the excitability of the spinal motoneurones manifested (via EMG) during the interspersed ‘at rest’ trials led to a 17% diminution in estimated MEP amplitudes.

Caveats and limitations

As each sample of MEP amplitude data is adjusted with respect to the contemporaneous ‘background’ EMG data, the present technique provides a means of comparing sets of observations obtained from a participant over the course of a testing session. It can be used to assist in the comparison of different experimental conditions. For example, adjusted MEP amplitude measures could be derived for separate instances in which the participants are instructed to engage in distinct forms of motor imagery. As has been illustrated, the technique can be applied to data recorded prior to and following an acute intervention, such as motor training.

It is recommended that the adjustment procedure should be undertaken only for samples comprising MEPs generated using the same TMS intensity. The differences in MEP amplitude variances that are known to occur as a function of stimulation intensity appear to be largely independent of fluctuations in background EMG (Capaday, 2021). Pooling samples of MEP amplitudes obtained using different TMS intensities is therefore likely to conflate two largely distinct sources of variability and thus reduce the precision with which the data can be modelled.

There are, however, certain factors of a more theoretical nature that warrant consideration. It has generally been assumed that the size of the subliminal fringe can (within limits) change independently of the magnitude of the discharge zone. In other words, the extent of automatic gain compensation can alter dynamically (e.g. Pruszynski et al., 2009). The belief is that this facility bestows flexibility in tailoring the recruitment of motoneurones to the (incipient) demands of goal‐oriented movement, including voluntary actions (e.g. Arányi et al., 1998; Kernell & Hultborn, 1990). The extent of gain compensation also appears to vary across muscles (Turton & Lemon. 1999). When using the present technique to compare experimental conditions, it is implicitly assumed that the degree of covariation in the size of the subliminal fringe (comprising motoneurones amenable to discharge by the stimulation induced excitatory volley) and the extent of the discharge zone, for which EMG signal amplitude provides a proxy, remains constant. Should the degree of covariation (i.e. the extent of automatic gain compensation) change in response to an experimental manipulation, the adjusted MEP amplitude values may no longer provide a valid basis for comparisons. The veracity of the assumption can, however, be verified by determining whether the slopes and intercepts of the modelled relationship between MEP amplitude and r.m.s. EMG are equivalent across conditions. It is important to note that the impact of dynamic changes in automatic gain compensation applies with equivalent force to conventional comparisons based on unadjusted MEP amplitude values (even following the rejection of trials based on ‘background’ EMG). While monitoring the slopes and intercepts of the modelled data does not force constancy in the magnitude of automatic gain compensation, unlike conventional approaches, the present technique at least provides the means to determine whether it holds in the specific experimental conditions under consideration. Of course, the slopes may also serve as dependent measures or as covariates.

Since it is impossible to be assured that the positions of EMG recording electrodes with respect to the muscle fibres have remained constant, the usual caveats apply in respect of comparisons undertaken using MEP amplitude values obtained on different occasions of testing. For similar reasons, as indeed is case for the conventional measure, it is difficult to conceive of circumstances in which adjusted MEP amplitude values could provide a basis for direct contrasts between participants.

The magnitudes of the associations for which compensation is indicated may sometimes be small (see also Carson, 2026), yet their presence can be discriminated across multiple participants with a high degree of confidence. It was also shown that the proportion of variance in MEP amplitude accounted for by fluctuations in r.m.s. EMG recorded ‘at rest’ can approach a third of that which is characteristic of ‘active’ recruitment curves. There are several factors that are likely to limit the extent of the quantitative estimates that can be obtained using experimental data derived from human participants. Sources of (extraneous) variation arise through the transduction and digitisation of EMG signals. The fidelity with which these signals can be represented is also constrained by the resolution of analog to digital conversion. Notwithstanding steps that may be taken to maintain constant the position and orientation of the stimulating coil with respect to underlying brain tissue, trial‐to‐trial variability and an overall diminution of MEP amplitude occurs due to phase cancelation of the electrical potentials generated in muscle fibres through the asynchronous discharge of motoneurones induced by the descending volley that TMS generates (Groppa et al., 2012).

An important additional finding was that the presence of extraneous (i.e. non‐physiological) sources of variation in the EMG recording (such as those generated by mains powered equipment) impedes accurate modelling of the association between pre‐stimulus r.m.s. EMG and the amplitude of the TMS evoked potential. Should non‐physiological sources constitute a sufficiently large portion of the recorded EMG signal content, the relationship that exists between the excitability of spinal motoneurones and the magnitude of MEPs will be obscured.

It can be appreciated that the high pass filter setting used in the present study was higher than would be the case in most studies. The r.m.s. EMG values obtained were, however, comparable to those reported previously for the FCR muscle – with the hands and forearms stabilised effectively – when different filter settings and amplification systems have been used, both by our research team (e.g. Calvert & Carson, 2023) and by others (e.g. Vercauteren et al., 2008; Zult et al., 2015). Although this filter setting will have attenuated the fundamental frequency (50 Hz) of the alternating current which supplied the mains powered instrumentation, it is likely that it also removed that portion of the EMG signal with a frequency content between 20 Hz and 100 Hz. As this may have diminished the signal (reflecting excitability of the spinal motoneurons) to (exogenous non‐physiological) noise ratio, its likely impact would have been to diminish the extent of the estimated associations between the r.m.s. EMG and MEP amplitude.

The more general point is that the metrics reported here, generated using methods such as correlation and regression, are likely to be underestimates of the true (physiological) magnitude of the associations thus revealed.

Conclusion

In 2012, it was estimated that 20,000 articles concerning TMS had been published in the previous 10 years (Chipchase et al., 2012). Search results returned by ‘PubMed’ indicate that there has been no subsequent diminution in the rate at which such papers have appeared. In reviews of this ever‐expanding literature (e.g. Wang et al., 2021), as in many of the individual reports, the magnitude of the MEP generated by TMS is frequently interpreted in terms of the ‘excitability of the motor cortex’. In this context, most practitioners of TMS would recognise and endorse the point made by Matthews (1999, p. 868) that ‘the cortical response is normally viewed via its actions on motoneurones and their contribution has to be assessed before understanding can be achieved’. It has been demonstrated here and elsewhere (Carson, 2026; O'Sullivan et al., 2025) that the step most often taken to ameliorate the potential ‘contribution’ of motoneurones to the cortical response, i.e. rejection of trials if the ‘background’ EMG activity preceding the delivery of TMS exceeds a pre‐defined threshold, does not achieve the desired outcome. This is illustrated by the fact that trial‐to‐trial fluctuations in r.m.s. EMG over a range that is below the lowest reported ‘rejection threshold’ are associated with systematic variations in the amplitude of MEPs. While the registered association can be relatively small (rho = 0.09–0.15), for conventional stimulation and recording procedures, this most likely underrates the true physiological magnitude of the effect. It has been estimated independently (Rösler et al., 2008) that trial‐to‐trial variability in MEP amplitude reflects momentary changes in the extent of the subliminal fringe encompassing 10–15% of the spinal motoneurone pool innervating the target muscle.

The technique described here provides a means of compensating for the observed association between MEP amplitude and ‘background’ muscle activity. The extent of the compensation matches, for each individual sample, the magnitude of the observed relationship between variations in the r.m.s. EMG recorded from the target muscle and the amplitude of the evoked response. In principle, its application can be generalised to other circumstances (e.g. responses evoked by peripheral nerve stimulation) in which the effects of corresponding associations are also likely to be manifested.
