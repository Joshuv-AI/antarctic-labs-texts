Chondroblastoma of the femoral head: curettage without dislocation
Authors: Di Yang, Haiping Ouyang, Ziyu Zhou, Zhongliang Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChondroblastoma (CBL) of the femoral head is a rare disease, typically encountered in the epiphysis of long bones, with its occurrence in the femoral head being particularly uncommon. The unique location and aggressive nature of this tumor pose substantial challenges in its treatment, leading to ongoing controversies regarding the therapeutic approaches. In this study, we introduce a technique of curettage without surgical dislocation as a treatment option for CBL in this challenging location.MethodsA total of 6 children diagnosed with chondroblastoma of the femoral head underwent a surgical procedure consisting of curettage, the application of anhydrous alcohol as an adjuvant therapy, followed by bone grafting. The epiphyseal plate status of the femoral head was classified as open, closing, or closed. To evaluate the children's postoperative functional outcome, the Musculoskeletal Tumour Society (MSTS) scoring system was utilized. Additionally, the Lodwick classification was employed to assess the extent of bone destruction. Furthermore, the kappa coefficient was calculated to quantify the level of inter-observers agreement in assessing the status of the epiphyseal plate.ResultsThe epiphyseal plate status was closing in two patients and closed in four patients. According to the Lodwick classification, one patient was classified as IA, one as IB, and four as IC. The mean MSTS score was 28. Notably, one patient developed a femoral neck fracture three months post-curettage.ConclusionsCurettage without surgical dislocation, combined with the use of anhydrous alcohol as an adjuvant therapy, followed by bone grafting, constitutes an effective treatment technique for femoral head chondroblastoma.

Background

Chondroblastoma (CBL) is a rare non-malignant tumor characterized by local invasion. Nevertheless, it exhibits metastatic and recurrent tendencies similar to those observed in malignant tumors. This tumor commonly arises in the long bone epiphysis of adolescents and has the potential to penetrate the epiphyseal plate, invading the metaphysis. The male-to-female ratio is 3:2 [1].

In children, the most prevalent locations for CBL are the distal femur, proximal tibia, and proximal humerus, with curettage being the primary treatment modality. However, the femoral head is not a typical site for CBL, accounting for only 3.8% -16.7% of all CBL cases [2–8]. Nonetheless, chondroblastomas of the femoral head pose a therapeutic challenge in determining the optimal surgical approach, primarily due to the femoral head epiphysis being entirely within the joint capsule. Accessing the lesion necessitates breaching either the growth plate or the articular cartilage.

Six pediatric patients with CBL of the femoral head, treated at the Children's Hospital affiliated with Chongqing Medical University, were included in this study. All patients underwent curettage without surgical dislocation, anhydrous alcohol dehydration, and subsequent bone grafting. The aim of this study is to present our experience in the managing of CBL affecting the femoral head.

Methods

From 2017 to 2023, we included 6 children with chondroblastoma of femoral head who underwent surgical treatment in Children's Hospital affiliated with Chongqing Medical University. There were 3 males and 3 females. Inclusion criteria were: (1) Pathological diagnosis confirming chondroblastoma; (2) The lesion located at the femoral head; (3) Detailed hospitalization records available; (4) Regular postoperative follow-up records available. Exclusion criteria were: (1) Children who lost follow-up; (2) Children with lesions in other sites; (3) Children with irregular follow-up; (4) Children with incomplete hospitalization records; (5) Children with follow-up less than one year.

The following data were documented: age at diagnosis, gender, tumor location, tumor size, status of the femoral head epiphyseal plate, presence of epiphyseal plate invasion by the lesion, coexistence of aneurysmal bone cyst (ABC), surgical method, disease progression, presenting symptoms, recurrence, timing of complication onset, and treatment administered for the complication (Table 1). The status of the femoral head epiphyseal plate was defined as open, closing and closed. Open meant the epiphyseal plate was wide and transparent in X-ray; closing meant the epiphyseal plate was thin and irregular; closed meant the epiphyseal scar was present. The kappa coefficient was used to analyze interobserver variability in assessing the status of the epiphyseal plate. Preoperative CT imaging was utilized to determine the dimensions of the lesion and assess whether the epiphyseal plate was involved. The Lodwick classification system was applied to evaluate the degree of bone destruction, with the observed appearance described as follows: Type I represented a geographic pattern; Type IA signified absent or partial cortical penetration with the presence of a sclerotic rim and an expanded shell measuring less than 1 cm; Type IB denoted absent or partial cortical penetration lacking a sclerotic rim or featuring an expanded shell exceeding 1 cm; and Type IC indicated complete cortical penetration [9]. The Musculoskeletal Tumour Society scoring system (MSTS) [10] was used to evaluate the postoperative functional outcome of pediatric patients based on several criteria, including pain, function, emotional acceptance, supports, walking ability, and gait. Surgical technique: The patient underwent general anesthesia and local nerve block anesthesia. A cushion placed underneath the buttock on the affected limb to position the body at a 30-degree angle relative to the operating table. An incision was made extending from anterior superioriliac spine to the front of the greater trochanter. The skin, hypodermis, and deep fascia of the buttocks were dissected to access the space between the gluteus medius and tensor fascia lata, thereby exposing the joint capsule. Subsequently, a 'Z'-shaped incision was performed on the capsule to reveal the femoral head and neck. If the preoperative CT scan indicated that the lesion was located on the medial side of the femoral head, the affected lower extremity was maximally externally rotated. Localization of the femoral head lesion's edge was achieved using a C-arm X-ray machine, and an articular window was created at the lesion closest to the articular cartilage. One edge of the articular window was extended by 5 mm to facilitate fixation with absorbable cartilage pins upon replantation of the cartilage lid (Fig. 1). After curettage of the tumor though the window with a spatula, the cavity was irrigated with anhydrous alcohol for 5 min to dehydrate the residual tumor tissue, taking care to prevent spillage that could potentially damage surrounding tissues. Following irrigation, the cavity wall was scraped once more, and the cavity was thoroughly washed with normal saline. The cavity was then refilled with anhydrous alcohol, and this step was repeated to achieve a total alcohol contact time of 10 min. Subsequently, the cavity was copiously irrigated with normal saline solution, and irregular-shaped autogenous bone or strip-shaped artificial bone grafts (composed of a mixture of calcium phosphate and calcium sulfate) were utilized to fill the bone defect. Thereafter, the tumor on the lesion surface of the cartilage lid was removed, and the lid was replanted to ensure a smooth and flat articular surface. Two absorbable cartilage pins were used to stabilize the lid and the incisions were sutured routinely. Postoperatively the affected limb was immobilized with a brace. The patient was encouraged to commence early rehabilitation training and continuous passive motion starting on the second postoperative day, with partial weight-bearing allowed six weeks postoperatively and full weight-bearing permitted three months postoperatively.Fig. 1A 'Z'-shaped incision was made in the capsule, and an articular window was opened to expose the tumor

After the operation, patients underwent X-ray imaging every three months for the first two years, every six months for the subsequent three years, and annually thereafter. Recurrence was suspected if any new zone of bone destruction or enlargement of a low-density area was observed in the primary lesion on X-rays, compared to the postoperative baseline film. Patients with suspected recurrence underwent CT and MRI scans of the lesion site, accompanied by a chest CT.

Results

Among our six cases of chondroblastoma of the femoral head, there were three males and three females. Four cases were located on the left and two on the right, respectively. The average age at diagnosis was 12 years old (range: 11–13 years). The mean follow-up time was 33 months (range: 19 to 70 months). Based on preoperative radiographic findings, the epiphyseal plates of all patients were invaded by the tumor. Among them, the epiphyseal plates of two patients were in the process of closing, while those of four patients had already closed. According to the Lodwick classification system, one patient was classified as IA, one as IB, and four as IC. All children underwent curettage and bone grafting procedures. Specifically, four children opted for artificial bone implantation, one child chose autologous bone, and another child combined both artificial and autologous bone grafts. Postoperative biopsies confirmed that all cases were diagnosed as chondroblastoma, without any evidence of aneurysmal bone cyst (Table 1). During the follow-up, one child developed a pathological fracture of the femoral neck. At the last follow-up, the gait of the other children was normal, and a review of X-ray results showed normal joint spaces in bilateral hip joints, smooth surfaces of bilateral femoral heads without obvious depression, and no signs of recurrence. The postoperative MSTS score was 28. The inter-rater reliability of epiphyseal plate status was assessed using Kappa statistics, which showed a remarkable agreement between the two raters, with a kappa coefficient of 1 (p = 0.008).

Case 1

A 13-year-old male patient presented with pain and a limp in the left hip joint lasting for 17 days. Physical examination revealed limited range of motion in the left hip joint due to the pain. X-ray imaging demonstrated a low-density osteolytic lesion within the femoral head, characterized by internal septations and an irregularly defined border with a sclerotic rim. CT scans further delineated this low-density osteolytic lesion within the femoral head, featuring a well-circumscribed boundary, intact cortical bone, a sclerotic rim, and internal septations. Case 2

A 12-year-old female patient presented with a 2-week history of left hip pain and a limp. She reported no significant family or medical history. Physical examination revealed restricted mobility of the hip joint, particularly during external rotation and abduction movements. X-ray images demonstrated a low-density osteolytic lesion in the left femoral head, with involvement of the epiphyseal plate (Fig. 3). The lesion extended from the femoral head to the distal femoral neck, exhibiting a well-defined boundary. CT scans further confirmed a low-density osteolytic lesion in the anteromedial aspect of the left femoral head and neck, characterized by a clear boundary, a sclerotic rim, and a defect in the medial cortical bone. Given the lesion's extension from the femoral head to the femoral neck, the patient underwent curettage through the femoral neck. Histopathological examination revealed proliferating chondroblasts with a chondroid matrix and multi-nucleated giant cells, along with a fine network of pericellular "chicken wire" calcifications. Immunohistochemical staining showed positivity for S-100, CD68, and SATB2, leading to a consideration of chondroblastoma.Fig. 3X-ray image showing a 2 cm × 9 cm osteolytic lesion with a marginal sclerotic rim

Three months postoperatively, the patient was readmitted due to pain following a self-inflicted fall. Physical examination upon readmission indicated significant tenderness in the left hip, with shortening of the left lower limb compared to the right. Discussion

Chondroblastoma is a rare, non-malignant yet invasive tumor. The most prevalent locations of chondroblastoma in children are the distal femur, proximal tibia, and proximal humerus, although there is ongoing debate regarding which of these sites has the highest incidence rate [4, 5, 7, 11]. Nonetheless, chondroblastoma affecting the femoral head remains uncommon. Patients often present with mechanical symptoms such as pain, sometimes accompanied by lameness [12], prompting them to seek medical consultation. Chondroblastoma is typically treated with curettage, and adjuvant therapies including burring, liquid nitrogen, phenol, and bone cement can be employed to reduce the risk of local recurrence [13]. However, the use of these adjuvant treatments remains controversial due to the potential for damage to the joint cartilage [7, 11, 14]. The treatment of chondroblastoma located in the femoral head poses substantial difficulties, as the epiphysis of the femoral head is entirely intracapsular, and surgical intervention in this region increases the risk of ischemic necrosis of the femoral head [15].

The current surgical modalities for the treatment of femoral head chondroblastoma primarily encompass four approaches. The first approach involves accessing the lesion through a bone tunnel from the outside of the proximal femur, along the long axis of the femoral neck, to the femoral head (known as curettage via the femoral neck, or CVFN). This surgical approach is associated with a relatively high recurrence rate, primarily due to the inability to directly visualize the lesion located in the femoral head and the difficulty in precisely delineating between the lesion and healthy tissue. Additionally, the CBL tissue is soft and may be inadvertently left behind during the procedure, potentially increasing the risk of recurrence [4, 16]. Furthermore, this surgical approach could harm the epiphyseal plate of the femoral head in children [4, 16], as evidenced by an 11-year-old patient who underwent CVFN surgery and experienced a minor shortening (< 1 cm) of the affected lower limb a year after the operation [16].

The second procedure involves a direct approach through the head-neck junction, below the growth plate or the femoral neck. Although undeniably enhancing the surgeon's field of vision, achieving comprehensive tumor curettage through this technique remains challenging during the actual surgical procedure, particularly for lesions located in the anterior part of the femoral head [17]. Furthermore, despite Strong's report of no incidences of femoral head necrosis [16], the second surgical approach poses a potential risk of damaging the blood vessels that supply the femoral head, as the primary vascular supply traverses this region [18]. Consequently, this may elevate the likelihood of necrosis. Additionally, this approach also has the potential to harm the growth plate.

The third approach is the traditional trapdoor procedure, first reported by Iwai et al. in 2008 [19]. This technique involves dislocating the femoral head and creating a window through the articular surface to reveal the underlying subchondral tumor. It addresses the limitation of poor surgical visualization in previous methods, reduces the risk of recurrence due to residual tumor, and minimizes damage to the femoral head epiphyseal plate. Subsequently, Liu et al. and Xu et al. proposed modified trapdoor procedures, where access to the lesion is gained around the ligamentum teres, rather than directly on the articular surface, and the window closed using this ligament [14, 20]. During follow-up, two cases of femoral head necrosis and one case of osteoarthritis were reported, but no local recurrence was observed. However, it should be noted that this modified approach may not be suitable for lesions located close to the junction between the femoral head and neck. Additionally, surgical excision of the ligamentum teres can compromise the blood supply to the femoral head, thereby elevating the risk of femoral head necrosis. Ganz et al. have previously demonstrated that the blood supply to the femoral head primarily originates from the deep branch of the medial femoral circumflex artery (MFCA) [21]. However, numerous authors have reported the existence of ligamental arteries and their significant contribution to femoral head perfusion [22, 23]. The role of the ligamentum teres remains controversial. We tend to preserve the ligamentum teres, believing it will result in a more favorable prognosis for children. Akkaya et al. hypothesize that complete tears of the ligamentum teres are associated with subchondral changes, including bone marrow lesions and subchondral cysts, in the later stages of osteoarthritis. Furthermore, they observed that pulvinar effusion-synovitis, a shallow fovea capitis, and perifoveal osteophytes are also correlated with abnormalities involving the ligamentum teres, suggesting a potential role in the pathogenesis and progression of osteoarthritis [24]. The potential for developing secondary osteoarthritis among children treated with the traditional or modified trapdoor procedure remained uncertain, especially in long-term follow-up studies. Therefore, we recommend autologous articular cartilage replantation to ensure the smoothness of the femoral head surface. The trapdoor procedure necessitates extreme caution during the hip joint dislocate, as even with precautions, there is still a risk of damaging the articular surface of the femoral head, which could increase the chance of femoral head necrosis [21, 25]. Despite the surgical technique for hip dislocation proposed by Ganz et al. [21], which reported no cases of avascular necrosis among 213 patients, the dislocation process itself still can potentially damage the articular surface and compromise blood supply, thereby increasing the risk of necrosis. [15, 25–27]. Notably, Hirohisa Katagiri et al. reported on two patients who underwent focal curettage of the femoral head without hip dislocation, with no complications during the 6 and 12-year follow-ups, respectively. Emphasis was placed on selecting a non-weight-bearing region for accessing the articular surface, avoiding an opening directly on the articular surface proximal to the lesion. This approach, targeting a site located on the joint surface outside the acetabulum, eliminated the need for surgical dislocation and ensured that cancellous bone was retained between the tumor and articular cartilage [17].

The fourth modality, Radiofrequency Ablation (RFA), has demonstrated a relatively low recurrence rate in the management of chondroblastoma (CBL) of the femoral head, ranging from 0 to 12% [28–30]. Nonetheless, the high temperatures during the procedure pose a risk of iatrogenic damage to the femoral head. Specifically, Rybak et al. [28] reported a case of articular collapse in 1 out of 13 patients (6%), while Tins et al. [31] observed this complication in 2 of 4 patients (50%). Furthermore, Petsas et al. [32] reported a case of insufficient bone regeneration within one year following RFA, subsequent to diagnostic CVFN, in 2 adult patients with femoral head chondroblastoma. These findings underscore the ongoing uncertainty regarding the definitive efficacy of RFA in treating CBL of the femoral head.

It has always been a concern to avoid the injuring the epiphyseal plate of the femoral head during surgical procedures, as it promotes the elongation of femoral neck [33]. Simple curettage has been shown to increase the likelihood of local recurrence. Tomic et al. observed a high recurrence rate (up to 30%) with surgical treatment involving simple curettage, which they deemed unacceptable, particularly for invasive lesions [34]. Extended curettage can significantly reduce the risk of local recurrence in chondroblastoma of the femoral head, albeit with the potential to exacerbate damage to the epiphyseal plate [11]. However, Suneja et al. suggested that damage to the epiphyseal plate can be mitigated through appropriate postoperative nursing care. In their study, all patients underwent aggressive intralesional curettage as the sole treatment, which proved curative in most cases, albeit without comprehensive data on the status of the epiphyseal plate for all patients [3]. Liu Qing et al. posited that the epiphyseal plate near the knee joint exhibits resilience and has the potential to regenerate following extensive curettage. This assertion was based on two primary reasons: Firstly, only a minor portion of the epiphyseal plate is involved with the tumor; secondly, the blood supply to the epiphyseal plate originates from the soft tissue connected to the epiphysis, which remains largely unaffected during the surgical procedure [35]. Another study found that out of 20 children treated with extended curettage, only 2 experienced relapses, with no cases of leg length discrepancy (LLD) reported. Notably, most patients had epiphyseal plates that were either closing or already closed, with only one child having an open epiphyseal plate [5]. It is widely believed that the risk of LLD is lower when the epiphyseal plate is closed compared when it is open. Mashhour and Abdel Rahman recommended extended curettage during the initial surgery to reduce the chances of recurrence [36].

Anhydrous alcohol has been utilized to deactivate any residual tumor cells within the surgery cavity. Among the locally available adjuvants, including burring, liquid nitrogen, phenol, and cement, high-speed burring is predominantly used and widely acknowledged as the most effective adjuvant for preventing local tumor recurrence [2, 7, 37]. However, Cong Huang et al. have expressed concern that the heat generated by the burr could potentially harm the epiphyseal plate [11]. Similarly, cement has also been considered detrimental to the epiphyseal plate due to the heat it emits during the solidification process [7]. Furthermore, in the management of comparable tumors, such as giant cell tumors, phenolic compounds have been shown to reduce the risk of osteoarthritis compared to liquid nitrogen [38, 39]. Karem has advocated for the use of phenol and anhydrous alcohol as adjuvants in curettage [5]. Currently, there is a scarcity of published data examining the use of anhydrous alcohol as an adjuvant in the treatment of CBL. Anhydrous alcohol can induce protein denaturation in tumor cells, cause cytoplasmic degeneration, and lead to embolism in the small vessels that supply the tumors. Moreover, it has been established that anhydrous alcohol exerts minimal adverse effects on surrounding tissues [40].

When addressing CBL of the femoral head, the choice of surgical approach varied based on the lesion's location and extent. If the lesion was confined entirely within the femoral head and had minimal or no impact on the epiphyseal plate, we opted to create a window through the articular cartilage of the femoral head. Conversely, if the lesion significantly involved the epiphyseal plate and the CBL extended substantially into the femoral neck, we considered opening a window through the femoral neck to extract the tumor. This approach aimed to minimize damage to the articular cartilage, thereby preserving joint integrity and functionality.

Our research encountered several limitations. Firstly, there were few cases of femoral head involvement, which limited our ability to obtain more precise risk factors through our research. Secondly, our follow-up period was insufficient to adequately assess long-term complications.

Conclusions

Curettage, performed without surgical hip dislocation, in conjunction with bone grafting and the utilization of anhydrous alcohol as an adjuvant, demonstrates significant efficacy. This treatment approach serves to safeguard the femoral head articular cartilage and minimize the recurrence of CBL of the femoral head.
