MetALD-ALD prediction index exposes an unresolved reassignment rule in MetALD
Authors: Hiroyuki Suzuki, Shuhei Fukunaga, Tsubasa Tsutsumi, Keisuke Amano, Toru Nakamura, Takumi Kawaguchi
Published: 2026

Tavaglione et al. developed the MetALD-ALD Prediction Index (MAPI), a phosphatidylethanol (PEth)-anchored model based on routine clinical variables to identify steatotic liver disease with excessive alcohol use (1). The study addresses a pressing implementation problem: alcohol exposure is frequently incompletely ascertained, whereas direct PEth testing is not universally available. Importantly, the authors position MAPI not as a replacement for objective exposure assessment, but as a practical gate to confirmatory PEth testing [area under the receiver operating characteristic curves (AUROCs) 0.76 and 0.75], with final subclassification anchored to PEth rather than to MAPI itself (1).

In our view, however, the significance of this work extends beyond improved cross-sectional detection. MAPI makes operationally visible what metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease with increased alcohol intake (MetALD) currently lacks: an explicit rule for how changing exposure should alter classification over time. Under current nomenclature, MetALD is defined at the intersection of metabolic dysfunction and alcohol exposure (2). Yet alcohol exposure is inherently time-varying and only partially captured by one-time history-taking, even when supplemented by biomarkers such as PEth (3,4). Under these conditions, misclassification is not merely a technical limitation (5). It directly affects how risk is interpreted, how follow-up is structured, and how patients are stratified in research and clinical trials (6).

The authors appropriately acknowledge that a single PEth measurement may not fully reflect longer-term drinking patterns and that serial PEth assessment may help capture intraindividual variability (1). We agree but believe the central unresolved issue lies one step further downstream. The problem is not repeated testing itself. The problem is what repeated testing means for the category. A classification anchored to a short-window exposure biomarker implicitly treats MetALD as a revisable state, whereas clinical care tends to use diagnostic labels as relatively durable management categories. This mismatch remains underdefined.

That tension is particularly relevant in the context of MAPI. PEth reflects recent alcohol exposure over a relatively short time window. By contrast, the clinical features incorporated in MAPI do not necessarily operate on the same time scale. Mean corpuscular volume (MCV) is constrained by erythrocyte turnover and may remain abnormal for weeks to months after alcohol exposure has decreased, whereas hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) reflects average glycemia over approximately 3 months. Gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) may reflect prior hepatobiliary injury or enzyme induction in addition to current alcohol exposure, while high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) reflects the underlying metabolic substrate as well as alcohol-related effects. These are not delayed versions of the same signal. They are different biological layers on different time scales. Such discordance should not be dismissed as noise. It is the predictable consequence of combining information sources that are not expected to move in synchrony, and current MetALD definitions do not specify how such asynchrony should be interpreted. MAPI does not create this problem, and PEth-based subclassification already points in the same direction (3,4). These variables were not selected to model temporal discordance, but their coexistence within a PEth-anchored framework makes this tension more visible in longitudinal use.

This matters clinically. Consider a patient initially classified as MetALD who subsequently reduces alcohol intake and shows substantial PEth decline, while MCV remains elevated and metabolic dysfunction persists. Should that patient still be managed as MetALD, reassigned to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), or regarded as having a prior alcohol-modified phenotype that continues to inform risk (7,8)? Premature reassignment to MASLD in this setting could obscure the prior alcohol-modified phenotype and may lead clinicians to underemphasize historical alcohol-related injury when planning long-term monitoring. This is particularly relevant for patients with advanced fibrosis or elevated risk of hepatocellular carcinoma, in whom surveillance and risk communication should not depend solely on the most recent exposure biomarker. The current framework offers no operational rule. This scenario exposes the central unresolved rule in MetALD: whether a change in current exposure should revise the category itself, or whether prior alcohol-modified status should remain visible even after current exposure declines. The same problem arises in the opposite direction, when a patient initially labeled as MASLD later demonstrates objective evidence of increased alcohol exposure. Without predefined reassignment rules, classification status becomes highly dependent on the timing of reassessment and on how discordant biological layers are interpreted rather than on a stable and clinically interpretable disease construct (Figure 1) (9).

After alcohol exposure decreases, PEth declines rapidly, while injury-related features (MCV, GGT) decline slowly and substrate-related features (HDL-C, HbA1c) may remain altered. This creates a discordance window in which current exposure state and MetALD classification may no longer align. Viewed in this light, the sequential framework proposed by Tavaglione et al. does more than improve diagnostic efficiency (1). It highlights what remains undefined when MetALD is used longitudinally: how updated exposure information should modify subclassification over time. A category whose membership depends on a dynamic and incompletely ascertained exposure cannot serve as a durable management framework unless reassessment is built into the category itself.

The implications extend beyond diagnosis and beyond any single population. Because MAPI was developed in a Southern California cohort enriched for individuals with overweight or obesity and validated in a Swedish population-based cohort (1), its performance should not be assumed to generalize directly to populations with different alcohol-use patterns and metabolic phenotypes. Validation in Asian populations is particularly important because genetic variation in alcohol-metabolizing enzymes, including aldehyde dehydrogenase 2 (ALDH2) polymorphisms, and the higher prevalence of non-obese steatotic liver disease may alter the baseline distribution and predictive behavior of MCV, GGT, HDL-C, and HbA1c (6,10,11). In clinical trials, eligibility, stratification, and treatment effect interpretation may shift depending on whether alcohol exposure is captured only at baseline or repeatedly during follow-up. In observational cohorts, retrospective application of MAPI may improve subclassification when PEth is unavailable (1), but it also raises the question of whether a single baseline assignment is sufficient when the underlying exposure state may change. In routine care, the same issue affects surveillance planning and risk communication.

Accordingly, the next step is not simply broader implementation of serial PEth testing. The field also needs an explicit longitudinal framework for subclassification. At minimum, such a framework should distinguish current exposure state from prior alcohol-modified phenotype, specify whether reassessment is triggered by predefined follow-up intervals, objective exposure change, or both, and define how discordance between exposure markers and slower-moving injury or substrate markers should inform category revision. MAPI does not solve these questions, nor should it be expected to. Its value is that it brings them into sharper operational focus.

MAPI is useful not simply because it improves detection of excessive alcohol use when PEth is unavailable, but because it forces a more consequential question into view: how MetALD should be reassigned when exposure changes, but injury-related and substrate-related signals do not move in synchrony. Its most durable contribution may therefore be not the score itself, but the operational gap it makes impossible to ignore.
