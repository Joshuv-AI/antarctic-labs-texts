Symmetry resolved entanglement entropy after an inhomogeneous quench
Hui-Huang Chen, Xin-Liang Zhou, Jiayu Yin, Ming Zhang

Abstract
We investigate the non-equilibrium time evolution of symmetry-resolved entanglement entropy (SREE) following an inhomogeneous quench in a critical one-dimensional free fermion system. Using conformal field theory, we derive an exact expression for the SREE and analyze its behavior. We find that, at leading order in the long-time limit, the SREE grows logarithmically as log ⁡ t \log t . While the equipartition of entanglement holds at leading order, we identify subleading corrections that break it. Our numerical simulations corroborate the analytical predictions with excellent agreement.

2
Möbius/SSD deformation in CFT

In this section, we will briefly review some basic facts about Möbius/SSD deformation in CFT.

2.1
Möbius and SSD Hamiltonians

Let’s consider a finite one-dimensional system with length
L
and open boundary conditions are imposed at its ends
x=0
and
x=L
. The undeformed Hamiltonian can be written as

H_{0}=\int_{0}^{L}dx~h(x)\,,

(2.1)

where
h(x)
is the energy density. The SSD and Möbius deformations introduce position-dependent modulation via an envelope function
f(x)
,

H_{0}\rightarrow\int_{0}^{L}dx~f(x)h(x)\,.

(2.2)

The SSD Hamiltonian
H_{1}
is obtained by choosing
f(x)=2\sin^{2}\left(\pi x/L\right)
,

H_{1}=\int_{0}^{L}2\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi x}{L}\right)h(x)dx\,.

(2.3)

It’s convenient to introduce the Möbius deformation, which generalizes SSD with a tunable parameter
\theta\geq 0
. The Möbius deformed Hamiltonian (or Möbius Hamiltonian for short)
H_{1}(\theta)
corresponds to choosing
f(x)=1-\tanh(2\theta)\cos(2\pi x/L)
, and we can rewrite it as

H_{1}(\theta)=H_{0}-\frac{\tanh(2\theta)}{2}\left(H_{+}+H_{-}\right)\,,

(2.4)

where we have defined
H_{\pm}=\int_{0}^{L}e^{\pm 2\pi ix/L}h(x)dx
. For
\theta\to\infty
, the Möbius Hamiltonian
H_{1}(\theta)
reduces to the SSD Hamiltonian
H_{1}
in (
2.3
), while
\theta=0
recovers the undeformed Hamiltonian
H_{0}
in (
2.1
). The Möbius deformation interpolates between these limits, scaling the effective system size to
L_{\text{eff}}=L\cosh(2\theta)
. The SSD suppresses energy density at boundaries
x=0,L
, effectively removes the boundary effects of a finite system.

In terms of Virasoro generators, we have

H_{1}(\theta)=N_{\theta}^{-1}(\mathcal{L}_{0}+\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{0})\,,

(2.5)

where

\displaystyle N_{\theta}

\displaystyle=\cosh(2\theta)\,,

(2.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{0}

\displaystyle=\cosh(2\theta)L_{0}-\sinh(2\theta)\frac{L_{1}+L_{-1}}{2}\,.

(2.7)

Remarkably, the SSD ground state coincides with the uniform CFT vacuum under periodic boundary conditions, as
(L_{1}+L_{-1})/2
annihilates the vacuum.

In
[
16
]
, the author suggests that Möbius deformed CFT can most conveniently be viewed as CFT quantized on the so-called Möbius coordinate. The procedure is found to be equivalent to consider the following Möbius transformation

z\mapsto\zeta=-\frac{z\,\cosh\theta-\sinh\theta}{z\,\sinh\theta-\cosh\theta}

(2.8)

and define new Virasoro generators in terms of the mapped coordinate

\mathcal{L}_{n}=\oint\frac{d\zeta}{2\pi i}\zeta^{n+1}T(\zeta)\,,

(2.9)

where

T(\zeta)=\left(\frac{dz}{d\zeta}\right)^{2}T(z)=(\cosh\theta-z\sinh\theta)^{4}T(z)\,.

(2.10)

Here we have used the fact that the Schwartz derivative of any Möbius map is zero.

These new Virasoro generators
\mathcal{L}_{n}
satisfy the same Virasoro algebra as the radial quantized CFT. However, at the SSD point, i.e. when
\theta\rightarrow\infty
, and if we define the scaled generator and index as
\mathcal{L}/N_{\theta}\rightarrow\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{n},\,n/N_{\theta}\rightarrow\kappa
, we obtain a continuous Virasoro algebra

[\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\kappa},\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\kappa^{\prime}}]=(\kappa-\kappa^{\prime})\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\kappa+\kappa^{\prime}}+\frac{c}{12}\kappa^{3}\delta(\kappa+\kappa^{\prime})\,,

(2.11)

where
c
is the central charge and
\kappa
becomes a continuous index as
\theta\to\infty
. Consequently, SSD CFTs exhibit a continuous energy spectrum, mimicking an infinite system despite finite
L
.

3
SREE in free Dirac fermion CFT

In this section, we will review the fluxed twist field method of computing SREE in free Dirac fermion CFT. Let’s first introduce the concept of symmetry-resolved entanglement entropy. In the next subsection, we will discuss the fluxed twist field method.

3.1
Symmetry-resolved entanglement entropy

SREE quantifies the entanglement contribution from individual symmetry sectors of a quantum system. For a system with a conserved charge
Q
, the reduced density matrix
\rho_{A}
decomposes into blocks labeled by eigenvalues
q
of
Q_{A}
, the charge restricted to subsystem
A
. The SREE for sector
q
is defined as

S_{A}(q)=-{\textrm{Tr}}\left[\rho_{A}(q)\log\rho_{A}(q)\right]\,,\quad\rho_{A}(q)=\frac{\Pi_{q}\rho_{A}\Pi_{q}}{\text{Tr}(\Pi_{q}\rho_{A})}\,,

(3.1)

where
\Pi_{q}
projects onto the
q
-sector.

To compute the SREE, we first introduce the charged moments

Z_{n}(\alpha)={\textrm{Tr}}\left(\rho_{A}^{n}e^{i\alpha Q_{A}}\right)\,.

(3.2)

Then the symmetry-resolved Rényi entropies
S_{A}^{(n)}(q)
are derived via Fourier transform,

\mathcal{Z}_{n}(q)=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{d\alpha}{2\pi}e^{-i\alpha q}Z_{n}(\alpha)\,,\quad S_{A}^{(n)}(q)=\frac{1}{1-n}\log\left[\frac{\mathcal{Z}_{n}(q)}{\mathcal{Z}_{1}(q)^{n}}\right]\,.

(3.3)

The SSRE
S_{A}(q)
is obtained from
S_{A}^{(n)}(q)
by taking
n\to 1
limit. A direct computation leads to

S_{A}(q)=\log\mathcal{Z}_{1}(q)-\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{1}(q)}\frac{\partial\mathcal{Z}_{n}(q)}{\partial n}\Big|_{n=1}\,,

(3.4)

where
\mathcal{Z}_{1}(q)
can be interpreted as the physical probability distribution of the charge
Q_{A}
.

3.2
Fluxed twist fields method

In this paper, we will focus on the case where the post quench dynamics is governed by the Möbius/SSD Hamiltonian of a 2D massless free Dirac fermion CFT.
The action of the massless free Dirac fermion is

\mathcal{S}=\int dtdx\bar{\psi}i\gamma^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}\psi\,,

(3.5)

where
\bar{\psi}=\psi^{\dagger}\gamma^{0}
and we choose
\gamma^{0}=\sigma_{1},\gamma^{1}=\sigma_{2}
. This theory has a global
U(1)
symmetry:
\psi\to e^{i\alpha}\psi,\bar{\psi}\to e^{-i\alpha}\bar{\psi}
. The corresponding conserved charge is
Q=\int dx\psi^{\dagger}\psi
.

In CFT, SREE is computed using fluxed twist fields
\mathcal{T}_{n,\mu}
, which unify the role of twist operator and flux insertion. The conventional twist field creates an
n
-sheeted Riemann surface for Rényi entropy, and a flux insertion introduces a phase factor
e^{i\mu Q_{A}}
to resolve symmetry sectors.

The charged moments are expressed as

Z_{n}(\alpha)={\textrm{Tr}}\left(\rho_{A}^{n}e^{i\alpha Q_{A}}\right)=\left\langle\mathcal{T}_{n,\alpha}(u)\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n,\alpha}(v)\right\rangle\,,

(3.6)

where
u,v
mark the endpoints of subsystem
A=[u,v]
.

In the free Dirac fermion CFT, we can compute SREE using the fluxed twist field method. This method leverages the replica trick and the bosonization technique to rewrite the computation in terms of correlation functions of special vertex operators. Below, we outline the key steps in this approach.

To compute the charged moments, we use the replica trick, which reformulates the computation as a partition function on an fluxed
n
-sheeted Riemann surface. Instead of working directly on this complex topology, we equivalently introduce
n
replicated copies of the Dirac field

\Psi=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{1}\\
\psi_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\psi_{n}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(3.7)

where each
\psi_{j}
corresponds to a field living on the
j
-th replica sheet.

The key observation is that the topology of the fluxed Riemann surface can be encoded in the boundary conditions imposed at the entangling points (i.e., the edges of the subsystem). These boundary conditions are enforced by inserting fluxed twist field operators
\mathcal{T}_{n,\alpha}
and fluxed anti-twist field operators
\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n,\alpha}
, which cyclically permute the replica fields while adding a phase
e^{i\alpha/n}
related to the flux.

When the field
\psi_{j}
is transported around the twist field insertion at
u
, it transforms as

\psi_{j}\to e^{i\alpha/n}\psi_{j+1}\,,

(3.8)

while the fluxed anti-twist field
\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n,\alpha}
acts as the inverse transformation

\psi_{j}\to e^{-i\alpha/n}\psi_{j-1}\,.

(3.9)

The transformation (
3.8
) is encoded in a twist matrix

T_{\alpha}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-e^{i\alpha/n}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&-e^{i\alpha/n}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
e^{i\alpha/n}&0&0&\cdots&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

(3.10)

To decouple the replicas, we diagonalize this transformation by defining a new basis of fields

\tilde{\psi}_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{-i(2\pi kj/n-\pi j)}\psi_{j},\quad k=-\frac{n-1}{2}\,,\dots,\frac{n-1}{2}\,,

(3.11)

where the new fields
\tilde{\psi}_{k}
transform independently under the twist operator

T_{\alpha}\tilde{\psi}_{k}=e^{i(\alpha+2\pi k)/n}\tilde{\psi}_{k}\,.

(3.12)

Since each mode
\tilde{\psi}_{k}
is now independent,
\mathcal{T}_{n,\alpha}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n,\alpha}
can be decomposed into
n
different twists
acting independently on each replica

\mathcal{T}_{n,\alpha}=\prod_{k=-\frac{n-1}{2}}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\mathcal{T}_{n,k,\alpha}\,,\qquad\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n,\alpha}=\prod_{k=-\frac{n-1}{2}}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n,k,\alpha}\,.

(3.13)

The partition function also factorizes, allowing us to express the charged moments as a product

Z_{n}(\alpha)=\prod_{k=-\frac{n-1}{2}}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\left\langle\mathcal{T}_{n,k,\alpha}(u)\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n,k,\alpha}(v)\right\rangle\equiv\prod_{k}Z_{k}(\alpha)\,,

(3.14)

where each factor
Z_{k}(\alpha)
corresponds to a correlation function of twist fields for a single decoupled mode.

As shown above, by diagonalizing the matrix
T_{\alpha}
through a unitary transformation, we obtain
n
decoupled fields
\tilde{\psi}_{k}
, each associated with an eigenvalue
e^{i(\alpha+2\pi k)/n}
. These fields satisfy the twisted boundary conditions

\tilde{\psi}_{k}(e^{2\pi i}u)=e^{i(\alpha/2\pi+k)/n}\tilde{\psi}_{k}(u)\,,\qquad\tilde{\psi}_{k}(e^{2\pi i}v)=e^{-i(\alpha/2\pi+k)/n}\tilde{\psi}_{k}(v)\,.

(3.15)

Instead of dealing with these multivalued fields directly, we introduce an external gauge field
A_{\mu}^{k}
, which enforces the same boundary conditions while allowing
\tilde{\psi}_{k}(x)
to remain single-valued. Thus the Lagrangian density is

\mathcal{L}_{k}=\bar{\tilde{\psi}}_{k}\gamma^{\mu}(\partial_{\mu}+iA_{\mu}^{k})\tilde{\psi}_{k}\,.

(3.16)

The gauge field
A_{\mu}^{k}
is a pure gauge everywhere except at the points
u
and
v
, where it has a vortex-like singularity. The flux through any contour surrounding a branch point is given by

\oint_{C_{u}}dx^{\mu}A^{k}_{\mu}=-\frac{2\pi k+\alpha}{n}\,,\quad\oint_{C_{v}}dx^{\mu}A^{k}_{\mu}=\frac{2\pi k+\alpha}{n}\,.

(3.17)

These conditions imply that
A_{\mu}^{k}
satisfies the equation

\epsilon^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\nu}A_{\mu}^{k}(x)=\frac{2\pi k+\alpha}{n}\left[\delta(x-u)-\delta(x-v)\right]\,.

(3.18)

With these transformations, the original partition function factorizes into a product over independent modes

Z_{n}(\alpha)=\prod_{k=-\frac{n-1}{2}}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}Z_{k}(\alpha)\,.

(3.19)

Each
Z_{k}
can now be rewritten in terms of a vacuum expectation value in the presence of the external gauge field as

Z_{k}(\alpha)=\left\langle e^{i\int A_{\mu}^{k}j^{\mu}_{k}d^{2}x}\right\rangle\,.

(3.20)

3.3
Bosonization

Bosonization is a powerful technique in 1+1D quantum field theory that maps fermionic systems to bosonic fields, making certain calculations more tractable. In the case of free Dirac fermions, the bosonization procedure allows us to express charged moments
Z_{n}(\alpha)
in terms of correlation functions of vertex operators in a free bosonic theory. The fermion current
j_{k}^{\mu}
, which generates the global
U(1)
symmetry associated with charge conservation, is mapped to the bosonic field as

j_{k}^{\mu}=\bar{\tilde{\psi}}_{k}\gamma^{\mu}\tilde{\psi}_{k}\quad\longrightarrow\quad\frac{1}{2\pi}\epsilon^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\nu}\phi_{k}\,.

(3.21)

The free Dirac fermion
\tilde{\psi}_{k}
is mapped to a free compact boson field
\phi_{k}(x)
with the action

S=\frac{1}{8\pi}\int d^{2}x\,(\partial_{\mu}\phi_{k})^{2}\,.

(3.22)

Then using Eq. (
3.18
) and integrating by part, the right-hand-side of Eq. (
3.20
) becomes correlation function of vertex operators

Z_{k}(\alpha)=\left\langle e^{i\frac{k+\alpha/2\pi}{n}\phi_{k}(u)}e^{-i\frac{k+\alpha/2\pi}{n}\phi_{k}(v)}\right\rangle\,.

(3.23)

Comparing the equation above with Eq. (
3.14
), one can identify

\mathcal{T}_{n,k,\alpha}=e^{i\frac{k+\alpha/2\pi}{n}\phi_{k}}=V_{a,a}\,,\qquad\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n,k,\alpha}=e^{-i\frac{k+\alpha/2\pi}{n}\phi_{k}}=V_{-a,-a}\,.

(3.24)

Recall that the conformal dimensions of vertex operator
V_{a,\bar{a}}\equiv e^{ia\varphi+i\bar{a}\bar{\varphi}}
are
(h,\bar{h})=(\frac{1}{2}a^{2},\frac{1}{2}\bar{a}^{2})
. Here
\varphi,\bar{\varphi}
are the holomorphic and anti-holomorphic part of the scalar field
\phi
. Therefore, the conformal dimensions of the fluxed twist fields in Eq. (
3.13
) are the same,

h_{n,\alpha}=\bar{h}_{n,\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=-\frac{n-1}{2}}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\left(\frac{k}{n}+\frac{\alpha}{2\pi n}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{24}\left(n-\frac{1}{n}\right)+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{8\pi^{2}n}\,.

(3.25)

4
Inhomogeneous quench dynamics from fluxed twist fields

In this section, we will compute the time evolution of SREE after a quench from uniform to non-uniform CFT
[
21
]
. Specificly, we will consider a critical free-fermion chain of length
L
with open boundary conditions, initially prepared in its ground state, i.e. the ground state of the free Dirac fermion CFT (the uniform one). At
t=0
, we evolve the system according to the Hamiltonian of the sine-square deformed CFT, which is certainly a non-uniform one.

4.1
Time evolution under SSD Hamiltonian

The initial state is the ground state
\ket{\psi_0}
of a uniform CFT, defined on a finite spatial interval
[0,L]
with open boundary conditions. The subsystem
A
is
[0,l]
. We choose open boundaries because, as highlighted in Section
2
, a periodic boundary condition yields a ground state identical to that of the SSD system, leading trivial dynamics. At time
t=0
, we abruptly switch the Hamiltonian to the Möbius Hamiltonian
H_{1}(\theta)
. In the Schrödinger picture, the state evolves as
\ket{\psi(t)}=e^{-iH_{1}(\theta)t}\ket{\psi_0}
and the correlation functions evolves as
\bra{\psi_0}e^{iH_{1}t}O(x_{1})\cdots O(x_{n})e^{-iH_{1}t}\ket{\psi_0}
, where
O(x_{i})
are operators inserted at spatial points
x_{i}
. Then the charged moments of the reduced density matrix
\rho_{A}
at time
t
are

Z_{n}(\alpha,t)=\bra{\psi_0}e^{iH_{1}(\theta)t}\mathcal{T}_{n,\alpha}(w_{0},\bar{w}_{0})e^{-iH_{1}(\theta)t}\ket{\psi_0}\,.

(4.1)

The system has conformal boundary conditions imposed at
\sigma=0
and
\sigma=L
, which are assumed to be identical here for simplicity.
Here we introduce the complex coordinate
w=\tau+i\sigma
where
-\infty<\tau<\infty
and
0\leq\sigma\leq L
, and
w_{0}=il
marks the fluxed twist operator’s position.

It’s convenient to shift to Euclidean spacetime by setting
it=\tau
, transforming the charged moments Eq. (
4.1
) into

Z_{n}(\alpha,\tau)=\bra{\psi_0}e^{H_{1}(\theta)\tau}\mathcal{T}_{n,\alpha}(w_{0},\bar{w}_{0})e^{-H_{1}(\theta)\tau}\ket{\psi_0}\,.

(4.2)

Rather than evolving the state in the Schrödinger picture, we turn to the Heisenberg picture and evolve the operator
\mathcal{T}_{n,\alpha}
under
H_{1}(\theta)
. It turns out that a suitable conformal transformation simplifies this evolution, making it tractable. The Möbius Hamiltonian
H_{1}(\theta)
can be viewed as a regularized version of
H_{1}
that avoids potential infrared (IR) divergences tied to the SSD’s continuous spectrum. We first consider the Möbius Hamiltonian
H_{1}(\theta)
, then take the limit
\theta\to\infty
to recover the SSD case.

The Möbius Hamiltonian in the
w
-plane, expressed via the stress-energy tensor, is

H_{1}(\theta)=H_{0}-\frac{\tanh(2 \theta)}{2}(H_{+}+H_{-})\,,

(4.3)

where

H_{0}=\int_{0}^{L}\frac{d\sigma}{2\pi}T_{\tau\tau}(\sigma)=\int_{0}^{L}\frac{d\sigma}{2\pi}[T(w)+\bar{T}(\bar{w})]\,,

(4.4)

and

H_{\pm}=\int_{0}^{L}\frac{d\sigma}{2\pi}\left[e^{\pm 2\pi w/L}T(w)+e^{\mp 2\pi\bar{w}/L}\bar{T}(\bar{w})\right]\,.

(4.5)

In the limit
\theta\to\infty
, this reduces to the SSD Hamiltonian
H_{1}=H_{0}-\frac{1}{2}(H_{+}+H_{-})
.

Then we map the strip in the
w
-plane to the whole complex plane via
z=e^{2\pi w/L}
. In the
z
-plane, the Möbius Hamiltonian
H_{1}(\theta)
becomes

\begin{split}H_{1}^{(z)}(\theta)=&\frac{2\pi}{L\cosh(2\theta)}\left[\cosh(2\theta)\oint\frac{zdz}{2\pi i}T(z)-\frac{\sinh(2\theta)}{2}\oint\frac{z^{2}+1}{2\pi i}T(z)dz\right]\\
&+\frac{2\pi}{L\cosh(2\theta)}\left[\cosh(2\theta)\oint\frac{\bar{z}d\bar{z}}{2\pi i}\bar{T}(\bar{z})-\frac{\sinh(2\theta)}{2}\oint\frac{\bar{z}^{2}+1}{2\pi i}\bar{T}(\bar{z})d\bar{z}\right]-\frac{\pi c}{6L}\,,\end{split}

(4.6)

where
c=1
for Dirac fermion CFT. This form remains complicated, so we apply a further Möbius transformation to the
\zeta
-plane (see also Eq. (
2.8
)

\zeta(z)=-\frac{z\cosh\theta-\sinh\theta}{z\sinh\theta-\cosh\theta}\,,

(4.7)

simplifying the Hamiltonian to

H_{1}^{(\zeta)}(\theta)=\frac{2\pi}{L\cosh(2\theta)}(L_{0}^{(\zeta)}+\bar{L}_{0}^{(\zeta)})-\frac{\pi c}{6L}\,,

(4.8)

where
L_{0}^{(\zeta)}
is the zero-mode Virasoro generator in the
\zeta
-plane. From the equation above, it follows that in the
\zeta
-plane, the time evolution acts as a dilatation,

e^{H_{1}^{(\zeta)}(\theta)\tau}\mathcal{T}_{n,\alpha}^{(\zeta)}(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})e^{-H_{1}^{(\zeta)}(\theta)\tau}=\lambda^{2h_{n,\alpha}}\mathcal{T}_{n,\alpha}^{(\zeta)}(\lambda\zeta,\lambda\bar{\zeta})\,,

(4.9)

with
\lambda=\exp\left(\frac{2\pi\tau}{L\cosh(2\theta)}\right)
.
Mapping back to the
z
-plane, this corresponds to shift the location of
\mathcal{T}_{n,\alpha}^{(z)}
from
z
to
z^{\prime}
, where
z^{\prime}
is determined through the equation
\zeta(z^{\prime})=\lambda\zeta(z)
. Solving this equation, one obtains

z^{\prime}=\frac{[(1-\lambda)\cosh(2\theta)-(\lambda+1)]z+(\lambda-1)\sinh(2\theta)}{(1-\lambda)\sinh(2\theta)z+[(\lambda-1)\cosh(2\theta)-(\lambda+1)]}\,.

(4.10)

After a series of conformal mappings described above, the computation of the charged moments are equivalent to evaluate the one-point function of the fluxed twist field on the
z
-plane

\langle\psi_{0}|e^{H_{1}(\theta)\tau}\mathcal{T}_{n,\alpha}^{(w)}(w_{0},\bar{w}_{0})e^{-H_{1}(\theta)\tau}|\psi_{0}\rangle=\left(\frac{\partial z}{\partial w}\frac{\partial\bar{z}}{\partial\bar{w}}\frac{\partial z^{\prime}}{\partial z}\frac{\partial\bar{z}^{\prime}}{\partial\bar{z}}\Big|_{w=w_{0}}\right)^{h_{n,\alpha}}\langle\mathcal{T}_{n,\alpha}^{(z)}(z^{\prime},\bar{z}^{\prime})\rangle\,.

(4.11)

Then we need to compute the one-point function
\langle\mathcal{T}_{n,\alpha}^{(z)}(z,\bar{z})\rangle
in the
z
-plane with a slit along the real axis from

4.4
Late time behavior

The right panel of Fig.
1
illustrates the time evolution of the SREE and the von Neumann entropy. Notably, these quantities exhibit no revivals despite the finite size of the system. This behavior contrasts with the uniform case, where entanglement revivals arise from quasiparticle reflections at the boundaries. The absence of revivals suggests that sine-square deformation effectively suppresses boundary effects in finite systems. A quasiparticle picture provides insight into this phenomenon, at least for the von Neumann entropy
[
21
]
. Specifically, the local group velocity of quasiparticles, given by

, implying an infinitely long reflection time at the boundaries. Given that the long-time behavior of the SREE is governed by the leading-order contribution of the von Neumann entropy, the suppression of revivals is expected to extend to the SREE as well.

We next analyze the late-time dynamics of the SREE. In the long-time limit

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we investigate the dynamics of symmetry-resolved entanglement entropy (SREE) in a finite system following an inhomogeneous quench—specifically, the Möbius/SSD quench—using both analytical and numerical approaches. Our analytical treatment employs techniques from 2D CFT, leveraging the solvability of the Möbius deformation. Meanwhile, our exact numerical method is broadly applicable to SREE computations in other inhomogeneous quench problems.

Key findings include:

(a) Equipartition of entanglement: At leading order in the large-
L
limit, the entanglement entropy exhibits equipartition among symmetry sectors, while subleading corrections break this behavior.

(b) Unbounded logarithmic growth: At late times, the SREE grows as

\log t
without saturation, indicating the suppression of boundary effects.

These results suggest intriguing future directions, such as studying SREE dynamics in other quench protocols within the Möbius deformation framework
[
18
,
20
]
.

Appendix B
Time evolution of fermionic correlation function

To derive the time evolution of the fermionic two-point correlation function for the given Hamiltonian
H=\sum_{m,n}h_{mn}c_{m}^{\dagger}c_{n}
. Here
h
is a real matrix. The hermiticity of the Hamiltonian further requires
h=h^{T}
. We start by considering the Heisenberg equations of motion for the fermionic operators
c_{m}^{\dagger}(t)
and
c_{n}(t)
. For
c_{m}^{\dagger}(t)
, we have

\frac{d}{dt}c_{m}^{\dagger}(t)=i[H,c_{m}^{\dagger}(t)]=i\sum_{p}h_{pm}c_{p}^{\dagger}(t)\,,

(B.1)

and similar equation hold for
c_{n}(t)
as

\frac{d}{dt}c_{n}(t)=i[H,c_{n}(t)]=-i\sum_{q}h_{nq}c_{q}(t)\,.

(B.2)

The fermionic two-point correlation function is
C_{mn}(t)=\langle c_{m}^{\dagger}(t)c_{n}(t)\rangle
. Using the product rule, its time derivative is

\frac{d}{dt}C_{mn}(t)=\left\langle\frac{dc_{m}^{\dagger}(t)}{dt}c_{n}(t)\right\rangle+\left\langle c_{m}^{\dagger}(t)\frac{dc_{n}(t)}{dt}\right\rangle\,.

(B.3)

Substituting the Heisenberg equations of motion, we have

\frac{d}{dt}C_{mn}(t)=i\sum_{p}h_{pm}\langle c_{p}^{\dagger}(t)c_{n}(t)\rangle-i\sum_{q}h_{nq}\langle c_{m}^{\dagger}(t)c_{q}(t)\rangle\,.

(B.4)

Recognizing that
\langle c_{p}^{\dagger}c_{n}\rangle=C_{pn}
and
\langle c_{m}^{\dagger}c_{q}\rangle=C_{mq}
, we rewrite the time derivative as

\frac{d}{dt}C_{mn}(t)=i\sum_{p}(h_{pm}C_{pn}(t)-h_{nq}C_{mq}(t))\,.

(B.5)

This can be expressed in matrix form as

\frac{d}{dt}C(t)=i[h,C(t)]\,,

(B.6)

where we have used the fact that
h
is a symmetric matrix. The solution of this differential equation is given by

C(t)=e^{iht}\cdot C(0)\cdot e^{-iht}\,.

(B.7)
