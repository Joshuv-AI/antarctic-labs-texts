Obesity and Cardiovascular Disease: An Obesity Medicine Association (OMA) Clinical Practice Statement (CPS) 2026
Authors: Harold Edward Bays, Catherine Varney, Lydia Alexander, Jennifer Ng, Justin Tondt, Christopher Weber, Sandra Christensen, Karlijn Burridge, Carolynn Francavilla, Shagun Bindlish, Bharti Shetye, Marisa Censani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObesity is a chronic disease that directly contributes to cardiovascular disease (CVD) through biomechanical “fat mass disease” and dysfunction of epicardial adipose tissue, or “sick fat disease.” It also indirectly promotes CVD through adiposopathic immune and endocrine abnormalities that drive type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and thrombosis – all major CVD risk factors. This Clinical Practice Statement (CPS) reviews obesity-related cardiovascular disease pathology and outlines evidence-based strategies for clinical management.MethodsThis CPS is based on a review of relevant medical literature, interpreted through the clinical expertise of authors specializing in obesity medicine, all members of the Obesity Medicine Association (OMA). Organized into 26 sections, each includes an introduction, essential principles, a “top ten” list of key points, and a summary figure.ResultsThe CPS reviews how obesity intersects with major cardiovascular risk factors, including type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, dyslipidemia, metabolic syndrome, and thrombosis. Additional metabolic complications such as metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and chronic kidney disease further elevate CVD risk. The CPS details obesity's impact on cardiac structure and function, including its role in heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), atrial fibrillation, and CVD considerations in patients with obstructive sleep apnea, cancer, and psychiatric disease. It also addresses the “obesity paradox,” social determinants of health, pediatric considerations, and disparities by age, sex, and race. Also included are CVD diagnostic challenges and comprehensive management strategies, including medical nutrition therapy, physical activity, behavioral intervention, cardiometabolic medications, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists, and bariatric procedures.ConclusionEffective reduction of obesity-related CVD risk requires shifting from treating downstream risk factors to addressing excessive adipose tissue itself, along with improving adipose tissue function. Clinically, this approach is often manifest by a “treat obesity first” approach. Precision diagnostics, lifestyle therapy, obesity medications, and bariatric procedures can modify both adiposopathy (“sick fat disease”) and fat mass disease, improving long-term cardiovascular outcomes.

Ten things to know about obesity, epidemiology, and cardiovascular disease (CVD) [1].

Obesity, Epidemiology, and Cardiovascular Disease (CVD). Global obesity rates have climbed sharply, doubling in 73 countries since 1980 and contributing to millions of deaths each year—most from cardiovascular disease. In the United States, over 40% of adults have obesity, independently accelerating cardiovascular risk beyond traditional metabolic complications. Commercial, social, and environmental forces are major drivers of this epidemic and its downstream CVD burden. Meaningful reductions in obesity-related cardiovascular disease require a comprehensive, equity-focused, system-level, team-based approach. Sustained weight reduction lowers CVD risk, and the most effective and equitable outcomes arise from coordinated, optimized, multidisciplinary care. Although a higher body mass index (BMI) correlates with cardiovascular risk at the population level, increased visceral adipose tissue accumulation is a more accurate indicator of pathogenic adipose tissue distribution and a stronger predictor of individual cardiometabolic complications and cardiovascular disease risk.

Between 1980 and 2015, the prevalence of obesity doubled in more than 70 countries and continued to increase in most other countries. In 2015, an estimated 603.7 million adults and 107.7 million children worldwide were living with obesity. This surge in increased adiposity is linked to 4.0 million deaths annually, over two-thirds being the result of cardiovascular disease (CVD). In the United States (August 2021–August 2023), the prevalence of obesity was above 40%, with severe obesity in adults at 9.4% (higher in women than men in each age group); overall obesity prevalence has plateaued while severe obesity continues to rise [2]. Beyond its contribution to risk factors like type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), hypertension, and dyslipidemia (as well as thrombosis, sleep apnea, and fatty infiltration of body organs – including fat infiltration of the heart) obesity is an independent driver of CVD development and mortality. Childhood and adolescent obesity trends suggest a continued rise in future global CVD risk. Essential principles include:

While the main function of body fat is to store excess energy, adipocytes and adipose tissue are active endocrine and immune tissues whose function and dysfunction have implications regarding cardiovascular disease (CVD).[3,43]2. Increased adipocyte hypertrophy and visceral adipose tissue accumulation can cause adipose tissue to become “sick” or become diseased or dysfunctional. Adiposopathy is analogous to the “pathies” of other body organs (e.g., myopathy, encephalopathy, ophthalmopathy, retinopathy, enteropathy, nephropathy, neuropathy, and dermopathy).[3,8]3. Cardiomyopathy describes a “disease” wherein pathologic enlargement of heart cells and the heart organ results in anatomic/functional abnormalities leading to adverse clinical consequences. Similarly, adiposopathy describes a “disease” wherein pathogenic enlargement of fat cells and the adipose tissue organ result in anatomic/functional abnormalities leading to adverse clinical consequences.[3,8]4. “Fat Mass Disease” reflects the biomechanical and structural burden of excess adiposity, driving conditions such as obstructive sleep apnea, diastolic dysfunction, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), and venous thrombosis through direct mechanical compromise of airway, cardiac, and vascular function. These complications are further amplified by obesity-related physical inactivity, which worsens cardiopulmonary loading, impairs venous return, and accelerates the adverse downstream consequences of excess fat mass.[3,8]5. The adverse “sick fat” adiposopathic consequences of obesity often contribute to endocrine and immune dysfunction leading to insulin resistance and development of major cardiovascular risk factors including type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and dyslipidemia. These pathologic adipose-tissue signals also promote a pro-thrombotic state, further amplifying cardiometabolic risk.[3,8]6. Illustrative adiposopathic endocrine abnormalities include hypoadiponectinemia, hyperleptinemia, excess circulating free-fatty-acids, and dysregulated adipose-derived renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) activity, which collectively, can drive insulin resistance and major cardiometabolic risk factors (e.g., type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and dyslipidemia). These maladaptive hormonal signals may contribute to “ectopic fat deposition,” endothelial dysfunction, and pro-thrombotic adipokine patterns, thereby increasing the risk of CVD.[3,8]7. Illustrative adiposopathy immune abnormalities include macrophage infiltration of adipose tissue, increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and impaired resolution of inflammation. The increased proinflammatory response coupled with reduced anti-inflammatory response promotes insulin resistance and cardiometabolic dysfunction (e.g., type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and dyslipidemia). These immune-mediated disturbances also contribute to endothelial injury and a pro-thrombotic state, accelerating the development of CVD.[1,3,44]8. Treatment of the consequences of obesity must not only reduce adiposity but also improve adiposopathy.[3,8]9. Obesity-related adiposopathy and the accumulation of adipose tissue in the visceral region, as well as ectopic fat accumulation in organs such as liver, skeletal muscle, pancreas, and kidneys drive insulin resistance, inflammation, lipotoxicity, and metabolic dysfunction, key mechanisms that accelerate the development of cardiovascular disease. These dysfunctional fat depots act not merely as markers of disease but as active contributors to atherogenesis, cardiomyopathy, and vascular injury.[3,45]10. Adiposopathy can drive inflammation, insulin resistance, and ectopic lipid spillover to other body areas. Epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) is the fat depot in contact with the myocardium. EAT is a potential local mediator of cardiovascular injury. Dysfunctional epicardial adipose tissue releases pro-inflammatory and pro-atherogenic factors that accelerate coronary heart disease and other CVD, making it a mechanistic etiological bridge between systemic and local CVD progression.[3,46]

Obesity, Sick Fat Disease (Adiposopathy), Fat Mass Disease, and Cardiovascular Disease (CVD). Obesity promotes CVD through two complementary mechanisms: the mechanical effects of excess fat mass (“fat mass disease”) and the metabolic–inflammatory dysfunction of adipose tissue (“sick fat disease”). Fat mass disease increases cardiac workload and impairs cardiopulmonary function, contributing to left-ventricular hypertrophy, diastolic dysfunction, sleep apnea, and reduced physical activity. Adiposopathy (“sick fat disease”) arises when hypertrophic, dysfunctional adipocytes trigger hormonal and immune disturbances that elevate glucose, blood pressure, atherogenic lipids, and thrombosis risk, collectively accelerating the risk of CVD.

Obesity can contribute to CVD via “fat mass disease.” Obesity can also contribute to CVD via adiposopathy or “sick fat disease.” Regarding “fat mass disease,” the biomechanical accumulation of adipose tissue may increase blood volume and cardiac contraction (due to increased adipose tissue blood supply), resulting in increase in heart size (i.e., left ventricular hypertrophy), cardiac dilation, and limitations on post contraction refill (i.e., “diastolic heart failure”), all contributing to heart failure with preserved ejection fraction. Fat mass disease can also contribute to obstructive sleep apnea, physical inactivity, and arterial vascular compression. Adiposopathy or “sick fat” arises when, unlike healthy adipose tissue that serves as a safe energy reservoir and endocrine/immune regulator, fat cells (adipocytes) undergo excessive enlargement (hypertrophy) with subsequent limitations on further energy storage in peripheral subcutaneous adipose tissue, thus resulting in visceral adipose tissue accumulation. The ensuing endocrinopathies, immunopathies, and free fatty acid release promote insulin resistance, high blood glucose, high blood pressure, and elevated atherogenic blood lipids, as well as increased risk of thrombosis. Ultimately, the adiposopathic consequences of obesity may lead to an array of CVD. Essential principles include:

•Obesity contributes to CVD in two main ways: through excess fat mass (“fat mass disease”) and through dysfunctional fat biology (“sick fat disease”).

Non-adipose tissues help to determine the degree that obesity leads to glucose dysregulation and cardiometabolic disease, including CVD. Ectopic fat delivery to non-adipose tissue can result in either physiological or pathophysiological responses that can either help mitigate or facilitate vascular injury and cardiac dysfunction.[3,4,45]2. Healthy expansion of peripheral subcutaneous adipose tissue can buffer excess energy and prevent harmful ectopic fat deposition. By limiting lipid overflow into the heart, vessels, and liver, this protective mechanism reduces cardiovascular disease risk despite increased fat mass.[3,4,45]3. When adipocyte proliferation or differentiation in peripheral subcutaneous adipose tissue is impaired, then adipocytes may become enlarged (i.e., hypertrophied) and dysfunctional with limitations in further energy storage. The ensuing energy spillover drives ectopic fat deposition in cardiovascular-relevant organs, promoting inflammation, endothelial injury, and atherosclerosis.[3,4,45]4. Ectopic fat accumulation in the liver and muscle can cause “lipotoxicity,” which can directly impair organ function, resulting in insulin resistance. One illustrative adverse consequence is the accumulation of ceramides, which are sphingolipids that accumulate when tissues are exposed to excess fatty acids. Ceramides can act as key mediators of lipotoxicity by blocking insulin signaling, damaging mitochondria, and promoting inflammation. The end result of lipotoxicity is disruption of the function of the liver, muscle, pancreas, and heart, contributing to insulin resistance and CVD risk factors and CVD. For example, in the cardiovascular system, ectopic fat in epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) can contribute to lipotoxicity, myocardial steatosis, diastolic dysfunction, dysrhythmogenic remodeling, and accelerated atherosclerotic disease. Pancreatic fat can contribute to beta cell dysfunction and diminished insulin secretion, increasing the risk of onset of diabetes mellitus, a major CVD risk factor. Renal fat may activate the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), increasing the risk of onset of hypertension, a major CVD risk factor.[3,4,47]5. Prediabetes, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia arise from multi-organ insulin resistance, which is worsened when ectopic fat infiltrates organs such as the liver, muscle and pancreas. Insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidemia are CVD risk factors.[3,4,48]6. Age, sex, race, genetics, medications, and comorbidities may potentially influence whether excess energy (caloric intake beyond energy absorbed and expended) is stored safely in adipose tissue or diverted into ectopic depots. These same factors therefore shape susceptibility to CVD driven by ectopic fat. For example, aging compromises the storage capacity of peripheral adipose tissue, contributing to a ‘lipid spillover’ effect that shifts fat accumulation toward visceral and ectopic sites. Compared to White individuals and males, Black individuals and females tend to sequester excess energy in subcutaneous adipose tissue, resulting in lower visceral fat mass for any given body mass index. Individuals of Asian descent typically exhibit a limited capacity for subcutaneous adipose tissue expansion, causing excess lipid to overflow into visceral adipose tissue depots at a lower Body Mass Index (BMI) compared to other populations. Thiazolidinediones (TZDs) activate PPAR-gamma which promotes the creation of a “metabolic sink” where energy storage is enhanced via the proliferation and differentiation of more healthy, insulin-sensitive subcutaneous adipose tissue - which in turn - helps mitigate the otherwise harmful ectopic fat deposition such as energy spillover into visceral adipose tissue depots.[3,4,45]7. During weight-reduction interventions, peripheral subcutaneous adipose tissue (by far the largest fat depot) typically shows the greatest absolute reduction, increasing the potential for energy storage, which helps reduce lipid spillover. The greater capacity for energy storage via weight reduction reduces the substrate available for ectopic fat deposition, thereby lowering CVD risk.[3,4,40]8. Weight reduction improves the function of multiple organs, but the degree of improvement varies widely among individuals. These variable responses influence how effectively ectopic fat is cleared from organs, thereby modifying CVD risk.[3,4,49]9. Adipose-tissue and hepatic insulin sensitivity often improve with modest weight reduction, reducing systemic lipid spillover to other body organs and reducing hepatic secretion of atherogenic triglyceride-rich lipoproteins, both which may slow progression of CVD.[3,4,48]10. Obesity promotes ectopic fat deposition within epicardial adipose tissue (EAT), creating a metabolically active reservoir that releases pro-inflammatory cytokines, free fatty acids, and oxidative stress mediators that directly injure adjacent coronary vessels and myocardium. This inflammatory and lipotoxic microenvironment accelerates atherosclerosis, impairs vascular function, and increases the overall risk of CVD.[3,4,46]

Obesity, “Ectopic Fat” (e.g., Visceral, Cardiac, Hepatic, Muscle, Pancreas, Kidney) and Cardiovascular Disease (CVD). Obesity leads to CVD not simply through excess body weight, but through limits in adipose tissue expandability in peripheral subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT), triggering both adiposopathy (i.e., resulting in adipocyte and adipose tissue endocrinopathies and immunopathies/inflammation) and energy overflow. During limitations in SAT energy storage, free fatty acids spill into the circulation and deposit as ectopic fat in visceral adipose tissue depots and within organs such as the liver, muscle, heart, and pancreas. In addition to adiposopathic immunopathies and endocrinopathies, the “ectopic fat” infiltrations of non-adipose tissue organs may result in lipotoxicity, insulin resistance, worsening cardiometabolic risk factors, and ultimately, CVD (i.e., atherosclerosis and myocardial damage).

The progression from obesity to adverse health outcomes is best understood not just by an assessment of body weight, but by body composition and the concept of energy overflow and adiposopathy (dysfunctional “sick” adipose tissue). When peripheral subcutaneous adipose tissue stores reach their expansion limit or become inflamed, then they fail to safely sequester additional or excess energy. This failure forces an energy metabolic “spillover,” resulting in increased circulating free fatty acids and the pathogenic accumulation of “ectopic fat” in areas other than peripheral subcutaneous adipose tissue, such as visceral depots (abdominal and cardiac), the liver, skeletal muscle, pancreas, and kidneys. This ectopic deposition induces organ-specific toxicity (lipotoxicity) and systemic insulin resistance, serving as a driver of atherosclerosis and structural heart changes that increase the risk of CVD. Excess adiposity is a dose-dependent risk factor for type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), raising the risk roughly three-fold for those who are overweight and sevenfold or more among those with obesity. T2DM is a major cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk factor. Higher body weight further increases both these risks. For most non-pregnant adults with T2DM, a hemoglobin A1c goal is <7.0%, with an even lower goal of <6.5% among select patients with diabetes mellitus and obesity who can achieve it safely through evidence-based weight reduction interventions (e.g., healthful nutrition, physical activity, behavior modification, obesity medications, and/or bariatric procedures).[4,50,51]2. About 40% of US adults have obesity; over 70% of US adults have overweight or obesity; about 25% of patients with obesity have T2DM; about 80–90% of adults with T2DM have overweight or obesity.[4,52]3. The adiposopathic effects of increased body fat are not the only causes of hyperglycemia. Other potential causes of hyperglycemia include hemochromatosis, hypercortisolism, excessive growth hormone, pancreatic insufficiency due to pancreatitis or surgical excision, side effects of concomitant therapies, genetic syndromes of insulin resistance, and genetic syndromes of limited pancreatic insulin secretion.[3,4]4. Adipocyte hypertrophy and visceral adipose tissue accumulation is reflective of an anatomic body composition pattern that results in endocrinopathies and immunopathies that directly contribute to glycemic-related CVD and indirectly contribute to CVD risk factors such as insulin resistance, hyperinsulinemia, and hyperglycemia.[3,4]5. Both obesity and T2DM are CVD risk factors that contribute to atherosclerotic coronary heart disease, endothelial dysfunction, microvascular disease, and left ventricular dysfunction (i.e., heart failure with preserved ejection fraction). An important unifying pathogenic mechanism among many patients with overweight or obesity is insulin resistance, which often leads to compensatory hyperinsulinemia. In addition to being a metabolic hormone, insulin has growth factor activities such that hyperinsulinemia promotes cardiovascular disease through pathway-selective resistance. While the metabolic signaling pathway (Phosphoinositide 3-Kinase or PI3K) is impaired with hyperinsulinemia - causing endothelial dysfunction and reduced nitric oxide - the growth-promoting pathway (Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase or MAPK) remains sensitive to the high insulin levels. Consequently, hyperinsulinemia drives excessive anabolic signaling via MAPK, triggering inflammation and pathological cardiac hypertrophy, effectively acting as a potent, independent risk factor for heart and vascular disease.[4,53]6. Hyperglycemia develops when pancreatic beta-cell hyperinsulinemia can no longer compensate for insulin resistance. Hyperglycemia itself can drive cardiovascular disease through mitochondrial superoxide overproduction that activates four major injurious pathways: increased flux through the polyol pathway with depletion of antioxidant defenses, heightened hexosamine pathway activity, accelerated formation of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs), and activation of protein kinase C (PKC). Together, these mechanisms produce endothelial dysfunction, vascular inflammation, vasoconstriction, impaired nitric oxide signaling, and a pro-thrombotic state that promotes adverse CVD consequences such as atherosclerosis, cardiomyopathy, microvascular dysfunction, and even autonomic cardiac dysregulation potentially resulting in resting tachycardia, silent ischemia, orthostatic hypotension, and cardiac dysrhythmias.[4,54]7. While from a population standpoint, central obesity (increased waist circumference) is associated with both increased risk of T2DM and CVD, from an individual standpoint, an increase in waist circumference has variable correlation to an increase in visceral adiposity, especially upon consideration of age (i.e., older individuals have increased visceral adiposity), sex (i.e., females have less visceral adiposity than males), and race (i.e., Black individuals generally have decreased visceral adiposity while those of Asian descent may have increased visceral adiposity).[4,55]8. Hyperglycemia is one component of the metabolic syndrome (MetSyn), with MetSyn being a constellation of CVD risk factors. MetSyn includes central adiposity (as the only anatomic physical exam component), as well as high blood sugar, high blood pressure, and abnormal blood lipids.[4,8]9. In individuals with obesity and T2DM, weight reduction not only lowers CVD risk but is also the most reliable predictor of diabetes remission, whether achieved through dietary intervention, obesity medications, or bariatric procedures.[4,56]10. While some diabetes medications potentially worsen obesity (e.g., sulfonylureas, insulin), medications whose mechanisms reduce both blood glucose and body weight have among the strongest degree of evidence of reducing the risk of CVD (e.g., incretin mimetics such as glucagon-like peptide – 1 receptor agonist based therapies and sodium glucose transporter - 2 inhibitors).[4,57]

Hypertension increases the risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) such as coronary heart disease, stroke, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, peripheral artery disease, and aortic aneurysm. The 2025 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association blood pressure goal for nonpregnant adults is <130/80 mmHg, with encouragement to achieve a systolic blood pressure <120 mmHg (when it can be safely attained) for potential additional health benefits. The lower optimal blood pressure goal is relevant for patients with obesity who can achieve it safely through evidence-based weight reduction interventions (e.g., healthful nutrition, physical activity, behavior modification, obesity medications, and/or bariatric procedures).[5,58]2. The adiposopathic effects of increased body fat are not the only causes of hypertension. Other potential causes of high blood pressure include pheochromocytoma, primary hyperaldosteronism, hypercortisolism, hyperthyroidism, renal artery stenosis, kidney diseases, side effects of concomitant therapies, and familial or genetic syndromes.[5,58]3. The relationship between body fat and blood pressure is approximately linear on a population level. Obesity-related hypertension arises partly from “fat mass disease,” in which visceral adipose tissue mechanically compresses the kidneys - activating the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS). Expanded adipose tissue increases total blood volume, cardiac output, and results in venous compression. Unlike the kidney, arteries are not confined within a rigid capsule, so perivascular adipose tissue (PVAT) does not raise blood pressure by physically squeezing them; arterial pressure far exceeds the pressure of surrounding soft fat, and healthy PVAT even acts as a buttress that stabilizes the vessel. Instead, dysfunctional, obesity-associated PVAT drives hypertension through an “outside-in” process: inflammation and fibrosis stiffen the adipose tissue into a rigid scaffold that limits arterial expansion, loss of normal PVAT stress-relaxation keeps vessels under higher tension, and paracrine signaling promotes collagen deposition within the arterial wall, collectively increasing arterial stiffness and promoting hypertension.[5,59]4. Increased fat mass contributes to obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), which promotes hypertension through activation of the sympathetic pathways and the RAAS, leading to increased blood pressure. Treating obesity and OSA - through weight reduction (e.g., including the use of tirzepatide) or continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) - reduces blood pressure and improves other CVD risk factors.[5,58]5. Adiposopathic “sick fat” contributors to hypertension include hyperinsulinemia and hyperleptinemia, which increase sympathetic nervous system activity; increase angiotensinogen, mineralocorticoids, RAAS activity, and sodium reabsorption; increase the adiposopathic release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., tumor necrosis factor and interleukin-6) that impair endothelial function; and reduce anti-inflammatory adiponectin release.[3,5]6. Ensuring an accurate diagnosis of hypertension requires consistent measurement techniques tailored for patients with obesity. Improper cuff size can significantly distort readings, sometimes (depending on the cited reference) by as much as ∼5–20 mmHg. Given the bias and discrimination against those with obesity, and/or fear of potential bias and discrimination (even within the healthcare setting), standardized measurements are necessary to control for “white coat syndrome,” which better allows for clinicians to distinguish true hypertension from stress-related elevations and avoid misdiagnosis.[5,58,60,61]7. Medical nutrition therapy (MNT) uses individualized nutrition assessment, education, and behavioral counseling to help patients modify dietary patterns in ways that improve obesity and hypertension. MNT emphasizes calorie-appropriate, nutrient-dense eating patterns such as reducing sodium and saturated fat while increasing fruits, vegetables, and whole grains to support weight reduction and improve blood pressure control. Illustrative examples of recommended, healthful eating pattern to help manage obesity and hypertension include the “Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension” or DASH diet or Mediterranean Diet.[5,62]8. While obesity medications such as orlistat do not generally alter heart rate, and while weight reduction among patients with obesity generally decreases blood pressure, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists may mildly increase heart rate by 2–3 beats per minute. Phentermine/topiramate, phentermine monotherapy, and naltrexone/bupropion may have a higher heart rate at the end of studies compared to placebo, but many have the same heart rate or lower than baseline.[5,63]

Epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) lies directly between the myocardium and visceral pericardium, with no fascial barrier. Its shared microcirculation with the coronary arteries places it in intimate structural continuity with the heart. EAT thickness is often measured via echocardiography (parasternal long axis), while volumetric quantification is done via cardiac computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging. This is relevant because EAT volume is an independent predictor of coronary events, separate from body mass index.[12,98]2. Paracardial adipose tissue resides outside the parietal pericardium, is supplied by non-coronary vessels, and is anatomically separated from the myocardium, preventing the direct paracrine diffusion of inflammatory or lipotoxic mediators that characterizes EAT. Both depots expand in obesity and are often grouped under the umbrella term “pericardial fat,” and their structural differences determine their distinct cardiovascular effects. EAT drives injury through two separate tracks — direct paracrine/vasocrine vascular toxicity (via shared microcirculation) and mechanical restraint on diastolic filling (via its location inside the pericardial sac). Paracardial fat, lacking shared circulation and lying outside the sac, contributes mainly as a systemic visceral-adiposity marker rather than through either of those local mechanisms.[12,99,100]3. EAT and visceral abdominal adipose tissue arise from the same mesodermal embryonic origin, with EAT representing the visceral adipose tissue depot of the heart. These two adipose tissue depots expand in parallel, and both correlate to a higher risk of CVD. Both EAT and visceral abdominal adipose tissue are also associated with greater coronary artery calcification, underscoring their shared contribution to adverse cardiometabolic outcomes. Thus, during an evaluation of body composition, an increase in visceral adipose tissue may suggest an increase in EAT. That said, unlike the white adipose tissue found in the abdomen, EAT expresses Uncoupling Protein-1 (UCP-1), giving it characteristics of brown or “beige” adipose tissue making it more metabolically active than paracardial or subcutaneous adipose tissue. The loss of this “brown” phenotype during obesity (whitening of fat cells) is a key driver of dysfunction.[12,100]4. Adiposopathy is characterized by a shift toward a pro-inflammatory local and systemic immune environment, with increased inflammatory macrophages, T-lymphocytes, and mast cells. Obesity cardiomyopathy reflects the combined impact of EAT, lipotoxicity, inflammatory signaling, and chronic hemodynamic load. These processes drive structural and functional myocardial changes that manifest as impaired cardiac performance, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), atherosclerotic coronary disease, cardiac dysrhythmias, and increased coronary calcium. In the development of dysrhythmias specifically, EAT can infiltrate the myocardium, separate cardiomyocytes, and create fibro-fatty disruption that interferes with electrical conduction and promotes micro-reentry circuits. Additionally, because EAT houses cardiac autonomic ganglia, local inflammation can heighten autonomic activity, further predisposing the heart to dysrhythmogenesis.[12,46]

Obesity and Cardiovascular Structural Changes. Obesity contributes to CVD not only through excess body fat but also through the specific location of adipose tissue and its direct structural effects on the heart and blood vessels. Epicardial and perivascular adipose tissue can release inflammatory signals and toxic lipids directly into the heart and coronary arteries, promoting hypertrophy, steatosis, and fibrosis through an “outside-to-inside” mechanism. At the same time, circulating inflammatory factors and atherogenic lipoproteins can injure coronary arteries from the lumen outward, creating an “inside-to-outside” assault that accelerates arterial stiffness and atherosclerosis.

With obesity, cardiovascular pathology is driven by the specific anatomical location of adipose tissue and its subsequent structural impact on the heart and vasculature. Epicardial Adipose Tissue (EAT) lies within the pericardial sac directly contacting the myocardium, and without a separating fascia, can direct “outside-to-inside” diffusion of inflammatory cytokines, fatty acids, and lipotoxic intermediates (e.g., ceramides, diacylglycerols) into coronary arteries and heart muscle. This local toxicity, combined with systemic volume overload, systemic inflammation, RAAS activation, and metabolic dysregulation, can contribute to left ventricular hypertrophy, myocardial steatosis (intracellular lipotoxicity), and interstitial fibrosis that stiffens the heart and disrupts electrical pathways. In addition to “outside-to-inside” damage that stimulates vascular smooth muscle hypertrophy, resulting in generalized arterial stiffness and accelerated atherosclerosis, coronary arteries may suffer a dual assault via “inside-to-outside” damage from intraluminal proinflammatory factors and atherogenic lipids that generate inflammation and plaque and result in adverse metabolic consequences such as the uncoupling of endothelial nitric oxide synthase. Thus, both the “outside-to-inside” pathogenic effects from EAT and “inside-to-outside” from atherogenic promoting circulating factors within the vascular lumen to vessel wall increase the risk for CVD. Kidney Disease Obesity Paradox: In chronic kidney disease, higher BMI is associated with lower mortality. Obesity, “Obesity Paradox,” and Cardiovascular Disease. The so-called “obesity paradox” is largely explained by the poor outcomes seen in people with low body weight due to underlying illness, smoking, frailty, or loss of muscle mass, rather than any true protective effect of excess body fat. Higher-weight individuals may fare better during acute illness because they often have greater metabolic and nutritional reserves, while body mass index cannot distinguish between healthy lean mass and high-risk sarcopenia in those labeled as “normal weight.” Overall, physical fitness—not body fat—is the strongest predictor of survival, with fit individuals who have obesity generally experiencing better outcomes than unfit individuals of lower weight.

An apparent “obesity paradox” arises when individuals with higher body weight appear to experience better health outcomes than those with lower body weight. However, this pattern becomes less paradoxical when the clinical context and physiological role of body fat are considered. Many people at low body weight have serious underlying conditions (e.g., cancer, chronic infections, advanced pulmonary disease, malnutrition, or severe cardiovascular disease) that drive unintentional weight reduction. Behaviors like cigarette smoking can also reduce body weight. It is expected, and not “paradoxical,” that individuals who are underweight due to life-threatening illness or cigarette smoking have poorer outcomes than individuals with overweight or obesity who do not have these chronic illnesses. Even in the absence of chronic disease, elevated mortality among the lowest-weight individuals may stem from frailty, sarcopenia, and reduced metabolic reserves. Higher-weight individuals may possess greater nutritional and energy stores, which can be beneficial during episodes of acute severe illness. Because body mass index (BMI) cannot distinguish between fat mass and lean mass, those classified as having “normal” weight may actually have sarcopenia, which is a high health-risk state marked by a substantial loss of muscle mass, potentially accompanied by loss of muscle function. In this case, any apparent survival advantage attributed to obesity may better reflect the increased mortality associated with low muscle mass in leaner patients, rather than a true protective effect of excess adiposity. Obesity most commonly causes false-negative ECG detection of left ventricular hypertrophy via voltage attenuation (worsened, not offset, by an accompanying leftward axis shift), and separately reduces the accuracy of ECG-based coronary artery disease testing through mechanisms like limited exercise tolerance and baseline ST-T abnormalities rather than the same voltage/axis effect.[1,125,129]4. Excessive body weight often limits a patient's ability to perform exercise stress testing due to early fatigue, breathlessness, and mechanical constraints, requiring pharmacologic stress testing instead. Common pharmacologic stress testing agents include regadenoson, adenosine, dipyridamole, or dobutamine, which are typically paired with nuclear myocardial perfusion imaging (MPI) or cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR). However, Single Photon Emission Computed Tomography (SPECT) myocardial perfusion imaging (MPI) often performes poorly in obesity because soft-tissue attenuation - particularly from breast or diaphragmatic fat – can obscure the signal. This artifact often produces apparent ‘fixed defects’ that mimic prior infarction or ischemia, prompting avoidable invasive angiography. For this reason, many diagnostic pathways favor PET or coronary CT angiography (CCTA) in individuals with obesity, as these modalities offer superior attenuation correction and more reliable image quality.[1,86,125]5. Excess body weight can exceed the table or gantry limits of common imaging modalities, including dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), which often cap at 350–450 pounds, depending on the scanner. Although newer systems may accommodate higher weights, image quality can still be compromised by increased tissue thickness. Obesity is also closely associated with atrial fibrillation and elevated resting heart rates. Because coronary CT angiography (CCTA) and cardiac MRI rely on electrocardiographic (ECG) gating to capture motion-free images, dysrhythmias such as atrial fibrillation or tachycardia significantly hinder image acquisition. Achieving adequate image quality may require aggressive beta-blockade for rate control, which introduces additional risk in patients with reactive airway disease - a condition frequently seen alongside obesity.[86,125]6. Increased soft-tissue mass in obesity attenuates ultrasound waves, making cardiac structures harder to visualize and reducing the reliability of echocardiographic evaluation, especially for valvular assessment and diastolic function. Ultrasound Enhancing Agents (UEAs), such as perflutren lipid microspheres or sulfur hexafluoride lipid-type A microspheres, are guideline-recommended for patients with obesity who have suboptimal acoustic windows to improve endocardial border definition. The threshold for using UEAs should be low, as they are often essential for rescuing non-diagnostic studies and ensuring accurate measurement of left ventricular ejection fraction.[125,130]7. Perivascular Fat Attenuation Index (FAI) provides a noninvasive CT-based biomarker of coronary inflammation. In many clinical practices, Coronary Artery Calcium (CAC) scoring is a more common CT-based assessment that correlates not only with coronary plaque burden but also is associated with epicardial adipose tissue volume. Increased abdominal visceral adiposity similarly tracks with higher CAC scores, reflecting shared pathophysiologic pathways (e.g., adiposopathy, insulin resistance, hypertension, dyslipidemia, as well as immunopathies and endocrinopathies such as systemic inflammation and oxidative stress). CAC is reported in Agatston scores that quantify calcified plaque in heart arteries:•0: No calcified plaque, unless accompanied by diabetes mellitus, cigarette smoking, or strong family history of cardiovascular disease, suggests very low risk for major adverse cardiovascular event (MACE) for the subsequent 5 or more years•1–10: Minimal plaque, low MACE risk•11–100: Mild plaque, moderate MACE risk•101–400: Moderate plaque, moderately high risk for MACE•Over 400: Extensive plaque, high risk for MACEIf only evaluated with traditional CVD risk factors via common CVD risk assessment tools, the risk for atherosclerotic CVD may be underestimated. Additionally, increased CAC scores correlate better with increased visceral adiposity, rather than BMI. Once statin therapy is initiated, CAC scores may worsen in some patients such that the value of serial CAC scores in patients with elevated CAC scores at baseline treated with statins is uncertain.[125,131,132]8. CT coronary angiography and calcium scoring often require higher radiation doses in individuals with larger BMI to maintain adequate image quality. Increased adipose tissue can also necessitate higher contrast volumes.[1,125,133]9. Obesity can make vascular access, catheter navigation, and fluoroscopic imaging more difficult, often lengthening procedures and raising the likelihood of complications. In patients with increased adiposity, transradial access (TRA) is favored over transfemoral access (TFA) because femoral access carries a significantly higher risk of retroperitoneal bleeding and hematoma. The femoral head - an essential anatomical landmark - is harder to palpate or visualize under fluoroscopy in patients with obesity, and achieving adequate post-procedural compression is more challenging. For these reasons, TRA is generally considered the safer and more dependable approach in individuals with obesity.[125,134]10. Obesity lowers circulating levels of B-type Natriuretic Peptide (BNP) and N-terminal pro–B-type Natriuretic Peptide (NT-proBNP) through a combination of increased clearance and reduced myocardial secretion. This suppression can mask even acute heart failure (HF), leading clinicians to miss the diagnosis when relying on standard BNP cut-points. Since BNP remains a central biomarker for identifying HF, diagnostic strategies in patients with obesity should consider lower BNP thresholds to avoid false reassurance and delayed recognition of cardiac decompensation.[125,135,136]

Obesity can complicate cardiovascular evaluation and requires specific adjustments in risk assessment, diagnostic imaging, and procedural planning. Many standard risk calculators do not account for increased adiposity, perhaps making Body Mass Index (BMI) inclusive tools such as the Predicting Risk of Cardiovascular disease EVENTS (PREVENT) model or QResearch (QRISK) algorithms more appropriate. Obesity may also suppress B-type Natriuretic Peptide (BNP) levels, masking acute heart failure. Finally, anatomical challenges in obesity often favor transradial over transfemoral catheterization to reduce the heightened bleeding risk associated with femoral access. Medical nutrition therapy is most effective when dietary interventions are grounded in evidence and delivered with clear quantitative and qualitative guidance. Approaches that support long-term adherence consistently yield better outcomes in obesity and cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk reduction.[24,137]2. Low-calorie diets, typically providing 1200–1800 kcal per day, offer a structured reduction in energy intake to support clinically meaningful weight reduction. Very low-calorie diets (VLCDs), generally defined as <800 kcal per day, require close medical supervision due to their intensity but have demonstrated short-term improvements in cardiometabolic risk factors. Among adults with obesity and type 2 diabetes, VLCD therapy can produce short-term and rapid reductions in body weight, blood glucose, blood pressure, and blood atherogenic lipids.[24,138]3. Fat-restricted dietary patterns, which typically limit fat intake to 10–30% of total daily calories, can facilitate weight reduction and improve cardiometabolic risk when paired with nutrient-dense food choices. A comprehensive umbrella review of randomized trials found that reducing saturated fat intake - an essential component of fat-restricted approaches - significantly lowered CVD events.[24,139]4. Low-carbohydrate dietary patterns generally limit carbohydrate intake to 50–150 g per day, with very low-carbohydrate diets restricting intake to <50 g per day. In a meta-analysis of 21 randomized controlled trials involving adults with obesity and type 2 diabetes, low-carbohydrate ketogenic diets produced significantly greater improvements in key cardiovascular risk factors than non-ketogenic comparators, including reductions in body weight, body mass index, waist circumference, fasting glucose, HbA1c, and triglyceride levels, alongside increases in high density lipoprotein cholesterol levels. These diets emphasize minimizing refined and ultra-processed foods, high-glycemic carbohydrates, and trans fats. For some individuals, nutritional ketosis may also reduce hunger and facilitate sustained caloric reduction.[24,141]5. The Mediterranean dietary pattern emphasizes extra-virgin olive oil, vegetables, fruits, legumes, whole grains, nuts, seeds, seafood, fermented dairy, poultry, and eggs. It limits red and processed meats as well as ultra-processed carbohydrates, fostering a nutrient-dense, cardioprotective eating pattern. In the Prevención con Dieta Mediterránea (PREDIMED) randomized controlled trial, adherence to a Mediterranean diet supplemented with either extra-virgin olive oil or nuts significantly reduced major cardiovascular events compared with a low-fat control diet, establishing this approach as one of the most robustly supported dietary strategies for CVD prevention. Of note, PREDIMED was retracted and republished in 2018 following identification of randomization irregularities, with the primary finding preserved.[24,142,148]6. The DASH dietary pattern emphasizes vegetables, fruits, whole grains, low-fat dairy, lean proteins, nuts, seeds, legumes, and foods naturally rich in potassium, calcium, and magnesium. It limits high-sodium foods, excessive total fat, cholesterol-dense items, red and processed meats, sugar-sweetened beverages, and added sugars. In a secondary analysis of the DASH-Sodium randomized controlled trial, the DASH diet significantly lowered estimated 10-year atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) risk compared with a typical American diet, with even greater reductions when combined with sodium restriction.[24,143]7. Vegetarian dietary patterns center on plant-based foods - including vegetables, fruits, whole grains, legumes, seeds, and nuts - while limiting or excluding meat. Vegan diets extend this approach by eliminating all animal products and relying entirely on plant-derived nutrition. In a meta-analysis of 20 randomized clinical trials, vegetarian diets produced significant improvements in major cardiometabolic risk factors among adults with or at high risk for cardiovascular disease, including reductions in LDL cholesterol, hemoglobin A1c, and body weight. These findings support the cardioprotective potential of vegetarian eating patterns. A caveat is that beyond the absence of meat, it is the nutritional quality of the diet that most contributes to reduced CVD risk, a benefit negated if the diet consists of calorie dense, ultra-processed foods, high in refined starch and sugar, and resulting in obesity and major CVD risk factors such as type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and dyslipidemia.[24,144]8. Both ultra-processed carbohydrates and saturated fats increase cardiovascular disease risk when consumed in excess. Exchanging one for the other on an isocaloric basis does not meaningfully improve cardiovascular outcomes.[24,145]9. Dietary principles of healthful nutrition in patients with obesity include limiting ultra-processed, energy-dense, and low-nutrient foods such as sugary drinks, ultra-processed meats, trans fats, excess sodium, alcohol, and simple sugars. Nutrient rich options are encouraged and include minimally processed foods and marine oily fish, nuts, seeds, legumes, whole grains, high-fiber carbohydrates, and no-sugar-added dairy, with a focus on foods that support cardiometabolic health.[24,146]10. Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RA) are indicated for weight reduction, and in some cases, to reduce CVD events. In most patients with obesity who are treated with GLP-1 RA, the primary goal of nutritional therapy shifts away from guidance regarding weight reduction through additional caloric restriction. Instead, the emphasis switches to healthful nutritional intake of nutrient-dense foods that provide sufficient vitamins, minerals, and macronutrients. Adequate protein consumption helps preserve lean body mass, including skeletal muscle. As importantly, adequate hydration is necessary to support overall metabolic and renal health because reduced food intake can significantly decrease water consumption from food sources. This, combined with common side effects such as nausea, vomiting, and diminished thirst, may increase the risk of dehydration and acute kidney injury in patients treated with GLP-1 RA.[24,147]

Finally, while GLP-1 RA therapies generally lower blood pressure, they can modestly increase resting heart rate, a well-recognized class effect.[28,194,[199], [200], [201]]6. While much needs to be confirmed in humans, rodent studies suggest that GLP-1 RA can favorably affect “sick fat.” Adiposopathy describes hypertrophic, hypoxic, fibrotic adipose tissue infiltrated by pro-inflammatory immune cells that release cytokines and maladaptive adipokines which results in adipocyte and adipose tissue dysfunction, insulin resistance, ectopic lipid deposition, and elevated cardiometabolic and CVD risk. GLP-1 receptors are found in the beta cells of the pancreas, brain, gastrointestinal tract, liver, kidneys, and heart, as well as adipose tissue. Human adipocytes express fewer GLP-1 receptors compared with the pancreas and brain; thus, the effects of GLP-1 RA on adipocytes likely reflect both direct effect as well as indirect effects via sympathetic pathways. Direct effects of GLP-1 RA may include cellular signaling, reduced adipose inflammation and relief of adipocyte endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress. Facilitated by weight reduction, as adipose tissue function normalizes, insulin sensitivity improves, peripheral subcutaneous adipose tissue can store energy appropriately, visceral adipose tissue declines, and ectopic fat is reduced. By correcting adiposopathic endocrine and immune signaling, GLP-1 RAs improve cardiometabolic risk factors, enhance vascular function, and lower CVD risk. Additionally, GLP-1 RA may remodel adipose tissue, shifting it from energy storage toward energy expenditure and stimulating lipolysis, suppressing de novo lipogenesis, shrinking adipocyte size, and promoting browning of white fat to increase thermogenesis. Thus, beyond reducing fat mass, GLP-1 RA may improve adipose function by decreasing chronic inflammation and increasing glucose uptake via glucose transporter 4 translocation, producing smaller, more insulin-sensitive, less toxic, and more thermogenic adipocytes.[28,194,202,203]7. Phentermine is a sympathomimetic amine that suppresses appetite and is FDA-approved only for short-term use - typically up to 12 weeks - for the treatment of obesity. Because it increases norepinephrine release, phentermine can raise both heart rate and blood pressure, requiring caution in individuals with uncontrolled hypertension or established cardiovascular disease. No prospective, randomized, long-term cardiovascular outcome trial has shown that phentermine monotherapy reduces major adverse CVD events.[28,194,204,205]8. The combination of phentermine and topiramate in an extended-release formulation is approved for chronic weight management and typically achieves greater weight reduction than either agent alone. It has been associated with improvements in cardiometabolic risk factors, including blood glucose, blood pressure and atherogenic blood lipid levels; however, the phentermine component can produce a modest rise in heart rate, warranting routine monitoring. Although observational studies support its overall safety, no prospective, randomized cardiovascular outcome trial has yet demonstrated that this combination reduces cardiovascular morbidity or mortality.[28,194]9. The naltrexone/bupropion combination works centrally to reduce appetite and food cravings, producing modest weight loss in many patients. Its cardiovascular safety was evaluated in the “Light” trial, but the study was stopped early, leaving the long-term cardiovascular benefits or risks inconclusive. While it has the potential for several drug interactions, naltrexone/bupropion can improve cardiometabolic risk factors. That said, the bupropion component can cause transient increases in blood pressure and is contraindicated in patients with uncontrolled hypertension.[28,194]10. Orlistat is a reversible gastrointestinal lipase inhibitor that inhibits the absorption of dietary fats, resulting in modest weight reduction and modest improvements in blood pressure. Orlistat may lower low density lipoprotein cholesterol beyond what weight reduction alone would predict, likely due to reduced intestinal lipid absorption. No large-scale cardiovascular outcome trial has demonstrated that orlistat reduces major CVD such as myocardial infarction or stroke.[28,194]

Thus, among patients with obesity, a >10% reduction of body weight may be considered a clinical threshold for improvement in CVD.

Long-term studies consistently show that childhood BMI, blood pressure, and lipid levels strongly forecast adult cardiovascular events, and that youth with obesity are likely to maintain obesity into adulthood, reinforcing the need for early identification and intervention.[32,239]5. High daily screen time (>2 h) is independently associated with hypertension and endothelial dysfunction, even among otherwise active children. Although sedentary behavior and screen use overlap, screen exposure also promotes mindless eating and increases contact with food marketing—mechanisms that operate separately from physical inactivity. Screen time beyond 2h/day is also strongly linked to reduced sleep duration and poorer sleep quality. Insufficient sleep disrupts appetite-regulating hormones (raising ghrelin and lowering leptin) and increases insulin resistance, creating an additional, distinct pathway contributing to CVD risk.[32,240]6. Children with obesity show increased arterial stiffness, impaired endothelial function, and thicker carotid intima–media layers. These measurable vascular changes predict higher adult CVD risk.[32,241,242]7. Obesity in youth clusters with insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and dyslipidemia, which are often accompanied by metabolic-dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), obstructive sleep apnea, and polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS). These conditions amplify long-term CVD risk and often persist into adulthood.[32,243]8. Social determinants of health - specifically food insecurity and neighborhood disadvantage – often coexist with systemic bias and weight stigma to intensify cardiometabolic risk in children. This risk is further amplified by Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs), including abuse, and mental health disorders such as depression and anxiety. These factors are not merely comorbidities but independent drivers of CVD that operate through dual pathways: behaviorally, by creating barriers to lifestyle adherence and fostering healthcare avoidance due to perceived judgment, and physiologically, where the chronic stress of discrimination and stigma acts as an allostatic load, upregulating systemic inflammation. Children with behavioral or mental health issues treated with atypical antipsychotics (e.g., risperidone, aripiprazole, olanzapine) can experience significant metabolic side effects, including rapid weight gain, insulin resistance and dyslipidemia.[32,234]9. Beyond obesity, major modifiable CVD risk factors include consumption of caloric dense, ultra-processed foods, physical inactivity, hyperglycemia, hypertension, elevated low density lipoprotein cholesterol, cigarette smoking, vaping and e-cigarette use and excessive alcohol intake. Non-modifiable CVD risk factors include genetic abnormalities and adverse consequences of chronic structural heart diseases or chronic metabolic and immune diseases. While childhood risk factors track strongly into adulthood, normalizing weight before adulthood can significantly mitigate this excess CVD risk.[32,244]10. Healthful nutrition, routine physical activity and behavior modification are foundational in the treatment paradigm of childhood obesity. Unfortunately, these approaches are often insufficient for severe pediatric obesity, prompting the use of obesity medications. Current FDA-approved options include semaglutide, liraglutide, orlistat, and phentermine/topiramate (all approved for ages 12 and older), as well as short-term phentermine (approved >16 years). Setmelanotide is approved for ages 2 and older for specific genetic conditions. Tirzepatide and naltrexone/bupropion are currently approved only for adults (18+) for weight management, although tirzepatide has been approved in children and adolescents 10 years and older with type 2 diabetes mellitus. In select cases of severe obesity, metabolic surgery is considered for adolescents (generally age 13+) with BMI ≥120% of the 95th percentile with an obesity driven disease or BMI ≥140% of the 95th percentile. Such interventions can substantially reduce CVD risk factors. Early intervention is recommended because the longer the exposure to obesity, then the longer the individual is exposed to adiposopathic endocrinopathies and immunopathies, which often leads to insulin resistance, vascular aging, and end-organ injury. Early identification and treatment in childhood can meaningfully alter this trajectory, preventing the cumulative burden of complications that otherwise accelerate across adolescence and adulthood.[32,245,246]

Obesity, Pediatrics, and Cardiovascular Disease (CVD). Childhood cardiovascular risk emerges from early biological programming, persistent obesity, and clustered metabolic conditions that accelerate vascular aging. Many pediatric cardiovascular diseases occur independently of obesity, underscoring the importance of universal lipid screening, early evaluation in high-risk families, and careful physical examination. Increased childhood CVD risk emerges from a complex interplay of early biological programming (e.g., genetics, epigenetics), metabolic disease, social adversity, and behavioral factors. Excessive maternal adiposity in fetal life accelerates atherosclerosis through inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and metabolic derangements, while rapid infant weight gain and persistent obesity in childhood can amplify CVD risk via adiposopathic consequences such as insulin resistance, hypertension, dyslipidemia, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), sleep apnea, and polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS). That said, as part of the differential diagnosis, it is important to note that pediatric cardiovascular diseases arise independently of obesity. Examples include congenital heart disease, cardiac dysrhythmias, myocarditis, cardiomyopathies, Kawasaki disease sequelae, rheumatic heart disease, chemotherapy-induced cardiotoxicity, familial hypercholesterolemia, congenital coronary anomalies, autoimmune conditions, and substance-related injury. Both the potential for obesity dependent and obesity independent CVD underscores the need for universal and routine screening (including targeted physical exam evaluation) for CVD risk factors. Traditional screening tools such as body mass index trajectory, blood glucose, blood pressure, and blood lipid panel can help assess early CVD risk. However, CVD risk factors such as these are often inadequate in fully predicting CVD risk, prompting interest in other assessments in select cases, such as inflammatory biomarkers and advanced imaging. Complicating CVD risk is that weight stigma itself acts as a toxic stressor that raises cortisol and inflammation. Excessive screen time, poor sleep, food insecurity, neighborhood disadvantage, adverse childhood experiences, mental health disorders, and the metabolic effects of some medications (e.g., atypical antipsychotics) may further elevate CVD vulnerability. In many cases, reduction of body mass index to normal range in childhood and adolescence in individuals with obesity can lower cardiometabolic risk in adulthood similar to that of an adult who never had obesity [32]. Treatment with healthful nutrition, physical activity, and behavior modification are foundational. In addition, use of evidence-based obesity medications and, in select adolescents, metabolic surgery can meaningfully reverse risk trajectories and improve long-term CVD outcomes. Obesity is a chronic, biologically mediated disease marked by disrupted appetite regulation, altered energy balance, and dysfunctional adipose tissue. As excess adipose tissue accumulates, lipotoxicity allows lipid deposition in non-adipose organs - including the heart - driving cardiometabolic stress and accelerating the pathways that lead to CVD. CVD often reflects years of metabolic dysfunction rooted in excess adiposity, with chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, and endothelial injury forming the core mechanistic link between obesity and cardiovascular pathology. While traditional risk factors such as type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia are important, social determinants also critically shape the onset of CVD risk factors, CVD risk, and CVD outcomes.[[33], [34], [35]]2. Structural conditions such as access to nutritious foods, safe spaces for physical activity, and stable housing - strongly shape the prevalence of obesity and long-term CVD risk. Among socioeconomically disadvantaged individuals, living locations may have unavailability of healthful foods (i.e., “food deserts”), inability to access healthful foods (“food insecurity”), or a disproportionate exposure to promotion and access to unhealthful, ultra-processed, energy-dense foods (i.e., “food swamps”). Another potential food access contributor to obesity includes the feast-or-famine cycle, which involves periods of scarcity followed by access to calorie-dense foods, which drives metabolic adaptations that favor fat storage and insulin resistance, effects more pronounced than steady overeating. Based on 2022 data, although food insecurity affects 12.8% of US households overall, rates are far higher among Black (22.4%) and Hispanic (20.8%) households, reinforcing disparities in obesity and CVD.[[33], [34], [35]]3. Minority youth are disproportionately exposed to marketing of sugar-sweetened beverages and ultra-processed foods — products consistently linked to excess adiposity and to early cardiometabolic dysfunction. These patterns illustrate the commercial determinants of health: food and beverage companies invest heavily to shape dietary preferences within specific demographic groups, competing with - and often outweighing - parental guidance, clinician counseling, and public health messaging, and helping entrench eating habits associated with greater lifelong obesity risk and, plausibly, higher downstream CVD burden. A 2022 Rudd Center analysis found that in 2021 Black youth and adults viewed 9% to 21% more food and beverage TV ads than their White counterparts after adjusting for total TV viewing time, and an earlier analysis of 2017 data found more than $1 billion spent in a single year on Spanish-language and Black-targeted TV advertising, overwhelmingly for candy, sugary drinks, and snacks.[[33], [34], [35]]4. Structural racism shapes the environments in which people live, the healthcare they can access, and the chronic stress they endure. These forces elevate allostatic load, which represents the cumulative physiologic stress that raises cortisol, blood pressure, and metabolic dysfunction - driving higher rates of both obesity and cardiovascular disease. Black Americans ages 35–64, for example, are about 50% more likely to have hypertension than White Americans, reflecting the cumulative physiologic burden of structural stressors, differences in health-care access, and social and economic conditions. Pregnancy also functions as a metabolic “stress test,” and complications such as gestational diabetes and preeclampsia independently predict future CVD. Women with prior GDM face a seven-fold increased risk of developing type 2 diabetes and substantially higher lifetime CVD risk. These burdens fall disproportionately on Black women, who are nearly three times more likely to die from pregnancy-related causes than White women - underscoring the intersection of structural racism, maternal health, and obesity-related cardiometabolic vulnerability.[[33], [34], [35]]5. Health disparities often originate before birth and in early childhood, when maternal stress, unhealthful nutrition, and environmental exposures shape lifelong metabolic vulnerability. Epigenetic changes arising during these periods can “program” increased susceptibility to obesity and cardiovascular disease, helping explain why equalizing healthcare access in adulthood does not immediately erase disparities. The “thrifty gene” hypothesis adds important context: some populations carry genetic traits that historically promoted efficient fat storage - a survival advantage in environments marked by scarcity. In calorie-dense, highly processed food environment, those same traits can become liabilities. Emphasizing this mismatch helps reduce stigma by underscoring that many individuals are biologically predisposed to struggle in a modern obesogenic landscape, rather than being at fault for their physiology.[[33], [34], [35]]6. Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs) are a defined clinical framework encompassing abuse, neglect, and household dysfunction. ACEs are a powerful, independent predictor of both obesity and CVD risk. High ACE scores are associated with a persistent “fight-or-flight” physiological state, leading to dopamine resistance that promotes comfort-seeking eating behaviors and cortisol dysregulation that accelerates visceral adipose tissue accumulation. These biologic effects compound over time, contributing to severe obesity and heightened cardiometabolic strain. Adults with four or more ACEs face a two- to three-fold increased risk of heart disease and are substantially more likely to develop severe obesity compared with individuals reporting no ACEs.[[33], [34], [35]]7. Limited access to preventive care and pharmacotherapy (such as glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists) widens the gap. When effective obesity treatments are restricted by cost or insurance tiers, metabolic dysfunction progresses unchecked in lower-income populations, leading to preventable obesity-related conditions such as type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and dyslipidemia, and thus limits prevention of CVD. Late detection allows metabolic dysfunction to progress unchecked, increasing the likelihood of clinically significant CVD. These gaps disproportionately affect underserved populations. It is the official position of the Obesity Medicine Association that all individuals with obesity have access to health improving, if not lifesaving, evidence-based obesity treatments – across the socioeconomic spectrum.[[33], [34], [35]]8. Weight stigma and bias within healthcare systems erode trust, reduce patient engagement, and contribute to therapeutic inertia. At the same time, many underserved communities face environmental stressors such as noise, light pollution, and the demands of shift work - that disrupt circadian rhythms and worsen insulin resistance. Poor sleep and chronic stress can reinforce one another, creating a metabolic feedback loop that accelerates both obesity and CVD. When coupled with the disproportionate environment of “food deserts,” “food insecurity,” and “food swamps,” all these social determinants translate into measurable disparities. CDC data show obesity prevalence is substantially higher in rural counties (34.2%) than in metropolitan areas (28.7%), with rural residents experiencing a 40% higher rate of preventable CVD deaths compared with their urban counterparts.[[33], [34], [35]]9. Communities without sidewalks, parks, or safe transportation options are effectively forced into sedentary patterns, limiting energy expenditure regardless of individual motivation. This constrained physical activity accelerates obesity progression and erodes cardiovascular resilience. Beyond the absence of supportive infrastructure, many lower-income and minoritized neighborhoods face an added burden: environmental toxicity. These communities are disproportionately located near highways, factories, and industrial zones, where exposure to obesogens and endocrine disruptors [e.g., bisphenols (BPA, BPS, BPF), phthalates, certain pesticides (atrazine, DDT), perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA), and organotins] trigger insulin resistance, systemic inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction, which increase the risk of CVD.[[33], [34], [35]]10. Effective strategies require moving beyond individual “advice” toward coordinated clinical, community, and policy interventions. Such multilevel approaches can meaningfully reduce disparities and improve cardiometabolic health.[[33], [34], [35]]

Epigenetic and generational influences underscore the need for preventive strategies that start before conception.[36,37]2. Adipose tissue is an active endocrine and immune organ whose dysfunction fuels inflammation, insulin resistance, and atherogenesis. This understanding underscores the need to move beyond a simplistic “fat-mass” framework and toward true body-composition assessment for more accurate risk stratification and targeted treatment. Some guidances emphasize anthropometric assessments such as waist circumference (previously discussed in metabolic syndrome section), waist-to-hip ratio of ≥0.9 for men and ≥0.85 for women, waist to height ratio ≥0.5, and direct measures of body composition (i.e., percent body fat and visceral adipose tissue assessment), rather than body mass index (BMI) alone. Confronting obesity early and decisively reshapes the trajectory of CVD by targeting the upstream metabolic disturbances that drive cardiometabolic risk. With rare exception, treatment of obesity requires sustained, lifelong management comparable to treatment of other chronic conditions like diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and dyslipidemia. Tailored approaches improve equity, relevance, and clinical effectiveness.[36,37]9. Future Directions of Obesity, Paradigm Shift, and Cardiovascular Disease (CVD). Early, lifelong obesity management is emerging as a cornerstone of future cardiometabolic care, reflecting a paradigm shift toward intervening before CVD risk becomes entrenched. Treating obesity first - using evidence-based, disease-modifying strategies - positions clinicians to meaningfully reduce long-term cardiovascular risk, especially when initiated early in life. Accurate body composition analyses, precision tools, individualized phenotyping, integrated Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) - aligned management, and coordinated multidisciplinary care represent the next generation of CVD prevention, strengthening outcomes by targeting the full spectrum of cardiometabolic drivers, including sleep apnea.

Treat obesity first. Treatment priorities for patients with co-occurring obesity and complications such as type 2 diabetes (and other cardiometabolic diseases) may best be based on the severity and immediacy of the cardiometabolic abnormality. For patients without acute disease, the primary goal is treating obesity, preferably with evidence-based treatment known to improve glucose and other cardiometabolic risk factors. For patients presenting severe acute illnesses like very high blood glucose, blood pressure, or triglycerides, the immediate priority shifts to stabilizing the acute condition, preferably while also addressing obesity.

Obesity is a generational disease. Optimal management of obesity should begin no later than during the prenatal period, extending through childhood, and continued into adulthood. Given that CVD risk is shaped by epigenetic and generational influences, then one might argue that “optimal” obesity management might best begin with grandparents or earlier. In the past, adipose tissue was considered as little more than an energy storage organ. However, adipose tissue is now recognized as an active endocrine and immune organ. Additionally, since adipose tissue is like other organs (such as the heart) where anatomy often reflects function, this reinforces the diagnostic need to move beyond body mass index alone towards a more detailed assessment of body composition. Evidence acquired over decades supports some anthropometric measurements as being superior to BMI in assessing CVD risk. While BMI is sufficient in population studies, it may lack precision in the individual. For example, an increase in BMI can be due to increased muscle mass and not necessarily fat mass. A normal BMI may be misleading in a patient with sarcopenic obesity. Thus, percent body fat is a better diagnostic measure than BMI. That said, among the most validated prognostic anatomic measure for CVD risk is the accumulation of visceral (and epicardial) adipose tissue, which is an anatomical reflection of global adipose tissue dysfunction. It is the combination of both “fat mass disease” and “sick fat disease” that are the most common contributors to the most common cardiometabolic complications encountered by clinicians. Obesity commonly leads to fat mass and adiposopathic multisystem adverse consequences, such as multiple CVD complications (e.g., atherosclerotic coronary artery disease, heart failure, cardiac dysrhythmias, and stroke), MASLD, as well as Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) Syndrome. Because most of the increase in CVD risk due to obesity is due to the adverse consequences of obesity itself, and because obesity-related adverse consequences are modifiable (at least early in the disease process), then this often supports the “treat obesity first” paradigm. Comprehensive obesity management integrates the control of blood glucose, blood pressure, and blood atherogenic lipids, as well as sleep apnea. Optimal care depends on coordinated, multidisciplinary teams and expanded access to evidence-based obesity therapies that include access to, and education regarding healthful nutrition, routine physical activity, behavior modification, obesity medications and bariatric procedures. Conclusion (See Fig. 27)

Overall Summary of Obesity and Cardiovascular Disease (CVD). Obesity drives cardiovascular disease through two primary pathways: “fat mass disease” causing biomechanical stress and “sick fat disease” (adiposopathy) leading to endocrinopathies and immunopathies. Determining CVD risk among patients with obesity goes beyond simple body mass index to include factors like body composition, racial, sex and age differences, assessing obesity complications (e.g., insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, dyslipidemia, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, chronic kidney disease), and social determinants of health. Finally, optimal CVD risk reduction includes the engagement of a team approach, whose collective goal is to integrate healthful nutrition, physical activity, behavioral modification, evidence-based obesity medications, and bariatric surgery to improve the health of patients, and reduce CVD risk.

This Obesity Medicine Association Clinical Practice Statement characterizes obesity as a primary driver of cardiovascular disease (CVD) operating through two distinct but complementary mechanisms. First, “fat mass disease” refers to the direct mechanical effects of excess adipose tissue, which impose biomechanical stress and contribute to conditions such as heart failure and sleep apnea. Second, “sick fat disease” (adiposopathy) reflects dysfunction of adipose tissue, which indirectly elevates CVD risk by promoting major risk factors such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and dyslipidemia, and directly increases CVD risk through the adverse immune and endocrine activity of epicardial adipose tissue. The overall risk of CVD is strongly influenced by age, race, and sex, as well as by social determinants of health. For many individuals with excess adiposity, a rational clinical approach follows a “treat obesity first” paradigm, which includes healthful nutrition, physical activity, behavioral modification, and evidence-based medical and surgical interventions.
