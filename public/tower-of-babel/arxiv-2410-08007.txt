Time Can Invalidate Algorithmic Recourse
Giovanni De Toni, Stefano Teso, Bruno Lepri, Andrea Passerini

Abstract
Algorithmic Recourse (AR) aims to provide users with actionable steps to overturn unfavourable decisions made by machine learning predictors. However, these actions often take time to implement ( e.g. , getting a degree can take years), and their effects may vary as the world evolves. Thus, it is natural to ask for recourse that remains valid in a dynamic environment. In this paper, we study the robustness of algorithmic recourse over time by casting the problem through the lens of causality. We demonstrate theoretically and empirically that (even robust) causal AR methods can fail over time except in the – unlikely – case that the world is stationary . Even more critically, unless the world is fully deterministic, counterfactual AR cannot be solved optimally. To account for this, we propose a simple yet effective algorithm for temporal AR that explicitly accounts for time under the assumption of having access to an estimator approximating the stochastic process. Our simulations on synthetic and realistic datasets show how considering time produces more resilient solutions to potential trends in the data distribution.

Throughout, we indicate (random) variables
X

Causality
.
Structural Causal Models
(SCMs)
[
61
]
allow us to formalize and reason about the causal behaviour of a system.
An SCM
\mathcal{M}=({\bm{\mathrm{X}}},{\bm{\mathrm{U}}},P,\mathcal{F})
encompasses
endogenous variables
{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}=\{X_{i}\}^{d}_{i=1}
,
noise variables
{\bm{\mathrm{U}}}=\{U_{i}\}^{d}_{i=1}
distributed according to
P({\bm{\mathrm{U}}})
,
and structural assignments
\mathcal{F}
of the form
X_{i}\coloneqq f_{i}(\mathbf{Pa}_{i},U_{i})
that describe all causal relationships between variables and their
direct causes
(or parents)
\mathbf{Pa}_{i}\subseteq{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}\setminus X_{i}

e.g.
, the link between education level and income.

An SCM induces a pushforward distribution
P({\bm{\mathrm{X}}},{\bm{\mathrm{U}}})=P({\bm{\mathrm{X}}}\mid{\bm{\mathrm{U}}})P({\bm{\mathrm{U}}})
, where
P({\bm{\mathrm{X}}}\mid{\bm{\mathrm{U}}})
is deterministic.

Hard interventions

do({\bm{\mathrm{X}}}_{\mathcal{I}}={\bm{\mathrm{\theta}}})
allow implementing external actions on an SCM
to simulate their causal effect on the system,
e.g.
, understanding the impact of getting either a PhD or a Master’s on our salary.

They replace a subset of variables
{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}_{\mathcal{I}}\subseteq{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}
with constants
{\bm{\mathrm{\theta}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}_{\mathcal{I}}|}
, detached from their original parents, yielding a new SCM
\mathcal{M}^{do({\bm{\mathrm{\theta}}})}
with updated structural assignments
\mathcal{F}^{do({\bm{\mathrm{\theta}}})}
and an associated
interventional distribution

P^{do({\bm{\mathrm{\theta}}})}({\bm{\mathrm{X}}})
.
Instead,
soft interventions

do({\bm{\mathrm{X}}}_{\mathcal{I}}={\bm{\mathrm{x}}}_{\mathcal{I}}+{\bm{\mathrm{\theta}}})
change how the affected variables depend on their parents without detaching them.
We shorten both kinds of intervention as
do({\bm{\mathrm{\theta}}})
for readability.
SCMs also enable us to reason
counterfactually
on what would have happened if the world were different due to an intervention
do({\bm{\mathrm{\theta}}})
, all else being equal.

For example, we could simulate questions such as
“What would have happened if we had undertaken a PhD rather than a Master?”
.

Given a realization
{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}
, the
counterfactual distribution

P^{do({\bm{\mathrm{\theta}}}),{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}={\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}({\bm{\mathrm{X}}})
is obtained by first
abducing
the exogenous factors
{\bm{\mathrm{U}}}
in the original SCM and then inferring the state of
{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}
in the intervened SCM, that is,
P^{do({\bm{\mathrm{\theta}}}),{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}={\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}({\bm{\mathrm{X}}})=P^{do({\bm{\mathrm{\theta}}})}({\bm{\mathrm{X}}}\mid{\bm{\mathrm{U}}})P({\bm{\mathrm{U}}}\mid{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}={\bm{\mathrm{x}}})

[
61
, Theorem 7.1.7]
. If the structural equations are invertible,
P({\bm{\mathrm{U}}}\mid{\bm{\mathrm{x}}})
is deterministic, and so is the counterfactual distribution.

Causal algorithmic recourse
.
In AR, the key quantities of interest are the
user’s state

{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}\sim P({\bm{\mathrm{X}}})
, which encapsulates the user’s features relevant to the decision-making task (
e.g.
, for a loan application: salary, debt-to-income ratio, occupation, etc.), and the

y\sim P(Y\mid{\bm{\mathrm{X}}})

, whether the user will repay their loan).

The SCM underlying

yielding an undesirable outcome,

that, once implemented by the user, leads in expectation to a more favourable outcome.

Algorithmic Recourse in Time

In real-world applications, users do not implement nor complete suggested interventions immediately. For instance,
improving our debt-to-income ratio to a satisfactory level can take months
.
This is problematic because
P({\bm{\mathrm{X}}},Y)
might change over time due to,
e.g.
, inflation rates, seasonality of loan interests, and classifier updates, meaning that
recourse produced by existing approaches could become ineffective or even counterproductive in the future

Unfortunately,
current robust (non-)causal recourse methods are increasingly fragile over time proportionally to the trend’s strength.

3.1
Formalizing Temporal Causal AR

Before proceeding, we need to extend causal AR with a time dimension.
We do so by considering a stochastic process
P({\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t},Y^{t})
capturing the evolution of the user’s state and its relationship to the target variable over time
t\in\mathbb{N}
.
In the following, we assume that

is induced by an SCM over the same variables
in which the parents of each variable lie in the past, and specifically within a fixed (but otherwise arbitrary) horizon
\rho\geq 1

i.e.
,
\mathbf{Pa}_{X_{i}^{t}}\subseteq\bigcup_{\delta=0}^{\rho}{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t-\delta}
, and similarly for
Y^{t}

We also assume that the outcome
Y^{t}
only depends on the user’s states
{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{1},\ldots,{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t}
up to time
t
.
We investigate the effect of time on recourse by considering stochastic processes where
P({\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t})
might not be stationary, while the conditional distribution
P(Y^{t}\mid{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t})
is unchanged, similarly to
covariate shift

[
68
]
.
For this reason, we can assume the classifier
h^{t}
is fixed.

3

If this is not the case, one option is to leverage existing techniques for addressing changes due to retraining, such as
Upadhyay et al. [74]
and
Pawelczyk et al. [59]

How do we represent user interventions in time?

Intuitively, the validity of recourse can only be compromised by changes occurring
after
the time
t
at which the recourse is issued, if these changes influence the distribution of the future state
{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t+\tau}
.
In the unlikely scenario where recourse is framed as a hard intervention,
do({\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t+\tau}={\bm{\mathrm{\theta}}})
, affecting
all
variables
{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}
, the future state
{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t+\tau}
becomes entirely independent of the past states. This is because hard interventions
detach
all variables from their causal parents, effectively overriding temporal dependencies and eliminating potential temporal effects
[
19
]
. Consequently, recourse in this case remains valid
by construction
.
In practice, however, recourse:

(i)
is typically
restricted to a subset of variables

\mathcal{I}
, due to constraints such as actionability or cost; and

(ii)
may be implemented as a
soft intervention
, represented as
do({\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t}_{\mathcal{I}}={\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{t}_{\mathcal{I}}+{\bm{\mathrm{\theta}}})
, without fully detaching
{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t}
from its causal structure
[
19
]
.
In this work, we focus on this more realistic setting of soft interventions, where only a limited subset of variables is targeted, and temporal dependencies persist.

The next challenge lies in capturing the
temporal nature
of actions that decision subjects can realistically perform within the causal framework.
Consider the following example: at time
t
, Alice is denied a loan application, and the recourse action is “get a degree.” Suppose it takes Alice
\tau
timesteps to complete the degree. She begins studying at
t
(
e.g.
, enrolling in university) and obtains the degree at
t+\tau
.
The action “get a degree” represents a process initiated at time
t
(enrolling) but whose causal effect materializes at
t+\tau
(degree completion).
To model this, we simplify the causal framework by treating the “enrol/get a degree” process as a single intervention
{\bm{\mathrm{\theta}}}
applied at
t+\tau
, assuming its causal effects are fully realized at that point.
Thus, since all user actions require time to implement, we define any action as an intervention occurring at a future
t+\tau
, where
\tau>0
.

For the rest of the paper, in the context of temporal algorithmic recourse, we will use the
do({\bm{\mathrm{\theta}}})
notation to represent an intervention
always
applied at time
t+\tau
, unless specified otherwise.

Additionally, this formulation assumes our SCM describing the time series must exhibit
instantaneous effects

[
62
]
.
As we will demonstrate in the remainder of this paper, even under this simplified intervention model, the validity of algorithmic recourse is highly sensitive to potential trends or changes in the underlying stochastic process.

3.6
),
T-CAR
is intrinsically more challenging (as we discuss in
Section

3.2

Naïvely recomputing recourse is not possible.

A seemingly straightforward “solution” to address the impact of time is to allow users to recompute recourse at time
t+\tau
, using their updated state
{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{t+\tau}
. However, this approach is simply not possible because the implementation of the newly suggested action may itself require additional time,
\tau^{\prime}>\tau
.
For instance, even if Alice were prepared to act on the updated recourse suggestion (e.g., obtain a degree) at
t+\tau
, it is unrealistic for her to achieve this instantaneously. By the time Alice completes the action—at some later time
t+\tau^{\prime}
where
\tau^{\prime}>\tau
—her state may no longer satisfy the updated requirements for recourse. In such a scenario, we might need to propose yet another action, such as “raise yearly income by 10%” potentially leading to the same negative outcome.

3.2
Uncertainty Over Time Compromises Counterfactual Recourse

We begin by studying temporal counterfactual AR (
T-CAR
).
It provides
individualized
suggestions, thus achieving the true optimal intervention for a given user

42
]
.
According to

2

Unfortunately, this turns out to be problematic. The following proposition shows that this distribution cannot be recovered exactly except under strong assumptions.

Proposition 1
.

Let

satisfy TiMINo.
Given a realization

\mathbb{E}[{\bm{\mathrm{U}}}^{t}]
are constant.

All proofs can be found in
Appendix

A
.
In words, given

optimally

Corollary 2

(Informal)
.

Let
P({\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t})
satisfy TiMINo
and consider a constant injective classifier
h

Given a realization
{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{t}
, a counterfactual recourse
{\bm{\mathrm{\theta}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
applied at time

We remark that

We explore this issue empirically in

We first start by defining robust causal AR:

Definition 3

\displaystyle{\bm{\mathrm{\theta}}}^{*}\in\argmin_{{\bm{\mathrm{\theta}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ \mathbb{E}[C(\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}},{\bm{\mathrm{x}}})]\quad\mathrm{s.t.}\;\;\mathbb{E}[h(\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}})]\geq 1/2\;\;\forall\;\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}\sim B({\bm{\mathrm{x}}};{\bm{\mathrm{\Delta}}})

Consider a fixed

\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{t+\tau}\sim P^{do({\bm{\mathrm{\theta}}})}({\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t+\tau}\mid{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}_{nd(\mathcal{I})}^{t+\tau}={\bm{\mathrm{x}}}_{nd(\mathcal{I})}^{t+\tau},{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t}={\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{t})}{[h({\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{t+\tau})]}<1/2

3.5
On the Stability of Recourse Over Time

In
Sections

3.2
,
3.3
and
3.4
, we showed how recourse validity can be compromised by the uncertainty and non-stationarity of the stochastic process
P({\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t},Y^{t})
, and we also showed how set-based robustness techniques fail over time.
However,
decision subjects
might be willing to accept recourses that slowly become less effective rather than performing more challenging interventions.
Thus, we now characterize instead the
rate
at which our recourse suggestion validity decreases,
while assuming a non-stationary
P(Y^{t}\mid{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t})
approximated by a sequence of classifiers
h^{t}
, one for each
t\in\mathbb{N}
.

Definition 4

(Temporal recourse invalidation rate)
.

Consider a discrete-time stochastic process
P({\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t},Y^{t})
, and any classifier
h^{t}
approximating
P(Y^{t}\mid{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t})
for each
t\in\mathbb{N}
.
Given a realization
{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{t}
and an intervention
{\bm{\mathrm{\theta}}}
such that
\mathbb{E}[h(\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}^{t})]\geq 1/2
, where
\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}^{t}\sim P^{do({\bm{\mathrm{\theta}}})}({\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t}\mid{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}_{nd(\mathcal{I})}^{t}={\bm{\mathrm{x}}}_{nd(\mathcal{I})}^{t})

\tau>0
as:

\Delta h({\bm{\mathrm{\theta}}};\tau)=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|h^{t+\tau}(\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}^{t+\tau})-h^{t}(\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}^{t})\right|\right]

(8)

where
\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}^{t+\tau}\sim P^{do({\bm{\mathrm{\theta}}})}({\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t+\tau}\mid{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}_{nd(\mathcal{I})}^{t+\tau}={\bm{\mathrm{x}}}_{nd(\mathcal{I})}^{t+\tau})
.

Let us now consider the setting in which we have a bounded stochastic process
P({\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t},Y^{t})
, where
-k\leq X^{t}_{i}\leq k
for some
k\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
.
If we have access to a dataset
\mathcal{D}^{t}\sim P({\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t},Y^{t})
, we can use it to train a classifier
h^{t}
via empirical risk minimization.
Let us consider a
linear
classifier
h^{t}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})=\langle{\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}}^{t},{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{t}\rangle
with bounded weights
-k\leq\beta^{t}_{i}\leq k

e.g.
, trained via Bounded Least-Squares (BLS)
[
72
]
.
Then, given an intervention
{\bm{\mathrm{\theta}}}
, we can derive the following upper bound on the recourse instability within a time interval
(t,t+\tau)
.

Theorem 6

(Upper-bound invalidation rate)
.

Consider a discrete-time stochastic process
P({\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t},Y^{t})
and a sequence of linear classifiers
h^{t}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{t})=\langle{\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}}^{t},{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{t}\rangle
approximating
P(Y^{t}\mid{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t})
.
We assume
-k\leq\beta_{i},X_{i}\leq k

.
The temporal invalidation rate is upper-bounded as follows:

\Delta h({\bm{\mathrm{\theta}}};\tau)\leq k\sqrt{d}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert{\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}}^{t+\tau}-{\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}}^{t}\rVert\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}^{t+\tau}-\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}^{t}\rVert\right]

(9)

where
\lVert\cdot\rVert
is the
\ell_{2}
-norm and the expectation is over
\mathcal{D}^{t}\sim P({\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t},Y^{t})
and the training process.

Theorem

6
shows how the recourse instability is upper bounded by how much the world varies between
t
and
t+\tau
in terms of the data distribution
P({\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t})
, and the classifier.
Moreover, the size of the problem

concurs by increasing the worst-case error at a sublinear rate.

The upper bound can be useful for non-linear classifiers
h
if we consider a linear function approximating locally
[
69
]
their decision function close to a realization
{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{t}
, such as LIME
[
67
]
.

If our stochastic process is

as in
Example

1
, we can derive the following upper bound:

Corollary 7
.

Consider a discrete-time trend-stationary stochastic process
P({\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t})
, where
m_{i}:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
represents the trend for
X_{i}

and the classifiers
h^{t}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{t})=\langle{\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}}^{t},{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{t}\rangle
.

Let us define

as the largest trend for
t\in\mathbb{N}

Then, we have the upper bound:

\Delta h({\bm{\mathrm{\theta}}};\tau)\leq k\left(\sqrt{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert{\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}}^{t+\tau}-{\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}}^{t}\rVert\right]+d\left(m^{*}(t+\tau)-m^{*}(t)\right)\right)

(10)

These results assume that the cost function remains constant over time.
In
Appendix

G
, we show an analogous result when this is not the case, as in
personalized cost functions

[
17
,
22
]

In

5
Discussion and Limitations

We now discuss some limitations of our work, which open up interesting avenues for future work.

Feasibility of temporal recourse and its evaluation.

Algorithm

1
depends on the quality of the estimator
\tilde{P}({\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t})
and, if the estimator is flawed,
Algorithm

1
could provide sub-optimal recourse.
Our experiments on synthetic and realistic datasets confirm that time presents a non-trivial challenge for AR and show how an estimator approximating
P({\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t})
can still be useful in some scenarios.
However, practical temporal recourse needs reliable time series forecasting, which is well-known to be challenging in various settings
[
51
]
, because of issues like concept drift
[
27
]
. Additionally, it requires effective implementations of causal inference over time
[
13
]
, which is an ongoing research topic.
In practice, we could not fully evaluate the effectiveness of
Algorithm

1
in real-world situations as this requires temporal datasets for recourse, which are currently not available or hardly obtainable.
For instance, in the case of lending applications, while banks hold temporal data on loan applicants -
e.g.
, records of the same individual reapplying for a loan after some time — these datasets are usually proprietary and not publicly accessible
[
15
,
12
]
.

Indeed, the scarcity of suitable data is a well-known issue affecting the evaluation of AR approaches at large
[
43
,
44
,
50
]
.
Suitable datasets for temporal recourse tasks, in particular, should at a minimum include the features used by decision-makers to train classifiers. For instance, in credit lending, financial institutions may rely on both frequently updated data (
e.g.
, credit card spending patterns) and more static attributes (
e.g.
, collateral assets).
To capture evolving trends in data distributions, these datasets will likely need to incorporate not only internal institutional records but also external sources such as governmental statistics (
e.g.
, census data).
Moreover, certain features may be considered ‘‘fixed’’ and thus not require temporal modeling.
In addition, regulatory frameworks could facilitate access to richer datasets; for example, Article 40 of the European DSA
7
7

7

https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2022/2065/oj
permits vetted researchers to access data from large online platforms (
e.g.
, Facebook, Twitter) for research purposes.
Extending such provisions to other domains could significantly benefit algorithmic recourse research.
However, privacy and ethical concerns remain central, necessitating rigorous data handling and anonymization practices.

Causal models, trends and interventions.

Our formalization assumes that the stochastic process adheres to the TiMiNo framework
[
62
]
, but it leaves room for exploring more complex Structural Causal Models (SCMs).
Addressing such complexities would necessitate more versatile models for causal inference in time series, such as integrating classical approaches like VAR
[
83
]
. Additionally, we do not examine trend models for the classifier
h
or the cost function
C(\cdot)
, even though such trends may coexist with those of
P({\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t})
.

Lastly, our task also aligns with alternative paradigms, such as the
dynamic treatment regime

[
53
]
, which emphasize constructing a
sequential policy
rather than a single counterfactual to ensure a desired effect at time
t+\tau
. We believe our theoretical results can extend to these settings, and developing their connection with AR represents a potential future direction.

Further ethical considerations.

Our work seeks to achieve
algorithmic contestability

[
49
]
through actionable counterfactual explanations, empowering users to challenge decisions made by machine learning models.
We believe recourse can offer tangible benefits to decision subjects.
For instance, in credit lending, recourse actions could help
scarred borrowers

[
14
]
, individuals who self-exclude from (re-)applying due to a perceived negative response from banks, by providing tools that enhance their understanding of the decision-making process.
However, AR introduces both technical and ethical challenges
[
76
]
.
For example, counterfactuals must avoid exhibiting
adversarial characteristics

[
47
]
and should aim to improve the user’s ground truth classification.
8
8

8

Consider an imaginary university application screening system that evaluates the number of books owned by an applicant’s family, based on the assumption that this metric correlates with academic performance
[
24
]
. While purchasing a crate of 1000 books might artificially improve the applicant’s chances of acceptance, it does not enhance their actual academic abilities (unless, of course, they read the books). Such an action would represent an example of an
adversarial recourse suggestion
.

Additionally, fairness considerations must be addressed
[
79
]
before implementing recourse solutions in real-world settings.
Related research has explored fairness in sequential decision-making within dynamical systems
[
37
,
65
]
, and extending these principles to recourse actions represents a critical step for realistic deployment.
Lastly, recourse methods have been shown to pose risks of
private data leakage

[
60
]
, potentially exposing sensitive user information to malicious entities.
To be effective in the wild, AR applications must take into account all these factors to ensure their effectiveness.

6
Conclusions

In this paper, we have investigated the impact of time on algorithmic recourse.
Our formalization of temporal causal recourse extends both counterfactual and sub-population causal AR by modelling the world as a (possibly non-stationary) causal stochastic process
P({\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t},Y^{t})
.
It allows us to theoretically demonstrate how
standard and robust AR approaches are fragile
, as their solutions become invalid in the presence of trends and future uncertainty. We also show that a simple algorithm, leveraging an estimator of the stochastic process fitted on historical data, can deliver more robust solutions.
Our experiments with causal and non-causal approaches support our findings.
With this work, we aim to highlight the negative impact of time on existing AR approaches while demonstrating how these challenges can be at least partially mitigated by leveraging historical data.

\epsilon>0
and for the timestep
t
.

Consider the following trend function
{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}(t)=\bm{1}\{t\geq\tau\}(-{\bm{\mathrm{\theta}}})
which is adding the inverse of the optimal intervention if an only if
t\geq\tau
.
Specifically, we define each trend component as
m_{i}(t)=\bm{1}\{t\geq\tau\}(-\theta_{i})
.
If our stochastic process exhibits such a trend, then, for any fixed

\mathbb{E}[h(\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}^{t+\tau}])]<1/2

Moreover, we can always build such a trend for any
{\bm{\mathrm{\theta}}}
and any unrestricted trend-stationary stochastic process.

∎

Appendix D
Analysis of the Trade-off Between Validity and Cost

We present an analysis of the trade-off between the validity and cost of recourse generated by
Algorithm

1
.
Using the same experimental setup and evaluation procedure detailed in
Sections

4.1
and
4.2
, we evaluate the impact of varying the
\lambda
parameter, which governs the strength of the cost constraint (line 5,
Algorithm

1
).
For this analysis, we focus on time series with a complex linear and seasonal trend.
The results, shown in
Fig.

7
, demonstrate a clear cost-validity trade-off across all experimental scenarios:
cheaper interventions result in lower validity over time
.
As the weight of the cost constraint (
\lambda
) increases during optimization, the interventions become more affordable but exhibit reduced validity.
These findings align with prior research on cost-validity trade-offs in non-temporal settings
[
59
]
, extending the observations to temporal scenarios.

For example, in
Adult
, we observe a reduction in the validity over time (
\sim 0.05
), but a decreased cost as shown by the lighter dots.
In
COMPAS
, the trade-off presents a smoother behaviour since we have only one actionable feature.
Lastly, we observe how the reduction in validity is not consistent across time series: we suspect this might depend on the quality of the estimator
\tilde{P}({\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t})
and on the decision boundary of the classifier
h
.

Appendix E
Further Analysis on the Empirical Cost and Sparsity

In this section, we report further analysis and results when considering the empirical average
cost
and
sparsity
(# of
\mathcal{I}
achieving recourse) of the valid interventions.
They are both common metrics in the algorithmic recourse literature
[
44
,
77
]
.

Sparsity
.

Figs.

8
and
9
shows the empirical average sparsity of the causal recourse methods for both synthetic and realistic stochastic processes.
We do not report values for
IMF
since it always acts on
all
the actionable features (
|\mathcal{I}|=3
).
In
Fig.

8
,
T-SAR
presents a similar or lower sparsity than other methods. However, as
Section

4.1
shows,
T-SAR
is the only method achieving good validity over time.
Thus, these results suggest that incorporating time might not increase the sparsity of the solutions.
In the case of approximate SCMs,
Fig.

9
shows how
T-SAR
provides sparse interventions for
Adult
, but increasingly larger interventions for
Loan
.

COMPAS
has only one actionable feature, thus the sparsity is equal for all approaches.
As highlighted in
Section

4.2
,
T-SAR
performance is also dependent on the quality of the estimator
\tilde{P}({\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t})
.

Cost.

Figs.

10
and
11
shows the empirical average cost for the users for which all (non-)causal recourse methods found a valid intervention.
We consider only the top-3 methods achieving recourse for each time step
t
.
In realistic and synthetic settings,
T-SAR
can provide
cost-adaptive
interventions which follow the underlying trend.
For example, we can observe this phenomenon in both
COMPAS
and
Loan
(
Fig.

11
).
It is also visible for
m(t)\in\{\hbox{Linear},\hbox{Linear+Seasonal}\}
in the Non-linear and Linear ANMs, respectively.
We also notice how
T-SAR
seems to provide costlier recourses than the standard robust methods.
However, in
Adult
,
T-SAR
produces cheaper interventions than the other approaches.
We can explain this behaviour for
Adult
by looking at the analysis of the successful intervention sets
\mathcal{I}
in
Section

4.2
.

In conclusion, by incorporating an estimator of the stochastic process, we can provide
sparse
interventions more resilient to time.
These interventions tend to be costlier and the cost varies with the time lag
\tau
when they will be applied.
However, robust (non-)causal methods achieve dissimilar validity, thus making them not fully comparable to each other by measuring their cost.
Nevertheless, we believe the analysis has merit since it hints at a tradeoff between sparsity, cost and validity.

Appendix G
Additional Theoretical Results on Cost Stability

In this section, we provide an upper bound on the cost stability of recourse suggestions under a more expressive cost function.

Previous research has shown how providing recourse without considering the user’s preferences can lead to sub-optimal interventions
[
17
]
.
This is why
personalized
AR, in line with multi-attribute decision making
[
45
,
64
]
, models the cost function as an
additive independence model

C(\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}},{\bm{\mathrm{x}}})={\bm{\mathrm{w}}}^{\top}|\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}-{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}|
, where the weights
{\bm{\mathrm{w}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
encapsulate the user’s preferences
[
16
,
17
]
.
We assume we can learn these weights, either from historical data,
e.g.
, surveys and interviews
[
66
]
, or by interacting with the end-user
[
17
]
.
In the following, we explicitly consider the evolution of the user’s preferences
{\bm{\mathrm{W}}}
, as it also impacts the effectiveness of recourse, although doing so can be avoided for non-personalized AR approaches.

We assume the user preferences can be represented as a stochastic process
P({\bm{\mathrm{W}}}^{t})
.
We do not put any prior assumption on how
P({\bm{\mathrm{W}}}^{t})
factorizes, since it is not relevant for our results. In line with previous work
[
17
]
, we could imagine
P({\bm{\mathrm{W}}}^{t})
follows a causal model.
Then, we can provide the following upper bound:

Theorem 8
.

Consider the discrete-time stochastic processes
P({\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t},\allowbreak Y^{t}\allowbreak)
,
P({\bm{\mathrm{W}}}^{t})
and a parametrized cost function
C(\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}},{\bm{\mathrm{x}}};{\bm{\mathrm{w}}})=\langle|\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}-{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}|,{\bm{\mathrm{w}}}\rangle
with bounded
-k\leq w_{i}^{t},X_{i}^{t}\leq k
for
k\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
Given a realization
{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{t}
and user’s preferences
{\bm{\mathrm{w}}}^{t}
, the variation of the cost of an intervention
{\bm{\mathrm{\theta}}}
is upper bounded by:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|C(\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}^{t+\tau},{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{t+\tau};{\bm{\mathrm{w}}}^{t+\tau})-C(\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}^{t},{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{t};{\bm{\mathrm{w}}}^{t})\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq k\sqrt{d}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert{\bm{\mathrm{w}}}^{t+\tau}-{\bm{\mathrm{w}}}^{t}\rVert+\lVert|\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}^{t+\tau}-{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{t+\tau}|-|\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}^{t}-{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{t}|\rVert\right]

where
\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}^{t}\sim P^{do({\bm{\mathrm{\theta}}})}({\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t}\mid{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}_{nd(\mathcal{I})}^{t}={\bm{\mathrm{x}}}_{nd(\mathcal{I})}^{t})
and
\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}^{t+\tau}\sim P^{do({\bm{\mathrm{\theta}}})}({\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t+\tau}\mid{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}_{nd(\mathcal{I})}^{t+\tau}={\bm{\mathrm{x}}}_{nd(\mathcal{I})}^{t+\tau})
.

Theorem

8
shows how the recourse cost changes based on how the users’ preferences evolve, and also over the relative difference of the proposed changes given the starting value.

Proof.

We first apply the following substitutions (a)
{\bm{\mathrm{w}}}^{\prime}={\bm{\mathrm{w}}}^{t+\tau}
and
\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}^{\prime}=\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}^{t+\tau}
(b)
{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{\prime}={\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{t+\tau}

\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}^{\prime\prime}=\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}^{t}
(c)
{\bm{\mathrm{w}}}={\bm{\mathrm{w}}}^{t}
and
{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{\prime\prime}={\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{t}
, to improve the clarity of the proof.
Further, consider
\Delta C=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|C({\bm{\mathrm{x}}},{\bm{\mathrm{x}}};{\bm{\mathrm{w}}}^{\prime})-C({\bm{\mathrm{x}}},{\bm{\mathrm{x}}};{\bm{\mathrm{w}}})\right|\right]
.
Then, the proof is the following:

\displaystyle\Delta C

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\langle\left|{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{\prime}-{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}\right|,{\bm{\mathrm{w}}}^{\prime}\rangle-\langle\left|{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}-{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}\right|,{\bm{\mathrm{w}}}\rangle\right|\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\langle\left|\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}^{\prime}-{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{\prime}\right|-\left|\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}^{\prime\prime}-{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{\prime\prime}\right|,{\bm{\mathrm{w}}}^{\prime}\rangle+\langle\left|{\bm{\mathrm{w}}}^{\prime}-{\bm{\mathrm{w}}}\right|,\left|\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}^{\prime\prime}-{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{\prime\prime}\right|\rangle\right|\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{\leq}\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert\left|\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}^{\prime}-{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{\prime}\right|-\left|\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}^{\prime\prime}-{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{\prime\prime}\right|\rVert\cdot\lVert{\bm{\mathrm{w}}}^{\prime}\rVert\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert\left|{\bm{\mathrm{w}}}^{\prime}-{\bm{\mathrm{w}}}\right|\rVert\cdot\lVert\left|\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}^{\prime\prime}-{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{\prime\prime}\right|\rVert\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(ii)}{\leq}k\sqrt{d}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert\left|\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}^{\prime}-{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{\prime}\right|-\left|\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}^{\prime\prime}-{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{\prime\prime}\right|\rVert\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+k\sqrt{d}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert\left|{\bm{\mathrm{w}}}^{\prime}-{\bm{\mathrm{w}}}\right|\rVert\right]

\displaystyle=k\sqrt{d}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert\left|\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}^{\prime}-{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{\prime}\right|-\left|\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}^{\prime\prime}-{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{\prime\prime}\right|\rVert+\lVert\left|{\bm{\mathrm{w}}}^{\prime}-{\bm{\mathrm{w}}}\right|\rVert\right]

\displaystyle=k\sqrt{d}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert{\bm{\mathrm{w}}}^{t+\tau}-{\bm{\mathrm{w}}}^{t}\rVert+\lVert|\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}^{t+\tau}-{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{t+\tau}|-|\hat{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}^{t}-{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{t}|\rVert\right]

where (
i
) follows from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, and (
ii
) from the bounds we placed on
{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}
and
{\bm{\mathrm{W}}}
. On the last step, we reorder the terms and we substitute the temporary variables with the original values.
∎
