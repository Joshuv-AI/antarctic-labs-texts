Latent-space clustering and feature interpretation in GraphCROC-Encoded periodontal disease, focusing on anterior and posterior bone loss prediction
Authors: Sarvagya Sharma, Deepavalli Arumuganainar, Pradeep Kumar Yadalam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeriodontitis is a widespread inflammatory disease marked by progressive loss of the periodontal ligament and alveolar bone, often leading to tooth loss. Its complex etiology involves dental plaque, the immune response, behavior, and systemic factors such as diabetes. While traditional diagnostic tools such as probing depth, clinical attachment loss, and radiographs are useful, they often miss the heterogeneity of disease progression. Conventional assessments evaluate clinical and radiographic data separately, missing key patterns and interactions. Recent advances in artificial intelligence, especially graph neural networks (GNNs), model the oral cavity as a network of teeth as nodes and relationships as edges. This approach provides a more comprehensive and biologically relevant understanding of periodontal disease.MethodsWe developed GraphCROC, a dual-stream model that separately encodes 11 node-level clinical features and five edge-level bone loss metrics, integrating them via a cross-correlation module. The model was trained on data from 90 patients using a clinically weighted reconstruction loss and evaluated against VAE, GCN, and DEC baselines.ResultsGraphCROC outperformed all baselines in reconstruction (MAE = 0.402, R2 = 0.607) and predicted CAL with the lowest RMSE (0.369). Unsupervised clustering revealed three biologically meaningful disease patterns. Dimensionality reduction revealed clear gradients that represent inflammation and structural loss.ConclusionGraphCROC successfully reconstructs clinical data, identifies disease subtypes, and supports personalized diagnosis by connecting individual risk factors to spatial bone loss. This graph-based model demonstrates potential for AI-driven periodontal risk assessment and treatment planning.Keywords: Graph neural networks, Autoencoders, Periodontal disease, Latent space, Bone loss, Deep learning, Clustering

Introduction

Periodontal and peri-implant diseases are complex, multifactorial conditions driven by a combination of patient-level, site-level, and behavioral factors. Variables such as microbial plaque, gingival inflammation, smoking, systemic conditions, and genetic predispositions all interplay in the initiation and progression of periodontitis. Traditional clinical assessments capture these factors as separate indices (e.g., plaque index, probing depth, etc.), but integrating them to discern overall disease patterns remains challenging 1, 2, 3. Multivariate representation learning can offer a holistic view by encoding multiple clinical features into a low-dimensional space that preserves underlying structure.4 The integration of advanced computational models in periodontal diagnostics holds potential to enhance early detection, objectively quantify bone-loss patterns, and support periodontists in selecting personalized treatment strategies based on patient-specific disease profiles. Current periodontal diagnostics analyze clinical indices and radiographic bone loss separately, but AI models often overlook spatial patterns or lack interpretability for clinical use. In particular, existing models poorly capture differences in anterior and posterior bone-loss patterns, which have distinct prognostic and therapeutic implications.

In recent years, unsupervised autoencoders, such as the variational autoencoder (VAE), have been utilized to learn latent representations of biomedical data. Likewise, graph neural networks (GNNs) enable the modeling of relational data (e.g., tooth-to-tooth contacts) alongside individual attributes.4,5 However, standard VAEs assume independent features and may not capture relational patterns, while typical GNNs with global readouts can “smooth out” important patient-specific details. Clustering methods such as Deep Embedded Clustering (DEC) add a clustering loss to latent features but may sacrifice reconstruction accuracy or interpretability.6 There is a need for a method that jointly encodes node-level and edge-level periodontal features in a compact representation while also yielding clinically interpretable patient clusters.

Contemporary periodontal diagnostics face challenges such as patient variability, subjective interpretation, and a lack of effective early-detection tools. Recent studies have shown that AI frameworks can enhance diagnostic consistency and facilitate personalized care by integrating radiographic, microbial, and genomic data.7 Graph-based and latent-space models are promising for understanding disease complexity through networked representations, thereby aiding individualized treatment and risk assessment.8, [a], [b] This research presents GraphCROC, an interpretable deep learning framework that combines patient-level clinical data and site-specific bone loss patterns into a cohesive latent representation, addressing a gap in periodontal diagnostics. Traditional methods analyze these data independently, overlooking their combined effect on disease progression and treatment response.9 GraphCROC captures the multidimensional nature of periodontal disease in a biologically meaningful way by encoding both node features (such as plaque, clinical attachment loss, and the gingival index) and edge features (such as anterior/posterior bone loss and craters). This dual-stream encoding replicates the clinical reasoning process, wherein both systemic risk factors and local bone architecture are concurrently assessed to guide prognosis and treatment planning.10

This study highlights differentiating anterior and posterior bone-loss patterns, which have distinct clinical implications for disease progression, prognosis, and treatment planning. Capturing these spatial variations in a unified model remains a key challenge this work aims to address. Traditional diagnostic methods in periodontology separate clinical and radiographic data, which limits the detection of site-to-site patterns, such as differences between anterior and posterior bone loss, and overlooks complex interactions between structural and biological changes. This, in turn, constrains the identification of complex disease patterns. Hence, there is a need for integrative, interpretable models that combine data types to enhance personalized diagnosis and treatment planning. Currently, there is no method to integrate these multi-scale features into a cohesive model. To address these limitations, we have developed GraphCROC. This dual-stream graph autoencoder jointly encodes node-level clinical features and edge-level interdental bone-loss descriptors into an interpretable latent space, explicitly preserving anterior and posterior bone-loss information for improved phenotype discovery and downstream prediction. In addition, GraphCROC addresses the gap by illustrating the connection between local bone loss and systemic risk factors, facilitating early diagnosis, tailored treatment planning, and scalable decision support, while safeguarding patient privacy across all clinical settings.

Despite advances in AI for periodontal diagnostics, models primarily analyze clinical parameters and radiographic features separately. This limits the ability to capture spatial disease patterns, especially between anterior and posterior bone loss. Current methods like VAEs and GNNs either lack effective relational modeling or reduce interpretability and detail. Therefore, there's a need for an integrated framework that combines clinical and spatial features into a single, understandable representation. In clinical practice, GraphCROC can be integrated into digital periodontal charts to support risk stratification, identify high-risk sites, and help clinicians choose targeted treatments. In this study, we applied this model to a periodontal dataset containing 16 clinical features with the following objectives: (1) to encode high-dimensional data into a latent space reflecting underlying disease patterns; (2) to identify patient clusters in this latent space and compare their clinical profiles; and (3) to evaluate the model's performance against benchmarks including a VAE, a GNN-based model and DEC (2,3,5)The study aims to: (1) develop a dual-stream graph autoencoder (GraphCROC) combining clinical and bone-loss features into a latent space; (2) identify periodontal disease subtypes via clustering; (3) compare model performance with baselines (VAE, GCN, DEC) across various metrics; (4) evaluate the clinical interpretability of latent features related to inflammation and bone loss.

Materials and methods

Dataset and preprocessing

This retrospective, single-center, AI-based observational study collected anonymized patient data from the Saveetha clinical system between March 2024 and March 2025, IHEC/SDC/PERIO-2304/24/156 without direct patient contact. Data was used solely for AI model development and analysis Institutional ethical committee clearance per institutional policy was obtained. The analysis used a cross-sectional dataset of 90 periodontal patients. Each patient record comprised 16 variables capturing both individual attributes and site-level contact lesions. Of these, 11 were node features (per-patient attributes): GENDER (male/female), PLAQUE index, GINGIVAL index (a measure of gingivitis), CAL (clinical attachment level, mm), PD (probing depth, mm), CAIROS (Caries risk score), FURC (furcation involvements count), MOB (tooth mobility score), crowding (anterior crowding score), spacing (anterior spacing score), and smoking (pack-years). The remaining 5 were edge features summarizing interdental bone loss patterns between teeth: (horizontal bone loss in the anterior region), (vertical bone loss in the anterior region), (horizontal loss in the posterior region), (vertical loss in the posterior region), and craters (count of crater-like two-wall defects). These edge features characterize the contacts or bone loss between adjacent teeth, effectively forming a graph of the dentition for each patient.

Data preprocessing was performed to ensure feature comparability. Gender was one-hot encoded (female = 0, male = 1). All variables were standardized (using z-scores) to have a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1. The dataset was then randomly split into an 80% training set (n = 72) and a 20% hold-out test set (n = 18), ensuring a representative mix of disease severity across the subsets. This split was used for model training and evaluation of reconstruction error on unseen patients.

GraphCROC architecture

GraphCROC is an “hourglass” autoencoder with dual input streams for node and edge features (Fig. 1). It has separate node and edge encoders that extract 11-dimensional node and 5-dimensional edge features, each with three layers of batch normalization (BN) and ReLU, compressing to 64-dimensional embeddings. These are concatenated and processed by a cross-correlation layer to produce a shared 64-dimensional latent vector z that represents the patient's periodontal status. The decoding mirrors this: z is fed into node and edge decoders, which are structurally similar to the encoders, to reconstruct the original features. In essence, the model learns a compressed joint representation while still preserving distinct pathways for node and edge reconstruction, which aids in interpretability (since we can trace how each latent dimension contributes to node versus edge outputs).

Schematic of the GraphCROC autoencoder architecture. Node features (11 inputs) and edge features (5 inputs) are encoded in parallel streams into 64-dimensional embeddings ($f_{node}$ and $f_{edge}$). A cross-correlation layer concatenates and transforms these into a 64-D latent vector $z$. The decoder network then splits this latent representation to reconstruct the original node and edge variables.

The GraphCROC architecture has three-layer encoders for node and edge streams (input → 128 → 64 → latent 64), using ReLU and batch normalization. It has ∼40,000 parameters. Using the Adam optimizer (learning rate $10^{-3}$), we trained for 100 epochs with mini-batches of 16 patients, achieving convergence. Unsupervised clustering and visualization

To identify natural groupings of patients, we applied K-means clustering to the latent space Z. We empirically tested k values ranging from 2 to 6 clusters using the elbow method and average silhouette scores (evaluated in the full 64-D space) to determine the optimal number of clusters. The best balance of compactness and separation was achieved with k = 3 clusters, which yielded a silhouette coefficient of approximately 0.20 (indicating moderate structure); higher k values yielded only small marginal improvements. We therefore fixed k = 3 and partitioned the patients into three latent clusters.

For visualization, we projected high-dimensional embeddings down to two dimensions using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP), a non-linear dimensionality reduction technique that preserves local structure, purely for plotting. We used 2D UMAP with 15 neighbors and a minimum distance of 0.1, using the Euclidean metric on Z. Cluster labels from K-means were carried into UMAP space, which only provided a layout. This visualizes cluster separation in a scatter plot, although clustering validity indices are based on the original 64-D space.

Each model was trained and evaluated with the same data-splitting strategy and evaluation pipeline. To assess stability, GraphCROC and all baseline models were repeated across three random initializations. Performance metrics, including MAE, RMSE, R2, CAL prediction RMSE, and silhouette score, were summarized as mean ± standard deviation. Pairwise comparisons between GraphCROC and baselines were performed using paired statistical tests across runs. Given the limited repetitions and exploratory design, these comparisons were interpreted cautiously and primarily supported performance trends. Model performance was assessed using metrics such as mean absolute error, root-mean-square error, coefficient of determination, CAL prediction RMSE, and silhouette coefficient. Results were averaged over repeated runs under identical data splits to reduce variability.

Results

The GraphCROC model demonstrated strong performance in reconstructing data on periodontal clinical and bone loss, with the lowest reconstruction error among all the models tested. GraphCROC outperformed the variational autoencoder (VAE), graph convolutional network (GCN), and deep embedded clustering (DEC) methods on all reconstruction metrics. Specifically, it had a mean absolute error (MAE) of 0.402, a root-mean-square error (RMSE) of 0.529, and an R2 of 0.607 on the test set. The model was especially effective at reconstructing clinically important features with low variance, such as the plaque index. This was because it used a feature-weighted loss function that doubled the penalty for incorrect plaque values. This focus led to a fourfold increase in plaque-specific R2 (from 0.03 to 0.12), without significantly complicating the explanation of global variance. The test set reconstruction MSE (0.5413) and the training loss curve, which continued to decrease, indicated that the model was stable and could be applied in other situations.GraphCROC achieved precise reconstruction of periodontal parameters and effectively differentiated patient subgroups based on inflammation status and structural bone loss. The reduced reconstruction error highlights model robustness, with latent clusters aligning to distinct clinical phenotypes—active disease, residual destruction, and maintenance—supporting its potential for precision diagnosis and personalized treatment in periodontology.

Unsupervised clustering of the 64-dimensional latent space identified three patient subgroups. Cluster 0 showed extensive past tissue damage (CAL and PD > 6.5 mm) and moderate plaque, suggesting worsening or treated disease. Cluster 1 had high plaque and gingival scores and significant bone loss, mainly among younger males, indicating active inflammation. Cluster 2 exhibited a maintenance phenotype, characterized by low plaque, minimal inflammation, and mild bone loss, predominantly in females. UMAP and t-SNE visualizations helped interpret these clusters biologically. The silhouette score of 0.20 outperforms DEC (0.18) and GCN (0.15), indicating moderate structural separation. Latent vectors from GraphCROC predicted CAL with an RMSE of 0.369, outperforming the baselines, indicating that the embeddings retain relevant disease information. These results confirm that GraphCROC's dual-stream approach is a clinically relevant and efficient tool for identifying periodontal phenotypes.

The analysis of clusters in GraphCROC's latent space reveals two main findings: Clusters 0 and 1 exhibit similar disease extents but differ in terms of inflammation and bone loss, whereas Cluster 0 differs from Cluster 2 in disease extent despite having low plaque levels. The autoencoder effectively distinguishes between tissue destruction and current inflammation, with UMAP-1 correlating with bone loss and UMAP-2 with plaque and gingival indices. This indicates a relationship between current inflammation and past attachment loss in periodontal disease. Additionally, clusters exhibit compactness, with lower within-cluster standard deviations (23–31% lower) than the overall dataset, confirming that the clusters reflect meaningful clinical structures rather than projection artifacts.

Reconstruction performance and feature-specific accuracy

The enhanced model, utilizing a deeper network and a weighted-loss scheme, exhibits improved performance on the unseen test set, with MAE 0.402 and RMSE 0.529. Additionally, random forest regression with latent vectors demonstrated strong predictive performance for clinical attachment loss, achieving an RMSE of 0.369, despite not explicitly including CAL in the features.

The top two latent dimensions (dimensions 9 and 8) account for nearly 30% and 20% of the model's total importance, respectively, while the remaining dimensions contribute less than 10% each. This highlights that a few dimensions dominate predictive power, emphasizing the model's sparsity and interpretability for clinical use.

GraphCROC demonstrated strong reconstruction accuracy on the hold-out test set, with a mean absolute error (MAE) of 0.402 and an R-squared (R2) of 0.607, indicating that it explains approximately 61% of the variance in features for unseen patients. While this R2 is lower than that of an unweighted autoencoder (approximately 0.69), the model improved its MAE and RMSE by 10-12% after feature weighting, thereby enhancing reconstruction fidelity, especially for plaque levels, which saw a significant rise in R2 from 0.03 to 0.44. Most other variables also maintained high R2 values, ranging from 0.5 to 0.8. Additionally, GraphCROC's latent codes were tested for predictive value in a downstream task using a random forest regressor to predict CAL, achieving an RMSE of 0.369, outperforming baseline models. This suggests that the latent features retain clinically relevant information that is potentially useful for risk-scoring models while preserving patient privacy.

Discussion

This study introduces GraphCROC, a graph-based deep learning model that integrates clinical and radiographic features to improve the clustering and interpretability of periodontal disease phenotypes. Compared to conventional models, such as VAEs, DEC, and standard GCNs, GraphCROC demonstrated superior performance in reconstructing clinically important, low-variance variables, including plaque levels, while also preserving meaningful disease representations in the latent space. These findings are consistent with recent efforts in biomedical graph learning that emphasize the value of integrating structural and attribute information for enhanced phenotype discovery.11,12 The dual-stream architecture of GraphCROC allowed it to capture both global patterns (e.g., inter-tooth relationships) and local node-specific variations (e.g., probing depth, plaque levels). Prior work by Kipf and Welling13 highlighted the limitations of traditional GCNs in modeling attribute-heavy biomedical data, particularly when graph topology is redundant or homogeneous across subjects—an issue observed in our data as well. By contrast, GraphCROC leveraged edge features and a weighted loss function to emphasize clinically relevant dimensions, thereby mitigating the common issue of “variance overshadowing” observed in unweighted VAEs.14 The stability and interpretability of GraphCROC's latent representations mark another key advancement (Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5)(Table 2, Table 3, Table 4, Table 5)

Disentangled and feature-specific decoding has been shown to improve transparency in models applied to healthcare.15,16 GraphCROC's ability to associate individual latent dimensions with distinct clinical features (e.g., one dimension affecting plaque and horizontal bone loss) provides clinicians with actionable insights—an interpretability advantage not typically available in models like DEC or standard VAEs.17 GraphCROC also outperformed DEC in clustering performance, as evidenced by the higher silhouette score (0.20 vs 0.18). Interestingly, although DEC optimizes an explicit clustering loss, it primarily clusters based on high-variance features, such as CAL and PD, often ignoring subtler distinctions. This limitation is supported by work from Min et al.,18 who found that DEC-based embeddings often lack semantic granularity in biomedical applications. By contrast, GraphCROC's representation captured both inflammatory activity and historical damage, yielding more clinically interpretable clusters—similar to the disease subtyping models proposed by Choi et al..19 While attention-based GNNs (e.g., GATs) are gaining popularity for medical applications for their ability to assign importance weights,20 we observed high instability in attention scores across training runs. Similar instability has been reported in prior work, where attention weights were sensitive to noise in small datasets.21 In contrast, GraphCROC generated stable cluster-level patterns—e.g., one cluster representing “high plaque and vertical defects”—enabling treatment pathways (e.g., phase I therapy or regenerative surgery) to be mapped directly from the latent space.22 Moreover, GraphCROC's lightweight design (∼40k parameters and <0.2s/epoch training time) makes it more feasible for integration into real-world clinical workflows than heavier alternatives, such as GCN + GAT, which show diminishing returns with added complexity. Similar conclusions were drawn by Thomas et al.,23 who noted that deep graph models must balance performance and clinical usability. GraphCROC's architecture aligns with this design philosophy, requiring no iterative clustering or adjacency recomputation during training. Additionally, the proposed model can assist periodontists by automatically analyzing radiographs and clinical data to generate interpretable maps of bone-loss patterns. It highlights high-risk anterior or posterior sites, supporting more objective diagnosis. and personalized treatment planning within existing clinical and genomic workflows.24

Clinically, the identified phenotypes can guide patient-specific periodontal management. Patients with high plaque and active inflammation may need intensive cause-related therapy and closer monitoring, while residual structural destruction with lower inflammation may require site-specific regenerative or reconstructive approaches. Maintenance-dominant phenotypes may be managed through individualized supportive therapy. Thus, GraphCROC enhances traditional assessments by translating clinical and bone-loss patterns into understandable risk profiles.

In practical dental workflows, the GraphCROC model can be embedded within electronic periodontal charting systems to visually flag anterior or posterior regions with higher risk, as derived from latent-space representations. Such integration enables clinicians to employ AI-based stratification as a supportive tool for case diagnosis, treatment selection, and individualized maintenance scheduling. Importantly, the identification of three distinct clusters — (1) high plaque with active inflammation, (2) residual destruction with moderate inflammation, and (3) well-maintained, low-inflammation cases — has tangible implications for clinical triage. This aligns with stratification strategies proposed in periodontal precision medicine,25 in which patients are categorized not only by disease stage but also by ongoing risk and response potential. Cluster-specific management (e.g., regenerative therapy for residual destruction vs maintenance therapy for low-risk cases) has the potential to personalize care and optimize outcomes. GraphCROC also opens opportunities for privacy-preserving multi-center analytics. By converting raw features into latent vectors, patient data can be shared without revealing identifiable health records—a method increasingly adopted in federated healthcare learning.26,27 The modest silhouette score (0.20) reflects limited cluster distinctiveness, likely due to sample size and feature variability. Future studies using expanded datasets and semi-supervised frameworks could enhance cluster separation and model robustness. The application of this information in clinical practice can be very useful for making decisions related to periodontics. For example, patients presenting with the phenotype of the high-plaque and high-inflammatory nature might need more intensive plaque control and periodontal therapy in the etiology-oriented mode along with more frequent recall periods because of their persistent inflammatory status. Patients with considerable attachment and structure loss but low current inflammatory activity need careful assessment to decide on the necessity of any regenerative procedures after the adequate elimination of inflammation. Phenotype of maintenance type with low disease activity of inflammatory and structural nature can be helped mainly with individualized supportive periodontal care. Consequently, GraphCROC could be used as a tool for decision support that takes into account many different factors, including inflammation and bone loss.

Repeated analyses and paired tests were conducted to improve statistical reporting, but the small sample size and few repetitions limit the ability to draw inferences. The differences between GraphCROC and baseline models are preliminary and hypothesis-generating. Larger datasets with repeated validation are needed to confirm these findings. Our preliminary results, which predict CAL from latent embeddings, highlight the potential for such compressed vectors to serve as quantitative phenotypes, as suggested in recent studies on disease endophenotyping using graph-based encoders.28 However, several limitations remain. The dataset size (90 patients) is relatively small, and although the model generalized well during internal validation, larger, more diverse cohorts are needed to assess external reproducibility. Similar concerns have been raised in early biomedical graph learning literature, emphasizing the need for population-level validation.29 Although standard machine-learning metrics were used to evaluate the model, no formal statistical validation, such as confidence intervals or hypothesis testing, was performed. Results are preliminary, and future studies should include bootstrap confidence intervals, formal tests, and external validation. Furthermore, while our empirical weighting scheme improved performance, future work could explore automated or learned weighting mechanisms to enhance performance further.

Conclusion

GraphCROC illustrates the unique capacity of combining clinical periodontal metrics and spatial patterns of anterior-to-posterior bone loss, highlighting three distinct clinical phenotypes that might otherwise remain undetectable via traditional one-parameter evaluation methods. By differentiating between active inflammation and residual destruction, the model facilitates a biological rationale for personalized risk stratification and treatment planning. The enhanced capability of reconstruction and prediction of CAL supports the potential use of latent space phenotyping in clinical decision support in the future; nevertheless, these early observations still need further validation in large multi-center longitudinal studies.
