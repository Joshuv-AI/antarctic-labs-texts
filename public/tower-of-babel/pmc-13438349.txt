Real-world analysis of perioperative strategies for driver gene-positive resectable non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Xingpeng Wang, Kaiyue Wang, Baiyang Huang, Jiarui Zhao, Min Li, Jingyu Zhu, Jing Xu, Chaoran Zhu, Zihan Liu, Xiaohan Wang, Menglin Bai, Xue Meng, Jinming Yu, Chenggang Wang, Guoxin Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:The optimal perioperative treatment strategy for driver gene-positive non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) remains unclear.Objectives:To compare the prognostic outcomes of different perioperative strategies for patients with resectable driver gene-positive NSCLC.Design:Multicenter retrospective observational cohort study.Methods:We retrospectively evaluated patients with resectable AJCC 8th clinical stage II–IIIB driver gene-positive NSCLC who underwent neoadjuvant therapy or upfront surgery with adjuvant targeted therapy (US + ATT) at two institutions between 2021 and 2023. Treatment was classified as neoadjuvant targeted therapy (NTT), neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy (NCIT), neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NCT), or US + ATT. The primary endpoints were event-free survival (EFS) and overall survival (OS). Survival was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared with the log-rank test.Results:In total, 290 patients were included; the median follow-up was 31.2 months. Compared with NCT, NCIT was associated with longer EFS (p = 0.048) and a nonsignificant favorable OS trend (p = 0.135). NCIT also achieved higher pathological complete response (29.8% vs 4.9%) and major pathological response (MPR; 52.1% vs 16.4%) rates and comparable R0 resection rates (96.8% vs 93.4%). Survival outcomes were comparable between NCIT and NCT for the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-mutant subgroup, but NTT showed better EFS (p = 0.093), without an OS advantage. NTT + chemotherapy achieved superior EFS (p = 0.038) and favorable OS trend (p = 0.146) versus NCT alone and longer EFS versus NTT alone (p = 0.062). Postoperative ATT did not confer survival advantage over no ATT in the NTT + chemotherapy subgroup. MPR to NCIT correlated with improved EFS (p = 0.001) and OS (p = 0.009), while MPR to NTT correlated with improved EFS (p = 0.049).Conclusion:NCIT was associated with improved EFS in resectable driver gene-positive NSCLC, although a corresponding OS improvement was not observed. NTT + chemotherapy may offer better prognosis than NCT alone in EGFR‑mutant NSCLC.

Introduction

Lung cancer is the most common malignancy and the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide. The majority of lung cancer cases are non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), accounting for approximately 80%–85%. 1 Further, 60% of non-squamous NSCLCs harbor driver gene mutations, with epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations comprising nearly half of these. 2 Among patients with NSCLC, 25%–30% are eligible for surgical resection at diagnosis. 3 For resectable NSCLC patients with EGFR-sensitive mutations, osimertinib is associated with significantly longer disease-free survival (DFS) than placebo (65.8 vs 21.9 months; hazard ratio (HR) = 0.23; p < 0.001) and an improved 5-year overall survival (OS) rate from 73% to 85%.4,5 In addition to EGFR-directed adjuvant therapy, the phase III ALINA trial showed that adjuvant alectinib significantly improved DFS compared with platinum-based chemotherapy in patients with completely resected stage IB–IIIA anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK)-positive NSCLC. 6 Together, these findings support adjuvant targeted therapy (ATT) as an important component of perioperative management for selected patients with oncogene-driven NSCLC, although recommendations vary according to driver alteration, disease stage, and regulatory setting.2,7

Neoadjuvant therapy, including neoadjuvant targeted therapy (NTT) for oncogene-driven tumors, has recently become a research focus for early stage cancers owing to its advantages of early micrometastatic control and improved R0 resection rates. 3 It also allows drug sensitivity assessment and can guide subsequent therapy. For patients with resectable NSCLC with driver gene mutations, growing evidence supports the use of NTT. The NEOS study, which involved 38 patients with resectable stage II–IIIB EGFR-mutant NSCLC treated with neoadjuvant osimertinib, reported an objective response rate (ORR) of 71.1% and a major pathological response (MPR) rate of 10.7%. 8 The NeoADAURA phase III trial further supported the efficacy of neoadjuvant osimertinib with or without platinum-based chemotherapy in patients with resectable EGFR-mutant stage II–IIIB NSCLC. Compared with chemotherapy alone, osimertinib plus chemotherapy and osimertinib monotherapy were associated with significantly higher MPR rates (26% and 25% vs 2%). 9 However, although NTT has shown preliminary therapeutic potential, most studies have reported immature survival data. Further studies with longer follow-up are warranted to validate its clinical efficacy and to identify the patient populations most likely to benefit.

The widespread use of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) in recent years has led to significant changes in the perioperative treatment strategies for resectable NSCLC. Several clinical trials, such as CheckMate 816, 10 AEGEAN, 11 KEYNOTE-671, 12 and CheckMate 77T, 13 have demonstrated that compared with chemotherapy alone, neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NCT) combined with programmed death 1 (PD-1)/programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) inhibitors is associated with significantly better MPR and/or pathological complete response (pCR) rates and event-free survival (EFS). The planned OS analysis of CheckMate 816 further demonstrated a statistically significant OS benefit with neoadjuvant nivolumab plus chemotherapy versus chemotherapy alone in patients without known EGFR/ALK alterations. 14 As such, it is recommended as a first-line perioperative strategy by the NCCN. 7 However, because most clinical trials of neoadjuvant ICIs excluded patients with EGFR or ALK alterations, the efficacy of neoadjuvant immunotherapy in these subgroups remains unclear.

Given that EGFR-mutant NSCLC typically exhibits lower tumor mutational burden (TMB), reduced PD-L1 expression, and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, its responsiveness to immunotherapy is generally limited. 15 However, the CTONG2104 trial recently reported a promising MPR rate (44%) with neoadjuvant sintilimab plus chemotherapy in resectable EGFR-mutant NSCLC, with a manageable safety profile. 16 Similarly, a multicenter retrospective analysis reported encouraging MPR (37.5%) and pCR (12.5%) rates in 40 patients with oncogene-mutant NSCLC receiving neoadjuvant immunotherapy. 17 Although current evidence is limited, these findings suggest that neoadjuvant immunotherapy may confer potential clinical benefits in driver gene-altered NSCLC.

However, there is currently no consensus regarding the optimal perioperative treatment strategy for resectable NSCLC with driver gene alterations, and the populations that are most likely to benefit remain unclear. Thus, this study aimed to compare the outcomes of various perioperative regimens, including targeted therapy, immunotherapy, and chemotherapy, and explore potentially more effective treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

Study design and patients

This multicenter, retrospective observational cohort study evaluated patients with resectable clinical stage II–IIIB driver gene-positive NSCLC, staged according to the AJCC 8th edition, who underwent neoadjuvant therapy and upfront surgery (US) with ATT at Shandong Cancer Hospital and Institute (Shandong, China) or Qilu Hospital of Shandong University (Shandong, China) between January 2021 and December 2023. Stage IB disease was excluded to maintain a more homogeneous cohort of stage II–IIIB patients for whom perioperative systemic therapy is more consistently recommended. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age 18–75 years; (2) histologically or cytologically confirmed NSCLC; (3) sensitizing oncogenic alterations; and (4) receiving neoadjuvant therapy before radical surgery or US followed by ATT. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) other malignancies, (2) distant metastatic lesions, (3) multiple lines of preoperative therapy, (4) major organ dysfunction, (5) concomitant autoimmune disease, and (6) incomplete survival data. Treatment strategies

Because this was a retrospective study, treatment was not assigned according to a prespecified protocol. Treatment strategies were determined by a multidisciplinary team based on routine clinical practice, disease stage, driver alteration type, tumor burden, and performance status. Patients received one of four approaches: NTT, NCT, neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy (NCIT), or US followed by ATT. Among neoadjuvant options, treatment selection was primarily guided by the need for tumor downstaging and conversion to resectability, with the specific regimen individualized according to molecular characteristics, disease burden, and patient and physician preference. US + ATT was reserved for patients with early-stage resectable disease for whom immediate surgery was considered optimal.

Observed neoadjuvant regimens comprised platinum-based doublet chemotherapy with or without PD-(L)1 blockade, or alteration-matched tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs). Postoperatively, patients in the US + ATT group received adjuvant TKIs according to molecular status. In the remaining groups, adjuvant treatment was administered according to institutional practice, guided by pathological findings and molecular characteristics. Although the agents used were approved for relevant advanced-disease or other clinical indications, their use in the perioperative setting was largely off-label during most of the study period. Detailed regimens and targeted agents are summarized in Supplemental Table 2.

Assessments and follow-up

Driver gene alterations were identified by next-generation sequencing using DNA extracted from biopsy tissue sections or peripheral blood samples. Treatment response during neoadjuvant therapy was assessed every two cycles until completion of therapy, according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1. Treatment-related adverse events (AEs) were evaluated according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0. Pathological response was independently evaluated by at least two experienced pathologists blinded to treatment assignment and clinical outcomes. Discordant assessments were resolved by consensus review. Inter-institutional consistency was maintained through adoption of predefined harmonized criteria based on established guidelines and cross-institutional validation of representative cases. Postoperatively, the patients were followed up every 3 months for the first 2 years and every 6 months thereafter. The last follow-up date was October 31, 2025. The primary endpoints were EFS and OS. EFS was defined as the time from diagnosis to disease progression, recurrence, death, or the date of last follow-up for patients without an EFS event. OS was defined as the time from diagnosis to death from any cause or the date of last follow-up for patients who were alive. The secondary endpoints were the pCR rate (defined as the absence of viable tumor cells in both the resected primary tumor and all sampled regional lymph nodes) and the MPR rate (defined as ⩽10% residual viable tumor cells in surgical specimens).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as medians (ranges), with means (standard deviations) reported where appropriate, and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies (%) and analyzed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared between groups using log-rank tests. To ensure comparability, 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) was performed between the NCIT and NCT cohorts based on age, sex, smoking history, ECOG score, T stage, pathological type, PD-L1 expression level (%), and gene mutation status, with a separate 1:1 PSM analysis conducted between the NTT and US + ATT groups based on age, sex, smoking history, ECOG score, T stage, pathological type, and use of third-generation targeted therapy. Multivariate analysis was performed using the Cox proportional hazards model. Survival curves were generated using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.1.2; GraphPad Software, LLC, Boston, Massachusetts, USA). All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 26.0; IBM Corporation, Armonk, New York, USA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Baseline patient characteristics

A total of 290 patients were included. In the overall cohort, the median age was 60 years (range, 36–75), and 64.5% (187/290) of patients were male. The predominant histological subtype was adenocarcinoma, accounting for 88.6% (257/290) of cases. Clinical stages included stage II in 33.8% (98/290) and stage IIIA–IIIB in 66.2% (192/290) of patients. The most common driver alteration was EGFR mutation, detected in 72.4% (210/290) of patients, followed by KRAS mutation in 11.7% (34/290), ALK/ROS1/RET rearrangement in 10.0% (29/290), and other alterations in 5.9% (17/290). Among the overall cohort, 94, 61, 43, and 92 received NCIT, NCT, NTT, and US + ATT treatment, respectively. The observed treatment regimens included platinum-based chemotherapy, PD-(L)1 blockade, and alteration-matched TKIs, including both EGFR-TKIs and ALK-TKIs (Supplemental Table 2). The median follow-up period for the total population was 31.2 months.

Comparison of outcomes between NCIT and NCT

For the NCIT and NCT comparison, male patients accounted for 64.9% (61/94) of the patients in the NCIT group and 63.9% (39/61) in the NCT group. In total, 68.1% (64/94) and 65.6% (40/61) of the patients in the NCIT and NCT groups had stage III disease before treatment, respectively. Further, 89.3% (84/94) and 86.9% (53/61) of the patients in the NCIT and NCT groups had adenocarcinoma, respectively. The distribution of major driver alterations was also comparable between the two groups (overall p = 0.174). EGFR mutations were observed in 42 (44.7%) versus 38 (62.3%), KRAS mutations in 23 (24.5%) versus 11 (18.0%), and ALK/ROS1/RET fusions in 14 (14.9%) versus 7 (11.5%) in the NCIT and NCT groups, respectively. Overall, the baseline patient characteristics were not significantly different between the two groups (Table 1). After PSM, baseline characteristics remained well balanced between the NCIT and NCT groups (Supplemental Table 3).

Analysis in the EGFR mutation subgroup

Given that EGFR mutations accounted for most driver alterations (72.4%, 210/290), further analyses were conducted in the EGFR-mutant subgroup. To ensure baseline comparability between the NTT and US + ATT groups, we performed a 1:1 PSM in the two cohorts. Ultimately, the NCIT group included 42 patients; NCT, 38 patients; NTT, 40 patients; and US + ATT, 36 patients after PSM. Compared with the NCT group, the NTT group showed a trend toward better EFS (HR = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.32–1.10, p = 0.093), but no significant difference in OS was observed (HR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.26–1.33, p = 0.203). Upon further stratification of NTT, NTT + chemotherapy was associated with significantly better EFS (HR = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.23–0.91, p = 0.038) and a trend toward longer OS (HR = 0.48, 95% CI: 0.19–1.18, p = 0.146) compared with NCT alone. Compared with NTT alone, NTT + chemotherapy was also associated with a trend toward better EFS (HR = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.16–1.17, p = 0.062), whereas OS remained similar (HR = 0.56, 95% CI: 0.14–2.30, p = 0.387). Meanwhile, the EFS and OS of the NTT with or without chemotherapy subgroup were not significantly different from those of the NCIT group (Figure 3(e) and (f)). The 2-year EFS rates in the NTT alone and NTT + chemotherapy subgroups were 46.2% and 70.4%, respectively, and the corresponding 2-year OS rates were 69.2% and 88.9%, respectively. In the NTT + chemotherapy subgroup, postoperative ATT did not yield a significant survival benefit over no postoperative ATT (Figure 3(g) and (h)). Subgroup analyses by EGFR driver mutations in exons 19 and 21 showed no significant differences in EFS or OS across the four treatment groups (Supplemental Figure 1). The pCR rates in the NTT, NCIT, and NCT groups were 10.0%, 16.7%, and 2.6%, respectively; the MPR rates were 27.5%, 35.7%, and 13.2%, respectively. R0 resection was achieved in 95.0%, 97.6%, 92.1%, and 94.4% of patients in the NTT, NCIT, NCT, and US + ATT groups, respectively. For patients with KRAS mutations who received NCIT, the pCR and MPR rates were 39.1% (9/23) and 60.9% (14/23), respectively.

Subgroup analysis by pathological response and PD-L1 expression

Pathological response was assessed in patients who underwent surgery after neoadjuvant therapy. In this pathological-response evaluable cohort, the overall pCR and MPR rates were 18.2% (36/198) and 35.9% (71/198), respectively. Patients who achieved pCR had longer EFS than those who did not achieve pCR (HR = 0.13, 95% CI: 0.08–0.22, p < 0.001), and OS was also improved (HR = 0.07, 95% CI: 0.04–0.13, p < 0.001; Supplemental Figure 2(A) and (B)). Similarly, patients who achieved MPR had longer EFS (HR = 0.37, 95% CI: 0.24–0.55, p < 0.001) and improved OS (HR = 0.37, 95% CI: 0.22–0.64, p = 0.002) compared with those who did not achieve MPR (Supplemental Figure 2(C) and (D)).

Among patients who achieved pCR, 2 of 28 patients in the NCIT group developed postoperative recurrence (at 9.6 and 23.6 months). In the NCT group, one of three patients who achieved pCR experienced disease progression (at 5.8 months) and died (at 12.8 months). In the NTT group, one of five patients who achieved pCR experienced disease progression after surgery (at 25.0 months). All other patients who achieved pCR were alive and disease-free at the last follow-up. Because pCR and MPR were achieved by relatively few patients in the NCT group, subsequent treatment-specific survival analyses according to pathological response focused on the NCIT and NTT groups.

In the NCIT group, those who achieved MPR demonstrated significantly improved EFS (HR = 0.35, 95% CI: 0.18–0.65, p = 0.001) and OS (HR = 0.33, 95% CI: 0.14–0.74, p = 0.009; Figure 4(a) and (b)). In the NTT group, those who achieved MPR experienced markedly longer EFS than their counterparts who did not (HR = 0.31, 95% CI: 0.13–0.78, p = 0.049); however, OS did not differ significantly between them. The EFS and OS of the NTT group were not significantly different from those of the US + ATT group regardless of MPR status (Figure 4(c) and (d)). Among patients with EGFR‑mutant disease in the NCIT group, achieving MPR was associated with a trend toward improved EFS (HR = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.18–1.01, p = 0.088) but not with OS (Figure 4(e) and (f)).

PD-L1 expression was evaluated by immunohistochemistry in 79 of the 94 patients (84.0%). Among them, 19 (20.2%) patients showed PD-L1 <1% and 60 (63.8%) showed PD-L1 ⩾1%. Survival was then compared between patients with PD-L1 ⩾1% and those with PD-L1 <1% in the NCIT group. No significant differences in EFS or OS were observed between the patients (Supplemental Figure 3(A) and (B)), and similar results were obtained for the EGFR-mutant subgroup (Supplemental Figure 3(C) and (D)). Furthermore, Spearman’s correlation analysis revealed no significant association between PD-L1 expression and pathological response, although a negative correlation was observed (r = −0.180, p = 0.113; Supplemental Figure 3(E)).

Safety

Overall, 91.0% of the total population developed AEs, the most common of which were anemia, nausea, fatigue, and vomiting (Supplemental Table 5). In the NCT group, 88.5% developed AEs, including anemia, fatigue, nausea, and vomiting. Meanwhile, 92.6% of the patients in the NCIT group developed AEs; these included anemia, fatigue, nausea, and vomiting. Among those with EGFR-mutant disease, 89.5%, 85.7%, 80.0%, and 83.3% in the NCT, NCIT, NTT, and US + ATT (PSM) groups developed AEs, respectively. Grade ⩾3 AEs were reported in 21.1%, 19.0%, 12.5%, and 16.7% of the patients in these groups, respectively. No grade 4 or 5 AEs were reported, and no new toxicity symptoms were observed.

Discussion

The optimal perioperative strategy for patients with resectable oncogene-positive NSCLC and the subpopulations most likely to benefit from specific treatments remain undefined. The current study shows that NCIT is associated with longer EFS and a favorable but statistically nonsignificant OS trend in comparison to conventional NCT for patients with driver gene-positive resectable NSCLC. Among those with EGFR mutations, NTT is associated with a trend toward better EFS than NCT, but there is no significant difference in OS. Furthermore, compared with NCT alone, NTT combined with chemotherapy is associated with significantly better EFS and a trend toward longer OS. This suggests that adding targeted therapy to chemotherapy in the neoadjuvant setting may offer additional survival benefits for EGFR-mutant NSCLC. Within the NTT plus chemotherapy subgroup, postoperative ATT was not associated with improved survival. This finding should be interpreted with caution. The limited sample size and low event rate may have reduced the statistical power to detect a benefit. Moreover, prior exposure to targeted therapy in the neoadjuvant setting may have achieved early systemic disease control, thereby attenuating the incremental effect of postoperative treatment. Neoadjuvant TKI use may also reshape the clonal landscape of residual disease, potentially diminishing its sensitivity to subsequent targeted therapy. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first real-world study to comprehensively assess the diverse perioperative strategies for driver gene-positive resectable NSCLC. Although the findings should be interpreted with caution, they provide valuable real-world data to better understand the prognostic implications of different perioperative approaches. In addition, the findings provide preliminary evidence that may help refine individualized treatment planning for patients with driver gene alterations.

Previous studies, including the EMERGING-CTONG 1103 study, 19 the NEOS study, 8 and the phase II study by Blakely et al., 20 that examined the neoadjuvant use of TKIs in EGFR-positive NSCLC reported pCR and MPR rates ranging from 0% to 12.1% and from 0% to 24.2%, respectively. Notably, all these neoadjuvant regimens involved TKI monotherapy, which may have contributed to the limited pathological response. Subsequent studies have accordingly explored the efficacy of combining neoadjuvant EGFR-TKIs with other therapeutic modalities. Recently, the large-scale phase III randomized controlled NeoADAURA trial reported their initial findings, showing MPR rates of 26%, 25%, and 2% in the osimertinib plus chemotherapy, osimertinib monotherapy, and placebo plus chemotherapy groups, respectively. The preliminary 12-month EFS rates were 93%, 95%, and 83% (15% data maturity), respectively. 9 In our study, the MPR rates in the EGFR-mutant subgroup in the NTT combined with chemotherapy, NTT alone, and NCT alone groups were 53%, 25%, and 13.2%, respectively, and the corresponding 12-month EFS rates were 85.2%, 76.9%, and 65.8%, respectively. These trends are similar to those observed in the NeoADAURA trial. We look forward to the mature survival results from NeoADAURA, which are expected to further clarify the efficacy of neoadjuvant-targeted combination therapy.

Prospective studies on NCIT for resectable oncogene-positive NSCLC remain limited. The NEOTIDE (CTONG2104) trial, a two-stage, open-label, single-center phase II study, evaluated neoadjuvant immunotherapy plus chemotherapy in early stage EGFR-mutant NSCLC and reported interim results. The MPR and pCR rates were 34.3% and 11.4%, respectively, and the 12- and 18-month EFS rates were 90.7% and 71.9%, respectively, among 35 patients with stage IIB−IIIB (N2) disease. 16 Additionally, a multicenter pooled analysis assessing PD-1 blockade combined with chemotherapy in oncogene-positive NSCLC reported pCR and MPR rates of 10.5% and 42.1%, respectively, in EGFR mutations. 17 Consistent findings were observed in our study. For patients with EGFR-mutant disease in the NCIT group, the pCR and MPR rates were 16.7% and 35.7%, respectively, and the 18-month EFS rate was 66.7%.

Targeted therapies are not uniformly available across driver alterations in the perioperative setting. During most of the study period, routine perioperative targeted therapy was largely EGFR-directed, and no KRAS-targeted therapy was approved or administered for early-stage KRAS-mutant NSCLC. The ALINA trial has now expanded adjuvant targeted-therapy evidence to resected ALK-positive NSCLC, 6 but evidence for neoadjuvant or perioperative TKIs across other driver alterations remains limited. Recent and ongoing studies, including NeoADAURA and platform trials such as NAUTIKA1, are exploring targeted therapy in the neoadjuvant or perioperative setting for EGFR and other actionable alterations.3,9 These studies may help define whether driver-matched TKIs can improve pathological response and long-term survival beyond selected EGFR-mutant populations.

Pathological response serves as both an efficacy measure and a prognostic indicator in the neoadjuvant setting, showing considerable clinical potential. 21 In the CheckMate 816 trial, patients who achieved pCR had markedly longer EFS than those who did not achieve pCR. 10 A recent review of neoadjuvant NSCLC trials further indicated that the MPR is a more reliable predictor of long-term OS than the ORR. In our cohort of driver gene-positive NSCLC patients, those who achieved MPR had superior EFS and OS, regardless of whether they received NCIT or NTT. In the EGFR-mutant NCIT subgroup, MPR was also associated with a trend toward improved EFS, although OS did not differ significantly. However, this was likely because of the limited sample size and relatively short follow-up period. Overall, these findings support pathologic response as a promising prognostic marker in this population.

PD-L1 is a reliable predictive biomarker for immunotherapy. 22 However, in the present study, PD-L1 expression was not significantly associated with survival outcomes. This discrepancy may be attributable to the small sample size, spatiotemporal heterogeneity of PD-L1 expression, and limitations of the current detection methods in accurately capturing dynamic changes. Further studies integrating longitudinal sampling and advanced detection technologies are warranted to clarify the predictive value of PD-L1 expression.

This study had some limitations. First, its retrospective design and limited sample size, particularly within certain subgroups, may have introduced selection bias and reduced the statistical power. Second, heterogeneity in treatment regimens and targeted agents across centers could have affected the comparability of the outcomes. Third, the median follow-up of 31.2 months may be insufficient to fully assess long-term survival, particularly OS. Finally, as majority of the patients had EGFR mutations, our findings may not be generalizable to patients with other oncogenic alterations. Prospective multicenter studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up periods are warranted to validate the findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, for patients with resectable driver gene-positive NSCLC, NCIT is associated with improved EFS compared with conventional NCT, although a statistically significant OS improvement was not observed. These findings suggest that NCIT may represent a promising neoadjuvant strategy, warranting further validation in prospective studies. Among patients with EGFR mutations, the combination of EGFR-TKIs and NCT provides greater survival benefits than NCT alone. This indicates that this combined approach may represent a more effective perioperative treatment option. The pathological response following NCIT or NTT is correlated with prognosis, highlighting its potential as a useful indicator for prognostic prediction. These findings provide real-world evidence that may enhance our understanding of prognostic differences across perioperative strategies and support individualized treatment planning for patients with resectable driver gene-positive NSCLC. Future large-scale studies are needed to clarify the long-term efficacy of these strategies and identify the patient populations that are most likely to benefit.
