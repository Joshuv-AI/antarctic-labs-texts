Characterising exchange of stability in scalar reaction-diffusion equations via geometric blow-up
S. Jelbart, C. Kuehn & A. Mart\́mathrm{i}nez Sánchez

Abstract
We study the exchange of stability in scalar reaction-diffusion equations which feature a slow passage through transcritical and pitchfork type singularities in the reaction term, using a novel adaptation of the geometric blow-up method. Our results are consistent with known results on bounded spatial domains which were obtained by Butuzov, Nefedov & Schneider using comparison principles like upper and lower solutions in [ 7 ] , however, from a methodological point of view, the approach is motivated by the analysis of closely related ODE problems using geometric blow-up presented by Krupa & Szmolyan in [ 34 ] . After applying the blow-up transformation, we obtain a system of PDEs which can be studied in local coordinate charts. Importantly, the blow-up procedure resolves a spectral degeneracy in which continuous spectrum along the entire negative real axis is ‘pushed back’ so as to create a spectral gap in the linearisation about particular steady states which arise within the so-called entry and exit charts. This makes it possible to extend slow-type invariant manifolds into and out of a neighbourhood of the singular point using center manifold theory, in a manner which is conceptually analogous to the established approach in the ODE setting. We expect that the approach can be adapted and applied to the study of dynamic bifurcations in PDEs in a wide variety of different contexts.



Keywords:
Geometric blow-up, Reaction-diffusion equations, Dynamic bifurcation, Slow passage, Exchange of stability

The primary aim of this article is to show that the geometric blow-up method can also be applied to the study of “slow passage” or “dynamic bifurcation” phenomena in scalar reaction-diffusion equations of the form

\mu>0

While arguments based on the identification of suitable upper and lower solutions can lead to elegant, succinct and rigorous results (as is the case in many of the works mentioned above), there are well-known methodological disadvantages to such approaches. In particular, comparison methods of this kind tend to become untenable in multi-component systems, and even for scalar equations, there is no systematic way to identify the ‘correct’ upper and lower solutions. In the ODE setting, the geometric blow-up method offers an established and far more constructive alternative, which can be adapted and applied in a wide variety of different contexts. In the particular case of system (

In this article, we provide a rigorous and geometric description of exchange of stability phenomena which mirror the results for bounded domains in
[

[
34
]

, which we then proceed to study in detail. The
main technical challenge stems from the fact that the geometric blow-up method is still being developed for PDEs. Our approach is based on recent work in

Crucially, the blow-up procedure allows us to resolve a spectral degeneracy which occurs in the static system (
1
)
|_{\varepsilon=0}
when

Following geometric blow-up and desingularization, the linearised equations in the phase-directional (entry/exit) charts exhibit a spectral gap property, whereby the essential spectrum is pushed into the left-half plane, and two point eigenvalues appear at the origin. We interpret this as a direct generalisation of the key mathematical advantage of the blow-up method in the ODE setting, namely, that it can be used to resolve the loss of hyperbolicity at a degenerate fixed point by mapping the problem to a blown-up space in which the dynamics are governed by a desingularised vector field that it is either hyperbolic or partially hyperbolic when evaluated at its singularities.

The spectral gap property allows us to analyse the dynamics in the phase-directional charts using center manifold theory. This is directly analogous to the approach taken in
[
34
]
, which is typical in problems of this kind, except that we work with PDEs and therefore require a variant of center manifold theory which applies in Banach spaces. The variant that we use is based on
[

, in the well-known formulation that has been presented and extended in
[

2

[

(where they can be viewed as extensions of Fenichel slow manifolds), except that in our case, they are embedded and strongly attracting in a suitable
(infinite-dimensional)

\varepsilon

[
34
]

In order to do so, we show that a priori estimates on the size of the separation between solutions and the connecting extended center manifold can be controlled using a direct but relatively constructive approach which is conceptually similar to the established approach to obtaining finite time approximation results for the validity of modulation equations as in, e.g.
[

32

36

40

More generally, the real power of the approach developed herein is that the methodology has a clear parallel to the established approach to the local analysis of dynamic or slow-fast singularities in ODE systems using geometric blow-up. Based on this fact, we are hopeful that the methods developed herein can – as they do in the ODE setting – be transferred and adapted in order to obtain rigorous and geometrically informative information about the dynamics close to a wide variety of dynamic bifurcations occurring in a wide variety of PDEs.

Remark 3.4
.

The

) and (

Theorems

and

can be viewed as (geometrically formulated) counterparts to the exchange of stability results which apply on bounded spatial domains, as presented in

. However, both theorems are proven using a novel adaption of the geometric blow-up method in Section

below and, from a conceptual and methodological point of view, it is more natural to view them as extensions of the corresponding results on fast-slow ODEs in

]
to the case of scalar reaction-diffusion equations. We reiterate that we consider the method of proof – as opposed to the particular results in Theorems

and

– to be our main contribution in this article. Nevertheless, Theorems
3.2
and

are of independent interest, and in the following we collect a number of observations which relate to their interpretation and implications:

a)

Equations (

), (
18
) and (

) show that solutions are exponentially close to one of the slow manifolds of spatially independent solutions from Lemma

in the
\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}
(and therefore also

) norm when they leave a neighbourhood of the origin. Indeed, the proof in Section

shows that after an initial transient which occurs for negative

-values bounded away from zero, the PDE solutions track the extension of

in the case of Theorem

, and the extension of

in the case of Theorem

, through an entire neighbourhood of the origin.

b)

In general,

still depends on

via the higher order terms in (

), (

) or (

), but this dependence is exponentially small in the

c)

The constant

can be obtained. In the case of Theorem

there exists an

and

d)

Corresponding statements for the original system (

) can be derived by inverting the linear and near-identity transformations used in the proof of Lemma

. In particular, the ‘threshold values’ of the parameter

are given by (

e)

Theorem

only covers the case

shows that solutions are also strongly attracted to a spatially homogeneous slow manifold in the “entry chart” when

. This leads to technical issues in the blow-up, which we have not yet been able to resolve (we refer Remark

below for more on this point).

f)

We expect to find spatio-temporal canard phenomena in both systems (

) and (

which shrinks to zero as

direct PDE solutions with

and

. The question of whether spatio-temporal canard phenomena can be understood via geometric blow-up in this context is open, and left for future work.

4
Proof via geometric blow-up

In order to resolve the spectral degeneracy described in Section
2
, we consider the extended system obtained by appending the trivial equation
\varepsilon^{\prime}=0
to system (
8
):

\begin{split}\partial_{t}u&=\partial_{x}^{2}u+u\mu-u^{s}+\varepsilon\lambda+\mathcal{R}^{(s)}(u,\mu,\varepsilon),\\
\dot{\mu}&=\varepsilon,\\
\dot{\varepsilon}&=0,\end{split}

(20)

which we shall hereafter view as an evolution equation on a Banach space. More precisely, we shall work with

\mathcal{X}:=\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}\times\mathbb{R}^{2},\qquad\mathcal{Y}:=\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}\times\mathbb{R}^{2},\qquad\mathcal{Z}:=\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}\times\mathbb{R}^{2},

(21)

where
\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}
is the polynomially weighted space defined in (
11
),
\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}
is the space defined in (
12
) and
\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}
is a polynomially weighted space defined in a similar fashion to
\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}
although only up to the first derivative, i.e.

\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}:=\left\{v\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{\textup{b,unif}}(\mathbb{R}):\|v\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}}<\infty\right\},

(22)

where

\|v(\cdot)\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}}=\|v(\cdot)\|_{\infty}+\|(1+|\cdot|)v^{(1)}(\cdot)\|_{\infty}.

The spaces
\mathcal{X}
,
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{Z}
are Banach spaces when endowed with the norms

\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{X}}=\|\cdot\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}+|(\cdot,\cdot)|,\qquad\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{Y}}=\|\cdot\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}}+|(\cdot,\cdot)|,\qquad\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{Z}}=\|\cdot\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}}+|(\cdot,\cdot)|,

respectively, and the inclusion map induces a natural continuous embedding
\mathcal{Z}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{Y}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{X}
. We now rewrite system (
20
) as

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\bm{u}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}={\mathrm{L}}\bm{u}+{\mathrm{N}}(\bm{u}),

where
\bm{u}=(u,\mu,\varepsilon)^{\textnormal{T}}\in\mathcal{Z}
, and
{\mathrm{L}}:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{X}
,
{\mathrm{N}}:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{Y}
are linear respectively nonlinear operators defined by

{\mathrm{L}}\bm{u}=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{x}^{2}u+\lambda\varepsilon\\
\varepsilon\\
0\end{pmatrix},\qquad N(\bm{u})=\begin{pmatrix}\mu u-u^{s}+\mathcal{R}^{(s)}(u,\mu,\varepsilon)\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

Remark 4.1
.

We choose
\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathcal{C}^{1}_{\textup{b,unif}}(\mathbb{R})
instead of
\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{X}
in (
21
) because the range of the nonlinearity which appears in the the phase-directional (entry/exit) charts of the blow-up analysis to come includes first derivatives in space. Choosing
\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathcal{C}^{1}_{\textup{b,unif}}(\mathbb{R})
allows us to work with the same choice of spaces throughout.

We now define a blow-up transformation

\Phi:\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathcal{Z}

via

\Phi:\left(\bar{u},(\bar{\mu},\bar{\varepsilon}),r\right)\ \mapsto\ \begin{cases}u=r\bar{u},\\
\mu=r^{s-1}\bar{\mu},\\
\varepsilon=r^{2(s-1)}\bar{\varepsilon},\end{cases}

(23)

where
\mathbb{S}^{1}
denotes the unit circle; in particular,
\bar{\mu}^{2}+\bar{\varepsilon}^{2}=1
. We also emphasise that the radial variable
r\geq 0
depends on time only, i.e. it is spatially independent, due to the relation
\mu=r^{s-1}\bar{\mu}
.

Remark 4.2
.

The fact that
r
only depends on time is a consequence of the fact that
\mu=\mu(0)+\varepsilon t
only depends on time. This is typical in dynamic bifurcation problems, i.e. in PDEs with ‘parameters’ which slowly vary in time (but not space). In more general PDE settings the radial variable
r
, which measures the ‘distance’ between a solution and the blow-up manifold, will depend on both time and space. This poses a significant analytical obstacle when it comes to the ‘spatial desingularisation’ of the blown-up problem; we refer to Remark
4.4
below.

Remark 4.3
.

In the ODE counterpart to the problem considered in
[
34
]
, a non-normally hyperbolic point at the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
(the extended phase space in which
\varepsilon
is considered as a variable) is blown-up to a
2
-sphere. In contrast, the blow-up transformation defined in (
23
) blows the set
\{(u,0,0):u\in\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}\}
up to a ‘cylinder’ which is naturally identified with
\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
, as shown in Figure
3
. The fact that the blown-up object is an infinite-dimensional object is a direct consequence of the fact that we are working in infinite-dimensional Banach spaces. However, the fact that the blown-up object is ‘cylindrical’ as opposed to ‘spherical’ is a consequence of the fact that we keep
\bar{u}
independent from
\bar{\mu}
and
\bar{\varepsilon}
, as opposed to requiring, for example, that

\bar{\mu}^{2}+\bar{\varepsilon}^{2}+\|\bar{u}\|^{2}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}}=1.

(24)

This simplifies the geometry and suffices for the problems considered herein, however it is worthy to note that the incorporation of
\bar{u}
into the blow-up via constraints such as (
24
) is expected to be necessary in the study of other singularities. The question of how to define coordinate charts which in some sense correspond to “
\bar{u}=\pm 1
” in this setting is an open problem.

-axis under
\Phi
in blue and red respectively (cf. Figure
1
), as well as the circular pre-image
\Phi^{-1}(\mathcal{Q})
of the singular point
\mathcal{Q}
(both are shown in bold green), which corresponds to either
T
or

in Figure

, depending on whether

or

respectively.

As is usual in blow-up analyses, we shall work in projective coordinates. Three coordinate charts are needed for the analysis, namely,

K_{1}:\bar{\mu}=-1,\qquad K_{2}:\bar{\varepsilon}=1,\qquad K_{3}:\bar{\mu}=1,

which (in accordance with conventions) we will often be referred to as the entry, rescaling and exit charts respectively. We denote local coordinates in each chart by

\begin{split}K_{1}&:(u,\mu,\varepsilon)=\left(r_{1}u_{1},-r_{1}^{s-1},r_{1}^{2(s-1)}\varepsilon_{1}\right),\\
K_{2}&:(u,\mu,\varepsilon)=\left(r_{2}u_{2},r_{2}^{s-1}\mu_{2},r_{2}^{2(s-1)}\right),\\
K_{3}&:(u,\mu,\varepsilon)=\left(r_{3}u_{3},r_{3}^{s-1},r_{3}^{2(s-1)}\varepsilon_{3}\right),\end{split}

and note that the change of coordinate formulae are given by

\begin{split}\kappa_{12}^{(s)}&:(u_{1},r_{1},\varepsilon_{1})=\left((-\mu_{2})^{-1/(s-1)}u_{2},(-\mu_{2})^{1/(s-1)}r_{2},\mu_{2}^{-2}\right),\\
\kappa_{23}^{(s)}&:(u_{2},r_{2},\mu_{2})=\left(\varepsilon_{3}^{-1/2(s-1)}u_{3},\varepsilon_{3}^{1/2(s-1)}r_{3},\varepsilon_{3}^{-1/2}\right),\end{split}

(25)

where the first (second) coordinate change is defined for
\mu_{2}<0
(
\varepsilon_{3}>0
).

4.1
The pitchfork case (
s=3
)

We consider the pitchfork case with

first. We proceed as usual by considering the dynamics in each coordinate chart
K_{i}
, before combining everything in order to prove Theorem

.

4.1.1
Chart
K_{1}

After rewriting system (
20
) in chart
K_{1}
coordinates and applying a state-dependent transformation of time and space which is defined via the relations

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}t_{1}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}=r_{1}^{2},\qquad\frac{x_{1}}{x}=r_{1},

(26)

we obtain a set of equations in chart
K_{1}
which can be written as

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\bm{u}_{1}}{{\mathrm{d}}t_{1}}={\mathrm{L}}_{1}\bm{u}_{1}+{\mathrm{N}}_{1}(\bm{u}_{1}),

(27)

where
\bm{u}_{1}=(u_{1},r_{1},\varepsilon_{1})
,
{\mathrm{L}}_{1}
is a linear operator defined via

{\mathrm{L}}_{1}\bm{u}_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{x_{1}}^{2}u_{1}-u_{1}\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix},

(28)

and
{\mathrm{N}}_{1}
is a nonlinear operator defined via

{\mathrm{N}}_{1}(\bm{u}_{1})=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{1}u_{1}-u_{1}^{3}+\lambda r_{1}\varepsilon_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{1}x_{1}\partial_{x_{1}}u_{1}+r_{1}\mathcal{R}_{1}(u_{1},r_{1},\varepsilon_{1})\\
-\frac{1}{2}r_{1}\varepsilon_{1}\\
2\varepsilon_{1}^{2}\end{pmatrix},

where
\mathcal{R}_{1}(u_{1},r_{1},\varepsilon_{1}):=r_{1}^{-4}\mathcal{R}(r_{1}u_{1},-r_{1}^{2},r_{1}^{4}\varepsilon_{1})
, which is well-defined and
\mathcal{O}(u_{1}^{2},r_{1}u_{1},r_{1}^{2}\varepsilon_{1})
due to the asymptotics in Lemma
2.2
Assertion (ii).

Remark 4.4
.

The additional transport term
-\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{1}x_{1}\partial_{x_{1}}u_{1}
appearing in the right-hand side of system (
27
) is a consequence of the change of independent variables from
(x,t)
in system (
20
), to
(x_{1},t_{1})
in system (
27
). Explicitly, the transformation
u(x,t)=r_{1}(t_{1})u_{1}(x_{1},t_{1})
, in combination with the relations in (
26
), implies that

\partial_{t}u=\frac{{\mathrm{d}}t_{1}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\partial_{t_{1}}(r_{1}u_{1})+\frac{{\mathrm{d}}x_{1}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\partial_{x_{1}}(r_{1}u_{1})=r_{1}^{2}\left[r_{1}\partial_{t_{1}}u_{1}+r_{1}^{\prime}u_{1}+x_{1}r_{1}^{\prime}\partial_{x_{1}}u_{1}\right],

(29)

where we use the fact that

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}x_{1}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}=\frac{{\mathrm{d}}x_{1}}{{\mathrm{d}}t_{1}}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}t_{1}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}=r_{1}x_{1}r_{1}^{\prime}.

Rearranging and evaluating (
29
) leads to the equation for
\partial_{t_{1}}u_{1}
in (
27
).

Similar observations and approaches can be found elsewhere in the literature. In their blow-up analysis of a PDE system posed on a bounded interval
[-a,a]
,
[
16
,
17
]
apply a state-dependent rescaling of the form
a=a_{1}/r_{1}
(or a suitable scaling thereof) instead of a spatial rescaling
x=x_{1}/r_{1}
. This leads to a free boundary problem (instead of introducing an additional transport term).
There is also a formal similarity between the transformation

and time-dependent transformations of space that are commonly applied in order to transform problems with moving boundaries to a coordinate system in which the boundary is fixed; see e.g.
[
44
]
for a recent application of this approach in setting which is closely related to the study of slow passage problems in PDEs.

Remark 4.5
.

Transformations that are similar to those in (
26
) are common in the study of finite-time blow-up effects by means of dynamic renormalization; see e.g.
[

,
10
,

,

]
. In this setting, the left-most transformation in (

) would typically be formulated as an integral transformation

t=\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\frac{1}{r_{1}(s)}{\mathrm{d}}s.

(30)

In our case it suffices to define
t_{1}
via in terms of
{\mathrm{d}}t/{\mathrm{d}}t_{1}
, which only determines
t_{1}
up to an additive constant, since the right-hand side in (

t_{1}\mapsto t_{1}+\alpha
for any real constant
\alpha
.

System (
27
) has a branch of spatially homogeneous steady states

\mathcal{P}_{1}=\left\{(0,r_{1},0):r_{1}\in[0,\nu]\right\}

where
\nu>0
is a small constant. For

,
\mathcal{P}_{1}
corresponds to the branch of steady states along

in the extended system (

, viewed in chart

. Our aim is to apply center manifold theory in a neighbourhood of the point
\mathcal{Q}_{1}:=(0,0,0)\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
, which lies at the intersection of

with the blow-up manifold (where

).

In order to apply center manifold theory, we need to check three hypotheses, namely, Hypotheses

,
2
and

in Appendix

, which correspond to Hypotheses 2.1, 2.4 and 2.15 in

respectively. In the following we aim to present a relatively complete treatment of each hypothesis in the particular context of our problem, however we shall refer occasionally to Appendix

for details.
The first of these – Hypothesis

– is easy to verify. Here we simply state the relevant properties without proof:

(i)

{\mathrm{L}}_{1}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{X})
;

(ii)

There exists a

and a neighbourhood
\mathcal{V}_{1}
of

in

such that
{\mathrm{N}}_{1}\in\mathcal{C}^{k}(\mathcal{V}_{1},\mathcal{Y})
,
{\mathrm{N}}_{1}(\mathcal{Q}_{1})=\bm{0}
and
{\mathrm{D}}{\mathrm{N}}_{1}(\mathcal{Q}_{1})=\bm{0}
.

Remark 4.6
.

In order to apply center manifold theory, we also need that
{\mathrm{L}}_{1}
is a closed linear operator. This follows from the fact that the resolvant set satisfies
\textup{Res}({\mathrm{L}}_{1})\neq\emptyset
(due to Lemma

below) and the closed graph theorem; see e.g.
[

.

Remark 4.7
.

The choice to restrict to polynomially weighted spaces
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{Z}
for which the first (and in the latter case also the second) derivative decays as

is made so that the requirement
{\mathrm{N}}_{1}\in\mathcal{C}^{k}(\mathcal{V}_{1},\mathcal{Y})
in property (ii) above is satisfied. In particular, the decay ensures that the transport term
\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{1}x_{1}\partial_{x_{1}}u_{1}
which appears in
{\mathrm{N}}_{1}(\bm{u}_{1})
is ‘higher order’ over the entire domain.

In order to verify the second hypothesis – Hypothesis
2
– we need to show that the spectrum associated with the linearised problem possesses a spectral gap. This is assured by the following result.

Lemma 4.8
.

The spectrum associated with the linear operator
{\mathrm{L}}_{1}
is given by
\sigma({\mathrm{L}}_{1})=\sigma_{-}({\mathrm{L}}_{1})\cup\sigma_{0}({\mathrm{L}}_{1})
, where

\sigma_{-}({\mathrm{L}}_{1})=(-\infty,-1],\qquad\sigma_{0}({\mathrm{L}}_{1})=\{0\}.

The
0
-eigenvalue has algebraic multiplicity
2
, and the corresponding center subspace
\mathcal{E}_{0}
is
2
-dimensional and spanned by the constant eigenfunctions
(0,1,0)^{\textnormal{T}}
and
(0,0,1)^{\textnormal{T}}
.

Proof.

We calculate the eigenvalues first. Let
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}
,
\bm{v_{1}}=(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})\in\mathcal{Z}
and consider the eigenvalue problem
{\mathrm{L}}_{1}\bm{v}_{1}=\zeta\bm{v}_{1}
or, equivalently,

\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}^{\prime\prime}(x_{1})-(1+\zeta)v_{1}(x_{1})\\
-\zeta v_{2}\\
-\zeta v_{3}\end{pmatrix}=\bm{0},

(31)

where
v_{1}^{\prime\prime}
denotes the second derivative of
v_{1}
with respect to
x_{1}
. We consider the cases
\zeta=0
and
\zeta\neq 0
separately.

If
\zeta=0
, then a direct calculation together with the requirement that
v_{1}\in\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}
is uniformly bounded shows that solutions of (
31
) must be of the form
\bm{v}_{1}=(0,v_{2},v_{3})
. Since
\bm{v}_{1}=(0,v_{2},v_{3})
is a solution for any choice of
(v_{2},v_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, it follows that
0\in\sigma_{0}({\mathrm{L}}_{1})
and that the eigenspace corresponding the
\zeta=0
is spanned by
(0,1,0)^{\textnormal{T}}
and
(0,0,1)^{\textnormal{T}}
.

If
\zeta\neq 0
, then equation (
31
) immediately yields the requirement
v_{2}=v_{3}=0
, implying that eigenfuctions (if they exist) must be of the form
\bm{v}_{1}=(v_{1},0,0)
. Directly solving the second order ODE for
v_{1}
, we obtain

v_{1}(x_{1})=\begin{cases}c_{0},&\zeta=-1,\\
c_{1}{\mathrm{e}}^{\sqrt{1+\zeta}x_{1}}+c_{2}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\sqrt{1+\zeta}x_{1}},&\zeta\neq-1,\end{cases}

for any constants
c_{0},c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
. It follows that
-1\in\sigma_{-}({\mathrm{L}}_{1})
, with constant eigenfunctions of the form
\bm{v}_{1}=(c_{0},0,0)
for any
c_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
. Other values with
\zeta\neq-1
are ruled out by the additional requirement that
v_{1}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}
, since the derivatives of
v_{1}(x_{1})
do not decay as
|x_{1}|\to\infty
. Thus, the eigenvalues are
\{-1,0\}
.

In order to determine the essential spectrum associated with
{\mathrm{L}}_{1}
, we need to determine the essential spectrum associated with the linear operator
\widetilde{\mathrm{L}}_{1}:=\partial_{x_{1}}^{2}-1\in\mathcal{L}(\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}},\widetilde{\mathcal{X}})
. First, we note that the spectrum of the corresponding operator in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{C}_{\textup{b,unif}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}),\mathcal{C}_{\textup{b,unif}}^{0}(\mathbb{R}))
, which we denote by
\widehat{\mathrm{L}}_{1}
, is given by
(-\infty,-1]
. This follows from standard results; see e.g.
[
39
, Sec. 3]
or
[
25
,
38
]
, which guarantee that the point spectrum is empty and that the essential spectrum can be determined by substituting
\bm{u}_{1}={\mathrm{e}}^{\zeta t_{1}+ikx_{1}}\bm{v_{1}}
into
\partial_{t_{1}}\bm{u}_{1}=\widehat{\mathrm{L}}_{1}\bm{u}_{1}
, leading to the dispersion relation

\zeta=-1-k^{2},\qquad k\in\mathbb{R}.

Returning to the case at hand, i.e. to the operator
\widetilde{\mathrm{L}}_{1}\in\mathcal{L}(\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}},\widetilde{\mathcal{X}})
, one can verify directly when
\zeta=1-k^{2}
for any
k\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, the range
({\mathrm{L}}_{1}-\zeta)(\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}})
does not contain the non-zero constant functions. Thus,
(-\infty,-1)\subseteq\sigma(\widetilde{L}_{1})
. For all
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,1]
, i.e. for all
\zeta
in the resolvent set for
\widehat{\mathrm{L}}_{1}
, elements in the range of
(\widehat{\mathrm{L}}_{1}-\zeta)^{-1}(\widetilde{X})
are contained in
\mathcal{C}_{\textup{b,unif}}^{2}(\mathbb{R})

We turn now to the third and final hypothesis – Hypothesis
3
. In our case, this is equivalent to satisfying the pair of resolvent bounds described in the following result.

Lemma 4.10
.

For any
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
such that
|\omega|\geq 1
,
i\omega\in\textup{Res}({\mathrm{L}}_{1})
and there exists a constant
c>0
such that the following resolvent bounds are satisfied:

\|(i\omega\textup{Id}-{\mathrm{L}}_{1})^{-1}\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X})}\leq\frac{c}{|\omega|},\qquad\|(i\omega\textup{Id}-{\mathrm{L}}_{1})^{-1}\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{Z})}\leq\frac{c}{|\omega|^{1/2}}.

(32)

Proof.

The fact that
i\omega\in\textup{Res}({\mathrm{L}}_{1})
for all
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
such that
|\omega|\geq 1
follows immediately from the fact that
\sigma_{0}({\mathrm{L}}_{1})=\{0\}
, as shown in Lemma
4.8
(in fact
i\omega\in\textup{Res}({\mathrm{L}}_{1})
for all
\omega\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
). It therefore remains to verify the bounds in (
32
).

Since
i\omega\in\textup{Res}({\mathrm{L}}_{1})
, the operator
i\omega\textup{Id}-{\mathrm{L}}_{1}
is invertible. Thus for any
\bm{b}=(b_{1},b_{2},b_{3})\in\mathcal{X}
(known) and
\bm{a}=(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})\in\mathcal{Z}
(unknown), the equation
(i\omega\textup{Id}-{\mathrm{L}}_{1})\bm{a}=\bm{b}
has a unique solution. A direct computation using the definition of
{\mathrm{L}}_{1}
in (
28
) shows that
a_{2}=-ib_{2}/\omega
and
a_{3}=-ib_{3}/\omega
, whereas
a_{1}
is (uniquely) determined by the equation

a_{1}^{\prime\prime}(x_{1})-(1+i\omega)a_{1}(x_{1})=-b_{1}(x_{1}),

(33)

together with the requirement that
a_{1}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}
. A somewhat lengthy but nevertheless direct computation shows that the unique bounded solution is given by

a_{1}(x_{1})=\frac{{\mathrm{e}}^{\eta x_{1}}}{2\eta}\int_{x_{1}}^{\infty}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\eta\xi}b_{1}(\xi)d\xi+\frac{{\mathrm{e}}^{-\eta x_{1}}}{2\eta}\int_{-\infty}^{x_{1}}{\mathrm{e}}^{\eta\xi}b_{1}(\xi)d\xi,

(34)

where
\eta:=\sqrt{1+i\omega}
; we refer to the Masters thesis
[
37
]
for details. In order to prove the first inequality in (
32
), we show that

\frac{\|\bm{a}\|_{\mathcal{X}}}{\|\bm{b}\|_{\mathcal{X}}}\leq\frac{\|a_{1}\|_{\infty}}{\|b_{1}\|_{\infty}}+\frac{|(a_{2},a_{3})|}{|(b_{2},b_{3})|}\leq\frac{c}{|\omega|}

(35)

for some constant
c>0
(the left-most inequality follows directly from the definition of
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{X}}
, whereas the right-most needs to be shown). Using the expressions for
a_{2}
and
a_{3}
obtained above, we have that

\frac{|(a_{2},a_{3})|}{|(b_{2},b_{3})|}=\frac{|(-ib_{2}/\omega,-ib_{3}/\omega)|}{|(b_{2},b_{3})|}=\frac{1}{|\omega|},

(36)

so it remains to bound the left-most term of the middle expression in (
35
). One can show directly with a triangle inequality that the integrals appearing in the formula for
a_{1}(x_{1})
in (
34
) can be both be bounded by
\|b_{1}\|_{\infty}/(2|\eta|^{2})
, so that

\frac{\|a_{1}\|_{\infty}}{\|b_{1}\|_{\infty}}+\frac{|(a_{2},a_{3})|}{|(b_{2},b_{3})|}\leq\frac{1}{2|\eta|^{2}}+\frac{1}{2|\eta|^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{|\omega|}.

Combining this with the bound in (
36
) shows that (
35
) is satisfied with
c=2
.

In order to prove the second inequality in (
32
), we need to show that

\frac{\|\bm{a}\|_{\mathcal{Z}}}{\|\bm{b}\|_{\mathcal{Y}}}\leq\frac{\|a_{1}\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}}}{\|b_{1}\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}}}+\frac{|(a_{2},a_{3})|}{|(b_{2},b_{3})|}\leq\frac{c}{|\omega|^{1/2}}

(37)

for a constant
c>0
(which may of course be different from the constant
c
appearing in the first bound in (
32
)). Since the second term in the middle expression can again be bounded by (
36
), and
1/|\omega|\leq 1/|\omega|^{1/2}
since
|\omega|\geq 1
, it remains to show that

\frac{\|a_{1}\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}}}{\|b_{1}\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}}}=\frac{\|a_{1}\|_{\infty}+\|(1+|\cdot|)a_{1}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+\|(1+(\cdot)^{2})a_{1}^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}}{\|b_{1}\|_{\infty}+\|(1+|\cdot|)b_{1}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}}\leq\frac{\tilde{c}}{|\omega|^{1/2}}

for some constant
\tilde{c}>0
. We shall actually show that there exists a
\tilde{c}>0
such that the second inequality in

\frac{\|a_{1}\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}}}{\|b_{1}\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}}}\leq\frac{\|a_{1}\|_{\infty}}{\|b_{1}\|_{\infty}}+\frac{\|(1+|\cdot|)a_{1}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}}{\|(1+|\cdot|)b_{1}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}}+\frac{\|(1+(\cdot)^{2})a_{1}^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}}{\|(1+|\cdot|)b_{1}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}}\\
\leq\frac{\tilde{c}}{|\omega|^{1/2}}

(38)

is satisfied (the first follows directly from the preceding expression). We have already shown that
\|a_{1}\|_{\infty}/\|b_{1}\|_{\infty}\leq 1/|\omega|\leq 1/|\omega|^{1/2}
, so it remains to bound the latter two terms in the middle expression.

The derivatives
a_{1}^{\prime}
and
a_{1}^{\prime\prime}
are given by

\begin{split}a_{1}^{\prime}(x_{1})&=\frac{{\mathrm{e}}^{\eta x_{1}}}{2\eta}\int_{x_{1}}^{\infty}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\eta\xi}b_{1}^{\prime}(\xi)d\xi+\frac{{\mathrm{e}}^{-\eta x_{1}}}{2\eta}\int_{-\infty}^{x_{1}}{\mathrm{e}}^{\eta\xi}b_{1}^{\prime}(\xi)d\xi,\\
a_{1}^{\prime\prime}(x_{1})&=\frac{{\mathrm{e}}^{\eta x_{1}}}{2}\int_{x_{1}}^{\infty}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\eta\xi}b_{1}^{\prime}(\xi)d\xi-\frac{{\mathrm{e}}^{-\eta x_{1}}}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{x_{1}}{\mathrm{e}}^{\eta\xi}b_{1}^{\prime}(\xi)d\xi,\end{split}

where the former is obtained by differentiating (
34
) and integrating by parts, and the latter can be determined by rearranging (
33
) for
a_{1}^{\prime\prime}(x_{1})
, inserting (
34
) and integrating by parts; we refer to
[
23
, pp.43-45]
for similar calculations in the context of a parabolic reaction-diffusion equation on a bounded domain. Using the expression for
a_{1}^{\prime}(x_{1})
, we obtain the bound

\begin{split}\|(1+|\cdot|)a_{1}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}&=\sup_{x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}}\|(1+|x_{1}|)a_{1}^{\prime}(x_{1})\|\\
&\leq\frac{1}{2|\eta|}\sup_{x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}}\left|(1+|x_{1}|){\mathrm{e}}^{\eta x_{1}}\int_{x_{1}}^{\infty}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\eta\xi}b_{1}^{\prime}(\xi)d\xi\right|+\frac{1}{2|\eta|}\sup_{x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}}\left|(1+|x_{1}|){\mathrm{e}}^{-\eta x_{1}}\int_{-\infty}^{x_{1}}{\mathrm{e}}^{\eta\xi}b_{1}^{\prime}(\xi)d\xi\right|\\
&\leq\frac{\|(1+|\cdot|)b_{1}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}}{2|\eta|}\left({\mathrm{e}}^{\eta_{r}x_{1}}\int_{x_{1}}^{\infty}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\eta_{r}\xi}d\xi+{\mathrm{e}}^{-\eta_{r}x_{1}}\int_{-\infty}^{x_{1}}{\mathrm{e}}^{\eta_{r}\xi}d\xi\right)\\
&=\frac{\|(1+|\cdot|)b_{1}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}}{|\eta|\eta_{r}},\end{split}

where
\eta_{r}=\text{Re}(\eta)
. Using
\eta=\sqrt{1+i\omega}
one can show that
\eta_{r}=|\omega|^{1/2}/\sqrt{2}
, which yields

\frac{\|(1+|\cdot|)a_{1}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}}{\|(1+|\cdot|)b_{1}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{|\omega|^{1/2}}.

It therefore remains to bound the right-most term in the first line of (
38
). This can be done using the expression for
a^{\prime\prime}(x_{1})
derived above, however, we have opted to defer the details to Lemma
C.1
, Appendix
C
, in order to preserve clarity in the overall argument within the main text. Combining the bound in Lemma
C.1
with the bounds obtained above proves (
38
), thereby completing the proof.
∎

. As in Lemma
4.18

Lemma 4.27
.

For

-function

such that

4.27

Proposition 4.28
.

Fix
\lambda>0
. For
\beta_{-},R>0
sufficiently small but fixed, the map
\pi_{2}
is well-defined and given by

\pi_{2}:\begin{pmatrix}u_{2}\\
-\Omega\\
r_{2}\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}\Psi_{2}(\Omega,r_{2})+\mathcal{Q}_{2}(u_{2},r_{2})\\
\Omega\\
r_{2}\end{pmatrix}

where
\|\mathcal{Q}_{2}(u_{2},r_{2})\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}}\leq C\|u_{2}-\Psi_{2}(-\Omega,r_{2})\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}}
for some constant
C>0
.

Proof.

Following the arguments given in the proof of Proposition
4.19
, we introduce an error function

E(\cdot,t_{2},r_{2}):=\tilde{u}_{2}(\cdot,t_{2},r_{2})-\Psi_{2}(\mu_{2}(t_{2}),r_{2}),

where
\tilde{u}(\cdot,t_{2},r_{2})
denotes a solution to system (
57
) on the time interval
[0,T_{2}]
,
\mu_{2}(t_{2})=-\Omega+t_{2}
, and
\Psi_{2}
is the function described in Lemma
4.27
. Differentiating with respect to
t_{2}
leads to the following evolution equation for
E
:

\partial_{t_{2}}E=\partial_{x_{2}}^{2}E+\mathcal{K}(E,\Psi_{2})E,

where
\mathcal{K}(E,\Psi_{2}):=\mu_{2}(t_{2})-2\Psi_{2}-E+r_{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{2}(E,\Psi_{2},\mu_{2},r_{2})
, where

\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{2}(E,\Psi_{2},\mu_{2},r_{2}):=\frac{\mathcal{R}_{2}(E+\Psi_{2},\mu_{2},r_{2})-\mathcal{R}_{2}(\Psi_{2},\mu_{2},r_{2})}{E},

which by Taylor’s theorem is well-defined as
E\to 0
.

Let

\tilde{T}_{2}:=\inf\left\{T_{2},\sup\left\{t_{2}\geq 0:\|E(\cdot,t_{2})\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}}\leq C_{2}\right\}\right\},

where the constant
C_{2}\geq\|E(\cdot,0)\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}}\geq 0
is fixed but small enough to ensure that
\tilde{u}_{2}(\cdot,0,r_{2})
and
\Psi_{2}(-\Omega,r_{2})
correspond to initial conditions in
\Sigma_{2}^{\textup{in}}
, as well as the validity of the Taylor expansion about
E=0
which allows us to define the function
\widetilde{R}_{2}
above. On
t_{2}\in[0,\tilde{T}_{2}]
, the variation of constants formula yields

E(\cdot,t_{2})={\mathrm{e}}^{t_{2}\Lambda_{2}}E(\cdot,0)+\int_{0}^{t_{2}}{\mathrm{e}}^{(t_{2}-s_{2})\Lambda_{2}}\mathcal{K}\left(E(\cdot,s_{2}),\Psi_{2}(s_{2})\right)E(\cdot,s_{2}){\mathrm{d}}s_{2},

where
({\mathrm{e}}^{t_{2}\Lambda_{2}})_{t_{2}\geq 0}
denotes the (heat) semigroup generated by
\Lambda_{2}:=\partial_{x_{2}}^{2}
. Note that for notational simplicity, we omit the dependence on
r_{2}
in our notation.

We want a bound for
\|E(\cdot,t_{2})\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}}
. In order to obtain one, notice first that
\Psi_{2}(t_{2})
is bounded (due to the analysis in
[
34
]
), and that
E(\cdot,t_{2})
is bounded (by the definition of
\tilde{T}_{2}
). Therefore,

\|\mathcal{K}\left(\tilde{E}(\cdot,t_{2}),\Psi_{2}(t_{2})\right)\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}}\leq C_{1}

for some constant
C_{1}>0
, for all
t_{2}\in[0,\tilde{T}_{2}]
(we also use the fact that
\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}
is an algebra – we refer again to Lemma
A.2
in Appendix
A
– to control the higher order terms). Moreover, the operator norm on the semigroup obeys the same bound as in (
49
) for all
t_{2}\in[0,\tilde{T}_{2}]
. Using a triangle inequality together with both of these bounds and a Grönwall inequality, we obtain the estimate

\|E(\cdot,t_{2})\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}}\leq{\mathrm{e}}^{C_{1}t_{2}}\|E(\cdot,0)\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}}

for all
t_{2}\in[0,\tilde{T}_{2}]
. Fixing
\beta_{-}>0
(and therefore also
C_{0}
) such that

\|E(\cdot,0)\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}}\leq C_{0}{\mathrm{e}}^{-C_{1}C_{2}T_{2}}

ensures that the corresponding bound for
E(\cdot,t_{2})
holds for all
t_{2}\in[0,\tilde{T}_{2}]
with
\tilde{T}_{2}=T_{2}
.

The preceding arguments show that

u_{2}(\cdot,T_{2})=\Psi_{2}(T_{2})+E(\cdot,T_{2}),

where
\|E(\cdot,T_{2})\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}}\leq C\|E(\cdot,0)\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}}
. Equating
\mathcal{Q}_{2}(u_{2},r_{2})
with
E(\cdot,T_{2})
concludes the proof.
∎

The key implication of Proposition
4.28
and its proof is that solutions to the PDE system (

and initial conditions that are close to the manifold

, remain close to
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{a}
up until their intersection with
\Sigma_{2}^{\textup{out}}
.

is well-defined and given by

\pi:\begin{pmatrix}u_{1}\\
\rho\\
\varepsilon_{1}\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}\Psi_{3}(\rho,\varepsilon_{1})+\mathcal{O}({\mathrm{e}}^{-\gamma/2\varepsilon_{1}})\\
\rho\\
\varepsilon_{1}\end{pmatrix}.

Applying the blow-down transformation to the right-hand side – specifically the relations
u=\rho u_{3}
and
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon/\rho^{2}
– yields

u(x,T)=\rho\Psi_{3}(\rho,\varepsilon/\rho^{2})+\mathcal{O}({\mathrm{e}}^{-\gamma\rho^{2}/2\varepsilon})=\phi(\rho,\varepsilon)+\mathcal{O}({\mathrm{e}}^{-\gamma\rho^{2}/2\varepsilon}),

where the local coincidence of the center manifold
\mathcal{M}_{3}^{a}|_{r_{3}>0}
and the slow manifold
\mathcal{S}_{a}^{+}\times\{0\}
, as described in Remark
4.25
, is used in order to obtain the equality
\rho\Psi_{3}(\rho,\varepsilon/\rho^{2})=\phi(\rho,\varepsilon)
. This concludes the proof.

5
Summary and outlook

The primary aim of this article has been to show that the geometric blow-up method can be used to obtain rigorous and geometrically informative information about the dynamics close to dynamic bifurcations in scalar reaction-diffusion equations in the general form (
1
), which are posed on the entire real line
x\in\mathbb{R}
. We considered two particular cases, corresponding to a dynamic transcritical or pitchfork bifurcation in the reaction dynamics. After deriving local normal forms in each case in Lemma
2.2
, we proved a number of exchange of stability properties, as described in Theorems
3.2
and
3.3
, which are dynamically very similar to the exchange of stability properties that were proven for the corresponding problems on bounded spatial domains using upper and lower solutions in
[
7
]
. The results in Theorems
3.2
and
3.3
are of independent interest – we refer back to the list of implications a)-f) in the discussion towards the end of Section
3
– however, we consider the primary contribution of this article to be the method of proof itself. In light of the notable success of approaches to the study of dynamic bifurcations and singularities in fast-slow systems in the ODE setting (we refer again to
[
27
]
and the many references therein), we are hopeful that the geometric blow-up based approach for scalar reaction-diffusion systems developed herein may provide a blueprint for a rigorous, geometrically informative and constructive approach to the study of dynamic bifurcations in PDEs more generally.

Relative to the recent adaptations and applications of the geometric blow-up method in the PDE setting in
[
16
,
17
,
26
,
28
,
48
]
, the problems considered in this work are ‘dynamically simpler’ because of the simple form of the slow equation
\dot{\mu}=\varepsilon
, and because we do not consider more complicated spatial dynamics associated with e.g. pattern formation. As a result, the proofs that we presented in Section
4
can more easily be compared and contrasted with the geometric blow-up based proofs for the corresponding ODE problems presented in
[
34
]
. We would like to reiterate the following points in particular:

(a)

The blow-up transformation
\Phi
defined in (
23
) is applied directly to the extended PDE system (
20
), leading to PDE systems in the entry, rescaling and exit charts
K_{1}
,
K_{2}
and
K_{3}
respectively after a suitable desingularisation in both time and space (we refer again to Remarks
4.4
and
4.5
).

(b)

The linearisation about the steady states corresponding to the extension of attracting critical manifolds up to the blow-up manifold features a spectral gap; we refer to Lemma
4.8
and Figure
4
. This resolves the marginally stable situation observed in the blown-down problem with
\mu=\varepsilon=0
, which is shown in Figure
2
. This is analogous to one of the most important benefits of geometric blow-up based approaches in the ODE setting, namely, that a loss of hyperbolicity can often be resolved within the blown-up space; we refer again to Remark
4.9
for details.

(c)

The spectral gap property described in (b) allowed for the application of center manifold theory for evolution equations in Banach spaces. We applied these results, as they are formulated in
[
23
]
, in order to identify
2
-dimensional, strongly attracting local center manifolds which in turn allowed us to provide a rigorous and geometric description of the local dynamics within the entry and exit charts
K_{1}
and
K_{3}
.

(d)

The extension of the local center manifold
\mathcal{M}_{1}^{a}
in chart
K_{1}
into and through the rescaling chart
K_{2}
is described by Lemma
4.18
in the pitchfork case, and by Lemma
4.27
in the transcritical case. In both cases, the details of this extension follow directly from the analysis of the corresponding ODE analysis in
[
34
]
, because of the fact that
\mathcal{M}_{1}^{a}
(and therefore also its extension) only contains spatially independent solutions.

(e)

We showed that PDE solutions in the rescaling chart
K_{2}
‘track’ the extended manifold described above in (d), using direct a priori arguments to provide a bound on an error function which ensures that solutions stay within a tubular neighbourhood for finite time intervals; this is described in Propositions
4.19
and
4.28
. Here it is also worthy to emphasise that the proofs of Propositions
4.19
and
4.28
are inspired by established and relatively constructive approaches to the validation of modulation equations; we refer again to
[
40
]
and the many references therein.

With the possible exception of (d), the features raised in (a)-(e) above do not appear to rely in any critical way on the specific structure of the equations (although the precise manner in which a spectral degeneracy may be resolved is of course expected to vary on a case-by-case basis).

Looking forward, there are a number of analytical obstacles which remain to be overcome. The question of precisely how to incorporate
u
into the geometry of the blown-up space in situations where a ‘fast escape’ occurs (e.g. near a fold, or in the transcritical case considered in this work with
\lambda<0
) is still open; we refer again to Remark
4.3
. The question of whether canard-type dynamics for
\lambda=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
can be understood using geometric blow-up is also open. Of course, one could also allow for more complicated slow dynamics, different singularities, or more complicated background states. These and other related questions remain for future work.

Appendix A

In the following we state and prove a number of basic properties of the polynomially weighted spaces

and
\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}

Lemma A.1
.

The weighted spaces
\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}
are dense in
\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}
.

Proof.

Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
be either
\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}
or
\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}
. We show that for any
u\in\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}
and
\epsilon>0
, there exists a function
u\in\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
such that
\|u-v\|_{\infty}<\epsilon
. The constant
\epsilon
should be distinguished from the small parameter
\varepsilon
used throughout the main body of the paper.

Fix
u\in\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}
and an arbitrarily small
\epsilon>0
. Since
\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}\subset\mathcal{C}^{0}_{\textup{b,unif}}(\mathbb{R})
and
\mathcal{C}^{2}_{\textup{b,unif}}(\mathbb{R})
is dense in
\mathcal{C}^{0}_{\textup{b,unif}}(\mathbb{R})
, there exists a function
\tilde{v}\in\mathcal{C}^{2}_{\textup{b,unif}}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\|u-\tilde{v}\|_{\infty}<\epsilon
. The idea is to define
v:=\phi_{M}\tilde{v}
, where
\phi_{M}
is a
C^{\infty}
‘cut-off-like’ function which is defined so that
v(x)
satisfies

v(x)=\begin{cases}u_{-\infty},&x<-M-1,\\
\tilde{v}(x),&x\in(-M,M),\\
u_{+\infty},&x>M+1,\end{cases}

where
u_{\pm\infty}:=\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}u(x)
. Notice that
v\in\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
. Choosing
M
sufficiently large and assuming that
\phi_{M}
has been suitably defined for
|x|\in[M,M+1]
ensures that
\|u-v\|_{\infty}<\epsilon
, as required.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}
are algebras, i.e. if
u,v\in\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}
(
u,v\in\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}
), then
uv\in\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}
(
uv\in\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}
).

Proof.

We prove that
\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}
is an algebra; the proof that
\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}
is an algebra is similar and simpler. Let
u,v\in\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}
. In order to show that
uv\in\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}
, we need to show that
\|uv\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}}<\infty
. Direct calculations involving a product rule and several triangle inequalities leads to

\begin{split}\|uv\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}}=&\ \|uv\|_{\infty}+\|(1+|\cdot|)(uv)^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+\|(1+(\cdot)^{2})(uv)^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}\\
\leq&\ \|u\|_{\infty}\|v\|_{\infty}+\|u\|_{\infty}\|(1+|\cdot|)v^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+\|v\|_{\infty}\|(1+|\cdot|)u^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+\|u\|_{\infty}\|(1+(\cdot)^{2})v^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}\\
&+2\|(1+(\cdot)^{2})u^{\prime}v^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+\|v\|_{\infty}\|(1+(\cdot)^{2})u^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}.\end{split}

The first
5
terms appearing in the right-hand side of the inequality above can bounded from above by
\|u\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}}\|v\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}}
; this follows from direct calculations if one expands the
\|u\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}}\|v\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}}
using the definition of the
\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}
-norm. Since
1+x^{2}\leq(1+|x|)^{2}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we can also bound the last term using

\|(1+(\cdot)^{2})u^{\prime}v^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq\|(1+|\cdot|)u^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\|(1+|\cdot|)v^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\\
\leq\|u\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}}\|v\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}}.

It follows that

\|uv\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}}\leq 3\|u\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}}\|v\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}},

implying that
uv\in\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}
, as required.
∎

Lemma A.3
.

Constant spatial scalings yield equivalent norms in the following sense: for any
u\in\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}
(
u\in\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}
) and for any fixed
\alpha>0
, let
x=\alpha\tilde{x}
and
u(x)=\tilde{u}(\tilde{x})
. Then
\|u(\cdot)\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}}\sim\|\tilde{u}(\cdot)\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}}
(
\|u(\cdot)\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}}\sim\|\tilde{u}(\cdot)\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}}
).

Proof.

As in the proof of Lemma
A.2
above, we only present a proof in
\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}
because the proof in
\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}
is similar and simpler. Fix
\alpha>0
. We need to show that there are positive constants
c_{+}\geq c_{-}\geq 0
such that

c_{-}\|\tilde{u}(\cdot)\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}}\leq\|u(\cdot)\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}}\leq c_{+}\|\tilde{u}(\cdot)\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}}.

(58)

Since
\|u(\cdot)\|_{\infty}=\|\tilde{u}(\cdot)\|_{\infty}
follows directly from the definition of the supremum norm, it remains to consider the terms involving derivatives. Writing
x=\alpha\tilde{x}
and
u(x)=\tilde{u}(\tilde{x})
as in the statement of the lemma, we obtain

\begin{split}|(1+|x|)u^{\prime}(x)|&=\left|(\alpha^{-1}+|\tilde{x}|)\tilde{u}^{\prime}(\tilde{x})\right|,\\
|(1+x^{2})u^{\prime\prime}(x)|&=\left|(\alpha^{-2}+\tilde{x}^{2})\tilde{u}^{\prime\prime}(\tilde{x})\right|,\end{split}

where
\tilde{u}^{\prime}(\tilde{x})=\partial\tilde{u}/\partial\tilde{x}
and
\tilde{u}^{\prime\prime}(\tilde{x})=\partial^{2}\tilde{u}/\partial\tilde{x}^{2}
. From here one can show directly that

C_{1}^{-}|(1+|\tilde{x}|)u^{\prime}(\tilde{x})|\leq|(1+|x|)u^{\prime}(x)|\leq C_{1}^{+}|(1+|\tilde{x}|)u^{\prime}(\tilde{x})|,

and

C_{2}^{-}|(1+\tilde{x}^{2})u^{\prime\prime}(\tilde{x})|\leq|(1+x^{2})u^{\prime\prime}(x)|\leq C_{2}^{+}|(1+\tilde{x}^{2})u^{\prime\prime}(\tilde{x})|,

where the (
\alpha
-dependent) constants are given by

\begin{split}C_{j}^{-}&=C_{j}^{-}(\alpha):=\min\{1,\alpha^{-j}\},\\
C_{j}^{+}&=C_{j}^{+}(\alpha):=\max\{1,\alpha^{-j}\},\end{split}

for
j\in\{1,2\}
. These inequalities imply that (
58
) is satisfied with

c_{-}=2\min\{C_{1}^{-},C_{2}^{-}\},\quad c_{+}=2\max\{C_{1}^{-},C_{2}^{-}\},

thereby concluding the proof.
∎

Lemma A.4
.

Let
({\mathrm{e}}^{t_{2}\Lambda_{2}})_{t_{2}\geq 0}
denote the heat semigroup generated by
\partial_{x_{2}}^{2}
. Then

\|{\mathrm{e}}^{t_{2}\Lambda_{2}}\|_{\widetilde{Z}\to\widetilde{Z}}\leq 1

(61)

for all
t_{2}>0

Proof.

This can be shown directly using the solution formula

u(x,t)={\mathrm{e}}^{t\Lambda}u(x,0)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi t}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\mathrm{e}}^{-(x-y)^{2}/4t}u(y,0){\mathrm{d}}y,

where here and in the remainder of the proof we drop the subscript “2” for notational simplicity. A direct application of a triangle inequality yields

\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{\infty}\leq\|u(\cdot,0)\|_{\infty},

\partial_{x}u(x,t)={\mathrm{e}}^{t\Lambda}\partial_{x}u(x,0),\qquad\partial_{x}^{2}u(x,t)={\mathrm{e}}^{t\Lambda}\partial_{x}^{2}u(x,0),

\|(1+|\cdot|)\partial_{x}u(\cdot,t)\|_{\infty}\leq\|(1+|\cdot|)\partial_{x}u(\cdot,0)\|_{\infty}

\|(1+(\cdot)^{2})\partial_{x}^{2}u(\cdot,t)\|_{\infty}\leq\|(1+(\cdot)^{2})\partial_{x}^{2}u(\cdot,0)\|_{\infty}.

for all

for all

Appendix B
Hypotheses for the center manifold theorem

The results in
[
23
]
apply to evolution equations on Banach spaces of the form

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\bm{u}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}={\mathrm{L}}\bm{u}+{\mathrm{N}}(\bm{u}),

where
{\mathrm{L}}
is a closed, densely defined linear operator, and
{\mathrm{N}}
is a nonlinear operator. In this paper, we work with the particular spaces
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathcal{Z}
defined in (
21
), but the more general requirement in
[
23
]
is that
\mathcal{Z}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{Y}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{X}
with continuous embedding (for the particular choice of spaces in this work, the embedding is naturally indued by the identity map).

In order to apply center manifold theorems from
[
23
]
, three main hypotheses need to be verified. These are formulated below in a notation which is consistent with the notation presented in the main body of the article.

The first hypothesis ensures that
\bm{u}=\bm{0}
is a steady state, and that
{\mathrm{L}}
is the linearisation. In order to state it, we let
\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
denote the space of bounded/continuous linear operators from
X
to
Y
.

Hypothesis 1
.

[
23
, Hypothesis 2.1]

The linear resp. nonlinear operators
{\mathrm{L}}
and
{\mathrm{N}}
satisfy the following assumptions:

(i)

{\mathrm{L}}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{X})
;

(ii)

There exists an integer
k\geq 2
and a neighbourhood
\mathcal{V}
of
\bm{0}
in
\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}
such that
N\in\mathcal{C}^{k}(\mathcal{V},\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}})
,
{\mathrm{N}}(\bm{0})=\bm{0}
and
{\mathrm{D}}{\mathrm{N}}(\bm{0})=\bm{0}
.

The second hypothesis is the well-known ‘spectral gap requirement’, which applies to the spectrum
\sigma({\mathrm{L}})
. In order to state it succinctly, we write

\sigma({\mathrm{L}})=\sigma_{-}({\mathrm{L}})\cup\sigma_{0}({\mathrm{L}})\cup\sigma_{+}({\mathrm{L}}),

where

\sigma_{-}({\mathrm{L}})=\left\{\lambda\in\textup{Re}\lambda<0\right\},\qquad\sigma_{0}({\mathrm{L}})=\left\{\lambda\in\textup{Re}\lambda=0\right\},\qquad\sigma_{+}({\mathrm{L}})=\left\{\lambda\in\textup{Re}\lambda>0\right\}.

The hypothesis can then be formulated as follows:

Hypothesis 2
.

[
23
, Hypothesis 2.4]

The spectrum
\sigma({\mathrm{L}})
possesses a spectral gap, i.e. there exists a constant
\gamma>0
such that

\sup_{\lambda\in\sigma_{-}}\text{Re}(\lambda)<-\gamma,\qquad\inf_{\lambda\in\sigma_{+}}\text{Re}(\lambda)>\gamma.

Moreover,
\sigma_{0}
contains only a finite number of eigenvalues, each of which has finite algebraic multiplicity.

Notice in particular that Hypothesis
2
implies that any center subspace
\mathcal{E}_{0}
is finite-dimensional.

The third and final main hypothesis pertains to the existence and uniqueness of solutions to the linearised problem which is obtained after projection onto the linear subspace which is complementary to the center eigenspace
\mathcal{E}_{0}
. In general settings, one must check the existence and uniqueness of solutions to this problem directly, i.e. one has to verify
[
23
, Hypothesis 2.7]
, which can be difficult in applications. Fortunately, a simplification holds in the case of semilinear equations
[
23
, Sec. 2.2.3]
, for which this problem reduces to the problem of verifying a pair of resolvant estimates.

Hypothesis 3
.

[
23
, Hypothesis 2.15]

There exists constants
\omega_{0}>0
,
c>0
and
\alpha\in[0,1)
such that for all
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
satisfying
|\omega|\geq\omega_{0}
,
i\omega\in\textup{Res}({\mathrm{L}})
and the following resolvent estimates are satisfied:

\|(i\omega\text{Id}-{\mathrm{L}})\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X})}\leq\frac{c}{|\omega|},\qquad\|(i\omega\text{Id}-{\mathrm{L}})\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{Z})}\leq\frac{c}{|\omega|^{1-\alpha}},

where
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X}):=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X})
.

Appendix C
Bounds for the proof of Lemma
4.10

The following result is used to prove the second resolvent bound in Lemma
C.1
.

Lemma C.1
.

Let
a_{1}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}
be the function defined by (
34
),
b_{1}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}
, and
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
be such that
|\omega|\geq 1
. There exists a constant
C\geq 0
such that

\frac{\|(1+(\cdot)^{2})a_{1}^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}}{\|(1+|\cdot|)b_{1}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}}\leq\frac{C}{|\omega|^{1/2}}.

(62)

Proof.

In order to simplify notation, we rewrite the formula for
a_{1}^{\prime\prime}(x_{1})
in (
34
) as

a_{1}^{\prime\prime}(x_{1})=\frac{{\mathrm{e}}^{\eta x_{1}}}{2}I_{1}(x_{1})-\frac{{\mathrm{e}}^{-\eta x_{1}}}{2}I_{2}(x_{1}),

where

I_{1}(x_{1}):=\int_{x_{1}}^{\infty}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\eta\xi}b_{1}^{\prime}(\xi)d\xi,\qquad I_{2}(x_{1}):=\int_{-\infty}^{x_{1}}{\mathrm{e}}^{\eta\xi}b_{1}^{\prime}(\xi)d\xi.

To prove the bound in (
62
), we shall consider the corresponding bound for
|x_{1}|\leq M
and
|x_{1}|>M
separately, where
M>0
is a constant which shall be chosen to be sufficiently large for the validity of asymptotic arguments as
|x_{1}|\to\infty
in the latter case. It is straightforward to obtain a bound corresponding to (
62
) on the bounded set
\{|x_{1}|\leq M\}
, since

\frac{\sup_{x_{1}\in[-M,M]}(1+x_{1}^{2})|a_{1}^{\prime\prime}(x_{1})|}{\sup_{x_{1}\in[-M,M]}(1+|x_{1}|)|b_{1}^{\prime}(x_{1})|}\leq(1+M^{2})\frac{\sup_{x_{1}\in[-M,M]}|a_{1}^{\prime\prime}(x_{1})|}{\sup_{x_{1}\in[-M,M]}|b_{1}^{\prime}(x_{1})|}\leq(1+M^{2})\frac{1}{\eta_{r}}

(63)

where
\eta_{r}=\text{Re}(\eta)
and the last inequality follows by a direct calculation using the expression for
a_{1}^{\prime\prime}(x_{1})
on
\{|x_{1}|\leq M\}
and a pair of triangle inequalities.

It remains to prove a similar bound for the far field, i.e. for
|x_{1}|>M
. We start by deriving bounds for the integrals
I_{1}(x_{1})
and
I_{2}(x_{1})
which are valid when
|x_{1}|>M
(assuming
M
is chosen large enough). Consider first the integral
I_{1}(x_{1})
as
x_{1}\to\infty
, which can be rewritten as

I_{1}(x_{1})=\int_{1}^{\infty}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\eta x_{1}s}\tilde{b}_{1}^{\prime}(s){\mathrm{d}}s

after making the substitution
s=\xi/x_{1}
and writing
\tilde{b}_{1}^{\prime}(s):=b_{1}^{\prime}(\xi/x_{1})
. Notice that the supremum norm of
\tilde{b}_{1}^{\prime}(s)
and
b_{1}^{\prime}(\xi/x_{1})
coincide on
\{x_{1}>M\}
, and that the lower integration bound is constant now. It follows that

\sup_{x_{1}>M}I_{1}(x_{1})\leq\sup_{x_{1}>M}|b_{1}^{\prime}(x_{1})|\int_{1}^{\infty}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\eta_{r}x_{1}s}{\mathrm{d}}s={\mathrm{e}}^{-\eta_{r}x_{1}}\frac{\sup_{x_{1}>M}|b_{1}^{\prime}(x_{1})|}{\eta_{r}x_{1}}.

(64)

The integral
I_{2}(x_{1})
can be rewritten as

I_{2}(x_{1})=\int_{-\infty}^{0}{\mathrm{e}}^{\eta\xi}b^{\prime}(\xi){\mathrm{d}}\xi+\int_{0}^{1}{\mathrm{e}}^{\eta x_{1}s}\tilde{b}_{1}^{\prime}(s){\mathrm{d}}s.

The first integral can be bounded directly using

\sup_{x_{1}>M}\left|\int_{-\infty}^{0}{\mathrm{e}}^{\eta\xi}b^{\prime}(\xi){\mathrm{d}}\xi\right|\leq\frac{\sup_{x_{1}>M}|b_{1}^{\prime}(x_{1})|}{\eta_{r}},

(65)

and the second, which we denote by
\tilde{I}_{2}(x_{1})
, can be bounded using arguments similar to those used in the derivation of a bound for
I_{1}(x_{1})
in (
64
) above. We obtain

\sup_{x_{1}>M}\tilde{I}_{2}(x_{1})\leq{\mathrm{e}}^{\eta_{r}x_{1}}\frac{\sup_{x_{1}>M}|b_{1}^{\prime}(x_{1})|}{\eta_{r}x_{1}}.

Taking the supremum norm of
a_{1}^{\prime\prime}
, applying a triangle inequality and substituting the bounds obtained above leads to

\sup_{x_{1}>M}a_{1}^{\prime\prime}(x_{1})\leq C\frac{\sup_{x_{1}>M}|b_{1}^{\prime}(x_{1})|}{\eta_{r}x_{1}},

for a constant
C>1
(which we introduce in order to absorb the exponentially small contribution due to (
65
)). It follows that

\frac{(1+x_{1}^{2})|a_{1}^{\prime\prime}(x_{1})|}{(1+x_{1})|b_{1}^{\prime}(x_{1})|}\leq C\frac{1+x_{1}^{2}}{x_{1}(1+x_{1})}\frac{1}{\eta_{r}}\leq\frac{\tilde{C}}{\eta_{r}},

where
\tilde{C}=C+c
for some small but fixed constant
c>0
(whose size depends on
M
), for all
x_{1}>M
. This implies that

\sup_{x_{1}>M}(1+x_{1}^{2})|a_{1}^{\prime\prime}(x_{1})|\leq\frac{\tilde{C}}{\eta_{r}}\sup_{x_{1}>M}(1+x_{1})|b_{1}^{\prime}(x_{1})|,

and therefore that

\frac{\sup_{x_{1}>M}(1+x_{1}^{2})|a_{1}^{\prime\prime}(x_{1})|}{\sup_{x_{1}>M}(1-x_{1})|b_{1}^{\prime}(x_{1})|}\leq\frac{\tilde{C}}{\eta_{r}}.

Similar arguments – which we omit here for brevity – can be used to derive the analogous bound on
\{x_{1}<-M\}
, i.e.

\sup_{x_{1}<M}a_{1}^{\prime\prime}(x_{1})\leq C\frac{\sup_{x_{1}<M}|b_{1}^{\prime}(x_{1})|}{\eta_{r}|x_{1}|},

leading in turn to

\frac{\sup_{x_{1}<M}(1+x_{1}^{2})|a_{1}^{\prime\prime}(x_{1})|}{\sup_{x_{1}<M}(1-x_{1})|b_{1}^{\prime}(x_{1})|}\leq\frac{\tilde{C}}{\eta_{r}}.

Combining these bounds with the bound obtained on
[-M,M]
, recall (
63
), it follows that

\frac{\|(1+(\cdot)^{2})a_{1}^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}}{\|(1+|\cdot|)b_{1}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}}\leq\frac{1+M^{2}}{\eta_{r}}.

Solving
\eta=\sqrt{1+i\omega}
for
\eta_{r}
leads to
\eta_{r}=|\omega|^{1/2}/\sqrt{2}
. Substituting this into the above yields the bound (
62
), thereby completing the proof.
∎
