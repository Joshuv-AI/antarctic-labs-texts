Transient osmotic flows in a microfluidic channel: measurements of solute permeability and reflection coefficients of hydrogel membranes
Julien Renaudeau, Pierre Lidon, Jean-Baptiste Salmon

Abstract
We first highlight theoretically a microfluidic configuration that allows to measure two fundamental parameters describing mass transport through a membrane: the solute permeability coefficient ℒ D \mathcal{L}_{D} , and the associated reflection coefficient σ \sigma . This configuration exploits the high confinement of microfluidic geometries to relate these two coefficients to the dynamics of a transient flow induced by forward osmosis through a membrane embedded in a chip. We then applied this methodology to hydrogel membranes photo-crosslinked in a microchannel with in situ measurements of osmotically-induced flows. These experiments enable us to estimate ℒ D \mathcal{L}_{D} and σ \sigma and their dependence on the molecular weight of the solute under consideration, ultimately leading to a precise estimate of the molecular weight cut-off of these hydrogel membranes.



I
Introduction

Synthetic membranes, which by definition are physical barriers enabling the selective transport of compounds, play a fundamental role in many separation processes, such as reverse osmosis desalination or water purification by ultrafiltration
Baker 2012
;
Shannon et al. 2008
.
Integrating a membrane into a microfluidic chip opens up many opportunities to design innovative miniaturized membrane-based processes, see the reviews
Wang, Devoe, and Lee 2001
;
Chen and Shen 2017
;
de Jong J., Lammertink, and Wessling 2006
;
Bacchin, Leng, and Salmon 2022
. In this context, the coupling of microfluidics with membranes capable of selectively retaining molecular-sized solutes in aqueous solutions has enabled applications such as the concentration of analytes
Kaufman et al. 2012
;
Jajack et al. 2019
, micro-dialysis for the purification
Song et al. 2004
and crystallization
Junius et al. 2020
of proteins, or the generation of osmotic flows for drug release
Su and Lin 2004
, passive pumping
Shay, Dickey, and Velev 2017
;
Chuang and Chiang 2019
, and fundamental studies of sap transport in plants
Jensen et al. 2009
. Mass transport through the membrane can be described by mechanistic models of the
pore-flow
type for nanoporous membranes, or of the
solution-diffusion
type for dense membranes
Wang et al. 2014
, two visions recently reconciled in the case of swollen polymer membranes using poro-elastic modeling (
fluid-solid
model)
Hegde, Doherty, and Squires 2022
.

More phenomenologically,
the volume flux
J_{v}
(
{\mathrm{m}}^{3}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{-2}
) and the solute flux
J_{s}
(
\mathrm{mol}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{-2}
) are often modelled by the Kedem and Katchalsky equations
Kedem and Katchalsky 1958
;
Kedem and Katchalsky 1961
:

\displaystyle J_{v}=-\mathcal{L}_{p}(\Delta P-\sigma\Delta\Pi)\,,

(1)

\displaystyle J_{s}=-\mathcal{L}_{D}\Delta C+(1-\sigma)\langle C\rangle J_{v}\,,

(2)

with
\Delta C
(
\text{\,}\mathrm{mol}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{-3}
),

\Delta P
and
\Delta\Pi
being respectively the differences in concentration, hydrostatic, and osmotic pressure across the membrane, and

\langle C\rangle
the average solute concentration on both sides of the membrane.
These linear equations are derived within the framework of the thermodynamics of
irreversible processes assuming van’t Hoff law
\Delta\Pi=RT\Delta C
(ideal dilute solutions,
R
the universal gas constant,
T
the absolute temperature) and
\Delta C\ll\langle C\rangle
, even if they are also often used in more concentrated regimes
Marbach, Yoshida, and Bocquet 2017
. This model
treats the membrane as a
black box
with three characteristic parameters: its hydraulic permeability
\mathcal{L}_{p}
(
\mathrm{m}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\text{\,}{\mathrm{Pa}}^{-1}
), its permeability to a given solute
\mathcal{L}_{D}
(
\mathrm{m}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
), and its reflection coefficient
\sigma\leq 1
which characterizes the membrane/solute interaction
Kedem and Katchalsky 1961
;
Manning 1968
;
Marbach and Bocquet 2019
. The case
\sigma=1
describes an ideal semi-permeable membrane, i.e., only permeable to the solvent, with
J_{s}=0
because
\mathcal{L}_{D}\to 0
for
\sigma\to 1

Kedem and Katchalsky 1961
;
Manning 1968
;
Marbach and Bocquet 2019
.
In this regime, the interactions between the solute and the membrane lead to the highest available osmotic pressure difference
\Delta\Pi
in eqn (
1
). Mass transport is then entirely characterized by hydraulic permeability
\mathcal{L}_{p}
alone, estimated from measurements of
J_{v}
vs.
\Delta(P-\Pi)
.
For a membrane that deviates from ideality,
\sigma<1
, the solute can permeate through the membrane (
J_{s}\neq 0
), and the membrane/solute interactions result in a reduced effective osmotic pressure difference
\sigma\Delta\Pi
in eqn (
1
), that cancels out for
\sigma\to 0
, the membrane being
transparent
to the solute in that limiting case. The first term in eqn (
2
) corresponds to the diffusive transport of solute through the membrane, while the second term describes
the advection of solute by the flow, with
\sigma
describing the
reflection
of solute by the membrane.

The reflection coefficient
\sigma
for a given membrane/solute pair is often evaluated as

\sigma=(\Delta P/\Delta\Pi)_{J_{v}=0}

and estimated by measuring permeation volume fluxes
J_{v}
through a membrane separating two macroscopic reservoirs with imposed
\Delta\Pi
and
\Delta P
, see e.g., Refs
Schultz, Valentine, and Choi 1979
;
Dainty and Ginzburg 1964
;
Opong and Zydney 1992
. The coefficient
\mathcal{L}_{D}
can be estimated by measuring, in similar experimental conditions, the solute permeation rate
J_{s}
but either in the absence of trans-membrane flow
(diffusion cell,
J_{v}=0
)
Matsuyama, Teramoto, and Urano 1997
;
Li et al. 2011
, or with simultaneous measurements of
J_{v}
and imposed
\Delta P

Pusch 1986
. In all cases, these experiments are difficult to implement and require to account for the effects of the unstirred boundary layers in the vicinity of the membrane, which can affect the estimates of
\mathcal{L}_{D}
and
\sigma

Schultz, Valentine, and Choi 1979
;
Dainty and Ginzburg 1964
;
Opong and Zydney 1992
;
Pusch 1986
.
Furthermore, in the aforementioned microfluidic context, these macroscopic characterizations of synthetic membranes should be performed
ex situ
, before their integration in chips, usually by mechanical clamping
de Jong J., Lammertink, and Wessling 2006
.
However, there are many practical cases in which the membrane is manufactured
in situ

de Jong J., Lammertink, and Wessling 2006
, and for which these measurements of
\sigma
and
\mathcal{L}_{D}
cannot be carried out.

This is particularly true for hydrogel membranes, which are integrated into microfluidic chips by chemical or UV-induced reticulation and act as water-permeable
walls
separating microchannels
de Jong J., Lammertink, and Wessling 2006
. Depending on both hydrogel formulation and crosslinking conditions, these membranes display various selectivity and permeability and have been used for applications ranging from the preconcentration of solute
Kim et al. 2010
;
Kim and Kim 2013
;
Shin et al. 2014
;
Randall, Schultz, and Doyle 2006
, desalination
Gumuscu et al. 2016
, microdialysis
Song et al. 2004
;
Paustian et al. 2013
, and colloid transport by diffusiophoresis
Paustian et al. 2015
;
Gu, Hegde, and Bishop 2018
.
In this context, we have more specifically studied the case of poly(ethylene glycol) diacrylate (PEGDA) hydrogel membranes in several microfluidic applications: microfiltration
Decock, Schlenk, and Salmon 2018
, crystallisation by dialysis
Nguyen et al. 2020
, and osmotic compression
Keita, Hallez, and Salmon 2021
.
Some of these works report microfluidic measurements of the hydraulic permeability
\mathcal{L}_{p}
of these hydrogels, and even of solute permeability
\mathcal{L}_{D}
in the absence of flow
Paustian et al. 2013
;
Nguyen et al. 2020
, but there are no
in situ
measurements of the reflection coefficient
\sigma
, either for this type of hydrogel membrane or more generally for mechanically integrated membranes.

In the present work,
we first demonstrate theoretically that a specific microfluidic configuration allows for the simultaneous measurement of both
\sigma
and
\mathcal{L}_{D}
for a given solute and a membrane integrated in a chip, see Sec.
II
. Qualitatively, this
forward osmosis
configuration exploits the solute/membrane interaction to induce osmotic flow in a dead-end microchannel, transient in nature due to solute permeation through the membrane and accumulation in the channel. We show that microfluidic confinement allows for a simple model of the dynamics of this flow with the two parameters
\sigma
and
\mathcal{L}_{D}
.
In a second step, Sec.
III
and Sec.
IV
, we apply this methodology to PEGDA hydrogel membranes integrated in microfluidic chips as in Ref.
Paustian et al. 2013
;
Nguyen et al. 2020
, and measure
\sigma
and
\mathcal{L}_{D}
for different solutes.
Finally, we discuss the observed correlation between these two parameters and the effect of the molecular weight of the solute.

A simple model for transient osmotic flows in a microfluidic channel

Figure 1:
Geometry of the model.
A dead-end microfluidic channel (transverse cross-section
h\times w
) is separated from another channel playing the role of solute reservoir (concentration
C_{0}
) by a membrane
(area
h\times L
). The dead-end channel is in equilibrium with the atmospheric pressure
P_{\text{atm}}
.
The osmotic pressure difference between the reservoir and the channel induces a flow in the channel with an entrance volume flux
V_{i}
. Because of the solute permeation through the membrane and its accumulation in the channel, this flow is transient and decays in time.

J_{v}
and
J_{s}

We consider the geometry depicted in Fig.
1
: a semi-permeable membrane separates a dead-end microchannel of rectangular transverse cross-section
h\times w
from another channel playing the role of reservoir of solute. This configuration can be applied to the most common experimental cases: a membrane mechanically clamped between two
aligned microchannels, or a membrane integrated in the transverse dimension of a microchannel, as in the present work.

II.1
Constitutive equations

We assume that solvent and solute transport across the semi-permeable membrane can be described by the Kedem and Katchalsky eqns (
1
) and (
2
) with uniform parameters,
\mathcal{L}_{p}
,
\mathcal{L}_{D}
, and
\sigma
, along the membrane.
Symbol
\Delta
in eqns (
1
) and (
2
) is defined as
\Delta F=F(y=0^{+})-F(y=0^{-})
and fluxes are oriented in the direction of increasing
y
.
A steady flow of solute is imposed by a pressure drop
\delta P
in the reservoir channel resulting in a uniform concentration
C_{0}
upstream of the membrane (
y=0^{+}
), and thus a uniform osmotic pressure
\Pi_{0}=RTC_{0}
along its length
L
. The dead-end channel is in mechanical equilibrium with atmospheric pressure
P_{\text{atm}}
(no external mechanical pressure imposed) and only contains water at time
t=0
.

In the following, we consider the limit case of dominant forward osmosis,
\Delta\Pi\gg\Delta P
.
The initial difference in solute concentration across the membrane
\Delta C(t=0)=C_{0}
, associated with an initial osmotic pressure difference
\Delta\Pi=\Pi_{0}
, induces a volume flux through the membrane (local flux
J_{v}\simeq\mathcal{L}_{p}\sigma\Delta\Pi
), and thus a flow in the channel with an entrance volume flux
V_{i}
.
As the membrane is also permeable to the solute (
\sigma<1
), the difference in solute concentration across the membrane tends to homogenize the concentration, see the term
\sim-\mathcal{L}_{D}\Delta C
in the solute flux
J_{s}
in eqn (
2
), while the term
\sim(1-\sigma)\langle C\rangle J_{v}
corresponds to a replenishment of the reservoir channel by advection of solutes by the osmotic flow from the channel through the membrane.
In all cases, the solute flux
J_{s}
leads to a solute accumulation in the channel until equilibrium with the reservoir at long times, so the osmotically-induced volume flux
V_{i}
should decrease with time.

To describe this dynamics, we consider the limit in which the solute transport through the membrane is dominated by diffusion, so that the advective term in eqn (
2
) is negligible, and thus
J_{s}\simeq-\mathcal{L}_{D}\Delta C
which make constitutive equation linear. We thus assume:

\mathrm{Pe}=\frac{\sigma\mathcal{L}_{p}RTC_{0}}{\mathcal{L}_{D}}\ll 1.

(3)

To consider a 1D model with almost uniform concentration across the transverse dimensions, i.e.,
C(x,y,z,t)\simeq C(x,t)
,
we also assume that the membrane resistance to solute transfer
\sim 1/\mathcal{L}_{D}
is larger than that of the channel
\sim w/D_{s}
(
D_{s}
being the solute diffusion coefficient in solution), i.e.

\mathcal{R}=\frac{w\mathcal{L}_{D}}{D_{s}}\ll 1.

(4)

This assumption ensures that the concentration is uniform across the channel cross-section in the absence of flow. When trans-membrane osmotic flows are present, the Péclet number relative to mass transfer in the channel is
\sigma\mathcal{L}_{p}RTC_{0}w/D_{s}=\mathrm{Pe}\mathcal{R}
, thus small compared to one, and concentrations are also unifom in this case.

With these two assumptions, the average concentration in the channel, defined by
\bar{C}=\int_{0}^{L}C(x,t)\text{d}x/L
, verifies the equation of solute conservation:

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}{\overline{C}}}{\text{d}t}=-\frac{1}{Lw}\int_{0}^{L}J_{s}(x,t)\text{d}x=\frac{\mathcal{L}_{D}}{w}(C_{0}-\overline{C}),

(5)

due to the zero flux boundary conditions on all boundaries, except the membrane, for which the local flux is given by eqn (
2
).
Time integration of eqn (
5
) finally leads to an exponential increase:

\displaystyle\overline{C}=C_{0}\left[1-\exp\left(-\frac{\mathcal{L}_{D}t}{w}\right)\right].

(6)

Note that if there is no osmotic flow (
J_{v}=0
) but only permeation of the solute through the membrane (
J_{s}\neq 0
), this same equation also describes the evolution of the concentration in the channel, uniform in that case
C(x,t)=C(t)

Nguyen et al. 2020
.
The fact that
\overline{C}
in the case of osmotically-induced flows follows the same equation than
C(t)
without flow stems from the assumptions (
3
) and (
4
), and the dead-end geometry of the channel.

Because of the global conservation of mass, the osmotically-driven entrance volume flux in the channel
V_{i}
is given by:

\displaystyle V_{i}=\frac{1}{w}\int_{0}^{L}J_{v}(x,t)\text{d}x=\frac{L}{w}\mathcal{L}_{p}\sigma RT(C_{0}-\overline{C})\,,

(7)

and thus evolves along:

\displaystyle V_{i}=\sigma V_{0}\exp\left(-\frac{\mathcal{L}_{D}\,t}{w}\right),

(8)

with
V_{0}=(L/w)\mathcal{L}_{P}RTC_{0}
the expected entrance volume flux for an ideal semi-permeable membrane (
\sigma=1
).

The trivial behavior of
\overline{C}
and
V_{i}
expressed in eqn (
6
) and (
8
), despite the spatio-temporal dynamics of both the concentration
C(x,t)
and volume flux
V(x,t)
fields, stems from assumptions (
3
) and (
4
) which allow a 1D description and which imply the linearity of the fluxes
J_{v}(x,t)
and
J_{s}(x,t)
with the concentration difference
\Delta C=[C_{0}-C(x,t)]
.

II.2
Limit of forward osmosis and concentration boundary layer

The above analysis first requires that the trans-membrane pressure difference
\Delta P
is negligible compared to
\Delta\Pi
.
The flow imposed in the reservoir channel, at a pressure drop
\delta P
, and that induced by osmosis in the channel, associated to pressure drop
\delta P_{\text{osm}}
, both set the order of magnitude of the trans-membrane pressure term
\Delta P\sim(\delta P+\delta P_{\text{osm}})
in eqn (
1
).
The limit of forward osmosis thus imposes that
\delta P\ll\Pi_{0}
and the following constraint on the hydraulic resistance
R_{c}
of the channel:
\delta P_{\text{osm}}\sim R_{c}(hw)V_{i}\ll\Pi_{0}
.

Importantly, the previous model also assumes that there is no concentration boundary layer in the reservoir channel so that the concentration upstream of the membrane is
C(y=0^{+})=C_{0}
over its entire length.

Sketch of the configuration studied by Pedley

C_{0}

\sigma=1

J_{v}

\delta

y=0^{+}
along the membrane, negligible when condition (
9

This corresponds to the crucial issue of the external concentration polarization in forward osmosis processes
Cath, Childress, and Elimelech 2006
, and for which microfluidics allow direct observations, see for example
Jiao et al. 2019
.
Pedley
Pedley 1981
has theoretically studied this effect in the case of forward osmosis and
a pure shear flow over an ideal semi-permeable membrane (
\sigma=1
), assuming shear flow is undisturbed by trans-membrane flow, see Fig.
2
.
In particular, he has shown that the solute concentration at
y=0^{+}
remains close to
C_{0}
along the membrane as long as:

\displaystyle\beta=J_{v}\left(\frac{9L}{\dot{\gamma}D_{s}^{2}}\right)^{1/3}\ll 1\,,

(9)

in which
\dot{\gamma}
is the shear rate,
J_{v}=\mathcal{L}_{p}RTC_{0}
, and
\beta
a dimensionless group that compares the time scale
\sim\delta/J_{v}
of advection across the concentration boundary layer of width
\delta\simeq(D_{s}L/\dot{\gamma})^{1/3}
, with the time scale
\sim\delta^{2}/D_{s}
for the solute diffusion across
\delta
. Pedley also demonstrated that his analysis remains valid for a flow in a channel of width
w
, provided that
\delta\ll w
(i.e., shear rate is almost constant across the boundary layer). The condition of undisturbed flow is in this case
Q_{\text{osm}}=(hL)J_{v}\ll Q
, with
Q
the flow rate in the reservoir channel. The 2D case studied by Pedley differs from the 3D Poiseuille flow in Fig.
1
, but it is reasonable to consider that condition (
9
) still holds as soon as
\delta
is also smaller than
h
and by evaluating
\dot{\gamma}
as the height-averaged shear rate at
y=0
. The validity of eqns (
6
) and (
8
), therefore depends on the validity of condition (
9
),
Q_{\text{osm}}\ll Q
, and of
\delta\ll h\leq w
.

Experimental conditions are chosen such that hypothesis of the previously described model are valid. We then perform time-resolved measurements of entrance volume flux to estimate both
reflection coefficient
\sigma

\mathcal{L}_{D}
of the membrane for multiple solutes, over a wide range of molecular weight and chemical nature.

III
Materials and Methods

III.1
Microfluidic chip and membrane integration

The geometry of the microfluidic chip used throughout this study is shown on Figure
3

, width

\simeq 460\times$30\text{\,}{\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}}^{2}$

IV.1

We first report

along the membrane. Indeed, the continuity equation imposes:

\frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}x}=-\frac{J_{v}}{w}=\frac{\mathcal{L}_{p}\Delta P}{w}\,,

(10)

and the volume flux field is thus
V(x)=V_{i}(1-x/L)
for a uniform hydraulic permeability
\mathcal{L}_{p}
, with:

V_{i}=V(x=0)=\frac{L}{w}J_{v}=-\frac{L}{w}\mathcal{L}_{p}\Delta P\,.

(11)

The linear volume flux field obtained in Fig.
5
a confirms the uniformity of the membrane, and eqn (
11
)
leads to a permeability
\mathcal{L}_{p}\simeq$73\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\text{\,}{\mathrm{bar}}^{-1}$
, thus a membrane resistance
R_{m}\simeq$0.37\text{\,}\mathrm{bar}$
.
This latter value validates the previous hypothesis of uniform trans-membrane pressure difference
\Delta P
, as
R_{m}\gg R_{c}\simeq$2\text{\times}{10}^{-6}\text{\,}\mathrm{bar}$
, with
R_{c}
the hydraulic resistance of channels AB and CD (numerically estimated for water and assuming a rectangular cross-section
Bruus 2007
).

To check whether the hydraulic permeability
\mathcal{L}_{P}
depends on trans-membrane pressure drop
\Delta P

and for a different membrane than the one considered in Fig.

The linear relation

with
\mathcal{L}_{p}=$68\pm 1\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\text{\,}{\mathrm{bar}}^{-1}$

demonstrates both the robustness of the anchoring of the hydrogel membrane to the walls of the microfluidic channel and its low deformation with the imposed mechanical pressure.
Data shown in Fig.
5
b were obtained in about
1\text{\,}\mathrm{h}
(including both data acquisition and images analysis), and thus in a simpler way than the tedious measurements in Ref.
Nguyen et al. 2020
.
This allows us to measure systematically
\mathcal{L}_{p}
for each microfluidic chip discussed in the present work. For ten membranes fabricated according to the same protocol and characterized in the same way, we found
\mathcal{L}_{p}
ranging from
\simeq 60
to
90\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\text{\,}{\mathrm{bar}}^{-1}
.
Assuming Darcy’s law, we relate
\mathcal{L}_{p}
to the Darcy permeability
\kappa
of the hydrogel using:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{p}=\frac{\kappa}{\eta_{w}w_{m}}\,,

(12)

in which
\eta_{w}
is the water viscosity.
Over all the characterized membranes,
w_{m}
varied between
29
and
33\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
(measured by image analysis) and we found
\kappa
ranging from
\simeq 1.8
to
2.6\text{\times}{10}^{-20}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{2}
, values that are close to this reported by Nguyen et al.
Nguyen et al. 2020
, measured for a single membrane (
\kappa=$2.6(0.5)e-20\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{2}$
). This moderate dispersion characterizes the reproducibility of the formulation and integration process of the membrane.

IV.2
Membrane solute permeability

The transport of solutes through an hydrogel is often described by two parameters: the diffusivity
D_{m}
of the solute in the hydrogel and the partition coefficient
k=C_{m}/C_{0}
, which is the ratio at equilibrium between the solute concentration in the hydrogel
C_{m}
and in solution
C_{0}

Kotsmar et al. 2012
;
Paustian et al. 2013
;
Matsuyama, Teramoto, and Urano 1997
. Assuming Fick’s law and homogeneity of the hydrogel membrane, the solute permeability coefficient in eqn (
2
) is thus given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{D}=\frac{kD_{m}}{w_{m}}\,.

(13)

Nguyen et al.
Nguyen et al. 2020
estimated the product
kD_{m}
by measuring the permeation rate of fluorescent probes of various molecular weights
M_{w}
through the membrane. These experimental data, shown in Fig.
6
(red squares) highlight a powerlaw dependence with
M_{w}
,
kD_{m}\sim M_{w}^{-\alpha}
with
\alpha\simeq 2.3
. This allows to estimate that these hydrogel membranes have a molecular weight cut-off (MWCO) close to a few
\text{\,}\mathrm{kg}\text{\,}{\mathrm{mol}}^{-1}
in their microfluidic geometry (channel width
150\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
, membrane width
25\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
).

(

.

. Observations also evidence that there is no visible color gradient in the transverse dimensions of channel CD, demonstrating that the mass transport process is limited by the permeation of methylene blue through the membrane and not by diffusion across channel CD, so that condition (
4
) is valid for this solute.
The dye concentration also remains homogeneous along the channel (data not shown), suggesting that convection is negligible. In this regime, the solute flux through the membrane is dominated by diffusion (eqn (
2
) reduces to
J_{s}\simeq-\mathcal{L}_{D}\Delta C
), so that the solute concentration in channel CD is expected to evolve as:

(14)

ww_{m}/kD_{m}
being a characteristic time of the filling of channel CD with the solute. Note that eqn (
14
) implicitly assumes a quasi-steady gradient of solute within the hydrogel membrane, and thus is valid at times
t\gg w_{m}^{2}/D_{m}
.
As noted in Sec.
II
, this relation is similar to eqn (
6
) [with
\mathcal{L}_{D}
given by eqn (
13
)] that describes the average solute concentration
\bar{C}
within channel CD but in the case of transient osmotic flows.

I
in channel CD and we computed the absorbance
A=-\log_{10}(I/I_{\text{ref}})
with
I_{\text{ref}}
the average intensity in channel CD at
t=0
(only water). We then normalized the absorbance by this measured in channel AB, and converted the result into a normalized concentration
C_{n}=C/C_{0}
.
Data shown in Fig.

kD_{m}\simeq$1.5\text{\times}{10}^{-11}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{2}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}$
for this molecular dye.
This value also further confirms
the validity of eqn (
4
) as
\mathcal{R}\simeq 0.2
(the diffusivity in solution of methylene blue in solution is
D_{s}\simeq$6.7\text{\times}{10}^{-10}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{2}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}$

Selifonov et al. 2019
).
The value of
kD_{m}
for methylene blue plotted in Fig.
6
(black diamond) is also in agreement with the measurements previously obtained by Nguyen et al.
Nguyen et al. 2020
. In particular, Nguyen et al. found
kD_{m}\simeq$1.2\text{\times}{10}^{-11}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{2}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}$
for fluorescein, a solute with a molecular weight (
M_{w}\simeq$332\text{\,}\mathrm{g}\text{\,}{\mathrm{mol}}^{-1}$
) close to that of methylene blue (
M_{w}\simeq$320\text{\,}\mathrm{g}\text{\,}{\mathrm{mol}}^{-1}$
).
It should be noted that our measurements show no clear lag times linked to the establishment of the solute concentration gradient within the membrane at early times.
This is consistent with a rough estimate of
D_{m}
assuming

as in Ref.
Nguyen et al. 2020
leading to
w_{m}^{2}/D_{m}\simeq$30\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$
. Such a time scale is of the order the time required to impose a fixed concentration of methylene blue in channel AB
(
\simeq$10\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$
). Nevertheless, more precise estimates of

could be obtained from the expected lag time, but with significantly larger membrane thicknesses
w_{m}
.

IV.3
Steady osmotic flows

The hydrogel membranes characterized above are permeable to water and have a molecular weight cut-off MWCO of a few
\text{\,}\mathrm{kg}\text{\,}{\mathrm{mol}}^{-1}
, see Figs.
5
and
6
. They could therefore generate steady osmotic flows when solutes of molecular weight
M_{w}>\mathrm{MWCO}
are involved.
Indeed, one expects for such solutes that the reflection coefficient
\sigma\to 1
and the solute flux
J_{s}\to 0
. Stable osmotic pressure difference
\Delta\Pi
across the membrane should thus generate steady volume flux
J_{v}
given by eqn (
1
).

) at concentration

was imposed in channel AB with a pressure difference

\delta P=P_{\text{A}}-P_{\text{B}}\simeq$50\text{\,}\mathrm{mbar}$
(

, the trans-membrane pressure difference is of order of

has been measured prior to these experiments, the measured volume fluxes

of the polymer solution is displayed in Fig.

M_{w}=$17.5\text{\,}\mathrm{kg}\text{\,}{\mathrm{mol}}^{-1}$

Comtet et al. 2017

\Pi\simeq aRTC_{0}

with

and

Marbach, Yoshida, and Bocquet 2017

The previous results are conditioned by the assumption that the concentration boundary layer in channel AB plays a negligible role, see Sec. (

), and in particular the validity of eqn (
9

In data presented in Fig.

and

that ensures the condition
\beta\ll 1
over a wide range of shear rates.

IV.4
Transient osmotic flow: the case of sucrose

In this section, we address the case of solutes with a molecular weight smaller than the molecular weight cut-off of the hydrogel, i.e.,
M_{w}\leq\mathrm{MWCO}
. In that case, the solute can both permeate through the hydrogel membrane and induce osmotic flows as soon as the reflection coefficient is not strictly
\sigma=0
. We first studied the case of solutions of sucrose of molecular weight
M_{w}=$342\text{\,}\mathrm{g}\text{\,}{\mathrm{mol}}^{-1}$
and concentration
C_{0}=$0.148\text{\,}\mathrm{mol}\text{\,}{\mathrm{L}}^{-1}$
(
5\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
wt). At this concentration, the osmotic pressure of the sucrose solution is well-approximated by the van’t Hoff relation, and
\Pi_{0}=RTC_{0}\simeq$3.6\text{\,}\mathrm{bar}$

Starzak and Peacock 1997
.
The experimental protocol is the following, see Fig.
8
b: channel CD was first filled with dispersed fluorescent tracers and outlet D was closed.
At
t=0
, a steady flow of the sucrose solution was imposed in channel AB by applying a pressure drop
\delta P=P_{\text{A}}-P_{\text{B}}=$100\text{\,}\mathrm{mbar}$
(
P_{\text{B}}=P_{\text{atm}}
) while measuring the entrance volume flux
V_{i}
in channel CD as a function of time
t
.

Measurements shown in Fig.
10
demonstrate a volume flux directed towards the membrane due to osmosis, as in the case of the dextran solution, but which decreases continuously from
V_{i}\simeq$11\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}$
at
t\simeq$0\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$
to
V_{i}\leq$1\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}$
at
t\simeq$1\text{\,}\mathrm{h}$
. This decay is associated with the permeation of sucrose through the membrane and its accumulation in channel CD until equilibrium.
In these conditions,
\beta\simeq 0.01\ll 1
and
\Delta P\sim\delta P\ll\Delta\Pi
(see ESI†), so that the model of Sec.
II
can be applied, taking channel AB as reservoir and channel CD as a dead-end channel in which solute accumulates.

V
Conclusions

In the present work, we have theoretically revealed a microfluidic configuration to measure simultaneously the permeation coefficient of a solute through a membrane
\mathcal{L}_{D}
and the associated reflection coefficient
\sigma
.
This configuration exploits microfluidic confinement to relate these two parameters to the relaxation dynamics of an osmotic flow in a dead-end microchannel,

V_{i}
vs.
t
in eqn (
8
), separated from another channel that plays the role of solute reservoir.
The range of validity of this configuration has been studied and depends both on channel geometry, membrane characteristics, solute concentration and diffusion coefficient, see Sec.
II
.
Interestingly, if the dead-end channel were closed,
J_{v}=0
in eqn (
1
), solute permeation through the membrane would result in a temporal relaxation of hydrostatic pressure
P
in the channel following the same dynamics as that of
V_{i}
vs.
t
.
This configuration, close to that of membrane osmometers used to measure the osmotic pressure, could then also be used to measure
\mathcal{L}_{D}
and
\sigma
, by a simple pressure measurement,
P

We also believe that the tools and methods described in the present work could be relevant to microfluidic models of sap transport in plants
