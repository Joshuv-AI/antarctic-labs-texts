Development and clinical validation of a multiplex visual POCT platform based on RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a for porcine diarrheal viruses
Authors: Ying Chen, Meijun Yu, Jinghan Lin, Zhaojun Wang, Yijie Yang, Xijiao Shao, Yin Zhu, Ping Han, Tianyu Han, Jiaxuan Li, Wen Cui, Yanping Jiang, Lijie Tang, Xiaona Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPorcine epidemic diarrhea virus (PEDV), porcine rotavirus A (PoRVA), and porcine sapelovirus (PSV) are major causative agents of severe diarrhea in swine and frequently occur as co-infections, leading to significant financial losses. To address the urgent demand for rapid, on-site, and multiplex detection, we developed a multiplex reverse transcription recombinase-aided amplification (RT-RAA)-CRISPR/Cas12a assay for visual point-of-care testing (POCT) diagnosis. The assay exhibited high specificity for the three target viruses, with no cross-reactivity against non-target swine pathogens. Under optimized conditions, the system achieved single-copy-level sensitivity, with limits of detection (LOD) of 5.2, 5.9, and 0.57 copies/μL for PEDV, PoRVA, and PSV, respectively, and demonstrated excellent repeatability (coefficient of variation (CV) < 10%). Validation with 20 clinical fecal swabs samples and a portable detection toolbox revealed 95%–100% concordance with standard reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) methods. The entire “sample-to-answer” workflow was completed within 55 min. This platform provides a powerful and user-friendly tool for POCT of mixed viral infections in swine herds.Keywords: CRISPR/Cas12a, RT-RAA, Multiplex detection, Point-of-care testing (POCT), Porcine diarrheal viruses

Introduction

Making up roughly 34% of global meat consumption, the swine industry serves as a key component of worldwide food security, the agricultural economy, and international trade [1,2]. In swine husbandry, improving pork production efficiency remains a core industrial objective; based on research in swine nutrition and production, gut health is highlighted as a pivotal focus [1]. Porcine diarrheal diseases represent the primary obstacle to maintaining gut health, clinically characterized by growth retardation induced by diarrhea and an extremely high mortality rate in infected neonatal piglets. Notably, viral diarrhea spreads rapidly, is difficult to control, and frequently involves co-infections, resulting in suboptimal vaccine efficacy and clinical treatment outcomes [3]. This not only inflicts substantial economic losses on the global swine industry but also poses a significant threat to human food safety.

Porcine epidemic diarrhea virus (PEDV), porcine rotavirus A (PoRVA), and porcine sapelovirus (PSV) are the principal pathogens causing swine diarrhea [[4], [5], [6]]. These viruses often manifest as co-infections with indistinguishable clinical symptoms, posing significant challenges for differential diagnosis [7]. PEDV belongs to the Alphacoronavirus genus in the Coronaviridae family and is an enveloped, single-stranded RNA virus with a positive sense and a genome of about 28 kb [8,9]. PEDV infection primarily affects neonatal piglets and is characterized by acute watery diarrhea, vomiting, dehydration, and high mortality rates, especially in piglets less than 7 days old. Infected pigs typically exhibit severe enteritis and villous atrophy in the small intestine, leading to malabsorption and rapid weight loss. These clinical manifestations often result in substantial economic losses to the swine industry [8,9]. PoRVA is a segmented double-stranded RNA virus from the Rotavirus genus within the Reoviridae family, with a genome size of about 18 kb [10]. As a critical pathogen causing diarrhea in young pigs, it results in high mortality and growth retardation; its high strain diversity and widespread transmission severely threaten the development of the global swine industry [11]. PSV, a member of the genus Sapelovirus in the family Picornaviridae, is a non-enveloped, single-stranded, positive-sense RNA virus with a genome of approximately 7500 bp [12]. PSV infection shows up as multiple symptoms, such as severe diarrhea, breathing difficulties, skin damage, brain inflammation, and fertility issues, that get milder as pigs age, and the infection sources are common, including groups with hidden infections [12]. The high rates of asymptomatic infection and co-infection associated with PSV mean its clinical manifestations are frequently overlooked, constituting a potential risk to the swine industry.

Currently, a diverse array of methods is available for detecting swine viruses. Among these, reverse transcription quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) is regarded as the “gold standard” for laboratory diagnosis due to its high sensitivity, strong specificity, and mature technical foundation [4]; Other common laboratory methods include conventional polymerase chain reaction (PCR), immunofluorescence assays, and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) [13]. Although these methods are sensitive and specific, they are labor-intensive, time-consuming, and reliant on sophisticated instrumentation, making them difficult to implement for on-site detection in remote areas such as swine farms. Considering the rapid transmission and short window period of viral diseases, “early detection, early diagnosis, and early prevention” are critical for interrupting transmission chains. Thus, it is imperative to establish a point-of-care testing (POCT) molecular technology that is simultaneously rapid, efficient, sensitive, specific, and cost-effective.

During recent years, clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR) and CRISPR-associated (Cas) proteins have emerged as a research hotspot in pathogen detection due to their efficient and precise capabilities for nucleic acid recognition and cleavage [[14], [15], [16]]. CRISPR/Cas12a (also known as CRISPR/Cpf1), a Class 2 Type V RNA-guided single-effector protein, was found by Doudna et al. to possess non-specific ssDNA cleavage activity upon binding to target dsDNA [17]; based on this property, a nucleic acid detection technology named DETECTR (DNA Endonuclease-Targeted CRISPR Trans Reporter) was developed by combining Cas12a with recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA). Based on this strategy, isothermal amplification plays a crucial role in CRISPR-based detection systems by providing sufficient target nucleic acid for signal generation. Recombinase-aided amplification (RAA), a rapid isothermal amplification technique, operates at a constant temperature of 37-42 °C and relies on the synergistic action of recombinase, single-stranded DNA-binding proteins, and strand-displacing DNA polymerase to achieve efficient amplification without thermal cycling. Due to its rapid amplification kinetics and minimal equipment requirements, RAA is particularly suitable for point-of-care testing [18]. Based on this, we combined reverse transcription recombinase-aided amplification (RT-RAA) with the CRISPR/Cas12a system to develop a highly sensitive, specific, and low-cost multiplex RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a detection method, which significantly improves amplification and detection efficiency. Additionally, we designed and assembled a field detection toolbox (Fig. 1), equipped with a portable blue light flashlight and anti-blue light goggles. This system enables rapid, visual “sample-to-answer” detection within 55 min without the need for expensive commercial lateral flow strips.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

Synthetic oligonucleotides, encompassing all crRNAs, ssDNA reporters, and primers, were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) and Kumei Biology (Changchun, China), respectively. The essential enzyme, EnGen® Lba Cas12a (Cpf1) (Catalog No. M0653S), along with NEB buffer r2.1, was procured from New England Biolabs (Ipswich, MA, USA). The RT-RAA Nucleic Acid Amplification Kit (Catalog No. B00R00A) was sourced from Jiangsu Qitian Gene Biotechnology (Jiangsu, China). Escherichia coli DH5α competent cells and the pMD-19T Simple Vector were acquired from Takara Biomedical Technology (Beijing, China). Regarding biological materials, the viral isolates used in this study were isolated, molecularly identified, and preserved by our laboratory. These included porcine epidemic diarrhea virus (Strain CH/HLJ/18), porcine rotavirus A (Strain HLJ/2021), porcine sapelovirus (Strain HLJ 2204), porcine deltacoronavirus (Strain CH-HLJ-20), transmissible gastroenteritis virus (Strain TH-98), porcine teschovirus (Strain HLJ 2024), and porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus (Strain BX/CH/22). Prior to use in this study, the identity and purity of all viral stocks were experimentally re-validated using virus-specific RT-qPCR/RT-PCR and confirmed to be positive for their respective targets. Clinical diarrhea samples were collected from a commercial pig farm in Harbin, Heilongjiang Province, China, in strict accordance with ethical guidelines.

Construction of recombinant standard plasmids

Reference sequences of PEDV, PoRVA, and PSV were obtained from GenBank (NCBI). Multiple sequence alignments revealed conserved regions in the N gene of PEDV (GenBank Accession No. MW561264.1), the VP6 gene of PoRVA (GenBank Accession No. OR091161.1), and the 2C gene of PSV (GenBank Accession No. PP842133.1). PCR primers were designed for these regions (Table S1), and the gene fragments were amplified and cloned into the pMD-19T Simple Vector. The recombinant plasmids were introduced into Escherichia coli DH5α cells and their sequences were verified through Sanger sequencing at Kumei Biology, Changchun, China.

Viral RNA extraction

The PEDV (Vero-E6 cells), PoRVA (MA104 cells), and PSV (PK-15 cells) strains were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Viral culture supernatants were harvested when the cytopathic effect (CPE) reached 80%. Total viral RNA was extracted from 200 μL of viral culture supernatants or clinical sample suspensions using TRIzol reagent (Solarbio, Beijing, China). Briefly, 1 mL of TRIzol reagent and 200 μL of chloroform were added, mixed thoroughly, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The upper aqueous phase was collected, and RNA was precipitated with an equal volume of isopropanol, washed with 75% ethanol, and finally dissolved in 50 μL of RNase-free water. The RNA concentration and purity were determined using a spectrophotometer. The extracted RNA was then aliquoted and stored at -80 °C until use.

Design and screening of RAA primers, crRNAs, and ssDNA reporters

RAA primers targeting conserved regions of the PEDV N gene, PoRVA VP6 gene, and PSV 2C gene were designed using Oligo 7 software (Table S2). RT-RAA was performed using a commercial RT-RAA kit (Jiangsu Qitian Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, the reaction mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 40 min (recommended standard conditions). After amplification, the products were purified using a DNA extraction reagent (Meiji Biotechnology Co., Guangzhou, China). Briefly, an equal volume of DNA extraction reagent (1:1, 50 μL:50 μL) was added to the reaction mixture, vortexed thoroughly, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was collected as the purified amplicon for subsequent analysis. The amplification products were purified and analyzed by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis to select the optimal primer combination.

Each crRNA consisted of a conserved scaffold sequence (5′-UAAUUUCUACUAAGUGUAGAU-3′) and a target-specific spacer. Candidate spacers (23 nt) adjacent to TTTV protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) sites within conserved target regions were screened using the zlab CRISPR guide design tool (https://zlab.bio/guide-design-resources). The predicted cleavage efficiency and off-target risk were assessed using CCTop (https://cctop.cos.uni-heidelberg.de/), and RNAfold was used to exclude guides with unfavorable secondary structures. In addition, an A/T-rich ssDNA reporter labeled with FAM and BHQ1 was designed to match the collateral cleavage preference of Cas12a (Table S3) [19].

Establishment and optimization of the RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay

Based on the protocol by Broughton et al., the CRISPR/Cas12a detection method was created with modifications [20]. The standard 12 μL reaction system contained 1.2 μL of 10 × NEB buffer r2.1, 1 μL of LbCas12a (1 μM), 0.5 μL of crRNA (5 μM), 3 μL of ssDNA reporter (10 μM), 1 μL of purified RAA amplicon, and DEPC-treated water to volume. The reactions were incubated for 30 min at 37 °C in a Gentier 96E Real-time PCR System (Xian Tianlong, China) with fluorescence recorded every 5 min, followed by endpoint visualization under a blue/UV transilluminator upon reaction completion. This system was also used for crRNA screening. To verify the feasibility and necessity of each component, control reactions lacking Cas12a, crRNA, template, or the ssDNA reporter were performed. Additionally, the detection performance of the CRISPR/Cas12a system alone versus the coupled RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a system was compared using plasmid templates (100 ng/μL). To assess cis-cleavage activity, reactions without the ssDNA reporter were incubated at 37 °C for 90 min, heat-inactivated at 85 °C for 5 min, and analyzed by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis.

To maximize detection sensitivity, reaction conditions were optimized. Cas12a (25, 50, 75, 100, 150 nM) and crRNA (50, 100, 150, 200, 300 nM) were cross-titrated to determine the optimal ratio, followed by optimization of the ssDNA reporter concentration (0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 μM) [21], incubation time (0–60 min), and temperature (31–41 °C). Optimal conditions were determined based on peak fluorescence intensity (RFU) and visual detection performance.

Establishment and optimization of the multiplex RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay

Optimization of primer concentrations

To determine the optimal primer concentrations, assays were carried out with recombinant plasmids (100 ng/μL) as the templates. Initially, singleplex assays were assessed using primer concentrations ranging from 0.5 to 10 μM. Based on the effective concentration range obtained in the singleplex assays, the primer concentrations for duplex (PEDV + PoRVA) and triplex (PEDV + PoRVA + PSV) systems were further optimized at a narrower range of 1.25, 2.5, and 5 μM for each target. Amplification products were analyzed using 2% agarose gel electrophoresis as well as the CRISPR/Cas12a assay. Subsequently, in order to simulate complex clinical matrices to address the potential competition of primers, viral culture supernatants (collected at 48 h post-infection) were adopted to optimize the primer ratios of the multiplex system (Table S4). Fluorescence intensity and visual inspection under blue light were used for determining the optimal concentrations.

Optimization of reaction conditions

The reaction temperature (35, 37, 39, 41 °C) and time (15, 25, 35, 45 min) of the multiplex RT-RAA assay were optimized. Products have been analyzed by gel electrophoresis to determine band specificity and intensity. To further ensure sensitivity for low virus loads, reaction time was optimized with the best temperature using a template concentration gradient (1, 10, 20 ng/μL). This step was aimed at the determination of the minimum effective amplification time required for the detection of low-level concentrations of targets [22].

Feasibility validation of the multiplex system

Comprehensive feasibility validation was performed under optimized conditions. Seven reaction groups were established: singleplex (PEDV, PoRVA, or PSV), duplex (PEDV + PoRVA, PEDV + PSV, or PoRVA + PSV), and triplex (PEDV + PoRVA + PSV). Notably, for all feasibility and clinical assays, the RT-RAA step was conducted in a single tube using a unified multiplex master mix containing all three primer pairs. This setup ensured a consistent competitive environment for all reaction groups. The RT-RAA products were analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis. Subsequently, aliquots from the single-tube amplification were added to separate CRISPR/Cas12a detection systems, each containing a target-specific crRNA. Fluorescence signals were visualized under blue light to evaluate the system's ability to accurately discriminate between single and mixed targets.

Analytical performance evaluation

Sensitivity

To establish the limit of detection (LOD), recombinant plasmids (pMD-19T-PEDV, pMD-19T-PoRVA, and pMD-19T-PSV) were diluted in a series of 10-fold steps, ranging from approximately 5 × 104 to 10-1 copies/μL (specifically, 5.2 × 104 for PEDV, 5.9 × 104 for PoRVA, and 5.7 × 104 for PSV). The optimized multiplex assay was performed at 37 °C for 30 min, with fluorescence recorded every 5 min, followed by endpoint visual inspection under a blue/UV light source after the reaction was completed.

Specificity

Specificity was evaluated using nucleic acids from target viruses (PEDV, PoRVA, PSV) and non-target swine pathogens, including PDCoV, TGEV, PTV and PRRSV. DEPC-treated water served as the no-template control (NTC). Following multiplex RT-RAA reaction, the products were individually tested against each specific CRISPR/Cas12a system (targeting PEDV, PoRVA, or PSV). Specificity was confirmed by the exclusive generation of fluorescence signals in the presence of the corresponding target virus.

Repeatability

Repeatability was assessed using RT-qPCR-confirmed positive clinical samples (PEDV, PoRVA, PSV) and negative controls. Intra-and inter-assay variability were evaluated by testing samples in triplicate within a single run and over three separate trials conducted on different days, respectively. The coefficient of variation (CV) of the fluorescence intensity was calculated, with a value of < 10% considered acceptable [23,24].

Stability of the fluorescence signal

To evaluate signal stability for on-site applications, positive and negative reaction mixtures (n = 3) were stored at room temperature (RT, approximately 25 °C) in the dark over a 6-day period. Fluorescence signals were monitored at 6 h, 12 h, 24 h, 48 h, 96 h, and 144 h post-reaction [22].

Determination of the cut-off value

The cut-off value was established using a previously outlined method with slight alterations [23]. Thirty clinical samples in total were analyzed, which were confirmed negative for PEDV, PoRVA, and PSV by RT-qPCR, and these samples were included in the present study. To evaluate the discriminative power of the cut-off value, 10-fold serial dilutions of standard plasmids for PEDV, PoRVA, and PSV (ranging from 104 to 10-1 copies/μL) were prepared and tested as positive controls alongside the negative samples. Each sample was tested in triplicate. The mean fluorescence intensities (mean) and standard deviations (SD) of these negative samples were calculated. The cut-off point was defined as the mean + 3SD. Samples that gave fluorescence signals greater than this were classified as positive.

Detection of clinical samples

To evaluate clinical accuracy, 20 fecal swab samples collected from swine farms were analyzed using the multiplex assay, conventional RT-PCR, and RT-qPCR simultaneously. Primers for the reference methods are listed in Table S1. Diagnostic positivity was defined as follows: (1) multiplex assay: fluorescence intensity > cut-off and visual detection; (2) RT-PCR: presence of specific bands via gel electrophoresis; and (3) RT-qPCR: cycle threshold (Ct) value ≤ 35 [25,26].

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was conducted three times (n = 3), and statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Differences between two groups were assessed using Student's t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by a multiple comparison test. Data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical significance was defined as ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001; “ns” indicates not significant.

Results

Construction and feasibility verification of a visual RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a detection strategy

The CRISPR/Cas12a detection mechanism relies on Cas12a-crRNA complexes identifying and cutting target sequences, which subsequently activate collateral cleavage activity (crRNA design shown in Fig. 2A). To verify the feasibility of this design, the collateral cleavage activity was first evaluated. Fluorescence signals were observed exclusively in the complete reaction system containing Cas12a, crRNA, ssDNA reporter, and target templates, whereas no detectable fluorescence was generated in control groups lacking any individual component (Fig. 2B). These results indicate that activation of the CRISPR/Cas12a system strictly depends on the integrity of all essential reaction components. The cis-cleavage capability of the system was further assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis. As shown in Fig. 2C–E, the designed crRNAs effectively guided Cas12a to cleave the corresponding substrates in the presence of the enzyme, confirming the high targeting efficiency of the crRNAs. Additionally, the contribution of RT-RAA pre-amplification was assessed. The incorporation of RT-RAA significantly enhanced the fluorescence intensity compared to direct CRISPR/Cas12a detection (using 100 ng/μL of PEDV recombinant plasmid as a representative template), indicating a substantial improvement in detection sensitivity (Fig. 2F). Collectively, these results demonstrate the successful establishment of the RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a approach and its feasibility for highly sensitive visual detection.

Screening of optimal RAA primers and crRNAs

To establish a high-efficiency RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay, RAA primers were first screened. Multiple primer pairs targeting the PEDV N, PoRVA VP6, and PSV 2C genes were designed and amplified using the corresponding recombinant plasmids as templates. Agarose gel electrophoresis and band densitometry showed that the PEDV-F1/R1, PoRVA-F2/R2, and PSV-F2/R2 pairs produced the highest amplicon yields and were selected for subsequent crRNA screening (Fig. 3A). Next, candidate crRNAs for each virus were evaluated. RT-RAA amplicons generated with the selected primers were added to CRISPR/Cas12a reactions containing individual crRNAs. Endpoint fluorescence and kinetic analyses revealed that PEDV-crRNA1 (Fig. 3B), PoRVA-crRNA2 (Fig. 3C), and PSV-crRNA2 (Fig. 3D) yielded the strongest signals. Thus, the combinations PEDV-crRNA1-F1/R1, PoRVA-crRNA2-F2/R2, and PSV-crRNA2-F2/R2 were used in all downstream experiments.

Panels display endpoint fluorescence (left), real-time kinetics (middle), and visual detection (right). All results are from three independent experiments. Data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 3). ns, not significant; ∗∗∗P < 0.001; ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001.

Optimization of the single-plex RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a system

To increase sensitivity and shorten reaction time, the CRISPR/Cas12a reaction conditions were systematically optimized using the PEDV recombinant plasmid as a representative template. Orthogonal tests revealed that a combination of 75 nM Cas12a and 200 nM crRNA yielded the highest fluorescence intensity (Fig. 4A). Under these conditions, the ssDNA reporter concentration was titrated; signal increased with probe concentration and plateaued at 3 μM, with no visible enhancement beyond this point (Fig. 4B), so 3 μM was chosen as the working concentration. Reaction time and temperature were then optimized; fluorescence reached a plateau after 30 min (Fig. 4C–S1A) and was maximal at 37 °C (Fig. 4D, Fig. S1B).

A no-template control (NTC), containing the ssDNA reporter and all reagents without the target template, was included in each experiment to monitor background fluorescence. Establishment, optimization, and compatibility evaluation of the multiplex RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a detection system

To allow for the concurrent detection of PEDV, PoRVA, and PSV, the multiplex RAA primer system was optimized to resolve primer competition (Fig. 5). In single-plex assays using the PEDV recombinant plasmid template (Fig. 5A), robust amplification was observed across primer concentrations of 1.25-10 μM. In the duplex system (PEDV + PoRVA) and triplex system (PEDV + PoRVA + PSV), increasing the total primer concentration generally improved yields, with 5 μM providing the highest total fluorescence (Fig. 5B and C). However, gel electrophoresis revealed an imbalance indicative of competition, where PEDV band intensity was notably lower than that of PoRVA and PSV (Fig. 5C). To correct this bias using clinically relevant templates, we further tuned the primer ratios using viral RNA (Table S4). Fixing the total concentration at 5 μM, a specific volume ratio of 2.5:1:0.5 (PEDV: PoRVA: PSV) was found to generate balanced, high-intensity signals for all targets (Fig. 5D–F). These optimized parameters effectively minimized competition and were applied in the final multiplex CRISPR/Cas12a assay.

(C) Optimization of total primer concentration in the triplex system (PEDV + PoRVA + PSV) using recombinant plasmids. (D–F) Optimization of specific primer ratios using viral RNA. Stepwise adjustment was performed to mitigate primer competition and balance fluorescence intensity among PEDV, PoRVA, and PSV targets. Reaction parameters were optimized for the balance between sensitivity and increased assay speed. As shown in Fig. 6A, using a fixed concentration of recombinant plasmids (20 ng/μL for each target), 37 °C was found to always have the highest amplification efficiency throughout the tested time points, and distinct amplification products were evident as early as 15 min. However, in order to ensure robust detection of low-abundance targets, the reaction time was further optimized. Experiments with diluted templates (1–20 ng/μL) showed a time-dependent signal accumulation (Fig. 6B) so that the final optimal condition of 37 °C for 25 min could be determined. Under these optimized conditions, the compatibility of the system was tested with 7 target combinations (singleplex to triplex). Agarose gel electrophoresis showed the presence of expected-length amplicons with no background products (Fig. 6C). Consistently, the CRISPR/Cas12a assay gave target-specific fluorescence signals for all the test groups with negligible background (Fig. 6C).

Comprehensive evaluation of analytical sensitivity, specificity, repeatability, and signal stability

In order to determine the detection sensitivity of the multiplex RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a platform, recombinant plasmids containing PEDV, PoRVA, and PSV sequences (initial concentrations of 5.2 × 109, 5.9 × 109, and 5.7 × 109 copies/μL, respectively) were subjected to ten-fold serial dilutions, resulting in concentrations ranging from 104 copies/μL to 10-1 copies/μL. Following RT-RAA and CRISPR/Cas12a detection, the limit of detection (LOD) was determined to be 100 copies/μL for both PEDV and PoRVA, and 10-1 copies/μL for PSV. Fluorescence signals at these concentrations were considerably elevated compared to the negative control (Fig. 7A–C). Linear regression analysis demonstrated a strong linear correlation between fluorescence intensity and template copy number (Fig. 7A–C).

Panels display endpoint fluorescence intensities (left), real-time kinetic curves (middle), and linear regression of fluorescence versus log copy number (right). Insets: visual detection under blue light. (D–F) Specificity evaluation for PEDV (D), PoRVA (E), and PSV (F) against non-target viruses (PDCoV, TGEV, PTV, and PRRSV). Data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 3). R2 indicates the coefficient of determination. ns, not significant; ∗∗∗P < 0.001; ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001.

To evaluate platform specificity, other common swine diarrhea-associated viruses (PDCoV, TGEV, and PTV) and PRRSV were used as controls. RNA from these viruses was introduced into the multiplex system. Fluorescence signals were observed exclusively in the channels corresponding to PEDV, PoRVA, and PSV templates. In contrast, negligible fluorescence was detected in the PDCoV, TGEV, PTV, PRRSV, and negative control groups (Fig. 7D–F, Fig. S2). No significant difference in fluorescence intensity was found between non-target virus groups and the negative control. These results confirm the high specificity of the platform in accurately distinguishing target viruses from interfering pathogens.

The multiplex RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a system was subsequently tested in terms of its reproducibility. Intra-assay and inter-assay repeatability tests were conducted on PEDV, PoRVA, and PSV templates. The findings indicated that the CV of the fluorescence signals of all three targets were below 5 and 10 percent in intra-and inter-assays, respectively (Fig. 8A–C, Table S5). In all the negative controls, no signals of fluorescence were observed. The cut-off value of the system was identified by the test of 30 clinical samples, which were confirmed to be negative by RT-qPCR. Based on the mean fluorescence value (mean) plus three standard deviations (3SD) of these negative samples, the cut-off values of PEDV, PoRVA, and PSV were established to be 1.09 × 103, 1.18 × 103, and 1.04 × 103 RFU, respectively (Fig. 8D–F). Notably, the inclusion of 10-fold diluted positive plasmids as controls (Fig. 8D–F) demonstrated that even at the limit of detection, the fluorescence signals of positive samples remained significantly higher than the established cut-off thresholds. This confirms that the mean + 3SD method provides an objective and reliable criterion for distinguishing positive from negative clinical samples. This is an objective point where clinical sample positivity can be determined. Moreover, product stability is imperative for field application. The reaction products stored at room temperature (RT, approximately 25 °C) in the dark revealed stable and specific intensities of fluorescence during a period of 6 days (Fig. 8G).

Field and clinical validation of the multiplex RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a platform

The developed multiplex RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a visual detection method was assessed with regard to its practical utility on 20 swine fecal swab samples, using DEPC-treated water as the negative control (Sample 21). To compare the performance, parallel analysis on the same samples was performed using conventional laboratory methods, namely RT-PCR and RT-qPCR. The multiplex RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay effectively identified viruses of interest, and the fluorescence signals were substantially higher than the cut-off point (Fig. 9A). Agarose gel electrophoresis of standard RT-PCR (Fig. 9B) and Ct values of RT-qPCR (Fig. 9C) confirmed these findings. The results of the detection of PEDV and PoRVA using our technique were in agreement with those of RT-PCR and RT-qPCR at 100%. The consistency rate between our method and the PSV standard methods was 95%. No fluorescence signal was observed in the negative control. These findings suggest that the established multiplex RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a method demonstrates high practicality and reliability for clinical applications.

Discussion

To address the challenges posed by mixed infections of PEDV, PoRVA, and PSV-specifically the difficulty in clinical differentiation and the lack of effective field diagnostics-we developed a portable detection box enabling a complete “sample-in, result-out” workflow without reliance on complex instrumentation. In this study, RT-RAA was combined with CRISPR/Cas12a to improve both the specificity and sensitivity of detection. Based on alignment of conserved regions, we screened RAA primers and crRNA combinations that balanced amplification efficiency with recognition specificity. Studies have shown that off-target effects of Cas proteins can lead to false-positive results [27]; therefore, we pre-evaluated potential off-target risks to ensure the reliability of the detection outcomes. In contrast to single isothermal amplification techniques, which lack high-temperature thermal cycling to prevent primer dimer formation and thus inevitably produce some non-specific amplification, the activation of Cas12a′s trans-cleavage function occurs only after crRNA binds to its designated target [19]. By integrating RT-RAA with CRISPR/Cas12a, this dual-specificity mechanism effectively overcomes the susceptibility of single isothermal amplification to non-specific signal generation. Lu et al. previously developed a Cas12a-based portable paper-based diagnostic platform (Cas-gold) for African swine fever virus (ASFV), achieving a detection limit of 200 copies per reaction [28]. Through systematic optimization of reaction conditions in the present study, we significantly improved detection sensitivity, attaining limits of 5.2 copies/μL for PEDV, 5.9 copies/μL for PoRVA, and as low as 0.57 copies/μL for PSV. These findings indicate that the platform attains single-copy-level sensitivity comparable to the laboratory gold standard RT-qPCR. Interestingly, the LOD for PSV was approximately one order of magnitude lower than those for PEDV and PoRVA. This sensitivity bias is likely attributed to two factors. On one hand, the shorter amplicon size of PSV facilitates more efficient strand displacement during the isothermal RT-RAA process, as recombinase-based isothermal amplification (e.g., RPA/RAA) generally performs better with relatively short amplicons [29]. On the other hand, the variation in crRNA spacer sequences can lead to differences in Cas12a activation and trans-cleavage efficiency [30]. The PSV-specific crRNA may exhibit optimized binding thermodynamics or fewer inhibitory secondary structures, thus enhancing the system's ability to discriminate signals at near-limit concentrations. Conversely, the RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a method previously developed by Qian et al. for GII-type PEDV had a detection limit of 100 copies/reaction [26]. We successfully resolved the amplification competition of various targets by adjusting the ratio of multiplex primers and optimizing the reaction conditions, which managed to stabilize the multiplex amplification system in this study. This improvement not only increased the sensitivity of detection to the single-copy limit but also allowed the three viruses to be detected simultaneously, thereby greatly improving detection efficiency and usefulness in practice.

Compared with RT-qPCR, which depends on expensive thermal cyclers, we optimized the reaction temperature to a constant 37 °C, eliminating the need for complex thermal cycling equipment; only basic constant-temperature devices (such as water baths or portable heating modules) are required, and the reaction can even be driven by body heat in resource-limited settings. Furthermore, the workflow established in this study-from initiation of amplification to visual readout using a portable blue-light viewer-can be completed within 55 min. This extremely low dependence on equipment and the efficient “sample-in, result-out” workflow make it highly compatible with the urgent demands of point-of-care testing (POCT) in field settings [31,32]. To amplify detection signals, Xie et al. employed ROX-labeled ssDNA reporter probes, enabling direct visual interpretation through color change [33]. However, many existing studies rely on complex nanomaterial synthesis and modification, such as gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) or liposome-enhanced surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) [[34], [35], [36], [37], [38]], which not only increase experimental costs but also involve tedious material preparation steps. In contrast, this study utilized universal, low-cost FAM-BHQ1-labeled ssDNA probes. Combined with a portable blue-light observation device, this approach avoided the use of expensive materials and significantly reduced the cost per test while maintaining high sensitivity.

In recent years, CRISPR diagnostic platforms, represented by SHERLOCK and DETECTR, have significantly propelled innovations in molecular diagnostics [39,40]. The Cas12a protein used in this study cleaves ssDNA, unlike Cas13a, which mainly targets ssRNA. This feature is more suitable for on-site detection applications since DNA reagents are much more stable during storage and transportation. Nonetheless, there are some limitations associated with this approach; because Cas12a relies on the PAM sequence (TTTV) to increase the specificity of its detection, it also limits flexibility in the selection of target regions and primer design. This can be solved in future research by adding artificial PAM sequences or using engineered Cas proteins (e.g., Cas12b or PAM-less Cas variants) to increase the applicability of the assay [41].

Additionally, we understand that merging amplification and detection into a unified system can minimize aerosol contamination risks. For instance, Liu et al. combined multiplex RT-LAMP with CRISPR/Cas12a to construct a single-tube detection system, which not only achieved ultra-high sensitivity at the single-copy level but also effectively circumvented aerosol contamination risks through closed-tube operation [42]. However, constrained by the inherent “indiscriminate trans-cleavage” activity of activated Cas12a, such methods face severe signal cross-talk in a single homogeneous system, rendering them unable to distinguish specific pathogens. Consequently, this study adopted a “multiplex amplification-single tube detection” strategy. Although this protocol cannot eliminate the risk of aerosol exposure during operation, it successfully secures the capability for the precise genotyping of mixed infection samples. To address potential aerosol contamination in multiplex detection, subsequent research could consider combining different enzyme systems, such as Cas12a (targeting DNA) and Cas13a (targeting RNA), based on their distinct substrate cleavage preferences. Combined with multicolor fluorescent probes, this approach could enable multidimensional signal output within a single tube [43]; alternatively, developing spatial isolation technologies based on microfluidic chips could physically block signal cross-talk [44]. Nevertheless, when integrating novel biosensors to enhance performance, cost control must be simultaneously considered to ensure the economic feasibility of the technology for large-scale grassroots deployment.

Conclusion

A new multiplex nucleic acid detection platform, consisting of RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a, has been established in this paper to identify PEDV, PoRVA, and PSV. This technique is characterized by rapid speed, high sensitivity, and high specificity. The whole procedure from sample processing to visual detection can be accomplished in approximately 55 min, which is a major advantage for on-site testing. Moreover, a portable detection kit has been developed, and this significantly enhances the utility as well as the feasibility of this technology for point-of-care testing (POCT) in pig farms. This work provides powerful technical support for the early monitoring and control of transmission risks associated with these related viruses in swine herds and the global swine industry.
