Microbial fermentation for riboflavin biofortification in African traditional fermented foods
Authors: Giuseppe Spano, Pasquale Russo, Mariagiovanna Fragasso
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lactic Acid Bacteria (LAB) are commonly employed in the food industry as microbial starters, probiotics and for in situ biofortification of fermented foods. The use of vitamin-overproducing LAB represents a promising strategy for enhancing the nutritional value of staple home-made fermented foods. This approach could play a significant role in addressing deficiencies of B-group vitamins, especially vitamin B2, in underdeveloped regions.

B-group (or B-complex) vitamins are water-soluble compounds that play a crucial role in metabolism, particularly in the cellular processing of carbohydrates, proteins and lipids. Among them, vitamin B2, or riboflavin, is a key micronutrient essential for all animals. Vitamin B2 is an essential precursor of the coenzymes flavin mononucleotide (FMN) and flavin adenine dinucleotide, which are involved in numerous redox reactions and energy metabolism. Riboflavin contributes to the conversion of tryptophan into niacin and is required for the activation of vitamins B6 and B91. Riboflavin is naturally present in a variety of foods (e.g. milk and dairy products, cereals, meat, eggs, green leafy vegetables), although it may be partially lost during food processing or cooking. Therefore, it is sometimes added to food products and is available as a dietary supplement. Riboflavin deficiency is relatively uncommon in developed countries, where average daily intake in adults (1.4–2.2 mg/day) generally meets the EFSA dietary recommendations (1.6 mg/day) for healthy individuals2. However, cases of suboptimal riboflavin status have still been reported with a higher prevalence of deficiency in regions where diets are mainly based on rice and consumption of milk and meat is limited3. It has been documented among women and children in the Gambia, children in Côte d’Ivoire, elderly people in Guatemala and refugee populations in Nepal, where it is associated with impaired energy metabolism, iron homoeostasis, anaemia and characteristic mucocutaneous lesions4. A sustainable approach to counteract riboflavin deficiency is represented by food-grade LAB able to produce vitamin B2 during traditional food fermentations5,6.

Traditional African foods are promising candidates as ideal targets for LAB-based biofortification, considering that cereals and dairy-based fermentation products are widely used and often rely on indigenous microbial consortia7. Therefore, fermentation with selected indigenous LAB provides a complementary, food-based route to enrich diets with riboflavin8 even though that autochthonous LAB are increasingly being replaced by imported and industrially relevant commercial strains. This aspect may decrease microbial biodiversity, also affecting vitamin B2-producing strains and resulting in the loss of species introduced with the diet.

The ability to synthesise riboflavin is species- and strain-dependent among LAB and relies on the presence of a functional rib operon, whose expression is regulated by an FMN riboswitch. In a typical selection workflow, prototrophic isolates are first identified by growth on a riboflavin-free medium and then exposed to roseoflavin, a riboflavin analogue that selects spontaneous mutants carrying mutations in the FMN riboswitch aptamer, leading to riboflavin overproduction9,10. This food-grade strategy overcomes the regulatory and consumer acceptance challenges associated with genetically modified microorganisms. Using this approach, naturally riboflavin-overproducing strains belonging to technologically relevant species, including Lactiplantibacillus plantarum, Limosilactobacillus fermentum and Limosilactobacillus reuteri, have been successfully exploited for in situ vitamin B₂ biofortification of cereal- and bakery-based products, as well as plant-based and dairy beverages11,12. The same workflow could be applied to indigenous LAB isolated from African fermented foods and beverages (AFFBs) to identify candidate starter cultures for natural vitamin B₂ biofortification, integrating nutritional enrichment directly into customary preparation practices while preserving the sensory properties of traditional foods 12–14 (Fig. 1). Microbial riboflavin biofortification aligns with sustainable food systems. Since fermentation is still predominantly carried out at household and small-scale commercial levels15, the application of naturally vitamin-producing LAB might reduce dependence on industrial fortification, empowering local producers. However, the nutritional impact should be confirmed under real household fermentation conditions, as recently emphasised for folate (vitamin B9) and cobalamin (vitamin B12) biofortification in Ethiopian injera16. Adapting this strategy to local contexts requires the selection and validation of autochthonous LAB strains suitable under native fermentation conditions and compatible with indigenous microbiota. Depending on the production system, their implementation may rely on starter cultures or low-cost approaches such as back-slopping, which have already shown potential to improve the vitamin content of African fermented foods while requiring only minimal modifications to traditional processing practices17,18. Therefore, employing indigenous LAB as natural riboflavin bio-factories in widely consumed African fermented foods represents a promising eco- friendly approach to reduce riboflavin deficiency in underdeveloped countries. Nonetheless, a major challenge for the successful implementation of this strategy will be the development of standardised starter cultures and scalable fermentation processes adapted to local production systems, ensuring both product quality and safety while facilitating wider adoption.
