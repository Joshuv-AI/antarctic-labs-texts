On the supremum and its location of the standardized uniform empirical process
Dietmar Ferger

Abstract
We show that the maximizing point and the supremum of the standardized uniform empirical process converge in distribution. Here, the limit variable ( Z , Y ) (Z,Y) has independent components. Moreover, Z Z attains the values zero and one with equal probability one half and Y Y follows the Gumbel-distribution.



keywords
weighted uniform empirical process, maximizer, maximal inequality

1
Introduction and Main Result

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be
n\in\mathbb{N}
independent real random variables uniformly distributed on the interval
(0,1)
.
If
F_{n}
denotes the corresponding empirical distribution function, then

V_{n}:=\sqrt{n}\sup_{0<t<1}\frac{|F_{n}(t)-t|}{\sqrt{t(1-t)}}

is the supremum of the standardized uniform empirical process

Q_{n}(t)=\frac{|\sqrt{n}\{F_{n}(t)-t\}|}{\sqrt{t(1-t)}},\;0<t<1.

Chibisov
Chibisov
proves that
V_{n}
converges to infinity in probability.
Whereas Jaeschke
Jaeschke
shows that a certain affine-linear transformation of
V_{n}
converges in distribution. More precisely,
let

a_{n}=\sqrt{2\log\log n}\quad\text{ and }\quad b_{n}=2\log\log n+\frac{1}{2}\log\log\log n-\frac{1}{2}\log\pi.

(1)

Then

a_{n}V_{n}-b_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\rightarrow}}Y,

(2)

where
Y
follows the Gumbel-distribution, i.e.
\mathbb{P}(Y\leq x)=\exp\{-2e^{-x}\},x\in\mathbb{R}.

Elementary arguments show that

V_{n}=\sqrt{n}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\max\big\{\frac{\frac{i}{n}-X_{i:n}}{\sqrt{X_{i:n}(1-X_{i:n})}},\frac{X_{i:n}-\frac{i-1}{n}}{\sqrt{X_{i:n}(1-X_{i:n})}}\big\},

(3)

where
X_{i:n}
denotes the
i
-th order-statistic,
1\leq i\leq n
.

In this paper we also consider the random variable
\tau_{n}\in(0,1)
, which maximizes
Q_{n}
in the following sense:

\tau_{n}=\min\{t\in(0,1):\max\{Q_{n}(t),Q_{n}(t-)\}=V_{n}\}.

(4)

The definition takes into account that
Q_{n}
is right-continuous with left limits on its domain
(0,1)
. It turns out that the location of the supremum is given by

\tau_{n}=X_{r:n},

where

r=\raisebox{-5.12149pt}{${{\displaystyle\rm arg\,\!max}\atop{\scriptstyle 1\leq i\leq n}}$}\max\big\{\frac{\frac{i}{n}-X_{i:n}}{\sqrt{X_{i:n}(1-X_{i:n})}},\frac{X_{i:n}-\frac{i-1}{n}}{\sqrt{X_{i:n}(1-X_{i:n})}}\big\}.

(5)

Moreover,
\tau_{n}
is almost surely (a.s.) unique. Let
X_{i}^{\prime}:=1-X_{i},1\leq i\leq n.
Then
(X_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{n}^{\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{=}}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
and
X_{i:n}^{\prime}=1-X_{n-i+1:n},1\leq i\leq n.
Thus it follows from (
3
) and (
5
) that

(\tau_{n},V_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{=}}(1-\tau_{n},V_{n}).

(6)

Our main result says that as
n
tends to infinity the whole probability mass of
\tau_{n}
is shifted equally into the boundary points zero and one of the
open unit interval. Moreover,
\tau_{n}
and
V_{n}
are asymptotically independent.

Theorem 1
.

For all
x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}
the following limit-theorem holds:

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{n}\leq x,a_{n}V_{n}-b_{n}\leq y)=\mathbb{P}(Z\leq x)\exp\{-2e^{-y}\}=\mathbb{P}(Z\leq x)\mathbb{P}(Y\leq y),

(7)

where
\mathbb{P}(Z=1)=\mathbb{P}(Z=0)=\frac{1}{2}.

In particularly,

(\tau_{n},a_{n}V_{n}-b_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\rightarrow}}(Z,Y)

with
Z
and
Y
independent.

Observe that there is a strong relationship between
\tau_{n}
and
V_{n}
, which according to (
4
) is given by

\max\{Q_{n}(\tau_{n}),Q_{n}(\tau_{n}-)\}=V_{n}.

Considering this fact, the asymptotic independence of
\tau_{n}
and
V_{n}
is quite surprising.

Indeed, this independence does not apply in the non-weighted case. More precisely consider

W_{n}:=\sqrt{n}\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}|F_{n}(t)-t|=\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}|u_{n}(t)|,

where
u_{n}(t)=\sqrt{n}(F_{n}(t)-t)
denotes the uniform empirical process. Let
\sigma_{n}
be the corresponding maximizing point, i.e.

\sigma_{n}:=\min\{t\in[0,1]:\max\{|u_{n}(t)|,|u_{n}(t-)|\}=W_{n}\}.

Let
M
be the sup-functional and
a
the argmax-functional as defined in Lemma A.2 in Ferger
Ferger1
. Then
(\sigma_{n},W_{n})=L(u_{n})
, where
L:=(a,M)
is a functional on the Skorokhod-space
D[0,1]
with values in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, which by Lemmas A.3 and A.4 in Ferger
Ferger1
is measurable and continuous on
C[0,1]
. By Donsker’s Theorem
u_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\rightarrow}}B
in
D[0,1]
, where
B
is a Brownian bridge. Therefore, the Continuous Mapping Theorem (CMT) yields that

(\sigma_{n},W_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\rightarrow}}(T,W),

where
T=a(|B|)
is the maximizing point of
|B|
, which can be shown to be a.s. unique. Moreover,
W=M(|B|)=\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}|B(t)|
is the infinity-norm of the Brownian bridge.
According to Theorem 2.6 in Ferger
Ferger0
the limit variable
(T,W)
has density

f_{(T,W)}(x,y)=\sqrt{\frac{8}{\pi}}\psi(x,y)\psi(1-x,y),\quad x\in(0,1),y\geq 0,

(8)

where

\psi(x,y)=yx^{-\frac{3}{2}}\sum_{j\geq 0}(-1)^{j}(2j+1)e^{-\frac{1}{2}(2j+1)^{2}x^{-1}y^{2}}

In view of (
8
) the components
T
and
W
are far away from being independent.

Remark 1
.

Notice that the random index
r
of the maximizing order-statistic
X_{r:n}
depends on
n
. Since
\frac{r}{n}=F_{n}^{-1}(X_{r:n})
and the uniform empirical quantile function
F_{n}^{-1}
converges uniformly on
(0,1)
to the identity with probability one, we obtain from Theorem
1
and Slutsky’s theorem that

\frac{r}{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\rightarrow}}Z.

2
The proof

Our proof of Theorem
1
relies on a maximal-inequality for the process
Q_{n}
.

Lemma 1
.

For every
a\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
and each
\lambda>0
it follows that

\mathbb{P}\big(\sup_{a\leq t\leq 1-a}\frac{|F_{n}(t)-t|}{\sqrt{t(1-t)}}>\lambda\big)\leq 2\;\lambda^{-2}\;n^{-1}\big(\log(\frac{1-a}{a})+1\big).

(9)

Proof.

It is well-known that the process
\frac{F_{n}(t)-t}{1-t},t\in[0,1),
is a centered martingale with respect to the natural filtration, confer Gaenssler and Stute
Gaenssler
, p.4. Therefore,
S(t):=(\frac{F_{n}(t)-t}{1-t})^{2},t\in[0,1),
is a non-negative sub-martingale.
From Lemma 3.3 in Ferger
Ferger1
we can infer with
w(t)=\frac{1-t}{t}
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\sup_{a\leq t\leq 1/2}\frac{|F_{n}(t)-t|}{\sqrt{t(1-t)}}>\lambda\big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\sup_{a\leq t\leq 1/2}w(t)S(t)>\lambda^{2}\big)

(10)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lambda^{-2}\{\int_{a}^{\frac{1}{2}}H(t)(-w)(dt)+w(\frac{1}{2})H(\frac{1}{2})\}.

Here,
H(t)=\mathbb{E}[S(t)]=(1-t)^{-2}\text{Var}[F_{n}(t)]=n^{-1}\frac{t}{1-t}.
Since
-w^{\prime}(t)=t^{-2}
, the integral in (
10
) simplifies to

n^{-1}\log(\frac{1-a}{a})
and
w(\frac{1}{2})H(\frac{1}{2})=n^{-1}
. Thus we obtain from (
10
) that

\mathbb{P}\big(\sup_{a\leq t\leq 1/2}\frac{|F_{n}(t)-t|}{\sqrt{t(1-t)}}>\lambda\big)\leq\lambda^{-2}\;n^{-1}(\log(\frac{1-a}{a})+1).

Now, the assertion follows, because

\sup_{a\leq t\leq 1/2}\frac{|F_{n}(t)-t|}{\sqrt{t(1-t)}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{=}}\sup_{1/2\leq t\leq 1-a}\frac{|F_{n}(t)-t|}{\sqrt{t(1-t)}}.

∎

For the proof of Theorem
1
let
(\alpha_{n})
be a positive sequence converging to zero. Put
I:=(0,1)
and
I_{n}:=[\alpha_{n},1-\alpha_{n}]
. By assumption there exists an integer
n_{0}
such that
\emptyset\neq I_{n}\subseteq I
for all
n\geq n_{0}
. If

Y_{n}:=\sup_{t\in I_{n}}Q_{n}(t)\quad\text{ and }\quad Z_{n}:=\sup_{t\in I\setminus I_{n}}Q_{n}(t),

then
V_{n}=\sup_{t\in I}Q_{n}(t)=\max\{Y_{n},Z_{n}\}.

With the above maximal-inequality it follows that

\mathbb{P}(a_{n}^{-1}|Y_{n}|>\epsilon)\leq\epsilon^{-2}\frac{\log(\alpha_{n}^{-1})+1}{\log\log n}\quad\text{for all }\epsilon>0.

Consequently, if for example
\alpha_{n}=(\log\log n)^{-1}
, then

a_{n}^{-1}Y_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\rightarrow}}0.

(11)

Put
\tilde{Y}_{n}:=a_{n}V_{n}-b_{n}
. Then
a_{n}^{-1}V_{n}=a_{n}^{-2}\tilde{Y}_{n}+a_{n}^{-2}b_{n}
, and thus by (
1
) and (
2
)

a_{n}^{-1}V_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\rightarrow}}1.

(12)

Next, notice that

\{\tau_{n}\in(\alpha_{n},1-\alpha_{n}]\}\subseteq\{Z_{n}\leq Y_{n}\}.

(13)

To see this assume that
\tau_{n}\in(\alpha_{n},1-\alpha_{n}]
, but
Z_{n}>Y_{n}
. Recall that by definition (
4
) the maximizer satisfies
\max\{Q_{n}(\tau_{n}),Q_{n}(\tau_{n}-)\}=V_{n}.
We distinguish two cases. If
Q(\tau_{n})=V_{n}
, then
V_{n}\leq\sup_{\alpha_{n}<t\leq 1-\alpha_{n}}Q_{n}(t)\leq Y_{n}<Z_{n}\leq V_{n}
, a contradiction. If
Q_{n}(\tau_{n}-)=V_{n}
, then
V_{n}=\lim_{t\uparrow\tau_{n},t\in(\alpha_{n},1-\alpha_{n}]}Q_{n}(t)\leq\sup_{\alpha_{n}<t\leq 1-\alpha_{n}}Q_{n}(t)\leq Y_{n}<Z_{n}\leq V_{n}
, once again a contradiction. This shows (
13
) and therefore

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tau_{n}\in(\alpha_{n},1-\alpha_{n}])\leq\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}\leq Y_{n})\leq\mathbb{P}(V_{n}=Y_{n})

(14)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(V_{n}=Y_{n},a_{n}^{-1}Y_{n}\leq\epsilon)+\mathbb{P}(a_{n}^{-1}Y_{n}>\epsilon)\quad\forall\;\epsilon>0.

The second probability in (
14
) converges to zero for all positive
\epsilon
by (
11
). The first probability in (
14
)
is less than or equal to

\mathbb{P}(V_{n}\leq a_{n}\epsilon)\leq\mathbb{P}(V_{n}\leq a_{n}\epsilon,|a_{n}^{-1}V_{n}-1|\leq\epsilon)+\mathbb{P}(|a_{n}^{-1}V_{n}-1|>\epsilon).

Here, the second probability converges to zero by (
12
) and the first probability actually vanishes whenever
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
, because in this case the inequalities
V_{n}\leq a_{n}\epsilon
and
V_{n}\geq(1-\epsilon)a_{n}
are mutually exclusive.
In summary, we have shown that

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{n}\in(\alpha_{n},1-\alpha_{n}])\rightarrow 0,\;n\rightarrow\infty.

(15)

Next, observe that

\mathbb{P}(\tilde{Y}_{n}\leq y)=\mathbb{P}(\tau_{n}\leq\alpha_{n},\tilde{Y}_{n}\leq y)+\mathbb{P}(\tau_{n}\in(\alpha_{n},1-\alpha_{n}),\tilde{Y}_{n}\leq y)+\mathbb{P}(\tau_{n}\geq 1-\alpha_{n},\tilde{Y}_{n}\leq y).

Here, the second probability on the right side converges to zero as a consequence of (
15
). Moreover, the third probability is
equal to the first probability by symmetry (
6
). Thus

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{n}\leq\alpha_{n},\tilde{Y}_{n}\leq y)=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}(\tilde{Y}_{n}\leq y)+\beta_{n},

(16)

where
\beta_{n}\rightarrow 0
. Finally, let
x\in(0,1)
. Then there exists some integer
n_{1}=n_{1}(x)
such that
\alpha_{n}<x\leq 1-\alpha_{n}
for all
n\geq n_{1}
.
It follows that

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{n}\leq x,\tilde{Y}_{n}\leq y)=\mathbb{P}(\tau_{n}\leq\alpha_{n},\tilde{Y}_{n}\leq y)+\mathbb{P}(\tau_{n}\in(\alpha_{n},x],\tilde{Y}_{n}\leq y).

(17)

Since the second probability on the right side in (
17
) is less that or equal to
\mathbb{P}(\tau_{n}\in(\alpha_{n},1-\alpha_{n}])
for all
n\geq n_{1}
, it converges to zero by (
15
). Therefore, it follows from (
17
), (
16
) and (
2
) that

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{n}\leq x,\tilde{Y}_{n}\leq y)\rightarrow\frac{1}{2}\exp\{-2e^{-y}\}\quad\forall\;x\in(0,1)\;\forall\;y\in\mathbb{R}.

For
x<0
or
x\geq 1
the validity of (
7
) is trivial, because
\tau_{n}\in(0,1)
a.s. and (
2
) holds. As to the second part of Theorem
1
notice that in particularly we have
convergence at every continuity point of
Z
so that we can apply a well-known characterisation for distributional convergence of random variables in the euclidian space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, confer for instance Proposition 5.58 in Witting and Müller-Funk
Witting
.
