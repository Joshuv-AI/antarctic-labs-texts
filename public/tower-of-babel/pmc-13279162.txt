PAF1c depletion confers chemoresistance to topoisomerase inhibitors
Authors: Dingwei Li, Chunbo Zhu, Tailun Wang, Qihui Chen, Qiang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The human RNA polymerase II-associated factor 1 complex (PAF1c) functions in transcriptional elongation and mRNA maturation. PAF1c is composed of several subunits: PAF1, CDC73, LEO1, CTR9, RTF1, and SKIC8. Besides its role in transcription, PAF1c subunits have been reported to be associated with tumorigenesis through maintaining cancer genome stability. In this study, we show that depletion of PAF1c leads to a pronounced accumulation of R-loops, which in turn results in an increase in DNA damage. Moreover, we confirmed that PAF1c deficiency increases the cytotoxicity of several DNA-damaging agents, including hydroxyurea (HU), cisplatin (CDDP), methyl methanesulfonate (MMS), and bleomycin (BLM). Unexpectedly, PAF1c depletion confers tolerance specifically to topoisomerase inhibitors, such as camptothecin (CPT), etoposide (ETOP), and doxorubicin (DOX). Further investigation revealed that the resistance to topoisomerase inhibitors induced by PAF1c depletion occurred specifically in G1-but not S-phase cells. Mechanistically, PAF1c depletion paradoxically decreases CPT-induced accumulation of R-loops by impeding mRNA elongation in G1-phase cells, thereby reducing CPT-induced cell death. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that loss of PAF1c subunits not only promotes genomic instability through R-loop accumulation but also alters cellular responses to DNA-damaging agents, conferring resistance particularly to topoisomerase inhibitors. This study underscores the critical role of PAF1c in maintaining genome stability and provides a rationale for developing new therapeutic strategies in cancer treatment.

Introduction

In metazoans, transcription by RNA polymerase II (Pol II) is a highly orchestrated process comprising initiation, promoter-proximal pausing, pause release, elongation, and termination, with dynamic transitions between these stages (Bunch et al., 2014; Chen et al., 2015; Fant et al., 2020; Jonkers and Lis, 2015; Naganuma et al., 2025; Sainsbury et al., 2015; Su and Vos, 2024; Wang et al., 2023). After initiation, RNA Pol II does not continuously progress along the DNA template but instead pauses approximately 20–60 bp downstream of the transcription start site (TSS). This phenomenon, termed promoter-proximal Pol II pausing (PPP), represents a regulatory checkpoint to fine-tune the transcriptional machinery and ensure accurate progression of subsequent transcriptional events (Gilmour, 2009; Naganuma et al., 2025). Following this transient pause, RNA Pol II faces two alternative fates: resuming productive elongation or undergoing promoter-proximal premature termination (Adelman and Lis, 2012). The decision is largely governed by a balance between the pausing factors DSIF and negative elongation factor (NELF) and the positive transcription elongation factor b (P-TEFb), thereby shaping the ultimate outcome of gene expression (Chen et al., 2018). Notably, the polymerase II-associated factor 1 complex (PAF1c) plays vital roles in the regulation of this process.

PAF1c is a highly conserved multiprotein complex composed of the subunits PAF1, CDC73, LEO1, CTR9, RTF1, and SKIC8 (Barman et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2021, 2022; Xie et al., 2018). As a critical transcription-associated factor, PAF1c orchestrates diverse biological processes, including communication with transcriptional activators (Wier et al., 2013); recruitment and activation of histone-modifying enzymes (Fetian et al., 2023; Warner et al., 2007); promotion of elongation on chromatin templates (Francette and Arndt, 2024); and recruitment of 3′ end processing factors required for accurate transcription termination (Liu et al., 2022). During transcription initiation, PAF1c interacts with the C-terminal domain (CTD) of RNA Pol II, thereby facilitating pre-initiation complex (PIC) assembly and establishing the foundation for productive transcription (Francette et al., 2021). As a transcription elongation factor, PAF1c is broadly involved in promoter-proximal Pol II pausing, which facilitates the release of RNA Pol II into the gene body region (Wang et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2015). Beyond its role in transcription, PAF1c also participates in multiple DNA repair pathways, including nucleotide excision repair, mismatch repair, and transcription-coupled repair (Tatum et al., 2011; Kokic et al., 2021; Van Den Heuvel et al., 2021). PAF1 enhances DNA damage repair following chemotherapy by promoting RAD52 activity; consequently, silencing PAF1 sensitizes tumor cells to apoptosis induced by cisplatin (CDDP) or gemcitabine (Rauth et al., 2023). In addition, PAF1c can be recruited by the SMC5/6 complex to DNA double-strand break (DSB) sites, where it mediates histone H2B monoubiquitination, thereby facilitating homologous recombination (HR) repair (Li et al., 2023). PAF1c has also been implicated in tumor progression. PAF1 is upregulated in pancreatic and ovarian cancer stem cells, and its ectopic expression in NIH3T3 cells promotes cell proliferation and tumorigenicity (Karmakar et al., 2020; Moniaux et al., 2006). Mutations in CDC73 are strongly associated with parathyroid carcinoma (Li et al., 2020). CTR9 functions as a scaffold protein within PAF1c and promotes hepatocellular carcinoma progression and metastasis by enhancing PEG10 transcription (Zhang et al., 2022).

During transcription, nascent RNA can transiently hybridize with the DNA template at the active site of RNA Pol II, forming a short RNA–DNA hybrid structure known as an R-loop (Wang et al., 2021). R-loop formation is a natural event in the nucleus and plays important roles in regulating gene expression and shaping higher-order chromatin architecture (Niehrs and Luke, 2020). However, aberrant accumulation of R-loops can be deleterious. When encountered by RNA Pol II during transcription, R-loops may block or slow down elongation (Rinaldi et al., 2020). During DNA replication, unresolved R-loops can interfere with the progression of replication forks, leading to fork instability or collapse and ultimately resulting in DSBs, thereby compromising genome stability (Hamperl et al., 2017).

Given the dual role of PAF1c in transcriptional regulation and DNA damage repair, we sought to investigate how PAF1c modulates genome stability in response to different DNA-damaging agents. To this end, we treated PAF1c-depleted cell lines with a panel of agents, including hydroxyurea (HU), CDDP, methyl methanesulfonate (MMS), bleomycin (BLM), camptothecin (CPT), etoposide (ETOP), and doxorubicin (DOX). Strikingly, depletion of PAF1c subunits conferred resistance specifically to topoisomerase inhibitors (CPT, ETOP, and DOX). We discovered that this selective resistance is linked to the role of PAF1c in fine-tuning transcription, which may prevent topoisomerase inhibitor-induced R-loop accumulation.

Results

PAF1c depletion induces genome instability

Recent studies have reported that PAF1 depletion induces R-loop accumulation and DNA damage in mammalian cells (Liu et al., 2023; Shivji et al., 2018). LEO1 and CDC73 have also been reported to maintain genome stability through other pathways, such as transcription-coupled nucleotide excision repair (TC-NER) (Melnick et al., 2023; Tiwari et al., 2021). Here, we explored whether disruption of PAF1c subunits generally induces R-loop accumulation, thereby causing DNA damage. We first depleted PAF1 using three independent shRNAs against PAF1 (Fig. 1(A) and (B)). Western blot analysis revealed a marked increase in the DNA damage marker γH2AX in PAF1-deficient cells compared with control cells (Fig. 1(A)). We further quantified DNA damage by counting γH2AX foci per cell; cells with more than 20 nuclear γH2AX foci were defined as having significant DNA damage. The proportion of cells with more than 20 γH2AX foci increased by approximately two-fold in PAF1-depleted lines (Fig. 1(C) and (D)). The PAF1c complex is composed of six subunits: PAF1, CDC73, LEO1, CTR9, RTF1, and SKIC8 (Fig. 1(E)). To examine whether depletion of other PAF1c subunits induces DNA damage similar to PAF1 depletion, we established shRNA-mediated knockdown of CDC73, LEO1, CTR9, RTF1, and SKIC8 (Fig. 1(F)). In cells depleted of these PAF1c subunits, γH2AX foci increased markedly, and the proportion of γH2AX-positive cells roughly doubled compared with controls (Fig. 1(G) and (H)), resembling the phenotype of PAF1-depleted cells (Fig. 1(C) and (D)). Consistent with previous reports, co-immunofluorescence staining for R-loops (S9.6) and γH2AX revealed a significant increase in R-loops in PAF1-depleted cells (Fig. 2(A) and (B)). 5,6-Dichloro-1-β-D-ribofuranosylbenzimidazole (DRB) inhibits Pol II transcriptional elongation by blocking CDK9/P-TEFb, thereby preventing C-terminal domain phosphorylation of Pol II (Wada et al., 1998). DRB-mediated transcriptional inhibition alleviates R-loop accumulation and DNA damage arising from intrinsic cellular defects, such as loss of essential proteins (Pérez Di Giorgio et al., 2019). To determine whether DNA damage associated with R-loop accumulation upon PAF1c depletion is linked to Pol II transcriptional arrest, we used DRB as a positive control and assessed its effect on R-loop–associated DNA damage in PAF1c-depleted cells. PAF1- or CDC73-depleted cells were treated with DRB. Western blotting revealed a strong increase in γH2AX in PAF1- and CDC73-depleted cells relative to controls, which was largely rescued by DRB treatment (Fig. S1A). Consistently, DRB markedly reduced the proportion of γH2AX-positive cells in PAF1- and CDC73-depleted cells (Fig. S1B and S1C). Moreover, R-loop levels were significantly elevated in PAF1- and CDC73-depleted HeLa cells and in DRB-treated HeLa cells; DRB treatment partially suppressed the R-loop accumulation in PAF1- and CDC73-deficient cells (Fig. S1B and S1D). These results indicate that transcriptional inhibition by DRB suppresses DNA damage and R-loop accumulation caused by PAF1c depletion, consistent with a transcription-dependent mechanism for PAF1c depletion–induced DNA damage.

Finally, to directly test whether R-loop accumulation underlies PAF1c disruption-induced DNA damage, we ectopically expressed RNase H1, which specifically resolves R-loops, or a catalytic mutant RNase H1-D210N, which binds but cannot degrade R-loops (Lockhart et al., 2019; Nguyen et al., 2017) (Fig. S1E). Overexpression of RNase H1 significantly reduced the proportion of γH2AX-positive cells in PAF1-depleted lines, whereas RNase H1-D210N had no effect (Fig. 2(E) and (F)). Consistently, RNase H1 overexpression decreased R-loop levels in PAF1-depleted cells, while RNase H1-D210N did not (Fig. 2(G) and (H)). To further confirm this result, we overexpressed RNase H1-WT and the catalytic mutant RNase H1-D210N in cells with depletion of other PAF1c subunits. RNase H1-WT reduced both the proportion of γH2AX-positive cells and R-loop accumulation, whereas RNase H1-D210N had no such effect (Figs. S1F and 2(I); Figs. S1G and 2(J)). These results indicate that depletion of other PAF1c subunits (CDC73, LEO1, CTR9, RTF1, SKIC8) leads to aberrant R-loop accumulation and DNA damage. Consistently, Shivji et al. reported that cancer-associated BRCA2 mutations reduce PAF1 recruitment to Pol II, leading to irregular R-loop accumulation at promoter-proximal pausing sites and DNA breaks (Shivji et al., 2018). Therefore, we propose that depletion of PAF1c subunits impairs the overall function of the complex, disrupts the transition from promoter-proximal pausing to productive elongation, and results in the accumulation of R-loop–associated DNA damage.

PAF1c-depleted cells exhibit resistance to topoisomerase inhibitors

During transcription, PAF1c promotes RNA Pol II elongation and facilitates the recruitment and function of DNA repair proteins, thereby maintaining genome integrity when gene transcription encounters DNA damage (Kokic et al., 2021). Defects in the genes encoding the PAF1c subunits can cause increased genome instability after treatment with DNA-damaging agents (Nene et al., 2018). To investigate how PAF1c depletion affects cellular responses to DNA-damaging agents, we treated PAF1-depleted cells with HU, CDDP, MMS, BLM, CPT, ETOP, and DOX. Among them, HU inhibits ribonucleotide reductase (RNR), blocking the conversion of ribonucleotides (NTPs) to deoxyribonucleotides (dNTPs) and thereby interfering with DNA synthesis (Wozniak and Simmons, 2021). CDDP forms intra- and interstrand crosslinks in DNA, which obstruct replication and transcription (Yimit et al., 2019). MMS methylates DNA bases (especially adenine and guanine), causing alkylation damage (Mustafa et al., 2024). BLM induces single- and double-strand breaks, partly through generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Duecker et al., 2018). CPT, a topoisomerase I inhibitor, stabilizes topoisomerase I–DNA cleavage complexes and induces single-strand breaks (Das et al., 2016). ETOP and DOX inhibit topoisomerase II activity, disrupting DNA unwinding and re-ligation, and consequently cause DNA damage and cell death (Van Ravenstein et al., 2022). The mechanisms and types of DNA damage induced by these DNA-damaging agents are shown in Fig. S2A.

To examine the effect of PAF1c depletion, we performed Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assays to evaluate cell viability following drug treatment. PAF1 depletion increased sensitivity to HU, CDDP, MMS, and BLM, as shown with three independent shRNAs (Fig. 3(A)). Strikingly, PAF1-depleted cells were resistant to treatment with CPT, ETOP, or DOX (Fig. 3(B)). We selected CPT and ETOP as representatives and tested both low-dose, long-term and high-dose, short-term treatment regimens. In both cases, PAF1-depleted cells showed improved survival compared with controls (Fig. 3(C)). Similar results were observed in PAF1-depleted HCT116 and A549 cell lines (Fig. S2B). In both cell lines, PAF1 depletion increased sensitivity to HU and CDDP, but conferred resistance to CPT and ETOP (Fig. 3(D) and (E)). We further found that the same effect could be observed upon depletion of other PAF1c subunits (Fig. 3(F)). Resistance of PAF1c-depleted cells to topoisomerase inhibitors primarily occurs during transcription

Previous studies have shown that topoisomerase inhibitors such as CPT, ETOP, and DOX induce DNA damage throughout the entire cell cycle (Das et al., 2016; Kciuk et al., 2023; Soliman et al., 2023; Van Ravenstein et al., 2022). During G1 phase, topoisomerase inhibitors mainly induce R-loop formation by affecting RNA Pol II elongation (Racko et al., 2018). During S phase, topoisomerase inhibitors can delay DNA replication fork progression which induces DNA replication stress (Amoiridis et al., 2024; Sarni et al., 2022). Fig. S2C shows that topoisomerase inhibitors suppress transcription in G1 phase and DNA replication in S phase. As a transcription elongation factor, PAF1c regulates promoter-proximal pausing (PPP) and controls release of RNA Pol II into productive elongation; PAF1c deficiency therefore reduces transcriptional elongation (Francette and Arndt, 2024). We therefore hypothesized that the resistance of PAF1c-depleted cells to topoisomerase inhibitors may occur mainly during transcription, particularly in the G1 phase.

To determine how PAF1c depletion affects sensitivity to topoisomerase inhibitors, we synchronized PAF1- and CDC73-depleted cells in either G1 or S phase and then exposed them to CPT or ETOP to induce DNA damage (Fig. 4(A)). PAF1- and CDC73-depleted cells displayed increased resistance to CPT or ETOP in G1 but not in S phase (Fig. 4(B) and Fig. S3A). Consistently, γH2AX signals were slightly decreased in PAF1- and CDC73-depleted G1-phase cells after CPT or ETOP treatment (Fig. 4(C) and Fig. S3B). By contrast, in S phase, CPT or ETOP treatment further increased γH2AX levels in PAF1- and CDC73-depleted cells (Fig. 4(C) and Fig. S3B). Immunofluorescence staining further supported these observations. In both G1-and S-phase cells, CPT or ETOP treatment led to significantly increased nuclear γH2AX foci and the proportions of γH2AX-positive cells compared with controls. However, PAF1 depletion partially reduced CPT- or ETOP-induced γH2AX foci in G1-phase cells (Fig. 4(D) and (E)). In contrast, in S-phase cells, γH2AX signals induced by CPT or ETOP treatment were enhanced by PAF1 depletion (Fig. 4(D) and (E)). We further examined the distribution of γH2AX signals. In G1 phase, γH2AX staining in PAF1- and CDC73-depleted cells remained punctate following CPT or ETOP treatment, whereas in S phase, the staining pattern became diffuse across the nucleus (Fig. 4(D) and Fig. S3C). Quantification of the number of γH2AX foci in each cell revealed a significant increase in PAF1- and CDC73-depleted cells compared with controls, whereas CPT or ETOP treatment reduced foci numbers in depleted cells relative to controls (Fig. 4(F) and Fig. S3E). Together, these findings indicate that depletion of PAF1c suppresses topoisomerase inhibitor–induced DNA damage primarily in G1-phase cells.

PAF1c depletion antagonizes topoisomerase inhibitor–induced global R-loop accumulation by suppressing transcription elongation

Topoisomerases (TOP1, TOP2 and TOP3B) can remove DNA supercoiling to prevent R-loop formation in G1-phase cells (Das et al., 2016; Pommier et al., 2022; Saha et al., 2020; Tumini et al., 2025). The above data demonstrated that PAF1c depletion confers resistance to topoisomerase inhibitor–induced stress in G1-phase cells. We speculated that PAF1c deficiency may confer resistance to topoisomerase inhibitors by reducing R-loop formation. We examined the accumulation of R-loops in PAF1- and CDC73-depleted G1-phase HeLa cells after CPT or ETOP treatment. CPT or ETOP treatment induced significant accumulation of R-loops in G1-phase cells, which was markedly decreased after PAF1 or CDC73 depletion (Fig. 5(A) and (B)). This result indicated that PAF1 or CDC73 depletion can decrease CPT- or ETOP-induced R-loop accumulation. Next, we compared the effect of PAF1 depletion with DRB-induced transcription inhibition on CPT-induced R-loops. In G1-phase cells, DRB treatment markedly reduced S9.6 signal intensity in control HeLa cells, indicating that transcription inhibition could prevent CPT-induced R-loop accumulation. No significant differences in S9.6 signal intensity were observed between DRB-treated and PAF1- or CDC73-depleted G1-phase cells (Fig. 5(C) and (D)). To further validate this conclusion, we selected genes from published datasets that showed overlapping genes (log2(FoldChange) ≤ −1) after PAF1 depletion or CPT treatment (Carson et al., 2004; Kim et al., 2013) (Fig. S4A). Thirteen overlapping genes with significant downregulation were examined. RT–qPCR analysis revealed that seven of these genes (RPS6KA2, RASA1, STAG1, RBMS1, COBL, ZCCHC14, and RNF13) were downregulated by both PAF1 depletion and CPT treatment in G1-phase cells (Fig. S4B), indicating that PAF1 depletion may affect CPT-induced global R-loop formation by suppressing transcription elongation. Furthermore, total transcriptional elongation in G1-phase HeLa cells — under PAF1 depletion or DRB treatment, with or without CPT — was assessed by immunoblotting for RNAPII-Ser2P and RNAPII-Ser5P. PAF1 depletion or DRB treatment reduced elongation-associated RNAPII-Ser2P levels and concomitantly increased promoter-proximal pausing-associated RNAPII-Ser5P levels; these effects were also observed in the presence of CPT, with an additive increase in RNAPII-Ser5P (Fig. 5(F)). To determine whether PAF1 depletion suppresses transcriptional elongation at the gene level and thereby antagonizes CPT effects, five overlapping genes (for which PAF1 depletion significantly attenuated CPT-induced transcriptional changes) were analyzed by ChIP-based traveling ratio analysis. Both PAF1 depletion and DRB treatment caused RNA Pol II accumulation at promoters in G1-phase HeLa cells, whereas CPT treatment promoted RNA Pol II progression into gene bodies (Fig. 5(G)). Notably, CPT treatment of PAF1-depleted or DRB-treated cells resulted in persistent promoter-proximal RNA Pol II accumulation (Fig. 5(G)). Thus, similar to DRB, PAF1 depletion induces promoter-proximal RNA Pol II pausing and prevents CPT-driven elongation into gene bodies. Collectively, these data indicate that PAF1c depletion antagonizes topoisomerase inhibitor–induced global R-loop accumulation by suppressing transcriptional elongation.

Discussion

PAF1c is a multifunctional protein complex that in vertebrates consists of six subunits: PAF1, CDC73, LEO1, CTR9, RTF1, and SKIC8. Previous studies have revealed interdependencies among PAF1c subunits. For example, deletion of yeast CTR9 reduces the abundance of all other subunits (Mueller et al., 2004). CTR9, CDC73, and RTF1, but not LEO1, require normal levels of PAF1 for stability, and loss of CDC73 reduces RTF1 abundance (Mueller et al., 2004; Squazzo et al., 2002). In human cells, depletion of CTR9 or SKIC8 decreases PAF1 and LEO1 (Zhu et al., 2005), and CDC73 is required to maintain the expression level of PAF1 and LEO1 (Lin et al., 2008). When CDC73 or RTF1 is depleted, the remaining PAF1c components remain associated with one another but fail to interact with Pol II or chromatin, and instead relocalize to the nucleolus (Mueller et al., 2004; Nordick et al., 2008; Porter et al., 2005; Qiu et al., 2006). Recent evidence indicates that CTR9 and PAF1 form a structural core that organizes complex assembly (Rodrigues and Lydall, 2018; Xie et al., 2018), and that CDC73 directly engages elongation factors such as Spt6 to secure PAF1c occupancy on chromatin (Ellison et al., 2023). These findings suggest that PAF1c is a multifunctional transcriptional complex whose subunits influence each other and collectively regulate Pol II transcription. Based on our data, we propose a model in which PAF1c depletion likely leads to RNAPII stalling at PPP sites, resulting in localized R-loop accumulation (Fig. 6(A)). We show that all subunits of PAF1c are essential for maintaining genome stability: depletion of each PAF1c subunit markedly increased the accumulation of R-loops and subsequent DNA damage, highlighting the critical role of PAF1c in safeguarding genome integrity.Fig. 6Model in which PAF1c depletion induces local R-loop accumulation and prevents topoisomerase inhibitor–induced global R-loop accumulation.(A) Mechanism of R-loop accumulation in PAF1c-depleted cells. PAF1c is essential for transcriptional elongation, histone modification, and cell survival (Chu et al., 2013). Disruption of PAF1 function directly impairs cellular viability: PAF1 knockdown increases apoptosis and reduces viability in cervical cancer cells (Zheng et al., 2020), and inhibition of PAF1 SUMOylation compromises post-irradiation growth and proliferation of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cells (Rauth et al., 2021). These studies identify PAF1 as a critical determinant of cancer cell survival under stress. Moreover, various DNA-damaging agents induce genomic instability and decrease cell viability (Bankoglu et al., 2021; Jeong et al., 2022; Tonon, 2024; Zhang et al., 2021); treatment of PAF1c-deficient cells with such agents may further compromise DNA repair capacity and promote lesion accumulation, thereby markedly enhancing cell death. For example, targeting the PAF1–RAD52 axis in combination with CDDP or gemcitabine strongly reduces the survival of resistant cells (Rauth et al., 2023). Consistent with previous reports (Herr et al., 2015; Li et al., 2023; Tiwari et al., 2021), we showed that depletion of each PAF1c subunit increases the sensitivity to most DNA-damaging agents including HU, MMS, CDDP, and BLM (Fig. 3(A) and (F)). Surprisingly, we found that PAF1c depletion conferred resistance to DNA damage induced by topoisomerase inhibitors (Fig. 3(B) and (F)). Depletion of each PAF1c subunit exhibited the same resistant effect to topoisomerase inhibitors, suggesting that altered transcriptional control induces this distinct response to topoisomerase inhibitors in PAF1c-depleted cells. Notably, PAF1c depletion decreased CPT- or ETOP-induced R-loop accumulation and DNA damage when these drugs were administered in G1 phase (Fig. 4(E) and Fig. S3D). In contrast, PAF1c depletion enhanced CPT- or ETOP- induced DNA damage when S-phase cells were treated (Fig. 4(E) and Fig. S3D). Topoisomerase inhibitors disrupt DNA topology and transcription, strongly inducing R-loop formation and compromising genome stability (Promonet et al., 2020; Sarni et al., 2022). Topoisomerase inhibitors mainly affect gene transcription in G1-phase cells and lead to R-loop accumulation. However, topoisomerase inhibitors can also impede the DNA replication process and induce DNA replication stress in S-phase cells. Moreover, ChIP-based traveling-ratio analysis revealed that PAF1 depletion induces RNA Pol II promoter-proximal pausing, thereby suppressing CPT-induced global R-loop accumulation (Fig. 5(D) and (G)). This mechanism largely accounts for the tolerance of PAF1-depleted cells to topoisomerase inhibitors. Thus, our findings indicate that PAF1c deficiency confers resistance to topoisomerase inhibitors only when cells are treated in G1 phase, suggesting that transcriptional abnormalities induced by PAF1 depletion underlie this resistance.

Loss of PAF1 or BRCA2 has been shown to cause RNA Pol II stalling at promoter-proximal sites, leading to R-loop accumulation and γH2AX formation (Shivji et al., 2018). Previous studies have also shown that PAF1 depletion impairs the release of RNA Pol II from promoter-proximal pausing (Hou et al., 2019), thereby disrupting transcriptional elongation (Rondón et al., 2004). We therefore propose that the resistance of PAF1c-depleted cells to topoisomerase inhibitors arises from transcriptional elongation defects caused by PAF1c depletion. During transcription (particularly in G1 phase), topoisomerase inhibitors prevent DNA relaxation, thereby inducing widespread RNA Pol II stalling, R-loop accumulation, and DNA damage. Our results show that PAF1c depletion in HeLa cells induces promoter-proximal RNA Pol II pausing (Fig. 5(F) and (G)), leading to a modest, localized increase in R-loop–associated DNA damage (Fig. 1, Fig. 2(D)). In contrast, topoisomerase inhibitor treatment of G1-phase HeLa cells promotes widespread RNA Pol II stalling across gene bodies (Fig. 5(F) and (G)), causing a marked, global increase in DNA damage and R-loop accumulation. These effects are attenuated when G1-phase PAF1c-depleted cells are treated with topoisomerase inhibitors: although DNA damage and R-loop levels are reduced compared with treated controls, they remain higher than in untreated depleted cells (Fig. 4, Fig. 5(B)). Thus, PAF1c depletion drives RNA Pol II retention at promoter-proximal pausing sites and elicits local R-loop accumulation, whereas topoisomerase inhibition induces extensive gene-body stalling and global R-loop accumulation. Concurrent PAF1c depletion and topoisomerase inhibition redirect RNA Pol II to promoter-proximal sites rather than into gene bodies, producing primarily a local increase but a global decrease in R-loop levels. Therefore, we propose the following model: in PAF1c-depleted cells, RNA Pol II elongation is impaired, which may reduce the global R-loop burden in the gene body region induced by topoisomerase inhibitors during transcription (Fig. 6(B)). A recent study further showed that single-stranded DNA generated at paused promoters is recognized by the SOSS-INTAC complex, which, together with exosome and XRN2 nucleases, removes nascent RNA–DNA hybrids to limit R-loop accumulation and terminate transcription (Xu et al., 2023). This may help explain how PPP site–associated R-loops in PAF1c-depleted cells are resolved, thereby reducing the risk of widespread genome instability.

It was recently reported that PAF1c plays a dual role in regulating both pause-release and processivity. PAF1 depletion leads to RNA Pol II release and upregulation of short genes, whereas transcripts from longer gene bodies are mostly downregulated, due to decreased RNA Pol II processivity and premature termination (Hou et al., 2019; Kim et al., 2013). In addition, depletion of PAF1 increases nucleosome occupancy near TSSs, leading to truncated transcripts and reduced levels of full-length transcripts (Aoi and Shilatifard, 2023). CTR9 depletion preferentially downregulates long genes involved in DNA replication and repair, redistributing Pol II and associated elongation factors toward promoter-proximal regions (Gaballa et al., 2024). In actively transcribed long genes, multiple elongation complexes operate simultaneously; inhibition of topoisomerases causes severe transcriptional stalling and topological stress, leading to extensive DNA damage (Baranello et al., 2025). Analysis of commonly downregulated genes under both PAF1 depletion and CPT treatment revealed that, for certain genes (e.g., RBMS1 and ZCCHC14), PAF1 depletion attenuated the transcriptional repression caused by CPT (Fig. S4B). Western blotting for RNAPII-Ser2P/Ser5P and ChIP-based traveling-ratio analysis showed that DRB treatment or PAF1 depletion reduces CPT-induced global R-loop accumulation by enforcing promoter-proximal RNA Pol II pausing; this promoter-proximal arrest helps explain the tolerance of PAF1c-deficient cells to topoisomerase inhibitors (Fig. 5(F) and (G)). These findings further support the proposed model (Fig. 6(B)). Interestingly, TOP1 deficiency downregulates long transcripts (Carson et al., 2004), and PAF1c depletion preferentially impairs elongation of long genes, suggesting overlap between genes affected by PAF1c loss and TOP1 disruption. Thus, PAF1c deficiency may stall transcription of long genes, thereby reducing topoisomerase inhibitor-induced R-loop formation on these genes and mimicking the effect of DRB treatment.

In summary, our study reveals that PAF1c regulates R-loop formation to maintain genome stability and influence DNA damage responses. Depletion of each PAF1c subunit not only promotes R-loop accumulation and genome instability but also confers resistance to topoisomerase inhibitors. Topoisomerase inhibitors have shown great potential for cancer therapy (Koster et al., 2007; Matias-Barrios and Dong, 2023). Our findings suggest that elevated PAF1c function in tumor cells may potentiate the cytotoxic effect of topoisomerase inhibitors.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Human embryonic kidney 293T, cervical carcinoma HeLa, colorectal carcinoma HCT116, and non–small cell lung carcinoma A549 cells were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM; Basalmedia, Shanghai, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco), at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2.

For synchronization, cells were serum-starved in DMEM without FBS for 24 h to arrest cells in G0 phase, then released into complete medium and treated with topoisomerase inhibitors for 6 h to induce DNA damage in G1-phase cells. To synchronize cells at the G1/S boundary, a double-thymidine block was applied: cells were treated with 2 mM thymidine for 16 h, released for 10 h, and subsequently treated again with 2 mM thymidine for 16 h. After release into complete medium, cells were treated with topoisomerase inhibitors for 6 h to induce DNA damage in S-phase cells.

Plasmid construction and transfection

RNase H1 cDNA was amplified by PCR from HeLa-derived cDNA using primers listed in Supplemental Table 1. The GFP-C1 vector was digested with BglII and EcoRI and recombined with the PCR product to generate GFP-RNase H1. Point mutagenesis with primers (Supplemental Table 1) was performed to obtain GFP-RNase H1-D210N. Transfections were carried out using polyethyleneimine (PEI; Sigma) at a 3:1 ratio of PEI to plasmid DNA; PEI–DNA complexes were prepared in DMEM, incubated with cells for 8 h, then replaced with complete medium. Cells were maintained for the indicated times depending on the experimental requirements.

For stable knockdown, shRNA oligonucleotides targeting PAF1, CDC73, LEO1, CTR9, RTF1, and SKIC8 (Supplemental Table 2) were annealed and cloned into the pLKO.1 vector.

Generation of stable cell lines

Lentiviruses were produced in 293T cells using the second-generation packaging system (Sigma). Cells were transfected with 6 μg lentiviral expression plasmid, 3 μg psPAX2, and 3 μg pMD2.G per 10 cm dish. After 8 h, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium. Viral supernatants were harvested 48 h later, filtered (0.45 μm), and stored at −80 °C.

Target cells were seeded in 6-well plates, infected with viral supernatants in the presence of 8 μg/mL polybrene (Sigma) for 8 h, and then the medium was replaced with fresh medium. Infected cells were selected with 2 μg/mL puromycin for 3–5 days with daily medium replacement until stable resistant colonies were obtained.

RNA isolation and RT–qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Vazyme) after washing cells with ice-cold PBS. RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit (Vazyme) following the manufacturer's instructions. qPCR was performed using SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) on a CFX Connect Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad). Relative expression was normalized to GAPDH. Primer sequences are listed in Supplemental Table 3.

Western blot analysis

Cells were lysed on ice for 30 min in NETN420 buffer (420 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5% NP-40, plus protease inhibitors). Protein concentration was determined using a BCA kit (Abbkine). Equal amounts of protein (10 μg) were resolved on 12% SDS–PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, IPVH00010). Membranes were blocked with 5% milk in PBST at room temperature for 30 min, then incubated with primary antibodies for 1 h: PAF1 (1:1 000, Proteintech #15441-1-AP), γH2AX (1:5 000, Abcam #ab81299), RNAPII-Ser5P (1:1 000, Abcam #ab5408), RNAPII-Ser2P (1:1 000, Abcam #ab5095), and GAPDH (1:10 000, Proteintech #60004-1-Ig). After PBST washes, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h, washed, and visualized with ECL (Bio-Rad) using X-ray film (Fujifilm).

Immunofluorescence

Cells were grown on coverslips, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and blocked with 1% BSA (BioFroxx) for 30 min at 37 °C. Cells were incubated with primary antibodies diluted in 1% BSA for 1 h at 37 °C: γH2AX (1:100, Abcam #ab81299) and S9.6 (1:300, Sigma #ZMS1017). After three PBS washes, fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500) were added for 1 h at 37 °C. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Sigma) for 10 min in the dark. Coverslips were mounted with antifade mounting medium (Sigma), imaged on an Olympus fluorescence microscope, and analyzed using ImageJ software.

Cell growth assay

Cells were harvested, resuspended, and seeded at 3 000 cells/well in 96-well plates (100 μL medium per well). After 24 h, cells were treated with the indicated drugs. Cell viability was assessed using Cell Counting Kit-8 (Abbkine); reagent was diluted 1:9 in complete medium, incubated with cells for 2 h, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm on a microplate reader (Potenov, Beijing, China). Cell viability was calculated, and dose–response curves were fitted using GraphPad Prism 9.5.1.

ChIP–qPCR

Approximately 1 × 107 cells were crosslinked for 20 min at room temperature in 1% (V/V) formaldehyde in PBS and quenched with glycine. Cells were collected, lysed in cell lysis buffer, and nuclei were isolated and resuspended in nuclear lysis buffer. Genomic DNA was sheared by sonication (10 cycles of 10 s on, 50 s off) at 40% output power, and the clarified lysate was incubated overnight at 4 °C with magnetic beads preconjugated to antibody. Immune complexes were washed, eluted, and decrosslinked, followed by RNase A and Proteinase K treatment. DNA was purified and analyzed by qPCR. The ChIP-based traveling ratio was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method: (Ct(Input) − Ct(ChIP)) was determined for the promoter and for the gene body (approximately 5 kb downstream of the promoter), the difference between these regions was calculated, and values were normalized to the control sample. Primer sequences are listed in Supplemental Table 4. The ChIP antibody used was anti-RNAPII (Cell Signaling Technology, #14958).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 software. An unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test was used for comparisons between two groups. Results are shown as mean ± SD. Significance is indicated as ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001, ns indicates non-significance (p > 0.05). All data were obtained from at least three independent experiments.
