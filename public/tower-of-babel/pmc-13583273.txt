Predictors of intramuscular glucocorticoid bridge therapy failure in rheumatoid arthritis: a real-world observational study
Authors: Wenhui Xie, Dai Gao, Hong Huang, Zhuoli Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Intramuscular glucocorticoid (GCs) bridge therapy is an equivalently recommended bridging strategy to oral GCs for rheumatoid arthritis (RA) under predefined tapering and discontinuation schemes.Objectives:We aimed to investigate the risk factors of intramuscular GCs bridge therapy failure in RA.Design:A retrospective analysis of collected data on RA patients initiating intramuscular betamethasone as bridging therapy.Methods:In this longitudinal real-world cohort study, 153 RA patients initiating intramuscular betamethasone as bridging therapy combined with csDMARDs (without concurrent biologics/JAK inhibitors) were included. Bridging strategy success under required: (1) GCs discontinuation within 3 months; (2) remission or low disease activity at discontinuation; (3) sustained control without short-term relapse post-discontinuation; (4) no transition to oral GCs and no initiation of b/tsDMARDs during the bridging period. Failure encompassed all other outcomes. Independent predictors were identified via multivariate logistic regression.Results:Among 153 patients receiving intramuscular betamethasone as bridging therapy, 94 (61.4%) experienced bridge therapy failure versus 59 (38.6%) successes. The most common mode for bridging therapy failure was transition to oral GCs (n = 47). The failure group exhibited persistently higher disease activity over 12 months (p < 0.001). Disease activity at intramuscular GCs initiation was significantly elevated in the failure group (median Clinical Disease Activity Index (CDAI): 23.0 (14.8–34.0) vs 13.0 (8.0–22.0), p < 0.001). Multivariate analysis confirmed higher CDAI (OR = 1.05 per unit, 95% CI 1.02–1.10, p = 0.003) and concomitant leflunomide use at GCs initiation (OR = 2.78, 95% CI 1.22–6.25, p = 0.020) as independent failure predictors.Conclusion:High disease activity and concurrent leflunomide use at GCs initiation independently predict failure of intramuscular GCs bridge therapy in RA. Early risk stratification may optimize induction therapy selection and potentially improve bridge therapy success rates.Keywords: bridging therapy, cohort study, discontinuation, glucocorticoids, rheumatoid arthritis

Introduction

In rheumatoid arthritis (RA), glucocorticoids (GCs) have long been integral to pharmacological management due to their potent anti-inflammatory effects.1,2 However, recognizing their time-dependent adverse effects, current guidelines emphasize short-term GCs use as a bridging therapy alongside conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (csDMARDs) until the latter demonstrate efficacy.3 –5 This strategy is endorsed by major professional associations, including the European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) and the Chinese Rheumatology Association.5,6 Despite these recommendations, chronic oral GC exposure remains a significant challenge in real-world RA management, with approximately half of patients receiving long-term therapy. 7 A recent nationwide survey among Chinese rheumatologists revealed that over half reported a decline in initiating oral GCs co-therapy with csDMARDs over time. 8 Key concerns driving this shift include adverse events and the difficulties associated with GCs tapering and cessation. Consequently, strategies to minimize chronic GCs dependency have become a critical focus in clinical practice.

Addressing the specific need for effective bridging strategies, the EULAR management guidelines specifically recommend either a single parenteral dose of GCs (e.g., intramuscular methylprednisolone) as a bridge or the implementation of a predefined tapering and discontinuation scheme when initiating oral GCs, with the goal of stopping GCs within 3 months. 5 While intramuscular GCs are recognized in guidelines, robust clinical evidence supporting their efficacy specifically as a bridging strategy in RA is currently lacking. Our prior real-world study suggested a potential advantage: the intramuscular betamethasone (depot formulation) bridging regimen was associated with a significantly reduced risk of long-term GCs use compared to oral GCs regimens, indicating it may be a superior alternative administration route for bridging. 9 Nevertheless, factors predicting successful bridging strategy after intramuscular GCs for RA remain unexplored. Therefore, this study aims to identify predictors of successful bridging therapy in RA patients receiving intramuscular betamethasone combined with csDMARDs.

Methods

The reporting of this study conforms to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement. 10

Study design and participants

The TARRA (Treat-to-TARget in Rheumatoid Arthritis) cohort is a prospective, longitudinal, observational cohort established at Peking University First Hospital in 2009. The cohort enrolls consecutive RA patients managed under a treat-to-target strategy. Detailed methodology has been published previously.7,11 –13 In brief, comprehensive data including demographics, clinical features, laboratory parameters, and treatment details are systematically collected at baseline and during regular follow-up visits: every 1–3 months during moderate (MDA)/high disease activity (HDA) and every 3–12 months during sustained low disease activity (LDA) or remission. Additional visits occur as clinically indicated. Treatment decisions are made by the attending rheumatologist following treat-to-target principles. The study received approval from the Institutional Review Board of Peking University First Hospital, and all participants provided written informed consent.

Patients included in this analysis met the following criteria: (1) fulfilled either the 1987 ACR 14 or 2010 ACR/EULAR RA classification criteria 15 ; (2) enrolled in the TARRA cohort between January 2009 and December 2019; (3) initiated intramuscular GCs as bridging therapy combined with csDMARDs at the baseline visit; (4) had a minimum of three documented follow-up visits and at least 6 months of follow-up data. Exclusion criteria were (1) prior or concurrent use of biologics or JAK inhibitors (biologic/targeted synthetic DMARD, b/tsDMARDs), ensuring that all included patients were b/tsDMARD-naïve at the time of intramuscular GCs initiation; (2) prior history of severe adverse events to GCs; (3) active infection or contraindications to GCs therapy; (4) pregnancy or lactation; (5) initiation of intramuscular betamethasone for indications other than RA bridging (e.g., osteoarthritis or other inflammatory diseases); and (6) incomplete medical records for the key variables of interest. For intramuscular GCs, all patients received 1 mL injection of compound betamethasone (Diprophos®), which contains 5 mg/mL of betamethasone dipropionate (prolonged-release ester, with a duration of 3–4 weeks) and 2 mg/mL of betamethasone sodium phosphate (a rapid-acting salt, with an onset <2 h).

Clinical assessments and data collection

Data were collected longitudinally from GC initiation until the earliest of December 2023, the last documented follow-up visit, or initiation of b/tsDMARD therapy. Collected variables included: (1) demographics: age, sex; (2) baseline RA characteristics: disease duration, rheumatoid factor (RF) titer, anticyclic citrullinated peptide antibody (anti-CCP) titer, tender joint count (TJC), swollen joint count (SJC), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C-reactive protein (CRP), patient global assessment (PGA), evaluator global assessment; (3) treatment: GC administration route (intramuscular vs oral), GC dosage, specific csDMARDs used (methotrexate, leflunomide, hydroxychloroquine, and sulfasalazine).

DMARD-naïve was defined as no prior DMARD exposure or exposure duration <3 months. Disease activity was assessed at each visit using the Disease Activity Score based on 28 joints (DAS28-ESR/CRP), Simplified Disease Activity Index (SDAI), and Clinical Disease Activity Index (CDAI). For risk stratification and clinical interpretability, CDAI was categorized according to validated cut-offs: LDA or remission (⩽10), MDA (11–22), and HDA (>22). Achievement of clinical remission or LDA according to at least two of the three composite indices (DAS28, SDAI, CDAI). Combination csDMARD therapy was defined as concurrent use of ⩾2 csDMARDs.

Study outcome

The primary outcome was successful bridging therapy, defined as meeting all of the following criteria: (1) GC cessation within 3 months of initiation; (2) achievement of LDA or clinical remission at the time of GC cessation (assessed using the closest available datapoint within ±4 weeks); (3) absence of disease flare within 6 months following GCs discontinuation; (4) no transition to oral GCs and no initiation of b/tsDMARDs during the bridging period. The transition to oral GCs or addition of b/tsDMARDs during the bridging period was considered as a failure of bridging therapy in the context of GCs strategy. The timepoint of GCs discontinuation of intramuscular GCs group was defined as 1 month after the last intramuscular GCs injection (accounting for the extended-release pharmacokinetics). Disease flare within 6 months post-GC cessation was defined by either an increase in DAS28 >0.6 and a current DAS28 >3.2, 16 or therapeutic escalation with re-initiation of GCs or intensification of DMARD therapy (including initiation of b/tsDMARDs or increase in csDMARD dose/number) due to uncontrolled disease activity as documented. To assess the robustness of our findings, we performed a series of sensitivity analyses: (1) using an alternative outcome definition that allowed GCs discontinuation within 6 months (instead of 3 months) while retaining all other criteria of the primary outcome; (2) using a more homogeneous endpoint of “lack of efficacy” (failure to achieve LDA or remission at GCs discontinuation, or transition to oral GCs or b/tsDMARDs due to insufficient disease control, excluding relapse); and (3) restricting the analysis to the DMARD-naïve subgroup.

Statistical analysis

Baseline patient characteristics are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed continuous variables and median (interquartile range, IQR) for nonnormally distributed variables. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages (n, %). Longitudinal changes in disease activity measures were analyzed using generalized estimating equations with an unstructured working correlation matrix and a robust covariance matrix. Between-group comparisons were employed using parametric (Student’s t test) and nonparametric (Mann–Whitney U) tests for continuous variables, based on distribution normality, alongside chi-square tests for categorical variables. Predictors of bridging therapy failure were identified using univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses. Variables demonstrating an association with the outcome in univariable analysis (p < 0.20) or deemed clinically relevant based on prior literature were considered for inclusion in the multivariable model. Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). To explore potential effect modification, we tested multiplicative interaction terms by adding product terms to the multivariable logistic regression model. Discrimination of the multivariable logistic regression model was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) with its 95% CI. Calibration was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. To address potential overfitting, we performed bootstrap optimism correction with 200 resamples. The optimism-corrected AUC was calculated following the method described by Harrell and colleagues, and the corrected value is reported as an exploratory internal validation metric. For the sensitivity analysis, alternative successful bridging therapy was used as the outcome using the Cox proportional hazards models. Results are reported as hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CI. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A two-sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of patients

Of the 179 RA patients who initially received intramuscular betamethasone combined with csDMARDs, 25 were excluded due to lack of at least 6 months of follow-up and/or three documented follow-up visits, and one was excluded due to concomitant use of targeted therapy at baseline. The remaining 153 consecutive RA patients initiating intramuscular compound betamethasone injection combined with csDMARDs were included, contributing 1790 protocol-scheduled clinic visits (Table S1). Within this analytic cohort, all patients had complete data for all outcomes and predictor variables, with no missing values. Baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. At therapy initiation, patients had a mean age of 53.5 ± 14.5 years and median disease duration of 21 months (IQR 6–96). The cohort was predominantly female (82.4%), DMARD-naïve (70.6%), and seropositive (76.5% for RF/anti-CCP). Most exhibited moderate-to-high disease activity, with median DAS28, SDAI, and CDAI scores of 5.03 (IQR 4.10–6.07), 21.7 (IQR 13.1–33.9), and 19.0 (IQR 11.0–31.0), respectively. Concomitant csDMARDs included methotrexate (90.2%), hydroxychloroquine (47.1%), and leflunomide (33.3%), with 68.6% receiving combination therapy.

Successful bridging therapy

During follow-up, successful bridging therapy was achieved in 38.6% (59/153) of patients. The reasons for bridging therapy failure included the following, listed in order of frequency: failure to discontinue GCs within 3 months with transition to oral GCs (n = 47), failure to achieve LDA or clinical remission at the time of GCs cessation (n = 34), short-term disease flare (n = 26), initiation of b/tsDMARD therapy during the bridging period (n = 5; Figure 1). The transition to oral GCs occurred within the 1- to 3-month period postinjection, most commonly around the 2-month timepoint.

The reasons for bridge therapy failure under intramuscular glucocorticoids bridging therapy.

Disease activity progressively decreased over time in both patient groups throughout the follow-up period. However, patients in the successful bridging group consistently exhibited significantly lower disease activity levels at all assessed timepoints compared to those in the bridging failure group (Figure 2).

Predictors of intramuscular GC bridge therapy failure

Compared to patients with successful bridging, those experiencing bridging failure were more frequently male (p = 0.075) and demonstrated significantly higher baseline composite disease activity scores (e.g., DAS28, SDAI, CDAI) as well as higher individual disease activity measures (e.g., TJC, SJC, CRP, ESR, PGA; p < 0.001; Table 1). Regarding pharmacotherapy, the use of single csDMARDs and csDMARD combination therapy was generally comparable between the two groups. Nevertheless, initial leflunomide use was numerically more frequent in the bridging failure group, though this difference did not reach statistical significance (38.3% vs 25.4%, p = 0.10).

In univariable analyses, significant differences were observed for both individual and composite disease activity measures. Given the comprehensive nature of disease activity indices and concerns regarding multicollinearity, we ultimately selected CDAI, ESR, and CRP as representative disease activity indicators for inclusion in subsequent analyses. Finally, gender, CDAI, ESR, CRP, leflunomide use, and csDMARD combination therapy were entered into the multivariable logistic regression model. Results demonstrated that higher disease activity at GCs initiation (CDAI: OR = 1.05, 95% CI 1.02–1.10, p = 0.003) and concomitant leflunomide use (OR = 2.78, 95% CI: 1.22–6.25, p = 0.020) were independently associated with bridging therapy failure (Table 2). In exploratory interaction analyses, a significant positive interaction was observed between baseline CDAI and leflunomide use (OR = 1.057 per unit CDAI, 95% CI 1.013–1.102, p = 0.011), indicating that the detrimental effect of higher disease activity on bridging failure is amplified in patients receiving leflunomide. No significant interactions were found between CDAI and csDMARD combination therapy (OR = 1.008, 95% CI 0.982–1.033, p = 0.555) or between leflunomide use and csDMARD combination therapy (OR = 1.96, 95% CI 0.90–4.27, p = 0.091).

As an exploratory analysis of model performance, the area under the receiver AUC was 0.728 (95% CI 0.646–0.809), and the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test yielded a p value of 0.899, indicating acceptable discrimination and calibration of the multivariate model. Bootstrap optimism correction gave a corrected AUC of 0.70 (95% CI 0.61–0.79), suggesting only modest overfitting and supporting the robustness of our findings. These metrics are provided for descriptive purposes only, as the primary focus of this study is risk factor identification rather than prediction model development. Additionally, using clinically validated CDAI categories (LDA/remission ⩽10 as reference), multivariate logistic regression showed that MDA (11–22) and high disease activity (HDA, >22) were independently associated with bridge therapy failure, with adjusted ORs of 2.60 (95% CI 1.18–5.74, p = 0.018) and 6.82 (95% CI 2.66–17.45, p < 0.001), respectively, demonstrating a clear dose–response relationship.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis using the alternative outcome definition (which omitted the 3-month timeframe requirement) yielded consistent findings (CDAI: HR = 1.03, 95% CI 1.01–1.06, p = 0.003; leflunomide use: HR = 2.44, 95% CI 1.11–5.56, p = 0.020; Table 3). A sensitivity analysis using a “pure lack of efficacy” endpoint (excluding relapse) confirmed that higher baseline CDAI remained a significant predictor (OR = 1.05 per unit, 95% CI 1.02–1.08, p = 0.002). Leflunomide use showed a directionally consistent effect (OR = 2.06, 95% CI 0.93–4.56, p = 0.074), with a wider CI due to reduced events. Additional sensitivity analysis restricted to DMARD-naïve patients (n = 108) yielded consistent results: higher baseline CDAI (OR = 1.04 per unit, 95% CI 1.00–1.09, p = 0.041) and concomitant leflunomide use (OR = 3.42, 95% CI 1.22–9.64, p = 0.019) remained independent predictors of bridge therapy failure, comparable to the primary analysis in the full cohort.

Discussion

GCs have long been integral to RA management, valued for their potent anti-inflammatory effects, rapid onset of action, and demonstrated capacity to retard radiographic progression. Contemporary guidelines now strongly advocate for short-term GC use as bridging therapy alongside csDMARDs, explicitly recommending discontinuation within 3–6 months upon achievement of csDMARD efficacy. Despite these targeted recommendations, chronic oral GC exposure remains prevalent in real-world settings, underscoring an urgent need for strategies to minimize cumulative GC burden. While oral and intramuscular administration represents the primary systemic routes for GC bridging, the extended-release pharmacokinetics of intramuscular GCs formulations may confer an inherent advantage in reducing the risk of prolonged exposure and dependency. Nevertheless, clinical evidence characterizing the real-world profiles of an intramuscular betamethasone bridging strategy in RA is still lacking, especially for predictive factors for subsequent bridging therapy failure.

This real-world cohort study provides the first comparative evidence for intramuscular betamethasone as a bridging strategy in RA. There only 38.6% achieved successful bridging (defined by stringent composite endpoints) with intramuscular bridging strategy, with transition to oral GCs as commonest reason for bridging therapy failure. Moreover, two modifiable predictors of bridging failure: elevated baseline disease activity (CDAI: OR 0.95, p = 0.002) and concomitant leflunomide use (OR 0.36, p = 0.015). The inverse association between baseline CDAI and bridging success is physiologically plausible. High inflammatory burden may overwhelm the transient anti-inflammatory effects of depot GCs, causing the failure of bridging therapy. Similarly, the experience from oral GCs bridging therapy in indicated a higher DAS at GCs initiation was associated with lower probability of successful GCs discontinuation in patients with early RA and undifferentiated arthritis. 17 While dynamic measures may offer additional predictive value, baseline disease activity using validated CDAI thresholds provides an immediately available and clinically actionable risk estimate. The strong dose–response association, particularly the sevenfold risk for patients with HDA, can guide upfront decisions on whether intramuscular GCs bridging is appropriate. Surprisingly, leflunomide, a potent csDMARD, correlated with bridging failure. While leflunomide use showed no significant association with bridging failure in univariable analysis, it emerged as an independent predictor in the multivariable model. This apparent paradox likely reflects confounding by csDMARD combination therapy. Specifically, leflunomide-treated patients exhibited significantly higher rates of combination csDMARD regimens compared to those not receiving leflunomide (94.1% vs 62.7%, p < 0.001). Consequently, leflunomide use may reflect a clinical status for patients with complex disease profiles, typically those with methotrexate contraindications, inadequate prior responses, or inherently refractory disease; thereby explaining its adjusted association with bridging failure. Given the observational design, leflunomide use may serve as a marker of a distinct disease profile (e.g., methotrexate intolerance, prior treatment failure, or inherently refractory disease) rather than a direct pharmacological interaction. Therefore, caution is warranted when considering intramuscular GCs bridging in this subgroup. Future prospective studies are needed to determine whether leflunomide itself or the underlying disease phenotype drives this association. Given ongoing controversies surrounding GCs use in RA, early identification of patients likely to fail intramuscular GCs bridging therapy is critical.18,19 Consequently, clinicians should exercise caution when considering intramuscular GCs bridging in these high-risk individuals, and alternative strategies may be considered. When clinically indicated, early escalation to biological or targeted synthetic DMARDs may be warranted to achieve rapid inflammation control and symptomatic improvement.

While our study identifies two independent predictors of intramuscular GCs bridge therapy failure, these findings do not yet constitute a clinically ready prediction model from a TRIPOD (Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis) perspective. For example, our model lacks an externally validated risk scoring system. In future research, we aim to develop and externally validate a robust prediction model following TRIPOD guidelines. This will include a larger multicenter cohort, incorporation of dynamic predictors such as early disease activity response at 4 weeks and patient-reported outcomes, and rigorous internal validation to avoid overfitting. Such a model would help clinicians identify patients most likely to benefit from intramuscular GCs bridging versus those who require upfront escalation to b/tsDMARDs.

The most common mode of failure was transition to oral GCs. Compound betamethasone contains both a rapid-acting and a prolonged-release ester, with an effective duration of approximately one month. Patients switched to oral GCs either because of primary nonresponse or because of symptom rebound at 2–3 months before csDMARDs achieved full efficacy. Although current guidelines would recommend escalation to b/tsDMARDs in this situation, most patients in our cohort instead received oral GCs. This likely reflects real-world practice in China during the study period, where the high cost of biologics/targeted synthetic DMARDs and a preference for familiar, inexpensive oral GCs influence clinical decisions. Early initiation of oral prednisone after failed intramuscular bridging may temporarily postpone b/tsDMARD escalation, a practice pattern that warrants further prospective evaluation.

Our findings contribute to the ongoing debate regarding the optimal role of GCs in RA: whether to minimize GCs exposure by early biologic escalation or to rely on short-term GCs bridging as a cost-effective initial strategy.18,20 Current guidelines recommend GCs bridging for up to 3 months, with rapid tapering and discontinuation. 5 However, in our real-world cohort, only 38.6% of patients achieved successful bridging under a stringent composite definition. More importantly, we identified subgroups—those with high baseline disease activity (HDA, CDAI >22) and those receiving concomitant leflunomide—with markedly elevated failure risks (adjusted OR 6.82 and 2.78, respectively). For these high-risk patients, the likely failure of intramuscular GC bridging suggests that an alternative approach, such as early initiation of b/tsDMARDs without a prolonged GC trial, may be more appropriate. Thus, our findings do not argue against GC bridging in all patients but rather support risk-stratified decision-making: patients with LDA or MDA may be suitable for a GC bridging attempt, whereas those with HDA or leflunomide use may benefit from upfront advanced therapy.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations inherent to its single-center observational design. First, while a wide range of adjustments were applied to minimize measured confounding, unmeasured factors (e.g., smoking, clinician tapering preferences, and patient adherence behaviors) may influence outcomes. Second, the findings of a single-center study may limit the external generalizability, though internal validity is greatest. Our single-center treat-to-target cohort with high-frequency follow-up may limit external generalizability to routine practices with less intensive monitoring. Multicenter validation in diverse settings is needed. Third, safety outcomes and long-term follow-up were not systematically assessed. Fourth, although early oral prednisone after failed intramuscular bridging may sometimes defer b/tsDMARD initiation, this does not affect our outcome classification because any transition to oral GCs was already counted as bridge therapy failure. Fifth, we did not collect serial serum GCs levels or individual pharmacodynamic parameters (e.g., cortisol suppression or drug concentration). Therefore, we cannot directly correlate the duration or magnitude of GCs exposure with clinical outcomes. Future prospective studies incorporating pharmacokinetic sampling are needed to explore these relationships. In addition, due to the real-world design, early follow-up data (e.g., at 1 month) were not uniformly available, so we could not include early treatment response as a dynamic predictor. Baseline CDAI categories provide immediate risk stratification at treatment initiation, but dynamic measures may offer additional predictive value. Finally, whether intramuscular GCs bridging therapy delays or accelerates the initiation of b/tsDMARDs remains an unanswered question. Future studies with a no-GCs comparator are planned to investigate the impact of intramuscular bridging on the timing of advanced therapy initiation.

Conclusion

In intramuscular betamethasone bridging therapy combined with csDMARDs, successful bridging was achieved in approximately one-third of patients, with transition to oral GCs constituting the most common failure cause. High disease activity and concomitant leflunomide use at GCs initiation independently predicted failure. Risk stratification using these factors may guide bridging decisions. In patients with high baseline disease activity or concomitant leflunomide use, clinicians should exercise caution with intramuscular GCs bridging and consider closer monitoring or alternative induction therapies.
