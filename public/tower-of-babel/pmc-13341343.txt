Enriched Metabolic Phenotyping Refines Phenotypic Resolution of Type 2 Diabetes
Authors: Min Wang, Yang Ou, Heng Su, Bing Zhang, Ben Niu, Anwar Ahmed, Guoqiang Xing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsData‐driven clustering studies of type 2 diabetes (T2D) based on limited routine clinical variables have reproducibly described major metabolic subgroups, including severe insulin‐deficient diabetes (SIDD), severe insulin‐resistant diabetes (SIRD), mild obesity‐related diabetes and mild age‐related diabetes. We examined whether deeper metabolic phenotyping can improve phenotypic resolution beyond these low‐dimensional frameworks.MethodsWe performed an exploratory cross‐sectional clustering analysis in 94 adults with newly diagnosed T2D and complete data, drawn from an initial 97 screened participants who underwent deep metabolic phenotyping. Principal component analysis (PCA) guided selection of 13 relatively independent variables spanning six physiological domains: glycaemic exposure, insulin sensitivity and secretion, visceral adiposity, lipid metabolism, skeletal muscle status, hepatic injury and haematological indices. K‐means clustering was applied to standardised variables. Robustness was assessed across prespecified 5‐, 9‐ and 13‐variable panels, exclusion of age, alternative muscle and adiposity representations and comparison with hierarchical and Gaussian mixture clustering. Autoimmune diabetes was not examined due to insufficient case numbers.ResultsFive phenotypic clusters were observed, including preserved insulin sensitivity with compensatory hyperinsulinaemia (IS‐HI). Two clusters aligned with canonical SIDD‐like and SIRD‐like phenotypes. Within the enriched model, two additional physiologically coherent patterns were resolved: a myogenic–anaemia phenotype (MA), characterised by reduced skeletal muscle percentage and altered haematological indices and a hepato‐lipotoxic phenotype (HL), marked by dyslipidaemia and elevated hepatic enzymes. In an unconstrained sensitivity rerun of the same 13‐variable panel, however, a distinct SIDD‐like cluster did not re‐emerge, underscoring that higher‐dimensional clustering depends in part on variable selection and biologically anchored interpretation. Internal robustness analyses supported preservation of the overall cluster architecture across variable panels, although alternative clustering methods yielded non‐identical partitions.ConclusionsPhenotypic depth may improve the mechanistic resolution of type 2 diabetes beyond routine‐variable frameworks. However, the present single‐centre findings are hypothesis‐generating only, do not support clinical decision‐making and all treatment‐related inferences remain speculative without longitudinal outcome data and external validation.Keywords: data‐driven clustering, deep metabolic phenotyping, insulin secretion, precision medicine, skeletal muscle, type 2 diabetes, visceral adiposity

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes (T2D) is a heterogeneous metabolic disorder encompassing diverse trajectories of insulin resistance, β‐cell dysfunction, adiposity and cardiometabolic risk. Conventional classification based primarily on glycemic thresholds fails to capture this heterogeneity and offers limited guidance for prognosis or personalised intervention. This limitation prompted the development of data‐driven clustering approaches to identify biologically and clinically meaningful diabetes subtypes beyond traditional diagnostic categories [1, 2, 3].

These studies demonstrated that adult‐onset diabetes could be subdivided into reproducible phenotypes using a small set of routinely available clinical variables, including age at diagnosis, body‐mass index (BMI), glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) and HOMA‐derived indices of insulin resistance and β‐cell function. These studies consistently identified subgroups corresponding to severe insulin‐deficient diabetes (SIDD), severe insulin‐resistant diabetes (SIRD), mild obesity‐related diabetes (MOD) and mild age‐related diabetes (MARD), with an additional autoimmune subgroup when antibody data were available. Importantly, these clusters differed in disease progression and risk of complications, establishing a widely adopted reference framework for T2D heterogeneity [1, 2, 3].

To achieve population‐scale reproducibility, these investigations deliberately prioritised low‐dimensional, standardised biomarker panels that could be measured consistently across centres and time periods. While methodologically appropriate, this design choice imposed inherent constraints on biological resolution. Variables related to visceral adiposity independent of BMI, skeletal muscle composition, hepatic metabolic stress and haematological status were rarely incorporated, largely because they complicate harmonisation across large multi‐centre cohorts. As a result, important secondary and tertiary pathophysiological axes may remain unresolved within conventional clustering frameworks.

BMI, although widely used, does not distinguish visceral from subcutaneous adiposity and fails to capture ectopic lipid deposition, which is more strongly associated with hepatic insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, non‐alcoholic fatty liver disease and cardiovascular risk than total adiposity [4]. This limitation is particularly relevant in Asian and older populations, in whom substantial metabolic risk may occur at relatively modest BMI levels [5, 6]. Composite indices such as the Chinese Visceral Adiposity Index (CVAI) have been shown to outperform BMI in predicting insulin resistance, incident T2D, fatty liver disease and cardiometabolic outcomes, particularly in East Asian populations [7, 8, 9].

In parallel, growing evidence highlights skeletal muscle as a critical yet under‐represented determinant of metabolic health in T2D. Skeletal muscle is the principal site of insulin‐mediated glucose disposal and relative muscle depletion contributes to insulin resistance, metabolic inflexibility and adverse outcomes [10]. Recent studies incorporating muscle‐related measures into clustering frameworks suggest that diabetes subgroups characterised by relative skeletal muscle depletion exhibit increased risk of sarcopenia and metabolic deterioration, independent of BMI and age [11]. These muscle‐defined phenotypes often coexist with distinct haematological profiles, including alterations in haemoglobin concentration and red blood cell indices, which may reflect impaired oxygen‐carrying capacity, chronic inflammation and nutritional stress [12, 13]. Despite their routine availability in clinical practice, such haematological parameters remain largely unexplored in diabetes clustering models.

Large epidemiological cohorts are indispensable for defining the macroscopic architecture of T2D heterogeneity, but higher‐order biological signals may be diluted when analyses are restricted to a limited set of routine variables. Smaller cohorts with deep phenotyping, when analysed using principled dimension‐reduction strategies such as principal component analysis (PCA), offer a complementary approach by resolving finer physiological structure across independent metabolic domains while minimising redundancy and overfitting [14].

The aim of the present study was to determine whether PCA‐guided enrichment of metabolically informative variables can refine phenotypic resolution of newly diagnosed T2D beyond conventional BMI‐centred models, while preserving the established cluster architecture described in large population‐based studies. By explicitly comparing low‐ and high‐dimensional clustering within a deeply phenotyped cohort, this study addresses a critical gap between population‐scale reproducibility and mechanistic resolution in precision diabetes phenotyping.

Methods

Study Population

We conducted a cross‐sectional analysis of computer‐stored clinical data from the Department of Endocrinology, First People's Hospital of Yunnan Province (Kunming, China). Eligible participants were adults with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes, defined according to the 1999 WHO criteria (fasting plasma glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, 2‐h plasma glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L following a standardised carbohydrate challenge, or HbA1c ≥ 6.5%), with diagnosis made within the preceding 12 months. Of 97 initially screened participants, 94 had complete data across the variables required for the primary 13‐variable analysis and were included in the final complete‐case model. Individuals with long‐standing diabetes, acute metabolic complications, severe hepatic or renal dysfunction, pregnancy, malignancy, or major systemic disease were excluded.

The present analysis focused exclusively on clinically diagnosed type 2 diabetes. Autoimmune diabetes, including type 1 diabetes and latent autoimmune diabetes in adults, was not examined because case numbers were insufficient for balanced clustering analysis. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the institutional ethics committee.

Clinical and Laboratory Measurements

Anthropometric measurements included height, weight and waist circumference. Blood pressure was measured using standard clinical procedures. Visceral adiposity was estimated using the Chinese Visceral Adiposity Index (CVAI) [7]. Skeletal muscle mass and skeletal muscle percentage (SMp) were estimated using validated anthropometric prediction equations [15].

Glycemic and hormonal responses were assessed using a standardised postprandial carbohydrate challenge, in which participants ingested steamed bread providing a carbohydrate load equivalent to 75 g of anhydrous glucose, a method commonly used in Chinese clinical practice and epidemiological studies. Venous blood samples were obtained in the fasting state and 2 h after ingestion for measurement of plasma glucose, insulin and C‐peptide.

Insulin sensitivity was assessed using the Matsuda insulin sensitivity index (ISI_M), calculated from fasting and postprandial measurements [16]. β‐cell function was characterised using fasting C‐peptide (FCP), dynamic C‐peptide response (ΔC‐peptide [ΔCP]) and the disposition index at 120 min (DI_120) [17]. Glycemic exposure was summarised using HbA1c and postprandial glycemic excursion was calculated as the difference between 2‐h postprandial and fasting glucose concentrations (Δglucose [ΔG]).

Lipid parameters were summarised using the atherogenic index of plasma (AIP). Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) was used as a marker of hepatic metabolic stress and uric acid as a marker of purine‐related metabolic dysfunction [18]. Haematological indices included haemoglobin (HGB) and red cell distribution width coefficient of variation (RDW‐CV). The equations of the key parameters are shown in Table S1.

Variable Selection and Clustering Strategy

Variable selection was guided by principal component analysis (PCA) to balance biological coverage and minimise redundancy. Three prespecified variable panels reflecting increasing phenotypic resolution were constructed. The core panel (5 variables) comprised ΔC‐peptide (ΔCP), the Matsuda insulin sensitivity index (ISI_M), HbA1c, the Chinese visceral adiposity index (CVAI) and the atherogenic index of plasma (AIP). The intermediate panel (9 variables) expanded β‐cell, skeletal muscle and hepatic dimensions by adding fasting C‐peptide (FCP), disposition index at 120 min (DI_120), skeletal muscle percentage (SMp) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT). The full panel (13 variables) further incorporated postprandial glycemic excursion (Δglucose), uric acid and haematological indices (haemoglobin [HGB] and red cell distribution width coefficient of variation [RDW‐CV]).

Variable selection for principal component analysis (PCA) and cluster analysis followed four principles: representation of distinct pathophysiological domains, minimisation of redundancy among correlated measures, clinical accessibility at diagnosis and robustness to inclusion or exclusion of age. Candidate variables were prioritised when they showed strong contribution to one of the leading principal components (typically absolute loading ≥ 0.40), acceptable interpretability and limited redundancy with alternative measures from the same physiological domain. Pairwise correlations were examined in a full correlation matrix (Figure S7) and when two candidate variables were highly correlated, the more stable and biologically interpretable metric was retained. This is why three secretion‐related measures were retained in the full model: FCP to reflect basal secretory reserve, ΔCP to capture dynamic postprandial responsiveness and DI_120 to index secretion in the context of insulin sensitivity.

For the primary analysis, clusters were biologically anchored by post hoc interpretation of centroid profiles to assign labels consistent with established diabetes subtypes, whereas sensitivity analyses were performed without such anchoring to assess the intrinsic stability of cluster structure. The unconstrained rerun of the 13‐variable model was prespecified to test whether an independent SIDD‐like cluster emerged in higher‐dimensional metabolic space.

All variables were standardised before analysis. K‐means clustering was used because it provides transparent centroid‐based partitions comparable with the original ANDIS‐style literature and performs well with standardised continuous variables in modest samples. The choice of k = 5 was based on combined biological interpretability, avoidance of over‐fragmentation, consistency with prior diabetes‐clustering studies and examination of adjacent k solutions using silhouette, Calinski–Harabasz and Davies–Bouldin diagnostics (k = 4–6; Table S6). In this modest dataset, these internal indices were informative but not individually decisive. Alternative clustering using hierarchical Ward linkage and Gaussian mixture models was performed as a sensitivity analysis rather than the primary model.

Sensitivity Analyses

Prespecified sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of cluster structure. These included substitution of alternative skeletal muscle metrics, exclusion of age from clustering variables to evaluate potential age‐driven circularity and repetition of clustering across the core, intermediate and full variable panels. Concordance of cluster assignments and preservation of core cluster architecture were assessed qualitatively and quantitatively.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are summarised as mean (SD) or median (IQR), as appropriate. PCA was used for dimensional assessment and variable guidance and k‐means clustering was applied to standardised variables. Quantitative robustness was assessed with average silhouette width, Calinski–Harabasz index, Davies–Bouldin index and adjusted Rand index across alternative models. Pairwise correlations were examined for all clustering variables. Descriptive analyses were performed in SPSS version 24 (IBM). For transparency, the cluster‐number diagnostics reported in this revision were regenerated directly from the archived dataset in a documented Python 3.13.5 environment (pandas 2.2.3, NumPy 2.3.5, SciPy 1.17.0, scikit‐learn 1.8.0 and matplotlib 3.10.8). Earlier exploratory analyses had also been conducted locally in R, but exact installed package versions from that original workflow were not archived; we therefore removed unsupported package‐version claims from the manuscript. All analyses were exploratory and hypothesis‐generating. The full pairwise correlation matrix for the 13‐variable primary panel is provided in Figure S7. Quantitative robustness and cluster‐number diagnostics are summarised in Figure S8 and Table S6. Cross‐model cluster‐size shifts, alternative‐method concordance, component/index sensitivity analyses, age‐ and BMI‐adjusted haematological comparisons and participant transition matrices are detailed in Tables S7–S12. A schematic physiological summary of the observed clusters is shown in Figure S9.

Despite relatively modest variation in BMI, the cohort exhibited marked heterogeneity across multiple metabolic domains. Measures of visceral adiposity (CVAI), skeletal muscle percentage, insulin sensitivity and β‐cell function spanned wide ranges. Routine haematological indices, including haemoglobin and red blood cell distribution width, also varied substantially, consistent with early metabolic and nutritional differences.

Principal Component Analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) of standardised variables was used to characterise the structure of metabolic heterogeneity and to guide clustering. In enriched models, the first three principal components explained a substantial proportion of total variance (Figure S1), in which the loading plot highlights the clustering‐panel variables together with selected non‐redundant anchor variables (ALT, AST, HOMA‐IR, HOMA‐B, age, FM%, TG, HDL and BMI) while preserving large‐font non‐overlapping labels.

The first principal component was dominated by markers of adiposity‐associated insulin resistance, including ISI_M (inverse loading), CVAI, AIP and alanine aminotransferase. Skeletal muscle percentage (SMp or SM%) loaded in the opposite direction, consistent with an inverse relationship between relative muscle mass and insulin resistance.

The second principal component was primarily defined by glycemic exposure and β‐cell function, including HbA1c, fasting C‐peptide, ΔC‐peptide and disposition index at 120 min, reflecting variation in insulin secretory capacity relative to glycemic burden. Haematological indices, particularly haemoglobin and red cell distribution width, loaded alongside skeletal muscle–related variables rather than adiposity measures, suggesting a shared biological axis related to muscle status and systemic metabolic stress.

Cluster Identification Across Variable Sets

Clustering analyses were performed using progressively enriched variable sets comprising 5, 9 and 13 variables. Across all models, five clusters were observed and the overall cluster architecture remained interpretable despite differences in variable composition (Figure 1, Tables S2–S5 and Figures S2–S6). On the same 94 complete‐case participants, cluster sizes shifted from 22/2/31/20/19 in the 5‐variable model to 7/2/29/29/27 in the 9‐variable model and 9/2/20/32/31 in the 13‐variable model for IS‐HI, SIDD‐like, SIRD‐like, MA and HL, respectively, indicating phenotypic refinement rather than simple proliferation of clusters (Table S7).

Using the 5‐variable core model, clusters broadly corresponded to insulin‐deficient, insulin‐resistant, obesity‐related and age‐related phenotypes, consistent with previously described T2D subgroups (Table S2, Figure S3). However, substantial within‐cluster heterogeneity was evident.

Expansion to the 9‐variable model refined separation of insulin‐resistant phenotypes by incorporating visceral adiposity and skeletal muscle percentage, without altering the number of clusters (Figure S4). The largest shifts involved IS‐HI, MA and HL, suggesting that muscle and hepatic information redistributed individuals from broader obesity‐related categories into more mechanistically specific phenotypes.

Accordingly, while the primary 13‐variable no‐age model retained a SIDD‐like phenotype for interpretability (Figure 1), an unconstrained sensitivity rerun using identical variables did not yield an independent SIDD‐like cluster (Figure S5). We interpret this as evidence that insulin deficiency in this cohort occupies a relatively small region of high‐dimensional metabolic space and that the SIDD‐like label in the primary model is partly dependent on biologically anchored interpretation rather than on a fully isolated cluster centroid.

Quantitatively, the primary 13‐variable k = 5 model had an average silhouette width of 0.154. Across adjacent k solutions, the internal cluster‐number diagnostics were mixed rather than unanimous: at k = 4, average silhouette width 0.157, Calinski–Harabasz 13.553 and Davies–Bouldin 1.584; at k = 5, 0.154, 13.149 and 1.556; and at k = 6, 0.121, 12.976 and 1.485 (Table S6). We therefore retained k = 5 on combined grounds of biological interpretability, avoidance of over‐fragmentation, consistency with prior diabetes‐clustering studies and qualitative preservation of the major phenotype structure. Specifically, Table S7 reports cluster‐size shifts across variable panels, Table S8 summarises alternative‐method concordance, Table S9 reports component/index sensitivity analyses and Figure S8 visualises the corresponding robustness and k diagnostics.

Skeletal Muscle and Visceral Adiposity Across Clusters

Marked differences in skeletal muscle percentage and CVAI were observed across clusters (Table 1, Figure 1). Clusters characterised by severe insulin resistance exhibited significantly higher CVAI despite only modest differences in BMI, indicating disproportionate visceral adiposity.

Relative skeletal muscle depletion was most pronounced in insulin‐resistant clusters with elevated CVAI. These clusters exhibited significantly lower skeletal muscle percentage compared with insulin‐deficient or age‐related clusters, despite comparable or lower BMI. Skeletal muscle percentage provided clearer discrimination of insulin‐resistant versus insulin‐deficient phenotypes than absolute skeletal muscle mass.

Haematological Profiles Across Clusters

Haematological indices differed systematically across clusters (Table 1, Figure 1). Clusters characterised by lower skeletal muscle percentage exhibited lower haemoglobin concentrations and altered red blood cell indices, including differences in red cell distribution width and mean corpuscular haemoglobin concentration.

These differences remained significant in ANCOVA models adjusted for age and BMI, with cluster effects persisting for HGB (F = 13.36, p < 0.001) and RDW‐CV (F = 32.48, p < 0.001) (Table S10). In contrast, clusters with preserved skeletal muscle percentage demonstrated more favourable haematological profiles.

Sensitivity Analyses

Substitution of skeletal muscle percentage with absolute skeletal muscle mass attenuated discrimination between insulin‐resistant and insulin‐deficient phenotypes, with increased cluster overlap (Tables S2–S5 and Figures S2–S6). Skeletal muscle index or height‐indexed skeletal muscle mass (SMI, calculated as SM/height² and expressed in kg/m²) yielded clustering results more concordant with the primary model than absolute muscle mass alone.

Exclusion of age from the clustering variables did not materially alter cluster composition or interpretation, indicating that observed clustering patterns were not driven by age‐related circularity. Likewise, replacing SM% with SMI, AIP with TG plus HDL, or CVAI with BMI plus waist circumference preserved the major cluster architecture, although composite indices generally yielded cleaner separation than their individual components (ARI range 0.671–0.806; Table S9).

Direct comparison of 5‐, 9‐ and 13‐variable models demonstrated that phenotypic enrichment progressively improved resolution of insulin‐resistant and muscle‐related phenotypes without introducing instability or excessive fragmentation. These findings support the view that additional variables mainly repartitioned broad obesity‐related categories into physiologically coherent MA and HL phenotypes rather than generating spurious clusters. Transition matrices in Tables S11 and S12 further illustrate how reduced‐variable partitions were redistributed into the richer 13‐variable phenotypes.

Discussion

In this study, we applied PCA‐guided variable selection and data‐driven clustering to a deeply phenotyped cohort of adults with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes (T2D). By systematically comparing clustering performance across progressively enriched variable panels, we found that incorporation of visceral adiposity, skeletal muscle, hepatic and haematological indices improved phenotypic resolution beyond BMI‐based models while preserving the core metabolic architecture described in prior large‐scale studies. Using a 13‐variable model spanning six physiological domains, five phenotypic clusters were observed: an insulin‐sensitive hyperinsulinaemia subtype (IS‐HI), SIDD‐like and SIRD‐like phenotypes and two additional clusters characterised by myogenic–haematological alterations (MA) and hepato‐lipotoxic features (HL).

Large‐scale clustering studies established that adult‐onset diabetes can be reproducibly classified into four to six broad subgroups using a small number of routinely available variables [1, 2, 3]. These studies prioritised low‐dimensional biomarker panels—typically age, BMI, HbA1c and HOMA‐derived indices—to maximise reproducibility, feasibility and cross‐cohort harmonisation in populations exceeding tens of thousands of individuals (Table 2). Our findings, while confirming this macroscopic framework, demonstrate that minimal‐variable models may obscure biologically meaningful substructure within established phenotypes, particularly when obesity is operationalised using BMI alone.

A key distinction between our study and earlier reports lies in phenotypic resolution rather than cluster existence. In population‐scale studies, insulin‐resistant phenotypes often encompass heterogeneous individuals differing in visceral adiposity, hepatic stress and skeletal muscle status. In our cohort, incorporation of CVAI, skeletal muscle percentage, liver enzymes and haematological indices separated insulin‐resistant phenotypes into physiologically coherent HL and MA clusters. We do not interpret these as final immutable disease entities; rather, they may represent stable high‐density regions along partially orthogonal muscle–haematological and hepato‐lipotoxic axes within broader insulin‐resistant diabetes.

Visceral adiposity emerged as a stronger discriminator of insulin resistance than BMI, consistent with evidence that ectopic fat deposition drives hepatic insulin resistance and dyslipidemia, particularly in East Asian populations and at modest BMI levels [5, 6, 7, 38]. Similarly, skeletal muscle percentage provided superior discrimination of insulin‐resistant versus insulin‐deficient phenotypes compared with absolute muscle mass. These muscle‐related phenotypes persisted after exclusion of age, arguing against age‐driven circularity.

Inclusion of routine haematological indices further strengthened biological coherence. Clusters with reduced skeletal muscle exhibited lower haemoglobin and altered red cell indices, consistent with impaired oxygen‐carrying capacity and chronic metabolic stress. Sarcopenia and reduced muscle strength are increasingly recognised as risk factors for incident T2D, likely because skeletal muscle is a major site of insulin‐mediated glucose disposal [10, 11]. A plausible interpretation is that the MA phenotype reflects a shared muscle–haematological stress axis rather than unrelated clustering variables: lower RBC/HGB may reduce oxygen‐delivery reserve and impair mitochondrial oxidative metabolism, exercise tolerance, muscle protein turnover and muscle strength, while higher RDW‐CV may mark inflammation, oxidative stress, nutritional deficiency, renal/erythropoietic stress, or altered red‐cell turnover [12, 39, 40, 41, 42, 43]. This interpretation is supported by the internal correlation structure of the primary panel: haemoglobin correlated positively with skeletal muscle percentage (r = 0.58) and inversely with RDW‐CV (r = −0.44) (Figure S7).

Aerobic and resistance exercise may therefore be a useful testable intervention because exercise improves glycemic control and muscle function in T2D and may improve oxygen‐carrying capacity in selected settings [44]. More speculatively, future studies could examine whether the MA phenotype is also accompanied by impaired gastrointestinal smooth‐muscle function, gut‐barrier disruption, endotoxemia, albumin depletion, or inflammatory cytokine activation; however, direct evidence for equivalent involvement of skeletal and intestinal smooth muscle remains limited [45]. These mechanistic interpretations require longitudinal validation with direct biomarkers, body‐composition imaging and outcome data.

Comparison across 5‐, 9‐ and 13‐variable models demonstrated that enriched phenotyping refined core clusters without destabilising architecture. Although silhouette values were modest, as expected in a small cross‐sectional clinical dataset with overlapping biological continua, the ARI, transition matrices and alternative‐method analyses all indicated preservation of the dominant structure. Minimal‐variable models captured broad metabolic categories; intermediate models incorporating visceral adiposity and muscle improved discrimination of insulin‐resistant phenotypes; further enrichment enhanced mechanistic interpretability without excessive fragmentation.

A central methodological question in diabetes subtyping is whether increasing the number of variables improves or degrades clustering performance. The answer depends not on quantity per se, but on physiological independence, measurement reliability and conceptual relevance. Early clustering studies deliberately restricted analyses to 5–6 variables—age, BMI, HbA1c, HOMA2‐B, HOMA2‐IR and lipids—based on clinical availability, cross‐cohort harmonisation and minimisation of batch variability. Our pairwise correlation analysis showed expected within‐domain relationships but no single collinear block dominating the 13‐variable model, supporting the view that the observed clustering reflected structured physiological heterogeneity rather than simple variable duplication (Figure S7).

However, reliance on correlated or proxy variables introduces limitations. BMI conflates lean and fat mass; HOMA indices approximate dynamic insulin physiology imperfectly; age embeds disease duration, cohort effects and physiological decline. As a result, age‐related and obesity‐related clusters often overlap with hypoinsulinemia or hyperinsulinemia phenotypes, raising questions about whether these represent distinct biological entities or composite effects driven by variable choice.

Our study explicitly addresses these issues by applying PCA‐guided variable selection, adhering to three core principles: (1) physiological independence (minimising redundancy); (2) coverage of distinct metabolic axes (glycaemia, insulin action, adiposity, muscle, liver, haematology); and (3) biological interpretability, not merely statistical separation. Accordingly, CVAI was used to capture visceral adiposity beyond BMI alone, SM% to represent skeletal muscle relative to body size and AIP to summarise dyslipidaemic patterning while reducing redundancy from separate TG and HDL terms. Importantly, the three secretion‐related variables were not intended to overweight β‐cell function; rather, they captured complementary aspects of basal reserve (FCP), dynamic postprandial responsiveness (ΔCP) and secretion adjusted for insulin sensitivity (DI_120), which behaved differently across clusters and across reduced models.

Age‐related and obesity‐related T2D clusters frequently overlap with insulin‐deficient or insulin‐resistant phenotypes in prior reports [1, 2]. This overlap likely reflects variable entanglement rather than true biological identity. Age correlates with sarcopenia, anaemia and declining β‐cell reserve; BMI correlates imperfectly with visceral fat and muscle depletion. When clustering relies heavily on age and BMI, these variables act as surrogates for unmeasured physiology.

By incorporating muscle, visceral adiposity, hepatic and haematological measures—and by excluding age from primary clustering, we show that phenotypes traditionally labelled ‘age‐related’ or ‘obesity‐related’ decompose into mechanistically distinct subgroups defined by muscle depletion, lipotoxicity, or insulin compensation. This finding suggests that age and obesity may be descriptors, not drivers, of metabolic subtype.

Although many clustering studies exist, most follow‐up investigations have reused the same limited variable sets to evaluate prognosis or treatment response. This strategy risks reinforcing existing categories while overlooking subgroups driven by unmeasured physiology. Broad clustering does not equate to precise mechanistic classification at the individual level; cluster membership is inherently contingent on variable selection.

Our study demonstrates that deep phenotyping combined with principled dimension reduction can uncover clinically coherent subtypes that are invisible to routine‐variable frameworks, even in modestly sized cohorts. These refined subgroups may have therapeutic implications that merit explicit testing. For example, MA‐like patients may be candidates for strategies that preserve or restore muscle mass and nutritional status and avoid treatments that exacerbate weight or lean‐mass loss, whereas HL‐like patients may preferentially benefit from interventions targeting visceral adiposity, hepatic steatosis and dyslipidaemia, such as insulin‐sensitising, weight‐reducing, or liver‐directed therapies. At present, however, these implications remain speculative because outcome, treatment‐response and cost‐effectiveness data were not available.

The goal of precision diabetes classification is not merely to label populations, but to understand which physiological systems dominate disease in a given individual. Our findings suggest that future precision frameworks should integrate metabolic, anthropometric, hepatic and haematological dimensions rather than rely exclusively on glycaemia and BMI. A schematic summary of these standardised physiological axes is provided in Figure S9. In this sense, phenotypic depth may be especially important for mechanistic understanding and for generating treatment hypotheses, even before such clusters can be used for immediate clinical decision making.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference and does not allow assessment of longitudinal outcomes, treatment response, complication risk, or cost‐effectiveness across clusters. Second, skeletal muscle and fat mass were estimated using validated anthropometric prediction equations rather than direct imaging modalities such as DXA or MRI. However, sensitivity analyses using alternative muscle normalisation strategies yielded highly concordant clustering results, supporting the robustness of the observed patterns. Third, the study was conducted at a single centre in a Chinese cohort with a modest sample size and without an external validation cohort. This limits generalisability and means the observed clusters should be viewed as hypothesis‐generating rather than definitive population‐level subtypes. At the same time, the Chinese‐only setting is also relevant because visceral adiposity at comparatively modest BMI may be especially informative in East Asian populations. Finally, autoimmune diabetes was not included because of limited sample size; however, the focus on clinically diagnosed type 2 diabetes and the absence of extreme insulin deficiency at diagnosis reduce the likelihood of substantial misclassification.

Conclusions

In conclusion, comparison with earlier large‐scale clustering studies suggests that enriched phenotyping incorporating visceral adiposity, skeletal muscle, hepatic and haematological dimensions can improve phenotypic resolution of newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes beyond BMI‐centred models while preserving the broad cluster architecture described in prior work. These observed patterns should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating, complementary to large‐scale studies and not yet suitable for clinical decision‐making. Validation in larger and more diverse cohorts, with longitudinal outcomes and treatment response, is now needed before clinical application.
