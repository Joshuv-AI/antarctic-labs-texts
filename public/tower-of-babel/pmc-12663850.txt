Redox-sensitive m6A regulation in atherosclerosis: METTL3 at the crossroads of mitochondrial quality control and pyroptosis
Authors: Qiaomin Wu, Yongyuan Cai, Zhaoqi Yan, Yanli Wang, Jinfeng Liu, Xuanke Guan, Siyuan Zhou, Zhiming Liu, Xing Chang, Ruxiu Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractN6-methyladenosine (m6A) RNA methylation, primarily mediated by methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3), has emerged as a redox-sensitive epitranscriptomic switch in atherosclerosis (AS). Oxidative stress (OS) remodels the vascular transcriptome via m6A reprogramming, driving endothelial dysfunction, vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) phenotypic transition, and macrophage inflammatory activation. Reactive oxygen species directly influence METTL3 expression and activity, while METTL3 reciprocally modulates oxidative stress by regulating antioxidant defenses, ROS-scavenging enzymes, and redox homeostasis. Mechanistically, METTL3 promotes mitochondrial fission by enhancing Drp1 translation and suppresses mitochondrial biogenesis via destabilizing PGC-1α transcripts, converging to impair redox resilience and amplify inflammation. Inhibition of METTL3 alleviates intimal hyperplasia, restores contractile phenotypes, and may synergize with antioxidant therapy, mitochondrial quality control reinforcement, or pyroptosis suppression. Beyond METTL3, METTL4-mediated mitochondrial DNA 6 mA methylation also contributes to AS, with selective antagonists providing vascular protection. Natural products such as tanshinone and quercetin modulate m6A pathways, offering molecularly defined yet culturally rooted interventions. Deciphering the bidirectional crosstalk between ROS and epitranscriptomic regulation will guide precision therapeutics that bridge oxidative biology, RNA regulation, and translational cardiovascular medicine.Keywords: METTL3, m6A RNA methylation, Atherosclerosis, Reactive oxygen species, Pyroptosis, Mitochondrial quality control

Introduction

Atherosclerosis (AS), characterized by lipid accumulation, immune cell infiltration, and vascular remodeling, is a chronic inflammatory disease that underlies major cardiovascular events such as myocardial infarction and stroke. It remains one of the leading causes of mortality and disability worldwide. Extensive research has established oxidative stress—defined as an imbalance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and antioxidant defenses—as a key driver in the development and progression of AS. Oxidative stress contributes directly to endothelial dysfunction, inflammatory activation, and plaque instability [1]. Beyond causing molecular damage to proteins, DNA, and lipids, ROS also function as signaling molecules that influence cellular fate at the levels of transcription, translation, and post-translational modification.

Among various stress-responsive regulatory mechanisms, epitranscriptomic modifications—particularly N6-methyladenosine (m6A) methylation of mRNA—have gained considerable attention. This reversible RNA modification plays pivotal roles under various pathological stress conditions. In cardiovascular disease, METTL3, a core m6A methyltransferase, has been implicated in regulating vascular inflammation, immune cell activation, and phenotypic switching of endothelial and smooth muscle cells [2]. However, its upstream regulation and downstream effectors under oxidative stress remain incompletely defined. Recent evidence indicates that oxidative stress modulates METTL3 expression and enzymatic activity via multiple redox-sensitive pathways [3]. Conversely, METTL3 affects the expression of antioxidant enzymes, inflammasome components, and mitochondrial function-related proteins [4]. This reciprocal regulation suggests a potential feedback loop between oxidative stress and m6A-dependent post-transcriptional control, which may play a critical role in vascular pathophysiology.

Notably, two redox-sensitive mechanisms—pyroptosis and mitochondrial quality control (MQC)—have recently emerged as key processes contributing to vascular dysfunction under oxidative stress [5]. Pyroptosis, a form of programmed inflammatory cell death mediated by the NLRP3 inflammasome and gasdermin D (GSDMD), is closely associated with endothelial injury and plaque progression. MQC, a complex system that maintains mitochondrial homeostasis—including mitophagy, fusion/fission dynamics, mitochondrial biogenesis, and mitochondrial-derived vesicles (MDVs)—plays a decisive role in determining cell fate under stress.

Taken together, these findings raise a fundamental scientific question: How does METTL3 integrate redox signaling to modulate downstream pyroptosis and mitochondrial quality control processes, thereby influencing endothelial homeostasis and the progression of atherosclerosis? To address this question, this review will focus on three major themes (Fig. 1): (1) The regulation of METTL3 by oxidative stress in AS-related models; (2) The role of METTL3-mediated m6A modification in the control of pyroptosis and MQC pathways; (3) The therapeutic potential and challenges of targeting the "oxidative stress–METTL3–pyroptosis/MQC axis" in the treatment of atherosclerosis.

The Process of Atherosclerosis Development. The development of atherosclerosis involves multiple pathological processes, including pyroptosis, mitochondrial quality control, and oxidative stress.

METTL3: A central epigenetic regulator in atherosclerosis

m6A is the most prevalent internal modification of eukaryotic mRNA. As a rapid, reversible, and stress-sensitive mechanism of gene regulation, m6A has garnered considerable attention in cardiovascular research in recent years. Within the m6A “writer” complex, METTL3 functions as the core catalytic subunit, forming a complex with METTL14 and WTAP to mediate methylation deposition [6]. Through recognition by “reader” proteins such as YTHDF1/2/3, m6A-modified transcripts undergo dynamic regulation of stability, translational efficiency, and decay, thereby precisely influencing cellular function [7]. Increasing evidence indicates that METTL3 plays a critical role in the pathogenesis and progression of AS, exhibiting context- and cell type-specific functions during different stages of the disease. METTL3 in endothelial cells

Endothelial dysfunction represents an early and pivotal event in the initiation of atherosclerosis. Various pathological stimuli—such as oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL), hyperglycemia, hyperlipidemia, and oscillatory shear stress—have been shown to significantly upregulate METTL3 expression in vascular endothelial cells [8,24,25]. This upregulation is not incidental but reflects a fundamental molecular adaptation of endothelial cells to injurious signals. Upon induction, METTL3 acts as a key m6A methyltransferase, targeting transcripts involved in inflammatory and apoptotic signaling. By enhancing m6A modification on these mRNAs, METTL3 stabilizes their expression and promotes translation, thereby shifting endothelial cells toward a pro-inflammatory and pro-death phenotype, exacerbating vascular injury.

Mechanistically, in human aortic endothelial cells stimulated with oxLDL, METTL3 is activated and increases m6A methylation on NLRP3 (NOD-like receptor family, pyrin domain containing 3) mRNA, enhancing its stability and preventing degradation [25]. Sustained NLRP3 expression leads to inflammasome assembly and caspase-1 activation, which promotes the maturation and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-18. Activated caspase-1 also cleaves GSDMD, triggering pyroptosis, thereby amplifying the inflammatory microenvironment in a feed-forward manner [26,27]. In addition, METTL3 mediates endothelial responses to atheroprone flow by upregulating NLRP1, downregulating KLF4, and activating the NF-κB signaling cascade [8].

Beyond inflammation, METTL3 has also been implicated in the regulation of mitochondrial quality control. It represses the expression of key mitophagy-related genes, including FUNDC1 and PINK1 [9,10]. FUNDC1 is a hypoxia-responsive receptor mediating receptor-dependent mitophagy, whereas PINK1/Parkin represents the canonical pathway for eliminating damaged mitochondria. Suppression of these genes impairs the clearance of dysfunctional mitochondria in endothelial cells [10,28], resulting in mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS accumulation. Excessive ROS not only damages cellular components (e.g., lipids, proteins, DNA) but also serves as a potent amplifier of injury signaling. ROS can further activate the NLRP3 inflammasome [29,30] and may feed back to enhance METTL3 expression or activity. This creates a deleterious loop, thereby perpetuating endothelial dysfunction.

These mechanistic insights are strongly supported by in vivo studies. METTL3 knockout reduces neointima formation in a partial carotid ligation model in mice [8]. Under oscillatory shear stress, METTL3 expression is increased, which promotes m6A modification of metabolic regulators HK1, PFKFB3, and GCKR. This results in enhanced glycolysis, elevated endothelial dysfunction, and suppressed GCKR expression [31]. In ApoE−/− mice with endothelial-specific deletion of METTL3, mitochondrial integrity is preserved, mitochondrial membrane potential is restored, and oxidative and ER stress markers (CHOP, PERK, and ATF5) are attenuated [11]. Concurrently, ROS production is significantly reduced, leading to the preservation of microvascular endothelial function.

Taken together, METTL3 serves as a pivotal regulatory hub in endothelial cells during the early stages of atherosclerosis. It is upregulated in response to pathological stimuli and orchestrates m6A-dependent modulation of key cellular processes—including NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis and mitochondrial homeostasis. These interconnected mechanisms converge to drive endothelial dysfunction and promote disease progression.

METTL3 in macrophages

Macrophages are central regulators in the development and progression of AS. Their dysfunction manifests primarily through excessive lipid uptake, sustained pro-inflammatory cytokine release, and foam cell formation—the latter being a predominant component of the atherosclerotic lipid core. Studies have demonstrated that in response to atherosclerosis-relevant stimuli such as oxLDL and lipopolysaccharide (LPS), the expression of METTL3 is significantly upregulated in macrophages [32,33]. As a pivotal m6A methyltransferase, METTL3 dynamically regulates the stability and translational efficiency of mRNAs associated with inflammation and lipid homeostasis, thus contributing to immune dysregulation and metabolic imbalance in macrophages.

Once activated, METTL3 enhances m6A methylation on transcripts encoding key inflammatory mediators, including interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and NLRP3 inflammasome [32,34]. These modifications stabilize the mRNAs and promote their translation, sustaining a pro-inflammatory state. NLRP3 inflammasome activation leads to caspase-1 cleavage of GSDMD, initiating pyroptosis in macrophages and releasing large amounts of inflammatory contents (e.g., IL-1β, IL-18), further amplifying the inflammatory cascade. Moreover, METTL3-mediated m6A enrichment on SLC7A11 accelerates its mRNA degradation via YTHDF2, promoting ferroptosis in macrophages [35]. In addition, METTL3 enhances the YTHDF1-dependent translation of inflammatory mediators by activating the RAS/RAF/MEK/ERK signaling pathway, further augmenting IL-6 and TNF-α expression and contributing to plaque development [12]. In vivo evidence supports this mechanism: myeloid-specific METTL3 deletion in atherosclerotic models reduces BRAF expression and ERK phosphorylation, thereby decreasing pro-inflammatory cytokine levels and slowing lesion progression [12].

Beyond inflammation, METTL3 also modulates lipid metabolism in macrophages. METTL3 has been shown to increase HDGF expression via m6A modification in M1-polarized macrophages. HDGF upregulation promotes glycolysis, inflammation, and lipid accumulation, collectively exacerbating atherosclerotic plaque progression [13]. While FTO, another m6A-related enzyme, has been extensively studied in the context of adipogenesis and obesity [36], METTL3, together with METTL14 and WTAP, facilitates mitotic clonal expansion (MCE) during adipocyte differentiation by regulating cell cycle transitions [37]. Furthermore, inhibition of METTL3 has been shown to restore autophagy via AKT1S1 mRNA stabilization, leading to reduced lipid accumulation [14]. Similar regulatory patterns have been observed in nonvascular disease models. For example, high-fat diet exposure enhances METTL3-mediated m6A modification of CPT1A, promoting M2 macrophage polarization and immune evasion in hepatocellular carcinoma [15]. In addition, METTL3 modulates the expression of CD36—a key scavenger receptor for modified lipoproteins—thereby contributing to mediate foam cell formation and insulin resistance in polycystic ovary syndrome [16,17]. These findings suggest that METTL3 not only participates in inflammation but also plays an important role in shaping macrophage lipid fate and energy metabolism under pathological stimuli.

Recent studies have also linked METTL3 to mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress in macrophages. METTL3, in concert with YTHDF2, represses the expression of PGC-1α, cytochrome c (CYCS), and NDUFC2 (NADH: Ubiquinone Oxidoreductase Subunit C2), leading to decreased ATP production and oxygen consumption rate (OCR) [18]. These defects increase both cytosolic and mitochondrial ROS, further elevating pro-inflammatory cytokine levels. Impaired PGC-1α signaling disrupts mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle function, and fatty acid oxidation, all of which exacerbate cellular stress [38,39]. Moreover, the accumulation of damaged mitochondria that are not properly cleared through mitophagy results in excessive ROS leakage—particularly superoxide (O2−)—from the electron transport chain (ETC). ROS act as direct inducers of DNA, protein, and lipid damage, and importantly, promote NLRP3 inflammasome activation and GSDMD cleavage, thus further facilitating pyroptosis [40]. This underscores a vicious cycle wherein METTL3-induced mitochondrial dysfunction aggravates oxidative stress and cell death, reinforcing inflammation in atherosclerotic plaques.

METTL3 in vascular smooth muscle cells

Under physiological conditions, vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) in the medial layer of blood vessels exhibit a contractile phenotype characterized by the expression of contractile proteins such as smooth muscle α-actin (SM α-actin) and SM22α, which are essential for maintaining vascular tone. Upon exposure to pathological stimuli—including ox-LDL, hyperglycemia, and pro-inflammatory cytokines—VSMCs undergo a phenotypic switch. This transformation involves downregulation of contractile proteins and acquisition of proliferative, migratory, and synthetic capabilities [41]. This phenotypic transition exerts dual effects on AS progression: in the early stages, VSMC migration into the intima and excessive proliferation accelerate plaque development; in later stages, collagen synthesis by synthetic VSMCs contributes to fibrous cap stability, although aberrant differentiation may promote plaque calcification and necrotic core expansion.

ROS directly drives this phenotypic modulation by activating pro-inflammatory pathways and inducing DNA damage. ROS also stimulates the secretion of monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1) by VSMCs, enhancing leukocyte recruitment and local inflammation [42]. Notably, aging further exacerbates this vulnerability. In aged individuals, VSMCs exhibit heightened sensitivity to oxidative insults, with significantly increased MCP-1 expression, compromised Nrf2-mediated antioxidant defense, and exaggerated responses to hydrogen peroxide [43,44]. METTL3 plays a pivotal role in these processes by regulating m6A RNA methylation in VSMCs under stress conditions. Suppression of METTL3 has been shown to attenuate neointimal hyperplasia, restore contractile phenotype markers such as α-SMA and SM22α, and concurrently reduce synthetic phenotype indicators including OPN and PCNA [20]. Several m6A-modified downstream effectors of METTL3 have been implicated in VSMC phenotypic regulation.

(1)PFN1/p-ANXA2/STAT3 axis: METTL3, in conjunction with YTHDF3, enhances translation of PFN1 in an m6A-dependent manner. PFN1 recruits Src kinase to promote STAT3 phosphorylation, which interacts with phosphorylated ANXA2—an essential step for STAT3 activation and subsequent VSMC phenotypic switching [21].

Oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction converge as central drivers of VSMC malfunction, with METTL3 acting as a critical integrator. Overexpression of METTL3 has been shown to impair mitophagy via reduced m6A methylation of genes in the PINK1/Parkin pathway, resulting in mitochondrial accumulation and dysfunction [10]. This leads to compromised ETC function, increased ROS leakage (notably superoxide anions, O2•-), and reduced mitochondrial antioxidant defense (elevated MDA, decreased SOD) in ox-LDL-stimulated VSMCs [45]. Importantly, METTL3 also regulates ferroptosis-related antioxidant pathways. METTL3 facilitates m6A methylation of the long noncoding RNA NORAD in a YTHDF2-dependent manner, enhancing its interaction with RNA-binding protein HuR [46]. The NORAD-HuR complex stabilizes GPX4 mRNA, upregulating GPX4 protein levels [46]. As a core ferroptosis suppressor, GPX4 reduces lipid ROS and iron accumulation while maintaining GSH levels, thus protecting VSMCs from oxidative injury and slowing aortic dissection progression [46].

Oxidative stress and redox regulation of METTL3 in atherosclerosis

Oxidative stress is a central driver of atherosclerosis, primarily through the overproduction of ROS, which causes oxidative damage and activates a broad spectrum of stress-responsive signaling pathways (e.g., MAPK, NF-κB, Nrf2) that reshape cellular transcriptional programs [1]. Beyond its role as an upstream inducer of the m6A regulatory network, oxidative stress can directly modulate both the expression and enzymatic activity of METTL3. This modulation influences the epitranscriptomic landscape of RNA, while METTL3-mediated m6A modifications, in turn, feed back to regulate redox homeostasis—together forming a dynamic, bidirectional regulatory loop [19].

How oxidative stress regulates the expression and activity of METTL3

Under conditions that elevate ROS—such as exposure to oxLDL, H2O2, high glucose, or high lipid levels—METTL3 mRNA abundance is markedly increased. This upregulation appears to occur via multiple, non-mutually exclusive mechanisms.

Transcriptional regulation

Oxidative stress has been shown to enhance the interaction between PCAF and METTL3, increasing METTL3 acetylation. This modification disrupts the association between METTL3 and EIF3H, thereby impairing ribosomal translational efficiency and suppressing cell proliferation [3]. In an age-related hearing loss model, oxidative stress reduced the m6A methylation of SIRT1 mRNA, which was accompanied by a corresponding decrease in METTL3 expression [3]. In pancreatic β-cells, METTL3 expression is influenced by both ER stress and ROS levels, suggesting that oxidative stress may modulate METTL3 to alter immune-related functions [3].

Translational control and protein stability

Oxidative stress may also modulate METTL3 protein abundance and activity through post-translational mechanisms:

Ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation: E3 ubiquitin ligases play a pivotal role in protein ubiquitination and turnover. Members of the NEDD4 family (e.g., ITCH, WWP1, Smurf1, Smurf2) have been implicated in regulating LDL receptor expression and cholesterol metabolism, thereby influencing atherosclerotic progression. Pharmacological inhibition or activation of these ligases can ameliorate cardiovascular dysfunction [47]. TRIM21, an E3 ubiquitin ligase, interacts directly with METTL3 to promote K48-linked polyubiquitination and proteasomal degradation [48]. TRIM21 overexpression has been shown to suppress LPS-induced neutrophil infiltration, cytokine release, and NF-κB pathway activation [49]. Similarly, RNF113A interacts with circ_0001187 to facilitate METTL3 degradation [50]. Excessive ROS may perturb the activity of E3 ligases, altering substrate recognition and ubiquitination efficiency [51]. Collectively, these findings suggest that oxidative stress–induced ROS can indirectly regulate METTL3 expression by modulating E3 ligase activity.

Post-translational modifications: METTL3 undergoes multiple covalent modifications, including phosphorylation, acetylation, and SUMOylation. For example, extracellular signal-regulated kinases (ERKs) phosphorylate METTL3 at three conserved serine residues (S43, S50, S525), reducing its ubiquitination and degradation, thereby enhancing its stability and enzymatic activity [52]. METTL3 can also be SUMOylated at lysine residues K177, K211, K212, and K215, which significantly suppresses its methyltransferase activity without affecting stability, localization, or interactions with METTL14 and WTAP [53]. ROS have been reported to influence acetylation [54], phosphorylation, and ubiquitination [55]. Although direct evidence for ROS-mediated regulation of METTL3 modifications remains lacking, indirect findings strongly support this possibility.

Epigenetic and RNA-based regulation

Oxidative stress can induce specific microRNAs or lncRNAs that interact with METTL3 mRNA or protein, thereby modulating its expression. For instance, miR-33a targets the 3′UTR of METTL3 to suppress its expression in non-small cell lung cancer, attenuating cell proliferation and invasion [56]. The lncRNA MALAT1 interacts with METTL3 to modulate site-specific m6A deposition [56]. In gastric cancer, miR-1269b, miR-4429, and miR-181–5p downregulate METTL3, thereby inhibiting malignant behaviors (He, Wu, Sun, & [[57], [58], [59]]. Additionally, circRPMS1 forms a complex with Sam68 and p53 that accumulates at the METTL3 promoter to promote its transcription, influencing EBV-associated gastric carcinoma aggressiveness [60,61]. These findings underscore that METTL3 expression is also subject to fine-tuned epigenetic and RNA-based control.

How METTL3 modulates oxidative stress responses

As an m6A “writer,” METTL3 orchestrates redox homeostasis by methylating transcripts of oxidative stress–related genes, thereby influencing their stability and translation efficiency. Through these epitranscriptomic modifications, METTL3 can fine-tune intracellular ROS levels both by modulating ROS production and by affecting antioxidant capacity. One well-characterized mechanism involves the regulation of antioxidant signaling via non-coding RNA processing. METTL3 promotes miR-873–5p maturation through DROSHA and DGCR8, which in turn upregulates Nrf2, leading to enhanced expression of downstream antioxidant genes and suppression of ROS accumulation [62]. In endothelial cells, OS upregulates METTL3 expression, increases global m6A methylation, augments NF-κB p65 Ser536 phosphorylation, and promotes monocyte adhesion [8]. Knockdown of METTL3 abolishes OS-induced hypermethylation and inflammatory activation, whereas METTL3 overexpression phenocopies the OS effects [8].

Beyond antioxidant pathways, METTL3 has been shown to repress mitochondrial oxidative metabolism. Coordinated with YTHDF2, METTL3 downregulates PGC-1α, CYCS, and NDUFC2, thereby reducing ATP production and OCR. This metabolic suppression leads to intracellular and mitochondrial ROS accumulation, accompanied by increased pro-inflammatory cytokine release in activated monocytes [63]. In contrast, in macrophages challenged with LPS, METTL3 knockdown markedly enhances IL-6, TNF-α, and NADPH oxidase (Nox1, Nox2, Ncf1, Ncf2) expression, ROS generation, and phosphorylation of MAPKs and AKT [64]. This apparent context-dependent discrepancy suggests that METTL3 may target distinct gene sets in different cell types or activation states, thereby exerting either pro-oxidant or antioxidant effects.

METTL3 also modulates ROS levels by regulating antioxidant enzyme expression. In several models, METTL3 targets mRNAs encoding key antioxidant enzymes such as SOD2, GPX4, and catalase, promoting their degradation or suppressing their translation, thereby diminishing antioxidant defense capacity. Conversely, METTL3-mediated m6A methylation of Hmox1 mRNA in an IGF2BP3-dependent manner can stabilize the transcript, leading to tubular epithelial cell iron overload and ferroptotic death [65]. In another axis, METTL3 enhances pri-miR-34a maturation via DGCR8, and miR-34a overexpression markedly suppresses SIRT1 expression, further weakening cellular antioxidant defense [66].

Collectively, oxidative stress modulates METTL3 expression and activity through multiple signaling pathways, while METTL3 reciprocally shapes oxidative stress intensity and persistence via m6A-dependent gene regulation. This bidirectional network not only governs the functional state of endothelial cells, macrophages, and other atherosclerosis-associated cell types but also likely intersects with downstream processes such as pyroptosis and mitochondrial quality control, influencing cell fate decisions in the atherosclerotic microenvironment.

METTL3-mediated m6A modification in the regulation of pyroptosis

During the progression of atherosclerosis, pyroptosis occurs extensively in vascular endothelial cells, macrophages, and vascular smooth muscle cells. This process promotes local inflammatory amplification, membrane rupture, and atherosclerotic plaque destabilization. Pyroptosis is initiated by inflammasome activation and depends on caspase-1–mediated cleavage of GSDMD, which forms membrane pores and enables the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β. Recent studies have revealed that m6A RNA modification regulates the fate of key pyroptotic transcripts, with METTL3 serving as a central catalytic component. Clinical evidence supports this notion: in macrophages derived from peripheral blood mononuclear cells of patients with acute coronary syndrome, circ_0029589 expression is reduced. IRF-1 represses circ_0029589 via METTL3-dependent m6A modification, thereby facilitating macrophage pyroptosis and inflammatory cytokine release [67].

NLRP3 has emerged as a critical m6A-regulated target. In oxLDL-stimulated HUVECs, METTL3 expression is markedly upregulated and deposits m6A marks on target transcripts, increasing the stability and translation efficiency of pyroptosis-related mRNAs, including NLRP3, cleaved caspase-1, IL-1β, IL-18, and GSDMD-N [68]. This modification promotes inflammasome assembly and activation. METTL3 cooperates with the m6A “reader” YTHDF1 to enhance NLRP3 protein translation [69], thereby augmenting GSDMD cleavage and driving pyroptotic execution [68]. YTHDF2 also contributes by regulating the stability of IL-1β and IL-18 transcripts [68], positioning it as another key METTL3 cofactor in inflammatory amplification. Furthermore, as discussed earlier, METTL3 responds to pro-atherogenic hemodynamic forces in endothelial cells by promoting pro-inflammatory signaling—manifested as NLRP1 upregulation, KLF4 suppression, and NF-κB pathway activation (Chien et al., 2021)—all of which may create a cellular environment primed for pyroptotic initiation. This suggests that METTL3 not only modulates inflammasome components directly via m6A but also shapes the upstream inflammatory milieu that sensitizes cells to pyroptotic triggers.

METTL3 and the regulation of mitochondrial quality control

The MQC system is a central mechanism for maintaining cellular homeostasis by eliminating dysfunctional mitochondria, sustaining energy metabolism, and limiting ROS release. MQC encompasses mitophagy, mitochondrial fusion and fission, mitochondrial biogenesis, and the generation of MDVs. Oxidative stress is a primary activating signal for MQC. Recent evidence indicates that m6A RNA modification acts as a novel upstream regulatory layer in MQC, with METTL3 emerging as a pivotal modulator (Fig. 2).

Mitochondria-driven crosstalk between oxidative stress and pyroptosis underlies atherosclerotic progression. Excessive ROS, triggered by pro-atherogenic stimuli (e.g., oxLDL, high glucose/lipid levels, LPS, ischemia), drive mitochondrial quality control imbalance, characterized by impaired mitophagy, enhanced mitochondrial fission, and disrupted energy metabolism. These events lead to mitochondrial fragmentation and reduced redox resilience. In parallel, the accumulation of mitochondrial ROS and damaged organelles amplifies inflammasome activation, pro-inflammatory cytokine release, and gasdermin D–mediated plasma membrane pore formation, culminating in pyroptotic cell death. The vicious cycle between oxidative damage and inflammatory cell death contributes to vascular endothelial dysfunction, foam cell formation, and plaque destabilization in AS. Therapeutic targeting of these pathways—by restoring MQC and reducing ROS-driven pyroptosis—may offer promising strategies to mitigate atherosclerotic burden.

METTL3 and the mitophagy pathway

Mitophagy is a protective process that selectively removes damaged mitochondria, thereby reducing ROS production and preventing cell death. Multiple studies have shown that METTL3 modulates the expression of key mitophagy regulators via m∧6A modification, thereby controlling autophagy flux (Pu et al., 2025). Two canonical mitophagy pathways have been the primary focus.

(i)The FUNDC1 pathway:

The FUNDC1 pathway:

FUNDC1 is a hypoxia-inducible receptor located on the outer mitochondrial membrane that binds to the autophagy marker LC3, mediating receptor-dependent mitophagy [70]. Under hypoxic conditions, FUNDC1 interacts with the ER membrane protein calnexin, recruiting the mitochondrial fission protein DRP1 to mitochondria-associated ER membranes (MAMs). This facilitates mitochondrial fission and autophagosome formation, ultimately leading to mitochondrial degradation [71]. Moreover, as an anchoring protein at MAMs, FUNDC1 regulates ER-mitochondrial Ca2+ signaling and mitochondrial dynamics. In vascular endothelial cells, FUNDC1 modulates cytosol–mitochondria Ca2+ flux, influencing the phosphorylation status of serum response factor and enhancing its binding to the VEGFR2 promoter, thereby activating angiogenesis-related gene expression [72]. In cardiomyocytes, FUNDC1 knockdown improves mitochondrial function, lowers mitochondrial Ca2+ concentration, reduces pyroptosis, and promotes ubiquitination-dependent degradation of IP3R2 [9]. Silencing METTL3 suppresses FUNDC1 mRNA stability via m∧6A reader IGF2BP2, attenuating cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammatory responses, and pyroptosis [9]. The stability of FUNDC1 transcripts has also been confirmed to be directly regulated by METTL3-mediated m∧6A modification [73]. This evidence collectively suggests that METTL3–FUNDC1 signaling integrates mitochondrial clearance, Ca2+ homeostasis, and inflammation control, thereby linking MQC to vascular and cardiac pathophysiology.

(ii)The PINK1/Parkin pathway:

The PINK1/Parkin pathway:

The PINK1/Parkin axis is the most extensively studied mitophagy pathway, activated primarily in response to loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) and mitochondrial injury. In the context of AS, this pathway not only mediates the clearance of damaged mitochondria but also influences macrophage polarization, endothelial cell function, and VSMC migration and proliferation—key processes affecting plaque formation and stability [[74], [75], [76]]. oxLDL stimulation induces ΔΨm depolarization, increases mtROS production, and upregulates Drp1 phosphorylation, thereby promoting mitochondrial fission and damage. Subsequently, PINK1 accumulates on the outer mitochondrial membrane, recruiting Parkin from the cytosol to mitochondria, and enhancing MAP-LC3 colocalization with mitochondria, thus activating mitophagy flux [77]. This is accompanied by reduced expression of mitochondrial membrane proteins such as TOMM40, TOMM20, and COX IV-1, and by suppression of the pro-apoptotic protein Bax, establishing a crosstalk between autophagy and apoptosis that lowers VSMC death and promotes plaque stability [77]. Given that m∧6A modifications have been implicated in the regulation of mitochondrial fission and autophagy-related transcripts, it is plausible that METTL3 also modulates PINK1/Parkin-driven mitophagy in vascular cells, although direct mechanistic evidence in AS models remains to be established.

Although the direct role of METTL3 in regulating the PINK1/Parkin pathway in AS remains to be fully elucidated, studies from other disease models offer valuable mechanistic insights. For instance, in lung cancer, METTL3 promotes m6A modification of DCP2 mRNA, leading to its degradation, which indirectly upregulates PINK1 expression. This activation of the PINK1/Parkin pathway enhances mitophagy and contributes to chemoresistance [78]. Conversely, in a renal injury model, silencing METTL3 in human proximal tubular epithelial cells reduces the m6A modification level of PINK1 transcripts, thereby increasing their stability in a manner dependent on the m6A reader protein YTHDF2 [10]. Taken together, these findings suggest that METTL3 may regulate the PINK1/Parkin axis by modulating the m∧6A status of PINK1 mRNA and its recognition by specific reader proteins. Such regulation could integrate mitochondrial homeostasis with cellular stress responses, potentially influencing vascular pathology; however, its precise contribution to AS pathogenesis warrants further investigation.

METTL3 in the regulation of mitochondrial dynamics and biogenesis

Mitochondrial dynamics, encompassing fusion and fission events, are essential determinants of organelle functional status and prerequisites for selective mitophagy. Although direct evidence linking METTL3 to these processes remains limited, emerging studies suggest that METTL3 upregulation promotes excessive mitochondrial fission, thereby driving cardiac fibroblast proliferation and migration, ultimately contributing to cardiac fibrosis [79]. In contrast, inhibition of METTL3 has been shown to reduce the translation efficiency of Drp1 mRNA via 5′UTR m∧6A modification, leading to decreased Drp1 protein levels and attenuated mitochondrial fragmentation following hypoxia–ischemia injury [80].

In addition to its role in fission, METTL3 may negatively regulate mitochondrial biogenesis through m6A-dependent destabilization of PPARGC1A (PGC-1α) transcripts. Coordinated action of METTL3 and the m6A reader YTHDF2 facilitates degradation of PGC-1α mRNA in monocytes, leading to reduced PGC-1α protein abundance and an amplified inflammatory phenotype [18]. This suppression of PGC-1α-driven mitochondrial biogenesis likely diminishes cellular antioxidant capacity, thereby creating a feed-forward loop in which metabolic stress and inflammatory signaling mutually reinforce each other.

How dysregulation of METTL3–MQC axis accelerates the progression of atherosclerosis

ROS overproduction is a central pathological driver in AS, linking oxidative stress to vascular inflammation and endothelial dysfunction. LPS has been shown to trigger NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis via enhanced ROS generation, thereby damaging endothelial cells [81]. Mitochondria-derived ROS is a particularly potent contributor to endothelial injury. For instance, deletion of nicotinamide nucleotide transhydrogenase markedly increases mitochondrial ROS levels, elevates glutathione peroxidase activity, but reduces glutathione reductase activity, ultimately impairing antioxidant defense [82]. Inhibition of the ROS/NLRP3/caspase-1 axis can effectively suppress LPS-induced pyroptosis in endothelial cells [83]. Consistently, a macrophage-biomimetic drug delivery system using ROS-responsive nanoparticles has been shown to attenuate AS by selectively releasing atorvastatin in high-ROS microenvironments, reducing peri-lesional CD31+ neovessel formation and suppressing Ki67+ endothelial proliferation, thereby optimizing lipid profiles [84]. These findings underscore ROS as both a mediator and amplifier of vascular injury in AS.

Clinical evidence supports a strong association between METTL3 and vascular inflammation. In patients with coronary artery disease, METTL3 expression positively correlates with proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α [85]. Mechanistic studies reveal that METTL3 promotes macrophage inflammatory responses and AS progression by modulating Braf mRNA through m∧6A methylation [12]. At the redox level, METTL3 regulates ROS production by stabilizing Pyk2 mRNA, which activates MAPK and AKT signaling cascades, subsequently upregulating NADPH oxidases and driving proinflammatory cytokine release [64]. Conversely, METTL3 deletion reduces ROS levels, attenuating inflammation and vascular injury [68].

Accumulating evidence also implicates METTL3 in the regulation of mitochondrial function—a pivotal component of the MQC network. METTL3-mediated m6A methylation downregulates GPX4 and SLC7A11, aggravating mitochondrial oxidative stress and triggering ferroptosis [86]. Inhibition of METTL3 mitigates cardiac fibrosis and inflammation under I/R conditions by disrupting DNA-PKcs/Fis1-dependent mitochondrial fission [87]. Additionally, m6A modification in the 5′UTR of Drp1 mRNA reduces its translational efficiency, lowering Drp1 protein expression and limiting mitochondrial fragmentation [80]. METTL3 also regulates mitochondrial fission, autophagy, and M1 macrophage polarization through m6A-dependent modulation of lncRNAs such as GAS5 and MALAT1 [79,88]. In the context of I/R injury, METTL3 promotes EGR1 mRNA methylation and expression, which suppresses JAK2/STAT3 signaling, thereby impairing mitophagy, disturbing mitochondrial dynamics, and inducing pyroptosis [89]. Silencing METTL3 enhances autophagic flux and reduces apoptosis under hypoxia/reoxygenation, whereas its overexpression has the opposite effect, suggesting a context-dependent role as a negative regulator of mitophagy [90]. Notably, natural compounds such as leonurine have been reported to stabilize the METTL3–A1S10K complex, modulating autophagic activity, improving lipid metabolism, and attenuating AS progression [14].

Taken together, METTL3-mediated m6A modification acts as a nodal regulator connecting oxidative stress, inflammation, and metabolic imbalance in AS. Through its impact on mitochondrial ROS homeostasis, fission–fusion dynamics, mitophagy, and biogenesis, METTL3 dysregulation perturbs the MQC network, creating a self-perpetuating cycle of redox imbalance and vascular injury (Fig. 3).

ROS as a central driver linking mitochondrial dysfunction, pyroptosis, and Oxidative stress in atherosclerosis. ROS induces endothelial dysfunction and mitochondrial injury, while simultaneously activating the NLRP3 inflammasome, ultimately leading to pyroptotic cell death. Moreover, oxidative stress upregulates METTL3 expression and enzymatic activity, resulting in aberrant N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification across key RNA transcripts. Through these m6A marks, METTL3 modulates mitochondrial quality surveillance (MQS) by regulating diverse molecular targets involved in mitochondrial biogenesis, antioxidant defense, and dynamics. In parallel, excessive ROS promotes inflammasome assembly (↑ NLRP3, ↑ caspase-1), gasdermin D (GSDMD)-mediated membrane pore formation, and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (↑ IL-1β, ↑ IL-18), thereby exacerbating pyroptosis. Inflammatory signaling, in turn, exacerbates mitochondrial dysfunction, creating a vicious cycle of oxidative stress, mitochondrial damage, and cell death. These interconnected processes collectively intensify vascular oxidative stress, inflammation, and metabolic dysregulation, accelerating the progression of atherosclerotic plaque formation.

Translating mechanistic insight into therapeutic strategies

Therapeutic exploration of METTL3 modulation spans pharmacological agents, natural compounds, metabolic interventions, and RNA-targeted technologies. Collectively, these approaches aim to recalibrate the m6A epitranscriptomic landscape to restore vascular homeostasis (Fig. 4).

Modulators of inflammatory signaling

The mTOR pathway, a central hub in cell growth and metabolism, intersects with METTL3-dependent regulation in AS. Pharmacological activation of mTOR (e.g., MHY1485) can offset the inhibitory effects of METTL3 overexpression on VSMC autophagy, whereas mTOR inhibition (e.g., rapamycin) promotes autophagy to counteract VSMC hyperproliferation caused by METTL3 deficiency [91]. This duality suggests that fine-tuning mTOR activity may enhance the efficacy of METTL3-targeted interventions.

Targeted protein degradation

Selective METTL3 depletion via small-molecule inhibitors or PROTACs (e.g., KH12) effectively suppresses tumor proliferation and metastasis [92]. While these studies originate in oncology, they offer a technological blueprint for METTL3-directed degradation in AS, particularly in advanced lesions characterized by proliferative VSMC phenotypes.

Natural compounds

A growing body of evidence supports the efficacy of natural bioactive molecules in modulating METTL3-associated signaling cascades to attenuate inflammation and dysregulated autophagy. For instance, berberine suppresses METTL3 activity, reduces m6A modification of NLRP3 mRNA, and downregulates pyroptosis-associated factors [93]. Salvianolic acid exerts anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects by targeting multiple pathways, including AMPK/FoxO4/KLF2 and Syndecan-4/Rac1/ATF2 [94], while tanshinones modulate METTL3 to alleviate mitochondrial–endoplasmic reticulum stress responses and reduce oxidative injury [11]. Additionally, leonurine enhances METTL3-mediated stabilization of AKT1S1, activates autophagy, reduces lipid accumulation, and exerts anti-atherosclerotic effects [14]. These compounds illustrate the feasibility of dietary and phytochemical interventions in epitranscriptomic modulation.

Metabolic interventions

Metabolic modulators can indirectly influence METTL3 expression and function. Vitamin D3 downregulates METTL3 via AMPK inhibition, reducing m6A modification of mitochondrial calcium uniporter (MCU) mRNA, thereby suppressing VSMC apoptosis [95]. The SGLT2 inhibitor dapagliflozin reduces plaque instability by attenuating NLRP3, caspase-1, IL-1β, IL-18, and matrix metalloproteinase expression [96]. Empagliflozin (EMPA) decreases METTL3 protein levels, reversing oscillatory shear stress–induced glycolytic dysregulation and restoring endothelial metabolic homeostasis [31]. This metabolic–epigenetic crosstalk offers a new dimension to cardiovascular drug repurposing.

RNA-targeted therapeutics

Advances in RNA-based therapeutics have enabled precise modulation of METTL3 expression in vascular tissues. In vivo silencing of METTL3 in murine arterial walls significantly reduces plaque burden, inflammation, and pyroptotic marker expression, supporting its candidacy as a therapeutic target. The small-molecule METTL3 inhibitor STM2457, already validated in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), reduces global m6A levels and inhibits tumor proliferation [97]. Similarly, the METTL3 inhibitor Cpd-564 attenuates cisplatin- or ischemia/reperfusion-induced acute kidney injury by reducing TAB3 m6A modification and stability, thereby mitigating inflammation, apoptosis, and renal dysfunction [98]. Although no METTL3-targeted drug has yet entered clinical trials for AS, these preclinical successes provide a solid rationale for translational development.

Risks and challenges

Although the role of METTL3 in AS has been extensively investigated, its precise mechanistic functions under distinct physiological and pathological contexts remain incompletely defined. Evidence indicates that METTL3 exerts divergent regulatory effects in endothelial cells, vascular smooth muscle cells, and macrophages, suggesting a high degree of cell type–specificity. This heterogeneity underscores the necessity of delineating METTL3's context-dependent functions to avoid either excessive or insufficient therapeutic modulation, which could inadvertently exacerbate vascular injury.

A second major concern relates to the systemic inhibition of m6A methylation, which may disrupt transcriptomic homeostasis in non-target cell populations. As a pivotal regulator of RNA metabolism, m6A modification influences RNA stability, translational efficiency, and alternative splicing across diverse biological systems. Broad METTL3 inhibition could therefore provoke widespread transcriptomic alterations with unintended functional consequences. For example, interruption of METTL3–METTL14 complex activity has been shown to impair essential cellular processes, potentially precipitating detrimental outcomes. Given that AS patients often present with comorbidities such as metabolic syndrome or chronic inflammation, these off-target transcriptomic effects could interact with existing pathological states to amplify adverse effects. Consequently, the design of METTL3-targeted therapies must incorporate strategies to preserve m6A-dependent homeostatic functions in unaffected tissues.

Finally, there is currently a paucity of pharmacological agents capable of selectively modulating METTL3 activity at specific RNA loci. METTL3's catalytic activity as the core component of the m6A methyltransferase complex is governed by multiple factors, including protein–protein interactions and RNA sequence context. However, the development of inhibitors remains in its infancy, and many fundamental questions regarding METTL3–METTL14 complex structure–function relationships are unresolved. Innovative approaches, such as allosteric modulators or RNA-guided epitranscriptomic editing, may provide the means to achieve locus-specific regulation of METTL3 activity.

Conclusion and perspectives

In summary, although METTL3 has emerged as a promising therapeutic target in AS, key challenges remain, including the incomplete understanding of its cell type–specific mechanisms, the systemic consequences of m6A perturbation, and the absence of highly specific modulators. Given METTL3's established involvement in oxidative stress regulation, the construction of an AS-specific m6A epitranscriptomic atlas—particularly one mapping the reprogramming of post-transcriptional fates under altered ROS levels—represents an important direction for future mechanistic studies. Furthermore, combinatorial therapeutic strategies that integrate METTL3 modulation with antioxidant interventions, mitophagy promotion, and pyroptosis inhibition hold promise for enhancing efficacy while minimizing adverse effects.

For instance, the sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitor empagliflozin (EMPA) has been shown to markedly reduce METTL3 protein abundance, reverse oxidative stress–induced aberrant glycolytic gene expression, and restore endothelial metabolic homeostasis [31]. In addition, METTL4-mediated mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) 6 mA methylation has been implicated in mitochondrial dysfunction and AS progression [99]. The recently developed METTL4-specific antagonist pemetrexed selectively ameliorates AS, offering a potential new therapeutic avenue [99]. These findings highlight the broader therapeutic relevance of RNA methyltransferase targeting beyond METTL3, suggesting a coordinated network of epitranscriptomic regulators in vascular pathology.

Beyond synthetic drugs, the integration of traditional Chinese medicine (TCM)–derived natural products into epitranscriptomic research is an emerging frontier. Compounds such as tanshinone and quercetin warrant systematic investigation to determine whether their anti-atherosclerotic effects—traditionally attributed to “restoring balance” and anti-inflammatory regulation—are mediated via m6A-dependent pathways. Such work could bridge ethnopharmacology and modern RNA biology, enabling the rational design of hybrid therapeutic regimens that couple cultural heritage with molecular precision. Current evidence indicates that certain natural compounds can modulate m6A methylation to influence inflammation and apoptosis, thereby exerting protective effects against AS.
