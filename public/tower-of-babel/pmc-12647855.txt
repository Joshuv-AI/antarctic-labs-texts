The loss to follow-up of participants excluded from the NAMSAL trial: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Zabdielle Blonde Goufack Kenfack, Sylvain Raoul Simeni Njonnou, Solange Whegang Youdom, Eunice Vanessa Yuego Chedjou, Charles Kouanfack
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite advances in Human Immune Virus (HIV) treatment, loss to follow-up (LTFU) remains a challenge, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa. Understanding its predictors is vital to improve retention in care and hence, better treatment outcomes. While clinical trials result in better follow-up and possibly better treatment for the patients included, little data is available on those excluded. We aimed to assess the LTFU rate and associated factors among patients excluded from the New Antiretroviral and Monitoring Strategies in HIV-infected Adults in Low-income Countries (NAMSAL) trial. A hospital-based retrospective cohort study was conducted from December 2022 to July 2023 across three health facilities in Yaoundé, Cameroon. Data from 132 patients who were excluded from the NAMSAL trial was analyzed using R software. LTFU was defined as no recorded data for three months or more after missing scheduled visits. Kaplan–Meier estimates and Cox proportional hazards regression were used to describe and analyze LTFU rates and associated factors, with a p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant. We analyzed data from 132 (51% male) participants excluded from the NAMSAL trial, with a median age of 34 (IQR: 23–51) years. Most participants were employed (63.6%) and single (72.7%). This population was mostly in WHO clinical stages 1 (37.1%) and 3 (33.3%), and 57.6% had baseline viral loads below 100,000 copies/mL. More than half (74, 56.1%) were LTFU over the study period. The median time to LTFU was 108 weeks. Cox regression analysis revealed that being female (aHR = 0.27, [95% CI 0.13–0.52]; p˂0.001) and having a high baseline viral load (aHR = 0.34, [95% CI 0.16–0.71]; p = 0.004) were associated with reduced LTFU risk. The high LTFU rate among excluded NAMSAL trial patients highlights the need for targeted retention strategies, particularly for male patients, patients with multiple comorbidities and advanced disease stages. These findings can inform interventions to improve HIV care retention in Cameroon.Keywords: HIV, Loss to follow-up (LTFU), NAMSAL trial, Cameroon, Retention in careSubject terms: HIV infections, Epidemiology

Introduction

Over the decades since the discovery of Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV), significant progress has been made to improve the lives of people living with HIV (PLHIV). Highly Active Antiretroviral Therapy (HAART) has transformed HIV into a manageable chronic condition, yet it remains a major global public health challenge, especially in sub-Saharan Africa1. By the end of 2022, approximately 39 million people worldwide were living with HIV, with two-thirds residing in the World Health Organization (WHO) African Region2. In Cameroon, about 500,000 individuals were living with HIV in 2021, resulting in a prevalence rate of 2.9%3.

The Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) targets aim for 95% of people living with HIV (PLHIV) to know their status, 95% of those who know their status to receive treatment, and 95% of those on treatment to achieve viral load suppression by 20253. However, despite considerable efforts made, Cameroon still fall behind these targets, with only 67% of PLHIV aware of their status, 53% on treatment, and 42% achieving suppressed viral loads in 20214. To address this issue, clinical trials for effective HIV/AIDS treatments have been conducted. These studies offer a unique opportunity for meticulous long-term monitoring of participants. However, due to inclusion or exclusion criteria, some patients may not have access to the close follow-up provided during clinical studies and may see their follow-up deprioritized5. While trial results demonstrate the efficacy of treatments within specific, homogeneous groups, they may not accurately reflect how the treatments will perform in a more diverse patient population. This discrepancy can lead to situations where treatments are less effective, or even harmful, in real-world settings6. Therefore, clinical practice guidelines, often based on trial data, may not be universally applicable, which can impact treatment decisions6. Despite the clear criteria for exclusion from clinical trials, people who are excluded are at increased risk of being lost to follow-up (LTFU), which may introduce bias into study outcomes. Patients excluded due to disease severity or potential side effects are often those most likely to experience adverse events or discontinue participation7. Given all those factors, this population may be more prone to being LTFU.

One such effort addressing HIV was the NAMSAL (New Antiretroviral and Monitoring Strategies in HIV-infected Adults in Low-income Countries) trial, a phase III study conducted across three health facilities in Yaoundé, Cameroon. It aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of dolutegravir (DTG)-based and low-dose efavirenz (EFV)-based regimens for initial HIV-1 treatment8,9. Certain participants were excluded because of factors such as pregnancy, breastfeeding, severe hepatic or renal impairment, cytopenia, HIV-2 co-infection, severe psychiatric illness, or unstable tuberculosis co-infection8. These excluded participants, newly initiating HAART, have characteristics that could bias trial findings, but are still representative of the general population.

Lost to follow-up (LTFU), defined as not receiving an ART refill or follow-up for three months or more since the last scheduled visit10, varies worldwide. A study in Mozambique among newly diagnosed HIV patients in 2016 found a 51.6% LTFU rate11 after 12 months. Similarly, a comprehensive study spanning 432 PEPFAR-supported facilities in Nigeria reported that 30.6% of patients who started HAART between 2004 and 2017 were lost to follow-up12. A large retrospective study in adults in Zimbabwe reported a rate of 5.75 per 100 person-years among those who initiated treatment between 2004 and 201713, whereas a similar study in Eastern Ethiopia observed a 16.6% LTFU rate after six months14. Various studies have identified factors predicting LTFU, including being male, aged 15–24, poor adherence, advanced WHO clinical stage (III/IV), low CD4 counts, substance abuse, tuberculosis or opportunistic infections, stigma, rural residence, lack of motivation from services, high transportation costs, long distances, and extended waiting times12,15–18.

Disruption of ART can lead to worsening immunological and clinical conditions, treatment failure, and increased mortality from AIDS. It may also cause medication resistance, drug toxicity, and a higher risk of viral transmission19. Nonetheless, high levels of LTFU persist in the HIV care cascade20. Despite these challenges, limited research exists on LTFU and its predictors in Cameroon. Our study aimed to assess LTFU rates and associated factors among individuals excluded from the NAMSAL trial, using retrospective data collected from 2022 to 2023, to guide strategies for improving retention in care and achieving better health outcomes.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a hospital-based retrospective cohort study from December 2022 to July 2023 to assess LTFU among excluded participants of the NAMSAL study. The study was carried out in three healthcare facilities in Yaoundé, where the NAMSAL study was initially implemented: Yaoundé Central Hospital (HCY), Yaoundé Military Hospital (HMY), and Yaoundé Cité-Verte District Hospital (HCVY).

Study population

The study population included the files of the 204 individuals who were initially eligible but later excluded from the NAMSAL study.

Exclusion criteria

Participants with incomplete records concerning follow-up status or those with missing data on relevant variables were excluded. This ensured data validity.

Data collection

Data were obtained from routine clinical assessments and medical records, using a computer-based case report form (CsPro®). This included demographic information (age, sex, education, marital and employment status), clinical characteristics (baseline viral load, disease stage, co-infections), and follow-up outcomes.

Operational definitions

Advanced HIV disease: Stage 3 or 4 of HIV. Stage 1 and 2 were considered as less advanced.

Advanced HIV disease: Stage 3 or 4 of HIV. Stage 1 and 2 were considered as less advanced.

Co-infections: The presence of other infectious diseases alongside HIV in a patient, as documented in medical records.

High viral load (HVL): HIV RNA levels in blood above 100,000 copies/mL

Loss to follow-up (LTFU): Patients who missed scheduled clinic visits for at least 3 consecutive months and could not be reached despite contact attempts, do not have recorded data for this period and were not classified as deceased or transferred.

Viral suppression: Achievement of HIV viral load below 200 copies/mL, indicating effective antiretroviral treatment.

Data management and analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R software. The Kaplan–Meier method was used to estimate the cumulative proportion of individuals lost to follow-up over time, and log rank test for comparison. A significance level of p < 0.05 was set for hypothesis testing.

The primary outcome of interest was LTFU, defined as individuals who had missed scheduled clinic visits for at least 3 consecutive months and could not be reached despite contact attempts, do not have recorded data for this period and were not classified as deceased or transferred. We equally identified factors associated with LTFU using Cox proportional hazards regression, adjusting for potential confounders (such as age, recruitment site, marital status). The hazard ratio measures the relative risk of an event between two groups, while Cox regression is used to estimate this effect while taking into account other variables influencing the result.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Centre Regional Ethics Committee for Human Health Research (N° 0714/CRERSHC/2024). We obtained permission from the hospital administration to access the data. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, (full name of IRB) waived the need of obtaining informed consent. All patient information was anonymized and stripped of identifiers before analysis. Access to the database was secured with a password to maintain confidentiality. The data and results were used for purely scientific purposes. This research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

General characteristics of the study population

A total of 204 files that were initially eligible but later excluded from the NAMSAL study were reviewed. 204 people excluded from NAMSAL were identified. However, 132 files of patients (64.7%) were finally included in the analysis due to missing files, missing data and withdrawal of consent, (Fig. 1). The median age of participants was 34 years (IQR: 23–51 years). Just over half of the study participants (68, 51.5%) were males. Nearly two-thirds of participants (84, 63.6%) were employed, and the majority (96, 72.7%) were single. Regarding study site distribution, 42.4% of the participants were from the Yaoundé Central Hospital (HCY), with the remaining distributed between Yaoundé Military Hospital (HMY) and Yaoundé Cité-Verte District Hospital (HCVY). Loss to follow-up

Of the 132 participants, 74 (56.1%) were lost to follow-up and 6 died during the followup. The median time to loss to follow-up was 108 weeks, indicating an increasing likelihood of loss over time. The survival curve showed a steady increase, with a higher LTFU rate in the first year (Fig. 2).

Bivariate analysis

Preliminary analysis of potential risk factors identified several variables that appeared to be associated with loss to follow-up. Based on Kaplan–Meier curve comparisons, the variables sex, occupation, baseline viral load, presence of opportunistic infections, and disease stage were selected for inclusion in the multivariate model (Figs. 3 and 4).

Cox regression analysis

Variables selected for multivariate analysis based on univariate screening included sex, occupation, baseline viral load, opportunistic infections, and disease stage. After adjustments, female sex remained significantly protective against loss to follow-up (aHR = 0.27, [95% CI 0.13–0.52], p < 0.001), as did baseline viral load ≥ 100,000 copies/mL (aHR = 0.34, [95% CI 0.16–0.71], p = 0.004). Employment status (HR = 0.90, [95% CI 0.44–1.82], p = 0.8) and absence of opportunistic infections (HR = 0.65, [95% CI 0.35–1.22], p = 0.2) showed no significant association with retention in care, as seen in Table 3.

Discussion

HIV/AIDS is a leading cause of death worldwide. The fight against HIV/AIDS is hinged on initiation and adherence to HAART, and has increased the attainment of viral load suppression and reduction in HIV/AIDS-related deaths. Unfortunately, LTFU and rebound remain a barrier to worldwide viral suppression, increasing the risk of treatment failure and the potential for transmission. The NAMSAL study, which took place in 2016, included patients aged 18 years or older, ART-naive, with HIV-1 group M infection, and a viral load of at least 1000 copies/mL. Exclusion criteria were pregnancy; breastfeeding; severe hepatic impairment, renal failure, cytopenia, HIV-2 co-infection, HIV infection with HIV-1 O, N, P, or HIV-2, severe psychiatric illness, or unstable tuberculosis co-infection. These excluded participants’ medical records constituted our study population. Of the 204 files expected, 176 (82.3%) files were found and 46 (22.5%) of those were excluded due to incomplete record information. We aimed to evaluate the outcomes of these excluded participants: viral load suppression, loss to follow up and death rate at 48, 96 and 144 weeks after ART initiation, and factors associated with LTFU. We observed a loss to follow-up rate of 50% at week 48, and 60.5% at week 144. The median time before being lost to follow-up was significantly lower in participants with an initial viral load < 100,000 copies/mL. Being female and having a high baseline viral load were significantly associated with a reduced risk of loss to follow-up.

The 56.1% LTFU rate after 3 years observed in our study is comparable to rates reported in other African settings, including 57.5% in Guinea-Bissau21, 56% in South West Nigeria22, and 50.28% in Harare, Zimbabwe13. Similar findings were reported in other studies from Malawi (54%)23. However, these findings contrast with a previous study in Cameroon that reported a substantially lower LTFU rate of 17.1%24. Similarly, research in Amhara, North West Ethiopia, observed a 40.8% LTFU rate25, while studies in Zimbabwe26 and Uganda reported LTFU rates of 23.5% and 20%, respectively, after 2 years. These variations in LTFU rate may be attributed to differences in study population, as well as differences in socio-economic contexts, sample size variations, socioeconomic factors or definitions of LTFU. The overall high LTFU rate also largely reflects the high proportion: more than 50% of participants with physiological impairments and diseases in our population. Patients excluded from the NAMSAL study had specific characteristics that could impact the results of the clinical trial. This included breastfeeding, severe hepatic or renal impairment, cytopenia, HIV-2 co-infection, severe psychiatric illness, or unstable tuberculosis co-infection8.

Most patients in this study were either newly diagnosed with HIV or knew of their status and were not yet on HAART. It has been found some studies have found that patients receiving ART for the first time are more likely to be LTFU in the first 6–12 months25,27,28, which aligns with our findings, where majority were lost within the first year. This highlights the importance of early supportive intervention in the first weeks after initial diagnosis in the prevention of early defaulting from care.

We found, overall, several independent risk factors associated with an increased risk of being LTFU. Analysis identified male sex as an independent risk factor of being LTFU, which is consistent with findings from several studies across different settings29–31. Fewer studies identify an association between female sex and being LTFU32. Gender disparities in retention may reflect differences in healthcare-seeking behaviors, competing priorities, and social support structures that influence men’s engagement with HIV services15,17.

Studies by Kebede et al. 33. and Aliyu et al. 34. found that unemployment was associated with a higher risk of loss to follow-up, whereas our study found that employed individuals were more likely to be lost to follow-up. These differences may be due to differences in study populations, healthcare systems, or cultural contexts. Moreover, in certain situations, the workplace can induce a significant risk of LTFU due to work-related barriers (clinic hours, stigma at workplace). It is therefore necessary to recognize it and propose for this population tailored interventions (night shift or home evaluation…).

This study also found that low baseline viral load was negatively associated with higher risk of loss to follow-up. These results, although different from those found elsewhere34–43, highlight the retention in care of people who are sicker, which may be due to the therapeutic education they have received. Classically, LTFU are more common in people with higher viral load, co-infections and low CD4 cell count36,37.

A qualitative study showed that individuals who were feeling well were more likely to be lost to follow-up21. This relates to our findings where most of our participants had no co-infections and with a low initial baseline viral load, felt fewer symptoms and were more likely to be lost to follow-up. However, contrasting results observed in Mizan-Aman could be attributed to enhanced healthcare-seeking patterns and heightened community education among Stage III and IV individuals41.

Other studies have identified additional factors associated with loss to follow-up, including long distance to healthcare facility23, feeling healthy physically37, poor adherence35,44 and being single44.

This study had several limitations, including its focus on patients excluded from the NAMSAL trial, who represent a specific population with physiological impairments or comorbidities. Hence, our results may not be generalizable to the broader HIV-positive population. Second, we relied on routinely collected clinical data, which may have had incomplete documentation of all relevant variables. In addition, some psychological variables such as stigma, mental health issues, drugs or alcohol abuse have not been accessed, as well as the impact of transport cost. Finally, we were unable to determine the actual outcomes of patients classified as LTFU, some of whom may have transferred to other facilities, died, or later returned to care.

Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable insights into the retention challenges facing patients who are often excluded from clinical trials but represent a significant portion of the HIV-positive population in real-world settings. The high LTFU rates observed highlight the need for targeted interventions to improve retention, particularly during the first year of treatment and among men, employed individuals, and those with lower viral loads or without co-infections.

Future research should focus on developing and evaluating interventions tailored to these high-risk groups, including flexible service delivery models for employed individuals, enhanced counselling strategies for patients with lower viral loads, and integrated support systems that address the specific barriers faced by men. Additionally, improved tracking systems to distinguish between true LTFU and silent transfers would enhance our understanding of patient movement within the healthcare system.

While the issue raised in this article concerns the retention of individuals living with HIV who are excluded from clinical trials, it also presents an ethical dilemma. Individuals recruited for clinical trials, regardless of their exclusion status, should be entitled to the same benefits and proper follow-up care. In practice, this is not the case, and many patients who are excluded from clinical trials find themselves abandoned and lost to follow-up. This issue must be addressed urgently in all clinical trials, with specific measures implemented to tackle it.

Conclusion

More than half of the participants were lost to follow-up, with male sex, low baseline viral load, and absence of multiple co-infections being associated with higher risk of loss to follow-up. Further research, particularly focusing on people excluded from HIV clinical trials, is needed to understand the complex factors influencing their loss to follow-up and to develop effective interventions to improve retention in care.
