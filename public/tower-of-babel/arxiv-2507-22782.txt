Enhancing Multi-Agent Collaboration with Attention-Based Actor-Critic Policies
1 st Hugo Garrido-Lestache Belinchon, 2 nd Jeremy Kedziora

Abstract
This paper introduces Team-Attention-Actor-Critic (TAAC), a reinforcement learning algorithm designed to enhance multi-agent collaboration in cooperative environments. TAAC employs a Centralized Training/Centralized Execution scheme incorporating multi-headed attention mechanisms in both the actor and critic. This design facilitates dynamic, inter-agent communication, allowing agents to explicitly query teammates, thereby efficiently managing the exponential growth of joint-action spaces while ensuring a high degree of collaboration. We further introduce a penalized loss function which promotes diverse yet complementary roles among agents. We evaluate TAAC in a simulated soccer environment against benchmark algorithms representing other multi-agent paradigms, including Proximal Policy Optimization and Multi-Agent Actor-Attention-Critic. We find that TAAC exhibits superior performance and enhanced collaborative behaviors across a variety of metrics (win rates, goal differentials, Elo ratings, inter-agent connectivity, balanced spatial distributions, and frequent tactical interactions such as ball possession swaps).



I
Introduction

When human organizations address a large cooperative problem or a task, they scale the number of individuals at the expense of having to organize and coordinate them. Over thousands of generations, we have honed our ability to organize many individuals to collaborate at scale to reach a common goal, and woven it into our largest architectural and organizational achievements. As we integrate reinforcement learning into real-world applications, we need multi-agent systems that efficiently scale collaborative action to large numbers of agents to address broader needs and problems. In this paper, we study methods to manage cooperative multi-agent environments or systems. Our goal is to teach groups of AI agents strong collaborative skills that allow them to act as a team in a scalable fashion, thereby tackling complex problems and challenges efficiently.

The best methods for training a team to collaborate in a cooperative environment remains an open question. The main paradigms that have been used to learn collaboration across agents in cooperative MARL are Decentralized Training/Decentralized Execution (DTDE), Centralized Training/Decentralized Execution (CTDE), and Centralized Training/Centralized Execution (CTCE). In DTDE agents operate independently without shared information both during training and inference while in CTDE information is shared during training (usually to estimate values) but not when agents select actions or during inference. Finally, CTCE shares information during execution, which allows delegation of control to a single controller which determines the joint-action of all agents. DTDE and CTDE schemes present challenges due to the need to decide whether and how to share information across agents; these modeling choices are critical for team collaboration.
1
1

1

Decentralized execution schemes can also suffer from convergence problems due to the inherent non-stationarity that arises when multiple agents learn and adapt concurrently.
The CTCE single controller avoids these difficulties but poses another problem: the full joint-action space under a CTCE scheme is the product set of individual agent action sets and so its size grows exponentially in the number of agents. Effective team collaboration would seem to hinge on a robust search of the joint-action space for complimentary actions across agents, and so a CTCE scheme imposes scalability constraints on collaborative activity.

To address these challenges, we introduce and test an RL algorithm that leverages shared information to foster inter-agent collaboration. Our proposed model, Team-Attention-Actor-Critic (TAAC), is a CTCE scheme that incorporates an attention mechanism directly into the actor to model an information-sharing “conversation” between multiple agents which enables them to dynamically query teammates during decision-making. This allows our agents to manage the joint-action space in a scalable fashion. We further introduce a loss that encourages agents to learn diverse yet complementary roles, enhancing collaboration. We evaluate TAAC in a simulated soccer environment, an ideal testbed for teamwork and spatial coordination, and benchmark its performance against PPO (as an example of DTDE) and MAAC (as an example of CTDE) using both standard metrics (e.g., win rate, Elo) and multiple indicators of collaboration (e.g., pairwise distance, possession swaps, connectivity).

Background and preliminaries

Our strategy to model collaboration will build upon actor-critic algorithms. Below, we introduce this algorithm along with the principles and notation needed to formalize it. Following this section, we provide a complete description of our architecture.

In reinforcement learning, control problems with multiple agents can be modelled as Markov Decision Games (MDG), which are defined by:

A set of

•

A set of states

•

\displaystyle\vec{a}=\langle a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}\rangle\in A(s);

•

•

A function:

\displaystyle\vec{r}(s^{\prime},\vec{a},s)=\langle r_{i}(s^{\prime},\vec{a},s)\rangle_{i=1,\ldots,n}

Throughout this paper, for a generic vector

\displaystyle\vec{z}_{\backslash i}=\langle z_{1},\ldots,z_{i-1},z_{i+1},\ldots,z_{n}\rangle

In environments that take place over a finite number of discrete periods

o_{i,t}=O_{i}(s_{t})

The goal of each agent is to learn a policy which describes the probability that a trained agent should take action

III-B

Actor-Critic Algorithms

A common choice for the baseline function
b(\cdot)
is an estimate of the long-term value, for example, the state-value function,
V(o_{i,t})
, which predicts the return to an agent given an observation
o_{i,t}
(see
[
20
]
). If we model the state value function as a differentiable function of parameters
\mathbf{w}
and write

then this yields the following baseline:

\displaystyle b(o_{i,t})

\displaystyle=V_{\mathbf{w}}(o_{i,t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in A_{i}}\pi_{\mathbf{u}}(a|o_{i,t})Q_{\mathbf{w}}(o_{i,t},a)

where the second equality follows from the usual definitions of the state-value function
V_{\mathbf{w}}(\cdot)
and the state-action value function
Q_{\mathbf{w}}(\cdot)
. We will use this form of the baseline below to model multi-agent structure.

The policy is referred to as the actor and the long-term value estimate as the critic, and the resulting model is called an actor-critic algorithm. The actor’s parameters are learned through the policy gradient estimates and the critic’s parameters are learned through value-based methods (e.g. gradient descent on the difference between the critic’s predicted state value and a target value, which can be either the observed

[
21
]

IV
Team-Attention-Actor-Critic (TAAC)

IV-A

Attention Mechanism

We extend
[
7
]
by introducing a separate attention mechanism in the actor. The intuition behind doing this is to allow agents to use the internal representations of other agents directly when making choices, with the idea being that enabling this is analogous to allowing agents to “put themselves into the shoes” of their colleagues and thereby giving them insight into what other agents will choose. If they build an understanding of what their colleagues will choose then they may be able to create collaboration by selecting actions that dovetail well.

In general, attention works as follows (see
[
22
]
). An initial input
z
is suitably embedded as a sequence of vectors
M(z)=\{m_{1}(z),\ldots,m_{n}(z)\}
and multiplied with learned matrices
W_{Q}
,
W_{K}
, and
W_{V}
. Attention is then computed as:

A(M(z)|W)=\text{Softmax}\left(\frac{M(z)W_{Q}(M(z)W_{K})^{T}}{\sqrt{d_{K}}}\right)M(z)W_{V}

(3)

where
W=(W_{Q},W_{K},W_{V})
and
d_{K}
is the dimensionality of the
M(z)W_{K}
matrix.
8
8

8

The subscripts refer to the usual presentation of
W_{Q}
,
W_{K}
, and
W_{V}
as the query, key, and value matrices, respectively. Note that in all other sections of the paper
Q(\cdot)
will refer to the state-action value function. Division by
d_{K}
is done to scale the values for numerical stability.

In a natural language application, the input might be a sequence of embedded tokens (e.g. words). In our application, these embedded input tokens will represent individual agent observation and/or action information. We apply multiple attention heads in parallel, each with its own set of weight matrices. The updated outputted sequence of tokens from these heads are concatenated by agent to create an updated set of agent-specific embedding information. Specifically, given
h
attention heads, for agent
i
we gather updated agent-specific embedding information by concatenating:

\displaystyle E_{i}(z|W)=\{A_{i,j}(M(z)|W_{j})\}_{j=1}^{h}

(4)

where
A_{i,j}(\cdot|W_{j})
refers to the values in the
i
th row of the
j
th head’s output.

These tokens are individually forwarded through the rest of the architecture to generate either a distribution over actions (in the actor) or a state-action value estimate (in the critic); we describe both below.

Multi-Agent Baseline

We adopt the approach taken in
[
15
]
and set the baseline in the policy gradient by adapting the traditional critic to the multiagent setting in the following way:

\displaystyle b_{i}(\vec{o}|\vec{a}_{\backslash i})

\displaystyle=V(\vec{o}|\vec{a}_{\backslash i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{a_{i}\in A_{i}}\pi^{(i)}_{\mathbf{u}}\left(a_{i}\mid\vec{o}\right)\,Q^{(i)}_{\mathbf{w}}(\vec{o},\vec{a}).

Here, baseline models the multiagent setting in the sense that it estimates the expected value for a specific agent, conditioning on the observations and actions of all other agents.

IV-E

Conformity Loss

We further model and encourage collaboration by adjusting the actor loss function to promote diversity of actions among agents, under the assumption that effective collaboration arises when agents work on separate and diverse tasks.
11
11

11

The value of diversity may not hold in all environments, but we found it to be a useful addition for our experiments.
We call this adjustment conformity loss. It is computed by measuring the cosine similarity between each unique pair of updated agent-specific embeddings derived from attention layer outputs.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle(u_{m_{1}^{\pi}},u_{A})

\displaystyle=\theta_{S}\max\left\{\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i}\sum_{i^{\prime}\neq i}S_{ii^{\prime}}(u_{m_{1}^{\pi}},u_{A}),\theta_{B}\right\}

where:

\displaystyle S_{ii^{\prime}}(u_{m_{1}^{\pi}},u_{A})=\frac{E_{i}(\vec{o}|u_{m_{1}^{\pi}},u_{A})^{\top}E_{i^{\prime}}(\vec{o}|u_{m_{1}^{\pi}},u_{A})}{\|E_{i}(\vec{o}|u_{m_{1}^{\pi}},u_{A})\|\,\|E_{i^{\prime}}(\vec{o}|u_{m_{1}^{\pi}},u_{A})\|}

and
\theta_{S}
and
\theta_{B}
are hyperparameters used to scale and bound the loss. This contribution to overall loss attains high values when agents’ attention layer outputs are very similar (indicating low diversity) and low values when they are not.
12
12

12

In our implementation, we lower bound this loss at a minimum of
\theta_{B}
to ensure that parameter gradients are directed toward an intermediate level of action diversity during learning.

V
Experimentation

In this paper, we compare multiple algorithms against our novel architecture (TAAC) in a simulated soccer environment. In this environment, teams of 3 agents work together to score goals against an opposing team. A goal is scored when the ball is kicked into the opponent’s goal. Each team is composed entirely of agents running the same algorithm to ensure a fair comparison.

V-A

Environment Structure

The simulation represents players as solid circles on a 2D plane capable of moving toward the ball and colliding with it. When a player collides with the ball while in mode “kicking”, the ball gains additional velocity. Additionally, the ball collides nearly elastically with the walls, preventing it from leaving the play area. Figure
3
illustrates the simulation environment.

Fig. 3:
Simulation Environment for Soccer

At each time step, every agent derives an individual, local observation from the global state. In our environment, the state consists of:

•

The relative position vector to each teammate.

•

The relative position vector to each agent on the opposing team.

•

The relative position vector to the ball.

•

The velocity vector of the ball.

•

The relative position vector to the opponent’s goal.

•

The relative position vector to the agent’s own goal and opponents goal.

•

Raycast measurements in the directions of North, East, West, and South, indicating the distance to the nearest boundaries of the play area.

Based on its observation, an agent selects an action.
In our environment, an action is defined by five Boolean inputs: move forward, move right, move down, move left, and kick.
An agent may choose any combination of these inputs, provided they are not contradictory (e.g., moving left and right simultaneously). Overall, the total number of possible actions is 18.

After an action is chosen and the simulation advances by one time step, rewards are assigned to each agent. The reward structure is designed as follows:

•

Exploration Reward:
Each agent receives a small reward for moving toward the ball. This reward is computed as the dot product of the agent’s chosen direction
\vec{d}
and the vector from the agent to the ball
\vec{v}_{a}
, scaled by a factor
\theta_{\text{exp}}
:

\displaystyle r_{\text{explore}}=\theta_{\text{exp}}\,(\vec{d}\cdot\vec{v}_{a})

(5)

•

Team Reward:
All agents on the same team receive a larger reward for moving the ball towards the opponent’s goal. This reward is calculated as the dot product of the ball’s velocity vector
\vec{v}_{b}
and the vector from the ball to the opponent’s goal
\vec{g}
scaled by a factor
\theta_{\text{ball}}
:

\displaystyle r_{\text{ball}}=\theta_{\text{ball}}\,(\vec{v}_{b}\cdot\vec{g})

(6)

•

Scoring Reward:
The biggest reward
r_{\text{goal}}
is granted when a goal is scored, defined as the ball being fully inside the goal box.

•

Distance Reward:
This reward is allocated based on the average distance to all teammates, to prevent players from clustering together.
First, the average distance is computed, then it is capped at a maximum threshold, and finally scaled by a constant factor to prevent excessive rewards for large spacing. Specifically, if
d_{i}
represents the distance to teammate
i
and there are
N
teammates, the average distance is calculated as

\bar{d}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}d_{i}

and the reward function is defined as:

\displaystyle f(\bar{d})=\theta_{\text{dist}}\cdot\min\left(\bar{d},\ \theta_{\text{max}}\right).

(7)

Here,
\theta_{\text{dist}}
is the scaling factor and
\theta_{\text{max}}
is the cap applied to the average distance.

It is important to note that all rewards (but the distance reward) can be negative, acting as penalties for behaviours such as moving away from the ball, moving the ball away from the opponent’s goal, or being scored on.

Games are configured to last
T
time steps, and an episode is defined as a sequence of time steps that terminates either when a goal is scored or when the game concludes. As a result, a single game may consist of multiple episodes.

V-B

Training Procedure

We employ curriculum learning
[
23
]
to progressively build up skills in this complex environment. The training is divided into the following stages:

1.

Stage 1:
Score goals against an inactive opposing team, with random ball and player spawns.

2.

Stage 2:
Introduce an opposing team with randomly selected actions, encouraging players to navigate obstacles and opponents. Continuing with random ball and player spawns.

3.

Stage 3:
Engage in league play, where a team competes against a randomly selected past version of itself while maintaining random spawn positions.

4.

Stage 4:
Continued league play with fixed starting positions for each team.

This staged learning approach allows agents to first master movement and scoring before tackling competitive play.

V-C

Evaluation Metrics

Performance is evaluated using win rates, goal differentials, and ELO ratings
[
24
]
.
Each team is assigned an Elo rating, which gets updated based on victory, lost or tie and the Elo rating of the opponent.
When interpreting Elo ratings, it is important to note that the absolute values are less meaningful than the relative differences between them.
Additionally, Goal differential is measured as the difference between the teams’ scores.
In addition to performance, we assess collaboration using several key indicators:

•

Average Pairwise Distance:
A higher average distance suggests that agents are not clustering excessively, which may indicate diverse task allocation.

•

Possession Frequency:
This measures the number of times a ball swaps possession within the team. A high number may indicate that players are passing and, therefore, collaborating.

•

Connectivity:
This metric measures the number of unobstructed graph-like connections between players, calculated as the ratio of observed connections to the maximum possible connections
\frac{N(N-1)}{2}
.
To ensure only meaningful connections are counted, connections only within a range of distance are included.
An optimal connectivity level indicates balanced spacing and collaborative positioning.

Using these metrics, we can comprehensively assess both the performance and collaborative behaviour of the teams.

VII
Analysis

Following extensive training of all algorithms, our results indicate that TAAC is the top performer, as evidenced by its superior ELO rating and win rate (see Figures
4
and
5
), with PPO closely trailing. In contrast, MAAC significantly underperforms compared to expectations for a state-of-the-art MARL method, possibly due to insufficient training time or suboptimal hyperparameter settings.
Additionally, Figure
6
illustrates head-to-head comparisons, highlighting that TAAC outperformed all other algorithms.

When examining collaboration-oriented metrics (Figures
7
–
9
), the data suggest that TAAC exhibits more coordinated teamwork.
For instance, while Figure
9
indicates that PPO agents are more spread out, Figure
7
shows that TAAC’s agents are more closely connected, which may be linked to more advantageous positioning.
In addition, the possession swap metrics in Figure
8
imply that TAAC and PPO frequently swapped possession of the ball, potential indicating tactical passing. MAAC demonstrates an incredibly high possessions frequency, potentially indicating frequent passing.

Video recordings further substantiate these findings.
The footage confirms that PPO frequently employs a dedicated goalie while the remaining players focus on attacking, explaining their pronounced spatial separation.
Additionally, PPO exhibits clustering among its attackers and pursues the ball aggressively, which also explains its lower overall connectivity and relatively high possession frequency
In contrast, TAAC consistently maintains a stable offensive formation that only transitions to a defensive position when necessary, which is evident through its connectivity score.
TAAC’s dynamic strategy, characterized by frequent ball possession exchanges and timely passes, demonstrates superior tactical coordination.
Video recording of MAAC indicates a similar approach to PPO, but instead having the entire team pursue the ball aggressively, this explain their low pairwise distance and connectivity.
Furthermore, MAAC’s high possession frequency is also explained by this behaviour, as possession likely rapidly changes between players when the players are grouped together.
Additionally, MAAC was consistently outperformed by both PPO and TAAC, as seen in the performance results.

Overall, these results suggest that our novel algorithm demonstrates enhanced collaborative behaviour and improved performance in environments where teamwork is critical. However, due to MAAC’s suboptimal performance and limited training, we plan to investigate its shortcomings further to enable a fairer comparison with state-of-the-art methods. Looking ahead, a logical next step is to evaluate TAAC in alternative environments and scenarios to better understand its limitations. Moreover, selecting an environment where collaboration is more directly tied to performance will allow us to more rigorously test our contribution.

VIII
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced TAAC, a multi-agent reinforcement learning algorithm that leverages multi-headed attention in both the actor and critic networks to foster robust collaboration.
Our experiments demonstrate that TAAC outperforms competing methods in win rates, goal differentials, and Elo ratings while exhibiting superior coordinated behaviour, as confirmed by game statistics and game recording analysis.
Future work will involve a deeper investigation into MAAC to ensure it performs to its full potential and also to explore this new algorithm in different environments to fully test its capabilities.
