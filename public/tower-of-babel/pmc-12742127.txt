Assessing the carbon footprint of French academic clinical trials using the NIHR method
Authors: Cécile Auriault, Martine Tching-Sin, Laetitia Biron, Elodie Faurel-Paul, Laurent Flet
Published: 2025

Introduction

The climate crisis is recognised as one of the greatest threats to global health in the 21st century.1 Its main cause is the accumulation of CO2 produced by human activities in the atmosphere.2 It is therefore crucial to reduce greenhouse gas emissions in all sectors of activity. According to the 2019 report from Health Care Without Harm, the healthcare sector accounts for 4.4% of global greenhouse gas emissions.3 In France, it represents over 8% of national emissions, amounting to nearly 40 million tonnes of CO2 equivalent (CO2e).4

Clinical trials are essential to the development of healthcare and new therapies, but they also have a significant environmental impact. In 2021, greenhouse gas emissions from the 350 000 clinical trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov were estimated at 27.5 million tonnes of CO2e.5 In this regard, several studies have assessed the carbon footprint of clinical trials, especially those sponsored by industry.68 Although public healthcare institutions are the main sponsors of interventional studies in France and more broadly worldwide, publications related to academic clinical trials remain scarce and mostly from the UK.911 In 2023, the National Institute for Health and Care Research (NIHR) published a method for calculating the carbon footprint applicable to academic clinical trials, along with a detailed guide.12 It describes all the processes involved in a clinical trial, listing the main activities that are necessary in order to calculate the carbon footprint. The authors then applied this methodology to two British academic clinical trials. Subsequently, the method was updated and applied to 10 other British clinical trials, and a questionnaire was developed to collect the information needed to calculate the carbon footprint of a clinical trial.13

Thus, the objectives of this research were to assess the applicability of the NIHR tool to French academic studies and to identify the carbon hotspots, with a view to discussing ways of mitigating their environmental impact for the development of future academic clinical trials.

Method

First, we used version V0.5 of the detailed guide from January 2024, along with the data collection questionnaire developed by the NIHR to calculate the carbon footprint of two clinical trials sponsored by the Nantes University Hospital. Since this tool uses emission factors specific to the UK, we replaced them with their most recent French equivalents, when available.13 For this, we used the public Base Empreinte database from the French Environment and Energy Management Agency (ADEME), V.23.6.14

We then selected studies sponsored by our institution Nantes University Hospital in France to retrospectively assess their carbon footprint. They also had to have been completed recently so that we could collect complete data. Finally, we wanted to evaluate studies with different profiles in terms of the number of centres, the number of patients, their phase and their therapeutic area. As a result, only two studies met these criteria. First, we chose a single-centre study, conducted at our institution, the TOCILAM one (NCT04547062). This was a phase I clinical trial involving dose escalation of tocilizumab in combination with idarubicin and cytarabine chemotherapy in patients with poor-prognosis acute myeloid leukaemia (AML). Adult patients with poor-prognosis AML in first-line or relapse settings, eligible for intensive 3+7 induction therapy, were considered. Three escalating doses were tested of intravenous tocilizumab (4, 6 and 8 mg/kg) administered at day (d) 8 of a standard AML induction chemotherapy (intravenous idarubicine 8 mg/m2 d1 to d5 + intravenous cytarabine 100 mg/m2 d1 to d7). After hospitalisation, the patient was seen twice a week at the day hospital until day 45 of induction for the same assessment. This is standard practice during AML induction. Twelve patients were enrolled between 2020 and 2023.15 16 We then selected a second, larger study in another therapeutic area: REMICRUSH (NCT03960801). This was a phase III, multicentre, controlled, randomised, single-blind study evaluating remifentanil as a replacement for curare for rapid sequence induction anaesthesia in patients at risk of aspiration. The patients included were admitted to the operating room or resuscitation room and required rapid sequence induction of anaesthesia for a procedure requiring general anaesthesia with orotracheal intubation. They were randomised either to the control group consisting of a hypnotic and a curare, or to the intervention group consisting of a hypnotic and remifentanil. On day 7, data concerning the occurrence of complications during hospitalisation were collected (episodes of postoperative pneumonia, postoperative respiratory distress, admission to intensive care or continuous care unit, or death). In cases where the patient was discharged before day 7, they were considered free of these complications. It was conducted in 15 centres in France from 2019 to 2023 and included 1150 patients (online supplemental table 1).1719

The retrospective activity data needed to calculate the carbon footprint were collected between February and July 2025 for each of the 10 modules identified by the NIHR (trial setup, clinical trial unit (CTU) emissions, meetings and travel, treatment intervention, data collection and exchange, trial supplies and equipment, trial-specific patient assessments, samples, laboratory and trial close out). These data related to sponsor and investigation activities were sourced from the Research Department of Nantes University Hospital and the investigation centres. The activity data for each of the two studies were compiled in a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet.13 The carbon footprint calculation was performed by multiplying these data by the appropriate emission factors, thereby identifying the hotspots (online supplemental table 2).

Patient and public involvement

No patients or members of the public were directly involved in this study.

Results

Overall, the tool proved to be easily transposable to the French context. The processes identified by the NIHR were comprehensive and relevant. Likewise, all activities related to the two clinical trials were found among those identified for each process by the NIHR.

Most emission factors were available in the French public Base Empreinte database.14 However, those related to black-and-white and colour printing were not available. Since the data used by the NIHR come from the European database Ecoinvent (V.2.2), we applied these emission factors as well. Additionally, in the absence of French data for binder production and teleworking, UK emission factors were used.20

The tools provided by the NIHR, such as the questionnaire and calculation method, greatly facilitated the collection of the necessary activity data and the calculation of the carbon footprint of our two clinical trials.

The total carbon footprint of the TOCILAM study was estimated at 3.2 tonnes of CO2e. The meetings and travel module was the largest contributor, accounting for 2151 kg of CO2 equivalent (kgCO2e), or more than two-thirds of the total carbon footprint. Within this module, the communication of the study results at a congress in the USA accounted for almost the entire emissions (2148 kgCO2e). Other major contributors were CTU emissions, followed by laboratory module. Most of the emissions linked to the CTU were attributable to heating, electricity and teleworking. Staff travel accounted for just under 2% (9 kgCO2e) of the carbon emissions from this module. The carbon footprint associated with trial setup and data collection and exchange was negligible (figure 1, table 1). The carbon footprint per patient was approximately 269 kgCO2e.

Regarding the second study, the total carbon footprint of REMICRUSH was estimated at 5.8 tonnes of CO2e. The meetings and travel module was also the largest carbon emitter, accounting for 3963 kgCO2e. This represented approximately 68% of the total carbon footprint of this study. However, unlike the TOCILAM study, the highest carbon-emitting activities within this module were the travel of clinical research associates (CRAs) for the various visits to participating investigator sites (initiations, monitorings and closures). CTUs emissions and trial closure visits were the two other modules that contributed most to the carbon footprint. Heating and electricity accounted for the largest portion of CTUs carbon emissions. The carbon footprint related to trial initiation, data collection and exchange, and treatment intervention was very low (figure 2, table 1). The carbon footprint per patient was approximately 5 kgCO2e.

Discussion

The NIHR method proved to be transposable, facilitated by the availability of adapted national emission factors, which is not always the case, as evidenced by the article by Siegfried et al, whose objective was to quantify the carbon footprint of a South African clinical trial.21

The total estimated carbon footprint of the TOCILAM and REMICRUSH clinical trials was generally lower than that reported in the literature for academic studies. Although one of the studies had an estimated carbon footprint of about 8 tonnes of CO2e, estimates for other studies range from 16 to over 900 tonnes of CO2e.101322 This demonstrates heterogeneity in the carbon footprint depending on the profile of the studies. The geographical scope of the studies, research phase, country of the coordinating unit, type of intervention, study duration, medical specialty and study design are all factors that can explain the wide variability in carbon dioxide emissions.

Although the two presented clinical trials are completely different in terms of research phase, number of patients and number of participating investigation sites, the two hotspots were meetings and travel, followed by CTU emissions. These results align with the literature. For example, Griffiths et al found that these two modules are also major contributors to carbon dioxide emissions in the majority of clinical trials.12 13 Similarly, Subaiya et al showed that CTU emissions are the greatest contributor to carbon impact in the CRASH-2 study, as well as in the six studies whose carbon footprint was assessed by Cranley et al.10 22 Patient travel to hospital for visits also generates carbon dioxide emissions, which was not observed in our two trials.23 This is explained by the fact that there were no patient travels specific to these studies, aside from standard of care.

The TOCILAM study showed that the largest contributor to carbon dioxide emissions was the principal investigator’s travel to an international conference. Without this, the carbon footprint of this small single-centre study would have been minimal. This result should be balanced by the fact that the investigator did not only communicate research results but also took the opportunity to participate more generally in the congress itself.

For the REMICRUSH study, travel by CRAs for the various visits to participating sites (initiations, monitorings and closures) accounted for the largest portion of carbon dioxide emissions. Although some of these visits were conducted virtually, one way to drastically reduce the carbon footprint would be to conduct these visits remotely as much as possible, or to outsource these activities to local providers, as sometimes done between academic sponsors. Similarly, combining investigation site visits with those of partner departments and using more sustainable means of transport could also reduce the carbon footprint.

Regarding the clinical research unit, it is more complicated to take action to reduce carbon dioxide emissions. Commuting was not the largest contributor to carbon impact, as the majority of staff use carbon-free transportation (cycling, walking) or public transportation. It is also difficult to reduce heating and electricity consumption. However, the prospect of moving into new offices in 2027 will certainly reduce the carbon footprint of our clinical research unit at Nantes University Hospital. Indeed, energy consumption in the new hospital’s is expected to be reduced by one-third compared with the current level, thanks to the use of innovative devices.25

Limitations

The NIHR method was applied to only two clinical trials, which does not reflect the diversity of academic-sponsored trials. Even if the results of the hotspots were similar, this does not predict identical results for other clinical trials. It is necessary to assess the carbon footprint of new academic clinical trials in order to confirm these results or, conversely, to identify new contributing carbon emission modules.

For some activity data, the absence of freely available French emission factors led to the use of British ones from two UK publications. However, this only concerned an extremely limited amount of data, resulting in a very low impact on carbon footprint estimates. Some activity data from the trials were sometimes difficult to retrieve, such as the number of emails sent during the trial and the proportion of emails with or without attachments. Similarly, as this was a retrospective assessment, some of the people involved in these studies no longer work at our institution, which complicated the collection of some data, particularly their commuting. In the same vein, for the REMICRUSH multicentre study, we extrapolated the home-to-work travel results of the staff of our clinical research unit to other study centres. The carbon footprint of this module is certainly underestimated due to the predominant use of eco-friendly transportation by our hospital staff.

It should also be taken into account that both studies were conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic. From the patients’ perspective, neither the flowcharts nor the visit procedures were modified during this period. From an organisational standpoint, some study staff therefore teleworked more than usual, which meant fewer home-to-work travels, but this should be put into perspective with the fact that the majority of staff use eco-friendly means of transport and consequently a slight underestimation of the carbon footprint.

Finally, the method proposed by the NIHR does not strictly distinguish between the carbon consumption of sponsorship-related activities and those related to investigation. This could be an area to explore for a future version of the NIHR methodology.

This method also has other limitations. It does not consider some parameters such as water use, land use and social and economic impacts, as pointed out by its authors. Similarly, activities over which we have no control, such as the manufacture of interventions (drugs, medical devices, etc.) and the activities of regulatory authorities are not considered and could significantly increase the carbon footprint of trials. For example, the production of tocilizumab in TOCILAM was not considered in the carbon footprint assessment, even though patients received an injection as part of the trial that they would not have received under standard care.26 For REMICRUSH, this point would have an even more negligible impact, as the study drug replaces the medication that is routinely used in standard practice.

Conclusion

This work allowed us to confirm that the NIHR tool is easily applicable to French academic clinical trials using adapted emission factors, the vast majority of which are available in the French public Base Empreinte database from ADEME.14 Although the carbon footprint of the two presented clinical trials is rather low compared with the data published for this type of research, areas for improvement have been identified. It could also be interesting to replicate this work for other larger-scale studies or studies involving different therapeutic interventions, for example, and to carry out a prospective estimate of the carbon footprint. This work also shows that the hotspots are relatively similar for both studies and that associated measures could be implemented during the design of future similar clinical trials to reduce their carbon footprint. Finally, it has helped to continue raising awareness among hospital professionals, both administrative and healthcare staff, about environmental issues related to clinical research.
