Effects of channelization on macroinvertebrate assemblages in a small stream: The case of Awetu in Jimma City, Ethiopia
Authors: Mahmud Ahmednur, Remla Sabir, Dechassa Lenjissa, Argaw Ambelu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractFreshwater ecosystems are increasingly modified worldwide by anthropogenic activities. Land use change is one of the leading factors responsible for stream ecosystem degradation. Physical habitat disturbance due to channelization is among the factors responsible for the loss of biodiversity and degradation of river water quality worldwide. Since 2020, Jimma City municipality has been beautifying the city and one of the activities is channelizing using concrete embankment, a part of the Awetu stream that runs through the city. The main aim of this study is to assess the effect of channelization on macroinvertebrate assemblages and the water quality of the Awetu stream. A cross-sectional study was conducted in April 2022. Macroinvertebrates, water samples, and habitat data were collected from 21 sampling sites along the three segments of the stream (upstream, channelized, and downstream). Data were analyzed for different macroinvertebrate metrics and water quality parameters. Canonical Correspondence Analysis was used to examine the overall relationship between macroinvertebrate assemblages and water quality parameters. The upstream site has better macroinvertebrate assemblages than channelized and downstream sites (p < 0.05). The downstream site showed better assemblage compared to the channelized site, though it was not significant. Regarding water quality parameters, a significant variation was observed between channelized and, un-channelized sites (upstream and downstream) (p < 0.05). The habitat condition score varied from 47 (poor) at channelization to 150 (suboptimal) upstream. Upstream sites were found to have relatively better macroinvertebrate assemblages, better water quality, and good habitat conditions. The downstream sites had lower macroinvertebrate assemblages, poor habitat conditions, and degraded water quality compared to the upstream sites. The channelized segment had the poorest habitat, with poor macroinvertebrate assemblages and water quality. Thus, the conservation of habitat conditions along the channelized segments of the Awetu stream is recommended to improve the water quality and macroinvertebrate assemblage.Keywords: Channelization, Habitat condition, Macroinvertebrates, Water quality, Urban stream

Introduction

Freshwater ecosystems are increasingly modified worldwide by anthropogenic activities [1,2]. Modern stream ecologists have long recognized that streams are well interconnected and influenced by watershed activities. They are potentially affected by the changes in the vegetation and land use of their riparian zones [[3], [4], [5]]. Urbanization is one of the activities that affect aquatic ecosystems due to hydrological modification, disposal of waste, industrial activities, and clearing of riparian vegetation [[6], [7], [8]]. The most notable impact on biodiversity and river water quality worldwide is the change in physical habitat due to channelization [9,10]. Channelization is commonly practiced throughout the world for flood control, urban development, transportation, navigation, and other purposes [10].

Many studies usually have used macroinvertebrates to assess aquatic environment quality. Macroinvertebrate assemblages in freshwater ecosystems integrate the human impacts in the watershed [11]. Their occurrence, abundance, and richness measurement scores indicate the status of freshwater quality [12]. These metrics of macroinvertebrates are often recommended for the assessment of freshwater ecosystem conditions because of macroinvertebrates’ ecological roles in aquatic ecosystems and their easily traceable characteristics [11,13]. However, the combination of biological assessment with physicochemical water quality assessment is the most appropriate means of detecting effects on aquatic systems. It can indicate cumulative physical, chemical and biological impacts of adverse activities [14].

Understanding the ecology of streams has become a subject of key importance for the maintenance and rehabilitation of aquatic ecosystem resources worldwide. The degradation of stream water quality cannot end up by impacting the stream ecosystem alone as streams join the other freshwater resources including wetlands, lakes and rivers. Human activities are especially intense in urban areas and threaten stream ecosystems. These activities often have unintended and undesirable consequences in streams such as reducing biodiversity, altered ecosystem functioning, and losses of ecosystem services [3,15,16]. As the impact of anthropogenic activities on the water resource ecosystem becomes increasingly severe and complex, it demands sustainable water resource management for both human and ecosystem health stability. Specifically linked with urbanization, protecting the ecological quality of streams is challenging and a growing problem whose solutions will require extensive effort and investigation.

In the urbanization process, a significant portion of the stream around the world became channelized [17]. This causes significant changes in the physical and biological processes of the aquatic ecosystem [10]. The changes affect large bottom invertebrates, fish, and aquatic plants or riparian vegetation, (from algae and large plants to riparian shrubs and trees). The effect is also for terrestrial animals including amphibians, reptiles, birds, and mammals [10,18,19].

In natural settings, there exists a dynamic and distinct boundary between the channel and the surrounding terrestrial areas, known as the transition zone. This zone is a diverse habitat capable of supporting a wide range of ecological entities. Moreover, it is also a source of energy inputs from terrestrial subsidies for aquatic populations. However, homogenization of habitat allows domination of a certain ecological characteristic [20]. Channelizing the natural steam process can affect bankside vegetation and disturb in-stream which changes the bank and stream profile respectively. Changes in bankside profiles include events such as the loss of vegetation and habitats of birds and other inhabitants. Bankside vegetation is a chief energy source in the form of allochthonous organic matter including tree leaves and associated organisms. Losing this important ecological component will reduce the aquatic energy flow [21,22]. On the other hand, altering the stream profile, such as depth, width, and straightening, affects the stability of the aquatic environment. The subsequent changes in sediment accumulation level, flow velocity, etc. will affect naturally existing and stable aquatic organisms such as aquatic plants which are sources of energy for other creatures including macroinvertebrates and fish [21,23]. Thus, the channelizing natural stream is connected to the energy flow of the aquatic ecosystem. It also affects the physicochemical characteristics of stream water. For instance, accumulation of sediment from bankside erosion and accumulation of sediment due to widening of the channel and subsequent lower flow velocity can be a continuous source of pollutants that can alter the physicochemical quality of stream water thereby affecting the aquatic organisms including macroinvertebrates [21].

The decline in water quantity and quality has led to water crises, ecosystem threats, diminished quality of life, and reduced ecosystem services [24]. Several studies have been conducted on the Awetu stream before the channelization project and on other rivers and streams around the city and in Ethiopia but no evidence was found on the effect of habitat alteration due to channelization on macroinvertebrate assemblages and water quality in Ethiopian streams, unlike in other countries [10,17,25]. Due to the differences in climatic conditions, urban stream responses may vary across physiographic regions. Thus, this study aims to generate evidence from a tropical climate stream of Ethiopia on.

Materials and methods

Study design, period, and area description

A cross-sectional study was conducted in April 2022 on the Awetu stream in Jimma City which is located in Southwestern Ethiopia in Oromia regional state. The regional climate varies from tropical hot and semi-arid in the lowlands to temperate and wet in the highlands [26]. The area is well known for its coffee plantation [27] where nearly half of the country's remaining natural forests are found [28]. Jimma City is 352 km far from the capital, Addis Ababa. Geographically, it is located between 7°39′ N to 7°41′N and 36°49′ E to 36°50′ E [24] and lies at an altitude between 1740 and 1760 m above sea level [29]. The Awetu stream runs through the city, flowing southward, and is vital to the daily lives of the city's residents and the surrounding communities [5,30]. Awetu stream channelization started in 2020 and covers 1020 m of the stream length (Fig. 1). Minimizing flooding hazards that occur on the streamside surface, promoting streamside runoff protection, making streamside open spaces for public recreational places and economic income generation was the purpose of the channelization [31].

Map of the study area with sampling sites along the segments of Awetu stream in Jimma City, April 2022.

Sample site selection

Data were collected from a total of 21 sampling sites in April 2022: eight from upstream, eight from the channelized and five from downstream reaches of the Awetu stream. All these sites are situated within the city stretch where the influence of anthropogenic activities on the stream is almost similar regardless of the channelization project. The upstream and downstream sites are located within the un-channelized segment of the stream (Fig. 1).

Macroinvertebrate collection and identification

Macroinvertebrates were collected at each of the 21 sampling sites using a rectangular kicking net (15 × 25 × 40 cm) with a mesh size of 300 μm. The collection was made for 10 min along a 10 m reach [32]. The substrate was agitated by the foot to dislodge and collect the benthic macroinvertebrates. Macroinvertebrates sampled in the field were collected into vials containing 80 % ethanol, labelled, and then transported to the laboratory for identification and enumeration. Macroinvertebrates were identified at the laboratory to family level using a stereomicroscope and an identification key [33].

Water sample collection and analysis

Water temperature, dissolved oxygen (DO), electrical conductivity (EC), and pH were measured on-site using a multi-probe meter (HQd40 single-input Multi-Parameter Digital meter). Turbidity was also measured onsite using a turbidity meter (EUTECH TN). Other parameters we measured included five-day biological oxygen demand (BOD5), chemical oxygen demand (COD), total suspended solids (TSS), total hardness (TH), calcium hardness, magnesium hardness, chloride (Cl−), nitrate (NO3−) and orthophosphate (PO43−) concentrations. These parameters were not measured on-site, so a water sample with a 2-L plastic container was collected from each site. Samples were labelled, stored in an icebox (≤4 °C) and transported to the Laboratory of Environmental Health Sciences and Technology, Jimma University. BOD5, COD, TSS, TH, calcium hardness, magnesium hardness, Cl−, NO3− and PO43− were measured in the laboratory using the kit method and spectrophotometers (HACH DR5000) following procedures set for each parameter according to the American Public Health Association [34].

Habitat condition assessment

The stream's physical habitats were observed and identified at each sampling site. The data sheet for assessing the habitat conditions of the stream was created following the standard method outlined in the USEPA Rapid Bioassessment Protocol, applicable to streams and wadeable rivers [13]. Habitat condition assessment involved rating 10 habitat parameters: Epifaunal substrate/available cover, pool substrate characterization, pool variability, sediment deposition, channel flow status, channel alteration, channel sinuosity, bank stability, vegetative protection, and riparian vegetative zone width. All the parameters were evaluated and rated on a numerical scale from 0 to 20. The rated total number was used as a score to classify the habitat condition of each site as poor (<60), marginal (60–99), suboptimal (100–159) and optimal (160–200).

In addition, the other habitat characteristics were also assessed at each site. Water flow velocity was measured using a timer watch and leaf, as the time is taken for a leaf to move a 1-m distance. Alternatively, the depths of water and sludge, as well as the stream width, were measured using a dipstick and a tape measure, respectively. Latitude and longitude were measured at each site using a Garmin GPS reading.

Data analysis

Diversity indices such as family abundance, family richness, Shannon diversity index (H′) [35], Simpson index (1-D) [36], and Margalef diversity index [37] and biotic indices such as family biotic index (FBI), Biological Monitoring Working Party (BMWP) and functional feeding group were analyzed to compare the macroinvertebrate assemblages along the stream segments. Diversity indices were analyzed using the past Paleontological Statistics software package for education (Past 4.03). FBI was calculated following Hilsenhoff's (1988) [38] estimation. To evaluate the water quality, biological indices reflecting the sensitivity of various macroinvertebrate families to water contamination were utilized. The BMWP scores were calculated by adding the individual scores of all indicator organisms at a family level. The macroinvertebrates were identified at a family level and then each family was allocated a score between 1 and 10 [39]. A feeding group was assigned for each macroinvertebrate family following Barbour et al. (1999) [13]. Microsoft Excel was used to calculate the metrics. As the data is not normally distributed, a non-parametric ANOVA test, Kruskal Wallis H test combined with Dunn's post hoc pairwise test was performed at a significance level of 0.05 to evaluate the statistical variation of macroinvertebrate metrics and environmental variables across different segments of the Awetu stream.

Multivariate statistical analyses were used to determine the relationship between macroinvertebrate taxa and environmental variables. The macroinvertebrate and water quality data except pH were standardized and log transformed log10(x+1) prior to analysis to obtain homogeneity of variance. Detrended correspondence analysis (DCA) was applied using CANOCO 4.5 [40] to determine the appropriate response model (linear or unimodal) for both species and the environmental data. Since DCA yielded gradient lengths greater than three standard deviations, Canonical Correspondence Analysis (CCA) was used to investigate the relationship between macroinvertebrate taxa and measured environmental variables. Spearman rank order correlation was performed for the water quality data to examine the extent of correlation. If variables showed a high correlation (p < 0.05 and Spearman coefficient greater than or equal to 0.75), only one was included in the analysis. Following that temperature, calcium and magnesium hardness, chloride, TSS, nitrate, and turbidity were excluded from the ordination analysis. The eigenvalues and species-environment correlations significance were tested using Monte Carlo tests with 999 permutations.

Results

Macroinvertebrate abundances and richness

A total of 6482 individual macroinvertebrate taxa belonging to 24 families and 12 orders were collected from the 21 sampling sites. The most dominant families were Caenidae and Chironomidae, which accounted for 4077 (62.90 %) and 977 (15.07 %) respectively. The richest order was Hemiptera consisting of five families with a relative abundance of 20.83 %. Belostomatidae was the most frequently occurring family in this order.

Of the total individual macroinvertebrates, 3157 (52.66 %, 8 sites) individual macroinvertebrates (representing 21 families and 10 orders), 898 (14.98 %, 5 sites) individual macroinvertebrates (representing 12 families and 9 orders) and the remaining 1940 (32.36 %, 8 sites) individual macroinvertebrates (representing 14 families and 9 orders) were collected from the upstream, downstream and channelized segments of the Awetu stream respectively (Table 1).

Macroinvertebrate metrics

The value of family richness, BMWP, and %EOT recorded ranged from 3 to 16, 10–69, and 16.66–66.66 respectively. The upstream was the segment of the stream where the higher family richness (11.38), BMWP (52.13), Margalef index (1.78) and percent of sensitive taxa EOT (44.90) were recorded. Among the macroinvertebrate metrics, family richness, Margalef index, BMWP, %EOT and all functional feeding groups except %scraper showed mean variation between or among the stream segments. Besides, the variation was insignificant for FBI, Shannon index (H’), Simpson index (1-D), and %scraper (Table 2).

Water quality parameters

The highest average concentration values of DO (6.21 mg/L) and Nitrate (0.62 mg/L) were measured at the upstream sites. Whereas the highest average value of turbidity (131.60 NTU), temperature (27.30 °C), EC (215.66 μS/cm), BOD5 (32.44 mg/L), COD (41 mg/L), total suspended solid (57.84 mg/L) and chloride (22.59 mg/L) were measured at the downstream sampling site. Whereas the highest average values of total hardness (88.75 mg/L) and orthophosphate (4.05 mg/L) were measured at the channelized sampling sites (Fig. 2).

Boxplots show the water quality parameters; a) total hardness, b) EC, c) turbidity, d) temperature, e) DO, f) PO43− g) chloride, h) BOD5, i) pH and j) TSS along the different stream segments of the Awetu stream, April 2022.

A statistically significant variation was observed among the stream segments in the concentration of total hardness, electrical conductivity, temperature, DO, BOD5, and chloride. On the other hand, no significant difference was recorded in water pH, TSS, PO43− and turbidity across the Awetu stream segments.

Habitat conditions

The habitat conditions score measured varied considerably among the stream segments (upstream, downstream and channelized). Most of the upstream reach sites had a total habitat condition score greater than 100 and belongs to suboptimal habitat. Most of the downstream sampling sites had total habitat condition ranges from 60 to 99 and thus, were categorized as marginal habitat. Alternatively, sites in the channelized stream segment fall under the poor habitat condition category where a below 60 score value was recorded (Fig. 3).

Mean and standard deviation (SD) of habitat conditions assessment score along the different stream segments of the Awetu stream, April 2022.

The highest stream width average value (17.32 m) was observed in the channelized segment. The finding indicated a significantly wider stream width in channelized stream segment sites compared to upstream and downstream segments (p < 0.05). However, the lowest sludge depth average value (0.11 m) was recorded in the upstream stream segment while the highest average value (0.56 m) was observed in the channelized segment sites. The sludge depth variation across the stream segments was statistically significant (p < 0.05). On the other hand, the lowest average stream depth (0.41 m) and water flow velocity (0.27 m) were recorded in the channelized segment. There was no statistically significant difference detected in stream depth and flow velocity among the stream segments (p > 0.05).

Relationship between macroinvertebrates taxa and environmental variables

The first and the second canonical axes explained 32.3 % and 17.7 % of the total variation in the macroinvertebrate taxa data, respectively. The eigenvalues of the first two axes were 0.20 and 0.11, respectively. The species-environment correlation of the first and the second axis was statistically significant in the Monte Carlo permutation test (p < 0.05). In this ordination, the macroinvertebrate-environment correlation of the first and second axes was 0.977 and 0.897, respectively. The CCA tri-plot separated sites into the corresponding stream segments. The first axis of the ordination revealed a gradient primarily associated with EC, total hardness, and flow velocity. The channelized segment of the stream was characterized by higher EC, total hardness, sludge depth, PO43−, stream width and lower stream depth and flow velocity in terms of environmental variables. Chironomidae, Lumbriculidae and Culicidae were the most encountered taxa in the channelized segment of the stream (Fig. 4).

Discussion

The present study showed that a higher diversity of macroinvertebrate taxa was recorded in the upstream sites of the stream compared with the downstream and channelized segments. The diversity indices such as the Shannon index (H′), Simpson index (1-D), and Margalef index were higher in the upstream segment. The increase in diversity correlates with the increased health of the assemblage and suggests that niche space, habitat, and food source are adequate to support the survival and proliferation of many species [13]. The upstream sampling sites had a natural channel, riparian vegetation and high substratum heterogeneity compared with downstream and channelized sites. The undisturbed habitat supports a high diversity of species [13]. The downstream sites' macroinvertebrate taxa diversity was not significantly different from the channelized segment. However, the lower average value of the Shannon index (H’), Simpson index (1-D) and Margalef index were recorded in the channelized segment. Our finding is in agreement with the other study [9], in which the Shannon index and richness of sensitive taxa were lower for channelized sites than the un-channelized ones.

The lowest family richness was recorded in the channelized stream segment which is significantly lower as compared to what is recorded in the upstream but not the downstream. The variation from the upstream could be attributed to the instability of the habitat condition [41]. Human influences and habitat conditions have affected the stream water quality and the distribution of macroinvertebrates [42]. Unstable aquatic environments are marked by the presence of tolerant invertebrates and tend to have low biodiversity [16]. The current finding is consistent with the previous studies [10,[43], [44], [45]] that reported benthic macroinvertebrates were considerably richer in the family in un-channelized segments than channelized ones and habitat diversity is minimal in the channelized segment [10,44]. Channelization changes habitat conditions, reduced habitat variability and streams invertebrate drift densities, resulting in taxonomic shifts and biodiversity loss [46,47]. In addition, the loss of protected vegetation increases stream bank erosion and sediment transport to streams [4]. Increased sedimentation or erosion rates due to channel instability can replace existing substrates with more uniform substrates that do not promote diversified colonization of aquatic invertebrates [20,47,48]. Eroded sediment may deposit in new areas, covering benthic macroinvertebrates or altering the instream environment [10].

BMWP indicated the variability between the un-channelized (upstream and downstream) and channelized stream segments. The percentage of EOT was not significantly different between upstream and channelized segments of the stream. This finding does not corroborate the findings of the other, in which the %EOT was lower in degraded water habitats [49]. This could be explained by the fact that short-term effect of the Awetu stream channelization. This study was conducted within a short period just after the channelization project activities and its effect on %EOT could be seen in the long run.

The findings of this study revealed that among functional feeding groups, the %predator macroinvertebrate family was higher in the upstream and downstream segments relative to the channelized segment of the stream (p < 0.05). This might be attributed to the unsuitable change of bank habitat. For instance, dragonfly larvae are climbers that cannot firmly climb on the substrate or the stem of macrophytes. The channelization apparently changed this condition for these predators. The absence of prey communities in the channelized sites available for the predators due to vegetation clearance and anoxic conditions where the prey are solely dependent on water-dissolved oxygen not from the air might also be another reason [20]. Alternatively, the reduction in predators in the channelized section most likely resulted from diminished lateral subsides from the aquatic environment. Lateral interactions connected aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems, supporting and maintaining biodiversity in both habitats. Stream channelization has isolated rivers from their riparian areas in many places, resulting in the loss of biotic diversity [4,20,47]. Our finding is similar to other studies [20] that revealed a significant reduction in predator families in the channelized stream reaches and a higher abundance and richness of predators in the un-channelized reaches.

The percentage of collector/gatherer families unlike predators were highly abundant in the channelized segment of the stream as compared to the other stream reaches. The percentage of collector/filterer families was higher in the upstream and channelized segments of the stream (Table 2). The imbalance in the functional feeding groups in the channelized segment might be attributed to the lack of food sources, such as faecal particles, leaf pact, organic colloid flocculates, and particulate detritus. The loss of riparian vegetation, lateral interaction and different substrate composition increasingly create uniform habitat conditions. As a result, it affects the food webs of the lotic system and causes changes in the distribution and relative abundance of functional feeding groups. A river is an open watercourse ecosystem that continuously interacts with its banks and flows from its source to its mouth. The change in physical environmental conditions such as the width, depth, water flow characteristics, temperature, and complexity of the water can change the overall system and affect the food webs [50]. Without relatively stable food dynamics, an imbalance in functional feeding groups will result and reflect stressed habitat conditions [13]. The higher %collector/gatherer macroinvertebrate families at the channelized segment could be attributed to the instability of habitat conditions that increase sedimentation. They use modified mouth parts to sieve or collect fine small particles (organic matter) that accumulate on the stream bottom. The accumulation of this fine particle and sludge was one of the characteristics of the channelization process (Fig. 4) which might support the collector/gatherer [51].

Regarding water quality, most of the parameters measured at the upstream segment showed a significant variation from the other (p < 0.05). However, most water quality parameters such as chloride, total hardness, EC and temperature at the downstream segment did not significantly vary from the channelized segment. This might be attributed to the effect of channelization on the downstream segment. Stream channelization not only affects the channelized stream section, but it also influences the downstream reaches, sometimes large parts of a whole stream system. The erosion from the channelized section is transported downstream as far as the stream power allows [47].

The CCA tri-plot separated channelized sampling sites from other sampling sites (Fig. 4). Pollution-tolerant families; such as Chironomidae, Lumbriculidae and Culicidae were encountered in the channelized sites where the influences of EC, total hardness, PO43−, sludge depth, stream width and stream depth, flow velocity and DO were higher. Among the water physicochemical variables, DO, EC, total hardness, and PO43− were important factors that influence the abundance of macroinvertebrates. The higher DO in the upstream segment may be attributed to the presence of riparian vegetation and lower temperatures. The lower values of DO observed in downstream and channelized sampling sites were most likely a consequence of the clearance of riparian vegetation that increased the water temperature. The level of DO varies depending on factors including water temperature and the loss of waterside vegetation due to channelization resulting in reduced shade and less coverage from sunlight which can increase water temperatures. Temperature affects the solubility of oxygen in water, a higher temperature reduces water's ability to absorb dissolved oxygen [52,53]. Higher EC has been associated with organic waste accumulation which might be attributed to less biological degradation (decomposition process) and indicates poor macroinvertebrate assemblage [49]. Moreover, the comparative lower water flow velocity at the channelized segment may also lead to a decreased water-air oxygen exchange [54].

However, in the downstream case, the lower DO is due to the consumption of available oxygen by high accumulation of organic matter from the channelized section remaining undegraded (organic enrichment) in the presence of degrading organisms where the other physical disturbance (bankside vegetation clearance and in-stream substrate) is lower in this stream segment (downstream). However, this condition might be sustained for a short period and changed afterwards. There will be either a higher depletion of macroinvertebrate diversity (as survival of organisms decreases when DO concentrations are depressed lower than organism-specific critical levels for extended periods of time) or a depletion of organic matter (if it is below the assimilative capacity of biodegradation organisms) [53].

The concentration of orthophosphate and total hardness was higher in the channelized segment of the sampling site. The increase in orthophosphate in the channelized segment may be attributed to the straightening of the channel banks, loss of swamp floodplains and riparian vegetation. Riparian areas improve water quality by acting as natural filters [55]. The presence of vegetation within the channel and along the banks forms buffer zones that mediate nutrient and sediment transport from the land into the stream [4]. The total hardness concentration is related to total dissolved solids and represents the total concentration of Ca2+ and Mg2+ ions [56]. The higher level of hardness in the channelized stream could be attributed to cement-lined channelization and other construction materials. Rock (limestone) is the most common source of Ca2+ and Mg2+ [57].

On the other hand, sludge depth, stream width, stream depth and flow velocity were among the physiographic habitat conditions of the sites that influence the macroinvertebrate assemblage. Macroinvertebrates are directly or indirectly vulnerable to changes in habitat conditions [3]. The channelized sites were characterized by higher sludge depth, stream width and lower stream depth and flow velocity where pollution-tolerant families such as Chironomidae, Lumbriculidae and Culicidae were encountered. The channelization project widened the surface water area, minimized the water depth and increased the chance of sunlight radiation reaching the stream which raises water temperature [58]. This can have negative physical, behavioural and physiological consequences on organisms that adapt to low temperatures [59,60]. Similarly, as indicated in Fig. 2, the habitat condition of channelized segment sites falls under the poor category. The poor habitat condition and water quality might be contributing to the lower number of taxa in the channelized site. Physical habitat conditions such as vegetation protections, substrate composition and turbulence affect macroinvertebrate assemblage [25]. This is attributed to a lack of epifaunal substrate/available cover, heavy deposits of sediment that increased bar development, removal of vegetative protection, channel alteration and no riparian vegetation due to channelization. One of the main stressors of aquatic ecosystems is changed habitat structure [13]. Degradation of habitat occurs due to changes in hydrological flow, changes in the energy base of the system, or direct impacts on the physical habitat structure. Channelization can modify channel parameters such as length, width, depth, slope, runoff, sediment size, or sediment runoff resulting in unstable stream systems. These unstable stream systems result in accelerated loss of streamside habitats and changing aquatic habitats [9,18,47].

Conclusion

In conclusion, macroinvertebrate assemblage, water quality and habitat condition showed variation among the segments of the Awetu stream. Channelization changes the habitat conditions of the stream (both in-stream and bankside conditions) resulting in decreased macroinvertebrate assemblage and water quality degradation. Upstream sites were found to have relatively better macroinvertebrate assemblages, better water quality and good habitat conditions than downstream and channelized sampling sites. The downstream sites had a lower macroinvertebrate assemblage, degraded habitat conditions, and poor water quality than the upstream sites. The channelized segment was found the poorest habitat, with poor macroinvertebrate assemblages and water quality. Thus, in the context of small urban streams in Ethiopia, channelization has an effect on changing environmental conditions such as alteration of habitat (bankside and in-stream beds) and water quality which in turn have an impact on the survival and proliferation of aquatic biological community (macroinvertebrate assemblage).

The alteration of habitat conditions due to channelization poses significant effects on the Awetu stream in the channelized segment. The Jimma City municipality should restore and revegetate the riparian floodplain in the channelized segment of the Awetu stream. In this regard, it is recommended to plant native plant species at the streamside within the channel. It enables the benthic macroinvertebrates to colonize the habitats and balance/restore the natural ecological process. It can also reduce the stream width and stagnation of water thereby reducing disease vector breeding like mosquitoes. For sustainable management and health of Awetu and other streams, the short and long-term effects of the channelization project on the environment and human health should be considered.
