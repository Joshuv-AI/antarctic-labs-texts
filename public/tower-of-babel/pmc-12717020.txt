Blue Toe Syndrome as a Rare Complication of Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm: Case Report
Authors: Javad Salimi, Ghazal Dahaghin, Pejman Pourazari, Afshin Bighamian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAbdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) is a vascular disease that often remains asymptomatic until rupture or the occurrence of complications. The mortality rate of rupture exceeds 80%. Most AAAs are discovered incidentally; rare presentations may occur due to peripheral embolization from intra‐aneurysmal thrombi. One of the manifestations is blue toe syndrome. It is painful cyanotic toes with preserved peripheral pulses. We report the case of a 67‐year‐old man with a history of ischemic heart disease, smoking, and opium addiction who presented with bilateral toe pain, numbness, and cyanosis without abdominal symptoms or claudication. peripheral pulses were present in the physical examination. The laboratory tests excluded vasculitis and hypercoagulable states. CT angiography demonstrated a large (75 mm) infrarenal AAA with mural thrombosis, specifying the source of emboli. The patient underwent successful endovascular aortic aneurysm repair (EVAR) with adjunctive coil embolization of the inferior mesenteric artery. Postoperatively, limb perfusion was restored, symptoms resolved, and the patient was discharged on dual antiplatelet and statin therapy. At 12‐month follow‐up, he was symptom‐free with no evidence of ischemia or endoleak. This case explains an uncommon presentation of AAA as blue toe syndrome. Diagnosis of peripheral embolic events as a symptom of AAA is necessary for timely imaging and treatment. Timely diagnosis and intervention prevent ischemic complications and decrease the risk of aneurysm rupture. EVAR is a safe and effective intervention, especially in cases with significant comorbidities.

Introduction

Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) is one of the most important vascular diseases in the elderly. Epidemiological studies have reported a prevalence of AAA of up to 8% in men over 65 years of age, and these aneurysms often remain asymptomatic until complications develop; AAA rupture is a critical condition with a mortality rate of more than 80% [1]. The main risk factors for the occurrence of AAA include advanced age (especially over 65 years), male gender, a history of smoking, and atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases (such as coronary heart disease) [2]. In particular, smoking is known to be the strongest modifiable risk factor, and a history of coronary artery disease is also associated with an increased risk of AAA due to underlying atherosclerosis [2].

Most AAAs are asymptomatic until they become serious and are discovered incidentally. However, unusual clinical presentations can also be a clue to the diagnosis. Blue toe syndrome is a rare manifestation of AAA that presents as sudden cyanosis and pain in one or more toes due to peripheral embolism [3, 4]. In this case, plaques or thrombi within the aneurysm lead to arterial microemboli, causing digital ischemia. Although the initial presentation of AAA with severe limb ischemia (including blue toe syndrome) is rare, the observation of such a finding in an elderly patient with cardiovascular risk factors should raise the alarm for a proximal embolic source, such as an occult aortic aneurysm [5]. There have been reports of small AAAs with intra‐aneurysmal thrombi presenting as the first symptom of peripheral emboli and necrosis of the toes [5]. Although this complication is uncommon, its early recognition is important because peripheral perfusion may be a sign of aneurysmal occlusion and the potential risk of rupture [6]. Therefore, in elderly patients with acute pain and cyanosis of the toes (with preserved peripheral pulses), cholesterol embolism syndrome and AAA should be considered as differential diagnoses.

The choice of the AAA treatment method depends on the size of the aneurysm, the patient's clinical condition, and the presence of complications. In the past decades, the standard treatment for AAA was open surgery (traditional aneurysm repair with graft placement). Today, with the advancement of interventional technologies, endovascular aortic aneurysm repair (EVAR) has become popular as a less invasive option [7]. Studies and comparative trials have shown that EVAR significantly reduces mortality and short‐term perioperative complications compared with open surgery in anatomically eligible patients [7, 8]. For example, the 30‐day mortality rate after EVAR has been reported to be about 1.4%, which is significantly lower than traditional open surgery (about 4.2%) [7]. EVAR also does not require a large abdominal incision and is performed through a small incision in the groin area, and has a shorter recovery period with less hospitalization [9]. These advantages are especially important in older or high‐risk patients and have made EVAR the preferred treatment option for many unruptured AAAs [7, 9]. However, it should be noted that EVAR requires lifelong follow‐up to monitor graft leakage and other complications, and not all patients are suitable candidates for EVAR due to their anatomical conditions. However, in the present patient, who had a large AAA with peripheral emboli, EVAR as a definitive treatment was able to eliminate the source of embolism and prevent aneurysm rupture, which led to complete improvement in the patient's symptoms.

Case Presentation

Case History/Examination

A 67‐year‐old man of Persian ethnicity was referred to the vascular surgery clinic with the chief complaint of bilateral pain, numbness, and blue discoloration of the legs for 1 month. (Figure 1) He had not had a history of intermittent claudication, pain at rest, or abdominal pain before his symptoms. He had a history of ischemic heart disease and percutaneous coronary intervention 7 years ago. He was on Aspirin 80 mg daily and atorvastatin 40 mg daily. He had a 25‐pack‐year history of smoking and 15 years of opium addiction. In the physical examination, the vital signs were stable. Abdominal examination was normal, and there was no sign of a pulsatile mass. Bilateral proximal and distal lower extremities pulses were present and symmetric.

Distal digital ischemia (blue toe syndrome).

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

Differential Diagnosis

Emboli, Burger's disease, vasculitis, Raynaud's phenomenon, Hypercoagulable states.

CT angiography was done from the aortic root to the bilateral distal lower limbs. 75 mm AAA with 18 mm infrarenal landing zone. There was no significant aortic angulation. The infra‐mesenteric artery was patent. (4.6 mm). Intraluminal diameter of the distal aorta was 26 mm. The right common iliac artery was 16 mm in the proximal segment and 20 mm distally. The left common iliac artery was moderately tortuous and 20 mm at the iliac bifurcation. Access vessels were 9‐10 mm bilaterally (Figure 2).

CT angiography of the aortic abdominal aneurysm (infrarenal aneurysm sac).

Based on the patient's history, physical examination, and CT angiography report, it was deduced that the blue toes were a result of ischemia due to emboli sent from the AAA. Due to normal lab tests, the hypercoagulable states and vasculitis were ruled out. Based on history and physical examination, Raynaud's phenomenon was ruled out. The CT angiography excluded Burger's disease.

The vascular team designed Standard EVAR from the renal arteries to the iliac bifurcations as the optimal treatment. The left common iliac artery required a femoral brachial wire for device passage. The infrarenal artery was coiled to reduce the risk of T2E post‐EVAR (Figure 3).

Endovascular aneurysm repair.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

After the procedure, the patient stayed in the ICU for 24 h, then was transferred to the vascular surgery ward, where the blue discoloration, pain, and numbness resolved within 3 days (Figure 4). He was discharged in good condition with aspirin 80 mg daily, atorvastatin 40 mg daily, and clopidogrel 75 mg daily.

Symptom resolution 3 months after EVAR.

The follow‐up sessions were 1, 3, 6, and 12 months, including clinical evaluation and CT angiography. He appeared well in these sessions and had no complaints, which were signs of emboli (the source of emboli was resolved) (Figure 5).

Discussion

This report describes a rare case of the initial presentation of AAA as blue finger syndrome. Peripheral manifestations due to aortic emboli are very uncommon in patients with AAA and are often not the first sign of the disease [5]. Most patients with AAA are asymptomatic or present with symptoms such as abdominal pain, back pain, or pressure on adjacent limbs, and the presentation of lower extremity digital ischemia (e.g., cyanosis and necrosis limited to the toes) is uncommon [10]. In blue finger syndrome, the large peripheral arteries are usually patent, and the peripheral pulses are intact, but microscopic emboli of proximal origin cause occlusion of the small arteries of the fingers and local ischemia [11]. This can mislead the physician and obscure the source of the embolus. In the present patient, a 67‐year‐old man with a history of severe atherosclerosis (coronary artery disease, smoking, and opium use), such an unusual presentation was observed, and the presence of cardiovascular risk factors made it necessary to carefully investigate the source of the embolus. Bilateral patients' symptoms are more suspicious for a proximal source of embolism, such as the aorta, while in unilateral symptoms, iliac or other lower limb arterial involvement is considered.

Correct diagnosis of the source of the embolus is of vital importance in such cases. Case reports have shown that peripheral emboli can originate from parietal thrombi within aortic aneurysms and cause acute ischemia of the fingers [5, 11]. In these patients, if the underlying cause (AAA) remains unknown, continued embolism can lead to loss of limb tissue (such as necrosis and gangrene) [5]. Therefore, after the observation of blue finger syndrome in a high‐risk individual, careful imaging is necessary to search for an aortic aneurysm or other sources of embolism [11]. In our patient, despite the absence of obvious clinical signs in the abdomen, CT angiography was performed correctly and identified the AAA as the source of embolism. CT angiography is the method of choice to confirm AAA and determine its dimensions, the presence of intra‐aneurysmal thrombosis, and involvement of branch vessels, and is especially important in symptomatic cases [10]. In this patient, CT angiography led to a definitive diagnosis of AAA and allowed for appropriate treatment planning.

Treatment options for AAA include traditional open surgery and endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR). Over the past decades, EVAR has been increasingly used in older, high‐risk patients due to its less invasive nature [10]. In the present patient, who had a history of coronary artery disease and smoking, the risk of open surgery was considered high, and EVAR would have been a more desirable option. Studies have shown that EVAR is associated with lower mortality and perioperative complications than open surgery [12]. Patients have a shorter recovery period after EVAR and require less long‐term intensive care [10]. On the other hand, open surgery is considered a more definitive treatment and requires fewer long‐term follow‐up procedures. Long‐term follow‐up has shown that although the overall survival rate at 5–6 years is not significantly different between EVAR and open surgery, EVAR is associated with a higher rate of aneurysm recurrence or the need for reintervention [12]. However, in high‐risk patients (such as the present patient), the benefits of EVAR outweigh its disadvantages, and it is considered the strategy of choice [10]. In this case, the treatment decision was made with an emphasis on EVAR to prevent further embolism and reduce perioperative risks, which seems reasonable given the patient's condition.

To better understand the importance of the issue, it is useful to review similar cases in the scientific literature. In the study by Popov et al., two patients with a similar condition were reported, whose initial presentation was severe toe ischemia [5]. Their first patient was a 61‐year‐old man with rest pain in the toes and bilateral cyanosis of the feet, who, despite normal peripheral pulses, had a palpable pulsatile mass in the abdomen [5]. On imaging, a relatively small AAA (37 mm) with irregular parietal thrombosis was seen, and the patient underwent open surgery and aorto‐iliac bypass [5]. Despite the measures taken, the severity of the ischemia forced the amputation of the fifth toe on the left foot [5]. The second patient in that study was a 77‐year‐old woman who presented with severe leg pain, and a pulsatile abdominal mass was found on examination [5]. On examination, a 40 mm AAA with mobile parietal thrombosis was diagnosed, and the patient underwent emergency open repair (aorto‐bifemoral reconstruction) [5]. These two reports emphasize the rarity of this presentation and recommend prompt aggressive treatment in the event of peripheral embolism from an AAA, regardless of aneurysm size [5]. Another similar case was published by Tartari et al. in 2019, in which their patient was an 82‐year‐old man with a chronic toe ulcer [11]. He had a history of hyperlipidemia and hypertension, and investigations revealed a previous stable AAA with intra‐aneurysm thrombosis [11]. In that patient, due to his advanced age and comorbidities, unlike the previous two cases, no surgical or endovascular intervention was performed, and the patient was discharged with conservative treatment. Comparison of these patients with the present case shows that the common factor in all of them was the presence of risk factors for atherosclerosis (old age, male gender in most cases, smoking or hyperlipidemia, and cardiovascular disease). Also, in all cases, peripheral pulses were preserved, and the main finding was limited to cyanosis and necrosis of the extremities, which highlights the importance of paying attention to seemingly minor symptoms in high‐risk patients [11]. The response to treatment also depended on the patient's systemic status and the size of the aneurysm; Younger patients recovered with immediate invasive measures (both open and endovascular), while the very elderly patient in the Tartari report practically did not benefit from the intervention due to high risk and remained likely to suffer recurrent embolism [11]. Fortunately, our 67‐year‐old patient was treated promptly with endovascular intervention after the correct diagnosis of AAA and was spared the progression of ischemic lesions and subsequent risks.

Conclusion

This case focuses on considering AAA as a cause of peripheral embolic events like blue toe syndrome. In patients with cardiovascular risk factors, distal ischemia of the limbs with present distal pulses AAA should be considered. Early vascular imaging to rule out aneurysmal disease can prevent emboli. EVAR is a safe and effective option in high‐risk patients. It resolves symptoms rapidly and has favorable long‐term outcomes.
