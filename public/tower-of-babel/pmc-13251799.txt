A low-cost approach to dimple grinding using a rotary tool
Authors: Hideyuki Magara, Daisuke Morikawa, Kenji Tsuda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Conventional transmission electron microscopy (TEM) specimen preparation often relies on expensive commercial dimple grinders. This study evaluates a highly affordable alternative for mechanical thinning, utilizing a consumer-grade rotary tool. This represents a significant cost saving of two orders of magnitude, making TEM preparation much more accessible. The objective is to determine whether such low-cost equipment can produce high-quality samples comparable to those prepared using specialized laboratory devices.The methodology is outlined as follows:A dimple fabrication system, functionally equivalent to conventional commercial products, was developed using an inexpensive rotary tool.TEM specimens were prepared following a dimpling process performed using a standard commercial dimple grinder and the developed rotary tool system (evaluating both low-cost and ultra-low-cost methods).The resulting crystalline quality was quantitatively evaluated using convergent-beam electron diffraction (CBED) and symmetry analysis.Experimental results on single-crystal silicon demonstrate that the proposed low-cost method achieves crystalline quality comparable to the conventional approach. Furthermore, even the ultra-low-cost variation utilizing standard steel bits maintains structural integrity without introducing dislocations or stacking faults. These findings indicate that inexpensive rotary tools offer a practical, highly accessible solution for routine, high-quality TEM specimen preparation.

Background

Common methods for preparing transmission electron microscopy (TEM) samples of inorganic materials include the crushing method, focused ion beam (FIB) milling, and techniques combining mechanical polishing with Ar-ion milling. Compared to FIB milling, Ar-ion milling offers the advantage of providing a wider observable area, which enables a more accurate characterization of the average bulk structure. In this approach, it is standard practice to form a dimple in the specimen after mechanical polishing, typically utilizing a dedicated commercial dimple grinder. However, these commercially available dimple grinders are highly expensive (Costing several tens of thousands of USD). Therefore, this study investigates the feasibility of using a significantly more cost-effective consumer rotary tool (costing only a few hundred USD, excluding the cost of an optical microscope) to fabricate these dimples. This method is considered highly effective for inorganic materials that primarily undergo brittle fracture during the grinding process, such as semiconductors, ceramics, and glass. Additionally, it serves as an accessible entry point for materials researchers seeking to implement TEM observation on an exploratory basis, potentially enabling high-quality specimen preparation with minimal capital investment. Furthermore, even in established microscopy facilities, this readily constructible apparatus can serve as a highly practical contingency solution during temporary constraints, such as unexpected equipment failures, funding gaps, or supply chain delays.

To evaluate this approach, samples were prepared using both a standard commercial dimple grinder and the proposed rotary tool setup. Each specimen was subsequently thinned for TEM observation, and the crystalline quality of the resulting samples was systematically compared.

Method details

Three single-crystal Si specimens with a [001] orientation (2 × 2 mm, Nilaco Co., Ltd.) were prepared. One side of each specimen was left in its as-received, mirror-finished state, while the opposite side was successively polished using abrasive papers (#280, #600, #1200, and #2400; Struers) until the specimen thickness was reduced to approximately 40 μm.

Dimples were then formed on these mechanically polished surfaces under the conditions summarized in Table 1. Dimples were fabricated using two distinct approaches: the conventional method employing a commercial dimple grinder (Model 656, Gatan) and the proposed method utilizing a consumer rotary tool (MM50, PROXXON). For the proposed method, two variations were evaluated: one using the standard stainless-steel bit supplied with the tool (the ultra-low-cost method) and the other using the exact same phosphor bronze/felt wheels as the commercial device (the low-cost method). Because dimple fabrication is performed while the specimen rotates, a eucentric adjustment must be conducted beforehand using an optical microscope (SZ61, Olympus Co.) to perfectly align the center of the specimen with the rotational center of the stage. The fundamental principles of this eucentric adjustment are similar for all methods; Fig. 2 illustrates the specific procedure adapted for the low-cost and ultra-low-cost approaches. First, as shown in Fig. 2(a), the rotational center of the turntable (B09G2R7N1Q, Mcbazel) is determined by applying Thales's theorem using a square. Because the turntable is not perfectly circular, multiple intersecting lines are drawn to accurately identify the center as the convergence point. This identified center is then optically aligned with the center of a cross-reticle (R1220–24, SHIBUYA OPTICAL Co., Ltd.) integrated into the microscope's eyepiece. Next, for height adjustment, a spacer (GFW-303, SUS Corp.) is placed on the turntable Fig. 2(b). Crucially, this spacer must either have a through-hole or be completely transparent to ensure the turntable's center mark remains visible. A glass plate, onto which the specimen is fixed using wax, is then attached to the spacer with double-sided tape Fig. 2(c). Finally, the turntable is rotated to check for any eccentricity, and the glass plate is manually repositioned to precisely align the center of the specimen with the center of the cross-reticle Fig. 2(d). The mechanisms for adjusting the bit rotation speed, the applied load, and the target dimple depth differ significantly between the conventional and proposed methods. The conventional grinder operates at a fixed tool rotation speed of 26 rpm. In contrast, the minimum native rotation speed of the consumer rotary tool is 8000 rpm, which is excessively high and easily causes specimen cracking. To mitigate this, a dimmer (TLC-150, TOYOSTAR Corp.)—functioning as a variable resistor—was connected and set to its maximum resistance, effectively reducing the bit rotation speed to a gentle 65 rpm, closely approximating the conventional conditions. Regarding the applied load, the conventional method utilizes a built-in standard load of 10 g. For the proposed methods, the load applied upon tool contact was finely controlled via the vertical movement of a lab jack (Model 89,289,304, MonotaRO Co., Ltd.) secured with a vise (V-65A, Fujiwara Sangyo Co., Ltd.), and was quantitatively measured using an electronic balance (BEE6002, As ONE). Finally, while the conventional device features built-in numerical control for target depth, the dimple depth in the rotary tool setup was geometrically evaluated and monitored based on the measured dimple diameter (Fig. 3). Method validation

Fig. 1(c), (f), and (h) illustrate the macroscopic appearance of dimples fabricated via the conventional, low-cost, and ultra-low-cost methods, respectively. These images confirm that the low-cost method produced dimples visually comparable to those formed using the conventional approach. In contrast, the dimple generated by the ultra-low-cost method exhibited an irregular shape, primarily due to the radial run-out of the stainless-steel bit. To quantitatively evaluate the quality of the specimens prepared by both the conventional and proposed methods, the samples were further processed for TEM characterization. Final thinning was performed using Ar-ion milling (TEM MiLL Model 1051, Fischione). The ion energy was initially set to 5 kV and was subsequently reduced to 1 kV and then 0.4 kV, with incident angles of 6° and 4° from two opposing ion guns at each respective step.

Crystallinity was examined using a TEM (JEM-2010FEF, 100 kV; JEOL) equipped with a CMOS camera (RIO16; Gatan). For this analysis, the 4D-STEM convergent-beam electron diffraction (CBED) technique [1] was employed, and the symmetry breaking of the resulting CBED patterns was quantified utilizing the Symmetry Breaking Index, S [2]. This index, S, calculates the relative difference between an original CBED pattern and its symmetry-operated counterpart (in this case, a pattern subjected to a mirror operation for the Si [001] orientation). It is defined by the following formula: S = 100 × √[Σ(ΔIi)² / Σ(Ii) ²] (%), where Ii is the intensity of the i th pixel in the original CBED pattern, and ΔIi is the intensity difference at the i th pixel between the original and the symmetry-operated patterns. Consequently, a larger S value implies a greater deviation from ideal symmetry, indicating more severe degradation of the crystalline quality. Each measurement area for the S evaluation was 100 nm², consisting of a 20 × 20 scanning array with a probe step size of 0.5 nm (CBED pattern resolution: 512 × 512 pixels). For each specimen condition, the S value was averaged across 20 distinct regions (Fig. 4).Fig. 4Measurement configuration for calculating the Symmetry Breaking Index (S) using the 4D-STEM technique. The ultra-low-cost method yielded the highest mean S value, signifying the greatest degradation of crystallinity among the tested conditions. Several factors can contribute to the observed symmetry breaking in CBED patterns, including extended crystal defects, residual strain, structural inhomogeneity, and variations in specimen thickness. Regarding thickness, regions within the examined measurement areas were consistently evaluated to be 90–95 nm thick across all processing conditions (Fig. 6). Observations of the area around the hole created by ion milling indicate that the low-cost methods provide a usable thin region comparable in extent to the conventional method, which is sufficiently large for routine TEM observations. Furthermore, within the ultra-low-cost specimen, the thicknesses at the two most spatially separated measurement areas were nearly identical (Fig. 7). Based on the discussion above, it can be concluded that the observed differences in S-values Fig. 5(d) are primarily attributable to the specific dimpling method employed. It is well known that CBED patterns can be affected by internal sample non-uniformity, and local variations in specimen thickness and bending. To minimize these influences, we utilized high-quality semiconductor-grade Si wafers with exceptional crystallinity as starting materials. Furthermore, the specimen thickness prior to ion milling was finished to nearly identical levels as described in the text, and the subsequent ion milling conditions were kept constant across all methods. In each TEM specimen, rather than strictly relying on the physical distance from the hole, S-value measurements were specifically performed in regions exhibiting minimal bend contour contrast, which indicates negligible local strain. Because the physical distance from the hole does not always guarantee identical local conditions due to minute differences in milling behavior, we consider uniform thickness and absent bend contours to be more reliable indicators for fair comparison. Given these controlled experimental parameters, we concluded that the differences in S-values reflect the impact of the different dimpling techniques.

In summary, the proposed low-cost method enables routine TEM specimen preparation with a structural quality substantially comparable to that of conventional methods. While the evaluation of the symmetry breaking index S did detect a slight degradation in crystallinity, it remained minimal. As indicated in Table 1, this subtle degradation is likely attributable to the higher wheel rotation speed and larger applied load used in the low-cost method compared to the conventional approach, which inherently introduces slightly more mechanical strain. Conversely, while no apparent extended defects were observed in the TEM images for the ultra-low-cost method, an unambiguous degradation in localized crystallinity was recorded. This is primarily because the inherent low machining accuracy of the standard steel bit resulted in a substantial radial run-out of approximately 100 μm. This dynamic instability subjected the specimen to repeated, localized mechanical impacts during dimple milling, thereby introducing significantly more residual strain than the low-cost method and ultimately resulting in the lowest observed crystallinity among the tested conditions.

Limitations

This method is exclusively applicable to inorganic materials.
