Bacteroides-derived endocannabinoid-like commendamide attenuates skeletal muscle ferroptosis in vitro: implications for Duchenne muscular dystrophy
Authors: Noemi Di Muraglia, Martina De Vito, Elisabetta Panza, Ester Pagano, Rosa Maria Vitale, Fabiana Piscitelli, Rosaria Villano, Raffaele Capasso, Vincenzo Pota, Caterina Pace, Salvatore Dongiovanni, Vincenzo Di Marzo, Fabio Arturo Iannotti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe gut microbiota regulates skeletal muscle physiology, with an increasingly recognised role in Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD), the most severe X-linked myopathy. Unlike previous studies, we focussed on the genus Bacteroides and its metabolites, assessing their abundance in DMD mice and patients to clarify their potential contribution to the disease.MethodsThe relative abundance of Bacteroides species was analysed in fecal samples from dystrophic mdx mice and DMD patients, compared with age-matched healthy controls, using PCR-based tecniques. Synthetic and analitical chemistry approaches followed by cell-based assays, in silico and bioinformatic analyses, were employed to identify an unknown mechanism of action of the Bacteroides-derived metabolites.ResultsDMD patients and mdx mice exhibited a significant reduction in commensal Bacteroides species, including Bacteroides vulgatus, known producers of SCFAs and commendamide, an endocannabinoid-like molecule with largely uncharacterized biological functions. In skeletal muscles of mdx mice, we observed biochemical features consistent with increased susceptibility to ferroptosis. In murine C2C12 cells and primary human myotubes exposed to the ferroptosis inducer erastin, commendamide conferred significant protective effects, which were further enhanced in the presence of SCFAs. Additionally, we discovered that commendamide acts as an endogenous activator of PPARα and PPARγ, with PPARα preferentially promoting the transcription of the antioxidant genes Gpx4 and Nrf2.ConclusionThese findings provide new insights into the gut–muscle axis in DMD, suggesting that the depletion of Bacteroides vulgatus and its metabolites may contribute to skeletal muscle degeneration. In vitro evidence demonstrates that commendamide, through PPARα signaling; and SCFAs, enhances antioxidant mechanisms. Overall, these results support further investigation of microbiota-derived metabolites as postbiotic candidates for DMD therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08407-4.Keywords: Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD), Ferroptosis, Gut microbiota, Short chain fatty acids (SCFAs), Commendamide, PPAR receptors

Introduction

Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is an X‑linked recessive neuromuscular disorder caused by mutations in the DMD gene, which encodes dystrophin, a cytoskeletal protein essential for maintaining sarcolemmal stability through the dystrophin‑associated protein complex [1, 2]. Loss of dystrophin leads to progressive muscle degeneration due to repeated cycles of myofiber damage, impaired regeneration, and chronic inflammation. In addition to skeletal muscle pathology, DMD is characterized by severe comorbidities, including cardiomyopathy, respiratory insufficiency, gastrointestinal dysfunction, and cognitive impairment, which significantly contribute to disease severity and rapid progression, reducing life expectancy [3–7]. Over the past decades, considerable efforts have been devoted to dystrophin‑restoring strategies, including gene replacement, exon skipping, cell‑based therapies and genome editing approaches [8, 9]. Despite important advances, these strategies face substantial limitations, such as mutation‑specific applicability, immune responses, and long‑term efficacy and safety [10]. Consequently, corticosteroids remain the standard of care, despite the harmful effects of their prolonged use [11]. These limitations highlight the need to identify complementary, non–dystrophin‑restoring mechanisms that may help delay disease progression and improve the quality of life of affected patients.

In this context, increasing attention has been directed toward the gut–skeletal muscle axis, a bidirectional communication system in which gut microbiota modulates muscle metabolism, inflammation, and function. Experimental studies have consistently shown that disruption of the gut microbiome alters muscle mass, fiber composition, mitochondrial activity, and contractile performance, while microbiota restoration can partially rescue these defects [12–16]. Collectively, these findings indicate that microbiota-derived metabolites act as key mediators linking gut ecology to muscle physiology.

Recent evidence suggests that gut microbiota dysregulation may also contribute to DMD. Using the mdx mouse model, we and others have demonstrated that altered gut microbiota composition is associated with reduced production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and exacerbated muscle inflammation and degeneration [17, 18]. Notably, oral supplementation with butyrate or restoration of a eubiotic microbiota improves muscle strength, reduces fibrosis, cardiomyopathy and attenuates immune dysregulation in dystrophic mice [17, 19].

Despite these advances, knowledge concerning specific bacterial taxa and metabolites linking gut dysbiosis to muscle degeneration in DMD remains poorly defined. In the present study, we focused on Bacteroides species, dominant symbionts of the human gut with well-established roles in host metabolic homeostasis [20]. Through targeted microbiota and metabolite analyses, combined with in vitro functional assays, we identify commendamide, a Bacteroides-produced endocannabinoid-like molecule (N-acyl amide), as a regulator of ferroptosis-associated pathways in skeletal muscle cells, providing additional mechanistic insight into a previously unrecognised microbiota–muscle axis.

Materials and methods

Animal model, feces collection and tissue dissection

All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals and were approved by the Italian Ministry of Health and the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC; protocol no. 536/2018). The study complied with Italian legislation (D.L. no. 116 of January 27, 1992) and the relevant European directives (86/609/EEC and 2010/63/EU).

For this study, 5-week-old control (C57BL/10ScSnJ) and dystrophic (C57BL/10ScSn-DMDmdx/J) mice, each weighing approximately 20–25 g, were obtained from Charles River Laboratories (Milan, Italy). Animals were housed in individually ventilated cages under a 12-hour light/dark cycle and had ad libitum access to standard chow (Harlan Teklad) and water for 2 weeks. For fecal sample collection, mice were placed individually into clean, empty cages (without bedding or nesting material) between 7:30 and 8:00 AM, a time when intestinal transit is most active. By the time the last cage was set up, the first animals typically produced fecal pellets. Fresh feces were collected using sterile forceps and immediately stored at − 80 °C until further analysis.

Fecal sample collection from hospitalized patients

Fecal samples were collected from hospitalized patients after obtaining informed consent (study authorization n° AOC/0032057/2021 by the local ethics committee) under sterile conditions to minimize environmental or cross-contamination. When possible, patients were instructed to defecate into a sterile, disposable bedpan or commode liner, taking care to avoid contact with urine. In cases where patients were unable to do so independently, trained clinical staff performed the collection immediately after defecation using sterile gloves and tools. Approximately 5–10 g of stool were transferred into a sterile screw-cap container using a sterile spatula. Samples were labelled with patient ID, date, and time of collection, then stored at 4 °C if processed within 24 h, or frozen at − 80 °C for long-term storage. All equipment and surfaces were disinfected after collection. This procedure was adapted from protocols developed by the Human Microbiome Project [21].

Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis

Fecal samples collected from human individuals and mice were weighed in 1.5 ml Eppendorf tubes, subsequently, the lipid fraction was extracted by following the procedure modified from [22]. Briefly, samples were sonicated and then extracted with chloroform/methanol (2:1, v/v) containing internal deuterated standard for commendamide quantification by isotope dilution (50 pmol for IS, d2 commendamide). Organic phases were collected and dried down under nitrogen. For ULPC-MS/MS analysis each extract was resuspended in 100 µl of MeOH [23].

Chromatographic separations were performed by a Phenomenex Kinetex polar C18 column (50 × 3 mm, 2.6 μm) on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC System. Eluent A: 10 mM ammonium formate in H2O- formic acid 0.1% and eluent B: MeOH/ACN 90:10- formic acid 0.1%. The elution program consisted of a gradient elution from 35% to 65% B in 2 min, followed by an increase to 100% B in 10 min, holding 2 min at 100%B, then returning at 35% in 0.5 min; 2.5 min of a re-equilibration step was included in the program for a total elution time of 17 min. The flow was set at 0.5 ml/min. The injection volume was 2 µl. The autosampler was maintained at 10 °C. Weak needle wash solvent: H2O: ACN (80:20); strong needle wash solvent: H2O: ACN (25:75). Commendamide d0 and IS were eluted at 8.1 min. Mass spectrometry (MS) analysis was carried out by an MRM method developed on API 3200 Triple Quadrupole (ABSciex, Foster City, CA, USA) equipped with a Turbo V Ion Spray Source used in negative-ionization mode. Quantitation was achieved by monitoring the [M-H]- ion at m/z 328.4 (d0 commendamide) and in the first quadrupole and the product ions at m/z 74.1 (quantifier) and m/z 116.1 (qualifier) in the third quadrupole for each precursor ion. For IS, the transition of the precursor ion [M-H]-at m/z 330.0 > 76.0 was considered. The following source parameters were used: curtain gas at 20 psi, ion source gas (GS1) at 45 psi, turbo gas (GS2) at 55 psi, desolvation temperature at 450 °C, collision gas (CAD) at 5 a.u. and ion-spray voltage at − 4500 V. The collision energy (CE), declustering potential (DP), and entrance potential (EP) were optimized for each analyte and the internal standard by infusing the tuning solution (1 µg/ml). The dwell time was set to 50 msec. Analyst software (version 1.5.2; ABSciex) was used for data recording and Multiquant software (version 2.0.2; ABSciex) for quantitative analyses.

Cell culture and reagents

Murine C2C12 myoblasts were purchased from ATCC (cat. no. CRL-1772) and propagated in growth medium (GM) consisting of Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; cat. no. 11995065, Life Technologies) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; cat. no. 16000044, Life Technologies), 5000 U/mL penicillin and 5000 µg/mL streptomycin (cat. no. 15070063, Life Technologies), and 1% L-glutamine (cat. no. A2916801, Life Technologies). To induce differentiation into myotubes, proliferating C2C12 cells were switched to differentiation medium (DM), composed of DMEM supplemented with 0.1% fetal bovine serum (FBS; cat. no. 16000044, Life Technologies), 5000 U/mL penicillin and 5000 µg/mL streptomycin (cat. no. 15070063, Life Technologies), and 1% L-glutamine (cat. no. A2916801, Life Technologies) plus 1% Insulin-Transferrin-Selenium (ITS -G; cat. no. 41400045 Life Technologies) for three days. C2C12 differentiation into myotubes was systematically verified using established molecular and morphological criteria, following the same experimental approach previously described in our published studies [24, 25]. Primary human myoblasts were obtained from Innoprot (cat. no. P10977; Bizkaia, Spain) and cultured in skeletal muscle cell medium (cat. no. P60124), as recommended by the supplier. Cells were routinely screened for mycoplasma contamination using a PCR Mycoplasma Test Kit (cat. no. MP0050). Sodium butyrate (cat. no. 303410); sodium propionate (cat. no. A17440.36); sodium acetate (cat. no. A13184.30) and erastin (cat. no. E7781) were purchased from Thermo Scientific IT.

Cell transfection and luciferase assay

C2C12 cells were seeded in 24-well plates and transfected the following day at approximately 60–70% confluence using Lipofectamine™ 2000 Reagent (cat. no. 11668019; Life Technologies, MI, Italy), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were transfected with one of the following plasmid mixtures: [MIX1]: (a) CMX-Gal4-hPPARα, (b) TK-MH100 × 4-Luc (containing the UAS enhancer), and (c) Renilla luciferase control plasmid (pRL, cat. no. E2231; Promega); [MIX2]: (a) CMX-Gal4-hPPARγ, (b) TK-MH100 × 4-Luc, and (c) Renilla luciferase (pRL); [MIX3]: (a) CMX-Gal4-hPPARδ, (b) TK-MH100 × 4-Luc, and (c) Renilla luciferase (pRL).

On day 3 post-transfection, cells were treated for 24 h with commendamide at 1, 10, or 30 µM, or with either Rosiglitazone (0.1 µM; cat. no. 5325, Tocris, UK) or GW7647 (cat. no. 1677, Tocris, UK) for 18–20 h. Luciferase reporter activity was measured using the Dual-Glo® Luciferase Assay System (cat. no. E2920; Promega) and quantified with a GloMax® Discover microplate reader [26].

RNA and DNA extraction, and quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated from C2C12 and human myoblasts or muscle tissues using TRIzol Reagent (cat. no. 15596018, Life Technologies), treated with DNase I (cat. no. AMPD1, Merck) for 15 min at room temperature, and then quantified by spectrophotometry. Subsequently, the RNA integrity number (RIN) for each RNA sample was analyzed on the Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer. Purified RNA was reverse-transcribed by the use of the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (cat. no. 1708841, Biorad). Reverse transcription of total miRNAs was performed using miScript II RT Kit (cat. no. 218161, Qiagen). Microbial DNA from murine and human fecal samples was extracted and purified using QIAGEN QIAamp® Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit (cat. no. 51604). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was carried out in a real-time PCR system CFX384 (Bio-Rad) using the SYBR Green PCR Kit (cat. no. 1725274, Bio-Rad) and specific primer sequences (supplementary Table 2). Quantitative PCR was performed on independent biological samples 4–5 for each experimental group. Each sample was amplified simultaneously in quadruplicate in a one-assay run with a nontemplate control blank for each primer pair to control for contamination or primer-dimer formation, and the cycle threshold (Ct) value for each experimental group was determined. The housekeeping gene ribosomal protein S16 was used to normalize the Ct values, using the 2^−ΔCt formula. Differences in mRNA content between groups were expressed as 2^−ΔΔCt, as previously described [24].

TBARS and glutathione measurement

Lipid peroxidation was assessed by quantifying thiobarbituric acid-reactive substances (TBARS) using a fluorometric assay kit (Elabscience, cat. # E-BC-K298-F, Ann Arbor, MI, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, biological samples were diluted in PBS (0.01 M, pH 7.4) to fall within the assay’s detection range (0.09–10 µmol/L). A volume of 100 µL of each sample or standard was added to a 96-well plate, followed by 100 µL of the TBA reagent. The mixture was thoroughly vortexed and incubated at 95 °C for 60 min to allow the formation of the fluorescent TBARS–TBA adduct. After cooling the samples to room temperature, fluorescence intensity was measured at an excitation wavelength of 530 nm and emission at 550 nm using a microplate reader.

The total glutathione (GSH) content was measured using the Arbor Assays DetectX Glutathione (GSH) Colorimetric Detection Kit (cat. no. K006-H1), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Samples were prepared by diluting them appropriately in an assay buffer to ensure concentrations were within the assay’s detection range. A standard curve was generated using the provided GSH standard. The assay was performed in a 96-well microplate format, with 50 µL of sample or standard added to each well. Subsequently, 50 µL of the detection reagent was added to each well, and the plate was incubated at room temperature for 20 min. The absorbance was measured at 405 nm using a microplate reader. All measurements were performed in quadruplicate, and appropriate blank and standard controls were included in each assay.

Bioinformatic analysis and gene cloning

Putative PPAR-responsive elements within the 5’-untranslated regions (5’-UTRs) of the murine and human Nrf2 and Gpx4 genes were identified using the online tool TFSEARCH (free version). To functionally assess promoter activity, the 5’-UTR region of the Gpx4 gene (positions – Chr10:79882400 to 79883210) was cloned into the pGL3 promoter vector (cat. no E1761, Promega IT). The construct was then sequence-verified by VectorBuilder, which also provided the cloning service.

Computational methods

Ligands were built with UCSF Chimera 1.17 [27] followed by initial energy minimization (EM) at the molecular mechanics level, using AM1-BC charges. The molecules were then fully optimized using the GAMESS program [28] at the Hartree–Fock level with the STO-3G basis set and subjected to HF/6-31G*/STO-3G single-point calculations to derive the partial atomic charges using the RESP procedure [29]. Docking studies were performed with AutoDock 4.2 [30] by using PPARα and PPARγ crystallographic structures (PDB id: 2P54 for PPARα and 2F4B for PPARγ). Both proteins and ligands were processed with AutoDock Tools (ADT) package version 1.5.6rc1 [30] to merge non-polar hydrogens and calculate Gasteiger charges. Grids for docking evaluation with a spacing of 0.375 Å and 60 × 60 × 60 points, centered on the ligand binding site, were generated using the program AutoGrid 4.2 included in Autodock 4.2 distribution, following the docking protocol already published [26]. The complexes, selected based on binding energy and cluster population, were completed by the addition of all hydrogen atoms, and they underwent energy minimization (EM) and then molecular dynamics (MD) simulations with Amber20 pmemd.cuda module using the 14SB version of the AMBER force field for the protein and gaff parameters or the ligand. To perform MD simulations in the solvent, the complexes were confined in TIP3P water periodic boxes exhibiting a minimum distance between solute atoms and box surfaces of 10 Å, using the tleap module of the AmberTools20 package. The systems were then neutralized by the addition of counterions (Na+) and subjected to 1000 steps of EM, with solute atoms harmonically restrained to their starting positions (Kr = 10 kcal·mol-1·Å-1). Then, 500 ps of restrained MD (Kr = 5 kcal·mol-1·Å-1) at constant pressure was run on each solvated complex, gradually heating the system to 300 K, followed by 500 ps of restrained MD (Kr = 5 kcal·mol-1·Å-1) at constant temperature (300 K) and pressure (1 atm) to adjust system density. Production MD simulations were carried out at constant temperature (300 K) and pressure (1 atm) for 100 ns, with a time step of 2 fs. If rearrangements were observed during MD, the production run was prolonged by an additional 100ns to evaluate possible drifts. Bonds involving hydrogens were constrained using the SHAKE algorithm. The cpptraj module of AmberTools20 and program UCSF Chimera 1.17 were used to perform MD analysis and to draw the figures, respectively.

Procedure for commendamide’s synthesis

Commendamide, in normal or deuterated form, was synthesized as previously reported [31]. This synthetic strategy involved a total of six steps to obtain highly pure commendamide 9 from the commercially available Meldrum’s acid 1 and myristoyl chloride 2 (supplementary Fig. 5).

After the C-acylation of Meldrum’s acid, the crude product 3 was subjected to alcoholysis with methanol at 72–75 °C to give the β-ketoester 4 that was converted into β-hydroxy-ester 5 by reduction with NaBH4 in methanol. Synthetic intermediate 5, isolated by column chromatography in high yield, was submitted to basic hydrolysis of the methyl ester group with NaOH in water to obtain the corresponding β-hydroxy-palmitic acid 6 as a colorless solid. Then, a TBTU-mediated coupling of 6 with glycine methyl ester hydrochloride 7a, in the presence of Et3N and 3Å molecular sieves, provided the glycinate 8a that, after purification by column chromatography, was deprotected by basic hydrolysis to give commendamide 9a. The final product 9a was obtained as a colourless solid and, after crystallization, was collected by filtration (total yield 52% from 1). Deuterated commendamide 9b was produced with the same synthetic strategy described for 9a, by using deuterated glycine methyl ester hydrochloride 7b instead of 7a in the TBTU-mediated coupling reaction. Synthetic procedures and spectroscopic data of all products are reported in the literature [31]. Commendamide used in the experimental assays was in its racemic form.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and correlation analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 10. All datasets were subjected to outlier identification and subsequent removal, using the ROUT method in Prism. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to consider the normal distribution of the data. Normal data were assessed via one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s analysis or T-test to determine statistically significant differences between two or more independent biological groups. All analyses were conducted in a hypothesis-driven manner on predefined sets of bacterial taxa and ferroptosis-related genes. Therefore, no formal correction for multiple comparisons was applied. The reported p-values should be interpreted in the context of these targeted analyses and with appropriate caution.

Results

Fecal Bacteroides species are altered in mdx mice and DMD patients

Our study began with a targeted transcriptomic analysis to assess the relative abundance of specific Bacteroides species, symbiotic gut bacteria known to promote host health [20], in fecal samples from mdx and control mice (n = 6 per group). In dystrophic mice, we found a higher relative abundance of Bacteroides eggerthii and Bacteroides coprophilus. Conversely, the abundance of Bacteroides dorei, Bacteroides uniformis, Bacteroides plebeius, Bacteroides caccae, Bacteroides intestinalis and Bacteroides vulgatus was significantly reduced. No differences were observed for the other species analyzed (Fig. 1A and B).

Subsequently, the same analysis was conducted on faecal samples from 12 healthy individuals and 11 patients with DMD (see supplementary Table 1 for clinical indices). Following normalisation for age, the results revealed a significant reduction in Bacteroides caccae, Bacteroides intestinalis, and Bacteroides vulgatus in DMD patients, mirroring the findings observed in the mouse model. In contrast, the abundance of other Bacteroides species was unchanged (Fig. 1C and D). A Spearman correlation analysis revealed distinct associations between clinical variables and gut microbial composition, with corticosteroid and ACE-inhibitor treatment showing the strongest positive relationships with several Bacteroides species. Notably, patients with dilated cardiomyopathy or gastrointestinal complications exhibited characteristic microbial signatures, including increased B. fragilis, B. uniformis, and B. ovatus, suggesting clinically relevant gut–disease interaction patterns (see supplementary Fig. 1).

Most Bacteroides species are known producers of SCFAs [32]; in this study, we focused specifically on Bacteroides vulgatus due to its distinctive ability to produce additional bioactive metabolites, including the recently identified N-acyl-3-hydroxy-palmitoyl glycine, or commendamide [33] whose involvement in the context of skeletal muscle diseases is unknown. Thus, using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC/MS-MS) (see Materials and Methods for details), we found that commendamide levels tended to be reduced in fecal samples of mdx mice and DMD patients in agreement with the lower abundance of Bacteroides vulgatus and other Bacteroides species (Fig. 1E and F). These findings provide further insights into the involvement of the gut microbiota in DMD, revealing that alterations of specific symbiotic Bacteroides species, including B. vulgatus, are associated with the disease.

Ferroptosis-related genes are dysregulated in skeletal muscles of dystrophic mice and in primary myoblasts from DMD donors

A growing body of research shows that ferroptosis, a form of iron-dependent cell death caused by increased free iron, lipid peroxidation and ROS-induced oxidative stress, is implicated in a variety of human disorders, including those affecting the skeletal muscle system [34–36]. However, there is a significant gap in research on the potential dysregulation of ferroptosis in DMD. To investigate this, we analyzed the expression of key ferroptosis-regulating genes in three different types of skeletal muscles (gastrocnemius, quadriceps femoris anterior - QFA, and soleus) from mdx mice compared to age-matched wild-type controls. Notably, expression of Acls4 (acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4), which encodes an enzyme that facilitates the synthesis of peroxidation-prone phospholipids enriched in polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), was significantly upregulated in all three muscle types of dystrophic mice. In contrast, in the same mice, the expression of Gpx4 (Glutathione Peroxidase 4), a selenoprotein essential for detoxifying lipid peroxides via glutathione (GSH), was significantly reduced in the gastrocnemius but increased in the soleus and quadriceps. The expression of Nrf2 (Nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2), which encodes a transcription factor that activates genes involved in GSH biosynthesis, recycling, and antioxidant defence, showed reduced expression solely in the quadriceps. Finally, the expression of Slc7a11 (Solute Carrier Family 7 Member 11) and Slc3a2 (Solute Carrier Family 3 Member 2), encoding the subunits that together form the Xc⁻ system, a cystine/glutamate antiporter critical for importing cystine required for GSH synthesis, was significantly decreased in the three types of muscles analyzed, except for Slc7a11 in the soleus, which remained unchanged (Fig. 2B).

Ferroptosis in dystrophic muscle and protective effect thereupon of commendamide and SCFAs. (A–C) Balloon plot illustrating the relative expression of ferroptosis-associated genes in 7-week-old wild-type (WT) versus dystrophic (mdx) mice

Based on these findings, we treated C2C12 cells, a commonly used murine immortalised skeletal muscle cell line, with erastin (1 µM), a known ferroptosis inducer, either alone or in combination with commendamide (10 µM), or in the presence of a SCFAs mixture (acetate, propionate, and butyrate in a 1:1:1 ratio, 3 mM). Additionally, the effect of the SCFA mixture was assessed independently, without commendamide. As shown in Fig. 3A, commendamide prevented the dysregulation of ferroptosis-associated genes induced by erastin. This protective effect was comparable to that of the SCFA mixture, suggesting that the presence of gut bacteria capable of producing both SCFAs and commendamide is essential for preventing the activation of ferroptosis, which is harmful to muscle cell processes. Remarkably, the protective effects of commendamide and the SCFA mix against erastin-induced toxicity were also observed in primary human myotubes (Fig. 3B). Commendamide at 1 µM was ineffective alone or in combination with SCFAs (data not shown).

Commendamide protects skeletal muscle cells from oxidative stress

To gain deeper insight into the protective effects of commendamide and SCFAs against erastin-induced ferroptosis, we measured the levels of TBARS (Thiobarbituric Acid Reactive Substances) and the ratio of reduced (GSH) to oxidized (GSSG) glutathione in C2C12 myotubes. Briefly, elevated TBARS levels are indicative of increased oxidative stress and are associated with lipid peroxidation and cell membrane damage [37]. Similarly, a decreased GSH/GSSG ratio reflects a shift toward a more oxidized intracellular environment, typically linked to elevated ROS production and compromised antioxidant defences [38]. As shown in Fig. 4A and B, commendamide alone reduced erastin-induced TBARS levels and partially restored the GSH/GSSG ratio. Notably, these protective effects were more pronounced when commendamide was combined with the SCFA mix (Fig. 4A, B).

Commendamide activates PPARα and PPARγ receptors

In a pioneering study conducted by Cohen et al. in 2015, commendamide was screened against a library of 242 G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), revealing selective activity exclusively at GPR132/G2A, with no other orphan or non-orphan receptors identified as targets [33]. In our study, the expression of GPR132/G2A was neither detected in C2C12 cells nor in human primary myotubes. Therefore, to decipher the molecular mechanism through which commendamide confers protection against muscle ferroptosis and oxidative stress, we investigated its potential affinity for peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs), a family of three distinct members (PPARα, PPARγ, and PPARβ/δ) playing a major regulatory role in energy homeostasis and metabolic function [39, 40]. The decision to explore the potential affinity of commendamide for PPARs was based on evidence that host-derived N-acyl amides, including N-acyl glycine and other palmitic acid-containing amides, have been shown to influence various cellular processes by activating this class of receptors [41–43]. Furthermore, recent evidence suggests that PPARs play a protective role against ferroptosis [44–46].

Toward this hypothesis, we performed a luciferase reporter assay. Briefly, C2C12 myoblasts were transiently transfected with plasmids encoding luciferase under the control of PPARα, PPARγ, or PPARβ/δ response elements, along with a Renilla luciferase plasmid as an internal control. Twenty-four hours after transfection, cells were exposed to increasing concentrations of commendamide (1, 10, and 30 µM) for 18–20 h. Following treatment, cells were lysed, and luminescence was measured (see Materials and Methods for details). Notably, the results revealed that commendamide, in a concentration dependent manner, activated both PPARα and PPARγ, whereas PPARβ/δ remained unresponsive. Known PPAR agonists GW7647 (0.1 µM, PPARα), rosiglitazone (0.1 µM, PPARγ), and L-165,041 (0.1 µM, PPARβ/δ) were used as positive controls (Fig. 5A–C).

Next, to further investigate the interaction of commendamide with PPARα and PPARγ, we employed a combined molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) approach. Given that commendamide is a chiral molecule, both R- and S-enantiomers (hereafter referred to as R-COM and S-COM, respectively) were considered in our study. For each enantiomer, representative docking poses were selected and subjected to 100-ns MD simulations to assess binding stability. MD trajectory analysis revealed that both R-COM and S-COM stably interact with the LBD of PPARα, as indicated by the root mean square deviation (RMSD) values throughout the simulations (see supplementary Fig. 2A-C). Representative MD frames for PPARα in complex with R- and S-COM are shown in Fig. 6. In both complexes, the alkyl chain occupies a hydrophobic cleft formed by helices H3 and H7 and the β-sheet (Fig. 6A and B). However, the two enantiomers exhibit distinct patterns of intra- and intermolecular hydrogen bonds (H-bonds) as revealed by the MD simulations. Specifically, the R-COM hydroxyl group engages in a stable intramolecular H-bond with the amide group’s oxygen atom (> 60% occurrence), while its carboxylate group forms an H-bond network with His440 (H10/11; ~29% occurrence), Gln277 (H3; ~38% occurrence), and Tyr464 (H12; >80% occurrence). In contrast, the S-COM complex lacks an intramolecular H-bond for the ligand, and its carboxylate group H-bond network differs from R-COM’s, involving Ser280 (H3; >90% occurrence), His440 (H10/11; ~60% occurrence), Tyr314 (H4/5; ~58% occurrence), and Tyr464 (H12; ~29% occurrence). The latter H-bond is less persistent than in the R-COM complex, being lost during the MD simulations. The differing H-bond patterns suggest that the enantiomers may have different agonistic properties, with R-COM potentially acting as a full agonist and S-COM as a partial agonist.

For the PPARγ LBD, we identified two distinct binding modes for each enantiomer, denoted as pose1 and pose2. Pose1 (Fig. 5C and D) mirrors the binding observed in the PPARα complex, with the carboxylate group oriented towards Tyr473 (H12). In pose2 (Fig. 6E and F), the ligand adopts a reversed orientation relative to pose1, with the carboxylate group pointing towards Arg288 (H3). As with the PPARα complex, an intramolecular H-bond is observed in R-COM pose1 (Fig. 6C), while the carboxylate group engages in H-bonds with Ser289 (H3; ~59% occurrence), His449 (H10/11; ~58% occurrence), His323 (H4/5; ~15% occurrence), and Tyr473 (H12; ~64% occurrence). In pose2 (Fig. 6E), the carboxylate group forms stable H-bonds with Arg288 (H3; >90% occurrence), while the hydroxyl group participates in an intramolecular H-bond with the carbonyl group (~ 20% occurrence). In S-COM pose1 (Fig. 6D), the carboxylate group forms a network of stable H-bonds with Tyr473 (H12; >90% occurrence), Ser289 (H3; >90% occurrence), and Gln286 (H3; ~70% occurrence), while in pose2 (Fig. 6F), the carbonyl group engages in an H-bond with Ser289 (H3; >90% occurrence), and the carboxylate group participates in ionic and H-bond interactions with Arg288 (H3; ~74% occurrence). Overall, both enantiomers in pose1 directly stabilize helix H12 via H-bonds with Tyr473, while pose2 exhibits greater flexibility than pose1, primarily due to alkyl chain rearrangements (supplementary Fig. 2A-C). In this study, we additionally investigated the potential affinity of commendamide for the two cannabinoid receptors (CB1 and CB2) as well as various TRP channels (TRPV1, TRPV2, TRPA1 and TRPM8), which are known targets of endocannabinoid-like molecules of either eukaryote or prokaryote origin. However, no detectable affinity of commendamide for these receptors was detected.

Commendamide promotes the expression of antioxidant genes via PPARα signaling

Next, we measured the expression levels of the antioxidant genes Gpx4 and Nrf2 in C2C12 myotubes treated with commendamide or selective agonists of PPARα (GW7647, 0.1 µM) and PPARγ (rosiglitazone, 0.1 µM). Our results show that commendamide strongly upregulates Gpx4 expression, to a greater extent than Nrf2, reaching a level comparable to that induced by the PPARα agonist GW7647. In contrast, the PPARγ agonist rosiglitazone did not significantly affect the expression of either gene. Importantly, co-treatment with the PPARα antagonist GW6471 abolished the effect of commendamide, confirming that the effect of commendamide on Gpx4 is mediated through PPARα (Fig. 7A and B). Because PPAR receptors, as also mentioned above, are intracellular receptors that function as ligand-activated transcription factors [40], these results prompted us to search for the potential presence of PPARα- and PPARγ-responsive elements in the 5’-UTR containing the promoter region of Gpx4 and Nrf2 genes. Using bioinformatic tools, we identified distinct PPARα and PPARγ responsive sequences in the promoter and 5’-UTR regions of the Gpx4 and Nrf2 genes, while in human GPX4 and NRF2 genes, we found responsive sequences only for PPARα (supplementary Figs. 3 and 4). Following this analysis, we cloned the portion 5’-UTR of Gpx4 containing part of the non-coding exon 1 and PPAR responsive sequences in a commercial plasmid upstream of the luciferase (luc) gene to validate the bioinformatic prediction. The plasmid carrying the portion of 5’-UTR Gpx4/Luc was then transfected into C2C12 cells for luciferase assays. In detail, subconfluent myoblasts were treated the day after transfection for 18 h with commendamide 10 µM, GW7647 (0.1 µM), and rosiglitazone (0.1 µM) alone or in combination with the latter two drugs.

In brief, the results obtained demonstrate that, similar to GW7647 (0.1 µM), commendamide was effective in promoting Gpx4 transcriptional activity (Fig. 7C). Additionally, in the 5’-UTR portion of Gpx4 gene analysed, we also identified NRF2 responsive elements. Taken together, these findings indicate that PPARα promotes Gpx4 expression not only by directly binding to response elements within the 5’-UTR and promoter region, but also most indirectly by inducing the expression of Nrf2, which in turn activates Gpx4 through binding to its responsive elements (see supplementary Fig. 6).

Discussion

This study provides novel mechanistic insights into the gut–muscle axis in DMD, demonstrating how alterations in the gut microbiota, particularly the reduced abundance of Bacteroides and other SCFA-producing species accompany susceptibility to ferroptosis and impair antioxidant defences in dystrophic skeletal muscle. We identified commendamide, a lipid metabolite produced by Bacteroides species and possessing a chemical structure resembling those of endocannabinoid-like N-acyl amides (e.g. palmitoylethanolamide and palmitoylglycine), as a modulator of cellular redox balance in both murine and primary human skeletal muscle cells exposed to ferroptotic stress. In this context, we focused on Bacteroides vulgatus as one of the best-characterized and most prominent producers of commendamide; however, other Bacteroides species, including B. dorei, B. thetaiotaomicron, and B. fragilis, have also been reported to encode pathways for the biosynthesis of commendamide or closely related N-acyl amides [33, 47]. Consistently, microbiota profiling revealed a reduced abundance of B. vulgatus and other taxa in both mdx mice and DMD patients, extending previous evidence linking gut dysbiosis to muscle dysfunction [17, 18, 48]. However, while SCFAs such as butyrate are well established mediators of anti-inflammatory and metabolic effects in skeletal muscle [17, 19, 49, 50], commendamide represents a largely unexplored microbial lipid signal. Reduced commendamide levels in samples from dystrophic individuals suggest a loss of microbiota-derived protective signaling that may ultimately increase susceptibility to ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death driven by lipid peroxidation and glutathione depletion [34, 35, 51]. Accordingly, in vitro induction of ferroptosis with erastin led to dysregulation of key antioxidant genes (Gpx4, Slc7a11, Slc3a2) in murine and human myotubes; effects that were efficiently counteracted by commendamide. Restoration of redox homeostasis was further supported by normalization of TBARS levels and the GSH/GSSG ratio. Notably, the additive protection observed when commendamide was combined with SCFAs suggests that these microbial metabolites may act through partially distinct yet complementary pathways, pointing to a cooperative network of microbiota-derived signals rather than a single effector molecule.

Importantly, when integrating these mechanistic findings with fecal microbiota profiling in DMD patients, correlation analyses revealed distinct associations between clinical indices and Bacteroides abundances. However, these relationships must be interpreted with caution, as it remains unclear to what extent they reflect disease-intrinsic mechanisms versus secondary effects driven by comorbidities or chronic pharmacological treatments. Furthermore, these findings should be interpreted in light of the relatively small patient cohort, an inherent limitation when studying rare diseases such as DMD. Taken together, these complex interaction patterns suggest that gut microbial alterations may not simply mirror the dystrophic milieu but could potentially modulate its progression, thereby warranting dedicated longitudinal and interventional studies. To date, only a limited number of studies have provided converging evidence that dystrophin deficiency predisposes both skeletal muscle and cardiac tissues to ferroptotic stress. In the dystrophin/utrophin double-knockout mouse model (mdx: utr⁻/⁻), increased iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation are accompanied by altered expression of ferroptosis-related markers and heightened sensitivity of cardiomyocytes to ferroptotic inducers, supporting ferroptosis as a contributor to DMD-associated cardiomyopathy [17]. Additionally, in Nrf2−/− mice compared to age-matched wildtype mice other investigators found a reduced mitochondrial oxygen consumption, increased mitochondrial ROS production, increased protein nitrosylation, cellular redox dysregulation, and reduced acetylcholine receptor expression [52]. Additonally, dysregualted expression of ACSL4, a key determinant of ferroptosis sensitivity due to its role in enriching polyunsaturated fatty acid-containing phospholipids, has also been associated with muscle atrophy and degeneration in multiple models [53, 54]. Similarly, alterations in the cystine/glutamate antiporter system Xc⁻ (SLC7A11) have been implicated in muscle susceptibility to oxidative stress and ferroptosis. Reduced system Xc⁻ activity limits cysteine availability and glutathione synthesis, thereby lowering GPX4 catalytic efficiency even in the absence of changes in GPX4 expression. In models of muscle atrophy and disuse, modulation of the xCT–GSH–GPX4 axis directly controls ferroptotic susceptibility and muscle integrity [55, 56]. In this framework, our data suggest that commendamide effectively prevents the dysregulation of these key genes restoring glutathione homeostasis, as indicated by normalization of the GSH/GSSG ratio, which reflects a shift toward a more oxidized intracellular environment typically linked to elevated ROS production and compromised antioxidant defences, and normalised Gpx4 expression.

It is worth mentioning that the expression of ferroptosis-related genes was not uniform across skeletal muscles, with distinct patterns observed in gastrocnemius, quadriceps, and soleus. This heterogeneity seems to mirrors prior observations in mdx mice and human DMD muscle, where fiber-type composition and metabolic specialization strongly influence oxidative stress responses and degeneration rates. Fast glycolytic muscles appear particularly vulnerable to ferroptotic damage, whereas slow oxidative muscles display relative resistance [57, 58].

Mechanistically, we demonstrate that commendamide engages nuclear PPAR signaling. Although luciferase assays indicated activation of both PPARα and PPARγ, only PPARα activation translated into functional antioxidant gene induction. Docking analyses supported stable binding of commendamide to both receptors; however, pharmacological experiments showed that selective PPARα activation increased Gpx4 and Nrf2 expression, whereas PPARγ activation (with rosiglitazone) did not. Promoter analyses further supported this selectivity by identifying conserved PPARα response elements in Gpx4 (and murine Nrf2), providing a mechanistic basis for pathway specificity.

Despite the novelty, this study has several limitations that should be explicitly acknowledged. First, the lack of in vivo validation and pharmacokinetic characterization of commendamide represents a significant constraint, and therefore our findings should be interpreted as mechanistic and hypothesis-generating rather than directly translational. Second, although commendamide exerts robust protective effects in vitro, our data, together with the observed additive benefit of SCFAs, suggest that administration of commendamide alone may be insufficient to fully recapitulate the protective effects of a healthy microbiota. Instead, these results point toward a synergistic model in which multiple microbiota-derived metabolites cooperate to regulate muscle redox homeostasis and ferroptosis susceptibility. In this context, it is plausible that therapeutic strategies should not be limited to single-metabolite supplementation but may need to consider approaches aimed at restoring the microbial ecosystem itself. These could include microbiota-targeted interventions to re-establish Bacteroides populations, for example with Bacteroides vulgatus strain-containing probiotics, as well as postbiotic strategies combining commendamide with SCFAs or other microbial metabolites. Such approaches would better reflect the complexity of microbiota–host interactions and may provide an effective means of modulating the gut–muscle axis in DMD.

In conclusion, future in vivo studies will be essential to validate these mechanisms and to determine whether combined microbiota- and metabolite-based interventions can be translated into therapeutic benefit for Duchenne`s muscular dystrophy.

Conclusions and prospectives

From a translational perspective, the identification of a microbiota-derived lipid capable of modulating ferroptosis highlights the potential of targeting gut-derived metabolic signaling as a complementary strategy to dystrophin-focused therapies in DMD. Rather than replacing current treatments, modulation of the gut–muscle axis may offer a means to attenuate oxidative vulnerability and disease progression. Although substantial preclinical validation is required, these findings provide a conceptual framework for developing microbiota-based or postbiotic interventions aimed at attenuating muscle damage in chronic myopathies.
