Effects of accelerated intermittent θ burst stimulation on depressive episodes and cognitive function in adolescents with bipolar disorder
Authors: Yingshuang Xue, Hong Wang, Jihui Yue, Shenglin Wen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPharmacological treatments for adolescent bipolar depression often show limited efficacy, safety, and significant side effects concerns. Accelerated intermittent theta burst stimulation (aiTBS) has shown potential in treating depressive episodes, but its application in adolescents with bipolar depression remains unclear.MethodsEighty-six adolescents diagnosed with bipolar depression were randomized to receive 10 sessions of active aiTBS, sham aiTBS, or high-frequency repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (HF-rTMS; 10 Hz) targeting the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC). Treatments were administered once daily over a two-week period. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 24-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-24) at baseline and following the completion of treatment. Performance on specific cognitive tasks (assessing working memory, processing speed, and executive function) was evaluated pre- and post-intervention using the Digit Span Test (DST), Digit Symbol Substitution Test (DSST), Trail Making Test Part A (TMT-A), and Trail Making Test Part B (TMT-B).ResultsParticipants in the aiTBS group demonstrated a greater reduction in HAMD-24 scores compared to the sham group. Background

Bipolar disorder (BD) is a severe, recurrent psychiatric illness affecting approximately 1–3% of youth, particularly adolescents [1], and has emerged as a leading cause of disability in this age group [2]. In adolescents, depressive episodes are often the initial and predominant mood states, occurring more frequently than (hypo)manic or mixed episodes, and are associated with a substantial disease burden [3, 4]. The initial treatment of bipolar depression in adolescents typically involves a combination of pharmacotherapy and psychotherapy. However, to date, no medications have been approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) specifically for the treatment of bipolar depression in this population. Furthermore, antidepressants are sometimes used off-label despite concerns regarding their potential to induce manic switches [5].

Emerging evidence suggests that adjunctive psychotherapy can alleviate depressive symptoms in adolescent patients with BD [6]. Nevertheless, psychotherapy often requires a prolonged course of treatment with variable efficacy across individuals. Modified electroconvulsive therapy (MECT) has been recognized as a rapid-acting and effective physical intervention; however, its use is limited by short-term cognitive side effects, such as headache, amnesia, and impaired memory, which contribute to patient apprehension and treatment reluctance. Collectively, these limitations underscore the pressing need for novel, effective, and better-tolerated therapeutic strategies for adolescent bipolar depression.

High-frequency repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (HF-rTMS) targeting the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) has demonstrated efficacy in treating depressive symptoms in adult populations [7]. In a meta-analysis of 19 randomized controlled trials (RCTs), McGirr et al. reported a clinical response rate of 48.5% in patients with BD receiving active repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) compared to 30.0% in the sham group, indicating a therapeutic effect comparable to that observed in major depressive disorder [8]. Additionally, HF-rTMS has shown potential cognitive benefits, including improvements in visual learning, verbal acquisition, and processing speed, in patients with BD [9, 10]. However, conventional HF-rTMS protocols typically involve lengthy treatment sessions (20–40 min) and relatively high stimulation intensities (80–120% of the resting motor threshold [RMT]), which may result in scalp discomfort and limit tolerability [11, 12].

Intermittent theta burst stimulation (iTBS) is an alternative rTMS protocol that may offer greater clinical feasibility. iTBS involves delivering bursts of three 50 Hz pulses at 5 Hz for 2 s, followed by an 8-second rest, totaling 600 pulses in a 192-second session. This paradigm, characterized by embedding high-frequency “gamma” bursts within a lower-frequency “theta” rhythm, is thought to enhance cortical excitability and induce robust long-term potentiation-like effects [13]. Several studies have investigated the potential of iTBS for treating bipolar depression. For instance, Beynel et al. demonstrated greater improvement on the Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) in adult patients with BD treated with iTBS compared to a sham stimulation after three weeks of daily sessions [14]. Similarly, McGirr et al. confirmed the antidepressant efficacy of iTBS in their meta-analysis [15].

Accelerated intermittent theta burst stimulation (aiTBS), which delivers multiple iTBS sessions per day, has been developed to shorten overall treatment duration and expedite clinical response. A previous study in adolescents with depressive episodes reported significantly greater reductions in depressive symptoms following two weeks of daily 1800-pulse aiTBS sessions targeting the left DLPFC than a sham stimulation [16]. Another study involving adolescents with either bipolar or unipolar depression found significant post-treatment cognitive improvements in the iTBS group [17]. These findings suggest that aiTBS may represent a promising therapeutic approach for adolescent depression.

Despite the encouraging results in adult populations, there is a paucity of research on the efficacy and safety of aiTBS in adolescents with bipolar depression. Current evidence is insufficient to draw definitive conclusions regarding its effectiveness or the risk of treatment-emergent affective switching in this demographic. Moreover, although aiTBS has shown potential cognitive benefits in adults with BD [18], studies specifically addressing the cognitive outcomes in adolescents remain limited. Given that adolescence is a critical period of neurodevelopment—and that neurodevelopmental status may influence the safety and tolerability of TMS interventions [19]—further investigation in adolescent samples is essential.

Therefore, the primary objective of this study is to evaluate the therapeutic and cognitive effects of aiTBS in adolescents with bipolar depression.

Methods

Characteristics of the study participants

This randomized controlled trial was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and their guardians before enrollment. Patients meeting the criteria for BD-Ⅱ during a current depressive episode were recruited from the inpatient department of the Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University between February 2023 and January 2024. All diagnoses were confirmed independently by two experienced psychiatrists based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5).

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age between 10 and 19 years; (2) a score ≥ 20 on the 24-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-24) [20, 21] at screening; (3) an education level of primary school or above; and (4) the ability to comprehend the study content, provide voluntary participation, and sign informed consent.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) diagnosis of substance or alcohol abuse; (2) presence of significant neurological conditions, such as traumatic brain injury, stroke, dementia, other neurodegenerative disorders, a history of seizures, or neurosurgery; (3) presence of cardiac pacemakers, intracranial implants, or ferromagnetic materials in the skull deemed incompatible with transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) based on safety assessments; (4) electroencephalographic abnormalities suggestive of epileptic predisposition; (5) receipt of electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) or TMS within the past 6 months; and (6) participation in structured psychotherapeutic interventions, such as psychoeducation or cognitive remediation, within the past 2 years. This exclusion criterion was implemented to minimize potential confounding effects on the assessment of the intervention’s efficacy.

To clarify, all participants underwent routine clinical electroencephalography (EEG) as part of the safety screening prior to enrollment to exclude those with epileptiform abnormalities. The technical limitations referred to in the Discussion pertain to the research-grade EEG we initially planned to use for exploring neurophysiological mechanisms.

Theta-burst stimulation procedures

Eligible participants who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria and provided informed consent were randomly allocated into one of three groups: aiTBS, sham stimulation, or HF-rTMS, using a random number table method.

Stimulation was delivered using a YRD CCY-I magnetic simulator (YIRUIDE Inc., Wuhan, China) [16] with a figure-of-eight shaped coil. The resting motor threshold (RMT) was determined as the minimum stimulus intensity required to elicit a motor evoked potential (MEP) of > 50 µV in the relaxed right abductor pollicis brevis muscle in at least 5 out of 10 trials. The stimulation position for the left DLPFC was localized by using the standard “5 cm” method. Participants in the aiTBS group received a total of 10 treatment sessions over a two-week period (five sessions per week), with 1800 pulses delivered per session. This dosage was selected based on prior findings demonstrating that 1800 pulses can induce robust and sustained changes in cortical excitability and optimize cellular-level effects [22, 23].

Stimulation was applied to the left DLPFC. Each session consisted of 60 triplet bursts delivered in trains of 2 s, with an inter-train interval of 8 s, repeated until 1800 pulses were administered. A 30-minute interval was maintained between each session [24].

In the sham group, participants received stimulation using a placebo coil that was visually identical to the active coil and produced similar auditory and tactile sensations but did not generate a magnetic field capable of inducing neuronal activity.

For the HF-rTMS group, an intensity of 110% RMT [25] was used to optimize tolerability in adolescents, as higher intensities can increase scalp discomfort.

Clinical assessments

At baseline, demographic and clinical data were collected. Assessments of depressive symptoms and cognitive function were conducted at baseline and repeated after the first and second weeks of treatment. Depressive symptoms were evaluated using the HAMD-24 and the Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS) [26]. Cognitive performance was assessed using a battery of neuropsychological tests targeting key domains: Digit Symbol Substitution Test (DSST) [27, 28], Digit Span Test (DST) [29], and Trail Making Tests A and B (TMT-A, TMT-B) [30].

The reduction rate in HAMD-24 scores was calculated to evaluate treatment efficacy. A reduction rate of ≥ 50% was defined as “response,” and a post-treatment total score ≤ 7 was defined as “remission“ [31]. YMRS was utilized to detect and monitor potential emergent manic symptoms.

Clinical raters who performed the HAMD-24 and cognitive assessments were blinded to the participants’ group allocation.

Drug regimen

To minimize confounding variables, all participants continued their regular pharmacological regimens throughout the study period. All participants continued their stable pharmacological regimens, which primarily included mood stabilizers (lithium or anticonvulsants) and/or atypical antipsychotics. The use of benzodiazepines was permitted for anxiety or insomnia but was restricted to a maximum lorazepam-equivalent dose of 2 mg/day, and administration was avoided within 12 h prior to TMS sessions to minimize potential modulation of cortical excitability. No changes in psychotropic medication were permitted during the course of the intervention.

Safety and adverse events

The safety and tolerability of the stimulation protocols were evaluated at each session. Adverse events were monitored through spontaneous reports and systematic inquiries regarding potential symptoms. Common adverse events associated with rTMS include seizures, headaches, and dizziness. Other reported side effects included scalp discomfort, nausea, chest tightness, anxiety, eyelid twitching, tinnitus, gastrointestinal disturbances, irritability, and general malaise [32–34].

Statistical analysis

Sample size estimation was performed using G*power before the start of the study, with a test power of 0.8, an effect size of 0.25, and α = 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0. Continuous variables with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (± s), and comparisons within groups were performed using paired t-tests. In contrast, between-group comparisons were conducted using independent samples t-tests. One-way ANOVA was applied for comparisons among the three groups, and Welch’s correction was used when the homogeneity of variances was violated. When the overall ANOVA was significant, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Bonferroni or Games-Howell tests, as appropriate, to identify specific group differences.

For non-normally distributed data, medians and interquartile ranges were reported. Within-group comparisons were conducted using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, while between-group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis test, as appropriate.

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies or percentages and analyzed using the chi-square (χ²) test. Repeated measures ANOVA was used to analyze data collected at multiple time points. Correlations between cognitive function and demographic or clinical variables were analyzed using Spearman’s or Pearson’s correlation coefficients, depending on the data distribution. Linear regression analysis was employed to identify predictors of HAMD-24 score reduction after one and two weeks of treatment.

Results

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics

The participant flow is illustrated in Fig. 1. A total of 102 adolescents with bipolar depression were screened for eligibility, among whom 3 declined participation. Ultimately, 99 patients were enrolled and randomly assigned to one of three groups: aiTBS (n = 33), sham stimulation (n = 33), and HF-rTMS (n = 33). During the treatment period, 1 patient in aiTBS group withdrew from the treatment after only four treatments because she did not want to wait for the treatment time. 5 patients in the sham group withdrew from the study because the number of treatments did not reach the standard due to discharge during the treatment. In the HF-rTMS group, 3 patients requested to stop the treatment and withdrew from the study due to feeling the treatment was ineffective (they had completed a mean of 4 (range: 3–5) sessions.), while another 4 patients withdrew because the number of treatment sessions did not reach the standard due to discharge.from the hospital. The data of the above 13 patients were not included in the final statistics. A total of 86 participants completed the entire study protocol and were included in the final analysis (aiTBS, n = 32; sham, n = 28; HF-rTMS, n = 26).

Changes in cognitive function test scores before and after treatment

To assess cognitive outcomes, repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted on cognitive test scores at baseline and after 2 weeks of treatment. Significant improvements were observed across all three groups in DST (F = 36.001, p < 0.001, η² = 0.303) and DSST scores (F = 17.016, p < 0.001, η² = 0.170), as well as reductions in time required to complete TMT-A (F = 32.297, P < 0.001, η² = 0.280) and TMT-B (F = 61.999, P < 0.001, η² = 0.428)(Supplement Table 1). However, no significant between-group differences were observed in the improvement on the specific cognitive tasks administered (Fig. 2A–D).

Within the aiTBS group, Pearson correlation analysis revealed a significant positive relationship between baseline cognitive function test scores and their respective post-treatment change scores (DST: r = 0.562, P < 0.001; DSST: r = 0.511, p = 0.003; TMT-A: r = 0.424, p = 0.016; TMT-B: r = 0.626, p < 0.001; composite score: r = 0.637, p < 0.001) (Fig. 3A-E). Predictors of clinical efficacy in the aiTBS group

Correlation analyses were conducted to identify factors associated with treatment response (i.e., reduction in HAMD-24 scores at weeks 1 and 2) within the aiTBS group. A significant positive correlation was found between baseline HAMD-24 scores and the magnitude of HAMD-24 reduction after both 1 week (r = 0.446, p = 0.012) and 2 weeks of treatment (r = 0.562, p = 0.001).

Subsequent linear regression analyses confirmed that baseline HAMD-24 scores significantly predicted a reduction in depressive symptoms at both time points (week 1: F = 7.204, p = 0.012, B = 0.356; week 2: F = 12.406, p < 0.001, B = 0.497). The model accounted for 17.1% and 27.5% of the variance in treatment outcomes at weeks 1 and 2, respectively (Supplement Tables 1, 2, 3 and 4).

Safety and adverse events

No serious adverse events were reported across the three treatment groups. One patient in the aiTBS group experienced a transient headache, which resolved spontaneously without intervention. The incidence of adverse events did not differ significantly among the groups (p > 0.05). YMRS scores remained below 6 across all groups throughout the study period, indicating that no patients experienced manic switch during treatment.

Discussion

aiTBS has been demonstrated to effectively alleviate depressive symptoms, with its efficacy in treating unipolar depression well established in previous studies [31]. While emerging evidence supports the use of aiTBS in adult patients with bipolar depression, its efficacy and safety in adolescent populations remain insufficiently explored and warrant further investigation.

The findings of this study are consistent with our initial hypothesis. After one and two weeks of treatment, the aiTBS group exhibited significantly greater reductions in HAMD-24 scores compared to the sham group, indicating a superior antidepressant effect. Specifically, the clinical response rate in the aiTBS group reached 34.38% at the end of the two-week treatment period, significantly exceeding that of the sham group (10.71%) and closely aligning with previously reported remission rates (32%) in 3D trials of iTBS [35]. Notably, all patients in the aiTBS group achieved a ≥ 25% reduction in HAMD-24 scores, indicating the onset of meaningful antidepressant effects within a relatively short time frame. Although conventional TMS protocols often require 4–6 weeks to achieve clinically significant antidepressant effects [36], accelerated regimens aim to compress the treatment course. For instance, intensive protocols delivering multiple sessions per day can achieve comparable outcomes to standard regimens but in a significantly reduced number of treatment days [37]. Our results lend support to this accelerated approach, suggesting that aiTBS may induce more rapid therapeutic responses in hospitalized adolescents with bipolar depression.

Furthermore, no significant difference was observed in the reduction of HAMD-24 scores between the aiTBS and HF-rTMS groups, suggesting that the clinical efficacy of aiTBS is comparable to that of HF-rTMS. This finding is consistent with the conclusions of multiple prior studies comparing iTBS or aiTBS to HF-rTMS in depressive populations [35, 38, 39].

An important methodological consideration in aiTBS protocols is the inter-session interval. As reported by Yu et al., a 30-minute interval between sessions optimally enhanced motor cortical excitability, with 75% of participants exhibiting increased cortical responsiveness [24]. Accordingly, our study adopted a 30-minute inter-session interval to maximize potential therapeutic benefits. However, other studies have posited that cumulative synaptic plasticity effects are more robust when inter-session intervals range between 50 and 90 min, whereas intervals of 40 min or less may not elicit such cumulative effects [40–43].

This mechanistic discrepancy may partly explain the high remission rates (90.5%) reported in the Stanford Accelerated Intelligent Neuromodulation Therapy (SAINT) protocol [44], which was pioneered by the late Professor Donald Williams and his colleagues. This protocol implemented 10 aiTBS sessions per day with 50-minute intervals over five days. Unfortunately, our study did not include treatment arms with varying inter-session intervals, which precluded a direct evaluation of the interval effect. Future studies should investigate the influence of inter-session timing on clinical outcomes in adolescent bipolar depression and explore the underlying neurobiological mechanisms of aiTBS.

Previous research has demonstrated that adolescents with BD often exhibit impairments in executive function, attention, and working memory [45], a finding that was also corroborated by our baseline assessments. However, after two weeks of intervention, no significant cognitive improvement was observed in any of the treatment groups. This outcome contrasts with prevailing evidence suggesting that iTBS can enhance cognitive function [11, 46, 47]. While structural neuroplastic changes, such as increases in hippocampal volume, have been observed following iTBS in patients with BD [18, 48], such changes may not immediately translate into observable cognitive improvements. The relatively short two-week duration of our intervention, combined with the absence of neuroimaging assessments, may have been insufficient to capture both the structural and functional sequelae of aiTBS. Therefore, the lack of significant cognitive improvement in our study does not preclude the possibility of underlying neuroplasticity that might manifest as cognitive benefits over a longer period.

A key clinical concern in the treatment of bipolar depression is the risk of mood switching. In our study, aside from one case of mild headache, no significant adverse events or manic/hypomanic episodes were observed, suggesting that aiTBS is both safe and well tolerated in adolescents with bipolar depression.

Nonetheless, several limitations of this study must be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size may have limited our ability to detect subtler treatment effects, underscoring the need for larger-scale trials. Second, our findings are derived from a sample of hospitalized adolescents with bipolar depression. The efficacy and safety of aiTBS in outpatient settings or in less severely ill populations remain to be investigated and may differ from our results. Meanwhile, due to the average hospitalization period of 2–3 weeks, the treatment duration was relatively short, and we did not conduct post-treatment follow-up to assess the sustainability of therapeutic effects. Longitudinal data are essential to evaluate the persistence of aiTBS’s antidepressant effects, and future studies with extended treatment and follow-up periods are necessary to evaluate the long-term durability of the antidepressant effects observed in our short-term trial. Thirdly, the use of a per-protocol analysis and the differential dropout rates across groups, particularly in the HF-rTMS group, may limit the generalizability of the comparisons between active treatments. However, we believe this approach best reflects the efficacy of the complete aiTBS protocol, which was the primary focus of the study. Finally, although we initially planned to incorporate electroencephalography (EEG) to explore the neurophysiological mechanisms underlying aiTBS, technical limitations precluded the completion of EEG data collection. Future studies should integrate objective biomarkers such as EEG or functional MRI (fMRI) alongside clinical assessments to elucidate the mechanisms of action and optimize aiTBS protocols for adolescent bipolar depression.

Conclusions

Two weeks of daily aiTBS targeting the left DLPFC effectively reduced depressive symptoms in adolescents with bipolar depression, showing comparable efficacy to traditional HF-rTMS. Greater baseline depression severity was associated with better treatment response. While performance on measures of working memory, processing speed, and executive function improved in the aiTBS group, the difference was not significant compared to HF-rTMS and sham groups. The effects of aiTBS on the assessed cognitive domains require further validation through longer follow-up and objective measures.
