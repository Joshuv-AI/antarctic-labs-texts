Model-agnostic Mitigation Strategies of Data Imbalance for Regression
Jelke Wibbeke, Sebastian Rohjans, Andreas Rauh

Abstract
Data imbalance persists as a pervasive challenge in regression tasks, introducing bias in model performance and undermining predictive reliability. This is particularly detrimental in applications aimed at predicting rare events that fall outside the domain of the bulk of the training data. In this study, we review the current state-of-the-art regarding sampling-based methods and cost-sensitive learning. Additionally, we propose novel approaches to mitigate model bias. To better asses the importance of data, we introduce the density-distance and density-ratio relevance functions, which effectively integrate empirical frequency of data with domain-specific preferences, offering enhanced interpretability for end-users. Furthermore, we present advanced mitigation techniques (cSMOGN and crbSMOGN), which build upon and improve existing sampling methods. In a comprehensive quantitative evaluation, we benchmark state-of-the-art methods on 10 synthetic and 42 real-world datasets, using neural networks, XGBoosting trees and Random Forest models. Our analysis reveals that while most strategies improve performance on rare samples, they often degrade it on frequent ones. We demonstrate that constructing an ensemble of models – one trained with imbalance mitigation and another without – can significantly reduce these negative effects. The key findings underscore the superior performance of our novel crbSMOGN sampling technique with the density-ratio relevance function for neural networks, outperforming state-of-the-art methods.

Introduction

Data imbalance remains a pervasive challenge in regression tasks, impacting the performance and reliability of predictive models, such as neural networks. Traditional loss functions, like mean squared error or mean absolute error, tend to favor majority classes, resulting in biased model performance towards frequently occurring data points
(
Wibbeke et al. 2025
)
. This bias is particularly detrimental in applications that aim to predict rare events outside the domain of the bulk of the training data, such as extreme weather conditions, building energy consumption, or fault detection in industrial systems
(
Steininger et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2021
;
Ramentol et al. 2016
)
.

While data imbalance has been extensively studied in classification problems, regression-specific strategies have only gained attention in the past years. The field still lacks a coherent framework for measuring and mitigating imbalance in continuous target variables.

In general, when considering the mitigation of imbalance, strategies are categorized into data-level and algorithm-level methods
(
Sadouk et al. 2021
;
Krawczyk 2016
)
. On the one hand, data-level methods alter the distribution of the data, for example, by over- and/or under-sampling
(
Torgo et al. 2013
;
Branco et al. 2017
;
Wibbeke et al. 2022
)
. On the other hand, algorithm-level methods directly influence the model by for example modifying the model architecture
(
Yang et al. 2021
)
or applying cost-sensitive learning techniques like weighting the loss function
(
Steininger et al. 2021
;
Sadouk et al. 2021
)
.

To apply any imbalance mitigation technique, it is necessary to first use some form of relevance function to derive a value of importance/rarity/relevance of each sample. Subsequently, the score is used to treat each sample according to the mitigation method.

In order to avoid confusion, we therefore use the terms “relevance function” for functions to obtain the relevance of the data points, “mitigation method” for methods that use the relevance as weights for sampling or cost-sensitive learning and “mitigation strategy” for the complete package of relevance function and mitigation method. Our full taxonomy in this regard is also shown in Fig.
1
.

In previous work, we introduced a method for quantifying imbalance by comparing the empirical distribution of the data to the distribution associated with the relevance of the prediction task’s domain
(
Wibbeke et al. 2025
)
. This concept was originally proposed by
Kowatsch et al. 2024
, who suggested the use of two distributions to define imbalance, a framework that has proven to be more interpretable and to avoid the pitfalls of arbitrary thresholds used in prior methods.

However, no current relevance function and thus no mitigation methods for regression tasks effectively utilize both the empirical and domain relevance distribution. Furthermore, no comprehensive comparison of existing approaches has been conducted. This gap leaves an open question as to how different mitigation techniques perform evaluated through a unified framework and how to derive new relevance functions considering both distributions.

The central research question addressed in this paper is: How can model-agnostic strategies, including novel methods utilizing both the empirical and domain relevance distribution, improve performance in regression tasks under data imbalance conditions?

In this paper, we focus on model-agnostic mitigation strategies for regression tasks. These include mostly data-level methods, while making a single exception for algorithm-based methods by including cost-sensitive learning. This exception is justified by the model type independence of cost-sensitive learning, unlike other algorithm-based methods that are model-specific (e.g., additional output layers in neural networks, which cannot be applied to regression trees). We compare the performance of several established approaches such as SMOGN by
Branco et al. 2017
, and WSMOTER by
Camacho and Bacao 2024
, as well as the cost-sensitive learning approaches by
Sadouk et al. 2021
and DenseWeight by
Steininger et al. 2021
. In addition, we propose new relevance functions and mitigation methods.

The main contributions of this paper are the following:

•

We propose two novel relevance functions to assess the importance of the data samples.

•

We introduce two novel model-agnostic mitigation methods based on sampling to reduce the model bias caused by data imbalance in regression tasks.

•

We compare the performance of different combinations of relevance functions and mitigation methods, including the newly proposed methods, in a quantitative benchmark to find the best suitable approach to decrease the biasing effect of data imbalance on regression models.

•

We demonstrate that constructing an ensemble of two models can mitigate the negative effects of imbalance mitigation strategies on model performance and achieves superior performance across various regression datasets.

2
Related Work

Many studies published to date have focused on imbalanced classification. However, few deal with imbalanced regression
(
Branco et al. 2016
)
, albeit it was shown that classification and regression cannot be handled the same way
(
Yang et al. 2021
;
Kowatsch et al. 2024
)
.

Regarding the definition of imbalance,
Branco et al. 2016
and
Ribeiro 2011
describe imbalance as the situation in which the user assigns more importance to the performance on a subset of the range of the target variable
\mathbf{Y}
. This user preference bias is expressed by a relevance function
\phi(\mathbf{Y}):\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,1]
, mapping the target variable into a range of importance between 0 and 1, where 1 is the maximal importance. Imbalance exists if
\phi(\mathbf{Y})
it not uniform. This way the relevance of each sample can be determined, however the magnitude of imbalance in the dataset can only be obtained if a relevance threshold is assumed to split the dataset into low and high relevance subsets.

Another definition of data imbalance is presented by
Kowatsch et al. 2024
, where they define balance, and thus postulate imbalanced data as a deviation from balanced data. Data imbalance therefore is present, if the probability measure of the empirical data deviates from a relevance measure. As the two respective measures the probability density function of the empirical data and the probability density function associated with the relevance of the target domain are used. To quantify the imbalance, the Wasserstein metric is proposed, which is related to the distance between the two probability density functions. This definition was taken up by
Wibbeke et al. 2025
proposing new metrics to quantify the imbalance in data, suggesting not to use the Wasserstein metric, but the ratio of the two distributions to provide better generalisation and improved interpretability.

As already elaborated by
Krawczyk 2016
and
Sadouk et al. 2021
, most approaches to avoiding data imbalance can be divided into two categories. On the one hand, data level methods alter the distribution of the data, for example, by over- and/or under-sampling
(
Torgo et al. 2013
;
Branco et al. 2017
;
Wibbeke et al. 2022
;
Branco et al. 2019
)
. In principle, most sampling-based mitigation methods are using random sampling, interpolation between existing samples, creation of new samples by Gaussian noise or a combination of said approaches
(
Branco et al. 2019
;
Avelino et al. 2024
)
. On the other hand, algorithm level methods directly influence the model by for example modifying the model architecture
(
Yang et al. 2021
)
or applying cost-sensitive learning techniques like weighting the loss function
(
Steininger et al. 2021
;
Sadouk et al. 2021
;
Yang et al. 2021
)
. A variety of methods to mitigate the effect of imbalanced data is discussed in detail in Sec.
3
.

However, all mitigation methods use some form of importance/rarity/relevance score, which is attributed to a data point in order to subsequently weight, replicate or drop the sample. These relevance functions are also one of the major differences between imbalanced classification and regression. In the case of imbalanced classification, it is usually assumed that the minority and majority classes are known or can be easily deduced based on the frequency of the classes, such as in the well-known synthetic minority over-sampling (SMOTE) by
Chawla et al. 2002
. However, this is not easily transferable to continuous variables. This raises the question of how frequent or relevant each sample is.

Torgo and Ribeiro 2007
propose that the relevance of a sample is inversely proportional to its rarity, and thus linked to the probability density function of the target variable. To determine each sample’s relevance, they divide the target variable into quantiles and calculate the relevance using a sigmoid-based function, with parameters based on the interquantile range. However, the authors limit this approach to distributions with normal-like characteristics and marked tails.

Ribeiro 2011
refined the approach of
Torgo and Ribeiro 2007
by using box plot statistics to define control points, then interpolating between these points with piecewise cubic Hermite interpolating polynomials. This method, which allows for smoother transitions between relevance values, was also adopted by subsequent works
(
Branco et al. 2017
;
Branco et al. 2019
;
Ribeiro and Moniz 2020
;
Silva et al. 2022
)
. Alternatively,
Ribeiro 2011
proposed that the relevance function could be constructed with user-defined control points, providing improved flexibility in relevance estimation.

Adding to the notion that the relevance of a sample is inversely proportional to its rarity,
Aminian et al. 2025
and
Yang et al. 2021
propose to calculate the relevance based on the normalized inverse of the target value distribution discretized by histogram bins, where
Yang et al. 2021
smooth the bins beforehand using a symmetric kernel.

(
Steininger et al. 2021
;
Sadouk et al. 2021
;
Camacho and Bacao 2024
;
Yang et al. 2021
)
propose to calculate the relevance of the data points based on the inverse of the target value rarities, estimated by kernel density estimation. Subsequently,
Sadouk et al. 2021
;
Yang et al. 2021
and
Steininger et al. 2021
use the relevance as weights in a cost-sensitive learning scheme to mitigate the imbalance, whereas
Camacho and Bacao 2024
propose to over-sample the data based on the relevance of the data points. In addition,
Yang et al. 2021
also propose a distribution smoothing on the feature space by integrating a feature calibration layer into the model.

However, none of the relevance functions and mitigation methods take advantage of the proposed ideas by
Kowatsch et al. 2024
and
Wibbeke et al. 2025
to include the difference between the domain relevance distribution function and the distribution function of the empirical data into the mapping scheme. In addition, a comprehensive comparison of all imbalance mitigation methods is missing. The first steps of a comparison are presented in
(
Avelino et al. 2024
)
, but the influence of different relevance functions is not taken into account here, thus an extensive overview of the most popular relevance functions and mitigation methods is provided in the following section.

3
Methods

In this section, we present various model-agnostic strategies to mitigate the biasing effect of data imbalance on model performance. Without aiming to be exhaustive, we cover most popular algorithms in current use. To do so, the topic of data imbalance mitigation strategies is split into relevance functions and mitigation methods.

Relevance functions aim at calculating some value of importance/rarity/relevance of each sample, which is later on used in the respective mitigation method to alleviate the negative effect of data imbalance on model performance which causes the model error to be biased towards frequent samples.

The section consists of three subsection. In the first subsection relevance functions are presented. The second subsection discusses imbalance mitigation methods. In the last subsection, approaches to form ensembles are presented.

Regarding the mathematical notation, we consider a dataset consisting of
N
samples with corresponding target values and features. We denote the dataset as
\mathbf{D}=(\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{X})
, where
\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the target vector
\mathbf{Y}=[y_{1},y_{2},\cdots,y_{N}]
, containing the target value
y_{i}
for each sample
i=1,\dots,N
. Similarly, the model
M
approximating the target variable is denoted as
\mathbf{\hat{Y}}=M(\mathbf{X})=[\hat{y}_{1},\hat{y}_{2},\cdots,\hat{y}_{N}]
. The feature matrix
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
contains the
d
-dimensional feature vectors
\mathbf{x}_{i}
for each sample. Each individual sample is thus represented by a pair
(y_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i})
, where
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This notation will be used throughout the following sections to refer to the dataset structure.

3.1
Relevance functions

A relevance function uses knowledge about the empirical distribution of the data and/or knowledge about domain preference to derive a value of relevance that can be attributed to each sample. To derive the relevance function, either application-specific domain knowledge of the modeling task and/or, in absence of said knowledge, a function inversely proportional to the probability density function of the target variable can be used.

We define a relevance function as a continuous function
\phi(\mathbf{Y}):\Re\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that maps the empirical distribution of data and/or application-specific domain bias into a scale of relevance, where higher values indicate higher relevance/importance of the sample. Note that our definition deviates from the established one proposed by
Torgo and Ribeiro 2007
, which restricts the value range of the relevance to between 0 and 1, i.e.,
\phi(\mathbf{Y}):\Re\rightarrow[0,1]
.

In the following, several relevance functions are presented and discussed. These methods include:

•

Interpolation with control points, histogram-based relevance, label distribution smoothing, kernel density relevance and DenseWeight

All of these assume that relevance can be quantified by a single distribution, whether being the distribution of the empirical data itself or an arbitrary distribution associated with importance/relevance of the target domain.

Based on the premise set out in
(
Wibbeke et al. 2025
)
and
(
Kowatsch et al. 2024
)
, which suggests that data imbalance should better be quantified by two distributions — one representing the empirical distribution of the data and the other a measure of domain relevance — two new methods are introduced, transferring the concept to relevance functions:

•

Density-distance relevance and density-ratio relevance

3.1.1
Interpolation with control points

To derive a function inversely proportional to the target density
Torgo and Ribeiro 2007
proposed a sigmoid-like function to establish a smooth relevance function using the statistics provided by boxplots that summarize the distribution of the target variable as control points. However, this limited the approach to distributions of normal-like shape with very marked tails.

The approach was taken on by
Ribeiro 2011
, who proposed to interpolate between the control points using piecewise cubic Hermite interpolating polynomials (pchip). The resulting relevance function goes through the set of
n
provided points, giving the user more freedom to shape the function, as user-defined control points can be added. Is is assumed that the control points refer to local extrema of the relevance, have a derivative of zero and are limited between 0 and 1. As a non-parametric approach to obtain the control points
Ribeiro 2011
propose the aforementioned boxplot statistics. This way, the relevance is inversely proportional to the frequency of the sample. However, using the boxplot statistics limits the approach to normal-like or uni-modal distributions.

3.1.2
Histogram-based relevance

The histogram-based relevance approach was originally proposed for data stream processing, with a particular emphasis on low computational complexity
(
Aminian et al. 2025
)
. It determines the relevance of individual data points based on the discretized distribution of the target variable, as obtained through histogram binning.

First, an equal-width histogram of the target variable is constructed using
k
bins. The “density” of each bin is evaluated by calculating the relative frequency of samples in each bin, denoted as
p_{j}
. This is done by normalizing the sample count in each bin with respect to the maximum bin count across all bins according to

p_{j}=\frac{b_{j}}{\text{max}\{b_{l}:l=1,\ldots,k\}},

(1)

where
b_{j}
and
b_{l}
represent the number of samples in the
j
-th and
l
-th bin, respectively. The relevance of a sample with target value
y_{i}
is then computed as

w(y_{i})=1-p_{j},

(2)

where
p_{j}
corresponds to the relative sample count of the bin to which
y_{i}
belongs.

3.1.3
Label distribution smoothing

Label Distribution Smoothing also assumes that the relevance of a sample is dependent on the distribution of the target variable. However,
Yang et al. 2021
argue that a smoothed distribution of the target variable should be considered, since data-driven models may extrapolate from frequently occurring samples to nearby rare ones. Therefore, they propose estimating the effective label density distribution
\tilde{p}(y_{i})
by convolving the empirical density
p(y)
with a Gaussian kernel
k(y,y^{\prime})
according to

\tilde{p}(y_{i})=\int k(y,y_{i})p(y)dy.

(3)

Here, the empirical density
p(y)
is approximated using a histogram, with the density for
y_{i}
determined by the number of occurrences in the corresponding histogram bin. To compute the relevance, the effective density is then inverted as

w(y_{i})=\frac{1}{\tilde{p}(y_{i})}.

(4)

However, this inversion causes weights to approach infinity for very small densities, which is particularly problematic for out-of-distribution samples and makes the relevance function highly sensitive to extreme distortions. In the work by
Yang et al. 2021
, this issue is addressed on a per-dataset basis by clipping the effective density
\tilde{p}(y)
to avoid extreme values. Nonetheless, the resulting relevance function is not bounded between 0 and 1, which limits its applicability in many mitigation methods.

To ensure compatibility, it is possible to first scale the effective density to the range [0, 1] and then invert it according to

w(y_{i})=\max(1-p_{\text{scaled}}(y_{i}),\epsilon),

(5)

where
p_{\text{scaled}}
denotes the scaled effective density, and
\epsilon
is a small constant introduced to avoid zero weights.

3.1.4
Kernel density relevance

Kernel density relevance makes use on the assumption, that the relevance of data is inversely proportional to the target variable probability distribution. To estimate the distribution, kernel density estimation (KDE) is used
(
Sadouk et al. 2021
)
.

To ensure general applicability independent of the respective dataset the density values are normalized between 0 and 1. Subsequently, the normalized density is inverted, such that low-density regions, typically associated with rare or extreme values in the target variable, are assigned higher relevance, while high-density regions, where the target values are more common, receive lower relevance.

3.1.5
DenseWeight

Complementary to kernel density relevance, DenseWeight approximates the density of the dataset using KDE
(
Steininger et al. 2021
)
. However, in addition to normalizing the density between 0 and 1 and then inverting the normalized density, a scaling factor
\alpha
is added according to

w^{\prime}(\alpha,y)=\max(1-\alpha p^{\prime}(y),\epsilon),

(6)

where
p^{\prime}
is the normalized density function and
\epsilon
a small positive constant to prevent a weight from being zero;
\alpha
is a hyper-parameter with which the impact of the density can be calibrated depending on the modeling task at hand. However, in their evaluation
Steininger et al. 2021
find evidence for
\alpha=1
being the most effective value, which allows it to be neglected.

In contrast to kernel density relevance, after inversion the weights are normalized by the mean of all weights in order to be independent from the respective learning algorithms’ learning rate.

3.1.6
Density-distance relevance

Density-distance relevance is a new relevance function, which is a generalization of DenseWeight and Kernel density relevance. It is based on the assumption that the relevance
w(y_{i})
of the
i
-th sample
y_{i}
results from the distance
\Lambda_{\text{dist}}
between the empirical probability density function of the target variable
f_{\text{x}}(\mathbf{Y})
and the probability density function associated with the domain relevance of the target variable
f_{\text{r}}(\mathbf{Y})
, according to

\Lambda_{\text{dist}}(y_{i})=f_{\text{x}}(y_{i})-f_{\text{r}}(y_{i}).

(7)

In this way, both a non-uniform distribution of the data and a non-uniform relevance of the domain can be taken into account. Theoretically, the concept can also be extended to include the feature domain into the density functions
(
Wibbeke et al. 2025
)
.

The density-distance
\Lambda_{\text{dist}}
can take on values within the interval
(-\infty,\infty)
centered around zero. This means that
y_{i}
is under-represented if
\Lambda_{\text{dist}}(y_{i})<0
and over-represented if
\Lambda_{\text{dist}}(y_{i})>0
. To align the density-distance relevance with other relevance functions it is subsequently normalized and inverted, according to

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\prime}_{\text{dist}}(y_{i})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0.5-0.5\cdot\frac{\Lambda_{\text{dist}}(y_{i})}{\min\left(\Lambda_{\text{dist}}\left(\mathbf{Y}\right)\right)}&\text{if }\Lambda_{\text{dist}}(y_{i})<0\\
0.5+0.5\cdot\frac{\Lambda_{\text{dist}}(y_{i})}{\max\left(\Lambda_{\text{dist}}\left(\mathbf{Y}\right)\right)}&\text{if }\Lambda_{\text{dist}}(y_{i})\geq 0\end{cases},

(8)

\displaystyle w(y_{i})

\displaystyle=\max\left(1-\Lambda^{\prime}_{\text{dist}}(y_{i}),\epsilon\right),

(9)

where
\epsilon
is a small clipping value to avoid values of zero. The relevance are thus bounded within in the interval
[\epsilon,1]
. The normalization centered around the point 0.5 preserves the knowledge at which point the probability density function of the target variable
f_{\text{x}}
and the probability density function associated with the domain relevance
f_{\text{r}}
are balanced. At the same time, individual samples with
\Lambda_{\text{dist}}(y_{i})\ll 0\ll\Lambda_{\text{dist}}(y_{i})
do not cause the center of the relevance function to shift.

If uniform domain relevance is considered,
f_{\text{r}}(y_{i})
is constant over all
i
samples. The density-distance relevance is then similar to the kernel density relevance or DenseWeight (without normalization by the mean), apart from the fixed midpoint value. Density-distance relevance can thus be seen as an generalization of the aforementioned approaches, granting more flexibility by including empirical sample density and domain relevance and by proving the user with a fixed midpoint value for better interpretation.

An example for visualization is shown in Fig.
2
. It shows the influence of a non-uniform domain relevance distribution on the density-distance relevance.

Figure 2:
Distribution of the target variable (electrical energy) of the combined-cycle-power-plant dataset (see Appendix
B
) together with empirical distribution (estimated with KDE) and different domain relevance distributions. It can be seen that the resulting density-distance relevance is influenced by the domain relevance distribution.

3.1.7
Density-ratio relevance

Density-ratio relevance is based on the assumption that the ratio of the values of two probability density functions at different points
y_{i}
yields the relative probability of occurrence of an event. The probability ratio
\Lambda_{\text{ratio}}(y_{i})
, according to

\Lambda_{\text{ratio}}(y_{i})=\frac{f_{\text{x}}(y_{i})}{f_{\text{r}}(y_{i})},

(10)

directly expresses how often the sample
y_{i}
occurs compared to how often it should occur with respect to its domain relevance. Here,
f_{\text{x}}(\mathbf{Y)}
denotes the probability density function of the empirical distribution of the target variable
\mathbf{Y}
and
f_{\text{r}}(\mathbf{Y})
denotes the probability density function associated with the relevance of the domain of the target variable. The concept can also be extended to the feature space, as in
(
Wibbeke et al. 2025
)
.

\Lambda_{\text{ratio}}
can take values within the interval
(0,\infty)
, where
\Lambda_{\text{ratio}}(y_{i})=1
denotes that the empirical distribution of the data matches the domain relevance distribution, indicating that the sample is as frequent as expected. If
\Lambda_{\text{ratio}}(y_{i})<1
the sample
y_{i}
is under-represented, with
\Lambda_{\text{ratio}}(y_{i})=0.5
indicating that the sample occurs half as often as assumed. The sample is over-represented if
\Lambda_{\text{ratio}}(y_{i})>1
, with
\Lambda_{\text{ratio}}(y_{i})=2
indicating that the sample occurs twice as often as assumed.

However, as a relevance function, a larger relevance should indicate higher importance of the sample. Thus, to obtain the relevance of
\mathbf{Y}
, we use the inverted ratio, given by

w(y_{i})=\frac{1}{\Lambda_{\text{ratio}}(y_{i})}.

(11)

Now
w(y_{i})=0.5
indicates that the relevance of the sample is half as high as the optimal/nominal relevance of a sample, which is
w(y_{i})=1
.

Using the ratio makes the density-ratio relevance function non-symmetric and not bound between 0 and 1. It therefore requires dedicated mitigation methods to be used in. However, the density-ratio yields the advantage that
w(y_{i})
directly expresses how severe the under-/over-representation of sample
y_{i}
is and thus offers improved interpretability.

3.2
Mitigation methods

In the following, we discuss model-agnostic methods to mitigate the effects of data imbalance during model training. Besides sampling methods, we include cost-sensitive learning methods that only alter the loss function into said category, as altering the loss function does not influence the model architecture and loss functions can be chosen freely for many model types. The presented methods include:

•

SMOTER, SMOGN, WERCS, WSMOTER, DenseLoss, probabilistic loss and BMC.

In addition, we propose two new sampling-based mitigation strategies:

•

cSMOGN and crbSMOGN

3.2.1
SMOTER

The synthetic minority over-sampling technique (SMOTE)
(
Chawla et al. 2002
)
combines the under-sampling of frequent classes with the over-sampling of the minority class. As it is only designed for classification problems, SMOTER represents the extension of SMOTE to regression tasks
(
Torgo et al. 2013
)
and adopts large parts of it.

SMOTER divides the dataset into minority and majority datasets based on a user-defined relevance threshold, where the sample relevance values have to be bounded by 0 and 1. The number of samples in the majority subset is reduced by under-sampling with random selection of
n
samples, where
n
results from the ratio between majority and minority subset specified by the user. The number of samples in the minority subset is increased by over-sampling, based on SMOTE, with adjustments for continuous data.

SMOTER generates synthetic instances by interpolating between similar instances within the subset. This involves computing the
k
-nearest neighbors (
k
NN) of each minority sample, and creating new synthetics samples that lie between the minority sample and one of its nearest neighbors. The value of the target variable of the new sample is chosen based on the weighted average by the two seed samples, where the weights are determined by the distance of the new sample to the two seed samples.

As a relevance function
Torgo et al. 2013
use the interpolation between control points with pchip by
Ribeiro 2011
(see Sec.
3.1.1
).

3.2.2
SMOGN

The synthetic minority over-sampling with Gaussian noise (SMOGN) method was proposed by
Branco et al. 2017
and combines random under-sampling with either over-sampling using SMOTER or the generation of new samples based on Gaussian noise. A bounded relevance between 0 and 1 is expected. Similar to SMOTER, the dataset is partitioned into subsets containing consecutive samples in the target domain with relevance values either above or below a threshold. Partitions containing samples below the threshold are under-sampled by randomly dropping samples. Partitions containing samples above the threshold are over-sampled. The core idea for over-sampling is the same as SMOTER, but with SMOGN a new sample is only interpolated if at least one of the
k
-nearest-neighbors is “close enough” to the seed sample. If this is not the case, the new sample is created by adding Gaussian noise to the seed sample. The threshold value that is used to decide whether the neighbor is close enough is defined to be half of the median distance between the seed samples and all other samples in the partition to be over-sampled. This has the advantage that no synthetic sample is assumed in empty and probably impossible feature spaces between two distant seed samples. Compared to SMOTER, SMOGN can therefore be regarded as a more conservative approach. However, calculating the median distance increased the computational burden. Building multiple partitions containing consecutive samples in the target domain limits the partition size and thus eases the computational burden to calculate the distances between samples.

As a relevance function
Branco et al. 2017
use the interpolation between control points with pchip by
Ribeiro 2011
(see Sec.
3.1.1
).

3.2.3
WERCS

The weighted relevance-based combination strategy (WERCS) is a data-level imbalance mitigation strategy based on resampling, proposed by
Branco et al. 2019
.

In contrast to SMOTER and SMOGN, WERCS does not require a user defined relevance threshold to split the data, as the data are not divided into minority and majority subsets. WERCS treats all samples as candidates for either under- or over-sampling, but the probability of a sample being selected depends on its relevance (a relevance between 0 and 1 is expected). This means, that more relevant samples have higher probability of being replicated, whilst low-relevance samples have higher probability of being removed. Nevertheless, high-relevance samples can also get removed, albeit with lower probability. This approach keeps the continuous nature of regression data without forming discretized subsets. In addition, the user only has to define the amount of under- and over-sampling.

For under- and over-sampling WERCS uses replication or dropping of the respective sample. In doing so, WERCS can be seen as one of the simplest sampling approaches, not necessitating any sort of sample imputation or interpolation.

As a relevance function
Branco et al. 2019
use the interpolation between control points with pchip by
Ribeiro 2011
(see Sec.
3.1.1
).

3.2.4
WSMOTER

Weighting SMOTE for regression (WSMOTER) is a data-level approach that uses over-sampling to mitigate imbalance in the dataset
(
Camacho and Bacao 2024
)
.

In contrast to SMOTER and SMOGN, WSMOTER does not split the dataset into minority and majority subsets based on a relevance threshold. Instead, similar to WERCS, each sample can be used as a seed for over-sampling. Whether a sample is selected as seed is decided by the relevance of the sample, where the relevance values have to be bounded by 0 and 1. Thus, the larger the relevance, the larger the probability of being selected for over-sampling. If a seed sample is selected, a second seed for interpolation is searched in the dataset. For this purpose, the
K
samples are considered whose target variable values are closest to the first seed sample, where
K=10\cdot k
. Subsequently, the
k
-nearest neighbors are determined from the
K
samples in combined target and feature domain and one of the neighbors is randomly selected for interpolation. The actual interpolation method between the two seed samples is similar to SMOTER.

Camacho and Bacao 2024
use the DenseWeight method by
Steininger et al. 2021
to determine the relevance of each sample (see Sec.
3.1.5
).

3.2.5
DenseLoss

DenseLoss is a model-agnostic cost-sensitive learning approach proposed by
Steininger et al. 2021
.

It expects relevance values between 0 and 1 and includes the relevance
w(y_{i})
of each sample
y_{i}
to weight the loss metric
m(\hat{y}_{i},y_{i})
of the prediction
\hat{y}_{i}
to affect the gradients of the respective training algorithm according to

L_{\text{DenseLoss}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{i=1}w(y_{i})\cdot m(\hat{y}_{i},y_{i}).

(12)

This adaptive weighting approach ensures, that high-relevance samples contribute more prominently during model training, without artificially altering the data distribution. It effectively re-balances the training process by giving more focus to samples that would otherwise be overlooked, aiming to improve model performance in predicting values across the full target range. In not specifying the respective loss function, the approach is loss function-agnostic. In addition, it is model-agnostic, making it compatible with a variety of regression models.

Steininger et al. 2021
propose DenseLoss together with DenseWeight as the relevance function (see Sec.
3.1.5
).

3.2.6
Probabilistic loss

Probabilistic loss is a model-agnostic cost-sensitive learning approach proposed by
Sadouk et al. 2021
.

In contrast to DenseLoss, the probabilistic loss is not calculated by scaling the loss function by the relevance
w(y_{i})
of the sample
i
, but by adding an additional weighted loss term to the
l_{2}
-loss, resulting a regularized loss according to

L_{\text{ProbLoss}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{i=1}\left((y_{i}-\hat{y}_{i})^{2}+\sqrt{\left(\left(y_{i}-\hat{y}_{i}\right)^{2}+\epsilon\right)}\cdot w(y_{i})\right),

(13)

with
\epsilon
being a small constant (
10^{-9}
) added to ensure that the loss is differentiable at
\hat{y}_{i}=y_{i}
. The aim of this regularized loss function is to achieve a trade-off between fitting the data well and giving more importance to high-relevance cases. However, this comes with the limitation that the regularized loss only works on the basis of an
l_{2}
-loss. It is also assumed that the target variable is scaled between 0 and 1, as otherwise the difference between the
l_{2}
-loss and the regularization becomes too large
(
Sadouk et al. 2021
)
.

The probabilistic loss is proposed together with kernel density relevance as the relevance function (see Sec.
3.1.4
).

3.2.7
BMC

Batch-based Monte Carlo (BMC) is a cost-sensitive learning approach proposed by
Ren et al. 2022
. It is mathematically equivalent to directly weighting the loss function, as done in DenseLoss. However, unlike other mitigation methods,
Ren et al. 2022
assume that only the training label distribution
p_{\text{train}}(y)
is imbalanced, while the test label distribution
p_{\text{test}}(y)
is uniform. An interesting aspect of BMC is that it does not rely on a relevance function. Instead of explicitly weighting the loss function, BMC reformulates it as a temperature-scaled softmax function according to

L=-\log\frac{\exp(-\|y_{\text{pred}}-y\|^{2}/\tau)}{\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\text{Batch}_{y}}\exp(-\|y_{\text{pred}}-y^{\prime}\|^{2}/\tau)},

(14)

where
\tau=2\sigma^{2}_{\text{noise}}
is the temperature coefficient, and
y^{\prime}
denotes the true labels in the current training batch. The noise standard deviation
\sigma_{\text{noise}}
is treated as a hyperparameter, which can either be manually specified or learned during training. The form of loss function is equivalent to classifying within a batch.

3.2.8
cSMOGN

The first new mitigation method we propose is continuous SMOGN (cSMOGN). It is based on the concept of SMOGN and draws together the most important features of SMOTE, SMOTER, and WSMOTER.

cSMOGN does not subdivide the dataset into minority and majority subsets, but continuously over-samples the distribution of the data in dependence on the relevance of the sample (like WSMOTER). The over-sampling is realized either by interpolation (like SMOTE) with distance-based weighting of the target variable (like SMOTER) or by replication with Gaussian noise (like SMOGN), if no second interpolation seed can be found. The space for searching for an interpolation seed is restricted by discretizing the dataset based on the method of
Wibbeke et al. 2022
. This prevents the interpolation from creating samples over large distances in a possibly empty and unreachable space and, at the same time, limits the search space to be examined to determine the
k
-nearest neighbors samples, which reduces the computational complexity.

In the following the cSMOGN approach is outlined:

1.

Assign a value of relevance between 0 and 1 to each sample of the dataset using a respective relevance function, where a higher value indicates higher importance.

2.

Randomly select a seed sample in dependence on the squared sample relevance. Squaring the relevance gives extra emphasis to high-relevance samples as the chance to select a sample with low relevance
w\ll 1
gets significantly reduced. This can be illustrated by looking at a binary problem. Assuming that class 1 occurs twice as often as class 2 and therefore has half the relevance of class 2, a weighted sampling without squaring the relevance would produce the same number of samples for each of the two classes.

3.

For each over-sampling step, find the second seed sample to pair with the first seed. This is done by randomly selecting one of the
k
-nearest-neighbors of the first seed sample. However, searching the nearest neighbors over the full dataset is computationally exhaustive. Defining the subset to search for the
k
-nearest-neighbors is accomplished as follows:

(a)

Discretize each continuous feature of the dataset into
n_{\text{bins}}
consecutive bins. Categorical features are kept discrete.

(b)

Get the bin numbers of the first seed sample.

(c)

Select a subset of the fully discretized dataset. The subset contains all samples which bin numbers are at maximum
\delta_{b}
bins away from the seed sample bins. Categorical features have to be equal to the seed sample. This way the subset contains only samples that bare similarity to the seed sample in target as well as feature domain.

(d)

Get the undiscretized subset by using the index of the discretized subset. Subsequently, use the undiscretized subset to find the
k
-nearest neighbors.

4a

In case a second seed sample is found: Create the new synthetic sample by interpolation between the two seed samples using SMOTE. Calculate the target value of the synthetic sample using the weighted distance between the seed samples as in SMOTER.

4b

In case no second seed sample is found: Create the new synthetic sample by applying Gaussian noise to the seed sample as in SMOGN.

The approach is repeated until the user-defined number of samples is reached. For better understandability we provide the pseudo-code in Alg.

3.2.9
crbSMOGN

The second new mitigation method we propose is the sampling technique continuous ratio-based SMOGN (crbSMOGN). Opposed to all other mitigation methods, crbSMOGN does not necessitate sample relevance values scaled between 0 and 1, but relies on a ratio-based relevance. Ratio-based relevance values express the importance in relativity to the nominal relevance of
w(y_{i})=1
, thus, for example, a sample with relevance
w(y_{i})=2
is twice as relevant.

In principle, the over-sampling in crbSMOGN works very similarly to cSMOGN. However, due to the high expressiveness of the ratio-based relevance function, crbSMOGN does not require a user-defined input to specify the number of samples to create. crbSMOGN obtains the number of samples to be over-sampled from the ratio-based relevance values. The data are sampled until the entire target domain has an equal relevance of 1. However, whether this is possible depends on the distribution of the data, as it is not possible for crbSMOGN to generate data in areas where none exist.

The over-sampling is realized by either interpolation (like SMOTE) with distance-based weighting of the target variable (like SMOTER) or by replication with Gaussian noise (like SMOGN), if no second interpolation seed can be found. The space for searching for an interpolation seed is restricted by discretizing the dataset, based on the method of
Wibbeke et al. 2022
. This prevents the interpolation from creating samples over large distances in a possibly empty and unreachable space and, at the same time, limits the search space to be examined to determine the
k
-nearest neighbors samples, which reduces the computational complexity.

In the following, the crbSMOGN approach is outlined:

1.

Assign a value of relevance to each sample of the dataset using a respective ratio-based relevance function.

2.

Over-sample each sample with a ratio-based relevance
w(y_{i})>1
in accordance to its relevance. This means that a sample with
w(y_{i})=1.5
has 50% chance of being over-sampled and a sample with
w(y_{i})=3
is over-sampled twice.

3.

The over-sampling is done with interpolation. Thus, a second seed sample to pair with the first seed is needed. This is done by selecting the
k
-nearest-neighbors of the first seed sample, where
k
is the over-sampling rate of first seed. However, searching the nearest neighbors over the full dataset is computationally exhaustive. Defining the subset to search for the
k
-nearest-neighbors is accomplished as follows (similar to cSMOGN):

(a)

Discretize each continuous feature of the dataset into
n_{\text{bins}}
bins. Categorical features are kept discrete.

(b)

Get the bin numbers of the first seed sample.

(c)

Select a subset of the full discretized dataset. The subset contains all samples which bin numbers are at maximum
\delta_{b}
bins away from the seed sample bins. Categorical features have to be equal to the seed sample. This way the subset contains only samples that bare similarity to the seed sample in target as well as feature domain.

(d)

Get the undiscretized subset by using the index of the discretized subset.

(e)

If the subset is larger than the over-sampling rate of the seed sample return the
k
-nearest neighbors in the subset with regard to the seed sample, where
k
is the over-sampling rate. If the subset is smaller than the over-sampling rate of the seed sample return the full subset. In this case, the calculation of the nearest neighbors is omitted, as the discretization already ensures that all samples in the subset are similar to the seed.

4a

In case the subset is empty: Create the new synthetic samples by applying Gaussian noise to the seed sample as in SMOGN.

4b

In case the subset contains
k
samples: Create a new synthetic samples for each sample in the subset by interpolation between the seed sample using SMOTE. Calculate the target values of the synthetic samples using the weighted distance between the seed sample and the neighbors as in SMOTER.

4b

In case the subset contains less than
k
samples: Create a new synthetic sample by interpolation for each sample in the subset. In addition, create synthetic samples by applying Gaussian noise to the seed sample until in total
k
samples are created.

3.3
Ensembles

In many applications, it has been found that forming an ensemble from several models leads to better results
(
Galar et al. 2011
;
Moniz et al. 2017
;
Moniz et al. 2018
;
Branco et al. 2018
)
. The aim of an ensemble is to balance out the disadvantages of individual models, for example by forming a weighted average of all models.

We propose to form an ensemble of a “normal” model
M_{\text{normal}}
trained without any imbalance mitigation strategy and a model
M_{\text{imbalanced}}
that has been adjusted for data imbalance. To obtain the final approximation
\hat{y}_{i}
of the ensemble, we propose to use either the mean of both models, the weighted mean or select one model based on a relevance threshold.

The mean of both models can be formed according to

\hat{y}_{\text{mean},i}=\frac{M_{\text{imbalanced}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})+M_{\text{normal}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})}{2}.

(15)

The weighted mean of an ensemble of the two models can be formed according to

\hat{y}_{\text{weighted},i}=\frac{\beta_{1}(y_{i})\cdot M_{\text{imbalanced}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})+\beta_{2}(y_{i})\cdot M_{\text{normal}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})}{\beta_{1}(y_{i})+\beta_{2}(y_{i})},

(16)

where
\beta_{1}(y_{i})
and
\beta_{2}(y_{i})
are the positive weights of the models considering the respective sample
y_{i}
, which can be substituted by the relevance value.

Under the assumption that
\beta_{2}
being the complement of
\beta_{1}
, leading to
\beta_{1}(y_{i})+\beta_{2}(y_{i})=1
and
\beta_{1}(y_{i})=w_{\text{scaled}}(y_{i})
, Ep.
16
can be simplified to

\hat{y}_{\text{weighted},i}=w_{\text{scaled}}(y_{i})\cdot M_{\text{imbalanced}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})+\left(1-w_{\text{scaled}}\left(y_{i}\right)\right)\cdot M_{\text{normal}}(\mathbf{x}_{i}),

(17)

where
w_{\text{scaled}}(y_{i})
denotes positively scaled relevance values bounded by 0 and 1.

Now the question arises of how to obtain
y_{i}
if the true value of
y_{i}
is unknown during inference. On the one hand,
y_{i}=M_{\text{imbalanced}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
would result in a bias towards
M_{\text{imbalanced}}
, on the other hand
y_{i}=M_{\text{normal}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
biases towards
M_{\text{normal}}
. We therefore propose to use the mean according to Eq.
15
, to mitigate the issue. Resulting in

\hat{y}_{\text{weighted},i}=w_{\text{scaled}}(\hat{y}_{\text{mean},i})\cdot M_{\text{imbalanced}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})+\left(1-w_{\text{scaled}}\left(\hat{y}_{\text{mean},i}\right)\right)\cdot M_{\text{normal}}(\mathbf{x}_{i}).

(18)

Considering ratio-based relevance, the ratio between the two models is already included in the relevance values
w_{\text{ratio}}(y_{i})
. Thus, under the assumption that
\beta_{1}(y_{i})=w_{\text{ratio}}(\hat{y}_{\text{mean},i})
and
\beta_{2}(y_{i})=1
the ensemble equation
16
can be simplified according to

\hat{y}_{\text{weighted},i}=\frac{w_{\text{ratio}}(\hat{y}_{\text{mean},i})\cdot M_{\text{imbalanced}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})+M_{\text{normal}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})}{w_{\text{ratio}}(\hat{y}_{\text{mean},i})+1}.

(19)

Considering the ensemble based on a relevance threshold, density-distance relevance and density-ratio relevance offer improved interpretability. Both provide an equilibrium point at which the target empirical density is equal to the domain relevance density. This point (
w_{\text{dist}}=0.5
,
w_{\text{ratio}}=1
) can be used as a threshold
t
for model selection in an ensemble according to

\hat{y}_{\text{threshold},i}=\begin{cases}M_{\text{normal}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})&\text{if }w(\hat{y}_{\text{mean},i})<t\\
M_{\text{imbalanced}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})&\text{if }w(\hat{y}_{\text{mean},i})\geq t,\end{cases}

(20)

where
t
is the set relevance threshold. For other relevance functions scaled between 0 and 1 we suggest to use
t=0.5
.

Benchmark

In the following, we analyse the performance of various strategies to avoid model bias due to data imbalance. For this purpose, combinations of relevance functions and mitigation methods are tested and the performance of the individual strategies is compared. More specifically, the sampling methods SMOGN, WERCS, WSMOTER, cSMOGN and crbSMOGN as well as the cost-sensitive learning methods DenseLoss and probabilistic loss are examined. In addition to all relevance functions presented in Sec.
3.1
.

The evaluation is split into multiple parts:

•

Analysis of the influence of resampling methods on the target variable distribution.

It is shown that different mitigation methods have different effects on the distribution of the target variable, where only crbSMOGN can achieve close-to-uniform distribution.

•

Comparison of the model performance without mitigation strategy on real world data.

It is shown that all analysed model types inhibit a substantial performance bias resulting in a higher error for rare samples.

•

Analysis of the performance of mitigation method and relevance function combinations on synthetic datasets.

It is shown, that all efforts to increase performance for rare samples lead to a decrease in performance for frequent samples. In addition, it is shown that resampling with crbSMOGN yields the best performance for rare samples.

•

Analysis of the performance of mitigation method and relevance function combinations on real world datasets.

It is shown that resampling with crbSMOGN also archives the best model performance for rare samples for real world datasets. In addition, it is shown that under-sampling further decreases the performance for frequent samples.

•

Analysis of the performance of proposed ensemble methods.

It is shown that the ensemble based on the mean mitigates the performance decrease for frequent samples.

To estimate the empirical distribution, most relevance functions use kernel density estimation, with Silverman’s rule of thumb for bandwidth estimation. Silverman’s rule of thumb is a non-optimal estimator for non-normal distributions as it tends to over-smooth non-normally or even non-parametic distributed target value domains. A more precise solution is the improved Sheather-Jones (ISJ) algorithm
(
Botev et al. 2010
)
. However, up to now, all density-based relevance functions used the Silverman bandwidth, due to its simplicity. Thus, we refrained from using the ISJ bandwidth as it is much more susceptible to domain scaling than Silverman’s rule of thumb and does not converge to a solution for any given empirical distribution.

4.1
Influence on the target variable

To highlight the differences between the individual data imbalance mitigation methods, the effect on the target variable of the dataset is examined. The target variable is decisive for loss functions during model training, which makes it particularly important. For this purpose, five mitigation methods are applied to the synthetic Nernst dataset (see Appendix
B
Table
5
). As relevance functions, the functions used in the respective publication of the methods are used. These are SMOGN and WERCS with control points interpolation using pchip, WSMOTER with DenseWeight, cSMOGN with distance-based relevance and crbSMOGN with ratio-based relevance. The result is shown in Fig.
3
. No under-sampling could be carried out for WSMOTER, as this is not intended in the method.

Figure 3:
Comparison of the effect of different data imbalance mitigation methods on the target variable of the synthetic Nernst dataset. Shown are the original distribution of the data, the distribution after over-sampling and the distribution after over- and under-sampling. The colored values indicate the mean imbalance ratio (mIR) of the over-sampled data (red) and the over- and under-sampled data (green).

The figure shows that, as intended, under-sampling essentially affects the areas of very frequently occurring samples. SMOGN is the only method that divides the dataset into a majority and minority subset. Since all samples in the minority subset are over-sampled to the same extent, data points that lie on the boundary between the subsets are represented significantly more frequently after sampling. This leads to the two dips that can be seen in the distribution.

Furthermore, it is notable that WSMOTER exhibits a relatively consistent rate of introducing new samples to the domain, irrespective of the frequency of similar existing samples. This behavior stems from the fact that the probability of generating a new sample in WSMOTER is linearly proportional to the sample’s relevance, which, due to the implementation of DenseWeight, is directly tied to the sample’s frequency. Consequently, while a frequent sample might be over-sampled at half the rate of a less frequent sample, its higher prevalence in the dataset causes the absolute number of over-sampled instances to be similar. This effect is circumvented by cSMOGN, which employs a sampling probability dependent on the square root of the sample relevance. This modification results in a larger concentration of generated samples within the region of high-relevance samples.

All of the methods presented use the empirical distribution of the data as the basis for relevance estimation, but only crbSMOGN succeeds in obtaining a (virtually) uniform distribution through sampling. However, this is only possible because the domain is completely covered by the data record. If there are gaps in the domain, these cannot be filled by crbSMOGN either.

The mean imbalance ratio (mIR) provides a value to assess how imbalanced the data are on average. A mIR = 1 indicates that the empirical distribution matches the relevance distribution (in this case uniform) and the dataset is not imbalanced. The imbalance increases with increasing mIR, where, for example, mIR = 2 denotes that the frequencies of the samples deviate on average by 2-times from the expected uniform frequency
(
Wibbeke et al. 2025
)
. Since under-sampled datasets are closer to a uniform distribution, the mIR values of under-sampled datasets are generally lower than without under-sampling. However, all presented approaches decrease the mIR. Based on the calculated mIR values, crbSMOGN with under-sampling should be the best method to avoid data imbalance.

Performance Evaluation

In this section the performance of various relevance function and mitigation method combinations is evaluated on synthetic and real world datasets. We speak of synthetic data if it is created artificially. The functional relationship between the target variable and the input features is therefore known. The key difference between synthetic and real world data is that real world data has unknown noise levels and is potentially not based on parametric/regular distributions, which makes modeling more difficult.

A list of the real world datasets used is provided in Appendix

.

(
Liu et al. 2012
)
.

All datasets are randomly split into training (70%) and testing (30%) sets. When using imbalanced data, it is important that the training and testing data have comparable distributions. Therefor, each split is performed 100 times and the similarity of the target value distribution of the subsets is evaluated using the mean imbalance ratio (mIR)
(
Wibbeke et al. 2025
)

As models, multilayer perceptron (MLP) feed forward neural networks, extreme gradient boosting trees (XGBoost) and Random Forests (Forest) are used. The probabilistic loss weighted loss-function and BMC are not available off the shelf in common machine learning libraries. Due to the poor performance for the MLP model, the implementation of both for XGBoosting tree and Random Forest was omitted.

For density-distance relevance and density-ratio relevance, the probability density function associated with the domain relevance
f_{\text{r}}
is required in addition to the empirical distribution of the target variable
f_{\text{x}}
. If necessary,
f_{\text{r}}
can be adjusted by the user to give more weight to certain areas of the domain. We use the mean squared error loss, which is based on a uniform cost assumption
(
Anand et al. 1993
)
. For better comparability with other relevance functions, we thus assume that there is no domain preference and the domain relevance distribution is therefore assumed to be uniform.

The mean squared error loss is not optimal for capturing the performance in imbalanced scenarios. Another possibility would be the
F_{1}
-measure for regression
(
Torgo and Ribeiro 2009
)
. However, it has the disadvantage that the dataset for evaluation has to be split in two based on a user-defined relevance threshold to artificially create a minority and majority subset.

Another possibility is the squared error-relevance area (SERA) metric, which needs no user-defined threshold
(
Ribeiro and Moniz 2020
)
. However, both
F_{1}
-measure and SERA have the disadvantage that when comparing two relevance functions, it is not possible to distinguish whether the performance improvement comes from a better adapted model or from a difference in the relevance functions. For the comparison of combinations of relevance function and mitigation method, we therefore follow the evaluation of
Steininger et al. 2021
and
Camacho and Bacao 2024
, who evaluate the performance based on the discretized target domain.

To do so, the target domain is divided into five bins of equal width and each bin is assigned a rank depending on the number of samples in it. The bin with the fewest samples is called very rare, the one with the most samples very frequent. For each bin the average loss over all the samples in the bin is calculated. To allow for a comparison of performance across different datasets, the mean bin error per dataset is calculated for all bins across all models. The individual bin errors of each combination are then normalized using the mean bin error of the respective dataset. In this way, the average expected bin error per dataset across all combinations is 1. An example evaluation of the bin errors is shown in Fig.
4
. Each line represents the results of one combination of relevance function, mitigation method and model type with color coding in dependence of the mitigation method. A declining line in the plot means, that for the particular combination of relevance function, mitigation method and model type the resulting model is biased towards frequent samples. Thus having the smallest error means that the mitigation strategy is outperforming the other mitigation strategies, as can be seen in “Mitigation Method 4” (purple line) in the figure. In the following this plot will be denoted as a bin-error-plot.

Figure 4:
Exemplary bin-error-plot. The model error for each sample frequency bin is normalized and averaged over all datasets. Each line represents the results of one combination of relevance function, mitigation method and model type.

Assessing statistical significance is essential to determine whether the observed differences in model performance across datasets are meaningful or merely due to random variations. By statistically validating the effectiveness of different mitigation strategies, we ensure that any performance gains are not coincidental but rather attributable to the applied techniques.

To evaluate significance, populations are formed from the mean errors of 50 modeling repetitions per dataset and bin. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test, a non-parametric test, is used to determine whether a statistically significant difference exists between two paired samples. The null hypothesis assumes no difference between the tested populations, and a significance threshold of 0.05 implies a 5% probability of incorrectly rejecting the null hypothesis.

A dataset is counted as won by a mitigation strategy if the test determines a statistically significant difference and the model using the strategy exhibits lower error in the respective bin than the model without mitigation. Conversely, a dataset is counted as lost if the model without mitigation outperforms the strategy. Significant wins and losses highlight where differences are statistically meaningful, providing insights into the relative effectiveness of different mitigation approaches across datasets.

Fig.
5
presents the number of datasets won and lost by models employing different mitigation strategies, compared to models trained without any mitigation strategy. The evaluation focuses on specific sample frequency bins rather than the full target domain, providing insights into how mitigation strategies perform across different regions of the target distribution. In the following this kind of plot will be referred to as a bin-win-plot. The statistical significance of these results is always evaluated using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with a significance threshold of 0.05.

Figure 5:
Exemplary bin-win-plot. Number of datasets won and lost by models employing different mitigation strategies compared to models trained without any mitigation strategy. The evaluation is performed separately for specific sample frequency bins rather than the full target domain. Statistical significance is determined using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with a significance threshold of 0.05. Significant wins and losses indicate where differences in model performance are statistically meaningful.

4.2.1
Model type comparison with real world data

In this section, the performance of the three different model types without any data imbalance mitigation strategies is compared with respect to the model error caused by imbalanced data. For this purpose, the 42 real world datasets from different domains are used. The performance is shown in Fig.
6
.

The left side shows the bin-error-plot. It can be seen that the performance of the models is comparable. For very rare samples the XGBoosting trees model has the lowest error. For very frequent samples the Random Forest has the lowest error. However, the differences are very small and therefore may not be statistically significant.

To analyse the statistical significance, the right-hand side of Fig.
6
shows the bin-win-plot. However, instead of mitigation strategies, model types are compared here. This means that a model type that has won a bin offers better performance than the other two model types for the respective dataset.

Figure 6:
Performance evaluation of the three tested model types without any imbalance mitigation methods over 42 datasets. Left: The bin-error-plot. Right: The bin-win-plot. Not all bars have the same hight, as some datasets have bins without samples.

Focusing only on the statistical significant wins, the number of datasets won confirms that MLP models offer the best performance across all sample rarities. Albeit not having the smallest average error it wins more datasets in comparison to XGBoost and Random Forest. However, in about half of the datasets one of the other models performs better.

Overall, the XGBoosting trees model outperforms the Random Forest for (very) rare samples. This is understandable, if we have a look how the models work. The Random Forest model is an ensemble of multiple small tree models, which are generated by bootstrap-sampling. Each tree is not explicitly focused on rare points unless the points are significant enough to be included in the majority of trees. On the other hand, the XGBoosting trees model is also an ensemble, but it is trained with boosting and thus focuses iteratively on the hard-to-predict samples. This is done by assigning higher weights to them during training, as a result of which XGBoosting trees models inherently reduce model bias due to data imbalance.

The different properties of the models raise the question whether they also react differently to the various mitigation strategies. Therefore, in the following, the effect of different mitigation strategies on the model performance is evaluated.

4.2.2
Case study with synthetic data

In this section, we evaluate the performance of the relevance function and mitigation method combinations on 10 synthetic datasets. Each dataset contains 1000 samples. The case study is conducted with both clean and noisy data. For noise, each feature is assigned a normally distributed noise with a standard deviation of 3% of the standard deviation of said feature.

During the evaluation, we found that the results do not differ significantly between noisy and clean data. Since noisy data corresponds more to the reality of measured data, only the noisy results will be discussed.

In addition, we noticed that the interpolation-based relevance function is not able to reliably find relevance values for 6 of the 10 datasets (depending on the respective random split). For a further 2 datasets, the relevance function is unable to determine any relevance values at all. This is expressed by all the samples receiving the same binary relevance. The suspected cause is that the relevance function is only intended for distributions of normal-like shape with very marked trails due to the control point definition with boxplot statistics
(
Ribeiro 2011
)
.

The bin-error-plot across all synthetic datasets is shown in Fig.
7
. The left part of the graph shows the results from all tested combinations of relevance function, mitigation method and model type. For a better overview, only the best combination per mitigation method is shown on the right-hand side.

Figure 7:
Bin-error-plot of the synthetic datasets. Left: representation of all tested combinations of relevance function, mitigation method and model type. Right: presentation of the only best combination per mitigation method with respect to the very rare sample bin.

In the figure on the left, three clusters lines can be identified. The topmost cluster includes some outliers. The middle cluster includes models with acceptable performance, but with a significantly higher error for rare samples. The lowest cluster includes the mitigation strategies with the best performance.

All combinations in the upper cluster use cost-sensitive learning (DenseLoss or probabilistic loss) and the interpolation-based relevance function. As already mentioned, the relevance function cannot be used adequately for all datasets. Due to the parameterization by boxplot statistics, it also tends to rigorously assign a relevance of either 0 or 1 to many samples. It was originally developed for SMOGN and thus for sampling. However, for example, with DenseLoss, a large proportion of samples with a relevance of 0 has the effect that all these samples have close to zero influence on model training. This explains the poor performance for very frequent samples in particular. DenseLoss and probabilistic loss also perform significantly better with other relevance functions, suggesting that the outliers are not caused by the mitigation method.

The middle cluster contains all Random Forest and XGBoosting trees models. The lowest cluster only contains neural networks.

This is unexpected when considering the model type comparison in Sec.
4.2.1
, where the average error of all three model types without any mitigation methods is roughly on par. The difference can be explained by the fact that the synthetic datasets originate from regular (parametric) distributions. MLP neural networks are effective at capturing continuous, smooth, and well-structured relationships in the data and can thus efficiently learn the functional mapping between inputs and outputs. At the same time Random Forest and XGBoost model, based on decision trees, partition the feature space into discrete regions and do not benefit from the well-structured relationship. This hypothesis is supported by the fact that the normalized bin error of XGBoost and Random Forest is comparable in both scenarios (compare Fig.
6
and Fig.
7
), but the MLP performs significantly better when using synthetic data.

In the right figure of Fig.
7
, only the best mitigation strategy with the best model type is shown with respect to the normalized error in the very rare bin. In this case, all models are MLP neural networks and all have a decreasing error with increasing sample frequency. It is also noticeable that the best models trained with probabilistic loss or BMC on average perform worse in all bins than a model trained completely without a data imbalance mitigation strategy. This is somewhat surprising, but it is difficult to verify. The probabilistic loss article by
Sadouk et al. 2021
performs an evaluation based on a relevance threshold, which is not transferable to the approach used here. The BMC article by
Ren et al. 2022
only considers datasets for visual regression. However, according to the authors, BMC should be comparable to DenseLoss. The fact that BMC performs so much worse could be due to the fact that in BMC the weighting of the sample is formed depending on the training batch size. In order to train accurate models, we chose a small batch size of 16, but BMC would presumably benefit from a larger one.

All sampling-based mitigation methods help to improve performance for rare samples. However, this comes at the expense of decreasing the performance for frequent samples. For (very) frequent samples, not using any mitigation strategy offers the best performance, which is expected. It should be noted that WERCS, WSMOTER and cSMOGN do not have a significantly higher error in the frequent bins than the model without mitigation strategy. For rare and very rare samples, crbSMOGN with density-ratio relevance and SMOGN with density-distance relevance offer the best performance.

4.2.3
Case study with real world data

In this section, the applicability of the data imbalance mitigation strategies to real world datasets is evaluated. For this purpose, 42 datasets from different domains are used.

As in the case study with synthetic data, the interpolation-based relevance function is not able to reliably find relevance values or can not determine any relevance at all. In this case, 11 of the 42 datasets are affected. The computation of the BMC mitigation methods was omitted as the case study with synthetic data showed inconclusive results.

The bin-error-plot across all real world datasets is shown in Fig.
8
. The left part of the graph shows the results from all tested combinations of relevance function, mitigation method and model type. For a better overview, only the best combinations per mitigation method are shown on the right-hand side.

Figure 8:
Bin-error-plot of the real world datasets. Left: representation of all tested combinations of relevance function, mitigation method and model type. Right: presentation of the only best combination per mitigation method with respect to the very rare sample bin.

Compared to the case study with synthetic data, there is no clear preference for one model type using real world data. However, a rough tendency of the mitigation method performance can be observed independent of the relevance function used. The order is approximately crbSMOGN better than WSMOTER/cSMOGN better than WERCS better than DenseLoss/SMOGN better than probabilistic loss.

This is in line with
Camacho and Bacao 2024
who identified WSMOTER to outperform DenseLoss,
Steininger et al. 2021
and
Branco et al. 2019
who identified DenseLoss and WERCS to outperform SMOGN. However, all three studies are limited to the respective original relevance functions.

Considering the right side of Fig
8
, which shows only the best combination for each mitigation method, the following can be seen: Probabilistic loss has a negative impact on performance for real world data as well. Besides that, all other mitigation methods lead to a lower model error for (very) rare samples, compared to the model without mitigation strategy. However, with increasing sample frequency, the error gets greater than when no mitigation strategy is used. The use of imbalance mitigation strategies therefore seems to always lead to a loss of model performance at the other end of the frequency spectrum. This effect is especially evident for DenseLoss, which, as explained previously, is caused by assigning frequent samples a relevance of 0. Nevertheless, the strategies differ significantly in the amount of performance loss for frequent samples and in the amount of performance increase for rare samples. In a holistic view of the target value range, crbSMOGN with density-ratio relevance offers both the largest performance increase for rare samples as well as only a moderate performance loss for frequent samples.

From the normalized error, we can determine how large the influence of the mitigation strategies is on average across all 42 datasets. In order to test the generalizability to as broad a selection of datasets as possible, we also analyse the influence of the mitigation strategies on the number of datasets won in comparison to the models without mitigation strategies. In Fig.
9
shows the bin-win-plot.

Figure 9:
Bin-win-plots for the three model types comparing model trained with mitigation strategy to models trained without considering the very rare sample bin.

With regard to the XGBoosting trees models, it is noticeable that fewer significant wins are achieved compared to the other model types. In addition, the cost-sensitive learning approach DenseLoss performs better for XGBoosting trees. It can be deduced that XGBoosting trees models are less easily influenced by sampling-based mitigation methods. This can be explained, as the rare samples are already more strongly taken into account by the boosting procedure during training. In contrast, the MLP models and Random Forests with mitigation strategy achieve more significant wins. It can also be seen, that most mitigation strategies that are good for MLP models also tend to be effective for Random Forest models. However, the effectiveness is higher for MLP models.

A comparison of the individual mitigation strategies for MLP models with only over-sampling and with additional under-sampling shows that under-sampling always leads to a lower number of (significant) datasets won. Thus the conclusion can be made, that under-sampling does have a negative effect when using MLP models. This is not the case for the decision tree-based models, where under-sampling can have a positive effect for some strategies.

When looking at the normalized average error (Fig.
8
), crbSMOGN with density-ratio relevance turns out to be by far the best mitigation strategy to reduce the error for (very) rare samples using MLP models. Looking at the datasets won (Fig.
9
), crbSMOGN continues to perform best (most significant wins), but other methods can keep up. We conclude from this that even if multiple mitigation strategies have a positive effect on the error of rare samples and thus lead to a high number of datasets won, the magnitude is considerably larger for crbSMOGN.

Analysis of the other sample bins shows that the positive effect of a mitigation strategy decreases with increasing sample frequency. Looking at the frequent or very frequent sample bin, the models without mitigation strategy win almost all datasets. This is to be expected and has already been shown when looking at the normalized error. For the sake of space, we have therefore refrained from further illustration.

The question therefore arises as to whether this negative effect of mitigation strategies for (very) frequent samples can be avoided.

4.2.4
Case study with real world data using ensembles

This section investigates to which extend the negative effects of imbalance mitigation strategies on model performance for frequent samples can be avoided. For this purpose, the ensemble formation approaches presented in Sec.
3.3
based on the mean, the weighted mean or a threshold are examined. Each ensemble consists of a model that was trained without mitigation strategy and a model trained with the respective mitigation strategy.

The previous sections have shown that MLP models provide the best performance for imbalanced data and can also be influenced the most by imbalance mitigation strategies. Therefore, only MLP models are considered. In addition, not all combinations of mitigation methods and relevance functions are examined, but only those that achieved good results in the case study with real world data (see Sec.
4.2.3
).

Fig.
10
shows the bin-win-plots considering different the different ensemble-formation techniques and mitigation strategies.

Figure 10:
Bin-win-plots for different different mitigation strategies comparing ensemble-formation techniques considering the very rare and very frequent sample bin.

The left column shows the number of wins for the very rare sample bin. It can be seen that for all four mitigation strategies tested, the ensemble based on the mean wins the most datasets. Looking at the very frequent bin (right column), it can be seen that the ensemble formation also has a positive effect, as significantly more datasets can be won compared to individual models. For about half of the datasets, however, the model without mitigation strategy is still better.

The bin-error-plot is shown in Fig.
11
. It shows that the formation of an ensemble has only a small influence on the error of the (very) rare sample bins, but a large influence on the (very) frequent bins. An exception is crbSMOGN. In the very rare sample bin the crbSMOGN-based ensemble model has a higher approximation error than the individual model trained with crbSMOGN. However, the error is still lower than with all other mitigation strategies. For samples in the very frequent sample bin, the performance of all ensemble models is roughly similar regardless of the mitigation strategy, which is consistent with the number of datasets won (see Fig

).

In summary, by forming a mean-based ensemble, it is possible to avoid the performance loss of the model caused by the mitigation strategy for frequent samples to a large extent. It is often possible to create a model that has a lower error over the entire target value range than a model without a mitigation strategy. Here too, crbSMOGN with ratio-based relevance has proven to be the strongest mitigation strategy.

5
Discussion

In the previous sections, new relevance functions and mitigation methods were presented and evaluated. However, the approach chosen here is by no means beyond doubt, which is why we would like to put some points up for discussion.

First of all, it should be noted that none of the relevance functions really reflects how important a sample is for the modeling task in question. All relevance functions presented transfer the principle of the empirical frequency of the data points to a relevance value, which, depending on the model type, does not necessarily has to be decisive for the model performance. Only density-distance relevance and density-ratio relevance provide the flexibility to combine empirical frequency and domain preference, which makes it possible to incorporate the importance of the sample for the modeling task. However, this may also be detached from the difficulty the model has in correctly predicting the sample. The perfect relevance function would therefore give a high relevance to samples that are “hard-to-predict” rather then based on sample rarity. Determining in advance which sample is “hard to predict” is model- and application-dependent and thus a very difficult task, which is why we restrict ourselves to the empirical frequency and domain preference.

In this work, domain preference was assumed to be uniform for density-distance and density-ratio relevance. Theoretically, it would have been possible to use domain preference, however, then the comparability with relevance functions without domain preference would not be possible. In addition, expert knowledge of the domain is required to include the domain preference, which is not feasible to archive in a quantitative analysis.

Due to the assumption of uniform domain preference, density-distance relevance, kernel density relevance and DenseWeight differ only slightly. This is also reflected in the small performance differences in the evaluation, which can be largely attributed to statistical noise. The newly presented density-distance relevance should therefore by no means be seen as competition to kernel density relevance and DenseWeight, but rather as a generalization with possibility for expansion by including domain preference.

The evaluation showed that crbSMOGN is best able to avoid the biasing effect of data imbalance when using MLP models. However, we would like to highlight WERCS at this point. WERCS was originally introduced with the interpolation-based relevance using pchip
(
Branco et al. 2019
)
. However, if kernel density relevance or density-distance relevance are used, WERCS is surprisingly powerful despite its simplicity. It provides reasonable mitigation of the model bias while need low computational resources. This is also confirmed by other studies
(
Avelino et al. 2024
;
Branco et al. 2019
)
. The evaluation of the ensembles also showed that the simplest methods are often the effective ones. The threshold-, weighted mean-, and mean-based ensemble were implemented. Although the mean-based ensemble is the simplest approach, it turned out to be the most effective considering the full domain of target value.

We therefore recommend using WERCS in a mean-based ensemble, if the computational resources are small, the implementation should be simple or it is not necessary to achieve the best possible performance for rare samples. However, if the focus is mitigating the model bias as much as possible, we recommend a mean-based crbSMOGN. If the focus is on the lowest possible error for rare samples, we recommend a single model trained with crbSMOGN.

Finally, we would like to address the validity of the results. Even if many of the mitigation strategies have a positive influence on the models of most regression tasks, this is by no means always the case. The evaluation approach chosen in this article is quantitative. We have aimed to put it on statistically sound footing by selecting a large number of datasets from various application domains, repeating the modeling 50 times and using established statistical tests to test for statistical significance. However, outliers cannot be ruled out and it must be expected that the chosen mitigation strategy may not achieve any improvement for a specific dataset.

Furthermore, for the time being, no reliable statements can be made about which properties a dataset or a modeling task must fulfill for the use of a mitigation strategy to be successful in general.

6
Outlook

At this point, we would like to address open questions and problems that have arisen from our work.

At present, it is not possible to say in advance whether a mitigation strategy will be successful for the specific modeling task. During the evaluation is has become evident that not all mitigation strategies fail with the same datasets. It would therefore be interesting to investigate which specific characteristics datasets or modeling tasks must have in order for mitigation strategies to work particularly effectively or not. This would have the advantage that a priori of applying a mitigation approach a statement about the applicability could be made. This probably also depends on the distribution and importance of the individual features, which was not part of this work.

In addition, the questions arise as to how expert knowledge can be obtained to determine the domain preference and which of the two relevance functions allowing domain preference (density-distance and density-ratio relevance) is more powerful when using domain preference.

So far all density-based relevance functions used the Silverman’s rule of thumb to assess the bandwidth for the kernel and we adhered to it for comparability and simplicity. However, the Silverman bandwidth is non-optimal for non-normal distributions. In the future, we therefore recommend evaluating whether other bandwidth estimators, like the ISJ bandwidth, are more suitable.

The distribution of the modeling error over the target value domain is far from uniform even when using mitigation methods. Moreover, all sampling-based mitigation methods are based on the same "building blocks", such as interpolation between data points using SMOTE or the use of Gaussian noise. The development of new building blocks could bring substantial progress and allow the formation of new mitigation methods. For example, creating new samples with data-driven generative methods could come into play here.

In previous work by
Wibbeke et al. 2025
the mean imbalance ratio mIR was proposed to quantify data imbalance. However, here it was shown that imbalance mitigation strategies have different effects depending on the type of model. For example, although under-sampling can lead to a lower mIR, it has a negative effect on the performance of MLP models. A possible remedy could be the quantification of data imbalance using the imbalanced sample percentage of under-represented samples ISP
{}_{<t_{u}}

(
Wibbeke et al. 2025
)
, instead of the mIR. The ISP
{}_{<t_{u}}
only includes data points that are too rare and thus accounting for the fact that under-sampling might be harmful.

From a general perspective, however, we suggest separating the concept of purely empirical data imbalance (detectable by the mIR or ISP) from the non-uniform error distribution of the model over the target domain (e.g. model error imbalance). However, a way to quantify the model error imbalance requires further elaboration and investigation.

7
Conclusion

Data imbalance is a pervasive challenge in regression tasks, impacting the performance and reliability of predictive models to favor majority classes, resulting in biased performance towards frequently occurring data points. In this article, various approaches to avoid this model bias were presented, based on cost-sensitive learning or sampling.

It was shown that every mitigation strategy consists of two components. The relevance function, which assigns a value of importance to the data points, and the mitigation method, which uses the relevance for sampling or cost-sensitive learning. Together, the combination of relevance function and mitigation method results in a mitigation strategy to reduce the model bias. Based on this separation, the most common relevance functions and mitigation methods were identified and compared. In addition, two new approaches were presented for each.

The new relevance functions density-distance relevance and density-ratio relevance are based on the difference between the empirical frequency of the data points and any domain specific preference of the target variable, making it possible to combine the two for the first time. They offer a generalization of established approaches, which only consider either the empirical frequency or the domain preference. The newly proposed mitigation methods cSMOGN and crbSMOGN are based on established sampling methods, but offer advantages in that the user does not have to decide on a relevance threshold, as well as better sample selection process by discretization of the search space in the dataset.

In the evaluation, various combinations of the relevance functions and mitigation strategies were examined and the performance compared in a quantitative study. MLP neural network models, XGBoosting tree models and Random Forest models were used for this purpose. In order to be able to make statistically reliable statements, 10 synthetic and 42 real world datasets were used and the results were tested for statistical significance using the Wilcoxon signed rank test.

It has been shown that all mitigation strategies that reduce the model error for rare samples simultaneously increase the error for frequent samples. However, this effect can be circumvented by forming an ensemble of a model trained with mitigation strategy and a model trained without mitigation strategy.

The newly introduced mitigation method crbSMOGN with the associated ratio-based relevance function offers the best performance for rare samples in the case of a single model. If an ensemble is formed, the performance improvement for rare samples is not quite as strong, but the performance loss for frequent samples can be avoided. This effect can go so far that the ensemble has a lower error over the entire target value domain than a single model that was trained without the mitigation strategy.

The main take-away points of this work are:

•

Cost-sensitive learning approaches are better suited to mitigate the effect of data imbalance on XGBoosting tree models compared to sampling-based methods.

•

Sampling-based mitigation strategies are best to mitigate the biasing effect of data imbalance on error of MLP models.

–

Under-sampling causes a higher model error for frequent samples, while not increasing the performance for rare samples. Thus we recommend to only use over-sampling.

–

The best mitigation strategy to increase model performance for rare samples evaluated on 42 datasets is crbSMOGN with density-ratio relevance.

–

The choice of relevance function has less influence than the choice of mitigation method.

•

While decreasing the model error for rare samples, mitigation methods tend to increase the error for frequent samples.

–

This effect can be mitigated when constructing an ensemble.

•

The WERCS mitigation method with density-distance relevance shows surprisingly good performance albeit its simplicity.

•

Application recommendations:

–

If the predictive focus is on rare samples, use a MLP model trained with crbSMOGN and density-ratio relevance.

–

If the focus is to limit the model bias caused by data imbalance whilst also providing good model performance for frequent samples, use a mean-based ensemble of a MLP model trained with crbSMOGN and one without any mitigation strategy.

–

If the computational resources are small, a simple implementation is required or it is not necessary to achieve the best possible performance for rare samples, use a MLP model trained with WERCS and density-distance relevance in a mean-based ensemble.

As a future perspective, we suggest to separate the concept of data imbalance from the non-uniform distribution of the model error over the target domain (e.g. model error imbalance). However, a way to quantify the model error imbalance requires further elaboration and investigation.
