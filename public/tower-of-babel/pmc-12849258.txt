Targeting the MIR4435-2HG/miR-29c-3p/FSP1 axis overcomes lenvatinib resistance by inducing ferroptosis in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Congyue Zhang, Mengjiao Sun, Yue Shi, Jiawei Cui, Yaoyao Mao, Zhandong Lin, Xiaoqing Wu, Shaoya Li, Yuemin Nan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLenvatinib resistance is a major clinical challenge in advanced hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). While ferroptosis has emerged as a promising target to overcome therapy resistance, the mechanistic link between ferroptosis and lenvatinib resistance in HCC remains incompletely understood.MethodsWe analyzed clinical data from TCGA-LIHC and performed functional assays in two HCC cell lines. RNA antisense purification sequencing (RAP-seq) was used to identify downstream targets, and the mechanisms were validated by dual-luciferase reporter assays and Western blotting. Ferroptosis was assessed by measuring lipid reactive oxygen species (lipid ROS) and labile iron levels. Therapeutic efficacy was evaluated both in vitro and in vivo.ResultsMIR4435-2HG was upregulated in HCC and associated with poorer overall survival. It promoted malignant phenotypes and was further upregulated in lenvatinib-resistant cell lines. Mechanistically, MIR4435-2HG functioned as a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) by sponging miR-29c-3p, resulting in upregulation of FSP1. This axis suppressed ferroptosis, thereby conferring lenvatinib resistance. Knockdown of MIR4435-2HG or inhibition of FSP1 synergized with lenvatinib to induce ferroptosis and overcome resistance. The combination strategy significantly suppressed tumor growth in vivo without apparent systemic toxicity.ConclusionOur findings identify a previously uncharacterized MIR4435-2HG/miR-29c-3p/FSP1 axis that promotes lenvatinib resistance by inhibiting ferroptosis, highlighting that targeting this axis may provide a mechanistic basis and preclinical rationale for overcoming lenvatinib resistance in HCC.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-025-04147-5.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Lenvatinib resistance, LncRNA, MIR4435-2HG, Ferroptosis, FSP1, CeRNA

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with therapeutic options for advanced-stage disease remaining limited [1–5]. Lenvatinib, a multi-kinase inhibitor targeting Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor Receptor (VEGFR)1–3, Fibroblast Growth Factor Receptor (FGFR)1–4, Platelet-derived growth factor receptor (PDGFR)α, rearranged during transfection (RET), and Receptor Tyrosine Kinase (KIT), has emerged as a first-line therapy for unresectable HCC [6–8]. Despite initial efficacy, the development of resistance constitutes a significant clinical challenge that often leads to treatment failure [9, 10]. Real-world evidence indicates that while lenvatinib offers improved efficacy and tolerability over sorafenib, a substantial number of patients ultimately develop primary or acquired resistance [11, 12]. Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms underlying lenvatinib resistance is crucial for devising new strategies to improve patient outcomes.

Current evidence suggests that lenvatinib resistance is multifactorial, involving diverse molecular mechanisms. These include the activation of bypass signaling pathways such as the Nuclear Receptor Coactivator 5 - Serine/Threonine Kinase - mammalian target of rapamycin (NCOA5-AKT-mTOR) axis [13], Phosphatidylinositol-3 kinase/AKT/hypoxia inducible factor-1α (PI3K/AKT/HIF-1α) signaling [14], and the Hedgehog pathway [15]. Epigenetic regulation, mediated by factors like the ubiquitin specific peptidase 22 - Jumonji domain-containing protein 8 (USP22-JMJD8) axis and Enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2), has also been implicated [16, 17], with the latter potentially influencing ferroptosis. Additionally, metabolic and transport abnormalities, such as those involving the cholesterol transporter StAR Related Lipid Transfer Domain Containing 4 (STARD4) and the Ubiquitin Protein Ligase E3A - Sterol Regulatory Element Binding Transcription Factor 1 (UBE3A-SREBF1) axis, contribute to the resistant phenotype [18, 19]. Other reported mechanisms encompass elevated methyltransferase-like 8 (METTL8) expression and loss of Eva-1 homolog A (EVA1A) [20, 21]. Collectively, these findings illustrate the complex nature of resistance and hint at the potential involvement of ferroptosis-related pathways, including FSP1.

In this context, ferroptosis—an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death driven by lipid peroxidation—has emerged as a promising target for overcoming resistance to multiple cancer therapies [22–28]. Notably, the FSP1 pathway constitutes a glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) independent ferroptosis suppression mechanism [29]. A key regulator of this process is FSP1, which mediates a glutathione-independent antioxidant pathway and has been linked to therapy resistance in various cancers [30, 31]. Mechanistically, FSP1 suppresses ferroptosis primarily via the FSP1–CoQ10–NAD(P)H axis, utilizing NAD(P)H to reduce coenzyme Q10, thereby limiting lipid peroxidation [32, 33]. Its activity is regulated by N-myristoylation and can undergo phase separation, adding further layers of control [34, 35]. The recent development of icFSP1, a specific FSP1 inhibitor, provides a valuable tool to probe this pathway and a potential therapeutic strategy to reverse resistance [36]. Furthermore, FSP1 expression is modulated by upstream factors such as the transcription factor NRF2 and non-coding RNAs [37, 38]. Although FSP1 is a recognized ferroptosis inhibitor in cancer, its specific role in lenvatinib resistance in HCC remains incompletely understood. Given that long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are frequently dysregulated in cancer and serve as key regulators of gene expression, we hypothesized that an oncogenic lncRNA might contribute to FSP1 overexpression.

LncRNAs play an important role in cancer pathogenesis and therapy resistance [39–42]. Among them, MIR4435-2HG has been identified as an oncogene in multiple cancer types and often functions as a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) to modulate gene expression [43–46]. In HCC, recent studies describe MIR4435-2HG as an oncofetal lncRNA that is reactivated in tumors and correlates with poor prognosis [47]. Functionally, its overexpression enhances stem-cell-like traits and promotes tumor initiation [48]. Mechanistically, MIR4435-2HG can bind to the ribonucleoprotein Nucleolar Protein 58 (NOP58), stabilizing it to increase global 2’-O-methylation of rRNA and facilitate IRES-dependent translation of oncogenic mRNAs [47]. Its expression is also upregulated by the RNA-binding protein Insulin-like growth factor 2 mRNA-binding protein 1 (IGF2BP1) via N-6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification [46]. Outside HCC, MIR4435-2HG participates in various ceRNA networks and interacts with pathways such as Focal Adhesion Kinase (FAK)/AKT/β-catenin, transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), and PI3K/AKT [48–50]. Interestingly, it exhibits a tumor-suppressive function in colorectal cancer [51], highlighting its tissue-specific nature.

Collectively, these reports establish MIR4435-2HG as an oncogenic and oncofetal lncRNA in HCC, promoting stemness and malignant progression. Notably, emerging evidence has linked MIR4435-2HG to lenvatinib resistance; for example, Zhu et al. demonstrated that MIR4435‑2HG overexpression increases the IC₅₀ of lenvatinib and reduces drug sensitivity in HCC cells [47], and a related review on RNA modifications in HCC further supports its role in diminishing lenvatinib susceptibility [52]. Nevertheless, the precise molecular mechanism through which MIR4435‑2HG confers resistance—particularly whether it involves the regulation of ferroptosis—remains unclear. Moreover, the potential ceRNA network by which MIR4435‑2HG might modulate ferroptosis‑related effectors such as FSP1 in the setting of lenvatinib resistance has not been elucidated.

To address these gaps, we investigated whether MIR4435-2HG contributes to lenvatinib resistance in HCC by modulating ferroptosis. We identified a novel ceRNA network wherein MIR4435-2HG sponges miR-29c-3p, leading to the upregulation of FSP1, inhibition of ferroptosis, and promotion of lenvatinib resistance. Our results reveal a previously uncharacterized molecular pathway and suggest the therapeutic potential of targeting this axis to restore lenvatinib efficacy.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and transfection

LX2 (human hepatic stellate cells) and HCC cell lines (PLC/PRF5, Huh7, and HCCLM3) (Procell) were cultured at 37 °C in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO₂. Lenvatinib-resistant cell lines were established by continuously exposing parental Huh7 and PLC/PRF5 cells to stepwise-increasing concentrations of lenvatinib for approximately 6 months. Briefly, cells were first cultured in 2.5 µM lenvatinib for 3 weeks and then sequentially exposed to 5, 10, 20, 50, and 100 µM, with those concentrations maintained for 3, 3, 4, 5, 8 weeks. The drug concentration was increased only after the cells had regained normal proliferation for at least 3–4 consecutive passages. The IC50 of lenvatinib was assessed every 4 weeks using CCK-8 assays to monitor the development of resistance. For transfection, 2.5 × 105 PLC/PRF5 or Huh7 cells per well were seeded in 6-well plates and incubated overnight, then transiently transfected using Lipofectamine™ 3000 (Invitrogen, cat. No. L3000015) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Three siRNAs targeting MIR4435-2HG (si-MIR4435-2HG), a control siRNA (si-NC), a MIR4435-2HG overexpression plasmid (pcDNA3.1-MIR4435-2HG), and the empty pcDNA3.1 vector were purchased from GenePharma. miR-29c-3p mimics, inhibitors, and negative controls (miR-NC) were obtained from Ribobio. siRNA and primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

Quantitative Real-Time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol (Invitrogen, cat. No. 15596018CN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA synthesis was performed using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa, cat. No. RR047A), and qRT-PCR was conducted with TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (TaKaRa, cat. No. RR091A) on a 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). For miRNA quantification, the miDETECT A Track miRNA qRT-PCR Starter Kit (Ribobio) was used. Primer and siRNA sequences are provided in Table S1. mRNA expression levels were normalized to β-actin, miRNA levels to U6, and relative expression was calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method.

EdU cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was measured with the E-Click EdU Cell Proliferation Flow Cytometry Assay Kit (Elabscience, cat. No. E-CK-A370) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Transfected HCC cells were seeded at 5 × 104 cells per well in 6-well plates and incubated overnight, then labeled with 10 µM EdU for 2 h. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized for 15 min, and subjected to the click reaction for 30 min. EdU incorporation was analyzed by flow cytometry.

CCK-8 assay

Transfected Huh7 and PLC/PRF5 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 2 × 103 cells per well. At designated time points (0, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h), 100 µL of culture medium containing 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (GLPBIO, cat. No. GK10001) was added to each well. After 2 h of incubation at 37 °C, absorbance was measured at 450 nm with 630 nm as the reference using a microplate reader (BioTek).

Colony formation assay

To phenotypically validate stable lenvatinib resistance, colony formation assays were performed in parental and lenvatinib-resistant Huh7 and PLC/PRF5 cells. Briefly, cells were seeded at low density (500–1000 cells/well) in 6-well plates and cultured for 10–14 days. The medium was refreshed every 3–4 days. Given the observed altered growth morphology of the resistant cells (forming dense clusters instead of discrete colonies), the plates were photographed under a phase-contrast microscope to document the growth patterns. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for visualization.

TUNEL staining assay

Apoptosis was assessed using the One-Step TUNEL Apoptosis Assay Kit (Beyotime, cat. No. C1086) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Transfected cells were seeded at 8 × 104 cells per well in 12- or 24-well plates containing cover slips and cultured overnight. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, incubated with TUNEL solution for 1 h, and counterstained with DAPI for 10 min in the dark. Images from at least three randomly selected fields were captured using an Olympus fluorescence microscope and analyzed with ImageJ.

Ferroptosis assays

Lipid ROS was measured using C11-BODIPY 581/591 (Thermo Fisher, cat. No. D3861, 5 µM, 30 min incubation) and labile iron was quantified with FerroOrange (Dojindo, cat. No.F374, 1 µM, 30 min), analyzed by flow cytometry. Cell Total Iron Colorimetric Assay Kit (Elabscience, cat. No. E-BC-K880-M) via CCK8.

RAP-seq

RAP-seq was performed to identify RNAs directly associated with MIR4435-2HG. Briefly, approximately 4 × 10⁷ Huh7 or PLC/PRF5 cells were cross-linked with 1× PBS (containing 1.08 mL of 37% formaldehyde, resulting in a final concentration of 1% formaldehyde) for 10 min at room temperature to preserve RNA-protein and RNA-RNA interactions. After lysis and DNase treatment, the RNA was subjected to pull-down using biotinylated antisense DNA probes tiling the full-length MIR4435-2HG transcript. A parallel negative control pull-down was performed using probes targeting the E. coli LacZ gene. RNA-probe complexes were captured with streptavidin magnetic beads, stringently washed, and eluted. Following Proteinase K treatment and purification, sequencing libraries were constructed using the NEBNext® Ultra™ II RNA Library Prep Kit and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform (~ 50 million paired-end reads per sample).

Bioinformatic analysis followed a standard RNA-seq pipeline. Raw reads were quality-controlled (FastQC), trimmed (Trimmomatic), aligned to the GRCh38 genome (STAR), and counted (featureCounts). Differential RNA enrichment between the MIR4435-2HG and LacZ control samples was determined using DESeq2, with significant hits defined by |log₂(fold change)| ≥ 1 and adjusted p-value (FDR) < 0.05.

Tumor xenograft model

For in vivo studies, MIR4435-2HG-knockdown or control PLC/PRF5 cells (5 × 106 cells per mouse) were subcutaneously injected into the flanks of male BALB/c nude mice (4 weeks old). When the tumor volume reached approximately 500 mm³, the mice were randomly assigned into different treatment groups (n = 4 mice per group) using a random number table. Tumor dimensions (length and width) were measured with calipers every 3 days, and volumes were calculated using the formula: Volume = (length × width²)/2. Mice were weighed weekly and monitored for tumor growth. All animals were euthanized 7 weeks after injection by cervical dislocation under deep anesthesia induced with 5% isoflurane. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of The Third Hospital of Hebei Medical University (Approval No. W2024-004-1) and were conducted in accordance with the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020 edition).

Nuclear-cytoplasmic RNA isolation

Subcellular fractions from PLC/PRF5 and Huh7 cells were isolated using the PARIS Kit (Invitrogen, cat. No. AM1921) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The distribution of MIR4435-2HG in nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions was quantified by qRT-PCR, with β-actin as the internal control for the cytoplasmic fraction and U6 for the nuclear fraction.

RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)

Subcellular localization of MIR4435-2HG was determined by RNA FISH using a kit (RiboBio, cat. No. C10910) per established protocols. Cy3-labeled MIR4435-2HG probes were provided by RiboBio; human U6 and 18 S rRNA were used as nuclear and cytoplasmic controls, respectively. Nuclei were stained with DAPI, and images were acquired with an FV3000 confocal microscope.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

Potential interactions between MIR4435-2HG and miRNAs were predicted using RNAhybrid, and AIFM2 (FSP1)/miRNA interactions were assessed using TargetScan. For MIR4435-2HG, a wild-type (WT) fragment containing the predicted miR-29c-3p binding site was cloned downstream of the luciferase reporter. A mutant (MUT) construct was generated by site-directed mutagenesis of the 7-nt seed-matching region to disrupt base pairing with miR-29c-3p. WT or MUT MIR4435-2HG reporter constructs were co-transfected with miR-29c-3p mimics or negative control (miR-NC) into HEK293T cells using Lipofectamine™ 3000. After 48 h, Renilla luciferase activity was determined using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, cat. No.E1910) and normalized firefly luciferase activity.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted using RIPA buffer (Solarbio, cat. No.89900). Equal amounts of protein were separated on 8% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto 0.45 μm PVDF membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked in TBST containing 5% nonfat milk for 1 h and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against FSP1/AIFM2 (Proteintech, cat. no. 20886; 1:3,000) GPX4 (Abways, cat. No. CY6959; 1:1500) and GAPDH (Abways, cat. no. AB0037; 1:3,000). After three 10-minute washes in TBST, membranes were incubated with DyLight 800-conjugated secondary antibodies (Abbkine, cat. No A23920; 1:7,000) for 1 h at room temperature. Blots were imaged using an Odyssey infrared imaging system (LI-COR). GAPDH was used as a loading control for whole-cell lysates and Lamin B1 for nuclear extracts. All Western blot experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates.

Statistical analysis

Data presented in this study are from at least three independent biological replicates unless otherwise specified. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v8.0 and R (RStudio 4.4.1). RNA-seq and clinical data for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) patients were obtained from the TCGA-LIHC dataset. Overall survival was used as the clinical end point, and all survival analyses in this study are based on this cohort. For survival analysis, patients were dichotomized into high and low MIR4435-2HG expression groups based on the median expression value (50% cutoff). The combination index (CI) was calculated using the Chou-Talalay method, where CI < 1 indicates synergy. For experimental data, intergroup differences were assessed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test or Student’s t-test where appropriate. Data are presented mean ± SD. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

MIR4435-2HG is upregulated in HCC and predicts poor prognosis

To identify key molecules involved in lenvatinib resistance, we first analyzed the TCGA-LIHC dataset and found that MIR4435-2HG was significantly upregulated in HCC tissues compared with adjacent non-tumor tissues (Fig. 1A). Consistently, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed that patients with high MIR4435-2HG expression (based on the median cutoff) had significantly poorer overall survival than those with low expression (Fig. 1B). Additionally, MIR4435-2HG expression was elevated in HCC cell lines compared to normal hepatic stellate cells (Fig. 1C). Functionally, knockdown of MIR4435-2HG suppressed proliferation of Huh7 and PLC/PRF5 cells, whereas MIR4435-2HG overexpression promoted proliferation (Fig. 1D). Furthermore, after approximately six months of establishing lenvatinib-resistant cells in Huh7 and PLCPRF5 cell lines, the successful resistant cells exhibited a significantly higher IC50 compared to normal cells (Fig. 1E). MIR4435-2HG expression was further increased in acquired lenvatinib-resistant cells (Fig. 1F). In addition, colony formation assays were performed to phenotypically validate the resistant phenotype. In the presence of lenvatinib, Huh7-L-R and PLC/PRF5-L-R cells formed significantly more and larger colonies than their parental counterparts, indicating that the acquired resistance was stable and maintained at the clonogenic level (Fig. S1A). In these resistant cells, MIR4435-2HG knockdown inhibited cell migration and invasion, whereas MIR4435-2HG overexpression promoted these malignant phenotypes (Fig. 1G). Conversely, in parental (non-resistant) cells, MIR4435-2HG knockdown increased apoptosis, and this effect was further enhanced when combined with lenvatinib treatment. Rescue experiments confirmed that MIR4435-2HG overexpression attenuated lenvatinib-induced apoptosis (Fig. 2A). EdU proliferation assays demonstrated that MIR4435-2HG knockdown potentiated the inhibitory effects of lenvatinib on cell proliferation (Fig. 2B).

FSP1 is directly regulated by MIR4435-2HG and indicates poor prognosis in HCC

Having established MIR4435-2HG as a key player in lenvatinib resistance, we sought to elucidate its mechanism of action. Given that lncRNAs often exert their functions through interactions with other RNA molecules, we employed RNA antisense purification sequencing (RAP-seq) to comprehensively identify the direct RNA targets bound by MIR4435-2HG in HCC cells (Fig. 3A). This unbiased approach aimed to uncover the downstream effectors through which MIR4435-2HG promotes resistance. Sequencing of purified RNA from two HCC cell lines followed by KEGG enrichment analysis revealed significant associations with the spliceosome, RNA transport, and pathways in cancer in both cell lines (Fig.3 B, C). GO enrichment analysis indicated shared enrichment of oxidative stress-related terms. From the RAP-seq data, we identified seven mRNA candidates that consistently associated with MIR4435-2HG in both cell lines (Fig.4 A). Among these, we prioritized Ferroptosis Suppressor Protein 1 (FSP1) for further investigation based on two key criteria: firstly, its well-established role as a key inhibitor of ferroptosis, a process central to our hypothesis; and secondly, our cross-referencing of the RAP-seq results with StarBase and TCGA databases revealed a significant positive correlation between FSP1 and MIR4435-2HG expression in the TCGA-LIHC cohort (Fig.4 B; Spearman’s r = 0.36, 95% CI: 0.27–0.45). Analyses of TCGA and The Human Protein Atlas indicated that FSP1 expression was significantly elevated in HCC tumor tissues compared with normal liver tissues (Fig.4 C, D). And the mRNA and protein expression levels of FSP1 were observed to be higher in HCC cell lines than in LX2 cells via qRT-PCR and WB experiments (Fig.4 E, F). To directly assess the regulatory effect of MIR4435-2HG on FSP1 expression, we performed qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses. The results showed that knockdown of MIR4435-2HG decreased both mRNA (Fig.4 G) and protein (see Fig. 6A) levels of FSP1 in Huh7 and PLC/PRF5 cells, whereas its overexpression led to a marked upregulation.

MIR4435-2HG functions as a CeRNA by sponging miR-29c-3p

Since MIR4435-2HG does not encode a protein, we investigated how it regulates FSP1 expression. A common mechanism for cytoplasmic lncRNAs is to function as competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs) that ‘sponge’ miRNAs, thereby de-repressing their target mRNAs. To test this hypothesis for the MIR4435-2HG/FSP1 axis, we implemented a systematic screening strategy. We first cross-referenced our RAP-seq-identified MIR4435-2HG-associated transcripts with the StarBase database to predict miRNAs that could potentially target these mRNAs. We then refined this list by requiring candidate miRNAs to possess predicted binding sites not only for FSP1 (via TargetScan) but also for MIR4435-2HG itself (via RNAhybrid). To assess the clinical relevance of the MIR4435-2HG/miR-29c-3p/FSP1 axis, we analyzed their co-expression in the TCGA-LIHC cohort (Fig. S1B). This multi-step bioinformatic screening identified miR-29c-3p as the prime candidate capable of forming a coherent ceRNA network with MIR4435-2HG and FSP1 (Fig.5 A). qRT-PCR analysis confirmed low expression of miR-29c-3p in HCC cells (Fig.5 B). Knockdown of MIR4435-2HG increased miR-29c-3p expression (Fig.5 D), and subcellular localization analysis confirmed that MIR4435-2HG is predominantly localized to the cytoplasm (Fig.5 E, F), supporting its role as a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA). Based on binding sites predicted by RNAhybrid for MIR4435-2HG/miR-29c-3p and identified by manual alignment within the FSP1 3′-UTR, dual-luciferase reporter assays showed that miR-29c-3p directly interacts with both MIR4435-2HG and the FSP1 3′-UTR, as miR-29c-3p significantly reduced the luciferase activity of the WT MIR4435-2HG reporter, whereas this suppression was largely abolished when the miR-29c-3p seed-matching site in MIR4435-2HG was mutated (Fig.5 C, G; Fig. S1C). In line with these findings, MIR4435-2HG expression showed a negative correlation with miR-29c-3p and a positive correlation with FSP1 in HCC samples, supporting a regulatory relationship within this axis. To definitively establish that miR-29c-3p is the functional linker between MIR4435-2HG and FSP1, we performed rescue experiments. Critically, the upregulation of FSP1 protein induced by MIR4435-2HG overexpression was effectively reversed by co-transfection with miR-29c-3p mimics (Fig.5 H). Conversely, the reduction of FSP1 following MIR4435-2HG knockdown was restored by inhibiting miR-29c-3p. These data provide direct functional evidence that MIR4435-2HG regulates FSP1 expression by sequestering miR-29c-3p.

The MIR4435-2HG/FSP1 axis modulates ferroptosis with therapeutic potential

We next explored the functional role of the MIR4435-2HG/FSP1 axis. Western blot analysis confirmed that MIR4435-2HG regulates FSP1 protein levels (Fig. 6A) establishing FSP1 as a key effector of this axis. To precisely delineate the mechanism by which the MIR4435-2HG/FSP1 axis suppresses ferroptosis, we first examined its relationship with key established ferroptosis regulators. Western blot analysis revealed that GPX4 protein levels remained unaltered upon modulation of MIR4435-2HG or treatment with ferroptosis suppressor protein-1 (FSP1) inhibitor (icFSP1) (Fig. S1D), indicating that this axis functions independently of the canonical GPX4-mediated pathway. Furthermore, the mRNA expression of Acyl-CoA Synthetase Long Chain Family Member 4 (ACSL4), a critical enzyme for synthesizing pro-ferroptotic lipids, was not significantly changed in MIR4435-2HG knockdown or overexpression (Fig. S1E). These results demonstrate that the MIR4435-2HG/FSP1 axis constitutes a specific ferroptosis-suppressive pathway without engaging GPX4 or ACSL4. Next, we assessed the role of the MIR4435-2HG/FSP1 axis in this form of cell death. Knockdown of MIR4435-2HG significantly increased intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), lipid ROS, intracellular Fe2+, and total cellular iron levels in the presence of the FSP1 inhibitor icFSP1, indicating that the combination enhanced ferroptosis. Conversely, MIR4435-2HG overexpression produced the opposite effects, as confirmed by rescue experiments (Fig. 6B, C; Fig. S2, S3). Having established this genetic evidence, we assessed the therapeutic potential of pharmacologically targeting the MIR4435-2HG/FSP1 axis. We next examined the effect of icFSP1 and lenvatinib on cell viability. As shown in Fig. 6D, icFSP1 alone dose-dependently reduced cell viability in HCC cells and its IC₅₀ values were determined. We then combined icFSP1 with lenvatinib and calculated the combination index (CI) based on CCK-8 data from icFSP1 alone, lenvatinib alone, and icFSP1 + lenvatinib groups. The CI values (Fig. 6E) were consistently < 1 across the tested concentrations, indicating a synergistic interaction between the two drugs.

Combined targeting of MIR4435-2HG and FSP1 overcomes lenvatinib resistance

In lenvatinib-resistant models, FSP1 protein expression was markedly elevated (Fig. 7A). Notably, the combination of MIR4435-2HG knockdown and icFSP1 treatment most effectively reversed the resistant phenotype (Fig. 7B, C), supporting the therapeutic potential of this combinatorial strategy. In parental cells, the combination produced significantly greater suppression of proliferation and induction of apoptosis compared with monotherapies (Fig. S4A, B). Collectively, these findings indicate that the MIR4435-2HG/FSP1 axis plays a critical role in regulating drug sensitivity and resistance via suppression of ferroptosis.

Targeting the MIR4435-2HG/FSP1 axis restores lenvatinib sensitivity in vivo

Finally, we evaluated the efficacy of this combination strategy in vivo. In a subcutaneous xenograft model established with both lenvatinib-resistant and parental cells and treated with lenvatinib, the combination of MIR4435-2HG knockdown (shMIR4435-2HG), icFSP1, and lenvatinib produced the most pronounced suppression of tumor growth (Fig. 8A), as evidenced by reduced tumor volume and weight (Fig. 8B, C). No significant body weight loss was observed, suggesting a favorable safety profile (Fig. 8D). Iron content is significantly elevated within tumor tissues, suggesting that this enhanced antitumor effect is mechanistically linked to ferroptosis induction (Fig. 8E). Histological analysis confirmed decreased proliferation (Ki-67) and increased cell death (TUNEL) in the combination group (Fig. 8F). These in vivo data demonstrate that co-targeting the MIR4435-2HG/FSP1 axis represents a viable strategy to overcome lenvatinib resistance and enhance therapeutic efficacy.

Discussion

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains one of the most challenging malignancies worldwide due to its high incidence and mortality rates [1]. Lenvatinib, a multi-kinase inhibitor targeting VEGFR, FGFR, PDGFRα, RET, and KIT, has been established as a first-line treatment for advanced HCC [6, 7]. However, the development of resistance is a major clinical obstacle. Recent advances have revealed that lenvatinib resistance arises from a multifactorial network involving bypass signaling activation (e.g., PI3K/AKT, NCOA5–AKT), metabolic reprogramming, epigenetic alterations, and dysregulation of regulated cell death pathways [13–19]. Within this complex landscape, our study identifies the MIR4435-2HG/miR-29c-3p/FSP1 axis as a previously uncharacterized mechanism operating at the interface of lncRNA‑mediated gene regulation and ferroptosis suppression. Rather than acting in isolation, this axis complements and extends the existing understanding of resistance by revealing how a ceRNA network can directly modulate a key ferroptosis defense protein (FSP1), thereby contributing to lenvatinib tolerance. Rather than supplanting previously described pathways, our work adds a significant layer of understanding by linking lncRNA-mediated ceRNA networks to the suppression of ferroptosis and the modulation of lenvatinib sensitivity.

Our findings robustly establish the suppression of ferroptosis as a pivotal mechanism in lenvatinib resistance, while acknowledging that clinical resistance likely stems from the convergence of multiple parallel pathways. Within this complex network, the MIR4435-2HG/miR-29c-3p/FSP1 axis functions as a critical effector. FSP1 exerts its protective role through the well-characterized FSP1–CoQ10–NAD(P)H antioxidant axis, which operates independently of the canonical GPX4 and system Xc − pathways. Pharmacological inhibition of FSP1 by agents such as icFSP1—which disrupts its membrane localization and induces phase separation effectively sensitized HCC cells to lenvatinib-induced ferroptosis [34, 35], confirming the therapeutic relevance of this axis. Moreover, we unveil a novel post-transcriptional regulatory layer, wherein FSP1 expression is controlled by the MIR4435-2HG/miR-29c-3p ceRNA network. This adds a new dimension to the regulation of FSP1, complementing known upstream factors such as the transcription factor NRF2 and other epigenetic mechanisms [36–38]. The integration of lncRNA-mediated regulation with FSP1’s enzymatic and structural functions underscores the sophisticated control of ferroptosis in the context of therapeutic resistance.

Our findings do not merely add another parallel pathway but integrate the MIR4435-2HG/miR-29c-3p/FSP1 axis into the broader resistance network through multiple points of convergence with previously reported mechanisms. First, it directly extends the emerging understanding of ferroptosis as a determinant of lenvatinib response, showing that lncRNA‑mediated FSP1 upregulation represents a novel regulatory layer over this cell death process. This aligns with and reinforces previous reports that inducing ferroptosis via other pathways (e.g., UBE3A–SREBF1) can restore drug sensitivity [19], collectively solidifying ferroptosis induction as a critical therapeutic strategy. Second, it complements studies on metabolic reprogramming (e.g., STARD4, UBE3A‑SREBF1) by demonstrating that lipid peroxidation defense can be orchestrated through a non‑coding RNA circuit. Third, it bridges epigenetic regulation (e.g., EZH2, m6A modification) and upstream oncogenic signaling (e.g., METTL1/WDR4/EGFR [53]) with ferroptosis suppression, suggesting that MIR4435-2HG may serve as a downstream effector that integrates multiple upstream inputs to promote survival. Finally, it connects with the tumor microenvironment (TME) and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT): MIR4435-2HG has been shown to promote EMT [44], and lenvatinib resistance is often linked to stemness/EMT features [54, 55]. Thus, MIR4435-2HG may coordinate a dual program—FSP1‑mediated ferroptosis blockade and EMT/stemness activation—that together fortify the resistant phenotype. In summary, the MIR4435-2HG/miR-29c-3p/FSP1 axis acts as a critical integrative node within a multi-layered resistance network, linking upstream signaling, epigenetic regulation, and TME influences to downstream control of ferroptosis and cell state plasticity.

Our work connects two pivotal areas in cancer biology: lncRNA-mediated gene regulation and ferroptosis. Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death driven by lipid peroxidation, has emerged as a promising avenue to overcome therapy resistance [25–29]. We demonstrate that FSP1, a key glutathione-independent inhibitor of lipid peroxidation [30, 31], is significantly upregulated in lenvatinib-resistant HCC cells. The potent synergistic effect observed between lenvatinib and the FSP1 inhibitor icFSP1 underscores the therapeutic potential of co-targeting kinase signaling and ferroptosis defense pathways. Notably, our data conclusively show that the MIR4435-2HG/FSP1 pathway operates without altering the expression of GPX4 or ACSL4. This positions FSP1 as the primary and non-redundant effector in this context. The profound induction of ferroptosis upon disrupting this axis, even with intact GPX4 and baseline ACSL4, highlights that overcoming resistance specifically hinges on inactivating the FSP1-based defense system.

The oncogenic roles of the lncRNA MIR4435-2HG in HCC are increasingly recognized. Recent studies have characterized it as an oncofetal lncRNA, driven by m6A modification and capable of enhancing stemness and tumor initiation [47, 56]. Mechanistically, it has been shown to stabilize NOP58, thereby modulating ribosomal function and promoting the translation of oncogenic mRNAs [47]. However, prior to our study, its function in the context of targeted therapy resistance and its potential regulation of ferroptosis remained unexplored. We now extend this knowledge by demonstrating that MIR4435-2HG expression is further elevated in acquired lenvatinib-resistant cells and that it critically modulates ferroptosis to promote the resistant phenotype.

To molecularly link MIR4435-2HG to FSP1, we employed a systematic screening strategy that integrated RAP-seq data with miRNA target predictions. This approach identified miR-29c-3p as the key linker, revealing that MIR4435-2HG functions as a molecular sponge for miR-29c-3p, which in turn directly targets and represses FSP1. This specific MIR4435-2HG/miR-29c-3p/FSP1 circuitry is distinct from the previously described mechanisms involving NOP58-dependent ribosomal modification [47] or other miRNA axes like miR-1252-5p/STAT1 that regulate stemness [48]. Our discovery directly links MIR4435-2HG to the regulation of a specific cell death pathway, namely ferroptosis, in the setting of lenvatinib resistance, thereby positioning it not only as a driver of tumorigenesis but also as a central mediator of treatment failure.

This finding significantly refines the understanding of MIR4435-2HG’s functional spectrum across cancers, underscoring its context-dependent roles. While MIR4435-2HG has been reported to confer resistance to chemotherapeutic agents like cisplatin in several malignancies [56–58], and to drive oxaliplatin resistance in colorectal cancer via the miR-378 g/ABCB9 axis [48], or promote gemcitabine resistance in pancreatic cancer [47], our data reveal a distinct and previously unreported mechanism in hepatocellular carcinoma. Here, MIR4435-2HG drives resistance to the targeted agent lenvatinib specifically through a ferroptosis-suppressive mechanism mediated by FSP1 upregulation. This highlights a tissue- and therapy-specific role of MIR4435-2HG and underscores the unique contribution of our work in linking a lncRNA-driven ceRNA network to ferroptosis regulation in the context of targeted therapy resistance.

The translational implications of our findings are promising. We demonstrate that the combined targeting of this axis—through genetic silencing of MIR4435-2HG and pharmacological inhibition of FSP1—synergizes with lenvatinib to induce ferroptosis and suppress tumor growth in vivo, without apparent systemic toxicity. This provides a compelling preclinical rationale for a novel combination strategy against lenvatinib-resistant HCC.

Despite these insights, our study has several limitations. First, analysis of the MIR4435-2HG/miR-29c-3p/FSP1 axis in the TCGA-LIHC cohort revealed correlation trends consistent with our model (negative between miR-29c-3p and both FSP1 and MIR4435-2HG); however, these associations did not reach statistical significance. This may be attributable to the cellular heterogeneity of bulk tumor tissues, which can dilute cell type-specific ceRNA interactions. While the directional consistency provides tentative support, it also underscores the importance of functional validation in controlled systems. Moreover, the clinical relevance is based on a cohort lacking lenvatinib treatment history, necessitating validation in cohorts receiving this therapy. Second, although we established lenvatinib-resistant cells and confirmed their phenotype in vitro, further in vivo validation of the resistance model would strengthen our conclusions. The sample size in our animal studies, while sufficient to demonstrate primary effects, was relatively small. Third, while our investigation focused on FSP1-mediated ferroptosis, we did not assess key ferroptosis markers like lipid peroxidation or GPX4/FSP1 expression in clinical specimens. Thus, the in vivo evidence for ferroptosis remains indirect. We also acknowledge that FSP1 has functions beyond ferroptosis suppression, including roles in mitochondrial metabolism [31]. Although our data strongly support ferroptosis induction as the primary mechanism, contributions from the disruption of other processes cannot be fully excluded. Future prospective studies collecting pre- and post-lenvatinib treatment tumor samples are essential to validate MIR4435-2HG and FSP1 as predictive biomarkers. Investigations involving larger animal cohorts and exploring the axis’s interplay with other FSP1 functions or immunotherapy are also warranted.

In conclusion, our study unveils a novel MIR4435-2HG/miR-29c-3p/FSP1 signaling axis that confers lenvatinib resistance in HCC by inhibiting ferroptosis. These findings significantly expand the functional repertoire of MIR4435-2HG and provide a solid mechanistic foundation for overcoming lenvatinib resistance by co-targeting this newly identified pathway.
