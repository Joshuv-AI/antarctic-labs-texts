Polymer semiconductor blends with remarkably stable semiconducting performance under large and cyclic mechanical deformation
Authors: Chenying Gao, Kaiyuan Chenchai, Lin Huang, Cheng Li, Xisha Zhang, Guanxin Zhang, Jingjing Shao, Haoran Zhang, Guoming Liu, Zhaomin Hou, Deqing Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this paper, we report deformable blend thin films of polymer semiconductors with PDPPTT (p-type) and N2200 (n-type) as the examples by using the hydrogenated polyisoprene (H-PIP) as the newly developed elastomer. As compared to the respective blends with other elastomers, the blends with H-PIP bear lower elastic moduli and higher crack on-set strains, and in particular exhibit remarkably stable semiconducting performance under large and cyclic mechanical deformations. This aligns with the observation that the assembly structures of polymer semiconductors are stable within the H-PIP matrix based on AFM and GIWAXS characterizations. This exceptional performance is attributed to the unique structure of H-PIP, which is solely composed of mobile aliphatic-hydrocarbon chains without chemical/physical crosslinks. This enables the blend thin films with H-PIP to follow the mechanical deformations of the substrate without generating internal stress and affecting the interconnected assembly networks of the polymer semiconductor, thus leading to stable semiconducting performance.Subject terms: Electronic devices, Conjugated polymers

Introduction

In recent decades, significant advancements have been made in the field of polymer semiconductors1–5. Various new conjugated backbones have been designed and synthesized, successfully incorporated into the structures of polymer semiconductors6–16, and new methods for improving interchain packing and thin-film crystallinity have been developed17–27. These efforts have led to the emergence of high-performance p-, n-, and ambipolar polymer semiconductors with improved charge mobilities21,28–32. Furthermore, these materials have demonstrated promising applications in thin-film field-effect transistors and other functional devices, including sensors, synaptic devices, and flexible, large-area displays33–36.

Simultaneously, the development of polymer semiconductors with high charge mobility and excellent ductility, capable of withstanding mechanical deformation such as stretching, bending, and poking while maintaining their performance, has garnered increasing attention in recent years37–45 Such materials are essential for wearable and conformable electronics, which hold significant potential in fields such as robotics46, health monitoring33, and human/machine interfaces47,48. Polymer semiconductors rival polycrystalline silicon in terms of softness, stretchability, and mechanical tolerance for large strains (50–80%)49–51. However, most polymer semiconductors exhibit limited ductility due to their semi-crystalline thin-film structures and morphologies52. Specifically, the microstructure of polymer semiconductors presents a trade-off between their semiconducting performance and ductility: while closely packed, ordered interchain structures enhance charge transport, they also contribute to rigidity and brittleness43.

To address this, various strategies have been explored to enhance the ductility of polymer semiconductors. These approaches include geometric designs53, buckling induction54, and molecular-level modifications45,50,55 to provide intrinsic stretchability. For instance, Bao and other groups reported a series of intrinsically stretchable polymer semiconductors by incorporating rigid, fused conjugated rings with non-conjugated segments56, ligand units57, or additional donor groups40,45,57 into the backbones to reduce thin-film crystallinity while retaining charge mobility. Our recent work has demonstrated that incorporating non-centrosymmetric spiro[cycloalkane-1,9’-fluorene] units into diketopyrrolopyrrole (DPP)-based conjugated polymers results in stretchable semiconductors with record mobility of up to 3.1 cm2V−1s−1 at 150% strain, and 1.4 cm2V−1s−1 after 1000 stretching-releasing cycles45. Meanwhile, side-chain modifications were also successfully utilized to improve ductility of polymer semiconductors58–61. However, charge mobilities were compromised in most cases.

Alternatively, physical blending of polymer semiconductors with low-modulus elastomers has emerged as an efficient and cost-effective approach to generate elastic semiconducting blends that maintain semiconducting performance under mechanical deformation, such as stretching39,62–65. This strategy leverages the nano-confinement effect37: the polymer semiconductor is confined within the elastomer matrix, enhancing the polymer chain dynamics in the amorphous regions and restricting large crystallite growth, which improves mechanical ductility. Additionally, nanofibrils of the polymer semiconductor are formed inside the elastomer matrix, and their good connectivity maintains charge transport. For example, Bao and co-workers introduced the conjugated-polymer/elastomer phase separation-induced elasticity methodology37. This approach imparted superior mechanical properties to the blend, allowing the film to stretch to 100% strain without compromising semiconducting performance. However, the charge mobility of the blend thin film significantly decreased after 100 cycles of stretching and releasing at 100% strain. Han and coworkers optimized the solution-processing conditions to improve the mechanical and semiconducting performances of the blend thin films of polymer semiconductors and elastomers66.

Encouraged by the success of nano-confinement strategy, blends of high-performance polymer semiconductors with various elastomers have been recently explored (Fig. 1a). Moreover, new elastomers with amide groups were specifically designed to tune the phase separation between the polymer semiconductor and elastomer and the mechanical and electrical properties of the resulting blends67. It is interesting to note that the blends of polymer semiconductors with a multiblock polyurethane elastomer entailing oligothiophene show high charge mobility and good stretchability simultaneously68. While these blends inherit good mechanical ductility from the elastomers, charge mobility often decreases after repeated stretching-releasing cycles under large strains (see Supplementary Table S1). Improving the long-term electrical stability of these blends, particularly under high mechanical deformation, remains challenging.

Ideally, the elastomeric phase-separated network should efficiently dissipate strain energy while preserving the integrity of the polymer semiconductor network during cyclic mechanical deformation. Given that elastomers not only contribute to mechanical ductility but also play a critical role in maintaining stable charge transport behavior through repeated stretching cycles, exploring the potential of new elastomers with unconventional structures and components for the fabrication of high-performance polymer semiconductor blends is of great interest and importance.

In this work, we report the use of an elastomer, hydrogenated polyisoprene (H-PIP)69, for the fabrication of a family of deformable high performance polymer semiconductor blends (Fig. 1b). The H-PIP elastomer, prepared by the hydrogenation of polyisoprene with a 69/31 ratio of 3,4- and cis−1,4-units, exhibits good mechanical ductility and self-healability69. The selection of H-PIP as the elastomer is based on the following considerations: i) Unlike conventional elastomers such as SEBS, PDMS, or PU37,39,63,65, H-PIP is composed solely of aliphatic hydrocarbon chains without rigid hard segments. Its elasticity originates from the entanglements of polymer chains at the nanoscale; ii) As a result, the mobile chains of H-PIP are expected to undergo rapid disentanglement and re-entanglement in response to mechanical deformations. This adaptive mechanism helps maintain the integrity of the polymer semiconductor network embedded in the H-PIP matrix even after prolonged cycles of stretching and releasing (as illustrated in Fig. 1c); iii) In addition, the chemically inert nature of H-PIP—free from aromatic rings and polar functional groups—minimizes specific interactions with the polymer semiconductor, thereby promoting clean phase separation and preserving the purity and continuity of the semiconducting domain; iv) Finally, H-PIP exhibits low mechanical modulus and an exceptionally high elongation at break, making it particularly well-suited for highly stretchable electronic applications.

The results show that a blend thin film of H-PIP and a representative p-type polymer semiconductor, poly{[2,5-bis(2-decyltetradecyl)3,6-di(thiophen-2-yl)diketopyrrolo[3,4-c]pyrrole1,4-dione]-alt-thieno[3,2-b]thiophen} (PDPPTT), exhibits good deformability, with a mechanical modulus as low as 6.5 MPa, while maintaining high charge mobility up to 1.8 cm2V-1s-1 without compromising the semiconductor’s performance. Remarkably, the blend thin film also exhibits stable electrical performance under 100% strain for up to 5000 cycles of stretching. Moreover, it can retain stable charge mobility under other large and cyclic mechanical deformations, such as poking, twisting, and bending. A blend thin film of H-PIP with a typical n-type polymer semiconductor, poly{[N,N’-bis(2-octyldodecyl)-naphthalene-1,4,5,8-bis(dicarboximide)−2,6-diyl]-alt-5,5’-(2,2’-bithiophene)} (N2200), also maintains satisfactory semiconducting performance after 5000 cycles of stretching at 100% strain, further demonstrating the high potential of the H-PIP elastomer for preparing high performance polymer semiconductor blends.

Results

Preparation and characterizations of PDPTT and H-PIP blend thin films

The blend thin film of PDPPTT and H-PIP (Mn = 232 kDa, Mw/Mn = 1.4 with the hydrogenation level of 98%, see page S3) can be prepared at various weight ratios. As it will be discussed below, the charge transport properties of PDPPTT are maintained even when the weight ratio of H-PIP reaches 80% (see Supplementary Figs. S9, S10). On the basis of the trade-off between mechanical property and electrical performance, the blend thin films of PDPTT and H-PIP, in which the weight ratio of PDPPTT vs H-PIP was kept to be 2:8, were prepared by spin-coating for the following studies. Figure 2 presents the atomic force microscopy (AFM) phase images of both the top and bottom surfaces of the PDPPTT & H-PIP blend thin film. To obtain the AFM image of the bottom surface, the blend thin film on an octadecyltrichlorosilane (OTS)-treated Si/SiO2 substrate was first peeled off using a polydimethyl-siloxane (PDMS) stamp, then transferred to another PDMS stamp to flip the film, and finally transferred back to the Si/SiO2 substrate. Based on previous studies on blends of elastomers and semiconducting polymers37,39,63,65,70, the AFM image of the top surface indicates phase separation, with PDPPTT polymer chains aggregating into interconnected nanofibers with an average width of ~20 nm, and the nanofibers are uniformly distributed (see Supplementary Fig. S13). This is further confirmed by the observation of interconnected nanofibers after immersing the blend thin film in cyclohexane to remove H-PIP (see Supplementary Fig. S14). Meanwhile, the bottom surface image reveals bundles of nanofibers with tighter entanglement than the top surface (see Supplementary Fig. S15). This difference may arise from the interactions between the PDPPTT polymer chains and the OTS-treated Si/SiO2 substrate. In comparison, in the PDPPTT&SEBS blend, PDPPTT chains aggregate into longer nanofibers, which further bundle into larger fiber domains ranging from 30 to 210 nm in width as shown in Supplementary Fig. S13.

Phase separation also occurs vertically within the PDPPTT&H-PIP blend, as evidenced by time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) characterization results. Figure 2b shows the ToF-SIMS depth profile of the blend thin film. The mass spectrometric signals of C3H3- (representing H-PIP) and S- (representing PDPPTT) were used to track the depth distribution of the components. The intensity of the S- signal, representing the content of PDPPTT, is initially high at the surface but decreases with depth, reaching a minimum at approximately one-fifth of the total thickness. Beyond this depth, the S- signal intensity increases again. In contrast, the C3H3- signal intensity, reflecting the content of H-PIP, follows an opposite trend. To further verify the vertical phase separation, XPS depth profiling was conducted, showing that the atomic ratio of S to C first decreases and then increases as the depth increases. Given that PDPPTT contains sulfur atoms while H-PIP does not, the XPS data align well with the ToF-SIMS results. These mass spectrometric and XPS findings confirm that PDPPTT is predominantly located at the top and bottom layers of the blend thin film, which is consistent with previous reports37.

Phase separation within the PDPPTT&H-PIP blend is also supported by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) measurements. Supplementary Figure S12 shows the DSC curves of the PDPPTT&H-PIP blend and pristine H-PIP, from which the respective glass-transition temperatures (Tg) were measured to be −9.0 °C and −8.9 °C. It is noted that no phase transitions were detected for pristine PDPPTT under the same conditions. The Tg of the blend closely matches that of the pristine H-PIP elastomer, indicating effective separation of the two components within the blend, preserving their distinct physical properties. For comparison, the DSC curves of the PDPPTT&SEBS blend (at a weight ratio of 80% for SEBS) and pristine SEBS were measured similarly as shown in Supplementary Fig. S12. The Tg of the PDPPTT&SEBS blend is found to be 2 °C higher than that of pristine SEBS, likely due to interactions between the phenyl groups in SEBS and the conjugated backbone of PDPPTT, which alter the glass transition temperature. In contrast, H-PIP lacks conjugated units, reducing interactions with PDPPTT and leading to a Tg similar to that of pristine H-PIP.

Mechanical ductility of the blend of PDPPTT and H-PIP

The stretchability of the PDPPTT&H-PIP blend was evaluated by transferring the blend thin film onto a PDMS substrate and subjecting it to various mechanical strains. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S19, no cracks were observed in the blend thin film even under 150% strain, beyond which the PDMS substrate was ruptured. This demonstrates that the PDPPTT&H-PIP blend exhibits good stretchability, with a crack onset strain greater than 150%. Additionally, a film-on-water tensile test71 was performed to measure the strain-stress behavior. Based on the strain-stress curve, the elastic modulus and the fracture strain of the PDPPTT&H-PIP blend was estimated to be 6.5 MPa and 1620%, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S20). In comparison, the elastic modulus and the fracture strain of the PDPPTT&SEBS blend at an 80% weight ratio, measured under the same strain-stress conditions, was found to be 12.8 MPa and 828%, respectively. Furthermore, stress increases linearly with strain up to 15.5% for the PDPPTT&H-PIP blend, whereas the stress-strain relationship for the PDPPTT&SEBS blend is only linear up to 6.2% strain. These results highlight that the PDPPTT&H-PIP blend inherits the good mechanical ductility of H-PIP elastomer.

Furthermore, the cyclic stress–strain curves (Supplementary Fig. S21) were measured and the strain recovery and hysteresis energy loss were quantitatively evaluated. Both pristine and blend films of H-PIP display progressive softening (stress decrease at 100% strain) over cycles due to chain slippage and rearrangement, while those of SEBS show much less variation after the first cycle, attributed to the physical crosslinks formed by PS (polystyrene) domains that restrict chain mobility72,73. Both the pristine and blend thin films of H-PIP show a pronounced non-reversible stress-strain behavior as compared to SEBS and the respective blend with PDPPTT. Clearly, the pristine and blend thin films of H-PIP can be mechanically deformable, but the mechanical deformation is not reversible. Therefore, it can be concluded that the pristine and blend thin films of H-PIP display inferior elastic property as compared to SEBS and its blend with polymer semiconductor. In addition, the self-healing capability was not observed for the blend thin film of H-PIP with polymer semiconductor.

Polarized ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectroscopy studies also reveal that the PDPPTT&H-PIP blend exhibits good tolerance to cyclic stretching at large strains. The blend thin film was stretched to various strains up to 150%, and absorbances at 830 nm of the stretched films, with polarized light both parallel and perpendicular to the strain direction, were measured (Supplementary Figs. S23–S25). The corresponding dichroic ratios were calculated. As shown in Fig. 3a, the dichroic ratio of the blend steadily increased with strain due to strain-induced chain alignment, even at 150% strain. This indicates that the blend exhibits excellent stretchability during a single stretching process. Moreover, the blend thin film underwent cyclic stretching and releasing for 5000 cycles at 100% strain, followed by stretching to different strains to measure the respective dichroic ratios (Fig. 3b). Interestingly, the dichroic ratios increased continuously with strain up to 150%, further confirming that the PDPPTT&H-PIP blend can withstand large and cyclic mechanical deformations. In contrast, the dichroic ratios of the PDPPTT&SEBS blend, subjected to the same stretching conditions, began to decrease once the strain reached 125% during a single stretching process and 75% after cyclic stretching (Fig. 3). This suggests that the polymer chain arrangement in the PDPPTT&SEBS blend was damaged, possibly due to polymer chain slipping or rearrangement during cyclic loading, which negatively affected the semiconducting properties of the blend thin film.

Stable semiconducting performance under mechanical deformation

The blend thin films of PDPPTT and H-PIP were prepared by spin-coating the respective solutions at different weight ratios onto OTS-treated Si/SiO2 substrates with Au electrodes. Based on the transfer and output curves, the blend thin films exhibit typical p-type transport behavior, and hole mobilities were extracted using standard methods. Supplementary Figures S9, S10 show the plot of charge mobility versus the weight ratios of H-PIP in the blends. Clearly, the charge mobility of the blend thin films is not significantly affected by the weight ratio, which did not exceed 80%. The average charge mobility reached 2.13 ± 0.10 cm2V−1s−1 when the weight ratio of H-PIP in the blend was 80%. On the basis of the trade-off between mechanical property and electrical performance, the blend thin film of PDPPTT and H-PIP, in which the weight content of H-PIP was 80%, was used for the following studies.

Next, we investigated how the hole mobility of the blend thin film varies after mechanical deformation, such as cyclic stretching. The measurement details are provided in the Supporting Information. Briefly, the blend thin film on the OTS-treated Si/SiO2 substrate was transferred to a PDMS substrate after thermal annealing. Subsequently, the thin film on the PDMS substrate was subjected to cyclic mechanical deformation at different strain levels. Afterward, the film was transferred back to the OTS-treated Si/SiO2 substrate with Au electrodes to fabricate a bottom-gate bottom-contact (BGBC) transistor. For comparison, the blend thin film of PDPPTT&SEBS was prepared and treated similarly, and used for BGBC devices via the transfer-printing technique. Figure 4 shows the variations in charge mobility and drain currents after 3000 and 5000 cycles of repeated stretching and releasing under 100% strain. The charge mobilities of the transport channels, measured in both parallel and perpendicular directions to the stretching, were assessed. In the perpendicular direction, the charge mobility of the PDPPTT&H-PIP blend decreased slightly from the initial value of 2.04 ± 0.05 cm2V−1s−1 to 1.78 ± 0.17 cm2V−1s−1, then slightly increased to 1.82 ± 0.09 cm2V−1s−1 after 3000 and 5000 cycles of stretching and releasing, respectively. In the parallel direction, the charge mobility was measured to be 2.00 ± 0.05 cm2V−1s−1 and 1.83 ± 0.19 cm2V−1s−1 after 3000 and 5000 cycles of stretching and releasing, respectively. Remarkably, charge mobility of the blend of PDPPTT&H-PIP was only negligibly affected by the repeated stretching, even after 5000 cycles. Furthermore, the saturated drain currents in both directions were largely unaffected by the large and cyclic stretching. In comparison, the blend of PDPPTT&SEBS at an 80% weight ratio for SEBS showed a clear decrease in both charge mobility and saturated device current after undergoing the same cyclic stretching in both parallel and perpendicular directions (see Fig. 4). For instance, the charge mobility for the PDPPTT&SEBS blend was reduced by 63.9% and 81.1%, respectively, after 3000 and 5000 cycles of repeat stretching and releasing at 100% strain in parallel direction.

Considering the fact that some devices exhibited non-ideal transfer characteristics, we extracted the charge mobilities from the respective plots of Ids1/2 vs Vg at both low Vg and high Vg regions, leading to μ1 (at low Vg) and μ2 (at high Vg), as shown in Supplementary Figs. S29, S30. As listed in Supplementary Tables S9, S10, the PDPPTT&H-PIP blend shows good retention of both μ1 and μ2, while those of the PDPPTT&SEBS blend decrease in a large degree after cyclic mechanical deformations. Apart from charge mobility and on-state current, the on/off current ratio and threshold voltage were also compared. As summarized in Supplementary Tables S6–S8, the on/off current ratios of the PDPPTT&H-PIP blend-based devices remained nearly unchanged after cyclic deformation. The threshold voltages exhibited irregular variations. In contrast, devices based on the PDPPTT&SEBS blend showed clear reductions in both maximum current and on/off ratio under the same mechanical stress. Clearly, these results agree well with the observation that the PDPPTT&H-PIP blend shows remarkably stable semiconducting performance under cyclic and large mechanical deformations.

We also fabricated and characterized OFET devices based on PDPPTT&H-PIP and PDPPTT&SEBS blend thin films that were firstly subjected to 5000 cycles of stretching and releasing at 100% strain, and then stretched once more to 100% strain. The charge mobilities in the stretched state were measured to be 0.69 cm² V−1 s−1 for the PDPPTT&H-PIP blend and 0.22 cm² V−1 s−1 for the PDPPTT&SEBS blend (Supplementary Table S11). These values are significantly reduced compared to those obtained in the respective released states of both blends. This decrease in mobility correlates well with the observation of disconnection of PDPPTT nanofibers in the stretched state for both blends as it will be discussed below. Nonetheless, it is worth noting that even under the mechanically demanding stretched state, the PDPPTT&H-PIP blend retains relatively higher electrical performance than the PDPPTT&SEBS blend, further confirming the superior fatigue resistance and structural stability provided by the H-PIP matrix.

To further demonstrate the ability to withstand ultra-high mechanical deformation, charge mobilities were measured after the blend thin films underwent cyclic bending, poking, and twisting with a sharp object for 5000 cycles each. As shown in Fig. 5, the charge mobilities remained to be 1.79 ± 0.10 cm2V−1s−1, 0.88 ± 0.03 cm2V−1s−1, and 1.36 ± 0.11 cm2V−1s−1, respectively, after repeated bending, poking, and twisting for 5000 cycles. Moreover, the saturated device currents exhibited negligible changes despite these extensive deformations (see Fig. 5). These results underscore the good mechanical ductility and remarkably stable semiconducting performance of the blend of PDPPTT&H-PIP under large and cyclic deformations.

To understand why the thin film of PDPPTT&H-PIP exhibits remarkably stable semiconducting performance under large deformations, we performed AFM and GIWAXS characterizations. Figure 6 shows the AFM height image of the pristine PDPPTT&H-PIP blend, and those of the blend after repeated stretching-releasing cycles under 100% strain for 5000 cycles as well as the blend with one more time of stretching additionally. For comparison, the corresponding AFM height images for the PDPPTT&SEBS blend, which was treated in the same mechanical ways as the PDPP4T&H-PIP blend, were measured and included in Fig. 6. All thin films were pre-soaked in cyclohexane to dissolve the respective elastomers H-PIP and SEBS before recording the AFM images. In their pristine states, both blends exhibit well-organized nanostructures as shown in Fig. 6b, e. Upon cyclic stretching and releasing for 5000 cycles at 100% strain, the fiber network of the PDPPTT&H-PIP blend remains highly continuous in the released state (Fig. 6c) and slightly disconnected in the stretched state (Fig. 6d). These results indicate that the PDPPTT polymer chains retain their interconnected nanofiber structures, even after extensive mechanical deformation.

In comparison, the PDPPTT&SEBS blend shows significant disruption in the fiber network after cyclic stretching. Notably, the damage is more pronounced in the stretched state (Fig. 6g), where the PDPPTT domains become more disordered and disconnected. This comparison highlights the superior mechanical stability and continuity of the PDPPTT nanofiber network when blended with H-PIP, suggesting that H-PIP effectively maintains the integrity of the semiconducting phase during deformation. STEM (scanning transmission electron microscopy) images shown in Supplementary Fig. S16 also show that the integrity of the inter-connected nanofibers was well kept for the PDPPTT&H-PIP blend, whereas disconnection of nanofibers was observed for the PDPPTT&SEBS blend after repeated stretching and releasing for 5000 cycles. These findings provide crucial insights into the stable semiconducting performance of the PDPPTT&H-PIP blend under large and cyclic mechanical deformations. It is also noted that no obvious wrinkles were observed for the PDPPTT&H-PIP blend films, which were transferred onto PDMS substrates and subjected to 5000 cycles of stretching and releasing, on the basis of the optical microscopy images (see Supplementary Fig. S19).

Figure 7 shows the 2D GIWAXS patterns and the corresponding 1D line-cuts of the cyclically stretched blend thin film of PDPPTT&H-PIP, and the pristine blend of PDPPTT&H-PIP. The scattering pattern of the blend of PDPPTT&H-PIP after experiencing 5000 times of stretching-releasing cycles is similar to that of the pristine blending thin film. The lamellar (due to the stacking of alkyl chains) and interchain π-π stacking signals of the cyclically stretched blend of PDPPTT&H-PIP are almost overlapped with those of the pristine blend of PDPPTT&H-PIP on the basis of the in-plane and out-of-plane line-cuts (Fig. 7g–j). These results suggest that the interchain packing and orientations of PDPPTT within the blend of PDPPTT&H-PIP are preserved even after 5000 cycles of stretching and releasing. This is consistent with the stable semiconducting performance observed for the blend after large and cyclic mechanical deformations. In comparison, noticeable changes in GIWAXS patterns and the 1D line-cuts were observable for the blend of PDPPTT&SEBS after repeated stretching and releasing for 5000 times, by comparing with the respective pristine blend of PDPPTT&SEBS (see Fig. 7d–f). Noting that while the overall packing and orientation are maintained, slight stress-induced improvements in packing ordering of polymer chains may occur during repeated mechanical deformation45. This agrees well with the slight enhancement of charge mobility observed for the device with the PDPPTT&H-PIP blend after prolonging the repeated stretching and releasing from 3000 cycles to 5000 cycles.

The remarkably stable semiconducting properties of the PDPPTT&H-PIP blend under large and cyclic mechanical deformations can be attributed to the unique structural characteristics of the H-PIP elastomer, which consists solely of flexible aliphatic hydrocarbon chains free from chemical/physical crosslinks. As a result, the chains of H-PIP are mobile and constitute a temporary network of entanglements. Such structural characteristics of H-PIP enables H-PIP and the semiconducting blend thin film to follow the deformation of the PDMS substrate. Consequently, the strain energy due to the mechanical deformations of the substrate can be well dissipated without generating internal stress. Therefore, the integrity of the assembly networks of polymer semiconductor within the H-PIP matrix can be well maintained even after large and cyclic deformations. This aligns well with the fact that the interconnected assembly structures of PDPPTT were well kept after repeated stretching and releasing on the basis of the respective AFM and STEM images (see Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. S16) as well as the GIWAXS patterns (Fig. 7). As a result, the fact that the semiconducting blend with H-PIP shows remarkably stable semiconducting performance under large and cyclic mechanical deformations can be well understood.

In comparison, the SEBS blend with polymer semiconductor on PDMS substrate shows different behavior after mechanical deformations through the substrate. SEBS is an ABA-type block copolymer composed of polystyrene (PS) blocks as the hard segment and ethylene-butylene (EB) copolymer sequences as the soft segment. The soft segments are mobile upon mechanical deformations to show mechanical resilience, while the rigid segments are hardly movable. The blend thin film of SEBS and PDPPTT shows a recovery ratio of 73.7% (see Supplementary Fig. S21), which is higher than that of PDPPTT& H-PIP. The plastic deformation of SEBS is probably generated by the deformation, separation, and reorganization of the PS microdomains74. On the other hand, the PDMS substrate exhibits nearly 100% strain recovery (see Supplementary Fig. S22). Thus, unlike the blend thin film of H-PIP with mobile polymer chains, internal stress can be generated within the PDPPTTT&SEBS blend when the blend thin film is forced to return back to its original length. Such internal stress can be detrimental to the integrity of the assembly networks of polymer semiconductor after prolonging the stretching and releasing cycles. This agrees very well with the AFM and STEM images (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. S16) which clearly show the disconnection of the nanofiber networks of PDPPTT upon mechanical deformations through the PDMS substrate. Therefore, it becomes understandable that the charge transport property of the blend thin film of SEBS and PDPPTT decreases largely after mechanical deformations through the PDMS substrate.

Additionally, we prepared a partially hydrogenated PIP (PH-PIP) as an elastomer, in which ca. 60% carbon-carbon double bonds were hydrogenated, to demonstrate the effect of the elastomer structure on the semiconducting property of the blends after mechanical deformations. The physical blending of PDPPTT with PH-PIP was prepared as for the PDPPTT&H-PIP blend, and the weight ratio of PH-PIP in the blend was kept to be 80%. The AFM phase image (Supplementary Fig. S18) shows that polymer chains of PDPPTT are aggregated into inter-connected nanofibers within the PH-PIP matrix as in the H-PIP matrix. The charge mobility of the pristine PDPPTT&PH-PIP blend thin film was measured to be 0.80 cm² V−1 s−1, notably lower than that (2.04 cm² V−1 s−1) of the pristine PDPPTT&H-PIP blend. This is probably due to the low crystallinity of the aggregation domains of PDPPTT within the PH-PIP matrix. Moreover, the charge mobility of the PDPPTT&PH-PIP blend was further reduced to 0.27 cm2 V−1 s−1 after 5000 cycles of repeat stretching and releasing at 100% strain parallel to the charge transport direction (Supplementary Fig. S37). This agrees with the partial disconnection of the nanofibers of PDPPTT within the PDPPTT&PH-PIP blend after 5000 cycles of stretching and releasing at 100% strain (Supplementary Fig. S18). Being different from H-PIP, PH-PIP contains carbon-carbon double bonds and alkyl chains. Thus, as for SEBS hard and soft domains can be generated for PH-PIP through phase separation. Consequently, it can be expected that internal stress may be generated and thus the integrity of the assembly structures of PDPPPTT can be damaged, leading to further reduction of charge mobility for the PDPPTT&PH-PIP after mechanical deformations through the soft PDMS substrate.

Preparation, characterization and properties of N2200 and H-PIP blend thin film

To further demonstrate the versatility of our strategy for preparing stretchable polymer semiconductors, we investigated the blend of N2200 (a representative n-type polymer semiconductor, Fig. 1) and H-PIP at an 80% weight ratio for H-PIP. The AFM, XPS, and ToF-SIMS studies show phase separation within the N2200&H-PIP blend, being similar to that observed in the PDPPTT&H-PIP blend (see Supplementary Fig. S17). The AFM image of the top surface shows interconnected nanofibers formed by the assembly of N2200 polymer chains within the H-PIP matrix. Both ToF-SIMS and XPS analyses also show phase separation in the vertical direction, with N2200 predominantly distributed at the top and bottom layers of the blend thin film. Furthermore, the Tg of the N2200&H-PIP blend was measured to be −8.8 °C, which is similar to that of the H-PIP elastomer (see Supplementary Fig. S12). This can be also ascribed to the fact that H-PIP’s chemical composition—free from aromatic and polar functional groups—minimizes specific interactions with the polymer semiconductor, promoting clean phase separation and preserving the purity and continuity of the semiconducting network.

Similar to the PDPPTT&H-PIP blend, the N2200&H-PIP blend demonstrates excellent mechanical stretchability and stable semiconducting properties under large and cyclic strains (Fig. 8). No cracks were detected in the blend thin film even when subjected to 150% strain (Supplementary Fig. S19). The electron mobility of the N2200&H-PIP blend, which was evaluated by fabricating the BGBC devices, decreased slightly from 0.1 cm2V−1s−1 to 0.08 cm2V−1s−1 and 0.07 cm2V−1s−1 after 3000 and 5000 cycles of stretching and releasing in the parallel direction. When the repeated stretching and releasing was performed in the perpendicular direction, the charge mobility of the blend decreased from 0.1 cm2V−1s−1 to 0.08 cm2V−1s−1 and 0.07 cm2V−1s−1, respectively. Moreover, the variations of device currents in both parallel and perpendicular directions were rather small after the repeated stretching and releasing for 3000 and even 5000 cycles. Therefore, the semiconducting property of the blend of N2200&H-PIP can be largely maintained under cyclically stretching even at 100% strains. In comparison, the charge mobility of the blend of N2200&SEBS, which was prepared and subjected to cyclic stretching and releasing, followed by estimating the charge mobility in the same ways as for the blend of N2200&H-PIP, decreased in much larger degree (see Supplementary Figs. S34, S35) after repeated stretching and releasing for 3000 and 5000 times at 100% strain. Though the threshold voltages showed noticeable shifts in both elastomer systems after mechanical deformations, the higher retention of electron mobility, on-state current and on/off current ratio in the N2200&H-PIP blend clearly demonstrates its superior fatigue resistance (see Supplementary Tables S13–S16) by comparing the N2200&SEBS blend.

All error bars were calculated for 5 devices from at least 2 batches.

Discussion

In this paper, we present a strategy for preparing deformable polymer semiconductor thin films using a recently developed elastomer, H-PIP69. The blends of polymer semiconductors (such as PDPPTT and N2200) and H-PIP exhibit good mechanical ductility and remarkably stable semiconducting performance even under large-scale mechanical deformations. Specifically, these blends withstand repeated stretching and releasing (up to 5000 cycles) at 100% strain, as well as 5000 cycles of bending, poking, and twisting, with minimal degradation of semiconducting performance. The good mechanical and electrical properties of these semiconducting thin films correlate closely with their microstructures, as revealed by AFM and GIWAXS characterizations. AFM images reveal that the interconnected nanofibers of polymer semiconductors within the H-PIP matrix remain intact after cyclic stretching and releasing. Meanwhile, GIWAXS data show that the interchain packing of the polymer semiconductors is minimally affected by cyclic stretching and releasing of the blend thin film. In comparison to blends with other elastomers, such as SEBS, the H-PIP-based blends exhibit remarkably stable semiconducting property. This can be attributed to the structural characteristics of H-PIP, which is composed solely of mobile aliphatic hydrocarbon chains without rigid hard segments. This structural feature will enable the disentanglement and re-entanglement of polymer chains of H-PIP to follow the mechanical deformations without generating internal stress and affecting integrity of the assembly networks of polymer semiconductor within the H-PIP matrix. Moreover, H-PIP contains no aromatic and polar groups, leading to weak interactions between the chains of the polymer semiconductor and H-PIP, which is beneficial for promoting phase separation and formation of continuous semiconducting domains. Our findings highlight the critical role of elastomer structure in determining both the mechanical and semiconducting properties of the blends. Among the elastomers explored for preparing semiconducting blend, H-PIP stands out as the most effective one, enabling the fabrication of polymer semiconductor blends with good mechanical ductility and exceptionally stable semiconducting performance under large and cyclic mechanical deformations. The use of H-PIP as the elastomer makes this blending strategy-simple, scalable, and highly versatile-particularly attractive for the preparation of stretchable semiconducting thin films, which are crucial for flexible electronics.

Methods

Device fabrication with blend thin films after deformations

For the fabrication of bottom-gate/bottom-contact FETs, the n-type Si wafer with a SiO2 layer of 300 nm and capacitance of 11.0 nF/cm2 was used as the gate electrode and dielectric layer, respectively. The gold source and drain bottom electrodes (with Ti as the adhesion layer, W/L = 50 μm/1400 μm) were patterned by photolithography on a silicon dioxide surface. The substrates were firstly cleaned with immersion in Piranha solution (volume ratio of H2SO4 and H2O2 was 2:1) for 30 min. Then, the substrates were rinsed sequentially with deionized water, acetone, and isopropanol, and were dried with N2 and baked at 80 °C for 1.0 h in the vacuum drying oven. Last, the substrates were placed into a petri dish with one drop of octadecyltrichlorosilane (OTS) in the middle of the petri dish. After being stored under vacuum at 120 °C for 3 h, the substrates were washed sequentially with chloroform, hexane, and isopropanol.

The blend thin films of polymer semiconductors were prepared as follows: (i) solutions of polymer semiconductors (5 mg/mL in chloroform) and elastomers (60 mg/mL in chloroform) were separately prepared, (ii) the solutions of polymer semiconductors and elastomers were mixed at designed weight ratios and stirred at 60 °C for at least 1.0 h, and (iii) approximately 20 μL of the mixture solution was drop-casted onto OTS-treated Si/SiO2 substrates, followed by spin-coating at 2000 rpm for 1.0 min, and thermal annealing at 120 °C for 10 min. Samples containing N2200 were handled under nitrogen atmosphere.

For transistors with thin films after cyclic stretching, blended films were spin-coated onto OTS-treated Si/SiO2 substrates at 2000 rpm for 60 s and annealed at 120 °C for 10 min. The thin films were then transferred onto PDMS substrates and subjected to repeat stretching–releasing cycles at 100% strain under nitrogen. After mechanical cycling, thin films were transferred back onto OTS-treated Si/SiO2 substrates with Au electrodes to fabricate FETs.

For transistors with thin films after cyclic bending, the initial distance l0 between the two ends of the stretching machine was set to 2.0 cm, and the PDMS substrate with the attached film was securely fixed onto the machine. The X-axis deformation distance l1 is then set to 1.0 cm, and the cyclic number was set to 5000 times. After completing the cyclic bending, the PDMS was returned to its initial position, and the film was carefully transferred from the PDMS onto a Si/SiO2 wafer with pre-patterned source and drain electrodes to fabricate FETs.

For transistors with thin films after cyclic poking, a standard pencil with a tip diameter of ~2 mm was fixed onto the experimental platform. The PDMS substrate with the attached film was secured onto glass slides for ease of handling. The backside of the PDMS (the side without the polymer film) was aligned with the pencil tip, and pressure was applied to depress it by ~1.5 cm before releasing. This process was repeated 5000 times, after which the PDMS was restored to its initial position. The film was then transferred onto a Si/SiO2 wafer with source and drain electrodes to fabricate FETs.

For transistors with thin films after cyclic twisting, the PDMS substrate with the attached film was similarly fixed onto glass slides for ease of operation. The two glass slides were simultaneously twisted in opposite directions by ~180 degrees and then twisted back in the reverse direction by another 180 degrees. This twisting process was repeated 5000 times, after which the PDMS was returned to its initial position. Finally, the film was transferred from the PDMS onto a Si/SiO2 wafer with source and drain electrodes to fabricate FETs. All the cyclic strain processes under different deformation modes were operated under nitrogen atmosphere.

Thin film characterizations

For the XPS and ToF-SIMS characterizations, blend thin films (PDPPTT&H-PIP, PDPPTT&SEBS, N2200&H-PIP, and N2200&SEBS) at different weight ratios were prepared by spin-coating the mixed solutions onto Si substrates, at 2000 rpm for 60 s, followed by annealing at 120 °C for 10 min. The n-type semiconducting films were prepared under nitrogen atmosphere.

For the GIWAXS and AFM characterizations of the cyclically stretched thin films, the thin films were prepared and cyclically stretched in the same ways as for the FETs measurements. Once the stretching-releasing cycles on the PDMS were completed, the thin films were transferred to the substrates for measurements.

For the STEM-HAADF characterizations, blend thin films before mechanical stretching were directly transferred using pre-cast PDMS stamps onto Si/SiO2 substrates pre-coated with a poly(styrene sulfonic acid) sodium salt (PSSNa) sacrificial layer, followed by thermal annealing at 120 °C for 10 min. For the post-cyclic-strain samples, the blend films were first transferred onto PDMS, subjected to 5000 cycles of stretching and releasing at 100% strain, and subsequently transferred onto PSSNa-coated Si/SiO2 substrates under the same conditions. Each sample was then slowly immersed into water at an angle of 45°, allowing the sacrificial PSSNa layer to dissolve. The floating blend films were carefully collected onto copper TEM grids.

For the SEM characterizations, thin films were firstly spin-coated onto Si substrates at 2000 rpm for 60 s and then annealed at 120 °C for 10 min. Considering the blend thin films may be poorly conductive, additional specimens were sputter-coated with a thin Pt layer to enhance conductivity for comparison.

The strain-stress behaviors of the PDPPTT&H-PIP and PDPPTT&SEBS blend thin films and H-PIP, SEBS elastomer thin films were investigated using the film-on-water (FOW) method.
