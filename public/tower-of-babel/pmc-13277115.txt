The six phases of prolonged veno-venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenator support: a conceptual framework
Authors: Kenneth R. Hoffman, Arne Diehl, Paul Nixon, Sarah A. Yong, Eddy Fan, Carol Hodgson, Aidan J. C. Burrell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Veno-venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (V-V ECMO) is a complex and invasive intervention used increasingly in the management of severe respiratory failure. Once established, the management of prolonged V-V ECMO support involves balancing priorities of lung protection, prevention of pulmonary complications, sedation weaning and safe reduction in ECMO support. These occur variably as the pulmonary pathology resolves and ECMO support is reduced. Evidence based strategies to assist clinicians during prolonged V-V ECMO support are lacking, with the majority of literature focussing on ventilation strategies following ECMO initiation and the criteria for separation from V-V ECMO once liberation is considered safe. Practice is largely clinician and institution dependent with significant heterogeneity. There are numerous questions which remain unanswered regarding the prioritisation and strategies for safe V-V ECMO weaning. This review aimed to provide a novel conceptual framework to assist clinicians by dividing prolonged V-V ECMO support into six phases: ultra-lung-protective, lung protective, transition to spontaneous breathing, liberation trial, decannulation and post decannulation support. We reviewed existing literature and identified knowledge gaps for future research.

Background

Veno-venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (V-V ECMO) is a complex, invasive and resource intensive intervention used in the management of severe refractory hypoxic respiratory failure and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) [1, 2]. Initiation of V-V ECMO is indicated for severe but reversible lung disease, or as a bridge to transplant in severe irreversible lung failure [1, 3]. Weaning is the process whereby ECMO support is gradually reduced as the recovering lung contributes to a greater amount of oxygen and carbon dioxide gas exchange. In the setting of prolonged V-V ECMO support, the timing and management of weaning is important as weaning ECMO too rapidly risks ventilator inducted lung injury (VILI) potentially delaying recovery [4]. Weaning too slowly risks excessively prolonged ECMO runs, exposing patients to risk of infection, more sedation, anticoagulation, potentially worse long-term disability and increased resource usage and costs.

Prolonged V-V ECMO support can be defined as > 14 days or > 21 days, with reported survival rates with both definitions around 45% [5, 6]. In our practice, we consider > 14 days to be prolonged, as those supported for shorter durations than this typically have a more predictable clinical course with rapid resolution of lung pathology. Most literature examining V-V ECMO management focuses on the period of mandatory mechanical ventilation immediately following initiation and the final stage of liberation from ECMO [7]. However, there is a lack of guidance on how best to progress patients from mandatory ventilation modes to spontaneous breathing and subsequent ECMO liberation [8]. Patients supported on V-V ECMO with COVID-19, where median duration of V-V ECMO was often extended (increased from 9.1 to 14.8 days [9], highlighted that each of these periods could be prolonged and associated with complex decisions balancing risks and benefits. Unanswered questions include: how long should lung rest ventilator settings be maintained [1, 10, 11], when should sedation weaning commence, when to allow resumption of spontaneous breathing, whether to prioritise weaning of V-V ECMO or mechanical ventilation and when should extubation on V-V ECMO occur.

The goal of this review was to create a conceptual framework to describe clearly the different stages of management during prolonged V-V ECMO support. The intention was not to provide an exhaustive review of each phase, rather to provide an overall framework to guide clinical practice. Knowledge gaps for further discussion and consensus are identified to guide future prospective research evaluating the optimisation of lung protection during V-V ECMO support.

Current understanding and literature

Initial ventilator settings

The 2021 V-V ECMO Extracorporeal Life Support Organisation (ELSO) guidelines [1] provide an overview of ventilation management during V-V ECMO support. These state that the initial priority during V-V ECMO is to support gas exchange via the membrane oxygenator and use ventilator settings aimed to limit VILI. The selected mode is a mandatory mode with moderate positive end expiratory pressure (PEEP) (≥10 cmH2O), driving pressure (distending pressure above PEEP required to generate a tidal volume (Vt), calculated under static conditions with plateau pressure – PEEP) [12] limitation using either inspiratory pressure (≤15 cmH2O) or plateau pressure (≤25 cmH2O) and low to normal respiratory rate to provide “lung rest”. Collectively, this strategy is termed ultra-lung-protective ventilation [13–16]. The typical Vt and pressure limits used for standard ARDS management may provide insufficient protection in the most severe cases of ARDS with smaller regions of ventilated parenchyma [16]. As such, Vt is not targeted specifically and is dependent on respiratory system compliance in pressure-controlled modes of ventilation. During ultra-lung-protective ventilation, Vt typically ranges from 0 to 4 mL/kg predicted body weight depending on the distribution and severity of pulmonary involvement. If these settings do not achieve CO2 targets, the ECMO sweep gas flow (SGF) should be modified, rather than increasing ventilation and contributing to additional lung injury.

Literature discussing ventilation in V-V ECMO focuses largely on the initial 3-day period [17] during which Vt, PEEP, plateau pressure, driving pressure and respiratory rate are consistent with the ELSO recommendations [1, 17]. The underlying principle is that mechanical power through the lung should be minimised whilst the V-V ECMO can safely provide gas exchange to facilitate lung recovery. However, the approach to ventilation varies during this period with insufficient data supporting a specific method [18]. Collectively termed “lung rest”, approaches are ill-defined and range from ultra-lung-protective ventilation targeting ≤4mL/kg, pressure-controlled ventilation with no Vt target, through to apnoeic ventilation, an extreme extension of the principle [18, 19]. V-V ECMO for severe ARDS

VILI results from energy transfer from the ventilator to the alveoli causing damage. Heterogenous pulmonary parenchyma in ARDS [20] results in tidal overdistension of inflated alveoli (volutrauma) and cyclical collapse and expansion of recruitable alveoli (atelectrauma) with subsequent alveolar inflammation (biotrauma) [16–18]. Maintaining open alveoli with PEEP, whilst minimising injury through cyclic inflation is desirable. There may be a role for electrical impedance tomography for titration of optimal PEEP in this cohort [21, 22], although there is currently a lack of data on patient centred outcomes.

Apnoeic oxygenation relies on the sole use of PEEP to maintain open alveoli, with all gas exchange performed by the V-V ECMO. In a prospective trial comparing ultra-lung-protective ventilation with apnoeic oxygenation on V-V ECMO, apnoeic oxygenation reduced driving pressure, mean airway pressure and lung strain [18]. The somewhat reduced oxygen saturations with apnoeic oxygenation were offset by an apparent improvement in haemodynamics through right ventricular afterload reduction, with higher oxygen delivery [18]. Driving pressure is the only ventilator setting that is independently associated with mortality on V-V ECMO [17, 18], and apnoeic oxygenation may mitigate further VILI.

Liberation from V-V ECMO

Numerous articles have been published or are recruiting (Table 2) on the V-V ECMO liberation process. We define V-V ECMO liberation to include completion of a successful ECMO liberation trial and decannulation. Unsafe liberation as defined in the literature occurs if within 48 h of decannulation any of the following occur: death due to decannulation, the requirement for re-cannulation or a clinically significant deterioration attributable to premature removal of V-V ECMO support [23, 24].

Weaning usually occurs over several days with progressive reduction in SGF and ECMO blood flow as native pulmonary gas exchange improves [1]. This is supplemented by an ECMO liberation trial, during which the SGF is lowered or turned off, followed by a variable observation period [7, 24, 25]. Liberation trials are either clinician guided, when the treating clinician feels comfortable that native lung function will support gas exchange, or standardised, where a protocol is followed to reduce subjectivity [7, 26–31]. The standardised trials involve the patient meeting specific criteria prompting a trial of SGF cessation, or progressive, such as a proportional SGF reduction (i.e. 25% reduction in SGF over a number of hours [7). If native lung function allows adequate gas exchange during the liberation trial on acceptable ventilator settings decannulation is considered, whereby access and return cannulas are removed. Centres with protocolised approaches may recognise suitability for liberation earlier, conduct earlier spontaneous breathing trials and decannulate from higher levels of V-V ECMO support, resulting in shorter V-V ECMO durations [27, 28, 32].

Patients receiving V-V ECMO

A recent scoping review examined V-V ECMO liberation strategies, readiness criteria, liberation trials, criteria to perform decannulation and predicting decannulation outcomes [7]. This paper highlighted clinician and institution practice heterogeneity and the paucity of publications related to V-V ECMO liberation. Despite the growing literature on V-V ECMO, the complexity of demonstrating biologically plausible links between management strategies and measurable outcomes in a heterogenous cohort has meant few randomised controlled trials have compared different approaches [10, 11].

An international survey of 303 clinicians demonstrated the inconsistency of the perceived importance of criteria used to identify patients suitable for weaning from V-V ECMO and the criteria for successful SGF cessation [33]. This survey highlights the need for consensus statements and guidelines to support clinicians, including the broader challenges of managing mechanical ventilation throughout the weaning and liberation process. There may be a future role for machine learning to assist clinicians with decision making through integration of patient, ventilator and V-V ECMO data into predictive modelling [34–36].

The proposed phases of prolonged V-V ECMO support model

Phase 1 – Ultra-lung-protective ventilation

This phase starts when V-V ECMO commences for severe respiratory failure. Typically, V-V ECMO is initiated according to guideline criteria [3, 11] for hypoxia and/or ventilatory criteria [37–40]. Once cannulated, the initial priority is to prevent further ventilator induced lung injury. The highly inflamed lung is particularly vulnerable to further mechanical and biotrauma, so reducing mechanical power through the lung is prioritised. Given the risk of overdistension and the lack of demonstrated benefit, particularly with stepwise high-pressure recruitment strategies, we avoid routine recruitment manoeuvres in patients supported with V-V ECMO and instead prioritise ECMO-supported gas exchange [41, 42]. This period often requires high flow V-V ECMO support (> 5 L/min blood flow) [37]. Ventilation typically follows the ELSO ultra-lung-protective ventilator guidelines [1, 43]. However, with emerging literature associating driving pressure and mortality, apnoeic ventilation may provide further benefit [16–18] with several trials assessing this (Table 1).

On commencement of V-V ECMO, many patients also have a hypercapnic acidosis secondary to permissive hypercapnia or ventilatory failure [44]. As the ECMO circuit clearance of CO2 is efficiently controlled by the SGF rate, caution is required, as relative reductions in CO2 of > 50% in the first 24 h are associated with increased neurological complications including seizures, intracranial haemorrhage and brain death through rapid alterations in cerebrovascular tone [44]. ELSO guidelines recommend commencing sweep gas at 2 L per minute, and titrating to allow a gradual correction of CO2 and pH following initiation [1].

The presence of right ventricular dysfunction in patients supported with V-V ECMO is associated with high mortality [45, 46]. However, the commencement of V-V ECMO is a treatment in itself for right ventricular dysfunction. Following commencement, there is a reduction in right ventricular afterload with correction of both hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction and treatment of hypercarbia contributing to reductions in mean pulmonary artery pressures [47]. It is likely there is additional benefit of right ventricular afterload reduction from the reported decrease in ventilatory pressures following commencement of V-V ECMO [17].

A controversial question is the ideal duration of adhering to ultra-lung-protective settings before allowing increased ventilatory support. Progressing too early may worsen VILI, whilst delaying will prolong V-V ECMO support and risk additional sedation, paralysis and immobility.

Phase 2 – Lung protective ventilation

The transition to the lung protective phase occurs with improvements in pulmonary function. There is no clear definition of lung improvement in this setting; evaluation relies on improvements in oxygenation, CO2 clearance, respiratory system compliance and radiological improvements in aeration. There is no specifically defined threshold, but previously set ultra-lung-protective ventilation settings will yield increasing Vt with reduced plateau pressures as respiratory system compliance improves and gas exchange will improve on the same ECMO settings.

As static compliance is most reliably measured during passive conditions, we obtain measurements when the patient is sedated and paralysed, often for clinically indicated procedures such as bronchoscopy. Static compliance (Cstat) is calculated as Vt/(Pplateau-PEEP) during an end inspiratory hold. Whilst the number is arbitrary, our practice is to progress from the ultra-lung-protective phase to the lung protective phase when the Cstat improves to around 20mL/cmH2O.

To maintain lung protection, the ventilator should be reviewed frequently to ensure Vt is <6mL/kg predicted body weight to prevent volutrauma [48] and driving pressure minimised (ideally < 15cmH2O) to prevent barotrauma [17, 49]. Given the severity of lung damage in this cohort a cautious approach is warranted as ventilator settings traditionally considered safe may still result in VILI [50]. Ventilation may be transitioned from a pressure controlled to a volume-controlled mode, to reduce the risk of inadvertent volutrauma as compliance improves, with care to minimising driving pressures. In our practice in the lung protective phase the requirement for paralysis will cease, if not already ceased during the ultra-lung-protective phase.

Anecdotally, the lung protective phase is the most variable for prolonged support, possibly extending for weeks. In this setting, patients transition from the ultra-lung-protective phase and either stagnate with incomplete pulmonary recovery, or complications delay further progress. Ventilator acquired infections, pneumothoracies or sepsis with increased metabolic demand are the commonest complications delaying this phase. Remaining patient with weaning if these occur is paramount, as trying to progress too rapidly through this phase may contribute to complications and potential preventable mortality.

The primary clinical question that occurs is when to transition to spontaneous breathing, thereby reducing sedation, and commencing weaning of V-V ECMO support.

Phase 3 – Transition to spontaneous breathing

This phase begins when native lung function contributes to progressively more gas exchange, allowing reduction of membrane oxygenator support. Transitioning to spontaneous breathing is considered once several factors have improved: oxygenation, radiological appearance, resolving inflammatory reaction and acceptable Cstat (typically > 25mL/cmH2O). If Cstat subsequently falls or clinical instability develops, we defer spontaneous breathing, return to controlled ventilation and reoptimise sedation, ventilation and fluid balance before reassessing.

Balancing spontaneous respiratory effort and sedation are common clinical challenges. The actual process of weaning V-V ECMO support occurs through progressive reduction in SGF whilst monitoring CO2 and pH targets and patient respiratory effort. The procedure of weaning itself is straight forward; complexity arises from assessing the downstream effects with particular focus on preventing patient self-induced lung injury (P-SILI) through modest, non-injurious mechanical ventilator settings. Optimal ventilator management during the transition phase remains unresolved and complications from VILI may alter the subsequent clinical course.

P-SILI is a form of VILI which theoretically occurs when a patient makes large inspiratory efforts [51–57]. The key unifying mechanism is excessive transpulmonary pressure (alveolar pressure - pleural pressure) [58], whether generated by the ventilator, the patient, or both during assisted ventilation. Vigorous patient effort can produce disproportionally high and regionally heterogenous transpulmonary pressure on injured lungs, increasing regional stress and strain, particularly at the interface between dependent and non-dependent lung. This promotes atelectrauma and volutrauma through intrapulmonary gas redistribution (Pendelluft) thereby amplifying lung injury [56, 59].

There is evidence that establishing spontaneous ventilation during the transition phase can be beneficial by preventing diaphragm atrophy [38, 39, 60]. However, given the fragility of the recovering lung it is likely that further additional injury will result in prolongation of the requirement for V-V ECMO. Safe Vt during the transition period has not been elucidated, although excessive Vt >8mL/kg should be avoided.

Reducing sedation may result in significant spontaneous breathing effort, often with large Vt. Work of breathing describes the energy expended moving gas into the lung and broadly includes elastic work from the lung and chest wall and resistive work overcoming tissue and airway resistance [61]. It is commonly the limiting factor during weaning, with some patients exerting high respiratory effort, high respiratory rate, or both[29, 62]. The nature of these respiratory efforts varies, but there is a clear subgroup with ongoing poor compliance who generate transpulmonary pressures sufficient to cause P-SILI. Static compliance (Cstat=(Vt/(Pplateau-PEEP)) measured whilst paralysed during a mandatory breath is a measurable surrogate marker of the work required for lung inflation[63].

If the spontaneous respiratory pattern clinically appears to reflect observable respiratory distress or high work of breathing (tachypnoea, high respiratory effort, intercostal/subcostal recession, accessory muscle use), the compliance may still be poor with high measured transpulmonary pressures (via surrogate measures such as the P0.1 or oesophageal manometry [64) which may contribute to P-SILI [51]. Evidence supporting the use of P0.1 or oesophageal pressure monitoring with defined cut off values guiding decision making is lacking [7, 51]. For patients with severely reduced respiratory compliance, we adopt a conservative physiology guided strategy to transition from controlled ventilation to spontaneous breathing.

Unfortunately, respiratory drive is not reliably susceptible to control through standard chemical feedback of CO2 and pH through the medullary respiratory centre, particularly in ARDS [65]. Possible explanations include respiratory drive initiated by systemic inflammation and pulmonary and chest wall afferent inputs [9, 64]. The degree to which respiratory drive can be suppressed through V-V ECMO control of CO2 and pH through pharmacologic management is variable and needs to be established on an individual patient basis. Whether spontaneous ventilatory effort is safe for the patient should be judged case-by-case. If deterioration occurs, sedation should be increased and ventilation should return to a mandatory mode to facilitate lung protective ventilation and prevent P-SILI [51].

The transition to spontaneous breathing provides an opportunity to actively engage the patient in their care with a lower requirement for sedation. Around this time, discussion regarding the requirement and timing for tracheostomy is common. Prior to the point of reducing sedation, tracheostomy offers limited benefits and has risks, particularly given the anticoagulation requirements on ECMO [66]. In patients not suitable for sedation weaning, commonly due to delirium which is challenging to manage with concerns regarding securing ECMO cannulas, we delay tracheostomy insertion as the timing of tracheostomy does not appear to effect survival [66, 67]. In an alert patient, tracheostomy performed for patient comfort can facilitate mobilisation and active rehabilitation. In carefully selected patients with multidisciplinary expertise and appropriate staffing levels mobilisation and active rehabilitation may help preserve respiratory muscle function, reduce deconditioning, support more physiological patterns of ventilation and are associated with a lower probably of death on V-V ECMO [68].

However, for patients who tolerate spontaneous ventilation whilst still on V-V ECMO without any deterioration, extubation may be considered whilst still on ECMO [1]. This is a common strategy in those being bridged on V-V ECMO to lung transplant, especially those where expectoration is essential such as the cystic fibrosis population who benefit from physiotherapy prior to transplant availability. Prioritising mechanical ventilation liberation over liberation from ECMO is a safe strategy in appropriate patients[69], albeit associated with longer durations of V-V ECMO support [7].

Phase 4 – Liberation trial

Liberation trials occur as the final step in V-V ECMO weaning and are largely clinician and institution specific, although a protocolised approach may result in earlier liberation [11]. Once the patient is thought to have adequate pulmonary function, SGF is turned off with monitoring of adequate oxygenation and CO2 clearance, whilst observing clinical parameters such as respiratory rate and work of breathing[9] and objective measures such as dead space fraction, tidal volume, ventilator parameters and oesophageal pressure swing [7]. The trial duration prior to consideration of decannulation varies widely, ranging from one to 24 hours [7]. Standardised trials reduce the duration of V-V ECMO support, with patients decannulated on higher levels of support than clinician guided liberation [7].

Pulmonary efficiency should be considered at a liberation trial, as performing work without gas exchange due to dead space ventilation will increase the patient’s required minute ventilation. Dead space describes the proportion of Vt not participating in gas exchange and is associated with mortality in ARDS [70–72]. It causes a significant increase in respiratory effort by reducing the pulmonary clearance of CO2 for the same minute ventilation [70, 72, 73]. Factors associated with dead space ventilation are more closely associated with the likelihood of liberation success than oxygenation [7].

Direct dead space measurement requires collection of expired gases and calculation via the Bohr method. However, EtCO2 and PaCO2 are routinely measured, and the ratio between them should correlate with the dead space fraction via the modified Enghoff equation (PaCO2-EtCO2/PaCO2)70. Calculation of the dead space fraction using the EtCO2 and PaCO2 is difficult whilst on V-V ECMO support due to the CO2 clearance via the membrane oxygenator. However, integrating calculations of the dead space fraction into a liberation trial whilst the SGF is off and there is no CO2 clearance via the membrane oxygenator may assist clinical decision making at the bedside. This has recently been confirmed in a cohort using EtCO2/PaCO2 ratio, with an area under the ROC of 0.87 and a ratio ≥0.84 showing a positive likelihood ratio of 4.6 for successful decannulation [29].

Phase 5 – Decannulation

Decannulation occurs following a successful liberation trial and is usually protocolised with institution specific guidelines related to the initial cannulation strategy. Issues to consider include: checking a patient on pressure supported ventilation can be ventilated safely in a mandatory mode, antibiotic prophylaxis to mitigate septic shower risk when decannulating, haemoglobin adequacy to maintain adequate oxygen delivery, cessation of anticoagulation prior to cannula removal, the requirement for additional sedation for manual pressure required for haemostasis and the subsequent need to remain supine for several hours without excessive movements or coughing resulting in bleeding at the cannulation sites.

Phase 6 – Post decannulation

Management following decannulation is analogous to the recovery phase of very severe ARDS. The optimal management of ventilation following decannulation is undefined, although criteria are based on lung protective ventilation parameters [25–29]. Monitoring of respiratory function is essential however, as a proportion of patients deteriorate and require re-cannulation for V-V ECMO. Deterioration related to increases in metabolic demand or ventilatory requirements for secondary insults (e.g. sepsis or ventilator associated pneumonia) can be difficult to differentiate from those who deteriorate due to premature decannulation. The number of patients receiving multiple runs of V-V ECMO is increasing [74]. In the ELSO registry, adult patients who receive two runs of V-V ECMO have a survival of 53.6%, although case selection may result in survival bias in this group [74].

Future research

Future research in V-V ECMO requires the development of uniform language and definitions for terms such as ultra-lung-protective ventilation and weaning/liberation. Additionally, defining patient centred outcomes for comparison between interventions is required. Conclusion

The outlined framework provides a basis to conceptualise the phases of prolonged V-V ECMO support and the key considerations during each phase. The identification of these distinct phases draws attention to specific unanswered questions, with particular reference to ventilator management and allowing patient respiratory effort with spontaneous modes of ventilation. Future research will require the development of uniform definitions and patient centred outcomes regarding V-V ECMO weaning. Ultimately, phenotypic characterisation of patients using individual patient data will guide progression through these phases of weaning and become a key domain in clinical practice in V-V ECMO.
