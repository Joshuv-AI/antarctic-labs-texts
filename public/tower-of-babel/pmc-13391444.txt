Intraoperative respiratory exchange ratio to predict complications after cardiopulmonary bypass surgery: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Sophia Riesemann, Luca J Wachtendorf, Ben L P Braeuer, Sebastian D Sahli, Béla-Simon Paschold, Theresa Tenge, Carsten Marcus, Peter Santer, Robina Matyal, Maximilian S Schaefer, Mark J Robitaille
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe respiratory exchange ratio (RER), defined as the ratio of carbon dioxide (CO2) production to oxygen (O2) consumption, may be a non-invasive and continuously measurable alternative to lactate for identifying patients at risk of postoperative complications that has been examined in non-cardiac surgery. We investigated whether intraoperative RER predicts blood lactate levels and postoperative complications in cardiac surgery. This retrospective cohort study included adult patients undergoing cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston, USA, between 2008 and 2020. Intraoperative minute-by-minute data of inspired and expired fractions of CO2 and O2 were analyzed. Univariable and a priori-defined multivariable logistic regression models were used to evaluate the association between the median RER during surgery, intraoperative lactate and 7-day major postoperative complications (European Perioperative Clinical Outcome Definitions). 324,646 RER calculations of 4,058 patients were included. 1,745 (43.0%) patients experienced 7-day postoperative complications. The median (IQR) RER was 0.64 (0.48–0.92) before and 0.65 (0.48–0.97) after bypass. The RER within 10 min prior to lactate testing was associated with lactate levels > 2 mmol/l (adjusted odds ratio [ORadj] 1.11, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.01–1.23, p = 0.031). Neither RER before nor after bypass was associated with 7-day postoperative complications (before bypass: ORadj 1.02, 95%CI 0.95–1.10, p = 0.55; after bypass: ORadj 1.03, 95%CI 0.95–1.13, p = 0.46). While RER showed a modest relationship with hyperlactatemia, its utility as a predictor of postoperative complications appears limited in real-world data. Introduction

Inadequate systemic oxygen delivery is a hallmark of multiple conditions driving morbidity and mortality following cardiac surgery [1]. Markers of impaired oxygen delivery, such as rising lactate, decreased mixed-venous oxygen saturation (SvO2), or changes in the respiratory quotient (RQ), defined as ratio of carbon dioxide production (VCO2) to oxygen consumption (VO2), are widely used in clinical practice and have repeatedly demonstrated utility in predicting morbidity and mortality [2–6]. However, particularly lactate levels lag behind the onset of anaerobic metabolism, leading to delayed identification of impaired systemic oxygen delivery. Furthermore, the use of lactate may necessitate frequent blood draws or sampling from invasive lines and laboratory analyses [7]. In the case of SvO2 and RQ, invasive pulmonary artery catheterization or indirect calorimetry is required [4, 6].

By contrast, the respiratory exchange ratio (RER) is a novel method to estimate the RQ directly from inspiratory and expiratory flows of anesthesia gas analyzers and is thereby ubiquitously and continuously available in mechanically ventilated patients, potentially serving as early marker for adverse events [8–10]. In patients undergoing non-cardiac surgery, higher RER values (i.e., ≥ 1) have been shown to precede elevated lactate levels and to occur among patients who subsequently developed major postoperative complications, including acute kidney injury or stroke [10–14]. However, the use of RER as a predictor of inadequate systemic oxygen delivery in patients undergoing cardiac surgery has not been studied before, and previous results may not be generalizable to cardiac surgery due to the unique physiologic and ventilatory conditions.

In this retrospective cohort study, we evaluated the ability of the intraoperative RER, calculated from real-world clinical data, to predict intraoperative lactate levels and postoperative complications in patients undergoing cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

We included adult patients undergoing general anesthesia for cardiac surgery requiring cardiopulmonary bypass between 2008 and 2020 at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC), Boston, Massachusetts, USA with available data on continuous fractional O2 and CO2 before or after bypass. Patients with an American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification of ≥ V were excluded. The complete case approach was applied for all analyses. Intraoperative anesthesia gas analyzer data, patient and procedural characteristics, as well as postoperative outcome data were extracted from internal hospital electronic databases, merged into a unified dataset, and de-identified (Online Resource 1, 1.1). This retrospective cohort study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at BIDMC and the requirement for written informed consent was waived (protocol number: 2024P0000620). The manuscript follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (Online Resource 2) [15].

Definition of exposure and outcome

RER was calculated as a dimensionless value from minute-by-minute recordings of inspired (Fi) and expired (FET) O2 and CO2 fractions [%]. A validated formula by Hoffman et al. and Waldau et al. was applied: RER = (FETCO2 – FiCO2) / (FiO2 – FETO2) [8–10]. Data on FiCO2 were unavailable from the anesthesia record and were assumed to equal zero [16]. FETCO2 [%] was obtained by dividing the end-tidal CO2 [mmHg] by the atmospheric pressure (760 mmHg) and multiplying by 100. Data were included from 15 min after intubation until the start of cardiopulmonary bypass, and from 10 min after bypass cessation until disconnection from the anesthesia ventilator. RER was only computed for time points in which the FiO2 was below 80%, based on graphical evaluation of the FiO2 and RER relationship (Online Resource 1, Fig. S1-S3). The primary exposure was intraoperative RER, calculated as medians before and after bypass for each patient, respectively. The primary outcome was a composite of postoperative complications within 7 days after surgery, defined according to the European Perioperative Clinical Outcome Definitions and identified from International Classification of Diseases (9th /10th Revision, Clinical Modification) diagnostic codes, laboratory values, and imaging data [12, 17]. Individual outcome components included acute kidney injury, acute respiratory distress syndrome, anastomotic breakdown, arrhythmia, cardiac arrest, cardiogenic pulmonary edema, deep vein thrombosis, delirium [18], gastrointestinal bleed, infection with uncertain source, laboratory-confirmed bloodstream infection, myocardial infarction, pneumonia, paralytic ileus, postoperative hemorrhage, pulmonary embolism, stroke, surgical site infections, urinary tract infection, and death within 7 days after surgery [17]. Detailed definitions are provided in Online Resource 1, Table S1.

Confounding model

Confounding variables were selected a priori based on clinical plausibility, literature review, and then compiled into a directed acyclic graph (Online Resource 1, Fig. S4). They included patient age, sex, body mass index, ASA physical status, Elixhauser Comorbidity Index [19], a diagnosis of heart failure one year prior to surgery, the lowest intraoperative hemoglobin, the amount of intraoperatively transfused red blood cell units, and crystalloid and colloid infusions. We additionally included aortic cross clamp duration, type of cardiac surgery (coronary artery bypass grafting versus valve surgery versus other procedures requiring cardiopulmonary bypass), and the year of surgery (Online Resource 1, Fig. S5).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted in STATA (Version MP 18.0, StataCorp LLC, College Station, Texas). Sample size and power calculations are provided in Online Resource 1, Sect. 1.4. Results are reported as unadjusted odds ratio (OR) and adjusted OR (ORadj) with 95% confidence intervals (95%CI), per increase by one unit respectively. Area under the receiver operating characteristics curves were computed. In primary univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses, we investigated whether the median RER before and after cardiopulmonary bypass was associated with 7-day postoperative complications. In post-hoc secondary logistic regression analyses, the median RER during the 10 min preceding each lactate measurement was calculated and associated with intraoperative lactate levels > 2 mmol/l [20]. Subsequently, a patient’s peak intraoperative lactate level was used to investigate its association with the primary outcome. We additionally assessed the association of both RER and lactate with 30-day mortality separately. Since higher RER values, typically around 1, have been linked to adverse outcomes, we examined the predictive value of RER greater 1, both as a binary variable (any RER > 1) [21], and as the cumulative time where RER exceeded 1 (time-weighted average) [22], in relation to postoperative complications with a post-hoc exploratory intent (Online Resource 1, 1.2) [10, 12, 21]. To test the robustness of our findings, we evaluated the median RER for its association with the primary outcome during intraoperative periods when the FiO2 was in specific ranges (21–40%, 41–60%, 61–80%). We conducted a sensitivity analysis using the median RER of the last hour of anesthesia. A sensitivity analysis included RER calculations only when FiO2-FETO2 was ≤ 10% to further ensure steady-state ventilation, as FiO2 typically lags after ventilator adjustments. Finally, we corrected the FETCO₂ for dry gas conditions in a sensitivity analysis using the following formula: FETCO₂ = (etCO₂ / [760 − 47 mmHg]) [23].

Results

Study cohort and characteristics

4,098 patients undergoing cardiopulmonary bypass surgery at BIDMC between 2008 and 2020 were eligible for inclusion. After exclusion of 37 patients with an ASA status ≥ V, and 3 patients with missing data for body mass index, 4,058 patients were included in the primary analysis. 239,179 values of RER were calculated before bypass start (median 85, interquartile range [IQR] 62–111, measurements per patient [n = 3,641]), and 85,467 values were calculated after the cessation of bypass (median [IQR] 54 [41–67] per patient [n = 2,297], Fig. 1). Primary analyses

The median RER before cardiopulmonary bypass was 0.64 (0.48–0.92) and after cardiopulmonary bypass 0.65 (IQR 0.48–0.97; Fig. 2; Online Resource 1, Fig. S6, S7 for the components of RER). Overall, 1,745 (43.0%) patients experienced complications within 7 days after surgery. The median RER was not associated with 7-day postoperative complications following unadjusted (before bypass: OR 1.00, 95%CI 0.93–1.07, p = 0.95; after bypass: OR 1.03, 95%CI 0.95–1.12, p = 0.43) and adjusted analysis (before bypass: ORadj 1.02, 95%CI 0.95–1.10, p = 0.55; after bypass: ORadj 1.03, 95%CI 0.95–1.13, p = 0.46). Model performance is displayed in Fig. 3.

Secondary analyses

54.1% (n = 2,113) of all lactate measurements yielded values > 2 mmol/l. The median (IQR) RER in the 10 min before the lactate measurement was 0.67 (0.49–1.02) when lactate exceeded 2 mmol/l and 0.65 (0.48–0.95) when it did not. In secondary analysis, the median RER at any point during surgery was associated with lactate levels > 2 mmol/l in the subsequent 10 min (OR 1.12, 95%CI 1.02–1.23; p = 0.018; ORadj 1.11, 95%CI 1.01–1.23, p = 0.031). Peak lactate levels for each patient were associated with a higher risk of 7-day complications (OR for each 1 mmol/l increase: 1.31, 95%CI 1.24–1.39; p < 0.001; ORadj 1.11, 95%CI 1.04–1.18, p = 0.001, Fig. 3).

In adjusted analysis, an increase of the peak intraoperative lactate level by 1 mmol/l was associated with 45% higher odds of 30-day mortality (n = 55 [1.4%]; ORadj 1.45, 95%CI 1.28–1.65; p < 0.001). Unlike lactate, the median RER before or after bypass was not associated with mortality (ORadj 1.21, 95%CI 0.96–1.52; p = 0.10; and ORadj 1.00, 95%CI 0.67–1.44; p = 0.98, respectively, Fig. 4; Online Resource 1, Table S2).

Exploratory analyses

2,218 (54.7%) patients had a RER above 1 at any point during surgery. Neither any RER above 1 (ORadj 1.09, 95%CI 0.95–1.24; p = 0.21) nor the time-weighted average were associated with 7-day complications (ORadj 0.94, 95%CI 0.81–1.09; p = 0.42).

Sensitivity analyses

RER measurements with a FiO2 ≤ 40% were available for 211 patients. 2,774 patients had RER values recorded at FiO2 levels between 41% and 60%, and 3,767 patients had recordings limited to minutes with FiO2 61–80%. None of the unadjusted or adjusted analyses in those FiO2 strata showed significant associations between RER and 7-day complications (Table 2), and neither did the analyses employing the median RER in the last hour or the RER calculations based on minutes with FiO2-FETO2 ≤ 10%. Correcting for dry gas conditions did not affect the primary results (Online Resource 1, Tables S2, S3).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of 4,058 patients undergoing procedures requiring cardiopulmonary bypass, the intraoperative RER, calculated from 324,646 routinely collected data points, was associated with peak intraoperative lactate levels but not 7-day postoperative complications. By contrast, higher peak lactate levels were associated with higher risks of postoperative complications as well as 30-day mortality.

Main findings

Previous prospective studies found that a higher intraoperative RER was associated with higher risks of postoperative complications among patients undergoing high-risk non-cardiac surgery, including laparoscopic and cancer surgery as well as liver transplantation [10, 12–14, 21]. In those investigations, RER was prospectively calculated at a limited number of time points using specialized research equipment in a tightly controlled anesthetic setting (e.g., etCO2 was set between 30 and 35 mmHg) [21]. By contrast, our study utilized real-world data, and we did not observe a significant association between the intraoperative RER and 7-day complications in patients undergoing surgery under cardiopulmonary bypass. This discrepancy might be explained by several differences to previous studies: first, in our study, the RER was calculated using routine clinical data, which introduces variability in anesthetic management and ventilation strategies, potentially influencing the reliability of RER calculations. This might be reflected in our overall lower RER values compared to previous studies, which resulted from a broader range in CO2 and O2 gradients. Second, cardiac surgical patients experience distinct physiological alterations that may limit the applicability of the RER as a predictive marker in this population [24–26]. Third, although anesthesia gas analyzer data were recorded and stored automatically, artifacts due to technical malfunctions, brief leakages or disconnections in the ventilatory circuit cannot be entirely ruled out. Finally, the RER is a highly dynamic parameter, fluctuating with each breathing cycle and is directly dependent on ventilation settings. Our analysis focused on the median RER over the entire period before or after cardiopulmonary bypass, which may attenuate the impact of brief, transient shifts in RER caused by episodes of anaerobic metabolism. Our results suggest that the utility of RER as a prognostic marker in the cardiac surgical population remains uncertain and warrants further investigation in a real-world setting.

Impact of ventilation settings on RER

To ensure the reliability of RER calculations in our study, we implemented a comprehensive data-cleaning strategy based on clinical discussions, prior literature, and plots of the relationships between the individual RER components and the RER (Fig. 1, Online Resource 1, Fig. S1-3). FiO2 was a key focus given its impact on O2 partial pressure and, consequently, the CO2 binding capacity of hemoglobin, as described by the Haldane Effect [27]. Bar et al. proposed an upper FiO2 threshold of 60% for reliable RER assessment [10], while Padhy et al. conducted measurements at an FiO2 of 40% [13]. In our analyses, we included data obtained during periods when FiO2 was below 80% and performed additional sensitivity analyses stratified by the FiO2 at the time of RER calculation. These subgroup analyses revealed no association between RER and 7-day postoperative complications (Table 2). However, the group including data from minutes with FiO2 21–40% was small, and this patient collective may not be representative.

Second, a larger FiO2-FETO2, the denominator in the RER calculation, leads to a lower RER, while a smaller difference yields higher RER values. For instance, a patient with a FiO2 of 40%, a FETO2 of 35%, and an etCO2 of 35mmHg (thus with a resulting FETCO2 of 4.6%) would have a RER of 0.92 (more examples in Online Resource 1, 1.2). On the one hand, an increase in FiO2-FETO2 might result from an increased VO2 during hypermetabolism or because of peripheral vasoconstriction in patients undergoing surgery [10]. On the other hand, after an increase of the set FiO2 on the ventilator, FETO2 typically lags and remains lower for up to several minutes, temporarily increasing the FiO2-FETO2 difference, and emphasizing the importance of steady-state measurements to obtain reliable FiO2-FETO2 calculations. Therefore, we conducted an additional sensitivity analysis allowing for a maximum FiO2-FETO2 of 10% (instead of 15% in the primary analysis). Nevertheless, patient factors such as pre-existing lung disease, individual provider preferences, or intraoperative complications may have necessitated FiO2 adjustments during surgery, potentially affecting RER reliability [28, 29].

Third, FETCO₂ was derived by dividing EtCO₂ (mmHg) by 760 mmHg, not accounting for water vapor pressure in fully-humidified expired gas in our RER calculation, which was developed for dry gas conditions by Waldau et al. [9]. Notably, our ventilatory circuits are not actively humidified and measurements are taken on the “dry” side of the heat and moisture exchanger. Importantly, accounting for water vapor pressure did not alter the primary results.

Therefore, future prospective studies are needed to define the specific anesthetic conditions, including, but not limited to FiO2, under which the RER can be computed reliably and evaluated meaningfully for outcome prediction.

The RER in cardiac surgery

This study is the first to examine intraoperative RER in patients undergoing cardiac surgery. Following cardiac surgery, a small pilot study demonstrated higher RQ, derived from mixed-venous and arterial blood gas analyses obtained via pulmonary artery catheterization, in patients who died within 30-days [6]. Pulmonary artery catheters, however, are not routinely used during surgery, and early identification of patients at risk of complications is crucial, necessitating alternative methods such as the RER. The physiologic response to cardiopulmonary bypass includes increased cardiac output and oxygen delivery (DO2), which is followed by an increase in oxygen extraction (EO2) [30, 31]. Imbalance between DO2 and EO2 causes relative tissue hypoxia [32]. This imbalance leads to a shift from oxidative phosphorylation to anaerobic metabolism, whose byproducts are hydrogen ions and lactic acid. The hydrogen ions are buffered by the enzyme carbonic anhydrase, which leads to a decrease in bicarbonate and an increase in CO2 [6]. These alterations in CO2 might be detected early through an increase in RER. It is possible, however, that the increase in EO2 may occur later in the intensive care unit (ICU), affecting patients who appeared clinically stable at the end of surgery, but deteriorated in the first hour of ICU admission. Moreover, cardiac surgery often requires repeated interruptions of ventilation as well as manual manipulation of the thorax, which may alter pulmonary dynamics and thereby affect the reliability of the RER [29].

RER, lactate, and postoperative complications

In our study, RER was associated with high lactate levels, and subsequently, peak lactate was associated with both 7-day complications and 30-day mortality, while RER was not. 43% of patients developed postoperative complications, within the 17–76% range reported after cardiac surgery before [33, 34]. Postoperative complications are typically multifactorial in origin. They may result from circulatory disturbances such as hemorrhage or thrombosis, impaired oxygenation, or metabolic derangements associated with prolonged cardiopulmonary bypass or surgical duration [17]. Given its high variability and dependence on respiratory dynamics, the RER may capture only a limited aspect of oxygenation-related complications. Lactate, as a more direct indicator of anaerobic metabolism due to e.g., impaired tissue perfusion, ischemia-reperfusion injury, and the metabolic effects of cardiopulmonary bypass, could provide a more comprehensive signal for identifying the range of postoperative complications examined in this study [3, 35]. Based on our data and these previous studies, we may speculate that the discrepancy in predictive performance between RER and peak lactate levels reflects greater variability in RER compared to lactate. While RER alone may not be used as a reliable predictor of outcomes, it may serve as a rapidly responsive adjunct to lactate in short-term risk stratification.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, this study did not use calorimetry to measure VO2 and VCO2. Instead, the RER was indirectly estimated by a validated non-volumetric formula [8, 9]. We additionally assumed the FiCO2 as zero. Second, by employing retrospective RER calculation, various anesthetic management strategies are included which might alter the distinction between artifacts and true anaerobic metabolism. However, a large amount of intraoperative minute-by-minute data was available and served for exploring the relationships between ventilator settings, the individual RER components and the final RER itself. This was used to develop a comprehensive data cleaning strategy to ensure reliable RER calculation. Still, the data may not accurately reflect true physiological measurements during routine capture in the operating room. Finally, the European Perioperative Clinical Outcome Definitions were developed for prospective outcome sampling [17], while our data was limited to the medical record. However, we enhanced outcome detection by incorporating radiology reports, pre- and postoperative laboratory values, and manual chart review of discharge summaries, which can substantially improve outcome ascertainment beyond frequently underreported diagnostic codes (Online Resource 1, Table S1).

Conclusion

In summary, while RER shows a modest relationship with hyperlactatemia, its utility as a predictor of early postoperative complications was limited in a real-world scenario. Further research is needed to clarify its role and optimize its clinical applicability.
