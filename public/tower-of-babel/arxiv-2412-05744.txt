PICS: A sequential approach to obtain optimal designs for non-linear models leveraging closed-form solutions for faster convergence
Suvrojit Ghosh, Koulik Khamaru, Tirthankar Dasgupta

Abstract
D-Optimal designs for estimating parameters of response models are derived by maximizing the determinant of the Fisher information matrix. For non-linear models, the Fisher information matrix depends on the unknown parameter vector of interest, leading to a weird situation that in order to obtain the D-optimal design, one needs to have knowledge of the parameter to be estimated. One solution to this problem is to choose the design points sequentially, optimizing the D-optimality criterion using parameter estimates based on available data, followed by updating the parameter estimates using maximum likelihood estimation. On the other hand, there are many non-linear models for which closed-form results for D-optimal designs are available, but because such solutions involve the parameters to be estimated, they can only be used by substituting “guestimates” of parameters. In this paper, a hybrid sequential strategy called PICS (Plug into closed-form solution) is proposed that replaces the optimization of the objective function at every single step by a draw from the probability distribution induced by the known optimal design by plugging in the current estimates. Under regularity conditions, asymptotic normality of the sequence of estimators generated by this approach are established. Usefulness of this approach in terms of saving computational time and achieving greater efficiency of estimation compared to the standard sequential approach are demonstrated with simulations conducted from two different sets of models.



Introduction

An extensively researched problem in design of experiments literature is to determine, under resource constraints, the optimal values of one or more controllable input variables
\mathbf{X}
that would lead to the most efficient estimation of the parameters of a fitted specified model of a response
Y
on
\mathbf{X}
. The general approach to finding such
optimal designs

(
Atkinson et al.,, 2007
)
is to find
\mathbf{X}
that optimizes a functional of the Fisher information matrix, such as its determinant or trace. This procedure is straightforward for linear models where the Fisher information matrix does not depend on the unknown model parameters. The problem, however, is much more complicated for situations where the Fisher information matrix depends on the unknown parameters, a phenomenon that is common in non-linear models such as standard nonlinear regression or generalized linear models. Consider, for example, the nonlinear exponential growth model considered in
Zhu et al., (2014)
:

y_{i}=\alpha_{1}\exp\left(-\alpha_{2}/x_{i}\right)+\epsilon_{i},\ 0<x_{\min}<x_{i}<x_{\max},i=1,\ldots,n,

(1)

where for
i=1,\ldots,n
,
y_{i}
and
x_{i}
denote the observed values of
Y
and
X
, and the residuals
\epsilon_{i}
’s are independently and identically distributed (iid) as
N(0,\sigma^{2})
. As seen in
Zhu et al., (2014)
, the total Fisher information matrix for this model given the observed data points
(x_{i},y_{i})_{i=1,\ldots,n}
is

\mathbf{I}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\exp\left(-2\alpha_{2}/x_{i}\right)\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1&-\alpha_{1}/x_{i}\\
-\alpha_{1}/x_{i}&\alpha_{1}^{2}/x_{i}^{2}\end{array}\right].

(2)

According to the criterion of D-optimality, the optimal solution to the problem of maximizing the determinant of the matrix in (
2
) with respect to the input points
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
is known as the
locally optimal design

(
Chernoff,, 1953
)
. Clearly, the solution to this optimization problem depends on the unknown parameters
{\bm{\theta}}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})^{\text{T}}
.
Cochran, (1973)
described this dependency: “You tell me the value of
{\bm{\theta}}
, and I promise to design the best experiment for estimating
{\bm{\theta}}
”.

One way to get around this problem is to maximize the determinant of the Fisher information with respect to
\mathbf{x}
at some “guessed” value or initial estimate of
{\bm{\theta}}={\bm{\theta}}_{0}

(
Box and Lucas,, 1959
, e.g.,)
. An obvious drawback of this approach, as noted by several authors is that, such a prior estimate
{\bm{\theta}}_{0}
may be far from the true value of
{\bm{\theta}}
and the behavior of the locally optimal design may be quite sensitive to even small perturbations in the parameter value. An alternative way to tackle this problem is to use a Bayesian approach
(
Chaloner,, 1989
, e.g.)
in which a suitable prior distribution is postulated for
{\bm{\theta}}
, and the expected value of the determinant of the Fisher information with respect to that prior is maximized to find the D-optimal design.

A more natural approach is to follow a sequential strategy consisting of two parts - an initial static design from which initial parameter estimates are obtained, followed by a fully sequential design where at every step the criterion function is optimized to find a new design point, and the parameter estimate is updated after observing the response at the new design point. Such a strategy was formalized by
Chaudhuri and Mykland, (1993)
, who proposed selecting design points sequentially using a D-optimality criterion, and showed that such a strategy leads to convergence of the design to the true D-optimal design under regularity conditions. Implementation of this procedure requires two optimization algorithms to be applied at each step - one to optimize the D-optimality criterion and the other to obtain the maximum likelihood estimate of the parameters.

On the other hand, in many problems involving derivation of optimal designs for non-linear models, researchers are able to come up with analytical, closed-form solutions of such optimal designs, typically represented as a probability measure on the experiment space. For example, as shown by
Zhu et al., (2014)
, maximization of the Fisher information matrix in (
2
) leads to a two-point balanced D-optimal design with equal support at points

x_{1}^{*}=\max\left(\frac{\alpha_{2}x_{\max}}{\alpha_{2}+x_{\max}},x_{\min}\right),x_{2}^{*}=x_{\max}.

(3)

Because such optimal designs depend on the unknown values of the parameters to be estimated, they cannot be utilized to their fullest potential and often remain of academic interest only.

The work in this paper is motivated by the intuition that incorporating such existing analytical solutions into a sequential algorithm would create a win-win situation for both the approaches. On one hand, it would absolve the experimenter from optimizing the criterion function at each step leading to significant saving of computational time for complex models with a large number of parameters. On the other hand, it would effectively leverage the analytical solutions obtained through tedious mathematical derivations.

In this paper, we propose such a hybrid sequential strategy that replaces the optimization of the objective function at every single step by
plugging in
the estimates into the available closed form solutions, and generating new design points as draws from the probability distributions induced by the optimal design. We refer to this sequential strategy as PICS (Plug into closed-form solution). We provide theoretical guarantees for the proposed approach by establishing that the key results derived by
Chaudhuri and Mykland, (1993)
, i.e., convergence of the sequence of design points to the true D-optimal design and asymptotic normality of the sequence of parameter estimates, also hold in the PICS approach.

The paper is organized as follows: in Section
2
, we introduce key notations, briefly review D-optimal designs and provide two specific examples of our setting. We present the two stage approach proposed by
Chaudhuri and Mykland, (1993)
in Section
3
. In Section
4
, we describe the proposed PICS approach, and state the key theoretical results. In Section
5
we re-visit the motivating examples presented in Section
2
, and demonstrate how the proposed approach outperforms the standard approach using extensive simulations. The final section presents some concluding remarks and directions for future work.

Background, notation, problem set up and examples

Our set up is similar to the one in
Chaudhuri and Mykland, (1993)
, where the aim is to fit a model governing the non-linear relationship between a response variable
Y
and a deterministic
p
-dimensional vector of regressors
{\mathbf{X}}
using data
(y_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i})
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, obtained from a controlled experiment. The response
Y
takes values from the response space
\mathbf{\mathcal{R}}
. The regressor
\mathbf{X}
takes values chosen by the experimenter from an experiment space
\bm{\Omega}
, which can be a finite set, a subset of the Euclidean space, or a combination of both depending on whether
\mathbf{X}
is categorical, continuous, or a combination of both types of variables. The conditional distribution of
Y
given
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}
will be denoted by
f(y|\bm{\theta},\phi,\mathbf{x})
, assumed to be completely known except for the values of
\bm{\theta}
and
\phi
. The parameter
\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}
is the parameter of interest, and
\phi
is a nuisance parameter. As in
Chaudhuri and Mykland, (1993)
, we will assume that the nuisance parameter
\phi
has no influence on the computation of the maximum likelihood estimate (MLE) of
\bm{\theta}
, and hence for simplicity of notation, will write our model as
f(y|\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x})
. The Fisher information matrix at
\bm{\theta}
is defined as:

\mathbf{I}(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x})=\int_{\mathcal{R}}\nabla\log f(y|\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x})\{\nabla\log f(y|\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x})\}^{\text{T}}f(y|\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x})\mu(dy),

where
\nabla
is the gradient operator denoting differentiation with respect to
\tilde{\bm{\theta}}\in\bm{\Theta}
, and
\mu
denotes the standard Lebesgue or counting measure with respect to
\mathcal{R}
.

Following
Kiefer, (1959)
;
Kiefer, (1961)
and
Kiefer and Wolfowitz, (1959)
, the collection of all probability measures on
\bm{\Omega}
is called the
design space
, and is denoted by
\bm{\Xi}
. A specific design
\mathbf{\xi}\in\bm{\Xi}
is a probability measure on
\bm{\Omega}
. A D-optimal design
(
Atkinson et al.,, 2007
)
is obtained by maximizing the determinant of
\mathbf{I}(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x})
over all possible designs
\mathbf{\xi}\in\bm{\Xi}
. Based on the definition of “locally optimal” design in
Chernoff, (1953)
, a locally D-optimal design is defined as follows:

Definition 1
.

A design
\mathbf{\xi^{*}}\in\bm{\Xi}
is called a
locally D-optimal design
at
\bm{\theta}
if
\det\left(\int_{\bm{\Omega}}\mathbf{I}(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x})\mathbf{\xi^{*}}(d\mathbf{x})\right)=\sup_{\mathbf{\xi}\in\bm{\Xi}}\det\left(\int_{\bm{\Omega}}\mathbf{I}(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x})\mathbf{\xi}(d\mathbf{x})\right).

As pointed out by
Chaudhuri and Mykland, (1993)
, such a
\mathbf{\xi^{*}}
will always exist in an application, as
\bm{\Omega}
will typically be a finite set or some nice subset of an Euclidean space. However a closed-form solution to this optimization problem may not exist.
Chaudhuri and Mykland, (1993)
gave several examples of the setting described above. We consider cases where
\mathbf{\xi^{*}}
is available in the form of a known probability distribution over
\bm{\Omega}
that is completely known if
\bm{\theta}
is known, so that for a given value of
\bm{\theta}
, it is possible to generate a draw
\mathbf{X}^{*}(\bm{\theta})
from the distribution of the locally optimal design
\mathbf{\xi^{*}}
. We provide two specific examples of such a setting to motivate our approach and to demonstrate its effectiveness and advantage.

Example 1

(Non-linear regression)
.

Consider a non-linear regression model where
y
and
\mathbf{x}
are related via the equation

y_{i}=g(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x}_{i})+\epsilon_{i},\ i=1,\ldots,n,

(4)

where
g(\cdot)
is any real-valued non-linear function with a known form, and the random errors
\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{n}
are iid normal variables with zero mean and unknown variance
\sigma^{2}
. A specific example is (
1
), where
\mathbf{x}
is a scalar,
g(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x})=\alpha_{1}\exp(-\alpha_{2}/x)
and the experiment space
\bm{\Omega}
is the closed interval
[x_{\text{min}},x_{\text{max}}]
. The vector
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
is the parameter
\bm{\theta}
of interest and
\sigma^{2}
is the nuisance parameter. As mentioned in Section
1
, the locally D-optimal design
\mathbf{\xi^{*}}
can be expressed as a known probability distribution on
\bm{\Omega}
, which for model (
1
) is a balanced design with equal support at the two points given in (
3
).

Zhu et al., (2014)
also considered other forms of
g(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x})
, and derived closed form expressions for the optimal designs under each model, the details of which will be provided in Section
5
.

Example 2

(Logistic regression in factorial experiment)
.

Consider a
2^{2}
factorial experiment in which the response is binary and each of the two input variables
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
can take two possible coded levels
+1
and
-1
, so that the experiment space consists of four possible level combinations
(+1,+1),(+1,-1),(-1,+1)
and
(-1,-1)
. We assume that the conditional distribution of
Y
given
X_{1}=x_{1}
and
X_{2}=x_{2}
is Bernoulli with parameter

\pi(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{\exp(\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}x_{1}+\beta_{2}x_{2})}{1+\exp(\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}x_{1}+\beta_{2}x_{2})},

(5)

where

{\bm{\theta}}=(\beta_{0},\beta_{1},\beta_{2})^{\text{T}}
is the parameter to be estimated.

In this example, a design
\mathbf{\xi}
can be defined in terms of the proportion of units
p_{rs}
allocated to the
r
th level of
x_{1}
and the
s
th level of
x_{2}
, for
r,s=1,2
. For example,
p_{11}
represents the proportion of units allocated to level combination
(+1,+1)
. These proportions
p_{rs}
depend on the unknown parameter
\bm{\theta}
. Under specific assumptions,
Yang et al., (2012)
derived closed form solutions for the D-optimal design
\mathbf{\xi^{*}}
as the proportions
p_{rs}^{*}
. The explicit forms of these optimal designs will be provided in Section
5
.

The two-stage approach

Chaudhuri and Mykland, (1993)
proposed a two-stage sequential design strategy in order to resolve the awkward situation encountered in non-linear models, where designing an efficient experiment requires knowledge of the parameter, but the purpose of the experiment is to generate data to yield parameter estimates. We will refer to this approach as the C-M approach throughout the remainder of this paper.

The C-M procedure, described algorithmically in Figure
1
, assumes a predetermined design size
n
based on resource constraints, and divides the process of generating these
n
design points into the following two stages:

(1)

an initial
static
stage consisting of
n_{1}
trials,

(2)

a
fully adaptive sequential
stage consisting of the remaining
n-n_{1}
trials.

In stage
(1)
, the
n_{1}
design points are chosen with minimal prior knowledge on
\bm{\theta}
. The initial design points
\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n_{1}}
are drawn from an initial distribution
\mathbf{\xi}_{0}
. For example,
\mathbf{\xi}_{0}
can be a uniform distribution over
\bm{\Omega}
, or can constitute a Latin hypercube sample
(
McKay et al.,, 1979
)
from
\bm{\Omega}
. Let
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n_{1}}
be the responses observed after the initial experiment is carried out. The goal of this stage is to find a starting estimate of
\bm{\theta}
based on the data
\left(Y_{j},\mathbf{X}_{j}\right),j=1,2,\ldots,n_{1}
, observed from the initial experiment. One can use the method of maximum likelihood to obtain an estimate
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n_{1}}
of
\bm{\theta}
.

This estimate
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n_{1}}
is used to initiate the sequential stage
(2)
, where the experimenter finds the next design point and the estimate of
\bm{\theta}
at each iteration. Prior to the
i^{th}
iteration, where
n_{1}+1\leq i\leq n
, the data
\left(Y_{j},\mathbf{X}_{j}\right),j=1,2,\ldots,i-1
and the ML estimate
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{i-1}
of
\bm{\theta}
based on this data are available. The subsequent design point
\mathbf{X}_{i}\in\bm{\Omega}
is chosen by maximizing the criterion function

\det\left[\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\mathbf{I}\left(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{i-1},\mathbf{X}_{j}\right)+\mathbf{I}\left(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{i-1},\mathbf{X}_{i}\right)\right],

(6)

with respect to
\mathbf{X}_{i}
. Note that the criterion function represents the determinant of the total Fisher information matrix
\sum_{j=1}^{i}\mathbf{I}\left(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{j}\right)
till stage
i
.

Next, the response
Y_{i}
at
\mathbf{X}_{i}
is observed, and the estimate of
\bm{\theta}
is updated based on
\left(Y_{j},\mathbf{X}_{j}\right),j=1,2,\ldots,i
. The updated estimate is denoted as
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{i}
. These actions are repeated until a set of
n
design points are obtained.

(a)

.

A key aspect of the sequential procedure described above is that, it leads to a dependent structure of the data. As a result, the total Fisher information
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{I}\left(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{j}\right)
becomes a random variable.
Chaudhuri and Mykland, (1993)
established the following asymptotic results under this setting:

1.

The maximum likelihood estimator
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
of
\bm{\theta}
based on the data
(Y_{j},\mathbf{X}_{j}),j=1,2,\ldots,n
, is shown to be consistent and asymptotically normal and efficient
(
Chaudhuri and Mykland,, 1993
, Theorem 3.10 and Corollary 3.11)
.

2.

The generated sequential design is shown to converge to the true locally D-optimal design at
\bm{\theta}
in probability
(
Chaudhuri and Mykland,, 1993
, Theorem 3.5)
when
n_{1}\rightarrow\infty
and
\frac{n_{1}}{n}\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

However, it is worthwhile to note that this method involves
two
primary optimization algorithms at each step: (i) determining the subsequent design point by optimizing equation (
6
) and (ii) updating the parameter estimates from the augmented data by maximizing the likelihood. The computational burden associated with (i) can be prohibitive, especially as the model becomes more complex and the dimension of the input space increases. We aim at harnessing existing analytical results to eliminate the first optimization and reduce the computational effort, while retaining the nice asymptotic properties of the C-M procedure.

The sequential PICS design: algorithm and theoretical guarantees

We now describe our proposed approach, the PICS algorithm, which is very similar to the C-M procedure discussed in Section
3
but takes advantage of closed-form locally D-optimal designs when they are available. To the best of our knowledge, the significant amount of research that has led to identification of closed-form solutions of optimal designs for non-linear models have primarily been used to identify locally optimal designs by substituting estimates or “guestimates” of the parameters into such solutions. Some researchers have taken the Bayesian route by postulating prior distributions for the parameters
(
Zhu et al.,, 2014
, e.g.,)
. Whereas the Bayesian approach permits the experimenter to use prior knowledge about the parameter, it makes the optimization problem more complicated than its sequential counterpart. We propose to use such results to effectively eliminate the computationally expensive step (a) of Stage 2 of Figure
1
by plugging in the parameter estimates obtained from the previous stage directly into the closed-form solution, and consequently boost the computational efficiency of the C-M procedure while maintaining its nice asymptotic properties. The PICS algorithm, shown in Figure
2
, is thus essentially the same as the C-M algorithm summarized in Figure
1
, except for the first step of Stage 2, where instead of optimizing over the criterion function (
6
), the new design point
\mathbf{X}_{i}
is generated as a draw from the known optimal design measure.

:

(a)

Choose the initial design points
\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n_{1}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}\mathbf{\xi}_{0}
on
\bm{\Omega}
, where
\mathbf{\xi}_{0}
is an initial probability measure.

(b)

Observe the corresponding set of responses
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n_{1}}
.

(c)

Calculate the ML estimate
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n_{1}}
based on the datapoints
\left(Y_{j},\mathbf{X}_{j}\right)
,
j=1,2,\ldots,n_{1}
.

2.

Stage 2 (Sequential stage):
for

,

(a)

Given
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{i-1}
, choose
\mathbf{X}_{i}
by generating a draw from the optimal design
\mathbf{\xi^{*}}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{i-1})
, i.e., by substituting
\bm{\theta}=\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{i-1}
in the probability distribution associated with the closed-form optimal design.

(b)

Observe response
Y_{i}
at
\mathbf{X}_{i}
.

(c)

Update the ML estimate
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{i}
based on the data
\left(Y_{j},\mathbf{X}_{j}\right),j=1,2,\ldots,i
.

Thus replacing the optimization step involved in obtaining the next design point by a draw from the known optimal design measure is the only difference between the C-M and PICS approaches. Although drawing from a known distribution is straightforward, implementation of a
fully
sequential design (i.e., generating
one
new design point at every step) involves some nuances. Consider, for example, the optimal design for Example
1
given in (
3
) with equal support at
X^{*}_{1}
and
X^{*}_{2}
. At each step, if we select one of the two points with probability
1/2
, for a small
n
, the design can be heavily imbalanced with a moderate probability. Such possibilities, however, can be handled by slightly adjusting the drawing procedure associated with step (a) of Stage 2 in the PICS algorithm shown in Figure
2
. For example, if
\mathbf{\xi^{*}}
is a balanced design with
k
unique support points at
\mathbf{X}^{*}_{1}(\bm{\theta}),\ldots,\mathbf{X}^{*}_{k}(\bm{\theta})
, instead of a fully sequential algorithm, it will be pragmatic to use a batch-sequential algorithm that generates
k
new points at each iteration in the algorithm with loops of
k
iterations, where in each loop each of the support points is chosen once and exactly once, in a random order. This procedure can also be used to accommodate unbalanced designs with finite supports. For example, if the optimal design has support at three points
\mathbf{X}_{1}^{*}
,
\mathbf{X}_{2}^{*}
and
\mathbf{X}_{3}^{*}
with probabilities 1/4, 1/2 and 1/4 respectively, then one can use a batch-sequential algorithm as above with a batch size of four by re-expressing the optimal design as a four-point balanced design with equal support at
\mathbf{X}_{1}^{*},\mathbf{X}^{*}_{2},\mathbf{X}_{2}^{*}
and
\mathbf{X}_{3}^{*}
. We will refer this batch sequential procedure as the
balanced PICS algorithm
because it enforces the exact balance of the true optimal design into the PICS procedure.

Like the C-M approach, we assume that for the PICS approach, the total number of experimental trials
n
that can be conducted is fixed and determined apriori by resource constraints. However, in more flexible situations, one can also use a stopping criterion based on the relative improvement achieved with the D-optimality criterion in comparison to the previous step, i.e.,

\left\lvert\frac{\det\left(\sum_{j=1}^{i}\mathbf{I}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{i},\mathbf{X}_{j})\right)-\det\left(\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\mathbf{I}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{i-1},\mathbf{X}_{j})\right)}{\det\left(\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\mathbf{I}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{i-1},\mathbf{X}_{j})\right)}\right\rvert<\delta,

where
\delta
is a pre-assigned threshold.

4.1
Theoretical guarantees of the PICS algorithm

(A.3)
impose a few smoothness assumptions (Lipschitz property) on the Fisher information matrix
\mathrm{\bf I}(\bm{\theta},\bf X)
. These assumptions are mild and hold for most non-linear models.
In Assumption
(A.4)
, we posit that the sequence of maximum likelihood estimators
\{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}\}
obtained from the PICS method is strongly consistent. For generalized linear models, the strong consistency of
\{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}\}
for sequentially collected data, similar to PICS, is guaranteed under a very mild assumption
(
Chen et al.,, 1999
)
. In particular, if the number of initial design points
n_{1}
satisfies
n_{1}^{-1}(\log n)\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, the design points are uniformly bounded, and the initial design points are chosen at random, then this strong assumption is satisfied. For nonlinear models, a similar condition can be found in the works of
Lai, (1994)
.Conditions
(A.5)
-
(A.8)
impose smoothness conditions on the log-likelihood function and are analogous to the smoothness conditions from
Chaudhuri and Mykland, (1993)
.

Remark 2
.

Theorem
2
provides assurance that the ML estimate
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
of
\bm{\theta}
maintains asymptotic efficiency, akin to the conventional maximum likelihood estimate derived from independent observations. Furthermore, the theorem implies that inference procedures for
\bm{\theta}
remain robust even in scenarios where the design is generated sequentially. Theorem
2
also allows us to construct confidence ellipsoids for
\bm{\theta}
using the maximum likelihood estimate
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
and the average Fisher information matrix.

Remark 3
.

Theorem
2
ensures that our sequential estimates
\{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
obtained during the PICS-strategy are asymptotically efficient (optimal limiting variance) for the parameter
\bm{\theta}
. We can, in fact, provide a similar guarantee on the sequence of designs
\left\{\mathbf{\xi^{*}}{(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{i})}\right\}_{i\geq 1}
that are used to draw samples at each round of the PICS algorithm. Let
\mathbf{\xi^{*}}
be the optimal design, and we assume that
\bm{\theta}\mapsto\varphi(\mathbf{\xi^{*}}{(\bm{\theta})})
— the map from a point
\bm{\theta}
to the characteristic function of the design
\mathbf{\xi^{*}}{(\bm{\theta})}
— is a continuous function of
\bm{\theta}
. Then, using the strong consistency of
\{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{\xi^{*}}{(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathbf{\xi^{*}}\qquad\text{as}\;\;n\rightarrow\infty.

Put simply, the estimated designs used in the PICS algorithm themselves converge to the optimal design. Figure
3
provides a numerical validation of this convergence of the estimated design to the optimal design.

Remark 4
.

We note that the primary challenge in proving the above results stems from the dependency among the sequence of points. Hence, unlike using the concepts of the usual Central Limit Theory, we must draw upon ideas rooted in martingale limit theory to derive these results.

Concluding remarks and discussion

In this paper, we propose an approach to enhance the efficiency of the standard sequential procedure for obtaining locally D-optimal designs for nonlinear models by leveraging existing closed-form solutions. The key idea is to “plug-in” the current parameter estimates into the available solutions, or alternatively to draw new design points from the known optimal design measure after substituting the current parameter estimates. We establish theoretically that the proposed procedure preserves the nice convergence properties of the standard approach and demonstrate its superiority with respect to efficiency and computation time via simulations. In addition to the two examples discussed in the paper, there are several other situations where closed-form solutions of D-optimal designs are available, making the proposed approach applicable to such settings. See, for example,
Kalish and Rosenberger, (1978)
,
Abdelbasit and Plackett, (1983)
,
Haines et al., (2018)
.

A general problem associated with locally optimal designs is their reliance on assumed models. For instance, in Example
1
discussed in the paper, the experimenter would not know if the model
M_{2}
or model
M_{3}
explains the data generating mechanism better. One way to tackle such situations is to use model robust designs
(
Dette,, 1990
;
Dette and Franke,, 2001
;
Tsai and Gilmour,, 2010
;
Smucker et al.,, 2011
;
Smucker et al.,, 2012
;
Smucker and Drew,, 2015
, e.g.,)
. Adopting ideas from this literature on model robust designs to make the PICS approach more robust to model uncertainty would be an interesting extension of this work.

Bayesian sequential designs
(
Dror and Steinberg,, 2008
)
have been a popular alternative to the C-M type approach for non-linear models. This approach was used by
Zhu et al., (2014)
in the context of Example
1
. Integrating the PICS approach with the Bayesian framework would be an interesting possibility to consider and explore.

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{J}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}_{1})-\mathbf{J}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}_{2})\|_{\mathrm{op}}

(A.2)

(Control on the variance of the entries of

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{\mathbf{X}\sim\mathbf{\xi}({\bm{\theta}}_{1})}\left([\mathbf{I}({\bm{\theta}},\mathbf{X})]_{r,s}\right)\leq V_{\max}\qquad\text{for all}\;\;\bm{\theta}\;\text{and}\;{\bm{\theta}}_{1}\in{\bm{\Theta}}\subseteq\mathbf{R}^{d}.\;\;\text{and}\;\;1\leq r,s\leq d.

(18)

(A.3)

The function
\mathbf{I}(\cdot,\mathbf{x})
is Lipschitz for every fixed
\mathbf{x}\in\bm{\Omega}
.

(A.4)

The sequence of estimators
\{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}\}
generated through the PICS algorithm is a strongly consistent estimator of
\bm{\theta}
.

(A.5)

Support of
f(y|\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x})
does not depend on
\bm{\theta}
or
\mathbf{x}

\nabla\log f(y|\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x})=G(y,\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x})
be the gradient vector, that satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{R}}G(y,\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x})f(y|\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x})\mu(dy)=0

and

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\bm{\Omega}}\int_{\mathcal{R}}\|G(y,\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x})\|_{\infty}^{2+t}f(y|\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x})\mu(dy)<\infty

for some
t>0
.

(A.7)

Let
H(y,\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x})
denote the Hessian matrix (of order
d
x
d
) of
\log f(y|\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x})
corresponding to the second-order differentiation with respect to
\bm{\theta}
. Then
H(y,\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x})
satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{R}}H(y,\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x})f(y|\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x})\mu(dy)=

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathcal{R}}G(y,\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x})\{G(y,\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x})\}^{\text{T}}f(y|\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x})\mu(dy)=-\mathbf{I}(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x}),

and

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\bm{\Omega}}\int_{\mathcal{R}}\|H(y,\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x})\|_{\infty}^{2}f(y|\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x})\mu(dy)<\infty

(A.8)

The Hessian matrix
H(y,\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x})
is L-Lipschitz in
\bm{\theta}
for every fixed
\mathbf{x},y
.

7.2
Proof of Theorem
1

Throughout, we use
\mathbf{X}({\bm{\theta}})
to denote a random sample drawn from the distribution
\mathbf{\xi}^{*}(\bm{\theta})
. Using definition (
16
),
\mathbf{J}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}}_{i-1})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}\sim\mathbf{\xi}^{*}(\bm{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}}_{i-1})}\left[\mathbf{I}({\bm{\theta}},\mathbf{X})\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\right]
, where
\mathcal{F}_{i-1}
is the sigma-field generated by
(Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{i-1},\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{i-1})
, we have:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{I}(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{i}(\bm{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}}_{i-1}))

\displaystyle=\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{I}(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{i}(\bm{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}}_{i-1}))-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{J}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}}_{i-1})\right\}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{J}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}}_{i-1})

\displaystyle=T_{1}+T_{2},

denoting the first and second terms by
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
respectively.

By definition,
\mathbf{I}^{*}=\mathbf{J}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta})
and we have

\displaystyle\|T_{2}-\mathbf{I}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{J}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}}_{i-1})-\mathbf{J}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta})\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\frac{L}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\bm{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}}_{i-1}-\bm{\theta}\|_{2},

where the last step follows from condition
(A.1)
.

Using condition
(A.4)
, i.e., strong consistency of
\bm{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}}_{i-1}
and using the fact that the average (cesaro-mean) of a convergent series of real number converges to the same limit, we conclude that
\|T_{2}-\mathbf{I}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\rightarrow 0
almost surely. It remains to control the term
T_{1}
.

Control on
T_{1}
:

Note that
T_{1}
is an average of Martingale difference sequence (MDS) with respect to the sigma-field
\mathcal{F}_{i-1}
, and we aim to show that
T_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}0
. We have

\displaystyle T_{1}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\mathbf{I}({\bm{\theta}},\mathbf{X}_{i}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{i-1}))-\mathbf{J}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}}_{i-1})\right\}\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\mathbf{Z}_{i}}{n}

Fix any entry
(p,q)\in[d]\times[d]
, where
d
is the dimension of the parameter
\bm{\theta}
, and denote the
(p,q)^{\text{th}}
entry of the matrix
\mathbf{Z}_{i}
by
\mathbf{Z}_{i,p,q}
. Clearly,
\{\mathbf{Z}_{i,p,q}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
is a MDS with respect to the filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
, the
\sigma
-field generated by the data. Fix any
\zeta>0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\sum_{i\leq n}\mathbf{Z}_{i,p,q}\right|>n\zeta\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{i\leq n}\mathbf{Z}_{i,p,q}\right)^{2}}{n^{2}\zeta^{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{=}}\frac{\sum_{i\leq n}\mathbb{E}\mathbf{Z}_{i,p,q}^{2}}{n^{2}\zeta^{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(ii)}}{{\leq}}\frac{nV_{\max}}{n^{2}\zeta^{2}}\rightarrow 0

In other words,
\sum_{i\leq n}\mathbf{Z}_{i,p,q}/n\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}0
.
Step
(ii)
above uses condition
(A.2)
. Step
(i)
above uses the following standard identity for second moment of sum of MDS

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{i\leq n}\mathbf{Z}_{i,p,q}\right)^{2}=\sum_{i\leq n}\mathbb{E}\mathbf{Z}_{i,p,q}^{2}+2\sum_{i<j}\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Z}_{i,p,q}\cdot\mathbf{Z}_{j,p,q}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}]=\sum_{i\leq n}\mathbb{E}\mathbf{Z}_{i,p,q}^{2}

Putting together the pieces we conclude
T_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}0
and
\|T_{2}-\mathbf{I}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\rightarrow 0
almost surely. This completes the proof of (
8
).

Proof of (
9
):

This part follows from condition
(A.3)
, i.e., the assumed Lipschitz property of
\mathbf{I}(\cdot,\mathbf{x})
. We have

\displaystyle\Big\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{I}(\bm{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}}_{n},\mathbf{X}_{i}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{i-1}))-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{I}(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{i}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{i-1}))\Big\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big\|\mathbf{I}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n},\mathbf{X}_{i}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{i-1}))-\mathbf{I}(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{i}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{i-1}))\Big\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\leq L\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta}\|_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}0

The last line above uses condition
(A.4)
. This completes the proof of Theorem
1
.

7.3
Proof of Theorem
2
.

We now establish the proof of Theorem
2
. Let
\mathcal{F}_{n,i}
be an increasing sequence of
\sigma
-fields generated by
(Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{i},\mathbf{X}_{1},\mathbf{X}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{i+1}
),
i\leq n-1
. Then, the gradient of the log-likelihood, i.e.,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}G(Y_{i},\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{i})
is a sum of square integrable martingale difference sequence (MDS) with respect to the sigma-field
\mathcal{F}_{n,i}
by condition
(A.6)
. Similarly, the sequence
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{H(Y_{i},\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{i})+\mathbf{I}\left(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{i}\right)\right\}
, i.e., the sum of the Hessian matrix of the log-likelihood and the Fisher information matrix is also a square integrable MDS with respect to the sigma-field
\mathcal{F}_{n,i}
by condition
(A.7)
.

To establish the CLT for
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
, we will prove the following:

\displaystyle n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}G(Y_{i},\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{i})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{d}\left(0,\mathbf{I}^{*}\right),

(19)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}H(Y_{i},\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{i})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}

\displaystyle-\mathbf{I}^{*},

(20)

where
\mathbf{I}^{*}=\displaystyle\int_{\bm{\Omega}}\mathbf{I}(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x})\mathbf{\xi^{*}}(d\mathbf{x})
is the true optimal Fisher information.

Proof of (
19
):

Invoking the Cramer-Wold’s theorem it suffices to show that for any vector
a\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
with
\|a\|_{2}=1
we have

\displaystyle n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a^{\text{T}}G(Y_{i},\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{i})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}\left(0,a^{\text{T}}\mathbf{I}^{*}a\right)

(21)

It suffices to show that

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a^{\text{T}}G(Y_{i},\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{i}))^{2}}{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}a^{\text{T}}\mathbf{I}^{*}a,\quad\text{(Variance Stability)}

(22)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[(a^{\text{T}}G(Y_{i},\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{i}))^{2}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{|a^{\text{T}}G(Y_{i},\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{i})|>\sqrt{n}\zeta}\bigg|\mathcal{F}_{n,i-1}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}0\quad\forall\zeta>0,\quad\text{(Lindeberg Condition)}

(23)

To prove (
22
), note that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[G(Y_{i},\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{i})\{G(Y_{i},\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{i})\}^{\text{T}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n,i-1}\right]}{n}\overset{(i)}{=}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{I}(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{i})}{n}\overset{(ii)}{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}}\mathbf{I}^{*}\quad,

where (i) follows from condition
(A.7)
and (ii) from Theorem
1
. Next, to prove (
23
), note that for any
t>0
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[(a^{\text{T}}G(Y_{i},\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{i}))^{2}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{|a^{\text{T}}G(Y_{i},\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{i})|>\sqrt{n}\cdot\zeta}\bigg|\mathcal{F}_{n,i-1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\Big[(a^{\text{T}}G(Y_{i},\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{i}))^{2}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{|a^{\text{T}}G(Y_{i},\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{i})|>\sqrt{n}\cdot\zeta}\cdot\frac{\|G(Y_{i},\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{i})\|_{\infty}^{t}}{|\sqrt{n}\zeta|^{t}}\bigg|\mathcal{F}_{n,i-1}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\zeta^{t}n^{1+t/2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\|G(Y_{i},\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{i})\|_{\infty}^{2+t}\bigg|\mathcal{F}_{n,i-1}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle\frac{n}{\zeta^{t}n^{1+t/2}}\rightarrow 0\quad[\text{as}\;\;n\rightarrow\infty].

where step (i) follows from Condition
(A.6)
. This completes the proof of claim (
19
).

Proof of (
20
):

By condition
(A.7)
,
\{H(Y_{i},\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{i})+\mathbf{I}(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{i})\}_{i\geq 1}
is a Martingale difference sequence. Also, by condition
(A.7)
, the second moment of entries of
H(Y_{i},\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{i})
are uniformly bounded. Thus invoking the weak-law of large numbers
(
Hall and Heyde,, 1980
, Theorem 2.13)
, we conclude

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{H(Y_{i},\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{i})+\mathbf{I}\left(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{i}\right)\right\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}0.

(24)

We are now ready to complete the proof of Theorem
2
. We have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}G(Y_{i},\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n},\mathbf{X}_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}G(Y_{i},\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{i})+\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}H(Y_{i},\tilde{\bm{\theta}},\mathbf{X}_{i})\right\}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta})

where
\tilde{\bm{\theta}}
lies on the line joining
\bm{\theta}
and
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
. Rearranging the last relation we have that for
a\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
with
\|a\|\neq 0
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}G(Y_{i},\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{i})

\displaystyle=\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}-H(Y_{i},\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{i})\right\}\sqrt{n}\cdot(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta})+\sqrt{n}R(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{=}}\big(\mathbf{I}^{*}+o_{p}(1)+R\big)\cdot\sqrt{n}\cdot(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta})

(25)

where step
(i)
follows from (
24
) and the matrix
R
is defined as

\displaystyle R=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{H(Y_{i},\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{i})-H(Y_{i},\tilde{\bm{\theta}},\mathbf{X}_{i})\right\}

(26)

Using the Hessian Lipschitz property
(A.8)
, we have

\displaystyle\|R\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|H(Y_{i},\tilde{\bm{\theta}},\mathbf{X}_{i})-H(Y_{i},\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{i})\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq L\cdot\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta}\|_{2}\rightarrow 0\quad\text{almost surely.}

(27)

Putting together the equations (
27
), (
19
), and (
25
) we conclude

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\cdot(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\big(\mathbf{I}^{*}+o_{p}(1)+R\big)^{-1}\cdot\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}G(Y_{i},\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{i})\right\}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathcal{N}_{d}\left(0,\mathbf{I}^{*-1}\right)

This completes the proof of Theorem
2
.
