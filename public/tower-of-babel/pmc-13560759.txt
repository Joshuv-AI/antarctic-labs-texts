Generation of Herbicide‐Resistant Alfalfa Germplasm via Prime Editing
Authors: Shihao Li, Xiuzhi Xia, Zhengzheng Wei, Na Wang, Hao Lin, Lifang Niu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAlfalfa (Medicago sativa) is the most widely cultivated forage crop worldwide, owing to its high protein content, balanced amino acid profile, and abundance of vitamins and minerals. However, weed infestation severely threatens alfalfa yield and quality. The lack of herbicide‑resistant cultivars, combined with the diversity of weed species, has made herbicide tolerance a key breeding objective. To address this challenge, we developed a highly optimized prime editing system and, for the first time, achieved prime editing in alfalfa by enhancing the expression efficiency of its components. Specifically, we introduced mutations in three target genes MsALS1, MsALS2 and MsACC1 to confer herbicide resistance: mutations in MsALS1 and MsALS2 were designed to confer resistance to ALS‑inhibiting herbicides (based on evidence from their rice homologs), while those in MsACC1 were targeted to confer resistance to aryloxyphenoxypropionate (APP) herbicides. Using the Csy4 nuclease system, we performed simultaneous prime editing of all three genes, ultimately generating alfalfa lines resistant to nicosulfuron and haloxyfop‑P‑methyl. This study represents the first successful multi‑gene prime editing in alfalfa, providing a novel tool and a valuable approach for alfalfa molecular breeding.

Alfalfa ( Medicago sativa ) is a widely cultivated forage crop worldwide. Severe weed competition during seedling establishment and post‐cutting regrowth severely restrict its yield potential and forage quality (Ye et al. 2025). Alfalfa materials resistant to sulfonylurea‐ and imidazolinone‐type herbicides were developed using cytidine base editors (Bottero et al. 2022). However, the long‐term application of a single herbicide carries risks including the evolution of herbicide‐resistant weeds and soil herbicide residues. Therefore, the development of new herbicide‐resistant and multi‐resistant alfalfa germplasm is essential for sustainable alfalfa cultivation.

In rice, the OsALSW548M mutation confers broad‐spectrum resistance to ALS‐inhibiting herbicides without compromising plant growth or yield (Chen et al. 2021), and the OsACCW2125C mutation confers resistance to aryloxyphenoxypropionate (APP) herbicides (Li et al. 2020). Introducing the analogous mutations in alfalfa requires an efficient genome‐editing toolkit. The upgraded prime editing (PE) system ePPEplus has proven effective in hexaploid wheat ( Triticum aestivum ) (Ni et al. 2023), and enhanced expression of editing components substantially boosts PE efficiency in tomato ( Solanum lycopersicum ) (Lu et al. 2021). Accordingly, we optimized the ePPEplus system by increasing the expression of core editing components and established an efficient PE platform for alfalfa. Using this platform, we simultaneously edited multiple target genes, generating diverse herbicide‐resistant alfalfa lines.

To adapt this system for alfalfa, we replaced the maize ( Zea mays ) Ubiquitin (ZmUbi) promoter with the double cauliflower mosaic virus (CaMV) 35S promoter (2 × 35S), which is widely used for transgene expression in dicotyledonous species, or the M4 promoter, which confers robust transcriptional activity in soybean ( Glycine max ), another Fabaceae family member (Bai et al. 2020), creating the PE vectors pK‐CMPE‐ePPEplus‐MsV1 and pK‐CMPE‐ePPEplus‐MsV2, respectively (Figure 1a).

PE guide RNAs (pegRNAs) were designed to target three conserved functional sites (Figures S1–S3) and introduce a TG>AT conversion in MsALS1 (W554M), a TG>AT conversion in MsALS2 (W559M), and a G>C conversion in MsACC1 (W1961C). Given target site constraints, single pegRNAs were designed for MsALS1 and MsACC1. For MsALS2, suitable pegRNA sites occur on both strands flanking the mutation, and dual pegRNAs were used to boost editing efficiency as previously reported (Li et al. 2024) (Figure S4, Table S1). Sanger sequencing across different alleles excluded SNPs at pegRNA target sites (Figures S5–S7). The pegRNAs were synthesized according to the design and assembled into pK‐CMPE‐ePPEplus‐MsV1 and pK‐CMPE‐ePPEplus‐MsV2 using the Golden Gate reaction (Figure S8).

In hairy roots transformed with pK‐CMPE‐ePPEplus‐MsV1‐MsALS1‐MsACC1‐MsALS2, the average allelic editing frequency was 0.01% for MsALS2 and Hi‐TOM deep sequencing did not detect any desired edits in MsALS1 or MsACC1. Roots transformed with pK‐CMPE‐ePPEplus‐MsV2‐MsALS1‐MsACC1‐MsALS2 exhibited desired allelic editing frequencies of 1.76% for MsALS1, 1.23% for MsALS2 and 0.29% for MsACC1 (Figure 1b). We quantified the expression level of nCas9 in transgenic positive roots, which showed that the M4 promoter drove significantly higher nCas9 accumulation (Figure S9). Thus, the high transcriptional activity of the M4 promoter enhanced PE efficiency in alfalfa.

We introduced pK‐CMPE‐ePPEplus‐MsV2‐MsALS1‐MsACC1‐MsALS2 into ‘Zhongmu No. 1’ via Agrobacterium tumefaciens –mediated transformation. Among 798 regenerated events, we identified 719 T0 transgene‐positive plants, including 8 with desired edits at MsALS1 (1.11%), 13 at MsALS2 (1.81%) and 1 at MsACC1 (0.14%) (Figure 1c). Category 1 line exhibited mono‐allelic editing at the MsALS1 locus (Line #149); Category 2 lines displayed mono‐allelic editing at the MsALS2 locus (Lines #33, #74, #131, #477); Category 3 line carried bi‐allelic editing at the MsALS2 locus (Line #111); Category 4 lines harboured mono‐allelic mutations at both MsALS1 and MsALS2 (Lines #6, #54, #90, #91, #326, #389, #391); Category 5 line contained bi‐allelic editing at both MsALS1 and MsALS2, along with mono‐allelic editing at the MsACC1 locus (Line #284) (Figure 1d,e).

We vegetatively propagated wild‐type (WT) and Category 1–5 lines from shoot cuttings in the greenhouse. All transgenic lines exhibited robust growth similar to WT, indicating that the edits in MsALS1, MsALS2 and MsACC1 did not impair growth and development (Figure 1f). We sprayed 2‐month‐old plants at the six‐leaf stage with 200 mg/L nicosulfuron 1 week after cutting. One month later, WT plants showed widespread leaf yellowing, wilting, severe necrosis and almost complete growth arrest. The Category 1 line #149 exhibited milder damage, with only marginal yellowing on a few leaves and robust overall growth. The Category 2 line #74 exhibited weaker resistance, with leaf yellowing and partial necrosis. The Category 3 line #111 was more resistant, with more limited chlorosis and necrosis. The Category 4 line #326 exhibited strong resistance, with only slight yellowing on a few leaves and no clear necrosis; plant growth remained largely unaffected. Notably, the Category 5 line #284 displayed near‐complete herbicide resistance, with treated plants showing green, intact leaves with normal chlorophyll contents and vigorous growth (Figure 1f, Figure S10). Combining phenotypic observations and quantitative data, we conclude that MsALS1 acts as the major‐effect gene, with MsALS1 and MsALS2 showing dosage‐dependent and additive effects. We also quantified the MsALS1 and MsALS2 transcript levels in WT leaves; this showed higher expression of MsALS1, consistent with its function as the major nicosulfuron resistance gene (Figure S11).

Owing to the natural tolerance of dicots to ACCase‐inhibiting herbicides, WT and Category 5 line #284 were treated with 216 mg/L and 648 mg/L haloxyfop‐P‐methyl. No evident phenotypic differences were observed between genotypes at 216 mg/L 1 week after treatment (Figure S12). At 648 mg/L, WT exhibited severe leaf curling and extensive necrosis, whereas line #284 displayed only mild marginal necrosis on a small number of leaves (Figure 1f). Therefore, Category 5 line #284 exhibited some resistance to haloxyfop‐P‐methyl and nearly complete resistance to nicosulfuron.

Overall, using our improved PE system, we successfully edited MsALS1, MsALS2 and MsACC1 to create alfalfa germplasm resistant to multiple herbicides.
