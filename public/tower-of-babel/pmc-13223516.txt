Mutations in the superoxide-responsive regulator SoxR confer fluoroquinolone resistance in Stenotrophomonas maltophilia
Authors: Poonyaporn Kanawong, Parinya Tipanyo, Suleepon Poomchuchit, Skorn Mongkolsuk, Paiboon Vattanaviboon, Nisanart Charoenlap
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•soxR mutations cause constitutive activation of SmeVWX and MfsA efflux pumps.•Mutations in soxR confer resistant to levofloxacin, a drug option for S. maltophilia.•Single R49 substitution activates SoxR and drives multidrug resistance.Keywords: Acquired resistance, Antibiotic, MfsA, Mutation, SmeWVX, SoxR, Stenotrophomonas maltophilia

Introduction

Stenotrophomonas maltophilia is an aerobic, non-fermentative Gram-negative bacterium that causes challenging hospital-acquired infections, especially in immunocompromised patients globally (Brooke 2021). Despite being categorized as a less virulent pathogen, it poses a significant threat because its infections are associated with a high mortality rate, driven by its intrinsic resistance to multiple antibiotics (Mikhailovich, et al. 2024). Its chromosomes carry an arsenal of inherent antimicrobial resistance traits, including genes that encode multidrug efflux transporters, enzymes that degrade or modify antibiotics, and drug-target-binding proteins (Crossman, et al. 2008, Ma, et al. 2020, Sanchez 2015), rendering it highly resistant to a wide array of antibiotics. S. maltophilia also acquires resistance genes, such as sul genes, which encode sulfonamide-insensitive variants of dihydropteroate synthase, from neighboring bacteria through horizontal gene transfer (Chung, et al. 2015, Furlan, et al. 2019, Hu, et al. 2016). This gene confers high-level resistance to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (co-trimoxazole) in S. maltophilia (Chung, et al. 2015), which is the first-line drug used to treat its infections (Canton and Ruiz-Garbajosa 2023).

The redox-responsive regulator SoxR belongs to the MerR family of transcriptional regulators (Hidalgo and Demple 1994). It exists as a homodimer, with each subunit consisting of a helix-turn-helix DNA-binding domain at the N-terminus and a [2Fe-2S] cluster-binding domain at the C-terminus (Watanabe, et al. 2008). SoxR responds to superoxide anions, redox-cycling agents, which generate superoxide anions, and reactive nitrogen species by undergoing a reversible one-electron oxidation of the [2Fe-2S] cluster, transitioning from the 1+ to the 2+ oxidation state (Gu and Imlay 2011, Hidalgo, et al. 1997, Singh, et al. 2013). Several lines of evidence indicate that SoxR can be oxidized through direct interaction with redox-active compounds (Imlay 2015, Kobayashi 2017, Kobayashi, et al. 2025). The redox change converts SoxR from an inactive, reduced form to an active, oxidized form. Moreover, guanidine radical-generating photooxidants (Lee, et al. 2009) have been shown to activate SoxR.

In Escherichia coli, soxS is the sole gene regulated by SoxR. SoxS is a transcriptional regulator belonging to the AraC/XylS family and is up-regulated once SoxR is activated (Amabile-Cuevas and Demple 1991). The synthesized SoxS then activates the expression of more than 100 genes that contribute to protection against superoxide stress, repair of cellular damage occurring during stress (Pomposiello and Demple 2001), and antimicrobial resistance (Duval and Lister 2013). This two-step SoxRS paradigm does not apply to non-enteric bacteria. For instance, in the plant pathogen alphaproteobacterium Agrobacterium tumefaciens, SoxR regulates its regulon, including sodBII, which encodes Fe-SOD, by directly binding to the target promoters and activating their transcription upon exposure to redox cycling drugs/superoxide generators (Saenkham, et al. 2007). In the gammaproteobacteria Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Xanthomonas campestris, SoxR also directly controls target genes that play minor roles in protection against superoxide stress, even though it can sense and respond to redox cycling drugs (Mahavihakanont, et al. 2012, Palma, et al. 2005). The filamentous bacterium Streptomyces avermitilis SoxR directly modulates the regulation of genes involved in various cellular processes, such as primary metabolism, antibiotic production (avermectin and oligomycin A), morphological development, and thiol-mediated oxidative stress responses (Wang, et al. 2022).

In S. maltophilia, limited studies have been performed to elucidate the physiological functions of SoxR. S. maltophilia SoxR directly regulates the transcription of sodA1 (encoding Mn-SOD) in response to superoxide stress, thereby protecting against oxidative damage (Giengkam, et al. 2025, Jair, et al. 2019). Furthermore, S. maltophilia SoxR plays a crucial role in the regulation of antibiotic resistance genes, showcasing its versatility and importance in bacterial survival under antibiotic pressure. Two multidrug efflux pumps, namely, MfsA (belonging to the major facilitator superfamily) and SmeVWX (belonging to the resistance-nodulation-cell division family, RND), are regulated by SoxR through both direct and indirect mechanisms (Srijaruskul, et al. 2015, Vattanaviboon, et al. 2025, Wu, et al. 2018). SmeVWX is encoded by an operon encompassing five genes: smeU1, smeV, smeW, smeU2, and smeX (Chen, et al. 2011). Among these, SmeV, SmeW, and SmeX form a tripartite complex consisting of a membrane fusion protein, an RND-type transporter, and an outer membrane protein, respectively. By contrast, SmeU1 and SmeU2 are members of the short-chain dehydrogenase/reductase family (Chen, et al. 2011). This smeU1VWU2X operon is hereafter referred to as smeVWX.

MfsA is an efflux transporter for fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides, tetracycline, certain cephalosporins, and chloramphenicol (Dulyayangkul, et al. 2016). Upon overexpression, it confers high-level resistance to fluoroquinolones (Vattanaviboon, et al. 2018). The SmeVWX transporter expels quinolones, chloramphenicol, tetracycline, and co-trimoxazole (Chen, et al. 2011, Sanchez and Martinez 2018). SoxR modulates mfsA expression in a typical manner by binding to the mfsA operator, with its reduced form repressing and its oxidized form activating (Srijaruskul, et al. 2015). However, the regulation of SmeVWX is more complicated, involving several transcriptional regulators, including SmeRv (Chen, et al. 2011), AzoR (Lu, et al. 2021), and SoxR (Vattanaviboon, et al. 2025, Wu, et al. 2018). Under normal conditions, SmeRv and AzoR negatively regulate smeVWX operon expression (Chen, et al. 2011, Lu, et al. 2021). When S. maltophilia encounters superoxide stress, such as exposure to redox cycling drugs, the oxidized SoxR upregulates SmeRv expression, which in turn activates SmeVWX expression (Wu, et al. 2018). An experimental evolutionary study identified a point mutation in SoxR that is associated with ciprofloxacin resistance in S. maltophilia (Ochoa-Sanchez, et al. 2024). Therefore, SoxR links the oxidative stress response with antibiotic resistance. In this study, we demonstrated that mutations in SoxR can lead to the constitutive activation of mfsA, smeVWX, smeRv, and sodA1 expression, conferring high-level resistance to fluoroquinolones, including levofloxacin, one of the therapeutic options for treating S. maltophilia infections.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and plasmids

Stenotrophomonas maltophilia clinical strain K279a (Crossman, et al. 2008) was used as the parental wild-type strain. Routinely, a single freshly streaked colony from S. maltophilia strains was used to inoculate lysogeny broth (LB), and cultures were incubated overnight at 35°C with constant shaking at 180 rpm. The overnight culture was then subcultured into fresh LB medium at an initial optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.1. Cells in the exponential growth phase, corresponding to an OD600 of 0.5, were used for all experiments. For menadione induction experiments, the exponential cultures were treated with 200 μM menadione sodium bisulfite for 15 min.

Molecular biology techniques

General molecular genetics techniques, including bacterial genomic DNA extraction, total RNA extraction, agarose gel electrophoresis, gene cloning, polymerase chain reaction (PCR), and transformation, were carried out according to standard protocols (Sambrook and Russell 2001). Plasmid DNA was extracted using the Qiagen plasmid extraction kit (Hilden, Germany). Transformation of S. maltophilia was performed using electroporation (Srijaruskul, et al. 2015). Construction of pSoxR plasmid

The full-length soxR gene (GenBank accession number AM743169; locus tag: smlt1084), including the putative ribosome binding site, was amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using primers BT2787 and BT2788, with S. maltophilia K279a genomic DNA as the template. The resulting 457-bp PCR product was cloned into the broad-host-range plasmid pBBR1MCS-5 (Kovach, et al. 1994) cut with EcoRV to generate pSoxR. The inserted gene was validated by DNA sequencing.

Construction of the soxR mutant library using PCR-based error prone mutagenesis

The soxR gene mutant library was generated using PCR-based random error-prone mutagenesis with the GeneMorph II Random Mutagenesis Kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The plasmid pSoxR, containing the full-length soxR gene, was used as the template in PCR amplification using the vector primers M13 Forward and M13 Reverse. The PCR products were digested with Acc65I and SpeI and subsequently cloned into the pBBR1MCS-5 vector digested with the same restriction enzymes. After ligation, the recombinant plasmids were transformed into E. coli DH5α and plated on LB agar supplemented with 15 μg/mL chloramphenicol for selection. All colonies were pooled for plasmid isolation using the Qiagen Plasmid Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). This plasmid library was kept at -80°C.

Selection of ciprofloxacin-resistant transformants

The soxR gene mutant plasmid library was introduced into the S. maltophilia ΔsoxR strain (Srijaruskul, et al. 2015). Transformants capable of growing on LB agar plates containing 10 μg/mL ciprofloxacin (minimal inhibitory concentration [MIC] of 8 μg/mL for the ΔsoxR mutant) were selected and purified for further analysis. The inserted soxR gene in each isolate was analyzed by DNA sequencing.

Site-directed mutagenesis

Single amino acid substitutions in the soxR gene were generated using the QuikChange II Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA), a PCR-based technique. PCR was conducted according to the manufacturer’s instructions using pSoxR as the DNA template and the overlap primers containing the desired mutations. Table 1 lists the primer pairs used for each amino acid substitution. The amplified plasmid products were treated with DpnI to digest the parent DNA template, thereby selectively retaining the newly synthesized mutant plasmids. Each amino acid substitution was confirmed by DNA sequencing.

Antibiotic susceptibility testing

The antibiotic susceptibility of S. maltophilia strains was assessed using the Kirby–Bauer disc diffusion method (Wood and Washington 1995). After incubation at 35°C for 24 h, the diameters of the inhibition zones were measured and recorded. The following antibiotic discs (Oxoid, Hampshire, UK) were tested: nalidixic acid (NA, 30 µg), ofloxacin (OFX, 5 µg), ciprofloxacin (CIP, 5 µg), levofloxacin (LEV, 5 µg), moxifloxacin (MXF, 5 µg), minocycline (MH, 30 µg), co-trimoxazole (SXT, 25 µg), tigecycline (TGC, 15 µg), amikacin (AK, 30 µg), and ceftazidime (CAZ, 30 µg). Results are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three independent experiments.

Real-time reverse transcription-PCR analysis

The expression levels of mfsA, smeRv, smeVWX, and sodA1 in S. maltophilia strains were measured using real-time reverse transcription (RT)-PCR, as previously described (Boonyong, et al. 2025). Total RNA was extracted from exponential-phase cultures and treated with DNase I. Subsequently, 1 μg of RNA was used for reverse transcription with RevertAid Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and random hexamers, following the manufacturer’s protocol. The resulting cDNA (10 ng) was used as a template in PCR reactions containing SYBR Select Master Mix for CFX (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and gene-specific primers (BT7111-BT7112, BT8566-BT8567, BT8139-BT8140, and BT7117-BT7118 for mfsA, smeRv, smeVWX, and sodA1, respectively). PCR amplification was performed on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific), beginning with an initial heat activation step at 95°C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, annealing at 60°C for 20 s, and extension at 72°C for 30 s. Expression of 16S rRNA, amplified using primers BT2781 and BT2782 (Table 1), served as the internal reference for normalization. Data were analyzed using the 2-ΔΔCT method with StepOne software v2.1, and gene expression levels were expressed as fold changes relative to the wild-type strain K279a. Experiments were performed independently in triplicate, and the results are reported as the mean ± SD.

Protein structure prediction and comparative analysis

The protein structure of SoxR homodimer from S. maltophilia K279a (Smlt1084) (Crossman, et al. 2008, Srijaruskul, et al. 2015) was predicted using ESMFold structure prediction (Lin, et al. 2023). Prediction quality was validated through multiple confidence metrics. The predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT) scores ranged from 77.5 to 90 (mean: 84.4), indicating reliable structural predictions, as pLDDT values above 70 correspond to accurate backbone modeling (Abramson, et al. 2024, Guo, et al. 2022). All structures showed high global quality metrics, with predicted template modeling scores (pTM) ranging from 0.876 to 0.951 and interface predicted template modeling scores (ipTM) ranging from 0.874 to 0.944. Values exceeding 0.8 indicate confident, high-quality structural predictions suitable for comparative analysis (Wee and Wei 2024).

Structural deviations between SoxR variants and the wild type were quantified using Kabsch superposition in PyMOL (Version 3.1, Schrödinger, LLC) (Kabsch 1976; Schrödinger, 2025). Per-residue Cα deviations were calculated following optimal rigid-body alignment, with structures superimposed over all equivalent Cα atoms. Mean deviations, standard deviations, and distributions were computed across all residues and within functionally defined domains: DNA-binding domains (residues 1–74 and 145–218) and [2Fe-2S] cluster-binding sites (residues 113–124 and 257–268). Structural hotspots were identified as residues exhibiting maximum local deviations within each region.

Molecular dynamics simulations and solvent accessibility analysis of the [2Fe-2S] cluster

All-atom molecular dynamics simulations were performed using OpenMM 8.1.2 with the AMBER14SB force field (Eastman, et al. 2024, Maier, et al. 2015). Each SoxR homodimer structure was solvated in a cubic box containing transferable intermolecular potential with three points (TIP3P) water molecules, with a minimum distance of 1.0 nm maintained between protein atoms and the box boundaries (Jorgensen, et al. 1983). Systems were neutralized with Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions at a concentration of 0.15 M.

Energy minimization was carried out using the steepest descent algorithm (maximum 50,000 steps, force tolerance 1000 kJ/mol/nm). Equilibration was carried out in two phases: first, an NVT ensemble (100 ps at 300 K using a Langevin thermostat), followed by an NPT ensemble (100 ps at 1bar using a Monte Carlo barostat). Production simulations were run for 10 ns at 300 K and 1 bar with a 2 fs time step. Long-range electrostatics were calculated using the Particle Mesh Ewald method, with a real-space cutoff of 1.0 nm. Van der Waals interactions employed a 1.0 nm cutoff, with force switching applied between 0.8 nm and 1.0 nm. Coordinate snapshots were saved every 10 ps for subsequent analysis.

Solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) was computed using molecular dynamics trajectories (MDTraj) with a probe radius of 0.14 nm and 960 sphere points (McGibbon, et al. 2015). Total protein SASA was calculated for each frame within the 2–10 ns trajectory window after confirming equilibration. Per-residue SASA values were extracted and averaged over this equilibrated window. Changes in solvent accessibility (ΔSASA) for each variant were determined as the difference between the mean SASA values of the variant and the wild type for each residue. Domain-level ΔSASA distributions were analyzed for DNA-binding helices and [2Fe-2S] cluster-binding sites, with 95% confidence intervals calculated as mean ± 1.96 × (standard deviation / √n), where n is the number of residues in each domain.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 26 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). Difference in antimicrobial susceptibility profiles, gene expression levels, Per-residue ΔSASA analysis between the S. maltophilia variant strains, and K279a wild-type were evaluated using independent sample t-test. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Selection of S. maltophilia soxR gene mutations

SoxR is a regulatory protein conserved among several bacteria, including S. maltophilia, E. coli, and X. campestris (Fig. 1A). In S. maltophilia, SoxR functions as a transcriptional activator involved in antibiotic resistance by modulating the expression of multidrug efflux pumps MfsA and SmeVWX. Given that ciprofloxacin is a known substrate of both MfsA and SmeVWX efflux pumps, we designed experiments to investigate whether mutations in soxR influence ciprofloxacin resistance. The plasmid library that carries the mutated soxR gene was introduced into an S. maltophilia ΔsoxR mutant and selected for transformants resistant to ciprofloxacin at a concentration of 10 μg/mL. We obtained a total of 21 colonies capable of growing on ciprofloxacin-containing plates. The plasmid DNA was extracted from these clones, and the inserted soxR gene was sequenced and analyzed to determine the mutation points.

As presented in Table 2, the mutations in soxR from these ciprofloxacin-resistant clones could be classified into five distinct patterns, labeled A through E. Pattern A was identified in 10 clones and comprised substitution mutations across key regions of the SoxR protein: V56I and E65L within the DNA-binding domain, L88M and G112Y within the dimerization domain, and a nonsense mutation at E135, which resulted in truncation of the C-terminal region (Fig. 1B). Additional mutations, including G139S and a –1 frameshift at P140, were also detected. However, these mutations occurred downstream of the premature stop at E135 and thus did not affect the amino acid sequence of this SoxR variant (Table 2). Pattern B occurred in six ciprofloxacin-resistant clones involving mutations in both the DNA-binding domain (R49S) and the dimerization domain (A90S, R91C, and Q104L) (Fig. 1B). Pattern C, detected in two clones, showed substitution mutations concentrated within the DNA-binding domain, R49H and A66S. Pattern D consisted of two clones in which mutations occurred in both the DNA-binding domain (R45S) and in the C-terminal region (D131V and R136L) (Fig. 1B). Finally, a clone in Pattern E contained mutations in the dimerization domain (A90S and L106M) and the [2Fe-2S] cluster-binding site (L121P). The pSoxR plasmids derived from each transformant group, designated as patterns A through E, were named pSoxRA, pSoxRB, pSoxRC, pSoxRD, and pSoxRE, respectively.

Antibiotic susceptibility profiles in ΔsoxR strains carrying mutant soxR

Next, the antibiotic susceptibility profiles of the ΔsoxR mutant strains carrying plasmids pSoxRA, pSoxRB, pSoxRC, pSoxRD, and pSoxRE were determined against fluoroquinolones and various other classes of antibiotics using the Kirby–Bauer disc diffusion method. A strain that exhibited a zone of inhibition at least 5 mm smaller than that of the ΔsoxR mutant was considered to have significantly increased resistance, as described previously (Dulyayangkul, et al. 2020). As illustrated in Fig. 2, all ΔsoxR mutants expressing SoxR variants demonstrated increased resistance to quinolones, including nalidixic acid, ofloxacin, ciprofloxacin, and moxifloxacin. According to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines (CLSI, 2025), all ΔsoxR mutants complemented with SoxR variants showed a levofloxacin-resistant phenotype (inhibition zone ≤ 13 mm), except for SoxRE, which exhibited an intermediate phenotype (inhibition zone 14–16 mm). Similarly, all SoxR variants, except SoxRE, increased the resistance of the ΔsoxR mutant against co-trimoxazole. However, despite this increased resistance, the strains were still categorized as susceptible to co-trimoxazole based on CLSI criteria (inhibition zone ≥ 16 mm). No significant changes in susceptibility were observed for other antibiotic classes, including tetracyclines (tigecycline and minocycline), aminoglycosides (amikacin), and cephalosporins (ceftazidime).

Expression profiles of genes in SoxR regulon

SoxR plays a regulatory role in the expression of the multidrug efflux pumps MfsA and SmeVWX, the transcriptional regulator SmeRv, and the superoxide dismutase SodA1 (Giengkam, et al. 2025, Srijaruskul, et al. 2015, Wu, et al. 2018). Fig. 3 shows that, relative to the K279a wild type, the expression levels of smeV (representing the smeU1VWU2X operon) were markedly elevated in all ΔsoxR mutants expressing variant SoxR, ranging from 120.6 ± 43.0 fold to 186.0 ± 25.9 fold. Likewise, sodA1 expression increased substantially, varying from 158.2 ± 17.8 fold to 280.2 ± 49.9 fold. By contrast, mfsA expression showed a moderate increase, ranging from 19.9 ± 4.9 fold to 25.8 ± 12.8 fold, while smeRv expression rose slightly, ranging from 1.6 ± 0.7 fold to 3.4 ± 0.1 fold. Therefore, under physiological growth conditions, SoxR variants function similarly to the oxidized form in terms of activating genes within the SoxR regulon.

The [2Fe-2S] cluster-binding motif was required for the upregulation of efflux pumps and reduced antibiotic susceptibility in ΔsoxR strains carrying mutant soxR

SoxR senses and responds to superoxide anions through oxidation of its [2Fe-2S] cluster center. The observation that mutations in SoxR lead to the upregulation of genes in its regulon suggests two possibilities: Either the mutations trigger a conformational state similar to the oxidized, active form of SoxR, or SoxR variants are constitutively maintained in an oxidized, active form. If the latter is correct, the SoxR variant proteins would still rely on the [2Fe-2S] cluster center for activation. To investigate whether the [2Fe-2S] cluster is required for the activation of genes in the regulon by the SoxR variants, site-directed mutagenesis was employed. Typically, SoxR proteins contain a four-cysteine motif (CX2CXCX5C), corresponding to the sequence C113-IG-C116-GC118-LSLQR-C124 in S. maltophilia SoxR, which is essential for [2Fe-2S] cluster coordination (Watanabe, et al. 2008). Three of the four conserved cysteine residues, C113, C116, and C118, were individually substituted with serine, a residue known to minimally affect protein structure, in the constructs pSoxR, pSoxRA, pSoxRB, pSoxRC, pSoxRD, and pSoxRE. The resulting plasmids, designated pSoxR*, pSoxRA*, pSoxRB*, pSoxRC*, pSoxRD*, and pSoxRE*, were introduced into the ΔsoxR mutant, and the expression levels of smeVWX and mfsA were measured in bacterial cultures grown under uninduced physiological conditions. As illustrated in Fig. 4, substitution of the three cysteine residues in the [2Fe-2S] cluster-binding motif with serine diminished the upregulation of smeVWX and mfsA across all SoxR variants (pSoxRA*–pSoxRE*) under uninduced conditions.

The redox cycling drug menadione is a known agent capable of activating SoxR (Srijaruskul, et al. 2015, Wu, et al. 2018). Characterization of amino acid substitutions affecting SoxR function

To investigate the effect of single amino acid substitutions on SoxR activation, PCR-based site-directed mutagenesis was performed using pSoxR as the DNA template. Based on the mutations identified in group A–E mutants, residues conserved among Stenotrophomonas and related species, including Xanthomonas campestris, were selected for further substitution analysis. The targeted amino acid substitutions included R45S, R49H, R49S, E65L, A66S, L88M, A90S, L106M, G112Y, L121P, D131V, and E135stop. Plasmid pBBR1MCS-5 carrying each SoxR variant was introduced into the ΔsoxR mutant, and susceptibility against quinolones, namely nalidixic acid, ciprofloxacin, ofloxacin, moxifloxacin, and levofloxacin, and co-trimoxazole was assessed. The results revealed that only mutations at R49 (R49S and R49H) rendered the strains resistant to levofloxacin and increased their resistance to other quinolones and co-trimoxazole (Fig. 6A). Consistent with the susceptibility profiles, the R49S and R49H substitutions significantly increased the relative expression of smeVWX (106.5 ± 26.6 and 82.6 ± 10.1 fold, respectively) and mfsA (29.6 ± 0.3 and 52.2 ± 21.1 fold, respectively) compared to the wild type (Fig. 6B and 6C). These findings indicate that substitution at R49 is pivotal for activation of SoxR function.

Computational structural analysis of SoxR variants and the wild type

SoxR is activated through oxidation of its [2Fe-2S] cluster centers, a process mediated by superoxide anions or redox-cycling drugs (Imlay 2015). Crystal structure analysis reveals that the plane of the [2Fe-2S] cluster is not perpendicular but bent by approximately 20°, showing an asymmetric environment configuration (Watanabe, et al. 2008). The upper sulfur atom and both iron atoms are fully exposed to the solvent. The solvent-exposed [2Fe-2S] cluster likely facilitates efficient electron transfer between SoxR and superoxide anions, as well as various redox-active agents (Watanabe, et al. 2008). To assess whether the mutations present in the SoxR variants affect the solvent accessibility of the [2Fe-2S] cluster center, computational structure modeling was performed.

Structure predictions for all SoxR homodimer proteins achieved high confidence scores, validating the reliability of subsequent comparative analyses. The predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT) scores ranged from 77.5 (wild-type SoxR) to 90.0 (SoxRA), with a mean of 84.4 across all structures (Supplementary Fig. 1). Notably, the SoxRR49S (pLDDT: 88.8) and SoxRR49H (pLDDT: 89.1) exhibited higher per-residue confidence scores than the wild type, suggesting that these mutations stabilize local structural features that are reflected in the prediction algorithm’s confidence output. All structures showed consistently high global quality metrics, with pTM scores ranging from 0.876 to 0.951 and ipTM scores ranging from 0.874 to 0.944, indicating confident predictions of both overall fold topology and inter-chain interfaces.

Per-residue Cα deviation analysis following Kabsch superposition revealed variant-specific structural deviations relative to the SoxR wild type (Fig. 7A). However, comparative analysis demonstrated that these perturbations were largely confined to the flexible termini. When restricted to the core structure (residues 6–130), all systems exhibited a highly conserved global topology, with mean Cα deviations consistently remaining below 3.0 Å. This magnitude is consistent with commonly used practical thresholds in structural analyses, where ∼3 Å Cα RMSD is often considered a reasonable cutoff for grouping or defining closely related conformations and conserved core structures after superposition (Michino and Brooks III 2009, Ozboyaci, et al. 2016). Although local deviations occasionally approached 3 Å, these were confined to loop regions and did not propagate into secondary structure elements.

Although the overall structures of the variants showed no substantial deviations from the SoxR wild-type model, domain-resolved structural analysis revealed differential impacts on specific structural regions. The DNA-binding domain (residues 1–74) remained structurally conserved across all SoxR variants, exhibiting minimal perturbations with mean deviations ranging from 0.60 ± 0.33 Å to 3.04 ± 1.28 Å (e.g., SoxRB: 2.49 ± 0.94 Å; SoxRR49S: 2.99 ± 1.30 Å; SoxRR49H: 3.04 ± 1.28 Å). This structural preservation suggests that the fundamental DNA recognition capability is maintained regardless of the mutations. By contrast, the Fe–S cluster-binding site (residues 113–124) exhibited conformational alterations, especially in the SoxRB and R49 variants, suggesting that these mutations may perturb Fe–S coordination, domain stability, or the redox-dependent regulatory function associated with this region. While other variants maintained a rigid cluster environment (SoxRA: 0.94 ± 0.32 Å; SoxRC: 1.03 ± 0.34 Å; SoxRD: 0.93 ± 0.36 Å), SoxRB displayed a marked structural shift (3.35 ± 2.63 Å) in this region. Similarly, substitutions at R49 (SoxRR49S and SoxRR49H) induced significant remodeling of the cluster-binding loop (3.77 ± 0.42 Å and 3.95 ± 0.41 Å, respectively). These findings indicate that the SoxRB variant and R49 substitutions in SoxR selectively modulate the redox-sensing center without compromising the global fold or the DNA-binding interface.

Solvent accessibility analysis using molecular dynamics simulations

All systems reached stable surface area values within 2 ns of simulation, as evidenced by the stabilization of total protein SASA (Fig. 7B). Wild-type SoxR maintained a stable mean SASA of 154.93 ± 3.11 nm². SoxR variants exhibited divergent structural trends: SoxRE, SoxRB, and SoxRC showed expanded surface areas (+6.2%, +5.5%, and +2.4%, respectively), while SoxRA and SoxRD showed decreased SASA (-2.4% and -1.4%). Both R49 substitutions were associated with increased total SASA: SoxRR49H showed the highest mean SASA among all variants (165.45 ± 5.39 nm², +6.8%), while SoxRR49S exhibited a mean SASA of 162.25 ± 1.68 nm² (+4.7%). This indicates that substitutions at the R49 position induced the most significant structural opening.

Per-residue ΔSASA analysis revealed domain-specific patterns (Fig. 7C). The DNA-binding domain (residues 1–74 and 145–218) remained near baseline across all variants, with absolute mean ΔSASA values ranging from 0.12 to 0.16 nm². By contrast, the [2Fe-2S] cluster-binding site (residues 113–124 and 257–268) exhibited a pronounced variant-specific increase in solvent exposure. Notably, SoxRB and R49 substitutions showed the largest positive shifts, with mean ΔSASA of SoxRB (+0.38 ± 0.64 nm²), SoxRR49S (+0.49 ± 0.49 nm²), and SoxRR49H (+0.47 ± 0.44 nm²). Overall, the data suggest preferential remodeling of the redox-sensing surface around the [2Fe–2S] cluster while preserving the solvent exposure pattern of the DNA-binding interface.

Domain-level ΔSASA distributions (Fig. 7D) provided statistical validation. In the DNA-binding domain, variants exhibited no significant deviation from the wild type (mean ΔSASA: -0.035 ± 0.027 nm2; p = 0.86), confirming that the structural integrity of this motif was strictly maintained.

For the [2Fe-2S] cluster-binding site, SoxRB and the R49 substitution variants exhibited statistically significant increases in solvent exposure: SoxRB (mean: +0.376 nm², p = 0.015), SoxRR49S (mean: +0.488 nm², p = 0.002), and SoxRR49H (mean: +0.470 nm², p = 0.002). These findings suggest that such substitutions may facilitate the oxidation of the [2Fe-2S] cluster, potentially rendering the protein hypersensitive to oxidative stress.

Discussion

Although SoxR proteins are conserved among bacteria, their regulatory roles differ across species. SoxR senses and responds to several radical-generating agents, primarily redox-cycling drugs/superoxide generators, and its regulatory functions encompass a variety of cellular processes. In S. maltophilia, SoxR is activated in response to exposure to redox-cycling drugs, such as menadione, paraquat, and plumbagin (Srijaruskul, et al. 2015). It regulates the expression of SodA1, which directly detoxifies the superoxide anions generated by these drugs. SoxR also contributes to the regulation of two multidrug efflux pumps, MfsA and SmeVWX, highlighting its critical role in antibiotic resistance (Srijaruskul, et al. 2015, Vattanaviboon, et al. 2018, Wu, et al. 2018). In this study, we experimentally demonstrate that mutations in SoxR can lead to increased resistance to multiple antibiotics, including fluoroquinolones and co-trimoxazole, due to the overexpression of SmeVWX and MfsA. All five SoxR variants (SoxRA–SoxRE) also drove elevated expression of SodA1, whose product has been shown to confer resistance to redox cycling drugs (Giengkam, et al. 2025). This suggests that strains expressing these SoxR variants may better tolerate superoxide anions, one of the ROS generated as part of innate immunity during host–microbe interactions in phagocytic cells (Giengkam, et al. 2025).

The contribution of SoxR to antimicrobial resistance has been documented in other organisms. P. aeruginosa harbors the RND-type antibiotic efflux system encoded by mexHGI-opmD, an operon whose expression is induced by SoxR (Rozner, et al. 2022). In E. coli, SoxR plays a role in antimicrobial resistance by activating SoxS transcription. Together with MarA and Rob, SoxS positively modulates the transcription of the AcrAB-TolC multidrug efflux system, which is a prototypical member of the RND-type transporter family in E. coli (Duval and Lister 2013). This efflux pump expels a wide range of structurally dissimilar substances, including diverse classes of antibiotics, such as fluoroquinolones, certain β-lactams, macrolides, and tetracyclines, as well as detergents, bile salts, and cationic dyes (Nikaido and Takatsuka 2009). Moreover, elevated levels of SoxS downregulate the ompF gene, which encodes an outer membrane porin, thereby decreasing membrane permeability to antibiotics (Miller and Sulavik 1996). S. maltophilia also possesses a homolog of AcrAB-TolC, known as SmeABC. However, its regulation of this efflux system does not involve SoxR; instead, it is controlled by the two-component regulatory system SmeSR, a homolog of the E. coli BaeSR system (Li, et al. 2002, Vattanaviboon, et al. 2025). These observations suggest that different bacterial species have adapted SoxR to play distinct roles in antimicrobial resistance.

As a MerR-type transcriptional regulator, SoxR exists in two forms, repressor and activator, depending on the oxidative state of its [2Fe-2S] cluster centers. The reduced SoxR with [2Fe-2S]+ functions as a repressor. Upon oxidation one-electron oxidation, the [2Fe-2S]+ cluster center is converted to [2Fe-2S]+2, triggering a conformational change from a repressor to an activator (Hidalgo, et al. 1997). Generally, both forms of SoxR bind to the target gene operator site, which is located in the intervening sequence between the -35 and -10 promoter motifs with an unusual spacer length of 19 bp (Hidalgo and Demple 1997). Binding of the reduced form represses gene expression; by contrast, binding of the oxidized SoxR to the promoter induces a conformational change in the DNA structure, facilitating RNA polymerase binding and activating the transcription of target genes (Hidalgo, et al. 1998, Watanabe, et al. 2008). The oxidized SoxR protein is rapidly inactivated by reducing systems, such as rsxABCDGE gene products, RseC, and ApbE in E. coli, which use NADPH or NADH as the electron donor (Lee, et al. 2024, Lee, et al. 2022).

In our study, each SoxR variant harbored multiple point mutations, resulting in amino acid substitutions. Notably, SoxRA also contained a nonsense mutation at residue E135, resulting in a truncated SoxR protein lacking 10 amino acids from the C-terminus. However, this E135Stop mutation did not appear to inactivate SoxR, as a variant carrying only the E135Stop exhibited a phenotype comparable to that of wild-type SoxR. A single amino acid substitution at position R49, specifically R49S in SoxRB and R49H in SoxRC, was sufficient to activate SoxR. By contrast, the remaining variants required multiple amino acid substitutions, such as SoxRA (V56I, E65L, L88M, G112Y, and E135Stop), SoxRD (R45S, D131V, and R136L), and SoxRE (A90S, L106M, and L121P). The majority of amino acid substitutions occurred within the dimerization domain, except for R45 and R49, which are located in the DNA-binding domain and L121, which resides in the [2Fe-2S]-binding domain. However, the single amino acid substitution L121P failed to activate the SoxR protein.

There are several possible mechanisms by which amino acid substitutions can activate SoxR. One possibility is that they alter the redox properties of the [2Fe-2S] cluster centers, making them hypersensitive to oxidation, even under normal aerobic growth conditions (Hidalgo, et al. 1997). Another is that the mutations may induce conformational changes in the SoxR protein that mimic its oxidized state. Lastly, the mutations may disrupt the reduction (deactivation) process of the oxidized [2Fe-2S] cluster center (Hidalgo, et al. 1997).

The SoxR-reducing mechanisms in S. maltophilia have not yet been elucidated. We used the E. coli SoxR-reducing proteins RsxABCDGE, RseC, and ApbE as queries in a BLAST search against the S. maltophilia K279a genome sequence (Camacho, et al. 2009). No open reading frames with significant homology were identified, suggesting that S. maltophilia may employ SoxR reducing systems distinct from those in E. coli. Furthermore, the possibility that amino acid substitutions in SoxR variants interfere with the reduction process, for example, by disrupting protein–protein interaction or electron transfer, cannot be excluded.

The results from the computational structural modeling analysis of SoxR variants revealed no significant changes in the overall structure, particularly in the DNA-binding domain, compared to wild-type SoxR. This observation suggests that these mutations are unlikely to induce a conformational state mimicking the oxidized, active form of SoxR.

The role of the [2Fe-2S] cluster centers in constitutively activating SoxR variants was evaluated using constructs with mutations in the [2Fe-2S]-binding site (from SoxRA* to SoxRE*). These constructs were expected to produce SoxR variants with reduced [2Fe-2S] binding affinity, thereby functioning analogously to apo-SoxR, the cluster-free form of the protein. The observation that these constructs failed to upregulate smeVWX and mfsA underscores the essential role of the [2Fe-2S] cluster in the constitutive activation of SoxR variants. The [2Fe-2S] cluster centers of SoxR are located in close proximity to the protein surface, and this solvent-exposed environment is a key determinant of its redox potential (Watanabe, et al. 2008). In E. coli SoxR, the [2Fe-2S] cluster centers are surrounded by an asymmetric charge environment, with positively charged main-chain amides on one side and negatively charged carbonyl oxygens on the other (Watanabe, et al. 2008). This asymmetry drives redox-dependent conformational changes (Watanabe, et al. 2008).

Analysis of the solvent accessibility of the [2Fe-2S] cluster in all SoxR variants showed significant differences in ΔSASA for the SoxRB variant. The increased solvent accessibility suggests that the [2Fe-2S] cluster in these SoxRB and SoxRR49 variants is more susceptible to oxidation by water-soluble superoxide anions. By contrast, no significant ΔSASA changes were observed in the SoxRA, SoxRC, SoxRD, and SoxRE variants, which may reflect the limitations of the modeling approach. Direct measurement of the redox potential of SoxR variants would help interpret their susceptibility to oxidation, as a more negative midpoint redox potential indicates greater ease of electron transfer and thus a higher propensity for oxidation (Hidalgo, et al. 1997, Kobayashi, et al. 2025, Singh, et al. 2013). Alternatively, mutations in these SoxR variants may diminish interactions with unidentified reductase systems, thereby disrupting the balance by disfavoring reduction.

The finding that single amino acid substitution at R49 constitutively activates SoxR is clinically relevant, as a single point mutation can render S. maltophilia more resistant to multiple drugs. R49 is a highly conserved residue located in the DNA-binding domain (Fig. 1A). Based on the crystal structure of E. coli SoxR (Watanabe, et al. 2008), the residue corresponding to R49 in S. maltophilia, R55 in E. coli, stabilizes the [2Fe-2S] cluster centers by forming hydrogen bonds with G123 and C124, which correspond to G117 and C118 in S. maltophilia SoxR, respectively. Therefore, mutations at R49 may influence the redox status of the [2Fe-2S] cluster center. Consistently, data from ΔSASA analysis showed enhanced solvent accessibility of the [2Fe-2S] clusters in both SoxRR49S and SoxRR49H (Fig. 7C).

Studies using E. coli have demonstrated that constitutive activation of SoxR leads to continuous induction of SoxS expression, which in turn activates genes in the SoxRS regulon (Hidalgo, et al. 1997). A single amino acid substitution in the dimerization domain, S95L, results in the constitutive activation of SoxR by shifting the redox potential of the [2Fe-2S] cluster centers, as determined by electron paramagnetic resonance spectroscopy. This renders SoxR hypersensitive to oxidation under normal aerobic growth conditions (Hidalgo, et al. 1997). Other substitutions, such as G143D in the C-terminal region and R20C in the DNA-binding domain, also alter the redox properties of SoxR, leading to its constitutive activation. Mutations in the [2Fe-2S] cluster-binding domain, such as R127L and P131V in E. coli SoxR, have been shown to shift redox potential, increasing sensitivity to oxidation. Similarly, when expressed in E. coli model system, P. aeruginosa SoxR carrying mutations at the equivalent site (L125R or L125A) can constitutively activate soxS expression. By contrast, the L121P substitution in S. maltophilia SoxR, located within the [2Fe-2S] cluster-binding domain, behaves similarly to the wild-type protein and does not result in constitutive activation.

The direct regulation of mfsA expression by SoxR in S. maltophilia has been elucidated (Srijaruskul, et al. 2015). However, whether SoxR directly regulates smeRv and smeVWX expression remains unclear, as no experimental evidence has demonstrated a direct regulatory effect on these genes. Under exposure to the redox-cycling drug menadione, oxidized SoxR upregulates smeRv expression (Wu, et al. 2018). Experimental data further reveal that oxidized SoxR-mediated regulation of smeVWX is partially dependent on SmeRv (Wu, et al. 2018). SmeRv is a member of the LysR-type transcriptional regulator family and can function as both a repressor and an activator (Boonyong, et al. 2025, Wu, et al. 2018). It has been proposed that in the absence of specific ligand molecules, SmeRv acts as a repressor of smeVWX expression. However, in the presence of ligands, which may be produced under conditions such as oxidative stress, SmeRv undergoes a conformational change and activates smeVWX expression. Due to limited studies, the identity of effector molecules for SmeRv remains unknown (Wu, et al. 2018). Notably, insertional mutation affecting the configuration of its ligand- and DNA-binding domains can convert SmeRv into a strong transcriptional activator (Boonyong, et al. 2025). To our knowledge, an additional SmeRv-independent pathway exists under menadione stress, in which SoxR-mediated upregulation of smeVWX requires another as-yet-unidentified regulator that may itself be activated by oxidized SoxR (Wu, et al. 2018) (Fig. 8). We speculate that this alternative pathway is likely the main mechanism of smeVWX induction in our SoxR constitutively activated strains, as under normal growth conditions, no SmeRv-specific ligand is expected to be produced. Consequently, SmeRv should remain in its repressor form. Supporting evidence comes from an S. maltophilia strain that, despite high smeRv expression, failed to activate smeVWX transcription (Boonyong, et al. 2025).

Proposed mechanisms of gene activation mediated by SoxR variants.

Amino acid substitutions resulting from mutations in soxR may alter the redox status of SoxR. This render SoxR hypersensitive to oxidation, allowing it to be constitutively activated by low levels of superoxide anions generated during physiological aerobic respiration. Alternatively, the mutations may disrupt the SoxR reduction system. Oxidized SoxR then upregulates the expression of sodA1, mfsA, and smeRv. Activation of smeVWX may require an additional unidentified transcriptional regulator, as there is currently no evidence that oxidized SoxR directly controls smeVWX expression. In the absence of inducing ligands, SmeRv negatively regulates smeVWX. Overproduction of the SmeVWX and MfsA efflux pumps confers resistance to antimicrobials that serve as substrates for these pumps.

Conclusions

Our study highlights the risk of acquired antimicrobial resistance in clinically relevant S. maltophilia strains through chromosomal mutations in soxR, which may arise under selective pressure from antibiotics, including fluoroquinolones. Compared to E. coli, S. maltophilia exhibits a natural mutation rate approximately 6-fold higher, estimated at 2.4 × 10-7 mutations per cell generation (Chatree, et al. 2023). Supporting evidence comes from an experimental evolution study showing that S. maltophilia can acquire ciprofloxacin resistance via point mutations in soxR (Ochoa-Sanchez, et al. 2024). However, the impact of these mutations on the expression of antimicrobial resistance genes remains unclear. In the current study, we demonstrate that single amino acid substitutions at residue R49, as well as combinations of multiple substitutions, lead to the constitutive activation of SoxR, resulting in the overexpression of genes within its regulon, including those encoding the efflux pumps SmeVWX and MfsA. This overexpression enhances resistance to antibiotics that are substrates of these pumps, including fluoroquinolones (e.g., levofloxacin) and the first-line drug co-trimoxazole. This could further complicate the treatment of S. maltophilia infections, which are already challenging to manage given the limited therapeutic options available. Nevertheless, the clinical relevance of soxR-mediated resistance requires further investigation through comprehensive molecular surveillance of antimicrobial resistance in S. maltophilia.
