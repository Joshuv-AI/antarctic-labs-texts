Case 25: A 37-Year-Old Woman With Fanconi Anemia and a Hepatic Mass
Authors: Min Gon Kang, Kwon Yong Tak, Kyoung Il Min, Ji Won Han
Published: 2026

CASE PRESENTATION

Dr. Min Gon Kang: A 37-year-old woman presented with persistent oral ulcers and oral pain lasting for several months. She had been followed at our hematology clinic for seven years for mild thrombocytopenia and macrocytosis. While her clinical constellation—including short stature (height 146.8 cm), a café-au-lait macule on the abdomen, and a history of four corrective surgeries for left thumb hypoplasia—was highly suggestive of Fanconi anemia (FA),1 she was initially managed under a presumptive diagnosis of chronic immune thrombocytopenic purpura or aplastic anemia (AA) based on her complete blood count result. Despite repeated recommendations for a bone marrow biopsy and genetic evaluation, the patient refused further invasive testing, and her hematological condition remained stable without specific treatment for seven years.

However, the recent development of a neck mass and its subsequent excisional biopsy, which revealed a neurofibroma, served as a catalyst for a more definitive investigation. Prompted by this new finding and the persistent cytopenia, she finally underwent a formal genetic evaluation. Chromosome breakage testing with diepoxybutane demonstrated markedly increased chromosomal fragility (3.8 breaks/cell vs. 0.17 in controls). Subsequent targeted genetic analysis confirmed a diagnosis of FA caused by compound heterozygous mutations in the FANCA gene2: a germline frameshift mutation (c.2730_2731del [p.Trp911AspfsTer31]) and a somatic splice-site variant (c.4168-2A>G; variant allele frequency [VAF] 10.9%), the latter being consistent with somatic mosaicism (Table 1). Following the definitive diagnosis of FA, a comprehensive multidisciplinary evaluation was immediately initiated. This included consultations with pediatric hematologists and formal assessments for other associated bone marrow failure syndromes, such as AA and myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS). During this systemic workup, the patient disclosed a history of chronic, recurrent periodontitis. Given the well-documented risk of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) in the FA population, she was referred for a specialized dental evaluation.

Dental evaluation revealed development of a progressively symptomatic ulcerative lesion on the left anterior floor of the mouth several months prior. An initial biopsy revealed SCC in situ with focal stromal invasion, leading to her referral to our institution for multidisciplinary management. On physical examination, she was alert and hemodynamically stable. Examination of the oral cavity confirmed an ulcerative lesion in the left anterior buccal region, without palpable cervical lymphadenopathy. No hepatosplenomegaly was noted. Initial laboratory findings showed a significant reduction in white blood cell (2.24 × 109/L) and platelet counts (35 × 109/L) along with macrocytic anemia. Other biochemical tests, including liver functions, were relatively preserved with only marginal elevations in alkaline phosphatase and γ-glutamyl transpeptidase (Table 2).

For staging of the oral SCC, contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the neck, chest, and abdomen, as well as positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT), were performed. While the neck and chest CT were unremarkable, the abdominal CT incidentally revealed a 2.8 cm-sized hypodense mass in the left lateral section of the liver. PET-CT showed focal FDG uptake isolated to this hepatic lesion (Fig. 1). Although protein induced by vitamin K absence or antagonist-II (PIVKA-II) and carbohydrate antigen 19-9 levels were within normal limits, serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) was markedly elevated at 164 ng/mL (Table 2).

CLINICAL IMPRESSION

Dr. Min Gon Kang: The patient is a known case of FA with a newly diagnosed SCC of the oral cavity. While FA is associated with a broad spectrum of solid tumors due to defective DNA repair, the incidence of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is particularly high and represents a major clinical concern in these patients.2,3 Although differentiating between a primary hepatic malignancy and metastasis was challenging based solely on the monophasic CT findings, several factors led us to strongly suspect a synchronous primary HCC. First, the oral SCC was at a relatively early stage, making distant liver metastasis less likely. Second, HCC is a well-documented complication in patients with FA. Finally, this clinical suspicion was strongly reinforced by the markedly elevated serum AFP level of 164 ng/mL.

IMAGE PRESENTATION

Dr. Min Gon Kang: Further characterization with gadoxetate disodium (Primovist®)-enhanced liver magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed (Fig. 2). The MRI revealed a 2.8 cm-sized lobulated solid mass in the left lateral hepatic section. On T2-weighted imaging, the lesion showed mildly increased signal intensity relative to the surrounding liver parenchyma. Arterial-phase imaging demonstrated peripheral rim enhancement with mild internal enhancement; the portal venous and delayed phases showed progressive internal enhancement without a definitive washout appearance. Hepatobiliary phase imaging showed hypointensity of the lesion, indicating reduced functional hepatocyte uptake, while diffusion-weighted imaging revealed marked diffusion restriction. Although the clinical context of FA and the markedly elevated AFP strongly suggested HCC, these MRI features—specifically the peripheral rim enhancement and progressive internal enhancement without typical washout—were atypical for classic HCC. These imaging characteristics rather raised the possibility of other primary hepatic malignancies, such as intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma or a combined tumor. Given this discrepancy between the serological findings and the atypical imaging features, histopathologic confirmation was strictly necessitated to establish a definitive diagnosis.

PATHOLOGICAL FINDINGS

Dr. Min Gon Kang: An ultrasound-guided percutaneous core-needle biopsy of the hepatic lesion was performed for definitive histopathologic characterization. Hematoxylin and eosin staining demonstrated a poorly differentiated carcinoma arranged in a predominantly solid sheets and nested growth patterns with a loss of typical trabecular architecture, accompanied by marked nuclear pleomorphism (Fig. 3A).

Histopathological and immunohistochemical findings. (A) Hematoxylin and eosin staining (×100): poorly differentiated carcinoma with nuclear pleomorphism. (B) Alpha-fetoprotein: focally positive. (C) Glypican-3: diffuse positive. (D) Cytokeratin-19: focally positive. (E) MOC-31: positive.

To determine the tumor lineage, a comprehensive immunohistochemical (IHC) panel was performed (Table 3). Hepatocellular differentiation was supported by strong positivity for Arginase-1, glutamine synthetase, and Glypican-3, along with focal expression of AFP (Fig. 3B and C). Simultaneously, the tumor expressed biliary and progenitor markers, including cytokeratin (CK)-7, CK-19 (focal), MOC-31, and polyclonal CEA (focal) (Fig. 3D and E). This co-expression profile is characteristic of a progenitor-phenotype HCC, a distinct subtype hypothesized to originate from bipotent hepatic progenitor cells.4 Importantly, this biphenotypic nature well correlates with and explains the atypical, cholangiocarcinoma-like imaging features observed on the prior MRI.

To further investigate the molecular pathogenesis and clonal relationship between the two tumors, next-generation sequencing (NGS) was performed. The hepatic tumor harbored a truncating FANCA mutation (p.Trp911AspfsTer31; VAF 79.81%)—reflecting the underlying germline defect—and an additional somatic TP53 missense mutation (p.Gly245Ser; VAF 61.11%), the latter acting as a common secondary driver in HCC.5 Other notable findings in the HCC included copy number alterations such as NTRK1 and DDR2 amplifications, stable microsatellite status, and a low tumor mutational burden (2.85 mutations/Mb).

Notably, subsequent NGS analysis of the surgically resected oral cavity SCC specimen revealed a distinct somatic mutational profile. While the oral SCC shared the identical FANCA alteration (VAF 46.7%), it lacked the TP53 mutation. Instead, it exclusively exhibited 11q13.3 amplifications involving CCND1, FGF19, FGF3, and FGF4. These molecular findings conclusively demonstrate that the two tumors did not share a metastatic clonal relationship, but rather emerged as independent primary malignancies driven by the shared germline chromosomal instability.

DIAGNOSIS

Dr. Min Gon Kang: HCC, poorly differentiated, progenitor phenotype.

GENERAL INTRODUCTION OF THE DISEASE MANAGEMENT

Dr. Kyoung Il Min: The management of FA requires a highly tailored, multidisciplinary approach due to the underlying defect in DNA interstrand crosslink (ICL) repair, which confers extreme cellular hypersensitivity to DNA-damaging agents.5 For hematologic complications, such as progressive bone marrow failure or malignant clonal evolution (e.g., MDS or acute myeloid leukemia), allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation remains the only curative therapy. However, to minimize severe regimen-related toxicities and mitigate the long-term risk of secondary malignancies, modified conditioning regimens with reduced-intensity chemotherapy and radiation-sparing approaches are strictly required.5 Conversely, the management of FA-associated solid tumors—most notably SCCs of the head and neck, esophagus, and gynecological tracts—poses a profound clinical challenge.6 Because patients with FA poorly tolerate standard platinum-based chemotherapy or conventional radiotherapy, early detection through rigorous surveillance and radical surgical resection with clear margins remain the absolute mainstay of treatment. When adjuvant therapies are unavoidable, extreme caution with strict dose attenuation and individualized risk-benefit assessments are mandatory.6

Dr. Kwon Yong Tak: Management of HCC follows the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) staging system, which integrates tumor burden, liver function, and patient performance status to guide treatment selection.7 According to the BCLC algorithm, this patient’s single 2.8-cm lesion with a preserved liver function and Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status 0 placed her in the early-stage (BCLC-A) category, for which surgical resection is the recommended first-line option.

Nevertheless, recent guidelines emphasize that the management of early-stage HCC should not be limited to a rigid protocol but rather follow a highly tailored, patient-centered approach. The 2026 BCLC update introduces the CUSE (Complexity, Uncertainty, Subjectivity, Emotion) framework, which allows for alternative options such as transarterial interventions when standard curative treatments are not feasible or do not align with the patient's specific context.7 Similarly, the 2022 Korean Liver Cancer Association-National Cancer Center (KLCA-NCC) guidelines explicitly state that transarterial treatments, such as conventional transarterial chemoembolization, can be actively considered as an alternative therapeutic strategy with curative intent for early-stage HCC patients who face high surgical risks or decline surgery.8

In this case, several patient-specific factors precluded conventional resection. First, FA-associated thrombocytopenia (35 × 109/L) significantly increased the risk of perioperative hemorrhage. Second, her physiologic reserve was limited following recent major oral reconstructive surgery. Finally, given the inherent genomic instability of FA, the high likelihood of future hepatic malignancies makes repeated resections functionally impractical. Therefore, transarterial radioembolization (TARE) was selected as a curative-intent alternative treatment.

Simultaneously, the synchronous oral SCC was managed with curative intent through radical surgical intervention. The procedure included the removal of the malignant tumor in the oral cavity, marginal mandibulectomy, and partial glossectomy, followed by reconstruction with a free anterolateral thigh flap for coverage.

Following these curative-intent treatments, a structured surveillance protocol has been implemented. For the oral SCC, a follow-up MRI is scheduled at 1-month post-resection to evaluate early surgical outcomes and local control. For the HCC, a follow-up MRI is planned at 3 months to allow sufficient time for the peak radioembolic effect of TARE to manifest. Provided no recurrence or residual disease is identified on these initial assessments, imaging intervals will be gradually extended. Given the patient’s underlying FA, this lifelong, multidisciplinary surveillance strategy is mandatory to monitor for potential recurrence or the development of new primary malignancies.

PROGNOSIS

Dr. Min Gon Kang: Following TARE, serial tumor markers showed a favorable response: serum AFP rose transiently to a pre-TARE peak of 202 ng/mL and subsequently declined to 79.8 ng/mL at follow-up, while PIVKA-II decreased from 465 mAU/mL to 15 mAU/mL (Fig. 4). Follow-up dynamic liver CT four weeks post-TARE demonstrated partial intratumoral necrosis, consistent with locoregional tumor response (Fig. 5).

DISCUSSION

Dr. Kyoung Il Min: FA is caused by defective DNA ICL repair, which drives progressive chromosomal instability and a substantially elevated risk of solid tumors, most notably head and neck SCC and HCC.9 As seen in this patient, multiple independent malignancies can arise over time from the same germline background through divergent somatic mutations.10 Somatic reversion can attenuate cytopenia and chromosomal instability, thereby masking the typical presentation of FA.11 Therefore, clinicians should maintain a low threshold for FA chromosome breakage testing in adults with unexplained cytopenia, especially when subtle physical findings such as short stature, thumb anomalies, or café-au-lait spots are present. Once FA is confirmed, structured cancer surveillance is essential. Previous literature and clinical guidelines report that HNSCC are the most frequent solid tumors in FA with HCC also showing notable incidence.12,13 Surveillance should rely on comprehensive clinical examinations of the head, neck, and anogenital regions to detect early SCC, alongside regular hepatic ultrasound and AFP monitoring for HCC. As in this case, multiple primary malignancy is still rare in FA, occur in only 3.1% of cancer-bearing FA patients.9

Dr. Kwon Yong Tak: TARE delivers high-dose, conformal internal radiation selectively to the tumor via the hepatic artery, based on the preferential arterial supply of HCC.14 Pre-procedural mapping angiography confirmed a hypervascular left lateral section tumor. Tc-99m macroaggregated albumin scintigraphy verified an acceptable lung shunt fraction, and Y-90 radioembolization was performed. Recent prospective data strongly support TARE as a curative-intent option for selected early-stage HCC. The LEGACY trial demonstrated an objective response rate of 88.3% (localized mRECIST) and a complete response rate of 84% in patients with solitary HCC ≤ 8 cm treated with Y-90 TARE (Child-Pugh A, ECOG 0–1).15 The RASER study, enrolling patients with mean tumor diameter 2.1 cm, reported 1-year target lesion progression of only 4% and 2-year overall survival of 96%, outcomes comparable to those achieved with thermal ablation and surgical resection.16 Reflecting this accumulating evidence, the 2026 BCLC update explicitly lists TARE (alongside microwave ablation, radiofrequency ablation, and external beam radiation therapy) as an acceptable locoregional modality for selected early-stage patients.

Dr. Ji Won Han: The synchronous occurrence of oral SCC and progenitor-phenotype HCC in this adult FA patient highlights significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. Characteristically, malignancies in FA appear at a much younger age—often in the second or third decade—compared to the sixth or seventh decade typical of sporadic cases.17 This markedly earlier onset suggests that conventional screening protocols are insufficient for the FA population, who require intensive surveillance from a much earlier age.

Unlike typical HCC, which is diagnosed non-invasively via the Liver Imaging Reporting and Data System (LI-RADS) criteria in the context of cirrhosis or chronic viral hepatitis, this case presented in a non-cirrhotic liver with an atypical MRI enhancement pattern. Thus, despite a significantly elevated AFP (164 ng/mL), a liver biopsy was mandatory. This underscores that HCC in FA patients frequently lacks classical imaging features, such as the typical “washout” pattern, necessitating strict histopathologic confirmation.

Pathologically, the tumor was confirmed as a progenitor-phenotype HCC, a subtype recognized for poor differentiation, high AFP production, and aggressive biology including frequent early vascular invasion. In FA, defective DNA ICL repair induces a high structural variant burden, driving this malignant progression. The profound genomic instability promotes the dedifferentiation of hepatocytes into a more aggressive, stemness-related lineage. This biological trajectory explains why FA-associated HCC often exhibits cholangiocarcinoma-like imaging features—such as peripheral enhancement and a lack of classic washout—rather than the radiological patterns of typical sporadic HCC, requiring comprehensive IHC for a definitive diagnosis.

Notably, the HCC harbored a somatic TP53 mutation (p.Gly245Ser) not found in the synchronous oral SCC. This disparity illustrates that a single germline FA defect can drive divergent somatic evolutionary trajectories in different organs. Furthermore, comparative NGS analysis of the synchronous tumors provided compelling molecular evidence of divergent clonal evolution. Both the oral SCC and HCC shared a truncating FANCA mutation (p.Trp911AspfsTer31), reflecting the underlying germline chromosomal instability. However, their subsequent somatic alterations were entirely distinct: the oral SCC selectively harbored 11q13.3 amplifications (including CCND1 and FGFs), whereas the HCC independently acquired a pathogenic TP53 mutation (p.Gly245Ser) and 1q amplifications (NTRK1 and DDR2). This molecular dichotomy elegantly illustrates how a singular germline DNA repair defect can drive completely divergent somatic evolutionary trajectories depending on the organ microenvironment.

For this BCLC stage A HCC, an individualized therapeutic approach was required. Surgical resection was contraindicated due to underlying thrombocytopenia and the patient’s immediate post-operative status following oral SCC resection. Furthermore, conventional TACE was avoided, as DNA-crosslinking chemotherapeutic agents can induce fatal bone marrow failure in FA patients. Despite the known radiosensitivity of FA, super-selective Y-90 TARE was performed to deliver localized radiation while minimizing systemic toxicity with the curative intent.
