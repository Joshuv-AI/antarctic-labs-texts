Spatial concordance patterns of breast and thyroid cancer based on burden inequalities and related risk factors analyses
Authors: Li Yuan Wei, Li Da Liao, Kai Wang, Hui Huang, Wen Sheng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBoth breast cancer (BC) and thyroid cancer (TC) are the most common malignancies, particularly for female patients. A well-established bidirectional association exists between them. However, the distribution of spatial concordance patterns remains incompletely elucidated. This study aims to provide a comprehensive assessment of the global burden and spatial concordance patterns of BC and TC, and identify the population-level exposure factors associated with the burden of these two cancers.MethodsBy utilizing data from the Global Burden of Disease Study and GLOBOCAN 2022, we examined the incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) rates across 204 countries and territories from 1990 to 2022. They were then classified into three distinct spatial concordance patterns according to different levels of incidence and mortality. An XGBoost prediction model was used to evaluate the association between country-level exposure profiles and BC-related DALYs, and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values were used to quantify the contribution of each feature.ResultsGlobally, from 1990 to 2022, the age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) of TC increased with an estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) of 1.25%, while BC ASIR increased with an EAPC of 0.38%. Spatial analysis identified 3 distinct spatial concordance patterns: consistent areas, BC-dominant areas and TC-dominant areas. Machine learning identified low physical activity and a diet high in red meat as the strongest ecological predictors of BC-related DALYs, while high body-mass index was a major predictor for TC-related DALYs globally.ConclusionsThe comorbidity of BC and TC is a global health issue, with significant demographic and regional diversity. The identified spatial concordance patterns and population-level associated exposure risk factors underscore the necessity for tailored public health policies.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Using Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2022 and GLOBOCAN 2022 data from 204 countries and territories, we found that breast cancer (BC) and thyroid cancer (TC) incidence has risen globally since 1990 and clusters into three spatial concordance patterns, with high-Socio-demographic Index (SDI) regions having high incidence but relatively low disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) and many middle- and low-SDI regions bearing a disproportionate and growing disability burden.

What is known and what is new?

• BC and TC are common in women, share hormonal, metabolic and lifestyle risk factors, and show an excess bidirectional risk of second primaries, but their global spatial concordance patterns and the demographic and epidemiologic drivers of their burden have not been systematically quantified.

• This study provides the integrated global mapping of breast-thyroid cancer spatial concordance, decomposes trends in incidence, mortality and DALYs into population growth, ageing and epidemiologic change, and uses an XGBoost-SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) framework to identify low physical activity and high red meat intake as leading population-level exposure factors associated with BC DALYs and high body-mass index as the ecological predictors for TC DALYs at the national level.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Our findings support coordinated prevention, screening and survivorship strategies for BC and TC, with region-specific priorities: balanced control in consistent areas, prioritizing BC screening and treatment in BC-dominant regions, strengthening TC control and obesity-focused lifestyle interventions in TC-dominant regions, and risk-stratified long-term follow-up for patients at increased risk of second primary BC and TC.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) and thyroid cancer (TC) are the second and seventh diagnosed cancers all over the world, responsible for almost 2.3 million and 0.8 million new cases in 2022 (11.6% and 4.1% of all cancers globally), respectively (1). Despite the declined death rate owing to early detection and advancement of treatment, the incidence of BC remains an upward trend, growing by 1% annually from 2012 to 2021. In 2026, about 324,580 new cases will be diagnosed in the United States and 42,670 patients will die from BC (2). The incidence varies from populations with differing racial, ethnic, and socioeconomic backgrounds. Similarly, because of the widespread use of high-resolution thyroid ultrasound and fine-needle aspiration biopsies, the incidence of TC has been reported to be rising by 313% in the past four decades. Approximately 45,240 new cases will be diagnosed in 2026 in the US and 2,320 deaths from TC are projected (2). These conditions pose serious health risks and create a substantial financial healthcare burden.

Substantial evidence confirms a significantly elevated, bi-directional risk of co-occurrence between BC and TC, with a 1.32-fold increased risk of secondary BC in TC patients and a 1.55-fold increased risk of secondary TC in BC patients (3). Considering the secretory function of breast and thyroid glands regulated by the hypothalamic-pituitary axis, BC and TC may share some common oncogenic risk factors. The stimulation of hormonal factors, obesity, and radiation exposure have been hypothesized as a source of the high rates of co-occurrence of these cancers. Increased level of estrogen, progesterone, and androgen receptors has been shown to promote thyroid carcinogenesis, and similarly, higher serum thyroid stimulating hormone level has been shown to facilitate the pro-tumorigenic progress of BC (3-5). Obesity has been noted as another risk factor for both BC and TC (6-8).

Although most previous studies have focused either on individual-level second primary cancer risk between BC and TC or on temporal trends of a single malignancy within specific countries or regions, an integrated global analysis jointly examining long-term burden trends, spatial concordance patterns, demographic decomposition, and population-level exposure profiles for both cancers is still lacking. The present study was designed to address this gap.

Because globally harmonized individual-level datasets capturing synchronous or metachronous dual primary BC and TC across countries are currently unavailable, in this study we used a population-level spatial proxy based on national burden metrics. We explored the cancer burden of BC and TC worldwide based on the latest updates of the GLOBOCAN 2022 project and the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study in 2022, and focused on population-level spatial concordance in incidence and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) burden rather than individual-level synchronous or metachronous co-occurrence. By integrating GBD and GLOBOCAN 2022 data, we sought to characterize global burden patterns of BC and TC from a spatial perspective and identified the potential key country-level risk factors which can provide the guidance for cancer-control planning, tailored prevention, and control strategies, thereby alleviating the global disease burden and incidence of TC and BC. We present this article in accordance with the GATHER reporting checklist (available at https://gs.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/gs-2026-1-0047/rc).

Methods

Data sources

In this study, we presented a comprehensive overview of BC and TC burden worldwide and explored the spatial distribution and the concordance patterns based on the GBD 2022 database and the latest GLOBOCAN estimates. The incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs, along with their respective age-standardized rates per 100,000 population (ASR) of BC and TC, stratified by sex, age, and region, across the 204 countries and territories were analyzed from GBD estimates (9). The 95% uncertainty intervals (95% UIs) were calculated for the estimates to account for potential biases. These data were applied to analyze the long-term trends of these two malignancies burden from 1990 to 2022. The related data used in this study were obtained from the GBD query tool (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/).

GLOBOCAN database offered 36 cancer estimates, including the age-standardized incidence and mortality rates, in 185 countries for the year 2022 (1). To further validate the global distribution of the latest incidence rates of BC and TC, data from GLOBOCAN 2022 were used and compared with them from the GBD dataset. The number of new cases of BC and TC extracted from it were identified by the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) code: Breast (C50) and Thyroid (C73). The complete data used in this project could be downloaded from Cancer Today (gco.iarc.who.int/today) on the Global Cancer Observatory. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Socio-demographic Index (SDI)

The 204 countries and territories were stratified into 5 SDI quintiles (high SDI, high-middle SDI, middle SDI, low-middle SDI, and low SDI), a composite index which was calculated as the geometric mean value of the fertility rate, average years of schooling, and per capita income (10). It was used to analyze the relationship of the region socioeconomic development level with the age-standardized rates of incidence, mortalities, and DALYs.

Decomposition analysis

Decomposition analysis developed in demographic research by Das Gupta (11) was applied to describe the relative contributions of population growth, aging, and epidemiological change on incidence, mortality, and DALYs of BC and TC between 1990 and 2021. This approach decomposed the total change in disease burden into components uniquely attributable to each factor. The aging effect referred to the burden change deriving from the shifting age structure of the population. The population growth effect captured the change attributable to the increase of the total number of individuals. And the epidemiological change effect meant the component resulting from the temporal changes in age-specific incidence, prevalence, or DALY rates (12).

Definition and regional division of spatial concordance patterns of BC and TC

To delineate the spatial distribution characteristics of the two malignancies, we categorized the age-standardized incidence and DALYs rates of each malignancy into four levels: low (<25th percentile), lower-middle (25th–50th percentile), upper-middle (50th–75th percentile), and high (>75th percentile). Countries or territories were then classified into three distinct spatial concordance patterns according to these categorizations. A region was defined as “consistent” if both BC and TC rates fell within the same quartile level. If BC rate level was higher than that of TC, the region was classified as “BC-dominant” area; conversely, it was designated as “TC-dominant” area. Consequently, the global map was partitioned into three exclusive regions (BC-dominant area, TC-dominant area, and consistent area), each representing a unique pattern of population-level spatial concordance between the two malignancies. Moreover, the spatial concordance in our study refers to the relative alignment of BC and TC burden levels within the same country or territory, based on quartile categories of age-standardized incidence rates or DALY rates, rather than the individual-level co-occurrence of two primary malignancies.

Filter and estimation of DALY-related risk factors

To estimate the exposure levels associated with BC and TC, 88 risk factors in 204 countries and territories and 811 subnational locations were obtained from GBD database. Methodologically, relative risks (RRs) were derived from meta-analyses using the Burden of Proof approach. Population exposures were estimated with Bayesian models, and attributable burdens [DALYs, deaths, years lived with disability (YLDs), and years of life lost (YLLs)] were computed based on population attributable fractions (PAFs), which integrate relative risks (RRs), summary exposure values (SEVs), and theoretical minimum risk exposure levels (TMRELs) (13). In our study, to investigate the impact of risk factors associated with DALYs, deaths, YLDs, and YLLs of BC and TC, we retrieved all relevant risk factors data from the GBD database. The association between these risk factors and BC-related DALYs was evaluated through XGBoost prediction model by using the xgboost and caret packages in R platform. To interpret the model and quantify the contribution of each feature, we computed SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values and visualized them by using the SHAPforxgboost R package. It should be noted that, because the risk factor analysis was based on country-level aggregated estimates, the results of machine learning were interpreted as ecological associations with country variation in burden rather than as individual-level causal effects.

Statistical analysis

The age-standardized incidence rates of BC and TC per 100,000 population were applied to calculate the estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) to quantify the temporal incidence trends from 1990 to 2022. Considering the potential distortion in cancer incidence estimates caused by the COVID-19 pandemic, data for the year 2020 were excluded from the trend analysis. Therefore, the EAPC was estimated based on the remaining time series, with the most recent data updated to 2022.

The EAPC was calculated by using a log-linear regression model fitted to the natural logarithm of age-standardized rates across calendar years. Specifically, the regression line y = α + βx + ɛ (where y = ln(ASR), and x = calendar year) was used to fit the natural logarithm and EAPC value was calculated as 100 × (exp(β) − 1) with 95% confidence interval (CI) obtained from the linear regression model (14). An increasing trend in the ASR was defined as an EAPC with both its value and the lower limit of its 95% confidence interval (CI) greater than zero. Conversely, a decreasing trend was defined when both them were less than zero (15).

To ensure consistency with the most recent GLOBOCAN 2022 estimates used for cross-database comparison in this study, we updated the GBD-based temporal analysis to 2022 but did not extend it to 2023. This approach allowed us to maintain a harmonized analytical time frame across the major data sources used in the manuscript. All of the analyses were conducted by using R software, version 4.4.1.

Results

Incidence rate of BC and TC from 1990 to 2022

Globally, both TC and BC exhibited a consistent increase in incidence from 1990 to 2022. The BC incidence increased from 935.37 thousand (95% UI: 844.31–1,046.89) to 2,262.34 thousand (95% UI: 1,998.11–2,563.95), and the ASR increased from 22.63 to 25.61 per 100,000. Similarly, the number of incident cases of TC rose from 101.77 thousand (95% UI: 78.95–127.90) in 1990 to 293.08 thousand (95% UI: 217.27–371.49) in 2022, with the ASR increasing from 2.3 to 3.38 per 100,000. The EAPC further confirmed this upward trend, with BC showing an EAPC of 0.19% (95% UI: 0.13–0.26%) and TC with an EAPC of 1.13% (95% UI: 1.08–1.18%).

Notably, both BC and TC demonstrated sex disparities. The BC incidence was overwhelmingly higher in females, with ASRs of 42.37 and 48.55 per 100,000 in females versus only 0.60 and 1.02 in males in 1990 and 2022, respectively. Similarly, for TC, the incidence in females was higher than it in males, with female ASRs of 3.21 and 4.70 per 100,000 in 1990 and 2022, respectively, compared to male ASRs of 1.34 and 2.06. These patterns underscore a pronounced female predominance in both malignancies over the three-decade period (Tables 1,2).

The magnitude of the increase varied with the different SDI levels. For BC, the most pronounced annual increase occurred in low-middle SDI regions [EAPC: 2.76% (95% UI: 2.53–2.99%)], followed by low SDI regions [EAPC: 2.44% (95% UI: 2.09–2.78%)]. For TC, almost all countries showed an increase from 1990 to 2022, except Central Europe [EAPC: −0.97% (95% UI: −1.15% to −0.80%)]. The highest increase rate was observed in Andean Latin America [EAPC: 3.24% (95% UI: 2.95–3.53%)] followed by Australasia [EAPC: 2.83% (95% UI: 2.44–3.21%)] and Southeast Asia [EAPC: 2.68% (95% UI: 2.64–2.72%)].

At the sex level, the ASIR of BC for females showed a general increasing trend over the three decades except the high SDI regions, while the trends were various for males, although the ASIR remained lower throughout the period. In the high-middle and middle regions, the ASIR exhibited a striking increase between 2000 to 2010, and then the increase slowed down. In the low-middle regions, it showed a stable increasing trend. In contrast, the age-standardized DALYs rate (ASDR) and age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) for females exhibited a more variable trajectory, with notable declines in high-SDI areas but persistent or even rising trends in the middle, low, and low-middle SDI regions. The male-ASDR and male-ASMR in low SDI regions were significantly higher than other regions with a slight increase from 2010 to 2020. The rates in high SDI regions remained relatively low and showed a stable decline trend from 2000 to 2020.

The ASIR of TC for both males and females demonstrated a substantial and consistent increase from 1990 to 2010, especially in the high SDI regions. Over the past decade, the growth rate has slowed down slightly globally, except the high SDI regions, where the ASIR pronouncedly declined. Discussion

Both BC and TC are frequently diagnosed malignancies, especially among women globally. In this study, we estimated the epidemiological burden from 1991 to 2022 of BC and TC worldwide based on age, gender, region, and socioeconomic status by leveraging the GBD and GLOBOCAN estimates, and delineated the intricate global landscape.

Our findings confirmed the consistent upward trends of the global incidence of both malignancies over the past 30 years, although the specific growth patterns varied substantially across different socio-demographic and racial regions. For BC, the most rapid increase in incidence and mortality was observed in low-middle and middle SDI regions, represented by Northern Africa, a trend strongly linked to unavailability of tests, ignorance of BC, rising obesity, shortage of trained medical personnel, and inaccessibility of treatment and detection in these areas (16,17). Furthermore, the insufficient capacity of healthcare system in dealing with the increased disease burden also leads to a rising tendency of death (18). In contrast, high-SDI regions with high ASIR in 2022 have experienced a slight decline in incidence and significant decrease in mortality. Although the disparities of the incidence among these regions are multifactorial, the continued improvement majorly stems from equities in access to early diagnosis and timely medical care (18).

On the other hand, the pattern for TC incidence is more complicated. The ASIR is significantly higher in the high-SDI regions (e.g., the United States) with a sharp reduction in mortality and DALYs, especially in female patients, which are similar to the patterns of BC. The 5-year survival rate of TC is 98.1% (19). The high incidence rate but combined with a good prognosis is highly suggestive of the pervasive influence of over-detection of small and indolent asymptomatic nodules, largely due to the widespread application of high-resolution neck ultrasonography screening and fine-needle aspiration biopsies (20). However, the rising trend of incidence peaked at 2010 and then declined, reflecting a paradigm shift in clinical practice. Updated guidelines advocating active surveillance for low-risk thyroid nodules are beginning to recommend against screening for TC in asymptomatic adults to reduce the overdiagnosis and overtreatment (19). Instead, consistent increases of the incidence rates and sharp rise in mortality rates, particularly in male patients, were observed in the in middle and low-middle SDI regions but this trend among female patients was less obvious. This situation may stem from unequal access to get healthcare and/or environmental factors like ionizing radiation or iodine deficiency (21). Moreover, it also suggests the existence of overdiagnosis of TC for female patients in the lower-income regions (22). For male patients, the future interventions and medical care strategies of TC must be designed to bridge these gaps and secure improved TC outcomes for all, regardless of the geographic location or socio-economic status.

Our findings also highlight marked global health inequities in the burden of BC and TC. High-SDI regions tended to show high incidence but relatively lower DALYs burden, which likely reflects greater diagnostic capacity, earlier detection, broader treatment availability, and better survivorship care. In contrast, many middle- and low-SDI regions exhibited disproportionate DALYs burdens despite only moderate incidence levels, suggesting persistent deficiencies in early diagnosis, treatment access, and long-term cancer management. These disparities should not be viewed merely as differences in cancer burden metrics. Rather, they reflect deeper inequalities in health-system capacity, including unequal access to diagnostic imaging, pathology confirmation, specialist treatment, survivorship care, and long-term follow-up. For TC in particular, in high-SDI regions, the discrepancy between incidence and DALYs burden may partly represent the unintended consequence of technology-intensive diagnostic surveillance and overdiagnosis (20,23). In contrast, in many middle- or lower-SDI settings, relatively modest incidence but high DALYs burden more likely reflects underdiagnosis, delayed presentation, undertreatment, and weaker continuity of care (24,25). These suggest that cancer control should focus not only on detecting more cases, but also on improving the appropriateness of diagnosis and management.

Our decomposition analysis offered an understanding of the drivers behind the rising case numbers. Population growth emerged as the predominant force amplifying the absolute number of new cases for both BC and TC worldwide, underscoring the immense pressure exerted by global population expansion on cancer burden. Notably, epidemiological changes constituted the largest share of the increase for both cancers in the middle-SDI regions, indicating a similar increase pattern between these two cancers among the middle-SDI regions. On the other hand, the consistent negative contribution of epidemiological changes to mortality and DALYs of both BC and TC in the high and high-middle SDI regions underscores a resounding success in cancer control over the study period, which is largely due to the synergistic advancements of the cancer medical care and management.

Besides, we also found an opposite impact of population aging on BC mortality versus DALYs. While the aging factor contributed positively to the death count, reflecting the increased absolute risk from a growing elderly population, its negative contribution to DALYs may reveal the success of medical management, such as earlier detection and revolutionary treatment modalities, which have substantially minimized long-term disability, prolonged survival, improved quality of life, reduced years of life lost and thereby offset the burden attributable to demographic aging (26,27).

Notably, clinical observations indicate that the co-occurrence of BC and TC, either synchronous or metachronous, is greater than would be expected by chance alone. A bidirectional association between BC and TC is increasingly recognized (3,28,29). Patients with a history of TC exhibit an elevated risk of developing subsequent BC (OR =1.32, 95% CI: 1.23–1.42), and BC survivors also face a higher likelihood of being diagnosed with thyroid cancer later in life (OR =1.55, 95% CI: 1.44–1.67), especially micropapillary carcinomas at the early stage without lymph node metastasis (3,30,31). In a retrospective case-controlled study, approximately 0.49% of BC patients and 0.91% of the thyroid patients can develop to TC and BC, respectively (32). The standard incidence ratio (SIR) of TC diagnosed after BC has been reported to be 4.48 (95% CI: 3.95–5.44) in a Chinese-female cohort (30). Similarly, the SIR was 2.45 (95% CI: 1.83–2.96) among the TC patients with subsequent BC (33). This reciprocal association indicates a potential shared pathogenic mechanism between them. Although the association between TC and BC has been discussed over the last decades, the precise biological or environmental factors driving this connection remain unclear. Stefanos et al. have identified the history of abortion and multiparity as the risk factors for the synchronous or metachronous co-occurrence of BC and TC, probably due to the hormonal changes in women. However, the pathophysiological mechanism is still indefinite (34).

The observed positive correlation between breast and thyroid carcinomas points to a potential shared heritable predisposition in certain instances. Several biological mechanisms may partly explain the observed epidemiological linkage between BC and TC reported in previous individual-level studies. These include hormonal crosstalk involving estrogen- and thyroid-related signaling pathways, shared endocrine susceptibility, and hereditary predisposition (4). The expression of estrogen receptors and progesterone receptors has been proven to be significantly higher in BC patients with metachronous or synchronous TC. In contrast, elevated serum levels of thyroid globulin antibody (TGAb) and thyroid peroxidase antibody (TPOAb) in thyroid cancer patients are associated with an increased risk of subsequent primary BC (32). The change of thyroid hormones causing hypo- or hyperthyroidism has also been reported to be related to the incidence of BC, but with conflicting results. In a large nationwide cohort study, researchers found hypothyroidism was associated with a decreased risk of BC, but no relationship between hyperthyroidism and the incidence of BC was found (35). Besides, the observed positive correlation between breast and thyroid carcinomas also points to a potential shared heritable predisposition in certain instances. For example, PTEN mutations increase the risk of Cowden syndrome in women, a condition characterized by both thyroid malignancies and breast tumors (36). In addition, mutations in the cell cycle checkpoint kinase 2 (CHEK2) tumor suppressor gene have also been reported to be related with double primary malignances of BC and TC (37). Furthermore, altered sodium-iodide symporter (NIS)-related metabolism has also been discussed in the literature as a potential shared feature of breast and thyroid tissues (38). Higher expression of NIS is linked to activation of FOXA1, suggesting the chemo- and radiotherapy resistance in triple-negative BC (39,40).

From the epidemiological perspective, the enhanced follow-up surveillance for BC can lead to the earlier detection of TC, potentially due to increased screening intensity (3,31,41). Interestingly, after the exclusion of cases that were incidentally detected on follow-up imaging for BC, the SIR for secondary TC attenuated from 2.18 to 1.73 (33).

In order to reveal the connection and delineate the spatial relationship between BC and TC, we further analyzed the spatial concordance patterns between them. First, for both of the two cancers, we observed a distinct dissociation between the global distributions of incidence and DALYs rates. As mentioned before, regions with high incidence rates, such as North America, Western Europe, and Australasia, consistently demonstrated relative lower DALYs rates, reflecting the success of robust healthcare systems in these high-income countries due to the early diagnosis, effective treatment methods, and well-developed long-term management. Conversely, many low and middle SDI regions (e.g., South Asia, Southern Africa, and South America) suffered from high DALYs rates despite only intermediate or even low incidence rates, sharply highlighting significant deficiencies in access to timely diagnosis, standard-of-care treatment, and palliative care, leading to shorter survival and poorer quality of life.

Next, we classified 204 countries and territories into three different spatial concordance areas. The geographical disparities highlighted in these distributions provide crucial insights for developing targeted prevention and early detection strategies tailored to the specific cancer profile of each region. The consistent areas of incidence comprised countries such as the United States, Russia, mainland China, Australia, and parts of Africa. For DALYs, they were represented by the United States, Canada, Australia. These areas encompassing over one-third of countries globally, indicate an equal cancer incidence burden between BC and TC. This calls for balanced cancer control strategies, with resource allocation and policy attention directed equitably towards both malignancies.

The BC-dominant areas primarily distributed across parts of Latin America and Canada, face a disproportionately higher incidence burden from BC. Public health efforts should prioritize strengthening screening and early diagnosis of BC. Whereas, regions like Russia and parts of South Africa countries confront with a higher DALYs burden, indicating a critical need to outline strategies for optimizing the treatment and enhance personalized medical approaches for BC.

TC-dominant areas for both incidence and DALYs exhibited a similar geographic distribution, with dense clustering observed in Central Asia, South Asia, and parts of North Africa, represented by China, Japan, Korea, and Iran. The elevated burden of both incidence and DALYs in these regions suggests that, in addition to variable diagnostic practices, region-specific risk factors such as iodine status, ionizing radiation, and environmental pollutants are also likely to contribute to the high TC burden in these regions (21). Furthermore, the concentration of TC-dominant countries in East Asia (Japan and Korea) aligns with known epidemiological features. For these nations, enhancing evidence-based TC screening and standardizing the management of thyroid nodules to minimize over-treatment should be high priorities (42).

At the country level, our machine learning model identified low physical activity as the most potent predictor associated with BC-related DALYs, in accordance with extensive previous studies establishing physical inactivity as an independent risk factor for BC progress and effect of adjuvant therapy (43-45). It is noteworthy that while its predictive weight was highest, its Population Attributable Fraction (PAF) was relatively modest (2.0% globally), indicating an effect for particular populations. Public health initiatives aimed at reducing population-wide exposure to low physical activity may still yield substantial cumulative benefits in reducing the overall DALYs of BC. In contrast, a diet high in red meat with higher predictive value in SHAP model carried the largest PAF for DALYs of BC globally (11.9%), with its impact most pronounced in high SDI regions (13.6%) and Southern Sub-Saharan Africa (13.5%). This provides a powerful public health perspective for dietary interventions (e.g., promoting Mediterranean-style diets) to support long-term dietary changes for BC patients (46,47). Furthermore, the positive correlation between the attributable burden of high-risk factors (such as high alcohol use and high BMI) and SDI levels clearly also illustrates a correlation between high burden of BC and unhealthy dietary patterns including added sugars, refined grains, and processed foods (48). Similarly, for TC, high BMI is confirmed as a significant risk factor at the national level, holding a positive correlation with SDI levels. Previous studies have hypothesized that obesity may promote thyroid carcinogenesis through mechanisms such as insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, and estrogen production in adipose tissue (49-51). Consistent with these individual-level studies, high level of BMI leading to obesity is associated with both BC and TC (52). Different from BC, the attribution of high BMI is highest in the North Africa and Middle East regions, suggesting a priority in regulating the dietary patterns, enhancing physical exercise to maintain a healthy level of BMI to control TC among these regions.

Limitations

The key strengths of our study are the combined analyses of representative and large-scale global data from GBD and GLOBOCAN databases to evaluate the cancer burden and spatial concordance patterns of BC and TC. We also conducted machine learning algorithms to explore risk factors related to the cancer burden to provide a novel worldwide perspective for understanding the synergistic epidemic of these two cancers. However, there are several limitations in our study which cannot be ignored. First, the GBD and GLOBOCAN data rely on cancer registry reports from individual countries, and variations in data quality, coverage, and completeness across regions cause the discrepancy on incidence of these two cancers. Second, we cannot analyze the incidence-related factors of BC and TC due to the unavailability of data from GBD dataset. And the data about risk factors of TC only contain the high BMI. Several potentially important risk factors such as detailed history of ionizing radiation exposure, quantity of diet iodine intake, and specific genetic backgrounds are deficient, which limit the models’ ability to fully explain the cancer burden and find the common risk factors of two cancers. Third, because the risk-factor analysis was based on country-level aggregated data, the identified associations should not be interpreted as individual-level causal effects, and ecological fallacy cannot be excluded.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study presented a thorough and comprehensive synopsis of the global burden of BC and TC. Our findings established that these two malignancies share important similarities in their global distribution of burden, while also demonstrating substantial regional heterogeneity in incidence, disability burden, and associated exposure profiles. The alignment of national-level prevention strategies and individual-level interventions for both BC and TC should be emphasized, especially in the consistent regions. Efforts should be directed towards developing risk-stratified, individualized cancer screening guidelines and enhancing long-term follow-up surveillance for BC and TC as second primary malignancies. In the high-SDI regions, priorities should include optimizing screening strategies, minimizing overdiagnosis and overtreatment, particularly for TC, and improving risk-stratified survivorship care. In the middle- and low-SDI regions, greater emphasis should be placed on strengthening diagnostic infrastructure, pathology capacity, treatment accessibility, and long-term cancer management. In the BC- or TC-dominant regions, the public health priorities should be tailored to the dominant cancer type in each region. In BC-dominant regions, BC screening and treatment pathways should be reinforced, whereas in TC-dominant regions, obesity prevention, rational thyroid nodule management, and evidence-based diagnostic strategies may be particularly important. By adopting such tailored, evidence-based, and comprehensive prevention and control strategies, the global community can effectively mitigate the dual challenge posed by BC and TC and ultimately alleviate their worldwide burden.
