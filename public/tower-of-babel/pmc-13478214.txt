Mechanism of Artemisia annua active component quercetin in treating psoriasis by regulating ferroptosis
Authors: Ke Xu, Fangrong Liu, Min Li, Yuntong Wang, Fengming Hu, Jian Gong, Xiongfei Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to investigate the mechanism by which active components of Artemisia annua modulate ferroptosis in the treatment of psoriasis vulgaris through an integrated approach combining network pharmacology, molecular docking, and in vivo validation. Therapeutic targets of Artemisia annua active components and psoriasis vulgaris-related genes were retrieved from the TCMSP and GeneCards databases, respectively. Common targets were identified using Venn analysis. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed using Cytoscape, with core targets extracted via the CytoHubba plugin. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed. Ferroptosis-related genes were acquired from the FerrDb database. Molecular docking assessed the binding affinity between key active components and critical targets. In vivo experiments using an imiquimod (IMQ)-induced psoriasis-like mouse model were conducted to validate the network pharmacology predictions. Network pharmacology identified 22 active components of Artemisia annua, 202 potential targets, and 1,312 psoriasis-related genes, yielding 96 common targets. Further analysis revealed 485 ferroptosis-related genes, with 26 overlapping targets associated with 17 active components. PPI network analysis indicated TP53(P53), IL6, and IL1B as core targets. Quercetin, kaempferol, and luteolin were identified as the primary active components. Molecular docking demonstrated strong binding affinity between quercetin and TP53(P53). In vivo experiments showed that quercetin significantly alleviated psoriatic-like skin lesions, increased epidermal glutathione (GSH) levels, reduced Fe²⁺ accumulation and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and improved mitochondrial morphology. Furthermore, quercetin downregulated the protein and mRNA expression of P53, SAT1, and ALOX15. This study demonstrates that quercetin is the major active component of Artemisia annua responsible for mediating epidermal ferroptosis in psoriasis treatment. Experimental results indicate that quercetin alleviates the inflammatory response in psoriasis by inhibiting epidermal ferroptosis, a process that may be associated with the regulation of the P53/SAT1/ALOX15 signaling pathway.Keywords: Artemisia annua, Network pharmacology, Psoriasis, Ferroptosis, Quercetin, P53/SAT1/ALOX15 pathwaySubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Drug discovery

Introduction

Psoriasis is an immune-mediated chronic inflammatory skin disease affecting approximately 2–3% of the global population1,2. In China, its prevalence has risen steadily, with the total number of cases increasing by 90.97% from 1990 to 20213. Characterized by its chronic and relapsing nature, psoriasis significantly impairs patients’ quality of life and mental health4,5. The prolonged disease course and associated costs increase economic burdens on patients and society6,7. Although biological agents have improved clinical outcomes, the risk of relapse and potential adverse events underscore the need for safer, more effective treatments8,9.

Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death marked by lipid peroxidation, ROS accumulation, and iron overload, and is involved in multiple human diseases10,11. Growing evidence links ferroptosis to psoriasis pathogenesis, supported by observed iron dysregulation in patients12,13 and elevated lipid ROS and Fe²⁺in lesions, with ferroptosis activation confirmed in both human and mouse studies14,15. Targeting ferroptosis thus represents a promising therapeutic strategy.

Artemisia annua, a traditional Chinese herb, contains bioactive compounds such as artemisinin and dihydroartemisinin, which exhibit anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects16–18. While some components alleviate psoriasis in models19–22, their influence on ferroptosis in this disease remains unclear.

Network pharmacology integrates systems biology and computational tools to decipher complex mechanisms of traditional medicines23–25. Here, we combined network pharmacology, molecular docking, and in vivo validation to systematically investigate whether Artemisia annua active components treat psoriasis via ferroptosis regulation.

Materials and methods

Screening of active components and target prediction

Active components of Artemisia annua were screened from the TCMSP database (https://tcmsp-e.com/tcmsp.php) based on oral bioavailability (OB) ≥ 30% and drug-likeness (DL) ≥ 0.1826,27.The TCMSP we used is Version 2.3, and the access date is July 15, 2023.

Finally, to standardize protein target information, the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/) was used to uniformly convert protein databases into gene symbols28.

Screening of psoriasis targets

Psoriasis vulgaris-related targets were retrieved from GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/), OMIM (https://omim.org/), and DisGeNet (https://www.disgenet.org/) using “psoriasis vulgaris” as the keyword29–31. For the GeneCards database, a strict criterion of composite score ≥ 60 was adopted to screen highly relevant core targets and exclude non-specific targets. All targets were uniformly standardized to the official NCBI gene names and merged to remove duplicates using Venn tools.

Construction of protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks

A Venn diagram of Artemisia annua-psoriasis common targets was generated using a bioinformatics platform (http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/static/others/jvenn/). Overlapping targets were imported into the STRING database (https://string-db.org) with “Homo sapiens” as the species and a minimum interaction score of 0.400 to construct a PPI network32,33.

GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses

GO functional annotation and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses of the common targets were performed using the DAVID platform(www.kegg.jp/kegg/kegg1.html)34,35. The top 10 enriched terms in BP, CC, and MF categories of GO, and the top 10 KEGG pathways were visualized.

Screening of ferroptosis-related genes and correlation analysis

Ferroptosis-related genes were retrieved from the FerrDb database (http://www.zhounan.org/ferrdb/)36. The FerrDb (a comprehensive database for ferroptosis research) we used is Version 2.0, and the access date is August 7, 2023. Venn analysis identified overlapping targets among Artemisia annua components, psoriasis, and ferroptosis. A PPI network was constructed, and a “disease-ferroptosis-drug-active component-target” network was visualised using Cytoscape 3.7.2.

Molecular docking

Core targets (TP53, IL6, IL1B) and the top active components (quercetin, kaempferol, luteolin) were selected as receptors and ligands, respectively. Protein structures (PDB format) were obtained from the RCSB PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org). Molecular docking simulations were performed using AutoDockTools-1.5.6 with the Lamarckian genetic algorithm (LGA) to calculate binding free energy. Results were visualized using PyMOL.

Drugs and reagents

Quercetin (CAS: 117-39-5) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Imiquimod was sourced from Sichuan Mingxin Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. (Sichuan, China). Assay kits for GSH, Fe²⁺, and ROS were obtained from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China), Wuhan Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China), and Jiangsu KGI Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Liyang, China), respectively. P53, ALOX15, and SAT1 antibodies were obtained from Abcam (Cambridge, UK) and Wuhan Aibotec Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) and Wuhan Pro Tech Biological Research Center Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China), respectively.

In vivo experiments

Animals and modeling

6–8 week old, 18–22 g specific pathogen free (SPF) male BALB/c mice (provided by Sibeifu (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) were randomly divided into 6 groups (n = 8): blank control group, model group, methotrexate (MTX, 1 mg/kg) group, and low, medium, and high-dose quercetin groups (QR-L, QR-M, QR-H; 30, 60, 120 mg/kg)37. The back of mice was depilated by 2 × 3 cm, and the last five groups were induced with psoriasis like dermatitis by local application of 5% imiquimod (IMQ) cream on the back38. The treatment was administered orally by gavage once a day for 7 days. On the 8th day, mice were euthanized by excessive inhalation of 2.7% isoflurane and skin tissue was collected. During the experiment, the status of the mice was closely observed, and the benevolent endpoint was set to a weight loss of over 20% in the mice. Euthanasia was performed by inhaling 2.7% isoflurane. The animal experiment was approved by the Ethics Committee of Wuhan Beisai Model Biology Center (No. BSMS Animal (Fu) No. 2025-07-21 A), and the animal experiment was conducted in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines. During the experiment, no animals met the euthanasia criteria. After the experiment, mice were euthanized uniformly.

Skin lesion evaluation

The Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) scores of mice were evaluated at a fixed time every day (9:00–10:00 AM), and the changes in PASI scores of the 6 groups of mice from day 0 to day 7 after treatment were observed. The PASI score was assessed based on three indicators: erythema, scales, and epidermal thickening. Each indicator was scored on a 0 ~ 4 scale (0 point: no symptoms; 1 point: mild; 2 points: moderate; 3 points: severe; 4 points: extremely severe), and the total score was the sum of the scores of the three indicators.

Histopathological analysis

After the mice were sacrificed, skin tissue samples (1 × 1 cm) were collected from the center of the hairless area on the back, placed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and fixed at room temperature for 24 h. The fixed tissues were dehydrated with gradient ethanol (75%, 85%, 90%, 95%, 100%) and cleared with xylene, followed by paraffin embedding to prepare 3 μm-thick serial sections. Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining was performed on the sections: after dewaxing and hydration, hematoxylin staining was conducted for 5 min, differentiation with 1% hydrochloric acid ethanol for 30 s, bluing with 1% ammonia water, eosin staining for 3 min, and after re-dehydration and clearing, the sections were mounted with neutral gum.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Skin tissue samples were collected from the lesional area on the back of mice, placed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution, fixed at 4 ℃ for 4 h, and then rinsed 3 times with 0.1 mol/L phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH7.2), 15 min each time. Subsequently, the samples were placed in 1% osmium tetroxide, fixed at 4 ℃ for 2 h, and rinsed again 3 times with PBS; after dehydration with gradient ethanol (45%, 55%, 70%, 85%, 95%, 100%) and propylene oxide, Epon812 resin embedding was performed. A Leica EM UC7 ultramicrotome (Germany) was used to prepare 70 nm ultrathin sections, which were double-stained with 2% uranyl acetate and lead citrate. A Hitachi HT7700 transmission electron microscope (Japan) was used to observe mitochondrial cristae, membrane integrity and swelling degree to analyze mitochondrial morphology, and images were collected.

Biochemical assays

Levels of GSH, Fe²+ in skin lesions were quantified using commercial kits.

ROS detection

Frozen sections (8 μm thick) of mouse dorsal skin were prepared. The sections were rinsed 3 times with PBS. Subsequently, ROS-DHE was dissolved in DMSO to prepare a 5mM stock solution, which was then diluted with PBS to make a working solution at a ratio of 1:500. The working solution was preheated and incubated with the sections at 37℃ in the dark for 30 min. After incubation, the sections were rinsed 3 times with PBS, then DAPI staining solution was added for nuclear staining; after rinsing with PBS to remove excess DAPI, glycerol was added for mounting. Subsequently, a fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX53, Japan) was used to observe and capture the fluorescence signals of ROS in the skin tissue.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

After dewaxing and hydration, the 3 μm-thick paraffin sections were placed in 0.01 mol/L citrate buffer (pH6.0) for antigen retrieval. After the sections cooled to room temperature, they were incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 15 min to block endogenous peroxidase activity, followed by rinsing 3 times with PBS, 5 min each time. Subsequently, the sections were incubated overnight at 4℃ with primary antibodies against P53 (1:50), SAT1 (1:50), and ALOX15 (1:200), respectively. On the next day, they were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled secondary antibody (1:200) at 37℃ for 30 min. 3,3’-Diaminobenzidine (DAB) was used for color development for 3 ~ 5 min. After counterstaining with hematoxylin, the sections were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted. Subsequently, the sections were observed and photographed under an upright bright-field microscope (CX31, Olympus, Japan).

Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIZOL® reagent. According to the manufacturer’s instructions, the reaction was performed using PrimeScript™ RT reagent and DNA Eraser Kit. Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) reactions were carried out with EnTurbo™ SYBR Green PCR SuperMix, and RT-qPCR analysis was performed using the QuantStudio 6 Flex system (Life Technologies, USA). The 2-ΔΔCt method was used to quantify the relative mRNA expression levels of P53, SAT1, and ALOX15, with β-actin serving as the internal reference.Primer sequences are listed in Table 1.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 23.0 software. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (x ± SD). Differences between groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Acquisition and correlation analysis of ferroptosis-regulating genes

A total of 369 ferroptosis driver genes, 348 suppressor genes, and 11 marker genes were obtained from the FerrDb database. After summarization and deduplication, 485 ferroptosis-related genes were finally identified. Venn analysis was performed between these genes and the 96 overlapping targets of Artemisia annua active components and psoriasis vulgaris, resulting in 26 intersection targets associated with 17 active components (Fig. 1E).

GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis

GO enrichment analysis of the 96 common targets yielded 801 significant terms, primarily involved in processes like positive regulation of gene expression and inflammatory response (Fig. 1D). KEGG analysis identified 153 significant pathways, including pathways in cancer, TNF signaling pathway, and Th17 cell differentiation (Fig. 1C).

PPI Network of ferroptosis-Artemisia annua active components psoriasis

A PPI network was constructed for the above 26 targets, showing 26 nodes and 225 edges (Fig. 1F, G). In the figure, the larger the node, the higher the degree value; the thicker the edge, the higher the combined score, indicating a more important role in the interaction. The three targets with the largest nodes were TP53, IL6, and IL1B, suggesting that they may be the key targets of Artemisia annua in regulating ferroptosis for the treatment of psoriasis.

KEGG pathway analysis of Ferroptosis-related targets

KEGG analysis of the 26 ferroptosis-related targets identified 85 significant pathways, with the top 10 including pathways in cancer, fluid shear stress and atherosclerosis, and Th17 cell differentiation (Fig. 1H).

In vivo experimental validation

Quercetin ameliorates psoriatic-like skin lesions

On the 7th day of modeling, the PASI score of the model group was significantly higher than that of the blank group (P < 0.05). The dorsal skin of mice showed erythema and scales with infiltration, and the lesions were obviously elevated, presenting typical psoriatic lesions, indicating successful modeling. Compared with the model group, the PASI scores of the methotrexate group, high-dose quercetin group (QR-H), and medium-dose quercetin group (QR-M), and low-dose quercetin (QR-L) were all significantly decreased (P < 0.05), and the dorsal skin lesions of mice were significantly improved, with mild symptoms such as erythema, scales, and infiltration, suggesting that quercetin could alleviate imiquimod-induced psoriatic lesions in mice (Fig. 2B, D).

Quercetin improves pathological changes

The results of H&E staining showed that compared with the blank group, the skin of mice in the model group exhibited hyperkeratosis, obvious epidermal thickening, and inflammatory cell infiltration. Compared with the model group, the epidermal thickening was significantly alleviated and the inflammatory cell infiltration was reduced in the methotrexate group, as well as the medium-dose and high-dose quercetin groups. The results of pathological Baker scoring showed that the methotrexate group, QR-H and QR-M groups are significantly decreased the pathological Baker score (P < 0.05), suggesting that quercetin could effectively improve the pathological manifestations of imiquimod-induced psoriatic lesions in mice (Fig. 2E-G).

Quercetin ameliorates mitochondrial morphology

The results of transmission electron microscopy (TEM) showed that the mitochondria in the skin tissue of mice in the blank group had a complete double-membrane structure, regular inner membrane cristae, and normal mitochondrial morphology. Compared with the blank group, the mitochondrial matrix in the model group was swollen with increased internal water content, the mitochondrial cristae were shortened and arranged disorderly, and the matrix electron density was significantly decreased. Mild matrix swelling and cristae shortening were observed in the QR-M group and QR-L group; the mitochondrial double-membrane structure in the methotrexate and QR-H groups was nearly complete and clear, the inner membrane cristae were basically intact, and the mitochondrial morphology was normal, suggesting that the QR-H group and methotrexate could effectively improve the damaged mitochondrial morphology (Fig. 3A).

Quercetin modulates GSH and Fe²⁺ levels

The results of GSH kit and ferrous ion (Fe²⁺) detection showed that compared with the blank group, the GSH level in the lesions of the model group was significantly decreased (P < 0.05), while the Fe²⁺ concentration was significantly increased (P < 0.05). Compared with the model group, the GSH levels in the lesions of the high-dose quercetin group (QR-H), medium-dose quercetin group (QR-M), low-dose quercetin group (QR-L), and methotrexate group were significantly increased (P < 0.05), and the Fe²+ concentrations were significantly decreased (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3D, E).

Quercetin attenuates oxidative stress

The results of ROS fluorescent probe detection showed that compared with the blank group, the ROS fluorescence intensity in the skin tissue of mice in the model group was significantly increased (P < 0.05). Compared with the model group, the ROS fluorescence intensity in the skin tissue of the QR-H group, QR-M group, QR-L group, and methotrexate group was significantly decreased (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3B, C).

Quercetin downregulates P53, SAT1, and ALOX15 expression

The results of immunohistochemistry (IHC) showed that compared with the blank group, the protein expressions of P53, SAT1, and ALOX15 in the epidermis of mice in the model group were significantly increased (P < 0.05). Compared with the model group, the protein expressions of P53, SAT1, and ALOX15 in the epidermis of mice in the QR-H group, QR-M group, QR-L group, and methotrexate group were significantly decreased (P < 0.05). The results of real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) showed that compared with the blank group, the mRNA expressions of P53, SAT1, and ALOX15 in the epidermis of mice in the model group were significantly up-regulated (P < 0.05); compared with the model group, the mRNA expressions of P53, SAT1, and ALOX15 in the epidermis of mice in the QR-H group, QR-M group, QR-L group, and methotrexate group were all significantly down-regulated (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4).

Discussion

In this study, network pharmacology was used to screen the active components and potential targets of Artemisia annua for the treatment of psoriasis. A total of 22 active components, 202 predicted targets of Artemisia annua, and 1312 psoriasis-related genes were obtained, with 96 common targets between the two. Further analysis combined with 485 ferroptosis-related genes from the ferroptosis database revealed that there were 26 intersection targets among Artemisia annua active components, psoriasis, and ferroptosis, corresponding to 17 active components of Artemisia annua. These results suggest that Artemisia annua may regulate ferroptosis through a multi-component and multi-target manner to exert anti-psoriatic effects, and also clarify the potential molecular link of Artemisia annua in the treatment of psoriasis through the ferroptosis pathway (Fig. 5).

Schematic diagram of the mechanism by which quercetin improves psoriasis by regulating ferroptosis.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis of the 26 intersection targets showed that TP53, IL6, and IL1B are the core targets of Artemisia annua in regulating ferroptosis in psoriasis. TP53 is a key transcription factor in ferroptosis regulation; IL-6 can regulate the redox system and iron metabolism by driving the JAK2-STAT3 signaling pathway, thereby affecting lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis. In contrast, IL1B is highly secreted under the stimulation of NF-κB pathway activation, and it can further induce cellular oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, and ferroptosis by regulating the formation of NLRP3 inflammasome39. The “psoriasis-ferroptosis-Artemisia annua-active component-target” regulatory network further confirmed that quercetin, luteolin, and kaempferol are the main active components of Artemisia annua, exerting the above effects. Molecular docking results further verified that the binding free energy between quercetin and TP53 is -5.19 kcal/mol. Binding free energy is a core indicator to measure the binding strength between ligand and receptor; the lower the value, the more stable the binding and the higher the affinity. This result suggests that quercetin has good binding potential with TP53 and may be the key chemical component and target of Artemisia annua in regulating ferroptosis in psoriasis.

Quercetin is a natural plant polyphenolic flavonoid40, which has significant immunomodulatory, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antifibrotic, and antitumor effects, and exhibits therapeutic effects on various skin diseases such as psoriasis, urticaria, melanoma, and acne41–44. The therapeutic effect of quercetin on psoriasis has been confirmed by numerous studies. Li et al. found that quercetin can inhibit the production of inflammatory factors such as TNF-α and IL-6 in inflammatory cells, and can inhibit the IL-23/IL-17 A inflammatory axis by mediating STAT3 phosphorylation, thereby inhibiting the secretion of IL-17 A and exerting anti-psoriatic effects45. In addition, quercetin can also treat psoriasis by inhibiting the PI3K/AKT/GLUT1 signaling pathway, suppressing the proliferation of human keratinocytes, and promoting their apoptosis46. A recent study showed that quercetin can induce keratinocyte apoptosis and promote their differentiation by jointly inhibiting the Notch, PI3K/AKT signaling pathways and GLUT1, thereby improving psoriasis conditions47. The above studies fully demonstrate the multi-target regulatory potential of quercetin in the treatment of psoriasis; exploring its regulatory effect on ferroptosis is expected to provide a new direction for the development of new anti-psoriatic drugs.

Ferroptosis induced by iron overload is a novel and key mechanism in the pathogenesis of psoriasis48,49. In psoriatic lesions, the characteristic manifestations of ferroptosis are decreased intracellular glutathione (GSH) levels, accumulation of lipid peroxides, and damage to mitochondrial ultrastructure50. As a key intracellular antioxidant substance, GSH plays an important role in inhibiting the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS); its depletion can significantly increase the susceptibility of epidermal cells to ferroptosis51, while mitochondria can act as a promoter or amplifier of ferroptosis triggered by glutathione depletion52. In addition, excessive accumulation of lipid ROS in the epidermis can further trigger ferroptosis, aggravate oxidative stress in local tissues, disrupt iron homeostasis, and lead to increased intracellular ferrous ion (Fe²⁺) levels. Moreover, this iron-dependent oxidation reaction can further damage mitochondrial morphology and function, forming a vicious circle and exacerbating the occurrence and development of psoriasis53–56.

The tumor suppressor gene protein P53 (TP53) is a key transcription factor in ferroptosis regulation with dual regulatory effects. Its downstream targets SAT1 and ALOX15 can synergistically promote the occurrence of ferroptosis by amplifying oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation cascades57. On the one hand, wild-type P53 can promote the production of hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) by transcriptionally activating SAT1 (spermine oxidase 1), thereby exacerbating Fe²⁺-mediated lipid peroxidation in epidermal cells58,59. On the other hand, SAT1 can regulate the expression of ALOX15; ALOX15 can catalyze the oxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids to generate a large number of lipid peroxidation products, further amplify ROS production, damage mitochondrial membrane structure, and ultimately lead to ferroptosis of epidermal cells60.

The P53/SAT1/ALOX15 signaling pathway has been confirmed to be involved in the regulation of ferroptosis in various diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease and osteoporosis; inhibiting this pathway can effectively suppress ferroptosis and exert a protective effect on lesion tissues61–63. In addition, active components of traditional Chinese medicine such as tanshinone and sea buckthorn extract have been confirmed to regulate P53-mediated ferroptosis, and quercetin has also been reported to improve diabetic retinopathy by inhibiting P53-mediated ferroptosis64–66. However, before the conduct of this study, it was unclear whether quercetin improves psoriasis by inhibiting P53-mediated ferroptosis. Studies have shown that in imiquimod-induced psoriatic lesions in mice, the levels of ferroptosis inhibitors are decreased, the levels of ferroptosis promoters are increased, the level of glutathione (a key oxidative stress gene) is decreased, and the level of lipid ROS is increased; these data support that ferroptosis is significantly activated by IMQ induction67. It is worth noting that the psoriasis model induced by IMQ is characterized by a typical IL-23/IL-17 inflammatory axis. Recent studies have confirmed that LCN2 dependent ferroptosis is involved in the pathogenesis of psoriasis, and inhibiting ferroptosis can effectively alleviate psoriasis inflammation67. These results suggest that ferroptosis may interact with the IL-23/IL-17 axis and amplify inflammatory signals, thereby promoting skin inflammation and lesion formation in the IMQ model.Therefore, in this study, an IMQ-induced psoriatic mouse model was established, and key ferroptosis markers (ROS and Fe²+ levels, mitochondrial morphology, and core antioxidant index GSH) in the psoriatic lesions of mice were detected. Meanwhile, real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and immunohistochemistry (IHC) were used to detect the mRNA and protein expression levels of P53, SAT1, and ALOX15, so as to explore whether the regulation of psoriasis ferroptosis by quercetin is related to the P53/SAT1/ALOX15 signaling pathway.

Conclusion

The results of network pharmacology and molecular docking showed that quercetin is the key active component of Artemisia annua in regulating ferroptosis in psoriasis. Further in vivo experimental results confirmed that quercetin monomer can significantly increase the epidermal GSH level of psoriatic mice, reduce Fe²⁺ accumulation and ROS production, improve the damaged mitochondrial morphology in the lesion area, and down-regulate the mRNA and protein expressions of P53, SAT1, and ALOX15 in the epidermis. The above results indicate that quercetin may improve psoriasis by interfering with the P53/SAT1/ALOX15 signaling pathway and affecting epidermal cell ferroptosis.

In summary, this preliminary study combined network pharmacology, molecular docking and in vivo animal experiments to screen and verify that quercetin serves as the core active ingredient of Artemisia annua and exerts protective effects against psoriasis via regulating epidermal ferroptosis. Our in vivo results demonstrated that single quercetin treatment significantly alleviated epidermal pathological damage in IMQ-induced psoriatic mice. Mechanistically, the therapeutic effect of quercetin was closely associated with antioxidant capacity and ferroptosis homeostasis, as evidenced by the balanced regulation of GSH, ROS and Fe²⁺, as well as the negative modulation of the P53/SAT1/ALOX15 signaling cascade.

Nevertheless, several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, our current data only clarified the correlative relationship between quercetin intervention, the expression of P53/SAT1/ALOX15 and ferroptosis-related phenotypic alterations. Direct molecular evidence and functional experiments are still lacking to confirm the definite causality and upstream-downstream regulatory mechanism of this signaling axis. Second, although the IMQ-induced psoriasis mouse model is widely adopted for basic psoriasis research, it cannot fully recapitulate the complex pathological characteristics, immune microenvironment and clinical heterogeneity of human psoriasis. Third, this study mainly focused on the pharmacological action of quercetin, while other potential active compounds in Artemisia annua, such as kaempferol and luteolin, were not further validated in vivo. In addition, key canonical ferroptosis markers, including GPX4, SLC7A11, ACSL4, 4-HNE and MDA, were not detected at the protein level, and specific inhibitor-mediated rescue experiments were not conducted to strictly verify ferroptosis dependency. Collectively, the systematic validation of ferroptosis in our research remains insufficient, and the relevant mechanistic conclusions should be regarded as preliminary exploratory findings. Moreover, the detailed molecular mechanism by which quercetin modulates ferroptosis in psoriatic epidermal cells requires more comprehensive cellular and molecular experiments in the future. Meanwhile, chemical composition identification and quantitative analysis of raw herbal materials and extracts were not performed in this work.

To address the above shortcomings, we will optimize the research design in subsequent studies. We will further identify and quantify the predicted active components of Artemisia annua, and carry out in-depth functional validation, pathway intervention and mechanistic exploration. Continuous efforts will be made to supplement more rigorous experimental evidence to enrich and improve the regulatory mechanism of Artemisia annua and quercetin on ferroptosis in psoriasis.
