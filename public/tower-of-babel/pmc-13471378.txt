Clitocine suppresses TNBC progression by boosting CCRL2 to block survival signals and neutrophil-driven inflammation
Authors: Qi Chen, Yanyun Ruan, Shuanshuan Liu, Hozeifa M. Hassan, Shaoyin Bao, Weiliang Liu, Jianguo Sun, Hongsheng Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study investigates the anti-tumor mechanisms of clitocine, a natural compound, in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), with a focus on its role in modulating the immune microenvironment via C-C motif chemokine receptor like 2 (CCRL2). The anti-cancer effects of clitocine were assessed in vitro using TNBC cell lines (MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468) through functional assays, flow cytometry, and immunoblotting. RNA sequencing was performed to identified clitocine-induced transcriptional changes. The subcutaneous 4T1 xenograft models in BALB/c mice were used for in vivo validation. Additionally, mass cytometry (CyTOF) profiled tumor immune infiltration in tumor tissues, and the role of CCRL2 in modulating the tumor immune microenvironment and inflammatory pathways was investigated through database mining (TCGA) and gain/loss-of-function experiments.ResultsClitocine potently inhibits TNBC cell proliferation, metastasis, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), while inducing mitochondrial apoptosis. It alters cancer-related gene expression (upregulating CCRL2), suppresses PI3K-AKT, NFκB, and ERK pathways, and exhibits potent antitumor activity in vivo without systemic toxicity. Mechanistically, clitocine remodels the tumor immune microenvironment by reducing neutrophil infiltration and suppressing neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) formation, effects mediated through the CCRL2/chemerin axis. Furthermore, CCRL2 is identified as a tumor suppressor, with low expression in TNBC patients correlating with poor survival. Credibly, CCRL2 knockdown in vitro and in vivo significantly abrogates the anti-tumor and immunomodulatory effects of clitocine.ConclusionOur findings demonstrate that clitocine exerts potent anti-TNBC effects by upregulating CCRL2 expression, thereby activating the clitocine/CCRL2/chemerin axis. This axis disrupts critical oncogenic signaling pathways and reprograms the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13036-026-00707-z.

Introduction

Breast cancer, a leading cause of malignancy-related deaths among women worldwide, exhibits significant epidemiological diversity and molecular heterogeneity [1, 2]. The evolving understanding of breast cancer’s molecular subtypes has revolutionized its diagnosis and treatment, emphasizing the need for precision oncology [3, 4]. Approxinately 15%~20% of breast cancer are characterizaed by the absence of estrogen receptors (ER), progesterone receptors (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), a subtype clinically defined as triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) [5, 6]. Notably, TNBC exhibits greater aggressiveness compared to other breast cancer subtypes [3, 7], characterized by a larger average tumor size, higher histological grade, poorer five-year overall survival outcomes, and elevated rates of both recurrence and metastasis [8, 9]. Despite advancements in therapeutic strategies, drug resistance and immune evasion mechanisms remain a formidable challenge in TNBC management [10–12]. The emergence of chemo-resistance in TNBC may be attributed to a multifaceted interplay of factors, including tumor heterogeneity, the presence and activity of cancer stem cells (CSCs), aberrant signaling pathway activation, immune dysregulation, hypoxic microenvironment conditions, and additional molecular determinants [13–16]. Therefore, the discovery of novel agents capable of overcoming drug resistance and enhancing therapeutic efficacy is of paramount importance.

Recent studies have highlighted the potential of natural compounds, such as Clitocine, derived from mushrooms, as promising therapeutic agents [17, 18]. Our previous research has established that clitocine effectively reverses P-glycoprotein-associated multidrug resistance by suppressing the nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) signaling pathway in R-HepG2 cells [19]. Furthermore, clitocine directly targets anti-apoptotic pathways, notably by inhibiting the expression of myeloid cell leukemia-1 (MCL-1), a key B-cell lymphoma protein 2 (Bcl-2) family member frequently overexpressed in resistant cancers, thereby sensitizing tumor cells to apoptosis and enhancing the efficacy of agents like ABT-737 [20–23]. Natural compounds offer a promising avenue for developing novel anti-cancer strategies with potentially improved safety profiles, including TNBC [24, 25]. However, although reported mechanisms have highlighted clitocine’s potential in overcoming classical drug resistance, its broader effects on tumor cell signaling, the tumor microenvironment (TME), and anti-tumor immune responses remain unexplored, particularly in the context of TNBC, necessitating further investigation. Therefore, this study aims to elucidate these novel mechanisms underpinning clitocine’s efficacy in TNBC. Here, for the first time, we demonstrated that clitocine exerted its anti-tumor effects by inhibiting tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-mediated proliferation, activating Toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling and immune responses in a C-C motif chemokine receptor-like 2 (CCRL2)-dependent manner, and disrupting the chemerin-neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) axis via CCRL2 to overcome immune evasion. By delineating these pathways, we provide a comprehensive understanding of clitocine’s multi-faceted anti-tumor actions, supporting its potential as a valuable therapeutic agent, particularly in the context of TNBC.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and cell culture

Human breast cancer cell lines MDA-MB-231 (YDT-0384), MDA-MB-468 (YDT-0391), MDA-MB-436 (YDT-0389), SKBR3 (YDT-0597), SUM159 (YDT-0625), and Hs578T (YDT-0269), along with the murine mammary carcinoma line 4T1 (YDT-0024), were procured from INDIT Bio-Technology Co., Ltd (Hangzhou, China). Cells were cultured in specialized media supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Procell, China, 164210) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S; Procell, PB180120). Specifically, SKBR3 cells were cultured in McCoy’s 5 A medium (Procell, PM150710). MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-468, MDA-MB-436, and Hs578T cells were maintained in DMEM medium (Procell, PM150210). 4T1 and SUM159 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Procell, PM150110). All cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ and 21% O₂. Cell lines were routinely confirmed to be mycoplasma-free.

Drug treatment

Clitocine (Bide Pharmatech Co., Ltd., China, BD01295067) or Chemerin (MCE, USA, HY-P70099) was dissolved in DMSO as a stock and diluted with saline to prepare the working concentration, which was used for experiments on cells in the logarithmic growth phase. For in vivo studies, clitocine was administered at 10/30 mg/kg via tail vein injection. This dose was determined from in vitro IC₅₀ data and a preliminary dose-finding pilot study, which showed effective therapeutic exposure without apparent systemic toxicity. Clitocine stock aliquots were stored at -80 °C until use.

Western blotting (WB)

Protein levels of key factors were assessed by WB. After the sample has been processed, total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA, 89900) and quantified via a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 23228). 20 g proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Merck Millipore, USA, IPVH00010). Membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA; Solarbio® life sciences, China, A8020), then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, followed by corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Proteins were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL; Biosharp® life sciences, China, BL520B) kit. β-actin served as the loading control. Band intensity was quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA, Version 1.8.0). Antibodies are listed in Table 1.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

To quantify mRNA expression in processed samples, total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Ambion, USA, 15596018), purified by precipitation with isopropanol (Sigma-Aldrich, USA, I9516), and washed with 75% ethanol. Purified RNA was resuspended in nuclease-free water treated with diethylpyrocarbonate (DEPC; BioSharp® life sciences, BL510B). First-strand complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from DNase-treated RNA using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, K1622). qPCR was conducted using the SYBR Green Pro Taq HS Premixed qPCR Kit (Accurate Biotechnology Co., ltd, China, AG11718) on a real-time PCR system with β-actin as the reference gene. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2-ΔΔCT method, and the specific primer pairs used are shown in Table 2.

Cell transfection

To establish CCRL2-overexpressing constructs, TNBC cells were transiently transfected with a CCRL2-targeting plasmid (NM_003965) or an empty pCDH vector (Youbio, China) using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 11668019) for 24 h. For transient silencing, the recombinant small interfering RNA (siRNA) was designed. TNBC cells were transfected with either CCRL2-targeting siRNA (siR-CCRL2) or a control siRNA (siR-NC) (Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd, China) using Lipofectamine 2000 for 24 h. For stable silencing, we generated lentiviral short hairpin RNA (shRNA) vectors derived from the effective siR-CCRL2 sequence. We packaged recombinant lentiviruses in HEK-293T cells (YDT-0019), collected filtered viral particles after 48 h, and transduced 4T1 cells at ~ 70–80% confluence using Lipofectamine 2000. Following a 24 h incubation, we selected stable positive cells with puromycin (2.5 µg/mL; Solarbio® life sciences, P8230). Subsequently, the efficiency of protein expression in transfected cells was confirmed by WB assays. siRNA sequences are provided in Table 3.

Methyl thiazolyl tetrazolium (MTT) assay

Cell survival and proliferation were assessed using the MTT assay. Following suitable processing, breast cancer cells were plated into 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 10³ cells per well and incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, the culture medium was removed, and 50 µL of MTT reagent (Sigma-Aldrich, M2128) was added to each well. After a 3 h incubation, formazan crystals were solubilized with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sinopharm, China, 30072418). The relative cell viability was then determined by measuring the absorbance at 570 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 51119570).

Cell migration and invasion

TNBC cell migration and invasion were quantified using Transwell chambers (Corning, 3422). Briefly, preconditioned cells (5 × 10⁵ in serum-free medium) were added to the upper chamber, while the lower chamber was filled with medium containing 10% FBS. After 24 h of incubation, unmigrated cells were removed from the upper membrane surface. Migrated cells attached to the lower surface were fixed in methanol (Sinopharm, 10014118) for 20 min and stained with crystal violet (Aladdin, China, C110703) for 40 min before microscopic imaging. For invasion analyses, the upper chambers were pre-coated with Matrigel (Corning, 356234) for 30 min before cell seeding.

Colony formation assay

The clonogenic capacity of post-treatment TNBC cells was determined by a standard colony formation assay. Briefly, 5 × 10³ cells were plated per well in a 6-well plate and grown in 10% FBS-containing medium for two weeks, with regular medium changes every three days. Upon completion, colonies were fixed in methanol (30 min), stained with 0.1% crystal violet (20 min), digitally imaged, and counted using ImageJ software.

Flow cytometry assay

We employed flow cytometry to detect apoptosis in TNBC cells after specific treatments. For this purpose, cells were labeled with the Annexin V-FITC/7-AAD detection kit (4 A Biotech, China, FXP018) following the manufacturer’s protocol, and the stained samples were subsequently analyzed using a flow cytometer (Invitrogen, USA, Attune NxT).

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

RNA was extracted from control and clitocine-treated (0.5 µM; 48 h post-treatment) MDA-MB-231 cells, with three biological replicates per group, followed by RNA-seq by Novogene (Beijing, China). We processed raw RNA-seq reads (FASTQ format) by performing quality control and adapter trimming with ‘’Fastp’’ to remove low-quality sequences and adapter contaminants. Filtered reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) with ‘’HISAT2’’, generating BAM files. We then assembled transcripts de novo using ‘’StringTie’’ and quantified gene-level counts with ‘’featureCounts’’ to generate expression matrices. Differential expression analysis was executed using the ‘’DESeq2’’ R package, applying thresholds of “|log2FoldChange|≥1” and “adjusted p-value (padj) ≤ 0.05”. Volcano plot highlighting differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was created with ‘’ggplot2’’, and a heatmap (row Z-score normalized) was generated using ‘’pheatmap’’ to display global expression profiles of DEGs. Finally, functional enrichment of DEGs for Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways and Gene Ontology (GO) terms was performed using ‘’clusterProfiler’’ in R.

Bioinformatics analysis

Within the Cancer Genome Atlas Program (TCGA; https://www.cancer.gov/ccg/research/genome-sequencing/tcga) breast cancer RNA-seq dataset, TNBC and adjacent normal samples were extracted and then categorized into tumor and normal subsets. Using the ‘’ggplot2’’ package in R, a box plot was generated to illustrate CCRL2 expression across cohorts. Additionally, patients were re-divided into high- and low-expression groups for CCRL2 according to median expression levels. Differential expression analysis was performed using the ‘’DESeq2’’ package in R, with thresholds of “|log2FoldChange|≥1” and “p < 0.05”. KEGG and GO enrichment analyses of DEGs were conducted using ‘’clusterProfiler’’ package in R. Furthermore, tumor immune cell composition was estimated using the ‘’CIBERSORT’’ algorithm implemented via the ‘’deconvo_tme()’’ function from the IOBR package in R. Subsequently, ‘’spearman’’ correlation analysis was performed to assess associations between CCRL2 expression levels and infiltration degrees of diverse immune cell types. The result was visualized as lollipop plots.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

The human CCRL2 promoter region (-1000 ~ + 1 bp relative to transcription start sites) was amplified from MDA-MB-231 cell genomic DNA by high-fidelity PCR (Merck, 4738250001), and truncated mutants (Mut1: -800 ~ + 1 bp, Mut2: -600 ~ + 1 bp, Mut3: -400 ~ + 1 bp, Mut4: -200 ~ + 1 bp; PGL3 as empty control) were generated by sequential 200 bp 5’-deletion (Detailed sequence information can be found in the Supplementary material); PCR products were cloned into pGL3-Basic vector (Promega) and high-purity plasmids were extracted for transfection in MDA-MB-231 cells. After 24 h clitocine (0.5 µM) treatment, cells were washed with pre-cooled PBS, lysed with 100 µL Passive Lysis Buffer, and luciferase activity was detected using Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay kit (Beyotime, China, RG027); firefly and renilla luciferase activities were measured sequentially, relative luciferase activity was calculated as their ratio, and experiments were performed in 3 biological replicates with 3 technical replicates each.

Animal model assay

All animal experimental protocols received approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and were executed in strict accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals in China. Specifically, 5-week-old female BALB/c mice (Slaccas, China) were acclimatized for one week under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with a 12/12-hour light/dark cycle at 22°C, after which they were subcutaneously inoculated with 4T1 cells at a dose of 5 × 10⁵ cells/100 µL. Once tumors reached approximately 80 mm³ in volume, the mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 6 per group), with the clitocine low-dose and high-dose groups receiving 10 mg/kg and 30 mg/kg, respectively, via tail vein injection once daily, while the positive control group received drug paclitaxel (15 mg/kg) via intraperitoneal injection twice weekly. Concurrently, another cohort of mice was inoculated with 4T1 cells (5 × 10⁵ cells/100 µL) stably silenced for CCRL2 and, upon tumor volumes reaching ~ 80 mm³, were randomized into three groups (n = 5 per group) and treated with 30 mg/kg clitocine via tail vein injection once daily. Tumor volume was monitored every three days starting from day seven after inoculating the tumor cells. Upon tumors reaching 1500 mm³ (calculated as length×width²/2), mice were humanely euthanized, with body weights recorded, and tumors excised, photographed, and weighed, with tumor dimensions precisely measured using digital calipers (SYNTEK, China, SY-119-200). Additionally, mouse blood was collected via the tail vein across each group for cardiac/hepatic injury markers, including creatine kinase MB isoenzyme (CKMB), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) in Servicebio® for automatic biochemical detection.

Mass cytometry assay

To characterize the immunocomposition of TNBC tumor tissues in clitocine-treated xenograft models, the collected tumor tissue (30 mg/kg clitocine treatment) and control samples were enzymatically dissociated into single-cell suspensions using 2% FBS DMEM, containing 1 mg/mL collagenase I (asegene, China, A430371) and 200 µg/mL DNAase I (Solarbio®, D8071), and then sent to PLT TECH (Zhejiang, China) for mass cytometry analysis. Specifically, cells were stained with metal isotope-labeled antibody panels targeting surface and intracellular biomarkers. Following signal acquisition by Fluidigm, the raw data underwent normalization and processing on the Cytobank platform to eliminate amphicytes, debris, and dead cells. From the resulting quality-controlled FCS files, all metal-channel signals were extracted. Flow cytometry clustering algorithms were then applied to group cells into clusters, with cell types annotated based on inter-cluster expression patterns and known surface biomarkers. A heatmap was generated using the expression intensity of different markers within clusters to visualize marker gene expression across immune cell subpopulations. On the right side of the heatmap, cluster-specific cell proportion bar charts were presented to show relative abundance. Cytometry by time-of-flight (CyTOF) single-cell data was dimensionally reduced using the t-SNE algorithm, which projected high-dimensional protein expression data into a two-dimensional space. Cell clusters were color-coded to depict their spatial distribution in the control (tumor_A) and clitocine-treated (tumor_B) sample. To quantify these observations, the relative abundance of each cluster was compared between groups using box plots.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

Target protein expression in TNBC clinical/xenograft tumor tissues was assessed by IHC. Briefly, tissues were fixed in general-purpose tissue fixative (Servicebio®, G1101) for one week, dehydrated through graded ethanol (Sinopharm, 100091192)/xylene (Sinopharm, 10023418), and paraffin-embedded. 5-µm sections were deparaffinized using dewaxing/clearing reagent for histology (Servicebio®, G1128), rehydrated through a series of ethanol solutions, and subjected to antigen retrieval with a 20×Tris-EDTA antigen repair solution (Servicebio®, G1206). After blocking endogenous peroxidase activity with 3% H₂O₂ (ANNJET®, China, D.40036) for 25 min and non-specific binding sites with serum/BSA for 30 min, primary antibodies (detailed in Table 1) were applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber. Subsequently, sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 50 min and stained with diaminobenzidine (DAB; Servicebio®, G1212) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Hematoxylin counterstaining was performed for ~ 3 min. Stained sections were then examined microscopically, with images captured. IHC results were quantified using ImageJ software based on integrated optical density (IOD), analyzing three randomly selected fields per sample.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

Paraffin-embedded xenograft tumor/TNBC clinical sections underwent sequential deparaffinization, rehydration, antigen retrieval, peroxidase blocking, species-appropriate serum/BSA blocking, overnight primary antibodies (detailed in Table 1) incubation at 4 °C, fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies incubation (50 min), 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Servicebio®, G1012) nuclear counterstaining (10 min), autofluorescence quenching (Servicebio®, G1221) for 5 min, and mounting in anti-fade medium (Servicebio®, G1401). Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany, Leica STELLARIS8).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Following the collection of blood samples from the tail vein of mice bearing a tumor across each group, an ELISA kit was employed to quantitatively determine the levels of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH; MEIMIAN, Jiangsu, China, MM-43732M1) in mouse serum samples. Concurrently, ELISA kits (MEIMIAN, MM-47177M1) were utilized to measure the concentrations of chemerin in both subcutaneous tumor tissues and blood samples obtained from the same cohort of mice. Specifically, serum was isolated from the collected blood via centrifugation, while tumor tissues were homogenized to prepare tissue lysates, which were then subjected to ELISA analysis alongside the serum samples, following the manufacturer’s instructions for each assay kit.

Additionally, primary neutrophils were isolated from the bone marrow of 5‑week‑old female BALB/c mice employing a protocol described previously [26] and treated with either DMSO (control) or clitocine at 0.1, 0.5, or 1 µM. NET formation was assessed by measuring CitH3 and MPO levels via ELISA (MEIMIAN, MM‑1005M1 and MM‑0338M2, respectively) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Separately, MDA‑MB‑231 cells were subjected to the following treatments: pCDH+Control, pCDH+Clitocine (0.5 µM; 48 h), pCDH+Clitocine+Chemerin (80 nM; 24 h), CCRL2‑OE+Control, and CCRL2‑OE+Chemerin. After treatment, the culture supernatants were collected and used to treat primary neutrophils, after which CitH3 and MPO levels were again measured by ELISA.

TdT-mediated dUTP Nick-End Labeling (TUNEL) assay

TUNEL assay was performed to assess apoptosis in TNBC cells using a fluorescein (FITC) TUNEL cell apoptosis detection kit (Servicebio®, G1501) following the manufacturer’s instructions. In xenograft tumor sections, briefly, paraffin-embedded tissues were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through graded ethanol concentrations. Following antigen retrieval with proteinase K (Servicebio®, G1205) for 20 min, sections were incubated with the TUNEL reaction mixture for 1 h at 37 °C in a humidified and light-protected chamber. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 10 min. After dehydration and mounting, apoptotic cells were visualized and imaged under a fluorescence microscope.

Statistical analysis

To guarantee result reliability, each experiment was repeated at least three times. Data were processed with GraphPad 6.0 software (USA, SCR_002798), using T-tests for two-group comparisons and one-way ANOVA for multiple groups. Data are shown as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with significance at *p < 0.05 and **p < 0.01.

Results

Clitocine exerts anti-cancer effects on TNBC cells by inhibiting the metastatic behaviors and triggering apoptosis

We investigated the anti-cancer effects of clitocine on breast cancer cells, with its chemical structure presented in Fig. 1A. Firstly, using the MTT assays, we found that clitocine dose-dependently inhibited the viability of various breast cancer cell lines (MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-468, SKBR3, SUM159, and Hs578T), indicating a inhibitory effect on cell proliferation of breast cancer (Fig. 1B). In subsequent experiments aimed at optimizing concentration parameters and investigating functional effects, we employed MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468 cells, which exhibited superior responsiveness, to further elucidate and substantiate our findings. Specifically, functional assays showed that clitocine at 0.5 µM significantly suppressed the clone formation, migration, and invasion abilities of MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468 cells (Fig. 1C&D). Molecular analysis confirmed that this cytotoxicity was accompanied by a phenotypic shift, characterized by increased E-cadherin and decreased N-cadherin and Vimentin expression (Fig. 1E), indicating an inhibition of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [27]. Moreover, flow cytometry analysis demonstrated that clitocine (MDA-MB-231 cells at 4 µM; MDA-MB-468 cells at 8 µM) significantly induced apoptosis (Fig. 1F). Mechanistically, clitocine activated mitochondrial apoptosis in representative lines (Fig. 1G), evidenced by inducing the cleavage of poly ADP-ribose polymerase (c-PARP), caspase8 (c-caspase8), and caspase9 (c-caspase9) in MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468 cells. Overall, our findings demonstrate that clitocine exerts potent anti-cancer effects on TNBC cells, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic agent for TNBC.

Clitocine inhibits TNBC progression by upregulating CCRL2 expression and disrupting pro-survival signaling in vitro and in vivo

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms of clitocine’s anti-cancer action on TNBC cells, we conducted an RNA-seq using 0.5 µM clitocine-treated and control MDA-MB-231 cells. Our analysis revealed a significant number of DEGs, comprising 138 up-regulated and 55 down-regulated genes, as shown by the volcano plot (Fig. 2A), The heatmap illustrated the cluster analysis of DEGs across multiple samples (Fig. 2B). KEGG pathway and GO enrichment analyses indicated that these DEGs were enriched in pathways related to cancer progression and cellular functions, such as regulation of angiogenesis, mitochondrial ATP synthesis, TLR, and TNF signaling pathway (Fig. 2C&D), suggesting clitocine may interfere with these pathways to exert anti-cancer effects. Notably, in the RNA-seq data, putative target genes, including CCRL2 and CEA cell adhesion molecule 1 (CEACAM1), showed significant transcriptional activation, whereas insulin-like growth factor binding protein 5 (IGFBP5) demonstrated marked transcriptional suppression, as evidenced by their FPKM values under clitocine exposure (Fig. 2E). This transcriptional upregulation of CCRL2 in TNBC cells was further validated via RT-qPCR, confirming significant increase in mRNA levels (Fig. 2F). Consistent with transcriptomic changes, WB assay demonstrated an augmented protein expression of CCLR2 in clitocine-treated TNBC cells (Fig. 2G). To clarify the temporal relationship between clitocine treatment and CCRL2 induction, we performed time-course analyses in MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468 cells. Significant upregulation of CCRL2 mRNA was detected as early as 6 h post-treatment, with expression increasing progressively up to 24 h (Fig. S1A). A corresponding time-dependent elevation in CCRL2 protein was confirmed by Western blot (Fig. S1B). These data demonstrate that clitocine triggers early and sustained induction of CCRL2 at both mRNA and protein levels, supporting its role as an early mediator of clitocine’s anti-tumor effects. To further clarify the regulatory mechanism by which clitocine modulates CCRL2, we subsequently constructed a series of luciferase reporter plasmids carrying truncated mutants of the CCRL2 promoter with different lengths. These reporter constructs were transfected into MDA-MB-231 cells, followed by clitocine stimulation. Dual-luciferase reporter assay was then conducted to detect the changes in promoter transcriptional activity. Further fragment verification confirmed that the core regulatory region responding to clitocine is located within the − 600~-400 bp fragment of the CCRL2 promoter (Fig. S1C). Given that DEGs in clitocine-treated TNBC cells were mainly enriched in the TNF signaling pathway, key for cell survival and inflammation [28, 29], we further explored clitocine’s downstream effects on key signaling cascades. Surprisingly, immunoblotting revealed that clitocine treatment significantly suppressed phosphorylation of pivotal mediators in the PI3K-AKT, NFκB, and ERK pathways, indicating broad inhibition of pro-survival signaling cascades in TNBC cells upon clitocine treatment (Fig. 2H). Collectively, these integrated analyses establish a comprehensive molecular framework linking Clitocine’s epigenetic effects to downstream regulatory networks governing TNBC cell viability.

Furthermore, in a subcutaneous 4T1 cell xenograft model, daily intravenous administration of clitocine produced a significant, dose-dependent reduction in tumor growth. Following injection of 5 × 105 cells into 4-week-old BALB/c mice, treatment was initiated once tumors reached ~ 80 mm3. At the endpoint of the experiment, visual inspection (Fig. 3A) and volume of tumors (Fig. 3B) clearly showed diminished tumor growth in clitocine-treated mice. This translated to a significant decrease in final tumor weights (Fig. 3C), particularly at the high dose of 30 mg/kg. The observed antitumor efficacy was comparable to that of the positive control agent, Drug paclitaxel, highlighting Clitocine’s potent in vivo activity against TNBC. Serum biochemical analysis revealed no significant changes in hepatic or cardiac enzymes (CKMB, LDH, ALT, and AST), indicating that clitocine treatment was well-tolerated without overt systemic toxicity (Fig. 3D). Crucially, clitocine treatment led to a significant reduction in both tumor tissues and serum levels of chemerin, the commonly recognized ligand for CCRL2 [30, 31] (Fig. 3E). Consistent with this, IHC assay confirmed upregulated CCRL2 expression and reduced phosphorylation of key downstream effectors (p-Akt, p-NFκB, and p-ERK) within tumor tissues (Fig. 3F). TUNEL assay further demonstrated a marked increase in apoptotic cell within tumor tissues from clitocine-treated mice relative to the control group, characterized by dense TUNEL-positive staining (Fig. 3G). These data support a model whereby clitocine exerts its antitumor effects, at least partially, through the regulation of the chemerin/CCRL2 pathway and its associated pro-survival signaling cascades.

To investigate the relationship between clitocine treatment and TBNC inflammation, we performed mass cytometry analysis on the composition of immune cells in xenograft tumor tissues (tumor_A: control tissues; tumor_B: 30 mg/kg clitocine-treated tissues). Tumor mononuclear cells were liberated by enzymatic digestion and profiled using a high-throughput CyTOF assay with a metal-tag antibody panel. After quality control in Cytobank, live, single-cell events were exported for analysis. As shown in Fig. 4A, unsupervised clustering of the data identified distinct 35 immune cell populations, which were functionally annotated based on marker co-expression patterns (left panel). A heatmap illustrated the characteristic protein expression profiles for each cluster (middle panel), alongside a bar chart detailing their relative abundance (right panel). Notably, the C18-C21 cluster was identified as neutrophils. Global cellular dynamics were visualized using t-SNE plots, separating control (tumor_A) and treated (tumor_B) samples (Fig. 4B). Differential abundance analysis between conditions was presented as box plots for each significant cluster (Fig. 4C), indicating that there was a significant difference in the quantity of neutrophils between the two groups, especially the C18 cluster. To further validate clitocine’s direct effect on neutrophils, we isolated bone marrow-derived neutrophils from female BALB/c mice and treated them with clitocine (0, 0.1, 0.5, 1 µM); ELISA results showed clitocine dose-dependently inhibited NET formation, demonstrating its direct anti-inflammatory effect on neutrophils independent of tumor cells (Fig. 4D).

CCRL2 regulates pro-tumor inflammatory pathways in TNBC through TLR signaling and chemerin-mediated NET formation

To further explore, WB analysis revealed variable basal expression of CCRL2 across breast cancer cell lines, with MDA-MB-231/MDA-MB-436/SKBR3/Hs578T showing low and MDA-MB-468 exhibiting high endogenous levels (Fig. 6A). Subsequently, the efficacy of CCRL2 overexpression plasmids in MDA-MB-231 cells (CCRL2) and CCRL2 siRNA interventions in MDA-MB-468 cells (siR-CCRL2) was validated through WB, confirming successful modulation of CCRL2 expression (Fig. 6B). Functional assays indicated that CCRL2 overexpression significantly suppressed cell migration and invasion in MDA-MB-231 cells, whereas CCRL2 knockdown in MDA-MB-468 cells led to a marked increase in these capabilities (Fig. 6C). Colony formation assays corroborated these findings, showing reduced clonogenic potential upon CCRL2 overexpression and increased colony numbers following its knockdown (Fig. 6D). To dissect the mechanisms, WB assays evaluated the TLR pathway activation that was identified as significantly enriched in CCRL2-associated DEGs. Results showed that CCRL2 overexpression upregulated TLR4, phosphorylated inhibitory kappa B kinase α (p-IKKα), and tumor necrosis factor receptor- associated factor 6 (TRAF6), indicating pathway activation [32], while CCRL2 knockdown suppressed their expression (Fig. 6E). IHC staining of tumor xenografts tissues treated with clitocine further revealed increased TLR4 and p-IKKα levels compared to control tissues (Fig. 6F), confirming in vivo activation of TLR signaling following clitocine/CCRL2-targeted therapy.

Moreover, IHC analysis of TNBC tissues stratified by CCRL2 expression (high vs. low) revealed inverse correlation between CCRL2 and chemerin protein levels, with high-CCRL2 tumors exhibiting significantly lower chemerin immunoreactivity compared to low-CCRL2 samples (Fig. 7A). IHC analysis of subcutaneous tumor samples treated with clitocine further revealed a significant reduction in chemerin expression levels compared to control groups (Fig. 7B). Prior studies establish the mechanism of CCRL2 competing with chemerin for binding in lung cancer immune surveillance [33], and chemerin attracts neutrophil reverse migration by interacting with CCRL2 [34]. Expanding this to TNBC, we investigated CCRL2’s relationship with neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) markers, myeloperoxidase (MPO) and citrullinated histone H3 (CitH3) [35], in TNBC. IF staining revealed a negative correlation between CCLR2 expression and levels of both MPO and CitH3 across high- and low-CCLR2 expressing TNBC clinical tissues, suggesting CCLR2 influences NETs formation or associated inflammation (Fig. 7C). Supporting a functional role, IF analysis of clitocine-treated subcutaneous tumor tissues demonstrated significant reductions in MPO and CitH3 intensities (Fig. 7D), indicating that clitocine suppresses NET-associated inflammatory processes in vivo. These data collectively establish that after clitocine treatment, CCRL2 expression on tumor cells is upregulated, activating the TLR signaling pathway to trigger immune responses. Meanwhile, the upregulated CCRL2 competitively binds chemerin in the TME, reducing its concentration, thereby inhibiting neutrophil recruitment and NET formation, and disrupting the immune escape provided by NETs for tumor cells, at least partially.

CCRL2 silencing reverses clitocine’s therapeutic effects for TNBC through TLR signaling and chemerin-mediated NET formation in vitro and in vivo

Our results of the Transwell assay demonstrated that clitocine treatment significantly inhibited the migration and invasion of MDA-MB-468 cells. However, when combined with siR-CCRL2, which silences CCRL2 expression, the inhibitory effects of clitocine on cell migration and invasion were notably reversed, as evidenced by the increased number of migrated and invaded cells compared to the clitocine-alone group (Fig. 8A). Similarly, the colony formation assay revealed that clitocine reduced the colony-forming ability of the cells. Still, this reduction was partially counteracted by the co-treatment with siR-CCRL2, leading to an increase in colony number (Fig. 8B). The MTT assay further showed that clitocine suppressed cell proliferation over time, while the combination of clitocine and siR-CCRL2 attenuated this suppressive effect (Fig. 8C). WB analysis indicated that clitocine activated the TLR signaling pathway, as evidenced by the upregulation of TLR4, p-IKKα, and TRAF6 proteins. Importantly, the silencing of CCRL2 reversed the activation of this pathway, with decreased levels of these proteins observed in the clitocine + siR-CCRL2 group compared to the clitocine-alone group (Fig. 8D). To further dissect the downstream regulation, we validated the role of chemerin, the key ligand of CCRL2. siRNA-mediated chemerin knockdown in MDA-MB-468 and MDA-MB-231 cells (validated by WB, Fig. 9A) significantly suppressed cell proliferation, migration & invasion, and the levels of NET-associated markers CitH3 and MPO in primary neutrophils, which phenocopied the anti-tumor phenotypes induced by clitocine (Fig. 9B-D). Conversely, chemerin add-back in clitocine-treated or CCRL2-overexpressing MDA-MB-231 cells reversed the clitocine/CCRL2-mediated inhibition of cell migration, invasion, and proliferation (Fig. 9E-F). Moreover, either clitocine intervention or CCRL2 overexpression alone in MDA-MB-231 downregulated the NET-related markers CitH3 and MPO in primary neutrophils, and such inhibitory effects were effectively abrogated by adding exogenous recombinant chemerin protein (Fig. 9G). Collectively, these results confirm that clitocine modulates malignant biological behaviors and NET formation through the clitocine/CCRL2/chemerin regulatory axis.

To establish a reliable model for studying CCRL2 function, we generated stable CCRL2-knockdown 4T1 murine TNBC cells using lentiviral shRNA constructs, with lenti-sh-CCRL2-3 exhibiting the most efficient silencing confirmed by WB assay (Fig. 10A). The subcutaneous tumor macrographs revealed visible differences in tumor size among the groups (Fig. 10B). Notably, the subcutaneous final tumor weights (Fig. 10C) and volumes (Fig. 10D) were significantly reduced in the clitocine-treated group compared to the control group, and this reduction was partially reversed in the lenti-sh-CCRL2 + clitocine group. Analysis of tumor tissue demonstrated that clitocine treatment resulted in a reduction of chemerin levels, and that stable silencing of CCRL2 could partially restore these levels (Fig. 10E). Furthermore, IHC analyses revealed that clitocine treatment elevated the expression of CCRL2 and TLR4 while suppressing chemerin in subcutaneous tumors. These effects were reversed upon stable CCRL2 silencing (Fig. 10F). Consistently, IF analysis confirmed that the inhibitory effect of clitocine on the expression of MPO and CitH3 was abrogated by CCRL2 silencing, evidenced by heightened fluorescence signals for these markers in tumors treated with both lenti-sh-CCRL2 and clitocine compared to clitocine alone (Fig. 10G). These data collectively indicate that CCRL2 deficiency disrupts the clitocine/chemerin/CCRL2 axis, preventing chemerin sequestration and increasing local chemerin availability. This leads to enhanced TLR4 pathway activation and augmented neutrophil recruitment/NET formation, thereby counteracting clitocine’s antitumor effects.

Discussion

The management of TNBC remains a formidable clinical challenge due to its aggressive phenotype, characteristic lack of targetable receptors, and high propensity for metastasis and recurrence [36, 37]. The pursuit of therapeutic agents capable of countering the dual challenges of aggressive tumor biology and immunosuppressive microenvironments is particularly critical for TNBC [38]. Our previous work has established the natural compound clitocine as a potent antagonist of classical cancer survival pathways, demonstrating its efficacy in reversing P-glycoprotein-mediated multidrug resistance via suppression of the NF-κB pathway and directly targeting the anti-apoptotic regulator MCL-1 to trigger apoptosis in tumor models [19–23]. This study extends the therapeutic effect of clitocine into the realm of TNBC, revealing a potent, multi-faceted mechanism of action. Our data delineate a novel and intricate mechanism whereby clitocine suppresses tumor growth and metastasis by directly inducing apoptosis and inhibiting EMT in vitro, and, more significantly, by upregulating the atypical chemokine receptor CCRL2. Clitocine activates TLR signaling while sequestering chemerin to limit NET formation and immune escape in TNBC. These findings position the clitocine/CCRL2 axis as a critical regulator of TNBC progression and a promising therapeutic target, warranting further exploration in preclinical and clinical settings.

Strategic targeting of nucleoside metabolism, a pivotal axis in tumor biology, is a cornerstone of cancer therapeutics, as evidenced by the enduring clinical utility of antimetabolites like gemcitabine and 5-fluorouracil [39, 40]. These agents take advantage of the increased metabolic demands of proliferating tumor cells, primarily by incorporating into DNA/RNA or inhibiting key biosynthetic enzymes [41–44]. Our study builds upon this foundational principle but pivots to a novel and overlooked aspect of nucleoside biology: the role of nucleosides themselves as potent extracellular signaling molecules, with adenosine being the most representative example [45–47]. While adenosine is a fundamental component of ATP, its accumulation in the TME exerts profound immunosuppressive effects, facilitating immune evasion and tumor progression [48, 49]. At the pivotal nexus where metabolic processes intersect with immunological mechanisms, our exploration of clitocine, a scarce nucleoside analog [50], assumes profound significance. Our study reveals that clitocine exerts its antitumor effects via dual actions: [1] direct modulation of cancer hallmark pathways (EMT suppression, mitochondrial apoptosis induction) and [2] disruption of pro-survival and immune signaling networks through upregulation of CCRL2, a critical regulator of chemerin-mediated neutrophil dynamics. These findings align with emerging paradigms in cancer biology, where combinatorial targeting of survival pathways and immune evasion mechanisms synergize to enhance therapeutic efficacy [51]. Notably, our data confirmed that clitocine exhibits strong cytotoxic and anti-metastatic effects on TNBC cell lines, dose-dependently curbing proliferation, clonogenicity, migration, and invasion. This molecular linkage is attributed to clitocine’s capacity to reverse EMT by upregulating E-cadherin and downregulating N-cadherin/Vimentin, which aligns with previous studies showing that EMT inhibitors can diminish cancer stemness and metastatic potential [52]. Similarly, clitocine’s activation of mitochondrial apoptosis (cleaving PARP and caspase-8/9) underscores its apoptotic potency, aligning with synthetic lethality in drug-resistant cancers [53], and confirming its role as a direct TNBC cell death inducer.

The identification of CCRL2 as the early and time-dependent factor through which clitocine mediates the transcriptional activity of the CCRL2 promoter in TNBC establishes a novel mechanistic framework. CCRL2, an atypical heptahelical receptor that binds chemerin but does not initiate classical G-protein signaling [30], has a poorly defined role in cancer, with context-dependent pro- or anti-tumorigenic effects [54, 55]. Contrary to canonical chemokine receptors that promote immune cell migration, CCRL2 uniquely suppresses chemotaxis and invasion by disrupting CCL2-p38 MAPK signaling in breast cancer, as demonstrated by prior work [56]. Additionally, CCRL2 exhibits constitutive (higher in immune-infiltrated tumors) rather than transient expression as in normal inflammation, and is upregulated by pro-inflammatory cytokines in breast cancer, especially IFN-γ [57]. Furthermore, Li et al. reported that high miR-937-3p or low CCRL2 (a direct target of miR-937-3p) levels correlate with shorter overall survival in breast cancer, making them potential prognostic biomarkers for treatment guidance [58]. These findings position CCRL2 as a potential biomarker for immune infiltration and a target for modulating chemokine-driven metastasis, particularly in inflammatory breast cancer subtypes. In this study, CCRL2’s upregulation in response to clitocine treatment was validated across transcriptomic, RT-qPCR, and WB analyses, establishing it as a therapeutic target. This is further supported by TCGA genomic data showing CCRL2 expression links to better survival in TNBC patients, reinforcing its role as a tumor suppressor gene [33]. Moreover, CCRL2 silencing reverses clitocine’s antitumor effects, underscoring its crucial role and suggesting CCRL2 agonists/stabilizers could enhance clitocine’s clinical efficacy. Importantly, the discovery that clitocine modulates neutrophil dynamics via the chemerin/CCRL2 axis represents a significant advancement. Neutrophils, traditionally viewed as pro-inflammatory cells [59], have emerged as complex regulators of cancer progression, contributing to both tumor promotion and immunosuppression [60, 61]. Our mass cytometry and IF staining data revealed that clitocine shifts neutrophil polarization toward a tumor-suppressive state, evidenced by decreased levels of MPO and CitH3, markers of NET formation [35], which can encapsulate tumor cells to facilitate their immune escape [62]. This aligns with our observations that TNBC tumors with high CCRL2 expression exhibit reduced chemerin bioavailability and diminished NET activity. In the context of the TNBC, these findings support that CCRL2 functions as a negative regulator of neutrophil-driven inflammation by limiting chemerin availability. The expression between CCRL2 and TLR signaling provides additional mechanistic depth. TLR4 activation, a hallmark of inflammasome-driven cancer progression [63], was suppressed by CCRL2 overexpression and restored upon its silencing. This corroborates prior studies implicating TLR4 in chemoresistance and immune evasion [64], suggesting that clitocine may disrupt adaptive resistance mechanisms through CCRL2-dependent TLR4 inhibition. Together, these findings position CCRL2 as a molecular switch governing crosstalk between cancer cells and the immune system, offering opportunities for combination therapies targeting this axis.

Clitocine exerted robust in vivo anti-tumor efficacy in the 4T1 xenograft model comparable to paclitaxel, with no obvious hepatic or cardiac toxicity at effective doses (30 mg/kg), supporting its clinical translational potential. However, this study has inherent limitations. Additionally, this study still has inherent translational pharmacology limitations. We centered on clarifying clitocine’s core anti-tumor and immune-remodeling mechanisms; rigorous pharmacokinetics (PK) / pharmacodynamics (PD) profiling, intratumoral penetration and exposure detection, and dynamic on-target biomarker monitoring demand long-term animal studies and professional platforms, which are limited by current time and resource availability. The synergistic effects and optimal administration sequence of clitocine combined with chemo- or immunotherapy also remain speculative. Expanding the study to subtype-specific TNBC cell lines, patient-derived organoids, PDX and orthotopic metastasis models would improve finding generalizability, which we plan to pursue in future work. While CCRL2 is suggested as a candidate biomarker for clitocine responsiveness in TNBC, clinical validation in larger cohorts is still needed. Future research may explore combinations with immune checkpoint or NET-targeting agents. As a tumor suppressor, CCRL2 could be genetically or pharmacologically augmented for adjuvant therapy. Moreover, CCRL2-mediated suppression of TLR4 signaling provides a rationale for developing TLR4-NFκB crosstalk inhibitors to synergize with clitocine against TNBC.

Conclusion

This study advances our understanding of clitocine’s anti-TNBC mechanisms by integrating epigenetic reprogramming, survival pathway disruption, and immune microenvironment modulation. The elucidation of the CCRL2 axis as a central mediator underscores its potential as a therapeutic target and biomarker. These findings lay the groundwork for clinical trials testing clitocine in TNBC patients, particularly those with CCRL2-low expression profiles or high neutrophil infiltration, thereby addressing unmet medical needs in this deadly disease.
