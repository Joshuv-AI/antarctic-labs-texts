Abstract
This work presents a low-rank tensor model for multi-dimensional Markov chains . A common approach to simplify the dynamical behavior of a Markov chain is to impose low-rankness on the transition probability matrix. Inspired by the success of these matrix techniques, we present low-rank tensors for representing transition probabilities on multi-dimensional state spaces. Through tensor decomposition, we provide a connection between our method and classical probabilistic models. Moreover, our proposed model yields a parsimonious representation with fewer parameters than matrix-based approaches. Unlike these methods, which impose low-rankness uniformly across all states, our tensor method accounts for the multi-dimensionality of the state space. We also propose an optimization-based approach to estimate a Markov model as a low-rank tensor. Our optimization problem can be solved by the alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM), which enjoys convergence to a stationary solution. We empirically demonstrate that our tensor model estimates Markov chains more efficiently than conventional techniques, requiring both fewer samples and parameters. We perform numerical simulations for both a synthetic low-rank Markov chain and a real-world example with New York City taxi data, showcasing the advantages of multi-dimensionality for modeling state spaces.



I
Introduction

Dynamic processes provide a mathematical framework for modeling the evolution of complex systems
[
1
,
2
]
.
When the system and its dynamics are known, control theory has a long history of successful applications
[
3
,
4
]
.
However, the dynamics are not always available and oftentimes probabilistic, and they must instead be estimated from sampled trajectories.
Learning the dynamics of complex systems is challenging due to non-linear behavior, the presence of
uncertainties
, high dimensionality, and data scarcity
[
5
]
.
Assuming specific properties of the system can render the problem tractable.
In this work, we rely on two assumptions: Markovianity, where future behavior relies only on the current state of the system, and low-rankness
[
6
]
, where the system dynamics can be represented by a small set of hidden variables
[
7
]
.

Low-rank methods are widely used for learning the transition probabilities of Markov systems, which can be represented via probability transition matrices.
When the full matrix is known, low-rank techniques enable compression for lower-dimensional models
[
8
,
9
]
.
Moreover, low-rankness has shown success in estimating an unknown probability matrix from samples, a classical problem in estimation theory
[
10
,
11
]
.
Markov chains pose a challenging case, where samples within a trajectory of observed states are typically correlated.
Many works propose low-rank Markov transition matrix estimation for a simpler problem that is feasible for limited samples
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
.
Beyond practical considerations, the concept of low-rankness arises naturally when transitions occur in a low-dimensional hidden space, such as for hidden Markov chains
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
.

However, past literature does not fully exploit the ubiquitous multi-dimensionality of state spaces.
Tensors provide natural models for transition probabilities between multi-dimensional states, where tensor dimensions correspond to those of the state space
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
.
By considering the tensor to be low-rank, we postulate a parsimonious model that also deals with the curse of dimensionality.
We thus leverage the notion of low-rankness for tensors to formulate an interpretable model of the transition probabilities, both for the entire state space and for each dimension.
Previous works exploit tensor low-rankness for learning Markov models via higher-order moments
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
, for locally-interacting Markov chains
[
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
]
, and for value functions of multi-dimensional Markov decision processes
[
28
,
29
,
30
,
31
,
32
]
.
However, this is the first work to use low-rank tensors for modeling transitions in such spaces.
Moreover, we consider the practical task of estimating a transition tensor from trajectories via a provably convergent optimization approach.
Our contributions are as follows.

C1

We propose a tensor-based probabilistic model for Markov chains on multi-dimensional state spaces, which provides a link between probabilistic models and low-rank tensor decompositions.

C2

We present an optimization-based approach to estimate a low-rank Markov transition tensor from trajectories, which enjoys convergence when solved with alternating minimization.

C3

We demonstrate the advantages of tensors for modeling Markovian systems and the viability of their estimation through synthetic and real-world simulations.

II
Tensor low-rank model

In this section, we first relate tensors to Markov models.
Then, we introduce our probabilistic tensor model, for which we describe the consequences of tensor low-rankness.

II-A

Preliminaries

We consider an ergodic Markov chain with a finite
D
-dimensional state space
{\mathcal{S}}:={\mathcal{S}}_{1}\times\cdots\times{\mathcal{S}}_{D}
, where
{\mathcal{S}}_{d}:=[I_{d}]=\{1,2,\dots,I_{d}\}
for every
d\in[D]
.
For each
{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}
, the transition probability
p({\mathbf{s}}^{\prime}|{\mathbf{s}})
is the conditional probability of transitioning to state
{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime}
from state
{\mathbf{s}}
.
Learning a Markov model is equivalent to estimating the transition distribution
p
.
Since we assume ergodicity, the Markov model has a unique stationary distribution
\pi
, where
\pi({\mathbf{s}})
is the probability of being in the state
{\mathbf{s}}\in{\mathcal{S}}
.
Additionally, let the joint probability distribution be
q
, where
q({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime})=p({\mathbf{s}}^{\prime}|{\mathbf{s}})\pi({\mathbf{s}})
models the likelihood of the co-occurrence of two consecutive states
{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}
.
We exploit our knowledge of the multi-dimensional state space by arranging the probabilities
p({\mathbf{s}}^{\prime}|{\mathbf{s}})
in a tensor format.
Specifically, consider the state
{\mathbf{s}}\in{\mathcal{S}}
, which is a vector
{\mathbf{s}}=[s_{1},\dots,s_{D}]^{T}
for
s_{d}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{d}
.
We can thus arrange the transition probabilities
p(s^{\prime}_{1},\dots,s^{\prime}_{D}|s_{1},\dots,s_{D})
into the transition probability tensor
{\hbox{\hskip 3.93053pt\hskip-3.93053pt\hbox{$\mathbf{P}$}\hskip-3.93053pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-1.2pt}{\hbox{\rule{3.44444pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 3.93053pt}}\in[0,1]^{I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{D}\times I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{D}}
, where
{\hbox{\hskip 3.93053pt\hskip-3.93053pt\hbox{$\mathbf{P}$}\hskip-3.93053pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-1.2pt}{\hbox{\rule{3.44444pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 3.93053pt}}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime})=p({\mathbf{s}}^{\prime}|{\mathbf{s}})
for every
{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}
.
Analogously, we also define the stationary probability tensor
{\hbox{\hskip 4.31247pt\hskip-4.31247pt\hbox{$\mathbf{R}$}\hskip-4.31247pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-1.2pt}{\hbox{\rule{3.44444pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.31247pt}}\in[0,1]^{I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{D}}
for
\pi
and the joint probability tensor
{\hbox{\hskip 4.31941pt\hskip-4.31941pt\hbox{$\mathbf{Q}$}\hskip-4.31941pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-3.14444pt}{\hbox{\rule{3.44444pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.31941pt}}\in[0,1]^{I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{D}\times I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{D}}
for
q
.

II-B

Probabilistic Model

Similar to existing works that learn Markov chains via low-rank decomposition
[
13
]
, we propose first estimating the joint distribution
q
, from which we derive the transition distribution
p
.
This enables a connection between probabilistic models and low-rank tensor decompositions, as we will show. In particular, we model the joint probability tensor

\mathbf{Q}

with a low-rank tensor decomposition.
To derive the decomposition of

\mathbf{Q}

, we first note that any joint distribution can be represented using a naive Bayes model
[
33
]
, which assumes the existence of a hidden dimension with
F
possible values that generates
q

[
34
, Proposition 1]
.
Formally, for every
{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}
we model
q
as

q({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime})=\sum_{f=1}^{F}\text{Pr}(f)\prod_{d=1}^{D}\text{Pr}(s_{d}|f)\prod_{d=1}^{D}\text{Pr}(s^{\prime}_{d}|f),

(1)

which can be interpreted as a tensor decomposition with rank
F

[
35
,
36
,
34
]
.
To see this, first assume that

\mathbf{Q}

has rank
F
, which means that it can be represented by the sum of at minimum
F
rank-1 tensors.
This sum, known as the canonical polyadic decomposition (CPD)
[
37
]
, is defined as
{\hbox{\hskip 4.31941pt\hskip-4.31941pt\hbox{$\mathbf{Q}$}\hskip-4.31941pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-3.14444pt}{\hbox{\rule{3.44444pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.31941pt}}=\sum_{f=1}^{F}\boldsymbol{\lambda}(f){\mathbf{Q}}_{1}(:,f)\circ\cdots\circ{\mathbf{Q}}_{D}(:,f)\circ{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime}_{1}(:,f)\circ\cdots\circ{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime}_{D}(:,f)
, where
\circ
is the outer product,
{\mathbf{Q}}_{d},{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{d}\times F}
are known as factor matrices, and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
is a normalization term.
We denote this decomposition as
{\hbox{\hskip 4.31941pt\hskip-4.31941pt\hbox{$\mathbf{Q}$}\hskip-4.31941pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-3.14444pt}{\hbox{\rule{3.44444pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.31941pt}}=[[\boldsymbol{\lambda},{\mathbf{Q}},{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime}]]
, with
{\mathbf{Q}}:=\{{\mathbf{Q}}_{d}\}_{d=1}^{D}
and
{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime}:=\{{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime}_{d}\}_{d=1}^{D}
.
Then, under the CPD model, the entry of

\mathbf{Q}

associated with the states
{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}
is computed as

{\hbox{\hskip 4.31941pt\hskip-4.31941pt\hbox{$\mathbf{Q}$}\hskip-4.31941pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-3.14444pt}{\hbox{\rule{3.44444pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.31941pt}}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime})=\sum_{f=1}^{F}\boldsymbol{\lambda}(f)\prod_{d=1}^{D}{\mathbf{Q}}_{d}(s_{d}|f)\prod_{d=1}^{D}{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime}_{d}(s^{\prime}_{d}|f),

(2)

which is equivalent to (
1
) substituting our tensor formulation of

\mathbf{Q}

and its decomposition
{\left\llbracket\boldsymbol{\lambda},{\mathbf{Q}},{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime}\right\rrbracket}
, that is,
{\mathbf{Q}}_{d}(s_{d}|f)=\text{Pr}(s_{d}|f)
,
{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime}_{d}(s^{\prime}_{d}|f)=\text{Pr}(s^{\prime}_{d}|f)
, and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}(f)=\text{Pr}(f)
.
Thus, estimating the CPD decomposition of

\mathbf{Q}

is equivalent to estimating
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
as the distribution over the hidden variable and the columns of
{\mathbf{Q}}_{d}
and
{\mathbf{Q}}_{d}^{\prime}
as distributions over the dimensions of the state space.
Critically, under mild dimensionality conditions the decomposition in (
2
) and thus the naive Bayes model in (
1
) are unique
[
37
]
, and so are the distributions for the latent and state dimensions
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
,
{\mathbf{Q}}_{d}
, and
{\mathbf{Q}}_{d}^{\prime}
.
However, there may exist other naive Bayes representations with more than
F
hidden states that generate the same distribution
q
.

Next, we obtain a tensor representation

\mathbf{R}

for the stationary probability distribution
\pi
from the CPD model for

\mathbf{Q}

.
To do so, we compute the marginal distribution
\pi({\mathbf{s}})=\sum_{{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}}q({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime})
as

\displaystyle{\hbox{\hskip 4.31247pt\hskip-4.31247pt\hbox{$\mathbf{R}$}\hskip-4.31247pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-1.2pt}{\hbox{\rule{3.44444pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.31247pt}}({\mathbf{s}})=\sum_{{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}}{\hbox{\hskip 4.31941pt\hskip-4.31941pt\hbox{$\mathbf{Q}$}\hskip-4.31941pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-3.14444pt}{\hbox{\rule{3.44444pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.31941pt}}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{f=1}^{F}\boldsymbol{\lambda}(f)\prod_{d=1}^{D}{\mathbf{Q}}_{d}(s_{d}|f)\sum_{{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}}\prod_{d=1}^{D}{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime}_{d}(s^{\prime}_{d}|f)

\displaystyle=\sum_{f=1}^{F}\boldsymbol{\lambda}(f)\prod_{d=1}^{D}{\mathbf{Q}}_{d}(s_{d}|f).

(3)

Note that the decomposition of

\mathbf{R}

in (
3
) may not be the CPD decomposition of

\mathbf{R}

if the tensor rank of

\mathbf{R}

is lower than
F
.
Moreover, by ergodicity we may also obtain

\mathbf{R}

by marginalizing over
{\mathbf{s}}
instead of
{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime}
since
\pi({\mathbf{s}})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in{\mathcal{S}}}q({\mathbf{s}},\boldsymbol{\tau})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in{\mathcal{S}}}q(\boldsymbol{\tau},{\mathbf{s}})

[
38
]
.
Thus, we may exchange
{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime}_{d}
for
{\mathbf{Q}}_{d}
in the second line of (
3
).

Lastly, we derive the transition probability tensor

\mathbf{P}

.
We apply the chain rule of probability with

\mathbf{Q}

and

\mathbf{R}

to obtain

{\hbox{\hskip 3.93053pt\hskip-3.93053pt\hbox{$\mathbf{P}$}\hskip-3.93053pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-1.2pt}{\hbox{\rule{3.44444pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 3.93053pt}}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime})=\frac{{\hbox{\hskip 4.31941pt\hskip-4.31941pt\hbox{$\mathbf{Q}$}\hskip-4.31941pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-3.14444pt}{\hbox{\rule{3.44444pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.31941pt}}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime})}{{\hbox{\hskip 4.31247pt\hskip-4.31247pt\hbox{$\mathbf{R}$}\hskip-4.31247pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-1.2pt}{\hbox{\rule{3.44444pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.31247pt}}({\mathbf{s}})}.

(4)

Recall that the primary objective in learning Markov chains is to estimate

\mathbf{P}

.
Given

\mathbf{Q}

, we can compute

\mathbf{R}

using (
3
) then

\mathbf{P}

using (
4
).
In practice, however, we do not have direct access to

\mathbf{Q}

.
The next section addresses the challenge of estimating the joint probability tensor

\mathbf{Q}

from observed trajectories sampled from the chain.

III
Low-rank Probability Tensor Estimation

Given our proposed tensor model, we now aim to learn the transition probabilities in

\mathbf{P}

from a trajectory of length
N
of multi-dimensional states
{\mathcal{X}}=\{{\mathbf{s}}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
observed on
{\mathcal{S}}
.
To this end, we exploit the naive Bayes representation of
q
by estimating the CPD decomposition of

\mathbf{Q}

in (
2
), from which we obtain estimates for

\mathbf{R}

via (
3
) and

\mathbf{P}

via (
4
).
First, let
\tilde{{\hbox{\hskip 4.31941pt\hskip-4.31941pt\hbox{$\mathbf{Q}$}\hskip-4.31941pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-3.14444pt}{\hbox{\rule{3.44444pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.31941pt}}}
be the empirical estimate of the joint distribution from
{\mathcal{X}}

[
39
]
, where for every
{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}
,

\tilde{{\hbox{\hskip 4.31941pt\hskip-4.31941pt\hbox{$\mathbf{Q}$}\hskip-4.31941pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-3.14444pt}{\hbox{\rule{3.44444pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.31941pt}}}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{N}\left|\left\{n\in[N-1]~|~{\mathbf{s}}_{n}={\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{n+1}={\mathbf{s}}^{\prime}\right\}\right|.

(5)

Thus, each entry
\tilde{{\hbox{\hskip 4.31941pt\hskip-4.31941pt\hbox{$\mathbf{Q}$}\hskip-4.31941pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-3.14444pt}{\hbox{\rule{3.44444pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.31941pt}}}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime})
denotes the empirical frequency of the co-occurrence of consecutive states
{\mathbf{s}}
and
{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime}
.
Note that we may also estimate the transition distribution
\tilde{{\hbox{\hskip 3.93053pt\hskip-3.93053pt\hbox{$\mathbf{P}$}\hskip-3.93053pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-1.2pt}{\hbox{\rule{3.44444pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 3.93053pt}}}
, where for every
{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}
such that
{\mathbf{s}}
is observed at least once, that is,
{\mathbf{s}}_{n}={\mathbf{s}}
for some
n\in[N]
,

\tilde{{\hbox{\hskip 3.93053pt\hskip-3.93053pt\hbox{$\mathbf{P}$}\hskip-3.93053pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-1.2pt}{\hbox{\rule{3.44444pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 3.93053pt}}}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime})=\frac{N\tilde{{\hbox{\hskip 4.31941pt\hskip-4.31941pt\hbox{$\mathbf{Q}$}\hskip-4.31941pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-3.14444pt}{\hbox{\rule{3.44444pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.31941pt}}}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime})}{\left|\left\{n\in[N-1]~|~{\mathbf{s}}_{n}={\mathbf{s}}\right\}\right|},

(6)

otherwise, if
{\mathbf{s}}
is never observed, then we let
\tilde{{\hbox{\hskip 3.93053pt\hskip-3.93053pt\hbox{$\mathbf{P}$}\hskip-3.93053pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-1.2pt}{\hbox{\rule{3.44444pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 3.93053pt}}}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime})=I^{-1}
for
I:=\prod_{d=1}^{D}I_{d}
.
With
\tilde{{\hbox{\hskip 4.31941pt\hskip-4.31941pt\hbox{$\mathbf{Q}$}\hskip-4.31941pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-3.14444pt}{\hbox{\rule{3.44444pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.31941pt}}}
, we propose the following optimization problem to estimate the decomposition
{\left\llbracket\boldsymbol{\lambda},{\mathbf{Q}},{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime}\right\rrbracket}
for
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in[0,1]^{F}
,
{\mathbf{Q}}_{d},{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime}_{d}\in[0,1]^{I_{d}\times F}
as our rank-
F
estimate of

\mathbf{Q}

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},{\mathbf{Q}},{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime}}~

\displaystyle g(\boldsymbol{\lambda},{\mathbf{Q}},{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime}):=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\tilde{{\hbox{\hskip 4.31941pt\hskip-4.31941pt\hbox{$\mathbf{Q}$}\hskip-4.31941pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-3.14444pt}{\hbox{\rule{3.44444pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.31941pt}}}-{\left\llbracket\boldsymbol{\lambda},{\mathbf{Q}},{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime}\right\rrbracket}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}

(7)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}~~~~~

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\boldsymbol{\lambda},{\mathbf{Q}},{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime}\geq{\mathbf{0}},~{\mathbf{1}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\lambda}=1,~{\mathbf{Q}}_{d}^{\top}{\mathbf{1}}=({\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime}_{d})^{\top}{\mathbf{1}}={\mathbf{1}},~\forall d\in[D],

with constraints to ensure valid distributions of
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
,
{\mathbf{Q}}_{d}
, and
{\mathbf{Q}}_{d}^{\prime}
.
In particular, the first constraint imposes element-wise non-negativity, while the equality constraints ensure that
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
and each of the columns of
{\mathbf{Q}}_{d}
and
{\mathbf{Q}}_{d}^{\prime}
sum to 1.
From (
7
), we obtain an estimate
\hat{{\hbox{\hskip 4.31941pt\hskip-4.31941pt\hbox{$\mathbf{Q}$}\hskip-4.31941pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-3.14444pt}{\hbox{\rule{3.44444pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.31941pt}}}
, which we use to obtain
\hat{{\hbox{\hskip 4.31247pt\hskip-4.31247pt\hbox{$\mathbf{R}$}\hskip-4.31247pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-1.2pt}{\hbox{\rule{3.44444pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.31247pt}}}
using (
3
).
Then, we use (
4
) to estimate
\hat{{\hbox{\hskip 3.93053pt\hskip-3.93053pt\hbox{$\mathbf{P}$}\hskip-3.93053pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-1.2pt}{\hbox{\rule{3.44444pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 3.93053pt}}}
.

Alternating minimization methods are popular choices for obtaining tensor decompositions

40
]
.
Indeed, while the objective
g
in (

) is non-convex, it is convex in each element of the decomposition,
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
,
{\mathbf{Q}}_{d}
, and

The problem (

41
,

]
.
We thus obtain
\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
,
{\hat{\mathbf{Q}}}
, and
{\hat{\mathbf{Q}}}^{\prime}
via ADMM, which yields our estimate
\hat{{\hbox{\hskip 4.31941pt\hskip-4.31941pt\hbox{$\mathbf{Q}$}\hskip-4.31941pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-3.14444pt}{\hbox{\rule{3.44444pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.31941pt}}}
, from which we derive
\hat{{\hbox{\hskip 4.31247pt\hskip-4.31247pt\hbox{$\mathbf{R}$}\hskip-4.31247pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-1.2pt}{\hbox{\rule{3.44444pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.31247pt}}}
and then
\hat{{\hbox{\hskip 3.93053pt\hskip-3.93053pt\hbox{$\mathbf{P}$}\hskip-3.93053pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-1.2pt}{\hbox{\rule{3.44444pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 3.93053pt}}}
.

\boldsymbol{\psi}
,
{\mathbf{S}}=\{{\mathbf{S}}_{d}\}_{d=1}^{D}
, and
{\mathbf{S}}^{\prime}=\{{\mathbf{S}}^{\prime}_{d}\}_{d=1}^{D}
, which act as copies of
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
,
{\mathbf{Q}}
, and
{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime}
, respectively, and present an equivalent problem

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},{\mathbf{Q}},{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\psi},{\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{S}}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle~~~g(\boldsymbol{\lambda},{\mathbf{Q}},{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime})+{\mathbb{I}}\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq{\mathbf{0}},{\mathbf{Q}}\geq{\mathbf{0}},{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime}\geq{\mathbf{0}}\}

(8a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\qquad

\displaystyle~~~\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\boldsymbol{\psi},~{\mathbf{Q}}_{d}={\mathbf{S}}_{d},~{\mathbf{Q}}_{d}^{\prime}={\mathbf{S}}_{d}^{\prime},~

(8b)

\displaystyle~~~{\mathbf{1}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\psi}=1,~{\mathbf{S}}_{d}^{\top}{\mathbf{1}}=({\mathbf{S}}^{\prime}_{d})^{\top}{\mathbf{1}}={\mathbf{1}},~\forall d\in[D],

(8c)

where
{\mathbb{I}}\{\cdot\}
denotes an indicator function that is 0 when its argument is true and infinity otherwise. We introduce the dual variables
{\mathbf{u}}
,
{\mathbf{U}}=\{{\mathbf{U}}_{d}\}_{d=1}^{D}
,
{\mathbf{U}}^{\prime}=\{{\mathbf{U}}^{\prime}_{d}\}_{d=1}^{D}
, which are associated with the constraints in (
8b
), and
v
,
{\mathbf{v}}=\{{\mathbf{v}}_{d}\}_{d=1}^{D}
,
{\mathbf{v}}^{\prime}=\{{\mathbf{v}}^{\prime}_{d}\}_{d=1}^{D}
, associated with (

V
Conclusion

In this work, we modeled Markov chains on multi-dimensional state spaces using low-rank tensors.
First, we showed that the CPD decomposition of our tensor model is equivalent to a naive Bayes representation of the joint transition probabilities.
This led to our approach to estimate a multi-dimensional Markov model from a sample trajectory as a low-rank tensor optimization problem.
Furthermore, we showed that solving the problem with ADMM converges to a stationary point.
Our synthetic and real-world simulations demonstrated our tensor model to be an efficient representation for multi-dimensional dynamical systems.
In particular, compared to traditional matrix-based algorithms, our low-rank tensor estimation achieved similar or superior performance with fewer parameters and less data.
In future work, we will investigate the effect of tensor rank on Markov models for multi-dimensional state spaces, such as the implications of rank for reversibility.
Moreover, we can extend to other tensor decompositions for alternative notions of low-rankness.

{\mathbf{v}}
, and
{\mathbf{v}}^{\prime}
.
In particular,
{\mathbf{w}}
is defined as

\displaystyle{\mathbf{w}}:=[{\mathbf{u}};\mathrm{vec}\left({\mathbf{U}}_{1}\right);\dots;\mathrm{vec}\left({\mathbf{U}}_{D}\right);\mathrm{vec}\left({\mathbf{U}}^{\prime}_{1}\right);

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\dots;\mathrm{vec}\left({\mathbf{U}}^{\prime}_{D}\right);v;{\mathbf{v}}_{1};\dots;{\mathbf{v}}_{D};{\mathbf{v}}^{\prime}_{1};\dots;{\mathbf{v}}^{\prime}_{D}].

Indeed, the objective function values in matrix form in (
8a
) and in vectorized form (
10
) are equivalent, and the squared
\ell_{2}
norm and the inner product in
{\mathcal{L}}_{\beta}
as in (
11
) are separable and can be split into the terms in (
9
).

With the optimization problem in the form (
10
), we may now prove that the five necessary conditions hold for the conclusion of
[
44
, Theorem 1]
.
In particular, we require that

AS1

The objective function
\phi
is coercive;

AS2

The solution is feasible, that is,
\mathrm{Im}\left({\mathbf{A}}\right)\subseteq\mathrm{Im}\left({\mathbf{B}}\right)
;

AS3

The problem (
10
) yields Lipschitz continuous sub-minimization paths for each primal vector
{\mathbf{x}}_{i}
;

AS4

For
g({\mathbf{x}})=\frac{1}{2}\|\tilde{{\hbox{\hskip 4.31941pt\hskip-4.31941pt\hbox{$\mathbf{Q}$}\hskip-4.31941pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-3.14444pt}{\hbox{\rule{3.44444pt}{0.32289pt}}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 4.31941pt}}}-{\mathcal{T}}({\mathbf{x}})\|_{F}^{2}
and
f_{i}({\mathbf{x}}_{i})={\mathbb{I}}\{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\geq{\mathbf{0}}\}
for every
i\in\{0\}\cup[2D]
,
g
is Lipschitz differentiable in any bounded set,
f_{0}
is lower semi-continuous, and
f_{i}
is restricted prox-regular for every
i\in[2D]
; and

AS5

The function
\phi
is Lipschitz differentiable with respect to
{\mathbf{y}}
.

It is trivial to see that
AS2
and
AS5
hold;
\mathrm{Im}\left({\mathbf{A}}\right)
is indeed a subspace of
\mathrm{Im}\left({\mathbf{B}}\right)
, and
\phi
is constant with respect to
{\mathbf{y}}
.
Thus, we continue with
AS1
and show that
\phi
is coercive.
Let
\phi({\mathbf{x}})<\infty
be bounded for some
{\mathbf{x}}
.
By the presence of the indicator functions, each entry of
{\mathbf{x}}
and thus of
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
,
{\mathbf{Q}}
, and
{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime}
is bounded.
Therefore, the tensor
{\mathcal{T}}({\mathbf{x}})=\llbracket\boldsymbol{\lambda},{\mathbf{Q}},{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime}\rrbracket
has bounded entries, which are multilinear polynomials of entries of
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
,
{\mathbf{Q}}
,
{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime}
, or equivalently of
{\mathbf{x}}
.
Then, we must have that
\left\lVert{\mathbf{x}}\right\rVert_{2}<\infty
, so
\phi
is coercive.

Assumption
AS3
requires that, for every
i\in\{0\}\cup[2D]
, the solution map
H_{i}({\mathbf{z}})=\{\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{{\mathbf{x}}}\phi({\mathbf{x}})~\mathrm{s.t.}~{\mathbf{A}}_{i}{\mathbf{x}}_{i}={\mathbf{z}}\}
is Lipschitz continuous.
Since each
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}
is full column rank,
H_{i}
is a linear operator and thus Lipschitz continuous, satisfying
AS3
.

Next, we verify that
AS4
holds.
For a given
{\mathbf{x}}
and a bounded open set
{\mathcal{C}}
containing
{\mathbf{x}}
, we choose a radius
r
small enough such that the closed ball
{\mathcal{N}}
centered at
{\mathbf{x}}
with radius
r
is contained in
{\mathcal{C}}
, that is,
{\mathcal{N}}\subset{\mathcal{C}}
.
Let
G(t)=\nabla_{{\mathbf{x}}}g({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}+t({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}))
for some
{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{N}}
.
By the mean value inequality, we have that

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{{\mathbf{x}}}g({\mathbf{x}})-\nabla_{{\mathbf{x}}}g({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})\|_{2}^{2}~=~\|G(1)-G(0)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad~\leq~\sup_{c\in(0,1)}\|G^{\prime}(c)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad~=~\sup_{c\in(0,1)}\|\nabla_{{\mathbf{x}}}^{2}g({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}+c({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}))({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad~\leq~\sup_{c\in(0,1)}\|\nabla_{{\mathbf{x}}}^{2}g({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}+c({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})\|_{F}\cdot\|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad~\leq~M\|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\|_{2},

where the last inequality holds since
\nabla_{{\mathbf{x}}}g
is a multivariate polynomial and the ball
{\mathcal{N}}
is compact, so the mapping
{\mathbf{x}}\mapsto\|\nabla^{2}_{{\mathbf{x}}}g({\mathbf{x}})\|_{F}
is continuous and attains a maximum
M
over
{\mathcal{N}}
by the extreme value theorem.
Thus,
\nabla_{{\mathbf{x}}}g({\mathbf{x}})
is locally Lipschitz, that is,
g({\mathbf{x}})
is Lipschitz differentiable in any bounded set.
Moreover, indicator functions on convex sets are both lower semi-continuous and restricted prox-regular
[
44
]
.
Thus, we have that
AS4
holds.

We then have that all five conditions
AS1
to
AS5
hold for the result in
[
44
, Theorem 1]
.
This states that ADMM initialized at any point
({\mathbf{x}}^{(0)},{\mathbf{y}}^{(0)},{\mathbf{w}}^{(0)})
applied to (
10
) will yield a sequence of solutions
\{({\mathbf{x}}^{(k)},{\mathbf{y}}^{(k)},{\mathbf{w}}^{(k)})\}_{k\geq 0}
that converges to a stationary point of (
10
) for a large enough
\beta>0
.
Moreover, observe that
g({\mathbf{x}})
is a semi-algebraic function
[
48
]
and thus
{\mathcal{L}}_{\beta}
a Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz function.
By Theorem 1 of
[
44
]
, we can further guarantee that the sequence will converge to the same stationary point regardless of the initial point
({\mathbf{x}}^{(0)},{\mathbf{y}}^{(0)},{\mathbf{w}}^{(0)})
.
Finally, as
{\mathbf{x}}
,
{\mathbf{y}}
, and
{\mathbf{w}}
contain vectorizations of the primal variables
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
,
{\mathbf{Q}}
,
{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime}
, the auxiliary variables
\boldsymbol{\psi}
,
{\mathbf{S}}
,
{\mathbf{S}}^{\prime}
, and the dual variables
{\mathbf{u}}
,
{\mathbf{U}}
,
{\mathbf{U}}^{\prime}
,
v
,
{\mathbf{v}}
,
{\mathbf{v}}^{\prime}
, this implies that the convergence result holds when ADMM is applied to the matrix version of the problem (
8a
), which is equivalent to the original problem (
7
).
