An Order of Magnitude Time Complexity Reduction for Gaussian Graphical Model Posterior Sampling Using a Reverse Telescoping Block Decomposition
Zejin Gao, Ksheera Sagar and Anindya Bhadra

Abstract
We consider the problem of fully Bayesian posterior estimation and uncertainty quantification in undirected Gaussian graphical models via Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) under recently-developed element-wise graphical priors, such as the graphical horseshoe. Unlike the conjugate Wishart family, these priors are non-conjugate; but have the advantage that they naturally allow one to encode a prior belief of sparsity in the off-diagonal elements of the precision matrix, without imposing a structure on the entire matrix. Unfortunately, for a graph with p p nodes and with n n samples, the state-of-the-art MCMC approaches for the element-wise priors achieve a per iteration complexity of 𝒪 ⁡ ( p 4 ) , \mathcal{O}(p^{4}), which is prohibitive when p ≫ n p\gg n . In this regime, we develop a suitably reparameterized MCMC with per iteration complexity of 𝒪 ⁡ ( p 3 ) \mathcal{O}(p^{3}) , providing a one-order of magnitude improvement, and consequently bringing the computational cost at par with the conjugate Wishart family, which is also 𝒪 ⁡ ( p 3 ) \mathcal{O}(p^{3}) due to a use of the classical Bartlett decomposition, but this decomposition does not apply outside the Wishart family. Importantly, the proposed benefit is obtained solely due to our reparameterization in an MCMC scheme targeting the true posterior, that reverses the recently developed telescoping block decomposition of Bhadra et al., 2024 , in a suitable sense. There is no variational or any other approximate Bayesian computation scheme considered in this paper that compromises targeting the true posterior. Simulations and the analysis of a breast cancer data set confirm both the correctness and better algorithmic scaling of the proposed reverse telescoping sampler.



Keywords:
Element-wise prior; Graphical horseshoe; Graphical horseshoe-like; Iterative proportional scaling; Markov random field.

1
Introduction

Gaussian graphical models (GGMs) offer a simple yet powerful framework for modeling conditional independence relationships in multivariate real-valued data. These relations can be directed (representing causality), undirected (representing interactions) or a combination thereof
(
Lauritzen, 1996
)
. In the current work, we focus solely on undirected GGMs, where it is known that there is a one-to-one correspondence between the off-diagonal zeros of the precision matrix
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
and a Markov random field (MRF) factorization of the conditional independence relationships between the variables.

We denote by
p
the number of variables at hand and by
n
the sample size. Our specific focus in this article is on Bayesian estimation of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}=\{\theta_{jk}\}
in the regime
p\gg n
. To begin, we note that this setting is computationally challenging even for routine priors, such as the conjugate Wishart family. Assume one observes
\mathbf{y}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}_{n}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{-1})
and the prior on
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
is
\mathcal{W}_{p}(b,\boldsymbol{\Phi})
for some
b>(p-1)
and
\boldsymbol{\Phi}\in\mathcal{M}_{p}^{+}
, where
\mathcal{M}_{p}^{+}
denotes the space of
p\times p
positive definite matrices, and
\mathcal{W}_{p}(b,\boldsymbol{\Phi})
denotes a Wishart random variable with shape parameter
b
and scale matrix
\boldsymbol{\Phi}

(
Gupta and Nagar, 2000
, Chapter 3)
. Then, by routine calculations, the posterior of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
is also Wishart, given as:
\boldsymbol{\Theta}\mid\mathbf{y}\sim\mathcal{W}_{p}(b+n,\;\boldsymbol{\Phi}+\mathbf{y}^{T}\mathbf{y})
. In this setting, the standard algorithm for posterior sampling is Bartlett’s decomposition of Wishart
(
Bartlett, 1934
)
, which requires a Cholesky decomposition of a
p\times p
matrix, and thus has a computational cost of
\mathcal{O}(p^{3})
. Unfortunately, the situation is worse for non-conjugate priors. A crucial bottleneck in the posterior sampling of
(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\mid\mathbf{y})
under
any prior
is that one must ensure the posterior samples also belong to
\mathcal{M}_{p}^{+}
. This restriction is automatically satisfied in the conjugate Wishart family, which puts mass only on the space of positive definite matrices, but must be enforced explicitly under all other non-conjugate priors. Therefore, unlike in regression problems with real-valued coefficients, it is far more challenging to put element-wise priors on
\theta_{jk}
and then to design a valid posterior sampler respecting the constraint
\boldsymbol{\Theta}\in\mathcal{M}_{p}^{+}
. A naïve accept–reject sampler is not likely to be effective in enforcing this constraint at a large enough
p
, because the space
\mathcal{M}_{p}^{+}
constitutes a very small subset of
\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
. A Cholesky-based modeling approach, where one writes
\boldsymbol{\Theta}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{T}
, is a possibility for maintaining positive definiteness while allowing for unconstrained modeling of
\mathbf{L}

(
Pourahmadi, 1999
;
Daniels and Pourahmadi, 2002
)
, but again, a prior on
\mathbf{L}
in general induces a complicated prior structure on
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
, and vice versa, apart from special cases such as the Wishart or G-Wishart
(
Roverato, 2002
;
Uhler et al., 2018
)
. Thus, if one desires a sparsity-inducing prior directly on
\theta_{jk}
to learn a sparse graphical structure, it is unclear what induced prior on
\mathbf{L}
would achieve this goal, apart from very specific cases.

An ingenious approach that indeed allows one to put element-wise non-conjugate priors on
\theta_{jk}
and still perform Gibbs sampling while respecting the positive definite constraint, was developed by
Wang, 2012
in the context of the Bayesian graphical lasso (BGL) prior. We describe this in detail in Section
2
. However the parameteization of
Wang, 2012
is not specific to the BGL, and similar sampling approaches have been used later for other priors with better statistical properties, such as the graphical horseshoe or GHS
(
Li et al., 2019
)
, the graphical horseshoe-like or GHSL
(
Sagar et al., 2024a
)
, and the graphical R2-D2
(
Gan et al., 2022
)
, among others. In particular,
Sagar et al., 2024a
studied the posterior concentration properties of the element-wise graphical priors and showed that the GHS and GHSL priors enjoy the optimal rates of posterior contraction established by
Banerjee and Ghosal, 2014
. The penalized maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate under these element-wise priors have also been studied. For the BGL prior, the MAP corresponds to the frequentist graphical lasso
(
Friedman et al., 2008
)
, whereas a novel local linear approximation scheme for MAP estimation under generic element-wise priors was developed by
Sagar et al., 2024b
. While MAP estimation is faster, it does not provide full posterior uncertainty quantification, and we do not discuss this further in the current work. Nevertheless, the decomposition of
Wang, 2012
is also fundamental to the MAP approaches
(
Wang, 2014
;
Sagar et al., 2024b
)
.

Despite its utility, Wang’s parametrization has a fundamental bottleneck, which is a
\mathcal{O}(p^{4})
complexity per iteration to generate a sample of
(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\mid\mathbf{y})
, which is one order-of magnitude worse than the
\mathcal{O}(p^{3})
complexity for the conjugate Wishart model, and is prohibitive when
p
is large. We describe in detail in Section
2
why this complexity is inherent to Wang’s approach, and there is no way to bypass this, even if
n
is much smaller than
p
. Intuitively, this happens because
Wang, 2012
’s estimation procedure only
sees
the
p\times p
scatter matrix
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{y}^{T}\mathbf{y}
, defined as
n
-times the sample covariance matrix, and not the
n\times p
data matrix
\mathbf{y}
itself. Thus, even if
n
is much smaller than
p
, there is no way for
Wang, 2012
to take advantage of this.

In this work, we demonstrate that a valid Gibbs sampler targeting the exact posterior is possible with a worst case per iteration complexity of
\mathcal{O}(p^{3})
when
p\gg n
, providing a one order of magnitude reduction in computation cost, and bringing the cost down at par with the conjugate Wishart family, even for non-conjugate models. The key to our work is to
reverse
, in a suitable sense, the
telescoping block decomposition
of the precision matrix recently developed by
Bhadra et al., 2024
for estimating the marginal likelihood in GGMs under element-wise priors. However, the possibility of using this decomposition for
sampling
was not developed by
Bhadra et al., 2024
, who end up using a
\mathcal{O}(p^{5})
algorithm. As we demonstrate in the current work, this decomposition is also particularly amenable to
fast sampling
under Gaussian scale mixture priors, developed in the context of linear
regression problems
by
Bhattacharya et al., 2016
. Using this, we demonstrate that our sampler has a per iteration complexity of
\mathcal{O}(\max{(n^{2}p^{2},p^{3})})
, which is
\mathcal{O}(p^{3})
in the
p\gg n
regime, i.e., if
n=\mathdutchcal{o}(p^{1/2})
. We take particular care to distinguish our methodology with existing approaches that also use a regression setting, such as the Cholesky factor based methods
(
Pourahmadi, 1999
, e.g.)
or pseudolikelihood methods
(
Meinshausen and Bühlmann, 2006
, e.g.)
. We perform extensive simulations under the GHS and GHSL priors to verify both the correctness of our algorithm, and its improved scaling over
p
.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we outline the existing approaches for posterior sampling in GGMs under element-wise priors, with a particular focus on the fundamental
\mathcal{O}(p^{4})
barrier for this approach. Our main innovation is developed in Section
3
, with an explicit demonstration of its
\mathcal{O}(p^{3})
complexity when
p\gg n
. We provide numerical demonstrations under the graphical horseshoe
(
Li et al., 2019
)
and graphical horseshoe-like
(
Sagar et al., 2024a
)
priors in Section
4
to verify both the correctness and improved scalability of our method. An application on breast cancer data is discussed in Section
5
, and a summary of the main findings along with some future directions are presented in Section
6
.

2
Existing Approaches for Posterior Sampling in GGMs under Element-wise Priors

2.1
Data Generating Model and Basic Notations

Consider the Gaussian model,
\mathbf{y}_{n\times p}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{n\times p},\mathbf{I}_{n}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{p\times p}^{-1})
. Under this model, each row of
\mathbf{y}_{n\times p}
is an independent Gaussian random vector of length
p
with mean zero and covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{p\times p}^{-1}
. We drop the subscripts by simply writing
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
or
\mathbf{y}
when there is no room for confusion; otherwise we make them explicit. The notation
\mathbf{y}_{j},\;j=1,\ldots,p
is reserved for the
j
th column of
\mathbf{y}
, and
\mathbf{y}_{j:k}
denotes the
n\times(j-k+1)
sub-matrix of
\mathbf{y}
by concatenating columns
j
through
k
for
j\leq k
, whence, trivially,
\mathbf{y}_{1:p}=\mathbf{y}
. When needed,
\mathbf{y}_{i\bullet}
denotes the
i
th row of
\mathbf{y}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. Let
\mathcal{M}_{p}^{+}
denote the space of
p\times p
positive definite matrices. We denote by
\mathbf{A}_{j\times j}
the upper-left
(j\times j)
sub-matrix of a
p\times p
matrix
\mathbf{A}_{p\times p}
and
\mathbf{a}_{j}=(\mathbf{a}_{\bullet j},a_{jj})\in\mathbb{R}^{j-1}\times\mathbb{R}
denotes the last column of
\mathbf{A}_{j\times j}
.

2.2
Element-wise Priors on
\boldsymbol{\Theta}

To motivate the need for element-wise priors, one may first look at the Wishart prior
\boldsymbol{\Theta}\sim\mathcal{W}_{p}(b,\boldsymbol{\Phi})
, with density given as
f(\boldsymbol{\Theta})\propto|\boldsymbol{\Theta}|^{(b-p-1)/2}\exp\{-(1/2)\mathrm{tr}({\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Theta})\}
for
b>(p-1)
and
\boldsymbol{\Phi}\in\mathcal{M}^{+}_{p}
. Although this prior is conjugate to a multivariate Gaussian likelihood, the properties on the marginal prior
f(\theta_{jk})
under this prior on the entire
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
are not directly modeled, even though much is known regarding these
(
Gupta and Nagar, 2000
, Chapter 3)
. This is unfortunate, since in high-dimensional problems, a natural assumption is sparsity in the off-diagonal elements of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
, which one may wish to directly model under sparsity-inducing priors on
\theta_{jk}
, without further assuming any group or low rank structures among the
p
variables, a setting that we term
unstructured sparsity
. With this motivation, a set of element-wise priors, such as the BGL and GHS, have been proposed, defined hierarchically for
j,k=1,\ldots,p;\;j<k
, as a normal scale mixture:

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\Lambda},\tau)

\displaystyle\propto\prod_{j<k}\mathcal{N}(\theta_{jk}\mid 0,\;\tau^{2}\lambda_{jk}^{2})\mathbf{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\in\mathcal{M}^{+}_{p}\right),

(1)

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mid\tau)

\displaystyle\propto\prod_{j<k}f(\lambda_{jk}\mid\tau).

(2)

The choice of the mixing distribution on
\lambda_{jk}
allows one to model a wide variety of marginal priors on
\theta_{jk}
, available as
f(\theta_{jk}\mid\tau)=\int f(\theta_{jk}\mid\lambda_{jk},\tau)f(\lambda_{jk}\mid\tau)d\lambda_{jk}
.
For example, in BGL,
(\lambda^{2}_{jk}\mid\tau)
is given an exponential distribution, giving a Laplace or double exponential marginal distribution for
\theta_{jk}

(
Andrews and Mallows, 1974
)
, while in GHS,
(\lambda_{jk}\mid\tau)
is set as a half-Cauchy distribution, resulting in a horseshoe distribution for
\theta_{jk}

(
Carvalho et al., 2010
)
. These respectively lead to exponentially decaying and polynomially decaying marginal prior tails for
\theta_{jk}
. Other similarly defined priors include the GHS-like
(
Sagar et al., 2024a
)
, the graphical R2-D2
(
Gan et al., 2022
)
, and the point mass mixture prior of
Wang, 2015
. The truncation
\mathbf{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\in\mathcal{M}^{+}_{p}\right)
in Equation (
1
) cannot be ignored, for otherwise the prior is not valid. Given our interest in off-diagonal sparsity, we assume throughout that the priors on the diagonal terms are
uninformative
, i.e., one has
f(\theta_{jj})\propto 1
, but if desired, other priors that put mass on
(0,\infty)
can also be used for
\theta_{jj}
, such as the exponential, as in
Wang, 2012
.

2.3
Model Posterior and the Cyclical Gibbs Sampler of
Wang, 2012

Consider sampling from the posterior distribution under the prior of Equations (
1
)–(
2
) and a multivariate Gaussian likelihood.
The full conditional posterior of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
is:

f(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\mid\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{2},\tau)\propto\left|\boldsymbol{\Theta}\right|^{\frac{n}{2}}\mathrm{exp}\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{S}\boldsymbol{\Theta})\right\}\prod_{j<k}\exp\left(-\frac{\theta_{jk}^{2}}{2\lambda_{jk}^{2}\tau^{2}}\right)\mathbf{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\in\mathcal{M}^{+}_{p}\right),

(3)

where the matrix
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{2}
has entries
\lambda^{2}_{jk}
with diagonal elements set to
1
. The main challenge in posterior sampling is the constraint
\mathbf{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\in\mathcal{M}^{+}_{p}\right)
. This constraint is automatically respected if the prior on
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
is Wishart, in which case the posterior is also Wishart, which only assigns mass to the space of positive definite matrices. However, in the case of non-conjugate element-wise priors, it is clear that a naïve rejection or slice sampling scheme targeting the un-truncated posterior:
\left|\boldsymbol{\Theta}\right|^{\frac{n}{2}}\mathrm{exp}\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{S}\boldsymbol{\Theta})\right\}\prod_{j<k}\mathrm{exp}\left\{-{\theta_{jk}^{2}}/{(2\lambda_{jk}^{2}\tau^{2}})\right\}
has little chance to succeed, since the acceptance probability will then depend on:
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\in\mathcal{M}^{+}_{p}\right)]=\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\in\mathcal{M}^{+}_{p})
, which is prohibitively small for a large
p
. A Metropolis algorithm is likely to be hard to design for similar reasons, as it is not clear what a good proposal choice would be under a positive definite constraint. Obvious choices, such as the blind random walk proposal:
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{\mathrm{proposal}}=\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{\mathrm{current}}+\delta\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{O},\mathbf{I}_{p})
for some
\delta>0
, implies perturbing the eigenvalues of the current parameter in a very specific manner, which may not be suitable for efficient posterior exploration. The non-smooth indicator constraint also makes it impossible to compute gradients, precluding the use of gradient-based methods such as Langevin or Hamiltonian Monte Carlo for more informed proposal design, and automatic differentiation cannot resolve this issue.

However, an ingenious
\mathcal{O}(p^{4})
algorithm that allows Gibbs sampling even under this positive definite constraint of Equation (
3
) was developed by
Wang, 2012
under a suitable reparameterization, in the context of the element-wise Bayesian graphical lasso prior. In the face of poor mixing, a Gibbs sampler may offer no panacea, but at least it comes with two in-built benefits. The first is there is no scope for sample rejection. The second is there are no tuning parameters such as a random walk step size to choose, i.e., a Gibbs sampler is veritably
auto-tuned
. A similar strategy was later adopted by
Li et al., 2019
for the graphical horseshoe prior, which demonstrates better statistical properties. However, for posterior sampling purposes, the only difference between
Wang, 2012
and
Li et al., 2019
is the sampling of the lower level hyperparameters. The key step in both cases is the following decomposition:

\boldsymbol{\Theta}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{(p-1)\times(p-1)}&\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\bullet p}\\
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\bullet p}^{T}&\theta_{pp}\end{bmatrix},\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbf{S}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{S}_{(p-1)\times(p-1)}&\boldsymbol{s}_{\bullet p}\\
\boldsymbol{s}_{\bullet p}^{T}&s_{pp}\end{bmatrix},\hskip 10.00002pt\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{2}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{2}_{(p-1)\times(p-1)}&\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{2}_{\bullet p}\\
(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{2}_{\bullet p})^{T}&1\end{bmatrix},

(4)

where
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{(p-1)\times(p-1)}
is the upper left
(p-1)\times(p-1)
sub-matrix of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
; let
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\bullet p}
be a
(p-1)
vector of the off-diagonal elements of the
p
th column of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
, and
\theta_{pp}
, a scalar, is the corresponding diagonal element. The partitions of
\mathbf{S}
and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{2}
are interpreted analogously. With this, the posterior of Equation (
3
) is the following:

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\mid{\bf y},\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{2},\tau)

\displaystyle\propto f({\bf y}\mid\boldsymbol{\Theta})f(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{2},\tau)\propto|\boldsymbol{\Theta}|^{n/2}\exp\{-(1/2)\mathrm{tr}({\bf S}\boldsymbol{\Theta})\}f(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{2},\tau)

\displaystyle\propto|\theta_{pp}-

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-1.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\ \,p}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{(p-1)\times(p-1)}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-1.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\ \,p}|^{n/2}\left|\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{(p-1)\times(p-1)}\right|^{n/2}

\displaystyle\times\exp

\displaystyle\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left\{2{\bf s}_{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-1.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\ \,p}^{T}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-1.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\ \,p}+s_{pp}\theta_{pp}+\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf S}_{(p-1)\times(p-1)}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{(p-1)\times(p-1)}\right)\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\times\prod_{j<k}

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{\theta_{jk}^{2}}{2\lambda_{jk}^{2}\tau^{2}}\right)\mathbf{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\in\mathcal{M}^{+}_{p}\right),

(5)

where, applying the Schur formula for determinant of partitioned matrices to the decomposition of Equation (
4
), the second and third lines of the previous display give an expression for the likelihood,
|\boldsymbol{\Theta}|^{n/2}\exp\{-(1/2)\mathrm{tr}({\bf S}\boldsymbol{\Theta})\}
, up to a multiplicative constant, and the final line gives the contribution of the prior. Consider now the reparameterization of
Wang, 2012
:

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-1.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\ \,p}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-1.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\ \,p},\hskip 10.00002pt\tilde{\theta}_{pp}=\theta_{pp}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T}_{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-1.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\ \,p}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{(p-1)\times(p-1)}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-1.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\ \,p},

(6)

and note, by the properties of Schur complements, the equivalence of the following:

\displaystyle\{\boldsymbol{\Theta}\in\mathcal{M}_{p}^{+}\}=\{\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{(p-1)\times(p-1)}\in\mathcal{M}_{p-1}^{+}\}\cap\{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-1.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\ \,p}\in\mathbb{R}^{p-1},\;\tilde{\theta}_{pp}>0\}.

Thus, the full conditional posterior
f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-1.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\ \,p},\tilde{\theta}_{pp}\mid\mathrm{rest})
is given by:

\displaystyle f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-1.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\ \,p},\tilde{\theta}_{pp}\mid\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{(p-1)\times(p-1)},{\bf y}_{1:p},\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{2},\tau)\propto\exp

\displaystyle\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\left(2{\bf s}_{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-1.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\ \,p}^{T}\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-1.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\ \,p}+s_{pp}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-1.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\ \,p}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{(p-1)\times(p-1)}^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-1.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\ \,p}\right)\right\}\textstyle\tilde{\theta}_{pp}^{\frac{n}{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{s_{pp}}{2}\tilde{\theta}_{pp}\right)

\displaystyle\times\prod_{j<k}\exp\left(-\frac{\tilde{\theta}_{jk}^{2}}{2\lambda_{jk}^{2}\tau^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-1.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\ \,p}\in\mathbb{R}^{p-1},\tilde{\theta}_{pp}>0\mid\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{(p-1)\times(p-1)}\in\mathcal{M}_{p-1}^{+}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-1.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\ \,p}\mid-\mathbf{C}{\bf s}_{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-1.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\ \,p}\,,\,\mathbf{C})

\displaystyle\times\mathrm{Gamma}\left(\tilde{\theta}_{pp}\mid\mathrm{shape=}n/2+1,\mathrm{rate=}s_{pp}/{2}\right),

(7)

where
\mathbf{C}=\{s_{pp}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{(p-1)\times(p-1)}^{-1}+\tau^{-2}\mathrm{diag}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{2}_{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-1.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\ \,p})\}^{-1}
. An important remark is in order.

Remark 1

(Maintaining positive definiteness of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
)
.

The key benefit of the reparameterization of Equation (
6
) should now be apparent by comparing the truncation constraint
\mathbf{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\in\mathcal{M}^{+}_{p}\right)
of Equation (
2.3
) with
\mathbf{1}\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-1.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\ \,p}\in\mathbb{R}^{p-1},\tilde{\theta}_{pp}>0\mid\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{(p-1)\times(p-1)}\in\mathcal{M}_{p-1}^{+}\right)
in the second line of Equation (
7
). Suppose
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{(p-1)\times(p-1)}
, to be used in Equation (
6
), is positive definite. This means, during the sampling of
(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-1.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\ \,p},\tilde{\theta}_{pp}\mid\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{(p-1)\times(p-1)},{\bf y}_{1:p})
, it suffices to ensure
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-1.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\ \,p}
is real-valued and
\tilde{\theta}_{pp}
is positive, which the draws from normal and gamma distribution automatically satisfy. Hence, the lack of an explicit truncation by an indicator in the last line of Equation (
7
) is not an anomaly. The equivalence of the constraints is due to the fact that a matrix is positive definite if and only if all of its principal minors are positive definite.

Since the column label
j=p
is arbitrary, the sampling approach of
Wang, 2012
involves conditionally sampling for each column
(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-1.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\ \,j},\tilde{\theta}_{jj}\mid\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{-j,-j},{\bf y}_{1:p})
according to Equation (
7
), where
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{-j,-j}
is the sub-matrix of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
with the
j
th row and column removed, and then recovering
(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-1.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\ \,j},\theta_{jj})
using the inverse parameterization of Equation (
6
), which is one-to-one, given
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{-j,-j}
. If the starting value of the MCMC iterations is a positive definite
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
, then positive definiteness is maintained throughout, by simply sampling from un-truncated normal and gamma distributions, even though the prior of Equations (
1
)–(
2
) is clearly non-conjugate. The full conditional posterior sampling of the
\lambda_{jk}
hyperparameters depends on the specific element-wise prior at hand. We provide a few examples next.

1.

For the GHS prior of
Li et al., 2019
,
\lambda_{jk}\sim C^{+}(0,1)
, a priori, where
C^{+}
denotes the half Cauchy density. However, introducing one further level of augmentation, one can show:
\lambda_{jk}\sim C^{+}(0,1)
is equivalent to
\lambda_{jk}^{2}\mid a\sim\mathrm{IG}(1/2,1/a)
and
a\sim\mathrm{IG}(1/2,1)
, where IG denotes an inverse gamma random variable in shape–scale parameterization. This leads to Gibbs sampling from the posterior as in
Makalic and Schmidt, 2016
, with
(\lambda^{2}_{jk}\mid\mathrm{rest})
and
(a\mid\mathrm{rest})
both following inverse gamma distributions.

2.

For the BGL prior of
Wang, 2012
, the prior is
\lambda^{2}_{jk}\sim\mathrm{Exponential}(1)
, leading to a posterior
(\lambda^{2}_{jk}\mid\mathrm{rest})
following a generalized inverse Gaussian (GIG) distribution, which can be sampled using the method of
Devroye, 2014
, or some other mechanism.

Similar strategies are available for posterior sampling under the GHSL prior
(
Sagar et al., 2024a
)
, the graphical R2-D2 prior
(
Gan et al., 2022
)
and several others. However, it is important to note that the positive definite constraint on
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
does not affect the sampling of these parameters in a Gibbs step, which is
conditional
on the current
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
. Finally, for the global
\tau
, a standard half-Cauchy prior is typical
(
Li et al., 2019
)
.

2.4
Computational Complexity of
Wang, 2012
and Key Bottleneck

It is clear that computing
\mathbf{C}
in Equation (
7
)
involves inverting a
(p-1)\times(p-1)
matrix, which has complexity
\mathcal{O}(p^{3})
. Alternatives avoiding a direct inverse, such as Algorithm 2.5 of
Rue and Held, 2005
for sampling from a multivariate normal, lead to the same
\mathcal{O}(p^{3})
complexity in this case, since the matrix
\mathbf{C}
is not sparse in general. Since this operation is needed for sampling each column, the overall complexity is
\mathcal{O}(p^{4})
. This is the dominating term, since sampling the
{p}\choose{2}
lower level hyperparameters
\lambda_{jk}
costs
\mathcal{O}(p^{2})
. In fact, as is clear from Equation (
7
), the sampling procedure of
Wang, 2012
only requires the
p\times p
matrix
\mathbf{S}
and does not directly use the
n\times p
data matrix
\mathbf{y}
. Therefore, even if
n\ll p
, it is not possible for
Wang, 2012
to leverage this gainfully. We proceed to propose an alternative parameterization that works with
\mathbf{y}
instead of
\mathbf{S}
, still targeting the true posterior, but resulting in verifiable computational gains.

3
Posterior Sampling using a Reverse Telescoping Block Decomposition

3.1
The Reverse Telescoping Block Decomposition

We start by briefly recounting the telescoping block decomposition of
Bhadra et al., 2024
, as relevant for our purposes. For
{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{p\times p}=\{\theta_{jk}\}
, we seek a corresponding
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{p\times p}=\{\tilde{\theta}_{jk}\}
, such that (a) a one-to-one relationship holds between the elements of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{p\times p}
and
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{p\times p}
, and (b) sampling under the reparameterization
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{p\times p}
is cheaper compared to sampling under the original parameterization.

We seek to factorize the joint density as:
f(\mathbf{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\Theta})=\prod_{j=1}^{p}f(\mathbf{y}_{j}\mid\mathbf{y}_{1:j-1},\boldsymbol{\Theta})
, with a slight abuse of notation that for
j=1
there is no conditioning on any other
{\bf y}_{k},\;k\neq j
. We denote the pseudo-density as the product of node conditional densities, given as:
f_{\mathrm{pseudo}}(\mathbf{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\Theta})=\prod_{j=1}^{p}f(\mathbf{y}_{j}\mid\mathbf{y}_{-j},\boldsymbol{\Theta})
, where
\mathbf{y}_{-j}
denotes the
n\times(p-1)
sub-matrix of
\mathbf{y}
with the
j
th column dropped. Apply the decomposition:

\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{p\times p}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{(p-1)\times(p-1)}&\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\bullet{p}}\\
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\bullet{p}}^{T}&\theta_{pp}\end{bmatrix}.

Recall the notation that
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}:=(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\bullet j},{\theta}_{jj})^{T}
denotes the
j
th column of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j\times j}
.
We have:

\mathbf{y}_{p}\mid\mathbf{y}_{1:p-1},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{p\times p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{=}}\mathbf{y}_{p}\mid\mathbf{y}_{1:p-1},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p}\sim\mathcal{N}(-\mathbf{y}_{1:p-1}{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet p}}/{\theta_{pp}},({1}/{\theta_{pp}})\mathbf{I}_{n}),

(8)

where
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{=}}
denotes equality in distribution. We note two main points:

1.

The partial regression in Equation (
8
) depends only on the last column of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{p\times p}
.

2.

If we denote the Schur complement of
\theta_{pp}
as:

\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{(p-1)\times(p-1)}=\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{(p-1)\times(p-1)}-\frac{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\bullet{p}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\bullet{p}}^{T}}{\theta_{pp}},

(9)

then
(\mathbf{y}_{1:p-1}\mid\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{p\times p})
is a multivariate normal with precision matrix
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{(p-1)\times(p-1)}

(
Lauritzen, 1996
, Appendix C)
. It is instructive to compare our adjustment of Equation (
9
) with
Wang, 2012
’s adjustment of Equation (
6
). The former considers the Schur complement of
\theta_{pp}
, while the latter that of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{(p-1)\times(p-1)}
.

Using Equation (
9
), one can analogously decompose:

\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{(p-1)\times(p-1)}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{(p-2)\times(p-2)}&\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\bullet{(p-1)}}\\
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\bullet{(p-1)}}^{T}&\omega_{p-1,p-1}\end{bmatrix},

and noting that the last column of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{(p-1)\times(p-1)}
depends only on
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p-1}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p}
, write:

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{p-1}:=(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet(p-1)},{\tilde{\theta}}_{p-1,p-1})^{T}=(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\bullet(p-1)},{\omega}_{p-1,p-1})^{T}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{p-1}\mid\mathbf{y}_{1:p-2},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{p\times p}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{=}}\mathbf{y}_{p-1}\mid\mathbf{y}_{1:p-2},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p-1},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{=}}\mathbf{y}_{p-1}\mid\mathbf{y}_{1:p-2},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{p-1}\sim\mathcal{N}(-\mathbf{y}_{1:p-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet(p-1)}/{\tilde{\theta}}_{p-1,p-1},({1}/{\tilde{\theta}}_{p-1,p-1})\mathbf{I}_{n}).

(10)

Iterating the same argument reveals that for each
j
:

(\mathbf{y}_{j}\mid\mathbf{y}_{1:j-1},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{p\times p})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{=}}(\mathbf{y}_{j}\mid\mathbf{y}_{1:j-1},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{=}}(\mathbf{y}_{j}\mid\mathbf{y}_{1:j-1},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{j}),

(11)

with a univariate normal distribution that is i.i.d. over
n
samples, given by
\mathcal{N}(-\mathbf{y}_{1:j-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet j}/{\tilde{\theta}}_{jj},({1}/{\tilde{\theta}}_{jj})\mathbf{I}_{n})
and where for
j=p-1,\ldots,1
we have that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{j}
is a function of
(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})
, of the form:

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{j}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}-\phi(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j+1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p}),

(12)

for some nonlinear function
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{(p-j+1)\times j}\to\mathbb{R}^{j}
,
with the convention
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{p}={\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{p}
. As a consequence it is also true that:

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{j}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}-\gamma(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{j+1},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{p}),

(13)

for another nonlinear function
\gamma:\mathbb{R}^{(p-j+1)\times j}\to\mathbb{R}^{j}
.
We now clarify the mapping between
(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})
and
(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{p})
in more detail. The forward mapping
(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})\mapsto(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{p})
, which recursively performs the Schur complement adjustment of Equation (
9
), was proposed by
Bhadra et al., 2024
and was termed the
telescoping block decomposition
. This is shown in Panel (a) of Algorithm
3
, and was used by
Bhadra et al., 2024
for the purpose of hitherto intractable marginal likelihood calculations under element-wise priors. However, this decomposition is not directly useful for sampling purposes, as evidenced by the
\mathcal{O}(p^{5})
computational complexity of
Bhadra et al., 2024
. Consider now
reversing
this procedure, shown in Panel (b) of Algorithm

, where we seek the inverse mapping
(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{p})\mapsto(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})
. This is the key contribution of the current paper, and a crucial ingredient of our sampling procedure.

Since the original precision matrix
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
is uniquely determined by
(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})
, and the mapping
\boldsymbol{\Theta}\mapsto\boldsymbol{\tilde{\Theta}}
is one-to-one, one may equivalently characterize the likelihood using
\boldsymbol{\tilde{\Theta}}
. In the next section we show it is considerably cheaper to perform posterior simulation under the
\boldsymbol{\tilde{\Theta}}
parameterization. The samples are then converted to recover posterior draws of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
, at the completion of the reverse telescoping adjustment of Panel (b). A somewhat subtle notational issue is worth clarifying.

Remark 2

(The notations
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
vs.
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
)
.

We use the notation
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
and
\boldsymbol{\tilde{\Theta}}
to denote the matrices, including all their submatrices, that remain unchanged throughout the telescoping and reverse telescoping adjustments and all sampling steps; whereas the notations
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
and
\boldsymbol{\tilde{\Omega}}
denote placeholder matrices whose upper-left
(j\times j)
blocks are successively overwritten as the algorithms proceed over
j=(p-1),\ldots,1
. We clarify this distinction using keywords “Set” vs. “Update” in our algorithm description. This allows us to write
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{j}=(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\bullet{j}},\omega_{jj})^{T}
in Step 5 of the forward mapping, whereas
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{j}
is a complicated function of
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{j},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{p}
, as specified in Equation (
12
). The notational justification for the reverse mapping is similar, as is the justification for a lack of explicit functional form for the functions
\phi(\cdot)
and
\gamma(\cdot)
in Equations (
12
)–(
13
).

The factoring
(\mathbf{y}_{j}\mid\mathbf{y}_{1:j-1})
is natural for directed graphs. In contrast, for the undirected model at hand, preserving the true likelihood requires carefully
adjusting out
the precision matrix with respect to the variables being conditioned upon, which was the key challenge addressed by
Bhadra et al., 2024
. Nevertheless, the partial regressions of Equations (
8
) and (
10
) suggest intriguing possibilities for sampling as well, provided the following conditions can be met.

1.

It is possible to derive the induced priors on
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{p}
given a prior on
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{p\times p}
of the form of Equations (
1
)–(
2
).

2.

It is possible to sample from the posterior under the partial regressions of Equations (
8
) and (
10
), that use
\mathbf{y}
and not
\mathbf{S}
.

We proceed to specify the details in the next section. But first, the following clarifications are warranted regarding the distinctions of our partial regression approach with existing partial regression approaches for undirected graphical models.

Remark 3

(Differences with pseudolikelihood and neighborhood selection)
.

A critical point in Equation (
10
) is that the partial regression here is of
\mathbf{y}_{p-1}
on
\mathbf{y}_{1:p-2}
and not of
\mathbf{y}_{p-1}
on
\mathbf{y}_{-(p-1)}:=\mathbf{y}_{1:(p-2)\cup p}
. Therefore, the starting point of Equation (
10
) is the
Schur-complement adjusted
precision matrix
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{(p-1)\times(p-1)}
of Equation (
9
), and not
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{(p-1)\times(p-1)}
, as in partial regression based neighborhood selection approaches
(
Meinshausen and Bühlmann, 2006
)
, which regresses
\mathbf{y}_{j}
on
\mathbf{y}_{-j}
and hence, targets the pseudolikelihood and not the true likelihood. In contrast, in our approach,
\mathbf{y}_{p}
has been
adjusted out
when we consider the precision matrix of
\mathbf{y}_{1:p-1}
, as in iterative proportional scaling
(
Speed and Kiiveri, 1986
)
. Hence, we target the true likelihood, since by Equation (
11
),
f(\mathbf{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{p\times p})=\prod_{j=1}^{p}f(\mathbf{y}_{j}\mid\mathbf{y}_{1:j-1},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{p\times p})=\prod_{j=1}^{p}f(\mathbf{y}_{j}\mid\mathbf{y}_{1:j-1},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})
. However,
f(\mathbf{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{p\times p})\neq f_{\mathrm{pseudo}}(\mathbf{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{p\times p})=\prod_{j=1}^{p}f(\mathbf{y}_{j}\mid\mathbf{y}_{-j},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{p\times p})
. In a partial regression based approach, this difference is critical for targeting the true posterior vs. a pseudo-posterior. The distinction remains present if one replaces the pseudolikelihood with any other composite likelihood.

Remark 4

(Differences with Cholesky factor based partial regression)
.

Pourahmadi, 1999
and
Daniels and Pourahmadi, 2002
consider the model
{\bf y}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
and show that it can be written as a sequence of partial regressions:

{\bf y}_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\phi_{kj}{\bf y}_{k}+\varepsilon_{j};\;\varepsilon_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle ind}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{j}^{2}).

If
\mathbf{T}
is a unit lower triangular matrix with elements
-\phi_{kj}
and
\mathbf{D}=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{j}^{2})
, then
Pourahmadi, 1999
establishes that:

\mathbf{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{T}^{T}=\mathbf{D}.

Consequently,
Pourahmadi, 1999
proceeds to model
(\phi_{kj},\sigma_{j}^{2})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
, with no additional restrictions needed to ensure positive definiteness of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
. While this approach shares the similarity with us that the regression is indeed of
\mathbf{y}_{j}
on
\mathbf{y}_{1:j-1}
, our regression coefficients directly use the elements of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}
and not its Cholesky factor. Thus, we must explicitly enforce positive definiteness of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
. Apart from specific cases, the zero patterns of
\mathbf{T}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
(or their inverses) do not correspond to each other, and hence, a Cholesky-based approach is not useful for directly modeling a sparse
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
, only a sparse Cholesky factor. Another major difference is that since the Cholesky factors imply a directed acyclic graph (DAG) factorization of the likelihood, inference depends on the ordering of the
p
variables. In contrast, the proposed method is invariant to such ordering, for the same reason that iterative proportional scaling is invariant to ordering.

3.2
Posterior Sampling under the Reverse Telescoping Block Decomposition

We start by recalling from Equation (
11
) that the likelihood of
(\mathbf{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\Theta})
can be written as follows:

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\Theta})

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{p}f(\mathbf{y}_{j}\mid\mathbf{y}_{1:j-1},\boldsymbol{\Theta})=\prod_{j=1}^{p}f(\mathbf{y}_{j}\mid\mathbf{y}_{1:j-1},\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}_{j}),

(14)

where
\mathbf{y}_{j}\mid\mathbf{y}_{1:j-1},\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(-\mathbf{y}_{1:j-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet j}/{\tilde{\theta}}_{jj},({1}/{\tilde{\theta}}_{jj})\mathbf{I}_{n})
for
j=2,\ldots,p
and
\mathbf{y}_{1}\mid\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,({1}/{\tilde{\theta}}_{11})\mathbf{I}_{n})
. Recall, we set
\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}_{p}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p}
. For
j=(p-1),\ldots,1
, we proceed to derive the induced priors on
\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}_{j}
according to the prior specification of Equations (
1
)–(
2
). From Equation (
13
), given
\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}_{j+1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}_{p}
, we can express:

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet j}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet j}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\bullet{j}},\hskip 10.00002pt\tilde{\theta}_{jj}=\theta_{jj}-\gamma_{jj},

where
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{j}=(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\bullet{j}},\gamma_{jj})
is a function of
(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}_{j+1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}_{p})
or equivalently, of
(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j+1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})
. Consequently, the conditional prior distributions satisfy for each
j
:

f(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}_{j}\mid\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}_{j+1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}_{p})=f(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{j}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j+1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p}),

since the Jacobian of transformation for this linear shift is 1. Therefore, we can decompose the prior distribution into blocks:

\begin{split}f(\boldsymbol{\Theta})&=\prod_{j=1}^{p}f(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}_{j}\mid\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}_{j+1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}_{p})=\prod_{j=1}^{p}f(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{j}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j+1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p}).\end{split}

(15)

Since both the prior and likelihood can be factorized into blocks associated with the
j
-th column of the upper triangle, the posterior distribution can also be block-wise factorized using Equations (
14
) and (
15
) as:

\begin{split}f(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\mid\mathbf{y})&\propto f(\mathbf{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\Theta})f(\boldsymbol{\Theta})=\prod_{j=1}^{p}f(\mathbf{y}_{j}\mid\mathbf{y}_{1:j-1},\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}_{j})f(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}_{j}\mid\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}_{j+1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}_{p}).\end{split}

Based on this structure, we design a block Gibbs sampler where for each
j=p,\ldots,1
. We first sample
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{j}:=(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet j},{\tilde{\theta}}_{jj})^{T}
conditional on
\mathbf{y}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{j+1},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{p}
. Using this, we reconstruct:

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet j}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet j}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\bullet{j}},\hskip 10.00002pt\theta_{jj}=\tilde{\theta}_{jj}+\gamma_{jj},

and update
(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\bullet{(j-1)}},\gamma_{(j-1)(j-1)})
for use in the next block via the reverse telescoping decomposition. Within each block, the sampling of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{j}:=(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet j},{\tilde{\theta}}_{jj})^{T}
conditional on
\mathbf{y}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{j+1},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{p}
, can be further split into two Gibbs steps:
first sample
\tilde{\theta}_{jj}\mid\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet{j}},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}_{j+1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}_{p}
, and then sample
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet{j}}\mid\tilde{\theta}_{jj},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}_{j+1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}_{p}
. The following three propositions give the respective steps. All proofs are in Supplementary Section
S.1
.

Proposition 1

(Induced priors)
.

Under the joint prior on
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
according to Equations (
1
)–(
2
), we have:
f(\theta_{jj}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j+1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})\propto 1
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\bullet{j}}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j+1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\tau^{2}\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{2}_{\bullet j}))
, or equivalently,
f(\tilde{\theta}_{jj}\mid\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}_{j+1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}_{p})\propto 1
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet{j}}\mid\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}_{j+1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}_{p}\sim\mathcal{N}(-\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\bullet{j}},\tau^{2}\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{2}_{\bullet j}))
, where
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{2}_{\bullet j}
is a vector formed from
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{2}_{j\times j}
as in Equation (
4
).

Proposition 2

(Full conditional posterior for diagonal terms)
.

When
j=1
,
\tilde{\theta}_{11}\mid\mathbf{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}_{j+1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}_{p}\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(\mathrm{shape}=n/2+1,\;\mathrm{rate}=\mathbf{y}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{y}_{1}/2)
. When
j>1
,
\tilde{\theta}_{jj}\mid\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet{j}},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}_{j+1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}_{p}\sim\mathrm{GIG}(\lambda=n/2+1,\;\chi=(\mathbf{y}_{1:(j-1)}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet{j}})^{T}(\mathbf{y}_{1:(j-1)}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet{j}}),\;\psi=\mathbf{y}_{j}^{T}\mathbf{y}_{j})
, where
X\sim\mathrm{GIG}(\lambda,\chi,\psi)
denotes a generalized inverse Gaussian random variable with pdf on
(0,\infty)
given as:
f(x\mid\lambda,\chi,\psi)=\left[(\psi/\chi)^{\lambda/2}/\{2K_{\lambda}(\sqrt{\chi\psi})\}\right]x^{\lambda-1}\exp\left\{-\left(\chi x^{-1}+\psi x\right)/2\right\};\;\lambda\in\mathbb{R},\chi>0,\psi>0,

where
K_{\lambda}(\cdot)
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind with index
\lambda
.

Proposition 3

(Full conditional posterior for off-diagonal terms)
.

The off-diagonal terms
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet{j}}
follow multivariate normal distributions, of the form:

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet{j}}\mid\mathbf{y}_{1:j},\tilde{\theta}_{jj},\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\bullet{j}},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{2}_{\bullet j}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\{\mathbf{X}_{j}^{T}\mathbf{X}_{j}+\tau^{-2}\tilde{\theta}_{jj}\mathrm{diag}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{2}_{\bullet j})\}^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{j}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{j}\tilde{\theta}_{jj}^{1/2}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\bullet{j}},\;\{\mathbf{X}_{j}^{T}\mathbf{X}_{j}+\tau^{-2}\tilde{\theta}_{jj}\mathrm{diag}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{2}_{\bullet j})\}^{-1}\tilde{\theta}_{jj}\right),

where
\mathbf{X}_{j}=-\mathbf{y}_{1:(j-1)}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{j}=\mathbf{y}_{j}\tilde{\theta}_{jj}^{1/2}-\mathbf{y}_{1:(j-1)}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\bullet{j}}/\tilde{\theta}_{jj}^{1/2}
. Further, sampling from this distribution is possible using the method of
Bhattacharya et al., 2016
, with complexity
O(n^{2}p)
.

For completeness, we summarize the algorithm of
Bhattacharya et al., 2016
in Supplementary Section
S.2
. We also include succinct description of all steps of the reverse telescoping sampler, contrasting it with the cyclical sampler of
Wang, 2012
.

3.3
Computational Complexity

The computational complexity of the sampler of
Bhattacharya et al., 2016
is
\mathcal{O}(\max{(pn^{2},n^{3})})
for each
j=1,\ldots,p
. Therefore the overall complexity of sampling is
\mathcal{O}(\max{(p^{2}n^{2},pn^{3})})
. The complexity of the reverse telescoping updates in Steps 3–5 of Algorithm
3
is
\mathcal{O}(p^{3})
. This gives an overall complexity for the reverse telescoping sampler as
\mathcal{O}(\max{(n^{2}p^{2},pn^{3},p^{3})})
, which is
\mathcal{O}(p^{3})
when
n=o(p^{1/2}).

4
Numerical Experiments

We compare the proposed reverse telescoping (RT) MCMC sampler with the cyclical MCMC sampler under the graphical horseshoe
(
Li et al., 2019
, GHS,)
and graphical horseshoe-like
(
Sagar et al., 2024a
, GHSL,)
priors in terms of estimation results and computational efficiency. To ensure
n=o(p^{1/2})
, we set
n\approx 24\log p
for
p=100,150,200,250,300
; resulting in the following problem dimensions:
(n,p)\in\{(110,100),(120,150),(127,200),(132,250),(137,300)\}
. Recall the theory suggests in this regime, the RT sampler achieves
\mathcal{O}(\max\{n^{2}p^{2},p^{3}\})=\mathcal{O}(p^{3})
per-iteration complexity, whereas the complexity of the cyclical sampler remains
\mathcal{O}(p^{4})
.

The true precision matrix
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0}
is set as in
Li et al., 2019
and
Sagar et al., 2024b
. The diagonal elements are set to one and the off-diagonal elements are set to follow the
cliques positive
structure, which means the
p
rows and columns are partitioned into
K
disjoint groups
\{\mathbf{G}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
and we select 3 members within each group,
g_{k}\subset\mathbf{G}_{k}
. the off-diagonal entry is set to
-0.45
(corresponding to positive partial correlations) if
i\neq j
and
i,j\in g_{k}
, and
0
otherwise. we set
K=p/10
. The description and results for three other structures we consider (
tridiagonal
,
hubs
and
cliques negative
) and trace plots to assess MCMC mixing are deferred to Supplementary Section
S.3
.

For each
(n,p)
pair, we draw
50
replicate data sets from
\mathcal{N}_{p}(0,\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0}^{-1})
. Each MCMC run consists of
10,000
iterations with the first
5,000
discarded as burn-in and uses a single 2.0 GHz AMD EPYC Rome CPU core with 16 GB of memory, and a maximum run time limit of 4 hours. Our de novo implementation of the RT sampler is in
Rcpp
with underlying C++ code, whereas the existing
R
implementations of the cyclical sampler for GHS
(
Srivastava and Bhadra, 2018
)
and GHSL
(
Sagar et al., 2024a
)
are used. Given this difference in implementation, we take particular care to clarify the basis of our runtime comparison in the subsequent parts. The standard half-Cauchy prior is used on the global parameter
\tau
for both GHS and GHSL.

\boldsymbol{\Theta}

\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\|_{F}
under both samplers, (ii)
\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}-\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0}\|_{F}
under both samplers, and (iii)
\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{\mathrm{RT}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{\mathrm{Cyclical}}\|_{F}
, where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
denotes the Frobenius norm. Tables

and
2
report means, variances, and the 25%, 50%, 75% empirical quartiles averaged over the
50

(n,p)
settings.
Similarly, the deviations from the truth show negligible differences between the two samplers.
Moreover, the pairwise deviations between RT and the baseline sampler remain small for all
(n,p)

Given that the RT and cyclical samplers target the same posterior under two different reparameterizations, these results are expected, and indeed a statistically significant difference would indicate a problem with either or both the samplers.

6
Conclusions and Future Works

The objective of the current work is to present a reverse telescoping sampling scheme under element-wise graphical priors, of the form of Equations (
1
)–(
2
). Although for demonstration purposes we choose two such priors, the graphical horseshoe and the graphical horseshoe-like, the proposed sampler is generically applicable to all element-wise priors of the above form. In the high-dimensional regime
n=o(p^{1/2})
, the proposed approach attains a per iteration complexity of
\mathcal{O}(p^{3})
, providing a one order-of-magnitude improvement over the
\mathcal{O}(p^{4})
cyclical sampler. Considerable numerical results are presented to support our claim of improved algorithmic scaling.

There are several avenues of future investigations where the framework outlined in the current work may prove fruitful. Application to evidence estimation, following
Bhadra et al., 2024
, should be an immediate consequence. Extensions of the graphical horseshoe to multiple connected graphs have seen considerable activities in the recent past and approaches such as the
multiple graphical horseshoe

(
Busatto and Stingo, 2025
)
and
joint graphical horseshoe

(
Lingjærde et al., 2024
)
have been proposed. Application of the proposed methodology to alleviate the computational burden associated with sampling multiple graphs seems feasible in all these cases as well.

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{y}_{1:(j-1)}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet{j}}/\tilde{\theta}_{jj}+\epsilon_{j};\;\epsilon_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1/\tilde{\theta}_{jj}\mathbf{I}_{n}),

\displaystyle\text{i.e.,\; }\mathbf{y}_{j}\tilde{\theta}_{jj}^{1/2}-\mathbf{y}_{1:(j-1)}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\bullet{j}}/\tilde{\theta}_{jj}^{1/2}

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{y}_{1:(j-1)}\{(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet{j}}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\bullet{j}})/\tilde{\theta}_{jj}^{1/2}\}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{j};\;\tilde{\epsilon}_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}_{n}).

Let
\mathbf{X}_{j}=-\mathbf{y}_{1:(j-1)}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{j}=\mathbf{y}_{j}\tilde{\theta}_{jj}^{1/2}-\mathbf{y}_{1:(j-1)}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\bullet{j}}/\tilde{\theta}_{jj}^{1/2}
. Then the above regression is of the form:

\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{j}=\mathbf{X}_{j}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{j}.

Standard calculations now yield that the posterior be
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}
is:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}\mid\mathbf{y}_{1:j},\tilde{\theta}_{jj},\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\bullet{j}},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{2}_{\bullet j}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\{\mathbf{X}_{j}^{T}\mathbf{X}_{j}+\tau^{-2}\tilde{\theta}_{jj}\mathrm{diag}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{2}_{\bullet j})\}^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{j}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{j},\;\{\mathbf{X}_{j}^{T}\mathbf{X}_{j}+\tau^{-2}\tilde{\theta}_{jj}\mathrm{diag}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{2}_{\bullet j})\}^{-1}\right),

(S.2)

and noting the relation:
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet{j}}=\tilde{\theta}_{jj}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\bullet{j}}
, gives the desired posterior as:

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet{j}}\mid\mathbf{y}_{1:j},\tilde{\theta}_{jj},\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\bullet{j}},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{2}_{\bullet j}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\{\mathbf{X}_{j}^{T}\mathbf{X}_{j}+\tau^{-2}\tilde{\theta}_{jj}\mathrm{diag}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{2}_{\bullet j})\}^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{j}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{j}\tilde{\theta}_{jj}^{1/2}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\bullet{j}},\;\{\mathbf{X}_{j}^{T}\mathbf{X}_{j}+\tau^{-2}\tilde{\theta}_{jj}\mathrm{diag}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{2}_{\bullet j})\}^{-1}\tilde{\theta}_{jj}\right),

completing the proof. Algorithm
S.1
, due to
Bhattacharya et al., 2016
, provides an approach for sampling from posteriors of the form of Equation (
S.2
), which we outline in the next section for the sake of completeness.

S.2
Supplementary Algorithms

S.2.1
Bhattacharya et al., 2016
’s Algorithm for Linear Regression

For
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
{{\bf X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
, consider a linear regression model of the form:

\mathbf{z}={{\bf X}}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon},

where, a priori,
\boldsymbol{\beta}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,{\rm diag}{(\lambda_{j}^{2})})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}_{n})
. Standard calculations yield that the posterior of
\beta
in this model is:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid{\bf X},\mathbf{z},\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{2}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\{{\bf X}^{T}{\bf X}+{\rm diag}^{-1}{(\lambda_{j}^{2})}\}^{-1}{\bf X}^{T}\mathbf{z},\;\{{\bf X}^{T}{\bf X}+{\rm diag}^{-1}{(\lambda_{j}^{2})}\}^{-1}\right).

(S.3)

Direct simulation from this distribution requires an inversion of
({\bf X}^{T}{\bf X}+{\rm diag}{(\lambda_{j}^{2})})\in\mathcal{M}_{p}^{+}
, which costs
\mathcal{O}(p^{3})
. Algorithm
S.1
, due to
Bhattacharya et al., 2016
, provides an alternative exact sampler from this posterior that costs
\mathcal{O}(\max({n^{2}p},n^{3}))
, where the basic idea in Step 3 is to replace the
p\times p
matrix inverse by a solution of an
n\times n
linear system of equations, which is beneficial when
p\gg n
, and the prior covariance of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is diagonal, or at least, is such that its Cholesky decomposition costs lower than
\mathcal{O}(p^{3})
.

Algorithm S.1

FastGaussian (
{\bf X},\mathbf{z},\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{2}
): A sampler for
\boldsymbol{\beta}
of the form of Equation (
S.3
)

1:

Sample
\mathbf{u}\sim\mathcal{N}_{p}(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{2})
and
\boldsymbol{\delta}\sim\mathcal{N}_{n}(0,\mathbf{I}_{n})
independently, where
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{2}=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{j}^{2})

2:

Set
\mathbf{v}={{\bf X}}\mathbf{u}+\boldsymbol{\delta}
.
\triangleright\,\mathcal{O}(np)

3:

Solve
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
from
({{\bf X}}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{2}{{\bf X}}^{T}+\mathbf{I}_{n})\boldsymbol{\zeta}=\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{v}
.
\triangleright\,\mathcal{O}(\max({n^{2}p},n^{3}))

4:

Return
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\mathbf{u}+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{2}{{\bf X}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\zeta}.

\triangleright\,\mathcal{O}(np)

S.3
Additional Numerical Experiments

We provide additional numerical results under other structures where
the true precision matrix
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0}
is set as in
Li et al., 2019
and
Sagar et al., 2024b
. The diagonal elements are set to one and the off-diagonal elements have one of the following structures.

1.

Tridiagonal.
The precision matrix
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0}
is constructed to have nonzero entries only on the main diagonal and the first sub- and super-diagonals. Specifically,
\omega_{ij}=0.25
if
|i-j|=1
, and
\omega_{ii}=1
for all
i=1,\ldots,p
.

2.

Hubs.
The
p
rows and columns are partitioned into
K
disjoint groups
\{\mathbf{G}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
. For
i\neq j
, the off-diagonal entry is set to
0.25
if
i
and
j
belong to the same group, and
0
otherwise. In our simulations, we set
K=p/10
, with each group containing the same number of nonzero elements.

3.

Cliques negative.
The
p
rows and columns are partitioned into
K
disjoint groups. We select 3 members within each group,
g_{k}\subset\mathbf{G}_{k}
. the off-diagonal entry is set to
0.75
(corresponding to negative partial correlations) if
i\neq j
and
i,j\in g_{k}
, and
0
otherwise. we set
K=p/10
.

The results under GHS and GHSL priors are summarized in the next two subsections, and are consistent with the findings reported in the main manuscript, that is, the RT sampler is computationally as accurate as the cyclical sampler while being faster in both relative and absolute terms.
