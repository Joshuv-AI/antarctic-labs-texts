Greater exposure to PM2.5 and PM10 is associated with a higher BAD score: the Maastricht study
Authors: Magali M S Vandevenne, Sara B A Mokhtar, Hans Bosma, Carroll A B Webers, Rudy M M A Nuijts, Tos T J M Berendschot
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo investigate the associations between exposure to ambient air pollutants and the Belin/Ambrosio Enhanced Ectasia score (BAD score), a tomographic parameter for identifying corneas at risk for ectasia. This prospective cross-sectional study uses data from the Maastricht Study, a population-based cohort of adults aged 40–75 years living in and around Maastricht, the Netherlands. Participants underwent corneal tomography with a Pentacam HR. The BADD score was used to assess keratoconus risk. The annual average concentrations of air pollutants—PM2.5, PM10, nitrogen dioxide (NO₂), and soot—from the Geoscience and Health Cohort Consortium (GECCO) were used. Associations between standardized Z-scores of air pollutants and the BADD score were analysed via multiple linear regression models, adjusting for potential confounders, including age, sex, education, glucose metabolism status, smoking, and alcohol consumption. Data from 6343 eyes were included. Higher concentrations of PM2.5, PM10, and soot were significantly associated with higher BADD scores, indicating an increased risk of keratoconus [PM2.5: β = 0.03, 95% CI: 0.01–0.05; PM10: β = 0.03, 95% CI: 0.01–0.05; soot: β = 0.03, 95% CI: 0.00–0.04]. No significant association was found for NO₂ [stβ (95% CI): NO2 0.01 (-0.02; 0.03)] (p = 0.609). We found a significant influence of type 2 diabetes on the associations between PM2.5 or PM10 and the BADD score (p = 0.037 and p = 0.041, respectively). Our study revealed that exposure to relatively high levels of PM2.5, PM10, and soot are associated with relatively high BADD scores. Our results support the following hypotheses: air pollution is a risk factor for keratoconus, and T2DM may have a potential protective effect on the development of keratoconus.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-27471-2.Keywords: Keratoconus, Air pollution, Particulate matter, Tomography, BADD score, Pentacam, Diabetes mellitus, EpidemiologySubject terms: Diseases, Environmental sciences, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Over the past decade, there has been a significant increase in air pollution levels, marked by the presence of a lot of harmful substances in the atmosphere1. The known pollutants causing public health issues are particulate matter (PM), which are microscopic particles of solid or liquid material suspended in the air; nitrogen dioxide (NO2), which is generated during the combustion of fossil fuels such as coal, oil, gas, or diesel at high temperatures; and soot, which consists mainly of carbon particles resulting from the incomplete combustion of raw materials such as internal combustion engine emissions or forest fires2,3. Exposure to these air pollutants has a significant impact on our health3, increasing the risk of respiratory tract issues, cardiovascular diseases, ocular diseases, neurological disorders, cancer and mortality4–7.

Keratoconus is a bilateral, asymmetrical, progressive disease of the cornea characterized by thinning and bulging of the cornea, leading to a typical cone-shaped cornea with irregular astigmatism and a decrease in visual acuity8. The disease usually starts during puberty and affects young, active people9. Its prevalence worldwide shows wide disparity10, being lower in Europe and North America11–14 than in the Middle East and Asia15–19. These differences may be explained by several factors, including genetics and the environment. The diagnosis of keratoconus is based on clinical examination and tomography. However, in the early stages of the disease, there are no clinical signs, so we can rely only on tomography20. One of the parameters for the early detection of keratoconus is the Belin/Ambrosio Enhanced Ectasia Display (BAD)21, which identifies corneas at risk and early keratoconus using 5 tomographic parameters in a regression analysis22,23. The 5 parameters are as follows: change in anterior elevation from the standard to the enhanced reference surface (Df), change in posterior elevation (Db), corneal thickness at the thinnest point (Dt), thinnest point displacement (Da), and pachymetric progression (Dp). Each parameter is described as a standard deviation based on comparison with a large published normative database24,25. Finally, the overall Belin/Ambrosio Enhanced Ectasia Display “D”-score (BADD score) is based on a regression analysis of these parameters22,23. The goal of screening is to detect patients at risk and in an early stage of keratoconus.

The three most commonly cited risk factors for keratoconus are (1) eye rubbing, (2) atopy and (3) ultraviolet (UV) rays26,27. Air pollution is a risk factor cited in only a few articles and epidemiological evidence is lacking28–30. Therefore, in this study, we investigated the associations between ambient air pollutants (PM, NO2, and soot) and BADD scores via data from a large cohort study.

Methods

Study population

The data utilized in this study were obtained from The Maastricht Study, an ongoing observational prospective population-based cohort investigation. Comprehensive details regarding the study’s rationale and methodology have been previously documented31. In essence, this study focused on exploring the etiology, pathophysiology, complications, and comorbidities associated with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), using an extensive phenotyping approach. The inclusion criteria were individuals aged between 40 and 75 years, who were living in the southern region of the Netherlands. Recruitment strategies included mass media campaigns, solicitations via municipal registries, and outreach through the regional Diabetes Patient Registry. Notably, recruitment was stratified based on known T2DM status, with deliberate oversampling of individuals with T2DM for increased efficiency. The current report comprises cross-sectional data from 9187 participants who underwent baseline assessments spanning from November 2010 to October 2020. All examinations were conducted within a three-month time frame per participant. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the institutional medical ethical committee (NL31329.068.10) and the Minister of Health, Welfare and Sports of the Netherlands (Permit 131088-105234-PG) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion.

Assessment of BADD score

All participants underwent a tomography measurement with a Pentacam HR (Oculus Optikgeräte GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany). Both eyes were measured at baseline. The data were collected between November 2011 and October 2020. The measurements were performed by trained investigators according to a standard operation procedure. Only measurements of acceptable quality were included in the database. The quality of the measurements was determined by the Pentacam device, indicated by ‘OK’32.

The BADD score is defined as the standard deviation. The Pentacam device has cut-off values, if the BADD score is smaller than or equal to 1.6, between 1.6 and 3 or equal or greater than 3, the latter two indicating a deviation from the normative database and thus the risk of keratoconus.

Assessment of air pollution

We used the air pollution data provided by the Geoscience and Health Cohort Consortium (GECCO)2. The data are estimates of yearly, address-level, average amounts of ambient air pollutants, and detailed information on the assessment of exposure is described by Lakerveld et al.33 We evaluated the following pollutants: particulate matter (PM), nitrogen dioxide (NO2), and soot. PM is categorized as PM2.5 fine particles with aerodynamic diameters less than 2.5 μm and PM10 coarse particles with aerodynamic diameters less than 10 μm. For the level of soot, the reflectance of PM2.5 filters, which is a proxy for elemental carbon, was assessed. In the GECCO database, estimated concentrations of PM2.5, PM10, and soot were available for the years 2013–2020 and of NO2 from 2014 to 2020. The estimated annual concentrations of air pollutants were linked to the home address of the participants at baseline.

Covariates

Glucose metabolism status was determined according to the WHO 2006 criteria and was based on a 75-gram oral glucose tolerance test and the use of glucose-lowering medications: normal glucose metabolism, prediabetes, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and type 1 diabetes mellitus. The following covariates were determined via a questionnaire: education level (low, intermediate, high), alcohol use (none, low, high), and smoking status (never, former, current).

Statistical analyses

We used multiple linear regression analyses to investigate the associations of Z-scores for PM2.5, PM10, NO2, and soot with the BADD score. The analyses were performed with the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 28.0 (IBM SPSS, IBM Corp, NY, USA). A p-value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. All associations are expressed as standardized regression coefficients and the corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI).

If BADD scores were available for both eyes of a participant, one eye was chosen at random for inclusion in the study. This is necessary since the right and left eyes of a subject are correlated, which violates the assumption of statistical tests that samples are independent.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the characteristics of the study population. Assumptions of normality and linearity were inspected visually with residual plots, histograms, and Q-Q plots.

For the analysis, we developed three different models. Model 1 is a crude model. Model 2 adjusts for age, sex, education level, and glucose metabolism status. Model 3 additionally adjusts for smoking and alcohol consumption. We performed additional analyses to test for interactions with sex and glucose metabolism status by adding interaction terms to model 3.

Discussion

In this population-based cross-sectional study, we found that higher concentrations of the air pollutants PM2.5, PM10 and soot were associated with lower BADD scores. These findings confirm early hypotheses that air pollution plays a role in keratoconus30. We did not find an association between NO2 and the BADD score. In the additional analyses, we observed that higher air pollutant concentrations were not associated with BADD scores in participants with T2DM. These results are also in line with earlier findings that diabetes could have a protective effect on the development of keratoconus34–36.

Previous studies have reported a possible association between air pollution and keratoconus28,29. Jurkiewicz et al. investigated the correlation between the prevalence of keratoconus, based on epidemiological studies and air pollution rates available from World Health Association (WHO) data30. They found a positive correlation between PM2.5 and PM10 and the incidence of keratoconus, which is consistent with our findings.

The exact pathophysiology is not understood. An important function of the cornea is protection of the eye37. The epithelium, with its tight junctions, acts as a barrier and prevents foreign substances from entering the eye. Air pollution particles are in direct contact with the cornea and can have an indirect and direct impact30. The particles can destabilize the tear film and thus negatively impact its quality. This in turn leads to irritation of the eye and even dry eye, triggering eye rubbing, which is a known risk factor for keratoconus38,39. Balasubramanian et al. showed that one minute of eye rubbing could increase inflammatory particles in tears40. Sharma et al. hypothesized that eye rubbing and inflammation are involved in the pathogenesis and progression of keratoconus41. This hypothesis was examined by Shetty et al., they inhibited inflammatory particles, more specifically, metalloproteinase-9, interleukin-6, and tumor necrosis factor-α, which in turn lead to halting disease progression42. A study from Tan et al. in mice models revealed changes in the ocular surface, similar to dry eye, after direct exposure to PM2.543. They reported that the number of epithelial microvilli was significantly reduced. In addition, apoptosis was induced in the superficial and basal corneal epithelium but not in the stroma. A recent paper shows the impact of air pollution exposure on corneal nerve density44. Their results show that exposure to higher levels of PM2.5 and PM10 is linked to increased corneal neurodegeneration, as indicated by reduced corneal nerve measures. These findings imply that air pollution may play a role in corneal neurodegeneration and adversely affect ocular surface health.

Previous studies on the association between keratoconus and diabetes have reported conflicting findings45–47. Both positive and negative (protective) associations have been described36,48. Another study suggested that diabetes is not associated with a diagnosis of keratoconus but that having diabetes decreases the odds of having more severe keratoconus35. The hypothesis is that advanced glycation end products in diabetic corneas cause increased non-enzymatic cross-linking of the corneal tissue, resulting in greater stiffness49.

In line with studies that found a positive association between keratoconus and diabetes. We found in the supplemental analyses that the air pollutants PM2.5 and PM10 did not have an effect on participants with prediabetes and T2DM. In other words, participants with prediabetes and T2DM did not have a higher BADD score when exposed to higher levels of PM2.5 and PM10.

Our findings indicate no association between NO2 exposure and the BADD score. This could be due to the distinct structures and mechanisms by which particulate matter and soot impact the corneal surface. Additional research is needed to validate these associations. Many different parameters are used to diagnose keratoconus. However, not all parameters are able to detect subclinical keratoconus. For example, the central keratoconus index (CKI) and keratoconus index (KI) are valuable parameters for diagnosing and following-up frank keratoconus; however, they are less reliable in corneas at risk50. Since our cohort included individuals aged between 40 and 75 years living in the southern region of the Netherlands, there is a need for a sensitive parameter to detect keratoconus and corneas at risk. The BADD score is a good parameter for investigating the risk of keratoconus in this population-based cohort since it is sensitive for detecting corneas at risk and subclinical keratoconus50.

Our study had several strengths. To our knowledge, this is the first population-based study to determine the association between air pollution and the BADD score, which is an indicator of keratoconus. Unlike previous studies, which were based on epidemiological studies, we were able to correct for different confounding factors and even perform additional analyses. Finally, we compared the associations between determinants and outcomes across various characteristics and units by computing their Z-scores.

Our study also has several limitations. The first is its cross-sectional design, associations do not imply causality. However, we adjusted for different potential confounders, making our findings more plausible. Owing to the design, we were not able to investigate whether long-term exposure increases the risk or whether higher levels of air pollution lead to more severe keratoconus. To confirm our findings, a longitudinal study should be performed, preferably in a younger cohort, approximately 16 years old to 50 years old, since keratoconus develops, progresses and stabilizes in this age range9. Our study cohort included only people from a certain area in the Netherlands, possibly underestimating the associations because of the limited range of air pollution data. Moreover, the included population makes it more difficult to generalize our results to other populations. Finally, the associations in the prediabetes group (Table 3) were defined based on the results from the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT); however, according to the WHO, the test needs to be performed three times. In this study cohort the OGTT was performed only once. This could increase the risk of misclassification of participants who belong to the normal group or diabetes group. Thus, the interpretation of the results in this group must be performed with caution.

In Maastricht, where our study was conducted, the average concentrations of PM2.5 and PM10 (12.2 and 19.0 µg/m3, respectively) are below EU thresholds (25 and 40 µg/m3, respectively) but they exceed WHO-recommended levels (5 and 15 µg/m3, respectively)3,51. Our results highlight the importance of lowering the current European thresholds and improving air quality since the suggested thresholds are insufficient to protect our health.

In conclusion, our study showed that exposure to higher levels of PM2.5, PM10, and soot is associated with a higher BADD score. Our results support the hypothesis that air pollution is a risk factor for keratoconus.
