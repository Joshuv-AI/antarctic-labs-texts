Efficacy and safety of anticoagulation dose reduction for venous thromboembolism prophylaxis in low body weight patients: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ian Gabriel Alparaque Juyad, Rochelle Ivy A Cion, Genquen Philip Carado, Jerahmeel Aleson L Mapili, Deonne Thaddeus V Gauiran, Elaine B Alajar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVenous thromboembolism (VTE) is a common complication among hospitalized patients, hence VTE prophylaxis is essential. Low body weight patients are at an increased risk of bleeding however there are no guideline recommendations regarding dose adjustment in this population. We performed a meta-analysis to determine whether dose reduction for VTE prophylaxis in low-body weight patients reduces the risk of bleeding, without compromising its efficacy.MethodsWe searched electronic databases from inception to October 2024. We did a random-effects meta-analysis using RevMan 5.4. Data were pooled to obtain a risk ratio (RR) with 95% confidence interval (CI). Heterogeneity and publication bias were assessed with statistic and funnel plot, respectively. Risk of bias was assessed using ROBINS-I and certainty of evidence was assessed using GRADE.ResultsSeven observational studies with 5,575 individuals were included. Pooled analyses revealed no significant difference in risk of total bleeding [RR 0.78, 95% CI 0.48–1.26] and major bleeding [RR 0.62, 95% CI 0.33–1.15] with reduced dose compared to standard dose anticoagulation, however with high heterogeneity (I2 = 64%). Excluding studies with baseline characteristics increasing risk of bleeding in the reduced dose group, sensitivity analysis showed that dose reduction significantly decreases the risk of total [RR 0.49, 95% CI 0.30–0.82] and major bleeding [RR 0.42, 95% CI 0.22–0.80], with low heterogeneity (I = 0). In terms of efficacy, there is not enough evidence to show that anticoagulation dose reduction increases risk of VTE [RR 0.74, 95% CI 0.32–1.73].ConclusionAmong low body weight patients, dose reduction for VTE prophylaxis may reduce risk of bleeding, especially major bleeding, without compromising its efficacy in preventing VTE, hence it can be considered in this population. Randomized controlled trials are needed to further support this recommendation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12959-025-00806-9.Keywords: Anticoagulation, Venous thromboembolism prophylaxis, Dose reduction

Introduction

Venous thromboembolism (VTE), which includes deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), is a common complication among hospitalized patients that leads to significant in-hospital morbidity and mortality. In the US, VTE occurred in 1.2% of all admissions. Among those with VTE, there was a more than twofold increase in the risk of 180-day mortality and a more than threefold increase in the risk of readmission compared to those hospitalizations wherein VTE did not occur [1] Further, the risk of developing VTE is especially higher in patients with advanced age, active malignancy, acute infections, reduced mobility, and recent surgery or trauma [2]. VTE prophylaxis is then essential particularly in these patients. In a multicenter registry study, it was shown that failure to initiate thromboprophylaxis in the first 24 h of intensive care unit admission is associated with higher mortality [3].

Options for VTE prophylaxis include mechanical prophylaxis (e.g. pneumatic compression devices) and pharmacologic prophylaxis, i.e. anticoagulation, with the latter showing more robust evidence across different studies [4, 5] Anticoagulants often employed for thrombophylaxis include low molecular weight heparin (LMWH), unfractionated heparin (UFH), and direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs). In the US, LMWH, specifically enoxaparin, is the most utilized agent, in over 70% of cases [6]. A recent meta-analysis also demonstrated that LMWH had a more favorable risk-benefit ratio over UFH and DOACs [7]. Hence in line with this, the American Society of Hematology recommends pharmacological prophylaxis over mechanical prophylaxis, and more specifically LMWH over UFH and DOACs in acutely or critically ill medical patients for VTE prophylaxis [8].

An important complication of anticoagulation however is bleeding, which can occur in up to 7.2% of hospitalized patients on anticoagulants, with up to 78.5% of these bleeding events considered as major bleeding events [9]. Moreover, certain populations are at an increased risk of bleeding from anticoagulant therapy, which includes the elderly, those with certain comorbidities, such as hypertension and liver disease, and those with impaired creatinine clearance [10, 11] Low body weight has also been frequently associated with increased bleeding risk and mortality for patients on anticoagulant therapy [12-14]. This risk has been attributed in one study to the inverse correlation of anti-Xa activity and weight for patients on prophylactic enoxaparin, with a significant rise of anti-Xa activity as body weight decreases [15]. As such, dose adjustment may be necessary to reduce bleeding risk in these instances.

Guidelines on parenteral anticoagulation dosing for VTE prophylaxis have been established in the general population, as well as in those with severe renal impairment and with obesity. Manufacturer recommendations and clinical trials prescribe the standard dose of enoxaparin of 40 mg subcutaneously (SC) once daily, with renal dose adjustment in patients with creatinine clearance of less than 30 mL/min to a reduced dose of 30 mg SC once daily for VTE prophylaxis [16, 17] In obese patients, an increased dose of enoxaparin of 60 mg SC once daily or 40 mg SC twice daily has shown to decrease incidence of VTE, without an increase in bleeding risk [18, 19].

Literature regarding anticoagulation dose adjustment for low body weight or underweight patients however is lacking. To date, there are no clinical trials that have been done to determine whether a reduced dose of anticoagulation reduces bleeding risk, despite this population being at high risk for bleeding. Several observational studies have been employed in this regard but have non-significant results due to the limitations of sample size and the rare outcome of bleeding [20, 21] At present, there are still no guidelines for clinicians regarding appropriate prophylactic anticoagulant dosing for low body weight patients. This study was then designed to determine whether a reduced dose of anticoagulation for VTE prophylaxis decreases the risk of bleeding among patients with low body weight.

Objectives

A systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted to determine the efficacy and safety of a reduced dose of parenteral anticoagulation for VTE prophylaxis among medically ill hospitalized patients with low body weight or underweight. Specifically, it sought the following:

Safety in terms of total bleeding events, which includes major bleeding and clinically relevant non-major bleeding.

Safety in terms of total bleeding events, which includes major bleeding and clinically relevant non-major bleeding.

Efficacy in terms of prevention of VTE, which includes DVT and pulmonary embolism.

Methods

Study eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria for eligible studies were as follows: (1) all observational studies and randomized control trials, (2) involving adults aged 18 years old and above with low-body weight defined as either ≤ 60 kg or a BMI less than 18.5, (3) with the following exposures: parenteral anticoagulation at a reduced dose for the experimental group and the standard dose for the control group; and (4) with the outcome of bleeding events, whether major or minor, as well as documented VTE.

Candidate studies were then excluded based on the following criteria (1) studies such as case report or series, editorials, reviews, commentaries, or those reported only in abstract form; (2) animal studies, (3) duplicate publications, and (4) studies where data cannot be extracted.

Literature search strategy

The meta-analysis was conducted according to the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines. Electronic databases including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library were searched independently from inception to September 2024 by two investigators (IJ and GC). The following search terms were used: “low body weight”, “underweight”, “parenteral anticoagulation”, “venous thromboembolism prophylaxis”, “heparin”, “low molecular weight heparin”, “enoxaparin”, “tinzaparin”, “nadroparin”, “dalteparin” as free text and/or as MeSH terms. In addition, references of the acquired articles were manually searched for additional studies.

After the initial electronic search, the two investigators IJ and GC independently reviewed for duplicates to complete the identification phase. The screening phase was composed of reviewing titles and abstracts only. Studies were excluded during this screening phase if they do not meet the inclusion criteria. Studies which meet the inclusion criteria but are identified to have at least one exclusion criteria were still included in the eligibility phase for the full-text review. After screening for titles and abstracts, a list of eligible studies underwent a full-text review. Studies which met the inclusion and have not met the exclusion criteria were included in the final meta-analysis.

Any discrepancy between the independent literature searches was resolved by discussion to reach consensus. Consultation with the third and fourth investigator (RC and JM) was made in case these discrepancies are not resolved by consensus.

Data extraction and critical appraisal

The following information were extracted from eligible studies (1) article details (authors, year of publication, study design, sample size), (2) baseline characteristics of included participants (age, sex, weight or BMI, medication history, comorbidities), (3) type, dose, and duration of anticoagulant, (4) baseline laboratory values such as creatinine, hemoglobin, platelet count, coagulation parameters, and (5) outcomes (bleeding and thrombosis).

The risk of bias was independently assessed by two investigators (IJ and RC) using ROBINS-I for non-randomized studies. The risk of bias was displayed in table form from low, moderate, serious, and critical risk. An explanation for the risk of bias was also provided.

Overall confidence in the estimates for each outcome were independently assessed by two investigators (IJ and RC) based on the Grades of Recommendation, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) Working Group system for limitations in study design, evidence directness, consistency, precision of results and publication bias.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Review Manager (V.5.4 Cochrane Collaboration, London, United Kingdom) was used to perform statistical analyses for dichotomous outcomes. The results of each study were presented in the Forest plots, with summary statistics, 95% confidence intervals, and relative weights represented by the middle of the square, the horizontal line, and the relative size of the square, respectively. Heterogeneity was assessed through I2 statistic wherein a value of less than 25% suggested strong homogeneity, 25–50% moderate homogeneity, and > 50% high heterogeneity. In cases where high heterogeneity was noted, sensitivity analysis was done. In this meta-analysis, the investigators used a random-effects model in order to take into account the methodological variation and diverse clinical definitions. Preset subgroup analyses were done specifically for the type of bleeding, major bleeding or clinically relevant non-major bleeding, and the type of anticoagulant used, heparin or enoxaparin. For the outcomes of VTE, subgroup analysis was done for DVT and PE. Publication bias was assessed using comparison-adjusted Funnel plots. In all cases, a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Search results and study characteristics

After a thorough literature search (Supplementary Table S1), 714 studies were identified, of which 447 were screened, then 71 studies underwent full text review. A total of 7 studies were included in this meta-analysis, all of which were observational studies. A breakdown of the search is summarized in the PRISMA diagram below (Fig. 1).

PRISMA flow diagram

Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of the 7 included studies, 6 of which are retrospective cohorts, while 1, Jatis et al. (2023) [22], is a case-control study. Most studies defined low body weight as 45–60 kg, while Carter et al. (2016) [23] included underweight patients in their population, with a BMI 18.5. All studies used either UFH or enoxaparin or both as the type of anticoagulant. Standard dose anticoagulation was 15,000 units SC daily for UFH, while 40 mg SC daily or 30 mg SC twice daily for enoxaparin. Safety outcomes

Seven observational studies compared the total bleeding events in patients on reduced dose versus standard dose VTE prophylaxis. Pooled analysis of these studies revealed no significant difference in the incidence of total bleeding events between the two groups [RR 0.78, 95% CI 0.48–1.26], however with high heterogeneity (I2 = 64%) (Fig. 2).

Total bleeding events in reduced dose vs. standard dose VTE prophylaxis

Because of this, we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding four studies wherein baseline characteristics between the reduced dose group and standard dose group were not ensured to be similar. We excluded Dybdahl et al. (2019) and Nemeth et al. (2024) because the baseline characteristics of the reduced dose group favored increased risk of bleeding, namely that they were older and had a lower creatinine clearance (Table 2). We likewise excluded Jatis et al. (2023) and Kaylor et al. (2023) because they reported no baseline characteristics comparing the two groups. Sensitivity analysis of the three observational studies, including 1,014 individuals, revealed that there is a statistically significant decrease in risk of total bleeding in the reduced dose as compared with the standard dose anticoagulation [RR 0.49, 95% CI 0.30–0.82], with low heterogeneity (I2 = 0%) (Fig. 3).

Sensitivity analysis of total bleeding events in reduced group vs. standard group VTE prophylaxis

We also performed preset subgroup analyses on the type of bleeding and the type of anticoagulant used in the study (Table 3). For the type of bleeding, there is no significant difference in the incidence of both major bleeding (Supplementary Fig. 1) and clinically important non-major bleeding (Supplementary Fig. 2) between the reduced dose group and the standard dose group, however with high heterogeneity in the former (I2 = 51%). We then conducted a sensitivity analysis for the subgroup of major bleeding events, similar to the one above, excluding studies with significantly different baseline characteristics, i.e. Dybdahl et al. (2019), Kaylor et al. (2023), and Nemeth et al. (2024). Sensitivity analysis of three studies (Supplementary Fig. 3), including 1,014 individuals, showed that there is a significant decrease in the risk of major bleeding in the reduced dose group as compared to the standard dose group [RR 0.42, 95% CI 0.22–0.80], with low heterogeneity (I2 = 0%).

Evaluation of studies

Risk of bias

Using the ROBINS-I tool (Table 5), most studies included have a moderate overall risk of bias, often having moderate risk on the confounding bias and outcome measurement domains, which are expected to be difficult to control given these studies are observational studies. Generally, important domains are still controlled and these studies are still considered sound evidence for non-randomized trials. Exceptions would be Dybdahl et al. (2019), Jatis et al. (2023), and Kaylor et al. (2024) which have a serious overall risk of bias, due to the serious confounding bias present, especially with Dydahl et al. (2019), wherein age and creatinine clearance, important factors affecting risk of bleeding, were not controlled between the two groups and in fact were significantly different.

Quality of evidence

Certainty of evidence

Following the GRADE working group system in Supplementary Table S2, we assessed the level of confidence in the estimates of outcome, which showed very low to low certainty of evidence for outcomes of total bleeding events, major bleeding events, clinically relevant non-major bleeding, and total venous thromboembolic events. This is primarily due to the serious risk of bias and inconsistencies from the inclusion of studies by Dybdahl et al. (2019), Jatis et al. (2023), and Kaylor et al. (2024) which had high risk of confounding bias. Where sensitivity analyses were performed that excluded these studies for the outcomes of total bleeding events and major bleeding, results had a moderate to high certainty of evidence after performing the sensitivity analysis.

Discussion

Pharmacologic prophylaxis is the cornerstone of VTE prevention. However, there is a lack of data regarding the risk of bleeding in a population of low body weight patients on prophylactic anticoagulation. There is however available evidence to show that low body weight patients who are on therapeutic doses of anticoagulation have increased bleeding risk with rates ranging from 5.7%−21.4% [16, 26, 27]. In addition, there is limited data regarding the effect of dose adjustment of anticoagulants for VTE prophylaxis in this population.

Despite the supposed increased risk of bleeding in low body weight patients on anticoagulation, there is a lack of guideline recommendation regarding any dose adjustment or reduction in this population. Both the latest guidelines from the American Society of Hematology in 2018 and the American College of Chest Physicians in 2012 regarding VTE prophylaxis for hospitalized patients make no mention of dose adjustment or reduction among low weight patients [28, 29]. It is important to note however that only one of the observational studies included in our review was done before 2018 (Carter et al. in 2016) and none was done before 2012. Of the available literature regarding the matter to date, only the University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center institutional guideline released in 2023 recommends dose reduction of VTE prophylaxis among patients weighing < 55 kg with either Enoxaparin 30 mg once daily or UFH 5000 units SC every 8–12 hours [30]. However, they make no mention of the level of recommendation for this statement and referenced a study published in 2019 which assessed the in vivo attainment of therapeutic anti-factor Xa levels with dose reduction of anticoagulation in low weight patients.

In this pharmacologic observational study by Yam et al., it was shown that the use of a lower dose of enoxaparin at 30 mg daily for VTE prophylaxis may be sufficient to achieve a peak anti-factor Xa level within the goal range among low body weight patients of < 55 kg body weight [31]. Another study by Rojas et al. showed that the use of standard dose of enoxaparin at 40 mg daily among patients weighing less than 45 kg led to the detection of anti-factor Xa level well above the prophylactic target in a significant proportion of patients [15]. This could potentially mean that patients with low body weight receiving standard doses of anticoagulation may have supratherapeutic levels of anti-factor Xa, which may increase risk of bleeding. Consequently, these low body weight populations may then benefit from receiving lower doses of enoxaparin, achieving anti-factor Xa levels still within the therapeutic range, thereby maintaining their ability to prevent VTE but at the same time reducing the risk of bleeding. However, this has not yet been shown to translate to clinical outcomes.

Based on this pharmacodynamic background, our study investigated whether dose reduction in anticoagulation can indeed lower the risk of bleeding while still being effective in VTE prophylaxis. Our initial analysis showed no difference in bleeding risk with reduced dose as compared with standard dose anticoagulation, however with high heterogeneity. We attributed this heterogeneity to the studies of Dybdahl et al. (2019), Nemeth et al. (2024), Jatis et al. (2023), and Kaylor et al. (2024), wherein baseline characteristics between the reduced dose group and standard dose group anticoagulation were not ensured to be equal. This is important because several factors affect the risk of bleeding, and if these factors are not controlled baseline, this might favor the risk of bleeding in one group and affect our intended outcome. This may explain why in the studies of Dybdahl et al. (2019) and Jatis et al. (2023), risk of bleeding even tends to be higher in the reduced dose of anticoagulation.

In Dybdahl et al. (2019), the group on reduced dose enoxaparin (enoxaparin 30 mg once daily) were significantly older (60 years old vs. 51 years old) and had lower creatinine clearance (48.3 mL/min vs. 71.5 mL/min) than the group on standard dose enoxaparin (enoxaparin 40 mg once daily, or 30 mg twice daily). This is because in their institution, dosing of enoxaparin is adjusted by the physician and the pharmacist, hence patients who are older or have lower creatinine clearance tend to be prescribed with lower doses of anticoagulants as caution, given their increased risk of bleeding. This may likely explain why in Dybdahl et al. (2019), the reduced group anticoagulation tends to have a higher risk of bleeding. Indeed, it has been shown that the elderly population is at an increased bleeding risk possibly due to acquired disorders of coagulation [32]. Likewise, patients with a creatinine clearance < 60mL/min are also shown to have a 1.5x increased risk of bleeding [33]. Similarly, in Nemeth et al. (2024), their results were inconclusive likely because of these similar confounding factors that were not controlled baseline.

Jatis et al. (2023), on the other hand, is the only case-control study included in the analysis. Although the bleeding cases were matched with the control, the baseline characteristics of patients analyzed in the reduced dose and in the standard dose were not stated and may be a source of bias. Similarly, Kaylor et al. (2023) did not include baseline characteristics comparing the reduced dose versus the standard dose anticoagulation groups since their study primarily compared UFH versus enoxaparin, and hence baseline characteristics presented in their study primarily compared the latter two groups.

After performing sensitivity analysis excluding these four studies, it revealed that anticoagulation dose reduction reduces the risk of total bleeding by as much as 51% and major bleeding by as much as 58% in low body weight patients. This dose reduction did not result in a difference of the incidence of VTE, whether DVT or PE. These findings may provide initial evidence supporting the hypothesis earlier that lower doses of anticoagulation may be enough to achieve therapeutic levels in low body weight patients to prevent VTE, while decreasing risk of reaching supratherapeutic levels that favor risk of bleeding. The findings of this analysis should be interpreted with caution however, as they were derived from a post hoc evaluation. Nonetheless, the results provide preliminary evidence that anticoagulant dose reduction for VTE prophylaxis may help mitigate bleeding risk. Further studies are warranted to confirm this observation.

It is worth noting that some studies have reported findings that diverge from those of the present analysis. The recently published cohort by Abou Safrah et al. [35] observed no significant difference in bleeding incidence following dose reduction among patients with low body weight, a result consistent with that of Jatis et al. These discrepancies may be attributable to the cutoff values used to define low body weight—less than 60 kg in Jatis et al. and less than 57 kg in Abou Safrah et al. The cutoff values in these studies may not have been sufficiently low to capture patients at higher bleeding risk, as indicated in the pharmacological study by Yam et al. on Factor Xa activity mentioned earlier which used a cut off of 55 kg. In contrast, studies employing lower cutoff values generally demonstrated a reduction in bleeding incidence with dose adjustment, as in our analysis. Should further studies be pursued, a lower cutoff for the definition of low body weight may be more apt to provide a more precise assessment of bleeding risk.

Few systematic reviews have investigated this topic. Di Minno et al. (2014) [35] examined the effect of body weight on the incidence of bleeding and thrombosis, however they studied these outcomes comparing Vitamin K antagonists (VKA) with direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs). They found no significant difference in risk of bleeding and VTE recurrence or mortality in the low body weight population, however their study did not stratify these according to the dose and compared only between VKA and DOACs. They also included only those with acute thromboembolic disease, wherein anticoagulation is therapeutic, as compared to our study which focused on prophylactic anticoagulation. Grange et al. (2025) [36] evaluated the effect of weight-adjusted versus fixed-dose LMWH on thrombotic and bleeding outcomes in trauma patients. Although they found no significant difference between the two regimens, their analysis did not specifically target low body weight patients, who represent a subgroup at increased bleeding risk. Dose adjustment in patients within the range of the normal body weight may indeed have no impact in terms of affecting bleeding risk. In contrast, our study focused specifically on the special population of low body weight patients, encompassing a broader clinical context beyond trauma cases.

Based on the results from our meta-analysis, there is still a gap in terms of our understanding and future clinical implications of anticoagulation dose reduction for VTE prophylaxis in low body weight patients. Although our meta-analysis shows promising results, several limitations may be identified. Firstly, there are no randomized trials on the matter and our results arise solely from a pooled analysis of observational studies which in itself poses a noticeable risk of bias. Understandably however, randomized trials may be difficult to perform in this case, given the rare outcomes of bleeding and VTE, which would require larger sample sizes, and due to ethical considerations of the harm of drug-induced bleeding. Still, as we assessed the certainty of our outcomes using GRADE, these had very low to low certainty of evidence, aside from outcomes wherein sensitivity analyses were performed, which had moderate to high certainty of evidence. Ultimately, randomized trials are still necessary to have more robust recommendations for clinical practice, and given our preliminary findings may be worthwhile to pursue.

Secondly, the differences in the definition of low body weight across studies, ranging from 45 to 60 kg, may also be a potential source of heterogeneity. In fact, there is no standardized definition or an absolute weight cap to define an individual with low body weight aside from a body mass index < 18.5 kg/m which was only used in one of the included studies. The use of the BMI may be a reasonable definition of low weight as it indexes the absolute weight to an individual’s height. In addition, heterogeneity may also arise from the definition of bleeding used in the studies, either as BARC or ISTH, as there are slight differences in how they define major and clinically relevant non-major bleeding.

Nevertheless, our study provides initial evidence that dose reduction in prophylactic anticoagulation may reduce bleeding risk in low body weight patients, without affecting its effect on VTE prevention. These preliminary findings may serve as a foundation to pursue clinical trials leading to more robust clinical recommendations with regards to anticoagulation dose reduction in this population.

Beyond patients with low body weight, certain populations are also at an increased risk of bleeding compared with the general population. These include critically ill patients in intensive care units and individuals with cancer [37–39]. Variations in dosing strategies and the choice of anticoagulant among clinicians may influence clinical outcomes such as bleeding and thrombosis, representing important areas for future investigation. This highlights the need to individualize anticoagulation strategies in all patients.

Conclusion

Our meta-analysis showed that anticoagulation dose reduction for VTE prophylaxis in low body weight patients may reduce risk of bleeding, especially major bleeding, without compromising its efficacy in preventing VTE. Hence, dose reduction may be considered in this population especially if they are at a baseline higher risk for bleeding. Large powered randomized control trials are needed to further support this recommendation.
