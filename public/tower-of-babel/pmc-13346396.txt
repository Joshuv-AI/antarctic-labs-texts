A novel dynamic cardiac phantom for comparing blood pool and myocardial perfusion tracers: initial evaluation with CZT-ERNA and PET-MPI
Authors: Marc Østergaard Nielsen, Rebekka Wozniak, Theis Cornelius Hansen, Linus Leonhart Duchstein, Jonas Damm Hermansen, Svend Hvidsten, Lars Thorbjørn Jensen, Bo Zerahn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNuclear imaging modalities can quantify cardiac volumes and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), but systematic differences between techniques complicate direct comparison. Phantom-based evaluations comparing myocardial perfusion and blood-pool methods are scarce.MethodsWe used a dynamic two-compartment cardiac phantom (myocardium and left ventricular [LV] lumen) with adjustable stroke volume and heart rate. Static and gated acquisitions were performed on cadmium–zinc–telluride equilibrium radionuclilde angiocardiography (CZT-ERNA) and gated positron emission tomography myocardial perfusion imaging (PET-MPI) under identical volumes and heart-rate settings. Computed tomography (CT) served as the reference for end-diastolic volume (EDV) and end-systolic volume (ESV). LVEF was derived from these volumes for all modalities. We also assessed the impact of phantom position relative to detectors, as well as the effect of attenuation correction via a thorax phantom.ResultsCZT-ERNA overestimated static LV volumes relative to CT, whereas PET-MPI underestimated them. Both modalities underestimated LVEF compared with CT-derived values, with a larger bias for PET-MPI. On PET-MPI, attenuation correction produced similar volumes with and without the thorax phantom. CZT-ERNA volumes decreased as detector distance increased.ConclusionUnder controlled phantom conditions, CZT-ERNA and PET-MPI show inverse biases for LV volumes and both underestimated LVEF versus CT. These findings point to the influence of reconstruction and contouring algorithms, attenuation correction, and camera positioning. The dynamic phantom enables direct comparison of perfusion- and blood-pool-based measurements and may support modality-specific calibration when translating absolute values across techniques.

Introduction

Cardiovascular imaging plays an essential role in assessing cardiac function, particularly left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), in patients at risk of declining pump function during cancer treatment.

According to the 2021 European Society of Cardiology guidelines for heart failure, 3D-echocardiography (3DE) or cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) are recommended as first-line modalities. Multigated acquisition (MUGA), also known as equilibrium radionuclide angiocardiography (ERNA), serves as a third-line option when 3DE and CMR are unavailable or inconclusive [1].

Cadmium–zinc–telluride ERNA (CZT-ERNA), a type of 3D-MUGA using cadmium-zinc-telluride detectors, is an efficient tool for serial LVEF monitoring [2]. In patients, CZT-ERNA shows good correlation with CMR for assessing both LVEF and right ventricular ejection fraction (RVEF) [3, 4]. However, LVEF measured by CZT-ERNA correlates less strongly with 2D-echocardiography [5].

Cardiac phantom studies are valuable for evaluating and ensuring quality control in nuclear imaging techniques. For instance, myocardial perfusion imaging (MPI) with CZT-detectors or conventional Anger cameras has been tested for LVEF assessment. These studies highlight that CZT-MPI provides superior spatial resolution, sensitivity, and shorter acquisition times [6]. Yet, conflicting results include larger left ventricular (LV) volumes, especially end-systolic volume (ESV) [7], and decreased LVEF compared to Anger cameras [8]. To our knowledge, most cardiac phantoms estimate LVEF using MPI. Only one study has used blood-pool imaging with a static phantom to compare positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT), single-photon emission computed tomography-ERNA (SPECT-ERNA), and CMR [9]. No prior work has directly compared CZT-ERNA and PET-MPI.

The aim of this study is to address this gap by comparing CZT-ERNA and PET-MPI for the assessment of LVEF using both static and gated phantom acquisitions.

Background

Cardiac phantoms simulate the anatomy and/or function of the human heart and can be generally described as static or dynamic. Static cardiac phantoms are fixed models representing specific phases of the cardiac cycle, typically end-systole and end-diastole. They offer greater anatomical accuracy but lack the ability to assess the effect of motion. Dynamic cardiac phantoms replicate heart motion at varying volumes and heart rates to simulate the beating heart. Incorporating thorax phantoms to mimic soft tissue attenuation further enhances these studies. Cardiac phantoms are widely used in radiology to compare imaging modalities such as CMR, computed tomography (CT), and 3DE [10, 11].

Advancements in 3D printing have facilitated the development of anthropomorphic cardiac phantoms that closely resemble human anatomy. For example, Kiss et al. [12] created two phantoms from patient-derived PET data: one depicting healthy myocardium and another with a perfusion defect. These designs are cost-effective, quick to produce, and customizable, including features such as 'hot' backgrounds and liver inserts. Similarly, Verrecchia-Ramos et al. [9] built static phantom pairs from CMR scans at end-diastole and end-systole to validate cardiac volumes and LVEF across multiple imaging modalities. Despite their anatomical precision, static models limit assessments of cardiac volumes and LVEF by failing to include effects of cardiac motion.

Dynamic cardiac phantoms such as the Amsterdam-gated cardiac phantom (AGATE) support validation of gated MPI SPECT. AGATE allows variable LV volumes with realistic ejection fractions but is specifically designed for MPI, rendering it unsuitable for gated blood pool imaging [13]. AGATE has been applied in comparisons of CZT cameras under dual-isotope conditions [7], evaluation of LVEF from CZT cameras against conventional gamma cameras [8], and multicenter studies examining variations in LV function [14].

De Bondt et al. [15, 16] designed dynamic cardiac phantoms explicitly for gated blood pool imaging, featuring biventricular and four-chamber models with valves to evaluate LV and right ventricular (RV) function, though they do not support MPI.

Currently, no phantom enables direct comparison between MPI and blood-pool imaging for cardiac volumes and LVEF. Our custom-built phantom fills this void. In this study, we showcase its utility by comparing volumes and LVEF from CZT-ERNA and gated PET-MPI, using CT as the reference standard.

Methods

Gated heart phantom

The left ventricle is simulated using two silicone balloons. The outer lumen represents the myocardium, while the inner lumen simulates the cavity of the left ventricle. Fluid is continually pumped in and out using a pneumatic system driven by pressurized air, supplied through compressed air outlets commonly found in hospitals.

The pneumatic pump consists of a piston housed within a cylindrical chamber. Pressurized air is alternately supplied to the top and bottom of the chamber of the pump, driving piston movement. Its velocity is regulated by two adjustable outlet valves positioned at either end of the chamber, emulating a cardiac cycle, characterized by a rapid systolic phase and a slower diastolic phase.

Two reed switches are mounted to the pump housing and are magnetically activated as the piston approaches them. The switches control an electromagnetic solenoid valve, switching air supply between the top or bottom of the pump. The spatial distance between the reed switches defines the piston stroke length, thereby determining the simulated cardiac stroke volume.

Piston displacement is monitored by a microswitch, which generates a standardized 5 V trigger signal, simulating an electrocardiogram (ECG) trigger. This trigger signal is transmitted to the imaging system to synchronize data acquisition with the mechanical cycle.

The air pump piston operates a modified 100 mL plastic syringe (Pic Solution). The syringe has ports specifically for filling the inner lumen of the system, while the flange has ports for filling the outer lumen. The syringe is connected via a rubber tube to the flange, on which the balloons are attached with O-rings. Activation of the system moves fluid back and forth from the innermost balloon to simulate the beating heart. Thorax phantom

A thorax phantom mimicking the attenuation of human tissue for both 140 kV and CT energies was produced using the filament deposition modeling (FDM) print method, using a Prusa MK3s 3D printer and PBT + (Polybutylene Terephthalate) filament. The filament type was selected for having similar photon attenuation properties as human tissue.

The phantom consists of two types of tissue, lung tissue and ribcage tissue, the latter representing both bone and soft tissue. The shape of the tissue types was segmented on a single lung CT slice and extrapolated in a straight line along the height of the phantom.

A cavity for the dynamic heart phantom was then cut from the resulting phantom, allowing for uniform positioning of the heart within the thorax phantom, with a locking plug to fix the dynamic heart phantom in place.

To achieve similar attenuation characteristics, sample CT scans were used and an average HU value for lung (-642 HU) and ribcage tissue (10 HU) was calculated.

By modulating the ratio of PBT + plastic filament to air in the 3D printed phantom it was possible to achieve average attenuation characteristics of the two segments in the phantom that matched the average HU values of human tissue, using 31.5% infill for lung tissue and 92.0% infill for ribcage tissue. Volume estimation

The reference volume of the inner lumen was measured on CT-images using MIM Software (Cleveland, OH, USA) (Fig. 3). The outer lumen has a higher average HU than the inner due to the addition of iodized table-salt, this allowed us to contour the inner-lumen using the tool Region Grow. This tool allows the user to select an appropriate area that the software should contour depending on HU. By manually setting a boundary ROI at the edge of the flange, we can avoid the contour spilling into the feeding port of the inner balloon. The registered HU of the lumens varied slightly from scan to scan. To validate CT as the reference, we first determined the absolute lumen volume by disassembling the phantom, weighing the components, and calculating the water-filled volume gravimetrically. This approach is limited by meniscus reading uncertainty, but the resulting fixed bias relative to CT was < 2 mL. Proportional bias was assessed post-hoc by comparing recorded syringe volume changes to corresponding CT-measured changes across all fills; no systematic bias was observed. The small residual error (< 2 mL) is well below the variability relevant for cardiac volume quantification.

PET/CT

Our study utilized a Siemens Biograph mCT Flow PET/CT system (Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany). The system consists of a PET detector with 4 rings, with each ring containing 48 detector blocks. Each block is equipped with lutetium oxyorthosilicate (LSO) crystals of 4 × 4 × 20 mm, assembled in a 13 × 13 array and coupled to a 2 × 2 PMT array in each detector block. The system has an axial field of view (FOV) of 20 cm, and a sensitivity of 10 cps/kBq. The detector ring diameter is 84.2 cm. The time coincidence window is 4.1 ns, with a system time resolution of 540 ps and an energy window of 435–650 keV. This model features a bore diameter of 78 cm, a tunnel length of 136 cm, and a table capacity of 227 kg.

An integrated 64-slice CT is used for attenuation correction of PET data. PET images were reconstructed using a Point Spread Function and Time-of-Flight (PSF + TOF) algorithm with 3 iterations and 21 subsets. A Gaussian post-reconstruction filter of 10.0 mm was applied. The images were reconstructed into a 256 × 256 matrix with a pixel spacing of 1.59 mm and a slice thickness of 2.0 mm. Standard corrections included CT-based attenuation correction, model-based scatter correction, and delayed window randoms subtraction. All images were decay-corrected to the start of the scan. The acquired PET images were analyzed with Corridor4DM (INVIA, Ann Arbor, MI, USA).

CZT-ERNA

The CZT-ERNA acquisitions were performed on the GE Discovery NM530c (GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI) equipped with a stationary multi-pinhole collimator. The acquisition protocol used 16 bins per R-R interval, set to acquire a predefined number of accepted beats (200), and a 20% energy window centered on 140 keV. Images were reconstructed using the manufacturer’s dedicated 3D iterative Bayesian reconstruction algorithm (50 iterations, 2 subsets) into a 70 × 70 matrix with a voxel size of 4.0 × 4.0 × 4.0 mm with a post-reconstruction Butterworth filter (cutoff 0.40 cycles/cm, power 7) as per the manufacturer’s standard clinical protocol. For the analysis of the acquired gated images we used the Xeleris 3 Imaging workstation’s reorientation software (GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, version no. 3.0562) and Cedars-Sinai QBS processing software (Cedars-Sinai, Los Angeles, CA, revision 2009.0).

For static imaging, an ECG signal generator was connected to the scanner to simulate a trigger signal, as our acquisition protocol is designed for gated acquisitions. This produced end-systolic and end-diastolic volumes in the QBS software, and the mean of these values was reported for static acquisitions.

Study protocol

The study consisted of three series, each conducted over two days. Data acquisition was performed in two phases. Series 1 and 2 served as an initial evaluation of the phantom setup. Based on findings from these series, specifically the impact of compartment contact, the phantom configuration was optimized for the final acquisitions in Series 3.

Preparation for each series included filling the inner balloon with distilled water and a 3.5% iodized salt solution for the outer lumen. This setup ensured a clear difference in Hounsfield Units (HU) between the two compartments. After filling, the phantom was set to pump, allowing the balloons to reach equilibrium.

On the first day of each series, a PET-isotope was injected into the outer lumen of the phantom. Stepwise static acquisitions were then performed by adjusting the volume in the feeding syringe. After completing the stepwise acquisitions, the phantom was set to pump, and gated sequences were acquired. A CT scan was performed at each step including the end points of the pumping sequences.

After the PET acquisitions, the phantom was placed in a safe location for tracer decay. On the following day, Tc99m was administered to the inner lumen for CZT-ERNA acquisitions, and the process was repeated with the corresponding static and gated acquisitions. As our CZT-camera is not equipped with a CT-scanner the subsequent CT scans were performed on the same scanner used for the PET acquisitions to ensure consistent volume estimation.

Several static CZT-ERNA acquisitions were performed with increasing detector-to-patient distances to simulate progressively suboptimal camera positioning. The effect of scan length on measured volumes was also assessed for both CZT-ERNA and PET.

For Series 3, we measured a mean activity concentration for PET-MPI of 196 kBq/ml during static acquisitions and 257 kBq/ml during gated acquisitions. For CZT-ERNA, we measured a range between 584,102 and 1,101,736 counts from the smallest to largest static measurement, while the gated acquisitions on average recorded 1,200,000 counts.

Discussion

This study compared CZT-ERNA and PET-MPI with CT-derived volumes using a dynamic cardiac phantom. The main findings were as follows. PET volumes tracked below CT with increasing size (Fig. 4). CZT-ERNA volumes drifted above CT (Fig. 5). Both modalities underestimated LVEF, with a larger bias for PET (Fig. 6; Table 2). Attenuation correction stabilized PET-derived volumes and reduced dependence on the thorax phantom (Fig. 7). A key explanation for the LVEF discrepancy lies in the software. The PET package (Corridor4DM) allowed basal plane motion to be fixed at 0 mm to match the phantom, whereas the ERNA package (QBS) modeled basal displacement by default. This yields larger EDV, smaller ESV, and thus higher LVEF in QBS than in Corridor, highlighting that algorithmic assumptions can drive inter-modality differences as much as detector physics.

Volumes were not interchangeable across modalities because systematic scale biases persisted (Figs. 4, 5). LVEF behaved more robustly than volumes, as the EDV/ESV ratio reduces scale error. In this phantom, QBS’s modeled basal motion favored CZT-ERNA for LVEF. In vivo, where basal motion is present, this software-driven difference may narrow, and attenuation-related effects might dominate any remaining discrepancies.

Phantom design also influenced results. In our initial evaluation series (1 and 2), close contact between the inner and outer balloons created an apparent perfusion defect, leading to underestimated PET volumes and LVEF (Fig. 9). In series 3 we mitigated this issue, yielding values closer to CT, which is supported by the lower mean bias and narrower limits of agreement for PET shown in Table 2. Thus, volumes may be further underestimated if the edge-detection algorithm fails to constrain itself in myocardial regions with low uptake. CT served as the reference standard, offering clear boundary definition and excellent reproducibility. While CT contouring is simplified by sharp lumen contrast and may not fully replicate the blurred borders of real myocardium, it provided a consistent benchmark. By comparison, nuclear software is optimized for noisy, less well-defined endocardial edges, which could partly explain the systematic underestimation of LVEF.

Several limitations must be noted. The phantom reproduces circular contraction of the myocardium but lacks longitudinal motion, particularly basal plane displacement. This simplification likely contributes to the systematic underestimation of LVEF in the nuclear methods compared with CT. Importantly, inter-modality agreement between CZT-ERNA and PET-MPI was closer than either modality’s agreement with CT, suggesting that a more physiologically realistic phantom, including basal movement, might improve absolute agreement with CT. Lastly, extracardiac tracer activity was not simulated.

Conclusion

This study evaluated the accuracy of cardiac PET and CZT-ERNA for volume measurements using a novel dynamic cardiac phantom. PET progressively underestimates volumes in the static series, while CZT-ERNA progressively overestimates them. Both modalities underestimate LVEF, with a greater discrepancy in PET. Attenuation correction functions as expected in PET but is absent in CZT-ERNA, which may lead to patient size-dependent errors.

Overall, in this phantom, volumes were not interchangeable across modalities. LVEF behaved more robustly than volumes yet remained modality- and software-dependent. In clinical settings where basal motion is present, software-driven differences may diminish, and attenuation correction may dominate any residual discrepancies. Future work should aim to improve longitudinal/basal motion which would refine cross-modality comparisons.
