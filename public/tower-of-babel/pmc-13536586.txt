Malaria burden across 126 tropical countries and territories, 1990–2021: tropical-climate share, sociodemographic correlates, population dynamics, and projections to 2050
Authors: Qiangbing Liao, Xiaoyan Zheng, Xiaoyang Zhang, Zhiwei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalaria remains a major global health challenge, particularly in tropical regions. Although recent global reports describe persistent malaria burden, fewer studies have jointly assessed tropical-climate context, sociodemographic development, demographic decomposition, and future projections across tropical countries. This study aimed to provide an integrated assessment of malaria burden and its ecological correlates among tropical countries and territories.MethodsWe extracted malaria incidence, prevalence, deaths, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), years lived with disability (YLDs), years of life lost (YLLs), and corresponding age-standardized rates from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 for 126 tropical countries and territories from 1990 to 2021. We assessed spatial patterns, estimated annual percentage changes (EAPCs), age- and sex-specific distributions, ecological correlations with Köppen–Geiger processed-area tropical-climate shares and sociodemographic index (SDI), demographic contributions using Das Gupta decomposition, and autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) projections to 2050.ResultsIn 2021, malaria burden showed substantial geographic heterogeneity, with the highest age-standardized incidence, mortality, and DALY rates concentrated mainly in West Africa. From 1990 to 2021, most tropical countries experienced declines in age-standardized malaria burden, whereas the absolute number of incident cases increased from 209 to 245 million. Children younger than 5 years had the greatest burden across all indicators, and mortality rates showed a secondary increase among older adults. Decomposition analysis indicated that population growth contributed to increases in absolute malaria numbers, including approximately 103 million additional incident cases, whereas epidemiological improvements and population age-structure changes partially offset this demographic pressure. SDI showed strong negative associations with all age-standardized malaria burden indicators, while processed-area tropical-climate shares showed weak and inconsistent ecological associations. ARIMA projections suggested that age-standardized malaria burden may remain at a high plateau through 2050, with widening prediction intervals over time.ConclusionsDespite substantial progress in malaria control, tropical regions continue to experience persistent malaria burden associated with demographic expansion, socioeconomic disparities, and unequal vulnerability across age groups. Processed-area tropical-climate share alone was not a strong predictor in ecological comparisons, whereas SDI showed robust negative associations with burden. Sustained surveillance, strengthened vector control, health-system investment in low-SDI settings, and targeted interventions for young children and older adults are essential for further reductions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s41182-026-01070-x.Keywords: Malaria, Global Burden of Disease 2021, Tropical regions, Sociodemographic index, Population growth, Decomposition analysis, ARIMA forecasting

Introduction

Malaria remains one of the most important parasitic diseases worldwide and continues to impose a substantial public health burden, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions. Recent World Malaria Reports indicate that hundreds of millions of malaria cases and hundreds of thousands of malaria-related deaths still occur each year, with the burden concentrated mainly in Africa and especially in high-burden sub-Saharan countries [1, 2]. Although large numbers of cases and deaths have been averted since 2000, progress has slowed in recent years, and rebounds after 2015 and during the COVID-19 period have raised concern that current control gains remain fragile [1, 3, 4].

The distribution of malaria reflects the interaction of ecological suitability, human exposure, health-system capacity, and socioeconomic development. Climate affects vector ecology and parasite development through temperature, rainfall, humidity, and seasonality, and recent climate-malaria studies have linked warming scenarios, meteorological variation, and urban Anopheles stephensi transmission to malaria risk [5, 6]. However, climate suitability does not operate in isolation. Poverty, housing conditions, education, access to diagnosis and treatment, vector-control coverage, migration, urbanization, and public health infrastructure can substantially modify whether climatically suitable areas translate into high malaria burden [6, 7].

Cross-country comparisons, therefore, require a framework that considers both environmental context and socioeconomic development. The sociodemographic index (SDI), which integrates income, education, and fertility, provides a summary measure of development that may capture differences in health-system capacity and living conditions relevant to malaria prevention and treatment. Previous studies have examined malaria burden globally, in specific regions, or in selected populations, and some have assessed climate-related drivers or time-series forecasts [3, 8, 9]. Nevertheless, much of the literature has focused on single indicators, single countries, short time horizons, or specific climatic variables, leaving limited evidence on how tropical-country malaria burden patterns relate simultaneously to tropical-climate share, SDI, demographic change, and future trends.

A further gap is that changes in age-standardized rates do not necessarily explain changes in the absolute number of malaria cases, deaths, or DALYs. In high-fertility and rapidly growing populations, declining rates may coexist with persistent or increasing absolute burden. Decomposition analysis can separate the contributions of population growth, age-structure change, and epidemiological change, while forecasting can describe whether recent trends are likely to translate into sustained reductions or persistent plateaus. These complementary approaches are particularly relevant for tropical countries, where demographic pressure and uneven control capacity may shape future malaria burden.

Therefore, this study aimed to provide an integrated assessment of malaria burden across tropical countries and territories using GBD 2021 data. Specifically, we sought to: (1) describe the spatial distribution of six malaria burden indicators in 2021; (2) quantify temporal trends in age-standardized rates from 1990 to 2021 using EAPCs; (3) assess ecological associations of age-standardized malaria burden with processed-area Köppen–Geiger tropical-climate share and SDI; (4) decompose changes in absolute incidence, prevalence, deaths, DALYs, YLLs, and YLDs into contributions from population growth, population age structure, and epidemiological change; and (5) project age-standardized malaria burden indicators to 2050 using ARIMA models.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to combine GBD 2021 estimates, processed-area tropical-climate shares derived from the Köppen–Geiger classification, SDI correlation, Das Gupta decomposition, and ARIMA forecasting across tropical countries and territories. The findings are intended to support more targeted surveillance, resource allocation, and malaria-control planning in settings, where demographic growth and low socioeconomic development may sustain high-absolute burden despite progress in age-standardized rates.

Methods

Data sources

Malaria-related data from 1990 to 2021 were obtained from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 (GBD 2021) database [10]. Malaria was defined according to the GBD cause hierarchy and GBD 2021 comparative-estimation framework [4, 10], and estimates were extracted from the Global Health Data Exchange Results Tool (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/) on January 15, 2024. We extracted incidence, prevalence, mortality, years lived with disability (YLDs), years of life lost (YLLs), disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), corresponding age-standardized rates, and 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) for males, females, and both sexes combined. The SDI, ranging from 0 to 1, was used as a composite indicator of income per capita, educational attainment among people aged 15 years or older, and fertility among people younger than 25 years. The geographic tropical master list was constructed by intersecting Natural Earth 1:10 m Admin 0 Countries and Admin 0 Map Units polygons with the latitudinal band between the Tropics of Cancer and Capricorn (± 23.43656°) in WGS84 (EPSG:4326). This produced 154 countries and territories for geographic and climate description. We then retained the 126 locations with an independent malaria location_id in the downloaded GBD data for burden maps, correlations, aggregation, decomposition, and forecasting; 29 areas were not retained, because they lacked a separate GBD malaria estimate, predominantly territories or special areas. All 126 matched locations were evaluated for EAPC. EAPCs were estimable for 86 locations for ASIR, ASPR, DALY rate, and YLD rate and for 85 locations for ASMR and YLL rate; all-zero or insufficient positive rate series could not be analyzed on the log scale. The complete list of the 126 GBD-matched tropical countries and territories, together with their GBD location identifiers and EAPC eligibility, is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Köppen–Geiger climate classification

The study region was defined geographically as countries and territories with territory intersecting the band between the Tropics of Cancer and Capricorn (± 23.43656°), rather than as countries composed entirely of tropical climate. Köppen–Geiger climate classification was used to describe climatic heterogeneity within this geographic sample. The classification assigns climate classes from long-term temperature and precipitation regimes and groups them into tropical (A), arid (B), temperate (C), continental (D), and polar (E) climates [11, 12].

We used the 1991–2020 Köppen–Geiger raster released with the updated high-resolution global classification product [11]. The source product has a native spatial resolution of 0.00833333 degrees (approximately 1 km at the equator). To make global country or territory overlay computationally tractable, it was resampled by modal class to a regular 0.25° × 0.25° grid before extraction. Tropical climate was defined as Köppen–Geiger group A (Af, Am, Aw, and As).

All climate and boundary layers were handled in WGS84 (EPSG:4326). The 0.25° raster was cropped to the 38°S–38°N processing window used for the tropical-country maps, masked by the Natural Earth country or territory polygon, and extracted with R packages sf and terra. For each country or territory, tropical-climate share was calculated as 100 × (number of valid extracted cells classified as group A/number of all valid extracted Köppen–Geiger cells). In this study, tropical-climate share refers to the proportion of valid Köppen–Geiger raster cells classified as group A within the spatially processed portion of each country or territory in the 38°S–38°N processing window. It should not be interpreted as the proportion calculated over the complete national territory for countries extending beyond this processing window. Thus, the exposure is a processed-area climate-composition measure, not a measure of malaria transmission suitability.

Decomposition analysis

To quantify the contributions of demographic and epidemiological changes to temporal variation in malaria burden, we used the Das Gupta symmetric decomposition method [13]. Changes from 1990 to 2021 were partitioned for the absolute numbers of six malaria burden indicators, including incident cases, prevalent cases, deaths, DALYs, YLLs, and YLDs. ARIMA prediction

To predict future trends in malaria burden in tropical regions from 2022 to 2050, we employed autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) models for time-series forecasting [14]. The analysis was based on annual disease-burden estimates from tropical countries and territories for 1990–2021, with models fitted separately for age-standardized incidence, prevalence, mortality, DALY, YLL, and YLD rates. All time series were logarithmically transformed before modeling to reduce scale differences and ensure positive predicted values. Analyses were conducted using the R forecast package. Forecasts from 2022 to 2050 were generated with 95% prediction intervals. These intervals reflect ARIMA model-based forecast uncertainty and are not equivalent to GBD uncertainty intervals. Before fitting ARIMA (p, d, q) models, stationarity was evaluated using the Augmented Dickey–Fuller test, and differencing was applied according to the ADF-based stationarity assessment. Model order selection was based on minimizing the corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICc), with inspection of autocorrelation and partial autocorrelation functions. Residual diagnostics, including the Ljung–Box test, were used to assess remaining autocorrelation and forecast adequacy. Model-specific information, including the selected ARIMA order, ADF results, AICc, and Ljung–Box residual diagnostics. Candidate models were treated as nonseasonal annual time-series models, and model selection settings and residual checks were recorded to support reproducibility.

Statistical analysis

Tropical countries and territories successfully matched to the GBD 2021 database were used as the unit of analysis. We conducted descriptive analyses, EAPC trend analyses, climate and SDI ecological correlation analyses, Das Gupta decomposition, and ARIMA forecasting. Counts and age-standardized rates were reported as estimates with 95% UIs, with all rates expressed per 100,000 population. Pearson and Spearman correlation analyses were used to evaluate ecological associations between age-standardized burden indicators and processed-area tropical-climate share or SDI; these analyses were interpreted as country-level associations rather than causal effects. All statistical analyses and graphing were performed using R version 4.5.3 with packages, including sf, terra, stats, and forecast. All correlation analyses used the 126 GBD-matched tropical countries and territories unless otherwise specified. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals for Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated using Fisher’s z transformation. Confidence intervals for Spearman rank correlations were estimated using 5,000 bootstrap resamples. Secondary analyses, including EAPC estimation, ecological correlation analysis, decomposition analysis, and ARIMA forecasting, were conducted using the GBD point estimates. Uncertainty from the underlying GBD estimates was not propagated through these secondary analyses. The confidence intervals reported for EAPCs and correlation coefficients reflect statistical uncertainty from the corresponding secondary models and should not be interpreted as incorporating the full uncertainty of the GBD estimates.

Results

Spatial distribution of age-standardized malaria burden in tropical countries and territories in 2021

In 2021, age-standardized malaria burden varied substantially across the 126 GBD-matched tropical countries and territories (Fig. 1). The highest rates were predominantly concentrated in West Africa, with an additional high-burden cluster observed in Melanesia. ASIR ranged from 0 to 27,702.7 per 100,000 population. The highest ASIRs were observed in Liberia (27,702.7; 95% UI, 14,565.4–38,887.5), Benin (27,371.1; 18,769.5–35,280.9), Burkina Faso (26,759.1; 17,858.4–37,140.7), the Solomon Islands (26,543.4; 23,807.8–29,509.5), and Sierra Leone (25,205.7; 14,430.1–36,137.6). A similar spatial distribution was observed for ASPR, with the highest values recorded in Liberia (29,248.9; 13,845.0–47,808.4), followed by Benin, Burkina Faso, Sierra Leone, and the Democratic Republic of the Congo. In contrast, the point estimates for ASIR and ASPR were zero in 50 and 48 locations, respectively; among locations with non-zero estimates, the lowest ASIR and ASPR were observed in Saudi Arabia and Guatemala, respectively.

Spatial distribution of age-standardized malaria disease-burden rates in tropical countries and territories in 2021

Mortality and health-loss indicators showed a more pronounced geographic concentration, particularly in West Africa. ASMR ranged from 0 to 173.1 per 100,000 population, with the highest rates observed in Burkina Faso (173.1; 95% UI, 75.4–318.5), Sierra Leone (169.7; 53.6–334.2), Niger (140.0; 44.8–273.2), Liberia (136.3; 44.0–281.0), and Benin (131.4; 56.3–255.1). The spatial patterns of DALY and YLL rates were highly consistent with mortality distribution, with the highest DALY rate and YLL rate observed in Sierra Leone (DALY rate: 8,940.3; 95% UI, 3,029.8–17,358.5; YLL rate: 8,656.0; 2,787.7–16,971.7), followed by Burkina Faso, Niger, Benin, and Liberia. For nonfatal burden, YLD rate was highest in Liberia (354.7; 167.1–622.5), followed by Benin (320.6; 199.0–473.4), Burkina Faso (317.6; 175.3–490.3), Sierra Leone (284.3; 133.5–498.7), and Nigeria (271.8; 183.0–381.9).

Overall, the spatial distribution of malaria burden in 2021 demonstrated marked geographic clustering, with the highest age-standardized burden concentrated in a limited number of tropical countries, particularly in West Africa, while substantially lower burden levels were observed in many other tropical regions.

Trends in age-standardized malaria burden in tropical countries and territories for 1990 to 2021

From 1990 to 2021, EAPCs of age-standardized malaria burden indicators demonstrated substantial geographic heterogeneity across tropical countries and territories (Fig. 2). EAPCs were available for 86 locations for ASIR, ASPR, DALY rate and YLD rate, and for 85 locations for ASMR and YLL rate. Overall, decreasing trends predominated across tropical regions. Significant declines were observed in 77 locations for ASIR, 76 for ASPR, 73 for ASMR, 76 for DALY rate, 75 for YLL rate, and 79 for YLD rate.

Spatial distribution of the EAPC for age-standardized malaria disease-burden rates in tropical countries and territories, 1990–2021

For ASIR and ASPR, the most pronounced reductions were mainly observed in South Asia, North Africa, Central America, and parts of Southeast Asia. Sri Lanka showed the largest decline in ASIR (EAPC, − 37.99%; 95% CI − 42.68 to − 32.92), followed by Morocco and El Salvador. The greatest reduction in ASPR occurred in Morocco (EAPC, − 32.01%; 95% CI − 36.12 to − 27.63), followed by Malaysia and Sri Lanka. Conversely, Venezuela was the only location with significant increases in both ASIR (EAPC, 7.44%; 95% CI 4.82 to 10.13) and ASPR (EAPC, 3.75%; 95% CI 2.58 to 4.94).

Trends in mortality-related indicators showed similar but more heterogeneous patterns. The largest decreases in ASMR and YLL rate were observed in Sri Lanka (ASMR: EAPC, − 41.97%; 95% CI − 46.93 to − 36.54; YLL rate: EAPC, − 42.17%; 95% CI − 47.14 to − 36.72), followed by China and Oman. However, significant increases in ASMR and YLL rate were observed in Venezuela, the Central African Republic, and Benin. Among these locations, Venezuela exhibited the greatest increases in ASMR (EAPC, 5.63%; 95% CI 2.44 to 8.92) and YLL rate (EAPC, 5.59%; 95% CI 2.38 to 8.91).

For DALY rate and YLD rate, declining trends were observed in most tropical countries and territories. The steepest decline in DALY rate occurred in Sri Lanka (EAPC, − 32.82%; 95% CI − 36.13 to − 29.35), followed by Morocco and Ecuador. Significant increases in DALY rate were identified in Venezuela (EAPC, 5.40%; 95% CI 2.44 to 8.45), the Central African Republic, and Benin. Morocco showed the greatest reduction in YLD rate (EAPC, − 32.53%; 95% CI − 36.56 to − 28.24), followed by Sri Lanka and Paraguay, whereas Venezuela was the only location with a significant increase in YLD rate (EAPC, 3.92%; 95% CI 2.29 to 5.58).

Overall, EAPC analyses indicated substantial reductions in age-standardized malaria burden across most tropical countries and territories from 1990 to 2021. However, unfavorable trends persisted in Venezuela and selected sub-Saharan African countries, particularly for mortality and health-loss indicators, highlighting continued disparities in malaria control and elimination progress.

Geographic composition and Köppen–Geiger climate classification characteristics of tropical countries and territories

Among the 154 tropical countries and territories included in the geographical and climatic description, 121 are completely located between the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn, and 33 have only part of their territory within the geographical tropical zone. According to the United Nations continent division, the number of African countries and territories is the greatest, with 58 in total, followed by 49 in the Americas, 25 in Asia, and 22 in Oceania. At the subregion level, the Caribbean, Eastern Africa, Western Africa, Southeast Asia, and South America constitute the main components of the included tropical countries and territories.

On the basis of the Köppen–Geiger climate classification from 1991 to 2020, the tropical-climate group is the most common dominant climate type in these countries and territories. A total of 111 countries and territories are dominated by the tropical-climate group, accounting for 72.1%; among them, the tropical savanna climate (Aw) is the most common, with 51 countries and territories, accounting for 33.1%, followed by the tropical rainforest climate (Af), with 41 countries and territories, accounting for 26.6%, and the tropical monsoon climate (Am), with 19 countries and territories, accounting for 12.3%. In addition to the tropical climate, the arid climate group is also an important component, with 30 countries and territories dominated by arid climates, accounting for 19.5%, among which the hot desert climate (BWh) is the most common, with 21 countries and territories. Few countries and territories are dominated by the temperate climate group, with 12 in total, accounting for 7.8%, and 1 region is dominated by the polar tundra climate (ET).

In terms of spatial distribution, tropical rainforest and tropical savanna climates are concentrated mainly in the Amazon Basin, Central Africa, the coastal areas of the Gulf of Guinea, Southeast Asia, and the island regions of Oceania. Arid and semiarid climates are distributed mainly in regions, such as North Africa, the Horn of Africa, the Sahel zone, the Arabian Peninsula, and inland Australia. Most included countries and territories in the Americas and Oceania are dominated by tropical climates, while African countries and territories show more obvious climatic heterogeneity: among the 58 African countries and territories, 32 are dominated by tropical climates, and 23 are dominated by arid climates. The climate types of tropical countries and territories in Asia are also relatively complex, including tropical rainforest, monsoon, and savanna climates in Southeast Asia, as well as arid or temperate climates in parts of West Asia and South Asia. Overall, geographically defined tropical countries and territories are not equivalent to climatologically defined tropical-climate zones, as they internally include multiple dominant climate types, such as tropical, arid, and temperate.

Age- and sex-specific distribution of malaria burden in tropical countries and territories in 2021

In 2021, malaria burden across the 126 GBD-matched tropical countries and territories demonstrated a pronounced age-dependent distribution pattern, with the greatest disease burden concentrated among children under 5 years of age (Fig. 4). Across all evaluated indicators, including incidence, prevalence, deaths, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), years lived with disability (YLDs), and years of life lost (YLLs), children aged <5 years consistently represented the population group with the highest absolute burden and age-specific rates.

Age- and sex-specific distribution of malaria burden in 126 GBD-matched tropical countries and territories in 2021

The age distribution of malaria incidence showed a steep decline with increasing age. The highest number of incident cases and age-specific incidence rates were observed among children younger than 5 years, followed by a progressive reduction throughout childhood, adolescence, and adulthood (Fig. 4A). A comparable pattern was observed for malaria prevalence, with the largest number of prevalent cases occurring in young children and gradually decreasing with advancing age (Fig. 4B), indicating that early-life exposure remains a dominant contributor to malaria morbidity in tropical populations.

Mortality-related outcomes exhibited a distinct age-specific pattern. Although malaria deaths were overwhelmingly concentrated among children aged <5 years, mortality rates demonstrated a secondary increase among older adults, particularly those aged approximately 65–74 years (Fig. 4C). This pattern suggests that, in addition to childhood vulnerability, ageing populations may represent another high-risk group for fatal malaria outcomes.

The distribution of DALYs and its components further highlighted the disproportionate impact of malaria on young children. DALYs and YLLs showed highly similar age patterns, with the greatest burden occurring in children under 5 years and declining sharply in subsequent age groups (Fig. 4D, F). This indicates that premature mortality among young children remains the primary driver of malaria-related health loss. In contrast, YLDs exhibited a relatively broader age distribution, although the highest burden was still observed among children aged <5 years, reflecting persistent morbidity associated with malaria infection across the life course (Fig. 4E).

Sex-stratified analyses showed largely consistent age-specific patterns between males and females. However, modest sex differences were observed across outcomes. Males generally experienced slightly higher mortality-related burden, including deaths, DALYs, and YLLs, whereas females showed comparable or slightly higher prevalence and YLDs in several age groups. These findings indicate that malaria burden patterns are primarily associated with age-related vulnerability, with sex contributing relatively smaller variations.

Overall, the age-specific profile demonstrates that malaria remains predominantly a disease affecting young children in tropical regions, while the increasing mortality burden among older adults highlights the need for age-targeted prevention and clinical management strategies.

Correlations between the age-standardized burden of malaria and the proportion of the Köppen–Geiger tropical climate

In 2021, no significant linear correlation was observed between age-standardized malaria burden indicators and the proportion of Köppen–Geiger tropical climate in tropical countries and territories. Pearson correlation coefficients between processed-area tropical-climate share and ASIR, ASPR, ASMR, DALY rate, YLL rate, and YLD rate were low: 0.089 (95% CI − 0.088 to 0.261; P = 0.323), 0.122 (95% CI − 0.055 to 0.291; P = 0.175), 0.060 (95% CI − 0.117 to 0.233; P = 0.507), 0.068 (95% CI − 0.109 to 0.240; P = 0.453), 0.067 (95% CI − 0.110 to 0.240; P = 0.459), and 0.085 (95% CI − 0.092 to 0.257; P = 0.344), respectively. The complete correlation results, including analytic sample size, Pearson coefficients with 95% confidence intervals, Spearman coefficients with bootstrap 95% confidence intervals, and p values, are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

In contrast, Spearman’s rank correlation analysis revealed weak but statistically significant negative correlations between processed-area tropical-climate share and all six age-standardized indicators: rho= − 0.203 (95% CI − 0.384 to − 0.020; P = 0.023) for ASIR, − 0.201 (95% CI − 0.383 to − 0.018; P = 0.024) for ASPR, − 0.248 (95% CI − 0.430 to − 0.064; P = 0.005) for ASMR, − 0.237 (95% CI − 0.417 to − 0.056; P = 0.008) for DALY rate, − 0.250 (95% CI − 0.429 to − 0.068; P = 0.005) for YLL rate, and − 0.224 (95% CI − 0.405 to − 0.044; P = 0.012) for YLD rate. These results suggest that, although there is no stable linear dose–response relationship between processed-area tropical-climate share and malaria burden, countries with higher processed-area tropical-climate shares do not necessarily have higher age-standardized malaria burden. The divergence between Pearson and Spearman estimates may plausibly reflect the highly skewed distribution of tropical-climate share and the influence of a small number of countries with high burden but relatively limited tropical-climate area. Because no formal outlier-removal analysis was prespecified, this explanation should be regarded as interpretive rather than confirmatory.

High-burden countries were distributed across different levels of tropical-climate proportion: Liberia, Sierra Leone, and the Solomon Islands belonged to the fully tropical-climate group; Benin and the Democratic Republic of the Congo belonged to the core tropical-climate group; Burkina Faso belonged to the partial tropical-climate group, whereas Niger had a relatively low tropical-climate proportion but still exhibited high mortality and DALY burdens. Overall, processed-area tropical-climate share alone was insufficient to explain cross-country variation in malaria burden, which may also reflect differences in vector ecology, precipitation, altitude, health-system capacity, socioeconomic development, and control interventions.

Correlations between age-standardized malaria burden and sociodemographic indices

In 2021, the age-standardized disease-burden indicators of malaria in tropical countries and territories were significantly negatively correlated with the sociodemographic index (SDI), indicating that the higher the level of sociodemographic development was, the lower the overall malaria burden. Among the 126 countries and territories included in the disease-burden analysis, Pearson correlation coefficients between SDI and ASIR, ASPR, ASMR, DALY rate, YLL rate, and YLD rate were − 0.666 (95% CI − 0.753 to − 0.555; P < 0.001), − 0.621 (95% CI − 0.718 to − 0.500; P < 0.001), − 0.639 (95% CI − 0.732 to − 0.522; P < 0.001), − 0.647 (95% CI − 0.739 to − 0.532; P < 0.001), − 0.646 (95% CI − 0.738 to − 0.531; P < 0.001), and − 0.650 (95% CI − 0.741 to − 0.536; P < 0.001), respectively. Consistent results were obtained from Spearman correlation analysis, with coefficients of − 0.786 (95% CI − 0.845 to − 0.704; P < 0.001), − 0.789 (95% CI − 0.849 to − 0.705; P < 0.001), − 0.781 (95% CI − 0.846 to − 0.694; P < 0.001), − 0.795 (95% CI − 0.853 to − 0.713; P < 0.001), − 0.780 (95% CI − 0.844 to − 0.692; P < 0.001), and − 0.792 (95% CI − 0.852 to − 0.709; P < 0.001), respectively. Confidence intervals and analytic sample sizes for all SDI correlation estimates are reported in Supplementary Table S3.

From the perspective of scatter distribution, countries with high malaria burdens are concentrated mainly in the low to lower-middle SDI range. Countries such as Liberia, Benin, Burkina Faso, Sierra Leone, Niger and the Democratic Republic of the Congo are located in high-burden areas in terms of multiple indicators, and their SDI is mostly at a low level. In contrast, the age-standardized malaria burden in high-SDI countries and territories is generally close to zero. After grouping by SDI quartile, the median age-standardized incidence rate, prevalence rate, mortality rate, DALY rate, YLL rate and YLD rate in countries and territories in the lowest SDI quartile were 18419.82, 14165.73, 56.82, 3144.35, 2976.55 and 157.12 per 100,000 population, respectively, while the medians of the above indicators in countries and territories in the highest SDI quartile were all 0.

The nonlinear fitting curve further shows that the malaria burden does not decrease linearly with increasing SDI but remains at a high level in countries with low-to-low–middle SDI levels, then decreases rapidly with increasing SDI, and remains at a low level in the high-SDI range. Overall, SDI has a strong negative association with malaria burden that is consistent across Pearson and Spearman analyses, suggesting that socioeconomic development and improvements in educational level and population structure may be closely related to the reduction in malaria burden.

Decomposition analysis of changes in the absolute malaria burden in tropical countries and territories from 1990 to 2021

From 1990 to 2021, the absolute changes in malaria burden in 126 tropical countries and territories that could be matched with GBD data significantly differed across indicators. Overall, the number of malaria incident cases, prevalent cases, deaths, and YLDs increased, whereas DALYs and YLLs decreased. In both sexes combined, the number of incident cases increased from 209 million in 1990 to 245 million in 2021, with a net increase of approximately 35.2983 million; the number of prevalent cases increased from 157 to 172 million, with a net increase of approximately 15.1507 million; and the number of deaths increased from 709,100 to 738,900, with a net increase of approximately 29,700. In contrast, DALYs decreased from 57.0060 million to 54.5077 million, a reduction of approximately 2.4984 million; YLLs decreased from 54.9462 million to 52.1740 million, a reduction of approximately 2.7722 million. YLDs increased from 2.0598 million to 2.3336 million, an increase of approximately 273,800.

Decomposition analysis revealed that population growth was the main factor contributing to the increase in the absolute malaria burden. In both sexes combined, the population-growth component contributed approximately 103 million incident cases, 74.8769 million prevalent cases, 328,900 deaths, 25.4662 million DALYs, 24.4715 million YLLs, and 994,800 YLDs. In contrast, the age-specific-rate (epidemiological) component was negative for all indicators, contributing reductions of approximately 19.2894 million incident cases, 38.2188 million prevalent cases, 173,000 deaths, 13.0319 million DALYs, 12.6959 million YLLs, and 336,000 YLDs. These negative components indicate that changes in age-specific rates offset part of the increase associated with population growth; the decomposition itself cannot identify which interventions or contextual factors produced those rate changes.

The population-age-structure component was also negative across the six burden indicators. For both sexes combined, changes in the age structure reduced the number of incident cases, prevalent cases, deaths, DALYs, YLLs, and YLDs by approximately 48.1315 million, 21.5075 million, 126,100, 14.9327 million, 14.5478 million, and 384,900, respectively. Considering that the malaria burden is usually more concentrated in children and younger people, this component is consistent with a shift in the population distribution away from the youngest high-risk age groups; it should be interpreted as a descriptive decomposition component rather than a causal effect.

Sex-stratified results revealed that the net increases in the number of incident cases, prevalent cases, and YLDs were greater in females than in males, whereas the increase in the number of deaths was greater in males than in females. From 1990 to 2021, the number of incident cases increased by approximately 16.9246 million in males and 18.3736 million in females; the number of prevalent cases increased by approximately 6.7969 million in males and 8.3537 million in females; and the number of deaths increased by approximately 23,000 in males and 6,800 in females. DALYs and YLLs decreased in both males and females, with a greater decrease in females. Overall, the change in the absolute malaria burden in tropical regions from 1990 to 2021 was associated mainly with population growth, while the negative age-specific-rate and population-age-structure components offset a considerable portion of the positive population-growth component.

ARIMA prediction of age-standardized malaria burden in tropical regions, 2022–2050

An ARIMA model was established on the basis of age-standardized disease-burden trends of malaria in tropical regions from 1990 to 2021, and projections were made for 2022 to 2050. During the historical observation period, most indicators reached high levels around 2000, followed by an overall decline and a slight recovery after 2018. During the forecast period, the point estimates of the six age-standardized indicators generally tended to plateau and did not show a sustained and significant downward trend. For both sexes combined, the retained models were ARIMA(0,2,0) for ASIR, ARIMA(2,2,0) for ASPR, ARIMA(1,2,0) for ASMR, ARIMA(0,2,3) for DALY rate, ARIMA(0,2,3) for YLL rate, and ARIMA(0,2,1) for YLD rate. The Ljung–Box residual diagnostic p values ranged from 0.078 to 0.555, indicating no statistically significant residual autocorrelation in the retained models. Full ADF results, AICc values, and residual diagnostics are summarized in Supplementary Table S4.

Specifically, the age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) was 4179.25 per 100,000 in 2021, projected to be 4222.83 per 100,000 in 2022, and is projected to reach 4288.08 per 100,000 by 2050, an increase of 1.55% compared with that in 2022. The age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR) is projected to decrease from 2841.24 per 100,000 in 2022 to 2,769.63 per 100,000 in 2050, a decrease of 2.52%. The age-standardized mortality rate is projected to slightly decrease from 13.61 per 100,000 in 2022 to 13.42 per 100,000 in 2050, a decrease of 1.39%.

Health loss-related indicators also show a relatively stable or slightly decreasing trend. The age-standardized DALY rate is projected to decrease from 1025.02 per 100,000 in 2022 to 1004.59 per 100,000 in 2050, a decrease of 1.99%; the age-standardized YLL rate is projected to decrease from 985.43 per 100,000 to 964.86 per 100,000, a decrease of 2.09%; and the age-standardized YLD rate is projected to decrease from 39.46 per 100,000 to 39.13 per 100,000, a decrease of 0.83%. Overall, the ARIMA prediction results suggest that the age-standardized burden of malaria in tropical regions may remain at a high plateau level in the coming decades, especially as the incidence rate does not significantly decrease.

Notably, as the forecast horizon lengthens, the 95% prediction intervals for each indicator gradually increase, indicating substantial uncertainty in long-term predictions. By 2050, the 95% prediction interval for the ASIR is 3011.90–6105.00 per 100,000, the ASPR is 1543.48–4969.85 per 100,000, the mortality rate is 7.37–24.45 per 100,000, the DALY rate is 559.68–1803.17 per 100,000, the YLL rate is 528.06–1762.96 per 100,000, and the YLD rate is 24.82–61.70 per 100,000.

Discussion

Summary of key findings

This study provides an integrated assessment of malaria burden across 126 GBD-matched tropical countries and territories from 1990 to 2021. The main findings were that age-standardized malaria burden declined in most countries, but absolute incident cases and several nonfatal measures remained high, because population-growth offset part of the gains from epidemiological improvement. Burden was concentrated in West Africa and among children younger than 5 years, with a secondary mortality concern among older adults. SDI showed a strong inverse ecological association with all age-standardized indicators, whereas processed-area tropical-climate share showed weak and inconsistent associations. ARIMA forecasts suggested that age-standardized burden may remain at a high plateau through 2050, especially given widening prediction intervals.

These findings indicate that overall malaria control has improved, but progress is uneven and vulnerable to demographic pressure. They also support the study rationale: country-level climate classification helps describe ecological context, but it cannot by itself explain why some tropical countries carry much higher malaria burden than others.

Comparison with previous studies

GBD-based studies and spatial modeling analyses have reported substantial long-term declines in global malaria incidence and mortality, followed by slower progress and post-2015 or COVID-era rebounds in some settings [3, 15, 16]. Our tropical-country analysis is consistent with those reports but extends them by isolating 126 tropical countries and separating changes in absolute burden into population growth, age-structure change, and epidemiological change. This helps explain why population growth can offset nearly two-thirds of epidemiological gains in some aggregate indicators. The decomposition results should be interpreted as attribution to demographic and rate components, not as evidence that any specific intervention caused the observed epidemiological component.

Regional studies also show that malaria trends can rebound despite national or regional progress. Studies from China and ASEAN countries, Europe, and Brazil illustrate that socioeconomic conditions, importation, land-use change, mobility, and local control capacity can shape malaria persistence or resurgence [9, 17, 18]. Our findings similarly show that high burden was concentrated in selected countries rather than uniformly distributed across the tropical belt.

Explanation of spatial distribution patterns

Climate remains biologically relevant to malaria transmission, but our national-level measure captured the proportion of Köppen–Geiger tropical climate rather than fine-scale rainfall, humidity, temperature, altitude, vector density, or intervention coverage. The weak Pearson correlations and negative Spearman correlations may reflect skewed climate-proportion distributions, nonlinearity, and influential countries with high burden despite limited tropical-climate area. Local studies using meteorological variables and vector-specific data have shown clearer climate effects [19, 20], supporting the interpretation that national climate proportion is too coarse to represent transmission suitability by itself. Because no formal outlier-removal analysis was prespecified, this explanation should be regarded as interpretive rather than confirmatory.

The spatial distribution pattern of high burden in low-SDI countries and low burden in high-SDI countries provides quantitative support for the importance of socioeconomic context. After stratification by SDI quartiles, the median age-standardized incidence rate in the lowest SDI quartile was 18,419.82 per 100,000 population, whereas the median in the highest SDI quartile was 0. This pattern was consistent across multidimensional burden indicators and is compatible with evidence that socioeconomic development, household water management, housing, health resources, education, and vector-control capacity influence malaria prevention and care [21, 22]. The findings should be interpreted as ecological associations rather than individual-level causal effects.

SDI summarizes income, education, and fertility and may reflect living conditions, health-system capacity, and the ability to maintain prevention and treatment services. Our finding of a robust negative association between SDI and malaria burden is consistent with previous GBD-based analyses [8, 17]. Higher education may improve care seeking and prevention knowledge, higher household income may improve access to protective tools and treatment, and stronger health systems can support diagnosis, treatment, surveillance, indoor residual spraying, and community-based case management. Population growth adds another planning challenge: services and supplies must expand with population size; otherwise, per capita intervention intensity may decline.

Age structure is central to malaria burden. Children younger than 5 years accounted for the greatest burden across indicators, consistent with GBD-based evidence on childhood malaria [8]. The negative age-structure component in the decomposition suggests that demographic shifts away from the youngest high-risk ages reduced absolute burden in the aggregate, but rapidly growing and youthful populations in parts of West Africa may continue to sustain high-absolute numbers. Older adults also deserve attention, because mortality rates showed a secondary increase in later life.

The burden was highly concentrated in a small number of West African countries, where resources and interventions need to be focused. Coverage of insecticide-treated nets, indoor residual spraying, rapid diagnosis, effective treatment, and malaria vaccination should be expanded in high-risk populations; evidence from intervention modeling and systematic reviews supports the major role of vector control in reducing malaria transmission and burden [23–25]. Urban prevention against Anopheles stephensi should also be strengthened through surveillance, drainage, environmental management, and community governance, especially where urban water storage and unplanned expansion create vector habitats.

Public health strategies should, therefore, combine malaria-specific interventions with broader health-system and development policies. Strengthening surveillance, laboratory confirmation, treatment availability, case follow-up, and cause-of-death registration is essential in low-SDI settings and aligns with global malaria strategy and guideline recommendations [26–28]. Socioeconomic development, poverty reduction, education, and infrastructure improvement can support malaria control, but these ecological findings should be translated into policy cautiously and tested with subnational or longitudinal data where possible.

The RTS,S malaria vaccine was recommended by WHO in 2021, and the R21/Matrix-M vaccine was recommended by WHO in 2023, providing additional tools for reducing childhood malaria deaths. Their evidence base includes large phase 3 trials of RTS,S/AS01 and R21/Matrix-M in African children and recent synthesis of malaria vaccine development [29–31]. Population growth should also be incorporated into resource allocation: countries with rapid population growth need timely expansion of diagnostic capacity, antimalarial drug supply, vector-control commodities, vaccination delivery where eligible, and services for mobile and border populations. Cross-border surveillance and regional data sharing are particularly important, where transmission is sustained across neighboring high-burden countries.

The WHO Global Technical Strategy for Malaria calls for reductions of at least 90% in malaria incidence and mortality rates by 2030 relative to 2015 [27]. Our ARIMA projections are historical-trend extrapolations and are not directly equivalent to WHO target-monitoring metrics; nevertheless, the projected plateau and widening prediction intervals suggest that continuation of recent trends alone may be insufficient to achieve the intended pace of reduction. Accelerated, context-specific control efforts will, therefore, be required, particularly in high-burden, low-SDI settings.

Research limitations

First, the data in this study are derived mainly from GBD 2021 estimates. Although GBD uses multisource data and statistical modeling, estimates may be uncertain in countries with limited surveillance, incomplete case registration, or weak cause-of-death data. Second, the ARIMA forecasts are statistical extrapolations based on historical age-standardized rates and do not incorporate future changes in climate, policy, resistance, vaccine implementation, conflict, or health-system disruption; therefore, long-term point estimates should be interpreted together with the widening prediction intervals. Third, this study used country-level ecological comparisons, which may not capture subnational heterogeneity or individual-level risk, so ecological fallacy remains possible. Fourth, because the Köppen–Geiger raster was processed within a 38°S–38°N window, the derived tropical-climate share may not represent the complete national territory of countries extending beyond this window. This exposure should, therefore, be interpreted as a processed-area climate-composition measure rather than a complete national climatic exposure. Fifth, the secondary analyses were based on GBD point estimates and did not propagate the uncertainty of the underlying GBD estimates. Therefore, the reported EAPCs, ecological correlations, decomposition components, and ARIMA forecasts may under-represent the total uncertainty associated with the original disease-burden estimates. Future studies using higher-resolution longitudinal data are warranted.

Future research directions

(1) Constructing multifactor prediction models: Future studies should integrate high-resolution geographical, ecological, and socioeconomic data and use machine learning or Bayesian multilevel models to incorporate factors such as climate change, land use, urbanization, poverty, education, and public health investment into prediction models to reveal complex underlying mechanisms and improve prediction accuracy.

(2) Improving spatial resolution: Analyze data at subnational levels (such as provinces, cities, and districts) or urban levels to identify local hotspots and vulnerable groups, guiding precise prevention and control. Conclusion

Malaria burden in tropical countries and territories has declined in age-standardized terms over the past three decades, but high-absolute burden persists in low-SDI settings, especially in West Africa, and population growth continues to offset part of the gains from epidemiological improvement. Processed-area tropical-climate share alone was not a strong predictor of malaria burden in ecological comparisons, whereas SDI showed robust negative associations with all age-standardized indicators. Progress toward global malaria targets will require strengthened surveillance, sustained vector control, reliable diagnosis and treatment, malaria vaccination where appropriate, and health-system investment that keeps pace with demographic growth in low-SDI countries. Although these age-standardized GBD rates are not identical to WHO target-monitoring indicators, the projected plateau underscores the need for accelerated progress toward WHO 2030 targets.
