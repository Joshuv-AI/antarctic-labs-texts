Testosterone treatment for micropenis in partial androgen insensitivity syndrome
Authors: Marie Shuto, Tomoyo Itonaga, Fumika Matsuda, Miwako Maeda, Noriko Oyama, Kenji Ihara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe optimal approach to hormonal treatment aimed at increasing penis size has been established for infant males with micropenis; however, data are limited in partial androgen insensitivity syndrome (PAIS). We report a 6-month-old male patient treated with intramuscular testosterone for micropenis. At birth, he presented with atypical genitalia, including a microphallus, hypospadias, and a bifid scrotum. His karyotype was 46,XY, and endocrine evaluation revealed normal testicular function. A novel missense variant of the AR gene (p.G578E) was identified. Intramuscular testosterone enanthate 62.5 mg/dose was administered every 4 weeks for 3 doses (6-8 months). Stretched penile length increased from 18 to 30 mm (+67%) without adverse events. During treatment, luteinizing hormone secretion was suppressed, with trough levels falling below the limit of detection, consistent with androgen-mediated negative feedback. Luteinizing hormone levels during mini-puberty in response to testosterone treatment, supporting testosterone therapy in patients with PAIS.

Introduction

Androgen insensitivity syndrome (AIS) is a 46,XY difference in sex development (DSD) caused by loss-of-function pathogenic variants in the androgen receptor (AR) gene on the X chromosome. Based on the severity of AR resistance to androgens, AIS is clinically classified as complete AIS (CAIS) or partial AIS (PAIS) [1]. CAIS is characterized by complete resistance to the effects of androgens, resulting in a female phenotype with external genitalia corresponding to grades 6-7 on the Quigley scale. In contrast, PAIS presents with variable masculinization, ranging from grades 2 to 5 [2].

Testosterone therapy has been widely applied to infantile micropenis. The standard regimen is intramuscular injection of testosterone enanthate at doses of 25-50 mg every 4 weeks, 3-4 times [3-5]. Preoperative testosterone therapy for patients with microphallus in hypospadias repair can increase penile size, facilitate earlier surgery, and reduce complications [6]. As in the management of other forms of micropenis, testosterone therapy may also help achieve several clinically relevant objectives, including promoting external genital masculinization, increasing penile size, particularly glans width, thereby providing more tissue for surgical use during reconstructive procedures [7], and supporting broader physical, social, and emotional well-being [8-10].

In infants with PAIS, the response to hormone therapy depends on residual AR function. Since no consensus guidelines have been established regarding the indication for or regimen of testosterone therapy, hormonal treatment remains largely institution-dependent, with substantial variations in dosing or in the choice of androgen (testosterone or dihydrotestosterone) during infancy or adolescence [11-13].

Herein, we report the case of an infant with PAIS with a novel AR missense variant who responded well to testosterone enanthate injections for penile elongation. We compared this case with previously reported cases and discussed treatment strategies, as well as a potential surrogate marker, luteinizing hormone (LH) suppression, for testosterone treatment.

Case presentation

The patient was born at 38 weeks and 6 days of gestation via spontaneous vaginal delivery as the second child of healthy, unrelated Japanese parents, without any assisted reproductive technology. The birth size was appropriate for gestational age (weight: 2870 g; length: 47.0 cm). After birth, the patient was immediately transferred to the neonatal intensive-care unit of a regional general hospital because of atypical external genitalia. Hyperpigmentation was not observed. The genital tubercle was 15 mm long and 9 mm wide, and the urethral meatus was positioned at the genital tubercle base. The gonads were not palpable within the labioscrotal fold. The anogenital ratio is 0.63 (Fig. 1). No other morphological abnormalities were present, and there were no complications, such as hypoglycemia or respiratory distress. The patient had no family history of adrenal disease or DSD. The elder sister (3 years old) showed complete female external genitalia (grade 6/7 on the Quigly scale), and her karyotype by G-banding was 46,XX (performed with informed parental consent after the diagnosis of the present case).

The etiology of the external genital abnormality was assessed from endocrinological, imaging or genetic viewpoints. Blood tests at one day old showed that both testosterone and anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) levels were within the normal range in a male infant (Table 1). Adrenal hormone levels (serum cortisol and ACTH levels) were normal. Abdominal magnetic resonance imaging at 7 days old demonstrated a left gonad to the left of the bladder in the abdominal cavity and a right gonad in the inguinal canal. The gonads had a testis-like appearance, whereas uterine structures were not detected. At 9 days old, chromosome testing by fluorescence in situ hybridization confirmed SRY gene positivity. At 19 days of age, chromosomal testing (G-banding) revealed a 46,XY karyotype. Based on these findings, the attending physician proposed to the parents that the child's legal sex should be assigned as male at 22 days of age. He was referred to our hospital at 2 months old for further evaluation.

Diagnostic assessment

At 2 months of age, the stretched penile length (SPL) was 25 mm (average ± standard deviation [SD] at 2 months: 32.2 ± 3.6 mm) [14] with a width of 16 mm (including the foreskin). The urethral meatus was positioned at the base of the penis, which was a hypospadias. The scrotum was hypoplastic and bifid, and no gonads were palpable in the scrotum or the inguinal region. Neurological evaluation demonstrated an age-appropriate development. Blood tests showed normal levels of LH, 1.9 IU/L (1.9 mIU/mL) (reference range [RR] in male infants at 0-1 year old: 0.1-4.7 IU/L [0.1-4.7 mIU/mL]); follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), 2.5 IU/L (2.5 mIU/mL) (RR in male infants at 0-1 year old: 0.4-5.7 IU/L [0.4-5.7 mIU/mL]); 17beta-estradiol, <26 pmol/L (<7 pg/mL); AMH, 750 pmol/L (105 ng/mL) (RR in male infants at 1 month-0.9 years of age: 454-944 pmol/L [63.6-132.2 ng/mL]), with high testosterone, 8.63 nmol/L (2.49 ng/mL) (RR in male infants <1 year old: 0.42-0.73 nmol/L [0.12-0.21 ng/mL]). With parental consent, a comprehensive genetic analysis for 46,XY DSD was performed, targeting 11 genes (AR, HSD17B3, HSD3B2, NR5A1, SRD5A2, SRY, WT1, ANOS1, CHD7, FGF8, FGFR1). We identified a novel hemizygous missense variant (c.1733G > A: p.Gly578Glu) in the AR gene, which has not been previously reported in ClinVar. In silico analysis (Polyphen-2) predicted that it was probably damaging. The p.Gly578Glu variant was located in the DNA-binding domain of the AR receptor and in a previously reported case of PAIS with another variant at the same residue (p.Gly578Arg) [15]. According to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines [16], the p.Gly578Glu variant is likely pathogenic. Thus, the patient was diagnosed with PAIS caused by a hemizygous missense variant of the AR gene.

Treatment

At 6 months of age, the first intramuscular injection of testosterone enanthate (62.5 mg per dose) was administered. A higher than standard dose (2 mg/kg or 25 mg monthly) was selected because the basal testosterone level was markedly elevated, suggesting reduced androgen receptor function. When adjusted for the body surface area, the dose was comparable to the adult maximal dose (250 mg). SPL was measured according to the method described by Ishii et al [14]. Measurements at 2 months were performed by a single examiner, whereas measurements during treatment (6-9 months) were independently performed by 2 examiners and averaged. At 7 months, we considered him to have responded to treatment based on the following 3 findings (Fig. 2): (1) the SPL had increased from 18 to 22 mm (average ± SD at 7 months: 34.5 ± 3.9 mm) [14]; (2) the serum testosterone levels were maintained high (7.32 nmol/L [2.11 ng/mL] at 3 months to 6.10 nmol/L [1.76 ng/mL] at 7 months); and (3) LH was suppressed by the testosterone treatment (from 2.5 IU/L [2.5 mIU/mL] at 3 months to <0.3 IU/L [<0.3 mIU/mL] at 7 months). We administered the same dose of testosterone enanthate every 4 weeks for 7 and 8 months.

Outcome and follow-up

At 9 months of age, the SPL increased to 30 mm (average ± SD at 9 months: 35.2 ± 4.0 mm) [14] with increasing penile width. No apparent side effects, such as pubic hair development, penile pigmentation, liver dysfunction, or dyslipidemia, were observed during the course of treatment. He underwent the first urethroplasty procedure at one year and 2 months old at another hospital for perineoscrotal hypospadias and bilateral cryptorchidism.

Discussion

Here, we report the clinical course of testosterone therapy in a male infant with PAIS, caused by a novel missense variant of the AR gene. DHT, although internationally reported for use in infants with PAIS, was not available in most institutions in Japan; therefore, testosterone enanthate was administered instead. His SPL increased by 67% after 3 intramuscular injections of testosterone enanthate without adverse effects. Five infantile cases of micropenis, including our own, have been reported to show normal SPL after testosterone treatment (Table 2) [12, 13, 17]. Treatment regimens of intramuscular testosterone administration ranged from standard-dose (25-50 mg/dose, 2-3 times) to high-dose (62.5-100 mg/dose, 3 times), sometimes combined with topical DHT. Even among patients sharing the same AR variant (L712F; Cases 3-5), responses to testosterone injection varied widely (33–100%), indicating that genotype alone cannot predict treatment efficacy in PAIS. In our case, a higher dose than the standard regimen was selected, and gonadotropin levels were serially monitored. Testosterone enanthate was administered every 4 weeks, and hormone levels were measured immediately before the next dose (trough). Since LH suppression after a single injection is expected to persist for 2-4 weeks, repeated injections under higher dosing regimens of testosterone therapy are anticipated to suppress gonadotropin secretion under negative feedback in the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis [18-20]. The trough LH level was below the detection level, suggesting that circulating testosterone exceeded the threshold required to elicit an AR response of gonadotropin suppression. As the previously reported case did not document therapy-associated changes in hormone levels, additional evaluation in other patients with PAIS is warranted.

A case series of pubertal testosterone therapy demonstrated similar variability. Six pubertal cases were reported (Table 3) [11-13, 21]. Many reported patients were treated with topical DHT, and their response patterns differed substantially. Three patients underwent 2 or more treatment courses: one during infancy and puberty, and 2 after puberty alone. Case 5 (L712F) responded positively in both infancy and adolescence, suggesting that the therapeutic response observed during infancy may serve as an early indicator for predicting therapeutic efficacy in adolescence [12]. Cases 7 and 9 both harbored ligand-binding domain variants (L712F and Y571H, respectively) and underwent therapy twice after puberty; however, their outcomes diverged. Case 9 showed no elongation with intramuscular testosterone but responded to topical DHT [11], whereas case 7 showed no response to either modality, possibly due to desensitization following previous high-dose testosterone exposure [12]. These findings might indicate that penile response to testosterone enanthate is multifactorial and not predicted by genotype or prior therapy. Topical DHT may be advantageous in puberty because of its high AR affinity and resistance to aromatization, reducing gynecomastia [22]. We propose that monitoring of LH serves as a surrogate of systemic androgen activity and treatment response in pubertal PAIS. Although LH and FSH suppression has been reported in 5α-reductase type 2 deficiency treated with DHT [23, 24], they have not been used as therapeutic indicators. Penile elongation varies by genetic background; hormonal monitoring may enable individualized dose adjustment in patients with an intact HPG axis. The accumulation of such hormone-linked clinical data will help in the development of treatment strategies.

This study had some limitations. The apparent discrepancy whereby the SPL at 6 months was shorter than that at 2 months may reflect erectile state differences, although this is speculative. In addition, conclusions regarding dose selection and treatment response are limited by the single-case design.
