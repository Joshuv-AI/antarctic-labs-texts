Investigation of exon skipping therapy in kidney organoids from Alport syndrome patients derived iPSCs
Authors: Kensuke Yabuuchi, Tomoko Horinouchi, Tomohiko Yamamura, Kandai Nozu, Minoru Takasato
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlport syndrome (AS) is a hereditary disease caused by mutations in the COL4A5 gene and leads to chronic kidney disease. Currently, no specific treatment has been developed. However, a recent study using AS‐model mice demonstrated that the exon skipping method could partially rescue the symptoms. In this study, we evaluated the effects of the exon skipping method using kidney organoids generated from AS‐patient‐derived induced pluripotent stem cells (AS‐iPSCs). We generated kidney organoids from AS‐iPSCs, which exhibited nephron structures. As expected, the C‐terminus of COL4A5 was not expressed in AS‐organoids. Interestingly, anti‐sense oligonucleotides restored the expression of the C‐terminus of COL4A5 in vitro. Next, we transplanted AS‐organoids into mice and evaluated glomerular basement membrane formation in vivo. We found that AS‐organoids formed a lower slit diaphragm ratio compared to control organoids. Finally, we assessed the effects of exon skipping on transplanted organoids but observed minimum effects. These studies suggest that AS‐iPSCs can generate kidney organoids lacking the C‐terminus of COL4A5, and that exon skipping can induce its expression in vitro.

INTRODUCTION

The kidney is a crucial organ in mammals, responsible for regulating in vivo homeostasis and performing various functions such as blood filtration, urine excretion, and hormone secretion. Kidneys are composed of tubular structures (nephrons), interstitium, and blood vessels. Nephrons, which are the functional units of the kidney, consist of glomeruli, tubules, and collecting ducts. An adult kidney contains approximately one million nephrons (Kanzaki et al., 2020). However, kidneys have a limited regenerative capacity, making it challenging for them to fully recover function after severe injury (Andrianova et al., 2019). Consequently, patients with chronic kidney failure require continuous treatment with dialysis or kidney transplantation. Unfortunately, the number of available donors for all patients with chronic kidney disease is insufficient (Lentine et al., 2022).

Alport syndrome (AS) is an inherited disease primarily caused by mutations in the COL4A5 gene on the X chromosome (Savige et al., 2023). COL4A5 is expressed in the anterior part of the lens and in the basement membrane of the canaliculi, leading to symptoms such as hearing loss and visual impairment due to abnormal basement membrane formation in these areas (Bai et al., 2009; Jais et al., 2003; Yamamura et al., 2017; Zehnder et al., 2005). COL4A5 is also present in the kidneys, specifically in the basement membrane of glomeruli and proximal tubules, resulting in proteinuria in AS patients. COL4A5 gene is located in X chromosome and male have only one X chromosome, so the mutation in COL4A5 often causes severe symptoms and chronic renal failure, particularly in male patients (Jais et al., 2003). Currently, the standard care for AS is the chemical treatment such like renin angiotensin aldosterone system inhibitor and mineralocorticoid blocker, which aim to slow the progression of AS. However, there is no curative therapy for AS (Chavez et al., 2022).

Several studies have investigated diseases caused by genetic mutations, including AS. Recently, research using human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) have been conducted (Morais et al., 2022). For instance, Hirayama et al. induced kidney organoids from iPSCs derived from AS patients and evaluated the efficacy of compounds as potential therapeutic agents (Hirayama et al., 2023). Additionally, research on exon skipping has advanced (Matsuo, 2021). AS is caused by mutations in the COL4A5 gene, and the nature of these mutations significantly impacts the disease state. When a stop codon forms on an exon due to a terminal mutation or frameshift, a truncated COL4A5 protein is produced. Exon skipping can partially repair these truncation variants by splicing out specific exons using antisense oligonucleotides (ASO). The therapeutic efficacy of exon skipping has been demonstrated in Duchenne muscular dystrophy (Komaki et al., 2020). Furthermore, a study using a mouse model of AS showed that exon skipping improved AS symptoms based on histological and serological tests, with a significant increase in COL4A5 expression (Yamamura et al., 2020). These findings suggest that exon skipping may be a viable treatment option for AS, but it has not yet been applied to human patients.

In this study, we generated kidney organoids from human iPSCs derived from AS patients to investigate the effect of exon skipping on human kidneys. iPSC‐derived kidney organoids contain stroma and nephrons (Takasato et al., 2015). Moreover, more bio‐identical kidney induction methods have been reported, such as the formation of vascularized glomeruli in organ‐on‐chip systems or in vivo (Homan et al., 2019; Koning et al., 2022). Based on these advancements, kidney organoids are considered useful for validating human models of pathology (Tanigawa et al., 2018).

RESULTS

The generation of kidney organoids from Alport patients derived iPSCs

To investigate the pluripotency of Alport patients derived iPSCs (K001), we observed the morphology and compared with healthy iPSCs (c1502). Then, K001 showed the similar morphology to c1502 (Figure S1A). Moreover, mRNA expression of pluripotency markers in K001 was comparable to that in c1502 (Figure S1B).

Next, kidney organoids were generated using K001 and compared with kidney organoids generated from c1502. The patient, who is the origine of K001, has a mutation in exon24 in COL4A5 gene, resulting in a stop codon (Figure 1a). And, the patient is male and has only one X chromosome. Therefore, the RNA translation of COL4A5 gene is interrupted due to the mutation. As a result, OCL4A5 generated in the patient lacked the C‐terminus after exon 24.

When the expression of renal markers was examined in kidney organoids induced from c1502 to K001, both kidney organoids showed an increase from day 13 to day 25 of induction (NPHS1: glomeruli, LRP2: proximal renal tubules, SLC12A1: distal renal tubule, FOXD1: renal stroma, Figure 1c). Immunostaining revealed nephron structures (glomeruli and renal tubules) in both organoids, indicating that organoids of K001 have a nephron‐forming capacity similar to that of c1502 (Figure 1d).

Immunostaining with COL4A5 antibody, which reacts with a C‐terminal epitope, showed that C‐terminal of COL4A5 was expressed around the glomerulus in c1502‐derived organoids, suggesting the formation of glomerular basement membrane (GBM) structures (Figure 1e). However, K001‐derived organoids showed a laminin positive basement membrane but no C‐terminal of COL4A5, confirming that C‐terminal of COL4A5 was absent.

These results indicated that K001, like c1502, had the ability to induce kidney organoids but was unable to express C‐terminal of COL4A5.

The effects of the exon skipping in vitro

Recently, we reported that the exon skipping using antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) re‐expressed the C‐terminus of COL4A5 and improved renal function in an Alport mouse model (COL4A5 null) (Yamamura et al., 2020). However, there is no evidence yet in humans. Therefore, we validated the effects of exon skipping using kidney organoids derived from AS patient (Figure 2a).

The data from three independent experiments are represented as the means ± SEM (n = 3). * < 0.05 in Student's t test. (c) Immunofluorescence of kidney organoids on the day 25 of the induction for LAM (Basement membrane), NEPHRIN (podocytes) and COL4A5.

In this experiment, we administered ASO every 2 days from day 13 to day 25 of the differentiation. The mRNA expression in renal and endothelial markers showed no significant differences between ASO treated organoids and control organoids (Figure 2b). Immunostaining also showed the formation of nephron structures in ASO treated organoids, suggesting that ASO has no inhibitory effects on the development of the nephron formation (Figure S2A). To confirm ASO effects on COL4A5, the mRNA expression of the target region (exon24) was quantified by quantitative real time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). The results showed that the mRNA expression of exon24 in COL4A5 was significantly reduced by ASO (p < 0.05). On the other hand, no significant difference was observed in the mRNA expression level of exon49, which is off‐target region of ASO. The mRNA expression of exon49 in COL4A5 was decreased after d13. We found that c1502 showed the similar decrease of COL4A5 expression after d13 (Figure S2B), suggesting this decrease was not caused by the effect of COL4A5 mutation. Furthermore, we immunostained the kidney organoids using C‐terminus of COL4A5 specific antibody, and quantified the rate of COL4A5 positive area to pan‐laminin positive area (Figure 2c, Figure S2C). C‐terminus of COL4A5 was not observed in control organoids, but it was observed in organoids treated by 100 nM ASO. The COL4A5 positive ratio in organoids with 100 nM ASO was relatively increased to in organoids without ASO. These data suggested that exon skipping by this ASO can specifically restore the expression of the C‐terminus of COL4A5 in AS patient‐derived organoids without affecting kidney development.

Evaluation of the transplantation method

At present, the mechanism of glomerular vascular development is not clear, nor has an efficient method of inducing glomerular vessels in vitro been reported (Homan et al., 2019). Glomeruli in kidney organoids in vitro expressed COL4A5. Endothelial cells (ECs) were observed around glomeruli with a peak around d20 (Figure S3A), but not in glomeruli. Therefore, in this study, we transplanted organoids to induce vascularized glomeruli. We transplanted organoids at different induction stages to assess the optimal time of transplantation for vascularization (Figure 3a). We transplanted organoids at d7, d12, and d21. Transplanted organoids were harvested at 35 days after the induction and their morphology was assessed by staining (Figure 3b). In every condition, the formation of nephron structures was observed and, as in other publications, blood vessels invaginating into glomeruli was observed in vivo (Hirayama et al., 2023; Takasato et al., 2015). The presence of blood cells in the transplanted organoids indicated that these vessels were connected to the circulatory system of the host mouse and supply bloods (Figure 3c).

Transplantation of kidney organoids. (a) The protocol of kidney organoids transplantation. (b) Immunofluorescence of transplanted organoids for nephron markers and endothelial markers. (c) Stereo microscopy images of graft on the mesenteric. (d) Immunofluorescence of transplanted organoids on the indicated days for WT1 (podocytes), mMECA32 (mouse endothelia), and hCD31 (human endothelia).

Transplanted organoids were able to develop nephrons under in vivo at all stages. However, in transplantation experiments using K001‐derived organoids, organoids transplanted at d21 showed a more pronounced appearance of SOX9‐positive cartilage‐like cells compared to organoids transplanted at d13 (Figure S3B). Therefore, we transplanted organoids at d13 in the following experiments. At the same time, the ingrowth of mouse‐derived vessels and the distribution of organoid‐derived vessels over time were monitored in this experiment. Figure 3c showed the results of staining of transplanted organoids with mouse‐derived vessel‐recognizing antibody (mMECA32) and human‐derived vessel‐recognizing antibody (CD31). No eECs were present immediately after transplantation (+d2), whereas both of human‐derived ECs (hECs) and mouse‐derived ECs (mECs) are present in the organoids at an early stage (+d4). However, from +d6 onwards, mECs persist and are distributed throughout the organoids, while hECs are disappeared. As mentioned above, ECs enter the glomerulus in vivo, but they were only mECs.

Formation of slit diaphragm of kidney organoids in vivo

Next we compared the microstructures of glomeruli in c1502 and K001 in vivo. c1502 and K001 both showed glomerular vascularization (Figure 4a). The glomerular microstructure was then compared by electron microscopy. Slit diaphragms (SD) of podocytes and fenestrations of glomerular ECs were observed in both c1502 and K001 transplanted organoids (Figure 4b). In addition, the ratio of SD formation was significantly lower in K001‐derived organoids compared to WT (Figure 4c p‐value <.05). On the other hand, no significant differences were observed for GBM thickness or lamina densa. These suggested that the effect of COL4A5 C‐terminal expression in GBM on slit diaphragm formation is limited in kidney organoids in vivo.

The effects of the exon skipping in vivo

The results in Figure 4 showed that ASO can re‐express the C‐terminal expression of COL4A5 in human organoids. Since we reported a trend towards recovery of kidney function during ASO administration, it is expected that ASO will also improve GBM in transplanted human organoids. Finally, to verify the effect of ASO on kidney organoids in vivo, we administered ASO intraperitoneally to mice every 3 days after transplantation of kidney organoids and evaluated the morphology (Figure 5a). We found that the anti‐COL4A5 antibody detected human COL4A5 of iPSCs‐derived kidney organoids, but not cross‐reacted with mouse COL4A5 (Figure S4A), indicating that the COL4A5 expression in the grafts of c1502‐derived organoids was derived from human organoids, but not from host mouse. As expected, K001‐derived organoids did not show COL4A5(C‐terminal) expression, however, which was not recovered even after ASO administration (Figure 5b). We also confirmed the ultrastructure of grafts with and without ASO treatment (Figure 5c), and performed the ultrastructural analysis using electron microscopy (Figure 5d), showing no significant differences in the percentage of SD, GBM thickness or lamina densa.

DISCUSSION

In the present study, AS patient‐derived iPSCs (K001) with a COL4A5 mutation differentiated into nephrons, similar to iPSCs derived from healthy individuals (c1502). As previously reported, ECs also developed, but glomeruli within the organoids remained avascular. However, the C‐terminal expression of COL4A5 was absent in K001 kidney organoids, indicating that normal COL4A5 was not produced. Interestingly, ASO treatment restored COL4A5 expression in K001‐derived organoids in vitro but had no effect in vivo.

Previously, we validated that exon skipping could be a potential treatment for Alport syndrome in a mouse model. Similarly, in the present study, exon skipping restored the expression of the C‐terminus of COL4A5 in K001‐derived kidney organoids, suggesting that this treatment may also be effective in humans. However, no improvement was observed with ASO treatment in vivo. In this study, intraperitoneal administration was used for in vivo treatment, which may have reduced the effect on the transplanted organoids as the majority of the ASO might have been metabolized by the liver (Speyer et al., 1981; Teigen et al., 2022). Future studies should consider more effective routes and conditions, such as intravenous administration.

We evaluated the SD formation was assessed after kidney organoid transplantation. Quantitative assessment of the slit diaphragm and GBM revealed a higher percentage of abnormal slit diaphragm and GBM morphology at 2 weeks posttransplantation (Figure 4c). On the other hand, there was no difference between c1502 and K001 + Dulbecco's Phosphate‐Buffered Saline (DPBS) in the experiment of Figure 5d, where organoids were sampled 3 weeks posttransplantation. This difference of culture period in vivo may explain the reason why there were no difference between c1502 and K001 + DPBS. Actually, SD/GBM showed 11.0% in Figure 4, whereas it decreased to 6.8% in Figure 5, suggesting that long‐term culture in vivo is still suboptimal for the SD formation. Additionally, dominant proliferation of cartilage tissue was observed in kidney organoids cultured for more than 4 weeks posttransplantation (Figure S3C). These results suggest that the current transplantation environment is suboptimal for the maturation of kidney organoids.

Some reports using AS‐iPSCs derived kidney organoids have been reported (Haynes et al., 2018; Hirayama et al., 2023). Hirayama group reported the chemical chaperone, 4‐PBA (4‐phenyl butyric acid), recovered COL4A5 expression in mild AS patient‐derived iPSCs, but not in severe AS patient‐derived iPSCs. The present study showed that the exon skipping therapy recovered COL4A5 expression in organoids derived from severe AS patients with truncating mutation. We have previously studied that the exon skipping could recover COL4A5 expression and rescued the progression of renal failure using Alport mouse model, and we could confirm the usefulness of the exon skipping therapy in human cell lines. Human iPSCs‐derived kidney organoids mimic renal structures, thus are expected to reveal the effects of drugs on human cells. However, there are still some difficulties, one of that is that they are immature in structure and function. AS is the progressive disease, and 70% of men at the age of 30 had reached end‐dtage kidney disease (Jais et al., 2003), while few AS male achieved the severe symptoms until 10 years old. These suggest that current kidney organoids are not mature enough to develop AS disease. It was reported that shear stress on kidney organoids and inducible ETV2 induced the glomerular vascularization in vitro (Homan et al., 2019; Maggiore et al., 2024). Shear stress on kidney organoids did not affect only the glomerular vascularization, but also the maturation of renal tubules. And fluid shear stress promoted the cyst formation in organoids derived from polycystic kidney disease patient‐iPSCs and juvenile nephronophthisis patient‐iPSCs (Li et al., 2022; Arai et al., 2024). Transplanted kidney organoids also showed more mature glomerular structures and the renal maturation markers compared to kidney organoids in vitro. However, they were less mature than adult kidneys (Nam et al., 2019). The generation of more mature kidney organoids should be investigated to mimic the adult kidney and its medical effects.

This study suggests the potential efficacy of exon skipping as a novel treatment modality for genetic disorders like AS, for which there is no curative therapy. Exon skipping is currently being investigated not only for AS but also for other genetic diseases (Matsuo, 2021). With further research, it may become one of the effective treatment modalities for genetic diseases in the future.

EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES

Inclusion and ethics

Experiments using iPSCs were approved by the ethics committees of RIKEN BDR and Kobe University. The patients from whom AS‐iPSCs (K001) were derived provided a written informed consent. Animal Experiments were approved by the RIKEN BDR animal experiment committee.

Generation of iPSCs

The establishment of this AS‐patient‐derived iPSC line that was used in the experiments and symptoms of the patient were previously described (Hirayama et al., 2023). The patient provided a written informed consent. K001 was derived from the peripheral blood mononuclear cells of a Japanese male AS patient in his 20s, showing end‐stage renal disease. The healthy iPSC line (c1502) which was derived from the dermal fibroblast of the human female embryo was also previously described (Takasato et al., 2015).

All iPSCs were maintained using StemFit AK02N (Ajinomoto) on 6‐well plates at a density of 7.0 × 103 cells per well coated with 0.125 μg/cm2 iMatrix‐511 silk (Nippi, #892021). iPSCs were passaged with TrypLE select (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #12563011) every week (Figure S1A).

Kidney organoid induction

The day prior to the differentiation, iPSCs were seeded onto 12‐well plates at a density of 57.6 × 103 cells per well. To induce the kidney organoids from iPSCs, iPSCs were cultured in APEL2 medium (Stem cell technologies) containing 1% protein‐free hybridoma medium (Thermo Fisher scientific, #12040077) and 0.5X Anti‐Anti (Thermo Fisher scientific, #15240). A day after seeding, iPSCs were cultured in APEL2 medium containing 8 μM CHIR99021 (Tocris, #4423) for the first 5 days. At day 5, the medium was switched to APEL2 medium supplemented with 200 ng/mL hrFGF9h (Ajinomoto) and 1 μg/mL Heparin (Sigma Aldrich, #H4784). At day 7, cells were dissociated with TrypLE select (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #12563011) or 0.05% Trypsin–EDTA (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #25200‐072), diluted 5‐fold with FACS buffer (0.5% BSA: Sigma Aldrich, #6003; 2 mM EDTA: Thermo fisher scientific, #15575‐038; DPBS: Thermo Fisher Scientific, #14190) or MEF‐CM (10% ES cell FBS: 1% Glutamax: 0.5% Pen/Str: DMEM), collected to a 15 mL conical tube and centrifuge for 3 min at 400 g to pellet cells. After removing the supernatant, cells were resuspended in fresh APEL2 medium, and were counted using a cell counter. Cell dilutions were prepared in the final concentration of 8 μM CHIR, 200 ng/mL FGF9, 10 nM Y27632 and 1 μg/mL heparin. Cell dilutions were transferred to low‐binding M‐bottom 96‐well plates (Sumitomo bakelite). On the next day, spheroids were transferred onto 0.4 μm Transwell polyester membranes in 6‐well plates (Corning, #3450), and were subsequently cultured in APEL2 medium containing 200 ng/mL rhFGF9 and 1 μg/mL heparin for another 5 days. Until the day of the harvest, organoids were grown in APEL2 medium with no supplementary factors.

In Figures2 and 4, we froze the cells at day 7 of induction using Stem Cell Banker (Takara bio, #CB045) to stock the induced nephron progenitor cells. When thawing, cells were warmed in 37°C water bath, and resuspended in 5 times volume of APEL2. Cell dilutions were centrifuged at 400g for 3 min to form pellets. After removing the supernatant, cells were resuspended in fresh APEL2, and were counted. The following protocols were same as described previously.

Immunostaining

Organoids and grafts were fixed with 4% PFA (Sigma Aldrich, #158127) overnight at 4°C. Fixed organoids and grafts were washed by phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) for 10 min at 3 times on a rocking platform. For the cryosection, organoids, and grafts were incubated in 10%, 20%, and 30% sucrose in PBS until organoids and grafts sank, and were embedded in OCT‐compounds (Sakura). OCT‐embedded tissue was sectioned on a cryostat into 7 μm sections. Before the immunostaining, antigen retrieval was performed using HistVT one (nacalai tesque, #06380) at 90°C for 40 min.

Blocking was performed by blocking buffer incubation for 3 h at room temperature. Blocking buffer contained 10% donkey serum with 0.3% Triton X‐100 (Sigma Aldrich, #T9284). Subsequently, organoids and grafts were incubated in primary antibody diluted in blocking buffer at 4°C overnight. After washing organoids and grafts by PBS for 10 min at 3 times on a rocking platform, primary antibodies were detected with Alexa Fluor‐conjugated fluorescent secondary antibody (Invitrogen), diluted 1:400. Immunostained organoids and grafts were washed in PBS at 3 times for 10 min. Imaging was performed using a LSM 800 (Carl Zeiss). For whole mount staining, imaging was performed on the glass bottom dish. Information of primary antibodies is shown in Table S2.

In vivo culture of kidney organoids

Organoids at day 13 after the initial differentiation were implanted on the mesenteric or under renal capsule of BALB/c Slc‐nu/nu mice (SLC) or NOD‐SCID mice (Oriental yeast). Organoids were harvested 14–32 days after the transplantation.

ASO treatment

The sequence of ASO is: 5′‐cCcuGgcAauCcaTccTg‐3′; upper letters: ENA (2′‐O, 4′‐C‐ethylene‐bridged nucleic acid); lower letters: 2′‐O‐methyl RNA.

For in vitro treatment, ASO was transfected into organoids by using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX Transfection reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #13778) and Opti‐MEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #31985) from day 13 to day 25 or day 27 of the induction.

For in vivo treatment, a single dose of ASO was administrated twice a week. The dose of ASO administration was 50 mg/kg (Yamamura et al., 2020). After culture in vivo for 21 days (day 34 of the induction), grafts were harvested and fixed with 4% PFA at 4°C overnight.

Quantitative real time polymerase chain reaction

RNA was isolated using NucleoSpin RNA kit (Macherey‐Nagel, #740955250) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. The quantity of RNA was determined using a NanoDrop One (Thermo Fisher Scientific). cDNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara Bio., #RR037). The synthesized cDNA was mixed with TB green Premix EX TaqII (Takara Bio., #RR820) and specific primers. Amplification was performed for 40 cycles, each at 95°C for 5 s and at 60°C for 34 s using a QuantStudio 5 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). All measurements were performed in triplicate. Data were normalized to ACTB. Sequences of the primers used in this study were shown in Table S1.

Statistics and reproducibility

The number of replicates is three unless otherwise specified. All quantitative data are represented as the mean ± SE. Student's t test was used for statistical evaluations.

Electron microscopy

The tissue samples for TEM were fixed in phosphate buffered 2% glutaraldehyde, and subsequently postfixed in 2% osmium tetra‐oxide for 3 h in an ice bath. The specimens were then dehydrated in a graded ethanol series and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections were obtained using an ultramicrotome. Ultrathin sections were stained with uranyl acetate for 10 min and a lead staining solution for 5 min and then observed by TEM (HITACHI H‐7600 at 100 kV). Electron microscopy imaging of Figure 2d and Figure 4a were performed by Hanaichi electron microscopy technical research institute and the laboratory for ultrastructural research in RIKEN BDR.

Measurement of GBM formation in electron microscopy

The ratio of SD formation and the thickness of the GBM were quantified from the electron microscope images. ECs adjacent to GBM were traced and measured as the length of GBM. The area of SD formed by the foot processes of podocytes was also measured. The area between the ECs and podocytes was measured as the area of the GBM. We calculated the thickness of GBM by dividing the area by the length of GBM.
