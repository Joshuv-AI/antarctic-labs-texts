Genome-wide identification of the SnRK gene family in willow and expression analysis under abiotic stress
Authors: Hao Sun, Qianzhou Yuan, Jiaming Li, Min Ma, Haiyan Ren, Haifeng Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDrought and nitrogen (N) deposition significantly impact plant growth in northern regions. The balance between carbon (C) and N metabolism is crucial for plant adaptation to global environmental change. Salix gordejevii is widely distributed across the northern temperate zone and is characterized by rapid growth and high adaptability. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying its adaptation to drought and N deposition remain largely unclear. Sucrose non-fermenting 1-related protein kinases (SnRKs) represent a class of serine/threonine protein kinases that are extensively involved in plant C and N metabolism, as well as in responses to abiotic stress. We hypothesised that the SnRK gene family was essential for willow adaptation to drought and N deposition, with its evolutionary and structural features being closely linked to its stress-responsive expression patterns.ResultsA total of 43 SpSnRK genes were identified and classified into three subfamilies: SpSnRK1 (2 members), SpSnRK2 (11 members), and SpSnRK3 (30 members). These genes exhibited an uneven distribution across the genome. Among them, 37 genes contained both coding and non-coding regions, while 6 consisted solely of coding regions. Most SpSnRK proteins were predicted to localize in the cytoplasm. The promoters of SpSnRK genes were found to be enriched with numerous stress- and hormone-responsive cis-acting elements. Furthermore, the SnRK genes expression profiles in leaves and roots were analyzed under four treatments (control, drought, N deposition, and a combination of drought and N deposition). The expression profile analysis revealed that, under drought stress or N deposition, a greater number of responsive SgSnRK genes were detected in the roots. However, under the combined conditions of drought and N deposition, the number of responsive SgSnRK genes increased significantly in the leaves.ConclusionsThe SnRK gene family in willow consisted of three subfamilies that exhibit structural differences and were unevenly distributed across the chromosomes. These genes played critical roles in multiple stress signaling pathways. Furthermore, their expression in response to drought and N deposition showed tissue specificity and varies according to the mode of stress combination.These findings provide a basis for further exploration of the molecular mechanisms underlying SnRK-associated abiotic stress responses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09185-3.Keywords: Willow, Sucrose non-fermenting1-related protein kinase, Nitrogen deposition, Drought

Introduction

Abiotic stresses, such as drought and excessive nitrogen deposition, are primary factors contributing to the decline in crop yield and quality worldwide [1–3]. Over the past century, agricultural and industrial activities have led to widespread environmental changes, particularly intensified drought stress and N deposition [4–7]. Excessive N input into soils can induce acidification, which not only creates unfavorable conditions for plant growth but also alter the carbon (C)-to-nitrogen (N) ratio in plants, consequently affecting their metabolic processes [8–11]. Drought severely restricts N accumulation by disrupting nitrate signaling, thereby restricting protein synthesis and plant growth. Conversely, increased N availability may improve plant drought tolerance by enhancing osmotic adjustment and antioxidant defense [12–13]. N deposition and drought often interact to influence plant traits, owing to the crosstalk between their molecular regulatory pathways [14, 15]. As a crucial element in this crosstalk network, Sucrose non-fermenting1-related protein kinases (SnRKs) coordinate responses to N nutrition and drought stress [16, 17]. Therefore, investigating how plants adapt to drought in the context of N deposition is essential for understanding and improving plant drought resistance.

Protein phosphorylation is critical for regulating cellular metabolism by modulating the activity of key enzymes and coordinating the biosynthesis and transport of metabolites, thereby facilitating plant adaptation to fluctuating environmental conditions [18, 19]. SnRKs are serine/threonine protein kinases that regulate a wide array of physiological processes in plants and are involved in various signaling pathways during growth and development, particularly under abiotic stress [17, 20, 21]. Based on their structural and sequence characteristics, the SnRK gene family is classified into three subfamilies, namely SnRK1, SnRK2, and SnRK3 [22]. SnRK1 contains a highly conserved N-terminal protein kinase (Pkinase) domain that is homologous to the sucrose non-fermenting 1 (SNF1) domain in yeast and the adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) domain in mammals. In contrast, SnRK2 and SnRK3 represent plant-specific lineages, which contain more members than SnRK1 and are believed to have evolved from SnRK1, thereby acquiring specialized functions throughout the course of plant evolution [23, 24]. The SnRK2 family members possess an ATP-binding domain and a kinase domain. SnRK3, also referred to as calcineurin B-like (CBL)-interacting protein kinases (CIPKs), comprises two conserved domains, namely the NAF domain and the PPI domain [25]. The NAF domain, named for its conserved asparagine, alanine, and phenylalanine residues, mediates interaction with CBL proteins, whereas the PPI domain enhances binding to protein phosphatase 2 C (PP2C) [26].

The SnRK gene family is widely recognized for its role in enhancing stress resistance in plants by participating in various signaling pathways [27]. As a central energy sensor, SnRK1 integrates metabolic signals, endogenous hormones, and exogenous environmental stimuli to modulate NLP7 activity, thereby governing nitrate signaling pathways [28]. Based on the pathways involved in drought resistance, the SnRK2 gene family can be categorized into two groups including the abscisic acid (ABA)-dependent and the non-ABA-dependent pathway. In contrast, in the ABA-dependent pathway, SnRK2 perceives ABA signals and forms a regulatory module with PP2C [26]. In the ABA-independent pathway, some SnRK2 members (e.g., TaSnRK2.3 in wheat) respond directly to osmotic stress by phosphorylating target proteins and activating stress-responsive gene expression [29]. The SnRK3 subfamily mediates robust responses to both abiotic and biotic stimuli by regulating the CBL–CIPK signaling pathway. This plant-specific Ca²⁺ sensor system also interacts with multiple other pathways, including ABA signaling and nitrate sensing and transduction [30]. To date, most studies have focused on the functions of SnRK genes under drought or salt stress. However, the response patterns and regulatory mechanisms of the SnRK family under combined drought and N deposition stress remain largely unclear.

Salix gordejevii Y.L. Chang et Skv. is a shrub willow native to Inner Mongolia, China. This species is distinguished by its rapid growth, drought tolerance, and resistance to salt and alkali, alongside a robust root system. These adaptive traits render it an exemplary candidate for applications such as windbreaks, sand fixation, soil structure improvement, and biomass energy production, thus providing substantial ecological and economic benefits on a global scale [31, 32]. Nevertheless, the specific functions and mechanisms of the SnRK family in Salix in response to N deposition and drought conditions remain largely unknown.

The aim of this study is to systematically characterize the SnRK family in willow and elucidate its response to drought and N deposition. To achieve this, we screened for SnRK genes, calculated the Ka/Ks ratios, constructed a phylogenetic tree, and analyzed the cis-acting element as well as the expression of SnRK in leaves and roots under various treatments. These findings will provide a molecular basis for understanding how willow adapts to abiotic stress, offering valuable insights for the genetic improvement of stress tolerance in woody plants.

Materials and methods

Identification and classification of the SnRK gene family

The genome assemblies of S. purpurea (S. purpurea v5.1) and Populus trichocarpa (P. trichocarpa_533_v4.0) were retrieved from Phytozome (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/). Due to the unavailability of the genome of S. gordejevii, the genome of the closely related S. purpurea was utilized as the reference for gene family identification and bioinformatic analyses in this study. Employing a congeneric reference genome is a widely accepted and reliable strategy for gene family studies in non-model plants; however, this approach may result in incomplete annotation of species-specific genes or minor discrepancies in gene structure prediction. Consequently, the prefix “SpSnRK” for the genes was adopted from the S. purpurea reference genome to ensure consistency with the database. This methodology has been successfully applied to identify the PDR gene family in S. matsudana, utilizing the same S. purpurea reference genome [33]. The high evolutionary conservation of the SnRK superfamily across angiosperms further enhances the reliability of homologous gene identification [34]. Following a previous study, we identified the SnRK family genes in Arabidopsis thaliana and retrieved their corresponding protein sequences from the TAIR database (https://www.arabidopsis.org/) [35]. Candidate SnRK protein sequences in S. purpurea were subsequently acquired by conducting a local BLAST homology search with an E-value cutoff of 1e-5. Finally, all candidate SpSnRK and PtSnRK genes were meticulously screened and verified based on conserved domain information from the Pfam database (http://pfam.xfam.org/), the NCBI Conserved Domain Database (CDD, http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi), and the SMART database (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de/). Subsequently, the SnRK gene members of these three species were classified and renamed according to the structural characteristics of the three subfamily genes.

Phylogenetic analysis

Multiple sequence alignment of SpSnRK, AtSnRK, and PtSnRK protein sequences was performed using MEGA 5.2. A phylogenetic tree was then constructed using the neighbor-joining (NJ) method with ClustalW algorithm and 1000 bootstrap replicates. The default parameters in were applied, including the Poisson correction model for amino acid sequences, partial deletion with a 95% site coverage cutoff for gap handling, uniform rates among sites, and no assumption of a molecular clock.

Prediction of the characteristics and localization of SnRK proteins

The basic characteristics of SpSnRK proteins were analyzed using TBtools v2.378. These characteristics included protein length (number of amino acids), molecular weight (MW), isoelectric point (pI), instability index, aliphatic index and grand average of hydropathicity. Additionally, subcellular localization prediction was conducted using the CELLO website (http://cello.life.nctu.edu.tw/).

Analysis of the conserved motifs, gene structure and domains

Using the online MEME v5.5.7 tool (http://meme-suite.org/index.html), 10 conserved motifs were identified in the SpSnRK protein sequence. Subsequently, an online analysis was conducted using the NCBI CDD search tool (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/bwrpsb/bwrpsb.cgi). The Visualize Gene Structure function in TBtools v2.378 was utilized to visualize the gene structure of the SpSnRK sequences based on its GFF3 files. Then, the above three result files were merged. The SpSnRK sequences were subjected to multiple sequence alignment using ClustalW in MEGA5.2. The aligned sequences were then imported into Jalview for visualization and sequence screening. Finally, the Gene Location Visualize function from GTF/GFF in TBtools v2.378 was employed to obtain the positioal images of genes on chromosomes.

Ka/Ks and collinearity analysis

We conducted multiple sequence alignments of SpSnRK sequences using ClustalW within the MEGA5.2 software. Subsequently, DnaSP6 was employed to calculate the non-synonymous (Ka) and synonymous (Ks) substitution rates of gene duplication pairs. The Advanced Circos function in TBtools v2.378 was utilized to create and enhance the intraspecific synteny map of SpSnRK. Additionally, the Multiple Synteny Plot function in TBtools v2.378 was used to generate interspecific synteny maps for A. thaliana, P. trichocarpa and S. purpurea.

Heatmap of cis-acting elements

To obtain the 2000 bp upstream sequences of the SpSnRK promoters, TBtools v2.378 was utilized to extract the relevant data from both the GFF and DNA sequence files. The processed sequence file was then submitted to the PLANTCARE online platform (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) to predict the cis-acting elements in promoters. Subsequently, the file was imported into TBtools v2.378 to construct a heatmap.

Plant materials and stress treatments

Cuttings of S. gordejevii were collected from a plantation in Sangen Dalai, located in Zhenglan Banner, Xilingol League, Inner Mongolia (42°55′-42°57′ N, 116°00′ E). This species has long been utilized for windbreak and sand fixation in the arid and semi-arid regions of central and eastern Inner Mongolia. The corresponding voucher specimen (ID: 00074558) identified by Professor Ling Yan, is deposited in the Herbarium of Inner Mongolia Agricultural University. Uniform-length branch cuttings of willow were planted in sandy soil under the following conditions: 70–75% relative soil moisture, 25℃, and a photoperiod of 10 h. After one month of cultivation in the greenhouse, plants exhibiting consistent growth were selected for stress treatments. Natural atmospheric N deposition generally contains both NH₄⁺ and NO₃⁻, which can readily induce soil acidification. However, in sandy soils, nitrate is the predominant form of inorganic nitrogen and is the most accessible to plants. Consequently, N was supplied in the form of potassium nitrate (KNO3) dissolved in 200 mL of distilled water. For treatments without N addition, a potassium chloride (KCl) solution was applied instead to maintain uniform potassium (K+) concentrations across all groups. The N addition rate was set at 10 g N m− 2 yr− 1. Two soil moisture levels were established: 80% (well-watered) and 50% (drought stress). Therefore, the experiment comprised four treatments: CK (well-watered), D (drought, with soil moisture maintained at 50%), 10 N (well-watered, with an N addition of 10 g N m− 2 yr− 1), and 10ND (drought, combined with an N addition of 10 g N m− 2 yr− 1). Each treatment included three biological replicates. All plants were grown at 25 °C under a 10-hour photoperiod. After one month of treatment application, samples were collected, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored for subsequent analysis.

RT-qPCR

We submitted the sequences of 43 SnRK genes to Primer 3 (http://primer3.ut.ee/) for primer design and named as SgSnRK1.1-SgSnRK3.30. The primer length was set between 20 and 24 base pairs, and the Tm value was set at 60℃, while the other parameters were maintained at their default settings. Detailed information regarding all RT-qPCR primers is listed in Table S1. The ground powder of leaves and roots from S. gordejevii was utilized for RNA extraction with the FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2, adhering strictly to the provided instructions. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using SuperScript™ IV reverse transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The cDNA was at a ratio of 1:5 and 2.0 µL of the diluted product was utilized as a template in a 20 µL reaction system for RT-qPCR, following to the manufacturer’s instructions. The SgActin was selected as the internal reference gene after verifying its expression stability using geNorm and NormFinder software. Amplification efficiencies of both target and reference genes were determined using five-fold gradient diluted cDNA standard curves, with all efficiency values controlled within the range of 90%–110%. Finally, qPCR was performed using the Applied Biosystems 7500 Fast. Three independent biological replicates were performed for each sample. The relative expression levels of target genes were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method [36].

Chromosome location, nuclear localization signal (NLS) prediction and subcellular localization experiments

The coding sequences (CDSs) of the target genes, excluding the stop codons, were amplified using the primers listed in Table S2. These fragments were subsequently cloned into the pEG100-mCherry vector via a Hieff Clone® Universal One Step Cloning Kit (Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai), resulting in the generation of the following fusion constructs: SgSnRK1.2-mCherry, SgSnRK2.3-mCherry, SgSnRK3.5-mCherry, and SpSnRK3.9-mCherry. The empty vector mCherry-empty was served as a control. The constructed vectors were introduced into Agrobacterium strain GV3101 and subsequently infiltrated into leaves of approximately five-week-old Nicotiana benthamiana. After infiltration, the leaves were maintained in darkness for 24 h and then transferred to light conditions for 48 h. The mCherry fluorescent signal was observed using a Leica DM4 B fluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany). Nuclear localization signals of SpSnRK were predicted using the NLStradamus online server (http://www.moseslab.csb.utoronto.ca/NLStradamus/) with default hidden Markov model settings. Classical NLS motifs were identified according to the Viterbi path algorithm, and regions with a posterior probability threshold of ≥ 0.6 were utilized as auxiliary reference criteria.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Student’s t-test was employed to evaluate the differences between the control group and the various treatment groups. Bar charts were generated using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Significance levels were indicated as follows: *0.01≤p < 0.05, **0.001≤p < 0.01, ***0.0001≤p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001. No correction for multiple testing was applied in this study, which may increase the risk of Type I errors [37].

Results

Identification and protein characteristics

We systematically identified and categorized 43 members of the SnRK gene family, namely 2 members in the SnRK1 subfamily (SpSnRK1.1 to SpSnRK1.2), 11 members in the SnRK2 subfamily (SpSnRK2.1 to SpSnRK2.11), and 30 members in the SnRK3 subfamily (SpSnRK3.1 to SpSnRK3.30) (Table 1). In other species, A. thaliana contained 3 AtSnRK1 members, 10 AtSnRK2 members, and 26 AtSnRK3 members in (Table S3), while P. trichocarpa had 2 PtSnRK1 members, 12 PtSnRK2 members, and 31 PtSnRK3 members in (Table S4). The SnRK gene family in woody plants may have expanded during evolution to enhance adaptation to complex environmental stresses and has been evolutionarily conserved within the Salicaceae.

Identified SpSnRK genes and their protein physicochemical properties in S. purpurea

The 43 proteins exhibited amino acid lengths ranging from 275 (SpSnRK2.11) to 573 (SpSnRK3.17). The average molecular weights (MV) of the SpSnRK1, SpSnRK2, and SpSnRK3 subfamily proteins were approximately 60.8 kDa, 40.0 kDa, and 51.9 kDa, respectively. The isoelectric points (pI) of SpSnRK proteins varied from 4.86 (SpSnRK2.9) to 9.44 (SpSnRK3.11). Subcellular localization prediction indicated that all members of the SpSnRK1 and SpSnRK2 subfamilies were localized in the cytoplasm, while the SpSnRK3.1 member of the SpSnRK3 subfamily was localized in the chloroplast (Table 1). The physicochemical properties of SnRK proteins in A. thaliana and P. trichocarpa were presented in Table S3 and Table S4, respectively. The conserved molecular characteristics of SnRK proteins across three dicot species suggested a stable evolution of this gene family in dicotyledonous plants.

Phylogenetic tree and multiple alignment

To elucidate the phylogenetic relationship among SnRK of A. thaliana, S. purpurea and P. trichocarpa, we constructed a phylogenetic tree using NJ method with MEGA5.2. The 127 SnRK proteins were clearly categorized into three subfamilies: SnRK1, SnRK2 and SnRK3. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the SnRK1 subfamily contained the fewest members, while the SnRK3 subfamily exhibited the highest number. Furthermore, the distribution of SnRK was relatively uniform within each subfamily; however, the SnRK of S.purpurea and P. trichocarpa tended to cluster together (Fig. 1). This preferential clustering of SnRK from S. purpurea and P. trichocarpa suggested a closer phylogenetic relationship between these two species compared to A. thaliana.

The phylogenetic tree of SpSnRKs, AtSnRKs and PtSnRKs. The values beside the branches are the bootstrap support rates from 1,000 repeated samplings

To determine the characteristics of the conserved domain of SpSnRK, a multiple sequence alignment of its protein sequence was performed. The results indicated that all members of the SpSnRK2 subfamily encoded an ATP binding site and a serine/threonine protein kinase active site in the N-terminal region, as well as a domain referred to as Domain 1 in the C-terminal region (Fig. S1A). Additionally, the SpSnRK3 not only contained a protein kinase domain in the N-terminal region, but also featured a highly conserved NAF domain and PPI domain in the C-terminal region (Fig. S1B). Conserved domain analysis confirmed that the SpSnRK family retains a conserved kinase domain, while distinct C-terminal domains provide each subfamily with specific functional diversity.

Analysis of motif, conserved domains and gene structure

To gain a deeper understanding of gene evolution, we conducted a structural analysis of 43 SpSnRK genes. Members of the SpSnRK1 subfamily possessed 10 introns, while those in the SpSnRK2 subfamily contained between 6 and 9 introns. In contrast, the number of introns in members of the SpSnRK3 subfamily showed significant variation and was classified into two types: those with few or no introns and those with many introns. Among them, 14 SpSnRK3 genes had no introns, while SpSnRK3.4, SpSnRK3.6, and SpSnRK3.28 each contained one. The remaining 13 SpSnRK3 genes had between 11 and 16 introns These two types of exon–intron structures in the SpSnRK3 subfamily might reflect different evolutionary patterns, including intron loss events and rapid structural divergence during the evolution of this gene subfamily.

To further elucidate the structure of SpSnRK, we identified 10 conserved motifs and predicted their distribution across each SpSnRK. The motifs shared by the three SpSnRK subfamilies included motif 2, 8, 1, 7 and 4 (Fig. 2). Notably, SpSnRK2.11 is unique within the SpSnRK2 subfamily as it lacks motif 3 and 5, which are present in the other 42 SpSnRK. The unique motif of the SpSnRK3 subfamily is motif 6. The specific sequence of the motifs can be observed in Fig. S2.

Ka and Ks, as well as collinearity analysis of the SpSnRKs

To further explore the influence of selective pressure on the evolution of the SpSnRK, we conducted a collinearity analysis and calculated the Ka/Ks ratios. Based on the circos plot, a total of 22 pairs of SpSnRK gene duplications were identified (Fig. 3). Using DnaSP 6, we calculated the Ks, Ka, and Ka/Ks ratios for the 17 of the 22 homologous SpSnRK pairs (Table S5). Generally, a ratio of 1 indicates neutral selection, implying that DNA mutations do not affect on the organism. A Ka/Ks ratio of less than 1 indicates purifying (negative) selection, while a ratio greater than 1 suggested accelerated (positive) evolution. The Ka/Ks ratio of these 17 homologous SpSnRK pairs ranged from 0.052 to 0.250, indicating that the SpSnRK gene family had undergone purifying selection during its evolutionary process. A collinearity analysis was also performed to identify syntenic relationships among SnRK genes from A. thaliana, S. purpurea and P. trichocarpa (Fig. 4). A total of 54 collinear SnRK pairs were detected between S. purpurea and A. thaliana. In contrast, 80 such pairs were identified between S. purpurea and P. trichocarpa, indicating a higher level of syntenic conservation and a closer evolutionary relationship within the Salicaceae family.

Chromosome location, nuclear localization signal (NLS) prediction and subcellular localization

We mapped the chromosomal location of each gene, identifying SpSnRK2.1, SpSnRK2.8, SpSnRK3.13, SpSnRK3.20, SpSnRK3.21 and SpSnRK1.1 on chromosomes 01, 07, 08, 14, 16 and 17, respectively (Fig. 5). Additionally, the SpSnRK3.30 was located on a chromosome fragment. Notably, members of the SpSnRK3 genes were more frequently found in close proximity on the same chromosome compared to members of the other two subfamilies, which may indicate an expansion pattern of the SpSnRK3 genes during evolution.

Bioinformatics prediction provided preliminary subcellular localization information for all 43 SpSnRK, with prediction results presented in Table 1. Subsequent experimental verification revealed that SgSnRK3.5 was localized to the plasma membrane, while SgSnRK1.2, SgSnRK2.3, and SgSnRK3.9 were found both in the plasma membrane and the nucleus (Fig. 6). These experimental results were inconsistent with our prior bioinformatics predictions in Table 1, indicating that bioinformatics predictions serve only as preliminary references and that experimental validation is essential for determining actual subcellular localization.

Discussion

The three SpSnRK subfamilies exhibit distinct domains and evolutionary conservation

The SnRK family serves as a core regulator of plant responses to abiotic stress, with genome-wide characterization conducted across various plant lineages. In model organisms, 39 SnRK genes have been identified in A. thaliana and 50 in Oryza sativa, establishing a conserved classification framework of three subfamilies (SnRK1/2/3) across angiosperms [38]. Among the Salicaceae species closely related to S. gordejevii, 43 SnRK proteins were characterized in S. purpurea and 45 in P. trichocarpa. This analysis revealed a consistent pattern of expansion, with the SnRK3 subfamily representing the largest clade in woody plants [39]. Limited studies have reported on the SnRK family in desert-adapted woody species such as P. euphratica, highlighting a research gap in our understanding of the SnRK family’s response to combined drought and N deposition in xerophytic Salicaceae species [40]. Our study presents genome-wide identification of the SnRK family in the desert species S. gordejevii, enhancing our understanding of the trends in SnRK gene family alterations in drought-resistant plants under combined stress.

A total of 43 SpSnRK genes were identified and classified into three subfamilies based on phylogenetic analysis (Fig. 1). The SpSnRK1 subfamily comprised two genes, the SpSnRK2 subfamily consisted of 11 genes; and the SpSnRK3 subfamily included 30 genes. This distribution of subfamilies was highly consistent with genome-wide studies in P. trichocarpa, indicating a conserved evolutionary pattern of the SnRK gene family within the Salicaceae. Conserved domain analysis revealed distinct structural features among the SpSnRK subfamilies. While all members retained an N-terminal protein kinase domain, the SpSnRK2 subfamily contained a C-terminal Domain 1, whereas the SpSnRK3 subfamily possessed a C-terminal NAF domain (Fig. S1A and 1B). This subfamily-specific domain architecture is widely conserved in Salicaceae [40], and these domain differences are believed to underlie functional divergence across subfamilies. The presence of the NAF domain enables SpSnRK3 to interact with calcineurin B-like proteins (CBLs) in a calcium-dependent manner, thereby regulating intracellular ion homeostasis [30, 41].

Based on the results of the phylogenetic tree analysis (Fig. 1), the SnRK of S. purpurea and P. trichocarpa clustered, likely due to their belonging to the same family and close genetic relationship [42]. Interspecific collinearity analysis (Fig. 4) further revealed a high number of collinear genes between S. purpurea and P. trichocarpa, providing additional genomic evidence for the high evolutionary conservation of the SnRK family in willow and poplar. We observed a significant discrepancy between in subcellular localization prediction and experimental validation. This discrepancy was hypothesized to stem from the dynamic in vivo translocation of SnRK, which was regulated by post-translational modification or protein–protein interactions. This a regulatory pattern had been widely documented for plant SnRK kinases [34, 38, 43], indicating that the nuclear targeting of SpSnRKs was independent of classical nuclear import pathways [44]. Despite undergoing strong purifying selection to maintain conserved core structures, the flexible subcellular localization of these genes hypothetically conferred functional diversity in stress signal transduction, contributing to the adaptive advantages of S. gordejevii in harsh environments.

Structure diversification of SpSnRK and their functional implications

Gene structural diversity serves as a primary driver of evolution within multigene families [45]. An analysis of conserved motifs and gene structures indicated that the majority of the 43 SpSnRKs shared similar architectures within their respective subfamilies. Notably, the SpSnRK3 subfamily was categorized into two distinct groups based on intron count: one group contains 11–16 introns, while the other has 0–1 intron (Fig. 2). This unique intron distribution pattern was widely conserved across plant SnRK3 gene families, and our findings aligned with the evolutionary trajectory reported for HcSnRK3 in Hedychium coronarium [46]. Intron-poor gene structures facilitate more rapid transcriptional responses to stress, as the absence of introns reduces the time and energy associated with splicing. This may confer a selective advantage in fluctuating or harsh environments [47, 48]. Supporting this hypothesis, several intron-deficient SpSnRK3 genes (including SpSnRK3.4 and SpSnRK3.12) demonstrated significant transcriptional upregulation under drought stress. The emergence of intron-poor SpSnRK3 variants arose from recurrent intron loss events in intron-rich ancestral lineages throughout plant evolution. This finding hypothetically linked the deficiency of introns in SpSnRK3 genes to enhanced stress responsiveness, although further functional validation was necessary to confirm this causal relationship.

The analysis of motif distribution revealed that motifs 1, 2, 4, 7, and 8 were present in all 43 SpSnRKs (Fig. 2). These core motifs corresponded to the conserved N-terminal kinase domain of SnRK, and their universal retention across all SpSnRK members aligned with previous findings in S. purpurea [34, 42], emphasizing their crucial role in maintaining the fundamental kinase function of SnRK family proteins. In contrast, motifs 3 and 5 were exclusively absent from SpSnRK2.11, which exhibited a unique non-responsive expression pattern under all stress treatments. Within the SpSnRK2 subfamily (excluding SpSnRK2.11), members were further divided into two groups based on the presence or absence of motif 9, which might be linked to the differential ABA and abiotic stress responsiveness of various SpSnRK2 subgroups [49, 50]. Notably, SpSnRK3.6 uniquely contained two motif 9, a feature not observed in other SpSnRK3 members. The lineage-specific motif variations identified were correlated with the tissue-specific expression patterns of SnRK genes, suggesting that subfamily-specific motif differentiation during evolution drove the functional divergence of SnRK, shaping their distinct stress expression patterns [51].

Variations in the promoter region often underlie differential gene activity, as cis-acting elements play a crucial role in regulating gene expression during development and environmental adaptation [52, 53]. In particular, the ABA response element-binding proteins/factors (AREB/ABFs) are known to promote drought response and stress tolerance, with SnRKs serving a central role in the ABA signaling pathway [52–54]. Analysis of the SpSnRK gene promoters revealed that 34 genes contain ABA response elements, indicating their potential role in ABA signaling and the regulation of plant growth and stress tolerance (Fig. 2). MYB transcription factors are recognized for enhancing both salt and drought tolerance, as well as for their roles in signaling, redox homeostasis, and phosphorylation [55, 56]. The promoters of 33 genes contained MYB-binding sites, and those of 30 genes possessed MeJA-responsive elements, reflecting their potential response to salt stress [57].

SgSnRK genes display tissue-specific and stress-dependent responses

SnRK proteins are known to participate in responses to multiple abiotic stresses, including heat, salt, drought, and cold [21, 58–60]. A stronger and more extensive transcriptional response was observed in roots compared to in leaves under drought and N deposition in terms of the number of upregulated genes and expression level. This tissue-biased expression pattern suggested functional divergence shaped by long-term purifying selection and structural conservation within the SpSnRK family [39, 42]. Furthermore, among the ten SgSnRK2 genes, nine were significantly upregulated in roots, whereas only SgSnRK2.3 was significantly upregulated in leaves. The SnRK2 genes participate in drought response primarily through ABA signaling, with the high expression of SnRK2 members in roots serving as a crucial component of the regulatory mechanism [61]. This finding also indicated that the SpSnRK2 subfamily, which experienced the strongest purifying selection during Salicaceae evolution, demonstrated predominantly root-specific upregulation, consistent with the conserved role of SnRK2 in drought adaptation among woody plants [40, 62]. The SnRK2 subfamily maintained energy supply by regulating sugar metabolismsuch as inhibiting starch synthesis, with its expression in leaves increasing in response to C–N imbalance [61]. Meanwhile, the expression levels of StSnRK2.4/2.6 in roots increased 1.5–2-fold under combined drought and high N stress compared to single-factor stress in potato [63]. Under combined drought and N treatment, a greater number of SgSnRK genes were activated in leaves. In roots, however, although the number of upregulated genes decreased, their expression levels generally increased, suggesting that a moderate supply of N induced higher expression levels of SgSnRK genes. Under combined drought and N deposition, more SgSnRK genes exhibited a positive transcriptional response in leaves. The most highly induced genes under drought, N deposition, and combined stresses were SgSnRK2.9, SgSnRK3.4, and SgSnRK3.29 in roots and SgSnRK1.2 in leaves. Their pronounced responses to both individual and concurrent stresses suggested they hold particular significance for further investigation into stress adaptation mechanisms.

Conclusion

The SnRK genes play crucial roles in plants under abiotic stress conditions. In this study, a total of 43 SnRK genes were identified through genome-wide screening in S. purpurea, which were classified into three subfamilies. These genes exhibited uneven distribution, with the SnRK3 subfamily containing the most members. Subcellular localization prediction revealed that most SnRKs were localized in the plasma membrane. However, experimental validation of four proteins revealed inconsistent patterns, which may result from dynamic in vivo translocation regulated by post-translational modifications or protein–protein interactions. Gene duplication events and purifying selection reduced sequence diversity while maintaining functional specialization during evolution, as reflected by differences in exon-intron structures and conserved motifs. The promoters of SnRK contained abundant environmental response elements and hormone-responsive elements. SnRK genes exhibited root-biased responses to single stresses but balanced responses to combined stress. These analyses provided a theoretical basis for further investigations into SnRK-related mechanisms for enhancing stress tolerance.
