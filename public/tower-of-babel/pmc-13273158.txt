Palm and Eucalyptus waste-derived biochars for removing imidacloprid and myclobutanil from pesticide- contaminated water
Authors: Osama I. Abdallah, Sayed M. Saleh, Sherif B. Abdel Ghani, Saleh S. Alhewairini, Ahmed Helal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Two biochars, palm petiole biochar (PPB) and eucalyptus leaf biochar (ELB), were prepared from palm (Phoenix dactylifera) petioles and eucalyptus (Eucalyptus sp.) leaves and evaluated as low-cost sorbents for the removal of imidacloprid and myclobutanil from aqueous solutions. Batch adsorption experiments were conducted to examine the effects of adsorbent dose, pH, temperature, contact time, and initial pesticide concentration. The highest removal was achieved at pH 7, 25 ± 1 °C, 24 h contact time, a biochar dose of 5 g L⁻¹, and an initial concentration of 50 mg L⁻¹ for each pesticide. Thermodynamic analysis indicated that the adsorption process was spontaneous and exothermic. Adsorption kinetics were evaluated using pseudo-first order and pseudo-second-order models, whereas equilibrium data were analyzed using the Langmuir, Freundlich, Temkin, and Dubinin–Radushkevich isotherm models. Among the tested equilibrium models, Langmuir and Freundlich provided the best fit to the adsorption data. PPB showed stronger practical removal performance than ELB. Under rapid-treatment conditions using 100 mg of biochar, PPB removed 87.4% of imidacloprid and 92.1% of myclobutanil, whereas ELB removed 66.6% and 80.0%, respectively. These findings demonstrate that PPB and ELB, particularly PPB, are promising eco-friendly sorbents for the treatment of pesticide-contaminated water and concentrated agricultural point-source wastewaters.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51008-w.

Introduction

Pesticide contamination of water systems has become a major environmental concern because pesticide residues can reach surface water and groundwater through precipitation, runoff, spray drift, and leaching, thereby impairing water quality and threatening aquatic organisms, biodiversity, and food-chain integrity1–5. The problem is especially relevant for pesticides that are widely used in agricultural practice and repeatedly detected in aquatic environments. Among these, imidacloprid, [1-(6-chloro-3-pyridylmethyl)-N-nitro-imidazolidin-2-ylideneamine], a neonicotinoid insecticide, and myclobutanil, [(RS)−2-(4-chlorophenyl)−2-(1 H-1,2,4-triazol-1-ylmethyl) hexanenitrile], a triazole fungicide (Fig. 1a and b, respectively), are of particular interest because of their extensive agricultural use6–11. Imidacloprid is among the most widely used insecticides worldwide12 and is highly effective against homopteran pests13–15, whereas myclobutanil is a broad-spectrum systemic fungicide with preventive, curative, and eradicative activity16,17. Both compounds have also been reported among the pesticides detected in water and biota samples collected from aquatic systems in different countries18–22. These characteristics make them relevant model contaminants for evaluating adsorptive treatment strategies for pesticide-polluted water.

The increasing occurrence of pesticide residues in water has heightened the need for efficient, practical, and economically feasible treatment approaches. Among the available options, adsorption has attracted considerable attention for the removal of micropollutants, including pesticides, because of its simplicity, effectiveness, and relatively low cost23–29. The success of adsorption-based treatment, however, depends strongly on the adsorbent’s properties. This has created continued interest in developing low-cost sorbents from renewable biomass that can be produced locally and applied to pesticide-contaminated water.

Biochar has emerged as a promising adsorbent in this context. It is a carbonaceous porous material produced by pyrolysis of biomass under oxygen-limited conditions and has shown considerable potential for pesticide removal30–32. Its adsorption behavior is commonly linked to its porous structure, surface area, and the presence of functional groups such as carboxylic, hydroxyl, and amino groups, which can enhance interactions with organic contaminants33,34. In addition to its sorption performance, biochar production offers a sustainable route for converting agricultural residue into value-added materials35–41. This dual role makes biochar attractive for both waste valorization and water remediation.

Among the possible biomass feedstocks, date palm residues are especially relevant in arid and semi-arid regions. Date palm (Phoenix dactylifera) trees generate substantial quantities of waste annually42–45, much of which is often discarded by burning, incineration, or burial, thereby contributing to environmental pollution46,47. Egypt and the Arab Gulf countries are among the major producers of date palms and, therefore, generate abundant palm-derived residues that could be utilized beneficially as biochar feedstock. Eucalyptus (Eucalyptus sp.) leaf waste likewise represents an abundant, renewable, inexpensive lignocellulosic biomass that is potentially suitable for biochar production. The selection of palm petioles and eucalyptus leaves in the present work was therefore based on their regional availability, low cost, and suitability as precursors for the sustainable production of adsorbents.

Previous studies have demonstrated the applicability of palm-derived biochar in water decontamination48–50, including the adsorption of tetracycline48, antibiotics49, and ammonium or phosphate50. These reports support the broader value of palm-based biochar for pollutant removal. Still, they do not sufficiently address the comparative removal of imidacloprid and myclobutanil using biochars produced from palm petioles and eucalyptus leaves. This comparison is scientifically relevant because the two selected pesticides differ in physicochemical behavior, particularly in water solubility and hydrophobicity, and may therefore interact differently with biochar surfaces and pore systems. In parallel, comparing palm petiole biochar (PPB) with eucalyptus leaf biochar (ELB) provides insight into how two locally available biomass sources perform as low-cost sorbents for pesticide removal.

The novelty of the present work lies not only in the comparative evaluation of PPB and ELB for the adsorption of imidacloprid and myclobutanil, but also in the practical treatment scenario addressed. In contrast to studies focused mainly on dilute aqueous systems, the present work targets small-volume, high-strength agricultural point-source wastewater, such as leftover spray solutions and sprayer washing or rinsing water, which may contain pesticide concentrations in the range of tens of mg L⁻¹ immediately after field application. These waste streams represent an important but often overlooked pathway for pesticide release into the environment and therefore require efficient treatment before disposal.

Accordingly, the present study employed waste palm petioles and eucalyptus leaves as renewable precursors for biochar production to remove imidacloprid and myclobutanil from aqueous media. The study aimed to prepare and characterize PPB and ELB, investigate the effects of key adsorption variables, evaluate adsorption isotherms and kinetics, discuss possible adsorption mechanisms, and assess regeneration potential and adsorption performance using actual contaminated water samples.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Myclobutanil (99.5% purity) and imidacloprid (99.5% purity) reference standards were obtained from Chem Service Inc. (West Chester, PA, USA), while commercial pesticide formulations of imidacloprid (35% soluble concentrate SC) and myclobutanil (25% emulsifiable concentrate EC) were sourced from a local supplier. HPLC-grade acetonitrile and methanol were procured from Fisher Scientific (Loughborough, UK), and LC-MS grade formic acid and ammonium formate were supplied by Chem-Lab NV (Zedelgem, Belgium). Ultrapure water was produced using an Evoqua Ultra Clear system (Evoqua Water Technologies LLC, Günzburg, Germany).

Preparation and characterization of biochar

Preparation of palm petiole biochar (PPB) and eucalyptus leaf biochar (ELB)

A simple pyrolysis process was used to convert plant waste into biochar. The waste of date palm petioles and Eucalyptus leaves was used as biomass to produce the required biochar, i.e., Palm Petioles biochar (PPB), and Eucalyptus Leaves biochar (ELB). Air-dried palm petioles and eucalyptus leaves were finely chopped and pyrolyzed separately in sealed containers under oxygen-limited conditions. The biomass was heated from room temperature to 450 °C at a heating rate of 5 °C/min, held at 450 °C for 4 h, and then cooled to room temperature within the closed furnace/containers. The resulting biochars were ground and stored in clean airtight containers until use.

Physicochemical characterization of the prepared biochars

Scanning Electron Microscopy SEM (FEI Inspect S50, Netherland) and Energy Dispersive X-ray spectroscopy EDS, (Bruker, Germany), particle size distribution, specific surface area (SSA), total pore volume, average pore diameter (Belsorp Max II Microtrac, Japan) and carbon (C) content were all carried out in an accredited (ISO 17025) commercial laboratory. The obtained biochars were subjected to removal studies of two pesticides of different physicochemical properties, i.e., imidacloprid (Log p 0.57, solubility in water 610 mg L⁻¹) and myclobutanil (Log p 2.89, solubility in water 132 mg L⁻¹) in aqueous solutions.

Adsorption experiments

Batch adsorption procedure

The adsorption behavior of imidacloprid and myclobutanil was investigated using the batch equilibration technique. Generally, aqueous solutions of either imidacloprid or myclobutanil were prepared from a standard stock solution (1000 mg L⁻¹ in acetonitrile) using purified water to achieve the required dilution. Concentrations of each compound were prepared in consecutive steps for each experiment. 5 ml volume of the aqueous solution of each concentration was added to pre-weighed biochar (as required) in a 15 ml centrifuge tube. Tubes were capped tightly and left to equilibrate on a rotary shaker at ambient air temperature (22 °C). Tubes were centrifuged at 5000 rpm under cooling (5 °C) for 5 min. 1 mL of the supernatant was transferred to a PTFE-capped glass vial for direct chromatographic analysis to determine the concentration of imidacloprid or myclobutanil in the solution using LC-MS/MS, thereby calculating the removal percentage or adsorbed amount on biochar.

The following pesticide concentrations were used, depending on the experiment: 5 mg L⁻¹ for pH, contact time, and dose–response studies; 10 mg L⁻¹ for isotherm and kinetic modeling; and 50 mg L⁻¹ for simulated field-use conditions and regeneration tests. Biochar doses varied by the experiment’s purpose. A biochar dose of 5 mg per mL solution (5 g/L) was used for most standard tests. Variable doses (1–10 mg/ml) were applied in adsorbent dose–response experiments. A high dose of 100 mg/mL was used for short-contact time studies. In all cases, adsorption capacity (qe, mg/g) and removal efficiency (%) were calculated based on the actual biochar mass used, ensuring consistent normalization. All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and the standard deviation is provided in all figures to ensure statistical significance.

Effect of contact time

To investigate the time required for equilibration, 5 mg of biochar was mixed with 1 mL of an aqueous solution of imidacloprid or myclobutanil (5 mg L⁻¹) in a centrifuge tube and shaken on a rotary shaker. Samples were taken at 10, 30, 60, 180, 360, 720, 1440, and 2160 min. Sample tubes were centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 5 min, and 1 ml of the supernatant was transferred to a vial for analysis.

Effect of pH

Buffered aqueous solutions of imidacloprid or myclobutanil (5 mg L⁻¹) in different pH values (4, 7, and 10) and tap water were prepared. Then, 5 ml of each solution was transferred into a 15 ml centrifuge tube in triplicate, and 5 mg of biochar per ml of solution was added. Tubes were shaken at ambient on a rotary shaker for 24 h, and supernatant samples were collected after centrifugation from each tube to determine the removal percentage of imidacloprid or myclobutanil.

Effect of adsorbent dose

Successive amounts of biochar (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7.5, and 10 mg) were added to 15 mL centrifuge tubes containing 1 mL of aqueous solution of 5 mg L⁻¹ imidacloprid or myclobutanil. The tubes were shaken 24 h before centrifuging, and 1 ml of the supernatant was transferred to a sample vial for injection to determine the removal percentage of imidacloprid or myclobutanil.

Removal of pesticide formulations at equilibrium

Removal efficiency (adsorption percent) was tested for increasing amounts of the produced biochar per ml of water. 5, 10, 15, and 20 mg of biochar were added to 1 mL of a mixed formulation of imidacloprid and myclobutanil at 50 mg L⁻¹ for each compound (spray solution concentration) in a 15 mL centrifuge tube containing well water. Tubes were firmly capped and shaken for 12 h at 22 °C before centrifugation. The concentration of 50 mg L⁻¹ for each pesticide was selected to represent typical spray-tank and rinsates concentrations arising from recommended field application rates. Thus, this experiment is designed to simulate the treatment of highly contaminated agricultural wastewaters (e.g., leftover spray solutions and sprayer washing water), rather than background environmental concentrations. The supernatant was pipetted out, and 1 ml was taken for analysis to determine the removal percentage of imidacloprid and myclobutanil.

Removal at high biochar dose and short contact time

100 mg of biochar was added to each mL of a mixed formulation of imidacloprid and myclobutanil at 50 mg L⁻¹ in a centrifuge tube. Tubes were mixed for 30 min. The tubes were centrifuged at 5000 rpm, and a 1 mL aliquot of the upper solution was taken for analysis to determine the removal percentages of imidacloprid and myclobutanil.

Sample preparation for analysis

A direct injection approach was used for the aqueous clear supernatant samples obtained, without extraction, enrichment, or clean-up. Samples with concentrations above the linear range were diluted with matrix-matched extract. Hydrolysis of compounds and matrix effect were equalized using control tubes containing the same pesticide solution in biochar extract without biochar. Control experiments were retained as experimental tubes and analyzed concurrently with the respective test experiment. Where the control experiment was not applicable, a matrix-matched standard approach was employed to compensate for matrix effects.

LC–MS/MS conditions

Samples were analyzed using a Thermo Fisher Scientific (Austin, TX, USA) LC-MS/MS System consisting of a Dionex Ultimate 3000 RS UHPLC connected to a TSQ Altis triple quadrupole mass spectrometer, and Trace Finder (v4.1 SP4) software was used to operate the system. Validation of the analytical method

The LC–MS/MS method for the determination of imidacloprid and myclobutanil in aqueous samples was validated in accordance with SANTE guidelines51. Method validation covered selectivity, repeatability, linearity, limit of detection (LOD), and matrix effects in PPB and ELB extracts. Matrix-matched calibration was established separately for each biochar matrix to correct for matrix-related signal variation. Calibration curves were prepared over concentration ranges of 1–100 µg/L for imidacloprid and 10–200 µg/L for myclobutanil. Because the analyzed phase consisted of clear aqueous supernatants obtained after centrifugation and injected directly after dilution, when necessary, without extraction, clean-up, or enrichment, conventional recovery-based accuracy was not assessed.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three replicates. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 23. Data were initially tested for normality. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s HSD post hoc test was used to compare treatment means, and two-tailed t-tests. Differences were considered statistically significant at P ≤ 0.05.

Results and discussion

Characteristics of the prepared biochar

Surface area, pore size distribution, and morphology

Table 2 outlines the physical and chemical properties of the two adsorbents, PPB and ELB. PPB demonstrates a superior specific surface area of 6.56 m²/g compared to ELB’s 2.72 m²/g, suggesting PPB offers a larger surface area for adsorption. PPB exhibits a greater total pore volume (2.26 × 10⁻² cm³/g) compared to ELB (3.96 × 10⁻³ cm³/g), indicating that PPB has the capacity to hold a larger quantity of adsorbate within its pores. The pore diameter of PPB (13.77 nm) exceeds that of ELB (5.81 nm), potentially affecting the accessibility of adsorbate molecules to the internal surface area of the adsorbents. The particle size ranges for both adsorbents are relatively broad, with PPB 7–190 μm and ELB 5–190 μm. Both adsorbents exhibit high carbon content, with PPB at 54.61% and ELB at 61.84%, a characteristic that is advantageous for adsorption applications.

The SEM images (Fig. 3) show that the two biochar adsorbents have very different shapes. The surface of palm biochar (Fig. 3a) is more porous and uneven, with clear cracks and fissures. This means it has a larger surface area and may be better at absorbing things52. However, the surface of the leaves’ biochar (Fig. 3b) doesn’t seem to have as many different textures. It has more plate-like structures and fewer microporosities than the palm biochar. Because the original leaf material contained more volatile compounds (hemicellulose and cellulose) and thinner cell walls, it seems that during pyrolysis, more structural breakdown and cell-unit fusion occurred. Even though the microscope may make the surface look smoother, the internal hole structure may still be present, though harder to reach or see at this level of magnification. These structural differences likely affect how well biochar absorbs substances. The fact that palm biochar appears to have more open spaces on the outside suggests it may have more accessible sites, which could lead to better overall efficiency53.

XRD characteristics

The XRD patterns for both Palm (A) and Leaves (B) biochar exhibit broad, diffuse peaks, indicative of amorphous materials, as shown in Fig. 6. This suggests that the biochar possesses a disordered, non-crystalline structure, characterized by an absence of long-range order in their atomic arrangement. The existence of these broad peaks may indicate the presence of small, poorly defined crystalline domains within the amorphous matrix. The XRD data indicate that both biochars exhibit a predominantly amorphous structure, which may affect their physical and chemical properties, particularly their adsorption capacities.

The combination of mesoporosity (Type IV isotherms with H3 hysteresis), appreciable pore volume, and high carbon and oxygen contents supports the presence of an accessible pore network decorated with oxygen-containing functional groups (carboxyl, hydroxyl, carbonyl). These structural and chemical features are known to promote both physical entrapment within mesopores and specific interactions (hydrogen bonding, π–π interactions, and electrostatic attraction/repulsion) with organic pollutants, providing a mechanistic basis for the adsorption behavior observed in this work.

Validation of the analytical method

The LC–MS/MS method was validated for the determination of imidacloprid and myclobutanil in aqueous samples according to SANTE guidelines51, and the validation results are summarized in Table 5. The method showed satisfactory selectivity, with imidacloprid and myclobutanil detected at retention times of 8.70 and 12.38 min, respectively. Quantification was based on the transitions 256.1→175.1 for imidacloprid and 289.1→70.0 for myclobutanil, whereas the additional transitions 256.1→209.1 and 289.1→124.9 were used for identification.

As shown in Table 5, precision was satisfactory in both biochar-related matrices. For imidacloprid, the RSD values were 3.88% in PPB and 6.59% in ELB, whereas for myclobutanil, they were 5.41% in PPB and 7.24% in ELB, indicating acceptable repeatability under the applied conditions. Linearity was also satisfactory in both matrices. For imidacloprid, the coefficients of determination (R²) were 0.9998 in PPB and 0.9998 in ELB over the range 1–100 µg/L, while for myclobutanil, the corresponding R² values were 0.9986 in PPB and 0.9993 in ELB over the range 10–200 µg/L (Figure S1 and S2). Method sensitivity was adequate for both analytes, with LOD values of 0.8 and 0.6 µg/L for imidacloprid in PPB and ELB, respectively, and 3.4 and 2.3 µg/L for myclobutanil in PPB and ELB, respectively.

Matrix effects were minor and negative in all tested matrices, indicating slight ion suppression under the applied LC–MS/MS conditions (Table 5). For imidacloprid, the matrix effect was − 8.07% in PPB and − 4.33% in ELB, whereas for myclobutanil it was − 7.34% in PPB and − 6.77% in ELB. Accordingly, matrix-matched calibration was applied separately in each biochar matrix to compensate for matrix-related signal variation and ensure reliable quantification.

Conventional recovery-based accuracy was not evaluated because the analyzed phase was the clear aqueous supernatant obtained after centrifugation. This supernatant was diluted as needed and injected directly into the LC–MS/MS system without extraction, cleanup, or enrichment. Under these conditions, no sample-preparation step was involved that could influence analyte recovery. Therefore, the reliability of quantification was supported by direct injection of the aqueous phase, along with satisfactory selectivity, repeatability, linearity, low detection limits, and matrix-matched calibration. Collectively, the validation data presented in Table 5 confirm that the method was fit for purpose for monitoring imidacloprid and myclobutanil in the treated aqueous samples investigated in this study.

Adsorption behavior of imidacloprid and myclobutanil

pH Influence

pH substantially affects the adsorption of both myclobutanil and imidacloprid onto biochar, with maximum removal observed at pH 7 (Fig. 7). At this pH, the biochar surface is expected to be near-neutral or moderately charged, reducing electrostatic repulsion and favoring a closer approach of the pesticide molecules. The stronger adsorption of both pesticides at pH 7 can be rationalized by their speciation and the expected surface charge of biochar in this pH range. Imidacloprid and myclobutanil are predominantly present in their neutral molecular forms between mildly acidic and near-neutral conditions, so electrostatic repulsion between the solute and the biochar surface is minimized. Under these conditions, hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and π–π interactions between the aromatic and heterocyclic moieties of the pesticides and the oxygenated/aromatic domains of PPB and ELB are favored56. At lower or higher pH values, the ionization state of surface functional groups (e.g., deprotonation of carboxyl and phenolic groups) and changes in pesticide speciation can increase electrostatic repulsion or disrupt hydrogen bonding, thereby reducing uptake. Our interpretation is therefore consistent with the observed bell-shaped pH dependence and with prior reports on pesticide–biochar systems. However, direct surface-charge measurements were not available in this study.

In addition, examination of Fig. 7a shows that at moderately acidic pH (4.0–5.0), imidacloprid is removed more efficiently than myclobutanil, which can be attributed to differences in their physicochemical properties and specific interactions with the adsorption sites under these conditions.

Adsorption mechanism

Adsorption is an essential step in detecting pesticide residues. This is a physicochemical process in which pesticide molecules adhere to the surfaces of adsorbent particles via bonding, hydrogen bonding, ionic bonding, covalent bonding, and van der Waals interactions among charged potential sites on soil colloids58. This procedure assesses the accumulation and dispersion of pesticides in the environment and their effects on environmental and food safety. The mechanism of pesticide adsorption on biochar encompasses many processes. The charged functional groups on the PPB and ELB surfaces interact with the charged pesticide molecules via electrostatic attraction, a chemical adsorption phenomenon59. Furthermore, pesticide molecules may be physically confined within the biochar pores by size exclusion (physical adsorption). Due to biochar’s relatively small pore diameters, not all pesticide molecules can infiltrate and be sequestered within the pores. In conclusion, biochar may adsorb pesticides via both chemical and physical adsorption mechanisms. The two adsorption mechanisms frequently interact and collaborate during pesticide adsorption. Biochar adsorption diminishes the transport and transformation of pesticides in soil, thereby mitigating their potential environmental and ecological impacts60. The porous structure and extensive surface area of biochar influence the physical adsorption of pesticides.

The chemical characteristics of biochar dictate its capacity for the chemical adsorption of pesticides. The oxygen functional groups, including hydroxyl and carboxyl groups, present on the PPB and ELB surfaces can establish chemical interactions with the oxygen and nitrogen functional groups in imidacloprid and myclobutanil pesticides. Moreover, biochar’s surface charge can influence the adsorption characteristics of pesticides. Chemical adsorption includes π–π conjugation among compounds, hydrogen bonding between insecticides and biochar, and other interactions. The oxygen atom in the pesticide’s oxygen-containing group exhibits strong electronegativity, facilitating hydrogen bonding with functional groups on the biochar surface61. The molecules of imidacloprid and myclobutanil pesticides act as hydrogen bond donors and acceptors, enabling interactions with various functional groups. Hydrogen bonding is a fundamental interaction. Hydrogen bonds form when the hydroxyl groups on the biochar surface and the functional groups on the pesticide molecules meet appropriate geometric and electrostatic criteria. Establishing hydrogen bonds increases the adsorption stability of pesticide molecules on the biochar surface. Furthermore, biochar possesses several surface functional groups, including hydroxyl, carboxyl, amide, and aromatic structures, which can engage with adsorbed molecules via noncovalent interactions. The π-π electron donor-acceptor contact is a significant kind of noncovalent interaction. The π-π electron donor-acceptor interaction is a process involving the contact between electron-rich (donor) and electron-deficient (acceptor) regions in molecules, primarily through their π-electron systems, often linked to aromatic structures62. Taken together, the pH dependence, isotherm fits, kinetic modeling, and thermodynamic parameters suggest that adsorption of imidacloprid and myclobutanil onto PPB and ELB proceeds via a combination of physical adsorption (pore filling and van der Waals interactions in the mesoporous carbon matrix) and specific interactions involving the oxygen-containing functional groups on the biochar surfaces (hydrogen bonding and π–π donor–acceptor interactions)63.

Effects of temperature and thermodynamic analysis

Figure 15a depicts the research results that were carried out to evaluate the influence of temperature on the efficiency with which imidacloprid and myclobutanil molecules are removed. It was established that the adsorption efficacy of pesticide molecules using the adsorbents decreased with increasing temperature, suggesting that the removal process is exothermic. This was determined based on the observations. The increasing tendency of absorbed imidacloprid and myclobutanil molecules to detach from the surface and disperse into the liquid, contraction, alteration of active sites on the surface of the sorbent at elevated temperatures, and increased mobility of imidacloprid and myclobutanil molecules in aqueous solutions are all examples of factors that could be responsible for this phenomenon. A comprehensive investigation of the adsorption of imidacloprid and myclobutanil molecules on adsorbents was conducted by calculating ΔG◦, ΔH◦, and ΔS◦. This was accomplished using the Van’t Hoff equation, as seen in Fig. 15b. The results of this investigation are shown in Table 8. Within the provided temperature range, the ΔG◦ values for both adsorbents were negative, indicating that the abatement process proceeded spontaneously. The ΔG◦ parameter was also tested under controlled laboratory conditions, yielding values ranging from − 3.09 to −3.63 kJ/mol and − 2.39 to −1.35 kJ/mol for PPB with imidacloprid and myclobutanil, respectively. Also, the ΔG◦ values were recorded to be −1.95 kJ/mol to 2.47 kJ/mol and − 2.17 kJ/mol to −3.06 kJ/mol in the case of ELB for imidacloprid and myclobutanil molecules, respectively. According to Foroutan et al., a physical process predominantly determines the removal of imidacloprid and myclobutanil from solution using biochar made from PPB and ELB72. On the other hand, the enthalpy change (ΔH◦) values for the removal of imidacloprid and myclobutanil molecules using PPB and ELB adsorbents were found to be (−10.02, −14.83) and (−13.71, −10.28) kJ/mol, respectively. Considering these figures, it can be concluded that the adsorption process is both physical and exothermic in nature73. Additionally, it can be concluded that the random contacts between the sorbent surfaces and the imidacloprid and myclobutanil molecules decreased over the course of the process. This conclusion is based on the measured value of the ΔS° parameter. The relatively small negative enthalpy changes (ΔH° ≈ −10 to − 15 kJ mol⁻¹) and modest negative ΔG° values indicate that the process is spontaneous and predominantly physical in nature, consistent with physisorption enhanced by weaker specific interactions such as hydrogen bonding and π–π interactions. In this context, the good agreement with the pseudo-second-order kinetic model should be interpreted as describing the apparent rate of a surface-controlled, physisorption-dominated process, rather than as direct evidence of strong chemisorption.

Reusability of the prepared biochars

To satisfy the requirements of the environmental application, it is essential to have the capability to reuse biosorbent biochar made from PPB and ELB. For this reason, it is essential to repeat the adsorption and regeneration processes to verify the efficacy of the adsorption application. After each experiment, the nanomaterials made of PPB and ELB were filtered, washed, and then reused. The adsorption process was carried out five times, each time at conditions that were quite optimal. Figure 16 shows that after five cycles, the proportion of imidacloprid and myclobutanil speciation that has been removed using PPB is 79% and 68%, respectively. In contrast, the percentages removed using ELB are 73% and 69%, respectively. This demonstrates that the biochar made from palm or eucalyptus can successfully remove pesticide molecules.

Removal of pesticide formulations under practical application conditions

Removal efficiency at equilibrium

The applicability of the produced biochars for removing imidacloprid and myclobutanil from formulation solutions was evaluated at 50 mg L⁻¹ for each pesticide, representing concentrated spray-tank residues and sprayer-rinsing waste. Because formulated products contain both active ingredients and co-formulants, their removal may differ from that of pure active substances owing to competition for adsorption sites and possible effects on pesticide solubility. A mixed formulation containing both pesticides was therefore used to reflect practical agricultural waste conditions better. The produced biochars showed increasing removal efficiency with increasing biochar dose. As shown in Table 9, PPB achieved significantly higher removal than ELB at all tested doses (P < 0.05). In PPB, the removal of imidacloprid and myclobutanil did not differ significantly at the same dose levels (P > 0.05). In contrast, in ELB, myclobutanil removal was significantly higher than that of imidacloprid (P < 0.05), likely because the lower number of available sites in ELB favored the less water-soluble, higher log P myclobutanil. Removal efficiency of imidacloprid and myclobutanil formulation at high dose and short contact time

A rapid-treatment experiment (30 min) was conducted to evaluate the efficiency of the produced biochars in removing a mixed solution of imidacloprid and myclobutanil at 50 mg L⁻¹ for each pesticide, using a high biochar dose (100 mg mL⁻¹). The results were consistent with those obtained in the equilibrium experiment and confirmed that PPB was more effective than ELB for both compounds. Under these conditions, PPB removed 87.36% of imidacloprid and 92.10% of myclobutanil, whereas ELB removed 66.62% and 80.01%, respectively. In both biochars, myclobutanil showed higher removal than imidacloprid, and the superiority of PPB under short-contact, high-dose conditions further supports its stronger practical applicability for rapid treatment of concentrated pesticide-containing wastewaters.

Comparative performance with other biochar and activated carbon

Carbonaceous sorbents derived from agricultural and forestry residues exhibit wide variability in pesticide-removal capacity, which is governed by feedstock chemistry, pyrolysis conditions, and post-treatment. In regions such as MENA, palm and eucalyptus wastes are abundant and often mismanaged; converting these residues into biochar provides both a waste-valorization route and a low-cost adsorbent for water treatment. Our results with palm petiole biochar (PPB) and eucalyptus leaf biochar (ELB), therefore, emphasize not only performance but also regional practicality and scalability. Representative adsorption metrics from the literature, compiled from published studies rather than from side-by-side experiments under identical conditions, are summarized in Table 10.

For imidacloprid (IMI), reported capacities for unmodified biochars generally fall in the low-to-mid tens of mg g⁻¹ (e.g., qₘₐₓ ≈ 14.75 mg g⁻¹ for eucalyptus-wood biochar74. In contrast, commercial activated carbons often reach ~ 30 mg g⁻¹ or higher75. Chemical or metal modification can further increase capacity, as shown for Fe-modified sugarcane bagasse biochar (~ 10–15 mg g⁻¹)76 and peanut-shell biochar (≈ 18–23 mg g⁻¹, temperature-dependent)77. More strongly engineered sorbents such as Fe/Mg-modified water-hyacinth biochar (~ 114 mg g⁻¹)78, activated carbons from hazelnut/walnut shells (~ 76.9–83.3 mg g⁻¹)79, and Fe₃O₄/AC composites (~ 192 mg g⁻¹ at 25 °C)80 can exceed 100 mg g⁻¹. In our study, ELB exhibited the higher Langmuir capacity for IMI (qₘₐₓ = 9.205 mg g⁻¹) relative to PPB (qₘₐₓ = 5.043 mg g⁻¹). While these values are below those of the best-performing sorbents, they are consistent with those of low-temperature-prepared biochars and offer advantages in cost, ease of production, and local availability. For myclobutanil (MYC), quantitative adsorption data for biochars and ACs in water-treatment contexts are comparatively scarce. CTAB-functionalized zeolites show strong MYC uptake81, and studies on organic residues (e.g., sewage sludge, grape marc) report substantial affinity via Freundlich/Kd parameters rather than qₘₐₓ82. Our work, therefore, provides useful benchmarks: ELB displayed qₘₐₓ = 17.036 mg g⁻¹ and PPB qₘₐₓ = 10.040 mg g⁻¹ for MYC (Table 5). In formulation-matrix tests at 50 mg L⁻¹, PPB removed 90.7% (IMI) and 92.9% (MYC) at 20 mg mL⁻¹ (12 h), outperforming ELB (Table 8), and under high-dose/short-contact conditions (100 mg mL⁻¹, 30 min), PPB again showed higher removal (IMI 87.36%, MYC 92.10%) than ELB (IMI 66.62%, MYC 80.01%). This apparent discrepancy—ELB > PPB in qₘₐₓ yet PPB > ELB in formulation removal—can be rationalized by kinetic and matrix effects (e.g., faster site accessibility and larger mesopores of PPB in the presence of co-formulants), together with the higher specific surface area and pore volume of PPB.

The performance–sustainability trade-off is evident: activated carbons generally reach higher capacities for IMI but require energy-intensive activation and are more expensive65,66. PPB and ELB provide credible capacities at minimal processing severity, remove both target pesticides efficiently in realistic matrices, and remain reusable (Sect. 3.7), making palm- and eucalyptus-waste biochars attractive regionally optimized sorbents. Future optimization could explore mild surface modifications (e.g., iron or phosphorus doping) to narrow the capacity gap while preserving a low energy and cost footprint, as indicated by previous studies on modified biochars76,78. The concentration ranges and solid–liquid ratios used in this study should be interpreted in the context of the intended application. Here, pesticide concentrations up to 50 mg L⁻¹ were deliberately selected to mimic spray-tank solutions and sprayer rinsates, which represent small-volume but highly contaminated agricultural waste streams. Under such conditions, biochar doses of 5–20 g L⁻¹ are realistic for on-farm treatment units, particularly when PPB and ELB are produced locally from abundant palm and eucalyptus residues at very low cost. The extreme dose of 100 g L⁻¹ employed in the 30 min tests is better viewed as a rapid, emergency treatment scenario for limited volumes of residual pesticide formulations rather than as a typical operating point. For larger-scale or continuous treatment of more dilute waters (e.g., canals, drainage), PPB and ELB would be more practically implemented in fixed-bed filters or permeable reactive barriers, where high sorbent densities are confined within the treatment unit. The overall sorbent demand per cubic meter of treated water can be optimized by adjusting bed design, contact time, and regeneration cycles. While the present work focuses on concentrated point-source wastewaters, the Langmuir and Freundlich parameters obtained provide a basis for estimating sorption behavior.

In the low-concentration regime, where sorbent sites remain far from saturation, however, dedicated experiments at environmentally realistic concentrations and a detailed techno-economic analysis of dosing strategies will be necessary in future studies.

From a practical standpoint, the highest biochar dose investigated in the formulation tests (20 g L⁻¹ at 50 mg L⁻¹ of each pesticide) corresponds to a requirement of 20 kg of PPB or ELB per cubic meter of spray solution or rinsate. The cost of sorbent per cubic meter of treated waste is therefore directly obtained by multiplying this mass by the locally determined biochar production cost, which depends on feedstock type, pyrolysis scale, and energy costs; a full techno-economic analysis is beyond the scope of this work. Using the Langmuir capacities determined in this study and the same formulation concentration, each kilogram of PPB or ELB is expected, under ideal equilibrium conditions, to treat on the order of 10² liters of such concentrated waste before saturation, with the exact value depending on the pesticide–sorbent pair. These values should be regarded as upper bounds; actual breakthrough bed volumes in fixed-bed operation will be lower and need to be established in future column studies under realistic hydraulic and operational conditions.

Conclusions

Biochars prepared from palm petioles (PPB) and eucalyptus leaves (ELB) showed clear potential as low-cost sorbents for the treatment of concentrated agricultural point-source wastewaters containing imidacloprid and myclobutanil. PPB consistently showed stronger practical removal performance than ELB in formulation-based experiments and retained substantial removal efficiency after five reuse cycles. Under the tested conditions, PPB achieved removal efficiencies of 90.70% and 92.89% for imidacloprid and myclobutanil, respectively, compared with 67.17% and 82.38% for ELB. In the rapid-treatment experiment, PPB again outperformed ELB, confirming its stronger practical applicability.

Although ELB showed higher Langmuir capacities than PPB, PPB performed better in realistic formulation matrices, indicating that practical performance depended not only on equilibrium capacity but also on pore accessibility, textural properties, and matrix-related effects. Adsorption was spontaneous and exothermic within the studied temperature range, and the combined evidence supported a predominantly physisorption-driven process with contributions from weaker specific interactions.

This study was limited to batch experiments and concentrated on point-source wastewater rather than continuous-flow systems or real field wastewater matrices. In addition, direct spectroscopic and electrokinetic evidence for the proposed adsorption mechanism, as well as a detailed techno-economic assessment, were beyond the scope of the present work. Future studies should therefore evaluate PPB and ELB in real agricultural wastewater, in fixed-bed or column systems, with extended regeneration cycles, and use complementary surface characterization tools and cost analysis to support practical implementation.
