Isolation and characterization of LH5, a novel rodent Embecovirus from Rattus tanezumi with respiratory tropism in a mouse model
Authors: Qian Li, Keyao Xia, Yutong Hou, Xiang Le, Yayu Fan, Yushan Kui, Lulu Deng, Binghui Wang, Xueshan Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCoronaviruses (CoVs) have contributed substantially to recent global public health crises (e.g., SARS, SARS-CoV-2, and MERS-CoV). Wild animals are the primary natural reservoirs of CoVs prior to human transmission.ResultsIn this study, we report the isolation and characterization of a novel β-CoV strain, designated Murine-CoV-LH5, from Rattus tanezumi in Dehong Prefecture, Yunnan Province. Background

Coronaviruses (CoVs) are enveloped, single-stranded, positive-sense RNA viruses belonging to the family Coronaviridae and the subfamily Orthocoronavirinae. They have the longest genome among known RNA viruses, ranging from 26 to 32 kb. Based on genomic characteristics and serological properties, the Coronaviridae family is divided into four genera: α-, β-, γ-, and δ-CoVs. Among these, α- and β-CoVs primarily infect mammals, whereas γ- and δ-CoVs mainly infect bird [1].

Recent CoV pandemics, such as those caused by SARS-CoV, MERS-CoV, and SARS-CoV-2, have highlighted the potential zoonotic risks posed by these viruses [2, 3]. While much attention has been given to human CoVs, wild animals, especially rodents, are often the natural reservoirs of CoVs before they spill over into human populations. Bats, rodents, and other wildlife species harbor a diverse array of CoVs, some of which have been shown to have zoonotic potential. Genomic studies of these rodent-borne CoVs provide insight into their evolutionary history and potential for cross-species transmission.

CoVs possess distinctive genetic characteristics that allow them to infect a wide range of hosts. Their genome is unique, containing replicase genes (ORF1a/b) at the 5′ end and genes encoding structural (S, E, M, and N) and accessory proteins at the 3′ end. The high mutation rate and recombination capability of CoVs enable them to quickly adapt to new host environments, thereby increasing their potential for cross-species transmission. The furin cleavage site (RRAR) is a critical feature of the CoV spike protein. Furin, a host protease widely expressed in various tissues, cleaves S protein at the S1/S2 boundary, priming it for receptor binding and membrane fusion. This mechanism enhances viral infectivity and cross-species transmission. In SARS-CoV-2, acquisition of the furin cleavage site is considered a key factor in its high transmissibility and host adaptability. The host range of CoVs is extensive, encompassing various animals, such as bats, rodents, birds, and livestock, that can serve as natural reservoirs or intermediate hosts [4, 5]. Wild animals are often the natural reservoirs of these viruses before they spread to humans. For example, bats and rodents harbor diverse arrays of CoVs [6]. Detailed genomic studies of these viruses can reveal their evolutionary histories and potential for zoonotic transmission.

Rodents are widely distributed, often coexisting near human habitats, and serve as significant reservoirs for zoonotic pathogens, including CoVs, highlighting their potential to transmit viruses across species boundaries [7]. Ahn et al. investigated the presence of CoVs in wild rodents across various regions of South Korea and identified multiple CoV strains, including novel alpha and beta CoVs [8]. Their study highlighted the widespread distribution of CoVs among different rodent species and regions, suggesting that these viruses are not only diverse but also widely disseminated in rodent populations. Becsei et al. explored the presence of rodent CoVs in the urban environment of New York City, USA [9] and found broad diversity and widespread distribution of rodent CoVs in different urban settings. These findings revealed the impact of urbanization on the spread of CoVs in rodent hosts and highlighted the need to monitor these viruses in densely populated human areas.

Yunnan shares cultural and geographical connections with Myanmar, Laos, and Vietnam in mainland Southeast Asia, an area known as a biodiversity hotspot for various animals, including rodents. Climate change and habitat degradation alter wild animal populations and increase human-animal contact, enhancing spillover risks [10]. Many novel CoVs have been found in rodents in Yunnan, indicating that rodents may carry other unidentified CoVs [11]. Li et al. discovered a novel CoV species, RCoV-GCCDC4, in wild rodents in Yunnan with a potentially recombinant genome and a polybasic cleavage site, providing new insight into the evolution of CoVs [12]. The initial analysis of rodent-associated viruses in Yunnan provided valuable insights into viral ecology and evolution.

Given their widespread distribution and frequent human contact, investigating CoVs in rodents and their potential for cross-species transmission is crucial. Understanding the prevalence and diversity of rodent CoVs can help predict and mitigate the potential zoonotic risks. Owing to the genetic similarities between rodent and human CoVs, such research may contribute to the development of new diagnostic, therapeutic, and preventive strategies against future outbreaks. Additionally, studying the genomic characteristics and evolutionary mechanisms of these viruses offers valuable insights into how CoVs adapt and transmit across species.

This study aimed to investigate the presence of CoVs in Rattus tanezumi from Dehong and Xishuangbanna Prefectures, Yunnan Province, and determine their genetic characteristics. Two CoV strains were discovered and their full-length genome sequences were annotated and analyzed. Additionally, the CoV strain from Rattus tanezumi was isolated for the first time.

Results

Identification of Murine-CoV-LH5 and its genetic characteristics

Our surveillance revealed a 6.56% (63/960) positivity rate for CoVs among rodents in Yunnan Province. From this pool, we successfully obtained two novel β-CoV strains, designated LH5 and MH9, from wild rodents in the Dehong and Xishuangbanna Prefectures (Fig. 1D).Fig. 1Genome characteristics of murine HCoV-OC43-like CoV (LH5-CoV)

Genome characteristics of murine HCoV-OC43-like CoV (LH5-CoV)

Both LH5 and MH9 possess the typical genomic architecture of Embecovirus, with a ~ 30-kb genome containing the characteristic open reading frames and structural protein genes (HE, S, E, M, and N) (Fig. 1A). Phylogenetic analysis based on complete genomes placed both strains firmly within the Embecovirus subgenus, forming a distinct cluster with other known rodent CoVs, which suggests a shared evolutionary history within rodent hosts. This subgenus also includes human pathogens (e.g., HCoV-OC43, HCoV-HKU1), underscoring its relevance to public health (Fig. 1C).

Recombination analysis indicated that the LH5 genome is largely collinear with its closest relatives, except for notable fluctuations in similarity within the spike (S) protein gene region, suggesting potential recombination events in this area (Fig. 1B). Comparative genomic analysis confirmed that LH5 and MH9 are most closely related to each other (91.14% nucleotide identity across the genome). Notably, their S protein genes share exceptionally high identity (97.88%), yet both exhibit the lowest homology (< 80.96%) to other Embecovirus in this region, highlighting their uniqueness (Table 1). This pattern supports the possibility of a distinct evolutionary or recombinant history for their S proteins. We identified a putative furin cleavage site (position 746–749) in the S protein of both LH5 and MH9 (Fig. 1E). While such a site is uncommon among rodent Embecovirus and is often associated with enhanced pathogenicity or host range in other virus families, its functional role in LH5 requires direct experimental validation. Furthermore, sequence alignment revealed a region of high conservation (aa 145–156, 179–193) with other Embecovirus, potentially indicative of an important functional domain. In contrast, specific sequence variations were observed near a region corresponding to the receptor-binding domain of human Embecovirus (e.g., around aa 260–266 in LH5). These differences suggest that LH5 may employ a distinct host entry mechanism compared to HCoV-OC43 and HCoV-HKU1, reflecting its adaptation to rodent hosts.

Analysis of LH5 S protein binding to host receptors

To explore the potential mechanism of cellular entry for Murine-CoV-LH5, we performed in silico molecular docking simulations between its spike (S) protein and several known CoV host factors: ACE2, TMPRSS2, APN, DPP4, and 9-O-acetylated sialic acid (9-O-Ac-Sia) (Fig. 2). The docking results predicted strong binding interactions between the LH5 S protein and specific human host factors. Notably, the analysis indicated a high binding affinity for TMPRSS2 (Fig. 2C) and ACE2 (Fig. 2E), with detailed modeling revealing potential key amino acid contacts at the predicted interfaces. Comparative analysis showed that TMPRSS2 consistently scored the highest predicted binding affinity, while APN (Fig. 2D) showed the weakest among all tested receptors (Table 2). Based on these computational predictions, we propose a hypothetical entry model for LH5. TMPRSS2 and ACE2 are predicted as the most plausible primary receptor candidates, given their high docking scores and the predicted quality of interactions (e.g., hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic contacts). The strong predicted association with TMPRSS2 suggests it may play a critical role in facilitating S protein cleavage, a key step for viral entry. The predicted binding to human ACE2 raises the possibility that LH5 may have the potential to interact with human cells, although this requires urgent experimental confirmation. APN and DPP4 may serve as secondary or alternative receptors, albeit with lower predicted affinity. The interaction with 9-O-Ac-Sia is predicted to be strong, suggesting it could function in initial viral attachment, analogous to other Embecovirus.

It is crucial to emphasize that these findings are derived from computational simulations. While they provide valuable hypotheses, definitive identification of the functional entry receptor(s) for LH5 necessitates direct experimental validation in future studies. This includes expressing the recombinant LH5 S and HE proteins to confirm binding specificity through assays such as hemagglutination (HA), surface plasmon resonance (SPR), or virus overlay protein binding assays.

While these results provide preliminary hypotheses regarding potential interactions between LH5 and receptor(s) such as ACE2 and TMPRSS2, definitive identification of the functional entry receptor(s) for LH5 requires direct experimental validation. Such validation may include expressing recombinant LH5 S and HE proteins and assessing binding specificity using approaches such as hemagglutination (HA), surface plasmon resonance (SPR), or virus overlay protein binding assays.

Isolation of Murine-CoV-LH5 from Rattus tanezumi in the suckling C57 mice

To establish a tractable animal model for initial pathogenicity studies and considering the ethical and practical constraints of working with wild-caught rodents, we chose to attempt virus isolation in a standard laboratory animal. We performed serial intracerebral passages of the original field samples (LH5 and MH9) in neonatal C57BL/6 mice. While the MH9 strain failed to establish a productive infection, the LH5 virus was successfully isolated and adapted after ten passages, yielding a stable mouse-adapted strain (designated LH5-MA). Our analysis identified several amino acid substitutions between P1 and P10. Specifically, four mutations were observed in ORF1ab at positions 539 (Q → H), 1958 (S → L), 2532 (N → D), and 2534 (D → N). In addition, two substitutions (SA → FV) were identified at positions 196–197 in the N protein. These mutations likely represent adaptive changes acquired during intracerebral passage. However, their functional significance remains unclear and requires further investigation.

Electron microscopy of purified virus particles from infected mouse brain homogenate confirmed the presence of CoV-like particles approximately 100 nm in diameter, with characteristic surface spike proteins (Fig. 3A). Quantification of the viral RNA load in the brains of infected mice at passage 10 revealed a high titer of 4.13 × 106 copies/mL at 4 days post-inoculation (dpi), significantly exceeding the levels detected in other vital organs (Fig. 3B, C), indicating efficient neurotropic replication.Fig. 3Isolation of LH5-CoV

Isolation of LH5-CoV

We next characterized the replication dynamics and pathogenicity of the mouse-adapted LH5-MA strain in weaned C57BL/6 mice. Following intranasal inoculation, viral RNA was detected in both lung and brain tissues. The replication kinetics in these organs were similar, showing an initial lag phase, a peak on day 8 post-infection (reaching an average of 1.54 × 108 copies/g in the brain), followed by a rapid decline (Fig. 3D). Notably, the viral load in the brain was consistently higher than that in the lung throughout the course of infection.

Monitoring of body weight revealed a significant clinical impact. Mice infected with LH5-MA exhibited markedly slower weight gain compared to the mock-infected control group, which maintained a normal growth trajectory (P < 0.05 by Student’s t-test) (Fig. 3E). This growth retardation, coupled with observations of labored breathing in some animals, indicates that LH5-MA infection causes measurable morbidity in this mouse model.

Virus infection and pathogenic characteristics

The viral proliferation pattern of Murine-CoV-LH5 in Vero E6 and 3T3 cells was similar, with rapid replication within the first 36 h post-infection, reaching a peak, and then exhibiting a gradually declining viral load. Notably, the intracellular viral load measurements were consistently higher than those in the supernatant, implying that the virus exhibited limited release into the culture medium. Laser confocal imaging further confirmed viral activity by revealing significant expression of viral proteins within the cytoplasm of infected cells; no such protein expression was detected in the control group, underscoring the specificity of the infection and replication of the viral S protein in Vero E6 cells (Fig. 4A–C).Fig. 4Infection and pathogenic characteristics of LH5-CoV

Infection and pathogenic characteristics of LH5-CoV

In vivo infection studies were conducted to examine the pathogenicity of LH5 in C57 mice. Following infection, several mice exhibited symptoms consistent with respiratory distress such as labored breathing. Histopathological analysis using hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining revealed notable pathological alterations in the lung tissue, including significant infiltration of inflammatory cells, thickening of the alveolar septa, and congestion of vascular structures. These pathological changes are hallmarks of an acute inflammatory response and tissue damage directly attributed to viral infection, illustrating the potential of the virus to induce severe inflammatory damage in the lung tissue. These findings are significant in understanding the impact of Murine-CoV-LH5 on pulmonary health and highlight the potential for respiratory compromise upon infection, echoing similar pathogenic mechanisms observed in other CoVs (Fig. 4D and E).

To further elucidate tissue tropism and the distribution of viral replication, indirect immunofluorescence assays of the lung and brain tissues of infected C57 mice were performed. The results showed a substantial amount of red fluorescence, indicating broad expression of the viral S protein in both the alveolar and bronchial walls within lung tissues and within the cerebral cortex of brain tissues. This pattern of S protein expression confirms that Murine-CoV-LH5 not only infects and replicates efficiently in the lung, but also exhibits neurotropism, allowing it to establish infection in the brain tissue. The presence of viral proteins in the cerebral cortex suggests a potential neurological involvement, which may contribute to a wider spectrum of pathogenesis in infected hosts. These findings expand our understanding of the infective capacity of Murine-CoV-LH5 and its potential for dissemination within host tissues (Fig. 4F and G).

Exploring the infection characteristics and mechanism of Murine-CoV-LH5

To further investigate the infection characteristics of Murine-CoV-LH5, A129 mice were infected by aerosol inhalation. The results showed that the viral load of Murine-CoV-LH5 in the lung tissue of A129 mice was significantly higher than that in other tissues and organs, indicating that the virus can be transmitted through the respiratory tract and that the lungs are the primary site of replication. This is consistent with the characteristics of many CoVs, particularly those associated with the respiratory system. Viral loads in the liver, spleen, kidney, and brain were significantly lower than those in the lungs, indicating that these organs are not the primary sites of viral replication. These data suggest that LH5 primarily affects the respiratory system with minimal impact on other systems (Fig. 5A).Fig. 5Evaluation of LH5-CoV infection

Evaluation of LH5-CoV infection

Additionally, infected mice were co-housed with non-infected mice, and no virus was detected in the non-infected mice, indicating no mouse-to-mouse transmission and, therefore, no risk of such transmission. Histopathological analysis revealed significant inflammatory responses in the bronchial and alveolar tissues of the A129 mice. The bronchial tissue exhibited dense inflammatory cell infiltration, as indicated by the orange arrows (Fig. 5B), and notable epithelial thickening. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) further validated the localization of viral antigens within the lung tissues (Fig. 5C). The red arrows in the lung lobe, bronchus, and alveolus indicate strong viral antigen presence, particularly in the bronchial and alveolar epithelial cells. The specificity of staining in these regions emphasizes the propensity of Murine-CoV-LH5 to infect cells in the respiratory tract, consistent with the high viral RNA load observed in the lungs (Fig. 5A). IF staining of lung tissues confirmed that the virus primarily resided in the cytoplasmic region of infected cells (Fig. 5D). The absence of red fluorescence in the mock group confirmed the specificity of the LH5-S-RBD signal in the infected tissues. These data strongly supported the immunohistochemistry findings (Fig. 5C), further illustrating the preferential infection of respiratory epithelial cells by Murine-CoV-LH5 and corroborating its role as a respiratory pathogen. Due to the small sample size (2 infected and 2 control mice per group), we acknowledge that these results may not fully capture the transmission potential of LH5, and larger studies with increased sample sizes are necessary to provide more definitive conclusions.

Discussion

CoVs are a significant public health concern owing to their potential to cause widespread outbreaks. Although bats are widely recognized as the primary natural reservoir, most virologists agree that bat-derived CoVs typically require an intermediate host to cross species barriers and infect humans. For example, dromedary camels have been identified as intermediate hosts for Middle East respiratory syndrome (MERS). Among potential intermediate hosts, rodents stand out owing to their small size, extensive distribution, frequent interactions with diverse wildlife, and close association with human environment. These characteristics highlight the critical role that rodents may play in the transmission and emergence of zoonotic diseases, making them an essential focus for infectious disease prevention and control.

Our study revealed the diversity of CoVs in Yunnan rodents, highlighting the importance of rodents as natural hosts for CoVs. This finding underscores the need to closely monitor the prevalence of these viruses in rodent populations and pay attention to the potential for cross-species transmission of these rodent CoVs [10]. Yunnan is rich in ecological environment and wild animal resources and is located on the southwest border, adjacent to three Southeast Asian countries, making it an important position for monitoring wild animals as virus hosts [13, 14]. The recent frequent global pandemics make understanding and monitoring CoVs in wild animals crucial for preventing and controlling emerging infectious diseases [15].

LH5 clusters with several rodent CoVs, such as Longquan Ruili-66 Rat [16], RtMn-CoV/GD2015 Rodent [17], and RtRn-CoV/YN2013 Rodent [17]. MH9 also belongs to the Embecovirus subgenus but is slightly different from LH5 and more closely related to other CoVs, such as Longquan-708 Rat. The branches containing LH5 and MH9 were almost exclusively composed of rodent CoVs, suggesting that LH5 and MH9 may also primarily infect rodents. Although LH5 and MH9 are currently mainly associated with rodents, there are viruses within the Embecovirus subgenus that can infect humans, such as HCoV-OC43 and HCoV-HKU1, and HKU1 is also found in rodents. Therefore, there is a risk that they can infect humans, implying that monitoring their potential for cross-species transmission is necessary. Furin cleavage sites are commonly found in highly pathogenic human CoVs (e.g., SARS-CoV-2) but are relatively rare in rodent CoVs. The presence of a furin cleavage site in LH5 and MH9 may represent an intermediate evolutionary state, suggesting their role in bridging rodent-to-human transmission. This feature may have resulted from adaptive mutations that enabled cross-species transmission, warranting further phylogenetic and functional analyses.

The successful isolation and adaptation of Murine-CoV-LH5 in a laboratory mouse model provide a novel and tractable tool for Embecovirus research. While CoV strains like MHV have long been studied in rodents, LH5 represents, to our knowledge, the first Embecovirus isolate obtained from Rattus tanezumi that has been stably propagated in vivo. This addresses a practical need for accessible animal models, particularly under biosafety level 2 (BSL-2) containment. The LH5 model is especially valuable given its dual tropism—capable of establishing both respiratory infection and high-titer neural replication in mice—which offers a unique system to study organ-specific pathogenesis mechanisms. However, the current model has limitations that define key future directions. The failure of LH5 to propagate stably in conventional cell lines (Additional file 1: Fig. S1) underscores the need to identify its precise cellular tropism and optimal in vitro culture conditions, which is also a prerequisite for performing crucial receptor-binding assays with the HE and S proteins. Furthermore, comparing the genome of the mouse-adapted (P10) strain with the original sequence is essential to identify any adaptive mutations that may have emerged during intracerebral passage, which could influence tissue tropism. Finally, the unique genomic features of LH5, such as its furin cleavage site and predicted interaction with human receptors like TMPRSS2 and ACE2, provide specific, hypothesis-driven starting points for exploring its zoonotic potential and for evaluating broad-spectrum antiviral strategies that target conserved Embecovirus entry or replication pathways.

In this study, we confirmed the significant proliferation and replication of Murine-CoV-LH5 in the lungs of A129 mice following aerosol infection. This was consistent with the characteristics of many respiratory CoVs, further supporting that LH5 is a virus of the respiratory system. Additionally, our study demonstrated that LH5 has a significantly lower replication capacity in organs other than the lungs, indicating its limited impact on other systems. These findings provide important evidence for understanding the replication characteristics of CoVs in various host organs.

Through cohousing experiments, we found no evidence of LH5 virus transmission between mice, suggesting that the virus may not have the ability to spread through direct contact among populations. This is crucial for assessing the public health risks associated with virus; however, further studies are needed to confirm whether these results apply to other rodents or in a broader context of transmission potential. Future studies should explore the characteristics of LH5 infection across diverse animal models, particularly those closely related to humans, to assess its zoonotic potential.

TMPRSS2 and ACE2 were identified as the most likely primary entry receptors for LH5 by molecular docking analysis. ACE2 [18, 19] and TMPRSS2 [20, 21] are intracellular receptors of SARS-CoV-2 and HKU1, respectively. LH5 also has the potential to enter other animal cells, including human cells, for infection by binding to both of these receptors. Analyzing the interaction between the LH5 S protein and receptors, such as TMPRSS2 and ACE2, could reveal mechanisms associated with host susceptibility and cross-species transmission potential that are shared with other CoVs and may offer therapeutic targets to block viral entry. The high binding scores for TMPRSS2 and ACE2 suggest that LH5 may have the capacity for cross-species transmission and potential infectivity in humans. These structural features require further experimental validation, including cell-based infection assays and receptor-binding studies, to assess LH5’s pathogenicity in humans.

Conclusions

We have isolated and characterized Murine-CoV-LH5, a novel rodent Embecovirus with unique genetic features and the ability to cause respiratory and neurological infection in a standard mouse model. This virus serves as a valuable tool for studying Embecovirus biology in a BSL-2 setting. Its genomic makeup and predicted receptor profile signal a potential for zoonotic transmission, calling for further functional studies and enhanced surveillance of similar viruses at the human-wildlife interface.

Methods

Sample collection and processing

Samples of Rattus tanezumi were collected from Dehong and Xishuangbanna Prefectures, Yunnan Province. The samples were stored in virus preservation solution, transported on dry ice, and subsequently stored in a − 80 °C refrigerator in the laboratory. They were then dissected; the lung and intestine tissues were removed and placed in virus preservation solution and stored at − 80 °C until RNA extraction. This study was approved by the Institutional Ethical Committee of Kunming University of Science and Technology (protocol no. 16048). Rare and protected animals were not involved.

Host species identification and CoV screening

To confirm the presence of CoVs in the samples, total RNA was extracted from 200 µL of the sample homogenates using a TIANamp Virus RNA Kit (TIANGEN, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. CoV infection in samples was detected based on the RdRp gene present in the α and β CoV genomes using nested PCR. Primers for RdRp were designed to target conserved regions using Oligo Primer Analysis Software ver. 7 (Molecular Biology Insights, Inc., https://www.oligo.net). Modified bases were incorporated at the 5′ ends of the primers. For a broader context on CoV genome detection methodologies, see Wu et al. [17]. Similarly, bat species were identified using the cytochrome b gene (Cytb) through semi-nested PCR (Table 1) [18]. The nested PCR involved two rounds of amplification to increase specificity and sensitivity; the first round used outer primers (10 μM) and the second round used inner primers (10 μM) designed to target specific gene regions. The first-round PCR contained a 25 μL mixture, including 12 μL of PCR buffer (PrimeScrip One Step RT-PCR Kit ver. 2; Takara, San Jose, CA, USA), 1 μL of template RNA, 1 μL of each forward, reverse primers (10 μM), enzyme, and 9 μL of ddH2O. The second-round PCR contained a 25 μL mixture, including 12 μL of PCR Mix (Takara), 1 μL of template cDNA, 1 μL of each forward and reverse primer (10 μM), and 10 μL of ddH2O. The reaction conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 15 s, annealing at 50 °C for 30 s, extension at 72 °C for 30 s, and final extension at 72 °C for 7 min. The amplified products were identified using 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. Samples with positive target bands were sent to Kunming Qingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. for Sanger sequencing.

Genome annotation, homology analysis, and recombination analysis

The full-length genome sequences were obtained by segmental sequencing and assembly using Sanger sequencing. In addition to following basic principles of primer design, the primers were designed to amplify segments with at least 100 bp of overlap. Nested primers were designed using all reference sequences for the same cluster in phylogenetic analyses as the target. Sequencing results were first analyzed using Chromas software to confirm the accuracy of base calling based on peak plots. Preliminary editing and final splicing were then performed using the SeqMan module of DNAStar (ver. 7.1). The reference Cytb and RdRp sequences were downloaded from the GenBank database. Sequence comparisons and editing were performed using ClustalW (version 2.0) and BioEdit (version 7.1.9).

CoV genotypes were determined based on the nucleotide sequences of CoVs and known representative CoV sequences. To generate phylogenetic trees, ModelTest was used to determine the best-fit substitution model. The neighbor-joining method was used to construct phylogenetic trees using the Kimura 2-parameter model (Transitions + Transversions) with gamma-distributed rate variation implemented in MEGA 7.0. Branch support was evaluated using 1000 bootstrap replicates and the accuracy of typing was verified on the basis of bootstrap values > 70%.

The whole genome sequences were initially aligned using BLAST in NCBI (Basic Local Alignment Search Tool) to determine homology and identify conserved regions between our sample sequences and reference sequences. Based on the alignment results, we identified conserved and variable regions. For the conserved regions, we used known ORFs as references to locate corresponding ORFs in our sample sequences. Each identified ORF was then functionally annotated.

The CoV genome structure was drawn using SnapGene (ver. 6.0.2). For a recombination analysis, Simplot (ver. 3.5.1) (http://sray.med.som.jhmi.edu/SCRoftware/simplot/) was used to draw a whole genome sequence similarity map, with a window size of 1000 bp and a step size of 100 bp. BioAider (ver. 1.314) (http://www.mrdisease.com/bioaider/) was used to analyze the sequence identity of the CoVs.

Quantitative PCR

Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was conducted using gene-specific probes and primers. RNA was extracted from samples and used as a template for one-step qPCR. The 20 μL reaction mixture contained 2 × TaqMan Master Mix, 0.4 μM of each forward and reverse primer, 0.4 μM probe, and 1 μL of the RNA template. The qPCR cycling conditions were 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 62 °C for 20 s.

Virus isolation and purification

The Murine-CoV-LH5 strain was isolated from infected Rattus tanezumi. Intracerebral inoculation experiments were performed in suckling mice, and infection was confirmed through lung and brain tissue samples.

Brain inoculation in suckling mice

Two-day-old C57/BL neonatal mice were selected for intracerebral injection, with each mouse receiving 200 μL. Body weight was recorded daily. At 4 days post-injection (dpi), mice were sacrificed and brain tissues were collected and weighed. The tissues were homogenized in PBS, and the supernatant was collected to obtain a viral suspension.

Preparation of the polyclonal antibody

Purified RBD from S protein was used as an antigen to immunize New Zealand white rabbits via subcutaneous multi-site injections. The primary immunization was performed using complete Freund’s adjuvant (FCA) emulsified with the antigen, followed by four booster immunizations every 2 weeks using incomplete Freund’s adjuvant (FIA). Two weeks after the final immunization, blood was collected, and serum was obtained by centrifugation. IgG antibodies were purified using protein A affinity chromatography. Antibody titers were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA), and specificity was verified by western blotting.

Indirect immunofluorescence detection

An indirect immunofluorescence assay was employed to detect the distribution and localization of Murine-CoV-LH5 in tissues of infected mice and cells. Specific antibodies were used to bind virus antigens, and fluorescence signals from infected cells were visualized using fluorescent dyes.

Aerosol exposure and transmission assay

To assess the transmission potential of LH5, an aerosol exposure and cohousing experiment was performed using 3–6-week-old A129 mice. Virus-containing brain homogenates (4.13 × 106 copies/mL) were prepared and nebulized (1–5 mL) using an aerosolization system. Mice were exposed to the aerosol for 10–20 min in a sealed chamber. After exposure, two infected mice were co-housed with two naïve mice to evaluate mouse-to-mouse transmission. Animals were monitored daily for clinical signs. At 4 days post-infection, tissues (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, intestine, and brain) were collected for qPCR and histopathological analyses.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Samples of viral suspension were dropped onto a Cu grid for 5 min, dried, stained with 2% uranyl acetate for 2 min, and blotted. Samples were observed using a JEM-1400 Plus transmission electron microscope at 120 kV (Institutional Center for Shared Technologies and Facilities of Kunming Institute of Zoology (KIZ), Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS), Kunming 650,223, Yunnan, China).

Molecular docking

The sequence of the S protein from mouse CoV LH5 was used. The receptor structure was downloaded from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The receptor protein sequence was processed to remove signal peptides using SignalP (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/SignalP-6.0/). The 3D structure of the protein was then predicted using SWISS-MODEL (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/). For the predicted protein structure, AutoDock was used to remove unnecessary water molecules, ligands, cofactors, and ions, followed by hydrogenation. The final 3D structure was saved in PDBQT format for subsequent molecular docking. The small molecule ligand was preprocessed by converting the downloaded 2D structure SDF file to pdb format using Open Babel, and the pybel module was used to convert the 2D structure to 3D. The ligand was then hydrogenated and set up as a ligand using AutoDock. Molecular docking and visualization were performed using AutoDock Vina for blind docking, where the prepared 3D structures of the ligand and receptor, along with all docking parameter configurations, were input. Finally, the results with the lowest binding energies were visualized using PyMOL.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad software. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). Groups were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.
