New-onset diabetes after distal pancreatectomy–findings from a national cohort study
Authors: Henrik Bergenfeldt, Katarina Fagher, Caroline Williamsson, Bodil Andersson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgrounds/AimsPancreatic resection is associated with a high frequency of postoperative new-onset diabetes mellitus (NODM). The present study aimed to quantify the rate and investigate potential risk factors for NODM after distal pancreatectomy.MethodsData on patients undergoing distal pancreatectomy between 2010 and 2020 were obtained from the Swedish National Pancreatic and Periampullary Cancer Registry and cross-referenced with the Swedish National Diabetes Register. Uni- and multivariable competing risk regression analyses were conducted to model sub-distribution hazards (SHR) of NODM between groups.ResultsA total of 1,034 patients were included, with 290 (28%) developing NODM postoperatively. The risk of NODM was reduced for female patients (SHR 0.73, p = 0.014) and those in the minimally invasive surgery group (SHR 0.54, p = 0.002). Conversely, the risk was increased for obese patients (SHR 2.23, p < 0.001), older patients (SHR 1.01, p = 0.006), patients with American Society of Anesthesiologists classifications 2 and 3 (SHR 2.03, p < 0.001 and SHR 1.61, p = 0.037, respectively), and those with benign pancreatic tumors (SHR 1.43, p = 0.020).ConclusionsNODM was common among patients after distal pancreatectomy, with several patient and surgical factors influencing risk. A minimally-invasive approach was associated with a lower risk of NODM after surgery. This association warrants further study, but should be interpreted cautiously due to potential selection bias and residual confounding, but it warrants further study.Keywords: Distal pancreatectomy, Pancreatic resection, Diabetes mellitus, Risk factors, Outcome

INTRODUCTION

Pancreatic resection removes a significant portion of the endocrine pancreas and is associated with a high frequency of postoperative new-onset diabetes mellitus (NODM) [1-5]. Distal pancreatectomy, in particular, is associated with a higher rate of NODM compared to pancreatoduodenectomy [1,6], possibly due to the markedly higher concentration of islets of Langerhan–the cluster of endocrine cells responsible for insulin production in the pancreatic tail [7]. Furthermore, it has been suggested that pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) may itself be diabetogenic, and that resolution of diabetes mellitus might occur in patients with pre-existing diabetes after resection of a PDAC [8-10]. Diabetes mellitus is a common problem for long-term survivors after distal pancreatectomy. However, previous studies on the metabolic effects of distal pancreatectomy are small and heterogeneous [2,3]. Although beta cell loss as a cause of NODM following distal pancreatectomy is well established, limited data exist on the underlying risk factors and whether or not any of them are modifiable. Two previous meta-analyses have found that pancreatic resection for chronic pancreatitis is associated with a higher risk of postoperative diabetes mellitus [2,3]. However, these studies did not account for the significant differences in mortality between patients undergoing surgery for PDAC and those with other indications. Such mortality differences affect the time at risk and, consequently, the observed rate of NODM diagnosis after surgery. Spleen-preserving surgery has been associated with a lower incidence of NODM in some studies, but results are inconsistent [11-13]. Furthermore, there is no clear biological mechanism by which spleen preservation could influence the development of NODM. It has therefore been suggested that it is a surrogate marker for surgical technique.

In summary, the incidence of NODM following distal pancreatectomy remains unclear, as do the risk factors contributing to its development. The aim of the present study was to quantify the rate of NODM and to investigate potential patient- and surgery-related risk factors for NODM after distal pancreatectomy.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data source

The Swedish National Pancreatic and Periampullary Cancer Registry, founded in 2010, includes all patients diagnosed with pancreatic or periampullary cancer, as well as those undergoing surgery for benign or premalignant lesions. The registry gathers data on tumor characteristics, patient comorbidities, operative technical details, short- and long-term follow-up, and oncological treatment. The registry’s coverage rate exceeds 90% [14].

The Swedish National Diabetes Register (NDR), founded in 1996, compiles data from diabetes-related visits at both primary care and specialized endocrinology departments. The registry collects patient information such as year of onset, type of diabetes, treatment, HbA1c (for metabolic control), blood pressure, and diabetic complications. Registration ascertainment in the NDR is nearly 100% for children and adolescents and 85% for adults [15].

Study design

The design and reporting of this study adhere to the STROBE guidelines for observational studies [16].

Data on patients undergoing distal pancreatectomy between January 1st 2010 and December 31st, 2020, were obtained from the pancreatic registry, and was cross-linked with the NDR.

Patients who did not have a registered preoperative visit for diabetes mellitus in the NDR were considered not to have diabetes mellitus preoperatively. For these patients, postoperatively registered visits in the National Diabetes Registry were considered as NODM, and the latency time from pancreatic resection to the first postoperative diabetes visit was calculated. Body mass index (BMI) was categorized as underweight (< 18.5), normal/overweight (18.5–29.9), and obese (≥ 30). Patients with missing data on histopathological diagnosis in the pancreatic registry were excluded, as were those who were registered as having diabetes mellitus in the NDR preoperatively.

Statistical analysis

Parametric data are presented as mean (standard deviation), non-parametric continuous data as median (interquartile range [IQR]), and categorical data as frequency (relative frequency). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess normality. Student’s t-test was used for hypothesis testing of parametric data, Wilcoxon for non-parametric data, and the Chi-square test for categorical variables. Statistical analysis was conducted with Stata/MP 17.0 (StataCorp LLC). A Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard model (i.e., a competing risk regression) was used to analyze time to NODM, with death as the competing risk [17]. Results from the competing risk regression are reported as subdistribution hazard ratios (SHR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The multivariable competing risk regression model was constructed by the purposeful selection of clinically relevant variables [18]. The model included eight covariates (minimally-invasive surgery, spleen-preserving surgery, preoperative BMI, histopathological diagnosis, patient sex, age at operation and year of surgery categorized as 2010–2013, 2014–2016 and 2017–2020 and American Society of Anesthesiologists [ASA] classification). As data was > 95% complete, missing data was handled by casewise deletion [19,20]. A sensitivity analysis was conducted using a standard Cox regression model (i.e., the cause-specific hazard model). A second sensitivity analysis was also conducted, this time including tumor size as a covariate. Cumulative incidences for death and NODM were estimated as described by Coviello and Boggess [21] and plotted as stacked cumulative incidences.

The Swedish authority for Ethics in Clinical Research approved the study protocol (Dnr 2015/846 and 2018/995).

RESULTS

The final study population included 1,034 patients after exclusion of patients with missing data on histopathological diagnosis (n = 77) and those registered as having diabetes mellitus in the NDR preoperatively (n = 263). The mean age of patients undergoing distal pancreatectomy was 62 (± 15) years, and 593 (57%) were female. Baseline characteristics for patients with and without NODM are shown in Table 1. Median postoperative follow-up was 20 (10–40) and 45 (24–73) months for patients who died and survived, respectively, and 226 (22%) of patients died during the study period. In total, 290 (28%) patients developed diabetes postoperatively. The median time to the first diabetes-related visit in patients with NODM was 354 days (IQR 140–784). As shown in Fig. 1, the unadjusted rate of NODM was higher in obese patients (44%) compared to those who were normal- or overweight (25%, p < 0.001) and was also higher in males than in females (32% vs. 25%, p = 0.015). Further, the incidence of NODM was lower among patients who underwent minimally-invasive surgery compared to those treated with open surgery (15% vs. 32%, p < 0.001), and in spleen-preserving surgery compared to non-spleen-preserving surgery (14% vs. 29%, p = 0.001). There was no difference in the percentage of patients with NODM between those undergoing surgery for PDAC and those with chronic pancreatitis (p = 0.324).

In univariable competing risk regression analyses accounting for death as a competing event, obesity (SHR 2.04, 95% CI 1.58–2.63, p < 0.001) and increasing age (SHR 1.02, 95% CI 1.01–1.02, p < 0.001) were associated with a higher rate of diagnosis of NODM (Table 2). ASA classifications 2 and 3 were also associated with increased rates of NODM. Conversely, female sex, minimally-invasive surgery, spleen-preserving surgery, and surgery for malignant tumor of the endocrine pancreas as compared to PDAC were associated with a decreased rate of NODM. As shown in Fig. 2, there was no difference in the cumulative incidence of NODM between patients undergoing surgery for chronic pancreatitis and those undergoing surgery for PDAC (p = 0.324). The multivariable competing risk regression included 991 complete cases (96%). The association between NODM and obesity (compared to normal-/overweight), age at operation, female sex, minimally invasive to open surgery, and ASA classification 2 or 3 (Table 2) remained significant. However, in the multivariable analysis, there was no difference in the rate of NODM diagnosis for spleen-preserving surgery or surgery for malignant tumor of the endocrine pancreas. Similarly, there was no difference in the rate of NODM by time period of surgery or for surgery for chronic pancreatitis. However, after accounting for other risk factors in multivariable analysis, surgery for a benign pancreatic tumor was associated with an increased rate of NODM. The Cox regression (i.e., the cause-specific hazard model) sensitivity analysis replicated the findings of the Fine–Gray competing risk regression, apart from the risk of NODM when undergoing surgery for a benign pancreatic tumor (HR 1.04, 95% CI 0.36–1.57, p = 0.776) and for malignant tumor of the endocrine pancreas (HR 0.50, 95% CI 0.31–0.80, p = 0.004).

The sensitivity analysis, which included tumor size as a covariate, replicated the findings of the main multivariable analysis. However, benign pancreatic tumor (SHR 1.35, 95% CI, p = 0.069) and ASA classification 3 (SHR 1.42, 95% CI 0.88-2.30, p = 0.152) were not significantly associated with the risk of NODM.

DISCUSSION

This population-based study evaluated the impact of distal pancreatectomy on the risk of NODM. Almost one-third of patients without preoperative diabetes developed the disease during follow-up. Obesity, increasing age, ASA classification 2 or 3 and a benign pancreatic tumor as underlying diagnosis were associated with a higher risk of NODM in the main analysis, while female sex and minimally-invasive surgery were associated with a lower risk of NODM.

The present study found that 28% of patients without a preoperative diagnosis of diabetes mellitus developed the disease during follow-up after distal pancreatectomy. This incidence is similar to that reported in previous studies [1,3,4]. This study also replicates previous findings of a lower risk of diabetes in females compared to males and a higher risk in patients with a higher BMI [22]. Type 2 diabetes is also more common in males and obese individuals [23]. Obesity is associated with increased insulin resistance, a central component of the pathophysiology of type 2 diabetes [24]. The finding that obesity is associated with an increased risk of NODM suggests that insulin resistance is also important in post-pancreatectomy diabetes. In the earlier phases of type 2 diabetes (i.e., impaired glucose tolerance), adaptive strategies from the beta cells, such as hypertrophy and replication, lead to increased beta-cell mass, maintaining normoglycemia and glucose homeostasis even with increasing insulin resistance. However, as the disease progresses, there is marked beta-cell loss and a reduction in insulin secretion capacity [25]. Both adaptation and apoptosis occurs, at least partly, due to stress in the endoplasmatic reticulum of the beta cell which triggers the “unfolded protein response” [26]. However, there is much uncertainty regarding how the process shifts from adaptive strategies of replication and regeneration to a maladaptive cycle characterized by progressive beta cell dysfunction and loss due to apoptosis, eventually resulting in clinical diabetes mellitus.

Shirakawa et al. [27] found that the risk of postoperative NODM after pancreatic resection correlates with the volume of the remnant pancreas. The finding by Shirakawa et al. [27] suggests that acute beta cell loss resulting from pancreatic resection might push the pancreatic remnant beyond its adaptive capacity, triggering further beta cell apoptosis and loss. In this context, the present study’s finding that laparoscopic surgery was associated with a significantly decreased risk of NODM, even after covariate adjustment, suggests that the surgical stress response could also be involved. The magnitude of surgical insult has been shown to correlate with increased postoperative insulin resistance, and laparoscopic gastrectomy has been shown to confer less insulin resistance compared to open surgery [28,29]. Perhaps the increased demand on the beta cell population from the acute surgical stress response, combined with the drastically decreased beta cell mass, shifts the balance from adaptive to maladaptive beta-cell responses, resulting in clinical diabetes in some cases. Although this finding should be interpreted with caution, the possibility that minimally invasive surgery might prevent some cases of postoperative NODM is intriguing and warrants further study.

In contrast to previous studies there was no increased frequency of postoperative diabetes in patients with chronic pancreatitis as the indication for surgery [2]. The increased frequency of diabetes mellitus in patients with chronic pancreatitis is thought to result from a decreased capacity for a compensatory increase of insulin secretion from the pancreatic remnant, due to preoperative pancreatic beta-cell loss from pancreatitis and fibrosis [30]. However, previous studies have not accounted for the fact that patients have vastly different overall survival after distal pancreatectomy, depending on the underlying pathology. Patients with benign diseases, such as chronic pancreatitis, have improved survival compared to those with PDAC, resulting in a considerably longer time at risk of developing diabetes. Our finding that diabetes is diagnosed a median of 1 year after distal pancreatectomy further emphasizes the importance of accounting for death as a competing risk when analyzing the risk of NODM. The finding that spleen-preserving surgery was not associated with NODM after adjustment for potential confounders in multivariable regression supports the theory that spleen preservation is a surrogate marker rather than a causal factor in the development of diabetes [12]. The decision to preserve the spleen may be influenced by several factors, such as age and underlying diagnosis, which could explain the lack of significance after adjusting for confounders.

The decision to consider patients without a preoperatively registered diagnosis of diabetes mellitus in the NDR as not having diabetes could have impacted the results of this study.

Especially in patients with PDAC, previous studies have demonstrated that a significant proportion of patients undergoing pancreatic resection will have a pathologic oral glucose tolerance test without a pre-existing diabetes diagnosis. It is therefore possible that some patients included in this study had undiagnosed preoperative diabetes, which would lead to overestimation of the incidence of NODM in this study.

However, a strength of this study is that only patients with a clinical diabetes diagnosis are considered to have diabetes. Moreover, if a significant proportion of patients considered to have NODM in this study actually had perioperatively diagnosed diabetes, it would be expected that the first postoperative visit would occur within the first few weeks after surgery, not after a median of one year, as observed. Nonetheless, it is possible that some patients classified as having NODM may have had undiagnosed diabetes before surgery. The number of patients with chronic pancreatitis as the main underlying diagnosis was relatively small, and chronic pancreatitis might be underreported in the registry if it is a secondary pathology (e.g., in patients undergoing surgery for neoplasm). Furthermore, the results of subdistribution hazard regression and the cause-specific model differed for diagnoses of malignant or benign endocrine tumors.

This suggests that the difference in competing risk of mortality compared to patients with PDAC affects the observed incidence and risk of NODM in these patient groups, i.e. “survivorship bias”. It is therefore not possible to draw conclusions regarding these risk factors. As this is a registry study, there was no standardized follow-up of the postoperative patients. Therefore, it is possible that differences in factors such as barriers to seeking medical attention may have biased our results. Lastly, there was a significant difference in the timing of surgery when examining crude rates of NODM. As this was not replicated in competing risk regression, the most plausible reason is shorter follow-up in later time periods and, therefore, less time at risk of developing NODM. However, since minimally-invasive surgery was also increasingly adopted in the later periods of the study it is not possible to exclude time period as a confounder for the correlation between minimally invasive surgery and NODM and these findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

In conclusion, NODM is common among patients undergoing distal pancreatectomy, regardless of the underlying diagnosis. Obesity, increasing age, and an ASA classification of 2 or 3 were associated with a higher risk of NODM, while female sex and minimally invasive surgery were associated with a lower risk of NODM. Because this was a retrospective, registry-based study, the association between minimally invasive surgery and NODM should be interpreted cautiously, as it may reflect selection bias, surgical era, or residual confounding. The inconsistent findings for underlying histopathological diagnosis across sensitivity analyses suggest that differential mortality and competing risks influence observed NODM rates.
