Association Between Left Ventricular Diastolic Dysfunction and Subsequent Detection of Sick Sinus Syndrome and Atrial Fibrillation in Patients With Unexplained Syncope
Authors: Hironobu Sumiyoshi, Hidemori Hayashi, Akira Nakashima, Toshikazu Kono, Maki Ono, Akira Mizukami, Tohru Minamino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAtrial fibrillation (AF) and sick sinus syndrome (SSS) frequently coexist and may share common atrial structural and electrophysiological remodeling substrates. Left ventricular (LV) diastolic dysfunction frequently coexists with AF; however, its association with subsequent detection of SSS and AF remains incompletely understood. This study investigated whether LV diastolic dysfunction is associated with the detection of SSS and AF.MethodsThis study included patients who underwent insertable cardiac monitor implantation for recurrent unexplained syncope. Patients with prior AF, LV ejection fraction < 50%, or without remote monitoring were excluded. LV diastolic dysfunction was assessed using the HFA‐PEFF score. The primary endpoints were SSS requiring pacemaker implantation and AF during follow‐up.ResultsAmong 164 patients (median age 71 years; 34.1% women; median follow‐up 35 months), SSS was detected in 31 patients (18.9%), and AF episodes lasting ≥ 6 min were detected in 48 patients (29.3%). Patients with high HFA‐PEFF scores (5, 6) had a significantly higher risk of detection of SSS and AF. The HFA‐PEFF score remained independently associated with SSS (hazard ratio [HR], 1.51; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.07–2.13; p = 0.021) and AF lasting ≥ 6 min (HR, 1.94; 95% CI, 1.40–2.67; p < 0.001) after multivariable adjustment.ConclusionLV diastolic dysfunction, as quantified by the HFA‐PEFF score, is independently associated with the detection of SSS and AF, suggesting a potential association between LV diastolic dysfunction and arrhythmogenic atrial remodeling substrates.Keywords: atrial fibrillation, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, insertable cardiac monitor, sick sinus syndrome

Introduction

Sick sinus syndrome (SSS) and atrial fibrillation (AF) frequently coexist, and these conditions are not merely disorders of the sinus node and pulmonary veins but are increasingly recognized as conditions associated with atrial structural and electrophysiological remodeling [1, 2, 3].

Notably, many patients with AF have a preserved left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction (LVEF) and concomitant diastolic dysfunction. Epidemiological data suggest that approximately 10%–20% of patients with AF develop heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), whereas 15%–40% of patients with HFpEF have concomitant AF. These two conditions are therefore often referred to as “vicious twins.” [4] Moreover, previous studies have demonstrated that SSS is closely associated with HFpEF, primarily through exercise intolerance and chronotropic incompetence [5]. Diastolic dysfunction represents a potentially important link between common risk factors, such as hypertension, age, and obesity, and the detection of AF and SSS [6].

Recently, the HFA‐PEFF score was developed to assist in the diagnosis of HFpEF and has been validated against invasive gold‐standard diagnostic criteria. The HFA‐PEFF score, proposed by the Heart Failure Association of the European Society of Cardiology, comprises three domains incorporating echocardiographic and laboratory data [7]. Beyond its diagnostic utility, the score has demonstrated prognostic relevance in various clinical settings [8, 9]. A previous study demonstrated that the HFA‐PEFF score was associated with AF burden in patients with SSS after pacemaker implantation [10]. However, because that population already had established SSS and included patients with a history of AF, it remained unclear whether left ventricular diastolic dysfunction predicts the detection of AF and SSS.

Insertable cardiac monitors (ICMs) allow continuous long‐term rhythm surveillance and enable precise detection of both bradyarrhythmias and atrial tachyarrhythmias in patients with unexplained syncope [11]. Because atrial arrhythmias may remain clinically silent at baseline in this population, ICM monitoring provides a unique opportunity to evaluate the association between left ventricular diastolic dysfunction and subsequent detection of atrial arrhythmias and sinus node dysfunction.

Therefore, in this study, we sought to determine whether LV diastolic dysfunction, assessed by the HFA‐PEFF score, is associated with the detection of SSS and AF in patients undergoing ICM implantation for recurrent unexplained syncope and without prior AF.

Methods

Study Population

This single center observational study included patients who underwent ICM implantation (Reveal LINQ, Medtronic Inc., Minneapolis, USA) for unexplained syncope between January 2017 and December 2022. All patients underwent 12‐lead electrocardiography and 24‐h Holter monitoring before ICM implantation, and no arrhythmias responsible for syncope or AF were identified prior to implantation. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the local ethics committee. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Exclusion criteria were prior AF, prior cardiac surgery, LVEF < 50%, or without remote monitoring. Blood tests and transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) were performed 2 weeks prior to ICM implantation by certified sonographers, and measurements were analyzed by experienced echocardiographers and cardiologists according to current guidelines [12].

HFA‐PEFF Scores

The HFA‐PEFF score, described in detail in prior research, is part of the HFA‐PEFF diagnostic algorithm for suspected HFpEF [7]. Briefly, this multistep tool improves the accuracy and efficiency of HFpEF diagnosis. Step 1 identifies patients with HFpEF and excludes other specific causes of heart failure–like symptoms. Step 2 involves calculating the HFA‐PEFF score, which comprises three domains: (1) functional, (2) morphological, and (3) biomarker. Each domain is assigned major and minor criteria, contributing up to 2 points per domain, for a maximum score of 6. A score ≥ 5 is diagnostic of HFpEF, whereas a score < 2 makes HFpEF unlikely and warrants evaluation for alternative causes. Intermediate scores (2–4) require further testing, such as exercise echocardiography or cardiac catheterization. Because global longitudinal strain (GLS) was not measured, the score was calculated without the GLS parameter.

Follow‐Up and Arrhythmia Detection Process

Patients were followed for up to 36 months after ICM implantation. Clinical data—including age, sex, comorbidities, echocardiographic parameters, and medication use—were extracted from the medical records.

Arrhythmias were assessed weekly via remote monitoring, with in‐clinic device interrogation every 6 months. All device‐detected arrhythmia episodes were reviewed and adjudicated by at least two experienced electrophysiologists based on stored subcutaneous electrograms and clinical information.

Detection of Bradyarrhythmias

Bradyarrhythmias were detected by the ICM and confirmed by review of stored electrograms. Patients were clinically evaluated for symptoms at the time of documented bradyarrhythmias. Permanent pacemaker implantation was performed when clinically significant bradycardia was identified, defined as symptomatic bradycardia with a heart rate < 40 beats per minute and/or ventricular pauses ≥ 3 s. In patients in whom the ICM was removed after permanent pacemaker implantation, subsequent rhythm surveillance was continued using the implanted pacemaker with remote monitoring and in‐office device interrogation every 6 months.

Detection of Atrial Tachyarrhythmias

Atrial tachyarrhythmias (ATs) detected by the ICM were independently reviewed by the electrophysiologists using stored electrograms. Episodes were classified as AF or other ATs based on standard electrogram criteria.

AF was detected using the ICM's automated AF detection algorithm and confirmed by review of stored electrograms. AF occurrence was evaluated using three prespecified duration thresholds (≥ 2 min, ≥ 6 min, and ≥ 60 min), which were selected based on prior ICM‐based studies of subclinical AF and atrial high‐rate episodes [13, 14].

When ATs were considered temporally related to syncope or other symptoms, catheter ablation was performed. In patients in whom AF was not clearly associated with symptoms, catheter ablation was performed at the discretion of the treating physician and the patient.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are reported as median (interquartile range, IQR). Between‐group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test. Event‐free survival was analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were constructed to evaluate predictors of SSS and AF. Variables with p < 0.1 in univariate analyses were included in the multivariable models, and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. To avoid multicollinearity, variables included in the HFA‐PEFF score were not entered into the multivariate Cox regression models. We also performed a sensitivity analysis using the H2FPEF score and compared its discriminative performance with that of the HFA‐PEFF score using receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) analyses. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 31.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

We screened 226 potentially eligible patients with unexplained syncope who underwent ICM implantation. Of these, 62 patients were excluded: 32 with prior AF, 5 with a history of cardiac surgery, 15 with LVEF < 50%, 4 without remote monitoring, and 6 due to insufficient clinical or echocardiographic data. Ultimately, 164 patients were included in the final analysis.

Patients with a high HFA‐PEFF score were older and had higher CHA2DS2‐VA scores. In addition, patients with high HFA‐PEFF scores had higher B‐type natriuretic peptide (BNP) levels and lower estimated glomerular filtration rates (eGFR). Associations With Bradyarrhythmia

Over a median follow‐up of 35 months (IQR, 32–36), 31 patients underwent pacemaker implantation for SSS. Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated that patients with an HFA‐PEFF score ≥ 5 had a significantly higher risk of detection of SSS during follow‐up, with a 3‐year cumulative incidence of 38.0% (Figure 2A). In univariate analysis, the HFA‐PEFF score was significantly associated with the risk of pacemaker implantation for SSS (hazard ratio [HR], 1.81; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.35–2.42; p < 0.001). After adjustment for other relevant laboratory, clinical, and echocardiographic variables, the HFA‐PEFF score remained independently associated with SSS (HR, 1.51; 95% CI, 1.07–2.13; p = 0.021), whereas these covariates were not independently associated with SSS, except for age (HR, 1.04; 95% CI, 1.00–1.08; p = 0.049) (Table 2). The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) for predicting SSS was 0.75 (95% CI, 0.67–0.83; p < 0.001) for the HFA‐PEFF score and 0.69 (95% CI, 0.60–0.78; p < 0.001) for H2FPEF score [15]. The optimal cutoff value of the HFA‐PEFF score for predicting SSS was 5, as determined by the maximum Youden index, which corresponds to the threshold defining a high probability of HFpEF (Figure 3A).

Four patients underwent pacemaker implantation for atrioventricular (AV) block. Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated no significant association between the HFA‐PEFF score and the risk of pacemaker implantation for AV block (p = 0.388) (Figure 2B).

Associations With Atrial Tachyarrhythmia

Within 3 years, AF episodes lasting ≥ 2 min, ≥ 6 min, and ≥ 60 min were observed in 49, 48, and 39 patients, respectively. Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated that patients with an HFA‐PEFF score ≥ 5 had a significantly higher risk of AF episodes lasting ≥ 6 min during follow‐up, with a 3‐year cumulative incidence of 57.5%. Similar results were observed for AF episodes lasting ≥ 2 min and ≥ 60 min (Figure 4).

Catheter ablation for AF was performed in 13 patients during the follow‐up period, with a higher proportion in the high HFA‐PEFF score group (scores 5–6). No significant differences were observed between the HFA‐PEFF score groups with respect to energy source, additional ablation beyond pulmonary vein isolation, AF recurrence rate, or post‐procedural antiarrhythmic drugs (Table S1).

In univariate analysis, the HFA‐PEFF score was significantly associated with the risk of AF episodes lasting ≥ 6 min (HR, 2.32; 95% CI, 1.76–3.08; p < 0.001). After adjustment for other relevant laboratory, clinical, and echocardiographic variables, the HFA‐PEFF score remained independently associated with AF episodes lasting ≥ 6 min (HR, 1.94; 95% CI, 1.40–2.67; p < 0.001) (Table 3). The AUC for predicting AF episodes lasting ≥ 6 min was 0.85 (95% CI, 0.78–0.91; p < 0.001) for the HFA‐PEFF score and 0.66 (95% CI, 0.57–0.75; p < 0.001) for H2FPEF score. The optimal cutoff value of the HFA‐PEFF score for predicting AF episodes lasting ≥ 6 min was 5, as determined by the maximum Youden index, which corresponds to the threshold defining a high probability of HFpEF (Figure 3B).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to report that LV diastolic dysfunction, quantified by the HFA‐PEFF score, was independently associated with the detection of SSS and AF in patients undergoing ICM implantation for unexplained syncope. Importantly, this association persisted after adjustment for relevant clinical, laboratory, and echocardiographic variables and was observed not only for each arrhythmic phenotype individually but also for their coexistence.

SSS frequently coexists with AF, and both conditions may share common atrial structural and electrophysiological remodeling substrates [2]. Previous studies have reported a high prevalence of HFpEF among patients with AF [4]. However, the diagnosis of HFpEF in patients with AF or SSS remains challenging because symptoms, laboratory findings, and echocardiographic parameters often overlap. In patients with HFpEF, symptoms such as exertional dyspnea are clinically important; however, when patients present with more severe manifestations such as syncope, heart failure–related symptoms may be masked or difficult to distinguish from syncope‐related complaints.

Moreover, many previous studies relied on intermittent 12‐lead electrocardiograms or Holter monitoring to detect AF, which may have led to underdetection of asymptomatic or subclinical AF. In the present study, the combined use of the HFA‐PEFF score and long‐term continuous rhythm monitoring with an ICM enabled objective assessment of LV diastolic dysfunction and precise detection of subclinical AF. These complementary tools may therefore be particularly useful for identifying occult heart failure and underlying atrial remodeling‐related substrates in patients whose clinical presentation is dominated by syncope.

In the present study, age and the severity of heart failure were associated with the detection of SSS and AF. Importantly, the HFA‐PEFF score remained independently associated with the study endpoints even after multivariable adjustment that included these established risk factors (Tables 2 and 3). These variables have been consistently reported in previous studies to be associated with LV diastolic dysfunction, SSS, and AF [16, 17, 18]. Therefore, the HFA‐PEFF score may serve not only as an index of LV diastolic dysfunction but also as a surrogate marker for the detection of SSS and AF. ROC curve analyses demonstrated that the HFA‐PEFF score showed better discriminative performance for AF detection than the H2FPEF score in the present cohort. One possible explanation is that the H2FPEF score incorporates the presence of AF as a scoring component and was originally developed for HFpEF diagnosis rather than prediction of future AF detection in patients without prior AF. In contrast, the HFA‐PEFF score more directly reflects structural and functional cardiac abnormalities, including left atrial remodeling and LV diastolic dysfunction, through echocardiographic and biomarker‐based assessments. Furthermore, the relatively low BMI and elderly Japanese population in this study may also have influenced the differential performance of these scoring systems.

In contrast to SSS and AF, the detection of AV block and ATs other than AF was not significantly associated with the HFA‐PEFF score in the present study. AV block generally reflects abnormalities of the atrioventricular conduction system and is more often related to degenerative changes of the His–Purkinje system rather than atrial structural remodeling [19]. Given that the HFA‐PEFF score primarily reflects LV diastolic function and left atrial pressure overload, the absence of an association with AV block appears pathophysiologically plausible. In addition, with respect to AV block, unlike SSS, permanent pacemaker implantation is frequently performed based on findings from a standard 12‐lead electrocardiogram—such as complete AV block or advanced conduction disease (e.g., trifascicular block)—even when a clear temporal association between syncope and bradycardia is not established [19]. This selection process may partly explain the small number of AV block cases and the lack of a significant association with the HFA‐PEFF score.

Similarly, ATs other than AF often occur as isolated electrophysiological entities rather than as manifestations of atrial cardiomyopathy [20]. Therefore, the lack of an observed association between the HFA‐PEFF score and ATs other than AF in the present study may be explained, at least in part, by differences in the underlying arrhythmogenic mechanisms.

In this study, 41.5% of the patients with unexplained syncope were classified as having a high probability of HFpEF based on the HFA‐PEFF score. More than 20% of these patients experienced both SSS and AF over 3 years. These results demonstrate a strong association between SSS, AF, and HFpEF in this population. These patients may have a higher likelihood of requiring permanent pacemaker implantation, catheter ablation, initiation of guideline‐directed medical therapy for heart failure, and anticoagulation. Accordingly, careful longitudinal follow‐up incorporating remote rhythm monitoring should be considered in this population.

Study Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, this study was subject to potential selection bias related to patient enrollment. This was a single‐center observational study with a relatively limited sample size. Moreover, HFpEF is a highly heterogeneous syndrome with multiple underlying phenotypes and mechanisms. The present study specifically evaluated patients with unexplained syncope undergoing ICM implantation, who may represent a distinct subgroup potentially enriched for atrial cardiomyopathy‐related pathophysiology rather than the general HFpEF population.

Second, GLS data—considered a minor criterion in the functional domain of the HFA‐PEFF score—were unavailable in this study. Moreover, we did not evaluate left atrial strain or right atrial and right ventricular function, which have been reported to be associated with AF. Therefore, these findings may not be generalizable to all patients with unexplained syncope. Third, the final two steps of the HFA‐PEFF diagnostic algorithm—Step 3 (advanced functional testing with exercise stress) and Step 4 (etiological workup)—were not performed in the present study. Fourth, AF burden was reduced in some patients who underwent catheter ablation for AF. However, patients with higher HFA‐PEFF scores tended to have a greater AF burden and were more likely to undergo catheter ablation. Therefore, the true difference in AF burden according to the HFA‐PEFF score may have been underestimated in the present study.

Conclusions

In patients undergoing ICM implantation for unexplained syncope, LV diastolic dysfunction assessed by the HFA‐PEFF score was independently associated with the detection of SSS and AF, as well as their coexistence. These findings suggest that LV diastolic dysfunction may be associated with atrial remodeling‐related arrhythmogenic substrates in patients with unexplained syncope.
