Integrals involving arbitrary powers of the arcsine, with applications to infinite series Thanks: The first author was supported in part by the Natural Sciences and Engineering Research Council of Canada.
Karl Dilcher, Christophe Vignat

Abstract
. Using appropriate power series evaluations, we determine all moments of arbitrary positive powers of the arcsine. As consequences we evaluate several doubly infinite classes of power series involving central binomial coefficients and generalized multiple harmonic sums. By specializing the variable involved, we then evaluate classes of numerical sequences, mostly in terms of powers of π \pi . Finally, we obtain limit expressions for arbitrary powers of π \pi .



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary: 33E20; Secondary: 05A10, 33B10.

1.
Introduction

The arcsine function is of particular interest in the study of series for
\pi

and expressions involving
\pi
. For instance, the well-known power series

(1.1)

\arcsin{x}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\binom{2k}{k}}{4^{k}}\frac{x^{2k+1}}{2k+1},\qquad(\arcsin{x})^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{4^{k}}{\binom{2k}{k}k}\frac{x^{2k}}{2k}

lead to series representations for
\pi
and
\pi^{2}
by setting, for instance,

x=\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{2},\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{3}
, 1, or other related
algebraic numbers. These and many other results can be found, for instance,
in the interesting paper
[
9
]
by D. H. Lehmer.

Partly inspired by Lehmer’s use of integration as a method for obtaining series
identities, we made a systematic study in
[
3
]
of what amounts to
arbitrary numbers of repeated integrations of
\arcsin{x}
and a few of its
powers. For instance, we showed that for all even
n\geq 0
we have

(1.2)

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\binom{2k}{k}}{4^{k}}\frac{x^{2k+1}}{2k+n+1}=\frac{\binom{n}{n/2}}{(2x)^{n}}\left(\arcsin{x}-\frac{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{\frac{n-2}{2}}\frac{(2x)^{2j+1}}{(2j+1)\binom{2j}{j}}\right),

with similar results involving squares, cubes, and fourth powers of

\arcsin{x}
, also for odd
n\geq 1
. It is clear that the first identity
of (
1.1
) is the case
n=0
of (
1.2
). Furthermore, with
x=1
we get

(1.3)

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\binom{2k}{k}}{4^{k}(2k+n+1)}=\binom{n}{n/2}\frac{\pi}{2^{n+1}}\quad(n\;\hbox{even});

see
[
3
]
for further details. Although this is not relevant here, we
showed in
[
4
]
that (
1.3
) can be extended to all complex
parameters
n
.

One of the main ingredients for all these results was the following lemma
(Corollary 2.2 in
[
3
]
) which will also play an important role in the
current paper.

Lemma 1.1
.

Suppose that
f(x)
is either an even or an odd function, analytic in a
neighbourhood of
0
, with power series expansion

(1.4)

f(x)=\sum_{k=1-\delta}^{\infty}c_{k}\cdot\frac{x^{2k+\delta}}{2k+\delta}\qquad(|x|<R),

where
\delta
is either
0
or
1
, and
R
is the radius of convergence.
Then, for any
n\geq 0
and real
x
with
|x|<R
, we have

(1.5)

\sum_{k=1-\delta}^{\infty}c_{k}\cdot\frac{x^{2k+\delta+n+1}}{2k+\delta+n+1}=f(x)x^{n+1}-(n+1)\int_{0}^{x}t^{n}f(t)dt.

The key to the results in
[
3
]
was the evaluation of the integral on the
right of (
1.5
), with
f(t)=(\arcsin{t})^{q}
for
q=1,\ldots,4
.
The main purpose of the current paper is to extend this to all positive
integers
q\geq 1
by using a different method.

For integers
n\geq 0
and
q\geq 1
, we denote

(1.6)

I_{q}^{(n)}(x):=\int_{0}^{x}t^{n}(\arcsin{t})^{q}\,dt.

It appears that apart from the results in
[
3
]
mentioned above, only the
case
q=1
had previously been evaluated for all

n\geq 0
, separately for even and odd
n
, as identities 1.7.3.2 and
1.7.3.3 in
[
13
]
. In the current paper we will also need to treat the cases
of even and odd
n
separately, as well as distinguish between even and odd

q
. The corresponding four identities are stated in Section
2
.

The main reason for wishing to obtain evaluations for the integrals in
(
1.6
) for arbitrary integers
q
is the existence of the following two
classes of series expansions for
(\arcsin{x})^{p}
. They can be found in

[
1
]
or
[
15
, p. 124]
. Using a slight variant of the notation and
normalization from
[
1
]
, we have for integers
p\geq 0
,

(1.7)

(\arcsin{x})^{2p+1}=(2p+1)!\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\binom{2k}{k}G_{p}(k)}{4^{k}}\cdot\frac{x^{2k+1}}{2k+1},

where
G_{0}(k)=1
and for
p\geq 1
,

(1.8)

G_{p}(k)=\sum_{n_{1}=0}^{k-1}\frac{1}{(2n_{1}+1)^{2}}\sum_{n_{2}=0}^{n_{1}-1}\frac{1}{(2n_{2}+1)^{2}}\cdots\sum_{n_{p}=0}^{n_{p-1}-1}\frac{1}{(2n_{p}+1)^{2}}.

Similarly, for integers
p\geq 1
,

(1.9)

(\arcsin{x})^{2p}=(2p)!\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{4^{k}H_{p}(k)}{\binom{2k}{k}2k}\cdot\frac{x^{2k}}{2k},

where
H_{1}(k)=1
and

(1.10)

H_{p+1}(k)=\sum_{n_{1}=1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{(2n_{1})^{2}}\sum_{n_{2}=1}^{n_{1}-1}\frac{1}{(2n_{2})^{2}}\cdots\sum_{n_{p}=1}^{n_{p-1}-1}\frac{1}{(2n_{p})^{2}}.

For some further remarks on the sums
G_{p}(k)
and
H_{p+1}(k)
, see
Section
5
.3 below.
It is clear that with
p=0
in (
1.7
) and
p=1
in (
1.9
) we get the
two identities in (
1.1
). For the next smallest cases, namely
G_{1}(k)

and
H_{2}(k)
, see (
4.5
) below.

In Section
2
we will state and prove the main result of this paper,
namely the evaluation of the integral in (
1.6
) for all integer parameters

n\geq 0
and
q\geq 1
. We then use this together with Lemma
1.1
and
the expansions (
1.7
), (
1.9
) in Section
3
to obtain
various special cases, along with some specific examples. In Section
4

we use our main result to obtain certain limit expressions for all positive
integer powers of
\pi
. We finish with some remarks and further consequences
in Section
5
.

2.
The main results

To state our main results, we need to recall some mathematical objects and
introduce some notation. The Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind,
T_{n}(x)
,
and of the second kind,
U_{n}(x)
, are usually defined by

(2.1)

T_{n}(\cos{\theta})=\cos(n\theta)\quad\hbox{and}\quad U_{n}(\cos{\theta})=\frac{\sin((n+1))}{\sin{\theta}}

(see, e.g.,
[
6
, p. 993ff.]
or
[
14
]
). The first few Chebyshev
polynomials are
T_{0}(x)=1
,
T_{1}(x)=x
,
T_{2}(x)=2x^{2}-1
,
T_{3}(x)=4x^{3}-3x
, and

U_{0}(x)=1
,
U_{1}(x)=2x
,
U_{2}(x)=4x^{2}-1
,
U_{3}(x)=8x^{3}-4x
.

For greater ease of notation in what follows, we set

a=a(x):=\arcsin{x},

and we denote the partial sums of the Maclaurin series for cosine and sine by

(2.2)

c_{n}(z):=\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{j}\frac{z^{2j}}{(2j)!},\qquad s_{n}(z):=\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{j}\frac{z^{2j+1}}{(2j+1)!}.

We are now ready to state our main result, which evaluates the integral in
(
1.6
).

Theorem 2.1
.

For all integers
\ell\geq 0
and
p\geq 0
we have, with
a=\arcsin{x}
,

(2.3)

\displaystyle I_{2p+1}^{(2\ell)}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{2\ell+1}}{2\ell+1}a^{2p+1}+\frac{(-1)^{p}(2p+1)!}{4^{\ell}(2\ell+1)}\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}\binom{2\ell+1}{\ell-k}\frac{1}{(2k+1)^{2p+1}}

\displaystyle\times\left(s_{p-1}((2k+1)a)T_{2k+1}(x)+c_{p}((2k+1)a)U_{2k}(x)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}-(-1)^{k}\right),

(2.4)

\displaystyle I_{2p+1}^{(2\ell-1)}(x)

\displaystyle=\left(x^{2\ell}-\frac{\binom{2\ell}{\ell}}{4^{\ell}}\right)\frac{a^{2p+1}}{2\ell}+\frac{(-1)^{p}(2p+1)!}{2^{2\ell-1}2\ell}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\binom{2\ell}{\ell-k}\frac{1}{(2k)^{2p+1}}

\displaystyle\times\left(s_{p-1}(2ka)T_{2k}(x)+c_{p}(2ka)U_{2k-1}(x)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right),

and for
p\geq 1
,

(2.5)

\displaystyle I_{2p}^{(2\ell)}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{2\ell+1}}{2\ell+1}a^{2p}+\frac{(-1)^{p}(2p)!}{4^{\ell}(2\ell+1)}\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}\binom{2\ell+1}{\ell-k}\frac{1}{(2k+1)^{2p}}

\displaystyle\times\left(c_{p-1}((2k+1)a)T_{2k+1}(x)-s_{p-1}((2k+1)a)U_{2k}(x)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right),

(2.6)

\displaystyle I_{2p}^{(2\ell-1)}(x)

\displaystyle=\left(x^{2\ell}-\frac{\binom{2\ell}{\ell}}{4^{\ell}}\right)\frac{a^{2p}}{2\ell}+\frac{(-1)^{p}(2p)!}{2^{2\ell-1}2\ell}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\binom{2\ell}{\ell-k}\frac{1}{(2k)^{2p}}

\displaystyle\times\left(c_{p-1}(2ka)T_{2k}(x)-s_{p-1}(2ka)U_{2k-1}(x)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}-(-1)^{k}\right).

The main idea of the proof of this theorem lies in considering the
exponential generating function of the
sequence of integrals
(I_{q}^{(n)}(x))_{q\geq 0}
from (
1.6
), where

n\geq 0
is fixed. With this in mind, we begin with the following lemma.

Lemma 2.2
.

For any integer
n\geq 0
, we define the exponential generating function

(2.7)

I^{(n)}(x,w):=\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}I_{q}^{(n)}(x)\frac{w^{q}}{q!}.

Then, with
a(x)=\arcsin{x}
, we have

(2.8)

I^{(n)}(x,w)=\frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}e^{wa(x)}-\frac{w}{n+1}\int_{0}^{a(x)}(\sin{\theta})^{n+1}e^{w\theta}d\theta.

Proof.

Since
0\leq\arcsin{t}\leq\pi/2
for
0\leq t\leq 1
, we have by (
1.6
),

0\leq I_{q}^{(n)}(x)\leq\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)^{q}\int_{0}^{1}t^{n}dt=\frac{1}{n+1}\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)^{q},

and therefore the power series in (
2.7
) has radius of convergence of at
least
2/\pi
. We now substitute (
1.6
) into (
2.7
), obtaining

(2.9)

\displaystyle I^{(n)}(x,w)

\displaystyle=\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{0}^{x}t^{n}a(t)^{q}\,dt\right)\frac{w^{q}}{q!}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x}t^{n}\left(\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}a(t)^{q}\frac{w^{q}}{q!}\right)dt=\int_{0}^{x}t^{n}e^{wa(t)}dt,

where the interchange of integration and infinite series is justified since we
are dealing with convergent power series.

With the change of variables
t=\sin\theta
, we get from (
2.9
),

I^{(n)}(x,w)=\int_{0}^{a(x)}\left(\sin^{n}\theta\cdot\cos\theta\right)e^{w\theta}d\theta.

Noting that

\frac{d}{d\theta}\sin^{n+1}\theta=(n+1)\sin^{n}\theta\cdot\cos\theta
,
we find upon integration by parts that

I^{(n)}(x,w)=\left.\frac{\sin^{n+1}\theta}{n+1}e^{w\theta}\right|_{0}^{a(x)}-\frac{w}{n+1}\int_{0}^{a(x)}\sin^{n+1}\theta\cdot e^{w\theta}d\theta,

and (
2.8
) follows.
∎

The integral in (
2.8
) plays an important role, and we denote it by

(2.10)

J^{(n)}(x,w):=\int_{0}^{a(x)}(\sin{\theta})^{n+1}e^{w\theta}d\theta.

We also use the standard notation

(2.11)

\left[w^{m}\right]f(w)=c_{m}\quad\hbox{where}\quad f(w)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}c_{j}w^{j}.

We can now state and prove the next intermediate result.

Lemma 2.3
.

For all integers
n\geq 0
and
q\geq 1
we have

(2.12)

I_{q}^{(n)}(x)=\frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}a(x)^{q}-\frac{q!}{n+1}\left[w^{q-1}\right]J^{(n)}(x,w).

Proof.

We note that

e^{wa(x)}=\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}a(x)^{q}\frac{w^{q}}{q!},

and that by (
2.11
) we have

\left[w^{q}\right]\left(wJ^{(n)}(x,w)\right)=\left[w^{q-1}\right]J^{(n)}(x,w).

The result now follows from (
2.8
) and (
2.7
) upon multiplying both
sides by
q!
.
∎

The Chebyshev polynomials appear in the proof of Theorem
2.1
in a
natural way through the following identities, which are similar to the
defining identities in (
2.1
).

Lemma 2.4
.

For any real
x
and integers
k\geq 0
, we have

(2.13)

\displaystyle\cos(2k\arcsin{x})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}T_{2k}(x),

(2.14)

\displaystyle\sin((2k+1)\arcsin{x})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}T_{2k+1}(x),

(2.15)

\displaystyle\sin(2k\arcsin{x})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k+1}U_{2k-1}(x)\sqrt{1-x^{2}},

(2.16)

\displaystyle\cos((2k+1)\arcsin{x})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}U_{2k}(x)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}.

Proof.

We set
x=\sin{a}
throughout. To prove (
2.13
), we use some basic
trigonometric identities and the first part of (
2.1
) to obtain

T_{2k}(x)=T_{2k}(\sin{a})=T_{2k}(\cos(a-\tfrac{\pi}{2}))=\cos(2k(a-\tfrac{\pi}{2}))=(-1)^{k}\cos(2ka),

which is equivalent to (
2.13
). Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\sin((2k+1)a)

\displaystyle=\cos((2k+1)a-\tfrac{\pi}{2})=(-1)^{k}\cos((2k+1)(a-\tfrac{\pi}{2}))

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}T_{2k+1}\left(\cos(a-\tfrac{\pi}{2})\right)=(-1)^{k}T_{2k+1}(\sin{a}),

which is equivalent to (
2.14
). Next, we use the facts that

\frac{d}{dx}T_{n}(x)=nU_{n-1}(x),\quad\hbox{and}\quad\frac{d}{dx}\arcsin{x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}

to obtain (
2.15
) and (
2.16
) by differentiating both sides of
(
2.13
) and (
2.14
), respectively.
∎

We can now evaluate the integral
J^{(n)}(x,w)
, which is defined in
(
2.10
). This needs to be done separately for even and odd
n
.

Lemma 2.5
.

For all integers
\ell\geq 1
and with
a=\arcsin{x}
, we have

(2.17)

\displaystyle J^{(2l-1)}(x,w)

\displaystyle=\frac{\binom{2\ell}{\ell}(e^{wa}-1)}{4^{\ell}w}+\frac{e^{wa}}{2^{2\ell-1}}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\binom{2\ell}{\ell-k}\frac{V_{2k}(x,w)}{w^{2}+(2k)^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{2^{2\ell-1}}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}(-1)^{k}\binom{2\ell}{\ell-k}\frac{w}{w^{2}+(2k)^{2}},

(2.18)

\displaystyle J^{(2l)}(x,w)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{wa}}{4^{\ell}}\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}\binom{2\ell+1}{\ell-k}\frac{V_{2k+1}(x,w)}{w^{2}+(2k+1)^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{4^{\ell}}\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}(-1)^{k}\binom{2\ell+1}{\ell-k}\frac{2k+1}{w^{2}+(2k+1)^{2}},

where

(2.19)

V_{n}(x,w):=wT_{n}(x)-nU_{n-1}(x)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}.

Proof.

The indefinite integral corresponding to (
2.10
) can be found in

[
13
]
, separate for even and odd
n
. In particular, the identity
(1.5.49.8) with
a=w
and
b=1
reads

(2.20)

\displaystyle\int\sin^{2\ell}\theta\cdot e^{w\theta}d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{\binom{2\ell}{\ell}e^{w\theta}}{4^{\ell}w}

\displaystyle+\frac{e^{w\theta}}{2^{2\ell-1}}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}(-1)^{k}\binom{2\ell}{\ell+k}\frac{w\cos(2k\theta)+2k\sin(2k\theta)}{w^{2}+(2k)^{2}}.

The definite integral is then clearly (
2.17
) if we note that with
(
2.13
) and (
2.15
), the right-most numerator in (
2.20
) becomes

V_{2k}(x,w)
.

Similarly, rewriting the identity (1.5.49.10) in
[
13
]
, we have

(2.21)

\displaystyle\int\sin^{2\ell+1}\theta\cdot e^{w\theta}d\theta

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{e^{w\theta}}{4^{\ell}}\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}(-1)^{k}\binom{2\ell+1}{\ell+k}\frac{w\sin((2k+1)\theta)-(2k+1)\cos((2k+1)\theta)}{w^{2}+(2k+1)^{2}}.

The definite integral will now be (
2.18
) if we note that with (
2.14
)
and (
2.16
) the numerator on the right of (
2.21
) becomes

V_{2k+1}(x,w)
.
∎

To facilitate evaluating the coefficients of the powers of
w
in (
2.17
)
and (
2.18
), we state and prove another technical lemma.

Lemma 2.6
.

For any positive integer
n
and real
a
, we have

(2.22)

\displaystyle\frac{e^{wa}V_{n}(x,w)}{w^{2}+n^{2}}=\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{p}\left(c_{p}(na)T_{n}(x)-s_{p}(na)U_{n-1}(x)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)\frac{w^{2p+1}}{n^{2p+2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{p}\left(s_{p-1}(na)T_{n}(x)+s_{p}(na)U_{n-1}(x)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)\frac{w^{2p}}{n^{2p+1}},

with
c_{p}(z)
and
s_{p}(z)
as defined in (
2.2
) and
s_{-1}(z)=0
.

Proof.

We first take the Cauchy product of the two power series

e^{wa}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{a^{j}}{j!}w^{j}\quad\hbox{and}\quad\frac{1}{w^{2}+n^{2}}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{n^{2j+2}}w^{2j},

obtaining

\displaystyle\frac{e^{wa}}{w^{2}+n^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{p}\frac{a^{2j}}{(2j)!}\frac{(-1)^{p-j}}{n^{2p+2-2j}}\bigg)w^{2p}+\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{p}\frac{a^{2j+1}}{(2j+1)!}\frac{(-1)^{p-j}}{n^{2p+2-2j}}\bigg)w^{2p+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{p}(-1)^{j}\frac{(na)^{2j}}{(2j)!}\bigg)\frac{(-1)^{p}w^{2p}}{n^{2p+2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{p}(-1)^{j}\frac{(na)^{2j+1}}{(2j+1)!}\bigg)\frac{(-1)^{p}w^{2p+1}}{n^{2p+3}}.

With (
2.2
) we now get

(2.23)

\frac{e^{wa}}{w^{2}+n^{2}}=\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{p}c_{p}(na)\frac{w^{2p}}{n^{2p+2}}+\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{p}s_{p}(na)\frac{w^{2p+1}}{n^{2p+3}}.

Next, the product of (
2.19
) and (
2.23
) is

\displaystyle\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{p}c_{p}(na)T_{n}(x)\frac{w^{2p}}{n^{2p+2}}+\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{p}s_{p}(na)T_{n}(x)\frac{w^{2p+2}}{n^{2p+3}}

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{p}c_{p}(na)U_{n-1}(x)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\frac{w^{2p}}{n^{2p+1}}

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{p}s_{p}(na)U_{n-1}(x)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\frac{w^{2p+1}}{n^{2p+2}},

and upon shifting the summation in the second sum, we immediately get
(
2.22
).
∎

We are now ready to complete the proof.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

We distinguish between four cases related to
I_{q}^{(n)}(x)
defined in
(
1.6
).

Case 1:

n=2\ell-1
,
\ell\geq 1
. According to (
2.12
), we need to
determine the coefficients of
w^{q-1}
in (
2.17
). For this purpose, we
require in addition to Lemma
2.6
also the easy expansions

(2.24)

\displaystyle\frac{e^{wa}-1}{w}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{a^{j+1}}{(j+1)!}w^{j},

(2.25)

\displaystyle\frac{w}{w^{2}+(2k)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{(2k)^{2j+2}}w^{2j+1}.

Subcase 1(a):
q=2p+1
for
p\geq 0
. That is, for a given
p
we need to
determine the coefficient of
w^{2p}
on the right of (
2.17
). There is no
contribution from (
2.25
), while (
2.24
) and (
2.22
) give the
coefficient

\frac{\binom{2\ell}{\ell}a^{2p+1}}{4^{\ell}(2p+1)!}-\frac{(-1)^{p}}{2^{2\ell-1}}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\binom{2\ell}{\ell-k}\frac{s_{p-1}(2ka)T_{2k}(x)+c_{p}(2ka)U_{2k-1}(x)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}{(2k)^{2p+1}}.

With (
2.12
) we then get (
2.4
).

Subcase 1(b):
q=2p
for
p\geq 1
. In this case, we determine the
coefficient of
w^{2p-1}
on the right of (
2.17
). From (
2.24
),
(
2.22
) and (
2.25
), we get the coefficient

\frac{\binom{2\ell}{\ell}a^{2p}}{4^{\ell}(2p)!}-\frac{(-1)^{p}}{2^{2\ell-1}}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\binom{2\ell}{\ell-k}\frac{c_{p-1}(2ka)T_{2k}(x)-s_{p}(2ka)U_{2k-1}(x)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}-(-1)^{k}}{(2k)^{2p}}.

With (
2.12
), this now gives (
2.6
).

Case 2:

n=2\ell
,
\ell\geq 1
. In this case we need to determine the
coefficients of
w^{q-1}
in (
2.18
). This will be similar to Case 1, but
(
2.24
) will no longer be required, and in place of (
2.25
) we use
the geometric series

(2.26)

\frac{2k+1}{w^{2}+(2k+1)^{2}}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{(2k+1)^{2j+1}}w^{2j}.

Subcase 2(a):
q=2p+1
for
p\geq 0
. With (
2.22
) and (
2.26
),
the coefficient of

4.
Some classes of limit expressions

Since the four identities in Corollary
3.2
hold for all integers

\ell\geq 0
, it is a reasonable question to ask what happens as

\ell\to\infty
for a fixed
p
. For
p=1,\ldots,4
, this was answered in

[
3
]
: We have the following limits as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

(4.1)

\displaystyle\frac{2^{n+1}}{\binom{n}{n/2}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\frac{\binom{2k}{k}}{4^{k}(2k+n+1)}\rightarrow\pi,

(4.2)

\displaystyle\frac{2^{n+1}}{\binom{n}{n/2}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\frac{4^{k}}{\binom{2k}{k}k(2k+n)}\rightarrow\pi^{2},

(4.3)

\displaystyle 2\cdot\frac{2^{n+3}}{\binom{n}{n/2}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\frac{\binom{2k}{k}G(k)}{4^{k}(2k+n+1)}\rightarrow\pi^{3},

(4.4)

\displaystyle 6\cdot\frac{2^{n+3}}{\binom{n}{n/2}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\frac{4^{k}H(k)}{\binom{2k}{k}k(2k+n)}\rightarrow\pi^{4},

where

(4.5)

G(k):=G_{1}(k)=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{1}{(2j+1)^{2}}\quad\hbox{and}\quad H(k):=H_{2}(k)=\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{(2j)^{2}},

and where the central binomial coefficient
\binom{n}{n/2}
also makes sense
for odd
n=2\ell+1
if interpreted as

(4.6)

\binom{n}{n/2}=\binom{2\ell+1}{\ell+\tfrac{1}{2}}=\frac{\Gamma(2\ell+2)}{\Gamma(\ell+\tfrac{3}{2})^{2}}=\frac{4^{2\ell+1}}{\binom{2\ell}{\ell}(2\ell+1)\pi}.

This can be obtained from the well-known identity

\Gamma(m+\tfrac{1}{2})=\frac{(2m)!}{4^{m}m!}\sqrt{\pi},

valid for integers
m\geq 0
.

We can now state and prove the main result of this section.

Theorem 4.1
.

For any integer
p\geq 0
we have the following limits as

:

(4.7)

\frac{2^{2p+n+1}(2p)!}{\binom{n}{n/2}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\binom{2k}{k}G_{p}(k)}{4^{k}(2k+n+1)}\rightarrow\pi^{2p+1},

and for
p\geq 1
,

(4.8)

\frac{2^{2p+n-1}(2p-1)!}{\binom{n}{n/2}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{4^{k}H_{p}(k)}{\binom{2k}{k}k(2k+n)}\rightarrow\pi^{2p},

where
G_{p}(k)
and
H_{p}(k)
are as defined in (
1.8
) and (
1.10
), and
for odd
n
the binomial coefficient
\binom{n}{n/2}
is interpreted as in
(
4.6
).

We see that (
4.7
) with
p=0
and
p=1
immediately gives (
4.1
) and
(
4.3
), respectively, while (
4.8
) with
p=1
and

recovers
(
4.2
) and (

).

For the proof of Theorem
4.1
, we require a few lemmas.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
s>1
. Then we have the following limits as

\ell\to\infty
:

(4.9)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\binom{2\ell}{\ell}}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\binom{2\ell}{\ell-k}\frac{1}{k^{s}}

\displaystyle\to\zeta(s),

(4.10)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\binom{2\ell}{\ell}}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\binom{2\ell}{\ell-k}\frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{k^{s}}

\displaystyle\to\left(1-2^{1-s}\right)\zeta(s),

(4.11)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\binom{2\ell+1}{\ell+1}}\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}\binom{2\ell+1}{\ell-k}\frac{1}{(2k+1)^{s}}

\displaystyle\to\left(1-2^{-s}\right)\zeta(s),

(4.12)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\binom{2\ell+1}{\ell+1}}\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}\binom{2\ell+1}{\ell-k}\frac{(-1^{k})}{(2k+1)^{s}}

\displaystyle\to\beta(s),

where
\zeta(s)
is the Riemann zeta function and
\beta(s)
is the Dirichlet
beta function, respectively defined by

\zeta(s)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^{s}}\quad({\rm Re}(s)>1)\quad\hbox{and}\quad\beta(s)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{(2k+1)^{s}}\quad({\rm Re}(s)>0).

Proof.

It is easy to see that for a fixed
k
we have

\frac{\binom{2\ell}{\ell-k}}{\binom{2\ell}{\ell}}=\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{\ell-j}{\ell+1-j}\to 1,\quad\hbox{and}\quad\frac{\binom{2\ell+1}{\ell-k}}{\binom{2\ell+1}{\ell+1}}=\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{\ell-j}{\ell+2-j}\to 1,

as
\ell\to\infty
. By Tannery’s theorem (see, e.g.,
[
2
, p. 136]
or the
Appendix in
[
8
]
), the limit of the sum in (
4.9
) is then
\zeta(s)
,
as desired. Similarly, the limits of the sums in (
4.10
) and (
4.11
)
are respectively

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{k^{s}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j^{s}}-2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2j)^{s}}=\left(1-2^{1-s}\right)\zeta(s),

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2k+1)^{s}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j^{s}}-\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2j)^{s}}=\left(1-2^{-s}\right)\zeta(s),

again as desired, and (
4.12
) follows similarly. However, while
\beta(s)

is defined for all
s>0
, Tannery’s theorem applies only for
s>1
in this case.
∎

Important tools in dealing with zeta and related functions are the Bernoulli
and Euler numbers, which are usually defined by the exponential generating
functions

(4.13)

\frac{t}{e^{t}-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}B_{n}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad\frac{2}{e^{t}+e^{-t}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}E_{n}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}.

They satisfy, in particular,

(4.14)

B_{0}=E_{0}=1,\quad B_{1}=-\frac{1}{2},\quad\hbox{and}\quad B_{2j+1}=E_{2j-1}=0\quad\hbox{for all}\quad j\geq 1.

We will also use the Bernoulli and Euler polynomials, which can be defined by

(4.15)

B_{n}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom{n}{j}B_{j}x^{n-j},\qquad E_{n-1}(x)=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom{n}{j}\left(1-2^{j}\right)B_{j}x^{n-j},

respectively. They have the special values

(4.16)

B_{n}(\tfrac{1}{2})=\left(2^{1-n}-1\right)B_{n},\qquad E_{n}(\tfrac{1}{2})=2^{-n}E_{n}.

All these properties can be found, for instance, in
[
6
, Sec. 9.6]
or

[
12
, Ch. 24]
. The main connection with the proof of Theorem
4.1

is given through Euler’s well-known identity

(4.17)

\zeta(2n)=(-1)^{n-1}\frac{(2\pi)^{2n}}{2(2n)!}B_{2n},\qquad n=1,2,\ldots

(see, e.g.,
[
6
, eq. 9.616]
) and its analogue

(4.18)

\beta(2n+1)=(-1)^{n}\frac{\pi^{2n+1}}{2^{2n+2}(2n)!}E_{2n},\qquad n=0,1,\ldots

(see, e.g.,
[
6
, eq. 0.233.6]
). These last two identities are, in fact,
special cases of a general identity for Dirichlet
L
-series at either even
or odd positive integers.

In the proof of Theorem
4.1
we also require the following summation
formulas for Bernoulli numbers.

Lemma 4.3
.

For all integers
p\geq 1
we have

(4.19)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}\binom{2p+1}{2j+1}2^{2p-2j}B_{2p-2j}=2p,

(4.20)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}\binom{2p+1}{2j+1}2^{2p-2j}\left(2^{2p-2j}-1\right)B_{2p-2j}=(2p+1)(1-E_{2p}),

(4.21)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}\binom{2p}{2j}2^{2p-2j}B_{2p-2j}=2p-1+\left(2-2^{2p}\right)B_{2p},

(4.22)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}\binom{2p}{2j}2^{2p-2j}\left(2^{2p-2j}-1\right)B_{2p-2j}=2p.

Proof.

All four identities can be derived in basically the same way: We first factor
out the term
2^{2p}
and reverse the order of summation. Then, using the
properties in (
4.14
), we write the sums either as Bernoulli or as Euler
polynomials, as given in (
4.15
).
Denoting the sums in (
4.19
)–(
4.22
) by
S_{1},\ldots,S_{4}
,
respectively, we then get

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\binom{2p+1}{2j}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2p-2j}B_{2j}

\displaystyle=2^{2p}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{2p+1}\binom{2p+1}{j}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2p-j}B_{j}-\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2p}-(2p+1)\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2p-1}\left(\frac{-1}{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=2^{2p}\left(2B_{2p+1}(\tfrac{1}{2})+\frac{2p}{2^{2p}}\right)=2p,

where we have used the first identity in (
4.16
) and (
4.14
). Next,

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\binom{2p+1}{2j}\left(2^{2j}-1\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2p-2j}B_{2j}

\displaystyle=2^{2p}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{2p+1}\binom{2p+1}{j}\left(2^{2j}-1\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2p-j}B_{j}-(2p+1)\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2p-1}\left(\frac{-1}{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=2^{2p}\left(-2\frac{2p+1}{2}B_{2p}(\tfrac{1}{2})+\frac{2p+1}{2^{2p}}\right)=(2p+1)(1-E_{2p}),

where we have used the second identity in (
4.16
). Next, in analogy to

S_{1}
we have

\displaystyle S_{3}

\displaystyle=2^{2p}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{2p}\binom{2p}{j}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2p-j}B_{j}-\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2p}-2p\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2p-1}\left(\frac{-1}{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=2^{2p}\left(2B_{2p}(\tfrac{1}{2})+\frac{2p-1}{2^{2p}}\right)=\left(2-2^{2p}\right)B_{2p}+2p-1,

where we have used the first identity in (
4.16
). Finally, in analogy to

S_{2}
we have

\displaystyle S_{4}

\displaystyle=2^{2p}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{2p}\binom{2p}{j}\left(2^{2j}-1\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2p-j}B_{j}-2p\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2p-1}\left(\frac{-1}{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=2^{2p}\left(-2\frac{2p}{2}E_{2p-1}(\tfrac{1}{2})+\frac{2p}{2^{2p}}\right)=2p,

where we have again used the second identity in (
4.16
), followed by
(
4.14
). This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

To prove (
4.7
), we first multiply both sides of (

) by

Denoting
X:=\lim_{\ell\to\infty}X_{\ell}
, we get with (
4.9
) and
(

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=2(2p)!\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}\frac{(2\pi)^{2p-2j}}{2(2p-2j)!}B_{2p-2j}\frac{\pi^{2j+1}}{(2j+1)!}+\frac{\pi^{2p+1}}{2p+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi^{2p+1}}{2p+1}\left(1+\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}\binom{2p+1}{2j+1}2^{2p-2j}B_{2p-2j}\right)=\pi^{2p+1},

where the last identity comes from (
4.19
). This proves the limit
(
4.7
) for even
n
.

Next, we multiply both sides of (

) by

and denote the resulting

. Then we have

\displaystyle Y_{\ell}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{p-1}2^{2p+2}(2p)!\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}\frac{\binom{2\ell+1}{\ell-k}}{\binom{2\ell+1}{\ell+1}}\frac{1}{(2k+1)^{2p-2j}}\right)\frac{(-1)^{j}\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)^{2j+1}}{(2j+1)!}

\displaystyle\quad+(-1)^{p}2^{2p+2}(2p)!\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}\frac{\binom{2\ell+1}{\ell-k}}{\binom{2\ell+1}{\ell+1}}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{(2k+1)^{2p+1}}.

Denoting
Y:=\lim_{\ell\to\infty}Y_{\ell}
, we get with (
4.11
), (
4.12
)
and with (
4.17
), (
4.18
),

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=2^{2p+2}(2p)!\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}\left(1-2^{2j-2p}\right)\frac{(2\pi)^{2p-2j}}{2(2p-2j)!}B_{2p-2j}\frac{\pi^{2j+1}}{2^{2j+1}(2j+1)!}

\displaystyle\qquad+2^{2p+2}(2p)!\frac{\pi^{2p+1}}{2^{2p+2}(2p)!}E_{2p}

where the last identity comes from (
4.19

(4.23)

\frac{\binom{2\ell+1}{\ell+1}}{\binom{2\ell+1}{\ell+1/2}}=\frac{(2\ell+1)^{2}}{4(\ell+1)}\frac{\binom{2\ell}{\ell}^{2}}{4^{2\ell}}\pi,

odd

\displaystyle Z_{\ell}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{p-1}2(2p-1)!\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\frac{\binom{2\ell}{\ell-k}}{\binom{2\ell}{\ell}}\frac{1}{k^{2p-2j}}\right)\frac{(-1)^{j}\pi^{2j}}{(2j)!}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\pi^{2p}}{2p}+(-1)^{p-1}2(2p-1)!\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\frac{\binom{2\ell}{\ell-k}}{\binom{2\ell}{\ell}}\frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{k^{2p}}.

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi^{2p}}{2p}\left(1+\left(2^{2p}-2\right)B_{2p}+\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}\binom{2p}{2j}2^{2p-2j}B_{2p-2j}\right)=\pi^{2p},

5.
Further remarks and consequences

1.
In an unpublished preprint, C. Lupu
[
11
]
used direct
manipulations of the arcsine identities (
1.7
) and (
1.9
) to obtain
series evaluations that are quite similar to some of our corollaries in
Section
3
. For example, he proved (rewritten in our notation and
normalization) that for all integers
p\geq 0
,

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\binom{2k}{k}G_{p}(k)}{8^{k}}=\frac{1}{(2p)!\sqrt{2}}\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)^{2p}

and for
p\geq 1
,

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{2^{k}H_{p}(k)}{\binom{2k}{k}k}=\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{(2p-1)!}\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)^{2p-1},

which can be seen as supplementing our Corollary
3.6
. These and the
six other such evaluations obtained in
[
11
]
are of a particularly pleasing
form in that the right-hand sides are simple multiples of powers of
\pi
.

2.
Using partial fraction expansions, we can obtain further series
evaluation from some of the identities in Sections
2
and
3
.
For instance, with

(5.1)

\frac{1}{(2k+2+m)(2k+2+n)}=\frac{1}{n-m}\left(\frac{1}{2k+2+m}-\frac{1}{2k+2+n}\right)

(
n\neq m
) combined with the identity (
2.7
), we obtain the following.

Corollary 5.1
.

For all nonnegative integers
p
and
n\neq m
we have

(5.2)

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\binom{2k}{k}G_{p}(k)x^{2k+2}}{4^{k}(2k+2+n)(2k+2+m)}=\frac{(n+1)x^{-n}I_{2p+1}^{(n)}(x)-(m+1)x^{-m}I_{2p+1}^{(m)}(x)}{(n-m)(2p+1)!}.

Example 5.2
.

With
x=1,n=1
and
m=2
we get from (
5.2
),

(5.3)

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\binom{2k}{k}G_{p}(k)}{4^{k}(2k+3)(2k+4)}=\frac{3I_{2p+1}^{(2)}(1)-2I_{2p+1}^{(1)}(1)}{(2p+1)!}.

Using evaluations as in Corollary
3.2
, the identity (
5.3
)
yields with
p=0
and
p=1
, respectively,

(5.4)

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\binom{2k}{k}}{4^{k}(2k+3)(2k+4)}=\frac{\pi}{4}-\frac{2}{3},

(5.5)

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\binom{2k}{k}G_{1}(k)}{4^{k}(2k+3)(2k+4)}=\frac{\pi^{3}}{96}-\frac{47\pi}{144}+\frac{20}{27}.

Example 5.3
.

We choose
n=1
and
m=2
again and use (
5.2
) with (
2.3
) and
(
2.4
). Then we get more generally for
p=0
,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\binom{2k}{k}x^{2k+3}}{4^{k}(2k+2+n)(2k+2+m)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{3}+\left(\frac{x^{2}}{3}-\frac{x}{2}+\frac{2}{3}\right)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad+\left(x^{3}-x^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\arcsin{x},

and for
p=1
,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\binom{2k}{k}G_{1}(k)x^{2k+3}}{4^{k}(2k+2+n)(2k+2+m)}=\frac{20}{27}-\left(\frac{x^{2}}{27}-\frac{x}{8}+\frac{20}{27}\right)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad-\left(\frac{x^{3}}{9}-\frac{x^{2}}{4}+\frac{2x}{3}+\frac{1}{8}\right)\arcsin{x}+\left(\frac{x^{2}}{6}-\frac{x}{4}+\frac{1}{3}\right)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\cdot\arcsin^{2}{x}

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\frac{x^{3}}{6}-\frac{x^{2}}{6}+\frac{1}{12}\right)\arcsin^{3}{x}.

Setting
x=1
, we recover the identities (
5.4
) and (
5.5
).

It is clear that we can obtain more identities by using (
2.8
) to get an
analogue of Corollary
5.1
, or by using partial fraction expansions
of more than three factors in place of (
5.1
).

3.
The multiple sums
H_{p}(k)
and
G_{p}(k)
, defined in (
1.10
) and
(
1.8
), are special cases of so-called multiple harmonic and multiple

t
-harmonic sums which have recently been studied quite intensively.
In fact, we have

H_{p+1}=4^{-p}H_{k-1}(\{2\}^{p}),

where
\{2\}^{p}
denotes the
p
-tuple
(2,2,\ldots,2)
; see, e.g.,
[
7
]
.
Second, we have

G_{p}(k)=t_{k}(\{2\}^{p});

see, e.g.,
[
10
]
. The corresponding multiple sums with non-strict
inequalities between their summation indices are called multiple harmonic star
sums and multiple
t
-harmonic star sums, respectively. They were the main
object of study in
[
7
]
and
[
10
]
. We caution that our use use of
the letter
p
above should not be confused with the prime
p
used in

[
7
]
and in other related publications.

4.
As mentioned in the Introduction, we used a different method in

[
3
]
to evaluate
I_{q}^{(n)}(x)
for
q=1,\ldots,4
. The results are,
in fact, of different forms, and by equating them we get some nontrivial
combinatorial identities such as the following.

Corollary 5.4
.

For any integer
\ell\geq 1
we have

(5.6)

\displaystyle 4\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor\frac{\ell-1}{2}\rfloor}\binom{2\ell}{\ell-2k-1}\frac{1}{(2k+1)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\binom{2\ell}{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\frac{4^{j}}{\binom{2j}{j}j^{2}},

(5.7)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}\binom{2\ell+1}{\ell-k}\frac{1}{(2k+1)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{4^{2\ell}}{\binom{2\ell}{\ell}(2\ell+1)}\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\frac{\binom{2j}{j}}{4^{j}(2j+1)}.

Proof.

The rational part of (
3.6
) gives, after some simplification,

\frac{1}{2^{2\ell}}\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor\frac{\ell-1}{2}\rfloor}\binom{2\ell}{\ell-2k-1}\frac{1}{(2k+1)^{2}}.

On the other hand, the rational part of the same series in Corollary 5 in

[
3
]
gives

\frac{1}{2^{2\ell+2}}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\frac{4^{j}}{\binom{2j}{j}j^{2}}.

By equating the two, we immediately get (
5.6
).

The identity (
5.7
) is obtained analogously by equating the rational part
of (
3.5
) in the case
p=1
with that of the second identity in
Corollary 5 of
[
3
]
. (A note of caution: The notation
I_{q}^{(n)}(x)
in

[
3
]
is slightly different from that in the present paper.)
∎

We can obtain similar identities by taking
p=2
in (
3.5
) and
(
3.6
). It turns out that, along with (
5.6
) and (
5.7
), those

harmonic sums and related multiple zeta functions. However, this goes beyond

[

We finish with the observation that after dividing both sides of (

)
by
\binom{2\ell}{\ell}

4\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2k+1)^{2}}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{4^{j}}{\binom{2j}{j}j^{2}}.

The identity (
5.6
) can therefore be seen as a finite analogue of
the equality between the infinite series in (

This gets us back to the beginning if we note that the series on the left of
(
