Comparison of the antimicrobial, anti-biofilm, and cytotoxic effects of thymol/ampicillin and thymol/cefotaxime against Escherichia coli bacteria
Authors: Sajjad Jafari, Mina Shirmohammadpour, Arman Taran, Mina Tasoujlu, Bahman Mirzaei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveUsing essential oils in combination with antibiotics helps to combat antibiotic resistance. Thymol is a major phenolic compound found in many essential oils. The antimicrobial effects of thymol, ampicillin, cefotaxime, and their combinations were examined on Escherichia coli (E. coli) ATCC 25,922. Then, the antimicrobial effects of synergistic compounds were evaluated on twenty clinical strains. The chemical bonds, functional groups, and molecular interactions of the mentioned compounds were investigated with an FT-IR device. The time-killing curve, biofilm inhibition, hemolysis, and MTT methods were also conducted.ResultsAntimicrobial effects of thymol/ampicillin were greater than thymol/cefotaxime in both the ATCC and clinical strains. In the examination with the FT-IR device, both compounds showed a connection between components. The biofilm inhibition effect of thymol/ampicillin (62%) was greater than thymol/cefotaxime (39.28%). The bacterial killing time curve of Thymol/ampicillin was faster and more effective. The cytotoxic effects of compounds on red blood cells (RBCs) and human foreskin fibroblast (HFF) were lower than Triton X-100 (0.1%). In the treatment of diseases caused by E. coli bacteria, thymol/ampicillin had better effects than thymol/cefotaxime.

Introduction

Escherichia coli (E. coli), a rod-shaped member of Enterobacteriaceae, is the most prevalent cause of gastrointestinal tract to extraintestinal sites infections, such as urinary tract infections (UTIs), septicemia, and neonatal meningitis [1, 2].

Over the past ten years, increasing concerns for public health has arisen due to the rise in antibiotic resistance in Enterobacteriaceae isolates. In 2024, the World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that E. coli is responsible for both nosocomial and community-acquired infections [3]. Strains of this bacteria that produce extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBLs), plasmid-mediated AmpC (pAmpC), and carbapenemases are resistant to third-generation cephalosporins and the penicillin family [4]. A rise in resistance in E. coli isolates has also been reported in Iran [5].

A great deal of research is being conducted on how to make antibiotics more effective and combat antibiotic resistance. This includes several studies on the potential benefits of using essential oils (EOs) in combination with antibiotics. EOs contain several molecules with antibacterial properties, including thymol, a major phenolic compound found in many EOs [6].

The primary monoterpene, thymol (2-isopropyl-5-methylphenol), is naturally present in both black seed oil and thyme EO [7]. Thymol has the potential to enhance the concentrations of C16 and C18 saturated fatty acids while simultaneously reducing the levels of C18 unsaturated fatty acids. Additionally, it influences the enzymes responsible for synthesizing these fatty acids through competitive and non-competitive inhibition mechanisms. Furthermore, it may impede cell division by targeting bacterial proteins and diminishing ATP production, which ultimately disrupts the membrane respiratory chain [8, 9]. Thymol possesses immune system modulator, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer properties. EOs also can prevent the formation of biofilms created by gram-negative bacteria, such as E. coli [10, 11].

We investigated the antibacterial synergism of thymol combined with ampicillin and cefotaxime to address the prevalence of bacterial infections and antibiotic resistance.

Materials and methods

Antimicrobial compounds

Thymol, provided by Glaxir Pars Company (Mashhad, Iran), had a purity of 97% and a concentration of 4096 µg/mL. Ampicillin and cefotaxime provided by Dexir Company (Tehran, Iran), with a concentration of 1024 µg/mL. All materials stored at −20 °C. Thymol was diluted to 1024 µg/mL, Similarly, ampicillin and cefotaxime were diluted to 256 µg/mL using distilled water.

Bacterial strains

E. coli strain from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) 25,922 was obtained from the Microbiology Laboratory of Zanjan University of Medical. Twenty clinical strains from patients suffering from UTI of E. coli were obtained from laboratories and hospitals in Tehran Province. To identify these strains microbiology tests were conducted, including indole sulfide mobility (SIM), citrate, methyl red (MR), Voges Proskauer (VP), triple sugar iron (TSI), urease (urea), gelatinases, lysine decarboxylase (LDC), and oxidases [12]. The susceptibilities of the strains to several antibiotics were investigated (Table 1); some strains were multidrug resistant (MDR), and some were extensively drug resistant (XDR). The bacteria were cultured in MHB (Mueller-Hinton Broth, Aria Teb Company, Tehran, Iran) medium at 37 °C, and the prepared suspension was stored at 4 °C in 0.01 M MgSO₄ solution until use.

In vitro antimicrobial assay

The antimicrobial properties of thymol, ampicillin, and cefotaxime were evaluated separately and in combination using MIC-MBC method. Serial dilutions were performed in microplate wells separately for thymol, ampicillin, and cefotaxime. Then, 100 µl of E. coli ATCC 25,922 suspension containing 1.5 × 106 CFU/mL (Colony Forming Unit/mL) was added to each well. The growth control consisted of wells containing bacterial suspension and MHB medium without any antimicrobial agent. A negative control contained MHB medium without bacteria, while Ampicillin and MHB medium was used as the positive control. The samples were then incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. The well that corresponded to the MIC and the well containing higher concentration cultured in MHA medium (Mueller Hinton Agar, Aria Teb Company, Tehran, Iran) to determine the MBC of thymol, ampicillin, and cefotaxime. We recorded the concentration at which no bacterial growth occurred as the MBC and the lower concentration as the MIC [13]. The experiment was conducted three times.

Checkerboard assay

To evaluate the potential synergy between thymol and antibiotic (either ampicillin or cefotaxime) in four different combinations (Table 2), the checkerboard method was utilized. Thymol was diluted in columns 1 to 7, while antibiotic was diluted in rows A to G on a 96-well microtiter plate. Then microtiter plate was infected with bacterial suspension (McFarland standard), and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. This experiment was conducted three times.

Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy

The regular deconvolution method was applied to analyze the infrared spectra of Thymol, cefotaxime, ampicillin, thymol/ampicillin (A1 compound), and thymol/cefotaxime (A3 compound) (Table 2) [18, 19] Each compound was ground into a uniform powder and then mixed with spectroscopic grade potassium bromide (KBr) in an appropriate ratio. The mixtures were carefully homogenized and compressed into transparent pellets using a hydraulic press under vacuum to minimize moisture interference. The prepared KBr pellets were subsequently placed in the sample holder, and the FT-IR spectra were recorded using a Bruker TENSOR 27 spectrometer, while confirmation measurements were performed on a Thermo Scientific (USA) FT-IR instrument [18, 19]. Spectral deconvolution was then conducted to resolve overlapping bands and identify functional group interactions.

Antibiofilm assay

Three to five colonies of the bacteria were suspended in 5 mL of tryptone soy broth (TSB, Aria Teb Company, Tehran, Iran) containing 1% glucose. The bacterial suspension was adjusted to approximately 1 × 108 CFU/mL using the McFarland standard. The mixture was incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. A 96-well microtiter plate was filled with 100 µL of TSB. 10 µL of thymol, cefotaxime, ampicillin, thymol/ampicillin (A1 compound), and thymol/cefotaxime (A3 compound) (Table 2) was then added to the first row, and TSB was subsequently added until the total volume reached 200 µl. Serial dilutions were conducted in TSB with 1% glucose. A negative control contained TSB without bacteria, while a growth control contained TSB with bacteria. The samples were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. Then, the wells were washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), 200 µl of 99% methanol was added to each well and incubated at room temperature for 5 min. Methanol was removed, 200 µl of 1% crystal violet dye was added to each well, and incubated at room temperature for 5 min. The wells washed once more after the dye was removed. Following the injection of 200 µl of 96% ethanol, the optical density (OD, BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA) of each well was measured at a wavelength of 590 nm [20, 21]. This experiment was conducted three times. Time-kill assay

Thymol, cefotaxime, ampicillin, thymol/ampicillin (A1 compound), and thymol/cefotaxime (A3 compound) (Table 2) underwent a serial dilution in a 96-well microtiter plate. 100 µL of E. coli ATCC 25,922 suspension (1.5 × 106 CFU/mL) was added to wells, and incubated at 37 °C. 10 µL of each well was cultured in MHB every 6, 12, 18, or 24 h. A negative control contained MHB without bacteria, while a growth control contained MHB with bacteria. The count and lethality kinetics were evaluated after a 24 h cultivation period. The killing activity was characterized by the absence of colonies growth in MHA after 24 h [23]. This experiment was conducted three times.

In vitro hemolysis assay

5 mL of fresh human blood was mixed with 0/5 mL of sodium citrate dihydrate so that the blood could not clot. After centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 10 min, the plasma was removed, and the samples were washed twice with PBS. Then, 2 mL of fresh blood was mixed with 48 mL of PBS to prepare 2% RBCs. Thymol, cefotaxime, ampicillin, thymol/ampicillin (A1 compound), and thymol/cefotaxime (A3 compound) (Table 2) underwent a serial dilution in a 96-well microtiter plate, and 100 µl of 2% RBCs was added to each well. After 24 h incubation at 37 °C, the abs (absorbance) values were measured at 570 nm with an ELISA (Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay) Reader (Agilent, USA). This experiment was conducted three times. The toxicity percentage of each compound was calculated via the following formula [24]:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{Hemolysis}\:(\%)=\frac{\mathrm{(}{\mathrm{Abs}}_{\mathrm{sample}}-{\mathrm{Abs}}_{\mathrm{blank}}\mathrm{)}}{\mathrm{(}{\mathrm{Abs}}_{\mathrm{triton}}-{\mathrm{Abs}}_{\mathrm{blank}}\mathrm{)}} \times 100$$\end{document}

Triton X-100 (0.1%) was used as the positive control, and PBS as the negative control.

In vitro cytotoxicity assay

Cytotoxicity of thymol, cefotaxime, ampicillin, thymol/ampicillin (A1 compound), and thymol/cefotaxime (A3 compound) (Table 2) against a human foreskin fibroblast (HFF) cell line (Pasteur Institute, Tehran, Iran) evaluated using the MTT (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) assay. Initially, 3 × 104 cells were cultured in a 96-well microtiter plate with 100 µL of 90% Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (256 µg/mL) (Dexir Company, located in Tehran, Iran). The cells were subsequently cultured at 37 °C for 24 h in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Control groups included untreated cells, medium plus MTT without cells (blank), and wells containing the tested compounds plus MTT without cells to account for possible absorbance interference. Mentioned compounds underwent a serial dilution in the microplate, and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Then, 20 µL of MTT stock solution (5 mg/mL) was added to each well, resulting in a final concentration of 0.83 mg/mL. After 4 h incubation at 37 °C, the supernatant was removed, and 100 µL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added to each well. The microplate was shaken for 15 min to allow the cells to release insoluble purple formazan. Finally, the abs values were measured at 570 nm via a microplate spectrophotometer (model ELx808 made by BioTek). This experiment was conducted three times. Peptide-free cell suspensions and Triton X-100 (0.1%) were used as negative and positive controls, respectively.

Statistical analysis

A two-way multiple-group analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed using GraphPad software to statistically analyze the results, with a significance level set at p-value less than 0.05.

Software and graphical analysis

Data were analyzed and visualized using GraphPad Prism software (v10.4.0.621; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Semi-quantitative data were input into the software for descriptive analysis and graphical representation. GraphPad Prism was employed to generate all plots and charts, utilizing appropriate scales and statistical annotations based on the type and distribution of the data.

Evaluation of synergy via the checkerboard assay

In thymol/ampicillin, the A1 compound with FICI 1 had an additive effect (FICI 0.5 to 1), the A2 compound with FICI 4.25 had an antagonistic effect (FICI ≥ 2), and compounds A3 and A4 with FICI 0.5 had a synergistic effect (FICI ≤ 0.5) (Table 3). For thymol/cefotaxime, the A1 compound with an FICI of 0.25 had a synergistic effect, the A2 compound with an FICI of 1.5 had an indifferent effect (FICI ≥ 1 or 2), the A3 compound with an FICI of 1 had an additive effect, and the A4 compound with an FICI of 3 had an antagonistic effect (Table 4).

FT-IR spectroscopy

Thymol/ampicillin and thymol/cefotaxime contain peaks that ranged from 592.61 to 3442.64 cm− 1, which are associated with the C=O ester group [25]; the C=O amide I band [26]; the C‒N amide II band [26]; the C‒O‒C‒polysaccharide [27]; the CH stretching alkene group [28]; and the OH carbohydrates [29], proteins, and polysaccharides. Non-covalent interactions like hydrogen binding or π–π stacking between mentioned functional groups of thymol and antibiotics, could modify spatial arrangement and influence antibiotic effectiveness The C = C conjugated and C≡C, which denotes the connection between thymol/ampicillin and thymol/cefotaxime, separates the combined and individual states (Figs. 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5).

Time-kill assay

Thymol was able to reduce the number of bacteria to zero at a concentration of 512 µg/mL at 6, 12, 18, and 24 h, ampicillin at a concentration of 128 µg/mL in the first 6 h and at a concentration of 8 µg/mL at 18, 12, and 24 h, cefotaxime at a concentration of 256 µg/mL in the first 6 h, at a concentration of 128 µg/mL at 12 h, and at a concentration of 32 µg/mL at 18 and 24, thymol/ampicillin (A1 compound) at a concentration of 128 µg/mL (thymol)/32 µg/mL (ampicillin) in the first 6 h, at a concentration of 32 µg/mL (thymol)/4 µg/mL (ampicillin) at 12, 18, and 24 h (Fig. 7), and thymol/cefotaxime (A3 compound) at a concentration of 512 µg/mL (thymol)/128 µg/mL (cefotaxime) in the first 6 h, at a concentration of 256 µg/mL (thymol)/64 µg/mL (cefotaxime) at 12 h, and at a concentration of 28 µg/mL (thymol)/16 µg/mL (cefotaxime) at 18 and 24 h (Fig. 8). Thymol/ampicillin was faster and more effective at killing bacteria than thymol/cefotaxime.

Discussion

The widespread use of antibiotics caused the emergence of MDR bacteria [30]. Ampicillin and cefotaxime often use to treat infections caused by E. coli isolated from UTIs, but significant resistance to these drugs, even 30–90% or more, has been reported [31]. There is an urgent need for new strategies to combat antibiotic-resistant bacteria as a primary global health concern [32]. One of these strategies is combining thymol and antibiotics [33].

In the study of Zhuocheng Yao et al. in 2022, thymol/colistin had an antimicrobial synergistic effect (FICI: 0.5) on E. coli [34]. In a study by Sumaira Noreen et al. in 2018, antimicrobial effect of aqueous and ethanolic extracts of cloves, cinnamon, and garlic with ampicillin, imipenem, ciprofloxacin, norfloxacin, and nalidixic acid on E. coli were investigated [35]. In a study by Fatemeh Tavakoli et al. in 2022, the antimicrobial effects of a methanolic extract of Salvia limbata with ceftazidime on Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa) were investigated, which had a better antimicrobial effect [36]. In a study by Sohini Mitra et al. in 2024, the antimicrobial effect of an aqueous extract of Psidium guajava (guava) plants with cefotaxime was investigated on E. coli [37]. In a study by Oumaima Amassmoud et al. in 2023, ciprofloxacin and streptomycin had synergistic effect on Thymus pallidus and T. broussonnetii plants [38]. In a study of Anwesa Bag et al. in 2011, Terminalia chebula fruit extract with ceftazidime had synergistic effect [39]. The compounds in the phenolic plant extract induce an antimicrobial effect [40].

Combining thymol with ampicillin or cefotaxime enhanced antibacterial and antibiofilm activities, shortened bacterial killing time, and reduced cytotoxicity. Based on FT-IR analysis, hydrogen binding between OH groups of thymol and the C = O or amide groups in ampicillin or cefotaxime, and π–π stacking between the aromatic rings of thymol and antibiotics might contribute to the antimicrobial and antibiofilm effects [41]. To conclusively attribute bioactivity to specific bonds like C ≡ C, which creates the connection between substances, further chemical isolation and activity-guided fractionation are necessary.

Thymol is a phenolic compound extracted from the Shirazi thyme plant. Thymol has structural isomers and a phenolic hydroxyl group [42], which increases hydrophilicity, disrupts membrane integrity and permeability, and causes proton and potassium leakage, leading to the loss of membrane potential in E. coli [43]. Ampicillin and ceftazidime cause osmotic instability in bacteria by inhibiting the synthesis of the bacterial cell wall [44].

Limitations

This study was limited to in vitro experiments and a relatively small number of clinical isolates, which may not fully represent in vivo conditions. Future studies should include in vivo assessments, explore the molecular mechanisms underlying the synergistic effects of thymol with antibiotics, and evaluate thymol derivatives or thymol-rich plant extracts against a broader range of MDR and XDR bacterial strains to confirm and extend these findings.

Conclusion

Thymol/ampicillin showed greater antimicrobial and antibiofilm effects than thymol/cefotaxime. The cytotoxic effects of both compounds were lower than the Triton X-100 on human RBCs and HFF cells. According to the results, combination of thymol and ampicillin can be one of the solutions to reducing antibiotic resistance and treating urinary infections caused by E. coli after passing complementary tests.
