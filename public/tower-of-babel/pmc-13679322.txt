Safety and immunogenicity of a first-generation modified vaccinia virus Ankara-vectored SARS-CoV-2 vaccine candidate with mRNA-based booster vaccination: A phase 1 trial
Authors: Anahita Fathi, Christine Dahlke, Leonie Mayer, Sibylle C Mellinghoff, Verena Krähling, Sandro Halwe, Helena Müller-Kräuter, Rolf Fendel, Peter Kremsner, Meral Esen, Martha Klug, Marc Lütgehetmann, Leonie M Weskamm, MVA-SARS-2-S Study Group, Gerd Sutter, Stephan Becker, Svenja Hardtke, Marylyn M Addo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe rapid development of effective COVID-19 vaccines was enabled by the parallel assessment of multiple candidates early in the pandemic, including candidates based on the Modified Vaccinia virus Ankara (MVA) vector platform. This Phase 1 trial (NCT04569383) evaluated the MVA-based vaccine candidate MVA-SARS-2-S, expressing the full-length native SARS-CoV-2 spike glycoprotein (Wuhan-HU-1). Between October 2020 and May 2021, 30 healthy, SARS-CoV-2–naive adults received two doses of either 107 or 108 international units (IU) of MVA-SARS-2-S 28 days apart, with 6-month follow-up. A subgroup (n = 12) received two BNT162b2 booster vaccinations. Safety and reactogenicity were primary objectives; secondary and exploratory objectives included S1 seroconversion rates, S-domain-specific antibodies, SARS-CoV-2-neutralizing antibodies, and spike-specific T cell responses. MVA-SARS-2-S demonstrated a good safety profile. No severe or serious adverse events occurred. Immunogenicity was poor: S1-specific seroconversion occurred in 0/15 (107 IU group) and 3/15 (108 IU group) participants. Binding antibodies were predominantly directed against the S2 subunit, with peak median anti-S2 fold changes of 6.0 and 5.2 in the 107 IU and 108 IU groups, respectively. Neutralizing antibodies were detected in one participant (108 IU group); spike-specific T cell responses were observed in 5/15 (107 IU group) and 2/13 (108 IU group) participants. Heterologous BNT162b2 vaccination induced S1 seroconversion and detectable neutralizing antibody and T cell responses in all boosted participants, without safety concerns. In conclusion, while MVA-SARS-2-S was well tolerated and did not adversely affect subsequent BNT162b2 boosting, the vaccine candidate elicited insufficient immunogenicity, underscoring the need for optimized antigen design.KEYWORDS: SARS-CoV-2, MVA-SARS-2-S, BNT162b2, coronavirus vaccine, modified vaccinia virus ankara, vector vaccine

Introduction

In response to the COVID-19 pandemic, vaccine candidates have been developed at an unprecedented speed.1–4 This has not only led to the successful licensure of multiple COVID-19 vaccines, such as vector- and mRNA-based candidates, but has also provided opportunities to further dissect the characteristics of individual vaccine platform technologies. One of these technologies is based on the Modified Vaccinia virus Ankara (MVA) vector. MVA is a well-established vaccine platform that has been licensed in its non-recombinant form as a vaccine against smallpox and mpox,5,6 and, in its recombinant form, as part of a multivalent ebolavirus vaccine used in a heterologous immunization regimen.7 Due to its high degree of attenuation, resulting in abortive replication in mammalian cells, MVA exhibits an excellent safety profile8 that has also been demonstrated in immunocompromised and pediatric populations.9,10 In humans, MVA-based vaccines have further been shown to induce multimodal humoral and cellular immune responses,11 and have been administered through various routes, including subcutaneous, intradermal,12 intramuscular,13 and inhalational application (e.g., in NCT05226390).

We previously evaluated the MVA vector vaccine candidate MVA-MERS-S against the betacoronavirus Middle East Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus (MERS-CoV) expressing the full-length native spike (S) glycoprotein in a first-in-human trial (NCT03615911).13 Two vaccinations of MVA-MERS-S given 28 days (d) apart demonstrated an acceptable safety profile and induced seroconversion and neutralizing antibody (NAb) titers in 100% of participants, irrespective of dose, and S-specific T cell responses in 91% of participants who received a dose of 108 plaque-forming units [PFU]. Booster vaccination administered one year after initial vaccination resulted in a potent increase in insert-specific humoral immune responses,14 which persisted for at least 2 years (y).15

Building on these encouraging results, we designed the vaccine candidate MVA-SARS-2-S against COVID-19, expressing the full-length native SARS-CoV-2-S protein (Wuhan-HU-1), at the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020. Following preclinical studies of MVA-SARS-2-S confirming its tolerability, immunogenicity, and efficacy in mice,16 we initiated a dose-ascending clinical phase 1 trial in healthy SARS-CoV-2-naive individuals.

In a study amendment, we subsequently administered booster vaccinations with the mRNA-based COVID-19 vaccine BNT162b2 to a subgroup of the study cohort six months after initiation of the primary vaccination series. The evaluation of heterologous vaccination regimens is often challenging at early stages of vaccine development, as safety and immunogenicity must first be generated for the individual vaccine candidates. However, multiple post-licensure studies of heterologous COVID-19 vaccination regimens, specifically with adenovirus-based prime (ChAdOx1 nCoV-19) and mRNA-based booster vaccinations were facilitated by early adaptations in the COVID-19 vaccination guidelines, for example in Germany,17 and have pointed toward acceptable safety and increased immunogenicity of such a regimen.18–20

Here, we report on the safety, tolerability and immunogenicity of MVA-SARS-2-S alone and in combination with the mRNA-based vaccine BNT162b2.

Methods

Vaccines

MVA-SARS-2-S is a recombinant MVA vector vaccine encoding the native full-length SARS-CoV-2 S protein from the virus isolate Wuhan HU-1 (GenBank accession no. MN908947.1).16 The vaccine was produced using the immortalized chicken embryo fibroblast cell line DF-1 and was manufactured by IDT Biologika GmbH (Dessau, Germany) according to Good Manufacturing Practice. The clinical drug product was supplied as a ready-to-use formulation at the final clinical doses of 1 × 107 ± 0·5 log international units [IU] per 0·5 mL (hereafter referred to as low dose [LD]) and 1 × 108 ± 0·5 log IU (hereafter high dose [HD]). The lipid nanoparticle-formulated mRNA vaccine BNT162b2, which encodes a prefusion-stabilized full-length SARS-CoV-2 S protein, was obtained as a commercially available product (Comirnaty, BioNTech, Mainz, Germany). Vaccine vials were stored at ≤−65°C (MVA-SARS-2-S) and −90–−60°C (BNT162b2).

Study design and participants

This open-label, Phase 1 dose-escalation trial of MVA-SARS-2-S was conducted in healthy men and women aged 18–55 y without prior SARS-CoV-2 exposure or prior recombinant MVA immunization at the University Medical Center Hamburg-Eppendorf, Germany. Sex was self-reported by participants. The public was made aware of the study through public announcements and interested individuals were screened and subsequently enrolled if they met eligibility criteria. Volunteers were allocated to two dose cohorts and received two intramuscular injections of either LD or HD MVA-SARS-2-S into the deltoid muscle of the non-dominant arm; one on Day 0 (vaccination 1 [V1]) and another on Day 28 (V2). When licensed COVID-19 vaccinations became publicly available (summer 2021 for healthy young individuals, i.e., the study population of this trial), an amendment was submitted and continuation in a trial extension was offered to all study participants who had not received a SARS-CoV-2 vaccination other than the trial vaccine and had not developed COVID-19 during the trial. All consenting participants in the extension trial received two doses (V3 and V4) of 30 µg BNT162b2 as intramuscular injections into the deltoid muscle of the non-dominant arm 21 d apart, beginning on Day 168 of the initial trial. Participants were followed up until V4D28 (see graphical abstract). Details of the study conduct and the study protocol including a full list of inclusion and exclusion criteria are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Ethical approval statement

The trial was conducted in accordance with Good Clinical Practice and the Declaration of Helsinki (Fortaleza, 2013). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. An independent Local Safety Board was established to provide oversight. The trial was reviewed and approved by the Competent National Authority (Paul-Ehrlich-Institut, Langen, Germany, reference number 4227/01; approval date 30 September 2020) and the competent Independent Review Board, the Ethics Committee of the Hamburg Medical Association (registration number 2020–10164-AMG-ff, approval date 25 September 2020). The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04569383) and with the European Clinical Trials Database (EudraCT No. 2020-002998-10).

Procedures

Participants presented to the study site for ambulatory visits on Day (D) 0, which was the day of V1 (V1D0; baseline specimens were taken prior to vaccination), V1D1, V1D3, V1D7, and V1D14; on the day of V2 (i.e., D28 [V2D0]), V2D1, V2D7, V2D14, V2D28, V2D56, and V2D140 (End of Study). In the trial extension, participants received one vaccination with BNT162b2 on Day 168 (V3D0) and a second dose 21 d later (V4D0) and presented to the site on V3D1, V3D3, V3D7, V4D1, V4D3, and V4D7, as well as at the end of the trial extension (V4D28). Safety assessments were performed at all study visits and included clinical evaluations, safety laboratory testing and adverse event (AE) assessments. Participants were requested to maintain a diary and to record reactogenicity for seven days following each vaccination. Predefined trial holding rules requiring local safety board review were triggered for persisting (>48 hours) high Grade (3/4) local and systemic solicited AEs in ≥2 participants and unsolicited AEs considered treatment-related in ≥3 participants, as well as any treatment-related SAE. Further details are provided in the clinical trial protocol in the Supplementary Material.

Serum samples for humoral and whole blood samples for T cell immunogenicity analyses were obtained longitudinally (see graphical abstract) and processed according to Standard Operating Procedures of the laboratory.

A validated in-house enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for immunoglobulin G (IgG) with the SARS-CoV-2 S1 subunit as the target antigen was conducted to evaluate seroconversion rates after vaccination as previously published.21 A semi-quantitative IgG multiplex coronavirus immunoassay (MULTICOV-AB) was used as previously published22,23 to measure binding to the trimeric full-length S protein (S-trimer), the S1 and S2 subunits and the receptor binding domain (RBD). In addition, the following commercially available assays were used: (i) a qualitative anti-nucleocapsid (NC) SARS-CoV-2 total immunoglobulin (IgG/M/A) assay (Elecsys Anti-SARS-CoV-2; Roche, Mannheim, Germany) with a described cutoff index (COI) for positivity of ≥1, (ii) an automated quantitative anti-S1 subunit receptor-binding domain (S1-RBD) total immunoglobulin (IgG/M/A) assay (Elecsys Anti-SARS-CoV-2 Spike, Roche) with a predefined cutoff for positivity at 0·8 U/mL, and (iii) an automated anti-SARS-CoV-2 Trimeric S IgG assay (LIAISON; DiaSorin, Sallugia, Italy) with a predefined cutoff of ≥33·8 BAU/mL. Anti-SARS-CoV-2 NAbs were analyzed using a virus neutralization test (VNT) as previously described.24

Vaccine-induced T cell immunity to SARS-CoV-2-S was measured with a standardized interferon (IFN)-γ release assay (IGRA; EUROIMMUN, Lübeck, Germany) to assess the overall response. An IFN-γ Enzyme-Linked ImmunoSpot (ELISpot) assay (ELISpotPLUS (ALP) kit, Mabtech, Nacka Strand, Sweden) was performed to measure the magnitude of T cell responses toward SARS-CoV-2-S following MVA-SARS-2-S vaccination in more detail. Four peptide pools (M1–4) of 15-mer peptides overlapping by 11 amino acids and spanning the full S protein (GenBank ID: MN908947.3) were used, with pools M1 and M2 corresponding to the S1 subunit and M3 and M4 to its S2 subunit. For details on immunological assays see the Supplementary Appendix.

Outcomes

The primary objective of this study was to assess the safety, reactogenicity, and tolerability of two dose levels of MVA-SARS-2-S, as well as of a heterologous BNT162b2 booster vaccination in a subgroup, as measured by the occurrence of solicited local and systemic reactogenicity for 7 d, unsolicited AEs for 28 d after each vaccination, changes in safety laboratory parameters from baseline, and serious AEs throughout the trial.

Secondary objectives included the induction of anti-SARS-CoV-2-S-specific IgG, as determined by anti-S1 binding antibody units (BAU) and seroconversion rates (defined as BAU >7) measured by ELISA. Exploratory objectives comprised the assessment of anti-S NAb levels, anti-RBD, anti-S-trimer and anti-S2 binding antibody titers, as well as T cell immunity following MVA-SARS-2-S and BNT162b2 vaccination.

Statistical analysis

Due to the exploratory nature of this first-in-human trial, no formal sample size calculation was performed. The sample size was determined based on prior experience with other MVA-vectored vaccine candidates and was considered sufficient to detect general differences between the groups as well as potential safety signals.13 Safety analyses were performed in the safety population, which included all participants who had received at least one dose of MVA-SARS-2-S. Immunogenicity analyses were carried out in the per-protocol population, which was defined as all individuals who had received both vaccinations with MVA-SARS-2-S. Correspondingly, in the trial extension, the safety and per-protocol populations included all individuals who had received at least one or both vaccinations with BNT162b2, respectively.

AEs were coded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) version 24.1, and are reported as preferred terms (PT). Continuous data are presented as mean, standard deviation (SD), and coefficient of variation (CV), or as appropriate, as median with minimum and maximum or interquartile range (IQR). Absolute and relative frequencies are used to summarize categorical variables. Due to the sample size, hypotheses were not tested in a confirmatory sense. For safety analyses, relative and absolute values are given. Where appropriate, and assuming a non-normal distribution of data, Mann-Whitney U and Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank tests were used to analyze unpaired and paired samples, respectively. Correlations were calculated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient and two-sided 95% confidence intervals (CI). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value of ≤·05. Results were not adjusted for multiple comparisons. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism v.10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Figures were created with GraphPad Prism v.10 and BioRender.com.

Role of the funding source

The funder of this publicly funded investigator-initiated trial had no role in the study design, the collection, analysis, or interpretation of the data; the writing of the report; or the decision to submit the manuscript for publication.

Results

Study description

This study was conducted from 7 October 2020 to 24 August 2021 (First Participant First Visit to Last Participant Last Visit). Until 5 December 2020, a total of 76 participants were screened for eligibility. Of these, 30 were enrolled and 15 were allocated to each treatment group. Of the total study population, 40% were female. The mean age was 36·5 y. Baseline demographics were similar between groups (Table S2). All participants received both MVA-SARS-2-S injections, completed the trial, and were included in both the safety and per-protocol analyses. However, 5/15 participants in the LD cohort and 6/15 in the HD cohort either had documented SARS-CoV-2 infection or received a COVID-19 vaccination other than the investigational medical product (IMP) MVA-SARS-2-S during the trial period (between V2D42 and V2D135, i.e., after completion of the vaccination phase). Documented infections occurred in 1/15 participants in each cohort, while 4/15 (LD) and 5/15 (HD) received a non-IMP COVID-19 vaccination. Of the remaining SARS-CoV-2 infection- and non-IMP vaccination-naive participants (10/15 in the LD cohort and 9/15 in the HD cohort), 7 and 5 individuals, respectively, agreed to participate in the trial extension and were enrolled between 8 June and 6 July 2021. All trial extension participants completed the trial at V4D28 and were included in the safety and per-protocol analyses. There were no major protocol deviations. Safety evaluations

MVA-SARS-2-S was well tolerated. No serious or severe AEs occurred, and the majority of IMP-related AEs were solicited (77/82) and mild (93%, 76/82). All AEs were transient (median duration <1 d, IQR 0–1 d). Overall, 73% (11/15) of the LD and 87% (13/15) of the HD cohort developed at least one AE. Following V1, 77% of the total population (23/30) reported at least one AE, whereas only 57% (17/30) reported one or more AEs after V2. A higher percentage of participants in the HD cohort reported both injection site and systemic AEs (both 73%, 11/15) compared with the LD cohort, of whom 47% (7/15) and 53% (8/15) reported injection site and systemic AEs, respectively. Injection site pain was the most common local AE, affecting 40% (12/30) of the total population, while headaches were the most common systemic AE, reported by 43% (13/30). Figure 2(a,b) provides a detailed overview of injection site and systemic AEs following V1 and V2. Longitudinal monitoring of laboratory safety markers and vital signs revealed no clinically significant safety signals. Two cases of COVID-19 occurred, one in the HD and one in the LD cohort, at V2D42 and V2D99, respectively. The COVID-19 cases were associated with the only recorded AE of Special Interest (AESI) as defined per protocol; namely, moderate ageusia and anosmia in both participants. The disease courses were mild and self-limiting without treatment.

A total of 106 related AEs were reported after booster vaccination with BNT162b2 (V3/V4, Figure 3(a,b)) compared with a total of 32 AEs in the same participants after V1 and V2 (either LD or HD). Almost all participants in the trial extension (92%, 11/12) experienced at least one AE; all eleven experienced injection site reactogenicity, and nine (75%, 9/12) experienced systemic AEs. The AEs were predominantly mild (80%, 85/106). No serious AEs were reported and only one participant experienced severe AEs, namely fatigue and myalgia at V4D1, with durations of <1 d and 5 d, respectively. Overall, the reported BNT162b2-related AEs were either listed in the Summary of Product Characteristics (SmPC)25 or otherwise expected AEs (e.g., injection-site hematoma). The AE duration was comparable to AEs following MVA-SARS-2-S (V1/V2) with a median duration of <1 d (IQR 0–1 d), and all but one AE (mild injection site pain) had resolved by V4D28. Laboratory safety markers revealed transient decreases of lymphocyte counts one day after both V3 and V4 (mean counts 1·17 × 109/L [SD 0·34] on V3D1 vs. 1·54 × 109/L [SD 0·45] on V3D0 and 0·84 × 109/L [SD 0·37] on V4D1 vs. 1·64 × 109/L [SD 0·53] on V4D0; Figure 4(a)) and, while still within the normal range, an elevation of CRP levels (mean levels 5·00 mg/L [SD 2·60] on V3D1 vs. 4·25 mg/L [SD 0·87] on V3D0 and 9·50 mg/L [SD 10·75] on V4D1 vs. 4·58 mg/L [SD 2·02] on V4D0; Figure 4(b)), reflecting biologic activity after vaccination. However, none of these changes were clinically significant and lymphocyte and CRP values returned to baseline levels by Day 7 after each vaccination (mean lymphocyte counts: 1·57 × 109/L [SD 0·43] on V3D7, 1·79 × 109/L [SD 0·51] on V4D7; mean CRP levels 4·17 mg/L [SD 0·58] on V3D7, 5·25 mg/L [SD 3·50] on V4D7). Other laboratory safety parameters and vital signs did not reveal remarkable changes after V3 or V4.

Humoral immunogenicity

None of the participants developed IgG responses to the SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid at any of the measured time points following MVA-SARS-2-S (V1D0-V2D14) and BNT162b2 (V3D0-V4D28) vaccinations (Figure S1a), consistent with the absence of cases of COVID-19 during this period.

Following MVA-SARS-2-S vaccination, SARS-CoV-2-specific binding antibody and NAb responses were overall modest, peaking at V2D14. Overall, 0% (0/15) of participants in the LD cohort and 20% (3/15 at V2D14) in the HD cohort met the secondary endpoint of anti-S1 seroconversion as measured by ELISA (Figure 5(a)). However, anti-S1 IgG increased markedly following BNT162b2 immunization. At V3D7, 29% (2/7) and 80% (4/5) of participants originally allocated to the LD and HD cohorts showed seroconversion, respectively. Following V4, anti-S-specific antibody levels further increased and peaked at V4D7 (median BAU/mL 9,144, IQR 6,516–11,772), with a seroconversion rate of 100% (12/12, Figure 5(a)). In a semiquantitative multiplex assay (MULTICOV-AB) simultaneously assessing the reactivity against spike-trimer, S1, S2 and RBD, we next measured S1-specific IgG to verify the ELISA results (Figure S1b). In this assay, increases in IgG levels were detectable from V2D0, but peak MFI fold changes at V2D14 remained overall low, with median fold changes of 1·2 (IQR 1·0–1·6) and 1·4 (IQR 1·1–3·8) in the LD and HD cohorts, respectively, compared to V1D0. In contrast, at V4D7, median fold changes of 589·3 (IQR 416·9–636·6) and 497·2 (IQR 269·9–585·8), respectively, compared to V1D0 were observed in the original LD and HD cohort participants who received BNT162b2 booster vaccinations. Results of the MULTICOV-AB assay strongly correlated with the anti-S1 ELISA (Spearman’s r = 0·8, 95% CI 0·73–0·85, p < ·0001, Figure S1c).

To elucidate additional potential targets of vaccine-induced humoral immunogenicity, we next examined antibody responses to the S2 subunit (Figure 5b), and, in two independent assays per target, S-trimer and the RBD antibody responses (Figure S1d–i). At V2D14, RBD-specific total immunoglobulin above the cutoff for positivity was detected in 7% (1/15) and 33% (5/15) of participants in the LD and HD cohorts, respectively, using the quantitative Elecsys assay (Figure S1d). The median MFI fold change of RBD-specific IgG was 1·2 (IQR 1·1–1·7) in the LD cohort and 1·3 (IQR 1·2–3·1) in the HD cohort (MULTICOV-AB assay, Figure S1e). By comparison, 100% (12/12) of participants mounted RBD-specific antibody responses following one BNT162b2 booster vaccination (V4D0) (Elecsys assay, Figure S1d) and anti-RBD IgG levels peaked at V4D7 with observed median MFI fold changes of 370 (IQR 264·6–478·6) and 297 (IQR 269·9–317·4; MULTICOV-AB assay, Figure S1e). Both assays correlated strongly (Spearman’s r = 0·8, 95% CI 0·72–0·85, p < ·0001; Figure S1f). Anti-S-trimer IgG levels above the cutoff for positivity were detected in 7% (1/15) and 20% (3/15) of participants in the LD and HD cohorts, respectively, at V2D14 with the commercial LIAISON assay (Figure S1g). At the same time point, S-trimer-specific IgG fold changes from V1D0 peaked as measured by the MULTICOV-AB assay, with median fold changes of 8·3 (IQR 4·4–16·1) and 14·7 (IQR 8·3–26·5) in the LD and HD cohorts, respectively (Figure S1h). However, following BNT162b2 booster vaccination, 100% (12/12) of participants developed S-trimer-specific antibodies that exceeded the cutoff for positivity using the LIAISON assay (Figure S1g) by V4D0, and S-trimer levels peaked by V4D7 in both assays, with median fold increases of 581·6 (IQR 377·1–636·1) and 237·9 (IQR 90·3–308·2) in the former LD and HD cohorts, respectively, compared to V1D0 in the MULTICOV-AB assay (Figure S1h). As for the S1- and RBD-specific assays, a significant inter-assay correlation was seen for the S-trimer-specific antibody assays (Spearman’s r = 0·93, 95% CI 0·9–0·95, p < ·0001; Figure S1i).

In contrast, S2-specific IgG started to be detectable by V2D0, with a median of 2·1 (IQR 1·2–3·6) and 3·8 (IQR 1·4–10·5) fold changes to baseline in the LD and HD cohorts, respectively, and further increased to 6·0 (IQR 2·9–9·8) and 5·2 (IQR 3·6–23·7) fold at V2D14, by which 100% (30/30) of participants had developed S2-specific IgG levels >1–fold (min–max 1·1–40·3 in the LD and 1·3–73·3 in the HD cohort) compared to V1D0. BNT162b2 booster vaccination further increased S2-specific IgG, albeit to a lesser extent than observed for full S-trimer- and S1-subunit-specific antibody responses, with peak levels at V4D0 (median fold change of 31·1 [IQR 23·5–50·5] and 11·9 [IQR 7·2–25·9] in the LD and HD cohorts, respectively), translating to a median overall fold increase of 4·1 [IQR 2·0–6·5] compared to V2D14.

Neutralizing antibody titers, as measured by VNT, following vaccination with MVA-SARS-2-S were only induced in one participant (HD cohort) by V2D14 (geometric mean titer [GMT] = 10, Figure 5(c)). This participant had seroconverted earliest in the S1 ELISA and had the highest anti-S1-specific antibody levels. In contrast, NAb titers increased rapidly after V3 (measured at V4D0) and 100% (12/12) of participants showed seroconversion by V4D7, with a peak median GMT of 1,121 (IQR 1,055–1,188). The induction of NAbs followed the dynamics of antibody responses observed for S1-specific antibodies closely (Spearman’s r = 0·95 [CI 0·92–0·96] for S1 IgG ELISA vs. VNT100). We did not observe sex-related differences in the levels of anti-S1- or anti-S2-specific binding antibodies or NAbs after vaccination with either MVA-SARS-2-S or BNT162b2 (Figure S2).

T cell responses

The IFN-γ release following SARS-CoV-2-S antigen stimulation is illustrated in Figure 6(a). At baseline, 0% (0/15) and 7% (1/14) of participants in the LD and HD cohorts, respectively, had IFN-γ levels detectable above the cutoff for positivity (200 mIU/mL). Among participants who were negative at baseline, 33% (5/15) in the LD cohort and 15% (2/13) in the HD cohort developed a detectable IFN-γ response following one or two MVA-SARS-2-S vaccinations. Furthermore, IFN-γ levels did not differ significantly between V2D0 and V2D28 in either cohort (LD: mean 134 vs. 188 mIU/mL, p = ·26; HD: mean 122 vs. 92 mIU/mL, p = ·07). One additional vaccination with BNT162b2 induced SARS-CoV-2-S-specific T cell responses in 100% (12/12) of participants, but this was not increased by a second BNT162b2 vaccination (mean IFN-γ mIU/mL 4,694 [V4D0] vs. 4,879 [V4D28], p = ·68).

The ELISpot assay (shown in Figure 6(b)) revealed that stimulation with a peptide pool mix (M1-M4) spanning the full S antigen induced detectable IFN-γ secretion in 33% (5/15) and 21% (3/14) of participants in the LD and HD cohorts, respectively, between V2D0 and V2D28 (Figure S3). Discussion

We report the results of a phase 1 clinical trial evaluating MVA-SARS-2-S, a COVID-19 vector vaccine candidate encoding the native full-length SARS-CoV-2-S glycoprotein in SARS-CoV-2-naive healthy adults. Primary MVA-SARS-2-S vaccination was safe but poorly immunogenic; heterologous boosting with BNT162b2 was well tolerated and induced a robust immune response.

The spectrum of reported AEs was consistent with that of other MVA-based vaccine candidates.8 Replication-defective viral vectors, such as MVA, are generally associated with safety profiles that resemble those of inactivated vaccines,26 whereas the safety profiles of replication-competent viral vectors are more comparable to those of live-attenuated vaccines.27,28 Interestingly, the absolute number and prevalence of AEs were notably lower than for the anti-MERS-CoV vaccine candidate MVA-MERS-S,13 which is based on the same MVA vector, but unlike MVA-SARS-2-S, efficiently induced both cellular and humoral antigen-specific immune responses.13 A similar observation was reported for the COVID-19 vaccine candidate V591, which is based on the replicating measles virus platform. V591 elicited suboptimal immunogenicity and showed only limited reactogenicity,29 with local AEs being more than tenfold lower compared with a chikungunya vaccine candidate based on the same platform that had elicited robust immune responses.30 These observations suggest that, beyond the vaccine platform, the immunogenicity of the antigenic insert may also contribute to vaccine reactogenicity.

Vaccine-associated enhanced respiratory disease (VAERD) has been a concern for COVID-19 vaccines, especially when constructs do not induce optimal immunogenicity. While this first-in-human trial was not powered to assess the potential risk of VAERD, the two cases of COVID-19 that occurred following MVA-SARS-2-S vaccination were both mild and self-limiting. Thus, while we found MVA-SARS-2-S to be insufficiently immunogenic, we did not find any indication that MVA-SARS-2-S vaccination increases the risk of severe disease.

Heterologous booster vaccination with BNT162b2 was not associated with any safety signals. Increased reactogenicity following the second compared with the first BNT162b2 vaccination, and the transient, not clinically significant decrease in lymphocyte counts observed, are in line with previously published clinical data for BNT162b2.1,25,31 Reassuringly, AE rates after V3 and V4 were also comparable with those reported for heterologous ChAdOx1 nCoV-19 prime BNT162b2 booster vaccinations,32 indicating that priming with MVA-SARS-2-S does not increase the reactogenicity of subsequent BNT162b2 booster vaccinations.

Both humoral and cellular immunogenicity of MVA-SARS-2-S were limited and skewed toward S2-specific responses, in contrast to the robust induction of S1- and S-trimer-dominant humoral responses, as well as NAb titers and S-specific T cells following BNT162b2 vaccination. Notably, the dynamics of S2-specific antibody responses exhibited a distinct pattern: two doses of MVA-SARS-2-S increased baseline S2-binding IgG more markedly than BNT162b2 booster vaccination, and a second dose of BNT162b2 did not further increase S2-specific IgG levels. The S2 bias may have been driven by the native S antigen conformation encoded by MVA-SARS-2-S. Indeed, subsequent in vitro experiments confirmed that the S2 subunit is more efficiently expressed on MVA-SARS-2-S-infected cells than the S1 subunit.33 Moreover, the higher evolutionary conservation of the S2 subunit within the Coronaviridae family may further contribute to this bias.34 Vaccination with MVA-SARS-2-S may have potentiated anamnestic cross-reactive S2-specific antibodies, which may require a lower activation threshold.

We did not observe that the induction of S2-specific immunity following MVA-SARS-2-S vaccination interfered with BNT162b2 booster immunogenicity. Pre-existing immunity may impact subsequent antibody responses upon revaccination and potentially skew immune responses toward previously encountered epitopes. Notably, this phenomenon, termed immune imprinting, is relevant in the context of subsequent exposure to a similar but antigenically distinct variant or vaccine (e.g., as described in the context of SARS-CoV-2 variant vaccines) and requires significant levels of pre-existing immunity.35 Neither condition applies to the MVA-SARS-2-S prime/BNT162b2 boost regimen – the antigen sequences encoded by MVA-SARS-2-S and BNT162b2 are highly similar: both vaccines encode the spike protein of the ancestral SARS-CoV-2 strain (Wuhan-Hu-1) and differ only in two proline substitutions introduced in the BNT162b2-encoded antigen, reflecting a difference in antigen conformation rather than sequence. In addition, the immune response induced by MVA-SARS-2-S was overall poor and predominantly non-neutralizing, making significant antigen clearance and competition between pre-existing immune memory and de novo responses – two mechanisms underlying immune imprinting – unlikely. Moreover, several studies have shown that heterologous boosting with highly immunogenic mRNA-based SARS-CoV-2 vaccines following priming with comparably less immunogenic vaccines – including the adenoviral-vectored19 and inactivated whole-virion vaccines36 – elicits robust antibody responses, further supporting our findings.

Across the Coronaviridae family, the S glycoprotein, which comprises the receptor-binding and fusion domains, is a highly immunogenic structure and the primary target of NAbs. Receptor binding induces a conformational shift from its prefusion to its postfusion state.37 Based on the substantial and durable immunogenicity that we observed with our unmodified MERS-CoV-S vaccine candidate, MVA-MERS-S,13–15,38 we opted to encode native SARS-CoV-2-S in MVA-SARS-2-S. However, in contrast to MVA-MERS-S, we did not observe adequate induction of immune responses following MVA-SARS-2-S vaccination. This may be attributable to differences in the stability of the MERS-CoV and SARS-CoV-2 S proteins. Unlike MERS-CoV-S, SARS-CoV-2-S contains a distinct insertion at the S1/S2 furin cleavage site, which forms a polybasic furin cleavage motif, resulting in more efficient cleavage of SARS-CoV-2-S.39 However, the poor immunogenicity observed with MVA-SARS-2-S can only be partially attributed to the encoding of an unmodified S protein. Other COVID-19 vector vaccines utilizing unmodified S have demonstrated robust immunogenicity in humans, as shown for the efficacious and licensed ChAdOx1 nCoV-19 vaccine.3 With regard to MVA-based COVID-19 vaccines, several candidates encoding either prefusion-stabilized or native S proteins have been evaluated in preclinical models and have demonstrated immunogenicity and efficacy (reviewed in40). Notably, the only other MVA vectored COVID-19 vaccine candidate, COH04S1, that has been evaluated in humans to date, also encodes an unmodified SARS-CoV-2-S.41,42 COH04S1 utilizes a synthetic MVA vector that, in contrast to MVA-SARS-2-S, expresses both SARS-CoV-2 S and N antigens. In a phase 1 trial assessing dosages comparable to those used in the present study, COH04S1 induced durable S-specific seroconversion and T cells in all participants as well as NAbs in a majority of participants.42,43 The candidate has progressed to phase 2 development in immunocompromised populations, namely as a booster vaccination in patients with chronic lymphocytic leukemia (NCT05672355) and following hematopoietic cell transplantation (HCT) and chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy (NCT04977024) in patients with hematological malignancies, with preliminary results confirming immunogenicity in this population.44

Prefusion stabilization of surface antigens has been investigated as a mechanism to optimize vaccine immunogenicity. Building on experience with vaccine candidates against RSV, HIV, and influenza,45–47 this approach has subsequently been incorporated into several COVID-19 vaccines that have demonstrated robust immunogenicity and efficacy in humans,2,4,31,48 and has since been incorporated into the design of other viral vaccine candidates.49 Based on these data and the results of the present study, we proceeded to design MVA-SARS-2-ST, a prefusion-stabilized next-generation version of our vaccine candidate. Prefusion stabilization led to increased immunogenicity of the construct in preclinical models, as well as to protection in hamsters,33 and increased immunogenicity was also observed in a subsequent clinical trial (Mayer and Weskamm et al.,50 Grewe et al.,51 NCT04895449, manuscript in preparation). A prefusion-stabilized second-generation vaccine candidate based on COH04S1, GEO-CM02, has also been found to be immunogenic in a preclinical model.52 However, a head-to-head comparison with its predecessor or clinical data for this construct have not been published to date.

This trial has several limitations. As typical for first-in-human Phase 1 trials, the sample size was small and, consistent with the exploratory nature of this trial, not based on a formal sample size calculation but considered adequate to detect frequent events and relevant safety signals. Statistical hypotheses were, therefore, not tested in a confirmatory sense. The open-label design of this first-in-human Phase 1 trial may have introduced bias for AE assessments, and the lack of a placebo or comparator group introduces complexity in the interpretation of the safety assessments. In addition, only healthy, young adults were included in the trial, limiting the generalizability of the results.

In summary, this clinical phase 1 trial did not confirm the robust immunogenicity and efficacy of MVA-SARS-2-S observed in mice.16 While the data do not support further development of this vaccine candidate, they underscore the challenges of translating preclinical data into clinical settings and highlight the importance of rapidly advancing promising vaccine candidates into clinical testing, particularly in the context of pandemic preparedness.
