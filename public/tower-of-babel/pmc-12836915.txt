Effectiveness of a controlled trial of nutrition education in primary school, ZELIAKIDE project: knowledge and mid-morning snack consumption
Authors: Maialen Vázquez-Polo, Itziar Churruca, Arrate Lasa, Gesala Perez-Junkera, Idoia Larretxi, María Ángeles Bustamante, Olaia Martinez, Virginia Navarro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe consumption of unhealthy foods has increased considerably in recent years and in Spain, the diet is increasingly moving away from the healthy and traditional patterns of the Mediterranean diet. The aim of this article is to assess the effectiveness of a nutrition education programme designed to increase knowledge regarding balanced diet and improve the nutritional quality of mid-morning snacks.MethodsA one-month school-based nutrition education programme called Zeliakide (8 sessions), aimed at children, based on previously defined competencies and learning outcomes and delivered through a STEAM methodology (Science, Technology, Engineering, Arts and Mathematics centered methodology) was designed to be followed by the intervention group. The study was a non-randomised controlled trial with two clusters. The evaluation of the programme was carried out through pre, post and retention test questionnaires. Mid-morning snacks were also evaluated. The control group was a similar group of students, but they did not participate in any activities.ResultsTwo hundred ninety-nine children participated in the study. 155 in the intervention group and 144 in the control group. After the intervention, knowledge and behaviours related to a balanced diet improved. The mean punctuation of healthy mid-morning snacks also improved from an average of 0.6 to 0.8 on a scale of 0 (unhealthy) to 1 (healthy) (p < 0.001), although the latter decreased somewhat after one month.ConclusionThe Zeliakide programme was an effective nutrition education programme to promote healthy lifestyles. However, in order to prolong its impact, nutrition education programs could be consistently taught in schools over time.Trial registrationThe trial was registered prospectively at ClinicalTrial.gov with registration number NCT05467865 on 21 July 2022.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25821-3.

Introduction

The significance of maintaining a healthy and well-rounded diet since childhood is widely recognized. A balanced diet is one that fulfils an individual's nutritional requirements, providing the appropriate quantities of each nutrient in relation to others. This supports the body's upkeep, physiological processes, and facilitates growth and development, especially crucial for children who need to physically and cognitively grow and develop [1–3]. A diverse, balanced, and healthy diet will differ based on individual requirements like age, gender, lifestyle, and physical activity level, as well as cultural influences, local food availability, and gastronomic traditions. However, the fundamental principles of a healthy diet remain consistent [3]. Research indicates that a diet rich in whole grains, vegetables, fruits, legumes, and nuts, while being low in salt, sugars, and fats (especially saturated and trans fats), offers significant health advantages [4].

The Mediterranean diet is the customary eating pattern of individuals residing near the Mediterranean Sea, especially in regions abundant with olive trees. This diet fulfils the above conditions; it primarily consists of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, legumes, nuts, seeds, and olive oil as the primary source of added fat. Additionally, it involves moderate consumption of dairy products, limited intake of fish and poultry, and minimal consumption of red meat [5, 6].

The diet of a large part of the Spanish population, especially children and adolescents, is changing from the traditional Mediterranean diet to the consumption of processed foods and a decrease in the consumption of fruits and vegetables [7–10]. In a global study by Imamura et al., it was observed that the consumption of unhealthy foods and nutrients has increased considerably in recent years [11].

Moreover, beyond the three main meals, such as breakfast, lunch and dinner, snacking is very common in various parts of the world. Especially in Spain, where the midday meal is taken relatively late, as is the evening meal, mid-morning snacking is very common among children and young people [12–14]. Mid-morning snacks and mid-afternoon snacks have been found to be good facilitators for obtaining the necessary nutrients [15, 16]. However, concern regarding the nutritional quality of snacks has grown, as excessive consumption of foods and beverages high in energy, sugars, saturated fats and highly processed foods and beverages has been reported [17–21].

Following a balanced and healthy diet is important for the entire population, including people suffering from pathologies [22, 23]. In certain digestive pathologies, e.g. celiac disease, the diet is somewhat different from that of the general population [23]. Even so, it has been observed that the nutritional deficiencies that are common in the celiac population are also common in the general population [22, 24–26], so that healthy eating recommendations should be aimed at people without pathologies as well as at people with pathologies.

Recent studies have pointed out that encouraging healthy eating attitudes in schools could be the key to improving diet [27–32]. Eating habits are established in childhood and if established properly contribute to the prevention of chronic diseases such as obesity, diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, cancers and respiratory diseases, among others [33].

In order to address these nutritional deficiencies and establish good eating habits from childhood, nutrition education is necessary. Nutrition education is defined as any combination of educational strategies aimed at facilitating the voluntary adoption of food choices and other food-related behaviours that support health and well-being [34, 35].

Thus, many health education interventions, specifically nutrition education, tend to focus on children, as it is in childhood when eating habits and lifestyles are learned and established; moreover, they tend to focus on the school setting [36–39]. In addition, with the intention of achieving behavioural outcomes, the STEAM educational approach is nowadays being incorporated into nutrition education programmes. STEAM is based on the need to focus on Science (S), Technology (T), Engineering (E), Arts (A) and Mathematics (M) in a cross-curricular and interdisciplinary way to transform the teaching and learning process into integrated and creative processes [40–42]. For example, a study by Bayles et al. found that this educational approach can contribute to improving fruit and vegetable consumption in pre-school children [42].

Therefore, it is believed that a nutrition education programme, aimed at school children, through a STEAM methodology could be useful in improving their knowledge and behaviour on healthy and balanced diets. For that reason, the aim of this work was to assess a nutrition education programme, concretely analysing a) the increase of knowledge about balanced diet and b) the improvement of the nutritional quality of mid-morning snacks during the intervention and after a month.

Materials and methods

Zeliakide is a one-month (4 weeks, 8 sessions) intervention programme, two-armed cluster, non-randomized and controlled trial, conducted among 10–12 years old children with a pre and post intervention evaluation and 1 month follow-up to assess its effectiveness.

The overall objective of the programme is to increase the knowledge and improve the attitudes of the general population, in particular children, in order to promote the inclusion of people with celiac disease in society. To understand the complexity of celiac disease and its treatment, the gluten-free diet, the general population must first understand the importance of a balanced diet, not only in theory but also in practice, as this is the basis of the gluten-free diet. Therefore, one of the main specific objectives of the Zeliakide project, which is presented in this paper, is to increase knowledge about a balanced diet and to improve the nutritional quality of children's mid-morning snacks.

The program was designed to address eight specific competencies and their respective learning outcomes. The chosen teaching methodology was a STEAM methodology named Inquiry Based Learning (IBL), which promote learning through interactive activities and experiments. Its detailed protocol was previously published and it was registered in clinicaltrials.gov with registration number NCT05467865 on 21 July 2022 [43]. This article focuses at the first three competences and their respective learning outcomes, which were those related to the balanced diet (Table 1). The remaining competences and learning outcomes will be published in the near future.

Activities description

To achieve the competences and learning outcomes described in Table 1, the children carried out three activities. All activities were guided by members of the research team, together with the assistance of the school teachers.

Game about the food pyramid: A large poster with a picture of an empty pyramid was placed on the front wall of the classroom. The pyramid was divided into tiers. Pictures of different foods from different food groups were handed out. The pupils were asked to place their food items on the pyramid. The rest of the class had to show agreement or disagreement with the placement of the foods. Once all the foods had been placed on the pyramid, posters were handed out indicating different consumption frequencies, which were also to be placed on the pyramid.

Complete and adapt a 24-h recall: The children were asked to write on a sheet adapted from a 24 h recall the foods and quantities they had consumed the previous day (they did this task at home, with their parents). The following day in class, they had to identify the different food groups and see how many portions they had consumed from each group. At the end, they had to compare it with the recommendations, identify dietary strengths, improvements to be made, and draw conclusions.

Healthy snacks challenge: During the intervention period, students were grouped and required to record daily their mid-morning snacks in a designated section of a workbook. At the end of each week, research team members, along with the children, evaluated the snacks and assigned scores accordingly: 1 point for healthy snacks (such as fruits, natural dairy products, dried fruits, and vegetables), 0.5 points for moderately healthy lunches (in addition to the aforementioned foods, included some unhealthy items), and 0 points for unhealthy lunches (like processed pastries, cookies, sugary drinks, etc.). At the end of the programme, the group with the highest score was proclaimed the winner.

It is important to note that parents were involved in the activities, for example, in completing the 24 h recall. The children did another small activity at home: they had to look at three food advertisements that appeared in various media such as TV or social networks, even in offline media, and discuss their nutritional quality.

Participants

The study involved children between the ages of 10 and 12 who were in the final two years of primary school. To be eligible to participate in the study, the children needed to attend a primary school that was used to project-based learning and had an interest in joining the project. Furthermore, the schools needed to have at least one class in either the fifth or sixth grade. The number of students needed to detect a small–medium effect size was previously calculated [43]. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects involved prior to the start of the study. Schools, teachers and parents/guardians of the children agreed to participate in the study by signing an informed consent form.

The study was approved by the University of the Basque Country, UPV/EHU, Ethics Committee for Human Research (M10/2020/081, 01/07/2020). Participation in the study did not involve any risk for the participant; the only associated risk was the use of their time.

Data collection

Participants were asked to complete a questionnaire before and after the intervention to assess the effectiveness of the program (pre and post questionnaires respectively). Additionally, they were required to complete a third questionnaire one month after the end of the intervention (RT -retention-questionnaire) to evaluate if they had retained the knowledge and behaviour acquired. The questionnaire can be found in the Supplementary file 1.

Control group

The control group was comprised of a comparable number of students as the intervention group, attending the same school, and sharing similar socio-demographic traits, despite being in their final year of the academic cycle. They continued with their regular school curriculum with no intervention. Students in the control group were requested to complete a questionnaire (just once) during the same month when the intervention was being conducted for the other group. Control group mid-morning snacks were measured once, on the same day they completed the questionnaire. Members of the research group evaluated and assigned the snack scores following the same criteria established for the intervention group.

Statistical analysis

The open-ended responses were categorized [46]. One judge generated categories to classify the responses and two different judges classified the responses into the previously established categories. When consensus between the two evaluating judges was very high (K-value higher than 0.7), the classification of the categories was considered correct. The category of the answers in which the two assessors had different classifications was agreed upon. The categories ranked the answers on a scale from 0 to X (the maximum number depends on the categories set by the judges). 0 would be the least correct answers and X would be the most correct answers. Some of the responses were classified as "student knows the answer" or "student does not know the answer" because the scale achieved from the categorisation was 0–1. The categorised responses are indicated in the results tables. The judges were part of the research team.

The statistical analysis was carried out using the SPSS statistical package. A descriptive analysis of the socio-demographic variables, the response variables of the questionnaire and the punctuations of the healthy snacks challenge was carried out. The distribution of the data was non-normal. To compare pre, post and rt questionnaires, Wilcoxon tests were performed for quantitative responses, McNemar test for dichotomous qualitative variables, and Friedman and Marginal Homogeneity tests for qualitative variables with more than two response options. Differences between the pre, post and rt questionnaires were considered significant when p < 0.05.

To compare the results of the intervention group with the control group, Mann–Whitney U test for quantitative variables and Chi-square for qualitative variables were performed.

The scores obtained in the healthy snacks challenge were described as mean and standard error. The Wilcoxon test was used to compare mean punctuations at different times and Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare the mean punctuations with the control group mean punctuation.

Results

Sample characteristics

Two hundred ninety-nine children participated in the study, 155 in the intervention group and 144 in the control group. In the sample, 147 were girls, 149 boys, and 3 were identified with another gender.

The mean age was 10.1 ± 0.3 in the intervention group and 11.5 ± 0.5 in the control group. The intervention group consisted of children in the 5th grade of primary school and the control group consisted of children in the 6th grade of primary school. The control group consisted of children in a higher grade than the intervention group, as described before. Besides, all children were from the same school and a similar socio-economic background (data not shown).

Students were aware of the importance of a healthy diet before and after the intervention. They declared that their intake of healthy snacks at school improved after the intervention, but yet they did not improve their consumption of unhealthy foods during the weekend.

Once the intervention was over, participants were asked if they felt they were able to improve their diet with what they had learned in the sessions. Results are shown in Fig. 1. This question was included to complete the previous one in C2 in which it was detected if participants proposed changes to improve their diet. In this case, it was pretended to measure whether they felt capable of achieving it. It is notable that the results of both questions were in line.

In order to evaluate possible changes in their diet, they were also asked whether they had consumed more or less of different food groups (dairy products, fruits, vegetables, meat, fish, pulses, cereals, sweets) during the month of the intervention. It is noteworthy that the majority of the participants (53–70%) reported a healthy increase in fruit, vegetables and cereals consumption and a decrease in the consumption of sweets. The percentage of participants who self-reported having made unhealthy changes is considerably lower (< 24%). Detailed results can be seen in the Table 5.

Healthy snacks challenge

In addition to students-reported data, healthy snacks were measured over the duration of the intervention. Snack data were collected before starting the challenge (the first week of the intervention), three more times (once per remaining week) during the challenge and one month after the intervention.

Before starting the challenge, the daily mean number of healthy snacks, being 0 unhealthy and 1 healthy, was near 0.6 and the last week it exceeded 0.8 (Fig. 2), reaching statistically significant difference. One month after the intervention, it decreased slightly to 0.76. Discussion

In recent times, it has been evident that the majority of the population does not adhere to a nutritious and well-rounded diet. Embracing healthy eating practices, alongside other positive lifestyle choices, can lower mortality rates and slow down the deterioration of health. Therefore, promoting good health is crucial [11, 47–49]. Studies have shown that nutrition education can enhance dietary habits, emphasizing the importance of creating programs focused on nutrition education to facilitate this improvement [50–55].

This article presents the nutrition education programme called Zeliakide, which has several objectives, two of which are presented in this paper: to increase primary school children's knowledge of a balanced diet and improving the choice of healthy mid-morning snacks.

To effectively educate the general population, including children, it is essential to build upon their existing knowledge. This is particularly crucial when it comes to children [56–58]. In the Autonomous Region of the Basque Country, the primary school curriculum focuses on dietary concepts during the last cycle of primary education, which typically includes children aged 10–12 years (grades 5 and 6) [59].

The design of the intervention is based on competences and learning outcomes carefully defined previously according to Bloom's taxonomy, related to the level of cognitive abstraction [43, 44]. Although some efforts have been made in competency-based nutrition education, to the best of our knowledge these studies have focused on undergraduate studies and not on primary school children [60]. As mentioned before, this paper presents results of the competences related to the balanced diet: C1, C2 and C3.

The Zeliakide programme was able to increase knowledge of the distribution of food in the food pyramid (C1). In Spain, the food pyramid proposed by the Spanish Society of Community Nutrition (SENC) is currently the reference for healthy and balanced nutrition [61]. The pyramid shows the food groups, which, depending on their place in the pyramid, indicate the recommended frequency of consumption. Knowing the frequency of food consumption is necessary, as the frequency of consumption ensures the intake of nutrients in the proportions necessary to achieve an optimal health state. Furthermore, the food pyramid proposed by the SENC is adapted to Mediterranean dietary patterns and advocates the traditional diet (the Mediterranean diet) and other aspects such as a varied, local, sustainable and balanced diet, among others [61]. This traditional Mediterranean diet has proven to be one of the healthiest diets in the world [6, 62].

On the other hand, regarding C2, participants improved their ability to identify errors in their diet and after the intervention; the majority (81.3%) were able to propose ideas to improve their diet. Being able to propose improvements in the diet is the highest level of abstraction according to Bloom (level 6, assessment). The acquisition of this level of abstraction is complicated, so the fact that the vast majority were able to acquire it is a remarkable result [44] and may be due to the active methodology proposed in Zeliakide programme. Various authors have emphasized the importance of children understanding which foods to prioritize for consumption and which to limit in order to make informed dietary choices. Without this knowledge, children may struggle to make conscious decisions about their food intake [63–66]. For example, in the study by Hamilton-Ekeke et al. they concluded that children will make ‘healthy choices’ if they have the knowledge to support these ‘healthy choices’ [63]. In the study by Kandiah et al. they found that children who acquired more nutritional knowledge through a nutrition education programme were more compliant with dietary guidelines and dietary recommendations [64]. Lin et al. also found that children with better nutritional knowledge expressed a more positive nutritional attitude, considered their eating behaviour more frequently and had a better quality diet [66]. Additionally, it is crucial for children to recognize that a significant portion of food advertisements promote unhealthy options, as research indicates that such advertising can influence children to consume less healthy foods [67–69].

In C3, self-reported behaviour was assessed, whether participants reported to consume less of the foods at the top of the pyramid. Participants reported bringing healthier snacks to school during and after the intervention. Moreover, 60% indicated that they consumed less sweets and 70.3% consumed more fruit after the intervention. In addition to the data reported by the participants, the researchers assessed the children's snacks before, during and after the intervention, concluding that the intervention was able to improve the number of healthy snacks. However, another question was included in the questionnaire to assess whether the participants decreased their consumption of unhealthy foods during the weekend, where it was observed that this did not happen. During the intervention, there was specific and in-depth work on healthy snacks, explaining to the children why it was important and implementing the change through the “healthy snacks challenge”. In contrast, there was no change in the consumption of unhealthy foods during the weekend, as there was no particular emphasis on changing it, and other factors such as teamwork and peer pressure did not work. It has been seen, and also confirmed by other authors, that in order to achieve a change in attitude it is necessary to explain to people why they have to make a change and how to do it [70–72]. Furthermore, a systematic review by Thakur et al. concluded that in order to change eating behaviour in children and adolescents, nutrition education programmes need to be more holistic and not only seek to increase knowledge [73]. An example of this is the study by Joseph et al., who showed that a nutrition education intervention aimed at getting children aged 3–5 years to improve their healthy snacking behaviour improved their intention to choose healthy snacks but did not improve their real snack choices [74]. In the case of young adults, such as university students, it has also been observed that knowledge about healthy eating was not sufficient to choose healthy snacks [75]. Even if mid-morning snack is only a small part of the food consumed in a day, it can be the reflection of the rest of the diet, and its importance has been underlined in previous studies. Muthayya et al. showed that their consumption was linked to improved memory performance in school-age with low socioeconomic status children [76] and Almoraie et al. found it to be linked to an adequate impact on body weight and disease risk [77].

However, even if the intervention demonstrated to be effective in the short term, it is important to assess the level of remaining effectiveness it had with time. One month after the intervention, skills and learning outcomes were reassessed to see if the intervention was effective in the medium-term. It was found that although knowledge occasionally decreased one month after the intervention, it remained higher than before the intervention. Unfortunately, although participants felt that their snacks were healthier than before the intervention, the researchers' evaluation found that this was not the case. This underlines the importance of evaluating the intervention in the medium term [78–80], as this allowed us to realise that it would be interesting to carry out post-intervention recall activities in order to maintain the attitudes acquired during the intervention. In another study by Arufe et al., where several sessions led by health professionals were conducted to promote healthy snack choices in children, they also observed that the nutritional quality of the snacks decreased sometime after the end of the sessions [81]. In order to maintain the improvement in healthy snacking after the end of the intervention, it would be interesting to carry out reminder sessions, involve families in the appropriate choice of snacks or even encourage initiatives that facilitate access to healthy snacks in the school, and outside [82].

To comment on some of the weaknesses of the study, it has to be said that it has only been carried out in a single school and therefore in a single city. It would be interesting to expand it to other schools and cities. Furthermore, the control group only completed the questionnaire once, in order to minimize their consumption of time and disruption of their teaching process, and it was one year older. Nevertheless, both the students in the intervention group and the control group covered the topics in their regular school curriculum that corresponded to nutrition, except for the Zeliakide programme, carried out only in the intervention group. It is noteworthy that having a control group to compare results with is not frequent in this kind of interventions; therefore it provides more robustness and reliability to the study. In addition, questionnaires were not validated. However, several pilot sessions were carried out in previous years and they had been adapted in format and content to the capabilities and nature of children. On the other hand, it should be noted that one of the strengths of the study is that the Zeliakide project has focused on the childhood, a period in which children are gaining maturity but have not yet fully developed their cognitive abilities. This characterises their learning process and implies adapting the methodology and assessment tools to obtain robust results. Zeliakide stands out for being school-centred, based on the school curriculum and focusing on pedagogical learning through competences and learning outcomes [74, 82].

Conclusions

The first results of the Zeliakide project showed that the programme is effective in increasing knowledge related to a balanced diet, and it is also effective in improving the nutritional quality of mid-morning snacks and so, in promoting healthy lifestyles and moving a little closer to Mediterranean dietary patterns. Although the knowledge acquired through the programme is maintained, special care must be taken with behaviour in order to extend the consumption of healthy mid-morning snacks over time. Furthermore, for the health of the future population and to promote a healthy lifestyle, nutrition education programmes such as this one could be taught in schools on an ongoing basis.
