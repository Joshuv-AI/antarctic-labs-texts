Low-toxicity functionalized photopolymer for high-efficiency reflection holography with humidity response
Authors: José Carlos García-Vázquez, Manuel G Ramírez, Belén Nieto-Rodríguez, Tomás Lloret, Mireia Serrano-Beltrá, Inmaculada Pascual
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractReflection holograms stored in functionalized photopolymers offers a powerful approach to optical humidity sensing. In this work, unslanted volume gratings were recorded in Biophotopol, a low-toxicity, water-soluble acrylate-based photopolymer functionalized with 2-methacryloyloxyethyl phosphorylcholine (MPC). After controlled humidification, these gratings reached diffraction efficiencies above 70%, representing the highest values reported for environmentally friendly, non-commercial materials. The addition of MPC enhanced water uptake and produced controlled swelling, which modulated grating period, optical thickness, and Bragg wavelength. Spectral reconstruction, refractive index measurements, 3D profilometry, and structural modeling based on Kogelnik’s Coupled Wave Theory confirmed a strong correlation between MPC concentration and humidity-induced structural and optical changes. The dynamic humidity response and opto-mechanical hysteresis under severe hygroscopic stress (up to 80% relative humidity) were also evaluated. The kinetic analysis and hysteresis cycles revealed that while higher MPC concentrations increase sensitivity and water uptake, they also prolong water retention via hydrogen bonding, inducing an opto-mechanical lag during the desorption regime. All these advances position MPC-functionalized Biophotopol as a highly promising material for the future development of holographic humidity sensors, linking sustainable photopolymer design with applied holography in sensing technologies.Keywords: Functionalized photopolymer, Reflection holography, Humidity sensing, Swelling behaviorSubject terms: Chemistry, Engineering, Materials science, Optics and photonics

Introduction

The development of holographic volume gratings has received considerable attention in recent years due to their versatility in a wide range of applications, including healthcare1–3, security4, renewable energy5,6, and biosensing technologies7. Holographic techniques enable the creation of sensors capable of detecting various chemical analytes, such as glucose8, trypsin9, lactate3, and volatile gases10,11. In particular, holographic sensors have emerged as powerful tools for detecting chemical and physical stimuli, such as humidity and temperature variations12–14, with high sensitivity and real-time response capabilities15. These sensors operate by exploiting changes in the optical and structural properties of the materials used to store holograms, which are translated into measurable shifts in diffraction efficiency (DE), Bragg wavelength (), refractive index modulation (), and grating period ()16.

Previous studies have reported the influence of humidity on the optical performance of photopolymer-based holographic gratings. In some cases, this effect has been exploited for the development of holographic humidity sensors, where variations in relative humidity produce measurable changes in Bragg wavelength and grating period due to polymer swelling. For example, Naydenova et al. analyzed the humidity and temperature response of reflection holograms recorded in acrylamide-based photopolymers12, and color-changing holographic elements for visual humidity indication have also been reported17. In other contexts, humidity has been identified as a source of instability in holographic recordings intended for optical applications. Moisture absorption can induce grating swelling and shifts in the Bragg wavelength, leading to changes in the spectral response of the holograms. Humidity-induced variations in the Bragg angle and wavelength of slanted and volume photopolymer gratings have been described in the literature18, and general reviews on holographic sensors also acknowledge the sensitivity of photopolymer holograms to environmental humidity19.

Among the various materials available for hologram storage, photopolymers offer distinct advantages. Particularly, volume gratings recorded in these materials do not require chemical post-processing, making them suitable for real-time monitoring applications. Humidity response of photopolymers can be tailored through compositional design, allowing their optimization for different sensing or stability-oriented applications. By adjusting the chemical formulation, parameters such as water uptake, swelling behavior and the resulting optical response of the recorded gratings can be controlled. The water absorption capacity of these materials directly influences parameters such as swelling ratio (SR) and optical thickness (), which in turn modulate the diffraction properties of the stored gratings.

In this study, reflection volume gratings were recorded in Biophotopol, a modified water-soluble acrylate-based photopolymer. Biophotopol20 is a low-toxicity, environmentally friendly material that has been previously optimized for high-frequency reflection holography21,22. Although it has shown excellent performance in various holographic applications, its hygroscopic response must be enhanced for effective use in sensing applications23. One of the initial objectives was to optimize the concentration of triethanolamine (TEA), a radical initiator in holographic materials. The TEA-dye pair acts as a photoinitiator system that generates free radicals under light exposure. Additionally, due to its hydroxyl groups, TEA can interact with water molecules through hydrogen bonding, which may modulate the material’s swelling behavior18,24.

Beyond TEA, the incorporation of 2-methacryloyloxyethyl phosphorylcholine (MPC), a zwitterionic, biocompatible methacrylate monomer25–27, was also investigated. MPC features a highly polar phosphate group (PO43−), which enables strong interactions with water molecules and significantly alters water absorption and diffusion dynamics within the polymer network. MPC’s methacrylate group allows it to covalently bind to sodium acrylate (NaOA), integrating into the polymer matrix and enhancing water retention, thereby influencing swelling, thickness variation, and refractive index modulation. These changes are expected to improve the photopolymer response to variations in relative humidity (), making it a more effective material for holographic humidity sensing.

To assess the effects of MPC on Biophotopol optical and structural behavior, a series of spectral reconstructions, 3D profilometry measurements, and refractive index evaluations were conducted at different levels. In this work, the focus is placed on understanding the impact of MPC functionalization on the hygroscopic and structural–optical response of the material, rather than on the full calibration of a humidity sensor. For this reason, the environmental conditions were deliberately restricted to a narrow RH interval in order to isolate the effect of chemical composition on the holographic response. These experimental observations were further analyzed using Kogelnik’s Coupled Wave Analysis (KCWA)28 to quantify the influence of MPC concentration on key parameters such as refractive index modulation and diffraction efficiency. A strong correlation was found between the MPC/NaOA molar ratio and the swelling ratio29, leading to significant variations in and . Notably, higher MPC concentrations resulted in improved diffraction efficiency after humidification, exceeding 70%, a substantial enhancement over standard Biophotopol formulations. To the best of our knowledge, these are the highest diffraction efficiency values reported for reflection gratings recorded in low-toxicity, non-commercial photopolymers21,22,30. Furthermore, hydration caused shifts in Bragg wavelength and structural reorganization within the polymer network, confirming the critical role of MPC in tailoring the material response to environmental humidity. Finally, to assess the robustness and practical viability of the holographic sensor, the dynamic humidity response, absorption/desorption kinetics, and opto-mechanical hysteresis were evaluated under severe hygroscopic stress by subjecting the material to broader humidity cycles (up to 80% RH). These results underscore MPC-functionalized Biophotopol as a promising candidate for advanced optical humidity sensing applications.

Photopolymer fabrication process

The chemical composition of Biophotopol has been extensively analyzed in previous studies21. The average refractive index of the photopolymer without MPC was after bleaching, measured with an Abbe refractometer at 589 nm under controlled conditions (C, RH = 60%). This water-soluble photopolymer is based on a poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) matrix. TEA acts as both a sensitizer and a plasticizer. Sodium acrylate (NaOA) is used as the main monomer, and 5’-riboflavin monophosphate (RF) serves as a blue-sensitive dye. N, -(1,2-dihydroxyethylene)bisacrylamide (DHEBA) is incorporated as a bifunctional monomer to improve crosslinking, enhancing the reticulation of the polymer chains.

MPC was added to the selected formulations. When incorporated, MPC acts as a hygroscopic agent within the polymer chains. Compared to sodium polyacrylate and DHEBA alone, the resulting polymer structure includes phosphate groups that promote water interaction. Testing photopolymer layers in a humid environment

Modified Biophotopol layers were exposed to high-humidity environments at a constant temperature of C, both before and after the holographic recording. Prior to exposure to the recording setup, all samples were conditioned for several minutes in an environment at C and RH = 60%. This pre-equilibration ensured that the photopolymer reached a stable state relative to its surroundings, and allowed us to attribute any subsequent optical changes solely to the influence of the photopolymer chemical composition, particularly the concentration of MPC.

After this stabilization step, the reflection gratings were recorded and cured as described previously. Initial spectral reconstruction was performed immediately after curing, still at C and RH = 60%. These measurements established a reference state of the recorded grating under controlled humidity.

To evaluate the total humidity response, the samples were then transferred to a second controlled environment set at C and RH = 70-80%, where they were kept for 24 h. This post-reconstruction conditioning stage was designed to induce a controlled and measurable variation in the structural and optical properties of the gratings. The 24 h conditioning time was chosen to ensure that the photopolymer layers reached an equilibrium hydration state. Optical measurements reported after humidification were performed under this condition. Changes in were obtained for these two different RH values. Moreover, DE, and were monitored and directly correlated to the amount of MPC incorporated into the photopolymer formulation for RH = 70%. For each Biophotopol composition, three independent samples were prepared and characterised under identical conditions. The reported optical parameters represent averaged values, with uncertainties due to samples variability.

This two-stage approach (baseline measurement at RH = 60% and evaluation after 24 h at RH = 70%) enabled a precise analysis of water absorption and swelling behavior due to MPC, while avoiding confounding effects such as uncontrolled shrinkage or additional polymerization during handling. The RH values were intentionally limited to 60%, 70% and 80% for reconstruction stage. This narrow interval was selected to ensure controlled and reproducible hydration while isolating the contribution of MPC to water uptake and swelling, and to avoid irreversible effects that may arise at more extreme humidity conditions.

Moreover, humidification cycles of 60% 80% 60% RH were performed for studying hysteresis effects in the hydration/desorption regimes of the holographic sensing process by comparing the Bragg wavelength and swelling ratio.

Results and discussion

To structure the analysis of the results, the study was conducted in two main phases. First, the effect of varying TEA concentration on the formation of high-efficiency reflection gratings was investigated, with the aim of evaluating its potential influence on the hygroscopic behavior of Biophotopol. Once the TEA concentration was fixed, a second stage focused on the incorporation of MPC as a hygroscopic agent and its progressive increase in the formulation. This phase includes a comprehensive analysis of diffraction properties under controlled humidity, as well as an evaluation of swelling dynamics through gravimetric measurements, thickness profiling, and refractive index studies. The last section presents the results of this second phase, emphasizing the structural and optical changes induced by water uptake in MPC-functionalized Biophotopol layers.

At this point, it became essential to understand the mechanism behind this MPC-driven efficiency enhancement. For reflection volume holograms, the diffraction efficiency is governed by a squared hyperbolic tangent dependence on the product of , according to KCWA. This means that the observed increase must be associated with an increase in this product. Changes in diffraction efficiency may be attributed to variations in , , or their combined effect. This can be explained by polymer diffusion processes activated under humid conditions.

During the post-exposure curing stage, the entire Biophotopol layer polymerizes, stabilizing the recorded grating. However, within the holographic structure, the bright fringes form denser and more crosslinked regions than the dark fringes. In the dark zones, polymer chains are shorter and the viscosity is lower, facilitating greater chain mobility. Under higher humidity, especially in MPC-rich formulations, this mobility increases due to plasticization by absorbed water.

As a result, short polymer chains diffuse from the dark to the bright fringes, driven by a concentration gradient. This redistribution enhances the refractive index modulation across the grating and, consequently, increases the diffraction efficiency. Temperature variations are also known to affect the optical response of volume holographic gratings through thermal expansion of the polymer matrix and changes in the average refractive index. These effects were minimised by maintaining a constant temperature, allowing the humidity-induced response to be isolated.

Dynamic humidity response and hysteresis under high hygroscopic stress

The previous sections focused on characterizing the fundamental effect of MPC functionalization on the optical properties of the material when a humidity range of 60–70% RH was imposed. In this section the dynamic performance and hysteresis under a wider hygroscopic stress were evaluated. To test the robustness and reproducibility of the functionalized photopolymer, the dynamic range was expanded up to 80% RH.

Before analyzing the real-time dynamic behavior, the static optical response of the sensor was evaluated across this broader humidity range. Figure 13 shows the maximum reached at equilibrium as a function of the MPC/NaOA molar ratio for 60%, 70%, and 80% RH.

Bragg wavelength as a function of the MPC/NaOA molar ratio for three different RH values at equilibrium. The lines represent guides for the eye. The error bars represent the statistical error obtained from the dispersion among independent samples.

As observed, the sensitivity of the material to humidity increased drastically at higher RH levels. Specifically, when the RH was raised to 80%, two distinct effects were observed: first, the slope of the linear dependence of the Bragg wavelength on the MPC/NaOA concentration became significantly more pronounced; second, the absolute Bragg wavelength shift exhibited a non-linear behavior with respect to the humidity steps. The Bragg wavelength increment from 70 to 80% RH was considerably bigger than the shift observed from 60 to 70% RH, being even larger for highly functionalized samples. This nonlinear enhancement highlighted the massive water uptake capacity of the functionalized material under high humidity conditions, which significantly increased the hygroscopic stress on the polymer network.

To understand how this massive water uptake affected the sensor kinetics, the temporal evolution of were monitored under step-change humidity cycles. Initially, the samples were subjected to a RH change from 60 to 80%. Once equilibrium was reached, at which the value remains practically constant, a desorption process was carried out to 60% RH.

To ensure reliability, this single complete cycle was performed on 6 different samples for each composition (M1, M4, and M7), with the resulting dispersion represented by the error bars in Fig. 14a. As observed in this figure, when the RH steps up to 80%, the polymer matrix absorbed water and swelling effects appeared, causing a redshift in the Bragg wavelength. Conversely, during the desorption step, the matrix suffered shrinkage effects, and entered into a blueshift regime, demonstrating good reproducibility across different samples.

To further investigate the absorption and desorption kinetics, the time derivative of the Bragg wavelength (ddt) was analyzed (Fig. 14b). Rather than just the absolute peak values, the rate of variation of this derivative provided crucial insights into how the MPC concentration governs the water exchange dynamics. During the hydration regime (60% to 80% RH), the sample without MPC (M1) exhibited a sharp initial rate but rapidly decelerated, with its derivative returning to zero shortly. This suggests a fast water uptake that saturated quickly. The behavior of M4 samples during cycles was relatively similar, with a higher slope in the hydration regime. In contrast, samples with higher MPC concentrations (M7) sustained a positive rate of wavelength shift for a significantly longer time. This prolonged swelling process indicated that water molecules continuously interact with the abundant hydrophilic groups due to MPC presence. This enhanced water uptake capacity was translated into a larger total Bragg wavelength shift at equilibrium, reaching approximately 47 nm for M7, compared to 41 nm and 35 nm for M4 and M1, respectively (Fig. 14a).

A inverse, kinetic behavior was observed during the desorption regime (80–60% RH). The water retention capacity of the MPC became clearly evident in the relaxation range of the derivative. While M1 rapidly suffered shrinkage and its derivative quickly reached zero, M7 exhibited a prolonged negative tail, meaning the shrinkage rate decayed much more slowly. Again, M4 samples showed an intermediate desorption behavior, with higher shrinkage rate than M7. This demonstrated that water molecules were retained within the highly functionalized polymer network due to a greater number of hydrogen bonds formed with the more abundant MPC groups. This effect delayed the structural relaxation of the matrix compared to the samples with lower MPC content. Despite slower water loss rate, the extreme swelling experienced by M7 samples during the 80% RH step induced minor irreversible structural changes. As observed at the end of the cycle, the final Bragg wavelength for M7 fell slightly below its initial starting value. To study these structural changes, hysteresis behavior of the material was analyzed by plotting versus swelling ratio and obtaining the opto-mechanical hysteresis (, area between hydration and desorption processes) areas during the hydration and desorption regimes (Fig. 15). The hysteresis cycles revealed a deviation between the hydration and desorption evolution. This effect indicated an optical lag relative to the physical swelling of the matrix.

The pronounced widening of the hysteresis cycle graphically visualized the previously discussed kinetic behavior: the abundant hydrophilic MPC groups strongly retain water via hydrogen bonding, significantly delaying the structural contraction of the polymer network relative to the mass loss. Furthermore, these plots clearly depict an interesting phenomenon for M7 samples at the end of the desorption cycle. Although the desorption regime concluded with a swelling ratio above 0.2% (indicating a total mass increase due to strongly bound residual water), these samples exhibited a negative residual Bragg wavelength shift of approximately 6 nm. This behavior can be explained by the Bragg condition and the physical properties of the retained water. The highly hydrophilic MPC groups permanently bind a certain amount of water molecules within the free volume of the polymer matrix. While this trapped water increases the total final mass, it does not significantly expands the grating period once the macroscopic swelling has decreased. However, as water possesses a significantly lower refractive index than the dry photopolymer, its retention effectively lowers the average refractive index of the layer. Consequently, the combination of a recovered grating period and a reduced refractive index yields the observed slight negative baseline drift in the Bragg wavelength.

Conclusions

In this work, phase volume reflection holograms were recorded in a water-soluble, acrylate-based photopolymer whose hygroscopic properties were modified by incorporating MPC into its formulation. To evaluate the influence of TEA on Biophotopol water absorption capability, the relationship between diffraction efficiency and Bragg wavelength as a function of TEA concentration was analyzed. Beyond the T2 formulation, neither parameter showed significant changes. These results suggest that, for this concentration range, TEA has a limited effect on the photopolymer’s hygroscopic behavior.

In contrast, the incorporation of MPC into the photopolymer notably improved its sensitivity to humidity, making it highly suitable for holographic sensing applications. MPC addition significantly enhanced water uptake, which induced structural changes in the recorded gratings, including modifications in optical thickness and grating period. These changes directly impacted both the Bragg wavelength and the diffraction efficiency.

Notably, MPC-containing formulations achieved the highest diffraction efficiency ever reported for reflection gratings recorded in low-toxicity photopolymers, with DE values exceeding 70%. Higher MPC concentrations led to greater expansion of the photopolymer under humid conditions, as evidenced by the strong correlation between swelling ratio and the MPC/NaOA molar ratio.

Furthermore, Kogelnik’s Coupled Wave Analysis enabled a detailed assessment of how MPC influences refractive index modulation and diffraction performance. The hydration process at produced substantial changes in the optical response of the gratings, particularly in formulations with higher MPC content. These findings confirm MPC’s key role in regulating the photopolymer sensitivity to ambient humidity.

In addition, the dynamic performance and robustness of the functionalized photopolymer were evaluated under severe hygroscopic stress (cycling between 60 and 80% RH). The kinetic analysis demonstrated that higher MPC concentrations accelerate the initial water uptake but significantly delay the desorption process due to strong hydrogen bonding between the retained water molecules and the hydrophilic MPC groups. Consequently, the opto-mechanical hysteresis cycles revealed a wider optical lag for highly MPC-functionalized samples, along with a minimal negative baseline drift of approximately 6 nm after the desorption regime. This minor residual shift is completely explained by the lowering of the average refractive index caused by the strongly bound residual water, combined with a slight structural compaction of the grating period in the axial direction. Despite this, the sensor exhibited excellent reproducibility, confirming the high viability and robustness of this low-toxicity material for continuous humidity monitoring.

Overall, the results demonstrate that MPC-functionalized Biophotopol is a promising candidate for robust holographic-based humidity sensors. Future work will explore the performance of these reflection gratings in real-world sensing applications and the combined influence of humidity and temperature on the holographic response to enable fully compensated sensing operation.
