Precise diagnosis of small invasive pulmonary nodules driven by single-cell immune signatures in peripheral blood
Authors: Yang Xia, Yin Zhu, Sai Zhang, Mo Zhou, Jiaqi Zhou, Guanxin Xu, Baiqin Zhao, Guofei Zhang, Qiyuan Wang, Bin Wang, Zexin Chen, Hongyu Shi, Xiuning Le, Tony Mok, Wei Chen, Junqiang Fan, Weiwei Yin, Wen Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Early detection of lung cancer is crucial for improving patient outcomes. However, accurately diagnosing invasive pulmonary nodules and predicting tumor invasiveness remain major clinical challenges. Given the established role of immune dysfunction in cancer development, we hypothesize that peripheral immune profiling could provide a strategy for managing pulmonary nodules. In this multi-center, prospective study, we combine peripheral immune profiling via mass cytometry with machine learning algorithms to develop an integrated pulmonary nodule management platform. This platform accurately distinguishes invasive from non-invasive pulmonary nodules (AUC = 0.952), outperforming established clinical and radiomics-based models. Furthermore, it effectively predicts tumor invasiveness, differentiating minimally invasive from invasive adenocarcinoma (AUC = 0.949), thereby offering valuable guidance for surgical decision-making. In conclusion, the platform demonstrates substantial clinical utility and holds significant promise as a precision tool for future management of pulmonary nodules.

Introduction

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths around the world. Approximately 48% of lung cancer patients present with distant metastases at the time of diagnosis, with a 5-year relative survival rate of only 8%1. However, early-stage lung cancer offers a significantly better prognosis, with a 5-year survival rate of 62% for patients diagnosed in the early stages. Yet, early-stage lung cancer is detected in only 20% of cases2. As such, improving the early diagnosis of lung cancer is critical to reducing mortality.

The U.S.-based National Lung Screening Trial (NLST) and The Dutch–Belgian lung-cancer screening trial (NELSON) have demonstrated that low-dose computerized tomography (LDCT) for lung cancer screening reduces the mortality compared to chest X-ray or no screening3–5. However, these trials also highlighted a major limitation: a high false-positive rate, ranging from 56.5% to 96.7%6. Furthermore, the clinical effectiveness and cost-benefit ratios of LDCT remain uncertain, particularly for detecting small and subsolid nodules7. Existing predictive models, such as the Mayo Clinic8, Veterans Affairs (VA)9, and Brock10 models, which estimate the malignancy probability of pulmonary nodules based on radiological features, also show unsatisfactory performance (AUC = 0.65–0.83)11–15. Additionally, under current US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) guidelines, individuals under 50 years of age and non-smokers are excluded from screening, despite an increasing incidence of lung cancer among younger adults, particularly young, non-smoking women16–18. This underscores the urgent need for more inclusive and effective screening strategies, particularly for high-risk populations outside the traditional screening criteria.

Although the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 8th edition classified adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS) as Tis (stage 0) for clinical staging purposes, the 5th edition of the World Health Organization (WHO) classification of lung cancer has categorized atypical adenomatous hyperplasia (AAH) and AIS as non-invasive disease from the pathology point-of-view due to their indolent nature. Additionally, accumulating evidence suggests that minimal invasive adenocarcinoma (MIA) differs from invasive adenocarcinoma (IA), with MIA exhibiting less regional metastasis and a better prognosis. Consequently, sub-lobar resection is recommended for MIA patients without systematic lymph node dissection, while the surgical approach for IA patients should be determined based on clinical factors19. Preoperative distinction between MIA and IA is, therefore, of significant clinical importance.

Liquid biopsy is an emerging diagnostic approach with great promise for the early detection of lung cancer, offering the advantages of minimal invasiveness and ease of accessibility. Platforms based on circulating tumor cells (CTCs), circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), methylation signatures, and microRNAs have been explored as potential biomarkers for lung cancer diagnosis20. Notably, these methods are typically designed for high specificity and often suffer from limitations in sensitivity, particularly in the diagnosis of small, subsolid, or early-stage nodules. Diagnostic accuracy for stage I non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) remains suboptimal, ranging from 41% to 67%21–25, and none of these diagnostic models are capable of distinguishing between MIA and IA.

The tumor microenvironment (TME) undergoes dynamic changes during tumor development, and cancer-related immune alterations are often accompanied by systemic immune dysregulation26,27. For example, the neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio (NLR) has emerged as a potential prognostic marker for predicting survival outcomes in lung cancer28,29. A recent study found significant differences in the absolute counts of red blood cells, platelets, neutrophils, and lymphocytes in the peripheral blood of healthy individuals and cancer patients, while the monocyte counts remained similar. Moreover, machine learning approaches have identified distinct immunotypes, helping to classify patients based on therapy responses30. However, specific alterations in peripheral immunity during the early stages of pulmonary nodules remain poorly understood, and their potential role in the diagnosis of pulmonary nodules has not been thoroughly explored.

Mass cytometry (CyTOF) is a powerful technology that enables the detection of multiple markers at single-cell resolution, allowing for precise immunophenotyping of cell clusters, comprehensive analysis of intracellular communication networks, and exploration of functional interactions between cell subsets31,32.

In this work, we utilize CyTOF to map the peripheral immune landscape of patients with pulmonary nodules. Using artificial intelligence algorithms, we develop a nodule management platform, SMART (Single-cell Immune Signatures for Invasive Pulmonary Nodules), which facilitates the precise diagnosis of small invasive pulmonary nodules. The SMART platform consists of two modules: the Diagnostic Decision (DIA) module, which distinguishes invasive nodules from non-invasive ones, and the Surgical Decision (SUR) module, which predicts tumor invasiveness. Together, these modules provide an integrated and accurate strategy for managing pulmonary nodules, incorporating both malignancy diagnosis and invasiveness prediction.

Baseline characteristics of DIA

When comparing the invasive and non-invasive groups, we find that most clinically relevant characteristics, such as sex, smoking status, and nodule location, are well-balanced (Table 1). However, the average nodule size was significantly larger in the invasive group compared to the non-invasive group. Additionally, the proportion of pure ground-glass nodules is significantly higher in the invasive group. Upon focusing on the pathologically confirmed cases, we observe that nodule sizes and proportions of sub-solid nodules are more comparable between the invasive and non-invasive groups (Supplementary Table 4).

Peripheral immune atlas for invasive pulmonary nodules

To explore the peripheral immune characteristics between invasive and non-invasive samples, we performed CyTOF analysis using a pre-designed marker panel (Supplementary Data 1). Figure 3A shows the t-SNE plots, identifying 10 major immune subsets based on typical lineage marker expression (Supplementary Fig. 1). These subsets include γδT cells, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, double-negative T (DNT) cells, NK (natural killer) cells, B cells, dendritic (DC) cells, granulocytes, basophils, and monocytes. Cell density plots reveal distinct distributions between the invasive and non-invasive groups across these immune subsets (Fig. 3B). A comparison of the proportion of cells shows that the percentage of CD4+ T cells, DCs, basophils, and monocytes is significantly increased in the invasive group compared to the non-invasive group. Conversely, γδT cells, CD8+ T cells, and NK cells are significantly reduced in the invasive group (Fig. 3D), suggesting distinguishable peripheral immune features associated with early-stage lung cancer. Establishment and testing of DIA module

The SMART platform, developed for the precise diagnosis of small invasive pulmonary nodules driven by AI-mined single-cell immune signatures, consists of two modules. The DIA module is designed to predict the malignancy of pulmonary nodules based on immune features identified in our cohort. The development of this module utilized samples only from the training cohort, which included 178 non-invasive and 218 invasive pulmonary nodules.

Eighteen immune features were selected for the DIA module, with CD278 (ICOS)+ CD8+ T cells being the most prominent subset (Supplementary Table 1). The risk score for each sample was calculated based on these immune features. In the training cohort, the risk score for the invasive group is significantly higher than that of the non-invasive group (0.766 vs. 0.295, p < 0.001, Fig. 5B). In the testing cohort 1, the DIA module maintains diagnostic performance, achieving an AUC of 0.952 (95% CI: 0.932–0.973), sensitivity of 90.8%, and specificity of 91.1% (Fig. 5C, Supplementary Table 2). A significant difference in the risk score between invasive and non-invasive groups is also observed (0.321 vs. 0.666, p < 0.001, Fig. 5B), further validating the robustness of the DIA module.

In comparison with three commonly used clinical prediction models (Mayo, VA, and Brock models), the DIA module demonstrates significantly superior performance, with higher AUCs in both the training cohort (AUC = 0.983 vs. 0.733–0.840, P < 0.001) and testing cohort 1 (AUC = 0.952 vs. 0.649–0.858, P < 0.001) (Fig. 5C, Supplementary Table 2). To further benchmark our model’s performance against radiomics-based approaches, we compared SMART with DeepWise’s Dr. Wise platform, which has demonstrated robust diagnostic performance in differentiating invasive pulmonary nodules34. The SMART platform demonstrates higher AUC values than Dr. Wise in both the training cohort (AUC = 0.983 vs. 0.821) and testing cohort 1 (AUC = 0.952 vs. 0.855) (Fig. 5C).

To evaluate its generalizability, we assessed the DIA module’s performance on testing cohort 2, which included healthy controls and advanced lung cancer cases. The module demonstrates high accuracy, with an AUC of 0.944 (95% CI: 0.907-0.981), sensitivity of 83.3%, and specificity of 95.0% (Fig. 5C). Notably, the risk scores increase progressively from healthy controls to non-invasive pulmonary nodules, then to MIA, IA, and advanced lung cancer cases (Fig. 5B), suggesting the potential for pre-screening applications.

We performed a subgroup analysis for non-invasive pulmonary nodules, which were often suspected to be invasive before surgery. In this subgroup, the DIA module outperforms traditional clinical models, achieving an AUC of 0.933 (95% CI: 0.899–0.967), compared to AUCs of 0.610–0.650 for the Mayo, VA, and Brock models (Fig. 6B). This indicates that the DIA module has significant potential to reduce unnecessary surgeries and overtreatment for non-invasive pulmonary nodules that are clinically suspected to be invasive (Supplementary Tables 3, 4). Notably, in this challenging subgroup, the SMART platform demonstrates superior diagnostic performance compared to the Dr. Wise system (AUC = 0.933 vs. 0.619; specificity = 86% vs. 22.8%) (Fig. 6B, Supplementary Table 4). Another clinically challenging subgroup comprises nodules with size less than 10 mm. DIA module exhibits consistent and robust performance in regardless of the nodule size, including nodule size ≤10 mm (AUC = 0.948, sensitivity = 88.0%, specificity = 91.9%) and nodule size >10 mm (AUC = 0.941, sensitivity = 92.7%, specificity = 83.3%) groups (Fig. 6C, D, Supplementary Table 6). According to the US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) guidelines, annual lung cancer screening with LDCT is recommended for individuals aged 50 to 80 years with a 20 pack-year smoking history who currently smoke or have quit within the past 15 years. Hence, to evaluate the feasibility of our DIA module in non-screening populations for LDCT, we assessed its performance in both screening and non-screening populations. The module performs exceptionally well, with AUCs of 0.946 and 0.966 for the two populations, respectively (Fig. 6E, F).

We then analyzed non-smokers (766 patients) and individuals under 50 years old (303 patients). The DIA module maintains high predictive capability with AUCs of 0.965 for non-smokers and 0.968 for individuals under 50 years old (Fig. 6G, H).

Additionally, further subgroup analyses of the DIA module were conducted, including the nodule size, type, location as well as the patient sex and age. Our DIA module has demonstrated superior and robust performance across these subgroups (Fig. 6A, Supplementary Table 5). These results collectively suggest that the DIA module can accurately and robustly distinguish between non-invasive and invasive pulmonary nodule samples, regardless of the stage or nodule type.

Establishment and testing of SUR module

The SUR module is the second component of the SMART platform, designed to predict the malignancy grade of labeled invasive samples. The development of this module utilized invasive samples from the training cohort, which included 113 patients with MIA and 105 patients with IA. The final version of the module selected 14 peripheral immune features (detailed in the Methods section and Supplementary Table 8). The performance of the SUR module is subsequently evaluated using invasive samples from Testing Cohort 1, comprising 111 MIA and 106 IA patients (Table 2). The SUR module exhibits promising results in distinguishing between MIA and IA samples, achieving an AUC of 0.983 (95% CI 0.967–1.000) in the training cohort, and an AUC of 0.949 (95% CI 0.920–0.978) in Testing Cohort 1. Both sensitivity and specificity are high, with a sensitivity of 95.2% and specificity of 94.7% in the training cohort, and a sensitivity of 91.5% and specificity of 90.1% in the testing cohort (Fig. 7B, C, Supplementary Table 9). The clinicians’ accuracy is ranged from 67.7% to 75.6%, significantly lower than the performance of the SUR module. Baseline characteristics of SUR

We also performed subgroup analyses to assess the performance of the SUR module across various factors, including nodule size, type, location, patient sex, and age (Fig. 7D, Supplementary Table 10). The SUR module shows comparable performance in pure ground-glass nodules and part-solid nodules, with AUCs of 0.926 (95% CI: 0.874–0.978) and 0.934 (95% CI: 0.874–0.994), respectively (Supplementary Table 10). Part-solid nodules have a higher risk score than pure ground-glass nodules (0.594 vs 0.392, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 10). The module also demonstrates consistent diagnostic capabilities across different nodule sizes (≤10 mm: AUC = 0.947, 95% CI: 0.905–0.989; >10 mm: AUC = 0.918, 95% CI: 0.846–0.990) (Fig. 7E, F, Supplementary Table 10), as well as in both smokers (AUC = 0.955, 95% CI: 0.891–1.000) and non-smokers (AUC = 0.948, 95% CI: 0.915–0.980) (Supplementary Table 10). Nodules with papillary, micropapillary, and solid pathological patterns, which are generally associated with a worse prognosis, present significantly higher risk scores than other pathological patterns (0.692 vs 0.455, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 10).

Discussion

This study presents a prospective proof-of-concept validation of a peripheral immune profile-based diagnostic platform, SMART, for the diagnosis and management of small pulmonary nodule. By combining single-cell proteomic profiling via CyTOF with machine learning algorithms, SMART offers a non-invasive, high-resolution diagnostic tool that enhances clinical decision-making with both precision and scalability. The platform demonstrates robust performance across large, multi-cohort evaluations, including populations typically underrepresented in routine screening, such as non-smokers and individuals under 50 years of age, underscoring its potential to address key limitations in current diagnostic strategies.

Our comprehensive peripheral immune atlas revealed key immune distinctions between invasive and non-invasive pulmonary nodules. Invasive patients exhibited activated immune signatures, including elevated ICOS⁺CD8⁺ T cells35, known to promote tissue-resident memory T cell (Trm) differentiation and sustain anti-tumor activity36. Similarly, increased follicular helper T cells (Tfh) in invasive patients may facilitate tertiary lymphoid structure formation, promoting anti-tumor immune responses37. Conversely, non-invasive patients exhibited a more quiescent immune profile, with elevated CD28⁻CD8⁺ and CD27⁻CD8⁺ T cells lacking co-stimulatory molecules, indicative of immune hypoactivation38,39. Within the invasive group, further stratification by disease stage showed that MIA patients retained stronger effector immune features, whereas IA patients exhibited immunosuppressive signatures, including increased CD28⁻ and CD57⁺CD8⁺ T cells, suggestive of terminal differentiation and immune exhaustion40,41. In contrast, MIA patients showed enrichment of CD197⁺, CD45RA⁺, CD8⁺ central memory T cells and γδ T cells, both implicated in direct tumor suppression42,43. However, the concordance with intratumoral states likely varies by context, warranting validation in paired peripheral blood and tumor datasets before being considered surrogates. To explicitly test whether peripheral features recapitulate intratumoral programs, we will prioritize paired analyses in future cohorts by integrating peripheral blood and tumor immune signatures. Prespecifying these analyses will help determine when and to what extent peripheral immune features provide mechanistically informative and clinically actionable signals.

SMART platform achieved high diagnostic accuracy, with AUCs exceeding 0.9 across both DIA and SUR modules, outperforming the traditional models (AUC = 0.952 vs. 0.649–0.858) and even the radiomics-based Dr. Wise platform (AUC = 0.952 vs 0.855) (Fig. 5C, Supplementary Table 2). Unlike conventional liquid biopsy-based tools, which often trade off sensitivity for specificity21–25, particularly in stage I NSCLC, SMART maintained high sensitivity (90.9%) and specificity (90.8%) simultaneously. This balance minimizes missed diagnoses and reduces unnecessary interventions. Moreover, decision curve analysis further confirmed SMART’s clinical utility, showing superior net benefit over both traditional models and radiomics approaches across nearly all threshold probabilities (Supplementary Fig. 9). Notably, SMART platform outperformed traditional models in pathologically confirmed group (AUC = 0.933), compared to AUCs between 0.610 and 0.650 using existing approaches, offering clinically relevant and actionable insights across real-world scenarios.

SMART platform also serves as an auxiliary diagnostic tool for young individuals and non-smokers groups typically not covered by regular LDCT screening44, despite their rising lung cancer risk16. We observe an increasing trend in risk scores from healthy controls to non-invasive groups and finally to invasive groups, suggesting that the SMART platform may be applicable in pre-screening scenarios. When we tested SMART’s feasibility as a screening tool for these two groups, SMART achieves high accuracy (AUC 0.968 for under 50 s, 0.965 for non-smokers). These results suggest that SMART could be a potential alternative to LDCT screening, though further prospective validation in independent populations is necessary.

A particularly challenging diagnostic setting is the evaluation of small nodules (diameter ≤10 mm), where traditional models perform suboptimally (AUC < 0.8; sensitivity/specificity ranging from 50% to 80%)21,24,45,46. Our platform demonstrated diagnostic performance for these small nodules (AUC = 0.948, sensitivity 88.0%, specificity 91.9%, Supplementary Table 6). Furthermore, we notice that, within our cohort of 351 pathologically confirmed small nodules (≤10 mm), 229 were malignancies (65.2%), including 60 cases of IA (17.1%). The platform also achieved an AUC of 0.949, sensitivity of 90.8%, and specificity of 87.7% in this pathologically confirmed small nodule subgroup (Supplementary Table 7), underscoring its strength in high-stakes, low-volume detection scenarios.

Beyond binary malignancy classification, SMART also supports nuanced clinical decision-making by distinguishing invasive from minimally invasive adenocarcinomas. This distinction is vital for guiding surgical strategies, such as sub-lobar versus lobar resection, thereby avoiding overtreatment. The SUR module accurately differentiated IA from MIA (AUC = 0.949, sensitivity = 91.5%, specificity = 90.1%). While prior models rely heavily on CT-based metrics (e.g., nodule size, solid component proportion)47–49, we showed that even small (≤10 mm) or pure ground-glass nodules can harbor IA (26.2% and 30.7%, respectively), reinforcing the need for immune-based assessments. SMART demonstrated an AUC of 0.962 in this subgroup, offering a more reliable, biology-informed metric of invasiveness.

We acknowledge several limitations. Firstly, our lung cancer cohort was primarily composed of NSCLC patients, predominantly adenocarcinoma, which reflects real-world clinical scenarios. In our testing cohort 2, which included a diverse array of lung cancers (28 adenocarcinomas, 26 squamous cell carcinomas, 8 small cell carcinomas, and 4 other types of lung tumors), the SMART platform still demonstrated high accuracy. Second, while CyTOF provides deep immune profiling, its cost and operational complexity may limit routine use. We are actively working to reduce these barriers through cost optimization and streamlined panel validation in prospective cohorts. Third, given that our development and validation were based on an Asian population, additional studies are required to evaluate performance across other ethnicities, as immune signatures may vary geographically. Finally, while we highlight the power of immune profiling, molecular alterations such as EGFR mutations, which influence systemic immunity50, were not assessed. Ongoing studies aim to integrate genomic and immunologic data to enhance predictive precision and guide targeted therapy.

In conclusion, SMART represents a patient-centric, multi-functional diagnostic platform for pulmonary nodule management. It offers high diagnostic accuracy, minimal invasiveness, and broad applicability, particularly for small nodules and underserved populations. SMART stands to significantly advance the paradigm of lung cancer detection and management.

Methods

Participant cohorts

The study was approved by the institutional review board (IRB) or independent ethics committee at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine, Jiaxing First Hospital, and Huzhou Central Hospital. The IRB granted a waiver for informed consent for participants whose residual blood from routine examinations was used, while informed consent was obtained for participants requiring additional blood sampling. Sex and gender are not particularly considered in the current study.

This prospective study recruited 1032 individuals from three medical institutions: the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine, Jiaxing First Hospital, and Huzhou Central Hospital, between December 2021 and March 2024. The inclusion criteria were: (i) age ≥18 years; (ii) patients with pulmonary nodules scheduled for surgical resection; (iii) individuals with pulmonary nodules that had remained stable for at least 3 years; or (iv) individuals with pulmonary nodules ≤4 mm in diameter. The exclusion criteria included: (i) history of cancer within the past 5 years; (ii) acute infections within the previous 2 weeks; (iii) use of drugs that affect peripheral blood composition (e.g., recombinant human erythropoietin, interleukins) within the previous 2 weeks; (iv) blood transfusion within the past 6 months; (v) decompensated organ dysfunction; (vi) immunodeficiency syndromes; (vii) hematologic disorders; (viii) immunosuppressive therapy; and (ix) recurrent tumors.

After sample collection and processing, 102 samples were excluded due to either insufficient immune cells for CyTOF analysis or an unclear final diagnosis. A total of 930 participants were included in the final analysis, consisting of 369 samples from non-invasive pulmonary nodules, 435 samples from invasive pulmonary nodules (224 MIA, 211 IA), 60 healthy controls without pulmonary nodules, and 66 patients with advanced lung cancer. Pathological diagnoses were made according to the 5th edition of the WHO classification of lung cancer. Consistent with WHO classification, we classified AAH and AIS as non-invasive due to their indolent biology and minimal metastatic risk, thus requiring accurately differentiating these nodules from the invasive ones to prevent overtreatment. Healthy controls without pulmonary nodules were diagnosed using CT imaging, while non-invasive pulmonary nodules were diagnosed based on the following criteria: (i) pulmonary nodules with a diameter ≤4 mm or (ii) pulmonary nodules >4 mm in diameter that had been stable for at least 3 years.

Sample collection and processing

Peripheral blood samples (2 mL) were collected in EDTA anticoagulant tubes. Samples were transported to the laboratory within 12 h at room temperature or within 48 h at 4 °C. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from whole blood by density gradient centrifugation using Ficoll-Paque. The PBMCs were resuspended in 5 mL of pre-cooled fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) buffer (1× phosphate-buffered saline [PBS] supplemented with 0.5% bovine serum albumin), followed by centrifugation at 400 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was resuspended in FACS buffer. PBMCs were counted and assessed for viability, ensuring a minimum of 3 × 10⁶ cells and a viability rate greater than 85% before CyTOF analysis.

CyTOF staining and data analysis

CyTOF staining and data analysis were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A panel of 40 metal-conjugated antibodies was selected to identify cell types, with detailed information on the antibodies provided in Supplementary Data 1. PBMCs were washed with PBS and stained with 0.5 mM Cisplatin (Standard BioTools, 201194) to label dead cells, followed by Fc receptor blockade. Cells were incubated with the antibody cocktail for 30 min, washed, and fixed in 200 µL of intercalation solution overnight. After fixation, cells were washed, resuspended in distilled water, and 20% EQ beads (Standard BioTools, 201078) were added for mass cytometry analysis (Standard BioTools, PLT-MC601).

FCS files were normalized based on the EQ beads51, and then de-barcoded52 to obtain the individual FCS file for each sample. The FlowJo software (BD Biosciences, FlowJo X 10.0.7) was used to exclude debris, dead cells, and doublets, retaining only live, single immune cells. The PARC (Phenotyping by Accelerated Refined Community-partitioning) clustering algorithm was used to classify cells into clusters, which were then manually merged into 10 major immune subsets based on their typical lineage markers, including CD3, CD4, CD8, CD14, CD19, CD56, CD123, γδ TCR and HLA-DR. Given the large sample size, 20,000 cells were randomly sampled from each FCS file for PARC clustering. It was implemented in Python (3.0) with a resolution set as 0.8 and other parameters set as default. For visualization, 1000 cells from each FCS file were randomly sampled, and dimensionality reduction was performed using t-SNE (t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding)53.

Given the large number of enrolled samples and to maximize the utility of all single-cell information obtained from each sample, we also applied manual gating, performed using FlowJo software (BD Biosciences, FlowJo X 10.0.7), to extract detailed immune differences across groups (Supplementary Data 2). The manual gating strategy with the predefined immune subsets for one sample (Supplementary Fig. 2) was then used as a template for automatic gating applied to the other samples using a probability state modeling (PSM) theory-based method33. We employed dedicated packages in R 4.2.3 (dplyr and tidyverse packages) and Python 3.0 (pandas and numpy packages) to perform the automatic gating. To validate the effectiveness and accuracy of the automatic gating method, 30 samples were randomly selected for analysis by both manual and automatic gating, showing highly consistent results (Supplementary Fig. 7).

Model construction

The SMART platform is a multi-task system consisting of two modules: the DIA module for early diagnosis of pulmonary nodules and the SUR module for guiding surgical decision-making.

As depicted in Fig. 2, a total of 930 qualified samples were included in the analysis. It included 60 healthy controls without any pulmonary nodules, 369 with non-invasive pulmonary nodules, 435 with invasive pulmonary nodules for surgical resection, and 66 with advanced lung cancers. Among these, samples with non-invasive and invasive pulmonary nodules were used for model construction and model testing. And the corresponding training cohorts and the testing cohorts were divided according to the chronological order of enrollment. Specifically, individuals recruited from December 2021 to February 2023 formed the training cohort, while those recruited from March 2023 to March 2024 formed the testing cohort. As a result, in the DIA module, the training cohort included 178 non-invasive pulmonary nodules and 218 invasive pulmonary nodules, totaling 396 samples, while the testing cohort (named as testing cohort 1) included 191 non-invasive pulmonary nodules and 217 invasive pulmonary nodules, totaling 408 samples. The SUR module included only invasive pulmonary nodules from the training cohort for model training, consisting of 113 MIA and 105 IA samples. Testing cohort 1 for the SUR module included 111 MIA and 106 IA samples. Additionally, an independent testing set (named as testing cohort 2), which comprised 60 healthy controls without pulmonary nodules and 66 untreated locally advanced or metastatic lung cancer patients, was utilized for independent DIA model testing. The clinical information for these datasets is summarized in the Table 1.

The DIA and SUR modules were independently constructed following identical procedures encompassing feature selection and model construction, implemented in Python 3.0 and R version 4.2.3. Both modules were built exclusively on immune features derived from CyTOF data without incorporating any clinical or radiological parameters. We considered various immune features, including the frequencies of identified major immune subsets, the frequencies of refined immune subsets, and the marker expression of individual samples. To identify the most important features, we implemented a rigorous feature selection process, which was a supervised machine learning approach based on the Random Forest (RF) algorithm, exclusively using the training cohort. The RF algorithm for feature selection was implemented utilizing dedicated packages in R (dplyr and tidyverse packages) and Python (scikit-learn package). These ensemble of decision trees inherently models complex interactions among immune features, which linear methods may miss. For each module, candidate immune features were first evaluated through an embedded RF-based feature importance assessment. Specifically, we randomly sampled 80% of the training cohort to build an RF model, and the feature importance was assessed using 10-fold cross-validation. This process was repeated for 1000 times, generating 1000 feature importance scores for each feature. Features that consistently showed a high importance score (importance score >0.01 for over 350 times) were selected for the final model construction. The final RF model was then developed using these selected features from the training cohort, employing the scikit-learn package in Python. To ensure the model robustness, we applied 10-fold cross-validation and used a grid search method to optimize the model’s hyperparameters. Finally, the model’s predictive performance was evaluated using the testing cohort, which was not involved in the feature selection as well as the model construction process. Additionally, an independent testing cohort 2 was additionally utilized to assess the DIA model’s capability for classifying the healthy controls and advanced lung cancer patients.

For each module, a risk score was calculated separately for each participant. It ranges from 0 to 1, representing the collective probability assessed by the ensemble of decision trees within the final RF model, instead of a parametric statistical output. Higher values indicate a greater likelihood of a positive label (malignancy in DIA module and IA in the SUR module). Thus, this risk score serves as a quantitative metric to facilitate the stratification of individuals and inform clinical decision-making.

Statistics & reproducibility

Study Design and Sample Size

: This is a prospective, multi-center cohort study designed to develop and validate the SMART platform based on peripheral immune signatures. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. A total of 102 out of 1032 recruited participants were excluded from the final analysis due to unqualified samples or uncertain diagnosis. The assignment of participants to the training or testing cohort was based on the chronological order of enrollment. Therefore, randomization and binding are not applicable in our study.

Statistical Analysis

Data were presented using box plots, with the central mark representing the median and the edges of the box representing the 25th and 75th percentiles. Whiskers corresponded to 100% coverage of the data, which were approximately normally distributed. Statistical comparisons were performed using the unpaired Student t-test (two-sided) and the Mann-Whitney U-test (two-sided). Chi-square tests were applied to analyze baseline characteristics. The area under the curve (AUC) was used to evaluate the diagnostic performance of the prediction models. Data analysis was conducted using SPSS software (V.24; IBM) and GraphPad Prism (V 9.0). Sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated as follows:

Sensitivity = True Positive / (True Positive + False Negative)

Specificity = True Negative / (True Negative + False Positive)

Accuracy = (True Positive + True Negative) / (True Positive + False Positive + True Negative + False Negative)

Positive Predictive Value, PPV = True Positive / (True Positive + False Positive)

Negative Predictive Value, NPV = True Negative / (True Negative + False Negative)

Model comparison

To benchmark the performance of our SMART Platform, we conducted comparative analyses against widely used models based on CT imaging features and clinical parameters. The specific models evaluated include:

The Mayo Clinic model includes 3 clinical and 3 radiographic variables8:

(1) Probability of malignancy = ex/(1+ex)

(2) x = −6.8272 + (0.0391 × Age) + (0.7917 × Cigarettes) + (1.3388 × Cancer) + (0.1274 × Nodule diameter) + (1.0407 × Spiculation) + (0.7838 × Upper lobe).

The VA model includes 3 clinical and 1 radiographic variable9:

(1) Probability of malignancy = ex/(1+ex)

(2) x = −8.404 + (2.061 × Smoke) + (0.779 × Age/10) + (0.112 × diameter) – (0.567 × years quit/10).

The Brock model includes 3 clinical and 7 radiographic variables10:

(1) Probability of malignancy = ex/(1+ex)

(2) x = −6.7892 + (0.0287 × Age) + (0.6011 × Sex) + (0.2961 × Family history of lung cancer) + (0.2953 × Emphysema) + (−5.3854 × Nodule size transformation) + (−0.1276 × Nodule type) + (0.3770×Nodule type) + (0.6581×Nodule location) + (−0.0824 × Nodule count per scan) + (0.7729 × Spiculation).

Nodule size transformation = ((Nodule size /10)−0.5) − 1.58113883 (Nodule size was measured in millimeters).

This clinically validated radiomics system has demonstrated robust diagnostic performance in pulmonary nodule assessment, as previously reported in Radiology34.
