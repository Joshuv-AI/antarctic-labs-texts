Gaussian fluctuations for the nonlinear stochastic heat equation with drift
Raluca M. Balan, Michael Salins

Abstract
In this article, we prove the Quantitative Central Limit Theorem (QCLT) for the spatial average of the solution of the nonlinear stochastic heat equation with constant initial condition, driven by space-time Gaussian white noise in dimension 1. The novelty is that the equation contains a drift term. We assume that the drift and diffusion coefficients are twice differentiable with bounded first and second order derivatives. For the proof, we use Malliavin calculus, and the second-order Poincaré inequality due to [ 32 ] . To estimate the moment of the second Malliavin derivative of the solution, we develop a novel estimate for the product of two heat kernels, which is of independent interest. Finally, we provide the functional result corresponding to this CLT.

Introduction

In the recent years, there has been a lot of interest in examining the asymptotic behaviour
as
R\to\infty
of the
spatial average
:

F_{R}(t)=\int_{|x|\leq R}\big(u(t,x)-\mathbb{E}[u(t,x)]\big)dx

associated to the mild solution
\{u(t,x);t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
of a stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE), interpreted as a random field, in the Walsh-Dalang sense
[
33
,
12
]
.
When the noise is spatially homogeneous and the initial condition is constant, for any fixed
t>0
the solution
\{u(t,x)\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
is strictly stationary. With little additional effort, using the latest developments based Malliavin calculus (see e.g.
[
10
]
), it can also be proved that
\{u(t,x)\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
is spatially ergodic. Hence, by
the mean ergodic theorem, the following law of large numbers holds:

\frac{1}{R}F_{R}(t)\to 0\quad\mbox{a.s. and in $L^{2}(\Omega)$},\ \mbox{as $R\to\infty$}.

A natural question is whether the family
\{F_{R}(t)\}_{R>0}
also satisfies a central limit theorem. To investigate this, a novel approach was proposed in
[
20
]
, combining Malliavin calculus with Stein’s method for normal approximation. While this methodology was extensively developed in the monograph
[
24
]
, its first application to SPDEs appeared in the seminal work
[
20
]
.

The original method of
[
20
]
was developed for the
stochastic heat equation
(SHE):

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}(t,x)+\sigma\big(u(t,x)\big)\dot{W}(t,x),\quad t>0,x\in\mathbb{R}

(1)

with initial condition
u(0,x)=1
, where
\dot{W}
is the space-time Gaussian white noise and
\sigma
is a globally Lipschitz function. We recall that the solution of (
1
) satisfies:

u(t,x)=1+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{t-s}(x-y)\sigma\big(u(s,y)\big)W(ds,dy),

where the stochastic integral is interpreted in the Itô sense, and
G
is the heat kernel:

G_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\exp\left(-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2t}\right),\quad t>0,x\in\mathbb{R}.

The main result of
[
20
]
, the so-called
Quantitative Central Limit Theorem
(QCLT) states that for any
t>0
,

d_{TV}\left(\frac{F_{R}(t)}{\sigma_{R}(t)},Z\right)\lesssim R^{-1/2},

(2)

where
d_{TV}
is the total variation distance,
\sigma_{R}^{2}(t)=\mathbb{E}[F_{R}^{2}(t)]
and
Z
is a standard normal random variable. Moreover, the authors of
[
20
]
showed that
\sigma_{R}^{2}(t)\sim R
. Here, we write
a_{R}\lesssim b_{R}
, if
a_{R}\leq Cb_{R}
for all
R>0
, and
a_{R}\sim b_{R}
if
\lim_{R\to\infty}a_{R}/b_{R}\in(0,\infty)
.
We recall that
d_{TV}
is one of the metrics used for proving convergence in distribution, and is defined as follows: for any random variables
X
and
Y
,

d_{TV}(X,Y)=\sup_{B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})}\big|\mathbb{P}(X\in B)-\mathbb{P}(Y\in B)\big|,

where
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
is the class of Borel subsets of
\mathbb{R}
.

Subsequently, the QCLT problem has been studied for other models, possibly in higher dimensions. We recall the most important contributions below.

The case of (SHE) with Gausssian noise, which is white in time and colored/rough in space was studied in
[
21
]
, respectively
[
26
]
. The QCLT problem for (SHE) with
\sigma(u)=u
, also called the
parabolic Anderson model
(pAm), was studied in
[
26
,
27
]
for the colored noise in space, and in
[
25
,
26
]
for the rough noise in space. The QCLT problem for the
stochastic wave equation
(SWE):

\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial t^{2}}(t,x)=\Delta u(t,x)+\sigma\big(u(t,x)\big)\dot{W}(t,x),\quad t>0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

with initial condition
u(0,x)=1
,
\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(0,x)=0
, and a globally Lipschitz diffusion coefficient

\sigma
, driven by Gaussian noise that is white in time, was investigated in
[
15
,
7
,
28
]
for the colored noise in space in dimensions

d\leq 2
, when the spatial correlation of the noise is given by the Riesz kernel, or an integrable function. These results were extended to dimensions
d=3
, respectively
d\geq 4
in
[
16
,
17
]
, for the Riesz kernel. For the colored noise in time, the QCLT problem for (SWE) with
\sigma(u)=u
, also called the
hyperbolic Anderson model
(hAm), was treated in
[
2
,
1
]
for the colored/rough noise in space, respectively. Finally, the QCLT for time-independent Gaussian noise was analyzed in
[
4

[

5

These results can be summarized as follows: (see the table in
[
1
]
)

(a)

If the spatial covariance of the noise is given by the Riesz kernel
\gamma(x)=|x|^{-\beta}
for
\beta\in(0,d)
, then the rate of convergence in QCLT is
R^{-\beta/2}
and
\sigma_{R}(t)\sim R^{d-\beta/2}
. (The case
d=\beta=1
corresponds to the space-time Gaussian white noise.)

(b)

If the spatial covariance of the noise is given by a function
\gamma\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
then the rate of convergence in QCLT is
R^{-d/2}
and
\sigma_{R}(t)\sim R^{d/2}
.

(c)

If
d=1
and the noise is rough in space (i.e. it behaves in space like the fractional Brownian motion with Hurst index
H<1/2
), then the rate of convergence in QCLT is
R^{-1/2}
and
\sigma_{R}(t)\sim R^{1/2}
.

These results are valid for both (SHE) and (SWE), regardless of whether the noise is white in time, colored in time, or time-independent. This means the that the rate of convergence in QCLT and the order of magnitude of
\sigma_{R}(t)
are sensitive with respect to the spatial structure of the noise, but are robust with respect to the differential operator of the equation, and the time structure of the noise.

The main estimate of
[
20
]
is based on the
Stein-Malliavin bound
: for any random variable
F\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
such that
\mathbb{E}[F^{2}]=\sigma^{2}>0
and
F=\delta(v)
for some
v\in{\rm Dom}(\delta)
,

d_{TV}\left(\frac{F}{\sigma},Z\right)\leq\frac{2}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}|\sigma^{2}-\langle DF,v\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}|\leq{\frac{2}{\sigma^{2}}}\sqrt{{\rm Var}\big(\langle DF,v\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\big)}.

(3)

where
\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R})
,
(D,\delta)
are the Malliavin derivative, respectively the Skorohod integral with respect to
W
, and
\mathbb{D}^{1,2},{\rm Dom}(\delta)
are the respective domains of these operators. We refer the reader to Section
2.1
below for their precise definitions. The second inequality in (
3
) is due to Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, since by the duality between
D
and
\delta
,

\mathbb{E}[\langle DF,v\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}]=\mathbb{E}[F\delta(v)]=\sigma^{2}.

(4)

The insight of
[
20
]
was to represent

F_{R}(t)=\delta(v_{R,t})\quad\mbox{with}\quad v_{R,t}(s,y)=\sigma(u(s,y))\int_{|x|<R}G_{t-s}(x-y)dx,

(5)

and then bound the variance in (
3
) using the Clark-Ocone formula (given by (
21
) below) and the key estimate for the Malliavin derivative of the solution: for
p\geq 2
,
0<r<t\leq T
,

\|D_{r,z}u(t,x)\|_{p}\lesssim G_{t-r}(x-z),

(6)

where
\|\cdot\|_{p}
is the
L^{p}(\Omega)
-norm. Representation (
5
) was subsequently used in all other references dedicated to the QCLT problem, when the Gaussian noise is
white in time
, and possibly colored in space, replacing
\mathcal{H}
by the underlying Hilbert space of the noise.

When the noise is
colored in time
, the existence of the solution is only known in the case
\sigma(u)=u
. In this case, the method based on (
5
) and Clark-Ocone formula does not yield a suitable estimate. A new idea was introduced in
[
2
]
, which works for a variety of models, including the space-time Gaussian white noise, and can be summarized as follows:

(i)

use a basic fact from Malliavin calculus which says that any centered random variable
F\in L^{2}(\Omega)
can be written as
F=\delta(v)
with
v=-DL^{-1}F
, where
L^{-1}
is the pseudo-inverse of the OU generator, and represent
F_{R}(t)
in this way, instead of (
5
);

(ii)

to estimate the variance in (
3
), use the recent sharp bound for
{\rm Var}\big(\langle DF,-DL^{-1}F\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\big)
obtained by Vidotto in
[
32
]
for the space-time Gaussian white noise, called the
second-order Gaussian Poincaré inequality
, or an adaptation of this bound for the noise used in the equation, as for instance, Proposition 1.8 of
[
2
]
for the colored noise in space and time, or Proposition 2.4 of
[
26
]
for the rough noise in space, colored in time;

(iii)

depending on the form of the bound in
(ii)
, develop a suitable estimate for the second-order Malliavin derivative of the solution, and use it in conjunction with the estimate (
6
). For instance, for the colored noise in space and time, the estimate given by Theorem 1.3 of
[
2
]
states that for
p\geq 2
and
0<\theta<r<t\leq T
,

\|D_{(\theta,w),(r,z)}^{2}u(t,x)\|_{p}\lesssim G_{t-r}(x-z)G_{r-\theta}(z-w),

(7)

We conclude the literature review by mentioning some other related contributions.
The CLT for spatial averages of the form:

\int_{[0,R]^{d}}\big(g(u(t,x))-\mathbb{E}[g(u(t,x))]\big)dx

was proved in
[
11
]
for (SHE) in dimension
d\geq 1
, driven by Gaussian white noise, colored in time, when
g
is a globally Lipschitz function. The QCLT for (hAm) in dimension
d=1
, driven by a finite-variance Lévy noise was proved in
[
6
]
, using the recent advances Malliavin calculus on the Poisson space due to
[
31
]
. A large deviations principle for the solutions to (SHE) and (SWE) driven by a Gaussian noise which is white in time and colored in space has been recently obtained in
[
18
]
.

Assuming that
\mathbb{E}[u(t,x)]=1
, and letting
R=\varepsilon^{-1}
and
g(x)=1_{\{|x|\leq 1\}}
, we see that

\frac{F_{R}(t)}{R^{d/2}}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{d/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left[u\left(t,\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)-1\right]g(x)dx,

which converges to a normal distribution as
\varepsilon\to 0
,
in case
(b)
mentioned above. A different fluctuation result was obtained in
[
19
]
, which involves also time rescaling: if
u
is the solution of (SHE) in dimension
d\geq 3
with initial condition 1, driven by Gaussian noise white in time and colored in space with spatial covariance kernel
\gamma=\phi*\phi
for some
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then

\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{d/2-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left[u\left(\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}},\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)-1\right]g(x)dx\Longrightarrow\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{U}(t,x)g(x)dx\quad\mbox{as $\varepsilon\to 0$},

for any
g\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, where
\mathcal{U}
is the solution of the Edwards-Wilkinson equation.

All the studies cited above deal with SPDEs without a drift term.
The goal of the present article is to establish the QCLT for an equation
with drift
, which to the best of our knowledge, has not been treated before. For simplicity, we consider (SHE) driven by space-time Gaussian white noise in dimension
d=1
, and postpone the study of the same problem for higher dimensions with colored or rough noise for future work.

More precisely, we consider the equation:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial t}(t,x)=\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}(t,x)+b\big(u(t,x)\big)+\sigma\big(u(t,x)\big)\dot{W}(t,x),\ t>0,\ x\in\mathbb{R},\\
u(0,x)=1,\end{cases}

(8)

where
W
is a space-time Gaussian white noise, and
b,\sigma
are globally Lipschitz.

We recall that
W=\{W(\varphi);\varphi\in\mathcal{H}\}
is a zero-mean Gaussian process indexed by the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R})
, defined on a complete probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, and with covariance

\mathbb{E}[W(\varphi)W(\psi)]=\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
.
We let
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the filtration induced by
W
:

\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma\{W(1_{[0,s]\times A});s\in[0,t],A\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R})\}\vee\mathcal{N},

where
\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R})
is the class of bounded Borel subsets of
\mathbb{R}
, and
\mathcal{N}
is the class of
\mathbb{P}
-negligible sets.
A process
\{X(t,x);t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}\}
is
predictable
if it
\mathcal{P}
-measurable, where

\mathcal{P}
is the

\sigma
-field generated by elementary processes of the form

X(t,x)=Y1_{(a,b]}(t)1_{A}(x)
, where
0<a<b
,
A\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R})
and
Y
is
\mathcal{F}_{a}
-measurable.

For any predictable process
X
such that
\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|X(t,x)|^{2}dtdx<\infty
for any
T>0
, we can define the
Itô integral
of
X
with respect to
W
. This integral is an isometry:

\mathbb{E}\left|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}X(t,x)W(dt,dx)\right|^{2}=\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|X(t,x)|^{2}dtdx.

Moreover,
M_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}X(s,x)W(ds,dx)
is a continuous martingale with respect to
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, with predictable quadratic variation
\langle M\rangle_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|X(s,x)|^{2}dsdx
. To estimate the moments of
M_{t}
, we will use the
Burkholder-Davis-Gundy (BDG) inequality
: for any
p\geq 2
,

\|M_{t}\|_{p}^{2}\leq z_{p}^{2}\,\|\langle M\rangle_{t}\|_{p/2},

(9)

where
z_{p}>0
is a constant depending on
p
.

Definition 1.1
.

We say that
u=\{u(t,x);t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}\}
is a (mild)
solution
of (
8
) if
u
is predictable and satisfies the integral equation:

u(t,x)=1+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{t-s}(x-y)b\big(u(s,y)\big)dyds+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{t-s}(x-y)\sigma\big(u(s,y)\big)W(ds,dy).

(10)

Following
[
33
]
, we know that equation (
8
) has a unique solution
u
. Moreover, it was shown in
[
12
]
that the process
\{u(t,x)\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
is strictly stationary, and

K_{T,p}:=\sup_{(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}}\|u(t,x)\|_{p}<\infty\quad\mbox{for any $p\geq 2$ and $T>0$}.

(11)

We introduce the following assumptions.

Assumption 1.2
.

b,\sigma\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and
b^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}
are bounded.

Assumption 1.3
.

b,\sigma\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and
b^{\prime},b^{\prime\prime},\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime}
are bounded.

We are now ready to state the main results of this article.

Theorem 1.4
.

If Assumption
1.2
holds, then:

(i) the process
\{u(t,x)\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
is ergodic, for any
t>0
fixed;

(ii) for any

\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{R}\mathbb{E}\big[F_{R}(t)F_{R}(s)\big]=2\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho_{t,s}(x)dx=:\Sigma_{t,s}\ \mbox{is finite},

\rho_{t,s}(x-y)=\mathbb{E}[u(t,x)u(s,y)]
. In particular,
\sigma_{R}^{2}(t)\sim R
.

Theorem 1.5
.

If Assumption
1.3
holds, then:

(i) (QCLT)
for any
t>0
and
R>0
,

d_{TV}\left(\frac{F_{R}(t)}{\sigma_{R}(t)},Z\right)\leq C_{t}R^{-1/2},

(12)

where
Z\sim N(0,1)
and
C_{t}>0
is a constant depending on

;

(ii) (FCLT)
for any
R>0
, the process
F_{R}(\cdot)=\{F_{R}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
has a
\gamma
-Hölder continuous modification of order
\gamma\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, which we denote also
F_{R}(\cdot)
, and for which

\frac{1}{\sqrt{R}}F_{R}(\cdot)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\mathcal{G}(\cdot)\quad\mbox{in $C([0,\infty))$},

\mathcal{G}(\cdot)=\{\mathcal{G}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
is a zero-mean Gaussian process with

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{G}(t)\mathcal{G}(s)]=\Sigma_{t,s}

is the space of continuous functions on

, equipped with the topology of uniform convergence on compact sets.

The proof of Theorem
1.5
uses the steps
(i)
-
(iii)
outlined above, based on the second-order Poincaré inequality of Vidotto

32
]
, which we include in Proposition

\left\|D^{2}_{(r,z),(\theta,w)}u(t,x)\right\|_{p}\lesssim\left(1+|r-\theta|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)G_{8T}(z-w)\Big(G_{t-r}(x-z)+G_{t-\theta}(x-w)\Big).

(13)

To prove inequality (
13
), we use a novel technique for bounding the product of two heat kernels, which can be of independent interest; see Lemma
3.3
below. In the linear setting where
\sigma^{\prime\prime}(u)\equiv 0
and
b^{\prime\prime}(u)\equiv 0
, the tighter bound (
7
) is valid. We do not believe that (

) is valid in the nonlinear setting. The equation satisfied by the second Malliavin derivative of the solution

contains the Lebesgue integral

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{t-s}(x-y)b^{\prime\prime}(u(s,y))D_{r,z}u(s,y)D_{\theta,w}u(s,y)dyds

and the stochastic integral

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{t-s}(x-y)\sigma^{\prime\prime}(u(s,y))D_{r,z}u(s,y)D_{\theta,w}u(s,y)W(dyds).

These integrals are difficult to bound in the nonlinear setting.
Using (
6
), Minkowski’s inequality, and BDG inequality for the stochastic integral, we can bound the moments of these integrals by multiples of

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{t-s}(x-y)G_{s-r}(y-z)G_{s-\theta}(y-w)dyds

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}G^{2}_{t-s}(x-y)G^{2}_{s-r}(y-z)G^{2}_{s-\theta}(y-w)dyds.

The right-hand side of (
13
) is larger than product

(see Lemma

and

, which is useful for the proof of QCLT.

We denote

L_{b}:=\|b^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\quad\mbox{and}\quad L_{\sigma}:=\|\sigma^{\prime}\|_{\infty}.

(14)

and

Preliminaries

In this section, we introduce some preliminary material which is used in this article.

2.1
Malliavin Calculus

In this section, we present some basic facts from Malliavin calculus with respect to the isonormal Gaussian process
W=\{W(\varphi)\}_{\varphi\in\mathcal{H}}
. We refer the reader to
[
29
]
for more details.

\bullet

Chaos expansion.

Every random variable
F\in L^{2}(\Omega)

which is measurable with respect to
W
has the Wiener chaos expansion:

F=\mathbb{E}(F)+\sum_{n\geq 1}I_{n}(f_{n})\quad\mbox{for some}\quad f_{n}\in\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n},

(16)

where
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}=L^{2}\big((\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R})^{n}\big)
,
I_{n}:\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}\to\mathcal{H}_{n}

is the multiple Wiener integral with respect to
W
, and
\mathcal{H}_{n}
is the
n
-th chaos space.
We denote by
J_{n}
the projection on the
n
-th Wiener chaos.
By the orthogonality of the Wiener chaos spaces, if
F
has the chaos expansion (
16
), then

E|F|^{2}=[\mathbb{E}(F)]^{2}+\sum_{n\geq 1}E|I_{n}(f_{n})|^{2}=[\mathbb{E}(F)]^{2}+\sum_{n\geq 1}n!\,\|\widetilde{f}_{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}}^{2},

where
\widetilde{f}_{n}
is the symmetrization of
f_{n}
.

\bullet

Malliavin derivative.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be the class of “smooth” random variables:

F=f(W(\varphi_{1}),\ldots,W(\varphi_{n})),

(17)

where
f\in C_{b}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
\varphi_{i}\in\mathcal{H}
,

n\geq 1
, and

C_{b}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the class of bounded
C^{\infty}
-functions
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, whose partial derivatives of all orders are bounded. The

Malliavin derivative
of
F
of the form (
17
) is the

\mathcal{H}
-valued random variable given by:

D_{t,x}F:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}(W(\varphi_{1}),\ldots,W(\varphi_{n}))\varphi_{i}(t,x),\quad\mbox{for $t>0$ and $x\in\mathbb{R}$}.

We endow
\mathcal{S}
with the semi-norm

\|F\|_{\mathbb{D}^{1,2}}=(\mathbb{E}|F|^{2}+(E\|DF\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2})^{1/2}
. The
operator
D
can be extended to the space
\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
, the
completion of
\mathcal{S}
with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{D}^{1,2}}
.

Proposition 1.2.3 (on page 28) of
[
29
]
states that the Malliavin derivative satisfies the following chain rule:
for any
F\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
and
\varphi\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
with
\varphi^{\prime}
bounded,

D\varphi(F)=\varphi^{\prime}(F)DF.

(18)

Similarly to Corollary 1.2.1 (on page 34) of
[
29
]
, if
F\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable, then

D_{r,z}F=0\quad\mbox{for all $r>t$ and $z\in\mathbb{R}$}.

(19)

We recall the
Poincaré inequality
: for any
F\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}

{\rm Var}(F)\leq\mathbb{E}\|DF\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}|D_{t,x}F|^{2}dtdx,

(20)

and the
Clark-Ocone formula
: for any
F,G\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
,

F=\mathbb{E}(F)+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}[D_{t,x}F|\mathcal{F}_{t}]W(dt,dx).

(21)

Using the Clark-Ocone formula, we obtain that for any
F,G\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
,

\left|{\rm Cov}(F,G)\right|\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|D_{t,x}F\|_{2}\|D_{t,x}G\|_{2}dtdx.

(22)

We can define the iteration of the operator
D
such that for any
F\in\mathcal{S}
, the iterated derivative
D^{k}F
is an element in
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k})
. For any
p\geq 1
and natural number
k\geq 1
, we introduce the semi-norm on
\mathcal{S}
:

\|F\|_{\mathbb{D}^{k,p}}=\left(\mathbb{E}|F|^{p}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\|D^{j}F\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes j}}^{p}\right)^{1/p}.

The operator
D^{k}
is extended to the space
\mathbb{D}^{k,p}
, the
completion of
\mathcal{S}
with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{D}^{k,p}}
.

Let
F\in L^{2}(\Omega)
be arbitrary. For any
n\geq k
,
J_{n}F\in\mathbb{D}^{k,2}
,
D^{k}(J_{n}F)=J_{n-k}(D^{k}F)
and

\mathbb{E}\|D^{k}(J_{n}F)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k}}^{2}=n(n-1)\ldots(n-k+1)\mathbb{E}|J_{n}F|^{2}.

(23)

Moreover,
F\in\mathbb{D}^{k,2}
if and only if

S:=\sum_{n\geq k}n(n-1)\ldots(n-k+1)\mathbb{E}|J_{n}F|^{2}=\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{E}\|D^{k}(J_{n}F)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k}}^{2}<\infty,

(24)

and in this case
\mathbb{E}\|D^{k}F\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k}}^{2}=S
.

\bullet

Skorohod integral.

The
divergence operator

\delta
is the adjoint of
the operator
D
. The domain of
\delta
, denoted by
\mbox{Dom}(\delta)
, is the set of
u\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathcal{H})
such that

|\mathbb{E}\langle DF,u\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}|\leq c(\mathbb{E}|F|^{2})^{1/2},\quad\forall F\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2},

where
c
is a constant depending on
u
. If
u\in{\rm Dom}(\delta)
, then
\delta(u)
is the element of
L^{2}(\Omega)

characterized by the following duality relation:

\mathbb{E}(F\delta(u))=\mathbb{E}\left(\langle DF,u\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right),\quad\forall F\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}.

(25)

In particular,
\mathbb{E}[\delta(u)]=0
. If
u\in\mbox{Dom}(\delta)
,
we use the notation

\delta(u)=\int u(t,x)W(\delta t,\delta x)
, and
we say that
\delta(u)
is the
Skorohod integral
of
u
. If
u
is jointly measurable and
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
-adapted (in particular predictable), then
\delta(u)
coincides with the Itô integral of
u
.

Let
\mathbb{L}^{1,2}
be the class of processes
u\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathcal{H})
such that
u(t,x)\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
for all
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
, and there exists a measurable modification of

\{D_{t,x}u(s,y);(t,x),(s,y)\}
such that

\mathbb{E}\int_{(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R})^{2}}|D_{t,x}u(s,y)|^{2}dtdxdsdy<\infty.

Proposition 1.3.8 of
[
29
]
states that if
u\in\mathbb{L}^{1,2}
,
\{D_{t,x}u(s,y)\}_{(s,y)}\in{\rm Dom}(\delta)
for almost all
(t,x)
, and there exists a measurable modification of
\{\int D_{t,x}u(s,y)W(\delta s,\delta y)\}_{(t,x)}
which is in
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathcal{H})
, then
\delta(u)\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
and the following
Heisenberg commutation
holds:

D(\delta(u))=u+\delta(Du).

(26)

\bullet

Ornstein-Uhlenbeck generator.

If
F\in L^{2}(\Omega)
has the chaos expansion (
16
), we define the
Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) generator

LF=-\sum_{n\geq 1}nI_{n}(f_{n}),

(27)

whose domain is the set
{\rm Dom}(L)
of all random variables
F\in L^{2}(\Omega)
for which the series converges in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
If
F\in L^{2}(\Omega)
has the chaos expansion (
16
), we let:

L^{-1}F=-\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n}I_{n}(f_{n}).

L^{-1}
is the pseudo-inverse of
L
, in the sense that

LL^{-1}F=F-\mathbb{E}(F)
for any
F\in L^{2}(\Omega)
.

By Proposition 1.4.3 of
[
29
]
,

F\in{\rm Dom}\ L
if and only if
F\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
and
DF\in{\rm Dom}\ \delta
, and in this case,
LF=-\delta(DF)
. Applying this result to
F^{\prime}=L^{-1}F\in{\rm Dom}(L)
, we conclude that for any
F\in L^{2}(\Omega)
with
\mathbb{E}(F)=0
, the process
v=-DL^{-1}F
belongs to
{\rm Dom}\ \delta

and

F=\delta(-DL^{-1}F).

(28)

2.2
Properties of the heat kernel

In this section, we include some properties of the heat kernel which are used in the sequel.

\bullet

Product of squares.

For any
t>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

G_{t}^{2}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}}t^{-1/2}G_{t/2}(x).

(29)

By (
29
) and the semigroup property, for any
s\in[r,t]

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}G^{2}_{t-s}(x-y)G_{s-r}^{2}(y-z)dy

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}}(t-s)^{-\frac{1}{2}}(s-r)^{-\frac{1}{2}}(t-r)^{\frac{1}{2}}G_{t-r}^{2}(x-z).

(30)

Therefore,

\int_{r}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}G^{2}_{t-s}(x-y)G_{s-r}^{2}(y-z)dyds=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{4}}(t-r)^{\frac{1}{2}}G_{t-r}^{2}(x-z),

(31)

using the fact that

\int_{r}^{t}(t-s)^{-\frac{1}{2}}(s-r)^{-\frac{1}{2}}ds={\rm Beta}\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)=\pi.

(32)

\bullet

Monotonicty.
For any
0<s\leq t
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

G_{s}(x)\leq s^{-\frac{1}{2}}t^{\frac{1}{2}}G_{t}(x).

(33)

3
Moment bounds for the Malliavin derivatives

In this section, we prove the key estimates for the first and second Malliavin derivatives of the solution
u
, which are will be used for the proofs of Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
.
First, we will obtain these estimates for the Malliavin derivatives of an approximation
u_{n}
of the solution.
Then, by applying Lemma
A.1
below, we will conclude that the same estimates hold for
Du
and
D^{2}u
.

As approximating sequence, we consider
the Picard iterations defined by:
u_{0}(t,x)=1
and

u_{n+1}(t,x)=1+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{t-s}(x-y)b(u_{n}(s,y))dyds+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{t-s}(x-y)\sigma(u_{n}(s,y))W(ds,dy).

(34)

From Theorem 13 of
[
12
]
, it is known that for any
p\geq 2
and
T>0
,

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\|u_{n}(t,x)-u(t,x)\|_{p}\to 0\quad\mbox{as $n\to\infty$}.

(35)

Moreover, by Lemma 7.3 of
[
30
]
, for any integer
k\geq 1
and
p\geq 2
,
u_{n}\in\mathbb{D}^{k,p}
for any
n
, and

\sup_{n\geq 1}\sup_{(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}\|D^{k}u_{n}(t,x)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k}}^{p}<\infty,\quad\mbox{for any $p\geq 2,T>0$}.

Note that
b
and
\sigma
satisfy the following linear growth property: for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

|b(x)|\leq D_{b}(|x|+1)\quad\mbox{and}\quad|\sigma(x)|\leq D_{\sigma}(|x|+1),

(36)

where
D_{b}=\max\{L_{b},|b(0)|\}
and
D_{\sigma}=\max\{L_{\sigma},|\sigma(0)|\}
.
It follows that

\sup_{(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}}\|\sigma\big(u(t,x)\big)\|_{p}\leq D_{\sigma}(K_{T,p}+1)=:K_{T,p,D_{\sigma}},

(37)

where
K_{T,p}
is given by (
11
).

We will use the following result (see Lemma 1.2.3 of
[
29
]
).

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
(F_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence in
\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
such that
F_{n}\to F
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, and

\sup_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{E}\|DF_{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}<\infty.

Then
F\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
and the sequence
(DF_{n})_{n\geq 1}
converges to
DF
in the weak topology of
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathcal{H})
.

Theorem 3.2
.

If Assumption
1.2
holds, then
for any
p\geq 2
,
0<r<t\leq T
and
x,z\in\mathbb{R}
,

\|D_{r,z}u(t,x)\|_{p}\leq\mathcal{C}_{T,p}^{(1)}G_{t-r}(x-z),

(38)

where
\mathcal{C}_{T,p}^{(1)}
is a constant that depends on
(p,T,L_{b},L_{\sigma})
. Here
(L_{b},L_{\sigma})
are given by (
14
).

Proof.

We use a similar argument as in the proof of Theorem 1.6.(a) of
[
9
]
, except that these authors used this argument for the solution
u
itself, instead of
Du_{n}
.

Using Heisenberg commutation rule (
26
) followed by the chain rule (
18
), we infer that the sequence
(Du_{n})_{n\geq 0}
satisfies the following recurrence relation:

\displaystyle D_{r,z}u_{n+1}(t,x)

\displaystyle=G_{t-r}(x-z)\sigma\big(u_{n}(r,z)\big)+\int_{r}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{t-s}(x-y)b^{\prime}\big(u_{n}(s,y)\big)D_{r,z}u_{n}(s,y)dyds+

\displaystyle\quad\int_{r}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{t-s}(x-y)\sigma^{\prime}\big(u_{n}(s,y)\big)D_{r,z}u_{n}(s,y)W(ds,dy),

(39)

for any
n\geq 0
. Note that
D_{r,z}u_{0}(t,x)=0
since
u_{0}(t,x)=1
.

By Minkowski’s inequality and BDG inequality (
9
), for any
0<r<t\leq T
,
x,z\in\mathbb{R}
and
n\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\|D_{r,z}u_{n+1}(t,x)\|_{p}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 3\left\{K_{p,T,D_{\sigma}}^{2}G_{t-r}^{2}(x-z)+L_{b}^{2}\left(\int_{r}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{t-s}(x-y)\|D_{r,z}u_{n}(s,y)\|_{p}dyds\right)^{2}\right.

\displaystyle+\left.z_{p}^{2}L_{\sigma}^{2}\int_{r}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}G^{2}_{t-s}(x-y)\|D_{r,z}u_{n}(s,y)\|_{p}^{2}dyds\right\},

where
K_{p,T,\sigma}
is given by (
37
).
For fixed
(r,z)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
,
the function
f_{n}(t,x)=\|D_{r,z}u_{n}(t,x)\|_{p}
satisfies the assumptions of Lemma
A.2
on
I=(r,T]
, with
\rho(t,x)=G_{t-r}(x-z)
,
A=K_{T,p,D_{\sigma}}

and
C_{t}=C_{p,L_{b},L_{\sigma}}
given by

C_{p,L_{b},L_{\sigma}}=\max(1,L_{b}^{2},z_{p}^{2}L_{\sigma}^{2}).

(40)

Here
z_{p}
is the constant from the BDG inequality (
9
).
Therefore,there exists a constant
\mathcal{C}_{T,p}^{(1)}>0
depending on
(T,p,L_{b},L_{\sigma})
, such that for any
n\geq 1
,
0<r<t\leq T
and
x,z\in\mathbb{R}
,

\|D_{r,z}u_{n}(t,x)\|_{p}\leq\mathcal{C}_{T,p}^{(1)}G_{t-r}(x-z).

(41)

We apply Lemma
3.1
to
F_{n}=u_{n}(t,x)
and
F=u(t,x)
for fixed
(t,x)
.
It follows that
u(t,x)\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
and
\{Du_{n}(t,x)\}_{n\geq 1}
converges to
Du(t,x)
in the weak topology
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathcal{H})
.
Then, the conclusion follows by Lemma
A.1
and relation (
41
).
∎

Next, we will provide an estimate for the
p
-th moment of the second Malliavin derivative
D^{2}u
.
For this we will need to handle products of two heat kernels
G
.
Let us first remark that from Lemma A.4 of
[
8
]
, we know that for any
t,s>0
and
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
,

G_{t}(x)G_{s}(y)=G_{t+s}(x-y)G_{\frac{ts}{t+s}}\left(\frac{sx+ty}{t+s}\right).

(42)

As an application of (
42
), we obtain that for any
r<s<t
and
x,y,z\in\mathbb{R}
, we have:

G_{t-s}(x-y)G_{s-r}(y-z)=G_{t-r}(x-z)G_{\frac{(t-s)(s-r)}{t-r}}\left(y-\frac{(t-s)z}{t-r}-\frac{(s-r)x}{t-r}\right).

(43)

The exact expression (
43
) is very difficult to integrate in the
s
variable, and will not be used below.

The next technical lemma provides a different upper bound for products of Gaussian densities
G_{t-r}(x-z)G_{t-\theta}(x-w)
.
This bound is less accurate than (
43
), but is more convenient for calculations because the it includes only heat kernels evaluated at
x-z
,
x-w
and
w-z
.

Importantly, the upper bound in (
3.3
), as a function of
(t,x)
, is a solution to the heat equation. This enables us to prove that a similar expression is an upper bound for the
p
-th moment of
D^{2}_{(r,z),(\theta,w)}u(t,x)
. Additionally, the
G_{8T}(z-w)
factor is important for proving the QCLT because it shows that these products decay quickly if
|w-z|
is large.

Lemma 3.3
.

For any
0<\theta<r<t\leq T
and
x,w,z\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle G_{t-r}(x-z)G_{t-\theta}(x-w)

\displaystyle\leq 8T^{\frac{1}{2}}(1+e^{\frac{1}{16T}})(1+(r-\theta)^{-\frac{1}{2}})G_{8T}(z-w)\Big(G_{t-r}(x-z)+G_{t-\theta}(x-w)\Big).

(44)

Proof.

We split the analysis of this product into two cases: if
|w-z|\leq 1
or if
|w-z|>1
.

Case 1.
Assume that
|z-w|\leq 1
. In this case, we bound the factor
G_{t-\theta}(x-w)
. More precisely, using the fact that

1\leq e^{\frac{1}{16T}}\cdot e^{-\frac{|z-w|^{2}}{16T}}=e^{\frac{1}{16T}}\cdot(16\pi T)^{\frac{1}{2}}G_{8T}(z-w),

we can bound for any
0<\theta<r<t\leq T
and
x,w\in\mathbb{R}
,

G_{t-\theta}(x-w)\leq(2\pi)^{-\frac{1}{2}}(t-\theta)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\leq(2\pi)^{-\frac{1}{2}}(r-\theta)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\leq(r-\theta)^{-\frac{1}{2}}(8T)^{\frac{1}{2}}e^{\frac{1}{16T}}G_{8T}(z-w).

Multiplying this inequality by
G_{t-r}(x-z)
, we bound the product as follows:

G_{t-r}(x-z)G_{t-\theta}(x-w)\leq(r-\theta)^{-\frac{1}{2}}(8T)^{\frac{1}{2}}e^{\frac{1}{16T}}G_{8T}(z-w)G_{t-r}(x-z)

(45)

Case 2.
Assume that
|z-w|>1
.
By the triangle inequality, for any
x,y,z\in\mathbb{R}

|z-w|\leq|x-z|+|x-w|.

This implies that either

|x-z|\geq\frac{|z-w|}{2}
, or
|x-w|\geq\frac{|z-w|}{2}
.
If
|x-z|\geq\frac{|z-w|}{2}
, then

G_{t-r}(x-z)\leq G_{t-r}\left(\frac{z-w}{2}\right)=2G_{4(t-r)}(z-w).

Similarly, if
|x-w|\geq\frac{|z-w|}{2}
, then

G_{t-\theta}(x-w)\leq 2G_{4(t-\theta)}(z-w)
.

These observations imply that the product is bounded as follows:

G_{t-r}(x-z)G_{t-\theta}(x-w)\leq 2G_{t-r}(x-z)G_{4(t-\theta)}(z-w)+2G_{4(t-r)}(z-w)G_{t-\theta}(x-w).

Since
|z-w|>1
, we have

|z-w|^{2}>\frac{1}{2}+\frac{|z-w|^{2}}{2}
, and hence

G_{4(t-\theta)}(z-w)=(8\pi)^{-\frac{1}{2}}(t-\theta)^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-\frac{|z-w|^{2}}{8(t-\theta)}}\leq(8\pi)^{-\frac{1}{2}}(t-\theta)^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-\frac{1}{16(t-\theta)}}e^{-\frac{|z-w|^{2}}{16(t-\theta)}}.

We can remove the time singularities using the fact that
\sup_{x>0}x^{\frac{1}{2}}e^{-x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2e}}
:

(8\pi)^{-\frac{1}{2}}(t-\theta)^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-\frac{1}{16(t-\theta)}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2e}}\leq\pi^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

Therefore, for any
\theta\in[0,t]
and
|z-w|>1
,

G_{4(t-\theta)}(z-w)\leq\pi^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-\frac{|z-w|^{2}}{16T}}=4T^{\frac{1}{2}}G_{8T}(z-w).

Similarly, for any
r\in[0,t]
and
|z-w|>1
, we have:

G_{4(t-r)}(z-w)\leq 4T^{\frac{1}{2}}G_{8T}(z-w)
.
Therefore, when
|z-w|>1
, we have the bound:

G_{t-r}(x-z)G_{t-\theta}(x-w)\leq 8T^{\frac{1}{2}}G_{8T}(z-w)\Big(G_{t-r}(x-z)+G_{t-\theta}(x-w)\Big).

(46)

Finally, we can add (
45
) and (
46
) to obtain the desired conclusion (
3.3
).
∎

For the proof of the estimate on the second Malliavin derivative of the solution, we will use the following result, which is similar to Lemma
3.1
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
(F_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence in
\mathbb{D}^{2,2}
such that
F_{n}\to F
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, and

\sup_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{E}\|D^{2}F_{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2}}^{2}<\infty.

Then
F\in\mathbb{D}^{2,2}
and
(D^{2}F_{n})_{n\geq 1}
converges to
D^{2}F
in the weak topology of
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2})
.

Proof.

To prove that
F\in\mathbb{D}^{2,2}
, we will use criterion (
24
). By (
23
), for any
k\geq 2
,

\mathbb{E}\|D^{2}(J_{k}F_{n})-D^{2}(J_{k}F)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2}}^{2}=k(k-1)\mathbb{E}|J_{k}(F_{n}-F)|^{2}\leq k(k-1)\mathbb{E}|F_{n}-F|^{2}\to 0,

as
n\to\infty
. By Fatou’s lemma,

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 2}\mathbb{E}\|D^{2}(J_{k}F)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 2}\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\|D^{2}(J_{k}F_{n})\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2}}^{2}\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k\geq 2}\mathbb{E}\|D^{2}(J_{k}F_{n})\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\|D^{2}F_{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2}}^{2}\leq\sup_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{E}\|D^{2}F_{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2}}^{2}<\infty.

Hence,
F\in\mathbb{D}^{2,2}
.

To prove the second statement, we proceed as in the proof of Proposition 3.5 of
[
30
]
. A corollary of Banach-Alaoglu theorem states that in a Hilbert space, any bounded sequence has a weakly convergent subsequence. In our case, the sequence
(D^{2}F_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is bounded in
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2})
, and hence, it converges weakly to some
\eta\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2})
, along a subsequence
N^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{N}
. This means that for any
G\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2})
,

\mathbb{E}\langle D^{2}F_{n},G\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2}}\to\mathbb{E}\langle\eta,G\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2}}\quad\mbox{as $n\to\infty,n\in N^{\prime}$}.

In particular, this holds for the process
J_{k}G=\{J_{k}G(\xi_{1},\xi_{2});\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}\}
, which is also in
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2})
. Hence, for any
G\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2})
and
k\geq 2
,

\mathbb{E}\langle D^{2}F_{n},J_{k}G\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2}}\to\mathbb{E}\langle\eta,J_{k}G\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2}}\quad\mbox{as $n\to\infty,n\in N^{\prime}$}.

On the other hand, if
\delta_{2}:{\rm Dom}(\delta_{2})\subseteq L^{2}(\Omega;\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2})\to L^{2}(\Omega)
is the adjoint of
D^{2}
, then by duality and the fact that
F_{n}\to F
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, we have:

\mathbb{E}\langle D^{2}F_{n},J_{k}G\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2}}=\mathbb{E}\big[F_{n}\delta_{2}(J_{k}G)\big]\to\mathbb{E}\big[F\delta_{2}(J_{k}G)\big]=\mathbb{E}\langle D^{2}F,J_{k}G\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2}},\quad\mbox{as $n\to\infty$}.

Here we used the fact that
J_{k}G\in{\rm Dom}(\delta_{2})
.
It follows that for any
G\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2})
,
\mathbb{E}\langle\eta,J_{k}G\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2}}=\mathbb{E}\langle D^{2}F,J_{k}G\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2}}
for any

\|B_{R}(t)-B_{R}(s)\|_{p}\leq\|T_{3}\|_{p}+\|T_{4}\|_{p}\leq\frac{z_{p}K_{T,p,D_{\sigma}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}C_{T}R^{1/2}(t-s)^{1/2},

or equivalently,

\mathbb{E}|B_{R}(t)-B_{R}(s)|^{p}\leq\left(\frac{z_{p}K_{T,p,D_{\sigma}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}C_{T}\right)^{p}R^{p/2}(t-s)^{p/2}.

Choosing an arbitrary
p>2
allows us to apply the two criteria mentioned above, to infer that
\{B_{R}(t)\}_{t\in[0,T]}
has a
\gamma
-continuous modification for any
\gamma\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, and the family
\{R^{-1/2}B_{R}(\cdot)\}_{R>0}
is tight in
C[0,T]
.

Appendix A
Auxiliary Results

In this section, we include the approximation result and the Gronwall-type lemma, which were used in the proofs of Theorems
3.2
and
3.5
.

We start with the approximation result. Recall that a Radon measure is finite on compact sets.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
H=L^{2}(E,\mathcal{E},\mu)
, where
(E,\mathcal{E},\mu)
is a finite-dimensional normed space,
\mathcal{E}
is the Borel
\sigma
-field, and
\mu
is a Radon measure on
(E,\mathcal{E})
. Assume that the sequence
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subseteq L^{2}(\Omega;H)
converges to
X
in the weak topology of
L^{2}(\Omega;H)
. If there exist
p>1
and a measurable function
\phi:E\to{[0,\infty)}
such that

\sup_{n\geq 1}\|X_{n}(x)\|_{p}\leq\phi(x)\quad{\mbox{for $\mu$-almost all $x\in E$},}

(79)

then

\|X(x)\|_{p}\leq\phi(x)\text{ for $\mu$-almost all $x\in E$}.

Proof.

Define the sets

\displaystyle A_{\varepsilon,R}

\displaystyle:=\{x\in E;\|x\|_{E}\leq R,\ \phi(x)+\varepsilon\leq\|X(x)\|_{p}\text{ and }\phi(x)\leq R\}

\displaystyle B_{N}

\displaystyle:=\left\{(\omega,x)\in\Omega\times E;|X(\omega,x)|\leq N\|X(x)\|_{p}\right\}.

Note that
\mu(A_{\varepsilon,R})<\infty
,
since
A_{\varepsilon,R}
is contained in the closed ball
\{x\in E;\|x\|_{E}\leq R\}
(which is compact, since
E
is finite-dimensional), and
\mu
is Radon.

Define for
(\omega,x)\in\Omega\times E
,

Y(\omega,x):=1_{A_{\varepsilon,R}}(x)1_{B_{N}}(\omega,x)X(\omega,x)|X(\omega,x)|^{p-2}\|X(x)\|_{p}^{1-p}.

This process is designed to have the property that for each fixed
x\in E
,

\|Y(x)\|_{\frac{p}{p-1}}\leq 1_{A_{\varepsilon,R}}(x).

(80)

Indeed,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|Y(x)|^{\frac{p}{p-1}}

\displaystyle=1_{A_{\varepsilon,R}}(x)\mathbb{E}\big[1_{B_{N}}(x)|X(x)|^{p}\big]\ \|X(x)\|_{p}^{-p}

\displaystyle\leq 1_{A_{\varepsilon,R}}(x)\mathbb{E}|X(x)|^{p}\ \|X(x)\|_{p}^{-p}=1_{A_{\varepsilon,R}}(x).

The definition of
B_{N}
also guarantees that the process is bounded pointwise:

|Y(\omega,x)|\leq{N^{p-1}}1_{A_{\varepsilon,R}}(x).

An immediate consequence of this pointwise bound is that
Y\in L^{2}(\Omega;H)
, since

\mathbb{E}\int_{E}|Y(x)|^{2}\mu(dx)\leq{N^{2(p-1)}}\mu(A_{\varepsilon,R})<\infty.

By Hölder’s inequality and relations (
79
) and (
80
), for each
n\geq 1
we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int_{E}X_{n}(x)Y(x)\mu(dx)\leq\int_{E}\big|\mathbb{E}\big[X_{n}(x)Y(x)\big]\big|\mu(dx)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{E}\|X_{n}(x)\|_{p}\|Y(x)\|_{\frac{p}{p-1}}\mu(dx)\leq{\int_{A_{\varepsilon,R}}\phi(x)\mu(dx)}

Importantly, the defintion of
A_{\varepsilon,R}
guarantees that

\int_{A_{\varepsilon,R}}\phi(x)\mu(dx)\leq R\mu(A_{\varepsilon,R})<\infty.

From the definition of
Y
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int_{E}X(x)Y(x)\mu(dx)

\displaystyle=\int_{A_{\varepsilon,R}}\mathbb{E}\big[1_{B_{N}}(x)|X(x)|^{p}\big]\cdot\|X(x)\|_{p}^{1-p}\mu(dx).

By weak convergence,

\mathbb{E}\int_{E}X_{n}(x)Y(x)\mu(dx)\to\mathbb{E}\int_{E}X(x)Y(x)\mu(dx)
.
This leads to the statement

\int_{A_{\varepsilon,R}}\mathbb{E}\big[1_{B_{N}}(x)|X(x)|^{p}\big]\cdot\|X(x)\|_{p}^{1-p}\mu(dx)\leq\int_{A_{\varepsilon,R}}\phi(x)\mu(dx).

Letting
N\to\infty
, we obtain:
\int_{A_{\varepsilon,R}}\|X(x)\|_{p}\mu(dx)\leq\int_{A_{\varepsilon,R}}\phi(x)\mu(dx)
.
By the definition of
A_{\varepsilon,R}
,

\int_{A_{\varepsilon,R}}(\phi(x)+\varepsilon)\mu(dx)\leq\int_{A_{\varepsilon,R}}\phi(x)\mu(dx),

which proves that
\mu\left(A_{\varepsilon,R}\right)=0
because
\int_{A_{\varepsilon,R}}\phi(x)\mu(dx)<\infty
. Letting
\varepsilon\to 0
and
R\to\infty
, we conclude that

\|X(x)\|_{p}\leq\phi(x)
for
\mu
-almost all
x\in E
.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Fix
r>0
and let
\rho(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{t-r}(x-y)\mu(dy)
be a solution to the heat equation for
t>r
, where
\mu
is a non-negative measure on
\mathbb{R}
. Assume that
I=(r,T]
or
I=(r,\infty)
.
Let
f_{n}:I\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a sequence of functions such that for any
t\in I
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

f_{0}(t,x)=0

and for any
n\geq 1
,

f_{n+1}^{2}(t,x)\leq C_{t}\left\{A^{2}\rho^{2}(t,x)+\left(\int_{r}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{t-s}(x-y)f_{n}(s,y)dyds\right)^{2}+\int_{r}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{t-s}^{2}(x-y)f_{n}^{2}(s,y)dyds\right\},

(81)

where
A>0
and
C_{t}>0
is a constant which is non-decreasing in
t
, both of which are independent of
n
. Then for any
n\geq 1
,
t\in I
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

f_{n}(t,x)\leq\mathcal{C}_{t}A\rho(t,x),

(82)

where

\mathcal{C}_{t}=3^{\frac{1}{3}}C_{t}^{1/2}\exp\left\{\frac{3C_{t}^{3}}{2}\left[(t-r)^{6}+\frac{\Gamma(1/4)^{4}}{8\pi^{5/2}}(t-r)^{\frac{3}{2}}\right]\right\}.

Proof.

For all
n\geq 0
and
t\in I
, define

\widetilde{f}_{n}(t):=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{f_{n}(t,x)}{\rho(t,x)}\in[0,\infty].

Step 1.
In this step, we assume that
\widetilde{f}_{n}(t)<\infty
for all
t\in I
(for some fixed
n\geq 0
), and we derive a recurrence relation for
\widetilde{f}_{n+1}
.
By the semigroup property, we have:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}(x-y)f_{n}(s,y)dy

\displaystyle\leq\widetilde{f}_{n}(s)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}(x-y)\rho(s,y)dy

\displaystyle=\widetilde{f}_{n}(s)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{t-s}(x-y)G_{s-r}(y-z)\mu(dz)dy

\displaystyle=\widetilde{f}_{n}(s)\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{t-r}(x-z)\mu(dz)=\widetilde{f}_{n}(s)\rho(t,x).

(83)

Next we bound the
\int_{\mathbb{R}}G^{2}_{t-s}(x-y)f^{2}_{n}(s,y)dy
integral using a duality argument:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}^{2}(x-y)f^{2}_{n}(s,y)dy

\displaystyle\leq\widetilde{f}_{n}^{2}(s)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}^{2}(x-y)\rho^{2}(s,y)dy

\displaystyle=\widetilde{f}_{n}^{2}(s)\sup_{\|h\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=1}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{t-s}(x-y)G_{s-r}(y-z)\mu(dz)h(y)dy\right)^{2}.

Use the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality on the
dy
integral to bound

\displaystyle\sup_{\|h\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=1}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{t-s}(x-y)G_{s-r}(y-z)\mu(dz)h(y)dy\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}G^{2}_{t-s}(x-y)G^{2}_{s-r}(y-z)dy\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mu(dz)\right)^{2}.

This previous step required
\mu
to be a nonnegative measure. Then we use (
30
) to evaluate the
dy
integral.
Therefore, we can bound

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}^{2}(x-y)f^{2}_{n}(s,y)dy\leq\widetilde{f}^{2}_{n}(s)\left(\frac{t-r}{4\pi(t-s)(s-r)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\rho^{2}(t,x).

(84)

Then applying (
83
) and (
84
) to (
81
) leads to the estimate

\displaystyle f^{2}_{n+1}(t,x)

\displaystyle\leq C_{t}\rho^{2}(t,x)\left\{A^{2}+\left(\int_{r}^{t}\widetilde{f}_{n}(s)ds\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}}\int_{r}^{t}\left(\frac{t-r}{(t-s)(s-r)}\right)^{1/2}\widetilde{f}_{n}^{2}(s)ds\right\}.

Divide by
\rho^{2}(t,x)
and take the supremum for
x\in\mathbb{R}
. We get:

\tilde{f}_{n+1}^{2}(t)\leq C_{t}\left\{A^{2}+\left(\int_{r}^{t}\widetilde{f}_{n}(s)ds\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}}\int_{r}^{t}\left(\frac{t-r}{(t-s)(s-r)}\right)^{1/2}\widetilde{f}_{n}^{2}(s)ds\right\}.

(85)

We will bound the 6th power of
\tilde{f}_{n+1}(t)
, but any power bigger than 4 will work. By Hölder’s inequality,

\int_{r}^{t}\tilde{f}_{n}(s)ds\leq(t-r)^{\frac{5}{6}}\left(\int_{r}^{t}\tilde{f}_{n}^{6}(s)ds\right)^{\frac{1}{6}}

(86)

and

\displaystyle\int_{r}^{t}\left(\frac{t-r}{4\pi(t-s)(s-r)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{f}_{n}^{2}(s)ds

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{r}^{t}\left(\frac{t-r}{4\pi(t-s)(s-r)}\right)^{\frac{3}{4}}ds\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}\left(\int_{r}^{t}\tilde{f}_{n}^{6}(s)ds\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}

\displaystyle\leq(4\pi)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(B\left(\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4}\right)(t-r)^{\frac{1}{4}}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}\left(\int_{r}^{t}\tilde{f}_{n}^{6}(s)ds\right)^{\frac{1}{3}},

(87)

where
B\left(\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4}\right)
is a Beta function.

Therefore, raising (
85
) to the third power,

\tilde{f}^{6}_{n+1}(t)\leq 3^{2}{C_{t}^{3}}\left(A^{6}+\left((t-r)^{5}+{(4\pi)^{-\frac{3}{2}}}B\left(\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4}\right)^{2}(t-r)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\int_{r}^{t}\tilde{f}_{n}^{6}(s)ds\right).

(88)

Step 2.
In this step, we prove by induction on
n\geq 0
that

\widetilde{f}_{n}(t)<\infty\quad\mbox{for all $t\in I$}.

(89)

The statement holds trivially for
n=0
since
\tilde{f}_{0}(t)\equiv 0
.
For the induction step, we assume that (
89
) holds
for all
k=0,\ldots,n
, and we show that it holds for
n+1
. By Step 1, the recurrence relation (
88
) holds for all
k=0,\ldots,n
.

We fix
t\geq r
and set

\alpha=3^{2}C_{t}^{3}A^{6}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\beta=3^{2}C_{t}^{3}\left[(t-r)^{5}+(4\pi)^{-\frac{3}{2}}B\left(\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4}\right)^{2}(t-r)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right].

Then for all
k=0,\ldots,n
and
s\in[r,t]
,

\widetilde{f}_{k+1}^{6}(s)\leq\alpha+\beta\int_{r}^{t}\widetilde{f}_{k}^{6}(s)ds.

By Lemma
A.3
below, we infer that for any
k=1,\ldots,n+1
and
s\in[r,t]
,

\tilde{f}_{k}^{6}(s)\leq\alpha e^{\beta(s-r)}.

In particular,
\tilde{f}_{n+1}(t)<\infty
.

Step 3.
Having proved that (
89
) holds for all
n\geq 0
, we can now invoke the classical Gronwall lemma (e.g.
[
23
, Lemma 10.2.4]
) on the fixed interval
[r,t]
, for arbitrary
t\in I
. We infer that for all
n\geq 1
and
s\in[r,t]
,

\tilde{f}_{n}^{6}(s)\leq 3^{2}C_{t}^{3}A^{6}\exp\left\{3^{2}C_{t}^{3}\left[(t-r)^{5}+(4\pi)^{-\frac{3}{2}}B\left(\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4}\right)^{2}(t-r)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right](s-r)\right\}.

In particular, for
s=t

\tilde{f}_{n}^{6}(t)\leq 3^{2}C_{t}^{3}A^{6}\exp\left\{3^{2}C_{t}^{3}\left[(t-r)^{6}+(4\pi)^{-\frac{3}{2}}B\left(\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4}\right)^{2}(t-r)^{\frac{3}{2}}\right]\right\}.

Taking power
1/6
, we conclude that for any
n\geq 1
and
t\in I
,

\tilde{f}_{n}(t)\leq 3^{\frac{1}{3}}C_{t}^{1/2}A\exp\left\{\frac{3C_{t}^{3}}{2}\left[(t-r)^{6}+(4\pi)^{-\frac{3}{2}}B\left(\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4}\right)^{2}(t-r)^{\frac{3}{2}}\right]\right\}.

(Note: For
n=1
,
\widetilde{f}_{1}^{2}(t)\leq C_{t}A^{2}
gives a sharper bound.)
The conclusion follows using the fact that
B\left(\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4}\right)=\frac{\Gamma(1/4)^{2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}
.

∎

Lemma A.3
.

Let
(f_{n})_{n=0,\ldots,N+1}
be a sequence of non-negative functions on
[a,b]
such that for any
n=0,\ldots,N
and
t\in[a,b]

f_{n+1}(t)\leq\alpha(t)+\int_{a}^{t}\beta(s)f_{n}(s)ds

for some non-negative functions
\alpha
and
\beta
on
[a,b]
. We assume that
f_{n}(t)<\infty
for all
n=0,\ldots,N
, but
f_{N+1}(t)
may be
\infty
.
Then for any
n=1,\ldots,N+1
and
t\in[a,b]

f_{n}(t)\leq\alpha(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\big(\alpha(s)+f_{0}(s)\big)\beta(s)\exp\left(\int_{s}^{t}\beta(r)dr\right)ds.

(Note: For
n=1
, we have the sharper bound:
f_{1}(t)\leq\alpha(t)+\int_{a}^{t}\beta(s)f_{0}(s)ds
.)

In particular, if
\alpha
is non-decreasing and
f_{0}=0
, then for any
n=1,\ldots,N+1
and
t\in[a,b]
,

f_{n}(t)\leq\alpha(t)\exp\left(\int_{a}^{t}\beta(s)ds\right),

and hence
f_{N+1}(t)<\infty
for all
t\in[a,b]
.

Proof.

Using the recurrence formula for
f_{1}
, we see that

\displaystyle f_{2}(t)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha(t)+\int_{a}^{t}\beta(s_{1})f_{1}(s_{1})ds_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha(t)+\int_{a}^{t}\beta(s_{1})\alpha(s_{1})ds_{1}+\int_{a<s_{2}<s_{1}<t}\beta(s_{1})\beta(s_{2})f_{0}(s_{2})ds_{1}ds_{2}.

If we do one more iteration, we get:

\displaystyle f_{3}(t)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha(t)+\int_{a}^{t}\beta(s_{1})f_{2}(s_{1})ds_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha(t)+\int_{a}^{t}\beta(s_{1})\alpha(s_{1})ds_{1}+\int_{a<s_{2}<s_{1}<t}\beta(s_{1})\beta(s_{2})\alpha(s_{2})ds_{1}ds_{2}+

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\int_{a<s_{3}<s_{2}<s_{1}<t}\beta(s_{1})\beta(s_{2})\beta(s_{3})f_{0}(s_{3})ds_{1}ds_{2}ds_{3}.

By induction, for any
n=1,\ldots,N+1
, we have:

\displaystyle f_{n}(t)\leq\alpha(t)+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\mathcal{I}_{k}(t)+\mathcal{R}_{n}(t)

(90)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{a<s_{k}<\ldots<s_{1}<t}\beta(s_{1})\ldots\beta(s_{k})\alpha(s_{k})ds_{1}\ldots ds_{k}

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{a<s_{n}<\ldots<s_{1}<t}\beta(s_{1})\ldots\beta(s_{n})f_{0}(s_{n})ds_{1}\ldots ds_{n}.

Note that

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{t}\alpha(s_{k})\beta(s_{k})\left(\int_{s_{k}<s_{k-1}<\ldots<s_{1}<t}\beta(s_{1})\ldots\beta(s_{k-1})ds_{1}\ldots ds_{k-1}\right)ds_{k}

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{t}\alpha(s)\beta(s)\frac{1}{(k-1)!}\left(\int_{s}^{t}\beta(r)dr\right)^{k-1}ds

and hence

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\mathcal{I}_{k}(t)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{a}^{t}\alpha(s)\beta(s)\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{1}{(k-1)!}\left(\int_{s}^{t}\beta(r)dr\right)^{k-1}ds

\displaystyle\leq\int_{a}^{t}\alpha(s)\beta(s)\exp\left(\int_{s}^{t}\beta(r)dr\right)ds.

(91)

Similarly,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{t}f_{0}(s_{n})\beta(s_{n})\left(\int_{s_{n}<s_{n-1}<\ldots<s_{1}<t}\beta(s_{1})\ldots\beta(s_{n-1})ds_{1}\ldots ds_{n-1}\right)ds_{n}

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{t}f_{0}(s)\beta(s)\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\left(\int_{s}^{t}\beta(r)dr\right)^{n-1}ds

\displaystyle\leq\int_{a}^{t}f_{0}(s)\beta(s)\exp\left(\int_{s}^{t}\beta(r)dr\right)ds

(92)

Inserting estimates (
91
) and (
92
) into (
90
)
proves the first statement.

Assume now that
\alpha
is non-decreasing and
f_{0}=0
for all
t
. Then

f_{n}(t)\leq\alpha(t)+\alpha(t)\int_{a}^{t}\beta(s)\exp\left(\int_{s}^{t}\beta(r)dr\right)ds=\alpha(t)\exp\left(\int_{a}^{t}\beta(r)dr\right).

∎
