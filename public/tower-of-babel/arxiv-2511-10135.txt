Contextual Refinement of Higher-Order Concurrent Probabilistic Programs
Kwing Hei Li, Alejandro Aguirre, Joseph Tassarotti, Lars Birkedal

Abstract
. We present Foxtrot, the first higher-order separation logic for proving contextual refinement of higher-order concurrent probabilistic programs with higher-order local state. From a high level, Foxtrot inherits various concurrency reasoning principles from standard concurrent separation logic, e.g. invariants and ghost resources, and supports advanced probabilistic reasoning principles for reasoning about complex probability distributions induced by concurrent threads, e.g. tape presampling and induction by error amplification. The integration of these strong reasoning principles is highly non-trivial due to the combination of probability and concurrency in the language and the complexity of the Foxtrot model; the soundness of the logic relies on a version of the axiom of choice within the Iris logic, which is not used in earlier work on Iris-based logics. We demonstrate the expressiveness of Foxtrot on a wide range of examples, including the adversarial von Neumann coin and the 𝗋𝖺𝗇𝖽𝗈𝗆𝖻𝗒𝗍𝖾𝗌 ​ _ ​ 𝗎𝗇𝗂𝖿𝗈𝗋𝗆 \mathsf{randombytes\_uniform} function of the Sodium cryptography software library. All results have been mechanized in the Rocq proof assistant and the Iris separation logic framework.



1.
Introduction

Randomization and concurrency are, independently, two important and widely-used
language features in the world of computing. The former is used to implement
probabilistic data structures, randomized algorithms, and machine
learning algorithms; the latter is utilized to increase program throughput and
to model distributed systems and multi-core architecture. Together, the
combination of these two features can be found in concurrent randomized
algorithms
(
Morris 1978
;
Aspnes and Ruppert 2016
)
and in cryptography
protocols
(
Evans et al. 2018
)
.
However, formal methods for reasoning about concurrent probabilistic programs are limited and not very
expressive, in part because concurrency introduces nondeterminism, which in semantic models does not
compose straightforwardly with probabilistic choice
(
Varacca and Winskel 2006
)
.

1.1.
Motivating Example and Prior Work

Consider the following
\mathsf{fImpl}
function, where
\operatorname{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0.0117,0.2695,0.457}{rand}}}N

\{0,\dots,N\}

\displaystyle\mathsf{fImpl}\triangleq{}

\displaystyle\lambda\penalty\thinspace\_.\penalty\thinspace\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0.0117,0.2695,0.457}{let}}\penalty\thinspace(x,y)\mathrel{=}(\operatorname{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0.0117,0.2695,0.457}{rand}}}7\penalty\thinspace|||\penalty\thinspace\operatorname{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0.0117,0.2695,0.457}{rand}}}31)\penalty\thinspace\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0.0117,0.2695,0.457}{in}}\penalty\thinspace\ x\cdot 32+y

Though
\mathsf{fImpl}
appears simple, it already exhibits the combination of both probability and concurrency.
When we call
\mathsf{fImpl}
, it spawns two concurrent threads, which generate a random
3
-bit and
5
-bit number, respectively. Afterwards, it returns the binary concatenation of the two numbers generated.
Note that since the random samples of both threads are independent, this function behaves the same as one that directly samples a random
8
-bit number. Hence to reason about its correctness, one might want to show that its behavior is the same as the following
\mathsf{fSpec}
function.

\displaystyle\mathsf{fSpec}\triangleq{}

\displaystyle\lambda\penalty\thinspace\_.\penalty\thinspace\operatorname{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0.0117,0.2695,0.457}{rand}}}255

To formally argue that the two programs behave the same, we might want to prove that
\mathsf{fImpl}
is
contextually equivalent
to
\mathsf{fSpec}

Intuitively, an expression
e_{1}
is contextually equivalent to another expression
e_{2}
at
a type

\mathcal{C}

\emptyset\vdash e_{1}\simeq_{\text{ctx}}e_{2}:\tau

e_{1}
with
e_{2}
.
Contextual equivalence is defined as the symmetric interior of contextual refinement,
denoted by
\emptyset\vdash e_{1}\precsim_{\text{ctx}}e_{2}:\tau

mean that, for any context
\mathcal{C}
the observable behaviour of
\mathcal{C}{[}e_{1}{]}

is
included
in the observable behaviour of
\mathcal{C}{[}e_{2}{]}
. Proving contextual equivalence or refinement is generally a difficult problem as the statement quantifies over
all
possible contexts, including those that might use concurrency and probability as well. In fact, to the best of our knowledge, there is
no
prior work on verifying contextual equivalence of higher-order programs utilizing
both
probability and concurrency (see

1.2.
Contributions and Key Ideas

In this paper, we present
Foxtrot
, a higher-order probabilistic
concurrent separation logic, which we use to build
the
first
logical relations model for proving contextual refinement and
equivalence of concurrent probabilistic programs. At a high level, the Foxtrot
logic supports reasoning about contextual refinement of higher-order probabilistic
concurrent imperative programs written in
ConcRandML
, an ML-style
programming language with higher-order dynamically-allocated local state,
discrete probabilistic sampling, and dynamically allocated concurrently running
threads.

Since
ConcRandML
is probabilistic, the observable behaviour of a program refers to the
probability

of observing some behaviour, such as termination. Hence, by contextual
refinement
\emptyset\vdash e_{1}\precsim_{\text{ctx}}e_{2}:\tau{}
, we mean that the
(limit of the) probability of termination of
\mathcal{C}{[}e_{1}{]}

is below the (limit of the) probability of termination of
\mathcal{C}{[}e_{2}{]}
.
The precise definition can be found in
Section

2.4
; here it suffices to
know that the operational semantics is defined using (probabilistic stateful) schedulers, which
at every step of the execution decide which thread to execute next, and that the termination probability is
obtained by taking the supremum over all possible schedulers (following the notion of
may-termination
in previous work
(
Birkedal et al. 2013
;
Aguirre and Birkedal 2023
)
).

The central new notion in Foxtrot to reason about contextual refinement is
a
logical concurrent probabilistic refinement judgment
.
The refinement judgment
\Delta\vDash e\precsim e^{\prime}:\tau

intuitively means that
e
refines
e^{\prime}
at the type
\tau
.
The context
\Delta
assigns a relational interpretation to all
type variables in
\tau
, but can be ignored for now.
The logical refinement provides a sound method to prove contextual refinement,
i.e.
\emptyset\vDash e_{1}\precsim e_{2}:\tau{}
implies
\emptyset\vdash e_{1}\precsim_{\text{ctx}}e_{2}:\tau{}
.
Following the logical approach to relational refinement
(
Timany et al. 2024
)
,
the refinement judgment is defined in the Foxtrot logic, which
is in turn defined on top of the Iris base logic
(
Jung et al. 2018
)
.
The Foxtrot program logic is phrased using Hoare triples

{\left\{P\right\}}\penalty\thinspace e\penalty\thinspace{\left\{Q\right\}}
, which are defined (using ghost state) with an eye for
relational refinement, much as in earlier work
(
Gregersen et al. 2024
;
Haselwarter et al. 2025
)
.
Rules in Foxtrot are often expressed in terms of Hoare triples, so to prove
a refinement judgment, we often unfold its definition to the Hoare triple layer.
We later see how the refinement judgment is defined with Hoare triples in
Section

3.1
.

Roughly speaking, Foxtrot inherits proof rules for concurrency (including

invariants
and
ghost resources
) from earlier relational logics for concurrency
(
Frumin et al. 2021
)
, and

coupling-based proof rules
for probabilistic reasoning from
earlier relational logics for probability
(
Gregersen et al. 2024
;
Haselwarter et al. 2025
)
. In fact, these basic
rules are sufficient to verify a wide range of concurrent probabilistic programs, especially
those where the concurrency and probability features do not interact with each other in
complex ways.
However, some programs feature complex behaviors arising from the
complicated interaction between probability and concurrency, e.g. the
\mathsf{fImpl}
function from
Section

1
where one has to reason about the joint probability distribution produced by sampling in two separate threads.
To address these issues, Foxtrot includes more advanced probabilistic reasoning principles, including
asynchronous couplings using so-called
presampling tapes
(first introduced in
the Clutch logic
(
Gregersen et al. 2024
)
) and
fragmented couplings
for reasoning about
rejection samplers
(
Haselwarter et al. 2025
)
. Moreover, inspired by Eris
(
Aguirre et al. 2024
)
and Approxis

(
Haselwarter et al. 2025
)
, Foxtrot also supports
induction by error amplification through the use of
error credit
resources. We
explain these reasoning principles in
Section

3
, and we demonstrate their expressiveness in the concurrent setting by using them to reason about a
range of challenging program refinements in
Section

4
, e.g. an adversarial variant of the von Neumann coin program and the implementation of the
\mathsf{randombytes\_uniform}
function in the open-source cryptography software library Sodium. These examples (even including the smaller examples in
Section

3
) are, to the best of our knowledge, beyond the scope of previous techniques, as they require complex reasoning principles, e.g. reasoning about higher-order functions, local state, and execution of unknown code.

In light of this rough overview, the reader may wonder if Foxtrot is a trivial
combination of earlier logics and/or what, if anything, is challenging in its
development? The key technical novelty and
challenges lie in the
definition
of the program logic, specifically in
the weakest precondition predicate used in the definition of Hoare triples, and
the construction of the model of this logic, which ultimately allows us to prove
the soundness theorem above. One of the key challenges is that intuitively, to
show that
e_{1}
refines
e_{2}
, for every possible scheduler for the
left-hand side program, we have to exhibit a scheduler for the right-hand side
program
e_{2}
, so that
e_{1}
can be coupled with

e_{2}
, and this is done by “piecing together” schedulers for
subexpressions (to validate compositional program logic
rules). This is significantly different from earlier work on
relational logics for concurrency without probabilities
(
Frumin et al. 2021
;
Turon et al. 2013
)
, where one only has to construct a single
trace
for the
right-hand side. Now, with probabilistic features, the challenge is that
we have to compose
families
of schedulers (because of the probabilistic
branching we get a family of schedulers). To do so, we end up having to use a
version of the
axiom of choice
in the Iris logic which, to the best of
our knowledge, has not been used in earlier work on Iris-based program logics.
Indeed, Foxtrot’s model is significantly more sophisticated than that of its
predecessors, such as Clutch
(
Gregersen et al. 2024
)
and Approxis
(
Haselwarter et al. 2025
)
.
Luckily, users of the logic need not understand the model; it suffices to know
that we have used the model to prove adequacy of the logic (
Theorem

3.1
), which intuitively states that proving a Hoare triple in the
Foxtrot logic implies that the termination probability of a program is
upper-bounded by that of another, and which is used to prove the soundness
theorem above (
Theorem

3.3
).

We believe it is a
strong feature
of
Foxtrot that, with the new definition of weakest precondition and model, we are
in fact able to prove soundness of the many strong reasoning principles
mentioned above. We note that this is not automatic (all
the proof rules of Foxtrot have been proved sound from scratch) and not trivial;
in fact, as we later emphasize in
Section

3
, one of the proof rules that one might
expect to hold given earlier work
(
Gregersen et al. 2024
;
Haselwarter et al. 2025
)
, namely “presampling on the
right”, is
not
sound in the concurrent setting of Foxtrot.

•

(
Boldo et al. 2013
)
.

2.1.
Probability Theory

A
subdistribution
(henceforth simply
distribution
) on a countable set
A
is a function
\mu:A\rightarrow[0,1]
s.t.
\sum_{a\in A}\mu(a)\leq 1
. We write the collection of distributions on
A
as
\mathcal{D}(A)
.
Distributions admit monadic operations, with
(\mret\colon A\to\mathcal{D}(A))
defined as
\mret(a)(a^{\prime})\triangleq{}\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0.0117,0.2695,0.457}{if}}\penalty\thinspace a=a^{\prime}\penalty\thinspace\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0.0117,0.2695,0.457}{then}}\penalty\thinspace 1\penalty\thinspace\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0.0117,0.2695,0.457}{else}}\penalty\thinspace{0}

and
(\mbind\colon(A\to\mathcal{D}(B))\times\mathcal{D}(A)\to\mathcal{D}(B))
defined as
\mbind(f,\mu)(b)\triangleq{}\sum_{a\in A}\mu(a)\cdot f(a)(b)
.
We often use the notation
\mu\mathbin{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{\scalebox{0.75}{$\gg\mkern-6.0mu=$}}}f
for
\mbind(f,\mu)
.

Some examples of distributions include the
null distribution

\mathbf{0}:\mathcal{D}(A)
, given by
\lambda x.0
, and the uniform distribution
{\mathfrak{U}N}\colon\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{N})
, which returns
1/(N+1)
for every
n\in\{0,\dots,N\}
and
0
otherwise.

The
expected value
of
X\colon A\to[0,1]
w.r.t.
\mu
is defined as
\ExpectedValue_{\mu}\!\left[\mkern 1.5muX\mkern 1.5mu\right]\triangleq{}\sum_{a\in A}\mu(a)\cdot X(a)
.
The
mass
of
\mu
is defined as
|\mu|\triangleq{}\ExpectedValue_{\mu}\!\left[\mkern 1.5mu\lambda x.1\mkern 1.5mu\right]
.

Foxtrot uses
approximate
probabilistic coupling
(
Sato 2016
;
Haselwarter et al. 2025
)
to relate pairs of probabilistic programs.

Definition 2.1 (Approximate Coupling).

Let
\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{D}(A)
and
\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{D}(B)
.
Given
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
and a relation
R\subseteq A\times B
, we say that there exists an
(\varepsilon,R)
-coupling of
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
if for all
[0,1]
-valued random variables
X:A\rightarrow[0,1]
and
Y:B\rightarrow[0,1]
, s.t.
(a,b)\in R
implies
X(a)\leq Y(b)
, we have the inequality
\ExpectedValue_{\mu_{1}}\!\left[\mkern 1.5muX\mkern 1.5mu\right]\leq\ExpectedValue_{\mu_{2}}\!\left[\mkern 1.5muY\mkern 1.5mu\right]+\varepsilon
.
We write
\mu_{1}\lesssim_{\varepsilon}\mu_{2}:R
if such an
(\varepsilon,R)
-coupling exists.

\operatorname{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0.0117,0.2695,0.457}{rand}}}N

\Gamma
is a context assigning types to program variables, and
\Theta
contains type variables that may occur in
\Gamma
and
\tau
.

Thread Pools and Schedulers

The
\mathsf{tpStep}
function takes a configuration
\rho=(\vec{e},\sigma)
and an index
j
and returns a distribution over new configurations after stepping the
j
-th thread:

\mathsf{tpStep}(\vec{e},\sigma)(j)\triangleq\begin{cases}\mathbf{0}&\text{if $(\vec{e},\sigma)$ is final,}\\
\mret(\vec{e},\sigma)&\text{if $e_{j}\in\kern-1.99997pt\textit{Val}$ or $j\geq|\vec{e}|$,}\\
\mathsf{step}(e_{j},\sigma)\mathbin{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{\scalebox{0.75}{$\gg\mkern-6.0mu=$}}}\lambda\penalty\thinspace(e_{j}^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime},\vec{e}^{\prime}).\penalty\thinspace\mret(\vec{e}[j\mapsto e_{j}^{\prime}]\mathbin{+\kern-4.30554pt+}\vec{e}^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

If
\rho
is final, we return immediately. If
e_{j}
is a value or does not exist, we take a stutter step. Otherwise, we step the
j
-th thread and add the spawned threads to the thread pool.

The choice of which thread to step is determined by a
scheduler
. A (probabilistic, stateful) scheduler is defined by a transition function
\zeta:(\kern-0.50003pt\textit{SchedSt}\times\kern-0.50003pt\textit{Cfg})\to\mathcal{D}(\kern-0.50003pt\textit{SchedSt}\times\mathbb{N})
, which takes in an internal state
\Xi\in\kern-0.50003pt\textit{SchedSt}
and a configuration
\rho
, and returns a distribution on its new internal state and the index of the thread to step next.

Given a configuration
\rho
, a scheduler
\zeta
, and a scheduler state
\Xi
, we can now define the single scheduler-step reduction function
\mathsf{schStep}_{\zeta}{(\Xi,\rho)}\in\mathcal{D}(\kern-0.50003pt\textit{SchedSt}\times\kern-0.50003pt\textit{Cfg})
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathsf{schStep}_{\zeta}{(\Xi,\rho)}\;\triangleq\quad\zeta(\Xi,\rho)\mathbin{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{\scalebox{0.75}{$\gg\mkern-6.0mu=$}}}\lambda\penalty\thinspace(\Xi^{\prime},j).\penalty\thinspace\mathsf{tpStep}(\rho,j)\mathbin{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{\scalebox{0.75}{$\gg\mkern-6.0mu=$}}}\lambda\penalty\thinspace\rho^{\prime}.\penalty\thinspace\mret(\Xi^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})

Informally,
\mathsf{schStep}_{\zeta}{(\Xi,\rho)}
first evaluates
\zeta(\Xi,\rho)
to get a new state
\Xi^{\prime}
and index
j
, steps the
j
-th thread to obtain the new configuration
\rho^{\prime}
, and returns the new scheduler state and configuration.

Full Program Execution

We now define
n
-step program execution w.r.t. a scheduler
\zeta
with the recursive function
\mathsf{exec}_{\zeta,n}:(\kern-0.50003pt\textit{SchedSt}\times\kern-0.50003pt\textit{Cfg})\to\mathcal{D}(\kern-1.99997pt\textit{Val})

defined below.
Intuitively,
\mathsf{exec}_{\zeta,n}(\Xi,\rho)(v)
represents the probability of returning a value
v
in the first thread after at most
n
steps of
\rho
w.r.t. the scheduler
\zeta
with starting scheduler state
\Xi
.
The full program execution is then defined as the limit of
\mathsf{exec}_{\zeta,n}
, which exists by monotonicity and continuity, i.e.
\mathsf{exec}_{\zeta}(\Xi,\rho)\triangleq\mathsf{lim}_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathsf{exec}_{\zeta,n}(\Xi,\rho)
.

\mathsf{exec}_{\zeta,n}(\Xi,\rho)\triangleq\begin{cases}\mret v&\text{if $\rho=(v\cdot\vec{e},\sigma)$ for some $v\in\kern-1.99997pt\textit{Val}$},\\
\mathbf{0}&\text{if $n=0$ and }\rho\text{ is not final},\\
\mathsf{schStep}_{\zeta}{(\Xi,\rho)}\mathbin{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{\scalebox{0.75}{$\gg\mkern-6.0mu=$}}}\mathsf{exec}_{\zeta,n-1}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The probability that a starting configuration
\rho
terminates under scheduler
\zeta
with starting scheduler state
\Xi
is hence defined as
\mathsf{exec}^{\Downarrow}_{\zeta}(\Xi,\rho)\triangleq{}|\mathsf{exec}_{\zeta}(\Xi,\rho)|
.

The upper termination probability of an arbitrary configuration
\rho
is taken as the supermum of the termination probability ranging over all possible schedulers and initial scheduler states, i.e.
\mathsf{exec}^{\sqcup\Downarrow}(\rho)\triangleq\sup_{\zeta,\Xi}\mathsf{exec}^{\Downarrow}_{\zeta}(\Xi,\rho)
.
We use the upper termination probability
\mathsf{exec}^{\sqcup\Downarrow}
as the observation predicate for contextual refinement. In other words, we consider
may-termination

(
Birkedal et al. 2013
;
Aguirre and Birkedal 2023
)
as the main property of interest for the refinement
2
2

2

We leave the problem of
must-termination
refinement of concurrent probabilistic programs for future work (see
Section

7
).
.

We here also provide an auxiliary definition of partial program execution
\mathsf{pexec}_{\zeta,n}:(\kern-0.50003pt\textit{SchedSt}\times\kern-0.50003pt\textit{Cfg})\rightarrow\mathcal{D}(\kern-0.50003pt\textit{SchedSt}\times\kern-0.50003pt\textit{Cfg})
, used in the model of Foxtrot.

\displaystyle\mathsf{pexec}_{\zeta,n}(\Xi,\rho)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mret(\Xi,\rho)&\text{if $\rho$ is final or $n=0$},\\
\mathsf{schStep}_{\zeta}{(\Xi,\rho)}\mathbin{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{\scalebox{0.75}{$\gg\mkern-6.0mu=$}}}\mathsf{pexec}_{\zeta,n-1}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

We can view
\mathsf{pexec}
as a relaxation of
\mathsf{exec}
which keeps probability mass on configurations that are not final, whereas the latter only considers final configurations.

2.4.
Contextual Refinement

Since we are in a typed setting, we consider only typed contexts.
A program context is well-typed, written
\mathcal{C}:(\Theta\mid\Gamma\vdash\tau)\Rightarrow(\Theta^{\prime}\mid\Gamma^{\prime}\vdash\tau^{\prime})
, if for any term
e
where
\Theta\mid\Gamma\vdash e:\tau
we have
\Theta^{\prime}\mid\Gamma^{\prime}\vdash\mathcal{C}{[}e{]}:\tau^{\prime}
.
We say expression
e_{1}

contextually refines
expression
e_{2}
if for all well-typed program contexts
\mathcal{C}
resulting in a closed program, the upper termination probability of
\mathcal{C}{[}e_{1}{]}
is bounded by the upper termination probability of
\mathcal{C}{[}e_{2}{]}
:

\displaystyle\Theta\mid\Gamma\vdash e_{1}\precsim_{\text{ctx}}e_{2}:\tau\triangleq{}

\displaystyle\forall\tau^{\prime},(\mathcal{C}:(\Theta\mid\Gamma\vdash\tau)\Rightarrow(\emptyset\mid\emptyset\vdash\tau^{\prime})),\sigma.\penalty\thinspace

\displaystyle\ \mathsf{exec}^{\sqcup\Downarrow}([\mathcal{C}{[}e_{1}{]}],\sigma)\leq\mathsf{exec}^{\sqcup\Downarrow}([\mathcal{C}{[}e_{2}{]}],\sigma)

Note that the statement itself is in the meta-logic (
e.g
., Rocq) and makes no mention of Foxtrot or Iris.
Contextual equivalence
\Theta\mid\Gamma\vdash e_{1}\simeq_{\text{ctx}}e_{2}:\tau
is defined as a refinement in both directions,
i.e
.
(\Theta\mid\Gamma\vdash e_{1}\precsim_{\text{ctx}}e_{2}:\tau)\land(\Theta\mid\Gamma\vdash e_{2}\precsim_{\text{ctx}}e_{1}:\tau)
.
We simply write
e_{1}\precsim_{\text{ctx}}e_{2}
or
e_{1}\simeq_{\text{ctx}}e_{2}
if the type
\tau
can be inferred and that
\Gamma
and
\Theta
are empty.

;

)

, which
allows us to reason as if the two sampled values on both programs are related by a bijective function

under the same sampling space
\{0,\dots,N\}
.

\frac{f\penalty\ \text{bijection}\quad\forall n\leq N.\penalty\thinspace{\left\{j\Mapsto K{[}f(n){]}\right\}}\penalty\thinspace n\penalty\thinspace{\left\{\Phi\right\}}}{{\left\{j\Mapsto K{[}\operatorname{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0.0117,0.2695,0.457}{rand}}}N{]}\right\}}\penalty\thinspace\operatorname{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0.0117,0.2695,0.457}{rand}}}N\penalty\thinspace{\left\{\Phi\right\}}}\quad\textsc{right={{ht-couple}}}

ht-couple
is sufficient for reasoning about various concurrent probabilistic programs.

Figure

2

(
Zilberstein et al. 2024
)
. Here, we are given two sources of entropy,
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
, the former being unreliable as it can be adversarially controlled by the scheduler, while
x_{2}
is of high quality. Nonetheless, we are still able to “combine” these two sources and produce a high-quality output that behaves like a
\operatorname{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0.0117,0.2695,0.457}{rand}}}1

\operatorname{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0.0117,0.2695,0.457}{rand}}}1

\operatorname{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0.0117,0.2695,0.457}{rand}}}

), defining an invariant to share the reference

Rejection Sampling

We now turn our attention to another difficult-to-verify pattern in probabilistic computation: rejection sampling schemes.
Rejection samplers are a class of Las Vegas algorithms; they generate random samples from a target distribution by repeatedly sampling from another distribution and only accepting a sample if it satisfies certain conditions. Though this rejection sampling technique is ubiquitous, it is also challenging to reason about, particularly due to its use of unbounded looping in the implementation.

Consider the rejection sampler program on the left-hand side of the refinement in

\operatorname{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0.0117,0.2695,0.457}{rand}}}M
. We emphasize that this result is
novel
and is stronger than those found in previous work
(
Haselwarter et al. 2025
)
, as we show that this contextual equivalence holds even in the concurrent setting.

Again, one might ask, can we do the same for the other right-to-left direction of the refinement? Alas, reality is often disappointing and the answer is

Secondly, we cannot apply Löb induction for this direction; in the case that the right-hand side program samples a rejected program, we can only proceed by stepping the right-hand side program back to the start of the recursive function with the left-hand side program kept unchanged. We hence cannot eliminate the later modality in the hypothesis since we do not take any steps on the left program.

To overcome these two limitations, we utilize presampling tapes which we saw previously and

\varepsilon

\varepsilon

\varepsilon>0

We now review the basic rules of error credits.
The rule

\mathsf{False}

\UpTo{}\mkern-4.5mu\left(1\right)

\mathsf{False}

0

err-1

pupd-err

ind-err-amp

5.1.
Full-information Schedulers

As described in
Section

2.3
, since
ConcRandML
is concurrent, we resolve the non-determinism of scheduling the threads through a (stateful probabilistic) scheduler. However, the definition of a scheduler in
Section

that is only used internally for defining the logic, which we simply write as FIsch(es). We write
\mathsf{FIsch}
to denote the type of FIsches.

Basic Definition of FIsches

From a high-level perspective, FIsches are different from normal schedulers in three aspects.
Firstly, the internal state of FIsches are fixed to be
\mathsf{FISchSt}\triangleq{}List(\kern-0.50003pt\textit{Cfg}^{\prime}\times\mathbb{N})
, where
\kern-0.50003pt\textit{Cfg}^{\prime}\triangleq{}\textit{List}(\kern-0.50003pt\textit{Expr})\times\kern-0.50003pt\textit{Heap}
. We implicitly coerce configurations
\rho
of type
Cfg
to that of type
\kern-0.50003pt\textit{Cfg}^{\prime}
by removing the tape map (
Tapes
)
6
6

6

We remove the tape map to ensure FIsches cannot schedule threads based on the content of the tapes.
. Given two FIsch states
\Xi,\Xi^{\prime}\in\mathsf{FISchSt}
, we write
\Xi\leq\Xi^{\prime}
if
\Xi
is a prefix of
\Xi^{\prime}
.

Secondly, the type of FIsches is different from a normal scheduler. Specifically, FIsches are defined by a transition function
\Upsilon
of the type
(\mathsf{FISchSt}\times\kern-0.50003pt\textit{Cfg})\to\mathsf{option}(\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{N}))
.

Lastly, all FIsches
\Upsilon
satisfy a
consistency
condition, where for all FIsch states
\Xi
and configurations
\rho
, if
\Upsilon(\Xi,\rho)=\operatorname{\mathsf{None}}
, then for all other configurations
\rho^{\prime}
we have
\Upsilon(\Xi,\rho^{\prime})=\operatorname{\mathsf{None}}
.

Semantics of FIsches

We modify the operational semantics of
ConcRandML
with respect to FIsches, such that the thread-stepping function
\mathsf{FItpStep}
of FIsches is more “eager”, i.e. it attempts to step the corresponding thread regardless of whether the configuration is final or not:

\mathsf{FItpStep}(\vec{e},\sigma)(j)\triangleq\begin{cases}\mret(\vec{e},\sigma)&\text{if $e_{j}\in\kern-1.99997pt\textit{Val}$ or $j\geq|\vec{e}|$ ,}\\
\mathsf{step}(e_{j},\sigma)\mathbin{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{\scalebox{0.75}{$\gg\mkern-6.0mu=$}}}\lambda\penalty\thinspace(e_{j}^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime},\vec{e}^{\prime}).\penalty\thinspace\mret(\vec{e}[j\mapsto e_{j}^{\prime}]\mathbin{+\kern-4.30554pt+}\vec{e}^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Given a configuration
\rho
, a FIsch
\Upsilon
, and a FIsch state
\Xi

\mathsf{FIschStep}_{\Upsilon}{(\Xi,\rho)}\in\mathcal{D}(\kern-0.50003pt\textit{SchedSt}\times\kern-0.50003pt\textit{Cfg})

\displaystyle\mathsf{FIschStep}_{\Upsilon}{(\Xi,\rho)}\;\triangleq\begin{cases}\mu\mathbin{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{\scalebox{0.75}{$\gg\mkern-6.0mu=$}}}\lambda\penalty\thinspace j.\penalty\thinspace\mathsf{FItpStep}(\rho,j)\mathbin{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{\scalebox{0.75}{$\gg\mkern-6.0mu=$}}}\lambda\penalty\thinspace\rho^{\prime}.\penalty\thinspace\mret(\Xi\cdot[(\rho,j)],\rho^{\prime})&\Upsilon(\Xi,\rho)=\operatorname{\mathsf{Some}}\mu\\

The

\operatorname{\mathsf{None}}

\displaystyle\mathsf{FIexec}_{\Upsilon,n}(\Xi,\rho)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mret(\Xi,\rho)&\Upsilon(\Xi,\rho)=\operatorname{\mathsf{None}}\\

In contrast to the

The full program execution of a FIsch is taken as the limit of its

\mathsf{FIexec}_{\Upsilon}(\Xi,\rho)\triangleq\mathsf{lim}_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathsf{FIexec}_{\Upsilon,n}(\Xi,\rho)

6.
Related Work

Contextual Equivalences of Higher-Order Programs

There are various prior work in utilizing binary logical relations to prove contextual equivalences of higher-order programs.
For example, there is a long line of work in utilizing step-indexed logical relations for establishing refinements of higher-order concurrent languages. In particular, we highlight CaReSL
(
Turon et al. 2013
)
, one of the first logics for fine-grained concurrency, which was then mechanized in Iris
(
Krebbers et al. 2017b
;
Timany 2018
)
. The mechanized concurrent separation logic ReLoC
(
Frumin et al. 2021
)
extended CaReSL to internalize refinement judgements as first-class logical statements, providing more concise rules for reasoning about invariants and logical atomicity. Similarly, there are also many logical relations specialized for probabilistic languages.
Bizjak and Birkedal 2015
defined a step-indexed, biorthogonal logical relation for reasoning about contextual equivalences of a higher-order probabilistic language with discrete random variables and state. This was then extended for more complicated language features such as continuous random sampling
(
Culpepper and Cobb 2017
;
Wand et al. 2018
)
and countable nondeterminism
(
Aguirre and Birkedal 2023
)
. The mechanized logic Clutch
(
Gregersen et al. 2024
)
first introduced presampling tapes for asynchronous couplings, which we also use to implement advanced coupling rules for reasoning about rejection sampling and multiple concurrent samplings in Foxtrot. We also took inspiration from Approxis
(
Haselwarter et al. 2025
)
, which used error credits to establish contextual equivalences of rejection samplers by means of approximation.

There are other techniques other than logical relations to verify contextual equivalences, e.g.
Ehrhard et al. 2014
utilized probabilistic coherence spaces to construct a fully abstract denotational model for PCF programs with a discrete random primitive. Much work has also been done in using bisimulation techniques to establish the contextual equivalences of programs written in the probabilistic
\lambda
-calculus, whether it be call-by-value
(
Crubillé and Dal Lago 2014
)
, call-by-name
(
Dal Lago et al. 2014
)
, or call-by-need
(
Sabel et al. 2022
)
.

Compared to all the work above, our work in proving contextual equivalences of higher-order (stateful) programs is the
first
to be extended to the concurrent and randomized setting, which we believe is a
significant
contribution as reasoning about this mixture is notoriously hard.

Logics for Probability and Concurrency

There has been previous work on program logics for reasoning about properties (other than contextual refinement) of concurrent probabilistic programs.

McIver et al. 2016
first introduced the probabilistic rely-guarantee calculus for reasoning about the quantitative correctness of probabilistic concurrent programs without support for dynamically-allocated local state.
Fesefeldt et al. 2022
developed concurrent quantitative separation logic for reasoning about quantitative properties of concurrent, randomized, heap-manipulating programs. Polaris
(
Tassarotti and Harper 2019
)
is similar to our work in that it is also a mechanized relational logic in Iris, but the refinement is constructed between a concurrent probabilistic program and a simpler monadic representation model, which can then further be analyzed to establish properties of the original program, e.g. bounds on expected values. ExpIris
(
Lohse and Garg 2024
)
is another mechanized program logic in Iris; its Hoare triples are annotated with a parameter called the potential, which is used to prove bounds on expected costs of concurrent probabilistic programs. The recent Probabilistic Concurrent Outcome Logic
(
Zilberstein et al. 2024
)
, a variant of Outcome Logic
(
Zilberstein et al. 2023
)
, supports compositional reasoning of the distribution of possible outcomes from the execution of concurrent randomized programs.

Most similar to our logic is that of Coneris
(
Li et al. 2025
)
, a separation logic for proving error bounds of concurrent probabilistic programs. Firstly, the underlying language
ConcRandML
of Foxtrot is identical to that in Coneris. Moreover, our use of error credits is heavily inspired by that of Coneris
(
Li et al. 2025
)
(and similar logics such as Eris
(
Aguirre et al. 2024
)
and Approxis
(
Haselwarter et al. 2025
)
), where the error credits were originally used to prove error bounds of (concurrent) probabilistic programs. We also adopt Coneris’ probabilistic update modality and extend it into our relational setting for symbolically executing right-hand side programs in addition to presampling onto tapes.

7.
Conclusion

We presented Foxtrot, the first higher-order separation logic for proving contextual refinements of higher-order concurrent probabilistic programs with higher-order local state. In addition, we demonstrated its strengths on a wide range of examples involving complex local state, random sampling, and concurrent behavior, that are previously out of scope.

In the future, we would like to extend Foxtrot to reason about contextual refinement of programs under schedulers with restricted power for proving security guarantees of distributed cryptographic protocols, e.g. schedulers which can only rely on its internal state to decide which thread to step next. We also aim to implement prophecy variables
(
Abadi and Lamport 1991
;
Abadi and Lamport 1988
;
Jung et al. 2019
)
within Foxtrot so to prove more interesting equivalences. Lastly, it would be interesting to consider modifying Foxtrot for proving must-termination contextual refinements
(
Birkedal et al. 2013
;
Aguirre and Birkedal 2023
)
of concurrent probabilistic programs.

\mathsf{sstep}
