Beyond the Worst Case: Extending Differential Privacy Guarantees to Realistic Adversaries
Marika Swanberg, Meenatchi Sundaram Muthu Selva Annamalai, Jamie Hayes, Borja Balle, Adam Smith

Abstract
Differential Privacy (DP) is a family of definitions that bound the worst-case privacy leakage of a mechanism. One important feature of the worst-case DP guarantee is it naturally implies protections against adversaries with less prior information, more sophisticated attack goals, and complex measures of a successful attack. However, the analytical tradeoffs between the adversarial model and the privacy protections conferred by DP are not well understood thus far. To that end, this work sheds light on what the worst-case guarantee of DP implies about the success of attackers that are more representative of real-world privacy risks. In this paper, we present a single flexible framework that generalizes and extends the patchwork of bounds on DP mechanisms found in prior work. Our framework allows us to compute high-probability guarantees for DP mechanisms on a large family of natural attack settings that previous bounds do not capture. One class of such settings is the approximate reconstruction of multiple individuals’ data, such as inferring nearly entire columns of a tabular data set from noisy marginals and extracting sensitive information from DP-trained language models. We conduct two empirical case studies to illustrate the versatility of our bounds and compare them to the success of state-of-the-art attacks. Specifically, we study attacks that extract non-uniform PII from a DP-trained language model, as well as multi-column reconstruction attacks where the adversary has access to some columns in the clear and attempts to reconstruct the remaining columns for each person’s record. We find that the absolute privacy risk of attacking non-uniform data is highly dependent on the adversary’s prior probability of success. Our high probability bounds give us a nuanced understanding of the privacy leakage of DP mechanisms in a variety of previously understudied attack settings.

Unlike ad-hoc methods, DP provably limits the amount of information that a mechanism reveals about any individual by bounding the influence of any person’s data on the output distribution of the mechanism.

The DP guarantee is specified by privacy parameters

The lack of translation to more realistic threats is, in part, why setting the privacy parameters in real deployments is challenging. There is a wide range of DP parameter settings used in real deployments
[
20
]

In this work, we provide rigorous bounds that formalize the longstanding intuition that moderate privacy parameters protect against less-informed adversaries executing harder attacks

2
Background

In this section, we cover relevant background topics of DP and privacy risks. Informally, DP bounds the influence that any single user has on the output distribution of a mechanism. Throughout this paper, we assume the “replace-one” adjacency where exactly one record is replaced with another record.

Definition 2.1

(Approximate DP
[
26
]
)
.

A randomized mechanism
\mathcal{M}:\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}
satisfies
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP if, for any two adjacent datasets
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}
and
S\subseteq\mathcal{R}
, it holds:

\Pr[\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x})\in S]\leq\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon}\Pr[\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\in S]+\delta

There are many other variants of DP, e.g., the pure DP variant where
\delta=0
and the more recent Rényi DP (
(\alpha,\gamma)
-RDP)
[
47
]
,
f
-DP
[
25
]
, and
\mu
-GDP
[
25
]
variants, which characterize more complex relationships between the output distributions;

Definition 2.2

(Stochastic Dominance)
.

Let
X,Y\in\mathbb{R}
be random variables. We say
X
is
stochastically dominated
by
Y
if for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
\Pr(X\geq t)\leq\Pr(Y\geq t)
.

2.1
Threat Modelling

Several prior works have focused on systematizing and proposing a taxonomy of threat models in privacy attacks
[
53
,
55
,
18
]
.
Here, we cover four main axes relevant to prior work deriving privacy leakage bounds for DP.

Attack Goals.

In a
membership inference attack
(MI), the goal of the adversary is to infer whether a record
was used as input to the mechanism given its output, i.e., determine whether
x_{c}\in\mathbf{x}
given
a\leftarrow\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x})
.
In an
attribute inference attack
(AI), the adversary is instead given a partial record that is known to be from the input dataset consisting of all but one “secret” attribute. The goal of the adversary is to then infer the value of the secret attribute, i.e. assuming
x_{c}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{k},v_{s})\in\mathbf{x}
, determine
v_{s}
given
(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})
and
a\leftarrow\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x})
.
Lastly, in a
reconstruction attack
, the adversary is only given the output of the mechanism and attempts to reconstruct whole canaries from the input dataset, i.e., reconstruct
\mathbf{z}
given
a\leftarrow\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x})
such that
|\mathbf{z}\cap\mathbf{x}|\neq 0
.
Note that this is the
simplest
form of a reconstruction attack, also called “exact match”, but we discuss more advanced reconstruction success metrics below.

Prior Knowledge.

Even though in theory, DP guarantees hold against any prior knowledge held by the adversary, to ease evaluation, privacy attacks are typically evaluated against
uniform priors
, e.g., canaries are randomly included with probability 0.5 for MI
[
58
]
or chosen uniformly at random from a domain for reconstruction
[
43
]
.
However, real-world attacks often have non-uniform priors as canaries are not typically drawn from uniform distributions, which can boost the success of adversaries in practice
[
29
,
21
]
.

Number of Targets.

Although DP provides privacy protections against
worst-case
individual target records, the adversary may not always have access to all but one target record as assumed by DP.
Furthermore, the attack goal may not always be narrowly tailored to attacking a single target record but can also be to reconstruct or infer the secret attributes of as many (possibly unknown number of) records as possible
[
13
,
21
,
14
,
28
,
2
,
12
]
.

Success Metric.

The simplest and most common measure of an adversary’s success is exact match, which entails verifying whether the inferred secret (membership, attribute, or whole record) matches the original secret exactly.
However, this may not always be possible, especially when reconstructing high dimensional data such as images
[
6
,
10
,
31
]
.
In these settings, more complex success metrics may be more meaningful, e.g., approximate reconstruction according to an appropriate distance measure.

3
Our Privacy Leakage Framework

In this section, we bound the success of an adversary in a
generic attack game
. We begin by specifying the game and the setting it represents. Then in Theorem
3.2
, we state our bound for pure DP mechanisms in this generic setting. After dicussing multiple interpretations of the bound, we describe how to encode specific attack settings in the parameters of our framework. Finally, we extend our bounds to approximate DP mechanisms and discuss the nuances that arise in this challenging setting.

3.1
Generic Attack Game

We now define a
generic attack game
in Algorithm
1
that describes the family of attacks that our bounds cover. The game works as follows. First,
n
target records
X
are sampled from a given product distribution
\mathcal{D}
. The records
X
are fed to the mechanism
\mathcal{M}
, which produces an output
a
. The adversary
\mathcal{A}
uses the mechanism output
a
, the data generating distribution
\mathcal{D}
, and the mechanism specification
\mathcal{M}
to produce
k
attack attempts
\mathbf{z}
. Lastly, the quality of the attack attempt
\mathbf{z}
is measured against the true dataset
X
according to some success metric
\mathcal{L}
.

We call

over the records, and the prior

for person

encodes the knowledge that the adversary has for that target person’s data before seeing the mechanism output. Our bounds handle arbitrary priors

The

\mathcal{L}
encodes the goal of the adversary. The metric
\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\{0,\ldots,n\}
takes a dataset

\mathcal{L}

Definition 3.1

(Decomposable metric)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Z}
be the data and attack domains, respectively. A metric
\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\{0,\ldots,n\}
is
decomposable
into
\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{n}
if

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})=\sum_{i\in[n]}\ell_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{z}).

In Section
3.3
we discuss how to encode common attack goals—including MI, AI, and reconstruction—in the success metric. Although we do not explicitly state it, our generic attack game allows any number of fixed “non-target” records
\mathbf{x}_{fixed}
to be input to the mechanism in addition to the sampled target records. We assume that the adversary has full knowledge of these records—and can even select them. As a result, the attack success is not measured with respect to the non-target records. We omit the fixed records in our attack game and bounds, but one can imagine the mechanism
\mathcal{M}
takes these as a hard-coded input.

3.2
Bounding Pure DP Mechanisms

We now present our bound for
\varepsilon
-DP mechanisms. For a private dataset that is drawn from the prior distribution, and given any fixed mechanism output
a
, we present a high probability bound on the attack success as a function of: (1) the mechanism’s DP parameters, and (2) the adversary’s success probability if they had only used prior information. Formally:

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
satisfy
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP. Let dataset
X\sim\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes\mathcal{D}_{n}
be drawn from a product distribution. Let
\mathcal{A}
be an adversary and let
\mathcal{L}
be decomposable into
\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{n}
. Then, for all
v\in\mathbb{R}
and for all mechanism outputs
a\subseteq Supp(\mathcal{M})
:

\displaystyle\Pr_{X\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathcal{L}(X,\mathcal{A}(a))\geq v|\mathcal{M}(X)=a\right]\leq\Pr_{S_{i}\sim\mathsf{Bernoulli}(\beta_{i}(\mathbf{z},\varepsilon))}\left[\sum_{i\in[n]}S_{i}\geq v\right],

where
\mathbf{z}=\mathcal{A}(a)
and
\beta_{i}(\mathbf{z},\varepsilon)=\frac{e^{\varepsilon}}{e^{\varepsilon}-1+\frac{1}{\Pr_{X\sim\mathcal{D}_{i}}[\ell_{i}(X,\mathbf{z})=1]}}

In other words, the distribution over the attack success for any attacker and any mechanism output is
stochastically dominated
by a simple sum over Bernoulli random variables with different flipping probabilities. The bound is calibrated to the probability that the specific attack attempt
\mathbf{z}
would have succeeded according to
\mathcal{L}
against a mechanism with perfect privacy. Specifically, the flipping probability
\beta_{i}
for each target person
i\in[n]
depends on the adversary’s prior probability of successfully attacking a fresh sample from person
i
’s distribution,
\Pr_{X\sim\mathcal{D}_{i}}[\ell_{i}(X,\mathbf{z})=1]
. This succinctly captures the phenomenon that for natural (non-uniform) data distributions, some targets are easier to “attack” using only prior knowledge, and some attack success measures are more permissive than others. This calibration to the prior is the key for achieving such a general bound. In Proposition
B.1
we show Theorem
3.2
bound is tight for distributions that are sufficiently close to uniform. The analysis of
[
37
]
on which types of DP mechanisms yield tight auditing bounds in this “one run” framework, could likely be extended to our more general bounds, although the focus of our work is not auditing.

Our goal in applying Theorem
3.2
is to estimate the attack success
\mathcal{L}(X,\mathcal{A}(a))
on a fresh sample of data, for a fixed mechanism output
a
and attacker
\mathcal{A}
. We can frame this as a statistical estimation task with the goal of outputting a statistical upper bound on
\mathcal{L}(X,\mathcal{A}(a))
with a corresponding confidence level
\alpha
.

Our estimator runs the mechanism
\mathcal{M}
on one dataset sampled from
\mathcal{D}
, and subsequently runs the attack
\mathcal{A}
on the output of the mechanism. The estimator outputs
v_{UB}
and a confidence level
\alpha
with the following guarantee: with probability at least
1-\alpha
, the attack success
\mathcal{L}(X,\mathcal{A}(a))
is at most
v_{UB}
when evaluated on a fresh sample of data. In our experiments, we are interested in the distribution over
v_{UB}
, so we will report the values for a range of confidence levels.

There are a number of ways to view and use such an estimator. As in the
privacy auditing
literature
[
23
,
34
,
58
,
3
]
, one may convert the statistical estimator into a hypothesis test to detect violations of the DP guarantee for a particular mechanism that indicate it was not implemented correctly. If a real attack is more successful than
v_{UB}
for a high confidence level like
\alpha=0.95
then one can confidently refute the hypothesis that the mechanism is differentially private with the claimed privacy parameters. In this work, we are more interested in characterizing the privacy leakage of faithfully implemented DP mechanisms. To that end, we use the empirical estimator to measure
v_{UB}
for different significance levels and compare that to the success of our empirical attacks.

Estimating bit leakage.

Theorem
3.2
gives a precise characterization of the number of bits leaked per person by pure-DP mechanisms. Concretely, assume there is only one target
X
and one attempt
\mathbf{z}
, and define exact reconstruction as
\ell(X,\mathbf{z})=\mathbb{I}\{X=\mathbf{z}\}
. Now, fix some
\varepsilon
-DP mechanism. Let each
\mathcal{D}
be uniform on a domain of size
2^{d}
. In this problem setup, a reconstruction is successful if and only if the adversary recovers
d
bits about the target. Thus, the tradeoff between the bound on the right hand side (call it
\alpha
) and
d
characterizes the distribution over how many bits can be recovered by an adversary. Applying our bound, since we only have one target and one guess, the probability of leaking
d
bits about the target person is just the flipping probability:

\frac{e^{\varepsilon}}{e^{\varepsilon}-1+2^{d}}.
From this one can also work out that with probability at most
\alpha
, an
\varepsilon
-DP mechanism leaks

\log_{2}\left(e^{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}-1\right)+1\right)\quad\text{bits}.

For example, this tells us that there is a very low probability of successfully reconstructing 9 uniform digits from any
\varepsilon
-DP mechanism when
\varepsilon<17
, effectively ruling out the Secret Sharer extraction attack
[
8
]
.

3.3
Encoding Attacks

Our bound is flexible enough to encode most attack settings from the taxonomy developed by Cummings et al.
[
18
]
.

Setting attack goals.

We can encode MI in the same way as Steinke, Nasr, and Jagielski
[
58
]
do. In their bound there is a fixed set of
n
records, each of which is included independently in the final dataset with probability 0.5. In our notation, each
X_{i}
will denote the membership bit of record
i\in[n]
and they are sampled independently from
\mathcal{D}_{i}=\mathsf{Bernoulli}(0.5)
. The mechanism
\mathcal{M}
takes the membership bits
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
as input and uses these to determine which of the original
n
records to include in the computation.
The MI adversary produces an
n
-bit vector of guesses
\mathbf{z}
about each record’s membership bit. The attack’s success on each record
i\in[n]
is exact match
\ell_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{z})=\mathbb{I}\{\mathbf{x}_{i}=\mathbf{z}_{i}\}
. At the other extreme, to encode a reconstruction goal, in the bound we set the data distribution
\mathcal{D}_{i}
for each person
i\in[n]
to be the data generating distribution for that person’s data.

Interpolating between the two extremes, to encode AI with some known attributes
\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{known}}
about each target individual, we take person
i
’s data distribution
\mathcal{D}_{i}
to be the full data generating distribution
conditioned on
\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{known}}
. So
X_{i}
is a random variable over the unknown attribute(s) conditioned on the known ones.

When there is more than one unknown attribute we call this novel attack
multi-attribute inference
(M-AI). Specifically, given access to the output of the mechanism and a subset of attributes for all records in the dataset, the goal of the adversary is to reconstruct all remaining attributes for all samples in the dataset, i.e., assuming
X=(X^{\mathrm{known}}||X^{\mathrm{unknown}})
the adversary is given
o\leftarrow\mathcal{M}(X)
and
X^{\mathrm{known}}
and aims to reconstruct
X^{\mathrm{unknown}}
. In essence, M-AI generalizes AI to allow for reconstructions of multiple attributes and is an instance of reconstruction where the adversary has access to auxiliary information about each record.

Encoding complex success metrics.

The loss function(s)
\ell_{i}
(s) can be set to encapsulate individual-level approximate reconstruction as done in prior work
[
6
,
31
]
and can be extended to
population-level
reconstruction as well e.g., measure whether
any
reconstruction is within some
L_{2}
threshold
\tau
of the original record, i.e.,
\ell_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{z})=\mathbb{I}\{(\min_{j}||\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{z}_{j}||_{2})\leq\tau\}
.

Using non-uniform priors.

In Theorem
3.2
, the data generating distribution
\mathcal{D}_{i}
for each person
i\in[n]
can be set
arbitrarily
, as long as it is independent from others’ data distributions. The bound itself only depends on the prior probability of success for a particular attack attempt
\mathbf{z}
:
\Pr_{X\sim\mathcal{D}_{i}}[\ell_{i}(X,\mathbf{z})=1]
, that is, the probability that a freshly sampled record
X\sim\mathcal{D}_{i}
would be “close to” the attack attempt
\mathbf{z}
. Subsequently, bounds specific to a context (e.g., reconstructing passwords, names, credit card numbers) can be derived by setting this term either to a known exact distribution or estimated appropriately (see Section
5
for more details).

3.4
Bounding Approximate DP

Generalizing Theorem
3.2
to approximate DP comes with a number of challenges. Approximate DP mechanisms suffer from potentially unbounded privacy loss for a user (with some small probability
\delta
), so conditioning on the mechanism output requires more care.

We handle this challenge with approximate DP in a few ways. Theorem
3.3
gives a high probability bound conditioned on the mechanism output, however the bound depends on a (randomized) function of the mechanism output
F(a)
, which is left unspecified.
On the other hand, in Corollary
3.4
we give a simple closed-form version that takes the probability over the coins of the mechanism.
Here, we trade the simplicity of the bound with the computational overhead of having to estimate the probability over multiple runs of the mechanism.
In Theorem
3.5
we achieve the best of both: a particular, efficiently computable, instantiation for the function
F

4
Bridging Existing Attack Frameworks

In this section, we show how our framework bridges and extends the current state of the art frameworks bounding the privacy leakage for a handful of influential works.

4.1
Privacy Auditing in One Run

The influential work of Steinke, Nasr, and Jagielski
[
58
]
is perhaps the most closely related work to ours. At a high level, their work also gives a high probability bound on the success of an adversary with multiple targets, but only for MI. In Section
3.3
we describe how to encode their specific MI setting in our bounds.
We generalize their bounds beyond MI attacks in two ways. First, we allow the data to be generated according to general product distributions rather than just sampling membership bits. Second, we generalize beyond measuring success via exact match by incorporating complex success metrics into the prior success probability.

Auditing
f
-Differential Privacy in One Run (MMC).

Mahloujifar, Melis, and Chaudhuri
[
43
]
also derive MI bounds for
f
-DP mechanisms, which better captures common mechanisms like the Gaussian mechanism. They also generalize their membership inference bounds to what we call a “multi-membership inference” attack where each canary is drawn uniformly from a set of possible canaries, and the attacker is successful if they correctly guess the
index
of the true canary out of the possible ones.

Multi-membership has a number of limitations in real-world applicability. In multi-membership inference, canaries have to have some ordering within their domain and attacks typically search over the potentially large domain of canaries to find the correct index. Furthermore, multi-membership inference cannot handle approximate reconstruction, since the attack success is measured by exactly matching the
index
of the canary output by the adversary. On the other hand, in a full reconstruction attack, which is handled by our framework, the adversary outputs
the canary itself
(or an approximation of it) that does not need any ordering and can be compared against original records using potentially approximate distance functions. Lastly, the MMC bound is not flexible enough to handle cases where there are more guesses than canaries, i.e.,
k>n
.

4.2
Narcissus Resiliency

Perhaps the most interesting connection is that to the Narcissus Resiliency framework proposed by Cohen et al.
[
16
]
. They define a general framework for evaluating a wide range of data reconstruction attacks and protections, including: Predicate singling out
[
15
]
, membership inference, differential privacy, one-way functions
[
22
,
62
]
, and various guarantees of encryption schemes. The core insight of their framework is to measure the attacker’s success probability relative to its baseline probability of success on a fresh sample of data rather than using a fixed baseline for all attackers. A mechanism is considered “narcissus resilient” if the two success probabilities are close for all attackers. Specifically,

Definition 4.1

(Narcissus resiliency,

\mathbf{z}\leftarrow\mathcal{A}(a)\end{subarray}}[\mathcal{L}(X,\mathbf{z})=1]\leq e^{\varepsilon}\cdot\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\sim\mathcal{D}\\
Y\sim\mathcal{D}\\
a\leftarrow\mathcal{M}(X)\\
\mathbf{z}\leftarrow\mathcal{A}(a)\end{subarray}}[\mathcal{L}(Y,\mathbf{z})=1]+\delta.

We can directly compare our Corollary

to the Narcissus Resiliency bounds for
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP mechanisms. In Theorem F.1, they bound the RHS by

is the prior probability that predicate

is true. This is always strictly larger than our bound of
\frac{e^{\varepsilon}}{e^{\varepsilon}+1/p}+\delta

In addition, we give high probability bounds that hold even when conditioned on the mechanism output, which describes the dependence on the number of successful attacks and the privacy parameters. Therefore, while our bounds enjoy the same philosophical benefits that inspired the narcissus resiliency framework, our bounds on DP mechanisms are substantially tighter.

5
Bounding Data Extraction from Language Models

We now leverage the versatility of our bound to present novel reconstruction bounds for non-worst-case attack settings involving non-uniform priors.
Specifically, we focus on data extractions attacks in the context of language models (e.g., LLMs)
[
8

42
,
50

.
Although previous work has shown some success in effectively training language models on private data with DP
[
40
,
63
]
, sensitive data can still be extracted from such DP language models
[
42
]
.

The main reason for this is that data can be extracted from language models even if the extracted data was not part of the dataset, for instance if the data can be inferred from the context present in the sample
[
42
]
, similar data appears elsewhere in the dataset
[
41
]

In other words, the
prior probability
of extracting data from language models may be non-uniform.
However, these priors can be challenging to compute as the data-generating distribution of real-world text samples are not explicitly known.
Therefore, even though data extraction attacks have been shown to be possible, there have been no known bounds thus far.

In this section, we estimate the prior probabilities and derive
approximate
upper bounds for data extraction attacks instead.
Specifically, we focus on three types of data extraction—
uniform random canary
,
numerical password
, and
PII (Name)

Subsequently, we derive reconstruction bounds under these estimated priors and compare our theoretical bounds to a concrete reconstruction attack against a simple
n
-gram language model.

5.1
Estimating Priors

Uniform random canary.

Uniformly chosen canaries are most commonly used when evaluating data extraction attacks, as the explicit prior distribution makes it easy to identify when the attack is non-trivial.
Specifically, we derive bounds for the 9-digit uniform random canary from prior work
[
8
]
and set the prior probability to
10^{-9}
for all possible canaries.

Numerical password.

Data extraction attacks have also been used to extract sensitive data that appears
naturally
in the dataset such as credit card and social security numbers
[
8
]
.
Along this line, we consider 9-digit numerical passwords as they follow a similar format to uniformly chosen canaries.
We expect the prior distribution of numerical passwords to follow the Zipf’s law distribution under the assumption that a few passwords are exceedingly popular amongst users, e.g., the password ‘123456789’ is known to be used roughly 43M times in the wild
[
33
]
.
Therefore, we estimate the prior using a Zipf’s law distribution with parameter
s=1.1
following prior work in similar domains
[
29

27

Lastly, we consider the extraction of Personally Identifiable Information (PII)

However, unlike uniformly chosen canaries and numerical passwords, PIIs do not have a fixed format, are not expected to follow well-known distributions, and can possibly depend on the surrounding text

Therefore, estimating the prior probability of PII canaries can be particularly challenging.

To that end, we use a pre-trained LLM to estimate the prior over PIIs.
Specifically, following recent work
[
42

we focus on reconstructing names and sample 500 PII sequences from the Enron emails dataset
[

Each sequence consists of a single masked PII, which the adversary is tasked to reconstruct given the surrounding text.
To estimate the prior, we first extract a set of candidate PIIs that occur in these 500 PII sequences.
Next, we calculate the perplexity of the pre-trained GPT-2 (small) model
[
52

for each possible sequence and normalize it over the set of candidate PIIs to estimate the prior.

Disparate vulnerability within distributions.

First, unlike uniformly drawn canaries where the advantage bounds are the same for all possible canaries, for natural canaries (numerical passwords and PIIs), the advantage bounds can vary substantially depending on the prior probability of sampling each canary.
Specifically, canaries with higher priors admit attacks with substantially higher power (i.e., higher advantage bounds) than canaries with smaller priors.

Impact of non-uniform priors.

Furthermore, different distribution of priors can substantially affect the advantage bounds.
Specifically, even though numerical password canaries have the exact same length and format as the uniform canaries, the most probable numerical password canary has much higher advantage than the uniform canary for at all privacy levels, similar to the most probable PII canary.

Additionally, for PII canaries the advantage bounds for even the median probable canary is much larger than uniformly drawn canaries, suggesting that more than half of the PII canaries are at a substantially higher risk of privacy leakage than uniformly drawn 9-digit canaries commonly used in privacy evaluations.
Rather interestingly, the median numerical password canary has lower advantage bounds than uniform canary, probably owing to the heavily skewed nature of the Zipf distribution (priors are concentrated at the top few canaries).
Therefore, we observe that the exact distribution of canaries can significantly affect the privacy protections provided by DP.

This echoes our theoretical finding earlier that real-world non-uniform secrets are indeed more vulnerable to reconstruction than uniform random canaries that are sometimes used to test the privacy leakage from language models.

5.4
Key Takeaways

Our results suggest that non-uniform prior distributions commonly observed for natural canaries can have a large impact on the protections provided by DP.
Specifically, even within the same type of data, different canaries can have substantially different vulnerability to data extraction attacks.
Additionally, worst-case natural canaries are exposed to much higher privacy leakage than uniform canaries of similar length or format.
While there is a gap between our theoretical bounds and the achievable empirical attack success rate, this can be explained by the tightness of the privacy analysis and the ineffectiveness of practical attacks in combining prior information and information leakage from mechanisms.

In our experiments, we assume the “replace-one” adjacency and additionally assume that for a given set of attributes, full marginal vectors across all possible values are released.
Therefore, we calibrate the level of Gaussian noise added to a

Furthermore, as we are mainly focused on M-AI, we only calibrate the noise for the attributes that are

columns are unknown out of
d

\binom{d}{k}-\binom{d-d^{\prime}}{k}
compositions of the noisy

Lastly, we fix

[

Formal definitions of the noisy

Adapting RAP attack.

In prior work,
Dick et al. 2023
present a reconstruction attack against
exact

k
-way marginals by using the optimization objective introduced by
Aydore et al. 2021
, called the Relaxed Adaptive Projection (RAP) attack.
Specifically, they relax discrete datasets and the corresponding
k
-way marginals to the continuous domain and use standard optimization techniques such as Stochastic Gradient Descent to reconstruct the dataset using the following optimization objective:
\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\tilde{X^{\prime}}}||\tilde{Q}(\tilde{X})-\tilde{Q}(\tilde{X^{\prime}})||_{2}
where
\tilde{Q}
,
\tilde{X}
, and
\tilde{X^{\prime}}
are the relaxed marginals, original dataset, and reconstructed dataset, respectively.
Finally, the relaxed reconstructed dataset is projected back to the discrete domain using rounding techniques.

In our experiments, we make three main modifications to what has been done in prior work and refer to this modified attack as our “M-AI attack”.
First, we evaluate the effectiveness of the RAP attack against
noisy

k
-way marginals instead of exact.
By doing so, we measure the gap between our theoretical success bounds provided by DP and concrete attacks that can be run against DP mechanisms.
On the other hand,
Dick et al. 2023
focus on non-private mechanisms only.

Second, we adapt the RAP attack to perform
multi-attribute inference
(M-AI) instead of reconstruction.
Unlike reconstruction, in M-AI the adversary is given access to a subset of attributes of the original dataset.
Naively, the reconstructed dataset
\tilde{X^{\prime}}
could be instantiated with these known attributes as the prior
[
21
]
.
However, we note that doing so does not guarantee that the final reconstructed dataset will be consistent with these known attributes, which might weaken the M-AI attack.
Therefore, along with instantiating
\tilde{X^{\prime}}
with the known attributes, during the optimization process, we additionally freeze these parameters so that the optimization process focuses purely on reconstructing the unknown attributes.

Lastly, instead of deriving the prior implicitly from other similar datasets, in our experiments we
explicitly calculate the prior
.
Specifically, we fit a Bayesian Network (BayNet) model
[
39
]
to raw datasets and sample the “original” datasets from this model that the adversary then attempts to reconstruct.
We do so as this enables us to attack realistic looking datasets, while at the same time explicitly defining the prior distribution necessary for our bounds calculations.
Therefore, in our modified RAP attack, we initialize the unknown attributes of
\tilde{X^{\prime}}
to the explicit prior distribution conditioned on the known attributes calculated from the BayNet model, as opposed to initializing them to an adjacent dataset as done previously
[
21
]
.

6.5
Key Takeaways

We show that while our bounds hold empirically, they remain loose when compared to the reconstruction success observed from actual attacks.
We believe that the main reason for this is that current state of the art reconstruction attacks may not be optimal for DP mechanisms, even though they work well under the non-private setting.
Nevertheless, in this section we show how our framework is capable of modelling
novel privacy risks
, providing meaningful
population-level
attack success bounds, and accommodating
complex success metrics
, which have not been simultaneously captured by any of the prior work discussed.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced our novel framework, which can be used to derive bounds on the privacy leakage from DP mechanisms under complex threat models.
We showed that our framework is flexible and models a large family of attacks, including two attack settings, for which bounds were not previously known.
Furthermore, we instantiated concrete attacks and investigated how the different aspects of complex threat models impacted the tightness of our theoretical bounds.
Overall, we observed that while our framework successfully unifies prior frameworks and extends them to previously unexplored settings, the performance of practical attacks are still far from the theoretical bounds derived through our framework.

7.1
Future Work

Prior-aware attacks.

We find that our bounds are far from tight for commonly used mechanisms such as DP-SGD and noisy
k
-way marginals.
As we had seen earlier, part of the reason is that our bounds rely on
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP, which may not tightly characterize the privacy leakage from these commonly used mechanisms.
However, in our experiments, we had also observed that current state-of-the-art attacks may not necessarily combine prior information with the privacy leakage from DP mechanisms effectively.
This then leads to weak attacks that do not fully leverage the overall privacy leakage and seemingly loose bounds.
Although in our work we had attempted to provide attacks with prior information, our techniques do not seem to be enough and therefore we leave it to future work to develop attacks that can make better use of prior information when attacking DP mechanisms.

Extending our bounds to approximate priors.

A natural extension to our adversarial model is to assume the adversary has access to some estimate
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
of the true data distribution
\mathcal{D}
, possibly with some guarantee about how close the two are. In order to state the bound with respect to an estimate of the data distribution
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}=(\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{n})
, we will need to reason about how far the prior success under
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{i}
is from the prior success under
\mathcal{D}_{i}
for each target person
i
. Concretely, if one can upper bound
(\Pr_{X\sim\mathcal{D}_{i}}[\ell_{i}(X,\mathbf{z})=1]-\Pr_{X\sim\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{i}}[\ell_{i}(X,\mathbf{z})=1])
for each
i\in[n]
and the particular attack
\mathbf{z}
then our bounds immediately extend to the approximate prior setting. Notice that
\mathcal{D}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
themselves don’t necessarily need to be close as long as they are close in their prior attack success probabilities for the given success metric
\mathcal{L}
.

For example, suppose
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
is the empirical distribution over samples from
\mathcal{D}
. This substitution is already a standard practice in empirical evaluations of reconstruction attacks on complex data, however as yet there has not been a rigorous treatment of this question. Ideally the chosen success metric would be coarse enough to provably limit the effect of sampling error on the prior success rates.

Extending the bounds to approximate priors would also allow us to understand the theoretical limits of generating DP synthetic data with help from similarly-distributed public data. In this case the “reconstruction measure”
\mathcal{L}
might measure the fidelity between
X
and
\mathbf{z}=X_{synth}
on a desired query workload.
