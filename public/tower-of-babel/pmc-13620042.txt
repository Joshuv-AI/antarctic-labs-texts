Continuous Glucose Monitoring-Informed Basal Insulin Optimization System in Adults with Type 2 Diabetes: Initial Evaluation of Efficacy, Safety, and Impact on Patient-Reported Outcomes
Authors: Sean M. Oser, Chad Crystal, Kazanna C. Hames, Xuan Li, Peggy Gao, Margaret A. Crawford, Christian Hicks, Matthew Gerber, Thibault Gautier, Patricio Colmegna, Lei Yang, Harsimran Singh, Matthew L. Johnson, Stayce E. Beck
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionClinical inertia and the complexity of basal insulin titration are major barriers to optimal glycemic control in adults with type 2 diabetes (T2D). A prototype of the Dexcom Smart Basal system known as basal therapy optimization (BTO), a continuous glucose monitoring (CGM)-informed basal insulin optimization system, was evaluated for its efficacy, safety, and impact on patient-reported outcomes (PRO).MethodsIn this prospective, single-arm study, 14 adults with T2D (three initiating and 11 optimizing basal insulin) used the BTO system. The study consisted of three phases: a ten-day baseline, up to 35 days of active titration, and a 20-day post-titration follow-up. Outcomes included change in CGM-derived glycemic metrics (mean glucose, time in range [TIR], time above range [TAR], time below range [TBR]), fasting blood glucose (FBG), and PROs (diabetes treatment satisfaction, glucose monitoring satisfaction, and patient–doctor depth of relationship) assessed using validated questionnaires. Safety was also assessed by the number of adverse events.ResultsAll participants completed the study. The BTO system generated 401 insulin dose recommendations, with 96.5% accepted by the healthcare provider. After BTO-guided titration, mean glucose decreased by median (IQR) 26.3 (– 64.6, – 6.0) mg/dl (p = 0.0031), TIR 70–180 mg/dl increased by 16.0 (2.5, 43.7) percentage points (p = 0.0017), TAR > 180 mg/dl decreased by 15.9 (– 43.7, – 2.5) percentage points (p = 0.0017), and TBR < 70 mg/dl remained low (0.0–0.1%), with no clinically significant change in TBR. Median FBG decreased by 17.8 (– 54.4, 2.3) mg/dl (p = 0.052). No BTO- or study-related- adverse or severe adverse events were reported. Treatment satisfaction was significantly higher; median post-titration Diabetes Treatment Satisfaction Questionnaire (DTSQ) Change score was 17 (14, 18) on a scale ranging from – 18 to 18 and was significantly different from baseline DTSQ status score (p = 0.001). High satisfaction with glucose management and strong patient–provider relationships were preserved. Three illustrative cases highlighted successful insulin dose optimization through dose increases or decreases.ConclusionsCGM-informed basal insulin optimization with a prototype of Dexcom Smart Basal was associated with improvements in glycemic control and treatment satisfaction in adults with T2D, with a favorable safety profile. The system may help overcome clinical inertia and support individualized insulin titration in real-world settings.Trial RegistrationClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT07681375 (Submitted for retrospective registration on 1/28/2026 and registered on 7/1/2026).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s13300-026-01901-4.

Introduction

The prevalence of diabetes continues to increase worldwide, with type 2 diabetes (T2D) accounting for over 90% of all diabetes cases [1]. Effective management of T2D is critical for avoiding severe complications, including cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, retinopathy, neuropathy, and others [2, 3]. For some individuals with high levels of insulin resistance and/or decreased insulin secretion, initiation of basal insulin is necessary to achieve glycemic goals.

Initiation of basal insulin typically starts at a low dose (e.g., 10 units per day) and patients are either expected to continue with the prescribed dose until their next in-office visit or remote consultation with their healthcare provider (HCP) or to self-titrate using a titration protocol provided by their HCP that may require calculating the mean morning glucose levels over 3 days and using a written scale to determine a dosage change. The complexity—or perceived complexity—of titration protocols is one of many reasons that basal insulin initiation or titration may be delayed or avoided [4], which contributes to clinical inertia.

Clinical inertia is defined as the delay of treatment intensification despite glycemic parameters that would warrant escalation (i.e., A1C > 8.5%). A recent review found that rates of clinical inertia experienced by individuals with T2D were largely > 50% with the highest rate of 85.8% reported in a US study [5]. Barriers to treatment intensification among individuals with diabetes can include fear of hypoglycemia, weight gain, and injection pain [4]. Many individuals also lack confidence in their ability to self-manage their diabetes, including how to follow a basal insulin titration algorithm. HCPs and people with diabetes may also perceive insulin initiation and titration as complex and time-consuming [6]. This delay due to the perceived burden of care is especially acute among individuals with T2D treated by primary care providers, which applies to most people with T2D [7, 8].

Decisions around basal insulin initiation or titration are regularly made using point-in-time fasting blood glucose (FBG) values from fingerstick blood glucose monitoring (BGM) or A1C values. These volitional and, especially in the case of A1C, periodic measurements contribute to the inefficiencies that patients and providers face in finding an optimal dose. Notably, the American Diabetes Association recommends the use of real-time continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) for all individuals with diabetes using insulin [9]. The real-time alerts and retrospective data analysis tools available with CGM systems can identify periods of hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia, contribute to diabetes self-management, and help assess the safety and effectiveness of treatments [10].

Dexcom Smart Basal is an FDA-cleared feature available on some Dexcom CGM systems that is designed to help people with T2D initiate and titrate basal insulin. Smart Basal uses CGM data to recommend individualized daily doses following an HCP-defined treatment plan, intending to avoid hypoglycemia and identify the user’s optimal basal insulin dose. This study evaluated the safety, efficacy, and impact on patient-reported outcomes (PRO) of a Smart Basal prototype known as basal therapy optimization (BTO) in a multisite clinical trial.

Methods

Study System

A prototype of Dexcom Smart Basal known as BTO was used in this study. BTO is a CGM-integrated basal insulin optimization system for adults with T2D. It uses CGM data and logged historical doses to calculate personalized daily basal insulin dose recommendations. It is designed to minimize hyperglycemia, avoid hypoglycemia, and prevent overbasalization (historically defined as > 0.5 U/kg/day in T2D [11, 12] but current guidelines recommend looking for signs of overbasalization like high glycemic variability [13]).

HCPs used a web-based portal to onboard patients to BTO. The HCP configured BTO to include a starting dose, maximum dose, and maximum daily dose increase. The HCP also set a low glucose threshold (in mg/dl) and a dose decrease (in %) if that threshold is reached for at least 15 contiguous minutes.

Study Design and Participants

This prospective, single-arm study was conducted at two sites in the United States. Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years, diagnosis of T2D, and either initiating glargine U-100 or already using glargine U-100 with a clinical need for basal insulin optimization. Demographic variables, including age, sex and race/ethnicity, were collected by participant self-report at study enrollment. Key medication-related exclusions included prandial insulin use within the prior 30 days, concomitant therapies associated with increased hypoglycemia risk with insulin, and planned initiation or dose adjustment of other glucose-lowering medications during the study period.

The study was conducted over four phases. At the start of phase 1, “baseline,” participants were screened, vitals and required blood analysis tests were collected, a blood glucose meter (Ascensia CONTOUR NEXT) was issued, baseline questionnaires were administered, and a CGM system was initiated to obtain 10 days of baseline glycemic data. Phase 2, “Active Titration,” began with the HCP adding the participant to the BTO web portal and providing the dose parameters described above. In this study, active titration could continue for up to 35 days. Each day, the clinical staff used the web portal to review the daily recommended dose, modify the dose if necessary for safety reasons, and communicate (by phone call or text) the dose to the participant. Participants were instructed to log their dose in the G7 app daily. Participants applied a new CGM system as needed to ensure continuous CGM data collection. Participants’ BTO session could end in one of two ways: a final, optimized dose was reached in ≤ 35 days, or the study-imposed 35-day limit was reached. At the end of the phase 2 titration window, the HCP reviewed all participants’ final BTO-derived insulin doses for safety before transitioning participants to phase 3 of the study. If BTO did not reach a final optimized dose by the end of the 35-day titration window, the last dose recommended during titration was considered the final dose and confirmed by the HCP. Once a final basal insulin dose was ascertained, the participant transitioned directly into phase 3, “post-BTO.” In the post-BTO period, participants continued to use their final recommended basal insulin dose for 20 days. They were instructed to continue logging their daily dose in the G7 app and take FBG measurements on days 7, 8, and 9 using the study-provided blood glucose meter. In phase 4, “post-titration assessment,” participants returned to the clinic to complete follow-up questionnaires and return their study-provided blood glucose meter.

Ethical Approval

The study protocol and consent forms were approved by WCG, an independent institutional review board (approval number: 20226804). WCG provided the necessary ethics approvals for both study sites. Written informed consent was obtained from study participants before study initiation. Written informed consent to publish was obtained from the individuals described in each of the illustrative cases. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki of 1964 and its later amendments, and was retrospectively registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07681375).

Outcomes

Efficacy of BTO was assessed by the changes in CGM metrics between baseline and post-BTO. These metrics include mean glucose, coefficient of variation (CV), time in range (TIR) 70–180 mg/dl (3.9–10.0 mmol/l), time above range (TAR) > 180 mg/dl (> 10.0 mg/dl) or > 250 mg/dl (> 13.9 mmol/l), and time below range (TBR) < 70 mg/dl (< 3.9 mmol/l), 54–< 70 mg/dl (3.0– < 3.9 mmol/l) or < 54 mg/dl (< 3.0 mmol/l). These metrics and their changes were summarized for the 24-h period as well as the overnight (midnight to 6:00 AM) period. Change in FBG between baseline laboratory FBG values and the average of at least two SMBG measurements during the post-BTO period was also calculated.

Safety endpoints included hypoglycemia- or hyperglycemia-related adverse events, including diabetic ketoacidosis, hyperosmolar coma or hyperglycemic state, moderate hypoglycemia episodes requiring assistance from a third party, or severe hypoglycemia. In addition, participants were screened for any CGM device-related adverse events like hematoma, local infection, excessive pain or discomfort, excessive bleeding, or skin irritation.

Changes in PROs were assessed from baseline to post-titration assessment using three validated questionnaires. The Diabetes Treatment Satisfaction Questionnaire [14] includes a status version (DTSQs) and a change version (DTSQc) to assess satisfaction with the diabetes treatment regimen. The DTSQs (administered at baseline) measures current treatment satisfaction, including perceptions of convenience, flexibility, and glycemic control. The DTSQc (administered at post-titration assessment) measures perceived changes in satisfaction relative to prior treatment. The Glucose Monitoring Satisfaction Survey (GMSS) [15] measures satisfaction with glucose monitoring devices across four domains: emotional burden, behavioral burden, openness, and worthwhileness. The Patient–Doctor Depth of Relationship (PDDR) Scale [16] measures the strength of longitudinal patient–provider relationships. Higher scores indicate stronger perceived patient–doctor relationships. Permissions were obtained as necessary for use of the DTSQ and GMSS tools. Additional study-specific questions were also measured.

Statistical Analysis

Efficacy and safety analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 and PRO analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.1. No formal sample size calculation was conducted. Performance of the insulin dosing algorithm was characterized by the percentage of recommendations accepted by the HCP. The frequency of up- and down-titration and the magnitude of adjustment were also summarized. Changes in CGM-derived glycemic metrics between baseline and post-BTO phases were reported for 24-h and overnight, and statistical significance was assessed with a paired t test or Wilcoxon signed-ranks test where appropriate. Changes in FBG were also reported using the values available. p values were not reported if the distribution included many tied values (e.g., many 0.0% values for TBR).

PRO analyses differed for each questionnaire. Statistical significance of the DTSQc questionnaire was assessed with a one-sample t test comparing the mean DTSQc score to a no-change value of zero (μ = 0). GMSS (totals, subscales, and item-level result scores) and PDDR scores from baseline to post-titration assessment were compared using paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Differences were assessed between subgroups based on prior insulin use, age, and gender using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Statistical significance for all analyses was defined as p < 0.05.

Insulin Recommendations

The BTO system generated 401 insulin dose recommendations during the phase 2 active titration period. This consisted of 90 recommendations for initiators and 311 recommendations for optimizers. All 90 recommendations (100%) for the initiators were accepted by the HCPs. The vast majority (297/311, 95.5%) of recommendations for the optimizers were accepted by the HCPs. All participants used their final BTO-recommended dose from the active titration phase during their 20-day post-BTO phase.

During the phase 2 active titration period, a final BTO-titrated dose was reached for six participants: two initiators and four optimizers. For the remaining eight participants who did not reach an optimal dose with BTO, four were determined to be within five days of titration completion, two had met the HCP’s maximum dosage, and two would have required > 5 days to complete their titration successfully.

The median (IQR) recommended dose of glargine U-100 was 35 U (25 U, 50 U). Compared to the recommended dose of glargine U-100 on day 1 of active BTO use, 11 participants ended at a higher dose (mean [SD] change of 25.4 [17.9] U) and three participants ended at a lower dose (change of – 13.7 [8.0] U, Table 2). Glycemic Outcomes

CGM-Derived Outcomes

CGM-derived glycemic outcomes are summarized in Table 3. The time spent in active titration ranged from 14 days to the maximum of 35 days. During the post-BTO period, participants had lower mean glucose (p = 0.0031), higher percentage of TIR 70–180 mg/dl (p = 0.0017), and lower percentage of TAR > 180 mg/dl (p = 0.0017) compared with baseline, while maintaining a low TBR (0.0–0.1%). Glycemic metrics during the overnight period (midnight to 6:00 AM) were comparable to the all-day results (Table 4). The average TIR was higher across every hour of the day in the post-BTO follow-up phase compared to the baseline phase (Fig. 2). Fasting Blood Glucose

Among 12 participants with available FBG values, there was a median – 17.8 (– 54.4, 2.3) mg/dl (– 1.0 [– 3.0, 0.1] mmol/l) change in FBG (p = 0.052). This was derived from a baseline FBG of mean (SD) 173.6 (53.1) mg/dl (9.6 [2.9] mmol/l) and a follow-up FBG of 144.6 (51.7) mg/dl (8.0 [2.9] mmol/l). The two participants with missing follow-up FBG values were in the insulin initiation cohort and had baseline FBG values of 324 mg/dl (18.0 mmol/l) and 256 mg/dl (14.2 mmol/l).

Safety Outcomes

There were no adverse or severe adverse events related to the study, BTO system use, or CGM use.

Data related to patient-reported outcomes are available in the Supplementary Material. Illustrative Cases

To further illustrate a typical patient’s use of BTO, three illustrative cases are described here. In case 1, BTO facilitated basal insulin initiation; in case 2, the participant experienced significant improvements in glycemia after BTO use; and in case 3, the participant used BTO to titrate their insulin dose down to a safer level.

Case 1. Significant Improvements in Glycemic Metrics with Basal Insulin Initiation Using BTO

A 72-year-old man with a 17-year history of T2D was taking 1000 mg metformin twice daily and 14 mg oral semaglutide daily. Other comorbidities included hyperlipidemia, hypertension, carpal tunnel syndrome, and erectile dysfunction. His A1C at baseline was 9.4%. The individual and his HCP decided that basal insulin initiation was appropriate.

The HCP configured BTO to start the patient on 10 U of glargine U-100. The maximum daily dose was set to 100 U, and the maximum daily dose increase was set to 5 U. During active titration, there were five instances of a dose increase equal to the maximum allowed dose increase of 5 U. On day 14, the user received the highest dose recommendation of 30 U (Fig. 3A). Discussion

In this prospective multicenter study of individuals with T2D initiating or optimizing their basal insulin dose with BTO, participants experienced favorable changes in glycemic metrics and treatment satisfaction, in addition to maintained high satisfaction with glucose monitoring and strong patient–doctor relationships. BTO was also safe, with no adverse or severe adverse events occurring during the study. Three illustrative cases also described unique BTO use cases in which individuals initiated, increased, or decreased their doses using CGM-informed dose recommendations. The glycemic outcomes observed in this study are in line with or exceed those observed in studies of other basal insulin titration apps. The study evaluating icodec dosing algorithms found a 27.8 percentage point increase in TIR among users of the moderately aggressive algorithm [17]. Importantly, the study was conducted in insulin-naïve individuals with T2D and the average TBR < 70 mg/dl (< 3.9 mmol/l) at follow-up was 1.9%. Participants in the present study of BTO achieved a 20.7 percentage point increase in TIR, largely avoided hypoglycemia, and most were optimizing their insulin regimen, including some who decreased their insulin dose, so a similar TIR increase was not expected.

In the present study, the extent of glycemic improvements with BTO varied. For many people with T2D and likely for some participants in this study, the addition of basal insulin alone is not sufficient to achieve their glycemic goals. These individuals may benefit from further medication adjustments, including the addition of prandial insulin. The outcome of a BTO session could be used by HCPs to illustrate this point.

Management of T2D often requires initiation of basal insulin therapy as the disease progresses; however, many individuals experience hesitancy or delays due to fear of injections, concerns about hypoglycemia, and uncertainty regarding dose adjustment. Once initiated, many people with insulin-treated T2D may not titrate basal insulin as recommended due to difficulties determining appropriate doses and the complexity and time burden associated with manual dose calculations. Given the high emotional and cognitive burden associated with both initiating and maintaining basal insulin regimens, the observed improvements in diabetes treatment satisfaction among BTO study participants represent an important indicator of the system’s acceptability within a context typically characterized by heightened fear, uncertainty, and cognitive load. Additionally, participants demonstrated significant improvements in the GMSS Openness subscale following Dexcom CGM use and basal insulin titration, indicating greater openness to new and spontaneous life experiences. Together, these findings suggest that use of Dexcom CGM during basal insulin titration with the BTO system may support treatment satisfaction, enhance glucose monitoring-related openness, and maintain positive patient–doctor relationships in populations with T2D.

Dexcom Smart Basal, the commercial version of BTO, is the first CGM-integrated basal insulin dosing optimizer cleared by the FDA for T2D. Other commercialized basal insulin calculators typically rely on user-provided SMBG measurements [17–19], which are unlikely to capture the extent of glycemic variability, and, in particular, episodes of hypoglycemia. SMBG-based FBG values may not be the lowest glucose value or the only low glucose value that occurs during a 24-h period. Titration algorithms typically recommend dose changes based on FBG values [20–22], but with one of the most common reasons for clinical inertia and not intensifying basal insulin therapy being fear of hypoglycemia [4, 6], a CGM-integrated basal insulin optimizer that incorporates CGM data from the prior 24-h period has the potential to improve adoption.

It should be noted that the prototype BTO system used in the present study differs from the planned commercial Smart Basal system in a few key ways. The active titration period of BTO was limited to 35 days, whereas Smart Basal will allow up to 90 days of active titration. The longer active titration period with Smart Basal is expected to increase the number of users who reach an optimized dose before the end of active titration. Also, because Smart Basal is a feature planned for some Dexcom CGM apps, each day’s recommended dose will be delivered directly to the user. HCPs will continue to have access to their patient’s CGM metrics and dose recommendations through Dexcom Clarity but will not be required to communicate the dose directly to their patients each day. Finally, BTO required dosing in the evening for all participants, whereas Smart Basal will require the patient to select their preferred time of day for dosing. This can be any time of day, but the time must remain unchanged throughout the Smart Basal session.

In this study, basal insulin doses were adjusted using BTO. However, the participants also had access to unblinded CGM, which may have contributed to some of the observed glycemic outcomes. In the MOBILE randomized clinical trial, participants with T2D using long- or intermediate-acting basal insulin without prandial insulin were randomized 2:1 to use CGM or SMBG [23]. CGM users lowered their A1C by 0.4 percentage points more than SMBG users. While diabetes therapy changes were allowed during the MOBILE study, there were no significant differences between groups in the total daily insulin dose, or addition or reduction of medications.

While this study was not designed to assess whether BTO users achieved a final dose faster than conventional methods, this has been observed in studies of other app-based basal insulin titration tools [18, 24–26]. In addition, some studies have shown that the basal insulin dose is titrated to a higher level with the app-based tool compared to conventional methods [18, 19]; however, this relationship is not consistent and may be affected by the study design (i.e., non-inferiority design) [27].

Strengths of this study include its multisite design, collection of patient-reported outcome data using validated questionnaires, and collection of both modern CGM data and traditional FBG data. Limitations include the relatively small number of participants and the relatively short study duration. In addition, the single-arm design without a concurrent control group limits the extent to which observed changes can be attributed to the intervention. Because this initial study enrolled adults treated with insulin glargine U100, and because the BTO algorithm was specifically developed for the properties of this insulin, generalizability to other basal insulin formulations (e.g., glargine U300, degludec U100) remains to be established. This study was not designed as a structured diabetes self-management education or lifestyle intervention; diet, physical activity, and other lifestyle behaviors were not standardized or measured as study endpoints, and their contribution to observed glycemic changes cannot be determined. This study also did not include standardized diabetes self-management education and support (DSMES), which is recommended by the American Diabetes Association Standards of Care at key time points throughout the course of diabetes management. As such, the study does not assess the potential contribution of DSMES to the observed glycemic or patient-reported outcomes. While CGM-informed basal insulin optimization systems are designed to help reduce the burden associated with insulin initiation and titration, integration with DSMES may further support patient understanding, engagement, and appropriate use of therapy. Variation in access to DSMES in real-world settings should also be considered when interpreting and applying these findings. The study-imposed active titration period of ≤ 35 days likely limited the number of participants who found an optimized dose. The longer titration period available with the commercial version of BTO is expected to decrease the proportion of users not reaching an optimized dose. In addition, because the follow-up period was limited to 20 days, glycemic improvements are unlikely to be reflected in an A1C measurement. Therefore, a follow-up A1C measurement was not obtained.

Conclusions

In adults with T2D, use of a CGM-informed basal insulin optimization system was associated with improved glycemic outcomes and higher treatment satisfaction, without increased risk of hypoglycemia or adverse events. These findings support the integration of CGM-based titration tools to enhance individualized diabetes management and address barriers to insulin optimization.
