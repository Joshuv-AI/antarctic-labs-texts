Development of a PNI-Based Prognostic Model for Patients with Unresectable Hepatocellular Carcinoma Treated with Lenvatinib-Based Triple Therapy

Introduction

Unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma (uHCC) remains a lethal malignancy with marked clinical heterogeneity, particularly among patients with macrovascular invasion (MVI), extrahepatic spread (EHS), or impaired hepatic reserve.1,2 Although therapeutic options have expanded with the use of tyrosine kinase inhibitors, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), and locoregional therapy (LRT), survival outcomes in uHCC remain highly variable, underscoring the need for more individualized prognostic assessment.

Combination strategies integrating LRT with lenvatinib and PD-1 blockade have attracted growing attention because of their complementary antitumor effects. LRT may promote tumor antigen release and immune priming,3,4 lenvatinib can remodel the tumor microenvironment,5,6 and PD-1 inhibitors may restore exhausted antitumor T-cell activity.7 Emerging real-world evidence suggests that this triplet strategy is feasible and potentially effective in advanced uHCC;8–10 however, substantial interpatient heterogeneity in treatment response and survival persists.

Current prognostic systems for HCC, including Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) staging, mainly rely on performance status, tumor burden, hepatic reserve, and performance status.11 Although these frameworks are essential for treatment allocation, they do not fully capture host-related factors that may influence therapeutic response and survival. In clinical practice, patients with comparable stage and hepatic function often exhibit markedly divergent outcomes, highlighting the limited ability of conventional staging systems to capture prognostic heterogeneity.

The prognostic nutritional index (PNI), calculated from serum albumin and peripheral lymphocyte count, is a simple and reproducible marker that reflects both nutritional reserve and immune competence. Albumin represents protein synthesis and systemic catabolic status, whereas lymphocyte count reflects antitumor immune capacity. Therefore, PNI can capture the nutritional–immune status of patients. Accumulating evidence has linked low PNI with poorer outcomes in HCC, particularly in immunotherapy-based settings.12 However, whether incorporating PNI into a multivariable prognostic model can improve risk stratification for patients receiving LRT combined with lenvatinib and PD-1 inhibitors remains unclear.

In this multicenter retrospective cohort study, we aimed to develop and validate an integrated prognostic model for patients with uHCC treated with LRT plus lenvatinib and a PD-1 inhibitor. Specifically, we sought to determine whether adding PNI to tumor burden, liver function, and biomarker-related variables could improve survival prediction and facilitate individualized prognostic assessment. We further constructed a nomogram to support practical clinical decision-making.

Methods

Study Population

This multicenter retrospective cohort study included consecutive patients with uHCC treated at six tertiary academic centers between January 2021 and December 2024. Patients were eligible if they met all of the following criteria: (1) age 18 to 80 years; (2) diagnosis of uHCC, with unresectability determined by a multidisciplinary team (MDT) at each center based on factors such as tumor extent, vascular invasion, presence of extrahepatic spread, liver functional reserve, anticipated future liver remnant, and overall clinical condition; (3) treatment with LRT, including transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) or hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy (HAIC); (4) receipt of lenvatinib plus a PD-1 inhibitor; (5) Child-Pugh class A or B liver function; (6) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS) of 0 or 1; and (7) complete baseline, laboratory, treatment, and follow-up data. Patients were excluded if they had received prior systemic therapy or other locoregional treatments that could confound the analysis, had other active malignancies, combined hepatocellular-cholangiocarcinoma, fibrolamellar HCC, sarcomatoid HCC, Child-Pugh class C liver function, severe comorbidities precluding treatment, uncontrolled infection, major complications, incomplete follow-up, substantial missing data, or non-protocol anticancer therapy during the study period.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Cancer Hospital (K2026-136-01). Written informed consent was waived because of the retrospective design.

Procedures

TACE was performed using the Seldinger technique via femoral or radial arterial access. Angiography of the celiac and superior mesenteric arteries was used to identify tumor-feeding arteries, and a microcatheter was advanced selectively to infuse iodized oil, chemotherapeutic agents, and embolic materials until substantial reduction in tumor staining or stasis of arterial flow was achieved. Repeat TACE was determined by the multidisciplinary team according to tumor response and liver function.

HAIC was administered using a standard FOLFOX-based regimen. A microcatheter was positioned in the dominant tumor-feeding artery for continuous infusion. The regimen consisted of oxaliplatin 85 mg/m2, leucovorin 400 mg/m2, and fluorouracil 400 mg/m2 on day 1, followed by continuous fluorouracil infusion at 2400 mg/m2 over 24 to 46 hours, repeated every 3 weeks for at least 2 cycles. PD-1 inhibitors were administered intravenously every 3 weeks, with dose modification or interruption permitted in cases of severe adverse events. All patients received lenvatinib after discharge following LRT, at a dose of 12 mg/day for body weight ≥60 kg or 8 mg/day for body weight <60 kg, with dose adjustment allowed for intolerable toxicity.

Assessment and Follow-Up

Clinical data were extracted uniformly from the medical records of the six centers, including sex, age, ECOG PS, Child-Pugh class, tumor size and number, vascular invasion, EHS, BCLC stage, alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), and routine laboratory parameters. Portal vein tumor thrombosis (PVTT) was classified according to the Liver Cancer Study Group of Japan criteria.13 The Up-to-Seven,14 Up-to-Eleven,15 and Seven-to-Eleven16 criteria were calculated based on maximum tumor diameter and tumor number. The PNI was calculated as: 10 × serum albumin (g/dL) + 0.005 × total lymphocyte count (/mm3).17 A cutoff value of 45 was adopted based on previous studies and was used to stratify patients into high- and low-PNI groups.12 Potential multicollinearity among variables included in the final model was assessed using the generalized variance inflation factor (GVIF). An adjusted GVIF value (GVIF^(1/(2×Df))) <5 was considered indicative of acceptable collinearity.

Chest computed tomography and contrast-enhanced liver CT or MRI were performed every 6 to 8 weeks, with PET-CT obtained when clinically indicated. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the interval from initiation of treatment to death from any cause or last follow-up, whereas progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the time from treatment initiation to radiologic progression or death from any cause. Patients without events were censored at the last follow-up.

Development and Validation of Prognostic Models

Nine prognostic models were constructed using Cox proportional hazards regression to evaluate OS. The initial model included conventional clinical and laboratory variables, namely BCLC stage, ALBI grade, PNI, and the Up-to-Seven, Up-to-Eleven, and Seven-to-Eleven criteria. Tumor size, tumor number, PVTT, and EHS were then combined to generate a tumor burden model. To account for the contribution of liver function and tumor biology, ALBI grade and AFP were incorporated into the tumor burden variables to construct an integrated model. Finally, PNI was added to the integrated model to determine whether nutritional-immune status improved prognostic discrimination. Full details can be found in Table S1.

Two centers constituted the training cohort (n = 242), and four centers constituted the validation cohort (n = 105). The allocation of centers was determined based on the availability of outcome events, candidate predictors, missing data patterns, and model complexity to ensure adequate model development and independent evaluation. Model performance was assessed using time-dependent receiver operating characteristic analysis, calibration plots, and decision curve analysis at 12, 24, and 36 months. Based on the optimal integrated model, a prognostic score for OS was derived from the linear predictor of the final Cox model, and patients were stratified into tertiles according to the score. Patients in the training cohort were stratified into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups according to the lower and upper tertiles (1/3 and 2/3 quantiles) of the prognostic score distribution. The derived cutoff values were subsequently applied to the validation cohort for risk stratification. Kaplan–Meier analysis was used to compare OS and PFS across risk strata. A nomogram was developed from the final model to facilitate individualized prediction of 12-, 24-, and 36-month OS.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as median with interquartile range, and categorical variables as number with percentage. Between-group comparisons were performed using the Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U-test for continuous variables, and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. OS and PFS were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared with the Log rank test. Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to identify factors associated with OS and PFS. Variables with a p-value <0.10 in univariable analysis, together with clinically relevant covariates, were entered into the multivariable models. All tests were two-sided, and a p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using R software, version 4.2.2.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 347 patients with uHCC treated with LRT plus lenvatinib and PD-1 inhibitor were included, of whom 242 were assigned to the training cohort and 105 to the validation cohort (Table 1). The median age of the overall cohort was 57 years (IQR, 47–65), and 319 patients (92%) were male. ALBI grade 1 was present in 69% of patients, and 75% had underlying cirrhosis. PVTT was observed in 228 patients (66%), and 99 patients (29%) had EHS. Tumor size was ≥10 cm in 217 patients (63%), and 261 patients (75%) had multifocal disease. According to BCLC staging, 8.9% of patients had stage A disease, 25% had stage B disease, and 66% had stage C disease. Regarding locoregional treatment, 164 patients (47%) received TACE and 183 (53%) received HAIC. No significant differences were observed between the training and validation cohorts in age, sex, tumor burden, BCLC stage, treatment modality, or PNI (all P > 0.05).

Survival Outcomes

The median follow-up duration for the overall cohort was 21.0 months (IQR, 13.8–29.9). The median OS was 33.1 months (95% CI, 30.5–not reached), with 1-, 2-, and 3-year OS rates of 86.6%, 64.7%, and 45.8%, respectively (Figure 1A). The median PFS was 10.2 months (95% CI, 9.0–12.6), and the corresponding 1-, 2-, and 3-year PFS rates were 45.9%, 27.2%, and 20.4%, respectively (Figure 1B). Prognostic Factors for OS and PFS

In multivariable Cox analysis, cirrhosis (HR = 1.86, 95% CI: 1.16–2.99, P = 0.011), multifocal tumors (HR = 2.03, 95% CI: 1.24–3.31, P = 0.005), PVTT (VP2: HR = 2.76, 95% CI: 1.46–5.21, P = 0.002; VP3: HR = 2.91, 95% CI: 1.72–4.92, P < 0.001; VP4: HR = 4.22, 95% CI: 2.41–7.37, P < 0.001), and EHS (HR = 1.80, 95% CI: 1.25–2.58, P = 0.001) were independent adverse prognostic factors for OS. In contrast, higher PNI (HR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.39–0.88, P = 0.010) were independently associated with improved OS. For PFS, older age (HR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.97–0.99, P = 0.002), female sex (HR = 1.92, 95% CI: 1.23–3.01, P = 0.004), HBV infection (HR = 1.61, 95% CI: 1.15–2.26, P = 0.006), multifocal tumors (HR = 1.78, 95% CI: 1.27–2.48, P < 0.001), VP4 PVTT (HR = 1.62, 95% CI: 1.12–2.34, P = 0.011), and low PNI (HR = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.48–0.87, P = 0.004) were independent prognostic factors (Table 2). The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals, and no significant violations were observed for the multivariable Cox models of OS or PFS (global P > 0.05; Tables S2–S3).

Model Performance

Among all candidate models, the integrated model incorporating tumor burden, ALBI grade, AFP, and PNI showed the best predictive performance for OS in both cohorts. The model achieved a C-index of 0.780 (95% CI, 0.736–0.824) in the training cohort and 0.785 (95% CI, 0.725–0.845) in the validation cohort. Time-dependent ROC analysis showed stable discrimination, with 1- and 2-year AUCs of 0.892 and 0.807 in the training cohort, and 0.861 and 0.819 in the validation cohort (Figures 2A and B). Calibration plots demonstrated good agreement between predicted and observed 2-year OS in both cohorts (Figures 2C and D), supporting robust model calibration.

Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for overall survival (OS) in the training cohort (A) and validation cohort (B), and calibration curves for OS prediction in the training cohort (C) and validation cohort (D).

Risk Stratification

Using the prognostic score derived from the optimal integrated model, patients were classified into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups. Kaplan–Meier curves showed clear separation of OS across risk groups in both cohorts. The distribution of outcome events across risk groups in the training and validation cohorts is summarized in Table S4. In the training cohort, median OS was not reached in the low-risk group, 28.0 months (95% CI, 23.1–not reached) in the intermediate-risk group, and 10.5 months (95% CI, 6.3–17.5) in the high-risk group (P < 0.001). The corresponding hazard ratios were 5.15 (95% CI, 2.54–10.46) for intermediate versus low risk and 11.08 (95% CI, 5.69–21.91) for high versus low risk (Figure 3A). Similar stratification was observed in the validation cohort, with median OS of 46.3, 32.1, and 10.7 months in the low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups, respectively (P < 0.001) (Figure 3B). PFS also differed significantly among risk groups in both cohorts. In the training cohort, median PFS was 18.2, 10.5, and 5.4 months, and in the validation cohort, 10.5, 6.1, and 3.0 months, across the low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups, respectively (Figures 3C and D).

Kaplan–Meier survival curves for overall survival (OS) stratified by risk scores in the training cohort (A) and validation cohort (B), and progression-free survival (PFS) stratified by risk scores in the training cohort (C) and validation cohort (D).

Nomogram

Based on the optimal Cox regression model, a visual nomogram was constructed to predict individualized 12‑, 24‑, and 36‑month OS probabilities (Figure 4A). In the overall cohort, the nomogram showed good discrimination, with AUCs of 0.879 (95% CI, 0.835–0.924) at 12 months, 0.808 (95% CI, 0.753–0.863) at 24 months, and 0.787 (95% CI, 0.705–0.868) at 36 months (Figure 4B). Decision curve analysis further indicated favorable clinical utility for predicting 1-, 2-, and 3-year OS in patients with uHCC treated with LRT plus lenvatinib and a PD-1 inhibitor (Figure 4C).

Discussion

In this multicenter retrospective cohort study of patients with uHCC treated with LRT plus lenvatinib and a PD-1 inhibitor, we developed and externally validated an integrated prognostic model incorporating tumor burden, liver function, AFP, and PNI. The model demonstrated favorable discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility across both the training and validation cohorts, suggesting that it may serve as a practical tool for pre-treatment risk stratification in this heterogeneous population. Importantly, all variables included in the final model are routinely available in clinical practice, which increases the translational value of our findings.

A major finding of the present study is that the PNI provided independent prognostic information beyond conventional tumor- and liver function–related variables. Traditional staging systems for hepatocellular carcinoma primarily evaluate performance status, hepatic reserve, and tumor burden. Although these parameters are essential for treatment selection, they do not fully reflect the host’s nutritional and immune status, both of which may also influence treatment response and long-term survival. PNI reflects both nutritional reserve and immune competence. Adequate nutritional status may improve tolerance to triplet therapy, whereas preserved immune function may help maintain immune-mediated antitumor activity. Previous studies have shown that patients with poor baseline nutritional status are at significantly increased risk of developing grade ≥3 treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) during targeted therapy or immunotherapy.18,19 In addition, the lymphocyte count incorporated into PNI also serves as a direct foundation for maintaining immune-mediated antitumor activity. Adequate effector T-cell levels are a prerequisite for the efficacy of ICIs. Clinical studies have demonstrated that patients with low PNI often exhibit an exhausted immune microenvironment and significantly lower response rates than those with high PNI.20,21 Therefore, even among patients with similar tumor burden and liver function, differences in nutritional–immune status may still lead to markedly different clinical outcomes. However, most previous studies have evaluated PNI as a stand-alone prognostic biomarker or only within limited treatment settings.22–24 The present study further extends the existing literature by incorporating PNI into a multivariable prognostic model specifically developed for patients receiving locoregional therapy combined with lenvatinib and PD-1 inhibitors. The improved predictive performance of the integrated model suggests that PNI provides additional prognostic information beyond that captured by conventional tumor- and liver function–related variables.

However, the association between low PNI and poor prognosis should not be interpreted as evidence that nutritional intervention alone can improve the efficacy of lenvatinib-based triple therapy. PNI is likely a surrogate marker reflecting the complex interaction between tumor biology, liver function, inflammation, and nutritional status rather than an independent therapeutic target. Therefore, whether interventions aimed at improving nutritional status or modulating inflammation can translate into improved treatment response remains unclear.

Future prospective studies are warranted to investigate the clinical utility of dynamic PNI monitoring during treatment. Integrating serial PNI assessment with comprehensive nutritional evaluation and appropriate nutritional support may help identify patients at risk of deterioration and optimize individualized management strategies for patients receiving lenvatinib-based triple therapy.

Recent studies have attempted to refine prognostic prediction in uHCC by using biomarker-based scores, clinical decision models, and AI-driven radiomics approaches. For example, the CRAFITY score, which combines AFP and CRP, has been shown to stratify survival in patients receiving immunotherapy, with low-risk patients demonstrating markedly longer overall survival than high-risk patients in some cohorts.25 Similarly, in a recent real-world study of triplet therapy, pretreatment tumor burden features such as multifocal disease, PVTT, and EHS were associated with inferior outcomes, and models incorporating AFP, bilirubin, and extent of disease achieved better discrimination than conventional staging alone.26 Other studies have proposed treatment-specific scores for triplet therapy; for instance, a bilirubin–AFP–extrahepatic metastasis model was reported to identify patients with a higher probability of response, while early tumor shrinkage and conversion to resection have also been linked to prolonged survival, with hazard ratios for favorable responders often falling below 0.5.27–29 In parallel, radiomics and machine learning models based on pretreatment CT or MRI have shown promising predictive performance, with time-dependent AUCs frequently exceeding 0.80 for objective response or short-term survival prediction.30 However, these approaches often depend on standardized imaging protocols, complex feature extraction, and external validation, which limit widespread implementation. By contrast, our model relies on readily available baseline clinical, laboratory, and imaging variables and additionally incorporates PNI, thereby improving prognostic discrimination while remaining practical for routine use. Incorporation of the PNI into prognostic assessment may provide additional information beyond conventional risk factors by identifying patients with impaired overall status who cannot be adequately recognized by tumor staging systems alone. Patients stratified as high-risk using PNI-based models may benefit from closer surveillance, comprehensive evaluation of treatment feasibility, and timely consideration of supportive interventions. For select patients with potentially reversible risk factors, nutritional optimization and inflammation management can be explored as complementary strategies.

Beyond the nutritional and immune indices, our results confirm that cirrhosis, multiple tumors, PVTT, and EHS independently contribute to a poorer prognosis. The prognostic impact of cirrhosis is multifaceted, not only limiting the therapeutic window for loco-regional and systemic treatments but also predisposing patients to a pro-carcinogenic microenvironment.31 Furthermore, the presence of PVTT and EHS reflects biologically advanced disease characterized by enhanced vascular invasion and systemic dissemination. These features are closely associated with aggressive tumor behavior and an unfavorable tumor microenvironment, including increased angiogenic activity, immune evasion, and hypoxia-driven signaling pathways, which have been implicated in resistance to both locoregional and systemic therapies in HCC.32 Consistent with previous reports, PVTT and EHS are strong predictors of poor prognosis and limited treatment responsiveness, particularly in patients receiving multimodal or combined systemic regimens.33

Interestingly, our multivariable analysis showed that older age was associated with improved PFS, whereas female sex and HBV infection were associated with poorer PFS. The worse PFS in younger patients likely reflects their typically more aggressive tumor biology compared to the more indolent tumors in selected elderly patients.34 The higher risk in females may be driven by a greater prevalence of non-viral etiologies which often show inferior responses to therapies.35 Additionally, active HBV infection maintains an inflammatory microenvironment conducive to early multicentric recurrence and treatment interruptions,36 explaining its negative impact on PFS.

Our study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design entails potential selection bias and residual confounding. Although multicenter enrollment and external validation enhance result robustness, prospective verification remains necessary. In addition, consistent longitudinal data regarding treatment tolerance (severe adverse events, lenvatinib dose modifications, treatment interruptions, hepatic decompensation, and completion of locoregional therapy) were lacking. Hence, we were unable to assess whether PNI can predict treatment-related toxicity or impact treatment adherence. Second, the median follow-up duration was relatively limited, which may affect the precision of long-term survival estimates and model stability over extended time horizons. Third, the included patients received different PD-1 inhibitors and locoregional procedures, which may introduce treatment heterogeneity. However, this also enhances the real-world applicability of the study. Fourth, HBV infection was the predominant etiology in our cohort, so the generalizability of the findings to HCV-related, alcohol-related, or non-viral HCC remains uncertain. Finally, although the model performed well in both cohorts, independent prospective validation in broader populations and different healthcare settings will be required before routine adoption.

In conclusion, we developed and externally validated a clinically practical prognostic model for patients with uHCC treated with LRT plus lenvatinib and PD-1 inhibitors. The model improved pre-treatment risk stratification.
