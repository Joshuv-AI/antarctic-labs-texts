The Born-Oppenheimer approximation for a 1D 2+1 particle system with zero-range interactions
Claudio Cacciapuoti, Andrea Posilicano, Hamidreza Saberbaghi

Abstract
. We study the self-adjoint Hamiltonian that models the quantum dynamics of a 1D three-body system consisting of a light particle interacting with two heavy ones through a zero-range force. For an attractive interaction we determine the behavior of the eigenvalues below the essential spectrum in the regime ε ≪ 1 \varepsilon\ll 1 , where ε \varepsilon is proportional to the square root of the mass ratio. We show that the n n -th eigenvalue behaves as E n ​ ( ε ) = − α 2 + | σ n | ​ α 2 ​ ε 2 / 3 + O ⁡ ( ε ) , E_{n}(\varepsilon)=-\alpha^{2}+|\sigma_{n}|\,\alpha^{2}\varepsilon^{2/3}+O(\varepsilon)\,, where α \alpha is a negative constant that explicitly relates to the physical parameters and σ n \sigma_{n} is either the n n -th extremum or the n n -th zero of the Airy function Ai, depending on the kind (respectively, bosons or fermions) of the two heavy particles. Additionally, we prove that the essential spectrum coincides with the half-line [ − α 2 4 + ε 2 , + ∞ ) \left[-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4+\varepsilon^{2}}\,,+\infty\right) .

1.
Introduction

We consider two heavy particles (both bosons or fermions) of mass
M
and one light particle of mass
m
in one dimension. Let
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
denote the coordinates of the heavy particles, and
x_{3}
denote the coordinate of the light particle. The Hilbert space of the system is
L^{2}_{\text{\rm b}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
or
L^{2}_{\text{\rm f}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, i.e., the subspace of square integrable functions that are either symmetric (if the heavy particles are bosons) or antisymmetric (if the heavy particles are fermions) under the exchange of the coordinates
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
:

L^{2}_{\text{\rm b/f}}(\mathbb{R}^{3}):=\{\Psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}):\;\Psi(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})={(+)}_{\text{\rm b/f}}\,\Psi(x_{2},x_{1},x_{3})\},

where
{(+)}_{\text{\rm b}}:=+
and
{(+)}_{\text{\rm f}}:=-\,
.

We assume that the heavy particles do not interact between themselves and that they interact with the light particle through a contact interaction. We introduce, heuristically, the Hamiltonian

H^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{2+1}:=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2M}\,\partial^{2}_{x_{1}}-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2M}\,\partial^{2}_{x_{2}}-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\,\partial^{2}_{x_{3}}+\beta\delta(x_{3}-x_{1})+\beta\delta(x_{3}-x_{2})\,.

Here
\beta
is real valued and represents the interaction parameter, and
\delta(x_{3}-x_{1})
,
\delta(x_{3}-x_{2})
denote the Dirac delta distributions supported on the coincidence planes of the heavy particles with the light particle:

\Pi_{1}:=\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:x_{3}=x_{1}\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Pi_{2}:=\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}|\;x_{3}=x_{2}\}.

To proceed, we pass to units in which
\hbar=1
and define the Jacobi coordinates

x_{cm}=\frac{M(x_{1}+x_{2})+mx_{3}}{M_{tot}}\,,\qquad x=x_{1}-x_{2}\,,\qquad y=x_{3}-\frac{x_{1}+x_{2}}{2};

M_{tot}=2M+m\,,\qquad\mu=\frac{2Mm}{M_{tot}}\,.

In Jacobi coordinates, the Hamiltonian
H^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{2+1}
acts in the Hilbert space of square-integrable functions
\Psi
such that
\Psi(x_{cm},x,y)={(+)}_{\text{\rm b/f}}\Psi(x_{cm},-x,y)
and takes the form (we abuse the notation and continue to denote the Hamiltonian by
H^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{2+1}
)

H^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{2+1}=-\frac{1}{2M_{tot}}\,\partial^{2}_{x_{cm}}-\frac{1}{M}\,\partial^{2}_{x}-\frac{1}{2\mu}\,\partial^{2}_{y}+\beta\delta(y-x/2)+\beta\delta(y+x/2)\,.

Neglecting the coordinates of the center of mass and keeping the previous notation, we consider the Hilbert space

L^{2}_{\text{\rm b/f}}(\mathbb{R}^{2}):=\{\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}):\;\psi(x,y)={(+)}_{\text{\rm b/f}}\,\psi(-x,y)\}

and the coincidence lines

\Pi_{1}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\;y=x/2\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Pi_{2}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\;y=-x/2\}.

(1.1)

Additionally, since we consider the regime
m/M\ll 1
, we define the rescaled Hamiltonian

H^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon}=-\varepsilon^{2}\partial^{2}_{x}-\partial^{2}_{y}+\alpha\delta(y-x/2)+\alpha\delta(y+x/2)

where the small parameter
\varepsilon\ll 1
is defined by

\varepsilon^{2}=\frac{2\mu}{M}

and
\alpha
is kept fixed (independent of
\varepsilon
); we remark that
H^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon}
is to be considered as an operator in the Hilbert space

L^{2}_{\text{\rm b/f}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
At a heuristic level, the Hamiltonian
H^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon}
is given by
H^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon}=2\mu\left(H^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{2+1}-K_{cm}\right)
, where
K_{cm}=-\frac{1}{2M_{tot}}\,\partial^{2}_{x_{cm}}
represents the kinetic energy of the center of mass and
\alpha
relates to
\beta
through the formula
\alpha=2\mu\beta
.

We give the rigorous definition of the self-adjoint realization of
H^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon}
in Section
2
, see Theorem
2.12
. Our main result is the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\alpha<0
. Then,

\sigma(H^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon})\subseteq[-\alpha^{2},+\infty)\,,\qquad\sigma_{ess}(H^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon})=\left[-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4+\varepsilon^{2}}\,,+\infty\right).

(1.2)

Moreover, for any fixed integer
n\geq 0
there exists
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small such that
H^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon}
has at least
(n+1)
simple isolated eigenvalues

-\alpha^{2}<E^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon,0}<E^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon,1}<\dots<E^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon,n}<-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4+\varepsilon^{2}}\,,

E^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon,k}=-\alpha^{2}+s_{k}^{\text{\rm b/f}}\alpha^{2}\varepsilon^{2/3}+O(\varepsilon)\,,

(1.3)

where

s_{k}^{\text{\rm b}}=|\sigma_{2k}|\,,\qquad s_{k}^{\text{\rm f}}=|\sigma_{2k+1}|\,,

and the interlacing negative numbers
\sigma_{k}

\dots<\sigma_{2k+1}<\sigma_{2k}<\sigma_{2k-1}<\dots<\sigma_{2}<\sigma_{1}<\sigma_{0}<0\,,

are either the extrema or the zeros of the Airy function
\operatorname{Ai}
, i.e.,
\operatorname{Ai}^{\prime}(\sigma_{2k})=0
,
\operatorname{Ai}(\sigma_{2k+1})=0
.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is based on the Born-Oppenheimer approximation.
Such an approximation, introduced in the early years of quantum theory
[
8
]
, was developed as a method for deriving the molecular structure from quantum mechanical principles. We do not attempt to provide an overview of the extensive mathematical literature on the Born-Oppenheimer approximation. For an accessible introduction and references to both classical and recent developments, we refer the reader to the reviews
[
21
,
18
]
.

In the context of zero-range interactions, the study of the quantum dynamics of three or more non relativistic bosons interacting via contact interactions is plagued by the so-called ultraviolet catastrophe
[
24
,
25
,
35
]
. Overcoming this issue typically requires the introduction of nonlocal modifications and/or effective three-body interaction terms, see, e.g.,
[
13
,
14
,
16
,
6
]
and references therein. This problem affects also the study of a many particle system in three spatial dimensions within the Born-Oppenheimer framework, see, e.g.,
[
16
]
.

However, these difficulties do not arise whenever one considers particle systems in one spatial dimension, see, e.g.,
[
5
,
17
]
. In the present work, we provide a first rigorous derivation of the Born-Oppenheimer approximation for a one-dimensional system with zero-range interactions, where the absence of divergences allows for a direct mathematical treatment. Nevertheless, even in this case, the standard mathematical procedure (as given, e.g., in
[
12
]
) does not work and we use the general scheme developed in
[
22
]
.

Let us remark that the eigenvalue expansion given in Theorem
1.1
conforms with the one obtained (for the bosonic case) in
[
2
]
, where the authors make the ansatz that the Born-Oppenheimer approximation can be applied to the system; for a first insight into the two-dimensional case by the same authors, see
[
3
]
.

Summary of the paper

We warn the reader that in Sections
4
,
5
and
6
we prefer to work with positive definite quantities. To this end, we shift quadratic forms and operators by
\alpha^{2}
.
Specifically, all operators denoted by
H
and
\mathscr{L}
– regardless of superscripts or subscripts – satisfy the relation
\mathscr{L}=H+\alpha^{2}
. An analogous convention holds for quadratic forms:
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{B}+\alpha^{2}
and
q=b+\alpha^{2}
. Translating the results back to the original setting is straightforward, and we carry out such a translation in the subsequent discussion.

We begin our analysis with the rigorous definition of the Hamiltonian
H^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon}
. This is done in Section
2
, see Theorem
2.12
, by means of standard tools from the theory of self-adjoint extensions of symmetric operators, within the approach developed in
[
28
]
(by Remark
2.1
, our setting does not allow the use of standard boundary triple theory). In particular, we provide a resolvent formula for
H^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon}
; this allows a precise characterization of its essential spectrum (see Theorem
1.2
below). We note that having a lower bound for
\sigma_{ess}(H^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon})
is also relevant for the proof of second part of Theorem
1.1
.

We refer to the discussion in Section
2
for a precise characterization of
H^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon}
; here we mention that functions in its domain are regular outside the coincidence planes
\Pi_{1}
and
\Pi_{2}
(as defined in Eq. (
1.1
)), continuous on
\Pi_{1}
and
\Pi_{2}
, and satisfy the boundary condition

\Big(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\partial_{x}\psi+\partial_{y}\psi\Big)(x,(x/2)^{+})-\Big(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\partial_{x}\psi+\partial_{y}\psi\Big)(x,(x/2)^{-})=\alpha\psi(x,x/2)\;\text{for a.e. $x\in\mathbb{R}$},

(1.4)

(x/2)^{\pm}
denoting the right (respectively, left) limit. A similar condition holds true on the coincidence plane
\Pi_{2}
and is obtained as a consequence of the bosonic/fermionic symmetry. The left hand side in the previous equation represents (up to a normalization factor) the jump of the normal derivative relative to
\varepsilon^{2}\partial^{2}_{x}+\partial^{2}_{y}
across
\Pi_{1}
, see also Remark
2.10
.

We point out that
H^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon}
can be alternatively characterized as the self-adjoint, bounded from below, operator associated to the closed and bounded from below quadratic form

\mathcal{B}^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon}:H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{2}_{\text{\rm b/f}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}

\mathcal{B}^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon}(\psi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varepsilon^{2}\left|\partial_{x}\psi(x,y)\right|^{2}+\left|\partial_{y}\psi(x,y)\right|^{2}{\bf d}{\bf x}+2\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\psi(s,s/2)\right|^{2}ds,

see Proposition
2.14
.

Section
2
ends with the proof of the following result that has an interest on its own.

Theorem 1.2
.

For all
\varepsilon>0

\sigma_{ess}(H^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon})=\begin{cases}\sigma(H^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon})=[0,+\infty)&\alpha\geq 0\\
\left[-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4+\varepsilon^{2}}\,,+\infty\right)&\alpha<0\,.\end{cases}

The Born-Oppenheimer approximation is based on the idea that the dynamics of the system factorizes in a fast dynamics, relative to the light particle, and a slow one, describing the evolution of the heavy particles subsystem. In Section
3
we analyze the Hamiltonian of the light particle. We fix the relative position
x
of the heavy particles and study the spectrum of the Hamiltonian
h_{x}
associated to the quadratic form

b_{x}:H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}

b_{x}(u)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|u^{\prime}(y)|^{2}\,dy+\alpha|u(x/2)|^{2}+\alpha|u(-x/2)|^{2}\,.

A precise definition of the self-adjoint operator
h_{x}
is given in Section
3
. Here we point out that
h_{x}
is the Hamiltonian in dimension one with two delta interactions centered in
y=x/2
and
y=-x/2
, see
[
4
]
. Functions in the domain of
h_{x}
are regular outside the points
y=\pm x/2
, continuous in
y=\pm x/2
, and satisfy the boundary conditions

u^{\prime}((x/2)^{+})-u^{\prime}((x/2)^{-})=\alpha u(x/2)\qquad\text{and}\qquad u^{\prime}((-x/2)^{+})-u^{\prime}((-x/2)^{-})=\alpha u(-x/2).

In Section
3
we give a full characterization of the spectrum of
h_{x}
, see Lemmata
3.1
and
3.2
, and Theorem
3.4
. A relevant outcome of this analysis is that the lowest eigenvalue of
h_{x}
, denoted by
-\lambda_{0}(x)
, and the corresponding normalized eigenfunction,
\psi^{BO}_{x}
, can be explicitly computed. These results were already discussed in
[
4
]
, and were also obtained with a different approach based on heat kernels in
[
2
]
.

In Section
4
we start implementing the Born-Oppenheimer approximation by means of a dimensional reduction which extracts an effective contribution from the light particle component, this is achieved by means of a projection on the eigenfunction
\psi^{BO}_{x}
(more precisely, by the direct integral of a family of projections). This procedure produces an effective Hamiltonian for the heavy particles subsystem. We follow closely the very versatile and general approach developed in
[
22
]
. By doing so we reproduce and rewrite, in a slightly different way, some key estimates, giving simpler expressions for the bounds (see Propositions
4.4
and
4.6
, and Lemma
4.7
compared with
[
22
, Theorems 1.1 and 2.5, and Proposition 2.6]
).

The first observation is that for any function
\phi\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
its
x
-section
\phi_{x}(y):=\phi(x,y)
belongs to
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
for a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Moreover
for any function
\phi\in H_{\text{\rm b/f}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
there holds

\mathcal{B}^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon}(\phi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varepsilon^{2}|\partial_{x}\phi(x,y)|^{2}{\bf d}{\bf x}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}b_{x}(\phi_{x})dx.

The latter identity, together with Theorem
3.4
gives the following result which completes Theorem
1.2
.

Theorem 1.3
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
and
\alpha<0
,
\sigma(H^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon})\subset[-\alpha^{2},+\infty)
.

To proceed further, we notice that
\psi^{BO}_{x}
can be regarded as a function of two variables, denoted by
\psi^{BO}
, with the obvious identification
\psi^{BO}(x,y)\equiv\psi^{BO}_{x}(y)
, and introduce the orthogonal projection

\mathcal{P}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\,,\qquad\mathcal{P}\phi(x,y):=\psi^{BO}(x,y)f_{\phi}(x)\,,

where

f_{\phi}(x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\psi^{BO}(x,y)}\phi(x,y)\,dy=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle\psi_{x}^{BO},\phi_{x}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},dy)}\,dx\,;

additionally we set
\mathcal{P}^{\perp}:=1-\mathcal{P}
and point out that
\mathcal{P}
leaves invariant both
L^{2}_{\text{\rm b}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
L^{2}_{\text{\rm f}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

It turns out that the quadratic form

D(\widehat{\mathcal{B}}^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon}):=H_{\text{\rm b/f}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\qquad\widehat{\mathcal{B}}^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon}(\phi):=\widehat{\mathcal{B}}^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{P}\phi)+\widehat{\mathcal{B}}^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{P}^{\perp}\phi)

is well-defined, closed and bounded from below (see Remarks
4.1
and
4.3
), hence it defines a self-adjoint operator
\widehat{H}^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon}
. The main result of Section
4
is that the resolvent set and the resolvent of
\widehat{H}^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon}
and
H^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon}
are related by the estimates given in the following lemma

Lemma 1.4
.

(i) Defining

\mathrm{d}_{\varepsilon}\equiv\mathrm{d}_{\varepsilon}(\mu):=\operatorname{dist}(\mu,\sigma(H^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon}))\,,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widehat{\mathrm{d}}_{\varepsilon}\equiv\widehat{\mathrm{d}}_{\varepsilon}(\mu):=\operatorname{dist}(\mu,\sigma(\widehat{H}^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon}))\,,

one has

\left\{\mu\in[-\alpha^{2},+\infty)\cap\rho(\widehat{H}^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon}):\frac{4\varepsilon\delta}{\sqrt{\widehat{\mathrm{d}}_{\varepsilon}(\mu)}}\,\sqrt{1+\frac{\mu+\alpha^{2}}{\widehat{\mathrm{d}}_{\varepsilon}(\mu)}}<\frac{1}{2}\right\}\subseteq\left\{\mu\in\rho(H^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon}):\mathrm{d}_{\varepsilon}(\mu)\geq\frac{\widehat{\mathrm{d}}_{\varepsilon}(\mu)}{32}\,\right\}.

(ii) For any
\mu\in\rho(H^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon})\cap\rho(\widehat{H}^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon})
,
\mu>-\alpha^{2}
, one has

\big\|(H^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon}-\mu)^{-1}-(\widehat{H}^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon}-\mu)^{-1}\big\|_{\mathscr{B}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))}\leq 2\varepsilon\delta\left(\frac{1}{\widehat{\mathrm{d}}_{\varepsilon}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mathrm{d}_{\varepsilon}}\left(1+\frac{\mu+\alpha^{2}}{\mathrm{d}_{\varepsilon}}\right)}+\frac{1}{\mathrm{d}_{\varepsilon}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{\widehat{\mathrm{d}}_{\varepsilon}}\left(1+\frac{\mu+\alpha^{2}}{\widehat{\mathrm{d}}_{\varepsilon}}\right)}\ \right).

This lemma is equivalent to Lemma
4.7
. The constant
\delta
is defined in Eq. (
4.3
) and it is an upper bound for the operator norm of the commutator
[\partial_{x},\mathcal{P}]
.

Next we need to identify the effective Hamiltonian for the heavy particles subsystem. We start by recalling Lemma
4.5
, translated to the unshifted setting: the operator
\widehat{H}^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon}
can be written as a direct sum

\widehat{H}^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon}=\widehat{H}^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon,\mathcal{P}}\oplus\widehat{H}^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon,\mathcal{P}^{\perp}}\,,\qquad\sigma(\widehat{H}^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon})=\sigma(\widehat{H}^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon,\mathcal{P}})\cup\sigma(\widehat{H}^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon,\mathcal{P}^{\perp}})\,,

where

\widehat{H}^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon,\mathcal{P}}:D(\widehat{H}^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon,\mathcal{P}})\subset\operatorname{ran}(\mathcal{P}|L^{2}_{\text{\rm b/f}}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))\to\operatorname{ran}(\mathcal{P}|L^{2}_{\text{\rm b/f}}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))\,,

\widehat{H}^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon,\mathcal{P}^{\perp}}:D(\widehat{H}^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon,\mathcal{P}^{\perp}})\subset\operatorname{ran}(\mathcal{P}^{\perp}|L^{2}_{\text{\rm b/f}}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))\to\operatorname{ran}(\mathcal{P}^{\perp}|L^{2}_{\text{\rm b/f}}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))\,.

Additionally,

\sigma(\widehat{H}^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon,\mathcal{P}})\subseteq[-\alpha^{2},+\infty)\,,\qquad\sigma(\widehat{H}^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon,\mathcal{P}^{\perp}})\subseteq[-\alpha^{2}/4,+\infty)\,.

For the study of the eigenvalues at the bottom of the spectrum (near
-\alpha^{2}
), the most relevant operator is
\widehat{H}^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon,\mathcal{P}}
. Section
5
is devoted to the analysis of its spectrum. The first observation, see Remark
5.1
, is that, by means of a unitary map, the Hamiltonian
\widehat{H}^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon,\mathcal{P}}
can be reduced to an effective one dimensional operator on the heavy particles subsystem

D(H^{\mathrm{eff}\,b/f}_{\varepsilon})=H^{2}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{2}_{\text{\rm b/f}}(\mathbb{R})\qquad H^{\mathrm{eff}\,b/f}_{\varepsilon}=-\varepsilon^{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}-\lambda_{0}+\varepsilon^{2}\,R,

where
-\lambda_{0}(x)
is the lowest eigenvalue of
h_{x}
and
\varepsilon^{2}R(x):=\varepsilon^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\partial_{x}\psi^{BO}(x,y)|^{2}dy
is a perturbative potential term. We remark that
\operatorname{ran}(\mathcal{P}|D(H^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon}))\nsubseteq D(H^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon})
, because
\mathcal{P}\phi
does not satisfy the boundary condition (
1.4
). For this reason, contrarily to what is done in
[
22
]
, it is not possible to identify
\widehat{H}^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon,\mathcal{P}}
with the compression
\mathcal{P}H^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{P}
, which is not well defined.

The main result of Section
5
is the following theorem

Theorem 1.5
.

For any fixed integer
n\geq 0
there exists
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small such that
H^{\mathrm{eff}\,\text{\rm b}/\text{\rm f}}_{\varepsilon}
has at least
(n+1)
simple isolated eigenvalues

-\alpha^{2}<E^{\mathrm{eff}\,\text{\rm b}/\text{\rm f}}_{\varepsilon,0}<E^{\mathrm{eff}\,\text{\rm b}/\text{\rm f}}_{\varepsilon,1}<\dots<E^{\mathrm{eff}\,\text{\rm b}/\text{\rm f}}_{\varepsilon,n}<-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4+\varepsilon^{2}}\,,

E^{\mathrm{eff}\,\text{\rm b}/\text{\rm f}}_{\varepsilon,k}=-\alpha^{2}+s^{\text{\rm b}/\text{\rm f}}\alpha^{2}\varepsilon^{2/3}+O(\varepsilon)\,.

where
s^{\text{\rm b/f}}_{k}
are the same numbers as in Theorem
1.1
.

The latter result follows immediately from Theorem
5.9
. The proof is based on the seminal paper
[
32
]
. We remark that with respect to the case studied in
[
32
]
, where the potential is smooth, in our analysis the potential term
-\lambda_{0}
is only piecewise smooth, it is continuous, but not differentiable at

. Additionally,
-\lambda_{0}
is linear around

, rather than quadratic, as in the smooth case. For this reason the eigenvalue expansion involves zeroes and extrema of the Airy function, a result already pointed out in
[
2
]
. For related results with generic non-smooth potentials we refer to
[
7
]
and references therein.

Remark 2.5

Let

\|Tu\|_{\mathscr{H}}\geq c\|u\|_{\mathscr{H}}\qquad\forall u\in D(T).

and

. Hence,
T
is also surjective, and has inverse bounded by

.

Proposition 2.6

(i) Let

be an eigenvector of

By Proposition

This gives

Applying

On the other hand, assume that there exists

(ii) Let

\displaystyle(1+2\alpha M_{\varepsilon}(-\lambda))\tau\psi_{n}-2\alpha(M_{\varepsilon}(-\lambda)-M_{\varepsilon}(z))\tau\psi_{n}+2\alpha(z+\lambda)\breve{G}_{\varepsilon}(-\lambda)G_{\varepsilon}(z)\tau\psi_{n}

Lemma 2.18
.

For all
\varepsilon>0
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,
[0,+\infty)\subseteq\sigma_{ess}(H^{\natural}_{\varepsilon})
.

Proof.

The idea is to construct a singular Weyl sequence supported away from the coincidence lines, which also serves as a singular sequence for the free Hamiltonian. Let
\chi
be a

C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, spherically symmetric function, such that
\chi({\bf x})=0
for all
|{\bf x}|\geq 1
and
\|\chi\|=1
.

For
n\geq 1
, define a sequence of functions:

\chi_{n}({\bf x}):=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{n}\,\chi\left(\frac{{\bf x}-{\bf x}_{n}}{n}\right),\qquad{\bf x}_{n}=(3n,0)\,.

Note that for any
n\geq 1
, the support of
\chi_{n}({\bf x})
lies in an open disk, which we denote by
\mathcal{D}_{n}
, centered at
{\bf x}_{n}
with radius
R=n
. In particular,
\chi_{n}({\bf x})
vanishes on the coincidence lines
y=\pm x/2
. Next, for
n\geq 1
, we define

\psi_{n}({\bf x}):=e^{-i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf x}}\chi_{n}({\bf x})\qquad{\bf k}=(\varepsilon^{-1}\sqrt{\lambda/2},\sqrt{\lambda/2})\,,

and

\psi^{{\natural}}_{n}:=\frac{1{(+)}_{\natural}S}{2}\,\psi_{n},

with the orthogonal projection
\frac{1{(+)}_{\natural}S}{2}
defined in Eqs. (
2.22
) - (
2.23
).

Note that for any
n\geq 1
, the support of
\psi_{n}
lies in the open disk
D_{n}
, while the support of
S\psi_{n}
lies in an open disk centered at
{\bf x}_{n}=(-3n,0)
with radius
R=n
. The supports of
\psi_{n}
and
S\psi_{n}
are disjoint and both functions vanish on the coincidence lines
y=\pm x/2
. By construction,
\|\psi^{{\natural}}_{n}\|=1
, furthermore,
\psi_{n}^{{\natural}}\in D(H^{\natural}_{\varepsilon})\cap D(H^{0}_{\varepsilon})
and
H^{\natural}_{\varepsilon}\psi_{n}^{{\natural}}=H^{0}_{\varepsilon}\psi_{n}^{{\natural}}
, and we have (we use the notation
\nabla_{\!\varepsilon}\phi=(\varepsilon\partial_{x}\phi,\partial_{y}\phi
):

\begin{split}\lVert(H^{\natural}_{\varepsilon}-\lambda)\psi^{{\natural}}_{n}\rVert&=\lVert(H^{0}_{\varepsilon}-\lambda)\psi^{{\natural}}_{n}\rVert\\
&\leq\lVert(H^{0}_{\varepsilon}-\lambda)\psi_{n}\rVert=\left\lVert 2(\nabla_{\!\varepsilon}\chi_{n})\cdot(\nabla_{\!\varepsilon}e^{-i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf x}})+(H^{0}_{\varepsilon}\chi_{n})e^{-i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf x}}\right\rVert\\
&\leq 2\sqrt{\lambda}\,\lVert\nabla_{\!\varepsilon}\chi_{n}\rVert+\lVert H^{0}_{\varepsilon}\chi_{n}\rVert\\
&\leq c_{\varepsilon}\,\left(\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{n}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)\,.\end{split}

In the last inequality, we used the fact that
\chi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, so its gradient and Laplacian are bounded. Therefore,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\lVert(H^{\natural}_{\varepsilon}-\lambda)\psi^{{\natural}}_{n}\rVert=0\,.

Finally, for any
\phi\in L^{2}_{{\natural}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
one has, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and by the dominated convergence theorem,

\left|\langle\phi,\psi^{{\natural}}_{n}\rangle\right|^{2}=\left|\langle\phi,\psi_{n}\rangle\right|^{2}\leq\frac{2}{n^{2}}\,\text{Area}(D_{n})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left|\phi({\bf x})\chi\left(\frac{{\bf x}-{\bf x}_{n}}{n}\right)\right|^{2}{\bf d}{\bf x}\to 0\,.

Therefore
\psi_{n}\rightharpoonup 0
and this concludes the proof.

3.
The Hamiltonian for the light particle

Following
[
4
, Section II.2.1]
(see also
[
34
, Section 8.5]
), we introduce the self-adjoint Hamiltonian
h_{x}
modeling a delta-interaction of a 1D quantum particle with two centers placed at points
-x/2
and
x/2
. The operator
h_{x}
depends on a real parameter

\alpha
associated to the strength of the interaction; for notational simplicity, we prefer not to explicitly indicate such a dependency. One has

\displaystyle D(h_{x}):=\big\{

\displaystyle u\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}\backslash\{\pm x/2\})\cap H^{1}(\mathbb{R}):[u^{\prime}\,](\pm x/2)=\alpha u(\pm x/2)\big\}\,,

(3.1)

h_{x}u(y)=-u^{\prime\prime}(y)\qquad\text{for a.e. $y\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{\pm x/2\}$}\,,

(3.2)

where
[u^{\prime}](y)
denotes the jump of the derivative
u^{\prime}
across
y
.

Furthermore, the sesquilinear form associated to
h_{x}
is given by

b_{x}:H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\times H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\times L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{C}

(3.3)

b_{x}(u,v):=\langle u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\rangle+\alpha\bar{u}(x/2)v(x/2)+\alpha\bar{u}(-x/2)v(-x/2)\,.

(3.4)

3.1.
The spectrum of
h_{x}
.

For any
z\in\mathbb{C}\backslash[0,+\infty)
we denote by
r^{0}(z)
the resolvent of the self-adjoint Laplacian in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with domain
H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
; its integral kernel is

r^{0}(y-y^{\prime};z)=i\,\frac{e^{i\sqrt{z}\,|y-y^{\prime}|}}{2\sqrt{z}}\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}\backslash[0,+\infty)\,,\quad\operatorname{Im}\sqrt{z}>0\,.

Moreover, for any
z\in\mathbb{C}\backslash[0,+\infty)
we set:

\breve{\bf g}_{x}(z):L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{C}^{2}\,,\qquad\breve{\bf g}_{x}(z)u:=\big((r^{0}(z)u)(x/2),(r^{0}(z)u)(-x/2)\big)\,;

{\bf g}_{x}(z):\mathbb{C}^{2}\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\,,\qquad\quad{\bf g}_{x}(z):=\breve{\bf g}_{x}(\bar{z})^{*}\,,

({\bf g}_{x}(z)(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}))(y)=r^{0}(y-x/2;z)\zeta_{1}+r^{0}(y+x/2;z)\zeta_{2}\,,

and

{\bf m}_{x}(z):\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}\qquad{\bf m}_{x}(z):=\frac{i}{2\sqrt{z}}\,\begin{bmatrix}1&e^{i\sqrt{z}\,|x|}\,\\
e^{i\sqrt{z}\,|x|}&1\end{bmatrix}.

For all
z\in\mathbb{C}\backslash[0,+\infty)
and for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\det(1+\alpha\,{\bf m}_{x}(z))\not=0
, the resolvent of
h_{x}
is given by

(h_{x}-z)^{-1}=r^{0}(z)-\alpha{\bf g}_{x}(z)\big(1+\alpha{\bf m}_{x}(z)\big)^{-1}\breve{\bf g}_{x}(z).

The values
z=-\lambda<0
, such that
\det(1+\alpha{\bf m}_{x}(z))=0
equivalently, such that

\big(\alpha+2\sqrt{\lambda}\,\big)^{2}=\alpha^{2}e^{-2\sqrt{\lambda}\,|x|}\,,

(3.5)

correspond to the eigenvalues of
h_{x}
.
Obviously, (
3.5
) has no solution for
\alpha\geq 0
. For
\alpha<0
, Eq.

3.5
) admits one or two solutions according to the values of
\alpha
and
x
. We resume the spectral properties of

in the following

(i)
\sigma_{ac}(h_{x})=\sigma_{ess}(h_{x})=[0,+\infty)

(ii)
\sigma_{sc}(h_{x})=\varnothing

(iii)

\sigma_{p}(h_{x})=\sigma_{d}(h_{x})=\begin{cases}\varnothing&\alpha\geq 0\\

To better describe the behavior of the eigenvalues as function of

, we introduce the Lambert

-function, defined as the solution of the equation

As consequence of such a definition, one gets the following

Let

(ii)

\alpha^{2}/4<\lambda_{0}(x)\leq\alpha^{2}

We provide a dimensional reduction of

\mathcal{P}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\,,\qquad\mathcal{P}\phi(x,y):=\psi^{BO}(x,y)f_{\phi}(x)\,,

where

f_{\phi}(x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\psi^{BO}(x,y)}\phi(x,y)\,dy=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle\psi_{x}^{BO},\phi_{x}\rangle\,dx\,.

Let us point out the inequality

\|f_{\phi}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}{|\psi^{BO}(x,y)}|\,|\phi(x,y)|\,dy\right)^{\!2}\!dx\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|\psi^{BO}_{x}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\|\phi_{x}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\,dx=\|\phi\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}.

Remark 4.1
.

Notice that, since
\psi^{BO}
is an even function with respect to the
x
variable,
f_{\phi}
is an even function whenever
\phi\in L^{2}_{\text{\rm b}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and is an odd function whenever
\phi\in L^{2}_{\text{\rm f}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Hence,
\mathcal{P}
is an orthogonal projector in
L^{2}_{{\natural}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
as well.

Lemma 4.2
.

i)
\mathcal{P}
is a bounded operator in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
;
ii) the commutator
[\partial_{x},\mathcal{P}]:H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
extends to a bounded operator in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and

\|\,[\partial_{x},\mathcal{P}]\,\|_{\mathscr{B}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))}\leq\delta\,,

with

\delta:=2\left(\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\int|\partial_{x}\psi^{BO}(x,y)|^{2}dy\right)^{1/2}

(4.3)

and the bound
\delta\leq 4|\alpha|
.

Proof.

In the course of the proof, for the sake of brevity, we will often omit the dependence on
x
in
N(x)
and
\lambda_{0}(x)
. From the formula (
3.6
), one gets

\partial_{y}\psi^{BO}(x,y)={N}\sqrt{\lambda_{0}}\left(\sgn(x/2-y)e^{-\sqrt{\lambda_{0}}\,|x/2-y|}-\sgn(x/2+y)e^{-\sqrt{\lambda_{0}}\,|x/2+y|}\right),

and so

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\partial_{y}\psi^{BO}(x,y)\right|^{2}dy=

\displaystyle 2N^{2}\sqrt{\lambda_{0}}\left(1+e^{-\sqrt{\lambda_{0}}\,|x|}-\sqrt{\lambda_{0}}\,|x|e^{-\sqrt{\lambda_{0}}\,|x|}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}\,\frac{1+e^{-\sqrt{\lambda_{0}}\,|x|}-\sqrt{\lambda_{0}}\,|x|e^{-\sqrt{\lambda_{0}}\,|x|}}{1+e^{-\sqrt{\lambda_{0}}\,|x|}+\sqrt{\lambda_{0}}\,|x|e^{-\sqrt{\lambda_{0}}\,|x|}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}\leq\alpha^{2}\,.

Hence,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left|\partial_{y}\mathcal{P}\phi(x,y)\right|^{2}{\bf d}{\bf x}=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left|\partial_{y}\psi^{BO}(x,y)\right|^{2}|f_{\phi}(x)|^{2}dx\,dy

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\partial_{y}\psi^{BO}(x,y)\right|^{2}dy\right)\|f_{\phi}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}\|\phi\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\,.

Defining

\tilde{f}_{\phi}(x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big(\partial_{x}\psi^{BO}(x,y)\big)\phi(x,y)dy\,,

one has

\|\tilde{f}_{\phi}\|\leq\frac{\delta}{2}\,\|\phi\|\,.

Since

\partial_{x}\mathcal{P}\phi=\big(\partial_{x}\psi^{BO}\big)f_{\phi}+\psi^{BO}\tilde{f}_{\phi}+\psi^{BO}f_{\partial_{x}\phi},

there holds

\displaystyle\|\partial_{x}\mathcal{P}\phi\|\leq

\displaystyle\|\big(\partial_{x}\psi^{BO}\big)f_{\phi}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}+\|\psi^{BO}\tilde{f}_{\phi}\|+\|\psi^{BO}f_{\partial_{x}\phi}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\delta}{2}\,\|f_{\phi}\|+\|\tilde{f}_{\phi}\|+\|\partial_{x}\phi\|\leq(1+\delta)\|\phi\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}.

In a similar way, one obtains

[\partial_{x},\mathcal{P}]\phi=\big(\partial_{x}\psi^{BO}\big)f_{\phi}+\psi^{BO}\tilde{f}_{\phi},

and

\|\,[\partial_{x},\mathcal{P}]\phi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq\delta\,\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}.

We are left to prove the bound on
\delta
.
From the formula (
3.6
), one can explicitly compute

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\psi^{BO}(x,y)=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\frac{N^{\prime}}{N}\psi^{BO}}_{\varphi^{BO}_{1}}-\underbrace{\frac{{N}\lambda_{0}^{\prime}}{2\sqrt{\lambda_{0}}}\left(|x/2-y|e^{-\sqrt{\lambda_{0}}|x/2-y|}+|x/2+y|e^{-\sqrt{\lambda_{0}}|x/2+y|}\right)}_{\varphi^{BO}_{2}}

\displaystyle-\underbrace{\frac{{N}\sqrt{\lambda_{0}}}{2}\left(\sgn(x/2-y)e^{-\sqrt{\lambda_{0}}|x/2-y|}+\sgn(x/2+y)e^{-\sqrt{\lambda_{0}}|x/2+y|}\right)}_{\varphi^{BO}_{3}}.

Computing the norm of
\varphi^{BO}_{1}
one obtains

From Eq. (

We notice that

By Lemma

From the identity

(4.4)

Proposition 4.4
.

For all
\phi,\psi\in H^{1}_{\natural}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
there holds

\big|\mathcal{Q}^{{\natural}}_{\varepsilon}(\phi,\psi)-\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}^{{\natural}}_{\varepsilon}(\phi,\psi)\big|\\
\leq 2\varepsilon\delta\Big(\sqrt{\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}(\phi)}\,\|\psi\|+\|\phi\|\,\sqrt{\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}(\psi)}\ \Big).

Proof.

By the definition of
\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}
there follows

\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}(\phi,\psi)-\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}(\phi,\psi)=\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}(\mathcal{P}\phi,\mathcal{P}^{\perp}\psi)+\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}(\mathcal{P}^{\perp}\phi,\mathcal{P}\psi).

Defining
S_{\varepsilon}\phi:=-i\varepsilon\partial_{x}
, since
q_{x}(\mathcal{P}_{x}u,\mathcal{P}_{x}^{\perp}u)=q_{x}(\mathcal{P}_{x}^{\perp}u,\mathcal{P}_{x}u)=0
, by Definition
4.2
, there follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}(\phi,\psi)-\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}(\phi,\psi)=

\displaystyle\langle S_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{P}\phi,S_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{P}^{\perp}\psi\rangle+\langle S_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{P}^{\perp}\phi,S_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{P}\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle S_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{P}\phi,S_{\varepsilon}(1-\mathcal{P})\psi\rangle+\langle S_{\varepsilon}(1-\mathcal{P})\phi,S_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{P}\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle S_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{P}\phi,S_{\varepsilon}\psi\rangle+\langle S_{\varepsilon}\phi,S_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{P}\psi\rangle-2\langle S_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{P}\phi,S_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{P}\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle[S_{\varepsilon},\mathcal{P}]\phi,S_{\varepsilon}\psi\rangle+\langle\mathcal{P}S_{\varepsilon}\phi,S_{\varepsilon}\psi\rangle

\displaystyle+\langle S_{\varepsilon}\phi,S_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{P}\psi\rangle-2\langle[S_{\varepsilon},\mathcal{P}]\phi,S_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{P}\psi\rangle-2\langle\mathcal{P}S_{\varepsilon}\phi,S_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{P}\psi\rangle.

Note that

\displaystyle-2\langle\mathcal{P}S_{\varepsilon}\phi,S_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{P}\psi\rangle=

\displaystyle-2\langle S_{\varepsilon}\phi,\mathcal{P}S_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{P}\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\langle S_{\varepsilon}\phi,[\mathcal{P},S_{\varepsilon}]\mathcal{P}\psi\rangle-2\langle S_{\varepsilon}\phi,S_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{P}^{2}\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\langle S_{\varepsilon}\phi,[S_{\varepsilon},\mathcal{P}]\mathcal{P}\psi\rangle-2\langle S_{\varepsilon}\phi,S_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{P}\psi\rangle,

and

\langle\mathcal{P}S_{\varepsilon}\phi,S_{\varepsilon}\psi\rangle=\langle S_{\varepsilon}\phi,\mathcal{P}S_{\varepsilon}\psi\rangle=-\langle S_{\varepsilon}\phi,[S_{\varepsilon},\mathcal{P}]\psi\rangle+\langle S_{\varepsilon}\phi,S_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{P}\psi\rangle.

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}(\phi,\psi)-\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}(\phi,\psi)=

\displaystyle\langle[S_{\varepsilon},\mathcal{P}]\phi,S_{\varepsilon}\psi\rangle+\big(-\langle S_{\varepsilon}\phi,[S_{\varepsilon},\mathcal{P}]\psi\rangle+\langle S_{\varepsilon}\phi,S_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{P}\psi\rangle\big)

\displaystyle+\langle S_{\varepsilon}\phi,S_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{P}\psi)-2\langle[S_{\varepsilon},\mathcal{P}]\phi,S_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{P}\psi\rangle+\big(2\langle S_{\varepsilon}\phi,[S_{\varepsilon},\mathcal{P}]\mathcal{P}\psi\rangle-2\langle S_{\varepsilon}\phi,S_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{P}\psi\rangle\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle[S_{\varepsilon},\mathcal{P}]\phi,S_{\varepsilon}\psi\rangle-\langle S_{\varepsilon}\phi,[S_{\varepsilon},\mathcal{P}]\psi\rangle-2\langle[S_{\varepsilon},\mathcal{P}]\phi,S_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{P}\psi\rangle+2\langle S_{\varepsilon}\phi,[S_{\varepsilon},\mathcal{P}]\mathcal{P}\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle[S_{\varepsilon},\mathcal{P}]\phi,S_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{P}^{\perp}\psi\rangle-\langle S_{\varepsilon}\phi,[S_{\varepsilon},\mathcal{P}]\mathcal{P}^{\perp}\psi\rangle-\langle[S_{\varepsilon},\mathcal{P}]\phi,S_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{P}\psi\rangle+\langle S_{\varepsilon}\phi,[S_{\varepsilon},\mathcal{P}]\mathcal{P}\psi\rangle\,.

By Lemma
4.2
, this gives

\displaystyle\big|\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}(\phi,\psi)-\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}(\phi,\psi)\big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\varepsilon\delta\Big(\|\varepsilon\partial_{x}\phi\|\big(\|\mathcal{P}\psi\|+\|\mathcal{P}^{\perp}\psi\|\big)+\|\phi\|\big(\|\varepsilon\partial_{x}\mathcal{P}\psi\|+\|\varepsilon\partial_{x}\mathcal{P}^{\perp}\psi\|\big)\Big).

By the obvious inequality
a+b\leq 2\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}
, there follows

\displaystyle\big|\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}(\phi,\psi)-\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}(\phi,\psi)\big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\varepsilon\delta\Big(\sqrt{\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}(\phi)}\,\|\psi\|+\|\phi\|\,\sqrt{\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}(\psi)}\ \Big),

where in the latter inequality we used the fact that the quadratic form

Lemma 4.7
.

(i) Defining

(ii) For any

\big\|(\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}-\lambda)^{-1}-(\widehat{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}-\lambda)^{-1}\big\|_{\mathscr{B}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))}\leq 2\varepsilon\delta\left(\frac{1}{\widehat{d}_{\varepsilon}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{d_{\varepsilon}}\left(1+\frac{\lambda_{+}}{d_{\varepsilon}}\right)}+\frac{1}{d_{\varepsilon}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{\widehat{d}_{\varepsilon}}\left(1+\frac{\lambda_{+}}{\widehat{d}_{\varepsilon}}\right)}\ \right).

Proof.

In Proposition
4.6

\psi=(\widehat{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}-\lambda)^{-1}\phi

\displaystyle\left|\|\phi\|^{2}-\big\langle(\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}-\lambda)\phi,(\widehat{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}-\lambda)^{-1}\phi\big\rangle\right|

\displaystyle+\|\phi\|\sqrt{\left(\|\phi\|+\lambda_{+}\,\|(\widehat{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}-\lambda)^{-1}\phi\|\right)\|(\widehat{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}-\lambda)^{-1}\phi\|}\ \Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\varepsilon\delta\Bigg(\|(\widehat{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}-\lambda)^{-1}\chi\|\sqrt{\left(\|\tilde{\chi}\|+\lambda_{+}\,\|\left(\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}-\lambda\right)^{-1}\tilde{\chi}\|\right)\|(\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}-\lambda)^{-1}\tilde{\chi}\|}

\displaystyle+\|(\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}-\lambda)^{-1}\tilde{\chi}\|\sqrt{\left(\|\chi\|+\lambda_{+}\,\|(\widehat{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}-\lambda)^{-1}\chi\|\right)\|(\widehat{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}-\lambda)^{-1}\chi\|}\ \Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\varepsilon\delta\left(\frac{1}{\widehat{d}_{\varepsilon}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{d_{\varepsilon}}\left(1+\frac{\lambda_{+}}{d_{\varepsilon}}\right)}+\frac{1}{d_{\varepsilon}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{\widehat{d}_{\varepsilon}}\left(1+\frac{\lambda_{+}}{\widehat{d}_{\varepsilon}}\right)}\ \right)\|\chi\|\|\tilde{\chi}\|.

To conclude, take

\tilde{\chi}=\big((\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}-\lambda)^{-1}-(\widehat{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}-\lambda)^{-1}\big)\chi

so that

\Big\|\big((\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}-\lambda)^{-1}-(\widehat{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}-\lambda)^{-1}\big)\chi\Big\|\leq 2\varepsilon\delta\left(\frac{1}{\widehat{d}_{\varepsilon}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{d_{\varepsilon}}\left(1+\frac{\lambda_{+}}{d_{\varepsilon}}\right)}+\frac{1}{d_{\varepsilon}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{\widehat{d}_{\varepsilon}}\left(1+\frac{\lambda_{+}}{\widehat{d}_{\varepsilon}}\right)}\ \right)\|\chi\|\,.

∎

5.
The effective Hamiltonian

We begin by a rewriting of the quadratic form
\mathcal{Q}^{\natural}_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{P}\varphi,\mathcal{P}\psi)
associated to the operator
\widehat{\mathscr{L}}^{{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,\mathcal{P}}
given in Lemma
4.5
, i.e.,

\mathcal{Q}^{\natural}_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{P}\varphi,\mathcal{P}\psi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varepsilon^{2}{\partial_{x}(\overline{\psi^{BO}f_{\varphi}})}\partial_{x}(\psi^{BO}f_{\psi}){\bf d}{\bf x}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}q_{x}(\psi_{x}^{BO},\psi_{x}^{BO})\overline{f_{\varphi}}f_{\psi}\,dx.

We point out the identity

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}{\partial_{x}(\overline{\psi^{BO}f_{\varphi}})}\partial_{x}(\psi^{BO}f_{\psi}){\bf d}{\bf x}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\overline{f_{\varphi}^{\prime}}f_{\psi}^{\prime}\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\partial_{x}\psi^{BO}|^{2}dy\right)\overline{f_{\varphi}}f_{\psi}\,dx,

where we used
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi^{BO}\partial_{x}\psi^{BO}dy=0
, which is a consequence of the normalization condition
\|\psi_{x}^{BO}\|=1
. Also we notice that

q_{x}(\psi_{x}^{BO},\psi_{x}^{BO})=-\lambda_{0}(x)+\alpha^{2}\,.

Hence,

\mathcal{Q}^{\natural}_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{P}\varphi,\mathcal{P}\psi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big(\varepsilon^{2}\overline{f_{\varphi}^{\prime}}f_{\psi}^{\prime}+\big(V+\varepsilon^{2}\,R\big)\overline{f_{\varphi}}f_{\psi}\big)\,dx

with

V(x):=-\lambda_{0}(x)+\alpha^{2}=-\left(\frac{W\left(\frac{|\alpha||x|}{2}e^{\frac{-|\alpha||x|}{2}}\right)}{|x|}+\frac{|\alpha|}{2}\right)^{\!\!2}+\alpha^{2}\,,

and

R(x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\partial_{x}\psi^{BO}(x,y)|^{2}dy\,.

Remark 5.1
.

Let us define

L^{2}_{{\natural}}(\mathbb{R}):=\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}):f(x)={(+)}_{{\natural}}f(-x)\}\,,\qquad H^{1}_{{\natural}}(\mathbb{R}):=H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{2}_{{\natural}}(\mathbb{R})

and

U_{BO}:\operatorname{ran}(\mathcal{P}|L_{{\natural}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))\to L^{2}_{{\natural}}(\mathbb{R})\,,\qquad U_{BO}(\psi^{BO}f_{\phi}):=f_{\phi}\,.

By
\|\psi^{BO}_{x}\|=1
,

\langle\psi^{BO}f_{\phi},\psi^{BO}f_{\psi}\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle\psi^{BO}_{x},\psi^{BO}_{x}\rangle\overline{f}_{\phi}(x)f_{\psi}(x)\,dx=\langle f_{\phi},f_{\psi}\rangle\,,

i.e.,
U_{BO}
preserves the scalar product. It is a bijection with inverse
U_{BO}^{-1}f=\psi^{BO}f
; hence,
U_{BO}
is unitary and

\operatorname{ran}(\mathcal{P}|L_{{\natural}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))\simeq L^{2}_{{\natural}}(\mathbb{R})\,.

By the previous Remark,
\mathcal{Q}^{\natural}_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{P})
identifies with the sesquilinear form in
L^{2}_{{\natural}}(\mathbb{R})
defined by

D(\mathrm{Q^{eff}_{\varepsilon}}^{{\natural}}):=H_{{\natural}}^{1}(\mathbb{R})\times H_{{\natural}}^{1}(\mathbb{R})\,,\qquad\mathrm{Q^{eff}_{\varepsilon}}^{{\natural}}(f,g):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varepsilon^{2}\overline{f^{\prime}}g^{\prime}+\big(V+\varepsilon^{2}\,R\big)\overline{\text{\rm f}}g\,dx.

Since
V+\varepsilon^{2}\,R
is a bounded potential, the associated self-adjoint Hamiltonian in
L_{{\natural}}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is given by

D(\mathscr{L}^{\mathrm{eff}{\natural}}_{\varepsilon})=H_{{\natural}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}):=H^{2}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{2}_{{\natural}}(\mathbb{R})\qquad\mathscr{L}^{\mathrm{eff}{\natural}}_{\varepsilon}=-\varepsilon^{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+V+\varepsilon^{2}\,R.

Now, we analyze the spectrum of

\mathscr{L}^{\mathrm{eff}{\natural}}_{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon^{2}R=-\varepsilon^{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+V.

We note that:

(i)

V
is even on
\mathbb{R}
;

(ii)

0\leq V(x)<\frac{3}{4}\alpha^{2}
;

(iii)

V(0)=0
, and
\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}V(x)=\frac{3}{4}\alpha^{2}
;

(iv)

V
is strictly increasing on
(0,+\infty)
;

Furthermore, by

(W(ye^{-y})+y)^{2}-4y^{2}=-8y^{3}+O(y^{4})\,,\qquad y\ll 1\,,

there follows

V(x)=|\alpha|^{3}|x|+O(x^{2})\,,\qquad|x|\ll 1\,.

(5.1)

To proceed, we set
\Lambda:=\varepsilon^{-1}
and define

\mathrm{L}_{\Lambda}^{\natural}:=\Lambda^{2}\mathscr{L}^{\mathrm{eff}{\natural}}_{1/\Lambda}-R\equiv-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\Lambda^{2}V\,.

(5.2)

Denoting by
\mathrm{L}_{\Lambda}
the self-adjoint operator in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
defined by the same differential expression as
\mathrm{L}_{\Lambda}^{\natural}
, since
(V-\frac{3}{4}\alpha^{2})
is bounded and vanishes as
|x|\to+\infty
one has (see, e.g.,
[
30
, Theorem 3.8.2]
)

\sigma_{ess}(\mathrm{L}_{\Lambda}^{\natural})\subseteq\sigma_{ess}(\mathrm{L}_{\Lambda})=\sigma_{ess}\!\left(-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\frac{3}{4}\,\alpha^{2}\Lambda^{2}\right)=\left[\frac{3}{4}\,\alpha^{2}\Lambda^{2},+\infty\right)\,.

Since
\mathrm{L}_{\Lambda}
is in the limit point case at both ends
\pm\infty
, its eigenvalues are simple (see, e.g.,
[
36
, Section 10]
); hence the eigenvalues of
\mathrm{L}_{\Lambda}^{\natural}
are simple as well.

We denote by
\ell_{\Lambda,n}^{\natural}
,
n=0,1,2,\dots,
the eigenvalues of
\mathrm{L}_{\Lambda}^{\natural}
below the essential spectrum, numbered in increasing order

0<\ell^{{\natural}}_{\Lambda,0}<\ell^{{\natural}}_{\Lambda,1}<\cdots<\ell_{\Lambda,n}^{\natural}<\cdots.

The behavior as
\Lambda\gg 1
of such eigenvalues is provided in the following

Theorem 5.2
.

For any fixed integer
n\geq 0
and sufficiently large
\Lambda
,
\mathrm{L}_{\Lambda}^{\natural}
has at least
n+1
simple isolated eigenvalues and

\ell_{\Lambda,n}^{\natural}=e_{n}^{{\natural}}\Lambda^{4/3}+O(\Lambda)\,,\qquad e^{\text{\rm b}}_{n}:=e_{2n}\,,\quad e_{n}^{\text{\rm f}}:=e_{2n+1}\,,

where
e_{k}
denotes the
(k+1)
-th eigenvalue of the closure
K^{1}
of the essentially self-adjoint operator

D(\mathrm{\dot{K}^{1}})=C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R})\,,\qquad\mathrm{\dot{K}^{1}}:=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+|\alpha|^{3}|x|.

We prove this theorem by Barry Simon’s approach presented in
[
32
]
, where the author considered the case with a smooth, bounded from below potential. We adapt his strategy to our situation, where the potential is not differentiable at the origin. The proof of Theorem
5.2
follows from the combination of Lemmata
5.7
and
5.8
.

For later use, we recall several properties of the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of
{\mathrm{K}}^{1}
. We refer to
[
31
, Sect. 6.10]
for the details (in particular, see see
[
31
, Eqs. 6.10.9, 6.10.10, 6.9.1, 6.9.2]
; in the notation there,
\sigma_{2n}=\overline{\overline{\sigma}}_{n+1}
and
\sigma_{2n+1}=\overline{\sigma}_{n+1}
).

Lemma 5.3
.

The solutions
\phi_{k}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
of the eigenvalue equations

{\mathrm{K}}^{1}\phi_{k}=e_{k}\phi_{k}\,,\qquad k=0,1,2,\dots\,.

can be written in terms of the Airy function
\operatorname{Ai}
as

\phi_{2n}(x)=C_{2n}\operatorname{Ai}(|\alpha||x|+\sigma_{2k})\,,\qquad n=0,1,2,\dots\,,

(5.3)

\phi_{2n+1}(x)=C_{2n+1}\sgn(x)\operatorname{Ai}(|\alpha||x|+\sigma_{2n+1})\,,\qquad n=0,1,2,\dots\,,

(5.4)

where the
C_{k}
’s are normalization constants,
the
\sigma_{2n}
’s and
\sigma_{2n+1}
’s interlace,

\dots<\sigma_{2n+1}<\sigma_{2n}<\dots<\sigma_{1}<\sigma_{0}<0\,,

are the extrema
\operatorname{Ai}^{\prime}(\sigma_{2n})=0
and the zeros
\operatorname{Ai}(\sigma_{2n+1})=0
of the Airy function respectively. The eigenvalues

0<e_{0}<e_{1}<e_{2}<\dots<e_{k}<\dots.

are given by

e_{k}=|\sigma_{k}|\,\alpha^{2}\,.

Remark 5.4
.

Note that
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})=L^{2}_{\text{\rm b}}(\mathbb{R})\oplus L^{2}_{\text{\rm f}}(\mathbb{R})
, this is equivalent to decomposing any function in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
in the sum of its even and odd parts. Hence,
one has

\mathrm{K}^{1}=\mathrm{K}^{1\text{\rm b}}\oplus\mathrm{K}^{1\text{\rm f}}\,.

By Lemma
5.3
,
\{e^{\text{\rm b}}_{n},\phi_{n}^{\text{\rm b}}\}:=\{e_{2n},\phi_{2n}\}
and
\{e^{\text{\rm f}}_{n},\phi_{n}^{\text{\rm f}}\}:=\{e_{2n+1},\phi_{2n+1}\}
are the eigensystems of
\mathrm{K}^{1\text{\rm b}}
and
\mathrm{K}^{1\text{\rm f}}
respectively.

Remark 5.5
.

For
x\gg 1
the Airy function
\operatorname{Ai}
behaves as (see, e.g.,
[
1
, Sec. 10.4]
)

\operatorname{Ai}(x)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}x^{1/4}}\ e^{-\frac{2}{3}x^{3/2}}\left(1+O(x^{-3/2})\right)\,.

(5.5)

Remark 5.6
.

By
[
19
]
, one has the bounds

-\left(\frac{3\pi}{8}\,(4n+3)+\frac{3}{2}\arctan\frac{5}{18\pi(4n+3)}\ \right)^{2/3}\leq\sigma_{2n+1}\leq-\left(\frac{3\pi}{8}\,(4n+3)\right)^{2/3}\,.

Then, by
\sigma_{2n+1}<\sigma_{2n}<\sigma_{2(n-1)+1}
, one obtains

-\left(\frac{3\pi}{8}\,(4n+3)+\frac{3}{2}\arctan\frac{5}{18\pi(4n+3)}\ \right)^{2/3}\leq\sigma_{2n}\leq-\left(\frac{3\pi}{8}\,(4n-1)\right)^{2/3}.

As a first step toward the proof of Theorem
5.2
, we define an auxiliary self-adjoint operator
\mathrm{L}_{\Lambda}^{1{\natural}}
to compare with
\mathrm{L}_{\Lambda}^{\natural}
; it is defined as

\mathrm{L}^{1{\natural}}_{\Lambda}:=\Lambda^{4/3}U_{\Lambda}\mathrm{K}^{1{\natural}}U^{-1}_{\Lambda}\,,

(5.6)

where the unitary operator
U_{\Lambda}
in
L^{2}_{{\natural}}(\mathbb{R})
is defined by
\left(U_{\Lambda}f\right)(x):=\Lambda^{1/3}\,f(\Lambda^{2/3}x)
. For any
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{2}_{{\natural}}(\mathbb{R})
one has

\mathrm{L}^{1{\natural}}_{\Lambda}\psi(x)=-\psi^{\prime\prime}(x)+\Lambda^{2}|\alpha|^{3}|x|\psi(x)\,.

Notice that, by (
5.6
), the spectrum of
\Lambda^{-4/3}\mathrm{L}^{1{\natural}}_{\Lambda}
is independent of
\Lambda
; by Lemma
5.3
and Remark
5.4
, the eigensystem of
\mathrm{L}^{1{\natural}}_{\Lambda}
is given by
\{\Lambda^{4/3}e^{{\natural}}_{n},U_{\Lambda}\phi^{{\natural}}_{n}\}
.

We introduce a cut-off function on a suitable scale of
\Lambda
. Let
j\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with
0\leq j\leq 1
, even, and
j(x)=1
for
|x|\leq 1
and
j(x)=0
for
|x|\geq 2
. Define:

\mathrm{J_{1}}(x):=j(\Lambda^{1/2}x)\,,

(5.7)

and let

\mathrm{J_{0}}(x):=\sqrt{1-\mathrm{J^{2}_{1}}(x)}\qquad(\mathrm{J^{2}_{0}}+\mathrm{J^{2}_{1}}=1).

We point out that both
J_{0}
and
J_{1}
depend on
\Lambda
, to simplify the notation we omit this dependence. We also remark that in the definition of
J_{1}
, see Eq. (
5.7
), any exponent between
1/3
and
2/3
would work.

We first point out that, by the Taylor expansion of
V
, taking into account the fact that
J_{1}
is supported on
|x|\leq 2/\Lambda^{1/2}
, there follows that
\mathrm{J_{1}}\left(\mathrm{L}_{\Lambda}^{\natural}-\mathrm{L}^{1{\natural}}_{\Lambda}\right)\mathrm{J_{1}}=\mathrm{J_{1}}\left(\Lambda^{2}\left(V-|\alpha|^{3}|x|\right)\right)\mathrm{J_{1}}
is a bounded operator in
L^{2}_{{\natural}}(\mathbb{R})
and its norm satisfies the bound

\lVert\mathrm{J_{1}}\left(\mathrm{L}_{\Lambda}^{\natural}-\mathrm{L}^{1{\natural}}_{\Lambda}\right)\mathrm{J_{1}}\rVert=\lVert\mathrm{J_{1}}\left(\Lambda^{2}\left(V-|\alpha|^{3}|x|\right)\right)\mathrm{J_{1}}\rVert\leq C\lVert\mathrm{J_{1}}\left(\Lambda^{2}x^{2}\right)\mathrm{J_{1}}\rVert=O\left(\Lambda\right)\,.

(5.8)

5.1.
Lower bound

To establish a lower bound for
\ell_{\Lambda,n}^{\natural}
, we use the IMS (Ismagilov
[
20
]
, Morgan
[
26
]
, Morgan-Simon
[
27
]
) localization technique (see, for instance,
[
32
, Lemma 3.1]
or
[
11
, Chapter 11]
):

Lemma 5.7
.

Suppose that
\mathrm{L}_{\Lambda}^{\natural}
has
n+1
eigenvalues below the essential spectrum. Then

\ell_{\Lambda,n}^{\natural}\geq\Lambda^{4/3}e^{{\natural}}_{n}+O(\Lambda)\qquad\Lambda\gg 1\,.

(5.9)

Proof.

Denote
\mathrm{P}^{1{\natural}}_{\Lambda}
the orthogonal projection of the
n
-dimensional subspace spanned by the first
n
eigenvectors of
\mathrm{L}^{1{\natural}}_{\Lambda}
.
Our goal is to prove

\mathrm{L}_{\Lambda}^{\natural}\geq e^{{\natural}}_{n}\Lambda^{4/3}+{\mathrm{F}^{\natural}_{1}}+O(\Lambda)\,,

(5.10)

where
{\mathrm{F}^{\natural}_{1}}
is the symmetric operator

{\mathrm{F}^{\natural}_{1}}:=\mathrm{J_{1}}(\mathrm{L}^{1{\natural}}_{\Lambda}-e_{n}\Lambda^{4/3})\mathrm{P}^{1{\natural}}_{\Lambda}\mathrm{J_{1}}.

We remark that
{\mathrm{F}^{\natural}_{1}}
has finite rank at most equal to
n
. As usual, inequalities of the form of (
5.10
) have to be understood in the sense
(\psi,\mathrm{L}_{\Lambda}^{\natural}\psi)\geq e^{\natural}_{n}\Lambda^{4/3}\|\psi\|^{2}+(\psi,{\mathrm{F_{1}^{{\natural}}}}\psi)+O(\Lambda)
for all
\psi\in D(\mathrm{L}_{\Lambda}^{\natural})
.

Inequality (
5.10
) implies (
5.9
). This is easily seen by noticing that one can choose a normalized vector
\psi
in the span of the first
n+1
eigenvectors of
\mathrm{L}_{\Lambda}^{\natural}
such that
\psi\in\ker{\mathrm{F}^{\natural}_{1}}
. For such
\psi
we obtain

\ell^{{\natural}}_{\Lambda,n}\geq\langle\psi,\mathrm{L}_{\Lambda}^{\natural}\psi\rangle\geq e^{\natural}_{n}\Lambda^{4/3}+O(\Lambda)

which implies (
5.9
).

Let us first recall the IMS localization. Since
\mathrm{J_{0}}
and
\mathrm{J_{1}}
are smooth and
\mathrm{J_{0}}^{2}+\mathrm{J_{1}}^{2}=1
, one has

\mathrm{L}_{\Lambda}^{{\natural}}=\sum_{i=0}^{1}\mathrm{J_{i}}\mathrm{L}_{\Lambda}^{\natural}\mathrm{J_{i}}-\sum_{i=0}^{1}\left(\mathrm{J_{i}}^{\prime}\right)^{2}\,.

Therefore, we can re-write
\mathrm{L}_{\Lambda}^{\natural}
as

\mathrm{L}_{\Lambda}^{\natural}=\mathrm{J_{0}}\mathrm{L}_{\Lambda}^{\natural}\mathrm{J_{0}}+\mathrm{J_{1}}\mathrm{L}_{\Lambda}^{1{\natural}}\mathrm{J_{1}}+\mathrm{J_{1}}\left(\mathrm{L}_{\Lambda}^{\natural}-\mathrm{L}_{\Lambda}^{1{\natural}}\right)\mathrm{J_{1}}-\sum_{i=0}^{1}\left(\mathrm{J_{i}}^{\prime}\right)^{2}.

(5.11)

From the definition of
J_{0}
and
J_{1}
, there follows the bound on the operator norm:

\left\|\sum_{i=0}^{1}\left(\mathrm{J_{i}}^{\prime}\right)^{2}\right\|=O(\Lambda)\,.

(5.12)

By the definition of the orthogonal projection
P_{\Lambda}^{1{\natural}}
, there follows

\mathrm{L}^{1{\natural}}_{\Lambda}=\mathrm{L}^{1{\natural}}_{\Lambda}\mathrm{P}^{1{\natural}}_{\Lambda}+\mathrm{L}^{1{\natural}}_{\Lambda}\left(\mathrm{I}-\mathrm{P}^{1{\natural}}_{\Lambda}\right)={\mathrm{F}}^{\natural}+e^{\natural}_{n}\Lambda^{4/3}\mathrm{P}^{1{\natural}}_{\Lambda}+\mathrm{L}^{1{\natural}}_{\Lambda}\left(\mathrm{I}-\mathrm{P}^{1{\natural}}_{\Lambda}\right)\geq{\mathrm{F}}^{\natural}+e^{\natural}_{n}\Lambda^{4/3},

with

{\mathrm{F}^{\natural}}:=(\mathrm{L}^{1{\natural}}_{\Lambda}-e^{\natural}_{n}\Lambda^{4/3})\mathrm{P}^{1{\natural}}_{\Lambda}.

Hence,

\mathrm{J_{1}}\mathrm{L}^{1{\natural}}_{\Lambda}\mathrm{J_{1}}\geq{\mathrm{F}^{\natural}_{1}}+e^{\natural}_{n}\Lambda^{4/3}\mathrm{J_{1}}^{2}.

(5.13)

The latter inequality can be understood as an inequality for
\langle\psi,\mathrm{J_{1}}\mathrm{L}^{1{\natural}}_{\Lambda}\mathrm{J_{1}}\psi\rangle
for all
\psi\in D(\mathrm{L}_{\Lambda}^{\natural})
, since
\psi\in D(\mathrm{L}_{\Lambda}^{\natural})
implies
\mathrm{J_{1}}\psi\in D(\mathrm{L}_{\Lambda}^{1{\natural}})
.

Also, we need a control on the support of
\mathrm{J_{0}}
. Since
V
is even and increasing for
x>0
, by the expansion (
5.1
), one gets

V(x)\geq V(1/\Lambda^{1/2})\geq\frac{c}{\Lambda^{1/2}}\qquad\text{on $\operatorname{supp}(\mathrm{J_{0}})$},

for some positive constant
c
and
\Lambda
large enough. Hence, since
-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}\,}
is a positive definite operator, one has

\mathrm{J_{0}}\mathrm{L}^{{\natural}}_{\Lambda}\mathrm{J_{0}}\geq c\Lambda^{3/2}\left(\mathrm{J_{0}}\right)^{2}\geq e^{\natural}_{n}\Lambda^{4/3}\left(\mathrm{J_{0}}\right)^{2},

(5.14)

for
\Lambda
large enough. Taking into account (
5.11
), together with (
5.8
),(
5.12
),(
5.13
) and (
5.14
), the inequality (
5.10
) follows
∎

5.2.
Upper bound

To obtain the upper bound, we use the Rayleigh-Ritz variational principle. In this approach, we take the scaled eigenfunctions of
\mathrm{K}^{1{\natural}}
as trial wave functions for
\mathrm{L}^{1{\natural}}_{\Lambda}
.

Lemma 5.8
.

For any fixed integer
n\geq 0
and sufficiently large
\Lambda
,
\mathrm{L}_{\Lambda}^{\natural}
has at least
n+1
simple isolated eigenvalues and

\ell_{\Lambda,n}^{\natural}\leq\Lambda^{4/3}e^{\natural}_{n}+O(\Lambda).

(5.15)

Proof.

Recall that the functions
\phi^{{\natural}}_{n}
’s in (
5.3
) and (
5.4
) are the orthonormal eigenfunctions of
\mathrm{K}^{1{\natural}}
corresponding to the eigenvalues
e^{\natural}_{n}
. Therefore, the
U_{\Lambda}\phi^{{\natural}}_{n}
’s are the orthonormal eigenfunctions of
\mathrm{L}^{1{\natural}}_{\Lambda}
, that is

\mathrm{L}^{1{\natural}}_{\Lambda}\left(U_{\Lambda}\phi^{{\natural}}_{n}\right)=\Lambda^{4/3}e^{\natural}_{n}\left(U_{\Lambda}\phi^{{\natural}}_{n}\right)\,.

Defining

\psi^{{\natural}}_{n}:=\mathrm{J_{1}}U_{\Lambda}\phi^{{\natural}}_{n}

and taking into account the asymptotic behavior of the Airy functions, see Eq. (
5.5
), one has

\langle\psi^{{\natural}}_{n},\psi^{{\natural}}_{m}\rangle=\delta_{mn}+O(e^{-c\Lambda^{1/4}}),

(5.16)

for some positive constant
c
.
Furthermore, by

\mathrm{J_{1}}\mathrm{L}^{1{\natural}}_{\Lambda}\mathrm{J_{1}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathrm{J_{1}^{2}}\mathrm{L}^{1{\natural}}_{\Lambda}+\mathrm{L}^{1{\natural}}_{\Lambda}\mathrm{J_{1}^{2}}\right)+(\mathrm{J_{1}^{\prime}})^{2}\,,

by (
5.16
) and (
5.12
), one obtains

\displaystyle\langle\psi^{{\natural}}_{n},\mathrm{L}^{1{\natural}}_{\Lambda}\psi^{{\natural}}_{m}\rangle=

\displaystyle\Lambda^{4/3}\left(\frac{e^{\natural}_{n}+e^{{\natural}}_{m}}{2}\right)\langle\psi^{{\natural}}_{n},\psi^{{\natural}}_{m}\rangle+\big(U_{\Lambda}\phi^{{\natural}}_{n},\left(\mathrm{J_{1}^{\prime}}\right)^{2}U_{\Lambda}\phi^{{\natural}}_{m}\big)

(5.17)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Lambda^{4/3}e^{\natural}_{n}\delta_{nm}+O(\Lambda)\,.

Therefore, by (
5.8
),

\langle\psi^{{\natural}}_{n},\mathrm{L}_{\Lambda}^{\natural}\psi^{{\natural}}_{m}\rangle=\langle\psi^{{\natural}}_{n},\mathrm{L}^{1{\natural}}_{\Lambda}\psi^{{\natural}}_{m}\rangle+O\left(\Lambda\right)=\Lambda^{4/3}e^{\natural}_{n}\delta_{mn}+O(\Lambda)\,.

Let us fix
n\geq 0
. We want to apply the Rayleigh-Ritz variational method, however, since the vectors
\{\psi^{{\natural}}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
are not orthonormal (but almost orthonormal, see Eq. (
5.16
)), we proceed as follows. Noticing that the
\psi_{i}^{{\natural}}
’s are linearly independent, by the Gram-Schmidt algorithm we construct a set of orthonormal eigenfunctions
\{\tilde{\psi}_{i}^{{\natural}}\}_{i=0}^{n}
which, by construction, still satisfy Eq. (
5.17
), that is

\langle\tilde{\psi}^{{\natural}}_{n},\mathrm{L}^{1{\natural}}_{\Lambda}\tilde{\psi}^{{\natural}}_{m}\rangle=\Lambda^{4/3}e^{\natural}_{n}\delta_{nm}+O(\Lambda)\,.

(5.18)

Then, a direct application of the Rayleigh-Ritz variational method (see e.g.
[
29
]
, theorem XIII.3), taking as trial space the span of
\{\tilde{\psi}^{{\natural}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, gives the upper bound
\ell_{\Lambda,n}^{\natural}\leq\Lambda^{4/3}e^{\natural}_{n}+O(\Lambda)
. The fact that
\sigma_{ess}(\mathrm{L}_{\Lambda}^{\natural})\subseteq[\frac{3}{4}\alpha^{2}\Lambda^{2},+\infty)
and Eq. (
5.18
) guarantee that there are at least
n+1
eigenvalues.
∎

Our main result on the eigenvalues of
\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{eff}{\natural}}
is given in the following

Theorem 5.9
.

For any fixed integer
n\geq 0
and
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small, the
\mathscr{L}^{\mathrm{eff}{\natural}}_{\varepsilon}
has at least
n+1
simple isolated eigenvalues. The
(n+1)
-th eigenvalue is given by

\mathscr{E}^{\mathrm{eff}{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n}=s^{{\natural}}_{n}\alpha^{2}\varepsilon^{2/3}+O(\varepsilon)\,,

(5.19)

where
s^{\text{\rm b}}_{n}:=|\sigma_{2n}|
and
s_{n}^{\text{\rm f}}:=|\sigma_{2n+1}|
and the negative numbers
\sigma_{k}
are defined in Lemma
5.3
.

Proof.

Denote by
\tilde{\ell}_{\Lambda,n}^{\natural}
the eigenvalues of the self-adjoint operator (compare the following definition with Eq. (
5.2
))

\widetilde{\mathrm{L}}_{\Lambda}^{\natural}:=\Lambda^{2}\mathscr{L}^{\mathrm{eff}{\natural}}_{1/\Lambda}=\mathrm{L}_{\Lambda}^{\natural}+R.

The eigenvalues
\tilde{\ell}_{\Lambda,n}^{\natural}
satisfy the lower bound (
5.9
), because
R
is positive. Moreover, the
\tilde{\ell}_{\Lambda,n}^{\natural}
’s satisfy the upper bound (
5.15
), because in the Rayleigh-Ritz variational approach (see Eq. (
5.18
))
R
gives a contrubution of order
1
. Hence, for any fixed integer
n\geq 0
,
\widetilde{\mathrm{L}}_{\Lambda}^{\natural}
has at least
n+1
simple isolated eigenvalues, and
\tilde{\ell}_{\Lambda,n}^{\natural}=\Lambda^{4/3}e^{\natural}_{n}+O(\Lambda)
. Noticing that
\mathscr{L}^{\mathrm{eff}{\natural}}_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon^{2}\widetilde{\mathrm{L}}^{{\natural}}_{1/\varepsilon}
we obtain the expansion (
5.19
).
∎

6.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

The result in Eq. (
1.2
) about the essential spectrum is part of Theorem
1.2
. The lower bound on the spectrum in the same equation follows immediately from Theorem
3.4
and Eq. (
4.1
).

Fix
n\geq 0
. By Theorem
5.9
, one can take
\varepsilon
so small that
\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{eff}{\natural}}
has
n+1
eigenvalues,
\mathscr{E}^{\mathrm{eff}{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,0},\mathscr{E}^{\mathrm{eff}{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,1},\dots,\mathscr{E}^{\mathrm{eff}{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n}
. These are also eigenvalues for
\widehat{\mathscr{L}}^{{\natural}}_{\mathcal{P},\varepsilon}
, as a matter of facts they are the lowest eigenvalues (ordered in increasing order) of
\widehat{\mathscr{L}}^{{\natural}}_{\mathcal{P},\varepsilon}
, and of
\widehat{\mathscr{L}}^{{\natural}}_{\varepsilon}
,
see Lemma
4.5
. Choose
c_{n}>0
so that
\mathscr{E}^{\mathrm{eff}{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n-1}<\mu^{{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n}<\mathscr{E}^{\mathrm{eff}{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n}
and
\widehat{d}_{\varepsilon}(\mu^{{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n})>c_{n}\varepsilon^{2/3}
, where
\mu^{{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n}:=\mathscr{E}^{\mathrm{eff}{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n}-c_{n}\varepsilon^{2/3}
. Since
(\mu^{{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n}-\mathscr{E}^{\mathrm{eff}{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n})^{-1}
is the lowest eigenvalue of
(\mu^{{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n}-\widehat{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural})^{-1}
,

(\mu^{{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n}-\mathscr{E}^{\mathrm{eff}{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n})^{-1}=\min_{\psi\in L^{2}_{{\natural}}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),\|\psi\|=1}\big\langle\psi,(\mu^{{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n}-\widehat{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural})^{-1}\psi\big\rangle\,.

By Lemma
4.7
(i), possibly for smaller
\varepsilon
, there holds
\mu^{{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n}\in\rho(\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural})
and
d_{\varepsilon}(\mu^{{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n})>c^{\prime}_{n}\varepsilon^{2/3}
.
Let us define
\mathscr{E}^{{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n}
through the relation

(\mu^{{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n}-\mathscr{E}^{{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n})^{-1}=\inf_{\psi\in L^{2}_{{\natural}}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),\|\psi\|=1}\big\langle\psi,(\mu^{{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n}-\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural})^{-1}\psi\big\rangle\,.

By the spectral mapping theorem, either
\mathscr{E}^{{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n}
is an eigenvalue
of
\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}
or
\mathscr{E}^{{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n}=\inf\sigma_{ess}(\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural})
, where, by Theorem
1.2
,
\inf\sigma_{ess}(\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural})=\frac{3+\varepsilon^{2}}{4+\varepsilon^{2}}\,\alpha^{2}
.

By the bound on the resolvent difference in Lemma
4.7
(ii), there exists
c^{\prime\prime}_{n}>0
such that

\left|(\mathscr{E}^{{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n}-\mu^{{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n})^{-1}-(\mathscr{E}^{\mathrm{eff}{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n}-\mu^{{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n})^{-1}\right|\leq c^{\prime\prime}_{n}\,.

Therefore,

\displaystyle|\mathscr{E}^{{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n}-\mathscr{E}^{\mathrm{eff}{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n}|\leq

\displaystyle c^{\prime\prime}_{n}\,|\mathscr{E}^{{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n}-\mu^{{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n}|\,|\mathscr{E}^{\mathrm{eff}{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n}-\mu^{{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n}|\leq c^{\prime\prime}_{n}c_{n}\varepsilon^{2/3}|\mathscr{E}^{{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n}-\mathscr{E}^{\mathrm{eff}{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n}+c_{n}\varepsilon^{2/3}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{n}^{\prime\prime}c_{n}\varepsilon^{2/3}|\mathscr{E}^{{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n}-\mathscr{E}^{\mathrm{eff}{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n}|+c_{n}^{\prime\prime}c^{2}_{n}\varepsilon^{4/3}\,.

This inequality and Theorem
5.9
give

\mathscr{E}^{{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n}=\mathscr{E}^{\mathrm{eff}{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n}+O(\varepsilon^{4/3})=s_{n}^{{\natural}}\alpha^{2}\varepsilon^{2/3}+O(\varepsilon)\,.

This shows that
\mathscr{E}^{{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n}<\inf\sigma_{ess}(\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural})
whenever
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small; hence,
\mathscr{E}^{{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n}
is an eigenvalue below the essential spectrum of
\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural}
. One can repeat the argument above for any
k=0,\dots,n
, and, by the inequalities

\mathscr{E}^{\mathrm{eff}{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,k}-\tilde{c}_{k}\varepsilon^{4/3}\leq\mathscr{E}^{{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,k}\leq\mathscr{E}^{\mathrm{eff}{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,k}+\tilde{c}_{k}\varepsilon^{4/3}\qquad k=0,\dots,n

\mathscr{E}^{\mathrm{eff}{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,k-1}+\tilde{c}_{k-1}\varepsilon^{4/3}\leq\mathscr{E}^{\mathrm{eff}{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,k}-\tilde{c}_{k}\varepsilon^{4/3}\qquad k=1,\dots,n

which hold true for
\varepsilon
small enough, there follows that
\mathscr{E}^{{\natural}}_{\varepsilon,n}
is the
(n+1)
-th eigenvalue. The proof is then concluded by
\sigma_{d}(H^{\natural}_{\varepsilon})=\sigma_{d}(\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\natural})-\alpha^{2}
.
