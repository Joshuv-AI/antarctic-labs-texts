Spatial heterogeneity in the burden of respiratory syncytial virus infection attributable to low and high ambient temperature in Japan: a nationwide time-stratified case-crossover analysis
Authors: Keita Wagatsuma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRespiratory syncytial virus (RSV) infection poses a considerable disease burden worldwide. Although multiple studies have estimated the short-term effects of ambient temperature on mortality and morbidity, the burden of RSV attributable to temperature is poorly characterized. This nationwide study aimed to quantify the total burden of RSV infection in Japan attributable to non-optimum ambient temperature conditions, and to partition this burden into contributions from low and high temperatures and from moderate and extreme components of non-optimum temperature. We collected weekly time-series data on the number of reported RSV cases and meteorological variables (mean temperature, relative humidity, wind speed, and sunshine duration) for all 47 Japanese prefectures from 2006 to 2019. An extended two-stage approach was used: First, a time-stratified case-crossover design fitted with conditional quasi-Poisson regression was used to estimate prefecture-specific associations. Second, multivariate random-effects meta-analysis was used to obtain pooled estimates. The number of attributable cases were estimated for temperatures above and below the optimum minimum morbidity temperature (MMT) and further categorized as moderate or extreme using the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles as cutpoints. Data on 1,227,012 RSV cases reported during the study period were analyzed. The pooled exposure–response relationship between temperature and RSV incidence was U-shaped, with a higher RSV incidence at both low and high temperature levels. In most prefectures the MMT was between the 40th to 50th percentiles of the local temperature distribution. Overall, 21.32% (95% empirical confidence interval [eCI] 18.93% to 22.78%) of cases were attributable to non-optimum temperature, with marked geographic heterogeneity: from 8.24% (95% eCI: − 9.06–21.12%) in Akita, to 32.63% (95% eCI: 27.86–41.78%) in Kagoshima. A greater share of temperature-attributable cases was due to moderate high temperatures (12.27%, 95% eCI: 10.95–13.19%) than to low temperatures (9.05%, 95% eCI: 7.12–10.36%), whereas the contributions of extremely high and extremely low temperatures were relatively small. High weekly mean ambient temperatures over lag 0–3 weeks were associated with a substantial attributable risk of RSV infection. Introduction

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is a major respiratory pathogen that causes bronchiolitis in infants and severe pneumonia in young children [1]. Globally, RSV-associated acute lower respiratory tract infections are estimated to account for approximately 70,000 deaths among children aged under five each year and approximately 3.4 million hospitalizations [2]. RSV infections occur not only infants and young children but also in older adults, and RSV remains a substantial public health concern [3]. In temperate settings, RSV epidemics display marked seasonality, with transmission typically concentrated in the autumn and winter months (from September to February in the Northern Hemisphere). Autumn and winter peaks have consistently been reported in the United States and several European countries [4, 5]. In contrast, reports from Japan indicate a shift in epidemic timing toward summer to autumn (June to November), since 2017 [6–9]. These observations highlight the need for a refined characterization of RSV seasonality in Japan to inform effective prevention strategies.

Transmission dynamics of RSV are shaped by a range of biological and non-biological determinants. In addition to intrinsic drivers. such as host innate immunity, viral survival, and antigenic variation. extrinsic, climate-related factors are thought to modulate transmission [2]. Although the findings of individual studies vary, several broad patterns have emerged regarding meteorological influences, particularly ambient temperature [10]. For example, two systematic reviews suggest that lower temperatures are associated with increased transmission in temperate regions, whereas higher temperatures have been linked to outbreaks in tropical and subtropical settings [11, 12]. Epidemiological analyses from Europe, the United States, and mainland China have revealed an inverse association between ambient temperature and RSV incidence. However, two nationwide studies conducted in Japan found that both lower and higher temperatures were associated with increased RSV incidence, and a prefecture-level study in Fukuoka found that RSV incidence was correlated with diurnal temperature range [13–15]. Discrepancies between studies probably reflect differences in spatial scale, statistical methods, and the handling of potential confounders, and highlight the importance of conducting context-specific assessments when designing public health interventions.

Most epidemiological studies have quantified the association between short-term exposure to ambient temperature and RSV incidence using risk-ratio metrics such as relative risk (RR). These studies provide limited insight into the effect of extreme temperatures at a population level [16, 17]. The attributable fraction (AF), derived from the RR, is the estimated proportion of cases that would be prevented in the absence of exposure to a given risk factor [16, 17]. Assessing the AF is useful for estimating the potential benefit of preventive measures and for informing public health interventions and resource allocation. Nevertheless, limited data are available on the excess burden of RSV infection attributable to ambient temperature. Given the substantial global burden of RSV, quantifying the burden attributable to ambient temperature is crucial for evaluating public health interventions and control measures to reduce the number of cases, and for guiding policymakers in the equitable allocation of resources to protect vulnerable individuals from adverse temperatures. Because ambient temperature has been consistently identified as an important meteorological correlate of RSV seasonality, estimating the burden attributable to non-optimum temperature is directly relevant to climate-sensitive public health planning.

This study aimed to fill that gap by quantifying the RSV morbidity burden in Japan attributable to non-optimum weekly mean temperatures, including the relative contributions from moderate and extreme temperatures.

Materials and methods

Data collection

The study used national surveillance data collected between 2006 and 2019 in all 47 prefectures of Japan (Figure S1). Japan is located in the western Pacific region (latitude 26–43°N and longitude 127–141°E). Japan has a temperate climate, with four distinct seasons, characterized by a hot and humid summer from June to August and a cold and dry winter from December to February [18]. We collected prefecture-level weekly time-series counts of the number of laboratory-confirmed RSV cases from the Infectious Diseases Weekly Report, which is derived from the National Epidemiological Surveillance of Infectious Diseases database operated by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW), Japan [19]. The MHLW sentinel network comprises approximately 3,000 pediatric hospitals and clinics that report, the number of patients diagnosed with RSV infection to prefectural or municipal public health authorities on a weekly basis. These authorities collate submissions (approximately 60 units nationwide) and release the data electronically each week via the website. Cases were confirmed according to the MHLW criteria: either a positive result on a licensed rapid diagnostic test or laboratory confirmation, e.g., virus isolation or a rise in antibody titer in paired sera. The meteorological data were obtained from the Japan Meteorological Agency [20]. For each prefecture, we extracted hourly observations of temperature (°C), relative humidity (%), wind speed (m/s), and sunshine duration (hours) from the observation station located in the prefectural capital. Daily 24-h mean values were calculated for temperature, relative humidity, and wind speed, while daily total sunshine duration was calculated by summing hourly sunshine duration. We then computed weekly means of these daily values for the corresponding epidemiological weeks from 2006 to 2019. All data used were fully anonymized and publicly accessible; therefore ethical approval and informed consent were not required.

Statistical analysis

An extended two-stage analytical framework was used to assess the exposure–response relationship between weekly mean ambient temperature and RSV incidence over lag 0–3 weeks at both the national and prefectural levels [21]. In this study, short-term exposure refers to weekly mean temperature during the week of case reporting and the preceding three weeks. In the first stage, we used a time-stratified case-crossover design with a distributed lag non-linear model (DLNM) based on a conditional quasi-Poisson regression model to estimate the prefecture-specific association between temperature and RSV incidence [22]. We incorporated a time stratum in the model to control for seasonality and long-term time trends, which is equivalent to matching case and control weeks in the time-stratified case-crossover design by the same month within the same year. It enables control for seasonality, long-term time trends, and unmeasured time-invariant confounders, such as sex and socioeconomic status. To describe the non-linear and delayed associations, the model incorporated a DLNM using a cross-basis function for temperature and lag [23]. More specifically, we used a natural cubic B‐spline with knots positioned at the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles of the exposure–response relationship. The lag-response relationship was modeled using a natural cubic B-spline over lag 0–3 weeks, with two internal knots equally spaced on the log scale [24]. This lag period was selected to capture delayed associations between weekly meteorological conditions and reported RSV incidence, allowing for the incubation period, healthcare-seeking delay, reporting at the weekly scale, and short-term persistence of transmission. We extended the maximum lag to 5 weeks in a sensitivity analysis to assess the influence of a longer delayed association. We adjusted for several potential confounders, including relative humidity, wind speed, and sunshine duration, using a natural cubic B‐spline with three degrees of freedom. The logarithm of the weekly number of RSV cases was adjusted by including a one-week autoregressive term, defined as the 1-week lagged logarithm of weekly RSV counts, to account for short-term temporal dependence and epidemic persistence in RSV incidence [25]. After obtaining bidimensional estimates of the associations, we calculated the reduced coefficients to make them compatible for inclusion the meta-analysis in the second stage [26].

To obtain nationwide estimates, in the second stage, we used an extended multivariate meta-regression model to pool the prefecture-specific reduced coefficients obtained in the first stage [26]. The model was specified as a random-effects meta-regression with prefecture as a random-effect and the average temperature and temperature range as fixed-effects. We used a multivariate extension of the Wald test to test the combined contribution of the predictors to the variance. Residual heterogeneity was tested and then quantified using the multivariate extension of the Cochran Q test and I2 statistic. This meta-regression model was subsequently used to derive the best linear unbiased prediction (BLUP) of the overall cumulative exposure–response associations across all prefectures [21]. To avoid unstable reference values derived from the tails of the temperature distribution, we identified the minimum morbidity temperature (MMT) within the 5th–95th percentiles of each prefecture-specific weekly mean temperature distribution [27]. Specifically, the constrained MMT was selected as the temperature at which the BLUP-based lag-cumulative exposure–response curve reached its minimum within this percentile range. In this study, the constrained MMT was regarded as the optimum reference temperature. Non-optimum temperatures were defined as all weekly mean temperatures below or above the constrained MMT. The minimum morbidity temperature percentile (MMTP) was calculated as the percentile of the constrained MMT within the prefecture-specific temperature distribution. The constrained MMTs were then re-centered based on the BLUPs derived in the second stage. This re-centering set the RR to 1 at the constrained MMT, allowing RRs and AFs to be estimated relative to the optimum reference temperature within each prefecture. We computed lag-cumulative relative risks for low temperature, defined as the 2.5th percentile of the weekly mean temperature distribution, and high temperature, defined as the 97.5th percentile, using the constrained MMT as the reference.

The population AF was estimated to quantify the burden of RSV incidence attributable to non‐optimal temperatures using the constrained MMT as the reference value [16]. We calculated the attributable number (AN) of RSV cases due to non-optimum temperatures according to a previously described method [16]. Briefly, the AF and AN of each week and the corresponding lag periods (3 weeks) were calculated according to each week’s temperature and the constrained MMT. The total number of RSV cases attributed to non-optimum temperatures was then obtained by summing the ANs of all the weeks. We then calculated the total AF by dividing the total AN by the total number of RSV cases. We decomposed the burden into low- and high-temperature components by summing contributions from weeks with temperatures below or above the constrained MMT, respectively, and further partitioned each into moderate and extreme contributions. Extreme low temperature was defined as temperatures below the location-specific 2.5th percentile and extreme high temperature as temperatures above the location-specific 97.5th percentile. Moderate low temperature was defined as temperatures between the 2.5th percentile and the constrained MMT, and moderate high temperature as temperatures between the constrained MMT and the 97.5th percentile. To quantify uncertainty, we then calculated 95% empirical confidence intervals (eCIs) using Monte Carlo simulations based on the multivariate normal distribution of the BLUPs of the reduced coefficients. The constrained MMT itself was treated as a point reference value in the AF calculation.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses were carried out to assess the robustness of our results. First, we examined the influence of the constrained MMT search range by changing it from the 5th–95th percentiles to the 10th–90th percentiles of the prefecture-specific weekly mean temperature distribution. Second, we varied knot placement in the exposure–response function across three configurations: the 25th/50th/75th percentiles; the 10th/50th/90th percentiles; and the 10th/75th/90th percentiles. Third, we extended the maximum lag from 3 to 5 weeks. Fourth, we examined alternative autoregressive specifications by re-fitting models without the one-week autoregressive term and by additionally fitting models that included both one-week and two-week lagged logarithms of weekly RSV counts. We also re-fitted models with and without the one-week autoregressive term using the same analysis rows and compared model fit using the quasi-Akaike information criterion (QAIC). Fifth, we examined alternative adjustment sets for meteorological covariates, including models excluding all three meteorological covariates—relative humidity, wind speed, and sunshine duration—models excluding each covariate individually, and models including each covariate alone. Finally, we conducted an additional sensitivity analysis excluding observations from 2017 to 2019, corresponding to the period after the reported shift in RSV epidemic timing in Japan. Statistical significance was assessed using two-tailed tests, and p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant, where applicable. All analyses were conducted using R (version 4.1.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), using the “dlnm”, “mixmeta”, and “gnm” packages.

Associations between weekly mean temperature and RSV incidence over lag 0–3 weeks

The pooled cumulative exposure–response association between temperature and RSV incidence over a lag of 0–3 weeks is shown in Figure S2. Prefecture-specific cumulative exposure–response curves, together with the corresponding constrained MMTs, are shown in Figure S3 and Table S2. The histograms in Figures S2 and S3 show the distribution of weekly mean temperature observations used in the analysis. In most prefectures, the median MMTP was in the region of the 40th to 50th percentiles. Overall, the temperature-incidence curves were U-shaped, with elevated risks of RSV below and above the MMT. High-temperature-related risks ranged from an RR of 0.99 (95% CI: 0.97, 1.02) in Hokkaido to an RR of 2.90 (2.16, 3.89) in Kagoshima (Fig. 1). Although low-temperature-related risks were generally lower than high-temperature-related risks, the largest cold effect was observed in Kochi (RR: 1.92, 95% CI: 1.56, 2.38). The multivariate meta-regression revealed moderate residual heterogeneity after including temperature and temperature range as meta-predictors (I2 = 55.1%; Cochran’s Q test, p < 0.001; Table S3). Both temperature and temperature range significantly modified the temperature-incidence association in single-predictor and multivariable models, with temperature level accounting for a larger share of the between-prefecture heterogeneity than that of the temperature range.

Attributable fraction of RSV incidence from temperature

Table 2 shows the estimated AFs for total non-optimal temperature, and separately for low and high temperature, at the prefectural and national levels. Overall, 21.32% (95% eCI: 18.93–22.78%) of RSV cases were attributable to non-optimal temperature (Fig. 2). High temperatures accounted for the larger share of the burden (AF: 12.27%, 95% eCI: 10.95–13.19%) than that of low temperatures (AF: 9.05%, 95% eCI: 7.12–10.36%). Table S4 shows the decomposition of the non-optimal temperature-attributable burden into four components: extremely low, moderately low, moderately high, and extremely high temperatures. At the national level, most of the temperature-attributable risk was related to moderately high temperatures, whereas extremely low and extremely high temperatures contributed only a small fraction. Specifically, the AF for moderately high temperature was 11.19% (95% eCI: 9.94–12.08%; Table S4), whereas the AFs for extremely low and extremely high temperatures were 0.62% (95% eCI: 0.48, 0.71) and 1.10% (95% eCI: 1.00, 1.16), respectively.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses, including varying knot placement in the exposure–response relationship, extending the maximum lag weeks, and re-fitting models after removing the autoregressive term or omitting potential confounders, were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the results (Table S5). The effect estimates of total AF due to temperature were generally consistent across alternative knot placements, maximum lag periods, and covariate specifications, although estimates differed more clearly when the autoregressive term was excluded. QAIC-based model-fit diagnostics supported inclusion of the autoregressive term in the primary model; the model including this term had lower QAIC values in all 47 prefectures, with a median ΔQAIC of 82.10 (interquartile range: 44.48–102.38). In the sensitivity analysis excluding observations from 2017 to 2019, the total AF for non-optimal temperature remained broadly comparable with the main analysis, whereas the relative contributions of low and high temperatures differed. Specifically, the low-temperature AF increased and the high-temperature AF decreased after excluding 2017–2019, suggesting that the post-2017 shift in RSV epidemic timing may influence the partitioning of temperature-attributable burden into low- and high-temperature components.

Discussion

In this nationwide, time-stratified case-crossover analysis, we quantified the short-term association between temperature and RSV incidence in Japan and estimated the temperature-related attributable risk. At the national level, the results indicated that non-optimal weekly mean temperatures accounted for a substantial proportion of the RSV burden, with most of the excess incidence attributable to higher temperatures. However, this national pooled pattern should be interpreted alongside the moderate residual heterogeneity and uncertainty in some prefecture-specific estimates. Moderately high temperatures accounted for the largest share of temperature-related attributable incidence, whereas the contributions of extreme low and extreme high temperatures were comparatively small. This pattern should be interpreted in the context of both the estimated exposure–response association and the observed temperature distribution, because AFs reflect not only the magnitude of relative risk but also the frequency of exposure. Estimates at the extremes of the temperature distribution should be interpreted cautiously because observations are sparse in these ranges and spline-based estimates may be less stable. Given the weekly resolution of the data, this pattern should be interpreted as reflecting the observed weekly temperature distribution and exposure–response association, rather than the independent effects of short-lived daily extremes. These findings may inform the timing and targeting of preventive measures and support practical, regionally appropriate actions to mitigate the health effects of non-optimal temperatures in the context of ongoing climate change.

Notably, we found that most of the temperature-attributable risk of RSV was due to high temperatures, with moderately high temperatures accounting for a larger share of cases than extreme heat. Few studies have quantified the burden of RSV attributable to climatic conditions. Most previous studies on short-term temperature exposure and health risk have used risk ratios. In contrast to our findings, a time-series analysis of data from Suzhou, China found that 55.23% (95% eCI: 50.01–64.03%) of RSV hospitalizations were attributable to cold conditions, with the majority arising from moderate cold (AF: 54.62%, 95% eCI: 40.84–58.73%) [28]. These discrepancies may reflect differences in the number of study locations, climate zones, outcome definitions, exposure assessment methods, and statistical methods. Our study demonstrates that short-term exposure to temperature can account for a substantial proportion of the burden of RSV incidence.

Our findings are broadly consistent with those of previous studies on the effects of meteorological conditions on RSV incidence. Several studies have found that lower temperatures are associated with higher transmission. The negative association between temperature and RSV incidence in temperate settings is biologically plausible and is consistent with evidence from mainland China and Brazil [29, 30]. In vitro experimental evidence and studies using animal models have shown that cooler conditions can enhance viral viability and stability by altering viral surface glycoproteins and the lipid envelope, increasing the proportion of droplet nuclei, and can modify host susceptibility via physiological pathways [31–33]. By contrast, positive associations with higher temperatures have been documented in tropical and subtropical locations such as Hong Kong, Singapore, Malaysia and Colombia [34]. Consistent with our results, a national analysis of Japanese surveillance data [14, 35] identified weekly mean temperature thresholds that were strongly linked to elevated RSV incidence: < 10 °C (RR: 1.10, 95% CI: 1.08–1.12) and > 20 °C (RR: 1.13, 95% CI 1.12–1.15), whereas Yusuf et al. reported a bimodal temperature-incidence pattern across nine climatically diverse cities, with peaks at 2.0–6.0 °C and 24.0–30.0°C [14, 35].

Another plausible interpretation of our findings is that ambient meteorological conditions influence the balance of time spent indoors versus outdoors, thereby influencing opportunities for transmission. Spending prolonged time in enclosed or poorly ventilated spaces can facilitate the spread of respiratory viruses such as RSV and influenza [36, 37]. In temperate regions, increased indoor congregation during colder months can serve as a principal driver of surges in infectious diseases [36, 37]. Conversely, in subtropical settings, hot and humid summer weather often drives people into densely populated, air-conditioned environments, which may likewise favor transmission [38]. More broadly, meteorological variables such as temperature may act as proxies for other determinants of transmission, including human behavior and characteristics of the built environment. Collectively, these findings indicate that RSV transmission may be amplified under both relatively cool and relatively warm conditions. Although the mechanisms driving this complex pattern are not fully understood, our multi-location analysis demonstrates a U-shaped association between temperature and RSV incidence, offering insight into how regional thermal conditions may shape RSV epidemics in Japan. Despite geographical proximity and broadly similar climates, countries across the Western Pacific and South-East Asia, including Japan, exhibit subtle differences in the epidemiology of RSV epidemics, indicating that climate alone does not fully determine seasonal dynamics [39]. Carefully designed, multicounty and multicity studies across diverse epidemiological contexts, with long-term follow-up, are needed to disentangle these complex interactions and provide robust evidence.

We also observed pronounced spatial heterogeneity, evidenced by the I2 statistic and Cochran’s Q test, as well as prefecture-level variation in the MMT [27]. Although average temperature and temperature range significantly modified the temperature–RSV association in the second-stage meta-regression, these climate-related predictors did not fully explain the between-prefecture variation. The absence of a simple north–south or geographically continuous pattern suggests that the temperature–RSV relationship is unlikely to be determined by climate alone. This is plausible because the present design estimates short-term within-prefecture associations, whereas spatial variation in RSV transmission may also reflect differences in population structure, baseline epidemic activity, healthcare-seeking behaviour, surveillance intensity, urbanization, socioeconomic conditions, and indoor behavioural patterns. Accordingly, prefecture-level variation in the MMT should not be interpreted as evidence of climatic adaptation alone; rather, it may reflect a combination of climatic conditions, population behaviour, healthcare-seeking patterns, surveillance practices, and other contextual factors influencing RSV transmission. As these contextual factors were not available for meta-regression, we could not quantify their contribution to the residual heterogeneity. Future studies incorporating demographic, socioeconomic, healthcare, and behavioural indicators may help clarify the determinants of spatial variation in temperature-related RSV burden.

This study has several strengths. To our knowledge, it is the first study to quantify the attributable risk of RSV infection in Japan associated with both cold and hot ambient temperature conditions. First, we used case-based surveillance data, a sensitive indicator that better captures the acute, non-fatal impacts of non-optimal temperatures than mortality or hospitalization. Second, the 14-year observation window and complete spatial coverage of all 47 Japanese prefectures yielded a large sample and, consequently, strong statistical power to examine associations at both prefecture and national levels. Third, we evaluated the full temperature spectrum, encompassing effects of both heat and cold. Finally, the time-stratified case-crossover design coupled with a DLNM enabled precise estimation of the short-term and delayed effects of temperature on RSV incidence [22]. Indeed, sensitivity analyses using alternative knot placements in the exposure–response function and a longer maximum lag period yielded generally consistent estimates of the total temperature-AF.

This study also has some limitations. First, the ecological study design inherently constrains causal inference. Adjustment can only be performed for measured factors, leaving scope for unmeasured confounding and residual bias. Second, meteorological measurements from a single fixed-site monitoring station in each prefectural capital were used as proxies for prefecture-level exposure, introducing potential exposure misclassification, especially in geographically diverse prefectures with substantial within-prefecture climatic variation [40]. Such exposure misclassification is likely to be largely non-differential with respect to weekly RSV counts and may have biased the estimated associations toward the null, although the direction and magnitude of bias cannot be determined. Third, the spatial heterogeneity between prefectures is probably influenced by other factors in addition to prefecture-specific temperature and temperature range. Future studies should consider additional factors, including demographic, economic, and health-system variables as meta-predictors. Fourth, we were unable to incorporate vaccination or immunization coverage, individual-level exposure, or other key potential confounders such as age, sex, and additional environmental determinants. Although the main model adjusted for relative humidity, wind speed, and sunshine duration, residual confounding by other environmental factors, including precipitation, air pollution, and indoor environmental conditions, cannot be ruled out. Fifth, because the study was conducted only in Japan, these findings may not be generalizable to other regions. Sixth, the analysis was based on weekly data between 2006 and 2019. Extending the study period or using higher-resolution data could improve the precision of analysis of these complex associations. Finally, the analysis was based on weekly RSV surveillance and weekly mean meteorological variables. Weekly aggregation may smooth short-lived daily temperature extremes and cannot distinguish stable weekly temperatures from weeks with large day-to-day variability. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as broad population-level associations with weekly thermal conditions, not as evidence of daily temperature mechanisms. Extending the study period or using higher-resolution data could improve the precision and interpretation of these complex associations.

In conclusion, this nationwide time-stratified case-crossover study suggests that, at the national level, most of the temperature-related RSV burden in Japan is attributable to high temperatures, with the effects of extreme temperatures substantially smaller than those of moderate temperatures. Therefore, public health interventions to mitigate temperature-related RSV should intervene over a broad range of temperatures, not solely during temperatures at the extremes.
