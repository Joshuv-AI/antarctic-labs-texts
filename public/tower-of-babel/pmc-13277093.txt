Epidemiological investigation and genetic characterization of Coxiella burnetii carried by parasitic ticks in the southeastern coastal region of China
Authors: Tengwei Han, Fen Lin, Shuheng Zhou, Zhiwei Zeng, Jiaxiong Wang, Xiaoyan Yao, Shiju Hu, Kuicheng Zheng, Fangzhen Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe pathogen of Q fever, Coxiella burnetii, can persist in the environment for yearsand it can form infectious aerosols that facilitate long-distance airborne transmission of Q fever under certain conditions. Ticks are obligate hematophagous ectoparasites with a broad host range. Accumulating evidence suggests that, within natural ecosystems, ticks may contribute to the maintenance and circulation of Coxiella burnetii among wildlife and livestock, thereby representing a potential component of its transmission cycle. In recent years, cases of Q fever have been reported in Fujian Province, southeastern China. This study aimed to investigate the prevalence and genetic characteristics of Coxiella burnetii carried by parasitic ticks in coastal and inland regions of Fujian Province.MethodsOur research team conducted tick collection work in coastal and inland areas of Fujian Province from 2011 to 2023.Ticks were collected from different hosts, and species, developmental stage, and sampling location were recorded. DNA was extracted and screened for the IS1111 gene by real-time PCR. Positive samples were further tested by nested PCR targeting the groEL gene, followed by sequencing and phylogenetic analysis (neighbor-joining). Factors associated with infection were analyzed using Chi-square tests, Fisher-Freeman-Halton exact tests, and logistic regression analysis.ResultsA total of 746 tick samples were examined, representing 5 genera and 13 identified species, with additional specimens identified only to the genus level and recorded as Haemaphysalis sp. Forty-seven samples were positive for Coxiella burnetii, with an overall positivity rate of 6.30%. The infection rate in inland areas was significantly higher than that in coastal areas (10.22% and 2.60%). The dominant tick species in inland regions was Dermacentor taiwanensis, and higher infection rates were observed in Haemaphysalis Yeni (25.00%), Ixodes sinensis (25.00%), and Amblyomma testudinarium (19.35%). In coastal areas, Haemaphysalis formosensis was the dominant tick species, and Haemaphysalis hystricis showed a higher infection rate (9.84%, 6/61). Significant differences in infection rates were observed among tick genera, hosts, and regions (P < 0.05). Logistic regression showed that regional difference was the main influencing factor; ticks distributed in inland areas were 4.258 times more likely to be infected with Coxiella burnetii than those in coastal areas (P < 0.001). Phylogenetic analysis showed that the groEL sequences from both coastal and inland samples clustered with Coxiella burnetii, and sequence variation was minimally affected by regional and host differences. Three haplotypes exist in the Fujian region, with H1 being the predominant haplotype.ConclusionThe positivity rate of Coxiella burnetii in ticks from inland areas of Fujian Province was higher than that in coastal areas. Ticks parasitizing wild animals exhibit significantly higher infection rates, suggesting that ticks may participate in natural cycles in the wild. The groEL gene sequences of tick-derived Coxiella burnetii in Fujian Province were highly homologous to those from other regions.Keywords: Coxiella burnetii, Ticks, Prevalence characteristics, Fujian province, Haplotype

Introduction

Coxiella burnetii is a Gram-negative, obligate intracellular bacterium belonging to the order Legionellales, family Coxiellaceae, and genus Coxiella [1, 2]. It is the causative agent of Q fever, a globally distributed zoonotic disease [3]. C. burnetii is primarily shed in the urine, feces, milk, and birth products of infected animals. The organism is highly resistant to physical and chemical stress and can persist in the environment for years [4, 5]. Under certain conditions, it can form infectious aerosols that facilitate long-distance airborne transmission of Q fever. Owing to its strong infectivity and environmental stability, the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) classifies C. burnetii as a Category B potential bioterrorism agent [6]. Human infection mainly occurs through the inhalation of contaminated aerosols or dust particles [7, 8]. As few as 1–10 organisms are sufficient to cause infection, making C. burnetii one of the most infectious bacterial pathogens known [9]. In humans, Q fever presents as either acute or chronic disease. Acute Q fever typically manifests as a flu-like illness characterized by high fever, headache, malaise, and myalgia, whereas chronic Q fever is often associated with endocarditis and, less frequently, osteomyelitis [10]. In animals, infection is usually asymptomatic but may lead to reproductive disorders such as abortion and stillbirth [11].

Ticks are obligate hematophagous ectoparasites with a worldwide distribution and broad host range [12], and they may be an important component of the natural ecology of C. burnetii among wildlife and livestock transmission cycle [6]. Experimental evidence indicates that C. burnetii can replicate in tick gut epithelial cells and be shed in tick feces during blood feeding, thereby depositing heavily contaminated material on the host skin and potentially the surrounding environment [13]. Viable organisms have been demonstrated in shed feces, in some reports, up to 1010 organisms per gram of feces [14]. Accordingly, contaminated feces may become aerosolized as dust and represent a potential inhalational exposure route for animals and humans. Transmission of C. burnetii by arthropods, most notably ticks of species within the genera Ixodes, Dermacentor, and Hyalomma, has been reported between domestic animals [15]. However, it is worth noting that field evidence supports the rarity of tick bites leading to human infection [6]. While transstadial transmission has been demonstrated for the majority of tick species studied, evidence for transovarial transmission remains limited [13].

Surveillance studies show that tick-associated C. burnetii is geographically widespread: C. burnetii has been isolated from 40 tick species globally [16], and at least 14 tick species in China have been reported to carry or transmit the pathogen [17], and primary reports continue to document C. burnetii DNA in ticks from diverse parts of Asia. A study conducted in Xinjiang areas of China, revealed that C. burnetii was detected in 205/348 (58.91%) Dermacentor nuttalli and was detected in D. pavlovskyi for the first time [18]. Batu et al. detected five cases of positive C. burnetii in 385 samples of Haemaphysalis asiaticum collected in inner Mongolia of China [19]. Nooma et al. similarly detected C. burnetii infection in ticks in southern Thailand [20]. Another study reported that C. burnetii was found in the ticks Dermacentor marginatus (31.91%) in the Turkestan region [21]. PCR testing detected C. burnetii in 5 of the 408 tick sample pools (1.23%) originating from South Korea [22]. C. burnetii has also been found in some ticks in the European region, such as Slovakia [23]. During the molecular screening process in our study, as ticks commonly harbor Coxiella-like endosymbionts (CLE) and CLE is genetically closely related to C. burnetii, we first employed qPCR based on the highly abundant IS1111 gene sequence for high-sensitivity initial screening, followed by nested PCR targeting the groEL gene [24, 25]. Sequencing based on the groEL gene sequence is considered a reliable method for detecting C. burnetii in ticks [26, 27].

Given the intricate ecological interactions among C. burnetii and its tick vectors, understanding their distribution and infection dynamics under specific regional ecological conditions is critical for elucidating the natural transmission cycle of Q fever. Southeastern China, particularly Fujian Province, features a subtropical monsoon climate characterized by mild temperatures and abundant rainfall. The province is endowed with rich forest resources, boasting a forest coverage rate of 65.12%.The region’s diverse and lush vegetation, coupled with its mild climate, creates an optimal habitat for ticks. Ticks are recognized as the second most significant vector organisms after mosquitoes [28], and the transmission of C. burnetii by ticks represents a considerable threat to public health. However, despite sporadic Q fever cases reported in humans in this region, data on C. burnetii infection among local tick populations and their associated hosts remain extremely limited. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the infection status and epidemiological characteristics of C. burnetii in ticks from this region, to elucidate the genetic features of tick-borne C. burnetii in Fujian Province, and to understand its prevalence pattern. These findings will help improve understanding of the regional transmission risk and provide important scientific evidence for the early surveillance and prevention of Q fever.

Methods

Ethical aspects

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Center for Disease Control and Prevention (No: FJCDCNT2703-2022). All rodents and ticks were treated in accordance with the Guidelines of Regulations for the Administration of Laboratory Animals of the People’s Republic of China.

Sample collection and identification

Ningde City is located in the coastal region of Fujian Province, while Sanming City and Nanping City are situated in the inland areas of Fujian Province. The research team selected 3–4 sampling sites in both coastal and inland regions to conduct surveys and collect parasitic tick specimens from 2011 to 2023 (Fig. 1). Specimen collection employed the body surface tick inspection method, gathering ticks from the surfaces of various animals including cattle, wild rabbits, and wild rodents. Records were maintained detailing the time, location, and quantity of ticks collected. All host animals were released after surface ticks were removed. From each host animal, one tick per species and one per sex/developmental stage was retained for research purposes. The identification of tick species was confirmed by two personnel specialising in vector morphology using a stereomicroscope. The collected tick specimens were brought back to the laboratory. Using a stereomicroscope, morphological characteristics of the tick’s pseudocapitate head, palps, submandibular plate, scutellum, genital pore, anal groove, spiracles, and legs were observed. Morphological identification was conducted by consulting the tick classification key [29]. For species difficult to identify morphologically, DNA barcoding based on the COI gene was used as an auxiliary method [30]. Following identification, ticks were stored in a -80 °C freezer.

Molecular analyses

Nucleic acid extraction was carried out on a single tick each time. After washing the tick with double steaming water for 3 times, the abdomen was cut 2–3 times with a surgical blade. DNA was extracted using a bacterial genomic DNA extraction kit (Tianlong Science & Technology, Xi’an, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and stored at -20℃.

First, real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed on the extracted nucleic acid samples targeting the IS1111 gene [31, 32]. The 25 µl reaction system consists of: 12.5 µl Takara Probe PCR mix(Takara, Shiga, Japan), 1 µl upstream and downstream primer (10µM), 0.25 µl Probe(10µM), 8.25 µl double-distilled water and 2 µl DNA template. Next, nested PCR targeting the groEL gene [25] was performed on IS1111 gene-positive samples. The 25 µl reaction system consists of 12.5 µl Premix Taq™ (Premix Taq Version 2.0 plus dye; Takara), upstream and downstream primers (10µM) of 1 µl each, 8.5 µl double-distilled water and 2 µl DNA template. Positive and negative controls were set up for each PCR reaction. The primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech Company (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China), and the primers and reaction procedures are shown in Table 1.

DNA sequencing and phylogenetic analysis

5 µl PCR products were amplified by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis, and the fluorescence bands were observed by gel imaging system. The amplified products with the fluorescent band located at the 619 bp position were sent toSangon Biotech Company (Sangon Biotech) for sequencing. The obtained DNA sequences were processed using SnapGene Viewer software for sequence trimming [33]. The resulting sequences were queried against the NCBI BLAST database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) to determine the closest matches based on percentage identity [34]. After sequence in MEGA 11 software [35], multiple sequence alignment was performed using the ClustalW algorithm. A phylogenetic tree was then constructed using the neighbor-joining method, with branch support assessed by 1,000 bootstrap replicates [36, 37]. In addition, within-group sequence consistency was assessed by internal pairwise alignment in MegAlign (DNASTAR Lasergene 11.0) [38].

Constructing a haplotype network

The multiple-sequence alignment of the target groEL sequences generated in this study (after trimming and alignment) was imported into DnaSP v6 to identify polymorphic sites and haplotypes and to estimate the number of haplotypes, haplotype diversity, and nucleotide diversity [39]. Following export of the sequence data into NEXUS format, PopART v1.7 was employed to construct a haplotype network [40].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 25.0 statistical software (SPSS IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Chi-square test (χ2) and Monte Carlo estimate of the Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test (FFH exact test) were used to compare the differences in infection rate of C. burnetii among ticks in different species, genera, regions, developmental stages, sex, host species and collection seasons (spring: March-April, summer: June-August, autumn: September-November, winter: December-February). Binary logistic regression was used to analyze the influencing factors of tick infection with C. burnetii. P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Epidemiological distribution and characteristics of C. burnetii in ticks

A total of 746 tick samples were examined, representing 5 genera and 13 identified species, with additional specimens identified only to the genus level and recorded as Haemaphysalis sp., following reference [41]. The dominant tick species was Dermacentor taiwanensis, accounting for 25.33% (189/746), followed by Haemaphysalis formosensis (22.25%, 166/746) (Fig. 2). Sequencing results confirmed that 47 samples were positive for C. burnetii, with an overall positivity rate of 6.30%. Compared with other genera, Amblyomma sp. showed a higher infection rate of C. burnetii, and the differences in infection rates among genera were statistically significant (FFH exact test, P = 0.046). Amblyomma testudinarium exhibited the highest infection rate (18.75%, 6/32), followed by Ixodes sinensis (11.54%, 3/26), while Haemaphysalis formosensis showed the lowest rate (2.41%, 4/166). C. burnetii was not detected in Ixodes spinicoxalis, Haemaphysalis Iongicornis, Rhipicephalus microplus and Rhipicephalus sanguineus. The differences in infection rates among tick species were not statistically significant (FFH exact test, P = 0.061) (Fig. 3).

Among different hosts, the highest infection rate of C. burnetii was observed in ticks parasitizing wild rabbits (16.67%, 4/24), followed by ticks collected from wild boars (8.27%, 35/423). C. burnetii was also detected in ticks parasitizing domestic dogs, whereas no C. burnetii was detected in ticks collected from sheep or cattle (Fig. 3). Significant differences in infection rates were observed among ticks from different hosts (FFH exact test, P < 0.001).

Furthermore, the infection rate of ticks in inland regions was relatively high, at 10.22% (37/362), compared to 2.60%(10/384) in coastal areas. Dermacentor taiwanensis was the dominant tick species in inland regions. Higher infection rates were observed in Haemaphysalis yeni (25.00%, 2/8), Ixodes sinensis (25.00%, 3/12), and Amblyomma testudinarium (19.35%, 6/31) in inland regions. In coastal regions, Haemaphysalis formosensis was the dominant tick species, and Haemaphysalis hystricis showed a higher C. burnetii infection rate (9.84%, 6/61), and no Rhipicephalus ticks were collected in these areas. A statistically significant difference was observed in the infection rates of C. burnetii between ticks from coastal and inland regions (χ2 = 18.313, P < 0.001) (Tables 2 and 3).

Sequence comparison and phylogenetic analysis of C. burnetii based on groEL

After alignment, the groEL sequences generated in this study segregated into two major clades: 47 sequences clustered with C. burnetii, whereas 52 sequences clustered with CLEs (the nucleotide identity to the CLE reference sequences included in the analysis ranged from 87.6% to 97.6%). Within the analyzed groEL fragment, pairwise nucleotide identity among the 47 C. burnetii sequences ranged from 98.4% to 100.0%, and that among the 52 CLE sequences ranged from 77.4% to 100.0%. To reduce redundancy and improve readability of the phylogeny, sequences were summarized by sampling site, tick species, and sequence type; consequently, 18/47 C. burnetii sequences and 7/52 CLE sequences were selected as representative sequences for visualization in the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 4), while the clade assignment was based on the full set of sequences.

The phylogenetic tree indicated that C. burnetii sequences were detected in both coastal and inland sampling sites. All C. burnetii groEL sequences formed a distinct major clade (bootstrap support = 87%), clearly separated from the CLE clade; the nucleotide identity between the 47 C. burnetii groEL sequences and the CLE sequences ranged from 74.6% to 81.4%. Based on BLASTn results, 45 groEL fragments obtained from Haemaphysalis formosensis, H. hystris, H. yeni, Dermacentor taiwanensis, Ixodes ovatus, I. sinensis, and I. granulatus showed 99.47%–100% identity to the C. burnetii reference sequences included in Fig. 4 and clustered into a single group. In addition, two groEL sequences obtained from H. hystris and H. flava in coastal regions (ZR18HHY08 and ZN17HFL01) showed minor differences from the remaining C. burnetii sequences, exhibiting 98.94%–99.47% and 98.68%–99.21% identity to the C. burnetii reference sequences, respectively, and each forming a small subcluster. Overall, the C. burnetii sequences generated in this study were interspersed within a major cluster that also contained strains from diverse hosts and geographic regions worldwide, including sequences derived from humans (PX139702), ticks (MZ327921), sheep (EU888864), and Camelus dromedarius (MW057691, MW057692) (Fig. 4).

Haplotype network analysis of the groEL gene revealed three haplotypes (H1–H3) among the C. burnetii–positive tick samples (Fig. 5). The haplotype diversity index (HDI) was 0.084 and the nucleotide diversity index (NDI) was 0.00067. H1 was the predominant haplotype, accounting for the majority of sequences(45/47), and was detected across multiple tick species including D. taiwanensis, H. hystricis, A. testudinarium, and I. ovatus. Discussion

Q fever is a significant zoonotic disease caused by C. burnetii and has been reported in multiple outbreaks in China. Several surveys have detected C. burnetii DNA in ticks at relatively high positivity in certain settings [42]. Although the primary route of human infection is inhalation of contaminated aerosols/particles rather than tick bites, ticks can harbor C. burnetii and may contribute to its maintenance within enzootic cycles among animals [6, 14]. Human cases have been reported in Fujian Province [43], underscoring the need for strengthened surveillance of C. burnetii in animals, vectors, and the environment.

This study revealed that the overall infection rate of C. burnetii in ticks from Fujian Province, southeastern China, was 6.30%. This prevalence is comparable to findings from Gansu Province (11.56%, 17/147) [44] and Chuxiong City in Yunnan Province (7.29%, 14/192) [45] and slightly higher than the C. burnetii infection rate in ticks collected from rodents in Italy (4.4%) [46]. Higher infection rates have been reported in regions such as Iran (22.08%) and Jiangsu Province in China (70.5%) [47, 48]. Regional variations may stem from differences in detection targets and ecological environments. A significant difference in infection rates was observed between coastal and inland regions of Fujian Province (P < 0.001). And Binary logistic regression analysis showed that regional difference was a key influencing factor, with ticks collected from inland areas were 4.258 times more likely to be infected with C. burnetii compared with those from coastal areas. Previous studies have also reported regional variations in the infection rate of C. burnetii in ticks [49]. Based on the dataset of tick species and their distribution in China [50], H. formosensis is predominantly found in coastal regions such as Hainan, Fujian, and Taiwan, which is consistent with our findings. In this study, H. formosensis and D. taiwanensis were the dominant species in coastal and inland areas, respectively, suggesting that these ticks may serve as effective vectors of C. burnetii in their corresponding ecological regions. In contrast, H. longicornis was not collected from inland areas, and no C. burnetii–positive specimens were detected among H. longicornis from coastal sites. A meta-analysis focusing on H. longicornis [51] similarly reported extremely low infection rates of C. burnetii (0.0057%, 95% CI: 0–0.0451%), supporting our findings. Dominant tick species and variations in tick distribution may account for differences in infection rates across regions [26]. Some research has demonstrated a complex and inseparable relationship between tick-borne diseases and climatic factors, which can directly or indirectly influence tick vectors, their habitats, host availability and abundance, feeding intervals, and pathogen transmission [52, 53]. Therefore, the observed difference in infection rates between coastal and inland regions may be driven by climatic variations, warranting further investigation.

In this study, Statistically significant differences were observed in the infection rates of C. burnetii across different tick genera (P < 0.05), a finding also documented in the research by Mbiri et al. [49].

And a higher positivity rate of C. burnetii was observed in ticks collected from wild rabbits (16.67%, 4/24) and wild boars (8.27%, 35/423). A previous investigation on C. burnetii in wildlife [54] reported that 40% (24/60) of ticks collected from wild rabbits tested positive. Numerous studies have documented C. burnetii infections in wild boars in various countries. For example, a study conducted in South Korea [55] reported a positivity rate of 14.6% (142/975) in wild boars. Wild boars’ ranges may overlap with human activity areas, potentially playing a role in pathogen dispersal. Overall, ticks parasitizing wild animals exhibited relatively high infection rates. Ticks undergo four developmental stages—egg, larva, nymph, and adult—and all parasitic stages require blood feeding. Larvae and nymphs remain attached to the host for a shorter feeding duration than adults, and C. burnetii can be transmitted transstadially from larvae to nymphs and from nymphs to adults [56]. Thus, adult ticks have a greater likelihood of acquiring C. burnetii infection [57], consistent with the findings of this study. We observed no significant difference in infection rates between female and male ticks, consistent with previous research findings [49, 58]. However, this differs from Knap’s findings, which did not detect C. burnetii in male ticks in Slovakia [59]. The disparity in infection rates between female and male adult ticks varies across different regions, potentially related to factors such as the feeding characteristics of different tick species and the biological properties of C. burnetii [60]. Fujian Province is located in a subtropical region, with a mild climate throughout the year, and the infection rate of C. burnetii in ticks is comparable across all four seasons.

Ticks frequently harbor CLE, which are phylogenetically related to C. burnetii and may complicate Coxiella detection if confirmatory sequencing is not performed [24, 61]. The groEL gene test is widely employed in molecular studies of C. burnetii, as sequencing and phylogenetic positioning provide interpretable methods that support distinguishing CLE from C. burnetii beyond a single screening signal [13, 26, 62, 63]. This study investigated the genetic characteristics and phylogenetic relationships of C. burnetii detected in ticks based on the groEL gene. In this study, the extremely high pairwise nucleotide identity (98.4%–100.0%) amongst these 47 sequences indicates low variability within the groEL fragment region, yet overall divergence from the CLE sequences included in the analysis. The two tick sample sequences from this study (ZR18HHY08, ZN17HFL01) formed minor branches on the tree but remained stably assigned to the main C. burnetii lineage, suggesting their differences likely stem from minor variations at localized sites. In a tick screening study based on the groEL gene, Ali et al. also observed that certain groEL fragments showed high sequence identity with the C. burnetii reference sequence, and grouped C. burnetii and CLE into separate clusters on the phylogenetic tree [25]. The groEL sequences from inland and coastal samples in Fujian did not form distinct clusters according to geographic origin in the phylogenetic tree. These strains share highly conserved groEL gene sequences with isolates from Eurasia and even the Middle East, and cluster together with human-derived C. burnetii. This suggesting a potential risk of entering the human infection chain. Follow-up efforts should strengthen the coordination between animal-vector surveillance. Methodologically, our two-step strategy aligns with conventional diagnostic logic: qPCR targeting the IS1111 gene is commonly employed for high-sensitivity screening, as the IS1111 gene fragment constitutes a multi-copy element [64]. Meanwhile, IS1111 gene signal may exist in cross-references within tick-associated Coxiella phylogenies, therefore combining high-sensitivity detection with SNP genotyping methods provides a safeguard mechanism for the identification (and genotyping) of C. burnetii, enabling the avoidance of false-positive risks when processing tick samples [62]. Against this backdrop, incorporating sequencing based on the groEL gene in this study provides independent sequence validation. Existing literature explicitly identifies groEL as one of the sites utilised for gene sequence identification in tick research [26]. More broadly, multi-target designs (such as combining IS1111 with icd and 16 S rRNA) are frequently employed to enhance the reliability of molecular identification [65, 66]. Consequently, subsequent studies may strengthen inferences by incorporating additional sites (such as icd and 16 S rRNA) or employing multi-gene confirmation protocols for subsets of positive samples [13, 64, 67].

Haplotype networks are widely used to visualize genealogical relationships among closely related sequences and to summarize how common and rare variants are distributed across groups or locations [40, 68, 69]. Importantly, haplotype network analysis remains informative even at moderate sample sizes, because it directly displays whether diversity is dominated by one common type or split among multiple competing types [70, 71]. In our groEL dataset, the distribution of haplotypes is consistent with that of the phylogenetic tree. The haplotype network identified only three haplotypes (H1–H3) among 47 C. burnetii–positive tick sequences, with a very low haplotype diversity index (HDI = 0.084) and nucleotide diversity index (NDI = 0.00067). Because haplotype diversity reflects allelic differences and nucleotide diversity represents the average number of nucleotide differences among sequences, these values indicate that variation within the analyzed groEL fragment is minimal [72]. H1 dominated and occurred across multiple tick species (D. taiwanensis, H. hystricis, A. testudinarium, H. longicornis, and I. ovatus), whereas H2 and H3 were each singletons restricted to one tick species, suggesting that a single prevalent lineage is widespread while only sporadic micro-variants are present. This pattern is consistent with the notion that C. burnetii is a clonal pathogen with low nucleotide-level diversity, and that outbreak-associated or host-associated collections may show a single dominant genotype with only limited diversification [73, 74].

Conclusions

This study revealed that the prevalence of C. burnetii in ticks from inland areas of Fujian Province was higher than that in coastal regions, with distinct dominant tick species observed between these habitats. Ticks parasitizing wild animals exhibit significantly higher infection rates, suggesting that ticks may participate in natural cycles in the wild. The groEL gene sequences of C. burnetii detected in Fujian exhibit high genetic homogeneity, with three distinct haplotypes identified. The majority of these belong to haplotype H1, indicating limited genetic differentiation among circulating strains. This work provides the first comprehensive evidence of C. burnetii infection and genetic characteristics in ticks from the southeastern coastal region of China, offering valuable insights for understanding its ecological transmission and for guiding regional surveillance and control strategies.
