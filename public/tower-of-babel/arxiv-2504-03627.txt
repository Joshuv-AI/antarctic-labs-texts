Richardson’s model and the contact process with stirring: long time behavior.


Abstract




Richardson’s model and the contact process with stirring: long time behavior

Régine Marchand, Irène Marcovici and Pierrick Siest

Université de Lorraine, IECL, 54000 Nancy

Université de Rouen Normandie, LMRS, 76801 Saint-Étienne-du-Rouvray

E-mail address:
regine.marchand@univ-lorraine.fr, irene.marcovici@univ-rouen.fr, pierrick.siest@univ-lorraine.fr

Abstract.
We study two famous interacting particle systems, the so-called Richardson’s model and the contact process, when we add a stirring dynamics to them. We prove that they both satisfy an asymptotic shape theorem, as their analogues without stirring, but only for high enough infection rates, using couplings and restart techniques. We also show that for Richardson’s model with stirring, for high enough infection rates, each site is forever infected after a certain time almost surely. Finally, we study weak and strong survival for both models on a homogeneous infinite tree, and show that there are two phase transitions for certain values of the parameters and the dimension, which is a result similar to what is proved for the contact process.

1
Introduction

In 1973, Richardson introduced the so-called
Richardson’s model

[
26
]
, which can be seen as the simplest interacting particle system on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
to model the evolution over time of the spread of an epidemic: each infected site infects its neighbors at a fixed rate
\lambda>0
. In 1974, Harris
[
15
]
introduced the
contact process
, which is obtained by adding a healing dynamics to Richardson’s model: each infected site heals at rate
1
. The contact process is a non-permanent model, that is, the epidemic can die out. A natural question is then: for which infection rates
\lambda
has the epidemic a positive probability to spread forever? Harris
[
15
]
showed that, in all dimensions
d
, the contact process exhibits a phase transition. This means that there exists a constant
\lambda_{c}^{\mathrm{CP}}(d)\in(0,+\infty)
, called the
critical parameter
, such that the probability, starting from a single infected point, that the epidemic spreads forever is continuous in
\lambda
, and positive if and only if
\lambda>\lambda_{c}(d)
.

The aim of this paper is to obtain various results on Richardson’s model and the contact process, when we add a stirring dynamics to them: two neighboring sites exchange their states at a fixed rate
\nu>0
. When these models are considered to model the propagation of an epidemic, stirring represents the movements of individuals. Durrett and Neuhauser
[
10
]
initiated the study of this kind of models in the nineties: they worked on the phase transition of some interacting particle systems in which the particles are stirred at a fast rate. Katori
[
16
]
and Konno
[
17
]
obtained a more precise description of the asymptotic behavior of the critical parameter
\lambda_{c}(\nu)
, seen as a function of the stirring rate
\nu
. More recently, some improvements were made on the same question, see Berezin and Mytnik
[
1
]
, Levit and Valesin
[
19
]
, and Mytnik and Shlomov
[
23
]
. All these results are about the asymptotic behavior of the critical parameter
\lambda_{c}(\nu)
. Here we are interested in growth properties of Richardson’s model and the contact process with stirring, as detailed below.

For Richardson’s model, as well as for the contact process when the epidemic spreads forever, determining the asymptotic behavior of the set of the (once) infected sites is a natural question. One of the most famous results for this type of question was obtained by Cox and Durrett
[
4
]
for first-passage percolation on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
: they proved an
asymptotic shape theorem
for the set of sites reached before time
t
, when
t
goes to infinity.
Asymptotic shape theorems are proved, by Richardson
[
26
]
for Richardson’s model, and by Durrett–Griffeath
[
8
]
and Bezuidenhout–Grimmett
[
2
]
for the contact process, for the set of (once) infected sites, using ergodic subadditivity theory. Since then, a lot of variations of the contact process were studied. Some examples are the
contact process in random environment
, introduced by Bramson, Durrett and Schonmann
[
3
]
, the
boundary modified contact process
, (Durrett and Schinazi
[
11
]
), the
contact process in random environment
(Garet and Marchand
[
12
]
), the
contact process with aging
(Deshayes
[
5
]
), or more recently the
contact process in an evolving random environment
(Seiler and Sturm
[
27
]
). For each of these models, an asymptotic shape theorem is proved or conjectured. Here we prove an asymptotic shape theorem for Richardson’s model and contact process with stirring, for large enough infection rates. We prove it by showing that these two models satisfy some linear growth properties. This allows us to use Deshayes and Siest’s
[
6
]
result: they proved that a class of random linear growth models, which also contains some of the other models mentioned in this paragraph, satisfies an asymptotic shape theorem.

We also worked on the question of
fixation
: does a site stay infected forever after a certain time? It is not obvious for Richardson’s model with stirring, since an infected site can be healed if it exchanges its state with a healthy neighbor. But if an infected site is surrounded by other infected sites, it is possible that it cannot get healed by an exchange after a certain time, once the epidemic developed sufficiently. We prove that for large enough infection rates, each site fixes on the infected state forever, after a certain time.

Concerning the contact process with stirring, a site cannot be infected forever, since it recovers at rate
1
, independently of the rest of the configuration. However, it can be healthy forever after a certain time, even if the epidemic survives. This question is linked to the notions of
weak survival
and
strong survival
. We say that there is weak survival if the process survives with positive probability, and that there is strong survival if the set of times
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that the origin is infected at time
t
is infinite, with positive probability. Since the state
\emptyset
is absorbing, strong survival clearly implies weak survival. If there is weak survival but not strong survival, then almost surely each site is healthy forever after a certain time, despite positive probability of the presence of infected sites at any time: the set of (actually) infected sites "goes away" from any finite box over time.

For the contact process on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, it is known (see
[
20
]
) that once the epidemic has a positive probability to spread forever, there is strong survival. On a homogeneous tree, that is, an infinite tree in which all the vertices have the same degree, the behavior of the contact process is very different: Pemantle
[
25
]
and Liggett
[
21
]
proved that for certain infection rates, there is weak survival, but not strong survival, for the contact process on a homogeneous tree of degree
d\geq 3
. Note that Stacey
[
28
]
also proved that there are two phase transitions for all degrees
d\geq 3
, but he did not use bounds on critical parameters in his proof. We prove a similar result for Richardson’s model and contact process with stirring, strongly inspired by Liggett’s techniques (see
[
22
]
), and we obtain explicit bounds for the critical parameter of the strong survival for both the RMS and the CPS.

In Section
2
, we define Richardson’s model with stirring and the contact process with stirring. In Section
3
, we state the results we prove for these two models. In Sections
4
to
6
, we prove our results. In Section
7
, we discuss our results and propose some open questions.

2
Definitions of the models

All the processes that will be studied or used in this paper are nearest neighbor interacting particle systems on the graph
(\mathbb{Z}^{d},\mathbb{E}^{d})
, where
d\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
\mathbb{E}^{d}:=\{(x,y)\in(\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{2}:\sum_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}-y_{i}|=1\}
is the set of oriented edges between nearest neighbors of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, or on a homogeneous tree
T_{d}
of degree
d+1
. We give only the definitions on
(\mathbb{Z}^{d},\mathbb{E}^{d})
: they translate straightforwardly on
T_{d}
. The set
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
is called the set of
configurations
: vertices (which we call
sites
in the rest of the paper) with value
1
are called
infected
, and the others are called
healthy
.
Since the map

\begin{array}[]{cccc}&\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}&\to&\mathbb{Z}^{d}\\
&\omega&\mapsto&\{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}~:~\omega(x)=1\}\\
\end{array}

is bijective, then we can see a configuration as a subset of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
containing the infected sites.

For
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
\eta\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
, we denote by:

•

\eta_{x}
the configuration identical to
\eta
, except for
\eta(x)
which is replaced by
1-\eta(x)
(the state of site
x
is flipped).

•

\eta_{x,y}
the configuration identical to
\eta
, except for
\eta(x)
and
\eta(y)
, which are replaced respectively by
\eta(y)
and
\eta(x)
(we exchange the states of sites
x
and
y
). Note that
\eta_{x,y}=\eta_{y,x}
.

Let
\lambda,\gamma
and
\nu
be positive real numbers. Three interactions can occur in our models: the infection of a healthy site, the healing of an infected site, and the stirring, which corresponds to the exchange of states of two neighboring sites. We thus define three different infinitesimal generators, associated with each interaction.

We start by the generator
\mathcal{L}^{I}
, which describes the infections, and appears in all the models. We denote by
\mathcal{C}_{0}(\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}})
the set of continuous functions from
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
to
\mathbb{R}
which depend only on finitely many coordinates of the configuration. A site becomes infected at a rate equal to the number of its infected neighbors. For all
f\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}})
and every configuration
\eta\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
, we set:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{I}f(\eta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\left[\mathds{1}_{\{\eta(x)=0\}}\sum_{y\sim x}\eta(y)\right]\left[f(\eta_{x})-f(\eta)\right].

The second generator
\mathcal{L}^{H}
describes the healing dynamics, and appears in the contact processes. A site becomes healthy at rate
1
. For all
f\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}})
and every configuration
\eta\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
, we set:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{H}f(\eta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathds{1}_{\{\eta(x)=1\}}\left[f(\eta_{x})-f(\eta)\right].

The third generator
\mathcal{L}^{S}
describes the stirring dynamics. Two neighboring sites exchange their states at rate
1
. Note that the exchange modifies the configuration only if one site is infected and the other is healthy. For all
f\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}})
and every configuration
\eta\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
, we set:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{S}f(\eta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{e=(x,y)\in\mathbb{E}^{d}}\mathds{1}_{\{\eta(x)=1,\eta(y)=0\}}\left[f(\eta_{x,y})-f(\eta)\right]

(1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{e=\{x,y\}\in\mathbb{E}^{d}}\mathds{1}_{\{\eta(x)\neq\eta(y)\}}\left[f(\eta_{x,y})-f(\eta)\right].

Now we define the models that will be used in this paper. The first one is
Richardson’s model
with
infection rate

\lambda
(denoted by
\mathrm{RM}(\lambda)
). In this model, there are only infections. Richardson’s model is a Markov process with generator
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{RM}}_{\lambda}
, given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{RM}}_{\lambda}=\lambda\mathcal{L}^{I}.

The
contact process
with infection rate
\lambda
and
healing rate

\gamma
(denoted by
\mathrm{CP}(\lambda,\gamma)
) is an extension of Richardson’s model, to which we add a healing dynamics. The contact process is a Markov process with generator
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{CP}}_{\lambda,\gamma}
, given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{CP}}_{\lambda,\gamma}=\lambda\mathcal{L}^{I}+\gamma\mathcal{L}^{H}.

Richardson’s model with stirring
with infection rate
\lambda
and
stirring rate

\nu
(denoted by
\mathrm{RMS}(\lambda,\nu)
) is an extension of Richardson’s model, to which we add a stirring dynamics. Richardson’s model with stirring is a Markov process with generator
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{RMS}}_{\lambda,\nu}
, given by:

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{RMS}}_{\lambda,\nu}

\displaystyle=\lambda\mathcal{L}^{I}+\nu\mathcal{L}^{S}.

The
contact process with stirring
with infection rate
\lambda
, healing rate
\gamma
, and stirring rate
\nu
(denoted by
\mathrm{CPS}(\lambda,\gamma,\nu)
), is an extension of the contact process, to which we add a stirring dynamics. We can also see it as an extension of Richardson’s model with stirring, to which we add a healing dynamics. All the three interactions can happen: infection, healing and stirring. The contact process with stirring is a Markov process with generator
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{CPS}}_{\lambda,\gamma,\nu}
, given by:

(3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{CPS}}_{\lambda,\gamma,\nu}=\lambda\mathcal{L}^{I}+\gamma\mathcal{L}^{H}+\nu\mathcal{L}^{S}.

Richardson’s model with stirring and the contact process with stirring are the two models on which we obtain new results. Note that, by a rescaling of time, we can restrict ourselves to the study of the
\mathrm{RMS}(\lambda,1)
and the
\mathrm{CPS}(\lambda,1,\nu)
. Note also that the existence of these processes comes from Theorem B3 of Liggett
[
22
]
. They are all Feller Markov processes, therefore they verify the strong Markov property.

Results

3.1
Asymptotic shape theorem

We will see in Subsection
4.1
that we can define our models with a graphical construction. As a direct consequence of this construction, we will obtain the following properties:

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a
\mathrm{RMS}
or a
\mathrm{CPS}
.

1.

The law of the process is invariant by the translation
T_{x}
, for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

2.

The process is
additive
, that is: there exists a coupling such that, for all subsets
A\subset B
of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, the processes
(\xi_{t}^{A})_{t\geq 0}
and
(\xi_{t}^{B})_{t\geq 0}
verify, for all
t\geq 0
,

\xi_{t}^{A\cup B}=\xi_{t}^{A}\cup\xi_{t}^{B}.

3.

The empty set is an absorbing state, that is: if
\xi_{t_{0}}=\emptyset
, then for all
t>t_{0}
, we have
\xi_{t}=\emptyset
.

We are interested in the asymptotic behavior of the set of once infected sites: Figure
1
shows some simulations of the RMS and the CPS. We define, for all
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, the
lifetime of the process

(\xi_{t}^{x})_{t\geq 0}
:

\tau^{x}=\inf\{t\geq 0~:~\xi_{t}^{x}=\emptyset\},

and the
hitting time of the site

y
by the process
(\xi_{t}^{x})_{t\geq 0}
:

t^{x}(y)=\inf\{t\geq 0~:~y\in\xi_{t}^{x}\}.

When
x=0
, we simply write
\tau
and
t(y)
. When
\mathbb{P}(\tau=+\infty)>0
, we say that the process
survives with positive probability
. Durrett
[
7
]
proved in 1980 a one-dimensional version of an asymptotic shape theorem for this set, in the supercritical contact process. His result also holds for a larger class of models, which he calls growth models. By Lemma
3.1
, both the RMS and the CPS are growth models in the sense of Durrett. Therefore, in dimension one we directly obtain the following asymptotic shape theorems, in the entire supercritical region:

.

Suppose that

. We set
r_{t}=\sup\{x\in\mathbb{Z}:x\in\xi_{t}\}

1.

Let
\lambda>0

(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a
\mathrm{RMS}(\lambda,1)

\alpha>0
such that:

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{r_{t}}{t}=-\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{l_{t}}{t}=\alpha\quad\text{a.s.}

2.

Let
\nu>0
,
\lambda>\lambda_{c}^{\mathrm{CPS}}(1,\nu)
and
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a
\mathrm{CPS}(\lambda,1,\nu)
. There exists
\alpha>0
such that:

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{r_{t}}{t}=-\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{l_{t}}{t}=\alpha\quad\text{a.s. on }\{\tau=+\infty\}.

(a)

\mathrm{RMS}(1,1)
.

(b)

\mathrm{RMS}(0.2,1)
.

(c)

\mathrm{CPS}(2,1,10)
.

(d)

\mathrm{CPS}(2,1,1)
.

In higher dimensions, a shape theorem is harder to obtain. We want to apply Deshayes and Siest’s
[
6
]

\mathcal{C}
, is a class of what they called growth models, and is similar to the class of growth models Durrett
[

defined. By Lemma

, both the RMS and the CPS are in class

. The second class, the class
\mathcal{C}_{L}
, is a class of models which have linear growth properties.
In our context of invariance by spatial translations of the law of the RMS and the CPS, these two models are in class
\mathcal{C}_{L}
if there exist
C_{1},C_{2},M_{1},M_{2}>0
such that for all
t>0
and
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,

(4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tau=+\infty)

\displaystyle>0,

(5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\exists y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:t(y)\leq t\text{ and }\|y\|\geq M_{1}t\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\exp(-C_{2}t),

(6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(t<\tau<+\infty\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\exp(-C_{2}t),

(7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(t(x)\geq M_{2}\|x\|+t,\tau=+\infty\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\exp(-C_{2}t),

where
||x||=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|

) means that the process survives with positive probability, when it starts from the initial configuration
\xi_{0}=\{0\}

) (resp. (

Therefore, the epidemic starting from a singleton in the graphical construction is either a small finite connected component, or an infinite connected component, with high probability.

\overline{\mathrm{CP}}\subset\mathrm{RMS}\subset\mathrm{RM},

seeing the models as the set of their infected sites, in order to prove (

), (
5
), (
6
) and (

) for the RMS. We do the same coupling to prove these inequalities for the CPS (only the parameters of the CP and the RM change). These couplings will be given in Lemma

is supercritical, which is the case only for infection rates

[

in 1990.

For a norm

3.2
Fixation of the sites in the RMS

Among the models we study here, the RMS has a particularity: there is no direct healing as in the contact processes, but an infected site can heal if it exchanges its state with a healthy neighbor. It is a big difference since an infected site has to have healthy neighbors to heal, whereas in the contact processes a site can always heal, no matter the neighboring. Therefore, the question of fixation of sites naturally arises: does each site stay infected forever after a sufficiently long time, like in Richardson’s model? We prove a result for all infection rates in dimension
1
and
2
, and for
\lambda
large enough in higher dimensions. In high dimensions with low infection rates, we have a weaker result.

We say that a site
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}

fixes on the infected state
(resp.
fixes on the healthy state
) if:

\mathbb{P}(\exists T>0,~\forall t\geq T,~x\in\xi_{t})=1

\text{(resp. }\mathbb{P}(\exists T>0,~\forall t\geq T,~x\notin\xi_{t})=1).

We prove the following result:

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\lambda>0
and
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be
\mathrm{RMS}(\lambda,1)
starting from a non-empty initial configuration.

1.

All sites fix on the infected state or all sites fix on the healthy state.

2.

Suppose that
d\in\{1,2\}
. Then all sites fix on the infected state.

3.

Suppose that
d\geq 3
and
\lambda>2d\lambda_{c}(d)
. Then all sites fix on the infected state.

3.3
Weak survival and strong survival on a homogeneous tree

Here we study two different notions of survival for the contact process starting from the initial configuration
\{0\}
. The first one, which we call
weak survival
, is what we call "to survive with positive probability" in the other sections: the process
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
survives
weakly
if:

\mathbb{P}(\forall t\geq 0,~\xi_{t}\neq\emptyset)>0.

We also define a stronger version, which imposes that the set of infected sites does not "go away" from any finite box. We say that the process survives
strongly
if:

\mathbb{P}(\forall T\geq 0,~\exists t>T,~0\in\xi_{t})>0.

Since the configuration
\emptyset
is absorbing, strong survival implies weak survival.

On
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, when they survive weakly, both RM and CP also survive strongly: there is only one phase transition. It is obvious for RM, but for CP it is not easy to prove: it uses Bezuidenhout and Grimmett’s block construction
[
2
]
, a proof can be found in Liggett’s book
[
22
]
. On an infinite homogeneous tree
T_{d}
, that is, an infinite tree having with all vertices having degree
d+1
, Pemantle
[
25
]
proved that the contact process exhibits two phase transitions: there exist infection rates
\lambda
such that
\mathrm{CP}(\lambda,1)
survives weakly, but not strongly. Note that
\mathrm{RMS}(\lambda,1)
survives weakly for all
\lambda>0
, since
(\mathrm{Card}(\xi_{t}))_{t\geq 0}
is non-decreasing for this model, but there is no reason that it survives strongly for all
\lambda>0
. We prove that there are also two phase transitions for the RMS and for the CPS, for a set of couples
(d,\nu)
of dimension and stirring rate, on
T_{d}
:

Theorem 3.6
.

1.

Let
\lambda>0
and
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be
\mathrm{RMS}(\lambda,1)
on the homogeneous tree
T_{d}
. There exists
\lambda_{s}^{\mathrm{RMS}}(d)>0
such that:

4.1
Graphical construction

Both Richardson’s model and the contact process have a graphical construction with Poisson point processes on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
. It can be used to obtain a graphical construction, which allows us to use percolation techniques, see Harris
[
14
]
. We extend this kind of construction to the
\mathrm{RMS}(\lambda,1)
and the
\mathrm{CPS}(\lambda,1,\nu)
.

We endow
\mathbb{R}^{+}
with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathfrak{B}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
, and we denote by
M
the set of locally finite counting measures
m=\sum_{i=1}^{+\infty}\delta_{t_{i}}
on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
, which can be seen as sequences of jump times. We endow
M
with the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{M}
generated by the maps
m\mapsto m(B)
, with
B\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
. We define the measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
by

(10)

\displaystyle(\Omega,\mathcal{F})=(M^{\mathbb{E}^{d}}\times M^{\mathbb{E}^{d}}\times M^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}},\mathcal{M}^{\otimes\mathbb{E}^{d}}\otimes\mathcal{M}^{\otimes\mathbb{E}^{d}}\otimes\mathcal{M}^{\otimes\mathbb{Z}^{d}}).

Remember that
\mathbb{E}^{d}
is the set of
oriented
edges between nearest neighbors of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. On this measurable space, we define the probability measures

\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{RMS}(\lambda,1)}=\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}^{\otimes\mathbb{E}^{d}}\otimes\mathcal{N}_{1}^{\otimes\mathbb{E}^{d}}\otimes\delta_{\emptyset}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{CPS}(\lambda,1,\nu)}=\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}^{\otimes\mathbb{E}^{d}}\otimes\mathcal{N}_{1}^{\otimes\mathbb{E}^{d}}\otimes\mathcal{N}_{\nu}^{\otimes\mathbb{Z}^{d}},

where for all
\mu>0
,
\mathcal{N}_{\mu}
is the law of a Poisson process of intensity
\mu
, and
\delta_{\emptyset}
is the counting measure associated with an empty set of jump times. The subscripts in
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{RMS}(\lambda,1)}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{CPS}(\lambda,1,\nu)}
will often be removed when the choice of the model we study and the values of the parameters are clear.

We extend Harris’ graphical construction
[
14
]
to our models: we provide an informal description of it, see Figure
2
to visualize. We only present the case of the CPS, since the construction for the RMS is the same, except that there is no healing. Let
\omega=((\omega^{I}_{e})_{e\in\mathbb{E}^{d}},(\omega^{S}_{e})_{e\in\mathbb{E}^{d}},(\omega^{H}_{z})_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}})\in\Omega
. We draw a time line
\mathbb{R}^{+}
above each site. On top of each site
z
, we draw squares at times given by
\omega^{H}_{z}
, which correspond to healing times. On top of each edge
e
, we draw horizontal arrows between the two endpoints of
e
, with the same orientation as the edge
e
, at times given by
\omega^{I}_{e}
and
\omega^{S}_{e}
. Each of these arrows has a mark:
I
for a time given by
\omega^{I}_{e}
(infection time), and
S
for a time given by
\omega^{S}_{e}
(stirring time).

We define
open paths
on a configuration. An open path follows the time lines, using the horizontal arrows to jump from one time line to another, with the following constraints:

•

the path cannot go through a square,

•

the path can follow a horizontal arrow with a I mark (that is, an infection time) when it encounters one, but it can also ignore it and stay on the same time line,

•

when the path encounters a horizontal arrow with a S mark (that is, a stirring time), then it is forced to follow it if the endpoint of the arrow is healthy at this time, and cannot follow it if the endpoint is infected.

Note that the first two constraints are exactly those of an open path in the graphical construction of a contact process. Now, for any
A\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, we define a process
(\xi^{A}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
as follows: we fix
\xi_{0}=A
, and for all
t>0
,
y\in\xi_{t}^{A}
if and only if there exists
x\in A
such that there is an open path from
(x,0)
to
(y,t)
. A direct extension of Harris’s result
[
14
]
proves that for any
A\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, the process
(\xi_{t}^{A})_{t\geq 0}
is a well-defined Markov process with a generator
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{CPS}}_{\lambda,1,\nu}
, see (
3
), and initial configuration
\xi_{0}^{A}=A
.
Note that our exchanges are oriented: this will be convenient for coupling models with stirring and models without stirring.

Finally, to prove the additivity property (see Lemma
3.1
) for our models, we modify this graphical construction by changing the constraint for the stirring arrows: these arrows become non-oriented edges, and a path can always follow a stirring edge. The process obtained with this construction has the law of the same contact process: see the definition of the generator (
1
).

5.1
Proof of an isoperimetric inequality

Let
\lambda>0
and
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a
\mathrm{RMS}(\lambda,1)
. For all
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, we denote by
v(x)
the set of neighboring sites of
x
. We define, for all
1\leq i\leq 2d
,

\mathrm{Fr}_{i}(\xi_{t}):=\{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}~:~x\notin\xi_{t}\text{ and }\mathrm{Card}\left(v(x)\cap\xi_{t}\right)=i\},

the set of healthy sites that have exactly
i
neighbors infected at time
t
, and

\mathrm{Fr}(\xi_{t}):=\bigcup_{1\leq i\leq 2d}\mathrm{Fr}_{i}(\xi_{t})

the set of healthy sites that have at least one infected neighbor, which is also the set of healthy sites that can be infected at time
t
. A healthy site that has exactly
i
infected neighbors is infected at rate
i\lambda
. At time
t
, a new infection arises at rate
I(\xi_{t})\lambda
, where

I(\xi_{t}):=\sum_{1\leq i\leq 2d}i\mathrm{Card}[\mathrm{Fr}_{i}(\xi_{t})].

We state the following discrete isoperimetric inequality, proved for example by Hamamuki in
[
13
]
:

Lemma 5.1

(Discrete isoperimetric inequality)
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be a non-empty finite connected subset of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. There exists a constant
C=C_{d}>0
such that:

\mathrm{Card}(\mathrm{Fr}(A))\geq C\mathrm{Card}(A)^{1-\frac{1}{d}}.

This discrete isoperimetric inequality allows us to bound from below
I(\xi_{t})
, by bounding from below the cardinality of the set of healthy sites that can be infected at time
t
.

Proposition 5.2
.

There exists a constant
C=C_{d}>0
such that, for all
t>0
, we have

I(\xi_{t})\geq C\mathrm{Card}(\xi_{t})^{1-\frac{1}{d}}.

Proof.

We denote by
\mathcal{C}
the set of connected components of
\xi_{t}
. In order to bound from below the quantity
I(\xi_{t})
, we use Lemma
5.1
on each element of
\mathcal{C}
: there exists
C=C_{d}>0
such that

(12)

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{A\in\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{Card}(\mathrm{Fr}(A))\geq C\sum_{A\in\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{Card}(A)^{1-\frac{1}{d}}.

But we know that
I(\xi_{t})\geq\sum\limits_{A\in\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{Card}(\mathrm{Fr}(A))
. With these inequalities, and by (
12
), we deduce that:

\displaystyle I(\xi_{t})=\sum_{1\leq i\leq 2d}i\mathrm{Fr}_{i}(\xi_{t})

\displaystyle\geq\sum\limits_{A\in\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{Card}(\mathrm{Fr}(A))

(13)

\displaystyle\geq C\sum_{A\in\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{Card}(A)^{1-\frac{1}{d}}

(14)

\displaystyle\geq C\left(\sum\limits_{A\in\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{Card}(A)\right)^{1-\frac{1}{d}}\geq C\mathrm{Card}(\xi_{t})^{1-\frac{1}{d}}.

To go from (
13
) to (
14
) we used that, for all
d\geq 2
,
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
a_{1},...,a_{n}\geq 0
, we have

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{1-\frac{1}{d}}\geq\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{d}}.

∎

Proof of Theorem

We define a family of particles indexed by
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, whose positions evolve with time:

•

at any time, there is exactly one particle per site,

•

the particles move according to stirring times: two neighboring particles exchange their positions at a stirring time if and only if, at this time, they are located on two sites in different states.

Note that the particle
a\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is not necessarily located on site
a
. More formally, for every
a\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, we define the process
(P_{t}^{a})_{t\geq 0}
, called the
trajectory of particle
a
, using the graphical construction of the model (see Section
4.1
). The initial positions of the particles
(P_{0}^{a})_{a\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
define a partition of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Then, for all
t>0
,
P_{t}^{a}
is obtained by following the path in the graphical construction starting from
(P_{0}^{a},0)
, and moving along the temporal lines with the following constraints:

1.

The path does not pass through any infection arrow,

2.

The path follows a stirring arrow if and only if there is exactly one infected site incident to the arrow.

We define a process
(\tilde{\xi}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
that carries the information about the state and position of each particle over time. For all
t\geq 0
, we set
\tilde{\xi}_{t}=(\xi_{t}\circ P_{t},P_{t})
, where
P_{t}
is the map

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathbb{Z}^{d}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{Z}^{d}\\
a&\longmapsto&P_{t}^{a}\end{array}.

By construction, we immediately have the following lemma:

Lemma 6.1
.

1.

At every time
t>0
, the positions
(P_{t}^{a})_{a\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
of the particles at time
t
form a partition of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

2.

The process
(\tilde{\xi}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
satisfies the strong Markov property.

3.

Once a particle
a\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is infected, it remains infected forever:

\text{if }\tilde{\xi}_{t}(a)=(1,P_{t}^{a}),\text{ then: }\forall s\geq t,~\tilde{\xi}_{s}(a)=(1,P_{s}^{a}).

4.

A site
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is healthy at time
t
if and only if a healthy particle is located at
x
at time
t
:

x\notin\xi_{t}\iff\exists a\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},~\tilde{\xi}_{t}(a)=(0,x).

Point (
4
) of Lemma
6.1
justifies the introduction of these processes: we will obtain information about the state of sites by studying the trajectories of the particles.

7.1
Proof of Theorem
3.6
for the RMS

Clearly, no matter the infection rate
\lambda
, the
\mathrm{RMS}(\lambda,1)
survives weakly, since there is no healing in this model. We adapt Liggett’s proof
[
22
]
of the existence of an interval of infection rates
\lambda
such that the
\mathrm{CP}(\lambda,1)
survives weakly but not strongly on
T_{d}
. It amounts to prove that, for all
\nu>0
, there exists
\lambda_{s}^{\mathrm{RMS}}(d,\nu)>0
such that, for all
\lambda<\lambda_{s}^{\mathrm{RMS}}
, the
\mathrm{CPS}(\lambda,1,\nu)
does not survive strongly.

We define, for all
\rho\in(0,1)
and
A\subset T_{d}
finite, the quantity

w_{\rho}(A):=\sum_{x\in A}\rho^{l(x)}.

Step 1:
We start by proving that the process
(w_{\rho}(A_{t}))_{t\geq 0}
is a positive supermartingale, for
\rho=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}
and all
0<\lambda\leq\frac{d+1}{2\sqrt{d}}-1
. To do that, we show that we have, for all finite
A\subset T_{d}
:

(19)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}^{A}\left[w_{\rho}(\xi_{t})\right]_{|t=0}\leq 0.

By Theorem B3 of Liggett’s book
[
22
]
, we have:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}^{A}\left[w_{\rho}(\xi_{t})\right]_{|t=0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in A}\left[\lambda\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\sim x\\
y\notin A\end{subarray}}\rho^{l(y)}+1\times\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\sim x\\
y\notin A\end{subarray}}\left(\rho^{l(y)}-\rho^{l(x)}\right)\right]

(20)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in A}\rho^{l(x)}\left[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\sim x\\
y\notin A\\
l(y)=l(x)+1\end{subarray}}\left[(\lambda+1)\rho-1\right]+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\sim x\\
y\notin A\\
l(y)=l(x)-1\end{subarray}}\left[\frac{\lambda+1}{\rho}-1\right]\right].

Note that we always have
\frac{\lambda+1}{\rho}-1\geq 0
, but for some values of
\lambda
and
\rho
, we could have
(\lambda+1)\rho-1\leq 0
.

We can split the sum (
20
) into sums on the connected components of
A
, and prove that each of these sums is negative. Therefore, we can suppose without loss of generality that the set
A
is connected. In this case, there exists a unique site
x_{0}\in A
such that
x_{0}
’s parent is not in
A
. Moreover, this site
x_{0}
is the only site in
A
which verifies

l(x_{0})=\min\{l(y):y\in A\}.

For all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, we denote by:

•

A_{n}
the set of sites that are in
A
and have level
n
;

•

a_{n}
the quantity
\mathrm{Card}(A_{n})
.

Note that we have
w_{\rho}(A)=\sum_{n\geq l(x_{0})}a_{n}\rho^{n}
, and the quantity
da_{n}-a_{n+1}
corresponds to the number of sites of level
n+1
that are not in
A
, but have a parent in
A
. In other words, it is the number of sites of level
n+1
that are not in
A_{n+1}
, but can be infected by someone in
A
. With these notation, (
20
) can be rewritten as:

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq l(x_{0})}\rho^{n}(da_{n}-a_{n+1})\left[(\lambda+1)\rho-1\right]+\left[\frac{\lambda+1}{\rho}-1\right]\rho^{l(x_{0})}

\displaystyle=\left[dw_{\rho}(A)-\left(\frac{1}{\rho}w_{\rho}(A)-a_{l(x_{0})}\rho^{l(x_{0})-1}\right)\right]\left[(\lambda+1)\rho-1\right]+\left[\frac{\lambda+1}{\rho}-1\right]\rho^{l(x_{0})}

\displaystyle=\left[\left(d-\frac{1}{\rho}\right)w_{\rho}(A)+\rho^{l(x_{0})-1}\right]\left[(\lambda+1)\rho-1\right]+\left[\frac{\lambda+1}{\rho}-1\right]\rho^{l(x_{0})}.

For
\rho=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}
and
\lambda<\frac{d+1}{2\sqrt{d}}-1
, we have:

\displaystyle\left[\left(d-\sqrt{d}\right)w_{\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}}(A)+\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{l(x_{0})-1}\right]\left[\frac{\lambda+1}{\sqrt{d}}-1\right]+\left[\sqrt{d}(\lambda+1)-1\right]\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{l(x_{0})}

(21)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{l(x_{0})}\left[d\left[\frac{\lambda+1}{\sqrt{d}}-1\right]+\sqrt{d}(\lambda+1)-1\right]

(22)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{l(x_{0})}\left[2\sqrt{d}(\lambda+1)-(d+1)\right]<0,

where:

•

we obtain (
21
) by using that
w_{\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}}(C)\geq a_{l(x_{0})}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{l(x_{0})}=\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{l(x_{0})}
and that
\frac{\lambda+1}{\sqrt{d}}-1<0
,

•

we have (
22
) because of the choice of
\lambda
and
d\geq 2
,

and so we proved (
19
).

Now let us prove that (
19
) implies that
(w_{\rho}(\xi_{t}))_{t\geq 0}
is a supermartingale. We denote by
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
the natural filtration associated to the process
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, and by
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{RMS}}_{\lambda,1}
the generator of the process
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. By Theorem 6.2 of
[
18
]
, the process

\left(w_{\rho}(\xi_{t})-\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{L}w_{\rho}(\xi_{s})ds\right)_{t\geq 0}

is a
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-martingale. Since we have, for all finite
A\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}w_{\rho}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}^{A}[w_{\rho}(\xi_{t})]_{|t=0}<0,

then we have, for all
t,s\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[w_{\rho}(\xi_{t+s})|\mathcal{F}_{t}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[w_{\rho}(\xi_{t+s})-\int_{0}^{t+s}\mathcal{L}w_{\rho}(\xi_{u})du|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t+s}\mathcal{L}w_{\rho}(\xi_{u})du|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=w_{\rho}(\xi_{t})-\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{L}w_{\rho}(\xi_{u})du+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{L}w_{\rho}(\xi_{u})du+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{t+s}\mathcal{L}w_{\rho}(\xi_{u})du|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle\leq w_{\rho}(\xi_{t}),

and
(w_{\rho}(\xi_{t}))_{t\geq 0}
is a positive supermartingale.

Step 2:
Let us show that all sites are fixing on a state after a long enough time. Since the process
(w_{\rho}(\xi_{t}))_{t\geq 0}
is a positive supermartingale for
\rho=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}
and
0<\lambda<\frac{d+1}{2\sqrt{d}}-1
(by Step
1
), then for these parameters,
(w_{\rho}(\xi_{t}))_{t\geq 0}
converges a.s.

Let
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
x\in T_{d}
be a site such that
|l(x)|\leq n
. If an infection or an exchange happens on
x
at time
t
, then the quantity
w_{\rho}(\xi_{t})
increases or decreases of at least a constant
\alpha_{n}>0
, which does not depend on the time
t
. Since the process
(w_{\rho}(\xi_{t}))_{t\geq 0}
converges a.s., we deduce that each site
x
such that
|l(x)|\leq n
is fixing on infected or healthy after a long enough time. Moreover, two neighboring sites cannot fix on a different state, since they would have positive probability to interact. Therefore, they all fix on the same state.

Step 3:
Let us show that the sites fix on healthy after a long enough time a.s. Suppose by contradiction that a site fixes on infected after a long enough time a.s. Then all the sites fix on infected after a long enough time a.s.
This implies that:

\sum_{x\in T^{d}}\rho^{l(x)}\leq\lim_{t\to+\infty}w_{\rho}(\xi_{t})\quad\text{a.s.},

which is absurd since
\sum_{x\in T^{d}}\rho^{l(x)}\geq\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left(\frac{1}{\rho}\right)^{n}=+\infty
.

Step 4:
Finally, let us show (
8
). The bound from below has just been proved. For the bound from above, we couple the
\mathrm{RMS}(\lambda,1)
with a
\mathrm{CP}(\lambda,d+1)
in the same way as we did on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
in Lemma
4.1
. The healing rate is
d+1
here, since each site has
d+1
neighbors. Moreover, denoting by
\lambda_{s}^{\mathrm{CP}}(d)
the critical parameter for the strong survival of the contact process of infection rate
\lambda
and of healing rate
1
on
T_{d}
, then Theorem 4.65 of Liggett’s book
[
20
]
gives

\lambda_{s}^{\mathrm{CP}}(d)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}-1},\quad\text{and so }\lambda_{s}^{\mathrm{RMS}}(d)\leq\frac{d+1}{\sqrt{d}-1}.

7.2
Proof of Theorem
3.6
for the CPS

We follow the same steps as in the preceding proof.

Step 1:
Let us show that, for
\rho=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}
and all

0<\lambda<\nu\left(\frac{d+1}{2\sqrt{d}}-1\right)
, we have:

(23)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}^{A}\left[w_{\rho}(\xi_{t})\right]_{|t=0}\leq 0.

In the same manner as for the RMS, and with the same notations, we have:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}^{A}\left[w_{\rho}(\xi_{t})\right]_{|t=0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in A}\left[\lambda\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\sim x\\
y\notin A\end{subarray}}\rho^{l(y)}+\nu\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\sim x\\
y\notin A\end{subarray}}\left(\rho^{l(y)}-\rho^{l(x)}\right)-\rho^{l(x)}\right]

\displaystyle=\nu\sum_{x\in A}\left[\frac{\lambda}{\nu}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\sim x\\
y\notin A\end{subarray}}\rho^{l(y)}+1\times\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\sim x\\
y\notin A\end{subarray}}\left(\rho^{l(y)}-\rho^{l(x)}\right)\right]-\sum_{x\in A}\rho^{l(x)}.

Remember that in the proof for the RMS, we showed that:

\nu\sum_{x\in A}\left[\frac{\lambda}{\nu}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\sim x\\
y\notin A\end{subarray}}\rho^{l(y)}+1\times\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\sim x\\
y\notin A\end{subarray}}\left(\rho^{l(y)}-\rho^{l(x)}\right)\right]<0

for
\rho=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}
and
\frac{\lambda}{\nu}<\frac{d+1}{2\sqrt{d}}-1
. Then for
\rho=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}
and
\lambda<\nu\left[\frac{d+1}{2\sqrt{d}}-1\right]
, we have (
23
).

Step 2 and 3:
They are exactly the same as for the RMS.

Step 4:
Now let us bound from above the critical parameter
\lambda_{s}^{\mathrm{CPS}}(d,\nu)
. Similarly to the RMS case, we couple a
\mathrm{CPS}(\lambda,1,\nu)
with a
\mathrm{CP}(\lambda,(d+1)\nu+1)
. As for the RMS, we use that

\lambda_{s}^{\mathrm{CP}}(d)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}-1}
, which gives:

\lambda_{s}^{\mathrm{CPS}}(d,\nu)\leq\frac{(d+1)\nu+1}{\sqrt{d}-1}.

Therefore we have

\displaystyle\nu\left(\frac{d+1}{2\sqrt{d}}-1\right)\leq\lambda_{s}^{\mathrm{CPS}}(d,\nu)\leq\frac{(d+1)\nu+1}{\sqrt{d}-1}.

On the other hand, if we denote by
\lambda_{w}^{\mathrm{CP}}(d)
the critical parameter for the weak survival of the
\mathrm{CP}(\lambda,1)
, then Theorem 4.1 of Liggett’s book
[
20
]
gives

\lambda_{w}^{\mathrm{CP}}(d)\leq\frac{1}{d-1}.

So the coupling of a
\mathrm{CPS}(\lambda,1,\nu)
and a
\mathrm{CP}(\lambda,(d+1)\nu+1)
also gives:

\lambda_{w}^{\mathrm{CPS}}(d,\nu)\leq\frac{(d+1)\nu+1}{d-1}.

Therefore, our bounds give
\lambda_{w}^{\mathrm{CPS}}(d,\nu)<\lambda_{s}^{\mathrm{CPS}}(d,\nu)
if and only if:

(24)

\displaystyle\nu\left(\frac{d+1}{2\sqrt{d}}-1\right)-\frac{(d+1)\nu+1}{d-1}>0

A quick study of the map
f:x\mapsto x^{2}-4x^{\frac{3}{2}}-1
shows that
f
is negative on
(0,16]
and positive on
[17,+\infty)
. Therefore:

•

If
d\leq 16
, we have

\eqref{Condition arbre}\iff\nu<\frac{2\sqrt{d}}{d^{2}-4d^{\frac{3}{2}}-1}<0.

•

If
d\geq 17
, we have

\eqref{Condition arbre}\iff\nu>\frac{2\sqrt{d}}{d^{2}-4d^{\frac{3}{2}}-1}>0.

It implies that
W
is non-empty if and only if
d\geq 17
.

8
Discussion

8.1
Asymptotic shape theorem for low infection rates

In dimension
d>1
, Durrett and Griffeath
[
8
]
proved the asymptotic shape theorem for the contact process, for infection rates
\lambda
large enough, using embedded one-dimensional contact processes. One could want to proceed in a similar way for the RMS. The problem is that the natural coupling between a RMS and an embedded one-dimensional RMS is not monotone, in the sense that we do not necessarily have
\xi^{1}_{t}\subset\xi^{d}_{t}
, where
(\xi^{1}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
(resp.
(\xi^{d}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
) is an embedded one-dimensional RMS (resp. a RMS in dimension
d
): see Figure
6
. The problem is the same for the CPS: the stirring dynamics break the monotonicity.

Figure 6
:
A two-dimensional contact process (left side) and an embedded one-dimensional contact process on
\mathbb{Z}\times\{0\}
. Infected sites are black, healthy sites are white. A time
t=0
, both processes start from the initial state
\{0\}
. At time
t=T_{1}
, the first interaction occurs: site
(0,0)
infects site
(1,0)
, and so site
0
infects site
1
in the one-dimensional process. Time
t=T_{2}
is a stirring time: site
(1,0)
and site
(1,1)
exchange their states, and nothing happens in the one-dimensional process. At this time, we have
\xi_{T_{2}}^{1}\not\subset\xi_{T_{2}}^{d}
.

Another way to deal with the case of low infection rates could be to use Proposition
3.4
to prove that the growth is at least linear (
7
). For
x=0
, the idea is that at some time
t
, there are around
t^{d}
infected sites (Proposition
3.4
), relatively close to the origin (coupling with RM, Lemma
4.1
), with high probability. At this time, imagine that there is a particle placed on each infected site, each of them following the time lines of the graphical construction. These particles are all making a simple random walk at rate
2d\nu
, where
\nu
is the stirring rate. If these walks were independent, we could prove that the origin is infected at linear speed, since there are numerous infected particles doing a random walk near the site. But we did not manage to overcome the strong dependencies between these random walks.

8.2
Bezuidenhout and Grimmett’s block construction

Bezuidenhout and Grimmett
[
2
]
made a smart block construction in order to compare the contact process to an oriented percolation. It is possible to deduce from it many properties, such as the fact that the contact process dies out at criticality, or that the critical parameters for weak and strong survival are equal. It can also be used to prove (
6
) for the contact process. One could want to use a similar construction adapted to the RMS and the CPS to prove (
6
) and (
7
) for these models, or to prove that the CPS dies out at criticality. But this construction relies on the FKG inequality to prove that some events have high enough probabilities. By Theorem 2.14 of
[
20
]
, the RMS (resp. the CPS) on a finite subset
S\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
satisfies the FKG inequality if and only if its generator, rewritten in the form

\mathcal{L}f(\xi)=\sum_{\eta\in\{0,1\}^{S^{d}}}\rho(\xi,\eta)[f(\eta)-f(\xi)],

with
\rho(\xi,\eta)
the rate at which the process goes from the configuration
\xi
to
\eta
, satisfies:

\rho(\eta,\xi)>0\text{ implies that }\eta\leq\xi\text{ or }\eta\geq\xi.

But with the stirring, an infected site and a healthy neighbor on a configuration
\xi
can exchange their states at a positive rate, and the configuration
\eta
obtained does not check
\eta\leq\xi
nor
\eta\geq\xi
. Therefore, the FKG inequality appears to be false in general for both the RMS and the CPS on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and it is therefore not possible to directly adapt Bezuidenhout and Grimmett’s construction for our models.
