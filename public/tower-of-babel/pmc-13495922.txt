Combination of Lenvatinib and Antibiotics: A Potential Approach to Enhance Sensitivity in Differentiated Thyroid Cancer
Authors: Celina Ablinger, Petra Huber‐Cantonati, Daniela D. Weber, Sarah Pichler, Georg Schischkow, Marta Garcia‐Miralles, Christian Pirich, Gundula Rendl, Teresa Kiener, Thomas Felder, Martin Geroldinger, Robert Terkola, Barbara Kofler, Johanna Pachmayr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTApproved in 2015 for radioiodine‐refractory differentiated thyroid carcinoma (RR‐DTC), the multikinase inhibitor lenvatinib has demonstrated substantial efficacy; nevertheless, severe adverse effects, intrinsic resistance or development of acquired resistance often limit its use. A novel approach to sensitize tumor cells to therapy is the administration of antibiotics that target mitochondrial metabolism. We analyzed the effects of a combination of lenvatinib with the tetracycline‐class antibiotics tigecycline and eravacycline in DTC cells with reduced response to lenvatinib and explored the underlying mechanism of action. Cell viability was quantified after treatment with either single agents or combination therapies consisting of lenvatinib with tigecycline or eravacycline. Baseline oxidative metabolism and drug‐induced changes in oxygen consumption rate were measured. Three‐dimensional spheroid cultures were used to better mimic the in vivo tumor milieu. Apoptosis was assessed by caspase‐3/7 activity, and expression of apoptosis‐related proteins was elucidated by immunoblotting. We found that combining lenvatinib with tigecycline or eravacycline resulted in stronger reductions in 2D and 3D cell viability compared to single‐agent treatments in K1 DTC cells, which show limited lenvatinib responsiveness. Both combination therapies markedly impaired mitochondrial respiration, accompanied by down‐regulation of anti‐apoptotic Bcl‐2 family members, activation of caspase‐3/7, and cleavage of Poly (ADP‐ribose) polymerase (PARP), which are hallmarks of apoptosis. Although limited to in vitro models and subject to pharmacological limitations, our findings provide a mechanistic proof‐of‐concept that mitochondria‐targeting antibiotics may enhance lenvatinib responsiveness in a DTC cell line.

Introduction

Thyroid carcinoma is the most prevalent endocrine malignancy accounting for approximately 568,000 new cases and over 44,000 deaths worldwide. The global incidence rate is three times higher in women than in men [1]. Differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC), which arises from follicular cells of the thyroid gland, is the most common subtype accounting for approximately 90%–95% of all thyroid carcinomas and includes papillary, follicular, and oncocytic (Hürthle cell) carcinoma [2, 3]. Surgery is curative in most cases of well‐differentiated DTC and radioactive iodine (RAI) treatment after surgery improves overall survival in patients with high risk [3]. However, a subset of patients develops progressive disease that becomes refractory to RAI therapy (RR‐DTC). For those patients, the tyrosine‐kinase inhibitor (TKI) lenvatinib has been approved [2]. Although lenvatinib exhibits favorable anti‐tumor activity and extends progression‐free survival, progressive disease is common. In addition, severe adverse effects frequently occur, often necessitating dose reductions or treatment breaks [4, 5, 6, 7]. Given the limitations of current therapies, there is a need for novel, more efficient and better‐tolerated treatment options for DTC patients who do not respond properly to TKI. A possible way to sensitize tumor cells to TKI is the administration of combined therapy. Studies have shown that co‐inhibition of additional pathways can enhance the therapeutic efficacy and sensitize cells to lenvatinib [8].

There is growing evidence that changes in mitochondrial metabolism play a crucial role in the development and progression of various tumor entities. Mitochondrial reprogramming has been linked to uncontrolled cell proliferation, as cancer cells require more energy and building blocks than healthy cells [9]. Additionally, increased mitochondrial activity and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) have been associated with resistance to therapies like TKIs [10, 11].

The endosymbiotic hypothesis suggests that mitochondria originated from ancestral bacteria. Therefore, several antibiotics affect these organelles by targeting mitochondrial components. Certain antibiotics, particularly tetracyclines, have been shown to disrupt mitochondrial function by inhibiting mitochondrial biogenesis and increasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, leading to metabolic stress and anti‐proliferative or pro‐apoptotic effects [12, 13, 14]. Tigecycline, a representative of the tetracycline class, has demonstrated anti‐cancer properties across various tumor entities by impairing cell proliferation, disrupting aerobic metabolism, inducing mitochondrial dysfunction and sensitizing cancer cells to chemotherapeutic agents [13, 14]. Eravacycline, another synthetic tetracycline analogue, has been associated with a better toxicity profile compared to tigecycline, particularly regarding gastrointestinal side effects. Although its anti‐cancer effect has not yet been investigated, the mitochondrial‐targeting potential and improved tolerability makes it an interesting candidate for combinational approaches in cancer therapy [15, 16].

Combination therapies have gained increasing interest in oncology due to their potential to enhance therapeutic efficacy and reduce drug‐related adverse events by lowering the required doses. Furthermore, clinical evidence suggests that combining adjuvant chemotherapy with antibiotics can improve survival outcomes in patients with resected pancreatic cancer [17]. In the context of DTC, sensitizing tumor cells to TKI—particularly lenvatinib—might be a promising strategy to enhance treatment efficacy [8]. Considering the role of mitochondria in tumor progression and drug resistance, we hypothesized that combining lenvatinib with mitochondrial‐targeting antibiotics such as tigecycline or eravacycline might enhance treatment responses and increase lenvatinib sensitivity in vitro.

Materials and Methods

Cell Lines and Compounds

K1 (RRID:CVCL_2537), TPC‐1 (RRID:CVCL_6298), FTC‐133 (RRID:CVCL_1219) and non‐malignant Nthy‐Ori 3.1 (RRID:CVCL_2659) cells were obtained from Sigma‐Aldrich (Missouri, USA), whereas TT2609‐C02 (RRID:CVCL_2218) cells were purchased from the German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures GmbH (DSMZ, Braunschweig, Germany). All media contained 2 mM L‐glutamine (PAN Biotech GmbH, Aidenbach, Germany), 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin (PAN Biotech, Aidenbach, Germany) and 10% or 20% (TT2609‐C02) FCS (Sigma‐Aldrich, Missouri, USA). For K1, DMEM (Sigma‐Aldrich, Missouri, USA) was supplemented with Ham's F12 (Sigma‐Aldrich, Missouri, USA) and MCDB 105 (Sigma‐Aldrich, Missouri, USA). For FTC‐133, DMEM was supplemented with Ham's F12. TPC‐1 and TT2609‐C02 were cultivated in RPMI 1640 (PAN Biotech GmbH, Aidenbach, Germany), whereas for TT2609‐C02 0.1% insulin‐transferrin‐selenite were supplemented. Nthy‐Ori 3.1 were cultured in RPMI 1640 with sodium bicarbonate. All cells were cultivated at 37°C with 5% CO2 and constant humidity. Before cell seeding, all culture flasks and multi‐well plates (except for the Seahorse experiments) were coated with collagen G (0.001% in PBS, Sigma‐Aldrich, Missouri, USA). All experiments were performed with mycoplasma‐free cells. All human cell lines have been authenticated using STR profiling within the last 3 years (Microsynth, Vienna, Austria). For each experiment, cells were seeded and stimulated 24 h after seeding. Lenvatinib and tigecycline were obtained from Selleckchem (Houston, USA), and eravacycline dihydrochloride from THP Medical Products (Vienna, Austria). All compounds were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), a universal solvent for poorly water‐soluble compounds, with a final concentration in the culture medium not exceeding 0.5%.

Cell Viability

Cell viability was determined using the Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK8, Sigma‐Aldrich, Missouri, USA) after 72 h of treatment. CCK8 was added in a 1:10 ratio and incubated for 2 h before absorbance measurement at 450 nm on a Tecan Spark plate reader (Grödig, Austria). For IC50 determination, the equation log(agonist) vs. response—variable slope (four parameters) in GraphPad prism 10.2.3 was used.

3D Cell Culture Model

Cell viability of 3D spheroids was investigated using the CellTiter‐Glo 3D Cell Viability assay (CTG) from Promega (Wisconsin, USA). Cells were seeded into low‐attachment 96‐well U‐bottom plates, centrifuged, stimulated 72 h after seeding and incubated for 96 h. Spheroid pictures were taken using an Olympus CKX53SF microscope (Vienna, Austria). 3D cell viability was quantified according to the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, CTG was added in a 1:1 ratio, plate was shaken and incubated for 25 min at RT, the supernatant was transferred to a white plate and luminescence was recorded with an integration time of 10 ms on a Tecan Spark plate reader (Grödig, Austria).

Clonogenic Assay

For evaluation of long‐term cell survival, treated cells were re‐seeded at a density of 5 × 103 cells/well and incubated for 5 days. Attached cells were stained with crystal violet before pictures were taken with the ChemiDoc Imaging System of Bio‐Rad (Hercules, USA). The dye was released with an SDS/EtOH dissolving buffer and absorbance was measured at 550 nm with the Tecan Spark plate reader.

Caspase 3/7 Activity

For evaluation of apoptosis, the Promega Caspase‐Glo 3/7 Assay System was used. Briefly, cells were seeded and stimulated 24 h after seeding. Caspase substrate was added in a 1:1 ratio and the plate was shaken for 2 min. After 30 min at RT, the luminescence signal was measured on a Tecan Spark plate reader, with an integration time of 10 ms.

ATP Assay

For ATP level determination the Promega CellTiter‐Glo Cell Viability Assay was performed. Briefly, cells were seeded and stimulated 24 h after seeding. ATP substrate was added in a 1:1 ratio and the plate was shaken for 30 s. After 10 min at RT, the luminescence signal was measured on a Tecan Spark plate reader, with an integration time of 10 ms.

JC‐10

For determination of the mitochondrial membrane potential, the JC‐10 kit from Sigma‐Aldrich (Missouri, USA) was used according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, cells were seeded, grown overnight and stimulated for 72 h. JC‐10 dye (1:100, buffer A) was added, incubated for 1 h at 37°C in the dark, then buffer B was added. Green fluorescence intensity was recorded at an excitation (ex) wavelength of 490 nm and an emission (em) wavelength of 525 nm. Red fluorescence was measured at ex = 540 nm and em = 590 nm with a Tecan Spark plate reader. Mitochondrial membrane potential was calculated as follows: (red fluorescence)/(green fluorescence).

Live Cell Imaging

Mitochondrial morphology was assessed using MitoTracker Green FM (Invitrogen, Massachusetts, USA). Cells were seeded in ibidi‐μslides (Ibidi GmbH, Martinsried, Germany) and grown overnight. After 72 h of stimulation, cells were washed with PBS and incubated at 37°C, light protected, for 30 min with MitoTracker Green FM (1:5000). After washing, the Hoechst 33342 stain (1:12,300, Thermo Scientific, Massachusetts, USA) was added. Cells were incubated for 5 min at RT light protected and washed once before adding culture medium. Images were obtained using a Zeiss LSM700 confocal microscope (Oberkochen, Germany) and image analysis was performed with the Zeiss Zen software.

Western Blot

Proteins were separated via SDS‐PAGE, transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies diluted in 5% BSA or milk in TBS‐T: caspase 7 (1:1000, 35.20 kDa), Bcl‐xl (1:1000, 30 kDa), Mcl‐1 (1:1000, 40 kDa), Bcl‐2 (1:1000, 28 kDa), PARP (1:1000, 116.89 kDa) and cleaved PARP (1:1000, 89 kDa). After 1 h at RT with HRP‐linked anti‐rabbit or anti‐mouse secondary antibodies (1:2000 Cell Signaling, Massachusetts, USA), proteins were visualized using ECL and chemiluminescence was detected with the ChemiDoc Touch (Bio‐Rad, Munich, Germany). Protein expression was quantified by stain‐free technology (Image Lab Software) and normalized to protein load and mean control values.

Bioenergetic Measurements

For metabolic characterization, cells were seeded in XFe96/XF Pro microplates (Agilent Technologies, SC, US) at optimized densities and incubated 24 h prior to assay. K1 cells for drug treatments were seeded at 7500 cells/well and treated for 12 h. On the assay day, cells were washed and incubated 1 h in Seahorse XF RPMI medium supplemented with glucose (10 mM), pyruvate (1 mM) and glutamine (2 mM) at 37°C in a non‐CO2 incubator. Basal OCR and ECAR were measured using XF Cell Mito Stress Test and Glycolytic Rate Assay, with sequential injections of oligomycin (2.5 μM), FCCP (1 μM), rotenone/antimycin A (0.5 μM each) and 2‐DG (50 mM). Nuclear stain Hoechst 33342 (2 μM) was added for cell quantification. The assays were performed with the Seahorse XFe96 Analyzer, according to the manufacturer's protocol. Data were normalized to the acquired cell number (BioTek Lionheart FX and Gen5 Software) and analyzed using Wave (v2.6.4) and Seahorse Analytics (Agilent Technologies). Bioenergetic measurements were performed without Penicillin/Streptomycin to avoid potential confounding effects.

Statistics

For each experiment, treatment values were normalized to controls. Unless otherwise stated, experiments were performed in at least three independent biological replicates (n ≥ 3), data are presented as mean ± SEM and were statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10.2.3. Basal and compensatory glycolysis measurements were conducted as exploratory analyses in two independent experiments and are therefore reported as mean ± SD. Statistically relevant differences between the UTC and the treated samples were determined using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett's multiple comparison test.

Assessment of Combination Effects

Combination effects were assessed using the Bliss independence model as a descriptive approach [18]: YP=YA+YB−YAYB where YA and YB represent the effects of monotherapy A and B, respectively. The observed combined percentage inhibition (YO) was then compared with the predicted effect (YP). If YO > YP, the combination was interpreted as showing an enhanced effect compared with single treatments.

Results

DTC Cells Exert Heterogeneous Response to Lenvatinib and Mitochondria‐Targeting Antibiotics

To investigate the therapeutic responsiveness of DTC cells, we first analyzed the dose response effect of lenvatinib, a multi‐kinase inhibitor clinically used in advanced DTC, across two papillary (K1, TPC‐1), two follicular DTC (FTC‐133, TT2609‐C02) and one non‐malignant thyroid cell line (Nthy‐Ori 3.1) (Figure 1A). The cell lines displayed varying degrees of sensitivity to lenvatinib. TPC‐1 cells showed the strongest response, with an IC50 of 19.00 μM. TT2609‐C02 cells exhibited reduced sensitivity with a notably higher IC50 of 79.43 μM. FTC‐133 and K1 cells exerted the lowest sensitivity, as their viability did not drop below 50% even at the highest concentration tested (100 μM) and were therefore classified as “low‐responding cells”. These findings underscore the heterogeneous response of DTC to lenvatinib.

Next, we evaluated the response of the DTC cell lines to the mitochondrial‐targeting antibiotic tigecycline (Figure 1B). Three cell lines showed similar responses (IC50 K1: 11.00 μM, TPC‐1: 12.07 μM, Nthy‐Ori 3.1: 12.85 μM), whereas TT2609‐C02 cells were most sensitive (2.83 μM), and FTC‐133 cells were largely unresponsive to tigecycline. Afterwards, we tested eravacycline, a next generation synthetic tetracycline analogue with an improved toxicity profile (Figure 1C) [16]. The cell lines exhibited higher IC50 values compared to tigecycline and a more heterogeneous sensitivity pattern (K1: 15.29 μM, TPC‐1: 4.85 μM, TT2609‐C02: 28.37 μM, Nthy‐Ori 3.1: 10.23 μM). Again, FTC‐133 cells were non‐responsive to eravacycline, indicating that this cell line did not respond to mitochondrial‐targeting antibiotics. Selectivity indices, derived from monotherapy dose–response curves (Supporting Information Materials and Methods 1.1.), were calculated where IC50 values could be determined. The indices varied between cell lines and compounds, indicating heterogeneous sensitivity of tumor and non‐tumor cells (Table S1).

DTC Cells Exhibit Distinct Baseline Profiles of Mitochondrial Respiration and Higher Rates of Glycolysis Compared to Non‐Malignant Cells

In order to characterize the metabolism of DTC cells in comparison to non‐malignant thyroid cells (Nthy‐Ori 3.1), we performed a series of real‐time measurements of the oxygen consumption rate (OCR), indicating oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) capacity (Figure 1D). Moreover, the glycolytic proton efflux rate (glycoPER), indicating glycolytic rate, was investigated (Figure 1E). TPC‐1 and TT2609‐C02 cells showed lower rates of basal respiration and ATP‐linked respiration (Figure 1F,H). The level of maximal respiration was higher in K1 (Figure 1G). In FTC‐133 a higher proton leakage across the mitochondrial membrane and lower mitochondrial coupling efficiency was observed, indicating a dysfunction in mitochondrial respiration (Figure 1I,J). As expected from the Warburg effect, meaning that cancer cells produce energy via aerobic glycolysis rather than oxidative phosphorylation [19, 20], exploratory analysis showed that basal and compensatory glycolysis (except in TPC‐1 cells) were higher in DTC cells (Figure 1K,L). Nthy‐Ori 3.1 cells showed a higher OCR/ECAR ratio, indicating a stronger reliance on OXPHOS, while DTC cells depended more on glycolysis (Figure 1M). The energetic map supported these findings, clustering Nthy‐Ori 3.1 cells in the aerobic quadrant, TPC‐1 and TT2609‐C02 as glycolytic, and FTC‐133 and K1 as mixed, using both mitochondrial respiration and glycolysis (Figure 1N).

Combination of Lenvatinib With Mitochondrial‐Targeting Antibiotics Enhances Lenvatinib Sensitivity in K1 Cells

To further elucidate whether mitochondria‐targeting antibiotics can enhance the lenvatinib sensitivity in low‐responding DTC cells, we selected one cell line (K1) that showed minimal response toward lenvatinib (Figure 1A), but moderate sensitivity to tigecycline and eravacycline (Figure 1B,C). The second low‐responding cell line, FTC‐133, was excluded from further combinational investigations due to its lack of response to antibiotic monotherapy (Figure 1B,C).

K1 cells were treated with concentrations that allowed evaluation of combination effects without strong cytotoxicity of single agents (Figures S1 and S2). We found that combining 50 μM lenvatinib and 6.25 μM tigecycline reduced 2D cell viability more strongly compared to single agent treatments. While lenvatinib had no significant effect and tigecycline alone caused only a moderate reduction in cell viability, the combination led to a stronger cytotoxic effect reducing cell viability to 35% (Figure 2A). Similarly, the combination of 50 μM lenvatinib and 10 μM eravacycline reduced cell viability to 25% (Figure 2B). Combinations were also tested in FTC‐133 and TT2609‐C02 cells, where only minimal or modest additive effects were observed. Reduced viability was also observed in Nthy‐Ori 3.1 cells (Figures S3–S5).

To better mimic solid tumors in vivo, we also employed 3D cell cultures, where cells grow as spheroids in contrast to a flat layer, allowing for more natural cell–cell contacts, the formation of a hypoxic environment and more realistic drug diffusion for the investigation of treatment effects [21]. While monotherapies showed limited efficacy, combination treatments led to a stronger reduction in spheroid viability and integrity (Figure 2C,D). Representative images confirmed decreased spheroid size and disrupted structure after combination treatment.

We further assessed whether treated cells still have the capacity to form colonies and further proliferate once the treatment was terminated. While cells pre‐treated with the monotherapies were still able to form colonies almost similar to controls, the combination treatments led to an enhanced reduction in colony formation. These observation are also reflected in the representative images: While control cells formed numerous colonies, the number of visible colonies was markedly reduced in both combination treatments (Figure 2E,F). In a soft‐agar colony formation assay (Supporting Information Materials and Methods 1.2.), UTC cells formed numerous large colonies, confirming their anchorage‐independent growth, whereas the monotherapies markedly reduced colony formation. The combination caused only a slightly stronger effect (Figure S6). To support interpretation of the combination effects, Bliss independence analyses were performed (Figure 2G,H). In all tested conditions the observed effects (YO) exceeded the predicted effects (YP), indicating enhanced combination effects compared to single treatments.

For further mechanistic analyses, K1 cells were selected due to their low responsiveness to lenvatinib monotherapy, as their viability did not drop below 50% for the highest lenvatinib concentration, and reproducible combination effects.

Combinations of Lenvatinib and Antibiotics Affect Mitochondrial Function and Cellular Energy Metabolism in K1 Cells

As the combination of lenvatinib with tigecycline or eravacycline enhanced cytotoxicity, we next investigated mitochondrial function. Intracellular ATP levels were quantified following mono‐ and combination treatments. Tigecycline and lenvatinib alone had only minor effects on ATP levels, whereas eravacycline markedly reduced ATP content (Figures 3A and 4A). To gain deeper insight into potential effects on mitochondria, the analysis of the mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) showed that lenvatinib increased MMP, while both antibiotics markedly decreased it, indicating mitochondrial depolarization. Combination treatments did not further enhance this effect, suggesting that MMP loss was primarily antibiotic‐driven (Figures 3B and 4B).

Confocal microscopy of K1 cells revealed distinct mitochondrial changes after 72 h of treatment (Figures 3C and 4C). In controls, mitochondria were sparse and localized to one side of the nucleus. Lenvatinib seemed to increase mitochondrial number but not distribution, whereas the cells treated with antibiotics or the combinations showed an uniform mitochondrial distribution, surrounding the whole nucleus. To further characterize mitochondrial function, bioenergetic measurements were performed after 12 h of treatment (Figures 3D–N and 4D–N). Tigecycline and eravacycline markedly reduced basal and maximal respiration, whereas lenvatinib alone had only minor effects. Combination treatments did not further suppress respiration, indicating that these effects were mainly antibiotic‐driven. Similar patterns were observed for ATP‐linked respiration and glycolytic parameters. OCR/ECAR ratios decreased following combination treatments and the energy map revealed distinct metabolic shifts following treatment (Figures 3M,N and 4M,N): Control cells were located in the upper right quadrant, indicating high metabolic activity. Lenvatinib caused a slight downward shift, whereas tigecycline and the combination shifted centrally with reduced metabolic activity. In contrast, eravacycline and the combination shifted left, indicating decreased glycolysis with moderate oxidative metabolism.

We observed that Bcl‐xl expression was reduced upon combination treatment, while monotherapies had no or only a minor effect (Figures 5A and 6A). Mcl‐1 was consistently downregulated in all treatments, most pronounced in the combinations (Figures 5B and 6B). Bcl‐2 expression remained unchanged with lenvatinib or tigecycline alone and with their combination, but was markedly reduced by eravacycline, further enhanced when combined with lenvatinib (Figures 5C and 6C). To determine whether these changes were associated with caspase activation, caspase‐7 levels and caspase‐3/7 activity were assessed. Both combination treatments induced a strong increase in caspase‐3/7 activity after 48 h, whereas monotherapies showed weaker effects (Figures 5D,E and 6D,E). To validate caspase activation, we assessed PARP cleavage as a downstream apoptosis marker (Figures 5F and 6F). Cleaved PARP increased after both antibiotics alone and was futher enhanced by the combinations with lenvatinib, indicating a stronger apoptotic response (Figures 5F and 6F). These results suggest that antibiotics initiate apoptosis, which is markedly potentiated by lenvatinib.

To further explore possible mechanisms underlying the combination effects, we performed a proteomics analysis after 24 h of treatment. Exploratory proteomics and subsequent STRING analysis (Supporting Information Materials and Methods 1.3.) of downregulated proteins (identified by Perseus) indicated enrichment of proliferation‐related biological processes. While the lenvatinib‐tigecycline combination predominantly affected DNA metabolic and replication‐related processes, the lenvatinib‐eravacycline combination showed a stronger enrichment of cell‐cycle‐ and mitosis‐related pathways, which may contribute to enhanced apoptotic responses (Figure S7).

Discussion

Although lenvatinib is the current first‐line therapy for radioiodine‐refractory DTC (RR‐DTC), its use is limited by severe side effects and reduced sensitivity [4, 5, 6, 24]. Mitochondrial dysfunction has emerged as a relevant contributor to therapy resistance and targeting mitochondria with antibiotics has shown anti‐tumor effects in sorafenib‐resistant hepatocellular carcinoma [25]. Therefore, our study aimed to evaluate whether mitochondria‐targeting antibiotics can enhance the lenvatinib sensitivity of DTC cells. Lenvatinib sensitivity varied across the DTC cell lines, consistent with clinical observations that a proportion of patients does not benefit from lenvatinib treatment [4]. These observations align with previous reports showing limited sensitivity in DTC cell lines but stronger effects in xenograft models [26]. Therefore, our data supports the need to enhance treatment efficacy, particularly in low‐responding cells.

Given the increasing interest in targeting mitochondria, we investigated tigecycline, an inhibitor of mitochondrial translation [25] and eravacycline, a newer tetracycline class antibiotic with improved pharmacokinetic properties [27]. Both antibiotics reduced cell viability across all cell lines tested, consistent with their reported anti‐proliferative activity in various tumor models [14, 28, 29]. Although eravacycline exhibited higher IC50 values than tigecycline in our DTC models, likely due to structural differences [30], both agents showed relevant cytotoxic effects. Aminzadeh‐Gohari et al. compared different antibiotics in melanoma cells and found tigecycline to have a stronger anti‐proliferative effect than doxycycline and azithromycin [13]. Since some of the DTC cell lines showed only limited sensitivity to either lenvatinib or antibiotics, and mitochondria are known to contribute to therapy resistance, we further assessed the metabolic characteristics of DTC cell lines. Mitochondrial respiration was impaired in FTC‐133; however, this observation does not allow general conclusions regarding mitochondrial dependence or predictive response patterns. Although there were only minor differences between DTC and non‐malignant cells, it remains possible that DTC cells are more dependent on mitochondrial function under stress, as it was shown that DTC cells can shift between aerobic glycolysis and OXPHOS depending on the tumor microenvironment [31]. Thus, targeting mitochondria with antibiotics may still represent a therapeutic strategy.

The main aim of our study was to determine whether co‐treatment with mitochondria‐targeting antibiotics and lenvatinib enhances lenvatinib sensitivity in DTC cells. We found that the combination led to a stronger reduction in K1 cell viability than either lenvatinib or antibiotic monotherapy alone, which is consistent with previous work demonstrating that tetracycline antibiotics can enhance responses to chemotherapeutic agents [32]. Overall, our data provides a mechanistic proof‐of‐concept that co‐treatment with antibiotics may enhance lenvatinib responsiveness in K1 DTC cells under the experimental conditions tested.

Although tigecycline was shown to have anti‐cancer activity in various tumors, no unifying mechanism has been identified and the action of eravacycline remains poorly understood. The majority of studies suggest that mitochondria are the primary target via downregulation of mitochondrial protein synthesis, respiratory‐chain complexes, or reduced mitochondrial mass [12, 14, 32, 33]. Our results show that combination treatment affects both mitochondrial respiration and glycolysis after treatment, reflecting impaired mitochondrial ATP production, likely linked to loss of the MMP. However, the pronounced reduction in OCR is largely driven by the antibiotics alone, suggesting that bioenergetic disruption is not the main driver of the enhanced cytotoxic effect. Instead, the reduction in cell viability may result from the simultaneous inhibition of two distinct cellular pathways: mitochondrial energy metabolism by antibiotics and growth factor receptor signaling by lenvatinib. This dual interference may exceed the adaptive capacity of the tumor cells after treatment [34] and thereby lead to a stronger induction of cell death. These findings align with previous studies showing that chemotherapeutics have minimal impact on mitochondrial function in DTC but mitochondrial‐targeting can enhance chemotherapy sensitivity in multidrug‐resistant tumors [32, 35]. Since mitochondria are central regulators of apoptosis, we examined whether reduced cell viability was linked to apoptotic mechanisms. When anti‐apoptotic proteins are less abundant, the mitochondrial outer membrane starts to permeabilize. Executioner caspases cleave various cellular substrates, including PARP, which is widely recognized as a hallmark of apoptosis [23]. Our data shows that members of the anti‐apoptotic Bcl‐2 family were significantly downregulated following combinational treatment. In parallel, reduced procaspase‐7 protein levels and a strong increase in caspase‐3/7 activity indicated caspase activation and apoptotic progression. Interestingly, lenvatinib monotherapy decreased caspase‐3/7 activity, consistent with reports that lenvatinib resistance suppresses apoptotic signaling by enhancing anti‐apoptotic effects [36], thereby reducing cell death [37]. While total PARP levels remained largely unchanged, cleaved PARP markedly increased, indicating apoptosis induction be the combination therapy. Similarly, combining lenvatinib with AKT inhibitors was reported to enhance apoptotic markers and cell death, underscoring the potential of combination strategies to sensitize tumor cells to chemotherapeutics [38]. Since mitochondrial and apoptotic mechanisms may not fully explain the enhanced effect, we additionally performed an exploratory proteomics analysis. Besides the affection of mitochondrial pathways, the data revealed involvement of cell‐cycle related processes. In summary, our study suggests that combining lenvatinib with mitochondria‐targeting antibiotics may represent a potential mechanistic approach to enhance lenvatinib responses in K1 DTC by impairing mitochondrial function and promoting apoptotic cell death.

Limitations

Beyond providing a mechanistic proof‐of‐concept for enhancing lenvatinib sensitivity, our findings also raise important new questions. Mechanistic analyses were performed primarily in K1 cells, which showed the strongest combination response, and further validation in additional DTC models with distinct molecular backgrounds will be required. In this study, “lenvatinib low responsiveness” was defined functionally based on reduced sensitivity in viability assays and does not imply a specific molecular resistance mechanism. Future studies assessing molecular markers of lenvatinib target engagement would further strengthen the mechanistic interpretation.

While the in vitro settings allow exploration of interactions between lenvatinib and antibiotics, several aspects limit direct clinical feasibility. The concentrations used in this study exceed clinically achievable plasma levels and therefore limit direct translational interpretation. In patients receiving 24 mg lenvatinib daily, total plasma concentrations typically range between approximately 42–300 ng/mL at trough (C₀; i.e., immediately before the next dose) [39, 40, 41]. Given that lenvatinib is 98%–99% plasma protein‐bound, the pharmacologically active free fraction is substantially lower [42], estimated at less than 1–2 ng/mL (≈0.002–0.004 μM) [43], which is far below concentrations typically applied in vitro [26]. This discrepancy is further influenced by experimental conditions in cell culture, including the use of DMSO, which allows higher passive drug diffusion [44]. In patients, lenvatinib uptake is determined by gastrointestinal absorption, extensive protein binding, distribution in the body and a terminal half‐life of approximately 28 h [45], which is entirely absent in conventional 2D cell culture. Therefore, while the in vitro concentrations used in our study enabled mechanistic investigations they should not be interpreted as reflecting achievable intracellular drug levels in patients. Similar considerations apply to tigecycline, which exhibits a large volume of distribution and low plasma concentrations despite extensive tissue accumulation [46, 47]. Although comparable micromolar concentrations have been applied in previous preclinical studies [48], these findings should primarily be interpreted as mechanistic insights rather than evidence for clinical applicability.

Furthermore, tetracycline‐class antibiotics may affect non‐tumor tissue, promote antimicrobial resistance with repeated exposure, and alter the gut microbiome, which could influence the efficacy of multikinase inhibitors. The combination also reduced viability in an immortalized non‐tumorigenic thyroid cell line; however, such models exhibit high proliferative activity and altered metabolic characteristics and therefore do not adequately reflect the behavior of primary human thyrocytes. Quantitative selectivity data comparing tumor versus non‐tumor responses, including selectivity indices for the combination treatment, will be important to further define the therapeutic window of this approach more precisely. At the same time, these findings indicate that the therapeutic window of this approach cannot be estimated from the present in vitro data and that there might be potential effects on normal tissues. All these aspects highlight the need for appropriate in vivo studies to evaluate safety, dosing and potential long‐term consequences. Future investigations in xenograft or patient‐derived models will be essential to determine whether the combination effects observed in vitro are maintained in a physiological context.

Finally, it should be noted that all experiments were conducted in the presence of Penicillin/Streptomycin as a standard cell culture supplement. Although these antibiotics act primarily on bacterial cell wall synthesis and are not known to directly inhibit mitochondrial translation, potential confounding effects on cellular metabolism or mitochondrial function in the context of tetracycline co‐treatment cannot be entirely excluded and should be addressed in future studies. Taken together, our results provide mechanistic insight into how targeting mitochondria with antibiotics may increase lenvatinib responsiveness in K1 DTC cells. While the concentrations used exceed clinically achievable exposure and the study is limited to in vitro models, the data provide a rational basis for further support preclinical investigation of this approach, including validation in additional cell lines, in vivo efficacy and safety studies, and refined pharmacological assessment of clinically relevant dosing regimens.

Conclusions

We conclude that tetracyclines can sensitize K1 DTC cells to lenvatinib treatment. We further show that combination of lenvatinib with antibiotics induces mitochondrial dysfunction, ultimately leading to apoptosis in K1 DTC cells [49].
