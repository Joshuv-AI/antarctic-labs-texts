Lanadelumab in Hereditary Angioedema: Extending Dose Intervals from Every 2 Weeks to Every 4 Weeks
Authors: Marc A. Riedl, Daniel Soteres, Mark A. Davis-Lorton, Tara Piccolo, Matt Otule, Irmgard Andresen, Timothy Craig
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBoth clinical trial and real-world data demonstrate the effectiveness of lanadelumab long-term prophylaxis (LTP) to prevent hereditary angioedema (HAE) attacks, which may be maintained after dose interval extension from every 2 weeks (Q2W) to every 4 weeks (Q4W) in patients with well-controlled disease (attack free for >6 months).SummaryThis narrative review summarizes published data on lanadelumab treatment patterns collected from clinical trials and observational studies since US approval in 2018. Disease activity and HAE-related healthcare resource use at baseline and during the first 6 months of lanadelumab treatment may be useful indicators of patients with well-controlled disease who will be eligible for, and may derive benefit from, dose interval extension on lanadelumab. Patients who receive a dose interval extension could benefit from a reduction in treatment burden, but not all patients may be a good fit for interval extension.Key MessagesAlthough there are inherent limitations related to analyzing small sample sizes for subgroups of patients with a rare disease and for data derived from real-world studies, these data can inform physicians and patients about the characteristics and outcomes of patients who have successfully extended dosing intervals of lanadelumab LTP. This may in turn encourage regular monitoring to facilitate optimization of treatment and outcomes for each patient.

Introduction

Hereditary angioedema (HAE) is a rare disorder characterized by unpredictable and often painful swelling attacks that can affect the face, limbs, genitals, gastrointestinal tract, and upper airway [1]. HAE is commonly driven by mutations in the SERPING1 gene associated with reduced levels or dysfunction of the C1 esterase inhibitor (C1INH), resulting in HAE-C1INH-type1 or HAE-C1INH-type2, respectively [2]. C1INH normally functions to inhibit the kallikrein-kinin pathway, preventing excessive bradykinin production (plasma kallikrein breaks down high-molecular-weight kininogen [HMWK] to produce bradykinin and cleaved HMWK) [2, 3]. Impaired C1INH functioning leads to bradykinin-mediated vasodilation and increased endothelial permeability, resulting in angioedema [2, 4]. Available HAE treatments include on-demand therapy for acute attacks and short- (STP) or long-term prophylaxis (LTP) to reduce attack frequency, severity, and burden [1, 4]. Disease management guidelines in the United States (US) and the World Allergy Organization/European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology state that the goals of LTP for HAE are to achieve control of the disease and to normalize patients’ lives [1, 3]. In addition, patients on LTP should be routinely monitored for disease activity, impact, and control, to facilitate optimization of treatment dose regimens and outcomes [1, 3].

Lanadelumab is a fully human monoclonal antibody inhibitor of active plasma kallikrein indicated for LTP treatment of HAE in adult and pediatric patients in North America, Europe, and other regions; it is indicated in patients aged ≥2 years in the US and many other countries [5, 6]. Lanadelumab is administered as a subcutaneous injection and can be self-administered, although self-administration is not recommended in children (aged 2 to <12 years) [7]. Compared with orally administered medication for LTP that must be administered daily [8] or intravenous and subcutaneous C1 esterase inhibitor agents that require dosing every 3 or 4 days [9, 10], the long half-life of lanadelumab allows for less frequent dosing and, therefore, fewer opportunities to miss doses and experience breakthrough attacks [11]. In the US, the recommended starting dose of lanadelumab is 300 mg every 2 weeks (Q2W) in adults and adolescents (aged ≥12 years), 150 mg Q2W in children 6–<12 years of age, and 150 mg every 4 weeks (Q4W) in children 2–<6 years of age [6]. For US patients, dose interval extension from 300 mg Q2W to Q4W may be considered in adults and adolescents 12 years of age or older or from 150 mg Q2W to Q4W in children 6–<12 years of age with well-controlled disease (attack free for >6 months) [6]; however, not all patients with well-controlled disease extend dosing intervals [12], which may be due to a number of individual patient factors, including a fear of potential breakthrough attacks.

To assess lanadelumab effectiveness with extended dosing, pharmacokinetic data from a randomized clinical trial were used to develop a quantitative systems pharmacology model comparing the relative effectiveness of 300 mg Q2W and Q4W lanadelumab dosing for suppressing attacks in patients with HAE [11]. After 6 months of lanadelumab Q2W dosing, the dose interval was extended to Q4W, and a new steady state was reached approximately 70 days later. The resulting mean lanadelumab maximum concentration, but not minimum concentration, was above the 90% inhibitory concentration, mean cleaved HMWK (cHMWK) levels remained below 51%, and serum bradykinin concentrations were lower than the 0.02-nM threshold for risk of HAE attack. Although the model results indicated that the 300-mg Q2W regimen achieved the therapeutic goal of being attack free (fastest and greatest extent of cHMWK suppression over time, as well as the lowest average monthly attack rate relative to other dosing regimens), extension to Q4W dosing also provided significant HAE attack protection and may be appropriate for some patients according to individual treatment goals.

Extending lanadelumab dosing intervals from Q2W to Q4W may allow patients with well-controlled disease to maintain control while receiving less frequent injections. This has important implications for healthcare providers who manage the care of patients with HAE [13] and also consider treatment and cost burden. Collated data on the characteristics and treatment outcomes for patients who extend lanadelumab dosing intervals, including persistence on extended interval regimens, are needed to assist healthcare professionals and patients with making informed decisions about treatment plans. This narrative review is intended to provide a comprehensive synthesis of data on patients who have extended lanadelumab dosing from Q2W to Q4W to help physicians identify and manage patients with well-controlled HAE who may benefit from a lanadelumab dose extension.

Methodology

We conducted a search for clinical data on extended lanadelumab dosing to Q4W published in the scientific literature indexed in the PubMed database. The search included the terms “lanadelumab,” “DX-2930,” “Takhzyro,” or “SHP-643” covering the period from database inception to July 31, 2025. The search was limited to journal articles published in English, excluding pre-prints, review articles, meta-analyses, matching-adjusted indirect comparisons, editorial/commentary, congress abstracts, articles focused on the use of lanadelumab to treat conditions other than HAE-C1INH, and case reports from single patients. Search hits were reviewed, and relevant publications reporting data from studies of patients with HAE-C1INH treated with lanadelumab LTP were extracted. Full-text versions of publications were then reviewed to identify patient and disease characteristics, treatment patterns, and safety and efficacy data reported from patients with HAE on lanadelumab Q4W and from patients who extended dosing intervals from Q2W to Q4W during the study period.

We summarized data available from published studies reporting baseline characteristics and treatment outcomes from patients who extended lanadelumab dosing intervals from Q2W to Q4W, including children and adolescents. The reported outcomes focus on time to dose interval extension; proportion of patients who extended dosing intervals vs. those who remained on Q2W dosing; characteristics of patients who extended dosing intervals vs. those who remained on Q2W dosing; patient outcomes on Q4W dosing (including attack rates and attack severity, proportion of attack-free patients, and time to first attack); and duration on extended interval lanadelumab dosing.

Phase 3 Clinical Studies

Comparison of Q2W and Q4W Lanadelumab Dosing: Phase 3 HELP Study Data

The Hereditary Angioedema Long-term Prophylaxis (HELP) Study (NCT02586805) was a randomized placebo-controlled phase 3 study that provided efficacy and safety data on lanadelumab initiated as the label-specified starting dose regimen of Q2W or as Q4W dosing, compared across parallel groups of patients aged ≥12 years with HAE-C1INH over 26 weeks [14]. Patients completed a 4-week run-in period to assess baseline attack rates, which occurred after at least 2 weeks of wash-out for LTP therapy, as needed. As patients were randomly assigned to each treatment arm of the study, patient demographics and disease characteristics were balanced between lanadelumab Q2W and Q4W dosing groups, allowing for comparison of outcomes between study regimens (Table 1 and online suppl. Table S1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000551678). Attacks were self-reported to the study site. Patients receiving Q2W or Q4W lanadelumab dosing had significantly lower rates of HAE attacks per month, attacks requiring acute treatment, and moderate-to-severe attacks compared with placebo-treated patients. The mean (95% confidence interval [CI]) difference in model-based monthly attack rates for patients receiving lanadelumab 300 mg Q2W or Q4W vs. placebo was −1.71 (87% reduction vs. placebo; 95% CI = −2.09 to 1.33) and −1.44 (73% reduction vs. placebo; 95% CI = −1.84 to −1.04), respectively (p < 0.001). Additionally, a substantial proportion of patients who received lanadelumab Q2W or Q4W dosing remained attack free for the duration of the study (44% vs. 31%, respectively); only 2.4% of patients who received placebo were attack free. Patients treated with lanadelumab Q2W or Q4W also experienced statistically significant improvements in health-related quality of life (HRQoL), measured by change in the angioedema quality of life questionnaire (AE-QoL) total scores (least squares mean controlled for baseline scores Q2W: −21.3; Q4W: −17.4) compared with patients who received placebo (−4.7) [14].

Subgroup analyses of data from the HELP Study have been conducted by pre-specified categories of age, sex, race, body weight, body mass index (BMI), baseline run-in attack rate, HAE type, geographic region, prior LTP use, and history of laryngeal attacks [15]. For each lanadelumab dosing group, reductions in attack rates vs. placebo were similar across these different subgroups; no specific subgroups were identified with increased or decreased benefit, suggesting consistent lanadelumab efficacy across Q2W and Q4W dosing regimens in HELP Study patients. In an analysis of investigator-confirmed attack rates in the day 0–182 treatment period of the HELP Study (data from 27 patients on Q2W dosing, 29 on Q4W dosing, and 41 on placebo), Poisson regression models were developed to investigate attack rates in subgroups of baseline age, weight, HAE-C1NH type, and history of laryngeal attacks for each treatment group [15]. The model covariates included normalized attack rates for the baseline run-in period and treatment group, from which exploratory comparisons by subgroup and treatment group were derived and presented as mean rate ratios relative to placebo. Mean rate ratios for 300-mg Q2W and Q4W lanadelumab dosing were <1 for all subgroups (where ratio values of <1 indicate lower monthly attack rates with lanadelumab compared with placebo). In an ad hoc analysis evaluating time to onset of effect, lanadelumab rapidly reduced attack frequency vs. placebo in the Q2W and Q4W 300-mg lanadelumab dosing groups [16]. Attacks during the first 2 weeks of treatment decreased by 80.8% from baseline with Q2W dosing, 74.3% with Q4W dosing, and 37.6% in the placebo group.

The HELP open-label extension (OLE) Study (NCT02741596) included patients who completed the HELP Study and new participants who were followed for up to 33 months [17]. Patients from the HELP Study who continued treatment had a single 300-mg dose on day 0 (last visit [day 182] of the HELP Study), and treatment was then paused until the first investigator-confirmed attack occurred (dose and wait stage). For patients who received lanadelumab Q2W dosing previously in the HELP Study, the mean (95% CI) time to first attack was 76 (41–111) days compared with 53 (19–85) days for patients on Q4W dosing and 35 (26–45) days for patients who received placebo (online suppl. Table S1). After the first attack, patients who rolled over received lanadelumab 300 mg Q2W for the remainder of the study [17]. For the overall HELP OLE study period, mean monthly attack rates were reduced by 87.4% from a baseline of 3.05 to 0.25 attacks per month. Mean ± standard deviation (SD) duration of lanadelumab exposure was 2.5 ± 0.7 years. Most (81.6%) patients completed ≥30 months (2.5 years) in the study. Data from the HELP studies reflected the use of lanadelumab initiated as Q2W or Q4W dosing, respectively, without intrapatient dosing interval extension; with this in mind, the data demonstrated that although results were numerically lower compared with lanadelumab 300 mg Q2W dosing for most efficacy outcomes, extended interval Q4W dosing also reduced HAE attack rate and severity and improved HRQoL significantly vs. placebo [17, 18].

Dose Interval Extension Q2W to Q4W in Japanese Patients Aged ≥12 Years: Phase 3 Study Data

The SHP643-302 phase 3 study (NCT04180163) in Japanese patients with HAE aged ≥12 years was a single-group, multicenter, open-label study with one investigator-confirmed HAE attack during the 4-week run-in period, which occurred after a wash-out period of at least 2 weeks [19]. Attacks were self-reported by patients within 72 h of onset. The efficacy and safety of lanadelumab 300-mg LTP treatment was evaluated for 52 weeks. Dosing could be extended to Q4W in the second 26-week treatment period if patients had well-controlled disease for 6 months. Of the 12 patients who were treated with Q2W dosing during the first 26 weeks, 4 extended dosing intervals to Q4W and 8 remained on Q2W dosing for the second 26 weeks. There were no notable differences in monthly attack rates in these 4 patients before and after the change from Q2W to Q4W dosing (online suppl. Table S1). The mean ± SD monthly attack rate for the overall 52-week lanadelumab treatment period in patients who extended Q2W to Q4W on study decreased by 96.8% during Q2W dosing and by 94.1% during Q4W dosing from 3.0 ± 0.9 at baseline and by 74.0% from 3.8 ± 2.4 at baseline in all lanadelumab-treated patients (n = 12). One patient who extended dosing intervals to Q4W dosing changed back to Q2W due to new HAE attacks and then remained attack free until the end of the study.

Dose Interval Extension in Children Aged 6–<12 Years: Phase 3 SPRING Study Data

The SPRING Study (NCT04070326) included 21 pediatric patients aged 2–<12 years with HAE-C1INH who were divided into 2 age-groups: 2–<6 years (n = 4) and 6–<12 years (n = 17) [20]. Prior to receiving treatment, patients completed a 4- to 12-week LTP-free observation period to establish baseline data. Patients aged 2–<6 years then received fixed dosing with 150 mg lanadelumab Q4W. Patients aged 6–<12 years received a fixed dose of 150 mg lanadelumab Q2W for the first 26 weeks and were offered the option to extend dosing intervals to Q4W during the second 26-week study period if they were attack free during the first 26 weeks. For the first 26 weeks, the mean attack rate was reduced by 93.8% for the entire study population compared with baseline. Attack prevention efficacy did not appear to be affected by patient age or weight. The mean attack rate was reduced from baseline by 96.4% in patients aged 2 to <6 years and 94.4% in patients aged 6–<12 years. The mean attack rate was reduced from baseline by 95.1% and 94.2% among subgroups of healthy and overweight patients (n = 14) or obese patients (n = 7), respectively.

Seven of 17 patients aged 6–<12 years extended dosing intervals from Q2W to Q4W [20]. All 7 patients remained attack free during the second 26-week period. One patient in the 2- – <6-year age-group turned 6 years old during the study and extended dosing intervals from Q2W to Q4W and then returned to Q2W dosing due to recurrent attacks. Efficacy outcomes were similar across Q2W and Q4W dosing groups (online suppl. Table S1) and comparable with attack rate reduction achieved in adolescent patients aged 12–17 years treated with lanadelumab 300 mg Q2W in the HELP and HELP OLE studies (n = 21) [21]. The mean ± SD HAE attack rate was reduced by 94.7% from 1.58 ± 1.0 attacks per month at baseline to 0.11 ± 0.2 at the end of the study in these adolescent patients from HELP and 8/21 remained attack free (38.1%; two dose and wait rollover patients and six lanadelumab-naive patients receiving Q2W dosing) [21]. Not all patients who qualified for dose interval extension after being attack free for 26 weeks in the SPRING Study had extension [20]. Reducing the frequency of injections and/or physician visits for injections by extending dosing intervals in children, where indicated, could alleviate some of the treatment burden for patients and their caregivers [7] and, consequently, may also lead to improved treatment compliance and disease control. These findings support the efficacy of extending lanadelumab dosing intervals from 150 mg Q2W to Q4W in patients aged 6–<12 years whose disease is well controlled for >6 months.

Phase 4 Non-Interventional Studies

Dose Interval Extension in Patients Aged ≥12 Years: Phase 4 Retrospective Real-World INTEGRATED Study Data

The INTEGRATED Study (NCT04861090) is the largest non-interventional, observational study of lanadelumab effectiveness conducted to date. Patients aged ≥12 years with HAE-C1INH who initiated lanadelumab LTP at 19 centers in France, Germany, Austria, and Greece between August 2018 and May 2021 were included in this retrospective chart review and followed for up to 44 months on treatment [22]. The primary endpoint was the attack-free rate (proportion of patients with zero attacks), which was also analyzed in patients who extended lanadelumab dosing intervals. Of the 198 patients studied, most 144 (72.7%) extended dosing intervals during follow-up, but the treatment pattern varied between countries. The mean ± SD time to dose interval extension was 8.2 ± 6.1 months. The most frequent reason reported for dose interval extension was that the patient remained in a stable attack-free state (119/144; 82.6%) or their HAE improved or was “sufficiently maintained” (28/144; 19.4%). Attack-free rates improved from no patients being attack free (0%) before lanadelumab to a cumulative attack-free rate of 72/144 (50.0%) for the cohort with any extension of lanadelumab dosing intervals and 76.2% for patients (n = 25) who extended intervals from Q2W to Q4W vs. 61.2% for patients who remained on Q2W dosing (n = 54). For patients with any extension vs. Q2W dosing, mean ± SD time to first attack after lanadelumab initiation was 34.0 ± 34.4 vs. 25.3 ± 37.6 weeks and median (interquartile range) duration on lanadelumab treatment was 29.0 (22.2–35.8) vs. 26.6 (16.9–35.6) months, respectively.

A comparison of baseline characteristics between patients with vs. without dose interval extension showed that the extension cohort had a lower mean number of attacks during the 12 months prior to lanadelumab initiation (34.1 vs. 40.6, respectively) and a lower mean weight and BMI (79.3 kg and 27.2 kg/m3 vs. 82.4 kg and 28.3 kg/m3, respectively). The proportion with HAE-C1INH-type1 was 93.8% vs. 87.0%, and mean age was 44.0 vs. 41.7 years, respectively (Table 2). In total, 35 patients (24.3%) from the extended dosing interval group had a subsequent dose interval decrease, most (24/35) due to breakthrough HAE attacks. Comparing attack characteristics in 66/144 (45.8%) patients with dose interval extension with an attack, 55 (38.2%) patients had an attack requiring on-demand treatment vs. 34/54 (63.0%) of patients who remained on Q2W dosing. Attacks were mild in 28 (19.4%) vs. 22 (40.7%) patients, moderate in 39 (27.1%) vs. 25 (46.3%) patients, severe in 14 (9.7%) vs. 12 (22.2%) patients, and life threatening in 0 vs. 2 (3.7%) patients, respectively. For 66 patients in the extended dosing interval group with data on attack location, commonly affected areas were the abdomen (45 patients [68%]), peripheral extremities (21 [31.8%]), face (12 [18.2%]), and larynx (3 [4.5%]), comparable with data from 37 patients in the Q2W dosing group where attacks commonly affected the abdomen (33 [89.2%]), peripheral area (15 [40.5%]), face (9 [24.3%]), and larynx (4 [10.8%]). This study demonstrated that lanadelumab dose interval extension was common and often effective in routine clinical practice, with half of the patients achieving attack-free status during long-term treatment. However, some patients did not continue with extended dosing interval regimens due to the occurrence of breakthrough HAE attacks. This shows that it is important to identify patient characteristics that can help predict which patients will benefit most from a dose interval extension.

Dose Interval Extension in Patients Aged ≥12 Years: Phase 4 Prospective Real-World EMPOWER Study Data

The EMPOWER Study (NCT03845400) was a phase 4 prospective, non-interventional study evaluating the real-world effectiveness and safety of lanadelumab LTP in patients aged ≥12 years with HAE-C1INH in the US and Canada for up to 36 months of treatment [23]. Patients were considered “newly treated” or “established” on lanadelumab if they had received less than four or four or more lanadelumab doses at enrollment, respectively. A total of 77 patients received lanadelumab 300 mg Q2W dosing, 13 received Q4W dosing, and 18 received extended dosing intervals on study from Q2W to Q4W (4 newly treated and 14 established on lanadelumab). Baseline characteristics for patients who remained on Q2W dosing compared with those who extended from Q2W to Q4W, respectively, were mostly similar: mean age: 41.4 vs. 35.1 years; female: 66.2% vs. 61.1%; white: 92.2% vs. 94.4%; HAE-C1INH-type1: 84.4% vs. 77.8%; and mean body weight and BMI: 90.7 kg and 32.0 kg/m3 vs. 82.4 kg and 29.4 kg/m3. Patients in the Q4W dosing group had a mean age of 41.2 years, 76.9% were female, 100% were white, 53.8% had HAE-C1INH-type1, and mean body weight and BMI were 81.4 kg and 27.4 kg/m3, respectively (online suppl. Table S2). For the 4 newly treated patients who extended from Q2W to Q4W dosing on study, the mean ± SD number of HAE attacks observed for each time period per patient was 2.3 ± 3.2 in the pre-lanadelumab period, decreasing to 0.5 ± 1.0 during Q2W dosing, 0 during Q4W dosing, and 0.5 ± 1.0 for the cumulative post-lanadelumab period, with a mean ± SD of 644.0 ± 181.9 days of lanadelumab exposure per patient. For the 14 newly treated patients who remained on Q2W dosing, mean ± SD number of attacks pre-lanadelumab was 12.6 ± 13.8 and 4.6 ± 7.5 for the cumulative period, with a mean ± SD of 537.5 ± 344.6 days of lanadelumab exposure per patient. For the 14 patients established on lanadelumab at enrollment who extended from Q2W to Q4W dosing, the mean ± SD number of attacks per patient was 0.1 ± 0.4 and 0.3 ± 0.6 for the Q2W and Q4W dosing periods, respectively, and 0.6 ± 0.8 for the cumulative period (mean ± SD days lanadelumab exposure per patient: 752.4 ± 321.3). This was compared with a mean ± SD of 4.6 ± 7.2 and 3.1 ± 5.5 attacks observed for the cumulative period in established patients who remained on Q2W dosing (n = 60) and Q4W dosing (n = 12), respectively; mean ± SD lanadelumab exposure per patient was 775.4 ± 354.2 and 865.3 ± 233.9 days, respectively. Mean monthly attack rates were low regardless of dosing frequency in the study. Observed mean (95% CI) attacks per month for the cumulative period in patients newly treated and established on lanadelumab, respectively, were 0.25 (0.02–0.48) and 0.25 (0.11–0.39) for patients who remained on Q2W dosing, 0.03 (−0.07 to 0.14) and 0.04 (0.00–0.07 for patients who extended dosing from Q2W to Q4W, and 0.09 (−0.01 to 0.19) for patients established on Q4W dosing. Data from the EMPOWER Study demonstrated that patients treated in real-world practice with well-controlled disease who extend dosing to Q4W can continue to experience low attack rates after dose interval extension for up to 36 months.

Dose Interval Extension in Adolescents Aged 12–<18 Years: Pooled Phase 4 EMPOWER and ENABLE Study Data

The ENABLE Study (NCT04130191) was a non-interventional, 3-year, multicenter, prospective study that evaluated the effectiveness and safety of lanadelumab LTP in patients with HAE treated in real-world practice in Europe and the Middle East. Adolescent patient data pooled from the prospective, non-interventional real-world EMPOWER and ENABLE studies indicated a mean ± SD attack rate reduction of 84.2% in 13 adolescent patients newly treated with lanadelumab 300 mg Q2W (mean ± SD lanadelumab treatment duration of 680 ± 291.5 days) [24]. Seven adolescent patients already established on lanadelumab treatment (mean ± SD duration of 947.7 ± 87.0 days) had a mean ± SD monthly attack rate of 0.04 ± 0.03. Five of 20 adolescent patients extended lanadelumab dosing intervals on study, 3 directly to Q4W dosing, 1 to Q3W dosing, and 1 transitioned to Q4W dosing after an interim Q3W period, reflecting real-world practice among adolescent patients.

Other Real-World Studies on Lanadelumab Dosing Extension

A retrospective medical chart review (2018−2023) was conducted to evaluate the characteristics and treatment patterns of patients aged ≥12 years with HAE-C1INH for whom dosing intervals were extended after initiation of lanadelumab 300 mg Q2W [12]. Physicians identified 186 patients with ≥3 months of data on lanadelumab from US treatment centers, 75 of whom extended lanadelumab dosing intervals from Q2W. Most patients extended dosing intervals to Q4W (65/75; 86.7%). The most common reason was well-controlled disease achieved with Q2W dosing in 74/75 (98.7%) patients. The median time to dose interval extension was 6.5 (95% CI = 5.1–7.4) months. Most patients who increased dosing intervals did not reduce intervals back to Q2W. During the post-index period, 4/75 (5.3%) patients had an HAE attack on extended interval lanadelumab dosing compared with 56 (77.8%) in the pre-index period and 7/72 (9.7%) on initial Q2W dosing. Reduced attack frequency was maintained with lanadelumab dose interval extension. Healthcare resource use was also reduced after lanadelumab initiation compared with the pre-index period. Hospitalization and physician office visits, respectively, were reduced from 31.1% and 53.2% pre-index to 3.3% and 23.4% on Q2W dosing and to 0% and 20.0% with dose interval extension.

Patients with dose interval extension had a mean ± SD age of 32.7 ± 12.3 years, age at symptom onset of 21.2 ± 11.4 years, and age at HAE diagnosis of 29.1 ± 13.8 years [12]. Approximately half the patients were female (56%) and had no family history of HAE (54.7%). The most common comorbidities in the pre-index period were anxiety/depression (26.7%) and hypertension (13.3%). These characteristics were broadly similar to the overall patient cohort (n = 186). The proportion of patients with HAE-C1INH-type1 vs. HAE-C1INH-type2 was slightly lower (72.0% vs. 28.0%) in patients with a dose interval extension than in the overall cohort (90.3% vs. 9.7%). Of the 65 patients with an extension to Q4W dosing, 63 patients (84.0%) continued on Q4W dosing while the dose interval was extended further (off label) in 2 patients. Patients with a dose interval extension were maintained on lanadelumab treatment, as shown by 61/75 (81.3%) patients remaining on lanadelumab treatment at 12 months and 35/75 (46.7%) remaining on lanadelumab 24 months after treatment initiation.

A retrospective observational study using US healthcare claims data (Merative™ MarketScan® Commercial and Medicare Databases) investigated patients who were receiving lanadelumab LTP for ≥18 months [25]. Patients were included if they had one or more claim for lanadelumab treatment (index) between January 1, 2018, and June 30, 2022 (with only a ≤60-day gap in lanadelumab supply over 18 months), and were enrolled for ≥6 months pre-index (baseline period) and ≥18 months post-index. Dose interval extension by reduced frequency of lanadelumab administration was evidenced by a ≥25% decrease in lanadelumab costs during months 7–12 or 13–18 on lanadelumab LTP (25/54; 46.3%) compared with months 0–6. A further analysis compared the demographic and clinical characteristics of patients with (n = 25) vs. without (n = 29) evidence of dose interval extension [13]. Those with evidence of dose interval extension had a lower mean ± SD age (40.9 ± 15.3 vs. 43.0 ± 15.7 years), a lower baseline comorbidity burden as measured by the mean ± SD Deyo Charlson Comorbidity Index score (0.5 ± 1.2 vs. 0.8 ± 1.9), a higher proportion of males (44.0% vs. 37.9%), and a lower proportion of patients who initiated lanadelumab treatment immediately after its approval in 2018 (8.0% vs. 37.9%). In addition, patients with evidence of dose interval extension experienced fewer HAE attack symptoms and triggers during the 6 months before lanadelumab initiation (pre-index baseline period) compared with patients who did not extend dosing intervals. They also had fewer pre- and post-index inpatient admissions related to HAE, and a lower proportion had claims for acute/STP treatment pre- and post-index. In the pre-index period, patients with evidence of dose interval extension had lower costs for acute/STP, supportive care, and LTP, as well as lower overall mean HAE treatment costs. This suggests an overall lower baseline severity and/or burden of disease for patients with HAE who extended dosing intervals.

An analysis from the Adelphi HAE Disease Specific Programme™, a real-world, cross-sectional survey of physicians and patients with HAE in the US, evaluated treatment patterns and continuation rates for patients receiving lanadelumab [26]. A total of 37 physicians reported data on 109 patients who initiated lanadelumab and remained on treatment at the time of data collection. Most patients initiated lanadelumab 300 mg on Q2W dosing (93.6%). At the time of data collection, 34.9% of patients received Q4W dosing. Most patients extended dosing intervals from Q2W to Q4W after 4–6 months (47.2%) or ≥12 months of treatment (27.8%). No patients extended dosing intervals prior to 4 months of lanadelumab treatment. Physicians reported improvements in attack frequency in 70.0% of patients receiving Q4W dosing and improvements in attack severity in 73.3% compared with pre-lanadelumab baseline.

A single-center retrospective chart review, compiled by physician authors from the United Arab Emirates, Bahrain, and Germany, reported the effectiveness of lanadelumab in 9 patients with HAE by dosing regimen (Q2W, n = 5; Q4W, n = 4) [27]. Patients were assigned to their dose regimen group based on the number of HAE attacks per month, with a cutoff of four attacks per month. Of the 5 patients who initiated lanadelumab on Q2W dosing, 2 patients were attack free at the end of the study and three had a dose interval extension to Q4W dosing due to well-controlled disease (1 patient reverted back to Q2W dosing). There was a significant reduction in the number of HAE attacks from baseline for patients from both dose regimen groups (Q4W, p = 0.03; Q2W, p = 0.01).

Additional real-world studies have reported dose interval extension in lanadelumab-treated patients, although not all dose regimens were based on label guidance. A real-world, observational study investigated the use of lanadelumab for up to 4 years in 30 patients with HAE-C1INH at the Angioedema Center of Reference and Excellence in Germany [28]. In this study, the dosing protocol did not use a fixed 14-day (Q2W) interval. Instead, the second dose was administered by patients 14 days after the first dose, and, if no symptoms were reported, then injection intervals were extended by 3 days for the next injection. If an attack occurred, the next injection interval was shortened to the previous interval that had achieved attack-free status [29]. Patients with HAE C1-INH (n = 28) reported a median (range) interval of 35 (14–90) days, indicating effective treatment achieved with lanadelumab extended dosing intervals in these patients [28]. Patients on study also had improvements in HRQoL, as indicated by a 2-point decrease in the AE-QoL total score from baseline [28].

A retrospective longitudinal analysis based on anonymized HAE prescription data in Germany was conducted from January 2018 until June 2020 [30]. All patients with at least one prescription of lanadelumab from January 2019 were included, with the index date being the first lanadelumab prescription date and the previous 12-month period defined as baseline. Dose intervals were calculated using the number of prescribed vials from one prescription divided by the period of time to the following prescription. A total of 106 and 44 patients had received lanadelumab for ≥4 and ≥12 months, respectively. Of the 106 patients with ≥4 months of treatment, 33 (31%) used lanadelumab with extended dosing intervals (one vial/>17 days). This rate increased to 48% (n = 21/44) in patients with ≥12 months of lanadelumab treatment. The mean time until dose interval extension was >3 months (110 days) in patients with ≥4 months of treatment and >4 months (144 days) in patients with ≥12 months of treatment.

Data from the Hungarian HAE Registry (1979–2023) were used to assess the effectiveness and safety of LTP therapies, including lanadelumab, among Hungarian patients [31]. In total, 8 patients in the registry had received lanadelumab at the time of the analysis. Of these, 2 patients were receiving lanadelumab 300 mg Q2W, 5 were receiving 300 mg Q4W, and 1 stopped treatment after 4 months because the frequency and severity of attacks did not decrease. Five patients with no attacks or low attack frequency extended dosing intervals from Q2W to Q4W after 6 months of treatment. After 20 months of follow-up, the mean monthly attack rate for the 7 patients who remained on treatment decreased from 4.45 to 0.63 attacks per month, with 3 patients who were symptom free, 1 who had 1 attack, and one who had 2 attacks. The longest mean symptom-free duration for the 7 lanadelumab-treated patients increased from 29.3 to 438.6 days, and patients experienced improvements in QoL on treatment. The cohort included a patient aged 8 years who initiated lanadelumab 150 mg Q2W in the SPRING Study and whose monitoring continued through the post-trial access program [31]. Lanadelumab dosing intervals were extended to Q4W in this patient and then slowly increased further to every 7 weeks without breakthrough attacks until just before the patient’s twelfth birthday, when he experienced three attacks in 3 weeks and was returned to Q4W dosing. He was then changed to 300 mg Q4W lanadelumab at 12 years of age when he became eligible for the higher dose.

Lanadelumab LTP treatment patterns were reported for 77 patients with HAE-C1INH in a real-world study of patients from the French Authorization for Temporary Use program from October 2018 to March 2019, of whom 11 patients had a dose interval extension [32]. Patient and disease history data were collected from patient’s charts, and on-treatment clinical data were collected prospectively during routine visits up to September 2019. All patients started on lanadelumab 300 mg Q2W. At the first follow-up visit approximately 3 months after initiation, 2 patients had their dosing frequency extended to Q4W. At the second follow-up visit 6 months after initiation, 6 patients received Q4W dosing and 4 received other dosing frequencies. Lanadelumab lowered attack rates in all patients compared with rates prior to treatment, and 66% of patients were attack free for the first 6 months of treatment [32].

Conclusion

This narrative review highlights data published from clinical trials and real-world studies about patients who extended lanadelumab dosing intervals from Q2W to Q4W during the study period. The HELP Study and its open-label extension established the efficacy of lanadelumab initiated as Q4W dosing vs. placebo in patients for long-term prevention of HAE attacks and demonstrated that treatment outcomes, such as monthly attack rates, proportion of attack-free patients, and time to first attack, were similar, though numerically lower, to those achieved in patients receiving Q2W dosing [14]. It should be noted that Q2W, not Q4W, is the starting dose regimen specified in the product label [6]. Where data were available from large studies of routine clinical practice, average time to dose interval extension from lanadelumab 300 mg Q2W dosing ranged 6.5–8.2 months. Study data also showed that extending lanadelumab dosing intervals was relatively common among lanadelumab-treated patients with HAE (up to 73% of patients in one study [22]). Dose interval extension was also reported in 7 children (in a cohort of 17, aged 6 to <12 years) who remained attack free for 26 weeks on study after extension from 150 mg Q2W to Q4W dosing and 5 in a cohort of 20 adolescent patients aged 12–<18 years [20, 24]. In most studies, the majority of patients who extended dosing intervals did not revert back to more frequent Q2W dosing. Patients who reverted to Q2W from Q4W generally did so due to breakthrough HAE attacks. In the phase 4 EMPOWER Study, attack rates in patients who extended dosing from Q2W to Q4W were similar to those in patients receiving Q2W dosing [23].

Studies with data separated for extender and non-extender subgroups (Table 2) [13, 22, 23] appeared to indicate that patients who remained on Q2W dosing without extending dosing intervals had evidence of higher baseline disease activity, including higher baseline attack rates and more frequent HAE-related healthcare encounters, as well as a higher comorbidity burden compared with patients who were able to extend dosing intervals. There were also differences noted for demographic features, such as slightly lower mean body weight and BMI for extenders vs. patients remaining on Q2W dosing. Some studies indicated a higher proportion of males to females and a lower mean age in extender vs. non-extender patients. Even though no statistical testing has been performed to establish a link between patient or disease characteristics and successful lanadelumab dose interval extension, disease activity and HAE-related healthcare resource use at baseline and during the first 6 months of lanadelumab treatment could still be useful indicators of patients with well-controlled disease who may be eligible for, and benefit from, dose interval extension on lanadelumab. However, dosing regimens should be individualized based on response to treatment and patient preferences as some patients may require Q2W dosing for optimal disease control.

As with all studies on rare diseases, there are limitations to this narrative review related to the analysis of small sample sizes for subgroups of patients and an inherent selection bias for data derived from real-world studies. Additionally, as this was a narrative review without the rigorous search methodology used in systematic reviews, our searches may not have identified all relevant articles on lanadelumab dosing extension due to exclusion of articles published in languages other than English, review articles, meta-analyses, data published at scientific congresses, and publications not available via PubMed. There was also no risk-of-bias evaluation due to the nature of the review; therefore, there was a potential for bias in the search, selection, and data extraction processes. In addition, it is important to note that published research on extended dosing intervals may be biased toward patients who perform well and may limit reporting of patients who revert back to Q2W dosing. This review reinforces the need for individualized HAE treatment plans as Q2W dosing may be necessary for optimal control of HAE symptoms in some patients. Aggregating these data is useful in assisting physicians with shared decision-making with patients who may benefit from dosing interval adjustments on lanadelumab and may also encourage regular monitoring to facilitate optimization of treatment and outcomes for each patient.
