Literature citation and writer identity construction in linguistic academic discourse
Authors: Wan Liu, Yi Zhang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study revealed the distributional features of literature citation and writer identity in linguistic academic discourse, and further explored how writer identity is constructed through literature citation. Based on Petrić’s citation typology and Ivanič’s writer identity framework, the study investigated various types of citations and identities in thirty journal articles published in the Journal of English for Academic Purposes from 2017 to 2021. The results showed that there are a total of 1637 citation instances, among which the dominant type is attribution citation (36.59 %). Besides, the study revealed that 80.45 % of the total citations construct discoursal self only and 19.55 % reveal both discoursal self and authorial self. In terms of discoursal self, writers present themselves as a member of academic community, a contributor to a field of knowledge, a knowledgeable and professional scholar, and a reliable and credible writer. As for authorial self, they would like to position themselves to be a writer with authority and an evaluator. It is expected that the current study can help novice writers use citations strategically and establish their desired identity accordingly.Keywords: Literature citation, Writer identity construction, Linguistic academic discourse

Introduction

The construction of writer identity in academic discourse has long been considered essential [1,2] as what we write is a reflection of selves and the kind of relationship we want to build with readers. According to Burgess and Ivanič [3], writer identity is defined as the various identities available in academic contexts and the different selves writers create by choosing specific language features in their texts. By adopting a range of linguistic devices, writers attempt to build an authoritative, credible and professional image and thus gain acceptance of discourse community [4].

A wealth of studies has been conducted to explore identity in academic writings. A noteworthy study by Ivanič [2] put forward a conceptual framework comprising four aspects of writer identity: autobiographical self, discoursal self, authorial self and possibilities for self-hood. Extending her study, many scholars began to examine various linguistic devices for writer identity construction, among which first-person pronouns have attracted widespread attention [1,[4], [5], [6], [7]], as it is considered to be an explicit linguistic signal of authorial presence. In addition to this device, researchers have also discussed identity construction from other perspectives such as metadiscourse markers [8,9], metaphors [10], rhetorical moves and process types [11]. However, it seems that compared with other linguistic devices, especially first-person pronouns, citation operates less explicitly in constructing writer identity and has been paid less attention.

Citation, a rhetorical behavior of writing from sources, plays a crucial role in persuasion as it establishes intellectual linkages, provides justification for arguments, permits writers to evaluate previous studies and demonstrates alignment with particular scholarship [12,13]. Prior research has investigated various aspects of citations, including their textual characteristics like density [12,14,15], formal structure and syntactic position [16,17] and rhetorical functions [18,19]. Additionally, studies have explored the writer dimensions of citations such as stance-taking when citing others' works [[20], [21], [22], [23]], as well as their roles in shaping different identities [24]. For instance, in Zhang's latest study [25], she employed Martin and White's Appraisal theory [26], a development of the interpersonal metafunction theorized by systemic functional linguistics, to explore stance-related citation patterns in engineering research articles, comparing doctoral students' and experienced researchers' practices, and found that students displayed a more limited range of strategies compared to experts. In addition, Peng [22] mentioned that writers often blend statements of averral and attribution [27], i.e., their own voice and other researchers' voice, and make the formal more prominent by employing more evaluative reporting verbs, non-integral citations, and generalizations to construct an authoritative identity.

Novice writers often struggle to differentiate their own voice from that of others in source texts, resulting in inappropriate borrowing or citation [28]. Furthermore, research suggests that this improper textual appropriation can be traced back to their inability to establish their own authorial identity as they lack awareness of their identity as authors to present their own opinions, values and beliefs [29,30]. Consequently, they face challenges in correctly and effectively employing citations and constructing favorable identities, despite their crucial role in achieving academic success. As Harwood & Petrić rightly pointed out, the appropriate mastery of citations can help writers improve their professional image and reputation [31]. For instance, Liu and Deng's study showed that student writers' citation practices, such as manipulating the quantity and pertinence of their citations, establish an intelligent and trustworthy identity [24]. A recent study conducted by Alramadan draws on Coffin's model that conceptualizes categories of textual integration, the nature of the source, writer stance within the framework of the engagement sub-system of the appraisal theory, examines how writers alter their citation practices to construct different identities when writing in English as a foreign language, taking their cultural background and audience awareness into account [32].

To summarize, current studies demonstrate that insufficient and ineffective citations can be regarded as writers' weak sense of identity construction while strategic use of citation enables writers to construct an acceptable and appropriate identity [29,30]. Nowadays, novice writers, especially in China, may consider citations are simply “repeating others' sayings” and avoiding plagiarism [33]. Students in their primary schools and high schools were often taught to use expressions of famous scholars without citing them and without distinguishing between different viewpoints with their own opinions in their writing and thus failed to construct a proper identity. To date, there is a limited amount of research that has concentrated on literature citation and writer identity constructed through it concurrently. Therefore, it is worthwhile to understand the role of citation and further investigate the relationship between citation with identity. Moreover, many of the studies on identity construction have adopted the qualitative method, and few have examined the distributional features of expert writers’ various identities through citations in a quantitative method, which deserves more attention.

As argued above, identity construction is considered a thorny challenge for novice writers. Additionally, the association between citation practices and writer identity construction has been largely underexplored, notwithstanding the fruitful research on citation or identity construction. This study aims to explore the features of literature citation and writer identity in linguistic academic discourse, trying to find out how expert writers construct their identities through this linguistic resource. It would make a contribution to academic English writing by advancing a deeper understanding of how different identities are constructed via citations, facilitating novice writers' awareness of citation roles and better promoting their academic views. The study would also shed some light on informing English for Academic Purposes (EAP) instruction and can further provide important implications for enhancing students’ citation competence.

Establishment of the corpus

To answer the above-mentioned questions, a corpus of 30 empirical journal articles (JAs) in the area of linguistics published within the past 5 years (2017–2021) in the esteemed Journal of English for Academic Purposes was compiled. The Journal, established in 2002, holds an SSCI indexing and achieved an impact factor of 2.811 in 2022 according to Clarivate Analytics. This signifies the JAs’ overall exceptional quality and authoritative nature. Furthermore, our research specifically pertains to the field of linguistics since our research focus is not on disciplinary differences. Limiting the disciplinary scope allows us to concentrate on our research objectives effectively, while our expertise in linguistics facilitates data coding and analysis processes.

To ensure the corpus's timeliness and representativeness, a random selection process was employed to obtain six journal articles (JAs) per year from 2017 to 2021. These JAs were acquired directly from the website (www.sciencedirect.com) in PDF format and subsequently converted into TXT format for further data coding. Following previous studies [12,14,15,22], certain elements such as abstracts, figures, tables, acknowledgments, footnotes, endnotes, author notes, reference lists, and appendices were excluded from the selected articles. After the text-trimming process, the total number of tokens in the corpus amounted to 211,855, with an average article length of 7,062 tokens.

Analytical framework

Literature citation

Numerous classifications have been proposed in literature citation studies, encompassing surface forms [34], rhetorical functions [19,35], roles of cited authors [36], presentation of cited work [12], reporting verbs and tenses [12,34], as well as writer stance [20,25]. The present study adopts Petrić’s typology [19] as the analytical framework given the focus on the rhetorical functions of citations. This function-based typology was chosen over others because it effectively captured the rhetorical and communicative purposes of citations in EAP studies, allowing for a comprehensive exploration of diverse identities among expert writers. Derived from previous work and applied for the analysis of academic writing citations, Petrić’s typology [19] was deemed suitable and practical, offering a more fine-grained categorization of citation functions and proving valuable in distinguishing different citation purposes.

The typology is comprised of nine types: attribution, exemplification, further reference, statement of use, application, evaluation, establishing links between sources, comparison of one's own findings or interpretation with other sources, and other. As per Petrić [19], the final category “other” pertains to instances where the connection between the citing proposition and its source is unclear or ambiguous. Considering its rarity of occurrence in professional academic writings, this type was not utilized in the present study. To avoid confusion between the terms “author” and “writer”, we have adopted Thompson and Ye's distinction [23], wherein “author” refers to the individual whose work is being cited, while “writer” denotes the person incorporating citations into their writing. Writer identity construction

To examine writers' various identities, we utilized Ivanič’s seminal framework of writer identity [2]. Compared with alternative classifications of writer identity [1,6], which primarily rely on first-person pronouns, Ivanič’s framework is not restricted to certain linguistic resources. It enjoys wide acceptance and frequent utilization within the academic community [24,30,37]. As mentioned earlier, this conceptual framework consists of four aspects: autobiographical self, discoursal self, authorial self, and possibilities of selfhood. Out of these four facets, the autobiographical self is linked to one's individual history and previous experiences, including academic background, cultural beliefs, and dispositions. Possibilities of selfhood refer to social resources that shape and constrain writers to construct appropriate identity in socio-cultural context. However, the above two aspects were not taken into consideration given the study's primary emphasis on textual analysis, specifically examining the discoursal self and authorial self. The following provides detailed explanations of these two aspects.

(1)Discoursal self

Discoursal self

The discoursal self refers to the image or perception a writer portrays of themselves in written form, which is shaped by the discourse features of the text, as described by Ivanič [2]. It is related to Goffman's differentiation between “writer-as-character” and “writer-as-performer” [38]. The discoursal self, akin to the “writer-as-character”, represents the constructed identity of the “writer-as-performer” within a text. In essence, it pertains to how writers aim to present themselves through both discursive and non-discursive elements. This study specifically concentrates on the citation functional features employed in academic writing and attempts to investigate what discoursal identity these citations portray.

(2)Authorial self

Authorial self

The authorial self encompasses how writers perceive themselves as authors. It involves “the writer's voice” in the sense of their stance, viewpoints, and convictions [2]. This facet of identity holds importance as writers distinguish themselves from each other in their authority over the text's content and their authorial presence [2]. Some writers assert their responsibility and exhibit a strong authorial stance in presenting their writing, while others attribute most of the ideas to external authorities, playing safe but lacking a distinct authorial identity [22,30]. In our analysis, we discuss how writers establish authority for the content of their writing, and how they establish an authorial presence, through the various citation choices they make.

Data processing

Data coding

The 30 empirical JAs were input to the UAM corpus tool (version 3.3) [39] for repeated reading and coding citation features and writer identity. Following previous scholars' practice [19,22,23,40,41], instances of citations were identified and counted as such: (1) the canonical citation form (the author's name followed by a year of publication in parentheses), its variants (personal pronouns or possessive forms referring to previously cited authors), and citation forms like “ibid.” were identified and counted; (2) if a single source was cited in one parenthesis, it was counted as a single citation. And if multiple independent sources occurred in one parenthesis, it was still counted as one citation; (3) citations without an actual date were not included in the count; (4) second-hand citations were treated as a single citation since they referred to the same source; (5) our study focused on literature citations, excluding references to instruments and statistical methods, quotations from research participants, and producers of corpora.

Based on the definitions and identifications mentioned above, we assigned each citation its corresponding type and the writer identity it embodies. To code the data for the target citation features, we follow previous studies' methods [14] where each citation was assigned a single, most salient functional type due to software limitations. Determining the specific type of citation was relatively complicated and involved subjective judgment. As Samraj pointed out [35], each citation type is determined by an evaluation of not only the propositions in which citations appear but also the neighboring sentences. Accordingly, we read and analyzed carefully each cited proposition and its co-texts to make the possible best judgment. It is worth mentioning that while citations are generally attributions by definition, in this study we followed Petrić’s method [19] that a citation is identified as attribution if it has no explicit markers of other functions and merely presents information and displays writers' knowledge of the topic. In case of negligence, the author double-checked the annotations to ensure the consistency and accuracy of the coded data.

Data analysis

The current study adopted a mixed-method approach, integrating quantitative and qualitative analyses. Quantitative analysis was employed to describe the distributional features of literature citation and writer identity as manifested through citations. Specifically, the frequencies and percentages of each citation type and writer identity were calculated. SPSS (Chi-square test) was used to test whether there are significant differences among citation types and among writer identities because it is specifically suited for analyzing categorical data and all the necessary assumptions of it are met. This allows for the assessment of whether the observed distribution of citation types or writer identities significantly differs from the expected distribution. As for qualitative analysis, we concluded several specific discoursal and authorial identities and observed closely how writer identity is constructed by literature citation with specific examples drawn from the corpus.

It can be observed that the dominant citation type is attribution, with 599 instances, a percentage of 36.59 %. And the second prevalent function found in the corpus is establishing links between sources with 292 instances, making up 17.84 %, followed by exemplification (274, 16.74 % of the total instances). The two types, comparison of one's own findings or interpretations with other sources and application, have the same frequencies (118, 7.21 % of the sum). The remaining types occur less frequently, such as evaluation (6.17 %), further reference (5.07 %), and the smallest number belongs to statement of use, representing 3.18 %.

Attribution is found to be the most common function in the corpus. This finding is similar to Lee et al.’s research findings [14]. As Petrić explains, this type is generally regarded as the standard or default function and “rhetorically simplest one” for writers to demonstrate their knowledge of the subject [19]. Therefore, it is not a big surprise to find significantly more attribution citations than other types of citations for it is the foundational feature of citations in academic writings.

The least frequently used functional type of citation is statement of use, averaging only 2 instances per article. This lower frequency may be attributed to its specific usage within one section of articles. Typically, this citation type is found in the Methodology section, although occasionally it appears in the Introduction or Conclusion sections as well, serving to indicate the works utilized in the study. The remaining six citation functions vary in frequency, ranging from 5 % to 18 % each. This distribution suggests that expert writers recognize well the diverse rhetorical functions that citations play in constructing their own arguments or claims. It further confirms Petrić’s idea [19] that experienced writers adeptly apply and integrate various citations to establish connections between sources and their own ideas, while also proposing original perspectives. Novice writers, as observed in previous studies [14,19,41], rarely relying on other more sophisticated functional types, seemed to have used citations just to attribute the source authors to “tell the knowledge” rather than “transform the knowledge” [22]. Our findings, therefore, suggest that helping novice writers understand the diverse range of functional citations needs more attention.

Table 5 reveals that the most prevalent identity constructed by writers is that of a member of the academic community, comprising 59.44 % of the total. It is important to note that when assigning codes to each discoursal identity, the identity constructed by citation is categorized as a member of academic community only if the other three identities are not explicitly manifested. While acknowledging that a single citation may embody multiple identities, this study focuses on coding and counting the most prominent one for simplicity. In addition, the second most frequently constructed identity is a reliable and credible writer (23.95 %), followed by a knowledgeable and professional scholar (11.48 %), and the least frequently being a contributor to a field of knowledge (5.13 %).

The results indicate a propensity among writers to position themselves as members of the academic community. This inclination may be due to the fact that citing sources is a common practice expected and valued within the academic community, as highlighted by Ouellette [30]. By adhering to academic conventions and establishing links with scholars, writers present themselves as insiders of the discipline, demonstrating their allegiance to the scholarly community. Moreover, being part of the academic community is often esteemed and pursued among JA writers. It carries prestige and recognition that may shape writers' prioritization of this identity. Novice writers, especially student writers or young scholars who are newcomers to their discipline communities, perceive themselves negatively within the community, finding it challenging to identify with academic members and feeling distant from them [42]. This can be explained by drawing on Wenger's view [43]. These novice writers are confronting new practices of the discipline community and entering unfamiliar territory. Due to their lack of familiarity with academic conventions and inability to meet established expectations, novice writers should prioritize adhering to these conventions in their writing. Strategic and accurate citation usage serves as a key aspect of showcasing their commitment to the scholarly community.

The findings also reveal a comparatively lower frequency of identity construction through citations as a means of contributing to a field of knowledge. However, this does not diminish its importance. On the contrary, it holds great significance for writers who are seeking publication in English. It is widely recognized that publications should present novel ideas, findings, or research that enhance existing knowledge [42,44]. Skilled writers often utilize citations in the Introduction section to establish connections between existing literature and their new ideas, identifying gaps or limitations in current knowledge that their studies aim to address or expand upon [15]. Furthermore, writers typically draw upon various sources to make external comparisons or contrasting viewpoints, highlighting and explaining discrepancies in the Results and Discussion sections [45]. By acknowledging alternative perspectives, writers can demonstrate the novelty and significance of their original ideas, establishing themselves as contributors to knowledge.

Expert writers often strive to establish themselves as a reliable and credible writer by employing recognized research methods, which largely contribute to the reliability of their professional articles. In the Methods section, their primary objective is to provide an objective description of the data, research design, and analytical framework [34]. The use of citations in this section helps contextualize, identify, and justify the methodological decisions made by the writers, thereby aiding in the construction of a trustworthy identity. Another valued identity in professional articles is the portrayal of the writer as a knowledgeable and professional scholar. Usually, in the Literature Review section, experienced writers cite relevant literature that they perceive to be valued, demonstrating their familiarity with the topics and portraying themselves as intertextually knowledgeable [41]. Additionally, they consciously wield existing knowledge in a much more sophisticated manner, presenting themselves in a professional light. For novice writers seeking to integrate into the academic community, it is crucial to enhance their awareness of constructing a favorable identity by aligning with academic conventions and norms.

Distribution of discoursal identity and authorial identity

As Ivanič [2] stated, the authorial self refers to how writers view themselves as authors, in the sense of claiming responsibility for their content to establish authority [1] and taking a stance toward the topics of their articles [29]. Thus, it can be concluded that in terms of authorial identity, writers are inclined to project themselves as a writer with authority and an evaluator. As observed from the figure above, writers predominantly construct both the identity as a member of academic community and a writer with authority (107, a percentage of 33.44 %). The second most prevalent construction is that of being a member of academic community and an evaluator (80, 25 % of the sum). Notably, there are no instances where writers construct themselves as both a contributor to a field of knowledge and an evaluator. The result suggests that writers prefer to construct both the identity as a member of academic community and a writer with authority who are willing to show their alignment with the academic community, and meanwhile, make solid personal commitments to their claims based on assurance and command of their arguments [1]. It strongly supports Hyland's opposing concepts of proximity and positioning [4], which refer to how we position ourselves in relation to the communities and texts respectively. Shared disciplinary norms and conventions constrain writers' content selection and ways of expression, necessitating compliance with specific conventions in order to gain full membership and acceptance in the academic community. In addition, among the available choices of linguistic resources, writers have their own unique features and express their personal stances and interpersonal alignment [4]. Consequently, writers construct dual identities by demonstrating proximity to disciplinary communities and adopting specific positions toward texts, showcasing the interplay between commonality and individuality [4].

The construction of writer identity through literature citation

The construction of discoursal self

(1)Identity as a member of academic community

Identity as a member of academic community

It is acknowledged that obtaining membership of the academic community requires the competence to observe the norms and conventions of the community and communicate within the community [34]. Citation, as one of the academic conventions, enables writers to construct their identity as a member of academic community. Writers in the corpus all use one of the standard referencing systems for in-text citations as well as end-of-text references. This indicates that they possess a proper comprehension of the academic norm of using citations in their work [34]. What's more, more than just acknowledging the owners of the sources, writers strategically use citations for different intentions. As in Example 1, the writer employs the cited author's taxonomy to serve his/her study, which reveals the citation type statement of use, and in Example 2, the writer applies the adjective “reified” from the source to describe the feature of the register precisely, reflecting the function of application. These two examples show that the writers approximate the expectations of an academic community by adopting other scholars' work or concepts to support their own studies or arguments.

… a more metaphorical and ‘reified’ (Halliday & Matthiessen, 2014, p. 710) register … [JA15]

In addition, it is also common for writers to express praise of specific works or arguments created by well-known authors in the field (see Example 3). Through positive evaluation achieved by the attitudinal markers “ground-breaking” and “rigorous”, it seems to show the writer is “interacting directly with sources” [14] and communicating within the community, which positions himself/herself as an insider to the discipline. And it's worth noting that writers cite their own prior research to support the point they are making, as illustrated in Example 4. The self-citation presents the writer as a full member of the academic community as it is an important means of demonstrating one's disciplinary credentials [44].

(2)Identity as a contributor to a field of knowledge

Identity as a contributor to a field of knowledge

Not just simply retelling the existing knowledge, writers deploy citations to “transform knowledge” [14]. After reviewing the relevant literature on the topic of interest, writers identify a gap or limitation on what is currently known and thus contribute to the field by proposing a novel research perspective. In Example 5, the writer points out the limitations of the previous studies by using the verbs “overlooked” and “ignoring”, and then proposes to extend current knowledge in new directions.

… previous studies have overlooked ESL or EFL speakers' role … ignoring their professional status … [JA16]

Besides, writers often use citations with the function of comparison of one’s own findings or interpretation with other sources to point out the similarities and differences between writers' own findings and those of previous studies. On the one hand, by presenting similarities, the writer can provide additional support for previous findings (see Example 6), and thus contribute to the field by enriching empirical research on the topic. On the other hand, by presenting dissimilarities, writers uncover new knowledge and thereby position themselves in the role of a contributor. In Example 7, the writer first pointed out the general feature of other research, and then made a transition, as represented by the word “but”, to address the difference in the writer's results. In this way, the writer revealed a novelty in academic research and can be regarded as a contributor to the development of the field of knowledge.

These findings … lend support to the view that … (Hyland 2006; Hyland and Shaw 2016; Kosem 2010). [JA11]

… but the results shown here not only reflect wider shifts in publishing (Hyland, 2015) … [JA23]

We also observed that literature citation practices facilitate writers’ identity development as knowledgeable and professional scholars. Another valued quality in academic writing is the representation of self as being competent and professional. Writers strategically use citations as a rhetorical tool to show their competent scholarly identity and thus gain acceptance from readers. As illustrated in Example 9, the writer made links by concisely synthesizing different sources in one parenthesis to report identical or similar ideas, which embodies the citation function of establishing links between sources. This type attests to the writer's competence to identify the existing issues in the field such as “problems for students from different writing traditions”. Besides, the writer cited a number of sources to help justify his/her claim or idea and meanwhile, kept the flow of the text uninterrupted, which reveals a professional way to develop one's argument [9].

(4)Identity as a reliable and credible writer

Identity as a reliable and credible writer

It is well acknowledged that the use of citations is an effective way for writers to support and justify their own ideas [12,20,41]. Writers prove the authority and trustworthiness of their research through citations and thereby construct their identity as reliable and credible writers. A major way writers use to prove the legitimacy of their arguments is to cite recognized authorities. In Example 10, the writer added authority to the claim he/she made by citing Halliday, an authoritative scholar full of reputation. By doing so, the writer tried to establish themselves as reliable and credible, and readers who are initially unaware of writers’ viewpoints may eventually accept the claim [42].

This index shows “how closely packed the information is” (Halliday, 1985: 66) … [JA02]

Another way to enhance the reliability and credibility of the research is citations of exemplification. Examples and evidence can provide additional support for writers’ own claims or arguments, strengthen the true value of the proposition and thus create a reliable and credible image, as in Example 11.

There is abundant evidence on … (e.g. Nesselhauf 2005; Laufer and Waldman 2011; Peters 2016). [JA11]

From the above analysis, it can be concluded that writers construct diverse identities through various citation functions. To establish specific identities, particular citation functions should be employed. For inexperienced writers, particularly those unfamiliar with the academic conventions of the community, it is crucial to resort to citations that not only attribute related works (attribution) but also demonstrate the use and application of those works (statement of use and application). By citing relevant studies and acknowledging influential authors, writers position themselves within a lineage of scholars, emphasizing their connection to the broader intellectual tradition [24]. Furthermore, writers can solidify their identities as competent and engaged members of their discipline by using and extending the cited works and demonstrating how they have been applied in practical ways [45]. This not only showcases their expertise and comprehensive grasp of the subject matter but also allows them to form potential connections with fellow scholars within the academic community.

Secondly, utilizing citations for comparison of one's own findings and interpretations with other sources aids in establishing the writer's identity as a contributor to a field of knowledge. It highlights that the writer is actively engaging with existing knowledge in the field. Through comparison and contrast, this citation type facilitates positioning the writer's findings or interpretations within the broader context of the field, showcasing their unique perspective and contributions [25]. Therefore, it positions them as valuable contributors to the existing body of knowledge.

Thirdly, utilizing citations that serve as further references and establish links between sources enables writers to cultivate a knowledgeable and professional identity. These citations, which provide further references, showcase the writer's extensive familiarity with existing literature in their field. It indicates their comprehensive exploration of relevant sources to support their arguments or findings. Citations with the function of establishing links between sources demonstrate that the writer is well-versed in various perspectives, theories, methodologies, or approaches within their field. Moreover, these citations signify the writer's capacity to synthesize diverse viewpoints and engage with the work of others, reflecting their professionalism and deep understanding of the subject matter [28].

Fourthly, citations with the functions of exemplification play a crucial role in establishing a writer's identity as reliable and credible. Citations that exemplify support the writer's claims or arguments with concrete evidence from reputable sources [19]. This demonstrates that the writer has done thorough research and relies on credible information to back up their statements. The use of relevant examples from authoritative sources showcases the writer's expertise and deep understanding of the topic, thereby enhancing credibility.

The construction of authorial self

(1)Identity as a writer with authority

Identity as a writer with authority

Authority, in this study, is conceived as a dimension of authorial identity in the sense that it represents writers’ personal presence and their willingness to take responsibility for the content [2]. Through citing other sources, writers incorporate the ideas of the cited authors into their own new knowledge claims. Thus, citation can be regarded as a helpful means to construct an authoritative identity through which writers assert their claims to speak as an authority. In Example 12, the writer first acknowledges the findings of the previous scholar and then points out the new findings of their research. The writer makes himself/herself visible within the text and takes full responsibility for the proposition, creating an image as an authoritative writer with a right to claim.

We noted that … (see Nesi & Gardner, 2012), teaching of reflective writing skills was less evident. [JA25]

Another obvious way to show authorial presence is self-citation. It is perhaps one of the most effective ways for writers to develop a sense of authorship and authority [44]. As illustrated in Example 13, the writer cites his own work which is still unpublished, with the aim of promoting his research as part of disciplinary knowledge, showing his involvement in the area and thus enhancing the writer's authority as a researcher [42].

An overview … is given in Miller (forthcoming). [JA14]

(2)Identity as an evaluator

Identity as an evaluator

Another dimension of authorial self revealed by citations is the identity as an evaluator, that is, having a stance concerning the cited propositions, ideas, findings, studies, etc [1]. As Hyland stated, taking a stance and expressing evaluations clearly imply that the writer is a distinctive, independent self who has a firm position and rights to ownership of his/her views [1]. By citing other texts and authors and at the same time expressing his/her attitudes towards these sources, the writer conveys himself/herself as an evaluator. In this study, we identified the identity as an evaluator only if there is an explicit evaluative marker. Presented below are two instances where citations carry the writers’ positive and negative comments on the cited propositions, demonstrate a strong authorial presence, and project the writer as an evaluator who has unique opinions.

Other studies have also demonstrated that … (Bolton, Nelson, & Hung, 2002; Gardezi & Nesi, 2009; Basturkmen & von Randow, 2014) [JA10]

The wordlists is limited because they lack supplementary information … (e.g., Thompson & Alzeer, 2019). [JA29]

In Example 14, the reporting verb “demonstrate” indicates the writer's positive evaluation toward the sources, as it means “to show sth clearly by giving proof or evidence” (Oxford Dictionary). It shows the writer's alignment with viewpoints in previous studies. In Example 15, the adjective “limited” and the verb “lack” suggest the writer's negative evaluation of the previous wordlist. Through negative evaluation, the writer identifies a research gap, which helps to further put forward his/her own novel research.

To sum up, expert writers often construct an identity with authority through the strategic use of attribution, particularly through self-citation. By incorporating citations effectively and balancing them with original ideas and insights, writers demonstrate their unique perspectives and understanding, thus contributing to their authorial identity as a writer with authority [22]. Moreover, expert writers reference their own previous works to establish expertise and validate their claims. This type of citation highlights experienced writers’ authority by showcasing their reputation and credentials. In constructing an evaluator identity, writers employ evaluation citations to express their critical attitudes toward cited works. By reviewing previous literature and providing critical evaluations, writers identify gaps, inconsistencies, or limitations in existing research and provide insightful analysis, novel interpretations, or alternative viewpoints [42]. It helps develop their identity as an evaluator and meanwhile, establish themselves as independent thinkers by claiming their own unique opinions.

Previous research indicates that novice writers often struggle with establishing their authorial identity, primarily adopting a detached approach towards sources where citations are predominantly employed for attribution purposes, rather than involving critical evaluation or commentary on source content [42]. Our study demonstrated that both attribution and evaluation citations contribute to the construction of authorial identities. Consequently, it is recommended that novice writers focus on strategically employing attribution and critical evaluation, and that will allow them to establish both credibility and authority.

Conclusion

The study examined the distributional features of literature citation and writer identity in linguistic academic discourse and interpreted how literature citation helps construct different writer identities. A total of 1637 citation instances were identified in the selected 30 JAs and the citation density is 7.73 per 1000 tokens. The dominant citation type is attribution (599 occurrences, 36.59 %) and the least frequently used type is statement of use (52 occurrences, 3.18 %). With regard to the distributional features of identities, we found that 80.45 % of the total citations construct discoursal self only and 19.55 % reveal both discoursal self and authorial self. The Chi-square tests show that there is a significant difference both among different types of citation and different writer identities (p = .000 <0 .05). As for the construction of discoursal self, it was found that attribution, statement of use, and application help position writers as an insider of the academic community; comparison of one's own findings and interpretations with other sources aids in establishing the writer's identity as a contributor to a field of knowledge; further references and establish links between sources enables writers to cultivate a knowledgeable and professional identity; exemplification plays a crucial role in establishing a writer's identity as reliable and credible. In terms of authorial self, both attribution and evaluation citations contribute to the construction of identities as a writer with authority and an evaluator. It is recommended that inexperienced writers utilize the appropriate citation functions to portray a favorable representation of their desired persona.

The present study offers some implications. Firstly, it suggests that the teaching of citations should be incorporated into EAP courses, offering systematic training to novice writers. For more specific suggestions, the framework of this study can be adapted as pedagogical materials. By using and extending it, teachers can provide explicit instruction on citation functions and their role in constructing an academic identity. They can utilize diverse citation examples to help students identify various types of functions and delve into their underlying meanings. Moreover, explicitly labeling and describing these roles will raise awareness among novice academic writers about the multiple ways in which they can portray their desired identity. To illustrate different citation practices between novice writers and experienced scholars, examples from both sources, such as student essays and scholarly publications, can be collected. Teachers can initially highlight specific functions and identities that citations contribute to constructing, and subsequently encourage students to collaboratively deduce other functions and identities served by different citations either individually or in small groups.

There are also some limitations of the current study. Firstly, the sample size of the study is relatively limited, as only 30 JAs were chosen for analysis. Future studies could expand the range of investigation by encompassing more diverse and larger JAs. Secondly, the study solely focused on analyzing a single journal within the linguistic field, limiting the generalizability of the findings to other contexts. Future studies would benefit from investigating JAs from other leading journals and across various disciplines. Thirdly, our current citations solely pertain to literature, disregarding citations related to instruments and statistical methods, direct quotes from research participants, and the creators of corpora. This exclusion could potentially impact the findings of the study, as these citations may play a role in shaping the construction of the writer's identity. Therefore, future research should broaden its scope to include all these types of citations in order to obtain a more comprehensive understanding. Fourthly, our analytical framework primarily examines functional types of citations, with a specific emphasis on textual analysis rather than the writer's background or socio-cultural context. Subsequent studies may wish to expand upon this by incorporating other citation types, such as stance, and including additional aspects of identity to provide a more comprehensive and holistic view of writer identity construction through citations. Finally, although a few limitations exist in this study, it still contributes to the ongoing research on citations and identity construction and offers important insights for EAP instruction.
