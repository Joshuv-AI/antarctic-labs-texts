GLUT6-facilitated noncanonical glucose metabolic rewiring enables resistance to targeted cancer therapy
Authors: Huimin Lei, Ruixue Xia, Yabin Tang, Jun Lu, Chan Xiang, Yujing Li, Hongyu Liu, Peichen Zou, Ayinazhaer Aihemaiti, Wei Zhang, Ying Shen, Baohui Han, Yuchen Han, Hua Zhong, Hongzhuan Chen, Liang Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTargeting glucose metabolism has long been pursued as an anticancer strategy, yet its clinical translation remains challenging. Achieving therapeutic selectivity requires identifying actionable metabolic distinctions between different malignant traits. Here, we uncover a noncanonical, lactate-independent glucose metabolic pathway facilitated by the glucose transporter 6 (GLUT6), which confers targeted therapy resistance in lung cancer. Downstream, GLUT6 promotes glucose influx and diversion toward methylglyoxal production, leading to kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (KEAP1) dimerization and nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2) pathway activation, driving resistance. Upstream, GLUT6 expression is transcriptionally upregulated by therapy-induced MYC associated zinc finger protein (MAZ) activation. Targeting GLUT6 prevents and overcomes EGFR and KRAS inhibitors resistance. Moreover, the MAZ–GLUT6–NRF2 axis correlates with clinical treatment response and relapse. The preferential reliance on GLUT6—a noncanonical transporter with minimal systemic homeostasis perturbation—highlights its promise as a target for overcoming resistance and revitalizing glucose metabolism–based anticancer strategies.Subject terms: Cancer metabolism, Non-small-cell lung cancer

Introduction

Accumulating evidence indicates that metabolic reprogramming characterizes and determines tumor malignancy, representing a major advance in cancer research1–3. Cancer cells rewire their metabolism to meet aberrant bioenergetic and biosynthetic demands2,3, making targeting of rewired metabolism a promising anticancer strategy1–3.

Glucose, the principal cellular nutrient, was the first metabolite implicated in cancer metabolism, suggesting that targeting aberrant metabolism could be a viable therapeutic strategy3–5. However, this strategy has never been therapeutically successful in clinical practice despite long-term pursuits, mainly owing to the lack of understanding of the actionable distinctions between the malignancy traits to achieve enough therapeutic selectivity1,3,5. Therefore, the discovery and elucidation of the underlying mechanism will aid in identifying a therapeutic strategy capable of specifically affecting glucose-addicted cancer cells, making glucose rewiring a promising anticancer target in this context.

Targeted therapies against epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) using tyrosine kinase inhibitors such as gefitinib and osimertinib, and against KRAS mutations using inhibitors such as sotorasib and adagrasib, have reshaped lung cancer treatment and exemplify precision oncology; yet, their efficacy is often compromised by the emergence of resistance6,7. Here we report that a noncanonical Class III glucose transporter, GLUT6, facilitates a lactate-independent glucose metabolic rewiring that underlies resistance to targeted therapy in lung cancer. This pathway reveals a targetable metabolic vulnerability specific to treatment-resistant cells. These findings reveal a previously unrecognized role for glucose metabolism in selectively mediating cancer therapy resistance. This not only offers an opportunity to overcome resistance—a principal challenge and urgent unmet clinical need8,9, —but also demonstrates that glucose metabolism can be targeted more selectively, a long-standing goal in cancer research1,3.

Results

Exacerbated glucose uptake and addiction in therapy-resistant tumors

Cancer cells are characterized by increased glucose uptake and utilization, which can be assessed through 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-based positron emission tomography (PET) functional imaging to determine the maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax)3,10. In epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-mutant lung cancer, PET-computed tomography (CT) analysis revealed elevated tumor SUVmax signals that further increased upon relapse following EGFR inhibitor (EGFRi) treatment (Fig. 1A, left panel; 1B). In patients whose tumors relapsed (with regrown or metastatic lesions), the SUVmax was markedly higher compared with their non-relapsed state (before treatment or during treatment response) (Fig. 1A, left panel; 1B; Supplementary Data 1).

Consistent with these clinical observations, EGFRi-resistant cell lines exhibited a pronounced preference for glucose compared with their isogenic sensitive parental counterparts (Fig. 1A, right panel; 1 C; S1A–S1C). These clinically-relevant6,11 (Fig. S1D) resistant cell line models were authenticated in this study (Fig. S1E, Table S2) and in our previous report12–15. Under reduced glucose conditions, resistant cells showed greater vulnerability in viability (Fig. 1C, Fig. S1A), colony formation (Fig. S1B), and growth (Fig. S1C) than parental cells. In contrast, resistant cells did not display increased dependence on glutamine or serum (Fig. S1A), indicating a selective addiction to glucose.

The increase in glucose uptake in resistant cells was validated by an analysis of the transmembrane transportation of the fluorescent glucose analog 2-NBDG [2-(N-(7-nitrobenz-2-oxa-1,3-diazol-4-yl) amino)−2-deoxyglucose]) (Fig. 1D, Fig. S2A). This finding was corroborated by liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (LC‒MS/MS) and a tracer analysis (Fig. 1E) of the stable isotope U-13C-glucose, which was used to replace glucose in the culture medium at the same concentration; there was an increase in the intracellular abundance of labeled glucose and downstream glycolytic intermediates in resistant cells versus parental cells (Fig. 1F, Fig. S2B, Fig. S2C). We sorted parental HCC827 cells into low and high 2-NBDG uptake populations (Fig. S2D), and then tested their sensitivity to EGFRi and to glucose deprivation. The results show that the “High” uptake population was more resistant to osimertinib (Fig. S2E), but were more sensitive to glucose deprivation (Fig. S2F).

Stable isotope–resolved metabolic analysis (SIRM) of 13C-glucose further demonstrated that the enhanced glucose metabolism in resistant cells compared with that in parental cells tended to culminate in glycolysis, with a greater accumulation of glucose-derived, labeled intermediate metabolites in the glycolytic pathway than in other major pathways (Fig. 1E, 1F). An analysis of the live-cell extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) validated the increase in glycolysis in resistant cells compared with that in parental cells (Fig. S2G, Fig. S2H). This glycolytic propensity mirrored the acute response of sensitive cells to osimertinib, which increased intracellular glucose and glycolytic intermediates within 6 h (Fig. 1G).

Treatment with 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG), a non-metabolizable glucose analog that blocks glycolysis, revealed greater vulnerability in drug-resistant cells. Specifically: 1.25 mM 2-DG inhibited H1975OR cell growth by 72.7%, compared with 44.6% in H1975 parental cells (Fig. S3A, Fig. S3B). Similarly, 5 mM 2-DG caused 74.4% growth inhibition in PC9OR cells versus only 1.7% in PC9 parental cells (Fig. S3C, Fig. S3D). Furthermore, 0.625 mM 2-DG reduced viability by 31.6% in HCC827 parental cells, whereas ER-resistant and OR-resistant counterparts showed decreases of 56.5% and 76.4%, respectively (Fig. S3E, Fig. S3F).

Collectively, these clinical and in vitro data indicate that targeted therapy-resistant cancers are characterized by enhanced glucose uptake, glucose addiction, and glycolysis compared with their sensitive counterparts.

Glucose uptake and metabolism rewiring in resistant cells is driven by the noncanonical transporter GLUT6

The cellular uptake and utilization of glucose are limited by cognate cell membrane transporters (encoded by the SLC2A gene family). RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis of gene expression revealed that the most prominently upregulated transporter commonly expressed in resistant cell lines was GLUT6 (encoded by the SLC2A6 gene) (Fig. 2A), a noncanonical class III glucose transporter rather than the canonical transporters GLUT1 (SLC2A1) or GLUT3 (SLC2A3)5,16,17. The selective upregulation of GLUT6 mRNA expression in resistant cells was validated by reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT‒qPCR), whereas the mRNA expression levels of the canonical transporters GLUT1 and GLUT3 were not markedly different between resistant cells and parental cells (Fig. 2B). Western blot analysis reconfirmed the upregulation of GLUT6 protein expression in resistant cells (Fig. 2C). Immunofluorescence staining revealed an increase in cell membrane GLUT6 protein localization and abundance in resistant cells (Fig. S4A). This increase was also observed in KRAS inhibitor (KRASi)-resistant and KRASi-tolerant cells (Fig. S4B, Fig. S4C). In contrast to GLUT6, which is selectively upregulated in drug-resistant cells compared with parental or normal non-tumor cells, the protein levels of GLUT1 and GLUT3 showed no marked differences (Fig. S4D).

GLUT6 knockdown with small interfering RNA (siRNA) selectively abrogated the increase in glucose uptake and glycolysis in resistant cells, as determined by 2-NBDG-based flow cytometry (Fig. 2D, Fig. S4E). This abrogating effect of GLUT6 knockdown against enhanced glucose uptake and glycolysis selectively in resistant cells was validated by SIRM analysis (Fig. 2E) and reconfirmed by cell energy phenotype test analysis (Fig. S4F).

These results indicate that in resistant cells, the expression of the noncanonical transporter GLUT6 is upregulated, resulting in increased glucose uptake and metabolic reprogramming (Fig. 2F).

Pyruvate carboxylase is upregulated in multiple malignancies and facilitates cancer progression18,19. We analyzed the content of OAA (m + 4), OAA (m + 3), and the ratio of OAA (m + 3)/pyruvate (m + 3), along with their dependence on GLUT6 in drug-resistant cells. Of these, OAA (m + 3) and the OAA (m + 3)/pyruvate (m + 3) ratio serve as an indicator of pyruvate carboxylase activity. The results demonstrated that pyruvate carboxylase activity is elevated in drug-resistant cells in a GLUT6-dependent manner (Fig. S4G, Fig. S4H), a noteworthy finding that warrants further investigation.

The noncanonical transporter GLUT6 determines resistance to targeted therapy

We next investigated whether GLUT6 governs the resistance phenotype. GLUT6 knockdown selectively inhibited EGFRi-resistant cells, resulting in the greater inhibition of cell growth (Fig. 3A, Fig. S5A) and colony formation (Fig. 3B) in resistant cells than in parental cells. This selective effect of GLUT6 was mirrored in KRASi-resistant cells (Fig. 3C). Moreover, GLUT6 knockdown had a negligible effect on the viability of normal airway epithelial cells (Fig. S5B).

In contrast, the knockdown of the canonical glucose transporters GLUT1 (Fig. S5C left panel) or GLUT3 (Fig. S5C right panel) was cytotoxic to normal airway epithelial cells and generally failed to elicit the selective inhibition of resistance (Fig. S5D, Fig. S5E). These results indicate a selective dependence of drug resistance on GLUT6.

We next examined whether GLUT6 is necessary for the suppressed response to targeted therapy in resistant cells. GLUT6 knockdown resensitized resistant HCC827ER, HCC827GR, HCC827OR, PC9OR, and H1975OR cells to the EGFR inhibitors erlotinib, gefitinib, and osimertinib (Fig. 3D, 3E, Fig. S6A, Fig. S6B). The calculated combination index (CIs) for the inhibitory effect of the combination of GLUT6 knockdown and each inhibitor were less than 1.0 (Fig. 3E, Fig. S6B), indicating synergistic effects20. The resensitization effects of GLUT6 knockdown were recapitulated by KRAS-targeted therapy, in which GLUT6 knockdown resensitized H2122SR and H358ST2 cells to sotorasib (Fig. 3F, Fig. S6C, Fig. S6D). Consistent with these findings, an analysis of lung cancer patients in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database who underwent EGFRi treatment revealed that the increased expression of GLUT6 in tumors was associated with an inferior drug therapy response (Fig. 3G).

GLUT6-facilitated production of methylglyoxal derived from glucose metabolism induces resistance

Although glycolysis enhancement in resistant cells was accompanied by lactate accumulation (Fig. 1F, Fig. S2G, Fig. S2H)—itself dependent on GLUT6 (Fig. 2D, E, Fig. S4E, Fig. S4F)—exogenous lactate supplementation did not confer resistance in sensitive cells (Fig. 4A, Fig. S7A). Coincidentally, RNA-seq analysis revealed that the expression of neither the mRNAs of SLC16A gene family members, including the lactate transporters monocarboxylate transporters MCT1 (SLC16A1), MCT2 (SLC16A7), MCT3 (SLC2A8), and MCT4 (SLC16A3), nor those of the lactate dehydrogenases LDHA, LDHB, LDHC, and LDHD, were upregulated in various resistance models (Fig. 4B). These data suggest that lactate is a glycolytic epiphenomenon and an accompanying biomarker of enhanced glycolysis rather than a causal factor of resistance to targeted therapy.

In addition to being converted to pyruvate and lactate, glucose, via glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (GA3P), can be shunted to form methylglyoxal (MGO) (Fig. 4C), a reactive carbonyl species (RCS) compound that tends to induce posttranslational protein modifications21–23. Although not as well-known as lactate in glycolysis and not a target metabolite considered in usual metabolomic analyses, MGO has been reported to be highly upregulated in hyperglycemia and to play important roles in diabetes21,23. GA3P stood out among accumulated intermediate metabolites in glucose metabolism pathways in the established resistant cells (Fig. 1F) and in sensitive cells challenged by acute drug treatment (Fig. 1G). This increase in GA3P in resistant cells was dependent on GLUT6 expression, as shown by the abrogation of the increase after the knockdown of GLUT6 (Figs. 1F, 2E).

SIRM analysis via LC‒MS/MS, the current state-of-the art technique for the analysis of MGO21, revealed an intracellular increase in MGO derived from extracellular glucose when cells were challenged with osimertinib (Fig. 4D). Moreover, when exposed to MGO, sensitive cells acquired resistance to osimertinib (Fig. 4E, F). This desensitization effect of MGO was abrogated (Fig. 4F) by the ectopic expression of the MGO-eliminating enzyme GLO1 (Fig. S7B, S7C), validating pathway dependence.

GLUT6-glucose-derived methylglyoxal induces resistance through the upregulation of NRF2 expression via KEAP1 dimerization

MGO has been reported to upregulate the expression of the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2; encoded by the NFE2L2 gene) protein via the modification of KEAP122 which has been shown to be a drug resistance inducer by our group and others24–26. We hypothesized that the GLUT6-facilitated production of MGO induces resistance in a KEAP1-NRF2 axis-dependent manner. MGO treatment induced high-molecular-mass (HMM) KEAP1 dimer22 formation (Fig. 4G, H), an effect that was abrogated by the mutation of the key amino acid C151 in the KEAP1 protein for HMM formation (Fig. 4G, H). Osimertinib, coincident with its induction of glucose-derived MGO formation (Fig. 4D), promoted KEAP1 dimerization (Fig. 4I, J), as MGO did (Fig. 4G, H), a process that was dependent on the amino acid C151 in the KEAP1 protein (Fig. 4I, 4J). We then investigated whether NRF2 expression is upregulated in this context (Fig. 4K).

Consistent with its resistance-inducing (Fig. 4E, F) and KEAP1-dimerizing (Fig. 4G, H) effects, MGO upregulated NRF2 protein expression in sensitive cells (Fig. S8A, Fig. S8B left panel), an effect that was abrogated by the ectopic expression of the MGO-eliminating enzyme GLO1 (Fig. S8C).

The NRF2 protein-upregulating effect of MGO was recapitulated by EGFRi treatment (Fig. S8B right panel) and was dependent on the key amino acid(s) involved in the formation of HMM KEAP1 (Fig. S8B). Time-course Western blot analysis revealed that in response to drug stress, the upregulation of NRF2 protein expression (beginning at 48 h and 24 h in response to EGFRi treatment and KRASi treatment, respectively) followed that of GLUT6 protein expression (prior to 24 h and 6 h in response to EGFRi and KRASi treatment, respectively) (Fig. S8D). The upregulation of NRF2 (Fig. S8E) and GLUT6 (Fig. 2C) protein expression was sustained and fixed until the resistance-acquired phase, although the resistant cells were free from drug treatment challenge during this phase, indicating that NRF2 and GLUT6 expression increased throughout the process of resistance initiation and maintenance. Upregulated NRF2 functioned as a transcription factor, as evidenced by the increased expression of its downstream target gene repertoire in resistant cells (Fig. S8F left panel, Fig. S8G left panel).

The upregulation and hyperactivation of NRF2 in EGFRi- and KRASi-resistant cells were determined upstream of GLUT6, as shown by the abrogation of upregulated NRF2 protein expression (Fig. S8H) and the abrogation of increased NRF2 target gene repertoire expression (Fig. S8F right panel, Fig. S8G right panel) with GLUT6 knockdown and by the appearance of a drug stress-induced increase in the GLUT6 protein before the increase in the NRF2 protein (Fig. S8D). Accordingly, GLUT6 conferred resistance at least via NRF2 because the resensitization effect of GLUT6 knockdown after EGFRi inhibitor treatment in resistant cells was partially abrogated by treatment with Ki696 (Fig. S8I), a small-molecule activator of NRF227,28.

These data indicate that the GLUT6-facilitated production of glucose-derived MGO promotes resistance through the upregulation of NRF2 expression via the dimerization of KEAP1.

GLUT6 is transcriptionally upregulated by drug stress-activated MAZ

To identify the upstream mechanism that upregulates GLUT6 mRNA and protein expression in resistant cells, we identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) that were common between various resistant cell models and their isogenic parental cells by RNA-seq analysis and performed transcription factor (TF) predictions following chromatin immunoprecipitation enrichment analysis (ChEA) using the encyclopedia of DNA elements (ENCODE) and ReMap libraries (Fig. 5A). In addition, potential candidate TFs were predicted on the basis of TF binding sites in the promoter region of the GLUT6 gene using TF-prediction databases (Fig. 5A). MAZ stood out as the sole overlapping TF identified by the two methodologies (Fig. 5B).

Nuclear translocation is a prerequisite for transcription factors to execute their functions. In situ cellular immunofluorescence staining analysis demonstrated more nuclear MAZ in resistant cells than in parental cells (Fig. 5C). The nuclear translocation of MAZ was initiated by drug stress, as shown by an increase in nuclear MAZ abundance in sensitive cells subjected to acute osimertinib treatment (Fig. S9A). The ability of MAZ to control expression upstream of the GLUT6-NRF2 axis in resistant cells was validated by MAZ knockdown assays in which MAZ siRNAs downregulated GLUT6 mRNA expression (Fig. S9B), GLUT6 and NRF2 protein expression (Fig. 5D, Fig. S9C), and NRF2 target gene expression (Fig. 5E) in these cells.

Dual-luciferase reporter assays demonstrated that the ectopic expression of MAZ in sensitive cells increased the luciferase activity driven by the GLUT6 (SLC2A6) promoter; however, this effect was lost after mutating the MAZ-binding motif in the promoter (Fig. 5F, 5G, Fig. S9D), validating the direct transcriptional regulation of GLUT6 gene expression by MAZ. In line with the effects of MAZ on GLUT6, MAZ knockdown suppressed glycolysis (Fig. 5H) and colony formation (Fig. S9E), decreased viability (Fig. S9F) and restored sensitivity to osimertinib (Fig. S9G) in resistant cells. In contrast, the ectopic expression of MAZ in parental cells (Fig. S9H, Fig. S9I) induced the resistance of otherwise sensitive cells to EGFRi treatment (Fig. 5I, 5J). This resistance-inducing effect of MAZ was abrogated by the knockdown of GLUT6 (Fig. 5I, 5J).

Drug resistance acquisition and maintenance in vivo are dependent on GLUT6

We next investigated the role of GLUT6 in the acquisition and maintenance of resistance in vivo by conditionally interfering with GLUT6 expression (Fig. 6A).

HCC827 cell-derived xenograft (CDX) tumors showed an initial response to osimertinib, with a tumor volume decrease and growth delay but then acquired resistance, as demonstrated by a rebound of the tumor burden during continuous treatment (Fig. 6B, “Osimertinib” cohort). Doxycycline (Dox)-induced GLUT6 knockdown (Fig. S10A) blocked tumor relapse in response to osimertinib (Fig. 6B, C, “shGLUT6 on + Osi” cohort and “→ shGLUT6 on (Continuous) + Osi” cohort versus “Osimertinib” cohort), although knockdown alone without the combination of osimertinib treatment had a negligible effect on tumor growth (Fig. 6B, C, “shGLUT6 on” cohort versus “shGLUT6 off” cohort), indicating that the acquisition of drug resistance was dependent on GLUT6. This effect was confirmed when the knockdown was turned off by the withdrawal of doxycycline, when the blocked rebound was reversed, as shown by the regrowth of the suppressed tumor burden (Fig. 6B, 6C, “→ shGLUT6 off + Osi” Arm versus “→ shGLUT6 on (continuous) + Osi” Arm). Western blot analysis revealed increases in GLUT6 and NRF2 protein abundance in osimertinib-relapsed tumors (Fig. S10B), mirroring the in vitro results in which GLUT6 and NRF2 protein expression were upregulated in cancer cells during the acquisition of drug resistance (Fig. 2C, Fig. S8D, Fig. S8E). In tumors, NRF2 protein abundance positively correlated with that of GLUT6 (Fig. 6D). In osimertinib-treated tumors, the degree of relapse positively correlated with the protein abundance of GLUT6 and NRF2 (Fig. 6E). These data indicate that GLUT6 is necessary for drug resistance and is a potential target for resistance prevention.

We next sought to determine whether resistance maintenance is mediated by GLUT6 and whether targeting GLUT6 can overcome resistance. In resistant-established HCC827OR cell-derived xenograft (HCC827ORCDX) tumors, the use of osimertinib as a single agent failed to decrease the tumor volume (Fig. S10C, Fig. S10D) as in the HCC827CDX (Fig. 6B); however, when osimertinib was combined with GLUT6 knockdown (Fig. S10E), tumor size decreased, and tumor regrowth was suppressed (Fig. S10C, Fig. S10D). Importantly, when the knockdown was turned off (Fig. S10E), resistance recurred (Fig. S10C, S10D). These data indicate that GLUT6 is necessary for resistance maintenance and suggest that GLUT6 may be a promising target for overcoming drug resistance.

The in vivo effect of GLUT6 overexpression on the reversal of drug resistance by siMAZ was determined. GLUT6-overexpressing or empty vector (EV) control HCC827 cells were subcutaneously inoculated into nude mice (Fig. 6F, Fig. S10F). Mock treatment had no significant effect on tumor growth (Group “Mock EV”). Osimertinib monotherapy initially shrank tumors but was followed by recurrence (Group “Mock EV Osi”). In the GLUT6-overexpressing tumors, osimertinib failed to induce shrinkage, and growth continued (Group “Mock GLUT6 Osi”). siMAZ combined with Osimertinib could sustainably inhibit tumor growth (Group “siMAZ EV Osi”). Notably, high GLUT6 expression abrogated the effect of siMAZ, leading to resistance and tumor recurrence (Group “siMAZ GLUT6 Osi”). These findings indicate that siMAZ reverses drug resistance in a GLUT6-dependent manner in vivo (Fig. 6F).

To investigate the clinical relevance of increased MAZ-GLUT6-NRF2 signaling in therapeutic resistance, we examined these signaling molecules in matched pairs of pretreated and EGFRi treatment-relapsed non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) specimens. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis revealed upregulated GLUT6 and NRF2 protein expression in tumor cells (Fig. 6G, Fig. S10G, S10H) in patients with relapse during treatment. Moreover, higher GLUT6 or nuclear MAZ expression in tumors pretreatment was associated with worse progression-free survival after treatment (Fig. 6H, Fig. S10I), indicating that GLUT6 expression may predict drug response and relapse.

Discussion

The pursuit of targeting glucose metabolism for cancer therapy has been largely hampered by a lack of understanding regarding the metabolic distinctions between malignant traits necessary for achieving a selective therapeutic window. In patients with EGFR-mutant lung cancer, we observed that disease relapse—as per the response evaluation criteria in solid tumors (RECIST)29, involving tumor regrowth or metastasis—was associated with a significant increase in SUVmax on PET-CT, indicative of enhanced glucose avidity. Subsequent in vitro analyses confirmed that drug-resistant cells not only exhibited augmented glucose uptake but also a pronounced addiction to it. Further comprehensive studies demonstrated that upon acquiring resistance to targeted therapy, these cancer cells concurrently developed a dependency on the noncanonical glucose transporter GLUT6 for glucose uptake and utilization. By exploiting GLUT6, resistant cells sustain survival under drug pressure, thereby revealing a targetable metabolic vulnerability.

Glucose transporters, which are pivotal for cellular glucose entry and represent accessible membrane proteins for drug targeting, have long been the focus of research and therapeutic exploitation. Canonical class I GLUTs, such as GLUT1 and GLUT3, have been intensively studied for cancer intervention30–32, however, they are ubiquitously expressed across tumor and nontumor cells, are important for systemic glucose homeostasis, and are less likely to achieve a therapeutic window17. In contrast, GLUT6—a class III glucose transporter previously implicated in endometrial cancer cell growth33—is selectively upregulated in and required by resistant cells, as shown in this work. Its low basal expression and minimal role in systemic homeostasis under physiological conditions34,35 position GLUT6 as an ideal, specific target for overcoming drug resistance.

We found that GLUT6 conferred resistance by facilitating glucose influx and the production of methylglyoxal, which binds to KEAP1 to relieve the degradation-inducing effect of KEAP1 on NRF2. In the presence of GLUT6, the increased abundance of glucose was converted to more methylglyoxal. This finding is mirrored by the discovery that methylglyoxal formation is increased in diabetic hyperglycemic conditions21,36. The resulting NRF2 pathway activation protected cancer cells from drug insults and conferred resistance, similar to previous findings that NRF2 drives drug resistance through the transcription of cytoprotective genes24–26. Beyond this core pathway, we observed an enhancement in pyruvate carboxylase (PC) activity in a GLUT6-dependent manner. PC is a key anaplerotic enzyme that convert pyruvate to oxaloacetate, supporting biosynthetic demands and redox homeostasis. Its upregulation in resistant cells suggests a coordinated metabolic adaptation. This intriguing observation merits further investigation.

GLUT6 is transcriptionally upregulated upstream by MAZ, which is activated by drug insult. Glucose transporter expression is regulated to adapt to various conditions. GLUT1, the major glucose transporter ubiquitously expressed in tumor and nontumor cells without drug challenge, is transcriptionally regulated by HIF-1 and NF-κB37,38, whereas GLUT3, the predominant isoform in brain cancer cells, is transcriptionally upregulated by YAP/TAZ in glioblastoma39. MAZ is a stress- and inflammation-induced zinc finger transcription factor whose physiological and pathological functions remain incompletely characterized40,41.

Overall, we found that targeted therapy stress-induced MAZ activation transcriptionally upregulates GLUT6 and that GLUT6 facilitates glucose influx and subsequently increases the production of methylglyoxal, which increases KEAP1 dimerization and activates the NRF2 pathway to confer resistance. Cancer cells that utilize this mechanism acquire a survival advantage and resistance to therapy but at the expense of addiction to this metabolic reprogramming, which can be exploited and leveraged to selectively attack resistance in a metabolic vulnerability-targeted manner (Fig. 6I and Fig. S10J). To our knowledge, this study is pioneering in revealing the determining role and mechanism of the noncanonical transporter GLUT6 and the GLUT6-rewired glucose-methylglyoxal pathway in therapeutic resistance, as well as the direct regulatory effect of MAZ on GLUT6.

Our findings hold several important implications. First, we identified an unrecognized, nongenetic, metabolic rewiring that drives cancer resistance to targeted therapy and elucidated the underlying mechanism involved, expanding the understanding of resistance beyond genetic alterations. Second, we identify a noncanonical glucose metabolic pathway mediated by GLUT6 and shunted toward methylglyoxal driving resistance, distinct from the conventional pyruvate–lactate axis. In addition, we delineated their running scenario —— selectively required and sufficient for resistance to cancer-targeted therapy. We also investigated the underlying upstream and downstream mechanisms. Finally, the selective dependence of resistant cells on GLUT6—a transporter with minimal impact on systemic homeostasis—suggests a promising therapeutic target capable of achieving a selective therapeutic window. This addresses a long-standing challenge in cancer therapy aimed at targeting glucose metabolism.

Methods

Ethical statement

Animal studies were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and performed in compliance with the institutional guidelines of the Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. Patients’ samples and clinical information were obtained from Shanghai Chest Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, and the study protocol was approved by its Clinical Ethics Committee.

Cells and cell culture

Human lung cancer cell lines harboring EGFRi-targetable mutation(s), namely, HCC827 and H1975 cells, were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), and PC9 cells were obtained from Dr. Q. Dong (China State Key Laboratory of Oncogenes and Related Genes). The KRAS-G12C-mutated human H2122 and H358 cell lines were obtained from Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou Biotechnology and the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China), respectively. The human normal lung epithelial cell line BEAS-2B was obtained from FuHeng Cell Center (Shanghai, China). The cells were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) analysis. Isogenic resistant cell lines insensitive to the first-generation EGFR inhibitors gefitinib (HCC827GR) and erlotinib (HCC827ER) and the third-generation inhibitor osimertinib (HCC827OR, PC9OR, and H1975OR) were established, maintained, and authenticated as indicated (Fig. S1D, Fig. S1E) and previously described in refs. 12,14,15. H2122SR is the H2122-derived cell line with acquired resistance to sotorasib, and H358ST2 is the line that is tolerant to 2000 nM sotorasib; resistance and tolerance are defined and described in our previous article15. The cells were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2. HCC827, PC9, H1975 and their isogenic resistant cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium. BEAS-2B cells were cultured in DMEM. The media were supplemented with 10% (vol/vol) fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin and streptomycin, and 1% GlutaMax.

Clinical information

Positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) information from nine patients during the relapse phase and their paired non‑relapse phases (response and pretreatment), including 5 males and 4 females; forty paired EGFRi pretreatment and treatment-relapsed tumor samples for immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining analysis, derived from 11 males and 9 females; and patient information for progression-free survival (PFS) analysis were obtained from the Chest Hospital Affiliated with Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. Among these patients, twelve patients were younger than 65 years, and seventeen patients were aged 65 years or older. This study was approved by a clinical ethics committee. Clinical characteristics of PET‑CT patients are presented in Supplementary Data 1. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. As this study was a retrospective analysis without additional patient intervention, no participant compensation was provided for the use of retrospective clinical data. This study fully complies with the SAGER guidelines, and our study design is not focused on gender-related differences.

PET‒CT measurements of glucose uptake and utilization were calibrated via analysis of the maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax), and IHC samples were scored with the H-score as described in the corresponding figure legends.

Animal study and in vivo assays

Animal studies were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and performed in compliance with the institutional guidelines of Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. Female mice were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) and maintained under controlled environmental conditions (Room temperature: 22–24 °C; relative humidity: 40–70%) with a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle. Chow and water were available ad libitum. For xenograft formation, cells were subcutaneously inoculated into the flanks of 5-week-old BALB/c nu/nu athymic mice. Tumors were assessed with calipers every 2 or 3 days, and tumor volume was calculated using the following formula: volume = length × width2 × 0.5. When the tumor volume reached 200–300 mm3, the mice were randomly allocated to various treatment groups. For doxycycline inducible Tet-on-shGLUT6 cell-derived xenograft, groups: shGLUT6 off (GLUT6 non-knockdown administered with vehicle control); shGLUT6 on (GLUT6 knockdown induced by Dox); Osimertinib; and shGLUT6 on + Osi (GLUT6 shRNA + osimertinib). After the mice were administered the drugs for an indicated time, those in the “shGLUT6 on + Osi” group were randomly split and regrouped into the shGLUT6 on (continuous) + Osi arm, in which the knockdown was continued with Dox administration and shGLUT6 off + Osi arm, in which the knockdown was turned off by Dox withdrawal. Vehicle (1% dimethyl sulfoxide, once daily), doxycycline (50 mg/kg, once daily), or osimertinib (2 mg/kg, once daily) was administered through oral gavage. The experiment was terminated when the maximum tumor diameter in mice approached the predefined endpoint of 2.0 cm, which is the maximum tumor size permitted by the IACUC. At this endpoint, mice were euthanized, and tumors were excised, weighed, photographed, and collected for subsequent analysis.

Cell viability and growth assays

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates. Cell viability was analyzed by using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Dojindo) in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. Cell growth was monitored and quantified using an IncuCyte ZOOM live cell system (Essen Bioscience) every 4 h.

Colony formation assay

Cells were dissociated into single-cell suspensions were seeded at 1000 cells per well in six-well plates incubated for 7–10 days. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet, after which they were counted with an ECLIPSE TI-S imaging system (Nikon).

RNA extraction and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

Total RNA from cells was extracted using the RNA extraction kit (Takara) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse transcription was performed by using the PrimeScrip RT Master Mix Kit. Real-time PCR was carried out using SYBR Premix Ex Taq (Takara) and run on a LightCycler 480 II system (Roche). The primer sequences are shown in Table S1.

Western blot analysis

Cell and tumor samples were lysed on ice using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Beyotime) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein abundance was measured with bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (Thermo Fisher). The proteins were separated by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‒PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. The membranes were blocked in 5% nonfat milk for 1 h at room temperature and incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. After the membranes were incubated with the corresponding secondary antibodies, the membranes were subjected to electrochemiluminescence (ECL) and scanned by using the imaging analysis system.

The following antibodies were used in this study: GLUT6 (Sigma‒Aldrich, SAB2102200), MAZ (Immunoway, YT2669), NRF2 (Abcam, ab62352), Flag (Sigma‒Aldrich, F1804), β-actin (Proteintech Group, 66009-1-Ig), GAPDH (Cell Signaling Technology, #5174).

Gene knockdown by using RNA interference

Cells were seeded in 6-well or 96-well plates at a density of 200000 or 4000 cells/well, respectively. Small interfering RNA (siRNA) diluted in Opti-MEM serum-free medium and incubated with Lipofectamine 3000 for 5 min at room temperature was added to the cell culture medium. Forty-eight hours later, the silencing efficiency was detected by real-time qPCR and Western blot analysis. The sense sequences of the shRNAs are shown in Table S1.

Immunofluorescence and immunohistochemical staining

Cells grown on glass-bottomed dishes were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.4% Triton for 1 h, blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h, incubated with an anti-MAZ antibody (ImmunoWay) overnight, incubated with Alexa Fluor 555-labeled donkey anti-rabbit IgG(H + L) secondary antibody for 1 h, stained with DAPI (Beyotime) for 15 min, and then subjected to laser confocal microscopy imaging analysis (Leica TCS SP8). Immunohistochemical staining (IHC) was performed using formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue sections (3.5 µm thickness) from EGFR-mutant lung cancer patients. IHC for GLUT6 (1:50, Sigma Cat# SAB2102200), NRF2 (1:50, Abcam Cat# ab62352) and MAZ (1:25, Immunoway Cat# YT2669) was performed using a Ventana BenchMark ULTRA instrument (Ventana/Roche, AZ, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The degree of protein expression in tumor cells was assessed by two experienced pathologists, with reference to the H&E slides, based on a histoscore (H-score) method, which was calculated by multiplying the percentage of stained tumor area (1-100%) and the staining intensity score from 0 to 3, thus ranging from 0 to 300.

Luciferase reporter assay

Cells were transfected with MAZ and the corresponding empty vector (EV) plasmid with Lipofectamine 3000 reagent. Forty-eight hours later, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing puromycin for the selection of positive clones. EV and MAZ overexpressing cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 50000 cells per well. The next day, the cells were co-transfected with SLC2A6 WT or Mutant reporter and pGL4.74 hRluc luciferase reporter (E6921, Promega), and then incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. Cells were assayed according to the instructions of the manufacturer (Dual-Luciferase® Reporter (DLR™) Assay System, E1910, Promega).

Glucose uptake assay

Glucose uptake was measured using the Glucose Uptake Cell-Based Assay Kit (Cayman) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following corresponding treatment, cells were incubated in glucose-free medium supplemented with 100 μg/mL 2-NBDG ([2-(N-(7-nitrobenz-2-oxa-1,3-diazol-4-yl) amino)−2-deoxyglucose]) for 30 min, then digested, resuspended and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Liquid chromatography and mass spectrometry

Metabolites were measured using the ExionLCTM AD UPLC system (SCIEX, USA) coupled to a TripleTOFTM 6600 Plus mass spectrometer (SCIEX, USA) via electrospray ionization under the following source conditions: source temperature, 550 °C (ESI + ) and 450 °C (ESI − ); ion spray voltage, 5500 (ESI + ) and −4500 V (ESI − ); atomization gas pressure, 55 psi; auxiliary heating gas pressure, 55 psi; curtain gas pressure, 35 psi. The m/z range was 70 to 1000. Data were acquired using AnalystTM software (SCIEX, USA). Metabolite identification and quantitation were performed using the SCIEX OSTM 1.0 software (SCIEX, USA). The incorporation of 13C from U-13C-glucose induces an intensity shift from the unlabeled position (m + 0) to the labeled position (m + n, ‘n’ means the number of incorporated 13C atoms). The natural isotope abundance correction was made by the AccuCor R package (https://github.com/XiaoyangSu/ IsotopeNatural-Abundance-Correction).

Glucose metabolism analysis and glucose metabolism stable isotope-resolved metabolomics (SIRM) analysis

Cells were cultured in glucose-free RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS containing 11 mM glucose (for glucose metabolism) or U-13C-glucose (for glucose metabolism SIRM analysis) for 6 h. Metabolites in glucose metabolism were extracted from cells. After being placed on ice, the cells were washed twice with cold PBS buffer, after which 500 µL of ice cold 80% methanol was added to the cell dish. The cells were scraped, and vortexed for 10 min at 4 °C, and then centrifuged at 20,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were concentrated in a vacuum centrifugal concentrator for 2–3 h, and resuspended in 200 µL of a 50/50 (v/v) acetonitrile/water mixture. Metabolites were separated by injecting 5 μL of sample into a SeQuantTM ZIC-HILIC Polymeric column (2.1*150 mm, 5 µm, EMD Millipore). Mobile phase A consisted of 20 mM ammonium carbonate in water and B was acetonitrile. The gradient program was set as follows: 0-1 min: hold at 10% A, 1–25 min: linear gradient from 10% to 40% A; 25.1–33 min: hold at 10% A.

Glucose-derived methylglyoxal (MGO) analysis

Cells were cultured in glucose-free RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS containing 11 mM U-13C-glucose for 6 h. Metabolites from the harvested cells were extracted and centrifuged at 20,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were evaporated to dryness. Two hundred microliters of acetonitrile were used to reconstitute the dry extracts and then mixed with 200 μL of 1 mg/mL 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH) in 50% acetonitrile aqueous solution for MGO derivatization. After DNPH derivatization, the mixtures were evaporated to dryness. The dried derivatized metabolites were dissolved in 200 μL of acetonitrile for the following analysis: Metabolites (MGO m + 3, etc) were separated by injecting 5 μL of sample into a ZORBAX SB-C18 column (2.1*50 mm, 5 µm, Agilent). The mobile phase was as follows: (A) 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in water and (B) 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in acetonitrile. The gradient program was set as follows: 0-1 min: hold at 30% B, 1–12 min: linear gradient from 30% to 100% B; 12.1–15 min: hold at 30% B.

Extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) and Cell Energy Phenotype Test analyses

The extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) and Cell Energy Phenotype Test were performed using seahorse XFe96 analyzer (Agilent Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s kits and instructions. The day prior to assay, cells were seeded in cell plates at a density of 20000 cells/well and the sensor cartridge were hydrated in XF calibrant at 37 °C in a non-CO2 incubator overnight. 24 hours later, the cell medium was replaced with assay medium and incubated in non-CO2 incubator for 1 h. Compounds were prepared and loaded into the appropriate ports of hydrated sensor cartridge. For ECAR assays, compounds were injected during the measurements as follows: Glucose (10 mM), oligomycin (1 μM) and 2-DG (50 mM). For energy phenotype assays, oligomycin (1 μM), FCCP (0.125 μM) were used. The ECAR and OCR values were normalized to the number of cells.

Combination effect analysis

Treatment combinations demonstrate synergistic, antagonistic, additive, or potentiative effects. The effects were evaluated by a combination index (CI) calculation in accordance with the Chou–Talalay method20. The data were analyzed using CompuSyn software (CompuSyn Inc.): CI = 1, additive effect; CI > 1, antagonistic effect; CI < 1, synergistic effect (0.85 to 0.9, slight synergism; 0.7 to 0.85, moderate synergism; 0.3 to 0.7, synergism; 0.1 to 0.3, strong synergism; and CI < 0.1, very strong synergism).

Statistics and Reproducibility

The data were analyzed with Prism software (GraphPad). Statistical differences were determined by the two-tailed unpaired t test for comparisons between two treatments or by ANOVA followed by Tukey’s or Dunnett posttest comparing more than two treatments, unless otherwise mentioned. P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. All correlations were calculated using Pearson correlation. No data were excluded from the analyses. The experiments were randomized. The investigators were blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment.
