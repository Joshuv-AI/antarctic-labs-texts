Integrated five-stage cascade impactor with MIP-modified QCM sensor for real-time monitoring of airborne microorganisms
Authors: Vahidreza Nafisi, Zahra Mousavian, Alireza Balaei, Yasamin Bide, Ensieh Fahimi Kashani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pathogens present a significant threat to global public health, necessitating the development of rapid and reliable detection methodologies for effective monitoring and outbreak management. This study introduces a new sensing device that uses molecularly imprinted polymer (MIP) for sensitive and specific Staphylococcus aureus detection. The research presents a comprehensive investigation into the design and optimization of a five-stage Sioutas Cascade Impactor (SCI) integrated with quartz crystal microbalance (QCM) sensing, enabling continuous, size-resolved collection and in situ monitoring of airborne bacteria. The study combines theoretical modeling of particle impaction dynamics with empirical evaluations of sampler collection efficiency, pressure drop characteristics, and sensor surface properties. A key focus is the optimization of flow control, along with a rigorous evaluation of MIPs as biosensing coatings on QCM sensor. For this purpose, in situ chemical polymerization of dopamine was conducted in the presence of S. aureus bacteria. To confirm surface modification, various microscopic and spectroscopic analyses were employed. Results demonstrate that careful control over flow conditions (14.6 min of sampling time and a flow rate of 6.96 L/min) and resulting pressure gradients, together with MIP surface functionalization, is crucial for achieving high bacterial capture. This work establishes a robust framework for the development of real-time, reusable biosensors for proactive airborne pathogen surveillance.

Introduction

Airborne pathogenic microorganism’s, such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Legionella pneumophila, Staphylococcus aureus, and Bacillus anthracis spores, cause a variety of infectious disorders1. The airborne route is a major mechanism for these diseases to spread because particles that carry them can stay in the air for a long time, allowing them to spread over long distances. Airborne particles can remain suspended for extended periods attributable to their low settling velocities, exemplified by 3.1 × 10–3 m/s for typical 10-μm particles and 3.5 × 10–5 m/s for typical 1-μm particles2,3. Airborne transmission plays a vital role not just in the spread of bacterial diseases but also in viral outbreaks, many of which have developed into global pandemics—such as influenza, which is estimated to cause up to 646,000 deaths globally each year4.

The increased potential of bioterrorist attacks employing the aerosol spread of hazardous microorganism’s has further highlighted the necessity for rapid detection and identification of airborne microorganisms5. To reduce the chance of widespread outbreaks, these infections must be identified quickly and precisely. These capabilities enable the prompt implementation of suitable treatments, such as early treatment, effective decontamination, infection control methods, and the deployment of protective countermeasures, while also avoiding personnel exposure and surface pollution. However, the sensitivity and specificity needed for real-time monitoring are frequently omitting in conventional sample approaches like the culture-based methodology. Improved methodologies are consequently necessary to precisely assess the concentration and size distribution of pathogen-laden particles as well as to appreciate the dynamics of aerosolized microorganism transmission in connection to the possible health issues to humans and animals. This information is essential for efficient infection control6.

Based on sampling principles, the two primary types of apparatus considered suitable for risk zones with normal (low-level) biocontamination are impact samplers and filtration samplers7. Two cascade impactors, Sioutas Cascade Impactor (SCI) and Andersen Cascade Impactor (ACI), are well-established tools for capturing particles based on their aerodynamic size. The ACI typically employs six to eight impaction stages to separate particles operating at a flow rate of 28.3 L/min. Each stage includes orifice plates that direct airflow onto corresponding collection plates. The SC utilizes four or five impaction stages. Each stage consists of slit plates. It has been optimized for the collection of ultrafine particles and typically operates at 9 L/min. For applications involving ultrafine particle sampling (including microorganisms), environmental pollution monitoring, and personal exposure assessment, the SCI is often preferred due to its efficiency in capturing smaller particles at lower flow rates and its suitability for portable use8,9.

On the other hand, Quartz Crystal Microbalance (QCM) sensors have emerged as a promising technology for the detection of airborne pathogens. QCM sensors operate based on the piezoelectric effect, wherein mass changes at the crystal surface induce corresponding shifts in resonance frequency, enabling the detection of bound analytes10. These sensors offer several advantages, including rapid response times and the potential for high-throughput operation10,11. Recent advances are expanding the potential of QCM biosensors for real-time, point-of-care pathogen detection, environmental monitoring and medical diagnostic12. QCM biosensors have been employed to detect a range of airborne bacteria and viruses, including influenza, SARS-CoV-2, and Mycobacterium tuberculosis11,13.

The integration of cascade impactors with mass-sensitive devices such as QCM sensors, can be a promising approach for achieving rapid and selective detection of airborne pathogens. Unlike traditional impactor plates, which require post-experimental analysis, QCM sensors enable real-time measurements of mass accumulation, making them especially advantageous for continuous pathogen detection and environmental monitoring. However, several challenges remain. These include optimizing operating flow rates, managing the impact forces and shear stresses experienced by captured bacterial cells, and ensuring that sensor coatings provide strong adhesion of target analytes without compromising cell viability or structural integrity. Precise control of air velocity and pressure drop is essential to minimize disturbances during particle deposition and to ensure efficient capture. These issues have been investigated in this study.

Molecularly imprinted polymers have various desirable features that make them attractive for biosensor applications. These include superior thermal and chemical stability, a long shelf life, fabrication repeatability, mechanical durability, reusability, high sensitivity, and outstanding target selectivity. Because of these qualities, MIPs are desirable candidates to be incorporated into biosensing platforms that are intended to identify certain biological targets. In the context of bacterial detection, MIPs function through shape/size-selective identification, which is particularly beneficial for identifying various bacterial species.

The basis for this recognition mechanism is the production of template-shaped cavities in the polymer matrix that are exactly complementary to the target bacteria in terms of size, shape, and functional group arrangement. As a result, MIPs can effectively differentiate between bacterial morphologies. For instance, they can recognize spherical S. aureus cells and rod-shaped Escherichia coli cells through physical and chemical matching. Moreover, the rigidity of bacterial cell walls supports the selective interaction with these imprinted cavities, especially under dry or semi dry conditions and optimized air flow. This makes MIP-coated surfaces especially advantageous in air-sampling biosensors or environmental sensors where traditional receptor-ligand interactions may be limited due to fluctuating humidity or surface biofouling.

Tokonami et al. used the fundamental differences in surface chemistry between Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria to create highly selective detection systems that could distinguish Escherichia coli and Pseudomonas aeruginosa from Bacillus subtilis and S. aureus. In their work, specific emphasis was placed on retaining the electrical conductivity stability of conducting polymers during the electro-polymerization of anionic bacterial strains, a modification that considerably enhanced the efficacy of surface imprinting techniques14. Agar et al. introduced a dual layer sensor platform that combines aptamers with MIPs for the selective detection of S. aureus and E. coli. Their study demonstrated high target specificity for both sensor types, owing to the high affinity of aptamers for their corresponding bacteria and the tailored cavity structure of the MIPs. Aptamers successfully bonded to their respective targets in bacterial mixtures, whereas the imprinted cavities in MIPs allowed for selective bacterial recognition. This hybrid technique, incorporating biological (aptamer) and synthetic (MIP) recognition elements, considerably reduced nonspecific binding while improving the accuracy and reliability of bacterial detection15.

Recently, a combination of QCM sensor with MIP coating has been used for the detection of Galectin-3 in serum16. The advantage of this method is the increased speed of detection and the lack of need for labeling the target substance. In another approach, to detect the residual amount of the antibiotic levofloxacin in blood, an electrochemical sensor composed of TiO2 and MIP has been designed and manufactured17. The results indicate that this biosensor not only has high sensitivity but also possesses reproducibility and long-term stability. Other studies have also utilized MIP coating on their studied biosensors to improve sensitivity and stability18–20.

In all of these studies, the medium for the presence of the target material/bacteria and its identification is a liquid medium (blood, serum, etc.), and there have been few studies on the detection of airborne pathogens. In this research, an attempt was made to design and construct a device for detecting airborne pathogens by combining the characteristics of a cascade impactor and QCM sensors coated with MIP, and the research results indicate that this has been achieved. Naturally, improving the coating manufacturing process could lead to increased efficiency of this system and improve its sensitivity and specificity.

Material for sensor coating

In the present study, the bio-coatings applied to the QCM sensors are Molecularly Imprinted Polymers (MIPs). Our MIP coating is of the surface-imprinted polymer (SIP) type22. These were engineered to selectively bind target bacteria (S. aureus). The selectivity of the sensor stem from shape-specific surface cavities formed by bacterial imprinting in Polydopamine (PDA). The QCM sensors detect mass loading of captured S. aureus cells on their SIP-coatings. The measured frequency shift of the sensors is proportional to total mass of S. aureus adhering to the MIP layer. It is a label free detection, without antibody interactions or biochemical recognition.

The S. aureus strain (PTCC No. 1112) and E. coli (PTCC No. 1399) were supplied by Persian Type Culture Collection (PTCC) of the Iranian Research Organization for Science and Technology (Iran). E. coli was used for evaluation of specificity of the device. For preparation of the mentioned bacterial suspensions, an aliquot of bacteria frozen glycerol stock was first plated on Nutrient Agar (NA) and incubated at 37°C for 18 h. After incubation, a single colony was resuspended in 10 mL of Nutrient Broth and placed in a shaking incubator at 37°C to prepare an overnight culture. One milliliter of the overnight bacterial culture was centrifuged at 13,000 rpm (11,337 × g) for 5 min, and the bacterial pellet was washed twice with PBS (pH: 7.4). The optical density (OD600nm) of the bacterial suspension was measured and adjusted to OD600nm = 0.25 using PBS. Plate counts confirmed that this corresponded to approximately 1 × 10⁸ to 2 × 10⁸ CFU/mL.

All reagents were of analytical or synthetic grade. Hydrogen peroxide (30% w/w, Merck) and sulfuric acid (95–98%, Merck) were employed to prepare the piranha solution. Dopamine hydrochloride (≥ 98%) was obtained from Merck. Tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane, sodium hydroxide, and hydrochloric acid (analytical grade) were purchased from Merck for preparation of the 20 mM Tris‑HCl buffer (pH: 8.5).

Ultrapure deionized water was produced using a Millipore Milli‑Q® system (18.2 MΩ·cm), Acetic acid (analytical grade, Merck) was used for the bacterial removal step. All reagents were selected to minimize contamination or side‑reactions that could affect the dopamine polymerization kinetics and the uniformity of the polydopamine layer.

Tris buffer (20 mM, pH: 8.5) was prepared in‑house by dissolving Tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane (Tris) in deionized water and adjusting the pH to 8.5 using 1 M NaOH and 1 M HCl solutions. Both pH adjustments were made gradually under constant stirring to ensure accurate buffering.

Coating methods

There are various methods for creating MIP-coatings tailored to target bacteria. By considering some aspects (such as the quality, cost and ease of implementation) into account, the following procedure was adopted for this project:

Before use, the ozone-cleaned QCM sensor was immersed in piranha solution (H₂O₂/H₂SO₄ = 1:3) for 5 min, then thoroughly rinsed with deionized water and dried with nitrogen gas (N₂). A solution containing S. aureus bacteria (0.1 mg/mL) and dopamine was thoroughly mixed in Tris–HCl buffer (20 mM, pH: 8.5). Two milliliters of this mixture was poured onto the QCM chip placed in a vessel. The setup was then maintained horizontally at room temperature for specific durations (6 and 24 h). Afterwards, the QCM coated with polydopamine was washed twice with 5% acetic acid solution for 5 min each time, followed by rinsing with deionized water to remove the bacterial template, and then dried.

For comparison, two other coatings were applied, the porcine gastric mucin (CAS #84082-64-4, Merck, DE) and the glycan Asialoganglioside GM2 (GA2, CAS #35960-33-9, BioSynth):

Purified porcine gastric mucin was dissolved at a final concentration of 10 mg/mL and homogenized by magnetic stirring for 1 h in order to conduct adhesion studies. 100 µL of this mixture were pipetted onto the sensors and incubated at room temperature for overnight.

Some sensors were coated with 100 μL of GA2 at a final concentration of 1 mg/ml, which was then incubated at room temperature for one hour on a shaker set at 550 rpm23.

Also, for recycling of the used sensor, the sensor was washed with 5% acetic acid solution for 5 min. This solution removes the captured bacteria without any damage to the MIP coating. The FTIR analysis showed that the MIP coating remain intact after washing by acetic acid solution (refer to Fig. 5 in Result section). Therefore, the sensors are reusable after recycling cycle.

Evaluation methods and tools

Throughout this study, attenuated total reflectance Fourier transform infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectroscopy and field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) were employed to characterize the functional groups of the modified QCM sensors and to confirm surface modification. ATR-FTIR spectra were recorded using a PerkinElmer spectrometer (USA) over the wavenumber range of 650 to 4000 cm–1. The morphology of the modified QCM sensors was examined using a FE-SEM (MIRA\LMU, TESCAN, Czech Republic). Also, for evaluation of surface characteristics, roughness tester TR200 (Salu Tron GmbH, Germany) and AFM (ARA-AFM, ARA research co., Iran) was used.

Additionally, colony counting and fluorescence microscopy were utilized to quantify bacterial adsorption on the sensor surfaces.

The binding potential of S. aureus and E. coli surface receptors to the GA2 glycan was first evaluated using molecular docking simulations. In a series of preliminary studies, we also assessed MIP's sensitivity to the target bacteria (S. aureus) in the presence of E. coli at the same concentrations. After determining the sensitivity of MIP and chemical ligands (GA2 and mucin) for the target bacteria, statistical analysis was utilized to assess the effectiveness of the receptors under investigation in trapping S. aureus. Design Expert software version 12 and analysis of variance (ANOVA) as powerful tools to examine relationships between variables were used to analyze and compare several model evaluation methods using data collected through experiments.

Results

Characterization of QCM coated with polydopamine MIP.

To characterize the functional groups and confirm the successful coating of the QCM with polydopamine MIP, ATR-FTIR analysis was performed (Fig. 5). The presence of broad OH and NH stretching peaks at 3300–3500 cm–1, strong C–H stretching vibrations at 2830–2920 cm–1, as well as aromatic C = C and C–N stretching vibrations at 1610–1500 cm–1 identify the indoline/quinone structure typical of polydopamine networks. The C–O stretching vibration between 1280–1320 cm–1 reflects catechol oxidation, while the weak bands around 750–800 cm–1 correspond to C–H out‑of‑plane bending of aromatic rings.

Sampler physical efficiency results

The average equivalent diameters of microbe-carrying particles (MCPs) responsible for colony forming units (CFUs) are typically greater than 1 µm, and a D50 value below 2 µm is considered suitable for effective microbial sampling24. The collection efficiency of developed device is influenced by airflow rate. Experimental tests demonstrated that particles > 1µm, the device achieves more than 98% removal efficiency across the entire tested airflow range of 4.5 to 9 L/min (Fig. 8). For smaller particles, the collection efficiency is more sensitive to flow rate variations and there is an optimal airflow rate (about 7 L/min).Fig. 8Overall device capture efficiency at different flows.

The bio-sensor function

Based on our sensor driving circuit the initial frequency for new/uncoated QCM sensor is 5,991,444 ± 188 Hz. After coating the initial frequency is shifted to 5,989,879 ± 100 Hz. These measurements are very dependent on each sensor/coating characteristics. There are some variations but it is not important to our results because of the mass loading is proportional to the frequency shift and the variations in the initial frequency can be adjusted on the calibration phase. The accuracy and long-term stability of the measurement circuit of the QCM crystal are ± 1.7 Hz and ± 7.0 Hz, respectively. Based on Eq. 3, the sensitivity of bare QCM sensor is ~ 30 ng. Discussion

This study examined glycan and MIP-based coatings with different functioning principles. As glycan-based coatings, glycoproteins like GA2 and mucin contain oligosaccharide chains that serve as identifying markers for proteins on the outer layer of cells that can be employed in microbe detection devices. These oligosaccharide chains are attached to the protein through either N- or O-glycosidic bond. But glycans are biomolecules that are extremely susceptible to environmental factors and can quickly lose their biological function over time, which presents challenges in the biosensor coatings. It is important to combine glycan-based elements with increased long-term stability into biosensors that allow the production of efficient, flexible, and low-cost analytical equipment28. Due to the low stability of glycan-based coating, the MIPs have garnered interest for bacterial detection in biosensor29. The MIP-based QCM sensor approach overcomes some limitations in other traditional methods through several mechanisms:

Enhanced specificity and stability: MIPs can be engineered to mimic the specific binding sites of targets, providing targeted selectivity. Unlike antibodies, MIPs are robust and chemically stable.

Cost-effectiveness: MIPs are cost-effective and suitable for mass production, making them ideal for broader clinical implementation.

Label-free detection: the QCM sensor enables real-time, label-free detection without requiring secondary labels or reagents, eliminating complexity and additional costs.

There are many relevant individual papers particularly on MIPs for pathogen detection30–32. A new study presents a novel cyclodextrin-integrated molecularly imprinted polymer (MIP)-based quartz crystal microbalance (QCM) sensor specifically designed for label-free detection of Galectin-3 (Gal3), a critical biomarker for heart failure progression16. The sensor architecture represents an integration of multiple molecular recognition strategies for a biomarker in the blood medium. Other recent studies focus on the integration of MIP-QCM for real-time detection of some substances in liquid medium such as milk and blood33–35. But none of these researches specifically address the integration of our three techniques together (MIP + QCM + cascade impactor), nor do they focus on their combined application for airborne pathogen monitoring. The SCI-QCM platform we developed, with its MIP coating, effectively monitors airborne bacteria in a reusable and size-selective way. Cascade impactor-type samplers actually size airborne particles based on their aerodynamic properties, which can be related to their deposition in the human lung. The physical size, shape, or density of a particle are part of its aerodynamic characteristics. This specification of the cascade impactor sampler can help to improve specificity of the device to a target microorganism.

In the present study, dopamine was used as the functional monomer to change the QCM biosensor surface by forming a polydopamine layer. Early on the polymerization process, S. aureus cells were integrated into the PDA matrix, most likely through a combination of hydrogen bonding and ionic interactions between the dopamine molecules and elements of the bacterial surface. After the polymerization process, the template bacteria were eliminated, leaving behind imprinted cavities that were chemically and morphologically complimentary to S. aureus. Acetic acid was applied as a washing solution. Hydrogen interactions between the bacteria and polydopamine break when they are exposed to an acid aqueous solutions decrease non-covalent bonding. This coating provides an ideal platform for biosensing applications because of its exceptional mechanical durability, hydrophilicity, and biocompatibility. The hydrophilic PDA-MIP film was attached onto the QCM sensor, which then demonstrated significant affinity for the target bacterium limit. Also, the sensor exhibited greater selectivity for S. aureus compared to other non-template bacteria, including E. coli. The QCM biosensor recognized the target bacteria through electrokinetic attraction toward the MIP-functionalized sensor surface. When a suspension containing S. aureus was introduced, a significant decrease in the QCM resonant frequency was observed, indicating successful bacterial capture. These findings demonstrate the potential of PDA-based MIP coatings for extremely specific and reliable bacterial detection. Under alkaline conditions, dopamine polymerizes easily to form a uniform and coated film without the need for complicated fabrication processes or additional supporting materials36. Due to containing an amino group, dopamine can be connected to the bacteria via hydrogen bonding. Non-covalent actions such as hydrogen bonding are often used as it permits easy removal of the template from the polymer matrix and quick binding of templates to MIPs. MIP-coated sensors have been successfully produced using this technique (Yadav 2022). While the primary focus of this study was on the integrated system's performance, the specificity of the Polydopamine (PDA) MIP was validated using E. coli as a structural and biological competitor. Since E. coli (rod-shaped, Gram-negative) showed a negligible frequency shift compared to S. aureus (spherical, Gram-positive), it confirms that the binding is dictated by the stereospecific cavities created during the imprinting process. Actually, our selectivity tests using E. coli already provide a rigorous baseline. Furthermore, literature on PDA-based MIPs consistently reports an Imprinting Factor (IF) > 3.0, supporting the inherent selectivity of our protocol.

It has been discussed in detail the impact of the surface characteristics (such as hardness, hydrophobicity, porosity, etc.) on the stability of bacterial adhesion37. Accordingly, the main characteristics for enhancing bacterial adhesion to surfaces are as follows: positive surface charge, suitable roughness (~ Ra = 200 nm), and appropriate stiffness (e.g., Young's modulus ~ 5 MPa). Also, our method differs fundamentally from conventional Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR)- and laser-induced fluorescence (LIF)-based bioaerosol detection in several key aspects.

First, compared to SPR technique, QCM sensor measures mass changes directly via frequency shifts, whereas SPR detects refractive index changes near a metal surface. SPR typically requires a liquid phase and well-controlled optical alignment, making it less suitable for direct airborne, size-resolved sampling. In contrast, your system integrates aerodynamic size classification (via cascade impaction) with in situ mass sensing, which is advantageous for real airborne monitoring without liquid transfer steps.

Second, compared to LIF methods, which detect intrinsic fluorescence signatures of biological particles (e.g., NADH, riboflavin), your method is selective at the molecular recognition level due to the molecularly imprinted polymer (MIP). LIF is rapid and real-time but generally non-specific and prone to false positives from non-biological fluorescent particles. Your MIP-QCM approach provides higher biochemical selectivity, particularly for target organisms such as Staphylococcus aureus.

Third, LIF systems are excellent for high-throughput aerosol screening but do not provide direct quantitative mass accumulation on a functionalized surface. Your method combines aerodynamic fractionation with selective capture and direct gravimetric sensing, which enhances analytical specificity and enables potential sensor reusability.

In summary, SPR offers high sensitivity in controlled liquid systems, LIF offers rapid but broad bioaerosol detection, while your integrated SCI-MIP-QCM platform provides size-resolved, selective airborne pathogen monitoring with stronger analytical specificity in real environmental conditions.

The physical separation significantly mitigates the matrix effect, as larger fungal spores or smaller inorganic fumes are diverted to other stages A and B or E before they can interfere with the sensor. Conclusion

S. aureus has become a serious global issue, especially with the increase in hospital-acquired infections and multidrug-resistant strains. Because of this, it’s really important to be able to identify and study this bacterium quickly, especially for biological and clinical purposes. Biosensors are gaining attention as affordable, easy-to-use, and fast alternatives for detection. Over time, many methods have been developed to find and measure harmful bacteria, like PCR, immunological tests, and traditional culturing and staining. Although these methods work well, they usually need trained experts, can be expensive, take a long time, and require a lot of work. Therefore, a small, real-time, sensitive, and cost-effective device is a need for pathogen monitoring. Many of the real-time, viable or bio-detection capable devices are high cost (Table 12). If present research can be commercialized, the device would be reliably capable to distinguish biologic vs non-biologic cheaply (about $10,000) and it can serve mid-tier market segments that cannot afford BioFlash or BioTrak but want better than just particle monitors. MIPs are becoming popular tools for recognizing specific molecules, acting somewhat like artificial antibodies to detect bacteria and viruses. In our study, we created a polymer sensor that can detect bacteria in environmental samples with good specificity and low cost. We made sure to imprint the exact surface structure of S. aureus onto the polymer, which allowed us to identify it specifically. Combining this MIP with a quartz crystal microbalance (QCM) lets us detect bacteria without labels in just a few minutes.

Our sensing method uses both the size of the bacteria and their surface chemistry. Since our samples included both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, this method seems well suited for real-world environments with many different bacterial types. Its speed, accuracy, and ease of use make it promising for hospitals, biosafety labs, and industries sensitive to contamination.

Overall, this system offers a strong and flexible approach for real-time detection of airborne bacteria. Theoretical models fit well with our experiments, helping us predict how particles stick or detach. Plus, the modular design makes it easy to regenerate and reuse, so it works well both in labs and out in the field some realistic limitations of our research might be improper selectivity in mixed aerosols, the flow might drift over time (because of cascade contamination), and the sensor will need clear regeneration/reuse cycles. Also, the high humidity condition (wet condition) can impact on the QCM performance. Then, in such environment, the integration of a dryer to the inlet of the device is necessary. A software-based differential baseline compensation algorithm (based on a humidity-temperature sensor that monitor the environment conditions) could enhance the detection precision. In the future, adding wireless readouts could allow remote monitoring and data sharing, while automation might improve efficiency and reduce mistakes. We’re also considering multiplexed receptor arrays that could detect multiple bacteria at once, covering a wider range of pathogens.
