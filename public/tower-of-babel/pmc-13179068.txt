Anesthetic Management in Patients with Impella Devices: A Retrospective Review and Clinical Guide
Authors: Harrison Yang, Divakara Gouda, Kent B. Berg, Katherine Green, Suvan Sundaresh, Basavana Goudra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:The Impella family of percutaneous a left ventricular assist devices are increasingly used for hemodynamic support in patients with cardiogenic shock or advanced heart failure. However, guidance on anesthetic management for patients with Impella support undergoing noncardiac procedures remains sparse. This study reviews perioperative practices and outcomes to inform clinical management.Methods:Following institutional review board approval, a retrospective analysis was performed of adult patients receiving an Impella device (2.5, CP, 5.0, or 5.5) at Thomas Jefferson University Hospital, Philadelphia, USA, between December 2017 and October 2024. Patients, who subsequently underwent procedures involving anesthesia care were included. Demographics, procedure type, anesthetic technique, pharmacologic management, hemodynamic support, and intraoperative outcomes were analyzed descriptively.Results:Among 165 Impella recipients, 41 underwent anesthesia requiring procedures: Eight gastrointestinal (GI) and 33 non-GI interventions. All GI cases involved Impella 5.5 devices, commonly for urgent endoscopy in gastrointestinal bleeding. General anesthesia was used in 50% of GI and 94% of non-GI cases. Propofol and etomidate were predominant induction agents; fentanyl and rocuronium were widely utilized. Phenylephrine and norepinephrine were the most frequent vasopressors, with adjunctive use of dobutamine or epinephrine. No intraoperative device malfunctions, dislodgements, or arrests occurred.Conclusions:With individualized anesthetic planning and vigilant hemodynamic control, Impella-supported patients can safely undergo diverse procedures. Deep sedation may suffice for select endoscopic interventions, whereas complex non-GI operations generally require general anesthesia. These findings provide a foundation for developing standardized perioperative management protocols for this growing patient population.

BACKGROUND

The Impella system is a family of percutaneously implanted, catheter-based mechanical circulatory support devices that provide a left ventricular unloading and cardiac output augmentation in patients with cardiogenic shock or undergoing high-risk percutaneous coronary interventions (PCI).[1] Over the last several years, in the setting of conditions such as cardiogenic shock, the landscape has begun shifting away from intra-aortic balloon pumps (IABPs) toward the Impella, as the Impella provides superior LV unloading and perfusion.[2]

There are multiple types of Impella, including the 2.5, Cardiac Power (CP), 5.0, and 5.5. The 2.5 and CP are inserted percutaneously via the femoral artery. The 2.5 can provide up to 2.5 L/min of flow, while the CP provides up to 4.0 L/min of flow. These are typically used for high-risk PCI, and are mostly for short term support for patients, who have cardiogenic shock. The 5.0 and 5.5 differ in that they are implanted through axillary or direct aortic access, and have flow rates up to 5.5 L/min. These are intended for longer duration support, and for enhanced mobility in stabilized patients.[3]

From an anesthetic perspective, the type of device plays a critical role in guiding anesthetic management. Femoral devices have positioning restrictions, with increased risk of lower limb ischemia. Axillary and central devices allow for greater flexibility but must be carefully watched in case of dislodgement. The larger caliber pumps also offer more robust hemodynamic support, but are more preload and afterload sensitive, necessitating precise volume and vasopressor titration.[4] The choice of ventilatory mode, induction agent, and use of neuromuscular blockade can differ based on the type of device being used.

Despite the expanding role of the Impella in critical care, limited data exists on the perioperative anesthetic management of patients presenting with Impella for noncardiac procedures. These patients commonly undergo a variety of procedures post Impella insertion, and have high mortality rates.[5] Given the delicate physiology that requires Impella usage, these patients are a vulnerable population, and anesthesiologists must be vigilant in their induction and ventilation management. While case reports have described the use of monitored anesthesia care or regional anesthesia for Impella usage, the broader landscape of intraoperative management strategies remains undefined. This study aims to review anesthetic management in patients with Impella devices across multiple procedure types, and provide a framework for safe perioperative planning.

METHODS

This retrospective observational study was conducted at Thomas Jefferson University Hospital and approved by the institutional review board. Using a comprehensive electronic health record (EHR) query, we identified all adult patients (age > 18) who received an Impella device between December 2017 and October 2024. Device types included the Impella 2.5, CP, 5.0, and 5.5. Patients were included in the study if they underwent any procedural intervention involving the anesthesiology service after Impella implantation. The initial cohort was established (n = 165), and patients were stratified by device type and access route: femoral, subclavian, circumflex, and axillary. Procedural interventions were then reviewed manually through anesthesia notes and surgical records. Procedures not involving anesthesiology involvement were excluded. They were then categorized into two groups: GI Procedures, including esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD), colonoscopy, and ERCP, and non-GI Procedures, including surgical wound exploration, ECMO or IABP decannulation, dialysis catheter placement, craniotomy, tracheostomy placement or exchange, AV node ablation, and cardiac re-entry operations.

Data collected included patient demographics, ASA classification, Impella type and duration of support, procedure type and location, anesthetic technique (GA vs sedation), induction/maintenance agents, airway management, ventilator mode, vasopressor/inotrope use, intraoperative complications, and outcomes. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize findings. Continuous variables were reported as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, while categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages.

A total of 165 patients at our institution received an Impella device between 2017 and 2024. Of these, 41 underwent subsequent procedural interventions involving anesthesiology care, with 8 of these classifieds as GI procedures, and 33 non-GI procedures. These procedures spanned a wide range of surgical specialties, reflecting the diversity and acuity of patients requiring mechanical circulatory support. Patient demographics revealed that the mean age was 63.9 years (range 32–83), and 77% were male. The mean BMI was 28.9 ± 6.5.

There was a total of 8 GI procedures that included EGD, colonoscopy, antegrade double balloon enteroscopy, and ERCP. These patients all had Impella 5.5 devices inserted, with a mean age of 63.6, weight of 73.9 kg, and BMI of 27.7. Indications for Impella placement included heart failure with reduced ejection fraction secondary to non-ischemic cardiomyopathy (5/8 patients) and cardiogenic shock (3/8 patients). These underlying pathologies represent a cohort with a profound cardiovascular compromise that required significant hemodynamic monitoring throughout procedural care. In seven cases, the indication for endoscopy was active GI bleeding, only one case was performed for biliary obstruction. Unsurprisingly, this is a vulnerable population, and shows that the endoscopic procedures done are often in an urgent setting and thus requires careful anesthetic planning.

Anticoagulation strategies also varied across this cohort, with three patients managed on systemic heparin infusions, two on bicarbonate purge, and two others on heparin purge solutions. One patient had anticoagulation held entirely, likely due to active bleeding. The variability in anticoagulation strategy emphasizes the importance of multidisciplinary coordination between anesthesiology and gastroenterology teams to balance thrombotic and bleeding risks. Anesthetic techniques were evenly split, with four patients receiving general anesthesia and four managed under deep sedation. General anesthesia tended to be selected for more complex procedures such as ERCP, or when airway protection was deemed necessary due to bleeding or aspiration risk. Deep sedation instead was employed for shorter time frames. Ventilation strategies followed anesthetic technique as well, with GA cases employing volume-controlled ventilation and deep sedation cases maintaining spontaneous ventilation. Volume control methods were favored in intubated patients, likely to minimize fluctuations in preload and afterload that could impact Impella function. Induction agents were used in 8/8 cases. Propofol was the most used agent, administered in six cases with doses ranging from 20 to 100 mg (0.31–1.38 mg/kg). Etomidate was used in four cases, ranging at doses from 8 to 20 mg (0.11–0.31 mg/kg). Fentanyl was administered in four cases, with doses of 25–100 mcg (0.26–1.32 mcg). Rocuronium was used in four cases as well, with doses from 20 to 50 mg (0.22–0.66 mg/kg) for neuromuscular blockade. Maintenance strategies included propofol infusion in six patients (1.82–443.95 mg, 0.03–6.83 mg/kg), as well as fentanyl infusion in just one patient (25 mcg, 0.26 mcg/kg). Hemodynamic support was also universally required. Vasoactive agents included phenylephrine, norepinephrine, vasopressin, dobutamine, milrinone, and epinephrine. Six out of eight patients received at least one vasopressor or inotrope, and two patients required no vasoactive support at all. Phenylephrine was the most common agent, administered in six cases. Volume resuscitation with a crystalloid solution (either LR, Plasma Lyte, or NaCl) was also provided to six patients, with volumes ranging from 300 to 800 mL. While one patient experienced premature ventricular contractions, no major perioperative complications were documented. These results demonstrate that individualized anesthetic planning can be safely conducted in patients supported by Impella 5.5 devices.

Non-GI procedures included a spectrum of interventions for 33 patients: Nine patients underwent cardiac surgery or reoperation, such as sternotomy, CABG, or intraoperative TEE-guided repair, 20 patients had procedures related to device or vascular access, such as ECMO or IABP removal, Impella repositioning, and wound debridement, and the remaining four patients underwent other diverse procedures, including a craniotomy, tracheostomy, and AV node ablation. These patients had a mean age of 56.8, weight of 90.2 kg, and BMI of 29.6. Like the patients undergoing GI procedures, this cohort was a high-risk population, with several patients falling into the obese or morbidly obese categories.

Anesthetic management of patients undergoing non-GI procedures was consistent with high-acuity standards. General anesthesia was used in 94% of cases (31 of 33), with only two procedures completed under moderate or deep sedation. The two cases managed without intubation involved peripheral washouts and wound exploration, where airway protection and hemodynamic stability could be maintained with minimal interference. The overall reliance on general anesthesia is consistent with the complexity and invasiveness of most procedures in this cohort. Volume-controlled ventilation was the most common method, used in 21 patients. Pressure controlled ventilation was used in five cases, likely to better manage pulmonary compliance. Spontaneous ventilation was maintained in the remaining seven patients.

Complete data was available in 32 of 33 cases. Fentanyl was the most common induction opioid, used in 27 cases, with doses of 25–250 mcg (0.23–3.79 mcg/kg). Rocuronium was the most common neuromuscular blocker, being used in 28 cases with doses from 20 to 150 mg (0.18–2.19 mg/kg). For hypnotic induction, propofol was used in 14 patients with doses ranging from 10 to 80 mg (0.12–1.09 mg/kg), while etomidate was chosen for five patients at doses of 10–20 mg (0.09–0.29 mg/kg). Maintenance regimens included dexmedetomidine (11 patients), with doses ranging from 25.2 to 250.76 mcg (0.26–3.88 mcg/kg), fentanyl (11 patients) with doses ranging from 100 to 1337.5 mcg (0.94–12.86 mcg/kg), propofol (11 patients) with doses ranging from 70 to 1376.68 mg (0.93–12 mg/kg), and ketamine (two patients) with doses ranging from 94.63 to 150 mg (1.24–1.69 mg/kg). Hemodynamic support was also a central component of intraoperative management, with vasoactive agents required in 85% of cases (28 of 33). Vasopressin was the most frequently used agent, used in over half the cases (19 of 33), with doses ranging between 0.5 and 27.7 units (0.01–0.27 units/kg). Epinephrine was also commonly used (16 patients), with a much wider dose range, from 10 mcg to 5.23 mg (0.09 mcg/kg–0.056 mg/kg). Both phenylephrine and milrinone were used in 10 patients, with the former having significantly varied dosing from 100 mcg to 42.37 mg (1.17 mcg/kg–0.48 mg/kg), while the later had doses from 0.44 to 89.41 mg (0.005–0.91 mg/kg), mostly reflected in major cardiac interventions. Norepinephrine and dobutamine were each administered to eight and seven patients respectively, with total doses reaching up to 9.6 and 95.69 mg, respectively. Crystalloid resuscitation was provided to 13 patients as well, with volumes administered widely ranging from 50 to 1200 mL, emphasizing the need for careful patient-specific volume management. Despite the critical nature of these patients and procedures, no intraoperative Impella malfunctions, dislodgements, or escalations to ECMO were documented. Furthermore, no cardiac arrests or acute decompensations were recorded as well.

The median procedure duration for the GI procedures was 44 min and mean were 78.6 min. The total anesthesia duration (median and mean) was 111.5 and 116.5 min, respectively.

For non-GI endoscopy procedures, corresponding numbers were 136 and 166.4 min (procedure time) and 191 and 187.9 min (anesthesia duration).

DISCUSSION

The anesthetic management of patients supported with Impella devices necessitates a nuanced approach, as they constitute a physiologically precarious cohort. The principal challenge lies in balancing the need for adequate sedation or anesthesia against the potential for destabilizing a circulation that is critically dependent on device function. The most significant insight from this analysis is not the specific pharmacologic agents employed, but the underlying patterns of decision-making and how these choices are dictated by the pathophysiology of Impella-assisted circulation.

The output of the Impella pump is profoundly influenced by preload, afterload, and residual native a left ventricular function. Consequently, even modest vasodilation or myocardial depression, frequently induced by propofol, opioids, and other induction agents, can precipitously reduce effective pump flow. This physiologic reality rationalizes the observed clinical patterns: the consistent use of low-dose, meticulously titrated induction regimens and the proactive administration of vasopressors. The preference for etomidate in cases of severely limited myocardial reserve aligns with its favorable hemodynamic profile, while propofol was administered in reduced doses, often in combination with adjuncts like fentanyl, ketamine, or dexmedetomidine to mitigate hypotension.[67] The existing literature primarily addresses anesthesia management of patients undergoing implantation of an Impella circulatory support device, with far less attention given to anesthetic considerations during procedures performed while cardiac output and circulation are being actively supported by the pump.

Patients undergoing gastrointestinal procedures emerged as a distinct clinical subgroup. In one single-center retrospective cohort of 24 patients who received the Impella 5.5 device, the incidence of GI bleeding was 15% in the overall cohort, and 18% in the subgroup of devices implanted >14 days, which falls within the reported range of 13%–30%.[89] This is similar to other continuous-flow devices.[1011] As all these patients were supported with the more powerful Impella 5.5 and were already physiologically compromised as a result of ongoing bleeding, a preference for deep sedation was logical. This approach minimized hemodynamic fluctuations and preserved spontaneous ventilation, a critical consideration given that positive-pressure ventilation can compromise venous return and Impella filling. General anesthesia was reserved for procedures with a high aspiration risk, such as ERCP, where definitive airway control was imperative. Hemodynamic management in this subgroup was primarily accomplished with phenylephrine and minimal inotrope support, reflecting the shorter duration and reduced physiologic impact of these procedures.

In contrast, the cohort presenting for non-GI procedures was generally younger, with a higher body mass, and underwent more invasive and prolonged operations. Guarracini et al. described a case of abdominal surgery with the support of Impella.[12] Impella 5.5 was reported safe/feasible for non-cardiac interventions in patients with underlying heart failure, but more data needed.[13] In this panel discussion, authors presented four patients, who transitioned onto the Impella device for better hemodynamic support. The nearly universal requirement for general anesthesia in this group explains the more aggressive utilization of neuromuscular blocking agents and multimodal maintenance regimens. The rationale for this strategy is clear: major cardiac, vascular, and ECMO-related procedures necessitate controlled ventilation, profound immobility, reliable analgesia, and sustained sympathetic stability, which are objectives unattainable with sedation alone. The broader array of vasopressors and inotropes used in these cases mirrored the greater physiologic burden of the surgery and the need to maintain adequate perfusion amidst fluctuating vascular resistance and venous return.

A unifying theme across both cohorts was the near-ubiquitous use of vasopressor support, highlighting the preload- and afterload-sensitive nature of Impella mechanics. Phenylephrine was a frequently employed agent due to its ability to augment systemic vascular resistance without inducing tachyarrhythmias, thereby preserving the pressure gradient essential for effective pump flow. Other agents, including norepinephrine, vasopressin, dobutamine, and milrinone, were utilized selectively to augment contractility or address profound vasoplegia, particularly during major cardiac operations or ECMO decannulation.

It is critically important to note that, despite the inherent physiologic fragility of this population, no intraoperative device malfunctions, dislodgements, or episodes of irreversible cardiovascular collapse were documented. This outcome underscores that with a foundational understanding of Impella physiology, specifically the imperatives to maintain preload, avoid abrupt vasodilation, and prevent arrhythmias, both sedation-based and general anesthetic techniques can be implemented safely.

A comparison of morbidity and mortality between the subgroup requiring anesthesiology-managed procedures (n = 41) and the remainder of the Impella population (n = 124) was considered; however, this analysis was not performed for several reasons. First, the procedural cohort represents a clinically distinct subset with inherently different acuity, indications, and complication profiles, making unadjusted comparisons difficult to interpret. Many patients undergoing procedures did so because of acute bleeding, vascular access issues, or postoperative complications, each of which independently affects outcomes.

Second, severity of illness at the time of Impella implantation, duration of shock, concomitant use of ECMO or inotropes, and timing of intervention varied widely between groups. A meaningful morbidity or mortality comparison would therefore require multivariable adjustment or prospective data collection beyond the scope of this descriptive study.

The principal strength of this investigation is its illustration of real-world clinical decision-making within a patient population seldom detailed in the anesthesiology literature. However, the retrospective design inherently limits the capacity for systematic comparison of strategies or outcomes.

CONCLUSION

Patients with Impella support require meticulous anesthetic management due to their significant preload dependence, afterload sensitivity, and diminished myocardial reserve. This study confirms that through physiology-guided planning, both deep sedation for specific endoscopic procedures and general anesthesia for major surgical procedures can be administered safely. The careful titration of anesthetic agents, preservation of venous return, and early, proactive vasopressor support was central to maintaining stable device performance. The absence of intraoperative Impella malfunctions or hemodynamic collapses affirms the feasibility and safety of a structured, rationale-driven anesthetic strategy and emphasizes the need for standardized perioperative protocols customized for Impella-assisted circulatory support.
