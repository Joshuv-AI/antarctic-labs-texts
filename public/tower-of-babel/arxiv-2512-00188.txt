Dissipation and fluctuations of CMOS ring oscillators close to criticality
Ashwin Gopal, Massimiliano Esposito, Jan Meibohm

Abstract
We analyze a thermodynamically consistent model of CMOS-based ring oscillators near the onset of coherent voltage oscillations. For driving voltages close to the critical value, we derive the normal form of the Hopf bifurcation that underlies the oscillation transition in the thermodynamic limit. Using this normal form, we determine the phase and amplitude dynamics, and demonstrate that entropy dissipation decreases in the oscillating state for ring oscillators with more than three inverters. These findings culminate in a stability-dissipation relation, which links the observed decrease in dissipation to an increase in the local stability of the oscillating state. Next, we characterize finite-size fluctuations of the amplitude and phase near the critical voltage, using a stochastic version of the normal form. We demonstrate that close to the transition, finite-size fluctuations remain important at arbitrary system size, introducing oscillations even below the critical voltage. We further show that these noise-induced oscillations have an anomalously short decoherence time that scales sub-linearly with the system-size, in contrast to the behavior far from criticality.



I
Introduction

Self-sustained oscillations are paradigmatic phenomena in non-equilibrium physics, manifested across systems ranging from biochemical
Goldbeter 1996
;
Novák and Tyson 2008
, and electronic clocks
Strogatz 2024
;
Milburn 2020
, to nonlinear optics
Agrawal 2000
;
Buks and Martin 2019
and hydrodynamics
Rockwell and Naudascher 1978
. Driven far from equilibrium, these systems undergo a qualitative change from stationary states, whose probability distributions are concentrated near stable fixed points, to oscillating states, concentrated around the orbits of stable limit cycles. In the thermodynamic limit, this dynamical transition is sharp and well-described by deterministic bifurcation theory
Golubitsky et al. 1988
;
Nicolis 1995
;
Strogatz 2024
. Recent work has revealed relationships between dynamical stability and dissipation, close to criticality at the deterministic level
Meibohm and Esposito 2024a
;
Meibohm and Esposito 2024b
.
However, on the mesoscopic scale, where the magnitude of thermal fluctuations is comparable to the size of the order parameter, the distinction between deterministic dynamics and thermal noise blurs. At this “edge of instability,” emerges a regime of rich physical interplay, with complex dynamical and thermodynamical behaviors
Hohenberg and Halperin 1977
;
Epstein and Showalter 1996
;
Herpich et al. 2018
;
Nguyen et al. 2018
;
Gopal et al. 2024
.

Complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) circuits
Taur and Ning 2021
provide a practical platform to investigate this interplay between deterministic and stochastic dynamics, and its thermodynamic implications
Freitas et al. 2021
;
Gao and Limmer 2021
;
Gopal 2024
;
Helms et al. 2025
. As CMOS transistors scale down, with smaller physical dimensions and lower supply voltage, various sources of physical noise become relevant to the signal. This is particularly acute in ultra-low-power applications
Schrom and Selberherr 1996
;
Kim 2011
and neuromorphic computing
Mead 2002
;
Milo et al. 2020
;
Jelinčič et al. 2025
, where circuits often operate in the subthreshold regime with supply voltages approaching the thermal voltage.
Among the diverse digital, analog, and mixed-signal components built from CMOS transistors, ring oscillators constitute a fundamental building block for timing, frequency generation, and signal processing. Their minimal design, high tunability, and compatibility with standard fabrication processes make them indispensable in both high-performance and ultra-low-power applications
Baker 2019
.

Ring oscillators are electronic devices composed of an odd number of inverters connected in a loop
Razavi 2020
;
Mandal and Sarkar 2010
; see Fig.
1
. Their output voltages oscillate because of an inherent instability in the circuit’s logical states. This instability drives the loop into self-sustained oscillations, whose frequency is determined by the number of inverters and the propagation delay between them. This delay, in turn, is controlled by the voltage across the inverters, so ring oscillators are highly tunable and therefore commonly serve as voltage-controlled oscillators in phase-locked loops
Razavi 2020
;
Mandal and Sarkar 2010
. Their tunability and simplicity make ring oscillators essential components in CMOS technology, particularly in timing and frequency-synthesis applications.

Current fluctuations in the transistors – thermal or flicker – induce random variations of the oscillation phase producing phase noise, and causing ring oscillators to lose coherence over time, manifested as jitter
Hajimiri et al. 2002
. Although detrimental in clocking contexts, phase noise can be exploited to design “true random number generators”
Robson et al. 2014
;
Bucci et al. 2003
;
Sunar et al. 2006
. Unlike pseudo-random number generators, these devices rely on inherently noisy physical processes and are used in secure communication and cryptographic systems where unpredictability is essential
Liu et al. 2016
. True randomness can be generated by combining the uncertainties of multiple ring oscillators to produce a bit stream at specified sampling intervals. In this way, amplified phase noise is harnessed to construct efficient true random number generators even in the subthreshold regime of ultra-low power
Güler and Dündar 2012
;
Güler and Dündar 2013
.

. (b) CMOS implementation of the NOT gate (inverter) using pMOS and nMOS transistors, and a bi-Poissonian charge-transport model including the gate-body

In this paper, we analyze a thermodynamically consistent model of ring oscillators near the critical voltage, at which small-amplitude oscillations emerge. We derive the normal form for the Hopf bifurcation of the dominant oscillating mode that underlies the transition, enabling us to determine the amplitude and frequency of oscillations close to onset. Using the framework of stochastic thermodynamics
Seifert 2012
;
Van den Broeck and Esposito 2015
;
Peliti and Pigolotti 2021
, we show that entropy production decreases in the oscillatory state for ring oscillators with more than three inverters. Based on these findings, we derive a stability-dissipation relation, similar to that in Refs.
Meibohm and Esposito 2024a
;
Meibohm and Esposito 2024b
, linking the reduced entropy production to the enhanced local stability of the oscillating state.

We then characterize phase noise near the critical voltage, where conventional large-amplitude approaches
Demir et al. 1998
;
Vance and Ross 1996
;
Gaspard 2002
become inaccurate. Our analysis is based on a “stochastic normal form”
Xiao et al. 2007
that incorporates the leading noise-induced effects. Close to the oscillation transition, such effects remain relevant, regardless of the large system size. In particular, they generate noise-induced oscillations even below the critical voltage. In contrast to large-amplitude oscillations
Gaspard 2002
;
Remlein et al. 2022
, these oscillations exhibit unusually strong decoherence with an anomalously short coherence time. This property may indicate that noise-induced oscillations could be especially well suited for generating high-rate, statistically independent true random numbers.

The paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
we introduce the thermodynamically consistent formulation of electric circuits of Ref.
Freitas et al. 2021
and describe our model. In Sec.
III
we analyze the thermodynamic limit, identify the equilibrium fixed point and its instability at the critical voltage, and derive the Hopf normal form that characterizes small-amplitude oscillations. We then discuss the stability and thermodynamics of the deterministic system. Section
IV
addresses finite-size fluctuations: Here, we analyze the stochastic normal form, noise-induced oscillations, and the resulting (short) decoherence time. Finally, we present our conclusions in Sec.
V
.

II
Model

We consider a CMOS-based ring oscillator composed of (odd)
N
inverters [see Fig.
1
(a)], in contact with a heat bath at temperature
T
. Each inverter is composed of two metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistors (MOSFETs) [Fig.
1
(b), center], one
n
-doped (“nMOS”, bottom) and one
p
-doped (“pMOS”, top). The heat bath induces finite-temperature voltage fluctuations in the system. In order to consistently describe them, we need to account for the voltage fluctuations that arise from all
2N
MOSFETs in the circuit.

Using the formalism in Ref.
Freitas et al. 2021
, we model MOSFETs as conduction channels subject to thermal noise. We consider the subthreshold regime in which voltage changes across the transistors are caused by single-electron transitions through the conduction channels. Hence, electron transitions through the nMOS and pMOS transistors of the
i
th inverter are treated as bidirectional Poisson processes, characterized by a forward (
+
) and a backward (
-
) transition rate
\lambda^{\text{n/p}}_{i\pm}(v_{i-1},v_{i})
. Here,
v_{i}
denotes the output voltage of the
i
th inverter.

The inverters are connected in a loop, see Fig.
1
(a), so the input of the first inverter is the output of the last inverter, i.e.
v_{N}=v_{0}
and, more generally,
v_{i}=v_{i\!\!\!\mod N}
. Because MOSFETs typically have insulating gate terminals, changes in the
i
th component
v_{i}
of the output voltage are only due to charge transitions in the transistors of the
i
th inverter.

The allowed transitions in the voltage vector
\bm{v}=(v_{0},\ldots,v_{N-1})^{\sf T}
are given by

\displaystyle\bm{v}\to\bm{v}+\bm{\Delta}^{\text{p}/\text{n}}_{i\pm}v_{e}\,,

(1)

with

\displaystyle\quad(\bm{\Delta}^{\text{p}}_{i\pm})^{m}=\pm\delta_{im}\quad\text{and}\quad(\bm{\Delta}^{\text{n}}_{i\pm})^{m}=\mp\delta_{im}\,,

(2)

where
\delta_{mn}
is the Kronecker delta and
v_{e}\equiv q_{e}/C
represents the elementary change of the output voltage due to a single charge transfer. Furthermore,
C=2(C_{o}+C_{g})
describes the effective capacitance at the output node with contributions from the gate-body capacitance
C_{g}
and additional output capacitance
C_{o}
caused by the transistors connected to the node.

The current
I_{p}
through a pMOS transistor as a function of the input
V_{\text{in}}
, output
V_{\text{out}}
and driving voltages
V_{\text{dd}}
is given by
Freitas et al. 2021

I_{p}(V_{\text{in}},V_{\text{out}};V_{\text{dd}})=\\
I_{0}\text{e}^{(V_{\text{dd}}-V_{\text{in}}-V_{\text{th}})/V_{\text{T}}}\left[1-\text{e}^{-(V_{\text{dd}}-V_{\text{out}})/V_{\text{T}}}\right]\,,

(3)

where
V_{\text{th}}
denotes the threshold voltage and
I_{0}
is the characteristic current, which are parameters determined by the transistor specifications.

We consider CMOS devices with symmetric transistor parameters for pMOS and nMOS transistors and further apply the symmetric powering voltages
V_{\text{dd}}
and
-V_{\text{dd}}
, respectively. In such a setup, the current
I_{n}
through an nMOS transistor is obtained by inverting the input and output voltages in Eq. (
3
), i.e.,

I_{n}(V_{\text{in}},V_{\text{out}};V_{\text{dd}})=I_{p}(-V_{\text{in}},-V_{\text{out}};V_{\text{dd}})\,.

(4)

The deterministic
I
-
V
characteristics (
3
) and (
4
) together with the local detailed balance condition, required for thermodynamic consistency according to stochastic thermodynamics
Seifert 2012
;
Van den Broeck and Esposito 2015
;
Peliti and Pigolotti 2021
, uniquely determine the transition rates
\lambda^{\text{n/p}}_{i\pm}(v_{i-1},v_{i})
for all transistors
Freitas et al. 2021
. The transition rates for the
i
th pMOS transistor of the ring oscillator can be written as

\displaystyle\lambda^{\text{p}}_{i+}(\bm{v})

\displaystyle=\frac{\Omega}{\tau_{0}}\text{e}^{(V_{\text{dd}}-v_{i-1})/V_{\text{T}}}\,,

\displaystyle\lambda^{\text{p}}_{i-}(\bm{v})

\displaystyle=\frac{\Omega}{\tau_{0}}\text{e}^{(v_{i}-v_{i-1})/V_{\text{T}}}\text{e}^{-1/2\Omega}\,,

(5)

where we have defined the system size parameter
\Omega=V_{\text{T}}/v_{e}=k_{B}TC/q_{e}^{2}
and the characteristic timescale
\tau_{0}=[(V_{\text{T}}C)/I_{0}]\text{e}^{V_{\text{th}}/V_{\text{T}}}
with the thermal voltage
V_{\text{T}}=k_{B}T/q_{e}
. The previously described symmetries between the MOSFETs imply that the transition rates for nMOS are given by

\lambda^{\text{n}}_{i\pm}(\bm{v})=\lambda^{\text{p}}_{i\pm}(-\bm{v})\,.

(6)

Combining these ingredients, the dynamics of the probability
P(\bm{v},t)
to find the system in state
\bm{v}
at time
t
is given by the Master equation

\partial_{t}P(\bm{v},t)=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\sum_{\rho=n,p}\left\{\lambda_{i+}^{\rho}(\bm{v}-v_{e}\bm{\Delta}_{i+}^{\rho})P(\bm{v}-v_{e}\bm{\Delta}_{i+}^{\rho},t)\right.\\
\left.+\lambda_{i-}^{\rho}(\bm{v}-v_{e}\bm{\Delta}_{i-}^{\rho})P(\bm{v}-v_{e}\bm{\Delta}_{i-}^{\rho},t)\right.\\
\left.-\left[\lambda_{i+}^{\rho}(\bm{v})+\lambda_{i-}^{\rho}(\bm{v})\right]P(\bm{v},t)\right\}\,.

(7)

III
Small amplitude oscillations in thermodynamic limit

The onset of coherent oscillations upon increasing
V_{\text{dd}}
, observed in Fig.
2
, is most conveniently described in the thermodynamic limit
\Omega\to\infty
of large system size. Physically, this limit can be realized by simply increasing the physical dimensions of the transistor
Gopal et al. 2022
, so that the elementary voltage change
v_{e}
becomes negligible compared with all other voltages. Since the current flow scales with the physical dimensions of the channel, we define rescaled transition rates
\omega^{\pm}_{\text{n/p}}(\bm{v})
as

\omega_{i\pm}^{\text{n/p}}(\bm{v})\equiv\lim_{\Omega\to\infty}\frac{\lambda_{i\pm}^{\text{n/p}}(\bm{v})}{\Omega}\,.

(8)

To determine the dynamics in this limit, we expand the Master equation (
7
) to lowest order in
\Omega^{-1}
to obtain

\partial_{t}P(\bm{v},t)=-\nabla_{\bm{v}}\cdot[\bm{F}(\bm{v})P(\bm{v},t)]+\mathcal{O}(1/\Omega)\,,

(9)

where we have identified the deterministic drift

\displaystyle\bm{F}(\bm{v})=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\sum_{\rho=\text{n},\text{p}}\left[\bm{\Delta}_{i+}^{\rho}\omega^{\rho}_{i+}(\bm{v})+\bm{\Delta}_{i-}^{\rho}\omega^{\rho}_{i-}(\bm{v})\right]\,,

(10)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\tau_{0}}\left[\sinh\left(\frac{v_{i-1}(t)-v_{i}(t)}{V_{\text{T}}}\right)-\text{e}^{V_{\text{dd}}/V_{\text{T}}}\sinh\left(\frac{v_{i-1}(t)}{V_{\text{T}}}\right)\right]\,.

Due to the absence of a diffusion term in Eq. (
9
), the trajectories of the output voltages
v_{i}
follow a deterministic dynamics given by

\frac{\dot{v}_{i}(t)}{V_{\text{T}}}=F_{i}(\bm{v})=\tau_{0}^{-1}h\left(\frac{v_{i}}{V_{\text{T}}},\frac{v_{i-1}}{V_{\text{T}}}\right)\,,

(11)

where
\dot{v}_{i}=\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}v_{i}
denotes a time derivative and the function

\displaystyle h(x,y)=2\left[\sinh\left(y-x\right)-\text{e}^{V_{\text{dd}}/V_{\text{T}}}\sinh\left(y\right)\right]\,,

(12)

is identical for all
i
. In what follows, we discuss the basic properties of Eq. (
11
).

III.1
Symmetry

As one can anticipate by considering Fig.
1
, ring oscillators composed of identical inverters are invariant under cyclic permutations of the inverters. This symmetry directly carries over to the output voltages
\bm{v}
, and is thus reflected in the dynamics (
11
).

For the ring oscillator, we define the symmetry operation
\gamma
as
\gamma=\mathcal{R}^{n}
for some integer
n\geq 0
, where the generator
\mathcal{R}
cyclically permutes the components of
\bm{v}
by one, i.e.,

(v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{N-1})^{\mathrm{T}}\overset{\mathcal{R}}{\mapsto}(v_{N-1},v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{N-2})^{\mathrm{T}}.

(13)

Symmetry under the action of
\gamma
implies that the macroscopic dynamics of the system, described by Eq. (
11
), must be
equivariant
with respect to the action of
\gamma

Golubitsky et al. 1988
, which entails that

\mathbf{F}(\bm{v})=\gamma^{-1}\mathbf{F}(\gamma\,\bm{v}).

(14)

This property can be seen to hold true by considering the right-hand side of Eq. (
11
), because the function
h
is independent of the index
i
.

The cyclic permutation symmetry of inverters is a crucial property of the ring oscillator that we will use repeatedly in what follows.

III.2
Trivial fixed point

Since
\sinh(0)=0
, one finds by inspection that Eq. (
11
) admits a trivial fixed point when all voltages vanish, i.e.,
\bm{v}^{*}=(0,0,\ldots,0)^{\sf T}
. Without external drive (
V_{\text{dd}}=0
), the device is in thermal equilibrium, so that
\bm{v}^{*}
must be stable. Driven devices (
V_{\text{dd}}\neq 0
), by contrast, are intrinsically out of equilibrium, so
\bm{v}^{*}
may become unstable, giving rise to dynamic behavior. Generally, the stability of
\bm{v}^{*}
is governed by the eigenvalues of the stability matrix
\mathbb{M}
with elements

\displaystyle M_{ij}=V_{\text{T}}\frac{\partial F_{i}(\bm{v})}{\partial v_{j}}\bigg|_{\bm{v}=\bm{v}^{*}}=\tau_{0}^{-1}\left(h_{10}\delta_{ij}+h_{01}\delta_{i-1j}\right)\,,

(15)

where all indices are understood modulo
N
and where we defined

\displaystyle h_{ij}=\partial_{x}^{i}\partial_{y}^{j}h(x,y)\big|_{x=y=0}\,,

(16)

so that

\displaystyle h_{10}=-2\,,\qquad h_{01}=-2(\text{e}^{V_{\text{dd}}/V_{\text{T}}}-1)\,.

(17)

The fixed point
\bm{v}^{*}
is considered stable when all eigenvalues of
\mathbb{M}
have a negative real part. If one or more of the eigenvalues have positive real parts, by contrast, perturbations along the corresponding eigendirections grow over time, and the fixed point is said to be unstable. To determine the conditions under which
\bm{v}^{*}
is stable, we need to diagonalize the stability matrix
\mathbb{M}
.

From Eq. (
15
) we observe that
\mathbb{M}
is a circulant matrix
Davis 1979
, which means that
\mathbb{M}
commutes with
\gamma
,

\displaystyle\gamma\mathbb{M}=\mathbb{M}\gamma\,.

(18)

This commutation relation, in turn, is a consequence of the equivariance property (
14
) and thus of the symmetry of the ring oscillator. An important consequence of Eq. (
18
) is that the eigenvectors of
\mathbb{M}
are discrete Fourier modes

\hat{\bm{v}}_{k}=\left(1,\text{e}^{\frac{i2\pi k}{N}},\text{e}^{\frac{i4\pi k}{N}}\ldots,\text{e}^{\frac{i2\pi k(N-1)}{N}}\right)^{\sf T}

(19)

for
k=0,1,\ldots,N-1
and that
\mathbb{M}
is diagonalized by the discrete Fourier transform
\mathbb{F}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{FMF}^{-1},

(20)

where
\mathbb{D}
is the diagonal form of
\mathbb{M}
and
\mathbb{F}
and its inverse have the elements

\displaystyle F_{kn}=\text{e}^{\frac{i2\pi kn}{N}}\,,\quad F^{-1}_{nk}=\frac{1}{N}\text{e}^{-\frac{i2\pi kn}{N}}\,.

(21)

The eigenvalues
\mathscr{D}_{k}=\mathbb{D}_{kk}
of
\mathbb{M}
are then obtained as
\mathscr{D}_{k}=\mu_{k}+i\omega_{k}
, where

\displaystyle\mu_{k}=

\displaystyle h_{10}+h_{01}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi k}{N}\right)\,,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\left[1+(\text{e}^{V_{\text{dd}}/V_{\text{T}}}-1)\cos\left(\frac{2\pi k}{N}\right)\right]\,,

(22)

and

\displaystyle\omega_{k}

\displaystyle=h_{01}\sin\left(\frac{2\pi k}{N}\right)=-2(\text{e}^{V_{\text{dd}}/V_{\text{T}}}-1)\sin\left(\frac{2\pi k}{N}\right)\,.

(23)

In the undriven (equilibrium) case
V_{\text{dd}}=0
, all eigenvalues of
\mathbb{M}
are negative, implying that
\bm{v}^{*}
is stable, as previously anticipated. However, as the voltage
V_{\text{dd}}
increases, there is a change in sign of
\mu_{k^{*}}
, i.e., the fixed point becomes unstable, for the pair of Fourier modes
\pm k^{*}
(modulo
N
) with
k^{*}=(N-1)/2
and
-k^{*}=(N+1)/2
. At the bifurcation where
\mu_{\pm k^{*}}=0
, the imaginary parts
\omega_{\pm k^{*}}=\pm\omega_{k^{*}}
of the eigenvalues remain finite, which indicates a Hopf bifurcation
Nicolis 1995
;
Strogatz 2024
. The critical voltage
V^{*}_{\text{dd}}/V_{\text{T}}
at which this bifurcation occurs can be expressed as

\frac{V_{\text{dd}}^{*}}{V_{\text{T}}}=\log\left[1+\sec\left(\frac{\pi}{N}\right)\right]\,,

(24)

for odd
N
, where
\sec(x)=1/\cos(x)
. As
\sec(\pi/N)
decreases with increasing number of inverters for
N\geq 3
, the critical voltage
V_{\text{dd}}^{*}
decreases with increasing
N
.

If
V_{\text{dd}}
is increased even further, more pairs of Fourier modes
\pm k_{m}^{*}=[N\mp(2m-1)]/2
, where
m=2,\ldots,(N-1)/2
, become unstable through additional Hopf bifurcations. However, since these bifurcations emerge from the already unstable fixed point
\bm{v}^{*}
, they are much less relevant to the global dynamics than the primary bifurcation of the
k^{*}
mode at
V^{*}_{\text{dd}}
in Eq. (
24
), which renders the stable fixed point at
\bm{v}^{*}
unstable. We therefore call
k^{*}
the critical Fourier mode. Once this primary bifurcation has occurred, the system is driven away from
\bm{v}^{*}
and towards other stable states in the long-time limit. The occurrence of stable states in the vicinity of the bifurcation is determined by the bifurcation’s normal form, which we analyze in the following.

III.3
Hopf normal form

In order to determine the behavior of the system close to the bifurcation at
V^{*}_{\text{dd}}
, we consider small variations
\bm{x}=(\bm{v}-\bm{v}^{*})/V_{\text{T}}
around the fixed point
\bm{v}^{*}
with
|\bm{x}|\ll 1
. To this end, we Taylor-expand the deterministic dynamics Eq. (
11
) up to
\mathcal{O}(\bm{x}^{3})
, obtaining

\displaystyle\tau_{0}\dot{x}_{i}\sim

\displaystyle h_{10}x_{i}+h_{01}x_{i-1}+\frac{1}{6}\left(h_{30}x^{3}_{i}+3h_{21}x_{i}^{2}x_{i-1}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+3h_{12}x_{i}x^{2}_{i-1}+h_{03}x_{i-1}^{3}\right)\,,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2[x_{i}+(\text{e}^{V_{\text{dd}}/V_{\text{T}}}-1)x_{i-1}]+x_{i}^{2}x_{i-1}-x_{i}x_{i-1}^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{3}[x_{i}^{3}+(\text{e}^{V_{\text{dd}}/V_{\text{T}}}-1)x_{i-1}^{3}]\,,

(25)

where we have neglected
\mathcal{O}(\bm{x}^{4})
terms and used
h_{20}=h_{11}=h_{02}=0
,
h_{30}=h_{12}=-h_{21}=-2
, and
h_{03}=-2(\text{e}^{V_{\text{dd}}/V_{\text{T}}}-1)
. We observe that all quadratic terms in the expansion in Eq. (
III.3
) vanish. This property of the model makes the calculations that follow significantly simpler because it will allow us to simply read off the Hopf normal form from the Fourier-transformed dynamics without explicit calculations.

To get there, we decompose the variations
\bm{x}
into their discrete Fourier modes
\hat{\bm{x}}=(\hat{x}_{0},\hat{x}_{1},\ldots,\hat{x}_{N-1})^{\mathrm{T}}
by
\bm{\hat{x}}=\mathbb{F}\bm{x}
using the matrix
\mathbb{F}
in Eq. (
21
). We obtain

\displaystyle\tau_{0}\dot{\hat{x}}_{k}

\displaystyle\sim(\mu_{k}+i\omega_{k})\hat{x}_{k}+\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{k^{\prime},k^{\prime\prime}=0}^{N-1}\left[\frac{1}{6}(\mu_{k}+i\omega_{k})\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\text{e}^{\frac{i2\pi k^{\prime}}{N}}\left(1-\text{e}^{\frac{i2\pi k^{\prime\prime}}{N}}\right)\right]{\hat{x}}_{k^{\prime}}{\hat{x}}_{k^{\prime\prime}}{\hat{x}}_{k-k^{\prime}-k^{\prime\prime}}\,.

(26)

Since the variations
\bm{x}
are real, the Fourier components
\hat{x}_{k}
occur in complex conjugate pairs
z_{k}
and
\bar{z}_{k}
, such that

\displaystyle z_{k}=\hat{x}_{k},\qquad\bar{z}_{k}=\hat{x}_{-k}\,.

(27)

As we have observed in Sec.
III.2
, the trivial fixed point loses its stability when increasing
V_{\text{dd}}
across
V_{\text{dd}}^{*}
in Eq. (
24
), showing characteristics of a Hopf bifurcation in the plane spanned by
\hat{x}_{k^{*}}
and
\hat{x}_{-k^{*}}
. The parametric distance from the critical voltage
V_{\text{dd}}^{*}
is conveniently measured by the bifurcation parameter

\displaystyle\lambda=\frac{V_{\text{dd}}-V^{*}_{\text{dd}}}{V_{\text{T}}}\,.

(28)

For
\lambda>0
close to the bifurcation, i.e.,
\lambda\ll 1
, the long-term dynamics of the system are predominantly governed by the growing critical Fourier mode
z_{k^{*}}
with
k^{*}=(N-1)/2
, because
\mu_{k^{*}}>0
. All other modes
z_{k\neq k^{*}}
, on the contrary, have
\mu_{k}<0

In addition to the irrelevance of the non-critical modes, the analysis of

is further simplified by studying the normal form of the Hopf bifurcation of

. This simplified form of Eq. (

\displaystyle\tau_{0}\,\dot{z}_{k^{*}}\sim\left(\mathscr{D}_{k^{*}}-\mathscr{C}_{k^{*}}|z_{k^{*}}|^{2}\right)z_{k^{*}}\,.

(29)

The linear term

in Eq. (

, as discussed in Sec.

. We expand

to linear order in

\displaystyle\mu_{k^{*}}

\displaystyle=4\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{2N}\right)\lambda+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{2})\,,

(30)

\displaystyle\omega_{k^{*}}

\displaystyle=-\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{N}\right)\left(2+\mu_{k^{*}}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{2})\,.

(31)

The properties of small-amplitude oscillations are encoded in the cubic term in Eq. (

into real and imaginary parts,
\mathscr{C}_{k^{*}}=\mathscr{A}_{k^{*}}+i\mathscr{B}_{k^{*}}
. To leading order in

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}_{k^{*}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{N^{2}}\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{2N}\right)\left[4\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{2N}\right)-1\right]+\mathcal{O}(\lambda)

(32)

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}_{k^{*}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N^{2}}\left[\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{N}\right)+\cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{N}\right)\right]\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{N}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\lambda)\,.

(33)

To decompose the Hopf normal form [Eq. (

\displaystyle\tau_{0}\dot{r}_{k^{*}}

\displaystyle\sim\bigg(\mu_{k^{*}}-\mathscr{A}_{k^{*}}r_{k^{*}}^{2}\bigg)r_{k^{*}}\,,

(34)

\displaystyle\tau_{0}\dot{\varphi}_{k^{*}}

\displaystyle\sim\omega_{k^{*}}-\mathscr{B}_{k^{*}}r_{k^{*}}^{2}\,.

(35)

III.4
Stability

The stability of oscillating states is determined by a linear stability analysis of Eq. (
34
). The limit cycle corresponding to
r_{k^{*}}^{*}
is stable if the derivative
\mathbb{N}_{k^{*}}\equiv\partial\dot{r}_{k^{*}}/\partial r_{k^{*}}|_{r_{k^{*}}=r^{*}_{k^{*}}}
of the amplitude flow (
34
) is negative when evaluated on the limit cycle, i.e.,

\mathbb{N}_{k^{*}}=(\mu_{k^{*}}-3\mathscr{A}_{k^{*}}{r^{*}_{k^{*}}}^{2})=-2\mu_{k^{*}}<0\,.

(38)

Equation (
38
) confirms that the limit cycle is stable with respect to transverse disturbances for
\lambda>0
. Along the direction along the limit cycle, the stability exponent vanishes,
\partial\dot{\varphi}_{k^{*}}/\partial\varphi_{k^{*}}=0
, which means that longitudinal disturbances neither grow nor shrink. This enables free diffusion along the limit cycle, leading to long-time decoherence of the phase
\varphi_{k^{*}}
, which we explore in Sec.
IV.3
.

A simple measure for the overall local stability of a given state is the so-called phase-space contraction rate
\mathscr{L}
. It measures by how much infinitesimal phase-space volumes are contracted in the vicinity of a given state. We define the dimensionless phase-space contraction rate as
Ott 2002

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}=-\tau_{0}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\frac{\partial\dot{v}_{n}}{\partial v_{n}}\,.

(39)

Using the dynamics (
III.3
) close to the bifurcation and the Fourier transform, we can write
\mathscr{L}
as

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}\sim\mathscr{L}_{0}+\frac{8}{N}\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{2N}\right)r_{k^{*}}^{*2}\,,

(40)

where
\mathscr{L}_{0}=2N
is the phase-space contraction rate at the fixed point. Equation (
40
) shows that the rate of phase-space contraction increases in the presence of small amplitude oscillations for
\lambda>0
. We define the relative change
\Delta\mathscr{L}
of the phase-space contraction rate as

\displaystyle\Delta\mathscr{L}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathscr{L}-\mathscr{L}_{0}}{|\mathscr{L}_{0}|}\sim\frac{4}{N^{2}}\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{2N}\right)r_{k^{*}}^{*2}\,,

\displaystyle\sim\frac{2\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{2N}\right)}{\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{2N}\right)-\frac{1}{4}}\lambda>0\,,

(41)

for
\lambda>0
. We will return to this relation in Sec.
III.5
.

III.5
Entropy production rate

Small-amplitude oscillations for
\lambda>0
are facilitated by a constant non-equilibrium drive across each inverter. This drive keeps the system far from equilibrium and renders the dynamics irreversible. This irreversibility in turn is characterized by a finite rate of irreversible entropy production
\dot{\Sigma}
. In the large system-size limit (
\Omega\to\infty
), the time-averaged entropy production is equal to the time-averaged heat dissipated by the transistors
Freitas et al. 2021
;
Meibohm and Esposito 2024b
. Since the currents through the transistors and thus the entropy production scale with the size of the system
\Omega
, it is convenient to introduce the intensive entropy production rate
\dot{\sigma}\equiv\dot{\Sigma}/\Omega
.

Following the previous discussion,
\dot{\sigma}
can be directly computed from the macroscopic transition rates
\omega_{i\pm}^{\text{p/n}}

Falasco and Esposito 2025
, averaged over a period
T
of the limit cycle:

\displaystyle\dot{\sigma}=\frac{\tau_{0}}{T}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\sum_{\rho=\text{n},\text{p}}\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\text{d}t\left[\omega^{\rho}_{i+}(\bm{v})-\omega^{\rho}_{i-}(\bm{v})\right]\log\frac{\omega^{\rho}_{i+}(\bm{v})}{\omega^{\rho}_{i-}(\bm{v})}\,,

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2V_{\text{dd}}}{TV_{\text{T}}}\right)\!\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\text{d}t\left[\text{e}^{V_{\text{dd}}/V_{\text{T}}}\!\cosh(v_{n-1})\!-\!\cosh(v_{n}\!-\!v_{n-1})\right]\,.

(42)

In this dimensionless formulation, the
\Omega
-scaled entropy production rate
\dot{\sigma}
is measured in units of
k_{\text{B}}/\tau_{0}
, where
k_{\text{B}}
denotes Boltzmann’s constant. Close to the bifurcation, for
\lambda>0
,
\lambda\ll 1
, we have

\displaystyle\dot{\sigma}

\displaystyle\sim\dot{\sigma}_{0}+\left(\frac{2V_{\text{dd}}}{V_{\text{T}}N}\right)\left[\text{e}^{V_{\text{dd}}/V_{\text{T}}}-4\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{2N}\right)\right]r_{k^{*}}^{*2}\,,

\displaystyle\sim\dot{\sigma}_{0}\left\{1+\left[\frac{1}{2}-\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{N}\right)\right]\frac{4}{N^{2}}\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{2N}\right)r_{k^{*}}^{*2}\right\}\,.

(43)

where
\dot{\sigma}_{0}=\left(2V_{\text{dd}}N/V_{\text{T}}\right)\left(\text{e}^{V_{\text{dd}}/V_{\text{T}}}-1\right)
denotes the entropy production rate at the fixed point
\bm{v}^{*}
. Note that the time average in Eq. (
42
) disappears close to the bifurcation [see Eq. (
43
)], because
r^{*}_{k^{*}}
remains constant on the limit cycle to linear order in
\lambda
.

As
1/2-\cos\left(\pi/N\right)<0
for

, Eq. (
43
) shows that dissipation is reduced compared to
\dot{\sigma}_{0}
by the presence of small-amplitude oscillations. For

IV
Deviations from thermodynamic limit

In our analysis of the thermodynamic limit in Sec.
III
we have neglected finite-size voltage fluctuations. Such fluctuations are, however, present in any finite system, and are expected to be particularly relevant close to the oscillation transition. Going beyond the thermodynamic limit, we consider large but finite systems under the influence of weak finite-size fluctuations.

To this end, we perturbatively expand the Master equation (
7
) to next-to-leading order in the system size
\Omega
to capture the leading fluctuations. Although not generally suited to characterize global properties, such as fluctuation theorems, which depend on fluctuations of all magnitudes
Gopal et al. 2022
, such an expansion faithfully describes Gaussian fluctuations around the deterministic limit cycle discussed in Sec.
III.3
.

Expanding (
7
) up to
\mathcal{O}(1/\Omega)
, we obtain the Fokker-Planck equation

\partial_{t}P(\bm{v},t)\sim-\nabla_{\bm{v}}\cdot[\bm{F}(\bm{v})P(\bm{v},t)]+\frac{1}{\Omega}\nabla_{\bm{v}}^{\textsf{T}}\left[\mathbb{\hat{D}}(\bm{v})\nabla_{\bm{v}}P(\bm{v},t)\right]\,,

(46)

with diffusion matrix
\hat{D}_{ij}
given by

\hat{D}_{ij}=\frac{1}{\tau_{0}}\left[\cosh{\left(\frac{v_{i}-v_{i-1}}{V_{\text{T}}}\right)}+\text{e}^{V_{\text{dd}}/V_{\text{T}}}\cosh{\left(\frac{v_{i-1}}{V_{\text{T}}}\right)}\right]\delta_{ij}\,,

(47)

which is in diagonal form for the given model. Equation (
46
) corresponds to the following stochastic differential equation for the voltage vector
\bm{v}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}v_{i}}{V_{\text{T}}}

\displaystyle=F_{i}(\bm{v})\text{d}t+\sqrt{\frac{2\hat{D}_{ii}(\bm{v})}{\Omega}}\circ\text{d}W_{i}\,.

(48)

Here,
\circ\text{d}W_{i}
are independent Wiener increments in the Stratonovich convention
Gardiner 1985
, with
\langle\text{d}W^{2}_{i}(t)\rangle=\text{d}t
.

IV.1
Stochastic normal form

To analyze Eq. (
48
) further, we bring it into the stochastic version of the normal form (
29
), sometimes called “stochastic normal form”
Xiao et al. 2007
, which captures the fluctuations to leading order, close to the deterministic normal-form behavior.

In the weak-noise limit close to the bifurcation, there are two small parameters: The distance
\lambda
from the bifurcation and the inverse system size
\Omega^{-1}
, which fixes the noise strength. To identify the leading noise-induced effects close to the bifurcation, we expand
\hat{D}_{ij}
to the lowest order in
\lambda
, so that
D_{ii}(\bm{v})\sim(1+\text{e}^{V^{*}_{\text{dd}}/V_{\text{T}}})/\tau_{0}
. Taking the discrete Fourier transform of Eq. (
48
) and using the normal form in Sec.
III.3
for
\bm{F}
, we obtain the stochastic equation

\displaystyle\text{d}z_{k^{*}}

\displaystyle\sim\tau_{0}^{-1}\bigg(\mathscr{D}_{k^{*}}-\mathscr{C}_{k^{*}}|z_{k^{*}}|^{2}\bigg)z_{k^{*}}\text{d}t+\sqrt{\frac{2\bar{D}}{\Omega}}\circ\text{d}\hat{W}_{k^{*}}\,,

(49)

where
\bar{D}=N(1+\text{e}^{V^{*}_{\text{dd}}/V_{\text{T}}})/(2\tau_{0})
. Furthermore,

\displaystyle\text{d}\hat{W}_{k^{*}}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{N}}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\text{e}^{\frac{i2\pi nk^{*}}{N}}\circ\text{d}W_{n}\,,

(50)

is the weighted sum of
N
independent Wiener increments, which is itself a complex Wiener increment,
\text{d}\hat{W}_{k^{*}}=\text{d}\hat{W}^{\Re}_{k^{*}}+i\,\text{d}\hat{W}^{\Im}_{k^{*}}
, with
\langle\text{d}\hat{W}^{\Re}_{k^{*}}(t)^{2}\rangle=\langle\text{d}\hat{W}^{\Im}_{k^{*}}(t)^{2}\rangle=\text{d}t
and
\langle\text{d}\hat{W}^{\Re}_{k^{*}}(t)\text{d}\hat{W}^{\Im}_{k^{*}}(t)\rangle=0
.

We again transform into polar coordinates
(r_{k^{*}},\varphi_{k^{*}})
to obtain the stochastic phase-amplitude equations in the presence of finite-size fluctuations. For computational convenience, we first transform the coordinates and then move from the Stratonovich to the Itô convention
Gardiner 1985
to simplify the manipulations that follow.

In this way, we obtain stochastic phase-amplitude equations, given by

\displaystyle\text{d}r_{k^{*}}

\displaystyle\sim\tau_{0}^{-1}\left(\mu_{k^{*}}r_{k^{*}}-\mathscr{A}_{k^{*}}r_{k^{*}}^{3}+\frac{\tau_{0}\bar{D}}{r_{k^{*}}\Omega}\right)\,\text{d}t+\sqrt{\frac{2\bar{D}}{\Omega}}\cdot\text{d}\hat{W}^{r}_{t},

(51a)

\displaystyle\text{d}\varphi_{k^{*}}

\displaystyle\sim\tau_{0}^{-1}\left(\omega_{k^{*}}-\mathscr{B}_{k^{*}}r^{2}_{k^{*}}\right)\text{d}t+\frac{1}{r_{k^{*}}}\sqrt{\frac{2\bar{D}}{\Omega}}\cdot\text{d}\hat{W}^{\varphi}_{t}\,,

(51b)

where
\cdot\text{d}\hat{W}^{r}_{t}
and
\cdot\text{d}\hat{W}^{\varphi}_{t}
are independent Wiener increments in Itô convention with
\langle{\text{d}\hat{W}^{r}_{t}}^{2}\rangle=\langle{\text{d}\hat{W}^{\varphi}_{t}}^{2}\rangle=\text{d}t
and
\langle\text{d}\hat{W}^{r}_{t}\text{d}\hat{W}^{\varphi}_{t}\rangle=0
, which characterize finite-size fluctuations.

The presence of finite-size noise in the phase-amplitude equations has important consequences that we discuss in the following.

IV.2
Noise-induced oscillations

In the presence of finite-size voltage fluctuations, the system oscillates even for
\lambda\leq 0
, i.e., below and at the bifurcation. Such noise-induced oscillations arise as a combination of finite-size noise and the system’s oscillatory relaxation towards its fixed point. More precisely, for
\lambda\leq 0
, finite-size voltage fluctuations drive the system away from the stable fixed point, by means of the noise-induced term
\bar{D}/(r_{k^{*}}\Omega)
in Eq. (
51a
). This term, together with the noise term in Eq. (
51a
), vanishes as
\Omega\to\infty
, resulting in the deterministic dynamics discussed in Sec.
III
.

As a consequence of this, oscillations are no longer characterized by a single closed limit cycle as in Sec.
III
but by a probability density of possible amplitudes
r_{k^{*}}
and phases
\varphi_{k^{*}}
. To quantify the amplitudes of noise-induced fluctuations in the long-time limit, we first compute the steady-state probability density
P_{s}(r_{k^{*}})
from the Fokker-Planck equation corresponding to Eq. (
51a
). This leads us to

\displaystyle P_{s}(r_{k^{*}})=\mathscr{N}^{-1}r_{k^{*}}\,\exp\left[-\frac{1}{\zeta}\left(\frac{\mathscr{A}_{k^{*}}}{\mu_{k*}}r_{k^{*}}^{2}-1\right)^{2}\right]\,,

(52)

where the normalization
\mathscr{N}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathscr{N}=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{4}\frac{\zeta|\mu_{k^{*}}|}{\mathscr{A}_{k^{*}}}\left[\text{erf}\left(\frac{1}{\zeta}\right)+1\right]\,.

(53)

In Eq. (
52
), we have defined the dimensionless parameter

\zeta\equiv\sqrt{\frac{4\mathscr{A}_{k^{*}}\bar{D}\tau_{0}}{\Omega\mu_{k^{*}}^{2}}}\,,

(54)

which quantifies the importance of finite-size fluctuations. Sufficiently far away from the bifurcation for
\mu_{k^{*}}\propto\lambda\gg\Omega^{-1/2}
, fluctuations are mostly irrelevant, and we have
\zeta\ll 1
. In this case, Eq. (
52
) strongly focusses around the maximum of the distribution. Close to the bifurcation
\lambda\ll\Omega^{-1/2}
, by contrast, we have
\zeta\gg 1
. In this case, finite-size fluctuations are important even as the system size tends to infinity.

In particular, the significance of
\zeta
is understood by comparing the most likely amplitude
r^{ss}_{k^{*}}
with the magnitude of fluctuations around
r^{ss}_{k^{*}}
. The most likely amplitude
r^{ss}_{k^{*}}
is obtained from Eq. (
52
), by solving
P^{\prime}_{s}(r^{ss}_{k^{*}})=0
for
r^{ss}_{k^{*}}
, which gives

{r^{ss}_{k^{*}}}^{2}=\frac{|\mu_{k^{*}}|}{2\mathscr{A}_{k^{*}}}\left[\text{sign}(\lambda)+\sqrt{1+\zeta^{2}}\right]\,.

(55)

In the deterministic limit
\zeta\to 0
, Eq. (
55
) reduces to
{r^{ss}_{k^{*}}}^{2}=0
for
\lambda<0
and
{r^{ss}_{k^{*}}}^{2}=\mu_{k^{*}}/\mathscr{A}_{k^{*}}
for
\lambda>0
, in agreement with Eq. (
36
). For
\zeta\gg 1
, i.e., close to the bifurcation, we find
{r^{ss}_{k^{*}}}^{2}\sim\sqrt{\bar{D}\tau_{0}/(\Omega\mathscr{A}_{k^{*}})}>0
. Hence, as advertised earlier, finite-size fluctuations may induce coherent oscillations of finite amplitude, close to and even at the bifurcation, which are absent in the deterministic dynamics in Sec.
III
.

The magnitude of Gaussian fluctuations
\sigma_{k^{*}}^{ss}
around
r^{ss}_{k^{*}}
is estimated from
{\sigma^{ss}_{k^{*}}}^{-2}\sim P^{\prime\prime}_{s}(r^{ss}_{k^{*}})
, which gives

\displaystyle{\sigma^{ss}_{k^{*}}}^{2}\sim\frac{|\mu_{k^{*}}|}{2\mathscr{A}_{k^{*}}}\frac{\zeta^{2}}{4(1+\zeta^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}}\,.

(56)

This expression shows that in the deterministic limit
\zeta\to 0
, fluctuations around
r_{k^{*}}^{ss}
(of order
\sigma^{ss}_{k^{*}}
), decay as
\zeta\propto\Omega^{-1/2}
. Close to the bifurcation, for
\zeta\gg 1
, we have
{\sigma^{ss}_{k^{*}}}^{2}\sim\sqrt{\bar{D}\tau_{0}/(\Omega\mathscr{A}_{k^{*}})}/4
which is of the same order as
{r^{ss}_{k*}}^{2}
. This implies that when
\zeta

IV.3
Phase decoherence

As mentioned in the context of the stability analysis in Sec.
III.4
, finite-size fluctuations of the phase
\varphi
can propagate unobstructedly in the longitudinal direction along the limit cycle. This causes
\varphi_{k^{*}}
to diffuse freely along the limit cycle, leading to a long-term decoherence of the phase. This phase decoherence is harnessed when ring oscillators are used as true random-number generators
Robson et al. 2014
;
Bucci et al. 2003
;
Sunar et al. 2006
, because the phase difference between several ring oscillators is sensitive to “truely random” finite-size fluctuations. In this context, the rate at which independent, true random numbers can be generated is related to the decoherence time of
\varphi_{k^{*}}
. We now estimate this decoherence time from the stochastic normal form (
51
).

The decoherence time
\tau_{c}
of stochastic oscillations is quantified by the inverse exponential rate at which the auto-correlation function
\mathcal{C}_{n}(\tau)=\langle v_{n}(t)v_{n}(t+\tau)\rangle
decays as a function of
\tau
for
\tau,t\gg\tau_{0}
. In other words, the decoherence time
\tau_{c}
estimates the time over which an ensemble of initially coherent oscillators loses its coherence due to phase noise, eventually leading to uniform randomness of the output
v_{n}
.

At large but finite system size
\Omega\gg 1
, we obtain an expression for
\tau_{c}
by expanding the radial dynamics around the most probable value
r_{k^{*}}^{ss}
of the stationary distribution. In Appendix
A
, we show that such an expansion can be consistently formulated as long as the parameter

\displaystyle\bar{}\varepsilon\equiv f_{\text{sign}(\lambda)}\left(\zeta\right)\,,

(57)

becomes small for large system size, i.e.,
\bar{}\varepsilon\ll 1
for
\Omega\gg 1
. The function
f_{\pm}
has the form

\displaystyle f_{\pm}(\zeta)=\frac{\zeta}{2}\left(\sqrt{1+\zeta^{2}}\pm 2\right)\left(\sqrt{1+\zeta^{2}}\pm 1\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}(1+\zeta^{2})^{-\frac{3}{4}}\,.

(58)

From an analysis of
f_{\pm}(\zeta)
we find that
\bar{}\varepsilon
becomes small sufficiently far away from the bifurcation for
\zeta\ll 1
. In this case, one has

\displaystyle\bar{}\varepsilon=f_{+}(\zeta)\sim\frac{3\zeta}{\sqrt{8}}\ll 1\,.

(59)

This finding is intuitive because
\zeta\ll 1
corresponds to the case when fluctuations are small, as discussed in Sec.
IV.2
.

In the opposite limit, for
\zeta\gg 1
, i.e., close to the bifurcation,
\bar{}\varepsilon
remains of order unity even as
\Omega\to\infty
. The same holds outside the oscillating phase, for
\lambda<0
. In these regimes, expanding the dynamics around the maximum
r^{ss}_{k^{*}}
is uncontrolled.

If
\bar{}\varepsilon\ll 1
, on the other hand, the dynamics can be systematically expanded around
r^{ss}_{k^{*}}
. Based on such an expansion, we show in Appendix
A
that, to leading order, the decoherence time
\tau_{c}
takes the form

\displaystyle\tau^{-1}_{c}\sim\frac{\bar{D}}{\Omega}\left({r^{ss}_{k^{*}}}^{-2}+\frac{\mathscr{B}_{k^{*}}^{2}{r^{ss}_{k^{*}}}^{2}}{\mathscr{A}_{k^{*}}{r^{ss}_{k^{*}}}^{2}-\frac{\mu_{k^{*}}}{2}}\right)\,.

(60)

In the deterministic limit
\zeta\ll 1
, this expression agrees with that found in
Remlein et al. 2022
, where the expansion was carried out around the deterministic radius
r^{*}_{k^{*}}
in Eq. (
36
), instead of
r^{ss}_{k^{*}}
. The first term in Eq. (
60
) corresponds to decoherence due to phase diffusion with diffusion constant
D_{\varphi}(r^{ss}_{k^{*}})=\bar{D}/(\Omega{r^{ss}_{k^{*}}}^{2})
evaluated at the maximum
r^{ss}_{k^{*}}
of the radial distribution. The second term is a consequence of weak interactions between the radial and phase dynamics
Remlein et al. 2022
.

In the oscillating state sufficiently far away from the bifurcation, when
\bar{}\varepsilon\sim\zeta\ll 1
, we have
r^{ss}_{k^{*}}\sim\lambda^{1/2}
, so that Eq. (
60
) predicts
\tau_{c}
to grow linearly with system size, i.e.
\tau_{c}\sim\Omega

Gaspard 2002
.

Close to the bifurcation for
\zeta\gg 1
, Eq. (
60
) is not strictly valid, as it is based on an uncontrolled approximation. However, since the range of validity of Eq. (
60
) approaches the bifurcation at
\lambda=0
as
\Omega\to\infty
, we speculate that Eq. (
60
) still predicts the dominant scaling of
\tau_{c}
with
\Omega
, albeit possibly with wrong prefactor, in the limit of infinite system size. In the regime
\zeta\gg 1
,
r_{k^{*}}^{ss}
scales as
r_{k^{*}}^{ss}\sim\Omega^{-1/4}
, so that Eq. (
60
) predicts
\tau_{c}
to increase as
\tau_{c}\sim\Omega^{1/2}
, substantially slower than away from the bifurcation. We confirm these scalings numerically below. Furthermore, these results are consistent with the findings in Ref.
Nguyen et al. 2018
, where the authors numerically observed the same scalings close to and far away from bifurcations in several biochemical oscillator models.

Finally, we briefly comment on the relevance of the scaling properties of

for the

;

;

;
Remlein et al. 2022
;
Santolin and Falasco 2025
are irrelevant here. This statement is true in particular close to the bifurcation, where oscillations are exceptionally decoherent, leading to a scaled quality factor that shrinks as
\Omega^{-1/2}
.

V
Conclusions

We analyzed small-amplitude oscillations and their fluctuations in
N
-stage CMOS ring oscillators operating in the subthreshold regime. Starting with a thermodynamically consistent model, we demonstrated that, in the thermodynamic limit
\Omega\to\infty
, oscillations emerge through a Hopf bifurcation resulting in a stable limit cycle when the supply voltage
V_{\text{dd}}
exceeds a critical threshold
V_{\text{dd}}^{*}
. Exploiting the symmetries of the model, we used a normal-form analysis to obtain explicit expressions for the limit cycle and its frequency for any odd
N
, which are valid close to the bifurcation. Employing the framework of stochastic thermodynamics, we showed that the entropy production
\dot{\sigma}
of ring oscillators decreases in the presence of oscillations for
N>3
, but increases sub-linearly for
N=3
. The change
\Delta\dot{\sigma}
in the entropy production across the bifurcation is linearly related to the change in phase-space contraction
\Delta\mathscr{L}
through a stability-dissipation relation [Eq. (
44
)], similar to that found in Refs.
Meibohm and Esposito 2024a
;
Meibohm and Esposito 2024b
.

For large but finite devices
\Omega=V_{\text{T}}/v_{e}\gg 1
, we characterized finite-size fluctuations using a systematic system-size expansion to capture the lowest-order effects of fluctuations around the deterministic limit cycle. Using a stochastic version of the Hopf normal form, we showed that finite-size fluctuations remain relevant close to the bifurcation, even in the thermodynamic limit, which is a common feature in the vicinity of phase transitions. In this regime, we showed that fluctuations induce finite-amplitude oscillations, even at and below the critical voltage. These noise-induced oscillations are characterized by an anomalously short decoherence time that was shown to scale sub-linearly
\tau_{c}\sim\Omega^{1/2}
with
\Omega
.

This exceptional fast decoherence offers a route toward tunable randomness in probabilistic hardware, such as deterministic “Ising machines”
Sahoo 2017
;
Rahaman Nayan and Hassan 2025
;
Gonul and Taskin 2024
or stochastic probabilistic-bit systems
Aadit et al. 2022
. Assessing the practical feasibility of such approaches and benchmarking them against existing subthreshold-CMOS p-bit implementations
Jelinčič et al. 2025
, remains an important direction for future work.

A key limitation of our analysis is that supply voltages must be small, of the order of the thermal voltage (
\sim 26\,\text{m}V
at room temperature). Further investigations are required to determine whether the permutation symmetry used here allows for simplifications, even further away from the critical voltage.

Appendix A
Decoherence timescale calculation - Stochastic Normal Form

We present the details for the computation of the decorrelation time for the stochastic normal form (
51
). We again consider

\displaystyle\text{d}r_{k^{*}}

\displaystyle\sim-\tau_{0}^{-1}V^{\prime}(r_{k^{*}})\,\text{d}t+\sqrt{\frac{2\bar{D}}{\Omega}}\cdot\text{d}\hat{W}_{t}^{r}\,,

(61a)

\displaystyle\text{d}\varphi_{k^{*}}

\displaystyle\sim\tau_{0}^{-1}\left(\omega_{k^{*}}-\mathscr{B}_{k^{*}}r^{2}_{k^{*}}\right)\text{d}t+\frac{1}{r}\sqrt{\frac{2\bar{D}}{\Omega}}\cdot\text{d}\hat{W}_{t}^{\varphi}\,,

(61b)

where

\displaystyle V(r)=-\frac{\mu_{k^{*}}r^{2}}{2}+\mathscr{A}_{k^{*}}\frac{r^{4}}{4}-\frac{\bar{D}\tau_{0}}{\Omega}\log(r)\,.

(62)

We proceed by expanding
V(r)
around its minimum at
r^{ss}_{k^{*}}
, see Eq. (
55
). This yields for
V^{\prime}(r_{k^{*}})
,

V^{\prime}(r_{k^{*}})\sim V^{\prime}(r^{*})+V^{\prime\prime}(r^{ss}_{k^{*}})(r_{k^{*}}-r^{ss}_{k^{*}})\\
+\frac{1}{2}V^{\prime\prime\prime}(r^{*})(r_{k^{*}}-r^{ss}_{k^{*}})^{2}\,.

(63)

The minimum condition requires
V^{\prime}(r^{ss}_{k^{*}})=0
, and we have

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle\equiv V^{\prime\prime}(r^{ss}_{k^{*}})=2|\mu_{k^{*}}|\sqrt{1+\zeta^{2}}\,,

(64)

\displaystyle\xi

\displaystyle\equiv V^{\prime\prime\prime}(r^{ss}_{k^{*}})=6\mathscr{A}_{k^{*}}r^{ss}_{k^{*}}-\frac{2\bar{D}\tau_{0}}{{r^{ss}_{k^{*}}}^{3}\Omega}\,,

(65)

We thus obtain the following expression for the dynamics of
\rho=r_{k^{*}}-r^{ss}_{k^{*}}
:

\displaystyle\text{d}\rho

\displaystyle\sim-\tau_{0}^{-1}(\kappa\rho+\xi\rho^{2})\,\text{d}t+\sqrt{\frac{2\bar{D}\tau_{0}}{\Omega}}\cdot\text{d}\hat{W}^{r}_{t}\,\,.

(66)

We rescale
\rho
according to
\rho=\sqrt{\bar{D}\tau_{0}/(\Omega\kappa)}\bar{\rho}
and time as
t=\tau_{0}\bar{t}/\kappa
. This way, Eq. (
66
) depends on a single parameter. We obtain

\displaystyle\text{d}\bar{\rho}

\displaystyle\sim\left(-\bar{\rho}-\bar{}\varepsilon\rho^{2}\,\right)\text{d}\bar{t}+\sqrt{2}\cdot\text{d}\hat{W}^{r}_{\bar{t}}\,\,,

(67)

with the parameter

\displaystyle\bar{}\varepsilon\equiv\sqrt{\frac{\bar{D}\tau_{0}}{\Omega\kappa^{3}}}\xi\,,

(68)

which agrees with Eq. (
57
) when expressed in terms of
\zeta
. For small
\bar{}\varepsilon
, we expand

\displaystyle\bar{\rho}\sim\bar{\rho}^{(0)}+\bar{}\varepsilon\bar{\rho}^{(1)}+\bar{}\varepsilon^{2}\bar{\rho}^{(2)}+\ldots\,,

(69)

and evaluate the resulting equation (
67
) order by order in
\bar{}\varepsilon
. To linear order in
\bar{}\varepsilon
, we find

\displaystyle\text{d}\bar{\rho}^{(0)}

\displaystyle\sim-\bar{\rho}^{(0)}\,\text{d}\bar{t}+\sqrt{2}\cdot\text{d}\hat{W}^{r}_{\bar{t}}\,\,,

(70)

\displaystyle\text{d}\bar{\rho}^{(1)}

\displaystyle\sim-(1+2\bar{\rho}^{(0)})\bar{\rho}^{(1)}\,\text{d}\bar{t}\,\,.

(71)

To lowest order in
\bar{}\varepsilon
,
\bar{\rho}^{(0)}
is an standard Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, while
\bar{\rho}^{(1)}
is a non-Gaussian correction, that we disregard in the following. We note, however, that systematic expansions around the lowest order process
\bar{\rho}^{(0)}
can be constructed in this way.

We now consider the correlation function in the long-time limit

\displaystyle C_{n}(\tau)=\langle\nu_{n}(\tau)\nu_{n}(0)\rangle\sim\left(\frac{V_{\text{T}}}{N}\right)^{2}\mathcal{C}(\tau)+\text{c.c.}\,,

(72)

where
\mathcal{C}(\tau)=\langle z_{k^{*}}(\tau)\bar{z}_{k^{*}}(0)\rangle
and c.c. denotes the complex conjugate. To study the exponential decay of
C_{n}(\tau)
we thus evaluate

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\langle r_{k^{*}}(\tau)r_{k^{*}}(0)\,\text{e}^{i[\varphi_{k^{*}}(\tau)-\varphi_{k^{*}}(0)]}\rangle\,.

(73)

Integrating (
61b
), we obtain

\varphi_{k^{*}}(\tau)-\varphi_{k^{*}}(0)=\kappa^{-1}\!\!\int_{0}^{\tau\kappa}\!\!\!f[r_{k^{*}}(\bar{t})]\text{d}\bar{t}\\
+\sqrt{\frac{2\bar{}\varepsilon^{2}\kappa^{2}}{\xi^{2}}}\int_{0}^{\tau\kappa}\!\!\!g[r_{k^{*}}(\bar{t})]\cdot\text{d}\hat{W}_{\bar{t}}^{\varphi}\,,

(74)

where
f(r_{k^{*}})=\omega_{k^{*}}-\mathscr{B}_{k^{*}}{r_{k^{*}}}^{2}
and
g(r_{k^{*}})=1/r_{k^{*}}
.
In order to evaluate
\mathcal{C}(\tau)
, we first average over
\hat{W}_{t}^{\varphi}
, conditional on a fixed

\{r_{k^{*}}(t)\}_{t\in[0,\tau]}
, leading to

\mathcal{C}(\tau)=\Big\langle r_{k^{*}}(\tau)r_{k^{*}}(0)\Big\langle\text{e}^{i(\varphi_{\tau}-\varphi_{0})}\,\Big|\,\{r_{k^{*}}(t)\}\Big\rangle_{\{\hat{W}^{\varphi}_{\bar{t}}\}}\Big\rangle_{\{r_{k^{*}}(t)\}}=\Big\langle r_{k^{*}}(\tau)r_{k^{*}}(0)\text{e}^{i\kappa^{-1}\!\!\int_{0}^{\tau\kappa}\!\!f[r_{k^{*}}(\bar{t})]\text{d}\bar{t}-\frac{\bar{}\varepsilon^{2}\kappa^{2}}{\xi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\tau\kappa}\!\!g^{2}[r_{k^{*}}(\bar{t})]\text{d}\bar{t}}\Big\rangle_{\{r_{k^{*}}(t)\}}\,.

(75)

We express
r_{k^{*}}(\bar{t})
as
r_{k^{*}}(\bar{t})=r^{ss}_{k^{*}}+\bar{}\varepsilon(\kappa/\xi)\bar{\rho}(\bar{t})
and use the small-
\bar{}\varepsilon
expansion (
69
) to find to lowest non-vanishing order in
\bar{}\varepsilon

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\tau)\sim

\displaystyle{r^{ss}_{k^{*}}}^{2}\text{e}^{\left[i\omega^{ss}_{k^{*}}-\frac{\bar{}\varepsilon^{2}\kappa^{3}}{\xi^{2}}g^{2}(r^{ss}_{k^{*}})\right]\tau}\Big\langle\text{e}^{i\frac{\bar{}\varepsilon}{\xi}f^{\prime}(r^{ss}_{k^{*}})\!\!\int_{0}^{\tau\kappa}\!\!\bar{\rho}^{(0)}(\bar{t})\text{d}\bar{t}}\Big\rangle_{\{\rho(\bar{t})\}}\,,

(76)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle{r^{ss}_{k^{*}}}^{2}\text{e}^{\left[i\omega^{ss}_{k^{*}}-\frac{\bar{}\varepsilon^{2}\kappa^{3}}{\xi^{2}}g^{2}(r^{ss}_{k^{*}})\right]\tau-\frac{\bar{}\varepsilon^{2}}{2\xi^{2}}{f^{\prime}(r^{ss}_{k^{*}})}^{2}\text{Var}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau\kappa}\!\!\bar{\rho}^{(0)}(\bar{t})\text{d}\bar{t}\right)}\,,

(77)

where
\omega^{ss}_{k^{*}}=f(r^{ss}_{k^{*}})=\omega_{k^{*}}-\mathscr{B}_{k^{*}}{r^{ss}_{k^{*}}}^{2}
corresponds to the oscillation frequency at
r^{ss}_{k^{*}}
. Evaluating
\text{Var}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau\kappa}\!\!\bar{\rho}^{(0)}(\bar{t})\text{d}\bar{t}\right)
for long times
\tau\kappa\gg 1
gives

\displaystyle\text{Var}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau\kappa}\!\!\bar{\rho}^{(0)}(\bar{t})\text{d}\bar{t}\right)\sim 2\tau\kappa\,.

(78)

We thus obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\tau)\sim{r^{ss}_{k^{*}}}^{2}\text{e}^{i\omega^{ss}_{k^{*}}\tau-\tau/\tau_{c}}\,,

(79)

where we have identified the decorrelation time
\tau_{c}
as

\displaystyle\tau^{-1}_{c}\sim\frac{\bar{}\varepsilon^{2}\kappa}{\tau_{0}\xi^{2}}\left[\kappa^{2}g^{2}(r^{ss}_{k^{*}})+{f^{\prime}(r^{ss}_{k^{*}})}^{2}\right]\,,

(80)

to leading order in
\bar{}\varepsilon
. Reinstalling the original parameters and substituting the expressions for
f
and
g
we thus obtain

\displaystyle\tau^{-1}_{c}\sim\frac{\bar{D}}{\Omega\kappa^{2}}\left[\frac{\kappa^{2}}{{r^{ss}_{k^{*}}}^{2}}+4\mathscr{B}_{k^{*}}^{2}{r^{ss}_{k^{*}}}^{2}\right]\,,

(81)

which agrees with Eq. (
60
) in the main text.
