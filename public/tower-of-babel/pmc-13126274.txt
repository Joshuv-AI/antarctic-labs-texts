JAK2 V617F mutations and thrombosis in BCR-ABL1 negative myeloproliferative neoplasms: clinical profile and hematologic insights
Authors: Anna Mira Lubis, Muhammad Orri Baskoro, Gabriella Anindyah, Agnes Stephanie Harahap
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe interplay between JAK2 mutation, thrombosis, and the baseline clinical and hematologic profile of MPN remains poorly understood. This study aims to provide insight on the relationship between the clinical profile and hematologic findings, JAK2 V617F mutation, and the occurrence of thrombotic events in BCR-ABL1 negative MPNs.Materials and methodsWe retrospectively analyzed 198 patients with BCR-ABL1-negative MPNs to assess associations between JAK2 V617F mutation status, hematologic parameters, clinical features, and thrombotic events.ResultsPatients under 40 years old were associated with JAK2 V617F-negative MPN (p<0.05). In PV patients, cardiovascular comorbidities were associated with a 4.5-fold increased risk of thrombosis (OR 4.59; CI 1.57-13.43; p=0.005). Mutated-JAK2 V617F MPN was associated with lower platelets and higher WBC count, while thrombosis was associated with higher hemoglobin and hematocrit (p<0.01). However, we found no associations between JAK2 V617F mutation and thrombotic events across all MPN subtypes.ConclusionCardiovascular comorbidity are an independent risk factor for thrombosis in PV. While JAK2 V617F mutation was associated with distinct hematologic profiles, it was not independently linked to thrombotic risk. These findings highlight the significance of clinical comorbidities and hematologic parameters in thrombotic event occurrences in MPN patients.Keywords: MPN, Thrombosis, JAK2, Clinical profile

Introduction

The Myeloproliferative Neoplasms (MPNs) refer to a diverse set of hematopoietic stem cell disorders characterized by the increased proliferation of myeloid blood cell lineages in the bone marrow [1]. MPNs are classified by the World Health Organization into four subgroups, namely (1) Chronic Myeloid Leukemia (CML), (2) classical BCR-ABL1 negative (BCR-ABL1) MPNs (Essential Thrombocythemia, ET; Polycythemia Vera, PV; Primary Myelofibrosis, PMF), (3) non-classical BCR-ABL1 negative MPNs (Chronic Neutrophilic Leukemia, CNL; Chronic Eosinophilic Leukemia, CEL), and (4) MPN, unclassifiable (MPN-U) [2]. Our study will focus on classical BCR-ABL1 negative MPN, with the division of PMF into pre-PMF and overt PMF, to highlight the differences between these subtypes with ET, which is often challenging to differentiate clinically and pathologically. BCR-ABL1 negative MPN is characterized by its slow-growing clinical course, non-destructive hematopoiesis, and higher rates of fibrotic evolution [3]. While they have a relatively long survival, BCR-ABL1 negative MPN are challenging to diagnose and treat since they are more prone to develop overt myelofibrosis, thrombotic events, and transformation to acute leukemia [1,4].

Thrombotic events often accompany BCR-ABL1 negative MPN clinical course [5]. Pathogenesis of thrombosis MPN patients, however, is complex and influenced by a multitude of factors, such as clinical factors (age, previous history of thrombotic events, obesity, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia) and an increase in blood cell counts (leukocytosis, erythrocytosis, and thrombocytosis). These factors contribute to the higher risk of thrombosis in these patients to varying degrees [6,7]. Despite current medical management, thrombosis events in MPN patients cause lower survival, with ECLAP study reported that thrombosis accounted for 41% of deaths in PV, a significantly higher rate than general population [8,9]. Therefore, managing the risk of these complications is an important part of patient care [10,11].

Identifying mutation in the erythropoietin (EPO) receptor's Janus kinase 2 domain (JAK2 V617F) has significantly enhanced our comprehension of the molecular pathogenesis and pathophysiology of MPN [4,12]. JAK2 V617F mutation is reported to cause abnormalities in erythrocytes, platelet, and white blood cell structure and function, as well as endothelial cell dysfunction, leading to increased cell aggregation and activation of endothelium, which causes a higher risk of thrombosis [13]. Consequently, there have been updates to diagnostic criteria, the development of new prognostic models, and the inclusion of JAK2 inhibitors in clinical practice [14]. However, this discovery has not yet led to significant changes in the management of MPN and its risk of thrombosis. Additionally, there is still uncertainty about the factors that affect the variability in blood counts and thrombotic event occurrences in MPN patients [15]. This study will explore the association of JAK2 mutation with the clinical profile and hematologic findings of BCR-ABL1 negative MPN and how they may influence the occurrence of thrombotic events in these patients.

Methods

Study design and study population

This study was approved by the Ethical Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Universitas Indonesia, Indonesia, and performed at RSUPN Cipto Mangunkusumo Hospital. All BCR ABL1-negative MPN (PV, ET, pre-PMF, and PMF) patients who attended the Hematology Clinic from January 1st, 2016 through December 31st, 2023 were retrospectively reviewed for demographic data (age, sex, underlying diseases), clinical characteristics (cardiovascular comorbidity and splenomegaly), CBC at the diagnosis, JAK2V617F mutation analysis, treatment, and thrombotic event occurrences (e.g., imaging-confirmed DVT/PE, ischemic stroke by clinical/imaging criteria) diagnosed prior to or occurred during MPN diagnosis. Cardiovascular comorbidity is defined as the presence of at least one of the following conditions: hypertension, dyslipidemia, or diabetes. MPN diagnosis was based on 2016 WHO criteria for PV, ET, pre-PMF, and overt PMF. The study population was adult (≥ 18 years of age) MPN patients. The Exclusion criteria were patients with concomitant hematologic disorders (e.g., thalassemia, hemolytic anemia) that could interfere complete blood count to minimize confounding bias to the analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 25). Descriptive statistics were employed to summarize the numeric variable, with means and standard deviation presented for normally distributed data, whereas median with first and third quartile was presented for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were presented as proportions (percentages). Baseline characteristics were evaluated according to MPN subtypes, namely polycythemia vera (PV), essential thrombocythemia (ET), prefibrotic primary myelofibrosis (pre-PMF), and primary myelofibrosis (PMF), followed by a subgroup analysis based on their JAK2 V617F mutation. Chi-square test was utilized to compare proportions, while One-way ANOVA was employed for continuous variables. Fischer's exact test was applied for variables not meeting the chi-square condition, while Mann-Whitney was applied for continuous variables with abnormal data distribution. A significance level of p < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant. Thrombotic events were assessed in relation to age, sex, JAK2 positivity, splenomegaly, hypercellularity, and comorbidity through bivariate analysis using Chi-square tests. Variables with p < 0.25 were further analyzed in a multivariate analysis using logistic regression, with results presented as odds ratio (OR). Again, a p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The most prevalent subtypes were polycythemia vera (PV; n=72, 36.4%) and essential thrombocythemia (ET; n=71, 35.9%), followed by overt primary myelofibrosis (PMF; n=37, 18.7%) and pre-fibrotic PMF (pre-PMF; n=18, 9.1%).. Cardiovascular comorbidities were present in 53.5% of the cohort, most commonly as hypertension (34.3%). Thrombotic events were reported in 29.8% of patients, with arterial thrombosis accounting for 27.3% and venous thrombosis for 2.5%. PV patients exhibited the highest incidence of thrombosis (45.8%), while PMF had the lowest (8.1%). Arterial events predominated across all subtypes. Multivariate logistic regression identified cardiovascular comorbidities as a strong independent risk factor for thrombosis in PV patients (OR 4.59; 95% CI 1.57–13.43; p=0.005). No significant associations with thrombosis were found in other subtypes.

Splenomegaly was present in 39.9% of patients, most commonly in PMF (78.3%) and pre-PMF (66.7%). The majority of splenomegaly cases were classified as Schuffner grades 1–4; higher grades (>4) were uncommon but more frequent in JAK2-positive cases. Bone marrow hypercellularity was noted in 78.3% of the total cohort, with pre-PMF showing the highest rate (100%), followed by PV (90.3%), PMF (70.3%), and ET (64.8%).

The complete blood count profiles across different MPN subtypes are presented in Table 1. The mean hemoglobin (Hb) and hematocrit levels were 14.3 g/dl and 43.8%, respectively, with PV exhibiting the highest values (18.0 g/dl and 55%) and PMF the lowest (10.3 g/dl and 30.8%). The platelet counts mean was 890.8×109/L, with Pre-PMF displaying the highest count (1,252×109/L) and PMF the lowest (540.9×109/L). White blood cell (WBC) counts had a mean of 20.7×109/L, with PMF displaying the highest count (29.2×109/L) and ET the lowest (14.5×109/L). Notably, WBC subtypes exhibited a wide range of values, with significant variations in eosinophil, neutrophil, and lymphocyte percentages, although these differences were deemed clinically insignificant. Basophil and neutrophil levels were observed to be slightly elevated across all MPN subtypes, while lymphocyte counts were consistently low.

The complete blood count profile analysis based on JAK2 positivity and thrombotic event was presented in Table 3. Platelet counts were significantly higher in JAK2-negative patients, with a median of 1,037×109/L, compared to 714×109/L in JAK2-positive individuals. Conversely, white blood cell (WBC) counts were elevated in JAK2-positive patients, with a median of 18.96×109/L, compared to 13.31×109/L in JAK2-negative counterparts. Interestingly, it was found to be consistent in all MPN except PV, with PV JAK2-positive individuals exhibiting lower WBC (18.9×109/L vs 25.81×109/L). Furthermore, our study revealed notable associations between thrombotic events with hemoglobin and hematocrit levels (p<0.01). Patients experiencing thrombotic events exhibited higher mean hemoglobin (15.76 g/dl) and hematocrit (49.18%) compared to those without thrombotic events (13.61 g/dl and 41.53%, respectively). This finding was prominently observed in PMF patients, which showed higher hemoglobin in patients experiencing thrombotic events (13.37 g/dl vs 10.03 g/dl) (Table 4).

Discussion

This study aimed to provide insight into the clinical profile and hematologic findings of BCR-ABL1 negative MPN patients in relation to JAK2 V617F mutation and thrombotic events. The mean age of this study was 52.4 years old, comparable to previous studies in Malaysia [16] and Thailand [17] (54.2 and 59.6 years old, respectively), yet lower than studies reported from Norway [18] and the US [19] (69 and 65 years old, respectively), suggesting potential epidemiological and genetic differences in MPN presentation. Genetic and familial factors is a possible contributing factor for this differences. Rumi et al reported a younger age of onset in familial cases of MPN compared to sporadic ones, possibly linked to telomere shortening [20,21]. Therefore, investigation into the familial clustering of MPN within Asian populations is also recommended to provide deeper insight on MPN genomic epidemiology. Additionally, younger age has also been reported to be associated with the rare JAK2 exon 12 mutation, which is typically only detected in 3% of the cases, although this mutation is not tested in this study [22,23].

Our study highlights the importance of splenomegaly presentation in PMF patients, as it was observed in 78.3% of PMF and 66.7% of pre-PMF, supporting its inclusion for the minor diagnosis criteria of PMF in WHO classification [24]. In contrast, splenomegaly was least prevalent in ET (21.1%), with none of them exhibiting Schuffner grade >4. Therefore, splenomegaly, especially when presenting with Schuffner grade >4, can be a valuable finding in distinguishing pre-PMF from ET, which is commonly hard to differentiate [25]. Furthermore, while both of them demonstrated similar extreme thrombocytosis, our study found that pre-PMF had a significantly higher mean of WBC than ET (26.8×109/L vs 13.1×109/L respectively) and lower hemoglobin levels (11.9 g/dl vs 13.1 g/dl respectively), offering other useful hematologic insights.

The JAK2 V617F mutation remains the most commonly reported mutation in BCR-ABL1-negative MPNs, particularly in PV, where its prevalence is typically 95–98% [26]. Our study, however, JAK2 V617F was detected in only 81.9% of PV patients, comparable to the Malaysian[16] (77.9%), Thailand[17] (87.5%), and Turkey[27] (81.5%) populations. These findings suggest that the frequency of JAK2 V617F mutations in PV may be lower in Asian populations, In contrast, pre-PMF and PMF JAK2V617F mutation in our study was 83.3% and 81.1%, respectively, similar to Malaysia (82,8%), Thailand (100%), and Turkey (76,2%), higher than studies from other regions (50-65%) [[28], [29], [30]]. These findings suggest that reliance on JAK2 V617F as a diagnostic marker for PV may have reduced sensitivity in Asian populations, supporting the 2016 WHO update that elevated bone marrow morphology to a major diagnostic criterion [24].

Subgroup analysis based on JAK2 mutation status revealed a striking age distribution difference. JAK2-negative MPN patients were significantly younger, with 23.9% under 40 years of age, compared to only 8.4% of JAK2-positive patients. This finding was more pronounced in overt PMF, with 42.9% of JAK2-negative patients were below 40 years old, whereas only 3.3% of JAK2-positive patients were in this age group. Similarly, in ET, 27.3% of JAK2-negative patients were below 40 years old, compared to only 7.4% of JAK2-positive patients. These findings are consistent with Lee et al., who found that lower JAK2 allele burden was associated with younger age in ET but not in PV [31]. No significant differences were observed in PV or pre-PMF patients. We also found no meaningful sex-based differences in JAK2 mutational status across subtypes, consistent with prior literature [16,27].

Although splenomegaly is frequently reported at MPN diagnosis and has been associated with worse prognosis [32], its relationship with JAK2 mutation status remains unclear and varied [27,33], In our study, no significant associations were observed across all subtypes, although we observed relatively higher splenomegaly incidence in JAK2-positive ET and lower splenomegaly incidence in JAK2-positive PV, similar to the study by Sahin et al [27]. Previous study by Barosi reported that JAK2 mutation were associated with splenomegaly in primary myelofibrosis [33]. Our study, however, did not observe this finding.

Interestingly, among patients with splenomegaly with schuffner grade above 4, seven of eight were JAK2-positive, raising the possibility of a mutation-related spleen burden, though larger studies are needed to explore this further. Additionally, Patel et al. showed that spleen response to JAK2 inhibitors like ruxolitinib was more strongly influenced by mutations in ASXL1, EZH2, and IDH1/2 than by JAK2, CALR, or MPL [34]. This suggests spleen enlargement may reflect complex mutational interactions beyond JAK2 alone.

JAK2 V617F is known to cause constitutive activation of the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, promoting hematopoietic cells hypersesitivity to cytokines such as EPO, TMP, G-CSF, or GM-CSF, resulting in abnormal proliferation of blood lineages among JAK2-mutated MPN [35]. Previous studies by Yap et al. and Sahin et al. observed significantly higher WBC and lower platelet counts in patients with JAK2 mutations, which is consistent with our findings [16,27]. However, our study conducted a subanalysis of each MPN subtype and found no statistical differences in platelet and WBC counts in PV, ET, pre-PMF, or overt PMF, suggesting the overall pattern may be driven by subtype-specific distributions—especially ET, which had a high proportion of JAK2-negative patients. Furthermore, we also observed higher platelet counts in JAK2-negative ET, nearly reaching statistical significance, similar to previous studies by Campbell et al and Marchetti et al. [36,37]. Sahin et al. reported higher WBC counts in JAK2-mutated PMF patients, a trend we observed in our study as well, although it did not reach statistical significance [27]. Associations between JAK2 mutation with hemoglobin and hematocrit level remain controversial in previous studies, and we observed no associations across all MPN types [16,38,39].

Thrombosis remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality in MPN patients, influenced by complex factors such as age, obesity, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and blood cell abnormalities [10]. While the JAK2 V617F mutation has been linked to thrombosis in some studies, such as venous thrombosis in ET [36], others have not observed a clear association [17]. Our study similarly found no significant correlation between JAK2 status and thrombotic risk across all subtypes. Interestingly, however, all thrombotic events in PMF patients occurred in those with JAK2 mutations. This observation aligns with findings from Barbui et al., suggesting that JAK2 mutation may have a pronounced prothrombotic effect in PMF compared to PV or ET, likely due to its effect on elevated WBC counts [40]. This also could be mediated by the phenotypic and functional hypercoagulable alterations of WBC associated with JAK2 V617F [37].

We found no significant associations between thrombosis and WBC, platelet count, or hemoglobin/hematocrit in our multivariate models. This contrasts with some studies linking leukocytosis and thrombosis [8,41]. However thrombotic risk may may not only influenced by hematologic parameters quantitatively but also qualitatively. For example, Latagliata et al. observed that higher platelet counts were not associated with thrombosis and might be protective against thrombosis, potentially due to the acquired form of von Willebrand disease in extreme thrombocytosis [42]. Additionally, the increased formation of neutrophil-platelet aggregates, which is correlated with the higher expression of activation markers CD11b and CD62P in patients with ET and PV, was also found to be associated with thrombosis [43]. These findings suggest that peripheral blood counts alone may be insufficient to assess thrombotic risk, as functional alterations in coagulation and endothelial activation may also contribute significant role.

One of our key findings was that cardiovascular comorbidities significantly increased thrombosis risk in PV (OR 4.59; p=0.005), even after adjusting for other risk factors. This finding aligns with that of Komatsu et al [44] and supports the incorporation of comorbidity assessment in PV thrombotic risk stratification. Hypertension, the most common comorbidity in our study, is also known to impair platelet and endothelial function [45,46] which may contribute to this thrombosis risk. However, the ECLAP study [8] found that smoking, not hypertension, is predictive of arterial thrombosis, highlighting the need to consider population-specific risk factor distributions.

This study is limited by its retrospective design and relatively small number of pre-PMF and PMF patients. We also did not evaluate allele burden or other mutations (e.g., CALR, MPL), which are known to influence phenotype and thrombotic risk.

In conclusion, JAK2 V617F mutations were detected in the majority of PV, PMF, and pre-PMF cases and less frequent in ET. Patients under 40 were more likely to have JAK2-negative disease. Thrombosis was most common in PV and associated with cardiovascular comorbidities but not with JAK2 status, WBC, platelet, or hemoglobin levels. These findings underscore the complexity of thrombotic risk in MPNs and suggest that clinical comorbidities and functional cellular changes may outweigh traditional laboratory test. Comprehensive mutational profiling and hemostatic testing should be incorporated into future prospective multicenter studies to enhance thrombotic risk assessment in BCR-ABL1–negative MPN patients.
