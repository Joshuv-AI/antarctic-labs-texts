Vignette

A 21-year-old female with congenitally corrected transposition of the great arteries presented to the outpatient clinic with symptoms of dizziness, near syncope, and tachy-palpitations. Over 7 years, multiple evaluations including repeated ambulatory rhythm monitoring, exercise stress testing, computed tomography of the chest, and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging failed to identify a cause. Ambulatory monitors showed only rare supraventricular ectopy, and she was ultimately presumed to have postural tachycardia syndrome (POTS). Treatment with metoprolol succinate 25 mg daily provided minimal symptom relief.

Due to persistent orthostatic intolerance, she underwent formal tilt table testing with a 45-minute upright passive protocol without pharmacologic provocation using continuous beat-to-beat blood pressure monitoring, continuous telemetry, and intermittent 12-lead ECGs (both scheduled and on-demand) as per standard cardiology tilt protocol at Cleveland Clinic. The initial supine 12-lead ECG showed sinus rhythm with sinus arrhythmia (Figure 1A). During early upright tilt, the heart rate only mildly increased from 87 bpm supine to 109 bpm at 10 minutes, without hypotension. However, at minute 39 of the 70-degree tilt, she developed a sustained narrow complex tachycardia around 130 bpm (Figure 1B), reproducing her clinical symptoms. Twelve-lead ECG analysis revealed inverted P-waves in leads I, aVL, V5, and V6, with a positive P-wave in V1, consistent with an ectopic atrial tachycardia with left atrial origin rather than sinus tachycardia (Figure 2). A subsequent electrophysiology study with electroanatomic mapping showed earliest activation in the coronary sinus electrodes 1 and 2, consistent with a left atrial focus (Figure 3), with subsequent radiofrequency ablation done along the roof of the coronary sinus. She reported symptom improvement, and repeat tilt table testing, again with 12-lead ECGs, showed no sustained tachyarrhythmias.

Discussion

Tilt table testing is widely used to evaluate syncope and orthostatic intolerance. However, most protocols rely on limited-lead ECG monitoring, which may be insufficient for distinguishing sinus tachycardia from atrial arrhythmias.

Our report is the first to highlight the clinical utility in employing 12-lead ECGs during tilt table testing to differentiate an atrial tachycardia from sinus tachycardia. This case is instructive not only because a 12-lead ECG was obtained, but because systematic ECG interpretation during provoked tachycardia was a cornerstone of the diagnosis. Careful evaluation of P-wave morphology facilitated the identification of an atrial ectopic focus that clinically mimicked POTS. Without a multi-lead ECG assessment during the tilt table test, this rhythm had been misclassified as sinus tachycardia related to orthostatic stress.

POTS is a disorder of orthostatic intolerance that has gained increasing clinical recognition and patient awareness. Symptoms of orthostatic intolerance can be evaluated using tilt table testing1 though a typical protocol does not usually employ 12-lead ECGs,2 potentially overlooking subtle arrhythmias as was unmasked in this case.

Patients with anatomical substrates, particularly those with congenital heart disease, may be at higher risk of ectopic rhythms that require a more thorough assessment than traditional cardiac monitoring can provide; patients with congenitally corrected transposition of the great arteries exhibit a high incidence of atrial arrhythmias regardless of prior atrial instrumentation.3 This patient had an ectopic atrial rhythm that was not identified using standard evaluation and ambulatory monitoring. Atrial tachycardia may go undetected in patients irrespective of comorbid conditions and may be underrecognized during standard tilt table testing (such as for POTS) due to the limited ECG leads used in most centers. Sinus tachycardia during tilt testing should not be assumed without a P-wave analysis. Utilizing 12-lead ECGs during tilt table testing can offer critical diagnostic information to detect atrial arrhythmias that would otherwise go unrecognized. This case adds incremental value to existing literature by demonstrating how multi-lead ECG-based diagnostic reasoning during tilt table testing can uncover arrhythmic mechanisms beyond that of standard monitoring.
