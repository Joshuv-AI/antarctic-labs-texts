Peer-education strategies for enhancing sexual health services among female sex workers: a scoping review
Authors: Iman Navidi, Elham Shakibazadeh, Samaneh Akbarpour, Firoozeh Raisi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionFemale sex workers (FSWs) remain highly vulnerable to HIV, other sexually transmitted infections (STIs) and broader sexual and reproductive health challenges. Peer-led interventions are widely recommended, yet their specific contributions and implementation gaps are poorly synthesised. This scoping review mapped peer-education strategies used globally to promote sexual health among FSWs, with emphasis on intervention components, outcomes, and evidence gaps, including the role of peers.MethodsThis scoping review followed the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) manual and Arksey and O’Malley’s methodological framework. A comprehensive search was conducted across PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar for peer-reviewed studies published between January 1993 and December 2023. The search used the keywords “sex worker,” “sexual health,” and “peer education.” Eligible studies were required to focus on peer-led sexual health interventions among female sex workers (FSWs) and be published in English. The studies meeting these criteria were screened, charted, and narratively synthesized.ResultsOf 15,488 records, 31 studies from 16 countries (97% in low- and middle-income settings) were included. All incorporated peer-led components and consistently showed positive effects across three outcome domains: behavioural (increased consistent condom use and reduced high-risk behaviours in 23 studies), clinical/biomedical (higher HIV/STI testing, clinic referrals and, in several large-scale programmes, reduced HIV/STI incidence in 12 studies), and psychosocial & structural (improved knowledge, self-efficacy, mental health, human rights awareness and reduced violence in 11 studies). However, peer recruitment, training, supervision and compensation were described in detail in only a minority of studies, no study addressed ethical concerns including burnout, payment inequity and potential exploitation of peer educators.ConclusionPeer-led interventions are highly effective across behavioural, clinical/biomedical and psychosocial & structural domains among FSWs, primarily due to peers’ unique ability to build trust and access hidden networks. To strengthen evidence and equity, future research must systematically document peer processes, evaluate comparative effectiveness, and proactively address ethical and sustainability challenges for peer educators themselves.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-025-04208-x.

Introduction

Female sex workers (FSWs) experience disproportionately high burdens of HIV, other sexually transmitted infections (STIs), unintended pregnancy, violence, mental health problems, and restricted access to respectful healthcare services [1, 2]. Although sexual health is a broad concept that encompasses prevention and management of HIV/STIs, reproductive health and fertility, sexual rights, mental health, sexual function, identity, satisfaction, and the structural determinants that shape these outcomes [3], the vast majority of interventions and research targeting FSWs have focused narrowly on HIV/STI prevention and condom promotion, with far less attention to these wider dimensions.

Peer-led interventions in which current or former sex workers are trained to deliver education, outreach, counseling, or service linkage have been endorsed by the World Health Organization (WHO), UNAIDS, and major implementing programs (e.g., Avahan, Sonagachi, Pragati) as one of the most effective and cost-effective approaches for reaching stigmatized, criminalized, and hidden populations of FSWs [4]. The unique value of peers lies in their shared lived experience, which rapidly builds trust and credibility, reduces stigma, enables access to hard-to-reach networks, and facilitates more open and honest communication than is often possible with professional health workers alone [5]. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have repeatedly shown that peer-led programs achieve greater increases in consistent condom use, HIV/STI testing uptake, and risk-reduction behaviors compared with non-peer or standard-care interventions [6–8].

Despite more than three decades of widespread implementation, important evidence gaps persist. Most published studies do not clearly describe how peers are recruited, trained, supervised, compensated, and integrated into research or program teams; the specific mechanisms through which peer involvement produces change; the comparative effectiveness of purely peer-led, clinic-led, or hybrid models; and the ethical and practical challenges raised by peer involvement, including potential inequity between peers who deliver interventions and those who only receive them, risks of burnout, exploitation, or re-traumatization, and power imbalances within peer groups [9–11].

Study method

We conducted this scoping review based on the manual of the Joanna Briggs Institute [12] and the method described by Arksey and O’Malley [13]. A scoping review approach was chosen because the available studies on peer-education interventions for female sex workers are highly heterogeneous in terms of design, context, and outcomes. Unlike integrative or meta-analytic approaches, a scoping review allows for a comprehensive mapping of the breadth and nature of existing evidence rather than evaluating the quality or estimating the pooled effect of interventions The steps were carried out as follow: (1) identifying the purpose of the review and the relevant research question; (2) defining the search strategy; (3) selection of inclusion and exclusion criteria; (4) implementing the search strategy; (5) plotting and combining data; and (6) reporting the results in a summarized and categorized manner.

Search strategy

Three different sources were used to identify and select appropriate keywords: [1] the MeSH structure in the PubMed database [2], a comparison of terminology used in previous studies on similar topics, and [3] consultation with experts in the field. The PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science databases, as well as the Google Scholar search engine, were searched for relevant studies published from January 1993 to December 2023. The search period was set from January 1993 to December 2023 because the first peer-education interventions systematically described and evaluated among FSWs were published in the mid-1990s [14]. The detailed search strategy is presented in Appendix 1.

Full-text eligibility was independently assessed by two reviewers (IN and SA); disagreements were resolved by consensus or consultation with a third reviewer (ES). Reasons for exclusion at this stage were documented and are summarized in the PRISMA-ScR flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Data charting and synthesis:

A standardized spreadsheet was developed and shared among all team members to extract data from the included studies. The extraction form covered the title, authors, publication year, study design, objectives and sample size, methodology, type of intervention, outcomes, key findings, and other relevant information. To ensure consistency, the team conducted a pilot charting process on five studies before completing the full data extraction. Data were then synthesized descriptively and thematically. The entire research team collaboratively reviewed and refined emerging themes to ensure alignment with the overall objectives of the scoping review. All References were managed using EndNote X7.

Results

A total of 15,488 records were initially identified across the PubMed (4,583), Scopus (6,914), Web of Science (3,586), and Google Scholar (405) databases. After removing 6,178 duplicate records and 378 review articles, 9,310 records remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 9,200 were excluded as they did not meet the inclusion criteria. One hundred and eleven (n = 111) full-text articles were retrieved and assessed for eligibility, and 79 were excluded because they did not fully meet the inclusion and exclusion criteria (for example, lack of a peer-education component or not focusing on sexual health). Finally, 31 studies were included in the scoping review. (Fig. 1).

Out of the 31 articles, four studies had been conducted in low-income countries, including Malawi, Burkina Faso, Zambia, and Madagascar. Fourteen of the studies were conducted in lower- or upper-middle-income countries, including China, Brazil, India, Bangladesh, Indonesia, Zimbabwe, Kenya, Uganda, Philippines, and Vietnam. Study Countires

Most of the included studies were conducted in low- and middle-income countries across Asia, Africa, and South America. Individual studies were identified from Burkina Faso, Malawi, Singapore, Bangladesh, the Philippines, Peru, Vietnam, and Zimbabwe (one study each). Two studies were conducted in Brazil, Madagascar, Uganda, and Zimbabwe; three in Indonesia and Kenya; four in China; and eight in India. India accounted for the largest proportion of studies, reflecting its long-standing implementation of large-scale peer-led HIV prevention programs such as Avahan and Pragati.

Recruitment strategies

Most of the included studies (n = 15) recruited female sex workers from high-risk or workplace settings such as nightclubs, massage parlors, bars, pubs, cafés, motels, brothels, and intercity truck stops. Recruitment in these settings was often facilitated by local healthcare providers, peer educators, community leaders, or volunteers from non-governmental organizations [14, 16–30]. In two studies, participants were identified through large-scale behavioral surveillance systems, specifically the Behavioral Surveillance Surveys (BSS) and the Integrated Behavioral and Biological Assessments (IBBAs) [31, 32]. Several studies recruited participants from harm-reduction centers, where preventive and support services such as condom distribution, needle and syringe exchange, and counseling for HIV and other sexually transmitted infections (STIs) were routinely provided [30, 33, 34]. Two other studies accessed participants through social health centers and sexually transmitted disease (STD) care clinics [35, 36]. A few publications did not clearly describe their recruitment approach or the specific settings from which participants were drawn [18, 31, 37–42]. Overall, recruitment strategies varied widely, ranging from field-based outreach in entertainment venues to facility-based enrollment in health centers and survey-based data collection through national monitoring systems. This heterogeneity reflects the diverse structures of peer-education programs targeting female sex workers across different contexts.

Data collections

A variety of data collection methods were used across the included studies. Six studies relied solely on structured questionnaires to collect information on knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors [17, 21, 26, 27, 35, 39]. Four studies employed qualitative methods such as in-depth interviews and focus group discussions, often to explore sexual health needs and intervention acceptability [16, 23, 34, 41]. In some studies, questionnaires were combined with biomedical testing to enhance the validity of outcome measures. For example, one study used a questionnaire along with a rapid HIV diagnostic test [19] two studies combined questionnaires with vaginal examinations to detect symptoms of STIs [30, 33]. and four studies used serum testing for HIV and other STIs in addition to questionnaires [14, 20, 28, 31]. Furthermore, five studies used a combination of HIV serum testing, urine tests for chlamydia and gonorrhea, and additional serological testing for other STIs [22, 25, 32, 36, 42] In three studies, STI testing and physical examination were performed alongside questionnaires to provide triangulated, objective measures of infection status.

Most studies combined behavioral self-report measures with biological testing, which strengthens the reliability of findings and minimizes self-reporting bias. However, some studies relied solely on self-reported data without laboratory confirmation.

Outcomes

The studies assessed a range of behavioral, biomedical, psychosocial and structural outcomes.

Behavioral outcomes

Twenty-three studies reported significant increases in consistent condom use (with both paying and non-paying partners), reductions in unprotected sex acts, and decreases in number of sexual partners [8, 16, 17, 21, 23, 33, 43–53]. One study observed increased female condom use [54]; another found no significant change in condom use when peer counselling was compared directly with clinic-based counselling [55].

Clinical/biomedical outcomes

Peer-led interventions were associated with reduced HIV and STI incidence in several large-scale programmes [16–18, 32, 33] increased STI syndrome recognition [56], and higher uptake of HIV/STI testing and clinic referrals [27, 57]. One study found no reduction in laboratory-confirmed STIs despite improved knowledge [56].

Psychosocial & structural outcomes

Significant improvements were reported in knowledge and attitudes toward HIV/STIs [21, 23, 44, 50, 54] self-efficacy [30, 58], mental health and reduced alcohol use [30], perceived human rights [30, 58] and access to health and social services [30, 52, 58]. Community mobilisation components strengthened collective identity and reduced police violence in several settings [16, 52].

Interventions

The included studies implemented a wide range of peer-led interventions that can be broadly grouped into three categories: individual-level, community-based, and structural or policy-level strategies.

Individual-level interventions

Fourteen studies implemented cognitive-behavioral interventions focusing on individual behavior change. These included risk-reduction counseling for HIV/STIs, condom negotiation skills, HIV testing and self-examination training, provision of STI care and treatment, referral to STI clinics, and behavior change communication delivered through peer educators [16–18, 23, 27, 29, 34, 36, 37, 40, 42]. three studies focused solely on cognitive or educational approaches, such as integrated counseling to improve knowledge and attitudes toward STIs and mobile phone–based training modules to increase awareness of sexual health and infection prevention [24, 38, 39].

Community-based interventions

Six studies incorporated community mobilization and empowerment strategies, including social marketing of condoms, peer group support, psychosocial support for at-risk sex workers, community-based empowerment of vulnerable women, and environmental support through peer networks [19–22, 28, 31]. These approaches aimed to create enabling environments for safer sexual practices and stronger social support systems.

Structural and policy-level interventions

Six studies focused on expanding access to services and strengthening supportive structures. These interventions included improving access to counseling, STI care and treatment, establishing or expanding HIV/STI clinics, advocating for the social and health rights of female sex workers, and fostering collaborations with community-based organizations, social networks, and volunteer groups [26, 30, 32, 33, 37, 41].

Overall, the interventions reflect a multi-level approach to sexual health promotion, combining individual behavior change with community mobilization and structural support to create more sustainable impacts.

Key findings

Most of the studies reported positive behavioral and biomedical outcomes associated with peer-led HIV/STI prevention interventions among female sex workers.

Twenty-three studies [14, 16–22, 24–26, 29, 31–35, 37, 39, 41, 42] showed that peer-led interventions were associated with increased condom use, reduced unprotected sex, and greater uptake of HIV/STI testing. One study [36] that implemented a condom promotion intervention found that peer support combined with expert advice increased condom use. Another study. Another study [23] demonstrated that peer-based educational programs specifically increased the use of female condom.

Not all interventions were uniformly effective. One study [38] found that peer-led educational and prevention programs did not reduce STI incidence, but improved symptom recognition among participants. Another study [40] showed that clinic-based counseling by specialists had limited impact on condom use compared with peer-led approache.

Overall, these findings suggest that peer-led interventions can produce meaningful improvements in behavioral and biomedical outcomes, with more variable effects on structural and psychosocial domains.

Discussion

Main findings

This scoping review was conducted to comprehensively examine the types of interventions, implementation strategies, interventions, components, and implementation strategies used to promote sexual health among female sex workers globally. A total of 31 studies were included in the analysis, reflecting a broad range of peer-led and structural interventions. In reviewing the literature, it was found that most of the interventions were primarily designed around HIV and sexually transmitted infection (STI) prevention objectives, such as reducing the prevalence of HIV/STIs, increasing knowledge and positive attitudes, reducing high-risk sexual behaviors, and promoting consistent condom use. In contrast, only a limited number of studies explicitly addressed broader aspects of sexual health such as reproductive health, mental well-being, and empowerment, which indicates a gap between disease-centered and holistic sexual health promotion approaches.

We categorized the included studies into three major intervention levels, aligned with ecological model frameworks [1]. Individual-level interventions focused on changing personal behaviors and attitudes through education, counseling, or risk reduction strategies targeting female sex workers [2]. Community-based interventions emphasized collective action, peer engagement, and stakeholder participation, aiming to strengthen community capacity and ensure broader accessibility of prevention and support services [3]. Policy and structural-level interventions addressed enabling environments by integrating individual and environmental supports with broader policy measures, such as expanding access to health services, protecting rights, and creating supportive settings for sexual health promotion.

Among the notable patterns identified in the reviewed literature, the majority of studies were conducted in low- and middle-income countries across Asia and Africa, while only a small number originated from high-income settings. No studies from Europe or North America met the inclusion criteria, highlighting a geographic concentration of research efforts in resource-limited settings. This pattern may reflect the higher burden of HIV and STIs in these regions, the prioritization of prevention programs for key populations such as female sex workers, and the relative visibility of peer-led interventions in these contexts. In contrast, the lack of studies from high-income countries may be related to more effective STI/HIV control programs, stronger health systems, and lower reported prevalence among female sex worker [59].

Some national and long-term programs, such as the AVAHAN and PRAGATI initiatives in India, have been successfully implemented through strong collaboration between government, international agencies, and philanthropic foundations. These programs aimed to control HIV by mobilizing peer groups, engaging stakeholders, and integrating services within existing health systems, strategies that have been shown to reduce HIV prevalence and increase service uptake among female sex workers [32, 33]. The scale and success of these programs highlight the importance of sustained investment, multisectoral collaboration, and political commitment. However, replicating such large-scale interventions in other settings can be challenging, particularly in resource-constrained environments, where financial and human resources are limited.

The findings of this review indicate that condom use was the primary outcome in the majority of studies, underscoring its central role in preventing HIV/STI transmission among female sex workers. This emphasis reflects the continued importance of promoting condom use in both monetary and non-monetary sexual encounters as a cost-effective and scalable prevention strategy [60]. In addition to condom use, several studies also assessed HIV and STI infection status as key outcome measures. This finding highlights that epidemiological indicators such as HIV/STI prevalence and incidence can serve as practical and robust measures to evaluate the impact of sexual health interventions. However, focusing exclusively on these biomedical outcomes may overlook broader dimensions of sexual health, such as empowerment, self-efficacy, and access to care, which are also critical for sustaining long-term behavioral change.

In most of the reviewed studies, peer-led interventions demonstrated significant effectiveness in increasing condom use, reducing HIV/STI incidence, and decreasing high-risk sexual behaviors among female sex workers. These findings highlight the central role of peer engagement as both an outreach and behavior change strategy. Peer educators can build trust, facilitate open communication, and bridge gaps between health systems and marginalized populations, which may explain their effectiveness in promoting safer sexual practices [61].

This approach is also strongly supported by international guidelines, as the World Health Organization (WHO) has emphasized the importance of peer-based interventions in its protocols and recommendations for HIV prevention among key populations [62]. Integrating peer educators into sexual health programs not only enhances acceptability and reach but also strengthens community ownership and sustainability of interventions [4].

The results of Sam Hui’s (2019) study showed that peer education programs alone were not sufficient to reduce STI incidence, although they were effective in increasing knowledge and improving symptom recognition among female sex workers [27]. This limited impact may be partly explained by the fact that measurable reductions in STI incidence often require sustained interventions, longer follow-up periods, and broad population-level coverage. Similarly, Theresa H. Hoke et al. (2015) found that peer- and clinic-based counseling programs had minimal impact on condom use among sex workers. The study compared peer education interventions with clinic-based counseling provided by specialists and concluded that peer-led approaches were more acceptable and slightly more effective than clinic-based strategies [40]. Taken together, these findings suggest that while peer education is a valuable strategy, it may not be sufficient on its own to achieve substantial reductions in STI incidence. Integrating peer-led interventions with clinic-based services, biomedical approaches, and structural support may produce more sustainable and synergistic effects.

Strengths and limitations

Several important research gaps were identified in the reviewed literature. First, most studies primarily focused on HIV/STI prevention, condom promotion, and disease control, while only two studies [30, 58] directly addressed broader aspects of sexual health and fertility among female sex workers. This narrow focus suggests that interventions remain largely disease-centered, even though sexual health is a multidimensional concept encompassing sexual identity, functioning, relationships, desire, emotional well-being, and spirituality. Addressing these dimensions could lead to more comprehensive and sustainable improvements in the sexual health of this population.

Second, there was a notable lack of attention to female condoms compared with male condoms. Considering that some clients of female sex workers refuse to use male condoms, improving access to and acceptance of female condoms could provide an important alternative strategy for reducing STI transmission and preventing unintended pregnancies [60]. Third, another critical gap lies in the limited use of theoretical and behavioral models to guide intervention design and evaluation. Applying frameworks such as the Health Belief Model, Theory of Planned Behavior, or Social Cognitive Theory could enhance the rigor and consistency of intervention development and help achieve more predictable behavioral outcomes [63].

Implications for practice and research

In summary, peer-led interventions are effective in improving condom use and reducing HIV/STI incidence, but they often overlook broader aspects of sexual health, such as empowerment and reproductive health. Future interventions should integrate peer education with clinical services, promote female condoms, and address structural health determinants for a more holistic approach to sexual health.

Conclusion

Peer-led interventions are a highly effective and often essential strategy for improving condom use, HIV/STI testing, risk reduction, knowledge, mental health, human rights awareness and access to care among female sex workers in low- and middle-income settings. The unique strength of peers lies in their ability to rapidly build trust and reach hidden populations. However, important gaps persist: few studies detail peer recruitment, training, supervision or compensation; no direct comparisons with non-peer models exist; and ethical concerns including burnout, payment inequity and potential exploitation of peer educators remain largely unaddressed. Future programmes must systematically document peer processes, evaluate hybrid models, and proactively ensure equitable and sustainable conditions for the peers themselves to maximise both health impact and ethical standards.
