AMQA: An Adversarial Dataset for Benchmarking Bias of LLMs in Medicine and Healthcare
Ying Xiao, Jie Huang, Ruijuan He, Jing Xiao, Mohammad Reza Mousavi, Yepang Liu, Kezhi Li, Zhenpeng Chen, Jie M. Zhang, [0.5em] King’s College London

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are reaching expert-level accuracy on medical diagnosis questions, yet their mistakes and the biases behind them pose life-critical risks. Bias linked to race, sex, and socioeconomic status is already well known, but a consistent and automatic testbed for measuring it is missing. To fill the gap, this paper presents AMQA—an Adversarial Medical Question-Answering dataset—built for automated, large-scale bias evaluation of LLMs in medical QA. AMQA has 4,806 medical QA pairs sourced from the United States Medical Licensing Examination (USMLE) dataset, generated by a multi-agent framework to generate different adversarial descriptions and question pairs. Using AMQA, we benchmark five representative LLMs and find surprisingly substantial disparities: even GPT-4.1, the least biased LLM tested, answers privileged-group questions over 10 percentage points more accurately than unprivileged ones. Compared with the existing benchmark CPV, AMQA reveals 15% larger accuracy gaps on average between privileged and unprivileged groups. Our dataset and code are publicly available ( https://github.com/XY-Showing/AMQA ) to spur reproducible research and advance trustworthy, bias-aware medical AI.

Nevertheless, LLMs also produce confidently stated errors, especially when involving historically unprivileged groups, such as those defined by race or socioeconomic status.
In healthcare, where disparities already endanger unprivileged groups, these errors and biases pose not just theoretical and ethical risks but also life-threatening consequences.

The most biased LLM exhibits an accuracy gap of more than 28%.
Compared to the existing medical QA benchmark CPV, AMQA reveals 15% larger accuracy gaps on average. These findings underscore the critical need for automatic and systematic bias evaluation in medical LLMs, especially considering that even subtle disparities in high-stakes clinical settings can reinforce or worsen existing inequities in healthcare access and outcomes.

Construction of the AMQA Dataset

This section outlines how we design adversarial attacks to construct the AMQA dataset by combining artificial intelligence and human intelligence.
Figure
1
illustrates the workflow of our apprpoach, including clinical vignette filtering, adversarial variant construction, and manual quality control.

Figure 1:
Workflow of AMQA dataset construction, including (1) clinical vignette filtering, (2) adversarial variant construction, and (3) manual quality control. (1) Clinical vignettes are filtered and rewritten into neutralized versions without sensitive attributes. (2) Neutralized vignettes are passed to the Generation-Agent, which produces adversarial descriptions based on sensitive attributes. These are then fused with the neutral vignettes by the Fusion-Agent to create adversarial variants. 3.1
Clinical Vignette Filtering

Our goal is to construct a benchmark dataset that enables automated evaluation of medical bias in LLMs. To ensure objectivity and reproducibility, we focus on multiple-choice clinical questions with a single correct answer and exclude open-ended medical QA datasets, which typically lack clear criteria for determining whether a response is biased.
We begin with clinical vignettes sampled from a United States Medical Licensing Examination (USMLE) question bank
[
11
]
, which has also been used in prior work by OpenAI
[
23
]
and Google
[
32
]
. Following their settings, we initially select 1,273 closed-ended multiple-choice questions.
Each item consists of a clinical scenario described in natural language, four answer choices, the correct answer, and its corresponding index. An example is provided in Appendix
D.1
.

Clinical Vignette Selection.
To support fairness evaluation through counterfactual demographic manipulation, AMQA requires clinical vignettes where sensitive attributes (e.g., race, gender) do not affect the diagnosis results. For instance, a vignette describing a female patient with a gynecological condition would not be suitable for constructing a male counterfactual. To this end, we review all 1,273 items and define the following exclusion criteria: 1) Questions involving professional norms or basic medical knowledge without patient context; 2) Questions where sensitive attributes (e.g., sex, race) may directly affect the correct diagnosis; 3) Questions requiring visual information (e.g., accompanying images) for clinical reasoning.

We adopt rule-based and LLM-based (GPT-4o) filtering based on these criteria, which yields 801 clinical vignettes from 1,273 original instances. The 801 clinical vignettes serve as the seed set for constructing 4,806 adversarial variants (801
\times
3 sensitive attributes
\times
2 groups) in AMQA.

Clinical Vignette Neutralization.
To isolate the effects of sensitive attributes during evaluation, we first construct a neutralized version of each vignette by removing all explicit references to demographic characteristics such as race, gender, and socioeconomic status. This step ensures that the resulting vignette describes a clinically valid scenario that is demographically agnostic, serving as a clean base for introducing controlled adversarial modifications. Neutralization is performed using a combination of rule-based replacements and manual review to ensure semantic fidelity and clinical consistency. We provide a demonstrative example in Appendix
D
.

3.2
Adversarial Variant Generation

Adversarial attack is a widely adopted technique for evaluating the robustness and reliability of AI systems, where small, targeted perturbations are introduced to inputs to induce abnormal or undesirable behavior
[
41
,
31
,
22
]
. In this work, we adapt the concept of adversarial attack to the context of bias evaluation, where the perturbations correspond to demographic attribute manipulations (e.g., changing race or gender) rather than semantically irrelevant noise. Our goal is not to degrade model performance arbitrarily, but to reveal whether models exhibit biased behavior under controlled changes in sensitive attributes.

According to prior studies
[
9
,
25
]
, demographic bias in LLMs for healthcare is most frequently associated with race, gender, and socioeconomic status (SES). In particular, white, male, and high-income individuals are more likely to receive beneficial outcomes, while black, female, and low-income individuals are more often disadvantaged. Based on these findings, we select these three attributes as our attack dimensions, constructing six adversarial variants per vignette by systematically modifying sensitive information while preserving all clinical content.

To implement this, we propose a multi-agent adversarial vignette generation pipeline. Given the current limitations of LLMs, executing multi-step tasks with multiple constraints, such as inserting adversarial demographic background while preserving clinical consistency, can be challenging for a single model. In particular, there is an inherent trade-off between triggering biased behavior and maintaining medical validity. To address this, we decompose the generation process into three sub-tasks, each handled by a specialized LLM agent.

Agent 1 (Generation-Agent)
generates an adversarial demographic description (e.g., “a patient from a low-income background…”) intended to provoke potential bias in downstream predictions.

Agent 2 (Fusion-Agent)
integrates the adversarial description into the neutralized vignette while preserving the original clinical grounds.

Agent 3 (Evaluation-Agent)
evaluates whether the fused adversarial vignette successfully elicits differential model behavior between privileged and unprivileged demographic groups.

Foundation Model Selection for Agent Design.

The foundation models used for each agent directly impacts the effectiveness of the generated adversarial clinical vignette variants. For the Generation-Agent and Fusion-Agent, we select LLMs with strong reasoning capabilities. Our choices include both closed-source and open-source models, based on a combination of model quality, domain alignment, API accessibility, and service availability. Specifically, we adopt GPT-4.1
[
32
,
23
]
and DeepSeek-V3
[
18
]
as representative high-performing models from each paradigm.

In contrast, the Evaluation-Agent follows a different selection logic. Our goal is to construct an adversarial dataset that effectively evaluates bias across diverse LLMs in Medical QA. Specifically, the Evaluation-Agent assesses whether a given adversarial variant is capable of inducing biased behavior in the agent itself. If we choose an overly powerful and alignment-optimized model (e.g., GPT-4.1) for this role, very few variants will succeed in triggering bias. However, those that do are often universally strong, capable of eliciting biased responses from all tested models, regardless of their robustness. This “universal triggering” effect limits the capacity of AMQA to distinguish varying levels of bias across models. To avoid this issue and ensure the benchmarking capacity of AMQA, we adopt GPT-4o-mini, a medium-capacity model, as the Evaluation-Agent, which offers a better balance between the yield of valid adversarial variants and the ability of each variant to reveal bias across models. Additionally, all the model temperatures are set to 0.0 for more stable responses.

3.3
Manual Quality Control

Automated methods introduced before may not be sufficient to guarantee high-stakes dataset integrity. We therefore introduce a rigorous human review stage as a final safeguard, guided by three key criteria: no semantic disfluency, no clinical grounds shift, and no answer leakage.
Our review team consists of four individuals: two with over seven years of experience in AI development and two with more than five years of medical training. This interdisciplinary structure is critical for identifying and correcting both technical and clinical flaws. Reviewers systematically examine each vignette pair to ensure that demographic variations do not alter the clinical reasoning required, that the modified text remains fluent and natural, and that no clues unintentionally reveal the correct answer.
When issues are detected—such as shifts in clinical context or inadvertent hints toward the correct response—manual revisions are made to restore coherence and preserve diagnostic neutrality. After this comprehensive review process, fewer than 100 of the 4,806 vignette pairs required manual edits, reflecting the effectiveness of our automatic generation and validation pipeline in maintaining high-quality, unbiased medical QA data.

Evaluation Setup

4.1
Evaluation Metrics

We use individual and group fairness, the most established criteria in AI fairness
[
7
]
, for evaluation.

Individual fairness
emphasizes the consistency of model outcomes for similar individuals, commonly measured through metrics such as counterfactual fairness. Specifically, counterfactual fairness requires that the answer of an individual should remain invariant between the factual scenario and a counterfactual scenario where the sensitive attribute of an individual is altered
[
17
]
. Formally, given a decision problem, where A, X, Y denote the sensitive attribute input, the remaining input, and the output, the prediction
\hat{Y}
is counterfactual fair if
P(\hat{Y}(x,a)=y\mid X=x,A=a)=P(\hat{Y}(x^{\prime},a^{\prime})=y\mid X=x,A=a^{\prime})
holds for all outcomes
y
and attainable values
a^{\prime}
of
A

[
17
,
7
,
10
]

Group fairness
, on the other hand, relies on statistical analysis across demographic groups
[
3
,
6
,
4
,
37
]
. A common metric for assessing group fairness is the
Statistical Parity Difference (SPD)

[
6
]
, which measures the disparity in positive outcome probabilities between groups. In the context of medical QA, SPD can be effectively represented by the accuracy gap across demographic groups.

The AMQA dataset supports both individual and group fairness evaluations. Specifically, AMQA provides six adversarial vignette variants constructed as three pairs of counterfactuals, enabling direct measurement of individual fairness. Concurrently, we leverage statistical analyses of accuracy gaps across demographic groups to quantify group fairness. Thus, we adopt counterfactual fairness and accuracy gap as our metrics to measure the medical bias of LLMs at the individual and group levels.

4.2
Evaluation Configuration

Benchmarking LLMs using AMQA achieves two primary objectives simultaneously. (1) We evaluate the effectiveness of the AMQA dataset in benchmarking bias and present our advantages by comparing with the latest baseline CPV
[
1
]
.
Since the CPV dataset is not publicly available, we evaluate the same LLMs (gpt-4o-2024-05-13 and gpt-4-turbo-2024-04-09) used in the original CPV paper, allowing us to directly reuse their model outputs for comparison.
(2) We empirically investigate the bias of representative LLMs in medicao QA scenarios. To ensure a comprehensive and reliable evaluation, we select diverse performance-level models from both closed-source and open-source communities. Specifically, our closed-source evaluations include GPT-4.1 from OpenAI, Claude-3.7 Sonnet from Anthropic, and Gemini-2.5 from Google, while open-source models evaluated are DeepSeek-V3 from DeepSeek and Qwen-3 from Alibaba. All models are accessed via their official APIs. To ensure stable and deterministic outputs, we set the temperature to 0.0 for all model queries. The specific versions used in our evaluation are gpt-4.1-2025-04-14, gpt-4o-mini-2024-07-18, claude-3-7-sonnet-20250219, gemini-2.0-flash-001, qwen-max-2024-09-19, and deepseek-v3-0324.

Statistical Significance.

We apply the McNemar test
[
20
]
to each counterfactual pair (e.g., Male vs Female) for each question. As a standard statistical method for evaluating significance in paired categorical data
[
26
]
, this test allows us to assess the reliability of observed behavioral differences. The results indicate that, for all models, the differences in answers between the original and neutralized vignettes are not statistically significant (
p>0.05
), suggesting that the neutralization process does not substantially alter the major content and trigger model bias . In contrast, all three adversarial counterfactual pairs yield highly significant differences in model responses (
p<0.001
), demonstrating that our adversarial variants effectively induce biased behavior in the evaluated models.

6
Limitations

(1) Although AMQA covers multiple sensitive attributes,
we model them as binary variables in counterfactual pairs. This design choice follows common practice in prior fairness research
[
3
,
27
]
, and allows clearer interpretability and control. However, it may overlook important demographic subgroups. For instance, in our evaluation of racial bias, we include only black and white identities, and do not yet cover groups such as Asian, Hispanic, or multiracial individuals. (2) Although the adversarial generation pipeline is largely automated and yields high-quality outputs, it relies on explicitly predefined sensitive attributes and known bias-inducing patterns from prior literature. While this makes the framework targeted and replicable, it may limit the ability to uncover emergent, implicit, or context-dependent biases in more open-ended interactions.

7
Conclusion

This work introduces the AMQA dataset and its adversarial generation framework for benchmarking bias in LLMs within medical QA. The pipeline integrates multiple LLM agents and incorporates human review to ensure both validity and clinical fidelity. Experimental results demonstrate that AMQA effectively induces biased behaviors in state-of-the-art models. In particular, variants generated by GPT-4.1-based agents yield higher rates of bias triggering and produce larger accuracy gaps, indicating stronger benchmarking capacity than those generated by Deepseek-based agents. Benchmarking five representative LLMs reveals substantial variation in bias sensitivity across models.
AMQA fills a critical gap in the infrastructure for evaluating medical bias, supporting automated, scalable, and reproducible assessment.
It is intended to facilitate future research on fairness auditing, bias mitigation, and the reliable deployment of medical LLMs.
