An adaptive scheme for the optimization of damping positions by decoupling controllability spaces in vibrational systems
J. Przybilla, M. Ugrica Vukojević, N. Truhar, P. Benner

Abstract
In this work, the problem of optimizing damper positions in vibrational systems is investigated. The objective is to determine the positions of external dampers in such a way that the influence of the input on the output is minimized. The energy response serves as an optimization criterion, whose computation involves solving Lyapunov equations. Hence, in order to find the best positions, many of these equations need to be solved, and so the minimization process can have a high computational cost. To accelerate the process of finding the optimal positions, we propose a new reduction method. Our algorithm generates a basis spanning an approximation to the solution space of the Lyapunov equations for all possible positions of the dampers. We derive an adaptive scheme that generates the reduced solution space by adding the subspaces of interest, and then we define the corresponding reduced optimization problem that is solvable in a reasonable amount of time. We decouple the solution spaces of the problem to obtain a space that corresponds to the system without external dampers and serves as a starting point for the reduction of the optimization problem. In addition, we derive spaces corresponding to the different damper positions that are used to expand the reduced basis if needed. To evaluate the quality of the basis, we introduce an error indicator based on the space decomposition. Our new technique produces a reduced optimization problem of significantly smaller dimension that is faster to solve than the original problem, which we illustrate with some numerical examples.



An adaptive scheme for the optimization of damping positions by decoupling controllability spaces in vibrational systems

\novelty

We propose a new method that reduces the optimization problem of finding the suboptimal damping positions in mechanical systems.
For that, we invent a procedure that decouples the controllability spaces into parameter-independent and parameter-dependent subspaces.
We derive an adaptive reduced basis method that uses these subspaces to define a reduced optimization problem and hence accelerates the optimization process.
Also a new error indicator is invented that is used to evaluate the quality of the approximations.
The performance of our new method is illustrated in several examples.

1
Introduction

Structures such as buildings, bridges or damps are often disturbed by external forces, e.g. wind occurrences, earthquakes or pedestrian movements. These disturbances can cause vibrations, deflection or even damages in the constructions which can be prevented by adding external dampers.
This paper is about determining the best positions for dampers in order to minimize the effects of external forces on the structure.
We consider vibrational systems of the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbf{M}\ddot{\mathbf{x}}(t)+\mathbf{D}(c,g)\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)+\mathbf{K}\mathbf{x}(t)&=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}(t),\\
\mathbf{y}(t)&=\mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}(t),\end{split}

(1)

where
\mathbf{M},\penalty\ \mathbf{D}(c,g),\penalty\ \mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
are the mass matrix, the damping matrix, and the stiffness matrix, respectively,
and where the damping matrix depends on parameters
c\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{c}
and
g\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{g}
.
Moreover, the matrix
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
is the input matrix and
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
is the output matrix.
The vectors
\mathbf{u}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\mathbf{x}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbf{y}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
are respectively representing the input, the state and the output of the system.
Naturally, the matrices
\mathbf{M}
, and
\mathbf{K}
are symmetric and positive definite, while
\mathbf{D}(c,g)
is symmetric and generally positive semi definite matrix.

In our context, the damping matrix
\mathbf{D}(c,g)
is symmetric, positive definite, consisting of two parts: a parameter-independent internal damping
\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{int}}
which is positive definite and a parameter-dependent external damping
\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{ext}}(c,g)
, i.e.,

\mathbf{D}(c,g)=\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{int}}+\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{ext}}(c,g).

The external damping
\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{ext}}(c,g)
depends on two types of parameters.
The first ones are the damping gains

g=\begin{bmatrix}g_{1},\dots,g_{\ell}\end{bmatrix}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{g}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\ell}_{+}
,
which represent the viscosities of the dampers.
We assume that the viscosities
g_{j}
are fixed over time and lie in given intervals
[g_{j}^{-},\,g_{j}^{+}]
, for all
j=1,\dots,\ell
.
We collect these bounds by setting
g\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{g}
, where the parameter set
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{g}
contains all given conditions.
The second set of parameters consists of the damping positions
c\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{c}\subset\{1,\dots,n\}^{\ell}
which are stored in the matrix
\mathbf{F}(c)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell}
.
The structure of the matrix
\mathbf{F}(c)
depends on the damper type so that e.g. grounded dampers are described by unit vectors that are concatenated to build the matrix
\mathbf{F}(c)
.
This is described in more detail for the different numerical examples.
The resulting external damper is then given as

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{ext}}(c,g):=\mathbf{F}(c)\mathbf{G}(g)\mathbf{F}(c)^{\mathrm{T}},\qquad\mathbf{G}(g):=\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}\left(g_{1},\dots,g_{\ell}\right).

In addition to the external dampers, the system is internally damped.
There are several different models for internal damping.
In this work we use a small multiple of the critical damping that is

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{int}}:=2\alpha\mathbf{M}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\mathbf{M}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{M}^{\frac{1}{2}},

(2)

for
\alpha\ll 1
, which is a widely used convention and has been applied, for example, in
[
4
,
5
]
.
However, our theory can be applied to all modal dampings, which includes, for example, Rayleigh damping defined in
[
40
,
19
]
.
We assume that the number of external dampers
\ell
is significantly smaller than the dimension
n
.
Because of this structure, these systems are also asymptotically stable, i.e. all eigenvalues
\lambda(c,g)
of the polynomial eigenvalue problem
(\lambda(c,g)^{2}\mathbf{M}+\lambda(c,g)\mathbf{D}(c,g)+\mathbf{K})x(c,g)=0
have a negative real part.

In order to simplify the computations in this work, we consider a system transformation, that was introduced, e.g. in
[
37
]
, where it is shown that there exists a transformation
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
such that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\Phi}=\mathbf{I}_{n},\qquad\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{K}\boldsymbol{\Phi}=\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{2}=\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}\left(\omega_{1}^{2},\dots,\omega_{n}^{2}\right),\qquad\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{int}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}=2\alpha\boldsymbol{\Omega},

i.e. the matrix
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
simultaneously diagonalizes the mass matrix, the stiffness matrix and the internal damping where the transformed mass matrix is the identity matrix.
The values
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n}
are the eigenvalues of the undamped system and are called
eigenfrequencies
, see
[
38
]
.
The transformed external damping matrix consists of low-rank factors

\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{ext}}(c,g)\boldsymbol{\Phi}=\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}(c)\mathbf{G}(g)\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}(c)^{\mathrm{T}},\qquad\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}(c):=\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{F}(c).

We summarize the transformed damping matrix by defining
\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}(c,g):=2\alpha\boldsymbol{\Omega}+\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}(c)\mathbf{G}(g)\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}(c)^{\mathrm{T}}
.
Moreover, we define
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}:=\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{B}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}:=\mathbf{C}\boldsymbol{\Phi}
to obtain the transformed system

\displaystyle\begin{split}\ddot{\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}}(t)+\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}(c,g)\dot{\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}}(t)+\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{2}\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}(t)&=\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}\mathbf{u}(t),\\
\mathbf{y}(t)&=\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}(t),\end{split}

(3)

which is equivalent to the original system (
1
) in the sense that both systems have the same input-output behavior.

Vibrational systems and the optimization of their contained dampers have been studied in a large number of papers in the last decades, for example in
[
2
,
9
,
12
,
15
,
18
,
39
,
23
,
21
,
16
]
, where external dampers are considered in systems that already contain internal damping of small magnitude.
The aim is to choose external dampers that stabilize the system and shift eigenfrequencies in such a way that possible external loads do not lead to resonances.
There are two variables that can be optimized: the damper positions and the damping gains.
Moreover, depending on the application, different criteria are chosen to quantify the stability of systems and the response to external disturbances.
If homogeneous systems are considered, i.e. systems (
1
) with
\mathbf{B}\equiv 0
, then the spectral abscissa or the total average energy are used. Examples are described in
[
24
,
11
,
5
,
35
,
38
]
.

In the non-homogeneous case, i.e.
\mathbf{B}\neq 0
, as considered in this work, external disturbances are taken into account, which potentially play an important role in real-life scenarios.
In these cases, the average displacement amplitude can be evaluated, which minimizes the square of the norm of the displacement
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}(t)
averaged over a certain time period, see
[
20
,
36
]
.
Another criterion, which is used in this work, is the average energy amplitude corresponding to the minimization of the
system response
, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}(c,g):=\|\mathcal{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}}(\cdot;c,g)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}=\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{trace}(\mathcal{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}}(\mathrm{i}w;c,g)^{\mathrm{H}}\mathcal{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}}(\mathrm{i}w;c,g))\mathrm{d}w\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

where
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}(s;c,g):=\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}(s^{2}I+s\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}(c,g)+\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{2})^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}
is the
transfer function
of the system describing the input-to-output behavior in the frequency domain.
This optimization criterion was also used in
[
3
,
32
]
.
We choose this particular criterion since we want to minimize the maximum deflections, more precisely, the maximum time response
\max_{t\geq 0}\|\mathbf{y}(t)\|_{\infty}
, and therefore consider the
L_{\infty}
-norm of the output that satisfies the bound

\|\mathbf{y}\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}(\cdot;c,g)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{L_{2}}.

Alternatively, in
[
6
,
33
,
26
]
, the authors optimize the
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
-norm of the systems constraining the
L_{2}
norm of the output
\mathbf{y}
of the corresponding system, which can be interpreted as the worst-case amplification of the energy in the performance output by an input signal.

Most of the established methods consider the optimization of the damping gains.
The optimization of the discrete damper positions is still a difficult problem, especially for large systems, which has been studied in
[
14
,
30
,
10
,
34
]
.
In particular, in
[
4
,
31
]
the authors describe the optimization using a discrete-to-continuous approach, which is modified and used in this paper.

In order to simplify the computation of the system response
\mathbf{J}(c,g)
, we transform the second-order system (
3
) into a first-order system

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{\mathbf{z}}(t)&=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}(c,g)\mathbf{z}(t)+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}\mathbf{u}(t),\\
\mathbf{y}(t)&=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}\mathbf{z}(t)\end{split}

(4)

with corresponding matrices

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}(c,g):=\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathbf{I}_{n}\\
-\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{2}&-\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}(c,g)\end{bmatrix},\quad\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}:=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}\end{bmatrix},\quad\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}:=\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}&0\end{bmatrix}\;\text{ and }\;\mathbf{z}(t):=\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}(t)\\
\dot{\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}}(t)\end{bmatrix}.

(5)

As described in
[
41
]
, the
\mathcal{H}_{2}
-norm of the transfer function can be computed as

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}(c,g)=\sqrt{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}(c,g)\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathrm{T}}\right)},

(6)

where the matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}(c,g)
is the
controllability Gramian
of (
4
) and solves the Lyapunov equation

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}(c,g)\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}(c,g)+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}(c,g)\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}(c,g)^{\mathrm{T}}=-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathrm{T}}.

(7)

In the following, we determine damping positions
c\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{c}
and damping gains
g\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{g}
such that the system response described in (
6
) is locally minimal.
Since the computation of the system response includes solving a Lyapunov equation that is given in (
7
), the optimization process of the external dampers leads to high computational costs or is even unfeasible if the system is of too large dimension.
Hence, we apply some reduction techniques to reduce the optimization problem such that it becomes solvable in a reasonable time.
To that aim, we apply a Galerkin scheme, i.e. we determine bases
\mathbf{W},\penalty\ \mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n,r}
with
\mathbf{W}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{I}_{r}
that generate the reduced matrices

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{r}}(c,g):=\mathbf{W}^{\mathrm{T}}\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}(c,g)\mathbf{T},\quad\mathbf{K}_{\mathrm{r}}:=\mathbf{W}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{2}\mathbf{T},\quad\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{r}}:=\mathbf{W}^{\mathrm{T}}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}},\quad\mathbf{C}_{\mathrm{r}}:=\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}\mathbf{T}

(8)

and the corresponding reduced system

\displaystyle\begin{split}\ddot{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{r}}(t)+\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{r}}(c,g)\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{r}}(t)+\mathbf{K}_{\mathrm{r}}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{r}}(t)&=\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{r}}\mathbf{u}(t),\\
\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{r}}(t)&=\mathbf{C}_{\mathrm{r}}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{r}}(t).\end{split}

(9)

The goal is to find matrices
\mathbf{W},\penalty\ \mathbf{T}
so that the reduced system (
9
) approximates the input-to-output behavior of the system (
3
), i.e., the error
\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{r}}\|
is small in a suitable norm for all parameters
c\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{c}
and
g\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{g}
.
Then the external damping can be optimized for system (
9
) instead of system (
3
) which will save computational costs.
The system response is a highly non-convex function, and hence has several local minima.
The aim in this work is to find the same local minimizer as for the full-order model.

The paper is structured as follows:
First, in Section
2
, we review two different reduction methods for parameter-independent systems.
Then, in Section
3
, we derive a decoupling of the controllability space, which is then used in Section
4
to generate the reduced systems.
First, in Subsection
4.1
, we generate the projection spaces using the Gramians of the systems spanning the controllability spaces for different external dampers.
Then, in Subsection
4.2
, the reduced projection spaces are constructed using the iterative rational Krylov algorithm (IRKA) method that was introduced for second-order systems in
[
32
]
.
In Section
5
, we describe an error expression that describes the quality of the controllability space approximation, which can be used to improve the introduced methods.
Finally, in Sections
6
and
7
, we explain some details about the implementation of the methods and the numerical results for some selected examples.

Preliminaries

In this section, we consider the reduction of the second-order system (
3
) for fixed external dampers described by some
c\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{c}
,
g\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{g}
, and hence, we consider the parameter-independent case, i.e.
\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}\equiv\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}(c,g)
in this section.
Later, in Section
3
and the following ones, we will consider the case of several damping parameters
c
and
g
that are to optimize.
We review two reduction methods for second-order systems that will be used later in this work to derive a reduced optimization method.
In Subsection
2.1
, we explain balanced truncation for second-order systems, and afterwards, in Subsection
2.2
, we describe an IRKA method that is specified for second-order systems.

2.1
Balanced truncation

In this section, we describe balanced truncation suitable for second-order systems as presented in
[
22
,
8
,
7
,
28
]
.
We assume that the system (
3
) has homogeneous initial conditions, i.e.
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}(0)=0
and
\dot{\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}}(0)=0
.
For the inhomogeneous case, the authors in
[
25
]
derive two balanced truncation methods that follow a similar methodology as presented in this section.

First, we derive some input-to-state and state-to-output mappings that are used to derive suitable second-order Gramians.
To do so, we consider the (parameter-independent) transfer function

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}(s):=\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}(s^{2}+s\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}+\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{2})^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}

that we generate by applying the Laplace transform to the second-order system (
3
) with zero initial conditions.
From that, we can derive the two mappings that are introduced in the following definition.

Definition 2.1
.

The
input-to-state mapping

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{so}}
and the
state-to-output mapping

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathrm{so}}
of the asymptotically stable second-order system (
3
) with zero initial conditions are

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{so}}(s):=\left(s^{2}\mathbf{I}+s\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}+\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{2}\right)^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}},\qquad\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathrm{so}}(s):=\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}\left(s^{2}\mathbf{I}+s\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}+\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{2}\right)^{-1}.

The corresponding
second-order controllability Gramian

\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{so}}
and
observability Gramian

\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{so}}
are defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{so}}:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{so}}(iw)\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{so}}(-iw)^{\mathrm{T}}\mathrm{d}w,\qquad\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{so}}:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathrm{so}}(-iw)^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathrm{so}}(iw)\mathrm{d}w.

(10)

The matrix
\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{so}}
is also called
position controllability Gramian
and
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{so}}
is called
velocity observability Gramian
.
We want to emphasize that
\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{so}}
spans the controllability space and
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{so}}
the observability space of system (
3
).
In order to determine the second-order controllability Gramian and observability Gramian of system (
3
), we define the controllability Gramian and the observability Gramian of the respective first-order system (
4
) that are

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}:=

\displaystyle{}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{P}_{1}&\mathbf{P}_{2}\\
\mathbf{P}_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}&\mathbf{P}_{3}\end{bmatrix}:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(iw\mathbf{I}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathrm{T}}(-iw\mathbf{I}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}})^{-\mathrm{T}}\mathrm{d}w,

(11a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}:=

\displaystyle{}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{Q}_{1}&\mathbf{Q}_{2}\\
\ \mathbf{Q}_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}&\mathbf{Q}_{3}\end{bmatrix}:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(-iw\mathbf{I}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}})^{-\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}(iw\mathbf{I}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}})^{-1}\mathrm{d}w.

(11b)

The first order Gramians are computed by solving the Lyapunov equations

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathrm{T}}=-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathrm{T}},\qquad\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}=-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}.

Based on these first-order Gramians, the second-order Gramians can be computed as described in the following proposition from
[
25
,
27
]
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Consider a second-order system as described in (
3
), that is asymptotically stable.
The corresponding second-order controllability Gramian
\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{so}}
and observability Gramian
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{so}}
as defined in (
10
) satisfy

\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{so}}=\mathbf{P}_{1}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{so}}=\mathbf{Q}_{3},

where
\mathbf{P}_{1}
is the upper-left block of the first-order controllability Gramian
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}
, and
\mathbf{Q}_{3}
is the lower-right block of the first-order observability Gramian
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}
as defined in (
11
).

In order to derive projection matrices that reduce the system matrices as described in (
8
), we derive some low-rank factors or Cholesky factors
\mathbf{R}_{1}
and
\mathbf{S}_{3}
with
\mathbf{P}_{1}=\mathbf{R}_{1}\mathbf{R}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}
and
\mathbf{Q}_{3}=\mathbf{S}_{3}\mathbf{S}_{3}^{\mathrm{T}}
.
Those factors exist since
\mathbf{P}_{1}
and
\mathbf{Q}_{3}
are symmetric and positive semi-definite matrices.
They are then used to compute the singular value decomposition

\mathbf{S}_{3}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{R}_{1}=\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{T}}

and to derive the balancing and truncating projection matrices that are

\mathbf{W}:=\mathbf{S}_{3}\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{r}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{r}}^{-\frac{1}{2}},\qquad\mathbf{T}:=\mathbf{R}_{1}\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{r}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{r}}^{-\frac{1}{2}},

(12)

where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{r}}
is the diagonal matrix containing the
r
largest singular values of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
.
Moreover,
\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{r}}
and
\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{r}}
include the
r
leading columns of
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{X}
.
Projecting by
\mathbf{W}
and
\mathbf{T}
provides the reduced system (
9
).

2.2
Iterative rational Krylov method

In this section, we briefly present the iterative rational Krylov method (IRKA) suitable for the case of second-order systems, as introduced in
[
32
]
, where we aim to find a reduced system as in (
9
) with a transfer function

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{r}}(s):=\mathbf{C}_{\mathrm{r}}(s^{2}\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{r}}+s\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{r}}+\mathbf{K}_{\mathrm{r}})^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{r}}

(13)

and matrices as in (
8
).
Within the IRKA approach, we determine a reduced system that satisfies the left, right and bi-tangential interpolation conditions

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}(-s_{i})b_{i}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{r}}(-s_{i})b_{i},\qquad c_{i}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}(-s_{i})=c_{i}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{r}}(-s_{i}),\qquad c_{i}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}(-s_{i})b_{i}=c_{i}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime}(-s_{i})b_{i}

(14)

for interpolation points
s_{1},\dots,s_{r}
, and tangential directions
b_{1},\dots,b_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
c_{1},\dots,c_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
.
The transfer function
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{r}}(s)
can also be written in terms of their poles
\lambda_{k}
and residues
\widetilde{c}_{k}\widetilde{b}_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}
as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{r}}(s)=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\frac{\widetilde{c}_{k}\widetilde{b}_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}}{s-\lambda_{k}}.

(15)

The authors in
[
13
]
show that if a transfer function
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{r}}
of the form in (
15
) is a local minimizer of

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}(\cdot)-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{r}}(\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\dim(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}}(\cdot))=r\\
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}}(\cdot)\text{ is stable }\end{subarray}}\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}(\cdot)-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}}(\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}},

(16)

then
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{r}}
is a tangential Hermite interpolant of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}
at
-\lambda_{k}
,
\widetilde{c}_{k}
, and
\widetilde{b}_{k}
,
k=1,\dots,r
, i.e. the conditions in (
14
) are satisfied for these interpolation points and tangential directions.
The IRKA method applies a Newton method that derives in each iteration new interpolation points and tangential directions that converge to the optimal ones, and define a reduced system with optimal transfer function
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{r}}
.

Defining and computing the projecting matrices
\mathbf{W}
and
\mathbf{T}
that define the reduced matrices (
8
) and the system (
9
) that fulfill the properties in (
14
) was done in
[
1
,
13
]
.
However, the choice of the projecting bases also depends on additional conditions that are applied to the reduced systems.
For mechanical systems (
3
), the aim is to find bases that preserve the symmetry and the positive definiteness of the mass matrix, the damping matrix, and the stiffness matrix in order to obtain an asymptotically stable reduced system. Hence, we set
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{T}
.
Also, the reduced system is supposed to be of second-order structure.
Hence, the sym2IRKA method, presented in
[
32
]
, generates a reduced transfer function of the structure shown in (
13
) that represents a second-order system as in (
9
).
For that it uses a one-sided projection approach that generates a basis matrix
\mathbf{W}
with

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{range}}\left(\mathbf{W}\right)\supseteq\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\left\{(s_{1}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}+s_{1}\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}+\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{2})^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}b_{1},\dots,(s_{r}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}+s_{r}\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}+\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{2})^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}b_{r}\right\},

for interpolation points
s_{1},\dots,s_{r}
and directions
b_{1},\dots,b_{r}
and defines the matrices (

3
Decomposition of the controllability space

We consider the system response in (
6
) and insert the definition of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}
to obtain

\mathbf{J}(c,g)=\sqrt{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{so}}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{\mathrm{T}}\right)},

where
\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{so}}
is the position controllability Gramian from (
10
) that spans the controllability space of the second-order system (
3
).
Our aim in this article is to find a basis
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{r}}
that spans an approximation of this controllability space for all possible parameters
c\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{c}
and
g\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{g}
.
The space that contains the controllability space of system (
3
) for a parameter
(c,g)\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}
is denoted by
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}(c,g)
and the space that contains the controllability space for all parameters, that we aim to approximate, is denoted by
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}
in the following.
For a given damping
(c,g)\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}
, the controllability space
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}(c,g)
of the system is spanned by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}(c,g)=\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\left\{(s_{1}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}+s_{1}\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}(c,g)+\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{2})^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}b_{1},\dots,(s_{N}^{2}\mathbf{I}+s_{N}\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}(c,g)+\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{2})^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}b_{N}\right\}

(17)

if the parameters
s_{1},\dots,s_{N}
and tangential directions
b_{1},\dots,b_{N}
are chosen well and
N
is chosen large enough.

In this section, we introduce a decomposition of the controllability space into a parameter-independent space and a parameter-dependent one which can also be applied to the observability space.
Using this decomposition, we can simplify the computations within the reduction process and define an error indicator in a following section.
The following theorem provides a decomposition of the controllability space
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}(c,g)
into a parameter-independent subspace and several parameter-dependent ones that are used in the rest of this work.

Theorem 3.1
.

Consider the asymptotically stable second-order system (
3
) for a fixed parameter pair
(c,g)\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}
.
Let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}(c,g)
be the controllability space of this system that satisfies (
17
) for a given set of interpolation points
s_{1},\dots,s_{N}
and directions
b_{1},\dots,b_{N}
.
Define
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(s):=(s^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}+s2\alpha\boldsymbol{\Omega}+\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{2})^{-1}
, then the space
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}(c,g)
satisfies

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}(c,g)\subseteq\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{0}\cup\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{H}}(c,g)\subseteq\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{0}\cup\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{F}}(c)

(18)

for subspaces

\displaystyle\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{0}:={}&\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\left\{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(s_{1})\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}b_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(s_{N})\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}b_{N}\right\},\\
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{H}}(c,g):={}&\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\left\{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(s_{1})\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}(c)\mathbf{H}(s_{1},b_{1};c,g),\dots,\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(s_{N})\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}(c)\mathbf{H}(s_{N},b_{N};c,g)\right\},\\
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{F}}(c):={}&\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\left\{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(s_{1})\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}(c),\dots,\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(s_{N})\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}(c)\right\},\end{split}

(19)

and
\;\mathbf{H}(s_{i},b_{i};c,g):=\left(\frac{1}{s_{i}}\mathbf{G}(g)^{-1}+\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}(c)^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(s_{i})\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}(c)\right)^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}(c)^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(s_{i})\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}b_{i}\;

for
i=1,\dots,N
.

Proof.

We consider one element corresponding to the interpolation point
s_{i}
and the tangential direction
b_{i}
of the span in (
17
).
One can apply Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury formula to show that
\left(\frac{1}{s_{i}}\mathbf{G}(g)^{-1}+\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}(c)^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(s_{i})\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}(c)\right)^{-1}
exists.
Now, we apply the Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury formula and obtain

\displaystyle(s_{i}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}+s_{i}\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}(c,g)+

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{2})^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}b_{i}

\displaystyle=\left(s_{i}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}+2s_{i}\alpha\boldsymbol{\Omega}+\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{2}+s_{i}\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}(c)\mathbf{G}(g)\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}(c)^{\mathrm{T}}\right)^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}b_{i}

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1}(s_{i})+s_{i}\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}(c)\mathbf{G}(g)\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}(c)^{\mathrm{T}}\right)^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}b_{i}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(s_{i})\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}b_{i}-\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(s_{i})\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}(c)\left(\frac{1}{s_{i}}\mathbf{G}(g)^{-1}+\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}(c)^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(s_{i})\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}(c)\right)^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}(c)^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(s_{i})\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}b_{i}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(s_{i})\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}b_{i}-\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(s_{i})\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}(c)\mathbf{H}(s_{i},b_{i};c,g).

We observe that

(s_{i}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}+s_{i}\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}(c,\penalty\ g)+\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{2})^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}b_{i}\\
\in\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\left\{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(s_{i})\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}b_{i},\;\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(s_{i})\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}(c)\mathbf{H}(s_{i},b_{i};c,g)\right\}\subseteq\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\left\{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(s_{i})\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}b_{i},\;\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(s_{i})\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}(c)\right\}.

Applying the same argument to all elements corresponding to the interpolation points
s_{i}
and the tangential directions
b_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,N
, leads to the relation in (
18
) with spaces defined as in (
19
).
∎

A direct consequence from that theorem is the following corollary which provides certain spaces that contain the controllability space for all admissible parameters.

Corollary 3.2
.

Consider the asymptotically stable second-order system (
3
), the corresponding controllability spaces
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}(c,g)
, and the spaces
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{0}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{H}}(c,g)
,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{F}}(c)
as defined in (
19
) for the parameter pairs
(c,g)\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}
.
The spaces
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{F}}
, and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{H}}
defined as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}:=\bigcup_{(c,g)\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}(c,g),\qquad\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{H}}:=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{0}\cup\bigcup_{(c,g)\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{H}}(c,g),\qquad\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{F}}:=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{0}\cup\bigcup_{c\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{c}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{F}}(c)

(20)

contain the corresponding controllability space for all parameters
(c,g)\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}
and they satisfy

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}\subseteq\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{H}}\subseteq\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{F}}.

Note that the space
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{F}}(c)
is even independent of the damping gains and depends only on the positions
c\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{c}
of the external dampers.
Therefore, to determine
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{F}}
, only the spaces
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{F}}(c)
need to be calculated for a finite number of parameters, while for
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{H}}
an infinite number of parameters from
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}
need to be considered to form the corresponding spaces.
However, forming the controllability spaces for all parameters in
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{c}
is not practical, so that in the following we use the reduced basis method to approximate the different spaces from (
20
).
In the following, the decomposition of the controllability spaces into a parameter-independent and into parameter-dependent parts are used to derive a reduced basis in order to reduce the system as described in (
9
) and to derive error indicators that quantify the quality of the reduced system.

4
Reduction

In this section, we generate a basis
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{r}}
in an iterative scheme that is used to define a reduced optimization problem.
For the reduced second-order matrices as defined in (
8
) with
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{r}}
and
\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{r}}
, the reduced first-order system matrices are

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{r}}(c,g):=\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathbf{I}_{r}\\
-\mathbf{K}_{\mathrm{r}}&-\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{r}}(c,g)\end{bmatrix},\qquad\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathrm{r}}:=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{r}}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{r}}:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{C}_{\mathrm{r}}&0\end{bmatrix}.

Using these reduced first-order matrices, we define a reduced system response that we aim to minimize

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{\mathrm{r}}(c,g):=\sqrt{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{r}}\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{r}}(c,g)\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{T}}\right)},

(21)

where
\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{r}}(c,g)
is the reduced controllability Gramian that solves the reduced Lyapunov equation

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{r}}(c,g)\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{r}}(c,g)+\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{r}}(c,g)\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{r}}(c,g)^{\mathrm{T}}=-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathrm{r}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{T}}.

(22)

We aim to construct a space
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{H}}
, or
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{F}}
which spans the controllability space of the system in (
3
) for all admissible dampers.
For that we utilize Corollary
3.2
.
However, it is numerically unfeasible to compute the spaces
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}(c,g)
,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{H}}(c,g)
, or
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{F}}(c)
from (
19
) for all
(c,g)\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}
.
Hence, we aim to apply an adaptive reduced basis method scheme based on the methods presented in
[
29
,
26
]
to find a basis
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{r}}
(and the corresponding space
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{r}}=\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\left\{\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{r}}\right\}
) that is parameter-independent and that spans an approximation of the controllability space
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{r}}\approx\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}
.
The idea of our method is to successively add basis vectors to the basis
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{r}}
until the approximation of the controllability space is sufficiently good for all parameters
(c,g)\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}
.

First, we define the basis
\mathbf{V}_{0}
which spans the controllability space of the externally undamped system, i.e.
\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\left\{\mathbf{V}_{0}\right\}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{0}
and initialize
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{r}}
by setting
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{r}}=\mathbf{V}_{0}
.
As we observe in (
18
), the space
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{r}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{0}
is included in the controllability space for all different damper’s positions and, hence, is a reasonable first basis.
Then we check, whether
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{r}}
spans a space
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{r}}
that is already a good approximation of the controllability space
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}(c,g)
for all
(c,g)\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}
, and hence, of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}
.
If this is not the case, we select parameters
(c,g)\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}
for which the controllability space is not well-approximated by the current
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{r}}
, and add the basis vectors corresponding to the space
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{H}}(c,g)
or
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{F}}(c)
into the basis
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{r}}
.
We continue with this process until the controllability spaces for all
(c,g)\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}
are approximated sufficiently good by the basis matrix
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{r}}
.

We embed this basis building into the optimization process.
For that, we define an initial reduced optimization problem (
21
) with the first basis
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{r}}=\mathbf{V}_{0}
and run an optimization method with initial values
(c_{0},g_{0})
to obtain a minimizer
(c^{*},g^{*})
.
Corresponding to the variables
(c^{*},g^{*})
we determine a basis
\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{H}}(c^{*},g^{*})
or
\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{F}}(c^{*})
that spans either
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{H}}(c^{*},g^{*})
or
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{F}}(c^{*})
, respectively, and that is used to enrich the basis
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{r}}
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{r}}=\mathrm{orth}([\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{r}},\penalty\ \mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{H}}(c^{*},g^{*})])\qquad\text{ or }\qquad\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{r}}=\mathrm{orth}([\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{r}},\penalty\ \mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{F}}(c^{*})]).

(23)

We set the new initial values
(c_{0},g_{0})=(c^{*},g^{*})
and run a new optimization method.
We continue with this procedure until two consecutive minimizers are equal or the relative norm of their difference is smaller than a tolerance
\mathrm{tol}_{\mathrm{err}_{1}}
, which leads to Algorithm

\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}(c)\end{bmatrix},\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}\end{bmatrix}.

IRKA based approach

According to (
20
), we decompose the controllability space
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{H}}\supseteq\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}
into a parameter-independent space and several parameter-dependent ones as

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}\subseteq\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{H}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{0}\cup\bigcup_{c\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{c}\atop g\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{g}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{H}}(c,g),\\
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{H}}(c,g)=\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\left\{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(s_{1})\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}(c)\mathbf{H}(s_{1},b_{1};c,g),\dots,\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(s_{N})\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}(c)\mathbf{H}(s_{N},b_{N};c,g)\right\}.

depends on the damping positions
c\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{c}
and on the viscosities
g\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{g}

using the parameter-independent basis
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{r}}=\mathbf{V}_{0}
and by adding iteratively the bases

.

5
Error indicator

During the optimization and basis building process presented in Algorithm
2
, two consecutive optima
(c^{*},g^{*})
generated within this algorithm can have a distance smaller than a tolerance, even if the real optimum is not reached yet.
This situation might appear, if two consecutive bases
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{r}}
span spaces that are close to each other but do not approximate the controllability space for the used parameters well.
Also, the optimization process is executed until an optimum is reached, even if the basis leads to a bad approximation of the controllability space, and hence, the corresponding optimization is meaningless.
A solution to both the problems is provided by an error indicator that evaluates the quality of the controllability space approximation by
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{r}}
.
Hence, in this section, we aim to derive an error expression that indicates the quality of the basis
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{r}}
as an approximation of the controllability space
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}(c,g)
as defined in (
17
) for parameters
(c,g)\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}
.

For that, we consider the space
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{F}}(c)
which corresponds to the second-order system (
25
) and the corresponding position controllability Gramian
\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{F}}(c)
that is the upper left block
\mathbf{X}_{11}(c)
of the first-order Gramian

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathbf{F}}(c)=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{X}_{11}(c)&\mathbf{X}_{12}(c)\\
\mathbf{X}_{12}(c)^{\mathrm{T}}&\mathbf{X}_{22}(c)\end{bmatrix}

(29)

that solves the Lyapunov equation from (

) with the right-hand side

.
Using the basis

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}_{\mathbf{F}}(c)=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{Y}_{11}(c)&\mathbf{Y}_{12}(c)\\

by

Computational details

In this section, we describe some computational details. In Subsection

6.1
Diagonalization of the Lyapunov equations

We consider the Lyapunov equations from (
27
), where the matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{0}
consists of four blocks of diagonal matrices.
We can easily diagonalize such a matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{0}
to make the solving process of the Lyapunov equations even more efficient.
We consider the first equation in (
27
) with the right-hand side
-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathrm{T}}
while the second one is treated equally.

Lemma 6.1
.

Consider the Lyapunov equation from (
27
) with the right-hand side
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathrm{T}}
, where
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{0}
is defined as in (
28
).
Then there exist a nonsingular matrix
\mathbf{T}
such that
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{D}}=\mathbf{T}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{0}\mathbf{T}^{-1}
is a diagonal matrix and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{D}}=\mathbf{T}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{0}\mathbf{T}
, where

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{D}}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{D}}^{\mathrm{T}}=-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathrm{D}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathrm{D}}^{\mathrm{T}}

(38)

for
\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{D}}=\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{B}
.

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}
be the perfect shuffle permutation, which reorders a set of even cardinality by mapping the
k
-th entry as follows

k\mapsto\begin{cases}2k-1,&\mbox{if }k\leq n,\\
2(k-n),&\mbox{if }k>n\end{cases}

and which satisfies
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}=\mathbf{I}
.
Then we get that

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{0}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}=\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{n}\right),\quad\mathbf{D}_{j}:=\begin{bmatrix}0&\omega_{j}\\
-\omega_{j}&-2\alpha\omega_{j}\end{bmatrix},\qquad j=1,\dots,n.

Now, let
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{j}
be the matrix which diagonalizes the matrix
\mathbf{D}_{j}
, i.e.
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{j}^{-1}\mathbf{D}_{j}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{j}
is a diagonal matrix, for
j=1,\dots,n
.
We define the matrix
\boldsymbol{\Psi}:=\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}\left(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{n}\right)
, so that we obtain

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{D}}:=\mathbf{T}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{0}\mathbf{T},\qquad\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathrm{D}}:=\mathbf{T}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}\qquad\text{ for }\qquad\mathbf{T}:=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}\boldsymbol{\Psi}

where
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{D}}
is a diagonal matrix.
Multiplying the Lyapunov equation from (
27
) with the right-hand side
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathrm{T}}
from the left and from the right by
\mathbf{T}^{-1}
and
\mathbf{T}
, respectively, leads to

\mathbf{T}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{0}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{T}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{0}\mathbf{T}+\mathbf{T}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{0}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{T}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{0}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{T}=-\mathbf{T}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{T},

and hence, the Lyapunov equation (
38
) is solved by
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{D}}=\mathbf{T}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{0}\mathbf{T}
.
∎

6.2
Optimization

Our aim is to optimize the positions and the gains of the external dampers by minimizing the system response as defined in (
6
).
However, the damper positions
c\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{c}
are discrete values, and hence, optimization algorithms such as the Nelder-Mead method are not directly applicable.
Therefore, we modify the system response to be

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{J}}(c,g):=\mathbf{J}([c],g)=\mathbf{J}([c_{1}],\dots,[c_{\ell}],g_{1},\dots,g_{\ell})

(39)

as described in
[
17
]
, so that the positions
c
, that are optimized, are continuous variables.
When only the damper positions are to be optimized, i.e. the damping gains
g^{*}=[g_{1}^{*},\dots,g_{\ell}^{*}]\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
are fixed, we define a second system response function, that is

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{J}}_{\mathrm{pos}}(c):=\mathbf{J}([c],g^{*})=\mathbf{J}([c_{1}],\dots,[c_{\ell}],g_{1}^{*},\dots,g_{\ell}^{*}).

(40)

Optimizing this function using standard optimization methods such as the Nelder-Mead method might cause convergence problems if the step size is too small because of the discrete function values of (
40
).
Hence, we modify this function to make the function values continuous.
We first consider the case where we only have one damper with the position
c\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
We split the current position value
c=c^{\mathrm{int}}+c^{\mathrm{dec}}
where
c^{\mathrm{int}}:=\lfloor c\rfloor
and
c^{\mathrm{dec}}=c-c^{\mathrm{int}}
.
The corresponding function value is then defined as

\widehat{\mathbf{J}}_{\mathrm{pos}}(c):=\left(1-c^{\mathrm{dec}}\right)\mathbf{J}\left(c^{\mathrm{int}},g^{*}\right)+c^{\mathrm{dec}}\mathbf{J}\left(c^{\mathrm{int}}+1,g^{*}\right)

which provides a linear interpolation between the function values corresponding to two discrete damper’s positions.

This idea is now generalized for
\ell
dampers, i.e.
c\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}_{+}
.
We define for
c=[c_{1},\dots,c_{\ell}]\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\ell}
the values

\displaystyle c_{j}:=c_{j}^{\mathrm{int}}+c_{j}^{\mathrm{dec}}\quad\text{with}\quad c_{j}^{\mathrm{int}}:=\lfloor c_{j}\rfloor,\;\;c_{j}^{\mathrm{dec}}=c_{j}-c_{j}^{\mathrm{int}},\qquad\text{ for }j=1,\dots,\ell.

Accordingly, we define the continuous function values as

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{J}}_{\mathrm{pos}}(c):=

\displaystyle\left(1-c_{1}^{\mathrm{dec}}\right)\cdots\left(1-c_{\ell}^{\mathrm{dec}}\right)\mathbf{J}\left([c_{1}^{\mathrm{int}},\dots,c_{\ell}^{\mathrm{int}}],g^{*}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+c_{1}^{\mathrm{dec}}\left(1-c_{2}^{\mathrm{dec}}\right)\cdots\left(1-c_{\ell}^{\mathrm{dec}}\right)\mathbf{J}\left([c_{1}^{\mathrm{int}}+1,\,c_{2}^{\mathrm{int}},\dots,c_{\ell}^{\mathrm{int}}],g^{*}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\left(1-c_{1}^{\mathrm{dec}}\right)\cdots\left(1-c_{\ell-1}^{\mathrm{dec}}\right)c_{\ell}^{\mathrm{dec}}\mathbf{J}\left([c_{1}^{\mathrm{int}},\dots,c_{\ell-1}^{\mathrm{int}},c_{\ell}^{\mathrm{int}}+1],g^{*}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 100.0pt\vdots

\displaystyle\hskip 50.0pt+\left(1-c_{1}^{\mathrm{dec}}\right)c_{2}^{\mathrm{dec}}\cdots c_{\ell}^{\mathrm{dec}}\mathbf{J}\left([c_{1}^{\mathrm{int}},c_{2}^{\mathrm{int}}+1,\dots,c_{\ell}^{\mathrm{int}}+1],g^{*}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 50.0pt+c_{1}^{\mathrm{dec}}\cdots c_{\ell-1}^{\mathrm{dec}}\left(1-c_{\ell}^{\mathrm{dec}}\right)\mathbf{J}\left([c_{1}^{\mathrm{int}}+1,\dots,c_{\ell-1}^{\mathrm{int}}+1,c_{\ell}^{\mathrm{int}}],g^{*}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 150.0pt+c_{1}^{\mathrm{dec}}\cdots c_{\ell}^{\mathrm{dec}}\mathbf{J}\left([c_{1}^{\mathrm{int}}+1,\dots,c_{\ell}^{\mathrm{int}}+1],g^{*}\right).

We observe that the computation of the function values of
\widehat{\mathbf{J}}_{\mathrm{pos}}(c)
is only accessible for a small amount of external dampers or for very small system dimensions since the number of function evaluations rises exponentially with the numbers of dampers, where we need
2^{\ell}
Lyapunov equation solves if
\ell
is the number of the dampers.

Note that these convergence problems can also occur for the function defined in (
39
), however in our examples that was not the case, and hence, if both the positions and the gains are optimized, we use the system response function defined in (
39
).

Numerical results

In this section, we apply the methods presented above for two numerical examples.
For both examples, we first optimize only the damper’s positions and afterwards extend the approach to optimize the positions and the damping viscosities simultaneously.
We emphasise again that the system response optimized in this work is a non-convex function and has several local minima.
In this work, we determine approximations of the local minima of the system response using the full system.
If global optima are to be found, different initial conditions must be applied, see
[
4
]
.

The computations have been done on a computer with 2 Intel Xeon Silver 4110 CPUs running at 2.1 GHz and equipped with 192 GB total main memory.
We use MATLAB
®
2021a to run the examples and for the optimization, we use the Nelder–Mead method encoded in the MATLAB-function
\mathtt{fminsearch}
.

8
Conclusions

This paper has addressed the optimization of the positions and the viscosities of external dampers in vibrational systems.
For that, we have evaluated the corresponding system response that includes the computation of the controllability Gramian.
To accelerate the optimization process, we have decomposed the controllability space into a parameter-independent subspace and several parameter-dependent subspaces that have been used to derive an adaptive reduced basis method. This method determines a reduced basis of a subspace that approximates the controllability space for the required parameters.
Using this basis we have computed approximations of the Gramians and the corresponding system response values.
To assess the quality of the system response approximations, we have derived an error indicator that is based on the controllability space decomposition.
Finally, the efficiency of the methods has been illustrated for two examples.
