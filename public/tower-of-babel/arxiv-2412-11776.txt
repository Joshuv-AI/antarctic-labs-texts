Joint Reconstruction of the Activity and the Attenuation in PET by Diffusion Posterior Sampling: a Feasibility Study
Clémentine Phung-Ngoc, Alexandre Bousse, Antoine De Paepe, Hong-Phuong Dang, Olivier Saut, Dimitris Visvikis

Abstract




Abstract

This study introduces a novel framework for joint reconstruction of the activity and the attenuation in positron emission tomography using diffusion posterior sampling. By leveraging diffusion models, this approach directly addresses activity–attenuation dependencies, mitigating crosstalk issues prevalent in non-time-of-flight settings. Experimental evaluations, conducted using 2-dimensional XCAT phantom data, demonstrate that diffusion posterior sampling significantly outperforms traditional maximum likelihood activity and attenuation methods, producing consistent and high-quality reconstructions even in the absence of time-of-flight information. Ongoing work aims to extend our method to real 3-dimensional data with encouraging preliminary findings.

Introduction

Positron emission tomography plays a critical role in medical imaging such as in oncology and cardiology. For accurate quantification, positron emission tomography imaging requires attenuation correction, typically achieved using complementary modalities like computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging. However, these additional scans increase radiation exposure and the complexity of the imaging workflow.

To mitigate radiation dose—especially in scenarios such as follow-up studies or potential screening protocols—it is desirable to perform positron emission tomography acquisition in a low-dose setting. A promising approach to achieve this is to derive the attenuation correction directly from the positron emission tomography emission data with joint reconstruction of the activity and the attenuation techniques. Methods like maximum likelihood activity and attenuation have demonstrated reasonable success in time-of-flight positron emission tomography settings by simultaneously estimating activity and attenuation using
[
1
]
or by joint estimation of the activity and attenuation coefficients
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
. However, these methods struggle in non-time-of-flight positron emission tomography scenarios due to activity–attenuation crosstalk.

Recent advances in deep learning have opened new possibilities for enhancing positron emission tomography reconstruction. Techniques leveraging deep learning can predict attenuation-corrected images from non-corrected data or directly generate attenuation maps
[
5
]
.

In parallel, diffusion models have emerged as a powerful tool for solving inverse problems through diffusion posterior sampling
[
6
]
. These approaches have demonstrated promising performance in medical imaging tasks
[
7
]
and more particularly in positron emission tomography reconstruction
[
8
,
9
]
.

In this paper, we propose a novel framework for joint reconstruction of the activity and the attenuation directly from positron emission tomography emission data using diffusion posterior sampling. Our approach extends the maximum likelihood activity and attenuation algorithm by integrating the joint prior probability distribution function of the activity and attenuation maps derived through diffusion posterior sampling.

Materials and Methods

2.1

Background on Joint Activity and Attenuation Reconstruction

The task consists of retrieving an activity image (or volume)
\bm{\lambda}\penalty\ =\penalty\ [\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}]^{\top}\in\mathcal{X}\triangleq\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m}
,
m
being the number of pixels (or voxels), from a collection of positron emission tomography measurements
\bm{y}=\{y_{i,k}\}\in\mathcal{Y}\triangleq\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{l}}\cdot n_{\mathrm{t}}}
where
y_{i,k}
denotes the number of detected
\gamma
-photon pairs at the
i
th line of response and
k
th time bin (for time-of-flight positron emission tomography),
n_{\mathrm{l}}
and
n_{\mathrm{t}}
being respectively the number of lines of response and the number of time bins (
n_{\mathrm{t}}=1
in the non-time-of-flight case). The counting process is modeled with a Poisson random probability distribution function,

y_{i,k}\mid\bm{\lambda},\bm{\mu}\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\bar{y}_{i,k}(\bm{\lambda},\bm{\mu}))

(1)

where the expectation
\bar{y}_{i,k}(\bm{\lambda},\bm{\mu})
depends on the activity image
\bm{\lambda}
to reconstruct as well as on the
\gamma
-photon attenuation map represented by an image
\bm{\mu}=[\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{m}]^{\top}\in\mathcal{X}
. Ignoring background effects such as scatter and random coincidences, a standard forward model is

\bar{y}_{i,k}(\bm{\lambda},\bm{\mu})\triangleq a_{i}(\bm{\mu})\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{i,k,j}\lambda_{j}

(2)

where
p_{i,k,j}
is the probability that an emission from voxel
j
is detected in
(i,k)
(taking into account the system’s geometry, resolution and sensitivity) and
a_{i}(\bm{\mu})
is the
i
th attenuation coefficient, given by the Beer-Lambert law as

a_{i}(\bm{\mu})=\mathrm{e}^{-[\bm{R}\bm{\mu}]_{i}}\,,

(3)

\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
being the discrete Radon transform which computes line integrals along each line of response.

In multimodal imaging, the attenuation map
\bm{\mu}
can be derived from an anatomical image obtained from computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging. The activity image
\bm{\lambda}
can then be reconstructed by solving the maximum
a posteriori
optimization problem

\max_{\bm{\lambda}\in\mathcal{X}}\,p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\lambda},\bm{\mu})\cdot p(\bm{\lambda})

(4)

where the conditional probability distribution function
p(\bm{y}|\bm{\lambda},\bm{\mu})
is given by (
1
) (assuming conditional independence of the measurements) and
p(\bm{\lambda})
is the prior distribution on
\bm{\lambda}
. Since
p(\bm{\lambda})
is unknown, it is typically approximated by
p(\bm{\lambda})=\exp(-R(\bm{\lambda}))
where
R\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is a convex regularizer which ideally promotes image smoothness while preserving edges, in which case (
4
) is a penalized maximum likelihood problem that can be solved by means of iterative algorithms
[
11
]
.

When
\bm{\mu}
is not available, it can be jointly estimated with
\bm{\lambda}
from the emission data
\bm{y}
by solving

\max_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}^{2}}\,p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{x})\cdot p(\bm{x})

(5)

where
\bm{x}=(\bm{\lambda},\bm{\mu})
is the two-channel image comprising the activity and the attenuation. In absence of the prior
p(\bm{x})
, solving (
5
) corresponds to the maximum likelihood activity and attenuation problem which can be solved using an iterative algorithm
[
1
]
. However, the results are affected by the
\bm{\lambda}
–
\bm{\mu}
crosstalk when the time-of-flight resolution is too low. Moreover, this approach can only reconstruct the images up to a scaling factor due to the
\bar{y}_{i,k}(\bm{\lambda}/c,\bm{\mu}-\log c)=\bar{y}_{i,k}(\bm{\lambda},\bm{\mu})
identity.

2.2

Joint Activity and Attenuation Reconstruction using Diffusion Posterior Sampling

Joint reconstruction of the activity and the attenuation can leverage the utilization of the probability distribution function
p(\bm{x})
in (
5
) to take into account prior knowledge on the pair
(\bm{\lambda},\bm{\mu})
. This probability distribution function being unknown, we propose to sample
\bm{x}
from
p(\bm{x}|\bm{y})
using diffusion posterior sampling
[
6
]
. In the following
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
denotes the activity/attenuation map space.

In diffusion models, noise is incrementally added to an image
\bm{x}_{0}
, sampled from the training dataset with a probability distribution function
p^{\mathrm{data}}
, through a diffusion process, resulting in a noisy image
\bm{x}_{t}
at each time step
t=1,\dots,T
. The reverse process samples an image from a generalized version of
p^{\mathrm{data}}(\bm{x})
, approximating the theoretical prior
p(\bm{x})
. We used the denoising diffusion probabilistic model
[
12
]
which samples
\bm{x}_{t}
given
\bm{x}_{t-1}
as

\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{x}_{t-1}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}\bm{x}_{t-1},(1-\alpha_{t})\bm{I}_{\mathcal{Z}}\right)

(6)

where
\bm{I}_{\mathcal{Z}}
is the identity matrix on
\mathcal{Z}
and
\alpha_{t}
is a scaling factor defined such that
\bm{x}_{T}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0}_{\mathcal{Z}},\bm{I}_{\mathcal{Z}})
. An approximate reverse process, involving the score function
\bm{s}(\bm{x}_{t},t)\triangleq\nabla\log p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t})
—
p_{t}
being the probability distribution function of
\bm{x}_{t}
—can be derived to compute
\bm{x}_{t-1}
from
\bm{x}_{t}
as

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{t-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1})}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\bm{x}_{t}+\frac{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\beta_{t}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\hat{\bm{x}}_{0}(\bm{x}_{t})+\sigma_{t}\bm{z}

\displaystyle\bm{z}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0}_{\mathcal{Z}},\bm{I}_{\mathcal{Z}})\,,

(7)

where
\bar{\alpha}_{t}=\prod_{s=1}^{t}\alpha_{s}
,
\sigma_{t}=(1-\alpha_{t})(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1})/(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t})
,
\beta_{t}=1-\alpha_{t}
and
\hat{\bm{x}}_{0}(\bm{x}_{t})\triangleq\mathbb{E}[\bm{x}_{0}|\bm{x}_{t}]
is given by Tweedie’s formula:

\hat{\bm{x}}_{0}(\bm{x}_{t})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}}(\bm{x}_{t}+(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t})\bm{s}(\bm{x}_{t},t))\,.

(8)

The score function
\bm{s}
is unknown and therefore is approximated by a neural network
\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}\colon\mathcal{Z}\times[0,T]\to\mathcal{Z}
trained by score matching as

\min_{\bm{\theta}}\,\mathbb{E}_{t,\bm{x}_{0},\bm{\epsilon}}\left[\left\|\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{t},t)-\nabla_{\bm{x}_{t}}\log(p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{x}_{0})\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]

(9)

where
\bm{x}_{0}\sim p^{\mathrm{data}}
,
\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0}_{\mathcal{Z}},\bm{I}_{\mathcal{Z}})
and
\bm{x}_{t}=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\bm{x}_{0}+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\bm{\epsilon}
. An equivalent formulation for denoising diffusion probabilistic models can be to predict the added noise
\bm{\epsilon}

[
12
]
. In the following, this analogous formulation is used and
\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{t},t)
represents the neural network trained on noise estimation.

Diffusion posterior sampling aims at solving an inverse problem by combining the prior probability distribution function
p(\bm{x})
and the likelihood of the measurements
p(\bm{y}|\bm{x})
. It uses the conditional score
\nabla_{\bm{x}_{t}}p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}|\bm{y})
to guide the reverse diffusion process in order to recover an image matching the measurements
\bm{y}
. The conditional score is given by the Bayes’ formula as

\nabla_{\bm{x}_{t}}\log p(\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{y})=\nabla\log p(\bm{x}_{t})+\nabla_{\bm{x}_{t}}\log p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{x}_{t})\,.

(10)

There is no explicit relation between
\bm{y}
and
\bm{x}_{t}
, thus making the computation of
p(\bm{y}|\bm{x}_{t})
intractable. Instead, the following approximation is commonly used:

\nabla_{\bm{x}_{t}}\log p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{x}_{t})\simeq\nabla_{\bm{x}_{t}}\log p(\bm{y}\mid\hat{\bm{x}}_{0}(\bm{x}_{t}))\,.

(11)

The overall diffusion posterior sampling process, which consists in iteratively sampling
\bm{x}^{\prime}_{t-1}
from
\bm{x}_{t}
using (
2.2
) then performing a gradient descent step from
\bm{x}^{\prime}_{t-1}
using (
11
), is summarized in Algorithm
1
.

Experiments

3.1

Training, Emission Data Simulation and Evaluation

All methods (training and reconstruction) were implemented with PyTorch.

We generated a collection of activity-attenuation pairs
\bm{x}=(\bm{\lambda},\bm{\mu})
using the extended cardiac-torso software
[
10
]
with different morphology and respiratory motion parameters to create a diverse dataset—without tumors included. The images consist of 2-dimensional 128
\times
128 slices with a 3-mm pixel size. The image pairs were used as follows: 4,000 for training, 1,600 for validation and 10 for testing—training and testing slices were extracted from different phantoms. We solved the score-matching problem (
9
) using the Adam optimizer with approximately 250 epochs. The training was performed on standardized images, and the standardization was taken into account in the forward model (
1
).

We trained two score functions
\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot,t)\colon\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{Z}
. The first score uses the proposed model and was trained on matching
(\bm{\lambda},\bm{\mu})
pairs in order to account for dependencies between
\bm{\lambda}
and
\bm{\mu}
(referred to as diffusion posterior sampling), while the second is of the form
\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x},t)=[\bm{s}_{\bm{\phi}}(\bm{\lambda},t),\bm{s}_{\bm{\psi}}(\bm{\mu},t)]
,
\bm{\theta}=(\bm{\phi},\bm{\psi})
, where the two score functions
\bm{s}_{\bm{\phi}}(\cdot,t),\bm{s}_{\bm{\psi}}(\cdot,t)\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
were trained independently on the activity images and attenuation images respectively—this model assumes that
\bm{\lambda}
and
\bm{\mu}
are independent, i.e.,
p(\bm{x})=p(\bm{\lambda})\cdot p(\bm{\mu})
(referred to as diffusion posterior sampling2).

The emission raw data
\bm{y}
were generated following (
1
) using pairs
\bm{x}=(\bm{\lambda},\bm{\mu})
from the testing dataset. We generated time-of-flight and non-time-of-flight data using an homemade positron emission tomography projector with a 5-mm full width at half maximum spatial resolution, a 60-mm full width at half maximum temporal resolution and 315 projection angles. We used
\tau=5\cdot 10^{-1}
and we ignored background effects (
\bm{r}=\bm{0}_{\mathcal{Y}}
).

The images were reconstructed using diffusion posterior sampling, diffusion posterior sampling2 as well as maximum likelihood activity and attenuation—with 50 outer iterations—for comparison. We used the peak signal-to-noise ratio and structural similarity index measure—with the ground truth images as references—as figures of merit, computed using the functions
peak_signal_noise_ratio
and
structural_similarity
from the Python package
skimage.metrics
.

3.2

Results

Ground truth and reconstructed images are shown in Figure
1
. Maximum likelihood activity and attenuation-reconstructed images without time-of-flight suffer from
\bm{\lambda}
–
\bm{\mu}
crosstalk as expected, while maximum likelihood activity and attenuation with time-of-flight does a reasonably good job although images suffer from noise amplification and partial volume effects due to the intrinsic resolution of the imaging system. On the other hand, diffusion posterior sampling- and diffusion posterior sampling2-reconstructed images achieve near perfect-resolution and noise-free reconstruction, even in absence of time-of-flight data. In addition, we observe that diffusion posterior sampling-reconstructed
\bm{\lambda}
and
\bm{\mu}
share similar features while some discrepancies can be observed with diffusion posterior sampling2 (e.g., magnified area in the lungs).

Figure
2
shows structural similarity index measure vs peak signal-to-noise ratio plots of the diffusion posterior sampling and diffusion posterior sampling2 methods for the 10 testing data in the high-count setting—maximum likelihood activity and attenuation reconstructions were omitted for visibility as they are largely outperformed. We observe that diffusion posterior sampling outperforms diffusion posterior sampling2. More particularly, diffusion posterior sampling without time-of-flight outperforms diffusion posterior sampling2 with time-of-flight, suggesting that the reconstruction benefits of the dependencies between
\bm{\lambda}
and
\bm{\mu}
.

4

Discussion

The findings highlight the efficacy of diffusion posterior sampling for joint reconstruction of the activity and the attenuation in positron emission tomography using emission data only, particularly in non-TOF settings where conventional methods often falter due to activity–attenuation crosstalk. Unlike maximum likelihood activity and attenuation, the diffusion posterior sampling framework integrates prior knowledge through the learned joint probability distribution function of activity and attenuation, ensuring consistency between the reconstructed images and effectively addressing crosstalk challenges. The experimental results confirm that diffusion posterior sampling can generate high-quality, noise-free reconstructions, even under non-time-of-flight conditions, and that it surpasses its independently trained variant, DPS2, by leveraging activity–attenuation dependencies. Similar observations were made in multi-energy computed tomography reconstruction
[
13
]
.

Nevertheless, this study acknowledges certain limitations. The models were exclusively trained on 2-dimensional extended cardiac-torso phantom data, which, despite their widespread use in simulation studies, lack the anatomical diversity and complexity of real-world patient datasets. This constraint potentially limits the generalizability of the model to clinical scenarios. Furthermore, the extension of this framework to 3-dimensional volumes remains a critical next step. We are currently working on extending this method by incorporating real patient data and using data compression techniques to handle 3-dimensional data with encouraging results. These advancements are essential to validate the clinical applicability of diffusion posterior sampling and to realize its potential in routine positron emission tomography imaging workflows.

5

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the promise of diffusion posterior sampling as an innovative tool for joint reconstruction of the activity and the attenuation in positron emission tomography imaging. By directly modeling the dependencies between activity and attenuation, diffusion posterior sampling achieves superior performance, particularly in non-time-of-flight scenarios. While the results represent a significant advancement over traditional maximum likelihood activity and attenuation methods, ongoing work aims to extend the approach to 3-dimensional data and real-world clinical conditions with encouraging preliminary findings.
