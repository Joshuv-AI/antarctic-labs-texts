Site-neutral payment for routine services could save commercial purchasers and patients billions
Authors: Roslyn C Murray, Haroon Janjua, Christopher M Whaley
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMedicare and commercial payers pay more for services delivered in hospital outpatient departments than in physicians' offices or ambulatory surgery centers. These “site-of-care” payment differentials contribute to rising health care spending and drive hospital acquisition of physician practices. Because many non-hospital settings can provide equivalent or higher-quality care at lower costs, site-neutral payment has gained traction in Medicare, with the potential to save patients and taxpayers billions. A growing number of states are exploring similar policies in the commercial market by capping payments for routine services at a percentage of the Medicare payment in non-hospital settings. The impact of such policies for commercial purchasers and patients has not yet been modeled.MethodsThis study uses 2022 commercial claims data from the Health Care Cost Institute and estimates potential savings by capping prices for 2561 routine services (Current Procedural Terminology codes) at 150% of the Medicare payment in hospital, office, and ambulatory surgery center settings.ResultsSite-neutral payment for commercial purchasers and patients could save $10.8 billion across 48 states and Washington, D.C. State level aggregate hospital operating margins would decline modestly.ConclusionSite-neutral policies could yield substantial savings but require ongoing monitoring for unintended effects.

Introduction

By statute, Medicare pays more for services performed in the hospital outpatient department than for services performed in physicians' offices or ambulatory surgery centers (ASCs). For example, an epidural injection into the lumbar or sacral region costs $741 in the hospital outpatient setting, compared to just $256 in physicians' offices.1 Similarly, many commercial insurers pay more for services provided in hospitals than in non-hospital settings. In many cases, the payment differences between hospital and non-hospital facilities are even larger among commercial payers than in Medicare.2

These “site-of-care” payment differentials contribute to increased health care spending and drive hospital acquisition of physician practices. The share of physicians employed by a hospital or health system has nearly doubled, with over 55% of US physicians employed by a hospital.3 Research indicates that this relationship contributes to both price increases and changes in referrals to hospitals.4,5 For instance, studies show that in addition to collecting facility fees, professional fees for outpatient services also increase following acquisition.6-9 Hospital-employed physicians are also less likely to use an ASC compared to independent physicians.10

Given that many non-hospital settings provide equivalent or higher quality care at lower costs, site-neutral payment policies have emerged as a potential solution in the Medicare sector to address these payment differentials and curb vertical integration.11 A prominent proposal comes from the Medicare Payment Advisory Commission (MedPAC), which advises Congress on Medicare payment policy. MedPAC advocates for site-neutral payments for evaluation and management (E&M) visits and other low-complexity, low-intensity medical services, including X-rays, minor procedures, and drug injections. MedPAC estimates that implementing these recommendations could save patients and taxpayers about $8 billions.1

A growing number of states are considering adopting MedPAC's approach for the commercial market. For example, New York introduced legislation during the 2025-2026 session that would cap commercial payments for the routine services identified by MedPAC at a percentage of the Medicare payment in non-hospital settings, such as physician offices or ASCs. Two studies have estimated savings from hypothetical commercial site-neutral payment reforms.12,13 But no studies have evaluated an actual proposed policy, such as the New York Fair Pricing Act. The impact of the policy at a national scale remains unknown. This study estimates the potential savings for health care purchasers and their members from applying similar payment caps across all settings for routine services identified by MedPAC as appropriate for non-hospital care environments. We also evaluate the potential impact of these payment reductions on hospital operating margins at the state level.

Study data and methods

Study data

Our study used 2022 data from the Health Care Cost Institute (HCCI) data contributors. HCCI aggregates commercial claims data from major US insurers, capturing service utilization and negotiated prices. Alabama was excluded from the analysis due to data use restrictions on reporting price information. Additionally, we incorporated data from the Medicare physician fee schedule (PFS) and the Medicare ASC services payment system to calculate Medicare-equivalent payments for each procedure. We also used hospital cost report data from the National Academy for State Health Policy (NASHP) Hospital Cost Tool to estimate the impact of savings from site-neutral payment on state-level hospital operating margins. NASHP's measure of operating margins represents revenue and expenses from providing care to patients and excludes nonpatient-related income and expenses, and thus, is higher than the margins reported by hospitals. We focused on this measure as site-neutral payments would only impact revenue from patient care.

MedPAC identified E&M services and procedures associated with 66 ambulatory payment classifications (APCs) as safe to perform in non-hospital settings. Among these, E&M services and 57 APCs were considered safe to perform in physicians' offices, as they were delivered in that setting more than half the time. The remaining 9 APCs were considered safe in ASCs based on the same criteria (Appendix Table S1) (To access the appendix, click on the Details tab of the article online).1 Throughout this analysis, we refer to these services collectively as the “MedPAC services.”

While some commercial payers use APCs for outpatient hospital services, our data only included Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes. Using data from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services outpatient prospective payment system (OPPS) Addendum B, we mapped the 66 APCs to CPT codes and identified 2561 procedures fitting these classifications. Our analysis focused on outpatient and professional claims for these services. We excluded procedures for which we could not calculate a Medicare-equivalent price using CPT codes—or for the ASC services, using both CPT and APCs—and trimmed the top and bottom 1% of commercial prices to reduce the influence of outliers (for additional details see Appendix methods) (To access the appendix, click on the Details tab of the article online).

We used place of service (POS) codes to identify care settings. Claims with a POS of 19 and 22 were identified as occurring in the off- and on-campus hospital outpatient department, respectively. Claims with a POS of 11 were identified as occurring in the physician's office. Claims with a POS of 24 were identified as occurring in the ASC. We limited our analysis to these specific service locations. We further excluded claims from hospitals and ASCs that did not have a corresponding professional or facility claim for the same patient, date, and procedure, to mitigate potential data errors. Our final sample included 148 999 002 observations and 1633 unique CPT codes accounting for $17.4 billion in spending from 48 states and Washington, D.C.

Analysis

Before estimating savings from site-neutral payment policies, we first compared average commercial prices relative to Medicare non-hospital rates for each service category across the 48 states and Washington, D.C. For E&M services and the 57 APCs deemed safe for physician office settings, we used the Medicare PFS, which sets payment rates based on the expected labor and material costs of delivering services in a physician's office. Medicare PFS payments were linked to each claim using patient ZIP code, which was crosswalked to the corresponding Medicare Administrative Contractor locality codes, which determine geographically adjusted Medicare payment rates. For the remaining 9 APCs, we applied the Medicare ASC payment rate, which includes both the physician payment under the PFS and a facility payment that reflects the typical costs of providing care in an ASC, accounting for supplies, equipment, and staff time. To ensure an accurate comparison, we included only procedures that were performed in both the hospital outpatient department and the corresponding non-hospital setting (either physician office or ASC).

We estimated potential savings under a site-neutral payment policy that caps prices for the MedPAC services at 150% of the Medicare payment in the non-hospital setting. At the individual claim level, we calculated a price cap for each service by multiplying the relevant Medicare payment by 1.5. We then identified claims priced above this cap and calculated the savings per claim if prices were reduced to the capped amount. Because the Fair Pricing Act prohibits price increases, we did not model any upward price adjustments for services priced below the cap. Finally, we aggregated savings by service category (E&M, 57 APCs, 9 APCs), by care setting (hospital, physician's office, ASC), and at the state level.

Because the HCCI data represent only a portion of the commercially insured population in each state, we adjusted our savings estimates to reflect the broader commercially insured market. To adjust for the representativeness of the HCCI claims data, we used the ratio of unique members in the HCCI dataset to the Kaiser Family Foundation's state-level estimates of individuals with employer-sponsored insurance in 2022. We then scaled state-level savings by the inverse of this ratio to account for the share of lives represented in the HCCI data.

We repeated the analysis using higher price caps—ranging from 200% to 400% of the Medicare payment in the non-hospital setting—and estimated the effect on state-level aggregate hospital operating margins. For each scenario, we recalculated state-level hospital operating margins by subtracting the projected savings from hospitals' aggregate revenue from patient care within each state.

Limitations

Our study had several limitations. First, the HCCI data include claims from a subset of commercial insurers, which may limit representativeness. We assumed these rates reflect broader commercial prices, but research shows dominant insurers often negotiate lower rates.14-16 Therefore, we may have underestimated potential savings if other insurers pay more than those in the HCCI sample.

Second, we faced challenges in consistently identifying the care setting. Although we relied on place-of-service and type of bill codes, these sometimes conflicted. To address this limitation, we grouped claims by patient and date, assigning the site of service based on the most frequent code in each group. Third, we excluded claims with a facility fee but no corresponding professional claim for the same patient, procedure, and date (15.6% of total claims). While this helped ensure data accuracy, it may have excluded valid services and led to an underestimation of potential savings.

Fourth, when calculating Medicare ASC facility fees, we could not apply hospital-specific wage indices to account for geographic variation. Instead, we used an average county-level wage index to adjust the facility portion regionally. However, due to missing wage index data in Maine, we lost all relevant observations and were unable to include the state in our analysis. Fifth, because the HCCI data lack hospital identifiers, we cannot assess the impact of site-neutral payments at the hospital level. Financial impacts likely vary across hospitals depending on a variety of factors, including payer mix, service volume, and negotiated rates.

Finally, our study estimates a static change and does not incorporate general equilibrium effects. For example, price changes resulting from site neutral payment policies could lead to demand-side responses—such as increased utilization—or supply-side responses—such as price increases for services not targeted by the policy. We also do not account for broader market dynamics, including potential provider entry or exit. These responses could affect the magnitude of the policy's impact, potentially leading to an overestimation of savings, and should be explored in future research.

Results

In 2022, average commercial prices for E&M services and the 57 APCs deemed safe to provide in physician offices—measured as a percentage of the Medicare PFS rate, or “relative prices”—varied widely across 48 states and Washington, D.C. Five states—Arkansas, Hawaii, Massachusetts, Michigan, and Mississippi—had relative prices below 318%, while 4 states—Vermont, West Virginia, Wisconsin, and Wyoming—had relative prices exceeding 668%. Discussion

Our study estimated that site-neutral payments, implemented as a cap on commercial prices at 150% of the Medicare non-hospital payment for the MedPAC services, would have saved purchasers and patients with commercial insurance in 48 states and Washington, D.C., a total of $10.8 billion in 2022. These savings would represent nearly 1% of total annual private health care spending.17 We estimated that revenue losses from site-neutral payments would have had a modest impact on aggregate hospital operating margins. The MedPAC services represent a relatively small share of hospital volume, although the impact is likely to vary across individual hospitals.

A few peer-reviewed studies have estimated the potential impact of implementing site-neutral payments in the commercial market. One study found that aligning hospital payments for the MedPAC services with the median office price could have saved $58 billion in 2022.12 Another study focused on 89 routine services and estimated that purchasers and consumers could have saved $1.2 billion if payments were paid at the median office price.13 Our estimates fall between these 2 studies' findings. Similar to the first analysis, we focus on the same set of approximately 2500 procedures, but instead of setting hospital prices at the median office price, we apply a cap based on a percentage of the Medicare payment in the non-hospital setting. The larger savings estimates from other studies stem from paying a set price—the median office rate—whereas our approach targets only the highest prices in the distribution, as proposed in New York's legislation.

Capping commercial prices for the MedPAC services at a percentage of the Medicare payment in non-hospital settings offers a few important advantages.10 First, a price cap—rather than identifying a set payment based on the average price—helps policymakers to target the highest prices while allowing insurers and providers to negotiate prices that are below the payment limit. Second, Medicare payments are based on formulas that have been developed and refined over time with input and guidance from providers. These formulas attempt to estimate the underlying cost of providing care to patients. Medicare payment schedules also account for medical inflation and are not influenced by the relative bargaining power of insurers and providers as prices are in the commercial sector. Finally, price caps have been used in other state-level reforms targeting high hospital prices.18

As mentioned previously, hospitals could respond to lost revenue from site-neutral payment caps by requesting price increases for non-targeted services. This hypothesis assumes that hospitals possess unused pricing power that they have chosen not to exercise—an assumption similar to that underlying cost-shifting theory. Although this specific question has not been extensively studied, the literature on cost-shifting suggests that providers generally do not have sufficient reserve pricing power to raise prices for commercial payers when Medicare payments are cut.19-23 Therefore, we do not anticipate that price increases for these services would completely offset savings. Nevertheless, some hospital responses to offset lost revenue are possible, meaning that our estimates should be considered an upper bound. Assuming that savings are offset by 15%, total savings would still be around $9.2 billion, which is similar in magnitude.

Additionally, because the targeted services would be less lucrative under the price caps, hospitals may choose to provide fewer of them, potentially reducing supply. Prices for services currently below the cap could also rise toward the cap ceiling, as observed in Oregon's implementation of a similar policy.18 However, the New York legislation includes provisions to prevent this type of immediate upward price response.

Furthermore, capping total payments, rather than just the facility fee, may also create unintended consequences. Without a specific limit on facility fees, hospitals could collect most of the capped payment, leading to lower professional fees for physicians. In addition, because the cap applies across all settings, it could inadvertently reduce payments to independent physician practices, many of which are already financially strained and under pressure to consolidate.

An alternative approach would be to set a price cap on the facility fee only, setting payment at a very low percentage of the Medicare OPPS amount (eg, 50%). This would effectively eliminate facility fees for services without an OPPS payment and align any remaining facility payment more closely with the practice expense component used to compensate physician offices for practice costs.1 Although this policy would generate significantly less savings, as it would only apply to the hospital outpatient setting, it would avoid cutting payments to physician practices and focus reforms where prices are highest. However, similar to concerns about price increases for non-regulated services, hospitals could respond by increasing professional fees or combining facility fees with professional fees to offset revenue losses.

Conclusion

Site-neutral payments implemented through commercial price caps tied to Medicare payments have the potential to generate substantial savings for commercial purchasers and patients, with only modest average effects on hospital margins. However, policymakers must carefully balance these potential savings against the risk of unintended consequences that could arise from such reforms. Thoughtful implementation and ongoing evaluation will be critical to ensure that the benefits of site-neutral payments are realized without compromising access or quality of care.
