Occupational exposure to ionising radiation and central nervous system tumour mortality: a pooled analysis of Korean, French and US medical radiation workers
Authors: Clémence Baudin, Won-Jin Lee, Tae-Eun Kwon, Cato Milder, Altay Myssayev, Philippe Lestaevel, Corinne Mandin, Isabelle Thierry-Chef, Martha S Linet, Cari M Kitahara, Marie-Odile Bernier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHigh-dose ionising radiation (IR) is the only established modifiable risk factor for central nervous system (CNS) tumours, but the association with low- to moderate-dose IR is unclear.MethodsThe BECOME (Brain cancEr risk in joint COhort of MEdical workers exposed to ionizing radiation) study includes harmonised data from nested case-control studies of US, Korean and French medical radiation worker cohorts employed during 1926–2021. A total of 361 CNS tumour deaths were matched on sex, year of birth and cohort to 1851 controls alive at the time of death of the corresponding case. Badge doses, defined as Hp(10) (individual dose equivalent at 10 mm depth in tissue) and brain doses, were estimated using comparable protocols, and we evaluated age at first exposure, exposure duration and cumulative and annual mean doses (accounting for a 5-year lag). Conditional logistic regression also adjusted for number of years of education, duration of follow-up, exposure duration and age at first exposure.ResultsCumulative brain dose averages were 10.5 (±13) mGy and 9.8 (±12.5) mGy for cases and controls, respectively. Overall, cumulative brain dose was not associated with CNS tumour death (OR=1.05; 95% CI 0.93 to 1.18 for a 10-mGy increase in cumulative brain dose) in all workers nor when the analysis was restricted to radiological technologists. A borderline significant dose–response association was found between occupational exposure to IR and CNS tumour death in medical workers first exposed after 1970 (OR=1.19; 95% CI 1.00 to 1.42 for a 10-mGy increase in cumulative brain dose).ConclusionIn this pooled nested case–control study, cumulative brain occupational radiation exposure was not associated with CNS mortality overall. More studies are needed of workers first exposed after 1970 and those conducting fluoroscopically guided interventional and nuclear medicine procedures.Keywords: Radiation Effects, Epidemiologic Research Design, Health Personnel

Background

Ionising radiation (IR) at moderate-to-high doses (>100 milligray (mGy)) is a recognised risk factor for cancer.1–3 IR is currently the only established modifiable risk factor for brain tumours, although the evidence is largely based on studies of acute, high-dose exposure or therapeutic exposures during childhood, with few studies including individual dose estimates.4–6 For lower doses such as those experienced by most radiation worker groups and the general population more broadly, debate continues about cancer risks.7

Due to the rapid increase in medical IR use in the 20th century,8 medical workers comprise the largest group of workers occupationally exposed to IR, with an estimated 8 million IR-exposed practitioners worldwide by 2014.9 Annual doses received by medical radiation workers have decreased dramatically from an average of about 100 mSv before the 1940s to 0.7 mSv in 2000,1 owing to technological advances in X-ray equipment and radiation protection measures. However, some contemporary workers, such as those performing interventional or nuclear medicine procedures, are frequently exposed to higher doses than those experienced from working in conventional radiology.10–12 Long-term IR-related cancer risks13,14 and non-cancer risks15–17 have been reported for medical workers exposed to fluoroscopically guided interventional (FGI) or nuclear medicine procedures, but some of these studies lack individual occupational radiation dose estimates.

The brain, unlike most of the body, is unprotected against IR exposure for medical workers performing interventional procedures. Although several case reports have described central nervous system (CNS) tumours in interventional cardiologists,18 a link between occupational IR exposure and CNS tumours was not observed in a large, well-controlled epidemiological study that nevertheless lacked individual occupational radiation dose estimates.19

Currently, fewer than 10 cohorts of medical radiation workers are followed worldwide.20 Most of these cohorts include historical populations exposed since the beginning of the 20th century and present methodological shortcomings, including no dose reconstruction, no collection of individual lifestyle factors or end of follow-up before the 2000s. The US Radiological Technologists (USRT) cohort, established in the early 1980s, is the largest active cohort of medical workers, comprising over 100 000 radiological technologists certified for at least 2 years between 1926 and 1982 with individualised dosimetry, self-reported work history and comprehensive covariate information, and long-term follow-up for disease incidence and mortality. More recently, two cohorts of Korean and French medical workers were launched using data from national dosimetry monitoring registries. The ORICAMs (Occupational Radiation-Induced Cancer in Medical Staff) cohort includes all medical workers in France exposed at least once between 2002 and 2012,21 while in Korea, the MRWs (Medical Radiation Workers) cohort includes all medical radiation workers from diagnostic departments exposed at least once between 1996 and 2011.22 Given the rarity and high fatality of brain tumours, pooling data from multiple cohorts is necessary to increase statistical power for detecting potential associations with occupational IR exposure.

The BECOME (Brain cancEr risk in joint COhort of MEdical workers exposed to ionizing radiation) project was designed to pool original, individual-level data from three large national cohorts of IR-exposed medical workers to investigate the risk of death from CNS tumours in relation to cumulative lifetime occupational IR exposure.

Definition of cases and controls

The BECOME project is a pooled case–control study nested in three national medical radiation worker cohorts: the French (ORICAMs), the Korean (MRWs) and the US (USRT) cohorts. Individual-level data were pooled from these three studies to investigate the risk of death from CNS tumours in relation to cumulative lifetime occupational IR exposure.

Mortality from brain and CNS cancer was defined by selecting C70, C71 and C72 death codes (malignant neoplasm of meninges; malignant neoplasm of brain; malignant neoplasm of spinal cord, cranial nerves and other parts of CNS, respectively) as coded in the International Classification of Diseases-10th Revision (ICD-10), or 191 and 192 on ICD-9th Revision (malignant neoplasm of brain and malignant neoplasm of other and unspecified parts of nervous system, respectively), which was coded in the same way for all three cohorts.

In each cohort, each case was matched with five controls alive at the date of the corresponding death, based on sex, study cohort and year of birth—such that calendar time was the time scale, and matching on year of birth so as to provide control for attained age. A subject could serve as a control for more than one case. In addition, a control from one matched set could later be classified as a case, at which point the appropriate controls were selected for the new set. For each control, all information was truncated at a reference date, called the index year, which was set to the date on which the matched case died from a CNS tumour, meaning that all doses received after that reference date were not accounted for in the cumulative exposure calculation. If a control was matched to several cases, they were censored at the date of death of each case, respectively. Workers with a total career dose >1000 mSv were excluded from the BECOME study (N=14), as occupational doses this high were unlikely to occur. Also excluded were workers who had a previous cancer (except skin cancer) or reported having undergone head and neck radiotherapy at the time of the baseline survey (N=23). The pooled case–control study cohort comprised 2212 workers, including 361 who died of CNS tumours and 1851 matched controls, totalling 88 (24%) deaths in the French cohort, 24 (7%) in the Korean cohort and 249 (69%) in the US cohort. The pooled population included 56% women and 73% radiological technologists.

Exposure characterisation

Exposure to IR was measured by personal dosimeter for each individual with a badge (Film badge, Thermo Luminescence, Radio Photo Luminescence or Optically Stimulated Luminescence dosimeters depending on cohort and time period) worn at the chest level, under the apron in France and Korea, and above the apron (occasionally one badge above and one below the apron) in the USA. Annual mean badge doses, stored in national registers and derived from monthly or quarterly dose measurements, were used similarly for each worker. All dosimetric results were expressed as the operational quantity of personal dose equivalent as defined by the International Commission on Radiological Protection and abbreviated as Hp(10).23 This is the dose expected at a depth of 10 mm in soft tissue and is generally regarded as an operational surrogate for whole-body effective dose.

Badge readings were available from 1960 to April 2023 with recording thresholds of 0.2–0.05 mSv in the French cohort, and from 1996 to 2011 with a recording threshold of 0.01 mSv in the Korean cohort. In the US cohort, doses were estimated using badge readings when available, including film badges (1960–2001) and thermoluminescent dosimeters (1998–2020), with thresholds of 0.1 mSv and 0.02 mSv, respectively, while in the 1998–2001 transition period between dosimeter types, weighted badge readings were assigned based on probable individual adoption of new dosimeters. When badge readings were unavailable or not incorporated into the dosimetry, badge doses were derived from period-specific population distributions—developed from literature data (before 1956), extrapolations (1956–1965) or archival badge records (after 1965)—and individualised using self-reported work histories. While earlier dose reconstruction covered up to 1997, badge dose estimates were recently updated through 2020 by incorporating newly available badge readings and work history data.

Methods for estimating brain doses for workers in the US (cumulated through 1997) and Korean cohorts were published previously.24,25 A similar calculation method was applied to the French cohort, for which brain doses were estimated for the present study. Briefly, Hp(10) doses were converted to absorbed brain doses using appropriate conversion coefficients derived from the ICRP, namely the absorbed dose to the brain per unit of air kerma free-in-air (Gy per Gy) (Dt/Ka) and the personal dose equivalent per unit of air kerma free-in-air (Sv per Gy)(Hp(10)/Ka).26 The calculation included information on predominant energy and geometry of exposure, the lack of protection of the head, not being under the apron and the recording of doses as zero when measured below the threshold. More specifically, the dominant average energy from diagnostic radiological examinations was set between 30 and 40 keV, and the radiation geometry was defined in the anteroposterior direction for all medical staff, who are primarily exposed when facing the patient. Furthermore, when a zero dose appeared in the data, a value equivalent to half the minimum detection limit of the dosimeters was assigned.

For each worker, cumulative Hp(10) and cumulative absorbed brain doses for the worker’s entire career were calculated as the sum of annual doses, from the first year of exposure up to 5 years before the index year (assuming a minimum delay of 5 years between exposure and death due to CNS tumour). This 5-year period was a priori chosen based on current knowledge on the risk of brain tumours after exposure to IR.5 A category-based exposure variable (Dose_Hp10_cat) was also created, using the 50th (median), 75th and 90th percentiles of the 5-year lagged cumulative dose Hp(10) of cases by cohort. This division into percentiles was done to ensure sufficient numbers of cases in each dose category. It was not possible to create categories based on quartiles, as the doses corresponding to the 25th and 50th quantiles were similar within the French and Korean cohorts.

In addition, age at first exposure and exposure duration (between the date of first exposure and last date of exposure for cases and the index year for controls, both accounting for a 5-year lag) were calculated. The date of first exposure was determined by the first recorded annual dose (zero or non-zero) for the ORICAMs and MRWs databases, and by the first non-zero annual dose for the USRT database, in which a zero dose could reflect the absence of exposure.

Covariates

Sex and age (year of birth) are accounted for by the matching criteria. From an epidemiological perspective, no other risk factors for brain tumours are mentioned in the literature, apart from these with respect to exposure to IR.27 However, other covariates potentially related to IR occupational exposure, except the cumulative dose, were evaluated as potential confounders selected based on the existing literature.28 These factors included the following time-fixed variables calculated at the index date for each case and controls: profession (physician, radiological technician, nurse, other), number of years of education post high school (continuous, in years), age at first exposure (continuous, in years), duration of follow-up (continuous, in years) and exposure duration (continuous, in years).

Given that only small amounts of missing data (0.5%) were identified across all covariates, and that these covariates were never included all together in an adjusted model, workers with missing data were excluded only when the covariate in question was used in the bivariable analyses.

Statistical methods

Descriptive analyses were carried out to present the characteristics of the study population and the distribution of cases and controls according to sex, profession, age at death and different variables of exposure.

Conditional logistic regression models stratified by matched case–control sets were used to estimate ORs and 95% CIs for CNS tumour deaths in relation to the following variables of interest: cumulative Hp(10) or brain doses for each 10 mGy increase (taking into account a 5-year exposure lag), age at first exposure and profession. The impact of adjusting for potential confounders on the relationship between CNS risk and the variables of interest was tested by individually adding the following covariates, one at a time, to the unadjusted model: profession (model 1), number of years of education (model 2), duration of follow-up (model 3), duration of exposure (model 4), age at first exposure (model 5).

Because the USRT cohort only included radiological technologists and was the largest contributor of cases and controls, a sensitivity analysis was performed that restricted the study population to radiological technologists across all three cohorts and repeated the matching process to ensure radiological technologist cases were matched with radiological technologist controls. Moreover, as badges were not routinely available before the 1970s, another sensitivity analysis considered only workers who were first exposed after 1970. Finally, sensitivity analyses considering alternative 10- or 15-year lag periods in the calculation of cumulative exposure over the entire career were tested.

Heterogeneity between cohorts was assessed using meta-analysis, and an analysis of the non-linearity of the dose–response relationship was evaluated using restricted cubic splines. Finally, influence diagnostics and leave-one-stratum-out analysis were performed to evaluate whether the main estimate is driven by a limited number of influential matched sets.

All statistical tests were two-tailed, with a type I error of 5%. Analyses were performed using SAS software, V.9.4. Appendices were produced using R V.4.1.1 (‘rms’ package and ‘survival’ library) and Stata V.16.0 (meta command).

Results

Descriptive analyses

The pooled case–control study cohort comprised 2212 workers, including 361 who died of CNS tumours and 1851 matched controls, totalling 88 (24%) deaths in the French cohort, 24 (7%) in the Korean cohort and 249 (69%) in the US cohort. None of the subjects in the control group later became cases, but 39 subjects in that group were found to be matched with more than one case. The pooled population included 56% women and 73% radiological technologists. The three cohorts were significantly different with regard to sex, age at case death, duration of follow-up, number of years of education post-high school, exposure duration, age at first exposure and cumulative Hp(10) and brain doses (tables 1 and 2, online supplemental appendices 2 and 3). Workers in the USRT cohort were older than those in the other two cohorts, with birth dates ranging from 1904 to 1960, and were exposed in earlier calendar years, with only 39% first exposed after 1970 compared with 97% in the MRWs cohort and 99% in the ORICAMs cohort.

Additional adjustment for other covariates did not fundamentally change the ORs, except for exposure duration, which slightly strengthened OR for estimated brain doses when included in the model (model 4, online supplemental appendix 4).

The formal test of homogeneity indicated that the effect sizes across the three cohorts were highly consistent, with no evidence of statistical heterogeneity (Q=1.43, df=2, p=0.4897). This was further supported by I2=0.00%, H2=1.00 and τ2=0.0000, indicating that the observed variation was entirely due to within-study sampling error rather than true cohort heterogeneity (online supplemental appendices 5 and 6).

Regarding cubic splines, the Wald test for non-linearity showed no evidence of a non-linear relationship (χ²=0.39, df=1, p=0.5314). The overall dose–response association remained statistically non-significant (χ²=0.49, df=2, p=0.7825). These diagnostics formally confirm that our original simpler model using a per-10-mGy linear term was appropriate and that the lack of statistical significance is not an artefact of a missed non-linear pattern (online supplemental appendix 7).

For the influence diagnostics analysis, out of 2212 observations, only three individuals (0.13%) marginally exceeded the conventional dfbeta threshold of ±0.2 (Row 572: −0.202; Row 1082: 0.226; Row 1205: −0.305). Given that the vast majority of the data points reside well within the stable zone and the few outliers are only marginal, we can confidently conclude that our main dose–response estimate is structurally stable and not driven by a limited number of influential individuals or highly exposed outliers (online supplemental appendix 8). Concerning the leave-one-stratum-out analysis, we iteratively excluded each matched set (stratum) one at a time and re-estimated the beta coefficients for a 10 mGy dose increase with a 5-year lag. The resulting distribution of the beta estimates was highly stable and centred tightly around the original model’s estimate. This demonstrates that our overall non-significant dose–response association is structurally robust and not artifactually driven or distorted by any single matched set or highly exposed individuals (online supplemental appendix 9).

When the analyses were restricted to radiological technologists, no associations were observed (table 4).

Discussion

Based on a pooled analysis of three national nested case–control studies, the present study aimed to assess the relationship between CNS tumour mortality and occupational IR exposure in medical workers. Our results showed no evidence overall for an association between occupational exposure and risk of brain cancer mortality. Results were similar when only radiological technologists were considered in the pooled analysis. A borderline significant association between cumulative Hp(10) or brain doses and the risk of death from CNS tumours was found when the analyses were restricted to workers first exposed after 1970, showing good agreement between these two measures of exposure.

Previous analyses carried out separately in each national cohort have not yielded significant increased risks of brain tumour mortality. A lack of statistical power has been suspected in the French ORICAMs case–control study, as only 29 cases were included in the previous analysis.29 The USRT cohort showed no association between brain dose and malignant intracranial neoplasms, both in the overall cohort and in the subset of radiological technologists who reported having assisted with FGI procedures.13 The Korean cohort showed no significant associations between IR exposure and CNS tumour incidence within the cohort and when compared with the general population.30 Combining these three cohorts with different exposure periods (with USRT having the longest and the earliest) has made it possible to capture both the decrease in average doses received across the decades and the recent, smaller increase in doses related to the rising use of FGI and nuclear medicine procedures. The nested case–control design with matching cases and controls in each cohort allowed us to consider between-cohort differences in terms of data collection (including lack of dose data during early training), dose assessment, socioeconomic factors and different definitions of first occupational exposure (defined as ‘year first worked’ in the USRT). The inclusion of cases from three large cohorts increased the number of deaths and the IR dose distribution range considered, and therefore the statistical power of the analyses.

The present study, showing no association for the overall combined set of cases and controls, is consistent with those of other studies in which the adult population was exposed to IR in different situations (Chernobyl disaster, nuclear industry, medical setting, flight travels, nuclear weapons testing or environmental radiation).31 Increased radiation-associated risks of CNS tumours have only been reported in atomic bomb survivors exposed in childhood or adulthood and children and young adults exposed to CT.32,33

When considering only the workers first exposed after 1970, we observed a borderline significant dose–response for CNS tumour death. One goal of this sensitivity analysis was to reduce the uncertainties in dose assessment, as widespread monitoring of badge doses began in the 1970s. Furthermore, it allowed for tighter adjustment by year of birth, as all the French and all but one Korean CNS cancer cases were exposed only after 1970, compared with 118 out of 270 in the USRT. Risk estimates restricted to workers first exposed from 1970 onwards were higher than those for the workers as a whole, despite the lower levels of exposure. Considering the large increase in use of FGI procedures in cardiology and radiology since the 1970s and the lack of protection of the worker’s head during these procedures, we could not rule out that the observed increased risk may be related to the category of workers performing interventional procedures, especially physicians. However, only a few case reports have supported this hypothesis until now34 whereas larger studies did not report any increase in CNS tumour death in physicians19 or radiological technologists performing interventional procedures.13 Similar to our findings, studies of nuclear workers have reported higher radiation-related risk estimates for solid cancer among workers hired in more recent years compared with those hired earlier. Although the underlying reasons for this difference between early and later hires are not yet well understood, they warrant further systematic investigation.35

Unfortunately, our study did not allow specific investigation into the radiation risks for physicians, overall or by specialty, due to small case numbers. Furthermore, the Korean cohort did not capture nuclear medicine workers, and medical specialty was available only in a subset of French physicians. Despite potentially higher cumulative lifetime exposure compared with other medical workers, physicians may be less likely to regularly wear their dosimeters.36 Future studies should distinguish physicians potentially exposed to greater IR doses, such as interventional radiologists and cardiologists. For ORICAMS, efforts are underway to add information on medical specialties.

The strengths of our study include the large sample of medical workers with a wide spectrum of medical professions, a broad exposure period representing a wide range of doses and procedure types, and individualised estimates of cumulative lifetime brain absorbed doses for all workers. The dose reconstruction methods previously developed and applied in the USRT were adapted largely for use in the Korean and French cohorts, ensuring uniformity of dose assessment between the three databases.24 In addition, the use of national death registry-reported ICD codes in each cohort to assess CNS tumour mortality allowed good agreement across cohorts for outcome assessment.

However, malignant brain cancer histology, molecular characteristics and tumour stage could not be included in the analyses because little information on this topic was provided within the cohorts. It would nevertheless be important to consider this in future research, as the latency, progression and survival of different types of malignant brain cancer—even within specific categories of these cancers—vary greatly. In addition, although comparable protocols have been used, certain limitations must be mentioned in the reconstruction of brain dose. For the French and Korean cohorts, as the badges are worn under the apron, unlike the USRT, many zero-dose Hp(10) were observed (19% in MRWs, 55% in ORICAMs, 1% in USRT). It was not possible to differentiate between zero doses (ie, people who were genuinely unexposed), and those whose doses were below the badge’s detection threshold. For the reconstruction of brain doses, non-zero brain dose values were attributed when Hp(10) doses were zero, based on the expected level of exposure of workers and the badge threshold in each country, possibly leading to a slight overestimation of brain doses for the least exposed categories. Additional assumptions had to be made on the beam angle of exposure of workers and the energy spectrum of radiation from procedures to allow the use of the dose conversion coefficient provided by ICRP to convert Hp(10) doses into organ brain doses. Nonetheless, the results for brain dose considered have overlapping CIs with the Hp(10) dose analyses, showing good agreement between Hp(10) doses and brain doses in this analysis.

Other limitations include the potential for selection bias, as cases and controls in USRT were required to respond to at least one mailed survey compared with the selection in the Korean and French cohorts, based on national occupational dose registries. Although survey response rates in USRT were quite high, no information was available as to whether IR exposure levels were similar between respondents and non-respondents. Nevertheless, the dose–response associations remained relatively stable regardless of the combination of cohorts, although dose–response analyses including USRT tended to have lower effect estimates (results not shown). Few covariates could have been tested in the present study as potential confounders, since data such as weight, height, alcohol or tobacco consumption, for example, were collected differentially via surveys for the USRT and MRWs, and by consulting medical records from occupational medicine for ORICAMs. Nevertheless, the only established risk factor for CNS tumours is IR exposure, and other lifestyle covariates considered in a previous study did not change the risk estimates.

In conclusion, in this large case–control study nested within three national cohorts, we found no evidence of an association between occupational exposure to IR and CNS tumour death in medical workers as a whole or in radiological technologists. The borderline positive dose–response association in those first exposed after 1970 needs to be further investigated to assess more specifically the risks among today’s more highly exposed medical radiation workers, in particular those performing interventional and nuclear medicine procedures, whose numbers have increased dramatically over the past four decades.
