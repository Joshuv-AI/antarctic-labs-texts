Biologics and Drug‐Induced Immunogenicity: A Case Report of Acquired Hemophilia A Presenting After Immunotherapy
Authors: Kevin A. Murgas, Sundus Nasim, Stephen Poos, Jules A. Cohen, Anna Andrzejczyk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAcquired hemophilia A is a rare, potentially life‐threatening bleeding disorder caused by autoantibody inhibitors to coagulation factor VIII. We report the case of an 87‐year‐old female who presented with symptomatic bleeding and was subsequently diagnosed with acquired hemophilia A. Her clinical course, treatment, and recovery are described. Notably, her recent medication history included ixekizumab, an immunotherapy agent used for an underlying autoimmune condition, and a course of antibiotics, complicating the identification of a definitive etiology. This case raises the possibility of ixekizumab as a potential novel pharmacologic trigger for AHA. We highlight challenges in establishing causality and review current literature on hematologic manifestations of drug‐induced autoimmunity, with a focus on biologic immunotherapies.

Introduction

Acquired hemophilia A (AHA) is a rare autoimmune bleeding disorder caused by the development of autoantibodies that functionally inhibit Factor VIII (FVIII), thereby disrupting the intrinsic coagulation cascade and impairing thrombin generation [1]. The estimated incidence of AHA is 1.5 cases per million per year [2]. AHA often presents spontaneously in patients without a prior bleeding history, with clinical symptoms ranging from minor protracted bleeding to life‐threatening hemorrhage [3]. The incidence of AHA increases dramatically with age, with most cases occurring in adults over 65 years old [4]. Other risk factors include autoimmune conditions, obstetric and postpartum state, neoplasia, including hematologic and solid malignancies, and infections, including HIV, hepatitis B, and hepatitis C2.

Diagnosis of AHA is often suggested by an isolated prolongation of the activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT). Classically, this prolongation fails to correct upon mixing equal volumes of both patient and normal plasma, indicating the presence of an inhibitor rather than a factor deficiency; however, the degree of correction may vary depending on the inhibitor titer and specific assay conditions such as incubation time [5]. Factor‐specific activity assays can be performed using either one‐stage or two‐stage methods [6, 7]. In the one‐stage assay, the clotting time is measured after the patient's plasma is combined with plasma deficient in the specific factor being tested. In contrast, two‐stage chromogenic assays use color‐producing substrates that react with activated clotting factors, generating a measurable color change that reflects the factor's activity. While the one‐stage assay is standard for FVIII measurement, it is susceptible to interference. For instance, in patients actively treated with bypassing agents or emicizumab, a bispecific monoclonal antibody that mimics FVIII function, the one‐stage assay may falsely indicate normal FVIII activity. In such clinical scenarios, the two‐stage chromogenic assay is necessary for accurate assessment of the patient's endogenous FVIII activity and response to therapy. More specifically for AHA, the Bethesda assay quantitates inhibitor titer by measuring residual activity in the presence of the inhibitor‐containing plasma [8]. Differential diagnoses, including von Willebrand disease and platelet function disorders, should be evaluated simultaneously [9]. While antiphospholipid syndrome is typically associated with thrombosis, it should also be excluded, as the presence of lupus anticoagulant may also present with isolated aPTT prolongation, albeit via a different mechanism.

Management of AHA primarily involves immunosuppression to prevent further synthesis and accumulation of the inhibitor, along with concurrent hemostatic agents to control bleeding [10, 11]. First‐line immunosuppressive therapy includes corticosteroids, which may be augmented with rituximab or cyclophosphamide to deplete B cells [3, 12]. Bleeding is treated with bypass agents, such as activated Factor VII or activated prothrombin complex concentrate (aPCC), but these therapies carry a risk of thrombosis [2]. Recombinant porcine FVIII replacement may be used with similar hemostatic efficacy, enabling monitoring of FVIII activity and precise dose adjustment, but may be limited by cross‐reactive antibodies [11, 13]. Emicizumab, a bispecific monoclonal antibody that bridges Factor IX and Factor X to restore hemostasis, has emerged as a novel therapy reducing both bleeding and clotting risks [14]. With optimal management, AHA may have a high remission rate close to 90% [15, 16].

AHA is occasionally associated with postpartum and chronic inflammatory states, although most AHA cases are spontaneous. Numerous drug triggers have been characterized, including antibiotics (amoxicillin, trimethoprim‐sulfamethoxazole, ciprofloxacin), anticoagulants (warfarin, apixaban), antiplatelets (aspirin, clopidogrel), and biologic immunotherapies (alemtuzumab, omalizumab, peginterferon alfa‐2a, nivolumab) [17]. However, roughly 50% of cases have no identified cause [4, 10]. When diagnosing any drug‐induced disorder, an autoantibody is insufficient; one must identify a temporal association between a suspected trigger and subsequent development of symptoms that improve upon discontinuation [18]. Determining a trigger is a near‐impossible challenge in the setting of polypharmacy [19]. While research techniques such as immunoprecipitation, mass spectrometry, and epitope mapping can directly assess immunogenicity of triggers, these tests are technically challenging and costly; thus, they are seldom clinically feasible [20, 21]. Therefore, the clinician often must rely on temporal association and proceed to management without definitive knowledge of the trigger.

Over recent decades, biologic immunotherapies have become increasingly developed, studied, and applied for various clinical purposes, ranging from cancer therapy to disease‐modifying anti‐rheumatic drugs (DMARDs) [22, 23, 24, 25]. Biologic DMARDs exert their effect by selectively blocking various cytokines or surface receptors to thereby modulate (typically suppress) immune response [22, 25]. These biologics are now commonly used to treat a range of autoimmune inflammatory conditions, but while effective, they also have shown a potential to paradoxically induce or exacerbate further autoimmunity [26, 27, 28]. Along with immunomodulatory effects, their structure is often chimeric or humanized, lending to potentially increased immunogenicity [29, 30]. Numerous reported hematologic side effects of biologic DMARDs include cytopenias, thrombosis, vasculitis, and recurrent infections [31]. AHA specifically has been described in association with adalimumab (anti‐TNF‐α), etanercept (anti‐TNF‐α), alemtuzumab (anti‐CD52), omalizumab (anti‐IgE), and nivolumab (anti‐PD1) [31, 32, 33].

In this report, we discuss the case of a patient who had known exposure to anti‐IL‐17A‐directed biologic immunotherapy (ixekizumab) and antibiotics (amoxicillin‐clavulanate, ceftriaxone) and later developed acute bleeding symptoms leading to the diagnosis of acquired hemophilia A. We summarize the workup, diagnosis, and management of this patient's condition. We then discuss nuances and limitations to diagnosis and highlight future directions in research.

Case History

An 87‐year‐old female presented with painful swelling of the left wrist and was treated for presumed cellulitis with IV ceftriaxone and was subsequently discharged with a course of oral amoxicillin‐clavulanate (Figure 1). Notably, this patient had a history of psoriasis, which had recently been treated with injections of ixekizumab monthly over the 4 months prior to admission. The patient received an additional dose of ixekizumab one day after this initial discharge. Twelve days later (hospital day 1; HD1), the patient returned with worsening wrist swelling and new‐onset hematuria. She was initially treated again for cellulitis with IV ceftriaxone and fluid support.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

On workup, a coagulopathy was identified with aPTT significantly prolonged to 86.2 s (normal 25–35 s), along with anemia with hemoglobin of 8.6 g/dL (normal 12–16 g/dL in adult females). The aPTT remained elevated after 1:1 mixing with normal plasma, suggesting the presence of a coagulation inhibitor. Additional workup revealed FVIII activity measured at 1%, indicating a likely diagnosis of acquired hemophilia A, which was confirmed by a Bethesda FVIII inhibitor titer of 29.3 BU/mL. Alternative diagnoses were excluded; ANA, ANCA, lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin, and anti–β2‐glycoprotein antibodies were negative. The patient also demonstrated normal vWF activity, platelet count, and PT/INR.

Imaging revealed hemorrhagic fluid collections in the bilateral kidneys. At this time, urology deferred intervention due to bleeding risk. The patient was transferred to a tertiary care hospital to provide aPCC, which was initiated on HD3 at a dose of 7,000 U twice daily. Packed RBC transfusion was given for persistent anemia with a nadir of 6.9 g/dL. On HD6, prednisone was initiated at 60 mg daily, and a dose of rituximab at 375 mg/m2 was given. Continued monitoring of aPTT remained in the 80s–130s, with FVIII activity ranging 1%–8%. During this time, the patient continued to have hematuria, apparently refractory to aPCC. Following a final dose of aPCC on HD9, emicizumab therapy was initiated on HD10 at 3 mg/kg as a hemostatic agent. Two days later, the patient's hematuria resolved, and her hemoglobin/hematocrit remained stable, suggesting improvement with the use of emicizumab. Subsequently, the chromogenic FVIII assay was used to monitor the patient's response. Management continued with weekly emicizumab and rituximab (offset by three days) and monitoring of FVIII activity. Urology proceeded with cystoscopy and ureteral stenting on HD12.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

The patient recovered, her labs normalized, and she was discharged home on HD28, at which point emicizumab and rituximab were discontinued. She continued prednisone with a slow taper over several weeks and continued outpatient care with sustained resolution of coagulopathy and bleeding symptoms.

Discussion

This case report highlights the complex nature of diagnosing and managing acquired hemophilia A (AHA). The symptomatic presentation of AHA may mimic other conditions (in this case, joint swelling resembling cellulitis), leading to potential misdiagnosis or mismanagement. In our patient, only after gross hematuria developed was a coagulopathy identified, and an extensive panel of assays was necessary to establish AHA as the diagnosis over alternative bleeding conditions. Once AHA was confirmed by severely reduced FVIII activity and high FVIII inhibitor titer, bleeding was successfully controlled with specialized blood products, first with aPCC and later with emicizumab, and autoimmunity was managed with short‐term immunosuppression.

Our patient had psoriasis, an autoimmune condition that has been associated with increased risk of AHA [34]. Autoimmune diseases and advanced age are known to predispose individuals to AHA, thought to be related to immune dysregulation and increased development of autoantibodies [2, 4]. In psoriasis, proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF‐α, IL‐17, and IL‐23 contribute synergistically to chronic inflammation, which may increase the risk of autoantibody formation [35]. Psoriasis is primarily driven by Th17‐cell‐mediated inflammation, whereas AHA involves B‐cell‐mediated autoantibodies, implicating distinct but potentially interacting pathways of immune dysfunction [36, 37]. While psoriasis and age‐related immune changes may have contributed to this patient's condition, the potential interplay between her autoimmune disease and immunomodulatory treatments further complicates the etiology.

Beyond psoriasis, our patient had recent exposure to several medications that could have triggered AHA. In the preceding months, she had been receiving biologic immunotherapy for psoriasis, specifically anti‐IL‐17A monoclonal antibody ixekizumab, including a dose of ixekizumab in the week leading up to her second admission. The IL‐17A inhibitor secukinumab has been associated with various hematologic autoimmune effects, including thrombocytopenia and Henoch‐Schönlein purpura; other biologics, including anti‐CD52 and anti‐TNF‐α agents, have been implicated in AHA [17, 31, 32, 33]. To our knowledge, an association between AHA and ixekizumab is previously unreported [17]. Nevertheless, given the possibility of this patient's immunotherapeutic treatment as a potential trigger or facilitator alongside the antibiotics given, we therefore recommended avoiding repeated exposure to these agents for our patient.

It remains uncertain whether the AHA in this patient was caused by a single trigger or resulted from a multifactorial process. A combination of factors, including her underlying autoimmune condition, advanced age, and multiple medication exposures, may have collectively resulted in immune dysregulation that predisposed her to FVIII autoantibody development. No advanced testing was performed to confirm cross‐reactivity or pinpoint a specific trigger of this patient's AHA, owing largely to technical complexity and cost limitations, leaving a broad etiologic differential; in the setting of polypharmacy, it is nigh impossible to identify a single causative agent [18]. Ultimately, this case underscores the challenges of identifying causative factors in AHA, particularly in patients with multiple risk factors and exposures.

Conclusion

We hope this case report may serve as an important example of how AHA is recognized and managed in clinical practice. Further, we highlight the potential interaction of immunomodulatory biologic therapies in the pathogenesis of autoimmune hematologic disorders. Given the expanding use of biologic immunotherapies, further research is needed to clarify their potential role in AHA pathogenesis. Clinicians should remain vigilant when prescribing these agents and consider monitoring for hematologic disorders when complications arise. While no single trigger was confirmed in this case, we emphasize the broader challenge of attributing AHA to a specific medication, especially in the context of polypharmacy.
