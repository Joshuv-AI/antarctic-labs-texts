The generic Mott transition in the sine-Gordon model through an embedded worm algorithm
Oscar Bouverot-Dupuis, Laura Foini, Alberto Rosso

Abstract
The generic Mott transition in one-dimensional quantum systems can be described by the sine-Gordon model with a tilt via bosonization. Because the configuration space of the sine-Gordon model separates into distinct topological sectors, standard local Monte Carlo schemes are limited to very small system sizes. To overcome this limitation, we introduce the smooth worm (SmoWo) Monte Carlo algorithm which enlarges the configuration space to allow smooth transitions between topological sectors. The method combines worm updates with event-chain Monte Carlo moves. We explicitly prove its validity and quantify its performance. Thanks to the substantial acceleration achieved by the SmoWo algorithm, we are able to simulate large system sizes, providing a precise picture of the different phases and critical behaviour of the sine-Gordon model.



I
Introduction

Over the past decades, the Mott transition
Mott 1968
;
Fisher et al. 1989
;
Giamarchi 1997
;
Mott 2004
describing metal-insulator transitions driven by strong electronic interactions has attracted a great deal of interest. While in dimensions greater than one, a comprehensive theoretical framework remains elusive despite significant numerical efforts
Georges et al. 1996
;
Imada et al. 1998
, the one-dimensional (1D) case has seen major advances. Numerically, the Bose Hubbard model has become a paradigmatic model to observe the Mott transition with quantum Monte Carlo algorithms
Batrouni et al. 1990
and the density matrix renormalization group (DMRG)
Kühner et al. 2000
. Analytically, apart from strong-coupling expansions of the Bose Hubbard model
Elstner and Monien 1999
, most results rely on the description of the transition through the sine–Gordon model via bosonization
Haldane 1981a
;
Giamarchi 2004
;
von Delft and Schoeller 1998
;
Cazalilla 2004
;
Dupuis 2026
. It was thus established that 1D Mott transitions fall into two distinct universality classes: varying the interaction strength at fixed commensurate densities leads to a Berezinskii–Kosterlitz–Thouless (BKT) transition, while doping the system gives rise to the
generic
(or Mott-
\delta
) transition. The latter is also well-known for describing commensurate-incommensurate transitions in uniaxial surface structures
Pokrovsky and Talapov 1979
;
Schulz 1980
;
Haldane et al. 1983
and related systems
Pokrovsky and Virosztek 1983
;
Radzihovsky 2006
. Even within the sine-Gordon picture, however, its analysis proved non-trivial as a complete picture of the transition only came through the combination of many integrability
Yamamoto 1983
;
Haldane et al. 1983
;
Papa and Tsvelik 2001
and field-theoretical techniques
Schulz 1980
;
Giamarchi 1997
;
Horovitz et al. 1983
;
Aristov and Luther 2002
.

In this work, we propose a numerical study of the 1D generic Mott transition. Rather than simulating a microscopic quantum Hamiltonian as is done in quantum Monte Carlo studies, we focus on the bosonized picture provided by the sine-Gordon model. This enables direct comparison with analytical results on the same model and serves as a starting point for Monte Carlo simulations of more complicated bosonized systems. We work in the grand-canonical ensemble, thereby complementing the canonical-ensemble algorithm of Ref.
Bouverot-Dupuis et al. 2025
. In this setting, a Monte Carlo simulation of the sine-Gordon model must sample its different topological sectors. Since these sectors are widely separated in configuration space, standard Monte Carlo schemes are limited to very small system sizes. To overcome this limitation, we introduce the smooth worm algorithm (SmoWo) which operates in an enlarged configuration space where topological sectors are smoothly connected. The algorithm combines updates from the worm algorithm
Prokof’ev et al. 1998
;
Prokof’ev and Svistunov 2001
with local moves of the event-chain Monte Carlo (ECMC) scheme
Michel et al. 2014
;
Krauth 2021
. The worm algorithm is designed to sample loop models and has already proven effective to study Mott transitions in coarse-grained
(2+1)
D models
Alet and Sørensen 2003a
;
Alet and Sørensen 2003b
;
Alet and Sørensen 2004
;
Prokof’ev and Svistunov 2004
. In the SmoWo algorithm, the worm updates are further accelerated by the ECMC which performs persistent and non-reversible moves to smooth the worm throughout its construction.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
II
reviews the mapping of 1D systems onto the sine-Gordon model via bosonization, and then describes various lattice representations of the model. Section
III
introduces the SmoWo algorithm and evaluates its performance. Section
IV
defines the observables of interest and outlines their computation within the SmoWo algorithm. Section
V
presents large-scale numerical results, and Section
VI
concludes. Additional details on bosonization, a proof of the algorithm’s validity and implementation details can be found in Appendices
A
-
C
.

II
Model

We wish to study the generic Mott transition in 1D quantum systems. A system that exhibits such a transition is the following tight-binding model for spinless fermions hopping on
N
sites with lattice spacing
a
,

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\rm fermions}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}-t\left(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j+1}\hat{c}_{j}+\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}\hat{c}_{j+1}\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle V\left(\hat{n}_{j}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(\hat{n}_{j+1}-\frac{1}{2}\right)-\mu\hat{n}_{j},

(1)

where
\mu
is the chemical potential,
t
the exchange integral and
V
the amplitude of the nearest-neighbour interaction. This Hamiltonian can also describe an XXZ spin chain in a constant magnetic field
h=\mu
as the Jordan-Wigner transformation maps it onto

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\rm spin}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}

\displaystyle-2t\left(\hat{S}_{j}^{x}\hat{S}_{j+1}^{x}+\hat{S}_{j}^{y}\hat{S}_{j+1}^{y}\right)

\displaystyle+V\hat{S}_{j}^{z}\hat{S}_{j+1}^{z}-\mu\hat{S}_{j}^{z}.

(2)

Intuitively, particles have been replaced by up spins and holes by down spins.

II.1
Bosonization

At low energies, these models can be studied by bosonization around the half-filled density
\rho_{0}=1/2a

Giamarchi 2004
;
von Delft and Schoeller 1998
;
Gogolin et al. 2004
;
Dupuis 2026
. This boils down to working with two bosonic fields
\hat{\theta}(x)
and
\hat{\phi}(x)
which satisfy the commutation relation
[\hat{\theta}(x),\hat{\phi}(y)]=-i\frac{\pi}{2}{\rm sgn}(x-y)
. In terms of fermions, the former identifies with the fermion phase while the latter is related to the density fluctuations,

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{j}

\displaystyle\sim\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2}j-\hat{\phi}(x)\right)e^{i\hat{\theta}(x)},

(3)

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{n}_{j}}{a}

\displaystyle=\rho_{0}-\frac{1}{\pi}\nabla\hat{\phi}(x)+\frac{(-1)^{j}}{a\pi^{2}}\cos(2\hat{\phi}(x))\equiv\hat{\rho}(x).

(4)

with
x=ja
. In the language of spins,
e^{i\hat{\theta}(x)}
becomes roughly the spin ladder operator
\hat{S}^{-}_{j}
and the field
\phi
describes the fluctuations of the operator
\hat{S}^{z}_{j}
. Retaining only the slowly varying component of the density,
\hat{\rho}=\rho_{0}-\frac{1}{\pi}\nabla\hat{\phi}
, it appears that adding a particle on top of the background density
\rho_{0}
, i.e. a contribution of
1
to
\int{\rm d}x(\hat{\rho}(x)-\rho_{0})
, is represented by a downward kink of amplitude
\pi
(see Fig.
1
, top) since

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{L}{\rm d}x(\hat{\rho}(x)-\rho_{0})=\frac{\hat{\phi}(0)-\hat{\phi}(L)}{\pi}.

(5)

Because the lattice is half filled at
\rho_{0}
, one can also have half kinks of amplitude
-\pi/2
corresponding to two neighbouring particles (see Fig.
1
, middle). In general, full particles are expected to spontaneously decay into two half particles which possess a higher entropy. Similarly, additional holes are represented as kinks of height
+\pi
and can decay into two half-kinks of height
+\pi/2
(see Fig.
1
, bottom). In the language of spins, full excitations are called magnons while half excitations are spinons
Giamarchi 2004
.

Figure 1:
Top: In the bosonized picture, particles (or up spins) sitting above the density

. Bottom: Due to their larger entropy, half-excitations are expected to be much more frequent than full-excitations.

Using this mapping, the Hamiltonians (
II
,
II
) lead to the bosonized Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{H}=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{L}{\rm d}x\frac{u}{2\pi}\left[K(\nabla\hat{\theta})^{2}+\frac{1}{K}(\nabla\hat{\phi})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle-g\cos(4\hat{\phi})+\frac{\mu}{\pi}\nabla\hat{\phi},

(6)

where the sound velocity
u
, the Luttinger parameter
K
and the Umklapp strength
g
can be related to the microscopic couplings through the Bethe ansatz equations
Haldane 1980
. For our purposes, it is sufficient to know that
g\sim V
. In the path-integral formalism, integrating out the field
\theta
shows that the grand-canonical equilibrium partition function
Z={\rm Tr}\,e^{-\beta\hat{H}}
at inverse temperature
\beta=1/T
can be expressed as

Z=\sum_{N_{x},N_{\tau}=-\infty}^{+\infty}\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\phi(0,\tau)=\phi(L,\tau)+\pi N_{x}\\
\phi(x,0)=\phi(x,\beta)+\pi N_{\tau}\end{subarray}}{\int\mathcal{D}\phi}\,e^{-S[\phi]},

(7)

where the Euclidean action is the sine-Gordon action

\displaystyle S[\phi]=

\displaystyle\int_{x,\tau}\frac{1}{2\pi K}\left[u(\partial_{x}\phi)^{2}+\frac{1}{u}(\partial_{\tau}\phi)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle-g\cos(4\phi)+\frac{\mu}{\pi}\partial_{x}\phi,

(8)

with the notation
\int_{x,\tau}=\int_{0}^{L}{\rm d}x\int_{0}^{\beta}{\rm d}\tau
.
The boundary conditions in the path integral (
7
) are crucial to recover the correct physics. Indeed,
N_{x}
identifies with the number of downwards kinks of amplitude
\pi
encountered from
x=0
to
x=L
(see Fig.
2
). In other words,
N_{x}
is the total number of particles added to the half-filled system. The quantization of
N_{\tau}
is due to the fact that the field
\phi
and
\phi+\pi
encode the same physical state. A more formal bosonization-intensive derivation of the boundary conditions can be found in Appendix.
A
.

N_{x}
counts the number of full particles, and
N_{\tau}
encodes the average (imaginary-time) particle current
j(x,\tau)=\frac{1}{\pi}\partial_{\tau}\phi(x,\tau)
. The quantization of
N_{\tau}
can then be seen as arising from the indistinguishability of quantum particles (kinks). Indeed, the trace in
Z={\rm Tr}\,e^{-\beta\hat{H}}
ensures the particles return to their original position up to a permutation.

II.2
Lattice representations

The field theory defined by Eqs. (
7
,
II.1
) must be regulated by a short-distance cut-off to make sense. For numerical simulations, a convenient regulation is to put the field theory on a lattice with unit spacing in space and time. This means that the continuous field
\phi(x,\tau)
is replaced by its discretized version
\phi_{i}
such that, with
\hat{x}
and
\hat{\tau}
the unit vectors in the space and time directions,

\phi_{i=i_{x}\hat{x}+i_{\tau}\hat{\tau}}\equiv\phi(x=i_{x},\tau=i_{\tau}),

(9)

where
i\in\llbracket 1,L\rrbracket\times\llbracket 1,\beta\rrbracket
and
L
,
\beta
are now integers. The boundary conditions are
\phi_{i+L\hat{x}}=\phi_{i}-\pi N_{x}
and
\phi_{i+\beta\hat{\tau}}=\phi_{i}-\pi N_{\tau}
, and the action (
II.1
) becomes

\displaystyle S(\phi)\equiv\sum_{i}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi K}\left[(\phi_{i}-\phi_{i+\hat{x}})^{2}+(\phi_{i}-\phi_{i+\hat{\tau}})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle-g\cos(4\phi_{i})+\frac{\mu}{\pi}(\phi_{i+\hat{x}}-\phi_{i}),

(10)

where we have set
u=1
for simplicity. This is the
bosonic
representation of the lattice field theory.

Splitting the field
\phi
into its winding numbers
N_{x}
,
N_{\tau}
and a periodic field
\varphi_{i}=\phi_{i}+\pi N_{x}\frac{i_{x}}{L}+\pi N_{\tau}\frac{i_{\tau}}{\beta}
yields the
winding
representation of the action

\displaystyle S(N_{x},

\displaystyle N_{\tau},\varphi)\equiv\frac{\pi\beta L}{2K}\left[\left(\frac{N_{x}}{L}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{N_{\tau}}{\beta}\right)^{2}\right]-\mu\beta N_{x}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i}\frac{1}{2\pi K}\left[(\varphi_{i}-\varphi_{i+\hat{x}})^{2}+(\varphi_{i}-\varphi_{i+\hat{\tau}})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle-g\cos\left(4\varphi_{i}-4\pi N_{x}\frac{i_{x}}{L}-4\pi N_{\tau}\frac{i_{\tau}}{\beta}\right),

(11)

which shows that the windings and
\varphi
are only coupled through the cosine term. This winding representation is mainly exploited in Section.
IV
to discuss the physics of the model.

II.3
Current-fluctuation representation

We now present a
current-fluctuation
representation of the field theory in terms of loops formed by the coarse grained particle current, and small fluctuations of the density. The particle current is obtained by drawing the topographic lines (or contour lines) of the field
\phi
(see Fig.
3
). This representation will serve as the starting point of the Monte Carlo algorithm developed in Section
III
.

To be more precise, we first make apparent the discrete height map embedded in the field
\phi
by writing

\displaystyle\phi_{i}\equiv\frac{\pi}{2}(n_{i}+f_{i}),

(12)

with
n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
the discrete height field, and
f_{i}\in]-1/2,1/2]
the fluctuation field. The field
f_{i}
is periodic in all directions while the height map
n_{i}
obeys the boundary conditions
n_{i+L\hat{x}}=n_{i}-2N_{x}
and
n_{i+\beta\hat{\tau}}=n_{i}-2N_{\tau}
. With these variables, the action (
II.2
) becomes

\displaystyle S(n,f)=\sum_{i}

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{8K}\Big[(n_{i}-n_{i+\hat{x}}+f_{i}-f_{i+\hat{x}})^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+(n_{i}-n_{i+\hat{\tau}}+f_{i}-f_{i+\hat{\tau}})^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle-g\cos(2\pi f_{i})+\frac{\mu}{2}(n_{i+\hat{x}}-n_{i}).

(13)

Figure 4:
The fields
\phi_{i}
,
n_{i}
,
f_{i}
are all defined on the 2D lattice drawn in dashed lines. Its sites are labelled by
i
and its plaquettes by
p
. The current field
\vec{J}_{p}
(in solid lines) introduced in (
14
) lives on the edges of the dual lattice.

We now introduce the two-component current field
\vec{J}_{p}=(J^{x}_{p},J^{\tau}_{p})
. It connects plaquettes
p
of the lattice — hence the subscripts in
J_{p}^{x/\tau}
— and thus lives on the edges of the dual lattice (see Fig.
4
). It is defined by

\displaystyle J^{x}_{p}\equiv n_{i(p)+\hat{\tau}}-n_{i(p)},\quad J^{\tau}_{p}\equiv n_{i(p)}-n_{i(p)+\hat{x}},

(14)

where
i(p)=p-(\hat{x}+\hat{\tau})/2
connects the coordinates of the two lattices. From the previous definition, it follows that the current field is periodic in all directions and also divergenceless since

\displaystyle(\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{J})_{p}\equiv J^{x}_{p+\hat{x}}-J^{x}_{p}+J^{\tau}_{p+\hat{\tau}}-J^{\tau}_{p}=0,

(15)

which implies that the current is conserved and thus forms loops. Using this mapping, one arrives at the
current-fluctuation
representation of the action

\displaystyle S(\vec{J},f)\equiv\sum_{p}

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{8K}\Big[(J^{\tau}_{p}+f_{i(p)}-f_{i(p)+\hat{x}})^{2}

\displaystyle+(J^{x}_{p}+f_{i(p)+\hat{\tau}}-f_{i(p)})^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle-g\cos(2\pi f_{i(p)})-\frac{\mu}{2}J^{\tau}_{p}.

(16)

Note that if one neglects the fluctuations by setting
f_{i}=0
, one recovers a
(1+1)
D version of the
(2+1)
D link-current representation of the quantum rotor model
Wallin et al. 1994
. The link-current representation is obtained from the Bose-Hubbard model by using a Villain approximation and integrating out the phase fluctuations, while the current-fluctuation representation we propose relies on bosonization to decouple the density and the phase modes, before integrating out the phase fluctuations. Denoting by
\mathcal{C}_{0}
the set of divergenceless current fields, the partition function is

\displaystyle Z=\sum_{\vec{J}\in\mathcal{C}_{0}}\prod_{i}\int_{-\frac{1}{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\rm d}f_{i}\,e^{-S(\vec{J},f)}.

(17)

Since the bosonic field
\phi
has the same physical meaning as
\phi+\pi
, the mapping from
\phi
to
(\vec{J},f)
can be inverted by first requiring that
n_{i=0}=0
, then using Eq. (
14
) to recover the discrete height field
n_{i}
, and finally using Eq. (
12
) to get
\phi_{i}
.

III
Monte-Carlo algorithm

III.1
The need for an efficient sampling algorithm

A standard Monte Carlo algorithm to sample the probability distribution
\pi(\phi)\equiv e^{-S(\phi)}/Z
is the Metropolis–Hastings algorithm
Metropolis et al. 1953
where one proposes an update
\phi\to\phi^{\prime}
and accepts it with probability

p_{\rm Met.}=\exp(-[S(\phi^{\prime})-S(\phi)]_{+}),

(18)

with
[x]_{+}=\max(0,x)
. In order to be ergodic, the algorithm has to propose updates which change the topological numbers
N_{x}
,
N_{\tau}
. A possible straightforward update is to change
N_{x}\to N_{x}+1
(resp.
N_{\tau}\to N_{\tau}+1
) while keeping
\phi
constant, which amounts to creating a kink of amplitude
\pi
at the boundary
x=L
(resp.
\tau=\beta
). This implies an increase of the quadratic part of the action at the boundary of the order of
\beta\pi/2K
(resp.
L\pi/2K
). For
K=0.35
and
\mu\in[0,1]
(which we use in all our following simulations), the variation of the total action is dominated by the quadratic contribution, so one finds
p_{\rm Met.}\simeq\exp(-\pi/(2\times 0.35))^{\beta}=(1.12\times 10^{-2})^{\beta}
(and similarly for
\beta\leftrightarrow L
). This is a ridiculously small acceptance rate which, along with its exponential scaling, should discourage anyone from trying to attempt large system-size simulations with the Metropolis–Hastings algorithm. We also expect cluster algorithms
Swendsen and Wang 1987
;
Wolff 1989
to be very inefficient for sampling the sine-Gordon model. Indeed, cluster algorithms used for bosonized systems
Hasenbusch et al. 1994
;
Werner and Troyer 2005
;
Bouverot-Dupuis et al. 2025
cannot change the boundary conditions, and, furthermore, crucially rely on the particle-hole symmetry
\phi\to-\phi
which is broken by the chemical potential
\mu
.

To overcome this issue, we propose a
Worm algorithm
(Wo) and its enhanced version, the
Smooth Worm algorithm
(SmoWo), which perform two types of updates:

•

local
\phi
updates at constant
N_{x}
and
N_{\tau}
using the Event-Chain Monte Carlo (ECMC) algorithm,

•

N_{x}
and
N_{\tau}
updates using the worm algorithm, enhanced with ECMC moves for the SmoWo algorithm.

In a nutshell, ECMC algorithms
Michel et al. 2014
;
Krauth 2021
are a class of continuous-time and rejection-free algorithms that are non-reversible as they do not satisfy detailed balance but only the weaker global balance.

They were first introduced as a non-reversible extension of the Metropolis algorithm, and formally realise a piecewise deterministic Markov process
Monemvassitis et al. 2023
. They can be used for any system with continuous degrees of freedom and have been shown to perform far better than classical reversible schemes such as the Metropolis–Hastings algorithm in various contexts
Nishikawa et al. 2015
;
Michel et al. 2015
;
Klement et al. 2021
;
Bouverot-Dupuis et al. 2025
. The worm algorithm
Prokof’ev et al. 1998
;
Prokof’ev and Svistunov 2001
can be applied to any model whose configurations are made of loops, which for the sine-Gordon model we have identified in the current-fluctuation representation (
II.3
). By allowing intermediate configurations to have one open path (the “worm”), the worm algorithm drastically reduces the critical slowing down observed near phase transitions.

In the following, we first extend the
current-fluctuation
representation (
II.3
) to include a worm. We then describe the algorithms in detail and test their performance. Technical details such as a proof that both algorithms satisfy the global balance condition can be found in Appendix.
B
.

III.2
Extended current-fluctuation representation

To implement any worm algorithm, we must extend the loops formed by the current field
\vec{J}
in the current-fluctuation representation (
II.3
) by allowing for an open path called the worm. However, instead of attaching a single current line between the worm’s head and tail, we attach two lines (see Fig.
5
). This will enable the computation of the phase-phase correlation function in Sec.
IV.3
. Calling
\mathcal{C}_{2}(p_{t},p_{h})
the set of divergenceless current fields except at
p_{t}
(the worm’s tail) where
(\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{J})_{p_{t}}=2
, and at
p_{h}
(the worm’s head) where
(\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{J})_{p_{h}}=-2
, we therefore define the extended model by its partition function

\displaystyle Z_{\rm w}=\sum_{p_{t},p_{h}}\sum_{\vec{J}\in\mathcal{C}_{2}(p_{t},p_{h})}\prod_{i}\int_{-\frac{1}{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\rm d}f_{i}\,e^{-S(\vec{J},f)}.

(19)

In terms of this extended partition function, the physical partition function
Z
reads

Z=Z_{\rm w}\langle\delta_{p_{t},0}\delta_{p_{h},0}\rangle_{\rm w}=\frac{Z_{\rm w}}{\beta L}\langle\delta_{p_{t},p_{h}}\rangle_{\rm w},

(20)

where
\langle\bullet\rangle_{\rm w}
is the average with respect to the model defined in (
19
) and the second equality comes from the translational invariance of the system.

III.3
Worm algorithm (Wo)

The Wo algorithm consists of two types of moves: the ECMC moves, and the worm moves.

ECMC moves.

We first implement some ECMC moves to deal with the fluctuations
f
. To study the configuration space
\Omega=\{(\vec{J},f)\}
, an ECMC algorithm works in the augmented space
\Omega\times V
with
V=\{(i\in\llbracket 1,L\rrbracket\times\llbracket 1,\beta\rrbracket,e\in\{-1,1\})\}
. The elements
v=(i,e)\in V
are called
lifting
variables. As long as
v=(i,e)
is not updated, we continuously increase
f_{i}
if
e=+1
and decrease it if
e=-1
. The exact form of this deterministic motion is

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f_{j}(t)=\begin{cases}e\text{ if }j=i,\\
0\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

(21)

where
t
is the time of the Markov process. In principle, when
f_{i}
reaches
\pm 1/2
, its neighbouring currents should be updated so as to continue the motion from
f_{i}=\mp 1/2
and keep
f_{i}\in[-1/2,1/2]
. However, for the sake of simplicity, we decide not to do so since it does not affect the reconstructed field
\phi(\vec{J},f)
which caries the physical information. The evolution (
21
) proceeds until a random event updates
v\to v^{\prime}
, and then resumes with
v^{\prime}
. As is usually done in ECMC algorithms, we associate an event with each interaction in the model. In the current-fluctuation representation (
II.3
), we identify quadratic interactions like

S_{\rm q}^{i,i+\hat{x}}=\frac{\pi}{8K}(J^{\tau}_{p(i)}+f_{i}-f_{i+\hat{x}})^{2}
, and cosine interactions
S_{\rm c}^{i}=-g\cos(2\pi f_{i})
. The lifting variable
v=(i,e)
may trigger 4 possible quadratic events stemming from the interactions
S_{\rm q}^{i,j}
with
j=i\pm\hat{x},i\pm\hat{\tau}
and one cosine event linked to
S_{\rm c}^{i}
. The associated time-dependent event rates are

\displaystyle\lambda^{i,j}_{\rm q}(t)

\displaystyle=[e\partial_{f_{i}(t)}S_{\rm q}^{i,j}(t)]_{+},

(22)

\displaystyle\lambda^{i}_{\rm c}(t)

\displaystyle=[e\partial_{f_{i}(t)}S_{\rm c}^{i}(t)]_{+}.

(23)

Intuitively, these events are triggered when the deterministic motion stores too much energy (or action) in an interaction. Using the pair-wise symmetries of the interactions, an energy excess in
S_{\rm q}^{i,j}
obtained from
v=(i,e)
can be released by setting
v^{\prime}=(j,e)
. Similarly, an excess in
S_{\rm c}^{i}
with
v=(i,e)
can be mitigated by setting
v^{\prime}=(i,-e)
to undo the previous move. We therefore decide that the rate
\lambda_{\rm q}^{i,j}
triggers the update
(i,e)\to(j,e)
while
\lambda_{\rm c}^{i}
triggers
(i,e)\to(i,-e)
.
To ensure ergodicity, an additional
refreshment
event is customarily added. It has a constant (i.e. configuration-independent) rate
\lambda_{\rm r}
and uniformly draws a new lifting variable
v^{\prime}\in V
. Anticipating on the worm moves presented below, we also introduce a constant rate
\lambda_{\rm w}
for triggering such a move.

In practice, the ECMC algorithm is simulated by i) finding which event occurs first and at what event time, ii) updating the fluctuation field
f
to that time using (
21
), iii) performing the update
v\to v^{\prime}
triggered by the event and iv) repeating the procedure. For the algorithm described above, there are four types of event times to compute when moving
f_{i}
along
e
: the quadratic ones
t_{\rm q}^{i,j}
, the cosine one
t_{\rm c}^{i}
, the refreshment one
t_{\rm r}
and the worm one
t_{\rm w}
. These event times can be explicitly computed using inversion sampling (see Appendix
C.1
).

Worm moves.

In the general ECMC framework introduced in the above, we have added a rate
\lambda_{\rm w}
at which worm moves occur. We now describe these moves which update the current field
\vec{J}
. We consider two types of worm updates: the
shift
update (see Fig.
6
, top) and the
move
update (see Fig.
6
, bottom). If the worm’s head has reached its tail (
p_{h}=p_{t}
), with probability
1/2
we propose a move update which moves both
p_{h}
and
p_{t}
to a new common location
p_{h}^{\prime}=p_{t}^{\prime}
uniformly chosen on the dual lattice. Otherwise, we propose a shift update which randomly selects a neighbour
p_{h}^{\prime}\in\{p_{h}\pm\hat{x},p_{h}\pm\hat{\tau}\}
and shifts
p_{h}
to
p_{h}^{\prime}
. If
p_{h}\neq p_{t}
, we always propose a shift update. The move update does not change the action of the configuration, so it is always accepted to obey detailed balance. The shift update is uniformly picked among the 4 possible directions and is accepted with the Metropolis–Hastings filter

P(p_{h}\to p_{h}^{\prime})=\min(1,Re^{-\Delta S}),

(24)

with

R=\begin{cases}2&\text{ if }p_{h}=p_{t},\\
1/2&\text{ if }p_{h}^{\prime}=p_{t},\\
1&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

(25)

where
\Delta S
is the variation of the action (
II.3
). This ends the definition of the Wo algorithm, a pseudocode implementation of which can be found in Appendix
C.2
.

Performance

To test the Wo algorithm described previously, we define the algorithmic time
t
expressed in sweeps (i.e.
\beta L
operations) as increasing by
1/(\beta L)
each time an event time or a worm acceptance rate (
24
) is computed. The algorithmic time is expected to scale as the CPU time while being less sensitive to specific code implementation details. We then consider the autocorrelation function defined for an observable
\mathcal{O}
as

\displaystyle C_{\mathcal{O}}(\texttt{t})=\frac{\langle\mathcal{O}(\texttt{t})\mathcal{O}(0)\rangle-\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle^{2}}{\langle\mathcal{O}^{2}\rangle-\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle^{2}}.

(26)

We extract from it the integrated autocorrelation time
\tau_{\rm int}^{\mathcal{O}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\texttt{t}=-\infty}^{+\infty}C_{\mathcal{O}}(\texttt{t})
which is the time needed to generate a new independent sample once the Markov process has thermalized (or mixed
Levin and Peres 2017
). To assess the critical slowing-down of the algorithm, we perform this analysis at the critical point of the generic Mott transition by scaling
\beta\sim L^{2}
since the dynamical critical exponent is
z=2
(see Sec.
V
). We also take
\lambda_{\rm w}=1
which was numerically found to be optimal (the optimal region is actually quite large, roughly
\lambda_{\rm w}\in[0.5,2]
) and take
\lambda_{\rm r}=0.1/(\beta L)
to allow ECMC moves to be correlated on the scale of the system. The results for the compressibility
\kappa
and superfluid stiffness
\rho_{s}
are shown in Fig.
7
. These observables encode the large-scale fluctuations of the field
\phi
in the space and time directions (their exact definitions can be found in Sec.
IV.2
). It appears that for both observables
\tau_{\rm int}\sim L^{z_{\rm alg.}}
with
z_{\rm alg.}\simeq 5.8
the algorithmic dynamical exponent (not to be confused with the dynamical critical exponent
z
). Although this value of
z_{\rm alg.}
is quite high, the polynomial scaling of this algorithm is a dramatic improvement over the exponential scaling of the naive Metropolis algorithm described in Sec.
III.1
. The difference between both scalings is traced back to the fact that the worm algorithm can reach many configurations and therefore select a typical one, while the rigid Metropolis–Hastings updates only propose very atypical configurations.

The Wo algorithm can nevertheless be improved by noticing that is suffers from a backtracking problem: the current worm moves want to create sharp kinks (i.e. with a narrow width) in the field
\phi
which are atypical. This leads to a high rejection rate unless the attempted worm move undoes the previous one. Such backtracking behaviour is typical of simple worm algorithms and is usually minimized by using
directed
worm algorithms
Syljuåsen and Sandvik 2002
which locally minimize the backtracking (or bounce) probability. However, for the sine-Gordon model, we found that these algorithms do not provide any significant performance gain. We also tried, with no greater success, other worm-like algorithms such as the geometrical worm algorithm
Alet and Sørensen 2003a
and its directed version
Alet and Sørensen 2003b
;
Alet and Sørensen 2004
which leverage properties of Markov chains with absorbing states
Novotny 1995
;
Bortz et al. 1975
to grow an entire worm in a single worm event. The failure of these algorithms comes from the fact that they do not tackle the ”sharp-kink” issue discussed above.

III.4
Smooth worm algorithm (SmoWo)

In order to solve the ”sharp-kink” issue, we propose the SmoWo algorithm which, compared to the Wo algorithm, performs additional ECMC moves around the worm’s head to smooth it out while it is being built. To implement these new moves, we must add a new lifting variable
\nu=(\alpha,\varepsilon)\in\mathcal{V}
(we use Greek letters for the new lifting variable and Roman letters
v=(i,e)
for the old). The site
\alpha
should always be close to the worm’s head
p_{h}
to ensure we are smoothing around it. In practice, we choose for
\alpha
to always be one of the 4 closest sites to
p_{h}
, that is to say

\alpha\in\partial p_{h}=\left\{p_{h}\pm\frac{\hat{x}}{2}\pm\frac{\hat{\tau}}{2}\right\}.

(27)

To decide which of the lifting variables is being used for the deterministic motion (
21
), we introduce the variable
\sigma
which is
0
when using
v
and
1
when using
\nu
. If
\sigma=0
, the ECMC events include the refreshment event (which only updates
v
), the quadratic and cosine events and the worm update. The only event that we modify is the worm update. It is still triggered by a constant rate
\lambda_{\rm r}
but now consists in first setting
\sigma=1
, then attempting a worm move, and finally refreshing (resampling uniformly)
\nu\in\partial p_{h}\times\{-1,1\}
only if the worm move has been accepted (to ensure that
\alpha
stays in
\partial p_{h}
). If
\sigma=1
, we define three event types: the quadratic events, the cosine events and the
\sigma
-update (there is no need for a refreshment update since
\nu
can already be refreshed during the worm moves). When a quadratic event occurs with a neighbouring site
\gamma
, the lifting variable is updated to

\displaystyle(\alpha,\varepsilon)\to\begin{cases}(\gamma,\varepsilon)&\text{ if }\gamma\in\partial p_{h},\\
(\alpha,-\varepsilon)&\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

(28)

so as to satisfy the condition
\alpha\in\partial p_{h}
. For a cosine neighbour, we always perform

\displaystyle(\alpha,\varepsilon)\to(\alpha,-\varepsilon).

(29)

Finally, the new
\sigma
-update is triggered by the constant rate
\lambda_{\rm w}
and simply sets
\sigma=0
. A proof of the validity of the algorithm and a pseudocode implementation are given respectively in Appendix
B
and
C.2
.

Performance wise, the integrated autocorrelation time of the SmoWo algorithm is given in Fig.
7
for the compressibility
\kappa
and the superfluid stiffness
\rho_{s}
. The net result is a decrease of the algorithmic dynamical critical exponent from
z_{\rm alg.}\simeq 5.8
for the Wo algorithm to
z_{\rm alg.}\simeq 4.8
for the SmoWo algorithm. As shown in the next sections, this change is crucial as the finite-size effects of the model are very large and require going to sizes
L\geq 80
to analyse the phase transition, something not possible with the Wo algorithm. The exponent
z_{\rm alg.}\simeq 4.8
— which could also be stated as
\tau_{\rm int}\sim\beta^{2.4}
since we scale
\beta\propto L^{2}
— may still seem large since one usually expects a very low exponent
z_{\rm alg.}
for worm algorithms
Prokof’ev and Svistunov 2001
. A possible explanation for the remaining critical slowing down may lie in the slow separation dynamics of the worm of height
\pi
into two kinks of height
\pi/2
. We have also tried larger smoothing areas
\partial p_{h}
with no notable performance change.

IV
Observables and physical insights

IV.1
Phase diagram

The sine-Gordon action (
II.1
) can be used to get a intuitive understanding of the Mott transition. On the one hand, the chemical potential
\mu
wants to tilt the field
\phi
and create many kinks, while on the other hand, a strong coupling
g
penalizes their creation. In the limit of negligible
g
, the winding representation (
II.2
) of the action becomes

\displaystyle S_{\rm LL}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi\beta L}{2K}\left[u\left(\frac{N_{x}}{L}-\frac{K\mu}{\pi u}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{u}\left(\frac{N_{\tau}}{\beta}\right)^{2}\right]

(30)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i}\frac{1}{2\pi K}\left[u(\varphi_{i}-\varphi_{i+\hat{x}})^{2}+\frac{1}{u}(\varphi_{i}-\varphi_{i+\hat{\tau}})^{2}\right],

where we have explicitly reintroduced the speed
u
. This shows that the topological variables
N_{x}
,
N_{\tau}
decouple from the periodic field
\varphi
. The action for
\varphi
is that of a Luttinger liquid (LL). In the opposite limit of very large
g
, all kinks are suppressed so
N_{x}=N_{\tau}=0
, and the field
\varphi
gets pinned around a minimum of
\cos(4\varphi)
, e.g.
\varphi=0
. One can thus expand the cosine to obtain

\displaystyle S_{\rm MI}=\sum_{i}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi K}\left[u(\varphi_{i}-\varphi_{i+\hat{x}})^{2}+\frac{1}{u}(\varphi_{i}-\varphi_{i+\hat{\tau}})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle+8g\varphi_{i}^{2}.

(31)

This is the Mott insulator (MI). We thus expect the existence of two critical points
\pm\mu_{c}
(see Fig.
8
) such that the system is described by the LL action (
30
) for
|\mu|>\mu_{c}
(with potentially renormalized couplings
K,u,\mu\to K_{\rm R},u_{\rm R},\mu_{\rm R}
), and by the MI action (
IV.1
) for
|\mu|<\mu_{c}
(with, again,
K,u,g\to K_{\rm R},u_{\rm R},g_{\rm R}
). In the following, we focus on the transition at
+\mu_{c}>0
without loss of generality since the action (
II.1
) is invariant under
\mu,\phi\to-\mu,-\phi
.

IV.2
Scalar observables

An order parameter for the transition is the doping
\delta\rho=\langle\hat{\rho}\rangle-\rho_{0}
(or the magnetization
m=\langle\hat{S}_{j}^{z}\rangle
for spins) which, in terms of the bosonized variables, is given by

\displaystyle\delta\rho\equiv\left\langle\frac{N_{x}}{L}\right\rangle.

(32)

Since there are no kinks in the MI,
\delta\rho
vanishes. In the LL, in the limit of
L,\beta\to\infty
, it appears from Eq. (
30
) that the doping concentrates around
\frac{K_{\rm R}\mu_{\rm R}}{\pi u_{\rm R}}
.

Another order parameter can be found by considering the rapid fluctuations of the density
(-1)^{i_{x}}\cos(2\phi_{i=i_{x}\hat{x}+i_{\tau}\hat{\tau}})
(see Eq. (
4
)). Using the periodic field
\varphi_{i}=\phi_{i}+\pi N_{x}i_{x}/L+\pi N_{\tau}i_{\tau}/\beta
, these fluctuations can be rewritten as

\cos\left(2\varphi_{i}-2\pi\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{N_{x}}{L}\right)i_{x}-2\pi\frac{N_{\tau}}{\beta}i_{\tau}\right),

(33)

and thus appear as fluctuating in space at twice the Fermi wave-vector
k_{F}\equiv\pi(\rho_{0}+\delta\rho)=\pi(1/2+N_{x}/L)
. Neglecting for simplicity
N_{\tau}
, the amplitude of this
2k_{F}
modulation is

\displaystyle C_{2k_{F}}\equiv\langle\cos(2\varphi_{i}-2\overline{\varphi})\rangle,

(34)

where we have removed the average
\overline{\varphi}=(\beta L)^{-1}\int_{x,\tau}\varphi
which just fixes the overall sign of the density modulation
1
1

1

This is analogous to what is done for the Ising model. Instead of computing the magnetization
m
, one usually considers its absolute value
|m|

Newman and Barkema 1999
.
. In the LL, one can show using the action (
30
) that
C_{2k_{F}}
should vanish. In the MI,
C_{2k_{F}}>0
since
\varphi_{i}-\bar{\varphi}
gets locked around
0
.

The Mott transition is also characterized by the compressibility
\kappa
and the superfluid stiffness
\rho_{s}
which respectively measure the response of the system to a shift in the spatial boundary conditions (i.e. a change of the density) and in the temporal boundary conditions (i.e. a change of the particle-current). In terms of the bosonic variables, these observables are defined as

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle\equiv\lim_{q\to 0}\frac{q^{2}}{\pi^{2}}\langle|\varphi(q,0)|^{2}\rangle,

(35)

\displaystyle\rho_{s}

\displaystyle\equiv\lim_{\omega_{n}\to 0}\frac{\omega_{n}^{2}}{\pi^{2}}\langle|\varphi(0,\omega_{n})|^{2}\rangle,

(36)

with
\varphi(q,\omega_{n})=(\beta L)^{-1/2}\int_{x,\tau}e^{-i(qx+\omega_{n}\tau)}\varphi(x,\tau)
(see Appendix.
A.4
for additional comments on these definitions). For finite-size systems, the limit
q\to 0
(resp.
\omega_{n}\to 0
) is approached by using the smallest possible momentum,
\frac{2\pi}{L}
(resp. frequency,
\frac{2\pi}{\beta}
). From (
30
), it is straightforward to show that
\kappa=\frac{K_{\rm R}}{\pi u_{\rm R}}
and
\rho_{s}=K_{R}u_{\rm R}/\pi
from which one can extract
K_{\rm R}=\pi\sqrt{\kappa\rho_{s}}
and
u_{\rm R}=\sqrt{\rho_{s}/\kappa}
. For the MI action (
IV.1
), one instead gets
\kappa=\rho_{s}=0
.

The last
\varphi
-dependant observable we consider is the two-point function

\displaystyle C_{\varphi}(i)\equiv\langle e^{i(\varphi_{i}-\varphi_{i=0})}\rangle,

(37)

which, from (
30

IV.1
), decays algebraically in the LL as
C_{\varphi}(i_{x}\hat{x})\sim i_{x}^{-2K_{\rm R}}
(and similarly in time), and reaches a plateau in the MI because
\varphi
gets locked around a minimum. Physically, this two-point function is akin to
\langle e^{i2(\varphi_{i}-\varphi_{i=0})}\rangle
which is the
2k_{F}
part of the density-density correlation function.

The field
\phi
can only be rebuilt from
(\vec{J},f)
when
p_{h}=p_{t}
, i.e. when there is no worm. To compute an observable
\mathcal{O}(\phi)
, we thus check at fixed time intervals
T_{\rm sample}
if
p_{h}=p_{t}
, and, if so, we output a sample
\mathcal{O}(\phi(\vec{J},f))
. The statistical average
\langle\mathcal{O}(\phi)\rangle
is then given by the sample average. A pseudocode implementation of the computation of observables can be found in Appendix
C.2

IV.3
Phase-phase two-point function

We now focus on the phase-phase two-point function
C_{\theta}(x_{h}-x_{t},\tau_{h}-\tau_{t})=\big\langle e^{i(\theta(x_{h},\tau_{h})-\theta(x_{t},\tau_{t}))}\big\rangle
. At the operator level, it is defined as

\displaystyle C_{\theta}(x_{h}-x_{t},\tau_{h}-\tau_{t})\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm Tr}\left(\hat{\mathcal{T}}e^{\tau_{h}\hat{H}}e^{i\hat{\theta}(x_{h})}e^{(\tau_{t}-\tau_{h})\hat{H}}e^{-i\hat{\theta}(x_{t})}e^{-\tau_{t}\hat{H}}e^{-\beta\hat{H}}\right)}{Z},

(38)

with
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
the (imaginary) time-ordering operator. For bosons,
\hat{c}_{j}\sim e^{i\hat{\theta}(x)}
and this two-point function corresponds to creating a particle at
(x_{t},\tau_{t})
and destroying it at
(x_{h},\tau_{h})
. Since spins can be mapped onto hardcore bosons, one similarly finds that
S^{-}_{j}\sim(-1)^{j}e^{i\theta(x)}
. However, for fermions,
\hat{c}_{j}\sim\cos(j\pi/2-\hat{\phi}(x))e^{i\hat{\theta}(x)}
, so
C_{\theta}
just takes into account the phase of the ladder operators. The extra operator
\cos(j\pi/2-\hat{\phi}(x))
ensures that fermions anti-commute. Because we have previously identified kinks as world-lines, we expect
C_{\theta}(x_{h}-x_{t},\tau_{h}-\tau_{t})
to be linked to the field configurations with a worm ranging from
(x_{t},\tau_{t})
to
(x_{h},\tau_{h})
. In the following, we make this statement more explicit, and a rigorous derivation can be found in Appendix.
A.3
.

We first establish the discretized path integral representation of Eq. (
IV.3
). The key idea to derive it is to notice that, from the relation
[\hat{\theta}(x),\hat{\phi}(y)]=-i\frac{\pi}{2}{\rm sgn}(x-y)
, one can infer

[\hat{\phi}(y),e^{\pm i\hat{\theta}(x)}]=\mp\frac{\pi}{2}{\rm sgn}(x-y)e^{\pm i\hat{\theta}(x)}.

(39)

This is interpreted as the operators
e^{\pm i\hat{\theta}(x)}
inserting a kink of height
\pm\pi
in the field
\phi
at the position
x
. Consequently, the field
\phi(y,\tau^{-})
at a time right before the operator insertion at
(x,\tau)
differs by
\mp\frac{\pi}{2}{\rm sgn}(x-y)
from the field
\phi(y,\tau^{+})
just after.

Putting the field theory on a lattice, its is convenient to think of the coordinates
(x_{h},\tau_{h})
and
(x_{t},\tau_{t})
as plaquettes
p_{h}
and
p_{t}
(see Fig.
9
). This is because, according to (
39
), the path integral representation of
C_{\theta}
is given by the usual action
S
from Eq. (
II.2
) except for the quadratic interactions crossing the insertion times
(p_{h})_{\tau}
and
(p_{t})_{\tau}
which are modified as

(\phi_{i}-\phi_{i+\hat{\tau}})^{2}\to\left(\phi_{i}-\phi_{i+\hat{\tau}}\pm\frac{\pi}{2}\right)^{2},

(40)

where the sign is explicitly given in Fig.
9
.

which gives the interaction

\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\left(n_{i}-n_{i+\hat{\tau}}\pm 1+f_{i}-f_{i+\hat{\tau}}\right)^{2}.

(41)

This suggests to replace the old current definition
J^{x}_{p}=n_{i(p)+\hat{\tau}}-n_{i(p)}
by the new one
J^{x}_{p}\equiv n_{i(p)+\hat{\tau}}-n_{i(p)}\mp 1

\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\left(J^{x}_{p}+f_{i(p)+\hat{\tau}}-f_{i(p)}\right)^{2},

(42)

which is the same as in the action (

) already obtained for the partition function. However, from the modified definition of the current, one can show that the current field
\vec{J}
is now divergenceless everywhere but at
p_{h}
and
p_{t}
where
(\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{J})_{p_{t}}=2
and
(\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{J})_{p_{h}}=-2
. The current field therefore belongs to the ensemble
\mathcal{C}_{2}(p_{t},p_{h})
of current configurations with a worm from
p_{t}
to
p_{h}
, and one concludes that

\displaystyle C_{\theta}(p_{h}-p_{t})=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{\vec{J}\in\mathcal{C}_{2}(p_{t},p_{h})}\prod_{i}\int_{-\frac{1}{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\rm d}f_{i}\,e^{-S(\vec{J},f)}}{\sum_{\vec{J}\in\mathcal{C}_{0}}\prod_{i}\int_{-\frac{1}{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\rm d}f_{i}\,e^{-S(\vec{J},f)}},

(43)

where the denominator is just the partition function
Z
. In terms of the average
\langle\bullet\rangle_{\rm tot.}
with respect to the total probability distribution in the enlarged space containing the worm and the lifting variables (see also Eq. (
102

\displaystyle C_{\theta}(p)=

\displaystyle\frac{\langle\delta_{p_{h}-p_{t},p}\rangle_{\rm tot.}}{\langle\delta_{p_{h}-p_{t},0}\rangle_{\rm tot.}}.

(44)

This means that
C_{\theta}(p)
is simply proportional to the time that the worm head spends at a distance
p
from its tail and is normalized to give
C_{\theta}(0)=1
. The fact that the worm algorithm easily gives access to two-point functions is a general feature shared by many physical systems

IV.4
Scaling analysis

A critical point being scale invariant, it is believed that observables should display a universal scaling behaviour in its neighbouring
Dupuis 2026
. This so-called scaling hypothesis is not only useful for understanding the universal behaviour of observables, but is also a precise tool to analyse Monte Carlo data near criticality.

We now briefly recall the results of applying the scaling hypothesis to the generic Mott transition
Fisher et al. 1989
;
Wallin et al. 1994
. The relevant physical length and time scales involved in our model are: the system size
L
and inverse temperature
\beta
, the space and time correlation lengths
\xi
and
\xi_{\tau}
, and the microscopic length of the lattice spacing. At a distance
\delta=\mu-\mu_{c}
of the critical point, the correlation lengths are expected to diverge as

\displaystyle\xi\sim|\delta|^{-\nu},\quad\xi_{\tau}\sim|\delta|^{-\nu z},

(45)

which defines the two critical exponents
\nu
and
z
. The scaling hypothesis then consists in arguing that near the scale invariant critical point, the short distance details should be irrelevant and observables should only depend on
L
,
\beta
,
\xi
and
\xi_{\tau}
. Therefore, an observable
\mathcal{O}
with dimension
({\rm length})^{-[\mathcal{O}]_{x}}\times({\rm time})^{-[\mathcal{O}]_{\tau}}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(L,\beta,\xi,\xi_{\tau})=L^{-[\mathcal{O}]_{x}}\beta^{-[\mathcal{O}]_{\tau}}\mathcal{\tilde{O}}\left(\frac{\xi}{L},\frac{\xi_{\tau}}{\beta}\right),

(46)

which is usually written in the more convenient form

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(L,\beta,\delta)=L^{-[\mathcal{O}]}\mathcal{\tilde{O}}\left(\delta L^{1/\nu},\frac{\beta}{L^{z}}\right),

(47)

where
[\mathcal{O}]=[\mathcal{O}]_{x}+z[\mathcal{O}]_{\tau}
is the scaling dimension of
\mathcal{O}
, and
\mathcal{\tilde{O}}
is known as a scaling function. Note that
[\mathcal{O}]

To understand what happens at the transition, it is useful to define a correlation length
\xi
. In the LL, it is the kink-to-kink distance
\xi=1/(2\delta\rho)
(the factor
2
appears because a kink is a half-particle). As for any correlation length, deep in the LL (
\mu\gg\mu_{c}
)
\xi\to 0
since the kinks proliferate (
\delta\rho\to\infty
), and close to the transition
\xi\to\infty
as kinks become scarce (
\delta\rho\to 0
). It separates small scales at which the field seems flat, and thus looks like a MI, from large ones at which the field has many kinks and appears to be a LL. This crossover is particularly visible in the two-point function
C_{\varphi}
(see Fig.
12
). Below
\xi
,
C_{\varphi}
tends to be flat. Above
\xi
,
C_{\varphi}
displays the typical algebraic decay of a Luttinger liquid. Notice that along the space direction,
C_{\varphi}(x,0)
displays some clear bumps at
x=\xi,2\xi,3\xi,\cdots
due to the presence of kinks. In the MI, all curves are flat and collapse. The absence of any singular behaviour in
C_{\varphi}
(or any of the previous observables) as
\mu\to\mu_{c}^{-}
indicates that there is no diverging correlation length when coming from the MI and that, right at the critical point, the system is still a MI.

By only considering
\phi
-dependent observables, we have considered the physics of the sine-Gordon model rather than that of the generic Mott transition which also includes
\theta
-dependent observables. The previous analysis therefore has consequences for a renormalization group (RG) study of the sine-Gordon model. It appears that the sine-Gordon model has an RG fixed point corresponding to the MI and a continuum of LL fixed points (one for each value of
(u_{\rm R},K_{\rm R})
), but no intermediate critical fixed point separating both phases. Note that, strictly speaking, one has to distinguish the LL with
\delta\rho\neq 0
(which we are studying here) from that with
\delta\rho=0
as the free-energy is non-analytic at
\delta\rho\to 0
and signals the presence of a
n^{\rm th}
-order phase transition with

(see
Horovitz et al. 1983
). Neglecting for simplicity the transitions to the LL at
\delta\rho=0
, we are lead to infer the schematic RG flow depicted in Fig.

\mu\to\mu_{c}^{+}
are first attracted to the MI, before heading towards the LL, in agreement with the fact that they represent systems whose short-distance physics looks like that of a MI.

V.2
The phase-phase two-point function

We now consider the phase-phase two-point function
C_{\theta}
of 1D quantum systems. We solely concentrate on the time behaviour
C_{\theta}(0,\tau)
. The analysis of the full space-time dependency is more intricate and will be reported elsewhere. Since the system is not particle-hole symmetric for
\mu\neq\mu_{c}
,
C_{\theta}(0,\tau)
, which describes the phase of a particle, need not be equal to
C_{\theta}(0,\beta-\tau)
, which describes that of a hole. In Fig.
15
, left, we have plotted
C_{\theta}(0,\tau)
across the transition. For
\mu>\mu_{c}
, we observe an algebraic decay
C_{\theta}(0,\tau)\sim\tau^{-1/(2K_{\rm R})}
typical of a Luttinger liquid. Contrary to
C_{\varphi}
, this algebraic decay is still present at the critical point, indicating that
e^{i\theta}
is a scaling field whereas
e^{i\varphi}
is not. At the critical point (
\mu=0.325\simeq\mu_{c}
), fitting the algebraic time decay (for
\tau
close to
0
, not
\beta
) gives
C_{\theta}(0,\tau)\sim 1/\tau^{1.997\pm 0.008}
. Assuming that
z=2
from the previous section and using the scaling ansatz (
52
) specialized to
\delta=0
, this gives an estimate of the anomalous dimension

\eta=2.99\pm 0.02.

(54)

It is often stated that, at the critical point, the Luttinger parameter takes on the value
K_{\rm R}=1/4

Schulz 1980
;
Giamarchi 1997
;
Kühner et al. 2000
. Since the dynamical exponent is
z=2
at the transition, the critical point is evidently not a Luttinger liquid which has
z=1
. However, approaching it from the LL,
K_{\rm R}
seems to converge towards
1/4
(see Fig.
11
) and, right at the transition, the previous observation that
C_{\theta}(0,\tau)\sim\tau^{-2}
indeed corresponds to the scaling
C_{\theta}(0,\tau)\sim\tau^{-1/(2K_{\rm R})}
of a LL with
K_{\rm R}=1/4
. For
\mu<\mu_{c}
,
C_{\theta}
gradually transitions from an algebraic decay to a faster one. The crossover time
\xi_{\tau}^{p}
— the superscript
p
refers to
C_{\theta}(0,\tau)
describing the propagation of a particle — defines the previously missing correlation length
\xi=(\xi_{\tau}^{p})^{1/z}
which diverges as
\mu\to\mu_{c}^{-}
.

The behaviour of
C_{\theta}(0,\beta-\tau)
strongly differs from that of
C_{\theta}(0,\tau)
(see Fig.
15
, right). In the LL (
\mu>\mu_{c}
), its asymptotic decay is
\sim\tau^{-1/(2K_{\rm R})}
, just like
C_{\theta}(0,\tau)
, but sets in at much later times as the transition is approached. At the transition and in the MI,
C_{\theta}(0,\beta-\tau)\sim\exp(-\tau/\xi_{\tau}^{h})
where the superscript
h
is for hole. Not only does the decay length
\xi_{\tau}^{h}
not diverge at the transition, but it also decreases to a finite value as
\mu\to\mu_{c}^{-}
. This length can thus not be interpreted as the relevant correlation length near the transition. Its microscopic interpretation will be discussed in the next section.

V.3
Identifying the Hubbard bands

The difference between
C_{\varphi}(0,\tau)
, which does not change at all in the MI, and
C_{\theta}(0,\tau)
, which does, can be understood at the quantum level. Recall that for an operator
\mathcal{O}(\tau)
, its two-point function is given by

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{O}(\tau)\mathcal{O}(0)\rangle=

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{T}}\bra{0}e^{\tau\hat{H}}\hat{\mathcal{O}}e^{-\tau\hat{H}}\hat{\mathcal{O}}\ket{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n}|\bra{0}\hat{\mathcal{O}}\ket{n}|^{2}e^{-|\tau|E_{n}}

(55)

where
\ket{n}
are the energy eigenstates. This means that, at late times, the two point function decays as
\sim C+e^{-|\tau|E_{1\mathcal{O}}}
where
E_{1\mathcal{O}}
is the energy of the lowest-energy excitation created by
\mathcal{O}
, and
C
is a constant that appears for observables with a non-zero average
\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle
because of spontaneous symmetry breaking. For our purposes, we are interested in two operators. The first is the field
e^{i\varphi(x)}
which captures the fluctuations of the density. The second is the one-body operator which, in the MI, is
c_{j}\sim\cos(\pi/2j-\varphi(x))e^{i\theta(x)}\simeq\cos(\pi/2j-\langle\varphi\rangle)e^{i\theta(x)}
since the field
\varphi
fluctuates very little. According to Eq. (
V.3
), for
\tau\gg 1
,

\displaystyle C_{\theta}(0,\tau)\sim e^{-\tau E_{1p}},

(56)

where
E_{1p}
is the smallest energy needed to create a particle. Since it sets the asymptotic exponential decay of
C_{\theta}(0,\tau)
, we identify
E_{1p}=1/\xi_{\tau}^{p}
. Similarly, for the propagation of a hole,
C_{\theta}(0,\beta-\tau)\sim e^{-\tau E_{1h}}
with

E_{1h}\sim 1/\xi_{\tau}^{h}
the smallest energy needed to create a hole. We also have

\displaystyle C_{\varphi}\sim C+e^{-\tau E_{1p1h}},

(57)

where
E_{1p1h}
is the smallest energy needed to create a density excitation, i.e. a particle and a hole, and
C
reflects the spontaneous breaking of the symmetry
\varphi\to\varphi+\pi/2
, which microscopically consists in reversing the ”particle-hole-particle-hole” order of the MI to ”hole-particle-hole-particle”. The observation that
C_{\varphi}(0,\tau)
does not depend on
\mu
while
C_{\theta}(0,\tau)
does, implies that
E_{1p1h}
does not vary with
\mu
whereas
E_{1p}
does. This agrees with the picture of the MI having an upper Hubbard band with energies
E>\mu_{c}
and a lower Hubbard band with
E<-\mu_{c}
separated by a gap
2\mu_{c}
(see Fig.
18
)
Giamarchi 1997
.

Figure 18:
The MI can be described by two Hubbard bands, the upper Hubbard band (UHB) and the lower Hubbard band (LHB). At zero temperature, when the chemical potential lies in the gap, only the states in the LHB are occupied. The energy needed to create a particle is then

, and that of a hole is

.

When the chemical potential

is in between the two bands, the energy needed to create a particle is

which agrees with
\xi_{\tau}^{p}\sim|\delta|^{-1}
and explains why the critical scaling (

) holds throughout the MI and not just close to the critical region. The energy of a hole being
E_{1h}=\mu+\mu_{c}
, it increases to the finite value
2\mu_{c}
at the transition. As soon as
\mu>\mu_{c}
, particles start to populate the upper Hubbard band and
E_{1h}=0
. This discontinuity in
E_{1h}
is responsible for the large crossover in the LL between the early exponential decay and the late algebraic decay (see Fig.
15
, right). Finally, the energy of a particle and a hole is
E_{1p1h}=E_{1p}+E_{1h}=2\mu_{c}
, which, as expected, is
\mu
-independent. The MI can also be exited through
-\mu_{c}
instead of
+\mu_{c}
. The roles of
\xi_{\tau}^{h}
and
\xi_{\tau}^{p}
would then be reversed, with
\xi_{\tau}^{h}
diverging at the transition.

VI
Conclusion

In this work, we have proposed a Monte Carlo algorithm to study the generic Mott transition in one-dimensional quantum systems. More specifically, we considered the bosonized formulation of such models which, in the path-integral formalism, is given by the sine-Gordon model with a tilt (
II.1
). In the grand-canonical ensemble, the boundary conditions of this model are only periodic modulo
\pi
. This splits the configuration space into distinct topological sectors, rendering large-scale simulations totally out of reach for standard Metropolis-like algorithms. The key idea to build an efficient algorithm is thus to enlarge the model and work in a configuration space where one can smoothly transition between different boundary conditions. This idea has already been successfully used to study Mott transitions in
(2+1)
D
Alet and Sørensen 2003b
;
Alet and Sørensen 2003a
;
Alet and Sørensen 2004
;
Prokof’ev and Svistunov 2004
by introducing worm updates
Prokof’ev et al. 1998
;
Prokof’ev and Svistunov 2001
that gradually change the boundary conditions. However, these studies were made on very coarse-grained models whose
(1+1)
D counterpart would be obtained by neglecting the fine fluctuations in the sine-Gordon model. To efficiently deal with those fluctuations, we introduced a smooth worm algorithm (SmoWo) which combines the previous worm updates with event-chain Monte Carlo updates
Michel et al. 2014
;
Krauth 2021
. Both types of updates are tightly intertwined so as to smooth out each worm move and push even further the idea of generating a smooth path between configurations with different boundary conditions. We performed a detailed performance analysis and showed that the SmoWo algorithm has an integrated autocorrelation time which scales as
O(L^{4.8})
for a system of size
\sim L\times L^{2}
.

Although models describing generic Mott transitions are known to have very large finite-size effects
Alet and Sørensen 2004
;
Prokof’ev and Svistunov 2004
, the SmoWo algorithm proved powerful enough to clearly identify the two phases: the Luttinger liquid with quasi-long range order, and the Mott insulator with its characteristic Hubbard bands. At the critical point, we also retrieved the exponents
z=2.00\pm 0.01
,
\nu=0.51\pm 0.01
and the anomalous dimension for the particle-particle two-point function
\eta=2.99\pm 0.02
, all of which compare favourably with analytical results derived from the Bethe ansatz or refermionization techniques
Yamamoto 1983
;
Haldane et al. 1983
;
Schulz 1980
;
Papa and Tsvelik 2001
.

While the present work focused on simulating the sine-Gordon model which is by now relatively well understood, the SmoWo algorithm developed here is relevant for the study of any bosonized system in the grand-canonical ensemble — that is, provided the Euclidean action contains no imaginary part, which is the case if one can integrate out one of the bosonic fields
\phi
or
\theta
— and thus complements the algorithm proposed in Ref.
Bouverot-Dupuis et al. 2025
for bosonized systems in the canonical ensemble. For instance, the SmoWo algorithm can naturally be extended to study more complicated models including long-range interactions
Orignac 2025
, dissipative effects
Malatsetxebarria et al. 2013
, or multiple fields
Paris et al. 2025
.

Bosonization subtleties

In this appendix, we explain in greater detail the bosonization approach used in this work. We first recall our bosonization conventions as there are many competing conventions used in the literature, and we pay a particular attention to the treatment of the so-called 0-modes. Next, we explain how to obtain the path integral formulation of the bosonized Hamiltonian (
II.1
) and the phase-phase two-point function (
IV.3
), and finally comment on the bosonized definitions of some observables.

A.1
Bosonization conventions

Our bosonization conventions follow that of constructive abelian bosonization as presented in
Haldane 1981a
;
von Delft and Schoeller 1998
(see also Appendix B of
Chua et al. 2020
). For a periodic system of length
L
and Fermi momentum
k_{F}
, this approach requires first splitting the fermionic field
\hat{\psi}(x=aj)=c_{j}/\sqrt{a}
into
\sum_{r=\pm 1}\hat{\psi}_{r}
to distinguish left-movers
\hat{\psi}_{r=-1}
with momenta close to
-k_{F}
from right-movers
\hat{\psi}_{r=+1}
with momenta close to
+k_{F}
. The bosonization identity then states that these fermionic degrees of freedom can be expressed using bosonic fields
\hat{\phi}_{r}
as

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}_{r}(x)=

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{\eta}_{r}}{\sqrt{L}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}e^{irk_{F}x-i\hat{\phi}_{r}(x)}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}},

(58)

where the bosonic fields
\hat{\phi}_{r}
are defined through their mode decomposition

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{r}(x)=\hat{\phi}_{r}^{0}-r2\pi\hat{N}_{r}\frac{x}{L}+i\sum_{rq>0}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{L|q|}}\left(e^{iqx}\hat{b}_{r}(q)-e^{-iqx}\hat{b}_{r}^{\dagger}(q)\right),

(59)

with
q\in\frac{2\pi}{L}\mathbb{Z}
, and
\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\bullet\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}
denotes bosonic normal-ordering (all operators
\hat{\phi}_{r}^{0},\hat{b}^{\dagger}(q)
are to be put to the left of all
\hat{N}_{r},\hat{b}_{r}(q)
). The operators
\hat{\phi}_{r}^{0}
are the 0-momentum modes (
q=0
) and are only defined in terms of the original fermions through
e^{i\hat{\phi}_{r}^{0}}
. Thus, one has to identify
\hat{\phi}_{r}^{0}\sim\hat{\phi}_{r}^{0}+2\pi
, which means that the fields
\hat{\phi}_{r}(x)
are compact bosons with compactification radii
2\pi
. The integer-valued operators
\hat{N}_{r}
count the number of
r
-movers. The
\hat{b}_{r}(q),\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{r}(q)
correspond to the bosonic particle-hole excitations of the system. Finally, the
\hat{\eta}_{r}
in Eq. (
58
) are Majorana fermions which ensure the anti-commuting nature of the fermion. The only non-zero commutators amongst these operators are

\displaystyle[\hat{b}_{r}(q),\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{r^{\prime}}(q^{\prime})]=\delta_{rr^{\prime}}\delta_{qq^{\prime}},\quad[\hat{\phi}_{r}^{0},\hat{N}_{r^{\prime}}]=i\delta_{r,r^{\prime}},\quad\{\hat{\eta}_{r},\hat{\eta}_{r^{\prime}}\}=2\delta_{r,r^{\prime}}.

(60)

The phase fields introduced in the main text are given by (
R
is for
r=1
and
L
for
r=-1
)

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}(x)=\frac{\hat{\phi}_{R}(x)-\hat{\phi}_{L}(x)}{2},\qquad\hat{\theta}(x)=-\frac{\hat{\phi}_{R}(x)+\hat{\phi}_{L}(x)}{2}.

(61)

They can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}(x)=

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{0}-\frac{\pi\hat{N}x}{L}+\tilde{\phi}(x),\quad\hat{\theta}(x)=\hat{\theta}_{0}+\frac{\pi\hat{J}x}{L}+\tilde{\theta}(x),

(62)

where the topological operators are

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{0}=

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{\phi}_{R}^{0}-\hat{\phi}_{L}^{0}}{2},\qquad

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{0}=-\frac{\hat{\phi}_{R}^{0}+\hat{\phi}_{L}^{0}}{2},

(63)

\displaystyle\hat{N}=

\displaystyle\hat{N}_{R}+\hat{N}_{L},\qquad

\displaystyle\hat{J}=\hat{N}_{R}-\hat{N}_{L},

(64)

and the
L
-periodic operators are

\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}(x)=

\displaystyle i\sum_{q\neq 0}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2L|q|}}{{\rm sgn}(q)}\left(e^{iqx}\hat{b}_{{\rm sgn}(q)}(q)-e^{-iqx}\hat{b}_{{\rm sgn}(q)}^{\dagger}(q)\right),

(65)

\displaystyle\tilde{\theta}(x)=

\displaystyle-i\sum_{q\neq 0}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2L|q|}}\left(e^{iqx}\hat{b}_{{\rm sgn}(q)}(q)-e^{-iqx}\hat{b}_{{\rm sgn}(q)}^{\dagger}(q)\right).

(66)

The only non-vanishing commutation relations are

[\hat{N},\hat{\theta}_{0}]=i,\quad[\hat{\phi}_{0},\hat{J}]=i,\quad[\tilde{\theta}(x),\tilde{\phi}(y)]=-i\pi\left(\left\lfloor\frac{x-y}{L}\right\rfloor+\frac{1}{2}\right).

(67)

The total particle number operator
\hat{N}
and current operator
\hat{J}
are integer-quantized and subject to the parity condition
\hat{N}+\hat{J}\equiv 0\pmod{2}
. The
0
-modes
\hat{\theta}_{0}
and
\hat{\phi}_{0}
are compactified with radii
\pi
but are not independent: one has to simultaneously identify
\hat{\phi}_{0},\hat{\theta}_{0}\sim\hat{\phi}_{0}+\pi,\hat{\theta}_{0}+\pi
or
\hat{\phi}_{0},\hat{\theta}_{0}\sim\hat{\phi}_{0}+\pi,\hat{\theta}_{0}-\pi

3
3

3

Note that the compactification conditions are also seen in the harmonic fluid approach or phenomenological bosonization of bosons
Haldane 1981b
;
Cazalilla 2004
;
Cazalilla et al. 2011
;
Giamarchi 2004
.
. The last commutation relation implies that
[\tilde{\theta}(x),\frac{1}{\pi}\nabla\tilde{\phi}(y)]=i\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\delta(x-y-nL)
so
\tilde{\theta}(x)
and
\frac{1}{\pi}\nabla\tilde{\phi}(y)
form a pair of canonically conjugate variables. Note that in the thermodynamic limit
L\to\infty
, one recovers the commutator given in the main text

[\hat{\theta}(x),\hat{\phi}(y)]=i\pi\left(\frac{x-y}{L}-\left\lfloor\frac{x-y}{L}\right\rfloor-\frac{1}{2}\right)\overset{L\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}-i\frac{\pi}{2}{\rm sgn}(x-y).

(68)

Neglecting subleading terms
\propto 1/L
is common practice and is, of course, valid only if one is interested in the thermodynamic limit. Since this is the case in this work, we will systematically eliminate all such terms as they lead to lengthy equations. As a consequence, the simulations done at finite
L
do not strictly coincide with ”true” finite systems of size
L
, but they nevertheless converge to the correct thermodynamic behaviour as
L
is increased.

A.2
Path integral formulation of the partition function

Using the previous bosonization dictionary, the fermion (
II
) and spin (
II
) Hamiltonians are mapped onto the sine-Gordon Hamiltonian (
II.1
) which we recall here

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\int_{0}^{L}{\rm d}x\frac{u}{2\pi}\left[K(\nabla\hat{\theta})^{2}+\frac{1}{K}(\nabla\hat{\phi})^{2}\right]-g\cos(4\hat{\phi})+\frac{\mu}{\pi}\nabla\hat{\phi},

(69)

Details of the derivation of this standard result can be found in Refs.
Giamarchi 2004
;
Gogolin et al. 2004
;
Dupuis 2026
. We stress that this result only holds if one neglects contributions
\propto 1/L
in the spirit of the present work. Using the notations introduced in Eq. (
62
) and using the fact that
\tilde{\phi}(x)
and
\tilde{\theta}(x)
are periodic operators, the sine-Gordon Hamiltonian can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\frac{\pi u}{2L}\left(K\hat{J}^{2}+\frac{1}{K}\hat{N}^{2}\right)-\mu\hat{N}+\int_{0}^{L}{\rm d}x\frac{u}{2\pi}\left(K(\nabla\tilde{\theta})^{2}+\frac{1}{K}(\nabla\tilde{\phi})^{2}\right)-g\cos\left(4\hat{\phi}_{0}-\frac{4\pi\hat{N}x}{L}+4\tilde{\phi}\right).

(70)

We now wish to represent the canonical equilibrium partition function
Z={\rm Tr}\,e^{-\beta\hat{H}}
using a path-integral. Since the standard procedure of Trotterizing the partition function as
Z=\lim_{M\to\infty}{\rm Tr}\prod_{i=1}^{M}e^{-\beta/M\hat{H}}
always leads in the end to defining the Euclidean action as
S=\int{\rm d}\tau L
with
L
the classical Lagrangian in imaginary time, we decide to directly write down
L
. From Eq. (
67
), it appears that the conjugate momenta to the variables
\hat{X}=\hat{N},\hat{\phi}_{0},\tilde{\theta}(x)
are
\hat{P}=\hat{\theta}_{0},\hat{J},\frac{1}{\pi}\nabla\tilde{\phi}
. Using the notation

\partial_{t}\hat{X}=\frac{\delta\hat{H}}{\delta\hat{P}},

(71)

the real-time Lagrangian is defined as

\displaystyle L=

\displaystyle\theta_{0}\partial_{t}N+J\partial_{t}\phi_{0}+\int{\rm d}x\frac{1}{\pi}\nabla\tilde{\phi}\partial_{t}\tilde{\theta}-H.

(72)

Going to imaginary time
\tau=it
, the Euclidean action is thus given by

\displaystyle S=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\beta}{\rm d}\tau\Bigg[\frac{\pi u}{2L}\left(KJ^{2}+\frac{1}{K}N^{2}\right)-\mu N-i\theta_{0}\partial_{\tau}N-iJ\partial_{\tau}\phi_{0}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{L}{\rm d}x\frac{u}{2\pi}\left(K(\nabla\tilde{\theta})^{2}+\frac{1}{K}(\nabla\tilde{\phi})^{2}\right)-g\cos\left(4\phi_{0}-\frac{4\pi Nx}{L}+4\tilde{\phi}\right)-i\frac{1}{\pi}\partial_{x}\tilde{\phi}\partial_{\tau}\tilde{\theta}\Bigg],

(73)

and the grand-canonical equilibrium partition function
Z={\rm Tr}\,e^{-\beta\hat{H}}
is

\displaystyle Z=\int\mathcal{D}\tilde{\phi}\mathcal{D}\tilde{\theta}\mathcal{D}N\mathcal{D}J\mathcal{D}\phi_{0}\mathcal{D}\theta_{0}\,e^{-S},

(74)

where, following the considerations of the previous subsection, we integrate over

•

all space- and time-periodic functions
\tilde{\phi}(x,\tau)
and
\tilde{\theta}(x,\tau)
,

•

all time-periodic integer functions
N(\tau)
and
J(\tau)
such that
N(\tau)+J(\tau)\equiv 0\pmod{2}
,

•

all functions
\theta_{0}(\tau)
and
\phi_{0}(\tau)
such that
\theta_{0}(0)=N_{\tau}^{\prime}\pi+\theta_{0}(\beta)
and
\phi_{0}(0)=N_{\tau}\pi+\phi_{0}(\beta)
with
N_{\tau}+N_{\tau}^{\prime}\equiv 0\pmod{2}
.

The path-integrals over
\tilde{\theta}
and
\theta_{0}
can be done analytically. The first integration creates a term
\sim(\partial_{\tau}\tilde{\phi})^{2}
and the second yields the condition
\partial_{\tau}N(\tau)=0\Rightarrow N(\tau)=N_{x}
in agreement with the fact that at the Hamiltonian level
[\hat{N},\hat{H}]=0
. Next, for
L
large,
j=J/L
loses its discrete nature and can be replaced by a continuous real variable up to
\propto 1/L
corrections that we neglect. Integrating out this variable creates a term
\sim(\partial_{\tau}\phi_{0})^{2}
. Putting everything together, the action reduces to

\displaystyle S=

\displaystyle\int{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}\tau\Bigg[\frac{1}{2\pi K}\left(u(\partial_{x}\tilde{\phi})^{2}+u\left(\frac{\pi N_{x}}{L}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{u}(\partial_{\tau}\tilde{\phi})^{2}+\frac{1}{u}(\partial_{\tau}\tilde{\phi}_{0})^{2}\right)-g\cos\left(4\phi_{0}-\frac{4\pi N_{x}x}{L}+4\tilde{\phi}\right)-\mu\frac{N_{x}}{L}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}\tau\Bigg[\frac{1}{2\pi K}\left(u(\partial_{x}\phi)^{2}+\frac{1}{u}(\partial_{\tau}\phi)^{2}\right)-g\cos\left(4\phi\right)+\frac{\mu}{\pi}\partial_{x}\phi\Bigg],

(75)

where
\phi(x,\tau)=\phi_{0}(\tau)-\pi N_{x}\frac{x}{L}+\tilde{\phi}(x,\tau)
. The partition function can now be explicitly written as

\displaystyle Z=\sum_{N_{x},N_{\tau}=-\infty}^{+\infty}\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\phi(0,\tau)=\phi(L,\tau)+N_{x}\pi\\
\phi(x,0)=\phi(x,\beta)+N_{\tau}\pi\end{subarray}}{\int\mathcal{D}\phi}e^{-S[\phi]},

(76)

which is the result given in Sec.
II
.

A.3
Path integral formulation of the phase-phase two-point function

This appendix proves that the discretized path integral representation of the phase-phase two-point function
C_{\theta}(x,\tau)=\big\langle e^{i(\theta(x,\tau)-\theta(0,0))}\big\rangle
is Eq. (
90
). To this end, we first prove the following
gluing identity
,

\bra{\phi_{1}}e^{\pm i\hat{\theta}(x)}\ket{\phi_{2}}=\delta\left(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2}\pm\frac{\pi}{2}{\rm sgn}(x-\bullet)\right),

(77)

where the Dirac delta requires that, for all
y
,
\phi_{1}(y)-\phi_{2}(y)\pm\frac{\pi}{2}{\rm sgn}(x-y)=0
.

Proof of the gluing identity.

Starting from the commutation relation
[\hat{\theta}(x),\hat{\phi}(y)]=-i\frac{\pi}{2}{\rm sgn}(x-y)
, one infers the relation
[\hat{\phi}(y),e^{\pm i\hat{\theta}(x)}]=\mp\frac{\pi}{2}{\rm sgn}(x-y)e^{\pm i\hat{\theta}(x)}
. With the help of the eigenstates and eigenvalues
\ket{\phi},\phi(x)
of the operator
\hat{\phi}(x)
defined through
\hat{\phi}(x)\ket{\phi}=\phi(x)\ket{\phi}
, the previous operator identity yields

\displaystyle\bra{\phi_{1}}\hat{\phi}(y)e^{\pm i\hat{\theta}(x)}\ket{\phi_{2}}=\left(\phi_{2}(y)\mp\frac{\pi}{2}{\rm sgn}(x-y)\right)\bra{\phi_{1}}e^{\pm i\hat{\theta}(x)}\ket{\phi_{2}}.

(78)

Acting directly with
\hat{\phi}(y)
on
\bra{\phi_{1}}
, the matrix element
\bra{\phi_{1}}\hat{\phi}(y)e^{\pm i\hat{\theta}(x)}\ket{\phi}
can also be evaluated to

\bra{\phi_{1}}\hat{\phi}(y)e^{\pm i\hat{\theta}(x)}\ket{\phi_{2}}=\phi_{1}(y)\bra{\phi_{1}}e^{\pm i\hat{\theta}(x)}\ket{\phi_{2}}.

(79)

Putting together Eqs. (
78
,
79
) yields

\displaystyle\left(\phi_{1}(y)-\phi_{2}(y)\pm\frac{\pi}{2}{\rm sgn}(x-y)\right)\bra{\phi_{1}}e^{\pm i\hat{\theta}(x)}\ket{\phi_{2}}=0.

(80)

Since this identity is valid for all
y
,
\bra{\phi_{1}}e^{\pm i\hat{\theta}(x)}\ket{\phi_{2}}
can only be non-zero when
\phi_{1}(y)-\phi_{2}(y)\pm\frac{\pi}{2}{\rm sgn}(x-y)=0
for all
y
. This means that

\bra{\phi_{1}}e^{\pm i\hat{\theta}(x)}\ket{\phi_{2}}=\mathcal{N}^{(\star)}\,\delta\left(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2}\pm\frac{\pi}{2}{\rm sgn}(x-\bullet)\right),

(81)

with a normalization factor
\mathcal{N}
. One then notices that

\displaystyle\delta(\phi_{1}-\phi_{3})=\braket{\phi_{1}|\phi_{3}}=

\displaystyle\int{\rm d}\phi_{2}\bra{\phi_{1}}e^{i\hat{\theta}(x)}\ket{\phi_{2}}\bra{\phi_{2}}e^{-i\hat{\theta}(x)}\ket{\phi_{3}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int{\rm d}\phi_{2}\mathcal{N}\,\delta\left(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2}+\frac{\pi}{2}{\rm sgn}(x-\bullet)\right)\mathcal{N}^{\star}\,\delta\left(\phi_{2}-\phi_{3}-\frac{\pi}{2}{\rm sgn}(x-\bullet)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\mathcal{N}|^{2}\delta\left(\phi_{1}-\phi_{3}\right),

(82)

so
\mathcal{N}=e^{i\alpha}
. Since the matrix elements
\bra{\phi_{1}}e^{\pm i\hat{\theta}(x)}\ket{\phi_{2}}
appear as conjugate pairs in the following, only
|\mathcal{N}|^{2}
appears and one can safely ignore
\alpha
. This leads to the gluing condition Eq. (
77
) stated above.

The path integral representation of
C_{\theta}
defined in Eq. (
IV.3
) is found by first inserting resolutions of the identity as

\displaystyle C_{\theta}(x_{h}-x_{t},\tau_{h}-\tau_{t})=\frac{1}{Z}\int{\rm d}\phi_{1}{\rm d}\phi_{2}{\rm d}\phi_{3}{\rm d}\phi_{4}\,

\displaystyle\bra{\phi_{1}}e^{-(\beta+\tau_{t}-\tau_{h})\hat{H}}\ket{\phi_{2}}\bra{\phi_{2}}e^{i\hat{\theta}(x_{h})}\ket{\phi_{3}}

\displaystyle\times\bra{\phi_{3}}e^{-(\tau_{h}-\tau_{t})\hat{H}}\ket{\phi_{4}}\bra{\phi_{4}}e^{-i\hat{\theta}(x_{t})}\ket{\phi_{1}},

(83)

where, for the sake of simplicity, we consider
\tau_{h}>\tau_{t}
to get rid of time-ordering. We also use the eigenstates and eigenvalues
\ket{\phi},\phi(x)
of the operator
\hat{\phi}(x)
defined through
\hat{\phi}(x)\ket{\phi}=\phi(x)\ket{\phi}
. The matrix elements of the operators
e^{\pm i\hat{\theta}}
”glue” the boundary conditions of the evolution operators according to the gluing identity(
77

\displaystyle C_{\theta}(x_{h}-x_{t},\tau_{h}-\tau_{t})=\frac{1}{Z}\int{\rm d}\phi_{2}{\rm d}\phi_{4}\,

\displaystyle\bra{\phi_{4}-\frac{\pi}{2}{\rm sgn}(x_{t}-\bullet)}e^{-(\beta+\tau_{t}-\tau_{h})\hat{H}}\ket{\phi_{2}}

\displaystyle\times\bra{\phi_{2}+\frac{\pi}{2}{\rm sgn}(x_{h}-\bullet)}e^{-(\tau_{h}-\tau_{t})\hat{H}}\ket{\phi_{4}}.

(84)

The path integral representations of the evolution operator matrix elements are found similarly to that of the partition function (
7

\displaystyle\bra{\phi_{1}}e^{-\tau\hat{H}}\ket{\phi_{2}}=\sum_{N_{x}=-\infty}^{+\infty}\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\phi(0,\tau)=\phi(L,\tau)+N_{x}\pi\\

where

A.4
Compressibility and superfluid density

The compressibility
\kappa
and superfluid density
\rho_{s}
at finite
L
and
\beta
are usually defined in Monte Carlo simulations using the worm algorithm as the variance of the winding numbers
N_{x}
and
N_{\tau}
such that
Wallin et al. 1994

\displaystyle\kappa=\frac{\beta}{L}\left(\langle N_{x}^{2}\rangle-\langle N_{x}\rangle^{2}\right),

(92)

\displaystyle\rho_{s}=\frac{L}{\beta}\left(\langle N_{\tau}^{2}\rangle-\langle N_{\tau}\rangle^{2}\right).

(93)

However, these definitions yield results which do not converge well in the limit
L,\beta\to\infty
. In Fig.
20
left, we plot
\kappa
against the chemical potential
\mu
for a LL (
g=0
) with
K=0.5
and
u=1
. It is known that in the
\beta,L\to\infty
limit, one should have
\kappa=\frac{K}{\pi u}
and
\rho_{s}=\frac{Ku}{\pi}
, but the previous definitions converge to those results only
after averaging over a small window
, and the oscillations increase as
K
is lowered. This is why we prefer the more common definitions in the context of bosonization
Giamarchi 2004

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{2}}{\pi^{2}}\langle|\varphi(q,0)|^{2}\rangle|_{q=\frac{2\pi}{L}},

(94)

\displaystyle\rho_{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{n}^{2}}{\pi^{2}}\langle|\varphi(0,\omega_{n})|^{2}\rangle|_{\omega_{n}=\frac{2\pi}{\beta}},

(95)

which display much better convergence properties as shown by
\kappa
plotted in Fig.
20
right. The link between both definitions of, for instance
\kappa
, comes from the fact that the Fourier transform of
\partial_{x}\phi(x,\tau)=\pi N_{x}/L+\partial_{x}\varphi(x,\tau)
at
q
and
\omega_{n}=0
is
\pi N_{x}\sqrt{\beta/L}\delta_{q,0}+q\varphi(q,0)
. Equation (
92
) therefore quantifies the fluctuation of the
q=0
mode, while Eq. (
94
) quantifies that of the smallest non-zero mode
q_{\rm min}=\frac{2\pi}{L}
. The subtle issue at play is thus that both do not coincide in the limit
\beta,L\to\infty
despite the fact that
q_{\rm min}\to 0
.

Appendix B
Continuous-time Monte Carlo formalism

B.1
The infinitesimal generator

A continuous-time Markov process over a configuration space
\Omega=\{x\}
can be seen as the infinitesimal limit
\Delta t\to 0^{+}
of a discrete-time Markov Chain over
\Omega
with time steps
\Delta t
. The transition matrix
{\cal P}^{\Delta t}
of such a Markov chain encodes the probability to transition from
x
to
x^{\prime}
through the coefficient
{\cal P}^{\Delta t}(x,x^{\prime})
. If the time step
\Delta t
is small enough, we expect the transition matrix to admit the expansion
{\cal P}^{\Delta t}=\mathbbm{1}+\Delta t\mathcal{A}
where the operator
\mathcal{A}
is called the (infinitesimal) generator
Davis 1993
;
Guyon et al. 2024
;
Monemvassitis et al. 2023
. More formally, the generator is defined as acting on test functions (i.e. observables) as

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}f(x)=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0^{+}}\mathbbm{E}_{\varphi_{\Delta t}}\left[\frac{f(\varphi_{\Delta t}(x))-f(x)}{\Delta t}\right],

(96)

where
\varphi_{\Delta t}
is the time-evolution operator over a time
\Delta t
.

Given a target distribution
\pi(x)
, the ergodic theorem states that a discrete-time Markov-Chain will, after some mixing time, sample
\pi(x)
if it is irreducible, aperiodic and satisfies the stationarity condition (or equivalently the global balance condition)

\displaystyle\sum_{x^{\prime}}\pi(x^{\prime}){\cal P}^{\Delta t}(x^{\prime},x)=\pi(x).

(97)

Taking the infinitesimal time step limit
\Delta t\to 0^{+}
and introducing a test function, one deduces the global balance condition for the generator

\displaystyle\sum_{x}\pi(x)\mathcal{A}f(x)=0.

(98)

B.2
The generator of ECMC algorithms: a simple example

We now detail how to derive the infinitesimal generator of an ECMC algorithm from a simple example. We consider a particle whose coordinate is
y\in\mathbbm{R}
with probability
\pi(y)\propto e^{-S(y)}
. We lift the configuration space by adding a speed
e=\pm 1
, and decide that the deterministic motion is

\displaystyle\partial_{t}y(t)=e.

(99)

It is interrupted by events that occur with the rate
[e\partial_{y}S(y)]_{+}
and flip
e\to-e
. If the configuration is
(y,e)
at time
t
, then, for
\Delta t\ll 1
, the configuration at time
t+\Delta t
can be

•

(y+e\Delta t,e)
with probability
1-[e\partial_{y}S(y)]_{+}\Delta t
,

•

(y,-e)
with probability
[e\partial_{y}S(y)]_{+}\Delta t
.

From the definition (
96
), this leads to the generator

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}f(y,e)=

\displaystyle\lim_{\Delta t\to 0^{+}}\frac{\big(1-[e\partial_{y}S(y)]_{+}\Delta t\big)\big(f(y+e\Delta t,e)-f(y,e)\big)+[e\partial_{y}S(y)]_{+}\Delta t\big(f(y,-e)-f(y,e)\big)}{\Delta t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e\partial_{y}f(y,e)+[e\partial_{y}S(y)]_{+}(f(y,-e)-f(y,e)).

(100)

It turns out that this generator satisfies the global balance condition (
98
) for the stationary distribution
\pi_{\rm tot.}(y,e)=\frac{1}{2}\pi(y)
. Indeed, a small computation shows that

\displaystyle\sum_{e=\pm 1}\int{\rm d}y\,\pi_{\rm tot.}(y,e)\mathcal{A}f(y,e)=

\displaystyle\sum_{e=\pm 1}\int{\rm d}y\,\frac{1}{2}\pi(y)\Big(e\partial_{y}f(y,e)+[e\partial_{y}S(y)]_{+}(f(y,-e)-f(y,e))\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{e=\pm 1}\int{\rm d}y\,\frac{1}{2}\pi(y)\Big(e\partial_{y}f(y,e)+([(-e)\partial_{y}S(y)]_{+}-[e\partial_{y}S(y)]_{+})f(y,e)\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{e=\pm 1}\int{\rm d}y\,\frac{1}{2}\pi(y)\Big(e\partial_{y}f(y,e)-e\partial_{y}S(y)f(y,e)\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{e=\pm 1}\int{\rm d}y\,\frac{e}{2}\partial_{y}\Big(\pi(y)f(y,e)\Big)=0,

.

(101)

since
\pi(y)
vanishes at
\pm\infty
(otherwise
\pi(y)
is not normalizable).

B.3
Proving the global balance condition

In this section we prove that the SmoWo algorithm introduced in Sec.
III
is irreducible and statisfied the global balance condition (
98
) using the generator notation introduced in the previous subsection. At the end of the section we also discuss the Wo algorithm. Following the discussion of Sec.
III
, the model is defined over the space of all possible worm heads
p_{h}
and tails
p_{t}
, currents
\vec{J}
, fluctuations
f
, first lifting variables
v=(i,e)
, second lifting variables
\nu=(\alpha,\varepsilon)
, and move types
\sigma
. The SmoWo algorithm samples the variable
(p_{h},p_{t},\vec{J},f,v,\nu,\sigma)
. Because we have included refreshment updates, it is clear that the algorithm can reach any
v
and
\nu
and can update any component of
f
. Moreover, the worm updates generate the entire set of allowed variables
(p_{h},p_{t},\vec{J})
. Finally, the
\sigma
-updates can update
\sigma
from any configuration. Putting everything together shows that the algorithm is irreducible. We now prove that the global balance condition is satisfied for the following stationary distribution

\pi_{\rm tot.}(p_{h},p_{t},\vec{J},f,v,\nu,\sigma)=\pi(p_{h},p_{t},\vec{J},f)\mu_{V}(v)\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu|p_{h})\mu_{\Sigma}(\sigma),

(102)

where
\pi(p_{h},p_{t},\vec{J},f)=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{C}^{2}(p_{h},p_{t})}(\vec{J})e^{-S(\vec{J},f)}/Z_{\rm w}
encodes the physical part of the target distribution and all other variables are uniformly distributed over their possible values
\mu_{\Sigma}(\sigma)=1/2
,
\mu_{V}(v)=\frac{1}{2L\beta}
and
\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu=(\alpha,\varepsilon)|p_{h})=\frac{1}{8}\mathbbm{1}_{\partial p_{h}}(\alpha)
. Following the process outlined in the previous subsection, the SmoWo’s generator is found to be (we use the notation
\phi=(p_{h},p_{t},\vec{J},f)
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}f(\phi,v,\nu,\sigma)=

\displaystyle(1-\sigma)\Bigg[e\partial_{f_{i}}f(\phi,v,\nu,0)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{j\in\partial_{\rm q}i}[e\partial_{f_{i}}S_{\rm q}^{i,j}(\phi)]_{+}\left(f(\phi,(j,e),\nu,0)-f(\phi,(i,e),\nu,0)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+[e\partial_{f_{i}}S_{\rm c}^{i}(\phi)]_{+}\left(f(\phi,(i,-e),\nu,0)-f(\phi,(i,e),\nu,0)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\lambda_{\rm r}\sum_{v^{\prime}}\mu_{V}(v^{\prime})\Big(f(\phi,v^{\prime},\nu,0)-f(\phi,v,\nu,0)\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad+\lambda_{\rm w}\int{\rm d}\phi^{\prime}\sum_{\nu^{\prime}}\mathcal{P}(\phi,\nu\to\phi^{\prime},\nu^{\prime})\Big(f(\phi^{\prime},v,\nu^{\prime},1)-f(\phi,v,\nu,0)\Big)\Bigg]

\displaystyle+\sigma\Bigg[\varepsilon\partial_{f_{\alpha}}f(\phi,v,\nu,1)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{\gamma\in\partial_{\rm q}\alpha\cap\partial p_{h}}[\varepsilon\partial_{f_{\alpha}}S_{\rm q}^{\alpha,\gamma}(\phi)]_{+}\left(f(\phi,v,(\gamma,\varepsilon),1)-f(\phi,v,(\alpha,\varepsilon),1)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{\gamma\in\partial_{\rm q}\alpha\setminus\partial p_{h}}[\varepsilon\partial_{f_{\alpha}}S_{\rm q}^{\alpha,\gamma}(\phi)]_{+}\left(f(\phi,v,(\alpha,-\varepsilon),1)-f(\phi,v,(\alpha,\varepsilon),1)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+[\varepsilon\partial_{f_{\alpha}}S_{\rm c}^{\alpha}(\phi)]_{+}\left(f(\phi,v,(\alpha,-\varepsilon),1)-f(\phi,v,(\alpha,\varepsilon),1)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\lambda_{\rm w}\big(f(\phi,v,\nu,0)-f(\phi,v,\nu,1)\big)\Bigg],

(103)

where
\partial_{\rm q}i=\{i+\hat{x},i-\hat{x},i+\hat{\tau},i-\hat{\tau}\}
is the set of nearest neighbours of
i
,
\mathcal{P}(\phi,\nu\to\phi^{\prime},\nu^{\prime})
is the transition matrix encoding the worm move
\phi\to\phi^{\prime}
and the refreshment update
\nu\to\nu^{\prime}
if
\phi\neq\phi^{\prime}
. It can be more explicitly written down in terms of the transition matrix
{\cal P}_{\rm w}(\phi\to\phi^{\prime})
for the worm moves alone as

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\phi,\nu\to\phi^{\prime},\nu^{\prime})=\begin{cases}\mathcal{P}_{\rm w}(\phi\to\phi)\delta_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}&\text{ if }\phi=\phi^{\prime},\\
\mathcal{P}_{\rm w}(\phi\to\phi^{\prime})\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu^{\prime}|p_{h}^{\prime})&\text{ if }\phi\neq\phi^{\prime}.\end{cases}

(104)

Since
{\cal P}_{\rm w}
was designed to obey the detailed balance condition with respect to the measure
\pi(\phi)
, one can show that
{\cal P}
satisfies the larger detailed balance condition

\displaystyle\pi(\phi^{\prime})\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu^{\prime}|p_{h}^{\prime})\mathcal{P}(\phi^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}\to\phi,\nu)=\pi(\phi)\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu|p_{h})\mathcal{P}(\phi,\nu\to\phi^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}).

(105)

To ensure that the total probability flow is conserved, the transition matrix also satisfies the normalization condition

\displaystyle\sum_{\nu^{\prime}}\int{\rm d}\phi^{\prime}\,\mathcal{P}(\phi,\nu\to\phi^{\prime},\nu^{\prime})=1.

(106)

Equation (
B.3
) can be split into the first 5 lines which give the dynamics when
\sigma=0
, and the last 5 lines which concern
\sigma=1
. For each of these blocks, the first line is the deterministic shift, the next three are the events which do not change
\sigma
(including the refreshment term for
\sigma=0
) and the last line is the worm event which tries to move the worm if
\sigma=0
and always switches
\sigma\to 1-\sigma
. We now prove the global balance condition

\displaystyle\int{\rm d}\phi\sum_{v,\nu,\sigma}\pi_{\rm tot.}(\phi,v,\nu,\sigma)\mathcal{A}f(\phi,v,\nu,\sigma)=0,

(107)

following the lines of
Monemvassitis et al. 2023
;
Bouverot-Dupuis et al. 2025
. Injecting Eqs. (
102
,
B.3
) into the global balance condition (
107
), we thus have to show that the following expression vanishes

\displaystyle({\rm i})

\displaystyle(1-\sigma)\Bigg[\int{\rm d}\phi\sum_{v,\nu}\pi(\phi)\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu|p_{h})e\partial_{f_{i}}f(\phi,v,\nu,0)

\displaystyle({\rm ii})

\displaystyle\qquad+\int{\rm d}\phi\sum_{v,\nu}\pi(\phi)\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu|p_{h})\sum_{j\in\partial_{\rm q}i}[e\partial_{f_{i}}S_{\rm q}^{i,j}(\phi)]_{+}\left(f(\phi,(j,e),\nu,0)-f(\phi,(i,e),\nu,0)\right)

\displaystyle({\rm iii})

\displaystyle\qquad+\int{\rm d}\phi\sum_{v,\nu}\pi(\phi)\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu|p_{h})[e\partial_{f_{i}}S_{\rm c}^{i}(\phi)]_{+}\left(f(\phi,(i,-e),\nu,0)-f(\phi,(i,e),\nu,0)\right)

\displaystyle({\rm iv})

\displaystyle\qquad+\int{\rm d}\phi\sum_{v,\nu}\pi(\phi)\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu|p_{h})\lambda_{\rm r}\sum_{v^{\prime}}\mu_{V}(v^{\prime})\Big(f(\phi,v^{\prime},\nu,0)-f(\phi,v,\nu,0)\Big)

\displaystyle({\rm v})

\displaystyle\qquad+\int{\rm d}\phi\sum_{v,\nu}\pi(\phi)\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu|p_{h})\lambda_{\rm w}\int{\rm d}\phi^{\prime}\sum_{\nu^{\prime}}\mathcal{P}(\phi,\nu\to\phi^{\prime},\nu^{\prime})\Big(f(\phi^{\prime},v,\nu^{\prime},1)-f(\phi,v,\nu,0)\Big)\Bigg]

\displaystyle({\rm vi})

\displaystyle+\sigma\Bigg[\int{\rm d}\phi\sum_{v,\nu}\pi(\phi)\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu|p_{h})\varepsilon\partial_{f_{\alpha}}f(\phi,v,\nu,1)

\displaystyle({\rm vii})

\displaystyle\qquad+\int{\rm d}\phi\sum_{v,\nu}\pi(\phi)\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu|p_{h})\sum_{\gamma\in\partial_{\rm q}\alpha\cap\partial p_{h}}[\varepsilon\partial_{f_{\alpha}}S_{\rm q}^{\alpha,\gamma}(\phi)]_{+}\left(f(\phi,v,(\gamma,\varepsilon),1)-f(\phi,v,(\alpha,\varepsilon),1)\right)

\displaystyle({\rm viii})

\displaystyle\qquad+\int{\rm d}\phi\sum_{v,\nu}\pi(\phi)\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu|p_{h})\sum_{\gamma\in\partial_{\rm q}\alpha\setminus\partial p_{h}}[\varepsilon\partial_{f_{\alpha}}S_{\rm q}^{\alpha,\gamma}(\phi)]_{+}\left(f(\phi,v,(\alpha,-\varepsilon),1)-f(\phi,v,(\alpha,\varepsilon),1)\right)

\displaystyle({\rm ix})

\displaystyle\qquad+\int{\rm d}\phi\sum_{v,\nu}\pi(\phi)\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu|p_{h})[\varepsilon\partial_{f_{\alpha}}S_{\rm c}^{\alpha}(\phi)]_{+}\left(f(\phi,v,(\alpha,-\varepsilon),1)-f(\phi,v,(\alpha,\varepsilon),1)\right)

\displaystyle({\rm x})

\displaystyle\qquad+\int{\rm d}\phi\sum_{v,\nu}\pi(\phi)\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu|p_{h})\lambda_{\rm w}\big(f(\phi,v,\nu,0)-f(\phi,v,\nu,1)\big)\Bigg].

(108)

As we will see, it turns out that
({\rm i})+({\rm ii})+({\rm iii})=({\rm iv})=({\rm v})+({\rm x})=({\rm vi})+({\rm vii})+({\rm viii})+({\rm ix})=0
. Let us begin by showing that
({\rm i})+({\rm ii})+({\rm iii})=0
. Using the pair-wise symmetry
\partial_{f_{i}}S^{i,j}_{\rm q}(\phi)=-\partial_{f_{j}}S^{i,j}_{\rm q}(\phi)
, the term
({\rm ii})
becomes

\displaystyle({\rm ii})=

\displaystyle\int{\rm d}\phi\sum_{\nu}\pi(\phi)\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu|p_{h})\sum_{e,i}\sum_{j\in\partial_{\rm q}i}[e\partial_{f_{i}}S_{\rm q}^{i,j}(\phi)]_{+}\left(f(\phi,(j,e),\nu,0)-f(\phi,(i,e),\nu,0)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int{\rm d}\phi\sum_{\nu}\pi(\phi)\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu|p_{h})\sum_{e}\left[\sum_{i,j\in\partial_{\rm q}i}[-e\partial_{f_{j}}S_{\rm q}^{i,j}(\phi)]_{+}f(\phi,(j,e),\nu,0)-\sum_{i,j\in\partial_{\rm q}i}[e\partial_{f_{i}}S_{\rm q}^{i,j}(\phi)]_{+}f(\phi,(i,e),\nu,0)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int{\rm d}\phi\sum_{\nu}\pi(\phi)\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu|p_{h})\sum_{e}\sum_{i,j\in\partial_{\rm q}i}\left([-e\partial_{f_{i}}S_{\rm q}^{i,j}(\phi)]_{+}-[e\partial_{f_{i}}S_{\rm q}^{i,j}(\phi)]_{+}\right)f(\phi,(i,e),\nu,0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int{\rm d}\phi\sum_{v,\nu}\pi(\phi)\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu|p_{h})\sum_{j\in\partial_{\rm q}i}e\partial_{f_{i}}S_{\rm q}^{i,j}(\phi)f(\phi,(i,e),\nu,0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int{\rm d}\phi\sum_{v,\nu}\pi(\phi)\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu|p_{h})e\partial_{f_{i}}S_{\rm q}(\phi)f(\phi,v,\nu,0),

(109)

where
S_{\rm q}(\phi)=\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}S_{\rm q}^{i,j}(\phi)
is the total quadratic action. The term
({\rm iii})
is computed as

\displaystyle({\rm iii})=

\displaystyle\int{\rm d}\phi\sum_{\nu}\pi(\phi)\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu|p_{h})\sum_{e,i}[e\partial_{f_{i}}S_{\rm c}^{i}(\phi)]_{+}\left(f(\phi,(i,-e),\nu,0)-f(\phi,(i,e),\nu,0)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int{\rm d}\phi\sum_{\nu}\pi(\phi)\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu|p_{h})\sum_{e,i}\left([-e\partial_{f_{i}}S_{\rm c}^{i}(\phi)]_{+}-[e\partial_{f_{i}}S_{\rm c}^{i}(\phi)]_{+}\right)f(\phi,(i,e),\nu,0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int{\rm d}\phi\sum_{v,\nu}\pi(\phi)\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu|p_{h})e\partial_{f_{i}}S_{\rm c}^{i}(\phi)f(\phi,(i,e),\nu,0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int{\rm d}\phi\sum_{v,\nu}\pi(\phi)\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu|p_{h})e\partial_{f_{i}}S_{\rm c}(\phi)f(\phi,v,\nu,0),

(110)

where
S_{\rm c}(\phi)=\sum_{i}S_{\rm c}^{i}(\phi)
is the total action of the cosine terms. Putting
({\rm i})+({\rm ii})+({\rm iii})
together and using
\partial_{f_{i}}\pi(\phi)=-\pi(\phi)\partial_{f_{i}}\big(S_{\rm q}(\phi)+S_{\rm c}(\phi)\big)
thus yields

\displaystyle({\rm i})+({\rm ii})+({\rm iii})=

\displaystyle\int{\rm d}\phi\sum_{v,\nu}\pi(\phi)\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu|p_{h})e\left[\partial_{f_{i}}f(\phi,v,\nu,0)-\partial_{f_{i}}S_{\rm q}(\phi)f(\phi,v,\nu,0).-\partial_{f_{i}}S_{\rm c}(\phi)f(\phi,v,\nu,0)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int{\rm d}\phi\sum_{v,\nu}\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu|p_{h})e\partial_{f_{i}}\left[\pi(\phi)f(\phi,v,\nu,0)\right]=0,

(111)

since
\pi(\phi)
vanishes when
f_{i}\to\pm\infty
(remember that we relaxed the constraint
f_{i}\in[1/2,1/2]
in Sec.
III.3
). Similar computations show that

\displaystyle({\rm vii})=

\displaystyle-\int{\rm d}\phi\sum_{v,\nu}\pi(\phi)\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu|p_{h})\sum_{\gamma\in\partial_{\rm q}\alpha\cap\partial p_{h}}\varepsilon\partial_{f_{\gamma}}S_{\rm q}^{\alpha,\gamma}(\phi)f(\phi,v,\nu,1)

\displaystyle({\rm viii})=

\displaystyle-\int{\rm d}\phi\sum_{v,\nu}\pi(\phi)\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu|p_{h})\sum_{\gamma\in\partial_{\rm q}\alpha\setminus\partial p_{h}}\varepsilon\partial_{f_{\gamma}}S_{\rm q}^{\alpha,\gamma}(\phi)f(\phi,v,\nu,1)

\displaystyle({\rm ix})=

\displaystyle-\int{\rm d}\phi\sum_{v,\nu}\pi(\phi)\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu|p_{h})\varepsilon\partial_{f_{\alpha}}S_{\rm c}(\phi)f(\phi,v,\nu,1).

(112)

from which one deduces
({\rm vi})+({\rm vii})+({\rm viii})+({\rm ix})=0
. Since
\mu_{V}(v)
is uniform, the refreshment term
({\rm iv})
is trivially found to vanish

\displaystyle({\rm iv})=

\displaystyle\int{\rm d}\phi\sum_{v,\nu}\pi(\phi)\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu|p_{h})\lambda_{\rm r}\sum_{v^{\prime}}\mu_{V}(v^{\prime})\Big(f(\phi,v^{\prime},\nu,0)-f(\phi,v,\nu,0)\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int{\rm d}\phi\sum_{\nu}\pi(\phi)\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu|p_{h})\lambda_{\rm r}\Big(\sum_{v^{\prime}}f(\phi,v^{\prime},\nu,0)-\sum_{v}f(\phi,v,\nu,0)\Big)=0,

(113)

and the only remaining terms are
({\rm v})+({\rm x})
. The term
({\rm v})
is first rewritten using the detailed balance and normalization conditions (
105
,
106
) on the transition matrix
\mathcal{P}
.

\displaystyle({\rm v})=

\displaystyle\lambda_{\rm w}\Big(\int{\rm d}\phi\,{\rm d}\phi^{\prime}\sum_{v,\nu,\nu^{\prime}}\pi(\phi)\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu|p_{h})\mathcal{P}(\phi,\nu\to\phi^{\prime},\nu^{\prime})f(\phi^{\prime},v,\nu^{\prime},1)-\int{\rm d}\phi\sum_{v,\nu}\pi(\phi)\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu|p_{h})f(\phi,v,\nu,0)\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{\rm w}\Big(\int{\rm d}\phi^{\prime}\sum_{v,\nu^{\prime}}\pi(\phi^{\prime})\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu^{\prime}|p_{h}^{\prime})f(\phi^{\prime},v,\nu^{\prime},1)-\int{\rm d}\phi\sum_{v,\nu}\pi(\phi)\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu|p_{h})f(\phi,v,\nu,0)\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{\rm w}\int{\rm d}\phi\sum_{v,\nu}\pi(\phi)\mu_{\mathcal{V}}(\nu|p_{h})\Big(f(\phi^{\prime},v,\nu^{\prime},1)-f(\phi,v,\nu,0)\Big).

(114)

This is nothing but
-({\rm x})
so
({\rm v})+({\rm x})=0
, which finishes the proof of the global balance condition for the SmoWo algorithm.

The detailed balance condition for the Wo algorithm can be checked using very similar arguments, starting from its generator

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}f(\phi,v)=

\displaystyle e\partial_{f_{i}}f(\phi,v)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j\in\partial_{\rm q}i}[e\partial_{f_{i}}S_{\rm q}^{i,j}(\phi)]_{+}\left(f(\phi,(j,e))-f(\phi,(i,e))\right)

\displaystyle+[e\partial_{f_{i}}S_{\rm c}^{i}(\phi)]_{+}\left(f(\phi,(i,-e))-f(\phi,(i,e))\right)

\displaystyle+\lambda_{\rm r}\sum_{v^{\prime}}\mu_{V}(v^{\prime})\Big(f(\phi,v^{\prime})-f(\phi,v)\Big)

\displaystyle+\lambda_{\rm w}\int{\rm d}\phi^{\prime}\mathcal{P}_{\rm w}(\phi\to\phi^{\prime})\Big(f(\phi^{\prime},v)-f(\phi,v)\Big),

(115)

where there is, of course, no more the lifting variables
\nu
and
\sigma
, and
{\cal P}_{\rm w}
is the transition matrix of the worm moves alone.

Appendix C
ECMC in practice

C.1
Computing the event times

This section computes the various event times associated to the quadratic interactions, the cosine interaction, the refreshment term, and the worm events. For a generic event triggered by a rate
\lambda_{k}(t)
, we compute the event time
t_{k}
using inversion sampling. One draws
r\sim{\rm ran}([0,1])
(the uniform distribution over
[0,1]
) and solves

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=\exp\left[-\int_{0}^{t_{k}}\lambda_{k}(t){\rm d}t\right].

(116)

Note that we need to draw a new variable
r
for each event rate. We now solve this equation for each event type.

Worm and refreshment events.

The easiest case to solve is that of the refreshment and worm events. Since their rates
\lambda_{\rm r/w}
are constant, one directly finds

\displaystyle t_{\rm r/w}=-\frac{\ln r}{\lambda_{\rm r/w}}.

(117)

Quadratic events.

Next, considering for instance the quadratic event between
i
and
i+\hat{x}
, the associated event rate, for
v=(e,i)
, is
[e\partial_{f_{i}(t)}S^{i,i+\hat{x}}_{\rm q}(t)]_{+}=[e\frac{\pi}{4K}(J^{\tau}_{p(i)}+f_{i}(t)-f_{i+\hat{x}})]_{+}
where
f_{i}(t)=f_{i}+et
captures the time evolution since the last event. Eq. (
116
) therefore becomes

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=\exp\left[-\int_{0}^{t_{\rm q}^{i,i+\hat{x}}}\left[e\frac{\pi}{4K}(J^{\tau}_{p(i)}+f_{i}+et-f_{i+\hat{x}})\right]_{+}{\rm d}t\right].

(118)

Defining
y_{\rm q}=e\left(f_{i}-f_{i+\hat{x}}+J_{p(i)}^{\tau}\right)
, the previous equation can be rewritten as

\displaystyle-\frac{4K}{\pi}\ln r

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t_{\rm q}^{i,i+\hat{x}}}[t+y_{\rm q}]_{+}{\rm d}t=\int_{y_{\rm q}}^{t_{\rm q}^{i,i+\hat{x}}+y_{\rm q}}[t]_{+}{\rm d}t.

(119)

The lower bound of the integral can be replaced by
[y_{\rm q}]_{+}
since it takes on non-zero values when
t>0
and
t>y_{\rm q}
. The upper bound must be positive since
\frac{\pi}{4K}[t_{\rm q}^{i,i+\hat{x}}+y_{\rm q}]_{+}
corresponds to the event rate which triggered the event. This allows to replace the integrand
[t]_{+}
by
t
and leads to

\displaystyle-\frac{4K}{\pi}\ln r

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(t_{\rm q}^{i,j}+y_{\rm q})^{2}-\frac{1}{2}[y_{\rm q}]_{+}^{2}.

(120)

which, keeping the solution
t^{i,i+\hat{x}}_{\rm q}>0
, gives

t_{\rm q}^{i,i+\hat{x}}=-y_{\rm q}+\sqrt{[y_{\rm q}]_{+}^{2}-\frac{8K}{\pi}\ln r}.

(121)

Cosine event

The cosine event time
t_{\rm c}^{i}
occurs with the rate
[e\partial_{f_{i}(t)}S_{\rm c}^{i}(t)]_{+}=[e2\pi g\sin(2\pi f_{i}(t))]_{+}
with
f_{i}(t)=f_{i}+et
. It is sampled by solving

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=\exp\left[-\int_{0}^{t_{\rm c}^{i}}[e2\pi g\sin(2\pi(f_{i}+et))]_{+}{\rm d}t\right]=\exp\left[-\int_{ef_{i}}^{ef_{i}(t_{\rm c})}2\pi g[\sin(2\pi x)]_{+}{\rm d}x\right].

(122)

To compute the integral, we decompose the initial and final field as
f_{i}=e(n+f_{\rm init.})
and
f_{i}(t_{\rm c}^{i})=e(m+f_{\rm fin.})
with
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
and
f_{\rm init.},f_{\rm fin.}\in[0,1[
. This leads to

\displaystyle-\frac{\ln r}{2\pi g}

\displaystyle=\int_{n+f_{\rm init.}}^{m+f_{\rm fin.}}[\sin(2\pi x)]_{+}{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{n+f_{\rm init.}}^{n}[\sin(2\pi x)]_{+}{\rm d}x+\int_{n}^{m}[\sin(2\pi x)]_{+}{\rm d}x+\int_{m}^{m+f_{\rm fin.}}[\sin(2\pi x)]_{+}{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{f_{\rm init.}}[\sin(2\pi x)]_{+}{\rm d}x+\frac{m-n}{\pi}+\int_{0}^{f_{\rm fin.}}[\sin(2\pi x)]_{+}{\rm d}x.

(123)

Since an event occurs at
t_{\rm c}^{i}
, we must have
[e2\pi g\sin(e2\pi f_{\rm fin.})]_{+}\neq 0
, i.e.
f_{\rm fin.}\in[0,1/2[
, so

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{f_{\rm fin.}}[\sin(2\pi x)]_{+}{\rm d}x=\int_{0}^{f_{\rm fin.}}\sin(2\pi x){\rm d}x=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left[1-\cos(2\pi f_{\rm fin.})\right].

(124)

For
f_{\rm init.}
, there is no similar condition so we write
f_{\rm init.}=(s_{\rm c}+z_{\rm c})/2
with
s_{\rm c}=\lfloor 2ef_{i}\rfloor
the half-integer part, and
z_{\rm c}=\{2ef_{i}\}
the rest. Treating
z_{c}
as a boolean variable, one writes

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{f_{\rm init.}}[\sin(2\pi x)]_{+}{\rm d}x=(1-s_{\rm c})\int_{0}^{z_{\rm c}/2}\sin(2\pi x){\rm d}x+s_{\rm c}\int_{0}^{1/2}\sin(2\pi x){\rm d}x=\frac{(1-s_{\rm c})}{2\pi}\left[1-\cos(\pi z_{\rm c})\right]+\frac{s_{\rm c}}{\pi}.

(125)

Plugging Eqs. (
124
,
125
) into Eq. (
C.1
), one arrives at

\displaystyle y_{\rm c}

\displaystyle=m-n+\frac{1-\cos(2\pi f_{\rm fin.})}{2}.

(126)

where we have defined

y_{\rm c}=-\frac{\ln r}{2g}+s_{\rm c}+(1-s_{\rm c})\frac{1-\cos(\pi z_{\rm c})}{2},

(127)

which only depends on quantities at
t=0
and is thus known. Taking the integer part and the fractional part of the previous expression yields

\displaystyle\lfloor y_{\rm c}\rfloor

\displaystyle=m-n,

(128)

\displaystyle\{y_{\rm c}\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\cos(2\pi f_{\rm fin.})}{2}\Rightarrow f_{\rm fin.}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\arccos(1-2\{y_{\rm c}\}).

(129)

The event time
t_{\rm c}^{i}
is finally given by

\displaystyle t_{\rm c}^{i}=e(f_{i}(t_{\rm c})-f_{i})=m-n+f_{\rm fin.}-f_{\rm init.}=\lfloor y_{\rm c}\rfloor+\frac{1}{2\pi}\arccos(1-2\{y_{\rm c}\})-\{ef_{i}\}.
