A GNAI1 Pathogenic Variant Mimicking Cerebral Palsy: Expanding the Phenotypic Spectrum of GNAI1‐Associated Neurodevelopmental Disorder
Authors: Marco Antônio Veloso de Albuquerque, Fernando Kok
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNovel genes are increasingly associated with neurodevelopmental disorders featuring developmental delay, epilepsy, and behavioral abnormalities. We report a child with profound motor and speech delay, hypotonia, dystonia, and spasticity mimicking cerebral palsy. Molecular testing revealed a pathogenic GNAI1 variant, expanding the phenotypic spectrum of this rare condition.

Introduction

With greater availability and access to genetic testing, there has been a significant increase in the identification of genes associated with neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs)—a heterogeneous group of early‐onset conditions whose symptoms vary but commonly include global developmental delay, epilepsy, and features of the autism spectrum [1, 2]. Among the emerging genetic causes, mutations in the GNAI1 gene, located in 7q21.11 which encodes the G protein subunit alpha‐i1, have been recently described in cohorts of individuals presenting with a syndromic form of NDD [3, 4, 5].

GNAI1‐related NDD is most often characterized by severe developmental delay, axial hypotonia, impaired speech, and behavioral abnormalities (NEDHISB). About two‐thirds of patients have early‐onset seizures that range from intractable to self‐limiting. Mild dysmorphic features are also commonly observed in individuals with pathogenic variants in GNAI1 [3, 4, 5].

Here, we report the case of a patient harboring a pathogenic GNAI1 mutation, whose clinical phenotype closely mimicked that of cerebral palsy (CP). This report contributes to the growing understanding of the phenotypic spectrum associated with this novel and rare genetic condition.

Case History and Examination

A 2‐year and 6‐month‐old boy was referred for evaluation due to global developmental delay. He is the first child of a 39‐year‐old mother, conceived naturally in a planned pregnancy. Prenatal care was unremarkable until the diagnosis of placenta previa. At 35 weeks and 4 days of gestation, an emergency cesarean section was performed due to vaginal bleeding. The newborn was delivered in good condition, with Apgar scores of 9/9, a length of 44.5 cm, and a birth weight of 2285 g.

In the first hours of life, he developed tachypnea requiring CPAP support, without the need for endotracheal intubation. He remained in the neonatal ICU for 25 days, during which he experienced three episodes of apnea of prematurity. He also presented neonatal jaundice, successfully treated with 1 day of phototherapy. Screening tests, including transfontanellar ultrasound, automated brainstem response (ABR), and newborn heel prick tests, were normal.

Already in the first year of life, the patient showed significant delays in both motor and language milestones. Head control was achieved only at 8 months of age, and he is currently able to sit briefly without support. He has not acquired the ability to stand, walk, or speak. Physical examination revealed some dysmorphic features including tapered fingers, low‐set ears, and the appearance of an open mouth with a thin upper lip. Neurological examination showed good eye contact and normal ocular movements. Speech is completely absent. Other cranial nerves were normal. The patient showed reduced spontaneous movements and adopted a frog‐like resting posture. There was marked axial hypotonia. Distal hypertonia in the upper and lower limbs was observed, associated with fluctuated contorted movements that were worsened by stimulation—suggestive of dystonia. Muscle strength was grade IV in both upper and lower limbs, and deep tendon reflexes were brisk in all four limbs.

No seizures had been reported up to the most recent evaluation. EEG and polysomnography were normal. Brain MRI demonstrated mild prominence of frontal cerebrospinal fluid spaces and periventricular hyperintensities, findings that are nonspecific but may suggest a perinatal hypoxic–ischemic insult.

Differential Diagnosis

The clinical phenotype initially suggested a chronic non‐progressive encephalopathy, particularly dystonic‐spastic CP. However, the absence of perinatal injury, disproportionate neurological deficits, and inconclusive imaging/EEG raised suspicion of a genetic disorder.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

A genetic panel for movement disorders returned a negative result. However, whole‐exome sequencing identified a heterozygous de novo GNAI1 variant chr7:80,135,278 G>C (c.118G>C—ENST00000649796), located within the GTP‐binding domain (CADD score 28.9), which promotes substitution of glycine at codon 40 for arginine (p.Gly40Arg).

Discussion

This report presents clinical features that strongly resembled a chronic non‐progressive encephalopathy, such as dystonic‐spastic cerebral palsy—an association not previously described in patients with GNAI1‐related neurodevelopmental disorder (NDD). The absence of a clear perinatal insult and the disproportion between the rich neurological findings and the relatively mild abnormalities on EEG, neuroimaging, and metabolic workup prompted further investigation into a possible genetic etiology (Figure 1).

Diagnostic workflow in the GNAI1 case. The patient presented with developmental delay and dystonia, initially raising suspicion of cerebral palsy. Normal MRI and EEG findings prompted genetic investigation, which identified a pathogenic GNAI1.

Patients with pathogenic GNAI1 mutations are typically reported to present with delayed motor milestones, severe hypotonia, absence of speech and behavioral abnormalities [2, 3]. Another striking feature found in patients is epilepsy and complete absence of speech. Interestingly, while our patient exhibited significant motor delay, hypotonia and absence of speech, no seizures or behavioral abnormalities were reported up to the time of the last evaluation.

The heterozygous GNAI1 variant identified in our patient (c.118G>A; p.Gly40Arg) has been previously reported in independent studies [3, 5]. In these reports, affected individuals consistently presented with global developmental delay, hypotonia and epilepsy. The absence of speech was commonly present. Our case expands the known phenotypic spectrum by demonstrating dystonia and spasticity mimicking cerebral palsy, without seizures, despite harboring the same recurrent variant (Table 1).

The identification of a pathogenic GNAI1 mutation in this patient not only establishes a definitive diagnosis but also has implications for prognosis and potential inclusion in future clinical trials targeting this gene.

Conclusion

Our case highlights the expanding clinical spectrum of GNAI1‐associated neurodevelopmental disorders and reinforces the importance of whole‐exome sequencing in the diagnostic evaluation of early‐onset developmental syndromes, particularly those with complex movement abnormalities and no clear acquired etiology. By underlining the absence of speech as a consistent feature, our report adds to the limited but growing body of literature on GNAI1 mutations and may support future efforts in refining genotype–phenotype correlations.
