Th9 cells at the intersection of IBD and colorectal cancer: a context-dependent factor in carcinogenesis
Authors: Niloufar Afrough, Amin Soltani, Maryam Khosravi, Negin Yadolahi, Ali Khodadadi, Mojtaba Shohan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The differentiation of naïve T cells into the Th9 phenotype is driven by the synergistic effects of IL-4 and TGF-β, leading to the secretion of their hallmark cytokine, interleukin-9 (IL-9). Known for their critical roles in mucosal immunopathology, Th9 cells are particularly prominent in the gastrointestinal tract, where they contribute to autoimmunity, inflammation, and tissue damage. Recent studies have highlighted the paradoxical role of Th9 cells in colorectal carcinogenesis, revealing that their function is highly context-dependent. In the chronically inflamed microenvironment of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), Th9 cells exhibit pro-tumorigenic functions. Th9 cells drive the transition to colitis-associated cancer (CAC) by impairing intestinal barrier integrity and promoting epithelial cell survival through PU.1/IL-6/STAT3 signaling and direct oncogene upregulation. Conversely, in sporadic colorectal cancer (CRC), Th9-derived IL-9 exerts anti-tumorigenic effects by enhancing immune surveillance, specifically through the recruitment and activation of mast cells and CD8 + cytotoxic T lymphocytes. Given the complex and often contradictory reports in the literature, this review provides an in-depth analysis of the distinct molecular mechanisms by which Th9 cells shape the tumor microenvironment. By explicitly distinguishing between inflammation-driven carcinogenesis (CAC) and sporadic CRC, this article aims to reconcile current literature and discover emerging therapeutic strategies, such as targeted metabolic reprogramming, to selectively suppress pathogenic Th9 cells without compromising systemic anti-tumor immunity.

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) represents a group of chronic, relapsing inflammatory disorders of the gastrointestinal tract, comprising ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CD). Clinically, these conditions are characterized by severe symptoms including abdominal pain, diarrhea, and rectal bleeding, driven by a dysregulated mucosal immune response against intestinal microbiota in genetically susceptible individuals [1–3]. While the hallmark of IBD is immunopathology mediated by effector T cell subsets, long-standing mucosal inflammation significantly increases the risk of developing colitis-associated cancer (CAC) [4, 5]. Understanding the immunological bridges between chronic inflammation and malignant transformation is therefore critical for developing new therapeutic interventions.

In the late 2000 s, Th9 cells, a subset of CD4 + T cells predominantly secreting the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-9, were first identified [1]. Briefly, Th9 differentiation is driven by the synergistic effects of IL-4 and TGF-β, which induce key transcription factors including STAT6, IRF4, BATF, and the Th9-master regulator PU.1. These factors cooperate to remodel the Il9 locus epigenetically, although Th9 cells retain a degree of plasticity compared to other T helper subsets. Th9 cells are pleiotropic, influencing diverse immune cell types, including lymphocytes, mast cells, and antigen presenting cells (APCs) as well as non-hematopoietic cells, such as epithelial cells and tumor cells [6], and have been implicated in the pathogenesis of allergic inflammation and autoimmune disorders, particularly multiple sclerosis and IBD [7, 8]. Recent studies have highlighted the paradoxical role of Th9 cells in malignancy, primarily through IL-9 secretion.

Depending on the tissue context and tumor microenvironment, these cells can exhibit either pro-tumorigenic or anti-tumorigenic activities [9–11]. In the specific context of the gastrointestinal tract, Th9 cells are known to disrupt intestinal barrier integrity by altering tight junction (TJ) proteins expression, by contributing the chronic inflammation that promotes colitis [12–14]. However, their contribution to the transition from this chronic inflammation to colitis-associated cancer (CAC) remains a subject of intense debate.

While some studies suggest that IL-9 promotes tumor growth by inducing DNA damage and tissue disruption [15, 16], others indicate that it may enhance anti-tumor immune surveillance [17]. Given the conflicting evidence, a comprehensive understanding and reconciliation of these divergent observations are essential for leveraging Th9 cells as therapeutic targets in the management of CRC. Therefore, we aimed to review the latest findings on this topic in order to make a more clarified insight that could be helpful for designing future studies.

IL-9: the most prominent indicator of Th9 footprint

IL-9 is a multifunctional cytokine, originally emerged from murine helper T cell clones in 1980. Initially, it was identified as a T cell growth factor, named P40, despite its functional and structural differences from lymphocyte growth factors such as IL-2 and IL-4 [18, 19]. This glycoprotein is composed of 144 amino acids with a signal peptide of 18 amino acids, encoded on chromosome 5 in human and chromosome 13 in mouse. Human and murine IL-9 genes share similar structures with 5 exons and 4 introns with 56 to 74% homology in the exons [20, 21].

As Fig. 1 shows, IL-9 binds to a heterodimeric receptor complex composed of a specific chain (IL-9Rα) and belongs to the hematopoietic receptor superfamily and a common gamma-chain receptor, which is shared among the receptors for other interleukins such as IL-2, IL-4, IL-7, IL-15, and IL-21 [22, 23]. IL-9Rα includes 468 and 521 amino acids, with a 53% identity in mouse and human, respectively. Since IL-9R lacks tyrosine kinase activity, IL-9 triggers a signal by activating JAK which is bound to the Box 1 motif, a proline-rich region close to the transmembrane domain [23]. Specifically, JAK1 is activated for the IL-9Rα and JAK3 for the gamma-chain receptor, leading to the activation of the STAT, IRS-PI3Ks, and MAPK pathways, which are involved in IL-9 signaling [23–27]. Helper T lymphocytes are considered as the major sources of IL-9 [28, 29]. The first expression of IL-9 in a murine model of leishmaniosis with a Th2-biased immune response defined it as a Th2 cytokine [30]. Later on, generating Th9 cells in vitro with the ability to produce IL-9, unlike IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13, suggested them as a distinct helper T cell subset [31, 32]. Temporary expression of IL-9 against gastrointestinal worm infection showed that Th9 cells were also observed in vivo [33].

In vitro and in vivo secretion of IL-9 by Th17 cells and regulatory T cells also have been observed [34–39]. Follicular helper T cells are reported to secrete IL-9, promoting the differentiation of murine memory B cells [40]. Several reports suggested that type 2 innate lymphoid cells enhance allergic lung inflammation in allergic patients and also promote helminth immunity in both mice and humans [33, 41–43]. Mucosal mast cells are another source of IL-9, enhancing intestinal mastocytosis in food allergy [44, 45].

IL-9 impacts the function of various cell types, contributing to infection, allergic inflammation, and autoimmunity. Interestingly, IL-9 functions differently across various experimental models and diseases. For example, IL-9 increases the suppressive functions of regulatory T (Treg) cells, which can lead to inducing the resolution of inflammation in patients with rheumatoid arthritis [37, 46]. Additionally, the absence of IL-9 weakens the suppressive activity of Treg cells, resulting in rejection of lung tumor [46]. IL-9 promotes CD8+ T cell (CTLs) proliferation and cytolytic functions, along with lowering their exhaustion markers like PD-1 in patients with breast cancer and CRC [47, 48]. Several studies have reported that IL-9 affect germinal center B cells. Its role is to increase IgE and IgG production by inducing STAT3 and STAT5 activation [49–52]. Additionally, it promotes CD40-dependent proliferation and plasma cell differentiation of B memory cells, enhancing the activation of humoral recall responses [49].

Several studies report that IL-9 shows an anti-inflammatory function by inhibiting oxidative burst responses and cytokine release of both monocytes and macrophages [53–55]. For instance, in a lung cancer model, macrophages stimulated by IL-9 were more cytotoxic. This indicates that IL-9 can enhance the polarization and proliferation of M1 macrophages and inhibit metastasis [56]. Furthermore, in asthmatic patients, an increased level of IL-8 expression was observed in PMNs stimulated by IL-9, contributing to the amplification of the inflammatory response [57].

IL-9 also increases IL-5Rα expression, thus enhancing IL-5-mediated differentiation and maturation, showing its potential to potentiate airway and tissue eosinophilia [58]. In asthmatic patients, IL-9 induces the production of the CCL-11/Eotaxin-1 chemokine, contributing to eosinophilic inflammation [59]. IL-9 impairs intestinal barrier function by affecting epithelial cells and serves a crucial role in the pathogenesis of IBD [60]. By administering IL-9, the expression of mucin-related genes MUC2 and MUC5AC as well as mucus production increased in airway epithelial cells [61].

In type 2 inflammatory conditions like atopic dermatitis, the number of IL-9 receptors increases on epidermal keratinocytes, making them more responsive to IL-9. Thus, IL-9 regulates their expansion, migration, and cytokine production [62–66]. Additionally, IL-9 stimulates human primary keratinocytes (HPKs) by enhancing their survival, promoting their proliferation, and increasing secretion of cytokines such as IL-8 and VEGF [62–66]. Moreover, treatment with recombinant IL-9 regulates the actomyosin cytoskeleton and inhibits IFN-γ and IL-17A-induced HPK migratory potential [62].

Transcriptional regulation of Th9 cells

The differentiation of any T helper subset is characterized by preferential or enriched expression of a large number of genes, including the signature cytokine gene. Epigenetic changes that occur at the target loci, impact the ability of these genes to be induced by subsequent antigen receptor stimulation. This was initially shown in Th1 and Th2 cells with changes in DNase hypersensitivity, DNA methylation, and histone modifications. Lasting epigenetic modifications occur over a period of 5–14 days [67–70]. Epigenetic changes can be reversible or irreversible. If they are irreversible, helper T cell will be committed to a particular phenotype. In contrast, if they are reversible, the T cell will be plastic and can obtain new phenotype. IL-9 gene is regulated by three conserved non-coding sequences (CNS-1, CNS-6, and CNS-2) located beside. CNS-1 and CNS-6 are conserved across multiple species, while CNS-2 is specifically conserved between humans and mice [71, 72].

These elements are key binding sites for Th9 transcription factors, even though CNS-2 diverges in other species. STAT6 and STAT5 are critical enhancers of IL-9 production [73, 74]. STAT6, activated by IL-4, regulates transcription factors like BATF and IRF4, binding directly to the IL-9 CNS1 region [73, 74]. However, the exact function of STAT6 during the acute activation of IL-9 production remains unclear. IL-2 and TSLP activate STAT5, which directly binds to the IL-9 promoter, and enhance IRF4 expression. This pathway is negatively controlled by factors like BCL6 and TL1A, with STAT3 affecting IL-2 production [75, 76]. Additionally, the role of STAT1 is context-dependent, either inhibiting IL-9 production in response to IFN-γ and IL-27 or promoting it with IRF1 in response to IL-1β [76, 77].

The Smad proteins Smad2, Smad3, and Smad4 are essential for Th9 development, operating as a part of the TGF-β signaling pathway [78, 79]. The absence of Smad proteins leads to a severe reduction in IL-9 production, especially when the T cell lacks both Smad2 and Smad3 [78]. Although their function is still not completely known, it was found that they can bind to the IL-9 promoter and to conserved non-coding regions [78, 79]. They can also modulate histone acetylation and H3K4 trimethylation [78]. Interestingly, a severe loss of IL-9 production was observed in the Smad2/Smad3 compound mutant mice with decreased histone acetylation [78]. Additionally, the absence of Smad2/Smad3 resulted in higher expression of T-bet and Rorγ, which are both IL-9 repressors [73, 78]. Moreover, Smad3 also interacts with RBP-Jκ and the Notch intracellular domain (NICD) to control the IL-9 gene and affecting epigenetic modifications [80].

TAK1, a kinase activated by TGF-β, mediates Smad-independent signaling and activates MAPK and some other pathways that regulate IL-9. It is not completely clear if MAPK pathways are important for IL-9 production. TAK1 regulates an E box transcription inhibitor named Id3, which repress the production of IL-9 [81]. TAK1 inhibition or genetic deletion reduces IL-9 production and increases Id3 levels. If Id3 is absent, increased binding of E2A and GATA3 at the IL-9 promoter will be observed [81]. SIRT1 is a deacetylase, which repress mTOR and HIF1α to affect Th9 differentiation [82]. SIRT1 regulation of IL-9 is linked to HIF1α while the involvement of HIF2α is not clear [83]. PU.1 and ETV5 are the only ETS proteins which play a role in IL-9 production.

PU.1 is controlled by TGFβ while ETV5 requires IL-4/STAT6 signaling. PU.1 plays a critical role in IL-9 production in Th9 cells. It directly binds to IL-9 promoter, recruits histone acetyltransferases like Gcn5, and regulates chromatin remodeling [84]. If the PU.1 is absent, the expression of IL-9 will be reduced and Gcn5 association at the IL-9 locus will be impaired.

ETV5 is another ETS factor, which impacts IL-9 production by binding to different regions of IL-9 in comparison with PU.1 [85]. Interestingly, their activity is not dependent on each other and they both have overlapping but different roles in allergic inflammation [71, 85]. BATF and IRF4 are crucial trans-factors contributing to IL-9 production in Th9 cells [74, 86]. If either of them is deficient, IL-9 production and allergic inflammation will be reduced.

Both BATF and IRF4 bind to the IL-9 promoter and are activated as a result of IL-4/STAT6 signaling [73, 74]. They work cooperatively, and the absence of each factor can reduce the binding of the other to the promoter. Additionally, IRF4 interacts with Smad2/3 and links to IL-4 and TGFβ signaling, which leads to boosting IL-9 production [78]. Importantly, Smad3 cannot induce IL-9 production in IRF4-deficient T cells. These show significant cross-talk in the regulation of IL-9 [78]. NF-κB is crucial for IL-9 production in Th9 cells, interacting with both T cell receptors and TNF superfamily members [87]. NFAT1 is necessary for NF-κB p65 binding to the IL-9 promoter, by making the chromatin accessible. OX40, a member of TNF superfamily, enhances IL-9 production by activating the TRAF6/NF-κB RelB-p52 pathway, which binds directly to the IL-9 promoter. OX40 function is dependent on STAT6, while being independent of PU.1.

Several components of NF-κB play roles in IL-9 production, including direct gene activation, repression of repressors, and activation of STAT6 [88–90]. Th9 cells are not stable in vitro. They stop secreting IL-9 and gain other cytokine-secreting phenotypes in the presence of various differentiation signals after 3 days in vitro [91]. This instability and cellular plasticity make it difficult to identify the transcription factors for maintaining Th9 phenotype. However, it seems like negative regulators such as STAT3, BCL6, and Foxp3 promote instability [86, 92]. Interestingly, in vitro-derived Th9 cells can still secrete IL-9 in vivo and preserve their phenotype in living organism. This instability might be linked to an evolutionary purpose to prevent the effect of chronic production of IL-9. Other genes in Th9 cells are regulated by distinct transcription factors. STAT6 and BATF are necessary for expression of a large portion of the genes, while IRF4 impacts a subset of these genes. On the other hand, PU.1 regulate a small portion of these genes [74].

Th9 cells: inflammation-associated carcinogenesis in gut mucosa

Chronic inflammation is a well-established driver of carcinogenesis in IBD. While the comprehensive role of Th9 cells and IL-9 in the immunopathogenesis of ulcerative colitis (UC) has been extensively detailed in our previous review [93], the current focus is on how these inflammatory mechanisms precipitate malignant transformation. Beyond the complex inflammatory network underlying IBD immunopathogenesis, IL-9 has emerged as a critical mediator, particularly in UC [94, 95]. Mechanistically, IL-9 contributes to mucosal pathology by disrupting intestinal barrier integrity. Overexpression of IL-9 modifies tight junction proteins, specifically upregulating pore-forming claudin-2 while downregulating zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1), thereby increasing intestinal permeability [96]. Recent reviews emphasize that this IL-9-mediated impairment of mucosal healing is a critical first hit that perpetuates the chronic inflammatory state necessary for neoplastic transformation [97].

This barrier dysfunction is further intensified by the accumulation of Th9 cells in the inflamed mucosa. Studies in both human UC patients and murine models have confirmed that high levels of IL-9 and its master transcription factor, PU.1, correlate with disease severity and inflammation scores [3, 98, 99]. Furthermore, IL-9 signaling has been shown to activate the STAT3 pathway in colonic epithelial cells, a key oncogenic driver that links chronic inflammation to tumor development [100]. Despite its role in inflammation, evidence suggests that IL-9 can exert anti-tumor effects in specific contexts. In sporadic colon cancer models, IL-9 overexpression has been linked to reduced tumor growth and prolonged survival [101]. Clinically, analysis of colon carcinoma tissues reveals that reduced IL-9 expression correlates with advanced tumor node metastasis (TNM) stages, Dukes staging, and lymph node metastasis.

This protective effect is primarily mediated by the recruitment and activation of adaptive immune cells. IL-9 enhances the infiltration of CD4 + and CD8 + T cells into the tumor microenvironment and promotes dendritic cell survival via the CCL20-CCR6 axis [102, 103]. Additionally, IL-1β promotes IL-21 production in Th9 cells, leading to IFN-γ production from NK cells and CD8 + T cells.

Th9 cells contribute to tumor elimination by activating tumor-infiltrating mast cells and enhancing the cytotoxicity of CD8 + T cells through granzyme B production [47, 104–106]. Furthermore, IL-9 secretion in tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) is associated with high PD-1 expression, suggesting that blocking the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway could synergistically enhance Th9 cell antitumor activity [47]. Recently, it was also revealed that innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s) secrete IL-9, which further activates CD8 + T cells to target and eliminate cancer cells, suggesting a robust anti-tumor immunity in early-stage disease [107].

In contrast to these anti-tumor effects, recent findings strongly implicate Th9 cells as drivers of the transition from colitis to cancer (CAC). Recent clinical data identifies a progressive accumulation of Th9 cells along the inflammation-dysplasia-carcinoma sequence. Specifically, Th9 cell density is significantly higher in colonic dysplastic lesions compared to non-dysplastic UC tissues, suggesting that Th9 cells may drive the critical transition from chronic inflammation to early malignancy [108].

Mechanistically, this process is driven by the transcription factor PU.1, which regulates IL-6 secretion in tumor-infiltrating T cells. This Th9-derived IL-6 acts on intestinal epithelial cells to trigger STAT3 activation, promoting cell survival and proliferation in the inflammatory microenvironment [14]. Furthermore, IL-9 acts as a direct growth factor for colonic epithelial cells and its overexpression upregulates the oncogenes c-Myc and Cyclin D1, leading to enhanced proliferation independent of immune cells [109, 110].

Beyond direct cellular signaling, IL-9 impairs gut barrier function, allowing bacterial translocation that alters the microbiota-immunity axis. Specifically, IL-9 shows a positive association with Prevotella spp., promoting a tumor-supportive environment, while negatively correlating with protective Bacteroides spp. [111]. This dual signaling confirms the potent pro-tumorigenic role of IL-9 in the inflamed gut.

Understanding this duality is essential for therapeutic development. While neutralizing IL-9 may be beneficial in UC and CAC, it could potentially hinder anti-tumor immunity in other contexts. Emerging therapeutic strategies are now shifting from simple cytokine neutralization to metabolic reprogramming. Recent evidence suggests that Th9 differentiation relies heavily on mTOR-driven aerobic glycolysis. Conclusion

Th9 cells are recognized as critical participants in the pathogenesis of inflammation-associated carcinogenesis, particularly in the transformation of IBD into colorectal cancer. However, their precise function underscores a highly context-dependent paradigm. In the pathological microenvironment of chronic colitis, IL-9 acts as a pro-tumorigenic driver by disrupting tight junction proteins (upregulating pore-forming claudin-2 and downregulating ZO-1), impairing mucosal repair, and fueling oncogenic proliferation via the PU.1/IL-6/STAT3 signaling axis.

Conversely, in the context of sporadic colorectal cancer (CRC), Th9 cells emerge as potent enhancers of anti-tumor immunity. They orchestrate the recruitment and activation of CD8 + T cells, dendritic cells, and mast cells. Clinically, this protective function is evident, as high levels of IL-9 in early-stage CRC are linked to better survival rates, whereas decreased expression in advanced stages is associated with a poorer prognosis. Overall, IL-9 and Th9 cells play a context-dependent dual role in carcinogenesis. While neutralizing IL-9 may alleviate IBD symptoms and halt colitis-associated cancer (CAC) progression, it could inadvertently impair natural anti-tumor surveillance in other contexts. Therefore, future therapeutic interventions must move beyond systemic cytokine blockade. Selectively targeting downstream signaling pathways, such as the STAT3/SOCS3 axis or transcription factors like PU.1, offers significant therapeutic potential. Furthermore, exploiting the metabolic vulnerabilities of pathogenic Th9 cells, such as targeting mTOR-driven aerobic glycolysis, represents a highly promising frontier. Ultimately, a nuanced understanding of Th9 cell plasticity and their microenvironmental triggers is essential to safely harness their therapeutic potential without compromising systemic anti-tumor immunity.
