High-Order Splitting of Non-Unitary Operators on Quantum Computers
Peter Brearley, Philipp Pfeffer

Abstract
We present a high-order splitting method for simulating non-unitary dynamics by sequential real- and imaginary-time Hamiltonian evolutions. Complex-coefficient splitting methods with positive real parts are chosen for stable integration in a quantum circuit, avoiding the unstable, norm-amplifying negative steps that arise from real-coefficient splitting at high orders. The method is most beneficial for dynamics that naturally separate into unitary and dissipative components, with broad applications across science and engineering. These systems frequently admit compact spectral representations of the split operators, which we demonstrate by deriving efficient quantum circuits for simulating the damped-wave equation with up to sixth-order accuracy in time. A single sixth-order step in three dimensions on 35 trillion cells requires 1,562 CNOT gates, which can be executed within the coherence time of modern quantum processors.



I
Introduction

Quantum computers are naturally suited to simulating unitary dynamical systems, such as a quantum wavefunction evolving under a Hamiltonian. However, the vast majority of systems are dissipative, with irreversible losses due to friction, diffusion, or boundary flux. This extends beyond classical physics to the development of quantum technologies, such as computing and sensing. Qubits cannot be perfectly isolated from their environment, resulting in decoherence that motivates the study of open quantum systems to understand and reduce these effects
21
. In quantum sensing, this interaction is the very mechanism by which external fields are imprinted on the sensor’s quantum state
13
, but occurs alongside other unwanted interactions.

In classical physics, reversible propagation mechanisms for a scalar field
\phi
are pure advection by an incompressible velocity field
\vec{u}
, or wave propagation in a homogeneous, lossless medium at speed
c
, which are governed by
\partial_{t}\phi+\vec{u}\cdot\nabla\phi=0
and
\partial_{t}^{2}\phi=c^{2}\nabla^{2}\phi
, respectively. When these partial differential equations (PDEs) are discretized in space, they can be formulated as Schödinger equations and solved on quantum computers by Hamiltonian simulation
7
;
12
.

For real-world scalar transport processes, diffusion is invariably present, so they are more accurately described by the advection-diffusion equation,

\partial_{t}\phi+\vec{u}\cdot\nabla\phi=D\nabla^{2}\phi,

(1)

assuming a constant diffusivity
D
. Mechanical wave propagation is not truly lossless either. Surface water waves, audible sound in air, sonar in water, ultrasound in solids, or seismic waves in the Earth’s crust all experience losses due to friction or scattering from inhomogeneous media
16
. Such losses can be modelled by a linear damping coefficient
\gamma
in the damped-wave equation,

\partial_{t}^{2}\phi+\gamma\,\partial_{t}\phi=c^{2}\nabla^{2}\phi,

(2)

to varying degrees of accuracy. Equations (
1
) and (
2
) both consist of a unitary propagation mechanism and a dissipative loss mechanism, reflecting a general pattern seen across many physical systems where reversible and irreversible processes act together.

When time-evolving PDEs, such as Eqs. (
1
) and (
2
), are discretized in space with
N
grid points, for example by the finite difference or Fourier spectral methods, linear PDEs become the
N
-dimensional system of ordinary differential equations (ODEs),

\frac{d\vec{\phi}}{dt}=M\vec{\phi}.

(3)

Purely dissipative dynamics, such as damping or diffusion, are described by a Hermitian generator
M=M^{\dagger}
with non-positive eigenvalues, while unitary propagative dynamics, such as advection or wave propagation, are described by an anti-Hermitian generator
M=-M^{\dagger}
. Therefore, in simultaneous propagation-dissipation systems,
M=H_{1}+iH_{2}
naturally separates into a Hermitian term
H_{1}=H_{1}^{\dagger}
and an anti-Hermitian term
iH_{2}
, where
H_{2}=H_{2}^{\dagger}
. In fact, an arbitrary generator
M
can be separated into Hermitian and anti-Hermitian components by defining
H_{1}=(M+M^{\dagger})/2
and
iH_{2}=(M-M^{\dagger})/2

1
;
19
. This extends to systems of inhomogeneous ODEs of the form
d_{t}\vec{\phi}=M\vec{\phi}+\vec{b}
, which can be homogenized by a simple block-matrix construction
19
.

Propagative or dissipative linear PDEs frequently admit compact, efficient quantum circuits by diagonalization with quantum spectral transforms
24
, such as the quantum Fourier transform (QFT) for periodic functions. While the unitary dynamics of Hamiltonian simulation is a natural application of quantum computers, the simulation of purely dissipative dynamics has been extensively studied in the context of imaginary time evolution and block-encoding methods such as qubitization
23
and the quantum singular value transform (QSVT)
14
. Therefore, separating the problem into its Hermitian and anti-Hermitian components, then implementing them sequentially by operator splitting, is a practical approach to the quantum simulation of non-unitary dynamics.

The concept of operator splitting is widely regarded by the quantum computing community as a practical means of simulating unitary dynamics
22
with up to high orders of accuracy
26
. However, high-order operator splitting for simulating general non-unitary dynamics on quantum computers has yet to be demonstrated.
27

27
simulated scalar transport by Eq. (
1
) in two-dimensional laminar shear flows in fluid dynamics, where the non-commuting advection and diffusion operators were implemented sequentially with second-order accuracy
28
, which is the natural limit for stable integration under positive-only coefficients
6
. We overcome this limit by using complex coefficients
a_{i}
satisfying
\Re(a_{i})>0
for the dissipative evolution and real coefficients
b_{i}>0
for the unitary evolution. The coefficients ensure that the unitary and dissipative systems are both retained in the real-time evolution, while the dissipative system evolving in imaginary time becomes unitary. The success probability is therefore also retained, as the only additional computational burden is the identification of the unitary circuit to implement the dissipative evolution in imaginary time. This method improves the accuracy up to sixth order using the highest-order qualifying scheme available in the literature
3
.

II
High-order operator splitting

The system of ODEs in Eq. (
3
) has the solution
\vec{\phi}(t)=e^{Mt}\vec{\phi}(0)
, which can equivalently be written as
\vec{\phi}(t)=e^{(H_{1}+iH_{2})t}\vec{\phi}(0)
by decomposing
M
into its Hermitian and anti-Hermitian components. The sequential evolution
e^{(H_{1}+iH_{2})t}=e^{H_{1}t}e^{iH_{2}t}
only holds if
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
commute, i.e.
[H_{1},H_{2}]=0
, where
[H_{1},H_{2}]=H_{1}H_{2}-H_{2}H_{1}
. This occurs for evolutions that can be simultaneously diagonalized in the same basis, which often also admit analytical solutions
27
. In general, operator splitting is required to simulate
e^{(H_{1}+iH_{2})t}
.

The first-order Lie-Trotter splitting is defined as

e^{(H_{1}+iH_{2})t}=\left(e^{H_{1}\Delta t}e^{iH_{2}\Delta t}\right)^{T}+O(\Delta t),

(4)

and the second-order Strang splitting is defined as

\displaystyle e^{(H_{1}+iH_{2})t}=\left(e^{iH_{2}\frac{\Delta t}{2}}e^{H_{1}\Delta t}e^{iH_{2}\frac{\Delta t}{2}}\right)^{T}+O(\Delta t^{2}),

(5)

where the overall evolution by time
t
is separated into
T
steps of size
\Delta t=t/T
. Improving on the first-order Lie-Trotter splitting in Eq. (
4
) to the second-order Strang splitting in Eq. (
5
) is a natural extension, as it requires sandwiching one evolution between two half-steps of the other and yields a substantial improvement in accuracy. As such, Strang splitting is often preferred to Lie-Trotter splitting in demanding computations. This is analogous to the first-order Euler method for time marching, where extensions to higher order integrators, such as the fourth-order Runge-Kutta (RK4) method, provide substantially improved accuracy and algorithmic scaling with minimal computational overheads
8
.

Unlike explicit time marching, improving the splitting accuracy beyond second-order is non-trivial, as negative coefficients necessarily appear in stages of the evolution
6
. This is acceptable for the implementation of
e^{iH_{2}\Delta t}
, as evolving backwards time reverses the computation since

. However, when implementing the non-unitary evolution of

where
H_{1}
is negative semi-definite corresponding to dissipation, a backwards evolution in time results in an amplification, which is numerically unstable and cannot be block-encoded into a unitary operator without rescaling

. We overcome this by considering splitting methods with complex coefficients
5
. A real coefficient is required for the unitary operator to retain the unitary evolution, and a complex coefficient with a positive real part is required for the dissipative operator to avoid amplifications. Such a scheme that satisfies these constraints was derived by
10

10

,
a_{1}=4/15+2i/15
,
a_{2}=4/15-i/5

and

. The splitting scheme is constructed as

(6)

a_{i}

\Re(a_{i})>0
and

There is no known deterministic method of producing such coefficients besides numerical searches that solve the polynomial order conditions from many random initializations

III
Application to damped waves

The wave equation in one spatial dimension is
\partial_{t}^{2}\phi=c^{2}\partial_{x}^{2}\phi
, which we use to simplify the algorithm’s derivation and testing, with the extension to two and three spatial dimensions discussed towards the end of the section. After applying the Fourier transform, each spectral mode

e^{(\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{y}^{2}+\partial_{z}^{2})t}=e^{\partial_{x}^{2}t}e^{\partial_{y}^{2}t}e^{\partial_{z}^{2}t}
, which commutes among spatial dimensions and thereby allows each spatial dimension to be evolved sequentially with no loss in accuracy

, which depends on the square root of the sum of squared wavenumber components and can no longer be evolved independently. However, the discussed concepts of high-order operator splitting naturally extend to this problem, and to systems of ODEs that decompose into a larger number of unitary and dissipative contributions. For example, consider the evolution
e^{Mt}=e^{(H_{1}+iH_{2})t}
in Eq. (

is the sum of Hermitian generators
A_{j}
and

is the sum of anti-Hermitian generators

S_{i}(\Delta t)=\left(\prod_{j}e^{A_{j}a_{i}\Delta t}\right)\left(\prod_{k}e^{B_{k}b_{i}\Delta t}\right).

(16)

V
Summary and Outlook

In conclusion, we have presented a new application of high-order splitting methods with complex coefficients for simulating non-unitary dynamics on quantum computers. The method applies to arbitrary linear dynamics, but is best suited to systems that naturally separate into unitary and dissipative components, such as advection-diffusion or damped-wave equations. The high-order splitting can then be implemented as a series of real- or imaginary-time Hamiltonian evolutions by existing techniques for the simpler problems.

The method was demonstrated in the context of the damped-wave equation, using statevector simulations of up to sixth-order accuracy. This was performed by introducing an efficient, novel spectral quantum algorithm for evolving the wave equation and the damping operators. The designed circuits can be integrated with various quantum spectral transforms such as the QFT, QCT, or QST, permitting periodic, Neumann, or Dirichlet boundary conditions
20
. Higher-order schemes use several orders of magnitude fewer CNOT gates for simulations with a small error tolerance, and a comparable number of gates for simulations with a large error tolerance, so they can be chosen near universally.

The overall two-qubit gate count for
p
-order splitting scales as
O(2^{p/2}t^{1+1/p}\epsilon^{-1/p}d\log N)
using the case study of the damped-wave equation. The resulting circuits are highly compact when the individual dynamics are diagonalized by spectral transforms, demonstrated by a sixth-order splitting step of a 35 trillion-cell simulation requiring 1,562 CNOT gates, which is within the coherence-time limit of the current generation of quantum computers
15
. The error rapidly converges with increasing steps due to the high-order splitting.

Identifying suitable coefficients beyond sixth order and determining whether they lead to superior CNOT gate counts for various error tolerances remain open problems. The presented splitting constructions fundamentally rely on efficient techniques for simulating the individual Hamiltonian evolutions in both real and imaginary time. Developing such techniques for a broad range of applications is an important direction of future research.
