Linear Regression with Unknown Truncation Beyond Gaussian Features
Alexandros Kouridakis Anay Mehrotra UT Austin Stanford University alexkouridakis@utexas.edu anaymehrotra1@gmail.com Alkis Kalavasis Constantine Caramanis Yale University UT Austin alkis.kalavasis@yale.edu constantine@utexas.edu

Abstract
In truncated linear regression, samples ( x , y ) (x,y) are shown only when the outcome y y falls inside a certain survival set S ⋆ S^{\star} and the goal is to estimate the unknown d d -dimensional regressor w ⋆ w^{\star} . This problem has a long history of study in Statistics and Machine Learning going back to the works of [ Gal97a , Tob58a ] and more recently in, e.g. , [ DGTZ19a , DSYZ21a , LWZ23a , LMZ24a ] . Despite this long history, however, most prior works are limited to the special case where S ⋆ S^{\star} is precisely known. The more practically relevant case, where S ⋆ S^{\star} is unknown and must be learned from data, remains open: indeed, here the only available algorithms require strong assumptions on the distribution of the feature vectors ( e.g. , Gaussianity) and, even then, have a d poly ⁡ ( 1 / ε ) d^{\mathrm{poly}(1/\varepsilon)} run time for achieving ε \varepsilon accuracy. In this work, we give the first algorithm for truncated linear regression with unknown survival set that runs in poly ⁡ ( d / ε ) \mathrm{poly}(\nicefrac{{d}}{{\varepsilon}}) time, by only requiring that the feature vectors are sub-Gaussian. Our algorithm relies on a novel subroutine for efficiently learning unions of a bounded number of intervals using access to positive examples (without any negative examples) under a certain smoothness condition. This learning guarantee adds to the line of works on positive-only PAC learning and may be of independent interest.

Introduction

Selection biases, that is, systematic omissions in data, arise in almost all domains, from the physical sciences to econometrics and social network analysis.
Indeed, as Lisa Gitelman argues,
all
data exhibits selection biases and the term “raw data” is an oxymoron
[
Git13a
]
.
Among different types of selection biases,
truncation
is one of the most ubiquitous and challenging: truncation arises when samples are recorded only when they fall inside a “survival” region
S^{\star}
, and all other samples are deleted, or
truncated
, from the data,
e.g.
,
[
Tob58a
]
.
Truncation arises naturally across diverse domains: from econometrics
[
HW76a
]
and astronomy
[
Woo85a
]
to medical and biological sciences
[
KM03a
]
, engineering
[
JYZ20a
,
MEP21a
]
, and the social sciences
[
Bre96a
]
.
The difficulty of truncation lies in the fact that no samples are observed outside the survival region.
Thus, any algorithm must extrapolate from data observed within
S^{\star}
to the region outside it.
Standard estimation procedures such as ordinary least squares fail to handle this and produce biased estimates,
e.g.
,
[
Mad83a
,
Coh91a
,
Tob58a
]
.

In this work, we revisit the classical problem of
truncated linear regression
, which has a rich history of study in statistics
[
Coh91a
]
, machine learning (
e.g.
,
[
DGTZ18a
,
DGTZ19a
,
Ple21a
,
LWZ23a
,
LMZ24a
]
), and related fields
[
Mad83a
]
.
In this problem, we observe samples of the form
(x,y)
where
x
is a
d
-dimensional feature vector and
y
is a scalar outcome.
These samples are generated as follows: first, the feature vector
x
is drawn from a distribution
\euscr{D}
; then, the output
y=x^{\top}w^{\star}+\xi
is computed, where
w^{\star}
is an unknown regression vector and
\xi\sim\euscr{N}\left(0,1\right)
is standard Gaussian noise.
Unlike in standard linear regression, we do not observe every sample
(x,y)
.
Instead, there exists a set
S^{\star}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, called the
survival set
, such that we observe
(x,y)
if and only if
y\in S^{\star}
; otherwise, the sample is hidden from us.
The observed dataset thus consists of i.i.d. draws from the distribution of
(x,y)
conditioned on
y\in S^{\star}
, and the goal is to
estimate
w^{\star}
from these truncated samples (see
Definition

1
).

To gain some intuition, consider the following example from labor economics:

[
HW76a
]
studied the effect of education and (different measures of) intelligence (which are modeled as a feature vector
x
) on the earnings
y
in low-income populations, but discovered that the dataset only included an observation
(x,y)
when the annual income
y
lay below a prescribed eligibility threshold
\tau
, leading to the truncation set
S^{\star}=(-\infty,\tau]
.
More broadly, the survival sets arising in real-world datasets can be far more complicated than a single threshold on the response.
A representative example comes from modern astronomical surveys, where whether a star of a certain luminosity
y
is observed is governed by a multi-stage pipeline that can truncate different non-contiguous intervals of luminosities.
In practice, the resulting truncation set reflects a composition of instrument detection limits, survey design choices, and upstream catalog filters, among other factors, together making
S^{\star}
complex and hard to know explicitly
[
Tee97a
,
Woo85a
,
SDS22c
,
SDS22d
]

(See
Appendix

A
for a concrete list of various sources of truncation in large-scale astronomical surveys).
Beyond direct applications in problems where truncation arises, algorithms for truncated linear regression also have applications in other domains, including treatment effect estimation in observational studies (see,
e.g.
,
[
CKMM+25a
]
and references therein).

Prior Work.

Classical works in statistics laid the groundwork for truncated linear regression
[
Tob58a
,
HW76a
,
Coh91a
]
.
However, these works were restricted to simple survival sets and did not come with computational efficiency guarantees in high-dimensional feature-regimes that regularly arise in modern applications.
Indeed, no computationally efficient algorithm was known in high dimensions until the work of
[
DGTZ19a
]
, who studied the special case where the truncation set
S^{\star}
is precisely known via a membership oracle.
1
1

1

A membership oracle for
S^{\star}
is a primitive that, given a point

, outputs
Yes
if
x\in S^{\star}
and
No
otherwise.

Under certain assumptions (which we discuss later in this section), they gave a
\mathrm{poly}(\nicefrac{{d}}{{\varepsilon}})
-time algorithm to obtain an
\varepsilon
-approximation of
w^{\star}
.
Several subsequent works have continued the study of truncated linear regression in this known-survival-set setting,
e.g.
,
[
DRZ20a
,
DSYZ21a
,
LWZ23a
]
.

However, as the earlier examples illustrate, in many practical settings the survival set is not known to the analyst
[
Zam22a
]
.
When
S^{\star}
is unknown, no efficient algorithms are known in high dimensions.
In fact, even getting finite sample complexity bounds is non-trivial:
if we put
no
assumptions on
S^{\star}
, the parameter estimation problem is information-theoretically impossible

DGTZ18a
]
.
To ensure statistical tractability, one must assume that
S^{\star}
has bounded “complexity”, for instance, that
S^{\star}
is a union of at most
k

S^{\star}
is a subset of
\mathbb{R}

This leads us to
the following problem, which is the main focus of this paper.

Main Question.
Is there an algorithm that learns
w^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
\varepsilon
accuracy in
\mathrm{poly}(\nicefrac{{dk}}{{\varepsilon}})
time in truncated linear regression when
S^{\star}
is unknown and a union of (at most)
k

Under this assumption,
[
KTZ19a
]
established a sample complexity bound of
\mathrm{poly}(\nicefrac{{dk}}{{\varepsilon}})
but had an exponential-in-

running time and, hence, were not qualitatively faster than brute-force algorithms also running in exponential-in-
d
time (see
Section

1.1
).
The recent work of
[
LMZ24a
]
made progress by designing the first faster-than-brute-force algorithm for this problem, but only applied to the stylized case where the feature distribution
\euscr{D}
is Gaussian and its running time remains exponential, namely
d^{\mathrm{poly}(k/\varepsilon)}
.
2
2

2

When
d{\,\gg\,}k,\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}
, it holds that
d^{\mathrm{poly}(k/\varepsilon)}{\,\ll\,}\left({d/\varepsilon}\right)^{d}
.

This assumption of Gaussianity on the features is crucial for their algorithm to work, but it constitutes a very strong requirement on the feature vectors, which does not hold in real-world regression data.

Our Contributions.

Our main contribution (
Theorem

1.1
) is a
\mathrm{poly}(\nicefrac{{dk}}{{\varepsilon}})
-time algorithm that, given access to samples from the truncated regression model, outputs an
\varepsilon
-accurate estimate of
w^{\star}
without knowledge of
S^{\star}
. Our algorithm is computationally efficient and importantly operates under a set of assumptions that are the same as, or much weaker than, those in prior works.
Notably, we do not require the feature-distribution
\euscr{D}
to have a parametric form,
e.g.
, Gaussian.
Instead, we only need
\euscr{D}
to satisfy the significantly weaker requirement of sub-Gaussianity.
Concretely, we require the following assumptions.

Assumption 1

(Survival Probability)
.

There is a known constant
\alpha\in(0,1]
, s.t.,
\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\euscr{D}}[\Pr[y\in S^{\star}{\mid}x]]\geq\alpha.

Assumption 2

(Sub-Gaussianity and Boundedness)
.

There are known
\sigma,R{\,\geq\,}1
, s.t., the feature distribution
\euscr{D}
is
\sigma
-sub-Gaussian and regression parameter satisfies
\left\lVert w^{\star}\right\rVert\leq R
.

Assumption 3

(Identifiability)
.

Let
\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}
be the distribution of
x
in the observed data (see
Equation

1
).
There exists a constant
\rho\in(0,1)
such that
\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}}\!\left[xx^{\top}\right]\succeq\rho^{2}\cdot I
.

A few remarks about the assumptions are in order:

1.

The first assumption ensures that the amount of truncation is not too aggressive; at least a constant fraction of the points survive.
This assumption is information-theoretically necessary: indeed, as
\Pr[y\in S^{\star}]
approaches
0
, then an unbounded number of samples are required to estimate
w^{\star}
(as proved by
[
DGTZ18a
]
).

2.

The second assumption is a mild and standard tail condition on the features.
In fact,
2
is even weaker than the assumptions required by prior works studying the easier version of truncated regression with
known
survival sets: they require
\euscr{D}
to have a bounded support
[
DGTZ19a
,
DSYZ21a
]
.

3.

The third assumption is required to ensure that
w^{\star}
can be identified from data in the survival region; it is also made by prior works,
e.g.
,
[
DSYZ21a
]
.
We prove that it is
necessary
in
Appendix

E
.
We also mention that since we have sample access to
\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}
, we can estimate the parameter
\rho
from samples.

In short, Assumptions 1 and 3 are
necessary
for the problem to be solvable by a sample-efficient algorithm, while Assumption 2 is significantly weaker compared to corresponding assumptions in prior work.
We are now ready to state our main result in more detail.

Theorem 1.1

(Informal; see
Theorem

3.1
)
.

Assume

1
,
2
and
3
hold with
\alpha,\rho{\,=\,}\Omega(1)
,
\sigma{\,=\,}O(1)
,
R{\,=\,}\mathrm{poly}(d)
, and
S^{\star}
is a union of at most
k
intervals (for known
k
).
Then, there is an algorithm that given
n=\mathrm{poly}(\nicefrac{{dk}}{{\varepsilon}})
i.i.d. samples from the truncated linear regression model, with probability
0.99
, outputs an estimate
\widehat{w}
satisfying
\|\widehat{w}-w^{\star}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon
.
The algorithm runs in time
\mathrm{poly}(n).

Importantly, both the sample complexity and running time of our algorithms scale polynomially in the relevant parameters
d,k,\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}
.
Moreover, the success probability can be made
1-\delta
for any
\delta\in(0,1).

Since the algorithm only requires
3
and
2
on distribution
\euscr{D}
, it extends to the cases where
\euscr{D}
is non-Gaussian.
Further, in the special case where
\euscr{D}
is Gaussian, the above result improves the best running time (namely,
d^{\mathrm{poly}(k/\varepsilon)}
by
[
LMZ24a
]
) to polynomial in all parameters of interest (see
Theorem

3.1
).

Technical Overview.

Next, we briefly overview the main challenges in proving
Theorem

1.1
and how we overcome them, while comparing with the work of
[
LMZ24a
]
which requires Gaussian features.
A more detailed overview appears in
Section

3
.

Approach with known
S^{\star}
.

Without any truncation, the standard method for regression is ordinary least squares (OLS).
A principled way to derive OLS is to view it as a special case of minimizing the negative log-likelihood (NLL).
This viewpoint extends naturally to truncated linear regression: existing algorithms for estimating
w^{\star}
with known survival set
S^{\star}
minimize the (population version of the) NLL function
\euscr{L}_{S^{\star}}(\cdot)
using,
e.g.
, stochastic gradient descent (SGD)
[
LWZ23a
,
DGTZ19a
,
DSYZ21a
]
.
This approach works because the NLL satisfies two key properties:

P1.

\euscr{L}_{S^{\star}}(\cdot)
is convex and
w^{\star}
is its unique minimizer.

P2.

\euscr{L}_{S^{\star}}(\cdot)
is strongly convex near
w^{\star}
.

Convexity ensures that one can find
\widehat{w}
such that
\euscr{L}_{S^{\star}}(\widehat{w})\approx\euscr{L}_{S^{\star}}(w^{\star})
, and strong convexity then implies
\widehat{w}\approx w^{\star}
.
However, the NLL depends explicitly on
S^{\star}
, and hence new techniques are required when
S^{\star}
is unknown.

Existing approach with unknown
S^{\star}
.

A natural idea is to first learn an approximation
S
of
S^{\star}
and then minimize
\euscr{L}_{S}(\cdot)
.
However,
\euscr{L}_{S}(\cdot)
is not even well-defined when
S\neq S^{\star}
, so this direct approach fails.

[
LMZ24a
]
bypass this issue by designing a generalized objective
\smash{\widetilde{\euscr{L}}}_{S}(\cdot)
that remains well-defined for
S\neq S^{\star}
and approximately retains the good properties of the NLL when
\euscr{D}
is Gaussian.
Their algorithm works in two phases: first, (i) it learns an approximation
S
of
S^{\star}
in
d^{\mathrm{poly}(k/\varepsilon)}
time; then, (ii) it minimizes
\smash{\widetilde{\euscr{L}}}_{S}(\cdot)
using SGD.
Both phases (i) and (ii) require the Gaussianity assumption on the covariates, and the first set learning part runs in super-polynomial time. We examine these issues and discuss how to overcome them next.

Issue I: Properties and Optimization of
\smash{\widetilde{\euscr{L}}}_{S}(\cdot)
without Gaussianity.

We begin by examining phase (ii), and we will discuss phase (i) afterwards. Hence, let us assume that the learning algorithm is given an approximation to the true set
S\approx S^{\star}
. Now, we have to understand how to show the desired properties of NLL (phase (ii)) without Gaussianity.
In particular, using the fact that
x
is Gaussian,
[
LMZ24a
]
prove that
\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{x}[\boldsymbol{\mathchar 626\relax}\widetilde{\euscr{L}}_{S}(w;x)]\approx 0

3

\boldsymbol{\mathchar 626\relax}\widetilde{\euscr{L}}_{S}(w;x)
corresponds to the gradient of the NLL evaluated at the single point
x

w

x

\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}

The key technical ingredient for their proof is to show that the gradient

To go beyond Gaussian features, we follow a different route. We prove two important properties: for any

Finally, we must still minimize
\smash{\widetilde{\euscr{L}}}_{S}(\cdot)

Issue II: Efficiently learning
S^{\star}
from positive samples only.

For the above phase (ii) to be useful, we need to get an estimation for the true set
S^{\star}
(and in particular in time
\mathrm{poly}(\nicefrac{{dk}}{{\varepsilon}})
).
Hence, our goal is to efficiently find
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}
such that
\Pr[\mathds{1}\{y\in S^{\star}\}\neq\mathds{1}\{y\in S\}]\approx 0
;
the challenge is that we only observe samples
y
inside
S^{\star}
(
i.e.
, only positive samples) and no samples outside
S^{\star}
(
i.e.
, no negative samples). In particular, if we denote by
\euscr{D}_{y}
the marginal on
y
, then the learner observes points
y
from the distribution with density proportional to
\euscr{D}_{y}(y)\mathds{1}\{y\in S^{\star}\}
.
Most results on PAC learning from positive examples are negative: without assumptions on the underlying distribution
\euscr{D}_{y}
, even simple classes such as unions of
k>1
intervals are information-theoretically unlearnable
[
Nat87a
,
Shv90a
]
.
A standard way to circumvent this impossibility is to obtain auxiliary information about the marginal distribution
\euscr{D}_{y}
of
y
,
e.g.
, by finding (and using samples from) a distribution
\widetilde{\euscr{D}}_{y}
that is “close” to it
[
Den98a
,
LMZ26a
]
; see
Definition

2
.

In the truncated linear regression setting,
[
LMZ24a
]
show how to construct
\widetilde{\euscr{D}}_{y}
when
x
is Gaussian; this relies heavily on the parametric form of the Gaussian and even fails for other parametric distributions.
Our main contribution here is to give a new way to construct this auxiliary distribution
\widetilde{\euscr{D}}_{y}
(
Lemma

3.4
) by only using the sub-Gaussianity of
x
(without having any specific parametric form) and appropriate upper and lower bounds on the tail of the noise-distribution.

Finally, we mention that even with access to
\widetilde{\euscr{D}}_{y}
, we need to design an algorithm for learning the set that runs in time
\mathrm{poly}(d,k,\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}).
Existing algorithms require constrained variants of empirical risk minimization
[
LMZ26a
]
, which are often
NP
-hard in high dimensions.
We show that the constrained empirical risk minimization algorithm of
[
LMZ26a
]
can be implemented in
\mathrm{poly}(\nicefrac{{dk}}{{\varepsilon}})
time for one-dimensional sets (
Theorem

), using a carefully designed greedy procedure (see

3.1
Positive PAC Learning Unions of Intervals

Next, we introduce PAC learning from positive samples only (henceforth, positive-only learning) and give an efficient algorithm for learning unions of intervals in this model.

Positive-Only Learning.

In positive-only learning
[
Nat87a
]
, as in classical PAC learning, there is a distribution
\euscr{D}^{\star}
over a feature space
\euscr{X}
and some concept class
\mathdutchcal{H}
containing the target concept
h^{\star}
.
In contrast to standard PAC learning, here we are only given sample access to the conditional distribution
\euscr{D}^{\star}_{+}\coloneqq\euscr{D}^{\star}|_{h^{\star}(x)=1}
of positive samples (a distribution over
\euscr{X}
) and not given any information about the negative samples.
Our goal is to output a hypothesis
\widehat{h}
with small misclassification error under
\euscr{D}^{\star}
,
i.e.
,
\Pr_{x\sim\euscr{D}^{\star}}\!(\widehat{h}(x)\neq h^{\star}(x))

\leq\varepsilon
. Crucially, we aim for small misclassification error on both positive and negative examples, even though we only have sample access to positives.

Smoothed Positive-Only Learning.

[
LMZ26a
]

version of positive-only learning, where, in addition to positive samples, the learner also has access to a distribution
\euscr{D}
over the feature space which is “smooth” with respect to
\euscr{D}^{\star}
.
Intuitively, samples from
\euscr{D}
help the learner identify high-probability regions of the unconditional
\euscr{D}^{\star}
.
In particular,
[
LMZ26a
]
introduce the following notion of generalized smoothness, which extends notions of smoothness studied by works in smoothed online learning,
e.g.
,
[
HHSY22a
,
HRS20a
,
HRS24a
,
BRS24a
,
Bla25a
]
.

3.3.2
Algorithmic Considerations for PSGD

The above results yield a projection set

such that approximately minimizing

To actually run PSGD and obtain this approximate minimizer, we need to satisfy some algorithmic considerations.

\euscr{P}
for the first PSGD iteration, (C2) efficiently project onto
\euscr{P}

To circumvent this challenge, we allow our gradient estimate to be

In

We bound the runtime in

Finally, we combine these results with an analysis of PSGD with biased gradients to show that running PSGD with projection set

4
Conclusion

We provide the first polynomial-time algorithm for truncated linear regression with an
unknown
survival set (
Theorem

3.1
).
Our algorithm (
Algorithm

1
) operates under the mild assumption that the covariate vectors are sub-Gaussian, and has polynomial dependence on all key problem parameters.
An important ingredient in establishing this result is an efficient procedure for learning unions of intervals from
positive examples
under an appropriate smoothness condition (
Theorem

3.3
), which may be of independent interest.

Our results suggest several directions for future work.
First, truncated regression beyond the linear setting, such as Poisson and logistic regression, have been studied with known survival set
[
IZD20a
]
and extending our techniques to
unknown
survival set variants of these is a natural next step.
Further, we expect that natural extensions of our interval-learning algorithm can learn richer hypothesis classes in constant dimensions (
d=O(1)
) with just positive examples under smoothness, provided they are well-approximated by unions of axis-aligned hypercubes. Some such classes are studied by
[
DHZ23a
]
and even shown to include classes with infinite VC dimension.

Appendix A
Complex Survival Sets in Astronomical Surveys

In this section, we illustrate that survival sets arising in real-world data can be highly nontrivial: rather than being simple threshold rules, they are often induced by a layered sequence of instrument constraints, engineering decisions, and scientific priorities, many of which interact in ways that are difficult to formalize.

To make this concrete, we focus on astronomy, where modern
large-scale surveys
have become foundational infrastructure for astrophysics.
These surveys, such as the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS), Gaia, and the Vera Rubin Observatory’s Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST), systematically measure properties of millions to billions of objects, enabling population-level studies of stars and galaxies, precision tests of cosmological models, and the discovery of rare phenomena
[
YAAA+00a
,
PBBV+16a
,
IKTA+19a
]
.
At the same time, the scale and complexity that make these surveys powerful also make selection effects unavoidable, and careful statistical analysis requires understanding (or approximating) the induced survival set.

Warm-up.

We begin with a classical and widely studied selection effect that arises from physical limits of instruments.

Example A.1

(Malmquist Bias)
.

Malmquist bias is a selection effect in astronomical surveys driven by flux limits: any instrument can only observe objects whose
apparent
brightness
y
exceeds a detection threshold, while dimmer objects remain unobserved
[
Mal22a
,
Mal25a
]
.
This truncation can systematically bias downstream inferences if it is ignored, leading to incorrect conclusions about stellar populations and distances
[
Tee97a
]
.
For instance, among objects at a fixed true distance, the brightness cutoff removes faint members; consequently, the observed sample over-represents intrinsically brighter objects, unless the selection mechanism is explicitly accounted for (as we do in our algorithm).

Selection effects in large-scale survey pipelines.

While Malmquist bias is already a nontrivial form of truncation, real survey pipelines typically induce much richer survival sets.

Example A.2

(Truncation Biases in Large-Scale Astronomical Surveys)
.

Large-scale astronomical surveys form the backbone of modern astrophysics: they enable scientists to identify promising regions to study, evaluate foundational theoretical models, and discover rare and new phenomena (e.g.,
[
YAAA+00a
,
PBBV+16a
,
IKTA+19a
]
).
However, their massive scale and operational complexity inevitably introduce truncation biases that must be corrected to draw valid scientific conclusions.

A representative case study is the Apache Point Observatory Galactic Evolution Experiment (APOGEE)
[
MSFP+17a
]
, one of the most comprehensive stellar spectroscopic surveys conducted to date.
APOGEE collected high-resolution near-infrared spectra for over 146,000 red giant stars using custom-built spectrographs.
The survey’s scale—spanning multiple years, numerous institutions, and extensive operational logistics—illustrates why truncation effects are inevitable: each stage of the pipeline introduces constraints and trade-offs that shape which objects are ultimately observed and how.

Survey pipelines introduce numerous truncation biases, both known and unknown.
Some arise from fundamental physical limitations (such as Malmquist bias), while others emerge from practical engineering constraints and resource allocation decisions.
The question is therefore not whether truncation biases exist, but how to account for their combined effects on the observed sample.
Even within a single survey like APOGEE, truncation effects arise at multiple levels
[
MSFP+17a
]
.
To illustrate their diversity and the resulting complexity of the induced survival set, we group some common mechanisms by their underlying causes (see
[
SDS22c
]
for further discussion):

1.

Sampling-dependent biases
arise from target truncation strategies.
Surveys often partition targets into cohorts with different observation priorities, leading to truncation of some cohorts. For example, APOGEE stratifies targets by apparent brightness (H-band magnitude) and preferentially targets certain strata.
Importantly, cohort definitions can depend on modeling choices: APOGEE preferentially sampled red giants over dwarfs using a “redness” criterion, but this criterion depends on the chosen de-reddening model used to convert observed color
C_{\mathrm{o}bs}
into intrinsic color
C_{\mathrm{c}orr}
, making truncation model-dependent
[
SDS22d
]
.

2.

Physical limitations
of instruments impose unavoidable constraints.
Beyond flux limits, atmospheric absorption can prevent observation of certain objects, and instrument design can induce truncation over sky position.
For instance, APOGEE uses multi-fiber spectrographs that route light from many targets through optical fibers to a separate spectrograph for high-precision analysis
[
WHSM+19a
,
OCo20a
]
.
Physical constraints create an “exclusion zone” around each fiber that prevents placing another fiber nearby, which systematically under-samples dense regions such as star clusters and the inner Galaxy
[
SDS22d
]
, truncating some complex set of outcomes
y
.

3.

Cascading effects
propagate biases from upstream catalogs to downstream surveys.
Many surveys begin with a “parent catalog” and inherit its selection criteria.
For example, galaxy surveys that start from the Uppsala General Catalogue inherit criteria that omit small angular-size galaxies unless they exceed brightness thresholds
[
FCWG+95a
]
.
These inherited filters, composed with survey-specific constraints, yield selection functions that are effectively complex convolutions of multiple stages.

The consequences of ignoring truncation effects can be severe.
A particularly striking example comes from NASA’s
Kepler
mission: an incorrectly implemented veto in pipeline version 9.2 created an unexpected period-dependent detection bias, substantially reducing the detectability of planets with orbital periods exceeding 40 days relative to theoretical expectations
[
CCBJ+16a
]
.
The flaw went undetected until extensive validation efforts, and its discovery necessitated reprocessing the catalog and issuing cautionary guidance for analyses performed on earlier releases
[
CCBJ+16a
]
.

\left|\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\euscr{D}}\left[f(x)\right]-\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}}\left[f(x)\right]\right|\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\euscr{D}}\left|f(x)\right|.

Proof.

\displaystyle\left|\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\euscr{D}}\left[f(x)\right]-\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}}\left[f(x)\right]\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int f(x)\left(1-\frac{p_{S^{\star}}(x)}{\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{\euscr{D}}p_{S^{\star}}(x)}\right)\euscr{D}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int\left|f(x)\right|\left|1-\frac{p_{S^{\star}}(x)}{\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{\euscr{D}}p_{S^{\star}}(x)}\right|\euscr{D}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\euscr{D}}\left|f(x)\right|

where we used the fact that, by
1
,
1-\frac{1}{\alpha}\leq 1-\frac{p_{S^{\star}}(x)}{\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{\euscr{D}}p_{S^{\star}}(x)}\leq 1
,
and therefore

\left|1-\frac{p_{S^{\star}}(x)}{\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{\euscr{D}}p_{S^{\star}}(x)}\right|\leq\max\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}-1,1\right)\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}

∎

Lemma C.6
.

Let
\mu\coloneqq\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{\euscr{D}}x
and
\widehat{\mu}\coloneqq\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}}x
. Suppose that
1
and
4
hold. Then,

\text{for any unit vector $v$}\,,\qquad\left\langle v,\mu-\widehat{\mu}\right\rangle^{2}\leq\frac{\Lambda^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}\,.

Proof.

By definition of
\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}
in
Equation

6
, we have that

\displaystyle\left\langle v,\mu-\widehat{\mu}\right\rangle^{2}=\left(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{\euscr{D}}\left\langle v,x\right\rangle-\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}}\left\langle v,x\right\rangle\right)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\left(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{\euscr{D}}\left|\left\langle v,x\right\rangle\right|\right)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\cdot\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{\euscr{D}}\left\langle v,x\right\rangle^{2}\leq\frac{\Lambda^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}\,.

where the second line is by
C.5
, the third is by Jensen’s inequality, and the last is by
4
.
∎

Lemma C.7
.

Let
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
be a
truncated
random vector such that the marginal of
x
is
\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}
and the marginal of
y
conditioned on
x
is
\euscr{N}(\left\langle w^{\star},x\right\rangle,1,S^{\star})
. Suppose that
1
and
4
hold. Then,

\text{for any unit vector $v$}\,,\qquad\left|\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}\left\langle v,\left(y-\left\langle w^{\star},x\right\rangle\right)\cdot x\right\rangle\right|\leq\frac{3\Lambda}{2\alpha}\,.

Proof.

We have that

\displaystyle\left|\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}\left\langle v,\left(y-\left\langle w^{\star},x\right\rangle\right)\cdot x\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}}\left[\left|\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{y\sim\euscr{N}(\left\langle w^{\star},x\right\rangle,1,S^{\star})}\left[y\;\middle|\;x\right]-\left\langle w^{\star},x\right\rangle\right|\cdot\left|\left\langle v,x\right\rangle\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\euscr{D}}\left[p(x,w^{\star};S^{\star})\cdot\left(\sqrt{2\log\frac{1}{p(x,w^{\star};S^{\star})}}+1\right)\cdot\left|\left\langle v,x\right\rangle\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{2\alpha}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\euscr{D}}\left|\left\langle v,x\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3\Lambda}{2\alpha}\,.

where the first line is by Jensen’s inequality, the second is by
C.4
and
Lemma

C.1
, the third is because

\left(\sqrt{2\log\frac{1}{t}}+1\right)t\leq\frac{3}{2}

for all
t\in(0,1)
, and the last is by
4
.
∎

C.3
Consequences of Sub-Gaussianity of
\euscr{D}

In this section, we prove some concentration results that are required in many of our proofs. Specifically, we show that if
x\sim\euscr{D}
is a sub-Gaussian random vector (as in
2
) then
x\sim\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}
is also a sub-Gaussian random vector, and furthermore, for
(x,y)
drawn from our truncated regression model, the random vector
y\cdot x
is sub-exponential. We start by reviewing the definition of the sub-Gaussian and sub-Exponential norm, as well as some standard facts about them.

Definition 3

(sub-Gaussian and sub-exponential norms)
.

For any random variable
X
, its
sub-Gaussian
norm is defined as

\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\coloneqq\inf\left\{\sigma>0\colon\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}\exp\left(\frac{X^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)\leq 2\right\}

while its
sub-exponential
norm is defined as

\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\psi_{1}}\coloneqq\inf\left\{\sigma>0\colon\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}\exp\left(\frac{\left|X\right|}{\sigma}\right)\leq 2\right\}\,.

A fundamental relation between these two norms (see Lemma 2.8.6 of
[
Ver18a
]
) is that, for any random variables
X,Y

\left\lVert XY\right\rVert_{\psi_{1}}\leq\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\,.

(3)

It is also standard that the sub-Gaussian (or sub-exponential) norm of a centered random variable is at most a constant factor different from the same norm of the respective uncentered random variable (see Lemma 2.7.8 and Equation 2.26 of
[
Ver18a
]
). That is, there exist absolute constants
C_{1},C_{2}
such that, for any random variable
X

\left\lVert X-\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}X\right\rVert_{\psi_{1}}\leq C_{1}\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\psi_{1}}\quad\text{and}\quad\left\lVert X-\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}X\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\leq C_{2}\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\,.

(4)

Having established the above, we now turn to showing that if
\euscr{D}
is a sub-Gaussian distribution, then so is
\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}
.

Lemma C.8
.

Let
x\sim\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}
, and suppose
1
,
4
and
2
hold. Then, for any unit vector
v
,

\left\lVert\left\langle v,x\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\leq O\left(\sigma\cdot\sqrt{\log\frac{1}{\alpha}}+\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert_{2}\right)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left\lVert\left\langle v,x-\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}}x\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\leq\widetilde{O}\left(\sigma+\frac{\Lambda}{\alpha}\right)

where
\mu\coloneqq\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{\euscr{D}}x
.

Proof.

Note that, by
C.4
and
2

\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}}\exp\left(\frac{\left\langle v,x-\mu\right\rangle^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{\euscr{D}}\exp\left(\frac{\left\langle v,x-\mu\right\rangle^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{2}{\alpha}\,.

from which it follows that

\left\lVert\left\langle v,x-\mu\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\leq O\left(\sigma\cdot\sqrt{\log\frac{1}{\alpha}}\right)
.
Therefore, the first claim follows by

\left\lVert\left\langle v,x\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\lesssim\left\lVert\left\langle v,x-\mu\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}+\left|\left\langle v,\mu\right\rangle\right|\leq O\left(\sigma\cdot\sqrt{\log\frac{1}{\alpha}}\right)+\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert_{2}

where we used the triangle inequality and the fact that
\left\lVert 1\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}={1/\sqrt{\ln{2}}}
. For the second claim, let
\widehat{\mu}\coloneqq\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}}x
, and observe that

\left\lVert\left\langle v,x-\widehat{\mu}\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\lesssim\left\lVert\left\langle v,x-\mu\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}+\left|\left\langle v,\mu-\widehat{\mu}\right\rangle\right|\leq O\left(\sigma\cdot\sqrt{\log\frac{1}{\alpha}}\right)+\frac{\Lambda}{\alpha}

where we used the triangle inequality and
Lemma

C.6
.
∎

We continue by considering the random vector
y\cdot x
. First, we establish that, in the simplest setting where we have no truncation on
y
, this vector concentrates.

Lemma C.9
.

Let
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
be an
untruncated
random vector such that the marginal of
x
is
\euscr{D}
and the marginal of
y
conditioned on
x
is
\euscr{N}(\left\langle w^{\star},x\right\rangle,1)
. Let
\mu\coloneqq\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{\euscr{D}}x
, and suppose that
2
holds. Then there exists some absolute constant
C>0
such that

\left\lVert\left\langle v,y\cdot x-\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}y\cdot x\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{1}}\leq C\cdot\left(1+\left\lVert w^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}\right)\cdot\left(\sigma+\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert_{2}\right)^{2}\,.

Proof.

Let
\xi
be a standard normal random variable, independent of
x
, and let
v
be an arbitrary unit vector. By
Equation

4
, for some absolute constant
C^{\prime}
, we have that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left\langle v,y\cdot x-\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}y\cdot x\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}\left\lVert\left\langle v,y\cdot x\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{1}}

\displaystyle=C^{\prime}\left\lVert\left\langle w^{\star},x\right\rangle\left\langle v,x\right\rangle+\xi\left\langle v,x\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}\left(\left\lVert\left\langle w^{\star},x\right\rangle\left\langle v,x\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{1}}+\left\lVert\xi\left\langle v,x\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{1}}\right)

where we used the triangle inequality. We will bound each of those two terms separately. We have that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left\langle w^{\star},x\right\rangle\left\langle v,x\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\left\langle w^{\star},x\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\left\lVert\left\langle v,x\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\left\lVert\left\langle w^{\star},x-\mu\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}+\left|\left\langle w^{\star},\mu\right\rangle\right|\right)\cdot\left(\left\lVert\left\langle v,x-\mu\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}+\left|\left\langle v,\mu\right\rangle\right|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert w^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}\left(\sigma+\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert_{2}\right)^{2}

where the first line is by
Equation

3
, the second is by the triangle inequality, and the third is by the assumption on
x
and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. Similarly, we have that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\xi\left\langle v,x\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\left\lVert\left\langle v,x\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\lesssim\left(\left\lVert\left\langle v,x-\mu\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}+\left|\left\langle v,\mu\right\rangle\right|\right)\leq\left(\sigma+\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert_{2}\right)

where we used the simple fact that
\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}=\sqrt{\nicefrac{{8}}{{3}}}
for
\xi\sim\euscr{N}(0,1)
.
8
8

8

To see this, note that
\xi^{2}
is a chi-squared random variable whose MGF expression gives
\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}\exp\left(\frac{\xi^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)=\left(1-\frac{2}{t^{2}}\right)^{-1/2}
for
t\geq\sqrt{2}
, and the right-hand side of this expression becomes
2
when
t=\sqrt{\nicefrac{{8}}{{3}}}
The lemma follows by combining our above displays and using the fact that
\sigma\geq 1
.
∎

We now generalize to the case where we actually have truncation on
y
, as is the case in our truncated regression model. First, we show an auxiliary lemma which establishes the sub-Gaussianity of a one-dimensional truncated Gaussian with arbitrary truncation set.

Lemma C.10
.

Consider a one-dimensional truncated Gaussian
\euscr{N}(\mu,1,S)
, and let
a\coloneqq\euscr{N}(S;\mu,1)
be the mass that the untruncated Gaussian
\euscr{N}(\mu,1)
places on
S
. Let
\xi\sim\euscr{N}(\mu,1,S)
. Then

\left\lVert\xi-\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}\xi\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\leq O\left(\sqrt{1+\log\frac{1}{a}}\right)\,.

Proof.

We can assume without loss of generality that
\mu=0
, as the result for general
\mu
follows by shifting. We begin by upper-bounding the following expectation

\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}\exp\left(\frac{(\xi-\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}\xi)^{2}}{4}\right)\,.

For fixed
a=\euscr{N}(S;\mu,1)
, the above expectation is maximized by picking a set
S
of mass
a
that is as far away from
\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}\xi
as possible. Thus, the worst-case
S
is
(-\infty,-c)\cup(c,\infty)
, which consists of both tails of
\euscr{N}(0,1)
, each having mass
a/2
. In other words,
Q(c)\geq a
, where
Q(c)\coloneqq\Pr_{Z\sim\euscr{N}(0,1)}(Z\geq c)
is the Gaussian Q-function. It is standard that
Q(c)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{c^{2}}{2}\right)
, which implies that

c\leq\sqrt{2\log\frac{2}{a}}\,.

For the above
S
, it is also clear that
\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}\xi=0
. Thus, the expectation we wish to upper bound becomes

\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}\exp\left(\frac{\xi^{2}}{4}\right)=\frac{\int_{S}\exp\left(\frac{z^{2}}{4}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{z^{2}}{2}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z}{\int_{S}\exp\left(-\frac{z^{2}}{2}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z}=\frac{\int_{c}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{z^{2}}{4}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z}{\int_{c}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{z^{2}}{2}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z}=\frac{\sqrt{2}Q(c/\sqrt{2})}{Q(c)}

where the second step follows because the integrands are even functions and
S
is symmetric about the origin. We now claim that

\frac{\sqrt{2}Q(c/\sqrt{2})}{Q(c)}\leq 2(1+c^{2})\exp\left(\frac{c^{2}}{4}\right)\,.

If
c<1
, this bound can be checked analytically, while if
c\geq 1
the bound follows by the standard fact that
\frac{x}{1+x^{2}}e^{-x^{2}/2}\leq\sqrt{2\pi}\cdot Q(x)\leq\frac{1}{x}e^{-x^{2}/2}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Combining our last three displays with the fact that
1+x^{2}\leq 2e^{x^{2}/4}
, we obtain that

\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}\exp\left(\frac{\xi^{2}}{4}\right)\leq 4\exp\left(\frac{c^{2}}{2}\right)\leq\frac{8}{a}\,.

Therefore, we have shown for any truncation set
S
that

\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}\exp\left(\frac{(\xi-\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}\xi)^{2}}{4}\right)\leq\frac{8}{a}\,.

Picking
k\coloneqq\Theta(1+\log\nicefrac{{1}}{{a}})>1
such that
(\nicefrac{{8}}{{a}})^{1/k}=2
, and using Jensen’s inequality, we finally obtain

\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}\exp\left(\frac{(\xi-\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}\xi)^{2}}{4k}\right)\leq\left(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}\exp\left(\frac{(\xi-\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}\xi)^{2}}{4}\right)\right)^{1/k}\leq 2

and the lemma follows by recalling the definition of the sub-Gaussian norm.
∎

We are now ready to show concentration of the vector
y\cdot x
in our truncated regression model.

Lemma C.11
.

Let
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
be a
truncated
random vector such that the marginal of
x
is
\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}
and the marginal of
y
conditioned on
x
is
\euscr{N}(\left\langle w^{\star},x\right\rangle,1,S^{\star})
. Let
\mu\coloneqq\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}x
, and suppose that
1
and
2
hold. Then there exists some absolute constant
C>0
such that

\left\lVert\left\langle v,y\cdot x-\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}y\cdot x\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{1}}\leq C\cdot\left(1+\left\lVert w^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}\right)\cdot\left(\sigma^{2}\log\frac{1}{\alpha}+\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)\,.

Proof.

We proceed similarly to the proof of
Lemma

C.9
. Let
\widehat{\mu}\coloneqq\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}}x
. For any
x
, define the truncated Gaussian random variable
\xi_{x}\sim\euscr{N}(0,1,S^{\star}-\left\langle w^{\star},x\right\rangle)
, where
S^{\star}-\left\langle w^{\star},x\right\rangle=\left\{z\;\middle|\;z+\left\langle w^{\star},x\right\rangle\in S^{\star}\right\}
. Then we can write
y=\left\langle w^{\star},x\right\rangle+\xi_{x}
. Hence, letting
v
be an arbitrary unit vector, we have by
Equation

4
that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left\langle v,y\cdot x-\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}y\cdot x\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}\left\lVert\left\langle v,y\cdot x\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{1}}

\displaystyle=C^{\prime}\left\lVert\left\langle w^{\star},x\right\rangle\left\langle v,x\right\rangle+\xi_{x}\left\langle v,x\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}\left(\left\lVert\left\langle w^{\star},x\right\rangle\left\langle v,x\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{1}}+\left\lVert\xi_{x}\left\langle v,x\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{1}}\right)

(5)

where we used the triangle inequality. We will bound each of those terms separately. We have that

\left\lVert\left\langle w^{\star},x\right\rangle\left\langle v,x\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{1}}\leq\left\lVert\left\langle w^{\star},x\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\left\lVert\left\langle v,x\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\leq\left\lVert w^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}\cdot O\left(\sigma^{2}\log\frac{1}{\alpha}+\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)

where the first step is by
Equation

3
and the second is by
Lemma

C.8
. Similarly, we have that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\xi_{x}\left\langle x,x\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\xi_{x}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\left\lVert\left\langle v,x\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\left\lVert\xi_{x}-\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}\left[\xi_{x}\;\middle|\;x\right]\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}+\left\lVert\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}\left[\xi_{x}\;\middle|\;x\right]-\left\langle w^{\star},x\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}+\left\lVert\left\langle w^{\star},x\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\right)\cdot\left\lVert\left\langle v,x\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\,.

To bound the two terms appearing in the above expression, observe that, by
Lemma

C.10

\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(\frac{(\xi_{x}-\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}\left[\xi_{x}\;\middle|\;x\right])^{2}}{4}\right)\right]=\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}}\left[\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(\frac{(\xi_{x}-\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}\left[\xi_{x}\;\middle|\;x\right])^{2}}{4}\right)\;\middle|\;x\right]\right]\leq\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}}\left[\frac{8}{p_{S^{\star}}(x)}\right]\leq\frac{8}{\alpha}

where the last step is by
Equation

6
and
1
, and we denote
p_{S}(x)\coloneqq p(x,w^{\star};S)
for brevity. Hence,
\left\lVert\xi_{x}-\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}\left[\xi_{x}\;\middle|\;x\right]\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\leq O\left(\sqrt{1+\log\frac{1}{\alpha}}\right)
. Furthermore, by
Lemma

C.1
, if follows that

\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}}\left[\exp\left(\frac{(\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}\left[\xi_{x}\;\middle|\;x\right]-\left\langle w^{\star},x\right\rangle)^{2}}{4}\right)\right]\leq O(1)\cdot\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}}\left[\frac{1}{p_{S^{\star}}(x)}\right]\leq O\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)

so again
\left\lVert\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}\left[\xi_{x}\;\middle|\;x\right]-\left\langle w^{\star},x\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\leq O\left(\sqrt{1+\log\frac{1}{\alpha}}\right)
. Thus, applying
Lemma

C.8
, we obtain that

\left\lVert\xi_{x}\left\langle x,x\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{1}}\leq O\left(1+\left\lVert w^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}\right)\cdot O\left(\sigma^{2}\log\frac{1}{\alpha}+\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)\,.

We can now substitute our bounds into
Equation

5
to obtain the lemma.
∎

Appendix D
Detailed Proof of Main Result (
Theorem

3.1
)

In this section, we will formally prove
Theorem

3.1
, which our main result for learning
w^{\star}
in our truncated regression setting. We gave an overview of the proof in
Section

3
, but here we will rigorously present all technical parts and intermediate lemmas needed to prove the theorem. To begin with, we state the following definition that will be used repeatedly throughout the proofs.

Definition 4

(Survival Probability)
.

Given a measurable
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}
and two vectors
x,w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, define the survival probability of
x
and parameter
w
with respect to the survival set
S
as
p(x,w;S)\coloneqq\euscr{N}\left(S;\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\!,1\right)
.

We will often use the shorthand
p_{S}(x)\coloneq p(x,w^{\star};S)
. With this definition, the observed distribution of the features (see
Equation

1
) can be rewritten as

\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}(x)\coloneqq\frac{p(x,w^{\star};S^{\star})\cdot\euscr{D}(x)}{\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\euscr{D}}\left[p(x,w^{\star};S^{\star})\right]}=\frac{p_{S^{\star}}(x)\cdot\euscr{D}(x)}{\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\euscr{D}}\left[p_{S^{\star}}(x)\right]}\,.

(6)

Next, recall from our discussion in the main body that we are working under the following assumptions, which we state again using our new notation.

Assumption 1

(Survival Probability)
.

There is a known
\alpha\in(0,1)
such that
\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\euscr{D}}[p(x,w^{\star};S^{\star})]\geq\alpha.

Assumption 2

(Sub-Gaussianity of
x
)
.

There is a known
\sigma\geq 1
such that
x\sim\euscr{D}
is
\sigma
-sub-Gaussian. That is, for any unit vector
v
, it holds that
\left\lVert\left\langle x-\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{\euscr{D}}x,v\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\leq\sigma
.

Assumption 3

(Identifiability)
.

There is a constant
\rho>0
such that
\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}}\left[xx^{\top}\right]\succeq\rho^{2}\cdot I
.

In
2
as given in the main body, we additionally assume that
\left\lVert w^{\star}\right\rVert
is bounded by a known constant
R
. Also, in the statement of
Theorem

3.1
, we assume a constant upper bound on
\left\lVert\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{\euscr{D}}x\right\rVert_{2}
. Moving forward, in order to keep dependencies clear, we will not assume any such bounds and will instead explicitly write these norms. We will also often use the shorthand

Proof.

\Pr_{\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}}\left(\left|\left\langle w^{\star},x-\widehat{\mu}\right\rangle\right|\leq r\right)\geq 1-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}

Now, let

be the distribution over

\displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}}\left[p_{T}(x)\right]

\displaystyle=\Pr_{\euscr{P}}\left(y\in T\right)

\displaystyle\geq\Pr_{\euscr{P}}\left(y\in T\;\middle|\;\left|\left\langle w^{\star},x-\widehat{\mu}\right\rangle\right|\leq r\right)\cdot\Pr_{\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}}\left(\left|\left\langle w^{\star},x-\widehat{\mu}\right\rangle\right|\leq r\right)

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)\cdot\Pr_{\euscr{P}}\left(y\in T\;\middle|\;\left|\left\langle w^{\star},x-\widehat{\mu}\right\rangle\right|\leq r\right)\,.

To bound this last probability, observe that since

\displaystyle\Pr_{\euscr{P}}\left(y\in T\;\middle|\;\left|\left\langle w^{\star},x-\widehat{\mu}\right\rangle\right|\leq r\right)

\displaystyle\geq\Pr_{z\sim\euscr{N}(0,1)}\left(\left|z\right|\leq L-r\right)

D.2
Proof of Parameter Estimation Guarantees (

Throughout this section, we will assume that we have computed an approximation

\mathscr{L}_{S}(w)\coloneqq-\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}}\left[\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{y\sim\euscr{N}\left(\left\langle w^{\star},x\right\rangle,1,S\cap S^{\star}\right)}\left[\log\euscr{N}\left(y;\left\langle w,x\right\rangle,1,S\right)\;\middle|\;x\right]\right]\,.

In all that follows, we will drop the inner conditioning when it is clear from context. As we discussed, in the general case, the perturbed log-likelihood is not a log-likelihood and, hence, may not have any desirable typical properties of log-likelihood, such as a global minimum at
w^{\star}
. Note that the perturbed NLL is different from simply substituting
S

in the expression of the negative log-likelihood,

, it is different from the expression

The reason for this choice is that, to run our PSGD algorithm, we will need to implement a gradient sampler for
\mathscr{L}_{S}
, and this will require us to approximately sample from the distribution in the expression of

. That is, we will need to approximately sample from the distribution over

where the marginal of
x
is
\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}
and the marginal of
y
conditioned on

is
\euscr{N}(\left\langle w^{\star},x\right\rangle,1,S\cap S^{\star})
, and we will see that this is indeed possible. However, if we instead used the latter display for the expression of the perturbed likelihood, the marginal of
y
conditioned on
x
in the distribution we wish to sample from would be
\euscr{N}(\left\langle w^{\star},x\right\rangle,1,S)

\left\lVert w^{\star}-w_{\min}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\kappa}\left\langle\boldsymbol{\mathchar 626\relax}\mathscr{L}_{S}(w^{\star}),w^{\star}-w_{\min}\right\rangle\leq\frac{1}{\kappa}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathchar 626\relax}\mathscr{L}_{S}(w^{\star})\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert w^{\star}-w_{\min}\right\rVert_{2}\,.

The claim now follows by
Lemma

D.10
.
∎

Therefore,

be arbitrarily close to

on

(over which

.

Having established this result, we now turn to algorithmic considerations for PSGD.

D.2.5
Efficient Gradient Sampler

To run the PSGD algorithm we need, at each iteration, to produce a vector
g(w)
such that
\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}\left[g(w)\right]=\boldsymbol{\mathchar 626\relax}\mathscr{L}_{S}(w)
, where
w\in\mathbb{R}
is the point we are considering at the current iteration. Recall from
Lemma

D.5
that

\boldsymbol{\mathchar 626\relax}\mathscr{L}_{S}(w)=\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}}\left[-\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{y\sim\euscr{N}\left(\left\langle w^{\star},x\right\rangle,1,S^{\star}\cap S\right)}\left[y\cdot x\right]+\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{z\sim\euscr{N}\left(\left\langle w,x\right\rangle,1,S\right)}\left[z\cdot x\right]\right]\,.

Implementing an exact gradient sampler is challenging. For example, to sample from the distribution of the first term, one approach would be to draw samples
(x,y)
from our truncated regression model, and then perform rejection sampling to ensure that
y\in S
. This would indeed give the correct marginal of
y
given
x
(
i.e.
,
\euscr{N}\left(\left\langle w^{\star},x\right\rangle,1,S^{\star}\cap S\right)
), however the marginal of
x
would not be
\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}
, but a biased version of it. Furthermore, for the second term, one could first draw
x\sim\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}
from the truncated regression model to ensure that the marginal of
x
is correct, and then use rejection sampling to draw
z\sim\euscr{N}\left(\left\langle w,x\right\rangle,1,S\right)
conditionally on
x
. This would indeed give an unbiased sample for the second term; however, because we have not assumed that every
x
in the support of
\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}
has at least constant survival probability
p(x,w,S)
, we would be unable to bound the runtime of the rejection sampling procedure.

Therefore, instead of implementing an exact gradient sampler, we will instead focus on generating
approximate
gradient samples
g(w)
such that
\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}\left[g(w)\right]=\boldsymbol{\mathchar 626\relax}\mathscr{L}_{S}(w)+b
, for some controlled bias vector

Lemma D.12

We now show a change-of-measure inequality between two truncated Gaussians with the same mean but different truncation sets, in the case where the truncation set of one Gaussian is a subset of the truncation set of the other.

Proposition D.25

(Change-of-Measure between Truncated Gaussians)
.

Let
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
, and let
S,T\subset\mathbb{R}
be measurable sets, such that
S\subseteq T
. Define
a\coloneqq\euscr{N}(S;\nu,1)
and
b\coloneqq\euscr{N}(T;\nu,1)
the mass that the untruncated Gaussian
\euscr{N}(\nu,1)
places on
S
and
T
respectively. Then for any event
\euscr{E}
it holds that

\Pr_{z\sim\euscr{N}(\nu,1,S)}(\euscr{E})\leq\frac{\euscr{N}(T;\nu,1)}{\euscr{N}(S;\nu,1)}\cdot\Pr_{z\sim\euscr{N}(\nu,1,T)}(\euscr{E})\,.

Proof.

By definition of the truncated Gaussian we have

\displaystyle\Pr_{z\sim\euscr{N}(\nu,1,S)}(\euscr{E})

\displaystyle=\int\mathds{1}_{\euscr{E}}(z)\cdot\euscr{N}(z;\nu,1,S)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\int\mathds{1}_{\euscr{E}}(z)\cdot\frac{\euscr{N}(z;\nu,1)\cdot\mathds{1}\left\{z\in S\right\}}{\euscr{N}(S;\nu,1)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\frac{\euscr{N}(T;\nu,1)}{\euscr{N}(S;\nu,1)}\int\mathds{1}_{\euscr{E}}(z)\cdot\frac{\euscr{N}(z;\nu,1)\cdot\mathds{1}\left\{z\in S\right\}}{\euscr{N}(T;\nu,1)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\euscr{N}(T;\nu,1)}{\euscr{N}(S;\nu,1)}\int\mathds{1}_{\euscr{E}}(z)\cdot\frac{\euscr{N}(z;\nu,1)\cdot\mathds{1}\left\{z\in T\right\}}{\euscr{N}(T;\nu,1)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\frac{\euscr{N}(T;\nu,1)}{\euscr{N}(S;\nu,1)}\int\mathds{1}_{\euscr{E}}(z)\cdot\euscr{N}(z;\nu,1,T)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\frac{\euscr{N}(T;\nu,1)}{\euscr{N}(S;\nu,1)}\cdot\Pr_{z\sim\euscr{N}(\nu,1,T)}(\euscr{E})

where the inequality is because
S\subseteq T
.

Having established our helper lemmas, we now turn to showing the following key bound on the distance of the expectations of two truncated Gaussians with different truncation sets.

Lemma D.26
.

Let
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
and
S,T\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be measurable sets such that
S\subseteq T
. Let
p_{S}\coloneqq\euscr{N}(S;\nu,1)

p_{T}\coloneqq\euscr{N}(T;\nu,1)

\euscr{N}(\nu,1)

S

T

\left|\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{y\sim\euscr{N}\left(\nu,1,S\right)}\left[y\right]-\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{z\sim\euscr{N}\left(\nu,1,T\right)}\left[z\right]\right|\leq O\left(\sqrt{\left(1+\log\frac{1}{p_{T}}\right)\cdot\log\frac{p_{T}}{p_{S}}}\right)\,.

Proof.

Our argument is similar to that of Lemma B.4 of
[
LMZ24a
]
. Let
\zeta>0
be arbitrary, and let
\left\{y^{(i)}\right\}_{i\in[n]}

Given

C.10

\left|\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{y\sim\euscr{N}\left(\nu,1,S\right)}\left[y\right]-\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{z\sim\euscr{N}\left(\nu,1,T\right)}\left[z\right]\right|\leq\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}y^{(i)}-\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{y\sim\euscr{N}\left(\nu,1,T\right)}\left[y\right]\right|_{2}+\zeta\,.

Appendix E
Necessity of
3

In this section, we will give a simple example of a setting where
3
fails and recovering
w^{\star}
is impossible, even while the rest of our assumptions are satisfied. Intuitively, if there exists a direction of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
where we see almost no samples from the truncated regression model (
i.e.
, if the eigenvalues of
\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}}\left[xx^{\top}\right]
are not bounded away from zero), then we cannot hope to recover the component of
w^{\star}
along that direction.

To state our example, we begin with some notation. For
i\in[d]
, let
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the
i
-th standard unit vector of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, so that
\left\{x_{i}\right\}_{i\in[d]}
are the vertices of the simplex on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
\euscr{D}
be the uniform distribution over
\left\{x_{i}\right\}_{i\in[d]}
, and let
S^{\star}\coloneq[-1,1]
be our truncation set in one dimension. Since
\euscr{D}
has bounded support, it is immediate that
2
is satisfied, and clearly
S^{\star}
is a union of a bounded number of intervals. For large
B\gg 1
, we consider two possible parameter vectors
w^{(1)}\coloneq B\cdot x_{1}
and
w^{(2)}\coloneq-B\cdot x_{1}
. For
j=1,2
, let
\euscr{P}^{(j)}
denote the distribution over
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
induced by our truncated regression model (
Definition

1
) with base distribution
\euscr{D}
, survival set
S^{\star}
, and parameter vector
w^{(j)}
.

Our claim is that
\euscr{P}^{(1)}
and
\euscr{P}^{(2)}
have vanishingly small TV distance as
B
grows, and therefore, even if we know a priori that the true parameter vector is either
w^{(1)}
and
w^{(2)}
, we still cannot deduce which one it actually is using only sample access to the truncated regression model. To see this, we first compute the survival probability of each point
x_{i}
in the support of
\euscr{D}
. For
i=1
, by symmetry of the Gaussian distribution and of
S^{\star}
, the survival probability is the same under both
w^{(1)}
and
w^{(2)}
, and it is given by

\zeta\coloneq p_{S^{\star}}(x_{1})=\euscr{N}(B,1;[-1,1])\leq\exp\left(-\frac{(B-1)^{2}}{2}\right)

where we used standard Gaussian concentration. Clearly,
\zeta\to 0
as
B\to\infty
. For
j>1
, the survival probability is again the same under both
w^{(1)}
and
w^{(2)}
, and it is given by
\beta\coloneq p_{S^{\star}}(x_{j})=\euscr{N}(0,1;[-1,1])\approx 0.68
. Hence, in both cases we have

\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{\euscr{D}}\left[p_{S^{\star}}(x)\right]=\frac{1}{d}\cdot\zeta+\frac{d-1}{d}\cdot\beta\eqcolon\alpha\;.

Clearly,
\alpha\geq\nicefrac{{\beta}}{{2}}
for
d\geq 2
regardless of our choice of
B
, and so
1
is also satisfied in both cases. We now turn our attention to the observed distribution
\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}
of the features under
w^{(1)}
and
w^{(2)}
. By
Equation

6
, it is immediate that
\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}
is the same under both
w^{(1)}
and
w^{(2)}
, and its mass function is given by

\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}(x_{1})=\frac{\zeta}{\alpha d}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}(x_{j})=\frac{\beta}{\alpha d},\;\forall j>1\;.

It is clear, then, that
3
is violated, since
\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}}\left\langle x,x_{1}\right\rangle^{2}=\frac{\zeta}{\alpha d}
, and we can make this arbitrarily small by choosing
B
sufficiently large. Now, to show our claim, for
j=1,2
, let
\euscr{P}^{(j)}_{y|x_{i}}
be the
y
-marginal of
\euscr{P}^{(j)}
conditioned on
x_{i}
, so that
\euscr{P}^{(j)}_{y|x_{i}}=\euscr{N}(\left\langle w^{(j)},x_{i}\right\rangle,1,S^{\star})
. For
i>2
, it is clear that
\euscr{P}^{(1)}_{y|x_{i}}=\euscr{P}^{(2)}_{y|x_{i}}
by definition of
w^{(1)}
and
w^{(2)}
. Therefore

\displaystyle\operatorname{d}_{\mathsf{TV}}(\euscr{P}^{(1)},\euscr{P}^{(2)})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in[d]}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\euscr{P}^{(1)}(x_{i},y)-\euscr{P}^{(2)}(x_{i},y)\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sum_{i\in[d]}\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}(x_{i})\cdot\left|\euscr{P}^{(1)}_{y|x_{i}}(y)-\euscr{P}^{(2)}_{y|x_{i}}(y)\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}(x_{1})\cdot\left|\euscr{P}^{(1)}_{y|x_{1}}(y)-\euscr{P}^{(2)}_{y|x_{1}}(y)\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq\euscr{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}(x_{1})=\frac{\zeta}{\alpha d}

which, again, can be made vanishingly small by choosing
B
sufficiently large.
