Almost All Vectorial Functions Have No Extended-Affine Symmetries
Keita Ishizuka

Abstract
We prove that asymptotically almost all vectorial functions over finite fields have trivial extended-affine stabilizers. As a consequence, the number of EA-equivalence classes is asymptotically equal to the naive estimate, namely the total number of functions divided by the size of the EA-group, with vanishing relative error. Furthermore, we derive upper bounds on collision probabilities for both extended-affine and CCZ equivalences. For EA-equivalence, we leverage the trivial-stabilizer result to establish a matching lower bound, yielding a tight asymptotic formula that shows two independently sampled functions are EA-equivalent with super-exponentially small probability. The results validate random sampling strategies for cryptographic primitive design and show that functions with nontrivial EA-symmetries form an exponentially rare subset.



Keywords:
Boolean functions, extended-affine equivalence, group action, orbit counting, asymptotic enumeration

1
Introduction

Vectorial Boolean functions play a crucial role in symmetric
cryptography, serving as S-boxes in block ciphers and hash functions. The classification
of these functions is typically studied modulo certain equivalence relations that preserve
important cryptographic properties. Extended-affine (EA) equivalence is one of the most
fundamental such relations. It preserves differential uniformity, a key
measure of resistance against differential cryptanalysis, as well as the Walsh spectrum and
algebraic degree. A closely related notion is CCZ-equivalence, introduced
by Carlet, Charpin, and Zinoviev
[
1
]
, which also preserves some cryptographic
properties and is more general than EA-equivalence.

Two fundamental questions arise in the study of EA-equivalence:

1.

Classification:
How many EA-equivalence classes are there? Since EA-equivalent
functions share the same cryptographic properties, counting equivalence classes determines the
number of essentially distinct functions relevant for symmetric cryptography. This is crucial
for understanding the diversity of cryptographic primitives.

2.

Random sampling:
When searching for functions with desirable properties via random generation, is there
a risk of repeatedly sampling EA-equivalent functions? In other words, does random search remain
effective modulo EA-equivalence, or do collision events significantly limit the exploration of
the equivalence class space?

Both questions are intimately connected to the structure of EA-stabilizer groups. By the
orbit-stabilizer theorem, the size of each equivalence class is inversely proportional to the
stabilizer size, which directly impacts both the total number of classes and the collision
probability. However, explicit computation of stabilizers is infeasible except for very small
dimensions, as the function space grows doubly exponentially with the input dimension.

1.1
Our Contributions

This paper resolves these questions by establishing that asymptotically almost all vectorial
functions have trivial EA-stabilizers. Our main contributions are as follows:

1.

Main theorem
(Theorem
3.4
): We prove that for a uniformly random vectorial function over

\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
, the probability of having a nontrivial EA-stabilizer decays
super-exponentially.

2.

Counting equivalence classes
(Corollary
3.6
): We establish that the number of
EA-equivalence classes is asymptotically equal to the total number of functions divided by the
size of the EA-group, with vanishing relative error. This provides a simple and conceptually
clear asymptotic formula grounded in the triviality of stabilizers.

3.

Collision probabilities
(Proposition
3.7
and Corollary
3.8
): We derive upper bounds on collision probabilities
for both EA- and CCZ-equivalence, and show that for EA-equivalence, the upper bound is
asymptotically tight. This establishes that two independently sampled functions are EA-equivalent
with super-exponentially small probability, validating random sampling strategies for
cryptographic primitive design.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that functions with nontrivial EA-symmetries form an exponentially rare subset. This complements existing approaches focused on specific cryptographic properties and provides a theoretical foundation for understanding the prevalence of symmetries in the space of vectorial functions.

1.2
Related Work

Previous work on symmetries of Boolean functions has primarily focused on simpler group actions.
Clausen
[
3
]
proved that almost all Boolean functions (not vectorial) have no
linear symmetries under the action of
\mathrm{GL}(n,2)
. Clote and Kranakis
[
4
]
established
similar results for invariance groups under variable permutations. However, these results apply
only to Boolean functions
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
and do not address EA-equivalence, which involves
affine transformations on both domain and range, a key distinction for vectorial functions.

A different line of research focuses on counting equivalence classes.
Hou
[
5
]
obtained exact formulas for affine equivalence classes and asymptotic
formulas for EA-equivalence classes of Boolean functions using Burnside’s lemma and
compound matrix rank estimates.
Lu et al.
[
6
]
extended these results to vectorial Boolean functions, deriving
exact formulas via Möbius inversion and asymptotic formulas by applying Hou’s
matrix-theoretic techniques.

Our work unifies these two lines of research by establishing the connection between
symmetries and orbit enumeration for vectorial functions under EA-equivalence.
We prove that almost all vectorial functions have trivial EA-stabilizers,
extending the symmetry-breaking results of Clausen and Clote-Kranakis to the vectorial
EA setting.
As an immediate consequence via the orbit-stabilizer theorem, we recover the asymptotic
orbit counts of Hou
[
5
]
and Lu et al.
[
6
]
with an elementary probabilistic proof, and reveal
the underlying structural reason: the naive estimate (total number of functions divided by
the group size) is asymptotically exact because stabilizers are trivial with high probability.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Group Actions and Probabilistic Tools

Let
q
be a prime power and let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
denote the finite field with
q
elements.
Let
U=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
and
W=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
denote finite vector spaces over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
The
general linear group

\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
consists of all invertible
n\times n

matrices over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and has order

|\mathrm{GL}(n,q)|=\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}(q^{n}-q^{i}).

Definition 2.1

(Affine general linear group)
.

The
affine general linear group

\mathrm{AGL}(U)
is the semidirect product

U\rtimes\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
, acting on
U
by
\sigma(x)=Px+a
with
P\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
and
a\in U
.
Its order is
|\mathrm{AGL}(U)|=q^{n}\cdot|\mathrm{GL}(n,q)|
.

Definition 2.2

(Orbits and stabilizers)
.

Let
G
be a group acting on a set
X
. For
x\in X
, the
G
-orbit
of
x
is

G\cdot x:=\{g\cdot x:g\in G\}.

The
stabilizer
of
x
is

\mathrm{Stab}_{G}(x):=\{g\in G:g\cdot x=x\}.

For each
g\in G
, the
fixed-point set
is

\mathrm{Fix}(g):=\{x\in X:g\cdot x=x\}.

Lemma 2.3

(Orbit-stabilizer theorem)
.

For any
x\in X
, the size of the orbit
G\cdot x
satisfies

|G\cdot x|=\frac{|G|}{|\mathrm{Stab}_{G}(x)|}.

Lemma 2.4

(Burnside’s lemma)
.

The number of
G
-orbits is given by

|X/G|=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}|\mathrm{Fix}(g)|.

We use standard asymptotic notation: for functions
f,g:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,
we write
f(n)=O(g(n))
if there exist constants
C>0
and
n_{0}
such that

f(n)\leq C\cdot g(n)
for all
n\geq n_{0}
. We write
f(n)=\Omega(g(n))
if

g(n)=O(f(n))
, and
f(n)=o(g(n))
if
\lim_{n\to\infty}f(n)/g(n)=0
.

Lemma 2.5

(Union bound)
.

If
E_{1},\dots,E_{k}
are events, then

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}E_{i}\Bigr)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{P}(E_{i}).

2.2
Vectorial Functions and Equivalence Relations

Definition 2.6

(Vectorial functions)
.

We write
\mathcal{F}=\{F:U\to W\}
for the set of all functions from
U
to
W
.
Note that
|\mathcal{F}|=q^{mq^{n}}
.

When
q=2
, functions in
\mathcal{F}
are called
vectorial Boolean functions
.
They play a central role in symmetric cryptography, where they are used as
S-boxes in block ciphers and hash functions.
The cryptographic quality of a vectorial function is measured by several properties.

Differential uniformity
quantifies resistance to differential cryptanalysis and is
defined as the maximum number of solutions to
F(x+a)-F(x)=b
over all nonzero
a
and all

b
. The
Walsh spectrum
and
nonlinearity
measure resistance to linear cryptanalysis,
while the
algebraic degree
controls the complexity of algebraic attacks. Functions
optimizing these properties, such as almost perfect nonlinear (APN) and almost bent (AB)
functions, are of particular interest. Both EA-equivalence and CCZ-equivalence (defined below)
preserve these cryptographic properties. For further details on these properties and their
cryptanalytic significance, we refer to
[
2
]
.

Definition 2.7

(Extended-affine equivalence)
.

The
extended-affine (EA) group

\Gamma:=\mathrm{AGL}(U)\times\mathrm{AGL}(W)

acts on
\mathcal{F}
by conjugating the input and output with affine maps:

(A_{\rm out},A_{\rm in})\cdot F:=A_{\rm out}\circ F\circ A_{\rm in}.

Explicitly, if
A_{\rm in}(x)=Px+a
and
A_{\rm out}(y)=Qy+b
, then

\bigl((P,a,Q,b)\cdot F\bigr)(x)=Q\,F(Px+a)+b.

Two functions
F,G\in\mathcal{F}
are
EA-equivalent
, denoted by
F\simeq_{\mathrm{EA}}G
, if they lie in the same

\Gamma
-orbit. The
EA-stabilizer
of
F
is
\mathrm{Stab}_{\Gamma}(F)
.

Definition 2.8

(Graph of a function)
.

For a function
F:U\to W
, the
graph
of
F
is the subset

\Gamma_{F}:=\{(x,F(x)):x\in U\}\subseteq U\times W=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n+m}.

Definition 2.9

(CCZ-equivalence)
.

Two functions
F,G:U\to W
are
CCZ-equivalent
(Carlet-Charpin-Zinoviev equivalent)
if their graphs
\Gamma_{F}
and
\Gamma_{G}
are affine equivalent as subsets of
U\times W
.
That is,
F
and
G
are CCZ-equivalent, denoted by
F\simeq_{\mathrm{CCZ}}G
, if there exists
L\in\mathrm{AGL}(U\times W)
such that

L(\Gamma_{F})=\Gamma_{G}
.

CCZ-equivalence is more general than EA-equivalence: every pair of EA-equivalent functions
is also CCZ-equivalent.

3
Main Results

3.1
Fixed-Point and Orbit Structure of Affine Permutations

Before analysing EA-stabilizers, we establish key properties of affine permutations
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
that will control the orbit structure.

Lemma 3.1

(Fixed-point structure)
.

Let
\sigma(x)=Px+a
be an affine permutation of
U=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
with
(P,a)\neq(I,0)
.
Then the fixed-point set

\{x\in U:\sigma(x)=x\}

is either empty or an affine subspace of dimension at most
n-1
.
In particular,
|\{x\in U:\sigma(x)=x\}|\leq q^{n-1}
.

Proof.

The fixed-point equation
Px+a=x
is equivalent to
(P-I)x=-a
.
Since this is a system of linear equations, the solution set (if nonempty) is an
affine subspace. If
P\neq I
, then
\operatorname{rank}(P-I)\geq 1
, so the solution space
has dimension at most
n-1
. If
P=I
and
a\neq 0
(pure translation), there are
no fixed points.
∎

Lemma 3.2

(Orbit count)
.

Let
\sigma
be any permutation of a finite set of size
N
, with
f
fixed points
and
s
orbits. Then

s\leq\frac{N+f}{2}.

In particular, if
\sigma(x)=Px+a
is a nontrivial affine permutation of
U=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
,
then the number of orbits satisfies

s\leq\frac{q^{n}+q^{n-1}}{2}=\frac{q+1}{2q}\cdot q^{n}.

Proof.

The
f
fixed points each form singleton orbits. The remaining
N-f
non-fixed points
lie in orbits of size at least
2
, so they contribute at most
(N-f)/2
orbits.
Hence

s\leq f+\frac{N-f}{2}=\frac{N+f}{2}.

For a nontrivial affine permutation, Lemma
3.1
gives
f\leq q^{n-1}
,
so

s\leq\frac{q^{n}+q^{n-1}}{2}=\frac{q^{n-1}(q+1)}{2}=\frac{q+1}{2q}\cdot q^{n}.

∎

3.2
Probabilistic Analysis of EA-Stabilizers

We now analyse the stabilizer distribution and deduce
precise asymptotics for the number of EA-classes.

Lemma 3.3

(Fixed-point bound for EA-group elements)
.

Let
g=(P,a,Q,b)\in\Gamma
be nontrivial.
Then the number of
F\in\mathcal{F}
satisfying
g\cdot F=F
is bounded by

|\mathrm{Fix}(g)|\;\leq\;q^{cmq^{n}}

for some constant
c<1
independent of
n,m
.

Proof.

The fixed-point condition
g\cdot F=F
reads

Q\,F(Px+a)+b=F(x)\quad(\forall x\in U).

(1)

We distinguish two cases.

Case 1:
(P,a)\neq(I,0)
.

Let
\sigma(x):=Px+a
; by assumption
\sigma\neq\mathrm{id}
.
Lemma
3.2
shows that
\sigma
has at most

s\leq\frac{q+1}{2q}\cdot q^{n}

orbits.
Decompose
U
into
\sigma
-orbits:

U=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{s}O_{i},\qquad O_{i}=\{x_{i},\sigma(x_{i}),\sigma^{2}(x_{i}),\dots,\sigma^{L_{i}-1}(x_{i})\},

where
L_{i}\geq 1
is the length of orbit
O_{i}
.

Fix an orbit
O_{i}
of length
L_{i}
.
Iterating (
1
) starting from
x_{i}
gives

\displaystyle F(x_{i})

\displaystyle=Q\,F(\sigma(x_{i}))+b

\displaystyle=Q^{2}F(\sigma^{2}(x_{i}))+(I+Q)b

\displaystyle\;\;\vdots

\displaystyle=Q^{L_{i}}F(x_{i})+\sum_{j=0}^{L_{i}-1}Q^{j}b.

Hence
F(x_{i})
must satisfy

\bigl(I-Q^{L_{i}}\bigr)F(x_{i})=\sum_{j=0}^{L_{i}-1}Q^{j}b.

(2)

The number of solutions
F(x_{i})\in W
to (
2
) is at most

q^{\dim\ker(I-Q^{L_{i}})}\leq q^{m}
.
Once
F(x_{i})
is chosen, the values
F(\sigma^{k}(x_{i}))
for
1\leq k<L_{i}
are
uniquely determined by (
1
).

Repeating this argument for each orbit, we see that
F
is determined by
choosing at most one free parameter (of size
\leq q^{m}
) per orbit.
Since
s\leq(q+1)/(2q)\cdot q^{n}
, the total number of admissible
F
is at most

|\mathrm{Fix}(g)|\leq(q^{m})^{s}\;\leq\;q^{m\cdot\frac{q+1}{2q}\cdot q^{n}}=q^{\frac{q+1}{2q}mq^{n}}.

Hence
c=(q+1)/(2q)
suffices in this case.

Case 2:
(P,a)=(I,0)
but
(Q,b)\neq(I,0)
.

Now (
1
) becomes

Q\,F(x)+b=F(x)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad(I-Q)F(x)=b\qquad(\forall x\in U).

Since
(Q,b)\neq(I,0)
, either
Q\neq I
or
b\neq 0
.
If
Q\neq I
, then
\operatorname{rank}(I-Q)\geq 1
, so
\dim\ker(I-Q)\leq m-1
;
the equation
(I-Q)F(x)=b
has at most
q^{m-1}
solutions for each
x
.
If
Q=I
and
b\neq 0
, there are no solutions.
In either case, for each
x
independently,
F(x)
has at most
q^{m-1}
choices.
Hence

|\mathrm{Fix}(g)|\;\leq\;(q^{m-1})^{q^{n}}=q^{(m-1)q^{n}}.

Thus
c=1-1/m
works when
m\geq 1
.

Combining both cases, we may take
c=\max\bigl\{(q+1)/(2q),1-1/m\bigr\}<1
.
∎

Theorem 3.4

(Almost all functions have trivial stabilizers)
.

For a uniformly random
F\in\mathcal{F}
,

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\mathrm{Stab}_{\Gamma}(F)\neq\{\mathrm{id}\}\bigr)\;\leq\;|\Gamma|\cdot q^{-(1-c)mq^{n}}\;=\;q^{-\Omega(mq^{n})}.

Proof.

Observe that
\mathrm{Stab}_{\Gamma}(F)\neq\{\mathrm{id}\}
if and only if there exists a nontrivial

g\in\Gamma
such that
g\cdot F=F
. For each such
g
, define the event

E_{g}:=\{F\in\mathcal{F}:g\cdot F=F\}=\mathrm{Fix}(g).

By Lemma
3.3
, we have

\mathbb{P}(E_{g})=\frac{|\mathrm{Fix}(g)|}{|\mathcal{F}|}\leq q^{-(1-c)mq^{n}}.

Applying the union bound over all
|\Gamma|-1
nontrivial elements of
\Gamma
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigl(\mathrm{Stab}_{\Gamma}(F)\neq\{\mathrm{id}\}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\bigcup_{g\in\Gamma\setminus\{\mathrm{id}\}}E_{g}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{g\in\Gamma\setminus\{\mathrm{id}\}}\mathbb{P}(E_{g})

\displaystyle\leq(|\Gamma|-1)\cdot q^{-(1-c)mq^{n}}

\displaystyle\leq|\Gamma|\cdot q^{-(1-c)mq^{n}}.

To complete the proof, we bound
|\Gamma|
. Recall that

|\mathrm{GL}(n,q)|=\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}(q^{n}-q^{i})\leq(q^{n})^{n}=q^{n^{2}},

so
|\mathrm{AGL}(U)|=q^{n}\cdot|\mathrm{GL}(n,q)|\leq q^{n^{2}+n}=q^{O(n^{2})}
.
Similarly,
|\mathrm{AGL}(W)|=q^{O(m^{2})}
. Hence

|\Gamma|=|\mathrm{AGL}(U)|\cdot|\mathrm{AGL}(W)|\leq q^{O(n^{2}+m^{2})},

and the result follows.
∎

In the binary case (
q=2
), Lemma
3.2
gives
(q+1)/(2q)=3/4
,
so for
m\geq 4
we have
c=\max\{3/4,1-1/m\}=3/4
. The probability bound becomes

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\mathrm{Stab}_{\Gamma}(F)\neq\{\mathrm{id}\}\bigr)\;\leq\;2^{O(n^{2}+m^{2})}\cdot 2^{-(1/4)m2^{n}}\;=\;2^{-\Omega(m2^{n})}.

This probability decays super-exponentially in
n
, demonstrating that
vectorial Boolean functions with nontrivial EA-stabilizers are exponentially
rare. In other words, cryptographic S-boxes generically possess no EA-symmetries:
the overwhelming majority of functions in
\mathcal{F}
have trivial stabilizer groups,
and only an exponentially negligible fraction exhibit any symmetry under
extended-affine transformations.

Remark 3.5
.

Our upper bounds on
|\mathrm{Fix}(g)|
and the resulting estimate

\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{Stab}(F)\neq\{\mathrm{id}\}]\leq q^{-\Omega(mq^{n})}

are obtained via worst-case analysis.
In principle, one could refine the argument by exploiting the
cycle structure of affine permutations on
U
and
W
and by computing
the rank distribution of
Q-I
for
Q\in\mathrm{GL}(W)
.
Such refinements would slightly improve the constants in the exponent,
but would not affect the asymptotic type of the decay, and would require
a substantial amount of additional case analysis.
For the purposes of establishing asymptotic freeness, the present bounds
are entirely sufficient.

Corollary 3.6

(Asymptotic count of EA-classes)
.

Denote the set of EA-classes by
\mathcal{F}/\Gamma=\{\,\Gamma\cdot F:F\in\mathcal{F}\,\}
.
Then

\frac{|\mathcal{F}/\Gamma|}{|\mathcal{F}|/|\Gamma|}=1+o(1).

In other words, the relative error between the true number of EA-classes
and the naive estimate
|\mathcal{F}|/|\Gamma|
is
o(1)
.

Proof.

Burnside’s lemma gives

|\mathcal{F}/\Gamma|=\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\sum_{g\in\Gamma}|\mathrm{Fix}(g)|.

The identity contributes
|\mathcal{F}|
.
Each nontrivial
g
contributes at most
q^{cmq^{n}}
by Lemma
3.3
.
Hence

|\mathcal{F}/\Gamma|=\frac{|\mathcal{F}|}{|\Gamma|}+O\!\left(|\Gamma|\cdot q^{cmq^{n}}\right).

The relative error is

\frac{|\Gamma|\cdot q^{cmq^{n}}}{|\mathcal{F}|/|\Gamma|}=\frac{|\Gamma|^{2}\cdot q^{cmq^{n}}}{|\mathcal{F}|}=q^{-(1-c)mq^{n}+O(n^{2}+m^{2})}=o(1).

To justify the exponent, note that

|\mathrm{AGL}(U)|=q^{n^{2}+O(n)}
and
|\mathrm{AGL}(W)|=q^{m^{2}+O(m)}
,
so
|\Gamma|=q^{n^{2}+m^{2}+O(n+m)}
, hence

|\Gamma|^{2}=q^{2n^{2}+2m^{2}+O(n+m)}=q^{O(n^{2}+m^{2})}
.
Thus the relative error tends to zero.
∎

For the binary case (
q=2
), we have

\frac{|\mathcal{F}/\Gamma|\cdot|\mathrm{AGL}(n,2)|\cdot|\mathrm{AGL}(m,2)|}{2^{m2^{n}}}=1+o(1).

Consequently, the contribution of functions
with nontrivial EA-symmetries to the total count is exponentially negligible:
their number is exponentially smaller than the full space.
This observation is particularly relevant for structured searches in the design
of cryptographic S-boxes.

Proposition 3.7

(Upper bounds on collision probabilities)
.

For two independently and uniformly random functions
F,G\in\mathcal{F}
, the probability
that they are equivalent satisfies:

1.

EA-equivalence:

\mathbb{P}(F\sim_{\mathrm{EA}}G)\;\leq\;\frac{|\mathrm{AGL}(U)|\cdot|\mathrm{AGL}(W)|}{|\mathcal{F}|}\;=\;q^{-mq^{n}+O(n^{2}+m^{2})}\;=\;q^{-\Omega(q^{n})}.

2.

CCZ-equivalence:

\mathbb{P}(F\sim_{\mathrm{CCZ}}G)\;\leq\;\frac{|\mathrm{AGL}(U\times W)|}{|\mathcal{F}|}\;=\;q^{-mq^{n}+O((n+m)^{2})}\;=\;q^{-\Omega(q^{n})}.

Proof.

For both equivalence relations, the collision probability can be written as

\mathbb{P}(F\sim G)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\Bigl(\frac{|O_{i}|}{|\mathcal{F}|}\Bigr)^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{|O_{i}|}{|\mathcal{F}|}\cdot\frac{\max_{j}|O_{j}|}{|\mathcal{F}|}=\frac{\max_{j}|O_{j}|}{|\mathcal{F}|},

where
O_{1},\dots,O_{r}
are the orbits. By the orbit-stabilizer theorem, the maximum orbit
size is bounded by the group order. For EA-equivalence,

\max_{j}|O_{j}|\leq|\Gamma|=|\mathrm{AGL}(U)|\cdot|\mathrm{AGL}(W)|
, and for CCZ-equivalence,

\max_{j}|O_{j}|\leq|\mathrm{AGL}(U\times W)|
. The size estimates follow from

|\mathrm{AGL}(n,q)|=q^{n}\cdot|\mathrm{GL}(n,q)|=q^{n^{2}+O(n)}

and
|\mathcal{F}|=q^{mq^{n}}
, giving the stated bounds.
∎

The upper bounds in Corollary
3.7
follow directly from the
orbit-stabilizer theorem and hold for both EA- and CCZ-equivalence over arbitrary finite
fields. However, using our result on trivial stabilizers (Theorem
3.4
),
we can show that the EA upper bound is asymptotically tight:

Corollary 3.8

(Asymptotic collision probability for EA-equivalence)
.

For two independently and uniformly random functions
F,G\in\mathcal{F}
,
the probability that
F
and
G
are EA-equivalent satisfies

\mathbb{P}(F\sim_{\mathrm{EA}}G)=\frac{|\mathrm{AGL}(U)|\cdot|\mathrm{AGL}(W)|}{|\mathcal{F}|}\,(1+o(1)).

Proof.

Let
O_{1},\dots,O_{r}
be the EA-orbits. The collision probability is

\mathbb{P}(F\sim_{\mathrm{EA}}G)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\Bigl(\frac{|O_{i}|}{|\mathcal{F}|}\Bigr)^{2}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}|^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{r}|O_{i}|^{2}.

(3)

Let
\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{triv}}
be the set of orbits with trivial EA-stabilizer
and
\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{nt}}
the set of the remaining orbits.
By Theorem
3.4
, for every
\varepsilon>0
and all sufficiently large
n
,

|\{F\in\mathcal{F}:\mathrm{Stab}_{\Gamma}(F)=\{\mathrm{id}\}\}|\geq(1-\varepsilon)|\mathcal{F}|.

(4)

Hence

\sum_{O_{i}\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{triv}}}|O_{i}|=|\{F:\mathrm{Stab}_{\Gamma}(F)=\{\mathrm{id}\}\}|\geq(1-\varepsilon)|\mathcal{F}|.

Since each such orbit has size
|\Gamma|
, this implies

|\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{triv}}|\geq\frac{(1-\varepsilon)|\mathcal{F}|}{|\Gamma|}.

The contribution of the trivial-stabilizer orbits to (
3
) is therefore

\frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}|^{2}}\sum_{O_{i}\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{triv}}}|O_{i}|^{2}=\frac{|\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{triv}}|\cdot|\Gamma|^{2}}{|\mathcal{F}|^{2}}\geq(1-\varepsilon)\frac{|\Gamma|}{|\mathcal{F}|}.

For the nontrivial-stabilizer orbits, since

|O_{i}|\leq|\Gamma|
and

the probability of landing in the same EA-equivalence class is negligibly

useful for exhaustive searches of functions with special cryptographic
properties such as almost perfect nonlinear (APN) and almost bent (AB)

for redundancy modulo EA-equivalence, as collisions are exponentially rare.

4
Conclusion

We have established that asymptotically almost all vectorial functions over finite fields
have trivial EA-stabilizers. This fundamental result resolves the two questions posed in the
introduction.

1.

Classification:
The number of EA-equivalence classes is asymptotically
equal to the naive estimate (the total number of functions divided by the size of the
EA-group) with vanishing relative error. This shows that the EA group action is asymptotically
free: almost every function admits no nontrivial symmetries. For cryptographic applications, this
means that the vast majority of vectorial functions lie in distinct EA-classes, and functions
with nontrivial symmetries form an exponentially small subset.

2.

Random sampling:
Together with upper bounds on collision probabilities,
our results establish that for EA-equivalence, two independently sampled functions are equivalent
with super-exponentially small probability. This validates random sampling strategies for
searching cryptographic primitives such as APN or AB functions: exhaustive random searches
produce EA-inequivalent candidates with overwhelming probability, eliminating concerns about
redundancy modulo EA-equivalence.

While we have proved upper bounds on collision probabilities for CCZ-equivalence, the question
of whether most functions have trivial CCZ-stabilizers remains open. Establishing analogous results for CCZ-equivalence would require new techniques, as the
graph-based action is more complex than the EA group action.
