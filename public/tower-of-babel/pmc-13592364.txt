Intramedullary Ependymoma of the Cervicomedullary Junction: Gross Total Resection and Surgical Nuances in an Anatomically Rare Location
Authors: Seyed Reza Mousavi, Kamran Hosseini, Hossein Ramezani Abadeh, Mohammadhadi Amir Shahpari Motlagh, Masoud Delavari, Niloofar Borzou, Majid Reza Farrokhi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntramedullary ependymomas are rare, slow-growing tumors (WHO grade II in most cases) arising from the ependymal cells of the spinal cord’s central canal. In this case, histopathological examination confirmed a tanycytic ependymoma (WHO grade II). Although they have low potential for infiltration and seeding, and complete surgical excision is generally curative, the complex anatomy of the cervicomedullary region makes total resection particularly challenging.Case PresentationWe present the case of a 34-year-old woman who presented with subacute quadriparesis (McCormick grade III, MRC 3–4/5), diminished gag reflex, hyperreflexia, and spastic gait. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) suggested the diagnosis of a cervicomedullary junction (CMJ) ependymoma and definitive diagnosis was established histopathologically. The patient underwent successful microscopic total resection via a posterior midline approach, including suboccipital craniectomy, C1 and C2 laminectomy, and midline myelotomy, all performed under intraoperative neuromonitoring. At one-year follow-up, the patient demonstrated improvement to McCormick grade I with MRC grade 5/5 in all limbs. Postoperative MRI confirmed complete resection without evidence of local recurrence.ConclusionNeurological recovery was assessed using the McCormick Scale and Medical Research Council (MRC) muscle strength grading. The patient improved from a preoperative McCormick grade III (significant motor weakness requiring assistance for ambulation) to grade I (normal neurological function) at one-year follow-up. MRC muscle strength improved from 3–4/5 in all limbs preoperatively to 5/5 in all limbs postoperatively. Postoperative MRI at one-year follow-up confirmed complete resection without evidence of local recurrence. This case suggests that gross total resection of cervicomedullary ependymomas under intraoperative neuromonitoring is achievable and can result in excellent neurological outcomes, highlighting the importance of precise microsurgical techniques and intraoperative monitoring in managing these challenging tumors.

Introduction

Ependymomas are a subset of rare neuroectodermal tumors that account for approximately 2–8% of all primary central nervous system (CNS) tumors.1 These neoplasms arise from ependymal cells lining the ventricles of the brain and the central canal of the spinal cord and can manifest in various locations throughout the CNS.2 Among spinal cord ependymomas, a significant proportion—estimated at 25–42%—is localized within the cervical region, highlighting the anatomical specificity and clinical relevance of these tumors.3 Cervicomedullary junction (CMJ) is defined as the region from the obex to the C2 dorsal root entry zone – a distinct anatomical entity different from the lower cervical spinal cord. Characteristically, ependymomas are non-encapsulated, slow-growing tumors, with most spinal cases classified as WHO grade II, but they can extend across multiple spinal segments, ranging from one to as many as eight segments.4

The occurrence of intramedullary ependymomas in the cervicomedullary region is particularly rare and presents formidable clinical challenges.5 The proximity of these tumors to critical neuroanatomical structures, including the brainstem, raises concerns regarding potential neurological deficits resulting from compression. Patients may present with a spectrum of symptoms, including but not limited to motor weakness, sensory disturbances, and autonomic dysfunction, which can significantly impair quality of life and functional independence.6 Although cervical ependymomas account for 25–42% of spinal ependymomas, true CMJ ependymomas are exceptionally rare. According to Fei et al,7 among 155 consecutive patients with upper cervical spinal cord ependymomas, only 8 cases (5.1%) involved the true CMJ. Furthermore, a systematic review by Kasper et al8 reported that less than 3% of all spinal ependymomas occur at the CMJ.

The novelty of this case lies in three specific aspects: First, real-time intraoperative neuromonitoring (IONM) changes during CMJ tumor dissection and their impact on surgical decision-making are described in detail. Second, complete neurological recovery from severe preoperative quadriparesis (muscle power 3–4/5) following gross total resection is documented. Third, this report emphasizes surgical strategies to preserve vagal and respiratory function, specifically in CMJ tumors. Accordingly, this report aims to present the clinical, surgical, and postoperative features of this rare CMJ ependymoma and discuss relevant technical considerations for its management.

Case Presentation

A 34-year-old female, a homemaker, presented with paresthesia and dysesthesia in the upper limbs, which was followed by progressive motor weakness, bulbar dysfunction with swallowing difficulty, and lower cranial nerve involvement (vagus nerve, CN X). The progression was described as progressive and relatively gradual, with exacerbation noted over time. There was no history of neck pain or trauma. Family history was unremarkable for neurological disorders or malignancies. She had not received any prior medical treatment for this condition. On physical examination, the patient exhibited a diminished gag reflex and vagus nerve paresis. Neurological assessment revealed MRC muscle strength grading of right upper limb 3/5, left upper limb 4/5, right lower limb 3/5, and left lower limb 4/5, with exaggerated deep tendon reflexes in all four limbs, positive Hoffmann signs bilaterally, and extensor plantar reflexes (upgoing Babinski) bilaterally. Muscle tone examination demonstrated increased spasticity in both lower limbs with bilateral ankle clonus. Gait assessment revealed a spastic paraparetic gait requiring a cane or walker for ambulation, with circumduction and foot drag, and balance was impaired with difficulty performing tandem gait and an inability to perform heel-to-toe walking. Preoperative functional status was classified as McCormick Scale grade III (significant motor weakness requiring assistance for ambulation) and Modified Rankin Scale score 4 (moderately severe disability, unable to walk without assistance). Systematic lower cranial nerve examination revealed: CN IX and X (Glossopharyngeal and Vagus): diminished gag reflex bilaterally; no dysarthria or nasal regurgitation, but mild difficulty with swallowing liquids was noted (patient required longer time to swallow water); uvula deviated slightly to the right. CN XI (Spinal Accessory): no weakness of sternocleidomastoid or trapezius muscles; shoulder shrug and head rotation were intact bilaterally. CN XII (Hypoglossal): tongue protrusion midline without deviation, no fasciculations or atrophy. A moderate sensory deficit was observed in the upper limbs, extending from the nape of the neck downward. Local skull and spine examinations were unremarkable. Serial MRI examinations performed during the follow-up period demonstrated a clear and significant increase in tumor size, correlating with worsening neurological deficits. The patient’s hesitation to accept surgical risks—given the critical location of the tumor at the cervicomedullary junction—contributed to a delay in surgical intervention. Ultimately, due to progressive worsening of her neurological symptoms, the patient accepted the surgical risks and was scheduled for surgery. MRI of the brain and cervical spine revealed an intraxial tumor measuring approximately 2.5 cm in craniocaudal length and 1.8 cm in transverse width, extending from the medullary region to the second cervical vertebra. The tumor appeared iso-to hypointense on T1-weighted images and hyperintense on T2-weighted images, with moderate homogeneous enhancement following contrast administration (Figure 1). No associated peritumoral edema, syringomyelia, or cystic components were identified. Surgical Procedure

After general anesthesia and fiber-optic orotracheal intubation, neuromonitoring, including somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEP) and motor evoked potentials (MEP), was applied prior to positioning in order to have baseline parameters. In a standard prone position, after a linear midline incision, a posterior fossa midline craniectomy (2*2 cm) and laminectomy of the first and second cervical vertebrae were performed. A longitudinal dural opening, using stay sutures, to avoid overt tension on the dural sac and spinal cord, was achieved. The tumor was approached caudally through a myelotomy at the dorsal root entry zone (DREZ) of C2, where the tumor was most superficial and closest to the pial surface. A DREZ myelotomy was chosen to minimize damage to the dorsal columns, as this approach provides a safe corridor through the posterolateral spinal cord, preserving the critical dorsal column-medial lemniscus pathway. The tumor–spinal cord interface was carefully inspected under the microscope and found to be well-demarcated and non-infiltrative, allowing micro-dissection along a clear cleavage plane using micro-dissectors and cottonoid patties. During and between each step, SSEP and MEP were checked and compared carefully. The caudal end of a soft, moderately vascular mass was then identified with a well-demarcated margin relative to the surrounding neural tissue. The tumor was microscopically dissected under continuous neuromonitoring through its marginal plane. Intraoperative frozen section analysis suggested a glial lesion, prompting the decision for microscopic total resection using a piecemeal approach. The decision to proceed with piecemeal rather than en bloc resection was based on the frozen section diagnosis of a glial lesion and the tumor’s moderately vascular nature, which made en bloc removal potentially hazardous. After each major dissection step, MEP and SSEP signals were rechecked; no significant signal deterioration was observed throughout the procedure. The procedure continued until normal surrounding tissue and normal flow of CSF were observed. The patient was transferred to the intensive care unit for 2 days and then 3 days at the ward before discharge. She showed no new focal neurological deficits or major medical complications in her post-operative hospital course. Postoperatively, the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for close neurological monitoring for 48 hours. During this period, vital signs, neurological status, and wound condition were monitored hourly. No new neurological deficits or respiratory compromise developed. The patient was then transferred to the neurosurgical ward for an additional 72 hours. During this time, physical therapy and occupational therapy consultations were initiated for early mobilization. The patient was encouraged to ambulate with assistance and perform activities of daily living with minimal support. The wound was monitored for signs of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage; none was observed. She was discharged on postoperative day 5 with a rehabilitation plan.

Histopathology, Postoperative Imaging and Follow-Up

Definitive histopathological diagnosis confirmed the presence of an ependymoma. Postoperative imaging demonstrated complete tumor resection (Figure 2). The patient was monitored through regular follow-up appointments, and neurological examinations showed consistent improvement. The patient reported significant recovery in strength and functionality. At one-year follow-up, the patient demonstrated McCormick Scale grade I (normal neurological function) with MRC muscle strength 5/5 in all four limbs. Gait was normal without spasticity, and the patient required no assistive devices for ambulation. Deep tendon reflexes were normal with negative Hoffmann sign and flexor plantar reflexes bilaterally. Cranial nerve examination revealed normal gag reflex and swallowing function without dysphagia. Sensory examination revealed a sensory level at C4 bilaterally, with decreased perception of light touch and pinprick from the C4 dermatome downward. Vibration sense was diminished in both lower limbs, and proprioception was impaired in the feet. Two-point discrimination was reduced in both hands (C6-C8 distribution). Temperature sensation was relatively preserved in all areas. The sensory disturbance extended from the nape of the neck downward, involving the upper limbs, trunk, and lower limbs with a gradient of severity (more pronounced distally). Autonomic function was normal with no bowel or bladder dysfunction. The patient had returned to full-time employment as a school teacher with no limitations in activities of daily living. Follow-up MRI confirmed complete resection without evidence of local recurrence or new lesions. Figure 3 illustrates the histopathological findings. In the microscopic examination, the tumor displayed sharply circumscribed borders. The spindle-shaped tumor cells with moderate cellularity were arranged in interlacing fascicles. Hyalinization of blood vessels and deposition of hemosiderin were also prominent. In immunohistochemical staining of the permanent section, glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) was strongly positive, and S-100 was moderately positive. Additionally, epithelial membrane antigen (EMA) exhibited a perinuclear dot-like pattern typical of ependymoma, confirming the tanycytic type. Tanycytic ependymomas, characterized by long, slender cellular processes similar to those in pilocytic astrocytomas or schwannomas, most frequently occur in the spinal cord. Discussion

Intramedullary ependymomas arise from the ependymal cells lining the central canal of the spinal cord and the ventricular system of the brain. Ependymomas can occur at various locations, but a significant proportion of spinal cord ependymomas are found in the cervical region.4 Symptoms vary by tumor location and size; intratumoral hemorrhage may cause sudden neurological decline.7

Tumors in cervicomedullary region can lead to severe neurological deficits such as quadriparesis, dysphagia, and respiratory complications.9,10 The symptoms experienced by patients with intramedullary ependymomas can be diverse, encompassing motor weakness, sensory disturbances, and autonomic dysfunction, which severely impact quality of life and functional independence.5 In the cervicomedullary region, a greater risk for respiratory problems and involvement of the lower cranial nerves is present.7

Spinal ependymomas generally have a better prognosis than their cranial and posterior fossa counterparts.7,11–14 Gross total resection (GTR) remains the primary treatment modality for intramedullary ependymomas, and complete excision is often feasible due to the distinct cleavage plane associated with these tumors.15 However, the surgical challenges posed by the cervicomedullary region cannot be overstated. The complex anatomy and the risk of damaging critical neural structures necessitate meticulous planning and execution of surgical approaches.16 Previous studies have highlighted the importance of preoperative imaging and intraoperative monitoring to minimize risks and enhance surgical outcomes.17,18 The novelty of this case lies in three specific aspects: First, First, real-time intraoperative neuromonitoring (IONM) was utilized to guide surgical decision-making, particularly in the decision to proceed with piecemeal resection rather than en bloc removal based on tumor characteristics and frozen section findings. While no significant MEP or SSEP deterioration occurred during the procedure, the continuous monitoring provided reassurance and safety throughout the dissection; Second, complete neurological recovery from severe preoperative quadriparesis (muscle power 3–4/5) following gross total resection is documented; Third, this report emphasizes surgical strategies to preserve vagal and respiratory function, specifically in CMJ tumors.

A focused review of the literature identified three previously reported cases of true CMJ ependymomas. Joris et al19 reported a pediatric case of dual ependymoma spinal metastasis at C1-C2 with complete resection. Oberman et al20 described a giant cervicomedullary ependymoma in a 23-year-old female. Fei et al,7 in their large series of 155 upper cervical ependymomas, included a subset of true CMJ cases, reporting that gross total resection was achieved in the majority of cases with favorable outcomes. When comparing our case to these previously reported true CMJ ependymomas, several distinguishing features emerge. First, our patient presented with a more severe preoperative neurological deficit (quadriparesis with MRC 3–4/5, McCormick grade III, and diminished gag reflex with vagus nerve paresis) compared to the cases described by Oberman et al and Joris et al Second, the surgical strategy in our case incorporated a DREZ myelotomy specifically chosen to minimize damage to the dorsal columns, combined with continuous IONM guidance throughout the procedure—a level of technical detail not comprehensively described in prior reports. Third, despite the severity of preoperative deficits, our patient achieved complete neurological recovery (McCormick grade I, MRC 5/5 in all limbs) at one-year follow-up, which is a particularly favorable outcome given the critical location of the tumor and the presence of lower cranial nerve involvement. Fourth, our detailed documentation of the rationale for piecemeal resection based on frozen section diagnosis and tumor vascularity, along with the absence of IONM signal deterioration, provides practical insights for surgical decision-making in similar cases. Additionally, our detailed description of real-time IONM-guided decision-making adds practical value to the existing literature, particularly regarding the safety and feasibility of gross total resection in this challenging anatomical location. Compared to these reports, our case appears to have had one of the most severe preoperative presentations, with McCormick grade III status and significant lower cranial nerve involvement (quadriparesis with MRC 3–4/5 and diminished gag reflex). However, we acknowledge that direct comparison is limited by the small number of reported cases and variations in reporting methods. The advantage and disadvantages of the prone position versus SSP was compared.12 Considering that they did not mention the exact level of each case, for the cervicomedullary region, the catastrophic intraoperative complications (such as subdural hematoma and trans-foramen magnum herniation) may araise. Nichols et al studied the effect of collaboration between a cranial tumor surgeon team and a spinal surgeon in resecting spinal ependymomas on the achievement of GTR. They found that regardless of the level of the tumor, intraoperative complications and GTR were superior when cooperation took place.13

Recent literature emphasizes the significance of early diagnosis and intervention in managing ependymomas effectively. A study by Deghedy et al21 demonstrated that timely surgical intervention can lead to improved postoperative outcomes and increased survival rates for patients with ependymomas in the cervicomedullary region. Additionally, advancements in surgical techniques, including the use of intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring, have been shown to enhance the safety of resections in this complex anatomical area.22 The benign and slow-growing nature of these lesions, along with their tendency to grow circumferentially around pial structures rather than infiltrating the brainstem, allows for successful resection in many cases.23 Despite the generally benign nature of ependymomas, the potential for recurrence remains a concern, particularly in cases of incomplete resection or tumors with aggressive histological features.5 Long-term follow-up studies are essential to comprehensively understand the prognosis and recurrence patterns of ependymomas in the cervicomedullary region.24

Histopathological confirmation and regular neurological evaluations are essential for ensuring a good prognosis and minimizing recurrence risk. However, postoperative radiotherapy is not indicated for completely resected WHO grade II spinal ependymomas. Radiation therapy is reserved for specific circumstances: (1) subtotal resection with residual tumor, (2) tumor recurrence after previous GTR, or (3) anaplastic (WHO grade III) histology.5 Worse preoperative neurological status (McCormick score), cervical region, and involvement of more than 2 spinal cord segments have been considered as poor prognostic indicators.25 According to the 5th WHO classification, spinal ependymomas have four subtypes: spinal ependymoma, spinal ependymoma MYCN-amplified, myxopapillary ependymoma, and subependymoma. The latter two are classified as grade I WHO with a better prognosis. Spinal ependymoma MYCN-amplified is the worst and is categorized as grade-III. The classic spinal ependymomas can also be grade II or III based on mitotic activity.14 Various recurrence rates have been reported in the literature. While GTR and radiotherapy (for WHO grade-II or above) are against the risk, molecular features such as 1q gain and high Ki-67 index, MYCN-amplification raise the recurrence risk of these tumors. Chemotherapy has not shown any significant change in prognosis, so far.11,25 According to the WHO 2021 classification, our case is a tanycytic ependymoma, categorized as WHO grade II. This subtype is characterized by long, slender cellular processes and is most frequently found in the spinal cord. Perivascular pseudorosettes are less common than in typical ependymomas. Regarding prognosis, WHO grade II spinal ependymomas following gross total resection (GTR) have an excellent outcome, with 5-year recurrence rates reported at less than 5% in most series. Also, the risk of recurrence after gross total resection (GTR) of WHO grade II spinal ependymomas is low. Based on available systematic reviews and large series, the 5-year recurrence rate is estimated at less than 5% following complete resection. For CMJ ependymomas specifically, although data are limited due to rarity, GTR achieved in the three previously reported cases resulted in no early recurrences. Molecular data such as MYCN amplification, 1q gain, and Ki-67 index were not available for this case due to institutional resource limitations. We acknowledge this as a limitation of our report. However, based on the favorable histology (no mitotic figures, no necrosis, low cellularity) and complete resection, the recurrence risk in our patient is expected to be very low. This case underscores the importance of early diagnosis and individualized surgical strategies in managing intramedullary ependymomas. The successful outcome in this patient demonstrates the potential for positive clinical outcomes with appropriate intervention and careful postoperative care.

Tumors in this area can lead to severe neurological deficits such as quadriparesis, dysphagia, and respiratory complications.9,10 The symptoms experienced by patients with intramedullary ependymomas can be diverse, encompassing motor weakness, sensory disturbances, and autonomic dysfunction, which severely impact quality of life and functional independence.5

Surgical resection remains the primary treatment modality for intramedullary ependymomas, and complete excision is often feasible due to the distinct cleavage plane associated with these tumors.15 Adjuvant radiotherapy is not indicated for WHO grade II ependymomas following complete gross total resection. Radiation therapy is reserved for specific circumstances: (1) subtotal resection with residual tumor, (2) tumor recurrence after previous GTR, or (3) anaplastic (WHO grade III) histology. In cases of incomplete resection or higher-grade tumors, postoperative radiotherapy may be considered to improve local control and progression-free survival. However, the surgical challenges posed by the cervicomedullary region cannot be overstated. The complex anatomy and the risk of damaging critical neural structures necessitate meticulous planning and execution of surgical approaches.16 Previous studies have highlighted the importance of preoperative imaging and intraoperative monitoring to minimize risks and enhance surgical outcomes.17,18

A study by Deghedy et al21 demonstrated that timely surgical intervention can lead to improved postoperative outcomes and increased survival rates for patients with ependymomas in the cervicomedullary region. Additionally, advancements in surgical techniques, including the use of intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring, have been shown to enhance the safety of resections in this complex anatomical area.22

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that in a patient with severe preoperative quadriparesis (McCormick grade III) due to a CMJ ependymoma, gross total resection with preservation of neurological function was achievable through meticulous microsurgical dissection, intraoperative neuromonitoring, and careful surgical decision-making. At one-year follow-up, the patient achieved McCormick grade I status with complete neurological recovery. While this single case cannot establish generalizable recommendations, it suggests that favorable outcomes are possible in selected patients with this rare and challenging tumor location when appropriate surgical strategies are employed. Continued documentation of similar cases will enhance our understanding of optimal management approaches for these rare tumors.
