Exponential convergence of multiagent systems with lack of connection
Fabio Ancona, Mohamed Bentaibi, Francesco Rossi

Abstract
Finding conditions ensuring consensus, i.e. convergence to a common value, for a networked system is of crucial interest, both for theoretical reasons and applications. This goal is harder to achieve when connections between agents are temporarily lost. Here, we prove that known conditions (introduced by Moreau) ensure an exponential convergence to consensus, with explicit rate of convergence. The key result is related to the length of the graph (i.e. the number of connections to reach a common agent): if this is large, then convergence is slow. This general result also provides conditions for convergence of second-order cooperative systems with lack of connections.

Introduction and main results

In recent years, the study of multi-agent systems has drawn a huge interest in the control community. The reasons behind the impressive rise of this research topic lie in the simplicity of definition, robustness of results, extent of applications. For general books on this topic, see e.g.

Among multi-agent systems, the setting of cooperative systems plays a central role. Very generally, first-order cooperative systems (with binary interactions) are of the following form:

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{i}(t)=\frac{\lambda_{i}(x)}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\phi_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})(x_{j}(t)-x_{i}(t)).

(1)

This dynamics describes the evolution of
N\geq 2
agents on an Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where the position
x_{i}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
may represent opinion on different topics, velocity or other attributes of agent
i
at time
t
. The solution is then
x(t)=(x_{1}(t),\ldots,x_{N}(t))\in\mathbb{R}^{Nd}
. The term
\lambda_{i}
is a positive scaling parameter, which role will be discussed in detail in Section
2.3
below. The (nonlinear) influence functions

\phi_{ij}:=\phi_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j}):\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}

(2)

are used to quantify the influence of agent
j
on agent
i
, where
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
. We assume them to be positive, that corresponds to cooperativity of the system, i.e. to the fact that influence between agents tends to bring them closer. Models of this kind are now ubiquitous in networked systems and their applications. One of the most influential is certainly the bounded confidence model for opinion formation, first described (in discrete time) by Hegselmann and Krause in
[
4
]
, see also
[
5
,
6
]
. The natural goal for such kind of systems is to reach consensus: there exists a common
x^{*}
such that
\lim_{t\to+\infty}x_{i}(t)=x^{*}
for all agents
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
.

This idea of cooperation has also been extended to second-order systems, in which each agent is described by a pair
(x_{i},v_{i})
of position-velocity variables. The dynamics is then given by

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dot{x}_{i}(t)=v_{i}\\
\dot{v}_{i}=\frac{\lambda_{i}(x)}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\phi_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})(v_{j}(t)-v_{i}(t))\end{cases}

(3)

for
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
. As in (
2
), the interaction function
\phi_{ij}
is non-negative and depends on the positions
x_{i},x_{j}
, but here it is a multiplicative term for the velocity variable. The most famous example of this kind of dynamics was presented by Cucker and Smale in
[
7
]
. A first natural goal for this dynamics is alignment: the velocity variables converge to a common value
v^{*}
(i.e.
\lim_{t\to+\infty}v_{i}(t)=v^{*}
for all agents
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\})
. A second goal is the so-called flocking: one has both alignment and uniform boundedness of distances
1
1

1

i.e. there exists
K>0
such that
|x_{i}(t)-x_{j}(t)|<K
for all
t\geq 0
and
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
.

From the modelling point of view, each agent is expected to communicate with its neighbours through a
network topology
, influenced by sensor characteristics and the environment. While the easiest scenario involves a fixed network topology (e.g.
[
8
,
9
]
), practical situations often involve dynamic changes, due to factors like communication dropouts, security concerns, or intermittent actuation. In this setting, potential lack of connection between agents occurs; therefore, it becomes crucial to investigate whether consensus can still be achieved or not. For first-order systems, we model this scenario as follows:

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{i}(t)=\frac{\lambda_{i}(x)}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}M_{ij}(t)\phi_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})(x_{j}(t)-x_{i}(t))

(4)

for
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
. The terms
M_{ij}:[0,+\infty)\mapsto[0,1]
represent the weight given to the (directed) connection of agent
j
with agent
i
. They encode the time-varying network topology and account for lack of connection (e.g., when they vanish).
Given the connections
M_{ij}
, it is natural to build an associated directed graph
G(t)
where nodes are the
N
agents and the arrow
i\to j
exists only when
M_{ij}
is “sufficiently strong”. While several different concepts have been proposed in the literature (see Section
5.1
below), in this article we focus on the following integral condition.

Definition 1
(
(T,\mu)
-connectivity graph)

Let
T,\mu>0
. For each
t\geq 0
, define the
(T,\mu)
-connectivity graph associated to the connection functions
M_{ij}
as the following time-dependent oriented graph
G(t)
:

•

the set of nodes is the set of indexes
\{1,\ldots,N\}

•

the arrow
i\to j
exists if it holds

\frac{1}{T}\int_{t}^{t+T}M_{ij}(s)\,ds\geq\mu.

The key result by Moreau
[
10
]
can be stated as follows: if
G(t)
is constant with respect to time and admits a globally reachable node, then the system (
4
) exponentially converges to consensus for any initial condition, i.e. there exist
K,\delta>0
such that the solution satisfies

|x(t)|\leq K\exp(-\delta t)|x(0)|.

Yet, an explicit estimate of the parameters
K,\delta
is not provided.

The main goal of this article is to estimate these parameters under the Moreau condition. The interest of this result is that it provides formal evidence to the following intuition: if all paths to the globally reachable node are “short”, then exponential convergence is fast; otherwise, it may be slow for suitable choices of initial conditions and connection functions.

With this idea, we recall the concept of distance between nodes and length of the graph.

Definition 2

Let
G
be a graph with a globally reachable node
I
. Given an index
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, we denote by

the (directed) distance from
i
to
I
, that is the minimal length of a path from

to
I
, i.e.

d(i,I):=\min\Big\{n\mbox{~s.t.~}i\to j_{1}\to\ldots j_{n}=I\mbox{~path of~}G\Big\}.

(5)

We denote by
d(G,I)
the length of the graph
G
to
I
, i.e.

d(G,I):=\max_{i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}}d(i,I).

(6)

Let the system (

with

(8)

Moreover, for
\tau=d^{*}T
, for all

and

it holds

\displaystyle\max_{i,j}|x_{i}(n\tau+t)-x_{j}(n\tau+t)|\leq\phi(t)C^{n}\max_{i,j}|x_{i}(0)-x_{j}(0)|

with

\displaystyle\phi(t):=\begin{cases}1&~\mbox{for $t\in[0,\tau-\delta]$}\\
C\exp\left(2\frac{N-1}{N}(\tau-t)\right)&~\mbox{for $t\in[\tau-\delta,\tau]$}\end{cases}

where
\delta:=-\frac{N}{2(N-1)}\log(C)
.

Let the system (

and the following hold:

(H2)

Both the weight functions

Define

\displaystyle\underline{m}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\min\left\{\lambda_{i}(x)\phi_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})\mbox{~~s.t.~}|x_{k}|\leq\max_{i}|x_{i}(0)|\mbox{ for all }k\in\{1,\ldots,N\}\right\},

(10)

\displaystyle\overline{m}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\max\left\{\lambda_{i}(x)\phi_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})\mbox{~~s.t.~}|x_{k}|\leq\max_{i}|x_{i}(0)|\mbox{ for all }k\in\{1,\ldots,N\}\right\},

(11)

Define the
(T,\mu)
-connectivity graph and assume that there exits a constant graph
G^{*}
with length

d^{*}
as in Theorem

. Then, estimates (

) hold, with

C=1-\frac{1}{2}\left(\tfrac{\underline{m}\mu T}{N+\underline{m}\mu T}\right)^{d^{*}}\exp(-2d^*T\overline{m}),\qquad\phi(t):=\begin{cases}1&~\mbox{for $t\in[0,\tau-\delta]$}\\
C\exp\left(2\frac{N-1}{N}(\tau-t)\right)&~\mbox{for $t\in[\tau-\delta,\tau]$}\end{cases}

where

.

A very interesting generalization of our results about the system (

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dot{x}_{i}(t)=v_{i}\\

Theorem 3
(Main result - 2nd order systems)

Let the system (
12
) be given. Assume that both (H1) in Theorem
1
and the following hold:

(H2’)

The binary interaction function

\phi(r):[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{+}

is Lipschitz continuous, strictly positive, nonincreasing.

Define the
(T,\mu)
-connectivity graph and assume that there exits a constant graph
G^{*}
with length
d^{*}
as in Theorem
1
. Define the diameters in the position and velocity variables, respectively:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{X}(t):=\max_{ij}|x_{i}(t)-x_{j}(t)|,

(13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{V}(t):=\max_{ij}|v_{i}(t)-v_{j}(t)|.

(14)

Then, the following estimate holds

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{V}((n+1)\tau)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(n\tau)\mathcal{D}_{V}(n\tau),

(15)

where
\tau=d^{*}T/\phi(0)
and

\displaystyle C(n\tau):=1-\frac{1}{2}\left(\tfrac{\phi(\mathcal{D}_{X}(0)+(n+1)\tau\mathcal{D}_{V}(0))\mu T}{N+\phi(\mathcal{D}_{X}(0)+(n+1)\tau\mathcal{D}_{V}(0))\mu T}\right)^{d^{*}}\exp(-2\tfrac{N-1}{N}d^*T\phi(0)).

It also holds:

•

if
\phi(r)^{d^{*}}
is nonintegrable at

, i.e.
\int_{R}^{+\infty}\phi(r)^{d^{*}}\,dr=+\infty
for some

, then
\lim_{t\to+\infty}\mathcal{D}_{V}(t)=0
, i.e. alignment occurs;

•

if it moreover holds

\lim_{n\to+\infty}n\phi(n\tau\mathcal{D}_{V}(0))^{d^{*}}>\exp(2\tfrac{N-1}{N}d^*T\phi(0))\left(\tfrac{N}{\mu T}\right)^{d^{*}},

Remark 3
(Comparison with Cucker-Smale model)

In the original Cucker-Smale model, the interaction function is
\phi(r)=\frac{1}{(1+r^{2})^{\beta}}
. For full connection
(i.e. when every node is connected with every other note, and thus

), one has that alignment occurs for
\beta\leq\frac{1}{2}
, while flocking occurs for
\beta<1/2
. In our case, one has that alignment occurs when
\beta\leq\frac{1}{2d^{*}}
, while flocking occurs for
\beta<\frac{1}{2d^{*}}
. Also in this case, we see that the length

of the
(T,\mu)
-connectivity graph plays a central role in the long-time behavior of the system. We will provide examples of this phenomenon in Section
5

2
Cooperative systems

In this section, we provide main results about cooperative systems. After recalling well-posedness of the solution, we prove results about contractivity of the support. We then prove that we can restrict ourselves to the study of one-dimensional linear systems. We finally discuss several different models of cooperative systems available in the literature.

2.1
Well-posedness and contractivity of the support

In this article, we study cooperative first-order systems of the form (
4
) and second-order systems of the form (
12
). We always assume conditions (H1)-(H2) stated in Corollary
2
for systems of the form (
4
),
and conditions (H1)-(H2’) for systems of the form (
12
). These hypotheses ensure existence, globally in time, and uniqueness for the solution to the associated Cauchy problem, see e.g.
[
22
]
. Solutions are considered in the Carathéodory sense for the rest of the article: trajectories are absolutely continuous functions and (
4
) or (
12
) holds for almost every time.
General results about cooperative systems can also be found in
[
23
]
.

We now provide a key property of cooperative systems: the support of the solution is (weakly) contractive.

Proposition 4

Let
x(t)
be a solution of (
4
). Define the support of the solution at time
t
as

\mathrm{supp}(x(t)):=\mathrm{conv}(\{x_{i}(t)\}),

(16)

i.e. the (closed) convex hull of the set of
x_{i}
at time
t
. Then, for
0\leq t\leq s
it holds
\mathrm{supp}(x(t))\supseteq\mathrm{supp}(x(s))
.

In dimension
d=1
, this implies that the maximum function
x_{+}(t):=\max_{j}\{x_{j}(t)\}
is non-increasing and the minimum function
x_{-}(t):=\min_{j}\{x_{j}(t)\}
is non-decreasing.

Define the diameter of the solution as the diameter of the support, i.e.

\mathcal{D}(t):=\max_{i,j}|x_{i}(t)-x_{j}(t)|.

The previous result implies that the diameter is a nonincreasing function.

Proof.
First observe that
\mathrm{supp}(x(t))
is the convex hull of a finite number of points, hence it is a closed polygon.

Let
t
be a time in which
x(t)
is differentiable. If
x_{i}(t)
belongs to the interior of
\mathrm{supp}(x(t))
, by continuity it belongs to the interior of
\mathrm{supp}(x(t+h))
for
h>0
sufficiently small. Assume then that
x_{i}(t)
belongs to the boundary of
\mathrm{supp}(x(t))
: each term

\frac{\lambda_{i}(x)}{N}M_{ij}(t)\phi_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})(x_{j}-x_{i})

points inwards in the polygon, eventually being tangent: this is due to the fact that
x_{j}
belongs to the polygon and
\lambda_{i},M_{ij},\phi_{ij}
are positive. Then, the sum of all terms, that is
\dot{x}_{i}
, points inwards. Thus, one has
x_{i}(t+h)\in\mathrm{supp}(x(t))
for
h>0
sufficiently small. By merging the two cases, one has
x_{i}(t+h)\in\mathrm{supp}(x(t))
for all
i=1,\ldots,N
, hence by convexity
\mathrm{supp}(x(t+h))\subseteq\mathrm{supp}(x(t))
. This proves the first result.

The results in dimension
d=1
directly follows, since
\mathrm{supp}(x(t))
is an interval. The statement about the diameter is a direct consequence too.
\square

We strengthen the previous statement in dimension
d=1
, as follows, in the case of constant extremal values.

Lemma 5

Consider a trajectory
x(t)
of (
4
) in
\mathbb{R}
such that
x_{+}^{*}=\max\{x_{i}(t)\ :\ i=1,\dots,N\}
is constant.
Then the set
I^{+}(t)
of indices
i
that realize this maximum is non-increasing in time: if
i\notin I^{+}(t)
then
i\notin I^{+}(t+h)
for all
h>0
.

Similarly, assume that
x_{-}^{*}=\min\{x_{i}(t)\ :\ i=1,\dots,N\}
is constant.
Then the set
I^{-}(t)
of indices
i
that realize this minimum is non-increasing in time.

Proof.

Consider an index
i\notin I^{+}(T)
for some
T\geq 0
, which means
x_{i}(T)<x^{*}_{+}
. Define
f(t):=x^{*}_{+}-x_{i}(t)
, that satisfies
f(T)>0
. Let
t
be a point in which
x(t)
is differentiable. By the dynamic (
4
) it holds

\displaystyle\dot{f}(t)

\displaystyle=0-\frac{\lambda_{i}(x)}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}M_{ij}(t)\phi_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})(x_{j}-x_{i})\geq-\frac{\lambda_{i}(x)}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}M_{ij}(t)\phi_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})\left(x^{*}_{+}-x_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=-\psi(t)f(t),

where
\psi(t):=\frac{\lambda_{i}(x(t))}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}M_{ij}(t)\phi_{ij}(x_{i}(t),x_{j}(t))\geq 0
. In the first inequality we used that
x_{j}\leq x^{*}_{+}
, for all
j=1\,,\dots\,,N
, since
x^{*}_{+}
is a maximum.
Gronwall lemma now ensures

f(t)\geq f(T)\cdot\exp\left(-\int_{T}^{t}\psi(s)\,ds\right)>0.

By continuity, the estimate holds for all
t\geq T
, ensuring that
i\not\in I^{+}(t)
for all
t\geq T
.

The proof for the second statement is equivalent.

\square

2.3
Models of opinion formation

In this section, we discuss some models of opinion formation, of the form (
1
). Indeed, self-organization and emergence of patterns derived from collective dynamics have been widely studied in the literature, and many models have been proposed in order to account for more complex behavior or to catch some specific properties. In particular, key differences that we want to highlight are encoded in the choice of the scaling parameters
\lambda_{i}
in (
1
).

In the classical case for (
1
), the function
\phi(x_{i},x_{j})
is symmetric and
\lambda_{i}>0
is fixed for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, see
[
24
,
25
]
. A key property that facilitates the analysis of this model is the symmetry of the influence function with respect to
i
and
j
. For general symmetric influence functions, the mean value is preserved. The system is cooperative, and if the influence function remains uniformly bounded below by a strictly positive constant over time, the dynamics converge to the initial mean state. This symmetry also enables the use of spectral methods and
l^{2}
-based techniques to study the system’s variance. Anyway, in the case of lack of connection that we address in this article, symmetry is broken if we do not require the additional constraint
M_{ij}=M_{ji}
.

A limitation of the classical model with constant weightings is that each agent’s dynamics is influenced by the collective weight of all agents. Consequently, under such scaling, the internal interactions of a small group located far from a much larger cluster become negligible. To address this, and originally intended for second-order models, in
[
26
]
a more realistic formulation has been proposed with a modified weighting procedure:

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}=\frac{N}{\sum_{l=1}^{N}\phi(x_{i},x_{l})}.

In this setting, the influence of agent
j
on agent
i
is normalized by the total influence
\sum_{l=1}^{N}\phi(x_{i},x_{l})
acting on agent
i
. As a drawback, even when basic interactions
\phi(x_{i},x_{l})
are symmetric, the resulting model is not symmetric anymore.

We highlight that our results in this article cover all choices of weights
\lambda_{i}
, that are somehow included in the definitions of
\underline{m},\overline{m}
given in (
10
)-(
11
).

3
Proof of main result

In this section, we prove the main result of this article, i.e. Theorem
1
. As already stated above, we first consider the first-order 1-dimensional linear case

\dot{x}_{i}(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}M_{ij}(t)(x_{j}-x_{i})\mbox{~~with~~}M_{ij}:[0,+\infty)\mapsto[0,1],

(20)

that covers all the generalizations, thanks to Proposition
7
. Indeed, when we rewrite (
4
) as a linear system, as in Proposition
6
, the
(T,\mu)
-connectivity graph
G(t)
is transformed into the
(T,\underline{m}\mu)
-connectivity graph
\tilde{G}(t)
. On the other hand, applying Proposition
7
the graph
\tilde{G}(t)
is transformed into the
(T\overline{m},\underline{m}\mu/\overline{m})
-connectivity graph
\hat{G}(t\overline{m})
for a system of the form (
20
).

The proof is based on the following steps: in Section
3.1
, we introduce some barrier functions that allow to provide estimates about solutions of (
20
). In Section
3.2
, we provide estimates about the solutions based on the connectivity graph. In Section
3.3
, we finally give the proof of Theorem
1
and Corollary
2
in full generality.

3.1
Barrier functions

In this section, we provide some estimates about solutions of (
20
), based on some exponential functions.

Proposition 8
(Left barrier)

Define

\Psi_{L}(\bar{x},\alpha,t):=\alpha+\exp\left(-\tfrac{N-1}{N}t\right)(\bar{x}-\alpha).

Let
x(t)
be a solution of (
20
) with
x_{i}(0)\geq\alpha
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
,
and let
\bar{x}\geq\alpha
. If there exists an index
I
and a time
\tau\in[0,T]
such that
x_{I}(\tau)\geq\Psi_{L}(\bar{x},\alpha,\tau)
, then for all
t\geq\tau
it also holds
x_{I}(t)\geq\Psi_{L}(\bar{x},\alpha,t)
.

Proof.
We first observe that
x_{i}(0)\geq\alpha
for all
i=1,\ldots,N
implies
x_{i}(t)\geq\alpha
for all
t\geq 0
, due to Proposition
4
. Consider the function
f(t):=x_{I}(t)-\Psi_{L}(\bar{x},\alpha,t)
and, for
t\geq\tau
, compute

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}f(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x_{j}(t)\leq\Psi_{L}(\bar{x},\alpha,t),j\neq I}M_{Ij}(t)(x_{j}(t)-x_{I}(t))+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x_{j}(t)>\Psi_{L}(\bar{x},\alpha,t),j\neq I}M_{Ij}(t)(x_{j}(t)-x_{I}(t))+

(21)

\displaystyle\tfrac{N-1}{N}\exp\left(-\tfrac{N-1}{N}t\right)(\bar{x}-\alpha)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x_{j}\leq\Psi_{L}(\bar{x},\alpha,t),j\neq I}M_{Ij}(t)\bigg(\Big(\alpha-\Psi_{L}(\bar{x},\alpha,t)\Big)+\Big(\Psi_{L}(\bar{x},\alpha,t)-x_{I}(t)\Big)\bigg)+

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x_{j}(t)>\Psi_{L}(\bar{x},\alpha,t),j\neq I}M_{Ij}(t)\Big(\Psi_{L}(\bar{x},\alpha,t)-x_{I}(t)\Big)+\tfrac{N-1}{N}\exp\left(-\tfrac{N-1}{N}t\right)(\bar{x}-\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x_{j}\leq\Psi_{L}(\bar{x},\alpha,t),j\neq I}M_{Ij}(t)\Big(\alpha-\Psi_{L}(\bar{x},\alpha,t)\Big)+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\neq I}M_{Ij}(t)(\Psi_{L}(\bar{x},\alpha,t)-x_{I}(t))+

\displaystyle\tfrac{N-1}{N}\exp\left(-\tfrac{N-1}{N}t\right)(\bar{x}-\alpha)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\neq I}M_{Ij}(t)\Big(-\exp\left(-\tfrac{N-1}{N}t\right)(\bar{x}-\alpha)\Big)-\bigg(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\neq I}M_{Ij}(t)\bigg)f(t)+\tfrac{N-1}{N}\exp\left(-\tfrac{N-1}{N}t\right)(\bar{x}-\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\neq I}(1-M_{Ij}(t))\exp\left(-\tfrac{N-1}{N}t\right)(\bar{x}-\alpha)-\bigg(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\neq I}M_{Ij}(t)\bigg)f(t)\geq 0-r(t)f(t).

In the last inequality, we used
M_{Ij}(t)\in[0,1]
for
\mathcal{L}^{1}
-almost every
t\geq 0
and defined
r(t):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\neq I}M_{Ij}(t)
, that is independent on
f(t)
and satisfies
r(t)\leq\frac{N-1}{N}
for
\mathcal{L}^{1}
-almost every
t\geq 0
.
A direct application of Grönwall’s lemma at time
\tau
ensures that

f(t)\geq\exp\left(-\int_{\tau}^{t}r(s)\,ds\right)f(\tau).

Our hypothesis now reads as
f(\tau)\geq 0
, which implies that
f(t)\geq 0
for
t\geq\tau
. This proves the result.

\square

3.2
Estimates and connectivity graph

In this section, we provide estimates about trajectories of (
20
), based on connections of the connectivity graph.

We first prove an estimate about the distance between the trajectories of two agents
x_{i}(t),x_{j}(t)
, in the case the arrow
i\to j
exists in the connectivity graph.

Proposition 9

Let
T,\mu>0
be given. Let
x(t)
be a solution of (
20
) with
x_{k}(0)\geq\alpha
for all
k\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
. Assume that the
(T,\mu)
-connectivity graph
G(t)
contains the directed edge
i\to j
. It then holds

\displaystyle x_{i}(T)\geq\alpha+\eta\exp(-2\tfrac{N-1}{N}T)(x_{j}(0)-\alpha)

(22)

with
\eta=\frac{\mu T}{N+\mu T}
.

Proof.

We first define the following barrier:

\gamma_{j}(t):=\Psi_{L}(x_{j}(0),\alpha,t)=\alpha+\exp(-\tfrac{N-1}{N}t)(x_{j}(0)-\alpha).

As a first case, assume that
x_{i}(0)\geq\gamma_{j}(T)
. We rewrite it as
x_{i}(0)\geq\Psi_{L}(\gamma_{j}(T),\alpha,0)
and apply Proposition
8
. Remark that we can apply such proposition
to the left barrier
\gamma_{j}
, since
\gamma_{j}(T)\geq\alpha
as a consequence of
x_{j}(0)\geq\alpha
. It then holds

\displaystyle x_{i}(T)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\Psi_{L}(\gamma_{j}(T),\alpha,T)\geq\alpha+\exp(-\tfrac{N-1}{N}T)(\alpha+\exp(-\tfrac{N-1}{N}T)(x_{j}(0)-\alpha)-\alpha)=

\displaystyle\alpha+\exp(-2\tfrac{N-1}{N}T)(x_{j}(0)-\alpha).

This condition is stronger than the statement, since
1>\eta
.

We then assume that it holds
x_{i}(0)<\gamma_{j}(T)
from now on. We define

l:=\left(1+\frac{\mu T}{N}\right)^{-1}(\gamma_{j}(T)-x_{i}(0)),

which is strictly positive by hypothesis. We now define a second barrier function, that is

\displaystyle\gamma_{i}(t):=\Psi_{L}(\gamma_{j}(T)-l,\alpha,t)=\alpha+\exp(-\tfrac{N-1}{N}t)(\gamma_{j}(T)-l-\alpha)=

\displaystyle\alpha+\exp(-\tfrac{N-1}{N}(t+T))(x_{j}(0)-\alpha)-\exp(-\tfrac{N-1}{N}t)l.

We have two cases: either
x_{i}(t)\geq\gamma_{i}(t)
for some
t\in[0,T]
or
x_{i}(t)<\gamma_{i}(t)
for all
t\in[0,T]
. In the first case, by the definition of
l
and recalling that
x_{i}(0),x_{j}(0)\geq\alpha
, we first observe the following estimate:

\displaystyle\exp(-\tfrac{N-1}{N}T)(x_{j}(0)-\alpha)\geq\left(1+\frac{\mu T}{N}\right)^{-1}\exp(-\tfrac{N-1}{N}T)(x_{j}(0)-\alpha)\geq l.

This implies

\gamma_{j}(T)-l\geq\alpha
. We can then apply Proposition
8
to the left barrier
\gamma_{i}
, that ensures
x_{i}(T)\geq\gamma_{i}(T)
. This reads as

\displaystyle x_{i}(T)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\alpha+\exp(-2\tfrac{N-1}{N}T)(x_{j}(0)-\alpha)

\displaystyle-\exp(-\tfrac{N-1}{N}T)\left(1+\frac{\mu T}{N}\right)^{-1}(\alpha+\exp(-\tfrac{N-1}{N}T)(x_{j}(0)-\alpha)-x_{i}(0))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha+\exp(-2\tfrac{N-1}{N}T)\eta(x_{j}(0)-\alpha)+\exp(-\tfrac{N-1}{N}T)\left(1+\frac{\mu T}{N}\right)^{-1}(x_{i}(0)-\alpha)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\alpha+\exp(-2\tfrac{N-1}{N}T)\eta(x_{j}(0)-\alpha).

Here, we used that
x_{i}(0)-\alpha\geq 0
by hypothesis on the support, and that
\eta=1-\left(1+\frac{\mu T}{N}\right)^{-1}
. Also in this case, the statement is proved.

We are then left with the case
x_{i}(t)<\gamma_{i}(t)
for all
t\in[0,T]
. We prove that this case cannot occur, by contradition. We first observe that, due to the fact that left barriers are decreasing functions of time, for all
t\in[0,T]
it holds both
x_{i}(t)<\gamma_{j}(T)-l
and
x_{j}(t)\geq\gamma_{j}(T)
, i.e.
x_{j}(t)-x_{i}(t)\geq l
. We now provide an estimate similar to (
21
) with this added information. We define
f(t):=x_{i}(t)-\gamma_{i}(t)
, that is strictly positive, continuous and differentiable for almost every
t\in[0,T]
. We also observe that the following identity holds:

\dot{\gamma}_{i}(t)=-\tfrac{N-1}{N}\exp(-\tfrac{N-1}{N}t)(\gamma_{i}(0)-\alpha).

For points of differentiability, it then holds

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}f(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}M_{ij}(t)(x_{j}(t)-x_{i}(t))+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x_{k}(t)\leq\gamma_{i}(t),k\neq i}M_{ik}(t)(x_{k}(t)-x_{i}(t))

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k\not\in\{i,j\},x_{k}(t)>\gamma_{i}(t)}M_{ik}(t)(x_{k}(t)-x_{i}(t))+\tfrac{N-1}{N}\exp(-\tfrac{N-1}{N}t)(\gamma_{i}(0)-\alpha)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}M_{ij}(t)l+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x_{k}(t)\leq\gamma_{i}(t),k\neq i}M_{ik}(t)((\alpha-\gamma_{i}(t))+(\gamma_{i}(t)-x_{i}(t)))

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k\not\in\{i,j\},x_{k}(t)>\gamma_{i}(t)}M_{ik}(t)(\gamma_{i}(t)-x_{i}(t))+\tfrac{N-1}{N}\exp(-\tfrac{N-1}{N}t)(\gamma_{i}(0)-\alpha)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}M_{ij}(t)l+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x_{k}(t)\leq\gamma_{i}(t),k\neq i}M_{ik}(t)(\alpha-\gamma_{i}(t))+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k\not\in\{i,j\}}M_{ik}(t)(\gamma_{i}(t)-x_{i}(t))+

\displaystyle\tfrac{N-1}{N}\exp(-\tfrac{N-1}{N}t)(\gamma_{i}(0)-\alpha).

We now observe that it holds

\alpha-\gamma_{i}(t)=-\exp(-\tfrac{N-1}{N}t)(\gamma_{i}(0)-\alpha).

We then have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}f(t)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}M_{ij}(t)l+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k\neq i}M_{ik}(t)(-\exp(-\tfrac{N-1}{N}t)(\gamma_{i}(0)-\alpha))

(23)

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k\not\in\{i,j\}}M_{ik}(t)\right)f(t)+\tfrac{N-1}{N}\exp(-\tfrac{N-1}{N}t)(\gamma_{i}(0)-\alpha)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}M_{ij}(t)l+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k\neq i}(1-M_{ik}(t))\exp(-\tfrac{N-1}{N}t)(\gamma_{i}(0)-\alpha))-\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k\neq i}M_{ik}(t)\right)f(t)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}M_{ij}(t)l+0-\frac{N-1}{N}f(t).

Again, here we used
M_{ik}(t)\in[0,1]
. A direct application of Grönwall’s lemma now implies

\displaystyle f(T)\geq\exp(-\tfrac{N-1}{N}T)\left[f(0)+\int_{0}^{T}\exp(s)\frac{1}{N}M_{ij}(s)l\,ds\right]

\displaystyle\geq\exp(-\tfrac{N-1}{N}T)\left[x_{i}(0)-\gamma_{i}(0)+1\cdot\frac{l}{N}\int_{0}^{T}M_{ij}(s)\,ds\right].

Since
i\to j
is an arrow of
G(t)
, it holds
\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}M_{ij}(s)\,ds\geq\mu
. It then holds

\displaystyle x_{i}(T)-\gamma_{i}(T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(T)\geq\exp(-\tfrac{N-1}{N}T)\left(x_{i}(0)-\left(\gamma_{j}(T)-l\right)+\frac{l\,T}{N}\mu\right)=

\displaystyle\exp(-\tfrac{N-1}{N}T)\left(x_{i}(0)-\gamma_{j}(T)+l\left(1+\frac{\mu\,T}{N}\right)\right)=0.

This raises a contradiction with the condition
x_{i}(t)<\gamma_{i}(t)
for all
t\in[0,T]
.
\square

We now extend the previous result to a whole path from
i
to
j
. We highlight that the extensions requires that the path is in the connectivity graph for several time intervals at distance
T
.

Proposition 10

Let
T,\mu>0
be given. Let
x(t)
be a solution of (
20
) with
x_{i}(0)\geq\alpha
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
. Assume that the
(T,\mu)
-connectivity graph
G(t)
contains the directed sequence
{i_{0}}\to{i_{1}}\to\ldots\to{i_{k}}
for all times
t=0,T,\ldots,(k-1)T
. Then, for all
t\geq kT
it holds

x_{i_{l}}(t)\geq\alpha+\eta^{k}\exp(-2\tfrac{N-1}{N}t)(x_{i_{k}}(0)-\alpha)

(24)

for all
l\in\{0,\ldots,k\}
, where
\alpha:=\min_{j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}}x_{j}(0)
and
\eta:=\frac{\mu T}{N+\mu T}
.

Proof.
We first observe that

\eta\in(0,1)
. The case
l=k
is then a consequence of applying Proposition
8
for
k-1
times. We then focus on
l\in\{0,\ldots,k-1\}
. For simplicity of notation, we use the index
\ell:=k-l
.

We first prove the following recursive version of Proposition
9
: for
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
it holds

x_{i_{k-\ell}}(\ell T)\geq\alpha+\eta^{\ell}\exp(-2\tfrac{N-1}{N}\ell T)(x_{i_{k}}(0)-\alpha).

(25)

The proof is as follows: the case
\ell=1
is exactly Proposition
9
applied with

i=i_{k-1}
and
j=i_{k}
, and observing that
G(0)
contains the directed edge
i_{k-1}\to i_{k}
. We now assume that the formula holds for
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,k-1\}
and prove it for
\ell+1
.

We first apply Proposition
9
by choosing
x_{j}(0)=x_{i_{k-\ell}}(\ell\,T)
and observing that
G(\ell\,T)
contains the arrow
i_{k-(\ell+1)}\to i_{k-\ell}
by construction. It then holds

x_{i_{k-(\ell+1)}}((\ell+1)T)\geq\alpha+\eta\exp(-2\tfrac{N-1}{N}T)(x_{i_{k-\ell}}(\ell\,T)-\alpha).

(26)

Next, by applying the induction hypothesis (
25
), we deduce

\displaystyle x_{i_{k-\ell}}(\ell T)

\displaystyle\geq\alpha+\eta\exp(-2\tfrac{N-1}{N}T)(\alpha+\eta^{\ell-1}\exp(-2\tfrac{N-1}{N}(\ell-1)T)(x_{i_{k}}(0)-\alpha)-\alpha)

(27)

\displaystyle=\alpha+\eta^{\ell}\exp(-2\tfrac{N-1}{N}\ell T)(x_{i_{k}}(0)-\alpha).

Combining (
26
), (
27
), we deduce

\displaystyle x_{i_{k-(\ell+1)}}((\ell+1)T)\geq\alpha+\eta^{\ell+1}\exp(-2\tfrac{N-1}{N}(\ell+1)T)(x_{i_{k}}(0)-\alpha).

The recursive formula (
25
) is then proved.

We now need to estimate the evolution of
x_{i_{k-\ell}}
on the time interval
[\ell T,t]
. It is sufficient to apply Proposition
8
, first on the
\lceil\tfrac{t}{T}\rceil-\ell
intervals of length
T
, then on the time interval
\left[\lceil\tfrac{t}{T}\rceil T,\,t\right]
. Since the solution already starts on the right of the left barrier at time
\ell T
, this gives

\displaystyle x_{i_{k-\ell}}(t)\geq\Psi_{L}(x_{i_{k-\ell}}(\ell T),\alpha,t-\ell T)

\displaystyle\geq\alpha+\exp(-\tfrac{N-1}{N}(t-\ell T))(\alpha+\eta^{\ell}\exp(-2\tfrac{N-1}{N}\ell T)(x_{i_{k}}(0)-\alpha)-\alpha)

\displaystyle=\alpha+\eta^{\ell}\exp(-\tfrac{N-1}{N}(t+\ell T))(x_{i_{k}}(0)-\alpha)\geq\alpha+\eta^{k}\exp(-2\tfrac{N-1}{N}t)(x_{i_{k}}(0)-\alpha).

(28)

Here, we just used
\ell T\leq t
and
\eta\in(0,1)
. By writing
l=k-\ell
, one gets the result.

\square

3.3
Proof of Theorem
1
and Corollary
2

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1
.

Proof.
We first consider solution of (
20
), i.e. of (
4
) in 1 dimension with linear dynamics. Denote the support of the solution at time
t=0
as the interval
[\alpha,\beta]
. The diameter is then
\mathcal{D}(0)=\beta-\alpha
.

Under the hypothesis of the theorem, there exists a graph
G^{*}
such that for all
l\in\mathbb{N}
the graph
G(lT)
contains all its arrows. Moreover,
G^{*}
admits a globally reachable node
I
. Since the length of the graph
G^{*}
is
d^{*}:=d(G^{*},I)
, for each index
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
there exists a directed sequence
i\to\ldots\to I
of length
k\leq d^{*}
. Then, by applying Proposition
10
and observing that
d^{*}T\geq kT
, it holds

x_{i}(d^{*}T)\geq\alpha+\eta^{k}\exp(-2\tfrac{N-1}{N}d^*T)(x_{I}(0)-\alpha).

(29)

We now have two cases:

•

it holds
x_{I}(0)\geq\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}
. Then, (
29
) implies

x_{i}(d^{*}T)\geq\alpha+\eta^{d(G^{*},I)}\exp(-2\tfrac{N-1}{N}d(G^*,I)T)\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2}.

The estimate holds for any index
i
, then the support satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(d^{*}T)\leq\beta-\alpha-\eta^{d^{*}}\exp(-2\tfrac{N-1}{N}d^*T)\tfrac{\beta-\alpha}{2}=C\mathcal{D}(0),

(30)

with
C:=1-\tfrac{1}{2}\eta^{d^{*}}\exp(-2\tfrac{N-1}{N}d^*T).

•

it holds
x_{I}(0)<\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}
. Then, one replaces all
x_{i}(t)
with
y_{i}(t)=\alpha+\beta-x_{i}(t)
, that satisfy all the hypotheses of Theorem
1
. Moreover, it holds
y_{I}(0)\geq\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}
, thus the results of the previous item hold, i.e. (
30
) holds for the
y_{i}
variables. By writing
x_{i}(t)=\alpha+\beta-y_{i}(t)
, we have that (
30
) holds for the
x_{i}
too.

By merging the two cases, we have that (
30
) holds. By induction, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
it holds

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(nd^{*}T)\leq C^{n}\mathcal{D}(0).

We now aim to estimate the evolution of the support on the time interval
[nd^{*}T,(n+1)d^{*}T]
. On one side, the support is non-increasing, then for all
t\in[nd^{*}T,(n+1)d^{*}T]
it holds

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(t)\leq C^{n}\mathcal{D}(0).

(31)

On the other side, it is sufficient to observe that

|\dot{x}_{i}|\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}1\cdot|x_{j}(t)-x_{i}(t)|\leq\frac{N-1}{N}\mathcal{D}(t)

to deduce that it holds

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{D}(t)\geq-\max_{i,j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}}|x_{i}(t)|+|x_{j}(t)|\geq-2\frac{N-1}{N}\mathcal{D}(t).

This clearly implies that for all
\tau\in[0,d^{*}T]
(seen as reverse time from
(n+1)d^{*}T]
it holds

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}((n+1)d^{*}T-\tau)\leq\exp\left(2\frac{N-1}{N}\tau\right)\mathcal{D}((n+1)d^{*}T)\leq C^{n+1}\exp\left(2\frac{N-1}{N}\tau\right)\mathcal{D}(0).

(32)

By choosing the optimal available estimate, we have that (
31
) holds for
t\in[nd^{*}T,(n+1)d^{*}T-\delta]
with
\delta=-\frac{N}{2(N-1)}\log(C)
, while (
32
) holds for
t\in[(n+1)d^{*}T-\delta,(n+1)d^{*}T]
.

The
n
-dimensional case is a direct consequence, by applying Proposition
7
, with no rescaling of time.

\square

We now prove Corollary
2
.

Proof.
We first rewrite (
4
) as a linear system, as in Proposition
6
. The
(T,\mu)
-connectivity graph
G(t)
is transformed into the
(T,\underline{m}\mu)
-connectivity graph
\tilde{G}(t)
. We then apply Proposition
7
: the system is now 1-dimensional, the graph
\tilde{G}(t)
is transformed into the
(T\overline{m},\underline{m}\mu/\overline{m})
-connectivity graph
\hat{G}(t\overline{m})
and time is rescaled from
t
to
t\overline{m}
. For such system, Theorem
1
holds, where in the definitions of
\eta,C,\delta
one needs to replace
T
with
T\overline{m}
and
\mu
with
\underline{m}\mu/\overline{m}
. We then translate (
32
) back to the original system (
4
), by rescaling time and find the result.

\square

We first recall some properties of (

Proof.

5.1
Comparison with the literature

In this section, we provide some comparisons with available results in the literature. We basically have two quite different points of views: the symmetric and the non-symmetric ones.

In several key results, one imposes some form of symmetry of the communication weights. A strong form of symmetry, of the kind
M_{ij}=M_{ji}
, is explicitly imposed in several contributions, e.g. in
[
27
]
for first-order linear systems, in
[
28
]
for linear integrators, and in
[
17
]
for Cucker-Smale systems. A much weaker (thus more general) symmetry condition is known as the cut-balance condition, see
[
29
]
: there exists
K>0
such that for all subsets of agents
S\subset\{1,\ldots,N\}
and for all
t>0
one requires

\sum_{i\in S,j\not\in S}M_{ij}(t)\leq K\sum_{i\in S,j\not\in S}M_{ji}(t).

In
[
30
]
, a generalization, known as the arc-balance condition, is introduced. In
[
31
]
, the result is extended to allow for non-instantaneous reciprocity.
Even though these results are fundamental, this symmetric point of view is somehow opposite to ours.

Indeed, the non-symmetric point of view (dating back to Moreau in
[
10
]
) takes advantage of the direction of interactions: as the most explicit example, in his original contribution, Moreau builds a directed graph and requires a globally reachable node. This point of view, less discussed in the literature, can be encoded in the Integral Scrambling Condition discussed in Section
4.2
. A more general condition of convergence, similar to the one studied here, is presented in
[
32
]
. There, a directed graph is built based on the following condition for the existence of the edge
i\to j
:

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to+\infty}M_{ij}(t_{n}+t)=M_{ij}^{*}(t)\qquad\mbox{~with~}\int_{t}^{+\infty}M_{ij}^{*}(s)\,ds>0\mbox{~for all $t>0$.}

If the directed graph has a globally reachable node, then the first-order system converges to consensus. Under such a condition, no rate of convergence to consensus is available; indeed, one can introduce large intervals with no connection and still have the condition verified (by increasing the
t_{n}
).

6
Conclusions

In this article, we have proved sufficient conditions for convergence of first-order systems to consensus and of second-order systems to flocking, under communication failures. We introduced quantitative estimates about the minimum level of service, namely the PE and ISC conditions. We proved that, for each of these conditions, consensus and flocking can be achieved under the classical conditions for systems with no communication failures. Yet, the rate of convergence is slower. In both consensus and flocking problems, we provided explicit rates of convergence.

In the future, we aim to find even weaker conditions for communication failures, to understand the (theoretical) minimum level of service ensuring consensus or flocking. Moreover, we aim to find explicit rates of convergence for other conditions, such as the algebraic connectivity (see
[
20
]
).
