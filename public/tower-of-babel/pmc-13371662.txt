Retrospective evaluation of clinical performance of three measurement catheter fixation methods in urodynamic studies
Authors: Xiao Zeng, Zhihui Huang, Hong Shen, De-yi Luo, Tao Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCatheter displacement during urodynamic studies remains a common challenge, potentially introducing artifacts, compromising test accuracy, and decreasing patient comfort. Despite the clinical significance of stable catheter fixation, evidence-based recommendations for optimal fixation techniques are lacking. This study seeks to address this gap by comparing the effectiveness and patient comfort associated with three commonly used catheter fixation methods during urodynamic study.MethodsWe retrospectively collected data from non-randomized patients who underwent urodynamic studies (UDS) at West China Hospital of Sichuan University between April and June 2023. Patients were selected based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria and assigned to one of three catheter fixation methods. The effectiveness of the following fixation techniques was evaluated: waterproof tape fixation (Group 1: catheter secured to the skin with adhesive tape), (2) patient-manual fixation (Group 2: patient holds the catheter manually throughout the procedure), and (3) silk thread fixation (Group 3: catheter secured with silk suture tied and fixed externally).ResultsA total of 168 patients were enrolled in the study, with 56 patients in each group. The median ages for Groups 1, 2, and 3 were 66 (47.25, 76), 67 (61,71), and 66 (48, 76.75) years, respectively. There were no statistically significant differences among the three groups in terms of maximum cystometric capacity (MCC), bladder compliance (BC), maximum flow rate (Qmax), detrusor pressure at Qmax (Pdet.Qmax), bladder contractility index (BCI), or bladder outlet obstruction index (BOOI) (P > 0.05). The overall incidence of catheter displacement was 35.71% in Group 1, 0% in Group 2, and 14.29% in Group 3. Statistically significant differences in Comfort-B scale scores were observed between Group 1 and Group 2, and between Group 2 and Group 3 (P < 0.000). Similarly, visual analogue scale (VAS) scores also showed significant differences between Group 1 and Group 2, and between Group 2 and Group 3 (P < 0.000).ConclusionsOur preliminary assessment indicated that the three catheter fixation methods did not significantly influence urodynamic parameters. Notably, patient-manual fixation achieved the lowest catheter displacement rate (0%) but was associated with the highest pain and discomfort scores. In contrast, waterproof tape and silk thread fixation offered better patient comfort but with higher displacement rates. These findings highlight a trade-off between catheter stability and patient comfort, suggesting that fixation method selection should be individualized based on patient characteristics and procedural requireme.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Urodynamic study, Fixation methods, Waterproof tape fixation, Patient-manual fixation, Silk thread fixation

Introduction

Urodynamic study (UDS) plays a pivotal role in evaluating Lower urinary tract (LUT) function by integrating principles of hydrodynamics and electrophysiology [1]. It enables accurate quantification of functional and pathophysiological changes in the bladder and urethra [2]. The core methodology involves the use of two pressure-measuring catheters: one inserted into the bladder via the urethra to record Intravesical pressure (Pves), and another inserted into the rectum to measure Abdominal pressure (Pabd) [2, 3]. These pressure values are processed through a computerized system to derive real-time Detrusor pressure (Pdet) using the formula: Pdet = Pves – Pabd. High-fidelity acquisition and transmission of both Pves and Pabd signals are essential for ensuring the reliability and clinical accuracy of UDS interpretations [4, 5]. Moreover, the experience and skill of the urodynamicist play an equally important role in minimizing artifacts and avoiding common pitfalls during urodynamic testing [6, 7, 8, 9].

During the UDS process, various techniques are employed to secure measurement catheters. Currently, waterproof tape is the most commonly used method in many clinical centers. However, alternative fixation techniques have been described in the literature. For instance, Gleason et al. (1980) and Konda (1987) reported the use of silk thread to secure urodynamic catheters [10, 11, 12]. Additionally, some centers permit patients to manually hold the catheters during the examination [11]. Although the “Good Urodynamic Practice (GUP)” guidelines issued by the International Continence Society (ICS) are widely regarded as authoritative, Sect. 7.5 (“Transurethral Catheter”) does not offer specific recommendations on catheter fixation [2]. To enhance procedural clarity, the 2024 updated ICS guidelines and the 2023 European Association of Urology (EAU) guidelines have emphasized the need for standardization in catheter positioning, patient posture, and fixation techniques during UDS [13, 14]. Furthermore, the 2024 ICS standardization report on terminology for lower urinary tract function highlights the technical requirements for accurate detrusor pressure measurement, underscoring the importance of catheter stability to avoid signal drift and misinterpretation [15]. Despite the clinical relevance of catheter fixation, literature on this specific topic remains scarce, with only one relevant article identified through a search of major international academic databases [12].

This study was designed to generate evidence to inform the selection of optimal catheter fixation methods during real-world clinical urodynamic studies. Specifically, we evaluated the effectiveness of three commonly used catheter fixation techniques in routine UDS procedures.

Methods

Research inclusion criteria

Patients were eligible for inclusion in this study if they met the following criteria:1. Age ≥ 18 years; 2. Complete baseline clinical information; 3. Ability to understand the examination process and cooperate during the procedure; 4. Availability of Comfort-B scale and Visual analogue scale (VAS) pain score data; 5. Demonstrated urine flow during the pressure-flow study.

Research exclusion criteria

In addition to the inclusion criteria outlined in Sect. 2.1, patients were excluded from the study if they met any of the following conditions:1. History of urethral trauma or urethral stricture; 2. History of previous rectal surgery. 3. Patients with abdominal enterostomy or other anatomical abnormalities that prevented standard catheter fixation.

Research protocol

This was a retrospective, non-randomized study based on patient data from individuals who underwent UDS at West China Hospital between April and June 2023. The UDS procedures were performed across three independent urodynamic study rooms within the center. During the examinations, one of three catheter fixation methods was selected at the discretion of the operating urodynamicist, as current clinical guidelines do not mandate a specific fixation technique.The choice of catheter fixation method was determined by the operating urodynamicist based on patient characteristics (e.g., hand function, compliance, cognitive status, obesity, and whether video urodynamics was performed), not by room assignment. (For patients with adequate hand function and good compliance, Patient-Manual Fixation is generally preferred; for those with obesity, impaired hand function, cognitive deficits, or poor compliance, Waterproof Tape Fixation or Silk Thread Fixation are employed; while patients undergoing video urodynamic studies are immobilized using both Waterproof Tape Fixation and Silk Thread Fixation techniques.) This variation allowed for the collection and comparison of parameters across different fixation approaches.The three catheter fixation methods used during UDS were defined as follows:1. Study bias control

This study employed a retrospective non-randomized design to analyze urodynamic data from patients who underwent catheter fixation by one of three methods, selected according to the operator’s preference. To mitigate these concerns, strict inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied to ensure comparability across groups. Baseline patient characteristics were carefully assessed, and no significant differences were observed among groups, supporting internal validity. However, sex distribution was not fully balanced, and neurogenic status was not separately analyzed due to the retrospective nature of the data collection.

Urodynamic study process and catheter fixation methods

Urodynamic study process

Urodynamic studies were conducted with patients in the sitting position using the Laborie Triton system equipped with air-charged catheters (Laborie & Co., Aquarius TT Triton). Sterile normal saline maintained at 37 °C was used as the infusion medium. The bladder filling rate was set at 60 mL/min for patients with Non-neurogenic bladder dysfunction (NNBD) and 10 mL/min for those with Neurogenic bladder dysfunction (NBD).

Measurement catheter fixation methods

Three catheter fixation methods were employed during the urodynamic study, as detailed below: Waterproof Tape Fixation (Group 1): Pves catheter: In female patients, waterproof tape (Transpore™; 3 M, 2.5 cm width) was wrapped around the Pves catheter, with both ends affixed to the abdominal skin. In male patients, the Pves catheter was secured directly to the penile shaft using the same waterproof adhesive tape. Pabd catheter: In female patients, the Pabd catheter was secured to the abdominal or thigh skin with waterproof tape. In male patients, the Pabd catheter was secured to the skin of the thigh. Both catheters were stabilized through external fixation. 2. Patient-Manual Fixation (Group 2):Pves and Pabd catheters: Patients were instructed to manually hold both catheters in place throughout the examination, using the thumb and index finger to stabilize the catheters while keeping the remaining fingers and palm away from the perineal area. This method was applied identically for male and female patients, as described by previous studies [11]. 3. Silk Thread Fixation (Group 3): Pves catheter: A silk suture (0/3.5 Metric, MERSILK SA86G; Ethicon) was tied around the catheter. In male patients, both ends of the thread were extended along the penile shaft and fixed in place with adhesive tape around the mid-portion or base of the penis. In female patients, the thread was extended obliquely across the perineal region and secured externally [12]. Pabd catheter: In both male and female patients, the Pabd catheter was secured to the abdominal or thigh skin using adhesive tape after silk thread stabilization.

Effectiveness evaluation index

The primary outcome of this study was the detection of catheter displacement (of either the Pves or Pabd catheter), as displacement may introduce measurement artifacts and compromise the effectiveness of the UDS process. Additional recorded urodynamic parameters included:1. Maximum Cystometric Capacity (MCC); 2. Bladder Compliance (BC); 3. Maximum Uroflow Rate (Qmax); 4. Detrusor Pressure at Maximum Uroflow Rate (Pdet.Qmax); 5. Bladder Contractility Index (BCI), calculated as BCI = Pdet.Qmax + 5 × Qmax; 6. Bladder Outlet Obstruction Index (BOOI), calculated as BOOI = Pdet.Qmax − 2 × Qmax [16, 17]. The secondary outcome was patient comfort was evaluated using the Comfort-B scale and the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS). The Comfort-B scale, adapted from the original Comfort Scale developed by Ambuel et al. (1992), consists of six behavioral items scored on a 5-point Likert scale, yielding total scores ranging from 6 to 30, with scores ≥ 17 indicating the need for pain-relieving intervention [18, 19]. Since the Comfort-B scale is primarily designed for pain and comfort assessment in pediatric patients, the analytical results obtained from its application to adult populations require cautious interpretation. The VAS, a subjective assessment tool ranging from 0 to 10, was used to evaluate pain, discomfort, and satisfaction during the procedure [20, 21].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 21.0, Microsoft Excel, and GraphPad Prism 9, with data visualization performed in GraphPad Prism 9. For normally distributed data with homogeneity of variance, differences between groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For data that were not normally distributed or exhibited heterogeneity of variance, the Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum test was applied, with results expressed as median (interquartile range, P25–P75). When significant differences were identified among multiple groups, Bonferroni correction was used for pairwise comparisons. Differences in categorical variables among the three catheter fixation groups were assessed using Fisher’s exact test. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Medical ethics approval

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of -West China Hospital of Sichuan University(No. 2023–4421) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

Results

Baseline characteristics of enrolled patients

A total of 248 patient records were initially assessed, of which 34 were excluded for not meeting the inclusion or exclusion criteria. Subsequently, 56 patient records were selected for each study group based on the catheter fixation method employed during the UDS. Group 1 comprised 56 patients (38 males and 18 females) with a median age of 66 (47.25, 76) years. Group 2 included 56 patients (44 males and 12 females) with a median age of 67 (61, 71) years. Group 3 consisted of 56 patients (46 males and 10 females) with a median age of 66 (48, 76.75) years (Fig. 2; Table 1).

Discussions

Recent studies have emphasized the importance of catheter fixation in urodynamic measurements as a key component of quality control [4, 9]. However, specific strategies for catheter fixation during UDS remain underexplored in the literature. This study seeks to address this gap by presenting the first comparative analysis of commonly used catheter fixation methods during UDS. The primary objective is to conduct a preliminary evaluation of the effectiveness of three widely adopted fixation techniques. By assessing the clinical advantages and limitations of each method, the findings aim to inform their practical implementation and contribute to future efforts toward standardizing UDS procedures. A technical consideration regarding our methodology is the use of air-charged catheters. While the ICS GUP guidelines primarily recommend water-filled systems, air-filled systems are considered acceptable. The use of air-charged catheters in our study represents a relative deviation from the preferred standard, which we acknowledge as a limitation. Nevertheless, all three fixation groups were studied under identical equipment conditions, so the comparative findings remain valid.

In our study, we first analyzed the baseline characteristics of the enrolled patients and found no significant differences in age among the three groups. Urodynamic parameters obtained using the three different catheter fixation methods were then compared, focusing on two key phases of the urodynamic study: the bladder filling phase and the voiding phase [22, 23]. During the filling phase, MCC and BC were assessed, while during the voiding phase, Qmax, Pdet.Qmax, BCI, BOOI were evaluated. Statistical analysis revealed no significant intergroup differences in these parameters. Currently, waterproof tape fixation is the most commonly used method in clinical practice, while silk thread fixation—introduced over three decades ago—has received limited attention in terms of its clinical efficacy. Both methods rely on securing the catheter to the skin using adhesive tape or silk thread. Our findings suggest that these fixation techniques do not significantly influence uroflow dynamics during voiding. However, in practice, conditions such as abnormal skin integrity or excessive body hair may hinder effective fixation, increasing the risk of catheter displacement during UDS. To address these challenges, our center adopted a patient-manual fixation approach, whereby patients were instructed to stabilize the catheter by holding it between the thumb and index finger, with the remaining fingers and palm positioned away from the perineal area. Comparison of urodynamic parameters between this method and the other two fixation techniques revealed no statistically significant differences, indicating that all three methods provide consistent and reliable urodynamic outcomes.

In addition to evaluating urodynamic parameters, a key objective of our study was to compare catheter displacement rates associated with different fixation methods used throughout the entire UDS procedure [24]. Previous studies conducted in Southwest and mainland China have reported inconsistent findings regarding the displacement rates of Pves and Pabd pressure catheters. One study reported a higher displacement rate for Pves catheters compared to Pabd catheters (7% vs. 2%), whereas another study observed the reverse trend (4.9% vs. 2.0%)4,9. This discrepancy between the two Chinese studies may be attributed to differences in patient populations, catheter types (air-charged vs. water-filled), patient positioning (sitting vs. supine), or center-specific fixation protocols. While both studies were conducted in China, variations in urodynamic practices across centers may influence which catheter is more prone to displacement. These inconsistent findings underscore the need for standardized fixation protocols. However, neither of these original studies provided displacement rates stratified by patient sex or neurogenic versus non-neurogenic status, which limits direct comparison with the subgroup findings of the present study. In our investigation, no catheter displacement was observed in the patient-manual fixation group across the entire UDS process. This method, which engages patients in actively stabilizing their own catheters, demonstrated strong potential for preventing displacement. The finding that patient-manual fixation achieved zero catheter displacement warrants mechanistic explanation. Unlike tape or silk thread fixation, which provide static external stabilization, patient-manual fixation involves active patient participation throughout the urodynamic procedure. Several factors may contribute to its superior performance. First, patients holding the catheters can sense minor movements or traction forces in real time and instinctively adjust their hand position to counteract displacement. Second, continuous manual stabilization allows for dynamic adaptation to patient posture changes, coughing, or body movements during the study. Third, the absence of adhesive materials eliminates failures caused by sweat, oily skin, or body hair, which can compromise tape or thread fixation. Fourth, patients may unconsciously maintain a more stable posture when they are actively engaged in the procedure. These active, feedback-driven mechanisms likely explain why manual fixation outperformed the two passive external fixation methods in preventing catheter displacement. In contrast, the waterproof tape fixation group exhibited Pves and Pabd displacement rates of 14.29% and 21.43%, respectively, while the silk thread fixation group showed rates of 3.57% and 10.71%, respectively. The displacement rate of the Pabd catheter was significantly higher in both the waterproof tape and silk thread fixation groups compared to the patient-manual fixation group, with no statistically significant difference between the two. However, the Pves catheter exhibited a notably higher displacement rate in the waterproof tape group compared to the other methods.

In our study, patients’ subjective experiences during the UDS were assessed using two validated instruments: the VAS and the Comfort-B scale. The VAS, a widely used tool for quantifying perceived pain, revealed median scores of 4 (4, 5) in Group 1, 8 (7, 9) in Group 2, and 5 (4, 5) in Group 3. Notably, VAS scores in both the Group 1 and the Group 3 were significantly lower than those observed in the Group 2 (p < 0.05). The higher VAS scores in Group 2 may reflect the physical discomfort associated with manually stabilizing the catheters throughout the examination. This often required patients to maintain an upright posture and continuously engage both hands, potentially contributing to increased musculoskeletal strain and procedural fatigue. Results from the Comfort-B scale were consistent with the VAS findings, further substantiating the discomfort reported by patients in the manual fixation group. Median Comfort-B scores were 8 (7.25, 9) in Group 1, 14.5 (12.25, 16) in Group 2, and 7.5 (7, 9) in Group 3, indicating significantly higher discomfort levels in the patient-manual fixation group.

This study has several limitations. First, it was a retrospective, non-randomized controlled study, which may introduce selection bias and confounding factors. Second, the relatively small sample sizemay limit generalizability and statistical power; thus, the lack of statistically significant differences (all P > 0.05) should be interpreted with caution. The lack of prospective sample size calculation is a limitation inherent to this retrospective study, although post-hoc power analyses based on observed effect sizes indicated adequate power (85–92%) for the primary outcomes; future prospective randomized trials are needed to confirm our findings. Third, not all relevant urodynamic parameters (e.g., bladder sensation, detrusor overactivity) were assessed. Fourth, fixation methods were selected based on urologists’ assessments, introducing selection bias. Additionally, patient-manual fixation may be influenced by individual patient factors (e.g., hand dexterity, cognitive function), which may have affected fixation quality and comfort scores. Fifth, outcome assessors were not blinded to fixation method assignment, which may have introduced detection bias. Sixth, due to the retrospective design, neurogenic bladder dysfunction status was not systematically recorded, and the sample size was insufficient for reliable stratified analyses by sex or neurogenic status. Finally, this study focused exclusively on conventional urodynamic testing; ambulatory and video urodynamics were not included.

Conclusions

This study conducted a preliminary evaluation of three commonly used catheter fixation methods and found no significant differences in key urodynamic parameters among the groups, suggesting comparable effectiveness in maintaining measurement accuracy. Notably, the patient-manual fixation method was associated with the lowest rates of catheter displacement, highlighting its potential for improving procedural stability. However, this approach also resulted in higher patient-reported discomfort and pain levels. These findings underscore the importance of balancing fixation stability with patient comfort when selecting fixation techniques. Taken together, these results reveal a clear trade-off: patient-manual fixation provides superior catheter stability (zero displacement) but at the expense of patient comfort, while tape and silk thread fixation offer intermediate displacement protection with better tolerability. Therefore, fixation method selection should be individualized based on patient characteristics and procedural priorities. Future studies with larger sample sizes, expanded urodynamic parameters, and prospective randomized designs are needed to further refine fixation strategies and enhance both the quality and tolerability of urodynamic testing.
