Identifying generative AI use among genuine responders in online survey research
Authors: Suzannah Gerber, Sean B Cash
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Everyday use of AI has introduced more AI-generated content into surveys, diluting insights and distorting findings. While the concern about agentic “bots” responding to surveys is now widely recognized by most researchers, this comment discusses the simultaneous rise in use of AI by genuine human survey respondents and provides guidance on identification and implementation of the necessary safeguards that ensure surveys reflect public opinion rather than recycled or programmed content.Subject terms: Human behaviour, Research data, Communication

Introduction

The use of generative AI has become ubiquitous. As individuals increasingly incorporate AI into daily life – in search engines and tools for everyday correspondence – researchers face new challenges for both collecting and validating data in online survey research. As AI services evolve, the models become better informed and coherent; AI-powered search, chat, and automated tasking can easily be used to help respond to online questionnaires even with a genuine human at the helm1. As researchers who work with human research participants, we are concerned about the serious implications of this for the reliability of data.

AI technologies provide individuals with tools that expand human capability, but their widespread use can challenge researchers’ ability to capture true public understanding and opinions on critical scientific and social topics2. The problem of agentic “bots” completing surveys is well-described3–5; and a recent article in Nature has described that such chatbots may already be indistinguishable from actual human participants6. Adding to the researcher’s list of woes is our concern here – that as easy and prolific access to these tools increases, genuine survey respondents (i.e., actual human participants engaging with a survey) are also using AI to generate some of their responses. The increasingly accessible embedding of AI assistant tools into word processing software, smartphones, and browser software makes it increasingly easy for survey respondents to access these tools while responding to a survey. Such practices will inevitably lead to research that aligns more closely with search engine outputs and language model predictions than with diverse perspectives, societal discourse, and public opinion. Indeed, AI-generated responses to surveys may not even be a deliberate form of “cheating” by respondents – rather, they may believe that AI is a means of generating “correct” responses to questions they are asked. This human-guided, intentional use of AI to “improve” survey responses poses another significant challenge for researchers seeking to improve the validity of their survey responses.

There has been considerable methodological attention paid to potential use cases for AI to assist in the gathering of data for research7. Yet, as AI becomes increasingly integrated into data collection, it may give rise to a dystopian scenario in which social science becomes an echo chamber of AI-generated sources—where AI both asks and answers questions, and perhaps later reviews the paper8,9—resulting in insights and conclusions recycled from programmed responses, even nonsensical responses, rather than reflecting authentic human experiences and perspectives. If left unchecked, entire datasets could essentially consist of imputed variables, completely reliant on artificial data rather than genuine human input. This growing trend not only threatens the validity of online survey data but also the invaluable insights used to inform policy, programs, and communications.

These are not simply theoretical concerns. We have seen the increasing presence of AI-generated responses in recent surveys, and we suspect our experience is not unique. In recent work, we asked respondents how they defined “carbon-neutral” and when reviewing the data we observed a range of highly specific, nearly identical responses across genuine users. (Box 1) As with other online survey research, we identified genuine users by combining Qualtrics bot detection methods with logic checks, attention verification items, and response pattern analysis (e.g., timing, IP address, and straight-line behavior), and then further distinguished AI-assisted responses through semantic and syntactic similarity testing, iterative flagging, and comparative analysis across multiple free-text items. During the drafting of this commentary, researchers published findings about surveys recruiting from a popular online platform where 34% reported using LLMs to help answer open-ended survey questions10. Below, we discuss available detection tools and the methods we used with examples from our own work that we hope is illustrative for other survey researchers.

Designing Surveys and Analysis Plans to Reduce the Influence of AI Responses

AI-generated responses dilute genuine insights, obscure patterns of response (or emphasize patterns that human responses would not exhibit), and thus distort findings. To mitigate this, researchers must design surveys for the current AI-enabled landscape while also developing strategies to recognize AI-generated responses. Open-text items were once a reliable signal of human responses, where nonsensical or off-topic responses were viewed as practical flags for exclusion. Generative AI has inverted this, as now some of the most thoughtful and coherent-looking responses can be pasted from many accessible sources, including just a quick Google search, or even generated without leaving the app with which one is accessing a survey. Genuine human responses may even seem, at least at first glance, to be of lower quality than responses generated easily by AI.

Upstream design choices can reduce AI-assisted responding. Asking participants to commit at the outset to responding authentically, normalizing responses of “I don’t know” where uncertainty exists, can help shift the normative frame and lower the pressure of social desirability to be seen as knowledgeable before a single question is answered. Researchers can also consider replacing knowledge-retrieval prompts with questions that explicitly emphasize personal, emotional, experiential, and preferential targets while reminding respondents that AI does not have personal opinions and may not share their views. Lastly, researchers may also benefit from taking a harder look at streamlining survey length and complexity to reduce the cognitive burden of surveys that could otherwise make AI assistance feel more desirable in the first place.

Several tools have been developed to identify AI-generated text, analyzing linguistic patterns, response behavior, and metadata, but most academic discussion of this has been focused on the detection of AI in student work. Many of the limitations of such tools, such as incorrectly flagging non-native English speakers, unfortunately apply to survey research as well. However, the landscape of these tools shifts quickly; some tools widely cited are no longer available. For example, OpenAI’s Text Classifier was discontinued in 2023 following publicly acknowledged accuracy failures, particularly for short texts like those typical of survey items11,12. Survey and panel provider platforms like Prolific, Qualtrics, and others monitor response time and metadata to flag patterns that appear too fast or homogenous13 (e.g., straight-lining) – though these approaches risk misidentifying quick respondents as bots and, rarely, can identify inappropriate, nonsensical, or plagiarized text3,4. Most importantly, these flags were designed to catch inattentive or fraudulent completers, not for AI-assisted responses by human completers. The more fundamental problem is that the detection has been, and likely will continue to be, outpaced by available tools12. Westwood (2025) demonstrated that an autonomous AI agent could pass 99.8% of standard quality checks across >43,000 survey completions14.

As survey designers without advanced AI/AI-detection engineering expertise, we may need to accept that over-reliance on post-hoc detection is a losing strategy as model capabilities advance. Manual review of thousands of responses also presents obvious challenges. While researchers can and should develop standards for data quality review, manual review is both time-intensive and prone to error, and often just not possible for large datasets in population studies. The more tractable intervention is upstream, in how surveys are designed.

Despite challenges, using both manual review and automated tools together may offer more feasible and comprehensive results. For example, AI tools can first flag suspect responses, which can then undergo targeted manual review using predefined indicators. Employing two independent raters to assess a sample of responses can further improve reliability. Nonetheless, methods will have to constantly evolve as the technology gets increasingly sophisticated, and this will include methods for more directly engaging participants about authenticity, too.

Signs of AI-generated responses

As AI capabilities evolve, so must our methodological standards. In our own recent work, we conducted validation by running survey prompts through multiple LLMs (GPT, Gemini, Copilot, and Google AI Overview), finding several responses with near-verbatim matches. In one clear case –now the first entry in Box 1 – the response includes formatting artifacts such as duplicated quotation marks, the tag “+4”, and the label “AI Overview” itself, all characteristic of content copied directly from Google’s AI Overview interface. While we queried LLMs to assess response origins, model self-disclosure cannot always be relied upon, as some tools are trained to evade detection, or may not recognize syntax from older models. Thus, in our own work and what we advocate for here is not to rely on any single marker, but on a constellation of indicators. Below, we outlined what we did to look for syntactic uniformity, generic phrasing, lack of personal detail, and repeated patterns across users. When several of these indicators were present, especially with strong semantic similarity to known LLM outputs of the period, the likelihood of AI involvement was deemed high. The leading Box 1 example illustrates that both these artifacts and similarities in other entries are quite apparent.

These indicators should be understood as a snapshot; the natural quality of AI-generated text is rapidly evolving. As models have advanced and users have become more sophisticated in how they prompt and edit AI-generated text, several of these features, from formal tone to prompt-mirroring, may be less reliable signs. Also, some individual human responses unfacilitated by AI may naturally include some of these aspects, such as m-dashes, and as such comparisons looking at strange levels of similarity across respondents at a given time and place across a dataset may be most helpful. Observing patterns in our responses was what first created the need to develop the quality check protocol described above. In our data, at least one response was irrefutably AI-generated in that in included direct syntax from the Google AI Overview. Although we did not employ a full labeled dataset, this known instance and the use of platform model comparisons provide practical validation for interpreting these indicators as probabilistic, not definitive, markers of AI involvement. Future work using controlled comparisons and labeled datasets will be needed to fully validate, although this will likely need to be trained specifically to the subject area.

Research implication strategies

The increased prevalence of AI in survey responses necessitates better attunement to data authenticity verification. Researchers must consider complementing standard data quality review procedures with a review of open-response/free-text items (when still including them) to provide greater assurance of original thought. Open-ended questions worded to prompt for personal insights, such as “What does “carbon-neutral” mean to you?” or “Have you personally seen any signs of global warming?”, that require specific subjective insights can help identify verbal indicators of AI, because language models struggle with generating credible, personal opinions.

AI can also be leveraged to identify AI, and research teams can build and train their own AI agents to detect using labeled examples of human- and AI-generated text that is specific to the topic area. Conducting sensitivity analyses to test the effect of suspected AI responses on survey results can help guide researchers to assess the validity of their data and make decisions about excluding responses. However, the use of AI to detect fraudulent responses should itself be approached with caution, especially for research with personal information about human subjects, which among other things could inadvertently lead to re-identification or other privacy violations. Exposing participant responses to automated detection tools may violate IRB guidelines and national privacy laws. The use of AI to review individual survey responses may violate data privacy laws such as the U.S.’ Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act (FERPA), as well as the European Union’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), and similar laws in other regions.

Aside from the increasingly commonplace use of AI, it is also important to acknowledge that survey panels offer participants incentives to complete surveys, making the completion of more surveys faster and a substantial source of income for some respondents. We should therefore not be surprised by increased use of automation tools; this is part of the commonly discussed promise of AI. Researchers should expect that automation tools appreciated by many researchers themselves will continue to be used, allowing individuals to capitalize at scale.

Nonetheless, increased attention to the signs of AI in survey data is urgently needed, as this is a challenge that has fully arrived. While most of the AI in research is focused on intentional deception, our key insight here is that well-meaning use by genuine respondents is a significant and growing concern as well. Future research may wish to add items asking participants to indicate if they used assistance in preparing responses, or more explicitly ask participants not to do so—since, unlike other areas of AI presence in research, genuine human respondents may be both reachable and willing to forgo AI use with appropriate engagement. There may also be ways to prevent direct pasting of content into survey forms, as well as value in exploring respondent motivations for using AI-generated responses when subjective opinions are queried. For example, respondents may use AI due to a desire to appear more knowledgeable, to present socially acceptable answers, or out of a misguided belief that objective, rather than subjective, responses are preferred. Understanding motivations may empower researchers to encourage respondents to respond more authentically.

Our understanding of how best to detect AI and work with AI-enabled populations is just beginning. Implementing validation techniques can better ensure that research findings reflect genuine public opinions and knowledge, safeguarding the value of survey-based social science research. We hope this comment serves as a starting point for colleagues working in online research, and as a call to action for research teams equipped to develop sophisticated methods to safeguard future insights.
