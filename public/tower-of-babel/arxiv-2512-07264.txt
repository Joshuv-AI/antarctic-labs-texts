Quantum geometrical effects in non-Hermitian systems
Anton Montag, Tomoki Ozawa

Abstract
We explore the relation between quantum geometry in non-Hermitian systems and physically measurable phenomena. We highlight various situations in which the behavior of a non-Hermitian system is best understood in terms of quantum geometry, namely the notion of adiabatic potentials in non-Hermitian systems and the localization of Wannier states in periodic non-Hermitian systems. Further, we show that the non-Hermitian quantum metric appears in the response of the system upon time-periodic modulation, which one can use to experimentally measure the non-Hermitian quantum metric. We validate our results by providing numerical simulations of concrete exemplary systems.

II
Formalism and definitions: Quantum geometry for non-Hermitian systems

We consider generic linear systems whose dynamics are governed by a Schrödinger equation

\mathrm{i}\partial_{i}\ket{\psi}=H_{\bm{\lambda}}\ket{\psi}\,.

(1)

The linear operator
H_{\bm{\lambda}}\neq H_{\bm{\lambda}}^{\dagger}
is referred to as non-Hermitian Hamiltonian.
We emphasize that even though we are using terminologies from quantum mechanics, Eq. (
1
) governs various physical systems, not limited to the quantum realm.
Examples of this include classical non-conservative mechanical systems and electric field dynamics inside of complex refractive index materials
49
;
28
;
85
, among others, and the physical interpretation of the wavefunction depends on the observed system.
For an in depth discussion on systems governed by non-Hermitian Hamiltonians we refer to various review articles
8
;
5
;
9

The non-Hermitian Hamiltonian depends on the set of dimensionless parameters
\bm{\lambda}=\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
with
N
being the dimension of the parameter space.
The right eigenstates of the non-Hermitian Hamiltonian
\ket{\psi_{\bm{\lambda}}^n}_{\text{R}}
are found from the eigenvalue equation

H_{\bm{\lambda}}\ket{\psi_{\bm{\lambda}}^n}_{\text{R}}=\varepsilon_{n}(\bm{\lambda})\ket{\psi_{\bm{\lambda}}^n}_{\text{R}}.

(2)

The eigenvalues
\varepsilon_{n}(\bm{\lambda})
are generally complex-valued functions of the parameter set.
Due to the non-Hermiticity of
H_{\bm{\lambda}}
the right eigenfunctions associated with different eigenvalues are generally not orthogonal.
Further the adjoint of
\ket{\psi_{\bm{\lambda}}^n}_{\text{R}}
is not a left eigenstate of the non-Hermitian Hamiltonian.
Instead we need to construct a bi-orthogonal basis using the left eigenstates

, which are labeled such that

\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 5.44446pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\psi_{\bm{\lambda}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 5.44446pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\psi_{\bm{\lambda}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.65283pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\psi_{\bm{\lambda}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.65283pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\psi_{\bm{\lambda}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}\bra{\psi_{\bm{\lambda}}^n}H_{\bm{\lambda}}=\varepsilon_{n}(\bm{\lambda})\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 5.44446pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\psi_{\bm{\lambda}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 5.44446pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\psi_{\bm{\lambda}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.65283pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\psi_{\bm{\lambda}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.65283pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\psi_{\bm{\lambda}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}\bra{\psi_{\bm{\lambda}}^n}.

(3)

The right and left eigenstates form the bi-orthogonal basis
\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 5.44446pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{\psi_{\bm{\lambda}}^n}{\psi_{\bm{\lambda}}^m}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 5.44446pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{\psi_{\bm{\lambda}}^n}{\psi_{\bm{\lambda}}^m}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.65283pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{\psi_{\bm{\lambda}}^n}{\psi_{\bm{\lambda}}^m}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.65283pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{\psi_{\bm{\lambda}}^n}{\psi_{\bm{\lambda}}^m}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}\innerproduct{\psi_{\bm{\lambda}}^n}{\psi_{\bm{\lambda}}^m}_{\text{R}}\propto\delta_{nm}

\displaystyle\chi_{ij,n}

g_{ij,n}=\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}[\chi_{ij,n}]=\frac{1}{2}\left[\chi_{ij,n}+\chi_{ji,n}\right]\,.

Both Berry curvature and recently also the quantum metric have been related to a plethora of topological transport and response effects in Hermitian condensed matter physics and optics, c.f. Ref.

III
Adiabatic potentials in non-Hermitian systems

In Hermitian systems which contain a fast and a slow component, the quantum geometric effects influence the evolution of the wave function
10
.
If the fast component adiabatically follows the slowly varying component, the fast evolution can be integrated out resulting in effective scalar and vector potentials, which are given by the quantum metric and Berry connection of the fast degree of freedom.
This has been used in recent years to create and control artificial gauge fields in ultracold atom experiments
47
;
46
.
Extending this to the non-Hermitian realm allows us to treat fast-slow systems, which are dissipative and thus have non-Hermitian contributions.

Consider the non-Hermitian Hamiltonian

H_{\bm{x}}=\left(-\frac{\partial_{\bm{x}}^{2}}{2m}+V(\bm{x})\right)\mathbb{1}_{\text{F}}+H_{\text{F}}(\bm{x})\,,

(15)

where
\mathbb{1}_{\text{F}}
is the identity operator on the Hilbert space of the fast degree of freedom and
H_{\text{F}}(\bm{x})\neq H_{\text{F}}^{\dagger}(\bm{x})
is the non-Hermitian Hamiltonian of the fast degree of freedom.
Note that the position
\bm{x}
enters
H_{\text{F}}(\bm{x})
as a parameter, such that if
H_{\text{F}}(\bm{x})
contains spatial derivatives, they will be not with respect to
\bm{x}
.
The bi-orthogonal eigenstates of
H_{\text{F}}(\bm{x})
are given by the sets
\{\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 5.44446pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\phi_n(\bm{x})}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 5.44446pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\phi_n(\bm{x})}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.65283pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\phi_n(\bm{x})}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.65283pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\phi_n(\bm{x})}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}\bra{\phi_n(\bm{x})}\}

\{\ket{\phi_n(\bm{x})}_{\text{R}}\}

\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 5.44446pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{\phi_n(\bm{x})}{\phi_m(\bm{x})}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 5.44446pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{\phi_n(\bm{x})}{\phi_m(\bm{x})}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.65283pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{\phi_n(\bm{x})}{\phi_m(\bm{x})}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.65283pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{\phi_n(\bm{x})}{\phi_m(\bm{x})}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}\innerproduct{\phi_n(\bm{x})}{\phi_m(\bm{x})}_{\text{R}}=\delta_{nm}

Imposing that initially the fast degree of freedom is in a non-degenerate stationary state, meaning in an eigenstate
H_{\text{F}}(\bm{x})\ket{\phi_0(\bm{x})}_{\text{R}}=\varepsilon_{0}(\bm{x})\ket{\phi_0(\bm{x})}_{\text{R}}

Generically there will be regions in the parameter space

We stress that the results derived here do not hold, if the stationary states change during the evolution.
Further we note that in a generic non-Hermitian system the stationary state is unique, with the notable exception of pseudo-Hermitian systems, where in the unbroken regime all eigenenergies are real thus all states are stationary.
Additionally we impose that the stationary state is non-degenerate everywhere.
Changes in the stationary state and the implication of exceptional steady states are left for discussion in future works.
Within the additional constraint of a non-degenerate stationary state and given the adiabatic evolution of the fast part of the system we can treat both generic and unbroken pseudo-Hermitian systems on the same footing.

IV
Localization of non-Hermitian Wannier states

In Hermitian systems the connection between Bloch states and tight-binding descriptions of solid is made by employing Wannier states
80
.
These states are constructed from the Bloch basis and form an orthonormal basis.
The key property of Wannier states is their sharp localization within a single unit cell, thus forming the natural basis for any tight-binding description.
Their localization and spread is in turn governed by the quantum geometry associated with the Bloch states
80
;
24
.
In the following we derive similar results for the properties of non-Hermitian Wannier states.

Consider a continuous periodic non-Hermitian systems governed by the non-Hermitian Hamiltonian

H_{\bm{x}}=\frac{1}{2m}\left[-\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{x}}-\bm{A}(\bm{x})\right]^{2}+V(\bm{x})\,.

(29)

It is defined in terms of the mass
m=m^{*}
, the vector potential
\bm{A}(\bm{x})\neq\bm{A}^{*}(\bm{x})
and the scalar potential
V(\bm{x})\neq V^{*}(\bm{x})
, resulting in
H_{\bm{x}}\neq H_{\bm{x}}^{\dagger}
, and the vector and scalar potentials obey the same periodicity

\bm{A}(\bm{x}+\bm{R})=\bm{A}(\bm{x})\quad\text{and}\quad V(\bm{x}+\bm{R})=V(\bm{x}),

(30)

for all real-space lattice vectors
\bm{R}
.
Bloch’s theorem hold for non-Hermitian Hamiltonians, therefore the bi-orthogonal eigenstates are found from

\displaystyle H_{\bm{x}}\ket{\psi_{\bm{k}}^n(\bm{x})}_{\text{R}}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{n}(\bm{k})\ket{\psi_{\bm{k}}^n(\bm{x})}_{\text{R}}\quad\text{and}

(31)

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 5.44446pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\psi_{\bm{k}}^n(\bm{x})}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 5.44446pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\psi_{\bm{k}}^n(\bm{x})}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.65283pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\psi_{\bm{k}}^n(\bm{x})}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.65283pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\psi_{\bm{k}}^n(\bm{x})}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}\bra{\psi_{\bm{k}}^n(\bm{x})}H_{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{n}(\bm{k})\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 5.44446pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\psi_{\bm{k}}^n(\bm{x})}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 5.44446pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\psi_{\bm{k}}^n(\bm{x})}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.65283pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\psi_{\bm{k}}^n(\bm{x})}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.65283pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\psi_{\bm{k}}^n(\bm{x})}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}\bra{\psi_{\bm{k}}^n(\bm{x})}.

(32)

Both left and right Bloch eigenstates can be written as

\displaystyle\ket{u_{\bm{k}}^n(\bm{x})}_{\text{R}}

\displaystyle=e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}}\ket{u_{\bm{k}}^n(\bm{x})}_{\text{R}}\quad\text{and}

(33)

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 5.44446pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\psi_{\bm{k}}^n(\bm{x})}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 5.44446pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\psi_{\bm{k}}^n(\bm{x})}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.65283pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\psi_{\bm{k}}^n(\bm{x})}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.65283pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\psi_{\bm{k}}^n(\bm{x})}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}\bra{\psi_{\bm{k}}^n(\bm{x})}

\displaystyle=e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}}\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 5.44446pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{u_{\bm{k}}^n(\bm{x})}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 5.44446pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{u_{\bm{k}}^n(\bm{x})}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.65283pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{u_{\bm{k}}^n(\bm{x})}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.65283pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{u_{\bm{k}}^n(\bm{x})}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}\bra{u_{\bm{k}}^n(\bm{x})},

(34)

with the plane-wave contribution
e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}}
and the periodic part
\ket{u_{\bm{k}}^n(\bm{x})}_{\alpha}=\ket{u_{\bm{k}}^n(\bm{x}+\bm{R})}_{\alpha}
for all lattice vectors
\bm{R}
.
The periodic parts are eigenstates of the Bloch Hamiltonian
H_{\bm{k}}=e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}}H_{\bm{x}}e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}}
, which parametrically depends of the quasi-momentum
\bm{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Similar to their Hermitian limit, these models are often discussed using a tight-binding approximation.
The connection between continuous model and the tight-binding description can be made by constructing localized bi-orthogonal states, called non-Hermitian Wannier states
55
, defined by

\displaystyle\ket{w_{\bm{R}}^n}_{\text{R}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\text{BZ}}\!\frac{d^{d}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}\,\frac{e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}}\ket{\psi_{\bm{k}}^n}}{\sqrt{\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.30557pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{u_{\bm{k}}^n}{u_{\bm{k}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.30557pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{u_{\bm{k}}^n}{u_{\bm{k}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 5.3473pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{u_{\bm{k}}^n}{u_{\bm{k}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 5.3473pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{u_{\bm{k}}^n}{u_{\bm{k}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}\innerproduct{u_{\bm{k}}^n}{u_{\bm{k}}^n}_{\text{R}}}}\quad\text{and}

(35)

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 5.44446pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{w_{\bm{R}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 5.44446pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{w_{\bm{R}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.65283pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{w_{\bm{R}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.65283pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{w_{\bm{R}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}\bra{w_{\bm{R}}^n}

\displaystyle=\int_{\text{BZ}}\!\frac{d^{d}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{R}}\sqrt{\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.30557pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{u_{\bm{k}}^n}{u_{\bm{k}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.30557pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{u_{\bm{k}}^n}{u_{\bm{k}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 5.3473pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{u_{\bm{k}}^n}{u_{\bm{k}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 5.3473pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{u_{\bm{k}}^n}{u_{\bm{k}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}\innerproduct{u_{\bm{k}}^n}{u_{\bm{k}}^n}_{\text{R}}}\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 5.44446pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\psi_{\bm{k}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 5.44446pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\psi_{\bm{k}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.65283pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\psi_{\bm{k}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.65283pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\psi_{\bm{k}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}\bra{\psi_{\bm{k}}^n}.

(36)

They are bi-orthonormal
\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 5.44446pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{w_{\bm{R}}^n}{w_{\bm{R}'}^m}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 5.44446pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{w_{\bm{R}}^n}{w_{\bm{R}'}^m}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.65283pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{w_{\bm{R}}^n}{w_{\bm{R}'}^m}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.65283pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{w_{\bm{R}}^n}{w_{\bm{R}'}^m}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}\innerproduct{w_{\bm{R}}^n}{w_{\bm{R}'}^m}_{\text{R}}=\delta_{nm}\delta_{\bm{R}\bm{R}^{\prime}}
and the right Wannier functions of the same band are normalized
\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.30557pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{w_{\bm{R}}^n}{w_{\bm{R}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.30557pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{w_{\bm{R}}^n}{w_{\bm{R}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 5.3473pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{w_{\bm{R}}^n}{w_{\bm{R}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 5.3473pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{w_{\bm{R}}^n}{w_{\bm{R}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}\innerproduct{w_{\bm{R}}^n}{w_{\bm{R}}^n}_{\text{R}}=1
.
The Wannier functions of different lattice vectors
\bm{R}
are translational images of one another, meaning
for
w_{\bm{R}}^{n}(\bm{x})=\innerproduct{\bm{x}}{w_{\bm{R}}^n}_{\text{R}}
we find
w_{\bm{R}}^{n}(\bm{x})=w_{0}^{n}(\bm{x}-\bm{R})

A state of the system is usually described in terms of the right eigenstates, thus we are interested in the localization of the right non-Hermitian Wannier states.
First we relate the position matrix elements between right Wannier functions to the right-right Berry connection.
By rewriting

\displaystyle(\hat{\bm{x}}-\bm{R})\ket{w_{\bm{R}}^n}_{\text{R}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\text{BZ}}\!\frac{d^{d}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}\,e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot(\bm{x}-\bm{R})}\left[\mathrm{i}\partial_{\bm{k}}\left(\frac{\ket{u_{\bm{k}}^n}_{\text{R}}}{\sqrt{\smash[b]{\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.30557pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{u_{\bm{k}}^n}{u_{\bm{k}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.30557pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{u_{\bm{k}}^n}{u_{\bm{k}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 5.3473pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{u_{\bm{k}}^n}{u_{\bm{k}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 5.3473pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{u_{\bm{k}}^n}{u_{\bm{k}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}\innerproduct{u_{\bm{k}}^n}{u_{\bm{k}}^n}_{\text{R}}}}}\right)\right]

(37)

we find

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.30557pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\matrixelement{w_0^n}{\hat{\bm{x}}}{w_{\bm{R}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.30557pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\matrixelement{w_0^n}{\hat{\bm{x}}}{w_{\bm{R}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 5.3473pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\matrixelement{w_0^n}{\hat{\bm{x}}}{w_{\bm{R}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 5.3473pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\matrixelement{w_0^n}{\hat{\bm{x}}}{w_{\bm{R}}^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}\matrixelement{w_0^n}{\hat{\bm{x}}}{w_{\bm{R}}^n}_{\text{R}}=\int_{\text{BZ}}\!\frac{d^{d}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{R}}\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\left(\bm{A}_{n}^{\text{R}\text{R}}\right)\,,

(38)

where we used the definition of the right-right Berry connection Eq. (
13
).
For the details of the derivation we refer to Appendix
A
.
The special case
\bm{R}=0
simplifies Eq. (
38
) to

\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.30557pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\matrixelement{w_0^n}{\hat{\bm{x}}}{w_0^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.30557pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\matrixelement{w_0^n}{\hat{\bm{x}}}{w_0^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 5.3473pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\matrixelement{w_0^n}{\hat{\bm{x}}}{w_0^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 5.3473pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\matrixelement{w_0^n}{\hat{\bm{x}}}{w_0^n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}\matrixelement{w_0^n}{\hat{\bm{x}}}{w_0^n}_{\text{R}}=\bm{x}_{0}=\int_{\text{BZ}}\!\frac{d^{d}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\left(\bm{A}_{n}^{\text{R}\text{R}}\right)\,,

(39)

This integral over the right-right Berry connection determines the center of the Wannier state
\bm{x}_{0}
around which it is localized.
We can show, that the average position is gauge-independent.
Non-Hermiticity allows for gauge transformations
\ket{u_{\bm{k}}^n}\rightarrow e^{\mathrm{i}\phi(\bm{x})}\ket{u_{\bm{k}}^n}
with a complex-valued
\phi(\bm{x})
.
The gauge transformation needs to be periodic with respect to the reciprocal lattice vectors
\bm{G}
, thus we find

\phi(\bm{k}+\bm{G})=\phi(\bm{k})+\bm{G}\cdot\bm{R}\,.

(40)

It is clear that any non-Hermitian gauge transformation can be split into a shift
\phi(\bm{k})=\bm{k}\cdot\bm{R}
, a periodic gauging of the phase
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}[\phi(\bm{k}+\bm{G})]=\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}[\phi(\bm{k})]
and a periodic rescaling of the Bloch states
\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}[\phi(\bm{k}+\bm{G})]=\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}[\phi(\bm{k})]

Crucially the rescaling of the Bloch states, which carries no physical meaning, yields only changes in the imaginary part of
\bm{A}_{n}^{\text{R}\text{R}}
, thus the normalization of the Bloch states does not effect the central position of the Wannier states.
The shift
\phi(\bm{k})=\bm{k}\cdot\bm{R}
results only in a relabeling of the Wannier states, hence it only changes the assigning of the
\bm{R}=0
Wannier states.
Lastly the change of the Berry connection due to periodic real part of
\phi(\bm{k})
vanishes after integrating over the Brillouin zone.
Therefore the center of the Wannier states
\bm{x}_{c}
is gauge-invariant.

Therefore the spread of non-Hermitian right Wannier states is bounded from below by the right-right non-Hermitian quantum metric integrated over the Brillouin zone.
We note, that analogous results can be obtained if the left Wannier states are considered instead.
Their center and spread are determined by left-left Berry connection and non-Hermitian quantum metric respectively.

V
Measurement of non-Hermitian quantum metric through periodic driving

For Hermitian systems it has been demonstrated that the quantum metric can be extracted from the excitation rate of a quantum system under time-periodic modulation of the parameters.
This result is largely based on time-dependent perturbation theory, which up to our knowledge has not yet been fully generalized to generic non-Hermitian systems.
The perturbation of a Hermitian system with a non-Hermitian time-dependent perturbation has been studied before
48
;
64
;
26
and high-order perturbative expansions have been used to approximate the short-time dynamics
81
.
Additionally, the response of non-Hermitian wavepackets to time-dependent perturbations has recently been derived
7
.
However, transitions between the states of a generic non-Hermitian system induced by time-dependent perturbation have not been described and there is not yet a non-Hermitian analog of Fermi’s golden rule
42
.
Here, we derive the time-dependent perturbation of a generic non-Hermitian system with a unique steady state.
In Appendix
B
we extend this to non-Hermitian systems with multiple non-degenerate stationary states.
We use this method to extract the non-Hermitian right-right quantum metric from a generic two-band system.

Consider a non-Hermitian system governed by a time-independent Hamiltonian
H_{0}\neq H_{0}^{\dagger}
and the bi-orthogonal basis sets
\{\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 5.44446pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\psi_n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 5.44446pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\psi_n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.65283pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\psi_n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.65283pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\psi_n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}\bra{\psi_n}\}
and
\{\ket{\psi_n}_{\text{R}}\}
with
H_{0}\ket{\psi_n}_{\text{R}}=(\omega_{n}-i\gamma_{n})\ket{\psi_n}_{\text{R}}
and
\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 5.44446pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{\psi_n}{\psi_m}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 5.44446pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{\psi_n}{\psi_m}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.65283pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{\psi_n}{\psi_m}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.65283pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{\psi_n}{\psi_m}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}\innerproduct{\psi_n}{\psi_m}_{\text{R}}=\delta_{nm}
.
We assume that the system has a unique stationary state
n=0
with
\gamma_{0}=\min_{n}(\gamma_{n})
and
\gamma_{0}<\gamma_{n}\;\forall n\neq 0
.
For simplicity we set
\gamma_{0}=0
.
For systems with multiple nondegenerate stationary states, for example pseudo-Hermitian systems in the unbroken regime, we repeat the derivation in Appendix
B
.
Independent of how the system was initiated at time
t=-\infty
it will be in the state
\{\ket{\psi_0}_{\text{R}}\}
for finite time
t<0
.
At time
t=0
a time-dependent non-Hermitian perturbation
\epsilon f(t)H_{1}
is turned on, where
H_{1}
is a possibly non-Hermitian operator,
\epsilon\ll 1
,
f(t)\in\mathbb{R}
.
Our goal is to derive the occupation of all decaying "excited" states
\{\ket{\psi_n}_{\text{R}}\}
to first order in
\gamma
.

We start by expanding the state of the system in right eigenstates of the unperturbed Hamiltonian

\ket{\psi(t)}=\sum_{n}c_{n}(t)e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{n}t-\gamma_{n}t}\frac{\ket{\psi_n}_{\text{R}}}{\sqrt{\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.30557pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{\psi_n}{\psi_n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.30557pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{\psi_n}{\psi_n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 5.3473pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{\psi_n}{\psi_n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 5.3473pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{\psi_n}{\psi_n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}\innerproduct{\psi_n}{\psi_n}_{\text{R}}}}\,.

(44)

For
t<0
the system is in the steady state thus
c_{n}(t)=\delta_{n0}
.
After turning on the time-dependent perturbation the system evolves according to the Schrödinger equation

\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}\ket{\psi(t)}=\left(H_{0}+\epsilon f(t)H_{1}\right)\ket{\psi(t)}\,.

(45)

By acting with
\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 5.44446pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\psi_m}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 5.44446pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\psi_m}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.65283pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\psi_m}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.65283pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\bra{\psi_m}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}\bra{\psi_m}
from the left, we find for the complex-valued amplitudes
c_{m}(t)
the differential equation

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}c_{m}(t)=\sum_{n}\Bigl[

\displaystyle\epsilon\sqrt{\tfrac{\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 4.10695pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{\psi_m}{\psi_m}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.10695pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{\psi_m}{\psi_m}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 3.07639pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{\psi_m}{\psi_m}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 3.07639pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{\psi_m}{\psi_m}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}\innerproduct{\psi_m}{\psi_m}_{\text{R}}}{\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 4.10695pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{\psi_n}{\psi_n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.10695pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{\psi_n}{\psi_n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 3.07639pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{\psi_n}{\psi_n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 3.07639pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{\psi_n}{\psi_n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}\innerproduct{\psi_n}{\psi_n}_{\text{R}}}}\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 5.44446pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\matrixelement{\psi_m}{H_1}{\psi_n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 5.44446pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\matrixelement{\psi_m}{H_1}{\psi_n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.65283pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\matrixelement{\psi_m}{H_1}{\psi_n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.65283pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\matrixelement{\psi_m}{H_1}{\psi_n}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}\matrixelement{\psi_m}{H_1}{\psi_n}_{\text{R}}

(46)

\displaystyle\times c_{n}(t)f(t)e^{\mathrm{i}(\omega_{m}-\omega_{n})t}e^{(\gamma_{m}-\gamma_{n})t}\Bigr]\,.

The solution of this equation yields a power series in
\epsilon
, which usually is truncated to achieve the desired precision.
We are interested in the linear response of the system
\propto\epsilon
, thus we insert the unperturbed amplitudes
c_{n}(t)=\delta_{n0}
in Eq. (
46
).
The first order corrections to the amplitudes of the excited states
m\neq 0
are given by

\displaystyle c_{m}(t)=

\displaystyle-\mathrm{i}\epsilon\sqrt{\tfrac{\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 4.10695pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{\psi_m}{\psi_m}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.10695pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{\psi_m}{\psi_m}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 3.07639pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{\psi_m}{\psi_m}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 3.07639pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{\psi_m}{\psi_m}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}\innerproduct{\psi_m}{\psi_m}_{\text{R}}}{\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 4.10695pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{\psi_0}{\psi_0}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.10695pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{\psi_0}{\psi_0}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 3.07639pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{\psi_0}{\psi_0}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 3.07639pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\innerproduct{\psi_0}{\psi_0}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{R}}}}\innerproduct{\psi_0}{\psi_0}_{\text{R}}}}\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 5.44446pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\matrixelement{\psi_m}{H_1}{\psi_0}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 5.44446pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\matrixelement{\psi_m}{H_1}{\psi_0}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.65283pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\matrixelement{\psi_m}{H_1}{\psi_0}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.65283pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\matrixelement{\psi_m}{H_1}{\psi_0}}}_{\mathmakebox[0pt][r]{\text{L}}}}\matrixelement{\psi_m}{H_1}{\psi_0}_{\text{R}}

(47)

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{t}\!dtf(t)e^{\mathrm{i}(\omega_{m}-\omega_{0})t}e^{\gamma_{m}t}\,.

Note, that while for large
t
the amplitudes may diverge
\propto e^{\gamma_{m}t}
, this divergence is exactly canceled, by the decay of the excited states in the non-Hermitian case.
Therefore we expect that any periodic time-dependent perturbation will result in a non-zero, periodic occupation of the excited states.
The physical interpretation of this is, that the excitation due to the time-dependent drive and the the decay rate of the excited state are balanced for some non-zero time averaged occupation.
Experimentally this can be accessed by measuring as the time averaged occupation of an excited state given by

n_{m}(t)=|P_{m}^{\text{R}\text{L}}\ket{\psi(t)}|^{2}=|c_{m}(t)|^{2}e^{-2\gamma_{m}t}\,,

(48)

where we used a bi-orthogonal projector to extract the occupation of
\ket{\psi_m}_{\text{R}}
in the non-orthogonal right basis.
In the long-time limit for periodic perturbations
f(t)\propto\cos(\omega t)
the occupation is oscillating around a constant value
\langle n_{m}(t)\rangle_{T}
.
This is drastically different to the Hermitian case or to a non-Hermitian system with multiple steady states.
There the occupation grows linearly in time and only the excitation rate can be meaningfully discussed, which results in Fermi’s golden rule.
We stress, that the linear response of non-Hermitian system cannot be obtained by using Fermi’s golden rule derived for Hermitian systems.
In addition, due to the decay of the excited states, we do not obtain an exact resonance condition here.
Any time-dependent perturbation will result in finite occupation of all excited states.
However, for periodic perturbations
f(t)\propto\cos(\omega t)
the largest occupation of an excited state is still found for resonant driving
\omega=|\omega_{n}-\omega_{0}|
.
For non-Hermitian systems with multiple steady states there will be linear growth of the occupation of a initially empty steady states and this requires exact resonance.

To show how accurate the linear response of a non-Hermitian system to the time-dependent perturbation is described by Eq. (
48
), consider the system

H_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\mathrm{i}-1}{\sqrt{2}}&1\\
1&-\frac{\mathrm{i}-1}{\sqrt{2}}\end{pmatrix}+\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}[\sqrt{1-\mathrm{i}}]\mathbb{1}

(49)

perturbed by
\epsilon f(t)H_{1}
, with

f(t)=2\cos(\omega t)\quad\text{and}\quad H_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

(50)

This model is solved numerically for
\epsilon=0.02
and
\omega=2.2
for the initial state being the ground state of
H_{0}
given by
\ket{\psi(t=0)}=((1-\mathrm{i})/\sqrt{2}+\sqrt{1-\mathrm{i}},-1)^{T}
.
In Figure
2
the exact time evolution of the occupation of the excited state is compared with the result derived from non-Hermitian time-dependent perturbation theory.
The oscillatory behavior of the occupation is clearly visible.

Using the time-dependent perturbation theory developed above, we propose a measurement scheme to extract the right-right non-Hermitian quantum metric.
We consider a generic non-Hermitian 2-band system described by the parameter dependent Hamiltonian
H(\bm{\lambda})
.
Assume that the system has a unique stationary state
\ket{\psi_0}_{\text{R}}
.
For simplicity we assume that the
\ket{\psi_0}_{\text{R}}
is not decaying.
Initially the system is prepared in its stationary state at
\bm{\lambda}=\bm{\bar{\lambda}}
, and to measure the quantum metric at that point in parameter state we modulate one parameter
\lambda_{j}
as

\lambda_{j}(t)=\bar{\lambda}_{j}+2\epsilon\cos(\omega t)\,.

(51)

Given that
\epsilon\ll 1
we Taylor-expand the Hamiltonian

H(\bm{\lambda}(t))=H(\bar{\bm{\lambda}})+2\epsilon\cos(\omega t)\left(\partial_{j}H(\lambda)\bigr|_{\bm{\lambda}=\bar{\bm{\lambda}}}\right)\,.

(52)

VI
Conclusions

We have explored several non-Hermitian systems in which the geometry of quantum states results in measurable physical phenomena.
Our investigation revealed how evolution of fast-slow non-Hermitian systems is governed by an effective Schrödinger equation acting on the state of the slow system, in which the Berry connection and quantum metric of the fast system appear as vector and scalar potential, respectively.
These adiabatic potentials may be non-Hermitian and we showed qualitatively different evolution for real and complex quantum metric, resulting in oscillation or decay of the norm of the wavefunction.
For continuous periodic non-Hermitian systems bi-orthogonal Wannier states may be constructed and we derived the relation between their localization and the quantum geometry associated with the Bloch states, proving that their spread is limited by the quantum metric, similarly to Hermitian Wannier states.
Finally we proposed a scheme to extract the quantum metric from two-level non-Hermitian systems by periodically perturbing the system.
We showed how a time-dependent perturbation excites a non-Hermitian system from its stationary non-decaying state to decaying excited states, derived by considering the linear response of the system.
Contrary to Hermitian systems, in which Fermi’s golden rule results in a constant excitation rate but requires precise frequency matching, no exact frequency matching is required in non-Hermitian systems and the decay of the excited states results in a finite occupation of the excited states.
After some initial transient the occupation oscillates around a constant value.
We employed this to determine the quantum metric of a two-level system, by periodically varying the parameters of a two-level system and measuring the time-averaged occupation of the excited state.

Our results highlight the importance of quantum geometry in non-Hermitian settings.
Non-Hermitian quantum geometry has proven before to be an indicator signaling topological phase transitions, but here we showed measurable effects based on quantum geometry, which can be used as a resource to probe and design systems.
Going beyond semiclassical wavepacket dynamics, the notion of non-Hermitian adiabatic potentials enables the usage of artificial gauge fields for non-Hermitian systems.
Adjusting both Berry connection and quantum metric of the system evolving on the fast time-scale, enables us to shape the desired potentials, controlling the evolution of slow system.
This may be used as a non-Hermitian resource for controlling ultracold atomic gasses, in which internal degrees of freedom of the atoms act as fast system.
Furthermore, the proposed experimental extraction scheme of the quantum metric can be applied to topological photonic settings with multiple control parameters.
This makes the quantum metric accessible in a setting, for which non-Hermitian topology has recently found many applications.
