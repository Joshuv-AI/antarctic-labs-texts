Development and application of a multiple nucleotide polymorphism (MNP)-based molecular identification system for pear cultivars
Authors: Xiang Yang, Zhiwei Fang, Yufen Cao, Luming Tian, Hai Peng, Ying Zhang, Jiayu Xu, Junfei Zhou, Hongliang Huo, Dan Qi, Chao Liu, Tiantian Li, Xingguang Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPear is one of the most popular and widely cultivated fruits globally, with rich cultivar diversity. Pyrus species are characterized by self-incompatibility and the absence of reproductive barriers between species, leading to extensive gene flow and genetic recombination among different types of cultivars. As a result, cultivar identification technologies have considerable practical significance in pear production. Multiple nucleotide polymorphism (MNP) technology, which combines multiplex PCR amplification and high-throughput sequencing, offers high efficiency and accuracy in pear cultivar identification, and meets the needs of cultivar innovation and industry development.ResultsWe applied MNP technology to pear cultivar identification for the first time, establishing fingerprints for major pear cultivars and exploring the feasibility of MNP markers for pear population structure analysis. Based on genomic resequencing data from 143 pear accessions, 558 marker loci were initially developed, and 310 core markers were retained after screening. The 310 MNP markers showed high polymorphism, with an average of 18.14 alleles per marker locus and PIC values ranging from 0.57 to 0.99. Validation using 76 representative pear cultivars demonstrated reproducibility and accuracy rates exceeding 99% for the 310 MNP markers. A total of 2,850 pairwise comparisons among the 76 cultivars showed an average genetic differentiation of 90.89%. Population structure analyses based on MNP markers effectively reflected the classification relationships among the 76 cultivars, clearly distinguishing European from Asian pears.ConclusionIn summary, the developed pear MNP markers possess high polymorphism, stability, and cultivar-discrimination capability, promising extensive future applications in pear cultivar identification and population genetic research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07623-2.

Introduction

Pear (Pyrus L.) originated from the mountainous regions of western or southwestern China [1, 2], forming diverse species and cultivars through long-term dispersal, natural selection, and domestication. About 22 species of Pyrus have been recognized, and more than 21000 Pyrus accessions are currently conserved ex situ globally (https://www.fao.org/wiews/data/ex-situ-sdg-251/search/en/?no_cache=1) [3], mainly categorized into occidental and oriental pears [4]. Among these, the predominant cultivated species in Western countries is P. communis, whereas in Asia, the main cultivated species include P. pyrifolia, P. bretschneideri, P. ussuriensis, and P. sinkiangensis. Additionally, numerous cultivars that do not belong to the above-mentioned species are widely cultivated worldwide. In China alone, more than 1000 pear cultivars have been documented, with over 100 cultivars widely grown [5]. Furthermore, advancements in breeding technology have accelerated the breeding process of pears. Since 2016, applications for new pear cultivars in China have significantly increased, with the annual average number rising from single digits to over 40. As of the end of 2023, the cumulative number of applications for new pear cultivars in China alone reached approximately 295 (http://202.127.42.145/bigdataNew/Home/Index).

The genus Pyrus encompasses numerous cultivars with complex genetic backgrounds, a situation increasingly pronounced as cultivation areas expand and breeding activities progress. The diversification of pear cultivars has enriched the fruit market but has also led to frequent cases of synonyms, homonyms, and misuse of cultivar names. Furthermore, inconsistent transliteration and a lack of unified standards in the introduction of pear cultivars have exacerbated the difficulties in identification and classification within the genus Pyrus. Due to the absence of rapid and accurate cultivar identification methods, the collection, preservation, and utilization of pear germplasm resources have become increasingly challenging, leading to market disorder and difficulties in safeguarding the legitimate rights of breeders. Traditional identification of Pyrus species primarily relies on morphological and agronomic trait observations, or examination of pollen ultrastructure under microscopy. However, these methods are inefficient, subjective, and prone to misidentification in practical applications, thus failing to meet the requirements of modern management and industry development for pear resources. Therefore, the development of faster and more accurate cultivar identification methods is particularly necessary.

With the rapid advancement of DNA molecular marker technology, identification and systematic classification of the genus Pyrus have embraced entirely new research approaches. DNA molecular markers directly reveal genetic diversity at the gene level, offering significant advantages over traditional morphological, cytological, and biochemical markers, such as widespread distribution across genomes, high accuracy, simple operation, and immunity to environmental interference. In studies on the genus Pyrus, various classical DNA marker techniques have been extensively utilized, including RFLP (Restriction Fragment Length Polymorphism) [6, 7], RAPD (Random Amplified Polymorphic DNA) [8, 9], AFLP (Amplified Fragment Length Polymorphism) [10, 11], SSR (Simply Sequence Repeat Polymorphism) [12–14], SNP (Single Nucleotide Polymorphism) [15]. However, these DNA molecular marker methods still possess certain limitations in practical applications. For example, although AFLP and SSR markers have been adapted to capillary electrophoresis platforms, they still rely on complex PCR-based workflows with limited multiplexing capabilities. Moreover, PCR slippage can lead to genotyping errors, particularly in SSR analysis, thereby limiting their suitability for large-scale, high-throughput applications [16]. The increasing availability of pear genome sequences [17–20] and advancements in second-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies have established SNP as predominant markers for studying genetic relationships in Pyrus species. Despite their abundant quantity, SNP markers exhibit limitations in resolving complex polyploid allelic configurations due to their biallelic nature, coupled with relatively high detection costs. To overcome these shortcomings, the multiple nucleotide polymorphisms (MNP) marker technology has emerged. MNP refers to sequence polymorphisms caused by variations at one or more nucleotides, enabling the detection of multiple genotypic variations within a single locus. Combined with multiplex PCR amplification and high-throughput sequencing, MNP technology can simultaneously detect hundreds to thousands of marker loci in one reaction, achieving precise genotype information through bioinformatics analysis. Its high sensitivity, accuracy, and detection efficiency are notably superior to traditional methods. Additionally, MNP marker detection does not require expensive equipment, facilitates cross-platform and inter-laboratory data sharing, and simplifies the construction and application of DNA fingerprint databases. These features have enabled MNP markers to exhibit broad application potential across various crops (such as grape [21], chrysanthemum [22], shiitake mushroom [23]) in genetic evolutionary studies, gene mapping, breeding programs, germplasm resource evaluation, and intellectual property protection. Based on these advantages, MNP markers are expected to play a significant role in genetic research and breeding practices of the genus Pyrus, promoting a comprehensive upgrade in cultivar identification and systematic classification technologies.

In 2020, China released its first plant cultivar identification standard based on MNP markers [24], marking the formal application of this technology in plant cultivar identification. However, there are currently no research reports on the development and application of MNP markers in the genus Pyrus. To address recurring issues in the pear industry, such as infringement, fraudulent market labeling, synonyms, and homonyms, this study successfully developed MNP markers suitable for Pyrus based on genomic sequencing data from 143 pear cultivars and validated these markers across 76 pear cultivars. The results have laid a solid foundation for precise identification of pear cultivars and the construction of standardized databases, thereby filling the gap in molecular marker application for the genus Pyrus. Additionally, this study provides critical technical support for the intellectual property protection and genetic resource management of pear cultivars, promoting cultivar breeding and innovation, standardized market development, and improved global competitiveness in the pear industry.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

A total of 76 pear cultivars were provided by the National Repository of Pear and Apple (Xingcheng, China), including 15 P. hybrid, 24 P. pyrifolia, 7 P. ussuriensis, 15 P. bretschneideri, 8 P. communis, and 7 P. sinkiangensis (Table S1).

Genomic DNA extraction and reduced-representation genome sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from fresh pear leaves using the CTAB method with a novel plant genomic DNA extraction kit (DP320) produced by Tiangen Biotech (Beijing) Co., Ltd. DNA quality assessment was conducted by measuring absorbance ratios using a spectrophotometer, and an additional 4 μL DNA aliquot was electrophoresed on a 1% agarose gel to confirm DNA integrity. Genomic sequencing libraries were prepared using the Nextera XT Kit (Illumina). Subsequently, DNA samples were sequenced on the Illumina Novoseq platform provided by Novogene, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Sequencing data processing

To ensure the broad applicability of MNP markers across diverse pear types, genomic data from a total of 143 wild and cultivated pear accessions were utilized for marker development. Among them, 113 accessions were retrieved from the NCBI database (project number: PRJNA381668), and the remaining 30 accessions were newly sequenced using a reduced-representation genome sequencing approach at an average depth of 20 ×. Sequencing reads of all the 143 accessions were aligned to the reference genome GCA_000315295 [25] using bowtie2 (2.1.0) [26]. SNP detection was performed using SAMtools (1.18) [27], and the resulting SNPs were output in standard VCF format. Lastly, vcftools (0.1.16) [28] was used for filtering with the following parameters: maf 0.05, max-missing 0.8, minDP 2, maxDP 1000, minQ 30, minGQ 0, min-alleles 2, max-alleles 2. Other analyses were conducted with default parameters.

Development of MNP markers and primer design

Candidate MNP marker loci were screened from the filtered VCF file using custom scripts, applying a sliding window approach with a window size of 125 bp and a step length of 5 bp across the genome. For each candidate region, the number of SNPs and discriminatory power (DP) across 143 pear accessions were calculated.

“n” represents the number of sample pairs that can be distinguished in pairwise comparisons among all accessions, and “N” is the total number of sample pairs compared. Regions containing more than one SNP loci and exhibiting DP values greater than 0.2 were identified as candidate MNP marker loci. Then, by sliding forward by 20 bp, the above parameters were recalculated for further improvement. Candidate markers were selected according to the following criteria: (1) each MNP marker must contain at least one SNP locus; (2) the discriminatory power (DP) of the markers should be ≥ 0.2 and maximized as far as possible; (3) candidate MNP markers should be evenly distributed across the genome. Multiple PCR primers are designed according to the following principles: (1) the amplified fragment length should range between 150 bp and 275 bp; (2) There will be no significant competitive amplification or primer dimers formed in PCR reactions, thus enabling simultaneous amplification of all marker loci in a single reaction.

Multiplex PCR amplification and library construction

Primers were designed using Primer3 software [29], and both multiplex PCR amplification and library construction were carried out using a multiplex PCR kit from Shijiazhuang Boruidi Biotech Co., Ltd. PCR tubes were prepared by adding 4 μL of multiplex PCR primer mix (0.2 μM each primer), 4 μL genomic DNA, 10 μL GenoPlexs 3 × T Master Mix, and 12 μL sterile water, mixed thoroughly by vortexing. Multiplex PCR was performed as follows: 95℃ for 3 min; 15 cycles of 95℃ for 20 s, 60℃ for 4 min; followed by a final extension at 72℃ for 4 min. The multiplex PCR products were purified upon reaction completion. The purified multiplex PCR products were mixed with 10 μL GenoPlexs 3 × T Master Mix, 2 μL P5 primer (5 μM), 2 μL P7 barcode primer (5 μM), and 16 μL sterile water. The reaction mixture was vortexed, briefly centrifuged, and amplified as follows: 95℃ for 3 min; 8 cycles of 95℃ for 15 s, 58℃ for 15 s, and 70℃ for 30 s; followed by a final extension at 72℃ for 5 min. Following PCR, the resulting 30 μL sequencing libraries were purified and subsequently sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform.

Marker genotyping by sequencing read alignment

Sequencing reads from each sample were aligned to the reference genome using Bowtie 2 (2.1.0) [26], and allele genotypes and their abundance at each locus were recorded. The genotype with the highest abundance was identified as the major allele genotype. loci with major allele genotype abundance > 20 was considered successfully detected. The average marker coverage per sample was calculated by dividing the total number of reads aligned to all target loci by the number of target loci. Additionally, the number of allelic genotypes per locus, observed heterozygosity (Ho), and discriminatory power were calculated as described in a previous study [30].

Reproducibility tests were conducted for 57 randomly selected cultivars within the same laboratory and 5 cultivars across different laboratories. A marker was considered reproducible if its genotyping results were consistent across replicates. Reproducibility rate (R) was calculated as R = m/M, where m represents the number of reproducible MNP loci, and M represents the total number of compared MNP loci. Accuracy (A) was calculated as A = 1–(1–R)/2. For each MNP locus, two samples were considered identical if they had the same allele genotype. Genetic similarity (GS) was calculated as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$GS\;=\;\frac{n_{ij}}{N_{ij}}\times100\%$$\end{document}where nij represents the loci number with identical genotype and Nij means the loci number detected successfully in both samples.

Population structure analysis

Based on the distance matrix, a neighbor-joining (NJ) tree was constructed, and a principal component analysis (PCA) was performed for 76 pear cultivars using R software (4.3.3). Population genetic structure analysis was conducted using STRUCTURE software (2.3.4) [31] based on 310 MNP loci, with K values ranging from 2 to 7 and 15 independent runs for each K. The burn-in period was set to 10,000, followed by 20,000 MCMC replications. The optimal number of clusters (K) was determined using ΔK analysis implemented in Structure Harvester (0.6.94) [32].

Results

Characteristics of MNP loci

Based on genomic sequencing data from 143 pear accessions, 558 MNP marker loci were developed (Fig. 1). Among the 76 pear cultivars tested, 248 marker loci were discarded due to potential issues with detection rates, nonspecific amplification, and reproducibility of marker genotyping. The 310 retained MNP markers increased the minimum detection rate from 80.98% to 93.87%. The discriminatory power (DP) of individual loci ranged from 56.5% to 99.3%, averaging 90.9%; 203 loci had DP values exceeding 90%, indicating robust discrimination capability. The sequence lengths of the 310 MNP markers ranged from 157 to 295 bp, and each marker contained between 2 and 128 SNPs with a minor allele frequency (MAF) > 5% (low-frequency variants were excluded to ensure marker authenticity), averaging 25.41. Each MNP marker detected between 3 and 282 allelic genotypes (average: 18.14 ± 11.08), with polymorphic information content (PIC) values ranging from 0.57 to 0.99; 283 markers detected more than 10 allelic genotypes. The average observed heterozygosity (Ho) for MNP markers was 0.66, indicating abundant genetic diversity among the 76 cultivars (Fig. 2).Fig. 1Workflow for the development and application of MNP MarkersFig. 2Information of 310 MNP markers in Pyrus species. Impact of marker quantity on cultivar identification accuracy

According to “calibration principle” [33], results from statistical analysis using binomial distribution theory, and practical examples in breeding programs, the threshold for determining essentially derived pear cultivars was established at 90%, while the genetic similarity threshold for distinguishing different cultivars was set at 96%. Increasing the number of markers improved the accuracy of cultivar identification. For example, cultivars ‘Beurre Hardy’ and ‘Red D’ Anjou’ had a genetic similarity of 95.19%, exceeding the 90% threshold; thus, they were considered as “suspected essentially derived cultivars”. Assuming the total number of markers used is N, an erroneous conclusion due to sampling error can be avoided only if at least N × 90% of sampled markers are identical between tested and reference cultivars. It is evident that when using 250 markers or more, the probability of correctly identifying essentially derived cultivars approaches 100%. With our use of 310 markers, the probability of erroneous cultivar identification due to sampling errors was only 0.01% (99.99% accuracy) (Fig. 3A). Cultivars ‘Sha01’ and ‘Korla Fragrant Pear’ had a genetic similarity of 98.71%, exceeding the 96% threshold, thus were judged as “suspected identical cultivars”. Similar reasoning applied to essentially derived cultivar identification suggests that only when the marker count reaches 300 or more can accuracy surpass 99%. Reproducibility and accuracy of MNP marker genotyping

Reproducibility tests of 5 cultivars conducted in different laboratories showed 49 discrepant genotypes among 12,414 compared marker loci (0.39%), corresponding to genotyping reproducibility and accuracy rates of 99.61% and 99.80%, respectively. In a single identification, genotyping errors affected no more than 0.28 (310 × (1–99.91%)) markers, corresponding to only a 0.09% impact on genetic similarity coefficients, which is negligible. Cultivar discrimination power

Pairwise comparisons among the 76 cultivars yielded 2,850 combinations, for which genetic similarity coefficients were calculated (Figs. 4B and 5A). On average, 273 MNP markers differed between cultivars, corresponding to a mean genetic differentiation of 90.89%. In total, 2,837 pairs (99.69%) exhibited similarity coefficients below 96% and were thus considered genetically distinct, while 13 pairs (0.45%) showed similarity coefficients above 96%, indicating potential difficulty in discrimination. Further analyses included ten parent–offspring pairs (Fig. 5B) and six bud sport pairs (Fig. 5C). The parent–offspring combinations showed wide variation in similarity coefficients (ranging from 0 to 89.97%), consistent with their known genetic relationships. In contrast, all six bud sport pairs exhibited similarity coefficients above 98.71%, suggesting extremely low genomic variation between them. These results demonstrate the overall high discriminatory power of MNP markers for pear cultivar identification. However, as also reflected in the 13 indistinguishable pairs, the current marker set has limited resolution for detecting subtle genetic differences such as those found between bud sports. Discussion

Pear is among the oldest cultivated fruit trees globally, characterized by a wide variety, and challenges in cultivar identification have existed since ancient times. As early as the first century AD, Roman naturalists observed that differences in naming the same pear cultivar across regions caused synonymy issues [34]. Presently, inaccuracy, inconvenience, and high costs in pear cultivar identification still pose significant challenges for the industry. Unauthorized propagation without cultivar registration or breeder authorization, fraudulent labeling, homonyms, and synonyms frequently occur in the market. Furthermore, backward agricultural law enforcement, inefficient market supervision, lengthy rights-protection processes, and enforcement difficulties exacerbate infringement issues. For a long period, cultivar differentiation relied primarily on phenotypic differences and geographic distribution [4]. However, Pyrus species exhibit self-incompatibility without reproductive isolation among species, resulting in some cultivars lacking specific morphological differentiation, making morphological identification inaccurate. In recent years, AI-based image recognition technologies—widely applied in the identification of various crop species [35–37]—have similarly been constrained by phenotypic limitations and have shown limited effectiveness in distinguishing closely related pear cultivars. Subsequent identification methods based on chemical composition [38], isozymes [39, 40], and palynology [41] have also been unsatisfactory in terms of accuracy. SSR and SNP markers effectively distinguish closely related cultivars but require numerous loci to increase resolution due to limited information per locus, thus increasing costs. In this study, only two PCR amplification and purification steps were required for 310 markers, significantly improving efficiency and reducing costs. Although genotyping of MNP markers requires next-generation sequencing, the per-sample cost becomes increasingly affordable as sequencing platforms become more accessible and multiplexed. In large-scale applications, the high-throughput, reference-free, and automation-friendly nature of MNP genotyping offers practical advantages. Regarding accuracy, each MNP locus was sequenced to a mean depth > 500 ×, enabling single-nucleotide resolution and yielding > 99.80% accuracy. In contrast, SSR markers rely on length polymorphisms, which can be difficult to distinguish when fragment sizes differ by only a few base pairs, or when alleles of identical length differ in internal nucleotide composition—limitations that capillary electrophoresis may fail to resolve. Furthermore, PCR amplification of SSR regions is prone to polymerase slippage, resulting in stutter bands that complicate allele calling. With respect to SNPs, sequencing-based SNP genotyping—particularly for closely spaced SNP clusters in low-complexity or repetitive regions—can be more susceptible to miscalling due to sequencing noise or alignment artifacts. In comparison, MNP loci are carefully selected to avoid low-complexity or error-prone regions, and the use of deep sequencing ensures reliable genotype calls even in challenging genomic contexts. Array-based SNP chips are developed from carefully curated and validated loci and typically provide highly accurate genotypes; However, compared with MNP markers, SNP chips are fixed-content assays that are less flexible to redesign or update, making them less ideal for cultivar-specific applications such as variety protection and dispute resolution. These features collectively contribute to the high accuracy and reproducibility of the MNP-based identification system.

High polymorphism is a fundamental requirement for DNA markers used in cultivar discrimination. Compared to single-site SNP or SSR markers, MNP markers represent short haplotypes composed of multiple adjacent polymorphisms, providing higher allelic diversity and information content per locus. This contributes to their enhanced discriminatory power, particularly when distinguishing closely related cultivars. Previous studies indicated SSR markers exhibit superior polymorphism among various DNA markers and are frequently used in pear cultivar identification and genetic diversity studies. Transcriptome-derived SSR markers previously reported had an average allele number (Na) of 16.96 across 28 SSR markers, with an average PIC of 0.7483 [42]. Molecular characterization of Portuguese Pyrus communis landraces reported an average allele number of 11.3 per SSR locus, with PIC ranging from 0.675 to 0.876 [43]. Genetic polymorphism studies on 43 pear accessions cultivated in Belarus showed an average allele number of 12.8 per SSR locus and an average PIC of 0.81 [44]. Polymorphism evaluation of the MNP markers developed in this study showed an average allele number of 18.14 per locus with PIC values ranging from 0.57 to 0.99, comparable to previously reported SSR markers, confirming that MNP markers are highly informative and advantageous for pear cultivar identification.

Due to their widespread distribution across the genome and high density, SNP markers are commonly employed for population structure analysis, frequently appearing in pear population genetics studies [15]. However, the bi-allelic nature of SNPs inherently requires a large number of markers to ensure accuracy in population studies. Compared to the millions of SNP markers often required, MNP loci are highly informative, significantly reducing the required marker number. In this study, we used MNP markers to perform population structure analysis on 76 known pear cultivars. It was clear that even with only 310 markers, realistic population structure results were obtained, demonstrating the potential of MNP markers for population genetic analysis.

The MNP-based system demonstrated high overall accuracy in cultivar identification, its resolution was limited when applied to bud sport cultivars, as reflected by the high genetic similarity (> 98.71%) observed across all six bud sport pairs. This limitation is likely due to the extremely low level of genomic variation between bud sports and their original cultivars. To enhance discrimination in such cases, future efforts could focus on increasing marker density in genomic regions prone to somatic variation. Additionally, integrating MNP markers with complementary strategies may further improve the ability to differentiate closely related bud sport cultivars. It is worth noting that the current test panel comprises 76 pear cultivars, primarily representing widely cultivated and commercially important varieties in the market. While some cultivar groups are less represented, this panel provides a practical basis for evaluating the performance of the MNP markers. The results offer strong initial support for the method’s effectiveness, and future work incorporating more diverse and genetically related cultivars will further expand its applicability across broader germplasm resources. Among the 310 MNP markers, 287 markers had discriminatory power exceeding 80%. In 2,850 pairwise comparisons, 99.5% of cultivar pairs showed genetic similarity coefficients below 59.27%, and 97.54% had similarity coefficients lower than 30%. Considering mutations and degeneration occurring during cultivar breeding, we set the genetic similarity threshold for distinguishing different cultivars at 96% in this study. Analysis of genetic similarity coefficients among the 76 cultivars revealed that, excluding bud sports, similarity between different cultivars remained below 96%, with the highest observed similarity being 95.19%. Thus, a 96% similarity threshold effectively distinguished cultivars.

In summary, MNP markers hold broad potential for precise pear cultivar identification. With standardized industry management, gradual establishment of cultivar certification systems, and advances in molecular biology, the application potential of MNP markers will continue to expand and deepen. Additionally, in pear population genetics, MNP markers can naturally form informative haplotype blocks, thereby improving the resolution of population‐structure analyses and helping to detect selective forces that drive differentiation.

Conclusion

This study presents the first development and application of MNP markers in pear cultivars. A total of 310 MNP markers were developed from genomic resequencing data of 143 pear accessions, and subsequent DNA fingerprints were established for 76 representative pear cultivars. Statistical analyses and reproducibility tests demonstrated that the developed pear MNP markers exhibit high polymorphism, accuracy, and reproducibility. Genetic similarity validation of 76 cultivars using the 310 MNP markers confirmed the feasibility of these markers in pear cultivar identification. Additionally, population structure analysis performed on 76 cultivars revealed the potential of MNP markers in pear population genetics. Overall, this study facilitates rapid pear cultivar identification, mitigates the issue of cultivar homogeneity, and supports the enhancement of cultivar innovation.
