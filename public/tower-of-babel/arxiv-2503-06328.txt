Imperfect detectors for adversarial tasks with applications to quantum key distribution
Shlok Nahar, Norbert Lütkenhaus

Abstract
Security analyses in quantum key distribution (QKD) and other adversarial quantum tasks often assume perfect device models. However, real-world implementations often deviate from these models. Thus, it is important to develop security proofs that account for such deviations from ideality. In this work, we develop a general framework for analysing imperfect threshold detectors, treating uncharacterised device parameters such as dark counts and detection efficiencies as adversarially controlled within some ranges. This approach enables a rigorous worst-case analysis, ensuring security proofs remain valid under realistic conditions. Our results strengthen the connection between theoretical security and practical implementations by introducing a flexible framework for integrating detector imperfections into adversarial quantum protocols.



I
Introduction

Many analyses of quantum information (QI) processing tasks rely on exact models of the devices used, particularly in adversarial applications like quantum key distribution (QKD). However, as we near the practical implementation of QI protocols, it is of increasing importance to perform more refined analyses that take into account experimental imperfections. In particular, devices cannot be perfectly characterised, and thus the theoretical analysis must account for cases involving only partial models.
Moreover, for adversarial tasks such as QKD
bennett1984quantum
, entanglement verification
audenaert2006correlations
, and quantum-secure multiparty deep learning
sulimany2024quantum
, the adversary might even have some limited control over the devices. This further exacerbates the need for more refined theoretical analyses in the adversarial setting.

This has lead to a growing attention to addressing imperfections in quantum devices, particularly in the context of QKD; both for realistic sources
curras2023securityframework
;
arqand2024mutual
;
sixto2024quantum
;
zapatero2021security
;
curras2023security
, and for realistic detection setups
fung2008security
;
tupkary2024phaseerrorrateestimation
.
While this body of work constitutes a tremendous amount of progress towards implementation security of QKD protocols, it is primarily restricted to the entropic uncertainty relation (EUR)- and phase-error correction-based proofs. On the other hand, work
marwah2024proving
;
arqand2024mutual
addressing imperfections compatible with entropy accumulation theorem-based proofs either assume qubit sources
marwah2024proving
, or require bounds on quantities that cannot be easily related to physical device parameters
arqand2024mutual
. Thus, it is important to develop methods that are more broadly applicable; both across proof techniques within QKD as well as for applications outside QKD.

In this work we address the problem of imperfect detection setups with threshold detectors in a general framework, leaving the problem of imperfect sources for future work.
Our framework, introduced in
Section

III
, treats imperfectly characterised device parameters equivalently to untrusted device parameters by ‘giving’ the uncharacterised component to Eve. This allows us to consider a ‘worst-case’ scenario with exact device parameters that can then use existing theoretical analyses.
We emphasise that formally accomplishing this task is non-trivial as noticed in
(
li_improving_2020
, Section V.B.)
for the case of dark counts, and thus a rigorous analysis is needed.
Furthermore, our framework relies solely on the intrinsic properties of the detection setup, without making any assumptions about the specific protocol in which the detection setup might be employed. Thus, we expect this to apply to adversarial tasks, in general.

We describe the techniques that allow for such a rigorous analysis for the case of dark counts and detector loss in
Sections

IV.2.1
and
IV.2.2
respectively.
Importantly, our results are presented in terms of experimentally measurable parameters, including the maximum dark count rate and the range of potential detector loss values.
We also extend our analysis in
Section

IV.2.3
to a form (
Theorem

3
) that can be applied for arbitrary imperfections, though the application of this generic result to practical situations is not immediately clear. Finally, we detail the application of our results to the various proof techniques for QKD in
Section

V
.

II
Detector Model

In this section, we describe a commonly used model for threshold detectors, and setups that utilise these detectors.
Threshold detectors are devices that ideally ‘click’ in the presence of incident photons, and don’t click in the abscence of incident photons.
Deviations from ideality are termed dark counts, or detector efficiency as elaborated on in the following subsections.

II.1
Dark counts as classical post-processing

Dark counts are clicks that are registered by the detector in the absence of any incident photons. There are, broadly speaking, two mechanisms that explain this phenomenon.
Firstly, single-photon detectors sometimes have dark counts as a result of thermal noise, where thermally generated carriers or quantum tunneling of electrons can cause spurious detections
dao2024single
. This results in dark counts that are independent of any other click events. Secondly, after a click event, SPAD detectors sometimes have trapped charges that are released at a later time causing additional click events, commonly called afterpulsing
migdall2013single
.
In this work, we deal with the effect of dark counts that are independent of other click events, and each other.

The independent dark counts can be modelled as a classical post-processing being applied to the measurement outcomes. For example, consider a single threshold detector with dark count rate
d_{B}
having POVM
\{\Gamma^{d_{B}}_{\text{nc}},\Gamma^{d_{B}}_{{\text{c}}}\}
corresponding to click and no-click events respectively.
This can be written in terms of the POVM
\{\Gamma_{\text{nc}},\Gamma_{{\text{c}}}\}
of a threshold detector without dark counts as

\displaystyle\Gamma^{d_{B}}_{\text{nc}}

\displaystyle=(1-d_{B})\Gamma_{\text{nc}}

(1)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{d_{B}}_{{\text{c}}}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{{\text{c}}}+d_{B}\Gamma_{\text{nc}}.

Equation

1
can be more concisely written as

\displaystyle\vec{\Gamma}_{d_{B}}=\mathcal{P}^{d_{B}}\vec{\Gamma},

(2)

where
\mathcal{P}^{d_{B}}=\begin{pmatrix}1-d_{B}&0\\
d_{B}&1\end{pmatrix}

is a stochastic matrix that describes the classical post-processing, and
\vec{\Gamma}=\begin{pmatrix}\Gamma_{\text{nc}}\\
\Gamma_{\text{c}}\end{pmatrix}.

Similarly, for a more general detection setup with
k
threshold detectors, we can write the post-processing map acting on all
2^{k}
click patterns based on the dark count rates
{\mathbf{d_{B}}}=(d_{1},d_{2},\cdots,d_{k})
as
\mathcal{P}^{{\mathbf{d_{B}}}}
. While the exact structure of
\mathcal{P}^{{\mathbf{d_{B}}}}
is not important, we shall comment on the relevant properties of
\mathcal{P}^{{\mathbf{d_{B}}}}
where applicable.
In this work, we will refer to the

as

to represent the probability of outcome (alternately, click pattern)

II.2
Modeling detector efficiency

Detector efficiency refers to the fact that the detector does not always register a click when photons are incident on it. In this work, we assume that the detector efficiency is independent of prior detection events.
A typical quantum optical model for detection efficiency uses the single-photon detection efficiency
\eta
, which represents the probability of the detector registering a click for each incident photon.
This can also be represented as a beam splitter with transmission amplitude coefficient
\sqrt{\eta}
, followed by a lossless detector.

An important special case that we shall use in this work, is that of a lossy detector, with no dark counts with an input with
no more than a single photon
. This case might sound oddly specific, but its usage will become clearer in
Section

IV.2.2
. In this case, the loss can be represented as a classical post-processing analogous to
Eq.

2

\displaystyle\vec{\Gamma}_{\eta}=\mathcal{P}^{\eta}\vec{\Gamma},

(3)

where
\mathcal{P}^{\eta}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1-\eta\\
0&\eta\end{pmatrix}
, and
\vec{\Gamma}=\begin{pmatrix}\Gamma_{\text{nc}}\\
\Gamma_{\text{c}}\end{pmatrix}.
Similarly, for a more general detection setup with
k
threshold detectors, we can write the post-processing map
\mathcal{P}^{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}
acting on all
2^{k}
click patterns based on the loss of each detector
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}=(\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\cdots,\eta_{k})
. Note once again that this post-processing map is valid for detectors without dark counts, with an input with no more than a single photon.

We first give a brief, intuitive overview of the core idea of our framework, which is inspired by squashing maps

;

For an arbitrary detection setup with dark count rates

III.0.1
Does equivalence (ii) hold?

{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{sq}}
is the post-processing required for the specific squashing map we wish to use. For example, for an active BB84 detection setup, we can use Theorem 10 from Ref.
gittsovich_squashing_2014
to obtain the post-processing

0&1&0&1/2\\
0&0&1&1/2\end{pmatrix}

which maps double clicks in a particular basis to random single clicks in the same basis.

\mathcal{P}^{{\mathbf{d_{B}}}}
is the dark count post-processing for the detection setup. Thus, the equivalence amounts to asking if there exist stochastic matrices
{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{sq}}^{\prime}
,
{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{dc}}
such that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{sq}}^{\prime}\mathcal{P}^{{\mathbf{d_{B}}}}={\mathcal{P}}_{\text{dc}}{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{sq}}.

(4)

Note that
{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{sq}}^{\prime}
can be freely chosen by physically performing the appropriate classical post-processing on the measurement results. For simplicity, we choose
{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{sq}}^{\prime}={\mathcal{P}}_{\text{sq}}
in this work. We shall comment more on the post-processing
{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{sq}}
needed for the flag-state squasher and the WPFSS in
Section

V.1
.

This equivalence can be formally stated as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{dc}}\Tr[\vec{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}\rho]=\Tr[\vec{F}\ \Phi_{{\mathbf{d_{B}}},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}(\rho)],

IV.1
Flag-state squasher

The flag-state squasher

\displaystyle\Gamma_{i}=\Gamma_{i,0}\oplus\Gamma_{i,0<m\leq N}\oplus\Gamma_{i,m>N}.

Here,

acts on the space spanned by the vacuum state

The target measurements are given by

\displaystyle F_{i}=\Gamma_{i,0}\oplus\Gamma_{i,0<m\leq N}\oplus\outerproduct{i}{i},

where
\{\ket{i}\}_{i=0}^{{n_{\text{meas}}}-1}

\mathcal{H}_{F}

\mathcal{H}_{0}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{0<m\leq N}

Note that in adversarial applications, the idea is to ‘give’ the eavesdropper Eve the squashing map so that the analysis can then be restricted to the finite-dimensional POVM elements. However, in the case of the flag-state squasher the existence of the flags implies that there exists a classical state
1

IV.2.1
Noise channel for dark counts

Any detection setup has single-click events, defined as events where only a single detector clicks, and multi-click events, defined as events where more than one detector clicks.
We let
S
be the set of single-click events, and
M
be the set of multi-click events. The no-click event will always be denoted by the event

. The set of all events will be denoted by
U

We first list a set of sufficient conditions we require from the classical post-processing

in order to construct the noise channel. We then physically motivate these conditions.

1.

The post-processing does not turn one single-click event into another, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{{\mathbf{d_{B}}}}_{{s|s^{\prime}}}=0,

(13)

for all distinct
s
,
s^{\prime}\in S
.

2.

The post-processing does not turn any click event into a no-click event, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{{\mathbf{d_{B}}}}_{{0|i}}=0,

(14)

for all
i\neq 0
.

3.

The post-processing is such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{{\mathbf{d_{B}}}}_{{s|s}}\geq\mathcal{P}^{{\mathbf{d_{B}}}}_{{0|0}},

(15)

for all
s\in S
.

The first two conditions are true for any dark count post-processing as dark counts do not stop a detector from clicking. To see why the third condition is true, observe that
\mathcal{P}^{{\mathbf{d_{B}}}}_{{s|s}}=\prod_{i\neq s}(1-d_{i})
is the probability that none of the other detectors have a dark count. On the other hand,
\mathcal{P}^{{\mathbf{d_{B}}}}_{{0|0}}=\prod_{i}(1-d_{i})
is the probability that none of the detectors have a dark count. Since
(1-d_{s})\leq 1
, the claim follows.

We also need to make an additional assumption on the POVM without dark counts
\vec{\Gamma}_{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}
. Informally, the assumption is that we cannot have more clicks than photons. More formally, this can be expressed as

\displaystyle\Tr[\Gamma^{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}_{m}\rho_{1}]

\displaystyle=0

(16)

\displaystyle\Tr[\Gamma^{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}_{m}\outerproduct{\vac}{\vac}]

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\Tr[\Gamma^{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}_{s}\outerproduct{\vac}{\vac}]

\displaystyle=0,

for all
m\in M
,
s\in S
and any single-photon state
\rho_{1}
. This naturally holds for typical detection setups as dark counts are the only way single photons can result in multiple detectors clicking or vacuum can result in any detector clicking. Note that this immediately implies that
\Tr[\Gamma_{0}\outerproduct{\vac}{\vac}]=1
.

We combine all these properties to define a generic optical detection setup.

Definition 1

(Threshold detection setup with independent dark counts)
.

We define the POVM
\vec{\Gamma}_{{\mathbf{d_{B}}},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}
to be a
threshold detection setup with independent dark counts
if

1.

it is equivalent to a a POVM without dark counts
\vec{\Gamma}_{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}
followed by a dark count post-processing
\mathcal{P}^{{\mathbf{d_{B}}}}
as described in equivalence (i) of
Fig.

2
,

2.

the dark count post-processing satisfies
Eqs.

15
,
14
and
13
, and

3.

the POVM without dark counts
\vec{\Gamma}_{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}
satisfies
Eq.

16
.

We now have all the tools to construct the noise channel for dark counts.

Theorem 1

(Dark count noise channel)
.

Let
\vec{\Gamma}_{{\mathbf{d_{B}}},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}
be the POVM for a threshold detection setup with independent dark counts, where each
\Gamma^{{\mathbf{d_{B}}},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}_{i}=\Gamma_{i,0}^{{\mathbf{d_{B}}},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}\oplus\Gamma^{{\mathbf{d_{B}}},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}_{i,m=1}\oplus\Gamma^{{\mathbf{d_{B}}},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}_{i,m>1}
is block-diagonal with an associated Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{0}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{m>1}
corresponding to the total photon number. Then there exists a flag-state squashing map
\Lambda:\mathcal{H}_{0}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{m>1}\xrightarrow{}\mathcal{H}_{m\leq 1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{F}
, and a noise channel
\Phi_{{{\mathbf{d_{B}}}}}:\mathcal{H}_{0}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{F}\xrightarrow{}\mathcal{H}_{0}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{F}
such that
\Tr[\vec{\Gamma}_{{\mathbf{d_{B}}},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}\rho]=\Tr[\vec{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}\ \Phi_{{{\mathbf{d_{B}}}}}\left(\Lambda(\rho)\right)]
for all density matrices
\rho
. Here,

\displaystyle F^{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{i}=\Gamma^{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{i,m=0}\oplus\Gamma^{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{i,m=1}\oplus\outerproduct{i}{i},

where
\ket{i}
forms an orthonormal basis for the flag space
\mathcal{H}_{F}
. Moreover for any density matrix
\rho
,

\displaystyle\Tr[\Phi_{{{\mathbf{d_{B}}}}}(\rho)\Pi_{\leq 1}]=\mathcal{P}_{{0|0}}^{\mathbf{d_{B}}}\Tr[\rho\Pi_{\leq 1}],

(17)

where
\Pi_{\leq 1}
is the projection onto the space
\mathcal{H}_{0}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{1}
, and
\mathcal{P}^{{\mathbf{d_{B}}}}
is the stochastic matrix that models the dark counts.

The proof is given in
Appendix

B
.

Recall that equivalence (iii) in
Fig.

2
required the existence of a noise channel
\Phi_{{\mathbf{d_{B}}},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}
, and a bound on the weight outside the preserved subspace
W^{{\mathbf{d_{B}}},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}
after the application of the noise channel.

Theorem

1
is the first step towards equivalence (iii), showing that there exists a noise channel
\Phi_{{{\mathbf{d_{B}}}}}
for dark counts, and relating the weight outside the preserved subspace before and after the application of the dark count noise channel through
Eq.

17
—
W^{\mathbf{d_{B}}}=1-\mathcal{P}^{{\mathbf{d_{B}}}}_{{0|0}}(1-W)
. We now prove an analogous result for lossy detectors.

IV.2.2
Noise channel for loss

Theorem 2

(Loss noise channel)
.

Let
\vec{\Gamma}_{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}
be the POVM for a threshold detection setup with loss (and without dark counts), where for each POVM outcome
i
,
\Gamma^{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}_{i}=\Gamma^{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}_{i,m\leq 1}\oplus\Gamma^{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}_{i,m>1}
is block-diagonal with an associated Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{m\leq 1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{m>1}
corresponding to the total photon number. Let
\eta_{\min}
and
\eta_{\max}
be the minimum and maximum of all the detector efficiencies used in the detection setup. We can then define a family of noise channels
\Phi_{{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}}^{\eta^{*}}
and target POVMs
\vec{F}_{\eta^{*}}
parametrised by some parameter
\eta^{*}
as follows.
For any value of
\eta^{*}\in\left[\frac{\eta_{\min}}{1-(\eta_{\max}-\eta_{\min})},1\right]
,
there exists a flag-state squashing map
\Lambda:\mathcal{H}_{m\leq 1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{m>1}\xrightarrow{}\mathcal{H}_{m\leq 1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{F}
, and a noise channel
\Phi_{{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}}^{\eta^{*}}:\mathcal{H}_{m\leq 1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{F}\xrightarrow{}\mathcal{H}_{m\leq 1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{F}
such that
\Tr[\vec{\Gamma}_{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}\rho]=\Tr[\vec{F}_{\eta^{*}}\ \Phi_{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}^{\eta^{*}}\left(\Lambda(\rho)\right)]
for all density matrices
\rho
. Here,

\displaystyle F^{\eta^{*}}_{i}=\Gamma^{\eta^{*}}_{i,m\leq 1}\oplus\outerproduct{i}{i},

where
\ket{i}
forms an orthonormal basis for the flag space
\mathcal{H}_{F}
, and
\vec{\Gamma}_{\eta^{*}}
is the POVM with (common) efficiency
\eta^{*}
in all detectors.
Moreover for any density matrix
\rho
,

\displaystyle\Tr[\Phi_{{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}}^{\eta^{*}}(\rho)\Pi_{\leq 1}]=\frac{\eta_{\min}}{\eta^{*}}\Tr[\rho\Pi_{\leq 1}],

(18)

where
\Pi_{\leq 1}
is the projection onto the space
\mathcal{H}_{m\leq 1}
.

The proof is given in
Appendix

B
.

Note that
Theorem

2
leaves us with some freedom to choose how we define the ‘ideal’ POVM
\vec{F}_{\eta^{*}}
. Increasing the efficiency
\eta^{*}
of the ideal POVM decreases the weight
\frac{\eta_{\min}}{\eta^{*}}
of the state in the preserved subspace.
Additional work must be done to decide what the optimal choice would be. Intuitively, we would expect that the optimal choice would maximise the weight in the preserved subspace, as any part of the state outside the preserved subspace is entirely classical. For notational simplicity, we refer to the noise channel
\Phi_{{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}}^{\eta^{*}}
simply as
\Phi_{{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}}
.

Finally, combining
Theorems

1
and
2
gives us the noise channel
\Phi_{{\mathbf{d_{B}}},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}=\Phi_{{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}}\circ\Phi_{{{\mathbf{d_{B}}}}}
required for equivalence (iii). The above theorems additionally relate the weight outside the preserved subspace before and after the application of the dark count noise channel through
Eqs.

17
and
18
—
W^{{\mathbf{d_{B}}},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}=1-\mathcal{P}^{{\mathbf{d_{B}}}}_{{0|0}}\frac{\eta_{\min}}{\eta^{*}}(1-W)
.

Note that the usage of this theorem towards equivalence (iii) still requires a bound on the weight outside the preserved subspace
W
before the application of the noise channel. Although this bound can obtained independent of the dark count rates
{\mathbf{d_{B}}}
as shown in
(
li_improving_2020
, Appendix A)
and
(
kamin2024improved
, Corollary 4)
, an analogous (setup-independent) result independent of detector loss does not yet exist. Thus, this would need to be analysed individually for different detection setups, for instance by minimising the bounds obtained from
(
kamin2024improved
, Theorem 3)
over all allowed values of loss.

IV.2.3
Noise channel for generic setup imperfections

We will now state a theorem that can account for more generic detection setup imperfections, inspired by the proof of
Theorem

1
. At an intuitive level, the proof first decomposes the noisy POVM into an ideal POVM (with some probability
p
), and a non-ideal POVM (with probability
1-p
). Then, we ‘turn’ the non-ideal POVM into a flag through the noise channel, and are only left analysing the ideal POVM.
This intuitive idea is formalised in the following theorem.

Theorem 3

(Generic noise channel)
.

Let the flag-state squashed POVM
\vec{F}_{\text{noise}}
describing the actual imperfect detection setup be related to an ideal flag-state squashed POVM
\vec{F}_{\text{ideal}}
as

\displaystyle\vec{F}_{\text{noise}}-(1-q)\vec{F}_{\text{ideal}}\geq 0,

(19)

for some
q\in[0,1]
. Then there exists a noise channel
\Phi_{{\text{noise}}}
such that the following equations hold for any state
\rho
:

\displaystyle\Tr[\vec{F}_{\text{noise}}\ \rho]

\displaystyle=\Tr[\vec{F}_{\text{ideal}}\ \Phi_{{\text{noise}}}(\rho)]

(20)

\displaystyle\Tr[\Pi\rho]

\displaystyle=(1-q)\Tr[\Pi\Phi_{{\text{noise}}}(\rho)],

(21)

where
\Pi
is the projection onto the preserved subspace.

Unlike the other theorems, we leave the proof of this theorem in the main text as we believe the proof is simple to understand and instructive.

VI
Conclusion

In this work, we develop a general framework for rigorously addressing imperfections in detection setups. We accomplish this by constructing a channel that ‘gives’ the imperfection to the adversary Eve. The portion ‘given’ to Eve is formalised through a classical ‘tagged’ space as in the flag-state squasher
zhang_security_2021
. We have phrased our results generically to facilitate their application to a variety of adversarial tasks beyond the immediate use in QKD security proofs.
While our theoretical results can be stated generically (see
Theorem

3
), the usage of this theorem requires some notion of an ideal POVM
\vec{F}_{\text{ideal}}
, and the estimation of the deviation from ideality
q
as detailed in
Section

IV.2.3
.
Since deriving this data directly from experimentally measurable quantities is challenging, we use physically motivated models for threshold detectors that include dark counts and non-unit efficiencies.

Specifically, we assume that for all detectors used in the detection setup the dark count rates and efficiencies have specific values (possibly chosen by Eve) within some given range. We emphasise that such a range is typically obtained in characterisation experiments (for e.g. as directed in
(
chunnilall2016quantum
, Table 4.3)
). Thus, our specialised theorems for dark counts (
Theorem

1
) and loss (
Theorem

2
) addresses this practically relevant situation. The amount ‘given’ to Eve in this case depends on the maximum dark count rate and the difference in the maximum and minimum efficiency of all the detectors used in the detection setup.

We have also detailed the application of our work to a variety of QKD security proof techniques. Specifically, our results can be directly applied to EAT-based techniques via
(
kamin2024finite
, Theorem 5)
, and we describe the steps required to apply our results to the postselection technique. More work is needed to apply our techniques to EUR and phase-error based frameworks. One way to accomplish this task is by making the flag-state squasher compatible with these proof techniques. Indeed, this would constitute an alternate (to
tupkary2024phaseerrorrateestimation
) security proof in the presence of basis-dependent loss.

An interesting extension of our work would be to use our framework and construct noise channels (correlated over protocol rounds) for afterpulsing and detector dead times. Additionally, more work must be done to characterise the deviation from ideality
q
for arbitrary detection setups, for example, to incorporate imperfectly characterised beam-splitters.

Appendix A
Numerically checking the existence of a noise channel

In this appendix we describe a numeric method to check
Eqs.

4
and
5
for generic protocols with any squashing map, but for a specific value of the dark count rates
{\mathbf{d_{B}}}
and loss
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
.
Since it requires a specific value of the dark count rates
{\mathbf{d_{B}}}
and loss
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
(and although the analysis can be repeated a finite number of times for different values of
{\mathbf{d_{B}}}
,
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
), this method does not constitute a rigorous treatment of untrusted dark counts. However, it serves as a useful tool when searching for a possible post-processing that satisfies equation
Eq.

4
, and a possible noise channel satisfying
Eq.

5
.

A.1
Eq.

4
as a linear program

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{sq}}^{\prime}\mathcal{P}^{{\mathbf{d_{B}}}}={\mathcal{P}}_{\text{dc}}{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{sq}}

Under the simplifying assumption that
{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{sq}}^{\prime}={\mathcal{P}}_{\text{sq}}
, it is clear that this is just a linear constraint for the variable
{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{dc}}
. In addition to this constraint, we need to constrain it to be a stochastic matrix, i.e., all entries need to be positive and each row needs to sum to 1. As these are all linear constraints, this is a linear program (where we only need to find a feasible point).

A.2
Eq.

5
as a semidefinite program

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{dc}}\Tr[\vec{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}\rho]=\Tr[\vec{F}\ \Phi_{{\mathbf{d_{B}}},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}(\rho)]\quad\quad\forall\;\rho

Here
{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{dc}}
is obtained from
Section

A.1
.
This can be rephrased using the Choi isomorphism
J
of the noise channel
\Phi
as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{dc}}\Tr[\vec{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}\rho]=\Tr\left[\left(\rho^{\intercal}\otimes\vec{F}\right)J\right]\quad\quad\forall\;\rho.

(27)

It is sufficient to satisfy
Eq.

27
on a (finite) set of
\rho
that constitutes a basis for the space. This gives a finite set of linear constraints on the Choi map
J
.
Additionally, since
J
is the Choi representation of a channel, it must be postive semidefinite, and tracing over the second space must give the identity on the first space.
All of these are positive semidefinite or linear constraints and thus this constitutes a semidefinite program (where we only need to show there exists a feasible point).

As we are not using this as a rigorous proof technique — we only use it as an intuition building tool — we do not worry about numerical imprecision.
