Anaphylactic Shock Probably due to a Cross‐Reaction Between Cefuroxime and Piperacillin—Presentation of a Fatal Case, Diagnostic Considerations, and Literature Review
Authors: August Fiegl, Arndt Hartmann, Christian Führling, Niklas Carbon, Roland C. E. Francis, Andreas Wehrfritz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Although beta‐lactam antibiotics are widely used in intensive care, rare but potentially fatal anaphylactic reactions can occur due to cross‐reactions between penicillins and cephalosporins. We report the case of a 68‐year‐old man who suffered perioperative cardiac arrest following the administration of cefuroxime. During the postoperative period, the patient received ampicillin/sulbactam without any signs of hypersensitivity. Following a switch to piperacillin/tazobactam prior to a procedure, the patient suddenly developed cardiovascular arrest and died. The autopsy revealed no macroscopic cause of death, but immunohistochemistry showed mast cell degranulation (“starry sky pattern”) in the tissue, suggesting anaphylaxis. We present the clinical and histopathological findings of a case with severe allergic reactions to both cefuroxime and piperacillin/tazobactam, as a result of either cross‐reaction or isolated sensitization to both antibiotics. This case report highlights the potential risks of cross‐reactivity between beta‐lactam antibiotics and the importance of autopsy in ambiguous cases. Clinicians should be aware of cross‐reactivity or cosensitization to multiple beta‐lactams in patients with a beta‐lactam allergy and consider non–beta‐lactam alternatives in high‐risk cases.

1. Introduction

Antibiotics are among the most commonly prescribed drugs in intensive care medicine and are of major importance for critically ill patients with septic infections. The timely initiation of anti‐infective therapy is essential in critically ill patients, as delays in the administration of antibiotics are associated with less favorable outcomes. On the other hand, a significant proportion (42.6%) of severe cases of drug‐induced anaphylaxis are caused by β‐lactam antibiotics [1]. In developed countries, approximately 5%–15% of patients have a confirmed penicillin allergy [2, 3], whereas only 1.3%–1.7% are allergic to cephalosporins [2, 4]. Allergic cross‐reactions between cephalosporins and other beta‐lactam antibiotics (penicillins, monobactams, and carbapenems) can occur in rare cases. An evidence‐based study revealed that this cross‐reactivity depends mainly on similarities in the R1 side chain and, in certain cases, also on similarities in the core structure of the β‐lactam ring [5]. This article describes a lethal course of disease that may have been caused by anaphylactic shock, which is hypothesized to have occurred either as a result of cross‐reaction between cefuroxim and piperazillin or a cosensitization to both compounds. It also provides an overview of the published literature on similar allergic patterns.

2. Case Presentation

We report the case of a 68‐year‐old man with hereditary hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy (HOCM), who had recently been treated with the myosin inhibitor mavacamten. The patient also suffered from severe three‐vessel coronary artery disease, Type 2 diabetes, a lipid metabolism disorder, and obstructive sleep apnea syndrome (OSAS). He had undergone a gastrectomy several years ago due to adenocarcinoma. One year ago, he suffered an ischemic stroke in the left hemisphere with right‐sided hemiplegia, which had completely recovered. Years ago, he received an implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator (ICD) due to his existing HOCM. The patient had no history of prior allergic reactions. Due to increasing shortness of breath and declining physical capacity, he was scheduled for a septumectomy, aortic valve replacement, and coronary bypass surgery (Day 0, Figure 1). In accordance with the hospital protocol, cefuroxime was administered as standard perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis. Fifteen minutes after induction of general anesthesia and 10 min after the start of an intravenous infusion of cefuroxime, the patient suffered a sudden cardiac arrest with pulseless electrical activity (PEA). Urticaria developed, leading to suspicion of anaphylactic reaction triggered by cefuroxime. Resuscitation in accordance with guidelines was performed for 15 min, and an instable return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) was achieved. Due to the patient′s unstable circulation and high demand for noradrenaline, peripheral veno‐arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (va‐ECMO) was initiated. The cardiac surgery was cancelled, and the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for stabilization.

Blood samples were taken approximately 3 h after the event. The serum tryptase showed a significantly elevated level of 78.5 μg/L (reference range: < 11.4 μg/L). Twenty‐four hours later (Day 1), the baseline tryptase level had dropped to 14.9 μg/L. Due to rising inflammatory markers, an anti‐infective therapy with ampicillin/sulbactam was initiated in the ICU 1 day after resuscitation (Day 1), without any signs of an allergic reaction. Two days after the anaphylactic shock, the patient underwent the planned septumectomy (Morrow) and implantation of a biological aortic valve prosthesis, whereupon the va‐ECMO could be safely discontinued (Day 2).

Due to a delayed recovery from anesthesia following surgery, the patient underwent a cranial CT scan, which detected borderline infarcts on both sides. The neurology team diagnosed the need for early neurological rehabilitation. Due to persistent dysphagia, a percutaneous endoscopic jejunostomy (PEJ) was indicated to ensure adequate enteral nutrition. Due to rising inflammatory markers during ampicillin/sulbactam treatment and at the request of the gastroenterologists, a single dose of piperacillin/tazobactam was administered prior to the PEJ insertion on Day 14. The patient received this antibiotic for the first time immediately prior to the planned PEJ procedure. Ten minutes after the start of the procedure and 40 min after administration of the antibiotic, the patient developed PEA during gastric insufflation. Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) confirmed that the heart had almost completely arrested. After extensive diagnostic measures (echocardiogram, ultrasound, chest X‐ray, blood gas analysis, and review of medical records), no reversible cause could be identified. After 1 h of guideline‐compliant cardiopulmonary resuscitation, including the intravenous administration of a total of 12,000 μg of epinephrine, resuscitation was terminated by interdisciplinary consensus. Unfortunately, no serum tryptase level was measured at the point of this second event, and IgE levels were not determined at any point. No serious, documented arrhythmias occurred during any of the anesthesia inductions or in the ICU. During the events, there was an immediate transition from sinus rhythm to PEA. A 12‐lead ECG performed in the ICU revealed no signs of ischemia, and the PQ interval was always within the normal range. In the TEE examinations performed after the first event, there was no evidence of left ventricular strain suggestive of an embolic event. The ejection fraction (EF), which was reduced due to the HOCM, remained at the same level as before the first event. In both cases of cardiac arrest followed by resuscitation, hypoxia, hypothermia, low/high potassium levels, pericardial tamponade, tension pneumothorax, intoxication, and thromboembolism were systematically ruled out. No obvious cause for cardiac arrest could be identified. This was also confirmed in the autopsy that followed.

The postmortem autopsy was performed 1 day after the patient′s death. Macroscopically, both lungs showed signs of congestion (left: 1073 g, right: 878 g) with a foam‐like cut surface. Preparation of pulmonary arteries and veins revealed no evidence of emboli. The heart showed severe hypertrophy (840 g), consistent with HOCM. All three bypasses were carefully prepared to rule out hypoperfusion of the coronary bypasses with subsequent myocardial infarction; however, no anastomotic leaks or thrombi were found. The myocardium had a dark‐brown cut surface without areas of demarcation. Due to the absence of any obvious macroscopic cause of death, it was not possible to make a definitive diagnosis immediately following the postmortem examination. Conventional histology using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) revealed hyperemia of the alveolar capillaries, intra‐alveolar oedema, and mast cells (Figure 2A). The tissue removed during the myectomy was examined and compared with the heart tissue samples from the autopsy, providing a dynamic quantification of inflammatory cells before and after the possible, but unverified, fatal anaphylactic shock. On H&E preparation, the postmortem myocardial and liver samples demonstrated scattered inflammatory cells, including mast cells (Figure 2B,C). To increase sensitivity of mast cell detection CD117 (c‐KIT), immunohistochemistry was performed (Clone EP10, Roche Diagnostics) revealing 22 versus 8 mast cells per 10 HPF in postmortem versus antemortem myocardial samples, respectively. Multiple mast cells were also observed in the pulmonary interstitium and alveoli (Figure 2D). As there are no standardized cutoff values for the relative number of mast cells for the diagnosis of anaphylaxis, an immunohistochemical analysis for mast cell tryptase (MCT, clone AA1, Abcam) was performed. MCT staining revealed antitryptase particles in the cytoplasm and extracellular region of mast cells in postmortem myocardium and liver (Figure 2E,F), but not in antemortem samples. This immunohistochemical characteristic has been described as the “starry sky pattern” of mast cell degranulation and is a strong indicator of acute anaphylaxis [6, 7]. Based on the overall clinical picture and the immunohistological findings, we hypothesize that anaphylactic shock was the most probable cause of the patient′s death; however, this could not be conclusively proven due to a lack of dynamic tryptase levels before and after the patient′s death.

3. Discussion

Penicillins and cephalosporins belong to the class of beta‐lactams, which are the most commonly prescribed antibiotics and are effective against a wide range of infections due to their extensive spectrum of activity. On the other hand, beta‐lactam antibiotics are the most common cause of drug hypersensitivity. In the United States, approximately 10% of the population reports suffering from a penicillin allergy. However, in most of these patients, this reported allergy has never been investigated or confirmed. Epidemiological studies suggest that more than 90% of patients with a diagnosed penicillin allergy tolerate penicillins without clinically significant hypersensitivity [2, 8]. Confirmed IgE‐mediated allergic reactions to penicillins are therefore far less common than those suspected on the basis of anamnestic data. In a cohort study conducted by Caimmi et al., 650 patients with a β‐lactam allergy underwent comprehensive allergy testing to investigate the safety of cefuroxime as an alternative to penicillin. Of these, 143 patients showed sensitization to at least one other beta‐lactam antibiotic than cefuroxime, eight patients (5.6%) were sensitized to cephalosporins, and 17 (11.9%) to both penicillins and cephalosporins. Nine patients (6.3%) were sensitized to cefuroxime, and one patient (0.7%) experienced anaphylactic shock during the provocation test [9]. Although hypersensitivity to cephalosporins is generally rare, the prevalence of cefuroxime hypersensitivity in patients with sensitization to beta‐lactams was 6.3% in this study, which requires caution when selecting other beta‐lactams as alternatives. The choice of alternatives to beta‐lactam antibiotics following a confirmed hypersensitivity, however, is often limited by toxicity, the need for a broad spectrum of activity, and potential interactions with other medications, particularly in critically ill patients.

The patient described in this case report developed a severe anaphylactic reaction to cefuroxime for the first time during preoperative preparation. As expected, however, he showed no signs of hypersensitivity to ampicillin/sulbactam, which was administered over several days at standard intervals and doses. Due to again‐increasing signs of inflammation and in preparation for PEJ placement, and given the observed tolerance to ampicillin/sulbactam, there were no safety concerns regarding a change to piperacillin, which has a different R1 side chain than ampicillin/sulbactam (Figure 3). An evidence‐based study revealed that this cross‐reactivity depends on similarities in the core structure of the β‐lactam ring and, in certain cases, also on similarities in the side chains [5]. Several studies have demonstrated that identities or similarities of β‐lactam side‐chain structures are mainly responsible for cross‐reactivity among these antibiotics [10].

Chemical structures of the applied beta lactams cefuroxime, piperacillin, and ampicillin. Note the structural similarity of the carbon–nitrogen bond in all three antibiotics and the identical stereochemical conformation (dashed lines between C–NH, indicating projection into the paper) between cefuroxime and piperacillin/tazobactam as a possible motif for cross‐reaction.

In retrospect, the postmortem findings suggest that the patient may have suffered anaphylactic shock, which could either be attributed to an allergic reaction upon independent sensitization to both antibiotics or to a cross‐reaction between cefuroxime and piperacillin/tazobactam. Although further supportive allergy testing (skin testing, specific IgE testing, and immunologic inhibition studies such as RAST inhibition assays) was needed to distinguish these causes of anaphylaxis, these tests were unavailable in the present case. Therefore, both mechanisms are, in principle, possible. In favor of cross‐reaction between piperacillin/tazobactam and cefuroxim, the patient developed presumably severe anaphylaxis, without documented prior exposure to this antibiotic. Although more compelling evidence is needed to support this hypothesis, the clinical sequence and history are compatible with anaphylaxis arising due to cross‐reaction. Aside from the clinical course, immunologic inhibition studies, which investigate whether specific IgE can recognize both substances involved (e.g., IgE inhibition assays, ELISA inhibition experiments, and RAST inhibition assays inhibition assays), would provide stronger evidence to distinguish allergic cross‐reactions from cosensitization; however, these tests are not standardized and are mainly performed in a research setting, rather than in the clinic. Pharmacologically, antibiotics achieve their bactericidal effect by adopting a 3D shape, or conformation, that closely resembles the D‐Ala‐D‐Ala‐peptide segment used in bacterial peptidoglycan cross‐linking. Due to this stereochemical similarity, the antibiotics can fit into the active sites of penicillin binding proteins, forming a stable covalent complex that permanently halts cell wall synthesis. Compelling evidence in recent years suggests that the R1 side chain plays a key role in the cross‐reactivity of beta‐lactams. Furthermore, stereochemical similarity between two compounds can trigger or exacerbate allergic cross‐reactivity [11, 12]. The identical stereochemical conformation of the transitional binding between the R1 chain and the beta‐lactam ring (Figure 3) could, in theory, represent a potential molecular target for a cross‐reaction in this case [13], although this certainly represents a hypothesis that needs stronger biochemical evidence. The “starry sky pattern” of tryptase release from mast cells observed in the postmortem histological samples, as well as the timing between administration and the onset of shock, could suggest that anaphylaxis was the cause of death. Although anaphylaxis cannot be completely excluded in the context of systemic mastocytosis (SM), this seems unlikely, as the patient′s liver and spleen volumes were within the normal range and the patient showed no clinical signs of SM, such as itching, rash, gastrointestinal symptoms, or osteoporosis. The decrease in the tryptase level following the first anaphylactic reaction also argues against SM. Mastocytosis is a relatively rare condition characterized by accumulations of mast cells in the skin or internal organs. In patients with SM, the tryptase concentration is usually consistently above 20 μg/L and can often reach even higher levels [14]. The expected drop in tryptase concentration to 14.9 μg/L within 24 h after the initial event therefore argues against this diagnosis in this case. Certainly, further measurements would have been required to demonstrate that the tryptase level had fallen below the baseline value of 12 μg/L. Concentrations between 12 and 20 μg/L, however, indicate an excessive accumulation of mast cells, although this does not necessarily imply underlying mastocytosis, but rather a known history of systemic Type I allergy. Another ancillary study for the diagnosis of SM is the detection of c‐KIT (CD117) point mutation in bone marrow, peripheral blood, or extracutaneous tissue, which was not performed as SM was not suspected clinically. We also consider the possibility of an anaphylactic reaction to other intraoperative agents, such as rocuronium, to be unlikely, as the same anesthetics were used during anesthesia for the second surgery, which was uneventful. To definitively confirm an allergy to cefuroxime or piperacillin/tazobactam, testing for IgE antibodies to both antibiotics would have been the most effective approach.

From a pathological perspective, diagnosing fatal anaphylaxis can be a major challenge, as macroscopically detectable organ changes are nonspecific and often absent. Reported macroscopic findings include petechial hemorrhages of the conjunctiva, pulmonary congestion, edema of the glottis, or glassy bronchial mucus [15]. However, even when these findings are present, they are often subtle and too nonspecific to allow for a definitive diagnosis of anaphylactic shock based solely on macroscopic examination. Therefore, a typical clinical history, along with laboratory findings such as serum tryptase levels, combined with additional diagnostic tools, can be extremely valuable in diagnosing anaphylaxis. Although not confirmative for an allergic reaction, the detection of the “starry sky pattern” of mast cell degranulation via MCT immunohistochemistry in conjunction with typical clinical presentation can support the diagnosis of anaphylaxis; we therefore advocate for the use of MCT in autopsies when anaphylaxis is suspected [7].

From a pathophysiological perspective, the insufflation of the stomach during the gastroscopic procedure reduced venous return due to increased intra‐abdominal and intrathoracic pressure, which further exacerbated the hypotension caused by the suspected anaphylaxis. In the days prior to the gastroscopy, attempts had been made to achieve a negative fluid balance in the patient, as he had retained significant fluid in the preceding days and the heart needed to be relieved. Due to this reduced venous return, in addition to the muscular hypertrophy of the left ventricle caused by long‐standing HOCM, we assume that cardiac obstruction was exacerbated in the patient and global pump failure occurred. This required resuscitation for PEA, and epinephrine was administered regularly. The administration of epinephrine is, on the one hand, the treatment of choice for PEA and, on the other hand, the first‐line treatment for anaphylactic shock. Despite resuscitation in accordance with guidelines and the administration of epinephrine, it was never possible to attain ROSC.

In retrospect, the decision to use piperacillin/tazobactam was reconsidered. Due to increasing systemic inflammation while on ampicillin/sulbactam and the need to prevent bacterial spread during the PEJ procedure, changing to a broader spectrum antibiotic was considered appropriate. The administration of piperacillin/tazobactam was considered safe in accordance with current guidelines, partly because the patient tolerated ampicillin/sulbactam well. For future clinical use, it should also be noted that when administering antibiotics, consideration must be given not only to the R1 group but also to other components, such as the transitional bindings or the R2 chain of the antibiotics. This is intended to ensure even safer administration of antibiotics in view of potential cross‐reactivity. As a practical recommendation, following a documented case of anaphylactic shock, we would consider the use of alternative non–beta‐lactam antibiotics. On the other hand, however, it must be considered that these substances are usually broad‐spectrum antibiotics. While skin tests are a key component of allergy diagnosis, particularly for environmental allergies, it is questionable whether they are suitable for detecting cross‐reactions in critically ill patients. Furthermore, they have a low positive predictive value of 40%–60% [16, 17]. Although several studies recommend drug challenge tests over skin tests, these tests are resource‐intensive, time‐consuming, and often impractical in intensive care. Furthermore, intensive care patients frequently receive multiple medications, such as corticosteroids, H₂‐blockers, and catecholamines, which can interfere with the accuracy of drug challenge tests. Additionally, intensive care patients also receive other drugs that could cause an allergic reaction such as dipyrone, or rocuronium, which means that, in cases of doubt, a very large number of potential substances would have to be tested. These tests require specialized personnel, who are not usually available in intensive care settings.

Finally, it must be emphasized that in this case report, cefuroxime was never definitively identified as the causative agent of what were presumably two episodes of anaphylactic shock, which constitutes a limitation of this case report. The patient tolerated the first administration in 2012 without symptoms; this is where sensitization may have occurred. According to the recommendations of Christiansen et al., a provocation test should have been performed in the perioperative setting after the first event in 2024 [14]. However, the patient was administered an H1‐blocker and cortisone immediately following the incident. A minimum interval of up to 4 days following discontinuation of the medication would need to be observed prior to provocation testing. Furthermore, it is questionable whether one would wish to carry out this testing during the convalescence phase in an intensive care patient, or whether testing is recommended following rehabilitation. As mentioned above, skin tests are less reliable in cases of cefuroxime allergy.

Although confirmatory allergy tests are often unavailable in a critical care setting, as in this case, this report suggests that allergy upon co‐sensitization or cross‐reaction may occur after administration of different classes of beta‐lactam antibiotics (cephalosporins and penicillins), even if patients have previously tolerated other agents within a specific class well. It is often difficult to definitively attribute such reactions to an allergic process, as many pathophysiological mechanisms may be involved. This makes a retrospective clinical‐pathological investigation all the more helpful. In high‐risk situations, physicians should be especially vigilant and, when in doubt, consider alternative antibiotics outside the beta‐lactam group to reduce the risk of unexpected hypersensitivity reactions.
