CascadeMind at SemEval-2026 Task 4: A Hybrid Neuro-Symbolic Cascade for Narrative Similarity
Sebastien Kawada Dylan Holyoak

Abstract
We present a hybrid neuro-symbolic system for the SemEval-2026 Task 4 on Narrative Story Similarity. Our approach combines neural self-consistency voting with a novel Multi-Scale Narrative Analysis Ensemble that operates as a symbolic tiebreaker. The neural network component uses a large language model with multiple parallel votes, applying a supermajority threshold for confident decisions and escalating uncertain cases to additional voting rounds. When votes result in a perfect tie, a symbolic ensemble combining five narrative similarity signals (lexical overlap, semantic embeddings, story grammar structure, event chain alignment, and narrative tension curves) provides the final decision. Our cascade architecture achieves 81% accuracy on the development set, demonstrating that selective deferral to symbolic methods can enhance neural predictions on genuinely ambiguous narrative comparisons.



1
Introduction

Narrative similarity, the task of determining whether two stories share meaningful commonality, is a fundamental challenge in computational narratology
(
Piper 2021
)
. SemEval-2026 Task 4 uses the comparative judgment paradigm: given an anchor story and two candidates, systems must determine which candidate is more narratively similar to the anchor. Beyond surface-level text similarity, this requires understanding deeper storytelling aspects: the
abstract theme
(ideas and motives),
course of action
(sequence of events), and
outcomes
(results and resolutions)
(
Hatzel and Biemann 2024
)
. This task design reflects how humans naturally compare stories and has applications in narrative recommendation, plagiarism detection, and literary analysis.

While large language models (LLMs) excel at semantic understanding, previous research also demonstrate that LLMs can struggle on genuinely ambiguous cases where multiple valid interpretations exist
Keluskar et al. 2024
. To address this challenge, we take inspiration from human decision making where different reasoning strategies are applied depending on the task difficulty and available knowledge as opposed to treating all cases uniformly
Kahneman 2011
. We developed a hybrid neuro-symbolic model, called
CascadeMind
, by combining combining LLMs and rule-based symbolic reasoning. The CascadeMind model (1) uses self-consistency voting algorithm
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
to assess uncertainty in LLM decisions, (2) employs a supermajority threshold to identify confident vs. uncertain predictions, and (3) defers the most uncertain decisions (e.g., perfect tie) to a symbolic ensemble module based on narrative theory for further analysis.

Our contributions are:

•

A cascade architecture combining neural self-consistency with rule-based symbolic reasoning

•

A Multi-Scale Narrative Analysis Ensemble incorporating five signals from different levels of narrative abstraction which give auxiliary reasoning to the most ambiguous cases

•

Empirical analysis showing that cascade design provides more value than the symbolic component alone

2
Related Work

Self-Consistency Decoding

Self-consistency has roots in ensemble learning, where aggregating multiple hypotheses improves predictions
Hansen and Salamon 1990
.
Wang et al. 2023
introduced self-consistency as a decoding strategy that samples multiple reasoning paths and selects the most consistent answer through majority voting, showing that diverse reasoning traces yield significantly higher accuracy than single samples. This approach has been extended to medical question answering
(
Maharjan et al. 2024
)
and code generation
(
Huang et al. 2024a
)
, while recent work on mirror-consistency addresses overconfidence in minority responses
(
Huang et al. 2024b
)
.

Narrative Representation Learning

Computational approaches to narrative understanding have evolved from rule-based story grammars
(
Propp 1968
)
to neural network embedding representations.
Hatzel and Biemann 2024
introduced story embeddings that capture narrative similarity by training on reformulations of the same story. This work is directly relevant to our task as it comes from the task organizers. The CoRRPUS framework
(
Dong et al. 2023
)
demonstrated that neurosymbolic approaches can outperform pure neural methods on story understanding tasks by leveraging structured representations.

Ensemble Methods and Selective Prediction

Dietterich 2000
shows that ensembles can classify data points by weighted voting of predictions. Selective prediction allows models to abstain when uncertain, trading coverage for accuracy
(
Geifman and El-Yaniv 2017
)
. Our cascade architecture implements a form of selective prediction where uncertain neural predictions are escalated to symbolic reasoning rather than abstained entirely.

Narrative Theory

Our symbolic component draws on classical narrative theory. Propp’s morphology of the folktale
(
Propp 1968
)
identified recurring structural elements across stories. Freytag’s pyramid models narrative tension through exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, and resolution
(
Freytag 1894
)
. Todorov’s equilibrium theory
(
Todorov 1969
)
describes narratives as movements between stable states. We operationalize these theories as computable similarity signals.

3.1
Neural Self-Consistency Voting

We use Gemini 2.5 Flash as our base LLM, leveraging its native
candidateCount
parameter to generate 8 parallel responses per API call. Each response provides a binary decision (A or B) for which story is more similar to the anchor.

Decision Logic

Let
V=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{8}\}
be the set of votes where
v_{i}\in\{A,B\}
. We define the vote count
c_{X}=|\{v_{i}:v_{i}=X\}|
for
X\in\{A,B\}
.

Supermajority:
If
\max(c_{A},c_{B})\geq 7
, we return the majority decision immediately. This threshold (87.5%) indicates high model confidence.

Escalation:
If votes are split (5-3, 6-2, or 4-4), we request three additional independent API calls, each using the models
candidateCount=8
setting. Votes from all calls are compiled, totaling 32 votes. The majority across all votes determines the decision, unless a perfect tie (16-16 votes) occurs.

Signal 1: Lexical Similarity (TF-IDF)

We compute TF-IDF
Salton and Buckley 1988
vectors, which represent narratives as numerical feature vectors based on word usage and importance. For each problem, we compute TF-IDF vectors for anchor narrative and the two option narratives, and then measure cosine similarity between the anchor and option narratives:

s_{\text{lex}}^{X}=\cos(\text{tfidf}(\text{anchor}),\text{tfidf}(X))

(1)

This TF-IDF–based lexical similarity captures surface-level overlap in word usage, reflecting shared situations, characters, or domain-specific terminology.

Signal 2: Semantic Similarity

Using the all-MiniLM-L6-v2 sentence transformer that encodes narratives into dense vector embeddings
(
Reimers and Gurevych 2019
)
, we compute pairwise cosine similarity between the resulting embeddings:

s_{\text{sem}}^{X}=\cos(\text{enc}(\text{anchor}),\text{enc}(X))

(2)

This measure captures overall semantic similarity in the narrative content.

Signal 3: Story Grammar Similarity

Inspired by Propp
Propp 1968
and Todorov
Todorov 1969
, we segment each story into five narrative phases based on position: setting (first 20%), conflict (20-40%), rising action (40-60%), climax (60-80%), and resolution (80-100%). We compute sentence-transformer embeddings for each aligned phase, then compute story-grammar similarity via cosine similarity:

s_{\text{grammar}}^{X}=\frac{1}{5}\sum_{p\in P}\cos(\text{enc}(a_{p}),\text{enc}(x_{p}))

(3)

where
P
= {setting, conflict, rising, climax, resolution}.

Signal 4: Narrative Tension Curve

Following Freytag’s pyramid
Freytag 1894
, we model narrative tension as a function of position. For each sentence, we compute tension as the sum of sentiment intensity (absolute polarity) and subjectivity using TextBlob
Kaewyong et al. 2021
. We interpolate sentence tension to a fixed-length curve of 10 points via linear interpolation, and then compute correlation between anchor and option narratives:

s_{\text{tension}}^{X}=\text{corr}(T_{\text{anchor}},T_{X})

(4)

This captures similarity in emotional dynamics and pacing.

Signal 5: Event Chain Similarity

We extract action verbs using spaCy’s part-of-speech tagger, filtering to verbs matching a list of 47 narrative action words derived from Propp’s character functions
(
Propp 1968
)
and frequent plot-driving verbs in the CMU corpus (e.g.,
discover
,
fight
,
escape
,
transform
). These form event sequences
E_{A}
and
E_{X}
(ordered verb lemmas). We compute the longest common subsequence (LCS):

s_{\text{event}}^{X}=\frac{2\cdot\text{LCS}(E_{A},E_{X})}{|E_{A}|+|E_{X}|}

(5)

This captures plot structure similarity independent of specific wording.

Weighted Ensemble

The final pairwise similarity is determined using a weighted sum of the above five similarity scores:

\text{score}^{X}=\sum_{i=1}^{5}w_{i}\cdot s_{i}^{X}

(6)

The model returns option A if
\text{score}^{A}>\text{score}^{B}
, otherwise option B.

We estimated ensemble weights
w_{i}
using differential evolution optimization algorithm
(
Storn and Price 1997
)
on the official SemEval-2026 Task 4 training set comprising 1,900 narrative triplets. The training data provides ground-truth answer labels based on expert annotation, with pairs curated to share explicit narrative elements (same genre, similar plot structures, matching character archetypes) against stories that differ on these dimensions.

The optimization objective minimizes classification error:

\mathbf{w}^{*}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{w}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\Big[\text{sign}\big(\textstyle\sum_{i}w_{i}\Delta s_{i}^{j}\big)\neq y_{j}\Big]

(7)

where
\Delta s_{i}^{j}=s_{i}^{A}-s_{i}^{B}
and
y_{j}\in\{-1,+1\}
is the ground truth label. We use differential evolution due to its effectiveness on non-convex, non-differentiable objectives.

4
Experiments

4.1
Dataset

We evaluate on the SemEval-2026 Task 4 Track A development set. The dataset was created by the task organizers from CMU Movie Summary Corpus
(
Bamman et al. 2013
)
, which contains plot summaries extracted from Wikipedia for 42,306 movies. From this corpus, the organizers curated 200 story triplets for the development set, where each triplet contains an anchor story and two candidate stories. Ground truth labels were determined through expert annotation based on narrative similarity judgments, indicating which candidate is more narratively similar to the anchor across dimensions of theme, plot structure, and character dynamics. The training set comprises 1,900 triplets from the same source. Stories span multiple genres including drama, comedy, thriller, and romance, with lengths ranging from 100 to 2,000 words.

4.2
Implementation Details

We use Gemini 2.5 Flash via the Google AI API with
candidateCount=8
for parallel voting. Temperature is set to 1.0 to encourage response diversity. All other parameters remained set at default. The symbolic ensemble module uses scikit-learn for TF-IDF
Kaewyong et al. 2021
, sentence-transformers (all-MiniLM-L6-v2) for embeddings
Reimers and Gurevych 2019
, and TextBlob
Kaewyong et al. 2021
for sentiment analysis.

4.3.2
Symbolic Tiebreaker Analysis

The symbolic tiebreaker achieves 61% accuracy on perfect ties (n=18), an 11% improvement over random chance. Of the 7 incorrect cases, 3 (43%) failed due to misleading lexical overlap where stories shared domain vocabulary without narrative similarity, 2 (29%) involved non-standard narrative structures that violated our positional segmentation assumptions, and 2 (29%) were edge cases where the correct answer required world knowledge beyond surface signals. When applied to all cases regardless of neural confidence, accuracy drops to 53%, confirming the symbolic signals are specifically effective for high-uncertainty decisions.

5
Discussion

Outperforming State-of-the-Art LLMs

Our 10.5% improvement over Gemini 3 Flash demonstrates that cascade architectures with self-consistency voting can unlock performance gains beyond what single model calls achieve. The improvement is not due to using a more powerful model, but rather from leveraging uncertainty through vote aggregation and selective escalation. This suggests that for complex reasoning tasks like narrative similarity,
how
we query LLMs matters as much as
which
LLM we use.

Value of Cascade Design

The cascade architecture’s primary performance gains arise from the voting and escalation mechanism rather than the symbolic tiebreaker. The supermajority threshold effectively identifies confident predictions (85% accuracy on 74% of cases), while escalation provides additional signal for borderline cases. The key insight is that LLM vote distribution serves as a reliable uncertainty estimator: higher consensus correlates with higher accuracy.

Domain Mismatch

The symbolic ensemble achieves 99.5% accuracy on the training data but only 61% on development set ties. This gap suggests that training set pairs, while useful for optimizing the ensemble, may have different characteristics than the harder cases that reach the tiebreaker stage during inference.

Signal Complementarity

Optimized weights favor lexical (TF-IDF, 49%) and structural (Story Grammar, 40%) signals over semantic embeddings (8%), suggesting narrative similarity operates primarily at surface and structural levels. However, the non-zero semantic weight indicates a minor but distinct contribution to ensemble predictions.

Measure Limitations

The minimal weight for event chain similarity (1%) likely reflects sparsity caused by exact verb matching against a finite 47-word list. This highlights a need for richer event representations via fuzzy matching or semantic role labeling, rather than a lack of theoretical importance

6
Conclusion

We presented a hybrid neuro-symbolic cascade model for judging narrative story similarity that combines neural self-consistency voting with a Multi-Scale Narrative Analysis Ensemble. Our system achieves 81% accuracy on the SemEval-2026 Task 4 development set through a cascade design that routes confident predictions immediately while escalating uncertain cases.

The main contributions are: (1) a cascade architecture demonstrating that LLM vote distribution serves as a reliable uncertainty estimator, with supermajority predictions achieving 85% accuracy; (2) a Multi-Scale Narrative Analysis Ensemble that combines five theory-grounded similarity signals; and (3) empirical analysis showing that cascade design contributes more than the symbolic component on this task.

Our results suggest that narrative similarity remains challenging for current methods, with even 32-vote ensembles achieving low accuracy on uncertain cases. Future work should explore training symbolic components on in-domain data and incorporating narrative-specific embeddings.
