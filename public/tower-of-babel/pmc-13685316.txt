Prevalence of intestinal parasites in pre-school and school-aged Iranian children: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Hassan Taherahmadi, Nasim Rahmani, Sayed Hussain Mosawi, Zahra Gharibi, Faezeh Mohammadi, Ali Asghari, Morteza Habibi, Aida Vafae Eslahi, Milad Badri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntestinal parasitic infections (IPIs) are a major public health challenge for children in low- and middle-income countries, linked to malnutrition, stunting, and cognitive deficits. This meta-analysis estimated IPIs prevalence in Iranian preschool and school-aged children and identified associated factors.MethodsA systematic literature search was performed across five international bibliographic databases (Scopus, PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and Google Scholar) to identify articles published between January 2000 and May 2025. The study protocol was in accordance with the reporting guidelines for the Meta-analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (MOOSE). Subsequently, 36 studies met the eligibility criteria and were incorporated into the present meta-analysis. These studies in total accounted for a total sample size of 48,930 individuals, including 3,595 school-aged children in Iran with confirmed infections.ResultsOverall IPIs prevalence was 0.081 (95% CI 0.058–0.113). Among protozoa, the highest prevalence was related to Giardia lamblia (0.080, 95% CI: 0.056–0.112) and the most prevalent helminths, was Enterobius vermicularis (0.031, 95% CI: 0.020–0.048). Preschool children showed the highest rate of infection (0.130, 95% CI: 0.070–0.210).ConclusionTo reduce intestinal parasitic infections in Iranian children, regular and periodic screening of pre-school and school-aged children is recommended, particularly in humid regions where prevalence is highest. Health systems within schools must be evaluated and strengthened, including proper sanitation facilities, handwashing infrastructure, and hygiene education. Additionally, school-based deworming programs, environmental measures such as reducing soil contamination in playgrounds, and community health campaigns discouraging geophagy are essential to interrupt transmission cycles and prevent long-term complications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28706-1.

Background

Intestinal parasitic infections (IPIs) are among the most common and neglected of the world’s infectious disease burdens and occur in areas where individuals have little access to safe water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) [1, 2]. Worldwide, more than a thousand million people are infected with soil-transmitted helminths (STHs), the areas of most intense transmission lying in the tropical and sub-tropical belts where conditions are optimal for survival and reproduction of the parasites [3, 4]. The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) studies consistently identify IPIs among the top contributors to years lived with disability (YLDs) in low-and middle-income countries, demonstrating the enduring public health importance [5, 6]. The problem falls heavily on preschool and school-aged children. The vulnerability of this age group stems from a unique set of circumstances involving behavioral attributes (mouth touching, eating dirt and poor personal hygiene), biological status (developing gut immunity) and environmental exposure (school-related crowds, contaminated school grounds, school toilet facilities) [7, 8].

However, chronic childhood intestinal worm infestations cause much more serious consequences than minor gastro-intestinal disturbance. The repeated or prolonged infections affect absorption of important micronutrients such as iron, vitamin A and zinc resulting in iron deficiency anaemia, protein-energy malnutrition and stunting [9–12]. In addition to this, a body of research is accumulating suggesting that IPIs cause a reduction in cognitive abilities. Children with IPIs perform less well in memory, attention and verbal fluency tasks and in their school attainments. This reduction in abilities can continue after eradication of the infection, thus leading to an irrecoverable loss of human capital [13, 14].

The problem is further exacerbated from a public health point of view by silent transmission. Most infected children do not display, or have only slight, signs and symptoms, but shed eggs/cysts in feces and, through poor hygiene practices and their activities, contribute to reinfection cycles within families, schools and the wider community [15]. The stealthy nature of IPIs leads to a situation where they can escape ordinary clinical detection and have high under-reporting. Policy makers consequently have no good understanding of the prevalence of IPIs in a particular locale, which in turn compromises the sensible planning of mass drug administration (MDA) and WASH programmes, and school based deworming [16].

Iran, a large and climatically diverse country with population more than 85 million, is an interesting environment for study of IPI epidemiology. The last two decades show improvement of sanitation coverage and child survival, and decreased incidence of some infectious diseases in this country [17]. The probable cause of the geographical variations can be attributed to variations in climatic determinants (humidity, annual rain and mean temperature), socioeconomic development (Human Development Index, HDI) and local hygienic behavior. Nevertheless, no complete synthesis was ever conducted nationally which quantify these differences and determine relationship of environmental and developmental parameters with the incidence of IPI among the 31 provinces in Iran. The gaps mentioned above have substantial practical consequences. The lack of detailed provincial and district level data causes improper distribution of drugs and inadequate age-specific data makes the high-risk groups unaccounted or funds are wasted. Besides, it can make it hard to predict the climate implications in a case of doubt as to whether humidity or rain causes the disease. A comprehensive and updated review should therefore be prepared.

Methods

Search strategy

The study protocol was in accordance with the reporting guidelines for the Meta-analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (MOOSE) (Supplementary Table 1) [18]. A comprehensive literature search was conducted across five international databases: Scopus, PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and Google Scholar, to identify relevant studies published between January 2000 and May 2025 (Supplementary Table 2). No language limitation was applied at first step of search. At the screening phase, English and Persian full-text articles were included. The research protocol was registered in the PROSPERO database [CRD420261405452].

The search strategy combining Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and keywords for the 3 main concepts (1) intestinal parasites (intestinal parasitic infection, IPIs, helminthiasis, soil-transmitted helminths, helminthic parasites, protozoan parasites, protozoan infection), (2) population (pre-school child, school-aged child, pediatric, student, Iranian child), (3) epidemiology (prevalence, epidemiology, cross-sectional study). Boolean operator AND / OR was used to combine these searches. Duplicate records were removed automatically using EndNote X9 then manually confirmed. The reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews were searched manually for missed relevant articles.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Two independent reviewers (Z.G. and F.M.) screened the titles, abstracts, and full texts of the retrieved records. Disagreements were resolved by consensus or by consulting a third reviewer (A.V.E.). Cohen’s Kappa showed almost perfect agreement: 0.87 for title/abstract screening and 0.91 for full-text screening. Studies were included in this meta-analysis if they met the following criteria: (1) a cross-sectional or descriptive study design reporting the prevalence of intestinal parasitic infections (helminths and/or protozoa); (2) study population comprised apparently healthy pre-school or school-aged children residing in Iran; (3) diagnosis of intestinal parasites confirmed using standard laboratory methods (e.g., direct smear, formalin-ether concentration, flotation, Kato-Katz, Graham’s tape, staining techniques, or molecular methods such as PCR); (4) provided sufficient raw data (total sample size and number of positive cases) to calculate the prevalence with a 95% confidence interval (CI); and (5) full text was accessible.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) case reports, case series, letters, editorials, reviews, or theses without original data; (2) studies focusing exclusively on symptomatic, hospitalized, or immunocompromised pediatric populations (e.g., children with cancer, HIV, etc.); (3) studies with overlapping or duplicate data from the same institution (in such cases, the study with the larger sample size or more complete data was retained); (4) studies reporting only non-intestinal parasites (e.g., Malaria, leishmaniasis) or parasites from non-human samples; and (5) articles lacking essential methodological details or with unclear outcome reporting.

Data extraction and quality assessment

From each eligible study, two reviewers (S.H.M, and Z.G) independently extracted the following data into a standardized Microsoft Excel 2016 form: first author’s name, publication year, study year, province and district/city, sample size, number of infected children, age range, gender distribution, diagnostic methods, type of intestinal parasites identified (both helminths and protozoa), educational level (preschool, and school-aged children), and climatic data (average temperature, humidity, annual rainfall). Cross-verification was performed by a third author (N.R) to ensure data accuracy.

The quality and risk of bias of each included study were independently assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) adapted for cross-sectional studies [19, 20]. The NOS assigns a maximum of 10 stars across three domains: selection (representativeness of the sample, sample size, non-respondents), comparability (control for confounding factors), and outcome (assessment method, statistical test). Studies scoring 7–9 were classified as high quality, 4–6 as moderate quality, and ≤ 3 as low quality. Only studies with moderate or high quality (score ≥ 4) were included in the final meta-analysis.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

The primary outcome was the pooled prevalence of intestinal parasitic infections (overall, by parasite species, and by subgroup). Given the expected high heterogeneity across studies, we used a random-effects model with the DerSimonian–Laird method to stabilize variances and estimate the pooled prevalence with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Heterogeneity was quantified using the I² statistic and the Cochran’s Q test. I² values of 0–25%, 25–50%, 50–75%, and > 75% were interpreted as low, moderate, high, and very high heterogeneity, respectively. A p-value < 0.05 for the Q test was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using Comprehensive Meta-Analysis Software Version 3 (CMA V3) [21].

Subgroup analyses were conducted a priori to explore potential sources of heterogeneity based on: (1) educational level (preschool, and school-aged children); (2) age group (≤ 5 years, 6–10 years, > 10 years); (3) gender (male, female); (4) diagnostic method (direct smear alone, concentration techniques, staining, Graham’s tape, molecular methods, or combinations); (5) climatic variables (temperature: < 10 °C, 10–20 °C, > 20 °C; humidity: < 40%, 40–75%, > 75%; annual rainfall: < 400 mm, 400–1000 mm, 1001–1500 mm, > 1500 mm); (6) geographical subdivision (province and district/city); and (7) list the types of intestinal parasites that can be identified, both helminths and protozoa.

Publication bias and sensitivity analysis

Publication bias was evaluated visually using funnel plots and statistically using Egger’s regression test [22]. To assess the robustness of the pooled estimates, a sensitivity analysis was performed by sequentially omitting one study at a time (leave-one-out method) and recalculating the pooled prevalence. A study was considered overly influential if its removal changed the point estimate by more than 10% or resulted in a non-overlapping 95% CI.

Results

Characteristics of included studies

Totally 36 relevant Iran’s studies including 48,930 school children were included in this meta-analysis (Table 1; Fig. 1). All studies were published in the years 2000–2025, although the number of studies in the last five years has been much smaller. The pooled prevalence of these studies was calculated as 0.081 (95% CI 0.058–0.113) (Fig. 2). The highest number of studies was from Mazandaran (seven studies) and Tehran (six studies); the least number of studies (one each) was recorded from Bushehr, Ardabil, Guilan, Qom, Razavi Khorasan, South Khorasan, Lorestan, West Azerbaijan, Yazd, Zanjan, and Isfahan provinces (Table 2). Furthermore, when analysed by year of publication, studies published between 2014 and 2025 reported a higher prevalence (0.090, 95% CI: 0.050–0.150; I² = 98%, τ² = 0.650, p < 0.001) compared to those published between 2000 and 2014 (0.060, 95% CI: 0.040–0.090; I² = 97%, τ² = 0.530, p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Prevalence based on type of protozoan parasites

Among protozoan parasites, the highest pooled prevalence was observed for Giardia lamblia (0.080, 95% CI: 0.056–0.112; 25 studies; I² = 98%, τ² = 0.64, p < 0.001). This was followed by Entamoeba histolytica/dispar (0.044, 95% CI: 0.021–0.089; 6 studies; I² = 93%, τ² = 0.30, p < 0.001), Entamoeba coli (0.039, 95% CI: 0.026–0.060; 18 studies; I² = 98%, τ² = 1.13, p < 0.001), Blastocystis hominis (0.039, 95% CI: 0.025–0.059; 19 studies; I² = 98%, τ² = 1.23, p < 0.001), Endolimax nana (0.011, 95% CI: 0.006–0.019; 11 studies; I² = 80%, τ² = 0.36, p < 0.001), Iodamoeba buetschlii (0.009, 95% CI: 0.004–0.018; 8 studies; I² = 90%, τ² = 1.06, p < 0.001), Chilomastix mesnili (0.008, 95% CI: 0.004–0.018; 7 studies; I² = 93%, τ² = 4.25, p < 0.001), Dientamoeba fragilis (0.004, 95% CI: 0.001–0.012; 4 studies; I² = 96%, τ² = 4.44, p < 0.001), Entamoeba hartmanni (0.002, 95% CI: 0.000–0.014; one study), and Trichomonas hominis (0.002, 95% CI: 0.000–0.007; 2 studies; I² = 0.00%, τ² = 0.00, p = 0.57) (Table 2).

Prevalence based on diagnostic method

According to diagnostic methods, the combination of direct smear, concentration, staining, and Graham tape yielded the highest prevalence estimate of 0.440 (95% CI: 0.190–0.710) with high heterogeneity (I² = 97%, τ² = 0.740, p < 0.001), although this was derived from only two studies and should be interpreted with caution. The Graham and scotch tape method alone gave a prevalence of 0.110 (95% CI: 0.060–0.190; I² = 95%, τ² = 0.620, p < 0.001). Direct smear with concentration (sedimentation) resulted in 0.070 (95% CI: 0.040–0.090; I² = 98%, τ² = 0.690, p < 0.001), and adding staining to this combination reduced the prevalence to 0.040 (95% CI: 0.010–0.090; I² = 96%, τ² = 1.310, p < 0.001). A single study using a combination that included PCR reported a prevalence of 0.050 (95% CI: 0.010–0.220) (Table 2).

Prevalence based on educational level

Regarding educational level, the highest pooled prevalence was observed in preschool children at 0.130 (95% CI: 0.070–0.210) based on 10 studies with very high heterogeneity (I² = 97%, τ² = 0.850, p < 0.001), whereas school-aged children showed a prevalence of 0.060 (95% CI: 0.040–0.090) from 26 studies (I² = 98%, τ² = 0.710, p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Prevalence based on age and gender

Based on Table 2, the pooled prevalence of intestinal parasitic infections was 0.081 (95% CI: 0.043–0.147) in children aged 3–5 years, 0.129 (95% CI: 0.073–0.219) in those aged 6–10 years, and 0.053 (95% CI: 0.029–0.096) in children > 10 years, indicating the highest rate in the 6–10 years age group. Regarding gender, male children showed a slightly higher prevalence (0.160, 95% CI: 0.110–0.220) compared to females (0.150, 95% CI: 0.100–0.210), though the difference was not statistically significant, while studies reporting both sexes gave a prevalence of 0.090 (95% CI: 0.050–0.150).

Pooled prevalence estimates categorized by climatic conditions

In terms of climatic variables, regions with an average temperature of 10–20 °C had the highest prevalence at 0.090 (95% CI: 0.060–0.130; I² = 98%, τ² = 1.090, p < 0.001), compared to 0.060 in areas above 20 °C (95% CI: 0.020–0.140; I² = 98%, τ² = 1.390, p < 0.001) and only 0.010 in areas below 10 °C (95% CI: 0.000–0.120; I² = 98%, τ² = 3.270, p < 0.001). Regarding humidity, the highest prevalence was observed in regions with relative humidity greater than 75% (0.130, 95% CI: 0.040–0.320; I² = 99%, τ² = 1.880, p < 0.001), followed by areas with humidity below 40% (0.090, 95% CI: 0.040–0.200; I² = 82%, τ² = 0.210, p < 0.001) and those with humidity between 40 and 75% (0.070, 95% CI: 0.050–0.100; I² = 98%, τ² = 0.770, p < 0.001). For annual rainfall, regions receiving less than 400 mm showed a prevalence of 0.080 (95% CI: 0.050–0.120; I² = 99%, τ² = 1.130, p < 0.001), those receiving 400–1000 mm showed 0.070 (95% CI: 0.030–0.140; I² = 96%, τ² = 0.760, p < 0.001), and those receiving more than 1000 mm showed 0.090 (95% CI: 0.020–0.260; I² = 97%, τ² = 1.190, p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Highest and lowest pooled prevalence by province and district/city

At the provincial level, the highest pooled prevalence was observed in Bushehr (0.190, 95% CI: 0.030–0.630; 1 study), Semnan (0.190, 95% CI: 0.050–0.480; I² = 99%, τ² = 1.400, p < 0.001), Khuzestan (0.150, 95% CI: 0.040–0.430; I² = 97%, τ² = 1.190, p < 0.001), and Kordestan (0.140, 95% CI: 0.040–0.410; I² = 98%, τ² = 4.220, p < 0.001), while the lowest was found in Fars (0.000, 95% CI: 0.000–0.040; I² = 72%, τ² = 1.520, p < 0.05). Among districts and cities, the highest estimates were recorded in Gashki (0.420, 95% CI: 0.090–0.830), Shahroud (0.350, 95% CI: 0.070–0.780), Ali Abad-e-Katoul (0.340, 95% CI: 0.070–0.790), and Ahvaz (0.280, 95% CI: 0.050–0.730), whereas the lowest values were seen in Bandar Abbas (0.000, 95% CI: 0.000–0.020), Jahrom (0.000, 95% CI: 0.000–0.030), and Marvdasht (0.010, 95% CI: 0.000–0.120). For Babol (two studies), the prevalence was 0.090 (95% CI: 0.020–0.290; I² = 98%, τ² = 3.260, p < 0.001), and for Sari (four studies), the prevalence was 0.060 (95% CI: 0.020–0.160; I² = 97%, τ² = 0.820, p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Publication bias and sensitivity analysis

Based on the findings of the study, a funnel plot along with Egger’s test was used to assess publication bias. The results showed a p-value of 0.990 and a t-statistic of 0.012, indicating no significant asymmetry in the funnel plot (Fig. 4). Sensitivity analysis was performed by sequentially omitting each individual study and recalculating the pooled event rate. The resulting event rates ranged from 0.076 to 0.086, and all corresponding 95% confidence intervals overlapped substantially with the overall estimate (0.081; 95% CI: 0.058–0.113). All p-values remained less than 0.001 throughout the analysis. These findings indicate that no single study exerted undue influence on the pooled estimate, confirming the robustness and stability of the meta-analysis results (Fig. 5).

Meta-regression

Among all the covariates examined in the meta‑regression analyses (annual rainfall, average temperature, humidity, publication year, diagnostic methods, and province), only diagnostic methods showed a statistically significant overall effect (F = 2.72, p = 0.0472). The remaining covariates were not significant: annual rainfall (p = 0.9749), average temperature (p = 0.3157), humidity (p = 0.6501), publication year (p = 0.7684), and province (p = 0.3339). Therefore, while methodological differences across studies meaningfully influenced the effect sizes, climatic factors (rainfall, temperature, humidity), publication year, and geographic location (province) did not significantly moderate the results in this meta‑analysis (Fig. 6).

Following the quality assessment, it was revealed that among the 36 studies, 23 were classified as high quality (score of 7–9 points), and 13 were categorized as moderate quality (score of 4–6 points) (Supplementary Table 3).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis presents a complete and updated panorama of IPIs in pre-school and school children in Iran; with high number of subjects (n = 48,930) in 36 studies between 2000 and 2025. The general results show that IPIs continue to be a major public health problem in the Iranian child population, notwithstanding the advances made over the past few decades in health and sanitation infrastructure.

The higher incidence recorded in the present study of G. lamblia as the protozoon and E. vermicularis as the helminth is due to different but related biological and epidemiological factors. G. lamblia dominance due to its direct fecal-oral transmission and low infectious dose (as low as 10–25 cysts) [21, 23], and outstanding environmental resistance of the cysts (e.g., weeks to months in cold, moist conditions and even chlorine concentrations commonly found in drinking water and swimming pools) [24, 25].

A nationwide meta-analysis reported that the prevalence of G. lamblia in Iran is 14.7% (95% CI: 12.0-16.6), children younger than 10 have the highest infection rates (15.1%) and eastern provinces of Iran bordering with Afghanistan and Pakistan have the highest rate [26]. Additionally, daycare attendance, lack of sanitation and unhygienic conditions have also been implicated as major risk factors for Giardia infection in this age group [27]. Based on the previous study, E. vermicularis infection is highly prevalent among Iranian school and kindergarten children, with an overall estimated rate of 17.2%. In other words, nearly one out of every five children is infected. Such a high prevalence matters because the infection can lead to malnutrition, stunted growth, sleep problems, anal itching, and irritability. In rare cases, it may even affect the kidneys or fallopian tubes, and chronic infection can harm children’s cognitive development. The previous study also found that poor public hygiene, living in rural areas, low maternal education, and crowded environments like schools and kindergartens are key risk factors. Therefore, these findings strongly call for effective screening, health education, and preventive measures by Iran’s health and education authorities [2] .

The high proportion of infections among children aged 6–10 years (12.9%) supports the documented age specific distribution of intestinal parasitic infections and highlights key periods for targeted interventions. In preschool children, the relatively poor development of personal hygiene habits such as pica, geophagy, hand to mouth behavior, and inadequate training in the use of toilet facilities greatly increases exposure to and ingestion of infective agents present in soil, fomites, or food [28, 29].

The study clearly indicates a pressing need for integrated control approaches, including mass treatment campaigns, health education to improve handwashing practices and reduce geophagy, and environmental measures such as covering sandboxes and reducing soil contamination in school and public playgrounds [30].

Climatic variables had a significant influence in the IPI distribution, high relative humidity (> 75%), low/moderate temperature (10–20 °C) and medium rainfall (400–1000 mm) had the highest frequencies. The conditions for survival and development of parasitic cysts, eggs and larvae in the environment [3, 31]. This emphasizes the relevance of developing climate responsive interventions into public health planning in the face of climate change [3, 32].

Gender specific analysis revealed that the incidence was marginally higher in males than females, but the difference was not statistically significant. Similar trends were observed by other authors and may be due to behavioral variation such as greater outdoor exposure/activity by males [33].

Overall, our study suggests that behavioral, environmental, and methodological factors contribute to IPIs in children in Iran. Even though IPI prevalence remains relatively low compared to some lower-income countries, the continued presence of these infections particularly among young children mandates sustained public health interventions. A combined approach that incorporates ongoing screening, school-based deworming programs, health education, and WASH infrastructure development will be essential to further reduce IPIs in Iranian children.Some limitations are to be considered when drawing conclusions from the results of this systematic review and meta-analysis. The significant heterogeneity was present among almost all of the pooled estimates (I2 > 90%).This is mainly attributable to the variability in study design, size of population sample, diagnostic methods, and true epidemiological variation among different climatic and socio-economic regions of Iran. Subgroup analysis attempted to identify origins of heterogeneity, however a portion of variation remained unexplained. The majority of the studies (21 out of 36) used direct smear with concentration (sedimentation), which likely resulted in an underestimation of the true prevalence, especially for low-intensity infections and organisms such as D. fragilis or B. hominis. Only a few studies applied staining, Graham tape, or molecular methods, all of which offer much higher sensitivity. Given the level of heterogeneity, the pooled estimates should be interpreted as a broad epidemiological summary rather than a precise, generalizable prevalence figure; the consistency of the direction and approximate magnitude of the estimate across subgroups, sensitivity analyses, and the 25-year study period supports its use for general public health planning, but the true prevalence in any specific population may differ substantially from the pooled estimate.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the considerable heterogeneity observed across the included studies reflects substantial variability in study designs, populations, diagnostic methods, and regional conditions, which may reduce the reliability of the pooled estimates. Second, most studies relied on conventional microscopy rather than more sensitive molecular techniques, potentially leading to an underestimation of the true prevalence of intestinal parasitic infections. Third, the lack of detailed and consistent data on key confounding factors such as sanitation and hygiene practices limited our ability to perform more precise adjusted analyses. Despite these limitations, this study represents the most comprehensive and up-to-date assessment of intestinal parasites among preschool and school-aged Iranian children, offering the most accurate current picture of the epidemiological situation.

Conclusions

Intestinal parasitic infections remain a public health concern among preschool and school-aged children in Iran, with the highest infection rates peaking in children aged 6–10 years and younger; high humidity and moderate temperatures facilitate transmission. To effectively prevent these infections, dynamic surveillance systems (routine screening in kindergartens and schools, climate-sensitive monitoring) must be combined with strengthening WASH infrastructure (safe water, sanitation, and hygiene facilities), alongside targeted public awareness through social media campaigns, educational films shown in schools to teach students proper hygiene behaviors, and advertisements directed at parents to discourage geophagy and promote handwashing. Without integrating surveillance, WASH, and multi-channel awareness, purely curative interventions cannot prevent the chronic consequences of these infections on children’s growth and cognitive development.
