Socio-economic and geographical inequalities in neonatal mortality rates in Sierra Leone, 2008–2019
Authors: Augustus Osborne, Alpha Umaru Bai-Sesay, Camilla Bangura, Hassan Rogers, Bright Opoku Ahinkorah
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSierra Leone has reduced neonatal mortality rates(NMR) in recent years. Despite this progress, disparities in newborn survival persist across socio-economic and geographic areas. This study examined the inequalities in neonatal mortality rates in Sierra Leone between 2008 and 2019.MethodsWe utilized data from the Sierra Leone Demographic Health Survey rounds conducted in 2008, 2013, and 2019. We used the World Health Organisation Health Equity Assessment Toolkit to calculate simple measures of inequality (Difference, and Ratio), and complex measures of inequality (Population Attributable Risk, and Population Attributable Fraction). Inequality in neonatal mortality rate was calculated on six stratifiers: maternal age, maternal economic status, maternal level of education, place of residence, sex of the child, and sub-national province.Results Neonatal mortality rate decreased in Sierra Loene from 48.6 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2008 to 29.6 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2019. There was an increase in age-related inequality from a Difference of 0.7 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2008 to 4.3 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2019. Economic inequality decreased from a Difference of 26.8 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2008 to -3.4 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2019. Inequality in education decreased from a Difference of 4.6 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2008 to -4.2 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2019. Inequality increased from a Difference of − 0.5 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2008 to -4.2 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2019 for place of residence. For the child’s sex, the inequality increased from a Difference of − 7.9 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2008 to -11.1 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2019. Provincial inequality increased slightly from a Difference of 14.0 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2008 to 14.4 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2019.ConclusionThe findings show a decline in the national neonatal mortality rate from 2008 to 2019, indicating improvements in healthcare and maternal support. While economic and educational inequalities have decreased, especially in education, sustaining these gains is essential for equitable healthcare access. Despite this progress, inequalities based on age, residence, child’s sex, and province still exist, and have increased between 2008 and 2019. Policymakers should focus on targeted programs for vulnerable age groups and sexes, and develop geographical strategies to ensure uniform improvements in neonatal health.

Introduction

Neonatal mortality, defined as the death of a live-born baby within the first 28 days of life, represents the highest risk period for child mortality and is influenced by various medical, social, and environmental factors [1]. Key causes include complications from preterm birth, intrapartum-related events like birth asphyxia, infections such as sepsis and pneumonia, and congenital anomalies [2–4]. Each year, approximately 2.3 million newborns die during the neonatal period, accounting for nearly 47% of all under-five child deaths, with Sub-Saharan Africa facing the greatest challenges due to inadequate healthcare infrastructure, limited access to skilled birth attendants, high infection rates, and socioeconomic barriers like poverty and low education levels [5–7]. These issues devastate families, impact economic productivity, and erode trust in healthcare systems [4].

Sierra Leone has seen some improvements in neonatal mortality over the past decade. In 2008, the rate stood at a concerning 48.6 deaths per 1,000 live births. By 2019, this number had decreased to 29.6 deaths per 1,000 live births [8]. However, these national averages mask substantial disparities across regions and socio-economic groups. Rural areas and households with lower socioeconomic status consistently experience higher neonatal mortality rates compared to their urban and wealthier counterparts [9]. These disparities underscore the urgent need for targeted interventions that address the specific needs of these populations.

Collaborating with various partners, the Government of Sierra Leone has implemented several initiatives to combat neonatal mortality. A cornerstone of this effort is improving access to quality maternal and neonatal healthcare services [10]. This entails upgrading healthcare facilities, ensuring the availability of essential medicines and equipment, and providing comprehensive training for healthcare providers in newborn care [11]. Programs like the Free Healthcare Initiative, launched in 2010, aimed to provide free medical care for pregnant women, lactating mothers, and children under five [12]. Another crucial strategy focuses on strengthening infection prevention and control measures. Promoting hygiene practices, ensuring clean birthing environments, and providing vaccinations against common neonatal infections are essential components of this approach [13]. Additionally, community-based healthcare initiatives, like training traditional birth attendants and establishing community health worker programs for timely referral, have been implemented to extend healthcare services to remote and underserved areas [14].

Despite efforts to improve neonatal health, substantial challenges persist in Sierra Leone. Inadequate healthcare facilities, especially in rural areas, and a lack of adequately trained healthcare personnel limit the effectiveness of interventions [15]. Socioeconomic barriers such as poverty and poor access to education restrict access to healthcare services, while cultural beliefs influence health-seeking behaviours [16]. To our knowledge no national studies have documented trends and inequalities in neonatal mortality rates in Sierra Leone. This study aims to fill this gap by examining the socio-economic and geographical inequalities in neonatal mortality rates in Sierra Leone from 2008 to 2019, to provide insights to inform policy decisions and targeted interventions to reduce neonatal mortality and achieve health equity in Sierra Leone.

Methods

Study setting and data source

We utilised data from the 2008, 2013, and 2019 Sierra Leone Demographic Health Survey (SLDHS). The SLDHS is a nationwide survey that aims to uncover long-lasting trends and changes in demographic indicators, health indicators, and social issues among individuals of all genders and age groups [17]. The SLDHS utilised a cross-sectional design, where participants were chosen using a stratified multi-stage cluster sampling process. The SLDHS report thoroughly explains the sampling procedure [17]. This study primarily targeted women who had previously given birth before the corresponding periods of the SLDHS. The 2008, 2013, and 2019 SLDHS data were available using the World Health Organisation (WHO) Health Equity Assessment Toolkit (HEAT) online platform [18]. This study followed the criteria outlined in the recommendations for Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) [19].

Outcome variable and inequality measures

The outcome variable for the study was neonatal mortality rates. Inequality in neonatal health can be quantified using the Neonatal Mortality Rate, defined as the number of neonatal deaths (deaths within the first 28 days of life) per 1,000 live births in a specific year or period. This measurement allows for assessing inequalities in neonatal outcomes across different populations or regions, highlighting inequalities in access to socioeconomic conditions and overall maternal and neonatal health services. The birth history data in the SLDHS contains detailed records of the birth dates and ages at death of newborn infants. The study utilised data on live births five years before the surveys. Inequality in neonatal mortality rates among children under the age of five were calculated using six stratifiers: age groups (15–19 and 20–49), economic status measured by wealth quintile (quintile 1, 2, 3, 4, 5), level of education (no education, primary education, secondary/higher education), place of residence (rural, urban), child sex (female, male), and sub-national province (Eastern, Northern, Northwestern, Southern, Western).

Statistical analysis

The study utilised the online version of the HEAT software developed by the WHO, specifically version 6.1 [18]. HEAT is a statistical tool designed to analyze health disparities both within and between nations, focusing on various health indicators and socio-economic determinants, including child, maternal, and reproductive health [20, 21]. This study examined four key markers of inequality: Difference (D), Ratio (R), Population-Attributable Fraction (PAF), and Population-Attributable Risk (PAR). Difference an absolute measure represents the arithmetic difference in health outcomes between two groups. A Difference value of zero indicates no inequality, while larger values signify greater inequalities. It provides a simple understanding of the gap in neonatal death rates between subgroups. Ratio a relative measure compares the health outcomes of two groups by calculating the ratio of their rates. An R value of 1 indicates no inequality, whereas values greater than 1 suggest higher neonatal mortality rates in the subgroup of interest. This measure is useful for understanding the relative scale of inequality. PAF a relative measure indicates the proportion of a health outcome in the population that can be attributed to a specific risk factor. Positive PAF values reflect advantageous conditions, while negative values indicate disadvantageous conditions. Higher PAF values are associated with increased inequality. PAR an absolute measure quantifies the risk of a health outcome attributable to a specific factor within the population. Positive PAR values denote beneficial scenarios, while negative values highlight detrimental conditions. Increased PAR values correlate with greater levels of inequality. Difference was chosen to assess the absolute gap in neonatal death rates. This measure provides a clear numerical representation of the inequality, making it easy to interpret the extent of inequality. Ratio was utilised to evaluate relative inequalities in neonatal death rates. The ratio helps to contextualize disparities in relation to a baseline group, offering insights into how much worse off one group is compared to another. Population-Attributable Fraction was selected to understand the proportion of neonatal deaths that could be prevented if the risk factor were eliminated. This measure emphasizes the potential for improvement and highlights areas where interventions could be most effective. Population-Attributable Risk was included to quantify the overall impact of risk factors on neonatal mortality. By showing the risk associated with specific determinants, PAR helps to identify critical areas for public health intervention. The combination of these measures provides a comprehensive analysis of health disparities, allowing for both absolute and relative assessments of inequality in neonatal death rates. The literature offers an intricate and thorough explanation of HEAT’s functionalities and applications in this context [20, 21].

Results

The NMR decreased from 48.6 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2008 to 29.6 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2019, indicating progress in neonatal health (Fig. 1).

In terms of age, the NMR for children of mothers aged 15–19 years decreased from 47.5 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2008 to 33.1 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2019, while for children of mothers aged 20–49 years, it fell from 46.8 deaths per 1,000 live births to 28.8 deaths per 1,000 live births. Children of mothers in the poorest quintile experienced a dramatic decline from 67.7 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2008 to 29.7 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2019. In comparison, children of mothers in the richest quintile also saw a decrease from 40.9 deaths per 1,000 live births to 33.1 deaths per 1,000 live births. Educational attainment showed a consistent decline in NMR among children of mothers with no education, from 49.2 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2008 to 28.6 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2019. In terms of geographical disparities, children of mothers in rural areas showed a decrease in NMR from 48.5 deaths per 1,000 live births to 28.1 deaths per 1,000 live births, while children of mothers in urban areas also showed improvement from 49.1 deaths per 1,000 live births to 32.4 deaths per 1,000 live births. Gender disparities in neonatal mortality were evident, with male infants consistently having higher mortality rates compared to female infants, dropping from 52.6 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2008 to 35.1 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2019 for males, while females decreased from 44.7 deaths per 1,000 live births to 23.9 deaths per 1,000 live births. Provincially, children of mothers in the North showed a substantial decline from 53.5 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2008 to 21.6 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2019, indicating a positive shift in neonatal health (Table 1).

Discussion

Our study examined the trends in neonatal mortality rates and the associated inequalities in Sierra Leone over the past decade. The findings indicate a significant overall decline in neonatal mortality, alongside marked improvements in economic and educational inequalities related to health outcomes. Additionally, inequalities based on place of residence and child sex have also shown positive changes, suggesting a movement towards more equitable healthcare access. However, the slight increase in provincial inequality highlights ongoing challenges that need to be addressed.

The decline in NMR from 48.6 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2008 to 29.6 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2019 in Sierra Leone is a substantial achievement. Several factors could have contributed to this improvement, including improved availability of skilled birth attendants, antenatal care, and emergency obstetric care [10]. Building more health centres and hospitals, especially in rural areas, could have increased access to care [11]. Better training for healthcare providers, improved equipment, and better management of health facilities could have enhanced the quality of care [10]. Community education campaigns could have promoted healthy behaviours and early care-seeking [11]. Better nutrition for pregnant women and newborns would have reduced the risk of complications, and improved care for preterm babies could have reduced mortality rates.

The increase in inequality for mother’s age in NMR in Sierra Leone is a concerning trend. Early marriages can lead to higher rates of adolescent pregnancy, which is associated with increased risks for both mother and child [22]. Changes in fertility rates, such as increased births among adolescents or older mothers, could contribute to the observed trend [23]. Disparities in access to quality maternal and neonatal care can contribute to higher mortality rates among specific age groups [24]. Inadequate care for high-risk pregnancies, such as those among adolescent or older mothers, can increase neonatal mortality [25]. Societal norms and practices that discriminate against women can limit their access to education, healthcare, and economic opportunities, leading to higher risks for maternal and child health [26].

The study revealed a substantial decrease in economic disparity in neonatal mortality in Sierra Leone. This finding aligns with the previous studies [27, 28]. Possible explanation for the finding could be the expanding healthcare services, particularly maternal and child health services, to poorer populations [10]. Economic growth and social safety nets might have reduced income inequality, leading to a smaller gap in NMR [29]. Specific programs to reduce neonatal mortality among poor populations, such as conditional cash transfers or maternal and child health packages, could have contributed [11]. Better access to clean water, sanitation, and nutritious food can reduce the risk of neonatal mortality, especially among people experiencing poverty [11]. Also, the decrease in inequality in neonatal mortality based on maternal education could be attributed to higher education levels among women, often associated with better maternal and child health practices [30]. Public health campaigns targeting women’s education and health could have contributed to the decline [10]. Lower fertility rates among women with lower education levels can lead to better birth outcomes [31].

The slight increase in provincial inequality in neonatal mortality in Sierra Leone indicates that inequalities in neonatal health outcomes between provinces are widening. This finding aligns with the previous studies [27, 28]. Disparities in economic growth and development across regions can lead to differences in access to healthcare, education, and other essential services [32]. Higher poverty levels in certain regions can limit access to quality maternal and child health care [33]. Differences in infrastructure, such as roads, transportation, and communication, can hinder access to healthcare facilities in remote areas [34]. Disparities in the availability of skilled healthcare providers, equipment, and medicines can contribute to regional differences in neonatal mortality [35]. Provinces affected by conflict or instability may experience disruptions in healthcare services and increased vulnerability to maternal and child health risks [36].

Policy and practice implications

The findings of this study on neonatal mortality rates in Sierra Leone (2008–2019) suggest several policy and practice changes to reduce infant deaths further and address existing inequalities. The rise in age-based disparity necessitates targeted interventions for mothers of newborns of different age groups. Policies could include investment in neonatal intensive care units and training healthcare workers in specialised care for premature babies. Extend and strengthen postnatal care programs to address complications arising after birth, especially for newborns beyond the immediate delivery period. The success of economic interventions in reducing economic disparity is promising. Policies should continue or expand programs that address economic barriers to quality healthcare for pregnant women and newborns. Consider conditional cash transfer programs that incentivise essential health checkups for pregnant women and newborns. The plateauing of progress in education and residence-based disparities requires further investigation and policy changes: Increase access to educational programs on maternal health, newborn care, and hygiene practices, mainly targeting communities with lower education levels. Implement mobile clinics or improve transportation infrastructure to bridge the gap in healthcare access for residents in remote areas. The persistent provincial inequality demands geographically targeted policies: Conduct in-depth assessments to understand the specific challenges faced by each province with high neonatal mortality rates. Allocate resources strategically, focusing on provinces with the most substantial disparities in healthcare infrastructure, personnel, and access to essential services. By implementing these policy and practice changes, Sierra Leone can reduce neonatal mortality rates and ensure equitable access to quality healthcare for all newborns nationwide.

Strengths and limitations

The SLDHS 2008–2019 provides a rich source of nationally representative data on demographics, health, and socio-economic factors. This allows for a comprehensive analysis of neonatal mortality rates and their association with various factors. The WHO Health Equity Assessment Toolkit is designed to analyse and visualise inequalities in health data. This software can help you effectively identify and measure socio-economic and geographical disparities in neonatal mortality rates over time (2008–2019). Both SLDHS and HEAT utilise standardised methodologies, allowing for comparisons with other studies and ensuring the generalizability of your findings. SLDHS data may not capture all potential factors influencing neonatal mortality rates. Additional data sources might be needed to explore specific social determinants of health. SLDHS data is collected at the household level, so you can’t necessarily make individual-level conclusions about the causes of mortality. While the study can identify associations between factors and neonatal mortality, it cannot definitively establish causal relationships. Further research might be needed to explore causal pathways.

Conclusion

The findings indicate a decline in the national neonatal mortality rate, between 2008 and 2019, reflecting improvements in healthcare and maternal support. However, the increase in age inequality suggests that children of mothers aged 15–19 may still be at a higher risk, necessitating targeted interventions. While economic and educational disparities have notably decreased, with the latter showing a remarkable shift from positive to negative inequality, it is crucial to sustain these gains to ensure equitable access to healthcare for all demographics. Despite this progress, inequalities based on age, residence, child’s sex, and province disparities still exist, and have increased between 2008 and 2019. Therefore, it is recommended that policymakers implement targeted programs aimed at the most vulnerable age groups and sexes, and develop geographical healthcare strategies that specifically address the disparities observed, ensuring that improvements in neonatal health are experienced uniformly across all segments of the population.
