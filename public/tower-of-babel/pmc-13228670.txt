Trapeziometacarpal joint movement during pinching measured by ultrasonography
Authors: David B Jordan, Sohail Daulat, Trevour Greene, John C Elfar, C Kent Kwoh, Zong-Ming Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveFunctional hand activity induces complex motion of the trapeziometacarpal (TMC) joint. Analyzing the TMC joint motion in vivo can aid in understanding joint behavior and lead to advancements in joint health evaluation. The purpose of this study was to quantify TMC joint motion during pinching using ultrasonography.DesignHealthy participants (n = 10) held a pinch meter in key pinch configuration and pinched to three prescribed force levels. Ultrasonography was used to record the motion of the TMC joint. The position and rotation of the first metacarpal were calculated. Repeated measures one-way ANOVAs were used for comparisons (α = 0.05).ResultsWhen the pinch force was increased from 0 to 10, 20 and 30 N, the first metacarpal translated in the dorsal(+)/volar(-) direction -0.3 mm [95 % CI: (-0.5 mm, -0.1 mm); p = 0.0151], -0.5 mm [95 % CI: (-0.8 mm, -0.1 mm); p = 0.0113] and -0.8 mm [95 % CI: (-1.4 mm, -0.2 mm); p = 0.0146]. Significance was not observed for proximal(+)/distal(-) translation (p = 0.224). The metacarpal rotated in the abduction(+)/adduction(-) direction 0.7° [95 % CI: (-0.8°, 2.1°); p = 0.3239], 2.6° [95 % CI: (0.1°, 5.1°); p = 0.0416] and 3.3° [95 % CI: (0.2°, 6.3°); p = 0.0393], at pinch forces of 10, 20 and 30 N, respectively.ConclusionsThe TMC joint undergoes volar translation and abduction rotation during pinch tasks. Ultrasonography can be used to quantify this motion and aid in the advancement of joint behavioral study.Keywords: Trapeziometacarpal joint, Ultrasonography, Biomechanics, Hand, Wrist

Introduction

The trapeziometacarpal (TMC) joint of the hand is formed by the articulation between the first metacarpal and trapezium. The motion of the TMC joint is complex due to the diversity of the functional hand activity which the joint enables [1]. Functional hand activities illicit relative motion between the articulating surfaces of the TMC joint and generate mechanical influences that can, over time, lead to degeneration of the joint surface [2,3]. Studying the in vivo motion of the TMC joint during functional hand activity can aid in the understanding of these influences and lead to advances in TMC joint health evaluation.

The TMC joint has been studied with respect to a variety of functional hand activity using various quantitative techniques. Some functional hand activity includes actions that do not involve external force application, such as circumduction, whereby the thumb moves across the full range of motion. Imaging techniques, such as motion tracking of optical markers [4,5], and in vivo techniques, such as computed tomographic imaging [[6], [7], [8]] have been used to quantify thumb kinematics during such motion. Functional hand activity also includes actions that involve external force application. These external forces involve an applied force at the thumb which can produce a load at the TMC joint. Functional hand activities producing external forces include grasping, twisting and pinching. The respective effects of these actions with respect to the TMC joint, have been studied using a wide array of quantitative methods [[9], [10], [11], [12]].

Pinching is a functional hand activity which is commonly studied due to the ubiquity of the action in daily use and functional evaluation of the thumb TMC joint. Pinch strength is a common metric used to evaluate normative and compromised hand function [13], as well as the effects of hand and wrist interventions [14]. In vivo study of the TMC joint during pinching has shown that pinching activity can induce translation of the first metacarpal in multiple directions, with respect to the trapezium, and rotation about multiple axes [9,12,15]. These complex motion patterns are a contributing factor to degeneration of the TMC joint cartilage over time [16] and emphasize the importance of furthering the understanding of the in vivo joint kinematics during common functional hand activity.

Further understanding of TMC joint kinematics can be accomplished by using more readily available in vivo imaging modalities. The use of in vivo imaging techniques which are commonly used in clinical settings may have greater accessibility compared to previous methods and can improve the utility and translatability of in vivo TMC joint motion analysis. One such imaging technique is ultrasonography. Ultrasonography does not involve ionizing radiation and presents no risks to patients. Ultrasonographic imaging has been frequently used in the clinical [17] and experimental study of several joints throughout the body, including the elbow [18], knee [19] and hip [20] joints. Ultrasonographic imaging has been demonstrated to be useful in the morphological analysis of healthy and compromised TMC joint structure [21,22]; and can similarly be effective in analyzing the TMC joint behavior during functional hand activity. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the in vivo motion of the TMC joint during pinching using ultrasonographic imaging.

Methods

Study population

All procedures were performed in compliance with relevant laws and institutional guidelines and have been approved by the appropriate institutional committee. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Arizona (approval date 08/03/23; reference number 00,003,224). Ten healthy participants were recruited for the study (6 female, Age 31.7 ± 15.3 years, BMI 21.8 ± 2.1 kg/m2; 4 male, Age 33.5 ± 10.0 years, BMI 25.1 ± 3.5 kg/m2). Each participant provided written and informed consent prior to participation. Participants were recruited from the campus of the University of Arizona. Inclusion criteria included an age of at least eighteen years. All participants self-reported no history of injuries or musculoskeletal disorders associated with the hand or wrist and a BMI < 35.

Experimental protocol

The right hand and forearm of seated participants were placed supine onto a custom-designed fixation device (Fig. 1). A constraint was placed around the upper arm to prevent proximal/distal arm movement. The hand, four fingers and thumb were unrestrained. A pinch meter P100 model (National Instruments, Austin, TX) was placed between the thumb and index finger of the subject in a key pinch configuration. Ultrasound gel was placed along the volar aspect of the thumb, along the long axis of the first metacarpal and near the TMC joint at the wrist. An ultrasound transducer (Acuson S2000, Siemens Medical Solutions USA, Mountain View, CA) was secured into a custom-designed ultrasound transducer orientation device. The device was manipulated near the ultrasound gel to obtain an image of the TMC joint. The image included the echogenic long axis of the first metacarpal and the TMC joint as comprised of the visible proximal aspect of the first metacarpal and the distal aspect of the trapezium. The ultrasound transducer orientation device was locked in this configuration. The frequency, gain and depth of the ultrasound transducer were 17 MHz, 10 dB and 3.0 cm.

Experimental configuration for a participant. The participant hand was secured in a custom restraint while holding a pinch meter in key pinch configuration. The ultrasound transducer was fixed near the TMC joint using a custom-designed ultrasound transducer orientation device.

The pinch task was defined using the key pinch configuration. The standard definition of prehension for this type of pinch task was followed, whereby the pinch meter was positioned between the pad of the thumb and the radial side of the middle phalanx of the index finger [23]. A demonstration was provided for the proper holding technique of the pinch meter and for the testing procedure. Participants were instructed to face a monitor where the output of the pinch force data was displayed within the user interface of a data acquisition program (LabVIEW, National Instruments, Austin, TX). Participants were instructed to gradually increase the pinch force to 10, 20, and 30 N within 5 s. After reaching the prescribed force level, participants were instructed to maintain the constant force for approximately 5 s and then to release the pinch effort. Participants were given time to practice this protocol for each force level prior to the start of data collection. Data collection then proceeded for each specified force level. Each force level was repeated for 3 trials. A minimum of thirty seconds of rest was taken in between trials. During each trial, an ultrasound video was taken of the TMC joint. All ultrasonographic imaging was performed by a single operator with over three years of imaging experience.

Image and data analysis

For each ultrasound video, the two-dimensional position and rotation of the first metacarpal was calculated with respect to the trapezium using a custom LabView program (Fig. 2). The position of the first metacarpal was calculated by establishing an anatomical coordinate system at the trapezium and tracking a point on the articular surface of the first metacarpal. The anatomical coordinate system was defined by manually selecting two points on the first frame of the ultrasound video (Fig. 2A). The first point, point o, was selected as the most distal and most volar point on the trapezium articular surface. Point o defined the origin of the coordinate system. The second point, point a, was selected proximal to point o on the volar surface of the trapezium. The vector extending from point o to point a defined the proximal axis of the coordinate system. The dorsal axis was defined as a vector perpendicular to the proximal axis. The distal and volar axes were in the opposite direction of the proximal and dorsal axes, respectively, and designated as negative. A third point, point b, was selected on the articular surface of the first metacarpal. Point b was selected as the point with the same Y-coordinate as point o, as given in the global (X, Y) image coordinate system. The positions of point o, point a and point b were tracked for each frame of the ultrasound video using the Lucas-Kanade method. The position of point b, as given in the global (X, Y) image coordinate system, was transformed into the anatomical (proximal/distal, dorsal/volar) coordinate system of the trapezium at every frame of the video. For each trial, the initial position of point b was given by the position of point b at the first video frame. During the time period where the prescribed pinch force was maintained constant, the position of point b was calculated as the average of the location measurements across 50 video frames.

Ultrasonographic images of the TMC joint during pinching at (A) 0 N, (B) 10 N, (C) 20 N and (D) 30 N for a representative participant. An anatomical coordinate system (proximal/distal, dorsal/volar) was established at the trapezium to track the position of a point, b, on the metacarpal articular surface. A vector, v, was established along the body of the metacarpal to calculate the rotation of the first metacarpal.

The rotation of the first metacarpal was calculated by establishing a vector v, along the body of the first metacarpal (Fig. 2). Two points, point c and point d, were manually selected on the first frame of the ultrasound video. Point c was selected as the most proximal point on the metacarpal body. Point d was selected as a point on the metacarpal body distal to point c. The vector extending from point d to point c defined the vector v. Point c and point d were then manually selected on the final frame of the ultrasound video, defining the vector v at the time of applied pinch force. The angle between v at the first frame and last frame of the ultrasound video was defined as the rotation of the first metacarpal. The orientation of the hands of the participants was supine. In this orientation, the placement of the ultrasound transducer along the long-axis of the thumb produced a volar/dorsal cross-section of the TMC joint. The perpendicular axis to this cross-section is the abduction/adduction rotation axis. Abduction rotation was identified as counterclockwise rotation of the first metacarpal and was designated as positive. The rotation of the first metacarpal was calculated for each trial.

Statistical analysis

A correlation analysis was performed using the data of trial 1 and trial 2 to assess the repeatability of the measurements. The analysis was performed between trials 1 and 2 using the proximal/distal position data, distal/volar position data and rotation data. The participant proximal/distal and dorsal/volar position data for point b, and the rotation data, were then summarized as the average of the data for the three trials. Repeated measures one-way ANOVAs (α = 0.05) were then performed using force as the independent variable; and proximal/distal position, dorsal/volar position and rotation as dependent variables. Pairwise comparisons were performed using multiple pairwise paired t-tests, with Bonferroni correction, between the levels of force. Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.2.3 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Repeatability

At 10 N, 20 N and 30 N of pinch force, the correlation of the proximal/distal position measurements of point b between trials 1 and 2 was 0.969, 0.834 and 0.947. The correlation of the dorsal/volar position measurements was 0.601, 0.839 and 0.947. The correlation of the rotation measurements was 0.798, 0.927 and 0.913.

Position

At 0 N of pinch force, point b on the articular surface of the first metacarpal was located 2.9 ± 0.6 mm distal to the trapezium coordinate system (Fig. 3). Significance was not observed for the repeated measures one-way ANOVA analyzing the effect of force on the position of the first metacarpal in the proximal/distal direction (p = 0.224). When the pinch force was increased from 0 N to 10 N, the proximal/distal position of the first metacarpal changed by −0.1 mm [95 % CI: (−0.3 mm, 0.1 mm); p = 0.5323]. When the pinch force was increased from 0 N to 20 N, the proximal/distal position of the first metacarpal changed by −0.1 mm [95 % CI: (−0.4 mm, 0.2 mm); p = 0.5457]. When the pinch force was increased from 0 N to 30 N, the proximal/distal position of the first metacarpal changed by −0.2 mm [95 % CI: (−0.5 mm, 0.0 mm); p = 0.0934]. Pairwise comparisons showed that as the pinch force was increased from 10 N to 20 N, the proximal/distal position of the first metacarpal changed by 0.0 mm [95 % CI: (−0.3 mm, 0.2 mm); p = 0.8332]. As the pinch force was increased from 20 N to 30 N, the proximal/distal position of the first metacarpal changed by −0.1 mm [95 % CI: (−0.3 mm, 0.0 mm); p = 0.1399].

Position of point b on the articular surface of the first metacarpal in the trapezium (proximal/distal, dorsal/volar) coordinate system at 10, 20 and 30 N of pinch force. The proximal and dorsal directions were designated as positive.

At 0 N of pinch force, point b on the articular surface of the first metacarpal was located 0.2 ± 0.4 mm volar to the trapezium coordinate system. The first metacarpal translated in the volar direction as the pinch force was increased (Fig. 3). Significance was observed for the repeated measures one-way ANOVA analyzing the effect of force on the position of the first metacarpal in the dorsal/volar direction (p = 0.017). When the pinch force was increased from 0 N to 10 N, the dorsal/volar position of the first metacarpal changed by −0.3 mm [95 % CI: (−0.5 mm, −0.1 mm); p = 0.0151]. When the pinch force was increased from 0 N to 20 N, the dorsal/volar position of the first metacarpal changed by −0.5 mm [95 % CI: (−0.8 mm, −0.1 mm); p = 0.0113]. When the pinch force was increased from 0 N to 30 N, the dorsal/volar position of the first metacarpal changed by −0.8 mm [95 % CI: (−1.4 mm, −0.2 mm); p = 0.0146]. Pairwise comparisons showed that as the pinch force was increased from 10 N to 20 N, the dorsal/volar position of the first metacarpal changed by −0.2 mm [95 % CI: (−0.4 mm, 0.0 mm); p = 0.117]. As the pinch force was increased from 20 N to 30 N, the dorsal/volar position of the first metacarpal changed by −0.3 mm [95 % CI: (−0.7 mm, 0.1 mm); p = 0.1541].

Rotation

The rotation of the first metacarpal increased positively as the pinch force was increased (Fig. 4). Significance was observed for the repeated measures one-way ANOVA analyzing the effect of force on the rotation of the first metacarpal (p = 0.029). As the pinch force was increased from 0 N to 10 N, the rotation of the first metacarpal was 0.7° [95 % CI: (−0.8°, 2.1°); p = 0.3239]. As the pinch force was increased from 0 N to 20 N, the rotation of the first metacarpal was 2.6° [95 % CI: (0.1°, 5.1°); p = 0.0416]. As the pinch force was increased from 0 N to 30 N, the rotation of the first metacarpal was 3.3° [95 % CI: (0.2°, 6.3°); p = 0.0393]. Pairwise comparisons showed that as the pinch force was increased from 10 N to 20 N, the rotation of the first metacarpal was 1.9° [95 % CI: (0.3°, 3.5°); p = 0.0222]. As the pinch force was increased from 20 N to 30 N, the rotation of the first metacarpal was 0.7° [95 % CI: (−0.2°, 1.5°); p = 0.1037].

Rotation results for the first metacarpal at 10, 20 and 30 N of pinch force. Counterclockwise rotations in the ultrasound images were identified as positive and correspond to abduction rotation of the first metacarpal.

Discussion

This study quantified the in vivo motion of the TMC joint during pinching using ultrasonographic imaging. An anatomical coordinate system was defined using the echogenic surface of the trapezium and the position of a point on the articular surface of the first metacarpal was tracked within this coordinate system during pinching to three prescribed force levels. It was shown that the first metacarpal translates in the volar direction, with respect to the trapezium, during pinching and that this movement increases with increasing pinch force. It was additionally shown that the first metacarpal rotates about the abduction/adduction axis and that this rotation increases in the abduction rotational direction with increasing pinch force. The results of this work provide a means of evaluating the motion of the healthy TMC joint using ultrasonographic imaging which may have greater availability with respect to other imaging modalities; thus, increasing the potential for the results to have translatability within a clinical setting. The results of this study can aid in the advancement of in vivo TMC joint motion evaluation during functional hand activity.

Ultrasonography is an imaging modality that has been commonly used in the biomechanical study of various joints throughout the body. In the elbow joint, changes in the distance between the most distal portion of the trochlea to the most proximal portion of the sublime tubercle were investigated under the influence of valgus elbow loading [18]. In the hip joint, ultrasonography was used to measure changes in the linear distance between the most superior point of the acetabular rim and the most superior point of the femoral head, under the imposition of long axis distraction [20]. Ultrasonographic study of the knee joint has analyzed the linear distance between the margin of the femoral epicondyle and the margin of the tibia [19]. In the current study, the most distal and most volar point on the articular surface of the trapezium was used to establish a coordinate system for which to track the location of a point on the articular surface of the first metacarpal. The most proximal point on the body of the first metacarpal and a point on the body of the first metacarpal distal to this point, were used to establish an axis for rotation analysis. The anatomical definitions employed in this study utilize joint features that are similarly recognizable as the features mentioned in the previous studies and establish a standardized means for ultrasonographic analysis of TMC joint motion. This standardization increases the applicability of the methodology and can have translatability in experimental and clinical settings.

The kinematics of the TMC joint during the functional hand activity of pinching have been detailed in previous studies. Computed tomographic imaging with stepwise pinching to 80 % of subject-specific maximal pinch force showed volar translation of the first metacarpal of 0.7 ± 1.5 mm [9]. Four-dimensional computed tomographic imaging of continuous pinching activity to 100 % of subject-specific maximal pinch force showed volar translation of the first metacarpal of 0.8 ± 0.6 mm [15]. For the current study, it was also shown that the first metacarpal translates in the volar direction with increasing pinch force. At 30 N of pinch force, the volar translation was approximately 0.8 mm. The direction and magnitude of first metacarpal translation agreed with the results observed for similar pinch force levels using computed tomographic analysis [9,15]. In the current study, the position of the first metacarpal in the proximal/distal direction was not significantly different from the initial position when the pinch force was increased. This result agrees with computed tomographic analyses which also showed marginal proximal/distal translation of the first metacarpal during pinching [9,15]. The rotational movement of the TMC joint is expected to be small in an isometric pinch task. The rotation of the first metacarpal was about the abduction/adduction axis and increased in the abduction rotational direction with increasing pinch force. At 30 N of pinch force, the rotation of the metacarpal was approximately 3.3°. The degree of rotation of the first metacarpal about this axis is also within the range of reported abduction/adduction rotation measured using computed tomography for approximately similar levels of pinch force [15]. The agreement between translational and rotational results for the first metacarpal of this study, with the results of previous work utilizing three-dimensional analyses, corroborates the effectiveness of ultrasonography in the analysis of TMC joint motion.

The functional hand activity of pinching can generate mechanical loading at the TMC joint [24]. The volar translation and abduction rotation of the TMC joint measured in the current study, and previous studies [9,15], indicates that the mechanical influences at the TMC joint during pinching can include shearing. Shearing has been identified as the primary contributor to cartilage degeneration [16], due to the motion of the first metacarpal on the trapezium, and has been documented through measurements of cartilage wear patterns of arthritic cadaveric TMC joint specimens [2,25,26]. In looking to advance TMC joint health monitoring, the use of ultrasonographic imaging to monitor and quantify the motion pattern of the TMC joint in real-time can provide patient-specific information relating to the motion of the metacarpal on the trapezium and the trajectory of potential cartilage degradation. Expanding the work for measurement of in vivo joint motion for additional hand activities, such as twisting or grasping, is possible. Such expansion may present with additional limitations regarding the two-dimensional viewing of the joint and will require further exploration.

The analysis of TMC joint motion has been an important topic of discussion, particularly in providing insight related to differences between healthy and structurally compromised joint behavior. Analyses studying the joint range of motion have been used to define the physiological workspace of the thumb, showing differences in kinematic capability between healthy individuals and individuals with TMC joint osteoarthritis [27]. Studies analyzing pinching have also shown differences in the in vivo joint behavior between healthy and arthritic joints [28]. The methods used for the current study can further contribute to these efforts whereby, in future work, ultrasonography can be used to highlight differences in TMC joint motion between healthy and arthritic joints for equivalent levels of functional hand activity. Ultrasonographic imaging demonstrates effectiveness in analyzing TMC joint health, typically by static imagery [17,22]. Ultrasonographic-measured differences in TMC joint motion between healthy and structurally compromised joints could support clinical translation, whereby assessment of TMC joint motion could be used to complement traditional static assessment.

There are limitations with our work. First, the sample size consisted of ten participants. The size of the sample may have rendered significance difficult to observe. Future investigations looking to make comparisons between healthy and pathological TMC joints will incorporate a larger sample. Second, only the right hand of each participant was analyzed. This allowed for consistency of handedness but could not account for hand dominance. Differences in TMC joint characteristics for the dominant hand may exist due to greater frequency of use and could be considered in future work. Third, ultrasound can only be used to visualize the TMC joint motion in two dimensions. Out of plane motion in the radial/ulnar direction was not assessed. However, the agreement of the study results for dorsal/volar and proximal/distal translation, as well as for abduction/adduction rotation, with previous work utilizing three-dimensional analyses, shows that our planar ultrasonographic imaging captures the motion of the joint without radiation exposure. Finally, the location and inclination of the ultrasound transducer was based on the view of the TMC joint and was not standardized between participants. This standardization unlikely influenced the main results as position and rotation were calculated relative to similar configuration without pinch force.

In conclusion, the TMC joint undergoes volar translation and abduction rotation during key pinch. The use of ultrasonographic imaging represents an advancement in TMC joint behavioral study, allowing for real time quantification of the joint motion without radiation exposure and enhanced potential for clinical applicability. Future studies may seek to analyze differences in TMC joint motion between healthy and arthritic joints.
