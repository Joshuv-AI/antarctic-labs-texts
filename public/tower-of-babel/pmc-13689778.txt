Thoracic Duct Obstruction as a Cause of Chylous Ascites After Pacemaker Implant
Authors: Banveet Kaur, Maxim Itkin, Robert D. Schaller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTransvenous leads of cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs) can cause central venous stenosis, impairing thoracic duct outflow, and result in chylous effusions.Case SummaryA 48-year-old man developed refractory chylous ascites/chylothorax after pacemaker implantation. Magnetic resonance lymphangiography demonstrated thoracic duct dilatation and mesenteric reflux secondary to venous stenosis. Thoracic duct thrombolysis provided only temporary improvement. Transvenous lead extraction, balloon venoplasty, and leadless pacemaker implantation resulted in complete resolution.DiscussionCIED-related venous obstruction can cause lymphatic hypertension with chylous ascites/chylothorax by impairing thoracic duct drainage. Unlike direct thoracic duct injury, venous obstruction disrupts lymphatic outflow rather than causing immediate leakage. Definitive treatment requires relief of the venous obstruction. Lead extraction with venoplasty restores lymphatic drainage, whereas leadless pacing avoids further transvenous venous injury.Take-Home MessagesCIED-related venous stenosis should be considered in patients with unexplained chylous effusions. Lead extraction with venoplasty can restore lymphatic drainage, and leadless pacing is an effective alternative.

Transvenous leads of cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs) can result in significant venous stenosis in up to 50% of implanted patients. However, most cases remain clinically silent because of the development of collateral venous circulation. Symptomatic venous obstruction occurs in approximately 5% of patients and may lead to clinically important sequelae, including superior vena cava syndrome, upper extremity edema, and, rarely, impaired lymphatic drainage.1

The thoracic duct (TD), the largest lymphatic vessel in the body, drains lymph from the abdomen, pelvis, and lower extremities and typically empties into the junction of the left internal jugular and subclavian veins. Obstruction at this venous confluence, whether from thrombosis or stenosis, can impair TD outflow, resulting in increased lymphatic pressure and leakage of chyle into adjacent compartments.2

Chylothorax is the most reported manifestation of lymphatic disruption in patients with CIED-related venous obstruction.3,4 In contrast, chyloperitoneum in this setting has not been previously described. We present a case of chyloperitoneum after transvenous permanent pacemaker (PPM) implantation, highlighting the diagnostic evaluation and therapeutic considerations.

History of Presentation

A 48-year-old man presented with recurrent chyloperitoneum requiring repeated paracenteses, with increasing frequency now necessitating weekly drainage. He subsequently developed additional symptoms, including shortness of breath, and was found to have a chylous pleural effusion requiring thoracentesis, along with persistent ascites.

Past Medical History

His medical history was notable for autoimmune hepatitis status post liver transplantation 12 years earlier, for which he was maintained on cyclosporine, sinus node dysfunction status post dual-chamber PPM implantation 2 years prior, hypertension, heart failure with moderately reduced ejection fraction (40%) due to a suspected inferior infarct, and end-stage renal disease, with recent initiation of hemodialysis. He was undergoing evaluation for renal transplantation, but recurrent paracenteses had precluded further work-up.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis of concurrent chyloperitoneum, chylous pleural effusion, and ascites in a patient with a PPM includes both device-related and systemic etiologies. Hemodynamic causes such as PPM syndrome, worsening left-sided heart failure, or right ventricular pacing-induced cardiomyopathy may elevate filling pressures, although these typically produce nonchylous effusions. In a patient with prior liver transplantation, considerations include recurrent cirrhosis, chronic rejection, portal hypertension, and post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder, whereas end-stage renal disease on hemodialysis raises the possibility of volume overload. Malignancy with metastatic involvement must also be considered. Prior central venous instrumentation, including hemodialysis catheters and transvenous leads, may rarely cause direct TD injury, though the delayed onset of symptoms makes this less likely. In contrast, transvenous leads within the left subclavian system predispose to central venous stenosis, which can impair TD outflow.2

Investigations

A 12-lead electrocardiogram demonstrated atrial pacing with a prolonged PR interval and a narrow QRS complex, as well as inferior Q waves and nonspecific ST-T wave changes. PPM interrogation revealed 80% atrial pacing and <1% ventricular pacing, with no significant premature ventricular complexes. Transthoracic echocardiography showed a normal left ventricular size with a left ventricular ejection fraction of 40%, moderate proximal septal hypertrophy, and hypokinesis of the basal to mid inferior wall. The right ventricle was normal in size with borderline systolic function. There was moderate mitral and tricuspid regurgitation with normal pulmonary artery pressures. Moderate aortic sclerosis was present, and a large right-sided pleural effusion was also noted.

Abdominal ultrasonography demonstrated a liver normal in size and echogenicity without focal lesions. The kidneys exhibited increased cortical echogenicity, consistent with medical renal disease. The inferior vena cava was normal in caliber, and moderate ascites was present; there was no biliary ductal dilatation.

Paracentesis was performed, and ascitic fluid analysis revealed a glucose level of 113 mg/dL, an amylase level of 45 U/L, a lactate dehydrogenase level of 85 U/L, a total protein level of 3.5 g/dL, and an albumin level of 2.3 g/dL, with a corresponding serum albumin level of 3.7 g/dL. The triglyceride level was markedly elevated at 796 mg/dL, consistent with chylous ascites. Thoracentesis demonstrated similar fluid characteristics.

Magnetic resonance lymphangiography demonstrated a tortuous, dilated TD with suspected reflux of contrast into the mesenteric lymphatics, with associated mild ascites. Pelvic and abdominal lymphangiography was performed, followed by contrast injection of the TD, which demonstrated sluggish flow with filling defects in the mid-duct. Catheter-directed lysis was then performed with 2 mg of tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) in 5 mL of saline administered via a microcatheter, with a 25-minute dwell time, followed by repeat lymphangiography. A second dose of 2 mg of tPA in 2 mL of saline was administered with a 15-minute dwell time, again followed by repeat imaging. A final dose of 1 mg of tPA in 1 mL of saline was delivered before microcatheter removal. The patient initially improved but continued to require paracenteses at progressively increasing intervals over the ensuing months and subsequently developed dyspnea requiring right-sided thoracentesis. Given persistent symptoms, a decision was made to proceed with lead extraction, venoplasty, and implantation of a leadless device.

Management

The patient was brought to the electrophysiology laboratory and placed under general anesthesia. Femoral venous access was obtained, and an intracardiac echocardiography catheter was advanced to the heart per extraction protocol. The PPM pocket was opened, the active fixation helixes were retracted, and the leads were pulled away from the myocardium with simple traction (Figure 2). A retained wire extraction technique was then used for the right ventricular lead, with placement of a 0.014-inch wire into the lead lumen and advancement to the tip.5 The lead was then snared from below using a Goose Neck snare (Medtronic) and removed via the femoral access site, leaving the wire in place within the prior lead course. Local venography demonstrated severe subclavian vein stenosis. Serial overlapping venoplasty was performed using a noncompliant 8-mm balloon (Conquest; Bard), revealing 2 focal high-grade stenoses near the first rib and clavicle (Figure 2, Video 2). An 8-mm drug-coated balloon (IN.PACT AV; Medtronic) was then deployed at these sites, with subsequent angiography demonstrating improved flow.

A dual-chamber leadless PPM system (AVEIR; Abbott) was then implanted via femoral access under fluoroscopic and intracardiac echocardiographic guidance. The atrial device was positioned at the lateral base of the right atrial appendage, and the ventricular device at the apical septum with adequate distance from the tricuspid valve. Hemostasis was achieved with figure-of-eight sutures and manual pressure. The patient was extubated without difficulty, and no immediate complications occurred.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was discharged the following day and seen in follow-up at 1 and 6 months. He required 1 additional paracentesis and 1 thoracentesis, and repeat TD lysis was considered. However, his symptoms progressively improved, with complete resolution of swelling. At 6 months, he was asymptomatic with a normally functioning dual-chamber leadless PPM.

Discussion

Chylous effusions are characterized by triglyceride-rich lymphatic fluid, typically with triglyceride levels >110 mg/dL, low cholesterol content, and a milky appearance.2 Accumulation of chyle in extralymphatic spaces, most commonly as chylothorax, may result from either TD obstruction or disruption. Obstruction at the venous junction, often due to central venous disease, impairs lymphatic drainage and increases intraluminal pressure, leading to leakage through collateral channels and resulting in chylothorax, chylopericardium, or chyloperitoneum. In contrast, direct disruption of the TD from surgery, trauma, or invasive procedures causes direct leakage into the pleural space. Although presentations may be similar, distinguishing these mechanisms is important for determining etiology and guiding management.

Previous reports have described TD obstruction after CIED implantation. One case involved innominate vein thrombosis 6 years after PPM implantation, requiring venoplasty to restore flow.6 Similarly, 2 cases of combined chylothorax and chylopericardium have been reported, one due to PPM lead–related venous stenosis and the other to a hemodialysis catheter, both successfully treated with catheter removal and venous stenting.6

Our patient similarly had catheter-related venous stenosis, leading to TD dysfunction, and secondary ductal leakage, resulting in chylothorax and chyloperitoneum. The transient response to tPA suggested a superimposed thrombus contributing to ductal obstruction; however, the lack of durability suggested an ongoing upstream pressure gradient. Definitive management in this scenario depends on treatment of the underlying cause, which in this case was addressed with lead extraction and venous recanalization using balloon venoplasty to restore venous patency and re-establish normal lymphatic drainage. Importantly, we used a drug-coated balloon, which has been increasingly used in peripheral and dialysis access interventions to reduce restenosis through local antiproliferative drug delivery.7

By eliminating intravascular leads, leadless PPMs avoid recurrent mechanical injury to the venous endothelium and reduce the risk of future thrombosis or stenosis. If leads are left in place, even after successful venoplasty with a drug-coated balloon, there remains a significant risk of recurrence, which in this case represented an unacceptable risk. Even with lead removal, outcomes may vary based on patient-specific factors. Notably, one reported case of severe venous and lymphatic obstruction after PPM implantation in a patient with prior chest radiation progressed to anasarca and fatal hemodynamic compromise despite lead extraction and aggressive supportive therapy.3

Direct injury to the TD has also been described during device upgrade, most often due to wire, sheath, or lead manipulation. These injuries typically require TD embolization for definitive management.4 This approach is generally well tolerated and effective in controlling chyle leak, as lymphatic flow is redirected through collateral channels that are usually sufficient to maintain drainage. However, impaired lymphatic outflow may lead to complications such as lower extremity edema, abdominal lymphatic congestion, or persistent ascites, particularly in patients with limited collateralization or elevated central venous pressures.2

Similarly, traumatic chylothorax has been reported after implantable cardioverter-defibrillator lead extraction for pocket infection in a patient who developed worsening hypoxic respiratory failure due to a new chylous pleural effusion and subsequently deteriorated and died.8 Chyle has also been identified within device pockets after traumatic CIED insertion, mimicking infection.9 Conversely, some cases initially suspected to represent chyle may instead reflect pseudochylothorax, caused by breakdown of a chronic hematoma within a closed cavity over time.10

Importantly, beyond mechanical disruption of TD drainage, the lymphatic system plays an emerging role in heart failure. Impaired lymphatic outflow can promote interstitial fluid accumulation, contributing to myocardial dysfunction and systemic congestion.2 Advances in imaging have highlighted this relationship and supported novel therapies such as external lymphatic decompression. Together, these findings emphasize the close link between the lymphatic and cardiovascular systems.

Conclusions

This case underscores the importance of recognizing lymphatic complications as an under-recognized consequence of CIED-related venous obstruction. In patients presenting with unexplained chylous effusions or refractory ascites after device implantation, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for either early TD injury or delayed central venous stenosis impairing lymphatic outflow. Multimodality imaging, including venography and lymphangiography, is often required to establish the diagnosis and guide management. Restoration of venous patency with lead extraction and venoplasty, along with removal of transvenous leads, can result in complete and durable resolution.
